In the Alaksandu Treaty he is listed among the gods who protect the treaty and the city. His exact sphere (war, sun, prophecy, archery) is unknown. The later Greek Apollo's domains — archery, music, prophecy, healing, plague — cannot be retrojected onto Apaliunas without evidence.

Form

No visual representation of Apaliunas survives. No cult statue, relief, or seal impression can be securely linked to him. If he resembled later Anatolian deities, he might have been depicted as a standing male figure, possibly with a bow or spear, but this is pure conjecture. The absolute lack of imagery is itself a historical fact: we know the name but not the face.

Powers

Divine witness and oath enforcement In the Alaksandu Treaty, Apaliunas is invoked as one of the gods who will witness the treaty and punish any oath-breaker. This gives him a juridical and punitive function common to all major deities in Hittite diplomacy. Alaksandu Treaty (CTH 76), c. 1280 BCE No specific powers are attributed beyond the general divine role of witnessing and enforcing oaths. The treaty does not describe his methods of punishment. None within the evidence; the lack of detail is the only limitation.

Civic protection (inferred) As a city deity of Wilusa, Apaliunas would have been expected to protect the city and its inhabitants. This is inferred from the standard role of city deities in Hittite and local Anatolian religion, not from explicit statements about Apaliunas himself. Inference from Hittite treaty formulas and Anatolian religious norms This is a reasonable inference, not a documented fact. The texts do not say 'Apaliunas protects Wilusa' in so many words. None; the inference is widely accepted but remains an inference.

Behavior & Habitat

Unknown. The Hittite texts give no description of his personality, cultic demands, or mythological deeds. He appears only as a name in divine witness lists and a passing military reference. His behavior must be reconstructed from the general role of city-protector deities in Bronze Age Anatolia — gods who guarded the city gates, received offerings, and were invoked in treaties to punish oath-breakers.

Accounts

A Hittite vassal king writes to his overlord about a military crisis involving the city of Wilusa and a certain Piyama-Radu, a rebel or enemy figure. In the fragmentary text, the god Apaliunas is mentioned — possibly in connection with a divine image being moved, a ritual, or a battle. The context is too broken to reconstruct the story, but it places Apaliunas in a moment of Bronze Age geopolitical tension, with Troy and its gods caught between Hittite power and local ambitions.

King Muwatalli II of Hatti formalizes an alliance with Alaksandu, king of Wilusa. The treaty lists a long sequence of divine witnesses — storm gods, sun goddesses, protective deities, mountains, rivers, and springs. Among them, in the local section, stands Apaliunas of Wilusa. By invoking him, both parties place their agreement under his divine authority, calling on him to bless the faithful and destroy the treacherous. This is the clearest evidence of Apaliunas's status as a major god of Troy.

Scholarly Debates
Is Apaliunas the ancestor of Greek Apollo?
Yes — Apaliunas is a Luwian rendering of *Apeljōn, a Proto-Greek theonym, and evidence of Apollo's Anatolian/Asian origin. The name is nearly identical, and Wilusa (Troy) was in contact with the Greek world, making cultural transmission plausible. ⇌ No / Unproven — The name resemblance could be coincidental or reflect a shared Indo-European heritage rather than direct borrowing. No cultic attributes connect Apaliunas to Apollo, and the evidence is too sparse to support a confident genetic link.

He was a major god of Wilusa, perhaps the city's chief deity, given his inclusion in the treaty's divine witness list. ⇌ He was a minor deity, one of many local gods, whose inclusion in the treaty reflects standard practice of invoking all local gods, not special prominence.

What We Don't Know

1. No visual representation of Apaliunas has been identified. 2. No mythological narratives about him survive. 3. The exact nature of his cult (temple, priests, festivals) is unknown. 4. The relationship between Apaliunas and the broader Hittite pantheon is unclear. 5. The debate over his connection to Apollo remains unresolved due to the scarcity of evidence. 6. No Luwian-language texts (as opposed to Hittite) mention him, though the name is Luwian. 7. The archaeological context of Wilusa (Troy) has not yielded any inscription naming Apaliunas. 8. Access to full cuneiform editions of the relevant texts was limited; this research relies on translations and secondary scholarship. 9. Further research in Hittite archives (Boğazköy) may yield new fragments. 10. The potential connection to Greek Apollo requires continued philological and archaeological investigation.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Apaliunas as a single, faint star in a distant galaxy of ancient gods. We can measure its light (the name in two texts), we can guess at its size (a city deity), but we cannot see its surface (no myths, no images). The question of whether it is the same star as the bright Greek Apollo is like asking whether a faint radio signal from deep space is the echo of a civilization we already know — the evidence is tantalizing, the proof is lacking, and the debate will continue as long as there are scholars with sharp eyes and open minds. Apaliunas is the ultimate 'maybe' of ancient religion: the god who might have been the seed of a legend, or might have been a name that simply sounded like one.
