Who It Is

Imagine a god whose entire existence is known from two letters — not divine letters, but the dry diplomatic correspondence of a Bronze Age empire. In one, he is a witness to a treaty between a Hittite king and the ruler of a city that would one day be immortalized as Troy. In the other, he appears in a fragmentary report of a military crisis, a name flickering in the gap of a broken tablet. His name is Apaliunas, and he may be the ghost behind one of the most famous gods of all time — Apollo. For centuries, scholars have debated whether this shadowy Anatolian deity is the ancestor of the Greek god of light, music, and prophecy, or a local god whose name merely echoes across the ages. The evidence is maddeningly thin, the stakes surprisingly high, and the story — as far as it can be told — is a detective tale written in cuneiform.

Unknown. The Hittite texts give no description of his personality, cultic demands, or mythological deeds. He appears only as a name in divine witness lists and a passing military reference. His behavior must be reconstructed from the general role of city-protector deities in Bronze Age Anatolia — gods who guarded the city gates, received offerings, and were invoked in treaties to punish oath-breakers.

Etymology — The name Apaliunas is widely considered by scholars to be a Luwian or Hittite rendering of the theonym *Apeljōn, a reconstructed Proto-Greek form of the name Apollo. Powers
Divine witness and oath enforcement

In the Alaksandu Treaty, Apaliunas is invoked as one of the gods who will witness the treaty and punish any oath-breaker. This gives him a juridical and punitive function common to all major deities in Hittite diplomacy.

Limit: No specific powers are attributed beyond the general divine role of witnessing and enforcing oaths. The treaty does not describe his methods of punishment.

Civic protection (inferred)

As a city deity of Wilusa, Apaliunas would have been expected to protect the city and its inhabitants. This is inferred from the standard role of city deities in Hittite and local Anatolian religion, not from explicit statements about Apaliunas himself.

Limit: This is a reasonable inference, not a documented fact. The texts do not say 'Apaliunas protects Wilusa' in so many words.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No visual representation of Apaliunas survives. No cult statue, relief, or seal impression can be securely linked to him. If he resembled later Anatolian deities, he might have been depicted as a standing male figure, possibly with a bow or spear, but this is pure conjecture. The absolute lack of imagery is itself a historical fact: we know the name but not the face.

Symbols — None attested. The bow and lyre, so iconic for Greek Apollo, have no Bronze Age Anatolian evidence for Apaliunas. The only symbol that might connect them is the name itself, which is a philological symbol of a possible cultural transmission.

Domains — Best attested as a city deity of Wilusa (Troy). In the Alaksandu Treaty he is listed among the gods who protect the treaty and the city. His exact sphere (war, sun, prophecy, archery) is unknown. The later Greek Apollo's domains — archery, music, prophecy, healing, plague — cannot be retrojected onto Apaliunas without evidence.

Accounts I
The Manapa-Tarhunta Letter: A God in a War ZoneManapa-Tarhunta Letter (CTH 181), Hittite archives

c. 1290–1272 BCE

A Hittite vassal king writes to his overlord about a military crisis involving the city of Wilusa and a certain Piyama-Radu, a rebel or enemy figure. In the fragmentary text, the god Apaliunas is mentioned — possibly in connection with a divine image being moved, a ritual, or a battle. The context is too broken to reconstruct the story, but it places Apaliunas in a moment of Bronze Age geopolitical tension, with Troy and its gods caught between Hittite power and local ambitions. The letter demonstrates that Apaliunas was an active cult figure in Wilusa during the late 13th century BCE, with enough importance to be mentioned in royal correspondence. The exact outcome of the conflict is lost.

Accounts II
The Alaksandu Treaty: A God in the Witness BoxAlaksandu Treaty (CTH 76), Hittite archives

c. 1280 BCE

King Muwatalli II of Hatti formalizes an alliance with Alaksandu, king of Wilusa. The treaty lists a long sequence of divine witnesses — storm gods, sun goddesses, protective deities, mountains, rivers, and springs. Among them, in the local section, stands Apaliunas of Wilusa. By invoking him, both parties place their agreement under his divine authority, calling on him to bless the faithful and destroy the treacherous. This is the clearest evidence of Apaliunas's status as a major god of Troy. The treaty provides the definitive attestation of Apaliunas as a city deity of Wilusa and anchors his worship in the Bronze Age. It also gives scholars a fixed date for his cult's existence.

Is Apaliunas the ancestor of Greek Apollo?Yes — Apaliunas is a Luwian rendering of *Apeljōn, a Proto-Greek theonym, and evidence of Apollo's Anatolian/Asian origin. The name is nearly identical, and Wilusa (Troy) was in contact with the Greek world, making cultural transmission plausible.No / Unproven — The name resemblance could be coincidental or reflect a shared Indo-European heritage rather than direct borrowing. No cultic attributes connect Apaliunas to Apollo, and the evidence is too sparse to support a confident genetic link.
Was Apaliunas a major god or a minor local deity?He was a major god of Wilusa, perhaps the city's chief deity, given his inclusion in the treaty's divine witness list.He was a minor deity, one of many local gods, whose inclusion in the treaty reflects standard practice of invoking all local gods, not special prominence.

Apaliunas was 'the god of Troy' and the direct equivalent of Apollo, with the same powers and myths. — We know Apaliunas was a deity of Wilusa, but we have no evidence of his powers, myths, or attributes. The link to Apollo is a philological hypothesis, not a documented equivalence.

The Hittites 'worshipped Apollo' centuries before the Greeks. — The Hittites worshipped Apaliunas, a deity whose name may be related to Apollo's. This is not the same as worshipping the Greek god Apollo with his full panoply of attributes. The relationship is a matter of debate, not settled fact.

Apaliunas proves that Apollo came from the Near East. — It suggests a possible Anatolian origin or influence, but the evidence is limited to a name in two documents. Many scholars accept the connection, but it is not 'proof' of a Near Eastern origin.
