Who It Is

Before the thunder-god Perun, before the life-giving Dazhbog, there was the path itself — the silent, blazing road of the great Khors, the sun-disc that crossed the sky by day and the underworld by night, and whose road was so liminal that a werewolf-prince could run it in the dark. Khors is the most enigmatic of the Slavic gods: a solar deity with an Iranian name, a cosmic path with no face, a god who was worshipped in princely Kiev and then vanished so completely that every detail about him is a scholarly battlefield.

As a solar deity, Khors's 'behavior' would be the sun's own: he rises, crosses the sky, and sets. The Igor Tale's reference to 'the path of the great Khors' suggests a defined, almost mechanical, celestial route. His night-time aspect is implied by the werewolf crossing his path at night, suggesting he also governs the underworld passage of the sun, a common Indo-European solar motif (cf. the Greek Helios sailing his cup across Oceanus at night).

Powers
Solar Dominion

As a sun god, Khors controls the sun's daily journey across the sky. The Igor Tale's 'path of the great Khors' implies he is the architect of the solar route.

Limit: His power seems impersonal and mechanical — he is a path, not a personal agent. Unlike Perun, who throws lightning, Khors does not act; he merely exists as the sun's course.

Night Passage / Underworld Crossing

The Igor Tale's werewolf-prince crosses Khors's path at night, implying the sun also travels beneath the earth at night, a journey Khors governs. This is a standard Indo-European solar motif (Helios, Sol in his chariot, etc.).

Limit: This is inferred from the werewolf crossing, not explicitly stated. The night-crossing may be a poetic image rather than a theological claim.

Werewolf / Liminality Association

The Igor Tale explicitly connects Khors's path with a werewolf (Vseslav of Polotsk). This suggests Khors had an association with transformation, wolf-magic, and the liminal boundary between day and night, human and beast.

Limit: This is a single poetic reference, possibly a literary flourish rather than a cultic attribute.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No surviving description of Khors's physical form exists. Unlike Perun, who is described in the Primary Chronicle (wooden head, silver head, gold moustache), Khors is merely named. We can only infer: if he was a solar disc god, he may have been represented as a disc, a face, or a horse (given the name's possible connection to 'horse' in some folk etymologies, though this is linguistically unsupported). Symbols — The solar disc, the sun-wheel, the path/trail across the sky, possibly the horse (if the name is folk-etymologized), the wolf (as a night-crossing entity), and fire (as solar manifestation).

Domains — The solar disc itself; the celestial path across the sky; the night-crossing under the earth; possibly the liminal zone between day and night where wolves and werewolves roam.

Accounts I
Vladimir's PantheonPrimary Chronicle

c. 980

Prince Vladimir of Kiev erects a pantheon of six idols on a hill outside his palace: Perun, Khors, Dazhbog, Stribog, Simargl, and Mokosh. Khors is placed second in the list, after Perun, suggesting high status in the official state cult. This is the only narrative in which Khors appears as a worshipped idol. The pantheon was short-lived; Vladimir converted to Christianity in 988 and ordered the idols destroyed. Khors's idol was likely dragged through the streets and thrown into the Dnieper, as described for Perun.

Accounts II
The Werewolf Prince and the Path of KhorsThe Tale of Igor's Campaign

c. 1185-1200

The epic describes the legendary Prince Vseslav of Polotsk, a sorcerer-prince who 'prowled in the night like a wolf' and 'crossed the path of the great Khors' on his supernatural journeys. This is the only narrative that gives Khors a cosmic function: a path across the sky that supernatural beings traverse. This poetic reference is the basis for Khors's association with werewolves and the night-crossing sun. It also suggests Khors was still remembered as a celestial force a century after Vladimir's Christianization.

Khors is a god of dance or chorus. — This is a folk etymology based on the Slavic word 'khor' (chorus/dance). There is no evidence linking Khors to dance.

Khors is a horse god. — The name's similarity to 'horse' is coincidental. The Iranian etymology points to 'sun', not 'horse'. No source links Khors to horses.

Khors is the same as Dazhbog. — They are listed as separate gods in the Primary Chronicle. The Igor Tale pairing is ambiguous, but the default reading is that they are distinct.

Khors was a major, widely-worshipped god. — He appears only in elite, princely contexts (Vladimir's pantheon, the Igor Tale). There is no evidence of a popular cult, folk practice, or devotional tradition. He may have been a courtly or imported deity, not a folk god.
