Who It Is

Papa-tū-ā-nuku (Earth Mother Spread Wide) is the primordial earth mother in Māori cosmology, the partner of Rangi-nui (Sky Father) and mother of all gods and living beings. She and Rangi were locked in a tight embrace, creating a world of darkness where their children — the departmental gods — lived pressed between them. When Tāne pushed Rangi away, separating his parents, Papa groaned in pain and loss, and her body became the earth. She provides for all her children, offering them food, shelter, and the physical ground of existence. In some accounts, when the gods were starving after the separation, Papa helped Tāne find the kūmara (sweet potato) to feed them. In Hawaiian tradition, she is known as Papahānaumoku ('Papa Who Gave Birth to Islands'), the earth mother who, with Wākea (Sky), gave birth to the Hawaiian Islands. The Papahānaumokuākea Marine National Monument is named after her. Papa represents the feminine principle as earth: nurturing, patient, abundant, but also the one who bears the weight of the sky's loss and the one whose body we inhabit.

Origins

Papa and Rangi emerged from Te Kore (the Void). They embraced and had children who lived in darkness. After Tāne separated them, Papa turned toward the underworld and became the earth that sustains all life. In Hawaiian tradition, Papahānaumoku gave birth to the islands and kalo (taro).

The Approach

In the beginning, there was only darkness. From that darkness emerged Ranginui, the sky father, and Papatūānuku, the earth mother, locked in a tight embrace. Their children, trapped between them, yearned for light and space. This is the story of their separation—a tale of love, violence, and the birth of the world as Māori know it.

Content advisory. This entry includes descriptions of parental conflict, violence, and separation. It discusses themes of family betrayal and rebellion. No graphic depictions are included, but the emotional weight of the narrative may be intense for some readers.
Identity

Papatūānuku

Pronunciation Papa-too-ah-noo-koo

Known As

PapaPapa-tū-ā-nukuPapatuanukuEarth Mother

Etymology

Papatūānuku is often translated as 'Earth that spreads abroad' or 'Earth Mother'. 'Papa' means 'earth' or 'ground', 'tū' can mean 'stand' or 'rise', and 'nuku' refers to 'earth' or 'land'. The name is sometimes interpreted as 'the earth that extends everywhere'.

Cultural Ownership

Māori (indigenous people of Aotearoa New Zealand)

Disputed Identifications

Some early European scholars attempted to equate Papatūānuku with Gaia from Greek mythology, but this is a false equivalence. Within Māori tradition, there is no single canonical version; different iwi (tribes) have their own variations of the creation story, and the name can also refer to a specific rock formation or a general term for earth.

Description

Appearance

Papatūānuku is personified as the earth herself. She is often depicted as a reclining woman, with her body forming the land. In art, she may be shown with green skin to represent vegetation, or with hills and valleys as her contours. Her breasts are sometimes symbolized by hills, and rivers are her veins.

Symbols & Iconography

The earth itself is her symbol. She is associated with the colour green, with fertile soil, and with the land's life-giving properties. In contemporary Māori art, she is often depicted in a stylized form, with patterns representing the landscape.

Behaviour & Personality

Papatūānuku is a nurturing, life-giving figure, but she is also grieving and longing. She is a passive figure in the creation story, primarily acting through her children. She is seen as the source of all life and the final resting place for all things.

Domains & Habitats

Her domain is the earth itself—the soil, the rocks, the mountains, and the valleys. She is present everywhere on land. She is also associated with the underworld, as the dead return to her.

Version Differences

Different iwi have variations of the creation story. In some, Papa is the primary parent; in others, Rangi is more prominent. Some versions describe Papa as having multiple husbands or as being involved in other events. The separation story itself varies in details, such as which child initiated the separation and how it was accomplished.

Powers & Abilities

Primordial Earth Creation

Source Version Pan-Māori

Description

Papatūānuku, with Ranginui, is the source of all life. She is the earth from which all living things emerge and to which they return.

Limitations

She is not an active agent; her power is passive and generative.

Contradictions

Some versions give her more agency, but most present her as a passive figure.

Fertility

Source Version Pan-Māori

Description

She provides fertility to the land, allowing crops to grow and animals to thrive.

Limitations

Her fertility is tied to the natural cycle and can be affected by human actions.

Contradictions

None noted.

Reception of the Dead

Source Version Pan-Māori

Description

She receives the spirits of the deceased back into her body, as the earth is the final resting place.

Limitations

This is a passive function; she does not judge or guide the dead.

Contradictions

Some traditions may have specific underworld deities, but Papa is generally the earth that contains them.

Sighs and Tremors

Source Version Common Māori tradition

Description

Her sighs are said to be the mist that rises from the earth, and her trembling is felt as earthquakes.

Limitations

These are metaphorical interpretations of natural phenomena.

Contradictions

Some may attribute earthquakes to other gods, like Rūaumoko, the god of earthquakes, who is her unborn child.

Stories & Myths

The Separation of Rangi and Papa

Source Various Māori oral traditions; recorded by Grey, Best, and othersCulture MāoriPeriod Mythological time

Summary

In the beginning, Rangi (sky) and Papa (earth) lay in a close embrace, and their children lived in the darkness between them. The children, tired of the darkness, decided to separate their parents. Tāne, the god of forests, eventually pushed them apart, using his legs. Rangi was forced upward to become the sky, and Papa remained below as the earth. This separation brought light to the world.

Consequences

The separation brought light and space, but also conflict. Tāwhirimātea, the god of winds and storms, was angry at the separation and fought against his brothers, causing storms and chaos. Papa's grief is said to be the mist that rises from the earth. The separation also allowed for the creation of humanity and the world as we know it.

Variants

In some versions, it is Tāne who separates them; in others, it is Tūmatauenga or a collective effort. Some versions say that Papa and Rangi were separated by cutting, not pushing. The details of the children's complaints and the method of separation vary.

Papa and the Creation of the World

Source Various Māori traditionsCulture MāoriPeriod Mythological time

Summary

After the separation, Papa became the earth, and her body was used to create the features of the world. Her bones became the rocks, her blood became the rivers, and her hair became the forests. This is a common motif in Māori cosmology.

Participants

Papa

Consequences

The world is formed from her body, and she remains the source of all life.

Variants

Different iwi may have different details about which body part became what.

The Reunion Attempt

Source Some traditionsCulture MāoriPeriod Mythological time

Summary

In some versions, Papa and Rangi long for each other, and their longing causes natural phenomena. For example, the rain is Rangi's tears, and the mist is Papa's sighs.

Separation of Parents

Source Various Māori traditions

Description

The children forcibly separate their parents, causing grief and distress to both. This is a central act of violence in the creation story.

Scholarly Context

This is often interpreted as a necessary act for creation, but it also highlights themes of rebellion and consequences.

War Among Brothers

Source Various Māori traditions

Description

After the separation, Tāwhirimātea wages war against his brothers, causing storms and destruction. This conflict is part of the cosmological order.

Scholarly Context

This explains natural phenomena and the ongoing struggles between gods.

Earthquakes as Unborn Child

Source Some Māori traditions

Description

In some traditions, Rūaumoko, the unborn child of Papa, causes earthquakes as he moves in her womb. This can be seen as a source of natural disasters.

Scholarly Context

This provides a mythological explanation for seismic activity.

Tūmatauenga or another child did it.

Is Papa a passive or active figure?

Research Scope

This dossier focuses on the Māori primordial earth mother Papatūānuku, also known as Papa. It covers her identity, primary evidence from oral traditions and early ethnographic records, her role in the creation narrative, relationships with her children and husband, her representation in art and culture, and her modern reception. The research draws on scholarly works, Māori oral histories, and contemporary Māori perspectives. Limitations include the lack of pre-colonial written records and the diversity of tribal (iwi) traditions, which may vary. The research cutoff date is August 2026.
