Who It Is

In the beginning, there was only darkness. From that darkness emerged Ranginui, the sky father, and Papatūānuku, the earth mother, locked in a tight embrace. Their children, trapped between them, yearned for light and space. This is the story of their separation—a tale of love, violence, and the birth of the world as Māori know it.

Papatūānuku is a nurturing, life-giving figure, but she is also grieving and longing. She is a passive figure in the creation story, primarily acting through her children. She is seen as the source of all life and the final resting place for all things.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Papatūānuku is often translated as 'Earth that spreads abroad' or 'Earth Mother'. 'Papa' means 'earth' or 'ground', 'tū' can mean 'stand' or 'rise', and 'nuku' refers to 'earth' or 'land'. The name is sometimes interpreted as 'the earth that extends everywhere'.

Pronunciation — Papa-too-ah-noo-koo

Aliases — Papa, Papa-tū-ā-nuku, Papatuanuku, Earth Mother

Ownership — Māori (indigenous people of Aotearoa New Zealand)

Powers
Primordial Earth Creation

Papatūānuku, with Ranginui, is the source of all life. She is the earth from which all living things emerge and to which they return.

Limit: She is not an active agent; her power is passive and generative.

Fertility

She provides fertility to the land, allowing crops to grow and animals to thrive.

Limit: Her fertility is tied to the natural cycle and can be affected by human actions.

Reception of the Dead

She receives the spirits of the deceased back into her body, as the earth is the final resting place.

Limit: This is a passive function; she does not judge or guide the dead.

Sighs and Tremors

Her sighs are said to be the mist that rises from the earth, and her trembling is felt as earthquakes.

Limit: These are metaphorical interpretations of natural phenomena.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Papatūānuku is personified as the earth herself. She is often depicted as a reclining woman, with her body forming the land. In art, she may be shown with green skin to represent vegetation, or with hills and valleys as her contours. Her breasts are sometimes symbolized by hills, and rivers are her veins.

Symbols — The earth itself is her symbol. She is associated with the colour green, with fertile soil, and with the land's life-giving properties. In contemporary Māori art, she is often depicted in a stylized form, with patterns representing the landscape.

Domains — Her domain is the earth itself—the soil, the rocks, the mountains, and the valleys. She is present everywhere on land. She is also associated with the underworld, as the dead return to her.

Accounts I
The Separation of Rangi and PapaVarious Māori oral traditions; recorded by Grey, Best, and others

Mythological time

In the beginning, Rangi (sky) and Papa (earth) lay in a close embrace, and their children lived in the darkness between them. The children, tired of the darkness, decided to separate their parents. Tāne, the god of forests, eventually pushed them apart, using his legs. Rangi was forced upward to become the sky, and Papa remained below as the earth. This separation brought light to the world. The separation brought light and space, but also conflict. Tāwhirimātea, the god of winds and storms, was angry at the separation and fought against his brothers, causing storms and chaos. Papa's grief is said to be the mist that rises from the earth. The separation also allowed for the creation of humanity and the world as we know it.

Accounts II
Papa and the Creation of the WorldVarious Māori traditions

Mythological time

After the separation, Papa became the earth, and her body was used to create the features of the world. Her bones became the rocks, her blood became the rivers, and her hair became the forests. This is a common motif in Māori cosmology. The world is formed from her body, and she remains the source of all life.

The Reunion AttemptSome traditions

Mythological time

In some versions, Papa and Rangi long for each other, and their longing causes natural phenomena. For example, the rain is Rangi's tears, and the mist is Papa's sighs. This explains the connection between sky and earth.

Papatūānuku is the same as Gaia from Greek mythology. — While both are earth mothers, they are distinct figures from different cultures with different stories and contexts.

Papatūānuku is a passive figure with no power. — While she is often acted upon, she is the source of all life and has generative power.

The separation of Rangi and Papa was a peaceful event. — It was a violent act that caused grief and conflict.
