Who It Is

Somewhere between midnight and morning, while a child sleeps with a gap in their smile and a tiny enamel treasure under the pillow, a bargain older than memory and newer than you think is quietly completed. A tooth for a coin, a lost piece of the body for luck, growth, or pocket money — the Tooth Fairy is the smallest economist in childhood, and the most geographically confused.

Attested behavior (Anglophone): Nocturnal, stealthy, benevolent, transactional. Enters child's bedroom while child sleeps, removes tooth from under pillow or bedside table, leaves payment/gift, departs unseen. No speech attested in oral tradition; personality is projection. Parental mediation is explicit: folklore advises mothers to stock 5-cent counter trinkets (1908) and later to leave notes praising dental hygiene (Lansky). Variant behaviors: El Ratón Pérez collects tooth from under pillow and leaves coin/gift; La Petite Souris does same; Basque Mari Teilatukoa catches teeth thrown

Powers
Nocturnal stealth entry and exchange

Ability to enter child's bedroom undetected while child sleeps, locate tooth under pillow/table/shoe, remove it, and leave payment without waking child. Never described as requiring key or chimney; implied teleportation or miniaturization.

Limit: Cannot or does not appear while child is awake; exchange fails if child stays awake or tooth not placed correctly. Parents must facilitate. No attested ability to force tooth removal — 1908 source notes fairy as incentive for child to allow loose tooth removal.

Valuation and payment

Leaves coin, bill, or small gift in place of tooth. Value is socially determined, not fixed by lore.

Limit: Payment constrained by family economics, peer reports, inflation, and parental discretion. Author Vicki Lansky notes families may pay more for 'perfect' (non-decayed) teeth and leave hygiene-praise notes — implying conditional valuation.

Tooth collection and presumed use (unspecified)

Takes possession of deciduous tooth. Lore rarely states purpose; children's books invent uses (building fairy castle, making fairy dust, etc.) but oral tradition is silent.

Limit: No attested power derived from collected teeth in Anglophone lore, unlike medieval witch belief where possessing tooth gave power over person.

Flight / small-scale mobility (fairy form)

Wings, wand, pixie flight depicted in dominant visual tradition (Tinker Bell-type).

Limit: Not universal — mouse variants run/climb, Basque roof figure walks roof, Middle Eastern sun requires throw.

Apotropaic denial (medieval antecedent, not fairy power)

In medieval England/Europe, burning or burying tooth denied witches material for sympathetic magic and prevented eternal searching in afterlife.

Limit: This is human ritual action, not fairy ability; included to distinguish antecedent from fairy lore.

Accounts I
The First Printed Tooth Fairy (Chicago Daily Tribune Household Hints)Chicago Daily Tribune, 'Household Hints' column

1908, Progressive Era

The earliest known record of the tooth fairy tradition as a named gift-bringer. Presented as practical parenting advice: tell a refractory child about the tooth fairy who will take a tooth placed under the pillow at night and leave a small gift, so the child will allow a loose tooth to be removed. Mothers are advised to stock up at the 5-cent counter with trinkets for the exchange. No description of fairy's appearance or origin is given; function is purely incentive and comfort. Establishes pillow-exchange motif and parental orchestration that defines all later Anglophone practice; becomes template for century of folklore.

El Ratoncito Pérez for the Boy KingLuis Coloma, tale written for King Alfonso XIII

1894

When 8-year-old King Alfonso XIII loses a milk tooth, Jesuit author Luis Coloma invents a story at Queen Maria Cristina's request: a little mouse named Pérez lives with his family in a cookie box in Madrid, travels by city pipes, and collects children's teeth left under pillows, leaving a gift. The mouse is polite, brave, and mouse-sized, not fairy-like. The tale formalizes the Hispanic tooth-mouse as a distinct figure from the Anglophone fairy, though function is identical. Creates canonical Hispanic tradition almost universal in Spain and Latin America; later adopted by Colgate marketing in Venezuela and Spain; regional names diverge (Ratón de los Dientes in Mexico/Peru/Chile, Ratón Pérez in Argentina/Venezuela/Uruguay/Colombia).

The Burning Teeth and the Witch's PowerMedieval English and European oral superstition, recorded in folklore compendia

Middle Ages (medieval period, pre-1908)

Children in England were instructed to burn their baby teeth, under threat of spending eternity searching for them in the afterlife. Parallel belief held that witches could gain total power over a person if they obtained one of their teeth, so teeth were buried or burned to deny witches material. This is not a Tooth Fairy story but the protective rite most often cited as antecedent. Explains why teeth were hidden/destroyed rather than exchanged; provides contrast for later gift-exchange innovation. Shows tooth as potent bodily relic.

Accounts II
The Little Mouse Who Buys Teeth (France, Belgium, Scotland, Italy, South Africa)Oral folklore: La Petite Souris (France/French Belgium), white fairy rat (Lowland Scotland), Topolino/Fatina (Italy), Tandemuis (Afrikaans South Africa)

Undated folk tradition, documented 20th century

In France and French-speaking Belgium, La Petite Souris (The Little Mouse) visits at night to purchase teeth with coins. In Lowland Scotland, a white fairy rat does the same. In Italy, Fatina dei denti is often replaced by Topolino (little mouse) or Formichina (little ant, Veneto), and in some areas Saint Apollonia (Santa Polonia) holds the role — her martyrdom involved having teeth broken, making her patron of dentistry. In Afrikaans families, children leave teeth in a shoe for Tandemuis to replace with money. All share rodent/shoe/coin motif without fairy wings. Demonstrates independent convergence on mouse as tooth-collector (mice's prominent incisors, association with gnawing/growth); challenges idea of single fairy origin.

Throwing Teeth for Straight Growth and Luck (Japan and Korea)East Asian oral tradition, ethnographic reports

Traditional, documented 20th-21st century; Korean magpie link possibly Middle Korean etymology

Japan: lost upper teeth are thrown straight down to the ground, lower teeth straight up into the air, so incoming permanent teeth grow straight. Korea: both upper and lower teeth are thrown onto the roof; if the magpie (Korean national bird, 까치 Ka-chi) finds the tooth, it brings good luck. Scholars propose two etymological/mythological links: Ka-chi sounding like 'new teeth' in Middle Korean, or magpie's role as messenger between gods and humans. No fairy or mouse collects; trajectory and bird/sky are agents. Shows throwing paradigm vs collecting paradigm; links tooth loss to bodily growth and fortune rather than commerce.

Teeth to the Sun, to Allah, and to the Chicken CoopMiddle Eastern and West African oral tradition; 13th-century Arabic textual mention by Izz bin Hibat Allah Al Hadid; modern ethnography for Mali

13th century textual attestation (Middle East) to present; Mali contemporary

In Middle Eastern countries (Iraq, Jordan, Egypt, Sudan), children throw baby teeth up into the sky to the sun or to Allah — a tradition possibly originating in pre-Islamic offering, mentioned by Izz bin Hibat Allah Al Hadid in the 13th century. In Mali, children throw baby teeth into the chicken coop to receive a chicken the following day. In both, disposal is offering/exchange with celestial or domestic animal recipient, not a fairy. Attests deep historical depth for sky-offering tooth rites, predating Anglophone fairy by centuries; provides non-commercial exchange model (tooth for chicken).

The Playlet That Gave the Fairy a FaceEsther Watkins Arnold, 8-page children's play 'The Tooth Fairy'

1927

Children's playwright Esther Watkins Arnold brings to life an extraordinary, elf-like creature and playfully christens it 'the Tooth fairy' in a short playlet. This theatrical embodiment helps shift the fairy from household hint to character with personality and appearance, paving way for later book and film depictions. Helps standardize name and anthropomorphic portrayal; cited as early literary dramatization after 1908 newspaper hint.

The Tooth Fairy is an ancient European fairy dating back to the Middle Ages or earlier. — The named, gift-bringing Tooth Fairy is first attested in 1908 in an American newspaper household hint. Medieval tooth customs (burning/burying to thwart witches or avoid afterlife searching) were protective rites, not a fairy exchange, and show discontinuity in function.

Every culture has the Tooth Fairy, just with different names. — Only Anglophone Western cultures have a 'fairy' per se. Hispanic, Francophone, and other traditions have a mouse (Pérez, Souris, Topolino), while many cultures have no collector at all but throw teeth to sun, roof, magpie, or chicken coop for luck/growth. These are separate paradigms.

The Tooth Fairy always looks like Tinker Bell — female, wings, wand, tutu. — Survey and book data show wide variation: male fairies, bunnies, mice, dragons, bears, bats, dental hygienists, old men, and cigar-smoking flying men are all documented. 26% in Wells study did not imagine a female fairy at all.

There is a set price the Tooth Fairy pays (e.g., $1 or $5). — Payment varies by country, family economic status, peer reports, inflation, and whether it's the first tooth. US averages moved from $1.30 (1998) to $6.23 (2023) to $5.01 (2025) per Delta Dental, with wide distribution.

The Tooth Fairy's collected teeth are used to build a fairy castle or make fairy dust (canonical lore). — Earliest sources are silent on what happens to teeth after collection. Castle/dust explanations are modern children's book inventions to answer children's questions, not traditional folklore.
