Who It Is

In the bustling agora of Mantineia, among bronze heroes and stone gods, Pausanias was shown a single bronze woman — not a goddess, not a nymph, but a princess whose entire myth is a name, a father, and a statue. Diomeneia, daughter of Arcas, survives only because a second-century traveler bothered to write down what his local guide said: 'this is said to be Diomeneia.'

No behavior, personality, speech, or deeds attested. Pausanias records only kinship (daughter of Arcas) and the statue's presence. No myth narrates her actions, motives, or character. Any personality reconstruction would be fabrication. Status: unknown / unattested.

Powers
No supernatural powers attested

Diomeneia is presented as a mortal Arcadian princess (thugatēr Arkatos). No ancient source attributes to her any divine power, metamorphosis, magic, prophetic ability, martial feat, or superhuman trait. This absence is itself the attested state.

Limit: Mortal status implies standard human limitations, but no death, vulnerability, or weakness narrative survives.

Royal status and commemorative heroization

Social power only: as daughter of King Arcas, eponym of Arcadia, she holds princely rank in genealogical hierarchy. Posthumous honor via bronze eikōn in the civic agora suggests local heroization or ancestral commemoration, a common Arcadian practice for royal kin, but no cult, sacrifice, or hero powers are described.

Limit: No evidence of active cult, oracle, or intervention; statue is commemorative, not cult statue (agalma). The honor is civic memory, not deification.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No literary description of Diomeneia's appearance survives. Attested only as a bronze portrait-statue (eikōn chalkē) in the Mantineian agora, seen by Pausanias c. 170 CE. The term eikōn suggests a honorific portrait, likely idealized Hellenistic/Roman-style female figure, but Pausanias gives no details of dress, pose, attributes, or inscription. Attested: existence of bronze image, female. Unknown: height, style, date of statue, iconography, whet

Symbols — No symbols or attributes attested. As daughter of Arcas, she would belong iconographically to Arcadian royal genealogy, but no bear (Callisto), Zeus, or Arcadian mountain symbols are linked to her in text. The bronze medium itself is the only symbolic element: bronze portraiture in an agora signals civic honor / heroization of a local ancestor. Accounts I
The Bronze Eikon at Mantineia (The Only Direct Attestation)Pausanias, Description of Greece 8.9.5

Reported c. 170 CE; statue itself undated (likely Hellenistic or Roman Imperial honorific, but Pausanias gives no date)

In his tour of the Mantineian agora, Pausanias notes among other monuments a bronze portrait-statue 'said to be Diomeneia, daughter of Arcas.' He offers no story about her deeds, only the identification as provided by local guides (exegetai). The qualifier 'said to be' (legomenē) signals that the identification rests on local oral tradition, not an inscribed base he could read. This single sentence is the entire surviving narrative of Diomeneia. Preserves Diomeneia's name and kinship for posterity; demonstrates Mantineian practice of displaying Arcadian royal ancestors in civic space; becomes sole evidence for her existence in mythography.

The Birth and Kingship of Arcas, Father of DiomeneiaPausanias 8.4.1-8.4.3; Apollodorus Bibliotheca 3.8.2-3.9.1; Hesiodic Catalogue of Women fr. 129-131 M-W

Archaic to 2nd century CE (mythic past, narrated in Classical-Hellenistic sources)

Arcas, son of Zeus and the nymph Callisto (daughter of Lycaon), is exposed as infant, saved by Hermes or by shepherds, and raised to become king of Pelasgia, which he renames Arcadia after himself. He teaches his people agriculture (bread-making), weaving, and spinning, brought from Triptolemus. He marries (variously) Laodameia daughter of Amyclas, or Meganeira daughter of Crocon, or Leanira, or the nymph Erato, and fathers sons who divide Arcadia. Diomeneia is not named in these Panhellenic versions, but they provide the genealogical frame into which Pausanias's Mantineian notice inserts her as a daughter. Establishes Arcadian ethnogenesis; explains Arcadia's name and cultural identity; provides royal house to which Diomeneia belongs. Her absence from Apollodorus and Hesiod shows her tradition is local, not Panhellenic.

The Division of Arcadia Among the Sons of ArcasPausanias 8.4.3-8.4.7; Apollodorus 3.9.1

Mythic early kingship, narrated 2nd c. CE and earlier

Arcas divides his kingdom among his three sons: Azan receives Azania, Apheidas receives Tegea, and Elatus receives Mount Cyllene and later migrates to Phocis. A fourth son Triphylus or an illegitimate son Autolaus is sometimes added. Daughters are rarely included in the division narrative, reflecting patriarchal inheritance logic. Diomeneia's position as daughter implies she would have been excluded from territorial inheritance and instead figured in marriage alliance, but no marriage is recorded. Creates the three major Arcadian tribal divisions; explains toponyms; marginalizes female offspring in political geography, which may explain why Diomeneia survives only as a statue rather than a polis eponym.

Accounts II
Callisto, the Bear-Mother: Ancestral Trauma of the Arcadian HouseHesiodic Catalogue fr. 129; Apollodorus 3.8.2; Pausanias 8.3.6-8.4.1; Ovid Metamorphoses 2.401-530

Archaic Greek to Augustan Roman (mythic past)

Callisto, daughter of Lycaon and mother of Arcas, is a companion of Artemis sworn to virginity. She is seduced or raped by Zeus, becomes pregnant, is expelled, transformed into a bear (by Hera or Artemis), and nearly killed by her own son Arcas while hunting, before Zeus catasterizes both as Ursa Major and Arctophylax. This is the deep ancestry of Diomeneia: her paternal grandmother's story is one of sexual violence, metamorphosis, and near-filicide, central to Arcadian identity. No source connects Diomeneia directly to Callisto's fate, but genealogically she is Callisto's granddaughter. Explains Arcadian bear cult, the name Arcas ('bear-ward'), and the constellation myths; provides dark familial background to Diomeneia's lineage. The story's violence is often sanitized in later retellings.

Mantineia's Agora: City of Ancestors and Bronze MemoryPausanias 8.9.5-8.9.12; Fougères, Mantinée et l'Arcadie orientale (1898); Jost, Sanctuaires et cultes d'Arcadie (1985)

Classical to Roman Imperial (agora monuments accumulated over centuries; Pausanias's visit c. 170 CE)

Pausanias describes Mantineia's market-place as crowded with ancestral images: besides Diomeneia, he lists statues of local heroes, a temple of Hera, and monuments to the Battle of Mantineia (362 BCE). The display of a bronze Diomeneia among civic heroes suggests Mantineians claimed Arcadian royal ancestry for prestige, especially after the city's refoundation by Hadrian (as Antinoeia) and its rivalry with Tegea. The statue's presence is less a story about Diomeneia than a story about how Mantineians used genealogy to construct civic identity. Shows how obscure royal daughters could be monumentalized for civic legitimation; explains why a figure with no deeds received bronze honor; contextualizes Pausanias's method of recording local exegetic traditions.

The Lost Arcadian Genealogies: How Pausanias Preserved Women Otherwise ErasedPausanias 8.1-8.9 passim; Asius of Samos fr. 9 Bernabé; Hesiodic Catalogue of Women (general); Fowler, Early Greek Mythography (2013)

6th century BCE (Asius, Hesiod) to 2nd century CE (Pausanias's compilation)

Pausanias's Arcadica preserves many female names otherwise lost because Panhellenic handbooks (Apollodorus) omitted daughters who did not found cities or marry famous heroes. He cites earlier genealogists like Asius and local Mantineian guides. Diomeneia likely comes from such a lost local genealogy — perhaps a Mantineian prose chronicle or oral king-list — that listed daughters for completeness. Her survival is thus an accident of Pausanias's antiquarian thoroughness, not of her narrative importance. Modern scholars (Fowler, Jost) treat such singletons as evidence for the richness of lost local mythography. Highlights gendered transmission bias: sons become eponyms, daughters become statues with no stories; reminds researchers that absence of story is itself historical data about whose stories were valued.

Diomeneia was a goddess, nymph, or had magical powers — No source attributes divinity or powers. She is explicitly a mortal princess (daughter of King Arcas). The bronze eikōn is an honorific portrait, not a cult statue (agalma) of a goddess. Confusion likely arises from the -eia ending shared with nymph names.

Diomeneia is the same as Diomeia (the Attic deme) or Diomede — Diomeneia (Διομένεια) is distinct from Diomeia/Diomeis (Διόμεια, deme of Attica, modern Diomeia) and from Diomede (Διομήδη, multiple heroines). The names share Dio- but have different suffixes and mythic contexts. Diomeneia belongs solely to Arcadia/Mantineia.

We know Diomeneia's story, appearance, or adventures — We know only her name, her father (Arcas), and that a bronze statue in Mantineia was said to be her. No myth narrates her birth, marriage, deeds, or death. Any detailed biography, personality, or portrait description found online is modern invention.
