Who It Is

In the crowded agora of Mantineia, among generals and gods, Pausanias stopped before a bronze woman no poet ever sang about — Diomeneia, princess of Arcadia, remembered only because her city refused to forget her.

No myths narrate Diomeneia's deeds, speech, or character. Pausanias gives no anecdote. As daughter of Arcas, she belongs to the Arcadian royal house, implying high status, but no behavior is described. Any personality reconstruction would be fabrication. Comparative Arcadian princesses are often marginal in genealogy. Status: unknown.

Powers
Mortal status - no superhuman powers attested

Diomeneia is presented as a human princess, daughter of Arcas (himself son of Zeus and Callisto but mortal). No divine powers, transformations, or heroic feats are attributed to her in surviving sources. She is not described as a nymph, priestess, or heroine with cult power.

Limit: Mortality implied; no immortality, metamorphosis, or divine intervention attested. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, but no source claims powers.

Honorific commemoration (social power)

The sole 'ability' attested is posthumous civic memory: a bronze portrait-statue in the Mantineian agora, indicating sufficient status for public honor. This suggests elite lineage and local importance, comparable to other Arcadian heroines honored in civic space.

Limit: Statue may have been Hellenistic or Roman-era honorific, not contemporary with mythical lifetime. Attribution uncertain ('said to be'). No cult or political power described.

Accounts I
The Bronze Diomeneia in the Mantineian AgoraPausanias, Description of Greece 8.9.5

2nd century CE (Antonine), describing an undated bronze statue; mythical figure belongs to heroic-legendary era (pre-Trojan War generation)

While describing Mantineia, Pausanias notes the city's agora contained a bronze portrait-statue of a woman which the locals say is Diomeneia, daughter of Arcas. He gives no story about her life, deeds, death, or why she was honored, and moves on to other monuments. The notice is embedded in a periegesis of Mantineian sights: the theater, the agora, statues of heroes and notables. The brevity implies she was known to his guides but not part of Panhellenic myth. Only consequence is preservation of her name and lineage; no narrative consequence. The statue marks Mantineian claim to connection with the Arcadian eponymous king.

The Birth and Kingship of Arcas, Father of DiomeneiaPausanias 8.4.1-2; Apollodorus Bibliotheca 3.8.2, 3.9.1; Hesiodic fragments

Archaic to Hellenistic mythic tradition; Pausanias 2nd century CE compilation

Arcas, son of Zeus and Callisto (daughter of Lycaon), is exposed as infant, saved, and raised by Maia or by shepherds. He becomes king of Pelasgia, which he renames Arcadia after himself. He teaches his people agriculture (bread-making from acorns), weaving, and spinning, civilizing Arcadia. He marries and fathers sons who divide Arcadia. Diomeneia belongs to this generation as his daughter, though Panhellenic sources omit daughters. Her existence presumes this civilizing reign. Arcas's reign establishes Arcadian identity; his children become eponyms of Arcadian subdivisions (Azania, Apheidantia, Elateia). Diomeneia's status derives from this prestige; her statue in Mantineia asserts continuity with the founder.

Callisto, Grandmother of Diomeneia: The Bear and the StarsHesiod Catalogue of Women fr. 163 Merkelbach-West; Pausanias 8.3.6-7; Apollodorus 3.8.2; Ovid Metamorphoses 2.401-507

Archaic (Hesiod c. 600 BCE) through Roman (Ovid 8 CE) and Antonine (Pausanias)

Callisto, daughter of Lycaon, is a companion of Artemis who is seduced or raped by Zeus, becomes pregnant with Arcas, is transformed into a bear (by Hera, Artemis, or Zeus to hide her), and is placed among the stars as Ursa Major (Great Bear), with Arcas as Arctophylax (Bear-Guard). Pausanias gives the Arcadian rationalized version: Callisto was shot by Artemis and Zeus rescued the infant Arcas. This is the grandmother's story that makes Diomeneia granddaughter of both Zeus and the bear-constellation. Explains Arcadian bear cult, name of Arcadia, and astral catasterism. For Diomeneia, it establishes divine ancestry (Zeus) and Arcadian autochthony. No direct interaction with Diomeneia, but essential genealogy.

Accounts II
The Division of Arcadia Among the Sons of ArcasPausanias 8.4.2-3; Stephanus of Byzantium s.v. Azania

Classical-Hellenistic tradition compiled 2nd century CE

Arcas's sons divide Arcadia: Azan receives Azania, Apheidas receives Tegea, Elatus receives Mount Cyllene and later migrates to Phocis. Each becomes eponym and king. Daughters are not given territories in this patriarchal division, which may explain why Diomeneia is commemorated only by a statue in Mantineia (which fell in Apheidas's share - Tegea/Mantineia region) rather than as eponym. Her presence suggests Mantineian attempt to insert a female link to Arcas. Creates political geography of Arcadia; Mantineia's claim to Diomeneia may be competitive with Tegea's claim to Apheidas. No land or eponymy for Diomeneia attested.

Pausanias the Periegete Visits MantineiaPausanias 8.8.1-8.9.10 (Mantineia section)

c. 155-175 CE

Pausanias travels through Arcadia, describing Mantineia's walls, gates, temples of Zeus Soter and Dioscuri, tombs of Penelope and others, and the agora. His method is autopsy (seeing) plus inquiry from local exegetai (guides). The Diomeneia statue is one item in a catalog of sights, listed between other statues and buildings. The passage shows how heroic genealogy was embedded in civic space under Roman rule, when cities emphasized mythical antiquity. Preserves otherwise lost local memory; demonstrates Roman-era Arcadian identity construction. For modern scholarship, it is the sole witness to Diomeneia.

The Arcadian Royal Daughters: Comparative Context for a Lost PrincessPausanias 8.17.1 (Elateia), 8.35.1 (various daughters); Apollodorus 3.9.1; Parthenius Erotica Pathemata

Hellenistic to Antonine

While Diomeneia has no story, other daughters of Arcadian kings have fragmentary tales: e.g., daughters of Lycaon (Callisto's sisters) suffer Zeus's wrath; daughters of Arcas's sons (e.g., Stymphalus's daughters) appear in genealogies. This comparative story is not about Diomeneia but shows the typical narrative slot she would occupy: marriage alliance, eponymy, or tragic death. Her silence suggests either loss of oral tradition or that she was a purely genealogical figure invented to link Mantineia to Arcas. Highlights gap: Diomeneia lacks the marriage, abduction, or foundation story typical for her status, marking her as an exceptionally thin figure even by Arcadian standards.

Diomeneia was a goddess or nymph with powers and cult — She is explicitly a mortal princess (daughter of Arcas). No cult, powers, or divine status attested. No sanctuary or ritual. The bronze statue was honorific, not cult image.

Diomeneia appears in major myth collections like Apollodorus or Ovid — She appears only in Pausanias 8.9.5. She is absent from Apollodorus, Hesiod, Ovid, Hyginus, and other mythographic sources. Her absence from Panhellenic sources is why she is obscure.

Diomeneia is the same as Diomede (wife of Amphiaraus or daughter of Xuthus) — Distinct names: Διομένεια vs Διομήδη. Diomede is a common heroic name (Diomede wife of Pallas, Diomede daughter of Lapithes, etc.). Diomeneia is hapax, only Mantineian. Different etymology and lineage.

The bronze statue of Diomeneia still exists or its inscription is known — The statue is lost; no base, inscription, or fragment has been identified in Mantineian excavations. Only Pausanias's description survives. Fougères 1898 found no trace.

Diomeneia had a famous myth or love story — No narrative myth survives. No abduction, marriage, or tragedy is recorded. Any story attributed to her (e.g., on fan wikis) is modern invention.
