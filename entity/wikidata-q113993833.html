Form

No literary description of Artimpasa's appearance survives. Based on archaeological interpretations of Scythian gold plaques, she is often reconstructed as a seated goddess, wearing elaborate Scythian dress (a long robe, a headdress, and jewelry), holding a mirror (a symbol of divinity and the afterlife in Scythian culture) or a drinking vessel (rhyton). She is sometimes flanked by animals, particularly deer, birds (owls or griffins), or a serpent-footed goddess figure. This reconstruction is highly speculative and is based on the assumption that a major goddess would be depicted in the most prominent positions on royal regalia.

Powers

Oversight of Love and Fertility (as Aphrodite Urania) Herodotus's equation with Aphrodite Urania implies a role in love, desire, and procreation, specifically in a 'heavenly' or spiritualized form, as opposed to the more earthly Aphrodite Pandemos. Herodotus, Histories 4.59 No Scythian source describes her powers; all are inferred from the Greek interpretatio. Some scholars argue the Greek equation is misleading and that Artimpasa's primary role was in royal sovereignty and the afterlife, not romantic love.

Granting of Royal Favor (hypothesized) Scholars like Ivantchik have argued that Artimpasa, as a 'Great Goddess,' was central to Scythian royal ideology, legitimizing the king's power and ensuring the prosperity of the tribe. This is based on the prominence of goddess imagery in royal kurgans. Modern scholarly interpretation (e.g., Askold Ivantchik, 'A New Look at the Scythian Religion') This is an inference from archaeological context, not a documented belief. The specific role of the goddess in royal cults is debated; some see her as a patron of the dead, others as a protector of the home.

Connection to the Afterlife (hypothesized) The depiction of goddesses with mirrors in Scythian graves has led to the theory that Artimpasa or a similar goddess was a psychopomp or a guardian of the dead, guiding souls to the afterlife. Modern archaeological interpretation Unconfirmed; the mirror may have been a symbol of status or wealth, not a religious symbol. The identity of the goddess in funerary art is not certain; it could be Tabiti (Hestia) or another deity.

Behavior & Habitat

As a 'Urania' (heavenly) goddess, Artimpasa's domain was the celestial realm, in contrast to chthonic (earthly) deities. Herodotus's equation with Aphrodite suggests a role in love, fertility, and possibly sacred kingship. Some scholars have proposed a connection to ecstatic or shamanic practices, given the Scythian tradition of divination and the use of hemp in ritual (as described by Herodotus in Book 4, §74). However, this is speculative.

Accounts

Herodotus provides the only direct literary evidence for Artimpasa. He lists her among the seven principal Scythian gods, equating her with the Greek goddess Aphrodite Urania. He provides no myths or stories about her, only her name and her Greek counterpart. This lack of narrative is itself a crucial piece of evidence: the Scythians did not have a written tradition, and their myths were likely oral and have been lost.

Herodotus recounts the Scythian myth of their own origins, which involves Targitaus, the first man, and his three sons: Lipoxais, Arpoxais, and Colaxais. The youngest, Colaxais, won the kingdom by retrieving golden objects (a plough, a yoke, a battle-axe, and a cup) that fell from the sky and burst into flames. The golden objects are often interpreted as symbols of royal power and are seen by some as being associated with the goddess Artimpasa, who may have been the one to bestow them. This connection is speculative but intriguing.

Scholarly Debates
Her identity as a 'love goddess'
Herodotus's equation with Aphrodite Urania is accurate, and Artimpasa was a goddess of love and fertility. ⇌ The Greek equation is a superficial interpretatio. Artimpasa was primarily a goddess of sovereignty, the afterlife, or a 'Great Goddess' figure, and her connection to love was secondary or non-existent.
Evidence for A: Herodotus's explicit statement.
Evidence for B: The prominence of goddess imagery in royal and funerary contexts, which suggests a more political and eschatological rol
Her relationship to the Snake-Legged Goddess
Artimpasa and the Snake-Legged Goddess are the same deity, depicted in different forms. ⇌ They are separate deities, possibly from different cultural strata (e.g., an older autochthonous goddess vs. a newer Iranian-influenced one).
Evidence for A: The visual similarity of some depictions and the shared connection to royal power.
Evidence for B: The lack of any textual connection and the distinct iconography of the serpent-legged figure.
The nature of her 'Urania' (heavenly) aspect
'Urania' refers to a celestial, pure, and spiritual form of love, contrasting with the earthly Aphrodite (Pandemos). ⇌ 'Urania' is a Greek title that may not reflect a Scythian concept. It may simply mean 'royal' or 'high' goddess, without a specific celestial connotation.
Evidence for A: The standard Greek understanding of the epithet.
Evidence for B: The Scythian context, where royal power was often linked to the sky and celestial fire (as in the golden objects falling
What We Don't Know

The most significant gap is the complete absence of Scythian texts. We have no Scythian mythology, no Scythian theology, and no Scythian names for their gods beyond those recorded by Herodotus. All of our knowledge is filtered through a Greek lens. Further gaps include: 1) The exact etymology of the name 'Artimpasa' is unknown. 2) The relationship between Artimpasa and the other Scythian deities is unclear. 3) The iconography on Scythian artifacts is open to interpretation, and the identification of the goddess figure is not certain. 4) The potential connection to Iranian deities like Anahita is speculative. 5) The role of Artimpasa in Scythian rituals and daily life is completely unknown. 6) The reception of Artimpasa in modern popular culture is minimal, with no major films, games, or books featuring her directly.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Artimpasa as a 'deity in a locked room.' We have a single key (Herodotus's mention) that tells us she exists, but we cannot open the door to see her fully. The room is filled with beautiful, mysterious objects (the gold plaques), but we don't know which ones are hers. She is a challenge to historians and a blank canvas for the imagination—a reminder that history is full of lost worlds and that the past is a foreign country where we are all, ultimately, tourists.
