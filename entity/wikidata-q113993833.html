Who It Is

Imagine a goddess whose name survives in a single sentence, written by a curious Greek traveler who called her people 'barbarians.' She is a queen of the eternal steppe, a figure of gold and fire, who watched over a civilization that left no books, no temples, and no written prayers—only vast burial mounds filled with treasure and the bones of kings. Her name is Artimpasa, and she is a ghost in the archive of history, a deity we know existed but can barely begin to understand.

As a 'Urania' (heavenly) goddess, Artimpasa's domain was the celestial realm, in contrast to chthonic (earthly) deities. Herodotus's equation with Aphrodite suggests a role in love, fertility, and possibly sacred kingship. Some scholars have proposed a connection to ecstatic or shamanic practices, given the Scythian tradition of divination and the use of hemp in ritual (as described by Herodotus in Book 4, §74). However, this is speculative.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name's etymology is uncertain. Some scholars have proposed Iranian etymologies linking it to the concept of 'divine fortune' or 'glory' (xvarənah), while others suggest a connection to the Scythian word for 'bear' or to the Greek 'Artemis' via linguistic borrowing. No consensus exists.

Pronunciation — ar-tim-PA-sa

Oversight of Love and Fertility (as Aphrodite Urania)

Herodotus's equation with Aphrodite Urania implies a role in love, desire, and procreation, specifically in a 'heavenly' or spiritualized form, as opposed to the more earthly Aphrodite Pandemos.

Limit: No Scythian source describes her powers; all are inferred from the Greek interpretatio.

Granting of Royal Favor (hypothesized)

Scholars like Ivantchik have argued that Artimpasa, as a 'Great Goddess,' was central to Scythian royal ideology, legitimizing the king's power and ensuring the prosperity of the tribe. This is based on the prominence of goddess imagery in royal kurgans.

Limit: This is an inference from archaeological context, not a documented belief.

Connection to the Afterlife (hypothesized)

The depiction of goddesses with mirrors in Scythian graves has led to the theory that Artimpasa or a similar goddess was a psychopomp or a guardian of the dead, guiding souls to the afterlife.

Limit: Unconfirmed; the mirror may have been a symbol of status or wealth, not a religious symbol.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No literary description of Artimpasa's appearance survives. Based on archaeological interpretations of Scythian gold plaques, she is often reconstructed as a seated goddess, wearing elaborate Scythian dress (a long robe, a headdress, and jewelry), holding a mirror (a symbol of divinity and the afterlife in Scythian culture) or a drinking vessel (rhyton). She is sometimes flanked by animals, particularly deer, birds (owls or griffins), or a serpen

Symbols — The mirror, the deer, birds (owl/griffin), and possibly the serpent-footed goddess iconography found on Scythian artifacts. None of these are confirmed by textual evidence.

Domains — Love, fertility, sovereignty/royal power (hypothesized), the heavens, and potentially the afterlife (hypothesized). The exact scope of her power is unknown due to the lack of Scythian texts.

Accounts I
Herodotus's Account of the Scythian PantheonHerodotus, Histories, Book 4

c. 430 BCE

Herodotus provides the only direct literary evidence for Artimpasa. He lists her among the seven principal Scythian gods, equating her with the Greek goddess Aphrodite Urania. He provides no myths or stories about her, only her name and her Greek counterpart. This lack of narrative is itself a crucial piece of evidence: the Scythians did not have a written tradition, and their myths were likely oral and have been lost. Herodotus's brief mention has become the cornerstone of all modern scholarship on Scythian religion, but it also colors our understanding through a Greek lens.

Accounts II
The Scythian Origin Myth (as told by Herodotus)Herodotus, Histories, Book 4, §§5-12

c. 430 BCE

Herodotus recounts the Scythian myth of their own origins, which involves Targitaus, the first man, and his three sons: Lipoxais, Arpoxais, and Colaxais. The youngest, Colaxais, won the kingdom by retrieving golden objects (a plough, a yoke, a battle-axe, and a cup) that fell from the sky and burst into flames. The golden objects are often interpreted as symbols of royal power and are seen by some as being associated with the goddess Artimpasa, who may have been the one to bestow them. This connection is speculative but intriguing. This myth establishes the divine right of the Royal Scythians and their unique claim to the steppe. The golden objects are a central symbol of Scythian kingship.

Artimpasa was a major, widely-worshipped goddess with a rich mythology. — We have exactly one literary mention of her, with no myths, no stories, and no descriptions. She was clearly one of the principal deities, but almost everything about her is unknown. Her 'richness' is a projection of modern scholarship and imagination.

The goddess on Scythian gold plaques is definitely Artimpasa. — This is a common and reasonable hypothesis, but it is not proven. The plaques are unlabeled, and the goddess could be Tabiti, Api, or another deity. The identification is based on the assumption that a 'Great Goddess' would be depicted, but we don't know which one.

Artimpasa is the Scythian version of the Greek Aphrodite. — This is Herodotus's claim, but it is a Greek interpretation. The Scythians may have had a very different understanding of this goddess. Equating them too closely can obscure, rather than reveal, her true nature.
