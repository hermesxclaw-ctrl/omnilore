Who It Is

In the fog-choked streets of Victorian London, a respected physician's name became shorthand for the duality lurking within every soul—yet the true story of Dr. Henry Jekyll and Mr. Edward Hyde is far stranger and more disturbing than the popular catchphrase suggests. Robert Louis Stevenson's 1886 novella did not merely invent a monster; it crystallized a cultural anxiety about science, respectability, and the repressed self that still haunts us.

Jekyll: a respected, sociable, philanthropic doctor, but secretly tormented by his dual nature and a certain hypocrisy. Hyde: pure evil, without conscience, delighting in cruelty and violence. Hyde is impulsive, selfish, and utterly amoral, representing the id unleashed.

Powers
Transformation

Jekyll can transform into Hyde and back by drinking a chemical potion. The transformation is accompanied by pain and a sense of liberation. Over time, the transformations become involuntary and require increasing doses of the potion.

Limit: Requires the potion; becomes uncontrollable; eventually the transformation to Hyde becomes permanent as Jekyll loses the ability to revert.

Enhanced physicality

Hyde is described as being more agile and physically powerful than his small stature would suggest. He commits brutal murders with ease.

Limit: No supernatural powers; his strength is implied to be a result of his evil nature being more 'pure' and focused.

Dual identity

Jekyll and Hyde share the same body but have separate consciousnesses and memories. Hyde's memories are accessible to Jekyll, but Jekyll's memories are not fully accessible to Hyde.

Limit: The separation is not perfect; Jekyll retains some awareness of Hyde's actions.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Dr. Jekyll: a large, well-made man, smooth-faced, fifty years old, with something of a slyish cast perhaps, but every mark of capacity and kindness. Mr. Hyde: pale and dwarfish, deformed, with a displeasing smile, and a voice that is husky and broken. He gives an impression of deformity without any specific malformation, and exudes a sense of 'troglodytic' evil. Hyde is smaller and younger than Jekyll, reflecting the idea that evil is less develo

Symbols — The potion, the transformation, the door (Jekyll's back door), the fog, the night, the cane, the laboratory, the mirror. Hyde is often depicted as simian or ape-like in illustrations, reflecting Victorian racial and evolutionary anxieties.

Domains — Victorian London, specifically the West End and Soho. Jekyll's house and laboratory in a respectable square; Hyde's lodgings in Soho, a disreputable area. The story is set in the 1880s.

Accounts I
The Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr HydeRobert Louis Stevenson

1886

London lawyer Gabriel John Utterson investigates the strange relationship between his old friend Dr. Henry Jekyll and the sinister Edward Hyde. After Hyde is linked to a brutal murder, Utterson suspects foul play. Eventually, Jekyll's friend Dr. Lanyon reveals that he witnessed Hyde transform into Jekyll. Jekyll's full confession explains that he sought to separate his good and evil natures, using a potion to become Hyde. However, Hyde grew stronger and more evil, and Jekyll lost control, eventually committing suicide to destroy Hyde. Jekyll dies, Hyde is destroyed. Utterson and Enfield are left with the horror of the revelation. The novella became an instant classic and a cultural touchstone for the duality of human nature.

Jekyll and Hyde are two completely separate people. — They are the same person, with Jekyll transforming into Hyde. They share the same body and ultimately the same fate.

Jekyll is good and Hyde is evil. — Jekyll is not purely good; he has hidden vices and a sense of hypocrisy. Hyde is the embodiment of his evil impulses, but Jekyll is complicit.

The story is about split personality disorder (DID). — The story is a Gothic allegory about the duality of human nature, not a clinical depiction of dissociative identity disorder. The transformation is supernatural/chemical, not psychological in the modern sense.

Hyde is a werewolf-like creature. — In the novella, Hyde is human, albeit deformed and evil. The werewolf connection is a later adaptation invention.

Jekyll drinks a potion that turns him into a monster. — The potion is a chemical mixture, but the transformation is as much psychological as physical. In the book, Hyde is not a monster but a twisted version of Jekyll.
