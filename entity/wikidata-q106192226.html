The Approach

In the fog-choked streets of Victorian London, a respected physician's name became shorthand for the duality lurking within every soul—yet the true story of Dr. Henry Jekyll and Mr. Edward Hyde is far stranger and more disturbing than the popular catchphrase suggests. Robert Louis Stevenson's 1886 novella did not merely invent a monster; it crystallized a cultural anxiety about science, respectability, and the repressed self that still haunts us.

Content advisory. The narrative contains themes of violence, murder, suicide, substance abuse, and psychological horror. It reflects Victorian-era attitudes toward class, degeneracy, and criminality that may be offensive to modern readers.
Identity

Dr. Henry Jekyll / Mr. Edward Hyde

Pronunciation JEE-kill (rhymes with 'treacle'); Hyde rhymes with 'ride'.

Known As

Henry JekyllHarry JekyllEdward HydeMr. HydeJekyll and Hyde

Etymology

Jekyll: Possibly derived from the Danish name 'Jekyll' or a variant of 'Jackal'; Stevenson may have chosen it for its sound. Hyde: Suggests 'hide' (concealment) and 'hyde' (skin), evoking the idea of hiding one's true nature. The name 'Edward' may have been chosen for its commonness, contrasting with the exotic 'Henry'.

Cultural Ownership

Primarily British (Scottish author), now global public domain. The characters are iconic in English literature and have been adapted across many cultures.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars have speculated that Jekyll and Hyde represent Stevenson's own struggles with his dual nature (respectable author vs. bohemian), or that they allegorize contemporary figures like William Brodie (a respectable Edinburgh citizen by day, burglar by night). No definitive real-life model is established.

Description

Appearance

Dr. Jekyll: a large, well-made man, smooth-faced, fifty years old, with something of a slyish cast perhaps, but every mark of capacity and kindness. Mr. Hyde: pale and dwarfish, deformed, with a displeasing smile, and a voice that is husky and broken. He gives an impression of deformity without any specific malformation, and exudes a sense of 'troglodytic' evil. Hyde is smaller and younger than Jekyll, reflecting the idea that evil is less developed.

Symbols & Iconography

The potion, the transformation, the door (Jekyll's back door), the fog, the night, the cane, the laboratory, the mirror. Hyde is often depicted as simian or ape-like in illustrations, reflecting Victorian racial and evolutionary anxieties.

Behaviour & Personality

Jekyll: a respected, sociable, philanthropic doctor, but secretly tormented by his dual nature and a certain hypocrisy. Hyde: pure evil, without conscience, delighting in cruelty and violence. Hyde is impulsive, selfish, and utterly amoral, representing the id unleashed.

Domains & Habitats

Victorian London, specifically the West End and Soho. Jekyll's house and laboratory in a respectable square; Hyde's lodgings in Soho, a disreputable area. The story is set in the 1880s.

Version Differences

In the original novella, Jekyll is a middle-aged man, and Hyde is younger and smaller. In many adaptations, Hyde is often portrayed as larger and more beastly. The transformation is often depicted as a physical metamorphosis, whereas in the book it is more subtle. The ending differs: in the book, Jekyll commits suicide by poison; some adaptations have Hyde killed or defeated.

Powers & Abilities

Transformation

Source Version Original novella

Description

Jekyll can transform into Hyde and back by drinking a chemical potion. The transformation is accompanied by pain and a sense of liberation. Over time, the transformations become involuntary and require increasing doses of the potion.

Limitations

Requires the potion; becomes uncontrollable; eventually the transformation to Hyde becomes permanent as Jekyll loses the ability to revert.

Contradictions

In some adaptations, the transformation is triggered by an injection, or is a psychological change without physical alteration.

Enhanced physicality

Source Version Original novella

Description

Hyde is described as being more agile and physically powerful than his small stature would suggest. He commits brutal murders with ease.

Limitations

No supernatural powers; his strength is implied to be a result of his evil nature being more 'pure' and focused.

Contradictions

Some adaptations give Hyde superhuman strength and invulnerability.

Dual identity

Source Version Original novella

Description

Jekyll and Hyde share the same body but have separate consciousnesses and memories. Hyde's memories are accessible to Jekyll, but Jekyll's memories are not fully accessible to Hyde.

Limitations

The separation is not perfect; Jekyll retains some awareness of Hyde's actions.

Contradictions

Some adaptations treat them as completely separate personalities with no shared memory.

Stories & Myths

The Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde

Source Robert Louis StevensonCulture Victorian BritainPeriod 1886

Summary

London lawyer Gabriel John Utterson investigates the strange relationship between his old friend Dr. Henry Jekyll and the sinister Edward Hyde. After Hyde is linked to a brutal murder, Utterson suspects foul play. Eventually, Jekyll's friend Dr. Lanyon reveals that he witnessed Hyde transform into Jekyll. Jekyll's full confession explains that he sought to separate his good and evil natures, using a potion to become Hyde. However, Hyde grew stronger and more evil, and Jekyll lost control, eventually committing suicide to destroy Hyde.

Consequences

Jekyll dies, Hyde is destroyed. Utterson and Enfield are left with the horror of the revelation. The novella became an instant classic and a cultural touchstone for the duality of human nature.

Variants

Stevenson's original manuscript had a different ending, where Jekyll does not commit suicide but is arrested. The published version was shortened and revised at the insistence of his wife, Fanny, who thought the original was too dark.

Dark History

Violence and murder

Source Original novella

Description

Hyde tramples a young girl and later bludgeons Sir Danvers Carew to death with a cane. The murders are described with chilling detachment.

Scholarly Context

Scholars interpret the violence as a critique of Victorian society's repression and the dark underbelly of respectability.

Substance abuse and addiction

Source Original novella

Description

Jekyll becomes addicted to the potion, which is implied to be a metaphor for drug addiction. He describes the increasing need and loss of control.

Scholarly Context

Victorian-era concerns about drug use, particularly opium and cocaine, are reflected in the story. It has been analyzed as an allegory for addiction.

Suicide

Source Original novella

Description

Jekyll commits suicide by taking poison to destroy Hyde, sacrificing himself to prevent further evil.

Scholarly Context

The suicide is seen as a tragic but necessary act, raising questions about redemption and self-destruction.

Victorian racial and class anxieties

Source Original novella and scholarly analyses

Description

Hyde is described as 'troglodytic' and 'deformed', and is associated with the lower classes and evolutionary regression. This reflects Victorian fears of the 'other' and social degeneration.

Scholarly Context

Critics have noted the use of racial and class stereotypes in describing Hyde, linking him to contemporary theories of criminal anthropology and eugenics.

Contradictions & Disputes

Physical appearance of Hyde

Position A

Hyde is smaller and younger than Jekyll, reflecting the idea that evil is less developed.

Position A — evidence

Original novella description: 'pale and dwarfish, he gave an impression of deformity'.

Position B

Hyde is often portrayed as larger and more monstrous in adaptations.

Position B — evidence

Film adaptations such as the 1931 version show Hyde as a hulking, ape-like figure.

Scholarly Consensus

Scholars note that the original description is more subtle and symbolic, while adaptations tend to emphasize visual horror.

Nature of transformation

Position A

The transformation is purely chemical and physical.

Position A — evidence

Jekyll's scientific explanation in the confession.

Position B

The transformation is psychological or supernatural.

Position B — evidence

Some adaptations suggest a spiritual or demonic element, or interpret it as a metaphor for addiction.

Scholarly Consensus

Most literary critics view the transformation as a metaphor for the duality of human nature, not literally supernatural.

Jekyll's motivation

Position A

Jekyll seeks to separate good and evil to improve humanity.

Position A — evidence

Jekyll's stated goal in his confession.

Position B

Jekyll is driven by selfish desires for pleasure and freedom from conscience.

Position B — evidence

His admission that he enjoyed the freedom of Hyde and that he was 'more than forgiven' for his sins.

Scholarly Consensus

Scholars often see Jekyll's motivation as a mixture of noble ambition and personal indulgence, reflecting Victorian hypocrisy.

This dossier covers the original novella, its immediate historical context, Stevenson's life and influences, critical reception, and major adaptations across media up to 2025. It does not attempt to catalog every derivative work or fan interpretation, but focuses on documented and influential appearances.
