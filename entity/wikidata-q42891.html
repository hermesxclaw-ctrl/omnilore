Who It Is

He is a butter-thieving toddler, a flute-playing cowherd who enchants the universe, a philosopher delivering the most profound discourse ever spoken on a battlefield, and the Supreme Being whose cosmic form contains all worlds, all gods, all time. Krishna is not one god but a kaleidoscope: mischievous child, irresistible lover, cunning statesman, and terrifying Lord of the Universe — sometimes all in the same story. The Bhagavad Gita alone has shaped Indian thought for two thousand years, influencing everyone from Gandhi to Oppenheimer ('Now I am become Death…'). What does it mean for God to be not just powerful, but playful?

Krishna is the most multifaceted deity in Hinduism, a figure whose personality spans the entire spectrum of human and divine experience. As a child in Gokul, he is a mischievous butter thief whose pranks delight and exasperate his foster mother Yashoda — yet when she looks into his mouth, she sees the entire universe. As a youth in Vrindavan, he is the divine lover whose flute and beauty enchant every heart, performing the celestial Rasa Lila where he multiplies himself so each gopi dances with him alone. As a prince and statesman, he is a master strategist who guides the Pandavas to victor

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Sanskrit kṛṣṇa (कृष्ण), meaning 'black,' 'dark,' or 'dark blue' — referring to his skin color. Also associated with the Krishna Paksha, the waning (darkening) phase of the moon. As the 57th name in the Vishnu Sahasranama, Krishna is a name of Vishnu.

Pronunciation — /ˈkrɪʃnə/ (English), [ˈkr̩ʂɳɐ] (Sanskrit)

Aliases — Kṛṣṇa, Govinda, Gopala, Madhava, Vāsudeva, Keshava

Ownership — Hinduism (worshipped as eighth avatar of Vishnu and as Svayam Bhagavan — the Supreme God Himself — in Krishnaism)

Accounts I
The Birth of KrishnaBhagavata Purana, Book 10

Dvapara Yuga (mythological)

Krishna is born in a prison cell in Mathura to Devaki and Vasudeva, imprisoned by the tyrant king Kamsa. A prophecy had declared that Devaki's eighth child would kill Kamsa. At Krishna's birth, the prison doors miraculously open, the guards fall asleep, and Vasudeva carries the infant across the flooding Yamuna River — which parts for them — to the village of Gokul. There he exchanges Krishna for the newborn daughter of Nanda and Yashoda. When Kamsa attempts to kill the girl, she transforms into the goddess Yogamaya and warns him his destroyer still lives. Krishna is safely raised in Gokul, setting the stage for his childhood lila and eventual return to Mathura to fulfill the prophecy.

The Bhagavad GitaMahabharata, Bhishma Parva

c. 400 BCE - 400 CE

On the battlefield of Kurukshetra, the warrior Arjuna collapses in despair, unwilling to fight against his own kin. Krishna, serving as his charioteer, delivers a 700-verse discourse covering karma yoga (the yoga of action), bhakti yoga (devotion), jnana yoga (knowledge), the nature of the self (atman), the cosmic order (dharma), and the ultimate reality (Brahman). At Arjuna's request, Krishna reveals his Vishvarupa — the terrifying, all-consuming universal form containing all gods, all beings, past, present, and future. Arjuna, overwhelmed, begs Krishna to return to his gentle human form. The Bhagavad Gita became one of the most influential philosophical and spiritual texts in world history, central to Hindu theology, and a cornerstone of Indian thought.

The Rasa LilaBhagavata Purana, Book 10, Chapters 29-33

Medieval (text)

On an autumn full-moon night, Krishna plays his flute in the forests of Vrindavan. The gopis (cowherd women) abandon their homes, husbands, and duties to join him. Krishna multiplies himself so that each gopi believes she dances with him alone. The circular dance (rasa) symbolizes the soul's ecstatic union with God. When the gopis grow proud, Krishna vanishes, teaching them that divine love requires humility. He eventually returns, and the dance continues. This story is the devotional heart of Krishna bhakti. Established the paradigm of madhurya bhakti (sweet devotion) — love for God expressed through the metaphor of romantic love. Radha-Krishna became the central image of divine love in Indian art, poetry, and spirituality.

Accounts II
Krishna Lifts Govardhan HillBhagavata Purana, Book 10, Chapter 25

Medieval (text)

Young Krishna convinces the people of Vrindavan to worship Govardhan Hill instead of performing the annual sacrifice to Indra, king of the gods. Enraged, Indra sends a catastrophic storm to destroy Vrindavan. Krishna calmly lifts the entire Govardhan Hill on the little finger of his left hand and holds it for seven days as an umbrella, sheltering all the people and cattle beneath. Humbled, Indra withdraws the storm and acknowledges Krishna's supremacy. Demonstrates Krishna's supremacy even over the king of the Vedic gods. Govardhan Puja is celebrated annually. Indra's humiliation signals the shift from Vedic ritualism to bhakti devotion.

The Killing of KamsaBhagavata Purana, Book 10

Medieval (text)

After years of terrorizing Mathura and attempting to kill Krishna, Kamsa invites Krishna and Balarama to a wrestling tournament, planning to have them killed. Krishna defeats and kills Kamsa's champion wrestlers Chanura and Mushtika. Then, in a dramatic confrontation, Krishna seizes Kamsa by his hair, drags him into the wrestling arena, and kills him, fulfilling the prophecy. He liberates his parents Vasudeva and Devaki from prison and restores Ugrasena, Kamsa's father, to the throne of Mathura. The tyrant is destroyed. Krishna's earthly mission of restoring dharma takes a decisive turn. He establishes himself as a political force.

The Mahabharata War — Krishna as StrategistMahabharata

c. 400 BCE - 400 CE

Krishna serves as the strategic mastermind of the Pandava cause in the Kurukshetra War. Though he has sworn not to fight directly, he orchestrates key victories: arranging the sun's temporary eclipse to trick Jayadratha, having Bhima strike Duryodhana below the belt (violating combat rules but fulfilling destiny), and convincing the righteous Yudhishthira to lie — 'Ashwatthama is dead' — to break Drona's will. Each act is morally ambiguous but serves the larger purpose of dharma's victory over adharma. The Pandavas win the war. Krishna's morally complex strategies are studied as examples of dharma-yuddha (righteous war) where higher principles sometimes override conventional ethics.
