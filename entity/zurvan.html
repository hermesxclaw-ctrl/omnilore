Who It Is

Zurvan (Zrvan, meaning 'Time' or 'Infinite Time') is a primordial deity of time and destiny in Persian religion, most prominently worshipped in Zurvanism—a now-extinct branch or 'heresy' of Zoroastrianism that elevated Zurvan above Ahura Mazda and Angra Mainyu as the ultimate, transcendent source of all existence. According to Zurvanite theology, as reconstructed from hostile Zoroastrian, Armenian Christian, and Syriac sources, Zurvan was the supreme, androgynous, and impassive god of infinite time who existed alone in the primordial void. Desiring a son, Zurvan performed sacrifices for a thousand years. Toward the end of this period, a moment of doubt crossed his mind. From this doubt was born Angra Mainyu (Ahriman)—dark, malodorous, and evil. But from the sacrifices themselves, the true intended son, Ahura Mazda (Ohrmazd)—radiant, sweet-smelling, and good—was also born. Unable to choose between his two sons, Zurvan decreed that whichever of them first came before him would rule the world for a period of 9,000 years. Ahura Mazda, aware of this, informed Angra Mainyu, who immediately tore through the womb and presented himself. But because he was born of doubt and was dark and foul, the primordial contract was modified: Angra Mainyu would rule for 9,000 years, but Ahura Mazda would ultimately triumph and rule eternally thereafter. This myth solved a theological problem: if Ahura Mazda is supremely good and omnipotent, why does evil exist? Zurvanism answered that good and evil are twin brothers born from the same father, and that time itself is the ultimate arbiter that limits evil's reign. Zurvanism was particularly influential during the Sassanian period and may have been the state orthodoxy at certain points. It was ultimately suppressed by the orthodox Zoroastrian priesthood after the Islamic conquest. Zurvan's legacy, however, resonates in the concept of boundless time (Zurvan Akarana) in orthodox Zoroastrianism and in the fatalistic elements of Persian culture.
