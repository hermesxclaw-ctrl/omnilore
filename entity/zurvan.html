Who It Is

No anthropomorphic iconography of Zurvan survives. He is described in theological texts as an androgynous, transcendental being — 'both male and female' — who exists without passion, beyond the distinction between good and evil. As the hypostasis of Infinite Time, Zurvan has no fixed form; the 9th-century Menog-i Khrad and other Middle Persian sources treat him as an abstract principle made personal for cultic purposes. In non-Zoroastrian Hellenistic accounts (Eudemus of Rhodes via Damascius), he is identified with Space/Time as a cosmic father-figure. Later Mithraic scholarship briefly conflated Zurvan with the leontocephaline (lion-headed) deity of the Roman Mithraic Mysteries, but Robert Charles Zaehner retracted this identification as a 'positive mistake.'

Powers

Primordial Creation: As the first principle (or 'father of the twins'), Zurvan can engender supreme deities ex nihilo through ritual sacrifice spanning a millennium. In the classic Zurvanite cosmogony, his thousand-year sacrifice produces both Ahura Mazda (from the piety of sacrifice) and Angra Mainyu (from the moment of doubt). Temporal Sovereignty: Zurvan controls the allotment of time to the cosmic twins: 9,000 years to Angra Mainyu and all eternity to Ahura Mazda thereafter. This temporal sovereignty extends to the astral determinism that governs human destiny — the movements of stars and planets (divided between benefic zodiac signs and malefic planets) decide mortal fate. Transcendental Neutrality: Zurvan exists without passion, without good or evil — a state that permits him to parent both the benevolent and malevolent principles without moral compromise. This neutrality is itself a theological power: it makes Zurvan the only being capable of resolving the 'twin spirits' enigma of the Avesta.

Behavior & Habitat

Zurvan is portrayed as a neutral, dispassionate cosmic principle — neither good nor evil, but the indifferent substrate from which both arise. In the Zurvanite creation myth, he sacrifices for a thousand years to produce offspring. When he begins to doubt the efficacy of sacrifice in the final moments, that very doubt begets Angra Mainyu (Ahriman), while the sacrifice itself begets Ohrmuzd (Ahura Mazda). Zurvan then resolves to grant the first-born sovereignty over creation for 9,000 years, after which Ohrmuzd rules for all eternity. This narrative casts Zurvan as a fatalistic clockmaker: he sets the cosmic clock but is not personally invested in the moral struggle between his sons.

Scholarly Debates
What We Don't Know

The Zurvanite dossier has major evidentiary gaps because the cult's own texts were largely destroyed or suppressed. No indigenous Zurvanite scripture survives in full. The creation myth is reconstructed from non-Zoroastrian polemical sources (Armenian, Syriac, Manichaean). The Kartir inscription and Mihr-Narseh edict are fragmentary. The internal sociology of Zurvanism — whether it had separate temples, priesthoods, or lay organizations — is unknown. Materialist, ascetic, and fatalistic 'schools' are scholarly reconstructions, not indigenous categories. The extent of popular Zurvanite practice versus elite theological speculation remains unclear. Archaeological evidence for Zurvanite worship is absent. The relationship between Zurvanite fatalism and later Islamic philosophical determinism requires source-material in Middle Persian that has not survived.
