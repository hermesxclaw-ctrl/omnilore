Who It Is

He is a name that lives for exactly four words in Greek — Γρατίωνα δὲ Ἄρτεμις ἀνεῖλεν, 'and Artemis slew Gration' — and then vanishes. No birth story, no speech, no monument, no second chance. Of the two dozen earth-born Giants who stormed Olympus at Phlegra, hurling burning oaks and boulders at the gods, Gration is the one history almost forgot: listed once in a Hellenistic handbook, absent from every vase painter's label and every other poet's catalogue, remembered only because a virgin huntress drew her bow and a mythographer bothered to write it down.

No personality, speech, or motive is attributed to Gration in surviving sources. The Gigantes as a group are characterized in Hesiod and Apollodorus as hybristic, violent, and opposed to the Olympian order — born from Gaia to avenge the Titans, attacking Olympus by hurling rocks and tree trunks. Apollodorus 1.6.1 states they were invulnerable to gods alone and could only be slain when a god and a mortal fought together. Gration's only narrated action is being an enemy combatant in the Gigantomachy and being killed. No dialogue, cult, or moral exemplum attached. Adaptation-specific elab

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Ancient Greek Γρατίων. No secure etymology proposed in standard lexica (LSJ, Beekes). Not derived from Latin gratus 'pleasing' despite modern homophony. The name appears only in the catalogue of Gigantes in Pseudo-Apollodorus; it is hapax in that form. Francis Vian (1952) and subsequent commentators

Pronunciation — Ancient Greek: Γρα-τί-ων [ɡra.tí.ɔːn] (gra-TEE-ohn), with omega long final. Modern English conventional: /ˈɡreɪ.ʃən/ GRAY-shən or /ˈɡræʃ.ən/ GRASH-ən. Latinized: Gration, Gratiōn.

Aliases — Γρατίων (Gratiōn), Gratios (manuscript variant), Gration the Giant

Powers
Gigantic Strength and Stature

As a Gigas, Gration possesses superhuman size, strength, and martial prowess, capable of hurling boulders and tree trunks at the gods and withstanding divine weapons. This is a collective attribute of all Gigantes, not individually described for Gration.

Limit: Not individually quantified. No feat specific to Gration survives (e.g., no named opponent besides his killer).

Conditional Invulnerability (God + Mortal Requirement)

Apollodorus 1.6.1 records a prophecy: the Giants could not be killed by gods alone; a mortal ally fighting alongside the gods was required. Gaia sought a pharmakon (herb) to make them invulnerable even to mortals, but Zeus prevented her. This applies to all Giants including Gration.

Limit: The condition is collective, not personal. Gration is explicitly killed by Artemis (a goddess) in 1.6.2, which appears to contradict the rule unless Heracles (the mortal) was also present or the tradition is inconsistent. Apollodorus resolves this by stating Heracles shot those struck down by the gods.

Chthonic Vitality / Earth-Born Resilience

Born from Gaia (Earth), Gigantes have a connection to the earth. For Alcyoneus, this is explicit immortality on native soil; for others it is implied resilience. No text extends Alcyoneus's specific immortality to Gration.

Limit: No source states Gration had Alcyoneus's land-bound immortality. Cannot be assumed.

Weapon Use (Boulders, Tree Trunks, Makeshift Arms)

Gigantes fight with natural weapons — rocks, burning oaks, and in art, spears and shields. No weapon is named for Gration, but as a participant in the Gigantomachy he would be assumed to use such.

Limit: No attested personal weapon or artifact (unlike Enceladus buried under Etna).

Accounts I
The Birth of the Gigantes from the Blood of UranusHesiod, Theogony 183-186

ca. 700 BCE

After Cronus castrates his father Uranus (Sky) at Gaia's instigation, the blood (or seed) falling onto Earth (Gaia) impregnates her. From this blood arise three groups: the Erinyes (Furies), the Meliai (ash-tree nymphs), and the Gigantes (Giants), described as 'great, gleaming in armor, holding long spears.' This is the cosmogonic origin of the Gigantes as a race. No individual giant is named here; Gration belongs to this generation by later genealogical inference. Establishes the Gigantes as chthonic, autochthonous, and inherently opposed to the Uranian/Olympian order. Provides the divine ancestry that later mythographers use to explain the Gigantomachy. Sets the pattern of Gaia producing avenging offspring against the ruling gods.

The Catalogue of the Gigantes and the Prophecy of the GigantomachyPseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 1.6.1

1st-2nd century CE, drawing on earlier Hellenistic sources (possibly Asclepiades or Panyassis)

Apollodorus lists the Gigantes: 'Alcyoneus and Porphyrion excelled... there were also Enceladus, Ephialtes, Eurytus, Clytius, Mimas, Pallas, Polybotes, Hippolytus, Gration (Γρατίων), and Agrios and Thoon...' He records that Gaia, knowing the giants could only be slain by god and mortal together, sought a drug to make them invulnerable, but Zeus ordered Eos, Selene, and Helios not to shine and himself gathered the herb before her. Athena summoned Heracles as the mortal ally. Provides the only secure attestation of Gration's name and his membership in the canonical giant army. Establishes the narrative framework for his death in the next section. Codifies the 'god + mortal' rule that defines the Gigantomachy.

The Death of Gration at the Hands of ArtemisPseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 1.6.2

1st-2nd century CE

During the Gigantomachy at Phlegra (Pallene), the Olympians and Heracles engage the Giants. Apollodorus gives a kill-list: '...Ephialtes was shot by Apollo in the left eye and by Heracles in the right; Eurytus by Dionysus with his thyrsus; Clytius by Hecate with torches; Mimas by Hephaestus with molten iron; Enceladus was put to flight...; Gration (Γρατίωνα) was slain by Artemis (Ἄρτεμις ἀνεῖλεν), Pallas by Athena... etc.' Heracles then shoots with arrows those already wounded by the gods, fulfilling the prophecy. The sole narrative event specific to Gration. Establishes Artemis as his divine slayer, fitting her role as virgin huntress and protector of the Olympian order. No further myth (burial, transformation, offspring) is attached. The laconic entry suggests the author had no fuller story to tell.

Accounts II
The Gigantomachy in Archaic and Classical Art — The Visual Battle Without NamesAttic black-figure and red-figure vases; Pergamon Altar Gigantomachy frieze; literary ekphrases (Euripides Ion 987-997, Apollonius Rhodius)

ca. 560 BCE - 159 BCE

From the mid-6th century BCE, the Gigantomachy becomes a central motif in Greek art: Athena vs. Enceladus, Zeus vs. Porphyrion, Artemis with bow vs. an unnamed giant. Vases in the British Museum and Louvre show Artemis shooting a giant, sometimes labeled, often not. The Pergamon Altar (ca. 180-159 BCE) presents the most elaborate surviving Gigantomachy: over 100 figures, Artemis with her hounds and torch fighting an anguipede giant, but no inscription preserves the name Gration. Euripides describes a woven image of the battle on the temple at Delphi. Shows that Gration, if ever depicted, is now anonymous — his visual identity is lost. Demonstrates the cultural importance of the myth that preserved his name in text even when art did not label him. Provides the iconographic template later used for all giants including Gration in modern imagination.

The Variant Catalogues — Hyginus and the Fluid Giant ListsHyginus, Fabulae Praefatio 6; also scholia to Apollonius Rhodius 3.1226; Pausanias 8.29.1

1st century BCE - 2nd century CE

Hyginus gives a different giant genealogy and list: 'Ex Terra et Tartaro Gigantes... Alcyoneus, Enceladus, Porphyrion, etc.' Gration is absent. The scholia and Pausanias mention local Phlegraean traditions where different giants are buried under different volcanoes (Enceladus under Etna, Polybotes under Nisyros). The absence of Gration in these parallel lists indicates that giant catalogues were not fixed canon but regional, poetic inventions. Apollodorus's inclusion of Gration may reflect a lost epic (Titanomachy or Gigantias) or a local Chalcidic tradition. Explains why Gration is obscure: he belongs to a minority tradition that did not enter the mainstream Roman or later Byzantine handbooks. Highlights the problem of treating any one list as definitive. Modern encyclopedias that copy Apollodorus without noting Hyginus's omission create a false sense of completeness.

The Late-Antique Reimagining — Nonnus and Claudian's Rhetorical GigantomachiesNonnus, Dionysiaca 25.85-95, 48.6-40; Claudian, Gigantomachia (fragment)

5th century CE (Nonnus), ca. 395 CE (Claudian)

Nonnus reworks the Gigantomachy as a Dionysiac triumph: Dionysus, with satyrs and bacchants, defeats giants who are described with elaborate, almost baroque detail, but individual Apollodoran names including Gration are not reused; instead Nonnus invents or renames giants for metrical and allegorical effect. Claudian's unfinished Latin Gigantomachia similarly treats the battle as a cosmic allegory for order vs. chaos. These show the myth's shift from genealogical catalogue to literary set-piece, where obscure names like Gration fall away. Marks the end of Gration's active mythological life. After Apollodorus, no ancient author finds a use for the name. The tradition narrows to the most famous giants (Enceladus, Porphyrion, Alcyoneus), leaving Gration as a philological fossil.

Gration was a well-known Greek giant with his own myths, cult, and stories like Enceladus or Atlas. — Gration is attested in a single sentence in one mythographic handbook (Apollodorus 1.6.2) as a name in a kill-list. He has no individual stories, cult, iconography, or dialogue. His obscurity is the point — most giants beyond the 3-4 famous ones are just names.

The surname Gration (e.g., Harry Gration, Scott Gration) is derived from the Greek giant. — No etymological connection. The surname Gration is of English origin (likely from a place name or medieval personal name), entirely unrelated to Ancient Greek Γρατίων. The homophony is coincidental. The giant has no modern descendants or name legacy.

Gration was killed by Heracles (or Zeus) as shown in popular retellings. — Ancient source (Apollodorus 1.6.2) explicitly says Artemis slew Gration. Heracles's role was to finish those struck by gods, but the divine slayer credited is Artemis. Confusion arises because Heracles kills many giants and is the most famous giant-slayer.

All Gigantes, including Gration, were serpent-legged (anguipede) monsters from the beginning. — Anguipede form is Hellenistic (3rd century BCE onward, codified at Pergamon). Archaic and Classical giants were depicted as fully human hoplites. Gration's form is unattested; projecting the Pergamon look backward is anachronistic for earlier periods.

Gration appears in Hesiod's Theogony or Homer's epics. — Hesiod names no individual giants; Homer mentions Giants only collectively (Odyssey 7.59, 10.120). Gration appears nowhere before the Hellenistic period. Attributing him to Hesiod is a common error when sources say 'Hesiod says giants were born from Uranus's blood' and readers assume that includes the Apollodoran names.
