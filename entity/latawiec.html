Who It Is

Imagine looking up at a storm-dark sky and seeing not just lightning, but a blazing serpent of fire twisting above your village—then, that night, the lover you lost years ago slips through your chimney, whispering comfort that feels like salvation and leaves you weaker every dawn. In Slavic folklore, that visitor has a name: latawiec or latawica, a wind-riding demon born from tragedy and desire, part kite, part raven, part restless soul.

Latawiec’s behavior is ambivalent and strongly tied to wind, storms, and human emotional vulnerability. As a wind demon, it rides whirlwinds and storms, appearing during strong gusts or lightning-laden tempests, sometimes blamed for fires started by lightning. It can be capricious, knocking down roofs or trees, but also guiding winds to turn mill sails when appeased. As a restless soul of the unborn or suddenly dead, it is drawn to liminal situations—loneliness, grief, and moral uncertainty. Name & Tongue

Etymology — Polish "latawiec" and "latawica" derive from the verb "latać" (to fly), reflecting the demon’s aerial, wind-bound nature. In modern Polish, "latawiec" also means "kite", likely transferred from the image of a flying, wind-driven spirit to a children’s toy.

Pronunciation — Polish: [laˈta.vjɛt͡s] (latawiec), [la.taˈvi.t͡sə] (latawica); plural "latawce" [laˈta.ft͡sɛ].

Aliases — Latawica, Latawce, Lataniec

Powers
Control of wind and storms

Latawiec is repeatedly described as a demon of the wind, arriving with gusts, whirlwinds, and storms. It can ride air currents, stir up whirlwinds, and influence the direction and strength of winds. In some accounts, appeased latawce can calm winds or direct them to power windmills, while offended ones intensify storms and cause damage.

Limit: Its influence is strongest during naturally windy or stormy conditions; it is not portrayed as creating weather ex nihilo but amplifying or redirecting existing forces. Some traditions state that latawce can be killed by lightning, suggesting that extreme storms may also destroy them.

Seduction and erotic compulsion

Under Christian influence, latawiec (male) and latawica (female) become demons of sexual temptation. They appear as irresistibly attractive humans or winged figures, visiting lonely or grieving individuals at night, offering comfort and erotic pleasure. Victims gradually lose strength, social ties, and mental stability, becoming pale, withdrawn, and obsessed with nocturnal encounters.

Limit: Protection methods include avoiding windy nights, staying indoors, prayer, and carrying apotropaic items such as garlic in some modern retellings. The demon’s access is often limited to night and to households with emotional vulnerability.

Household familiar and wealth-bringer

In an alternative tradition, latawiec is a fiery spirit hatched from rooster eggs and raised as a household familiar. It can bring grain and money to its keeper, sometimes by stealing from neighbors or attracting supernatural abundance. It may resemble a small fiery serpent (żmija) and is kept hidden, fed, and ritually maintained.

Limit: Its loyalty depends on continuous offerings and secrecy; neglect or betrayal can cause it to turn against its keeper or leave. The practice is considered dangerous and morally dubious, exposing the household to demonic influence.

Lightning and fire association

Some regional beliefs attribute lightning strikes and resulting fires to latawiec’s activity, especially when storms follow sightings of fiery streaks in the sky. In other accounts, latawce themselves die when struck by lightning, making lightning both weapon and signature of their presence.

Limit: This power is not consistently described as deliberate control; often it is an interpretive attribution by villagers to explain destructive storms.

Shape-shifting

Latawiec can shift between forms: black bird, bird with human face, winged human, fiery serpent, blazing streak, or fully human seducer. This flexibility allows it to move between sky and household, between terrifying and alluring appearances.

Limit: Shape-shifting is implied rather than systematically described; some accounts may simply reflect different regional images rather than a single entity changing form.

Accounts I
Souls of the unborn riding the stormPolish regional folklore; Strzelczyk; Gieysztor; Lamus Dworski; Brendan Noble

Pre-modern folklore with Christian-era reinterpretations (medieval to modern)

In many Polish and borderland tales, latawiec and latawica are the souls of children lost before or during birth, or unbaptized infants. Denied proper ritual passage, these souls rise into the air as black birds or winged children, riding storms and whirlwinds. Villagers hear their cries in the wind and see them circling above fields and houses. Some stories say that during great storms, lightning strikes them down, ending their restless flight. Others describe them as occasionally descending to become household spirits, protecting the home if given offerings and prayers. These stories explain the tragedy of infant death and miscarriage, giving a narrative form to grief and fear of unbaptized souls. They also justify ritual care for infants and caution against neglecting religious rites.

The fiery lover over the villageNasze Szlaki, "Latawica i latawiec – demony ognia, nocy i ludzkich pragnień" (legendary retelling)

Modern retelling of older folklore

A widely told type of story describes latawica or latawiec as a fiery streak crossing the night sky above a village. When a lonely widow or widower gazes at the blazing sign, the demon descends in the form of a beloved, long-lost partner. It enters the house through the chimney, sits by the bed, and offers comfort, tenderness, and passionate nights. Over time, the victim grows pale and withdrawn, neglecting work and social ties, living only for nocturnal visits. Eventually, the person dies or goes mad, leaving neighbors to whisper that latawica has claimed another soul. The story warns against excessive grief and the lure of illusory comfort, framing erotic obsession and depression as demonic influence. It reinforces social norms about staying indoors during storms and seeking human, not supernatural, consolation.

The rooster-egg familiarPodgórski lexicon; Jack Wildman article; Wikipedia

Early modern to modern folklore

In an alternative tradition, a cunning villager collects eggs laid by a rooster—a transgressive act, since roosters are not supposed to lay eggs. From these eggs, a small fiery spirit, the latawiec, hatches. The villager hides and feeds it, and in return the demon brings grain and money, sometimes stealing from neighbors’ barns. The household prospers, but rumors spread. Eventually, a storm comes, and lightning strikes the house. Some say the latawiec was killed; others claim it fled, leaving the family ruined and cursed. The story functions as a moral tale about illicit wealth, envy, and the dangers of dealing with demonic forces. It also explains sudden prosperity and catastrophic fires in terms of supernatural causation.
