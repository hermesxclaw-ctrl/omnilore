Who It Is

Adamastor is the terrifying giant-spirit of the Cape of Storms (later Cape of Good Hope) — the monstrous embodiment of the dangers faced by Portuguese navigators rounding the southern tip of Africa. In Luís de Camões' epic poem 'Os Lusíadas' (Canto V), the Portuguese fleet under Vasco da Gama encounters a thunderous dark cloud on the horizon, which materializes into a colossal, deformed giant. Adamastor's face is 'sunk in gloom,' his beard filthy with sea-foam, his mouth blackened, and his eyes burning with hatred. He is one of the Gigantes (Giants) who rebelled against the gods, and he fell in love with the sea-nymph Thetis. Tricked by the goddess Doris into embracing a rocky mountain (which he mistook for Thetis), Adamastor was petrified — literally transformed into the rock of the Cape. Now he guards the passage to the Indian Ocean, raging against all who dare to sail where he was humiliated. His speech to the Portuguese is a terrifying prophecy: he foretells the shipwrecks, drownings, and disasters that will befall all who follow da Gama's route — including the eventual death of Bartolomeu Dias, the first European to round the Cape. Adamastor is one of world literature's great personifications of nature's hostility to human ambition — the sea as jealous, grieving, and vengeful. He represents Portuguese national trauma and triumph: the monster that must be faced and named for the empire to be born. Cape Town's 'Adamastor' statue and numerous literary references — from Fernando Pessoa to André Brink — testify to his enduring symbolic power.
