Who It Is

Palamaon never builds a labyrinth, never flies, never kills — and that is why he matters. In Athenian myth he is a name at the edge of the family tree, the shadow-father of Daedalus, whose very obscurity reveals how the Greeks argued over who owned the idea of craft itself.

No behavior, personality, speech, or moral character is attested for Palamaon. He does not act in any surviving story. The Suda and Tzetzes present him solely as a patronymic marker ('son of Palamaon'). Any personality would be hypothetical. Status: unknown.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Ancient Greek Παλαμάων, from παλάμη (palamē) 'palm of the hand, hand, skill, device, craft' + suffix -άων (-aōn), a common heroic patronymic/agent formation. The name is transparently eponymous for manual skill/craftsmanship, essentially 'Craftsman' or 'Handy One.' Related to verb παλάσσω (palassō)

Pronunciation — English: pal-a-MAY-on /pæləˈmeɪɒn/; Ancient Greek: [pa.la.má.ɔːn] Pa-la-MA-ōn, with stress on the penultimate long alpha; Modern Greek: PalaMAon

Aliases — Palamaōn, Παλαμάων

Ownership — Athenian Greek mythology, specifically the Attic craftsman genealogy centered on Daedalus. Palamaon belongs to the mythic Athenian royal-artisan stemma (Erechtheid/Metionid line) a

Powers
Genealogical Craft Inheritance (Eponymous)

Palamaon has no independently attested powers, spells, or feats. His name itself, derived from palamē 'hand/skill,' implies inherited craft excellence passed to his son Daedalus, the archetypal master craftsman. This is an etymological inference, not a narrated ability.

Limit: No text credits Palamaon with building, inventing, or performing any deed. All craft feats (Labyrinth, wings, statues) belong to Daedalus. Palamaon cannot be credited with Daedalus's abilities.

No Attested Supernatural or Heroic Abilities

Unlike Daedalus, Palamaon is not credited with flight, cunning, or divine favor, nor with weaknesses or vulnerabilities. No source gives him a weapon, transformation, or resistance.

Limit: Cannot be used to power-scale; any assignment of Daedalid powers to Palamaon would be fabrication.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No ancient source describes Palamaon's physical appearance, age, clothing, or attributes. He is a purely genealogical name with no narrative scene, no iconography, and no cult statue. Any visual depiction would be invention. Attested: name only. Unknown: everything else.

Symbols — No symbols, tools, animals, or iconographic attributes are directly attached to Palamaon. By etymological association with palamē, modern scholars infer a symbolic link to the craftsman's hand, but no ancient text or vase painting labels Palamaon with a tool. LIMC has no entry for Palamaon; all Daedalid iconography attaches to Daedalus himself. Status: reconstructed association, not attested.

Domains — Domain: Athenian genealogy, specifically the artisan lineage of the Metionids/Erechtheids. Habitat: Athens (mythic). Palamaon is placed in the generation before Daedalus, making him contemporary with Metion and Eupalamus in variant stemmata, and a descendant of Erechtheus in the Merope variant. No realm, workshop, or residence is described. Status: attested only as Athenian.

Accounts I
Palamaon as Father of Daedalus — The Suda TraditionSuda Encyclopedia, entry delta 145 s.v. Daidalos; Tzetzes, Chiliades 1.490-493

10th century CE (Suda) compiling Hellenistic/Classical material; 12th century CE (Tzetzes)

In the Suda's lexicon entry for Daedalus, the architect is identified as 'son of Palamaon, an Athenian.' Tzetzes repeats this as one of four canonical variant fathers. The entry is brief, offering no narrative beyond the patronymic, but it establishes Palamaon as a legitimate, if minority, father-figure in the Daedalid stemma. The Suda's compiler likely drew on earlier mythographers (perhaps Hellanicus or Philochorus) now lost. Creates an alternative Athenian lineage for Daedalus that bypasses the more common Eupalamus/Metion chain; demonstrates fluidity of Athenian genealogies in lexicographic tradition.

The Eupalamus Variant — The Dominant Athenian GenealogyApollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.15.8; Hyginus, Fabulae 39; Servius, In Aen. 6.14

1st century BCE - 2nd century CE

Apollodorus gives the most cited genealogy: Metion son of Erechtheus begets Eupalamus, who by Alcippe begets Daedalus. Hyginus and Servius follow this. In this version Palamaon does not exist; his slot is occupied by Eupalamus. The story is not a narrative event but a genealogical claim that anchors Daedalus in the Erechtheid royal house and explains his Athenian citizenship before his exile for murder. Establishes Daedalus as member of Athenian royalty, explaining his trial at the Areopagus and his status as more than a mere artisan. Palamaon tradition is implicitly rejected.

The Metion Direct-Father Variant — Diodorus's Rationalized VersionDiodorus Siculus, Bibliotheca Historica 4.76.1

c. 60-30 BCE

Diodorus simplifies the genealogy: 'Daedalus, an Athenian by birth, was the son of Metion.' He omits Eupalamus and Palamaon entirely, collapsing a generation. Diodorus then narrates Daedalus's murder of his nephew Talos/Perdix out of jealousy, his trial, and flight to Crete. The simplification suggests Diodorus or his source (perhaps Cleitodemus or Philochorus) found the multiple craft-names redundant. Shortens the lineage and makes Daedalus a direct Metionid, altering inheritance and political implications. Shows ancient authors themselves edited the family tree.

Accounts II
The Merope Variant — Daedalus as Grandson of ErechtheusScholia and Tzetzes, Chiliades 1.492; Hyginus Fabulae 244 variant; Apollodorus 3.15.8 scholia

Hellenistic scholia preserved in Byzantine sources

A fourth variant makes Daedalus the son of Merope, daughter of King Erechtheus, by either Eupalamus or Metion (or Palamaon in some conflations). This makes Daedalus the maternal grandson of Erechtheus, tightening his royal blood. Palamaon is sometimes inserted here as husband of Merope, though no primary text explicitly says 'Palamaon and Merope' together; the combination is a modern harmonization of two separate variants and must be flagged as reconstructed. Elevates Daedalus's status to direct Erechtheid prince, explaining his prominence in Athenian foundation myths.

The Murder of Talos/Perdix — The Crime of the House of PalamaonApollodorus 3.15.8; Diodorus 4.76.4-7; Hyginus Fabulae 39; Ovid Metamorphoses 8.236-259

Classical to Augustan (Ovid 8 CE)

Daedalus, son of Palamaon (in that variant) or Eupalamus/Metion, takes his sister's son Perdix (also called Talos or Calos) as apprentice. The boy invents the saw (from a fish spine or serpent jaw) and the compass, surpassing his master. In envy, Daedalus pushes him from the Acropolis (or temple of Athena). Athena saves the boy by turning him into a partridge (perdix). Daedalus is tried by the Areopagus and exiled. Though Palamaon does not act, the story defines his house's dark legacy: craft jealousy and kin-murder. Daedalus's exile to Crete, where he builds the Labyrinth for Minos; establishes the moral ambiguity of the craftsman lineage. Palamaon's name is thereafter associated with a family marked by envy.

Exile to Crete and the Labyrinth — The Son of Palamaon in Service to MinosDiodorus 4.76-77; Apollodorus 3.1.3-3.15.8; Pausanias 1.26.4, 9.39.8; Plutarch, Theseus 19

Classical to 2nd century CE

After the murder, Daedalus, identified in the Suda variant as son of Palamaon, flees Athens and is received by King Minos in Crete. There he builds the Labyrinth to house the Minotaur (offspring of Pasiphae and the Cretan Bull), crafts the wooden cow for Pasiphae, and later aids Theseus and Ariadne with the thread. His Athenian origin (via Palamaon/Eupalamus) is repeatedly stressed to explain why an Athenian works for Crete and why Theseus's later escape has pan-Hellenic significance. Makes the Palamaonid line central to the two most famous Cretan myths (Minotaur and Icarus). The Labyrinth becomes the defining symbol of Daedalid craft inherited, by implication, from Palamaon.

Palamaon is the same as Palaemon (Melicertes), the child-god of the Isthmian Games. — They are entirely separate figures. Palaemon (Παλαίμων, from palaios or palaimōn) is the deified son of Ino and Athamas, protector of sailors; Palamaon (Παλαμάων, from palamē) is an Athenian genealogical father of Daedalus with no cult, no sea association, and no transformation story. Confusion arises from similar Latinized spelling (Palamaon/Palaemon) and auto-complete errors.

Palamaon was a famous craftsman or inventor himself, like his son Daedalus. — No source credits Palamaon with any invention, building, or craft feat. All Labyrinth, wing, and statue traditions belong to Daedalus. Palamaon's name suggests craft etymologically, but he has no narrative or skill attributed. Attributing Daedalus's feats to Palamaon is a modern invention.

Palamaon is the canonical, universally accepted father of Daedalus. — Palamaon is the rarest variant, attested only in the Suda and Tzetzes (Byzantine lexicography). The dominant ancient tradition (Apollodorus, Diodorus, Hyginus, Servius) gives Eupalamus son of Metion or Metion directly. Most modern handbooks mention Palamaon only as a footnote variant.
