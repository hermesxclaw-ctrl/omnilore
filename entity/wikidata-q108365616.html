Who It Is

He was one of fifty sons conceived in a king's desperate gamble for grandsons — a Thespian prince born to carry Heracles' blood to the far edge of the Greek world, where his name survives only in a list, and his fate was to sail for Sardinia and vanish into colonization myth.

No individual personality, speech, or motive is attested for Homolippus. All behavior is collective: born at Thespiae, retained or dispatched according to Heracles' instructions. The narrative frames the sons as obedient instruments of Heracles' and Thespius' reproductive and colonial designs, later as founders under Iolaus. Any personality ascribed in modern fiction is invention.

'Homolippus' would reflect Greek Ὁμόλιππος (homó-lippos, from homos 'same, common' + lip- perhaps from leipō 'to leave, abandon' or lipos 'fat, grease' — etymology obscure and not explained in ancient sources). Aliases — Homolippos, Homolipos, Threpsippas (Θρεψίππας) — transmitted variant conflated with Homolippus in some modern summaries, Θρεψίππας (Greek manuscript form)

Powers
Heraclid lineage (presumed heroic vigor)

As a son of Heracles, Homolippus would be considered a Heraclid with implied exceptional strength and heroic status by birth, though no feat is narrated for him individually. The collective Thespiads are called Heracleidae and treated as founders/heroes.

Limit: Mortal, not deified; no invulnerability, no divine power attested; subject to Heracles' paternal authority and Thespius' arrangements

Colonial founder status (Sardinian Heracleidae)

If among the forty sent to Sardinia, Homolippus shares in the collective role of oikist (colonist-founder) under Iolaus, credited with Hellenizing Sardinia and establishing cults of Iolaus and Heracles.

Limit: Leadership is attributed to Iolaus, not to Homolippus individually; no city or sanctuary is named for him

No attested individual technique, weapon, or transformation

No ancient text gives Homolippus a weapon, craft, metamorphosis, or magical ability. Absence is documented.

Limit: Cannot be assigned lion-skin or club as personal attribute; those belong to Heracles

Accounts I
The Cithaeronian Lion HuntApollodorus 2.4.9-10; Diodorus Siculus 4.29.1

Mythic heroic age; texts 1st century BCE - 2nd century CE, reflecting earlier Boeotian tradition

At age eighteen, Heracles, living with Amphitryon, kills the lion of Cithaeron that was ravaging the herds of Amphitryon and Thespius. King Thespius of Thespiae entertains Heracles for fifty days during the hunt, using the occasion to have Heracles beget children on his fifty daughters. Establishes the hospitality trap that leads to the conception of the fifty Thespiads, including Homolippus; demonstrates Heracles' early heroic prowess before the Labours

The Night(s) with the Fifty ThespiadsApollodorus 2.4.10; Athenaeus 13.556F citing Herodorus of Heraclea; Diodorus 4.29.1; Tzetzes Chiliades 2.221-227

Archaic to Hellenistic mythography

Thespius offers his daughters to Heracles, either through deception (sending a different daughter each night while Heracles believes it is the same woman) or open arrangement, while Heracles is drunk or hospitably entertained. In Apollodorus, Heracles sleeps with all fifty in one night (or in some manuscripts, over successive nights), thinking each time it is the same daughter. Xanthis conceives Homolippus on her night. Conception of Homolippus and his 49 half-brothers; creates the Heraclid-Thespian kin group that will be divided for political/colonial purposes

The Birth and Naming of Homolippus son of XanthisApollodorus 2.4.10 (catalogue); Tzetzes Chiliades 2.221-227

Hellenistic mythographic catalogue

Xanthis, daughter of Thespius (mother's name varies by manuscript but standard list gives Xanthis as mother of Homolippus), bears a son to Heracles. The child is named Homolippus (or Threpsippas in variant manuscripts). He is raised at Thespiae alongside his half-brothers and cousins, part of the large Thespian royal household that claims Heraclean descent. Adds one to the fifty Thespian Heracleidae; secures Thespius' lineage; provides a pool of sons for Heracles' later colonial plan

Accounts II
Heracles' Division of the Sons: Seven, Three, and FortyApollodorus 2.4.10-11; Diodorus 4.29.2-3

Hellenistic

Years later, Heracles sends a message to Thespius (or returns in person in some versions) instructing him what to do with the grown sons: keep seven at Thespiae, send three to Thebes, and send the remaining forty, joined by his nephew and charioteer Iolaus, to colonize Sardinia. This tripartition reflects Boeotian, Theban, and western colonial interests. Determines Homolippus's fate: in most reconstructions he is among the forty Sardinian colonists, though no source singles him out by name for a specific group; establishes the mythic charter for Greek claims in Sardinia

The Sardinian Expedition with IolausDiodorus 4.29.3-4, 5.15.1-6; Pausanias 10.17.5; Apollodorus 2.7.6

Archaic colonization myth retrojected to heroic age; texts 1st century BCE - 2nd century CE

Iolaus, nephew of Heracles, leads the forty Thespiads (including Homolippus in the standard grouping) to Sardinia. They seize or settle the island, build works, introduce Greek customs, and are later heroized. Diodorus says they founded a colony, built gymnasia and temples, and that Iolaus was worshipped as a hero; Pausanias adds they seized the fertile plain and that the island was called Iolaea after Iolaus. Mythic aetiology for Sardinian Greek presence and for the cult of Iolaus in Sardinia; the Thespiads become eponymous ancestors of Sardinian peoples (Iolaeis); Homolippus disappears from narrative after settlement — no individual foundation attributed

The Thespian Memory: Hero Cult at ThespiaePausanias 9.27.6-7; Inscriptions from Thespiae (IG VII 1785 etc., collective Heracles cult)

Classical to Roman period cult practice

At Thespiae, the sons who remained (seven) and the memory of all fifty were honored as Heracleidae. Pausanias notes Thespiaeans worship Heracles and that the city claimed descent from the Thespiads. While Homolippus individually has no cult, collectively the Thespiads anchor Thespian identity and aristocratic genealogy. Provides religious and political legitimacy for Thespian elite claiming Heraclean blood; explains why catalogue was preserved

Homolippus was a famous hero with his own labours or adventures — He has no individual myth, feat, or cult. He is solely a name in the catalogue of 50 sons and a collective member of the Sardinian colonial group. All 'adventures' belong to Heracles or Iolaus.

Ancient Greek spelling of Homolippus is Θρεψίππας — Θρεψίππας spells Threpsippas, a different (or conflated) name meaning 'horse-rearer.' Homolippus would be Ὁμόλιππος. The equation in Wikipedia/Wikidata is a conflation error.

Heracles consensually seduced fifty willing princesses in a romantic episode — Ancient sources describe Thespius' orchestration, Heracles' deception/intoxication, and the daughters' passivity. Consent is not discussed; modern romanticization erases the power imbalance and reproductive exploitation.

The forty Thespiads historically colonized Sardinia and founded cities still identifiable as Heraclid — The Sardinian expedition is a mythic charter story (aetiology) with no archaeological support for a Heraclid Greek colony in Sardinia in the heroic age. Historical Sardinia was Nuragic, then Phoenician/Carthaginian. The myth legitimized later Greek claims.

Homolippus appears as a character in games like Assassin's Creed Odyssey or Hades — No major game, film, or series gives Homolippus an individual appearance or speaking role. Thespiae and Heracles appear, but Homolippus remains a genealogical entry.
