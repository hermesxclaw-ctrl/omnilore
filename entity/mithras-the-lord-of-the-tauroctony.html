Who It Is

Mithras is the central figure of the Roman Mithraic mysteries — a savior-deity of astral character whose cult was practiced in subterranean 'caves' (mithraea) scattered across the Roman Empire, from Syria to Britain, between the 1st and 4th centuries CE. Despite the name, Roman Mithraism diverged significantly from its Zoroastrian counterpart (Mithra): Mithras is not the judge of covenants but a cosmic bull-slayer whose tauroctony (the killing of the bull) is a soteriological act with astrological symbolism. The tauroctony relief shows Mithras — in Phrygian cap, billowing cape, and oriental trousers — physically subduing a bull, simultaneously plunging a dagger into its neck. A dog leaps toward the wound, a snake strikes the bull's testicles or blood, a scorpion attacks the bull's genitals, a raven perches on Mithras's cape, and Cautes (the torch-bearer with raised torch) and Cautopates (with lowered torch) flank the scene. The standard scholarly interpretation (Ulansey, Beck) reads the scene as astral: the bull represents Taurus, the dagger is the constellation Perseus (which in the precessional era 'killed' Taurus), and the entire tableau encodes the astrological knowledge of the precession of the equinoxes. Mithras's 'unconquered' status (Invictus) echoes Sol Invictus, and Mithras is both the sun's companion and the sun's superior.
