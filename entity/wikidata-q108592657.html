Who It Is

She enters without a name. In the Gospels she is only "the daughter of Herodias" — a girl who dances at a birthday banquet and leaves with a prophet's head on a platter. A few decades later the historian Josephus gives her a name, a lineage, and two royal marriages: Salome, princess of the Herodian dynasty, granddaughter of Herod the Great, queen of Armenia Minor. Between those two spare ancient notices, centuries of painters, poets, and composers have poured desire, dread, and decadence, turning a Judean aristocrat into the archetypal femme fatale who still watches from Moreau's jewelled canvases and Wilde's moonlit terrace.

Historical personality unknown. Gospels portray her as obedient to mother: she consults Herodias before requesting the head and acts 'with haste' (Mark 6:25) and brings head to mother, suggesting filial compliance rather than independent agency; Herod is 'exceedingly sorry' but bound by oath. Josephus implies a conventional dynastic princess whose marriages cement alliances. Patristic and medieval tradition reinterprets her as lascivious, foolish, and cold-hearted. Dynastic status and royal title

As daughter of Herod II and Herodias, granddaughter of Herod the Great, and later wife of tetrarch Philip and king Aristobulus, Salome held high aristocratic rank. Coins attest her as Basilissa (Queen) of Chalcis/Armenia Minor alongside Aristobulus.

Limit: Status derived entirely from male relatives; no independent political power attested. No evidence of ruling authority or cult.

Choreographic performance (dance)

In Mark 6:22 and Matthew 14:6, she performs a dance (Greek ōrchēsato) that 'pleased' Herod and his guests, sufficient to elicit a royal oath. Later tradition elaborates as the Dance of the Seven Veils.

Limit: No details of dance style given in Gospels. Erotic element is later Christian inference, not stated. Wilde's seven veils is pure invention.

Instrumental influence over sovereign via oath

By exploiting Herod Antipas's public oath ('whatever you ask, up to half my kingdom' - Mark 6:23, echoing Esther 5:3,6), she secures execution of a prisoner. Agency is mediated through mother Herodias's instruction.

Limit: Power is contingent, one-time, and dependent on Herod's honor/shame before guests. She does not command execution directly; Herod orders executioner.

Cultural immortality as archetype

In reception, Salome possesses enduring symbolic power as the femme fatale, embodying Decadent fears of female sexuality, Orientalism, and the gaze. This is a meta-ability: her image compels artistic reproduction.

Limit: Adaptation-specific; not an in-world power. Historical Salome had no such agency.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No contemporary physical description survives in 1st-century sources. As a Herodian princess, she would have dressed in elite Roman-Judaean fashion, but this is reconstruction, not attestation. Later artistic tradition invents appearance obsessively: medieval art shows her as a courtly maiden with platter; Renaissance (Titian, Caravaggio, Cranach) as an idealized young woman, often pale, richly dressed, holding or receiving the severed head; 19th

Symbols — Core iconographic attribute: the charger/platter (Greek pinax) bearing the head of John the Baptist, sometimes with blood. Secondary attributes: dancing pose, veils, jewels, peacock feathers (Moreau), severed head juxtaposed with her youth/beauty to signify contrast between innocence and cruelty. Accounts I
The Dance and the Beheading of John the BaptistGospel of Mark 6:14-29 and Gospel of Matthew 14:1-12

c. 65-90 CE (text composition); narrative set c. 28-32 CE under Herod Antipas

Herod Antipas has imprisoned John the Baptist for denouncing his marriage to Herodias (his brother's wife) as unlawful (Leviticus 18:16, 20:21). Herodias holds a grudge and wants John killed, but Herod fears John as righteous. At Herod's birthday banquet for nobles, military commanders, and leading men of Galilee, the daughter of Herodias enters and dances, pleasing Herod and guests. Herod swears to give her whatever she asks, up to half his kingdom. She goes out, consults her mother, returns 'with haste' and asks: 'I want you to give me at once the head of John the Baptist on a platter.' Herod is exceedingly sorry but, because of oaths and guests, sends an executioner to the prison, beheads John, brings head on a platter, gives it to the girl, who gives it to her mother. John's disciples bury his body. In narrative theology, John's death prefigures Jesus's death; Herod later confuses Jesus with risen John. Historically, story establishes Christian memory of John as martyr and Herodian court as decadent. Artistically generates two millennia of Salome iconography.

The Herodian Princess: Marriages and QueenshipJosephus, Antiquities of the Jews 18.136-137

Written c. 93-94 CE, describing events c. 14 BCE - 54 CE

Salome, daughter of Herod (son of Herod the Great and Mariamne daughter of Simon the high priest, i.e., Herod II) and Herodias, is born before Herodias divorces Herod II while he is alive - an act Josephus says 'confounded the laws of our country' - to marry her husband's half-brother Herod Antipas, tetrarch of Galilee. Salome is married to Philip the Tetrarch (son of Herod the Great and Cleopatra of Jerusalem), tetrarch of Trachonitis, Gaulanitis, and Batanea. Philip dies childless in 34 CE at Julias. Salome then marries her cousin Aristobulus of Chalcis (son of Herod of Chalcis, brother of Agrippa I). They have three sons: Herod, Agrippa, and Aristobulus. Through this marriage she becomes queen; coins attest her as Basilissa Salome in Chalcis and later Armenia Minor when Aristobulus is made king of Armenia Minor by Nero in 54 CE. Provides only prosopographical data for historical Salome, anchoring her in Herodian dynastic politics and showing a conventional elite life, not a brief femme fatale episode. Allows modern historians to date her birth c. 14-15 CE and death unknown after 6

The Death on the Ice: Medieval Legend of Salome's PunishmentPatristic and Byzantine chronicles: Nicephorus Callistus Xanthopoulos (Ecclesiastical History 1.20), later elaborated in medieval martyrologies and folk legend; also attributed to early apocryphal Acts

Legend first attested c. 9th-14th century, set after 34 CE

Divine retribution pursues Salome for John's death. While crossing a frozen river (variously identified as the Hebrus/Maritza, the Rhône, or a river in Gaul/Spain) in winter, the ice breaks. She falls through up to her neck, and the sharp ice severs her head, which slides across the ice while her body sinks - mirroring John's beheading. Variant says she danced on ice to test it. Another tradition (cited by William Smith via 'Joseph'?) places her birth in Italica (Spain) and death in Lleida (Ilerda), linking her to Iberian folklore. No historical value but demonstrates medieval Christian moral need to punish Salome, transforming her from obedient girl to archetypal sinner receiving talionic justice. Widely reproduced in sermons, Golden Legend-adjacent texts, and later folk preaching.

Accounts II
Hérodias: Flaubert's Archaeological SalomeGustave Flaubert, 'Hérodias' in Trois contes (Three Tales)

Published 1877, set in Machaerus c. 32 CE

Flaubert reconstructs Machaerus with archaeological precision from Josephus and contemporary travel. Antipas is a vacillating tetrarch caught between Romans (Vitellius), Arabs (Aretas), and Jewish sects. Herodias is a political schemer. Salome (explicitly named following Josephus) is a youthful, almost androgynous adolescent, largely absent until the climax. At the banquet, she appears on the terrace, performs a dance described with detached sensuality, and demands John's head almost as an afterthought at Herodias's prompting. Flaubert emphasizes the head's presentation and Antipas's horror, stripping away Decadent eroticism in favor of historical exoticism and irony. First modern work to consistently call the dancer Salome, cementing the identification for mass culture. Direct source for Massenet's opera Hérodiade (1881) and influence on Wilde. Established 'archaeological' mode of Salome fiction.

Wilde's Salome: The Princess Who Kisses a Severed MouthOscar Wilde, Salome: drame en un acte (French 1891, English 1894); Richard Strauss opera Salome, Op. 54 (1905) libretto after Hedwig Lachmann's German translation

1891-1905, set in a timeless Herodian palace

On a moonlit terrace, the young princess Salome, stepdaughter of Herod (Hérode), is fascinated by the imprisoned prophet Jokanaan (John). Rejected when she begs to kiss his mouth ('Thou didst reject me'), she performs the Dance of the Seven Veils for Herod after he begs her to dance, despite her initial refusal. Enraptured, Herod offers up to half his kingdom. Salome demands the head of Jokanaan on a silver platter. After hesitation, Herod complies. Salome seizes the head and kisses its dead mouth in ecstatic necrophilia: 'Ah! thou wouldst not let me kiss thy mouth, Jokanaan. Well! I will kiss it now.' Herod, horrified, orders soldiers to crush her: 'Kill that woman!' Definitive modern myth: transforms Gospel's obedient girl into a willful, erotic, death-driven femme fatale. Banned by Lord Chamberlain in Britain (1892) for biblical characters on stage; premiered in Paris 1896. Strauss's opera (1905) with its dissonant 'Dance' and final shrieking chord made Salome a musical icon. Source for countless films, ballets, paintings.

Salome at the Empty Tomb: Apocryphal ConflationBook of the Resurrection of Christ by Bartholomew the Apostle (apocryphal, Coptic/Arabic manuscripts), pseudonymously attributed to Bartholomew

Text in present form c. 5th-7th century CE, reflecting earlier traditions

Among the women who go to the empty tomb on Easter morning, the text lists 'Salome the temptress' (Salome hē peirastheisa) alongside Mary Magdalene and others. While canonical Mark 16:1 lists 'Salome' as a Myrrhbearer distinct from Herodias's daughter, this apocryphal epithet 'the temptress' suggests early confusion or deliberate identification of the dancer with the disciple, perhaps implying redemption or ironic presence at the Resurrection. Some scholars see this as evidence that early Christians already linked the two Salomes. Shows early tradition grappling with two New Testament Salomes and moralizing the dancer's legacy. No historical weight for the princess's biography, but important for reception history and naming confusion.

Salome performed the Dance of the Seven Veils in the Bible. — The Bible never mentions seven veils or describes the dance at all. 'Dance of the Seven Veils' was invented by Oscar Wilde in 1891 (stage direction: 'Salome dances the dance of the seven veils') and popularized by Strauss's opera. No ancient source attests veils.

Salome was a seductive adult who asked for John's head because John rejected her love. — In Mark and Matthew, Salome asks for the head only after consulting Herodias, who wants John dead for criticizing her marriage. The motive is maternal revenge, not Salome's erotic obsession. The rejected-lover motive is Wilde's 1891 invention (Salome desires Jokanaan, he rejects her, she kisses his severed head).

Salome is the same as Salome the disciple who witnessed the Crucifixion. — Mark 15:40 and 16:1 mention a different Salome, one of the women at the cross and tomb (often identified as mother of sons of Zebedee). The Gospels never link her to Herodias's daughter. The conflation appears only in some apocryphal texts and modern confusion.

Salome was a common prostitute or courtesan. — She was a Herodian princess, daughter of a tetrarch's wife, wife of two rulers, and queen of Armenia Minor. Coins attest her as Basilissa (Queen). Her status was aristocratic, not marginal. The prostitute image derives from Christian moralizing that cast her as personification of lust.

The story proves ancient Jewish princesses routinely performed erotic dances for male guests. — No evidence that Herodian princesses performed publicly; if historical, the dance would be exceptional and perhaps scandalous, which is why Gospels note it. Josephus, who details Herodian court life, never mentions such performances. The dance's historicity itself is debated.
