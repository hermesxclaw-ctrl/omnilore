Who It Is

Deep in the Sonoran Desert, where the saguaro cactus stands sentinel and the earth itself seems to hold its breath, there is a story of a creator who chose to live among the people he made. His name is I'itoi, the Elder Brother of the O'odham, a figure who is at once a god, a trickster, and a friend, whose footprints are said to be etched into the very rock of the land he shaped.

I'itoi is a complex figure: a creator, a teacher, a trickster, and a protector. He is known for his wisdom, but also for his mischief. He is said to have lived among the O'odham, teaching them how to live in the desert, but he also played tricks on them. He is generally benevolent, but can be wrathful if offended.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name I'itoi is often translated as 'Elder Brother' or 'Older Brother' in English. The Tohono O'odham word is sometimes given as 'I'itoi' or 'I'itʰoi'. The exact linguistic analysis is not fully verified; it may relate to a root meaning 'older brother' or 'elder'.

Pronunciation — ee-EE-toy (approximate, based on common pronunciations in English-language sources).

Aliases — Elder Brother, Older Brother, I'itoi (spelling variants), Se'he (possibly), Sihu (possibly)

Powers
Creation

I'itoi is said to have helped create the world and the O'odham people, often with the assistance of other beings (e.g., Coyote, the Creator). He may have formed the first humans from clay or other materials.

Limit: In some stories, he is not the sole creator; he works with others and may have limited power.

Shape-shifting

I'itoi can change his form, often becoming an animal like a spider, coyote, or old man.

Limit: He may be limited by his own nature or by the actions of others.

Immortality / Longevity

I'itoi is a deathless being, having survived great floods and other catastrophes.

Limit: He can be wounded or captured, but not permanently killed.

Teaching and Guidance

I'itoi taught the O'odham how to farm, hunt, and live in the desert. He gave them ceremonies and laws.

Limit: His teachings were sometimes given through trickery or indirect means.

Control over Nature

He can control weather, cause earthquakes, or manipulate natural forces.

Limit: His power may be limited by other supernatural beings.

Form & Signs

Appearance — I'itoi is often described as a small, old man with white hair, sometimes wearing a hat or carrying a cane. He is sometimes depicted as a trickster figure who can change shape. In some stories, he appears as a spider or other animal. No canonical fixed appearance exists; descriptions vary by community and story.

Symbols — I'itoi is often associated with the labyrinth symbol found on O'odham basketry and pottery, sometimes called the 'I'itoi labyrinth' or 'Man in the Maze'. This symbol is not universally agreed to represent I'itoi; some scholars suggest it may represent a person's journey through life. Other symbols include the saguaro cactus, the desert, and the spider.

Domains — I'itoi is associated with the Sonoran Desert, particularly with mountains and caves. He is said to have lived in a cave on Baboquivari Peak, which is a sacred mountain to the Tohono O'odham. He is also associated with the underworld and with the spirit world.

Accounts I
The Creation of the WorldVarious O'odham creation narratives, recorded by ethnographers such as Russell and Underhill

Oral tradition, recorded in late 19th-early 20th century

In the beginning, there was a dark world. I'itoi and other beings (like Coyote and the Creator) worked to bring light and life. In some versions, a great flood destroyed the earlier world, and I'itoi survived by floating in a pot or hiding in a cave. After the flood, he recreated the people and taught them how to live. The world as we know it was established, and the O'odham were placed in the Sonoran Desert.

The Battle with the MonsterRecorded by Russell, Underhill, and others

Oral tradition

I'itoi fought a fearsome monster or monsters (sometimes identified as a giant, a serpent, or a bird) that threatened the people. He used his wits and sometimes the help of animals to defeat the enemy, often by tricking it into destroying itself. The people were saved, and I'itoi established himself as their protector.

Accounts II
The Trickster TalesVarious ethnographic collections

Oral tradition

I'itoi often plays tricks on the people or on other animals, sometimes for his own amusement, sometimes to teach a lesson. He may steal fire, deceive a rival, or outwit a greedy person. These tales often explain natural phenomena or social customs.

The Departure of I'itoiSome oral traditions and ethnographic notes

Oral tradition

After teaching the people, I'itoi departed to his home in the mountains, promising to return in times of need. He is said to live in a cave on Baboquivari Peak, where he watches over the people. Baboquivari Peak became a sacred site, and I'itoi is still venerated.
