Who It Is

Azazel occupies a unique position at the intersection of biblical ritual, apocalyptic mythology, and demonology. In Leviticus 16, the Yom Kippur ritual involves two goats: one sacrificed to YHWH, the other — designated 'for Azazel' — is sent into the wilderness bearing the sins of Israel. The term itself is enigmatic: Azazel may refer to a cliff from which the goat was thrown, a demon of the wilderness, or a compound meaning 'the goat that departs' (the origin of the English word 'scapegoat'). Second Temple apocalyptic literature dramatically expanded Azazel into a full mythological figure. The Book of Enoch identifies Azazel as the tenth of the fallen Watchers — the angelic beings who descended to Mount Hermon, mated with human women (producing the Nephilim), and taught humanity forbidden knowledge: metallurgy and weapon-making, cosmetics and jewelry, sorcery and astrology. Azazel is specifically blamed for teaching men to make swords, shields, and armor — introducing the technology of war. God condemns Azazel with particular severity: the archangel Raphael binds him hand and foot, casts him into the darkness of the desert, and buries him in the pit of Dudael beneath jagged rocks, where he will remain until the final judgment when he is cast into the lake of fire. The Apocalypse of Abraham expands on this, portraying Azazel as the tempter of Abraham and a cosmic adversary: he appears as an unclean, seven-headed bird-demon that seeks to disrupt Abraham's covenant with God. The text describes Azazel as being covered in 'impurity and corruption,' his true nature hidden beneath angelic beauty. In later demonology, Azazel is often conflated with the demon of the desert, the personification of sin itself, and one of the chief standard-bearers of Hell's armies. His myth encapsulates a profound theological idea: that sin can be literally transferred and expelled, that evil has a location (the wilderness, the abyss), and that even fallen celestial beings are subject to divine binding. Azazel's legacy endures in the modern concept of the 'scapegoat' — the innocent who bears the guilt of the community.
