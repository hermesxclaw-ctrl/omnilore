Who It Is

Supay (also Supai, Zupay) is the Inca god of death, the underworld, and the mineral wealth of the mountains — a complex deity whose nature was profoundly distorted by colonial Christianity. In the Inca tripartite cosmology, the cosmos is divided into Hanan Pacha (the upper world of the sun, stars, and gods), Kay Pacha (the middle world of humans and living things), and Uku Pacha (the lower/inner world of the dead, the ancestors, and the mineral realm). Supay rules the Uku Pacha, presiding over the spirits of the dead and the treasures of the earth: gold, silver, and all metals extracted from mines. He was not, in pre-colonial Andean thought, a 'devil' — he was a necessary and powerful figure who governed the transition from life to death, the realm of the ancestors, and the dangerous but rewarding depths of the earth. When Spanish missionaries arrived, they equated Supay with the Christian Devil (Diablo), and this association stuck: in contemporary Quechua and Aymara, 'Supay' is used as the word for the Devil. This linguistic colonization is one of the most dramatic examples of indigenous theological concepts being overwritten by colonial categories. In some surviving traditional contexts, however, miners still make offerings to Supay (or 'El Tío' in Bolivian mines) before entering the earth, acknowledging his ownership of the mineral realm. During the Diablada festival (Dance of the Devils), celebrated in the Lake Titicaca region, dancers wear elaborate horned masks and costumes — and while the performance superficially appears Catholic, ethnographers note that it retains pre-colonial Andean elements honoring the spirits of the earth. Supay thus persists in a syncretic, transformed, but never entirely erased form.
