Who It Is

Did you know that Supay, the Inca god of death, was not originally a demon but a complex spirit who could both protect and punish? His image has been transformed over centuries, from a shadowy guardian of the underworld to a devil-like figure in Christian-influenced art, yet he remains a vital part of Andean culture today.

Supay is an ambivalent deity, embodying both benevolent and malevolent forces. He is a protector of the dead, guiding souls to the underworld and ensuring their safe passage, but he is also a punisher of those who violate moral codes, dragging them into the depths of the earth. He is not inherently evil but is a force of balance, representing the shadow side of existence that is necessary for life. Powers
Soul Guidance

Supay has the ability to guide the souls of the dead from the world of the living to the Uku Pacha, ensuring they reach their final resting place. He is often depicted as a psychopomp, leading souls through the treacherous paths of the underworld, protecting them from malevolent spirits, and helping them find peace.

Limit:

Shape-shifting

Supay can assume various forms, including that of a fox, a condor, a whirlwind, or a shadowy humanoid figure. This ability allows him to move between the worlds of the living and the dead, and to interact with humans in ways that are both helpful and deceptive.

Limit:

Control over Earth's Riches

As the lord of the underworld, Supay has dominion over the mineral wealth of the earth, including gold, silver, and precious stones. Miners and treasure seekers often make offerings to him to gain his favor and protection, and he is believed to punish those who exploit the earth without proper respect.

Limit:

Judgment of Souls

Supay acts as a judge of the dead, determining the fate of souls based on their actions in life. Those who lived virtuously are rewarded with a peaceful existence in the underworld, while those who committed evil deeds are subjected to punishments, such as being chased by demons or trapped in dark caves.

The Origin of Supay and the Uku PachaInca mythology, as recorded in the Huarochirí Manuscript and oral traditions

Pre-Columbian (c. 15th century) and colonial (1608)

In the beginning, the world was divided into three realms: Hanan Pacha (the upper world), Kay Pacha (the world of the living), and Uku Pacha (the underworld). Supay was born from the primordial darkness that existed before creation, and he was given dominion over the Uku Pacha. He was neither good nor evil but a necessary balance to the light of the upper world. He was tasked with guarding the dead and ensuring that the cycle of life, death, and rebirth continued. In this myth, Supay is not a fallen angel but a primordial force, as ancient as the gods of the upper world. Supay became the eternal lord of the underworld, and the Uku Pacha was established as the final resting place for all souls. This myth established the Andean concept of death as a continuation of life in another realm, rather than an end.

Accounts II
Supay and the Miner's PactOral tradition, recorded in modern ethnographic studies

Colonial to modern (16th century - present)

A poor miner named Huanca, desperate to feed his family, made a pact with Supay in a cave deep in the Andes. Supay promised him a vein of rich silver, but in exchange, Huanca had to give him his soul upon his death. Huanca agreed and became wealthy, but as he grew old, he feared his fate. He sought the help of a priest, who told him to make a cross and place it over his heart so that Supay could not claim him. When Huanca died, Supay appeared to take his soul, but the cross repelled him. Enraged, Supay cursed the mine, causing it to collapse, and Huanca's body was never found. It is said that his ghost still wanders the mine, searching for redemption. This story serves as a cautionary tale about the dangers of making deals with Supay and the conflict between indigenous beliefs and Christianity. It also explains why miners often make offerings to Supay before entering mines, to avoid his wrath.

The Festival of Supay at CarnivalEthnographic accounts of the Carnival of Oruro and other Andean festivals

Colonial to modern (17th century - present)

During the Carnival of Oruro, Supay is celebrated as the 'Diablo' (devil), and dancers in elaborate demon costumes perform a ritual dance called the 'Diablada'. The dance reenacts the struggle between good and evil, with the archangel Michael defeating Supay and his demons. However, in the Andean context, Supay is not wholly evil; he is a force of chaos and fertility, and the dance is a way to honor him and ensure a good harvest. The festival is a syncretic blend of Catholic and indigenous beliefs, where Supay is both feared and revered. The festival reinforces social cohesion and the continuity of Andean traditions, while also serving as a form of resistance against colonial oppression. It has become a major tourist attraction, but for locals, it remains a sacred ritual.
