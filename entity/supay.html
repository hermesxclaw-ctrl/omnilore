Supay

God of Death and the Underworld; Lord of the Uku Pacha; The Taker of Souls

also known asSupaySupaiZupayCupiCupaySupay Apu
TypeDeath/Underworld Deity
CultureInca
Who It Is
Form

Supay is described in various sources as a being of shadow and darkness, often taking the form of a tall, dark figure with glowing eyes. In colonial-era illustrations, he is depicted with horns, a tail, and cloven hooves, reflecting the Christian demonization of his image. However, in indigenous Andean art, he is more likely to appear as a skeletal figure or a shape-shifter that can assume the form of a fox, a condor, or a whirlwind. Some accounts describe him as having a face that is half-human and half-animal, or as a being that is entirely made of shadow, with no discernible features except for his piercing gaze. In modern festivals, dancers representing Supay wear elaborate masks with large, bulging eyes and fanged mouths, often adorned with serpents and other underworld symbols.

Powers

Soul Guidance Supay has the ability to guide the souls of the dead from the world of the living to the Uku Pacha, ensuring they reach their final resting place. He is often depicted as a psychopomp, leading souls through the treacherous paths of the underworld, protecting them from malevolent spirits, and helping them find peace.

Shape-shifting Supay can assume various forms, including that of a fox, a condor, a whirlwind, or a shadowy humanoid figure. This ability allows him to move between the worlds of the living and the dead, and to interact with humans in ways that are both helpful and deceptive.

Control over Earth's Riches As the lord of the underworld, Supay has dominion over the mineral wealth of the earth, including gold, silver, and precious stones. Miners and treasure seekers often make offerings to him to gain his favor and protection, and he is believed to punish those who exploit the earth without proper respect.

Judgment of Souls Supay acts as a judge of the dead, determining the fate of souls based on their actions in life. Those who lived virtuously are rewarded with a peaceful existence in the underworld, while those who committed evil deeds are subjected to punishments, such as being chased by demons or trapped in dark caves.

Accounts

In the beginning, the world was divided into three realms: Hanan Pacha (the upper world), Kay Pacha (the world of the living), and Uku Pacha (the underworld). Supay was born from the primordial darkness that existed before creation, and he was given dominion over the Uku Pacha. He was neither good nor evil but a necessary balance to the light of the upper world. He was tasked with guarding the dead and ensuring that the cycle of life, death, and rebirth continued. In this myth, Supay is not a fallen angel but a primordial force, as ancient as the gods of the upper world.

A poor miner named Huanca, desperate to feed his family, made a pact with Supay in a cave deep in the Andes. Supay promised him a vein of rich silver, but in exchange, Huanca had to give him his soul upon his death. Huanca agreed and became wealthy, but as he grew old, he feared his fate. He sought the help of a priest, who told him to make a cross and place it over his heart so that Supay could not claim him. When Huanca died, Supay appeared to take his soul, but the cross repelled him. Enraged, Supay cursed the mine, causing it to collapse, and Huanca's body was never found. It is said that his ghost still wanders the mine, searching for redemption.

During the Carnival of Oruro, Supay is celebrated as the 'Diablo' (devil), and dancers in elaborate demon costumes perform a ritual dance called the 'Diablada'. The dance reenacts the struggle between good and evil, with the archangel Michael defeating Supay and his demons. However, in the Andean context, Supay is not wholly evil; he is a force of chaos and fertility, and the dance is a way to honor him and ensure a good harvest. The festival is a syncretic blend of Catholic and indigenous beliefs, where Supay is both feared and revered.

Modern Interpretations

Literature The Royal Commentaries of the Incas Garcilaso de la Vega 1609 Mention of Supay as a demon Garcilaso, a mestizo chronicler, described Supay as the 'devil' of the Incas, reflecting the colonial Christian perspective. His work shaped European understanding of Inca religion for centuries. Film The Devil's Miner Kief Davidson and Richard Ladkani 2005 Documentary featuring Supay as a cultural figure This documentary about a young miner in Bolivia shows how Supay is still worshipped by miners, who make offerings to him in the mines. It highlights the enduring relevance of Supay in modern Andean society. Video Game Civilization VI Firaxis Games 2016 Supay as a Great Prophet (mod) In a popular mod for Civilization VI, Supay is added as a Great Prophet for the Inca civilization, allowing players to spread his influence. This reflects the growing interest in lesser-known mythologies in gaming. Music Diablada Various Andean musicians 20th century Musical genre and dance The Diablada music and dance, performed during Carnival, is a direct cultural expression of Supay's influence. It has become a symbol of Andean identity and resistance.

Scholarly Debates
Nature of Supay
Supay is a benevolent spirit who protects the dead and ensures the cycle of life. ⇌ Supay is a malevolent demon who punishes sinners and is equated with the Christian devil.
Origin of Supay
Supay is a primordial being who existed before creation, as old as the gods themselves. ⇌ Supay is a fallen angel or a rebellious servant of Viracocha, cast into the underworld for his sins.
Role in the Underworld
Supay is the sole ruler of the Uku Pacha, with absolute authority over the dead. ⇌ Supay is one of many spirits in the underworld, and his power is limited by other deities such as Pachamama.
What We Don't Know

Further research is needed to clarify the pre-Columbian nature of Supay, as most written sources are from the colonial period and are heavily influenced by Christian perspectives. The relationship between Supay and other Andean deities, such as the 'Anchanchu' or 'Muki', is not well-documented. Additionally, the regional variations in Supay's worship and iconography across different Andean communities remain understudied. The impact of modern tourism on the authenticity of Supay-related rituals is also a topic that requires investigation.

A way to hold it in your mind

Did you know that Supay, the Inca god of death, was not originally a demon but a complex spirit who could both protect and punish? His image has been transformed over centuries, from a shadowy guardian of the underworld to a devil-like figure in Christian-influenced art, yet he remains a vital part of Andean culture today.
