Who It Is

In the crowded pantheon of Chinese folk religion, where every hearth, threshold and loom has its guardian, a name surfaces only as a whisper in a database — Yinchou Jinnü, the Silver-Ugly Golden Woman. She is listed as a deity, divine archetype, nothing more. No temple mural is confidently labeled, no ancient poem quoted in English names her outright — yet the two characters that make her name carry a whole cosmology of beauty and ugliness, silver and gold, impurity and value. To chase her is to chase how Chinese folk deities are born, merged, forgotten, and re-catalogued.

No hagiographic personality traits verified for Yinchou Jinnü. Generic Jinnü behavior in folklore: obedient attendants, intermediaries between human women and high goddesses, guardians of women's quarters (guifang), helpers in childbirth and weaving, sometimes tricksters or moral judges testing male supplicants. If Yinchou Jinnü is a distinct figure, the 'ugly' epithet may signal a personality of humility, hidden wisdom, or apotropaic ugliness to ward off evil (common in Chinese nicknaming). All DISPUTED/RECONSTRUCTED.

Powers
Attendant Intercession

In generic Jinnü lore, Jinnü act as intercessors: carrying prayers from women to higher goddesses (Xiwangmu, Guanyin) and returning blessings. Not a creator power but bureaucratic mediation.

Limit: Cannot grant major fate changes; must petition superior deity. Efficacy limited to domestic sphere.

Weaving and Domestic Craft Blessing

Blessing of loom work, embroidery, and textile skill; protection against loom accidents and ensuring even thread. Linked to Zhinü (Weaver Girl) complex.

Limit: Power tied to ritual observance on specific days (7th day of 7th month, etc.) and to moral conduct of supplicant.

Dual Appearance / Moral Testing (Hypothetical)

If 'Yinchou' (Silver-Ugly) is intentional, ability to appear ugly to test virtue or to hide golden true form. Common motif: goddess appears as old/ugly woman to test hospitality, revealing beauty when respected.

Limit: Transformation is illusion/moral test, not combat shapeshifting.

Apotropaic Protection for Women and Children

Generic Jinnü as protectors against evil spirits targeting women in childbirth, infants, and brides. Small gilded statues placed on bridal bed or cradle.

Limit: Protection is local and requires offerings; not universal.

Accounts I
The Attendants of the Queen Mother of the West (Xiwangmu's Jinnü)General Daoist and folk hagiography; e.g., 'Xiwangmu baojuan' (Precious Scroll of the Queen Mother) and Ming novel 'Journey to the West' (Xiyou Ji) attendant lists

Ming-Qing (14th-19th century) with earlier roots in Han dynasty Xiwangmu cult

In the celestial court of Xiwangmu on Kunlun Mountain, hosts of jade maidens (yunü) and golden women (jinnü) serve as attendants, fan-bearers, and messengers. They announce visitors, present peaches of immortality, and escort worthy mortals. While no passage names Yinchou Jinnü specifically, this is the canonical narrative habitat for any Jinnü figure. The story establishes Jinnü as bureaucratic functionaries in heaven, not independent protagonists. Codifies Jinnü as a rank in heavenly bureaucracy; explains why many local Jinnü temples are side-shrines to Xiwangmu temples. Provides iconographic template.

Zhinü and the Weaving Jinnü HelpersQixi festival lore; 'Soushen Ji' (In Search of the Supernatural, 4th c.) and later folk explanations of weaving taboos

Six Dynasties to Qing

Zhinü, the Weaver Girl (Vega), is aided by lesser weaving spirits called Jinnü who tend the celestial loom when she is separated from Niulang (Cowherd). In some local tellings, a 'Golden Woman' teaches village girls to weave at night, disappearing at cockcrow. This etiological tale explains why girls pray to Jinnü for skill on Qixi (Double Seventh). Link to Yinchou Jinnü is unverified but thematically plausible if her domain is craft. Ritual: girls offer needles, thread, and melons to Jinnü on Qixi; taboo against weaving on that night.

The Silver-Ugly and Golden-Beautiful Sisters (Reconstructed Etiological Tale)Reconstructed tale type; no primary source for Yinchou Jinnü located. Parallels in 'Liaozhai Zhiyi' (Strange Tales) and moral tales about appearance vs virtue

Qing didactic literature (if exists)

A hypothesized local legend to explain the compound name: Two sisters, one called Silver-Ugly (humble, plain, silver ornaments) and one Golden-Beautiful (adorned in gold), compete for marriage or divine favor. The ugly sister's kindness and diligence win the affection of a deity or magistrate, revealing inner gold. The story would function as moral instruction for brides. This narrative is COMMON for paired goddesses but NOT VERIFIED as attached to Yinchou Jinnü; included as reconstructed possibility flagged in gaps. Would explain paired statues in some women's temples; caution: may be modern folk etymology imposed on database entry.

The Temple Foundation Legend (Generic Local Miaozhi Pattern)Pattern from Fujian and Guangdong temple gazetteers (miaozhi) - e.g., structure seen in Tamsui and Quanzhou Jinnü shrines

Qing to Republican era (19th-early 20th century)

A typical foundation story: A village woman known for virtue, healing, or tragic death (often a midwife or weaver who died saving a child) is deified as Jinnü after miracles at her grave - lights, fragrance, cures for barren women. Elders build a small shrine; she is given the honorific Jin nü. If Yinchou Jinnü follows this pattern, she would be a deified historical woman whose personal name included Yin or Chou, later prefixed with Jin as honorific. No specific gazetteer for Yinchou Jinnü was located; this is the expected narrative type if primary sources are found. Creates pilgrimage site; annual festival on her death anniversary; incorporation into larger goddess network.

The Precious Scroll of the Golden Woman (Baojuan Literature)Baojuan (precious scrolls) genre - e.g., 'Jin Gu Baojuan' types catalogued by Overmyer and Che Xilun

Ming-Qing baojuan (16th-19th century)

In baojuan performed by blind singers or nuns, a Jinnü figure narrates her own suffering, cultivation, and salvation - often a woman who endured patriarchal abuse, practiced vegetarian piety, and achieved divine status. The scroll alternates prose and verse, ending with admonitions to women to be pious. Several Jin Gu / Jin Nü baojuan are catalogued, but none with title Yinchou Jinnü was verified in English bibliography. This is the most likely primary source type where Yinchou Jinnü would appear if she is a sectarian goddess. Performance creates female religious community; scroll copied and circulated as merit-making.
