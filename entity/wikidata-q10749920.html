The Approach

In the shadowed groves of ancient Japan, where every rustle of leaves might be a whisper from the divine, there dwells a god whose very being is the tree itself. Kukunochi, the tree kami, stands as a silent sentinel of the forest, his roots entwined with the origins of the land and his branches reaching toward the heavens. His story, though sparse in the annals of myth, weaves through the fabric of Shinto belief, reminding us that the sacred is not always loud or grand, but often found in the quiet, enduring presence of nature.

Content advisory. None. This figure is a benign nature deity; no dark material is associated with him in the available sources.
Identity

Kukunochi

Pronunciation Koo-koo-no-chee

Known As

Kuku-no-KamiKukunochi no KamiKukuno-uchi no KamiKukunochi-no-Kami

Etymology

The name is generally interpreted as 'tree lord' or 'spirit of the trees.' The element 'kuku' is thought to relate to 'tree' (ki) or 'to grow,' and 'nochi' or 'nushi' means 'lord' or 'master.' Some scholars suggest a connection to 'kukuri,' meaning 'to bind,' possibly referencing the binding of tree spirits.

Cultural Ownership

Japanese (Shinto)

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars have equated Kukunochi with other tree kami such as Kaya-no-hime (goddess of grasses) or the generic concept of kodama (tree spirits), but these are speculative. In the Kojiki, Kukunochi is distinct from these entities, though his role is similarly tied to vegetation.

Description

Appearance

No visual depictions of Kukunochi are recorded in ancient texts. In later art, he is sometimes depicted as a male deity with a tree-like appearance, but this is speculative. In Shinto, kami are often not depicted in human form, and Kukunochi is likely venerated as an abstract presence within trees.

Symbols & Iconography

Trees, especially ancient and large ones, are the primary symbol of Kukunochi. In Shinto, sacred trees (shinboku) are often adorned with shimenawa (sacred straw ropes) to mark their divine nature, and these may be associated with Kukunochi.

Behaviour & Personality

As a nature deity, Kukunochi is not characterized with a distinct personality in the myths. He is a passive figure in the creation narrative, representing the essence of trees. In folk belief, tree spirits are often seen as protective but also capricious, though this is not directly attributed to Kukunochi.

Domains & Habitats

Kukunochi is the kami of trees, and his domain is the forest. He is associated with all types of trees, but especially with evergreens and ancient trees that are considered sacred in Shinto.

Version Differences

In the Kojiki, Kukunochi is born after the mountain deity, while in the Nihon Shoki, the order differs. Some versions of the myth may list him as the offspring of Izanagi alone (after Izanami's death), but this is not consistent.

Powers & Abilities

Control over trees

As the deity of trees, Kukunochi is believed to have dominion over the growth and health of trees. This is inferred from his role as a nature kami, though no specific myths demonstrate this power.

No specific limitations are recorded.

Blessing of fertility

Source Version Folk tradition

Description

In some agricultural contexts, tree kami are invoked for fertility of crops, as trees are associated with life and growth. This is a general folk belief, not specifically attributed to Kukunochi.

Limitations

Not directly attested in ancient texts.

Contradictions

Some scholars argue that Kukunochi's role is purely mythological and not part of active worship.

Stories & Myths

Birth of the Deities

Source Kojiki and Nihon ShokiCulture JapanesePeriod Mythic (Age of the Gods)

Summary

After Izanagi and Izanami created the islands of Japan, they gave birth to numerous deities representing natural phenomena. Among these were the mountain deity, the tree deity Kukunochi, and the deity of grasses, Kaya-no-hime. Kukunochi's birth is mentioned in passing, with no further narrative.

Participants

Izanagi

Izanami

Kukunochi

Kaya-no-hime

Consequences

Kukunochi's birth establishes the divine nature of trees, reinforcing the Shinto reverence for nature.

Variants

The order of birth differs between the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki, and the Nihon Shoki also lists Kukunochi as being born from Izanagi's urine or excrement in some passages, but this is not commonly emphasized.

Relationships

Izanagi

Type fatherSource Kojiki, Nihon Shoki

Description

Izanagi is the male creator deity who, along with Izanami, gave birth to the islands and many kami.

Version Note

In all versions, Izanagi is the father.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Izanami

Type motherSource Kojiki, Nihon Shoki

Description

Izanami is the female creator deity; she dies giving birth to the fire deity, but Kukunochi is born before that.

Version Note

In the Kojiki, Izanami is alive when Kukunochi is born; in some Nihon Shoki passages, she may be dead, but this is unclear.

Conflicting Accounts

The Nihon Shoki has an alternate version where Kukunochi is born from Izanagi's excrement, thus not from Izanami.

Kaya-no-hime

Type siblingSource Kojiki

Description

Kaya-no-hime is the goddess of grasses, born alongside Kukunochi, representing the complementary vegetation.

Version Note

In the Kojiki, they are siblings; in some Nihon Shoki versions, Kaya-no-hime is born from Izanagi's urine, making her a different kind of sibling.

Conflicting Accounts

The exact sibling relationship is consistent in the Kojiki, but the Nihon Shoki has variant birth stories.

Mountain deity (Yama-tsu-mi)

Type siblingSource Kojiki

Description

The mountain deity is born before Kukunochi, and together they represent the landscape.

Version Note

Order of birth varies.

Contradictions & Disputes

Birth order

Position A

Kukunochi is born after the mountain deity and before the grass deity.

Position A — evidence

Kojiki, Book I, Section 14

Position B

Kukunochi is born after the grass deity in some versions.

Position B — evidence

Nihon Shoki, Book I, Section 6 (alternate ordering)

Scholarly Consensus

The order is not considered significant; both texts are canonical, and the discrepancy is attributed to differing editorial traditions.

Parentage

Position A

Kukunochi is the offspring of Izanagi and Izanami.

Position A — evidence

Kojiki and most Nihon Shoki passages

Position B

Kukunochi is born from Izanagi's excrement.

Position B — evidence

A variant passage in the Nihon Shoki

Scholarly Consensus

The majority tradition holds the first view; the excrement birth is a later, less prominent variant, possibly a euphemism for natural processes.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

There is a lack of detailed scholarly studies dedicated solely to Kukunochi. Most information comes from passing mentions in creation myths. The exact nature of his worship in ancient times is poorly documented, and there are no known archaeological findings directly linked to him. His iconography is virtually nonexistent. The relationship between Kukunochi and other tree spirits (e.g., kodama) is under-researched. The variants in birth stories between the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki have not been exhaustively analyzed. Future research should examine Japanese-language sources, including shrine records and folkloric studies, to provide a more complete picture.

Research Scope

This dossier compiles all available information on Kukunochi from primary Shinto texts (Kojiki, Nihon Shoki), scholarly works on Japanese mythology, and academic databases. Due to the scarcity of dedicated studies, much of the description is derived from his brief appearances in origin myths. The research cutoff is August 2026.
