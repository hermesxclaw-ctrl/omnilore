Who It Is

Two girls share one reading — Shōkei. One is a twelve-year-old princess who watches her kingdom burn and must learn to live without a palace; the other is a two-character stamp — 正慶 — pressed onto the calendar for only thirteen months while two emperors claimed the same throne and Japan split into North and South. The same sound, different kanji, different worlds, and centuries of readers have tangled them together.

Historical: No personality; the era's 'behavior' is its political function — a Northern Court instrument to mark legitimacy after the Genkō War. Court behavior around it: rapid proclamation, dual dating confusion (documents from same month dated Genkō 2 vs Shōkyō 1 depending on allegiance). Fictional: Initially spoiled, sheltered, and arrogant — raised as beloved only daughter of King Shukou, ignorant of Hou's famine and misgovernment. Powers
Nengō Legitimation (Historical Institution)

The primary 'power' of an era name is to legitimize sovereignty by controlling time-reckoning. Proclaiming Shōkyō asserted Kōgon's status as emperor and Ashikaga Takauji's bakufu as the temporal authority, requiring officials, temples, and vassals to date documents accordingly. Effectiveness depended on military control, not metaphysics.

Limit: Power was purely political and geographically limited; Southern Court and loyalist temples rejected it, continuing Genkō. Lasted only 13 months; collapsed with Kamakura bakufu's fall and Kenmu Restoration. Modern historiography treats it as illegitimate per 1911 decree.

Calendrical Authority (Historical)

Shōkyō functioned as the civil calendar for taxation, ritual, and record-keeping in Northern Court territory, synchronizing lunar months, sexagenary cycle, and court festivals.

Limit: Dual dating created confusion; merchants and temples in contested zones sometimes double-dated. Conversion to Julian/Gregorian requires specialist tables; month lengths varied.

Royal Status — Former Princess of Hou (Fictional)

As daughter of King Shukou of Hou, Shoukei held highest court rank, with palace education, etiquette, poetry, and political immunity. Status was not supernatural; it conferred deference and material privilege while the kingdom stood.

Limit: Status void after Hou's fall; in Kei she is a stateless refugee with no legal protection, subject to exploitation. She has no combat, magical, or kirin-related powers.

Literacy and Court Education (Fictional)

Shoukei is literate in classical letters, court ritual, and administration — skills that later allow her to serve as a palace attendant and to understand Yoko's reforms. Demonstrated when she critiques Kei's bureaucracy from a princess's perspective.

Limit: Education is theoretical; she lacks practical survival skills, initially unable to cook, clean, or earn wages, leading to destitution.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Historical era: Not a person; its 'appearance' is textual and calendrical. Written vertically as 正慶 with seal script and kaisho variants on wooden edicts (senji), coinage marginalia, and temple dedication inscriptions dated 'Shōkyō gannen' or 'Shōkyō 1'. Surviving documents show the nengō placed before date, e.g., '正慶元年四月' (Shōkyō 1, 4th month). No distinctive iconography beyond standard nengō calligraphy; color and material follow generic Kamaku

Symbols — Historical: 正慶's two kanji themselves are the symbol — 正 (correct/righteous) evokes rectification of rule, 慶 (celebration) evokes felicity. Era names function as political symbols: proclaiming Shōkyō asserted that Kōgon's court in Kyoto controlled time itself, versus Go-Daigo's Genkō (元弘) in Yoshino. Temple bells and shrine votives dated by nengō reinforced this claim. Fictional: Shoukei's symbols are Hou's kingdom emblem

Domains — Historical: Domain is calendrical and territorial — used in Kyoto and Ashikaga-controlled central Japan (Kinai, parts of Kantō) from April 1332 to April 1333 (13 months). Not used in Yoshino Southern Court, which continued Genkō 2–3. Habitat is administrative: court edicts, bakufu correspondence, temple records, and later historiography. Accounts I
The Genkō War and Go-Daigo's Exile — Prelude to ShōkyōTaiheiki Books 5–6; Jinnō Shōtōki (Kitabatake Chikafusa, 1339–1343); Varley translation

1331–1332 (Genkō 1–2)

Emperor Go-Daigo, seeking to overthrow the Kamakura shogunate and restore direct imperial rule, raises loyalist forces with Prince Moriyoshi and Kusunoki Masashige. The bakufu crushes the initial uprising, burns Kasagi and Yoshino strongholds, captures Go-Daigo, and exiles him to Oki Island (March 1332). With Go-Daigo removed, the bakufu and court nobles enthrone his cousin Prince Kazuhito as Emperor Kōgon in Kyoto to maintain ritual continuity. Creates the institutional vacuum that requires a new Northern nengō; Kōgon's court needs to mark time independently from the exiled emperor's Genkō. Sets stage for Shōkyō proclamation.

Proclamation of Shōkyō — The Northern Court Claims TimeCourt edict records abstracted in Nussbaum (2005) p.878; Titsingh (1834) pp.282–283; National Diet Library Japanese Calendar overview

Genkō 2, 10th month (lunar) = April 1332 Julian; Shōkyō gannen

With Kōgon enthroned in Kyoto under Hōjō and Ashikaga protection, the court performs kaigen (era change) to mark the new reign. The previous era Genkō is terminated in the 10th month; the new era name 正慶 (Shōkyō/Shōkei) is promulgated. Documents in Kyoto and Kamakura switch dating to Shōkyō 1, while Go-Daigo's loyalists in Oki and Yoshino continue to date by Genkō 2, creating a split calendar that physically manifests the schism. For 13 months Japan has two concurrent year counts. Shōkyō becomes the bureaucratic signature of Northern allegiance; its use or rejection signals political loyalty. Later historians use the dual system to untangle chronology.

The Fall of Hou — Shoukei's Kingdom Collapses (Fictional Backstory)Ono Fuyumi, Kaze no Banri, Reimei no Sora, Vol.1 Chapters 1–2 (1994); anime Juuni Kokki episodes 14–15 (2002)

Fictional chronology: ~500 years into King Shukou's reign; narrative present c. early reign of Yoko in Kei

King Shukou of Hou, once wise, becomes tyrannical under the influence of his queen and prolonged rule; taxes, executions, and neglect cause famine. The kingdom's kirin, Hourin, sickens from the king's loss of the Way. Rebels rise; the capital is sacked, the king and kirin are killed (implying loss of Mandate of Heaven), and the palace is burned. Twelve-year-old Princess Shoukei, who had lived in oblivious luxury, escapes with a loyal attendant but is separated, becoming a refugee. Shoukei loses all status, family, and home; her belief that royalty is inherently virtuous shatters. She flees across the Yellow Sea to Kei, where she will live as a commoner. Thematically establishes Twelve Kingdoms' core thesis: kingship is responsibility, not privilege.

Accounts II
Refugee in Kei — Shoukei's Exile and DegradationOno Fuyumi, Kaze no Banri, Reimei no Sora, Vol.1 Chapters 3–5; anime episodes 16–19

Fictional: months following Hou's fall; parallel to Yoko's first year as Queen of Kei

Alone in Kei, Shoukei has no registry, money, or skills. She begs, steals, and takes menial work in inns in Meikaku, enduring abuse from employers and customers who exploit a stateless minor. She clings to pride in her former rank, despising Kei and its new, clumsy ruler Yoko, whom she sees as another false monarch. She is taken in briefly by a kind innkeeper, then exploited again, learning that commoners' suffering she once ignored is real. Shoukei's resentment deepens into nihilism; she attempts to assassinate Yoko at one point in her despair, believing all rulers are corrupt. Failure forces self-reflection. Sets up redemption arc.

Encounter with Yoko — Judgment and ServiceOno Fuyumi, Kaze no Banri, Reimei no Sora, Vol.2 Chapters 6–10; anime episodes 20–26

Fictional: Yoko's first year, Kei court at Gyouten

Shoukei, caught after a failed outburst against the Kei palace, is brought before Queen Yoko. Expecting execution, she instead receives a stern, compassionate interrogation: Yoko, herself a former lost girl from another world, recognizes Shoukei's grief and pride. Rather than punish, Yoko offers her a position as a palace attendant (chōsha) to learn governance from below. Shoukei witnesses Yoko's struggles to reform Kei's corrupt bureaucracy and begins to understand the burden of kingship. Shoukei accepts service, beginning moral reformation; she becomes a viewpoint character for Yoko's Kenmu-like restoration in Kei, paralleling historical Go-Daigo's idealism. Her arc resolves from entitled princess to accountable subject, later entrusted with responsibilities.

The Collapse of Shōkyō — Kenmu Restoration and Dual Courts FormalizedTaiheiki Books 7–9; Goble, Kenmu: Go-Daigo's Revolution (1996); Titsingh pp.285–287

Shōkyō 2 / Genkō 3, 3rd–6th month 1333

While Kyoto dates by Shōkyō 2, Go-Daigo escapes Oki (March 1333), rallies loyalists, and Ashikaga Takauji defects from the bakufu to the imperial cause. Nitta Yoshisada destroys Kamakura (May 1333). The Northern Court collapses; Kōgon is deposed, Shōkyō is abolished, and Go-Daigo declares the Kenmu Restoration, reuniting era count briefly under Kenmu (1334–1336) before Takauji turns against Go-Daigo and re-establishes a new Northern Court (Kōmyō) with era Shōkei? No, next Northern era is Kenmu as well, then Ryakuō — the split hardens into the 57-year Nanboku-chō period. Shōkyō ends after ~13 months, shortest Northern nengō of the period. Its abolition marks the failure of the first Ashikaga-backed court experiment, but the template recurs. Later Meiji historiography retroactively labels Shōkyō illegitimate, while pre-Meiji chronologies had listed Kōgon as 101st emperor.

Shōkyō was a long, stable era like Heisei or Edo. — Shōkyō lasted only about 13 months (April 1332–April 1333) and was geographically limited to Northern Court control. It is one of the shortest nengō and was abolished with the Kenmu Restoration.

Shoukei (Twelve Kingdoms) is a magical girl with kirin powers. — Shoukei is fully human with no supernatural abilities. She is a former princess whose only 'power' was status, now lost. Kirin powers belong to Hourin (Hou) and Keiki (Kei), not to her.

The Northern Court possessed the Imperial Regalia during Shōkyō, proving its legitimacy. — The Regalia (mirror, sword, jewel) were with Go-Daigo in exile and later in Yoshino; the Northern Court under Kōgon did not possess them, a key argument for Southern legitimacy cited since the 14th century and affirmed in 1911.

Shōkei and Shōkyō are two different eras. — They are variant readings of the same two kanji 正慶 for the same 1332–1333 period. No separate Shōkei era exists in official nengō lists.
