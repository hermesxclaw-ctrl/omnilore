Who It Is

She was the queen of heaven, the guardian of Roman women, and the most terrifying enemy the Trojans ever faced. Juno was a goddess of contradictions: a protector who could be a persecutor, a wife who could be a warrior, a counselor whose name is stamped on every coin in your pocket. She was the divine engine of Rome's greatest epic, and the silent patron of every Roman mother. Her story is a tale of sovereignty, spite, and the price of a grudge held for a thousand years.

In her native Roman cult, Juno was a protector and guide, especially for women. She oversaw the transition of a girl into womanhood, marriage, and childbirth. As a state goddess, she was a guardian of the Roman people and their sovereignty. The literary Juno of Virgil and Ovid, however, is a fierce, unforgiving, and deeply emotional goddess, who holds grudges and manipulates events to punish her enemies, particularly the Trojans and their descendants (the Romans).

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The etymology of the name 'Juno' (Latin: Iuno) is disputed. Ancient Romans connected it with the verb iuvare, meaning 'to help, benefit, or aid,' making her the 'helpful' or 'vitalizing' goddess. Modern linguists have proposed a derivation from a root meaning 'young' (iun-), connecting her to youth

Pronunciation — JOO-noh (English), YOO-noh (Classical Latin: Iuno)

Aliases — Juno Regina, Juno Lucina, Juno Sospita, Juno Moneta, Juno Caprotina, Juno Curitis

Ownership — Roman, with significant Etruscan (as Uni) and Sabine influences. Later syncretized with the Greek goddess Hera, though her native Roman character remained distinct and more complex

Powers
Sovereignty and State Protection

As Juno Regina ('Queen'), she granted and protected the sovereignty of Rome and other cities. She could be ritually 'evoked' (evocatio) from a besieged city, transferring her favor to Rome.

Limit: Her favor was not absolute; it required proper ritual observance and could be lost if the state became impious.

Protection of Women and Childbirth

As Juno Lucina, she brought children into the light (lux). She was invoked by women in labor and oversaw the rites of passage for Roman women. She was also the goddess of marriage (Iuga).

Limit: Her protection was focused on legitimate, Roman, married women and their offspring.

Counsel and Warning

As Juno Moneta ('the Warner'), she gave advice and warnings to the state. Her temple was the site of the mint, and she was associated with the financial and advisory health of Rome.

Limit: Her counsel was often cryptic, requiring interpretation by priests and magistrates.

Divine Wrath and Vengeance

In the literary tradition, Juno possesses immense power to stir up storms, command lesser deities, incite wars, and torment individuals, all to satisfy her personal grudges.

Limit: Even in the Aeneid, she cannot ultimately change the decrees of Fate, only delay and complicate them. She must eventually yield to Jupiter's will.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In Roman art, Juno is depicted as a matronly, dignified woman, similar to Hera. She is often shown veiled (capite velato), wearing a diadem or crown, and holding a scepter. She may be accompanied by a peacock, her sacred bird. In her aspect as Juno Sospita (the Savior), she is depicted in a more warlike manner, wearing a goatskin cloak (aegis) and carrying a spear and shield. As Juno Lucina, she may hold a child or a flower.

Symbols — The peacock, the scepter, the diadem, the veil, the goatskin (aegis), the spear, the shield, the lotus flower, and the cow (as a symbol of fertility). Her sacred month was June, which is named after her.

Domains — Marriage, women, childbirth, and the state. She was also a goddess of sovereignty, counsel (Juno Moneta, the 'Warner'), and light (Juno Lucina). She was the tutelary goddess of Rome and the Roman people, and the patron of the month of June.

Accounts I
The Evocatio of Juno from VeiiLivy, Ab Urbe Condita, Book 5

c. 396 BC (event), c. 27-9 BC (written)

During the Roman siege of the Etruscan city of Veii, the Roman dictator Camillus performed the evocatio, a ritual prayer to the city's tutelary goddess, Juno, promising her a magnificent temple in Rome if she would abandon Veii and come to the Romans. The city fell, and the statue of Juno was ritually transported to Rome, where it was installed in a new temple on the Aventine Hill. This ritual established the Roman practice of 'adopting' foreign gods, which was seen as a source of Roman power. It also cemented Juno's role as a goddess of Roman sovereignty and expansion.

Accounts II
The Wrath of Juno in the AeneidVirgil, Aeneid

c. 29-19 BC

Juno, remembering the judgment of Paris (where she was scorned) and her love for Carthage, which she knows is fated to be destroyed by Rome, relentlessly opposes Aeneas and the Trojan refugees. She stirs up a storm to wreck their fleet, incites Queen Dido to fall in love with Aeneas (only to be abandoned), and finally brings the goddess Allecto to earth to start a war between the Trojans and the Latins. Juno's wrath is the engine of the entire epic, creating the hardships that forge Aeneas into the founder of the Roman race. In the end, Jupiter persuades her to relent, with the promise that the Romans will absorb the Italian peoples and honor her above all others.

The Geese of JunoLivy, Ab Urbe Condita, Book 5 (and later sources)

c. 390 BC (event)

During the Gallic sack of Rome, the Gauls attempted to climb the Capitoline Hill by night. The sacred geese of Juno, kept in her temple on the Capitoline, heard the noise and began to cackle, waking the Roman defender Marcus Manlius and saving the citadel. The geese were honored for their service. The event became a legendary example of divine protection of Rome. The geese were thereafter held in high honor, and a dog was annually crucified, as punishment for not barking.

Juno is just the Roman version of Hera, and they are exactly the same. — While syncretized in literature, Juno had a distinct, native Roman character as a goddess of sovereignty, female life-force, and civic protection, which was not a primary aspect of Hera.

Juno was always the jealous, nagging wife of Jupiter. — This is a literary trope, primarily from Virgil and Ovid. In Roman cult, her relationship with Jupiter was more about shared sovereignty, and her role as a protector of women and the state was far more important.

The word 'money' comes from Juno's name because she was the goddess of wealth. — Money comes from 'Moneta,' her title as the 'Warner' or 'Advisor.' Her temple housed the mint, so the coins minted there were called 'moneta,' which became the word for money.

Juno was a virgin goddess. — Juno was the goddess of marriage and childbirth, and the mother of Mars and Juventas. She was the opposite of a virgin goddess.
