Form

No surviving textual description of Allanzu's physical appearance exists. Iconographic evidence is also ambiguous; she is not clearly identifiable in any known relief with certainty. She was a youthful goddess, suggesting she may have been depicted as a young woman, but this is inference. In Yazılıkaya, a goddess behind Ḫepat is sometimes identified as Allanzu, but this is debated. In the broader context of Hurrian-Hittite religious art, goddesses of her station were typically depicted wearing long pleated robes, a cylindrical polos headdress, and occasionally holding a mirror or a ritual vessel. The Yazılıkaya rock sanctuary near Ḫattuša—a monumental open-air temple carved into limestone cliffs—contains a procession of goddesses that some scholars interpret as the divine family of Ḫepat. If the identification of a young goddess in this procession as Allanzu is correct, she would be shown with her hands raised in a gesture of adoration, following closely behind her mother Ḫepat and preceding her brother Šarruma, who stands atop a panther.

Accounts

In this myth, the storm god Tarḫunna (Teššub) is challenged by the stone monster Ulikummi. In a desperate moment, Tarḫunna goes to Ḫepat for advice. She is described as being in a state of distress, and her children, including Allanzu, are present. Allanzu does not speak or act; her role is purely to be part of the divine family scene.

Allanzu is invoked in a variety of rituals, often alongside her mother Ḫepat and other deities. These rituals are primarily for purification, healing, or protection. She is listed among the deities who are called upon to witness or bless the ritual.

The rock sanctuary at Yazılıkaya, located two kilometers northeast of the Hittite capital Ḫattuša, contains two rock-cut chambers decorated with reliefs of nearly one hundred deities. Chamber A depicts a grand procession of gods and goddesses converging toward a central meeting scene between the storm-god Teššub and the sun-goddess Ḫepat. Among the goddesses following Ḫepat, a youthful female figure has been tentatively identified as Allanzu. If this identification is correct, it represents one of the only known visual depictions of this elusive deity and situates her cult firmly within the imperial religious program of the late Hittite Empire. The sanctuary was likely used for the celebration of the New Year festival and for royal ancestor veneration, connecting Allanzu to both cosmic renewal and dynastic legitimacy.

What We Don't Know

Significant gaps remain. The etymology of her name is unknown. Her specific cult center, if any, is not identified. Her iconography is ambiguous. The exact nature of her role (beyond being a daughter of Ḫepat) is unclear. The reason for the name change to Alasuwa is not fully understood. No primary-source images or detailed descriptions of her are available. Her potential syncretism with other goddesses is not fully explored.

A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine a royal family portrait where the youngest daughter is always standing just behind her mother, half in the shadows. She's in the picture, but you can't quite make out her face, and no one ever wrote down her name next to her image. That's Allanzu — a member of the divine household whose role is to be present, not to be the star. She's the 'and her daughter' in the divine genealogy, the footnote in the myth, the name on the ritual list that scholars have to squint to read.
