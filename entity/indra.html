Who It Is

Indra is the king of the devas (gods) and the lord of Svarga (heaven) in Hindu mythology. In the Rigveda, the oldest of the sacred Vedas, he is the supreme deity—celebrated in approximately 250 hymns, more than any other god. He is described as a golden-skinned warrior of immense stature who rides the divine white elephant Airavata, wields the thunderbolt weapon Vajra (forged from the bones of the sage Dadhichi), and drinks the intoxicating ritual beverage Soma, which fuels his superhuman strength. Indra's most celebrated myth is his defeat of Vritra, a serpentine demon (asura) who had hoarded all the world's waters within his coils, causing a cosmic drought. Armed with the Vajra and fortified by Soma, Indra slew Vritra, releasing the life-giving waters and restoring the cosmic order (rita). This act is simultaneously a creation myth, a fertility myth, and a paradigm of the Indo-European dragon-slaying hero archetype (cognate with Thor vs. Jörmungandr, Zeus vs. Typhon). Indra's character in the early Vedas is robustly heroic—passionate, boastful, sometimes morally ambiguous, and driven by both noble and personal motivations. However, in later Puranic Hinduism (c. 300–1000 CE), Indra's status declined dramatically. He was demoted from supreme deity to a regional king of the heavens, often portrayed as insecure, fearful of ascetics whose tapas (spiritual heat) might threaten his throne, and prone to sending celestial nymphs (apsaras) to disrupt their meditations. Despite this diminution, Indra remains a directional guardian (dikpala) of the east, and his name endures as the archetype of divine kingship throughout South and Southeast Asian cultures.

Form

Attested descriptions present Indra as a vigorous, heroic figure, often compared to a bull or a mighty warrior. In the Ṛgveda, he is frequently portrayed as large-bodied, with powerful arms, golden or shining in form, and radiant like the sun. He rides the white, many-tusked elephant Airāvata (later tradition) or a chariot drawn by bay or tawny horses, and he wields the vajra (thunderbolt) as his primary weapon. He is adorned with ornaments, sometimes described as golden, and is associated with a resplendent, royal presence befitting the king of the gods. Later iconography in Hindu art typically shows him with four arms, holding the vajra, a lotus, a conch, and sometimes a bow, seated or standing near Airāvata, with a crown and royal garments. These visual conventions are adaptation-specific, while the core Vedic image emphasizes his muscular, storm-like vigor and luminous power.

Powers

Vajra (thunderbolt) wielding Indra’s defining power is the wielding of the vajra, a thunderbolt weapon capable of shattering mountains, killing dragons (Vṛtra, Ahi), and breaking open the clouds to release waters. In the Ṛgveda, the vajra is sharpened by Tvaṣṭṛ and used to strike Vṛtra, freeing the rivers. Later texts describe the vajra as forged from the bones of the sage Dadhīci, adding a sacrificial dimension. Ṛgveda (e.g., RV 1.32, 1.80–1.84); Brāhmaṇas; Purāṇas Indra’s use of the vajra is often contingent on ritual support and soma-drinking; he requires the cooperation of priests and the performance of sacrifice to attain full strength. In some narratives, he hesitates or fears powerful opponents until properly empowered. While early Vedic hymns emphasize the vajra as a purely divine weapon, later Purāṇic stories introduce the Dadhīci bone origin, creating a layered tradition. Some scholars read the vajra symbolically as a meteorological or ritual force rather than a literal weapon.

Liberation of waters and control of rain Indra is repeatedly praised as the god who frees the pent-up waters, breaks dams, and causes rivers to flow and rain to fall. His battles against Vṛtra and Ahi are mythic expressions of this power. He is invoked for monsoon rains, agricultural fertility, and the removal of drought. Ṛgveda (e.g., RV 1.32, 2.11, 3.30); later ritual texts and Purāṇas Indra’s control of rain is not absolute; later narratives show him being overruled by Kṛṣṇa (Govardhana episode), and some traditions emphasize that higher deities or dharmic conduct ultimately regulate cosmic forces. Meteorological interpretations treat Indra as a personification of atmospheric processes, while devotional traditions maintain a more literal divine agency. Modern scholarship often reads the myth as multi-layered—meteorological, social, and spiritual.

Martial prowess and protection in battle Indra is invoked as a battle deity who grants victory, destroys enemies, and protects the sacrificial community. Hymns describe him killing ninety-nine Vṛtras, smashing fortresses, and scattering foes. He is a patron of warriors and kings. Ṛgveda (e.g., RV 1.80–1.84, 2.11); Brāhmaṇas; epic references His protection is conditional on proper worship and offerings; hymns frequently stress that those who neglect sacrifice or fail to honor him may be deprived of his aid or even punished. Later Hinduism shifts martial supremacy toward other deities (e.g., Skanda/Kārttikeya, Durgā), and Indra’s role as supreme war-god becomes more limited, creating tension between early Vedic and later devotional hierarchies.

Kingship of the gods (Svarga sovereignty) Indra rules Svarga and presides over the assembly of Devas. He commands celestial armies, oversees the distribution of rain and wealth, and maintains cosmic order in the heavenly realm. Ṛgveda; Brāhmaṇas; Mahābhārata; Purāṇas; Buddhist texts (as Sakka) His kingship is not absolute or eternal; he can be challenged, humbled, or temporarily displaced. Some Purāṇic traditions speak of multiple Indras over cosmic cycles, suggesting that the office of Indra is a role rather than a unique, unchanging individual. The rise of the Trimūrti and bhakti traditions re-centers ultimate sovereignty in Viṣṇu or Śiva, leading to theological debates about Indra’s status. Buddhist and Jain cosmologies further relativize his power by placing him below enlightened beings.

Soma-drinking and ecstatic empowerment Indra’s strength is repeatedly linked to his consumption of soma, the ritual drink. Hymns describe him drinking vast quantities, becoming energized, and then performing great feats such as slaying Vṛtra. Ṛgveda (e.g., RV 1.32, 4.17); Brāhmaṇas His dependence on soma can be read as a limitation: without it, he may lack the power or courage to face certain foes. Soma-drinking also contributes to later portrayals of him as intoxicated and sometimes reckless. Scholars debate whether soma is a literal plant-based drink, a visionary sacrament, or a complex ritual symbol. Indra’s relationship to soma is central to these discussions, but the exact botanical identity of soma remains disputed.

Behavior & Habitat

Indra’s behavior is complex and shifts across textual layers. In the Ṛgveda, he is the paradigmatic warrior: courageous, aggressive toward enemies, fiercely protective of his worshippers, and eager for battle. He drinks large quantities of soma, which both empowers him and marks him as a god of ecstatic intoxication. He is generous to those who perform sacrifice, granting cattle, wealth, and victory, and he is praised as a friend of the Arya communities. In Brāhmaṇa and epic literature, his personality acquires more human-like flaws: he can be jealous of rivals, fearful of losing status, prone to lust (as in the Ahalyā episode), and sometimes deceitful. Purāṇic narratives emphasize his pride and subsequent humiliation, turning him into a cautionary figure whose arrogance is corrected by higher deities. In Buddhist and Jain texts, Sakka/Indra is more restrained, often depicted as respectful toward enlightened beings. Overall, Indra is energetic, impulsive, and deeply tied to the dynamics of power, victory, and the maintenance (or disruption) of cosmic and social order.

Accounts

In the foundational Vedic myth, Indra, empowered by soma and armed with the vajra, confronts the dragon or serpent Vṛtra, who has blocked the rivers and hoarded the waters. Indra strikes Vṛtra on the mountains, shattering his body and releasing the pent-up waters, which rush forth like cows freed from a pen. This act restores fertility, light, and cosmic order (ṛta). Later texts add details: Tvaṣṭṛ forges the vajra; the sage Dadhīci sacrifices his bones to create the weapon; and the battle becomes a paradigm for the triumph of dharma over obstruction.

Some hymns describe Indra not merely killing a single Vṛtra but striking down ninety-nine Vṛtras, extending the myth from a singular cosmic battle to a repeated pattern of overcoming multiple obstructions. This amplifies his role as universal battle commander, fighting at every scale of conflict.

Indra, desiring the beautiful Ahalyā, wife of the sage Gautama, disguises himself as Gautama and seduces her. When Gautama discovers the deception, he curses Indra, often by marking his body with a thousand eyes (or other humiliating signs), and curses Ahalyā to become invisible or turned to stone until later redeemed. The story highlights divine lust, deception, and the consequences of violating ascetic households.

When the cowherds of Vraja, following Kṛṣṇa’s counsel, cease their annual worship of Indra and instead honor Govardhana Hill, Indra becomes enraged. He sends torrential rains and storms to destroy them. Kṛṣṇa calmly lifts Govardhana Hill with one hand, sheltering the community. Indra eventually realizes Kṛṣṇa’s superiority, ceases the storm, and offers homage. The story repositions Indra as a proud but limited deity humbled by the supreme Lord.

Arjuna, one of the Pāṇḍava brothers, is described as Indra’s son through Kuntī. Indra aids Arjuna by granting him celestial weapons, hosting him in Svarga, and instructing him in warfare. These episodes reinforce Arjuna’s status as a divinely favored warrior and link human heroism to Indra’s martial power.

A way to hold it in your mind

If you picture the Hindu pantheon as a long-running franchise, Indra is the original lead character—the storm-wielding protagonist who carried the first seasons with dragon-slaying and monsoon-saving arcs. As the series expanded, new fan favorites like Kṛṣṇa and Śiva stole the spotlight, and Indra was rewritten as the proud, sometimes problematic supporting character whose mistakes teach lessons about ego and power. Yet the whole universe of gods, rains, and rivers still rests on the template he set: a cosmos where breaking the dam, freeing the waters, and fighting obstruction—outside and inside—is the central plot.
