Who It Is

Imagine a baby born with a full set of teeth and a tiny, vestigial tail. In the villages of old Poland, this was not a medical curiosity; it was a death sentence, not for the child, but for the community. This child, it was said, possessed two souls — one human, one demonic — and when it died, the demonic soul would reanimate its body, turning it into a strzyga, a ravenous, blood-drinking horror that would crawl from its grave to feast on its own family. This is not a fairy tale for the faint of heart; it is a glimpse into the very real fears that haunted the pre-modern Slavic world, fears so potent that they drove communities to dig up the dead and sever their heads with sickles.

A strzyga is a malevolent and predatory being. It is driven by a hunger for blood and, in some accounts, for human flesh. It is known to attack its own family first, often appearing to them in the night or in dreams. It is cunning and patient, but also driven by a primal, bestial rage. It is not merely a mindless monster; it retains a semblance of its former personality, which it uses to lure victims. Powers
Dual Soul (Anima and Spiritus)

The strzyga is born with two souls. This is the source of its power and its curse. It is this second soul that allows it to rise from the dead and continue its existence as an undead. It is a state of being that is both a gift and a curse, as it is never truly at peace.

Limit: The second soul is also a point of vulnerability. Some rituals focus on separating or destroying this second soul to kill the strzyga for good.

Blood Drinking

The strzyga primarily feeds on the blood of the living. It is especially drawn to its own relatives, as their blood is 'closest' to it. This is a violation of family bonds and a perversion of the social order.

Limit: It must feed regularly to maintain its form. If it cannot feed, it may weaken.

Spectral Form / Shape-shifting

The strzyga can leave its grave in a spectral or shadowy form. It is often said to be able to transform into a bird (especially an owl or a crow), a moth, or a mist. This allows it to travel unseen and enter homes through keyholes or cracks.

Limit: It is bound to its physical body, which must remain in the grave. If the body is destroyed, the strzyga is destroyed.

Supernatural Strength & Tenacity

When encountered in a physical form, the strzyga is said to possess immense strength and is incredibly difficult to kill. It is resistant to normal weapons and can only be truly destroyed by specific rituals.

Limit: Vulnerable to specific counters: decapitation with a sickle, staking, burning, or being buried face-down.

Compulsive Counting

Like many Slavic undead, the strzyga has a compulsion to count small objects, such as poppy seeds, sand grains, or lentils. Scattering these in its path or in its grave can delay it until dawn, when it must retreat.

Limit: This is a distraction, not a permanent solution. It is a common motif across many vampire traditions.

Some accounts describe it as having a pale, gaunt face, long, unkempt hair, and a gaunt, skeletal body. Its eyes are sometimes described as glowing or having an unnatural glint. Symbols — Symbols associated with the strzyga include: the sickle (the tool used to decapitate it), the stake (used to pin it down), the brick or stone (placed in its mouth to prevent biting), the poppy seed (which it is compelled to count, a common motif in Slavic vampire lore), and the rooster (whose crowing signals the end of its night-time reign). The color red, symbolizing blood, and the number two (representing its dual souls) are also significant.

Domains — The strzyga is tied to its grave and the graveyard. After its death, it becomes a pestilence to its local village, rising at night to feed. It is also associated with forests, where it may lurk and ambush travelers. It is said to return to its home, especially if it has unfinished business or a deep grudge against its family. Accounts I
The Strzyga of the VillageCollected in various 19th-century Polish ethnographies (e.g., Kolberg)

19th century (oral tradition, likely much older)

A child is born with teeth and a tail, a clear sign of a strzyga. The parents, fearing the child, may try to baptize it immediately or even abandon it. If the child dies, it rises from its grave as a strzyga. It begins to haunt the village, appearing at night to its grieving mother, trying to suckle her blood. It attacks livestock and, eventually, people. The villagers, realizing what has happened, dig up the grave. They find the body intact, flushed, and with blood on its lips. They cut off the head with a sickle and place it face-down at the feet, or they drive a stake through the heart, or they burn the body. Only then does the village find peace. The destruction of the strzyga restores order and safety to the village. The rituals serve as a public act of community defense against a supernatural threat.

Accounts II
The Strzygoń as a Living WitchRegional Polish folklore

19th century

In this variant, the strzyga (or strzygoń, the male form) is a living person, born with teeth and a tail. They are not inherently evil but are marked by their condition. They are often feared and ostracized. They are believed to be able to project their spirit, which can cause harm. After death, they inevitably become a full-fledged undead strzyga. This variant highlights the social dimension: the living person is a 'monster' in waiting, a source of fear and suspicion. The person lives a life of isolation and fear. Their death is a double tragedy, as it also marks the birth of a monster.

A strzyga is just another word for a vampire. — While it is a vampiric entity, the strzyga has a distinct origin (being born with a second soul, teeth, and a tail) and a stronger connection to witchcraft than the generic 'vampire.' It is a specific West Slavic variant.

Strigas are a type of witch, not an undead creature. — The name is derived from the Latin for 'witch,' and there are 'living' variants, but the most prominent and detailed folklore describes the strzyga as an undead being that rises from the grave to feed on blood.

The strzyga from 'The Witcher' is an accurate representation of the folkloric figure. — Sapkowski's strzyga is a brilliant and tragic adaptation, but it is a modern invention. The folkloric strzyga is a purely malevolent monster, not a cursed princess with a tragic backstory. The Witcher's version adds a layer of sympathy and a 'cure' that doesn't exist in the original folklore.
