Who It Is

The Strzyga (also Striga, Strzygoń for males) is a uniquely West Slavic undead entity — a person born with two hearts and two souls (double-psyche), only one of which passes on at death. The remaining soul animates the corpse, which rises from the grave at night as a blood-drinking, flesh-eating monster. The Strzyga can appear as a owl-like creature or a human with iron teeth, attacking lone travellers in forests and draining sleeping victims in their homes. According to Polish tradition, a child born with teeth or a caul is destined to become a Strzyga unless preventive measures are taken (baptism with bells ringing, burial with a sickle across the throat). The Strzyga can be destroyed only by decapitation, staking, or burning, and even then, the second heart must be pierced. This double-soul concept is unique among European vampire traditions and reflects a sophisticated folk-psychological model: the split self, the person who is literally two people, one of whom dies and one of whom refuses to. The Strzyga legend was documented by Polish chroniclers including Jan Długosz in the 15th century and features in the Polish Romantic literature of Adam Mickiewicz. Related West Slavic entities include the 'Upior' (a broader vampire-ghost) and the 'Wieszczy' (a person destined to become a vampire). The Witcher video games popularized the 'Striga' as a cursed, feral monster born from a stillborn infant combined with a curse — a significant adaptation of the folkloric Strzyga's double-soul concept.

Form

The strzyga is described as having two souls, which is the root of its undead nature. It is often said to be born with teeth (sometimes two rows) and a small tail, which are signs of its demonic nature. Some accounts describe it as having a pale, gaunt face, long, unkempt hair, and a gaunt, skeletal body. Its eyes are sometimes described as glowing or having an unnatural glint. The most recognizable feature is the presence of an 'extra' soul, which allows it to leave its body after death and return to it, making it a formidable undead. The body is often described as being able to leave the grave in a spectral or semi-material form. In some accounts, it is said to have a beak or a sharp, pointed mouth, echoing its Latin 'strix' (owl) origins.

Powers

Dual Soul (Anima and Spiritus) The strzyga is born with two souls. This is the source of its power and its curse. It is this second soul that allows it to rise from the dead and continue its existence as an undead. It is a state of being that is both a gift and a curse, as it is never truly at peace. General Polish folklore The second soul is also a point of vulnerability. Some rituals focus on separating or destroying this second soul to kill the strzyga for good. Some accounts say the second soul is a demon that inhabits the body at birth, while others say it is a natural (if abnormal) part of the person.

Blood Drinking The strzyga primarily feeds on the blood of the living. It is especially drawn to its own relatives, as their blood is 'closest' to it. This is a violation of family bonds and a perversion of the social order. General Polish folklore It must feed regularly to maintain its form. If it cannot feed, it may weaken. Some accounts describe it as also eating flesh, while others focus solely on blood.

Spectral Form / Shape-shifting The strzyga can leave its grave in a spectral or shadowy form. It is often said to be able to transform into a bird (especially an owl or a crow), a moth, or a mist. This allows it to travel unseen and enter homes through keyholes or cracks. General Polish folklore It is bound to its physical body, which must remain in the grave. If the body is destroyed, the strzyga is destroyed. The ability to fully transform into a solid animal is debated; some say it is only a spectral illusion.

Supernatural Strength & Tenacity When encountered in a physical form, the strzyga is said to possess immense strength and is incredibly difficult to kill. It is resistant to normal weapons and can only be truly destroyed by specific rituals. General Polish folklore Vulnerable to specific counters: decapitation with a sickle, staking, burning, or being buried face-down. None significant.

Compulsive Counting Like many Slavic undead, the strzyga has a compulsion to count small objects, such as poppy seeds, sand grains, or lentils. Scattering these in its path or in its grave can delay it until dawn, when it must retreat. General Slavic folklore This is a distraction, not a permanent solution. It is a common motif across many vampire traditions. None.

Accounts

A child is born with teeth and a tail, a clear sign of a strzyga. The parents, fearing the child, may try to baptize it immediately or even abandon it. If the child dies, it rises from its grave as a strzyga. It begins to haunt the village, appearing at night to its grieving mother, trying to suckle her blood. It attacks livestock and, eventually, people. The villagers, realizing what has happened, dig up the grave. They find the body intact, flushed, and with blood on its lips. They cut off the head with a sickle and place it face-down at the feet, or they drive a stake through the heart, or they burn the body. Only then does the village find peace.

In this variant, the strzyga (or strzygoń, the male form) is a living person, born with teeth and a tail. They are not inherently evil but are marked by their condition. They are often feared and ostracized. They are believed to be able to project their spirit, which can cause harm. After death, they inevitably become a full-fledged undead strzyga. This variant highlights the social dimension: the living person is a 'monster' in waiting, a source of fear and suspicion.

Field Notes

† The strzyga's name is a direct linguistic descendant of the ancient Roman 'strix,' a blood-drinking owl-witch. The fear of the night-flying predator became the fear of the undead.

† The archaeological evidence for anti-vampire burials in Poland, which are often associated with the strzyga belief, includes skeletons with sickles across their throats, bricks in their mouths, and bodies buried face-down to confuse the dead.

† The strzyga is a favorite monster in modern fantasy, but its portrayal is almost always a sanitized, romanticized version. The original folk strzyga was a mindless, gluttonous predator, not a tragic heroine.

† The compulsion to count poppy seeds is a common weakness shared by many Slavic vampires, including the strzyga. This suggests a deep, shared folkloric root for these creatures.

Modern Interpretations

Literature The Witcher series (Saga o wiedźminie) Andrzej Sapkowski 1990s-present direct Sapkowski's strzyga is a tragic figure: a princess cursed by her father, transformed into a monstrous, ravenous being. Geralt of Rivia must defeat her, and his choice to use a magical sign to break the curse rather than kill her is a pivotal moment in the first major story. This portrayal has become the most famous modern interpretation. Video Game The Witcher 3: Wild Hunt CD Projekt Red 2015 direct The strzyga appears as a formidable boss monster. The game's bestiary entry provides a detailed, lore-accurate description of its origins and weaknesses, cementing its place in modern pop culture. Television The Witcher (Netflix series) Lauren Schmidt Hissrich 2019-present direct The strzyga is featured in the first season, bringing the creature to a global audience. The visual design is a terrifying blend of human and monstrous features. Scholarly Debates
Living vs. Undead
The strzyga is a living person (often a witch) with an evil spirit, capable of harming others through magic and malevolent intent. ⇌ The strzyga is strictly an undead creature, a corpse reanimated by a second soul that must feed on the living.
Origin of the Second Soul
The second soul is a demon or evil spirit that enters the child at birth. ⇌ The second soul is a natural, albeit abnormal, part of the person, perhaps a sign of being 'chosen' or cursed by fate.
Method of Destruction
Staking through the heart is the only sure way. ⇌ Decapitation with a sickle is the most common and effective method.

What We Don't Know

While the general outline of the strzyga is well-documented, specific regional variations are under-researched. The precise rituals for its destruction, while commonly described, may have many local variants that are not fully captured in the available literature. The relationship between the strzyga and other West Slavic undead (like the upiór) is also a subject of ongoing academic debate. Modern reception, particularly in non-Polish media, is a rapidly growing field that has not been fully cataloged.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of the strzyga as the terrifying, real-world ancestor of the modern pop-culture vampire. If Dracula is the suave, aristocratic, romanticized vampire, the strzyga is his dark, feral cousin from the Eastern European countryside—a creature of pure hunger and fear, born not from a gothic novel but from a genuine, visceral terror of death and the corruption of the body. It's the monster that makes you understand why people once staked corpses in their graves.
