Who It Is

The Invisible Man — Griffin, a brilliant but amoral former medical student — is the protagonist and antagonist of H.G. Wells's novel, embodying the corrupting influence of unchecked scientific power. Griffin discovers a method of altering a body's refractive index to match that of air, rendering it invisible. The process works on living tissue but cannot be reversed, and the albino Griffin (whose lack of pigment makes the process easier) finds himself permanently invisible. Far from being a superpower, invisibility becomes a curse: he must go naked to remain unseen (clothes remain visible), he cannot eat in public (food is visible until digested), he leaves footprints in mud, and he is trapped in a state of permanent isolation. The novel follows Griffin's arrival in the rural village of Iping, wrapped in bandages and goggles, where his increasingly desperate and violent attempts to maintain his secret culminate in a rampage. Griffin descends rapidly from ambitious scientist to megalomaniacal terrorist, declaring a 'Reign of Terror' in which invisible assassins would enforce his will — revealing that his isolation has severed his moral tether to humanity entirely. He is ultimately beaten to death by a mob of townspeople, his body slowly becoming visible again as he dies. The novel is less a horror story about a monster than a psychological study of how power without accountability destroys empathy. Griffin begins with scientific curiosity and ends with fantasies of mass murder and dictatorship. Wells's theme — that removing oneself from the social fabric removes the basis for morality — has proven remarkably prescient in an age of anonymous online discourse. The Invisible Man has been adapted countless times: James Whale's 1933 Universal film (with groundbreaking special effects), a 2020 Blumhouse reimagining focused on domestic abuse and gaslighting, and Ralph Ellison's literary masterpiece 'Invisible Man' (1952) which repurposes the concept as a metaphor for the African American experience of being unseen by society. The character represents one of the oldest human fantasies (invisibility) and its logical, horrifying conclusion: what would we become if no one could see us?

Cultural Significance

Explores the relationship between visibility and morality — what happens when we cannot be seen and therefore cannot be held accountable. Anticipates modern concerns about anonymous online behavior. The iconography of bandages and goggles is instantly recognizable.
