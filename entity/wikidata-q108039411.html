Who It Is

He never led an army, never held a bishopric, and never wanted to be a martyr — yet for thirty years all of Europe read Desiderius Erasmus as if he were its conscience. The illegitimate son of a priest who died orphaned and penniless, he taught himself to correct the Greek of the New Testament, mocked popes and princes in perfect Latin, and then watched both Catholics and Protestants decide he had betrayed them.

Attested in letters and by friends (More, Beatus Rhenanus, Beatus Rhenanus Vita Erasmi 1540): witty, ironical, hyper-productive, anxious about health and money, averse to conflict yet compulsively polemical in print. Wrote up to 40 letters a day in his own hand, waking at 3-4 a.m., never working after dinner. Habitual self-fashioning: presented himself as frail, peace-loving, independent scholar needing patronage but refusing permanent posts. Powers
Philological Textual Criticism

Pioneered systematic collation of Greek manuscripts, conjectural emendation, and annotation modeled on Lorenzo Valla's Adnotationes. Applied to New Testament, Jerome (1516), Cyprian, Hilary, Irenaeus, and Seneca. Introduced critical apparatus distinguishing Vulgate readings from Greek witnesses.

Limit: Had only 6-7 late minuscule Greek manuscripts in Basel (minuscules 1, 2, 817 etc.), none with Revelation complete; filled last six verses of Revelation by retro-translating Latin Vulgate into Greek, creating readings found in no Greek manuscript. Denied making a 'standard text' (Letter to Maarten van Dorp 1515).

Copia - Abundant Style and Pedagogy

Ability to generate 100+ variations on a single sentence (De copia 1512), teaching Latin composition through commonplace books (loci communes) carried in boxes. Created textbooks that dominated European grammar schools for a century: De copia, De ratione studii (1511), De pueris instituendis (1529), Colloquia familiaria (1518-1533), De civilitate morum puerilium (1530).

Limit: Method required extensive memorization and note-taking; criticized by Ciceronians (Ciceronianus 1528) for over-abundance and by reformers for worldliness.

Biblical Humanist Theology - Philosophia Christi

Theology centered on interior piety, ethics of the Sermon on the Mount, and accessible Scripture in vernacular paraphrase, minimizing scholastic speculation. Advocated pastoral tolerance on adiaphora (matters indifferent) and peaceable concord.

Limit: Rejected by Luther and Calvin as synergistic (human cooperation with grace) and by scholastics as insufficiently doctrinal. Never systematized; remained essayistic.

Satirical and Rhetorical Persuasion

Mastery of encomium, diatribe, dialogue, and Lucianic satire to critique clergy, war, and scholasticism without direct heresy charge. Praise of Folly (1511) uses Folly herself to praise folly, allowing deniability.

Limit: Irony was often missed; both sides quoted him out of context. Satire made him enemies who pursued him for decades (Béda, Lee).

Network Building and Epistolary Influence

Maintained correspondence network of ~500 correspondents including Henry VIII, Charles V, Francis I, Popes Leo X and Adrian VI, Thomas More, Thomas Wolsey, and Froben. Letters circulated in print (Farrago 1519, Epistolae aliquot 1520) as public opinion formation.

Limit: Dependence on patrons and printers made him vulnerable to piracy (Aldus, Froben competitors) and to accusations of opportunism. Letters were edited for publication, not always candid.

Translation and Paraphrase

Translated Greek Fathers, Euripides, Lucian, Plutarch, and extensive New Testament Paraphrases (1517-1524) intended as accessible theological commentary for clergy and educated laity, blending translation with exposition.

Limit: Paraphrases were criticized for inserting Erasmian theology into biblical text; placed on Index of Forbidden Books in some forms after 1559 but widely used in England by royal injunction 1547.

Accounts I
Orphaned Canon: From Deventer to SteynBeatus Rhenanus, Vita Erasmi (1540); Allen Ep. 1, 296; Erasmus, Compendium vitae (c.1524)

1466-1493

Born illegitimate to Gerard, a priest who had copied manuscripts, and Margaretha Rogerius, a physician's daughter, Erasmus and his elder brother Peter were left orphaned by plague c.1483-84. Guardians squandered inheritance and forced both into monastic life. Erasmus entered the Brethren of the Common Life school at Deventer (1475-84) where he encountered humanist Alexander Hegius, then was pressed into the Augustinian canonry at Steyn near Gouda (1487). He later described Steyn as wasted years but there mastered Latin poetry, formed a passionate friendship with Servatius Rogerus, and was ordained priest 25 April 1492. He obtained leave to serve as secretary to Bishop Henry of Bergen in 1493 and never returned to monastic enclosure. Monastic vows legally bound him for life until papal dispensation 1517; experience fueled lifelong anticlerical satire and advocacy for education over monastic discipline. Early poems and De contemptu mundi date from this period.

The English Spring: Colet, More, and the Philosophia ChristiAllen Epp. 118-142; Thomas More, Letter to Germain de Brie; Erasmus, Enchiridion militis Christiani (1503)

1499-1505

Invited to England by Lord Mountjoy (former pupil in Paris), Erasmus met John Colet at Oxford in 1499. Colet's allegorical but historically grounded lectures on St. Paul shocked Erasmus, who had dismissed theology as sophistry. Colet urged him to study theology. In London he befriended Thomas More, then a young lawyer. This triad forged the ideal of philosophia Christi — Christianity as way of life modeled on Christ's ethics rather than scholastic quiddities. Erasmus wrote Enchiridion militis Christiani (Handbook of a Christian Knight, 1503) for a soldier's wife, synthesizing this program. He also discovered Lorenzo Valla's Adnotationes on the New Testament at Parc Abbey near Louvain in 1504, which convinced him philology must precede theology. Redirected Erasmus from pure classics to biblical humanism; Enchiridion became bestseller and program for reform; Valla discovery set agenda for Novum Instrumentum.

In Aldus's Academy: Venice and the AdagiaAllen Epp. 200-300; Erasmus, Adagiorum Chiliades (1508); Aldine press records

1506-1509

Traveling as tutor to the sons of Battista Boerio, royal physician to Henry VII, Erasmus reached Venice in 1506 and was welcomed into Aldus Manutius's New Academy, where scholars spoke only Greek. Working 10 months in the Aldine press, he expanded his Adagiorum collectanea (1500) into the 1508 Adagiorum Chiliades with 3,260 proverbs, each a mini-essay on antiquity, including the famous Sileni Alcibiadis, Dulce bellum inexpertis, and Festina lente. He helped correct Aldine editions of Euripides, Plato, and Plutarch while perfecting his Greek. He was offered a professorship but declined, preferring independence. He visited Rome 1506-09, was offered preferment, and famously drafted Julius exclusus e caelis, a savage dialogue where Pope Julius II is denied entry to heaven. Adagia made him Europe's most famous humanist; royalties and fame secured patronage. Aldine method of collating manuscripts directly informed Novum Instrumentum. Julius exclusus circulated anonymously and was later used by Lutherans.

Accounts II
Folly in England and the Novum Instrumentum in BaselMoriae Encomium (1511); Novum Instrumentum omne (1516); Allen Epp. 384, 411

1509-1516

Returning to England in 1509 on hearing Henry VIII's accession, Erasmus lodged with More and in a week wrote Moriae Encomium (Praise of Folly), dedicated to More (moria = folly/More pun), where Folly praises herself and inadvertently praises true Christian folly. After teaching Greek at Cambridge (1511-14, Lady Margaret Professor of Divinity, unhappy, complained of ale and weather), he moved to Basel to work with printer Johann Froben. In a feverish six months (1515-16), he assembled Novum Instrumentum: a new Latin translation, Greek text in parallel column, and Annotations. Dedicated to Pope Leo X, it was the first published Greek New Testament (though not first printed; Complutensian Polyglot printed 1514 but published 1520). It included the Johannine Comma (1 John 5:7-8) only after pressure, and famously lacked it in first two editions. Created basis for Luther's German New Testament (1522), Tyndale (1526), and King James Version via Textus Receptus. Made Erasmus Europe's most printed author (10-20% of book sales 1530s). Provoked immediate attacks from Edward Lee and López de Zúñi

The Break with Luther: De libero arbitrioErasmus, De libero arbitrio (1524); Luther, De servo arbitrio (1525); Allen Epp. 1334, 1356

1517-1525

Initially Erasmus welcomed Luther: in 1519 he wrote that Luther's protest against indulgences seemed just and refused to denounce him, urging moderation. Luther admired Erasmus and used his Greek text. As Luther radicalized, Catholic pressure mounted on Erasmus to declare himself. After years of delay, at urging of Henry VIII and Pope Adrian VI, Erasmus published De libero arbitrio (1524), defending synergism — that human will cooperates with grace. Luther replied with De servo arbitrio (1525), accusing Erasmus of skepticism. The exchange ended their friendship and split humanist reform from Protestant Reformation. Erasmus's middle way collapsed; humanists divided (Hutten and others joined Luther, More stayed Catholic). Erasmus was branded coward by Protestants and crypto-Lutheran by Catholics; his books later censored by both sides. Defined Catholic synergism vs. Protestant monergism debate for centuries.

Exile in Freiburg and Return to Basel: Death without Sacraments?Allen Epp. 1800-3158; Beatus Rhenanus Vita; Basel council records 1529

1521-1536

As Basel turned Zwinglian after 1529 (iconoclasm, mass abolished), Erasmus, unwilling to live in a Reformed city, moved to Catholic Freiburg im Breisgau (1529-1535) under protection of Archduke Ferdinand. There he completed editions of Augustine (1528-29), Chrysostom, and Ecclesiastes (1535, his manual for preachers). Financially secure from pensions and Froben royalties, he continued defending his New Testament against Spanish and Parisian theologians (Apologiae). Suffering from gout, kidney stones, and dysentery, he returned to Basel in 1535 to oversee Froben's press and be closer to friends. He died 12 July 1536 in Basel, in the house of Hieronymus Froben, reportedly without last rites of the Catholic Church, uttering 'Lieve God' (Dear God) in Dutch. He was buried in Basel Minster (then Protestant), where his tomb remains. Death without Catholic sacraments fueled legends he died Protestant; Pope Paul III had offered him cardinalate in 1535, which he declined due to health. His library and estate funded scholarships. Basel burial symbolized his ambiguous confessional status.

Erasmus was a Protestant or founder of Protestantism. — He remained a Catholic priest his entire life, defended papal primacy and free will, and explicitly rejected Luther's sola fide and sola scriptura as formulated at Leipzig and Worms. Pope Paul III offered him a cardinalate in 1535. His books were placed on the Catholic Index after his death, but he was never excommunicated.

Erasmus created the Textus Receptus as a definitive critical text. — He denied making a critical edition and called his work Novum Instrumentum — a contribution to correction and understanding, not a standard. His Greek text was hastily assembled from 6-7 late manuscripts and included retro-translated passages (Rev 22:16-21) and the Johannine Comma only under pressure. Textus Receptus is a later commercial label (Elzevir 1633) based on his and others' editions.

Praise of Folly is a light, humorous student joke. — Moriae Encomium is a sophisticated Lucianic satire with three layers: Folly praises folly, then unwittingly praises true Christian folly (St. Paul's 'foolishness of the Cross'), while Erasmus praises More. It attacks war, papal pretension, scholastic theology, and monastic corruption, and was considered dangerous enough to be censored.

Erasmus was Dutch and wrote in Dutch. — He was born in the Burgundian Netherlands (Rotterdam) but wrote almost exclusively in Latin (and edited Greek). He lived only four years in Rotterdam, spent his career in France, England, Italy, and Switzerland, and corresponded in Latin with an international Republic of Letters. No Dutch vernacular works survive.

Erasmus was a pacifist saint who was universally tolerant. — He was an irenicist who wrote Querela Pacis (Complaint of Peace, 1517) condemning war, but he supported suppression of Anabaptists and radical reformers, used harsh anti-Jewish rhetoric, and engaged in vicious polemics against Lee, Béda, and Hutten. His tolerance was for adiaphora among elites, not modern pluralism.
