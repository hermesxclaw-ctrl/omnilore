Who It Is

The Keshalyi (also Keshali, meaning 'Fairies' in Romani; singular: Keshalya) are the fairy-nature spirits of Romani folklore — beautiful, ethereal women who dwell in forests, mountains, and caves. They are the daughters of the moon (a prominent figure in Romani cosmology), and their queen is Ana, the Fairy Queen. The Keshalyi are said to be invisible to most humans, but those with 'clean' hearts or those who have fasted and purified themselves may see them dancing in forest clearings at midnight. The Keshalyi are ambivalent: they can bless travellers with healing herbs and good fortune, but they also abduct Romani men as lovers — though unlike the fatal seductions of demonic entities, the abducted man returns after a time with gifts and knowledge (though often addled or changed). They are particularly associated with healing: Romani women who acted as herbal healers (drabarni) were said to have learned their craft from the Keshalyi, who revealed medicinal plants in dreams or trance states. The Keshalyi are also connected to the Romani concept of 'marimé' (ritual purity/pollution): the fairies are pure beings who can only interact with the pure, and their favour is contingent on maintaining ritual cleanliness. The Keshalyi represent the Romani relationship with the wild — as a travelling people, the Roma navigated forests and mountains that settled populations feared, and the fairies are the supernatural map of that landscape. They also reflect the high status of female spiritual power in Romani culture, where women were the primary mediators of the supernatural through healing, divination (dookerin), and purity maintenance.

Form

Attested secondary descriptions portray Keshalyi as "classic romantic fairies"—beautiful, gentle, and shimmering beings inhabiting remote forests and mountain glades. They are usually imagined as female, often described as fairy princesses or attendants of their queen Ana, dwelling in a magical palace atop a sacred mountain in the Carpathians. Their queen Ana is said to appear, in her withdrawn state, as a golden toad rather than in human form, suggesting that Keshalyi themselves may have liminal, shape‑shifting qualities, though explicit transformations for ordinary Keshalyi are not detailed. These visual and sensory details are largely reconstructed from modern occult and fantasy literature rather than from documented Romani oral descriptions.

Powers

Fertility bestowal Keshalyi are said to possess overflowing fertility and to be able to grant fertility to humans, particularly to those experiencing unexplained infertility. Petitioners invoke them using the phrase "Keshal yi lisperesn" and offerings, asking for conception and healthy childbirth. Judika Illes' Encyclopedia of Spirits; Occult World summary; Kaleidoscopic Path Wiki paraphrase. Their aid is described as most effective when no physical cause for infertility can be determined, implying a focus on spiritual or fate‑related obstacles. Ritual instructions emphasize respectful offerings and promises of future thanks, suggesting that their help is conditional on proper ritual etiquette. No explicit contradictions are recorded regarding this ability, but there is a tension between their role as fertility spirits and their queen's association with the birth of disease spirits, which some interpreters read as a moral paradox.

Wish‑granting Keshalyi are portrayed as very good at granting people's wishes, especially when approached in their mountain realm or invoked through ritual. Their queen Ana, in particular, is said to be able to fulfill any wish if encountered and approached with veneration and gentle manners. Encyclopedia of Spirits; Kaleidoscopic Path Wiki "Ana" entry. Access to Ana is rare; she hides within her palace and only occasionally ventures out in the form of a golden toad. The conditions for successful wish‑granting include recognition, respectful approach, and appropriate offerings. Some role‑playing adaptations emphasize their role as protectors and healers rather than general wish‑granters, narrowing the scope of their powers.

Magic‑working Keshalyi are described as magic‑working spirits, with their very name linked (speculatively) to words for wizard and magic. They are involved in diagnostic and healing practices in which shamans and fairy magicians negotiate with disease spirits born from Ana's union with the Loçolico king. Encyclopedia of Spirits; Kaleidoscopic Path Wiki; Creatures of Myth Wiki "The Children of Ana". Details of specific techniques, spells, or rituals are not provided in accessible sources; the description remains general. Their magic is framed as benevolent and healing rather than offensive. Because the linguistic derivation of their name from Akkadian and Hebrew terms for magic is speculative, the degree to which 'magic‑working' is an inherent traditional attribute versus a modern interpretive overlay is uncertain.

Healing of illness Ana, as queen of the Keshalyi, is said to be able to heal any illness, including those caused by her own children—the disease spirits. Keshalyi collectively participate in rituals aimed at negotiating with these spirits to cure sickness. Encyclopedia of Spirits; Kaleidoscopic Path Wiki "Ana"; Creatures of Myth Wiki narrative. Healing is contingent on successful negotiation with disease spirits and on proper ritual practice. The saga of Ana and the King of Demons is used as a diagnostic tool, implying that knowledge of each disease spirit's story is necessary. The same narrative that grants them healing power also frames them as indirectly responsible for the existence of disease, creating a moral and cosmological tension that different interpreters resolve in different ways.

Longevity and cursed marriage Keshalyi are said to live long enough that some reach the age of 999. A curse imposed as the price of Ana's freedom decrees that any Keshalyi who reaches 999 must marry one of the Loçolico spirits. Encyclopedia of Spirits; Occult World summary. This is more a narrative condition than a power; it implies extraordinary longevity but frames it as a burden rather than a gift. Role‑playing adaptations reinterpret this curse as a pact between the daughters of Ana and the sons of Loçolico to produce children free of the world's ills, softening the original tragic tone.

Accounts

Ana, the beautiful and benevolent queen of the Keshalyi, lives with her fairy entourage in a magical palace atop a sacred mountain. The king of the malevolent Loçolico spirits falls in love with her and repeatedly proposes marriage. When she refuses, he sends his minions to devour her Keshalyi attendants. To save her people from extinction, Ana agrees to marry him. Their union is coerced and tragic: Ana, a goddess‑like figure of fertility, conceives repeatedly, but each child is a horrific disease spirit that brings new illnesses into the world. After bearing multiple such offspring, Ana is horrified and begs for release. The Loçolico king eventually grants her a divorce, but only on the condition that any Keshalyi who reaches the age of 999 must marry one of his Loçolico. Ana withdraws to her mountain palace, appearing only as a golden toad, yet retains the power to grant wishes and heal illnesses. The saga functions both as a mythic explanation for the origin of disease and as a narrative framework for magical diagnosis and healing.

A ritual is described for petitioning the Keshalyi to heal infertility. The practitioner writes the phrase "Keshal yi lisperesn" ("Keshalyi spin") with glue on cardboard, sprinkles gold or red glitter over the letters, and decorates the card. While it dries, an offering table is prepared with milk, fruit brandy, cakes, and cookies. The practitioner then chants the phrase aloud like a mantra until feeling the presence of the Keshalyi, at which point they make their request and promise a larger offering table when the request is fulfilled, possibly annually or after a healthy birth.

Content Notes

The Loçolico king's pursuit of Ana involves coercion and violence: he sends his minions to devour her Keshalyi entourage, effectively threatening genocide unless she agrees to marry him. Ana's consent is obtained under duress, and her marriage is portrayed as degrading and tragic.

This narrative can be read as a mythic reflection on forced marriage, patriarchal violence, and the vulnerability of marginalized communities. However, detailed scholarly analysis from Romani studies or gender‑focused folklore research has not been located in accessible sources, so such interpretations remain tentative.

Encyclopedia of Spirits; Occult World; Kaleidoscopic Path Wiki.

Ana's repeated pregnancies produce monstrous offspring that personify diseases and plagues. These children spread boils, rashes, fevers, wasting sickness, plague, cholera, and smallpox among humans, introducing widespread suffering and death.

The motif of a divine or fairy figure giving birth to disease spirits is a common way of explaining illness in mythic terms. It may encode cultural experiences of epidemic disease and the moral ambivalence of fertility. Without direct access to Romani oral narratives and scholarly commentary, the deeper cultural meanings remain underexplored.

Creatures of Myth Wiki "The Children of Ana"; Kaleidoscopic Path Wiki; general references in Encyclopedia of Spirits.

As the price of Ana's freedom, a curse is imposed: any Keshalyi who reaches the age of 999 must marry one of the Loçolico. This binds future generations of Keshalyi to potentially abusive relationships and perpetuates the threat of exploitation.

This element can be interpreted as a mythic commentary on long‑term consequences of coerced agreements and the intergenerational transmission of trauma. No academic studies specifically addressing this motif in Romani folklore have been identified in accessible sources.

Encyclopedia of Spirits; Occult World.

What We Don't Know

No primary Romani-language texts, transcribed oral narratives, or ethnographic field recordings specifically detailing Keshalyi have been located in accessible online sources. Linguistic analysis of the name "Keshalyi" and its proposed connections to Romani, Akkadian, and Hebrew terms is absent from academic philological literature consulted indirectly through secondary references. The exact pronunciation in Romani, traditional ritual practices involving Keshalyi within Roma communities, and historical dating of the saga of Ana and the Loçolico king remain unknown. Visual iconography from Romani art or ritual objects depicting Keshalyi has not been identified. Scholarly commentary from Romani studies, folklore, or gender studies on the coercive marriage motif and disease‑spirit offspring is lacking in the accessible material. URLs for some cited web sources are not recorded here to avoid speculative fabrication; verification in a controlled bibliographic environment is needed.
