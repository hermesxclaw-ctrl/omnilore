Archive Dossier — Identity

Keshalyi

Pronunciation Reconstructed: commonly rendered in English-language occult and fandom sources as "KESH-ah-yee" or "KESH-ah-lee"; no phonetic transcription from Romani-language primary sources has been located, so this remains approximate.

Known As

KeshayliKeshalyi fairiesChildren of Ana (as a collective in some retellings)

Etymology

Attested modern occult and folklore syntheses state that the name "Keshalyi" is believed to derive from a Romani word meaning "spindle" and is therefore associated with spinning the threads of fate. The same sources note that the form "Keshalyi" resembles an ancient Akkadian word meaning wizard or magician and a Hebrew word meaning magic, and use this resemblance to underline their role as magic-working spirits; these linguistic links are speculative and not supported by primary philological studies, so they should be treated as reconstructed rather than firmly attested.

Cultural Ownership

Keshalyi belong to Romani (Roma) mythological and folkloric tradition, specifically narratives associated with Central and Eastern European Roma communities (often localized to Transylvania and the Carpathian region). The surviving descriptions are mediated through later collectors, occult authors, and role‑playing adaptations rather than directly recorded Romani oral narrators, so contemporary Roma cultural voices and scholarly ethnographies should be prioritized in any further work.

Disputed Identifications

Modern occult and fantasy literature sometimes treat Keshalyi as generic 'fairies' or nature spirits comparable to European fae, while other retellings emphasize their specificity as Romani mountain spirits tied to fertility and disease. Some writers conflate Keshalyi with Sânziene or other Romanian fairy beings, or treat 'Keshalyi' as a kith or subtype within broader fairy taxonomies. Because primary Romani-language sources are not readily available, the degree to which these identifications reflect traditional belief versus modern syncretic reinterpretation remains uncertain.

Archive Dossier — Approach

In the shadowed forests of the Carpathians, a people of shimmering fairy women carry a curse older than any plague: the Keshalyi, Romani spirits of overflowing fertility, whose queen’s coerced marriage to a demon king is said to have birthed disease itself.

We dance where the forest has no path, daughters of the moon, invisible to all but the pure. Bring us your illness, your hunger, your open heart — we heal or we take, as we choose.

Who It Is

The Keshalyi (also Keshali, meaning 'Fairies' in Romani; singular: Keshalya) are the fairy-nature spirits of Romani folklore — beautiful, ethereal women who dwell in forests, mountains, and caves. They are the daughters of the moon (a prominent figure in Romani cosmology), and their queen is Ana, the Fairy Queen. The Keshalyi are said to be invisible to most humans, but those with 'clean' hearts or those who have fasted and purified themselves may see them dancing in forest clearings at midnight. The Keshalyi are ambivalent: they can bless travellers with healing herbs and good fortune, but they also abduct Romani men as lovers — though unlike the fatal seductions of demonic entities, the abducted man returns after a time with gifts and knowledge (though often addled or changed). They are particularly associated with healing: Romani women who acted as herbal healers (drabarni) were said to have learned their craft from the Keshalyi, who revealed medicinal plants in dreams or trance states. The Keshalyi are also connected to the Romani concept of 'marimé' (ritual purity/pollution): the fairies are pure beings who can only interact with the pure, and their favour is contingent on maintaining ritual cleanliness. The Keshalyi represent the Romani relationship with the wild — as a travelling people, the Roma navigated forests and mountains that settled populations feared, and the fairies are the supernatural map of that landscape. They also reflect the high status of female spiritual power in Romani culture, where women were the primary mediators of the supernatural through healing, divination (dookerin), and purity maintenance.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

Attested secondary descriptions portray Keshalyi as "classic romantic fairies"—beautiful, gentle, and shimmering beings inhabiting remote forests and mountain glades. They are usually imagined as female, often described as fairy princesses or attendants of their queen Ana, dwelling in a magical palace atop a sacred mountain in the Carpathians. Their queen Ana is said to appear, in her withdrawn state, as a golden toad rather than in human form, suggesting that Keshalyi themselves may have liminal, shape‑shifting qualities, though explicit transformations for ordinary Keshalyi are not detailed. These visual and sensory details are largely reconstructed from modern occult and fantasy literature rather than from documented Romani oral descriptions.

Symbols & Iconography

The most prominent symbolic association is with the spindle and the act of spinning, linked to their name and to the phrase "Keshal yi lisperesn" ("Keshalyi spin") used in invocations. This connects them conceptually to fate‑spinning deities and to the threads of life and fertility. Offerings recommended in occult sources—milk, fruit brandy (palinka), cakes, and cookies—function as ritual symbols of nourishment and abundance. Ana's golden toad form and the palace atop a sacred mountain serve as iconographic motifs in narrative retellings. Because no traditional visual art or ritual iconography from Romani communities has been located in accessible sources, these symbols should be treated as adaptation‑specific rather than fully attested.

Behaviour & Personality

Keshalyi are consistently characterized as gentle, kind, generous, and benevolent spirits who grant wishes and bestow fertility. They are portrayed as reluctant participants in violence and as victims of predatory Loçolico spirits. Their behavior toward humans is described as helpful, especially in matters of infertility and illness, provided they are approached with respect and offerings. At the same time, their collective saga is tragic: they are bound by a curse that forces any Keshalyi who reaches the age of 999 to marry one of the Loçolico, and their queen Ana is depicted as traumatized, withdrawn, and ashamed after bearing disease spirits. These personality traits—benevolence, sorrow, and a sense of moral purity contrasted with imposed degradation—are interpretive reconstructions based on narrative framing in secondary sources.

Domains & Habitats

Keshalyi inhabit remote forests, mountain glades, and a magical palace atop a sacred mountain in the Carpathian region, often localized to Transylvania. Their realm is liminal—between human settlements and wild nature, and between the surface world and the underworld ruled by Loçolico. Their functional domain is fertility (especially human fertility and childbirth) and, paradoxically, the management or healing of disease, since their queen's offspring personify illnesses. They are invoked in rituals to heal infertility and to negotiate with disease spirits. These domains are attested in occult syntheses and fantasy retellings; direct ethnographic confirmation from Romani communities is lacking in the accessible material.

Version Differences

Across modern sources, Keshalyi shift from a collective of fairy beings in occult encyclopedias to a specific kith of all‑female fairies in role‑playing adaptations. Some texts emphasize their role as fertility spirits and wish‑granters, while others foreground their tragic entanglement with disease through Ana's coerced marriage to the Loçolico king. In some retellings, Ana herself is classified as a Keshalyi fairy princess or queen; in others, she is treated as a distinct goddess‑like figure whose people are the Keshalyi. Role‑playing adaptations introduce additional structures (Seelie/Unseelie courts, Sânziene and Iarnă Vâlvă) that are not part of the original Romani myth but build on regional Romanian folklore. Because primary Romani narratives are not available, it is impossible to definitively separate traditional elements from later syncretic or creative additions.

Archive Dossier — Powers

Fertility bestowal

Source Version Judika Illes' Encyclopedia of Spirits; Occult World summary; Kaleidoscopic Path Wiki paraphrase.

Description

Keshalyi are said to possess overflowing fertility and to be able to grant fertility to humans, particularly to those experiencing unexplained infertility. Petitioners invoke them using the phrase "Keshal yi lisperesn" and offerings, asking for conception and healthy childbirth.

Limitations

Their aid is described as most effective when no physical cause for infertility can be determined, implying a focus on spiritual or fate‑related obstacles. Ritual instructions emphasize respectful offerings and promises of future thanks, suggesting that their help is conditional on proper ritual etiquette.

Contradictions

No explicit contradictions are recorded regarding this ability, but there is a tension between their role as fertility spirits and their queen's association with the birth of disease spirits, which some interpreters read as a moral paradox.

Wish‑granting

Source Version Encyclopedia of Spirits; Kaleidoscopic Path Wiki "Ana" entry.

Description

Keshalyi are portrayed as very good at granting people's wishes, especially when approached in their mountain realm or invoked through ritual. Their queen Ana, in particular, is said to be able to fulfill any wish if encountered and approached with veneration and gentle manners.

Limitations

Access to Ana is rare; she hides within her palace and only occasionally ventures out in the form of a golden toad. The conditions for successful wish‑granting include recognition, respectful approach, and appropriate offerings.

Contradictions

Some role‑playing adaptations emphasize their role as protectors and healers rather than general wish‑granters, narrowing the scope of their powers.

Magic‑working

Source Version Encyclopedia of Spirits; Kaleidoscopic Path Wiki; Creatures of Myth Wiki "The Children of Ana".

Description

Keshalyi are described as magic‑working spirits, with their very name linked (speculatively) to words for wizard and magic. They are involved in diagnostic and healing practices in which shamans and fairy magicians negotiate with disease spirits born from Ana's union with the Loçolico king.

Limitations

Details of specific techniques, spells, or rituals are not provided in accessible sources; the description remains general. Their magic is framed as benevolent and healing rather than offensive.

Contradictions

Because the linguistic derivation of their name from Akkadian and Hebrew terms for magic is speculative, the degree to which 'magic‑working' is an inherent traditional attribute versus a modern interpretive overlay is uncertain.

Healing of illness

Source Version Encyclopedia of Spirits; Kaleidoscopic Path Wiki "Ana"; Creatures of Myth Wiki narrative.

Description

Ana, as queen of the Keshalyi, is said to be able to heal any illness, including those caused by her own children—the disease spirits. Keshalyi collectively participate in rituals aimed at negotiating with these spirits to cure sickness.

Limitations

Healing is contingent on successful negotiation with disease spirits and on proper ritual practice. The saga of Ana and the King of Demons is used as a diagnostic tool, implying that knowledge of each disease spirit's story is necessary.

Contradictions

The same narrative that grants them healing power also frames them as indirectly responsible for the existence of disease, creating a moral and cosmological tension that different interpreters resolve in different ways.

Longevity and cursed marriage

Source Version Encyclopedia of Spirits; Occult World summary.

Description

Keshalyi are said to live long enough that some reach the age of 999. A curse imposed as the price of Ana's freedom decrees that any Keshalyi who reaches 999 must marry one of the Loçolico spirits.

Limitations

This is more a narrative condition than a power; it implies extraordinary longevity but frames it as a burden rather than a gift.

Contradictions

Role‑playing adaptations reinterpret this curse as a pact between the daughters of Ana and the sons of Loçolico to produce children free of the world's ills, softening the original tragic tone.

Archive Dossier — Relationships

Ana

Type familySource Encyclopedia of Spirits; Kaleidoscopic Path Wiki "Ana"; Creatures of Myth Wiki.

Description

Ana is the queen or leading figure of the Keshalyi, often described as their queen, princess, or goddess of fertility. She embodies their collective fate and bears the burden of their tragic saga.

Version Note

In some retellings, Ana is explicitly classified as a Keshalyi fairy; in others, she is a distinct deity whose people are the Keshalyi.

Conflicting Accounts

Role‑playing adaptations treat Ana as queen of Sânziene Vâlvă rather than exclusively of Keshalyi, integrating Romanian fairy taxonomy; this may diverge from traditional Romani narratives.

King of the Loçolico

Type hostileSource Encyclopedia of Spirits; Occult World; Kaleidoscopic Path Wiki; Creatures of Myth Wiki.

Description

The Loçolico king is a malevolent spirit ruler who falls in love with Ana, coerces her into marriage by threatening and devouring her Keshalyi entourage, and fathers disease spirits with her.

Version Note

He is described as a king of demons or evil spirits, explicitly distinguished from Christian demons in some sources.

Conflicting Accounts

Role‑playing adaptations recast him as the king of Iarnă Vâlvă (Unseelie kith), emphasizing fairy court structures rather than demonology.

Loçolico spirits

Type hostileSource Encyclopedia of Spirits; Occult World.

Description

Loçolico are malevolent spirits under the king's command. They attack and devour the Keshalyi entourage, and later become the enforced spouses of Keshalyi who reach 999 years of age.

Version Note

They function as antagonists and as instruments of coercion in the saga.

Conflicting Accounts

In some adaptations, the sons of Loçolico are paired with the daughters of Ana in a pact intended to produce children free from disease, softening their purely hostile role.

Disease spirits (Children of Ana)

Type familySource Creatures of Myth Wiki "The Children of Ana"; Kaleidoscopic Path Wiki; Enyclopedia of Spirits (general mention).

Description

Ana's children with the Loçolico king are personified disease spirits, each associated with specific illnesses and plagues. They are blood‑relations to the Keshalyi and represent a dark extension of their fertility.

Version Note

Names and forms of individual children vary across retellings; Poreskoro is one prominent figure in some variants.

Conflicting Accounts

Some modern adaptations treat these spirits more abstractly as embodiments of 'all the world's evils' rather than specific diseases, and introduce narrative mechanisms for mitigating their curse.

Human petitioners and worshippers

Type worshipperSource Encyclopedia of Spirits; Occult World.

Description

Humans who seek fertility or healing approach the Keshalyi through rituals, offerings, and invocations. They form a devotional relationship in which Keshalyi are petitioned as benevolent spirits.

Version Note

The described rituals are modern occult practices inspired by folklore rather than documented traditional Romani religious rites.

Conflicting Accounts

Because ethnographic studies of Romani spiritual practice are not directly cited in accessible sources, it is unclear how closely these rituals align with traditional Romani customs.
