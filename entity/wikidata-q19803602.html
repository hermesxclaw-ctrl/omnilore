Who It Is

Before the first word of the oldest sacred text on Earth, there was fire. The Rigveda opens not with a creation myth or a hymn to a supreme creator, but with a shout to the flame itself: 'agnim īḷe' — 'I praise Agni.' For over three thousand years, this god of fire has been the mouth of the gods, the eater of offerings, the witness to weddings, and the purifier of the dead. He is the original messenger, the cosmic postman who carries your prayers to the heavens on a column of smoke. In the Tamil world, he is Agni kadavil — the Fire God — a name that hums with the heat of a thousand homa fires. He burns, he cleanses, he transforms. And if you've ever watched a flame and felt it was alive, you already understand why humanity made him a god.

As an aspect of Agni, he is the messenger between the human and divine worlds, the purifier of ritual offerings, and the devourer of sacrificial oblations. He is both a consuming force of destruction and a warm, domestic presence in the hearth. In Tamil domestic worship, fire is central to homa (fire offerings) and is treated with reverence as a living presence.

Powers
Messenger of the Gods

Agni carries offerings from the human realm to the divine realm, serving as the intermediary between worshippers and the pantheon.

Limit: Requires the ritual fire to be lit and maintained properly.

Purification

Fire burns away impurity, sin, and ritual pollution. Agni is invoked in purification rites, including funeral pyres (agni as the final purifier of the body).

Limit: Purification is understood as physical and symbolic; it does not negate karmic consequences in all traditions.

Destruction

As the devouring flame, Agni can consume, destroy, and transform. In his destructive aspect, he is associated with the end of a cosmic cycle (pralaya).

Limit: Destruction is typically directed at impurities, demons, or the old order; indiscriminate destruction is not celebrated.

Transformation

Fire changes the state of matter: raw to cooked, wood to ash, body to spirit. Agni is the alchemist of the cosmos.

Limit: Transformation is physical and ritual, not magical in the sense of altering reality at will.

Form & Signs

Appearance — As the Tamil rendering of the Vedic fire deity, Agni kadavil would conventionally be depicted with the attributes of Agni: red or ruddy complexion, two or more heads (often three, symbolizing the three sacrificial fires), four arms, riding a ram, and carrying a flaming spear, torch, or sacrificial ladle. Flames or tongues of fire typically emanate from his body. In Tamil folk tradition, fire deities may be represented more abstractly as a sacred

Symbols — The flame, the sacred fire pit (homa kunda), the ram (his vahana or mount), the spear or torch, the color red, the swastika (as a fire symbol in some traditions), the mantra 'om agnaye svaha' (offering to Agni).

Domains — Fire, sacrifice, purification, transformation, the hearth, the ritual altar, lightning (as a form of fire), and the sun's heat. He is the mouth of the gods, through which offerings are delivered.

Accounts I
The Birth of AgniRigveda, Puranas (e.g., Agni Purana, Vayu Purana)

c. 1500 BCE onward

Agni is said to have been born from the cosmic waters (Apam Napat, 'Child of the Waters') or from the friction of two fire-sticks (arani). He hides in the waters, afraid of his own duty, but is persuaded to return and serve as the sacrificial priest. His birth is often described as a mystery: he is both ancient and newborn. Agni becomes the eternal sacrificial priest and messenger.

Accounts II
Agni and the MahabharataMahabharata, Vana Parva (Book 3), Khandava-daha episode

c. 400 BCE – 400 CE

Agni, afflicted by indigestion after consuming too many oblations, seeks to burn the Khandava forest to cure himself. He is thwarted by Indra, who protects the forest. Agni enlists the help of Arjuna and Krishna, who fight Indra and his forces while the forest burns. Only a few beings escape, including the demon Maya. Arjuna receives the divine bow Gandiva and other weapons from Agni as a reward. Maya later builds the palace of the Pandavas.

Agni as WitnessRamayana, various Puranas

c. 500 BCE onward

Agni serves as the divine witness to oaths and marriages. In the Ramayana, Sita proves her purity by entering fire (Agni Pariksha), and Agni himself returns her unharmed to Rama. In Hindu weddings, the couple circles the sacred fire (agni) seven times, making Agni the eternal witness to the marriage. The fire-witness tradition becomes a central ritual in Hindu marriage.
