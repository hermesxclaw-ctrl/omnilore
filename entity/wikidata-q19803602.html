Form

As the Tamil rendering of the Vedic fire deity, Agni kadavil would conventionally be depicted with the attributes of Agni: red or ruddy complexion, two or more heads (often three, symbolizing the three sacrificial fires), four arms, riding a ram, and carrying a flaming spear, torch, or sacrificial ladle. Flames or tongues of fire typically emanate from his body. In Tamil folk tradition, fire deities may be represented more abstractly as a sacred flame (akni or thee) rather than an anthropomorphic figure. Since 'Agni kadavil' is not a widely attested iconographic name, no distinct Tamil visual tradition can be confirmed.

Powers

Messenger of the Gods Agni carries offerings from the human realm to the divine realm, serving as the intermediary between worshippers and the pantheon. Vedic (Rigveda) Requires the ritual fire to be lit and maintained properly. None significant.

Purification Fire burns away impurity, sin, and ritual pollution. Agni is invoked in purification rites, including funeral pyres (agni as the final purifier of the body). Vedic and Hindu Purification is understood as physical and symbolic; it does not negate karmic consequences in all traditions. Some traditions hold that fire purifies all, while others require additional rites.

Destruction As the devouring flame, Agni can consume, destroy, and transform. In his destructive aspect, he is associated with the end of a cosmic cycle (pralaya). Vedic and Puranic Destruction is typically directed at impurities, demons, or the old order; indiscriminate destruction is not celebrated. None significant.

Transformation Fire changes the state of matter: raw to cooked, wood to ash, body to spirit. Agni is the alchemist of the cosmos. Vedic and later Hindu philosophy Transformation is physical and ritual, not magical in the sense of altering reality at will. None significant.

Behavior & Habitat

As an aspect of Agni, he is the messenger between the human and divine worlds, the purifier of ritual offerings, and the devourer of sacrificial oblations. He is both a consuming force of destruction and a warm, domestic presence in the hearth. In Tamil domestic worship, fire is central to homa (fire offerings) and is treated with reverence as a living presence.

Accounts

Agni is said to have been born from the cosmic waters (Apam Napat, 'Child of the Waters') or from the friction of two fire-sticks (arani). He hides in the waters, afraid of his own duty, but is persuaded to return and serve as the sacrificial priest. His birth is often described as a mystery: he is both ancient and newborn.

Agni, afflicted by indigestion after consuming too many oblations, seeks to burn the Khandava forest to cure himself. He is thwarted by Indra, who protects the forest. Agni enlists the help of Arjuna and Krishna, who fight Indra and his forces while the forest burns. Only a few beings escape, including the demon Maya.

Agni serves as the divine witness to oaths and marriages. In the Ramayana, Sita proves her purity by entering fire (Agni Pariksha), and Agni himself returns her unharmed to Rama. In Hindu weddings, the couple circles the sacred fire (agni) seven times, making Agni the eternal witness to the marriage.

What We Don't Know

The specific entity 'Agni kadavil' is not widely attested in published sources. The Wikidata entity provides no description, aliases, or sitelinks, making it difficult to verify whether this is a distinct regional Tamil deity, a modern coinage, or a variant name for the standard Vedic Agni. Research into Tamil-language sources and regional temple records is needed to confirm the usage and context of this specific name.
