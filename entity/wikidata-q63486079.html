Who It Is

Hidden in the footnotes of Massenet's most beloved fairy-tale opera is a figure who never speaks, never solos, yet stands at the precise moment the opera's magic tips from spectacle into enchantment: the 6th spirit, one of a sextet of soprano and contralto voices who inhabit the enchanted forest, raise the arbor of flowers between two lovers who can only recognize each other by voice, and then watch over them as they fall asleep in each other's arms. She is a character who exists only in the plural—inseparable from her five sisters, defined entirely by her soprano timbre and her position in an ensemble—and yet the Wikimedia community thought her worth a Wikidata entity record of her own. What does it mean to individuate the anonymous?

The 6th spirit, as an ensemble operatic character without individual dialogue, arias, or named solo moments, is characterized collectively with the other five spirits rather than as a distinct personality. As a group, the six spirits function as obedient, graceful supernatural servants of the Fairy Godmother (La Fée), active participants in the magical choreography of Act 3 rather than mere background presences. Powers
Enchanted slumber induction

The six spirits, acting collectively under the direction of the Fairy Godmother, possess the power to induce an enchanted sleep in mortals. In Act 3 of Cendrillon, they cause both Cendrillon and the Prince to fall into a magical slumber while surrounded by the spirits in the enchanted forest. This sleep functions as a suspended liminal state between the fairy world and the mortal world—a classic motif in European fairy-tale literature dating to Perrault and beyond, in which the enchanted sleep protects the dreamer, tests fidelity, and enables transformation. The slumber lasts for months of subjective time: Act 4 opens with Pandolfe watching over his daughter who has been asleep (or in a dream-like state) since the forest encounter.

Limit:

Spatial illusion and concealment

The spirits create or control a magical arbor of flowers that prevents Cendrillon and the Prince from seeing each other in the enchanted forest, while still allowing them to hear each other's voices. This selective sensory barrier—blocking sight but not sound—serves as a magical test of the lovers' bond, forcing them to recognize each other through voice alone. The ability to construct and dissolve such illusory barriers is consistent with the wider fairy-spirit tradition in French opera and ballet, where supernatural beings routinely manipulate space and perception to guide or test mortal protagonists.

Limit:

Ethereal flight and supernatural locomotion

As supernatural spirit beings associated with the will-o'-the-wisp figures (les follets) who appear in the Act 3 ballet sequence, the spirits are implied to possess the power of ethereal movement unbound by mortal physical constraints. The production tradition consistently stages the spirits as beings who appear, disappear, and move through the enchanted forest with a weightlessness and speed unavailable to mortal characters—whether through elaborate stage machinery, lighting effects, or (in modern productions) choreographic conventions that differentiate spirit movement from human movement.

Limit:

Protective guardianship

While Cendrillon and the Prince sleep in the enchanted forest, the six spirits form a protective circle or guard around them, shielding the lovers from harm and maintaining the integrity of the magical space. This protective function positions the spirits as both servants and guardians—figures who execute the Fée's will and simultaneously serve as a supernatural defense for the mortal protagonists. The protective guardianship motif connects the spirits to a long lineage of supernatural protectors in opera and fairy-tale, from the Valkyries of Norse mythology to the fairy helpers of Perrault and the Brothers Grimm.

Limit:

Fairy-world singing and enchantment through music

As operatic soprano voices, the spirits exercise the fundamental operatic capacity to enchant through song. Massenet employs the high soprano ensemble in the spirit scenes to create a specifically musical form of enchantment: the shimmering, transparent vocal textures of the spirit voices signal the presence of the supernatural in acoustic terms, functioning as aural hallmarks of the fairy realm just as the blue lighting and enchanted forest set signal it visually. This musico-dramatic capacity aligns with the opera's broader argument—made most explicitly in the duet 'À deux genoux'—that voice and music are the most truthful channels of identity and love, capable of overcoming even the visual barriers constructed by magic.

Accounts I
The enchanted forest and the lovers' testCendrillon, Act 3, Scene 2 (libretto: Henri Caïn and Paul Collin; music: Jules Massenet, 1895)

1899 (premiere); composed 1894–1895

Under the Fairies' Oak in an enchanted forest lit by a bluish supernatural light, the six spirits gather as the Prince and Cendrillon are drawn together by fairy power. The spirits interpose a magical arbor of flowers between the two lovers, preventing them from seeing each other. The Prince and Cendrillon nonetheless recognize each other by voice and sing of their mutual love. The arbor of flowers dissolves, the lovers are finally united in each other's arms, and the spirits surround them as they fall into an enchanted slumber. This is the emotional and dramatic climax of the opera—the moment at which the fairy world most fully intervenes in the human love story. The lovers' enchanted sleep in the forest suspends the narrative: Act 4 opens months later with Cendrillon waking at home, uncertain whether the forest encounter was a dream or reality. The slumber functions as the crucible of love's proof—only when Cendrillon wakes and acts on her conviction that the prince is real does the plot move toward resolution.

The transformation of Cendrillon (implied spiritual participation)Cendrillon, Act 1 (libretto: Henri Caïn; music: Jules Massenet)

1899

In Act 1, the Fairy Godmother arrives to transform Cendrillon—dressing her magnificently for the ball and warning her to return before midnight. The libretto refers to the Fée addressing 'sylphes et lutins' (sylphs and imps) as her supernatural assistants in effecting the transformation. While the six named spirits are more closely associated with the Act 3 forest scene, the Act 1 transformation scene implies a broader household of supernatural helpers from which the six spirits may be drawn. In some productions, the spirit singers participate in the Act 1 scene, performing multiple narrative functions; in others, 'sylphes et lutins' are assigned to the ballet or to a separate chorus group. Cendrillon's transformation from servant to princess enables her appearance at the ball, her meeting with the Prince, and the entire love plot that follows. The spirits' implied role in the transformation positions them as instruments of narrative causation—the supernatural force that sets the mortal love story in motion.

Accounts II
The will-o'-the-wisp ballet (Les follets)Cendrillon, Act 3 ballet sequence (score: Jules Massenet)

1899

Act 3 of Cendrillon includes a ballet sequence featuring will-o'-the-wisps (follets)—spectral dancing lights that are a stock figure of French fairy-tale and folkloric imagination. In the score, this ballet occurs under a 'bluish light' in the enchanted forest, creating the atmospheric and visual context within which the spirit singers later participate. The will-o'-the-wisps of French folklore were traditionally souls of the unbaptized dead or mischievous nature spirits who led travelers astray in forests and marshes; Massenet and Caïn domesticate this potentially sinister figure into a benign, decorative element of fairy-tale spectacle. The will-o'-the-wisp ballet establishes the enchanted forest as a space of supernatural activity before the central love-test scene, preparing the audience emotionally and atmospherically for the lovers' encounter.

The premiere: Fouqué as the 6th spiritThéâtre National de l'Opéra-Comique, Paris, 24 May 1899; conductor Alexandre Luigini

1899

At the world premiere of Cendrillon on 24 May 1899, the role of the 6th spirit was created by a singer identified in the premiere cast list as Fouqué (or Fouquié). The premiere was a notable theatrical event: as one of the first operas staged in the newly rebuilt Salle Favart, it took advantage of modern electrical infrastructure and special stage effects. Massenet attended 60 of the 98 rehearsals but, following his usual practice, avoided the first night itself, waiting instead at Enghien-les-Bains for a telegram from bass-baritone Lucien Fugère reporting the triumph. The opera achieved fifty performances in its first season—an exceptional run. The premiere's success established Cendrillon in the operatic repertoire and secured the six spirit roles—including the 6th spirit—as a permanent feature of the opera. The premiere cast list became the canonical reference for the opera's character inventory, reproduced in all major operatic reference works.

Voice type specification for the spirit ensembleThe English Wikipedia article on Cendrillon (Massenet) lists the six spirits individually in the premiere cast table as: 1st spirit mezzo-soprano (Delorn), 2nd spirit soprano (Oswald), 3rd spirit soprano (Vilma), 4th spirit mezzo-soprano (Stéphane), 5th spirit soprano (Craponne), 6th spirit soprano (Fouqué). This assigns three sopranos and two mezzo-sopranos to the first five spirits, with the 6th as soprano—yielding four sopranos and two mezzo-sopranos total.The French Wikipedia article on the opera describes the spirit ensemble collectively as '4 soprani et 2 contralti' (four sopranos and two contraltos), while the English Wikipedia uses 'mezzo-soprano' rather than 'contralto' for the lower-voiced spirits. The term 'contralto' versus 'mezzo-soprano' reflects a genuine ambiguity in late nineteenth-century French vocal categorization, where the boundary between the two voice types was less rigidly defined than in the twentieth-century operatic tradition. The 6th spirit is soprano in both accounts, but the collective description of the ensemble varies between the two major Wikipedia language editions, reflecting differing editorial and scholarly traditions.
The 6th spirit's premiere performer name: Fouqué vs. FouquiéThe English Wikipedia article on Cendrillon (Massenet) lists the premiere performer of the 6th spirit as 'Fouqué', following the cast list reproduced in the Heugel full score and in Milnes 1998 (The New Grove Dictionary of Opera).The French Wikipedia article on the opera lists the six spirits' premiere performers collectively as 'Delorn, Oswald, Vilma, de Craponne, Stéphane et Fouquié'—rendering the 6th spirit's singer as 'Fouquié' with a terminal -ié rather than the simple -é. This discrepancy likely reflects orthographic variation between different contemporary programme books, newspaper reviews, and the published score's cast list, rather than a genuine dispute about the performer's identity. No substantial biographical record exists in major operatic reference works (Grove, Loewenberg) for a singer of either name, consistent with the opera's ensemble chorus roles being performed by non-celebrity comprimario singers.
Individual character vs. purely functional ensemble designationWikidata has assigned the 6th spirit its own discrete entity record (Q63486079), treating the character as an individual operatic character distinct from the other five spirits—each of whom presumably has or could have their own Wikidata entry. This approach implies that the six spirits have sufficient individuality (distinct voice types, distinct premiere performers, distinct ordinal identities) to merit individual scholarly tracking.In operatic performance practice and in most reference literature, the six spirits function as a single undifferentiated ensemble role: they have no individual dialogue, no individual arias, no individual dramatic arcs, and are almost universally credited as a collective ('six spirits') in reviews, production programmes, and critical commentary. StageAgent, the standard casting resource for operatic roles, lists them as a single 'Ensemble: Six Spirits' entry rather than six individual roles. The scholarly and practical convention thus resists the atomization that Wikidata's individual entity record implies, reflecting a genuine tension between bibliographic thoroughness and dramatic reality.
