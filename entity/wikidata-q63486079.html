Who It Is
Form

The 6th spirit is presented in all major productions as a luminous, ethereal female figure, costumed in the conventional visual vocabulary of nineteenth-century French supernatural theater: diaphanous robes or layered gauze in shades of silver, pale blue, or gold, designed to suggest translucency and other-worldly radiance. In the original 1899 Opéra-Comique production staged by Albert Carré—the first production to exploit the newly rebuilt Salle Favart's electrical infrastructure throughout—the spirits were likely lit from below or behind using early stage electrical effects to achieve the 'bluish light' (lumière bleuâtre) that Caïn's libretto prescribes for the enchanted forest scene. Massenet's vocal writing for the soprano spirit voices is characteristically airy and high-lying, employing the bright, translucent timbre that he associated throughout his career with supernatural female beings—a quality distinct from the warmer, more grounded sound of the mortal soprano Cendrillon or the coloratura writing of La Fée. In Laurent Pelly's celebrated production (Santa Fe 2006, Royal Opera House 2011, Met Opera 2018), the spirits inhabit a highly stylized visual world derived from the flattened perspective and bold outlines of early twentieth-century children's book illustration, appearing in exaggerated silhouette costumes that read as theatrical signifiers of 'fairyness' rather than realistic supernatural creatures. As one of six spirits forming an ensemble unit, the 6th spirit's visual identity is always defined in relation to the group—the set of six functions as a chorus of supernatural women distinguished from mortal characters by their collective costuming, movement vocabulary, and association with the magical flora of the Fairies' Oak.

Powers

Enchanted slumber induction The six spirits, acting collectively under the direction of the Fairy Godmother, possess the power to induce an enchanted sleep in mortals. In Act 3 of Cendrillon, they cause both Cendrillon and the Prince to fall into a magical slumber while surrounded by the spirits in the enchanted forest. This sleep functions as a suspended liminal state between the fairy world and the mortal world—a classic motif in European fairy-tale literature dating to Perrault and beyond, in which the enchanted sleep protects the dreamer, tests fidelity, and enables transformation. The slumber lasts for months of subjective time: Act 4 opens with Pandolfe watching over his daughter who has been asleep (or in a dream-like state) since the forest encounter.

Spatial illusion and concealment The spirits create or control a magical arbor of flowers that prevents Cendrillon and the Prince from seeing each other in the enchanted forest, while still allowing them to hear each other's voices. This selective sensory barrier—blocking sight but not sound—serves as a magical test of the lovers' bond, forcing them to recognize each other through voice alone. The ability to construct and dissolve such illusory barriers is consistent with the wider fairy-spirit tradition in French opera and ballet, where supernatural beings routinely manipulate space and perception to guide or test mortal protagonists.

Ethereal flight and supernatural locomotion As supernatural spirit beings associated with the will-o'-the-wisp figures (les follets) who appear in the Act 3 ballet sequence, the spirits are implied to possess the power of ethereal movement unbound by mortal physical constraints. The production tradition consistently stages the spirits as beings who appear, disappear, and move through the enchanted forest with a weightlessness and speed unavailable to mortal characters—whether through elaborate stage machinery, lighting effects, or (in modern productions) choreographic conventions that differentiate spirit movement from human movement.

Protective guardianship While Cendrillon and the Prince sleep in the enchanted forest, the six spirits form a protective circle or guard around them, shielding the lovers from harm and maintaining the integrity of the magical space. This protective function positions the spirits as both servants and guardians—figures who execute the Fée's will and simultaneously serve as a supernatural defense for the mortal protagonists. The protective guardianship motif connects the spirits to a long lineage of supernatural protectors in opera and fairy-tale, from the Valkyries of Norse mythology to the fairy helpers of Perrault and the Brothers Grimm.

Fairy-world singing and enchantment through music As operatic soprano voices, the spirits exercise the fundamental operatic capacity to enchant through song. Massenet employs the high soprano ensemble in the spirit scenes to create a specifically musical form of enchantment: the shimmering, transparent vocal textures of the spirit voices signal the presence of the supernatural in acoustic terms, functioning as aural hallmarks of the fairy realm just as the blue lighting and enchanted forest set signal it visually. This musico-dramatic capacity aligns with the opera's broader argument—made most explicitly in the duet 'À deux genoux'—that voice and music are the most truthful channels of identity and love, capable of overcoming even the visual barriers constructed by magic.

Accounts

Under the Fairies' Oak in an enchanted forest lit by a bluish supernatural light, the six spirits gather as the Prince and Cendrillon are drawn together by fairy power. The spirits interpose a magical arbor of flowers between the two lovers, preventing them from seeing each other. The Prince and Cendrillon nonetheless recognize each other by voice and sing of their mutual love. The arbor of flowers dissolves, the lovers are finally united in each other's arms, and the spirits surround them as they fall into an enchanted slumber. This is the emotional and dramatic climax of the opera—the moment at which the fairy world most fully intervenes in the human love story.

In Act 1, the Fairy Godmother arrives to transform Cendrillon—dressing her magnificently for the ball and warning her to return before midnight. The libretto refers to the Fée addressing 'sylphes et lutins' (sylphs and imps) as her supernatural assistants in effecting the transformation. While the six named spirits are more closely associated with the Act 3 forest scene, the Act 1 transformation scene implies a broader household of supernatural helpers from which the six spirits may be drawn. In some productions, the spirit singers participate in the Act 1 scene, performing multiple narrative functions; in others, 'sylphes et lutins' are assigned to the ballet or to a separate chorus group.

Act 3 of Cendrillon includes a ballet sequence featuring will-o'-the-wisps (follets)—spectral dancing lights that are a stock figure of French fairy-tale and folkloric imagination. In the score, this ballet occurs under a 'bluish light' in the enchanted forest, creating the atmospheric and visual context within which the spirit singers later participate. The will-o'-the-wisps of French folklore were traditionally souls of the unbaptized dead or mischievous nature spirits who led travelers astray in forests and marshes; Massenet and Caïn domesticate this potentially sinister figure into a benign, decorative element of fairy-tale spectacle.

At the world premiere of Cendrillon on 24 May 1899, the role of the 6th spirit was created by a singer identified in the premiere cast list as Fouqué (or Fouquié). The premiere was a notable theatrical event: as one of the first operas staged in the newly rebuilt Salle Favart, it took advantage of modern electrical infrastructure and special stage effects. Massenet attended 60 of the 98 rehearsals but, following his usual practice, avoided the first night itself, waiting instead at Enghien-les-Bains for a telegram from bass-baritone Lucien Fugère reporting the triumph. The opera achieved fifty performances in its first season—an exceptional run.

Scholarly Debates
Voice type specification for the spirit ensemble
The English Wikipedia article on Cendrillon (Massenet) lists the six spirits individually in the premiere cast table as: 1st spirit mezzo-soprano (Delorn), 2nd spirit soprano (Oswald), 3rd spirit soprano (Vilma), 4th spirit mezzo-soprano (Stéphane), 5th spirit soprano (Craponne), 6th spirit soprano (Fouqué). This assigns three sopranos and two mezzo-sopranos to the first five spirits, with the 6th as soprano—yielding four sopranos and two mezzo-sopranos total. ⇌ The French Wikipedia article on the opera describes the spirit ensemble collectively as '4 soprani et 2 contralti' (four sopranos and two contraltos), while the English Wikipedia uses 'mezzo-soprano' rather than 'contralto' for the lower-voiced spirits. The term 'contralto' versus 'mezzo-soprano' reflects a genuine ambiguity in late nineteenth-century French vocal categorization, where the boundary between the two voice types was less rigidly defined than in the twentieth-century operatic tradition. The 6th spirit is soprano in both accounts, but the collective description of the ensemble varies between the two major Wikipedia language editions, reflecting differing editorial and scholarly traditions.
The 6th spirit's premiere performer name: Fouqué vs. Fouquié
The English Wikipedia article on Cendrillon (Massenet) lists the premiere performer of the 6th spirit as 'Fouqué', following the cast list reproduced in the Heugel full score and in Milnes 1998 (The New Grove Dictionary of Opera). ⇌ The French Wikipedia article on the opera lists the six spirits' premiere performers collectively as 'Delorn, Oswald, Vilma, de Craponne, Stéphane et Fouquié'—rendering the 6th spirit's singer as 'Fouquié' with a terminal -ié rather than the simple -é. This discrepancy likely reflects orthographic variation between different contemporary programme books, newspaper reviews, and the published score's cast list, rather than a genuine dispute about the performer's identity. No substantial biographical record exists in major operatic reference works (Grove, Loewenberg) for a singer of either name, consistent with the opera's ensemble chorus roles being performed by non-celebrity comprimario singers.
Individual character vs. purely functional ensemble designation
Wikidata has assigned the 6th spirit its own discrete entity record (Q63486079), treating the character as an individual operatic character distinct from the other five spirits—each of whom presumably has or could have their own Wikidata entry. This approach implies that the six spirits have sufficient individuality (distinct voice types, distinct premiere performers, distinct ordinal identities) to merit individual scholarly tracking. ⇌ In operatic performance practice and in most reference literature, the six spirits function as a single undifferentiated ensemble role: they have no individual dialogue, no individual arias, no individual dramatic arcs, and are almost universally credited as a collective ('six spirits') in reviews, production programmes, and critical commentary. StageAgent, the standard casting resource for operatic roles, lists them as a single 'Ensemble: Six Spirits' entry rather than six individual roles. The scholarly and practical convention thus resists the atomization that Wikidata's individual entity record implies, reflecting a genuine tension between bibliographic thoroughness and dramatic reality.
What We Don't Know

The primary research gap is biographical: the premiere performer of the 6th spirit, listed as Fouqué or Fouquié, has left no retrievable record in the major operatic biographical reference works (Grove, Loewenberg, Wolff). The singer's full name, nationality, training, career trajectory, and subsequent roles are unknown from the sources consulted. Additionally, no libretto excerpt specifically attributing individual lines to the 6th spirit (as distinct from the spirit ensemble collectively) was located, making it impossible to determine whether any musical material in the score is individually assigned to the 6th spirit by voice or by stage direction, or whether all spirit material is purely choral-ensemble writing with no individual differentiation. Information about non-English productions of Cendrillon that might have cast notable performers as the spirit ensemble—particularly Italian, German, and Latin American productions from 1899–1910—was not recoverable from sources consulted. The Wikidata entry for the 6th spirit has no sitelinks to any Wikipedia language edition, confirming that no Wikipedia article in any language is dedicated to the character individually.

A way to hold it in your mind

Hidden in the footnotes of Massenet's most beloved fairy-tale opera is a figure who never speaks, never solos, yet stands at the precise moment the opera's magic tips from spectacle into enchantment: the 6th spirit, one of a sextet of soprano and contralto voices who inhabit the enchanted forest, raise the arbor of flowers between two lovers who can only recognize each other by voice, and then watch over them as they fall asleep in each other's arms. She is a character who exists only in the plural—inseparable from her five sisters, defined entirely by her soprano timbre and her position in an ensemble—and yet the Wikimedia community thought her worth a Wikidata entity record of her own.
