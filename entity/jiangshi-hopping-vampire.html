Who It Is

According to traditional accounts, imagine walking a mountain road at night and seeing a line of corpses, upright and rigid, bobbing in eerie unison behind a chanting priest. To some, it was a practical way to bring migrant workers home for burial; to others, it was proof that the dead could refuse to lie still. Out of that unsettling sight—and out of deep Chinese anxieties about improper death—emerged the jiangshi, the “stiff corpse” that hops, hunts, and turns ritual failure into a monster story.

In classical and folkloric accounts, jiangshi are predators of life force rather than blood: they sense the breath or qi of the living and pursue it, often at night. Some stories describe them as capable of running or even flying when sufficiently ancient or saturated with yang energy, contradicting the purely hopping image. They are drawn to improper burials, geomantic errors, and neglected corpses, functioning as embodiments of ritual failure. Powers
Qi-draining (life-force consumption)

Rather than drinking blood, the jiangshi is widely described as feeding on qi, the vital energy of the living. Victims grow weak, pale, and lifeless without obvious wounds, reflecting a cosmological rather than purely biological horror.

Limit: Requires proximity to living beings; often tied to nighttime activity and environments rich in yin energy. Strong yang forces (sunlight, fire, peachwood) can disrupt or destroy the jiangshi.

Enhanced strength and rigidity

Jiangshi possess unnatural physical strength and resilience, able to break doors, leap significant distances despite stiff limbs, and withstand ordinary blows. Their rigidity makes them difficult to grapple or restrain.

Limit: Rigidity also limits fine motor control and agility; they struggle with obstacles, stairs, and complex terrain unless portrayed as more evolved or ancient.

Sensing breath and life-force

Many descriptions note that jiangshi cannot see well but can detect the breath or exhalations of the living, homing in on respiration and body heat.

Limit: Holding one’s breath or masking qi with talismans or charms can temporarily evade detection.

Corpse evolution (age-based transformation)

Some accounts suggest that older jiangshi, or those that have absorbed significant yang energy, can evolve into more powerful forms—gaining speed, flight, or heightened intelligence.

Limit: Requires time and environmental conditions; younger or newly formed jiangshi are comparatively weak and limited to hopping or slow movement.

Taoist control and immobilization

Taoist priests can immobilize or direct jiangshi using talismans, incantations, and ritual implements, turning them into controlled undead or neutralizing their threat.

Limit: Requires ritual expertise, correct symbols, and sufficient yang energy; talismans can be removed or disrupted, releasing the jiangshi.

Vulnerabilities to sunlight and yang forces

Sunlight, fire, peachwood, rooster crows, and certain charms embody yang energy that disperses the yin accumulation sustaining the jiangshi, weakening or destroying it.

Limit: Effectiveness varies by narrative; some stories require direct sunlight or specific ritual timing.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Attested descriptions in folklore and scholarship emphasize rigidity: the jiangshi is a corpse whose limbs cannot bend, moving with stiff, jerking motions. In many accounts it is pale or bluish, with long, claw-like nails and sometimes fangs—features associated with corpse decay and the accumulation of yin energy. Modern Hong Kong cinema codifies a highly recognizable visual: a Qing-dynasty official’s robe (often blue-green), round hat, and a yel

Symbols — Key symbols include the peachwood sword (a Taoist implement of yang power), yellow talismans inscribed with characters or diagrams to immobilize or control the corpse, Bagua (八卦) charms representing cosmic order, mirrors and rooster crows as agents of dispersing yin, and sunlight as an ultimate purifier. The Qing official robe symbolizes both bureaucratic authority and historical distance, turning the jiangshi into a kind of undead civil servant

Domains — Jiangshi are strongly associated with liminal spaces of death and transition: graveyards, ancestral halls, abandoned houses, and mountain paths used for corpse-driving. They rest in coffins or secluded rooms during daylight, emerging at night when yin energy is strongest. Accounts I
The Lightning-Struck CorpseYuan Mei’s Zi Bu Yu (子不語), as paraphrased in modern mythological essays

late 18th century

A corpse, improperly buried and left exposed, is struck by lightning during a storm. Instead of being destroyed, it reanimates, its body described as “stiff as wood,” moving forward in jerky hops. It begins to wander, draining the vitality of those it encounters until a Taoist priest intervenes, using talismans and peachwood to immobilize it and perform proper burial rites. The tale reinforces the danger of neglecting burial and the necessity of ritual specialists; the community’s suffering is directly linked to ritual failure.

Corpse-Driving in Western HunanEthnographic and folkloric accounts of gan shi (赶尸) traditions

19th–20th century record of earlier practices

Taoist corpse-drivers transport the bodies of migrant laborers back to their home villages for burial. Corpses are bound upright to bamboo poles and carried at night along mountain paths. To observers, especially from a distance, the procession appears as a line of hopping corpses led by a priest. Taboos warn against crossing the path or mocking the dead, lest misfortune or spiritual retribution follow. The practice both alleviates families’ anxiety about distant death and fuels the imagination of animated corpses, contributing to the visual template of the jiangshi.

Accounts II
Mr. Vampire (殭屍先生)Hong Kong film, Mr. Vampire (1985) and its sequels

1980s–early 1990s

A Taoist priest and his disciples confront jiangshi that have arisen through ritual mishaps and malevolent forces. The films blend horror and slapstick comedy: hopping corpses in Qing official garb are controlled, immobilized, or fought using talismans, peachwood swords, and martial arts. The series codifies many modern conventions of the jiangshi—forehead talismans, hopping movement, breath detection—and embeds them in a narrative of ritual expertise versus supernatural chaos. Mr. Vampire becomes the definitive cinematic representation of jiangshi, shaping global perception and inspiring later films, television, and games.

Rigor MortisHong Kong film Rigor Mortis (2013)

early 21st century

Set in a decaying apartment block, Rigor Mortis revisits jiangshi imagery as part of a melancholic, meta-cinematic reflection on the genre. Undead beings, talismans, and Taoist rituals appear, but the film foregrounds trauma, nostalgia, and the fading of traditional superstition under modern pressures. The film functions as a “love letter” to earlier jiangshi cinema, acknowledging both its cultural impact and its marginalization under contemporary censorship and rationalist ideology.
