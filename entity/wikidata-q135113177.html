Who It Is

In the beginning, there was a jewel — or was there? Amatsukunitama, the 'Jewel of the Heavenly Land,' is a name that glitters with promise, but the deity behind it is one of the quietest figures in Japanese mythology. He is a functionary, a member of the divine entourage that escorted the sun goddess's grandson down from the heavens to claim the earthly realm. He has no epic adventures, no dramatic battles, no love affairs. He is, in the most literal sense, a supporting character in the greatest origin story of the Japanese imperial line. Yet his name — a name that fuses heaven, land, and jewel — has sparked centuries of debate. Is he the spirit of the sacred jewel that became one of the three imperial regalia? A personification of the celestial realm itself? Or simply a name that happened to fit the myth? The answer, like the jewel itself, remains hidden.

The deity's behavior is not described in any narrative detail. In the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki, Amatsukunitama is a functional figure — a member of the divine retinue assigned a specific task. The deity does not speak, act independently, or feature in any dramatic episode.

Divine Chieftaincy (Otomo no Muraji)

Amatsukunitama is one of the five chieftains (五人, itsutomo) of the heavenly deities. This is a military-administrative role, implying authority over the celestial retinue and the power to command other kami.

Limit: The role is functional and subordinate to the higher celestial authority (Amaterasu and the central deities). The deity does not wield independent cosmic power in the texts.

Jewel/Spirit Manifestation (Tama no Chikara)

The name 'tama' suggests a power associated with sacred jewels or spiritual essence. In Shinto, 'tama' can be a repository of spiritual power (mitama). The deity may embody the power of the sacred jewel as a source of divine authority.

Limit: This is an interpretive reading, not an attested power. The texts do not describe the deity using jewel-based powers.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No physical description exists in the primary sources. The name suggests a jewel-like or spirit-like form, but this is inference. In later artistic and ritual representations, the deity is often depicted as a humanoid kami in courtly dress, holding a jewel or orb, but this is a later artistic convention, not an attested description.

Symbols — The jewel (tama) is the primary symbolic association. The name itself is the symbol. The deity is also associated with the Yasakani no Magatama (the curved jewel of the imperial regalia), though the direct identification is a matter of debate. In ritual contexts, the deity would be associated with the sacred treasures (shinpō) of the imperial house.

Domains — Takamagahara (the High Plain of Heaven) — the celestial realm of the kami. The deity's name suggests a specific dominion over the 'heavenly land' (amatsu kuni), possibly as a tutelary spirit or territorial guardian of the celestial realm itself. In the context of the descent myth, the deity's role is to facilitate the transfer of divine authority from heaven to earth.

Accounts I
The Descent of the Heavenly Grandson (Tenson Kōrin)Kojiki, Book 1; Nihon Shoki, Book 2

Mythological age (traditionally dated to the age of the gods, before the founding of the imperial line)

Amaterasu, the sun goddess, decides to send her grandson Ninigi-no-Mikoto to rule the earthly realm (Ashihara no Nakatsukuni). Before the descent, she convenes a council of the heavenly deities and chooses a retinue to accompany Ninigi. Amatsukunitama is one of the five chieftains (otomo no muraji) appointed to lead the expedition. The deity, along with the other chieftains, is tasked with clearing the way, establishing order, and serving as the vanguard of the heavenly mandate. The descent party also includes the ancestral kami of the major clans (the five chieftains correspond to the ancestors of the Otomo, Kume, Mononobe, and other powerful clans). The descent marks the transfer of divine authority from the celestial realm to the earthly realm and establishes the legitimacy of the imperial line. The descent establishes the imperial lineage on earth. The five chieftains become the ancestors of the major courtly clans, giving them a divine mandate and a place in the ritual hierarchy of the imperial court. Amatsukunitama is the god of the imperial regalia. — The deity's name includes 'tama' (jewel), and the deity appears in the regalia myth, but the texts do not explicitly identify the deity as the guardian or personification of the physical regalia. This is an interpretation, not an attested fact.

The deity is a major god with a rich mythology. — Amatsukunitama is a minor functional figure in the descent myth. The deity has no individual stories, no personality, and no cult of its own. The deity's importance is structural (as one of the five chieftains) rather than narrative.

The deity is the same as Kunitokotachi or other 'earth' kami. — Kunitokotachi is a primordial earth deity who appears in the creation myth. Amatsukunitama is a celestial deity associated with the descent myth. They are distinct entities with different roles and different textual contexts.
