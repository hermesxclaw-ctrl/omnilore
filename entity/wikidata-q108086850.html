Who It Is

On moonless nights in the Meitei hamlet, you were told not to answer the voice at the wall — a voice that asked, politely, if your husband was home, while a hand as long as a tree branch slid through the thatch, fingers sharp as thorns, feeling for the sleeping child inside. She is Lai Khutsangbi, the Long-Handed Demoness, and her hands touch the ground when she walks.

Behavior is predatory, nocturnal, and cunning but ultimately outwittable. Attested pattern: she haunts hamlet peripheries near forest, steals and eats livestock and human babies, exploits isolation and loneliness, sows terror. She is not a mindless beast; she speaks, using a ruse of polite interrogation ('Is your husband at home?') repeated nightly, testing vulnerability. She fears adult male presence (Shasipa's bravery) and retreats when told he is home, indicating a taboo or vulnerability linked to patriarchal protection. Powers
Elongated Arms / Long-Handed Reach

Arms so long they touch/drag on the ground while walking; can extend through walls and into houses to seize children/livestock from outside.

Limit: Hands are vulnerable to sharp iron weapons; severance disables her. Reach requires proximity to house wall; she must ask/interrogate before entering, suggesting a boundary taboo.

Wall-Breaking Strength

Can break through wattle-and-daub / bamboo wall of a Meitei house with a single hand/ arm thrust.

Limit: Requires physical contact; wall is traditional hamlet construction, not masonry or fortified structure. Fails when arm is severed.

Thorn-Sharp Fingers/Nails

Fingers/nails as sharp as thorns, used to grasp, tear, and presumably kill prey.

Limit: Not described as projectile or poisoned; purely mechanical sharpness.

Nocturnal Stealth and Speech

Moves silently at midnight, speaks intelligible Meiteilon to interrogate household; uses social deception to assess vulnerability.

Limit: Speech is limited to this interrogation formula in recorded tales; no broader shapeshifting or illusion. She is deceived by the mother's lie, indicating limited discernment.

Predation on Infants and Livestock

Habitual theft and consumption of domestic animals and human babies; implied anthropophagy.

Limit: Prefers isolated, unprotected victims; deterred by presence of brave adult male. No attested ability to prey on armed groups.

Aetiological Blood Marking

Blood from severed arms permanently stains foliage, explaining red spots/patches on certain forest plants as her blood.

Limit: Folk aetiology, not a power she controls; passive consequence of injury. No attested healing or regeneration.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Attested core iconography is consistent across oral variants: an adult female figure of terrifying aspect, humanoid but monstrous. Her defining trait is enormously elongated arms/hands (khut sangbi) that hang to or drag on the ground when she walks. Fingers are described as sharp as thorns (Meitei: thorn-like nails), implying both weapon and predatory tool. She has a disproportionately large mouth with a long, protruding tongue, suggesting voraci

Symbols — No traditional yantra, weapon, vahana, or ritual icon. Domains — Domain is the ecotone between human settlement and wild forest. She dwells in the forest (umang) adjacent to an isolated hamlet/house, emerging at midnight to prowl village margins. Her habitat is not a palace, cave, or underworld but the undifferentiated forest and its edge. Time domain is night, especially midnight. Accounts I
The Nightly Interrogation — Shasi, Shasima and Shasipa and the Severed Hands (Core Legend)Meitei oral folktale, anthologized as 'Lai Khutshangbi' in Loitongbam Kalachand Singh, Folktales of Manipur (1980) and widely reproduced on E-pao.net and Manipuri folklore blogs

Oral tradition of indeterminate antiquity; written fixation 20th century (Hodson 1908 attests hingchabi type; tale anthologized 1980s)

In an isolated hamlet at the forest edge lives a couple with a small child Shasi (variants: Shachi/Leirik/Naocha). Nearby dwells Lai Khutsangbi, who preys on livestock and babies. She covets Shasi but fears the brave father Shasipa. When Shasipa leaves for days to work far away, Lai Khutsangbi comes at midnight and asks the mother Shasima, 'Is your husband at home?' Shasima, wise, lies that he is home, and the demoness leaves. She returns night after night with the same question, receiving the same lie. When Shasipa returns, Shasima tells him. He sharpens a sword and waits. The next midnight, Shasima truthfully says her husband is away. Lai Khutsangbi, emboldened, smashes through the house wall with one long hand to seize Shasi. Shasipa, hidden, chops off that arm with his sword. The demoness screams and flees, dragging her remaining arm, blood spurting onto plants along the forest path. First mutilation of the demoness; creation of blood-stained plants aetiology; demonstration of maternal cunning and paternal martial protection as apotropaic model.

The Blood on the Leaves — Aetiological CodaSame oral cycle, coda to core legend; reproduced in E-pao and blog retellings

Oral; 20th-century anthologization

As Lai Khutsangbi flees after her first hand is cut, blood pours from the stump and falls onto many plants along her trail into the woods. Elders say the reddish patches, spots, or streaks seen on certain leaves (often pointed out to children on forest walks) are her blood and will never wash away. Shasipa follows this blood trail to find her. Folk botanical explanation; moral mnemonic for children to recognize and remember the tale when seeing spotted leaves; reinforces that supernatural violence leaves permanent marks on landscape.

The Pursuit and the Second Amputation — BanishmentContinuation of core legend, Kalachand anthology and E-pao summary

Oral; 20th-century written

Wounded, Lai Khutsangbi cannot run as fast as usual. Shasipa follows the blood trail into the forest, catches her, steps on her remaining long hand to pin it, and chops off the second arm with his sword. She screams and escapes or is left incapacitated. The villagers thank Shasipa for his bravery. From that day, Lai Khutsangbi is never seen again in that hamlet/region. Permanent local banishment of the hingchabi; restoration of community safety; establishment of Shasipa as folk hero; tale functions as warning that courage and family cohesion defeat predatory spirits.

Accounts II
The Livestock and Baby Thief of the Hamlet (General Predation Pattern)Generic hingchabi lore summarized in Hodson (1908) and Parratt (1980) and as prologue to Lai Khutsangbi tale

Traditional belief pattern, colonial ethnographic attestation 1908

Before the Shasi episode, Lai Khutsangbi is described as a habitual night predator who steals and eats livestock (chickens, pigs, cattle) and human babies from the village, taking advantage of loneliness and isolated houses. She sows terror so that villagers fear to be alone or leave children unattended. This establishes her as an ongoing ecological threat rather than a one-time monster. Explains why the hamlet lives in fear; justifies the later collective relief; typifies hingchabi behavior in Meitei demonology (female spirits preying on fertility and domestic wealth).

The Wise Mother's Deception — Shasima's Trick (Variant Focus)Oral variant emphasizing maternal cunning; highlighted in feminist reading 'Lai Khutsangbi, female power and punishment' (E-pao) and multiple blog retellings

Oral; contemporary analysis 2010s

In this telling, the dramatic focus shifts to Shasima, who, left alone with an infant for many nights, repeatedly outwits a supernatural predator through calm lying. Each night she answers 'He is at home' without hesitation, despite terror, buying time until her husband returns. Her wisdom (not just her husband's sword) is credited with saving the child. Some tellers have her whisper to the child to stay silent while she speaks to the voice at the wall. Elevates female cleverness as apotropaic; used in modern moral retellings to teach children that wit can complement strength; cited in gendered readings of the tale.

Modern Didactic Retelling — Leirik/Naocha Variant for ChildrenChildren's illustrated folklore books and YouTube animations (Manipuri language channels: Mami Taibang, Manipuri Folktales, etc., 2015-present) and school folklore compilations

2010s-2020s

Retold for children as a cautionary tale: parents warn not to open doors to strangers at night, not to stay alone, and to trust parental protection. The child is called Leirik or Naocha (more familiar modern Meitei names), the demoness is rendered in cartoon style with comically long arms, and the gore is softened (blood shown as red spots, amputation implied off-screen). Ends with explicit moral: 'Listen to your parents and do not be alone at night.' Transmission of folklore to urban/diaspora Meitei children; sanitization of violence; visual canon formation via animation; tale becomes part of Meitei children's media ecology.

Lai Khutsangbi is a Meitei goddess who is worshipped. — She is a hingchabi — a malevolent female spirit/demoness in Meitei folklore, not a worshipped deity. Lai means spirit/divinity broadly, not 'goddess' in the Hindu sense. Unlike Umang Lai who have sacred groves (umang) and Lai Haraoba rituals, Lai Khutsangbi has no shrine, offerings, or worship.

The story comes from ancient Kangleipak scriptures or the Moirang Kanglei epic. — No pre-colonial manuscript (Puya) or Moirang Kanglei text attests Lai Khutsangbi. She belongs to oral folk narrative (phungawari) first anthologized in 20th-century folklore collections. Hodson 1908 attests the hingchabi type, but not this specific tale in manuscript form.

Her long hands are a unique superpower and she can stretch them infinitely. — Oral description is 'hands touch the ground when she walks' — elongated but not infinitely elastic. Wall-breaking is via reaching through/breaking wall, not cartoonish stretching across villages. Modern animations exaggerate for visual effect.

The red patches on leaves are literally her blood and prove the story is true. — This is a folk aetiology — a narrative explanation for natural reddish spots on certain plants (anthocyanin, fungal spots, etc.). It is a mnemonic and moral device, not botanical fact. No single species is universally identified.

Lai Khutsangbi is the same as the pan-Indian Churel/Pichal Peri or a Hindu Rakshasi. — Functional similarity (female child-stealing spirit) does not equal identity. Lai Khutsangbi is specifically Meitei, embedded in Sanamahi cosmology, Meitei language, and Manipur valley settlement patterns. Direct equation erases cultural specificity.
