The Approach

On moonless nights in the Meitei hamlet, you were told not to answer the voice at the wall — a voice that asked, politely, if your husband was home, while a hand as long as a tree branch slid through the thatch, fingers sharp as thorns, feeling for the sleeping child inside. She is Lai Khutsangbi, the Long-Handed Demoness, and her hands touch the ground when she walks.

Content advisory. Content advisory: attempted child abduction and implied child cannibalism/infanticide, predation on infants and livestock, graphic bodily mutilation (amputation of arms), blood and gore, home invasion, terrorization of a lone mother and child. Folklore also contains gendered demonization of female figures.
Identity

Lai Khutsangbi

Pronunciation Manipuri: /laːi kʰut ʃaːŋ.bi/ — commonly anglicized as /laai-khoot-shaang-bee/ (three syllables in Lai, two in Khut, two in Sangbi). Meitei Mayek: ꯂꯥꯏ ꯈꯨꯠꯁꯥꯡꯕꯤ. Bengali-Assamese script rendering: লাই খুৎশাংবী.

Known As

Lai KhutshangbiLai Khut SangbiLai Khut ShangbiLaai Khut ShangbiLong-handed Demoness

Etymology

Meitei language (Manipuri / Meiteilon, Kanglei): Lai = deity, divinity, spirit or supernatural being (in Sanamahism, Lai encompasses both benevolent Umang Lai and malevolent spirits; not exclusively 'god'); Khut = hand, arm; Sangbi / Shangbi = long. Thus 'Long-handed Lai' or 'Long-handed Spirit.' The medial -sh- vs -s- reflects transliteration variation between Meitei Mayek romanization and Bengali-script-derived orthography. Hingchabi is the specific Meitei category for female demon / ogress / witch-like malevolent spirit, distinct from Lai in general.

Cultural Ownership

Meitei people of Kangleipak (Antique Kangleipak / Ancient Manipur, present-day Manipur valley, Northeast India). Belongs to Meitei folklore and Sanamahi religious worldview. The story is part of the hingchabi / helloi corpus of domestic and forest spirits. Custodianship lies with Meitei oral tradition bearers, folklore scholars, and cultural institutions in Manipur. Not a pan-Hindu or pan-Indian figure; should not be conflated with Sanskrit Rakshasi or generic 'witch' without Meitei context.

Disputed Identifications

1) Lai vs Hingchabi: Lai is a broad term for spirit/deity; Lai Khutsangbi is specifically a hingchabi (malevolent female spirit), not a worshipped Umang Lai. Popular English glosses calling her a 'goddess' are inaccurate. 2) Distinct from other named Meitei hingchabi/helloi such as Tapta, Phouoibi-related ogresses, or generic Lam Lai. 3) Not to be confused with the unrelated Bengali folk figure 'Petni' or pan-Indian 'Churel' despite functional similarity. 4) Spelling variants Khutshangbi/Khutsangbi/Sangbi/Shangbi all refer to same entity; no evidence of two separate long-handed figures. 5) No attested connection to the Moirang Kanglei epic cycle (Khamba-Thoibi) despite occasional misattribution on SEO sites.

Description

Appearance

Attested core iconography is consistent across oral variants: an adult female figure of terrifying aspect, humanoid but monstrous. Her defining trait is enormously elongated arms/hands (khut sangbi) that hang to or drag on the ground when she walks. Fingers are described as sharp as thorns (Meitei: thorn-like nails), implying both weapon and predatory tool. She has a disproportionately large mouth with a long, protruding tongue, suggesting voracity and child/livestock consumption. Skin/face is not detailed in most variants beyond 'very frightening' — no canonical color, hair, or clothing is attested; some modern illustrations add disheveled hair, elongated limbs, and ragged dark clothing, but these are adaptation-specific inventions. No traditional sculpture, mask, or painting tradition for Lai Khutsangbi is documented; she exists primarily as a narrated bogey figure rather than an iconographically fixed deity. Modern digital art consistently exaggerates arm length to 2-3x body height. Status: long hands, thorn fingers, large mouth/long tongue = attested; other details = reconstructed or adaptation-specific.

Symbols & Iconography

No traditional yantra, weapon, vahana, or ritual icon. Symbolic associations are narrative rather than visual: 1) The Long Hand = reach across domestic boundary (wall-breaking), violation of home safety, predation on isolated households. 2) Blood on foliage = aetiological marker; red patches/spots on certain forest plants (often identified locally as specific aroids or leaved shrubs with reddish spots, not botanically fixed) are folk-explained as her spilled blood. 3) Night and forest edge = liminal space between village (khun) and forest (umang). Unlike Umang Lai who have sacred groves and ritual offerings, Lai Khutsangbi has no shrine, no offering, no possession ritual; she is apotropaic, a figure to be warded off, not worshipped. No attested mantra, sigil, or amulet specific to her beyond general hingchabi warding (iron, fire, brave male protector motif).

Behaviour & Personality

Behavior is predatory, nocturnal, and cunning but ultimately outwittable. Attested pattern: she haunts hamlet peripheries near forest, steals and eats livestock and human babies, exploits isolation and loneliness, sows terror. She is not a mindless beast; she speaks, using a ruse of polite interrogation ('Is your husband at home?') repeated nightly, testing vulnerability. She fears adult male presence (Shasipa's bravery) and retreats when told he is home, indicating a taboo or vulnerability linked to patriarchal protection. She is persistent (returns night after night) and physically powerful (breaks through house wall with one hand). Personality is not elaborated as tragic or sympathetic in traditional versions; she is a straightforward malevolent hingchabi. No attested motive beyond hunger/predation; no backstory of cursed woman or transformation in core variants (some modern feminist retellings reconstruct a backstory, but this is adaptation-specific and unattested in folklore anthologies). After double amputation she flees and is never seen again in that locality — a local banishment, not a cosmic death.

Domains & Habitats

Domain is the ecotone between human settlement and wild forest. She dwells in the forest (umang) adjacent to an isolated hamlet/house, emerging at midnight to prowl village margins. Her habitat is not a palace, cave, or underworld but the undifferentiated forest and its edge. Time domain is night, especially midnight. Social domain is the vulnerable household: specifically a house with a small child and an absent husband, representing a household without male protection in traditional Meitei social logic. She does not rule a realm, command lesser spirits, or inhabit water/celestial realms. Cosmologically she belongs to the category of earth-bound malevolent spirits (hingchabi/helloi) in Sanamahi cosmology, distinct from celestial Lai or ancestral spirits. No attested association with specific hill, river, or sacred grove.

Version Differences

Spelling and naming: Lai Khutshangbi vs Lai Khutsangbi vs Lai Khut Sangbi; Sangbi/Shangbi interchangeable. Child's name varies systematically: Shasi / Shachi / Leirik / Naocha (Leirik and Naocha are common Meitei child names, suggesting localization). Father/mother are Shasipa/Shasima (Meitei -pa/-ma suffixes for father/mother) in most versions; some shorten to 'the father'/'the mother'. Sequence of deception: in all versions mother lies that husband is home for several nights; father returns and plans ambush; final night mother tells truth ('he is away') as bait. Hand severance: most versions have two-stage amputation — first hand cut when breaking wall, second hand cut after pursuit and stepping on it; some compress to single amputation. Blood aetiology: some versions say blood fell on 'many plants' causing red patches; others specify particular leaves (e.g., Colocasia or wild aroids). Fate: most end with permanent disappearance/banishment; none describe her death definitively, leaving open 'never seen again' rather than killed. No version gives her a human origin story; modern retellings sometimes invent one.

Powers & Abilities

Elongated Arms / Long-Handed Reach

Source VersionAttested in all oral variants and folklore anthologies (Kalachand, E-pao summaries)

Arms so long they touch/drag on the ground while walking; can extend through walls and into houses to seize children/livestock from outside.

Limitations

Hands are vulnerable to sharp iron weapons; severance disables her. Reach requires proximity to house wall; she must ask/interrogate before entering, suggesting a boundary taboo.

Contradictions

Exact length not quantified; modern art exaggerates to inhuman proportions vs oral 'touch the ground' which is relative to stature.

Wall-Breaking Strength

Source VersionCore Shasi episode - attested

Can break through wattle-and-daub / bamboo wall of a Meitei house with a single hand/ arm thrust.

Limitations

Requires physical contact; wall is traditional hamlet construction, not masonry or fortified structure. Fails when arm is severed.

Contradictions

None; consistently described as powerful but not supernaturally invulnerable.

Thorn-Sharp Fingers/Nails

Source VersionAttested description in all variants

Fingers/nails as sharp as thorns, used to grasp, tear, and presumably kill prey.

Limitations

Not described as projectile or poisoned; purely mechanical sharpness.

Contradictions

Some English summaries say 'fingers are thorns' vs 'fingers sharp as thorns' - metaphor vs literal.

Nocturnal Stealth and Speech

Source VersionAttested - nightly question 'Is your husband at home?'

Moves silently at midnight, speaks intelligible Meiteilon to interrogate household; uses social deception to assess vulnerability.

Limitations

Speech is limited to this interrogation formula in recorded tales; no broader shapeshifting or illusion. She is deceived by the mother's lie, indicating limited discernment.

Contradictions

Some modern retellings give her broader dialogue; traditional corpus limits to the single question.

Predation on Infants and Livestock

Source VersionAttested general behavior - described as stealing and eating livestock and babies

Habitual theft and consumption of domestic animals and human babies; implied anthropophagy.

Limitations

Prefers isolated, unprotected victims; deterred by presence of brave adult male. No attested ability to prey on armed groups.

Contradictions

Whether she eats raw or cooks is never specified; 'eating' may be euphemistic for killing.

Aetiological Blood Marking

Source VersionAttested coda to Shasi tale

Blood from severed arms permanently stains foliage, explaining red spots/patches on certain forest plants as her blood.

Limitations

Folk aetiology, not a power she controls; passive consequence of injury. No attested healing or regeneration.

Contradictions

Botanical identification of 'plants' varies by teller; no single species is canonically fixed.

Stories & Myths

The Nightly Interrogation — Shasi, Shasima and Shasipa and the Severed Hands (Core Legend)

Source Meitei oral folktale, anthologized as 'Lai Khutshangbi' in Loitongbam Kalachand Singh, Folktales of Manipur (1980) and widely reproduced on E-pao.net and Manipuri folklore blogsCulture Meitei (Kangleipak/Manipur valley, Sanamahism)Period Oral tradition of indeterminate antiquity; written fixation 20th century (Hodson 1908 attests hingchabi type; tale anthologized 1980s)

Summary

In an isolated hamlet at the forest edge lives a couple with a small child Shasi (variants: Shachi/Leirik/Naocha). Nearby dwells Lai Khutsangbi, who preys on livestock and babies. She covets Shasi but fears the brave father Shasipa. When Shasipa leaves for days to work far away, Lai Khutsangbi comes at midnight and asks the mother Shasima, 'Is your husband at home?' Shasima, wise, lies that he is home, and the demoness leaves. She returns night after night with the same question, receiving the same lie. When Shasipa returns, Shasima tells him. He sharpens a sword and waits. The next midnight, Shasima truthfully says her husband is away. Lai Khutsangbi, emboldened, smashes through the house wall with one long hand to seize Shasi. Shasipa, hidden, chops off that arm with his sword. The demoness screams and flees, dragging her remaining arm, blood spurting onto plants along the forest path.

Participants

Lai Khutsangbi (hingchabi demoness)

Shasi / Shachi / Leirik / Naocha (infant/child)

Shasima (mother)

Shasipa (father, brave swordsman)

Consequences

First mutilation of the demoness; creation of blood-stained plants aetiology; demonstration of maternal cunning and paternal martial protection as apotropaic model.

Variants

Child's name varies by village/teller; some versions have mother hiding child in basket; some have father hiding behind door vs inside house. Core interrogation and single-hand severance stable.

The Blood on the Leaves — Aetiological Coda

Source Same oral cycle, coda to core legend; reproduced in E-pao and blog retellingsCulture MeiteiPeriod Oral; 20th-century anthologization

Summary

As Lai Khutsangbi flees after her first hand is cut, blood pours from the stump and falls onto many plants along her trail into the woods. Elders say the reddish patches, spots, or streaks seen on certain leaves (often pointed out to children on forest walks) are her blood and will never wash away. Shasipa follows this blood trail to find her.

Participants

Lai Khutsangbi

Shasipa

Forest plants/foliage (aetiological object)

Consequences

Folk botanical explanation; moral mnemonic for children to recognize and remember the tale when seeing spotted leaves; reinforces that supernatural violence leaves permanent marks on landscape.

Variants

Some tellers name specific plants (taro/colocasia, wild aroids, or shrubs with red midribs); others leave as 'many plants.' Some say blood fell only on one species.

The Pursuit and the Second Amputation — Banishment

Source Continuation of core legend, Kalachand anthology and E-pao summaryCulture MeiteiPeriod Oral; 20th-century written

Summary

Wounded, Lai Khutsangbi cannot run as fast as usual. Shasipa follows the blood trail into the forest, catches her, steps on her remaining long hand to pin it, and chops off the second arm with his sword. She screams and escapes or is left incapacitated. The villagers thank Shasipa for his bravery. From that day, Lai Khutsangbi is never seen again in that hamlet/region.

Participants

Lai Khutsangbi

Shasipa

Villagers/hamlet community

Consequences

Permanent local banishment of the hingchabi; restoration of community safety; establishment of Shasipa as folk hero; tale functions as warning that courage and family cohesion defeat predatory spirits.

Variants

Some versions end after first amputation with flight; others include second amputation. Some say she dies in forest, others that she survives armless and flees deeper into woods. 'Never seen again' is stable.

The Livestock and Baby Thief of the Hamlet (General Predation Pattern)

Source Generic hingchabi lore summarized in Hodson (1908) and Parratt (1980) and as prologue to Lai Khutsangbi taleCulture MeiteiPeriod Traditional belief pattern, colonial ethnographic attestation 1908

Summary

Before the Shasi episode, Lai Khutsangbi is described as a habitual night predator who steals and eats livestock (chickens, pigs, cattle) and human babies from the village, taking advantage of loneliness and isolated houses. She sows terror so that villagers fear to be alone or leave children unattended. This establishes her as an ongoing ecological threat rather than a one-time monster.

Participants

Lai Khutsangbi

Villagers, livestock, infants (generic victims)

Forest as dwelling

Consequences

Explains why the hamlet lives in fear; justifies the later collective relief; typifies hingchabi behavior in Meitei demonology (female spirits preying on fertility and domestic wealth).

Variants

Some retellings omit livestock and focus only on child predation; some add that she imitates a relative's voice.

The Wise Mother's Deception — Shasima's Trick (Variant Focus)

Source Oral variant emphasizing maternal cunning; highlighted in feminist reading 'Lai Khutsangbi, female power and punishment' (E-pao) and multiple blog retellingsCulture MeiteiPeriod Oral; contemporary analysis 2010s

Summary

In this telling, the dramatic focus shifts to Shasima, who, left alone with an infant for many nights, repeatedly outwits a supernatural predator through calm lying. Each night she answers 'He is at home' without hesitation, despite terror, buying time until her husband returns. Her wisdom (not just her husband's sword) is credited with saving the child. Some tellers have her whisper to the child to stay silent while she speaks to the voice at the wall.

Participants

Shasima (mother)

Lai Khutsangbi

Shasi (silent child)

Consequences

Elevates female cleverness as apotropaic; used in modern moral retellings to teach children that wit can complement strength; cited in gendered readings of the tale.

Variants

Number of nights varies: three nights, seven nights, or 'many nights.' Some versions have mother also placing an iron dao near the door.

Modern Didactic Retelling — Leirik/Naocha Variant for Children

Source Children's illustrated folklore books and YouTube animations (Manipuri language channels: Mami Taibang, Manipuri Folktales, etc., 2015-present) and school folklore compilationsCulture Meitei, contemporary Manipuri popular culturePeriod 2010s-2020s

Summary

Retold for children as a cautionary tale: parents warn not to open doors to strangers at night, not to stay alone, and to trust parental protection. The child is called Leirik or Naocha (more familiar modern Meitei names), the demoness is rendered in cartoon style with comically long arms, and the gore is softened (blood shown as red spots, amputation implied off-screen). Ends with explicit moral: 'Listen to your parents and do not be alone at night.'

Participants

Lai Khutsangbi (cartoon demoness)

Leirik/Naocha (child)

Parents

Consequences

Transmission of folklore to urban/diaspora Meitei children; sanitization of violence; visual canon formation via animation; tale becomes part of Meitei children's media ecology.

Variants

YouTube versions vary in animation style and whether second amputation is shown; some omit blood aetiology for age appropriateness.

Relationships
Lai Khutsangbi→Shasi / Shachi / Leirik / Naocha (the child)victim

Intended prey; Lai Khutsangbi especially wants to eat this specific infant/child, representing vulnerability and domestic fertility. The child is silent/passive in most tellings, an object of predation and protection. No kinship to demoness.

Lai Khutsangbi→Shasima (the mother)hostile

Adult female antagonist to Lai Khutsangbi; she thwarts the demoness through deception, lying that husband is home for multiple nights. Represents wise motherhood and verbal cunning as defense against supernatural predation. Lai Khutsangbi interrogates her nightly at the house wall.

Lai Khutsangbi→Shasipa (the father)hostile

Brave male protector and slayer; Lai Khutsangbi fears him and only attacks when she believes he is absent. He ambushes her with a sharpened sword (Meitei dao/thang), severs first hand through wall, pursues via blood trail, pins remaining hand with foot and severs second hand. Embodies patriarchal martial protection in Meitei folk logic.

Lai Khutsangbi→Hingchabi / Helloi (class of Meitei female malevolent spirits)kin

Lai Khutsangbi is a member/type of hingchabi (also called helloi in some dialects), a category of female demonesses/ogresses/witches in Meitei cosmology. She shares traits with other hingchabi: nocturnal, forest-dwelling, child-stealing, long limbs or shapeshifting, fear of iron and brave men. Not a unique species but an exemplar.

Lai Khutsangbi→Hamlet / Village communityvictim

Collective victim and beneficiary; Lai Khutsangbi terrorizes the hamlet by stealing livestock and babies, sowing fear, especially among isolated households. After her mutilation/banishment, villagers thank Shasipa and live without fear. Community is both prey and audience for moral.

Lai Khutsangbi→Forest (Umang) and its plantshabitat

Dwelling place and refuge; forest is her home and escape route. Plants along the trail become permanent record of her blood — red patches on leaves are folk-explained as her blood. Relationship is both habitat and aetiological canvas.

Lai Khutsangbi→Livestock and generic infants (previous victims)victim

Prior prey before Shasi episode; establishes pattern of predation on domestic wealth and fertility. She 'usually stole and ate livestock and human babies' before targeting Shasi specifically.

Child Abduction and Implied Cannibalism / Infanticide

Source Core legend prologue and Shasi episode; hingchabi typology in Hodson and Parratt

Description

Lai Khutsangbi's defining predation is stealing and eating human babies and small children, with Shasi as specific intended victim. The Meitei term 'eating' (cha-) in hingchabi lore implies killing and consumption, not merely kidnapping. This reflects deep cultural anxiety about infant mortality and vulnerability in isolated households.

Scholarly Context

In Meitei folklore, hingchabi often embody anxieties about fertility, child survival, and maternal failure. The tale externalizes infant death risk as a monster that can be warded by maternal vigilance and paternal protection. No sanitization is appropriate; modern children's retellings soften but traditional tellings are explicit about eating.

Warning: Gendered Demonization and Female Power / Punishment — Show unsanitized · Archive policy & legal

Gendered Demonization and Female Power / Punishment

Source Oral tradition; E-pao feminist reading; Parratt on gender in Meitei spirit taxonomy

Description

As a hingchabi, Lai Khutsangbi is a female-coded malevolent spirit whose power (long reach, boundary violation) is punished by male violence (double amputation by husband). Feminist analysis (E-pao article 'Lai Khutsangbi, female power and punishment') reads her as embodiment of anxieties about unruly female agency, punished through dismemberment. Her large mouth/long tongue and thorn fingers code female voracity and danger.

Scholarly Context

Scholars note that hingchabi/helloi tales often police gender norms: independent or transgressive female power is rendered monstrous and then violently contained. The tale's resolution — male hero stepping on and severing her remaining hand — is an explicit image of patriarchal containment. Responsible retelling should note this without glorifying the violence.

Graphic Mutilation and Bloodshed

Source Core legend climax and pursuit episode

Description

The climax involves two deliberate amputations with a sharpened sword/dao: first hand severed as it breaks through wall, second hand pinned underfoot and severed in forest. Demoness screams painfully, blood spurts and stains foliage. Violence is presented as heroic and justified, with no moral ambiguity in traditional telling.

Scholarly Context

Folk narrative violence is normative and didactic; the gore serves as proof of hero's bravery and as aetiology. Content warning is needed for contemporary audiences, especially children, as YouTube animations vary in how explicitly they depict severance.

Warning: Exploitation of Isolation and Vulnerability — Show unsanitized · Archive policy & legal

Exploitation of Isolation and Vulnerability

Source Legend prologue

Description

Lai Khutsangbi 'took advantage of people's loneliness, sowing terror' and specifically targets a household with absent husband and lone mother+infant. The tale encodes real social vulnerability of isolated hamlets and households without adult male protection in historical Meitei village structure.

Scholarly Context

Historical Meitei settlement pattern included isolated homesteads near forest (umang) for swidden or pastoral use; folklore reflects genuine risks of predation (wild animals, raids) mapped onto supernatural predator. The monster's interrogation ruse mirrors real-world caution about strangers at night.

Contradictions & Disputes

Orthography: Khutsangbi vs Khutshangbi vs Sangbi/Shangbi

Position A

Correct form is Lai Khutsangbi / Sangbi (with s)

Position A — evidence

Bengali-script transliteration often yields 's' and some E-pao titles use 'Khutsangbi'

Position B

Correct form is Lai Khutshangbi / Shangbi (with sh)

Position B — evidence

Meitei Mayek romanization and phonology favor /ʃ/ (sh) for Shangbi; source material title uses Khutshangbi; pronunciation given as /shaang-bee/

Scholarly Consensus

Both are transliteration variants of same Meitei word ꯁꯥꯡꯕꯤ (sangbi/shangbi = long); no semantic difference. Scholarly works note variation is orthographic, not two entities.

Child's name: Shasi vs Shachi vs Leirik vs Naocha

Position A

Original name is Shasi/Shachi

Position A — evidence

Kalachand 1980 and E-pao summaries use Shasi/Shachi; Shasipa/Shasima parent names derive from Shasi

Position B

Original name is Leirik/Naocha

Position B — evidence

YouTube animations and children's books use Leirik/Naocha, common modern Meitei names, suggesting localization for contemporary audiences

Scholarly Consensus

Variation is typical oral folklore localization; no single 'original' name is recoverable. Parent names Shasipa/Shasima are patronymic/matronymic formations (-pa/-ma) and shift with child's name.

Fate of Lai Khutsangbi: killed, permanently mutilated, or merely banished?

Position A

She is killed/caught and killed after second amputation

Position A — evidence

Some oral tellers and children's retellings say father 'caught and killed her' or 'chopped off that arm too and she died'

Position B

She is mutilated and flees, never seen again but not explicitly killed

Position B — evidence

Most anthologized versions (Kalachand, E-pao) end with 'fled, never seen again' without confirming death; 'never seen again' is folk formula for local banishment

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus; both endings circulate. Banishment formula is more widely attested in written anthologies; death variant may be later dramatization.

Botanical aetiology: which plants bear her blood?

Position A

Blood fell on many plants generally, explaining any red-spotted leaf

Position A — evidence

Core legend says 'many plants growing on her way to the forest' and 'red patches seen on some plants'

Position B

Blood fell on one specific species (e.g., Colocasia/taro or a particular forest shrub)

Position B — evidence

Local guides point to specific aroids or shrubs with red midribs/spots as 'Lai Khutsangbi's blood' during forest walks

Scholarly Consensus

Aetiology is folk-etymological and flexible; no single botanical species is canonically fixed. Variation reflects local ecology and teller's deictic pointing.

Classification: Is Lai Khutsangbi a Lai (deity) or Hingchabi (demoness)?

Position A

She is a Lai (goddess/deity) as name suggests

Position A — evidence

Name includes Lai, which popular English glosses as 'deity/goddess'; SEO sites call her 'demon goddess'

Position B

She is a Hingchabi (malevolent female spirit), not a worshipped Lai

Position B — evidence

Meitei taxonomy (Hodson, Parratt) defines Lai broadly as spirit; Umang Lai are worshipped, Hingchabi are malevolent and not worshipped. All folklore anthologies classify Lai Khutsangbi as hingchabi.

Scholarly Consensus

Scholarly consensus supports position B. Lai is a broad term for spirit; Lai Khutsangbi is specifically a hingchabi, a non-worshipped malevolent spirit. Calling her a goddess is a popular misconception.

Meitei oral folktale corpus - Lai Khutsangbi / Hingchabi cycle, as anthologized in Manipuri folklore collections

Type oralDate undated oral tradition; first written anthologization mid-20th century onwardLocator Core narrative: Shasi/Shasipa/Shasima episode; aetiological coda about blood on plantsLanguage Meiteilon (Manipuri), originally oral; recorded in Meitei Mayek and Bengali script, translated to English

Translation

English translations vary: child named Shasi / Shachi / Leirik / Naocha; father Shasipa, mother Shasima. Plot stable: nightly interrogation, mother's deception, father's ambush and double amputation, flight and blood trail.

T. C. Hodson - The Meitheis (1908)

Type textDate 1908Locator Chapter on religion and folklore: discussion of Lai, Hingchabi, Helloi, Umang Lai; pp. 96-130 in 1908 edition (David Nutt, London). Provides colonial-period ethnographic attestation of hingchabi category and forest spirits.Language English (with Meitei terms)

Translation

Hodson defines hingchabi as female evil spirits/demonesses haunting villages and forests; provides context that Lai Khutsangbi belongs to this class. Not a full transcription of the Lai Khutsangbi tale but establishes typology.

Loitongbam Kalachand Singh - Folktales of Manipur (and related anthologies: Folk Tales of Manipur, Manipuri Phungawari)

Type textDate 1980; reprinted 1990s-2000sLocator Tale titled 'Lai Khutshangbi' / 'The Long-handed Woman' - anthologized among Hingchabi talesLanguage English translation from Manipuri

Translation

Published English retelling containing the Shasi variant; source for most internet summaries. Locates story in isolated hamlet near forest, details long hands touching ground, thorn-like fingers, large mouth/long tongue.

Saroj Nalini Arambam Parratt - The Religion of Manipur: Beliefs, Rituals and Historical Development (and Parratt & Parratt, The Pleasing of the Gods)

Type textDate 1980 (PhD 1974, published 1980); 1997Locator Chapters on indigenous Sanamahi cosmology, Lai, Umang Lai, and malevolent spirits (hingchabi, helloi)Language English

Translation

Provides scholarly framework: Lai as spirit, Umang Lai as forest deities, hingchabi as distinct malevolent female spirits. Contextualizes Lai Khutsangbi within Meitei spirit taxonomy, not Vedic/Hindu demonology.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

1) No pre-colonial manuscript (Puya) attestation located; earliest written fixation is 20th-century folklore anthologies. Oral antiquity cannot be dated precisely. 2) Meitei-language primary sources in Meitei Mayek/Bengali script not fully consulted due to language and access limitations; reliance on English translations (Kalachand) may miss nuance. 3) No traditional iconography, sculpture, or ritual text for Lai Khutsangbi — she is a narrated figure, not a worshipped deity, so visual reconstruction relies on modern illustrations. 4) Botanical identification of 'blood-stained plants' not verified by field botany; local species varies by teller. 5) Music, proverb, riddle, or Lai Haraoba song references to Lai Khutsangbi not located — may exist in unpublished oral repertoire. 6) Comparative hingchabi corpus (other named hingchabi) not exhaustively indexed; full typology would require Manipur University folklore archives. 7) No evidence of pan-Manipur vs valley-specific distribution; Kuki, Naga, or Pangal parallels not researched. 8) Image rights: no public-domain traditional image exists; any illustration must be newly commissioned as 'no verified visual record' or openly licensed.

Research Scope

Exhaustive dossier on Lai Khutsangbi (Lai Khutshangbi), a hingchabi demoness of Meitei folklore (Kangleipak / Manipur valley, Sanamahism). Covers oral folk narrative, Meitei linguistic etymology, comparative hingchabi typology, colonial ethnography, modern folklore compilations, and contemporary digital reception. No pre-colonial manuscript attestation located; research based on 20th-century ethnographies, Meitei folklore anthologies, and verified Meitei cultural sites. Cutoff 2026-08-09.
