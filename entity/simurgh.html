Who It Is

The Simurgh (also Simorgh, Simurg) is a magnificent mythical bird in Persian mythology, a wise and benevolent creature of immense size and age who nests in the Tree of All Seeds at the center of the world or on the peaks of Mount Qaf (the cosmic mountain at the edge of the world). She has the body of a peacock, the head of a dog, and the claws of a lion — a composite creature symbolizing her mastery over the animal kingdom. The Simurgh is said to possess all knowledge and is the nurse of creation: in Zoroastrian tradition, she protects the sacred tree and, when she flies, the seeds of all plants scatter across the earth. She is the patron of heroes: in Ferdowsi's Shahnameh (the Persian Book of Kings), the Simurgh raises the abandoned hero Zal, teaches him wisdom, and grants him a feather to summon her in times of great need — a feather that saves Zal's son Rostam in his most desperate battles. In Attar's Conference of the Birds, the Simurgh is the ultimate goal of the birds' quest — the king they seek — and they discover that the Simurgh is themselves (a pun on 'si murgh' meaning 'thirty birds'). The Simurgh represents the highest wisdom, the mother of all knowledge, and the unity of the seeker with the sought. She is a symbol of Iran itself — ancient, wise, and eternal.

The Approach

In the misty heights of Mount Alborz, where the sky meets the earth, there dwells a creature of immense age and wisdom—the Simurgh. This benevolent bird, with the body of a peacock, the head of a dog, and the claws of a lion, has witnessed the destruction of the world three times over. It is said that its feathers shimmer like copper, and its nest rests in the Tree of Life, the source of all healing. The Simurgh is not merely a myth; it is a symbol of the connection between the divine and the mortal, a protector of kings, and a guide to heroes. Its story is woven into the very fabric of Persian culture, from ancient Zoroastrian texts to the epic poetry of Ferdowsi. This dossier delves into the depths of this majestic being, exploring its origins, its powers, and its enduring legacy.

Content advisory. The Simurgh's mythology includes themes of abandonment, violence, and death, particularly in the Shahnameh episodes involving Zal, Esfandiyar, and Rostam. These stories contain scenes of battle, killing, and prophetic doom. Readers should be aware of these elements.

Etymology

The Persian word 'sīmurğ' derives from Middle Persian 'sēnmurw' and earlier 'sēnmuruγ', which comes from Avestan 'mərəγō Saēnō' meaning 'the bird Saēna'. 'Saēna' is etymologically related to Sanskrit 'śyenaḥ' (raptor, eagle, bird of prey). The name has been subject to folk etymology connecting 'si' to 'thirty', leading to legends of the bird being as large as thirty birds or having thirty colors. Other proposed etymologies include Pahlavi 'sin murgh' (eagle bird) and Avestan 'saeno merego' (eagle).

Cultural Ownership

Persian (Iranian) mythology, with roots in ancient Zoroastrian tradition. The Simurgh is a prominent figure in Iranian art, literature, and folklore, and has influenced the iconography of Georgia, medieval Armenia, the Eastern Roman Empire, and other regions under Persian cultural influence.

Disputed Identifications

The Simurgh is sometimes conflated with the phoenix (Persian: qoqnos) and the Huma (Persian: homa), but they are distinct beings. The Simurgh is also compared to the Griffin, with some scholars viewing the Simurgh as the Western Iranian version and the Griffin as the Eastern Iranian version. There is debate over whether the Simurgh is a single entity or multiple (e.g., two different Simurghs in the Shahnameh).

Description

Appearance

The Simurgh is depicted as a gigantic bird, large enough to carry off an elephant or a whale. It often appears as a peacock with the head of a dog and the claws of a lion, sometimes with a human face. Its feathers are said to be the color of copper. It is part mammal, suckling its young.

Symbols & Iconography

The Simurgh represents purity, healing, wisdom, and the union of Earth and sky. It is associated with the Tree of Life (Gaokerena) and the divine glory (farr). In Sasanian art, it was a royal emblem symbolizing power and protection.

Behaviour & Personality

The Simurgh is inherently benevolent. It has an enmity towards snakes and prefers habitats with abundant water. It is ancient and possesses knowledge of all ages, having witnessed the destruction of the world three times. It acts as a mediator between Earth and sky, and serves as a messenger.

Domains & Habitats

The Simurgh roosts in Gaokerena, the Hōm Tree of Life, located in the middle of the world sea Vourukasha. It is also associated with Mount Alborz, where it raised Zal.

Version Differences

In the Avesta, the Simurgh (Saēna) is a divine bird associated with the tree of all seeds. In the Shahnameh, there are apparently two Simurghs: one that raised Zal and another that is slain by Esfandiyar. Later depictions sometimes show it with a human head or a dog's head.

Zal and the Simurgh

Abandonment of Zal

Source Shahnameh

Description

Zal's father Saam abandons him on a mountain because of his albinism, fearing he is a demon's child.

Scholarly Context

This reflects ancient attitudes towards physical differences and the belief in demonic offspring.

Violence and Death

Source Shahnameh

Description

The Simurgh is slain by Esfandiyar, and its offspring are also killed. Rostam kills Esfandiyar with an arrow made from a Simurgh feather, leading to Esfandiyar's death.

Scholarly Context

These episodes highlight the tragic nature of the epic and the inevitability of fate.

Contradictions & Disputes

Number of Simurghs

Position A

There is only one Simurgh.

Position A — evidence

The Simurgh is described as a unique, ancient being.

Position B

There are two distinct Simurghs in the Shahnameh.

Position B — evidence

One Simurgh is killed by Esfandiyar, while another raises Zal and helps Rostam.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars accept the existence of two Simurghs in the Shahnameh, though some argue the slain one is a different creature.

Simurgh vs. Phoenix

Position A

The Simurgh is the same as the phoenix.

Position A — evidence

Some legends describe the Simurgh living 1,700 years and then immolating itself, similar to the phoenix.

Position B

They are distinct creatures.

Position B — evidence

The Simurgh is not primarily a firebird; it is associated with water and the Tree of Life.

Scholarly Consensus

Scholars generally distinguish the Simurgh from the phoenix, noting different origins and characteristics.

Etymology of 'Simurgh'

Position A

The name derives from Avestan 'Saēna'.

Position A — evidence

Historical linguistics trace the word to Avestan.

Position B

The name is connected to 'thirty birds'.

Position B — evidence

Folk etymology and Attar's use of the pun.

Scholarly Consensus

The historical etymology is accepted; the 'thirty' connection is a folk etymology.
