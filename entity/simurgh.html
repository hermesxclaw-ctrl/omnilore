Who It Is

In the misty heights of Mount Alborz, where the sky meets the earth, there dwells a creature of immense age and wisdom—the Simurgh. This benevolent bird, with the body of a peacock, the head of a dog, and the claws of a lion, has witnessed the destruction of the world three times over. It is said that its feathers shimmer like copper, and its nest rests in the Tree of Life, the source of all healing. The Simurgh is not merely a myth; it is a symbol of the connection between the divine and the mortal, a protector of kings, and a guide to heroes. Its story is woven into the very fabric of Persian culture, from ancient Zoroastrian texts to the epic poetry of Ferdowsi. This dossier delves into the depths of this majestic being, exploring its origins, its powers, and its enduring legacy.

The Simurgh is inherently benevolent. It has an enmity towards snakes and prefers habitats with abundant water. It is ancient and possesses knowledge of all ages, having witnessed the destruction of the world three times. It acts as a mediator between Earth and sky, and serves as a messenger.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The Simurgh is depicted as a gigantic bird, large enough to carry off an elephant or a whale. It often appears as a peacock with the head of a dog and the claws of a lion, sometimes with a human face. Its feathers are said to be the color of copper. It is part mammal, suckling its young.

Symbols — The Simurgh represents purity, healing, wisdom, and the union of Earth and sky. It is associated with the Tree of Life (Gaokerena) and the divine glory (farr). In Sasanian art, it was a royal emblem symbolizing power and protection.

Domains — The Simurgh roosts in Gaokerena, the Hōm Tree of Life, located in the middle of the world sea Vourukasha. It is also associated with Mount Alborz, where it raised Zal.

Accounts I
Zal and the SimurghFerdowsi's Shahnameh

Mythical/Epic

Zal, born albino, is abandoned by his father Saam on Mount Alborz. The Simurgh hears his cries, rescues him, and raises him, teaching him wisdom. When Zal grows up and wishes to return to human society, the Simurgh gives him three golden feathers to summon her if needed. Zal becomes a great hero, marries Rudaba, and their son Rostam is born with the Simurgh's help.

The Birth of RostamFerdowsi's Shahnameh

Epic

Rudaba's labor is prolonged and life-threatening. Zal burns a feather to summon the Simurgh, who instructs him on performing a cesarean section, saving both Rudaba and the child, Rostam. Rostam is born and becomes the greatest Persian hero.

Accounts II
Esfandiyar's Fifth LaborFerdowsi's Shahnameh

Epic

During his Seven Trials, Esfandiyar encounters and fights a Simurgh. Despite its powers, Esfandiyar decapitates it. The Simurgh's offspring rise to fight but are also slain. Esfandiyar continues his quest, but this act may foreshadow his later death.

Rostam and EsfandiyarFerdowsi's Shahnameh

Epic

Rostam is wounded in battle with Esfandiyar. Zal summons the Simurgh, who heals Rostam and Rakhsh. The Simurgh warns Rostam against killing Esfandiyar, prophesying doom. Rostam ignores the warning, and with the Simurgh's guidance, fashions a double-headed arrow from a tamarisk branch and a feather, which he uses to blind Esfandiyar, leading to his death. Esfandiyar dies, fulfilling the prophecy. Rostam later dies, and the cycle of tragedy continues.
