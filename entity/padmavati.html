Who It Is

In the pantheon of Jainism, where renunciation and non-violence reign supreme, Padmāvatī stands as a striking paradox: a goddess of worldly power and protection, whose serpent banner and jeweled crown belie her role as a guardian of the faith. She is the consort of the serpent-king Dharaṇendra, a figure who once sheltered the enlightened Tīrthaṅkara Pārśva from a demonic assault. Yet Padmāvatī is no mere footnote to her husband's legend; she is a deity in her own right, with a thriving cult, a rich iconography, and a complex history that weaves together Jain doctrine, folk piety, and royal patronage.

As a yakṣī, Padmāvatī is a benevolent guardian deity who bestows boons, protects devotees from harm (especially snake bites and evil spirits), and assists in worldly matters such as marriage, progeny, and wealth. She is also a warrior goddess who defends the Jain faith and its followers. In her role as consort of Dharaṇendra, she shares his protective and kingly qualities.

Powers
Protection

Padmāvatī protects her devotees from physical and spiritual dangers, including snake bites, epidemics, and evil spirits. She is often invoked for safety during travel and in times of crisis.

Limit: Her protection is believed to be conditional on the devotee's faith and moral conduct (non-violence, truthfulness).

Granting boons

She grants boons related to wealth, progeny, health, and success. Devotees offer prayers and perform rituals to seek her favor.

Limit: Boons are believed to be granted according to the devotee's karma and merit.

Serpent control

Due to her association with Dharaṇendra, the serpent king, she has power over serpents and can protect against snake bites.

Limit: Specific rituals are required to invoke this power.

Divine wisdom

As a yakṣī, she possesses supernatural knowledge and can guide devotees on spiritual matters.

Limit: She is not omniscient; only Tīrthaṅkaras possess omniscience.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Padmāvatī is typically depicted as a beautiful, four-armed goddess seated on a lotus throne. She holds a lotus in one hand, a noose (pāśa) in another, and sometimes a goad (aṅkuśa) or a citron (bījapūraka). Her complexion is golden or lotus-pink. She wears a crown and rich ornaments. Symbols — Lotus (purity, divine birth), serpent (nāga, protection, association with Dharaṇendra), noose (control, binding of enemies), goad (power), child (nurturing aspect). Her vāhana (vehicle) is often a serpent or a lion.

Domains — Padmāvatī is worshipped in Jain temples, often in dedicated shrines or as a subsidiary deity in the temple complex. She is particularly associated with the Pārśvanātha temples. Her cult is widespread in western India and Karnataka. She is also a household deity for many Jains.

Accounts I
The Protection of PārśvaPārśvanātha-caritra and other Jain hagiographies

Early centuries CE (texts), legendary events in ancient times

The demon Meghamālin (or Kamatha) attacked the Tīrthaṅkara Pārśva with a fiery thunderbolt. The serpent king Dharaṇendra, who had been a previous incarnation of Pārśva's disciple, shielded him with his hoods. Padmāvatī, Dharaṇendra's consort, also protected Pārśva by spreading her hoods or by creating a canopy of lotuses. She is thus revered as a protector of the faith. Pārśva attained enlightenment and became a Tīrthaṅkara. Padmāvatī and Dharaṇendra became his attendant deities and are worshipped by Jains.

Accounts II
The Yakṣī of PārśvaJain canonical and commentarial texts

From early CE

Padmāvatī is identified as the yakṣī (attendant goddess) of the Tīrthaṅkara Pārśva. She is part of the pair of attendant deities (yakṣa and yakṣī) that each Tīrthaṅkara has. She is invoked in rituals and depicted in temple sculpture. Her cult developed independently, with temples and festivals dedicated to her.

The Goddess of Wealth and ProtectionFolk traditions, later medieval texts

Medieval to modern

Padmāvatī is worshipped as a goddess who grants material prosperity and protects against calamities. She is often compared to Lakṣmī and is invoked in business and household rituals. Her temples are popular pilgrimage sites, and she is a central figure in many Jain festivals.

Padmāvatī is the same as Rani Padmavati of Chittor. — Rani Padmavati was a historical figure (or legendary queen) from medieval India, not a goddess. The name is coincidental.

Padmāvatī is a Hindu goddess. — She is a Jain deity, though she shares some attributes with Hindu goddesses like Lakṣmī and is sometimes worshipped by Hindus as well.

Padmāvatī is a snake goddess in the sense of being a cobra. — She is associated with serpents (nāgas) as divine beings, but she is not a snake herself. She is a humanoid goddess with a serpent canopy.
