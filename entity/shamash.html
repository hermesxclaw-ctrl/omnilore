Who It Is

Every day, on a wheel of fire, a god who could see everything crossed the whole world — and because he saw every wrong and every traveler, he was the one a king held the rod-and-ring of law from, the one a shepherd-bridegroom begged to be saved from the demons dragging him to the underworld, and the one who, in the oldest epic on Earth, held his hand over a hero about to kill a monster. His name was Shamash — to the Sumerians, Utu, which is simply their word for 'sun.' He is the ancient Mesopotamian sun god of justice: the all-seeing judge whose radiant shoulders sit atop the Code of Hammurabi in a million history books, and whose name still lights a candle on a Hanukkah menorah today.

Utu/Shamash was the all-seeing god of justice — because he traveled the whole sky each day, he saw everything on earth and therefore judged right from wrong, and protected travelers and wayfarers far from cities. He was implored in the early morning outdoors, likely as a visible astral body. He acted as a benevolent, pro-active ally to heroes (Gilgamesh, and Uruk's legendary kings) and as a brother-in-law protector of Dumuzi. Powers
All-Seeing Divine Vision

Because Utu/Shamash traveled the entire sky from east to west every day, he saw everything that happened on earth. This was the stated basis of his role as the primary god of justice and of his protection of travelers. He was implored in the early morning as a visible astral body, and was widely invoked in prayers and literary compositions outside temples.

Limit: In the 3rd millennium he rested at night (no night-journey attested yet); the night journey through the nether-sky is a later doctrine.

Divine Judge and Upholder of Law

Shamash was the pan-Mesopotamian god of justice, the divine judge who 'cut' verdicts (kud / parāsum). He handed the rod-and-ring of authority to kings, including Hammurabi, who styled his law code as the product of Shamash's mandate. He could be assisted by his father Nanna, his sister Inanna, and various minor judge deities (the 'Six Judges of Shamash,' the 'Eleven Standing Gods of Ebabbar').

Limit: In the 3rd millennium Ishtaran was regarded as an equal-ranking divine judge; a hymn states the river-oracle god Idlurugu could not give judgment without Utu's presence.

God of Divination and Extispicy

Shamash and the weather god Adad were jointly regarded as the gods of divination, particularly extispicy (reading entrails). A late ritual text says they taught divination to the mythical king Enmeduranki, who then taught it to the people of Sippar, Nippur, and Babylon.

Limit: This association is not attested for the earlier Sumerian Ishkur (Adad's analog), suggesting it was imported from the Ebla/Syrian tradition where Hadad and the solar deity were linked.

Protector of Travelers and Heroes

Shamash guarded wayfarers in steppes and mountains and served as the divine patron of several legendary Uruk kings. In the Epic of Gilgamesh he grants the hero seven guiding constellations, is invoked (by Gilgamesh or his mother Ninsun) before the Cedar Forest quest, sends thirteen winds to incapacitate Humbaba at the decisive moment, and offers the mourning Gilgamesh counsel on the futility of seeking immortality.

Limit: His protection is not absolute — in the flood tablet he only announces the flood's dawn, and in Dumuzi's death he can at most delay, not prevent, the victim's demise.

Solar Journey and Chariot

Each day Shamash drove a solar chariot drawn by four named divine equids (in most sources horses, mules, or 'choice steeds'; possibly lions in early sources — disputed), passing through cosmic gates on twin mountains at the world's ends, then at night traversing the nether-sky AN.ŠAG4 above the underworld. At sunset he entered Buduhudug, 'the entrance of Shamash to Aya,' where he reunites with his wife Aya each day.

Limit: Krebernik argues the early chariot was lion-drawn (questioned by Bonechi); Wasserman only calls the animals 'beasts.'

Underworld/Nether-Sky Authority

As an extension of his judicial role (attested from the Old Babylonian period onward), Utu/Shamash could be associated with the underworld. In exorcisms he was implored to bring restless ghosts down to the land of the dead. In this capacity he could be associated with the deified legendary king Gilgamesh, portrayed in a similar role.

Limit: This underworld connection is NOT attested before the Old Babylonian period.

Bestower of Universal Rule (limited tradition)

Theophoric names like Shamash-bel-ili ('Shamash is the lord of the gods'), Shamash-Enlil-ili, and Shamash-ashared-ili ('Shamash is the foremost of the gods') indicate a tradition (Wilfred G. Lambert) in which Shamash was regarded as supreme among the gods. This view, however, never received official state support and was largely restricted to the clergy of Sippar and, to a lesser degree, Larsa.

Limit: Never official; restricted to Sippar/Larsa clergy.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Shamash was portrayed as a bearded, heroic male deity (etlu/qarrādu, 'youth/hero'), usually standing or seated on a throne before worshipers. His most recognizable attribute was the large saw (šaššaru) and rays of light emanating from his shoulders. In the Code-of-Hammurabi relief he wears the horned crown of divinity with flames spouting from his shoulders. Accounts I
Utu and the Death of Dumuzi (the brother-in-law's rescue attempt)Inanna's Descent to the Underworld; Dumuzi's Death; Dumuzi and Geshtinanna (ETCSL)

Old Babylonian copies; older oral tradition

When the galla demons of the underworld come to drag the shepherd-king Dumuzi down, Dumuzi pleads with Utu (as Inanna's brother, his brother-in-law) to save him. In some narratives he argues that Utu should help because he is his brother-in-law; in others he adds that he had paid respect to their mother Ningal. Utu fulfills his request — in one account transforming Dumuzi into a gazelle to enable his escape — but in no surviving version is this enough: the galla eventually recapture Dumuzi and drag him down. The most Utu can accomplish is a delay of the inevitable death. Cements Utu's role as a fraternal protector who nonetheless cannot overrule the underworld's claim — a parable of the limits of even an all-seeing solar god.

Shamash and the Slaying of HumbabaThe Epic of Gilgamesh (Old Babylonian and Standard Babylonian versions); Sumerian Gilgamesh and Humbaba

Old Babylonian through Standard Babylonian

In the Sumerian myth Gilgamesh and Humbaba, Enkidu advises Gilgamesh to ask Utu for permission before traveling to Humbaba's dwelling; Utu grants him seven constellations (described as zoomorphic creatures) to guide him safely. In the Old Babylonian Epic, Gilgamesh prays to Shamash before departing for the Cedar Forest and the elders have him dig wells for libations to the sun god and Lugalbanda. In the Standard Babylonian version it is Gilgamesh's mother Ninsun who invokes Shamash on the rooftop of a temple, blaming him for her son's wanderlust and asking Aya to intercede for the hero's safety. During the confrontation, Shamash sends thirteen winds that blind/incapacitate Humbaba, allowing Gilgamesh to land the decisive blow. Humbaba pleads with Utu (Shamash) for his life in vain, and is slain. After the heroes' later victory over the Bull of Heaven, they offer its heart to Shamash. The Marriage of Inanna (Utu as matchmaker)Inanna Prefers the Farmer (ETCSL 4.0.8.3.3)

Old Babylonian period

The poem opens with a playful dialogue in which Inanna's brother Utu incrementally reveals to her that it is time to marry. The shepherd-god Dumuzid courts her alongside a farmer named Enkimdu. Inanna initially prefers the farmer, but Utu and Dumuzid together persuade her that the shepherd can give her something better for every gift the farmer can offer, and she ultimately chooses Dumuzid. This sets up the brother-in-law relationship that recurs in the death cycle. Establishes the marriage of Inanna and Dumuzid, making Utu Dumuzid's brother-in-law — the very relationship Dumuzid later appeals to in his death narratives.

Accounts II
Utu Aids Inanna in Claiming EannaInanna and An (ETCSL)

Old Babylonian period

In this myth Inanna laments that the temple Eanna (in Uruk) is not yet within her dominion and resolves to claim it for herself; she and her brother Utu work together to bring the temple down from heaven. It is possible this served as a mythical explanation for the origin of Mesopotamian temples. Links Utu to the founding of the cult of Inanna at Uruk and to the mythic origin of temple-building.

The Sun God and the FloodThe Epic of Gilgamesh, tablet XI (flood myth, Standard Babylonian)

Standard Babylonian version (late 2nd-1st millennium BCE)

In the flood narrative embedded in the standard Epic, Shamash is responsible for announcing the beginning of the flood when he rises in the morning. The flood's onset at dawn (rather than mid-night, as in most other versions of the Mesopotamian flood story) appears to be a relatively young tradition; the compiler likely chose it because Shamash throughout the Epic functions as humanity's helper and protector — fitting that the sun god signals the impending cataclysm at daybreak. Reflects Shamash's benign, humanity-aiding characterization in the Standard Babylonian Epic.

The Origin of Ishum (fragmentary)Fragmentary Old Babylonian myth on the origin of the god Ishum

Old Babylonian period

A single fragmentary myth describes the god Ishum as a son of Ninlil and the sun god, abandoned in the streets as an infant. Manfred Krebernik considers this composition a result of confusion between the names Sud and Sudaĝ (Sudaĝ being one of the names of Utu's wife Aya), which led to Ninlil (identified with Sud) being conflated as Ishum's mother — not a genuine syncretism. The usual tradition makes Ishum the son of Utu and Aya. Illustrates how name-confusion in cuneiform god-lists generated alternative mythic parentage.

Enmesharra's Defeat (Shamash's radiance assigned)Enmesharra's Defeat (late myth; sole poorly preserved Seleucid/Parthian copy)

Seleucid or Parthian period (late)

In this late myth, Shamash's radiance (zīmû, a halo or solar rays) is bestowed upon him by Marduk after the imprisonment of Enmesharra, who was the rays' original owner. Wilfred G. Lambert speculated the scene might echo depictions of battles between gods on Sargonic cylinder seals, and that some ray-emitting figures on those seals might in fact be Enmesharra rather than the sun god. Late myth reassigns the sun-god's most iconic visual attribute to a divine grant, and raises the question of whether pre-2nd-millennium seal imagery misidentified the owner of the rays.
