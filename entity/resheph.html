Resheph.

Who It Is

Resheph.

Resheph. Plague, pestilence, fever, healing (as a counterbalance), war, battle, arrows, weapons, the underworld, the wilderness, the hunt, the sun's scorching heat, and possibly the morning star. He is a gatekeeper of the underworld and a warrior in the divine council.

Form

Resheph is typically depicted as a fierce warrior god. In Egyptian art, he appears as a standing male figure with a tall, pointed crown (often with a gazelle or antelope head attached to the front), a long beard, and a was-scepter in one hand and a shield, spear, or mace in the other. He wears a short kilt and often has a uraeus (cobra) on his forehead. In Levantine iconography, he is shown as a striding warrior with a raised weapon, sometimes with a bow and arrow. The gazelle or antelope head on his crown is a distinctive marker, possibly symbolizing speed, the wilderness, or the 'heat' of the sun.

Powers

Plague and Pestilence Resheph can inflict plague, fever, and epidemic disease upon individuals and populations. He shoots his 'arrows' of pestilence, striking down the healthy and the powerful alike. In the Bible, the word 'reshef' is used to describe the 'burning' of plague (Psalm 78:48, Habakkuk 3:5). Ugaritic, Phoenician, Biblical (as a common noun), Egyptian His plague is not always indiscriminate; it can be directed at specific enemies or withheld in response to proper worship. Magical texts invoke him to both cause and cure disease. Some texts emphasize his destructive power, while others focus on his role as a healer or protector. The two aspects are likely two sides of the same coin: the god who sends the plague is the only one who can stop it.

Divine Warrior Resheph is a fierce warrior god, wielding a spear, mace, and shield. He is a member of the divine host, fighting alongside or in service to the chief gods (El, Baal). He is described as 'the lord of the arrow', and his arrows are both literal weapons and metaphors for sudden divine punishment. Ugaritic, Egyptian, Phoenician In the Ugaritic Baal Cycle, he is a subordinate deity, serving the greater gods. He is not depicted as a supreme deity but as a powerful and fearsome member of the pantheon. No major contradictions, though the extent of his martial independence is unclear.

Gatekeeper of the Underworld In some texts, Resheph is associated with the gates of the underworld (the 'doors of Resheph' in Ugaritic texts). He guards the boundary between the living and the dead, and his arrows can send the living to the realm of Mot (death). Ugaritic His role as a gatekeeper is implied but not fully elaborated in surviving texts. None known.

Healing In Egyptian magical papyri, Resheph is invoked to heal the diseases he can inflict. He is called 'the great god who listens to prayer', suggesting a role as a healer or intercessor. Egyptian (New Kingdom) His healing role is more prominent in Egypt than in the Levant, where his destructive aspect is more emphasized. This is a classic 'plague god as healer' duality, common to deities like Nergal and Apollo.

Behavior & Habitat

Resheph is a god of dual nature: he is the bringer of plague and pestilence, striking down his victims with the 'arrows' of disease, but he is also a protector who can ward off the same calamities when properly worshipped. He is a fierce warrior, a guardian of the gates of the underworld, and a divine attendant in the courts of higher gods (such as El and Baal). He is invoked in treaties and oaths as a punishing witness, and he is called upon in magical texts to heal the very diseases he can inflict. His personality in surviving texts is largely implied through his epithets: 'Lord of the Arrow', 'Lord of the Host', 'He Who Listens'.

Accounts

Resheph is mentioned as a divine attendant in the court of El and as a warrior in the cosmic battles between Baal and Mot (Death). He is part of the divine assembly and participates in the struggles that shape the world.

In the epic of King Kirta, his son, who is cursed and rebellious, is named Yassib. The text mentions Resheph in a context of illness and the loss of children. Kirta's children are said to be 'as numerous as Resheph's flock' (i.e., numerous but vulnerable to death).

Resheph is mentioned in connection with the messengers of the gods and the cycle of life and death.

In Egypt, Resheph was worshipped as a foreign warrior god, often paired with the goddess Qetesh and the god Min. He was invoked in magical spells to protect against plague and to heal the sick. Votive stelae show him as a warrior with shield and spear, receiving offerings from devotees.

The bilingual (Phoenician-Luwian) inscription of King Azatiwada invokes 'Resheph of the Arrow' (ršp ṣṣ) as a divine witness to the king's treaty and building projects. He is called upon to protect the city of Azatiwadaya.

Scholarly Debates
Resheph as a solar deity
Some scholars see Resheph as a solar deity, based on his association with the scorching heat of the sun (the 'burning' of his name) and his crown with an antelope head (a solar symbol in some contexts). ⇌ Others argue that his association with heat is specifically related to fever and plague, not to the sun as a celestial body, and that his iconography is more clearly martial than solar.
Evidence for A: His name's root ršp, meaning 'flame' or 'burning'; the antelope head on his crown.
Evidence for B: His consistent depiction as a warrior with weapons; his role as a plague god, not a sun god, in the texts.
Resheph and the god of the 'Resheph of the Arrow'
The epithet 'Resheph of the Arrow' (ršp ṣṣ) in the Karatepe inscription refers to a specific, localized form of the god, possibly with a distinctive cult. ⇌ The epithet is a general description of the god's nature as an archer, not a reference to a distinct cult.
Evidence for A: The unique nature of the epithet in this inscription; the importance of the city's defense in the text.
Evidence for B: Resheph's association with arrows is widespread in his iconography and in other texts.
Resheph's relationship to the Greek Apollo
The syncretism of Resheph and Apollo in Cyprus was a natural result of their shared domains (plague, healing, archery) and was a genuine fusion of the two deities. ⇌ The identification was a superficial, political or cultural imposition by the Greeks, who saw Resheph as a 'barbarian Apollo' and absorbed his cult without fully understanding it.
Evidence for A: The bilingual inscriptions explicitly equate the two; the shared iconography of the bow and arrow.

What We Don't Know

The Ugaritic texts are fragmentary, and many references to Resheph are incomplete. The full extent of his mythology is unknown. The specific rituals of his cult are poorly documented. The relationship between the Levantine Resheph and the Egyptian Resheph is not fully understood. The 'Resheph of the Arrow' cult at Karatepe is a unique attestation that lacks clear parallels.

A way to hold it in your mind

If the ancient Near East had a 'grim reaper' with a bow, it would be Resheph. But unlike the modern grim reaper, Resheph wasn't just a personification of death; he was a complex deity who could be both the cause of your suffering and the answer to your prayers. He's like a divine mix of a plague doctor, a special forces soldier, and a border patrol agent for the underworld. He's the god you'd want on your side in a fight, but you'd also be terrified to see him coming.
