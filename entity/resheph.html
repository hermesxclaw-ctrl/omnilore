Who It Is

Imagine a god who doesn't just carry a bow and arrow — he IS the arrow. The flash of the weapon, the burning heat of fever, the sudden, unstoppable strike of plague. That's Resheph, the Canaanite god of pestilence and war, a deity who could kill you with a glance from his fiery gaze or, if you prayed hard enough, hold back the very epidemic he unleashed. He was the divine archer whose arrows were the sparks of death, and his worship spanned a thousand years and three very different cultures. He's the ancient world's original Patient Zero, and he's been hiding in plain sight in the Bible, in Egyptian temples, and on the island of Cyprus, waiting for you to draw back the bowstring of history.

Resheph is a god of dual nature: he is the bringer of plague and pestilence, striking down his victims with the 'arrows' of disease, but he is also a protector who can ward off the same calamities when properly worshipped. He is a fierce warrior, a guardian of the gates of the underworld, and a divine attendant in the courts of higher gods (such as El and Baal). He is invoked in treaties and oaths as a punishing witness, and he is called upon in magical texts to heal the very diseases he can inflict. Powers
Plague and Pestilence

Resheph can inflict plague, fever, and epidemic disease upon individuals and populations. He shoots his 'arrows' of pestilence, striking down the healthy and the powerful alike. In the Bible, the word 'reshef' is used to describe the 'burning' of plague (Psalm 78:48, Habakkuk 3:5).

Limit: His plague is not always indiscriminate; it can be directed at specific enemies or withheld in response to proper worship. Magical texts invoke him to both cause and cure disease.

Divine Warrior

Resheph is a fierce warrior god, wielding a spear, mace, and shield. He is a member of the divine host, fighting alongside or in service to the chief gods (El, Baal). He is described as 'the lord of the arrow', and his arrows are both literal weapons and metaphors for sudden divine punishment.

Limit: In the Ugaritic Baal Cycle, he is a subordinate deity, serving the greater gods. He is not depicted as a supreme deity but as a powerful and fearsome member of the pantheon.

Gatekeeper of the Underworld

In some texts, Resheph is associated with the gates of the underworld (the 'doors of Resheph' in Ugaritic texts). He guards the boundary between the living and the dead, and his arrows can send the living to the realm of Mot (death).

Limit: His role as a gatekeeper is implied but not fully elaborated in surviving texts.

Healing

In Egyptian magical papyri, Resheph is invoked to heal the diseases he can inflict. He is called 'the great god who listens to prayer', suggesting a role as a healer or intercessor.

Limit: His healing role is more prominent in Egypt than in the Levant, where his destructive aspect is more emphasized.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Resheph is typically depicted as a fierce warrior god. In Egyptian art, he appears as a standing male figure with a tall, pointed crown (often with a gazelle or antelope head attached to the front), a long beard, and a was-scepter in one hand and a shield, spear, or mace in the other. Symbols — The arrow (his most common symbol), the bow, the spear, the mace, the shield, the gazelle or antelope (particularly its head, used on his crown), the was-scepter, the uraeus, and the tall pointed crown. The arrow is both his weapon of war and the vector of plague, a powerful metaphor for the sudden, indiscriminate strike of disease.

Domains — Plague, pestilence, fever, healing (as a counterbalance), war, battle, arrows, weapons, the underworld, the wilderness, the hunt, the sun's scorching heat, and possibly the morning star. He is a gatekeeper of the underworld and a warrior in the divine council.

Accounts I
The Baal Cycle (Ugarit)KTU 1.2 III; KTU 1.5 I

c. 14th-12th century BCE

Resheph is mentioned as a divine attendant in the court of El and as a warrior in the cosmic battles between Baal and Mot (Death). He is part of the divine assembly and participates in the struggles that shape the world. Resheph's presence in the cycle establishes him as a powerful, if minor, member of the Ugaritic pantheon.

The Epic of Kirta (Ugarit)KTU 1.16 III

c. 14th-12th century BCE

In the epic of King Kirta, his son, who is cursed and rebellious, is named Yassib. The text mentions Resheph in a context of illness and the loss of children. Kirta's children are said to be 'as numerous as Resheph's flock' (i.e., numerous but vulnerable to death). The reference highlights Resheph's association with mortality and the fragility of human life.

Accounts II
The Aqhat Epic (Ugarit)KTU 1.22

c. 14th-12th century BCE

Resheph is mentioned in connection with the messengers of the gods and the cycle of life and death. The text reinforces Resheph's role as a divine messenger and agent of fate.

Egyptian Votive Stelae and Magical TextsVarious New Kingdom sources (e.g., Deir el-Medina stelae, Papyrus Chester Beatty VII)

c. 1550-1070 BCE

In Egypt, Resheph was worshipped as a foreign warrior god, often paired with the goddess Qetesh and the god Min. He was invoked in magical spells to protect against plague and to heal the sick. Votive stelae show him as a warrior with shield and spear, receiving offerings from devotees. Resheph became a popular deity in New Kingdom Egypt, particularly among the working class at Deir el-Medina.

The Karatepe Inscription (Phoenician)KAI 26 (Karatepe, modern Turkey)

c. 8th century BCE

The bilingual (Phoenician-Luwian) inscription of King Azatiwada invokes 'Resheph of the Arrow' (ršp ṣṣ) as a divine witness to the king's treaty and building projects. He is called upon to protect the city of Azatiwadaya. The inscription demonstrates Resheph's continued importance in the Iron Age as a guardian deity of cities and treaties.

Resheph as a solar deitySome scholars see Resheph as a solar deity, based on his association with the scorching heat of the sun (the 'burning' of his name) and his crown with an antelope head (a solar symbol in some contexts).Others argue that his association with heat is specifically related to fever and plague, not to the sun as a celestial body, and that his iconography is more clearly martial than solar.
Resheph and the god of the 'Resheph of the Arrow'The epithet 'Resheph of the Arrow' (ršp ṣṣ) in the Karatepe inscription refers to a specific, localized form of the god, possibly with a distinctive cult.The epithet is a general description of the god's nature as an archer, not a reference to a distinct cult.
Resheph's relationship to the Greek ApolloThe syncretism of Resheph and Apollo in Cyprus was a natural result of their shared domains (plague, healing, archery) and was a genuine fusion of the two deities.The identification was a superficial, political or cultural imposition by the Greeks, who saw Resheph as a 'barbarian Apollo' and absorbed his cult without fully understanding it.

Resheph is the same as the Egyptian god Horus. — Resheph is a distinct Semitic deity, not a form of Horus. While he was worshipped in Egypt and sometimes syncretized with Horus in specific contexts, he maintained his own identity, iconography, and cult.

Resheph is a demon or a devil in the Bible. — The Hebrew word 'reshef' in the Bible is a common noun meaning 'pestilence', 'flame', or 'arrow', not a proper name for a demon. The biblical authors used the word to describe natural phenomena and divine judgment, not to worship or invoke a specific deity.

Resheph is a minor, unimportant god. — While not a supreme deity, Resheph was a significant and widely worshipped god across the Levant, Egypt, and Cyprus for over a millennium. He was invoked in treaties, magical texts, and votive offerings, and his cult was well-established.
