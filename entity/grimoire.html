Who It Is

Somewhere in a Bavarian monastery in the 1400s, a cleric sat copying a book that could, if you believed it, make spirits appear in a triangle of chalk on the floor. He wrote in the same Latin he used for sermons, and the rites he copied read like exorcisms played backward. That book — Clm 849, the Munich Manual — is one of thousands. For two thousand years, the grimoire has been the West's technology of the unseen: a manual that treats the cosmos as an address book, in which Solomon, Moses, and a mad Arab poet are all on the same shelf, and in which a hanged man's hand can be a candle. This is the history of the books that were read at midnight, burned at noon, and never quite stopped being copied.

Grimoires behave like instruments with a temperament. They demand preparation: purity, fasting, the correct hour and moon phase, special inks and virgin materials, and the respectful or threatening recitation of long conjurations. They are also understood as dangerous to mishandle — spirits summoned must be dismissed, or the operator suffers. Many traditions insist the book itself carries power: Davies notes that handwritten copies remained prized after print because they 'were believed to contain inherent magical powers.' Powers
Evocation and constraint of spirits

The defining art of the demonological grimoires: conjuring angels, demons, or the dead into visible appearance and compelling them to answer questions or perform tasks. The Ars Goetia supplies the names, ranks, offices, and seals of 72 spirits (Bael, Agares, Vassago, and so on) plus the rite of circle, triangle, and license to depart. The Munich Manual's rites work the same logic with clerical exorcism language inverted to conjuration.

Limit: Requires ritual purity, correct timing, protective circle and talismans; the operator must dismiss the spirit properly. Failure is portrayed as physically dangerous — spirits may attack or deceive.

Manufacture of talismans, pentacles, and magical images

Instructions for crafting objects with fixed powers: the Key of Solomon's pentacles for protection, invisibility, love, and treasure; the Picatrix's planetary talismans cut from the correct metal under the correct star; amulets and charms for healing in the Petit Albert and the Anglo-Saxon leechbook tradition.

Limit: Materials must be 'virgin' or ritually prepared; timing by planetary hours is mandatory; mis-cutting a seal voids the work.

Divination and the acquisition of hidden knowledge

Grimoires promise knowledge: the Sworn Book of Honorius offers visions of God, Hell, and Purgatory and 'knowledge of all science'; the Ars Notoria claims to grant memory and learning; hydromancy and nigromancy appear in the Hygromanteia title of the Magical Treatise of Solomon; treasure-finding divination dominates the Libro de San Cipriano.

Limit: Knowledge comes at the price of long, repeated ritual labor and, in some books, moral risk; results are unverifiable by design.

The book itself as a power object

Beyond their instructions, grimoires are credited with inherent virtue: handwritten copies outrank printed ones because the labor and secrecy of copying transmit power (Davies 2009:53–54); possession of a black book confers status on Scandinavian cunning men; authorities treated the books as dangerous enough to burn, confiscate, and index.

Limit: The power is contingent on belief and on the book's provenance; a printed mass edition was considered weaker or worthless by some traditions.

Wealth magic and the spirit pact

A large late stratum of grimoires is frankly economic: the Grand Grimoire and Dragon rouge teach the conjuration of Lucifugé Rofocale, 'the devil's chief minister,' to obtain treasure; the Petit Albert includes spells for catching fish and a Hand of Glory that renders the bearer invisible; St Cyprian books append gazetteers of treasure sites.

Limit: Pact magic typically requires a signed or witnessed bargain with hellish forces and the abandonment of Christian practice; failure is punished.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The grimoire is above all a codex: bound leaves of parchment or paper, typically quarto or octavo, meant to lie open on a desk while the operator reads aloud. Surviving manuscripts like the Key of Solomon witnesses and Clm 849 are workmanlike books — ruled columns, diagrams, circles, and tables rather than illumination. What distinguishes them visually is the iconography of use: consecrated circles drawn to scale, the pentacles or seals of spirit

Symbols — The symbolic core is the pentacle and the sigil: the operator's controls on the spirit world. The Key of Solomon contains a famous series of pentacles — disks bearing divine names, planetary symbols, and protective figures — to be drawn on virgin parchment or metal at the correct planetary hour. Accounts I
The Ring and the Temple: the Testament of SolomonTestament of Solomon, cited in Davies 2009:12–13

1st–3rd centuries CE

While Solomon builds the Temple in Jerusalem, a demon torments a workman. The archangel Michael gives Solomon a ring engraved with the Seal of Solomon, and with it the king commands demons — imprisoning them in jars and forcing them to labor on the building. The narrative catalogs the demons by name, office, and the angel who counters each. Solomon eventually worships false gods — Moloch, Baal, Rapha — loses divine favor, and writes the Testament as a warning and a guide. Establishes the template of the Solomonic grimoire: the wise king who binds spirits by divine name and seal, and the caution that the operator's own fall is the genre's recurring moral.

Seventy-two Spirits and the Triangle: the Goetia riteThe Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King (Mathers & Crowley, 1904); Peterson's edition (2001)

Compiled mid-17th century; published 1904

The operator draws a circle with divine names, sets a triangle outside it, and at the appointed hour conjures one of the 72 spirits — Bael, a king appearing as a cat-like man; Agares, a duke riding a crocodile; Vassago, a prince who declares things past and to come. The spirit is bound by names and seals to appear, answer truthfully, and depart when licensed. Failure to dismiss leaves the operator exposed. The rite became the canonical image of demon evocation in Western occultism, endlessly reprinted and adapted; its 72-spirit list shaped later demonologies in fiction and practice.

The Cleric's Handbook: necromancy from the Munich ManualClm 849, studied and translated in Kieckhefer, Forbidden Rites (1997)

15th century

A literate owner — almost certainly a cleric — compiled a working handbook of experiments: conjurations that borrow the vocabulary of exorcism, rites to summon spirits to visible appearance, to learn arts and sciences, to find treasure or cause invisibility. Kieckhefer reads the book as evidence that 'learned magic' was a practical clerical pursuit, not a peasant superstition: the same men who administered the sacraments copied and used these rites. Forbidden Rites reframed the study of grimoires: the books are not folklore but literate technology, continuous with the religious practices they parody.

Accounts II
Black Books on Trial: Iceland and the witch huntsDavies 2009:70–73

16th–17th centuries (witch-trial era)

As the witch hunts ran their course, possession of a grimoire could be deadly — but the connection was rarer than legend claims: Davies notes that in most trials the accused had no access to such books at all. Iceland was the exception: a literate society where a third of the 134 witch trials involved people who owned grimoires (the Icelandic black books, galdrabækur, and the legendary Rauðskinna). Icelandic trials, unusually, prosecuted mostly men. The trials made the grimoire a legal category of evidence; in Iceland, book ownership itself became the crime's proof. The episode shows literacy and magic colliding at the sharp end of early modern law.

The Pact with Lucifugé Rofocale: the Grand GrimoireGrand Grimoire and Dragon rouge, discussed in Davies 2009:101–104 and Guardian 2009 (item 5)

Late 18th – early 19th century (published after the French Revolution; Dragon rouge reissue)

A grimoire 'considered particularly powerful' because it does what Solomonic books deny: it teaches the operator to make a pact with the devil's chief minister, Lucifugé Rofocale, trading a written undertaking for wealth. A new version, the Dragon rouge, sold in Parisian bookshops and later circulated across French colonies in the Caribbean, where it remains known today. The Grand Grimoire became the archetype of 'black magic' in popular culture — the book that sells the soul — and a fixture of Caribbean and Latin American magical practice.

The Book That Was Never Written: the NecronomiconH. P. Lovecraft, 'The Hound' (written 1922, published 1924); 'The Nameless City' (1921); Davies, Guardian 2009 (item 9)

20th century

Lovecraft invented the Necronomicon — the Book of the Dead, 'Kitab al-Azif,' written in the 8th century by the mad poet Abdul Alhazred — as the forbidden library of the Old Ones and their summoning rites. Though purely fictional, the book escaped fiction: Davies, including it in his top 10, noted that several 'real' Necronomicons have been published over the decades and that it 'has as much a right to be considered a grimoire' as any entry on his list. The best-known is the Simon Necronomicon (1977), a deliberate modern grimoire. The Necronomicon became the most famous grimoire in the world — a fiction that generated its own magical tradition, films (The Evil Dead's Necronomicon Ex-Mortis), and a publishing niche.

Ye Bok of ye Art Magical: the Book of ShadowsGerald Gardner's Book of Shadows (late 1940s–early 1950s); Wikipedia REST lead; Davies, Guardian 2009 (item 10)

c. 1949–1953 onward

Gerald Gardner, the retired civil servant, folklorist, and occultist who founded Wicca, compiled a manuscript grimoire — the Book of Shadows — for his Bricket Wood coven, claiming descent from an old witch tradition his sources had preserved. Hand-copied by initiates, it became the founding text of modern Wicca, and the name passed into countless pagan traditions and into popular culture via television (Charmed's Book of Shadows). Revived the handwritten-grimoire practice in the 20th century and gave the genre a new, living name; the Book of Shadows is the most copied grimoire format of the modern era.

Grimoires are all about summoning demons. — Demonology is one strand. The genre's mainstream was natural magic, healing charms, divination, treasure-finding, and talisman craft — the Key of Solomon's pentacles and the Petit Albert's fish-catching spells have no demons in them.

'Grimoire' and 'Book of Shadows' are interchangeable. — Book of Shadows is a specific 20th-century Wiccan genre term coined by Gerald Gardner (c. 1949–1953), though it descends from the older grimoire practice.

During the witch trials, witches were executed for owning grimoires. — In most trials the accused had no access to such books at all (Davies 2009:70–73). Iceland is the striking exception — a third of its 134 trials involved grimoire owners.

The Key of Solomon and the Lesser Key were written by King Solomon. — Both are pseudepigraphical: the Key's Greek ancestor is 15th-century, the Lesser Key a mid-17th-century compilation. No Solomonic text predates the common era (the Testament of Solomon is 1st–3rd century CE and itself legendary).

The Necronomicon is an ancient real book. — It is a fiction by H. P. Lovecraft (first named in 1924). Every printed 'Necronomicon' postdates the fiction; the best known, 'Simon's' (1977), is a modern construction.

The Church hunted grimoire owners ruthlessly. — The Church's record is contradictory: it condemned demonic magic and indexed grimoires (1599), yet the clergy were themselves 'the main practitioners of magic and therefore the owners, transcribers, and circulators of grimoires' (Davies 2009:36).

A grimoire is one specific book (e.g., 'the black book'). — It is a genre with hundreds of distinct titles and thousands of manuscripts, each existing in competing recensions; there is no canonical text.
