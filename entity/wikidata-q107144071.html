The Approach

Among the fifty daughters of Thespius, Terpsicrate is a name that survives only in the margins of Heracles' myth—yet her story, like that of her sisters, is woven into one of the most controversial episodes of Greek heroic legend: the mass begetting of the Thespiades, the fifty sons who would carry the hero's blood across Boeotia. Her son, Euryopes, is one of the few offspring recorded, but her own fate, whether as a willing bride or an unwitting victim, remains a shadowy footnote in the ancient sources.

Content advisory. This entry discusses sexual encounters that, in the ancient sources, are depicted as consensual but involve intoxication and deception. Modern readers may view these events as non-consensual. The myth is presented without romanticization, with attention to the ethical complexities.
Identity

Terpsicrate

Pronunciation turp-SIK-ruh-tee (English approximation); in Classical Greek: terp-si-KRA-tay.

Etymology

The name Terpsicrate (Τερψικράτη) is likely derived from the Greek verb τέρπω (terpō, 'to delight') and κράτος (kratos, 'strength, power'), thus possibly meaning 'delighting in strength' or 'she who rejoices in power.' However, this is a modern etymological conjecture; ancient sources do not provide an etymology.

Cultural Ownership

Greek mythology, specifically the Boeotian tradition of Thespiae. She is a minor figure in the Heracles cycle.

Disputed Identifications

No known disputes. She is a distinct name among the fifty daughters. Some sources list the daughters' names, but there are variations in the lists; Terpsicrate appears consistently in Apollodorus and in Tzetzes' account.

Description

Appearance

No physical description survives. As one of the fifty daughters of Thespius, she is presumed to be of noble birth and beauty, but the sources do not describe her appearance.

Symbols & Iconography

No known symbols or iconography. She is not depicted in ancient art or on coins.

Behaviour & Personality

No specific personality traits are recorded. In the myth, she is one of the daughters who sleeps with Heracles, though the circumstances vary. In one version, she is a willing participant; in another, she is deceived.

Domains & Habitats

Thespiae in Boeotia, the kingdom of her father Thespius.

Version Differences

The main differences concern the circumstances of the union: Apollodorus states that Heracles slept with all fifty daughters in one night or over seven nights, while some accounts say he bedded all but one in a single night. The one who refused was condemned to remain a virgin and serve as his priestess, but Terpsicrate is not identified as that daughter in the sources.

Stories & Myths

The Begetting of the Thespiades

Source Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.4.10; Diodorus Siculus 4.29; Pausanias 9.27.6-7; Tzetzes, Chiliades 2.221-222Culture GreekPeriod Mythical (Heroic Age)

Summary

Heracles, while hunting the Cithaeronian lion, was hosted by King Thespius for fifty days. Each night, Thespius sent one of his daughters to Heracles, and in his drunken state, Heracles believed he was with the same woman each time. All fifty daughters bore sons, including Terpsicrate, who gave birth to Euryopes. In some versions, the encounters happened over seven nights, with seven daughters each night. In another account, all but one daughter slept with Heracles in a single night; the one who refused was punished with perpetual virginity and service as his priestess.

Consequences

The fifty sons, known as the Thespiades, became the founders of Thespiae and spread Heracles' lineage throughout Boeotia. Terpsicrate's son Euryopes is one of the few named offspring.

Variants

The number of nights and the order of the daughters vary. Some sources say Heracles slept with all fifty in one night, others over seven nights. The daughter who refused is unnamed in the extant sources, but Terpsicrate is not that daughter.

Deception and intoxication

Source Apollodorus, Diodorus Siculus

Description

Thespius deliberately made Heracles drunk and sent his daughters to him without his knowledge, leading to sexual encounters he did not intend. This raises ethical questions about consent.

Scholarly Context

Modern scholars often interpret this as a tale of heroic fertility, but also note the problematic nature of the deception. Ancient audiences may have viewed it differently, but the myth's inherent power imbalance is evident.

Punishment of the daughter who refused

Source Apollodorus, Diodorus Siculus

Description

One daughter who refused to sleep with Heracles was condemned to perpetual virginity and servitude as his priestess. This is a punitive measure for exercising autonomy.

Scholarly Context

This detail underscores the patriarchal norms of ancient Greek society, where female refusal was punished. The story serves to reinforce the expectation of female submission to male heroes.

Contradictions & Disputes

Number of nights

Position A

Heracles slept with all fifty daughters in one night.

Position A — evidence

Diodorus Siculus 4.29

Position B

Heracles slept with the daughters over seven nights, seven each night.

Position B — evidence

Apollodorus 2.4.10 (some manuscripts)

Scholarly Consensus

Scholars note the inconsistency and suggest the one-night version may be a later exaggeration to emphasize Heracles' prowess.

Consent of the daughters

Position A

The daughters were willingly sent by their father and complied.

Position A — evidence

Apollodorus implies the daughters were aware of their father's plan.

Position B

The daughters were deceived or coerced.

Position B — evidence

The deception of Heracles suggests the daughters may have been unaware of the identity of their partner, but their own willingness is not explicitly stated.

Scholarly Consensus

Modern interpretations vary; some see it as a form of ritualized hospitality, others as exploitation.

The daughter who refused

Position A

All fifty daughters slept with Heracles.

Position A — evidence

Apollodorus and Diodorus mention all fifty in the main narrative.

Position B

One daughter refused and was punished.

Position B — evidence

A variant in later sources (e.g., Tzetzes) mentions the refusal and punishment.

Scholarly Consensus

The refusal story is considered a later addition to explain the existence of a virgin priestess of Heracles.
