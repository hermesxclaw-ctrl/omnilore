Comparative: The Bloodstone (Heliotrope) LorePliny the Elder, Naturalis Historia Book 37.165; Kunz 1913 pp. 104-110 summarizing classical and medieval lapidaries

1st century CE (Pliny) through medieval lapidaries (13th-17th century)

Heliotrope, called bloodstone (green jasper with red spots), was believed to staunch bleeding, render the wearer invisible, and confer courage when smeared with blood. Medieval lapidaries claimed the red spots were Christ's blood. The stone acts upon blood, rather than blood enchanting the stone. No sorcery system name is given. Demonstrates ancient linkage of blood and jewel without a unified 'Sorcery of the Blood and the Jewel' system; illustrates how later fantasy inverts the relationship.

c. 500-1000 CE (Puranic compilation)

Kaustubha is the divine jewel worn by Vishnu on his chest, born from the churning of the ocean; Syamantaka is a solar jewel that grants wealth but brings calamity when misappropriated, causing Krishna to recover it. Blood lineage (Vrishni clan) and jewel ownership drive the plot, but no blood-activated sorcery is described. Shows jewel as divine status object tied to lineage and moral legitimacy, not blood sorcery.

Comparative: Alchemical Blood and the Philosopher's StoneParacelsus, De mineralibus; later alchemical compendia summarized in Lawrence M. Principe, The Secrets of Alchemy (2013)

16th century (Paracelsus) with later elaborations

Paracelsian and later alchemists linked human blood (as microcosmic quintessence) and gem-like stones (philosopher's stone as perfected matter). Blood was sometimes used as a prima materia, and the stone was described as ruby-like. No single system called 'Sorcery of the Blood and the Jewel' appears; the linkage is philosophical analogy. Illustrates intellectual precedent for combining hematology and lithology in Western esotericism, but as allegory, not codified sorcery.
