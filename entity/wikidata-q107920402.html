Who It Is

In the dust of third-millennium Ebla, scribes writing in wedge-shaped signs recorded delivery after delivery for a god whose name began with a guttural gasp — 'A-da-bal. He was not a distant sky-father or a famous storm lord, but a local power so central that kings left their palace to seek him in his own towns, and so fragile that when Ebla burned, he vanished with it. We know him from ledger books, not myths — a god remembered in sheep and silver, whose true face remains blank.

Personality and mythology unattested. No myths, hymns, or prayers to Hadabal survive. Behavior must be inferred from cult practice: he receives regular offerings from the palace, from the king and queen personally, and from high officials; his sanctuaries are destinations for royal pilgrimage, suggesting a role as protector of kingship, territory, or fertility. He is not described as wrathful, benevolent, or trickster in any text. The absence of narrative means no motive, speech, or moral character can be reconstructed.

Powers
Hypothetical agricultural/fertility potency

Proposed but unattested power to ensure crop fertility, livestock abundance, or rural prosperity. Based on location of his cult centers in agricultural towns and frequency of sheep/goat and grain-related offerings. No text explicitly calls him 'lord of grain' or describes a fertility act.

Limit: No direct evidence; offerings of livestock are generic to all major gods. Cannot be cited as fact.

Hypothetical lunar affiliation

Early proposal that Hadabal was a moon god, based on speculative etymology and later Syrian moon cults at Harran. No Eblaite text links Hadabal to moon (Sumerogram EN.ZU / Eblaite Wariḫum is the distinct moon god at Ebla).

Limit: Ebla has a separate, well-attested moon god (Suinu/Wariḫum). No astral determinative or lunar epithet for Hadabal.

Royal legitimation and territorial protection (inferred function)

Inferred power to legitimize kingship and protect the kingdom's territory. Evidence: the king and queen's ritual pilgrimage to Hadabal's sanctuaries at Luban and Larugadu during kingship rituals; high rank in god lists alongside Kura (city god). Suggests he embodied the chora (countryside) complementing Kura's city.

Limit: Function inferred from ritual context, not stated as divine epithet. No text says 'Hadabal protects the king.'

Reception of offerings and clothing (cultic interaction)

Attested ability in cultic logic to receive and be pleased by offerings: textiles to clothe the statue, sheep/goats for sacrifice, silver, oil. Texts record 'clothing of Hadabal' (túg for the god), implying anthropomorphic statue that is dressed and fed.

Limit: Standard for all Eblaite gods; not a distinctive superhuman power. No miracle or feat narrated.

Accounts I
The Royal Pilgrimage to Hadabal of LubanARET XI 1-2, Ritual of Kingship (coronation ritual), edition Alfonso Archi 2015

c. 2400-2300 BCE, Early Bronze Age IVA

The central 'story' of Hadabal is not a myth but a ritual itinerary. Upon accession or renewal of kingship, the king (en) and queen (maliktum) leave Ebla and travel to the sanctuary of Hadabal at Luban (and Larugadu). There they make offerings, undergo purification, and spend multiple days. The text prescribes deliveries of textiles, livestock, and precious objects for Hadabal of Luban. The journey legitimizes the royal couple before the territorial god. No dialogue or mythic episode is narrated; the story is the performance itself. Ritual completion confirms kingship; failure to perform would presumably delegitimize rule. The pilgrimage integrates peripheral cult centers into the Eblaite state. After Ebla's destruction, the ritual ceases and Hadabal disappears.

Offerings to Hadabal of LarugaduAdministrative tablets ARET VII 150, TM.75.G.2428, TM.75.G.10074; discussed in Pomponio & Xella 1997

c. 2400-2300 BCE

Monthly and festival offering lists record deliveries to Hadabal of Larugadu: sheep (udu), KIN textiles, silver rings, and jars of oil. In one large offering list, Hadabal of Larugadu receives offerings immediately after Kura and Barama, indicating second-tier rank. The lists are bookkeeping, not narrative, but they constitute the narrative of cult maintenance: palace officials dispatch goods to the temple, where they are presumably sacrificed or used to clothe the statue. Demonstrates economic support for Hadabal's temple; frequency suggests he was among the most expensive cults for the palace. No mythic consequence narrated.

Accounts II
Hadabal in the Great Offering List alongside Kura and BaramaTM.75.G.2428 (= ARET VII 150) and parallel lists TM.75.G.1237

c. 2400-2300 BCE

A major state offering list enumerates the pantheon in hierarchical order for a collective sacrifice. Kura (city god) and his spouse Barama open the list, followed by Hadabal (often without geographic qualifier, representing the god in general or at Ebla itself), then Ishtar, Resheph, Hadda, and others. Each receives a sheep and textiles. The list's order is itself a theological statement: Hadabal's placement directly after the city couple signals his status as the foremost territorial god. Establishes pantheon hierarchy for modern reconstruction. Shows Hadabal's integration into the official state cult, not a folk or foreign deity.

The Clothing and Adornment of HadabalAdministrative textile deliveries ARET I, ARET VII; Archi 2015, p. 599

c. 2400-2300 BCE

Multiple tablets record deliveries of prestigious textiles (KIN, túg, íb) and silver ornaments 'for Hadabal' (ana 'A3-da-bal). These are interpreted as garments for the cult statue, which was periodically dressed and adorned. One text records a silver pectoral for Hadabal of Luban. No description of the statue's appearance is given, but the practice parallels better-documented dressing of Kura's statue. Indicates anthropomorphic cult image and ongoing investment in its maintenance. Provides the only indirect clue to how Eblaites visualized the god — as a clothed human-like figure.

Disappearance after the Destruction of EblaAbsence in post-destruction archives: Ebla Mardikh IIB2 (Ur III period) and Old Syrian Ebla (c. 2000-1600 BCE) texts; Archi 2015, Matthiae 2010

c. 2300 BCE destruction; verified absence c. 2300-1600 BCE

After the violent destruction of Palace G (attributed to Sargonic Akkad or internal warfare, c. 2300 BCE), the entire Early Eblaite pantheon of Kura, Barama, and Hadabal vanishes from the record. Later Ebla texts (when the city is reoccupied) invoke Hadda of Aleppo, Ishtar, and other Syrian gods, but never Hadabal. The 'story' is one of extinction: a god whose cult was so tied to the Early Bronze Age Eblaite state and its specific rural sanctuaries that the state's collapse ended his worship. No text narrates his death; the silence is the evidence. Demonstrates that Hadabal was not a pan-Syrian deity but a localized Eblaite territorial god. His disappearance is a key datum for understanding Early Syrian religion's discontinuity.

Hadabal was the Eblaite version of the Mesopotamian storm god Hadad/Adad — Ebla had a separate storm god Hadda (Hadad) attested alongside Hadabal in the same archives. Hadabal is a distinct, indigenous Eblaite deity with no storm attributes attested; the names are not linguistically related beyond superficial similarity.

We know what Hadabal looked like or that he was depicted with lunar crescents or agricultural symbols — No statue, seal, or description of Hadabal has been identified. No symbol is linked to him in texts. Any image labeled Hadabal online is hypothetical or misattributed. Proposals of lunar or agricultural iconography are modern hypotheses without textual or archaeological support.

Hadabal continued to be worshipped at Aleppo or in later Syrian religion — Hadabal is exclusively Early Bronze Age Eblaite (c. 2400-2300 BCE) and disappears completely after Palace G's destruction, unlike Ishtar, Resheph, and Hadda who continue. No later text at Ebla, Mari, Alalakh, or Aleppo mentions him.
