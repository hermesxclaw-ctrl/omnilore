Who It Is

She was born not from a womb but from a gamble between gods — when Amaterasu chewed her brother's sword and Susanoo chewed her jewels, the mist that rose from their breath became three sisters. Tagitsuhime, the Princess of the Rushing Rapids, was the middle daughter, given the wild water between open sea and sheltered shore to guard. For 1,300 years sailors crossing the Genkai Sea have whispered to her before daring the strait, and on a forbidden island where no woman may set foot, archaeologists still unearth gold and glass offered to her and her sisters.

In classical sources, Tagitsuhime has no independent speech or personality beyond being a product of the ukei oath proving Susanoo's sincerity (attested). As a Munakata goddess, her behavior is protective and purifying: she guards the sea strait, guides ships, prevents shipwreck, and mediates between the heavenly plain (Takamagahara) and the earthly/foreign realm (Korea/China route). Powers
Guardianship of the Sea Strait (Kaijo Heiwa / Funadama)

As one of the Michinushi (Way-Masters), Tagitsuhime protects vessels traversing the Genkai Nada and Korean Strait, calming waves and preventing wreck. Prayers for safe voyage (kaijo anzen, kotsu anzen) are directed to her and her sisters.

Limit: Power is locative and collective; she acts together with her sisters to cover open sea, middle current, and harbor. No myth shows her acting alone outside Munakata. Requires ritual purity and offerings; impurity or taboo violation is said to nullify protection.

Ukei Truth-Revelation (Ukei no Kami)

Born from the ukei (oath ordeal) between Amaterasu and Susanoo, her very existence proves Susanoo's pure intent (his claim that he had no evil heart). Thus she embodies divinatory truth and oath-verification.

Limit: Passive proof rather than active divination; she does not herself perform ukei after birth. Nihon Shoki variants dispute whether she proves Amaterasu's or Susanoo's truth depending on parentage version.

Purification and Barrier (Sae / Kekkai)

As a Munakata deity at the frontier, she purifies travelers entering Yamato and bars impurity, disease, and foreign malign influence from crossing the sea. Associated with sae no kami (barrier deity) function.

Limit: Not explicitly stated in Kojiki/Nihon Shoki; inferred from shrine placement and later exegesis. Adaptation-specific elaboration in some modern Shinto theology.

Bestowal of Maritime Prosperity (Gyogyo Hojo)

Fisherfolk pray to Tagitsuhime for abundant catch and safe return; Munakata fishermen historically offered first catch at Nakatsu-miya.

Limit: Folk belief, not canonical myth. No ancient text attributes fishing bounty specifically to Tagitsuhime alone vs. trio.

Accounts I
The Ukei at Ame no Yasugawara - Birth of the Three SistersKojiki Book 1; Nihon Shoki Book 1 Jindai

Nara period codification of earlier oral tradition (712/720 CE); mythic time = Age of the Gods

Susanoo, expelled from Takamagahara for his rampages, claims his heart is pure. Amaterasu proposes an ukei (oath ordeal) at the dry riverbed of Heaven. They exchange objects: Amaterasu receives Susanoo's ten-span sword, Susanoo receives Amaterasu's Yasakani magatama necklace. Each chews the object and spits out mist/children. In Kojiki, Susanoo chewing the jewels produces three goddesses: Okitsushima-hime, Tagitsu-hime, and Ichikishima-hime. Their birth proves Susanoo's sincerity, but he then goes on a victory rampage destroying rice paddies. In Nihon Shoki main text the parentage is reversed (Amaterasu chews the sword to produce the three), with variants preserving the Kojiki version. Establishes the Munakata goddesses' divine parentage and their role as proof of oath; provides charter for their descent to earth; precipitates Susanoo's expulsion and subsequent Yamata no Orochi myth.

Descent to Munakata - Enshrinement in the Genkai SeaNihon Shoki Book 1 Jindai, following ukei; Kogo Shui 807

Nara period codification; mythic time immediately after ukei

After their birth, Amaterasu instructs the three goddesses: 'You three deities shall descend and dwell in the center of the reed plain, assisting the heavenly grandson (Ninigi). You shall be worshipped by the Munakata clan.' They descend to Munakata (northern Kyushu) and divide the strait: one on Okinoshima (open sea), one on Oshima (middle), one on the mainland. This establishes the sacred geography still observed at Munakata Taisha. Tagitsuhime is traditionally the middle deity at Nakatsu-miya on Oshima. Charter myth for Munakata Taisha and the Munakata clan's hereditary priesthood; explains why the strait is sacred; links imperial lineage (Ninigi's descent) to sea protection.

The Okinoshima Rituals - Eighty Thousand OfferingsArchaeological record of Okinoshima iseki; Nihon Shoki Book 19 (Emperor Keitai) and Shoku Nihongi references to Munakata rites; Engishiki norito

Historical: 4th-9th century CE ritual practice; textual references 6th-10th century

For over 500 years, ritualists (likely Munakata clan priests and Yamato envoys) sailed to the isolated rock of Okinoshima to offer treasures to the Munakata goddesses before attempting the dangerous crossing to Korea. Deposits include 4th-century iron weapons, 5th-6th century Korean-style gilt mirrors, 7th-8th century Tang Chinese coins and glass, and gold rings. Rituals progressed from rock-top (iwakami) to open-air (iwaue) to semi-shrine forms. Though Okinoshima is primarily associated with Okitsushima-hime, shrine tradition holds all three sisters were worshipped together, and Tagitsuhime received offerings as middle guardian. The site's continuity demonstrates state-level investment in the goddesses' protection for diplomatic missions (kentōshi, kenzuishi). Created one of Japan's richest archaeological assemblages (National Treasure); underpins UNESCO World Heritage status; shows shift from natural rock worship to shrine architecture.

Accounts II
Empress Jingu's Korean Expedition and the Munakata OracleNihon Shoki Books 9-10 (Empress Jingu); Kojiki Book 2; Munakata Taisha engi (medieval shrine legends)

Nihon Shoki codification 720 CE describing legendary 3rd-4th century; medieval engi elaboration 13th-15th century

Before her legendary invasion of Silla (Korea), Empress Jingu prays for safe passage and receives an oracle from the Munakata goddesses (and Sumiyoshi). The goddesses promise to calm the waves and grant victory if properly worshipped. Jingu establishes rites at Munakata and dedicates spoils. While Kojiki/Nihon Shoki do not name Tagitsuhime individually in this episode, medieval Munakata engi explicitly list Tagitsuhime among the three who appeared to Jingu as female divers (ama) or as a storm-calming light. The story reinforces the goddesses' role as protectors of overseas military and diplomatic ventures. Links Munakata worship to imperial expansion ideology; used historically to justify rites for safe sea travel; medieval Munakata clan used Jingu legend to claim prestige and land rights.

Syncretism with Benzaiten - The Identification of Ichikishimahime and the TrioMedieval Shinto-Buddhist syncretic texts: Keiran Shuyoshu (1311), Munakata Taisha shrine records, Enoshima Engi (1047/ medieval)

11th-14th century syncretic theology

Under honji suijaku (original ground-manifest trace) theory, the Munakata goddesses were identified as manifestations of Buddhist deities. Ichikishimahime was equated with Benzaiten (Saraswati), goddess of water, music, and eloquence, at Enoshima and Itsukushima. By extension, all three Munakata sisters were sometimes called 'Munakata Benzaiten' or described as Benzaiten's attendants. Tagitsuhime thus appears in medieval temple-shrine records as a form of Benzaiten or as one of Benzaiten's fifteen attendants. This led to shared iconography (biwa lute, white snake) that is not native to early Shinto. The syncretism was officially separated (shinbutsu bunri) in 1868, restoring Tagitsuhime's independent Shinto identity, but popular conflation persists. Created distinctive water-goddess art where Tagitsuhime is depicted with Benzaiten attributes; caused centuries of conflated worship at Munakata and Enoshima; modern scholarship must disentangle Shinto and Buddhist layers.

The Munakata Clan Ancestry - Daughters as Clan MothersKojiki and Nihon Shoki genealogies; Shinsen Shojiroku (815 CE) - Kogetsu clan register; Munakata clan genealogy (Munakata-shi keizu)

Nara-Heian clan genealogy construction

The Munakata no Kimi (Lords of Munakata), a powerful northern Kyushu maritime clan, claimed descent from the Munakata goddesses or from their service. Some genealogies claim the clan's ancestor is a son or descendant of Tagitsuhime or her sisters, or that the clan was appointed as saishu (ritual masters) to serve them. The Shinsen Shojiroku lists Munakata clan as descendants of heavenly deities. This ancestry legitimized the clan's control over sea trade and ritual on Okinoshima. Tagitsuhime's specific genealogical role varies: some lines make her the ancestress of the Oshima sub-lineage. Secured hereditary priesthood at Munakata Taisha until modern times; explains state patronage; demonstrates how myth was used for political legitimation.
