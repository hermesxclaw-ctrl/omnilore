Who It Is

Anahita is the Iranian goddess of waters, fertility, wisdom, and healing. She appears in the Avesta primarily in Yasht 5 (the Aban Yasht), where she is praised as the personification of the mythical world-river Aredvi, whose waters sustain all creation. Historically, she merged during the Achaemenid period with a western Iranian deity *Anāhīti who had absorbed traits of the Mesopotamian Ishtar, acquiring identification with Venus and martial attributes. Her cult was patronized by Artaxerxes II, who erected the first known cult statues of the goddess across the empire. Temples to Anahita were built beside streams and rivers, and she remained a central figure of Zoroastrian piety into the Islamic period. Her legacy persists in the sacred wells and mountain shrines of contemporary Zoroastrian communities.

Form

In the Aban Yasht, Anahita is described as a beautiful, strong maiden wearing beaver skins (Avestan yakana, verses 5.3, 5.7, 5.20, 5.129). She rides a chariot drawn by four horses named Wind, Rain, Clouds, and Sleet (verse 5.120). Achaemenid and Sasanian art depicts her in flowing robes, often with lotus motifs symbolising purity and life. Sasanian rock reliefs at Taq-e Bostan show investiture scenes where she appears alongside Ahura Mazda and the king. In Greco-Roman sources she is conflated with Aphrodite/Ishtar, so classical depictions give her the iconography of a Venus-type goddess: sometimes semi-naked, sometimes armed. However, Zoroastrian textual evidence consistently associates her with water and fertility rather than sexuality per se.

Powers

Water Sovereignty: As the personification of Aredvi Sura, the mythical world-river, Anahita commands all fresh waters—rivers, springs, wells, rainfall. She is the source of all earthly fertility, making her the supreme water-yazata in the Zoroastrian pantheon. Priests make offerings to her at every Yasna service through the Ab-Zohr (offering to the waters). Fertility and Procreation: She purifies the seed of men and wombs of women, encourages lactation, and ensures the growth of crops and the increase of herds. She is invoked for both agricultural fertility and human reproduction. In popular belief her waters carry life-giving force to all living things. Healing and Purity: The Aban Yasht calls her 'wide-flowing and healing.' Her waters possess purificatory power against disease and daeva-influence. Zoroastrians continue to offer water from sacred wells and streams in her name, maintaining a tradition of ritual purity linked to her essence. Wisdom and Insight: Verse 5.86 of the Aban Yasht specifically counsels priests and pupils to pray to Aredvi Sura Anahita for insight and knowledge. This distinguishes her from purely agricultural water-deities and elevates her as a patron of spiritual and intellectual learning within the Zoroastrian framework. Apotropaic Protection: The hymn describes her as 'efficacious against the daevas,' meaning she defends creation from destructive, lying spiritual forces. This protective dimension aligns her with the broader Zoroastrian cosmic battle between order (asha) and chaos (druj). Venus / Celestial Association: Through conflation with Ishtar, Anahita acquired identification with the planet Venus. This added a celestial-astral dimension to her powers and linked her to cycles of dawn and evening star phenomena. The association with Venus also transmitted martial and sovereignty traits from Ishtar, which are visible in Achaemenid iconography.

Behavior & Habitat

Anahita acts as a cosmic river-goddess who fertilises the earth, nurtures crops and herds, purifies human seed and wombs, and encourages the flow of milk for newborns. She is efficacious against the daevas (destructive spirits), demonstrating a protective-apotropaic function. She grants wisdom and insight to priests and pupils who pray to her (Yasht 5.86), making her a patron of learning as well as fertility. In her cosmic role she is 'as great in bigness as all these waters which flow forth upon the earth'—not merely a local river but the source of all world waters.

Accounts

The most historically consequential narrative involving Anahita is the Achaemenid king Artaxerxes II Mnemon's (r. 404–358 BCE) decision to erect the first cult statues of the goddess. According to Berosus (c. 285 BCE), the Persians had previously held no images of their gods—a claim corroborated by Herodotus, who remarks that Persians considered statue-making foolish. Artaxerxes II broke with this aniconic tradition by commissioning statues of Aphrodite Anaitis (Anahita) and placing them in temples across the empire: Babylon, Susa, Ecbatana, Bactria, Persepolis, Damascus, and Sardis. Mary Boyce hypothesised that Zoroastrian iconoclasm and the rise of sacred-fire worship may have been a direct reaction to the introduction of Anahita's cult images. This reform fundamentally altered Zoroastrian ritual architecture and triggered an iconoclastic movement during the Sasanian period that eliminated cult images entirely, replacing them with sacred fires.

Before the 4th century BCE, the Iranian yazata Aredvi Sura was merged with a western Iranian goddess *Anāhīti, herself an analogue of the Semitic goddess Ishtar-Inanna. The merger transferred to Anahita the identification with Venus, the mantle of maidenly fertility, and the martial features of a war goddess. The Greek rendering Anaïtis reflects this syncretic form. This conflation explains why classical authors sometimes describe Anahita in terms borrowed from Aphrodite and Ishtar, while Avestan texts preserve her older, more purely Iranian character as a river and fertility deity.

The most enduring ritual story connected to Anahita is the Ab-Zohr ('offering to the waters'), performed during the Yasna service—the primary act of worship in Zoroastrianism. Verses from Yasht 5 form the greater part of the Aban Nyashes, the liturgy to the waters in the Khordeh Avesta. The ritual positions Anahita as the divine recipient of the primary Zoroastrian sacrifice, making her, in effect, the central divinity of the most important public ceremony. This centrality is reflected in the continuing Zoroastrian practice of making offerings to household wells and nearby streams.

According to comparative philology championed by Herman Lommel, Anahita's deepest layer derives from an Indo-Iranian divinity of the mythical world-river. Lommel proposed that the original name of this deity was *Sarasvati (still worshipped in Vedic India), with the Iranian cognate *Harahvatī—the name preserved in the Avestan Aredvi and in the region around modern Kabul (Avestan Harakhvati, Old Persian Hara(h)uvati, Greek Arachosia). The actual name eventually fell out of Iranian use, while the epithet Aredvi Sura Anahita remained. This origin story explains why Anahita, like the Vedic Saraswati, nurtures crops and herds and is simultaneously a divinity and the mythical river she personifies. Unlike Saraswati, however, she absorbed Ishtar's traits and became more complex.

After the Islamic conquest of Persia, Anahita's cult did not simply disappear. The mountain shrine of Bibi Shahrbanu at Ray (ancient Rhagae, central Media) is very probably a former dedication to Anahita, later assimilated into Shiite Islam as the shrine of Bibi Shahrbanu ('Lady of the King'), a figure linked to Husayn ibn Ali's sister or daughter. This survival pattern—where Zoroastrian sacred sites were re-dedicated to Islamic holy figures—is a common mechanism of Persian religious continuity. The parallel shrine of Banu-Pars ('the Lady of Persia') at Yazd remains active in Zoroastrian practice and echoes Anahita's original domain.

What We Don't Know

The Anahita dossier is substantially grounded in Avestan textual evidence, classical sources, and archaeological attestation. Key gaps remain: (1) The exact archaeological identification of Anahita in Achaemenid and Sasanian art is debated—Canby (1989) warns against over-identifying isolated female figures. (2) The precise mechanism of the Ishtar conflation before the 4th century BCE lacks contemporary documentary evidence; we rely on later Greek and Armenian sources. (3) The extent and nature of Anahita's cult in non-Zoroastrian communities (Elamite, Babylonian) is poorly attested outside Berosus and Herodotus. (4) The Iranian cognate *Harahvatī cannot be verified epigraphically; no Old Persian or Elamite inscription names the river-goddess directly. (5) Details of post-Islamic folk veneration in rural Persia require Persian-language ethnographic sources not yet consulted. (6) The relationship between Anahita and later Islamic-Bektashi sacred geography needs more systematic cross-referencing with Turkish and Kurdish shrine traditions.
