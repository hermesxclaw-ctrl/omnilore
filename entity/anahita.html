Who It Is

Anahita (Aredvi Sura Anahita, meaning 'the Moist, the Mighty, the Immaculate') is the great Persian goddess of waters, fertility, healing, and wisdom—one of the most widely worshipped deities of pre-Islamic Iran whose cult spread across the Persian Empire and influenced neighboring religious traditions. The Aban Yasht, the fifth hymn of the Avesta, is entirely dedicated to her and provides one of the most vivid divine portraits in Zoroastrian literature. She is described as a beautiful, powerful maiden, tall and strong, wearing a golden crown of a hundred stars with eight rays, golden earrings, a golden necklace, and a beaver-skin garment—the beaver being associated with water. She drives a chariot drawn by four divine horses: Wind, Rain, Cloud, and Sleet. She is the source of the celestial river that flows from the peak of the cosmic mountain Hara Berezaiti (Alborz) down into the earthly world, nourishing all rivers, lakes, and seas. Anahita is associated with human fertility as well: she purifies the seed of all males, the womb of all females, and grants easy childbirth. She also bestows wisdom, courage, and victory upon those who sacrifice to her properly, and Persian kings and heroes—including legendary figures like Yima (Jamshid) and the hero Thraetaona (Fereydun)—are recorded as having offered her sacrifices. Her cult was syncretized with several Near Eastern goddesses: in Babylon with Ishtar, in Anatolia with Artemis and Cybele, and in the Hellenistic period she was identified with Athena and Aphrodite. The Achaemenid king Artaxerxes II (404–358 BCE) was particularly devoted to Anahita and constructed temples to her throughout the empire—the first known temples dedicated to a named divinity in Zoroastrian history, which had previously favored open-air fire altars. Her cult remained powerful through the Sassanian period until the Islamic conquest, and her legacy persists in Persian cultural memory.
