Who It Is

In the deep, dark forests of the Ardennes, a land of dense trees and wild boars that could gut a man with a single tusk, there was a goddess who rode the most dangerous beast of all as if it were a pony. Her name was Arduinna, the High One, and she is one of the most mysterious figures in the Celtic pantheon — a huntress so powerful that we have her image, but no stories to tell of her deeds. She is a goddess of pure, untamed wilderness, a silent queen of the hills who has left behind only a whisper of her existence in the bronze and stone of the Roman era.

As a hunting deity, her behavior is inferred from her attributes: she is a mistress of the wild, capable of riding and controlling the most dangerous beast of the ancient European forest — the wild boar. She represents the power of the huntress who dominates nature, rather than a nurturing or agricultural figure.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The only definitive visual representation of Arduinna is the bronze figurine in the British Museum. She is depicted as a young woman riding a boar side-saddle. Her hair is styled in a classical Roman manner, and she wears a flowing tunic. She holds an object in her right hand, which is too small to be a spear and is more likely a knife or a patera. The boar is rendered with a bristling mane, emphasizing its wild nature. This iconography is unique

Symbols — The wild boar is her primary and defining symbol. The bow and arrow are attributed to her by Roman interpretation, though not confirmed by the figurine.

Domains — Forests, hunting, wild animals (particularly the boar), the Ardennes region, and by extension, the liminal space between civilization and the wild. Her name connects her to the physical heights of the Ardennes, suggesting she was also a guardian of mountain passes and high places.

N/A

No myths, legends, or stories about Arduinna have survived. She is known only through epigraphic and archaeological evidence. This is common for many regional Celtic deities, whose oral traditions were never written down and were lost with the decline of their cults. The lack of narrative means we can only understand her through her attributes and name, leaving her personality and deeds entirely in the realm of inference.

Arduinna was a major pan-Celtic goddess. — Her worship was highly localized to the Ardennes region. She is not attested anywhere else.

Arduinna is the same as the Irish goddess Áine. — This is a false equivalence. 'Arduinna' is Gaulish, 'Áine' is Irish. They share no etymological root or attested connection.

She was a 'boar goddess' in the sense of being a boar herself. — She is depicted as a human woman riding a boar, not as a boar. The boar is her mount and symbol of power, not her form.
