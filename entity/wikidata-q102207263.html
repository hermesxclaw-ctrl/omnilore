Who It Is

Standing at the edge of a cliff is to confront the raw power of geological time—a vertical slice of Earth's history, carved by wind, water, and ice over millennia. From the dizzying heights of Mount Thor's overhanging face to the submerged abysses of the Kermadec Trench, cliffs are nature's most dramatic sculptures, defining coastlines, mountain ranges, and the very limits of human endurance.

Cliffs are dynamic geological features, constantly changing due to erosion and weathering. They can retreat inland, collapse, and form new features over time. They are not living entities but are subject to physical processes.

Erosion resistance

Cliffs are composed of rock resistant to weathering, allowing them to maintain steep profiles.

Limit: Even resistant rock eventually erodes.

Gravity-driven mass wasting

Cliffs experience rockfalls, landslides, and scree formation due to gravity.

Limit: Varies with rock type and moisture.

Form & Signs

Appearance — A cliff is a vertical or near-vertical rock face, often with a sharp drop. They can be composed of various rock types, including sandstone, limestone, chalk, dolomite, granite, and basalt. Cliffs often have scree slopes (talus) at their base, formed from fallen rock. In moist areas, soil may obscure the talus. Some cliffs feature waterfalls, rock shelters, or mushroom-shaped rock formations.

Symbols — In art and culture, cliffs often symbolize danger, isolation, the sublime, or the boundary between land and sea. They appear in landscape paintings, literature, and film as dramatic backdrops.

Domains — Cliffs are found in coastal areas, mountains, along rivers, and in escarpments. They can be terrestrial or underwater. They provide habitats for various plants and animals, including nesting birds.

Geological time

Cliffs form through weathering and erosion, often at coastlines, in mountains, and along rivers. The process involves the removal of softer material, leaving resistant rock as vertical faces. Creation of dramatic landscapes, habitats, and hazards.
