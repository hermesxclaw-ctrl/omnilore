Circe, the enchantress of Aeaea, who knows the herbs of power and the secrets of transformation — in whose cup, beasts lie sleeping.

Who It Is

Circe is the enchantress-goddess of magic and transformation, daughter of Helios (the Sun) and the Oceanid Perse. She dwells on the island of Aeaea in a stone palace surrounded by enchanted beasts — men she has transformed into lions, wolves, and pigs through her magical potions and wands. In Homer's Odyssey, Odysseus's crew is turned into pigs by Circe's drugged wine, but Odysseus, protected by the herb moly (given by Hermes), resists her magic and forces her to restore his men. He then stays with Circe for a year as her lover, and she gives him crucial advice for his journey home (including how to consult the dead). Circe is also the mother of Telegonus (by Odysseus), who later accidentally kills his father. In later traditions, Circe is the aunt of Medea and a master of herbal magic. She purified the Argonauts after the murder of Medea's brother Apsyrtus. Circe embodies the dangerous, seductive power of female magic — the ability to see through men's natures and reveal their animal selves. She is not purely evil; she genuinely loved Odysseus and helped him after he proved worthy. Her story has been reclaimed in modern feminist retellings (especially Madeline Miller's Circe).

Attested in Homer's Odyssey / Hesiod's Theogony / Ovid's MetamorphosesEra Archaic to Classical Greece (c. 700-400 BCE)

Form

Homer calls Circe 'a dreadful goddess with lovely hair and human speech'. As a descendant of Helios, she has (per Apollonius of Rhodes) flashing golden eyes that shoot out rays of light; the Orphic Argonautica adds hair 'like fiery rays'. No Homeric description of her body is given, but ancient vase painting shows a seated or stirring woman in a gown. Renaissance and later painters established the standard visual iconography of an alluring sorceress in loose dress with a wand and a goblet. Miller's novel reimagines her as comparatively plain and powerless in her youth, gathering power only through practice — a deliberately non-Classical characterization.

Powers

Pharmacology and herbal potions (phármaka) Circe's defining power is her vast knowledge of herbs, roots, and potions (phármaka), which she compounds into draughts capable of transforming men into beasts. Her Homeric epithet polypharmakos, 'knowing many drugs', signals this mastery of drugs and charms. Ovid implies she learned her herbal lore from her mother Perse, who had similar skills. The change is presented as bodily but consciousness-laden: Homer's transformed men keep their minds within the swine, a detail later writers (Dobson's 'The Prayer of the Swine to Circe') exploit for horror. Homeric Odyssey; Ovid's The Cure for Love (herbal tutelage from Perse) The transformative draught can be neutralized by the magic herb moly supplied by Hermes; Hermes also warns that any man who drinks a second drugged cup while he has slept with her without her oath might be 'unmanned' — a lingering threat even after hospitality is established. Odysseus, protected by moly and warned by Hermes, resists and forces her to swear an oath of no further harm. Whether the transformation alters the soul or only the body is a central ancient philosophical question. Homer leans toward preserved inner humanity; the Gryllus and Gelli/Machiavelli traditions argue the change reveals a pre-existing bestial nature. Modern medical historians speculate the myth encodes anticholinergic intoxication from Datura stramonium (amnesia, hallucinations), while 'moly' fits the snowdrop, whose galantamine can counteract anticholinergics.

Magic wand / staff (rhábdos) She wields a magic wand or staff with which she touches or stirs to effect transformation. In Homer she uses the wand to 'drive' her victims into the pigsties after drugging their wine; ancient art almost invariably shows her stirring her potion with the wand, or striking a half-transformed man. Homeric Odyssey; vase painting The wand's power is partial in art: transformations are often depicted as only the head and sprouting tail animal, the rest human. Odysseus' sword drawn against her (and Hermes' protective moly) overrides the staff's influence. In the Carl Barks Oddball Odyssey comics, Circe's wand is a physical relic that can be wielded by another (Magica de Spell), implying detachability — a comic-convention elaboration not present in the ancient sources.

Luring song and weaving Circe lures sailors to her palace with her lovely singing while she weaves on an enormous loom. The song is part of her seductive hospitality in Homer: the crew hears it and is drawn in, predisposed to accept the drugged feast. Homeric Odyssey, Book 10 The song only works as an invitation; Eurylochus, who suspects treachery, stays outside and escapes. It does not compel by itself; the potion does the work. No ancient source treats the song as independently spellbinding (unlike the Sirens), so its role is usually read as charm rather than coercion.

Purification of murder/sacramental cleansing Circe possesses the ritual knowledge to purify those guilty of blood-guilt (miasma) from kin-murder, using a sacrificial piglet's blood. In the Argonautica she purifies Jason and Medea for the murder of Absyrtus, on account of kinship and pity, while nonetheless disapproving of their crime. Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 4 The purification does not require her to condone the deed; she immediately orders the couple off her island, refusing to shelter them further. This benevolent, quasi-priestess register sits uneasily beside her malevolent transformations; the two faces of Circe (healer-priestess vs vengeful enchantress) are reconciled in no single ancient source.

Necromancy and Underworld guidance Circe furnishes Odysseus with the knowledge, instructions, and protections needed to visit the Underworld (Hades' house) — a journey no mortal had yet completed — and to communicate with the dead. On his return she advises him on two possible homeward routes and warns of their dangers (the Sirens, Scylla, the cattle of Helios). Homeric Odyssey, Books 10-12 She does not herself bring the dead to the living; she lets Odysseus solicit the shades amid favorable wind in a prescribed ritual. Her powers are advisory here, not necromantic command. Later writers do not uniformly credit her with Underworld knowledge; this is a distinctly Homeric privilege that the epic does not extend to other enchantresses.

Bestowing a deadly poisoned weapon In the post-Odyssey Telegony tradition, Circe gives her son Telegonus a spear poisoned with a stingray/vinegar-derived toxin (later writers), which unwittingly kills Odysseus. This is a curse/protective blessing that misfires catastrophically. Telegony (lost epic); later summaries; Hyginus The weapon's power is unfocused: it kills rather than protects, because the target ends up being her own absent lover. Lycophron's Alexandra and Tzetzes add that Circe, grieving, used magical herbs to revive Odysseus — contradicting the simple accidental-patricide version.

Immortality-bestowal on mortals After her son's tragedy, Circe is said (in the Telegony continuation reported by later authors) to have made Odysseus' widow Penelope, and Telemachus, immortal, and to have married Telemachus while her son married Penelope. Telegony tradition; Hyginus The immortalities granted are within the divine prerogative she exercises as a goddess-sorceress, but the details vary by source. The variant in which Odysseus is revived contradicts the version where he dies of grief; both are attested.

Accounts

After landing on Aeaea, Odysseus' scout party, led by Eurylochus, enters Circe's forest palace; she feasts them on a pottage of cheese, meal, honey and wine laced with a drug, then strikes them with her wand and herds them into sties as swine. Eurylochus escapes and warns Odysseus, who sets out alone. Hermes, sent by Athena, meets him and gives him the herb moly to neutralize to drug, instructing him to draw his sword on Circe and, after she invites him to bed, to make her swear a solemn oath by the gods not to harm him. Odysseus follows this, frees his crew (whose minds remain human within the pigs), and stays on Aeaea for a year as Circe's lover.

The sea-god Glaucus loves the nymph Scylla, who spurns him; he asks Circe for a potion to win her. But Circe falls in love with Glaucus herself and, rejected, poisons the pool where Scylla bathes. Dogs spring from her thighs and she becomes the six-headed man-eating monster of the Strait, one of the perils Odysseus later confronts.

Picus, son of Saturn and king of Latium, devoted to his wife Canens, spurns Circe while hunting; enraged, she turns him into a woodpecker, and Canens wastes away in mourning. The tale adds the vice of violent jealousy to Circe's repertoire and is echoed in Moliere/Aicato emblem tradition.

Fleeing Colchis after killing Medea's brother Absyrtus, Jason, Medea and the Argonauts land at Aeaea. Circe is bathing; around her are primeval 'beasts, not resembling the beasts of the wild, nor yet like men in body, but with a medley of limbs'. The crew silently shows the still-bloody sword; Circe recognizes blood-guilt and purifies them by slitting the throat of a suckling pig and letting its blood drip on them. Medea tells the tale, omitting the murder, but Circe is not fooled; pitying the girl for kinship, she lets them go unharmed on condition they leave at once.

During the war between the gods and the giants, the giant Picolous fled to Aeaea and tried to chase Circe away; her father Helios killed him. From the slain giant's blood grew the herb moly, so named from the battle (malos) and bearing a white flower either for the white Sun or the white-faced (terrified) Circe; mortals cannot pluck it, but Hermes later gave it to Odysseus.

Circe tells her son Telegonus the identity of his absent father, Odysseus, and arms him with a poisoned spear before he sets out to find him. Telegonus lands on Ithaca while Odysseus is away fighting the Brygi; he ravages the island, and when Odysseus returns to defend it, Telegonus kills him unknowingly with the spear. Telegonus brings Odysseus' body back to Aeaea with Penelope, and Circe makes the pair and Telemachus immortal; later, by Athena's advice, Circe marries Telemachus and Telegonus marries Penelope.

While Odysseus stays with Circe, her island is pestered by the amorous attentions of King Calchus the Daunian; she invites him to a drugged dinner that turns him into a pig and shuts him up in her sties. He is only released when his army comes searching for him, on his sworn word never to set foot on the island again.

In Plutarch's playful dialogue, Circe allows Odysseus to interview a fellow Greek who has been turned into a pig, named Gryllus. The pig argues that his present existence is preferable to the human, and the dialogue systematically questions human values, showing beasts to be of superior wisdom and virtue.

A way to hold it in your mind

Before she was a witch, before the pigs, Circe was a goddess with the Sun for a father and fire-hair that matched her eyes — and a woman so feared and desired that a whole civilization spent two thousand years trying to decide whether the men she turned into swine had really deserved it. Cast her on a wooded island and hand her a goblet of honeyed wine, and she will show you the exact shape of every fear the ancient world (and every age since) kept about women, power, and the beast hiding just below a man's skin. The enchantress of Aeaea is the mirror the West has been holding up to itself since Homer first sat Odysseus down at her treadle.
