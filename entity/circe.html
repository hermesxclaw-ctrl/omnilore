Who It Is

Before she was a witch, before the pigs, Circe was a goddess with the Sun for a father and fire-hair that matched her eyes — and a woman so feared and desired that a whole civilization spent two thousand years trying to decide whether the men she turned into swine had really deserved it. Cast her on a wooded island and hand her a goblet of honeyed wine, and she will show you the exact shape of every fear the ancient world (and every age since) kept about women, power, and the beast hiding just below a man's skin. The enchantress of Aeaea is the mirror the West has been holding up to itself since Homer first sat Odysseus down at her treadle.

In Homer she is a deceptively hospitable enchantress who lures sailors into her palace with song, drugs them, and transforms them. She can be vengeful and jealous (Scylla, Picus, Calchus), but also generous and protective (purifying Jason and Medea, arming Odysseus with the Underworld route and protections, making Telegonus' widow Penelope immortal). Classical and later writers repeatedly frame her as the archetype of the predatory, sexually free female whose power threatens male autonomy. Name & Tongue

Powers
Pharmacology and herbal potions (phármaka)

Circe's defining power is her vast knowledge of herbs, roots, and potions (phármaka), which she compounds into draughts capable of transforming men into beasts. Her Homeric epithet polypharmakos, 'knowing many drugs', signals this mastery of drugs and charms. Ovid implies she learned her herbal lore from her mother Perse, who had similar skills. The change is presented as bodily but consciousness-laden: Homer's transformed men keep their minds within the swine, a detail later writers (Dobson's 'The Prayer of the Swine to Circe') exploit for horror.

Limit: The transformative draught can be neutralized by the magic herb moly supplied by Hermes; Hermes also warns that any man who drinks a second drugged cup while he has slept with her without her oath might be 'unmanned' — a lingering threat even after hospitality is established. Odysseus, protected by moly and warned by Hermes, resists and forces her to swear an oath of no further harm.

Magic wand / staff (rhábdos)

She wields a magic wand or staff with which she touches or stirs to effect transformation. In Homer she uses the wand to 'drive' her victims into the pigsties after drugging their wine; ancient art almost invariably shows her stirring her potion with the wand, or striking a half-transformed man.

Limit: The wand's power is partial in art: transformations are often depicted as only the head and sprouting tail animal, the rest human. Odysseus' sword drawn against her (and Hermes' protective moly) overrides the staff's influence.

Luring song and weaving

Circe lures sailors to her palace with her lovely singing while she weaves on an enormous loom. The song is part of her seductive hospitality in Homer: the crew hears it and is drawn in, predisposed to accept the drugged feast.

Limit: The song only works as an invitation; Eurylochus, who suspects treachery, stays outside and escapes. It does not compel by itself; the potion does the work.

Purification of murder/sacramental cleansing

Circe possesses the ritual knowledge to purify those guilty of blood-guilt (miasma) from kin-murder, using a sacrificial piglet's blood. In the Argonautica she purifies Jason and Medea for the murder of Absyrtus, on account of kinship and pity, while nonetheless disapproving of their crime.

Limit: The purification does not require her to condone the deed; she immediately orders the couple off her island, refusing to shelter them further.

Necromancy and Underworld guidance

Circe furnishes Odysseus with the knowledge, instructions, and protections needed to visit the Underworld (Hades' house) — a journey no mortal had yet completed — and to communicate with the dead. On his return she advises him on two possible homeward routes and warns of their dangers (the Sirens, Scylla, the cattle of Helios).

Limit: She does not herself bring the dead to the living; she lets Odysseus solicit the shades amid favorable wind in a prescribed ritual. Her powers are advisory here, not necromantic command.

Bestowing a deadly poisoned weapon

In the post-Odyssey Telegony tradition, Circe gives her son Telegonus a spear poisoned with a stingray/vinegar-derived toxin (later writers), which unwittingly kills Odysseus. This is a curse/protective blessing that misfires catastrophically.

Limit: The weapon's power is unfocused: it kills rather than protects, because the target ends up being her own absent lover.

Immortality-bestowal on mortals

After her son's tragedy, Circe is said (in the Telegony continuation reported by later authors) to have made Odysseus' widow Penelope, and Telemachus, immortal, and to have married Telemachus while her son married Penelope.

Limit: The immortalities granted are within the divine prerogative she exercises as a goddess-sorceress, but the details vary by source.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Homer calls Circe 'a dreadful goddess with lovely hair and human speech'. As a descendant of Helios, she has (per Apollonius of Rhodes) flashing golden eyes that shoot out rays of light; the Orphic Argonautica adds hair 'like fiery rays'. No Homeric description of her body is given, but ancient vase painting shows a seated or stirring woman in a gown. Renaissance and later painters established the standard visual iconography of an alluring sorcer

Symbols — The magic wand or staff (rhábdos) with which she stirs her potion and touches men into beasts; the great loom on which she sings while weaving; the bowl/cup of drugged wine; the potion (phármakon); the docile lions and wolves that prowl her palace; the herb moly (a snowdrop flower per one identification); her island Aeaea. Her name iconography also includes the falcon/hawk. Later emblem books (Alciato) made her the type of the prostitute holding

Domains — The island of Aeaea (Aiaia), in Homer left geographically vague. Apollonius locates it south of Aethalia (Elba) within view of the Tyrrhenian shore (western Italy); a scholiast follows Hesiod in having her brought there in Helios' chariot; Valerius Flaccus has her carried by winged dragons. Roman poets set her on the promontory of Circeo (Mount Circeo) in the Tyrrhenian Sea, where Strabo records a shrine and a bowl said to be Odysseus'.

Accounts I
The Transformation of Odysseus' Crew (Swine on Aeaea)Homer, Odyssey 10

8th-7th century BC (epic)

After landing on Aeaea, Odysseus' scout party, led by Eurylochus, enters Circe's forest palace; she feasts them on a pottage of cheese, meal, honey and wine laced with a drug, then strikes them with her wand and herds them into sties as swine. Eurylochus escapes and warns Odysseus, who sets out alone. Hermes, sent by Athena, meets him and gives him the herb moly to neutralize to drug, instructing him to draw his sword on Circe and, after she invites him to bed, to make her swear a solemn oath by the gods not to harm him. Odysseus follows this, frees his crew (whose minds remain human within the pigs), and stays on Aeaea for a year as Circe's lover. A year-long delay in Odysseus' return; Circe becomes the guide who sends him to the Underworld and then warns him of Scylla/Charybdis and Helios' cattle, where his doomed crew will perish.

Scylla's Transformation (Jealous Rival Poisoned)Ovid, Metamorphoses (also Hyginus)

1st century AD (Ovid)

The sea-god Glaucus loves the nymph Scylla, who spurns him; he asks Circe for a potion to win her. But Circe falls in love with Glaucus herself and, rejected, poisons the pool where Scylla bathes. Dogs spring from her thighs and she becomes the six-headed man-eating monster of the Strait, one of the perils Odysseus later confronts. A permanent monster-threat in the Odyssey's geography; Scylla figures again when Odysseus passes between her and Charybdis (and later kills six crewmen).

Picus Turned into a WoodpeckerOvid, Metamorphoses 14

1st century AD (Ovid)

Picus, son of Saturn and king of Latium, devoted to his wife Canens, spurns Circe while hunting; enraged, she turns him into a woodpecker, and Canens wastes away in mourning. The tale adds the vice of violent jealousy to Circe's repertoire and is echoed in Moliere/Aicato emblem tradition. Picus persists as a figure in Italian royal genealogies and in emblem literature; Hawthorne's 'Circe's Palace' later rewards the warnings of the transformed Picus.

The Purification of Jason and MedeaApollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 4

3rd century BC

Fleeing Colchis after killing Medea's brother Absyrtus, Jason, Medea and the Argonauts land at Aeaea. Circe is bathing; around her are primeval 'beasts, not resembling the beasts of the wild, nor yet like men in body, but with a medley of limbs'. The crew silently shows the still-bloody sword; Circe recognizes blood-guilt and purifies them by slitting the throat of a suckling pig and letting its blood drip on them. Medea tells the tale, omitting the murder, but Circe is not fooled; pitying the girl for kinship, she lets them go unharmed on condition they leave at once. They are ritually cleansed and leave immediately, allowed to continue westwards; Circe's disapproval of Medea's crime and her warning of Jason's fickleness prefigure the Medea tragedy.

Accounts II
Circe, Helios, and the Giant Picolous (the Origin of Moly)Scholiastic / late tradition (source of the moly aition)

Late antique (scholia)

During the war between the gods and the giants, the giant Picolous fled to Aeaea and tried to chase Circe away; her father Helios killed him. From the slain giant's blood grew the herb moly, so named from the battle (malos) and bearing a white flower either for the white Sun or the white-faced (terrified) Circe; mortals cannot pluck it, but Hermes later gave it to Odysseus. Provides the mythic aition (origin-tale) of the protective herb moly that defeats Circe's drug in the Odyssey.

The Telegony: The Poisoned Spear and the PatricideTelegony (lost epic, attributed to Eugammon of Cyrene); summarized by later writers; also Hyginus

c. 6th century BC; known via summaries

Circe tells her son Telegonus the identity of his absent father, Odysseus, and arms him with a poisoned spear before he sets out to find him. Telegonus lands on Ithaca while Odysseus is away fighting the Brygi; he ravages the island, and when Odysseus returns to defend it, Telegonus kills him unknowingly with the spear. Telegonus brings Odysseus' body back to Aeaea with Penelope, and Circe makes the pair and Telemachus immortal; later, by Athena's advice, Circe marries Telemachus and Telegonus marries Penelope. The accidental patricide of Greece's greatest hero, presided over (and inadvertently armed) by Circe — a dark culmination of the enchantress's role in the Trojan-homecoming cycle.

Calchus the Daunian Turned into a PigParthenius of Nicaea, The Sorrows of Love

1st century BC

While Odysseus stays with Circe, her island is pestered by the amorous attentions of King Calchus the Daunian; she invites him to a drugged dinner that turns him into a pig and shuts him up in her sties. He is only released when his army comes searching for him, on his sworn word never to set foot on the island again. An interpolation that widens Circe's program of transforming importunate males into swine.

The Gryllus Dialogue (The Reasoning Beast)Plutarch, Moralia

1st century AD

In Plutarch's playful dialogue, Circe allows Odysseus to interview a fellow Greek who has been turned into a pig, named Gryllus. The pig argues that his present existence is preferable to the human, and the dialogue systematically questions human values, showing beasts to be of superior wisdom and virtue. Launched the 'reasoning beasts' tradition that Gelli, Bruno, Machiavelli, La Fontaine, Gozzi, Peacock and others explored for 500 years.
