Who It Is

In the shadow of the Great Wall, where the Yellow River bends through the loess highlands, there once lay a province that was both a cradle of Chinese civilization and a crucible of nomadic power. Bingzhou—known in English as Bing Province—was not merely a line on a map; it was a shifting frontier where empires met, where the Han built walls against the Xiongnu, and where, centuries later, those same steppe peoples would rise to found their own dynasties. Its story is written in the soil of Shanxi and Hebei, in the annals of the Han and the chronicles of the Sixteen Kingdoms, and in the very concept of China's Nine Provinces, a division said to be ordained by the legendary Yu the Great.

Not applicable; Bingzhou is a region, not a sentient entity. It is characterized in historical records by its strategic importance, its role as a frontier zone, and its mixed population of Han Chinese and nomadic peoples.

Form & Signs

Appearance — As a geographical region, Bingzhou's physical appearance is defined by the terrain of the Shanxi plateau, the Taihang Mountains, and the Yellow River's northern loop. It includes fertile river valleys and arid steppe lands, with the Great Wall running through its northern frontier. No specific visual representation is attested.

Symbols — No specific symbols or iconography are associated with Bingzhou as a province. Its significance is administrative and historical.

Domains — Bingzhou covered areas of present-day Shanxi, Hebei, Inner Mongolia, and northern Shaanxi at various times. Its habitats ranged from agricultural plains to mountainous regions and steppe.

Accounts I
Yu the Great Tames the FloodBook of Documents, other classical texts

Legendary, c. 2200–2100 BC

Yu the Great, a legendary ruler, successfully controlled the Great Flood by dredging rivers and creating channels. As a result, he divided the land into Nine Provinces to facilitate governance, one of which was Bingzhou. This story establishes Bingzhou's legendary origin. Established the Nine Provinces as the traditional administrative framework of China, with Bingzhou as one of them.

The Great Wall and the FrontierHistorical records (e.g., Shiji, Book of Han)

5th century BC onwards

Since the fifth century BC, a series of walls were built to separate Bingzhou from the Ordos Desert and the nomadic peoples beyond. These walls would later be integrated into the Great Wall of China. This reflects Bingzhou's role as a defensive frontier. The fortifications shaped the region's military and demographic character.

Accounts II
The Rise and Fall of Gao GanRecords of the Three Kingdoms (Sanguozhi), Zizhi Tongjian

Early 3rd century AD

During the succession dispute among Yuan Shao's heirs, Gao Gan, Yuan's nephew, controlled Bingzhou. He surrendered to Cao Cao in 203, but rebelled in 205. Cao Cao defeated and killed him in 206, and Bingzhou was definitively annexed by Cao Cao. Cao Cao gained control of Bingzhou, which he used to settle Xiongnu herdsmen, leading to a large Xiongnu population by the 280s.

The Xiongnu Settlement and the Rise of Han-ZhaoBook of Jin (Jinshu)

3rd–4th century AD

Cao Cao moved Xiongnu herdsmen into Bingzhou and the Ordos Desert. By the 280s, approximately 400,000 Xiongnu lived there. These Xiongnu later played a key role in founding the states of Han-Zhao (304–319) and Later Zhao (319–351) during the Sixteen Kingdoms period. The settlement of Xiongnu in Bingzhou transformed the region's demographics and contributed to the political upheavals of the Sixteen Kingdoms.
