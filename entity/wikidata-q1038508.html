The Approach

In the shadow of the Great Wall, where the Yellow River bends through the loess highlands, there once lay a province that was both a cradle of Chinese civilization and a crucible of nomadic power. Bingzhou—known in English as Bing Province—was not merely a line on a map; it was a shifting frontier where empires met, where the Han built walls against the Xiongnu, and where, centuries later, those same steppe peoples would rise to found their own dynasties. Its story is written in the soil of Shanxi and Hebei, in the annals of the Han and the chronicles of the Sixteen Kingdoms, and in the very concept of China's Nine Provinces, a division said to be ordained by the legendary Yu the Great.

Content advisory. This dossier covers historical warfare, political violence, and the displacement of nomadic peoples. No explicit or graphic content is described.
Identity

Bingzhou

Pronunciation Bing-joe (approximately) in English; in Mandarin: Bìngzhōu (Pinyin).

Known As

Bing ProvinceBing Zhou并州 (Chinese characters)

Etymology

The name 'Bingzhou' (并州) is composed of 'Bing' (并) and 'zhou' (州), meaning 'province' or 'prefecture'. The character 'bing' may be related to the concept of 'combining' or 'merging', possibly reflecting the region's position as a meeting point of cultures and administrative areas, though its precise etymology is uncertain.

Cultural Ownership

Bingzhou is a historical administrative region of ancient China. It is part of Chinese historical geography and is referenced in Chinese classical texts. It is not a mythical location in the sense of a fictional realm, but it is associated with legendary history (e.g., Yu the Great) and has cultural significance in Chinese history.

Disputed Identifications

The exact boundaries of Bingzhou varied over time and between different historical sources. The identification of Bingzhou as one of the Nine Provinces is based on legendary and textual traditions, and its exact correspondence to modern geography is debated among scholars. Some sources may refer to a different 'Bingzhou' in other contexts, but this dossier focuses on the historical province.

Description

Appearance

As a geographical region, Bingzhou's physical appearance is defined by the terrain of the Shanxi plateau, the Taihang Mountains, and the Yellow River's northern loop. It includes fertile river valleys and arid steppe lands, with the Great Wall running through its northern frontier. No specific visual representation is attested.

Symbols & Iconography

No specific symbols or iconography are associated with Bingzhou as a province. Its significance is administrative and historical.

Behaviour & Personality

Not applicable; Bingzhou is a region, not a sentient entity. It is characterized in historical records by its strategic importance, its role as a frontier zone, and its mixed population of Han Chinese and nomadic peoples.

Domains & Habitats

Bingzhou covered areas of present-day Shanxi, Hebei, Inner Mongolia, and northern Shaanxi at various times. Its habitats ranged from agricultural plains to mountainous regions and steppe.

Version Differences

The boundaries of Bingzhou changed over time. In the Western Han, it was one of thirteen administrative divisions. In the Eastern Han, its capital was Jinyang. In 213, it was merged into Jizhou. It was restored in 220 under Cao Wei with reduced territory. In 396, its capital was at Puban, and it was abolished in 399.

Stories & Myths

Yu the Great Tames the Flood

Source Book of Documents, other classical textsCulture Chinese mythologyPeriod Legendary, c. 2200–2100 BC

Summary

Yu the Great, a legendary ruler, successfully controlled the Great Flood by dredging rivers and creating channels. As a result, he divided the land into Nine Provinces to facilitate governance, one of which was Bingzhou. This story establishes Bingzhou's legendary origin.

Participants

Yu the Great

Consequences

Established the Nine Provinces as the traditional administrative framework of China, with Bingzhou as one of them.

Variants

Different texts list the Nine Provinces with slight variations; the exact composition varies.

The Great Wall and the Frontier

Source Historical records (e.g., Shiji, Book of Han)Culture Chinese historical traditionPeriod 5th century BC onwards

Summary

Since the fifth century BC, a series of walls were built to separate Bingzhou from the Ordos Desert and the nomadic peoples beyond. These walls would later be integrated into the Great Wall of China. This reflects Bingzhou's role as a defensive frontier.

Participants

Various Chinese states

Xiongnu nomads

Consequences

The fortifications shaped the region's military and demographic character.

Variants

The Great Wall was built and rebuilt by different dynasties, so the exact line of walls varied.

The Rise and Fall of Gao Gan

Source Records of the Three Kingdoms (Sanguozhi), Zizhi TongjianCulture Chinese historical traditionPeriod Early 3rd century AD

Summary

During the succession dispute among Yuan Shao's heirs, Gao Gan, Yuan's nephew, controlled Bingzhou. He surrendered to Cao Cao in 203, but rebelled in 205. Cao Cao defeated and killed him in 206, and Bingzhou was definitively annexed by Cao Cao.

Participants

Gao Gan

Cao Cao

Yuan Shao's heirs

Consequences

Cao Cao gained control of Bingzhou, which he used to settle Xiongnu herdsmen, leading to a large Xiongnu population by the 280s.

Variants

The exact details of Gao Gan's rebellion are recorded in multiple sources with some inconsistencies.

The Xiongnu Settlement and the Rise of Han-Zhao

Source Book of Jin (Jinshu)Culture Chinese historical traditionPeriod 3rd–4th century AD

Summary

Cao Cao moved Xiongnu herdsmen into Bingzhou and the Ordos Desert. By the 280s, approximately 400,000 Xiongnu lived there. These Xiongnu later played a key role in founding the states of Han-Zhao (304–319) and Later Zhao (319–351) during the Sixteen Kingdoms period.

Participants

Cao Cao

Xiongnu tribes

Liu Yuan (founder of Han-Zhao)

Consequences

The settlement of Xiongnu in Bingzhou transformed the region's demographics and contributed to the political upheavals of the Sixteen Kingdoms.

Variants

The exact number of Xiongnu and the nature of their settlement is debated; some sources emphasize forced relocation, others voluntary migration.

Military conquest and violence

Source Records of the Three Kingdoms

Description

Bingzhou was a site of repeated military conflicts, including the rebellion of Gao Gan, which resulted in his defeat and death. The region was also a frontier zone with frequent raids and warfare.

Scholarly Context

The violence is typical of the period's political instability.

Displacement of Xiongnu

Source Book of Jin

Description

Cao Cao moved Xiongnu herdsmen into Bingzhou, likely as a means of control and to use their military capabilities. This involved forced relocation and disruption of their traditional way of life.

Scholarly Context

This was part of a broader pattern of Chinese states managing nomadic peoples within their borders.
