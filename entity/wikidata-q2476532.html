Who It Is

She was never one goddess with one face. Tonantzin — Our Revered Mother — was the title the Nahua whispered whenever the earth itself seemed to listen: when maize pushed through cracked soil, when a child was washed at birth, when pilgrims climbed the scrub hill of Tepeyac. Then the Spanish came, tore down a shrine, built a basilica on its shadow, and a new Mother in a blue mantle appeared to a man named Juan Diego. Was she the same Mother, renamed, or a different Mother altogether? The answer has been fought over for 500 years.

Attested: Maternal but not uniformly gentle. Cihuacoatl as Tonantzin is a wailing, predatory mother who roams at night crying 'my children, we must go' (Book 1, Book 6), steals children, brings omens of war, yet also protects midwives and women in labor. Toci as Tonantzin is nurturing grandmother and healer, patron of midwives (ticitl) and steam baths, but also demands ritual impersonation and sacrifice during Ochpaniztli festival where a woman impersonating her was sacrificed and flayed. Name & Tongue

Etymology — Classical Nahuatl Tonāntzin < to- (first-person plural possessive prefix 'our') + nān(tli) ('mother') + -tzin (reverential/honorific suffix, also diminutive and affectionate). Literally 'Our Honored Mother' or 'Our Revered Mother'. The -tzin suffix marks reverence, not diminutiveness here. Vocative

Pronunciation — Classical Nahuatl: [toˈnáːn.tsin] (to-NAHN-tsin), with long /aː/ in second syllable and stress on penultimate. Modern Mexican Spanish: /tonaˈntsin/ or /tonanˈtsin/. Vocatives: [toˌnaːntsinˈé] and [tonaːnˈtsín].

Aliases — Tonāntzin, Tonāntziné (vocative, used by men), Tonāntzín (vocative, used by women), Tonantzín (modern Spanish orthography), Our Revered Mother, Our Lady Mother

Powers
Maternal Creation and Earth Fertility

As Mother Earth (Tlalli Nantli) and Mother of Sustenance, causes maize, beans, and amaranth to grow; embodies fertile earth that births and devours. Chicomecoatl aspect ensures abundance of Seven Maize.

Limit: Fertility is conditional on sacrifice, offerings of blood, copal, and proper ritual calendar (e.g., Huey Tozoztli, Ochpaniztli). Drought and famine signal displeasure or neglect.

Midwifery, Childbirth Protection and Fate-Setting

Toci/Cihuacoatl as Tonantzin governs conception, pregnancy, labor, and the temazcal purification. Midwives invoke her; she determines whether child lives or dies and assigns tonalli (soul-heat).

Limit: Cannot prevent death if omens (e.g., Cihuacoatl's night wailing) foretell war or sacrifice; requires ritual sweeping, fasting, and offerings.

Omen, Prophecy and Night Terror

Cihuacoatl-Tonantzin appears at night weeping at crossroads, wailing 'O my children, we are lost!' foretelling war, conquest, or epidemic. Her cry was one of eight omens before Spanish arrival.

Limit: Omen is not preventable; functions as warning, not intervention. Power is auditory and psychological rather than physical combat.

Serpent Power and Coatl Identity

Coatlicue/Cihuacoatl aspects bear serpent skirts, serpent heads, and serpent-associated fertility and earth-bound power. Serpent symbolizes water, earth movement, and regenerative shedding.

Limit: Serpent power is ambivalent — both life-giving (water) and deadly (venom). Vulnerable to solar/warrior deities (Huitzilopochtli dismembers Coyolxauhqui).

Syncretic Intercession and Miraculous Apparition (Guadalupe as Tonantzin)

As Our Lady of Guadalupe identified with Tonantzin, intercedes for healing, protection, and national identity; tilma image believed incorruptible, roses bloom in winter at Tepeyac as sign.

Limit: In Catholic theology, intercession only through God; not autonomous divine power. Miraculous claims are faith-based, not empirically verified.

Ritual Impersonation and Sacrificial Demand

During Ochpaniztli and other veintena festivals, a woman impersonating Toci-Tonantzin was adorned, honored, then sacrificed and flayed; her skin worn by priest to ensure agricultural renewal.

Limit: Power requires human sacrifice; without it, renewal fails in Mexica cosmology. Practice ended with Spanish conquest and Christian prohibition.

Accounts I
The Pilgrims of Tepeyac — Sahagún's ReportBernardino de Sahagún, Florentine Codex, Book 11

Pre-conquest tradition recorded 1570s; describes practice before and after 1521

Sahagún, via Nahua informants, records that at the hill called Tepeyacac (now Tepeyac) people made pilgrimages from afar to honor Tonantzin. After the conquest, a church to Our Lady of Guadalupe was built there, and the Spanish called her Our Lady of Guadalupe, but the Nahua still called her Tonantzin, coming from afar as before. Sahagún expresses concern that the devotion is a covert continuation of idolatry under a Christian name, noting the persistence of the title and pilgrimage pattern. Foundation for 400 years of debate over syncretism at Tepeyac; primary evidence for both continuity and colonial anxiety. Used by later scholars to argue both for and against a pre-conquest temple at the exact basilica site.

The Nican Mopohua — The Apparitions to Juan DiegoNican Mopohua in Huei tlamahuiçoltica (Luis Laso de la Vega, ed.)

Narrative set December 9-12, 1531; text first printed 1649

Juan Diego (Cuauhtlatoatzin), a Nahua convert, passes Tepeyac hill on his way to Mass. A young woman calls him in Nahuatl, revealing herself as the Virgin Mary, 'in icel Teotl Dios' mother', and asks for a temple (teocalli) to be built at Tepeyac where she will show love and compassion. Juan Diego reports to Bishop Juan de Zumárraga, who demands a sign. After his uncle Juan Bernardino falls ill, the Virgin heals him and tells Juan Diego to gather roses blooming out of season on the hilltop in his tilma (cloak). When he opens the tilma before the bishop, the roses fall and the image of the Virgin is imprinted on the cloth. The bishop believes and builds the hermitage. Creates central national narrative of Mexico; tilma image becomes most venerated relic in Americas; Tepeyac becomes pilgrimage center for millions annually (December 12 feast). Nahua listeners reportedly recognized her as Tonantzin, facilitating conversion.

Coatlicue Gives Birth to Huitzilopochtli — The Mother of the War GodFlorentine Codex Book 3, Chapter 1; Codex Boturini tradition

Mythic time, recorded 16th century; reflects 14th-15th century Mexica state mythology

Coatlicue (Serpent Skirt), honored as Tonantzin and mother of the Centzon Huitznahua (400 Southerners) and daughter Coyolxauhqui, does penance sweeping the temple on Coatepec (Serpent Hill). A ball of down feathers falls from the sky; she tucks it in her bosom and becomes pregnant. Her children, shamed by the mysterious pregnancy, plot to kill her. At the moment of attack, her child Huitzilopochtli is born fully armed, with shield and darts, and dismembers Coyolxauhqui, casting her body down the hill, and scatters his brothers. Coatlicue is thus both violated mother and mother of the sun/war god who becomes Mexica patron. Explains Mexica patron deity's birth and legitimizes Tenochtitlan's dominance; Coatlicue monolith embodies Tonantzin as earth mother who births solar power through violence. Ritual reenactment at Templo Mayor.

Accounts II
Cihuacoatl Wails at Night — The Weeping Mother OmenFlorentine Codex Book 1, Ch. 6; Book 6, prayers; Book 12 omens of conquest

Pre-conquest belief, recorded 1570s; omen tradition linked to 1519-1521

Cihuacoatl (Snake Woman), called Tonantzin, is heard at night wailing through the streets of Tenochtitlan: 'O my children, we must flee! O my children, where shall I take you?' She appears as a skeletal woman in white, carrying a cradle. Midwives say she visits cradles and leaves an obsidian knife. Before the Spanish arrival, her wailing was counted among eight omens foretelling the fall of the empire, alongside a comet, temple fire, and boiling lake. She is both mourner and predator who steals children. Establishes Tonantzin's dark maternal aspect — mother who mourns and threatens. Later syncretized with La Llorona folklore; feminist readings see her as voice of Indigenous trauma and warning.

Chicomecoatl — Seven Serpent, the Sustenance Mother and XochilhuitlFlorentine Codex Book 2, Ch. 23 (Huey Tozoztli festival); Book 1, Ch. 14

Annual veintena calendar ritual, recorded 16th century

Chicomecoatl (Seven Serpent), also called Chicomexochitl (Seven Flower) and honored as Tonantzin, is the goddess of sustenance, maize, and food. During the movable feast Xochilhuitl (Flower Feast) and Huey Tozoztli, her impersonator — a young girl — is adorned with seven ears of maize, red feathers, and paper ornaments, honored for days, then sacrificed. Her blood is sprinkled on maize seeds to ensure germination. Households keep seed corn wrapped as her image (chicomecoatl bundle) for the year. She is 'Our Lady of Sustenance' who feeds the people but must be fed in return. Encodes agricultural cycle and debt-payment cosmology (nextlahualli). Explains why Tonantzin is called 'Goddess of Sustenance' and 'Mother of Corn' — not separate goddess but title for Chicomecoatl aspect.

Toci — Our Grandmother in the Sweatbath and OchpaniztliFlorentine Codex Book 1, Ch. 10; Book 2, Ch. 26 (Ochpaniztli)

Annual Ochpaniztli (Sweeping) festival, recorded 16th century

Toci (Our Grandmother), addressed as Tonantzin and Temazcalteci (Grandmother of the Sweatbath), is patron of midwives, healers, and the temazcal. During Ochpaniztli, a woman chosen to impersonate her is celebrated, then sacrificed at night. Her skin is flayed and worn by a male priest who then presides over rites of sweeping, warfare, and maize sowing. Midwives invoke Toci-Tonantzin in the temazcal to protect mothers: 'O our mother, O grandmother, come to aid this woman.' She is old, wise, and demanding. Shows Tonantzin's grandmother aspect — not just mother but aged healer. Ritual links birth, purification, and death. Spanish friars condemned rite as demonic, contributing to suppression of midwifery traditions.

Tonantzin was the Aztec Mother Earth goddess, a single deity equivalent to the Virgin Mary. — Tonantzin is an honorific title ('Our Revered Mother') applied to multiple goddesses — Cihuacoatl, Coatlicue, Chicomecoatl, Toci, and others — each with distinct iconography, myths, and rites. Equating her to one goddess flattens a complex maternal complex.

The name Guadalupe comes from the Nahuatl word Coatlaxopeuh meaning 'she who crushes the serpent.' — Guadalupe is a Spanish place-name from Extremadura (Arabic wadi + Latin lupus). Coatlaxopeuh is a later folk etymology not found in 16th-century sources; while culturally meaningful for many devotees, it is not supported by historical linguistics. The earliest Nahuatl apparition text (Nican Mopohua) does not use this word.

Aztecs worshipped Tonantzin at a great temple on Tepeyac hill that the Spanish destroyed and built the Basilica directly on top of. — Sahagún reports pilgrimages to Tepeyac to honor Tonantzin, but does not describe a great temple structure, and no archaeological remains of a pre-conquest temple have been verified at the basilica site. A shrine or oratory likely existed, but its scale and exact location remain unknown. The basilica was built at the hill's base, not demonstrably atop temple foundations.

Tonantzin and La Llorona are the same figure. — Cihuacoatl-Tonantzin's night wailing ('O my children, we must go') is a precursor that influenced later La Llorona folklore, but La Llorona is a distinct colonial and modern folk legend (often a mestiza woman who drowns her children) with different narrative, period, and moral function. Direct equation is anachronistic.

Tonantzin was a gentle, purely benevolent mother goddess with no dark aspects. — Documented aspects include skeletal terror (Cihuacoatl), demand for human sacrifice and flaying (Toci), child-stealing, and wailing omens of doom. Pre-conquest maternal power was ambivalent — life-giving and death-dealing — not sanitized benevolence.
