Amaterasu's primary domain is the sun, light, and the heavens (Takamagahara, the High Plain of Heaven). She is also the patron deity of the imperial family and the Japanese state. Her domains extend to weaving, agriculture (particularly rice cultivation, as the sun is essential for crops), and the fundamental cosmic order. As the ancestress of the imperial line, she is also a deity of sovereignty, legitimacy, and national identity.

Form

In the Kojiki, Amaterasu is not given a physical description, as she is a primordial being whose essence is light itself. The Nihon Shoki records that she was born from Izanagi's left eye, radiant and beautiful. In artistic depictions, she is typically shown as a mature, regal woman dressed in elaborate Heian-period court robes (jūnihitoe), often holding a jewel or a mirror. In the famous rock-cave episode, she is described as being so brilliant that her light illuminates the entire world. The sacred mirror (Yata no Kagami) is considered her physical embodiment or 'shintai' (divine body) at Ise Shrine.

Powers

Solar Radiance Amaterasu's fundamental power is her ability to generate and control light. Her very presence illuminates the heavens and the earth. When she withdrew to the rock-cave, the world was plunged into darkness. Her light is not just physical but also spiritual, representing truth, order, and life. Kojiki, Nihon Shoki Her light is not a weapon but a life-giving force. It is intrinsically tied to her presence and will. None. This is the core of her being.

Divine Weaving Amaterasu is depicted as a weaver, creating the garments of the kami and, symbolically, the fabric of the cosmos. This is a creative, ordering power that aligns her with other world-creating deities who use craft as a form of creation. Kojiki, Nihon Shoki (rock-cave episode) This is a domestic, creative power, not a martial one. It is disrupted by Susanoo's violence. None.

Authority over the Heavenly Realm (Takamagahara) As the ruler of Takamagahara, Amaterasu holds supreme authority over the heavenly kami. She commands them, delegates tasks, and makes final judgments on matters of cosmic importance. This authority is the model for imperial rule on earth. Kojiki, Nihon Shoki Her authority is challenged by Susanoo, but she prevails through a combination of strategy and the support of the other kami. None.

Granting the Imperial Mandate Amaterasu holds the power to bestow the right to rule the earthly realm. She sent her grandson, Ninigi, to pacify and rule Japan, a mandate that was passed down to the imperial line. This is her most politically consequential power. Kojiki, Nihon Shoki This power is exercised once, in the mythological past, and its legitimacy is a matter of historical and political interpretation. The Nihon Shoki contains variant accounts of the pacification of the earthly realm, including different agents and methods.

Hidden in the Rock-Cave (Amano-Iwato) Amaterasu's ability to withdraw her light from the world is a unique and dramatic power. It is a form of cosmic withdrawal that demonstrates the absolute dependence of the world on her presence. This is not a deliberate weapon but an act of profound grief and withdrawal. Kojiki, Nihon Shoki She is lured out by a combination of music, dance, and the sight of her own reflection, demonstrating that her withdrawal can be reversed. The Nihon Shoki presents several variants of the cave episode, with different details about the events and the objects used to lure her out.

Accounts

After returning from the underworld (Yomi), the creator god Izanagi performed a purification ritual in the river Woto in Tsukushi. As he washed his left eye, the goddess Amaterasu was born. In the Kojiki, she is born from his left eye. The Nihon Shoki offers multiple variants: she is born from his left eye, from a bronze mirror he held, or from his hand. She was radiant and beautiful, and Izanagi, overjoyed, gave her a necklace of jewels and decreed that she should rule the High Plain of Heaven (Takamagahara).

Amaterasu's brother, Susanoo, the storm god, was given dominion over the seas but wept and wailed, causing great calamities. When Amaterasu asked why, he said he wished to go to the land of his mother, Izanami. He then went to Takamagahara to say farewell, but his behavior became destructive and violent. He destroyed Amaterasu's rice fields, defecated in her palace, and, most grievously, flayed a heavenly horse and threw it through the roof of her sacred weaving hall, killing one of her weaving maidens. In grief and fury, Amaterasu retreated into the rock-cave (Amano-Iwato), plunging everything into darkness. The eight hundred countless kami gathered to devise a plan. They placed a rooster at the cave's entrance, gathered a mirror (Yata no Kagami) and jewels, and the goddess Ame-no-Uzume performed a lewd and boisterous dance on an overturned tub, causing the kami to laugh uproariously. Curious, Amaterasu opened the cave door a crack. The kami presented her with the mirror, and seeing her own radiant reflection for the first time, she was so startled and intrigued that she emerged fully, and light returned to the world. Susanoo was subsequently punished and banished from the heavens.

After the pacification of the earthly realm (Ashihara no Nakatsukuni) by the deities Ōkuninushi and others, Amaterasu decided to send her grandson, Ninigi-no-Mikoto, to rule Japan. She gave him the three sacred treasures: the Yata no Kagami (mirror), the Yasakani no Magatama (jewel), and the Kusanagi no Tsurugi (sword, which was originally found by Susanoo). She told Ninigi that the mirror was to be worshipped as her own spirit. Ninigi descended to the peak of Takachiho in Hyūga (southern Kyushu) and established the divine mandate for the imperial line.

What We Don't Know

The elongated name 'Amaterasu Kuniteruhiko Hikari Kushi Tama Hayasui no Mikoto' is not standard in the Kojiki or Nihon Shoki. It appears to be a later liturgical or ritual compilation of epithets. I was unable to find a specific primary source for this exact name form. Further research into obscure Shinto liturgical texts (Norito) or regional shrine records might reveal its origin. The specific epithets 'Kuniteruhiko' and 'Hayasui' are particularly obscure and require deeper investigation into classical Japanese philology. The 'wikidata-q140446112' identifier suggests this specific name form may be a modern compilation or a variant from a specific, possibly non-mainstream, tradition.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Amaterasu not as a person but as the principle of life-giving order itself. She is the light that makes growth possible, the authority that brings structure to chaos, and the bond that connects a nation to its divine origins. Her story is the story of how the world came to be bright, ordered, and worth ruling.
