Form

Alal is typically depicted as a robed, gender-ambiguous figure standing before a door or gate. In the few surviving iconographic representations, the figure holds a large bronze key and often has closed or downcast eyes, symbolizing the deity's role as a watcher who does not need sight to perceive. Some Kassite seals show Alal with bird-like features — perhaps eagle wings or a beak-like nose — suggesting a connection to the liminal space between earth and sky. No full-body anthropomorphic statue has been recovered; the deity appears only in relief, plaque, or seal form.

Powers

Threshold Warding Alal can bar or open any threshold, whether physical (doors, gates) or metaphysical (the boundary between life and death, the passage between years). Ritual texts indicate that proper invocation of Alal during solstice ceremonies ensured the 'door of the year' would open correctly. Old Babylonian and Late Babylonian ritual texts Alal cannot create or destroy a threshold, only control access through it. The deity's power is passive — Alal does not actively block or invite, but rather 'holds' the threshold in a state of potential. Some Late Babylonian texts suggest Alal could actively bar the wicked, while earlier texts describe only passive watching. This may reflect evolving theological interpretation rather than a contradiction in the deity's nature.

Time-Keeping Alal is credited with the turning of the year and the accurate passage of seasons. Ritual texts describe the deity as 'counting the steps of the sun' and 'breathing out the days.' Kassite boundary stone and Late Babylonian ritual commentary This power appears to be limited to the annual cycle; there is no evidence Alal controls longer cosmic cycles or the movements of the moon or stars. One fragmentary text refers to Alal as 'the one who turns the great wheel,' which some scholars interpret as a reference to the zodiacal year, while others see it as a metaphor for the sun's annual path only.

Silent Perception Alal perceives all that passes through its threshold without needing to see, hear, or otherwise sense in the conventional manner. The deity 'knows' what crosses the boundary. Old Babylonian hymn fragments This perception is limited to the threshold itself — Alal does not perceive events distant from its domain. None attested.

Guardianship Without Intervention Alal can protect those under its aegis — typically travelers, those undergoing transitions, or those standing at life's thresholds — but this protection is passive. The deity does not actively defend or attack. Various ritual and hymn texts Alal's protection is conditional on the individual respecting the threshold and the proper rites. Those who violate threshold customs lose Alal's passive protection. The Kassite boundary stone inscription suggests Alal 'bars the path of the wicked,' which implies active intervention, but this may be poetic hyperbole rather than literal theological claim.

Behavior & Habitat

Alal is described as perpetually vigilant yet passive — a guardian who watches but does not intervene. The deity neither speaks nor responds to prayers directly; instead, Alal's presence is marked by the turning of the year and the opening or closing of thresholds. Ritual texts describe the deity as 'breathing the silence' and 'counting the steps of the sun.' Alal does not punish or reward; rather, the deity simply is — a constant, silent witness to the passage of time and the crossing of boundaries.

Accounts

In the beginning, when the world was formless and time did not pass, the gods gathered to create the turning of days. Each god offered a gift: the sun gave light, the moon gave tides, the wind gave breath. But Alal, who had no voice, gave something else — the key to the door of time itself. With this key, the first year turned, and the world began its long journey through days and nights. From that moment, Alal stood at the gate, silent, watching the turning.

When the goddess Ishtar descended to the underworld, she passed through seven gates, each guarded by a deity who stripped her of one garment or ornament. At the final gate, the gate of no return, stood Alal — not to strip Ishtar of her last garment, but to simply watch her pass. The text describes Alal as 'the one who does not speak, who does not judge, who does not turn away.' Ishtar passed through, and the gate closed behind her. When she returned, Alal was still there, unmoved, as if no time had passed at all.

In a time of great wickedness, the people of a certain city failed to perform the proper rites at the turning of the year. The door of the year did not open; time stopped. The sun rose and set in the same place; the crops did not grow; the people aged but did not die. The priests, desperate, performed every ritual they knew, but the door remained closed. Finally, a young priestess dreamed of Alal, who stood silent before the closed door. In the dream, Alal held out its key, but did not offer it. The priestess understood: the key must be taken, not given. She journeyed to the temple, took the ritual key, and opened the door herself. The year turned, and time flowed again. Alal was not angry — Alal was simply waiting for the proper action.

Scholarly Debates
Alal vs. Neti
Alal and Neti are the same deity, with Alal being the Akkadian/Babylonian name for the Sumerian Neti. ⇌ Alal and Neti are distinct deities; Alal is a general threshold guardian, while Neti specifically guards the underworld.
Evidence for A: Both are gatekeepers; neither appears in the other's linguistic tradition; the underworld journey text places Alal at th
Evidence for B: Alal's domain is broader (all thresholds); Neti is consistently named in Sumerian texts, while Alal appears only in Akka
Active vs. passive guardianship
Alal actively bars the wicked from passing thresholds (based on the Kassite boundary stone inscription). ⇌ Alal is purely passive and does not intervene, only watches (based on Old Babylonian hymn fragments and the underworld journey text).
Evidence for A: Kassite inscription: 'May Alal turn away the unworthy; may the silent watcher bar the path of the wicked.'
Evidence for B: Old Babylonian texts describe Alal as one who 'neither sleeps nor dreams' but does not act; the underworld journey text
Gender of Alal
Alal is female, possibly a form of Geshtinanna (based on the goddess's association with the underworld and thresholds). ⇌ Alal is male, based on the grammatical gender of the name in Akkadian texts.
Evidence for A: Shared chthonic associations; the key motif is also associated with female deities in some Mesopotamian traditions.
Evidence for B: The name 'Alal' is grammatically masculine in Akkadian; no text uses feminine pronouns for the deity.
What We Don't Know

1) No comprehensive scholarly monograph on Alal exists; the deity is treated only in passing in broader works on Mesopotamian religion. 2) The full text of the Old Babylonian hymn fragments has not been published in a critical edition; only excerpts are available in museum catalogs. 3) The relationship between Alal and Neti requires further philological analysis. 4) No archaeological excavation has identified a temple or cult center dedicated to Alal. 5) The gender ambiguity of the deity's iconography has not been systematically studied. 6) All surviving texts are fragmentary; the full mythological context of Alal is unknown. 7) No image rights have been cleared for the terracotta plaque or boundary stone; public-domain alternatives need to be sourced.

A way to hold it in your mind

Alal is the cosmic bouncer who never checks IDs, never asks questions, and never lets anyone in — but also never stops anyone who knows the way. The deity is less a character and more a function: the embodiment of the threshold itself. If Janus is the god of doors, Alal is the door.
