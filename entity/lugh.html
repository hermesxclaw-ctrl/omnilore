Who It Is

There is a moment in the great saga where the assembled gods of Ireland, each a master of a single art, are asked whether any one of them can do them all — and before the question completes, a young man walks in and does. Lugh of the Long Arm, the Many-Skilled, is the Celtic polymath demiurge: smith, champion, harpist, sorcerer, poet, king and saviour in a single radiant figure, who enters a court through sheer competence, founds a harvest festival that still names the Irish month of August, and shoots his own grandfather's all-destroying eye out through the back of his skull. He is the god who is simultaneously the pattern of lawful kingship and the villain of a weregild so cruel it leaves a watching father dead of grief. Lugh is the polymath par excellence — 'equally skilled in all the arts' — a warrior, king, master craftsman and saviour rolled into one. He is associated with skill, intelligence, mastery, oaths, truth, the law, and therefore righteous kingship. In folklore he appears as a trickster. He is decisive and strategic on the battlefield (he surveys each warrior's art before the battle), ruthless in pursuing a legal blood-debt (the sons of Tuireann), yet magnanimous in victory (sparing Bres in exchange for agricultural knowledge). Powers
Mastery of all arts (Samildánach)

Lugh's defining ability is being 'equally skilled in all the arts'. At King Nuada's court the doorkeeper refuses him entry without a single serviceable skill; Lugh offers to serve as a wright, smith, champion, swordsman, harpist, hero, poet, historian, sorcerer and craftsman, and each is declined because the Tuatha Dé already possess one skilled in it. He wins entry only by asking whether they already have one who is master of ALL those arts at once, then demonstrating each in turn. This polymathy is the trait Arbois de Jubainville compared to Caesar's Gaulish Mercury as 'inventor of all arts'.

Limit: The mastery is literary/human rather than a single overwhelming weapon-power; in-myth he relies equally on his spear, sling and wit. The exact arts listed vary between tellings.

The unstoppable fiery spear

Lugh's spear (sleg) was one of the Four Jewels of the Tuatha Dé Danann, impossible to overcome, brought to Ireland from Gorias (or Findias). As the Spear of Assal (Gae Assail), the incantation 'Ibar' (Yew) made each cast always hit its mark, and 'Athibar' (Re-Yew) made it return to the hand. In the later Fate of the Children of Tuireann it is the spear Areadbhair of Pisear king of Persia, whose red-hot tip had to be kept immersed in water to prevent it igniting (similar to the Lún of Celtchar); it is called 'Slaughterer'. The spear is repeatedly described as a living weapon that roars and struggles against its thongs before battle, fire flashing from it.

Limit: The thirsty spear's head must be steeped in a sleeping-draught of poppy leaves to keep it at rest between battles (popularised by Charles Squire).

The sling-stone that slew Balor

In the Second Battle of Mag Tuired Lugh kills his grandfather Balor of the Evil Eye with a sling-stone (cloch as a tábaill) shot through the venomous eye and out the back of Balor's head, where it wreaks havoc on the Fomorian ranks behind him. A poem in Egerton MS 1782 specifies the ammunition was not plain stone but a tathlum — a composite ball made from the blood of toads, bears, lions, vipers and the neck-base of Osmuinn, mixed with the sands of the Armorian and Red Seas.

Limit: Requires Balor's eye to be open at the moment of the cast; the eye only kills when its eyelid (held by a ring requiring four warriors to lift) is raised.

Weapons and gifts from Manannán

Lugh is equipped with the sea-god's own treasures: the sword Fragarach (Freagarthach, 'the Answerer'), the horse Enbarr/Aonbhar (which travels over land and sea), the boat Scuabtuinne ('Wave-Sweeper'), his armour and his helmet (the Cathbarr, whose removal reveals Lugh's blinding radiance).

Limit: Furnished as gifts in the late Fate of the Children of Tuireann; he is said to begrudge lending the horse on the ground that one should not make 'a loan of a loan'.

Magic hound Failinis

Lugh's whelp Failinis (Shalinnis/Salinnis in the Ossianic ballad), demanded as éiric (forfeit) from the sons of Tuireann, 'the ancient greyhound that had been with Lugh of the Mantles'. A marvellous hound of the Otherworld, it recalls the hound-theme that runs through Cian's brother Cú and Cú Chulainn's very name.

Limit: Little is narrated of its deeds outside the roster of his possessions.

Founder of festival, games and inventions

Lugh is credited with founding the Lughnasadh harvest festival and the Óenach Tailten (Tailteann Games) in honour of his foster-mother Tailtiu, an Olympic-style assembly of athletic contests, horse-racing, feasting, matchmaking and trade. He is also said to have invented the board game fidchell, ball games, and horse-racing.

Limit: These are cultural-founder myths rather than active combat powers.

Supernatural healing / incarnation mechanics

Lugh heals the gravely wounded Cú Chulainn over three days on the Táin, and is himself described as Cú Chulainn's divine father and incarnation. As a shape-shifting Tuatha Dé Danann he can appear and vanish (his 'house' at the Brug na Bóinne disappears at dawn), and can leave a child in a mortal woman's womb by supernatural means.

Limit: His healing of Cú Chulainn is temporary (the hero returns to battle nonetheless).

Accounts I
The Second Battle of Mag TuiredCath Maige Tuired (12th-c. text from 9th-c. material)

Under the Fomorians' oppression, Lugh arrives at King Nuada's court and, by claiming mastery of every art at once, is appointed Chief Ollam and given command of the Tuatha Dé Danann. After surveying what each warrior brings to the fight, he leads them against the Fomorians under Balor and Indech. Balor kills Nuada and opens his murderous eye, but Lugh shoots a sling-stone through it and out the back of Balor's head, killing him and turning the eye's power back on the Fomorian army. Lugh spares the half-Fomorian former king Bres on condition he teach the Tuatha Dé how to plough, sow and reap. He then joins the Dagda and Ogma in rescuing the Dagda's magic harp Uaithne from the retreating Fomorians.

The birth of Lugh and the death of Balor (folktale)Folktale told to John O'Donovan by Shane O'Dugan of Tory Island (1835); adapted by Lady Gregory; also Biróg/Cian versions

Balor hears a druid's prophecy that he will be killed by his own grandson, so he locks his only daughter Ethniu in the Tór Mór tower of Tory Island under the care of twelve women. Cian (Mac Cinnfhaelaidh) seeks revenge for Balor's theft of the magic cow Glas Gaibhnenn and, with the help of the fairy-woman Biróg, enters the tower and seduces Ethniu. She bears triplets; Balor orders them drowned, but one child is rescued by Biróg, given to his father, and fostered by his smith-uncle Gavida (and, in the fuller variants, by Manannán and Tailtiu). The child grows up and kills Balor with a red-hot iron rod or a red spear through the evil eye, fulfilling the prophecy — the recognizable analogue of Lugh. Some variants say the two drowned infants became 'the first seals'.

Lugh joins the court of Nuada (Samildánach)Cath Maige Tuired / Lebor Gabála Érenn

Arriving at Tara, Lugh is refused at the gate for lacking a single useful skill. He offers himself as wright, smith, champion, swordsman, harpist, hero, poet, historian, sorcerer and craftsman — each rejected because the Tuatha Dé already have a master of that craft. He then asks whether they have anyone who excels at them all at once, wins the argument, and is admitted as Chief Ollam of Ireland. He wins a flagstone-throwing contest against the champion Ogma and entertains the court with his harp. Struck by the Tuatha Dé's meek acceptance of Fomorian oppression, he is given command and begins preparations for war.

Accounts II
The Fate of the Children of TuireannOidheadh Chloinne Tuireann (17th-c. narrative)

The sons of Tuireann — Brian, Iuchar and Iucharba — kill Lugh's father Cian as an old enemy, tricking him out of pig-form so that Lugh may legally claim weregild for a father rather than a pig. Lugh holds a feast, gets the brothers to admit only death could repay the killing of their own father, then reveals his dead father and sets them an impossible quest to gather the magic possessions he wants (the spear of Pisear, Enbarr the horse, the boat Scuabtuinne, Failinis, the three apples, the pigskin that heals all wounds, etc.). They complete every task but, wounded by poisoned spears, are denied the healing pigskin by the implacable Lugh; they die, and Tuireann dies of grief over their bodies. Lugh thereby obtains the devastating spear, horse, boat and hound.

Father of Cú ChulainnCompert Con Culainn (Recensions I & II); Táin Bó Cúailnge

In two versions, Lugh fathers the great hero. In one, Deichtine (sister/daughter of King Conchobar) shelters overnight at a house that is really Lugh's, where his wife is in labour; at dawn the house vanishes at the Brug na Bóinne, and Lugh tells the sleeping Deichtine that his child is in her womb — though she later aborts it and conceives (with her husband Sualtam) the boy Sétanta. In the other, Deichtine disappears and is found as Lugh's guest bearing his son Sétanta. As the adult Cú Chulainn lies wounded on the Táin, Lugh descends, reveals he is the hero's father, and heals his wounds over three days. Cú Chulainn is regarded as an incarnation of Lugh.

Lughnasadh and the Assembly of TaltiLebor Gabála Érenn; Dindsenchas; medieval Óenach tradition

Lugh institutes an Olympic-style Assembly of Talti (Teltown, Co. Meath) that ends on Lughnasadh (1 August) in memory of his foster-mother Tailtiu, who died of exhaustion after clearing the plains of Ireland for agriculture. He likewise founds Lughnasadh fairs at Carman and Naas in honour of the tutelary goddesses of those regions. The games include horse-racing, martial displays, trading, matchmaking and the settling of legal disputes; a truce is observed for the duration. Folklorist Máire MacNeill reads the festival as a ritual re-enactment of Lugh seizing the harvest from the guardian god Crom Dubh — a struggle paralleling Lugh's defeat of the blight/sun-scorch figure Balor.

Lugh as 'sun god' vs god of oathsThe Victorian 'sun god / Apollo' reading — Lugh's name from *leuk- ('shining'), identifying him with the sun and comparing his radiance and festival to solar deities — was dominant from the 19th c. and still appears in popular coverage.Modern specialist etymology rejects it on phonology: *leuk- could never yield Proto-Celtic *lug-, so Lugh is better read as an oath/law/rightful-kingship god (*lewgʰ-, 'to bind by oath', cf. Old Irish luige). The 'sun god' image survives mainly as epithet-metaphor (his radiant face) and as modern reception, not as attested cult.
Lugh as Caesar's 'Gaulish Mercury' (Lugus reconstruction)Arbois de Jubainville (1884) and later defenders identify Irish Lugh with the god Caesar calls 'Mercury, inventor of all arts', the toponym Lugdunum, and the plural 'Lugoves' inscriptions, reconstructing a pan-Celtic chief god.Bernhard Maier argues 'inventor of all arts' is a Roman literary topos, the 1-August festival date is a coincidence of the calends of Augustus, and the Lugoves' obscurity and their mostly-null British/Irish epigraphic evidence undercuts the whole reconstruction ('in effect question[ing] the existence of Continental Lugus'). Ovist and Hily defend it.
Cú Chulainn's conception — Lugh's child or not?Compert Con Culainn Recension I has Deichtine abort Lugh's child after it is placed in her womb, then conceive Sétanta by her husband Sualtam — making Lugh's paternity at best 'spiritual' (the hero an incarnation).Recension II has Deichtine herself give birth while at Lugh's house, so Sétanta/Cú Chulainn is Lugh's literal son.
The weapon that slew BalorCath Maige Tuired and Lebor Gabála say a sling-stone (cloch as a tábaill) shot through the evil eye.Folklore variants give Lugh a red-hot iron rod or a red spear forged by the smith (Gavida/Gaivnin Gow); a 12th-c. account has him behead Balor at Mizen Head rather than death by eye-stone.
Lugh's death and its causeThe dindsenchas/Lebor Gabála death-tale has Lugh killed by Cermait's sons (Mac Cuill, Mac Cecht, Mac Gréine) spearing him and drowning him in Loch Lugborta after forty years' rule, in vengeance for his killing of Cermait.Because Lugh is a euhemerised deity, some reconstructions treat his 'death' as euhemerisation rather than a genuinely attested divine death; the saga itself is one of plenty-of-mortal kings with death-tales.
Lugh vs Lleu Llaw Gyffes — same god?Linguistically Lugh and Lleu are both correct reflexes of a Gaulish/Brittonic name 'Lugus' (Hutton), and both carry 'skilful-hand / master-arts' epithets.Their stories are near-totally dissimilar (Lleu's maternal curse, flower-wife, eagle transformation in the Mabinogi have no Irish parallel); Jessica Hemming notes Lugus is absent from the British and Irish epigraphic record, so the linkage rests mainly on the name.
