Who It Is

Olodumare (also called Olorun, 'Owner of Heaven') is the supreme deity of the Yoruba people — the creator of the universe, the source of all life, and the ultimate authority over all orishas, spirits, and humans. Unlike the orishas who are actively involved in daily human affairs, Olodumare is a transcendent, somewhat remote figure who delegated the work of creation and governance to the orishas, particularly Obatala. He is the divine monarch who rules from the highest heaven, rarely intervening directly in earthly matters but accessible through the intercession of the orishas — especially Eshu, the divine messenger.

In Yoruba theology, Olodumare is not typically depicted in art or sculpture — he is considered too vast and abstract for human representation. His presence is felt in the sky (hence the name Olorun, 'Owner of the Sky'), in the breath of life (emi) that animates every living being, and in the destiny (ori) that each soul receives before birth. His colors are the white of pure light and the gold of the sun, and his symbols include the sun, the sky, and the breath of wind. He is sometimes described as having no shrine and no direct worship because he is beyond all physical forms — though in practice, many Yoruba people do pray directly to Olodumare, particularly in moments of ultimate crisis.

The central myth of Olodumare describes the creation of the world. In the beginning, there was only water and the sky — a vast, formless expanse. Olodumare summoned Obatala (or, in some versions, Oduduwa) and gave him the task of creating dry land. He provided a golden chain, a snail shell full of earth, and a five-toed hen. Obatala descended the chain, poured the earth onto the waters, and released the hen, which scratched and spread the soil to form the first land at Ile-Ife. Olodumare then sent the other orishas to populate and organize the world. When creation was complete, Olodumare withdrew to the highest heaven — but not before bestowing the breath of life into the first humans molded by Obatala.

Olodumare's relationship with humanity centers on destiny and accountability. Before birth, each soul (ori) appears before Olodumare and chooses its destiny for the coming life — its lifespan, its major challenges, its potential achievements. After death, the soul returns to Olodumare for judgment. Those who have lived good lives are permitted to join the ancestors (egungun); those who have lived evil lives may be sent back to earth for reincarnation as a form of correction. This theology establishes a moral universe governed by ultimate justice, even when earthly circumstances seem unjust.

The name Olodumare itself is revelatory: it combines 'Olo' (owner), 'Odu' (fullness/container of destiny), and 'Mare' (that does not move) — suggesting a being who contains all destinies and is eternally constant. In the African diaspora, particularly in Brazil and Cuba, the supreme deity is often called Olorun or Olodumare, with the orishas serving as intermediaries — a structure that facilitated syncretism with Catholic saints. Olodumare's transcendence has led some scholars to describe Yoruba religion as a form of 'diffused monotheism,' where a single supreme being is acknowledged alongside many lesser divine agents.

Form

Olodumare is typically not represented in physical form — He is understood to be formless, invisible, and beyond human comprehension. Unlike the orishas, there are no statues, masks, or anthropomorphic images of Olodumare in traditional Yoruba religion. He is associated with the sky (as Olorun, 'Owner of Heaven'), and His presence is acknowledged in the vastness above rather than in any specific icon. In some traditions, He is conceptually associated with light, brilliance, and the color white — colors connoting purity, transcendence, and absolute power. In diasporic traditions (Santería, Candomblé), Olodumare/Olorum remains similarly abstract, though syncretic associations sometimes link Him to the Christian God the Father or to the Holy Spirit. His remoteness is a core theological feature: He is too great to be approached directly, hence the necessity of the orishas as intermediaries.

Powers

Source of Àṣẹ (Divine Authority) Olodumare is the ultimate source of àṣẹ — the divine power, command, and life-force that makes all things possible. Every orisha, every ritual, every act of creation derives its power from Olodumare's àṣẹ. Without this fundamental animating principle, the universe would be inert. This is not a 'power' Olodumare exercises but His essential nature: He IS the source of all power. Yoruba theology (universal) Àṣẹ must be channeled through intermediaries — the orishas, ancestors, and properly performed rituals. Humans cannot access Olodumare's àṣẹ directly. None — this concept is universally acknowledged across all Yoruba and diasporic traditions.

Creator of the Universe (Èlédà) Olodumare created the universe ex nihilo or (in some versions) from the primordial waters. He established the separation between heaven (ọ̀run) and earth (ayé), created the primordial spirits (irunmọlẹ̀), and set the cosmic order in motion. In most traditions, He delegates the detailed work of creation to the orishas — particularly Obatala, who fashions human bodies from clay — but the creative initiative and power originate from Olodumare alone. Ifá Literary Corpus / Yoruba creation narratives After the initial creation, Olodumare does not continuously create — He delegated ongoing governance. New souls are created by His authority but the physical world runs on delegated power. Regional variants attribute different levels of direct involvement to Olodumare. In some Ifẹ̀ traditions, Obatala plays a more prominent creative role.

Giver of Life (Èmí) Olodumare alone can give the breath of life (èmí) to living beings. Even when Obatala fashions human bodies from clay, only Olodumare can animate them. This power is absolute and non-delegable — the one thing the orishas cannot do. At death, the èmí returns to Olodumare, completing the cycle. Ifá Literary Corpus / universal Yoruba belief Life, once given, follows its own destiny (àyànmọ́) and cannot be arbitrarily revoked by Olodumare — destiny is binding even on the divine. None significant.

Determiner of Destiny (Àyànmọ́) Before birth, each soul chooses or is assigned its destiny (àyànmọ́ or ipín) from Olodumare. This destiny encompasses the broad arc of one's life — lifespan, major events, success, failure, children, and death. The choosing of destiny is central to Yoruba theodicy: suffering is explained not as divine punishment but as the working-out of a destiny the soul itself chose. Ifá Literary Corpus / Yoruba cosmology Destiny can be modified through divination, sacrifice (ẹbọ), and the intervention of the orishas — it is not rigid fatalism. Bad destiny can be 'renegotiated' through proper ritual practice. Some traditions emphasize the immutability of destiny; others stress its negotiability through Ifá divination and sacrifice.

Omniscience and Ultimate Justice Olodumare is described as all-seeing and all-knowing (Arinú-rode Olúmọ-ọkàn — 'the One who sees inside and outside, the Knower of all hearts'). His justice is perfect and immanent — no act goes unaccounted for, and cosmic balance is maintained through the natural consequences of actions rather than through a final judgment. He is the ultimate arbiter of right and wrong, though He delegates immediate enforcement to the orishas (particularly Eshu). Yoruba theology (universal) Olodumare's justice operates through natural law rather than direct intervention. Individuals may not see justice in their lifetime, but cosmic balance is maintained across generations. None — this attribute is consistently attested.

Behavior & Habitat

Olodumare is not described as having a 'personality' in the way the orishas do. He is characterized by absolute power, perfect justice, and transcendent wisdom. Unlike the orishas — who have human-like flaws, rivalries, passions, and failings — Olodumare is beyond such attributes. He does not directly intervene in human affairs except at the most fundamental level: the granting of life (emi) and the determination of destiny (àyànmọ́). He is the source of àṣẹ — the divine power that animates the universe and empowers the orishas. Yoruba theology describes Him as 'the One who sees all and knows all' (Arinú-rode Olúmọ-ọkàn), 'the perfect judge,' and 'the one whose works are unassailable.' His justice is conceived as immanent and karmic rather than eschatological — wrongs are balanced within a person's lifetime or across generations, not in an afterlife judgment.

Accounts

In the beginning, there was only the primordial waters and the sky above them, and Olodumare dwelt in the heavens. Olodumare decided to create solid land and delegated the task to the orishas. He gave Obatala a chain, a handful of soil (or sand), and a five-toed chicken (or a chameleon in some versions). Obatala descended from heaven on the chain, scattered the soil on the waters, and the chicken scratched and spread it to form the first land at Ilé-Ifẹ̀. Obatala then created the first human bodies from clay. Olodumare breathed life (èmí) into them, making them living beings. In the variant where Obatala got drunk on palm wine during creation, Olodumare sent Oduduwa to complete the work, establishing the founding myth of Ifẹ̀ and the Yoruba people.

Before a soul is born into the world, it must appear before Olodumare (or, in some versions, before Orunmila acting as His agent) to choose its destiny (àyànmọ́). The soul kneels and selects its life's path — its lifespan, its prosperity, its spouse, its children, its death. After choosing, the soul passes through the 'gate of forgetfulness' (the border between ọ̀run and ayé) and forgets its chosen destiny. The purpose of Ifá divination is to help humans remember and align with their chosen destiny, and ẹbọ (sacrifice) can modify a negative destiny. The choosing of destiny is the foundational myth explaining human fate and suffering in Yoruba thought.

After creating the physical world, Olodumare summoned the irunmọlẹ̀ (primordial spirits) and assigned them their domains. To Ogun, He gave iron and the power of the warrior; to Yemoja, the oceans and motherhood; to Shango, thunder and fire; to Oshun, the rivers and fertility; to Orunmila, wisdom and divination; to Eshu, the crossroads and divine communication. Each orisha received a specific sphere of influence and the àṣẹ to govern it. Olodumare then withdrew to the highest heavens, delegating the day-to-day governance of the cosmos to the orishas. Only in matters of life, death, and destiny does He remain directly involved. This delegation explains why Yoruba religion focuses on orisha worship rather than direct worship of Olodumare.

In some Yoruba narratives, Olodumare once grew displeased with humanity's wickedness and sent a great flood to cleanse the world. Only a few righteous individuals survived — variants differ on who: some name Orunmila, others Obatala, others a specific pious person. After the flood subsided, Olodumare renewed the earth and repopulated it through the survivors. This flood narrative shows remarkable parallels to Mesopotamian (Utnapishtim/Ziusudra), Biblical (Noah), and other world flood myths, raising questions about diffusion versus independent development.

What We Don't Know

The pre-contact development of Olodumare theology — what did Yoruba people believe about the Supreme Being before Islamic and Christian contact? — is difficult to reconstruct due to the lack of pre-18th-century written sources. The regional variation in creation narratives (especially the Obatala vs. Oduduwa traditions) and what this reveals about pre-colonial Yoruba political-theological dynamics deserves further study. The reception of Olodumare in contemporary Yoruba Christianity and Islam — how indigenous concepts of God are negotiating with global monotheisms — is an active and contested field. The specifically female or gender-fluid aspects of Olodumare (some traditions suggest a primordial androgyny or a female consort) are understudied.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Olodumare as the sun. You cannot look at the sun directly — it will blind you. But you can feel its warmth, see the world it illuminates, and watch the plants grow from its energy. The orishas are like rays of that sun — each a different color, each illuminating a different aspect of the world. You can look at the rays, pray to the rays, build shrines to the rays. But the rays would not exist without the sun, and their power is the sun's power, refracted. That is Olodumare — not absent, but too present to perceive directly; not indifferent, but operating through a system so perfectly designed that direct intervention is unnecessary. He is the breath in every living thing that no orisha can replicate. He is the destiny that shaped your life before you were born, and the reason you need a diviner to help you remember what you chose.
