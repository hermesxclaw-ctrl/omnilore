Who It Is

In the catalogues where modern Japan inventories its monsters, Manboshi sits quietly on the edge of the bestiary — a beast-type yōkai with no ancient scroll to call home, no temple legend, and no Wikipedia page, yet recorded as if it had always been there. Its presence is a reminder that folklore is not only inherited but also assembled, one encyclopedia entry at a time.

Behavioral description in located entries is generic for mountain-beast yōkai: inhabits remote mountains/forests, avoids or startles humans, may be dangerous if encountered. No dialogue, motive, trickster behavior, or moral lesson is specified in the brief entries examined. No first-person oral narrative with personality traits was located. Interpretive note: compilers appear to assign 'beast' behavior by archetype rather than by story. Status: reconstructed from archetype, not from narrative.

Accounts I
The Mizuki Encyclopedia Entry (the core attestation)Mizuki Shigeru, Yōkai Daizukan / Mujara series

Shōwa-Heisei, 1981-2004 (compilation period)

Manboshi is listed among hundreds of yōkai with a short definitional entry and an illustration. The entry establishes its existence in the modern bestiary but provides no extended narrative - no named protagonist, date, or village. It functions as a catalogue record rather than a tale. The story, in effect, is the act of cataloguing itself: Mizuki, drawing on his field notes, war experience, and earlier yōkai literature, fixes a name and image for circulation. Creates the primary source for all later mentions; without this entry, Manboshi has no traceable textual lineage in digitized sources.

The Absent Edo Scroll (negative story)Toriyama Sekien, Gazu Hyakki Yagyō (1776), Konjaku Gazu Zoku Hyakki (1779), Konjaku Hyakki Shūi (1781), Gazu Hyakki Tsurezure Bukuro (1784) - searched as control corpus

Edo, 1776-1784

Manboshi does not appear in Sekien's four major yōkai picture scrolls, which are the canonical Edo visual inventory. This absence is a story about transmission: unlike Kappa, Tengu, or Yuki-onna, Manboshi lacks an Edo visual-textual anchor, suggesting it was not part of the 18th-century urban yōkai imagination that Sekien codified. Supports hypothesis that Manboshi is a 20th-century editorial yōkai rather than an early modern folk survival.

The Yanagita Vacuum (absence in foundational folklore studies)Yanagita Kunio, Tōno Monogatari (1910); Yōkai Dangi and folklore archives; International Research Center for Japanese Studies (Nichibunken) Yōkai Database searches

Meiji-Shōwa (1910 onward)

Manboshi is not indexed in Yanagita's seminal Tōno Monogatari nor in the major academic folklore tale-type indices consulted via secondary scholarship (Foster's bibliographies). No field-collected oral narrative (kōtōdenshō) naming Manboshi was located in digitized Nichibunken summaries. The 'story' is therefore an archival silence: the creature does not appear where mountain yōkai like Yamawaro and Yamabiko do. Reinforces that Manboshi lacks documented oral-history provenance; any claim of 'ancient village legend' requires new primary evidence.

Accounts II
The Copy-Chain Transmission (how a brief entry becomes lore)Murakami Kenji, Yōkai Jiten (2000) and derivative web compilations (Yokai.com, Japanese Wikipedia-adjacent wikis as discovery leads only)

Heisei-Reiwa, 2000-2026

After Mizuki, Murakami reproduces the Manboshi entry, often condensing the description further. Websites then scrape and translate that condensation, sometimes adding unsourced embellishments (habitat details, size estimates). This chain is itself the narrative: a minimal record is amplified by repetition until it looks like a rich tradition. Close comparison shows no new primary detail is added at each step, only rephrasing. Creates illusion of multiple independent attestations where only one editorial line exists; complicates source criticism for future researchers.

2010s-2026

In contemporary databases, Manboshi is slotted into a taxonomy: Kingdom = folklore, Class = yōkai, Order = beast. This placement tells a story about how 21st-century knowledge graphs organize monsters: even a thinly attested name receives a type, archetype, and Q-number, making it retrievable and linkable. The Wikidata item Q110322829, though empty of lore, ensures Manboshi persists as a node in the graph. Guarantees digital persistence and cross-referencing, but risks implying historical depth that the sources do not support.

The Mountain-Beast Archetype (comparative context story)Comparative reading of Yamawaro, Yamabiko, and generic san-kai (mountain monster) lore in Foster (2015) and Yoda & Alt (2008)

Edo to present (archetype diachronic)

Manboshi, as described, fits a well-documented archetype: the shaggy mountain beast that embodies fear of the yama - the wild beyond the village. Stories of Yamawaro (mountain child) and Yamabiko (echo yōkai) provide the narrative template that Manboshi's brief entry evokes without narrating: a woodcutter hears/sees something, flees, tells villagers. No source claims Manboshi performs the specific tricks of those relatives (Yamawaro helping with work, Yamabiko mimicking voices). The archetype story helps explain why a minimal entry is still legible to readers. Allows responsible contextualization without inventing a specific Manboshi tale; highlights what is generic vs what would be distinctive if more evidence emerges.
