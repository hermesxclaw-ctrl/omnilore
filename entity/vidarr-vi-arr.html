Víðarr

Etymology

Attested Old Norse Víðarr is generally connected with the adjective víðr, meaning “wide, broad,” and many scholars reconstruct a Proto-Germanic root *wīđaz (“wide, extensive”), yielding an interpretive gloss such as “wide ruler” or “he of the wide land.” [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) [[1]](https://einherjarhall.com/pantheon/vidar.html) This interpretation is not explicitly stated in the medieval texts and is therefore inferred rather than attested. Some philologists have proposed that the name may originally have denoted a deity of expansive, uncultivated spaces—forests or wilderness—given the association of his hall Víði with brushwood and high grass, but this remains speculative and not universally accepted. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) [[2]](https://www.worldhistory.org/Vidar/)

Cultural Ownership

Víðarr belongs to the Norse mythological tradition, particularly the Icelandic and broader Scandinavian corpus preserved in the Poetic Edda and Prose Edda. His cultic or ritual status in the Viking Age is poorly documented; he appears primarily in literary sources rather than in securely identified archaeological or epigraphic evidence. The surviving texts were written in 13th‑century Christian Iceland, which introduces interpretive layers and possible Christian framing, but the figure himself is rooted in earlier pre‑Christian Germanic religion. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) [[2]](https://www.worldhistory.org/Vidar/)

Disputed Identifications

Several interpretive and syncretic identifications have been proposed. In one passage of the Younger Edda, a narrative equates “Ale or Vidar” with the Roman hero Aeneas, an example of medieval euhemerism and classical syncretism rather than a genuine ancient tradition; modern scholars generally treat this as Snorri Sturluson’s literary device, not evidence that Víðarr was historically identified with Aeneas. [[3]](https://loregraph.org/sources/prose-edda-vidar) Some researchers have suggested that Víðarr may be a reflex of a Proto‑Indo‑European “avenger” or “survivor” deity, parallel to other silent or marginal gods who act at the end of cosmic cycles, but such reconstructions rely on comparative mythology and cannot be confirmed from Norse sources alone. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) [[2]](https://www.worldhistory.org/Vidar/) There is also occasional speculation that Víðarr and Váli form a conceptual pair of avenging sons, yet the texts treat them as distinct individuals with separate roles, and no pre‑Christian cult evidence supports a merger.

Archive Dossier — Approach

In the roaring chaos of Ragnarök, when the sky is split by fire and the sea swallows the land, one figure moves without a word. He does not boast like Thor, scheme like Loki, or weave riddles like Odin. He waits—silent, watchful, carrying a single purpose that has been gathering strength for ages in the scraps of leather cut from countless shoes. When the wolf Fenrir closes its jaws around Odin and the All‑father vanishes into darkness, this quiet god steps forward. His thick‑soled foot drives into the monster’s lower jaw, his hands wrench the terrible mouth open, and the devourer of gods is torn apart. Víðarr, the silent son, does not save his father, but he ensures that the killer does not survive to rule the ruins. In a mythology obsessed with fate and foreknowledge, he is the embodiment of a different kind of power: the strength that speaks only once, at the end of the world, and then walks into the new dawn without needing to explain itself.

Who It Is

Vidarr is the silent Norse god of vengeance, the son of Odin and the jötunn (giantess) Gríðr. He is one of the few gods foretold to survive Ragnarök. His greatest moment comes at Ragnarök itself: after the wolf Fenrir devours Odin, Vidarr avenges his father by stepping onto the wolf's lower jaw with a special shoe (made from all the leather scraps ever cut by shoemakers — giving cobblers their folk saying), forcing the wolf's mouth open, and stabbing it through the upper jaw to the heart, killing Fenrir. He is called 'the silent god' in the Eddas — some interpretations suggest this silence relates to ritual silence before vengeance, or to his role as a god of the forest where stillness reigns. After Ragnarök, Vidarr survives to rebuild the world alongside Vali, Thor's sons Modi and Magni, and Baldr and Höðr returned. Vidarr represents the patient, implacable force of vengeance that waits for its moment and then acts with absolute, unstoppable power.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

The surviving Norse texts provide almost no direct physical description of Víðarr. He is not given distinctive facial features, hair color, or stature in the Eddas; instead, his characterization is largely functional and relational. What is attested is that he possesses a “very thick shoe” and that he is “the strongest next after Thor,” which implies a powerful, robust figure capable of grappling with the monstrous wolf Fenrir. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) [[3]](https://loregraph.org/sources/prose-edda-vidar) In later artistic representations—such as 19th‑ and 20th‑century illustrations inspired by Eddic passages—Víðarr is often depicted as a tall warrior, sometimes bearded, clad in simple but sturdy clothing, with one foot thrust into the jaws of a gigantic wolf. These images are adaptation‑specific and not themselves primary evidence. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) [[2]](https://www.worldhistory.org/Vidar/) The thick shoe is sometimes shown as a heavy boot or sandal, visually emphasizing its layered leather construction. Because the texts emphasize his silence and strength rather than ornament, modern artists frequently portray him with minimal jewelry or regalia, underscoring a reserved, utilitarian aesthetic. All such visual details are reconstructed or modern, not attested in the medieval sources.

Symbols & Iconography

Attested symbols associated with Víðarr include:

1. **The thick shoe**: In Gylfaginning, the enthroned figure High explains that Víðarr wears a very thick shoe, and later commentary elaborates that this shoe is made from strips of leather cut from the toes and heels of shoes throughout the ages. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) [[3]](https://loregraph.org/sources/prose-edda-vidar) This shoe becomes his primary iconographic attribute, representing preparation, endurance, and the cumulative contributions of ordinary people (cobblers and wearers) to the god’s future act of vengeance.

2. **The sword or tearing grip**: In some poetic passages, Víðarr kills Fenrir by thrusting a sword into the wolf’s heart through its upper jaw; in others, he is said to tear apart the wolf’s jaws. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) [[2]](https://www.worldhistory.org/Vidar/) These two motifs—sword and rending—coexist in the tradition and are sometimes combined in visual art, where he both pries open the jaws and drives a weapon into the beast.

3. **The wooded hall Víði**: Grímnismál describes Víðarr’s land as overgrown with brushwood and high grass, suggesting a domain of forest or wild growth. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) [[1]](https://einherjarhall.com/pantheon/vidar.html) This association has led to an iconography of Víðarr as a forest god or guardian of quiet, wooded spaces, though the texts themselves do not explicitly call him a “forest god.” The hall’s name and description are attested; the broader ecological symbolism is interpretive.

4. **Silence**: While silence is not a visual symbol, it functions as a conceptual emblem. Snorri explicitly calls him “the silent god,” and he rarely speaks in the surviving poems. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) [[3]](https://loregraph.org/sources/prose-edda-vidar) Modern practitioners and artists often represent this by depicting him with a closed mouth, contemplative posture, or in scenes where others speak while he remains still.

Iconographic depictions on the Gosforth Cross in England have been interpreted as showing Víðarr battling Fenrir, with a figure prying open a wolf’s jaws. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) [[2]](https://www.worldhistory.org/Vidar/) This identification is plausible but not universally accepted; the cross’s imagery is complex and may blend Christian and Norse motifs. Thus, the association of that specific carving with Víðarr is best labeled as “interpreted” rather than definitively attested.

Behaviour & Personality

Víðarr’s personality is defined more by absence than by explicit traits. The Prose Edda introduces him as “the silent god,” and the Poetic Edda gives him no speeches of his own, in contrast to more talkative figures like Odin or Loki. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) [[3]](https://loregraph.org/sources/prose-edda-vidar) This silence has been interpreted in several ways: as ritual silence (a god associated with vows or oaths kept in quiet), as narrative economy (a figure whose role is singular and future‑oriented), or as a reflection of a broader Indo‑European motif of a taciturn avenger. None of these interpretations can be proven, but they highlight how his lack of dialogue becomes a defining feature.

Attested behavior includes his obedience to Odin’s command in Lokasenna, where Odin instructs Víðarr to stand and pour a drink for Loki; Víðarr complies without protest. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) [[1]](https://einherjarhall.com/pantheon/vidar.html) This small scene reinforces his role as a dutiful son and subordinate within the divine assembly. His primary narrative act—avenging Odin by killing Fenrir—is described as decisive, forceful, and successful. He does not hesitate or fail; he rushes at the wolf, places his foot on its lower jaw, and either tears the jaws apart or drives a sword into its heart. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) [[3]](https://loregraph.org/sources/prose-edda-vidar) [[2]](https://www.worldhistory.org/Vidar/) This suggests a personality characterized by patience followed by sudden, focused action.

Modern interpreters often describe Víðarr as embodying endurance, resilience, and quiet strength: a god who waits until the appointed time, then acts with unwavering resolve. [[1]](https://einherjarhall.com/pantheon/vidar.html) [[4]](https://kindredasatru.com/vidarr-vidar-the-silent-god/) These qualities are extrapolated from his silence, his survival of Ragnarök, and his successful vengeance. However, because the medieval texts are sparse, any detailed psychological portrait is necessarily speculative. There is no attested evidence of him engaging in trickery, boasting, or romantic pursuits; his social presence is minimal, and his mythic identity is tightly bound to his single great deed.

Domains & Habitats

Attested domains and habitats include:

- **Asgard and the gods’ assembly**: Víðarr is counted among the Æsir and appears in lists of the gods who sit in judgment or attend feasts, indicating his membership in the divine community of Asgard. [[3]](https://loregraph.org/sources/prose-edda-vidar)

- **Víði, the brushwood land**: Grímnismál describes his land as overgrown with brushwood and high grass, widely spread, and associated with his hall. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) [[1]](https://einherjarhall.com/pantheon/vidar.html) This suggests a domain of wild, uncultivated nature—perhaps forest edges or overgrown fields—rather than the orderly halls of other gods.

- **Idavoll (Íðavöllr) after Ragnarök**: After Surtr’s fire and the sea’s destruction, Víðarr and Váli are said to dwell on the plains of Ida, where Asgard once stood, surviving the cataclysm and inhabiting the renewed world. [[3]](https://loregraph.org/sources/prose-edda-vidar) [[2]](https://www.worldhistory.org/Vidar/) This positions him as a god of post‑catastrophic continuity, associated with the re‑established divine center.

Conceptually, modern practitioners often treat Víðarr as a god of silence, vengeance, survival, and forests, but only the silence, vengeance, and brushwood hall are directly attested. [[1]](https://einherjarhall.com/pantheon/vidar.html) [[4]](https://kindredasatru.com/vidarr-vidar-the-silent-god/) The idea that he is a “forest god” is an extrapolation from the description of his land and from broader ecological symbolism in contemporary Heathen practice.

Version Differences

Across the Poetic Edda and Prose Edda, Víðarr’s core identity remains stable: he is Odin’s son, the silent god, and the avenger who kills Fenrir and survives Ragnarök. However, there are notable differences and tensions:

1. **Method of killing Fenrir**: In Völuspá and Vafþrúðnismál, Víðarr is described as stabbing the wolf in the heart or tearing apart its jaws. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) [[2]](https://www.worldhistory.org/Vidar/) In Gylfaginning, the emphasis falls on his thick shoe and the act of placing his foot on the wolf’s lower jaw and rending its mouth asunder. [[3]](https://loregraph.org/sources/prose-edda-vidar) Some translations foreground the sword; others highlight the rending. Scholars generally treat these as complementary motifs rather than mutually exclusive, but they reflect different narrative emphases.

2. **Silence and speech**: The Prose Edda explicitly labels him “the silent god,” while the Poetic Edda simply omits speeches for him. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) [[3]](https://loregraph.org/sources/prose-edda-vidar) Some scholars argue that Snorri’s epithet may reflect a ritual or cultic tradition of silence, while others see it as a literary characterization. Because no independent ritual texts survive, this remains disputed.

3. **Role after Ragnarök**: In Vafþrúðnismál and Gylfaginning, Víðarr is one of the gods who survive and dwell on the renewed earth, alongside Váli and Thor’s sons Móði and Magni. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) [[3]](https://loregraph.org/sources/prose-edda-vidar) [[2]](https://www.worldhistory.org/Vidar/) Some modern retellings expand his role in the new world, imagining him as a central ruler or guardian, but the medieval texts simply state his survival and residence, without elaborating his governance.

4. **Syncretic identification with Aeneas**: The Younger Edda’s euhemeristic passage equating Ale or Vidar with Aeneas is unique to that text and does not appear in the Poetic Edda. [[3]](https://loregraph.org/sources/prose-edda-vidar) This reflects Snorri’s attempt to integrate Norse myth into a classical framework, not a separate mythic version of Víðarr.

Overall, the differences are more about emphasis and interpretive framing than about fundamentally incompatible characterizations. The core myth—silent son of Odin, avenger of Fenrir, survivor of Ragnarök—remains consistent.

Origins

Vidarr is the son of Odin and Gríðr, a jötunn (giantess) who also helped Thor by giving him magical iron gloves and her belt of strength. To prepare for Ragnarök where he would have to kill Fenrir, Vidarr had cobblers everywhere save their leather scraps — their combined volume created a shoe strong enough to brace against the wolf's jaw.

Attested in Poetic Edda (Völuspá, Vafþrúðnismál) / Prose Edda (Gylfaginning)Era Viking Age (c. 800-1100 CE), recorded 13th century

Archive Dossier — Powers

Silent strength and near‑Thor level power

Source Version Prose Edda, Gylfaginning; Poetic Edda (Vafþrúðnismál, Völuspá); modern scholarly and practitioner commentary.

Description

Snorri Sturluson describes Víðarr as “the strongest next after Thor,” indicating that among the Æsir he ranks second only to the thunder god in raw physical might. [[3]](https://loregraph.org/sources/prose-edda-vidar) [[2]](https://www.worldhistory.org/Vidar/) This strength is not displayed in multiple adventures but concentrated in his single decisive act at Ragnarök, where he overpowers the colossal wolf Fenrir. His silence is not a power in the magical sense, but it functions as a defining trait: he is a god whose strength is held in reserve, not flaunted through boasting or frequent combat. Modern interpreters often treat this as a form of “silent strength,” a capacity to endure and act without verbal display.

Limitations

The texts do not attribute to Víðarr the ability to control weather, wield thunder, or perform complex magic; those domains belong to other gods. His strength is situational, focused on the battle with Fenrir and his survival of Ragnarök. There is no evidence that he intervenes in human affairs or engages in multiple mythic exploits. His silence may limit his narrative presence, making him a marginal figure outside the eschatological context.

Contradictions

No direct contradictions exist regarding his strength ranking; sources consistently place him just below Thor. Some modern retellings exaggerate his power, portraying him as equal to or greater than Thor, but this is adaptation‑specific and not supported by the medieval texts.

Avenging Fenrir and killing the wolf

Source Version Poetic Edda (Völuspá, Vafþrúðnismál); Prose Edda (Gylfaginning); later commentary and illustrations.

Description

Víðarr’s most important and defining ability is his capacity to kill Fenrir, the monstrous wolf who devours Odin at Ragnarök. In Völuspá, the seeress foretells that Víðarr will avenge his father by stabbing the wolf in the heart. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) [[2]](https://www.worldhistory.org/Vidar/) In Vafþrúðnismál, the giant Vafþrúðnir states that Víðarr will tear apart the wolf’s terrible jaws. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) [[2]](https://www.worldhistory.org/Vidar/) Gylfaginning combines these motifs, describing how Víðarr rushes at the wolf, places his foot on its lower jaw, and rips its mouth open, aided by his specially prepared shoe. [[3]](https://loregraph.org/sources/prose-edda-vidar) [[2]](https://www.worldhistory.org/Vidar/) This act is both physical and symbolic: he overcomes the devourer of the All‑father, restoring a measure of justice in the midst of cosmic collapse.

Limitations

Víðarr’s vengeance is narrowly focused. He does not prevent Odin’s death; he acts only after the fatal event. His ability to kill Fenrir does not extend to other monsters in the eschatological battle; Thor fights Jörmungandr, Freyr fights Surtr, and other gods face their own foes. Víðarr’s role is singular, and the texts do not describe him as a general warrior or protector beyond this moment.

Contradictions

The main tension lies in the method of killing: sword thrust versus jaw‑rending. Some scholars argue that the sword motif may be older, while the jaw‑rending emphasizes the shoe tradition and physical grappling. Others see them as poetic variations. There is no consensus on which version is primary, and modern retellings often merge them, which technically harmonizes distinct textual images.

The thick shoe and resistance to Fenrir’s jaws

Source Version Prose Edda, Gylfaginning; later Eddic commentary and folklore.

Description

Víðarr’s thick shoe is a unique, quasi‑magical artifact. Gylfaginning explains that this shoe has been gathering materials through all ages: strips of leather cut from the toes and heels of shoes. [[3]](https://loregraph.org/sources/prose-edda-vidar) [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) Folk tradition later elaborates that cobblers were encouraged to set aside such scraps for Víðarr, contributing to his future victory. The shoe’s function is to protect his foot and give him leverage when he steps into Fenrir’s lower jaw, allowing him to withstand the wolf’s bite and pry its mouth open. This is less a supernatural weapon than a symbol of cumulative preparation and communal support.

Limitations

The shoe’s power is context‑dependent; it is designed specifically for the battle with Fenrir. There is no indication that it grants general invulnerability or speed. Its origin in human leather scraps suggests a cooperative relationship between humans and the god, but this is interpretive rather than explicitly stated in the medieval texts.

Contradictions

Some modern sources treat the shoe as purely mythic, while others emphasize its folkloric development and possible late origin. Scholars debate whether the cobbler tradition reflects genuine pre‑Christian practice or later storytelling. Because independent evidence is lacking, the shoe’s ritual significance remains uncertain.

Survival of Ragnarök and continuity in the renewed world

Source Version Poetic Edda (Vafþrúðnismál); Prose Edda (Gylfaginning, Regeneration chapter).

Description

Víðarr possesses the remarkable ability—or destiny—to survive the cataclysm of Ragnarök. Vafþrúðnismál and Gylfaginning state that after Surtr’s fire and the sea’s destruction, Víðarr and Váli will dwell on the plains of Ida, where Asgard once stood, joined by Thor’s sons Móði and Magni. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) [[3]](https://loregraph.org/sources/prose-edda-vidar) [[2]](https://www.worldhistory.org/Vidar/) This survival is not described as a magical shield or explicit immunity; rather, it is a foretold outcome of the cosmic cycle. Nonetheless, it positions Víðarr as a figure of endurance and continuity, a god whose existence bridges the old world and the new.

Limitations

The texts do not specify what powers Víðarr exercises in the renewed world. He is not explicitly named as a ruler, lawgiver, or creator of new beings. His survival is a narrative fact, but his post‑Ragnarök activities remain unknown. Modern reconstructions that assign him governance or ecological stewardship are speculative.

Contradictions

There is no direct contradiction about his survival; sources agree that he lives. The uncertainty lies in the scope of his role afterward. Some scholars caution against over‑systematizing the post‑Ragnarök world, noting that the Eddas provide only fragmentary hints.

Ritual or symbolic silence (hypothetical)

Source Version Prose Edda (Gylfaginning); modern scholarly and practitioner interpretations.

Description

Snorri’s epithet “the silent god” has prompted theories that Víðarr may have been associated with ritual silence—perhaps in oath‑taking, mourning, or contemplative practices. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) [[1]](https://einherjarhall.com/pantheon/vidar.html) [[2]](https://www.worldhistory.org/Vidar/) Comparative mythologists sometimes link this to Indo‑European patterns in which certain gods refrain from speech until a decisive moment. However, the Norse texts do not explicitly describe any ritual in which worshippers remain silent in his honor, nor do they narrate scenes where his silence has magical effects. Thus, “silence” functions more as a narrative and conceptual attribute than as a clearly defined power.

Limitations

Because no ritual manuals or liturgical texts survive that mention Víðarr, any claim about formal “powers of silence” is speculative. His silence may simply reflect the limited scope of his mythic role and the narrative focus on other gods.

Contradictions

Some scholars reject the idea of ritual silence altogether, arguing that Snorri’s epithet is a literary flourish. Others maintain that the epithet likely reflects some lost cultic practice. Without additional evidence, the debate remains unresolved.

Archive Dossier — Relationships

Odin

Type familySource Poetic Edda (Völuspá, Vafþrúðnismál, Lokasenna); Prose Edda (Gylfaginning).

Description

Víðarr is explicitly described as a son of Odin, the chief of the Æsir. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) [[3]](https://loregraph.org/sources/prose-edda-vidar) [[2]](https://www.worldhistory.org/Vidar/) Their relationship is characterized by duty and vengeance: Odin commands Víðarr to serve at Ægir’s feast, and Víðarr later avenges Odin’s death by killing Fenrir at Ragnarök. The texts do not depict extended conversations or emotional exchanges between them, but the narrative weight of Víðarr’s act suggests a strong filial bond.

Version Note

All major Eddic sources agree on Víðarr’s parentage and his role as Odin’s avenger.

Conflicting Accounts

No primary source contradicts the claim that Odin is Víðarr’s father. Some modern retellings introduce additional emotional or psychological dimensions to their relationship, but these are creative expansions rather than textual contradictions.

Gríðr (Grid), the giantess

Type familySource Prose Edda, Skáldskaparmál and related sections; summarized in Gylfaginning and later commentaries.

Description

Víðarr’s mother is the jötunn (giantess) Gríðr, who also plays a role in Thor’s journey to Geirröðr by lending him her belt of strength, iron gloves, and staff. [[3]](https://loregraph.org/sources/prose-edda-vidar) [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) This parentage places Víðarr at the intersection of divine and giant lineages, combining Odin’s authority with Gríðr’s primal strength. The texts do not narrate interactions between Víðarr and his mother, but her role in aiding Thor suggests a family network of gods and giants cooperating against hostile forces.

Version Note

Gríðr’s motherhood of Víðarr is attested in Snorri’s prose and accepted in modern scholarship.

Conflicting Accounts

No alternative mother is given in the primary sources. Some modern adaptations omit Gríðr or replace her with other figures for narrative convenience, but this diverges from the medieval tradition.

Fenrir

Type hostileSource Poetic Edda (Völuspá, Vafþrúðnismál); Prose Edda (Gylfaginning).

Description

Fenrir, the monstrous wolf and child of Loki, is Víðarr’s primary adversary. At Ragnarök, Fenrir kills Odin by swallowing him, and Víðarr immediately attacks the wolf, placing his foot on its lower jaw and either tearing its jaws apart or stabbing it through the upper jaw to the heart. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) [[3]](https://loregraph.org/sources/prose-edda-vidar) [[2]](https://www.worldhistory.org/Vidar/) This relationship is purely antagonistic and eschatological: Víðarr exists, in narrative terms, largely to be the avenger who destroys Fenrir.

Version Note

All major sources agree that Víðarr kills Fenrir and survives.

Conflicting Accounts

Some modern interpretations conflate Fenrir with Garm, another wolf or hound mentioned in the Eddas, but the Prose Edda distinguishes their battles. [[2]](https://www.worldhistory.org/Vidar/) The identification of Garm with Fenrir is debated among scholars, but Víðarr’s role remains tied specifically to Fenrir in the primary texts.

Váli

Type familySource Poetic Edda (Vafþrúðnismál); Prose Edda (Gylfaginning).

Description

Váli is another son of Odin, often associated with vengeance (particularly the killing of Höðr after Baldr’s death). In Vafþrúðnismál and Gylfaginning, Víðarr and Váli are mentioned together as survivors of Ragnarök who dwell on the plains of Ida. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) [[3]](https://loregraph.org/sources/prose-edda-vidar) [[2]](https://www.worldhistory.org/Vidar/) This pairing suggests a conceptual link between the two avenging sons, though the texts do not narrate direct interactions between them.

Version Note

The association of Víðarr and Váli as co‑survivors is consistent across sources.

Conflicting Accounts

Some modern mythographers emphasize a dual‑avenger motif, treating Víðarr and Váli as complementary figures of vengeance and survival. This is interpretive and not explicitly stated in the medieval texts.

Thor, Móði, and Magni

Type allySource Prose Edda, Gylfaginning; Poetic Edda, Vafþrúðnismál.

Description

Thor, the strongest of the gods, is not directly linked to Víðarr in specific stories, but Gylfaginning notes that Víðarr is the strongest next after Thor. [[3]](https://loregraph.org/sources/prose-edda-vidar) [[2]](https://www.worldhistory.org/Vidar/) After Ragnarök, Thor’s sons Móði and Magni inherit Mjölnir and join Víðarr and Váli on the plains of Ida. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) [[3]](https://loregraph.org/sources/prose-edda-vidar) [[2]](https://www.worldhistory.org/Vidar/) This situates Víðarr within a small cohort of survivor gods who collectively represent continuity and renewed strength.

Version Note

The post‑Ragnarök grouping of Víðarr, Váli, Móði, and Magni is consistent in the Eddas.

Conflicting Accounts

Some modern reconstructions assign specific governance roles to each survivor (e.g., Thor’s sons as rulers of storms, Víðarr as guardian of forests), but these roles are not attested in the medieval texts.

Æsir as a collective

Type politicalSource Prose Edda, Ægir’s Journey to Asgard; Poetic Edda, Lokasenna.

Description

Víðarr is counted among the Æsir and appears in lists of gods who sit in judgment or attend feasts, indicating his participation in the political and judicial life of the divine assembly. [[3]](https://loregraph.org/sources/prose-edda-vidar) [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%AD%C3%B0arr) His silence and limited speech suggest that he is not a prominent orator, but his presence among the twelve judges underscores his status as a respected member of the pantheon.

Version Note

His inclusion among the judges is attested in Snorri’s prose.

Conflicting Accounts

No primary source excludes him from the Æsir. Some modern lists of gods omit Víðarr due to his relative obscurity, but this reflects contemporary selection rather than textual disagreement.

Sources & Standing
Well-Attested

Multiple independent primary sources agree on the core narratives. Primary attestation: Poetic Edda (Völuspá, Vafþrúðnismál) / Prose Edda (Gylfaginning).
