Lich

The Undying King, Master of the Phylactery, Death's Defier

TypeUndead (Spellcaster)
CultureDungeons & Dragons (Wizards of the Coast / Hasbro)
RealmAncient Tombs, Dark Towers, Hidden Sanctums
ScaleIndividual (extreme threat)
AlignmentAny Evil (typically Neutral Evil or Lawful Evil)

I have seen empires rise and crumble to dust. I have outlived gods. Death is for the weak — I chose otherwise.

Who It Is

The Lich represents the ultimate expression of arcane ambition corrupted — a spellcaster who has willingly embraced undeath to cheat mortality, binding their soul to a phylactery (a magically prepared container, often a small box or jewel) and transforming their living body into an eternally animated skeletal form. The process of becoming a Lich is the most evil act a mortal can commit: it requires the creation of a potion of transformation brewed from the blood of an innocent sacrificed by the caster's own hand, mixed with lethal poisons and unspeakable reagents. Upon drinking it, the caster dies in agony, and their soul transfers to the phylactery, while the corpse rises as a Lich.

Physically, a Lich appears as a desiccated, skeletal figure wreathed in the tattered remnants of its burial robes. Points of crimson light burn in its empty eye sockets — the only visible sign of the intelligence that still animates the creature. The Lich retains all the spellcasting ability it possessed in life, typically amplified by centuries of study and the removal of mortal limitations. Its touch drains life force, and its mere presence can paralyze the living with supernatural terror. Most critically, a Lich cannot be permanently destroyed unless its phylactery is found and destroyed first — if its body is destroyed, it reforms near the phylactery within days.

D&D's most famous Lich is Vecna — a mortal wizard who achieved godhood and whose Hand and Eye became two of the most infamous artifacts in the game. Another is Acererak, the architect of the Tomb of Horrors, a legendary dungeon designed to kill adventurers through sadistic traps rather than combat. The Lich has become an archetype far beyond D&D: it appears in World of Warcraft (the Lich King Arthas), the Harry Potter series (Voldemort's horcruxes are effectively phylacteries), the Adventure Time cartoon, and countless other fantasy works. The word itself comes from the Old English 'līc' (corpse), related to the modern German 'Leiche'. The concept of an undying sorcerer-king who has traded humanity for eternal power resonates as a universal cautionary tale about the price of immortality.
