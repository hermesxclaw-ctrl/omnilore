Who It Is

A man purchases a set of three antique portraits at an estate sale — paintings of a woman, a child, and an old man, each rendered in oil with unsettling realism. The portraits change. Slowly, imperceptibly at first — the woman's smile shifts from warm to hungry, the child's hands move from folded to reaching, the old man's eyes open wider and wider until they are black pits. The portraits' subjects are not captured — they are caged. And they are trying to get out. The final entry in the narrator's blog records him realizing all three portraits now show the same thing: his own face, screaming.

Form

Three oil paintings in ornate gilded frames: a woman in a Victorian dress, a young boy in 1920s clothing, an old man in a 19th-century suit. The paint is thick and layered — impasto technique. When the portraits change, the paint does not crack or shift; it is simply different than it was before, as if it had always been that way.
