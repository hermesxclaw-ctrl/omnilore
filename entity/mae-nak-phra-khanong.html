Mae Nak Phra Khanong

Nang Nak, The Lady of Phra Khanong, The Most Famous Ghost of Thailand

Ong nak phra khanong — the ghost who loved so fiercely that even death could not part her from her husband.

Who It Is

Mae Nak (Nang Nak) of Phra Khanong is the most famous ghost in Thai folklore. The story dates to the reign of King Mongkut (Rama IV, mid-19th century). Nak was a pregnant woman who died in childbirth while her husband Mak was away at war. When Mak returned, Nak's spirit remained in their home, continuing the marriage as if nothing had happened. Neighbors and visitors who tried to tell Mak the truth met violent ends at Nak's hands. The truth emerged when Mak noticed that Nak's lime squeezing reached impossibly far or that her sleeping body stretched to inhuman lengths. A famous exorcist named Chao Phraya Bodindecha (or a powerful monk) finally subdued Nak, trapping her spirit. However, Nak's devotion to Mak was so powerful that she became a venerated figure. Today, the Mae Nak Shrine in Suan Luang, Bangkok is one of the most popular spirit shrines in Thailand — women pray to her for fertility, safe childbirth, and faithful husbands. The story has been adapted into countless Thai films (over 30), television dramas, novels, and even an opera. Mae Nak transcends the category of 'ghost' to become a national cultural icon representing undying love and the thin boundary between the living and the dead.

The Approach

On the banks of the Phra Khanong Canal, a young wife waits for her soldier husband to return from war. She holds their newborn child, her smile as warm as the Bangkok sun. But Mae Nak is dead—and her love is so fierce that it refuses to let go of the living. For over a century, her story has haunted Thailand, inspiring terror, devotion, and a shrine where devotees still pray for safe childbirth and faithful husbands. Is she a malevolent ghost, a tragic heroine, or a mother's love twisted by grief? The answer changes with every telling, and the truth lies somewhere between legend and history.

Content advisory. The story involves death in childbirth, grief, haunting, terrorization of a community, and violence (though not graphic). Some versions include themes of jealousy, possession, and supernatural revenge. The narrative contains cultural and religious elements (Buddhist cosmology, ghost beliefs) that may be unfamiliar to some readers. The shrine practices involve offerings and rituals that are part of living religious tradition; readers should approach with respect.

Etymology

Mae Nak (แม่นาก) means 'Lady Nak' or 'Mother Nak' (Mae = mother/lady, Nak is a Thai female name). Phra Khanong (พระโขนง) is the district and canal in Bangkok where the story is set. The full name translates to 'Lady Nak of Phra Khanong'. Nang Nak (นางนาก) means 'Miss Nak' or 'Mrs. Nak' (Nang = lady/miss). Amdaeng Nak (อำแดงนาก) is an archaic title for a married woman, used in historical accounts.

Cultural Ownership

Thai (central Thailand, specifically Bangkok and the Phra Khanong area). The story is deeply embedded in Thai folklore, Buddhist culture, and modern Thai popular culture. It is not owned by any single group but is a national cultural property.

Disputed Identifications

There is a historical claim that the ghost story is based on a real woman named Amdaeng Nak, but some scholars and folklorists question this. The name of her husband is disputed: the legend says 'Mak', but K.S.R. Kulap's 1899 article claims it was 'Chum'. Some versions conflate Mae Nak with other Thai female ghosts (e.g., Phi Krahang, Phi Krasue) but they are distinct entities.

Description

Appearance

Mae Nak is typically described as a beautiful young Thai woman with long black hair, wearing traditional Thai clothing (such as a pha sin and sabai). In her ghostly form, she is often depicted in a white or pale outfit, sometimes with a pale complexion. In some portrayals, she appears with her infant child. The statue at the shrine shows her holding a baby.

Symbols & Iconography

Key symbols include the lime (which she drops and retrieves with her stretching arm), the Phra Khanong Canal, the earthen jar (in which she was confined), the Blumea balsamifera bush (nat plant) that repels ghosts, and the Wat Mahabut temple (holy ground). In modern iconography, she is often shown as a beautiful woman in white, sometimes with a baby.

Behaviour & Personality

In the legend, Mae Nak is devoted and loving to her husband, but after her death, she becomes possessive and vengeful. She terrorizes the community when she feels threatened. She is also shown as a mother, caring for her child. Her behavior is driven by her undying love and her inability to accept her death. She is not inherently evil but becomes dangerous due to her circumstances.

Domains & Habitats

The story is set in the Phra Khanong area of Bangkok, along the Phra Khanong Canal. She haunts the house she shared with Mak and the surrounding area. After being confined, her spirit is associated with the canal and the shrine at Wat Mahabut. In some versions, she is bound to a relic (her forehead bone) in the possession of the royal family.

Version Differences

There are many variations: the cause of Mak's conscription (specific wars or not), the details of Nak's death (some say she died in childbirth, others say both mother and child died), the method of her capture (earthen jar vs. forehead bone), and the resolution (some say she was trapped forever, others say she was freed or voluntarily departed). Some versions are more comedic, others more tragic. The husband's name may be Mak or Chum.

Stories & Myths

The Common Legend of Mae Nak

Source Oral tradition, documented in various media and the provided sourceCulture ThaiPeriod Reign of King Rama IV (1851-1868)

Summary

A beautiful woman named Mae Nak lives with her husband Mak on the banks of the Phra Khanong Canal. When Nak is pregnant, Mak is conscripted into the army and sent to war, where he is seriously wounded. While he is recovering in Bangkok, Nak and their child die during childbirth. When Mak returns home, he finds his wife and child alive and well. Neighbors warn him that Nak is a ghost, but he doesn't believe them. One day, Nak drops a lime and stretches her arm impossibly to retrieve it, revealing her true nature. Mak flees, hiding behind a Blumea bush and then entering Wat Mahabut temple, which ghosts cannot enter. In grief and anger, Nak terrorizes the village. A powerful monk captures her spirit, confining it in an earthen jar, which is thrown into the canal. In some versions, she is later freed when the jar is opened. In other versions, a monk named Somdet To binds her spirit to her forehead bone, which becomes a relic.

Consequences

Nak is confined, but her story becomes famous. She becomes a subject of devotion at the shrine, and her story is retold in countless adaptations.

Variants

The cause of Mak's conscription, the details of Nak's death, the method of capture, and the resolution all vary. Some versions have Nak voluntarily departing after a monk promises she will be reunited with Mak in a future life.

Historical Account by K.S.R. Kulap

Source The Siam Prabheth newspaper, 1899Culture ThaiPeriod 1899 (article date)

Summary

The journalist K.S.R. Kulap claimed that the ghost story was based on a real woman named Amdaeng Nak, daughter of Khun Si, a leader of Phra Khanong. Amdaeng Nak died while pregnant. Her son, fearing his father would remarry and divide the inheritance, invented the ghost story. He dressed in women's clothing and threw rocks at passing boats to make people believe it was Nak's ghost. Kulap also suggested that Nak's husband was named Chum, not Mak.

Death in childbirth

Source Common legend

Description

Nak dies while giving birth, a common tragedy in the past. This is a central element of the story.

Scholarly Context

High maternal mortality rates in 19th century Siam made this a relatable tragedy.

Terrorization of community

Source Common legend

Description

After Mak flees, Nak terrorizes the people of Phra Khanong, causing fear and possibly harm.

Scholarly Context

This reflects the fear of restless spirits in Thai culture.

Exploitation of a grieving mother

Source K.S.R. Kulap's article

Description

In the historical account, the son allegedly exploits his mother's memory to secure his inheritance, dressing in women's clothing to fake hauntings.

Scholarly Context

This is a rationalist explanation, but it also highlights family conflict and economic motives.

Confinement of a spirit

Source Variant legends

Description

Nak is violently captured and confined by a monk, which can be seen as an act of violence against a grieving spirit.

Scholarly Context

This reflects Buddhist practices of dealing with malevolent spirits.
