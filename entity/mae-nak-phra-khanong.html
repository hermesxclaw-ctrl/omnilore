Who It Is

On the banks of the Phra Khanong Canal, a young wife waits for her soldier husband to return from war. She holds their newborn child, her smile as warm as the Bangkok sun. But Mae Nak is dead—and her love is so fierce that it refuses to let go of the living. For over a century, her story has haunted Thailand, inspiring terror, devotion, and a shrine where devotees still pray for safe childbirth and faithful husbands. Is she a malevolent ghost, a tragic heroine, or a mother's love twisted by grief? The answer changes with every telling, and the truth lies somewhere between legend and history.

In the legend, Mae Nak is devoted and loving to her husband, but after her death, she becomes possessive and vengeful. She terrorizes the community when she feels threatened. She is also shown as a mother, caring for her child. Her behavior is driven by her undying love and her inability to accept her death. She is not inherently evil but becomes dangerous due to her circumstances.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Mae Nak (แม่นาก) means 'Lady Nak' or 'Mother Nak' (Mae = mother/lady, Nak is a Thai female name). Phra Khanong (พระโขนง) is the district and canal in Bangkok where the story is set. The full name translates to 'Lady Nak of Phra Khanong'. Nang Nak (นางนาก) means 'Miss Nak' or 'Mrs. Nak' (Nang = lady/

Form & Signs

Appearance — Mae Nak is typically described as a beautiful young Thai woman with long black hair, wearing traditional Thai clothing (such as a pha sin and sabai). In her ghostly form, she is often depicted in a white or pale outfit, sometimes with a pale complexion. In some portrayals, she appears with her infant child. The statue at the shrine shows her holding a baby.

Symbols — Key symbols include the lime (which she drops and retrieves with her stretching arm), the Phra Khanong Canal, the earthen jar (in which she was confined), the Blumea balsamifera bush (nat plant) that repels ghosts, and the Wat Mahabut temple (holy ground). In modern iconography, she is often shown as a beautiful woman in white, sometimes with a baby.

Domains — The story is set in the Phra Khanong area of Bangkok, along the Phra Khanong Canal. She haunts the house she shared with Mak and the surrounding area. After being confined, her spirit is associated with the canal and the shrine at Wat Mahabut. In some versions, she is bound to a relic (her forehead bone) in the possession of the royal family.

Accounts I
The Common Legend of Mae NakOral tradition, documented in various media and the provided source

Reign of King Rama IV (1851-1868)

A beautiful woman named Mae Nak lives with her husband Mak on the banks of the Phra Khanong Canal. When Nak is pregnant, Mak is conscripted into the army and sent to war, where he is seriously wounded. While he is recovering in Bangkok, Nak and their child die during childbirth. When Mak returns home, he finds his wife and child alive and well. Neighbors warn him that Nak is a ghost, but he doesn't believe them. One day, Nak drops a lime and stretches her arm impossibly to retrieve it, revealing her true nature. Mak flees, hiding behind a Blumea bush and then entering Wat Mahabut temple, which ghosts cannot enter. In grief and anger, Nak terrorizes the village. A powerful monk captures her spirit, confining it in an earthen jar, which is thrown into the canal. In some versions, she is later freed when the jar is opened. In other versions, a monk named Somdet To binds her spirit to her forehead bone, which becomes a relic. Nak is confined, but her story becomes famous. She becomes a subject of devotion at the shrine, and her story is retold in countless adaptations.

Accounts II
Historical Account by K.S.R. KulapThe Siam Prabheth newspaper, 1899

1899 (article date)

The journalist K.S.R. Kulap claimed that the ghost story was based on a real woman named Amdaeng Nak, daughter of Khun Si, a leader of Phra Khanong. Amdaeng Nak died while pregnant. Her son, fearing his father would remarry and divide the inheritance, invented the ghost story. He dressed in women's clothing and threw rocks at passing boats to make people believe it was Nak's ghost. Kulap also suggested that Nak's husband was named Chum, not Mak. This account provides a rational explanation for the legend, but it is not widely accepted as definitive.
