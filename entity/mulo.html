Who It Is

The Muló (meaning 'dead one' or 'spirit' in Romani; also Mullo, plural Mulé) is the Romani vampire — the restless dead who returns to harass the living, particularly their own family. In Romani belief, the dead who were not properly honoured, those who died violently, or those who died unmarried may become a Muló. Unlike the blood-drinking vampire of Slavic folklore, the Muló is often described as returning to have sexual relations with their widow or widow-to-be, consuming food left out, and spreading disease. Some traditions hold that the Muló wears a single nostril, iron teeth, and has an insatiable appetite. The Muló may also be contagious — a person who dies from the Muló's attentions may themselves become a Muló. Prevention and disposal rituals are elaborate: driving a hawthorn or iron stake through the heart, placing thorns and millet seeds in the grave (the Muló must count each seed, delaying its emergence), decapitation, and burning the corpse. Some Romani groups practice the ritual of the 'pomana' — a feast honouring the dead — to prevent Mule formation. The Muló is significant because Romani folklore is one of the key sources (alongside Slavic and Romanian traditions) that fed the 18th-century vampire panic that swept across Europe — the word 'vampire' itself may have passed through Romani mediation. However, unlike the sensationalized literary vampire, the Romani Muló is intimately domestic: it haunts family, not strangers, and its crimes are often erotic (returning to a spouse) rather than purely predatory. The Muló reflects Romani mortuary customs and the deep importance of proper death rites in a culture that historically lacked permanent cemeteries.

Form

The Muló is not described as a pale, aristocratic vampire. It is a spirit of the dead, often returning in a form that is recognizable to the living, but with a difference. It may appear as a shadowy figure, a translucent apparition, or even in a physical form that is cold and lifeless. Some accounts describe it as having a gaunt, pale face, while others say it looks exactly as it did in life, but with a hollow or distant look in its eyes. It may be dressed in its burial clothes. The key is its wrongness—it is a person who is not quite a person anymore.

Powers

Spiritual Manifestation The Muló can appear as a ghost or apparition, visible to those it wishes to haunt. It can appear in dreams to deliver messages or warnings. Pan-Romani It is often bound to a specific location, such as its grave or the place where it died. Some accounts say it can appear anywhere, while others say it is limited to its home or graveyard.

Physical Harm In some accounts, the Muló can physically attack the living, causing injury, illness, or even death. It may drain a person's life force, leaving them weak and sickly. Balkan and Transylvanian Romani This ability is often associated with a specific grievance. It may not be able to harm those who are protected by ritual or who have not wronged it. The degree of physicality varies. Some accounts describe it as a purely spiritual force that causes harm indirectly through bad luck or madness, not direct physical assault.

Causing Misfortune The Muló can bring bad luck, crop failure, illness to livestock, or general misfortune to a family or community that has wronged it or failed to honor it. Pan-Romani This is often a form of punishment or a call for attention to an unfulfilled duty. This is sometimes seen as random malevolence, rather than a targeted punishment.

Shapeshifting (Limited) In some regional tales, the Muló is said to be able to transform into an animal, most commonly a horse or a dog, to move among the living undetected. Some Balkan and Central European traditions This is not a universal trait and is more common in areas with heavy Slavic influence. This ability is more often attributed to the 'strigoi' or 'vampir' in neighboring cultures, and its attribution to the Muló may be a result of cultural blending.

Behavior & Habitat

The Muló's behavior is driven by its state of unrest. It returns to the world of the living for specific reasons: to avenge a wrong, to fulfill an unfulfilled promise or obligation, or because it was not given a proper burial. It may haunt its former family, appearing to them in dreams or in person. It can be vengeful, bringing misfortune, illness, or even death to those who wronged it. However, it is not always purely evil; it is a tragic and restless figure, trapped between worlds. Its actions are a direct consequence of its unresolved life.

Accounts

A young Romani woman died of a broken heart after her betrothed abandoned her for a wealthier match. She was buried in a white wedding dress. Soon after, her former fiancé began seeing her in his dreams, her face pale and accusing. His new wife fell ill, and his livestock died. He consulted an elder, who realized the woman's spirit was a Mulo, restless for justice. The elder advised him to go to her grave, confess his wrong, and ask for forgiveness. He did so, leaving a gift of a red ribbon. The misfortune ceased, and the woman's spirit was seen no more.

A dying man promised his brother he would return to help him with the harvest. After the man's death, the brother was haunted by his spirit. The Mulo appeared each night, asking when the harvest would begin. The brother, terrified, went to the village elder. The elder said the spirit must be released from its promise. A ritual was performed where the brother stood at the grave and declared the promise fulfilled, thanking the spirit for its help and telling it to rest. The haunting stopped.

Field Notes

† The Romani word 'mulo' is related to the Sanskrit word 'mṛta', showing the ancient Indian roots of the Romani language and culture.

† In some traditions, the best way to stop a Muló is not with a stake, but with a formal, verbal release from a promise, showing the importance of social obligation.

† The belief in the Muló is a strong argument for the 'ancestor spirit' theory of vampire origins, suggesting that not all such entities began as blood-drinking demons.

† The Romani concept of 'marime' (ritual pollution) is key to understanding the Muló—it is a being that has violated the sacred boundary between the living and the dead.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of the Muló not as a vampire, but as a haunting made of unfinished business. It is the ghost of a debt, a promise, or a crime that demands to be settled. It is less like Count Dracula and more like a spectral lawyer for the dead, returning to file a claim against the living. Its power is not in its fangs, but in the weight of a broken word.
