Who It Is

On the limestone plateau of the Lingones, where the road from Langres to Dijon crosses the wind-scoured plain, the god did not live in the city. He lived at its edges — at Mâlain, at Aignay, at the Jura frontier — where altars to Mars Cicolluis and his earth-goddess Litavis marked the line between 'ours' and 'theirs.' His name meant Big-Muscle or Great-Breast, a body made of land itself, and only the Lingones ever spoke it. When Lingon soldiers shivered on the Rhine at Xanten, far from home, they carved those same two names again, carrying their soil with them as a pair of gods who were also a map.

No myth, hymn or narrative describes Cicolluis's personality, motives or behaviour. Functional evidence suggests a tutelary, civic protector role: he is invoked with Litavis for the collective welfare of the Lingones, and with the domus divina / Augusto sacrum for loyalty to Rome. Lajoye proposes a chthonic-primordial character based on the etymology ('muscular/earthy') and the Litavis pairing (earth as dark, fertile, death-linked), contrasting this pair with celestial healing gods like Grannus and Sirona. Powers
Tutelary protection of the Lingones

As the exclusive god of the Lingones, Cicolluis embodies and protects the civitas as a collective. Invocation is communal (dedications by the Lingones as a people or by Lingon soldiers acting as a group), not individual healing or personal favour. His protection is territorial and political.

Limit: Limited to the Lingones; no evidence of worship by neighbouring peoples (Sequani, Aedui, Remi) or adoption outside Lingon identity. No attested miracle, epiphany or intervention narrative.

Martial power via interpretatio romana with Mars

Through identification with Mars, Cicolluis assumes Roman martial domains: war, defence of the community, possibly oaths and military discipline. The extra-mural location of his sanctuary mirrors Roman Mars outside the pomerium, reinforcing a civic-military aspect.

Limit: No weapon, battle feat or martial myth is narrated; power is titular and cultic, not storied. No evidence of independent warlike iconography distinct from Mars.

Territorial and chthonic fertility through pairing with Litavis

Paired with Litavis, whose name derives from PIE *pleth2- 'broad' (earth), Cicolluis participates in a divine couple governing land, breadth, fertility and perhaps the underworld/ancestral earth. Lajoye reads them as chthonic-primordial pair (earth in dark aspect + muscular primordial being) responsible for agricultural abundance and ties to death.

Limit: Reconstruction; no agricultural ritual text, harvest offering or fertility myth survives. The chthonic reading depends on etymology and Irish comparison, not direct Gallo-Roman evidence.

Legitimation and imperial loyalty

Two dedications link Cicolluis/Litavis to the imperial cult (domus divina, Augusto sacrum). The god thus mediates loyalty between the Lingon community and Rome, sacralizing the Augustan civitas organization.

Limit: Not a supernatural power but a political-religious function; no evidence of emperor-worship independent of the pair.

Onomastic power / name as identity marker

The theonym itself functions as an ethnic marker: bearing the name Cicolluis signals Lingon identity. The same form as a personal name at Vindonissa shows its use as anthroponym, possibly conferring protection or lineage claim.

Limit: If Vindonissa is anthroponym not theonym, the power is human naming practice, not divine. No evidence of name-taboo or invocation formula.

Accounts I
The Creation of the Lingon Civitas and the Choice of Mars Cicolluis (Augustan Organization)Marie-Thérèse Raepsaet-Charlier, historical synthesis of epigraphy and Augustan administration; no single ancient narrative text survives

c. 16-12 BCE (reorganization of Tres Galliae under Augustus) to 1st century CE (first dedications)

When Augustus organized the Lingones as a civitas with capital at Andematunnum (Langres), the local elite selected an official interpretatio romana for their tutelary god: Mars Cicolluis. Raepsaet-Charlier argues this was not a slow blending but a deliberate act of translation, creating a Lingon god who was neither purely Gaulish nor purely Roman. The choice of Mars, placed extra-murally like the Campus Martius, and the pairing with Litavis, anchored the new polity's identity in religion. The absence of a Mars temple inside Langres and the prominence of boundary sanctuaries reflect this founding logic. No ancient author narrates the event; it is reconstructed from epigraphic distribution and Roman administrative history. Established a durable civic cult that defined Lingon territory for two centuries; provided a model parallel to Camulus of the Remi and Caturix of the Helvetii.

The Sanctuary of Mediolanum (Mâlain) — Heart of the CultArchaeological and epigraphic corpus from Mâlain (Côte-d'Or), CIL XIII 5606-5614 group; excavations of the fanum and courtyard sanctuary

1st-3rd century CE (peak 2nd century)

At Mediolanum, modern Mâlain, a Gallo-Roman sanctuary yielded the majority of Cicolluis dedications — at least six altars, most reading Marti Cicollui et Litavi. Pilgrims left votive altars fulfilling vows (votum solvit libens merito). One altar uniquely pairs Mars Cicolluis with Bellona instead of Litavis, revealing the interpretative flexibility of the cult. The sanctuary's location near the southern edge of Lingon territory, not at the capital, suggests it was a territorial sanctuary marking the civitas boundary rather than an urban temple. Finds include altars, architectural fragments and votive deposits, but no cult statue securely identified as Cicolluis. Mâlain becomes the diagnostic site for the cult; its assemblage allows definition of the god's exclusive Lingon character and his consort pairing.

The Dijon Dedication and the Imperial HouseVotive inscription from Divio (Dijon), CIL XIII 5598-5599: Marti Cicollui et Litavi et domui divinae

late 1st to 3rd century CE

A dedicant at Dijon, the northern vicus of the Lingones that would later eclipse Langres, honoured Mars Cicolluis and Litavis together with the domus divina — the divine imperial house. The text places the Lingon divine couple alongside the living emperor's family, merging civic religion with imperial loyalty. The act is a public vow, likely by a local notable, demonstrating how the tutelary cult was not oppositional to Rome but integrated into the provincial imperial cult. The stone was reused in later Dijon structures and recovered in urban archaeology. Shows the cult's official, civic status and its compatibility with Roman state religion; parallels Augusto sacrum at Aignay-le-Duc.

Accounts II
The Frontier Altars — Aignay-le-Duc and Mutigney Mark the LandInscriptions from Aignay-le-Duc (Côte-d'Or) and Mutigney (Jura), plus fragment from Ruffey-lès-Echirey

1st-2nd century CE

Two altars at the edges of Lingon territory explicitly locate the cult at boundaries. At Aignay-le-Duc, on the northwestern margin, the altar is consecrated Augusto sacrum to Mars Cicolluis and Litavis, again linking territory, god and emperor. At Mutigney, on the Jura border with the Sequani, Lingones dedicated to the same pair at the very limit of their land. A fragmentary altar from Ruffey-lès-Echirey, also in Côte-d'Or, adds a further find-spot. Raepsaet-Charlier reads this distribution as deliberate: the Lingones defined their civitas through religious sites at its periphery, and Cicolluis and Litavis were the gods who embodied that land. Cartographic proof of the god's territorial function; supports interpretation of Litavis as 'Broad Earth' and Cicolluis as her guardian.

Lingones on the Rhine — The Xanten Altar AbroadVotive altar from Xanten (Castra Vetera / Colonia Ulpia Traiana, Germania Inferior), CIL XIII 8606-8610 group, set up by Lingones

late 1st to 2nd century CE

Far from their homeland, Lingones serving on the Rhine frontier — likely as auxiliaries or vexillations — set up an altar to Mars Cicolluis and Litavis at Xanten. The text explicitly names the dedicants as Lingones, carrying their tutelary couple with them as an identity marker in a military, multi-ethnic environment. This is one of only two dedications outside Lingon territory (the other at Mutigney) and the only one far outside Gaul. It proves the cult was portable as an ethnic emblem, not confined to the soil of the plateau, and that soldiers maintained civic religious ties while serving Rome. Demonstrates diaspora cult and ethnic self-identification; confirms exclusivity (only Lingones invoke this pair, even abroad).

The Bellona Substitution — One Goddess, Two NamesMâlain altar: Marti Cicollui et Bellonae (CIL XIII 5613 group); discussed by Venceslas Kruta, Andreas Hofeneder, Patrice Lajoye

2nd century CE

Among the otherwise uniform Mâlain dedications to Mars Cicolluis and Litavis, one altar replaces Litavis with the Roman war-goddess Bellona. Kruta noted Bellona as companion of Mars Cicolluis in Côte-d'Or; Hofeneder argues this is not a different goddess but the same figure under a Roman name — Litavis interpreted as Bellona, just as Cicolluis is interpreted as Mars. The substitution is unique in the corpus and therefore precious: it shows the interpretative process in action, with the native earth-goddess rendered as a Roman war-goddess when paired with Mars, perhaps emphasizing her martial aspect. Hofeneder cautions, however, that other Mars-Bellona dedications in Celtic lands without the Cicolluis epithet cannot be assumed to be the same pair. Key evidence for interpretatio romana as a bilingual naming strategy; clarifies that Litavis's warlike guise is contextual, not essential.

Cicolluis was a pan-Gaulish war god worshipped across Gaul like Toutatis or Cernunnos. — All secure dedications are exclusively Lingon — within Lingon territory or by Lingones abroad. No dedication by non-Lingones, and no attestation outside the Lingon sphere except the two Lingon-made altars at Xanten and Mutigney. He is a tutelary civitas god, not a pan-Gaulish deity.

Cicolluis had a famous statue or known appearance (helmeted warrior, muscular giant) and myths about his battles. — No statue, relief, or iconographic type is securely identified as Cicolluis, and no myth, story or literary description of his appearance or deeds survives. Any image or narrative is modern invention. What we know is epigraphic and topographic, not mythic.

Cicolluis and Litavis are proven to be the same as the Irish Fomorian Cichol and an earth-goddess, showing a pan-Celtic primordial couple. — The names Cicolluis and Cichol are linguistically identical ('muscular one'), but Patrice Lajoye himself stresses their mythological identity is 'far from certain' with only weak links. Raepsaet-Charlier rejects the continuity assumption between Gallo-Roman Gaul and medieval Ireland. The comparison is a modern scholarly hypothesis, not an attested equivalence.
