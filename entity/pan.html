Who It Is

Forget the mischievous boy in green tights. The real Pan is a horned, hoofed, and utterly untamed force of nature — the god who invented panic, outplayed Apollo in a music contest, and once announced his own death from a ship off the coast of Greece. He is the wild at the edge of every field, the sudden fear in the forest, and the haunting melody of a shepherd's pipes on a lonely hill.

Pan is a god of the wild, and his personality reflects this. He is lusty, energetic, and loves music, dancing, and the chase. He is known for his unbridled sexual desire, pursuing nymphs and mortals alike. He is also a source of 'panic' — a sudden, irrational fear that can seize travelers in the wilderness. He is a companion of Dionysus and his retinue of satyrs and maenads. He is playful but can be dangerous if crossed, and his anger is swift.

Etymology — The name Pan (Greek: Πάν) is derived from the Greek word 'paein' meaning 'to pasture' or 'to graze', connecting him directly to shepherds and their flocks. Powers
Panic Inducement

Pan can project a sudden, overwhelming, and irrational fear into the minds of individuals or entire armies. This 'panic' is not a physical attack but a psychological one, causing confusion, rout, and even madness.

Limit: The effect seems to be a short-term, if potent, psychological attack. It doesn't cause physical harm but can lead to it as those affected flee in disorder.

Music (Syrinx)

Pan is a master of the syrinx or panpipes. His music is so enchanting that it can charm animals, humans, and even other gods. It can bring joy, sorrow, or a sense of wild abandon.

Limit: His music, while powerful, was not as skilled as Apollo's lyre, as shown in the musical contest where Apollo won.

Prophecy

Pan is a prophetic god, with his oracles located in caves, such as the one on Mount Lycaeus in Arcadia.

Limit: His oracles are not as prominent or well-documented as those of Apollo at Delphi.

Shapeshifting

Like many gods, Pan is capable of changing his form, as seen when he transformed himself into a goat to escape Typhon.

Limit: Not a frequently used power in surviving myths.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Pan is depicted as a half-man, half-goat. He has the upper body of a man, but with a goat's horns on his head, a beard, and the hind legs, hooves, and a tail of a goat. He is often shown naked or with a simple animal skin. He is typically portrayed in a wild, rustic setting, often with a syrinx (panpipes) or a shepherd's crook.

Symbols — Pan's primary symbols are the syrinx (panpipes), the shepherd's crook, and the pine tree. He is often associated with goats, which are his sacred animals. He is also linked to caves, which were his primary sanctuaries.

Domains — Pan's primary domain is the untamed wilderness of Arcadia — the mountains, groves, caves, and pastures. He is the god of shepherds, hunters, and all who live close to nature. He is a protector of flocks but also a force of the wild that is indifferent to human civilization.

Accounts I
The Birth of PanHomeric Hymn to Pan

Archaic

Pan is born to Hermes and a nymph (often Dryope). He is born a complete goat-man and his mother is horrified by his appearance, but Hermes is delighted and wraps him in a hare's skin, taking him to Olympus. The gods, especially Dionysus, are amused and delighted by him. He is named Pan, meaning 'all', because he delighted all the gods. He becomes the companion of Dionysus and the rustic gods.

Pan and SyrinxOvid's Metamorphoses

Classical

Pan pursues the chaste nymph Syrinx through the wilderness. As he is about to catch her, she prays to the river god (or the earth) to be saved, and she is transformed into a cluster of marsh reeds. Pan, disappointed but inspired, cuts the reeds and fashions them into his panpipes. The syrinx becomes Pan's most iconic instrument and a symbol of his unfulfilled desire.

Pan and Pheidippides (The Battle of Marathon)Herodotus's Histories

Classical

The Athenian messenger Pheidippides is running to Sparta to request aid against the Persian invasion. On Mount Parthenion, he encounters Pan, who calls him by name and asks why the Athenians do not honor him. Pan promises to help them. The Athenians win the Battle of Marathon, and they later establish a sanctuary to Pan under the Acropolis in gratitude. Pan's worship is formally introduced to Athens. His ability to cause 'panic' is credited with the Persian defeat.

Accounts II
The Contest with ApolloVarious, including Ovid's Metamorphoses

Classical

Pan challenges Apollo to a musical contest on Mount Tmolus. Pan plays his rustic pipes, and Apollo plays his lyre. The judge, King Midas (or the mountain god Tmolus), declares Apollo the winner. When Midas disagrees and says Pan's music was better, Apollo gives him donkey's ears. The myth establishes the superiority of formal, civilized music (Apollo) over rustic, wild music (Pan). It also explains Midas's donkey ears.

Pan and TyphonVarious, including Nonnus's Dionysiaca

Late Antiquity

When the monstrous giant Typhon attacked the gods, they fled to Egypt and transformed into animals to hide. Pan transformed into a goat. This story is a late myth explaining theriomorphic (animal) depictions of gods in Egypt. Explains Pan's goat form and his connection to the Egyptian god Banebdjedet (a ram-headed god).

The Death of PanPlutarch, 'The Obsolescence of Oracles'

Roman

During the reign of Tiberius, a ship's pilot named Thamus is sailing near the island of Paxos. He hears a divine voice telling him to announce 'Great Pan is dead!' When he does, a great cry of lamentation is heard. This story is often interpreted as a metaphor for the end of the pagan world and the rise of Christianity. The phrase 'The Great God Pan is dead' became a famous literary trope, representing the death of the old world and the triumph of a new order.

Pan is a satyr. — Pan is a singular god, while satyrs are a class of rustic spirits. While they share similar appearances, Pan is a distinct, powerful deity, not a generic creature.

Pan is a demon or the devil. — In ancient Greek religion, Pan was a benign, if wild, nature god. His demonization is a Christian-era development, based on his horns, hooves, and wild nature.

The 'Pan' in 'Pan's Labyrinth' is the Greek god Pan. — The character in the film is actually a faun, a different type of creature. The title is a reference to the god, but the character is not him.

Pan is a minor god. — While not one of the Twelve Olympians, Pan was a major deity in Arcadia and had a significant cult throughout Greece. He was a powerful, widely-worshipped god.
