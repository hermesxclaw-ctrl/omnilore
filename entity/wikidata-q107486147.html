The Approach

Along the sun-baked Malabar Coast, where the Poorappuzha River meets the Arabian Sea, lies the ancient port of Tanur—a place whose sands have known the footsteps of Roman merchants, Portuguese adventurers, and the great Zamorin's fleets. But beneath its modern guise as a bustling fishing town, Tanur guards a deeper secret: it may be the legendary Tyndis, a gateway to the fabled spice routes of antiquity. Yet the name 'Thanur' also echoes a divine title, 'the Lord,' a name that has graced gods and kings across cultures. Who is the true Thanur? The answer, it seems, is as layered as the tides.

Identity

Tanur

Pronunciation TAH-noor (Malayalam: താനൂർ, Tānūr). English: TAN-ur.

Known As

TanoreThanurThanni-urTyndis (possibly)Vettathunadu (kingdom)

Etymology

The name 'Tanur' is derived from Malayalam: 'Thanni' refers to the Bastard myrobalan tree (Terminalia bellirica), and 'ur' means settlement or village. Thus, Tanur means 'settlement of the Thanni tree.' The name 'Tyndis' is of Greek origin, possibly derived from the local name 'Thondi' or 'Tundi.'

Cultural Ownership

The historical town of Tanur is part of the cultural heritage of Kerala, India, specifically the Malabar Coast. It is associated with the medieval Kingdom of Tanur (Vettathunadu) and has connections to Hindu, Muslim, Christian, and Jewish communities due to its maritime trade history.

Disputed Identifications

The identification of Tanur with the ancient port of Tyndis is a matter of scholarly debate. While some historians and archaeologists support this identification based on geographical and textual clues, others argue that Tyndis may be located elsewhere, such as at Kadalundi or Ponnani. The exact location of Tyndis remains uncertain.

Description

Appearance

Tanur is a coastal town with a long shoreline along the Arabian Sea. It lies south of the Poorappuzha River estuary. The landscape includes sandy beaches, coconut groves, and laterite hills. The town has a mix of traditional Kerala architecture and modern buildings. As a fishing center, the harbor area is bustling with boats and fish markets.

Symbols & Iconography

The Thanni tree (Terminalia bellirica) is the namesake and symbolic plant. The town's historical significance as a port is represented by maritime symbols. The Vettathu Raja's emblem likely included traditional Kerala royal symbols, but specific details are not documented.

Behaviour & Personality

As a historical port town, Tanur has been characterized by trade, cultural exchange, and strategic importance. Its people have historically been involved in maritime activities, fishing, and agriculture. The town has a reputation for religious tolerance and cultural diversity, with Hindu temples, Muslim mosques, and Christian churches coexisting.

Domains & Habitats

Tanur is located in the Malabar Coast, a region known for its spice trade. The town's economy historically depended on trade in pepper, cardamom, and other spices. The surrounding region includes the Kadalundi River and the Bharathappuzha River, which facilitated inland trade.

Version Differences

The ancient port of Tyndis is described in Greek texts as a major trade center, while modern Tanur is a modest fishing town. The medieval Kingdom of Tanur was a significant political entity, but it declined after the British colonial period. There is also a divine epithet 'Thanur' used for deities like Shiva and Vishnu, which is distinct from the town.

Powers & Abilities

Maritime Trade Hub

Source Version Ancient and medieval periods

Description

Tanur was a major port on the Malabar Coast, facilitating trade between the Roman Empire, Middle East, and Asia. It was known for exporting spices, especially pepper, and importing goods like gold, silk, and ceramics.

Limitations

Trade declined after the Portuguese disruption and later British colonial policies.

Contradictions

Some sources claim Tanur was second only to Muziris, but others argue other ports like Naura were equally important.

Strategic Military Position

Source Version 16th century

Description

The Kingdom of Tanur controlled a strategic coastal area with rivers, making it a valuable ally or target for naval powers. It played a role in the Portuguese-Zamorin conflicts.

Limitations

The kingdom was a vassal state and depended on the Zamorin for protection.

Contradictions

Historical records show shifting allegiances, with the ruler sometimes siding with the Portuguese and sometimes with the Zamorin.

Cultural and Intellectual Center

Source Version 14th-16th centuries

Description

Tanur and the surrounding region (Vettathunadu) were home to the Kerala School of Astronomy and Mathematics, which made significant contributions to trigonometry and calculus. It was also the birthplace of Thunchaththu Ezhuthachan, the father of modern Malayalam literature.

Limitations

The intellectual flourishing declined after the Portuguese period and political instability.

Contradictions

Some scholars argue the Kerala School's achievements were independent of European influence, while others suggest possible transmission of ideas.

Stories & Myths

The Roman Trade Connection

Source Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, Pliny the ElderCulture Ancient Roman and IndianPeriod 1st century CE

Summary

Tyndis (possibly Tanur) was a thriving port on the Malabar Coast, trading with the Roman Empire. It was known for its pepper and other spices, which were highly valued in Rome. The trade was so lucrative that it was estimated to be worth 50 million sesterces annually.

Participants

Roman merchants

Chera dynasty

Local Indian traders

Consequences

The trade brought wealth and cultural exchange to the region, but also attracted pirates, as noted by Pliny.

Variants

The exact location of Tyndis is disputed, with some suggesting it was at Kadalundi or Ponnani.

The Portuguese Alliance and Betrayal

Source Portuguese chronicles, Malabar ManualCulture Portuguese and IndianPeriod 16th century

Summary

The ruler of Tanur (Vettathu Raja) initially allied with the Portuguese against the Zamorin of Calicut, leading to the construction of the Chaliyam Fort. However, the alliance was short-lived, and the Zamorin's forces, with the help of the Mappilas, defeated the Portuguese at the Battle of Chaliyam in 1571.

Participants

Vettathu Raja

Portuguese

Zamorin of Calicut

Kunjali Marakkars

Mappila merchants

Consequences

The Portuguese were expelled from the region, and Tanur came back under the Zamorin's influence. The kingdom's power declined.

Variants

Some accounts say the ruler of Tanur also sided with Cochin, and there are conflicting reports about the exact role of Tanur's forces in various battles.

The Legend of the Last Raja

Source Malabar Manual by William LoganCulture KeralaPeriod 18th century

Summary

The Kshatriya family of the Vettathu Rajas became extinct with the death of the last Raja on 24 May 1793. The circumstances of his death are not well-documented, but it marked the end of the kingdom's royal line.

Participants

Vettathu Raja

Consequences

The kingdom was absorbed into the British-administered Malabar District, and the region was later reorganized into taluks.

Variants

There are no known variants, but the exact cause of the Raja's death is unknown.

Relationships

Zamorin of Calicut

Type vassal and overlordSource Malabar Manual, Portuguese chronicles

Description

The Kingdom of Tanur was a vassal state under the Zamorin of Calicut. The Vettathu Raja owed allegiance to the Zamorin, who was the dominant power on the Malabar Coast.

Version Note

The relationship was often tense, with the Vettathu Raja sometimes siding with the Portuguese against the Zamorin.

Conflicting Accounts

Some sources suggest the Vettathu Raja was a loyal vassal, while others show him as a rebellious subordinate.

Portuguese

The Vettathu Raja allied with the Portuguese in the early 16th century, allowing them to build a fort at Chaliyam. Later, the Portuguese were defeated, and the alliance ended.

Version Note

The ruler of Tanur also had connections with Cochin, another Portuguese ally.

Conflicting Accounts

The exact nature of the alliance and the reasons for its breakdown are debated by historians.

Kunjali Marakkars

Type alliesSource Historical records

The naval chiefs of the Zamorin, the Kunjali Marakkars, had close relationships with Tanur, using it as a base for their operations against the Portuguese.

Version Note

The Marakkars were of Mappila descent and had strong ties to the local Muslim community.

Conflicting Accounts

Some accounts suggest the Marakkars were rivals of the Vettathu Raja, while others indicate cooperation.

Portuguese Colonial Violence

Source Portuguese chronicles, Malabar Manual

Description

The Portuguese arrival led to conflicts, massacres, and the destruction of local trade networks. The Battle of Chaliyam Fort in 1571 resulted in the defeat of the Portuguese, but the preceding period saw violent clashes between the Portuguese and the Zamorin's forces, with Tanur as a battleground.

Scholarly Context

Historians debate the extent of Portuguese brutality, but it is clear that the spice trade was contested through force.

Mysorean Invasion

Source Malabar Manual, colonial records

Description

In the late 18th century, the Kingdom of Mysore under Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan invaded Malabar, causing widespread devastation. Tanur became part of Mysore, and the local population suffered from forced conversions, destruction of temples, and heavy taxation.

Scholarly Context

The Mysorean invasion is a contentious topic in Kerala history, with some viewing it as a liberation from the oppressive caste system, while others emphasize the atrocities committed.

Slave Trade

Source Historical accounts, colonial records

Description

The Malabar Coast, including Tanur, was involved in the slave trade, particularly during the medieval and early modern periods. Slaves were exported to the Middle East and other parts of India.

Scholarly Context

The extent of the slave trade from Tanur is not well-documented, but it was part of the broader Indian Ocean slave trade.
