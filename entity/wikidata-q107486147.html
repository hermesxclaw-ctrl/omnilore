Who It Is

Along the sun-baked Malabar Coast, where the Poorappuzha River meets the Arabian Sea, lies the ancient port of Tanur—a place whose sands have known the footsteps of Roman merchants, Portuguese adventurers, and the great Zamorin's fleets. But beneath its modern guise as a bustling fishing town, Tanur guards a deeper secret: it may be the legendary Tyndis, a gateway to the fabled spice routes of antiquity. Yet the name 'Thanur' also echoes a divine title, 'the Lord,' a name that has graced gods and kings across cultures. Who is the true Thanur? The answer, it seems, is as layered as the tides.

As a historical port town, Tanur has been characterized by trade, cultural exchange, and strategic importance. Its people have historically been involved in maritime activities, fishing, and agriculture. The town has a reputation for religious tolerance and cultural diversity, with Hindu temples, Muslim mosques, and Christian churches coexisting.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Tanur' is derived from Malayalam: 'Thanni' refers to the Bastard myrobalan tree (Terminalia bellirica), and 'ur' means settlement or village. Thus, Tanur means 'settlement of the Thanni tree.' The name 'Tyndis' is of Greek origin, possibly derived from the local name 'Thondi' or 'Tundi.'

Pronunciation — TAH-noor (Malayalam: താനൂർ, Tānūr). English: TAN-ur.

Aliases — Tanore, Thanur, Thanni-ur, Tyndis (possibly), Vettathunadu (kingdom)

Maritime Trade Hub

Tanur was a major port on the Malabar Coast, facilitating trade between the Roman Empire, Middle East, and Asia. It was known for exporting spices, especially pepper, and importing goods like gold, silk, and ceramics.

Limit: Trade declined after the Portuguese disruption and later British colonial policies.

Strategic Military Position

The Kingdom of Tanur controlled a strategic coastal area with rivers, making it a valuable ally or target for naval powers. It played a role in the Portuguese-Zamorin conflicts.

Limit: The kingdom was a vassal state and depended on the Zamorin for protection.

Cultural and Intellectual Center

Tanur and the surrounding region (Vettathunadu) were home to the Kerala School of Astronomy and Mathematics, which made significant contributions to trigonometry and calculus. It was also the birthplace of Thunchaththu Ezhuthachan, the father of modern Malayalam literature.

Limit: The intellectual flourishing declined after the Portuguese period and political instability.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Tanur is a coastal town with a long shoreline along the Arabian Sea. It lies south of the Poorappuzha River estuary. The landscape includes sandy beaches, coconut groves, and laterite hills. The town has a mix of traditional Kerala architecture and modern buildings. As a fishing center, the harbor area is bustling with boats and fish markets.

Symbols — The Thanni tree (Terminalia bellirica) is the namesake and symbolic plant. The town's historical significance as a port is represented by maritime symbols. The Vettathu Raja's emblem likely included traditional Kerala royal symbols, but specific details are not documented.

Domains — Tanur is located in the Malabar Coast, a region known for its spice trade. The town's economy historically depended on trade in pepper, cardamom, and other spices. The surrounding region includes the Kadalundi River and the Bharathappuzha River, which facilitated inland trade.

Accounts I
The Roman Trade ConnectionPeriplus of the Erythraean Sea, Pliny the Elder

1st century CE

Tyndis (possibly Tanur) was a thriving port on the Malabar Coast, trading with the Roman Empire. It was known for its pepper and other spices, which were highly valued in Rome. The trade was so lucrative that it was estimated to be worth 50 million sesterces annually. The trade brought wealth and cultural exchange to the region, but also attracted pirates, as noted by Pliny.

Accounts II
The Portuguese Alliance and BetrayalPortuguese chronicles, Malabar Manual

16th century

The ruler of Tanur (Vettathu Raja) initially allied with the Portuguese against the Zamorin of Calicut, leading to the construction of the Chaliyam Fort. However, the alliance was short-lived, and the Zamorin's forces, with the help of the Mappilas, defeated the Portuguese at the Battle of Chaliyam in 1571. The Portuguese were expelled from the region, and Tanur came back under the Zamorin's influence. The kingdom's power declined.

The Legend of the Last RajaMalabar Manual by William Logan

18th century

The Kshatriya family of the Vettathu Rajas became extinct with the death of the last Raja on 24 May 1793. The circumstances of his death are not well-documented, but it marked the end of the kingdom's royal line. The kingdom was absorbed into the British-administered Malabar District, and the region was later reorganized into taluks.
