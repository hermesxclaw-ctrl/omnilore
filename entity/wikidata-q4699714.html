Form

No canonical iconographic representation is widely attested. In rural shrines, she is typically represented by an unhewn stone or a clay mound, often placed at the base of a peepal tree or near a granary. Where depicted in folk art, she appears as a woman with a python coiled around her body or hooded like a serpent. Some modern calendar art (bazaar prints) conflates her image with Manasa, showing a goddess seated on a lotus with a canopy of snake hoods.

Powers

Ophidian Command Ajgaibibi has authority over pythons and other serpents. She can command them to leave human dwellings and granaries, and can prevent them from attacking residents. Pan-regional folk belief Her power is primarily prophylactic and apotropaic; she is not typically invoked to cure snakebite (that role belongs to Manasa or the snake-charmer's deity), but to prevent encounters. Some narratives suggest she can also punish those who harm snakes, indicating a dual protective/retributive nature.

Domestic Guardianship She is believed to protect the home, especially the grain stores, from both literal serpents and metaphorical 'serpents' of misfortune, theft, or evil eye. Rural Bihar and Bengal Her protection is conditional on proper worship and respect; neglecting her shrine is believed to invite misfortune. None recorded.

Behavior & Habitat

Ajgaibibi is a protective, chthonic deity. She is believed to guard homes, granaries, and cattle sheds from pythons and venomous snakes. Unlike the more wrathful Manasa, who demands devotion and punishes neglect, Ajgaibibi is perceived as a benevolent, matronly figure who quietly ensures domestic safety. She is not a goddess of fertility in the agricultural sense, but rather of domestic security and the sanctity of the threshold.

Accounts

A poor farmer once found a massive python in his granary. He was about to kill it, but his wife stopped him, saying it was Ajgaibibi's mount. They offered it milk and rice. The python left, and from that year on, their granary was never empty, even in drought years. The story teaches that snakes are not enemies but agents of the goddess, and that hospitality to them brings prosperity.

A village was plagued by venomous snakes. A holy man (fakir or sadhu) dreamed of Ajgaibibi, who told him to establish a shrine at the village boundary. The villagers built a small earthen platform under a peepal tree. Snakes never entered the village again. The story emphasizes her role as a boundary deity, keeping wildness out of the domestic sphere.

Scholarly Debates
Relationship with Manasa
Ajgaibibi is a local form or alias of Manasa. ⇌ Ajgaibibi is an independent, pre-Aryan deity distinct from Manasa.
Evidence for A: Iconographic borrowing in modern art; regional overlap.
Evidence for B: Distinct name, distinct domain (pythons vs. cobras), distinct worship style (domestic vs. temple).
Benevolent vs. Retributive Nature
Ajgaibibi is purely benevolent and protective. ⇌ She punishes those who harm snakes or neglect her worship.
Evidence for A: Most oral narratives emphasize protection.
Evidence for B: A minority of narratives mention punishment for harming snakes.
What We Don't Know

Lack of primary textual sources; reliance on ethnographic observation from the 19th century onwards. No dedicated academic monograph exists. Iconographic history is poorly documented. The relationship with Manasa remains unresolved. Regional variations in ritual practice are under-documented. No linguistic analysis of the name's regional variants.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Ajgaibibi as the ancient, rural counterpart to a modern home security system. She is not a goddess of grand cosmic battles, but of the specific, practical fear of a snake in the kitchen or the grain store. She is the patron saint of the granary, the divine pest-control officer, and the guardian of the domestic threshold. If Manasa is the queen of serpents in the royal court of mythology, Ajgaibibi is the village headwoman who keeps the wild at bay, one clay lamp at a time.
