Who It Is

In the humid, monsoon-soaked villages of the Gangetic plain, where the python is both a feared predator and a silent guardian of the grain, there is a lady who rules the threshold. She is not in the grand temples, nor in the Sanskrit scriptures, but in the clay lamp lit at the base of a peepal tree, in the whispered prayer of a farmer's wife who asks that the great serpent stay out of the granary. She is Ajgaibibi, the Serpent Mistress, a goddess of the home who has survived not by conquest, but by quiet, persistent devotion.

Ajgaibibi is a protective, chthonic deity. She is believed to guard homes, granaries, and cattle sheds from pythons and venomous snakes. Unlike the more wrathful Manasa, who demands devotion and punishes neglect, Ajgaibibi is perceived as a benevolent, matronly figure who quietly ensures domestic safety. She is not a goddess of fertility in the agricultural sense, but rather of domestic security and the sanctity of the threshold.

Powers
Ophidian Command

Ajgaibibi has authority over pythons and other serpents. She can command them to leave human dwellings and granaries, and can prevent them from attacking residents.

Limit: Her power is primarily prophylactic and apotropaic; she is not typically invoked to cure snakebite (that role belongs to Manasa or the snake-charmer's deity), but to prevent encounters.

Domestic Guardianship

She is believed to protect the home, especially the grain stores, from both literal serpents and metaphorical 'serpents' of misfortune, theft, or evil eye.

Limit: Her protection is conditional on proper worship and respect; neglecting her shrine is believed to invite misfortune.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No canonical iconographic representation is widely attested. In rural shrines, she is typically represented by an unhewn stone or a clay mound, often placed at the base of a peepal tree or near a granary. Where depicted in folk art, she appears as a woman with a python coiled around her body or hooded like a serpent. Some modern calendar art (bazaar prints) conflates her image with Manasa, showing a goddess seated on a lotus with a canopy of snak

Symbols — The python (Ajgar), the unhewn stone (as an aniconic representation), the granary, the threshold of the home, the peepal tree (Ficus religiosa), clay lamps (diyas) offered on specific days.

Domains — Domestic protection, snake control (especially pythons), granary safety, household prosperity, protection of children from snakebite.

Accounts I
The Granary GuardianOral tradition, Mithila region

Contemporary, with deep roots

A poor farmer once found a massive python in his granary. He was about to kill it, but his wife stopped him, saying it was Ajgaibibi's mount. They offered it milk and rice. The python left, and from that year on, their granary was never empty, even in drought years. The story teaches that snakes are not enemies but agents of the goddess, and that hospitality to them brings prosperity. The family becomes prosperous; the story reinforces the taboo against killing pythons.

Accounts II
The Threshold PactOral tradition, Rarh region (West Bengal)

Contemporary

A village was plagued by venomous snakes. A holy man (fakir or sadhu) dreamed of Ajgaibibi, who told him to establish a shrine at the village boundary. The villagers built a small earthen platform under a peepal tree. Snakes never entered the village again. The story emphasizes her role as a boundary deity, keeping wildness out of the domestic sphere. The village is protected; the shrine becomes a site of annual worship, often integrated into existing Hindu or Sufi practices.

Ajgaibibi is a form of the Hindu goddess Manasa. — They are related but distinct. Ajgaibibi has a separate name, domain (pythons vs. cobras), and worship style (domestic vs. temple-based). The conflation is a modern phenomenon driven by calendar art and pan-Indian Hindu identity.

Ajgaibibi is worshipped exclusively by Hindus. — Her cult is syncretic and includes Muslim communities, particularly in rural Bengal and Bihar. She represents a shared folk stratum that predates and transcends communal religious boundaries.

Ajgaibibi is a 'minor' or 'obscure' goddess. — While she lacks a grand temple tradition, her domestic worship is widespread across a large geographical area. 'Minor' is a value judgment based on Sanskritic, textual, and temple-centric definitions of importance, which ignore the vitality of household religion.
