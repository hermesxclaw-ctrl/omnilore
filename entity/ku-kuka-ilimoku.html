Etymology

The name 'Kū' in the Hawaiian language means 'to stand' or 'to rise', reflecting his role as a steadfast, upright deity. The name 'Kūkaʻilimoku' translates as 'Snatcher of Land' or 'Land-grabbing Ku', referring to his association with conquest and territorial expansion.

Cultural Ownership

Kū is a Hawaiian deity, central to the traditional religion of the Hawaiian Islands. He is one of the four great gods (with Kāne, Lono, and Kanaloa) and holds particular significance in the history of the Kingdom of Hawaiʻi.

Disputed Identifications

Some feathered god images (akua hulu manu) are considered to represent Kū, but it is unclear whether all such images represent him. Additionally, some scholars have suggested a complementary dualism between Kū and Hina based on the meanings 'stand' and 'fall', but this is unsupported by comparative Polynesian linguistics.

Archive Dossier — Approach

In the churning surf of Hawaiian memory, a god stands firm—Kū, the war god who snatched islands from the sea and fed his altars with the blood of chiefs. He is the straight-backed pillar of governance and cultivation, the red-mouthed devourer of enemies, and the silent guardian whose feathered presence still watches over the bones of kings.

Kū is the Hawaiian god of war, the patron of chiefs and warriors — one of the four great gods (with Kāne, Lono, and Kanaloa). He is the most fearsome and demanding of the Hawaiian deities, requiring human sacrifice at his temple (luakini heiau).

Who It Is

Kū is the Hawaiian god of war, the patron of chiefs and warriors — one of the four great gods (with Kāne, Lono, and Kanaloa). He is the most fearsome and demanding of the Hawaiian deities, requiring human sacrifice at his temple (luakini heiau). Kū was the god to whom the highest-ranking chiefs prayed before battle, and his temples were the most sacred and tapu. His full name Kūkaʻilimoku means 'Kū the Snatcher of Islands.' Kū had many forms, including Kūkūlā (Kū the Fortress), Kūholo (Kū the Runner, associated with surfing), Kūkeʻoloʻewa (Kū the Patron of Outcasts), Kūkeea (Kū the Fisherman), and Kūʻula (Kū the Provider of Fish). The famous feather war helmets and cloaks of Hawaiian chiefs were dedicated to Kū. The last human sacrifices in Hawaii were made to Kū at the heiau at Puʻukoholā by Kamehameha I in 1804, who offered a rival chief to Kū to secure his conquest of the islands. Kū represents the absolute, often brutal power of kingship — the violence necessary to maintain order and expand territory.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

Kū is often represented in anthropomorphic wooden or feathered images. Feathered god images (akua hulu manu) typically have a woven basketry core covered with red and yellow feathers, with a mouth often depicting a snarling or open-mouthed expression, and may be adorned with dog teeth and pearl shells. The three colossal statues are carved from wood, likely ʻōhiʻa or koa, and depict a standing male figure with a defiant posture.

Symbols & Iconography

Kū is associated with the war god's spear, the digging stick, and the breadfruit tree. His colors are red and black, symbolizing war and sacrifice. The ʻōhiʻa lehua flower and the ʻieʻie vine are sacred to him. He is also symbolized by the hawk, shark, and dog.

Behaviour & Personality

Kū is a fierce, powerful, and demanding deity. He embodies the qualities of a warrior: strength, courage, and aggression. As a god of politics and governance, he is associated with the authority of chiefs and kings. He is also a provider, associated with farming and fishing, but his worship involves strict rituals and, in the form of Kūkaʻilimoku, human sacrifice.

Domains & Habitats

Kū presides over war, politics, farming, and fishing. He is present in the forest, the mountains, the cultivated fields, and the sea. His temples (heiau) were places of sacrifice and political power, often located at strategic points.

Version Differences

Kū has numerous forms or manifestations, each with a specific name and domain, such as Kūkaʻilimoku (war), Kū-ula (fishing), and Kū-ka-o-o (agriculture). These are not separate deities but aspects of the one god. In some traditions, Kū is paired with Hina, but the nature of this relationship varies.

Origins

Kū is one of the four primary Hawaiian gods, the first created by Kāne. His worship was universal throughout the Hawaiian Islands. The luakini heiau (war temples) dedicated to Kū were the most sacred structures, where high priests performed 'awa ceremonies and human sacrifices.

Hina

Type spouseSource Hawaiian religion

Description

Kū is considered the husband of the goddess Hina. Some interpretations suggest a complementary dualism (Kū standing, Hina falling), but this is linguistically unsupported.

Version Note

The dualism claim is disputed; Hina is more likely associated with the moon and silvery-grey color.

Conflicting Accounts

Some Polynesian traditions associate Hina with the moon, not with 'falling'.
