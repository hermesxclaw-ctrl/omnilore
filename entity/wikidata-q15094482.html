Form

There is no clear, distinctive iconography for 'Al Ana' specifically. The mother goddess Ḫannaḫanna is typically depicted as a mature woman, often seated on a throne, sometimes flanked by felines or birds. She may be shown with a child, emphasizing her role as a mother and birth-goddess. The 'Al Ana' name might have been associated with similar imagery, but this is speculative based on the fragmentary record.

Powers

Divine Counsel and Problem-Solving The mother goddess acts as the wise advisor to the divine assembly, providing solutions to crises, such as suggesting the bee to find the missing god. Hittite (Ḫannaḫanna) She does not directly command the other gods but influences them through counsel. None noted in the Hittite texts.

Control over Birth and Fertility As a mother goddess, she is the source of life, kingship, and fertility. She is invoked in rituals for safe childbirth and the prosperity of the land. Hittite/Hurrian/Sumerian (Ḫannaḫanna, Ḫannahannah, Nintu) Her power is focused on creation and nurture, not destruction or war. The extent of her role in creation is more explicitly detailed in Sumerian texts (Nintu) than in the Hittite corpus.

Control over Bees She commands bees, which she sends as her messengers to find the lost gods. The bee is her agent in the mythological narratives. Hittite (Ḫannaḫanna) This is a specific narrative function, not a general power. None noted.

Intercession She acts as an intercessor between the divine and human worlds, receiving prayers and offerings for the well-being of the king and state. Hittite (Ḫannaḫanna) This is a standard role for major deities in the Hittite pantheon. None noted.

Accounts

When Telipinu, the god of agriculture, disappears, all life on earth withers. The gods search for him in vain. The mother goddess Ḫannaḫanna suggests sending a bee to find him. The bee finds Telipinu, stings him to wake him, and brings him back. The god's anger is then ritually calmed, and the land is restored.

The Storm God disappears, causing chaos and famine. Ḫannaḫanna again suggests using a bee to find him. The bee finds him and the god is brought back, restoring order.

The mother goddess is invoked in rituals for purification, childbirth, and the well-being of the king. These rituals often involve offerings, symbolic gestures, and the recitation of myths to enact a desired outcome.

What We Don't Know

The primary gap is the extreme scarcity of evidence for the specific name 'Al Ana'. Most of the research necessarily focuses on the better-attested Ḫannaḫanna. Direct evidence for 'Al Ana' as a distinct deity is lacking. Further research would require access to the original cuneiform tablets and specialized Hittitological literature, which is beyond the scope of this initial study. The relationship between 'Al Ana' and other Anatolian mother goddesses remains unresolved.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of 'Al Ana' as a name you might see on a very old, very damaged library card. The card tells you a book exists, but the pages are mostly missing. The book, however, is the 'Grandmother of the Gods' — a figure who is more like a wise, powerful matriarch of a vast, ancient family. If you've ever seen a fantasy movie where the old, wise queen or grandmother figure gives the hero the one piece of advice that saves the world, you've met her spiritual descendants. She is the 'Mom' of the gods, the one who has seen it all and knows that a simple solution — like sending a bee — is often the best one.
