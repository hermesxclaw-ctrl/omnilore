Who It Is

In the shadow of the Hittite mountains, when the world went silent and the crops withered, the gods themselves panicked. Their savior was not a warrior or a storm-lord, but a grandmother: Ḫannaḫanna, the 'Grandmother of the Gods', who had the wisdom to send a single bee to find the lost world. 'Al Ana' may be her echo, a whisper of a name from a tablet fragment, but the power it represents is the oldest and most stubborn of all: the power of a mother to set things right.

In Hittite myths, the mother goddess (Ḫannaḫanna) is wise, concerned, and a problem-solver. When the Storm God or Telipinu disappears, she is the one who suggests sending a bee to find them, showing her practical wisdom and maternal care. She acts as a counselor to the gods. She is not a war-like deity but a source of life, nurture, and practical advice.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Al Ana' or 'Alana' appears in Hittite cuneiform texts. The more prominent Hittite mother goddess is Ḫannaḫanna, whose name is often interpreted as 'grandmother' (from the Hittite word 'ḫanna-' meaning 'grandmother'). Powers
Divine Counsel and Problem-Solving

The mother goddess acts as the wise advisor to the divine assembly, providing solutions to crises, such as suggesting the bee to find the missing god.

Limit: She does not directly command the other gods but influences them through counsel.

Control over Birth and Fertility

As a mother goddess, she is the source of life, kingship, and fertility. She is invoked in rituals for safe childbirth and the prosperity of the land.

Limit: Her power is focused on creation and nurture, not destruction or war.

Control over Bees

She commands bees, which she sends as her messengers to find the lost gods. The bee is her agent in the mythological narratives.

Limit: This is a specific narrative function, not a general power.

Intercession

She acts as an intercessor between the divine and human worlds, receiving prayers and offerings for the well-being of the king and state.

Limit: This is a standard role for major deities in the Hittite pantheon.

Form & Signs

Appearance — There is no clear, distinctive iconography for 'Al Ana' specifically. The mother goddess Ḫannaḫanna is typically depicted as a mature woman, often seated on a throne, sometimes flanked by felines or birds. She may be shown with a child, emphasizing her role as a mother and birth-goddess. The 'Al Ana' name might have been associated with similar imagery, but this is speculative based on the fragmentary record.

Symbols — Symbols associated with the mother goddess include the seated woman, the child, the bee (her messenger), the mountain, and possibly the feline. The bee is a particularly important symbol, as it is her agent in the 'Disappearing God' myths.

Domains — Her domain is the earth, mountains, and sacred springs. She is a chthonic and nature deity, associated with birth, fertility, and the cycle of life. Her seat is often a throne in the divine assembly or a sacred mountain. She is also connected to the 'sacred pool' or 'spring' where rituals for her might take place.

Accounts I
The Disappearance of TelipinuHittite mythological text (CTH 324)

c. 1650-1180 BCE

When Telipinu, the god of agriculture, disappears, all life on earth withers. The gods search for him in vain. The mother goddess Ḫannaḫanna suggests sending a bee to find him. The bee finds Telipinu, stings him to wake him, and brings him back. The god's anger is then ritually calmed, and the land is restored. The land is restored to fertility. The myth explains the cycle of seasons and the importance of the gods' presence.

Accounts II
The Disappearance of the Storm GodHittite mythological text (CTH 337)

c. 1650-1180 BCE

The Storm God disappears, causing chaos and famine. Ḫannaḫanna again suggests using a bee to find him. The bee finds him and the god is brought back, restoring order. Order is restored. The myth reinforces the mother goddess's role as a divine problem-solver.

Rituals of Purification and BirthHittite ritual texts

c. 1650-1180 BCE

The mother goddess is invoked in rituals for purification, childbirth, and the well-being of the king. These rituals often involve offerings, symbolic gestures, and the recitation of myths to enact a desired outcome. The rituals aim to ensure the king's health, the fertility of the land, and the safety of mothers and children.

'Al Ana' is a well-attested, major deity in the Hittite pantheon. — 'Al Ana' is a very obscure name with minimal textual support. The major, well-attested mother goddess is Ḫannaḫanna.

The Hittite mother goddess is the same as the Greek goddess Hera. — Hera is a distinctly Greek goddess with her own mythology. While she shares some roles (e.g., marriage), she is not the same as the Anatolian mother goddess. The closer Greek parallel is Rhea.

The Hittite mother goddess is a 'fertility goddess' in a simplistic, sexualized way. — Her domain is broader: birth, kingship, nature, and divine wisdom. She is a counselor and a creator, not just a symbol of sexual fertility.
