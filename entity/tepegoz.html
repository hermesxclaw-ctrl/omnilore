Who It Is

Tepegöz ('Single Eye' in Turkish) is a monstrous one-eyed giant from the Book of Dede Korkut, the foundational epic of the Oghuz Turks. He is the son of a human woman and the Perizat (a fairy or nature spirit), marked at birth by his single bulging eye and a voracious appetite that drives him to devour humans by the dozen. When the Oghuz tribe tries to flee from him, Tepegöz imprisons them in a cave, eating ten men and several hundred sheep each day. He is invulnerable to swords and arrows because only his eye (the only soft part of his body) can be wounded. The hero Basat, who was raised in the wilderness by a lioness after being lost as a child, finally confronts Tepegöz. Using wit as much as strength, Basat forces Tepegöz to fall asleep, then drives a hot iron spit through his eye, blinding him. A desperate struggle follows — Tepegöz, though blind, nearly kills Basat by choking him — but Basat finally cuts off Tepegöz's head, ending the terror. The Tepegöz story shares a clear Indo-European typology with the Cyclops Polyphemus from Homer's Odyssey (the blinding of a one-eyed giant who eats humans in a cave), suggesting either an ancient common source (Proto-Indo-European) or later cultural transmission. Tepegöz represents the blind, consuming force of brute power — the tyrant who eats the people and must be outwitted by a hero who knows that strength alone cannot defeat him.

The Approach

In the shadowed valleys of the Oghuz Turks, where the Book of Dede Korkut echoes with the deeds of heroes, there lurks a being that defies nature itself: Tepegöz, the one-eyed giant born of a shepherd and a peri, whose insatiable hunger and cunning made him the scourge of a people, until a hero's wit and a single burning brand brought him low.

Content advisory. Depictions of violence, mutilation, cannibalism, and the killing of children; also themes of supernatural birth and human sacrifice.
Identity

Tepegöz

Pronunciation teh-PEH-göz

Etymology

From Turkish 'tepe' (hill, top) and 'göz' (eye), literally 'hill-eye' or 'eye on the top of the head'. The name refers to his single eye located on his forehead.

Cultural Ownership

Turkic, specifically Oghuz Turkish; part of the oral epic tradition recorded in the Book of Dede Korkut (Kitab-ı Dede Korkut), a foundational work of Turkish literature.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars compare Tepegöz to the Cyclops Polyphemus in Homer's Odyssey, suggesting possible ancient Mediterranean or Indo-European influences. Others see him as a purely Turkic figure, with parallels in Central Asian Turkic mythologies (e.g., the one-eyed demon 'Depe Göz' in Altai legends). The relationship to Greek myth is debated and not definitively proven.

Description

Appearance

Tepegöz is depicted as a monstrous giant, with a single eye on his forehead, hence his name. He is described as having a huge body, a face like a lion's, and a voice like thunder. His fingers are like the branches of a tree, and his body is covered with coarse hair. He is often described as having a mouth that can swallow a man whole.

Symbols & Iconography

The single eye on the forehead is the primary symbol. In art, he is often shown as a giant with a cyclopean eye, sometimes with a turban or headgear, reflecting his semi-human origin. The eye is also a symbol of his vulnerability.

Behaviour & Personality

Tepegöz is portrayed as a cruel, gluttonous, and cunning monster. He devours humans and animals indiscriminately, and he demands tribute from the Oghuz people, requiring them to send him young men and women to eat. Despite his brute strength, he is not entirely stupid; he uses his size and cunning to terrorize the people, and he is capable of speech and reasoning.

Domains & Habitats

He dwells in a cave in the mountains, near a spring and a meadow, where he grazes his flocks. This location is often identified as being in the vicinity of the Oghuz homeland in Central Asia or Anatolia, but the story does not specify a precise geography.

Version Differences

In the Book of Dede Korkut, Tepegöz is clearly a monster with a single eye. In some later folk tales, he may be depicted as a dev (ogre) or a demon, but the Dede Korkut version is the most detailed and canonical. There are also variations in the story of his birth: in some versions, his mother is a peri (fairy) who was captured by a shepherd; in others, she is a human woman who was cursed. The method of his death also varies slightly, but the core elements remain consistent.

Stories & Myths

The Story of Basat and Tepegöz

Source Book of Dede KorkutCulture Oghuz TurkishPeriod Medieval (story likely older, oral tradition)

Summary

Tepegöz is born from a peri who was captured by a shepherd. The shepherd had been seduced by the peri, who then gave birth to a monstrous child. The child is abandoned, but is found and raised by a lioness, and later captured by the Oghuz. He is given to Aruz, a noble, to raise, but he becomes violent and is banished. He then terrorizes the Oghuz, demanding tribute of young men and women. Basat, the son of Aruz, confronts him. After a long struggle, Basat discovers Tepegöz's weakness: his eye. Basat blinds him with a burning brand and then kills him.

The Oghuz are freed from Tepegöz's tyranny. Basat becomes a hero.

Variants

In some versions, the peri gives birth to a child with a single eye; in others, she gives birth to a normal child who later becomes a monster. The method of killing also varies: sometimes Basat uses a sword, sometimes a burning brand, and sometimes he uses a special arrow.

Cannibalism

Source Book of Dede Korkut

Description

Tepegöz devours humans, including children and young adults, as a regular practice. He demands a tribute of 500 young men and 500 sheep per day, and he eats them.

Scholarly Context

This is typical of monster figures in epic literature, representing the threat of chaos and the need for heroic intervention.

Violence against children

Source Book of Dede Korkut

Description

Tepegöz specifically targets children and young people, and in one scene he kills a child in front of its mother.

Scholarly Context

This highlights the cruelty of the monster and the desperation of the people.

Human sacrifice

Source Book of Dede Korkut

Description

The Oghuz are forced to send their children to Tepegöz as tribute, effectively a form of human sacrifice to appease the monster.

Scholarly Context

This practice is common in myth and legend, reflecting the theme of appeasing supernatural forces.

Contradictions & Disputes

Origin of Tepegöz

Position A

He is a purely Turkic figure, with origins in Central Asian shamanic lore.

Position A — evidence

Similar one-eyed monsters appear in Turkic folklore, such as the Altai 'Depe Göz'.

Position B

He is influenced by Greek Cyclops myths, possibly through cultural contact in Anatolia.

Position B — evidence

The story of a hero blinding a one-eyed giant in a cave is strikingly similar to Homer's Odyssey.

Scholarly Consensus

There is no consensus; both possibilities are discussed. Some scholars see a common Indo-European heritage, while others argue for independent invention.

Nature of his invulnerability

Position A

His invulnerability is natural, due to his monstrous size and thick skin.

Position A — evidence

The text does not mention any magical protection.

Position B

It is magical, possibly from a charm or blessing from his peri mother.

Position B — evidence

His mother is a supernatural being, so it is plausible she granted him protection.

Scholarly Consensus

Unresolved; the text is ambiguous.
