Who It Is

When the lava lake at Kīlauea glows red against the night sky, many Hawaiians do not see mere molten rock—they see Pele, the volcanic woman whose passions shape islands, burn forests, and turn lovers into stone. To follow her story is to walk the rim of Halemaʻumaʻu, feeling both awe and unease, knowing that the ground beneath your feet is alive with a will older than any empire. This dossier traces that will through chants, legends, and contested histories, without smoothing the sharp edges of jealousy, devotion, and colonial conflict that make Pele one of the most compelling deities in the Pacific.

Pele is consistently portrayed as passionate, volatile, and intensely powerful. She loves deeply and jealously, as seen in her relationship with Lohiau and her rivalry with her sisters, especially Nāmakaokahaʻi. Her temper is quick; broken promises or perceived slights can provoke devastating eruptions, burning forests and consuming friends such as Hopoe. Yet she is not purely destructive: she creates new land, protects loyal kin like Hiʻiaka, and embodies the generative aspect of volcanic activity. Powers
Volcanic fire and lava control

Pele commands volcanic fire, lava flows, and eruptions. She can cause lava to burst forth from new vents, fill craters, and flow across the land, burning forests and reshaping coastlines. Her digging stick Pāoa is used to open pits and channels through which her fire emerges. Legends describe her as devouring land and trees, and as the animating force behind Kīlauea’s lava lake. [[3]](https://home.nps.gov/articles/holo-mai-pele.htm) [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm)

Limit: Her fire is opposed and sometimes quenched by the sea goddess Nāmakaokahaʻi, whose waves can flood her pits and extinguish her flames, forcing her to move to new locations. Salt water is repeatedly depicted as a counterforce to her lava.

Land creation and destruction

Pele both destroys and creates land. Lava flows burn existing forests and settlements, but once cooled, they form new ʻāina (land) extending the islands. Legends and modern narratives alike emphasize that Pele’s eruptions are part of a cycle of renewal, building new coastal plains and reshaping the geography of Hawaiʻi. [[4]](https://tianmu.org/good-work-library/oceanic/general-texts/hawaiian-legends-of-volcanoes-westervelt) [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm)

Limit: Her creative power is localized to volcanic regions; she is not described as creating the entire world, but specifically the lava-built portions of the Hawaiian archipelago.

Shapeshifting and spirit travel

Pele can travel in spirit form, leaving her body in the crater while her spirit journeys across the islands or into dreams. Emerson describes her meeting Lohiau in dreams and wandering in spirit-form before demanding physical union. She also appears in various human guises—young woman, old woman, or indistinct traveler—testing mortals’ hospitality and respect. [[2]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/ulh/ulh28.htm) [[0]](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/60279/60279-h/60279-h.htm)

Limit: No explicit limitations are stated, but her spirit travel is often linked to emotional states and dreams rather than arbitrary movement.

Prophetic dreams and foresight

Pele experiences dreams that reveal future events or potential lovers, such as her visionary encounter with Lohiau. These dreams motivate her actions and missions, suggesting a form of foresight or prophetic insight. [[2]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/ulh/ulh28.htm) [[0]](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/60279/60279-h/60279-h.htm)

Limit: Her foresight does not prevent jealousy or misjudgment; she still acts impulsively and sometimes destructively despite visionary knowledge.

Transformative power over bodies and stones

Pele can transform living beings into stone or other geological features. Lohiau is turned into a pillar of rock when she destroys him in a fit of jealousy. Hopoe becomes a dancing stone, and various hills and lava formations are said to be former humans or spirits altered by Pele’s fire. [[2]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/ulh/ulh28.htm) [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm)

Limit: Transformations are typically tied to emotional or moral contexts—jealousy, betrayal, or loyalty—rather than arbitrary acts.

Accounts I
How Pele Came to Hawaii / Holo Mai PeleW. D. Westervelt, "Hawaiian Legends of Volcanoes"; National Park Service article "Holo Mai Pele"

Traditional oral tradition, recorded in early 20th century texts and modern interpretive materials

Pele is born in Kahiki, a distant ancestral land. Recognized as powerful and potentially dangerous, she receives the digging stick Pāoa from her uncle Lonomakua and learns to wield volcanic fire. After accidentally burning sacred lands, she is pursued by her jealous sister Nāmakaokahaʻi, goddess of the sea. Guided by her brother Kamohoaliʻi and carrying her youngest sister Hiʻiaka in egg form, Pele voyages across the Pacific in the canoe Honuaiakea. She attempts to establish fire pits on Kauaʻi, Oʻahu (Leʻahi/Diamond Head), Molokaʻi, and Maui, but each time Nāmakaokahaʻi’s waves flood and extinguish her fires. Finally, on Hawaiʻi Island, Pele digs into the western slope of Mauna Loa and then into Kīlauea, creating Halemaʻumaʻu crater, where her fire is secure and she establishes her permanent home. Pele, Hiʻiaka, and LohiauNathaniel B. Emerson, "Pele and Hiiaka: A Myth from Hawaii"; Emerson, "Unwritten Literature of Hawaii" (Hula Pele)

Traditional oral tradition, compiled and translated in the early 20th century

Pele, residing at Kīlauea, dreams of the handsome chief Lohiau of Kauaʻi and falls in love with him. Desiring physical union, she sends her youngest sister Hiʻiaka to fetch Lohiau, after her older sisters refuse. Hiʻiaka secures promises from Pele that her beloved forest and her friend Hopoe will be protected during her absence. Accompanied by Wahine-omaʻo, Hiʻiaka undertakes a perilous journey, facing ghosts and kupua (supernatural beings). Upon reaching Kauaʻi, she finds Lohiau dead, a victim of Pele’s jealousy. Through ten days of prayer and incantation, Hiʻiaka restores Lohiau to life and begins the return journey. Meanwhile, Pele, consumed by jealousy and impatience, breaks her promise, burning Hiʻiaka’s forest and killing Hopoe. On arriving at Kīlauea, Hiʻiaka, in defiance and grief, openly embraces Lohiau at the crater’s edge. Enraged, Pele destroys Lohiau again, turning him into a pillar of rock and convulsing earth and sea. Only the intervention of the god Kāne prevents total cosmic ruin. Accounts II
Pele and KamapuaʻaW. D. Westervelt, "Hawaiian Legends of Volcanoes" (chapter VIII "Pele and the Snow-Goddess" and related material); secondary references in Beckwith’s "Hawaiian Mythology"

Traditional oral tradition, recorded in early 20th century

Pele engages in a tumultuous relationship with Kamapuaʻa, the pig-god associated with rain, agriculture, and fertility. In some legends they marry or become lovers, their union symbolizing the interplay of fire and water, lava and rain. In others they are fierce rivals, with Kamapuaʻa’s rains attempting to quench Pele’s fires and Pele’s lava threatening his domains. Their conflicts and reconciliations are played out across the landscape, with specific valleys, ridges, and lava flows attributed to their battles. [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm) [[4]](https://tianmu.org/good-work-library/oceanic/general-texts/hawaiian-legends-of-volcanoes-westervelt) These stories encode ecological and agricultural knowledge, linking rainfall patterns and volcanic activity to divine relationships. They also illustrate the erotic and combative dimensions of Pele’s character.

The Annihilation of Keoua’s ArmyW. D. Westervelt, "Hawaiian Legends of Volcanoes" (chapter XVIII); historical references in Hawaiian historiography

Late 18th century event, mythologized in early 20th century texts

During the wars of unification under Kamehameha I, the chief Keoua Kuahuʻula travels near Kīlauea with his army. A sudden explosive eruption and ash fall kill many of his warriors. Westervelt’s account, drawing on Hawaiian tradition, interprets this as Pele’s intervention, annihilating Keoua’s forces and aiding Kamehameha’s eventual victory. [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm) [[4]](https://tianmu.org/good-work-library/oceanic/general-texts/hawaiian-legends-of-volcanoes-westervelt) The event becomes a key example of Pele’s power in historical memory, reinforcing her role as a political and military force. It also illustrates how natural disasters are integrated into mythic frameworks.

Kapiolani and PeleW. D. Westervelt, "Hawaiian Legends of Volcanoes" (chapter XX); missionary and historical writings

Early 19th century event, narrated in 19th–20th century texts

The high chiefess Kapiolani, a Christian convert, travels to Kīlauea to publicly renounce Pele. Despite warnings that she will be destroyed, she descends toward Halemaʻumaʻu, prays to the Christian God, and drinks water and eats berries considered taboo in Pele’s presence. When she survives unharmed, missionaries proclaim this as proof of Christianity’s superiority and the defeat of Pele. [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm) [[4]](https://tianmu.org/good-work-library/oceanic/general-texts/hawaiian-legends-of-volcanoes-westervelt) The story becomes a cornerstone of missionary propaganda, symbolizing the transition from indigenous religion to Christianity. It contributes to the colonial marginalization of Pele worship while also preserving Pele’s name in historical memory.

Pele’s ontological status (literal deity vs. metaphor for volcanic processes)Traditional Native Hawaiian perspectives treat Pele as a literal akua, a personal deity whose will is expressed in volcanic activity and who can be addressed through ritual and chant.Modern scientific and some secular interpretations regard Pele as a metaphorical personification of geological processes, useful for storytelling but not ontologically real.
Pele’s relationship with Kamapuaʻa (marriage vs. rivalry)Some legends present Pele and Kamapuaʻa as married or in a stable romantic union, symbolizing the balance of fire and water.Other narratives emphasize ongoing conflict, with Kamapuaʻa’s rains attempting to quench Pele’s fires and their relationship characterized by struggle rather than harmony.
Interpretation of Kapiolani’s defiance of PeleMissionary and colonial narratives present Kapiolani’s survival at Kīlauea as proof that Pele is powerless and that Christianity has triumphed over indigenous religion.Modern Hawaiian cultural critiques view the story as a tool of religious and cultural suppression, arguing that it misrepresents Pele and Native Hawaiian spirituality.
Pele’s genealogies and parental figuresSome sources, such as the National Park Service article and certain chants, identify Kūwahailo and Haumea as Pele’s parents.Other genealogical traditions list different or additional ancestors, or place Haumea in a more distant generational position.
