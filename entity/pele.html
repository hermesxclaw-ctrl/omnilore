Archive Dossier — Identity

Pele

Pronunciation Hawaiian: [ˈpɛ.le] (two syllables, short e vowels, stress on the first syllable); extended form "Pelehonuamea" approximately [ˈpɛ.leˌhonu.aˈmɛ.a].

Known As

Etymology

The name "Pele" is widely attested in Hawaiian tradition as the personal name of the volcano and fire deity. Emerson notes extended forms such as "Pelehonuamea" (often glossed as "Pele of the sacred earth") and epithets like "ka wahine ʻai honua" (the woman who devours the land) and "ka wahine ʻai laʻau" (the woman who devours the forest), which describe her destructive volcanic power rather than provide a clear historical-linguistic derivation. A secure Proto-Polynesian etymology is not agreed upon; some scholars cautiously compare the name to other Polynesian fire or volcano beings, but this remains speculative and is best treated as unknown. [[0]](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/60279/60279-h/60279-h.htm) [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm)

Cultural Ownership

Pele belongs to the indigenous Native Hawaiian (Kānaka Maoli) religious and cultural tradition. Her stories, chants, and hula are part of a living body of practice and belief centered especially on Kīlauea and Mauna Loa on Hawaiʻi Island. Modern cultural practitioners and scholars emphasize that Pele is not merely a mythological character but a revered akua (deity) whose presence is felt in volcanic activity, ritual observances, and contemporary Hawaiian identity. [[2]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/ulh/ulh28.htm) [[3]](https://home.nps.gov/articles/holo-mai-pele.htm)

Disputed Identifications

Within comparative mythology, Pele is sometimes loosely equated with other fire or volcano deities such as Roman Vulcan or Greek Hephaestus, or with broader Polynesian figures like Tane or Tangaroa in popular discourse. These identifications are analogical and not grounded in Hawaiian primary sources. Some 19th–20th century missionary and colonial writings reframe Pele as a "devil" or superstition to be overcome by Christianity, which conflicts with Native Hawaiian understandings of her as a powerful, ambivalent, but respected akua. Scholarly work also debates whether Pele represents a single historical cult or a composite of multiple local volcanic spirits; this remains unresolved due to the fragmentary and regionally varied nature of the traditions. [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm) [[4]](https://tianmu.org/good-work-library/oceanic/general-texts/hawaiian-legends-of-volcanoes-westervelt)

Archive Dossier — Approach

When the lava lake at Kīlauea glows red against the night sky, many Hawaiians do not see mere molten rock—they see Pele, the volcanic woman whose passions shape islands, burn forests, and turn lovers into stone. To follow her story is to walk the rim of Halemaʻumaʻu, feeling both awe and unease, knowing that the ground beneath your feet is alive with a will older than any empire. This dossier traces that will through chants, legends, and contested histories, without smoothing the sharp edges of jealousy, devotion, and colonial conflict that make Pele one of the most compelling deities in the Pacific.

I fled across the ocean, my sister's waves at my heels. I dug my home in fire at the end of the island chain. The land I destroy, I also create. Do not mistake my passion for mere rage.

Who They Are

Pele is the Hawaiian goddess of volcanoes, fire, lightning, and lava — one of the most powerful, feared, and beloved deities in the Hawaiian pantheon. She dwells in the Halema'uma'u crater at the summit of Kīlauea on the Big Island of Hawai'i, and her moods determine whether the volcano is quiet or erupting in fountains of lava. Pele is simultaneously the creator of new land (every eruption adds to the island's mass) and the destroyer of what stands in her path — a volatile, passionate deity whose power literally reshapes the earth.

Physically, Pele is described in Hawaiian tradition as a woman of great beauty and terrifying power. She can appear as a beautiful young woman, a wizened old crone, or a white dog (her kino lau, or body form). In her full divine manifestation, she is a figure of volcanic fire — her hair is molten lava, her eyes are glowing cinders, and her body radiates unbearable heat. Traditional chants describe her as 'Pele-honua-mea' (Pele of the sacred earth) and 'Ka wahine 'ai honua' (the earth-devouring woman). She carries a magical digging stick (Pā'oa) with which she creates volcanic craters.

Pele's migration myth traces her journey to the Hawaiian Islands. According to tradition, Pele was born in the distant ancestral homeland of Kahiki (Tahiti or a more distant ancestral Pacific location; the exact referent is debated). She left Kahiki after a conflict with her older sister Nāmaka, the goddess of the sea — a conflict rooted in Pele's seduction of Nāmaka's husband. Pele fled across the ocean in a canoe, accompanied by her family of volcano deities. Nāmaka pursued her with great waves. Pele attempted to establish her volcanic home on each Hawaiian island, moving southeast: she dug craters on Ni'ihau, Kaua'i, O'ahu, Moloka'i, and Maui. But each time, Nāmaka flooded her fires with seawater. Finally, on the Big Island of Hawai'i, Pele dug deep into Mauna Loa — so deep that Nāmaka could not reach her fires — and there she made her permanent home. This myth explains the geological reality that the Hawaiian Islands increase in volcanic activity from northwest to southeast: the older, extinct volcanoes are Pele's failed attempts; Kīlauea and Mauna Loa are her triumph.

Pele's personality is famously volatile — she is a goddess of intense passion, jealousy, and creative destruction. Numerous Hawaiian stories tell of Pele falling in love with mortal men, often with tragic results. The most famous is the legend of Pele and 'Ōhi'a: Pele desired the handsome warrior 'Ōhi'a, but he loved a woman named Lehua. Enraged by his rejection, Pele turned 'Ōhi'a into a twisted tree (the 'ōhi'a lehua, still one of the first plants to colonize new lava flows). The other gods, taking pity on Lehua, transformed her into the beautiful red lehua blossom that grows on the 'ōhi'a tree — ensuring the lovers would remain together. It is said that picking a lehua blossom will cause rain, the tears of the separated lovers.

Pele remains a living deity in contemporary Hawai'i. Many Native Hawaiians and residents leave offerings to Pele at Kīlauea — typically gin, tobacco, and 'ōhelo berries. During eruptions, locals have reported seeing an old woman in white walking near the lava flows, believed to be Pele incarnate. Respect for Pele is taken seriously: removing lava rocks from Hawai'i is said to bring Pele's curse (bad luck until the rock is returned), a belief so persistent that the National Park Service regularly receives packages of rocks mailed back by guilt-stricken tourists.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

Attested descriptions of Pele emphasize her capacity for shapeshifting rather than a single fixed form. In many legends she appears as a beautiful young woman, often adorned with flowing black hair, red garments, and lei, whose beauty and charisma attract lovers such as Lohiau. In other accounts she manifests as an old woman or hag, testing the hospitality and respect of humans she encounters. Westervelt and Emerson both record episodes where Pele travels in human guise, only later revealing her divine identity through volcanic fire or miraculous acts. At a cosmic scale, Pele is identified with the lava lakes, fountains, and glowing vents of Kīlauea and Mauna Loa; her "body" is the molten rock and incandescent gases, and her "voice" is the rumbling and roaring of eruptions. [[0]](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/60279/60279-h/60279-h.htm) [[2]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/ulh/ulh28.htm) [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm)

Symbols & Iconography

Pele’s primary symbols are volcanic fire, lava flows, and the crater Halemaʻumaʻu, regarded as her home. The digging stick Pāoa, given by her uncle Lonomakua, is a key ritual and mythic implement used to open new fire pits and assert her presence in the land. Red and black colors, associated with lava and ʻiliʻili (small stones), appear frequently in chants and hula dedicated to her. Stones and lava formations said to be transformed bodies of her lovers or enemies—such as the pillar of rock representing Lohiau—function as living iconography in the landscape. In modern visual art and tourism imagery, Pele is often depicted as a woman emerging from lava or surrounded by flames, but these images are adaptations rather than direct traditional iconography. [[3]](https://home.nps.gov/articles/holo-mai-pele.htm) [[2]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/ulh/ulh28.htm) [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm)

Behaviour & Personality

Pele is consistently portrayed as passionate, volatile, and intensely powerful. She loves deeply and jealously, as seen in her relationship with Lohiau and her rivalry with her sisters, especially Nāmakaokahaʻi. Her temper is quick; broken promises or perceived slights can provoke devastating eruptions, burning forests and consuming friends such as Hopoe. Yet she is not purely destructive: she creates new land, protects loyal kin like Hiʻiaka, and embodies the generative aspect of volcanic activity. Emerson and Westervelt emphasize her dual nature—both nurturing and annihilating—while modern cultural narratives often stress her role as a guardian of the islands and a reminder of human humility before natural forces. [[0]](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/60279/60279-h/60279-h.htm) [[2]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/ulh/ulh28.htm) [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm)

Domains & Habitats

Pele’s principal domain is the volcanic landscape of Hawaiʻi Island, especially Kīlauea and its inner crater Halemaʻumaʻu, where she is said to reside. Legends trace her earlier attempts to establish herself on Kauaʻi, Oʻahu (Leʻahi/Diamond Head), Molokaʻi, and Maui, each thwarted by the sea goddess Nāmakaokahaʻi, until she finally secures a stable home at Kīlauea. Her influence extends to Mauna Loa and other volcanic vents, and she is invoked in connection with earthquakes, lava flows, and the creation of new land. In cosmological terms, Pele is part of a larger family of elemental deities governing fire, sea, wind, and forest, and her habitat includes both the physical crater and the spiritual realm of volcanic fire. [[3]](https://home.nps.gov/articles/holo-mai-pele.htm) [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm)

Version Differences

Different narrative cycles emphasize distinct aspects of Pele. The migration stories ("How Pele Came to Hawaii", "Holo Mai Pele") focus on her conflict with Nāmakaokahaʻi and the geological formation of the islands. The Pele–Hiʻiaka–Lohiau cycle foregrounds her jealousy, broken promises, and the complex emotional ties within her family. Legends involving Kamapuaʻa, the pig-god, highlight her erotic and combative relationships, sometimes presenting them as marriage, sometimes as rivalry. Historical-legendary episodes such as the annihilation of Keoua’s army and Kapiolani’s defiance recast Pele within Christian and colonial frameworks, portraying her as a defeated or superseded deity. Scholarly compilations like Beckwith’s attempt to systematize these variants, but the tradition remains inherently plural, with regional and performer-specific differences that resist full harmonization. [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm) [[2]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/ulh/ulh28.htm) [[3]](https://home.nps.gov/articles/holo-mai-pele.htm)

Archive Dossier — Powers

Volcanic fire and lava control

Source Version Traditional migration legends (Holo Mai Pele), Westervelt’s "How Pele Came to Hawaii" and related chapters, Emerson’s Pele narratives.

Description

Pele commands volcanic fire, lava flows, and eruptions. She can cause lava to burst forth from new vents, fill craters, and flow across the land, burning forests and reshaping coastlines. Her digging stick Pāoa is used to open pits and channels through which her fire emerges. Legends describe her as devouring land and trees, and as the animating force behind Kīlauea’s lava lake. [[3]](https://home.nps.gov/articles/holo-mai-pele.htm) [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm)

Limitations

Her fire is opposed and sometimes quenched by the sea goddess Nāmakaokahaʻi, whose waves can flood her pits and extinguish her flames, forcing her to move to new locations. Salt water is repeatedly depicted as a counterforce to her lava.

Contradictions

Some modern scientific interpretations treat Pele as a metaphor for volcanic processes rather than a literal agent, while traditional accounts insist on her personal agency. The tension between these views is interpretive rather than internal to the myth.

Land creation and destruction

Source Version Westervelt’s geological commentary and legends; National Park Service article linking Pele’s journey to island formation.

Description

Pele both destroys and creates land. Lava flows burn existing forests and settlements, but once cooled, they form new ʻāina (land) extending the islands. Legends and modern narratives alike emphasize that Pele’s eruptions are part of a cycle of renewal, building new coastal plains and reshaping the geography of Hawaiʻi. [[4]](https://tianmu.org/good-work-library/oceanic/general-texts/hawaiian-legends-of-volcanoes-westervelt) [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm)

Limitations

Her creative power is localized to volcanic regions; she is not described as creating the entire world, but specifically the lava-built portions of the Hawaiian archipelago.

Contradictions

Christianized accounts sometimes frame Pele’s destructive acts as punishment or as evidence of her inferiority to the Christian God, whereas indigenous narratives treat them as natural expressions of her elemental role.

Shapeshifting and spirit travel

Source Version Emerson’s "Hula Pele" summary and Pele–Hiʻiaka–Lohiau cycle; Westervelt’s legends of Pele visiting chiefs and commoners.

Description

Pele can travel in spirit form, leaving her body in the crater while her spirit journeys across the islands or into dreams. Emerson describes her meeting Lohiau in dreams and wandering in spirit-form before demanding physical union. She also appears in various human guises—young woman, old woman, or indistinct traveler—testing mortals’ hospitality and respect. [[2]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/ulh/ulh28.htm) [[0]](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/60279/60279-h/60279-h.htm)

Limitations

No explicit limitations are stated, but her spirit travel is often linked to emotional states and dreams rather than arbitrary movement.

Contradictions

Some versions emphasize her spiritual encounters more than physical ones, while others insist on her bodily presence at key events; these reflect different narrative emphases rather than direct contradiction.

Prophetic dreams and foresight

Source Version Emerson’s account of Pele’s dreams and the initiation of the Hiʻiaka mission.

Description

Pele experiences dreams that reveal future events or potential lovers, such as her visionary encounter with Lohiau. These dreams motivate her actions and missions, suggesting a form of foresight or prophetic insight. [[2]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/ulh/ulh28.htm) [[0]](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/60279/60279-h/60279-h.htm)

Limitations

Her foresight does not prevent jealousy or misjudgment; she still acts impulsively and sometimes destructively despite visionary knowledge.

Contradictions

Later Christian narratives downplay or reinterpret her prophetic role, framing her visions as delusions or demonic influence.

Transformative power over bodies and stones

Source Version Emerson’s "Hula Pele" narrative; Westervelt’s chapters on Hopoe and other transformed figures.

Description

Pele can transform living beings into stone or other geological features. Lohiau is turned into a pillar of rock when she destroys him in a fit of jealousy. Hopoe becomes a dancing stone, and various hills and lava formations are said to be former humans or spirits altered by Pele’s fire. [[2]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/ulh/ulh28.htm) [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm)

Limitations

Transformations are typically tied to emotional or moral contexts—jealousy, betrayal, or loyalty—rather than arbitrary acts.

Contradictions

Some local traditions attribute specific stones to other deities or spirits, creating overlapping attributions that are not fully resolvable.

Description

Kūwahailo is identified in some traditions as Pele’s father, part of the divine genealogy that situates her within a broader family of elemental deities.

Version Note

Genealogies vary; some sources emphasize other parental figures or omit detailed ancestry.

Conflicting Accounts

Different genealogical chants may list alternative or additional parents, reflecting regional and compositional variation.

Haumea

Type familySource National Park Service, "Holo Mai Pele"; Beckwith’s "Hawaiian Mythology" (secondary). [[3]](https://home.nps.gov/articles/holo-mai-pele.htm) [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm)

Description

Haumea, a goddess associated with fertility and earth, is presented as Pele’s mother in several accounts, reinforcing Pele’s connection to the land and its generative powers.

Version Note

Haumea’s roles and relationships differ across Polynesian and Hawaiian traditions; in some she is more central, in others more peripheral.

Conflicting Accounts

Alternative genealogies may assign different maternal figures or treat Haumea as an ancestor rather than direct mother.

Nāmakaokahaʻi

Type familySource National Park Service, "Holo Mai Pele"; Westervelt’s migration legends. [[3]](https://home.nps.gov/articles/holo-mai-pele.htm) [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm)

Description

Nāmakaokahaʻi is Pele’s eldest sister, a powerful sea goddess whose waves repeatedly extinguish Pele’s fires and drive her from island to island. Their relationship is marked by rivalry and jealousy.

Version Note

Some versions emphasize Nāmakaokahaʻi’s envy of Pele’s beauty and mana; others focus on the elemental conflict between sea and fire.

Conflicting Accounts

In certain chants, Nāmakaokahaʻi’s exact kinship position (older sister vs. other relation) may differ, but she is consistently a close relative and antagonist.

Hiʻiakaikapoliopele (Hiʻiaka)

Type familySource Emerson, "Pele and Hiiaka"; Emerson, "Unwritten Literature of Hawaii" (Hula Pele). [[0]](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/60279/60279-h/60279-h.htm) [[2]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/ulh/ulh28.htm)

Description

Hiʻiaka is Pele’s youngest and most beloved sister, carried as an egg during the voyage from Kahiki. She is entrusted with the mission to retrieve Lohiau and is both loyal and assertive, challenging Pele when promises are broken.

Version Note

Hiʻiaka’s role varies from devoted helper to near-equal protagonist in the epic cycle.

Conflicting Accounts

Some traditions emphasize multiple Hiʻiaka sisters; the specific identity of Hiʻiakaikapoliopele can blur with other similarly named figures.

Kamohoaliʻi

Description

Kamohoaliʻi, often described as Pele’s oldest brother, assists her in escaping Nāmakaokahaʻi and navigating the voyage to Hawaiʻi. He is associated with sharks and the sea, providing guidance and protection.

Version Note

Kamohoaliʻi’s exact relationship and attributes vary, but he is consistently a senior male relative and helper.

Conflicting Accounts

Some genealogies place Kamohoaliʻi in different generational positions or emphasize his shark-god identity more strongly.

Lonomakua

Type familySource National Park Service, "Holo Mai Pele". [[3]](https://home.nps.gov/articles/holo-mai-pele.htm)

Description

Lonomakua is Pele’s uncle who gives her the digging stick Pāoa and teaches her how to control her volcanic powers.

Version Note

His role is primarily functional, as a mentor and provider of tools.

Conflicting Accounts

Some sources mention Lonomakua briefly or not at all, reflecting selective emphasis in different narratives.

Lohiau

Type romanticSource Emerson, "Pele and Hiiaka"; Emerson, "Unwritten Literature of Hawaii" (Hula Pele). [[0]](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/60279/60279-h/60279-h.htm) [[2]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/ulh/ulh28.htm)

Description

Lohiau is a handsome chief of Kauaʻi whom Pele first encounters in dreams and desires as a lover. Her jealousy and impatience lead to his death and transformation into stone.

Version Note

In some versions, Pele and Lohiau consummate their relationship; in others, Hiʻiaka’s bond with Lohiau becomes central.

Conflicting Accounts

The balance of affection between Pele and Hiʻiaka toward Lohiau varies, creating tension in interpretations of who is his primary partner.

Kamapuaʻa

Type romanticSource Westervelt, "Hawaiian Legends of Volcanoes"; Beckwith, "Hawaiian Mythology" (secondary). [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm) [[4]](https://tianmu.org/good-work-library/oceanic/general-texts/hawaiian-legends-of-volcanoes-westervelt)

Description

Kamapuaʻa, the pig-god, is both lover and rival to Pele. Their relationship symbolizes the interplay of fire and water, lava and rain, and is marked by erotic tension and conflict.

Version Note

Some narratives present a formal marriage; others depict episodic encounters and battles.

Conflicting Accounts

Christian-influenced retellings may downplay the erotic aspects or recast Kamapuaʻa as morally problematic, altering the tone of the relationship.

Hopoe

Type friendSource Emerson, "Unwritten Literature of Hawaii" (Hula Pele). [[2]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/ulh/ulh28.htm)

Description

Hopoe is Hiʻiaka’s close friend, a dancer whose body is eventually transformed into stone by Pele’s fire when Pele breaks her promise to protect Hiʻiaka’s loved ones.

Version Note

Hopoe’s role is primarily as a victim of Pele’s broken oath, highlighting the moral stakes of the narrative.

Conflicting Accounts

Some local traditions may attribute Hopoe’s stone form to other causes, but Emerson’s account is widely cited.

Kāne

Type allySource Emerson, "Unwritten Literature of Hawaii" (Hula Pele). [[2]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/ulh/ulh28.htm)

Description

Kāne, a major Hawaiian god associated with life and freshwater, intervenes to prevent total destruction when Pele’s rage threatens to convulse earth and sea during the Lohiau episode.

Version Note

Kāne’s role underscores the interconnectedness of deities and the need for balance among elemental forces.

Conflicting Accounts

Other deities may be credited with similar peacemaking roles in variant traditions.

Kapiolani

Type hostileSource Westervelt, "Hawaiian Legends of Volcanoes" (chapter XX). [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm) [[4]](https://tianmu.org/good-work-library/oceanic/general-texts/hawaiian-legends-of-volcanoes-westervelt)

Description

Kapiolani, a Christian chiefess, publicly defies Pele by descending toward Halemaʻumaʻu and breaking taboos, framing Pele as powerless before the Christian God.

Version Note

The relationship is mediated through narrative rather than direct mythic interaction; Pele does not appear in person.

Conflicting Accounts

Indigenous perspectives on Kapiolani’s act are underrepresented; some modern Hawaiian commentators question the triumphalist framing.

Native Hawaiian worshippers and cultural practitioners

Type worshipperSource Emerson’s notes on hula Pele; modern cultural and park interpretive materials. [[2]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/ulh/ulh28.htm) [[3]](https://home.nps.gov/articles/holo-mai-pele.htm)

Description

Pele is venerated by Native Hawaiians through chants, offerings, hula, and respectful behavior toward volcanic sites. Her presence is acknowledged in contemporary practice, especially during eruptions.

Version Note

Practices vary by region and family; some emphasize formal ritual, others everyday respect.

Conflicting Accounts

Missionary and colonial sources often portray Pele worship as superstition, creating a conflict of perspectives that persists in historical narratives.
