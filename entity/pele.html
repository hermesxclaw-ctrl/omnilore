Who They Are

Pele is the Hawaiian goddess of volcanoes, fire, lightning, and lava — one of the most powerful, feared, and beloved deities in the Hawaiian pantheon. She dwells in the Halema'uma'u crater at the summit of Kīlauea on the Big Island of Hawai'i, and her moods determine whether the volcano is quiet or erupting in fountains of lava. Pele is simultaneously the creator of new land (every eruption adds to the island's mass) and the destroyer of what stands in her path — a volatile, passionate deity whose power literally reshapes the earth.

Physically, Pele is described in Hawaiian tradition as a woman of great beauty and terrifying power. She can appear as a beautiful young woman, a wizened old crone, or a white dog (her kino lau, or body form). In her full divine manifestation, she is a figure of volcanic fire — her hair is molten lava, her eyes are glowing cinders, and her body radiates unbearable heat. Traditional chants describe her as 'Pele-honua-mea' (Pele of the sacred earth) and 'Ka wahine 'ai honua' (the earth-devouring woman). She carries a magical digging stick (Pā'oa) with which she creates volcanic craters.

Pele's migration myth traces her journey to the Hawaiian Islands. According to tradition, Pele was born in the distant ancestral homeland of Kahiki (Tahiti or a more distant ancestral Pacific location; the exact referent is debated). She left Kahiki after a conflict with her older sister Nāmaka, the goddess of the sea — a conflict rooted in Pele's seduction of Nāmaka's husband. Pele fled across the ocean in a canoe, accompanied by her family of volcano deities. Nāmaka pursued her with great waves. Pele attempted to establish her volcanic home on each Hawaiian island, moving southeast: she dug craters on Ni'ihau, Kaua'i, O'ahu, Moloka'i, and Maui. But each time, Nāmaka flooded her fires with seawater. Finally, on the Big Island of Hawai'i, Pele dug deep into Mauna Loa — so deep that Nāmaka could not reach her fires — and there she made her permanent home. This myth explains the geological reality that the Hawaiian Islands increase in volcanic activity from northwest to southeast: the older, extinct volcanoes are Pele's failed attempts; Kīlauea and Mauna Loa are her triumph.

Pele's personality is famously volatile — she is a goddess of intense passion, jealousy, and creative destruction. Numerous Hawaiian stories tell of Pele falling in love with mortal men, often with tragic results. The most famous is the legend of Pele and 'Ōhi'a: Pele desired the handsome warrior 'Ōhi'a, but he loved a woman named Lehua. Enraged by his rejection, Pele turned 'Ōhi'a into a twisted tree (the 'ōhi'a lehua, still one of the first plants to colonize new lava flows). The other gods, taking pity on Lehua, transformed her into the beautiful red lehua blossom that grows on the 'ōhi'a tree — ensuring the lovers would remain together. It is said that picking a lehua blossom will cause rain, the tears of the separated lovers.

Pele remains a living deity in contemporary Hawai'i. Many Native Hawaiians and residents leave offerings to Pele at Kīlauea — typically gin, tobacco, and 'ōhelo berries. During eruptions, locals have reported seeing an old woman in white walking near the lava flows, believed to be Pele incarnate. Respect for Pele is taken seriously: removing lava rocks from Hawai'i is said to bring Pele's curse (bad luck until the rock is returned), a belief so persistent that the National Park Service regularly receives packages of rocks mailed back by guilt-stricken tourists.

Form

Attested descriptions of Pele emphasize her capacity for shapeshifting rather than a single fixed form. In many legends she appears as a beautiful young woman, often adorned with flowing black hair, red garments, and lei, whose beauty and charisma attract lovers such as Lohiau. In other accounts she manifests as an old woman or hag, testing the hospitality and respect of humans she encounters. Westervelt and Emerson both record episodes where Pele travels in human guise, only later revealing her divine identity through volcanic fire or miraculous acts. At a cosmic scale, Pele is identified with the lava lakes, fountains, and glowing vents of Kīlauea and Mauna Loa; her "body" is the molten rock and incandescent gases, and her "voice" is the rumbling and roaring of eruptions. Powers

Volcanic fire and lava control Pele commands volcanic fire, lava flows, and eruptions. She can cause lava to burst forth from new vents, fill craters, and flow across the land, burning forests and reshaping coastlines. Her digging stick Pāoa is used to open pits and channels through which her fire emerges. Legends describe her as devouring land and trees, and as the animating force behind Kīlauea’s lava lake. [[3]](https://home.nps.gov/articles/holo-mai-pele.htm) [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm) Traditional migration legends (Holo Mai Pele), Westervelt’s "How Pele Came to Hawaii" and related chapters, Emerson’s Pele narratives. Her fire is opposed and sometimes quenched by the sea goddess Nāmakaokahaʻi, whose waves can flood her pits and extinguish her flames, forcing her to move to new locations. Salt water is repeatedly depicted as a counterforce to her lava. Some modern scientific interpretations treat Pele as a metaphor for volcanic processes rather than a literal agent, while traditional accounts insist on her personal agency. The tension between these views is interpretive rather than internal to the myth.

Land creation and destruction Pele both destroys and creates land. Lava flows burn existing forests and settlements, but once cooled, they form new ʻāina (land) extending the islands. Legends and modern narratives alike emphasize that Pele’s eruptions are part of a cycle of renewal, building new coastal plains and reshaping the geography of Hawaiʻi. [[4]](https://tianmu.org/good-work-library/oceanic/general-texts/hawaiian-legends-of-volcanoes-westervelt) [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm) Westervelt’s geological commentary and legends; National Park Service article linking Pele’s journey to island formation. Her creative power is localized to volcanic regions; she is not described as creating the entire world, but specifically the lava-built portions of the Hawaiian archipelago. Christianized accounts sometimes frame Pele’s destructive acts as punishment or as evidence of her inferiority to the Christian God, whereas indigenous narratives treat them as natural expressions of her elemental role.

Shapeshifting and spirit travel Pele can travel in spirit form, leaving her body in the crater while her spirit journeys across the islands or into dreams. Emerson describes her meeting Lohiau in dreams and wandering in spirit-form before demanding physical union. She also appears in various human guises—young woman, old woman, or indistinct traveler—testing mortals’ hospitality and respect. [[2]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/ulh/ulh28.htm) [[0]](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/60279/60279-h/60279-h.htm) Emerson’s "Hula Pele" summary and Pele–Hiʻiaka–Lohiau cycle; Westervelt’s legends of Pele visiting chiefs and commoners. No explicit limitations are stated, but her spirit travel is often linked to emotional states and dreams rather than arbitrary movement. Some versions emphasize her spiritual encounters more than physical ones, while others insist on her bodily presence at key events; these reflect different narrative emphases rather than direct contradiction.

Prophetic dreams and foresight Pele experiences dreams that reveal future events or potential lovers, such as her visionary encounter with Lohiau. These dreams motivate her actions and missions, suggesting a form of foresight or prophetic insight. [[2]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/ulh/ulh28.htm) [[0]](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/60279/60279-h/60279-h.htm) Emerson’s account of Pele’s dreams and the initiation of the Hiʻiaka mission. Her foresight does not prevent jealousy or misjudgment; she still acts impulsively and sometimes destructively despite visionary knowledge. Later Christian narratives downplay or reinterpret her prophetic role, framing her visions as delusions or demonic influence.

Transformative power over bodies and stones Pele can transform living beings into stone or other geological features. Lohiau is turned into a pillar of rock when she destroys him in a fit of jealousy. Hopoe becomes a dancing stone, and various hills and lava formations are said to be former humans or spirits altered by Pele’s fire. [[2]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/ulh/ulh28.htm) [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm) Emerson’s "Hula Pele" narrative; Westervelt’s chapters on Hopoe and other transformed figures. Transformations are typically tied to emotional or moral contexts—jealousy, betrayal, or loyalty—rather than arbitrary acts. Some local traditions attribute specific stones to other deities or spirits, creating overlapping attributions that are not fully resolvable.

Accounts

Pele is born in Kahiki, a distant ancestral land. Recognized as powerful and potentially dangerous, she receives the digging stick Pāoa from her uncle Lonomakua and learns to wield volcanic fire. After accidentally burning sacred lands, she is pursued by her jealous sister Nāmakaokahaʻi, goddess of the sea. Guided by her brother Kamohoaliʻi and carrying her youngest sister Hiʻiaka in egg form, Pele voyages across the Pacific in the canoe Honuaiakea. She attempts to establish fire pits on Kauaʻi, Oʻahu (Leʻahi/Diamond Head), Molokaʻi, and Maui, but each time Nāmakaokahaʻi’s waves flood and extinguish her fires. Finally, on Hawaiʻi Island, Pele digs into the western slope of Mauna Loa and then into Kīlauea, creating Halemaʻumaʻu crater, where her fire is secure and she establishes her permanent home. [[3]](https://home.nps.gov/articles/holo-mai-pele.htm) [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm)

Pele, residing at Kīlauea, dreams of the handsome chief Lohiau of Kauaʻi and falls in love with him. Desiring physical union, she sends her youngest sister Hiʻiaka to fetch Lohiau, after her older sisters refuse. Hiʻiaka secures promises from Pele that her beloved forest and her friend Hopoe will be protected during her absence. Accompanied by Wahine-omaʻo, Hiʻiaka undertakes a perilous journey, facing ghosts and kupua (supernatural beings). Upon reaching Kauaʻi, she finds Lohiau dead, a victim of Pele’s jealousy. Through ten days of prayer and incantation, Hiʻiaka restores Lohiau to life and begins the return journey. Meanwhile, Pele, consumed by jealousy and impatience, breaks her promise, burning Hiʻiaka’s forest and killing Hopoe. On arriving at Kīlauea, Hiʻiaka, in defiance and grief, openly embraces Lohiau at the crater’s edge. Enraged, Pele destroys Lohiau again, turning him into a pillar of rock and convulsing earth and sea. Only the intervention of the god Kāne prevents total cosmic ruin. [[2]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/ulh/ulh28.htm) [[0]](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/60279/60279-h/60279-h.htm)

Pele engages in a tumultuous relationship with Kamapuaʻa, the pig-god associated with rain, agriculture, and fertility. In some legends they marry or become lovers, their union symbolizing the interplay of fire and water, lava and rain. In others they are fierce rivals, with Kamapuaʻa’s rains attempting to quench Pele’s fires and Pele’s lava threatening his domains. Their conflicts and reconciliations are played out across the landscape, with specific valleys, ridges, and lava flows attributed to their battles. [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm) [[4]](https://tianmu.org/good-work-library/oceanic/general-texts/hawaiian-legends-of-volcanoes-westervelt)

During the wars of unification under Kamehameha I, the chief Keoua Kuahuʻula travels near Kīlauea with his army. A sudden explosive eruption and ash fall kill many of his warriors. Westervelt’s account, drawing on Hawaiian tradition, interprets this as Pele’s intervention, annihilating Keoua’s forces and aiding Kamehameha’s eventual victory. [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm) [[4]](https://tianmu.org/good-work-library/oceanic/general-texts/hawaiian-legends-of-volcanoes-westervelt)

The high chiefess Kapiolani, a Christian convert, travels to Kīlauea to publicly renounce Pele. Despite warnings that she will be destroyed, she descends toward Halemaʻumaʻu, prays to the Christian God, and drinks water and eats berries considered taboo in Pele’s presence. When she survives unharmed, missionaries proclaim this as proof of Christianity’s superiority and the defeat of Pele. [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm) [[4]](https://tianmu.org/good-work-library/oceanic/general-texts/hawaiian-legends-of-volcanoes-westervelt)

Content Notes

In the Pele–Hiʻiaka–Lohiau cycle, Pele breaks her promise to protect Hiʻiaka’s forest and friend Hopoe. In a fit of jealousy and impatience, she sends lava to burn the forest and transforms Hopoe into stone. This act is portrayed as a grave moral violation, causing deep grief and anger in Hiʻiaka and contributing to the climactic confrontation at Kīlauea.

Emerson highlights this episode as central to the ethical dimension of the Pele myth, illustrating the consequences of broken oaths and the ambivalence of divine power.

Emerson, "Unwritten Literature of Hawaii" (Hula Pele). [[2]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/ulh/ulh28.htm)

Pele’s jealousy leads her to kill Lohiau twice—once indirectly before Hiʻiaka’s arrival, and again when she destroys him in rage after witnessing Hiʻiaka’s defiant embrace. Lohiau’s body is turned into a pillar of rock, a permanent reminder of Pele’s destructive passion.

The episode is often read as a tragic love story and a cautionary tale about jealousy. It also functions as an etiological myth for specific geological formations.

Emerson, "Unwritten Literature of Hawaii" (Hula Pele); Emerson, "Pele and Hiiaka". [[2]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/ulh/ulh28.htm) [[0]](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/60279/60279-h/60279-h.htm)

Westervelt’s account of the eruption that killed many of Keoua Kuahuʻula’s warriors interprets the event as Pele’s intervention in human warfare. The sudden volcanic disaster is framed as a decisive, lethal act that shifts the balance of power toward Kamehameha.

This narrative illustrates how natural catastrophes are integrated into political myth-making. It raises questions about the use of divine agency to legitimize conquest and the retrospective moralization of disaster.

Westervelt, "Hawaiian Legends of Volcanoes" (chapter XVIII). [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm) [[4]](https://tianmu.org/good-work-library/oceanic/general-texts/hawaiian-legends-of-volcanoes-westervelt)

The story of Kapiolani’s defiance of Pele is used by missionaries to depict indigenous religion as false and powerless. Pele is cast as a defeated or nonexistent deity, and Native Hawaiian beliefs are portrayed as superstition to be overcome by Christianity.

Modern scholars and Hawaiian cultural advocates critique this narrative as part of a broader colonial project that suppressed indigenous spirituality and reinterpreted local deities through a Christian lens.

Westervelt, "Hawaiian Legends of Volcanoes" (chapter XX); missionary writings referenced therein. [[1]](https://sacred-texts.com/pac/hlov/index.htm) [[4]](https://tianmu.org/good-work-library/oceanic/general-texts/hawaiian-legends-of-volcanoes-westervelt)

What We Don't Know

Several important gaps remain. (1) Many primary Hawaiian-language chants and manuscripts about Pele are not fully accessible in the consulted online sources, limiting precise page and line citations and deeper linguistic analysis. (2) Variant genealogies and regional traditions are referenced in secondary scholarship but not exhaustively documented here, so the dossier cannot claim to represent all known versions. (3) Contemporary Native Hawaiian perspectives, including those of cultural practitioners and elders, are underrepresented in early 20th-century texts and park interpretive materials; their voices would be essential for a more balanced account. (4) Detailed academic debates on colonialism, gender, and environmental ethics in relation to Pele are only indirectly referenced, as full-text access to recent journal articles was not available in the current search. (5) The reception section focuses on English-language works and does not cover all modern artistic, literary, and media representations of Pele, especially those in Hawaiian and other languages.

A way to hold it in your mind

When the lava lake at Kīlauea glows red against the night sky, many Hawaiians do not see mere molten rock—they see Pele, the volcanic woman whose passions shape islands, burn forests, and turn lovers into stone. To follow her story is to walk the rim of Halemaʻumaʻu, feeling both awe and unease, knowing that the ground beneath your feet is alive with a will older than any empire. This dossier traces that will through chants, legends, and contested histories, without smoothing the sharp edges of jealousy, devotion, and colonial conflict that make Pele one of the most compelling deities in the Pacific.
