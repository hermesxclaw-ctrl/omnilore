Who It Is

Tāne Mahuta is one of the most important gods in the Māori pantheon — the god of forests, birds, and light, and the being responsible for the single most consequential act in Māori cosmology: the separation of his parents, Ranginui (Sky Father) and Papatūānuku (Earth Mother), who lay in a tight, dark embrace that left no space for life. Tāne, driven by his desire for light and room to grow, lay on his back and pressed his feet against his father and his shoulders against his mother, and with a mighty push, separated them forever. Light flooded the world, and the universe as we know it was created — the sky above, the earth below, and the realm of life between them. After this act, Tāne clothed his mother the Earth in forests and filled the sky with stars. He then created the first woman, Hine-ahu-one, from red clay and breathed life into her nostrils, becoming the father of humanity. Tāne is the god who brought light, life, and humanity into existence. His sacred realm is the forest, and all birds (ngā manu) are his children. The magnificent kauri tree in Waipoua Forest on New Zealand's North Island — estimated at 2,500 years old and named 'Tāne Mahuta' — is considered a living embodiment of the god and is one of New Zealand's most revered natural sites. In traditional Māori thought, to enter the forest is to enter Tāne's body; to fell a tree is to wound a god. This ethic is central to Māori environmental philosophy and contemporary conservation efforts.
