Who It Is

Before the video game character who would become one of the most recognizable figures in modern entertainment, there was Kratos — not a Spartan warrior who killed gods, but a winged daimon who did the dirty work of Olympus. The mythological Kratos (Κράτος, 'Power') is the divine personification of sheer, unthinking strength. He has no temple, no worshippers, no independent will. His entire existence consists of standing at Zeus' throne and carrying out punishments. In the opening scene of Prometheus Bound — one of ancient Greece's most searing indictments of absolute power — Kratos appears as the talkative, brutally efficient jailer who mocks the suffering Prometheus and bullies the reluctant Hephaestus into binding him. In Prometheus Bound, Kratos is portrayed as brutal, impatient, and cruelly pragmatic. He mocks Hephaestus' reluctance, calls him a 'hateful thing' for pitying Prometheus, and insists on the necessity of violent punishment. He speaks at length for a daimon — unlike his silent sister Bia — and his language is sharp, sardonic, and imperious. He is utterly loyal to Zeus and dismissive of any argument that Zeus could be wrong. Powers
Superhuman Strength (Personification)

As the divine personification of strength and power, Kratos embodies the abstract concept. His strength is not a magical ability but the metaphysical essence of physical force itself. He is depicted physically restraining Prometheus and Ixion and is tasked with all acts requiring divine might.

Limit: His strength is constrained by Zeus' will — he acts only as an agent, never independently. He does not appear to contest other gods' power on his own initiative.

Inviolable Authority as Zeus' Agent

Kratos cannot be disobeyed when acting on Zeus' direct command. In Prometheus Bound, even Hephaestus — a god of Olympus and master craftsman — cannot refuse Kratos' orders. Kratos reminds him 'The father gave this work to you alone; take it up.' The fear of Zeus invested in Kratos makes him functionally irresistible.

Limit: This authority is derivative — it only applies when he is executing explicit commands of Zeus. It does not appear to be an inherent personal power.

Flight (Wings)

Kratos is described as a 'winged enforcer' along with his siblings, suggesting the ability to traverse freely between Olympus and the mortal realm. The Ixion skyphos depicts Kratos with wings.

Limit: Winged flight is implied but never described in the surviving literary texts. The extent and speed of flight are not specified.

Immortality and Eternal Presence at Zeus' Throne

Hesiod establishes that Kratos and his siblings 'dwell always with Zeus.' They are not subject to age, death, or banishment — they are eternal personifications who have existed since the Titanomachy and will continue as long as Zeus' reign endures.

Limit: Bound to Zeus' continued reign — their existence as Zeus' enforcers is tied to his cosmic order.

Accounts I
The Binding of Prometheus (Opening of Prometheus Bound)Aeschylus (attributed), Prometheus Bound, lines 1–127

ca. 460–424 BCE

The play opens with Kratos, Bia, Hephaestus, and the bound Prometheus arriving at a desolate Scythian mountain. Kratos speaks the first line, ordering the gods to stop: 'Here we have reached the edge of the earth, the Scythian land, a wilderness that is untrodden of men.' He commands Hephaestus to chain Prometheus to the rock with 'unbreakable adamant' fetters. When Hephaestus hesitates, reluctant to chain a fellow god, Kratos berates him: 'But why do you waste time and pity him in vain? Is not the god whom all gods hate worthy of your hate?' He accuses Hephaestus of being a slave to his art — 'You could not let go of your art, you hateful thing.' Kratos warns Hephaestus that if he does not comply, Zeus will punish him. Throughout the binding, Kratos jeers at Prometheus' suffering, telling him that he will never escape: 'So now, you are wise, but you'll learn that your cleverness is folly against Zeus.' The Titanomachy (Styx's Alliance)Hesiod, Theogony, lines 383–403; Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 1.2.4

ca. 700 BCE (Hesiodic composition); later collated in Apollodorus

During the ten-year Titanomachy, Zeus called all the gods to Olympus and promised that anyone who fought alongside him would retain his rights and offices. Styx, Kratos' mother, answered first, bringing her children: Zelus, Nike, Kratos, and Bia. Because of this loyalty, Zeus honored Styx as the 'great oath of the gods' — the water by which all gods swear — and decreed that Kratos and his siblings 'would dwell with him always.' This story serves as the foundation myth for Kratos' eternal presence at Zeus' throne. It explains both his origin (the reward for loyalty) and his character (the enforcement arm of the new Olympian order). Kratos gains permanent residence on Olympus and becomes one of Zeus' four perpetual companions. The Styx itself becomes the sacred oath of all gods — a river with the power to punish perjury with a year of coma-like unconsciousness and exile from divine gatherings.

Accounts II
The Punishment of IxionLate fifth-century BCE Attic red-figure skyphos (possibly after Euripides' lost Ixion)

ca. 420–400 BCE

Ixion, king of the Lapiths, attempted to seduce Hera. Zeus punished him by binding him to a flaming wheel that rolls eternally through the sky. The skyphos depicts Kratos and Bia actively participated in this binding, physically restraining Ixion as the wheel is applied. This confirms Kratos' role as Zeus' executioner extends beyond Prometheus to other divine and mortal transgressors. Ixion is bound to the wheel for eternity. The imagery echoes the binding of Prometheus, suggesting Kratos and Bia were standard instruments of Zeus' punitive justice.

Electra's Invocation (Libation Bearers)Aeschylus, Libation Bearers (Choephoroi), lines 244–245

458 BCE

In the second play of the Oresteia, Electra — the daughter of Agamemnon — prays to her father's tomb for aid in avenging his murder by Clytemnestra and Aegisthus. She calls upon 'Kratos (Strength) and Dike (Justice) and Zeus the third, greatest of all, be with me.' This is a remarkable invocation because Kratos is rarely addressed in prayer — he has no cult — but here Electra calls upon his abstract power as a force of vengeance, alongside Justice itself. The triad of Kratos, Dike, and Zeus suggests that righteous violence requires the combination of strength, justice, and divine authority. Electra's prayer is answered — Orestes kills Clytemnestra and Aegisthus. Kratos is here invoked not as a character but as an abstract force of strength that enables vengeance.
