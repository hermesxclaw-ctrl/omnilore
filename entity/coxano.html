At the crossroads, when the moon is dark, I will meet you. I will give you what you ask — the gold, the woman, the hands that play the violin like fire. And when you have it all, I will come for what is mine.

Who It Is

The Čoxano (also Choxani, Beng, or simply 'Devil' in some dialects) is the Romani devil-demon — a trickster-tempter figure who appears at crossroads, offering pacts for wealth, love, or power. Unlike the Christian Satan (a cosmic adversary), the Čoxano is more localized and transactional: he makes deals, and the deal is binding. He appears as a handsome dark stranger or a man in black with one foot of a horse or goat — the cloven hoof that betrays his nature. The Čoxano can also appear in dreams, offering temptation that the dreamer must resist. In Romani folklore, deals with the Čoxano are genuinely powerful — the promised wealth or love materializes — but the cost is always disproportionate: the bargain-maker loses their family, their health, or their soul. The Čoxano can be outwitted, however, and many Romani folk tales feature clever heroes (often musicians) who trick the Devil at his own game — winning the pact's benefits without paying the price. The famous violinist's pact with the Devil (popularized in the West by the legend of Paganini and the song 'The Devil Went Down to Georgia') has deep Romani roots: the Roma were Europe's most famous musicians for centuries, and the suspicion that their musical virtuosity was demonically sourced was a widespread prejudice. The Čoxano is thus doubly significant: as a genuine folk-belief figure and as a reflection of the outsider status imposed on the Roma — the slur that their talents must be unnatural. The Čoxano survives in Romani language and culture, and versions of the figure (sometimes called 'Beng') appear in Anglo-Romani and Welsh Romani traditions.
