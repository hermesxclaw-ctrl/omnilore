At the crossroads, when the moon is dark, I will meet you. I will give you what you ask — the gold, the woman, the hands that play the violin like fire. And when you have it all, I will come for what is mine.

Who It Is

The Čoxano (also Choxani, Beng, or simply 'Devil' in some dialects) is the Romani devil-demon — a trickster-tempter figure who appears at crossroads, offering pacts for wealth, love, or power. Unlike the Christian Satan (a cosmic adversary), the Čoxano is more localized and transactional: he makes deals, and the deal is binding. He appears as a handsome dark stranger or a man in black with one foot of a horse or goat — the cloven hoof that betrays his nature. The Čoxano can also appear in dreams, offering temptation that the dreamer must resist. In Romani folklore, deals with the Čoxano are genuinely powerful — the promised wealth or love materializes — but the cost is always disproportionate: the bargain-maker loses their family, their health, or their soul. The Čoxano can be outwitted, however, and many Romani folk tales feature clever heroes (often musicians) who trick the Devil at his own game — winning the pact's benefits without paying the price. The famous violinist's pact with the Devil (popularized in the West by the legend of Paganini and the song 'The Devil Went Down to Georgia') has deep Romani roots: the Roma were Europe's most famous musicians for centuries, and the suspicion that their musical virtuosity was demonically sourced was a widespread prejudice. The Čoxano is thus doubly significant: as a genuine folk-belief figure and as a reflection of the outsider status imposed on the Roma — the slur that their talents must be unnatural. The Čoxano survives in Romani language and culture, and versions of the figure (sometimes called 'Beng') appear in Anglo-Romani and Welsh Romani traditions.

Form

Čoxano is described as a shape-shifter, but his most common form is that of a tall, dark man with unnaturally bright eyes, often dressed in the worn clothes of a traveler or a foreign gentleman. He may appear as a stranger at the crossroads, a shadowy figure at the edge of a fire's light, or, in some tales, as a handsome young man who charms his victims. He is often accompanied by a smell of smoke or of the damp earth of the grave. He is not described as having hooves, horns, or a tail in the Romani tradition; these are later, Christian-influenced additions.

Powers

Shape-shifting The ability to assume various forms — a human stranger, an animal (often a black dog or a bird), or a shadow. This is used to approach victims, to spy, and to escape. Attested in oral tradition His transformations are deceptive but not perfect; he can be recognized by his eyes or by a telltale sign (e.g., a limp, a cloven shadow, or an inability to enter a sacred space). The extent of his shape-shifting varies between communities; some say he can only appear in human form.

Pact-making The power to grant wishes, wealth, or success in exchange for a binding agreement. The agreement is always kept literally, but the interpretation is always twisted to the detriment of the mortal. Attested He cannot force a person to make a pact; the agreement must be freely given. He is also bound by the terms of the pact once made, although he will exploit any ambiguity. In some tales, he is bound by the word of a holy man or by the sign of the cross, suggesting Christian influence on the tradition.

Illusion and deception The ability to create illusions, to lead travelers astray, to make a person see what is not there, and to hide his own true nature. Attested His illusions can be broken by clear sight, by the sound of a bell, or by a person who is not afraid. None noted.

Curses and misfortune He can bring misfortune, sickness, or madness upon those who break a pact or who offend him. Attested His curses are typically a consequence of a broken agreement or a personal slight, not random acts of malice. None noted.

Behavior & Habitat

Čoxano is a tempter and a bargainer. He does not force himself upon his victims; rather, he offers them a deal — wealth, power, love, or revenge — in exchange for their loyalty, their name, or a piece of their soul. He is a master of loopholes and contracts, and his promises are always kept in a way that is ultimately ruinous to the one who made the pact. He is also a trickster in a broader sense, playing pranks on travelers, leading them astray, and causing mischief in the boundary spaces he inhabits. He is not purely malicious; he is a force of chaos and consequence, rewarding the clever and punishing the foolish.

Accounts

A young fiddler, poor and in love, meets a dark man at a crossroads who offers him the skill to play like no other, so that he might win the heart of a wealthy man's daughter. The fiddler agrees, and his playing becomes legendary. He wins the girl, but his music is now a hollow echo of his former self, and his success brings him nothing but emptiness. When he tries to break the pact, Čoxano comes for him, and the fiddler is never seen again, though some say his ghost can still be heard playing at the crossroads on moonless nights.

A merchant, desperate to outdo his rivals, meets Čoxano at a crossroads at midnight. He asks for the secret to boundless wealth. Čoxano tells him that every coin he spends will return to him tenfold, but that he must never give anything away for free. The merchant becomes immensely rich, but his heart hardens. He turns away beggars, cheats his partners, and neglects his family. In the end, he is so consumed by his wealth that he cannot enjoy it, and he dies alone and miserable. Čoxano appears at his deathbed to claim the merchant's name, which was the true price of the pact.

A clever girl, the daughter of a poor widow, is approached by Čoxano, who offers to make her a queen. She agrees, but only if he can answer three riddles. He answers the first two, but the third — 'What is the sound of one hand clapping?' — he cannot. The girl laughs and says that if he cannot answer that, he is not so clever after all. Čoxano, outwitted, is bound by the rules of the pact and must leave her alone. The girl goes on to marry a prince, not through Čoxano's help, but through her own wit.

Field Notes

☵ The name Čoxano is related to the Romani word for 'thief', making him literally 'the Thieving One'.

☵ In some tales, Čoxano can be outwitted, not by prayer or holy water, but by pure wit — a clever riddle or a trick can make him back off, bound by his own rules.

☵ He is a figure of the 'in-between' — the crossroads, the threshold, the moment between day and night — places where the normal rules of the world are suspended.

Scholarly Debates
Christian Devil or autonomous spirit?
Čoxano is a local manifestation of the Christian Devil, a demon who tempts souls into sin. ⇌ Čoxano is an autonomous, pre-Christian trickster spirit of the crossroads, adapted to survive in a Christian world.
Gender of the figure
Čoxano is always male; Čoxani is a separate, female figure. ⇌ Čoxano/Čoxani are the same being, with the gender varying by region or by the teller of the tale.

What We Don't Know

Detailed, modern, and accessible monographs on Čoxano are rare. Most references are embedded in broader works on Romani culture or in 19th-century ethnographic accounts that are difficult to access and may be biased. The oral tradition is the primary source, but it is not systematically collected or archived. The relationship between Čoxano and Čoxani, and the regional variations of the figure, require more research.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Čoxano as the ultimate venture capitalist of folklore. He's not evil in the sense of wanting to destroy the world; he's a deal-maker who always gets his equity. He's the fine print in the contract, the hidden fee in the bargain, the 'terms and conditions' you never read. He's a reminder that every choice has a cost, and that the cleverest person in the room is the one who can see the loophole.
