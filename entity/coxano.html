Who It Is

Before the devil had a pitchfork and a PR team, there was Čoxano — a shadow at the crossroads who doesn't want your soul so much as your name. He's the original loophole artist, the fine print in every desperate bargain, the reason your grandmother told you never to take a stranger's offer at midnight. Forget Faust; the real cautionary tale is about a fiddler who sold his heart for a song and got a hollow echo in return.

Čoxano is a tempter and a bargainer. He does not force himself upon his victims; rather, he offers them a deal — wealth, power, love, or revenge — in exchange for their loyalty, their name, or a piece of their soul. He is a master of loopholes and contracts, and his promises are always kept in a way that is ultimately ruinous to the one who made the pact. He is also a trickster in a broader sense, playing pranks on travelers, leading them astray, and causing mischief in the boundary spaces he inhabits. Powers
Shape-shifting

The ability to assume various forms — a human stranger, an animal (often a black dog or a bird), or a shadow. This is used to approach victims, to spy, and to escape.

Limit: His transformations are deceptive but not perfect; he can be recognized by his eyes or by a telltale sign (e.g., a limp, a cloven shadow, or an inability to enter a sacred space).

Pact-making

The power to grant wishes, wealth, or success in exchange for a binding agreement. The agreement is always kept literally, but the interpretation is always twisted to the detriment of the mortal.

Limit: He cannot force a person to make a pact; the agreement must be freely given. He is also bound by the terms of the pact once made, although he will exploit any ambiguity.

Illusion and deception

The ability to create illusions, to lead travelers astray, to make a person see what is not there, and to hide his own true nature.

Limit: His illusions can be broken by clear sight, by the sound of a bell, or by a person who is not afraid.

Curses and misfortune

He can bring misfortune, sickness, or madness upon those who break a pact or who offend him.

Limit: His curses are typically a consequence of a broken agreement or a personal slight, not random acts of malice.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Čoxano is described as a shape-shifter, but his most common form is that of a tall, dark man with unnaturally bright eyes, often dressed in the worn clothes of a traveler or a foreign gentleman. He may appear as a stranger at the crossroads, a shadowy figure at the edge of a fire's light, or, in some tales, as a handsome young man who charms his victims. Symbols — The crossroads (the point of decision and danger), the boundary stone, the threshold, the fire's edge, the road, the shadow. His symbols are those of transition and the liminal, of places where the normal rules of the world are suspended.

Domains — Crossroads, night, boundaries, thresholds, barrows, abandoned places, the space between the village and the wild. He is also associated with the pact itself — the moment of agreement, the exchange of promises. His realm is the 'in-between' where rules are ambiguous and the outcome is uncertain.

Accounts I
The Fiddler's BargainRomani oral tradition (recorded in 20th-century ethnographic collections)

Undated (traditional)

A young fiddler, poor and in love, meets a dark man at a crossroads who offers him the skill to play like no other, so that he might win the heart of a wealthy man's daughter. The fiddler agrees, and his playing becomes legendary. He wins the girl, but his music is now a hollow echo of his former self, and his success brings him nothing but emptiness. When he tries to break the pact, Čoxano comes for him, and the fiddler is never seen again, though some say his ghost can still be heard playing at the crossroads on moonless nights. The fiddler loses his soul and his life, a cautionary tale about the price of ambition and the deception of easy success.

Accounts II
The Greedy MerchantRomani oral tradition (recorded in 20th-century ethnographic collections)

Undated (traditional)

A merchant, desperate to outdo his rivals, meets Čoxano at a crossroads at midnight. He asks for the secret to boundless wealth. Čoxano tells him that every coin he spends will return to him tenfold, but that he must never give anything away for free. The merchant becomes immensely rich, but his heart hardens. He turns away beggars, cheats his partners, and neglects his family. In the end, he is so consumed by his wealth that he cannot enjoy it, and he dies alone and miserable. Čoxano appears at his deathbed to claim the merchant's name, which was the true price of the pact. The merchant's wealth becomes a curse, and he loses his humanity and his name.

The Clever GirlRomani oral tradition (recorded in 20th-century ethnographic collections)

Undated (traditional)

A clever girl, the daughter of a poor widow, is approached by Čoxano, who offers to make her a queen. She agrees, but only if he can answer three riddles. He answers the first two, but the third — 'What is the sound of one hand clapping?' — he cannot. The girl laughs and says that if he cannot answer that, he is not so clever after all. Čoxano, outwitted, is bound by the rules of the pact and must leave her alone. The girl goes on to marry a prince, not through Čoxano's help, but through her own wit. The girl escapes the pact through her own intelligence, a tale that celebrates wit over supernatural power.

Čoxano is the Romani name for Satan. — While Čoxano is a demonic or diabolical figure, he is not Satan. He is a trickster and a tempter, but he is not the ruler of Hell or the embodiment of absolute evil. He is a more local, more ambiguous figure.

All Roma are devil-worshippers because they believe in Čoxano. — This is a harmful stereotype. Čoxano is a figure in folklore, not an object of worship. Romani people, like all people, have diverse beliefs, and many are devout Christians, Muslims, or other faiths.

Čoxano is the same as the 'crossroads demon' of African-American folklore (e.g., the one Robert Johnson met). — The two figures share a similar archetype (making a pact at a crossroads), but they are from different cultures with different histories. The African-American figure is rooted in the experience of slavery and the blues, while Čoxano is rooted in Romani history and culture.
