Who It Is

He sits without eyes, without limbs, a red dome that cannot be knocked down. Paint one eye when you make a vow, the other when you keep it — and if you fail, he keeps staring. In Japan the Daruma is everywhere: on shop counters, taxi dashboards, temple shelves stacked a thousand deep, his glare both comic and severe. He is a wish, a warning, and a monk who supposedly stared at a wall for nine years until his legs withered away.

Bodhidharma in hagiography: austere, taciturn, iconoclastic, anti-ritual. Refuses to flatter Emperor Wu ('No merit' — mu kudoku). Demands proof of sincerity from Huike (who cuts off arm in snow). Practices biguan (wall-gazing) — silent illumination without scriptures. In Japanese folklore, Daruma is gruff but protective, a temperamental luck god who punishes laziness (if you don't fulfill vow, he remains one-eyed) and rewards perseverance. Folk sayings: 'Daruma ni me wo ireru' (to put eyes in Daruma) = to set a goal.

Powers
Biguan (Wall-Gazing Meditation)

Nine years facing a wall in Shaolin cave without moving, attaining silent awakening beyond scriptures. In some versions eyelids fall off from staying awake.

Limit: Causes physical atrophy — legend explains loss of legs/eyelids. Not an active power but an ascetic feat requiring total stillness. Later Zen interprets as metaphor for 'just sitting' (shikantaza).

One-Reed Yangtze Crossing (Ichii Tokai)

After being rejected by Emperor Wu, crosses the Yangtze River standing on a single reed, demonstrating levitation/lightness and detachment.

Limit: One-time miraculous travel, not repeatable flight; requires prior enlightenment. In some variants, reed is given by a lay supporter.

Okiagari Resilience (Seven Times Down, Eight Times Up)

As doll, self-righting physical property: weighted bottom returns upright when knocked over. Symbolically grants perseverance, wish fulfillment, and recovery from failure to owner who makes a vow.

Limit: Symbolic/psychological only; requires owner to paint second eye upon goal completion and traditionally burn doll at temple at year end (Daruma kuyō). No autonomous agency.

Transmission of Dharma and Esoteric Insight

Ability to transmit awakening mind-to-mind outside scriptures (kyōge betsuden, furyū monji), recognize true successor (Huike), and compose verses on non-dependence on words.

Limit: Transmission requires worthy recipient who demonstrates sincerity (Huike's arm). Cannot be forced or bought. Textual attribution disputed — Erru Sixing Lun likely compiled by disciples.

Post-Mortem Apparition and Empty Coffin

After death and burial, encountered alive on the Pamir Mountains by traveler Songyun, carrying one shoe; upon opening tomb, only one straw sandal remains.

Limit: Posthumous miracle proving transcendence of death; not available during life. Requires prior poisoning/martyrdom.

Accounts I
The Voyage from the Western Regions to ChinaXu Gaoseng Zhuan (Daoxuan, 645) and later Zutang Ji (952)

Legend set c. 470-520 CE; text 7th century

Bodhidharma, third son of a South Indian Brahmin king (or Pallava king per later versions), converts to Buddhism under Prajñātāra, travels by sea for three years to arrive at Guangzhou (or Nanjing) during the Liu Song / Liang dynasty. He presents himself as a 150-year-old foreign monk seeking to transmit the Mahayana teaching of direct insight. Establishes Bodhidharma as 28th Indian Patriarch and 1st Chinese Patriarch of Chan, legitimizing Chan lineage. Maritime arrival story justifies Zen's claim to direct Indian transmission bypassing gradual scholastic Buddhism.

The Interview with Emperor Wu of LiangJingde Chuandeng Lu (1004), juan 3; Blue Cliff Record (Biyan Lu) Case 1

Legend set c. 520 CE; text 11th century

Emperor Wu, proud patron who built temples and copied sutras, asks Bodhidharma what merit he has earned. Bodhidharma answers 'No merit' (mu kudoku) — good deeds with attachment earn no true merit. Emperor asks the highest holy truth; Bodhidharma replies 'Vast emptiness, nothing holy.' Asked 'Who stands before me?' he says 'I don't know' and leaves. The emperor is nonplussed; Bodhidharma crosses the Yangtze on a reed to the north. Foundational koan for Zen iconoclasm: merit without attachment, emptiness beyond holiness, and rejection of imperial patronage. Establishes northward transmission to Shaolin rather than court Buddhism.

Nine Years Facing the Wall at ShaolinXu Gaoseng Zhuan; Jingde Chuandeng Lu; Shaolin stele inscriptions

Legend set c. 520-529 CE; texts 7th-11th century

Bodhidharma retreats to a cave on Mount Song behind Shaolin Monastery and sits facing a wall (biguan) for nine years in silent meditation. Monks consider him mad or possessed. His legs atrophy and fall off (folk explanation for legless Daruma doll). In one Japanese folk elaboration, he cuts off his eyelids to stay awake; they fall to earth and sprout as the first tea plants, explaining monks' use of tea to stay awake during zazen. Defines Zen practice as wall-gazing / just sitting (zazen, shikantaza). Shaolin cave becomes pilgrimage site. Iconographic source for legless, eyelid-less Daruma doll and association with tea culture (Eisai's Kissa Yōjōki links tea and Zen).

Accounts II
Huike Cuts Off His Arm in the SnowXu Gaoseng Zhuan; Jingde Chuandeng Lu; Denkoroku (Transmission of Light, Keizan 1300)

Legend set c. 528 CE; texts 7th-14th century

Seeking teaching, the scholar-monk Huike (Eka) stands all night in snow outside Bodhidharma's cave. To prove sincerity, he cuts off his left arm and presents it. Bodhidharma asks what he seeks; Huike says his mind is not at peace. Bodhidharma says 'Bring me your mind and I will pacify it.' Huike cannot find it. Bodhidharma: 'There, I have pacified your mind.' Huike awakens and becomes Second Patriarch, receiving Bodhidharma's robe and bowl. Establishes Chan transmission criteria: non-verbal awakening, extreme sincerity, and mind-to-mind succession. Justifies robe-and-bowl lineage and explains Huike's one-armed iconography. Later criticized as glorifying self-harm.

Death by Poison and the Single Shoe in the CoffinXu Gaoseng Zhuan; Jingde Chuandeng Lu

Legend set c. 532-536 CE; text 7th-11th century

Jealous rivals poison Bodhidharma (or he dies naturally at ~150 years old) and he is buried at Bear's Ear Mountain. Three years later, the official Songyun meets Bodhidharma alive on the Pamir Mountains, walking barefoot with one shoe in hand, returning to India. Songyun reports this; the tomb is opened and found empty except for one straw sandal. Proves Bodhidharma's transcendence of death and links him to immortal arhat lore. 'One-shoe Bodhidharma' becomes major painting subject symbolizing leaving attachments behind. Reinforces westward return motif — teaching transmitted, master departs.

From Monk to Tumbler: The Birth of the Daruma Doll at ShōrinzanShōrinzan Daruma-ji temple engi; Takasaki domain records; Edo-period hariko craft histories

1697 temple founding; doll innovation 1780s-1800s (Edo late period)

During the Tenmei famine (1780s), Shōrinzan Daruma-ji abbot Shinetsu, at Takasaki, taught farmers to make papier-mâché okiagari-koboshi dolls modeled on Bodhidharma's legless, resilient image as a side-income and talisman. The doll merged with existing tumbler toys and smallpox-protection charms (red color wards disease). Painting one eye on making a vow and the other on fulfillment (kaigan) ritualized perseverance. The Takasaki Daruma Market (Daruma-ichi) on New Year became one of Japan's great doll markets. Created Japan's most ubiquitous folk talisman (engimono), with Takasaki now producing ~80% of Daruma dolls. Annual Daruma kuyō (temple burning of old dolls) ritualizes renewal. Doll becomes secular business/political luck charm divorced from doctrinal Zen.

Daruma dolls are Buddhist gods to be worshipped — Daruma dolls are folk votive charms (engimono), not canonical Buddhist icons. Orthodox Zen temples treat Bodhidharma as patriarch to be venerated, but the doll's wish-making function is folk practice, often criticized by Zen clergy as superstition. Many owners are secular.

You must always buy a red Daruma — Traditional Takasaki Daruma are red (cinnabar wards smallpox/evil), but modern artisans produce five-color goshiki Daruma (red, white, gold, black, etc.) for different wishes: gold for money, white for love, black for business. Color symbolism is modern marketing layered on folk base.

Daruma is a Japanese invention with no foreign roots — The figure is explicitly foreign — an Indian/Central Asian monk — and Japanese sources stress his 'blue eyes, red beard' exoticism. The doll is Japanese craft, but the person is imported via China. Japanese folklore embraces his foreignness as mark of authenticity.

Bodhidharma brought Zen and kung fu together from India as a complete system — Zen as a school develops in China centuries after Bodhidharma's putative lifetime; Shaolin martial arts develop a millennium later. The Bodhidharma-kung fu link is a 17th-century retrojection, not historical transmission.

Painting both eyes at once is correct — Traditional ritual is to paint one eye (usually left) when making a vow/goal, and the second eye only upon fulfillment. Dolls with both eyes blank are unsold stock; dolls with both eyes filled are completed vows ready for kuyō burning. Painting both at purchase voids the vow logic.
