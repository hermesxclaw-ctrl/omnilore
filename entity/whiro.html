Whiro

Lord of Darkness; God of Evil and Death; The Great Lizard

also known asWhiro-te-tipuaWhiro-te-tupuaWhiro the Great LizardLord of DarknessGod of Evil and DeathWhiro of the Underworld
TypeEvil/Death Deity
CultureMāori
Who It Is
Form

Whiro is typically depicted as a lizard-like creature, often described as a giant reptile or a monstrous lizard. In some carvings, he is shown with a serpentine body, multiple heads, or sharp claws, reflecting his malevolent nature. He is said to inhabit the underworld, where he appears as a dark, shadowy figure that instills fear. Unlike other Māori deities who are often anthropomorphic, Whiro's reptilian form underscores his alien and sinister character, setting him apart from the more human-like gods. Some traditions describe him as having the ability to shape-shift, taking on the form of a lizard, a bird, or even a human to deceive his victims.

Powers

Control over darkness and the underworld Whiro has dominion over Te Pō, the realm of darkness, and can summon shadows, night, and the forces of the underworld to do his bidding. He can shroud the world in darkness, causing fear and confusion.

Infliction of disease and curses Whiro is responsible for sending diseases, plagues, and curses (makutu) upon humanity. He can afflict individuals with illness, madness, or death, often through the agency of lizards or insects.

Shape-shifting Whiro can transform into various forms, including a lizard, a bird, or a human, to deceive and manipulate others. This ability allows him to move unseen among mortals and gods.

Influence over malevolent creatures Whiro commands a host of evil beings, including lizards, insects, and taniwha (water monsters), which he sends to carry out his malicious plans. He can also incite wars and conflicts by whispering into the ears of mortals.

Accounts

In the beginning, the god Tāne ascended to the heavens to obtain the three baskets of knowledge (ngā kete wānanga) from the supreme being Io. Whiro, jealous of Tāne's success, sent a flock of birds and insects to steal the baskets. Tāne, with the help of his allies, fought off the attackers, but Whiro's forces managed to scatter some of the knowledge, which fell to the earth and became the source of evil and ignorance among humans. Eventually, Tāne defeated Whiro and cast him into the underworld, but Whiro continues to send his minions to disrupt the world.

Whiro, along with his brother Tura, was involved in a fratricidal conflict. Hua, a mortal chief, incited his people to seek vengeance against Whiro's tribe. Whiro induced his brother Tura to accompany him in a canoe, but under a magical spell, the canoe was destroyed, leading to a great battle. The conflict ended with the death of Hua and his tribe at the battle of Te Potiki-kai-rororo, where Whiro's forces emerged victorious, but at a great cost of life.

According to some traditions, Whiro was responsible for introducing disease and death into the world. After his defeat by Tāne, he retreated to the underworld and created various ailments, such as fever, consumption, and skin diseases, which he sent to afflict humanity. He also taught sorcerers the art of makutu (curses), which they used to harm their enemies. This story explains the origin of illness and the need for healing rituals.

Scholarly Debates
Relationship to Tāne
Whiro is the brother of Tāne, as stated in most sources. ⇌ Some traditions place Whiro as a cousin or unrelated deity, depending on the tribal genealogy.
Role in creation of evil
Whiro is the embodiment of all evil, responsible for all ills. ⇌ Some stories suggest that evil originated from the scattered knowledge from the baskets, not directly from Whiro's actions.
Physical form
Whiro is depicted as a lizard-like creature. ⇌ In some carvings, he is shown as a human-like figure with reptilian features, or as a bird.
What We Don't Know

There is limited primary source material on Whiro, as much of Māori mythology was transmitted orally and later recorded by European ethnographers. The exact origins of his cult and the full extent of his worship are unclear. Some stories, such as the battle of Te Potiki-kai-rororo, are only briefly mentioned in a single source, leaving many details unknown. Further research into tribal variations and archaeological evidence could shed light on his historical significance.

A way to hold it in your mind

Did you know that in Māori mythology, the god of evil is not a demon from hell, but a lizard-like deity who dwells in the underworld and sends diseases to afflict humanity? Whiro, the Lord of Darkness, is a figure of fear and awe, yet his stories reveal deep insights into the Māori worldview of balance between light and dark.
