Who It Is

Did you know that in Māori mythology, the god of evil is not a demon from hell, but a lizard-like deity who dwells in the underworld and sends diseases to afflict humanity? Whiro, the Lord of Darkness, is a figure of fear and awe, yet his stories reveal deep insights into the Māori worldview of balance between light and dark.

Whiro is the embodiment of all evil, malice, and death. He is described as envious, vengeful, and cunning, constantly seeking to undermine the works of the gods and bring suffering to humanity. Unlike his brother Tāne, who represents life, light, and knowledge, Whiro delights in chaos, disease, and destruction. He is known to send birds and insects to steal the baskets of knowledge from Tāne, and he incites wars and conflicts among mortals. His personality is one of relentless opposition to the cosmic order, making him a trickster figure but with a purely malevolent intent.

Powers
Control over darkness and the underworld

Whiro has dominion over Te Pō, the realm of darkness, and can summon shadows, night, and the forces of the underworld to do his bidding. He can shroud the world in darkness, causing fear and confusion.

Infliction of disease and curses

Whiro is responsible for sending diseases, plagues, and curses (makutu) upon humanity. He can afflict individuals with illness, madness, or death, often through the agency of lizards or insects.

Limit:

Shape-shifting

Whiro can transform into various forms, including a lizard, a bird, or a human, to deceive and manipulate others. This ability allows him to move unseen among mortals and gods.

Influence over malevolent creatures

Whiro commands a host of evil beings, including lizards, insects, and taniwha (water monsters), which he sends to carry out his malicious plans. He can also incite wars and conflicts by whispering into the ears of mortals.

Accounts I
The Theft of the Baskets of KnowledgeMāori oral tradition, recorded in various ethnographic texts

Pre-colonial, oral tradition

In the beginning, the god Tāne ascended to the heavens to obtain the three baskets of knowledge (ngā kete wānanga) from the supreme being Io. Whiro, jealous of Tāne's success, sent a flock of birds and insects to steal the baskets. Tāne, with the help of his allies, fought off the attackers, but Whiro's forces managed to scatter some of the knowledge, which fell to the earth and became the source of evil and ignorance among humans. Eventually, Tāne defeated Whiro and cast him into the underworld, but Whiro continues to send his minions to disrupt the world. Whiro was banished to the underworld, but the scattered knowledge caused the introduction of evil and suffering into the world. The battle established the eternal conflict between light and darkness, knowledge and ignorance.

Accounts II
The Battle of Te Potiki-kai-rororoPantheon.org - Whiro | Facts, Information, and Mythology

Mythological, pre-colonial

Whiro, along with his brother Tura, was involved in a fratricidal conflict. Hua, a mortal chief, incited his people to seek vengeance against Whiro's tribe. Whiro induced his brother Tura to accompany him in a canoe, but under a magical spell, the canoe was destroyed, leading to a great battle. The conflict ended with the death of Hua and his tribe at the battle of Te Potiki-kai-rororo, where Whiro's forces emerged victorious, but at a great cost of life. The battle resulted in the annihilation of Hua's tribe, but it also highlighted Whiro's manipulative nature, as he used his brother and others for his own ends. The story serves as a cautionary tale about the destructive power of vengeance and evil.

Whiro's Creation of DiseaseMythology Vault - Whiro Lord of Malice

Mythological, pre-colonial

According to some traditions, Whiro was responsible for introducing disease and death into the world. After his defeat by Tāne, he retreated to the underworld and created various ailments, such as fever, consumption, and skin diseases, which he sent to afflict humanity. He also taught sorcerers the art of makutu (curses), which they used to harm their enemies. This story explains the origin of illness and the need for healing rituals. Disease became a permanent part of the human condition, and the practice of makutu was established. Healers and tohunga (priests) developed rituals to counteract Whiro's influence.
