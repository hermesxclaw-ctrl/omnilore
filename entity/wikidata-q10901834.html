Who It Is

On Yang Mountain there waits a creature that should not be able to move at all — a jackal's body with a human face, a pair of bird's wings folded against its back, and no feet. It advances like a snake, dragging itself forward, and when it opens its mouth the sound is not a howl but a human scolding, a sharp chiding shout. Wherever that voice is heard, the old guide says, the town below will drown.

Attested behavior is minimal: inhabits the Yang River (Yangshui) on stony, barren Yang Mountain; moves in serpentine fashion; emits a loud human-like scolding cry; appears to human settlements as a presage, not as an attacker. No speech, intelligence, moral agency, or social behavior described. It is not said to cause the flood, only to be seen before it (見則其邑大水 - when seen, the town will have great water). Powers
Hydrological Omen (Flood Presage)

Appearance predicts imminent great flood (大水) in the nearby town/settlement (邑). The power is mantic, not causal in early text: the beast is a sign, not explicitly the agent of inundation. Later popular reading often upgrades this to flood-causing.

Limit: No control over timing or scale specified; omen is binary (seen = flood). No countermeasure given in text. Does not protect from flood or cause other disasters. Later adaptations invent water-manipulation abilities.

Serpentine Locomotion

Moves like a snake (其行如蛇) despite having a quadruped-like body and bird wings. Implies legless or vestigial-limbed undulation, an anomalous mode of movement that marks it as strange (guai).

Limit: No speed, endurance, or terrain limits given. Wings do not appear to be used for flight in early text; locomotion is described as crawling, not flying. Illustrations show both crawling and low hovering.

Human-like Vocalization (Chìhū Cry)

Cry is likened to a human scolding or shouting angrily (其音如叱呼 / 詈呼). The comparison emphasizes loud, harsh, articulate-adjacent sound, distinct from animal howls. In omenology, anomalous human-like sounds increase prodigy status.

Limit: Not language or speech; no intelligible words, no mimicry of specific phrases. No compulsion or curse effect described. Range not specified.

Avian Wings (Guo Pu Addition)

Possesses bird wings (鳥翼) on a mammalian/serpentine body, creating a chimera. Wings are not said to enable flight in early sources but imply potential aerial ability in later visual tradition.

Limit: No flight feat described; no mention of taking off, soaring, or wind generation. If wings are vestigial, they may be ornamental prodigy features.

Form & Signs

Accounts I
The Many Huàshé of Yang Mountain — The Base Omen AccountShanhaijing, Zhong Shan Jing, Juan 5 (Yangshan tiao)

Text compiled c. 4th century BCE - 1st century CE; Yangshan entry belongs to Zhong Shan Jing stratum often dated to late Warring States / early Western Han

The guide describes Yang Mountain as rocky and barren, source of the Yang River flowing east into the Yi. 'Within it there are many huashé. Their form is like a jackal with a human face, their movement like a snake, their sound like a person scolding. When they are seen, the town will have a great flood.' No hero, no slaying, no dialogue; the entry is a terse geographic prodigy notice. The flood is collective, affecting the yi (walled town/settlement) downstream, not a single victim. The passage ends without remedy or moral. Establishes Huàshé as a canonical flood omen and fixes its iconography and habitat for all later tradition. Provides the locus classicus cited by every leishu, commentary, and modern translation. No narrative resolution; the omen remains a warning sign within the Shanhaijing's system of correlative disaster prediction.

Guo Pu's Commentary — Codifying the ChimeraGuo Pu (郭璞, 276-324 CE) Shanhaijing Zhu

Eastern Jin, c. 310-324 CE

Guo Pu, the great exegete of anomalies, annotates the Yangshan entry: 'Huashé, human face, jackal body, bird wings, snake movement, sound like chìhū.' He does not add a new story but glosses the omen as a standard hydromantic prodigy and supplies the wing detail that will define the visual tradition. His note is terse, characteristic of his philological style, and is accompanied in later printed editions by a small woodcut illustration of the beast. Guo's authority makes the winged version canonical. Creates the canonical composite image (human face + jackal body + bird wings + snake crawl) reproduced for 1,700 years. Elevates Huàshé from a one-line notice to a stable bestiary entry with visual identity. Establishes the interpretive frame: omen, not demon or deity.

Encyclopedic Transmission — Taiping Yulan and the Leishu TraditionTaiping Yulan (太平御覽, 983 CE), Juan 890; echoed in Taiping Guangji, Gujin Tushu Jicheng (1726)

Song dynasty (983 CE) through Qing (1726 CE)

The Song imperial encyclopedia Taiping Yulan copies the Zhong Shan Jing passage verbatim under the category of beasts (shou bu), preserving the flood-omen notice without new narrative: 'Huashé... seen then great water.' The entry is stripped of geographic context and presented as a standalone prodigy. Ming and Qing leishu (e.g., Tushu Jicheng) repeat it and add a standardized illustration. This transmission shows Huàshé surviving not through storytelling but through bureaucratic knowledge organization — a sign that officials should watch for. Ensures Huàshé's survival even when Shanhaijing manuscripts were rare, making it available to Ming-Qing novelists, playwrights, and illustrators. Demonstrates that Huàshé was classified as a verifiable omen beast, not folklore fiction, in the imperial knowledge system.

Accounts II
The Illustrated Shanhaijing — Ming-Qing Visual NarrativeHu Wenhuan (胡文煥) Shanhaijing Tu (1593) and Jiang Yinghao (蔣應鎬) Shanhaijing Tu (c. 1667, reprinted in Qing)

Ming dynasty (late 16th c.) to Qing dynasty (17th-18th c.)

In the first widely printed illustrated Shanhaijings, Huàshé receives a full-page woodcut: a long-bodied, low-slung beast with a bearded, almost Confucian human face, feathered wings folded on its back, clawed forelimbs, and a serpentine tail dragging behind. The image is accompanied by the classic text and Guo Pu's note. Jiang Yinghao's more refined edition adds wavy water lines beneath the beast and a tiny walled town in the distance to visualize the flood omen. These images create a narrative vignette — the beast emerging from rocky Yang Mountain while dark clouds gather — that earlier text only implied. The illustrations become the source for all modern popular images. Transforms a textual omen into a visual monster with lasting iconographic stability. Establishes the 'human-faced jackal-serpent with wings' as the popular mental image, influencing contemporary games and comics. No new textual story, but creates a visual story of emergence and impending deluge.

Qing Philology — Hao Yixing and Bi Yuan Reconstruct the TextBi Yuan (畢沅) Shanhaijing Xinzhu (1780s) and Hao Yixing (郝懿行) Shanhaijing Jianshu (c. 1802-1825)

Qing dynasty, 18th-early 19th century

Qing evidential scholars treat Huàshé as a textual-critical case study. Bi Yuan collates Song editions and leishu to argue for the reading 豺 (jackal) over 豹 (leopard) and prefers 叱呼. Hao Yixing devotes a lengthy note to Huàshé, citing Taiping Yulan, Guangbo Wuzhi, and parallel omen beasts (Feiyi, etc.) to argue that huà does not mean shapeshifting and that the wings are Guo Pu's addition, not original text. He cross-references the Yangshui-Yishui hydrology to locate Yang Mountain in the Central Mountains system. The 'story' here is scholarly detective work: reconstructing the earliest reading from corrupted prints. Produces the modern critical text used by Yuan Ke and all contemporary translators. Clarifies that many popular details (wings, shapeshifting) are commentarial, not original. Provides the scholarly baseline for distinguishing attested vs reconstructed features.

Modern Retelling — Yuan Ke, Birrell, and Strassberg Reanimate the OmenYuan Ke (袁珂) Shanhaijing Jiaozhu (1951/1980); Anne Birrell, The Classic of Mountains and Seas (Penguin, 1999); Richard Strassberg, A Chinese Bestiary (UC Press, 2002); Remi Mathieu, Étude sur la mythologie et l'ethnologie de la Chine ancienne (1983)

20th-21st century (1951-present)

Yuan Ke's authoritative Jiaozhu (collated and annotated) presents Huàshé with full apparatus: base text, Guo Pu, Hao Yixing, and his own note that Huàshé belongs to the 'flood-omen' group with Feiyi and others, reflecting Han omenology. Birrell translates: 'There are many transforming snakes there. They look like jackals with human faces. They move like snakes and make a noise like a person being scolded. When they appear there will be a great flood in the city.' Strassberg gives: '...a beast...like a jackal with a human face. It moves like a snake and makes sounds like a human scolding. Whenever it appears, there will be a great flood in its city.' Mathieu's French follows similarly. These translations bring Huàshé to global audiences and frame it as an example of early Chinese teratology, not a living folklore creature. Yuan Ke also notes no independent myth or cult. Creates the modern reception baseline: Huàshé is now a globally searchable bestiary entry, cited in comparative mythology, game design documents, and popular science books. Huàshé is a shapeshifting snake that can turn into a human. — No early source describes shapeshifting. The name huà (化) is misread as 'to transform (into another form)'; in context it likely describes its snake-like movement or prodigious composite anatomy. Its human face is a fixed anatomical feature, not a transformation.

Huàshé is a flying serpent/dragon that soars on its bird wings and controls floods with magic. — Early text says it moves like a snake (crawls), not flies; wings are from Guo Pu commentary and no flight is described. Its flood association is mantic (omen), not active hydromancy. It is not a dragon (long) and is grouped with omen beasts, not dragons.

Huàshé is a single, unique monster that was slain by a hero. — The Shanhaijing says 'within it there are many huashé' (多化蛇) — a population on Yang Mountain, not a unique individual. No slaying narrative, hero, or defeat is attested in any early source; it is an omen beast, not a monster to be killed.

Huàshé lives in the ocean or is a sea serpent. — Its habitat is explicitly inland: Yang Mountain and the Yang River (a tributary of the Yi River in the Central Mountains system). No marine association in early texts; the flood it presages is a riverine inundation of a town (邑), not a sea surge.
