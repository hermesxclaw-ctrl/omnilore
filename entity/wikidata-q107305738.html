The Approach

At the confluence of three rivers, where the Erymanthus rushes down from the mountains and the torrent of the western ravine joins it in a roar, lies the forgotten city of Psophis. Once called Phegia, the 'oak town', it was a place of refuge for the matricide Alcmaeon, a stronghold that even Philip V of Macedon hesitated to assault, and a sanctuary where the river itself was worshipped. Today, its ruins whisper of a time when heroes walked its streets and gods were bound to the land.

Content advisory. Contains references to matricide and mythological violence.
Identity

Psophis

Pronunciation SOH-fis (English approximation); Greek: Ψωφίς, psō-FEES.

Known As

ErymanthusPhegiaPhegeiaTripotama (modern name for the site)Psophida (modern village)

Etymology

The name Psophis is said to derive from Psophis, the mother of Echephron and Promachus, sons of Heracles. Earlier names: Erymanthus (from the river) and Phegia (from the oak trees, Greek 'phegoi').

Cultural Ownership

Ancient Greek (Arcadia), specifically the region of Azania.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars have questioned the exact location of Psophis, but the modern consensus places it at the site of Psofida, near the confluence of the Erymanthus and the Lopesi (Skupi) rivers, based on topographical matches with Polybius' description.

Description

Appearance

The city was situated on a sharp ridge at the confluence of two rivers, with a third joining shortly downstream. The summit was fortified with a citadel, and the town walls were notable for their size and construction. The surrounding terrain was steep and rugged, with oak trees covering the hills.

Symbols & Iconography

The river Erymanthus was personified and had a temple dedicated to it. The city's name 'Phegia' associated it with oaks, which may have been a sacred tree. Coins from Psophis (if any) are not well-documented; the city's emblem may have featured the river god or the boar.

Behaviour & Personality

The city was known for its strong defensive position, making it a formidable fortress. Its inhabitants were proud of their connection to Alcmaeon and their refusal to join the Trojan War, which they justified by their ties to the Argive hero.

Domains & Habitats

Located in the northwest corner of Arcadia, bounded by Achaea to the north and Elis to the west. The site is near the modern village of Psofida in the municipality of Kalavryta, Greece.

Version Differences

The mythological accounts vary: some say Alcmaeon married Arsinoe, daughter of Phegeus, while others say Alphesiboea. The name of the city's founder is also disputed: Phegeus for Phegia, and the sons of Heracles for Psophis.

Powers & Abilities

Fortification

Source Version Polybius' description of Philip V's siege in 219 BC

Description

The city's natural and man-made defenses made it nearly impregnable. The rivers provided natural barriers on three sides, and the citadel on the hill provided a strong defensive position.

Limitations

Despite its defenses, the city was taken by Philip V through a direct assault, showing that it was not invulnerable.

Stories & Myths

The Naming of Psophis

Source Pausanias 8.24.1; Apollodorus 2.7.6Culture GreekPeriod Mythical

Summary

The city was originally called Erymanthus, then Phegia. It was renamed Psophis by Echephron and Promachus, sons of Heracles, who came from Sicily and named it after their mother.

Participants

Echephron

Promachus

Heracles

Psophis

Consequences

The city's name changed, and it became associated with Heracles' lineage.

Variants

Some traditions say the city was founded by Phegeus, and the name Phegia derives from him.

Alcmaeon's Refuge

Source Apollodorus 3.7.5; Pausanias 8.24.7Culture GreekPeriod Mythical

Summary

After murdering his mother Eriphyle, Alcmaeon fled to Phegia, where he was purified by King Phegeus and married his daughter Arsinoe (or Alphesiboea). He gave her the necklace of Harmonia, but later left her, and was eventually killed by her brothers.

Participants

Alcmaeon

Phegeus

Arsinoe

Alphesiboea

Eriphyle

Consequences

The Psophidians claimed descent from Alcmaeon and thus participated in the second expedition against Thebes and refused to join the Trojan War.

Variants

In some versions, Alcmaeon married Alphesiboea instead of Arsinoe; the name of the daughter varies.

The Siege by Philip V

Source Polybius 4.70-72Culture GreekPeriod Historical (219 BC)

Summary

Philip V of Macedon, allied with the Achaeans, attacked Psophis, which was held by the Eleians. Despite the city's strong defenses, Philip's forces scaled the walls and took the city. The Eleian commander Euripidas retreated to the citadel and then surrendered.

Participants

Philip V

Euripidas

Macedonians

Eleians

Consequences

Psophis came under Macedonian control and was later restored to the Achaeans.

Matricide

Source Apollodorus 3.7.5

Description

Alcmaeon murdered his mother Eriphyle as punishment for her betrayal of his father Amphiaraus. This event led to his exile and eventual death.

Scholarly Context

The myth reflects the theme of punishment for kin-killing and the cycle of vengeance.
