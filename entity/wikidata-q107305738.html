Who It Is

At the confluence of three rivers, where the Erymanthus rushes down from the mountains and the torrent of the western ravine joins it in a roar, lies the forgotten city of Psophis. Once called Phegia, the 'oak town', it was a place of refuge for the matricide Alcmaeon, a stronghold that even Philip V of Macedon hesitated to assault, and a sanctuary where the river itself was worshipped. Today, its ruins whisper of a time when heroes walked its streets and gods were bound to the land.

The city was known for its strong defensive position, making it a formidable fortress. Its inhabitants were proud of their connection to Alcmaeon and their refusal to join the Trojan War, which they justified by their ties to the Argive hero.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Psophis is said to derive from Psophis, the mother of Echephron and Promachus, sons of Heracles. Earlier names: Erymanthus (from the river) and Phegia (from the oak trees, Greek 'phegoi').

Pronunciation — SOH-fis (English approximation); Greek: Ψωφίς, psō-FEES.

Aliases — Erymanthus, Phegia, Phegeia, Tripotama (modern name for the site), Psophida (modern village)

Ownership — Ancient Greek (Arcadia), specifically the region of Azania.

Powers
Fortification

The city's natural and man-made defenses made it nearly impregnable. The rivers provided natural barriers on three sides, and the citadel on the hill provided a strong defensive position.

Limit: Despite its defenses, the city was taken by Philip V through a direct assault, showing that it was not invulnerable.

Sacred River Worship

The river Erymanthus was venerated as a deity, with a temple dedicated to it, indicating a form of nature worship.

Limit: The exact rituals are unknown.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The city was situated on a sharp ridge at the confluence of two rivers, with a third joining shortly downstream. The summit was fortified with a citadel, and the town walls were notable for their size and construction. The surrounding terrain was steep and rugged, with oak trees covering the hills.

Symbols — The river Erymanthus was personified and had a temple dedicated to it. The city's name 'Phegia' associated it with oaks, which may have been a sacred tree. Coins from Psophis (if any) are not well-documented; the city's emblem may have featured the river god or the boar.

Domains — Located in the northwest corner of Arcadia, bounded by Achaea to the north and Elis to the west. The site is near the modern village of Psofida in the municipality of Kalavryta, Greece.

Accounts I
The Naming of PsophisPausanias 8.24.1; Apollodorus 2.7.6

Mythical

The city was originally called Erymanthus, then Phegia. It was renamed Psophis by Echephron and Promachus, sons of Heracles, who came from Sicily and named it after their mother. The city's name changed, and it became associated with Heracles' lineage.

Accounts II
Alcmaeon's RefugeApollodorus 3.7.5; Pausanias 8.24.7

Mythical

After murdering his mother Eriphyle, Alcmaeon fled to Phegia, where he was purified by King Phegeus and married his daughter Arsinoe (or Alphesiboea). He gave her the necklace of Harmonia, but later left her, and was eventually killed by her brothers. The Psophidians claimed descent from Alcmaeon and thus participated in the second expedition against Thebes and refused to join the Trojan War.

The Siege by Philip VPolybius 4.70-72

Historical (219 BC)

Philip V of Macedon, allied with the Achaeans, attacked Psophis, which was held by the Eleians. Despite the city's strong defenses, Philip's forces scaled the walls and took the city. The Eleian commander Euripidas retreated to the citadel and then surrendered. Psophis came under Macedonian control and was later restored to the Achaeans.
