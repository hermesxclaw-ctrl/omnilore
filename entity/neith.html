Who It Is

Neith is one of the oldest Egyptian deities, a war goddess, creator goddess, and funerary goddess whose worship dates to the Predynastic period. She is depicted as a woman wearing the red crown of Lower Egypt, carrying a bow and arrows, or a weaving shuttle. Her cult center was Sais in the Delta. Neith was a complex, androgynous creator who was said to have given birth to Ra — making her both mother and daughter of the sun god. She was 'the veiled one,' a mysterious primordial goddess associated with the waters of chaos from which all life emerged. As a war goddess, she guided arrows and protected warriors. As a funerary goddess, she wove the mummy wrappings and was one of the guardians of the canopic jars (protecting the stomach). The Greeks identified Neith with Athena, noting their shared associations with weaving, war, and wisdom. Neith was so ancient that she was said to have existed before the gods, self-created and eternal.

Origins

Neith is among the earliest Egyptian deities attested archaeologically, appearing on Predynastic pottery and palettes. The Esna creation myth describes her as the primordial goddess who emerged from the primeval waters and created the world with seven utterances. She was a self-created, androgynous deity.

Attested in Pyramid Texts / Coffin Texts / Various Temple InscriptionsEra Predynastic to Roman Period (c. 3100 BCE - 300 CE)

Form

Neith is typically depicted as a woman wearing the Red Crown of Lower Egypt (the deshret) and holding a bow and arrows, reflecting her warrior aspect. In some representations she carries the was-sceptre (power) in one hand and the ankh (life) in the other. Her symbol, two crossed bows over a shield (or crossed arrows), appears above her head and doubled as the emblem of the city of Sais. In Esna-period art she was linked with the Lates niloticus (Nile perch), the sacred fish whose image surrounded her there.

Powers

Creation from Thought At Esna, Neith creates the first land from the primordial waters of Nun through her own thoughts, bringing the cosmos into being self-existently. She is the 'Mother and Father of all things' — a bivalent, self-contained creator who needs no consort to generate. Esna creation cycle (Late/Ptolemaic-Roman); New Kingdom 'Mother and Father of all things' theology Attested only in the New Kingdom and Late Period texts; the Predynastic Neith was not yet a cosmogonic creator. Hermopolitan creation gives the primordial role to the Ogdoad; Heliopolitan to Atum. Neith as self-creatrix is one local (Esna/Sais) theology among several.

Cosmic Reweaving / the Loom As patron of weaving and textile production, Neith is said to reweave the world daily on her loom — a cosmic textile metaphor uniting her creative, ordering, and regenerative powers with the material craft of the weaver. Various temple texts and modern synthesis of her weaver aspect The 'daily rewinding of the world on the loom' is a symbolic/theological interpretation, not a literal cult act. The weaver-goddess identity overlaps with but is distinct from the warrior identity; the two are often harmonized as her 'dual nature' without a single ancient source fusing them explicitly.

Warfare and the Crossed Bows Neith is a goddess of war and hunting, bearing a bow and arrows and the emblem of two crossed bows over a shield (also the emblem of Sais). Her name is plausibly 'the terrifying one'. As 'Opener of the Ways' (shared with Wepwawet) she clears paths in battle and guides souls through the afterlife and the tomb threshold. Pyramid/Coffin Texts; 'Opener of the Ways' epithet (shared with Wepwawet) Her martial epithet is attested, but unlike gods such as Montu she never became the engine of a 'holy war' theology; her warfare is protective and royal. The 'terrifying one' etymology is a hypothesis, not a confirmed translation; some scholars remain agnostic on the name's meaning.

Motherhood of the Gods Neith is a mother goddess whose children include the sun god Ra and the crocodile god Sobek (some traditions add the serpent Apep). The Pyramid Texts call Sobek 'Neith's son'. She is 'the Great Mother' and creator in some creation accounts. Pyramid Text PT 317; New Kingdom texts; Heraldry and Sais theology There is no single fixed divine genealogy; her mother-of-Ra and mother-of-Sobek roles co-occur rather than resolve into one consistent family tree. Different traditions assign her different spouses or none (she is sometimes a virgin self-creator). The Sethian equation (mother of Apep) locates her at the chaotic root of the world's adversary, complicating her benign mother-role.

Funerary Guardianship Neith is one of the four goddesses who protect the coffin and the internal organs of the deceased (with Isis, Nephthys, and Serket/Selket). Through her association with linen wrappings she becomes the divine patroness of mummification and the tomb, and is linked to the protection of Duamutef, one of the Four Sons of Horus who guard the canopic jars. Book of the Dead / coffin texts; funerary amulet traditions Her funerary role is real but secondary to her state/cosmic ones; she is the 'fourth' of the classic coffin quartet. The exact canopic guardian-to-jar assignment varies between New Kingdom and later versions; Neith's pairing with Duamutef is the later standard.

Divine Adjudication / Thebes of Ma'at When the Ennead disputed the Egyptian throne after Osiris's murder, they consulted Neith, who judged that the rightful heir Horus should become king. Her word functions as divine case-law, positioning her as an arbiter of legitimate kingship. Contendings of Horus and Set (Chester Beatty Papyrus I tradition) The full drama survives primarily in the Ramesside Chester Beatty Papyrus; Neith's role as adjudicator is a narrative episode, not a permanent cult function. The surviving telling interweaves Neith's verdict with the gods' prolonged and disorderly squabbling; her decision is authoritative yet the dispute drags on, showing the limits of even divine counsel.

Accounts

Before anything existed, Neith rose. Ascribed the power to think the cosmos into being, she created the first land from the primordial waters of Nun through her own thoughts. She is named 'the mother and father of everything' — generating existence from within a body that already was, with no consort and no seed. This is the classic late-Egyptian statement of a self-existent creatrix.

After the murder of Osiris, Horus and Set dispute the kingship of Egypt before the Ennead. The tribunal is deadlocked and the gods bicker for decades (80 years in some tellings). Seeking authoritative counsel, they consult Neith. She rules plainly that the throne passes to the rightful heir, Horus, the son of Osiris. Her verdict does not instantly end the quarreling, but it stands as the moral and legal touchstone toward which the disorderly gods are finally brought.

Neith is mother of the crocodile god Sobek. A Pyramid Text hymn (for king Unis) praises the pharaoh as the living incarnation of Sobek and names Sobek as 'Neith's son' — a title that recurs in Sobek's cult, which spread through the Faiyum and Kom Ombo. Her motherhood of the sun god Ra and (in some traditions) of the chaos-serpent Apep completes her ambivalent maternal register: she can birth both the ordered sun and its annihilating adversary.

Herodotus describes an annual festival in Neith's honor at Sais: participants lit and kept burning numerous lamps throughout the night, a vigil of flame that impressed the Greek traveler. The same festive night hosted the mystery through which the 'sufferings' of Osiris were displayed on an adjacent sacred lake.

A famous inscription associated with Neith's temple at Sais declared, 'I am all that has been, is, and will be. No mortal has ever lifted my veil.' The priests of Sais, conversing with Greek visitors (in the Platonist tradition including Solon's supposed visit), identified Neith with Athena and claimed a primordial kinship between Sais and Athens — a link that Plato's Timaeus/Critias dramatizes by making an old Saitic priest the source of the story of Atlantis. Diodorus even claimed Athenians founded Sais before the Deluge.

A way to hold it in your mind

She is the goddess who wove order out of nothing and then strung the two bows — creator of the world by pure thought, and its fiercest warrior, standing at the exact hinge where an armed Delta queen-goddess becomes the cosmic 'Mother and Father of all things.' Neith of Sais is the oldest identifiable mother of the Egyptian gods, a Predynastic huntress whose crossed-bow emblem doubles as the flag of a city, and whose priests once told a Greek visitor that she was all that has been, is, and will be — and that no mortal had ever lifted her veil. In the same breath she is the ancestor-deity of history's earliest female pharaohs, the judge who told the quarreling gods to crown Horus, the mother of the crocodile Sobek, and the goddess whom Plato himself identified with Athena.
