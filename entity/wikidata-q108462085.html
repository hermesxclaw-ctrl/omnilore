Who It Is

In the incense-thick halls of southern Fujian and Taiwan, a quiet title echoes — Zhongqing Dashi, the Great Being of Loyalty and Purity. He is not a thunder-wielding marshal or a famously merciful bodhisattva, but a deified exemplar: a human remembered for refusing to bend, whose moral uprightness was believed to have outlived his body and become protective power for the community that remembers him.

Hagiographic personality, as implied by the title, centers on moral rectitude, incorruptibility, loyalty to principle over personal safety, and posthumous compassion. In folk-deity logic, such figures are typically stern but protective, willing to punish corruption and impurity while responding to sincere petitions for justice, health, and community safety. They are often described as 'ling' (efficacious) rather than 'ci' (merciful) in the Guanyin sense - efficacy demonstrated through miracles rather than universal compassion. Powers
Moral Rectitude and Judicial Efficacy (Zhongqing)

Power to discern right/wrong, expose corruption, and vindicate the wronged. Petitioners seek help with lawsuits, official examinations, or grievances. In folk logic, the deity's own incorruptibility in life becomes a posthumous power to enforce moral order.

Limit: Efficacy said to depend on petitioner's own sincerity and moral standing; insincere or corrupt petitions are believed to fail or bring retribution. No liturgical text specifying invocation conditions was located.

Protection from Impurity and Plague (Qing)

Association with 'purity' suggests power to expel pestilence, purify water, and ward off impurity (xie, wu). Similar to Qingshui Zushi and other 'pure' deities, may be invoked during epidemics or for household purification.

Limit: In local religion, purification typically requires community jiao ritual, not individual prayer alone; power is mediated through Taoist ritual specialists. No fulu or incantation specific to this deity located.

Bodhisattva-like Compassion and Salvation (Dashi)

The title Dashi implies great-being compassion, potentially including hearing petitions (like Guanyin), granting safe passage, or assisting souls. May include dream revelation and guidance.

Limit: Folk Dashi titles do not confer full bodhisattva soteriology; deity remains within local bureaucratic pantheon, not universal savior. No vow (pranidhana) text located.

Dream Revelation and Divination

Common power of deified persons: appearing in dreams to reveal temple sites, warn of disaster, or answer divination blocks (jiaobei) and lots (qian).

Limit: Revelation said to require ritual purity of the recipient and correct interpretation by temple specialists.

Community Territorial Protection

As a local protective deity, power to guard village boundaries, ensure harvest, calm wind/water, and repel bandits or misfortune through annual processions where the deity's palanquin patrols its ritual territory.

Limit: Power is geographically bounded to the deity's incense territory (xianghuo); outside that area, efficacy is believed to diminish.

The Loyal Official Who Would Not Bend (Apotheosis Foundation Legend) - Type NarrativeOral hagiography / temple pamphlet (expected source type). No digitized text located; summary is a typological reconstruction of the standard 'loyal official deified' plot common to Fujian/Taiwan, not a verbatim attested story for Zhongqing Dashi.

Claimed historical setting typically Ming-Qing; narrative transmission contemporary (20th-21st century oral)

A civil official serving in Fujian is renowned for his incorruptibility (qing) and loyalty (zhong). He refuses to falsify records, accept bribes, or abandon his post during crisis, offending corrupt superiors or invaders. He dies in office - versions say from overwork, wrongful execution, or suicide to preserve integrity. After death, strange signs appear: his body does not decay, incense smoke forms auspicious shapes, or he appears in dreams to villagers. The court or the Jade Emperor, recognizing his virtue, canonizes him as Zhongqing Dashi, and villagers build a shrine. This is the charter myth that explains why a human became a god. No personal name, reign title, or place name specific to Zhongqing Dashi was verified in accessible sources. Establishes the deity's moral charter and explains temple founding; provides model for worshippers to emulate loyalty and purity. No historical consequence (e.g., imperial canonization edict) was located for this specific figure.

The Plague Expelled by Pure Water (Miracle Tale Type)Temple miracle tale (lingyan gushi) - expected genre. No specific tale text located for Zhongqing Dashi; reconstructed from regional plague-deity repertoire.

Claimed Qing or Republican period; told as recent memory in contemporary oral tradition

When epidemic (wenyi) strikes the market town, medicines fail and the City God temple's divination is unclear. Elders pray to Zhongqing Dashi, whose 'pure' nature is believed to counter impurity. The deity, via a spirit-medium or dream, instructs villagers to fetch water from a specific well, burn a particular incense, or parade his palanquin around the boundaries. After the rite, the fever breaks, and no further deaths occur. A plaque is hung in the temple: 'Qing dispels pestilence.' This miracle type justifies the deity's association with Qing as ritual purity, not just moral purity. Establishes healing efficacy; may lead to expansion of temple, annual purification jiao, or pilgrimage. No dated epidemic record linked to Zhongqing Dashi was located.

Prayer for Rain and the Clearing of Drought (Miracle Tale Type)Temple miracle tale / gazetteer anecdote (expected). No specific drought tale located for Zhongqing Dashi.

Claimed late imperial period

During prolonged drought, magistrate-led prayers to the Dragon Kings fail. Commoners, remembering that Zhongqing Dashi was 'clear and pure' like water, carry his statue to the fields or pray at his shrine. Clouds gather, and timely rain falls, but only over his ritual territory, demonstrating his bounded efficacy. The magistrate then memorializes the throne to grant an honorific title or plaque. Reinforces association with water/purity and territorial protection; may result in imperial plaque (bian'e) - none verified for this deity.

Night Patrol Against Bandits and the Vanishing Army (Protection Tale Type)Oral history / temple legend (expected). No specific bandit tale located for Zhongqing Dashi.

Claimed late Qing / early Republican (era of banditry and unrest)

Bandits plan to sack the village at night. Temple keepers dream that Zhongqing Dashi warns them, or watchmen see lanterns and hear marching where no soldiers are camped. The bandits, seeing what appears to be a large defending force or experiencing sudden confusion, flee. Villagers credit the deity's night patrol (xunye). This tale explains why the deity's palanquin is paraded at night during festivals and why households place his talisman over doors. Strengthens territorial cult and procession tradition; may establish a night patrol ritual. No historical bandit incident report linked to this deity was located.

The Dream That Found the Temple Site (Foundation Miracle)Temple foundation legend (miaoyu qiyuan) - expected stele prologue. No stele transcription located for Zhongqing Dashi.

Contemporary telling, claimed origin 100-300 years ago

After the initial miracles, the deity appears in the dream of a pious elder, child, or returning overseas worker, indicating where his incense should be settled - under an old banyan, by a stream, or where three roads meet. A divination confirms the site, and the first thatched shrine is built. Later, when the community prospers (often through overseas remittances), the shrine is rebuilt in brick and elaborated. This narrative is told to explain why the temple is where it is and why its incense is considered especially ling. Legitimates temple location and incense lineage (fenxiang) - important for pilgrimage and incense-division networks. No incense-division record for Zhongqing Dashi was located.

Zhongqing Dashi is just another name for Guanyin (Guanyin Dashi). — Guanyin Dashi is Avalokiteshvara, a pan-Asian bodhisattva with a distinct iconography (often female-presenting in China, with willow branch and vase) and universal compassion vows. Zhongqing Dashi, by epithet and classification as a local deity, appears to be a separate, likely deified human figure whose 'Dashi' title is an honorific borrowing, not an identity. Conflation erases the local history and community ownership of the cult.
