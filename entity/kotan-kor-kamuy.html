Kotan-kor-kamuy

The Snake God, Guardian of the Village, The Watcher of Borders

TypeSnake / Water Deity
CultureAinu
RealmRivers, wetlands, village boundaries, and watery pla
ScaleCultural (pan-Ainu, village-level guardian)

I coil at the edge of the village, where the water meets the land. Respect my boundary, leave your offerings at the river's bend, and I will keep your home safe from the spirits of the wild.

Who It Is

Kotan-kor-kamuy ('the kamuy who owns the village') is the snake god of the Ainu, a powerful but ambivalent deity associated with water, village boundaries, and protection. Kotan-kor-kamuy dwells at the edges of Ainu villages — in rivers, marshes, and wet ditches — and serves as the village guardian, protecting the settlement from evil spirits and misfortune. However, the deity is also dangerous: if offended or neglected, Kotan-kor-kamuy can cause illness, drowning, and bad luck. The Ainu traditionally treated snakes with great respect, leaving offerings of wine and millet at the village boundary to appease Kotan-kor-kamuy. Killing a snake was strictly forbidden unless it posed an immediate threat, and even then, a purification ritual was required afterward. The snake deity is also associated with fertility and the regenerative power of water — Kotan-kor-kamuy was invoked for fertility of the land, successful fishing, and healthy children. Some yukar (epic poems) describe Kotan-kor-kamuy as the younger brother of Kim-un-kamuy (the bear god), representing the complementary relationship between mountain and water, forest and village. In Ainu symbology, the snake is not the devilish figure of some cultures but a powerful boundary-crosser, able to move between the human world and the spirit world, between water and land, between life and death. Kotan-kor-kamuy's worship reflects the Ainu understanding of the sacred nature of margins, borders, and transitional spaces.

Attested in Ainu oral tradition (yukar), Ainu indigenous religionEra Pre-modern, still respected in traditional Ainu areas
