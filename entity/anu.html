Who It Is

Anu is the name the ancients gave to the sky itself — not a cloud-god or a storm-god, but the very vault of heaven made divine. He sits at the top of every Mesopotamian god list, yet he is the god who does nothing. Where Enlil commands storms, Inanna wages war, and Enki weaves mischief, Anu withdraws into the highest heaven and rules by silence. Understanding Anu means understanding how ancient Mesopotamia thought about authority, distance, and the strange idea that the supreme god might also be the most remote.

Anu is consistently characterized as distant, passive, and juridical rather than active. He convenes divine assemblies, renders judgments, and confers kingship, but rarely intervenes directly in human or even divine affairs. In the Adapa myth his compassion leads him to offer immortality — a decision that misfires because of Enki's counter-warning. In Enūma Eliš he prefers diplomatic negotiation over continued war. Powers
Celestial authority and divine investiture

Anu possesses ultimate authority over the divine order, conferring legitimacy on kings and other gods. His horned crown is the visible marker of this power. In the Epic of Gilgamesh he grants Ishtar the Bull of Heaven, demonstrating that even destructive cosmic forces require his permission. This is not a combat power but a juridical one — he opens divine assemblies and his word shapes cosmic decree.

Limit: Anu is consistently passive; he does not act directly. His power requires delegation through Enlil, Marduk, or other executive deities. In the Adapa myth his offer of immortality fails because the recipient declines it.

Cosmic genealogy and primordial generation

Anu functions as the generative ancestor of major divine lineages. The god list An = Anum makes him father of Enki, Enlil, Inanna, and Nergal, among hundreds. In theogonies he is sometimes the offspring of Anshar and Kishar (or Alala), placing him mid-way between primordial chaos and ordered cosmos. The Enūma Eliš traces descent from Apsu and Tiamat through Lahmu and Lahamu to Anu, establishing him as the pivot between watery chaos and structured creation.

Limit: Genealogical claims vary by city and period; there is no single fixed 'family tree' of Anu. Different god lists give different consorts and children.

Judicial arbitration and cosmic order

Anu convenes divine courts and renders judgment on gods and mortals. In the Adapa myth he summons the sage for breaking the south wind and determines his fate — offering immortality as both punishment and reward. In Enūma Eliš he seeks peace through negotiation rather than continued war between the younger and older gods. This judicial function makes him the cosmological equivalent of a supreme court justice: rarely visible, but the final arbiter of legitimacy.

Limit: His judgments are frequently overridden or circumvented by other gods (Enki in Adapa, Marduk in Enūma Eliš).

Form & Signs

Appearance — Anu is represented iconographically by the horned crown — the supreme symbol of divine kingship in Mesopotamian art — and by the tiara of heaven. He is not typically depicted in anthropomorphic form in early periods; when he appears in narrative art he is enthroned in the highest heaven, often attended by lesser deities. Symbols — Primary symbol: the horned crown (attested from the Early Dynastic period onward). Secondary symbols: the tiara of heaven, the circumpolar stars (mulUDU.IDIM), and temple ziggurats oriented to celestial observation. The planet Saturn was sometimes associated with him in later Babylonian astronomy. The Eanna precinct at Uruk, though primarily Inanna's, is argued by some scholars to have originated as Anu's celestial observatory.

Domains — Sky, heaven, cosmic authority, divine kingship, judgment, the highest celestial realm. His abode is the 'heaven of Anu' — the uppermost of the three divine heavens (Anu-Enlil-Ea). The Anu ziggurat at Uruk served as his earthly temple complex and as an astronomical observatory. He is associated with the fixed stars and the north celestial pole.

Accounts I
The Adapa and the Food of ImmortalityMyth of Adapa (Old Babylonian / Kassite tablets)

The sage Adapa, servant of Ea (Enki), breaks the wing of the south wind, paralyzing it for seven days. Anu summons him to the divine court to answer for this disruption. Enki warns Adapa in advance: when Anu offers the food and water of immortality, Adapa must refuse, because Anu will actually offer the food of death. Adapa follows the warning, declines the gift, and is sent back to earth mortal. Anu's compassion is genuine — he intended to make Adapa immortal — but Enki's counter-strategy preserves the mortal condition. The myth explains why humans die and frames Anu as a benevolent but thwarted benefactor. The narrative resonates with the biblical Fall of Man in Genesis 3:22-23, where Yahweh prevents Adam and Eve from eating the Tree of Life. Humanity remains mortal; Anu's direct gift is blocked by divine rivalry. The myth establishes a pattern: Anu's benevolent intentions are undermined by other gods' protective jealousy over human-divine boundaries.

Anu and the Bull of HeavenEpic of Gilgamesh, Standard Babylonian version

In Tablet VI of the Epic of Gilgamesh, Ishtar (Inanna) comes to Anu after Gilgamesh insults her, demanding the Bull of Heaven to avenge her wounded pride. Anu protests, warning that releasing the Bull would bring famine and devastation to the earth, but Ishtar threatens to raise the dead to devour the living if he refuses. Anu yields, handing her the Bull. Ishtar sends it to attack Gilgamesh and Enkidu in Uruk; Enkidu kills it by seizing it by the horns, and Gilgamesh tears out its shoulder and throws it at Ishtar's face. This murder triggers the gods' final punishment: Enkidu must die, setting up the epic's meditation on mortality. The Bull's death leads directly to Enkidu's death sentence. Anu's reluctant compliance shows his authority as conditional — he can be blackmailed by his own daughter.

Accounts II
The Castration of Anu: Kumarbi and the Hurrian SuccessionKumarbi Songs (Hurrian), Hittite translations

In the Hurrian Kumarbi Songs, known chiefly from Hittite translations, Anu is the former ruler of the gods who is overthrown by Kumarbi. Kumarbi seizes Anu and bites off his genitals. The severed organs fall to earth and impregnate the earth, producing monstrous offspring. Kumarbi then gives birth — through his own skull — to the storm god Teshub, who will later challenge him in turn. This violent theogeny mirrors the Greek castration of Ouranos by Kronos in Hesiod's Theogony, and scholars debate whether the Kumarbi cycle influenced Hesiod or whether both derive from a common Near Eastern prototype. Anu is removed from active rule, establishing a succession myth that legitimizes Kumarbi and then Teshub. The castration transfers creative potency from sky to earth.

The Seleucid Revival of Anu in UrukTemple archives from Uruk, Seleucid period cuneiform texts

By the Hellenistic period Anu had largely faded from active worship. In Uruk, however, a new theological movement under Seleucid rule redefined Anu as an active, monotheistic-style supreme deity. Temple archives from the final centuries of Mesopotamian civilization show Anu receiving regular sacrifices, prayers, and astronomical observations. A hymn from this period addresses him as 'Prince of the gods, whose utterance ruleth over the obedient company of the gods; Lord of the horned crown.' The Anu ziggurat continued in operation from c. 2000 BCE to c. 150 BCE, serving simultaneously as temple, observatory, and library. This late revival is unique: a deity who had become abstract and distant was re-imported into cult practice by a scholarly priesthood. The revival preserves Anu's memory into the final era of ancient Mesopotamian religion. The temple complex at Uruk becomes one of the last functioning ziggurat sites in antiquity.

Anu-Zeus identification

— Anu was supreme in rank but rarely worshipped directly in early periods. His cult was revived late (Seleucid Uruk) but he never achieved the popular devotion of Inanna, Marduk, or Nabu.

— Uranus is a Roman/Greek personification of the sky, while Anu is a specific Mesopotamian deity with a complex textual and cultic history. The castration parallel exists between Anu (via Kumarbi) and Ouranos, but Anu is not Uranus.

— Ancient sources consistently portray Anu as passive, juridical, and sometimes compassionate. His violence is structural (permitting the Bull of Heaven) rather than personal; his tyranny is a modern projection from later sky-god archetypes.
