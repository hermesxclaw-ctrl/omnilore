Who It Is

In the cold, dark winters of the Ainu world, when the sun barely lifts above the horizon, the Ainu people tell of Chup Kamuy, the divine being who governs the moon. She is not a distant, silent orb, but a living force whose monthly cycle shapes the tides of the sea and the rhythms of human life. Her story is one of celestial duty, of a goddess who once walked among humans, and whose pale light carries both blessings and warnings.

Chup Kamuy is generally benevolent, but she is also a powerful and sometimes capricious deity. She is responsible for the moon's phases and thus the passage of months. She may be invoked for protection during the night, and she is associated with the rhythm of human activities such as hunting, fishing, and agriculture. In some stories, she is a guardian of women and children, and she may punish those who break taboos.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — In the Ainu language, 'chup' means 'moon' (and also 'month'), and 'kamuy' means 'deity' or 'spirit'. Thus, Chup Kamuy literally translates to 'Moon Deity'.

Pronunciation — Approximately 'CHOOP KAH-moo-ee' (with 'ch' as in 'church', 'u' as in 'boot', 'a' as in 'father', and 'ee' as in 'see').

Aliases — Chup, Chup-kamuy, Moon Goddess, Moon Deity

Ownership — Ainu people, indigenous to Hokkaido (Japan), Sakhalin (Russia), and the Kuril Islands.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Chup Kamuy is typically described as a female deity, but specific visual descriptions are rare in the sources. She is associated with the moon, and may be depicted as a beautiful woman, sometimes with long hair, and often associated with the pale light of the moon. In some narratives, she may appear as an old woman or in a form that reflects her age or mood.

Symbols — The moon itself is the primary symbol. In Ainu art, the moon is sometimes represented as a disc or crescent. Chup Kamuy may be associated with the colors silver and white. She is also linked to the night sky, the tides, and the monthly cycle.

Domains — Chup Kamuy resides in the heavens, specifically in the moon. She is also associated with the night sky and with earthly places that are connected to the moon, such as bodies of water that reflect moonlight. She is believed to influence the tides, and thus she is connected to the sea and coastal areas.

Accounts I
The Moon Goddess and the Sun GodAinu oral tradition

Unknown (pre-modern)

In some Ainu narratives, Chup Kamuy is the sister or wife of the sun deity. They take turns crossing the sky, but the moon is sometimes reluctant to shine, and the sun must persuade her. In one story, the moon hides her face because she is ashamed of something, and the sun gives her a veil of clouds to cover herself. Explains the phases of the moon and the presence of shadows on the moon's surface.

Accounts II
The Moon and the RabbitAinu oral tradition (similar to East Asian folklore)

Unknown

The Ainu, like many cultures, see a rabbit in the moon. One story tells of a rabbit that was thrown into the moon by a deity as a punishment or as a reward, and it has been pounding rice or making mochi there ever since. The rabbit's image on the moon is a reminder of the story.

The Moon's EclipseAinu oral tradition

Unknown

During a lunar eclipse, the Ainu believed that Chup Kamuy was in danger, perhaps being attacked by a demon or a monster. They would make noise or shoot arrows into the sky to scare away the attacker and protect the moon. The eclipse ends, and the moon is saved.
