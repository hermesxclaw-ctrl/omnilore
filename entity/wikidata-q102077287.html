Form

No surviving visual description from primary Korean mythological texts survives. Later historiography and legend describe her as a woman born from a dragon, not as an anthropomorphic goddess with fixed iconographic attributes.

Powers

Rain-making and weather influence As a rain goddess, Aryong-Jong is called upon in times of drought. Ritual invocations paired physical action with prayer, pouring water through a sieve to simulate rain and appeal to her power. Swedish Wikipedia article 'Aryong-Jong'; Joseon-era ritual records No surviving detailed records describe the exact conditions under which the ritual succeeded or failed. None recorded in accessible sources.

Dragon-born origin Legend states she was born from a dragon, making her a liminal being between celestial, royal, and chthonic domains. This origin supports her later status as first queen of Silla. Samguk Yusa; Samguk Sagi; Swedish Wikipedia article 'Aryong-Jong' The historicity of dragon birth is mythological rather than literal. Samguk Yusa says left side of the dragon; Samguk Sagi says right side.

Behavior & Habitat

In Korean tradition, she is associated with rain rituals and fertility. According to later legend, she was called upon during drought through prayer while water was poured through a sieve, simulating rain.

Accounts

Lady Aryeong was born from the side of a dragon that appeared near a well. She married Hyeokgeose of Silla, founder of the Silla kingdom. Together, the dragon-born couple became the first royal pair of Silla. The two major chronicles differ on whether she emerged from the left side (Samguk Yusa) or the right side (Samguk Sagi) of the dragon.

After her death, Aryong-Jong was worshipped as a goddess of rain. During drought, she was invoked through prayer while water was poured through a sieve, an action meant to simulate rain and petition her aid. The pilgrimage site associated with her origin became a place where women prayed for fertility.

Some traditions trace the term 'Arirang' to the name of Lady Aryeong, suggesting the famous Korean folk song may have evolved from a poem praising her. This link connects royal foundation mythology with Korean musical folklore.

What We Don't Know

The figure is attested in medieval Korean chronicles and modern Wikipedia, but no full-length scholarly monograph is available in English through the channels accessed. Ritual details of the sieve rain-making ceremony remain sparse.
