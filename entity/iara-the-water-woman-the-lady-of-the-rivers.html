Who It Is

Iara (from Tupi-Guarani y-îara, 'Lady of the Waters') is the most famous water-spirit of Brazilian Amazonian and coastal mythology — a syncretic figure whose roots lie in Tupi-Guarani river-spirit traditions and whose appearance has been shaped by the African Yemanjá and European mermaid legends brought by colonization. She is described as a stunningly beautiful woman with long, flowing black hair, light-brown skin, and a fish-tail from the waist down. Iara dwells in an underwater palace of gold and crystal (a motif likely adapted from the Afro-Brazilian tradition of Yemanjá's underwater realm) at the bottom of rivers and lakes. At night, especially at the full moon, she rises to the surface, sits on a rock, and combs her hair while singing a song of such inescapable beauty that men hearing it fall into a trance and walk into the river, drowning as they attempt to reach her. Iara is simultaneously a figure of seductive danger and ecological guardianship: she protects the fish and the river's health. Fishermen who overfish or pollute the water will be capsized; those who approach humbly with offerings of tobacco, flowers, or mirrors may receive abundant catch. In the syncretic cosmology of Brazilian folk Catholicism, Iara is sometimes identified with the Virgin Mary or associated with the encantados (enchanted beings) of Afro-Brazilian Umbanda.
