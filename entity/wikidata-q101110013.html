The Approach

In the summer sky, a serpent glides between the stars—not as a single continuous figure, but as two distinct celestial fragments, its head and tail forever separated by the very man who holds it. This is Serpens, the only constellation split in two, a celestial paradox that has puzzled astronomers and mythographers for centuries.

Identity

Serpens

Pronunciation /ˈsɜːrpɛnz/

Known As

Serpens CaputSerpens Caudathe SerpentὌφις (Óphis)

Etymology

From Latin serpens, 'serpent' or 'snake'. The Greek name is Ὄφις (Óphis), meaning 'serpent'.

Cultural Ownership

Greek, Babylonian, Chinese, modern international astronomy

Disputed Identifications

The Babylonian constellation Bašmu is roughly identified with Serpens, but the correspondence is not exact. Some ancient atlases depicted Serpens and Ophiuchus as a single constellation, leading to ambiguity in identification.

Description

Appearance

Serpens is a constellation split into two non-contiguous parts: Serpens Caput (the head) to the west and Serpens Cauda (the tail) to the east. In figurative representations, the serpent's body passes behind Ophiuchus between Mu Serpentis in the head and Nu Serpentis in the tail. The head is marked by a distinctive triangle of stars (Beta, Gamma, Iota Serpentis), with Alpha Serpentis (Unukalhai) as the brightest star in the heart. The tail extends eastward, containing rich Milky Way star fields.

Symbols & Iconography

In Greek mythology, Serpens represents a snake held by Asclepius, the healer. It is a symbol of rebirth and renewal due to the snake's ability to shed its skin. In ancient atlases, it was sometimes depicted coiling around Ophiuchus, but more often passing behind his body or between his legs.

Behaviour & Personality

As a constellation, Serpens is static, but its mythological role is that of a creature held and controlled by Ophiuchus. It is associated with healing and medicine, and its split nature reflects the separation imposed by the serpent-bearer.

Domains & Habitats

Serpens is located in the northern celestial hemisphere, visible primarily during the Northern Hemisphere's summer. It covers 636.9 square degrees, ranking 23rd among the 88 constellations. It is bordered by Libra, Virgo, Boötes, Corona Borealis, Ophiuchus, Hercules, Sagittarius, Scutum, and Aquila.

Version Differences

In some ancient atlases, Serpens and Ophiuchus were depicted as a single constellation; Johann Bayer depicted them separately. The modern split into Caput and Cauda was established by Eugène Delporte in 1930. In Chinese astronomy, the stars of Serpens were part of different asterisms, such as Tianshi, Shilou, Liesi, and Tianru.

Stories & Myths

The Serpent of Asclepius

Source Greek mythologyCulture Ancient GreekPeriod Antiquity

Summary

Asclepius, the healer, once killed a snake. Another snake placed a revival herb on it, bringing it back to life. Asclepius used this technique to revive dead humans. The snake became associated with healing and rebirth, and was placed in the sky as the constellation Serpens, held by Ophiuchus (Asclepius).

Participants

Asclepius

Serpent

Consequences

The snake became a symbol of medicine and rebirth; Serpens and Ophiuchus are represented together in the sky.

Variants

Some versions depict the snake as coiling around Ophiuchus, while others show it passing behind or between his legs.
