The Approach

Deep beneath the roots of Yggdrasil, where the world's foundations groan under the weight of myth, lies Aurvangar—a name that whispers of ancient waters and hidden halls. Though barely a footnote in the surviving Norse texts, this place holds a quiet power: it is the home of dwarves, the forge of treasures that would shape the fate of gods and men. To step into Aurvangar is to descend into the earth's own memory, where the rock remembers the first sparks of creation.

Identity

Aurvangar

Pronunciation OWR-vahn-gar (approximate Old Norse: ['æurˌwɑŋɡɑr])

Known As

AurvangÖrvangarAurvangr

Etymology

The name is Old Norse, typically interpreted as 'mud flats' or 'gravel plains' (from 'aurr' meaning gravel or mud, and 'vangr' meaning field or meadow). Some scholars suggest a connection to 'aur' (water) or 'eyr' (gravel bank), reinforcing the watery, earthy connotation.

Cultural Ownership

Norse mythology, specifically the mythological geography of the Eddas. It is a place within the Norse cosmos, associated with the dwarves.

Disputed Identifications

Some have attempted to identify Aurvangar with specific real-world locations, such as the island of Samsø in Denmark, based on a dubious etymology linking 'Aurvangar' to 'Sámsey' (Samsø). This is generally rejected by scholars as a folk etymology. The exact location within the Norse cosmos is not specified; it is only said to be in the 'earth' (jörðu), possibly beneath the roots of Yggdrasil.

Description

Appearance

There are no direct descriptions of Aurvangar's appearance in the sources. Based on the etymology and the general Norse conception of dwarf dwellings, it would likely be an underground realm of caverns, forges, and stone halls, possibly near water or marshy ground, given the 'mud' or 'gravel' element in the name.

Symbols & Iconography

No specific symbols or iconography are associated with Aurvangar in the sources. In modern fantasy, it might be depicted with dwarven runes, anvils, and underground forges, but these are speculative.

Behaviour & Personality

As a location, Aurvangar does not have a personality. However, its inhabitants, the dwarves, are described in the Eddas as skilled craftsmen, often cunning and greedy, but also wise and powerful.

Domains & Habitats

Aurvangar is a dwelling place of dwarves, located within the earth. The exact location is unspecified, but it is likely part of the subterranean world beneath Midgard or near the roots of Yggdrasil. It may be one of several dwarf halls, such as Nidavellir or Svartalfheim, though those are sometimes considered separate realms.

Version Differences

The only significant difference is the use of 'Aurvangar' as a plural noun referring to the dwarves themselves in some readings, versus a place name. The Poetic Edda lists 'Aurvangr' as a dwarf name, and 'Aurvangar' appears as a plural, which could mean 'the Aurvangar' (i.e., the dwarves of Aurvangar) or 'the mud flats' as a place. Snorri does not mention Aurvangar at all, suggesting it was a minor location or that he considered it synonymous with other dwarf dwellings.

Powers & Abilities

Dwelling of Dwarves

Source Version Poetic Edda, Völuspá (dwarf list); Prose Edda (dwarves' origin)

Description

Aurvangar serves as a home or hall for a group of dwarves. The dwarves are renowned for their craftsmanship, creating powerful artifacts such as Thor's hammer Mjölnir, Odin's spear Gungnir, and the golden boar Gullinbursti.

Limitations

No specific limitations are given for the place itself. The dwarves' abilities are subject to the myths, but the location does not grant any special powers.

Contradictions

None directly, but the relationship between Aurvangar and other dwarf realms (e.g., Nidavellir) is unclear.

Stories & Myths

The Creation of the Dwarves and the Naming of Aurvangar

Source Poetic Edda, Völuspá, stanzas 13-16Culture NorsePeriod Viking Age / Medieval (poem likely composed before 1000 CE, recorded in 13th century)

Summary

In the creation myth, the gods create the dwarves from the blood of the giant Ymir and the earth's soil. The seeress Völva recites a list of dwarf names, including Aurvangr and Aurvangar. This suggests that Aurvangar is both a place and a collective name for the dwarves who dwell there. The dwarves are said to live in the earth, and Aurvangar is one of their halls.

Participants

The gods (Odin, Vili, Vé, etc.)

The dwarves (including Mótsognir, Durin, Aurvangr)

Consequences

The dwarves become the master craftsmen of the Norse cosmos, creating many of the gods' most important treasures.

Variants

The list of dwarf names varies slightly between manuscripts, but Aurvangar/Aurvangr appears consistently.

The Dwarves of Aurvangar in the Prose Edda

Source Prose Edda, Gylfaginning, chapter 14 (and Skáldskaparmál for dwarf names)Culture NorsePeriod 13th century (written by Snorri Sturluson)

Summary

Snorri Sturluson, in his retelling of Norse mythology, does not explicitly mention Aurvangar, but he describes the dwarves as living in the earth and in rocks. He also provides a list of dwarf names in Skáldskaparmál, which includes Aurvangr. This indicates that Aurvangar was known to Snorri, but he may have considered it a minor location.

Participants

Snorri Sturluson (author)

The dwarves

Consequences

The Prose Edda became the primary source for later understanding of Norse mythology, but its omission of Aurvangar as a place contributed to the obscurity of the name.

Variants

Snorri's list of dwarf names includes 'Aurvangr' but not 'Aurvangar' as a place.

Contradictions & Disputes

Aurvangar as a place vs. a group of dwarves

Position A

Aurvangar is a place name, referring to a specific location where dwarves live.

Position A — evidence

The word 'vangr' means 'field' or 'meadow', suggesting a place. In Völuspá, it appears in a list of dwarf names, but also as a plural form that could be a collective.

Position B

Aurvangar is a plural noun referring to the dwarves themselves, possibly meaning 'the mud-dwellers' or 'the gravel-people'.

Position B — evidence

The plural form 'Aurvangar' is used in the same stanza as the other dwarf names, which are all individual names. Some scholars argue it is a collective name for a group of dwarves.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars treat it as a place name, but the ambiguity is acknowledged.

Location of Aurvangar

Position A

Aurvangar is located in the underworld, beneath the roots of Yggdrasil.

Position A — evidence

The dwarves are said to have been created from the earth and to dwell within it, and Yggdrasil's roots reach into the underworld.

Position B

Aurvangar is simply a region within the earth, not necessarily connected to Yggdrasil.

Position B — evidence

The texts do not explicitly link Aurvangar to Yggdrasil. It is just one of many dwarf dwellings.

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus; the location is unknown.

Primary Evidence

Poetic Edda, Völuspá, stanza 13-16 (Codex Regius)

Type textDate c. 1270 (manuscript), poem likely older (10th-11th century)Locator Stanzas 13-16Language Old Norse

Translation

The stanzas list the names of dwarves, including 'Aurvangr' and 'Aurvangar' as a group. The exact translation: 'Then sought the gods their seats of doom, / The holy ones, and counsel held: / Who should the race of dwarfs create, / From the blood of the giant and the limbs of the earth. / There was Mótsognir the mightiest made, / All dwarfs' ruler, and Durin next; / Many a one of the race of men / From the earth they made, as I have said. / The dwarfs, the race of dwarfs, / From the rocks they made, / And from the earth's clay, / The dwarfs in the earth's deep.' (translations vary).

Translation Note

The name appears in the list of dwarf names, and also as a plural form referring to the dwarves collectively: 'Aurvangar' is used in some manuscripts as the name for the group of dwarves dwelling there.

Prose Edda, Gylfaginning, chapter 14 (Codex Regius)

Type textDate c. 1220Locator Chapter 14Language Old Norse

Translation

Snorri Sturluson writes: 'Then said Gangleri: "Where is the chief abode or holy place of the gods?" Hár answered: "That is at the ash Yggdrasil; there the gods must hold their courts every day." Then Gangleri asked: "What is told of that place?" Then Jafnhárr said: "The ash is the greatest and best of all trees... Under the roots, in the direction of the frost-giants, is the spring of Mímir, where wisdom and understanding are hidden... Under the root that turns toward the frost-giants, there is the well of Hvergelmir, and from it flow the rivers... The third root of the ash is in heaven, and under it is the well of Urd, the Norns' well." This does not mention Aurvangar directly, but the dwarves are said to have been created from the earth and to live in the earth, and Aurvangar is one of their dwellings.

Translation Note

Snorri does not explicitly name Aurvangar in this passage, but he describes the dwarves' origin and habitat, which aligns with the Poetic Edda's account.

Poetic Edda, Völuspá, stanza 15 (Codex Regius) - variant reading

Type textDate c. 1270Locator Stanza 15Language Old Norse

Translation

In some manuscript variants, the line 'Þar var Aurvangr ok Aurvangar' appears, meaning 'There were Aurvangr and Aurvangar'. This suggests that Aurvangr is a specific dwarf and Aurvangar is the plural, possibly referring to the group of dwarves or the place itself.

Translation Note

The ambiguity between the dwarf name and the place name is significant; the same word may refer to both.
