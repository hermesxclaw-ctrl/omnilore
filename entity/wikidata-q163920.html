Who It Is

Imagine a beauty so potent it makes the goddess of love herself abandon her throne on Olympus to chase a mortal boy through the woods. Now imagine that boy is so stubborn, so obsessed with the thrill of the hunt, that he ignores her desperate warnings and gets himself gored to death by a wild boar. That is Adonis. He is the original 'beautiful fool' — a youth whose face launched a thousand ships of art, poetry, and tragedy, but whose fatal flaw was simply being too handsome to listen. He is the ancient world's most tragic heartthrob, the god of spring who dies every year because he just couldn't stay out of the forest.

Adonis is characterized by a reckless, youthful passion for the hunt. He is not a warrior or a king, but a hunter, a man of the wilds. This passion is his defining trait and ultimately his undoing. He is portrayed as somewhat headstrong and heedless of advice, particularly the warnings of Aphrodite to avoid dangerous beasts. His behavior is that of a beautiful youth who is aware of his charm but is more interested in the thrill of the chase than in the politics of Olympus or the safety of his own life.

This is a direct linguistic borrowing from the Phoenician/Canaanite term for a deity or noble figure, adapted into Greek as a proper name. Powers
Supernatural Beauty

His defining 'power' is his extraordinary beauty, which is so potent it captivates Aphrodite, the goddess of beauty herself. This is not a magical charm but an inherent, godlike quality that transcends mortal standards. It makes him desirable to both goddesses and mortals and is the engine of the entire myth.

Limit: His beauty is passive and does not protect him from harm. It is a quality that inspires love in others but provides him no agency or defense.

Skilled Hunter

Adonis is a passionate and skilled hunter, spending his days in the mountains and forests. This is his primary activity and skill, and it is what brings him into contact with the fatal boar.

Limit: His skill is not supernatural; it is the expertise of a mortal youth. His recklessness and overconfidence in this skill are his fatal flaw.

Seasonal Resurrection

As a dying-and-rising deity, Adonis is granted the ability to return from the Underworld for a portion of each year. This is not a power he wields himself but a dispensation granted by Zeus (or through the arbitration of Calliope). His return symbolizes the return of spring and vegetation.

Limit: His time in the upper world is limited to half (or one-third) of the year. He must return to the Underworld for the rest, symbolizing the death of vegetation in winter.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Adonis is universally described in ancient sources as the epitome of male beauty. He is depicted as a youth in the first flush of manhood, with a physique that is athletic yet still soft and youthful. In art, he is often shown as a beardless youth, sometimes with a spear or a hunting dog, reflecting his passion for the hunt. Symbols — The boar is his primary symbol, representing both his hunting passion and his death. The anemone flower (the windflower) is the central floral symbol, believed by the Greeks to have sprung from his blood. The myrrh tree is his origin symbol. The 'Gardens of Adonis' (pots of fast-sprouting seeds) are the key ritual symbol of his cult. His attributes also include a hunting spear and a throwing stick.

Domains — His domain is the natural world, specifically the life-cycle of vegetation: the bloom of spring, the withering of summer, and the dormancy of winter. He is a vegetation deity, his death and rebirth mirroring the agricultural year. In his cult, he is associated with the 'Adonis gardens' — small, fast-growing plants that sprout and die quickly, symbolizing his short life and the transient nature of beauty and youth.

Accounts I
The Birth of Adonis: The Incest of MyrrhaOvid, Metamorphoses, Book X; Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca; Hyginus, Fabulae

Classical/Late Antiquity (myth likely older)

Myrrha (or Smyrna), the daughter of King Theias of Assyria (or Cyprus), is cursed by Aphrodite (or suffers from her own unchecked passion) to fall in love with her own father. Through a trick orchestrated by her nurse, she sleeps with her father in the dark for several nights. When Theias discovers the deception, he pursues her with a sword. Myrrha flees and prays to the gods for escape. They take pity on her and transform her into a myrrh tree. After nine months, the tree's bark splits and Adonis is born from the trunk. The nymphs anoint the newborn with his mother's tears (myrrh). Adonis is born from a tree, making him an orphan of incest and transformation. His beauty is immediately apparent. The story explains the origin of myrrh, a precious resin used in perfumes and funerals.

Accounts II
The Love of Aphrodite and the Death by the BoarOvid, Metamorphoses, Book X; Theocritus, Idyll XV; Bion, Lament for Adonis

Classical/Hellenistic

Aphrodite (Venus) sees the infant Adonis and is struck by his beauty. She hides him in a chest and gives him to Persephone (Proserpina) for safekeeping, but Persephone also falls in love with him and refuses to give him back. Zeus (Jupiter) arbitrates, decreeing that Adonis will spend part of the year with each goddess. As an adult, Adonis becomes a passionate hunter. Aphrodite, fearing for his safety, warns him to hunt only timid animals, not fierce beasts like boars or lions. He ignores her warning. While hunting, he is gored by a wild boar (which some say was Ares in disguise, jealous of Aphrodite's love). Aphrodite rushes to him, but arrives too late. As his blood soaks the earth, she causes the anemone (windflower) to spring up from it. Adonis dies, becoming a symbol of untimely death and lost beauty. The anemone flower is created. The myth establishes the Adonia festival, a time of mourning and lamentation.

The Lament for AdonisBion of Smyrna, Lament for Adonis (Epitaphios Adonidos)

2nd century BCE

This is a lyric poem that describes the death of Adonis in a highly emotional and visual way. It portrays Aphrodite's frantic search for her lover, her discovery of his body, and her lament. The poem is a key source for the emotional and ritualistic mourning of Adonis. It describes the blood turning to roses and the tears of Aphrodite turning to anemones. It also mentions the 'gardens of Adonis' withering. The poem crystallizes the image of the dying Adonis cradled by Aphrodite, an image that would become iconic in Western art. It is also a direct literary model for the ritual lamentation of the Adonia festival.
