Who It Is

Before Zeus was the thunderbolt-hurling king of Olympus, he was a crying baby in a cave on Crete, and the one who kept him alive was a goat. Not just any goat—a goat with a horn that could produce whatever you desired, a goat so beloved that she was lifted into the stars as the constellation Capricorn. Her name was Amalthea, and without her, the entire Greek pantheon might have ended up in Cronus's stomach. She is the original 'greatest of all time'—the GOAT, literally.

Amalthea is consistently depicted as nurturing, protective, and generous. As a goat, she tolerated the infant Zeus's rough play, including the breaking of her horn. As a nymph, she is wise and devoted, hiding the infant god from his father Cronus. She is also associated with abundance and the giving of gifts—her horn becomes the symbol of inexhaustible plenty.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Amalthea (Ἀμάλθεια) is traditionally derived from Greek ἀμαλός (amalos, 'soft', 'tender') or ἀμάλθεια ('to nourish', 'to enrich'). The name thus carries the sense of 'tender nourisher' or 'rich one', fitting her role as a nurturing figure in Greek myth.

Powers
Milk of the Divine Nurse

Amalthea's milk was not ordinary goat's milk—it nourished the king of the gods. In some traditions, her milk was so potent that it could confer immortality or divine strength. The infant Zeus's growth into the all-powerful god is attributed to her milk.

Limit: The power of her milk is only attested for Zeus; there is no evidence it would work for mortals.

The Cornucopia (Horn of Plenty)

Amalthea's broken horn, filled with fruit and flowers by the nymphs or by Zeus himself, became the Cornucopia—a vessel that endlessly produced whatever its owner desired. It became a symbol of abundance and prosperity.

Limit: The horn's power is described as inexhaustible, but its use is never shown to be weaponized or harmful.

The Aegis (Shield of Zeus)

According to some late sources (scholia on Homer), Zeus used Amalthea's skin to make his aegis, the divine shield. The skin was said to be impenetrable and terrifying.

Limit: This is a late and marginal tradition; the more common myth says the aegis was made from the skin of the giant Aegis (or from the goat Amalthea, but this is disputed).

Form & Signs

Appearance — Amalthea appears in two main forms. As a goat: a she-goat of extraordinary beauty and size, with a luxuriant white or golden coat, and a single horn that could produce whatever the owner desired (the Cornucopia). As a nymph: a beautiful young woman, sometimes depicted with a goat's horn in her hand or at her side, or with the infant Zeus in her arms. In later Roman art, she is shown as a dignified matronly figure holding the cornucopia.

Symbols — The Cornucopia (horn of plenty) is her primary symbol. She is also associated with the goat, with milk, with the infant Zeus, and with the aegis (the shield of Zeus made from her skin, according to some sources). In Roman coins and art, she is shown with the cornucopia as a symbol of imperial abundance.

Domains — Crete, specifically the Dictaean Cave (Dictaeon Andron) on Mount Dicte, or the Idaean Cave on Mount Ida—both are claimed as the site of Zeus's birth and nurture. The city of Amaltheia in Crete was named in her honor. In the heavens, she is identified with the constellation Capricorn (though this identification is not universal in ancient sources).

Accounts I
The Nursing of Zeus in the Dictaean CaveCallimachus, Hymn to Zeus; Hyginus, Fabulae; Diodorus Siculus, Library of History

Hellenistic and Roman (story told in detail); earlier references in Hesiod

Rhea, wife of Cronus, gives birth to Zeus in Crete, hidden from her husband who devours his children. She entrusts the infant to the care of the nymph Amalthea (or to the goat Amalthea, depending on the version). The goat nurses Zeus, and the Curetes (armed dancers) clang their shields to hide the baby's cries. Zeus grows to adulthood, overthrows Cronus, and rewards his nurse by placing her among the stars or by giving her horn divine power. Zeus survives to become king of the gods. Amalthea is honored with a constellation (Capricorn, in some versions) or with the Cornucopia as a gift. The city of Amaltheia in Crete is named after her.

Accounts II
The Broken Horn and the CornucopiaOvid, Fasti, Book 5; Hyginus, Fabulae 139

1st century CE

In Ovid's version, the goat Amalthea is nursing Zeus in a cave. The infant god, playing roughly, breaks one of her horns. The nymphs (or Zeus himself) fill the horn with fruits and flowers, and it becomes the Cornucopia—a symbol of endless abundance. The goat is then placed among the stars as Capricorn. The Cornucopia becomes a lasting symbol of prosperity in Greek and Roman culture, and later in Western art and iconography.

The Hidden Infant and the CuretesHyginus, Fabulae 139; Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 1.1.6-7

Hellenistic

The nymph Amalthea (sometimes with her sister Melissa) hides the infant Zeus in a cave. To prevent Cronus from hearing the baby's cries, the Curetes perform a wild dance, clashing their spears and shields. Amalthea's role is the tender care of the child, while the Curetes provide the martial cover. Zeus remains hidden and survives to overthrow Cronus. The myth explains the origin of the Curetes' ritual dance in Crete.

Amalthea is a Greek goddess. — Amalthea is a nymph or a goat, not a goddess. She has no cult, no temple, and no independent divine status. She is a supporting figure in the myth of Zeus's infancy.

The Cornucopia was always Amalthea's. — The cornucopia as a symbol predates the specific myth of Amalthea. It appears in art and literature before the name is attached to the goat. The river-god Achelous also has a horn that becomes the cornucopia in a separate myth.

Amalthea is the mother of Zeus. — Amalthea is the foster-mother or nurse of Zeus. His actual mother is Rhea. The confusion arises from the common use of 'nurse' in translations.

The goat Amalthea was a normal animal. — The goat that nursed Zeus was a divine or semi-divine creature, capable of producing the Cornucopia and being placed among the stars. She is not a literal farm animal.
