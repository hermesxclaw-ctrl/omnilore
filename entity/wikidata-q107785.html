Form

Amalthea appears in two main forms. As a goat: a she-goat of extraordinary beauty and size, with a luxuriant white or golden coat, and a single horn that could produce whatever the owner desired (the Cornucopia). As a nymph: a beautiful young woman, sometimes depicted with a goat's horn in her hand or at her side, or with the infant Zeus in her arms. In later Roman art, she is shown as a dignified matronly figure holding the cornucopia.

Powers

Milk of the Divine Nurse Amalthea's milk was not ordinary goat's milk—it nourished the king of the gods. In some traditions, her milk was so potent that it could confer immortality or divine strength. The infant Zeus's growth into the all-powerful god is attributed to her milk. Callimachus, Hymn to Zeus; Hyginus, Fabulae The power of her milk is only attested for Zeus; there is no evidence it would work for mortals. Some sources say Zeus was fed on milk, others on honey (the Melissae, bee-nymphs). The traditions are not reconciled in antiquity.

The Cornucopia (Horn of Plenty) Amalthea's broken horn, filled with fruit and flowers by the nymphs or by Zeus himself, became the Cornucopia—a vessel that endlessly produced whatever its owner desired. It became a symbol of abundance and prosperity. Ovid, Fasti; Hyginus, Fabulae; later Roman reception The horn's power is described as inexhaustible, but its use is never shown to be weaponized or harmful. Ovid says the horn was broken by Zeus himself; Hyginus says the goat broke it on a tree. The filling of the horn is variously attributed to the nymphs, to Zeus, or to the river-god Achelous.

The Aegis (Shield of Zeus) According to some late sources (scholia on Homer), Zeus used Amalthea's skin to make his aegis, the divine shield. The skin was said to be impenetrable and terrifying. Scholia on Homer's Iliad; late antique commentaries This is a late and marginal tradition; the more common myth says the aegis was made from the skin of the giant Aegis (or from the goat Amalthea, but this is disputed). The aegis is more commonly associated with Athena and Zeus as a separate divine object, not necessarily made from Amalthea's skin.

Accounts

Rhea, wife of Cronus, gives birth to Zeus in Crete, hidden from her husband who devours his children. She entrusts the infant to the care of the nymph Amalthea (or to the goat Amalthea, depending on the version). The goat nurses Zeus, and the Curetes (armed dancers) clang their shields to hide the baby's cries. Zeus grows to adulthood, overthrows Cronus, and rewards his nurse by placing her among the stars or by giving her horn divine power.

In Ovid's version, the goat Amalthea is nursing Zeus in a cave. The infant god, playing roughly, breaks one of her horns. The nymphs (or Zeus himself) fill the horn with fruits and flowers, and it becomes the Cornucopia—a symbol of endless abundance. The goat is then placed among the stars as Capricorn.

The nymph Amalthea (sometimes with her sister Melissa) hides the infant Zeus in a cave. To prevent Cronus from hearing the baby's cries, the Curetes perform a wild dance, clashing their spears and shields. Amalthea's role is the tender care of the child, while the Curetes provide the martial cover.

Content Notes

The story of Zeus's infancy is framed by Cronus's practice of swallowing his children to prevent a prophecy of being overthrown. Amalthea's role is to protect the infant from this fate. The dark backdrop is the attempted filicide, though it is not depicted graphically in the sources.

This is a foundational myth of succession in Greek cosmology. The theme of the devouring father is widely interpreted by scholars as reflecting anxieties about generational conflict and the cycle of power.

Hesiod, Theogony 453-491; Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 1.1.5

In the version where Amalthea is a goat, her horn is broken by the infant god, and her skin is used (in some late sources) to make the aegis. This is a mythic reflection of animal sacrifice and the use of animal products for divine purposes, not a historical account of animal cruelty.

The myth likely reflects ancient practices of animal sacrifice and the use of animal hides in ritual. The goat is honored with a constellation, suggesting a ritualized understanding of the animal's role.

Ovid, Fasti 5; Scholia on Homer's Iliad

Scholarly Debates
Identity: Goat or Nymph?
Amalthea is the goat herself (Callimachus, Hymn to Zeus). ⇌ Amalthea is a nymph who owned the goat (Ovid, Fasti; Hyginus, Fabulae).
Evidence for A: Callimachus explicitly calls the goat 'Amalthea'.
Evidence for B: Ovid and Hyginus describe Amalthea as a nymph, with the goat as a separate (unnamed) animal.
Location of Zeus's Birth
Mount Dicte (Dictaean Cave) in eastern Crete. ⇌ Mount Ida (Idaean Cave) in central Crete.
Evidence for A: Callimachus, Hymn to Zeus, places the birth on Dicte.
Evidence for B: Apollodorus and Diodorus Siculus place it on Ida.
The Fate of the Broken Horn
The horn was broken by Zeus and filled by the nymphs, becoming the Cornucopia. ⇌ The horn was broken by the goat accidentally on a tree, and Zeus later gave it divine power.
Evidence for A: Ovid, Fasti 5.
Evidence for B: Hyginus, Fabulae 139.
The Constellation Capricorn
Capricorn is Amalthea the goat. ⇌ Capricorn is a different goat (the one who broke her horn, or the goat of the sea-god Pan).
Evidence for A: Eratosthenes, Catasterismi.
Evidence for B: Hyginus, De Astronomia; some sources associate Capricorn with Pan.
What We Don't Know

The earliest textual references to Amalthea by name are Hellenistic (3rd century BCE); the original oral tradition is lost. The visual evidence from Crete is suggestive but not definitively identified as Amalthea in all cases. The exact relationship between the goat and nymph traditions cannot be fully resolved. The reception of Amalthea in non-Western cultures is not documented in the available sources. The naming of Jupiter's moon Amalthea is noted but the full history of the naming decision (including any alternative proposals) is not documented here.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Amalthea as the original foster mother to a future king. She's the wet nurse who raised a baby who would become the most powerful god in the Greek pantheon. She's also the origin of the 'horn of plenty'—the symbol of abundance you see at Thanksgiving. If you've ever seen a picture of a goat with a horn full of fruit, that's her. She's the mythological reason we associate goats with abundance and nurture.
