I learned my craft in the north, from the Sámi who taught me to travel in dreams and see the threads of fate. My sons are kings because I made them so. The skalds call me witch — I call myself mother.

Who It Is

Gunnhildr konungamóðir (Gunnhild the King-Mother) is one of the most compelling female figures in Norse saga literature — a queen, sorceress, and political mastermind who dominates Norwegian politics for decades. She is the wife of King Eiríkr Bloodaxe, then the mother of multiple kings after Eiríkr's death (hence 'king-mother'), ruling as de facto regent. According to Heimskringla, Gunnhildr spent her youth in Finnmörk (the far north) learning seiðr (seeress-magic) from two Sámi (Finnish) sorcerers. She is described as beautiful, intelligent, persuasive, and utterly ruthless in pursuit of power for herself and her sons. In Egils saga, she is the arch-nemesis of Egil Skallagrímsson: she attempts to poison him, uses sorcery to curse him, and when Egil erects a níðstöng (scorn-pole) against her and Eiríkr, she is the one who pushes for his execution in York. In Njáls saga, she is involved in the complex politics of Norwegian-Icelandic relations, manipulating jarls and kings. Gunnhildr's saga portrayal is deeply misogynistic (the male saga-authors depict politically powerful women as sexually voracious witches), and historians debate whether she was a real historical figure or a composite villainess. Some scholars argue she was a Danish princess, others that she was from Hålogaland in northern Norway. Regardless, the saga-Gunnhildr represents the anxiety of male power confronted with female political brilliance: she rules through her sons, controls kings through seduction and magic, and cannot be defeated by conventional male means. She is a witch-queen in the full mythic sense — female power that the patriarchal saga world can only understand as supernatural and threatening.
