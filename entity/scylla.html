Who It Is

Imagine being a sailor in the ancient world, your ship caught between a rock that barks like a puppy and a whirlpool that could swallow your entire crew. This is the choice Odysseus faced, and it has haunted the Western imagination for nearly three thousand years. Scylla — the six-headed, twelve-footed terror who plucks men from the deck like grapes — is not just a monster; she is the embodiment of the impossible choice, the lesser of two evils, the rock in 'between a rock and a hard place.'

Scylla is not a hunter in the active sense; she is a sit-and-wait predator. She extends her long necks from her cave, plucks sailors from the passing ship, and devours them. She does not pursue prey beyond her cave. Circe describes her as 'a thing that cannot die,' suggesting immortality or at least an inability to be killed by mortals. She is essentially a fixed hazard, a natural disaster with teeth.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name is generally thought to derive from the Greek verb skyllein (σκύλλειν), meaning 'to rend' or 'to tear' — a fitting name for a creature that literally tears sailors apart. Some ancient sources also connected it to skylax (σκύλαξ), 'puppy' or 'dog', referencing its canine-like heads.

Pronunciation — SIL-uh (English), SKEEL-lah (Classical Greek: Σκύλλα)

Aliases — Skylla, Scylla of the Rock, The Barking Monster, The Six-Headed Dog

Powers
Six-headed predation

Scylla has six heads, each on a long, extendable neck. Each head has three rows of teeth, allowing her to seize and devour multiple victims simultaneously. In the Odyssey, she takes six of Odysseus's men — one per head — in a single pass.

Limit: She cannot leave her cave; she is a fixed hazard, not a mobile predator.

Immortality (or invulnerability)

Circe tells Odysseus that Scylla is 'a thing that cannot die' — an immortal monster. This may mean she is a divine or semi-divine being, or simply that no mortal weapon can harm her.

Limit: Unknown — the text does not specify the nature of her immortality.

Metamorphosis (in later tradition)

In Ovid's version, Scylla was originally a beautiful nymph who was transformed into a monster by Circe's magic. This is a passive power — she did not choose her form.

Limit: The transformation is permanent; there is no known cure.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Homer's description is the canonical one: twelve feet (legs), six long necks, each ending in a grisly head with three rows of thick, close-packed teeth 'full of dark death.' Her voice is described as like that of a newborn puppy — a disturbing, pathetic contrast to her monstrous size. Symbols — In art, Scylla is symbolized by her six dog-like heads, her fish tail, and her association with the sea-cliff. In literature, she has become the archetype of the 'lesser of two evils' — the choice between a rock and a hard place. Her name is the root of the English word 'scylla' used in the idiom 'between Scylla and Charybdis.'

Domains — Her domain is the rocky promontory on the Italian side of the Strait of Messina, opposite the whirlpool Charybdis. In the Odyssey, Circe tells Odysseus that the two are so close that a ship passing between them risks being sucked into Charybdis while trying to avoid Scylla, and vice versa. The strait is the narrow waterway between Sicily and mainland Italy.

Accounts I
Odysseus Passes Scylla and CharybdisHomer, Odyssey 12

8th century BCE (traditional date of composition)

After Circe warns him of the danger, Odysseus must sail his ship between Scylla's rock and Charybdis's whirlpool. He chooses to sail closer to Scylla, reasoning that losing six men to the monster is better than losing the entire ship to the whirlpool. He does not tell his crew about Scylla, hoping to spare them the terror. As the ship passes, Scylla strikes, snatching six men — 'the most powerful and the strongest' — who cry out in their final moments, reaching their hands toward Odysseus. The ship passes through, and the survivors mourn their comrades. The loss of six men is a significant blow to Odysseus's crew, who are already depleted. It is one of several losses that eventually leave Odysseus alone on his journey home.

The Argonauts Pass Scylla and CharybdisApollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 4

3rd century BCE

Jason and the Argonauts must also pass the strait on their return journey. They are saved by the intervention of Hera and Thetis, who guide the ship safely through. Thetis (a sea goddess) steers the Argo past Scylla's rock, and the crew passes without loss. The Argonauts escape unharmed, demonstrating that divine aid can overcome even the most deadly hazards.

Accounts II
Heracles Kills Scylla (later tradition)Various (e.g., Servius's commentary on Virgil, later scholia)

Late Roman era (uncertain date)

In a tradition preserved in scholia (marginal notes in manuscripts), Heracles is said to have killed Scylla during one of his labors or travels. This contradicts the Homeric claim of her immortality, suggesting either that the later tradition demoted her to a mortal monster or that Heracles's divine strength was sufficient to kill her. If Scylla was killed, she was subsequently resurrected (or the tradition was not widely accepted, as she is clearly alive in later sources).

Glaucus and Circe (Ovid's backstory)Ovid, Metamorphoses 13-14

c. 8 CE

In Ovid's telling, Scylla was originally a beautiful nymph courted by the sea-god Glaucus. Glaucus, rejected by Scylla, seeks the help of the sorceress Circe. Circe falls in love with Glaucus herself, and when he rejects her, she transforms Scylla into a monster out of jealousy. Scylla, horrified by her new form, throws herself into the sea and becomes the monster of the strait. This backstory explains Scylla's monstrous form as a punishment inflicted by a jealous rival. It also creates a connection between Scylla and Circe, two figures who are otherwise separate in the Odyssey.

Scylla is a 'mermaid' with a fish tail and dog heads. — This is a later artistic convention (Greek vase painting, Roman mosaics). Homer describes her as having six long necks with heads and twelve feet, but no fish tail. The mermaid-like depiction is a visual shorthand, not a literary description.

Scylla and Charybdis are two separate monsters that can be avoided by sailing between them. — They are so close together that sailing between them is impossible. Circe explicitly says that a ship trying to avoid one will be caught by the other. The choice is between losing six men (Scylla) or the entire ship (Charybdis).

Scylla is a 'female' monster in the same way as a human woman. — In Homer, Scylla has no gender beyond the grammatical feminine. She is a 'monster' (pelôr) without human characteristics. The idea of her as a 'she' is a later literary and artistic convention.
