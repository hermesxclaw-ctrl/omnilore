Who It Is

Scylla is a legendary man-eating monster who dwells on one side of a narrow channel opposite the whirlpool Charybdis, terrorizing the Strait of Messina between Italy and Sicily. Homer describes her as having twelve legs, six long necks with grisly heads each containing three rows of razor-sharp teeth, and a bark like a puppy. She lurks in a cave high on a cliff, snatching sailors from passing ships and devouring them alive. In the Odyssey, Odysseus loses six men to Scylla as he navigates the strait, choosing to sacrifice a few rather than risk losing his entire ship to Charybdis. Later authors gave Scylla an origin story: Ovid's Metamorphoses recounts that she was once a beautiful nymph transformed by the jealous Circe (who was in love with the sea-god Glaucus, whom Scylla had rejected). Circe poisoned Scylla's bathing pool, turning her lower half into a ring of barking dogs' heads fused to her body. Scylla is one of the enduring symbols of impossible choices — being 'between Scylla and Charybdis' means choosing between two equally terrible fates.

Origins

In Homer, Scylla is simply a monster with no parentage given (her mother is 'Crataeis' — possibly a name for Hecate). Ovid provides the most famous backstory: Scylla was a nymph, beloved by the sea-god Glaucus. When Glaucus asked Circe for a love potion, Circe fell in love with him instead. Spurned by Glaucus, Circe poisoned Scylla's bath, transforming her lower body into a ring of snarling dogs.

Attested in Homer's Odyssey / Ovid's Metamorphoses / Virgil's AeneidEra Archaic Greece (c. 750 BCE onward)

Form

Homer's description is the canonical one: twelve feet (legs), six long necks, each ending in a grisly head with three rows of thick, close-packed teeth 'full of dark death.' Her voice is described as like that of a newborn puppy — a disturbing, pathetic contrast to her monstrous size. She lives in a cave high on a sheer cliff, so high that an arrow from a ship's deck cannot reach it. In later art (especially Greek vase painting), she is depicted as a mermaid-like creature with a woman's torso and a fish tail from which dog-headed protrusions emerge — a conflation of the Homeric monster with later iconographic traditions.

Powers

Six-headed predation Scylla has six heads, each on a long, extendable neck. Each head has three rows of teeth, allowing her to seize and devour multiple victims simultaneously. In the Odyssey, she takes six of Odysseus's men — one per head — in a single pass. Homer, Odyssey 12 She cannot leave her cave; she is a fixed hazard, not a mobile predator. None — this is consistent across all ancient sources.

Immortality (or invulnerability) Circe tells Odysseus that Scylla is 'a thing that cannot die' — an immortal monster. This may mean she is a divine or semi-divine being, or simply that no mortal weapon can harm her. Homer, Odyssey 12 Unknown — the text does not specify the nature of her immortality. Some later sources imply she was killed by Heracles (see stories below), suggesting she was vulnerable to a demi-god's strength.

Metamorphosis (in later tradition) In Ovid's version, Scylla was originally a beautiful nymph who was transformed into a monster by Circe's magic. This is a passive power — she did not choose her form. Ovid, Metamorphoses 13-14 The transformation is permanent; there is no known cure. Homer never mentions her human origins; Ovid's backstory is a later invention.

Behavior & Habitat

Scylla is not a hunter in the active sense; she is a sit-and-wait predator. She extends her long necks from her cave, plucks sailors from the passing ship, and devours them. She does not pursue prey beyond her cave. Circe describes her as 'a thing that cannot die,' suggesting immortality or at least an inability to be killed by mortals. She is essentially a fixed hazard, a natural disaster with teeth.

Accounts

After Circe warns him of the danger, Odysseus must sail his ship between Scylla's rock and Charybdis's whirlpool. He chooses to sail closer to Scylla, reasoning that losing six men to the monster is better than losing the entire ship to the whirlpool. He does not tell his crew about Scylla, hoping to spare them the terror. As the ship passes, Scylla strikes, snatching six men — 'the most powerful and the strongest' — who cry out in their final moments, reaching their hands toward Odysseus. The ship passes through, and the survivors mourn their comrades.

Jason and the Argonauts must also pass the strait on their return journey. They are saved by the intervention of Hera and Thetis, who guide the ship safely through. Thetis (a sea goddess) steers the Argo past Scylla's rock, and the crew passes without loss.

In a tradition preserved in scholia (marginal notes in manuscripts), Heracles is said to have killed Scylla during one of his labors or travels. This contradicts the Homeric claim of her immortality, suggesting either that the later tradition demoted her to a mortal monster or that Heracles's divine strength was sufficient to kill her.

In Ovid's telling, Scylla was originally a beautiful nymph courted by the sea-god Glaucus. Glaucus, rejected by Scylla, seeks the help of the sorceress Circe. Circe falls in love with Glaucus herself, and when he rejects her, she transforms Scylla into a monster out of jealousy. Scylla, horrified by her new form, throws herself into the sea and becomes the monster of the strait.

Field Notes

☘ Scylla's cry is described by Homer as 'like that of a newborn puppy' — a disturbing contrast to her monstrous size.

☘ The idiom 'between Scylla and Charybdis' is the ancient Greek equivalent of 'between a rock and a hard place.'

☘ Scylla is one of the few monsters in Greek mythology that is explicitly said to be immortal.

☘ In the video game Hades II, Scylla appears as a boss with a rock-band aesthetic, a modern reinterpretation of the ancient monster.

☘ The Strait of Messina, where Scylla was said to dwell, is a real place — and it does have dangerous currents and whirlpools, which likely inspired the myth.

What We Don't Know

The primary gap is the lack of a definitive ancient source for Scylla's origin. Homer does not provide a backstory, and later sources contradict each other. The Heracles tradition is particularly poorly attested. The visual tradition (vase painting) is well-documented but requires specialized art-historical analysis. Modern reception in games and anime is extensive but not exhaustively indexed.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Scylla as the ancient world's version of a natural disaster with a personality. She is not a villain with a plan; she is a hazard, a fixed point of danger that sailors must navigate. She is less like a dragon guarding a treasure and more like a hurricane or a reef — a force of nature that does not care about your intentions. Her immortality makes her less like a monster you can fight and more like a law of the sea: you do not defeat Scylla; you survive her.
