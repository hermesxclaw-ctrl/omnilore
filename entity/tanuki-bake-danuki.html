Bake-danuki (化け狸)

Etymology

From Japanese 'tanuki' (狸), the native name for the raccoon dog. 'Bake-danuki' (化け狸) means 'transforming raccoon dog.' The English translation 'raccoon dog' is misleading — Tanuki are canids, not raccoons.

Cultural Ownership

Japanese folklore, with roots in Chinese fox-spirit traditions that evolved into a distinctly Japanese yōkai form

Archive Dossier — Approach

Picture a moonlit night in rural Japan. From the forest comes the sound of drumming — not from any human instrument, but from a creature patting its swollen belly with childlike joy. It wears a straw hat, carries a sake bottle, and sports a grin too wide for any mortal animal. This is the Tanuki, Japan's most beloved trickster, and its drumming is an invitation to step into a world where raccoon dogs pay their bar tabs with leaves that turn back into forest litter by morning.

The tanuki is Japan's great comic shapeshifter — a real animal (the raccoon dog) wrapped in eight centuries of folklore that credit it with transformation magic, belly-drum music, and a legendary set of magical, expandable body parts capable of becoming eight tatami mats, fishing nets, umbrellas, or an entire shop. Where the kitsune's deceptions are elegant and often cruel, the tanuki's are earthy and absurd: it transforms into a teakettle and gets stuck halfway (the beloved Bunbuku Chagama story), turns leaves into counterfeit money to buy sake, and drums on its swollen belly under the moon for the sheer joy of it.

Who It Is

The tanuki is Japan's great comic shapeshifter — a real animal (the raccoon dog) wrapped in eight centuries of folklore that credit it with transformation magic, belly-drum music, and a legendary set of magical, expandable body parts capable of becoming eight tatami mats, fishing nets, umbrellas, or an entire shop. Where the kitsune's deceptions are elegant and often cruel, the tanuki's are earthy and absurd: it transforms into a teakettle and gets stuck halfway (the beloved Bunbuku Chagama story), turns leaves into counterfeit money to buy sake, and drums on its swollen belly under the moon for the sheer joy of it. Statues of tanuki — straw hat, sake flask, promissory note, guileless grin — stand outside restaurants and bars across Japan as charms for generosity and good business. The tanuki is proof that a culture's trickster does not have to be sinister; it can be a jolly, drunken failure who wins your affection precisely because its schemes never quite work.

Archive Dossier — Description

Summary

The Tanuki, or Japanese raccoon dog (Nyctereutes procyonoides viverrinus), occupies a unique position in Japanese folklore as both a real animal and a shapeshifting yōkai known as the Bake-danuki. Unlike the malevolent fox-spirit Kitsune, the Tanuki is characterized as a jovial, mischievous trickster — fond of sake, drumming on its belly, and playing pranks on humans. The creature's most distinctive folkloric feature is its enormous, malleable scrotum (kin-tama, literally 'golden balls'), which it can stretch to enormous sizes to serve as a drum, a raincoat, a boat, or even a weapon — a symbol of good fortune and financial luck through wordplay (kin-tama sounds like 'golden balls'). Tanuki are associated with eight special traits depicted on their lucky statues: a sedge hat for protection, big eyes for perception, a sake bottle for virtue, a promissory note for trust, a big belly for decisiveness, a friendly smile, a big tail for steadiness, and large testicles for financial luck. The most famous Tanuki legend is Bunbuku Chagama ('The Lucky Teapot'), in which a Tanuki transforms into a teapot to repay a poor man's kindness. In Buddhist contexts, Tanuki are sometimes depicted as disguised priests, parodying religious authority. The Shigaraki region of Japan is famous for producing ceramic Tanuki statues (Shigaraki-yaki), which are displayed outside businesses and homes across Japan as symbols of prosperity. The creature achieved global recognition through Studio Ghibli's 'Pom Poko' (1994), directed by Isao Takahata, which depicted a community of Tanuki fighting against suburban development using their shapeshifting abilities — including the famous 'testicle attack' scene rooted in traditional folklore. The Tanuki's association with good fortune is rooted in elaborate wordplay: 'tanuki' can be read as 'ta-nuki' (他抜き, 'surpassing others'), making the creature an emblem of competitive success in business. The eight special traits of the Tanuki statue — the 'happi coat' — each carry specific meanings: the sedge hat (kasa) protects against trouble; the big eyes (ōkina me) perceive the environment wisely; the sake bottle (tokkuri) represents the virtue of not losing one's temper; the promissory note (tegata) symbolizes trust and reliability; the big belly (ōki na hara) signifies decisiveness and composure; the friendly smile (egao) invites customers; the big tail (ōkina shippo) represents steadiness and strength until the end; and the large testicles (ōkina kintama) stretch to symbolize financial expansion and luck. In Buddhist folklore, Tanuki are sometimes depicted as yōkai who disguise themselves as priests — a satirical commentary on corrupt clergy, with the Tanuki's priestly robes barely concealing its animal nature. The creature's shapeshifting is not unlimited: Tanuki cannot transform into anything truly holy or sacred, and in some tales, observant humans can detect a Tanuki disguise by noticing that its reflection in water or mirrors reveals its true animal form. The Tanuki's belly drumming (hara-tsuzumi) is not merely playful — in some traditions, the sound can lull humans into enchanted sleep or disorient travelers, leading them deep into the forest where other yōkai await. The Shigaraki ceramic Tanuki tradition dates to the late Edo period and was popularized by a potter who made Tanuki statues as a symbol of prosperity during a time of economic hardship — the statues became so associated with the region that Shigaraki is now virtually synonymous with Tanuki iconography worldwide.

Origins

Early tales cast the tanuki as a sinister possessing spirit alongside the fox, but by the Edo period it had mellowed into a comic figure. The famous stretching-skin motif originated with Kanazawa goldsmiths, who wrapped gold in tanuki hide to hammer it thin — a tanuki skin can stretch gold to eight mats, the saying went, and folklore took the image and ran somewhere goldsmiths never intended.

Archive Dossier — Powers

Shapeshifting: known for eight classic transformations (hachi-henge) — can become humans, objects, or other animals

Leaf magic: uses leaves placed on the head to power transformations; can turn leaves into money (which later reverts)

Belly drumming (hara-tsuzumi): strikes its distended belly like a drum, producing music that lures or disorients travelers

Testicle manipulation: can stretch kin-tama to enormous sizes for various practical uses

Illusion creation and environment manipulation

Possession of humans (in more malevolent variants)

Form

A plump raccoon dog, often bipedal in art, wearing a straw hat and carrying a sake bottle. The classic eight-symbol statue form — hat, watchful eyes, friendly face, sake flask, promissory ledger, big belly, generous anatomy, sturdy tail — encodes eight business virtues.

Powers

Shapeshifting into people, objects, even Buddhist monks; illusion-craft including leaf-money and, in Meiji-era tales, phantom trains that vanished before collision; belly-drumming (tanuki-bayashi) heard on moonlit nights; prodigious anatomical elasticity.

Weaknesses & Wards

Vanity, sake, and overconfidence. Tanuki disguises fail when the creature gets drunk, laughs, or is startled; hunters' dogs see through the illusion instantly.

Archive Dossier — Stories

Bunbuku Chagama (The Lucky Teapot)

Source Edo period folktale, earliest written version c. 18th century

Summary

A poor priest or tinker rescued a Tanuki from a trap. In gratitude, the Tanuki transformed into a beautiful iron teapot. The man sold the teapot to a temple, but when the monks placed it over the fire, the Tanuki — unable to endure the heat — sprouted legs and a tail and ran back to its benefactor. The man then set up a traveling show featuring the 'dancing teapot,' becoming prosperous. The Tanuki's loyalty and the man's kindness are celebrated as the moral core of the tale.

The Tanuki and the Fox (Kachi-Kachi Yama variant)

Source Japanese folk tradition

Summary

In a darker tale later adapted for children, a Tanuki murders an old woman and serves her to her husband in soup. A rabbit befriends the grieving old man and exacts elaborate revenge on the Tanuki, tricking it into burning its back, coating the wounds with pepper paste, and ultimately drowning. This story presents the Tanuki in its more malevolent, trickster aspect and is notably darker than the friendly Tanuki of later commercial symbolism.

The Tanuki of Yashima

Source Kagawa Prefecture folklore

Summary

A legendary Tanuki named 'Yashima no Hage-tanuki' terrorized travelers on the Yashima plateau. It would transform into a massive wall blocking the road, then into a beautiful woman, and finally into a giant Tanuki to scare victims to death. A brave samurai eventually defeated it, and the Tanuki's spirit was enshrined to prevent further mischief. The site remains a local landmark.

Shojo-ji Tanuki Bayashi (The Tanuki Drummers of Shojo-ji Temple)

Source Chiba Prefecture folklore, popularized as a children's song

Summary

A group of Tanuki living near Shojo-ji Temple would gather nightly to beat their bellies like drums, producing irresistible music. Their drumming became so famous that people traveled from distant provinces to hear it. The story became a beloved nursery song: 'Sho- sho- shojo-ji, Shojo-ji no niwa wa, tsukiyo da, minna dete koi koi koi' — a call for everyone to come out and join the Tanuki's moonlit drum circle.

The Sake-Drinking Tanuki of Shigaraki

Source Shiga Prefecture tradition

Summary

A Tanuki took the form of a traveling monk and visited a sake brewery every night, drinking its fill and praising the brewmaster's skill. When the brewer discovered the deception, instead of anger, he left the best sake out as an offering. The Tanuki, touched by this kindness, blessed the brewery with eternal prosperity. The brewmaster became the wealthiest in Shigaraki, and Tanuki statues began appearing outside breweries across Japan as symbols of good fortune.

Archive Dossier — Contradictions

The real Tanuki (Nyctereutes procyonoides) is a shy, nocturnal canid; its folkloric counterpart is gregarious, boisterous, and magical — a complete inversion

Tanuki range from harmless pranksters to murderous monsters across different tales, with no clear regional pattern determining which aspect dominates

The relationship between Chinese fox-spirit (huli jing) traditions and Japanese Tanuki remains debated: some scholars see direct borrowing, others independent development

The distinction between Tanuki and Mujina (badger) is regionally inconsistent — in some prefectures, the two terms are used interchangeably, suggesting that the folkloric categories are more fluid than modern taxonomies suggest

The Tanuki's 'cute' modern image contrasts sharply with Edo-period depictions, in which Tanuki were frequently portrayed as malevolent, murderous tricksters — the sanitization represents a significant cultural shift whose causes are debated

Archive Dossier — Reception

One of the most beloved yōkai in Japanese popular culture — Tanuki statues appear outside an estimated 3 million+ businesses and homes across Japan

The Shigaraki ceramic Tanuki industry produces thousands of statues annually, with the largest standing over 8 meters tall at Yashima temple

Pom Poko (1994) brought Tanuki folklore to international audiences, grossing over ¥4.4 billion in Japan

Western reception often fixates on the 'testicle' aspect, which in Japanese culture is a playful symbol of luck rather than an object of shock — a cultural translation gap

In video games (Super Mario, Animal Crossing, Okami), Tanuki appear as recurring characters, usually retaining their playful and mercantile associations

The Tanuki's commercial success as a mascot has generated academic interest in the 'commodification of folklore' — scholars debate whether the cute ceramic statues represent cultural continuity or the hollowing out of authentic folk belief

In Japanese video games, Tanuki appear as recurring characters in the Mario franchise (Tanooki Mario), Animal Crossing (Tom Nook), and the influential Ōkami, where they feature as both allies and tricksters

Cultural Significance

The Bunbuku Chagama teakettle tale is taught to every Japanese child. Studio Ghibli's Pom Poko (1994) is a full-length elegy for tanuki folklore colliding with suburban development. Shigaraki ware tanuki statues are among Japan's most-produced folk ceramics.
