Who It Is

There is a moment, at the headwaters of the River Hi, when an exiled storm god looks up from a chopstick bobbing past and finds an old couple weeping over their last daughter — because something with 'eyes like red ground-cherries, covered in moss, cypress and cedar, spanning eight valleys and eight peaks, its belly oozing blood' has eaten the other seven, one every year. Japanese myth runs on that single image of the eight-headed, eight-tailed serpent Yamata no Orochi: a mountain that hunts, a river that drowns, and yet a vessel out of which, hacked apart and drunk on sake, the sacred Grass-Cutting Sword is born to crown an empire. In the same legends that raised it as a thing of dread, it survived its own death — as a mountain god at Mount Ibuki, as the drowned child-emperor Antoku returning to reclaim his blade, and eventually as the father of Shuten Dōji, the greatest oni of them all

In the myth it is an annual predator that devours one maiden per year (or newborn children, in the River E variant), and it is charmed/neutralized by intoxicating sake — greedily drinking from each of the eight tubs until it passes out. It is described as an 'awful god' (in the Heike and Nihon Shoki) rather than a mere beast, and it is propitiated as both bringer of flood/disaster and (in its Ibuki Daimyōjin form) as a deity requiring appeasement. Its defining behavioral trait across all versions is its vulnerability to alcohol and sleep.

Powers
Polycephaly / multi-headed and multi-tailed form

Eight heads and eight tails that span eight valleys and eight peaks; each head can drink a full cask of sake independently. The eight heads also give it the ability to menace or consume eight victims in a cycle.

Limit: Each head acts as a separate drinking 'mouth' — allowing Susanoo to neutralize all eight simultaneously with the sake trap.

Annual maiden/child predation

Devours one of the deity couple's daughters each year until only Kushinadahime remains; in the River E (Aki) variant it comes to devour each newborn as it is born.

Limit: None in the text — it is unstoppable by the earthly deities Ashinazuchi/Tenazuchi; only the heavenly kami Susanoo can defeat it.

Toxicity / poisoned breath (Jadokkeshin reading)

In the Gion-cult exegesis, Yamata no Orochi as an incarnation of Jadokkeshin (蛇毒気神, 'Deity of the Poisoned Snake Breath'), consort of the epidemic god Gozu Tennō, carries a snake-poison register that connects it to pestilence.

Limit: This is an interpretative/deified layer, not a narrative feat in the primary slaying myth.

Vulnerability to alcohol (the defining weakness)

The creature cannot resist sake: in the Kojiki/Shoki it drinks each of the eight casks of eightfold-refined sake and falls asleep, allowing Susanoo to slaughter it. This is the story's central mechanism and the reason the hero wins.

Limit: Complete when intoxicated — it does not rouse before being cut apart.

Incarnational / avataric persistence

Rather than being wholly destroyed, the serpent 'survives' as a deity: it is deified as Ibuki Daimyōjin at Mount Ibuki, is identified with the Nāga Maiden (Longnü) of the Lotus Sutra and with Jadokkeshin, and is said to reincarnate as Emperor Antoku to reclaim the stolen sword.

Limit: These are later religious/legendary layers, some contradictory, and mostly regional or exegetical rather than part of the Kojiki/Nihon Shoki slaying itself.

Embodiment of flood and iron (river/water dragon register)

As a water-dragon it is linked to destructive flooding (the Hii River repeatedly burst its banks) and to the discovery of iron/snakes-with-swords in its tail — reflecting the folk belief of dragons boring potholes with the sharp swords growing from their tails as they ascend to heaven.

Limit: Analytical/interpretive, not a discrete narrative power.

Accounts I
The Slaying at the River Hi (Kojiki version)Kojiki, 712

Kamiyo (Age of the Gods); recorded 8th century

Banished from Takamagahara, Susanoo descends to Izumo and finds a chopstick floating down the River Hi. Upstream he meets the earthly deities Ashinazuchi and Tenazuchi, weeping over their last surviving daughter, Kushinada-hime — the serpent from Koshi has devoured their other seven daughters one each year and is due to take her. It is 'eight forked and eight-tailed, with eyes like red ground-cherries, covered in moss, cypress and cedar, spanning eight valleys and eight peaks; its belly is oozing blood.' Susanoo agrees to kill it in exchange for Kushinadahime's hand, transforms her into a comb and hides her in his hair, and has the couple brew eightfold-refined sake in a fence with eight gates and eight vats. The serpent arrives, dips a head in each vat, drinks, and falls asleep. Susanoo unsheathes his ten-span sword, hacks it to pieces 'until the River Hi ran red with its blood,' but in the middle tail his blade snaps off. The Nihon Shoki's Six Versions and the Recovering of the SwordNihon Shoki, 720

Kamiyo; recorded 8th century

The Nihon Shoki offers six recountings of Susanoo's post-exile deeds; four mention the serpent. In the main version, Susanoo finds the weeping couple of Izumo; in Version Three he instead reaches the River E in Aki, where the couple's child is unborn and the serpent devours newborns, and the child (Inadahime, later his wife) is born only after the slaying. In every variant the serpent is intoxicated by sake, slain, and a divine sword is found in its tail — named Kusanagi-no-Tsurugi, or Ama-no-Murakumo-no-Tsurugi ('Sword of Gathering Clouds of Heaven') because clouds gathered over the place where it lay. Establishes the Kusanagi and the sword-variants; connects the myth to Owari/Atsuta (where the sword was venerated) and to Isonokami Shrine (where the serpent-killing sword Orochi-no-Aramasa was held).

The Swelled Eight Thunder GodsSendai Kuji Hongi

Composite text attributed to Prince Shōtoku; questionably 8th century

A distinctive elaboration: when Susanoo chops the eight-forked serpent into eight pieces, from the eight pieces spring eight thunder gods (yameno-ikazuchi), who ascend to heaven. The Kujiki is a composite that mingles Kojiki, Nihon Shoki and Kogo Shūi material with Mononobe-clan mythology. Explains the origin of a set of thunder deities from the serpent's dismemberment.

Accounts II
The Serpent's Reincarnation as Emperor Antoku and the Lost SwordHeike Monogatari (Kakuichi-bon), Book Eleven 'Tsurugi'

1371 (compilation)

Centuries after the slaying, at the Battle of Dan-no-Ura (1185), Taira no Tokiko throws herself into the sea carrying Kusanagi-no-Tsurugi, taking the child-emperor Antoku to the bottom with them. A 'learned doctor' in the text interprets this as the serpent's revenge: 'The great serpent slain of old by Susano-o... so profoundly desired the spirit sword he had lost that, in token of his eight heads and tails, he took the form of the eightieth human sovereign and... took it back and dove with it to the bottom of the sea.' The identification also appears in the Noh play Kusanagi, where the serpent assumes Antoku's identity to reclaim the sword and later impedes Yamato Takeru. Reinterprets a historical naval defeat as a mythic return of the serpent; links Yamata no Orochi to the fate of the imperial regalia.

The Origin of Shuten Dōji: the Serpent as Ibuki DaimyōjinŌeyama (Nara ehon), 14th century; Shuten Dōji legend corpus

14th century and later

A local Mount Ibuki tradition holds that Yamata no Orochi survived Susanoo and fled west to Ōmi, taking refuge on Mount Ibuki, where it was deified as Ibuki Daimyōjin (also responsible for the death of Yamato Takeru when the hero arrogantly strode over the serpent-shaped mountain god). In the Ōeyama tale, the mountain god — whose true identity is Yamata no Orochi — secretly impregnates Lord Sugawa's daughter through nightly visits. When exorcists are called in, the god makes Sugawa ill, and only by worshiping Yamata no Orochi as Ibuki Daimyōjin is he pacified. The child of that union is Shuten Dōji, the 'Demon King of the Sixth Heaven' (in the Ibuki-text group), who grows into the great oni of Mount Ōe and is later slain by Minamoto no Yorimitsu. Both father and son are noted for a matchless thirst for sake — a detail cited as linking evidence. Gives the serpent a monstrous legendary 'son' in Shuten Dōji and cements its local deification at Mount Ibuki, bridging the Yamata no Orochi myth and the later oni cycle.

Yamato Takeru's Battle with the Serpent God on Mount IbukiKojiki / Nihon Shoki (Yamato Takeru cycle)

Recorded 8th century

After his eastern campaigns, Yamato Takeru leaves his sword (the Kusanagi) with his wife Miyazu-hime at Owari and climbs Mount Ibuki in Ōmi to confront a reputed 'savage deity.' The mountain god, identifying himself as a great serpent, blocks the road. Yamato Takeru mistakes it for the deity's mere messenger and strides over it. Enraged, the god raises clouds and an icy rain that confounds the hero, who barely escapes but later falls ill and dies — the illness attributed to this affront. Serves as the death of the great imperial hero and links the Ibuki serpent-god to the Kusanagi narrative cycle.
