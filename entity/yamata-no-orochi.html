Who It Is

An eight-headed, eight-tailed serpent of immense size from Japanese mythology, slain by the storm god Susanoo. Its body was said to span eight valleys and eight hills; its eyes glowed red like winter cherries, and trees grew from its back. The legendary sword Kusanagi-no-Tsurugi was found in its tail.

Form

Gigantic serpent, eight heads and eight tails, red eyes like winter cherries, moss and trees growing on its body, belly constantly bloodied, body spans eight valleys and hills

Regeneration

Polycephaly / multi-headed and multi-tailed form Eight heads and eight tails that span eight valleys and eight peaks; each head can drink a full cask of sake independently. The eight heads also give it the ability to menace or consume eight victims in a cycle. Kojiki; all Nihon Shoki variants; Heike Monogatari Each head acts as a separate drinking 'mouth' — allowing Susanoo to neutralize all eight simultaneously with the sake trap. None major across versions, though the 'eight forked head and eight forked tail' phrasing varies (Kojiki 八俣, Shoki 八岐).

Annual maiden/child predation Devours one of the deity couple's daughters each year until only Kushinadahime remains; in the River E (Aki) variant it comes to devour each newborn as it is born. Kojiki; Nihon Shoki V1 and V3 None in the text — it is unstoppable by the earthly deities Ashinazuchi/Tenazuchi; only the heavenly kami Susanoo can defeat it. V1/Vojiki: devours grown daughters. V3: devours newborns. The two differ on whether the victim is a maiden or an infant.

Toxicity / poisoned breath (Jadokkeshin reading) In the Gion-cult exegesis, Yamata no Orochi as an incarnation of Jadokkeshin (蛇毒気神, 'Deity of the Poisoned Snake Breath'), consort of the epidemic god Gozu Tennō, carries a snake-poison register that connects it to pestilence. Ichijō Kaneyoshi, Nihon Shoki Sansō (15th century); Hoki Naiden; Gion ritual texts This is an interpretative/deified layer, not a narrative feat in the primary slaying myth. The identification as Jadokkeshin is a medieval Buddhist-Shinto syncretic development; the primary myth gives the serpent no explicit poison power beyond its size and hunger.

Vulnerability to alcohol (the defining weakness) The creature cannot resist sake: in the Kojiki/Shoki it drinks each of the eight casks of eightfold-refined sake and falls asleep, allowing Susanoo to slaughter it. This is the story's central mechanism and the reason the hero wins. All primary versions (Kojiki; Nihon Shoki V1, V3, V4; Heike; Kujiki) Complete when intoxicated — it does not rouse before being cut apart. V4 specifies 'poisonous sake' (a venom in the brew); other versions imply ordinary intoxicating sake. The Heike version has Susanoo make a 'likeness of her' whose reflection the serpent mistakes for the real maiden.

Incarnational / avataric persistence Rather than being wholly destroyed, the serpent 'survives' as a deity: it is deified as Ibuki Daimyōjin at Mount Ibuki, is identified with the Nāga Maiden (Longnü) of the Lotus Sutra and with Jadokkeshin, and is said to reincarnate as Emperor Antoku to reclaim the stolen sword. Local Mount Ibuki belief; Ōeyama; Ichijō Kaneyoshi; Heike Monogatari; Noh Kusanagi These are later religious/legendary layers, some contradictory, and mostly regional or exegetical rather than part of the Kojiki/Nihon Shoki slaying itself. The Kojiki/Shoki treat the creature as killed outright; the Ibuki and Antoku traditions insist it endures in other forms.

Embodiment of flood and iron (river/water dragon register) As a water-dragon it is linked to destructive flooding (the Hii River repeatedly burst its banks) and to the discovery of iron/snakes-with-swords in its tail — reflecting the folk belief of dragons boring potholes with the sharp swords growing from their tails as they ascend to heaven. Analysis layer (Miura, Tylor, Weiss, metallurgical readings); Izumo Fudoki for iron context Analytical/interpretive, not a discrete narrative power. Whether it is fundamentally a flood/nature force or a metallurgical allegory is contested among scholars.

Behavior & Habitat

Demanded annual sacrifice of maidens; intoxicated with sake to enable Susanoo's victory; represented chaos overcome by divine order

Accounts

Banished from Takamagahara, Susanoo descends to Izumo and finds a chopstick floating down the River Hi. Upstream he meets the earthly deities Ashinazuchi and Tenazuchi, weeping over their last surviving daughter, Kushinada-hime — the serpent from Koshi has devoured their other seven daughters one each year and is due to take her. It is 'eight forked and eight-tailed, with eyes like red ground-cherries, covered in moss, cypress and cedar, spanning eight valleys and eight peaks; its belly is oozing blood.' Susanoo agrees to kill it in exchange for Kushinadahime's hand, transforms her into a comb and hides her in his hair, and has the couple brew eightfold-refined sake in a fence with eight gates and eight vats. The serpent arrives, dips a head in each vat, drinks, and falls asleep. Susanoo unsheathes his ten-span sword, hacks it to pieces 'until the River Hi ran red with its blood,' but in the middle tail his blade snaps off. He slits the tail open and finds a keen broadsword — the Kusanagi (Grass-Cutter) — which he presents to Amaterasu, establishing it as one of the Three Imperial Regalia.

The Nihon Shoki offers six recountings of Susanoo's post-exile deeds; four mention the serpent. In the main version, Susanoo finds the weeping couple of Izumo; in Version Three he instead reaches the River E in Aki, where the couple's child is unborn and the serpent devours newborns, and the child (Inadahime, later his wife) is born only after the slaying. In every variant the serpent is intoxicated by sake, slain, and a divine sword is found in its tail — named Kusanagi-no-Tsurugi, or Ama-no-Murakumo-no-Tsurugi ('Sword of Gathering Clouds of Heaven') because clouds gathered over the place where it lay.

A distinctive elaboration: when Susanoo chops the eight-forked serpent into eight pieces, from the eight pieces spring eight thunder gods (yameno-ikazuchi), who ascend to heaven. The Kujiki is a composite that mingles Kojiki, Nihon Shoki and Kogo Shūi material with Mononobe-clan mythology.

Centuries after the slaying, at the Battle of Dan-no-Ura (1185), Taira no Tokiko throws herself into the sea carrying Kusanagi-no-Tsurugi, taking the child-emperor Antoku to the bottom with them. A 'learned doctor' in the text interprets this as the serpent's revenge: 'The great serpent slain of old by Susano-o... so profoundly desired the spirit sword he had lost that, in token of his eight heads and tails, he took the form of the eightieth human sovereign and... took it back and dove with it to the bottom of the sea.' The identification also appears in the Noh play Kusanagi, where the serpent assumes Antoku's identity to reclaim the sword and later impedes Yamato Takeru.

A local Mount Ibuki tradition holds that Yamata no Orochi survived Susanoo and fled west to Ōmi, taking refuge on Mount Ibuki, where it was deified as Ibuki Daimyōjin (also responsible for the death of Yamato Takeru when the hero arrogantly strode over the serpent-shaped mountain god). In the Ōeyama tale, the mountain god — whose true identity is Yamata no Orochi — secretly impregnates Lord Sugawa's daughter through nightly visits. When exorcists are called in, the god makes Sugawa ill, and only by worshiping Yamata no Orochi as Ibuki Daimyōjin is he pacified. The child of that union is Shuten Dōji, the 'Demon King of the Sixth Heaven' (in the Ibuki-text group), who grows into the great oni of Mount Ōe and is later slain by Minamoto no Yorimitsu. Both father and son are noted for a matchless thirst for sake — a detail cited as linking evidence.

After his eastern campaigns, Yamato Takeru leaves his sword (the Kusanagi) with his wife Miyazu-hime at Owari and climbs Mount Ibuki in Ōmi to confront a reputed 'savage deity.' The mountain god, identifying himself as a great serpent, blocks the road. Yamato Takeru mistakes it for the deity's mere messenger and strides over it. Enraged, the god raises clouds and an icy rain that confounds the hero, who barely escapes but later falls ill and dies — the illness attributed to this affront.

Content Notes

The core of the myth is that the serpent devours one of Ashinazuchi and Tenazuchi's daughters every year until only Kushinadahime remains. In the Nihon Shoki's River E (Aki) variant it devours each newborn infant instead. This is a myth of compelled annual sacrifice of young women (or infants) to a predatory river/water being.

Read as a contract with a river-god to ensure harvest (the wet-rice / flooding reading). The dark register is a 'tribute' model of propitiation common to water-dragon myths across cultures.

Kojiki; Nihon Shoki V1 and V3

Susanoo agrees to slay the serpent only on the condition that he is given Kushinadahime in marriage. The maiden is exchanged — consent is neither solicited from nor given by her. This is the classic patriarchal rescue-as-claim archetype.

Reflects patrilineal exchange marriage in early Japanese society; the woman is the prize/reward for the hero's feat, not an agent.

Kojiki; Nihon Shoki; Kushinadahime article

The Ōeyama layering has the serpent-as-Ibuki-Daimyōjin sneak nightly into the bed of Lord Sugawa's daughter, impregnating her against the household's wishes, and cursing the father with illness when exorcists are summoned. The child is Shuten Dōji, the great oni who later murders and devours humans.

The Ibuki/Orochi parentage is specific to the Mt. Ibuki text group; it grafts the earlier dragon myth onto the medieval oni cycle and its violent, amoral demon-logic.

Ōeyama; Shuten Dōji corpus (Mt. Ibuki group)

The serpent-god of Mount Ibuki, affronted when Yamato Takeru strides over it thinking it merely an envoy, raises clouds and an icy rain that disorients the hero; Yamato Takeru later dies, his death attributed to this encounter. A reminder that the 'defeated' monster retains lethal divine power in its localized, deified form.

Underscores the two-sidedness of the myth: the serpent is worshipped and fearsome, not merely a slain beast.

Kojiki / Nihon Shoki (Yamato Takeru cycle)

The Heike Monogatari records the collective-skeptic 'learned doctor' view that Emperor Antoku, who drowned at eight years old at Dan-no-Ura carrying the Kusanagi, was the serpent reborn reclaiming its stolen sword. The death of the child sovereign is recast as mythic vengeance.

Bialock and others analyze the tale of the Heike for its dragon-cult and outcast/emperorship registers; the identification is culturally freighted with the collapse of the Taira and the loss of imperium.

Heike Monogatari; Noh Kusanagi

In Gion-cult exegesis the serpent as Jadokkeshin ('Deity of the Poisoned Snake Breath') is a goddess of pestilence and a placenta deity who 'came into being from the gathering of placentas and menstrual blood that were thrown away in the Pond of Reverse Blood.' This registers the serpent's feminine, bloody, disease-bearing underside and its association with the epidemic god Gozu Tennō.

Jadokkeshin is a syncretic, gender- and body-marked deity; her plague-consort role positions the serpent-nature as something needing propitiation in early-modern epidemic religion.

Ichijō Kaneyoshi, Nihon Shoki Sansō; Hoki Naiden; Hokishō

Scholarly Debates
Was the serpent destroyed, or did it survive?
Annihilated by Susanoo, its body chopped to pieces and the River Hi running red (Kojiki; Nihon Shoki). ⇌ Survived and fled to Mount Ibuki, where it was deified as Ibuki Daimyōjin and fathered Shuten Dōji (local Mount Ibuki belief; Ōeyama).
Evidence for A: Primary Kojiki/Nihon Shoki slaying narrative.
Evidence for B: Mount Ibuki popular tradition; Ōeyama text; Heike's Antoku-reincarnation trope.
Is Yamata no Orochi fundamentally a river/flood being or an iron/metallurgy allegory?
A personification of the flood-prone River Hi / a nature force of chaos that culture (Susanoo) tames (Miura Sukeyuki; Edward Burnett Tylor's 'eight-mouthed river'; David Weiss's river-god reading). ⇌ A euhemeristic gloss on a mountain spirit and the arrival of iron-making from Korea, whose 'tail' holds the iron-sand swords (metallurgical readings; Izumo Fudoki iron context).
Evidence for A: River Hi prominence; dragon-as-water-provider/rain pattern; ATU type 300 dragon-slayer structure (Weiss).
Evidence for B: Nita District iron production exactly at the myth's setting; Susanoo's metalworker-son names; kusanagi etymology to Kore
Was the Kusanagi sword recovered from the serpent part of the original myth?
The sword's discovery in the tail is integral (all primary versions include it). ⇌ It may be a late scribal addition by the Yamato court to explain the origin of an imperial regalia and legitimize control over Izumo (David Weiss).
Evidence for A: Universal presence of the sword in every slaying variant.
Evidence for B: The Izumo Fudoki omits the whole slaying; the sword's centrality to the imperial chronicles points to court interest.
What is the correct identity of the serpent's avatars (Nāga Maiden vs Kushinadahime)?
Yamata no Orochi is an avatar of the Nāga Maiden (Longnü) of the Lotus Sutra (Ichijō Kaneyoshi, Nihon Shoki Sansō). ⇌ It is instead Susanoo's wife Kushinadahime who is identified with the Dragon King's daughter (Shaku Nihongi).
Evidence for A: Nihon Shoki Sansō Buddhist reinterpretation.
Evidence for B: Shaku Nihongi identification.
Which event does the 'eight' signify — eight maidens devoured or eight thunder gods born?
The serpent devours seven daughters and is due to take the eighth (Kojiki/Shoki tally of eight). ⇌ Chopping the serpent into eight pieces releases eight thunder gods (Sendai Kuji Hongi).
Evidence for A: Kojiki; Nihon Shoki.
Evidence for B: Sendai Kuji Hongi (a composite, questionably 8th-century text).

A way to hold it in your mind

There is a moment, at the headwaters of the River Hi, when an exiled storm god looks up from a chopstick bobbing past and finds an old couple weeping over their last daughter — because something with 'eyes like red ground-cherries, covered in moss, cypress and cedar, spanning eight valleys and eight peaks, its belly oozing blood' has eaten the other seven, one every year. Japanese myth runs on that single image of the eight-headed, eight-tailed serpent Yamata no Orochi: a mountain that hunts, a river that drowns, and yet a vessel out of which, hacked apart and drunk on sake, the sacred Grass-Cutting Sword is born to crown an empire. In the same legends that raised it as a thing of dread, it survived its own death — as a mountain god at Mount Ibuki, as the drowned child-emperor Antoku returning to reclaim his blade, and eventually as the father of Shuten Dōji, the greatest oni of them all. The beast that is meant to be slain refuses, across eighteen centuries, to stay dead.
