Who It Is

Sir Gawain is among the oldest and most complex of Arthur's knights — the king's nephew (son of Morgause and King Lot of Orkney), a paragon of courtesy, and the hero of what many consider the finest Arthurian poem in English: Sir Gawain and the Green Knight. In the earliest Welsh and Latin sources, Gawain (Gwalchmei ap Gwyar, 'Hawk of May') is Arthur's foremost warrior, his strength waxing with the morning sun and waning by evening — a solar hero with deep Celtic roots. In the French romances, he is transformed into a model of courtly behavior, though one whose very courtesy sometimes leads him into moral ambiguities.

Gawain's most famous adventure is the beheading game with the Green Knight. During a New Year's feast at Camelot, a gigantic green-skinned knight rides into the hall and issues a challenge: any knight may strike him with his own axe, on condition that the challenger receive a return blow one year later. Gawain accepts, beheads the Green Knight — who calmly picks up his severed head and rides away — and must journey north the following Christmas to receive the return stroke. His journey leads him to the castle of Sir Bertilak, where he faces a three-day test of honesty and chastity: his host's wife attempts to seduce him each day while her husband is hunting, and Gawain must navigate the impossible conflict between courtesy to the lady and loyalty to his host.

Gawain passes the sexual test — accepting only kisses from the lady, which he faithfully passes on to his host — but fails in a smaller way: he accepts a green silk girdle that the lady claims will protect him from harm, and he hides it from Bertilak. When the Green Knight delivers his blow, he gives Gawain only a nick on the neck, revealing himself as Bertilak transformed by Morgan le Fay's magic. The nick is punishment for the hidden girdle. Gawain returns to Camelot wearing the girdle as a baldric, a permanent badge of his imperfection. The Round Table adopts the green baldric in solidarity, transforming a token of shame into a symbol of shared human fallibility.

In the French Vulgate Cycle and Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur, Gawain's character is darkened. His refusal to forgive Lancelot for the accidental killing of his brothers Gareth and Gaheris drives the final tragic conflict. Gawain's vengeful obsession destroys the Round Table — he insists Arthur pursue war against Lancelot, leaving the kingdom vulnerable to Mordred's usurpation. Mortally wounded by Lancelot, Gawain writes a letter of reconciliation on his deathbed, recognizing too late that his pride has destroyed everything. This tragic arc — from the courtesy-hero of the Green Knight to the vengeance-consumed warrior of the end — gives Gawain the deepest psychological development of any Arthurian knight.
