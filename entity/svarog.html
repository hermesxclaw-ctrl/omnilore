Svarog is the smith-god of the Slavs — the celestial blacksmith who forged the sun and hung it in the sky, father of Dazhbog (the sun given) and of Svarozhich (the fire on the hearth, literally Svarog's little son). Who They Are

Svarog is the smith-god of the Slavs — the celestial blacksmith who forged the sun and hung it in the sky, father of Dazhbog (the sun given) and of Svarozhich (the fire on the hearth, literally Svarog's little son). His single direct attestation is a twelfth-century Slavic gloss in the Hypatian Codex that equates him with the Greek Hephaestus and adds a detail of enormous weight: in Svarog's reign, the gloss says, the smith-god gave humanity two gifts — the forging of metal, and the law of monogamous marriage, enforced (the text is specific) by burning transgressors in the furnace. Craft and covenant from the same anvil: the smith as the god who joins things permanently, whether iron to iron or person to person. Around this single text orbits a great reconstructed mythology: the heavenly fire whose sparks are hearth-fires, the golden smith who plows the first furrow with a plow of forged gold, the divine craftsman whose work, once completed, let him withdraw — for Svarog is often read as a deus otiosus, the maker who finished the world's forging and stepped back from its management, leaving the sky to his sons. Every Slavic hearth-fire, fed respectfully and never spat upon, was his embassy; the smithy, with its dangerous transforming fire, was his parish church.

Origins

The Hypatian Codex gloss (c. 1114) translating a Byzantine chronicle is the anchor attestation; his sons Dazhbog and Svarozhich are better attested, and the family structure — smith father, sun and fire sons — is the core of reconstructed Slavic celestial theology.

Attested in Hypatian Codex gloss; comparative reconstructionEra Pre-Christian Slavic Europe

Form

A mighty smith at a celestial forge, sometimes white-bearded and withdrawn from the world he finished; folk embroidery and modern reconstruction give him hammer, tongs, and the sun cooling on his anvil.

Powers

The forging of the sun and of civilization's tools; institution of marriage law; paternity of fire itself; the transformative mastery of the furnace, where ore becomes metal and oath becomes bond.

Weaknesses & Wards

Withdrawal — the finished-work god governs nothing daily, and his cult dissolved into his sons' fires so completely that one gloss is all history kept of him.

Cultural Significance

Svarog anchors the reconstructed Slavic pantheon's celestial tier, and his name lives in Slavic words and place-names; the smith-as-lawgiver motif made him patron of both metallurgy and marriage in folk revival.
