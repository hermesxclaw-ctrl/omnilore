Who It Is

In the Philippines, no other creature inspires such universal dread as the aswang. A shape-shifting monster that can be your neighbor, your spouse, or the quiet peddler at the market, the aswang embodies the ultimate betrayal of trust. Its many forms—vampire, viscera-sucker, weredog, witch, ghoul—reflect a deep cultural fear of the unknown and the fragility of the social order. To understand the aswang is to understand the shadows of Filipino folklore.

Aswang are inherently evil, with no motive beyond harming others. They prey on the vulnerable, especially pregnant women and children. They are nocturnal, fearing light and noise, and their powers diminish in daylight. They often infiltrate communities through marriage or as travelers, maintaining a facade of normalcy.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Derived from Greater Central Philippine *aswang meaning 'vampire,' 'evil spirit,' or 'witch.' Powers
Shape-shifting

Ability to transform into animals such as dogs, cats, pigs, or birds, and sometimes into human forms.

Limit: Powers are reduced or nullified during daylight.

Proboscis tongue

A long, tubular tongue used to suck blood or internal organs, or to remove fetuses from pregnant women.

Limit: The tongue is vulnerable to being cut or grabbed.

Self-segmentation

The ability to detach the upper half of the body from the lower half, growing wings to fly in search of prey.

Limit: The lower half must be hidden and protected; if salt or ash is placed on it, the creature cannot rejoin and dies at dawn.

Cursing

The ability to curse victims, causing objects like rice, bones, or insects to emerge from their orifices.

Limit: Requires a grudge or provocation.

Flight

The ability to fly, often using wings or by transforming into a bird.

Limit: Not all aswang can fly.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Aswang appear as ordinary humans during the day, often attractive and well-integrated into society. By night, they transform into various monstrous forms: a vampire with a proboscis-like tongue, a viscera sucker with wings and a segmented body, a weredog, a witch with inverted eyes, or a ghoul with sharp nails and teeth and a rank smell. Some accounts describe the tongue as black and flexible as silk.

Symbols — In folklore, the aswang is associated with the 'tiktik' bird, whose call announces its presence. In art, it is often depicted as a monstrous hybrid with bat-like wings, fangs, and a long tongue. In modern media, it is a staple of Filipino horror films.

Domains — Aswang dwell in forests, cemeteries, and the outskirts of towns, but they can also live within villages and cities, hiding in plain sight. They are particularly associated with the Visayan province of Capiz.

Accounts I
The Aswang in CatanduanesCustoms of the Tagalogs

16th century

Fr. Juan de Plasencia recounts that a creature called 'silagan' tore out the liver of anyone clothed in white, causing death. In Calavan, a Spanish notary had his intestines pulled out through his anus. Another creature, the manananggal, appeared at night without head or entrails. These accounts were used by Spanish colonists to describe the aswang as the most feared creature.

Accounts II
The Aswang as a NeighborModern folklore collections

20th century

A common narrative involves an aswang living in a village, often as a wife or peddler. At night, it transforms and attacks neighbors, especially pregnant women. The community may suspect the aswang but cannot prove it until it is caught. Suspected aswang are often driven out or killed.

The Aswang and the TiktikFerdinand Blumentritt

19th century

The tiktik bird announces the proximity of an aswang with its song. The aswang uses its long tongue to remove fetuses from pregnant women, causing birth pains. The tiktik's call serves as a warning to protect pregnant women.
