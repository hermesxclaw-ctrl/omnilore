Who It Is

The aswang is the most feared and versatile monster in Philippine folklore, a shape-shifting ghoul that combines traits of vampires, werewolves, and witches. By day, an aswang looks like an ordinary person — often quiet, aloof, or reclusive. By night, it transforms into a creature that preys on the living and the dead. Different regional subtypes exist: some aswangs have long proboscis-like tongues to suck fetuses from wombs; others are corpse-eaters who dig up fresh graves; still others turn into dogs, pigs, or birds to infiltrate human spaces. The aswang is repelled by garlic, holy water, and the buntot pagi (stingray tail). During Spanish colonization, the Boxer Codex (1595) documented aswang beliefs among the Tagalogs. The aswang is distinct from the manananggal (which only detaches its upper body) and the tikbalang (horse-headed trickster). The fear of aswangs has shaped rural Filipino architecture — houses often have steep roofs with no attics to deny the creature a perch from which to extend its tongue.

The Approach

In the Philippines, no other creature inspires such universal dread as the aswang. A shape-shifting monster that can be your neighbor, your spouse, or the quiet peddler at the market, the aswang embodies the ultimate betrayal of trust. Its many forms—vampire, viscera-sucker, weredog, witch, ghoul—reflect a deep cultural fear of the unknown and the fragility of the social order. To understand the aswang is to understand the shadows of Filipino folklore.

Content advisory. Depictions of violence, gore, body horror, pregnancy-related harm, and cultural stigmatization of marginalized individuals.

Etymology

Derived from Greater Central Philippine *aswang meaning 'vampire,' 'evil spirit,' or 'witch.' Cognates include Cebuano, Aklanon, Bikol, and Kapampangan aswang; Tagalog aswang, asuwang, or uswang; Maranao ansoang ('demon witch'); Mansaka asoang ('demon'); and Sangir ansuang ('giant'). Believed to be the origin of Eastern Indonesian suangi.

Cultural Ownership

Philippines (specifically Visayas, southern Luzon, parts of Mindanao); also related beliefs in Indonesia (Sulawesi, Maluku)

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars debate whether the aswang is a single entity or a category of creatures. The term has been applied to various regional monsters, leading to conflation. Also, some modern Western interpretations equate the aswang with vampires, but this is a simplification.

Description

Appearance

Aswang appear as ordinary humans during the day, often attractive and well-integrated into society. By night, they transform into various monstrous forms: a vampire with a proboscis-like tongue, a viscera sucker with wings and a segmented body, a weredog, a witch with inverted eyes, or a ghoul with sharp nails and teeth and a rank smell. Some accounts describe the tongue as black and flexible as silk.

Symbols & Iconography

In folklore, the aswang is associated with the 'tiktik' bird, whose call announces its presence. In art, it is often depicted as a monstrous hybrid with bat-like wings, fangs, and a long tongue. In modern media, it is a staple of Filipino horror films.

Behaviour & Personality

Aswang are inherently evil, with no motive beyond harming others. They prey on the vulnerable, especially pregnant women and children. They are nocturnal, fearing light and noise, and their powers diminish in daylight. They often infiltrate communities through marriage or as travelers, maintaining a facade of normalcy.

Domains & Habitats

Aswang dwell in forests, cemeteries, and the outskirts of towns, but they can also live within villages and cities, hiding in plain sight. They are particularly associated with the Visayan province of Capiz.

Version Differences

Regional variations exist: in some areas, the aswang is a vampire; in others, a viscera sucker or a witch. The weredog is more common in areas without wolves. The witch aswang is characterized by vindictiveness and curses. The ghoul aswang is a carrion-eater. These variations are not mutually exclusive; a single aswang may exhibit multiple traits.

Only the viscera sucker variant has this power.

Stories & Myths

The Aswang in Catanduanes

Source Customs of the TagalogsCulture Filipino (Catanduanes)Period 16th century

Summary

Fr. Juan de Plasencia recounts that a creature called 'silagan' tore out the liver of anyone clothed in white, causing death. In Calavan, a Spanish notary had his intestines pulled out through his anus. Another creature, the manananggal, appeared at night without head or entrails.

Participants

Silagan

Manananggal

Consequences

These accounts were used by Spanish colonists to describe the aswang as the most feared creature.

Variants

The silagan is specific to Catanduanes, while the manananggal is widespread.

Source Modern folklore collectionsCulture FilipinoPeriod 20th century

Summary

Source Blumentritt, Ramos

Aswang are said to attack pregnant women and remove fetuses, causing miscarriage and birth defects.

Scholarly Context

This may symbolize fears of maternal mortality and infant death in pre-modern societies.

Stigmatization of individuals

Source Modern news reports, historical accounts

Description

Accusations of being an aswang have historically led to ostracism, violence, and even murder of innocent people, often targeting women, the elderly, or the disabled.

Scholarly Context

Aswang accusations have been documented as a form of social control and scapegoating.

Contradictions & Disputes

Single entity vs. category

Position A

Aswang is a single specific monster.

Position A — evidence

Early Spanish accounts refer to 'osuang' as a specific sorcerer.

Position B

Aswang is an umbrella term for multiple creatures.

Position B — evidence

Ramos and modern scholars categorize aswang into five types.

Scholarly Consensus

Most modern scholars agree that aswang is a category.

Origin of the term

Position A

Aswang is of Philippine origin.

Position A — evidence

Linguistic reconstruction points to Greater Central Philippine origin.

Position B

Aswang is derived from Indonesian 'suangi'.

Position B — evidence

Similar beliefs exist in Indonesia, suggesting a shared origin.

Scholarly Consensus

Linguistic evidence supports a Philippine origin, with later spread to Indonesia.

Position A

Scholarly Consensus

Aswang can appear attractive to lure victims.

Translation

The eighth, they called osuang, which is equivalent to 'sorcerer', they say that they have seen him fly, and that he murdered men and ate their flesh. This was among the Visayas Islands; among the Tagalos, these did not exist.

Ferdinand Blumentritt, 'Asuang' entry in a dictionary of Philippine mythology

Type textDate 1895Locator Entry for 'Asuang'Language Spanish

Translation

It is the name of an elf, sorcerer, or demon, known in almost the entire archipelago... It is a nocturnal demon that takes the forms that it wants, like those of a dog, cat, bird, or other animal. The Asuang preferably picks up abandoned children and lonely walkers. With his tongue, horribly dilated, black, and flexible as silk, he removes fetuses from women who are on tape.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

Detailed regional variations are not fully documented. Specific rituals and countermeasures may vary by locality. Modern adaptations in film and television are not exhaustively indexed. The connection to Indonesian suangi requires further linguistic and cultural research. The role of the aswang in pre-colonial Filipino religion is not well understood due to limited primary sources.

Research Scope

This dossier compiles historical accounts, ethnographic studies, and modern cultural references. It draws from primary sources such as 16th-century Spanish colonial records and 19th-century ethnographic writings, as well as modern scholarly analyses by Maximo Ramos and others. The scope covers the pre-colonial to contemporary period, focusing on the Philippines, with some notes on related Indonesian beliefs.
