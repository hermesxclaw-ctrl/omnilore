Who It Is

In the tranquil Seto Inland Sea, on an island named for millet, there resides a goddess with a peculiar passion: she is the divine patron of needles, and she does not take kindly to a broken one. In fact, legend says she once blinded a careless seamstress by making the very needle she had broken fly up and pierce her eye. This is not a story of a wrathful, distant deity, but of a fiercely protective and deeply domestic goddess—the guardian of women, the patron of childbirth, and the celestial bookkeeper of every thread and stitch. Today, her most famous act of devotion is not a grand festival of fire, but the Hari-Kuyō, a solemn and tender funeral for the very tools of a woman's craft, where thousands of used and broken needles are thanked for their service and laid to rest in a block of soft tofu. Awashima Daimyōjin is characterized as a compassionate and maternal deity, deeply concerned with the welfare of women. She is seen as a protector of women during childbirth, a healer of gynecological ailments, and a guardian of the domestic arts, particularly sewing and needlework. She is also believed to have a temper, as seen in the legend of her anger at a careless seamstress. This duality—nurturing yet capable of wrath—is a common trait among Shinto kami who are both protective and demand respect.

Powers
Protection of Women and Safe Childbirth

Awashima Daimyōjin is primarily invoked by pregnant women and those hoping to conceive. She is believed to protect women through the dangers of pregnancy and labor, ensuring a safe and easy delivery. She is also seen as a healer of women's ailments, particularly those related to the reproductive system.

Limit: None documented, but her power is generally considered to be focused on women's health and well-being.

Mastery over Needlework and Weaving

The goddess is said to have taught the people of Awashima Island the art of sewing and weaving. She is the patron deity of seamstresses, tailors, weavers, and all who work with needle and thread. She blesses their work and skills.

Limit: Her blessing is conditional on the respectful treatment of one's tools, as demonstrated in the legend of the broken needle.

Calming Storms and Protecting Seafarers

According to one legend, the goddess calmed a violent storm to protect the island and its people. This has led to her being venerated as a protector of sailors and fishermen, ensuring safe passage across the waters of the Seto Inland Sea.

Limit: This power is less emphasized than her role as a women's deity.

Divine Wrath (Cursing Needles)

The goddess can curse inanimate objects, specifically needles that have been broken or treated with disrespect. In the central legend, she causes a broken needle to fly up and pierce the eye of a careless seamstress. This power serves as a moral lesson about respecting one's craft and tools.

Limit: This power is directed at those who are careless or disrespectful, not at the general public.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No definitive, widely-accepted visual depiction of Awashima Daimyōjin exists. In artistic representations, she is typically shown as a serene, aristocratic female figure in Heian-period court attire (jūnihitoe), reflecting her status as a high-ranking kami. She is often depicted holding a needle or a spool of thread, symbolizing her connection to sewing and weaving.

Symbols — Her primary symbols are the needle (hari) and thread (ito). The needle is both her tool and the object of her wrath, and the Hari-Kuyō festival is dedicated to it. Other symbols include: the island itself, which is considered sacred; a small boat, in which she is said to have crossed the sea; and the foxtail millet (awa), which gives the island its name.

Domains — Her primary domain is Awashima Island in the Seto Inland Sea, where the main shrine is located. She is also enshrined at numerous other Awashima Shrines across Japan, particularly in coastal areas. Her spiritual domains include: safe childbirth, women's health, needlework and weaving, the protection of seamstresses and tailors, and the warding off of evil and storms.

Accounts I
The Arrival of the GoddessLocal shrine legend

Unknown (ancient)

The goddess Awashima Daimyōjin crossed the sea to the island that would bear her name. She taught the local inhabitants the arts of weaving and sewing, bringing them civilization and prosperity. She also calmed a great storm that threatened the island, solidifying her role as its divine protector. The island became a sacred site, and a shrine was established to honor her. She became the patron deity of the island and of needlework.

Accounts II
The Curse of the Broken NeedleLocal shrine legend

Unknown (ancient)

A seamstress, while working, broke her needle. Instead of properly disposing of it, she carelessly threw it away or stuck it in a place of disrespect. The goddess, enraged by this disrespect for the tools of one's craft, caused the broken needle to fly up and pierce the seamstress's eye, blinding her. This story serves as the primary myth behind the Hari-Kuyō festival, where broken and used needles are given a proper funeral. The story established a taboo against disrespecting needles. It led to the tradition of the Hari-Kuyō, where needles are thanked for their service and laid to rest, preventing the goddess's wrath.

Awashima Daimyōjin is a goddess only of sewing and needles. — While she is the patron of needlework, her primary and most important role is as a protector of women, particularly in childbirth and for their overall health and well-being. The needlework connection is the origin of her most famous ritual, but her domain is much broader.

The Hari-Kuyō festival is a somber, sad event. — While it is a memorial service, it is also a festival of gratitude. It is a way to thank the tools that have served their purpose, and it is often a social and community-building event for women. The mood is respectful but not morbid.
