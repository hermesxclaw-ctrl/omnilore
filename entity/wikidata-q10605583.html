Who It Is

Before the first god, before the first land, there was only Nûn — the dark, boundless water that held all potential. This is the story of the primordial abyss from which all Egyptian creation emerged.

Nûn is generally passive and inert, representing potential rather than active force. He is the source of all life but does not actively intervene in the world.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Nûn is often linked to the Egyptian word for 'primeval waters' or 'abyss', though the exact etymology is debated. Some scholars connect it to the root meaning 'inert' or 'chaotic'.

Pronunciation — Noon (approximate)

Aliases — Nun, Nu, Noun

Ownership — Ancient Egyptian mythology; also appears in later Hermetic and alchemical traditions, but primarily Egyptian.

Powers
Primordial Existence

Nûn is the personification of the primeval waters that existed before creation. He is the source from which all things arise.

Limit: He is passive and does not create directly; creation occurs through the agency of Atum or other gods.

Generative Power

Through his waters, Nûn provides the substance for creation. He is often called 'father of the gods'.

Limit: He does not have individual will; he is a cosmic principle.

Eternal Existence

Nûn is eternal and unchanging, representing the undifferentiated chaos that precedes order.

Limit: He is not a creator god in the active sense; he is the raw material.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Nûn is typically personified as a male figure with a beard, sometimes with a double crown, but often depicted as a watery abyss. In art, he may appear as a god standing in a boat, or as a frog or serpent in some creation myths.

Symbols — Water, the primordial ocean, the number '8' (in Hermopolis, the Ogdoad), the bennu bird (as a form of the sun rising from Nûn), and the celestial Nile.

Domains — The primeval ocean that surrounds the earth; the abyss beneath the earth; the sky (as the celestial waters).

Accounts I
The Creation of AtumHeliopolitan tradition (Pyramid Texts, Book of the Dead)

Old Kingdom onwards

In the beginning, there was only Nûn, the primeval watery abyss. From these waters, the sun god Atum arose on a primordial mound (the benben). Atum then created Shu (air) and Tefnut (moisture), who in turn gave birth to Geb (earth) and Nut (sky). The establishment of the divine order and the creation of the world.

Accounts II
Nûn and the Sun's JourneyBook of the Dead, Amduat

New Kingdom

Each night, the sun god Ra travels through the underworld, and at dawn he is reborn from Nûn. Nûn is often depicted as the watery abyss from which the solar barque emerges. The daily cycle of sunrise and sunset, renewal of life.

The Destruction of MankindBook of the Heavenly Cow

New Kingdom

When humanity rebelled against Ra, he sent the Eye of Ra (Sekhmet) to punish them. Later, Ra decided to spare the survivors and withdrew to the heavens, but Nûn advised him on how to protect his creation. The establishment of the celestial order and the separation of heaven and earth.

Nûn is the Egyptian equivalent of the Greek Chaos. — While both represent primordial disorder, Chaos is more abstract and not personified as a deity with a role in creation. Nûn is a personified god who actively participates in creation myths.

Nûn is the same as the Mesopotamian Apsu. — Apsu is a freshwater ocean and a god in Babylonian myth, but Nûn is the primeval watery abyss in Egyptian thought. They serve similar functions but are distinct cultural concepts.

Nûn is a female deity. — Nûn is typically male, though his consort Naunet is female. In some texts, the waters are gender-neutral.
