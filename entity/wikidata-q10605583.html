The Approach

Before the first god, before the first land, there was only Nûn — the dark, boundless water that held all potential. This is the story of the primordial abyss from which all Egyptian creation emerged.

Content advisory. None.
Identity

Nûn

Pronunciation Noon (approximate)

Known As

NunNuNoun

Etymology

The name Nûn is often linked to the Egyptian word for 'primeval waters' or 'abyss', though the exact etymology is debated. Some scholars connect it to the root meaning 'inert' or 'chaotic'.

Cultural Ownership

Ancient Egyptian mythology; also appears in later Hermetic and alchemical traditions, but primarily Egyptian.

Disputed Identifications

Sometimes confused with the Greek Chaos or the Mesopotamian Apsu, but these are separate concepts. In Egyptian texts, Nûn is distinct from the god Hapi (Nile flood) and from the concept of the ocean.

Description

Appearance

Nûn is typically personified as a male figure with a beard, sometimes with a double crown, but often depicted as a watery abyss. In art, he may appear as a god standing in a boat, or as a frog or serpent in some creation myths.

Symbols & Iconography

Water, the primordial ocean, the number '8' (in Hermopolis, the Ogdoad), the bennu bird (as a form of the sun rising from Nûn), and the celestial Nile.

Behaviour & Personality

Nûn is generally passive and inert, representing potential rather than active force. He is the source of all life but does not actively intervene in the world.

Domains & Habitats

The primeval ocean that surrounds the earth; the abyss beneath the earth; the sky (as the celestial waters).

Version Differences

In Hermopolis, Nûn is one of the Ogdoad, paired with Naunet. In Heliopolis, he is the father of Atum. In Thebes, he is subordinate to Amun. In some texts, he is male; in others, the waters are neutral or female (Naunet).

Powers & Abilities

Primordial Existence

Source Version Heliopolitan creation myth

Description

Nûn is the personification of the primeval waters that existed before creation. He is the source from which all things arise.

Limitations

He is passive and does not create directly; creation occurs through the agency of Atum or other gods.

Contradictions

In some myths, Nûn is self-created; in others, he is created by Amun.

Generative Power

Source Version Coffin Texts

Description

Through his waters, Nûn provides the substance for creation. He is often called 'father of the gods'.

Limitations

He does not have individual will; he is a cosmic principle.

Contradictions

In some texts, he is the father of Atum; in others, Atum creates himself from Nûn.

Eternal Existence

Source Version Pyramid Texts

Description

Nûn is eternal and unchanging, representing the undifferentiated chaos that precedes order.

Limitations

He is not a creator god in the active sense; he is the raw material.

Contradictions

Some later theologies place him under the authority of a supreme god.

Stories & Myths

The Creation of Atum

Source Heliopolitan tradition (Pyramid Texts, Book of the Dead)Culture Ancient EgyptianPeriod Old Kingdom onwards

Summary

In the beginning, there was only Nûn, the primeval watery abyss. From these waters, the sun god Atum arose on a primordial mound (the benben). Atum then created Shu (air) and Tefnut (moisture), who in turn gave birth to Geb (earth) and Nut (sky).

Consequences

The establishment of the divine order and the creation of the world.

Variants

In Hermopolis, the Ogdoad (including Nûn and Naunet) exist before creation and generate the sun god through their interplay.

Nûn and the Sun's Journey

Source Book of the Dead, AmduatCulture Ancient EgyptianPeriod New Kingdom

Summary

Each night, the sun god Ra travels through the underworld, and at dawn he is reborn from Nûn. Nûn is often depicted as the watery abyss from which the solar barque emerges.

Variants

In some texts, Nûn is the father of Ra; in others, Ra emerges from Nûn spontaneously.

Primordial Chaos

Source Pyramid Texts, Coffin Texts

Description

Nûn represents the chaotic, undifferentiated state that precedes order. This chaos is sometimes associated with negativity, but in Egyptian thought it is not evil; it is simply the raw material of creation.

Scholarly Context

Scholars note that Nûn is not a malevolent force but a necessary precondition for existence.

Destruction of Mankind

Source Book of the Heavenly Cow

Description

In the Book of the Heavenly Cow, Nûn advises Ra on how to protect creation after the near-destruction of humanity. This story involves violence and divine punishment.

Scholarly Context

This myth is often interpreted as a theodicy, explaining why suffering exists despite the gods' benevolence.

Contradictions & Disputes

Nûn as creator vs. created

Position A

Nûn is the ultimate source, self-existent and uncreated.

Position A — evidence

Pyramid Texts and Coffin Texts refer to Nûn as the father of the gods, existing before creation.

Position B

Nûn is created by a higher god (e.g., Amun in Theban theology).

Position B — evidence

Leiden Papyrus I 350 states that Amun created Nûn.

Scholarly Consensus

Scholars see this as a theological development where local gods were elevated, not a direct contradiction.

Nature of Nûn

Position A

Nûn is a personified deity with consciousness.

Position A — evidence

Texts describe Nûn as 'father of the gods' and depict him in human form.

Position B

Nûn is an abstract concept of water/chaos, not a personal god.

Position B — evidence

Other texts treat Nûn as an element, not an agent.

Scholarly Consensus

The Egyptians likely viewed Nûn as both a concept and a deity, depending on context.
