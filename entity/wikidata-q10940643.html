Who It Is

Before Kusanagi became the sword of emperors, another heavenly blade had to do the bloody work. When Susanoo, the exiled storm god, stood on the banks of the Hi River and faced a serpent with eight heads, eight tails, and a taste for daughters, he did not reach for a jewel-encrusted regalia. He drew Ame-no-Habakiri — the Heavenly Feather-Cutter, a plain, ten-fist-long straight sword from the High Plain of Heaven — got the monster drunk on eight vats of sake, and hacked until the river ran red. The blade chipped on the last tail, and inside the wound lay something greater than itself.

As an object, the sword has no personality, but narrative behavior is consistent: it is a practical, unsacred weapon before the Orochi fight, not a deified relic. Susanoo wields it with cunning rather than brute force — he first intoxicates Orochi with eightfold sake, then systematically severs heads and tails. The sword succeeds where divine status alone failed, but is immediately superseded: upon cutting the fourth (or middle) tail, the blade chips or is blocked, revealing Kusanagi within. In that moment Habakiri is shown as powerful yet inferior to the sword it uncovers. Later tradition

Powers
Orochi-Slaying / Eightfold Severing

Ability to sever the eight heads and eight tails of Yamata no Orochi, a serpent whose body spans eight valleys and eight peaks. The text presents this as a feat of divine strength plus blade sharpness, not autonomous magic. Susanoo cuts sequentially after intoxicating the beast.

Limit: Requires Orochi to be incapacitated by sake; not described as able to kill an alert, unsedated Orochi in open combat. The sword chips or is obstructed on the tail containing Kusanagi, showing finite hardness.

Heavenly Forging / Divine Provenance

As an Ame-no- (heavenly) sword, it is understood to be of Takamagahara origin, superior to earthly blades. Not explicitly magical, but its heavenly status grants legitimacy and ritual purity.

Limit: No explicit supernatural effect described; superiority is narrative, not quantified. Later tradition does not attribute elemental powers.

Revelation of Kusanagi (Unintentional Discovery)

By cutting Orochi's tail, Habakiri reveals the superior sword Kusanagi no Tsurugi (Ame no Murakumo). In some readings, this is its most important 'power' — to uncover the true regalia.

Limit: One-time narrative function; not a repeatable ability.

Purification and Legitimation

Post-slaying, the act with Habakiri purifies Susanoo's earlier pollution (his rampage in Takamagahara) and legitimizes his lineage, leading to his marriage to Kushinadahime and descent of Okuninushi. The sword thus functions as a ritual tool of harai (purification).

Limit: Interpretive; not stated as an intrinsic sword power in the text.

The texts give no hilt ornament, color, hamon, or material beyond 'sword' (tsurugi). Accounts I
Susanoo Slays Yamata no Orochi with Ame-no-Habakiri (Kojiki Version)Kojiki, Book 1 (712 CE), translated Philippi pp. 89-92; Chamberlain pp. 52-56

Nara period codification of earlier oral tradition (mythic time: Age of the Gods)

Exiled from Takamagahara for his violent misdeeds against Amaterasu, Susanoo descends to Torikami at the headwaters of the Hi River in Izumo. There he meets Ashinazuchi and Tenazuchi weeping because Yamata no Orochi, an eight-headed, eight-tailed serpent with eyes like red cherries and a body spanning eight valleys, has devoured seven of their eight daughters and will come for Kushinadahime. Susanoo promises to save her in exchange for her hand. He transforms Kushinadahime into a comb and places her in his hair, orders eight vats of eightfold-brewed sake to be built behind an eight-gated fence, and waits. Orochi arrives, dips each head into a vat, drinks, and falls drunk asleep. Susanoo then draws Ame no Habakiri and systematically hacks the serpent to pieces, so that the Hi River runs with blood. The narrative emphasizes cunning and ritual preparation over brute force. Orochi's death ends the tribute-sacrifice, saves Kushinadahime, and allows Susanoo to marry her and build a palace at Suga. Directly leads to discovery of Kusanagi and to the lineage of Okuninushi, linking Izumo to Ya

The Eight-Vat Stratagem and the Drunken Serpent (Nihon Shoki Main Text)Nihon Shoki, Book 1, Age of the Gods, 8th Dan (720 CE), Aston Vol.1 pp. 52-54

Nara period (mythic time: Age of the Gods)

In the Nihon Shoki's main account, Susanoo meets the weeping earthly deity Ashinazuchi, who explains Orochi has eight heads and eight tails. Susanoo has Ashinazuchi brew eightfold sake, build a fence with eight gates, and place a sake vat at each gate. Orochi comes, puts a head in each vat, drinks, and becomes intoxicated. Susanoo then slays it with the sword called Orochi no Karasabi. The text does not call the sword Habakiri in the main line, reserving that name for a variant note. Same as Kojiki in outcome, but the naming shift shows the compilers preserved multiple oral traditions side-by-side without harmonizing them, preserving the Karasabi vs Habakiri distinction.

The Notched Blade and the Discovery of KusanagiKojiki Book 1; Nihon Shoki Book 1 (both 712/720 CE)

Nara period (mythic time)

While dismembering Orochi, Susanoo's sword strikes something hard in one tail. In Kojiki: 'When he cut the middle tail, the edge of his august sword was slightly notched. He thought it strange, split the tail open, and found a great sword inside.' In Nihon Shoki: 'When he came to the middle tail, his sword was slightly broken.' He extracts the sword, which he names Ame no Murakumo (Heavenly Gathering Clouds), later called Kusanagi no Tsurugi (Grass-Cutting Sword) after its later use by Yamato Takeru. Susanoo, recognizing its superiority, presents it to Amaterasu as a reconciliation gift, and it becomes one of the Three Imperial Regalia (Sanshu no Jingi). Ame-no-Habakiri is narratively demoted: it is the tool that finds the true treasure but is not itself treasured. Kusanagi's enshrinement at Atsuta and its role in Imperial legitimation overshadows Habakiri, which disappears from regalia lists. This hierarchy is central to Yamato ideology: Susanoo's violent line contributes the regalia to Amaterasu's line.

Accounts II
Izanagi Slays Kagutsuchi with Ame no Ohabari / Itsu no Ohabari — The Parallel Heavenly SwordKojiki Book 1, Birth of Kagutsuchi; Nihon Shoki Book 1, 5th Dan

Nara period (mythic time: primordial creation)

Earlier in the theogony, Izanami dies giving birth to the fire god Kagutsuchi (Homusubi). Grief-stricken, Izanagi draws his sword Ame no Ohabari (also called Itsu no Ohabari / Itsu no Ohakari) and beheads Kagutsuchi. From the blood and severed parts spring other deities: from the sword's blood come Iwasaku, Nesaku, and Iwatsutsu no Mikoto; from Kagutsuchi's body come eight mountain and fire deities. This sword is a Totsuka-no-Tsurugi like Habakiri but wielded a generation earlier. The parallel is deliberate: father and son both use a heavenly ten-span sword to cut a monstrous fire/serpent being, and both acts generate new gods from blood. Establishes the archetype of the totsuka sword as a creator-destroyer; explains origin of sword deities (e.g., Takemikazuchi later). Scholars note the similarity of names (Ohabari vs Habakiri) suggests they were originally the same epithet, later split into two swords to avoid duplication in genealogy. Motoori Norinaga argued they are distinct swords of same type.

Susanoo's Exile and Investiture — How the Sword Came to HandKojiki Book 1, Susanoo's Rampage in Takamagahara; Nihon Shoki Book 1, 6th-7th Dan

Nara period (mythic time)

Before descending, Susanoo wreaks havoc in Takamagahara: he destroys Amaterasu's rice fields, fills ditches, defecates in her palace, and flays a heavenly piebald horse and throws it into her weaving hall, causing a weaver to die. The heavenly kami fine him, cut his beard and nails, and expel him. In some readings, Ame-no-Habakiri is either his personal heavenly sword retained after expulsion, or a sword provided for his redemption quest. The texts do not describe its forging or gifting; it simply appears in his hand at Torikami, implying it is part of his divine equipment as a son of Izanagi. His possession of a heavenly sword despite expulsion signals residual legitimacy. Frames the Orochi slaying as atonement and reintegration: by using his heavenly sword to protect earthly people, Susanoo transforms from polluting trickster to culture hero, earning the right to found the earthly lineage that will yield Okuninushi. The sword is thus a symbol of retained heavenly mandate.

The Afterlife of the Sword — From Narrative Tool to Shrine Relic (Medieval to Modern Reception)Taiheiki (14th c.), Isonokami shrine records, and modern folklore; Engishiki 927 CE as antecedent

Heian to Edo (794-1868), with modern continuations

After the Nara chronicles, Ame-no-Habakiri vanishes as an active narrative object, but Totsuka-no-Tsurugi worship continues. Isonokami Jingū, the arsenal shrine of the Mononobe and later Yamato court, enshrines numerous ancient swords as shintai (god-bodies), and medieval texts retroactively identify one as Habakiri. War tales like Taiheiki invoke 'Habakiri' as a poetic epithet for any great dragon-slaying blade. In Izumo, local tradition at Susa Shrine and Yaegaki Shrine reenacts the Orochi slaying at festivals, with a wooden sword representing Habakiri. No excavation has identified a specific Habakiri blade; its afterlife is liturgical and literary rather than artifactual. Habakiri becomes an archetype rather than a relic: the name is used generically for 'dragon-slayer sword' in literature and later in games/manga, while Kusanagi retains status as a singular, enshrined regalia. This explains why modern pop culture freely reuses the name for powerful swords without shrine controversy.

Ame-no-Habakiri and Kusanagi no Tsurugi are the same sword. — They are distinct and sequential: Habakiri is the sword Susanoo uses to kill Orochi; Kusanagi (Ame no Murakumo) is the sword he finds inside Orochi's tail when Habakiri is notched. Habakiri is the tool, Kusanagi the treasure. Kojiki and Nihon Shoki both state this explicitly.

Ame-no-Habakiri is one of the Three Imperial Regalia (Sanshu no Jingi). — The regalia are Kusanagi (sword), Yata no Kagami (mirror), and Yasakani no Magatama (jewel). Habakiri is not enshrined as regalia and has no shrine like Atsuta for Kusanagi. Its role ends after the Orochi episode.

The sword was forged by a famous swordsmith or has a known maker. — No maker is given in any early source. As a heavenly sword (ame no), it is implied to be of divine origin in Takamagahara, not earthly forged. Later Shinto-Buddhist syncretism sometimes attributes it to Ishikoridome or other kami, but this is medieval speculation without textual basis.

Ame-no-Habakiri had magical elemental powers like controlling wind or feathers. — No early text attributes elemental magic; 'feather-cutter' is an etymological description of sharpness, not a wind power. Any elemental abilities are modern game mechanics (e.g., wind slash in Persona/SMT).

The sword still exists and can be seen at a specific shrine. — No extant blade has verifiable provenance as Habakiri. Isonokami Jingū and other shrines preserve ancient swords as shintai, but none is securely identified as Habakiri; shrine claims are traditional, not archaeological. The Imperial regalia sword (Kusanagi) itself is not publicly viewable, let alone Habakiri.
