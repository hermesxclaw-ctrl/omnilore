Who It Is

He was not a king, not a champion, not a god of thunder — Cían was the quiet hinge on which Irish mythology turns. The son of the divine physician, the lover who slipped invisible into Balor's impregnable tower, the father whose murder forced the sons of Tuireann to scour the world for impossible treasures. Without Cían's brief, shapeshifting life and brutal death, there would be no Lugh Lámfada, no slaying of Balor, no victory of the Tuatha Dé Danann.

Medieval Cían is cunning, amorous, and vulnerable rather than martial. In Cath Maige Tuired he participates in the dynastic alliance: he approaches Ethniu, daughter of Balor, in a politically motivated union that bridges Fomorian and Tuatha Dé Danann lines — implying diplomacy or opportunism. In Oidheadh Chloinne Tuireann he is resourceful but ultimately helpless: he uses shapeshifting to hide, begs for mercy, and is murdered despite offering compensation. Name & Tongue

Etymology — Old and Modern Irish cían = 'long, enduring, far, distant'; as a personal name interpreted as 'the enduring one' or 'the distant one'. Nickname Scal Balb = scal 'champion, hero' + balb 'dumb, speechless, stammering' — i.e., 'Dumb Champion', dumb in the sense of unable to speak, not stupid. Folkloric

Pronunciation — Modern Irish [ciənˠ] (KEE-ən), Munster [ciːnˠ]; Old Irish Cían [kʲiːan]; Anglicized Keean, Kian. Scal Balb [skalˠ balˠəbˠ].

Aliases — Cian, Cían, Scal Balb, Scal Balbh, Kian mac Cáinte, Kian mac Kontje

Ownership — Irish (Gaelic) mythology; member of the Tuatha Dé Danann. Attested in medieval Irish literature (Middle Irish period onward) and living oral folklore in Donegal and western Ireland

Powers
Shapeshifting (druidic transformation)

Cían transforms himself into a pig (muc) or oircce (lapdog/young pig) to evade the Sons of Tuireann. The transformation is presented as druidic magic (fáith?). He retains human consciousness but loses ability to escape because the form is imperfect (no tail, or betrayed by hounds).

Limit: Transformation does not grant speed or defense; he is still tracked and killed with spears/stones. Requires prior knowledge of pursuit; not used offensively.

Genealogical potency - father of Lugh

His union with the Fomorian princess Ethniu produces Lugh Lámfada, the central hero who kills Balor and leads Tuatha Dé Danann at Mag Tuired. The dynastic potency is political/magical: mixing Danann and Fomorian blood creates a liminal, powerful offspring.

Limit: No personal power beyond reproduction; his death is required to activate Lugh's revenge quest. He does not train or raise Lugh (fostered to Tailtiu/Goibniu/Biróg).

Otherworld travel via female helper (Biróg)

In folklore, Cían cannot reach Tory Island himself; Biróg (Biroge of the Mountain), his leanan sídhe/banshee/familiar spirit, transports him by magic wind to Balor's tower. This is not his own power but access through alliance.

Limit: Dependent on Biróg's will; requires her magic. In Bentley version the helper is a red-bearded dwarf with similar function.

Ownership of Glas Gaibhnenn (in folklore)

Possession of the inexhaustible milch cow Glas Gaibhnenn (Glas Ghaibhleann), a wonder-cow that gives milk abundantly and is coveted by Balor. The cow is not a power but a marvellous property whose theft motivates the bridal quest.

Limit: He loses the cow through brother's trickery; must regain via revenge/seduction, not force.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No detailed physical description survives in medieval texts — Cían is defined by lineage and action, not iconography. Accounts I
The Dynastic Union: Cían and Ethniu and the Birth of LughCath Maige Tuired §§23-33

Middle Irish, 9th-12th century composition

After the alliance between the Tuatha Dé Danann and the Fomorians, Cían son of Dian Cecht is given (or takes) Ethniu, daughter of Balor of the Evil Eye, as partner. Their union is explicitly political — to seal peace. Ethniu bears a son, Lugh. The child is of mixed blood, able to belong to both peoples. The text emphasizes the child's destiny over the parents' romance; Cían's role is to sire the savior who will later kill his maternal grandfather Balor. Birth of Lugh Lámfada, who becomes the central hero of the second Battle of Mag Tuired and kills Balor, ending Fomorian domination. Establishes the theme of liminal hero born from hostile union.

The Fosterage to TailtiuLebor Gabála Érenn §321 (Macalister Vol. IV)

11th-12th century

According to LGE, after Ethniu bears Lugh, Cían does not raise him. He hands the boy to Tailtiu, queen of the Fir Bolg and wife of Eochaid mac Eirc, to be fostered. Tailtiu raises Lugh until he is ready to enter the Tuatha Dé Danann. This explains Lugh's epithet 'Lugh mac Ethnenn' yet his upbringing away from both parents, and connects Fir Bolg and Danann traditions. Explains Lugh's fosterage tradition and Tailtiu's association with Lughnasadh; Lugh later clears the plain for Tailtiu and institutes her funeral games.

The Murder by the Sons of Tuireann and the ÉraicOidheadh Chloinne Tuireann (full romance) and LGE synopsis §347

Synopsis 12th-15th century; full romance 16th-17th century manuscripts

Cían encounters Brian, Iuchar and Iucharba, the three sons of Tuireann (Tuirell), who bear a feud against the Danann. To escape, Cían uses druidry to turn into a pig/lapdog (oircce/muc) and mingles with a herd. Brian, suspecting magic, turns his brothers into hounds and strikes the pig, which reveals itself as Cían. Even after Cían begs to be killed as a man and offers compensation, they stone/spear him to death and attempt to hide the body, burying him deep and covering with stones. The earth twice rejects the burial until properly covered. Lugh discovers the murder (the earth reveals it, or via divination) and demands éraic (legal compensation) from the killers: a series of impossible quests for global treasures including the apples of the Hesperides, the pigskin of Tuis, a spear, horses, etc. The sons achieve them but are mortally wounded on the last quest; Lugh denies them healing with the pigskin, they die, and their father Tuireann dies of grief on their bodies. The Theft of Glas Gaibhnenn and the Tower Seduction (O'Donovan/Larminie version)O'Donovan footnote (1835) + Larminie 'The Gloss Gavlen' (1893) + Lady Gregory retelling (1904)

Oral tradition of unknown antiquity, recorded 19th century

At Druim na Teine (Fiery Ridge, Drumnatinny, Donegal) three brothers keep a forge: Gavida (Goibniu), Mac Samthainn, and Mac Cinnfhaelaidh (=Cían). Mac Cinnfhaelaidh owns the Glas Gaibhnenn, a wonder cow whose milk never fails. Balor on Tory Island, who has one eye in front and a poisonous eye in the back of his head, covets the cow. A druid has prophesied Balor will be killed by his grandson, so he imprisons his only daughter Ethnea in the Tor Mór tower guarded by twelve matrons/virgins. Balor, disguised as a little red-haired boy, tricks Mac Samthainn into handing over the cow while Cían is away. Cían learns he can only recover the cow when Balor is dead. His leanan sídhe Biróg of the Mountain carries him on the wind to the tower top, where he seduces Ethnea (in some tellings with help of a magic apple or disguise). Ethnea bears triplets; Balor orders them drowned in a whirlpool. The messenger drowns two but drops one (Lugh/Dul Dauna/Lui Lavada) into the harbour where Biróg rescues him and returns him to Cían, who fosters him to Gavida the smith. The Bentley Variant: Ballar the Danish Giant and the Thirteen ChildrenBentley manuscript translation printed in O'Donovan correspondence (c.1830s)

Recorded 1830s, oral tradition earlier

Ballar is presented as a Danish giant, most ancient king of Tory Island, with a third eye hidden by a curtain. He keeps his beautiful daughter in a castle guarded by twelve virgins after a prophecy that he will live forever unless killed by his daughter's son. His mainland neighbours are Gabshegonal (the smith) who owns the white heifer Glassdhablecana, and Kien Mac Caunthca with two sons. Ballar demands tribute including the heifer; Gabshegonal refuses. Ballar sends servants Mool and Mullock to steal her. Gabshegonal hires Kien's sons to guard the cow in exchange for swords. The younger son is negligent, the cow is kidnapped, and Gabshegonal holds the elder son hostage threatening beheading. The younger son meets a red-bearded dwarf who carries him invisibly on the wind to Ballar's castle, where he spends a night with the princess and also with each of the twelve virgins. Nine months later he returns to find 13 children — one by the princess, one by each virgin. He puts them in sheets into a currach; a storm drowns all but the princess's son. The Genealogical Tract: Sons of Dian CechtLGE tract on the descendants of Dian Cecht (Macalister Vol. IV §316)

11th-12th century

A prose tract lists Dian Cecht's sons: Cían, Cú, Cethen, and Miach (the latter famous for healing Nuada's hand). This establishes Cían within the divine medical lineage — grandson of Esarg? Actually Dian Cecht son of Esarg — and explains his brothers' identities. A conflicting tract in the romance calls Cían, Cu and Cethen 'three sons of Cáinte', creating a genealogical crux. The tract serves to anchor Cían in the Tuatha Dé Danann kinship network and to distinguish him from other Cíans. Fixes Cían's place in divine genealogy; Miach's murder by Dian Cecht (jealousy) provides parallel family violence. The Cáinte variant fuels scholarly debate about epithets vs separate father figure.

Cían was a great warrior or king of the Tuatha Dé Danann. — Medieval texts never portray Cían as warrior or king; he is notable only as son of Dian Cecht and father of Lugh. His death is as a fugitive, not in battle. His nickname Scal Balb suggests non-martial status.

Cían is the same as Cian the father of the Milesians or other Cíans in Irish genealogy. — Multiple figures named Cían exist in Irish tradition (e.g., Cían son of Olioll Olum in Milesian genealogies). The mythological Cían son of Dian Cecht is distinct and not to be collapsed with historical/legendary Cíans.

Scal Balb means 'dumb' as in stupid. — Balb means 'mute, stammering, speechless' — a physical epithet, not intellectual. It may refer to a speech impediment or ritual silence, not foolishness.

The Glas Gaibhnenn story is ancient medieval myth. — The cow-and-tower tale survives only in 19th-century oral folklore; no medieval manuscript contains it. It likely reworks the medieval Cían-Ethniu union using international folktale motifs (ATU 930) and was retrofitted to Cían by folk etymology (Mac Cinnfhaelaidh).

Lugh's name 'mac Ethlenn' proves his father was Ethlend (=Cían). — The standard form is mac Ethnenn/Ethniu (matronymic, son of Ethniu). The Ethlend variant is a single interpolated gloss, not the primary tradition. Lugh is consistently son of Cían and Ethniu.
