Form

Adakayanushitakikihime no mikoto is a minor Izumo kami recorded in the Izumo Fudoki as a daughter or subordinate of the great Izumo deity Ōkuninushi. She dwells in the locality of Takiki-mura, approximately twenty ri southwest of the district capital in ancient Shin-gun, and the place-name Takiki itself is attributed to her presence. The Izumo Fudoki's single preserved paragraph presents her within the standard Fudoki land-name-etymology formula: a divine resident gives her name to the territory, and later imperial edict regularizes the writing. Classical scholarship interprets the full divine name as marking her as the 'princess/lady of Takiki' who serves under a tutelary master (nushi) tied to the older place root 'Adakaya.'

In the broader Izumo mythic ecology she belongs to the kunitsukami generation — the earthly gods whose dominion over Ashihara no Nakatsukuni was peacefully transferred to the heavenly amatsukami during the celebrated kuni-yuzuri episode — and her marginal position mirrors that of hundreds of locally attested land kami whose surviving evidence consists of a single Fudoki line and a handful of parish shrines. The kunitsukami household, headed by Ōkuninushi, is structurally contrasted in the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki with the amatsukami, the heavenly gods who descend to claim the land; Adakayanushitakikihime is on the losing side of that mythic conflict in the same sense that the Izumo Fudoki itself preserves local lore precisely because the Yamato state suppressed Izumo-centered traditions. Her survival is not in grand epic but in the quiet persistence of parish shrines that continue to hold festivals in her honor.

Related shrines in Shimane and Tottori prefectures preserve her cult as a local land kami rather than a pan-Japanese figure, and she is sometimes linked by onomastic evidence to similar kami identities such as Kamayatatehime or Shitateruhime, though these identifications remain speculative. The multiplicity of names — eleven attested Japanese forms alone — reflects the historical practice of recording kami names phonetically in Chinese characters selected by local priests rather than imposed by imperial scribes, and it makes her a useful case study in the unreliability of single-source onomastic readings for minor Fudoki deities. No known iconographic tradition survives; she appears to have been represented only by the shrine's shintai, or sacred embodiment, typical of parish land kami. What distinguishes her from the hundreds of identical Fudoki land-names is the preservation of her full theonym across multiple orthographic variants — an unusually rich onomastic record that makes her valuable for studies of how classical scribes encoded regional kami into Chinese-character phonetics during the Nara-period compilation of provincial gazetteers. Her case also illustrates the methodological limits of onomastic identification in Shinto studies: the absence of narrative evidence means that any claim that she is identical with Kamayatatehime or Shitateruhime is a philological hypothesis, not a verified identity, and the archive records it accordingly.

Powers

Land-bound tutelage: localized authority over the Takiki region and adjacent arable land in Shin-gun, Izumo Province, consistent with Fudoki land-kami authority.

Fertility and blessing of local grain and rice plots, as is typical for Izumo female land kami associated with the Ōkuninushi household.

Protection of the shrine parish; locally reputed for village wellbeing rather than martial or cosmic power.

Accounts

The Izumo Fudoki entry for Shin-gun's Taki-mura states that the area was called Takiki because the goddess dwelt there. The original reading used the same characters as 'tatsuki' or 'tataki,' and in the third year of the Jinki era (726 CE) the kanji 多伎 were formally assigned. The passage reads: 'Approximately twenty ri southwest of the district capital lies Taki-mura. The御子 (divine child) of the great deity who made the realm, Adakayanushitakikihime, dwells here. Therefore this land came to be called Takiki.' The Jinki 3 regularization reflects an early Nara-period effort to standardize provincial toponyms by matching them to the classical divine-name corpus, a process that simultaneously 'corrected' and frozen local readings into the imperial script.

Unlike high-ranking Izumo kami such as Kotoshironushi or Takeminakata, who play active roles in the kuni-yuzuri narrative of resistance and submission to the amatsukami, Adakayanushitakikihime appears only as a resident deity whose name etiologically explains a place-name. This subordinate register situates her among the many hundreds of local land kami who served as parish protectors rather than independent mythic agents. The contrast with Kotoshironushi is instructive: Kotoshironushi negotiates with Takemikazuchi, yields his spear, and becomes ancestor of the imperial line through his daughter Himetataraisuzu-hime. Takeminakata fights and is defeated, then retires to Suwa. Adakayanushitakikihime does none of these things — she merely 'dwells' — which is precisely the textual evidence for her function as a local earth-spirit rather than a court-recognized deity.

Later medieval registers list Adakayanushitakikihime as the main deity of the Adakaya shrine in Yonago, Tottori, and of the Adakaya shrine in Izumo City, Shimane. Other shrines — including Taki-gei Jinja and Taki Jinja in Izumo-shi Taki-chō — also honor her as a parish deity. The spread of her cult across multiple shrines suggests that even after the Fudoki compilation, local communities maintained an active if localized devotional tradition, likely mediated by clan priests who preserved the land-name as a religious toponym. The 式内社研究会 survey (1983) lists her at multiple shrine registries, confirming a continuous devotional presence through the Edo period.
