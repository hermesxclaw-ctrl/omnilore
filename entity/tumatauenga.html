Who They Are

Tūmatauenga (commonly called Tū) is the Māori god of war, humanity, and conflict — the fierce, violent son of Ranginui and Papatūānuku who alone among the gods stood against the fury of Tāwhirimātea (god of storms) after the separation of the primal parents. His name means 'Tū of the Angry Face,' and he embodies the aggressive, competitive, destructive aspect of human nature — the force that drives warriors into battle and gives humanity dominion over the other gods' domains.

Physically, Tū is depicted in Māori carving (whakairo) with the fierce, protruding tongue (whetero) and glaring eyes characteristic of war-god imagery. His face is often marked with pūhoro (war tattoo), and he may be shown wielding the taiaha, mere, or patu — traditional Māori weapons. In meeting houses, carvings of Tū often appear as guardian figures near the entrance, their aggressive stance protecting those within. His color is red — the color of blood, war, and the earth after battle.

Tū's defining mythic moment occurs during the rebellion of Tāwhirimātea. After Tāne separated Rangi and Papa, Tāwhirimātea (god of winds and storms) was enraged and refused to accept the separation. He ascended to the sky to join his father Rangi and from there unleashed terrible storms against his brothers. Tāne's forests were shattered, Tangaroa's seas were churned into chaos, Rongo and Haumia hid in the earth. Only Tūmatauenga stood firm against Tāwhirimātea's fury — and even he was eventually overwhelmed. But Tū did not forget his brothers' cowardice. After Tāwhirimātea's storms subsided, Tū turned on his brothers one by one, punishing them for abandoning him: he attacked Tāne's children (the trees, which humans cut for building and firewood), Tangaroa's children (the fish, which humans catch and eat), and Rongo and Haumia's children (the cultivated and wild foods, which humans harvest and consume). Thus, humanity — Tū's descendants — gained dominion over the natural world through Tū's vengeance: every tree felled, fish caught, and crop harvested is an act of war by Tū's children against the children of the other gods.

This myth establishes Tū as the ancestor and patron of humanity. While Tāne created the first human form, Tū gave humanity its defining characteristic: the capacity for aggression that makes survival possible. Humans are the descendants of Tū — not peaceful creatures but warrior beings who fight for their place in the world. This does not make Tū evil in Māori ethics; rather, he represents the necessary, acknowledged role of conflict in maintaining life.

Tū's worship was central to Māori warfare. Before battle, warriors invoked Tū through haka (war dances) and karakia (incantations) that called upon his power. The haka — now internationally famous through the All Blacks rugby team — is fundamentally a ritual of Tū: the aggressive postures, the protruding tongue, the fierce shouting all invoke the war god and prepare the warrior's spirit for combat. Captured enemies were sometimes offered to Tū, and the consumption of the enemy's mana (spiritual power) through ritual cannibalism was understood as a sacred act honoring Tū. In broader Polynesian tradition, Tū's counterpart — known as Kū in Hawaiian religion — was one of the four major gods and was worshipped with human sacrifices at heiau (temples), particularly during warfare.
