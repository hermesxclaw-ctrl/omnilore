N'approchez pas! C'est Bugul Noz! (Do not approach! It is Bugul Noz!) I am the fairest of the fairies — fairest in what matters, ugliest in what you see. Turn back, and let me guard your path from the dark you cannot see.

Who It Is

Bugul Noz (literally 'Night Shepherd' or 'Night Child' in Breton) is one of the most unusual fairy figures in European folklore — a being of such hideous appearance that he actively warns people away from himself to spare them the terror of seeing him. He is described as the last surviving member of a fairy race, wandering the night fields and forests in profound loneliness. His appearance is so grotesque — twisted, hairy, with mismatched features and an otherworldly wrongness — that anyone who sees him may die of fright. But Bugul Noz is not malevolent; he is a guardian of the night who protects lost children, wandering animals, and travellers who are out after dark. When he senses a human approaching, he cries out a warning — a ghostly, echoing call that can be heard for miles — so that people know to avoid his path. His cry is terrifying but protective: 'I am Bugul Noz! Do not come closer! Turn back, for your own sake!' This self-sacrificing isolation makes Bugul Noz a tragic figure, one of the most poignant creations of Breton folklore. He is the spirit of the dark that guards rather than harms, the monster who protects by warning, the lonely being who chooses eternal isolation over causing fear. In some versions, he was cursed to his form for some ancient transgression and can only be released if a mortal embraces him willingly — but his appearance makes this impossible. Bugul Noz represents the Breton ambivalence about the night: it is dangerous, but it also holds guardians who work unseen. Anatole Le Braz collected Bugul Noz stories in his foundational 'La Légende de la Mort en Basse-Bretagne' (1893), preserving this unique fairy for posterity.
