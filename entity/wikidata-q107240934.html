Who It Is

In the shadowed highlands of Arcadia, where the oldest myths of Greece cling to the mountains like mist, there once lived a prince whose very name has nearly vanished from the record—Physius, one of the fifty sons of the impious King Lycaon. While his brothers are remembered for their daring impiety and their monstrous fates, Physius stands as a ghost in the genealogies, a figure whose story was either lost or never fully told. Who was this forgotten son, and what does his obscurity reveal about the nature of myth, memory, and the ancient Greek imagination?

No behavioral traits or personality are recorded. As one of the fifty sons of Lycaon, he is implicitly a member of a large royal family, but no individual characterization exists.

Accounts I
The Sons of LycaonPseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.8.1-2; also Pausanias 8.2.3-6

Mythological era; sources date from the 1st-2nd century CE, but the myth is older

Lycaon, king of Arcadia, had fifty sons (and in some accounts, a daughter, Callisto). The sons were known for their impiety and arrogance. According to the myth, Lycaon tested Zeus by serving him the flesh of a sacrificed child (often identified as Nyctimus, one of his sons). Zeus, enraged, destroyed Lycaon's household with lightning and transformed Lycaon into a wolf. The sons were also punished, either by being struck by lightning or by being transformed into wolves. In some versions, Nyctimus was restored to life and became king. The story serves as an aetiological myth for the worship of Zeus Lycaeus and the practice of human sacrifice in Arcadia. The impiety of Lycaon and his sons led to divine punishment, the establishment of Zeus Lycaeus worship, and the association of Arcadia with werewolf legends.
