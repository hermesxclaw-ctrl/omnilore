The Approach

In the shadowed highlands of Arcadia, where the oldest myths of Greece cling to the mountains like mist, there once lived a prince whose very name has nearly vanished from the record—Physius, one of the fifty sons of the impious King Lycaon. While his brothers are remembered for their daring impiety and their monstrous fates, Physius stands as a ghost in the genealogies, a figure whose story was either lost or never fully told. Who was this forgotten son, and what does his obscurity reveal about the nature of myth, memory, and the ancient Greek imagination?

Content advisory. This entry discusses mythological themes including impiety, divine punishment, and the transformation of humans into animals (lycanthropy). No explicit violence or sexual content is depicted in the surviving sources. Readers should note that the subject is a minor figure from Greek mythology and that historical evidence is fragmentary.
Identity

Physius

Pronunciation In English, typically pronounced /ˈfɪsiəs/ (FISS-ee-us). In reconstructed Ancient Greek, it would be /pʰý.si.os/ (PHU-see-os).

Etymology

The name Physius (Φύσιος) is likely derived from the Greek verb φύω (phýō), meaning 'to bring forth, produce, or grow.' It may be related to the noun φύσις (physis), meaning 'nature' or 'origin.' Thus, Physius could be interpreted as 'the one who brings forth' or 'the natural one,' though this is speculative. The name is not otherwise attested in Greek mythology or historical records.

Cultural Ownership

Ancient Greek, specifically Arcadian regional mythology. The myth of Lycaon and his sons is part of the mythological tradition of Arcadia, a mountainous region in the Peloponnese. The story was incorporated into the broader pan-Hellenic mythological corpus by later mythographers.

Disputed Identifications

Physius is not identified with any other mythological figure. However, the list of Lycaon's sons varies among ancient sources, and some names may be corruptions or alternative forms. Physius appears only in Pseudo-Apollodorus's Bibliotheca; other sources (e.g., Pausanias) do not mention him, leading to questions about whether he is a genuine tradition or a scribal error.

No physical description of Physius survives. As a mythological figure, he is not described in any ancient source. His appearance is unknown and unattested.

No symbols or iconographic representations are known. No ancient art or artifacts depict Physius specifically.

Behaviour & Personality

No behavioral traits or personality are recorded. As one of the fifty sons of Lycaon, he is implicitly a member of a large royal family, but no individual characterization exists.

Domains & Habitats

As an Arcadian prince, his domain would be the region of Arcadia in ancient Greece, centered around the city of Lycosura (founded by his father). No specific territory or cult is associated with him.

Version Differences

Ancient sources vary on the names of Lycaon's sons. Pseudo-Apollodorus lists Physius among them, but other sources, such as Pausanias (Description of Greece 8.3.1-5) and the Suda, provide different lists that do not include Physius. This suggests that the tradition was fluid and that some names may be later inventions or corruptions.

Stories & Myths

The Sons of Lycaon

Source Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.8.1-2; also Pausanias 8.2.3-6Culture Ancient Greek (Arcadia)Period Mythological era; sources date from the 1st-2nd century CE, but the myth is older

Summary

Lycaon, king of Arcadia, had fifty sons (and in some accounts, a daughter, Callisto). The sons were known for their impiety and arrogance. According to the myth, Lycaon tested Zeus by serving him the flesh of a sacrificed child (often identified as Nyctimus, one of his sons). Zeus, enraged, destroyed Lycaon's household with lightning and transformed Lycaon into a wolf. The sons were also punished, either by being struck by lightning or by being transformed into wolves. In some versions, Nyctimus was restored to life and became king. The story serves as an aetiological myth for the worship of Zeus Lycaeus and the practice of human sacrifice in Arcadia.

Participants

Lycaon

Consequences

The impiety of Lycaon and his sons led to divine punishment, the establishment of Zeus Lycaeus worship, and the association of Arcadia with werewolf legends.

Variants

Different sources list different sons and different fates. Some say all sons were killed by lightning; others say they were transformed into wolves. Pausanias does not mention the sons by name and instead focuses on Lycaon's own punishment. The list in Apollodorus includes Physius, but this may be a unique inclusion.

Human sacrifice and cannibalism in the Lycaon myth

Source Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.8.1-2; Plato, Republic 565d-e; Pausanias 8.2.3-6

Description

Lycaon is said to have served the flesh of a human child to Zeus in order to test his divinity. This act of extreme impiety and violence is central to the myth and leads to the punishment of Lycaon and his sons.

Scholarly Context

Scholars interpret the myth as an aetiological tale explaining the origins of the cult of Zeus Lycaeus and the practice of human sacrifice, which may have occurred in archaic times. The story also reflects Greek anxieties about the boundaries between human and animal, civilization and savagery.

Contradictions & Disputes

Existence of Physius as a son of Lycaon

Position A

Physius is a genuine mythological figure, listed among Lycaon's sons by Pseudo-Apollodorus.

Position A — evidence

Apollodorus's list is detailed and includes many names not found elsewhere, suggesting he had access to a source now lost.

Position B

Physius is a scribal error or an invented name that does not reflect an authentic tradition.

Position B — evidence

Other ancient sources that list Lycaon's sons (e.g., Pausanias, the Suda) do not include Physius, and the name is not otherwise attested.

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus. Most scholars treat Apollodorus's list as a compilation of various traditions, but the inclusion of Physius may be an error or a unique local tradition. The name is not independently verified.
