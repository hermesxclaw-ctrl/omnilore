Who It Is

In the deep, still forests of Japan, there are spirits that are said to be the voices of the trees themselves. Known as kodama, these beings are both the soul of a single tree and the echo that lingers in the mountain air. To hear one is to know that the forest is alive, watching, and listening.

Kodama are generally considered shy and peaceful spirits. They are said to protect their tree and the surrounding forest. If a kodama's tree is cut down, the spirit may curse the person responsible, bringing misfortune or illness. Some tales describe kodama as playful, mimicking sounds to confuse travelers. They are not inherently malevolent but can be vengeful if provoked.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The word 'kodama' (木霊) combines the characters for 'tree' (木) and 'spirit' or 'soul' (霊). It literally means 'tree spirit' or 'tree soul'. The term also refers to an echo, reflecting the belief that echoes in mountains and forests are the voices of these spirits.

Pronunciation — KO-dah-mah (IPA: /ko̞da̠ma̠/)

Aliases — 木霊, 木魂, 木魅, こだま

Ownership — Japanese

Powers
Echo mimicry

Kodama are said to mimic sounds, especially in mountains, creating echoes that can mislead travelers. This is tied to the word 'kodama' meaning echo.

Limit: The mimicry is not harmful; it merely confuses or startles.

Tree possession

A kodama is the spirit of a specific tree, and the tree is its physical manifestation. Harming the tree harms the kodama.

Limit: The kodama is bound to its tree and cannot leave it for long.

Cursing

If a person cuts down a kodama's tree or damages the forest, the kodama may curse them, causing illness, misfortune, or death.

Limit: The curse is only triggered by provocation.

Invisibility and shapeshifting

Kodama are often invisible or can appear as lights, animals, or human forms.

Limit: Their ability to shapeshift is not consistently described.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Historical descriptions of kodama are vague. They are often said to be the spirit of a tree, and may appear as a shimmering light, a voice, or an indistinct humanoid shape. In some regional tales, they are said to look like ordinary people. The modern depiction of kodama as small, white, round creatures with no limbs and large eyes is a creation of Studio Ghibli's 1997 film 'Princess Mononoke' and is not based on traditional folklore.

Symbols — Kodama are associated with ancient, large trees, particularly cedar and camphor. In Shinto, such trees are often marked with shimenawa (rice-straw ropes) and are considered sacred. The sound of an echo in a mountain valley is also symbolically linked to kodama, as the word itself means 'echo'.

Domains — Kodama reside in ancient trees, especially in deep mountain forests. Each kodama is bound to a specific tree; if the tree dies, the kodama may also perish or become a wandering, homeless spirit. They are most commonly associated with the mountainous regions of Japan, such as the Yoshino and Kumano areas.

Accounts I
The Echo in the ValleyVarious Japanese folktale collections

Unknown, likely Edo period or earlier

A woodcutter enters a deep valley and calls out, only to hear his voice repeated. He believes it is a kodama playing tricks. He responds angrily, and the echo becomes distorted, growing louder and more threatening. Frightened, he flees and never returns to that valley. The woodcutter learns to respect the forest and its spirits.

Accounts II
The Sacred TreeShinto shrine legends

Various

A village has a massive cedar tree believed to house a kodama. The tree is marked with shimenawa and offerings are made. When a lord orders the tree cut down for timber, his men fall ill one by one. The lord relents, and the tree is spared. The tree remains a sacred site, and the village prospers.

The Grateful Tree SpiritRegional folklore, collected by Yanagita Kunio

Early 20th century

A farmer saves a sapling from being uprooted by a storm. That night, a woman appears in his dreams and thanks him. She reveals she is the kodama of the tree. Years later, when the farmer's house catches fire, the tree bends to block the flames, saving his family. The farmer's family continues to protect the tree for generations.
