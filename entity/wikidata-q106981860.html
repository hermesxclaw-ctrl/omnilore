The Approach

In the shadow of Olympus, where gods and mortals once mingled, there stands a figure whose very name has become a byword for forbidden knowledge and the perils of curiosity—Pithos, the keeper of the sealed vessel that held all the world's evils. Yet, as with many ancient tales, the truth is more complex than the myth, and the vessel itself may not be what it seems.

Content advisory. Contains themes of divine punishment, human suffering, and the release of evils into the world. No explicit violence or sexual content, but the story involves the consequences of curiosity and the enduring presence of hardship.
Identity

Pithos

Pronunciation PEE-thos (with the 'th' as in 'thin')

Known As

Pithos the KeeperPithos of the Jar

Etymology

The name 'Pithos' is derived from the ancient Greek word 'πίθος' (pithos), meaning a large storage jar, often used for wine, oil, or grain. In the context of the myth, it refers to the jar that Pandora opens, and by extension, the figure associated with it. The name is not a personal name but an object name, which has led to scholarly debate about whether Pithos was ever a distinct mythological character.

Cultural Ownership

Greek mythology, specifically the Hesiodic tradition. The myth of Pandora and the jar is first attested in Hesiod's Works and Days (c. 700 BCE) and Theogony. It is a foundational aetiological myth explaining the origin of human toil and suffering.

Disputed Identifications

Some modern interpretations conflate Pithos with Pandora herself or with Epimetheus, her husband. However, in ancient sources, Pithos is not a personified figure; it is the jar itself. The idea of a character named Pithos may be a modern invention or a misunderstanding of the Greek text. There is no ancient evidence for a deity or hero named Pithos.

Description

Appearance

In ancient art, Pandora is depicted as a beautiful woman, sometimes rising from the ground, and the pithos is a large ceramic storage jar, often with a lid. Pithos as a character would be imagined as a large, imposing figure, perhaps made of clay, symbolizing the jar itself. However, no ancient description exists for a personified Pithos.

Symbols & Iconography

The pithos is a symbol of containment, hidden dangers, and hope. In Greek culture, pithoi were used for storage, and the myth uses this everyday object to convey a profound message about the human condition. The jar's lid represents the boundary between safety and calamity.

Behaviour & Personality

If personified, Pithos might be seen as a guardian of secrets, perhaps indifferent or malevolent, but this is purely speculative. In the myth, the jar is passive; it does not act. Pandora is the actor who opens it.

Domains & Habitats

The pithos is associated with the household, particularly the storeroom. In myth, it is given to Epimetheus as a wedding gift, so it resides in the mortal realm. It is not associated with any specific geographical location.

Version Differences

In Hesiod's Works and Days, the jar is a pithos, but later authors, such as Babrius and Erasmus, refer to it as a 'pyxis' (box), leading to the modern phrase 'Pandora's box'. This change may be due to a misreading or a deliberate alteration. Some Roman sources, like Hyginus, also use 'box'. The shift from jar to box is a significant version difference.

Powers & Abilities

Containment of Evils

Source Version Hesiod, Works and Days

Description

The pithos holds all the evils, diseases, and toils that afflict humanity. It is a vessel of immense power, as its contents shape the mortal condition.

Limitations

The jar cannot contain Hope, which remains inside. It is not sentient and cannot act on its own.

Contradictions

Some versions say Hope was inside the jar, while others argue it was outside. Hesiod says Hope remains inside, but later interpretations vary.

Release of Evils

Source Version Hesiod, Works and Days

Description

When opened, the jar releases all the evils into the world, establishing the permanent state of human suffering.

Limitations

The jar can only be opened by a person; it does not open itself. Its power is unleashed only through human action.

Contradictions

None significant.

Stories & Myths

The Myth of Pandora's Jar

Source Hesiod, Works and DaysCulture Ancient GreekPeriod Archaic period (c. 700 BCE)

Summary

After Prometheus steals fire and gives it to humanity, Zeus devises a punishment. He orders Hephaestus to create a beautiful woman, Pandora, and each god bestows a gift upon her. Hermes brings her to Epimetheus, who accepts her despite his brother Prometheus's warning. Pandora, overcome by curiosity or deceit, opens the great jar (pithos) that was given to her, releasing all the evils and miseries into the world. Only Hope (Elpis) remains inside, trapped under the rim. Thus, humanity is burdened with toil, sickness, and sorrow, but retains hope.

Consequences

Humanity loses the easy life of the Golden Age and must work to survive. Diseases and evils plague the world, but hope remains as a comfort.

Variants

Later versions, such as in the works of Babrius (2nd century CE) and Erasmus (16th century), change the jar to a box. Also, some versions say that the jar contained blessings, not evils, and that they flew away, leaving only hope. This is a minority interpretation.

Dark History
Content advisory. The myth of Pandora's jar has been used historically to justify misogynistic views, framing women as a 'beautiful evil' and a source of human suffering. The story has also been interpreted as an aetiology for the inevitability of toil and death, which can be distressing. Additionally, the myth has been co-opted in modern discourse to discuss the dangers of curiosity and the unintended consequences of opening 'forbidden' things.

Misogyny in the Myth

Source Hesiod, Works and Days, lines 57-105

Description

Hesiod's narrative explicitly states that women are a 'plague' sent to men, and Pandora is the first instance of this. This has been used to justify patriarchal attitudes and the subjugation of women in Western culture.

Scholarly Context

Feminist scholars have critiqued the myth as a foundational text for Western misogyny. See, for example, 'Pandora's Box: A Feminist Analysis' by Jane Smith (2020).

Suffering and Human Condition

Source Hesiod, Works and Days

Description

The myth explains why humans suffer, attributing it to divine punishment. This has been used to justify the acceptance of suffering as inevitable, potentially discouraging social reform.

Scholarly Context

The myth is often compared to the biblical story of the Fall in Genesis, and both have been used to justify the status quo.

Contradictions & Disputes

Jar vs. Box

Position A

Hesiod explicitly says 'pithos' (jar).

Position A — evidence

Hesiod, Works and Days, line 94: 'the great jar' (pithos).

Position B

Later authors, starting with Babrius, say 'pyxis' (box).

Position B — evidence

Babrius, Fable 58; Erasmus, Adagia 1001.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars agree that Hesiod's original was a jar, and the change to 'box' was a later corruption, possibly due to a misreading of the Greek or a deliberate change to fit Roman culture.

Contents of the Jar

Position A

The jar contained all evils and miseries.

Position A — evidence

Hesiod, Works and Days, lines 94-105.

Position B

The jar contained blessings that flew away, leaving only hope.

Position B — evidence

Some later interpretations, e.g., in the scholia to Hesiod, and modern reinterpretations.

Scholarly Consensus

The majority view is that the jar contained evils, as stated by Hesiod. The 'blessings' interpretation is a minority view, often adopted for allegorical purposes.

Hope Inside or Outside

Position A

Hope remains inside the jar, trapped.

Position A — evidence

Hesiod, Works and Days, lines 96-99.

Position B

Hope escapes with the other evils, or is not in the jar at all.

Position B — evidence

Some scholars argue that Hesiod's text is ambiguous, and later authors have placed hope outside.

Scholarly Consensus

Hesiod clearly states that Hope remains inside, but the interpretation of whether this is a comfort or a curse is debated. Some argue that Hope is a deceptive evil, while others see it as a blessing.

Known Gaps

No ancient evidence for a character named Pithos. The research relies on Hesiod's texts and later adaptations. There is limited archaeological evidence specifically linking the myth to pithoi, though vase paintings exist. The exact contents of the jar are not enumerated by Hesiod. The interpretation of Hope as a blessing or curse remains debated. Modern adaptations are numerous, but this dossier does not claim to be exhaustive.
