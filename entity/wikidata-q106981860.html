Who It Is

In the shadow of Olympus, where gods and mortals once mingled, there stands a figure whose very name has become a byword for forbidden knowledge and the perils of curiosity—Pithos, the keeper of the sealed vessel that held all the world's evils. Yet, as with many ancient tales, the truth is more complex than the myth, and the vessel itself may not be what it seems.

If personified, Pithos might be seen as a guardian of secrets, perhaps indifferent or malevolent, but this is purely speculative. In the myth, the jar is passive; it does not act. Pandora is the actor who opens it.

Powers
Containment of Evils

The pithos holds all the evils, diseases, and toils that afflict humanity. It is a vessel of immense power, as its contents shape the mortal condition.

Limit: The jar cannot contain Hope, which remains inside. It is not sentient and cannot act on its own.

Release of Evils

When opened, the jar releases all the evils into the world, establishing the permanent state of human suffering.

Limit: The jar can only be opened by a person; it does not open itself. Its power is unleashed only through human action.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In ancient art, Pandora is depicted as a beautiful woman, sometimes rising from the ground, and the pithos is a large ceramic storage jar, often with a lid. Pithos as a character would be imagined as a large, imposing figure, perhaps made of clay, symbolizing the jar itself. However, no ancient description exists for a personified Pithos.

Symbols — The pithos is a symbol of containment, hidden dangers, and hope. In Greek culture, pithoi were used for storage, and the myth uses this everyday object to convey a profound message about the human condition. The jar's lid represents the boundary between safety and calamity.

Domains — The pithos is associated with the household, particularly the storeroom. In myth, it is given to Epimetheus as a wedding gift, so it resides in the mortal realm. It is not associated with any specific geographical location.

Accounts I
The Myth of Pandora's JarHesiod, Works and Days

Archaic period (c. 700 BCE)

After Prometheus steals fire and gives it to humanity, Zeus devises a punishment. He orders Hephaestus to create a beautiful woman, Pandora, and each god bestows a gift upon her. Hermes brings her to Epimetheus, who accepts her despite his brother Prometheus's warning. Pandora, overcome by curiosity or deceit, opens the great jar (pithos) that was given to her, releasing all the evils and miseries into the world. Only Hope (Elpis) remains inside, trapped under the rim. Thus, humanity is burdened with toil, sickness, and sorrow, but retains hope. Humanity loses the easy life of the Golden Age and must work to survive. Diseases and evils plague the world, but hope remains as a comfort.

Pandora opened a box. — Hesiod's original text says she opened a jar (pithos). The 'box' is a later mistranslation.

Pandora was the first woman. — In Greek mythology, Pandora is indeed the first woman, created by the gods. This is attested in Hesiod.

The jar contained all the evils of the world. — According to Hesiod, the jar contained 'evils' (kaka), which include toil, sickness, and other miseries. The exact contents are not listed individually.

Pithos is a mythological character. — There is no ancient evidence for a character named Pithos. It is simply the jar. The name 'Pithos' as a character is a modern invention, possibly from a misunderstanding of the object.
