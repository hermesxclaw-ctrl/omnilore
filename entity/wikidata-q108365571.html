Who It Is

On the feathered arms of a crinoid — an animal that looks like a fern but hunts like a starfish — lives a shrimp you will almost never see unless the crinoid lets you. Laomenes, the crinoid shrimps, are masters of disappearing in plain sight: translucent bodies painted with spots, stripes and tiger bands that dissolve against their host's pinnules, where they spend their entire adult lives as elegant, obligate guests.

Obligate ectocommensals (sometimes termed symbionts) of stalkless crinoids (Crinoidea: Comatulida). Individuals live singly, in pairs, or small groups (2-6) on a single host, positioned on arms or near the central disc, oriented parallel to pinnules. They are highly host-specific to varying degrees: L. amboinensis recorded from >15 crinoid species, while L. pardus and L. tigris show narrower preferences. Name & Tongue

Etymology — Genus name Laomenes erected by Austin Hobart Clark in 1919. Clark did not provide an explicit etymology in the original description. The name is formed as a classical Greek personal name (Laomenes) following Clark's pattern of naming crinoid-associated genera after figures associated with crinoids.

Pronunciation — lay-OM-en-eez /leɪˈɒməniːz/ (anglicized zoological Latin); singular Laomenes, plural Laomenes

Aliases — Ambon crinoid shrimp (common name for L. amboinensis), Tiger crinoid shrimp (common name for L. tigris), Corniger De Man, 1888 (preoccupied, original genus for type species), Periclimenes (former placement for several species)

Ownership — Scientific zoological nomenclature. No Indigenous cultural ownership. Biological taxon distributed across the Indo-West Pacific; knowledge held by international carcinological comm

Powers
Host-mimetic camouflage

Translucent body with opaque spots, stripes or reticulations that precisely match host crinoid coloration and pattern. Chromatophores allow close matching to host arm color (red, brown, yellow, white, purple). When stationary on pinnules, shrimp is visually indistinguishable at >10 cm distance.

Limit: Effective only on host; when removed to contrasting substrate shrimp is conspicuous. Preserved specimens lose color. No active color change observed beyond slow adaptation.

Pinnule gripping and clinging

Biunguiculate dactyli on pereiopods 3-5 with strong accessory tooth and curved unguis that locks around crinoid pinnule. Allows shrimp to maintain position even when crinoid swims or in strong current.

Limit: Requires crinoid host; poor swimming ability independent of host. If forcibly detached, must re-find host or perish.

Detritus and mucus feeding

Uses first and second pereiopods and mouthparts to collect particulate organic matter, host mucus, and plankton from water currents generated by crinoid arms. Acts as cleaner, removing epibionts from host.

Limit: Not a parasite; does not feed on host tissue. Dependent on host feeding currents; cannot efficiently feed off-host.

Escape tail-flip

Rapid abdominal flexion (caridoid escape reaction) propels shrimp backward when threatened, using uropods and telson. Used to evade fish predators.

Limit: Short-range; shrimp immediately seeks to re-attach to host. Vulnerable in water column.

Chemical and tactile host recognition

Larvae and adults locate and remain on specific crinoid hosts via chemical cues and tactile recognition of pinnule structure. Host specificity varies by species.

Limit: Mechanism not experimentally demonstrated for Laomenes; extrapolated from other pontoniines. Degree of specificity disputed.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Laomenes are small pontoniine shrimps, total body length typically 8-20 mm, with a slender, slightly compressed, translucent to semi-transparent body overlaid with species-specific opaque pigmentation. Rostrum well-developed, compressed, reaching beyond antennular peduncle, dorsal teeth 4-9, ventral teeth 0-3 depending on species; L. nudirostris uniquely lacks dorsal rostral teeth on the distal half (hence nudirostris = naked rostrum). In scientific iconography, Laomenes is illustrated in taxonomic line drawings emphasizing rostrum dentition, eye ornamentation, and second pereiopod chelae (Marin 2009 figs. 1-45). Accounts I
The Ambon shrimp: Corniger amboinensis and the preoccupied nameDe Man, J. G. 1888. Archiv für Naturgeschichte 53(1): 532-533; Clark, A. H. 1919. Smithsonian Miscellaneous Collections 69(5): 11

1888-1919

In 1888, German carcinologist Johannes Govertus de Man described a small shrimp collected at Ambon (Amboina) in the Moluccas by Dr. J. Brock, naming it Corniger amboinensis for its horn-bearing appearance. The name Corniger was already in use for a fish genus, making it unavailable. For 31 years the species sat in taxonomic limbo, usually referred to Periclimenes. In 1919, Austin Hobart Clark, the Smithsonian's crinoid specialist, recognized that crinoid-associated shrimps deserved their own genus and erected Laomenes to accommodate De Man's species, fixing amboinensis as type species. This established the genus that would later absorb other crinoid shrimps. Created the genus Laomenes and anchored its type species and type locality (Ambon, Indonesia). Established the taxonomic link between shrimp and crinoid hosts that defines the genus. All later species assignments refer back to this act.

Borradaile's horns: two Red Sea and Indian Ocean speciesBorradaile, L. A. 1915. Annals and Magazine of Natural History (8)15: 210-211

1915

Lancelot Alexander Borradaile, working at Cambridge, described two new Periclimenes species from crinoids: P. cornutus from the Sudanese Red Sea and P. ceratophthalmus from the Seychelles. Both were remarkable for a horn-like dorsal protuberance on the eye cornea, an adaptation for life among crinoid pinnules. Borradaile noted their association with crinoids but kept them in the large catch-all genus Periclimenes. Their distinctive eyes later became key diagnostic characters when Marin revised Laomenes. Added two morphologically distinct species that expanded the genus concept beyond the Ambon type. Their eye ornamentation remains the primary key character separating L. ceratophthalmus/cornutus from other Laomenes.

Bruce's long watch: nudirostris and jackhintoniBruce, A. J. 1968. Crustaceana Suppl. 1; Bruce, A. J. 2006. The Beagle 22: 43-48; Marin 2009 reassignment

1968-2006

Alexander James Bruce, the most prolific pontoniine worker, described Periclimenes nudirostris in 1968 from Hansa Bay, Papua New Guinea, noting its naked (unarmed) distal rostrum. In 2006, at age 75, he described Periclimenes jackhintoni from Port Essington, Northern Territory, Australia, collected from Comanthus crinoids in shallow water and named for Jack Hinton. Bruce kept both in Periclimenes, but Marin's 2009 revision transferred them to Laomenes based on shared generic characters (hepatic spine, biunguiculate dactyli, crinoid association), honoring Bruce's work while correcting generic placement. Extended genus range to Papua New Guinea and northern Australia, added morphological extremes (naked rostrum vs. horned eye) that define intrageneric variation. Demonstrated that Laomenes diversity was underestimated due to lumping in Periclimenes.

Accounts II
Marin's revision: splitting the amboinensis complexMarin, I. N. 2009. Zootaxa 2053: 1-76

2009

Ivan Marin, working with extensive collections from Vietnam (Nha Trang Bay), Japan, and museum holdings, undertook the first comprehensive revision of Laomenes. He found that specimens identified as 'Laomenes amboinensis' in museums and photographs actually comprised four distinct species differing in color pattern, rostral formula, and second pereiopod proportions. Marin redescribed L. amboinensis sensu stricto and described three new species: L. clarki (honoring Clark), L. pardus (leopard-spotted), and L. tigris (tiger-striped), each with distinct host preferences and distributions. He provided the first illustrated key to all seven species then known, with 45 figures. Doubled the known species diversity in one paper, resolved decades of misidentification in field guides, and established color in life as a reliable taxonomic character. The revision is now the standard reference for identification; WoRMS and subsequent checklists follow Marin 2009.

The Japanese endemic: Laomenes holthuisiMarin, I. N. & Okuno, J. 2010. Zootaxa 2332: 63-68

2010

A year after the major revision, Marin and Junji Okuno described Laomenes holthuisi from Sagami Bay and the Ryukyu Islands, Japan, based on specimens collected by Okuno from crinoids at 20-35 m. The species, named for Dutch carcinologist Lipke Holthuis, is distinguished by a rostrum with 7-8 dorsal teeth, a slender body, and a live coloration of translucent white with small red dots, differing from the ocellated spots of amboinensis. Its discovery showed that even in well-studied Japanese waters, cryptic crinoid shrimp diversity remained hidden due to perfect camouflage. Raised the genus to nine accepted species, extended confirmed range to temperate Japanese waters, and demonstrated ongoing discovery potential. Holotype deposited in Natural History Museum and Institute, Chiba (CBM-ZC).

Living on feathers: the ecology of obligate crinoid commensalismFishelson, L. 1966. Observations on crinoid-associated decapods; Marin 2009 ecological notes; Britayev & Lyskin 2002 symbiont surveys

1966-present (synthesis)

Unlike free-living palaemonids, Laomenes never occurs away from crinoids. Ecological surveys in the Red Sea, Vietnam, and Great Barrier Reef show each shrimp species perches longitudinally on crinoid arms, feeding on detritus and mucus trapped by the host's tube feet. The shrimp gains shelter and food; the crinoid apparently gains cleaning services and no measurable cost, hence classified as commensal or mutualist. Host specificity studies show L. amboinensis is a generalist (recorded on Comasteridae, Himerometridae, Mariametridae), while L. ceratophthalmus prefers Himerometra and L. nudirostris prefers Comanthus. When the crinoid swims by beating its arms, the shrimp clings with its biunguiculate dactyli, riding the host like a passenger. Explains distribution, camouflage evolution, and vulnerability: loss of crinoid hosts due to reef degradation directly threatens Laomenes. Provides model for studying symbiont-host coevolution in Pontoniinae.

Laomenes is a single species, the Ambon crinoid shrimp, found everywhere on crinoids — Laomenes is a genus of nine accepted species. What was once called 'Laomenes amboinensis' everywhere is now recognized as a complex of at least four species (amboinensis, clarki, pardus, tigris) plus five others, each with distinct morphology and coloration. Field guides before 2009 lumped them.

Crinoid shrimps are parasites that harm feather stars — Laomenes are commensals or mild mutualists. They cling to crinoid arms and feed on detritus, mucus and plankton, not host tissue. No damage to host has been documented; they may provide cleaning benefits. True parasites of crinoids are myzostomid worms, not shrimps.

Laomenes can live freely on the reef without a crinoid host — No verified records of Laomenes off its host. All collections and photographs show them on crinoids; planktonic larvae must settle on a crinoid to survive. They are obligate associates.

The genus name Laomenes refers to a Greek mythological figure and has a known meaning — Clark 1919 did not provide an etymology. While the name resembles a Greek personal name, its intended derivation was never explained in the original description and should not be asserted as 'ruler of the people' or similar without source.
