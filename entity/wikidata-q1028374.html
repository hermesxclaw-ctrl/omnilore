The Approach

In the murky depths of Japan's ancient rivers, a serpentine deity stirs—a being of water and spirit, feared and revered, whose name echoes through millennia. The mizuchi is no mere dragon; it is a force of nature, a guardian of watery realms, and a testament to the enduring power of folklore. From the venomous breath that felled travelers to the cunning challenges of gourds, the mizuchi's legend weaves through history, leaving a trail of mystery and awe.

Content advisory. Contains references to human sacrifice, violence, and death in mythological and historical contexts. Some content may be disturbing to sensitive readers.
Identity

Mizuchi

Pronunciation MEE-tsu-chee

Known As

Mi-tsu-chiŌmizuchiKōryūKyūryūChiryūMizushiMedochiMintsuchi

Etymology

The name is composed of 'mi' (water) + 'tsu' (possessive particle) + 'chi' (spirit). The 'chi' is a root used in compounds, often interpreted as 'spirit' or 'master'. Motoori Norinaga suggested it was an honorific.

Cultural Ownership

Japanese; water deity or dragon-like serpent in Japanese mythology and folklore.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars equate mizuchi with the kappa, a water imp. The Chinese characters used (蛟, 虬, 螭) are transliterations and may not reflect original Japanese form. F.J. Daniels warns against deducing forms from Chinese characters.

Description

Appearance

Described as a great water serpent or dragon. In the Nihon Shoki, it is called '大虬' (great water serpent). It can transform into a deer, as in the story of Agatamori. The Chinese characters suggest a four-legged dragon (蛟), a hornless dragon (虬), or a yellow dragon (螭), but scholars caution that these are transliterations and may not reflect original Japanese conception.

Symbols & Iconography

Associated with water, rivers, and pools. The calabash gourd is a symbol in stories, used as a challenge to the mizuchi. In art, it may be depicted as a dragon or serpent in water.

Behaviour & Personality

The mizuchi is malevolent, poisoning and killing passersby with its venomous breath. It is territorial and challenges those who dare to confront it. It is cunning, as shown when it transforms into a deer to try to sink the gourds.

Domains & Habitats

Inhabits rivers, pools, and other aquatic environments. In the Nihon Shoki, it dwells in the Kawashima River (now Takahashi River) in Kibi Province, and in a cave at the bottom of a pool.

Version Differences

The mizuchi is sometimes equated with the kappa, a water imp, based on local names and folklore. In some regions, it is seen as a water deity. The Chinese characters represent different dragon types, but the original Japanese conception may have been more fluid.

Stories & Myths

The Extermination of the Mizuchi by Agatamori

Source Nihon ShokiCulture JapanesePeriod Conventionally 379 CE

Summary

In Kibi Province, a great water serpent (mizuchi) dwelt in the Kawashima River and poisoned passersby. Agatamori, ancestor of the Kasa-no-omi clan, approached and cast three calabashes onto the water, challenging the beast to sink them. The mizuchi transformed into a deer and tried but failed. Agatamori slew it, then sought out its fellows in a cave at the bottom of the pool, killing them all, turning the water to blood. The pool was named 'Agatamori'.

Participants

Agatamori

Mizuchi

The mizuchi's tribe

Consequences

The mizuchi and its kind were exterminated, and the river became safe.

Variants

None noted.

The Sacrifice to the River God

Source Nihon ShokiCulture JapanesePeriod Conventionally 323 CE

Summary

The Mamuta dikes along the Yodo River kept breaking. Emperor Nintoku, guided by a dream, ordered two men, Kowa-kubi and Koromo-no-ko, to be sacrificed to the River God (Kawa-no-kami). One man resisted and cast a calabash into the river, daring the god to sink it as proof of divine will. A whirlwind tried but failed, and the man escaped death.

Participants

Kowa-kubi

Koromo-no-ko

River God (Kawa-no-kami)

Emperor Nintoku

Consequences

The man was spared, and the dikes were eventually completed.

Variants

Aston equates the River God with the mizuchi.

Dark History
Content advisory. References to human sacrifice, violence, and killing.
Warning: References to human sacrifice, violence, and killing. —

Human Sacrifice

Source Nihon Shoki

Description

In the Nintoku 11 story, two men were ordered to be sacrificed to the River God to appease it and stop the dike breaches. One man escaped using a calabash challenge.

Scholarly Context

Visser concludes that human sacrifices were made to dragon-shaped river-gods in ancient times. This is a rare historical record of such practice in Japan.

Violence and Extermination

Source Nihon Shoki

Description

Agatamori slew the mizuchi and its entire tribe, turning the river to blood.

Scholarly Context

This story reflects the human struggle against dangerous natural forces, and the establishment of human dominance.

Mizuchi as kappa or water spirit

Position A

Minakata conjectures that mizuchi became kappa in some regions.

Position A — evidence

Local names like mizushi, medochi, mintsuchi; kappa lore with calabash.

Position B

Yanagita emphasizes mizuchi as a water spirit, not necessarily kappa.

Position B — evidence

Etymology of chi as 'spirit'.

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus; both interpretations are plausible.

Known Gaps

The relationship to kappa remains speculative. Modern media appearances are numerous but not exhaustively catalogued. Language barriers may limit access to some Japanese scholarship.
