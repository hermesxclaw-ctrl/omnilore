Who It Is

Dr. Henry Jekyll and his monstrous alter ego Edward Hyde are the dual protagonists of Robert Louis Stevenson's novella, embodying the most famous literary exploration of the divided self. Dr. Jekyll is a respected London physician and scientist who believes that every person contains opposing impulses — the moral and the primal, the civilized and the savage — and that separating these natures could eliminate internal conflict. Through a chemical potion (not a scientific formula in the strict sense; the 'transcendental medicine' is left deliberately vague), Jekyll transforms into Edward Hyde, a smaller, younger, and palpably repulsive man who embodies all of Jekyll's repressed desires without conscience. The novella opens with Mr. Utterson, Jekyll's lawyer and the central narrative consciousness, investigating the relationship between his client and the mysterious, universally loathed Hyde. The story reveals itself through nested narratives: Utterson's investigation, Dr. Lanyon's letter describing the shock of witnessing the transformation, and ultimately Jekyll's own confession. Hyde's crimes escalate from trampling a child to the brutal, motiveless murder of Sir Danvers Carew — a crime so vicious it shocks all of London. Jekyll tries to abandon his transformations, but finds himself involuntarily becoming Hyde without the potion, and the original formula's ingredients prove irreproducible due to an unknown impurity in the original batch of salts. Trapped, Jekyll chooses suicide over surrendering entirely to Hyde. The novella is a masterpiece of Victorian anxiety: Stevenson understood that the horror of Hyde is not that he is entirely 'other' but that he is intimately 'us.' The narrative structure, withholding the twist (revealed only in the final chapter) and filtering everything through Utterson's limited perspective, creates a detective-story tension that was unprecedented for Gothic fiction at the time. The phrase 'Jekyll and Hyde' has entered the lexicon as shorthand for any dramatic duality in personality, particularly one involving a hidden dark side. The novella predates Freud's structural model of the psyche (id, ego, superego) by decades, yet maps remarkably well onto psychoanalytic concepts — Hyde as the unrestrained id, Jekyll as the conflicted ego — making it perennially relevant to discussions of repression, addiction, and the performance of social identity.

Cultural Significance

The definitive 'dual identity' narrative. Provided the archetype for psychological horror, repressed desire, and the monster within. The name has become universal shorthand for split personality and the duality of human nature. Anticipates Freudian psychoanalysis.
