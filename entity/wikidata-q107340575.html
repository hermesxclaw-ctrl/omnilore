The Approach

In the shadow of Troy, a man named Euchenor once stood at a crossroads: to die slowly of disease at home, or to fall swiftly in battle abroad. He chose the spear. But Euchenor is not one man—he is a name scattered across Greek myth, worn by princes, seers' sons, and wind-kings' offspring, each meeting a fate written by the gods.

Content advisory. The myth of the Danaids involves mass murder and violence. The story of Euchenor at Troy involves death in battle.
Identity

Euchenor

Pronunciation yoo-KAY-nor (English approximation); in Ancient Greek: [eu̯.kʰɛ̌ː.nɔːr]

Etymology

From Greek Εὐχήνωρ (Eukhēnōr), likely derived from εὐχή (eukhē, 'prayer, vow') and ἀνήρ (anēr, 'man'), thus 'man of prayer' or 'man who fulfills vows'.

Cultural Ownership

Greek mythology; multiple distinct figures across different regional traditions (Argive, Corinthian, Megarian, Liparan).

Disputed Identifications

The name is shared by at least four distinct mythological figures. Some ancient sources may conflate them, but modern scholarship treats them as separate. The Euchenor who is son of Polyeidos is sometimes confused with a grandson of the same name.

Description

Appearance

No specific physical description is given in ancient sources for any of the Euchenors. They are depicted as mortal men of heroic or royal status.

Symbols & Iconography

No distinctive symbols or iconography are associated with any Euchenor in ancient art or literature.

Behaviour & Personality

The Corinthian Euchenor is characterized by his decision to accept death at Troy rather than suffer a lingering illness at home, showing courage and fatalism. The other Euchenors are only listed in genealogies with no personality traits.

Domains & Habitats

The Egyptian prince is associated with Egypt and Libya (where the Danaids fled). The Corinthian is from Corinth. The Megarian is associated with Megara. The Liparan is from the island of Lipara.

Version Differences

The four Euchenors are distinct figures in different mythological cycles: the Argive (son of Aegyptus), the Corinthian (son of Polyeidos), the Megarian (grandson of Polyeidos), and the Liparan (son of Aeolus). Some sources may confuse the Corinthian and Megarian.

Stories & Myths

The Slaying of the Sons of Aegyptus

Source Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.1.5Culture GreekPeriod Mythic (Bronze Age)

Summary

Euchenor, son of Aegyptus by an Arabian woman, is one of the fifty sons of Aegyptus who marry the fifty daughters of Danaus. On their wedding night, all but Lynceus are killed by their brides, who obey their father's command. Euchenor is killed by Iphimedousa.

Participants

Euchenor

Iphimedousa

Danaus

Aegyptus

Lynceus

Consequences

The Danaids are later punished in the Underworld; Lynceus and Hypermnestra become ancestors of the Argive royal line.

Variants

Some accounts give different mothers for Euchenor (Eurryroe or Isaie). The lists of Danaids and sons vary among sources.

The Choice of Euchenor

Source Homer, Iliad 13.660-672Culture GreekPeriod Mythic (Trojan War)

Summary

Euchenor, son of the seer Polyeidos, knows from his father's prophecy that he will either die of a painful illness at home or fall in battle at Troy. He chooses to go to Troy and is killed by Paris, fulfilling the prophecy of death in battle.

Participants

Euchenor

Paris

Polyeidos

Consequences

His death is mourned by his men; the story illustrates the theme of fate and choice.

Variants

Later sources (Pausanias) mention a grandson of Polyeidos also named Euchenor, who dedicated a statue to Dionysus at Megara, possibly a different person.

The Sons of Aeolus

Source Tzetzes, Allegories of the OdysseyCulture GreekPeriod Mythic

Summary

Euchenor is one of the sons of Aeolus, keeper of the winds, who marries his sons and daughters to each other to preserve family harmony.

Mass murder of the sons of Aegyptus

Source Pseudo-Apollodorus

Description

The Danaids kill their husbands on their wedding night, including Euchenor. This act is ordered by Danaus and is a central myth of the Danaid cycle.

Scholarly Context

Scholars interpret this myth as reflecting ancient Greek anxieties about marriage, gender, and the founding of Argos.
