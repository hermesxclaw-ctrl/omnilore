Who It Is

Above Berat the limestone wall of Tomorr rises like a white throne, and for as long as Albanians have told stories, someone has sat upon it. He is Baba Tomor — Father Tomor — the old man of the mountain who watches oaths, commands the eagles, and sends the wind down the Osum valley when his beard shakes.

Baba Tomor is patriarchal, stern, just, and protective but quick to punish false oaths. He is a law-giver and oath-witness (besa): oaths sworn 'Për Baba Tomorin' or 'Për atë Tomor' are considered unbreakable; perjurers are struck by his wind, blindness, or fall from the mountain. He is a weather-maker who controls north winds, storms, and summer droughts; shepherds pray to him for rain and for protection of flocks. Aliases — Tomor, Baba Tomorr, At Tomor, Father Tomor, Baba Tomori, Mali i Tomorrit (as personified mountain)

Powers
Mountain Sovereignty and Presence

Embodies and rules Mount Tomorr; can make himself and his palace invisible, cause rockfalls, open or close mountain paths, and be physically present as the mountain itself.

Limit: Power is geographically bound to Tomorr massif; he does not act far beyond sight of the mountain in most tales. Cannot be summoned elsewhere.

Weather and Wind Mastery

Controls winds (especially north wind), clouds, thunder, rain and snow on Tomorr. Shaking his beard or cloak brings storms; prayer and sacrifice can bring rain in drought.

Limit: Requires ritual correctness: sacrifice of ram, or correct oath formula. In some accounts he withholds rain if blood feud pollutes the mountain.

Command of Eagles

Eagles are his servants, spies, and hounds. He sends them to watch humans, carry messages, and punish those who harm eagles. The Albanian double-headed eagle is folk-etymologically linked to his eagles in some modern retellings, though scholarly etymology rejects this.

Limit: Protection is specific to eagles on Tomorr; not all eagles everywhere. Killing an eagle on Tomorr brings curse of blindness or fall.

Oath-Witness and Curse (Besa)

Witnesses oaths and besa sworn in his name. Punishes perjury with illness, madness, loss of flock, or death by lightning/fall. His name makes an oath legally and spiritually binding in Kanun-adjacent customary law.

Limit: Only effective if oath is voluntary and correctly sworn facing Tomorr; coerced oaths are not binding. Punishment is often delayed, not instant.

Blessing of Fertility and Protection of Flocks

Grants fertility to fields below Tomorr, increase of flocks, and safe passage for shepherds and pilgrims who leave votive offerings (bread, wool, coins) at Kulmak.

Limit: Blessing requires annual pilgrimage or offering; neglect brings barrenness. Not a universal fertility god beyond Tomorr's watershed.

Shapeshifting and Invisibility

Can appear as cloud, old shepherd, eagle, or white horse rider; can become invisible to test hospitality. In one tale he appears as a poor traveler to test a shepherd's generosity.

Limit: Transformations are temporary and tied to moral testing; he does not use trickster shapeshifting for deception.

Form & Signs

In oral accounts collected by Tirta and summarized by Elsie, his beard and hair are the snowfields and clouds of Tomorr; when he shakes his beard, wind and mist descend the Osum and Vjosë valleys. Accounts I
Baba Tomor and the Eagles - Why Eagles Do Not Nest LowMark Tirta - Mitologjia ndër shqiptarë (2004) pp.71-72; variant in Robert Elsie - Albanian Folktales and Legends (2015) tale 12

Oral tradition recorded 19th-20th century; undated folk antiquity

When the world was young, eagles nested in the lowlands and were hunted. Baba Tomor took pity and called them to his mountain, declaring that from now on eagles would nest only on Tomorr's cliffs under his protection, and no man should kill them. He gave them the high winds to ride. A hunter who shot an eagle on Tomorr was turned to stone on the cliff, which shepherds still point to as Guri i Shqiponjës. Explains taboo against killing eagles on Tomorr and eagles' high nesting; reinforces Baba Tomor as protector of sacred animals.

The Oath on Tomorr - The Perjurer's FallJohann Georg von Hahn - Albanesische Studien (1854) Vol.1 p.158; corroborated by Tirta 2004 p.73 and Doja on Kanun oaths

Recorded 1854, practice attested into 20th century

Two brothers disputed a pasture boundary. The elder swore falsely 'Për Baba Tomorin se kjo tokë është imja' (By Father Tomor this land is mine) while facing the summit. Baba Tomor sent a sudden wind that threw him from the ridge, or in another telling struck him with lightning, while the truthful brother's flock doubled. The story is used to warn that no oath on Tomor can be broken without punishment. Establishes Baba Tomor as divine witness in customary law; oath formula remains in use; explains rockslide features as divine punishment.

Baba Tomor and the Bektashi Saint - Abbas Ali's ArrivalF.W. Hasluck - Christianity and Islam under the Sultans (1929) Vol.2 pp.527-529; Elsie 2019 on Bektashi shrines; oral pilgrimage legends at Kulmak Tekke

Bektashi overlay late 18th - 19th century; shrine rebuilt 1916

When the Bektashis came to Tomorr, they found shepherds already praying to Baba Tomor. To reconcile, the dervishes taught that Baba Tomor was in truth Abbas Ali, who had ridden his white horse from Karbala to Tomorr in one night and whose tomb is the shrine on Kulmak. Baba Tomor welcomed him and shared the mountain, or according to stricter Bektashis, revealed himself to be Abbas Ali all along. Pilgrims now climb for five days in August to honor both. Explains dual dedication of Kulmak shrine; annual pilgrimage August 20-25 draws thousands; creates layered identity still debated by ethnographers.

Accounts II
The Shepherd's Test - Hospitality on the SummitTirta 2004 p.74; Elsie - Dictionary p.24 summary of oral tale

Oral, collected 20th century

Baba Tomor descended as a ragged old traveler during a storm and asked a shepherd on Tomorr for bread and shelter. The shepherd shared his last loaf and covered the old man with his own cloak. Revealing himself, Baba Tomor blessed the shepherd's flock to never diminish and his spring to never dry, while a nearby rich man who had refused the same traveler found his sheep scattered by wolves the next day. Moral tale reinforcing hospitality (mikpritja) and respect for elders/strangers as divine test; told to children in Tomorr villages.

Baba Tomor Holds Back the DroughtEdith Durham - High Albania (1909) pp. 112-115 on Berat; Tirta field notes on rain rituals

Ritual practice attested 19th-20th century, story timeless

In a year of great drought, the elders climbed to Kulmak with a white ram, bread, and salt, praying to Baba Tomor to shake his beard and send rain. After sacrifice and all-night vigil, clouds gathered from Tomorr's peak and rain fell for three days, saving the Myzeqe crops. If the ram was not offered correctly or if blood had been shed unjustly on the mountain, Baba Tomor withheld rain until atonement. Explains annual kurban and rain-prayer at Tomorr; links moral purity and correct ritual to weather; still referenced during modern pilgrimage sacrifices.

The Mountain's Division - Tomor and His Brother MountainsOral cosmology summarized by Tirta 2004 and Elsie 2001; comparative note in Durham

Oral, undated

In the beginning mountains were brothers. Baba Tomor, as eldest, took the highest throne in the center, while his younger brothers Dajti, Shpirag, and Ostrovica took lesser peaks around him. They agreed to watch over their own valleys and not trespass. When Dajti sent his clouds over Tomorr's pastures, Baba Tomor struck him with thunder and set the boundary of winds that shepherds still observe. Cosmological map explaining hierarchy of sacred mountains in Albania; reinforces Tomorr's primacy in central Albania; analogous to other Indo-European mountain-brother myths.

Antiquity and Illyrian continuityBaba Tomor continues an Illyrian/Epirote mountain god of Tomaros, showing direct continuity from antiquity.Continuity is only of the mountain name Tomaros/Tomorr; no evidence proves continuity of cult or deity from Illyrian period to modern folklore.

Baba Tomor is the same as the Hungarian village Tomor or derives from Hungarian mythology. — No connection. Tomor, Hungary is a Slavic/Hungarian toponym of different etymology, and personal names Tomor Alizoti/Malasi are modern Albanian given names derived from the mountain, not sources of the myth. The myth is strictly central Albanian.

Baba Tomor is the Albanian equivalent of Zeus and lives on Mount Olympus. — While both are mountain/sky fathers, Baba Tomor is specifically tied to Mount Tomorr in Albania, not Olympus, and his functions (oath-witness, eagle-master, shepherd protector) are distinct from Zeus's pan-Hellenic kingship. Comparison is modern popular analogy, not folk identification.

The double-headed eagle on the Albanian flag is Baba Tomor's eagle. — The Albanian double-headed eagle derives from medieval heraldry (Kastrioti, Byzantine influence), not directly from Baba Tomor's eagles, though folk etymology sometimes links them. Ethnographers note eagles as his servants, but no historical source connects the flag design to Tomor.

Baba Tomor is purely a Bektashi saint with no pre-Islamic roots. — Bektashi veneration of Abbas Ali on Tomorr is real and dominant today, but ethnographic records (Hahn 1854, Durham 1909, Tirta) document a pre-existing mountain-father figure with oath and weather functions independent of Islam.
