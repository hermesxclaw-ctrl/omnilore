Who They Are
Form

Attested descriptions vary by source layer. The Russian encyclopedia tradition (Meletinsky 1990) describes her as descending from the eastern sky surrounded by a halo of light, in the form of a richly dressed elderly woman — or, alternately, in the form of a mare. The Turkish tradition (Beydili; Turkish Wikipedia) develops the equine form: Ayzıt can assume the shape of a silver-feathered mare and descends from the sky in that form, using her tail and mane like wings; in human form she wears a white kalpak (cap) and a white shawl over bare shoulders. The same Turkish source describes her daughters as maidens who, donning a magical white veil, transform into swans — swans being her sacred totem (ongun). No physical description survives from the oldest Yakut stratum beyond the 'richly dressed old woman or mare' formula; the elaborate Turkish iconography is a later layer and should be labeled as such.

Powers

Soul delivery (kut) Aisyt brings the soul (kut) of a newborn child from heaven to the world at the moment of birth, so that a complete human being comes into existence. In the Sakha-language account, the child's body grows in the mother's womb under her watch; at the instant of birth she sends the kut into the child with the first breath, and she has many methods of delivering it (the Sakha article's 'catapult' apparatus is a modern neo-traditional detail, not historical). English Wikipedia (Aisyt); Sakha Wikipedia (Айыыһыт); Cotterell 1997; Brooklyn Museum 2007 The soul must be sent by the supreme Aiyy (Ürüñ Aiyy) before she can deliver it — she is the carrier, not the origin, of the soul in the Sakha account: 'When Ürüñ Aiyy gives the child's kut, Aiyyhyt brings it into the destined person.' Boys' kuts are conveyed as a knife (byhah) or arrow (onogos), girls' kuts as scissors (kyptyy). Some sources imply she creates/brings the soul herself; the Sakha-language account makes her a delivery agent subordinate to Ürüñ Aiyy. The two need not conflict (carrier vs source) but are differently emphasized.

Fate-recording in the Golden Book She keeps a golden book in which she records every birth — and, in the fuller account, the name and destiny of the person. Only with this entry does the newborn become a full member of humanity. She is said to control the fate of the world from her seven-story mountain house. Loar 2010; Cotterell 1997; Dixon-Kennedy 1998; Brooklyn Museum 2007; Turkish Wikipedia ('Altın Kitap') None recorded; the golden book appears as an absolute register. Whether she decrees fate or merely records it is not stated in the sources — the difference is unresolved. Brooklyn Museum and Loar describe her as 'controlling' fate; Cotterell/Dixon-Kennedy emphasize recording. Recording can imply control of the register without specifying predestination.

Relief of labor pain Women in labor channel Aisyt, believing this relieves their pain during childbirth. In the ethnographic account of Friedrich & Buddruss, she eases the birth-pangs by enacting the birth on her own body, and is present as 'mother of cradles' when a devotee gives birth. Loar 2010; Friedrich & Buddruss 1955 None recorded; this is a cultic belief about her efficacy, not a narrative feat. The two mechanisms (channeling vs divine co-enactment) differ across sources.

Hundredfold nourishment From the root of the cosmic tree (or world pillar of Ürüñ Aar Toyon), beside a lake of milk, she suckles a pale youth who sought the tree's blessing, increasing his strength a hundredfold. English Wikipedia (Aisyt, Legends); Dixon-Kennedy 1998; Encyclopedia Mythica (Smart 1997) Single attested legend; the power is demonstrated once, on a mythic youth. The tree is identified variously as the cosmic tree and as the world pillar of Ürüñ Aar Toyon.

Species-specific birth patronage Within the collective reading of the aiyysyts, different manifestations govern births of different beings: Nyelbey Ayııhıt (Ньэлбэй Айыыһыт) — human children; Cöhögöy Toyon (Дьөһөгөй Тойон) — reproduction of horses; İhegey İeyiexsit (Иһэгэй Иэйиэхсит) — oxen/cattle; Noruluya — dogs and foxes; and further horse-cattle figures (Kienğ Kielie-Baly Toyon). The Russian source adds that the aiyysyt of human beings sits in the direction of the summer sunrise, the horse aiyysyt toward the winter sunrise, and the cattle aiyysyt under the earth. Meletinsky 1990 (as cited in EN/RU Wikipedia); English Wikipedia (Versions) The gender of the name shifts: for births of male animals (stallions, bulls) the word ayııhıt is understood as masculine; for mares or cows, feminine (Jordan 2004). This species-specific plurality coexists uneasily with the 'one supreme goddess' reading — the Meletinsky dictionary records both.

Domestic-animal guidance In the ancestral legend of Omogoy Baai, the gods İeyiexsit and Aiyyhyt assisted him in locating a mare and a pregnant cow when he arrived at the Tuymaada Valley of the Middle Lena — read by scholars as folk memory of livestock domestication. Yakut shamanism article (Alekseev, Emelyanov & Petrov 2005) Attested only in this founding-ancestor narrative. None recorded.

Post-natal protection (neo-traditional) In the contemporary Sakha-language account, Aiyyhyt continues to watch over the child as it grows, granting protective energy so the child does not fall ill, starve, or freeze; at age five an 'impulse ring' is set over the child's head and the 'silver thread of life' connects the person to her monitoring. This is modern religious re-mythologization, not attested historical lore. Sakha Wikipedia (Айыыһыт), contemporary section Neo-traditional layer; should be presented as modern belief, with the 'computer monitoring' imagery clearly flagged. None with the historical layer — it is a different genre of source (living-tradition catechesis).

Accounts

A pale (white) young man came upon the cosmic tree — alternatively the world pillar of Ürüñ Aar Toyon — standing beside a lake of milk. He praised/blessed the tree and asked its blessing. From the roots of the tree a goddess rose: Aisyt. She offered the youth milk from her breasts; he drank, and his strength increased a hundredfold.

The ancestor Omogoy Baai traveled to the Tuymaada Valley of the Middle Lena (near modern Yakutsk) on a shaman's advice. There the gods İeyiexsit and Aiyyhyt (Aisyt) assisted him in finding a mare and a pregnant cow. Scholars read this as a folk memory of livestock domestication by the ancestral Yakuts.

Aisyt lives on a mountaintop in a house with seven stories, from which she controls the fate of the world. At every human birth she brings the soul from heaven, attends the labor, and records the birth in a golden book; only with this entry does the newborn become a full member of humanity.

Aiyysyt lives in the eastern sky and descends at childbirth surrounded by a halo of light, appearing as a richly dressed elderly woman or a mare. She helps the labor to a good end, blesses the newborn child, and leaves the house of the woman on the third day after the birth. The Sakha-language account adds that the household must not shout or raise noise after a birth, lest the goddess be frightened away; she is invisible to 'closed' people, and where she turns her face joy settles, where she steps abundance falls.

Content Notes

During the Soviet period, adherents of Yakut shamanic tradition were persecuted and the faith had to be practiced in secret. The systematic Soviet campaign against 'religious survivals' suppressed the public cult of the Aiyy deities, including the birth-goddess rites, and nearly extinguished the Yhyakh festival (until 1990 the first Yakutsk Yhyakh was held, after decades of stagnation).

The Soviet anti-religion campaigns targeted shamanism as a 'survival of primitive society'; the suppression is documented in Siberian ethnography and in the revival literature (Balzer 2005).

Yakut shamanism article; Yhyakh article

From the Russian conquest of the Yakut homeland in the 17th century, Eastern Orthodox Christianity exerted assimilatory pressure on the Yakut religion. In 2001 a local Orthodox archbishop sharply criticized the construction of the 'pagan temple' (the House of Purification / Archie Diete, opened 2002) as kapishche; after a 2002 change of republican leadership the pro-Orthodox course prevailed and the building was transferred to the city as a house of culture.

This is a live religious-politics conflict in the Sakha Republic; ethnic-religion activists continue to demand the return of the temple and a 'Three Birch' national park and pagan school.

Aiyy Faith article (Vorontsova & Filatov 2006)

Pan-Turkic popular mythography (Turkish-language encyclopedias, nationalist websites) reframes Aisyt/Ayzıt as a moon-and-love beauty goddess with swan maidens, detaching her from her attested Sakha birth-and-fate core. The same tendency appears in modern Sakha neo-traditional texts that graft contemporary imagery ('computers', 'impulse rings') onto the goddess. Neither is malicious, but both risk erasing the documented tradition in favor of invented or modernized lore.

The Yakut religion scholar N. A. Alekseev and the Sakha intellectual tradition document the historical core; comparison across language versions reveals the accretion of nationalist and revivalist layers.

Scholarly Debates
Etymology: create-root vs moon-root
Ayııhıt derives from ay- ('to create, bring into being') + -hıt, meaning 'birthgiver' (Yakut-language tradition; Russian scholarship). ⇌ Ayzıt derives from ay ('moon'), meaning 'bright as the moon' (Turkish-language mythography; Beydili).
Evidence for A: Sakha phonology and morphology; Meletinsky 1990; Yakut sources uniformly gloss 'birthgiver'.
Evidence for B: Turkish nationalist-etymological tradition; association with lunar beauty symbolism.
One goddess or a class of goddesses?
Aiyysyt is a single supreme goddess of childbirth and fate. ⇌ Aiyysyt is a collective name for a class of birth-patron goddesses, each ruling births of a different species.
Evidence for A: Individual reading in Russian Wikipedia; the single figure with golden book in Western reference works (Loar, Cotterell,
Evidence for B: Meletinsky 1990 lists Nyelbey Ayııhıt (children), Cöhögöy Toyon (horses), İhegey İeyiexsit (oxen), Noruluya (dogs/foxes)
Residence: sky, mountain, or tree-root
She lives in the eastern sky (human aiyysyt toward the summer sunrise; horse aiyysyt toward the winter sunrise; cattle aiyysyt under the earth). ⇌ She lives in a seven-story house on a mountaintop from which she controls fate.
Evidence for A: Meletinsky 1990 (via Russian Wikipedia).
Evidence for B: Loar 2010; Cotterell 1997; Brooklyn Museum 2007.
The Nanaia/Nanaya connection
Nyelbey Ayııhıt is linked to the ancient Near Eastern goddess Nanaia/Nanaya (modern Sakha-language claim, with the epithet 'Nanaia'). ⇌ No classical or ethnographic source supports a direct lineage from the Achaemenid-era Nanaia to the Yakut birth-goddess.
Evidence for A: Sakha Wikipedia contemporary passage.
Evidence for B: Absence in Meletinsky, Jordan, and ethnographic literature; no documented transmission path.

A way to hold it in your mind

Somewhere above the Lena River, in the roof of the world the Yakuts call the Upper World, a goddess sits with a golden book open on her lap, waiting for the next breath. When a child is born, she is already there — descending from the eastern sky in a halo of light as a richly dressed old woman, or as a mare, to carry the soul from heaven into the newborn's first gasp and to write the name into her register. The Yakuts call her Aiyyhyt, 'the birthgiver'; popular Western goddess books call her the mother of cradles. She is the rare deity whose harshest surviving truth is a gentle one: her people say the Aiyy gods accept no blood, only milk and grass and kumys — and that a household must fall silent after a birth, because the goddess who decides the fate of the world can still be frightened away by noise.
