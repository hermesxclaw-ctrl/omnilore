Who It Is

Somewhere above the Lena River, in the roof of the world the Yakuts call the Upper World, a goddess sits with a golden book open on her lap, waiting for the next breath. When a child is born, she is already there — descending from the eastern sky in a halo of light as a richly dressed old woman, or as a mare, to carry the soul from heaven into the newborn's first gasp and to write the name into her register. The Yakuts call her Aiyyhyt, 'the birthgiver'; popular Western goddess books call her the mother of cradles. She is the rare deity whose harshest surviving truth is a gentle one: her people say the Aiyy gods accept no blood, only milk and grass and kumys — and that a household must fall silent after a birth, because the goddess who decides the fate of the world can still be frightened away by noise.

She is uniformly benevolent: she attends every birth, conducts the soul of the newborn from heaven to the body, blesses the child, and protects children and animal young. She relieves labor pain — either by being channeled by the woman (Loar) or by enacting the birth on her own body (Friedrich & Buddruss). She nourishes those in need from the root of the cosmic tree. Name & Tongue

Etymology — The Sakha (Yakut) name Айыыһыт (Ayııhıt) derives from the verb root ay- (ай-, 'to create, to bring into being') — the same root that yields айыы (aiyy), the class of benevolent creator deities — plus the agentive suffix -hıt, producing something like 'the one who brings into being' or 'birthgiver'.

Pronunciation — Sakha: /a.jɯː.ˈhɯt/ (Ayııhıt); common English approximation: AY-sit or ah-YIT; Turkish: /aj.zɯt/.

Aliases — Aysyt, Ajsyt, Ajyhyt, Ajyysyt, Ajysyt, Ayııhıt

Powers
Soul delivery (kut)

Aisyt brings the soul (kut) of a newborn child from heaven to the world at the moment of birth, so that a complete human being comes into existence. In the Sakha-language account, the child's body grows in the mother's womb under her watch; at the instant of birth she sends the kut into the child with the first breath, and she has many methods of delivering it (the Sakha article's 'catapult' apparatus is a modern neo-traditional detail, not historical).

Limit: The soul must be sent by the supreme Aiyy (Ürüñ Aiyy) before she can deliver it — she is the carrier, not the origin, of the soul in the Sakha account: 'When Ürüñ Aiyy gives the child's kut, Aiyyhyt brings it into the destined person.' Boys' kuts are conveyed as a knife (byhah) or arrow (onogos), girls' kuts as scissors (kyptyy).

Fate-recording in the Golden Book

She keeps a golden book in which she records every birth — and, in the fuller account, the name and destiny of the person. Only with this entry does the newborn become a full member of humanity. She is said to control the fate of the world from her seven-story mountain house.

Limit: None recorded; the golden book appears as an absolute register. Whether she decrees fate or merely records it is not stated in the sources — the difference is unresolved.

Relief of labor pain

Women in labor channel Aisyt, believing this relieves their pain during childbirth. In the ethnographic account of Friedrich & Buddruss, she eases the birth-pangs by enacting the birth on her own body, and is present as 'mother of cradles' when a devotee gives birth.

Limit: None recorded; this is a cultic belief about her efficacy, not a narrative feat.

Hundredfold nourishment

From the root of the cosmic tree (or world pillar of Ürüñ Aar Toyon), beside a lake of milk, she suckles a pale youth who sought the tree's blessing, increasing his strength a hundredfold.

Limit: Single attested legend; the power is demonstrated once, on a mythic youth.

Species-specific birth patronage

Within the collective reading of the aiyysyts, different manifestations govern births of different beings: Nyelbey Ayııhıt (Ньэлбэй Айыыһыт) — human children; Cöhögöy Toyon (Дьөһөгөй Тойон) — reproduction of horses; İhegey İeyiexsit (Иһэгэй Иэйиэхсит) — oxen/cattle; Noruluya — dogs and foxes; and further horse-cattle figures (Kienğ Kielie-Baly Toyon). The Russian source adds that the aiyysyt of human beings sits in the direction of the summer sunrise, the horse aiyysyt toward the winter sunrise, and the cattle aiyysyt under the earth.

Limit: The gender of the name shifts: for births of male animals (stallions, bulls) the word ayııhıt is understood as masculine; for mares or cows, feminine (Jordan 2004).

Domestic-animal guidance

In the ancestral legend of Omogoy Baai, the gods İeyiexsit and Aiyyhyt assisted him in locating a mare and a pregnant cow when he arrived at the Tuymaada Valley of the Middle Lena — read by scholars as folk memory of livestock domestication.

Limit: Attested only in this founding-ancestor narrative.

Post-natal protection (neo-traditional)

In the contemporary Sakha-language account, Aiyyhyt continues to watch over the child as it grows, granting protective energy so the child does not fall ill, starve, or freeze; at age five an 'impulse ring' is set over the child's head and the 'silver thread of life' connects the person to her monitoring. This is modern religious re-mythologization, not attested historical lore.

Limit: Neo-traditional layer; should be presented as modern belief, with the 'computer monitoring' imagery clearly flagged.

Accounts I
The pale youth and the lake of milkYakut legend, recorded in Dixon-Kennedy 1998; Cotterell 1997; Encyclopedia Mythica (Smart); English Wikipedia (Aisyt)

Undated oral tradition; recorded in modern reference works

A pale (white) young man came upon the cosmic tree — alternatively the world pillar of Ürüñ Aar Toyon — standing beside a lake of milk. He praised/blessed the tree and asked its blessing. From the roots of the tree a goddess rose: Aisyt. She offered the youth milk from her breasts; he drank, and his strength increased a hundredfold. The youth's strength multiplied a hundredfold; the legend is read as binding the mother-goddess to the world tree, which appears both as sky-pillar and as source of fertility.

Omogoy Baai and the gift of livestockYakut oral histories; written account by Lindenau (mid-18th century, Great Northern Expedition); scholarly retelling in Alekseev, Emelyanov & Petrov 2005

Legendary founding period; first written 18th century

The ancestor Omogoy Baai traveled to the Tuymaada Valley of the Middle Lena (near modern Yakutsk) on a shaman's advice. There the gods İeyiexsit and Aiyyhyt (Aisyt) assisted him in finding a mare and a pregnant cow. Scholars read this as a folk memory of livestock domestication by the ancestral Yakuts. Omogoy establishes the first homestead; the episode grounds the Yakut possession of horse and cattle herds in divine favor.

Accounts II
The seven-story house and the golden bookLoar 2010; Cotterell 1997; Brooklyn Museum 2007; Encyclopedia Mythica (Smart)

Undated tradition; transmitted via reference literature

Aisyt lives on a mountaintop in a house with seven stories, from which she controls the fate of the world. At every human birth she brings the soul from heaven, attends the labor, and records the birth in a golden book; only with this entry does the newborn become a full member of humanity. Every birth is registered in the cosmic record; the world's fate is administered from her seven-story dwelling.

Descent at birth and the third-day departureRussian encyclopedia tradition (Meletinsky 1990)

Documented in 20th-century Soviet scholarship

Aiyysyt lives in the eastern sky and descends at childbirth surrounded by a halo of light, appearing as a richly dressed elderly woman or a mare. She helps the labor to a good end, blesses the newborn child, and leaves the house of the woman on the third day after the birth. The Sakha-language account adds that the household must not shout or raise noise after a birth, lest the goddess be frightened away; she is invisible to 'closed' people, and where she turns her face joy settles, where she steps abundance falls. Safe delivery and blessing of the child; a three-day postpartum ritual window observed by the family.

Aisyt is a moon goddess. — The attested Sakha meaning is 'birthgiver', from ay- 'to create'. The moon association is a Turkish-language folk etymology (ay = 'moon') attached to the pan-Turkic layer of her reception, not part of the Siberian evidence.

Aisyt decides/dooms people's destinies like a fatalistic judge. — Her golden book is primarily a birth register: with the entry of name and destiny the newborn becomes a full member of humanity. Sources differ on whether she 'controls' fate or 'records' it; the record-keeping reading (Cotterell, Dixon-Kennedy) is the more precise.

Aisyt is a blood-sacrifice goddess like many other ancient deities. — The Aiyy deities, including the birth-goddess class, reject blood sacrifice; they receive vegetable and dairy offerings (kumys at Yhyakh). Her cult is explicitly non-violent.

The swan-maiden imagery (Ayzıt's daughters turning into swans, silver-feathered mare) is ancient Yakut lore. — Swan and crane symbolism appears in the pan-Turkic layer and in Altai parallels; the Yakut-layer sources describe her as a richly dressed old woman or a mare, with no swan-daughters motif. The swan material should be labeled pan-Turkic, not Yakut.
