Who It Is

Ymir (also called Aurgelmir in the Poetic Edda) is the primordial frost giant from whose body the world was created — the first living being in Norse cosmology. In the beginning, there was only Ginnungagap, the yawning void, with ice from Niflheim melting in the heat from Muspelheim. From the melting ice emerged Ymir, the first jötunn. As he slept, he sweated: from his armpits grew a male and a female jötunn, and his legs mated to produce a six-headed son — the first of the frost giant race. Ymir was nourished by the cosmic cow Auðumbla, who licked the salty ice blocks, revealing Buri (the first of the gods, grandfather of Odin). Odin and his brothers Vili and Vé killed Ymir, and from his body they built the world: his flesh became the earth, his blood the seas, his bones the mountains, his teeth the rocks and cliffs, his skull the sky (held up by four dwarves), and his brains the clouds. Ymir's blood caused a great flood that drowned all frost giants except Bergelmir, who survived with his wife to re-establish the race. Ymir represents the Norse concept of creation-through-sacrifice — the material cosmos created from the body of a slain primordial being, a theme found across Indo-European mythologies (Purusha in Vedic, Tiamat in Mesopotamian). He is not a deity but the raw, unformed potential of the universe, killed to give form to the world.

Attested in Poetic Edda (Völuspá); Prose Edda (Gylfaginning, from Vafþrúðnismál)Era Viking Age (c. 800–1100 CE); recorded 13th century

Form

Ymir is never described in any surviving text with physical detail—no height, no color, no number of heads. The only description is implicit: he is a giant, he sweats, he sleeps, and his body is so vast that the entire cosmos is made from it. The name 'Bláinn' (dark/blue-black) might suggest a dark or blue-tinged complexion, but this is speculative. In his role as the ancestor of frost giants, he is often imagined as a colossal, icy figure, but this is a modern artistic convention, not attested in the sources.

Powers

Asexual Reproduction Ymir produced offspring without a partner: from his left armpit grew a man and a woman, and from his legs grew a son. This is a unique form of parthenogenesis-attested only for Ymir. Gylfaginning (Prose Edda) The offspring were frost giants, not gods. The process was apparently unconscious—he was asleep when it happened. No other source mentions this method of reproduction.

Sweat-based Life Creation While sleeping, Ymir sweated, and from his sweat (or from the melting ice beneath him) more giants emerged. This is another form of unconscious creation. Gylfaginning (Prose Edda) The text is ambiguous about whether the giants came from his sweat directly or from the ice melted by his body heat. None.

Cosmic Scale Body Ymir's body is so vast that the entire world is made from it. His flesh becomes the earth, his blood the sea, his bones the mountains, his hair the trees, his skull the sky, and his brows the walls of Midgard. Vafþrúðnismál, Grímnismál, Hávamál, Gylfaginning This is a posthumous power—it only manifests after his death. It is also a one-time event; there is no sense that he could have done this while alive. The poems and Snorri agree on the body-parts-to-world mapping, but Snorri adds the detail about the brains becoming clouds, which is not in the poems.

Immortality (Implied) Ymir is the first being and is never said to die of natural causes. He is killed by the gods, but his body continues to exist as the world, suggesting a form of immortality-through-transformation. All sources He is mortal in the sense that he can be killed, but his substance is never destroyed—it is repurposed. None.

Behavior & Habitat

Ymir's behavior in the sources is minimal: he is born from melting ice, he sleeps, he sweats, and he is killed. He has no dialogue, no quests, no personality. His only 'action' is unconscious—his body becomes the world. He is a passive, almost geological force rather than a character.

Accounts

Before the world existed, there was only Ginnungagap, the yawning void. To the north lay Niflheim, cold and dark, with the spring Hvergelmir, from which flowed the poisonous rivers Élivágar. To the south lay Muspelheim, hot and bright. Where the rivers' poison-ice met the heat of Muspelheim, it melted and dripped, and from these drops of rime and poison emerged Ymir, the first giant.

Ymir was nourished by the cosmic cow Auðumbla, who licked the salty ice-blocks. From the ice emerged Búri, the first god. Búri's son Borr married Bestla, a giant's daughter, and their sons were Odin, Vili, and Vé. The three brothers killed Ymir. From his body they made the world: his flesh became the earth, his blood the sea, his bones the mountains, his hair the trees, his skull the sky, and his brains the clouds. From his brows they made Midgard, the realm of humans. The flood of Ymir's blood drowned all the frost giants except Bergelmir, who escaped with his wife and became the ancestor of the next generation of giants.

When the gods killed Ymir, so much blood flowed from his wounds that it drowned all the frost giants. Only Bergelmir and his wife escaped, by climbing onto a wooden chest or boat (the text says 'lúðr', which is ambiguous). They survived and became the ancestors of all later frost giants.

Scholarly Debates
Aurgelmir vs. Ymir
Aurgelmir is a separate, older name for the same being as Ymir. ⇌ Aurgelmir is a different giant, possibly the grandfather of Bergelmir, and distinct from Ymir.
The origin of Ymir
Ymir emerged from the melting ice of Élivágar when it met the heat of Muspelheim. ⇌ Ymir was created from the poison and rime of the rivers themselves, without the need for Muspelheim's heat.
The creation of the world from Ymir's body
The world was created from Ymir's body parts as listed in the poems: flesh, blood, bones, hair, skull, brows. ⇌ Snorri adds details not in the poems: the brains become clouds, and the eyebrows become Midgard.
The murder of Ymir
The gods (Odin, Vili, and Vé) killed Ymir. ⇌ The poems do not say who killed Ymir, only that his body was used to make the world.

What We Don't Know

The surviving sources are fragmentary and were written down centuries after the Viking Age. We have no record of the full oral tradition that preceded Snorri's Edda. The exact nature of Ymir's origin (how he emerged from the ice) is not described in detail. His physical appearance is completely unattested. The relationship between Ymir and the other primordial beings (Auðumbla, Búri) is only partially explained. The symbolic meaning of the creation-from-corpse myth is debated among scholars.

Sources & Standing
Well-Attested

Multiple independent primary sources agree on the core narratives. Primary attestation: Poetic Edda (Völuspá); Prose Edda (Gylfaginning, from Vafþrúðnismál).

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Ymir as the universe's raw material—a cosmic 'big bang' in the form of a giant. He didn't create the world; he was the world. The gods didn't build from nothing; they repurposed what was already there. It's like a sculptor who doesn't carve a statue from a block of marble, but instead kills the marble, cuts it into pieces, and assembles a city from the chunks. Ymir is less a character and more a cosmic event—a geological force with a heartbeat.
