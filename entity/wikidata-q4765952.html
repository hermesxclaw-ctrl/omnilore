Form

Ankalamma's iconography is not standardized. She is typically represented as a rough, unhewn stone (a 'mula virat' or 'moolasthanam') often smeared with turmeric (pasupu) and vermilion (kumkum). In more anthropomorphic representations, she is a fierce, four-armed goddess. She may hold a trident (trishula), a sickle, a drum, and a skull cup or a severed head. She is often depicted with a protruding tongue, like Kali, and is adorned with neem leaves (margosa). She is sometimes shown seated on a tiger or a dog, or standing on a demon (asura). She is often depicted as a mother, with one breast exposed, symbolizing her nurturing and fierce aspects simultaneously. A key feature is the presence of a 'pallem' (a large brass or bronze pot) which is central to her worship.

Powers

Control over Disease Ankalamma's primary power is her ability to both inflict and cure epidemic diseases, particularly smallpox, chickenpox, cholera, and plague. She is believed to 'strike' a person with the disease as a form of punishment or as a sign of her attention. Conversely, she can be propitiated to cure the afflicted. All versions Her power is largely focused on the village she protects. She is a localized deity, not a universal one. Modern medicine and public health campaigns have reduced the fear of these diseases, leading to a decline in her role as a disease deity in some urbanized areas, though she remains a protector figure.

Village Protection She is the guardian of the village's boundaries. She is believed to ward off evil spirits, demons, witches, and malevolent forces that might cross into the village. She also protects the village from natural calamities like drought, flood, and cattle disease. All versions Her protection is conditional on proper worship and appeasement. If neglected, she may withdraw her protection or turn her wrath upon the village. She is also believed to be vulnerable to pollution (e.g., during childbirth, death) and must be ritually purified. None.

Fertility and Prosperity As a mother goddess, she is associated with fertility — of the land, of cattle, and of women. She is invoked for good rains, bountiful harvests, and healthy offspring. All versions Her blessings are tied to the proper performance of agricultural rituals and her own festival. None.

Power over Spirits She acts as an intermediary between the living and the dead. She can command spirits and is often invoked to pacify restless souls or to protect against ghostly attacks. All versions This power is often delegated to her male attendants or to a possessed priest (a 'velama' or 'caran') who acts as her oracle. None.

Accounts

There is no single, canonical creation myth. In one common story, Ankalamma was a beautiful and virtuous woman who, along with her six sisters (often identified as the Saptamatrikas), was pursued by a demon. To escape his advances, she committed suicide or was martyred. After her death, she was deified for her chastity and courage, becoming the protector of the village. In another version, she is an aspect of the Goddess Parvati/Kali who was sent to Earth to vanquish a powerful demon (often named 'Ankasura' or 'Mahishasura'). After the battle, she chose to remain in the village to protect her devotees. The 'pallem' (pot) is often explained as the vessel in which she collects the demon's blood to prevent it from spawning more demons.

A common moral tale. A village becomes prosperous and neglects its annual sacrifice to Ankalamma. The goddess, angered by this disrespect, sends a terrible epidemic (often smallpox or cholera) upon the village. The people suffer greatly until they realize their mistake. They then perform a grand propitiation ceremony, offering sacrifices and prayers, and the goddess relents, restoring health and prosperity.

The buffalo sacrifice, a central part of her worship, is often explained as a re-enactment of her victory over a buffalo demon (Mahishasura). The buffalo represents the demon, and its sacrifice is both a symbolic re-enactment of her triumph and a way to channel her martial power into the community.

Scholarly Debates
Origin of the Name
Derived from 'ankam' (battle) — Mother of Battle. ⇌ Derived from 'anka' (mark, brand) — Mother of the Mark (possibly a sacrificial mark).
Evidence for A: Her fierce, martial nature and association with warrior heroes.
Evidence for B: The practice of branding cattle or marking sacrificial animals.
Sanskritic Identity
Ankalamma is a local form of Parvati/Kali, the consort of Shiva. ⇌ Ankalamma is an independent, pre-Vedic Dravidian goddess, not a form of any Sanskritic deity.
Evidence for A: Modern attempts to Sanskritize her worship, some iconographic similarities with Kali, and the inclusion of her stories i
Evidence for B: Her non-Brahminical priesthood, her local origin stories, and her distinct, non-Vedic iconography and rituals.
The Practice of Animal Sacrifice
Animal sacrifice is an essential part of her worship, required to appease her wrath and maintain her power. ⇌ Animal sacrifice is a barbaric practice that should be abolished, and her worship should be reformed to be vegetarian (sattvic).
Evidence for A: Centuries of tradition and the belief that she is a fierce goddess who requires blood.
Evidence for B: Modern animal-rights activism, reformed Hindu movements, and some interpretations of scripture.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Ankalamma as the ancient, pre-Vedic 'Mother of the Village' — a cross between a fierce protector and a stern matriarch. She's not a distant queen on a throne; she's the tough, no-nonsense guardian of the neighborhood who will fight off any intruder but will also scold you if you don't clean up your act. She's the village's immune system, its defense against chaos, and its source of life. She's the goddess you'd call if you needed a wall built against an army of demons, or if your child had a fever. She's the original 'mama bear' deity, and her temple is the whole village, not just a building.
