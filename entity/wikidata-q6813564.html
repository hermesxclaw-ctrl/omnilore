Who They Are

Their primary domain is the Apsu, the cosmic freshwater ocean beneath the earth, associated with their master Enki. They are also connected to the city of Eridu, the cult center of Enki, and are said to have been active in the antediluvian era. Their realm is wisdom, magic, healing, and the arts of civilization. They are the architects of the sacred temple, the E-engura of Eridu.

Form

The Apkallu are most famously depicted in Assyrian art as men wearing a cloak that resembles a fish's body, with the fish head acting as a mitre or hood over their heads. Their own human face is visible, and the fish's tail drapes down their backs. They are often shown as bearded, with long hair, and sometimes carrying a bucket (banduddu) and a cone-shaped object (mullilu) used in ritual purification. In some texts, they are described as 'pure ones' with 'spotless' bodies. Berossus describes Oannes as having the whole body of a fish, but with a human head under the fish's head and human feet. A more fantastical description is given for the later sages (Ummânū), but the apkallu are generally anthropomorphic.

Powers

Transmission of Divine Knowledge (The 'Me') The Apkallu are the bearers of the 'me', the divine decrees that underpin civilization. They bring the knowledge of writing, law, architecture, and all crafts to humanity. This is a passive but profound power: they are the channel through which divine order is made manifest on earth. Sumerian and Akkadian texts, including the Eridu Genesis and the Erra Epic. This power is not for personal gain; it is a sacred duty. They are bound to the will of Enki and the divine assembly. The exact nature of the 'me' is debated, but their role as its transmitter is consistent.

Ritual Purification & Apotropaic Magic The apkallu are experts in rituals to cleanse people, places, and objects from ritual impurity and demonic influence. They are shown on reliefs performing purification with a bucket and cone. They are the ultimate anti-demon authorities, capable of warding off evil. Assyrian reliefs, ritual texts (e.g., 'namburbi' rituals), and incantation series. Their power is tied to the correct performance of ritual and the invocation of the gods, particularly Enki and Asalluhi (Marduk). In later periods, their role is often taken over by the human 'āšipu' (exorcist) priests, who claim to work in their tradition.

Wisdom & Counsel They are the ultimate sources of wise counsel, advising the antediluvian kings. Their wisdom is not just knowledge but the ability to make sound judgments that ensure the prosperity of the city and the kingdom. Mythological narratives and royal inscriptions that invoke them as models of wisdom. Their counsel is for the benefit of the community, not for personal ambition. None.

Creation of the Temple (E-engura) In the Erra Epic, they are described as the ones who built the temple of Eridu. This is not just physical construction but a cosmic act, establishing the link between heaven and earth. Erra Epic, Tablet I. This is a foundational, one-time act in the past. None.

Behavior & Habitat

The Apkallu are depicted as wise counselors, advisors to kings, and masters of ritual purification. They are the bearers of civilization's arts: writing, law, architecture, medicine, and agriculture. They are associated with the god Enki (Ea), the god of wisdom and fresh water, and are often said to be his offspring or servants. They act as intermediaries between the divine and human realms, but are not typically portrayed as rulers or warriors. They are the 'good' sages who establish the cosmic order on earth.

Accounts

The gods, in their wisdom, created seven apkallu, 'pure ones' who were born from the Apsu, the freshwater ocean. They were sent to Eridu, the city of Enki, to establish civilization. They were the architects, advisors, and sages who brought the arts of writing, law, and building to the antediluvian kings. They are the collective embodiment of antediluvian wisdom.

Adapa, the sage of Eridu and a priest of Enki, is a model apkallu. He breaks the wing of the South Wind for disturbing his fishing, angering the sky god Anu. Summoned to heaven, he is warned by Enki not to eat or drink anything offered to him. Anu, impressed by his wisdom, offers him the bread and water of eternal life, but Adapa, following Enki's warning, refuses. He returns to earth, having lost the chance at immortality, but having secured the favor of the gods. The story explains why humanity is mortal, but also elevates the apkallu as a figure of great wisdom.

The apkallu are the keepers of antediluvian wisdom. Before the great flood that wiped out most of humanity, they are the ones who possess the knowledge of civilization. In the Sumerian story, the king Ziusudra is warned by Enki (often via a dream or a wall). The apkallu, as a group, are associated with this antediluvian era. After the flood, they are said to have returned to the Apsu, and the link between gods and men is broken. Berossus's account of Oannes (a single apkallu) describes him emerging from the sea to teach humanity, and then returning, with the implication that later sages came to interpret his writings.

What We Don't Know

The primary gap is the lack of a single, complete, canonical list of the seven apkallu. The names vary between texts, and the full mythology of each individual sage is not preserved. The exact nature of the 'evil apkallu' is also poorly understood. The relationship between the apkallu and the later 'ummiânu' is clear in broad strokes but the details of the transition are not fully documented.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of the apkallu as the 'Operating System' for Mesopotamian civilization. They are not the hardware (the kings) or the software (the laws and rituals), but the original coders who wrote the foundational code, installed it, and then logged off. They are the ultimate 'tech support' from the divine realm, and their absence after the flood is why humans have to muddle through with manuals (the ummânū) instead of direct access to the source code.
