Who It Is

Long before the Greeks dreamed of Prometheus, the cities of Mesopotamia had a different answer for who gave us the spark of civilization: not a fire-stealing rebel, but a cadre of seven fish-cloaked sages who emerged from the primordial sea, taught humanity the arts of writing, law, and temple-building, and then vanished beneath the waves, leaving behind a legacy of wisdom that would outlast their own cities.

The Apkallu are depicted as wise counselors, advisors to kings, and masters of ritual purification. They are the bearers of civilization's arts: writing, law, architecture, medicine, and agriculture. They are associated with the god Enki (Ea), the god of wisdom and fresh water, and are often said to be his offspring or servants. They act as intermediaries between the divine and human realms, but are not typically portrayed as rulers or warriors. They are the 'good' sages who establish the cosmic order on earth.

Powers
Transmission of Divine Knowledge (The 'Me')

The Apkallu are the bearers of the 'me', the divine decrees that underpin civilization. They bring the knowledge of writing, law, architecture, and all crafts to humanity. This is a passive but profound power: they are the channel through which divine order is made manifest on earth.

Limit: This power is not for personal gain; it is a sacred duty. They are bound to the will of Enki and the divine assembly.

Ritual Purification & Apotropaic Magic

The apkallu are experts in rituals to cleanse people, places, and objects from ritual impurity and demonic influence. They are shown on reliefs performing purification with a bucket and cone. They are the ultimate anti-demon authorities, capable of warding off evil.

Limit: Their power is tied to the correct performance of ritual and the invocation of the gods, particularly Enki and Asalluhi (Marduk).

Wisdom & Counsel

They are the ultimate sources of wise counsel, advising the antediluvian kings. Their wisdom is not just knowledge but the ability to make sound judgments that ensure the prosperity of the city and the kingdom.

Limit: Their counsel is for the benefit of the community, not for personal ambition.

Creation of the Temple (E-engura)

In the Erra Epic, they are described as the ones who built the temple of Eridu. This is not just physical construction but a cosmic act, establishing the link between heaven and earth.

Limit: This is a foundational, one-time act in the past.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The Apkallu are most famously depicted in Assyrian art as men wearing a cloak that resembles a fish's body, with the fish head acting as a mitre or hood over their heads. Their own human face is visible, and the fish's tail drapes down their backs. They are often shown as bearded, with long hair, and sometimes carrying a bucket (banduddu) and a cone-shaped object (mullilu) used in ritual purification. Symbols — The fish-cloak or fish-garb is their primary symbol. They are also associated with the purification bucket and cone, symbols of their ritual role. The 'me' (divine decrees or powers of civilization) that they carry are a more abstract symbol of their authority. In some contexts, they are associated with the constellations, particularly those of the 'Great One' (possibly Orion).

Domains — Their primary domain is the Apsu, the cosmic freshwater ocean beneath the earth, associated with their master Enki. They are also connected to the city of Eridu, the cult center of Enki, and are said to have been active in the antediluvian era. Their realm is wisdom, magic, healing, and the arts of civilization. They are the architects of the sacred temple, the E-engura of Eridu.

Accounts I
The Seven Sages of EriduErra Epic (Tablet I), various Sumerian and Akkadian king lists and literary texts.

Mythological past (antediluvian era), but the texts are attested from the 2nd and 1st millennia BCE.

The gods, in their wisdom, created seven apkallu, 'pure ones' who were born from the Apsu, the freshwater ocean. They were sent to Eridu, the city of Enki, to establish civilization. They were the architects, advisors, and sages who brought the arts of writing, law, and building to the antediluvian kings. They are the collective embodiment of antediluvian wisdom. Their work established the foundation of Mesopotamian culture. After the flood, they returned to the Apsu, and their wisdom was only partially preserved. The later human sages (ummânū) were seen as their successors, but not their equals.

Accounts II
Adapa and the South WindThe Adapa Myth (Amarna Tablets, EA 356; also found in Assyrian and Babylonian versions).

Mythological past, attested from the 14th century BCE.

Adapa, the sage of Eridu and a priest of Enki, is a model apkallu. He breaks the wing of the South Wind for disturbing his fishing, angering the sky god Anu. Summoned to heaven, he is warned by Enki not to eat or drink anything offered to him. Anu, impressed by his wisdom, offers him the bread and water of eternal life, but Adapa, following Enki's warning, refuses. He returns to earth, having lost the chance at immortality, but having secured the favor of the gods. The story explains why humanity is mortal, but also elevates the apkallu as a figure of great wisdom. Adapa's choice defines human mortality. He becomes the archetypal wise man, but also a cautionary tale about the limits of divine knowledge.

The Apkallu and the FloodSumerian Flood Story (Eridu Genesis), Atrahasis Epic, Berossus' Babyloniaca.

Mythological past, attested from the 17th century BCE.

The apkallu are the keepers of antediluvian wisdom. Before the great flood that wiped out most of humanity, they are the ones who possess the knowledge of civilization. In the Sumerian story, the king Ziusudra is warned by Enki (often via a dream or a wall). The apkallu, as a group, are associated with this antediluvian era. After the flood, they are said to have returned to the Apsu, and the link between gods and men is broken. Berossus's account of Oannes (a single apkallu) describes him emerging from the sea to teach humanity, and then returning, with the implication that later sages came to interpret his writings. The flood is a watershed moment. The apkallu's return to the Apsu marks the end of direct divine-human dialogue. Human history begins without their direct presence.

Mythical vs. HistoricalThe apkallu are purely mythical beings, created by theologians to explain the origins of civilization.The apkallu are a distorted memory of historical priest-scholars from the Uruk period (4th millennium BCE) who pioneered writing and other arts.
The 'Evil' ApkalluThe 'evil apkallu' are fallen or corrupted versions of the original seven, who turned to evil.The 'evil apkallu' are a separate class of demonic beings, unrelated to the wise sages, who are merely given the same title to indicate their supernatural nature.

The apkallu were fish-men, like mermen. — While Oannes is described with a fish body, the standard iconography shows them as fully human men wearing a fish-cloak. They are not mermen, but sages in ritual garb.

The apkallu are the source of all human knowledge. — They are the source of civilization's arts (writing, law, agriculture, etc.) in myth, but they are not the source of all knowledge. They are the bringers of the 'me', which are more about social and ritual order than empirical facts.

The apkallu were the same as the 'Nephilim' or 'Watchers' of the Book of Enoch. — There are thematic similarities (wise beings who teach humanity before a flood), but there is no direct textual connection. The apkallu are Mesopotamian, and the Watchers are from Jewish apocalyptic literature. It is more likely that the Enochic tradition drew on Mesopotamian mythology, but they are not the same beings.
