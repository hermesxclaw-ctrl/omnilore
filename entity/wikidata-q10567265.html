The Approach

On a bridge in ancient China, an old man deliberately threw his shoe into the water—and thereby changed the course of an empire. This is the story of Huang Shigong, the Yellow Rock Old Man, whose gift of a mysterious book transformed a fugitive into the architect of the Han dynasty.

Content advisory. None
Identity

Huang Shigong

Pronunciation Hwahng Shur-gong (approximate Mandarin)

Known As

Yellow Rock Old ManWei Che (probable original name)Huangshi Gong

Etymology

Huang Shigong (黃石公) literally means 'Yellow Rock Old Man', from huang (yellow), shi (stone/rock), and gong (old man/sir). The name is derived from the yellow rock he claimed to be after death.

Cultural Ownership

Chinese, specifically associated with Taoist and folk religious traditions.

Disputed Identifications

Some traditions identify him with Wei Che, a minister of King Zhuangxiang of Qin, but this is not universally accepted. The name 'Huang Shigong' may be a title or epithet rather than a personal name.

Description

Appearance

No detailed physical description survives in the sources; he is typically depicted as an old man with a white beard, often in Taoist robes.

Symbols & Iconography

The yellow rock is his primary symbol, representing his posthumous form. He is also associated with the bridge (Yishui Bridge) and the book of strategy.

Behaviour & Personality

Depicted as a wise but testing elder, who uses unorthodox methods to assess character. He is patient, enigmatic, and ultimately benevolent, guiding worthy individuals.

Domains & Habitats

Associated with the Huanghua Cave at Mount Huang in Pizhou, and later with Mount Gucheng, where he promised to appear as a yellow rock.

Version Differences

In the Shiji, the old man is unnamed; later traditions identify him as Huang Shigong and sometimes as Wei Che. The book given is variously called the 'Art of War by Taigong', 'Six Secret Teachings', or 'Three Strategies of Huang Shigong'.

Stories & Myths

The Bridge Test

Source Shiji, Xianzhuan Shiyi, Journey to the WestCulture ChinesePeriod Qin-Han transition (c. 3rd century BCE)

Summary

Zhang Liang, a fugitive, encounters an old man on a bridge who throws his shoe down and orders Zhang to fetch it. Zhang does so, and the old man then asks Zhang to put it on. Despite anger, Zhang complies. The old man tests Zhang's patience twice more, arriving earlier each time. On the third meeting, Zhang arrives at midnight and passes the test. The old man gives him a book of military strategy, saying he can become a tutor to a ruler. He promises to meet Zhang again in 13 years at the foot of Mount Gucheng, where he will be a yellow rock.

Participants

Huang Shigong

Zhang Liang

Consequences

Zhang Liang becomes a brilliant strategist, helping Liu Bang found the Han dynasty.

Variants

In Journey to the West, the old man knocks off his shoe three times in one encounter, and the book is given at night.

The Yellow Rock

Source ShijiCulture ChinesePeriod Han dynasty

Summary

Thirteen years after the bridge meeting, Zhang Liang finds a yellow rock at Mount Gucheng as promised. He builds a shrine to worship it and is later buried with it.

Zhang Liang's Ascension

Source Xianzhuan ShiyiCulture Chinese TaoistPeriod Tang dynasty (compilation)

Summary

After helping establish the Han, Zhang Liang practices self-cultivation using Huang Shigong's book, refines his qi, and eventually ascends to heaven as Taixuan Tongzi, attending Taishang Laojun.

Contradictions & Disputes

Identity as Wei Che

Position A

Huang Shigong is Wei Che, a former minister of Qin.

Position A — evidence

Xianzhuan Shiyi provides this identification.

Position B

Huang Shigong is a legendary figure with no historical basis.

Position B — evidence

Shiji does not name him, and no historical records mention Wei Che.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars treat the Wei Che identification as legendary.

The book given to Zhang Liang

Position A

It was the 'Art of War by Taigong' (Six Secret Teachings).

Position A — evidence

Shiji says 'Art of War by Taigong'.

Position B

It was the 'Three Strategies of Huang Shigong'.

Position B — evidence

Later tradition attributes the Three Strategies to Huang Shigong.

Scholarly Consensus

The exact text is uncertain; both are possible.
