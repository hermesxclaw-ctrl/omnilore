Who It Is

In the deep, misty forests of ancient Germania, a priest in a woman's gown led a tribe in worshipping twin gods so old their names were lost to time. We know them only through the eyes of a Roman historian, who saw in them a mirror of his own beloved Dioscuri. But the Alcis were not Castor and Pollux — they were something wilder, a pair of youthful protectors whose very name may mean 'the holy ones', and whose worship involved a profound mystery we can only glimpse from the outside.

No specific behaviors are recorded. The focus of the cult was on their duality and youth. They were worshiped as a pair, emphasizing fraternal bonds and youthful vigor.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Alcis is likely derived from the Proto-Germanic root *alh- or *elh-, meaning 'to protect' or 'to defend', related to the Old English ealh (temple) and the Gothic alhs (temple). This suggests a possible original meaning of 'the protectors' or 'the temple gods'.

Powers
Divine Protection

As gods of a 'very ancient worship', they were likely invoked for protection of the tribe and its people. The etymology of their name suggests a protective function.

Limit: Not explicitly attested in any source.

Fraternal Bond/Duality

Their primary power, as described by Tacitus, is their existence as a divine pair. This duality was central to their worship, and they were not given individual attributes.

Limit: Tacitus notes that they were worshipped 'not with any of the usual attributes of power', indicating a unique form of worship.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Tacitus does not describe their physical appearance directly. However, the analogy with Castor and Pollux suggests they were imagined as young, vigorous male twins. The lack of 'usual attributes of power' (i.e., weapons or symbols of authority) is a key detail — they were worshipped as brothers, not as kings or warriors with specific paraphernalia.

Symbols — The primary symbol is the grove itself. The worship was conducted by a priest in women's dress, which is a significant symbolic element, suggesting a ritual crossing of gender boundaries or a connection to a specific form of ecstatic or chthonic worship. No other specific symbols are attested.

Domains — Their domain is primarily the sacred grove of the Nahanarvali, a place of 'very ancient worship'. They are associated with protection, fraternity, and possibly initiation rites for young men, though this last point is speculative.

Accounts I
The Cult of the Alcis in the Nahanarvali GroveTacitus, Germania, Chapter 43

1st century CE (attested), but the cult is described as 'very ancient'

Tacitus reports that among the Nahanarvali, a Suebian tribe, there is a sacred grove dedicated to a pair of young gods. The worship is led by a priest who dresses in women's clothing. Tacitus interprets these gods as the Roman Castor and Pollux (the Dioscuri), but notes that the worship is distinct, focusing on the brothers' youth and fraternal bond rather than on any martial or political power. The cult is a key piece of evidence for the existence of a Germanic divine twin tradition, and for the practice of gender-bending in Germanic priesthood.

The Alcis are the same as the Norse gods Freyr and Njörðr. — This is a speculative theory, not a fact. There is no direct evidence linking the Alcis to any specific Norse gods. The name 'Alcis' does not match any Norse god name.

The Alcis were a major, pan-Germanic cult. — They are attested only among the Nahanarvali, a single Suebian tribe. Their cult may have been highly localized.

The cross-dressing priest indicates a gay or transgender cult. — The practice is not understood. It may have been a ritual role, a shamanic technique, or a symbolic act. Applying modern sexual or gender identities to ancient practices is anachronistic.
