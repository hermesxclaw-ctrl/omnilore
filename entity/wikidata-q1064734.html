Who It Is

In the blazing mandalas of East Asian Buddhism, a figure coils like a serpent of nectar, dispensing the elixir of immortality with a wrathful grimace. This is Kuṇḍali, the Wisdom King who turns poison into ambrosia, a guardian whose very name whispers of the coiled power of life itself.

As a dharmapala, Kuṇḍali is fiercely protective of the Dharma and its practitioners. He destroys obstacles, subjugates demons, and converts beings through his power. Despite his wrath, his ultimate purpose is compassionate, as he bestows the nectar of enlightenment.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The Sanskrit name Kuṇḍali likely derives from 'kuṇḍala' meaning 'ring' or 'coil', possibly referring to a serpent or to the coiled nature of the deity. The epithet Amritakundalin combines 'amṛta' (immortal nectar) with 'kuṇḍalin' (coiled), indicating the bearer of the nectar of immortality.

Pronunciation — Sanskrit: Kuṇḍali (koon-DAH-lee); Amritakundalin (ahm-REE-tah-koon-DAH-lin). Chinese: Jūntúlì (joon-too-lee). Japanese: Gundari (goon-DAH-ree).

Aliases — Amritakundalin, Juntuli Mingwang, Gundari Myōō, Amṛtakuṇḍalin, Kundali

Ownership — East Asian Esoteric Buddhism, particularly the Shingon and Tendai schools in Japan, and Chinese Tang-dynasty esoteric traditions.

Dispensation of Amrita

Kuṇḍali grants the celestial nectar of immortality, which purifies and grants spiritual liberation.

Limit: Amrita is a metaphor for spiritual bliss and not physical immortality.

Destruction of Obstacles

He removes physical, mental, and spiritual obstacles for practitioners.

Limit: Not specified; typical of Wisdom Kings.

He conquers demons and negative influences, often depicted trampling them.

Limit: His power is bound by the Dharma and compassion.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Kuṇḍali is typically depicted as a wrathful deity with a dark blue or green body, multiple arms (usually four or eight), and a fierce expression. He may have a third eye and wears a skull crown or a crown of five wisdom buddhas. His ornaments include serpents, and he often holds a vajra, a lasso, and a kundika (water flask) or a vessel of amrita. In some forms, he tramples on demons or obstacles. He is often shown with flames surrounding his body

Symbols — The kundika (nectar vase) symbolizes the amrita he dispenses. The vajra represents indestructible wisdom. The lasso binds evil influences. Serpents adorn his body, linking to his name and to the transformation of poisonous forces. His wrathful form represents the destruction of ignorance and obstacles.

Domains — He resides in the southern direction (when associated with Ratnasambhava) or the north-west (when associated with Akashagarbha). He is often found in mandalas, particularly the Womb Realm mandala, and is invoked in rituals for purification and protection.

Accounts I
Role in the Five Wisdom KingsEsoteric Buddhist texts

Tang dynasty (China) and Heian period (Japan)

As one of the Five Wisdom Kings, Kuṇḍali is an emanation of Ratnasambhava, the Buddha of the south, and protects the southern direction. He embodies the wisdom of equanimity. Practitioners invoke him for purification and to overcome pride and attachment.

Accounts II
Association with AkashagarbhaEight Wisdom King system

Later esoteric transmission

In the Eight Wisdom Kings, Kuṇḍali is the manifestation of the Bodhisattva Akashagarbha and guards the north-west. He is invoked for space-related rituals and for removing obstacles in the north-west.

Association with AmitabhaTen Wisdom King system

Later esoteric traditions

In some traditions, Kuṇḍali is an emanation of Amitabha, the Buddha of the Western Paradise, and thus connected with boundless light and compassion. He is linked with rituals for rebirth in the Pure Land.
