The Approach

In the blazing mandalas of East Asian Buddhism, a figure coils like a serpent of nectar, dispensing the elixir of immortality with a wrathful grimace. This is Kuṇḍali, the Wisdom King who turns poison into ambrosia, a guardian whose very name whispers of the coiled power of life itself.

Content advisory. The deity is wrathful in appearance and associated with destruction of obstacles, but there is no explicit dark material in the traditional sources. Some modern New Age interpretations conflate this figure with the Hindu concept of Kundalini; readers should be aware of this distinction.
Identity

Kuṇḍali

Pronunciation Sanskrit: Kuṇḍali (koon-DAH-lee); Amritakundalin (ahm-REE-tah-koon-DAH-lin). Chinese: Jūntúlì (joon-too-lee). Japanese: Gundari (goon-DAH-ree).

Known As

AmritakundalinJuntuli MingwangGundari MyōōAmṛtakuṇḍalinKundali

Etymology

The Sanskrit name Kuṇḍali likely derives from 'kuṇḍala' meaning 'ring' or 'coil', possibly referring to a serpent or to the coiled nature of the deity. The epithet Amritakundalin combines 'amṛta' (immortal nectar) with 'kuṇḍalin' (coiled), indicating the bearer of the nectar of immortality.

Cultural Ownership

East Asian Esoteric Buddhism, particularly the Shingon and Tendai schools in Japan, and Chinese Tang-dynasty esoteric traditions.

Disputed Identifications

There is scholarly debate over whether Kuṇḍali is identical with the Hindu deity Kuṇḍalī or related to the serpent goddess. Some modern interpretations conflate him with the Kundalini energy, but this is not supported by traditional Buddhist texts.

Description

Appearance

Kuṇḍali is typically depicted as a wrathful deity with a dark blue or green body, multiple arms (usually four or eight), and a fierce expression. He may have a third eye and wears a skull crown or a crown of five wisdom buddhas. His ornaments include serpents, and he often holds a vajra, a lasso, and a kundika (water flask) or a vessel of amrita. In some forms, he tramples on demons or obstacles. He is often shown with flames surrounding his body.

Symbols & Iconography

The kundika (nectar vase) symbolizes the amrita he dispenses. The vajra represents indestructible wisdom. The lasso binds evil influences. Serpents adorn his body, linking to his name and to the transformation of poisonous forces. His wrathful form represents the destruction of ignorance and obstacles.

Behaviour & Personality

As a dharmapala, Kuṇḍali is fiercely protective of the Dharma and its practitioners. He destroys obstacles, subjugates demons, and converts beings through his power. Despite his wrath, his ultimate purpose is compassionate, as he bestows the nectar of enlightenment.

Domains & Habitats

He resides in the southern direction (when associated with Ratnasambhava) or the north-west (when associated with Akashagarbha). He is often found in mandalas, particularly the Womb Realm mandala, and is invoked in rituals for purification and protection.

Version Differences

In the Five Wisdom Kings, he is associated with Ratnasambhava; in the Eight Wisdom Kings, with Akashagarbha; in the Ten Wisdom Kings, with Amitabha. These associations may reflect different ritual lineages and textual traditions. Iconography may vary slightly depending on the tradition and specific text.

Role in the Five Wisdom Kings

Source Esoteric Buddhist textsCulture East Asian BuddhismPeriod Tang dynasty (China) and Heian period (Japan)

Summary

As one of the Five Wisdom Kings, Kuṇḍali is an emanation of Ratnasambhava, the Buddha of the south, and protects the southern direction. He embodies the wisdom of equanimity.

Kuṇḍali
