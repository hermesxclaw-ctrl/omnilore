Who It Is

In the vast pantheon of Mahayana Buddhism, Samantabhadra stands as the embodiment of the bodhisattva's vow—the one who practices the Dharma with unwavering dedication. Known in Japan as Fugen Bosatsu, he is the guardian of practitioners, the patron of the Lotus Sutra, and a figure whose very name means 'All Good' or 'Always Auspicious.' Yet his identity branches across traditions, from a celestial bodhisattva to a primordial Buddha, revealing a complex tapestry of doctrine and devotion.

Samantabhadra embodies the ideal of the bodhisattva who actively engages in the world to benefit all beings. He is associated with meditation, practice, and the ten great vows that guide a bodhisattva's conduct.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Sanskrit 'samanta' (all, universal) and 'bhadra' (good, auspicious). The name is often translated as 'Universal Worthy' or 'All Good'.

Pronunciation — sah-mahn-tah-BHAH-drah

Aliases — Fugen Bosatsu, Kuntu Zangpo, Puxian, Samantabhadra Bodhisattva, Universal Worthy, All Good

Ownership — Pan-Buddhist (Mahayana), with significant cults in East Asia (China, Japan, Korea) and distinct Tibetan interpretation.

Names are cult layers, not one fixed label.

Ten Great Vows

Samantabhadra is known for his ten vows, which outline the bodhisattva's path of practice, including vowing to worship all Buddhas, praise all Buddhas, and make extensive offerings.

Limit: These are aspirational vows, not supernatural powers.

Power of Meditation

As a bodhisattva of meditation, he is believed to have mastery over deep meditative states, enabling him to perceive reality as it is.

Limit: Not explicitly described as a 'power' in texts; rather a quality.

Protection of the Lotus Sutra

In the Lotus Sutra, Samantabhadra promises to protect those who uphold the sutra, shielding them from harm and ensuring their practice flourishes.

Limit: Conditional on the devotee's sincere practice.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Typically depicted as a bodhisattva with a serene expression, wearing princely ornaments and robes. He is often shown seated on a white elephant with six tusks, symbolizing the overcoming of the six sense organs. In some representations, he holds a lotus flower or a sutra. In Tibetan art, Samantabhadra (Kuntu Zangpo) is depicted as a naked blue Buddha in union with his consort, representing the primordial state.

Symbols — White elephant with six tusks (symbol of wisdom and compassion), lotus, jewel, and sometimes a sword or a book. In Japan, he is often shown with hands in a gesture of meditation or holding a lotus.

Domains — He is a celestial bodhisattva residing in the Vairocana Buddha's Pure Land, but he is also present wherever the Dharma is practiced.

Accounts I
Samantabhadra's Ten VowsAvatamsaka Sutra

c. 3rd century CE

Samantabhadra expounds ten great vows that a bodhisattva should undertake, including honoring all Buddhas, praising them, making offerings, confessing misdeeds, rejoicing in others' merits, and dedicating all merits to all beings. These vows became a foundational practice in East Asian Buddhism, especially in the Huayan and Tiantai schools.

Accounts II
Encouragement of SamantabhadraLotus Sutra

c. 1st century BCE - 2nd century CE

Samantabhadra arrives from the east with a great multitude to protect the Lotus Sutra and its practitioners. He describes the practices that will lead to rebirth in the Pure Land and promises to appear before those who practice the sutra. This chapter established Samantabhadra as a protector of the Lotus Sutra, leading to his prominence in Nichiren Buddhism.
