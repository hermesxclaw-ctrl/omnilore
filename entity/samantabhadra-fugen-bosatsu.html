Samantabhadra

Known As

Etymology

From Sanskrit 'samanta' (all, universal) and 'bhadra' (good, auspicious). The name is often translated as 'Universal Worthy' or 'All Good'.

Cultural Ownership

Pan-Buddhist (Mahayana), with significant cults in East Asia (China, Japan, Korea) and distinct Tibetan interpretation.

Disputed Identifications

In Tibetan Buddhism, the name Samantabhadra (Kuntu Zangpo) refers to a primordial Buddha, not a bodhisattva. Some scholars argue these are distinct figures, while others see a connection. Additionally, there is a historical Jain monk named Samantabhadra, causing potential confusion.

Archive Dossier — Approach

In the vast pantheon of Mahayana Buddhism, Samantabhadra stands as the embodiment of the bodhisattva's vow—the one who practices the Dharma with unwavering dedication. Known in Japan as Fugen Bosatsu, he is the guardian of practitioners, the patron of the Lotus Sutra, and a figure whose very name means 'All Good' or 'Always Auspicious.' Yet his identity branches across traditions, from a celestial bodhisattva to a primordial Buddha, revealing a complex tapestry of doctrine and devotion.

Samantabhadra is the bodhisattva of universal virtue, right practice, and the protector of the Buddha's teaching — the embodiment of the perfection of practice and the active commitment to benefiting all beings. He is one of the most prominent bodhisattvas in Mahayana Buddhism, often depicted alongside the Buddha Śākyamuni and Mañjuśrī: Mañjuśrī on the left (wisdom), and Samantabhadra on the right (practice).

Who It Is

Samantabhadra is the bodhisattva of universal virtue, right practice, and the protector of the Buddha's teaching — the embodiment of the perfection of practice and the active commitment to benefiting all beings. He is one of the most prominent bodhisattvas in Mahayana Buddhism, often depicted alongside the Buddha Śākyamuni and Mañjuśrī: Mañjuśrī on the left (wisdom), and Samantabhadra on the right (practice). He is usually shown riding a six-tusked white elephant, symbolizing the victory of wisdom over defilements (the six tusks representing the six perfections). Samantabhadra is the author of the 'Vow of Beneficial Action' in the Avatamsaka Sutra — ten great vows that define the bodhisattva path, including honoring all Buddhas, praising all Tathāgatas, practicing the Dharma, and dedicating merit to all beings. He is the patron of Mount Emei, one of China's four sacred Buddhist mountains. In Vajrayana, Samantabhadra appears as the primordial Buddha (Samantabhadra/Adibuddha) in the Nyingma tradition's Dzogchen teachings — the naked, blue primordial Buddha who represents the pristine, uncontrived nature of mind itself. In East Asian Buddhism, he is the protector of the Lotus Sutra.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

Typically depicted as a bodhisattva with a serene expression, wearing princely ornaments and robes. He is often shown seated on a white elephant with six tusks, symbolizing the overcoming of the six sense organs. In some representations, he holds a lotus flower or a sutra. In Tibetan art, Samantabhadra (Kuntu Zangpo) is depicted as a naked blue Buddha in union with his consort, representing the primordial state.

Symbols & Iconography

White elephant with six tusks (symbol of wisdom and compassion), lotus, jewel, and sometimes a sword or a book. In Japan, he is often shown with hands in a gesture of meditation or holding a lotus.

Behaviour & Personality

Samantabhadra embodies the ideal of the bodhisattva who actively engages in the world to benefit all beings. He is associated with meditation, practice, and the ten great vows that guide a bodhisattva's conduct.

Domains & Habitats

He is a celestial bodhisattva residing in the Vairocana Buddha's Pure Land, but he is also present wherever the Dharma is practiced.

Version Differences

In the Avatamsaka tradition, he is a central figure, while in the Lotus Sutra he is a protector of the sutra. In Tibetan Buddhism, the name refers to a different, primordial Buddha. These are distinct figures with different roles and iconography.

Origins

Samantabhadra appears in the Lotus Sutra and the Avatamsaka Sutra (c. 1st-3rd centuries CE). He is the patron of Mount Emei, and his image is often paired with Manjushri in temples. In Tibetan Buddhism, he also exists as the primordial Buddha (Kuntuzangpo), representing the dharmakaya.

Archive Dossier — Powers

Ten Great Vows

Source Version Avatamsaka Sutra

Description

Samantabhadra is known for his ten vows, which outline the bodhisattva's path of practice, including vowing to worship all Buddhas, praise all Buddhas, and make extensive offerings.

Limitations

These are aspirational vows, not supernatural powers.

Contradictions

None known.

Power of Meditation

Source Version General Mahayana

Description

As a bodhisattva of meditation, he is believed to have mastery over deep meditative states, enabling him to perceive reality as it is.

Limitations

Not explicitly described as a 'power' in texts; rather a quality.

Contradictions

None.

Protection of the Lotus Sutra

Source Version Lotus Sutra

Description

In the Lotus Sutra, Samantabhadra promises to protect those who uphold the sutra, shielding them from harm and ensuring their practice flourishes.

Limitations

Conditional on the devotee's sincere practice.

Contradictions

None.

Archive Dossier — Relationships

Shakyamuni Buddha

Type attendantSource Various Mahayana texts

Description

Samantabhadra is often depicted as the right-hand attendant of Shakyamuni, with Manjushri on the left, representing practice and wisdom respectively.

Version Note

Common in East Asian iconography.

Conflicting Accounts

In some contexts, he is associated with Vairocana Buddha instead.

Manjushri

Type companionSource Avatamsaka Sutra

Description

Manjushri represents wisdom, while Samantabhadra represents practice; together they embody the two aspects of enlightenment.

Version Note

Often depicted together.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Vairocana Buddha

Type associateSource Avatamsaka Sutra

Description

In the Avatamsaka tradition, Samantabhadra is a central figure in the assembly of Vairocana, the cosmic Buddha.

Version Note

In some Chinese Buddhist schools, the triad is Vairocana with Manjushri and Samantabhadra.

Conflicting Accounts

None.
