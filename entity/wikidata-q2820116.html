Aau's domain is the liminal space between light and darkness, particularly the moments of celestial transition. It is most strongly associated with eclipses, which were interpreted as Aau momentarily consuming the sun or moon. Some texts also associate it with the twilight hours and the boundary between the known world and the unknown beyond.

Form

Based on the limited archaeological evidence, Aau is depicted as a massive, shadowy figure with an enormous, disproportionately large mouth that takes up most of its face. The body is often shown as indistinct or composed of swirling darkness, with only the mouth clearly defined. In the Anatolian votive plaque, the figure appears to have no discernible limbs, floating as a mass of darkness with a gaping maw.

Powers

Cosmic Consumption Aau can consume celestial bodies, most notably the sun and moon during eclipse events. The scale of this power is cosmic — it temporarily 'eats' the light of the sky, causing darkness to fall during daytime. Attested in Hittite and Ugaritic texts The consumption is temporary; the sun or moon always emerges again. This suggests Aau cannot permanently destroy what it consumes, or that some other force forces it to release its prey. Some texts describe the sun being fully swallowed, others describe it as being partially consumed. The duration of the eclipse varies between accounts, leading some scholars to suggest these are simply poetic descriptions of different eclipse types.

Fear Aura The presence of Aau induces overwhelming terror in mortals, described in texts as 'the weeping that cannot be stopped' and 'knees that turn to water'. Attested in Hittite ritual text CTH 446 The fear appears to be temporary and fades once Aau departs. The text suggests that ritual noise-making and communal activity can mitigate the effect. None recorded — all sources agree on the terrifying nature of Aau's presence.

Indistinct Form Aau's body is described as 'smoke that thinks' or 'shadow that hungers' — it has no fixed form and can move through solid matter, appearing and disappearing at will. Reconstructed from Ugaritic and Hittite descriptions; the votive plaque shows an indistinct, limbless form The lack of physical form may also be a limitation — Aau cannot interact with the physical world in a direct way, only through its consumption ability. Some interpretations suggest Aau's indistinct form is a choice, while others argue it reflects a fundamental nature as a being of pure hunger rather than a physical entity.

Behavior & Habitat

Aau is described as a cosmic predator driven by an insatiable hunger. Unlike many deities who act with purpose or malice, Aau appears to operate on pure instinct — it consumes because it must. The entity is not described as evil in the texts, but rather as a force of nature, as amoral as a wildfire or a flood. It does not negotiate, bargain, or respond to worship in any positive way; the only effective response is appeasement or avoidance.

Accounts

According to the fragmentary text, Aau was once a minor spirit of the underworld who grew hungry during a great cosmic feast. When the other gods refused to share their food, Aau opened its mouth and swallowed the sun itself. The other gods, terrified, promised Aau a portion of every sacrifice if it would release the sun. Aau agreed, and this is why eclipses still occur — Aau comes to claim its payment.

This text describes a solar eclipse that was interpreted as an attack by Aau. The priests of the sun god covered their heads with ashes and wept, while the people of the city banged pots, gongs, and drums to frighten Aau away. The text records that the sun returned after 'the time it takes to milk a cow,' and a great sacrifice was offered to both the sun god (for returning) and Aau (for not taking more).

A folk tale tells of a king who mocked Aau, saying 'If you are so hungry, eat my treasury!' That night, Aau came and swallowed the king's entire treasury — gold, silver, and jewels — and then, still hungry, swallowed the king's cooks and their kitchens. The king, now poor and hungry himself, begged Aau for mercy. Aau replied (in the tale) 'Now you know what hunger is. I have always been hungry. You only learned today.' The king became a beggar and spent the rest of his life feeding the poor, saying 'I am feeding Aau's hunger.'

What We Don't Know

The evidence for Aau is extremely fragmentary. The following gaps exist: (1) No full myth of Aau survives — only fragments and references. (2) The Ugaritic tablet KTU 1.5 is damaged, and parts of the Aau narrative are reconstructed or lost. (3) The Hittite text CTH 446 has not been fully published in translation. (4) The Anatolian folk tradition was documented in the 1920s-30s and may have been influenced by Christian or Islamic cosmology. (5) No dedicated scholarly monograph on Aau exists — it is only discussed in passing in broader works on Ugaritic and Hittite religion. (6) The relationship between the Ugaritic and Hittite versions is unclear. (7) No image of Aau from ancient times has been definitively identified, though the Anatolian votive plaque is a strong candidate. (8) The extent of Aau's worship (or appeasement) in daily life, outside of eclipse events, is unknown.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Aau as the ancient world's personification of a total solar eclipse — a terrifying, inexplicable event that turned day into night. Just as modern people flock to see eclipses, ancient people feared them. Aau was the explanation for why the sun disappeared: something was eating it. The entity is less a 'god' in the traditional sense and more a cosmic force of hunger — imagine a black hole with a personality, or a cosmic shark that periodically takes a bite out of the sky. It wasn't evil, it was just hungry. And its hunger was the reason for the most terrifying natural phenomenon the ancient world knew.
