Who It Is

Mēness is the moon god of Baltic mythology, husband of Saulė (the sun) and father of Auseklis (the Morning Star) and the Indrāns (lesser stars) in some traditions. He is depicted as a knight in silver armor riding a white horse, or as a handsome young man who drives a chariot across the night sky. Mēness's defining characteristic in the dainas is his unfaithfulness to Saulė: he falls in love with Auseklis (the Morning Star, who is sometimes his daughter in other traditions) or with the goddess Laima, causing Saulė to leave him. This mythic cycle explains the phases of the moon — Mēness waxes when with Saulė, wanes when separated, and disappears altogether (new moon) in rejection. Mēness is associated with water, tides, and the reckoning of time. In Latvian tradition, the new moon (jauns mēness) is a time for new beginnings, divination, and healing rituals. Soldiers would pray to Mēness for success in battle, and farmers would plant crops according to his phase. He is a melancholic figure — the eternal wanderer who seeks his beloved across the sky, never quite reunited. The Lithuanian expression 'mėnulis pasikorė' ('the moon has hanged itself') is used for the new moon, reflecting his tragic narrative.

The Approach

In the Baltic night sky, a silver chariot rides across the heavens, chased by the sun's fiery steeds. This is Mēness, the moon god of Latvian mythology, a divine wanderer whose light guards the dead and whose courtship with Saule shapes the celestial dance. But beyond the romantic tales, Mēness is a complex figure—a warrior, a healer, and a keeper of secrets, whose myths reveal the ancient Latvian understanding of the cosmos, life, and death.

Etymology

The name Mēness derives from the Proto-Indo-European root *mēns- meaning 'moon' or 'month', cognate with Latin mēnsis and English 'month'. It is related to the Lithuanian Mėnuo and the Slavic Měsęcь.

Cultural Ownership

Baltic peoples, primarily Latvians and Lithuanians, with shared origins in Proto-Indo-European mythology.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars have attempted to equate Mēness with the Greek Selene or Roman Luna, but these are superficial parallels. More significant is the debate over whether Mēness is a distinct deity or an aspect of Dievs (God). Dievturi, a Latvian neopagan movement, claims ancient Latvians were monotheistic, seeing all deities as aspects of one God, which would make Mēness a manifestation of Dievs. However, this view is not universally accepted by scholars.

Description

Appearance

Mēness is typically depicted as a handsome young man riding a chariot across the night sky. He is associated with silver and white light. In some dainas, he is described as wearing a silver cloak or crown. His chariot is often drawn by horses, though the color varies (gray, white, or black).

Symbols & Iconography

The moon itself is his primary symbol. He is also associated with the morning star (Auseklis) and the constellation of the Pleiades. In Latvian folk art, the moon is often depicted as a crescent or full disc, sometimes with a face.

Behaviour & Personality

Mēness is often portrayed as a lover and a warrior. He is brave and strong, but also sometimes foolish or impulsive, as seen in his abduction of Saule. He is also a protector, especially of travelers and soldiers. In some songs, he is a wise counselor.

Domains & Habitats

His domain is the night sky, but he is also connected to the earth, particularly bodies of water and forests. He is linked to the realm of the dead (Aizsaule), as he travels there during the day. He is also associated with the world tree (Austras koks) and the cosmic mountain.

Version Differences

In Latvian tradition, Mēness is a male god. In Lithuanian tradition, Mėnuo is also male. However, in some other Indo-European traditions, the moon is female. There are variations in his relationship with Saule: sometimes they are married, sometimes they are siblings, and sometimes they are enemies. The cause of their separation also varies: sometimes it is due to Pērkons (Thunder), sometimes due to Mēness's infidelity with the Morning Star (Auseklis).

The moon's phases are associated with healing and fertility. Mēness is called upon in folk charms for health and growth.

His healing power is strongest during the waxing moon.

Contradictions

Some folk practices attribute healing to the moon itself, not specifically to Mēness.

Guardian of the Dead

As the moon travels through the realm of the dead during the day, he is seen as a guide and protector of souls.

Limitations

His role is passive; he does not actively ferry souls but accompanies them.

Contradictions

Saule is also associated with carrying souls to the afterlife, leading to overlapping functions.

Stories & Myths

The Wedding of Mēness and Saule

Source Latvian dainasCulture LatvianPeriod Pre-Christian, recorded in 19th century

Summary

Mēness and Saule (the Sun) are married. They have a daughter, but Mēness is unfaithful with Auseklis (the Morning Star). Saule discovers the affair and divorces him. As punishment, Mēness is condemned to shine only at night, while Saule shines during the day. They are separated forever.

Participants

Mēness

Saule

Auseklis

Consequences

The separation of day and night; the phases of the moon are explained as Mēness's sorrowful wandering.

Variants

In some versions, Mēness and Saule are siblings, and the affair is with the daughter. In others, Pērkons (Thunder) is the one who strikes the tree and causes the separation.

Mēness and the World Tree

Source Latvian dainasCulture LatvianPeriod Pre-Christian

Summary

Mēness is often depicted near the Austras koks (tree of dawn), the cosmic tree. He and the other celestial deities meet there. In some songs, the tree is the site of Saule's abduction, where Mēness and his companions plot.

Participants

Saule

Consequences

The tree is a central axis of the cosmos, and the deities' actions there affect the world.

Variants

The tree is sometimes described as a birch, oak, or even a pillar.

Abduction of Saule

Source Latvian dainas

Description

In some myths, Mēness is involved in the abduction of Saule, either as the abductor or as a participant. This reflects a common Indo-European motif of the sun being taken by the moon.

Scholarly Context

Scholars interpret this as a myth explaining the eclipse or the separation of day and night.

Infidelity and Divorce

Source Latvian dainas

Description

Mēness's affair with Auseklis and the subsequent divorce from Saule is a prominent theme. It is used to explain the phases of the moon and the alternation of day and night.

Scholarly Context

Some scholars see this as a reflection of social norms and the importance of marital fidelity in ancient Baltic society.

Contradictions & Disputes

Gender of the Moon

Position A

Mēness is male (Latvian and Lithuanian tradition).

Position A — evidence

Latvian dainas refer to Mēness with masculine pronouns.

Position B

In some other Indo-European traditions, the moon is female (e.g., Greek Selene).

Position B — evidence

Comparative mythology suggests a possible earlier female moon deity in some cultures.

Scholarly Consensus

The Baltic tradition is consistently male, but the possibility of a pre-Indo-European substrate is debated.

Relationship with Saule

Position A

Mēness and Saule are spouses.

Position A — evidence

Many dainas describe their wedding.

Position B

They are siblings.

Position B — evidence

Some songs refer to them as brother and sister.

Scholarly Consensus

The spouse version is more common, but the sibling version may be older or a regional variant.

Role in Afterlife

Position A

Saule carries souls to the afterlife.

Position A — evidence

Dainas mention Saule crossing the sea with souls.

Position B

Mēness guides the dead.

Position B — evidence

The moon's nightly journey is associated with the realm of the dead.

Scholarly Consensus

Both deities have chthonic aspects, but their exact roles are not fully understood.

Research Scope

This dossier focuses on the Latvian moon god Mēness (also known as Mėnuo in Lithuanian), drawing primarily from Latvian folklore, particularly the dainas (folk songs), and scholarly interpretations. It covers his identity, roles, relationships, stories, and cultural significance, with attention to regional variations and historical context. The research is based on available English-language sources and may have limitations in accessing original Latvian texts.
