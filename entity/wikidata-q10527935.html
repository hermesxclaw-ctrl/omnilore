The Approach

Before the Indus was mapped, before the subcontinent's rivers were bound by treaties and dams, the Hydaspes thundered through Greek imagination as a titan-born god, a river whose name was carved into history by the hooves of Alexander's cavalry and the blood of a legendary battle. Today, the same waters are known as the Jhelum, flowing through Kashmir and Punjab, but the ancient deity still lingers in the currents, a forgotten divine witness to conquest and change.

Content advisory. This entry discusses ancient warfare, including the Battle of the Hydaspes, which involved significant casualties. It also references colonial-era interpretations and modern geopolitical disputes over water resources, which may be sensitive for some readers.
Identity

Hydaspes

Pronunciation hy-DAS-peez (Greek: Ὑδάσπης)

Known As

Etymology

The name 'Hydaspes' is the Greek rendering of the Old Persian 'Hydaspēs', likely derived from the Sanskrit 'Vitasta'. The Sanskrit name is explained in the Nilamata Purana as originating from the goddess Parvati, who was measured (vitasti) by Shiva's spear. The modern name 'Jhelum' is said to derive from 'Jal' (water) and 'Ham' (snow), referring to its snow-fed source.

Cultural Ownership

The Hydaspes is primarily a figure in ancient Greek mythology and historiography, associated with Alexander the Great's campaign in India. However, the river itself is sacred in Hindu tradition as the goddess Vitasta, and it holds cultural significance in Kashmir and Punjab. The modern river is shared between India and Pakistan, with water rights allocated by the Indus Waters Treaty.

Disputed Identifications

The identification of the Hydaspes with the modern Jhelum is widely accepted, but some scholars have debated whether the ancient river might correspond to another Punjab river. The Greek god Hydaspes, son of Thaumas and Electra, is distinct from the river, though ancient sources sometimes conflate the two. The exact location of the Battle of the Hydaspes remains uncertain, with several proposed sites along the Jhelum.

Description

Appearance

The Hydaspes is described as a powerful, swift river, often personified as a vigorous male deity. In Greek art, river gods were typically depicted as bearded men reclining on urns, with horns and reeds, symbolizing their watery nature. The modern Jhelum is a large river, about 725 km long, with a significant flow, especially during the monsoon season.

Symbols & Iconography

In Greek iconography, river gods like Hydaspes are associated with water, fertility, and boundaries. They often appear with a cornucopia, reeds, or a water urn. The Hydaspes is also linked to the horse, as it was the site of Alexander's crossing and the naming of Bucephala, and to the war-elephant, due to the battle.

Behaviour & Personality

As a deity, Hydaspes is not extensively characterized in Greek myth. He is a divine personification of the river, often invoked in poetry and myth as a witness to events. In Nonnus, he is depicted as a god who rejoices in the river's course and is associated with the cosmic order. In Hindu tradition, the river as Vitasta is a goddess who is believed to purify and bless the land.

Domains & Habitats

The Hydaspes flows through the Kashmir Valley, past Srinagar, and through the Punjab plains of Pakistan. It is a tributary of the Chenab River, ultimately joining the Indus system. The river's course has changed over time, and it is navigable for about 110 km from Khanabal to Baramulla.

Version Differences

The Greek Hydaspes is a male deity, son of Thaumas and Electra, while the Hindu Vitasta is a female goddess, an incarnation of Parvati. The Greek god is a personification of a foreign river, while the Hindu goddess is a sacred, purifying force. The modern river is a physical entity with geopolitical significance, distinct from its mythological identities.

Stories & Myths

The Birth of Vitasta

Source Nilamata PuranaCulture HinduPeriod Ancient

Summary

The sage Kashyapa requested Parvati to purify Kashmir from the evil pishachas. Parvati took the form of a river in the netherworld. Shiva struck his spear near the abode of Nila, creating a ditch through which the river emerged, and named her Vitasta.

Participants

Parvati

Shiva

Kashyapa

Nila

Consequences

The river Vitasta flows through Kashmir, purifying the land and its people.

Variants

Some versions say the river was created by Shiva's trident, while others attribute it to Parvati's own will.

The Battle of the Hydaspes

Source Arrian, Anabasis of AlexanderCulture GreekPeriod 326 BCE

Summary

Alexander the Great crossed the Hydaspes during the monsoon and defeated King Porus in a fierce battle. The river was in flood, making the crossing difficult and the battle costly.

Participants

Alexander the Great

Porus

Macedonian army

Indian army

Consequences

Alexander founded two cities, Nikaia and Bucephala, in honor of the victory. The battle marked the easternmost extent of Alexander's conquests.

Variants

Later historians like Curtius and Diodorus provide differing details on the battle tactics and casualties.

Battle casualties

Source Arrian, Anabasis

Description

The Battle of the Hydaspes was one of Alexander's costliest battles, with thousands of Indian and Macedonian soldiers killed.

Scholarly Context

Ancient sources give varying casualty figures; modern historians estimate total deaths in the tens of thousands.

Water disputes

Source Indus Waters Treaty, news reports

Description

The Jhelum River is a source of tension between India and Pakistan, with disputes over hydropower projects and water allocation under the Indus Waters Treaty.

Scholarly Context

The treaty allocates the Jhelum's waters to Pakistan, but India's upstream projects have raised concerns.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

Further research is needed on the archaeological identification of the battle site and on the reception of the Hydaspes in Indian literature. Access to primary Sanskrit texts in original is limited. The exact etymology of 'Hydaspes' remains uncertain. Modern geopolitical implications require up-to-date sources.

Research Scope

This research focuses on the ancient Greek and Indian conceptions of the Hydaspes River (modern Jhelum), its mythological identity, historical significance, and modern reception. Primary sources include Greek historians (Arrian, Curtius, Diodorus), the epic poem Dionysiaca by Nonnus, and Hindu puranic texts. Secondary sources include academic works on Alexander's campaigns and the geography of the Punjab. The research is limited by the availability of primary texts in translation and the inherent ambiguity of ancient geographical identifications.
