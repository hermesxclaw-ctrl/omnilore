Who It Is

For two thousand years the Magra has been a line drawn in water — Romans called it the Macra and made it the hard eastern edge of Liguria, medieval pilgrims forded it on the Via Francigena beneath the castles of the Malaspina, and today its 62 kilometres still cut a green, flood-prone trench between Tuscany and Liguria before spilling into the Ligurian Sea at Bocca di Magra.

Hydrological regime is pluvio-nival Apennine: torrential autumn-winter floods and severe summer low flows (almost wadable). Mean annual discharge at the mouth ~30-40 m3/s, but peak flood discharges exceed 2,500-3,000 m3/s (2011 flood ~3,400 m3/s at Aulla). Highly erosive and depositional, historically shifting its bed across the Santo Stefano-Sarzana plain. Behaviour is described by hydrologists as 'torrentizio' - flashy, with rapid rise times (6-9 hours from rainfall to peak). Human personality ascribed in local folklore and literature is liminal and melancholic - a frontier rive

Powers
Torrential flood power

Capacity for extreme, rapid flooding due to steep Apennine catchment and impermeable flysch geology. Historic floods repeatedly destroyed Pontremoli, Aulla and Santo Stefano di Magra.

Limit: Flood peaks are short-lived (12-24h); summer baseflow can drop below 5 m3/s, limiting sustained power. Mitigated by modern levees but not eliminated.

Sediment transport and plain-building

Transports large bedload of sand and gravel, building the Sarzana plain and the Bocca di Magra delta. Historically maintained beaches at Marinella di Sarzana.

Limit: 20th-century gravel mining (up to 2 million m3/year in 1970s) caused incision of 2-4m, coastal retreat, and loss of sediment supply; now regulated.

Ecological corridor

Connects Apennine headwaters to Mediterranean estuary, supporting migratory fish (eel Anguilla anguilla, historically sturgeon Acipenser naccarii) and bird migration along the Tyrrhenian flyway.

Limit: Fragmented by weirs, embankments and pollution; eel and sturgeon populations collapsed in late 20th c.

Boundary and passage function

For two millennia has functioned as a political and cultural boundary (Liguria/Etruria, Tuscany/Liguria, Diocese of Luni vs others) and simultaneously as a passage corridor (Via Francigena, Via Aurelia, modern A15 Autostrada).

Limit: Boundary status abolished administratively in 1923 creation of province of La Spezia; remains cultural.

Form & Signs

Appearance — A medium-sized Apennine river, 62 km long, with a steep upper course and a broad, braided lower plain. Upper Magra (Pontremoli to Aulla) flows in a narrow, gravel-bed valley flanked by chestnut and beech forest, with clear, fast water and extensive alluvial bars. In medieval Lunigiana, symbol of the Malaspina lordship and of the Via Francigena crossing. Modern iconography is naturalistic: herons, kingfishers and willows in park signage; the collapsed Caprigliola bridge (2020) became a national symbol of infrastructure fragility during COVID-19 lockdown. Accounts I
The Macra as the Edge of LiguriaStrabo, Geographica 5.1.2; Pliny, Naturalis Historia 3.48; Pomponius Mela 2.4.72

1st c. BCE - 1st c. CE

Greco-Roman geographers define the Macra (Magra) as the eastern limit of Liguria and the western limit of Etruria. Strabo places the important port of Luna (Luni) just west of the mouth, and Pliny states explicitly 'Macra, Liguriae finis.' This made the river a key Augustan administrative boundary between Regio VII Etruria and Regio IX Liguria. Established the Magra as a canonical frontier in Roman cartography, law and military logistics; Luna's prosperity as a marble port (Carrara) depended on its position at this boundary. The frontier concept persisted into the Middle Ages as the border of the Diocese of Luni.

The Via Francigena and the Malaspina of LunigianaSigeric's Itinerary (990 CE), Annales and Malaspina charters; Dante, Purgatorio VIII

9th-14th c.

The upper Magra valley formed the core of historic Lunigiana, contested between bishops of Luni, the Malaspina family, and the communes of Pontremoli and Sarzana. The Via Francigena, the pilgrim road from Canterbury to Rome, followed the Magra from Pontremoli through Filattiera, Villafranca and Aulla. Control of fords and bridges (notably at Pontremoli and Aulla) was a source of tolls and warfare. Lunigiana became a dense landscape of castles (Malaspina castles at Villafranca, Aulla, Fosdinovo) and parish churches (pieve di Sorano). The river's fords shaped settlement patterns still visible today.

Dante in Lunigiana and the Peace of CastelnuovoDante Alighieri, Epistola IV to Moroello Malaspina; Boccaccio, Trattatello in laude di Dante; Notarial act of Castelnuovo Magra 6 Oct 1306

1306-1308

Exiled from Florence, Dante found refuge with the Malaspina in Lunigiana in 1306 and acted as procurator for Franceschino Malaspina in negotiating the Peace of Castelnuovo Magra (6 Oct 1306) between the Malaspina and the Bishop of Luni. Dante later immortalized the courtesy of the Malaspina in Purgatorio VIII 109-139, calling Lunigiana's lords noble and the valley's fame enduring. The Magra valley is the explicit setting of this political episode. Dante's stay cemented Lunigiana's literary fame; the Peace temporarily stabilized the Magra valley. The episode is central to Dante biography and to Lunigiana identity; Castelnuovo Magra preserves Dante's house and annual commemorations.

Accounts II
The Gothic Line: Battles along the Magra, 1944-1945Allied Military Government reports; Istituto Storico della Resistenza; War diaries of 92nd US Infantry Division (Buffalo Soldiers)

September 1944 - April 1945

The lower Magra formed part of the western sector of the Gothic Line (Linea Gotica). German Wehrmacht and RSI fortified the river line at Aulla, Santo Stefano di Magra and Sarzana to block Allied advance from La Spezia toward the Po Valley. The US 92nd Division and Brazilian Expeditionary Force fought costly actions to cross the Magra and Vara in early 1945. German retreat involved destruction of all bridges over the Magra. Aulla was 70% destroyed by Allied bombing and German demolitions; civilian casualties and reprisals (e.g., Sant'Anna di Stazzema is nearby, though not on Magra). Post-war reconstruction rebuilt bridges and embankments, fundamentally reshaping the river's lower course.

The Flood of 25 October 2011Regione Toscana - Evento alluvionale 25 ottobre 2011, Relazione tecnica; ISPRA reports; newspaper archives (Il Secolo XIX, La Nazione)

25-26 October 2011

After 300-500 mm of rain in 6 hours on the Apennines, the Magra and Vara overflowed catastrophically. At Aulla the Magra rose 8 metres, inundating the historic centre and industrial zone; Borghetto Vara and Brugnato on the Vara were devastated. Three deaths in the basin, hundreds displaced, estimated damages >€300 million in the Magra-Vara basin alone. Led to revision of flood hazard maps (PGRA), reinforcement of levees, buyouts in high-risk zones, and a national debate on soil consumption and riverbed quarrying. The flood is now the benchmark 200-year event for planning.

The Caprigliola Bridge Collapse during LockdownANSA, BBC News, MIT (Ministero delle Infrastrutture) inquiry reports 2020-2021

8 April 2020

At 10:25 on 8 April 2020, the 260-metre road bridge of the former SS330 at Albiano Magra (connecting Caprigliola and Albiano Magra near Aulla) collapsed into the Magra. Due to the COVID-19 national lockdown, traffic was minimal; only two van drivers sustained minor injuries. The bridge, built in 1908 and repeatedly widened, had shown cracks and was under monitoring by ANAS. Triggered national inspection of 1,300+ similar bridges; judicial investigation for negligent maintenance; a new bridge opened in 2022. Became an international symbol of Italy's aging infrastructure and the paradoxical life-saving effect of lockdown. No fatalities, unlike the 2018 Genoa Morandi collapse.

The Magra's name comes from Italian 'magro' meaning thin or lean, describing its low summer flow. — Folk etymology. The ancient form Macra predates Italian magro by over a millennium and belongs to a pre-Latin hydronymic substrate (*mak-/*mag-). The resemblance to magro is coincidental; Latin macer (thin) is not the source.

The Magra is a major navigable river like the Arno or Po. — The Magra is not and was never navigable beyond small boats near its mouth at Luna. Its torrential regime, shallow gravel bed and summer low flows make navigation impossible upstream of Sarzana. Romans used nearby Portus Lunae, not the river itself, for shipping.

The Magra and Vara are the same river, or the Vara is the main stem. — They are distinct rivers; Magra is the main stem by historical convention and length (62 km vs Vara 58 km), though Vara has a slightly larger peak discharge at times. The confluence at Santo Stefano di Magra is clearly defined, and official hydrography keeps them separate.

The 2020 Caprigliola bridge collapse was caused solely by the Magra flooding and scour. — The MIT inquiry found primary causes were structural decay, inadequate maintenance, and design deficiencies of the 1908 bridge, with scour as a possible aggravating factor but not the sole cause. The river did not flood catastrophically that day.
