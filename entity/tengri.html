Who It Is

Before the Silk Road carried silk, spices, and Buddhism across the Central Asian steppe, it carried a quieter cargo: the name Tengri, spoken by herders, warriors, and shamans as they watched the endless blue vault overhead. For over two millennia, this word meant both the sky itself and the supreme being who dwelt within it—a deity so vast that the Göktürk khagans claimed to rule by his mandate, the Mongol Empire invoked his will to conquer Eurasia, and even today the Turkish language retains Tanrı as its generic word for God. Yet Tengri is not a monotheistic sky father in the Abrahamic mold. He is a white goose flying over infinite waters, a force that is simultaneously sky, ancestor, and cosmic judge, and the living pulse of a religion that still whispers in the Siberian taiga and the Mongolian steppe.

Tengri functions as a cosmic judge and source of life, eternal and supreme. The Mongolian concept of jayayan ('heaven's will') holds that Tengri may break its own usual laws to intervene by sending a chosen person to earth. Tengri is not capricious; rather, the tradition emphasizes harmony with the universe and adherence to Turkic customary law (törü). Cosmic Creation and Sustenance

Tengri is considered the chief god who created all things. In the Orkhon creation myth, Tengri and the brown earth together create humanity. Tengri's domain encompasses the entire celestial sphere; as the sky principle, Tengri sustains the cosmic order (törü) and grants life to all beings.

Limit: Tengri operates within a framework of heavenly law, though Mongolian tradition holds that jayayan (heaven's will) may break those laws for a chosen emissary.

Mandate of Heaven (kut)

Tengri grants kut—a heavenly and spiritual force—to legitimate rulers. Göktürk khagans wore titles such as tengrikut, kutluġ, or kutalmysh, signifying they ruled by Tengri's mandate. This investiture could be withdrawn, as when the Uighur khagans abandoned Tengrism for Manichaeism in the 8th century.

Limit: kut is not omnipotent; it can be lost through impiety or failure to uphold törü (customary law).

Jayayan (Intervention Beyond Law)

According to Mongolian belief, Tengri's will (jayayan) may break its own laws and intervene directly in the world by sending a chosen person (a messenger, hero, or ruler) to earth. This is the theological basis for divine-right kingship among Turkic and Mongolic peoples.

Limit: Intervention is exceptional, not routine. The chosen person must be sent, not self-appointed.

Ancestral Progenitorship

Tengri was thought to be the ancestral progenitor of mankind in Turkic regions and Mongolia. Genealogies of ruling clans traced descent from Tengri, making the khagan a living bridge between heaven and earth. This fused sky worship with ancestor worship, distinguishing Tengrism from purely abstract animism.

Limit: Ancestor worship varied by tribe and era; not all Tengrist groups claimed direct descent from Tengri.

Form & Signs

As the concept personalised, Tengri Ülgen ('Holy Tengri') withdraws into the highest heaven and communicates through sacred animals and ancestral spirits rather than direct physical manifestation. Symbols — Blue sky, white goose, celestial disc, sun, World Tree (Baiterek), the horse (as a sacred animal sent from Tengri), and the colour white (purity, celestial light). Göktürk standards carried a pole topped with a wolf-head or horse-hair tuft, representing Tengri's mandate. The Orkhon inscriptions invoke Tengri alongside 'brown earth' in a binary cosmic imagery.

Domains — Sky, heavens, celestial sphere, the highest of nine heavens (in some traditions). Tengri Ülgen resides in the fifth level of heaven, beyond which shaman priests cannot pass. The sky is simultaneously a physical domain and a metaphysical principle; Tengri is both the blue vault above and the will that sustains it. Sacred mountains—Burkhan Khaldun, Belukha Mountain, Khan Tengri, Otgontenger—are considered earthly anchors where heaven touches earth.

Accounts I
Creation of the World from WaterTurkic creation mythology (reconstructed from ethnographic and medieval sources)

Pre-Islamic oral tradition; recorded in 19th-20th century ethnography

In the beginning, Tengri (as a pure white goose) flies over an endless expanse of water representing time. Ak Ana (White Mother), the primordial water goddess, calls out to him: 'Create!' Tengri, disturbed from his calm flight, creates Er Kishi to overcome loneliness. Together they set up the world, but Er Kishi becomes demonic and strives to mislead humanity into darkness. Tengri assumes the name Tengri Ülgen and withdraws into the highest heaven, guiding humanity through sacred animals rather than direct presence. This myth establishes the moral dualism of Tengrism: the pure heavenly principle versus the corrupting underworld force. The world is created with an intrinsic moral split between heavenly guidance and underworld deception. Humanity is placed between these poles and must choose the path of törü (law/harmony) over the darkness of Erlik.

The Orkhon Inscriptions: Tengri's Mandate to Bilge KaghanOrkhon inscriptions (8th century CE)

Early 8th century CE

The inscriptions erected for Kul Tigin and his brother Bilge Kaghan present a state ideology in which Tengri actively intervenes in history. Bilge Kaghan declares himself 'Tengri-like and Tengri-born,' legitimizing his rule as divinely ordained. The inscriptions describe how the Turks were subjugated by the Tang dynasty and liberated by Ilterish Qaghan, presenting liberation as Tengri's will. 'Tengri creates death. Human beings have all been created in order to die' — a stark reminder of heavenly sovereignty over mortal affairs. The inscriptions fuse mythological origin, political legitimacy, and military victory into a single theological narrative. Established Tengri as the theological foundation of Göktürk imperial power. The 'Tengri-born' formula became a standard royal titulature across Central Asia for centuries.

Accounts II
Tengri and the Spread of Tengrism Across the SteppeHistorical scholarship (Ivanov, Roux, Taşağıl, Inan)

3rd century BCE to 14th century CE

Tengrism served as the prevailing religion of the Göktürk, Hun, Xianbei, Bulgar, Xiongnu, Yeniseian, and Mongolic peoples, and as state religion of the First Turkic Khaganate, Western and Eastern Turkic Khaganates, Old Great Bulgaria, First Bulgarian Empire, Volga Bulgaria, Khazaria, and the Mongol Empire. The Huns brought Tengri worship into Eastern Europe; Bulgars carried it into the Danube region. When the Uighur khagans proclaimed Manichaeism the state religion in the 8th century, Tengri lost its privileged position but never fully disappeared. Under Genghis Khan, Tengrism experienced a revival as the underlying animistic framework of Mongol imperial ideology, though Genghis Khan himself was religiously pragmatic. Tengri worship spread across the entire Eurasian steppe belt, from the Danube to the Pacific, creating a shared religious vocabulary that facilitated diplomatic and military alliances among Turkic and Mongolic peoples.

The Shamanic Descent to Tengri ÜlgenSiberian and Mongolian shamanic tradition (recorded in ethnographic work by Shiratori, Anisimov, and others)

Living oral tradition; documented 19th-20th centuries

Shaman priests (böö) attempt to ascend through the nine levels of heaven to reach Tengri Ülgen. They never pass beyond the fifth level, where the Ak Tengris (Pure Tengris) dwell. There the shaman conveys wishes to divine guides who relay them to Tengri Ülgen. The return to earth takes place in a goose-shaped vessel, echoing the primordial image of Tengri as the white goose. This shamanic cosmology encodes the primordial creation myth in ritual geography: the nine heavens map the stages between the material world and the presence of the supreme deity. Creates a structured cosmology where Tengri is simultaneously immanent (in the shaman's ritual ascent) and transcendent (beyond the fifth heaven). The goose vessel link closes the symbolic circle back to the creation myth.

Tengrism is a monotheistic religion. — Classical Tengrism was predominantly polytheistic, with Tengri as the supreme deity among many spirits, earth goddesses, underworld gods, and ancestor beings. Monotheistic elements increased during the imperial period (12th-13th centuries) due to contact with Abrahamic religions, but this was an evolution, not the original state.

Tengri is exactly the same as the Chinese Tian or the Indo-European Dyeus. — Tengri is cognate to the Proto-Indo-European *Dyeus ('sky god') in the sense that both derive from a shared Nostratic or Eurasiatic root for 'sky/heaven,' but they are distinct deities in their respective pantheons. The similarity is linguistic, not identity of worship.

Genghis Khan was a devout Tengrist who imposed Tengrism on his empire. — Genghis Khan invoked Tengri as his political authority but practiced religious tolerance, patronizing Buddhists, Muslims, Christians, and shamans alike. Tengrism was the implicit cultural framework, not an enforced state religion.
