Tengri

Kök Tengri; The Eternal Blue Sky

also known asTengri KhanKök TengriGök TanrıTaŋrïTeŋriTanrı
TypeSupreme Sky God
CultureTurkic / Mongol / Tengrist
Who It Is
Form

In the oldest mythological strata, Tengri is not depicted as a human-shaped deity but as the pure white goose that flies constantly over an endless expanse of water, representing time. As the concept personalised, Tengri Ülgen ('Holy Tengri') withdraws into the highest heaven and communicates through sacred animals and ancestral spirits rather than direct physical manifestation. In Orkhon inscriptions, khagans are described as 'Tengri-like and Tengri-born,' suggesting a radiant, other-than-human quality rather than a fixed anthropomorphic form. In later Turkic folk art and shamanic drum iconography, Tengri is sometimes represented as a disc or sun symbol, often in combination with Ülgen on the World Tree.

Powers

Cosmic Creation and Sustenance Tengri is considered the chief god who created all things. In the Orkhon creation myth, Tengri and the brown earth together create humanity. Tengri's domain encompasses the entire celestial sphere; as the sky principle, Tengri sustains the cosmic order (törü) and grants life to all beings. Orkhon inscriptions; Turkic creation mythology Tengri operates within a framework of heavenly law, though Mongolian tradition holds that jayayan (heaven's will) may break those laws for a chosen emissary. Some sources present Tengri as an impersonal force (mana-like), others as a personal god intervening in history. This reflects the evolution from animistic sky-worship to personal deity.

Mandate of Heaven (kut) Tengri grants kut—a heavenly and spiritual force—to legitimate rulers. Göktürk khagans wore titles such as tengrikut, kutluġ, or kutalmysh, signifying they ruled by Tengri's mandate. This investiture could be withdrawn, as when the Uighur khagans abandoned Tengrism for Manichaeism in the 8th century. Göktürk political theology; Orkhon inscriptions kut is not omnipotent; it can be lost through impiety or failure to uphold törü (customary law). The Mandate of Heaven concept resembles the Chinese tianming, but the Turkic kut is more explicitly personal and tied to ancestor veneration rather than moral virtue alone.

Jayayan (Intervention Beyond Law) According to Mongolian belief, Tengri's will (jayayan) may break its own laws and intervene directly in the world by sending a chosen person (a messenger, hero, or ruler) to earth. This is the theological basis for divine-right kingship among Turkic and Mongolic peoples. Mongolian Tengrism; later Mongol imperial ideology Intervention is exceptional, not routine. The chosen person must be sent, not self-appointed. No consensus on whether jayayan applies to all Tengrists or only Mongolian traditions.

Ancestral Progenitorship Tengri was thought to be the ancestral progenitor of mankind in Turkic regions and Mongolia. Genealogies of ruling clans traced descent from Tengri, making the khagan a living bridge between heaven and earth. This fused sky worship with ancestor worship, distinguishing Tengrism from purely abstract animism. Orkhon inscriptions; Turkic and Mongol genealogical traditions Ancestor worship varied by tribe and era; not all Tengrist groups claimed direct descent from Tengri. Some scholars argue the ancestor layer was added later, after Göktürk state formation, rather than being primordial.

Accounts

In the beginning, Tengri (as a pure white goose) flies over an endless expanse of water representing time. Ak Ana (White Mother), the primordial water goddess, calls out to him: 'Create!' Tengri, disturbed from his calm flight, creates Er Kishi to overcome loneliness. Together they set up the world, but Er Kishi becomes demonic and strives to mislead humanity into darkness. Tengri assumes the name Tengri Ülgen and withdraws into the highest heaven, guiding humanity through sacred animals rather than direct presence. This myth establishes the moral dualism of Tengrism: the pure heavenly principle versus the corrupting underworld force.

The inscriptions erected for Kul Tigin and his brother Bilge Kaghan present a state ideology in which Tengri actively intervenes in history. Bilge Kaghan declares himself 'Tengri-like and Tengri-born,' legitimizing his rule as divinely ordained. The inscriptions describe how the Turks were subjugated by the Tang dynasty and liberated by Ilterish Qaghan, presenting liberation as Tengri's will. 'Tengri creates death. Human beings have all been created in order to die' — a stark reminder of heavenly sovereignty over mortal affairs. The inscriptions fuse mythological origin, political legitimacy, and military victory into a single theological narrative.

Tengrism served as the prevailing religion of the Göktürk, Hun, Xianbei, Bulgar, Xiongnu, Yeniseian, and Mongolic peoples, and as state religion of the First Turkic Khaganate, Western and Eastern Turkic Khaganates, Old Great Bulgaria, First Bulgarian Empire, Volga Bulgaria, Khazaria, and the Mongol Empire. The Huns brought Tengri worship into Eastern Europe; Bulgars carried it into the Danube region. When the Uighur khagans proclaimed Manichaeism the state religion in the 8th century, Tengri lost its privileged position but never fully disappeared. Under Genghis Khan, Tengrism experienced a revival as the underlying animistic framework of Mongol imperial ideology, though Genghis Khan himself was religiously pragmatic.

Shaman priests (böö) attempt to ascend through the nine levels of heaven to reach Tengri Ülgen. They never pass beyond the fifth level, where the Ak Tengris (Pure Tengris) dwell. There the shaman conveys wishes to divine guides who relay them to Tengri Ülgen. The return to earth takes place in a goose-shaped vessel, echoing the primordial image of Tengri as the white goose. This shamanic cosmology encodes the primordial creation myth in ritual geography: the nine heavens map the stages between the material world and the presence of the supreme deity.

Content Notes

Under Genghis Khan and the Mongol Empire, Tengri was invoked as the divine authority behind campaigns that killed millions across Eurasia. The Secret History of the Mongols presents Tengri as actively choosing Genghis Khan and directing his victories, while the conquests included wholesale destruction of cities such as Nishapur and Merv.

David Morgan and other historians note that while Genghis Khan's personal religious practice may have been shamanic rather than dogmatic, Tengri provided the ideological legitimation for the most destructive military campaign in pre-modern history.

Secret History of the Mongols (13th century); Juvayni, Ta'rīkh-i Jahān-gushā (13th century)

Some Turkic and Mongolic ritual practices included animal sacrifice (white horses, sheep) and, in earlier periods, possible human sacrifice to accompany dead rulers. The Xiongnu and some early Turkic kurgans show evidence of retainer sacrifice, though this predates classical Tengrism and may reflect older shamanic practices rather than organized Tengrist theology.

Rudenko and other Soviet-era archaeologists documented horse and human sacrifices in Pazyryk and Xiongnu burials. The connection to Tengri worship is inferential, as the religious framework is not directly attested in the burial contexts.

Archaeological evidence from Xiongnu and early Turkic kurgans (Mongolia, 3rd century BCE-6th century CE)

Göktürk and Mongol rulers sometimes enforced Tengri worship as state ideology. The 8th-century Uighur khagan's switch to Manichaeism was accompanied by suppression of traditional Tengrist practices. Later, during the socialist period in Mongolia (1924-1992), Tengrism and shamanism were heavily repressed as 'backward' superstition, with shamans executed or imprisoned.

The repression of Tengrism under socialism parallels the suppression of shamanic traditions across Siberia and East Asia. The 'backward' label reflects Marxist-Leninist atheist ideology rather than neutral scholarship.

Mongolian socialist-era archives; Humphrey, Urgunge Onon, and other ethnographers

Scholarly Debates
Monotheistic vs. polytheistic character of Tengrism
Tengrism was fundamentally polytheistic, with Tengri as the supreme deity among many spirits, alp, and local divinities. Scholar Jean-Paul Roux and others emphasize the persistent polytheism of Turkic religion. ⇌ Imperial-period Tengrism (12th-13th centuries) became effectively monotheistic, with Tengri as the sole supreme god absorbing all other powers. This reflects influence from Abrahamic religions encountered along trade routes.
Evidence for A: The Irk Bitig and Orkhon inscriptions acknowledge many supernatural beings; shamanic practice throughout Siberia remains
Evidence for B: Later Mongol and Turkic sources describe Tengri as the unique, all-powerful creator. The Turkish Tanrı concept is entire
Tengri as impersonal force vs. personal deity
The original Turkic tengri was an impersonal force or principle—'heaven' itself—not a conscious god. This is supported by the semantic range of the word (it simply means 'sky') and the absence of anthropomorphic depictions in early art. ⇌ Tengri was always a personal, conscious deity who creates, judges, and intervenes in human affairs, as shown by the Orkhon inscriptions and creation myths.
Evidence for A: The Xiongnu term Cheng-li and the Proto-Turkic *Teŋri both derive from 'sky' semantics. Early Turkic religion had no tem
Evidence for B: The Orkhon inscriptions treat Tengri as a personal lord who creates, mandates kings, and wills death. The creation myth
Origins of the word 'Tengri'
Proto-Altaic etymology from *T'aŋgiri ('oath' or 'god'), emphasizing the god's divinity. ⇌ Proto-Turkic *Teŋri from 'sky', later extended to 'god'. Kononov's tän (morning) + injir (evening) compound supports this.
Evidence for A: Comparative Altaic reconstruction by Starostin, Dybo, and others.
Evidence for B: Linguistic analysis by Clauson, Doerfer, and Stefan Georg's Proto-Yeniseian loan hypothesis from *tɨŋgVr- 'high'.
Kayra Han vs. Tengri: same or different?
Kayra Han and Tengri are identical deities known by different names among different Turkic branches (Altai vs. Göktürk). ⇌ They are originally separate figures: Kayra is the Siberian/Altai creator god, Tengri is the sky god of the steppe. Syncretism during the Mongol Empire fused them.
Evidence for A: Folk traditions across Siberia show extensive conflation of the two names and attributes.
Evidence for B: Göktürk and Khazar sources mention Tengri without Kayra; Yakut and Altai sources mention Kayra without Tengri as creator
What We Don't Know

The original sound and grammar of the Xiongnu term Cheng-li remains speculative because no Xiongnu texts survive; reconstruction depends on Chinese transcriptions. The Proto-Yeniseian etymology (*tɨŋgVr-) is compelling but awaits further comparative work on Yeniseian-Turkic contact layers. Many early Tengrist practices are known only through later ethnographic analogy; primary sources from Göktürk and Hunnic periods are fragmentary. The relationship between Tengri and the Siberian creator god Kayra Han is still unresolved. Pop-culture appearances are sparse outside niche folk-metal and strategy games; no major Hollywood or anime adaptation exists. Image rights for Siberian shamanic drums and Orkhon inscription photographs require museum-level clearance not yet secured.

A way to hold it in your mind

Before the Silk Road carried silk, spices, and Buddhism across the Central Asian steppe, it carried a quieter cargo: the name Tengri, spoken by herders, warriors, and shamans as they watched the endless blue vault overhead. For over two millennia, this word meant both the sky itself and the supreme being who dwelt within it—a deity so vast that the Göktürk khagans claimed to rule by his mandate, the Mongol Empire invoked his will to conquer Eurasia, and even today the Turkish language retains Tanrı as its generic word for God. Yet Tengri is not a monotheistic sky father in the Abrahamic mold. He is a white goose flying over infinite waters, a force that is simultaneously sky, ancestor, and cosmic judge, and the living pulse of a religion that still whispers in the Siberian taiga and the Mongolian steppe.
