Who It Is

In the shadow of Rome's Capitoline Hill, where the city's oldest walls once stood, there was a gate named after a goddess who could see the future. Her name was Antevorta — 'she who turns before' — and she was the divine embodiment of foresight, the one who looked ahead while her sister Postverta looked back. Together, they saw all of time, a divine pair of seers whose story survives only in fragments, whispers, and a star that still bears their name.

Antevorta is characterized by her ability to see and announce the future — specifically, what is 'before' (ante) the present moment. She is a deity of foresight, prophecy, and the unfolding of fate. Unlike her sister Postverta (who knows the past), Antevorta embodies the forward-looking aspect of divination. She is associated with the Roman ritual of the Carmentalia, where her prophetic powers were honored.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Latin ante (before) and vorto/verto (to turn). The name literally means 'she who turns (or looks) before' — i.e., she who faces the future. The variant name Porrima derives from the same root, prorsus (forward), meaning 'she who looks forward.'

Powers
Foresight

Antevorta possesses the ability to see and reveal future events. She embodies the Roman concept of 'prorsa' or 'prosa' — looking forward — and her prophetic power is directly tied to her name.

Limit: Her power is limited to the future; the past is the domain of her sister Postverta. She does not control fate, only perceives it.

Divine Speech (Vaticinium)

Like her mother Carmenta, Antevorta is associated with the power of inspired utterance — the ability to speak prophecies in verse or song. This is a common trait of the Camenae.

Limit: Her utterances are bound by fate; she cannot change what she foretells.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No surviving artistic depictions of Antevorta exist. She is described primarily as a prophetic deity, likely imagined in the form of a veiled or inspired woman, similar to other prophetic figures like the Sibyl or the Camenae. Her sister Postverta is similarly unattested visually. In later Roman art, prophetic goddesses are often shown with a scroll or a staff, but this is speculative for Antevorta.

Symbols — No specific symbols are attested for Antevorta. She is associated with the prophetic arts, so scrolls, laurel (associated with prophecy), and perhaps a veil (symbolizing the hidden nature of the future) are plausible but unattested. Her sister Postverta is associated with the past, and together they represent the full scope of time.

Domains — Antevorta's domain is the future — specifically, the knowledge of things to come. She dwells in the realm of prophecy and divine knowledge, closely associated with the Camenae, the prophetic nymphs of Roman religion. Her cult was centered at the Porta Carmentalis in Rome, near the Capitoline Hill, where the festival of the Carmentalia was celebrated.

Accounts I
The Birth of Carmenta's DaughtersVarro, De Lingua Latina 7.2 (reconstructed from fragments)

Archaic (pre-1st century BCE)

Carmenta, the prophetic goddess and mother of Evander (who brought Greek culture to Italy), is said to have two daughters or companions: Antevorta, who knows the future, and Postverta, who knows the past. Together, they embody the totality of time and prophetic knowledge. They are associated with the Camenae, the prophetic nymphs who inspired Roman poets and seers. The pair became associated with the Carmentalia festival, where their prophetic powers were honored, and with the Porta Carmentalis, the gate through which they were believed to pass.

Accounts II
The Carmentalia FestivalOvid, Fasti 1.633-636; Plutarch, Life of Romulus 21

Archaic to Imperial

The Carmentalia, celebrated on January 11 and 15, honored Carmenta and her prophetic companions. The festival was associated with the founding of Rome, as Carmenta was said to have prophesied the city's greatness. Antevorta and Postverta, as aspects of Carmenta, were implicitly honored in these rites. The festival was particularly important to women, who sought Carmenta's blessings for childbirth and prophecy. The festival ensured the continued favor of the prophetic goddesses, securing Rome's future prosperity and the well-being of its women.

Antevorta and the Founding of Rome (Reconstruction)Reconstructed from Plutarch and Ovid

Archaic (legendary)

When Evander, son of Carmenta, arrived in Italy, his mother prophesied the future greatness of the site that would become Rome. Antevorta, as the embodiment of forward-looking prophecy, was part of this divine knowledge. The pair of sisters (or aspects) represented the full scope of time, ensuring that nothing was hidden from the gods' sight. The prophecy of Rome's greatness became a foundational myth of the city, linking the prophetic goddesses to the city's destiny.
