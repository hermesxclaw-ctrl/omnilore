Form

No surviving artistic depictions of Antevorta exist. She is described primarily as a prophetic deity, likely imagined in the form of a veiled or inspired woman, similar to other prophetic figures like the Sibyl or the Camenae. Her sister Postverta is similarly unattested visually. In later Roman art, prophetic goddesses are often shown with a scroll or a staff, but this is speculative for Antevorta.

Powers

Foresight Antevorta possesses the ability to see and reveal future events. She embodies the Roman concept of 'prorsa' or 'prosa' — looking forward — and her prophetic power is directly tied to her name. Roman (archaic and classical) Her power is limited to the future; the past is the domain of her sister Postverta. She does not control fate, only perceives it. Some sources treat Antevorta and Postverta as separate goddesses; others as two aspects of Carmenta, meaning Antevorta's independent agency is unclear.

Divine Speech (Vaticinium) Like her mother Carmenta, Antevorta is associated with the power of inspired utterance — the ability to speak prophecies in verse or song. This is a common trait of the Camenae. Roman (archaic) Her utterances are bound by fate; she cannot change what she foretells. None recorded.

Accounts

Carmenta, the prophetic goddess and mother of Evander (who brought Greek culture to Italy), is said to have two daughters or companions: Antevorta, who knows the future, and Postverta, who knows the past. Together, they embody the totality of time and prophetic knowledge. They are associated with the Camenae, the prophetic nymphs who inspired Roman poets and seers.

The Carmentalia, celebrated on January 11 and 15, honored Carmenta and her prophetic companions. The festival was associated with the founding of Rome, as Carmenta was said to have prophesied the city's greatness. Antevorta and Postverta, as aspects of Carmenta, were implicitly honored in these rites. The festival was particularly important to women, who sought Carmenta's blessings for childbirth and prophecy.

When Evander, son of Carmenta, arrived in Italy, his mother prophesied the future greatness of the site that would become Rome. Antevorta, as the embodiment of forward-looking prophecy, was part of this divine knowledge. The pair of sisters (or aspects) represented the full scope of time, ensuring that nothing was hidden from the gods' sight.

Scholarly Debates
Antevorta vs. Postverta vs. Carmenta
Antevorta and Postverta are separate goddesses, daughters of Carmenta (Varro). ⇌ Antevorta and Postverta are two aspects of Carmenta herself (Macrobius).
Evidence for A: Varro, De Lingua Latina 7.2, treats them as distinct entities associated with the Camenae.
Evidence for B: Macrobius, Saturnalia 1.7.20-21, explicitly states that Carmenta has two aspects, Antevorta and Postverta.
Porrima vs. Antevorta
Porrima is an alternative name for Antevorta. ⇌ Porrima is an alternative name for Postverta.
Evidence for A: Ovid uses the name Porrima in the context of the Carmentalia, and some later sources equate Porrima with Antevorta.
Evidence for B: Other sources (e.g., some manuscripts of Ovid) use Porrima for Postverta, leading to confusion.
What We Don't Know

No archaeological evidence, inscriptions, or artistic depictions of Antevorta have been found. The exact nature of her cult, if any, is unknown. The relationship between Antevorta, Postverta, and Porrima is unclear. No modern scholarly monograph focuses exclusively on Antevorta; she is treated only in broader studies of Roman religion.

A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine a pair of cosmic librarians: one who has read every book that will ever be written, and one who has read every book that has ever been written. Antevorta is the first — the archivist of the future, the seer of things to come. She is not a weaver of fate or a judge of destiny; she is a witness, a divine observer who stands at the edge of time and tells you what lies ahead. In a world obsessed with prediction — from weather forecasts to stock market algorithms — Antevorta is the ancient patron saint of foresight, the goddess who knew the future before it was fashionable.
