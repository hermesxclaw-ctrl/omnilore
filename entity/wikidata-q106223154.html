The Approach

A whisper from the ancient Levant, Pummay survives not in temples or tales, but in the names of kings and the echoes of a goddess's flight. Who was this shadowy deity, and why did his name endure on the lips of Phoenician rulers?

Etymology

The name is Phoenician, written 𐤐𐤌𐤉 (Pūmay) or 𐤐𐤏𐤌𐤉 (Pū(ġ)‘may). The exact meaning is uncertain; it may be related to the Phoenician word for 'mouth' (pū) or 'here' (pō), but no consensus exists.

Cultural Ownership

Phoenician, possibly also Cypriot.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars have suggested a connection with the Greek god Pygmalion or the Cypriot Adonis, but these are speculative.

Stories & Myths

Pygmalion of Tyre

Source Josephus, Against Apion; Vergil, AeneidCulture Phoenician/GreekPeriod Iron Age (Phoenician), Roman (Vergil)

Summary

Pygmalion was a king of Tyre, brother of Dido. He murdered Dido's husband Sychaeus for his gold, prompting Dido to flee and found Carthage. The name Pygmalion is derived from Pummay-aton, indicating that Pummay was a divine patron of the king.

Participants

Consequences

Dido's flight led to the founding of Carthage.

Variants

Vergil's version is a literary retelling; the historical Pygmalion is known from Tyrian king lists.
