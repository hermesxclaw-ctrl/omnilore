Who It Is

Before any wind ever had a name, before a single storm was called 'the weather's anger', there was a god who existed almost entirely as a list — a name carved among six others on a Kiev hill in the year 980, and for a thousand years after, a riddle wearing the shape of a deity. Stribog the wind-god, the wealth-scatterer, the jumper, the flowing-one, the sky-father: every school of philology reads him differently, because the only thing anyone truly agrees on is that three short medieval texts mention him and none of them explains him. He is the archaeology of a god built out of a single metaphor — 'the winds, sons of Stribog' — and out of the stubborn will of scholars to reconstruct weather into worship.

Nothing is directly recorded. From the Igor' metaphor the winds are his energetic, arrow-like forces that fight for the warrior Igor — implying a god whose temper can be martial and whose winds are weapons of campaign. The reconstruction (Jakobson) makes him a distributor of fortune/shares rather than a giver, a celestial accountant of destiny rather than a provider. Beyond this, any 'personality' is inference.

Powers
Command of the winds

Stribog's most famous (and only poetically attested) power: the winds are his 'sons' or 'grandsons' who obey his will, blowing 'from the sea like arrows on the valiant campaign of Igor' in The Tale of Igor's Campaign. This casts him as a marshal of weather who can unleash or still the winds that decide a battle or a voyage.

Limit: The Igor' line is literary metaphor, not cult testimony; it proves the poet associated Stribog with wind, not that priests did. The wind-reading itself is contested by the water/sky-father schools.

Distribution / apportionment of shares (reconstructed)

Under Jakobson's etymology (stri- 'to spread, scatter' + bog 'god'), Stribog is not a giver of wealth but a disperser/apportioner of rich shares and fate — the complementary counterpart to Dazhbog the 'giver'. This places the pair in the Indo-European distributive deities (Vedic Bhaga and Amsha; Greek Aisa and Poros).

Limit: Linguistic reconstruction builds the power; no text describes Stribog distributing anything. The whole foundation rests on accepting Jakobson's (contested) split of the theonym.

Agency of motion, speed, and flowing (water-school)

The 'jumping/flowing' school (Brückner, Ohonovskyy, Łuczyński) ascribes to Stribog a swift, mobile nature — one who 'moves at high speed', leaps, escapes, flies high, or runs/flows like water. This is grounded in the word-family strybati and in the hydronym survival of the name.

Limit: Reconstruction only. The modern 'jump' sense is argued to be late; the original is held to be 'move swiftly / flow'.

Sovereignty over goods / first-function divinity (Dumézilian)

Ivanov and Toporov, following Dumézil's trifunctional hypothesis, place Stribog in the first (sovereign) function alongside Perun — the group of chief deities who 'look after the community and watch over the distribution of goods'. This aligns the apportioner reading with an Indo-European sovereignty slot.

Limit: The applicability of Dumézil's tripartition to Slavic material is itself a debated scholarly position, not an attested power.

Destructive / annihilating power (marginal)

Zelenin connected the stri- root to стрити (*sъtьri, 'to annihilate, destroy') and read Stribog as a 'destroying god', a war-god. Supported by Orlov and Borovsky, partly via the Vayu parallel (Vayu as war god).

Limit: Minority view among the etymological schools; rests on a different root assignment.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No description of Stribog's appearance survives anywhere. He is never described in the texts, and no securely identified iconography exists. Roman Jakobson's etymology implies a gust-like, dispersive nature rather than a fixed anthropomorphic image. Accounts I
Stribog in Vladimir's pantheon (980)Primary Chronicle

980 (recorded 12th century)

When the newly ascendant Grand Prince Vladimir of Kiev set up a state pantheon of idols on a hill outside his palace, Stribog stood among them: Perun (chief, with the silver head and golden moustache), Khors, Dazhbog, Stribog, Simargl and Mokosh. The text emphasizes that the people 'sacrificed to them, calling them gods,' bringing sons and daughters as offerings and 'desecrating the earth with their offerings.' Stribog is thus one of the six cults Vladimir briefly elevated to state religion — the only record that he received worship at all. Eight years later (c. 988) Vladimir converted to Christianity, pulled down the idols, and had the hill rededicated; a church of Saint Basil reportedly replaced the pantheon. Stribog's cult officially ended here, yet his name outlived the idols in toponyms and in the Igor' poem.

Stribog's grandsons: the winds of Igor's campaign (c. 1185)The Tale of Igor's Campaign (Slovo o polku Igoreve)

c. 1185

In the epic lament for Prince Igor's disastrous campaign against the Polovtsy, the poet invokes the winds as Stribog's living descendants: 'Now the winds, sons of Stribog, blow from the sea like arrows on the valiant campaign of Igor.' The winds are not scenery but agents — they carry the battle against the Rus' forces, framing Stribog as their lord or ancestor. The same poem names the Rus 'grandchildren of Dazhbog', so Stribog and Dazhbog function as complementary ancestral deities of weather and fortune from the same native mythology. This single poetic line is the foundation of the entire modern wind-god reading of Stribog, and remains his only literary moment.

Accounts II
Horn-drinking to Stribog, Dazhbog and PereplutThe Word of John Chrysostom (anti-pagan homily)

11th–13th century

A Christian preacher, denouncing lingering paganism, attacks those who 'believe in Stribog, Dazhbog and Pereplut, for whom they drink from horns while cavorting, having forgotten God who created heaven and earth.' This shows cultic feasting and communal horn-toasting in honor of Stribog continuing well after Christianization — a riotous, celebratory worship remembered by opponents as licentious. Establishes Stribog among gods who received festive communal drink-offerings, not just the state idol-cult. It is the only source pairing Stribog with the obscure Pereplut.

Stribor's Forest (Šuma Striborova) — the Croatian afterlifeIvana Brlić-Mažuranić, Croatian Tales of Long Ago (Priče iz davnine), 1916

1916 (set in a folkloric past)

Though Stribog himself has no South Slavic cult record, the name Stribor lives on in folklore-influenced Croatian literature, most famously as the enchanted Stribor's Forest in Brlić-Mažuranić's collection — a tale of a young man who, with the help of his faithful animal companions, enters the magical forest of Stribor to save himself from his beloved's transforming magic. It is one of the most beloved tales in Croatian children's literature and keeps the 'Stribor' name alive in the South Slavic sphere. Constitutes a distinctly South Slavic, literary afterlife of the Stribor name, separated from the East Slavic wind god by a millennium and a half of folk-tradition filtering.

Stribog is a fully documented wind god whose powers are ancient and certain. — His function is nowhere stated in any source; the wind reading rests on one poetic metaphor (the Igor' poem) plus a contested etymology (Jakobson's). A serious, well-evidenced water/flowing reading directly opposes it.

'Stribor' the Croatian enchanted forest and 'Stribog' the Russian wind god are one continuous deity. — They are separated by region, millennium gap, and medium (a 1916 literary fairy tale vs 12th-century East Slavic attestations); any genetic link is unprovable. Stribor is a folk-literary name-survival, not a South Slavic worship of the wind god.

The modern Grand Power Stribog and the 'Striborg' black-metal project are 'worshiping' or 'continuing' the deity's cult. — These are name-references (a firearm, a musician's pseudonym) with no religious continuity; they attest only to name cachet.

Vladimir's pantheon was a fully integrated, equal hierarchy in which Stribog had a fixed role beside Perun. — The chronicle gives no roles at all; only Perun's primacy (silver head, golden moustache, chief position) is clear. Stribog's rank, function and even separate existence in the compounded 'Khors Dazhbog' reading are all open.

Stribog is the Slavic equivalent of the Greek Poseidon or the Norse Ægir as 'god of the sea/waters'. — He is not a sea god in any attestation; the only water link is the contested flowing-water etymology and hydronym survival, which is very different from a marine deity.
