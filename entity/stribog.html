Who They Are

Stribog is the wind-god of the Slavs — or more precisely, the grandfather of all winds, for that is how the Tale of Igor's Campaign names him in its one indelible line: the winds, Stribog's grandchildren, blow arrows from the sea against the brave regiments of Igor. As with Dazhbog and his human grandchildren, the epic's kinship-grammar preserves the theology: the winds are not Stribog's instruments but his family, each gale a grandson with a name and a temper, and the old god their common ancestor sitting at the center of the compass rose. He stood in Vladimir's Kiev pantheon of 980 beside Perun and Dazhbog — wind, storm, and sun, the weather cabinet complete — and his domain made him a god sailors, millers, and steppe travelers could not afford to neglect: the power that fills sails, drives mills, carries seed, spreads fire, and delivers both the rain-front and the arrow-volley. Folk practice long after Christianization kept feeding him without saying his name: bread and flour scattered to the wind, ribbons tied to trees on high ground, whistling regulated with superstitious care (whistle indoors and you summon his grandchildren into the house). His name may derive from a root meaning to spread, to scatter — the god as distribution itself: of weather, of wealth, of seed and ash and news. Every culture keeps a god it can feel on its face; Stribog is the Slavic one, still leaning on every west wind that crosses the steppe.

Origins

Named in the Primary Chronicle among Vladimir's Kiev idols (980) and immortalized by the grandchildren line in the Tale of Igor's Campaign — two attestations, both first-rank, making him one of the best-documented Slavic gods.

Attested in Primary Chronicle; Tale of Igor's CampaignEra Pre-Christian Slavic Europe

Form

An ancient grandfather with wind-tangled white hair and beard, often blowing a horn or carrying a bow whose arrows are gusts; the eight winds around him as a family portrait.

Powers

Sovereignty over every moving air — breeze, gale, steppe-storm, and the arrow-carrying battle-wind of the epic; distribution of seed, weather, wealth, and ruin; grandfatherhood of the whole aerial family.Command of the winds Stribog's most famous (and only poetically attested) power: the winds are his 'sons' or 'grandsons' who obey his will, blowing 'from the sea like arrows on the valiant campaign of Igor' in The Tale of Igor's Campaign. This casts him as a marshal of weather who can unleash or still the winds that decide a battle or a voyage. Reconstruction grounded in the Igor' poem's poetic metaphor (c. 1185). Attested as poetry, interpreted as theology. The Igor' line is literary metaphor, not cult testimony; it proves the poet associated Stribog with wind, not that priests did. The wind-reading itself is contested by the water/sky-father schools. If Stribog were actually a water/flowing deity (Łuczyński) or a sky-father reflex (Vey), the winds-as-grandsons line would be a poetic repurposing of a name the poet no longer understood, rather than a native power set.Distribution / apportionment of shares (reconstructed) Under Jakobson's etymology (stri- 'to spread, scatter' + bog 'god'), Stribog is not a giver of wealth but a disperser/apportioner of rich shares and fate — the complementary counterpart to Dazhbog the 'giver'. This places the pair in the Indo-European distributive deities (Vedic Bhaga and Amsha; Greek Aisa and Poros). Jakobson's reconstruction (1985), followed by Gieysztor. Linguistic reconstruction builds the power; no text describes Stribog distributing anything. The whole foundation rests on accepting Jakobson's (contested) split of the theonym. Łuczyński and others deny the stri-/-bog split, dissolving this entire power. Pisani's frigus etymology would instead make him a god of cold/frost.Agency of motion, speed, and flowing (water-school) The 'jumping/flowing' school (Brückner, Ohonovskyy, Łuczyński) ascribes to Stribog a swift, mobile nature — one who 'moves at high speed', leaps, escapes, flies high, or runs/flows like water. This is grounded in the word-family strybati and in the hydronym survival of the name. Brückner (1927), Ohonovskyy, Łuczyński (2020); reconstructed. Reconstruction only. The modern 'jump' sense is argued to be late; the original is held to be 'move swiftly / flow'. Directly opposed to both the wind and sky-father readings; the same evidence (toponyms) is marshalled for the water reading that Jakobson uses for wind.Sovereignty over goods / first-function divinity (Dumézilian) Ivanov and Toporov, following Dumézil's trifunctional hypothesis, place Stribog in the first (sovereign) function alongside Perun — the group of chief deities who 'look after the community and watch over the distribution of goods'. This aligns the apportioner reading with an Indo-European sovereignty slot. Ivanov & Toporov (1988); Dumézil's framework applied to Slavic. The applicability of Dumézil's tripartition to Slavic material is itself a debated scholarly position, not an attested power. Other scholars place Stribog in the second (war) function via the Vayu parallel (Vayu is also a war god, god of the dead and harvest), or deny the schema entirely.Destructive / annihilating power (marginal) Zelenin connected the stri- root to стрити (*sъtьri, 'to annihilate, destroy') and read Stribog as a 'destroying god', a war-god. Supported by Orlov and Borovsky, partly via the Vayu parallel (Vayu as war god). Zelenin, followed by Orlov and Borovsky; contested. Minority view among the etymological schools; rests on a different root assignment. Directly incompatible with every benevolent distribution reading; unattested in any primary text.

Weaknesses & Wards

Calm. The windless day is his absence, not his defeat — no tradition records him opposed, only lulled.

Accounts

When the newly ascendant Grand Prince Vladimir of Kiev set up a state pantheon of idols on a hill outside his palace, Stribog stood among them: Perun (chief, with the silver head and golden moustache), Khors, Dazhbog, Stribog, Simargl and Mokosh. The text emphasizes that the people 'sacrificed to them, calling them gods,' bringing sons and daughters as offerings and 'desecrating the earth with their offerings.' Stribog is thus one of the six cults Vladimir briefly elevated to state religion — the only record that he received worship at all.

In the epic lament for Prince Igor's disastrous campaign against the Polovtsy, the poet invokes the winds as Stribog's living descendants: 'Now the winds, sons of Stribog, blow from the sea like arrows on the valiant campaign of Igor.' The winds are not scenery but agents — they carry the battle against the Rus' forces, framing Stribog as their lord or ancestor. The same poem names the Rus 'grandchildren of Dazhbog', so Stribog and Dazhbog function as complementary ancestral deities of weather and fortune from the same native mythology.

A Christian preacher, denouncing lingering paganism, attacks those who 'believe in Stribog, Dazhbog and Pereplut, for whom they drink from horns while cavorting, having forgotten God who created heaven and earth.' This shows cultic feasting and communal horn-toasting in honor of Stribog continuing well after Christianization — a riotous, celebratory worship remembered by opponents as licentious.

Though Stribog himself has no South Slavic cult record, the name Stribor lives on in folklore-influenced Croatian literature, most famously as the enchanted Stribor's Forest in Brlić-Mažuranić's collection — a tale of a young man who, with the help of his faithful animal companions, enters the magical forest of Stribor to save himself from his beloved's transforming magic. It is one of the most beloved tales in Croatian children's literature and keeps the 'Stribor' name alive in the South Slavic sphere.

Cultural Significance

The Igor epic's wind-grandchildren line is among the most quoted fragments of Slavic paganism; scattered-flour wind offerings and whistling taboos carried his cult, unlabeled, into the twentieth century.

A way to hold it in your mind

Before any wind ever had a name, before a single storm was called 'the weather's anger', there was a god who existed almost entirely as a list — a name carved among six others on a Kiev hill in the year 980, and for a thousand years after, a riddle wearing the shape of a deity. Stribog the wind-god, the wealth-scatterer, the jumper, the flowing-one, the sky-father: every school of philology reads him differently, because the only thing anyone truly agrees on is that three short medieval texts mention him and none of them explains him. He is the archaeology of a god built out of a single metaphor — 'the winds, sons of Stribog' — and out of the stubborn will of scholars to reconstruct weather into worship.
