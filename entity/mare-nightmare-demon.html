Who It Is

A malevolent spirit or witch that sits on the chests of sleepers, causing terrifying nightmares, sleep paralysis, and sometimes death. The mare rides its victims through the night, leaving them exhausted. The English word 'nightmare' derives from this entity. It enters homes through keyholes.

Form

Invisible or shapeless presence; when visible, a shadowy woman with long hair, sometimes an old crone; may transform into animals or objects (straw, wisps of wool)

Powers

Sleep paralysis induction

Nightmares that drain life

Entering through keyholes

Shape-shifting (animal form)

Invisibility

Chest-riding / Suffocation The mare's signature power: it sits, walks, or lies on a sleeper's chest, inducing a crushing, suffocating weight, cold sweats, anxiety, and full or partial paralysis. Victims can groan but are otherwise speechless and spellbound, unable to wake unless called by their baptismal name (German tradition). Modern interpreters identify this with the medical phenomenon of sleep paralysis. Pan-Germanic and pan-Slavic; most explicit in Scandinavian (mareridt 'mare-ride') and Slavic accounts Traditionally ineffective against persons born in the daytime (Latvian lietuvēns) and partially countered by naming, moving a left toe, changing sleeping posture, or ward charms. Whether the paralysis is caused purely by weight or also by deliberate strangling varies: Croatian/Serbian mora is said to try to strangle (moriti), while the Latvian lietuvēns strangles with its weight; some Slavic accounts describe a tongue-blood-sucking suction rather than simple pressure.

Shapeshifting The mare can appear as a beautiful young woman, an ugly older woman, a man, or an animal — a cat, dog, owl, frog, hare, mouse, moth, mosquito, or (per Paul Devereux, compiling witch-and-trance lore) horse, ox, bird, bee, or wasp. Slavic tradition lists straw, grass, a mouse, dog, cat, mare, cow, white shadow, leather bag, and snake. It also famously takes the form of a female horse when captured. Nordic, Slavic, and German traditions; Devereux's witch-trance compendium Must re-enter and exit through the same opening (keyhole, crack, hole); can be bound with a saint's belt or halter (e.g. St Francis's belt) and ridden as a horse for years. Whether the shapeshifter is a separate demon or the outbound soul (hamr/fylgja) of a living witch varies by region.

Blood-and-Energy Drain The mare drains people, cattle, and horses of energy and/or blood at night. In Slavic lore it 'sticks in her tongue' and sucks blood from the victim's tongue, leaving them emaciated; it is likewise a 'sapsucker' of trees in Krakovian lore. Polish/Slavic (zmora); northern-horse lore Victims survive but wake exhausted, weak, and in cold sweat; protection (mirrors, red ribbons, iron blades) guards livestock. Blood-sucking is a Slavic emphasis; Scandinavian accounts dwell more on weight-anxiety-suffocation than on literal blood loss.

Entangling hair and foliage (mare-knots) The mare entangles the hair of sleeping humans and beasts into matted tangles (marelocks: marflätor/martovor in Swedish, marefletter/marefloker in Norwegian), an old folk explanation for the hair condition Polish plait (Plica polonica). It likewise tangles tree branches, and names the twisted costal pines martallar ('mare-pines'). Nordic and German folklore; folk-etiology of hair disease The tangling is cosmetic/harassment rather than lethal; it is the source of the characteristic 'mare-knots' in hair and branches. None major — the knotting is consistent across Scandinavian and German accounts.

Possession of dreamspace / nightmare-induction The mare injects terrible dreams and a felt presence into the sleeper's mind, producing the very condition that gives 'nightmare' its name. The dream is not a random bad dream but a visitation, and the sleeper's sense of a malevolent watcher pressing down is the defining symptom. All traditions; the root of the English word 'nightmare' Effectively a passive supernatural affliction rather than an attack spell; warded by prayer, signs of the cross, iron, and threshold charms. The relationship between the subjective dream and the objectively described 'riding' is the crux of the phenomenon's modern medical re-reading as sleep paralysis.

Lethal riding (fatal crushing) In the strongest attestations the mare can kill: the 13th-century Ynglinga saga recounts King Vanlandi of Uppsala dying under a mara that trod on his legs and then pressed down on his head fatally. Croatian/Slavic lore describes the mora visiting men in dreams, 'torturing them with desire before killing them.' Ynglinga saga (Vanlandi); Croatian mora accounts Fatal crushing is exceptional rather than routine; most accounts produce exhaustion, sweat, and paralysis on repeated visits. Whether the mare intends death or merely oppresses is inconsistent — the Ynglinga death is a targeted assassination (see stories), while general folklore presents the mare as an indiscriminate nighttime nuisance that rarely kills outright.

Behavior & Habitat

Returns night after night to the same victim; can be warded with charms, iron, or calling its true name

Accounts

King Vanlandi Sveigðisson of Uppsala married Drífa of Finland but broke his promise to return to her within three years. After ten years, the abandoned wife hired the Finnish sorceress Huld (or Hulda) either to lure the king back or, failing that, to assassinate him. Vanlandi, back in Uppsala, had barely gone to sleep before he complained that the nightmare 'rode him'. When his men held his head, the creature 'trod on his legs' at the point of breaking; when the retinue seized his feet, it pressed down fatally on his head. The mare killed the king in the night.

Thorkel Silver dreams of riding a red horse that barely touches the ground. He takes it as a positive omen, but his wife corrects him: a mare signifies a man's fetch (fylgja), and the red color bodes blood. The episode is notable because philologists regard the mare/fetch connection as an interpolation that 'confounds the words marr (horse) and mara (nightmare)'.

The sorceress Geirríðr is accused of assuming the shape of a kveldriða or marlíðendr ('night-rider'/'ride-by-night') and inflicting serious trampling bruises on Gunnlaug Thorbjornsson. Commentators equate the marlíðendr with the mara, linking the mare to witch-flight and harmful out-of-body attacks.

The môr (mare) enters a house through a hole the carpenter forgot to plug. In one tale, a male môr tormenting a woman is captured by plugging the hole; he becomes her husband and fathers her children, but leaves after being told about the hole, returning just once a year thereafter. In another, a female môr caught by having green paint put on her hands is set permanently on an oak, which withers but always shivers. The mare also rides horses and leaves their manes forever matted.

A woman jilted in love — promised to a man who then married another — becomes a zmora (mare) at night, entering through a crack as a moth to blood-suck her chosen man and his wife. She is bound with a belt of St Francis, which her captor converts into a halter, turns her into a female horse, and rides her for seven years to her death. In a Kraków variant a farmhand marries the zmora but 'tests' how she suffers by plugging her conduit, only unplugging it once she is pregnant; in a Lublin variant a farmhand catches the zmora in cat-form with the St Francis belt, only to find it is a girl in love with him.

A way to hold it in your mind

You are lying in bed, half-asleep, when a weight settles on your chest — heavy, crushing, impossible to throw off, while some presence crouches over you in the dark. You cannot move, you cannot cry out, you can barely breathe, and the feeling is so ancient and so precisely painstaking it has its own creature. She is called the Mare, and she has ridden sleepers for over a thousand years: she killed a king of Uppsala in the thirteenth century, she is the reason a tangle in your hair is called a 'mare-knot,' and she is why the word for a bad dream is literally a night-*mare*. Every culture that knows sleep paralysis has given it a name and a face — the Phi Am of Thailand, the Karabasan of Turkey, the Old Hag of Newfoundland. But the Western face of that midnight pressure is the mare, a shapeshifting female spirit who slips through keyholes, straddles your chest, and drains you of breath, blood, and peace until dawn.
