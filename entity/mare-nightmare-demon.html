Who It Is

You are lying in bed, half-asleep, when a weight settles on your chest — heavy, crushing, impossible to throw off, while some presence crouches over you in the dark. You cannot move, you cannot cry out, you can barely breathe, and the feeling is so ancient and so precisely painstaking it has its own creature. She is called the Mare, and she has ridden sleepers for over a thousand years: she killed a king of Uppsala in the thirteenth century, she is the reason a tangle in your hair is called a 'mare-knot,' and she is why the word for a bad dream is literally a night-*mare*. Every culture that knows sleep paralysis has given it a name and a face — the Phi Am of Thailand, the Karabasan of Turkey, the Old Hag of Newfoundland. The mare's defining behavior is to sit, walk, or 'ride' on the chests of sleeping victims — humans and livestock alike — inducing nightmares, cold sweats, a suffocating heaviness, paralysis, and (in the most severe accounts) death by crushing or blood-draining. It rides horses to limp exhaustion, leaves its victims drenched in sweat, able only to groan while otherwise rendered speechless and spellbound, and unable to waken unless called by their baptismal name (German tradition). Name & Tongue

Etymology — The English 'mare' descends through Middle English from Old English mære (variants mare, mere, mær). It is reconstructed from Proto-Germanic *marōn; cognates span Old Norse mara (whence Danish mare, Icelandic mara, Swedish mara, Norwegian mare), Old Dutch mare (Dutch nachtmerrie), and Old High Germa

Pronunciation — English: /mɛər/ ('mare', rhyming with 'air'). The word is a homograph/homophone of 'mare' (a female horse), which has caused folk-etymological confusion but is etymologically unrelated — the demonic mare's name traces to PIE *mer- ('crush/press'), not to the horse-word.

Aliases — Night-mare, Nightmare, mære (Old English), mare / mere / mær (Old English variants), mara (Old Norse / Swedish / Norwegian / Icelandic), Mahr (German, masculine der Mahr)

Ownership — Shared Germanic and Slavic folklore tradition, spanning Old Norse/Icelandic, Danish, Norwegian, Swedish, German (High and Low), Dutch, English, Polish, Czech, Slovak, Kashubian, Cr

Powers
Chest-riding / Suffocation

The mare's signature power: it sits, walks, or lies on a sleeper's chest, inducing a crushing, suffocating weight, cold sweats, anxiety, and full or partial paralysis. Victims can groan but are otherwise speechless and spellbound, unable to wake unless called by their baptismal name (German tradition). Modern interpreters identify this with the medical phenomenon of sleep paralysis.

Limit: Traditionally ineffective against persons born in the daytime (Latvian lietuvēns) and partially countered by naming, moving a left toe, changing sleeping posture, or ward charms.

Shapeshifting

The mare can appear as a beautiful young woman, an ugly older woman, a man, or an animal — a cat, dog, owl, frog, hare, mouse, moth, mosquito, or (per Paul Devereux, compiling witch-and-trance lore) horse, ox, bird, bee, or wasp. Slavic tradition lists straw, grass, a mouse, dog, cat, mare, cow, white shadow, leather bag, and snake. It also famously takes the form of a female horse when captured.

Limit: Must re-enter and exit through the same opening (keyhole, crack, hole); can be bound with a saint's belt or halter (e.g. St Francis's belt) and ridden as a horse for years.

Blood-and-Energy Drain

The mare drains people, cattle, and horses of energy and/or blood at night. In Slavic lore it 'sticks in her tongue' and sucks blood from the victim's tongue, leaving them emaciated; it is likewise a 'sapsucker' of trees in Krakovian lore.

Limit: Victims survive but wake exhausted, weak, and in cold sweat; protection (mirrors, red ribbons, iron blades) guards livestock.

Entangling hair and foliage (mare-knots)

The mare entangles the hair of sleeping humans and beasts into matted tangles (marelocks: marflätor/martovor in Swedish, marefletter/marefloker in Norwegian), an old folk explanation for the hair condition Polish plait (Plica polonica). It likewise tangles tree branches, and names the twisted costal pines martallar ('mare-pines').

Limit: The tangling is cosmetic/harassment rather than lethal; it is the source of the characteristic 'mare-knots' in hair and branches.

Possession of dreamspace / nightmare-induction

The mare injects terrible dreams and a felt presence into the sleeper's mind, producing the very condition that gives 'nightmare' its name. The dream is not a random bad dream but a visitation, and the sleeper's sense of a malevolent watcher pressing down is the defining symptom.

Limit: Effectively a passive supernatural affliction rather than an attack spell; warded by prayer, signs of the cross, iron, and threshold charms.

Lethal riding (fatal crushing)

In the strongest attestations the mare can kill: the 13th-century Ynglinga saga recounts King Vanlandi of Uppsala dying under a mara that trod on his legs and then pressed down on his head fatally. Croatian/Slavic lore describes the mora visiting men in dreams, 'torturing them with desire before killing them.'

Limit: Fatal crushing is exceptional rather than routine; most accounts produce exhaustion, sweat, and paralysis on repeated visits.

Accounts I
The Death of King Vanlandi (Ynglinga saga)Snorri Sturluson's Heimskringla (Ynglinga saga)

Composed circa 1230 (events set in the legendary Swedish royal past)

King Vanlandi Sveigðisson of Uppsala married Drífa of Finland but broke his promise to return to her within three years. After ten years, the abandoned wife hired the Finnish sorceress Huld (or Hulda) either to lure the king back or, failing that, to assassinate him. Vanlandi, back in Uppsala, had barely gone to sleep before he complained that the nightmare 'rode him'. When his men held his head, the creature 'trod on his legs' at the point of breaking; when the retinue seized his feet, it pressed down fatally on his head. The mare killed the king in the night. The earliest and clearest attestation of the mare as a lethal agent; establishes the belief that a hostile other — here a sorceress's conjuration — can send the night-mare to kill. It grounds the mare as a weaponized supernatural attack, not merely a nuisance.

Thorkel Silver's Red-Horse Dream (Vatnsdæla saga)Vatnsdæla saga

13th century (interpolation dated circa 1300)

Thorkel Silver dreams of riding a red horse that barely touches the ground. He takes it as a positive omen, but his wife corrects him: a mare signifies a man's fetch (fylgja), and the red color bodes blood. The episode is notable because philologists regard the mare/fetch connection as an interpolation that 'confounds the words marr (horse) and mara (nightmare)'. Demonstrates the mare's association with the fetch (fylgja), the guardian/companion spirit of Norse belief, and the omen tradition around dreams. Scholars treat the explicit mare-equals-fetch gloss as a late textual layer.

Accounts II
Geirríðr the Night-Rider (Eyrbyggja saga)Eyrbyggja saga

13th century

The sorceress Geirríðr is accused of assuming the shape of a kveldriða or marlíðendr ('night-rider'/'ride-by-night') and inflicting serious trampling bruises on Gunnlaug Thorbjornsson. Commentators equate the marlíðendr with the mara, linking the mare to witch-flight and harmful out-of-body attacks. Establishes the mare as the shape of the malevolent witch's nocturnal excursion — the woman whose spirit leaves her body to ride and bruise sleepers, consistent with the later hamr/soul interpretations.

The Captured Môr and the Plugged Hole (German Low German legend, Rügen/Pomerania)Low German folklore collected in Pomerania and Rügen

Oral tradition recorded in the 19th century

The môr (mare) enters a house through a hole the carpenter forgot to plug. In one tale, a male môr tormenting a woman is captured by plugging the hole; he becomes her husband and fathers her children, but leaves after being told about the hole, returning just once a year thereafter. In another, a female môr caught by having green paint put on her hands is set permanently on an oak, which withers but always shivers. The mare also rides horses and leaves their manes forever matted. Exemplifies the folk technique of trapping the mare at its point of entry and, unusually, shows the captured male mare integrated (as husband) into human domestic life. It also explains the marelocks left on horses.

The Jilted Zmora and the St Francis Halter (Polish/Slavic legend)Polish folklore (Kraków / Lublin County variants)

Oral tradition recorded in the 19th century

A woman jilted in love — promised to a man who then married another — becomes a zmora (mare) at night, entering through a crack as a moth to blood-suck her chosen man and his wife. She is bound with a belt of St Francis, which her captor converts into a halter, turns her into a female horse, and rides her for seven years to her death. In a Kraków variant a farmhand marries the zmora but 'tests' how she suffers by plugging her conduit, only unplugging it once she is pregnant; in a Lublin variant a farmhand catches the zmora in cat-form with the St Francis belt, only to find it is a girl in love with him. The most fully developed narrative linking the mare to rejected love, witch-transformation, the moth/keyhole entry, and the exploitation of the same-hole-exit rule. It also shows the mare-captured-and-ridden-as-a-horse motif central to Slavic night-mare folklore.
