Who It Is

Imagine a god who doesn't just create the world, but dreams it into being, sustains it with every breath, and, when the scales of justice tip too far, descends into his own creation as a fish, a boar, a man-lion, or a blue-skinned prince playing a flute. That is Vishnu, the Preserver, the cosmic thread that weaves through the fabric of Hindu mythology. He is the calm eye of the cosmic storm, the silent watcher on the ocean of milk, and the hero of a thousand stories. From the ancient hymns of the Rigveda to the modern memes of the internet, Vishnu is the god who never stops adapting to save the universe.

Vishnu is the Preserver, the sustainer of cosmic order (dharma). He is characterized by his benevolence, compassion, and willingness to descend into the material world to restore balance whenever it is threatened. He is a loving father and protector, yet also a stern judge who can wield his discus to vanquish evil. He is seen as a calm, serene, and majestic presence, embodying grace and tranquility, but his avatars (especially Narasimha and Rama) display fierce and heroic qualities.

Powers
Cosmic Pervasion

Vishnu pervades the entire universe, existing in all things as the inner controller (Antaryami). He is the source of all creation and the ultimate reality.

Limit: None known.

Avatara (Descent)

Vishnu has the power to incarnate in various forms (avatars) to restore dharma and protect the righteous. The standard list of ten avatars (Dashavatara) includes Matsya (fish), Kurma (tortoise), Varaha (boar), Narasimha (man-lion), Vamana (dwarf), Parashurama (warrior with axe), Rama (prince), Krishna (divine statesman), Buddha (teacher), and Kalki (future warrior). Some traditions include Balarama instead of Buddha.

Limit: The avatar is bound by the rules of the form it takes, though it retains divine knowledge.

Sudarshana Chakra

A divine discus that can be used as a weapon to destroy evil. It returns to Vishnu after being thrown and cannot be stopped.

Limit: It is primarily used against the wicked.

Panchajanya Conch

A divine conch that produces the primordial sound 'Om' and is used to announce the beginning of cosmic cycles and to frighten demons.

Limit: None.

Yoga Nidra (Cosmic Sleep)

Vishnu's ability to enter a deep, creative sleep on the serpent Shesha, during which the universe is created and dissolved. This state is a metaphor for his transcendence and his role as the dreamer of the cosmos.

Limit: This is not a state of unconsciousness but of supreme awareness.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Vishnu is typically depicted as a four-armed male deity with a dark blue or blue-black complexion, symbolizing the infinite and the all-pervading nature of the cosmos. He is often shown standing or reclining on the serpent Shesha (Ananta), the cosmic serpent, who floats on the causal ocean. His four hands hold a conch (shankha), a discus (chakra), a mace (gada), and a lotus (padma). He wears yellow silk garments (pitambara), a crown (mukuta), and

Symbols — Vishnu's key symbols are: the Sudarshana Chakra (discus), representing the mind and the cycle of time; the Panchajanya (conch), representing the five elements and the primordial sound of creation; the Kaumodaki (mace), representing the power of knowledge and the force of time; the Padma (lotus), representing purity, liberation, and the unfolding of creation; the Shrivatsa mark on his chest, symbolizing his consort Lakshmi; and the Kaustubha

Domains — Vishnu's primary domain is Vaikuntha, the celestial abode, a transcendent realm of bliss and perfection. He is also associated with the cosmic ocean (Kshira Sagara, the ocean of milk) where he reclines on Shesha. As the Preserver, his domain is the entire universe, which he sustains through his will. He is the lord of the past, present, and future.

Accounts I
The Three Strides of VishnuRigveda

c. 1500-1200 BCE

In the Rigveda, Vishnu, as a solar deity, takes three strides, covering the earth, the atmosphere, and the heavens. These strides establish the cosmos and provide space for all beings to dwell. The third stride is in a realm beyond mortal comprehension, the highest abode. Vishnu is celebrated as the one who created a dwelling place for all creatures and is associated with the sun's journey across the sky.

The Churning of the Ocean (Samudra Manthana)Vishnu Purana, Bhagavata Purana, Mahabharata

c. 4th century CE onwards

The devas (gods) and asuras (demons) churn the cosmic ocean to obtain the nectar of immortality (amrita). They use Mount Mandara as the churning rod and the serpent Vasuki as the rope. Vishnu, in the form of Kurma (tortoise), supports the mountain on his back. During the churning, many treasures emerge, including Lakshmi, the goddess of fortune, who becomes Vishnu's consort. A terrible poison (halahala) also emerges, which Shiva drinks to save the universe. The devas and asuras temporarily cooperate, but the nectar is ultimately secured by the devas through Vishnu's cunning (Mohini, his female form). Lakshmi's emergence establishes her as the goddess of prosperity.

Accounts II
The Avatars of Vishnu (Dashavatara)Various Puranas (Agni Purana, Garuda Purana, etc.)

c. 4th-10th century CE

Vishnu descends to earth in various forms to restore dharma. The most famous avatars include: Matsya (fish) who saves the Vedas from a flood; Kurma (tortoise) who supports Mount Mandara during the churning; Varaha (boar) who rescues the earth from the demon Hiranyaksha; Narasimha (man-lion) who kills the demon Hiranyakasipu; Vamana (dwarf) who humbles the king Bali; Parashurama (warrior) who rid the world of corrupt Kshatriyas; Rama (prince) who defeats the demon Ravana; Krishna (divine statesman) who delivers the Bhagavad Gita; Buddha (teacher) who teaches compassion; and Kalki (future warrior) who will end the Kali Yuga. Each avatar re-establishes cosmic order and provides a model of righteous living. The concept of avatars is central to Vaishnavism.

The Birth of KrishnaBhagavata Purana, Mahabharata

c. 9th-10th century CE (Bhagavata Purana)

Krishna, the eighth avatar of Vishnu, is born to Devaki and Vasudeva in a prison cell in Mathura, where his uncle, the tyrant King Kamsa, plans to kill him. By divine intervention, Vasudeva transports the infant Krishna across the Yamuna river to Gokula, where he is raised by the cowherd couple Nanda and Yashoda. Krishna's childhood is filled with miracles, including the killing of demons sent by Kamsa, and his youth is marked by his playful and romantic exploits with the gopis (cowherd girls). Krishna grows up to play a pivotal role in the Mahabharata war, serving as Arjuna's charioteer and delivering the Bhagavad Gita. He is considered by many to be the supreme personality of Godhead.

Vishnu is a 'god' in the same sense as Zeus or Odin. — In most Hindu traditions, Vishnu is not just a god among gods, but the Supreme Being, the ultimate reality (Brahman) from which all else emanates. He is the creator, preserver, and destroyer, though the latter role is often delegated to Shiva.

The 'Trimurti' (Brahma, Vishnu, Shiva) are three separate, equally powerful gods. — The Trimurti is a concept that represents three aspects of a single supreme reality. In Vaishnavism, Vishnu is the supreme, and Brahma and Shiva are his agents of creation and destruction.

Vishnu's avatars are just 'reincarnations' like in the cycle of samsara. — An avatar is a deliberate, voluntary descent of the divine into the material world, not a forced rebirth. Vishnu is not bound by karma. He chooses to incarnate to restore dharma.

Vishnu is a 'peaceful' god who never uses violence. — While Vishnu is the Preserver and is generally benevolent, his avatars, particularly Narasimha and Parashurama, are fierce warriors who use extreme violence to destroy evil. He is a protector who will use force when necessary.
