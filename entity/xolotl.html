Who It Is

He is the god who wept at the dawn of the world, a dog-headed deity of fire and lightning who tried to run from his own fate. Xolotl, the Evening Star, is the dark twin of the great Quetzalcoatl, a psychopomp who guides the dead through the nine levels of the underworld, and a patron of everything monstrous, deformed, and unlucky. He is the tragic trickster who turned himself into a salamander to escape the gods, and the axolotl, the creature that never grows up, still bears his name.

As a psychopomp, Xolotl guides the souls of the dead through the perilous journey to Mictlan, the underworld. He is a guardian of the road, a companion to the dead, and a keeper of the monstrous and deformed. He is also a god of the ballgame, perhaps as a patron of the players or of the game's ritual significance. In his role as the Evening Star, he is the dark counterpart to his twin, Quetzalcoatl, representing the sun's descent into the underworld and the transformative power of the night.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Classical Nahuatl, likely derived from 'xolotl' meaning 'page' or 'servant', or possibly related to the root 'xoloa' (to follow). Some scholars link it to the word for 'dog'. The name is also the root of 'axolotl' (water dog/monster), the amphibian named in his honor.

Pronunciation — SHOH-lohtl (Nahuatl: [ˈʃolot͡ɬ])

Aliases — The Evening Star, Twin of Quetzalcoatl, Lord of the Ballgame, Xolotl the Dog-Headed, Psychopomp of Mictlan

Ownership — Aztec (Mexica) civilization of central Mexico, pre-Columbian era; also present in broader Nahua cultural traditions. His worship and mythology were recorded by early colonial chron

Powers
Psychopomp (Guide of the Dead)

Xolotl guides the souls of the dead through the nine levels of Mictlan, protecting them from the dangers of the underworld. This role is crucial for the deceased to reach their final resting place.

Limit: He cannot prevent the soul's ultimate fate or alter the trials of the underworld, only guide the soul through them.

Lord of Fire and Lightning

He is associated with heavenly fire and lightning, wielding these forces as divine powers. This connects him to the sun's destructive and creative energies.

Limit: His power over fire is often seen as secondary to that of Xiuhtecuhtli, the central fire god.

Shapeshifting

Xolotl is a trickster-like figure who can transform himself. He famously turned himself into a maize plant, a maguey (agave) plant, and an axolotl to avoid being sacrificed by the gods.

Limit: His transformations are a means of escape, but he is ultimately caught and sacrificed, showing that his powers have limits against the collective will of the other gods.

Patron of the Ballgame

Xolotl is associated with the Mesoamerican ballgame, which held deep ritual and cosmological significance. He may be a patron of the game, its players, or the ball itself, which was seen as a celestial body.

Limit: His specific role in the game is not fully understood; he may be a patron of the game's underworld associations rather than its sportive aspects.

God of Monsters, Deformities, and Misfortune

Xolotl presides over the monstrous, the deformed, and the ill-fated. He is a god of the abnormal and the misfit, offering a divine connection to those who are marginalized or burdened by physical or social affliction.

Limit: This role is not one of healing but of patronage, meaning he represents these states rather than curing them.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Xolotl is typically depicted as a man with a dog's head, often with a curved, wrinkled eye and a torch in his hand. He may be shown with the body of a man but with the feet of a dog, or as a canine with singed or deformed features. In some codices, he appears as a dog with a human torso or with skeletal elements, emphasizing his underworld connections. Symbols — Dog head, torch, curved eye, flint knife, skeletal elements, ball, and the axolotl (the amphibian named after him, which he was believed to have transformed into).

Domains — The evening sky (as Venus), the underworld (Mictlan), fire, lightning, twins, monsters, death, misfortune, sickness, deformities, and the ballgame. He is a liminal deity, presiding over the boundaries between life and death, day and night, order and chaos.

Accounts I
The Creation of the Fifth SunFlorentine Codex (Sahagún)

Post-classic period, recorded in the 16th century

After the gods created the world, they gathered at Teotihuacan to create the sun. The gods Nanahuatzin and Tecuciztecatl sacrificed themselves to become the sun and moon. The other gods, including Xolotl, were required to sacrifice themselves to set the sun in motion. Xolotl refused and wept, fleeing from the gods. He transformed himself into a maize plant, then a maguey plant, and finally into an axolotl (a salamander) to hide in the water. He was eventually found and sacrificed, his tears and his transformation marking him as a reluctant and tragic figure in the creation of the world. The sun was set in motion, and Xolotl became associated with the axolotl, the creature he transformed into. His reluctance and tears are a testament to the cost of creation and the inevitability of sacrifice.

Accounts II
Quetzalcoatl's Journey to MictlanCodex Vaticanus B, various colonial texts

Post-classic period, recorded in the 16th century

Xolotl accompanies his twin brother, Quetzalcoatl, on a journey to Mictlan, the underworld, to retrieve the bones of the previous humanity. Quetzalcoatl must undergo trials and face the lords of the dead. Xolotl serves as his guide and companion, helping him navigate the dangers of the underworld. Their journey is a foundational myth for the creation of the current humanity. Quetzalcoatl retrieves the bones, which are ground up and mixed with his blood to create the new human race. The journey solidifies the bond between the twins and establishes Xolotl as a psychopomp.

The Ballgame of the GodsCodex Borgia

Pre-Columbian, c. 1400-1500

Xolotl is depicted in scenes of the ritual ballgame, which was a cosmic struggle between light and dark, life and death. He is shown as a player or a patron of the game, and the game itself is a metaphor for the movement of celestial bodies and the cycle of life and death. His involvement underscores the game's connection to the underworld and to sacrifice. The ballgame is a recurring ritual that reenacts the cosmic drama, with Xolotl as a key figure in its underworld associations.

Xolotl is the Aztec god of dogs. — While he is depicted with a dog's head and is associated with the axolotl, he is not a 'god of dogs' in the sense of a patron of the animal. He is a god of fire, lightning, twins, monsters, death, and the underworld, who happens to have canine features. Dogs were associated with the underworld and the journey of the dead, which explains his form.

Xolotl is the same as the Maya god of death, Ah Puch. — While they share some similarities as psychopomps and underworld deities, they are distinct gods from different cultures. Xolotl is Aztec, and Ah Puch is Maya. Their similarities are due to the broader cultural connections in Mesoamerica, but they are not the same deity.

Xolotl is a demon or evil god. — Xolotl is not a demon or an evil god. He is a complex deity with both positive and negative aspects. He is a guide for the dead, a patron of the marginalized, and a necessary figure in creation. His associations with death and misfortune do not make him evil; they reflect the Aztec understanding of the cyclical nature of existence.
