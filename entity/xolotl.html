Who They Are

Xolotl is the dog-headed twin of the radiant Quetzalcoatl — and everything his brother is not. Quetzalcoatl is the morning star, rising; Xolotl is the evening star, going down: god of twilight, sickness, deformity, bad luck, and the necessary descent. His head is a dog's because the dog is the animal that walks souls to the underworld; his body in the codices is knock-kneed backward, his eyes have fallen from their sockets from weeping — and yet this wretched, limping, backwards god is one of Mesoamerica's great heroes. When the gods gathered at Teotihuacan to create the Fifth Sun, the new sun would not move across the sky until every god gave their blood. The executioner appointed was Xolotl. He killed them all, one by one — then, the myth says, wept himself eyeless and fled, transforming into a maize stalk, an agave, and finally the axolotl, the eternally juvenile salamander of Lake Xochimilco, to escape his own turn under the knife. He was caught, and died last, the executioner executed — and the sun moved. Xolotl is the god of the ugly necessary things: the descent, the sacrifice nobody volunteers for, the deformed twin of every shining thing.

Origins

Postclassic central Mexican tradition; paired with Quetzalcoatl as the Venus twins — morning and evening apparitions of the same planet, divinity split into glory and its shadow.

Attested in Aztec codices, Florentine CodexEra Postclassic Mesoamerica

Form

A dog-headed man with ragged ears, backward feet, and empty weeping eyes; as the axolotl, a pink smiling salamander that never grows up — the god's last, permanent disguise.

Powers

Psychopomp descent — he escorted the sun itself through the underworld each night and guided Quetzalcoatl's raid on the bones of the dead to create humanity; mastery of transformation; patronage of the ballgame, twins, and the malformed.

Weaknesses & Wards

His own dread — the fleeing executioner transformed thrice and was still found; his story concedes that the descent cannot be dodged, even by the god of dodging.

Cultural Significance

The Aztecs bred the xoloitzcuintli — the Mexican hairless dog, named for him — to guide their dead, and the critically endangered axolotl carries his name and his myth in Mexican identity, science, and conservation campaigns to this day.
