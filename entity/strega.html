Who It Is

The Strega is the Italian folk-witch — a complex figure who stands at the intersection of pre-Roman nature worship, medieval Catholic demonization, and surviving folk magic practice. Unlike the uniformly demonized witch of Northern European trials, the Southern Italian Strega was often a respected (if feared) community figure who cured the malocchio (evil eye), prepared love potions, blessed crops, and communicated with the dead. The Strega's power was hereditary, passed through the female line, and could be used for both healing and cursing. Her tools included olive oil (for diagnosing the evil eye in water), red ribbons, keys, knives (for cutting curses), and herbs gathered under specific moon phases. The Strega tradition survived intense persecution during the Counter-Reformation (the Roman Inquisition executed hundreds of accused witches in the 16th-17th centuries), but folk-healing persisted in rural areas well into the 20th century, disguised as folk Catholicism. In the late 20th century, the 'Stregheria' revival (popularized by Raven Grimassi and others) claimed to reconstruct pre-Christian Italian witchcraft, blending surviving folk practices with neopagan ritual. However, academic folklorists distinguish between the historical Strega (a local practitioner of syncretic folk magic) and the modern religious reconstruction. The Strega represents the endurance of pre-Christian feminine magical traditions in Italy — the cunning woman who could 'do and undo,' bless and curse, living at the margins of the village but essential to its spiritual health.
