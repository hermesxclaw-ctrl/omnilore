Who It Is

In the darkness between the sky father and the earth mother, a child grew restless. Tāne, the god of forests and birds, would not kill his parents—he would tear them apart with his own legs, setting the stage for the world as we know it. His act of separation shaped the cosmos, but it also unleashed storms, created humanity, and left a legacy of love, incest, and death that still echoes in Māori tradition.

Tāne is portrayed as thoughtful and non-violent, preferring separation over killing his parents. He is persistent and successful where his brothers failed. He is also a progenitor, creating humans and other beings. In some stories, he is shown as having a sexual relationship with his daughter, which he may have entered unknowingly, but this act brings death into the world.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Tāne' is generally interpreted as 'male' or 'man' in Māori. The fuller forms 'Tāne-mahuta' ('Tāne of the forest') and 'Tāne-nui-a-Rangi' ('Tāne great son of Rangi') reflect his domains and parentage.

Pronunciation — Approximately TAH-neh (with a long 'a' and a short 'e'; the macron indicates a long vowel).

Aliases — Tāne-mahuta, Tāne-nui-a-Rangi, Tāne-te-waiora, Tāne te wānanga, Tāne i te pō

Form & Signs

Appearance — Tāne is not described in physical detail in the sources. He is often depicted as a powerful male figure, capable of great physical feats (separating his parents). In art, he may be shown with forest motifs, birds, or as a tall, strong man. No specific iconography is attested in the provided material.

Symbols — Tāne is associated with forests, birds, and trees. The kauri tree (Agathis australis) is sacred to him, and the famous tree Tāne Mahuta is named after him. He is also associated with the sun, moon, and stars, which he placed in the sky.

Domains — Tāne is the god of forests and birds, and by extension, the god of the dwelling places of birds and the trees they inhabit. He is also associated with the heavens, having placed the stars, moon, and sun. His realm includes the forest, and he is sometimes called Tāne-mahuta, 'Tāne of the forest'.

Accounts I
Separation of Rangi and PapaGrey 1956:2-3; Orbell 1998:145

Mythological (pre-colonial)

The sky father Rangi and earth mother Papa lie in a tight embrace, leaving their children in darkness. Tū proposes killing them, but Tāne suggests separating them. After his brothers fail, Tāne succeeds by using his legs to push them apart. Rangi rises to the sky, and Papa remains below. Tāne then decorates his father with stars, moon, and sun. The world is created with light and space. Tāwhirimātea, angry at the separation, attacks the earth and sea with storms, causing Tangaroa's descendants to flee, leading to ongoing conflict between Tāne and Tangaroa.

Accounts II
Tāne and the creation of humansOrbell 1998:145

Mythological

Tāne seeks a wife but finds only non-human females, fathering insects, birds, and plants. He then creates a woman by moulding her from soil. In some traditions, this woman is Hine-tītama, whom he later marries unknowingly as his daughter. Humans are created. The incest leads to Hine-tītama becoming the goddess of death, Hine-nui-te-pō.

Tāne and the baskets of knowledgeOrbell 1998:145

Mythological

Tāne ascends to the heavens and brings back three baskets of knowledge to humanity. Humans gain knowledge.

Tāne is the supreme god of Māori. — Tāne is an important god, but there is no single supreme deity in Māori mythology; various gods have different domains.

Tāne created humans from clay like in Greek mythology. — In Māori tradition, Tāne created a woman from soil, but the process is not identical to Greek myths; the context is unique.

Tāne is the god of peace and beauty in all Polynesian cultures. — In Tahiti, Tane is the god of peace and beauty, but in Māori tradition, he is primarily the god of forests and birds.
