Etymology

The name 'Tāne' is generally interpreted as 'male' or 'man' in Māori. The fuller forms 'Tāne-mahuta' ('Tāne of the forest') and 'Tāne-nui-a-Rangi' ('Tāne great son of Rangi') reflect his domains and parentage.

Cultural Ownership

Tāne is a central deity in Māori mythology (Aotearoa/New Zealand). He is also known in other Polynesian cultures: in Tahiti as Tane, god of peace and beauty; in Hawaii as Kāne. This dossier focuses on Māori traditions.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars have suggested that Tāne's role as creator of humans overlaps with that of Tiki, but traditions vary. The relationship between Tāne and Kāne (Hawaiian) is generally accepted as related, but specific attributes differ.

Archive Dossier — Approach

In the darkness between the sky father and the earth mother, a child grew restless. Tāne, the god of forests and birds, would not kill his parents—he would tear them apart with his own legs, setting the stage for the world as we know it. His act of separation shaped the cosmos, but it also unleashed storms, created humanity, and left a legacy of love, incest, and death that still echoes in Māori tradition.

Tāne (Tāne-mahuta) is the Māori god of forests, birds, and all living creatures — one of the most important deities in the Māori pantheon. He is the son of Rangi-nui (Sky Father) and Papa-tū-ā-nuku (Earth Mother).

Who It Is

Tāne (Tāne-mahuta) is the Māori god of forests, birds, and all living creatures — one of the most important deities in the Māori pantheon. He is the son of Rangi-nui (Sky Father) and Papa-tū-ā-nuku (Earth Mother). When Rangi and Papa were locked in an eternal embrace, Tāne alone had the strength to separate them, pushing his parents apart with his shoulders while lying on his mother and pushing up with his legs. This act created the world of light (Te Ao Mārama). Tāne is also credited with creating the first woman, Hine-wahine (or Hine-hau-one), from the clay of the earth, breathing life into her nostrils. She bore his children, and their daughter Hine-tītama fled to the underworld when she learned her father was also her husband. Tāne brought knowledge to humanity, specifically the three baskets of knowledge (te kete tuauri, te kete tuatea, te kete aronui) from the heavens. He is associated with the sun, the stars (especially the sun as a star that Tāne hung in the sky), and the insect world. In Hawaii, he is Kāne, one of the four great gods.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

Tāne is not described in physical detail in the sources. He is often depicted as a powerful male figure, capable of great physical feats (separating his parents). In art, he may be shown with forest motifs, birds, or as a tall, strong man. No specific iconography is attested in the provided material.

Symbols & Iconography

Tāne is associated with forests, birds, and trees. The kauri tree (Agathis australis) is sacred to him, and the famous tree Tāne Mahuta is named after him. He is also associated with the sun, moon, and stars, which he placed in the sky.

Behaviour & Personality

Tāne is portrayed as thoughtful and non-violent, preferring separation over killing his parents. He is persistent and successful where his brothers failed. He is also a progenitor, creating humans and other beings. In some stories, he is shown as having a sexual relationship with his daughter, which he may have entered unknowingly, but this act brings death into the world.

Domains & Habitats

Tāne is the god of forests and birds, and by extension, the god of the dwelling places of birds and the trees they inhabit. He is also associated with the heavens, having placed the stars, moon, and sun. His realm includes the forest, and he is sometimes called Tāne-mahuta, 'Tāne of the forest'.

Version Differences

There are variations in the creation of humans: some say Tāne made the first man Tiki, others say he made the first woman from soil. The story of his marriage to Hine-tītama is not universal; some traditions omit it. The number of baskets of knowledge varies (three in some accounts).

Origins

Tāne is the son of Rangi-nui (Sky Father) and Papa-tū-ā-nuku (Earth Mother). He and his brothers lived in darkness between their parents. Tāne tried to separate them, failing initially, then succeeded by lying on his back and pushing with his legs. He then created the first woman and obtained the three baskets of knowledge.

Limitations

Not specified.

Contradictions

Creation of life

Source Version Orbell 1998:145

Description

Tāne fathered insects, birds, and plants with non-human females, and created the first woman from soil.

Limitations

He initially failed to find a suitable wife; his first creations were not human.

Contradictions

Some traditions say he created the first man Tiki instead.

Acquisition of knowledge

Source Version Orbell 1998:145

Description

Tāne brought down three baskets of knowledge from the heavens, which are the source of human knowledge.

Limitations

Not detailed in source.

Contradictions

Number of baskets may vary.
