The Approach

In the winter of 1890s Michigan, you could buy a jail cell without committing a crime. For a few cents slipped to the sheriff, tramps called it a 'boodle jail' — a warm room, card games, and a walk in the yard. The word they used for the price of admission, boodle, had already traveled from a Dutch estate office to the diamond fields of South Africa and the bribe-stuffed aldermanic chambers of New York, meaning simply: money, especially money that shouldn't be yours.

Content advisory. No graphic content. Historical discussion of poverty, vagrancy laws, criminalization of homelessness, and Gilded Age political corruption and bribery.
Identity

boodle

Pronunciation English /ˈbuːdəl/ BOO-duhl. Dutch boedel /ˈbudəl/. Afrikaans boedel /ˈbudəl/.

Known As

boodle (money)boodle jailboodlingboodler

Etymology

From Dutch boedel 'property, estate, assets, inheritance' (from Middle Dutch boedel, related to buodel). Afrikaans boedel retains the Dutch meaning 'estate, insolvent estate'. English slang boodle 'money, especially illicit gains; crowd, lot; bundle' is widely attributed by lexicographers to Dutch/Afrikaans boedel via South African English and/or directly from Dutch in American English in the 19th century. The Oxford English Dictionary records earliest English sense 'money' from mid-19th century US. American political sense 'bribe money, graft' and verb 'to boodle' / noun 'boodler' proliferated 1870s-1890s.

Cultural Ownership

No single cultural owner. Etymon is Dutch; intermediary is Afrikaans; documented widespread slang use in two distinct English varieties: South African English (general slang for money) and American English (19th-early 20th century slang for money/bribe money and the institutional term 'boodle jail'). The term belongs to general slang lexicography, not to a closed folklore or franchise canon.

Disputed Identifications

1) Homonymy with boodle meaning 'crowd, lot, caboodle' (as in 'kit and boodle' / 'kit and caboodle') — OED treats as same etymon but some dictionaries treat as separate development. 2) Wikidata type 'Fictional character' (Q107665862) appears to be a misclassification; no attested fictional character named Boodle matching heroic-legend archetype was located in major databases; the term is lexical, not a character. 3) Confusion with 'boodle' as military slang for bundle/kit. 4) Folk etymology linking US 'boodle' to 'bundle' without Dutch mediation is disputed.

Description

Appearance

No physical appearance. As a lexical item, boodle is attested in print as a mass noun ('a lot of boodle', 'the boodle') and in compounds: boodle-jail, boodle-box, boodle alderman, boodler. In American newspapers 1870s-1900, it appears in political cartoons as bags of money labeled 'BOODLE'. In South African English, it appears in colloquial speech and literature as everyday slang for cash, without necessary illicit connotation.

Symbols & Iconography

Associated iconography is monetary: bags, rolls, and bundles of banknotes/coins. In Gilded Age US press, 'boodle' was visualized as sacks of dollars exchanged for votes or contracts (e.g., Tweed Ring, Milwaukee boodle scandals). No traditional folklore iconography; no deity or heraldic symbol. In tramp culture, no symbol — the phrase 'boodle jail' itself functioned as a euphemism for a pay-to-stay lockup.

Behaviour & Personality

Linguistic behavior: As slang, boodle is informal, often jocular or cynical. In US 19th-century use, strongly pejorative when linked to politics ('boodle aldermen', 'boodling'). In South African English, neutral to affectionate slang for money ('a bit of boodle'). Grammatically, it generates productive derivatives: to boodle (to take/give bribes), boodler (bribe-taker), boodling (bribery). Pragmatically, it camouflages illicit money as generic 'stuff' — a euphemism that normalizes graft.

Domains & Habitats

Domains: 1) Dutch/Afrikaans legal-administrative domain: boedel = estate in probate/insolvency law (boedelberedderaar = executor, boedelscheiding). 2) South African English colloquial domain: general commerce and everyday speech. 3) American English 1850-1930: two habitats — a) urban politics and newspapers (corruption, graft), b) transient/hobo subculture and vagrancy system (boodle jails in Michigan, Ohio, Illinois). Habitat declined after 1930s as 'boodle' was replaced by 'dough', 'bread', 'graft'.

Version Differences

Dutch/Afrikaans boedel = neutral legal term for estate/assets, no slang sense of bribe. South African English boodle = general slang for money (not necessarily illicit). American English boodle = often specifically illicit money, counterfeit money, or bribe money; also 'whole lot' sense. 'Boodle jail' sense is American tramp cant, not South African. Verb 'to boodle' and noun 'boodler' are American political slang, not South African. Pronunciation stable; spelling stable (variant 'budel' not attested in English).

Powers & Abilities

Euphemistic concealment (linguistic function)

Source VersionAmerican political English 1870s-1900s; attested in OED and Green's citations

As slang for bribe money or illicit gains, 'boodle' allows speakers to refer to corruption without legal specificity. Contemporary newspapers used it to allege graft while maintaining plausible deniability.

Limitations

Not a supernatural power. Effectiveness depended on audience familiarity; outside US political context, meaning could be misread as generic money.

Contradictions

None - function consistently attested.

Institutional access (boodle jail)

Source VersionMichigan county jails ca. 1880s-1900s, per Davies 1908 and tramp memoirs

Payment of a small 'boodle' fee to a jailer granted non-prisoners (tramps, hobos) temporary lodging, food, and relative safety in winter without formal arrest. Described by Davies as including card-playing, singing, smoking, reading, and walks.

Limitations

Access was conditional, informal, and at jailer's discretion; not a legal right. Could be revoked; conditions varied by county; not available in summer or in jurisdictions that enforced vagrancy strictly.

Contradictions

Some historians question how widespread the system was vs. anecdotal; Davies's account is primary but single-observer.

Stories & Myths

From Boedel to Boodle: The Estate That Became Money

Source OED; Philippa et al. Etymologisch Woordenboek van het Nederlands; DSAECulture Dutch → Afrikaans → South African English / American EnglishPeriod Middle Dutch (13th c.) to 19th century

Summary

Dutch boedel denoted a person's total property or inheritance, especially as administered after death or bankruptcy. Dutch colonists carried the word to the Cape Colony (1652 onward), where it became Afrikaans boedel with the same legal meaning. English speakers in South Africa, in daily contact with Dutch/Afrikaans legal and commercial language, adopted boedel phonetically as boodle, broadening it from 'estate' to 'money in general'. Lexicographers note this as the likely source for South African slang, with parallel or subsequent transmission to American English via Dutch immigrants or via independent broadening of 'bundle/property' to 'money'.

Participants

Dutch speakers

Afrikaans speakers

South African English speakers

Consequences

Established boodle as enduring South African slang for money, recorded in dictionaries from the 19th century to present; provided etymological anchor for American senses.

Variants

Alternative hypothesis: American boodle directly from English 'bundle' or 'caboodle' without Afrikaans mediation; OED notes both Dutch and bundle theories but favors Dutch boedel.

The Gilded Age Boodle: Bribery as a System

Source US newspapers 1870s-1890s; Green's Dictionary citations; contemporary political historiesCulture American political culture, Gilded AgePeriod 1870-1900

Summary

In post-Civil War America, 'boodle' became the standard newspaper word for graft. 'Boodle aldermen' sold streetcar franchises, paving contracts, and vice protection for cash. Major scandals were labeled 'boodle' scandals: the Milwaukee Bay View boodle (1880s), St. Louis boodling investigations, and New York's Tweed-adjacent aldermanic boodling. To 'boodle' meant to bribe; a 'boodler' was a corrupt official. Newspapers printed 'boodle' openly because it captured public cynicism that politics was a market.

Participants

City aldermen and legislators

Streetcar and utility corporations

Newspaper editors and cartoonists

Reform prosecutors

Consequences

Lexicalized corruption: 'boodler' entered dictionaries; anti-boodling laws and investigations followed; term remained in US slang until replaced by 'graft' and 'payola' in the 20th century.

Variants

Some cities used 'boodle' for counterfeit money specifically; others for any illicit fund. Regional newspapers varied in spelling and compound forms.

Boodle Jails: Buying a Winter in a Michigan Lockup

Source W. H. Davies, The Autobiography of a Super-Tramp (1908); US tramp memoirs and hobo ethnographiesCulture American tramp/hobo subculture, MidwestPeriod 1880s-1910s

Summary

County jails in Michigan and neighboring states operated an informal winter economy. On payment of a small fee (a few cents to a quarter) to the sheriff or turnkey — the 'boodle' — a tramp could be housed as a lodger rather than a prisoner. He was not booked, could come and go for walks, and shared the jail's warmth, food, and sociability with other tramps. The system exploited the vagrancy laws: jails had empty cells in winter and sheriffs supplemented income; tramps avoided freezing in boxcars or workhouses.

Participants

Tramps and hobos

County sheriffs and jailers

Local taxpayers

Consequences

Created a seasonal, semi-legal shelter system for transient workers before modern homeless shelters; documented in tramp autobiographies as a humane alternative to the workhouse; declined as professional social work and stricter jail regulation emerged in the 1910s-1920s.

Variants

Not all Michigan jails participated; some required a sham charge of vagrancy; fees and privileges varied by county. Davies names several Michigan towns but not all jail records survive to corroborate.

W. H. Davies's Michigan Winter

Source Davies, The Autobiography of a Super-Tramp, Chapters 12-15; Davies biographies (e.g., Richard J. Stonesifer, 1963)Culture Welsh-British tramp-poet in AmericaPeriod 1891-1893 (published 1908)

Summary

Welsh poet W. H. Davies (1871-1940), then a young tramp, crossed the Atlantic and tramped the United States. In Michigan he learned of boodle jails and deliberately used them to overwinter. He describes a routine of card-playing, singing, smoking, reading, relating experiences, and occasional exercise or walks outside — a convivial, almost club-like existence. Davies presents the jail as preferable to the road in deep winter and as evidence of American jailers' pragmatism. His account is the most detailed literary description of the boodle-jail institution.

Participants

W. H. Davies

Fellow tramps in Michigan jails

Michigan jailers

Consequences

Provided primary literary evidence for boodle jails; helped cement Davies's reputation when the book was championed by George Bernard Shaw; remains the most cited source for the term 'boodle jail' in slang dictionaries.

Variants

Davies's memory written years later; some details may be composite. Other tramp writers (e.g., Bart Kennedy, Josiah Flynt) describe similar pay-to-stay arrangements under different names.

Boodle in South Africa: Everyday Money

Source Dictionary of South African English on Historical Principles (1996); Jean Branford; Rhodes University DSAE citations; South African literature and pressCulture South African EnglishPeriod 19th century to present

Summary

Among English-speaking South Africans, boodle became unremarkable slang for money, without the American implication of corruption. Citations show usage in both white and Black South African English, in mining, farming, and urban contexts: 'to make a bit of boodle', 'out of boodle'. The word coexists with other Afrikaans-derived slang (e.g., 'dorp', 'braai') and illustrates the deep lexical influence of Afrikaans on South African English. Unlike the US, the political 'boodler' sense never took hold in South Africa.

Participants

Afrikaans speakers

English-speaking South Africans

Lexicographers

Consequences

Boodle remains listed as South Africanism in modern dictionaries; demonstrates divergent evolution of the same etymon in two English varieties.

Variants

Pronunciation and currency vary; some South African sources gloss boodle as 'a lot of money' rather than any amount; younger speakers increasingly replace it with 'cash' or 'bucks'.

The Bundle Question: Kit and Caboodle

Source OED s.v. 'caboodle, n.' and 'kit, n.'; Wentworth & Flexner Dictionary of American Slang; Green's DictionaryCulture American English slangPeriod 1840s-present

Summary

A parallel American phrase 'the whole kit and caboodle' (also 'kit and boodle') meaning 'the entire lot' has been linked by some etymologists to Dutch boedel ('property, lot') via 'boodle' meaning crowd or collection. The phrase first appears in the 1840s-1880s. Whether 'boodle' meaning 'crowd/lot' and 'boodle' meaning 'money' are the same word or two homonyms is debated, but most modern dictionaries treat them as one word with broadening from 'estate/property' to 'collection of things' to 'money'.

Participants

American slang speakers

Lexicographers

Consequences

If correct, it unifies three slang senses (money, crowd, bundle) under one Dutch etymon; if not, it suggests independent developments that converged in form.

Variants

Folk etymology derives 'caboodle' from 'caboodle' as corruption of 'boodle'; other theories derive it from 'kit and budget' or 'kit and boiling'.

Criminalization of poverty and vagrancy laws

Source Davies 1908; DePastino, Citizen Hobo (2003); Kusmer, Down and Out, On the Road (2002)

Description

Boodle jails existed because homelessness was criminalized. Tramps could be arrested for vagrancy and sentenced to hard labor; paying to stay in jail was a workaround that still depended on a carceral institution. The system reveals lack of non-punitive shelter and the use of jails as de facto poorhouses.

Scholarly Context

Historians of homelessness note that boodle jails, while described warmly by Davies, were part of a coercive vagrancy regime that disproportionately targeted unemployed men, immigrants, and Black migrants.

Gilded Age graft and disenfranchisement

Source US newspaper archives 1880s-1890s; Green's Dictionary citations for 'boodler'

Description

The 'boodle' system of bribery diverted public resources (streetcar lines, paving, waterworks) to private interests. Working-class residents paid higher fares and taxes while aldermen pocketed boodle. Reform press used the term to expose corruption but also to sensationalize.

Scholarly Context

Political historians treat boodling as emblematic of machine politics and weak regulation before Progressive Era reforms.

Contradictions & Disputes

Etymology of American boodle

Position A

American boodle 'money/bribe' derives from Dutch boedel via Afrikaans or direct Dutch contact in America (New York Dutch).

Position A — evidence

Phonological match boedel > boodle; semantic match 'estate/property' > 'money/lot'; attested Dutch/Afrikaans transmission for South African sense; OED and DSAE favor Dutch origin; Green notes Dutch origin.

Position B

American boodle derives from English dialect 'bundle' / 'caboodle' (i.e., a bundle of money) without Dutch mediation, or from 'buddle' (to bundle).

Position B — evidence

Early US citations for 'boodle' meaning 'crowd/lot' predate clear money sense; 'kit and caboodle' appears mid-19th c.; some 19th-century slang dictionaries gloss boodle as 'bundle'; lack of direct documentary link between Dutch immigrants and US political slang.

Scholarly Consensus

Major modern dictionaries (OED, Green's, DSAE) favor Dutch boedel as source, with 'bundle/crowd' sense as semantic extension of same word, not separate etymon. Bundle-only theory is minority but still cited as alternative.

How widespread were boodle jails?

Position A

Boodle jails were a common, recognized institution across Michigan and the Midwest in the 1880s-1900s.

Position A — evidence

Davies's detailed account naming multiple Michigan jails; corroborating tramp memoirs (Flynt, Kennedy) describing similar arrangements; slang dictionaries treat term as established cant.

Position B

Boodle jails were rare, localized, and possibly exaggerated by tramp memoirists; most jails did not offer pay-to-stay lodging.

Position B — evidence

Surviving county jail records rarely document informal payments; legal codes do not authorize lodger status; historians note memoirists may generalize from few experiences.

Scholarly Consensus

No systematic quantitative study exists; consensus is that informal pay-to-stay lodging occurred but its frequency and geographic extent remain uncertain — attested but not proven to be widespread.

Is Boodle a fictional character?

Position A

Wikidata Q107665862 classifies Boodle as fictional character, heroic-legend archetype.

Position A — evidence

Wikidata entry type statement (no supporting references found in entry).

Position B

Boodle is a slang term for money/institution, not a character; no fictional character named Boodle with heroic-legend traits was located in franchise or folklore databases.

Position B — evidence

Scholarly Consensus
