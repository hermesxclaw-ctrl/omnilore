Who It Is

In the winter of 1890s Michigan, you could buy a jail cell without committing a crime. For a few cents slipped to the sheriff, tramps called it a 'boodle jail' — a warm room, card games, and a walk in the yard. The word they used for the price of admission, boodle, had already traveled from a Dutch estate office to the diamond fields of South Africa and the bribe-stuffed aldermanic chambers of New York, meaning simply: money, especially money that shouldn't be yours.

Linguistic behavior: As slang, boodle is informal, often jocular or cynical. In US 19th-century use, strongly pejorative when linked to politics ('boodle aldermen', 'boodling'). In South African English, neutral to affectionate slang for money ('a bit of boodle'). Grammatically, it generates productive derivatives: to boodle (to take/give bribes), boodler (bribe-taker), boodling (bribery). Pragmatically, it camouflages illicit money as generic 'stuff' — a euphemism that normalizes graft.

Powers
Euphemistic concealment (linguistic function)

As slang for bribe money or illicit gains, 'boodle' allows speakers to refer to corruption without legal specificity. Contemporary newspapers used it to allege graft while maintaining plausible deniability.

Limit: Not a supernatural power. Effectiveness depended on audience familiarity; outside US political context, meaning could be misread as generic money.

Institutional access (boodle jail)

Payment of a small 'boodle' fee to a jailer granted non-prisoners (tramps, hobos) temporary lodging, food, and relative safety in winter without formal arrest. Described by Davies as including card-playing, singing, smoking, reading, and walks.

Limit: Access was conditional, informal, and at jailer's discretion; not a legal right. Could be revoked; conditions varied by county; not available in summer or in jurisdictions that enforced vagrancy strictly.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No physical appearance. As a lexical item, boodle is attested in print as a mass noun ('a lot of boodle', 'the boodle') and in compounds: boodle-jail, boodle-box, boodle alderman, boodler. In American newspapers 1870s-1900, it appears in political cartoons as bags of money labeled 'BOODLE'. In South African English, it appears in colloquial speech and literature as everyday slang for cash, without necessary illicit connotation.

Symbols — Associated iconography is monetary: bags, rolls, and bundles of banknotes/coins. In Gilded Age US press, 'boodle' was visualized as sacks of dollars exchanged for votes or contracts (e.g., Tweed Ring, Milwaukee boodle scandals). No traditional folklore iconography; no deity or heraldic symbol. In tramp culture, no symbol — the phrase 'boodle jail' itself functioned as a euphemism for a pay-to-stay lockup.

Domains — Domains: 1) Dutch/Afrikaans legal-administrative domain: boedel = estate in probate/insolvency law (boedelberedderaar = executor, boedelscheiding). 2) South African English colloquial domain: general commerce and everyday speech. 3) American English 1850-1930: two habitats — a) urban politics and newspapers (corruption, graft), b) transient/hobo subculture and vagrancy system (boodle jails in Michigan, Ohio, Illinois). Habitat declined after 1930

Accounts I
From Boedel to Boodle: The Estate That Became MoneyOED; Philippa et al. Etymologisch Woordenboek van het Nederlands; DSAE

Middle Dutch (13th c.) to 19th century

Dutch boedel denoted a person's total property or inheritance, especially as administered after death or bankruptcy. Dutch colonists carried the word to the Cape Colony (1652 onward), where it became Afrikaans boedel with the same legal meaning. English speakers in South Africa, in daily contact with Dutch/Afrikaans legal and commercial language, adopted boedel phonetically as boodle, broadening it from 'estate' to 'money in general'. Lexicographers note this as the likely source for South African slang, with parallel or subsequent transmission to American English via Dutch immigrants or via independent broadening of 'bundle/property' to 'money'. Established boodle as enduring South African slang for money, recorded in dictionaries from the 19th century to present; provided etymological anchor for American senses.

The Gilded Age Boodle: Bribery as a SystemUS newspapers 1870s-1890s; Green's Dictionary citations; contemporary political histories

1870-1900

In post-Civil War America, 'boodle' became the standard newspaper word for graft. 'Boodle aldermen' sold streetcar franchises, paving contracts, and vice protection for cash. Major scandals were labeled 'boodle' scandals: the Milwaukee Bay View boodle (1880s), St. Louis boodling investigations, and New York's Tweed-adjacent aldermanic boodling. To 'boodle' meant to bribe; a 'boodler' was a corrupt official. Newspapers printed 'boodle' openly because it captured public cynicism that politics was a market. Lexicalized corruption: 'boodler' entered dictionaries; anti-boodling laws and investigations followed; term remained in US slang until replaced by 'graft' and 'payola' in the 20th century.

Boodle Jails: Buying a Winter in a Michigan LockupW. H. Davies, The Autobiography of a Super-Tramp (1908); US tramp memoirs and hobo ethnographies

1880s-1910s

County jails in Michigan and neighboring states operated an informal winter economy. On payment of a small fee (a few cents to a quarter) to the sheriff or turnkey — the 'boodle' — a tramp could be housed as a lodger rather than a prisoner. He was not booked, could come and go for walks, and shared the jail's warmth, food, and sociability with other tramps. The system exploited the vagrancy laws: jails had empty cells in winter and sheriffs supplemented income; tramps avoided freezing in boxcars or workhouses. Created a seasonal, semi-legal shelter system for transient workers before modern homeless shelters; documented in tramp autobiographies as a humane alternative to the workhouse; declined as professional social work and stricter jail regulation emerged in the 1910s-1920s.

Accounts II
W. H. Davies's Michigan WinterDavies, The Autobiography of a Super-Tramp, Chapters 12-15; Davies biographies (e.g., Richard J. Stonesifer, 1963)

1891-1893 (published 1908)

Welsh poet W. H. Davies (1871-1940), then a young tramp, crossed the Atlantic and tramped the United States. In Michigan he learned of boodle jails and deliberately used them to overwinter. He describes a routine of card-playing, singing, smoking, reading, relating experiences, and occasional exercise or walks outside — a convivial, almost club-like existence. Davies presents the jail as preferable to the road in deep winter and as evidence of American jailers' pragmatism. His account is the most detailed literary description of the boodle-jail institution. Provided primary literary evidence for boodle jails; helped cement Davies's reputation when the book was championed by George Bernard Shaw; remains the most cited source for the term 'boodle jail' in slang dictionaries.

Boodle in South Africa: Everyday MoneyDictionary of South African English on Historical Principles (1996); Jean Branford; Rhodes University DSAE citations; South African literature and press

19th century to present

Among English-speaking South Africans, boodle became unremarkable slang for money, without the American implication of corruption. Citations show usage in both white and Black South African English, in mining, farming, and urban contexts: 'to make a bit of boodle', 'out of boodle'. The word coexists with other Afrikaans-derived slang (e.g., 'dorp', 'braai') and illustrates the deep lexical influence of Afrikaans on South African English. Unlike the US, the political 'boodler' sense never took hold in South Africa. Boodle remains listed as South Africanism in modern dictionaries; demonstrates divergent evolution of the same etymon in two English varieties.

The Bundle Question: Kit and CaboodleOED s.v. 'caboodle, n.' and 'kit, n.'; Wentworth & Flexner Dictionary of American Slang; Green's Dictionary

1840s-present

A parallel American phrase 'the whole kit and caboodle' (also 'kit and boodle') meaning 'the entire lot' has been linked by some etymologists to Dutch boedel ('property, lot') via 'boodle' meaning crowd or collection. The phrase first appears in the 1840s-1880s. Whether 'boodle' meaning 'crowd/lot' and 'boodle' meaning 'money' are the same word or two homonyms is debated, but most modern dictionaries treat them as one word with broadening from 'estate/property' to 'collection of things' to 'money'. If correct, it unifies three slang senses (money, crowd, bundle) under one Dutch etymon; if not, it suggests independent developments that converged in form.
