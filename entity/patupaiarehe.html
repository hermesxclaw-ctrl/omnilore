The patupaiarehe are the fairy people of Aotearoa: pale-skinned, red- or fair-haired beings who live on the cloud-wrapped summits and ridgelines — Pirongia, the Coromandel ranges, the misty crowns the Maori pointedly did not settle — in villages of their own that no one finds twice. They are daylight-averse (mist and twilight are their traveling weather), they eat their food raw (cooked food and its smell are poison and insult to them, and the reliable way to drive them off), and they are masters of the koauau and putorere — the flutes — whose music drifting down a ridge at dusk is both their signature and their lure.

Who It Is

The patupaiarehe are the fairy people of Aotearoa: pale-skinned, red- or fair-haired beings who live on the cloud-wrapped summits and ridgelines — Pirongia, the Coromandel ranges, the misty crowns the Maori pointedly did not settle — in villages of their own that no one finds twice. They are daylight-averse (mist and twilight are their traveling weather), they eat their food raw (cooked food and its smell are poison and insult to them, and the reliable way to drive them off), and they are masters of the koauau and putorere — the flutes — whose music drifting down a ridge at dusk is both their signature and their lure. Their relations with humans run the full fairy ledger: they abducted women (children of such unions, tradition says, carried the pale skin and reddish hair — urukehu — that appears in some Maori lineages), lured men with flute-song, and punished trespass on their peaks with mist that never lifted; but they also taught. The great gift of the patupaiarehe is the net: the hero Kahukura, watching them haul fish by night, joined their line in the dark and delayed them past dawn — and in their panicked flight they left the net behind, and with it the knowledge of net-making, which the tribes hold to this day. When Europeans arrived — pale-skinned, strange-voiced — some iwi reached for the obvious word: pakepakeha, cousins of the mist folk. The name pakeha may carry the fairies inside it still.

Origins

Maori tradition of pre-human or parallel peoples of the high country; possibly braided from memories of earlier populations, atmospheric phenomena, and the universal grammar of the hidden folk.

Attested in Maori oral traditionEra Precolonial to present

Form

Tall or small by region, always pale, red- or golden-haired, never tattooed (a marked difference from Maori custom), dressed in pale bark-cloth, seen at the edge of mist and never in full sun.

Powers

Mist-craft and weather-timing, flute-music that binds hearts and feet, abduction and enchantment, mountain-village concealment, and the taught technologies — netting above all — that leak from their world into ours.

Weaknesses & Wards

Cooked food and its steam repel them absolutely; sunlight strips their power; kokowai (red ochre) smeared on skin or thresholds is proof against their taking.

Field Notes

❀ Kahukura's net-theft-by-overtime is one of the great technology-transfer myths of the Pacific.

❀ Mist on Pirongia is still called the patupaiarehe's cloak in Waikato talk.

❀ The possible link between pakeha (European) and pakepakeha (mist folk) would make New Zealand's settlers officially named after fairies — an etymology both peoples have opinions about.

The Approach

On misty days in Aotearoa, when the fog rolls down from the mountains, some Māori say you can hear the faint, sweeter-than-human music of the patupaiarehe – pale-skinned, red-haired forest folk who shun sunlight and steam, and who may have been here long before the first canoes arrived.

Content advisory. Contains references to violence, abduction, and death in traditional narratives. Some accounts involve cultural conflict and displacement. Handle with respect for Māori cultural sensitivities.

Etymology

The name 'patupaiarehe' is of uncertain etymology. Some interpretations suggest 'patu' (to strike) and 'paiarehe' (a type of fairy or goblin), but this is speculative. 'Turehu' is a common synonym. 'Pakehakeha' is a variant that has been theorized as a possible origin for the word 'Pākehā' (European).

Cultural Ownership

Patupaiarehe are beings from Māori oral tradition, indigenous to Aotearoa (New Zealand). They are not a pan-Polynesian concept, though similar beings exist in other Polynesian cultures.

Disputed Identifications

Some early European observers speculated that patupaiarehe were a real pre-Māori people, perhaps of lighter skin, but this is not supported by archaeological evidence. Māori traditions themselves vary: some describe them as supernatural, others as an earlier people.

Description

Appearance

Patupaiarehe are typically described as pale-skinned (white, albino, or reddish like red ochre), with blonde or red hair. They are often said to be the same stature as ordinary people, though some iwi describe them as small (Ngāi Tūhoe) or very tall (Whanganui, Kaiheraki as a giantess). They never have tattoos (moko). Their eyes may be black or blue, and the women are often described as beautiful with fair complexions and shining hair.

Symbols & Iconography

Patupaiarehe are associated with mist, fog, and dawn/dusk. They are linked to the kōauau and pūtōrino (flutes), and their singing. They are also associated with certain trees like the rewarewa and plants like rangiora. Their dwellings are invisible to humans.

Behaviour & Personality

They are generally nocturnal or active on misty days, as sunlight can be fatal to them. They prefer raw food and are averse to steam and fire. They can be hostile to humans who intrude on their lands, but also may interact peacefully, even falling in love with humans. They are skilled in weaving and music, which is sweeter than human music. They are known to abduct humans, but also to be tricked by them.

Domains & Habitats

They live in deep forests and hilly or mountainous regions, in large guarded communities. Specific North Island locations include Mount Pirongia, the Coromandel Range (especially Mount Moehau), the Urewera Ranges, the Waitākere Ranges, and Mount Ngongotahā. In the South Island, they inhabit the hills of Banks Peninsula, the heights above Lyttelton Harbour, and mountains around Lake Wakatipu and the Tākitimu Mountains. They prefer dry places, avoiding humidity.

Version Differences

There are significant regional variations: North Island traditions emphasize their presence in forests and mountains, with complex social structures; South Island traditions (Kāi Tahu) often replace them with the Maero, but still have patupaiarehe stories. Physical descriptions vary: small vs. tall, skin colour, hair colour. Their nature also varies: some are aggressive, others peaceful. The story of their origin also differs: some say they were the original inhabitants, others that they are supernatural beings.

Kahukura and the Fishing Nets

Source Ngāi Tūhoe tradition, recorded in various sourcesCulture MāoriPeriod Pre-European

Summary

A man named Kahukura came across patupaiarehe pulling in their fishing nets at night. He offered to help, and they worked together. When they realized he was a mortal, they fled.

Patupaiarehe

Hinerehia and the Gift of Weaving

Source Hauraki Māori tradition, recorded by James CowanCulture Māori (Hauraki)Period Pre-European

Summary

Hinerehia, a patupaiarehe woman from Moehau, fell in love with a Māori man and lived with him at Mōtuihe. She wove only at night. The village women, wanting to learn her skill, tricked her into weaving past dawn by covering the windows and silencing the birds. When she realized she had been deceived, she fled back to Moehau in a cloud, leaving her husband and children.

Variants

The Five Hunters and the Calabash

Source Ngāti Rongoū tradition, recorded by Hoani NaheCulture Māori (Hauraki)Period Pre-European

Summary

Five men found a calabash hanging from a rewarewa tree. They cut it down and took it. Later, they found a fence of supplejack enclosing a patupaiarehe garden. They caught a pig and cooked it, but when they opened the hāngī, there was only skin and bone. That night, the man who carried the calabash was dragged from his home by patupaiarehe, who pulled up trees as he clung to them, and drowned him. The other four were also killed.

Participants

Patupaiarehe

Consequences

The men died as punishment for taking the calabash and trespassing.

Variants

The calabash may have been a decoy or a trap.

Violence against humans

Source Ngāti Rongoū tradition

Description

The story of the five hunters ends with all five men being killed, including one drowned. This is a punishment for trespassing and theft.

Scholarly Context

This is a cautionary tale about respecting the boundaries of the supernatural.

Displacement from lands

Source Hoani Nahe

Description

Patupaiarehe were driven from Mount Moehau by Māori, leading to their hostility.

Scholarly Context

This may reflect historical conflicts between different groups, or a metaphor for the arrival of Māori in New Zealand.

Abduction

Source Arawa tradition

Description

Patupaiarehe are said to abduct humans, as in the story of Īhenga, who was nearly kept against his will.

Scholarly Context

This is a common motif in fairy lore worldwide.

This dossier synthesizes published ethnographic and historical sources on patupaiarehe, focusing on 19th and early 20th century recordings of Māori oral traditions. It covers North and South Island variants, key figures, and cultural context. Modern reception is noted but not exhaustive.

Cultural Significance

The patupaiarehe explain the urukehu strain in some lineages, the origin of net-fishing, and the wariness owed to certain summits — several of which (Pirongia most famously) carry their traditions in living memory and local signage.
