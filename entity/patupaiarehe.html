Who It Is

On misty days in Aotearoa, when the fog rolls down from the mountains, some Māori say you can hear the faint, sweeter-than-human music of the patupaiarehe – pale-skinned, red-haired forest folk who shun sunlight and steam, and who may have been here long before the first canoes arrived.

They are generally nocturnal or active on misty days, as sunlight can be fatal to them. They prefer raw food and are averse to steam and fire. They can be hostile to humans who intrude on their lands, but also may interact peacefully, even falling in love with humans. They are skilled in weaving and music, which is sweeter than human music. They are known to abduct humans, but also to be tricked by them.

Powers
Mist manipulation

They can draw mist to themselves, and use it for concealment and travel. They are active on misty days.

Limit: They are vulnerable to direct sunlight, which can be fatal.

Invisibility of dwellings

Their buildings and structures are invisible to human eyes.

Limit: Humans can sometimes stumble upon them accidentally.

Supernatural strength

They are able to pull trees out of the ground when abducting a human.

Limit: Not consistently mentioned.

Musical ability

Their flute music and singing are sweeter than human music.

Limit: Music is often heard on foggy days.

Ability to abduct humans

They can drag humans away, even through obstacles.

Limit: Humans can escape with cleverness (e.g., shark oil).

Weakness to fire and steam

They avoid steam and fire, and are repelled by the smell of shark oil.

Limit: This is a vulnerability that can be exploited.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Patupaiarehe are typically described as pale-skinned (white, albino, or reddish like red ochre), with blonde or red hair. They are often said to be the same stature as ordinary people, though some iwi describe them as small (Ngāi Tūhoe) or very tall (Whanganui, Kaiheraki as a giantess). They never have tattoos (moko). Their eyes may be black or blue, and the women are often described as beautiful with fair complexions and shining hair.

Symbols — Patupaiarehe are associated with mist, fog, and dawn/dusk. They are linked to the kōauau and pūtōrino (flutes), and their singing. They are also associated with certain trees like the rewarewa and plants like rangiora. Their dwellings are invisible to humans.

Domains — They live in deep forests and hilly or mountainous regions, in large guarded communities. Specific North Island locations include Mount Pirongia, the Coromandel Range (especially Mount Moehau), the Urewera Ranges, the Waitākere Ranges, and Mount Ngongotahā. Accounts I
Kahukura and the Fishing NetsNgāi Tūhoe tradition, recorded in various sources

Pre-European

A man named Kahukura came across patupaiarehe pulling in their fishing nets at night. He offered to help, and they worked together. When they realized he was a mortal, they fled. Kahukura learned the art of net-making from the patupaiarehe.

Hinerehia and the Gift of WeavingHauraki Māori tradition, recorded by James Cowan

Pre-European

Hinerehia, a patupaiarehe woman from Moehau, fell in love with a Māori man and lived with him at Mōtuihe. She wove only at night. The village women, wanting to learn her skill, tricked her into weaving past dawn by covering the windows and silencing the birds. When she realized she had been deceived, she fled back to Moehau in a cloud, leaving her husband and children. The knowledge of weaving was passed to Māori, but Hinerehia left.

Accounts II
The Five Hunters and the CalabashNgāti Rongoū tradition, recorded by Hoani Nahe

Pre-European

Five men found a calabash hanging from a rewarewa tree. They cut it down and took it. Later, they found a fence of supplejack enclosing a patupaiarehe garden. They caught a pig and cooked it, but when they opened the hāngī, there was only skin and bone. That night, the man who carried the calabash was dragged from his home by patupaiarehe, who pulled up trees as he clung to them, and drowned him. The other four were also killed. The men died as punishment for taking the calabash and trespassing.

Īhenga and the Ngongotahā PatupaiareheTe Matehaere of Arawa, recorded by James Cowan

Pre-European (600 years ago)

Īhenga, an early explorer, encountered the patupaiarehe of Mount Ngongotahā. They were curious and wanted to keep him, with a beautiful woman desiring him as a husband. He drank water from a calabash, sensed a trap, and fled. He escaped by smearing foul-smelling shark oil on his skin. Īhenga escaped and later settled the area.

Kaiheraki of TākitimuHone Te Paina of Oraka, recorded by James Cowan

Pre-European

A hunter named Hautapu encountered a patupaiarehe woman named Kaiheraki in the Tākitimu Mountains. She was a spectrelike giantess who strode along the mountaintops on misty days. The story is incomplete in the source, but it suggests an encounter between the hunter and the woman. Unknown, as the story is cut off.

Patupaiarehe are the same as European fairies. — While they share some traits with European fairies, they are specific to Māori culture and have unique characteristics, such as aversion to steam and their association with mist.

The word 'Pākehā' comes from 'Pakehakeha'. — This is a theory, not proven. The etymology of 'Pākehā' is uncertain.

Patupaiarehe are ghosts or spirits of the dead. — They are living beings with their own communities, not ghosts.

All patupaiarehe are malevolent. — Many stories show them as neutral or even benevolent, capable of love and teaching.
