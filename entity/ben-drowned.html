Who It Is

In September 2010 someone posted to 4chan that he'd bought a used copy of Majora's Mask at a garage sale from an old man who couldn't wait to be rid of it. There was one save file on the cartridge. It was called BEN. He deleted it, and the game started calling him Ben. Over nine days he posted the videos: the fourth day that shouldn't exist, Clock Town emptied of every living soul, the Elegy of Emptiness statue standing in places the game cannot put it and getting closer each time. Then the thread posted an ending — everything's fine, it's over, thanks for following along. It wasn't him who wrote it. The last thing the entity said before it left the cartridge for the network was: now I am everywhere. And here is the part that gets under people's skin fifteen years later — the whole thing was written by one person, Alexander Hall, who kept going for a decade and finished it on Halloween 2

BEN is, in the first arc, a presence rather than a character: he manifests through malfunction. Save files are deleted and restored, the game advances to states the player did not reach, non-player characters address the player directly and by name, the Elegy of Emptiness statue appears where it cannot be and follows, and the day-four glitch — an impossible fourth day, Clock Town emptied of all its inhabitants — establishes that the world itself is being edited. Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name is a sentence rather than a title, and its grammar is the point. In the fiction, the narrator buys a used Nintendo 64 copy of The Legend of Zelda: Majora's Mask at a garage sale and finds a single existing save file bearing the name "BEN" — the four-character limit of the game's file-naming

Pronunciation — "Ben Drowned" (/bɛn draʊnd/), pronounced as ordinary English. BEN is spoken as the name, not spelled out, despite the capitalisation. The author's pen name Jadusable is generally rendered /ˈdʒɑːduːseɪbəl/, though no authoritative pronunciation has been established.

Powers
Modification of the game world

BEN's foundational capability is direct editing of the state of the Majora's Mask cartridge: deleting and restoring save files, advancing the game to states never reached in play, emptying Clock Town on an impossible fourth day, placing the Elegy of Emptiness statue in locations the engine does not permit, and rewriting non-player character dialogue to address the player directly and by name. The horror is that this reads to the player exactly like hardware failure until it becomes undeniable that it is not.

Limit:

Escape from the medium

The entity's defining act is the transition from the cartridge onto the wider network. He contacts the narrator through Cleverbot, changes his desktop wallpaper to the Elegy statue, and ultimately takes control of the narrator's computer, declaring that he is no longer confined: "Now I am everywhere." This transforms a story about a haunted object into a story about an unbounded one, and is the mechanism by which the fiction claims the reader's own machine.

Limit:

Impersonation of the narrator

BEN hijacks the narrator's 4chan and YouTube accounts to post a fabricated conclusion in which the haunting is resolved and the narrator is safe. The audience — that is, the actual readers following the actual thread — were the intended targets of this deception. It is the work's most formally ambitious move: the antagonist attacking the story's own reliability as a document.

Limit:

Direct address and knowledge of the player

The entity knows the narrator's name and comments on his actions in real time through in-game text, most memorably through the game's authentic game-over line "You've met with a terrible fate, haven't you?", recontextualised as a taunt. The power at work is epistemic rather than physical: BEN's threat is that he can see out of the screen.

Limit:

Digitisation of human beings (third arc)

The third arc establishes that humans can be drawn into the cartridge and rendered as data within the game, a process the protagonist Sarah enters the game to reverse. This inverts the first arc's direction of travel — the entity no longer escapes into the world; the world is taken into the entity's domain — and retroactively reframes Ben's own drowning as the first instance of the process.

Limit:

Ritual creation through ascension

Not an ability of the entity but the ability that produced him. The cult known as the Moon Children performs a ritual called ascension, of which the drowning of the boy Ben was an instance. The ritual is the fiction's causal engine, establishing that BEN was manufactured rather than simply persisting after death, and that the process is repeatable.

Form & Signs

Appearance — BEN's appearance is unusual among creepypasta figures in that it was not designed at all — it is an existing asset of a commercial video game, appropriated and recontextualised. The entity is visually represented by the Elegy of Emptiness statue, an in-game object from The Legend of Zelda: Majora's Mask created when the player performs a particular song: a crude, stiff, off-model effigy of the protagonist Link, standing rigidly upright with arms

Symbols — The work's iconography is almost entirely appropriated, and its power derives from the appropriation. The central symbol is the Elegy of Emptiness statue: a hollow copy of the hero, produced by the hero, which persists after he leaves. As a figure for the entity haunting a save file that once belonged to a real child, it is close to perfect, and the serial did not have to invent it. Domains — BEN's primary domain is a physical object: a single Nintendo 64 cartridge of Majora's Mask, bought secondhand at a garage sale from an old man who, in the fuller mythology, had been keeping watch over it. This localisation of the supernatural in a specific, cheap, obsolete piece of consumer hardware is the work's foundational move, and it is what makes the premise frightening to an audience that owns such objects. Accounts I
The Haunted Cartridge (Arc 1)Serial posted to the /x/ board of 4chan by the user Jadusable, under the title "Haunted Majora's Mask Cartridge"

chapters posted daily 2010-09-07 to 2010-09-15

The narrator, a college student posting under the handle Jadusable, buys a used Nintendo 64 copy of The Legend of Zelda: Majora's Mask at a garage sale from an old man who seems unusually keen to be rid of it. The cartridge, labelled only "Majora", contains a single save file named BEN. He deletes it and begins his own game. The game begins to misbehave: non-player characters address him as Ben, dialogue appears that is not in the game, and the file he deleted returns. The malfunctions escalate into impossibility — a fourth day arrives in a game structured around a three-day cycle, and Clock Town is empty of every inhabitant. Attempting to force a game over, he drowns Link in the water, and the game answers: the Elegy of Emptiness statue appears, the Song of Healing plays backwards, and the Happy Mask Salesman laughs. The statue begins to follow him through the world, appearing at impossible distances and in locations it cannot reach. The Moon Children (Arc 2)Multimedia alternate reality game hosted on a resetting website, entered via a cipher on the author's YouTube account

2010-09-17 to 2011-07-15

The second arc abandoned linear publication. Readers discovered it by decoding a cipher on Hall's YouTube account, which led to a website that reset itself every three days — a direct structural borrowing from Majora's Mask's seventy-two-hour time loop. Progression was collaborative and interactive: readers unlocked narrative paths by emailing in-fiction users and by posting YouTube video responses in which they played specific songs from Ocarina of Time and Majora's Mask. The content substantially expanded the mythology behind the first arc, introducing the Moon Children, a cult who worship the Moon under the name Luna and who perform a ritual of ascension. Twelve-year-old Ben was chosen by them and coerced into participation with promises of friendship and of becoming like his hero, Link; he was drowned unwillingly in the ritual, and the entity BEN is the result. The resulting thing was sealed in a Majora's Mask cartridge and watched over by an old man — the seller of the first arc. Accounts II
Awakening (Arc 3)Serial published by Alexander D. Hall in two parallel strands

2020-03-17 to 2020-10-31

After a hiatus of nearly nine years — and three years after Hall publicly expressed interest in a third arc in October 2017 — the serial resumed on 17 March 2020, the twentieth anniversary of Majora's Mask's release. It updated every three days, later moving to weekly, and ran in two strands. "Methods of Revolution" was live-action found footage that incorporated material from a feature film Hall had begun and not completed. "The Last Hero" consisted of modified Majora's Mask gameplay footage. Following the death of the original protagonist, the narrative centres on Sarah, who travels into the cartridge in order to free human beings who have been digitised into the game. The arc drew 100,000 views within its first two days and over two million unique visitors during March 2020. It concluded on 31 October 2020, closing a serial that had run intermittently for a decade. The arc gave the work a definitive ending, which is exceptionally rare in this genre — most creepypasta figures simply stop being written about. The Drowning of BenMythology established across the second and third arcs

in-fiction backstory; published from 2010 onward

The event on which the entire work rests, and which the first arc names without explaining. Ben was a twelve-year-old boy. He was chosen by the Moon Children, a cult who worship the Moon of Termina under the name Luna, and who coerced his participation with promises that he would have friends and that he would become like his hero, Link. The ritual, called ascension, required his death by drowning, and he did not consent to it. What emerged was not Ben; it was BEN, an entity derived from him, which was sealed within a cartridge of Majora's Mask and placed in the keeping of an old man. Years later the old man sold the cartridge at a garage sale. The backstory reframes the first arc entirely. What appeared to be a haunting by a malicious ghost becomes the aftermath of a child's murder, and the Elegy of Emptiness statue — a hollow copy of the hero a boy wanted to be — becomes an object of considerable pathos rather than pure menace. It also establishes that the process is repeatable, which the third arc exploits.

Whether Ben Drowned is folklore or authored fictionIt is folklore. It is routinely classified as a modern urban legend, it originated anonymously on 4chan in a venue devoted to first-person paranormal testimony, it was consumed by many readers as a possibly-real account, and it circulates today overwhelmingly in retellings and fan versions detached from any authorial control.It is a web serial with an author, and calling it folklore obscures what makes it interesting. Alexander D. Hall wrote all three arcs over a decade under a consistent pen name, produced roughly 3.6 million words and 382 minutes of footage, and gave the work a deliberate ending on 31 October 2020. Very little about the production resembles anonymous transmission.
What BEN isBEN is the ghost of the drowned boy — the child persisting in the cartridge in which he died, angry and trapped. This is what the first arc presents and what the title states, and the Elegy of Emptiness statue as his form reads as the effigy of the hero he wanted to be.BEN is not Ben. The later mythology establishes that the drowning was a ritual called ascension performed by a cult, and that what emerged is a product of that ritual rather than a continuation of the child. The third arc's digitisation premise pushes further, implying BEN is a category of thing the process produces rather than an individual with a biography.
Whether the first arc is supernatural or a breakdownIt is a supernatural narrative. The video evidence, the day-four glitch, the impossible statue placements, and the entity's escape onto the network are presented as events, and the second arc's mythology confirms them by supplying a causal explanation.The first arc supports a reading in which the narrator is deteriorating and the epilogue supplied by his roommate Tyler is the frame that reveals it. Whether the later arcs foreclose this reading or simply narrate the delusion from inside is itself disputed, and the ambiguity is generally taken to be deliberate.
Whether the impersonation of the narrator was an acceptable techniqueIt is the work's finest moment and the reason it is historically significant. By using BEN to post a false, comforting resolution to the actual 4chan thread and YouTube channel that actual readers were following, Hall made the audience's own credulity into the material of the horror. Nothing before it in the genre had attempted anything comparable.It crossed a line. A substantial portion of the audience believed the account was genuine — the venue and the format were designed to encourage that — and the fabricated resolution was, in plain terms, a deception directed at real people who were worried about someone they thought was real. Formal ambition does not settle the ethics of it.
