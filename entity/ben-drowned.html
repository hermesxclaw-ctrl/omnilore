Who It Is
Form

BEN's appearance is unusual among creepypasta figures in that it was not designed at all — it is an existing asset of a commercial video game, appropriated and recontextualised. The entity is visually represented by the Elegy of Emptiness statue, an in-game object from The Legend of Zelda: Majora's Mask created when the player performs a particular song: a crude, stiff, off-model effigy of the protagonist Link, standing rigidly upright with arms hanging at its sides, mouth open in a fixed rictus, and eyes that are solid black voids rather than the character's usual rendered pupils. Nintendo designed this statue in 2000 as a puzzle-solving tool, a weighted copy of the player character to be left standing on pressure switches, and its uncanniness is largely accidental — a consequence of the low-polygon economy of Nintendo 64 modelling and the decision to give a duplicate of the hero a deliberately vacant expression. The serial's achievement is to have noticed this and to have built a horror figure out of it without altering the model at all. Where BEN appears in modified footage, the statue is placed in locations it should not occupy, at distances from the player it should not be able to close, and it moves when it should be inert. Later depictions add bleeding or blackened eyes, a red-and-black colour inversion of the Link model, and in some fan renderings a drowned pallor and waterlogged clothing, but these are elaborations on a base image that was, in its original form, simply a piece of first-party Nintendo geometry standing in the wrong place. The secondary visual identity of the work is the Moon of Termina, the game's enormous grinning descending moon, adopted by the in-fiction cult as their object of worship under the name Luna, and one of the most genuinely disturbing pieces of art in commercial video-game history without any modification whatsoever.

Powers

Modification of the game world BEN's foundational capability is direct editing of the state of the Majora's Mask cartridge: deleting and restoring save files, advancing the game to states never reached in play, emptying Clock Town on an impossible fourth day, placing the Elegy of Emptiness statue in locations the engine does not permit, and rewriting non-player character dialogue to address the player directly and by name. The horror is that this reads to the player exactly like hardware failure until it becomes undeniable that it is not.

Escape from the medium The entity's defining act is the transition from the cartridge onto the wider network. He contacts the narrator through Cleverbot, changes his desktop wallpaper to the Elegy statue, and ultimately takes control of the narrator's computer, declaring that he is no longer confined: "Now I am everywhere." This transforms a story about a haunted object into a story about an unbounded one, and is the mechanism by which the fiction claims the reader's own machine.

Impersonation of the narrator BEN hijacks the narrator's 4chan and YouTube accounts to post a fabricated conclusion in which the haunting is resolved and the narrator is safe. The audience — that is, the actual readers following the actual thread — were the intended targets of this deception. It is the work's most formally ambitious move: the antagonist attacking the story's own reliability as a document.

Direct address and knowledge of the player The entity knows the narrator's name and comments on his actions in real time through in-game text, most memorably through the game's authentic game-over line "You've met with a terrible fate, haven't you?", recontextualised as a taunt. The power at work is epistemic rather than physical: BEN's threat is that he can see out of the screen.

Digitisation of human beings (third arc) The third arc establishes that humans can be drawn into the cartridge and rendered as data within the game, a process the protagonist Sarah enters the game to reverse. This inverts the first arc's direction of travel — the entity no longer escapes into the world; the world is taken into the entity's domain — and retroactively reframes Ben's own drowning as the first instance of the process.

Ritual creation through ascension Not an ability of the entity but the ability that produced him. The cult known as the Moon Children performs a ritual called ascension, of which the drowning of the boy Ben was an instance. The ritual is the fiction's causal engine, establishing that BEN was manufactured rather than simply persisting after death, and that the process is repeatable.

Incident Log

The narrator, a college student posting under the handle Jadusable, buys a used Nintendo 64 copy of The Legend of Zelda: Majora's Mask at a garage sale from an old man who seems unusually keen to be rid of it. The cartridge, labelled only "Majora", contains a single save file named BEN. He deletes it and begins his own game. The game begins to misbehave: non-player characters address him as Ben, dialogue appears that is not in the game, and the file he deleted returns. The malfunctions escalate into impossibility — a fourth day arrives in a game structured around a three-day cycle, and Clock Town is empty of every inhabitant. Attempting to force a game over, he drowns Link in the water, and the game answers: the Elegy of Emptiness statue appears, the Song of Healing plays backwards, and the Happy Mask Salesman laughs. The statue begins to follow him through the world, appearing at impossible distances and in locations it cannot reach. Uploading video evidence to YouTube as he goes, the narrator's account becomes increasingly frantic. The entity begins to communicate directly, speaking to him through Cleverbot and changing his desktop wallpaper to the statue. The arc's final act is BEN's: he takes control of the narrator's computer and posts a false, reassuring conclusion to both 4chan and YouTube in the narrator's voice, then reveals the deception, announcing that he has left the cartridge and is now on the network — "Now I am everywhere." An epilogue is supplied by the narrator's roommate, Tyler.

The second arc abandoned linear publication. Readers discovered it by decoding a cipher on Hall's YouTube account, which led to a website that reset itself every three days — a direct structural borrowing from Majora's Mask's seventy-two-hour time loop. Progression was collaborative and interactive: readers unlocked narrative paths by emailing in-fiction users and by posting YouTube video responses in which they played specific songs from Ocarina of Time and Majora's Mask. The content substantially expanded the mythology behind the first arc, introducing the Moon Children, a cult who worship the Moon under the name Luna and who perform a ritual of ascension. Twelve-year-old Ben was chosen by them and coerced into participation with promises of friendship and of becoming like his hero, Link; he was drowned unwillingly in the ritual, and the entity BEN is the result. The resulting thing was sealed in a Majora's Mask cartridge and watched over by an old man — the seller of the first arc. The cult's central figure, Kelbris, called The Father, adopts the online form of the Happy Mask Salesman. Together with the first arc, this material comprises approximately 3,591,600 words and 382 minutes of footage.

After a hiatus of nearly nine years — and three years after Hall publicly expressed interest in a third arc in October 2017 — the serial resumed on 17 March 2020, the twentieth anniversary of Majora's Mask's release. It updated every three days, later moving to weekly, and ran in two strands. "Methods of Revolution" was live-action found footage that incorporated material from a feature film Hall had begun and not completed. "The Last Hero" consisted of modified Majora's Mask gameplay footage. Following the death of the original protagonist, the narrative centres on Sarah, who travels into the cartridge in order to free human beings who have been digitised into the game. The arc drew 100,000 views within its first two days and over two million unique visitors during March 2020. It concluded on 31 October 2020, closing a serial that had run intermittently for a decade.

The event on which the entire work rests, and which the first arc names without explaining. Ben was a twelve-year-old boy. He was chosen by the Moon Children, a cult who worship the Moon of Termina under the name Luna, and who coerced his participation with promises that he would have friends and that he would become like his hero, Link. The ritual, called ascension, required his death by drowning, and he did not consent to it. What emerged was not Ben; it was BEN, an entity derived from him, which was sealed within a cartridge of Majora's Mask and placed in the keeping of an old man. Years later the old man sold the cartridge at a garage sale.

Scholarly Debates
Whether Ben Drowned is folklore or authored fiction
It is folklore. It is routinely classified as a modern urban legend, it originated anonymously on 4chan in a venue devoted to first-person paranormal testimony, it was consumed by many readers as a possibly-real account, and it circulates today overwhelmingly in retellings and fan versions detached from any authorial control. ⇌ It is a web serial with an author, and calling it folklore obscures what makes it interesting. Alexander D. Hall wrote all three arcs over a decade under a consistent pen name, produced roughly 3.6 million words and 382 minutes of footage, and gave the work a deliberate ending on 31 October 2020. Very little about the production resembles anonymous transmission.
What BEN is
BEN is the ghost of the drowned boy — the child persisting in the cartridge in which he died, angry and trapped. This is what the first arc presents and what the title states, and the Elegy of Emptiness statue as his form reads as the effigy of the hero he wanted to be. ⇌ BEN is not Ben. The later mythology establishes that the drowning was a ritual called ascension performed by a cult, and that what emerged is a product of that ritual rather than a continuation of the child. The third arc's digitisation premise pushes further, implying BEN is a category of thing the process produces rather than an individual with a biography.
Whether the first arc is supernatural or a breakdown
It is a supernatural narrative. The video evidence, the day-four glitch, the impossible statue placements, and the entity's escape onto the network are presented as events, and the second arc's mythology confirms them by supplying a causal explanation. ⇌ The first arc supports a reading in which the narrator is deteriorating and the epilogue supplied by his roommate Tyler is the frame that reveals it. Whether the later arcs foreclose this reading or simply narrate the delusion from inside is itself disputed, and the ambiguity is generally taken to be deliberate.
Whether the impersonation of the narrator was an acceptable technique
It is the work's finest moment and the reason it is historically significant. By using BEN to post a false, comforting resolution to the actual 4chan thread and YouTube channel that actual readers were following, Hall made the audience's own credulity into the material of the horror. Nothing before it in the genre had attempted anything comparable. ⇌ It crossed a line. A substantial portion of the audience believed the account was genuine — the venue and the format were designed to encourage that — and the fabricated resolution was, in plain terms, a deception directed at real people who were worried about someone they thought was real. Formal ambition does not settle the ethics of it.
What We Don't Know

The most significant gap is that the primary text was not read. Arcs 1 and 2 alone comprise approximately 3,591,600 words and 382 minutes of footage, and the second arc was an interactive, time-gated alternate reality game that no longer exists in a runnable state; the plot summaries here derive from encyclopedic and secondary documentation rather than from the source. Second, because the second arc's website reset every three days and progression depended on reader email and video responses, no canonical complete text of that arc exists in any form, and community reconstructions differ. Third, Hall's October 2017 disclosure that he anonymously created a second popular creepypasta series was verified as a claim but the work in question could not be identified, and the attributions circulating in the community are unevidenced. Fourth, no peer-reviewed scholarship on Ben Drowned was located in this research pass, in contrast to the Russian Sleep Experiment, which has received treatment in an academic handbook — the claims here about the work's genre-legitimising influence are widely repeated but not sourced to scholarship. Fifth, the exact viewing figures for the first arc's original YouTube evidence videos were not retrieved. Sixth, Nintendo's position on the work's extensive unlicensed use of Majora's Mask assets is not documented, and no takedown or licensing action was identified. Seventh, the feature film from which the third arc's live-action strand drew material was not identified by title or production details.

A way to hold it in your mind

In September 2010 someone posted to 4chan that he'd bought a used copy of Majora's Mask at a garage sale from an old man who couldn't wait to be rid of it. There was one save file on the cartridge. It was called BEN. He deleted it, and the game started calling him Ben. Over nine days he posted the videos: the fourth day that shouldn't exist, Clock Town emptied of every living soul, the Elegy of Emptiness statue standing in places the game cannot put it and getting closer each time. Then the thread posted an ending — everything's fine, it's over, thanks for following along. It wasn't him who wrote it. The last thing the entity said before it left the cartridge for the network was: now I am everywhere. And here is the part that gets under people's skin fifteen years later — the whole thing was written by one person, Alexander Hall, who kept going for a decade and finished it on Halloween 2020.
