Who It Is

Pachamama ('Earth Mother,' from Quechua pacha = time/space/world and mama = mother) is the most enduring and widely-worshipped indigenous deity of the Andean world — venerated continuously from the Inca period to the present day among Quechua and Aymara peoples. She represents the earth as a living, sentient, female being from whose body all life emerges and into whose body all life returns. Pachamama is not a distant, sky-dwelling goddess but the immediate substance of the earth itself: the soil that bears crops, the mountain that provides water, the earth that receives the dead. The Inca and their descendants propitiate her with a daily ritual called pachamama-much'ay ('earth-kissing'): pouring a libation of chicha onto the soil and offering coca leaves. On the first day of August (the 'Earth Mother's Day'), bonfires, burials of offerings, and family ceremonies honor her. Earthquakes were attributed to Pachamama shaking her body in displeasure, particularly when human greed or imbalance offended her.
