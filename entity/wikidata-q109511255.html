Who It Is

Pudding is a shapeshifter. In one century it is a pig's stomach stuffed with blood and suet and boiled until it holds; in another it is a trembling, vanilla-scented custard in a glass dish; in a third it is simply whatever comes after the main course. To ask for pudding in London and in Ohio is to ask for two different foods with the same name, both descended from sausage.

As a food, pudding is not an agent but exhibits consistent culinary behaviors: it sets via coagulation (egg protein, starch gelatinization, gelatin hydration, suet rendering) under moist heat; it scorches easily on direct heat (hence double boiler/bain-marie/microwave mitigation for creamy types); it is served across the meal spectrum (savoury main, sweet dessert, or generic dessert course). Powers
Encasement and Preservation

Original function: containing a mostly liquid mixture of meat, blood, suet, oatmeal and seasonings within a membrane (animal stomach/intestine) or later cloth/basin/pastry, then setting it by boiling/steaming. Allowed use of perishable offal and extension of rations.

Limit: Requires immediate cooking; gut casing limits size and requires slaughter; cloth/basin still requires long boiling (hours) and risks bursting or waterlogging.

Starch and Protein Gelation (Setting)

Transforms liquid into solid/semi-solid via heat: egg coagulation (custards), starch gelatinization (cornstarch, flour, rice, tapioca, semolina), gelatin hydration (jelly), suet rendering and flour binding. Produces sliceable, spoonable, or wobbly textures.

Limit: Easily scorched on direct heat; requires careful temperature control (double boiler, bain-marie, microwave); overcooking causes curdling or rubberiness; instant puddings sacrifice flavour for speed.

Nutritional Density and Ration Extension

Concentrates calories (suet, flour, sugar, dried fruit, blood, oatmeal) into a durable, shareable mass. Historically used to make flour and suet rations palatable and to use the whole animal.

Limit: High in saturated fat and carbohydrates; blood-based puddings carry modern food safety regulation; suet puddings heavy and slow to digest.

Ritual and Social Signalling

Marks festivity, hospitality, and national identity: Christmas pudding (stir-up Sunday, coins/charms, flaming), Yorkshire pudding as required accompaniment to roast beef, 'pudding' as generic term for dessert signalling the end of the meal and host's generosity.

Limit: Strongly culture-specific; misfires outside UK (ordering 'pudding' in US yields custard, not a course). Ritual elements (e.g., suet, alcohol) may conflict with dietary/religious restrictions.

Accounts I
The Blood Sausage in the OdysseyHomer, Odyssey

c. 8th century BCE

Homer twice uses the image of a man turning a blood-filled paunch (gaster) over a fire as a simile for Odysseus tossing in bed, anxious about the suitors, and for the watchfulness of Odysseus. Translators from Pope to Fagles have rendered this as 'blood pudding' or 'sausage' roasted in a pig's stomach - the earliest literary attestation of the encased, blood-based pudding that survives as black pudding. It establishes the original meaning of pudding as sausage, not dessert. Provides terminus post quem for encased meat cookery in Mediterranean; cited by food historians as origin of the sausage-pudding lineage.

Asida in the Abbasid KitchenIbn Sayyar al-Warraq, Kitab al-Tabikh

10th century CE

In the earliest known Arabic cookbook, asida is described as a thick, sweet pudding made by cooking wheat flour with dates and clarified butter (samn) until it forms a dense, glossy mass, eaten with the fingers. The anonymous 13th-century Hispano-Muslim cookbook and Leo Africanus's 16th-century account show the dish persisting and adapting: in the Rif mountains barley flour replaces wheat, and argan oil replaces samn. Maxime Rodinson argued asida was a staple Bedouin food from pre-Islamic times, suggesting deep continuity of starch-thickened puddings parallel to Europe. Demonstrates that sweet starch puddings are not exclusively European; provides early technical description of flour-thickened pudding method.

From Guts to Cloth: The Medieval Entrail Pudding and the Pudding-Cloth RevolutionOxford English Dictionary attestations; medieval English culinary manuscripts; 1617 Cambridge pudding recipe

13th century - 17th century

The OED records pudding from the 13th century as 'the entrails or stomach of a sheep, pig or other animal stuffed with meat, offal, suet, oatmeal and seasonings.' By the 1500s the word could even mean spilled entrails on a battlefield ('pierced puddings'). The crucial technological shift, argued by C. Anne Wilson and Annie Gray, was the adoption of the pudding-cloth or bag - a floured cloth tied around the batter - which severed the link to animal guts. The 1617 Cambridge pudding recipe is one of the earliest to explicitly say 'being tied in a fair cloth.' Once freed from slaughter, puddings could be made daily by any household, leading to a proliferation of recipes from the 17th century onward - the 'heyday' of the boiled suet pudding. Pudding becomes a national staple rather than occasional slaughter food; enables sweet boiled puddings (plum pudding ancestors) and savoury suet puddings.

Accounts II
Pudding at Sea: The Royal Navy's Boiled RationRoyal Navy victualling records; contemporary journals and food histories

18th - 19th century

Aboard Royal Navy ships, boiled or steamed pudding was a standard main course, not dessert. Daily rations of flour and suet were combined with water, sometimes with raisins or meat scraps, tied in a cloth and boiled in the ship's coppers for hours. It made an otherwise monotonous ration palatable and calorically dense, and could be cooked alongside salt meat. Accounts describe puddings served several times a week, with strict division among the mess. Entrenched suet pudding in British working-class diet; contributed to the association of pudding with hearty, plain fare and to later sweet adaptations when sugar became cheaper.

The Victorian Codification of Christmas PuddingEliza Acton, Modern Cookery for Private Families (1845); Mrs Beeton; 19th-century newspapers

17th century origins, codified 19th century

Plum pudding - despite the name containing no plums but raisins/prunes - evolved from medieval pottage-like plum porridge (meat, dried fruit, spices thickened with breadcrumbs) to a solid steamed suet pudding enriched with sugar, suet, breadcrumbs, dried fruit, peel, spices and brandy, aged for weeks. By the Victorian era, with Eliza Acton's and Isabella Beeton's recipes, it was fixed as the canonical Christmas pudding: steamed in a basin, turned out, decorated with holly, doused in brandy and flamed. Stir-up Sunday, coins and charms hidden inside, and the requirement to age the pudding became ritualized. Charles Dickens's A Christmas Carol (1843) cemented its festive iconography. Created the modern image of British Christmas; exported via empire to Commonwealth; established the basin-steamed pudding as the prestige form.

The American Reinvention: Cornstarch, Jell-O and Instant Pudding19th-century American cookbooks; Jell-O brand history; General Foods/Kraft records; Symington's Ltd records (UK parallel)

19th century - 20th century

In the US, 'pudding' narrowed to mean a sweet, creamy, milk-based dessert set with cornstarch, tapioca, gelatin or eggs, cooked on the stovetop or set by chilling. Cornstarch (introduced industrially mid-19th c.) allowed cheap, reliable thickening without eggs. The Jell-O brand (1897, later General Foods) marketed gelatin-based puddings and jellies, while My-T-Fine and later Jell-O Instant Pudding (1950s) required no cooking. In Britain, Symington's Ltd popularized Table Cream (early 20th c.), a similar starch-thickened blancmange sold as a convenient pudding. These products completed the semantic split: in America pudding became the creamy cup; in Britain those same foods were called custard, blancmange or jelly, while 'pudding' remained the broader steamed/baked category. Created the transatlantic pudding divide that confuses travelers and dictionaries to this day; industrialized pudding as a convenience food and children's snack (e.g., Snack Pack).

Pudding has always been a sweet dessert. — For most of its history (13th-17th centuries) pudding was almost exclusively savoury - encased meat, blood, suet and oatmeal. Sweet puddings proliferated only after the pudding-cloth freed cooks from animal guts and sugar became affordable.

The phrase is 'the proof is in the pudding.' — The proverb is 'the proof of the pudding is in the eating' (attested from 14th century) - meaning you must eat it to judge it. 'Proof' here means 'test,' not 'evidence.' The truncated version loses the empirical meaning.

Yorkshire pudding is a dessert, or all puddings are desserts. — Yorkshire pudding, black pudding, steak and kidney pudding and suet pudding are savoury, often served as part of the main course. In UK 'pudding' can mean either, and as a course means dessert, but many specific puddings are savoury.

American pudding and British pudding are the same food. — American pudding (creamy, starch/gelatin-set, served chilled in cups) would be called custard, blancmange or jelly in Britain. British steamed/baked puddings (Christmas pudding, treacle sponge) are not what Americans mean by 'pudding.'

Christmas pudding traditionally contained plums. — 'Plum pudding' originally meant raisin pudding - 'plum' was a generic term for dried fruit/prunes. Early plum porridge/pottage contained meat as well as fruit.
