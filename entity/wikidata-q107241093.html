Who It Is

In the shadowed annals of Greek myth, Lycius stands as a name whispered across conflicting tales—a warrior, a king, a son of gods, or perhaps a forgotten hero whose true story was lost to time. Depending on the source, Lycius may be a Lycian prince, a Trojan ally, an Argive hero, or even an epithet of Apollo himself. His identity is a puzzle of ancient texts, each fragment offering a different face to the same elusive figure.

Behavioral traits are not explicitly recorded. As a Trojan warrior, he would be expected to embody martial valor, but no individual characterization exists. As a son of Aegyptus, he is only known as a victim of the Danaids.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Lycius (Λύκιος) is derived from the Greek word 'λύκος' (lykos), meaning 'wolf'. It may also be connected to the region of Lycia (Λυκία), whose name is traditionally associated with wolves. The name thus carries connotations of wolf-like attributes or Lycian origin.

Pronunciation — LIS-ee-us (English approximation); in classical Greek: LOO-kee-os (with emphasis on the first syllable, 'Λύκιος').

Aliases — Lycius

Ownership — Greek mythology, with possible extensions into Roman literature. The name appears in various mythological contexts, often as a minor character or an epithet.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No specific physical description of Lycius survives in ancient sources. As a mythological figure, he is typically imagined as a warrior or prince, but no textual evidence provides details on his appearance.

Symbols — No distinct symbols are associated with Lycius. The wolf (λύκος) is a possible symbolic connection, given the etymology, but it is not attested in any specific iconography. In art, he is not known to be depicted.

Domains — Lycius is associated with Troy (as a Trojan ally) and with Egypt (as a son of Aegyptus). The Lycian region is also implied through the name's connection.

Accounts I
Death at the hands of AjaxHomer, Iliad

Heroic Age (Trojan War)

In the Iliad, Lycius, son of Lycaon, is killed by Ajax during the battle. The account is brief, with no further detail. No further consequences are recorded; his death is part of the broader Trojan War narrative.

Accounts II
The Danaid SlayingHyginus, Fabulae; Apollodorus, Bibliotheca

Mythical

Lycius is one of the fifty sons of Aegyptus. He is married to a Danaid (one of the fifty daughters of Danaus) and is killed by her on their wedding night, as part of Danaus's plan. His death contributes to the punishment of the Danaids in the afterlife.
