Who It Is

On the plain before Troy, a single warrior’s anger bends the fate of armies and gods alike. Achilles—Peleus’ son, swift-footed and godlike—chooses a short life of blazing glory over a long, quiet existence, then spends the rest of the Iliad testing what that choice truly costs. He drags a dead enemy around the city in a fury that nearly devours his own humanity, yet later weeps with that enemy’s father over the shared grief of mortal fathers and sons. Between the threat of cannibalism and the gift of burial, between divine armor and mortal tears, Achilles becomes less a simple ‘hero’ than a fault line where power, rage, love, and fate collide.

Attested: Achilles is characterized by extreme emotional intensity—wrath, grief, and tenderness. His anger at Agamemnon over Briseis (Il. 1) leads to withdrawal from battle, endangering the Achaean army. He is capable of deep affection and loyalty, especially toward Patroclus and his father Peleus, and of profound grief that manifests in violent rage after Patroclus’ death (Il. 18–22). He oscillates between brutal savagery (e.g., desecration of Hector’s corpse, threats of cannibalism) and humane compassion (e.g., his reconciliation with Priam in Il. 24). Powers
Superlative martial prowess

Attested: Achilles is repeatedly described as the greatest warrior among the Achaeans, unmatched in combat skill, speed, and strength. He routs entire Trojan forces single-handedly, kills numerous prominent warriors (including Hector), and inspires terror on the battlefield. His spear and armor enhance his effectiveness, but his prowess is fundamentally personal and semi-divine.

Limit: He is still mortal and ultimately fated to die. His withdrawal from battle shows that his power is constrained by his own emotional choices and by the authority structures of the Achaean coalition.

Extraordinary speed (swift-footed)

Attested: The epithet “swift-footed Achilles” underscores his exceptional speed in running and combat maneuvers. He can chase Hector around Troy, catch fleeing enemies, and move rapidly across the battlefield.

Limit: His speed does not make him invulnerable; he can be delayed by emotional turmoil, divine intervention, or strategic decisions.

Semi-divine resilience and strength

Attested: As son of the sea-goddess Thetis and mortal Peleus, Achilles exhibits resilience beyond ordinary mortals. He survives intense combat, wounds enemies with ease, and is described as “peer of gods.” However, Homer does not explicitly state that his body is invulnerable.

Limit: He is mortal and can be killed. His fate is fixed, and he cannot escape death at Troy.

Divinely forged armor and weapons

Attested: After Patroclus’ death, Thetis obtains new armor for Achilles from Hephaestus, including a shield with cosmic imagery and a spear of Pelian ash that only Achilles can wield. This equipment enhances his battlefield dominance and symbolizes his unique status.

Limit: The armor does not make him immortal; it can be lost (as when Patroclus wears his earlier armor and dies) and is later contested as a prize among the Achaeans.

Prophetic interactions and awareness of fate

Attested: Achilles receives prophetic warnings from his mother Thetis, from his horse Xanthus, and from the ghost of Patroclus. He knows that if he remains at Troy he will die young but gain everlasting glory, whereas if he returns home he will live long without fame. He consciously chooses the former.

Limit: He cannot change his fate; his knowledge only shapes his attitude toward it.

Capacity for extreme rage and near-bestial savagery

Attested: Achilles’ wrath leads him to desecrate Hector’s corpse, refuse burial, and utter threats of cannibalism—wishing he could eat Hector’s flesh for what he has done. His rage is described in terms that blur the line between human and beast, and scholars have analyzed this as a movement toward monstrous savagery that he later rejects.

Limit: His rage is ultimately tempered by divine and human appeals, especially the visit of Priam, leading to a restoration of humane behavior.

Accounts I
The Wrath of Achilles and the IliadHomer, Iliad

Archaic (c. 8th century BCE)

The Iliad begins with the invocation of Achilles’ wrath against Agamemnon, who has seized the captive woman Briseis from Achilles. In response, Achilles withdraws from battle, praying to his mother Thetis to persuade Zeus to honor him by allowing the Trojans to push the Achaeans back. The absence of Achilles leads to mounting Greek losses. An embassy offers rich gifts and entreaties, but Achilles refuses. When the Trojans threaten the ships, Patroclus enters battle wearing Achilles’ armor and is killed by Hector. Achilles’ grief transforms into ferocious rage; he reconciles with Agamemnon, receives new armor from Hephaestus, and returns to battle, slaughtering Trojans and finally killing Hector. He then desecrates Hector’s corpse by dragging it around Troy. The poem ends not with Achilles’ death but with his meeting with Priam, who begs for Hector’s body. Achilles is moved to pity, returns the corpse, and grants a truce for funeral rites, revealing a complex hero capable of both extreme violence and deep compassion. Achilles’ choice of fateHomer, Iliad 9 (Embassy to Achilles)

Archaic

During the embassy, Achilles reveals that his mother Thetis has told him of two possible fates: if he stays at Troy, he will die young but gain imperishable glory; if he returns home, he will live a long life without fame. Achilles declares that he is inclined to sail home, rejecting the heroic code, but ultimately he remains and later chooses the path of glory after Patroclus’ death. This story crystallizes the central ethical dilemma of heroic life and becomes a foundational motif in later literature. Achilles’ eventual choice of glory over life shapes his death and posthumous reputation.

Accounts II
Achilles in HadesHomer, Odyssey 11

Archaic

Odysseus encounters Achilles’ shade in the underworld. Odysseus praises Achilles’ glory, but Achilles replies that he would rather be a living servant than a dead king of the dead, expressing regret about the cost of heroic fame. This scene reinterprets Achilles’ Iliadic choice from a post-war perspective. The passage complicates the heroic ideal, suggesting that glory may not compensate for the loss of life. It has been central to philosophical and literary reflections on heroism and mortality.

Achilles’ youth and disguise on SkyrosStatius, Achilleid; Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca; Hyginus, Fabulae

Roman Imperial

Later mythographic and literary sources narrate Achilles’ upbringing by the centaur Chiron, who teaches him hunting, music, and medicine. To prevent his participation in the Trojan War, Thetis hides him on the island of Skyros among the daughters of King Lycomedes, where Achilles disguises himself as a girl and fathers Neoptolemus with Deidamia. Odysseus later reveals Achilles by presenting weapons among gifts, and Achilles joins the Achaean expedition. This story adds gender-disguise and erotic elements to Achilles’ biography, emphasizing his liminal status between childhood and heroic adulthood. It also establishes the lineage of Neoptolemus, who will later play a major role in the fall of Troy.

The death of AchillesQuintus Smyrnaeus, Posthomerica; Pseudo-Apollodorus; Hyginus; later summaries

Late Antique and Roman Imperial

Post-Homeric narratives describe Achilles’ death near Troy. Typically, he is killed by an arrow shot by Paris, often guided by Apollo, striking a vulnerable spot (sometimes specified as his heel). His death occurs either during a truce or in battle, and his body is recovered and honored by the Achaeans. His armor becomes a contested prize, leading to the rivalry between Ajax and Odysseus. Achilles’ death removes the greatest Achaean warrior from the war, shifting focus to other heroes and precipitating further tragedies. His tomb becomes a cult site, and his armor’s contest fuels additional conflict among the Greeks.
