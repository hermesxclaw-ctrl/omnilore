Who It Is

In Sikh scripture, the name 'Ram' echoes over two thousand times, yet it rarely points to the prince of Ayodhya. Instead, it becomes a thread woven into the fabric of the divine, a word transformed to speak of the formless, all-pervading Lord—a distinction that has shaped centuries of devotion and debate.

Rama is portrayed as a brave and virtuous king, an example of righteousness and devotion. In the Dasam Granth, his martial exploits are highlighted, emphasizing his role as a divine agent who restores dharma.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Rama' (Sanskrit: राम) is derived from the root 'ram', meaning 'to stop' or 'to rest', and is often interpreted as 'one who delights' or 'the pleasing one'. In Sikh usage, it is used both as a name for the historical king of Ayodhya and as a metaphor for the supreme reality.

Pronunciation — In Punjabi, 'Rama' is pronounced as 'Raam' (ਰਾਮ), with a long 'a' sound, as in 'father'.

Aliases — Ram Avatar, Raja Ram, Ram Chander

Powers
Divine agency

Rama, as an avatar of Vishnu, is believed to have been empowered by God to defeat evil and restore righteousness. However, Sikhism emphasizes that these avatars are not God but agents of God.

Limit: Avatars are subject to birth and death, unlike the eternal God.

Warrior prowess

Rama is depicted as a formidable warrior, skilled in archery and combat, as narrated in the Ram Avatar Bani.

Limit: His powers are limited to his earthly life and mission.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In Sikh tradition, Rama is not depicted in physical form, as Sikhism discourages idol worship. Descriptions are textual, focusing on his qualities as a warrior-king and righteous ruler, but primarily as an avatar of Vishnu who is subordinate to the formless God.

Symbols — The name 'Ram' itself is a powerful symbol in Sikhism, representing the divine presence. It is often used in hymns and meditations. There is no specific iconography, but the word 'Ram' is revered.

Domains — Rama's domain is primarily the earthly kingdom of Ayodhya, but in Sikh interpretation, the name 'Ram' transcends place, referring to the omnipresent divine.

Accounts I
Ram Avatar BaniDasam Granth

Late 17th century

This composition narrates the story of Rama as an avatar of Vishnu, focusing on his birth, exile, battle with Ravana, and return to Ayodhya. It is told with a martial tone, highlighting his bravery and divine mission. The story reinforces the idea that avatars are instruments of God, not God himself, and serves as a source of inspiration for courage and righteousness.

Accounts II
Guru Nanak's pilgrimage to Ram JanmabhoomiJanamsakhis and legal testimony

c. 1520 (according to some accounts)

Rajinder Singh, a Sikh expert, testified that Guru Nanak visited the Ram Janmabhoomi site in Ayodhya. This claim has been contested, with scholars arguing that the janamsakhis are later additions and that Guru Nanak's travels were for preaching, not pilgrimage. This has been cited in the Ram Janmabhoomi legal case, but the SGPC and many Sikh scholars reject the interpretation, emphasizing that Guru Nanak's visits were to spread his message.
