Huginn and Muninn fly the wide world over each day — one brings what is, the other what was, and Odin knows all.

Who It Is

Huginn ('Thought') and Muninn ('Memory' or 'Mind') are the pair of ravens who sit on Odin's shoulders and whisper all the news they have gathered into his ears. Every morning at dawn, they fly across the entire Nine Realms, and every evening they return to tell Odin everything they have seen and heard. In the Grímnismál (stanzas 20–21), Odin himself speaks: 'Huginn flies each day over the spacious earth; I fear for Huginn that he may not return, yet my anxiety is more for Muninn.' This stanza suggests that Memory is more precious and more fragile than Thought — a profound reflection on the nature of knowledge. The ravens are more than mere animal companions: they represent the god's own cognitive faculties projected outward. Odin is the god of wisdom precisely because he sends Thought and Memory out into the world; without them, he would be less than a god. The ravens also have a shamanic function — they are psychopomps who travel between worlds, and their croaking was considered prophetic. In battle, ravens were associated with Odin and victory: to see ravens before a battle was a favorable omen. Huginn and Muninn are the source of the kenning 'his ravens' for Odin, and they appear in shield poems and skaldic verse as symbols of the god's omniscience.

Attested in Poetic Edda (Grímnismál); Prose Edda (Gylfaginning)Era Viking Age (c. 800–1100 CE); recorded 13th century

Form

Two black ravens (the common raven, Corvus corax), identical in most sources, distinguished only by name and function. In the Prose Edda they are described as perching on Odin's shoulders; in the Third Grammatical Treatise and some skaldic verses they fly from his shoulders. Artifacts show raven imagery with powerful beaks and fan-shaped tails (Peter Vang Petersen on the Bejsebakke brooches), sometimes with a bird at the ear of the human or of the horse (bracteates), and pairs worn one on each shoulder. Their sexuality is never specified; Heimskringla emphasizes the speech-gift Odin gave them.

Powers

World-spanning flight / reconnaissance Huginn and Muninn fly across the entire mortal world (Midgard) daily, from dawn until dinner-time (dinner being the first meal in Valhalla per Prose Edda), observing all they see and hear and reporting back to Odin. They are Odin's intelligence-gathering eyes and ears. Poetic Edda (Grímnismál), Prose Edda (Gylfaginning ch. 38) Odin fears they may not return from a given flight — the return is never guaranteed (Grímnismál). They must physically fly and return; they do not teleport or transmit remotely. The Grímnismál anxiety about return has been read (Lindow) as echoing shamanic trance risk; Simek disputes the 'intellectual power' reading altogether, arguing the ravens' function derives from older Germanic raven-battle symbolism rather than personified thought.

Speech (Odin's gift) Odin gave Huginn and Muninn the ability to speak (Heimskringla). This is what enables them to convey the intelligence they gather — they literally tell Odin what they have seen and heard. Heimskringla (Ynglinga saga ch. 7) Only attested in the euhemerized Heimskringla account; the Poetic and Prose Edda present them reporting but do not dwell on the mechanics of speech. None material — all sources agree they report to Odin; only the speech-grant mechanism differs.

Odin's omniscience amplification Through the ravens' daily flights, Odin is 'kept informed of many events' and becomes (in Heimskringla's euhemerized framing) 'very wise in his lore.' The ravens functionally compensate for Odin's physical limitations — his one eye and his imperfect memory — as described in Bernd Heinrich's biological-symbiosis reading. In the wider concept, they embody 'thought' and 'memory' made external and functional. Prose Edda; Heimskringla; Heinrich's interpretive theory The ravens amplify only what they physically observe; they do not grant Odin true omniscience over what they have not seen. Their knowledge is cumulative over daily flights, not instantaneous or universal. Simek explicitly rejects the 'personification of the god's intellectual powers' reading as unsupported, arguing the names were likely late (9th-10th c.) while the raven-companions are older. The tension is between the 'shamanic external-soul' reading (Lindow, Winterbourne) and the 'battlefield raven' reading (Simek).

Corpse-seeking / battlefield affinity In the Third Grammatical Treatise, Huginn seeks out the hanged and Muninn the battle-slain — the ravens gravitate to the dead and dying that are the natural feeding-ground of ravens and the domain of Odin as god of the gallows. Third Grammatical Treatise, anonymous verse Not an offensive power; an affinity/behavior, not battlefield capability. None — consistent with raven natural behavior and Odin's death-god aspect.

Heraldic / shamanic soul-animal projection Winterbourne links Huginn and Muninn to the Norse concepts of the fylgja (a shape-shifting guardian-spirit/companion with good fortune) and the hamingja (the ghostly animal-form double of a person). On this reading, the ravens are a symbolic externalization of Odin's own thought and memory as a soul-journey. Anthony Winterbourne, interpretation; John Lindow (shamanic trance-journey) Fully interpretive — no primary text names the ravens as fylgjur or hamingjur; this is a scholarly model, not an attested title. Rejected as overreading by Simek ('can only be assumed from the names'); defended by Winterbourne because the names 'demand more explanation than is usually provided.'

Accounts

In Grímnismál, the god Odin (disguised as Grímnir) tells the young Agnarr about his animal companions. After describing his wolves Geri and Freki, he states that Huginn and Muninn fly daily across the entire world, Midgard. He reveals his worry: he fears Huginn (thought) may not come back, 'yet more does he fear for Muninn' (memory). No narrative outcome is given — the story is a vignette of constant vigilance and existential risk attached to the information-bearers.

The enthroned 'High' tells Gangleri (King Gylfi in disguise) that two ravens named Huginn and Muninn sit on Odin's shoulders, telling him everything they see and hear. Odin sends them out at dawn; they fly all over the world and return at dinner-time; thus Odin is kept informed of many events. For this, Odin is called 'raven-god' (Hrafnaguð). High quotes the Grímnismál stanza as evidence.

In Snorri's euhemerized account — Odin recast as a historical chieftain who migrated into Scandinavia — Odin has two ravens on whom he bestows the gift of speech. These ravens fly over all the land and bring him information, thereby making Odin 'very wise in his lore.' The supernatural ravens are naturalized into a legend of a cunning historical leader with informants.

An anonymous skaldic verse records the two ravens flying from 'Hnikar's [Óðinn's] shoulders' — Huginn to the hanged, Muninn to the slain (corpses). The image ties the ravens directly to Odin's gallows-god aspect and the raven's natural role as carrion-eater on battlefields and execution sites.

Simek ties Huginn and Muninn to the Germanic raven banner — a standard woven so that it appeared to flap its wings in the wind, as if the raven depicted were alive and beating its wings. The banner functioned as a battle-talisman under which Norse armies fought. The raven imagery unifies Odin's winged messengers with battlefield death-omens.

Content Notes

The ravens' role in the Third Grammatical Treatise — Huginn to the hanged, Muninn to the battle-slain — and the raven-banner's battlefield use tie Huginn and Muninn to Odin himself as a god of battle-death, execution, and the gallows. The ravens feed on and herald the fallen; Valhalla's own battle-and-death imagery is ravaged by wolves and ravens. This is the un-sanitized core: Odin's beloved birds are carrion-eaters who thrive where warriors die.

Odin is a death-god (god of the hanged, battle-slain), and ravens are natural corvid scavengers; the mythological pair therefore encodes real battlefield mortality rather than sentimental omniscience.

Third Grammatical Treatise; Valhalla traditions; raven banner (chronicles & sagas)

The raven banner under which Norse and early English armies fought appear to flap its wings in the wind, serving as a battle-standard of a death-god's blessing. Ravens were historically bearers of both intelligence and battlefield omen; the pair's association powered a war-cult that industrialized raven imagery for conquest. Modern fascination with 'Odin's ravens' often sanitizes this into soft fantasy, obscuring the birds' function as death-omens of armed conflict.

The raven banner is a documented historical standard (bequeathing/sacred-talisman function), linking mythology to lived martial culture.

English chronicles, sagas; Simek's discussion

The Grímnismál anxiety — Odin fears Huginn and Muninn may not return — encodes, on the shamanic-soul reading (Lindow, Winterbourne), the real danger of the externalized soul/trance journey. In a war-cult dominated by ecstatic shamanic elites, 'thought' and 'memory' sent abroad could be lost, which is a subtle register of the cost of Odin's form of power.

Interpretive; connects the world-spanning flight to shamanism rather than literal birds.

Grímnismál; Lindow; Winterbourne

The imagery of Odin's ravens (and wolves) was used historically by warrior-elites (via raven banners, wolf/raven-named warriors such as the Anglo-Saxon Wulfhroc 'Wolf-Frock' and the name Wolfram 'Wolf (and) Raven', and Germanic wolf-warrior bands). This martial identity — memorialized in names and iconography — served militarized elites and, in modern times, has occasionally been co-opted by right-wing and neo-pagan nationalist subcultures who weaponize Norse death-symbolism. This is an echoing cultural dark-thread rather than a narrative cruelty of the birds themselves.

The raven as war-talisman is ancient; its modern political co-option is a separate, documented cultural appropriation concern (as with runes and Mjölnir).

Speidel's analysis (as cited in Geri and Freki); general Norse revival literature

A way to hold it in your mind

In the grimmest corner of the Viking world there is a god who has ripped out his own eye, hung himself from a tree for knowledge, and fed a wolf that is fated to swallow him alive — and the only sight he trusts to learn what is happening everywhere else is a pair of black birds no larger than a man's forearm. Every dawn Huginn ('Thought') and Muninn ('Memory') launch from his shoulders and spiral out over a world full of battlefields and gallows; every dinner-time they come back and whisper to him what they have seen, eaten carrion among the hanged, and marked which warriors will die next. Odin sends them boldly and fears them constantly — 'more does he fear for Muninn' — because thought itself can be lost, and memory, once scattered across the earth, is the one thing even a god cannot regrow. To an age spellbound by omniscience in every pocket, the ravens stand as the oldest high-performance intelligence system on record: no satellites, no cables, just two corvids with a god's anxiety riding on their wings.
