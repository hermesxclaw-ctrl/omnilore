Who It Is

Mara (Māra) is the supreme antagonist of Buddhist cosmology—the personification of death, desire, delusion, and all forces that obstruct the path to enlightenment. His name derives from the Sanskrit root 'mṛ,' meaning 'to die,' and Mara embodies everything that binds sentient beings to the cycle of birth, death, and rebirth (samsara). Unlike the Abrahamic concept of Satan as a fallen angel, Mara is better understood as a cosmic function—the principle of obstruction and attachment woven into the very fabric of conditioned existence. His most famous appearance occurs on the night of the Buddha's enlightenment. As Siddhartha Gautama sat beneath the Bodhi Tree, vowing not to rise until he attained awakening, Mara perceived the existential threat: if the Buddha achieved enlightenment, he would teach others the path to liberation, draining Mara's kingdom of its subjects. Mara launched a threefold assault: first, he sent his three daughters—Tanha (Craving), Arati (Aversion), and Raga (Passion)—to seduce the Buddha with desire; then, he hurled a storm of terrifying weapons and monsters, a ninefold rain of wind, rocks, weapons, hot coals, hot ashes, sand, mud, and darkness; finally, he challenged the Buddha's very worthiness, questioning by what right he sought liberation. The Buddha touched the earth with his right hand (the bhumisparsha mudra), calling the Earth Goddess to witness his countless past-life virtues. The Earth quaked, Mara's armies dispersed, and dawn broke with the Buddha's enlightenment. In Buddhist psychology, Mara is interpreted as the inner demons of greed, hatred, delusion, doubt, restlessness, and laziness—forces present in every unenlightened mind. Buddhist practice is, in a fundamental sense, the ongoing battle with Mara.
