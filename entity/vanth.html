Who They Are

Vanth is the beautiful and terrifying Etruscan goddess of death, a winged psychopomp who guides souls to the underworld. Unlike the hammer-wielding Charun, Vanth is serene and composed — she embodies the inevitability of fate rather than the violence of death. She carries a torch to light the soul's path, a key to unlock the gates of the underworld, and a scroll containing the decrees of fate.

Cultural Significance

Vanth appears in most major Etruscan painted tombs from the 5th to 2nd centuries BCE. She is a uniquely Etruscan figure — unlike Charun, who shares ancestry with Greek figures, Vanth has no direct Greek or Roman counterpart. She reflects the Etruscan view of death as a journey rather than an end, and the importance of proper funerary rites for ensuring a safe passage. Etruscan women enjoyed higher status than their Greek or Roman counterparts, and Vanth's prominent role as a female death deity may reflect this cultural difference. Vanth's image — the winged female psychopomp — may have influenced the later Roman figure of Victoria and early Christian depictions of angels.
