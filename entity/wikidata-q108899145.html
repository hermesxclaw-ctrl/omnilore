The Butter-Cat (Smjörköttur) Steals Butter — Icelandic Folklore (analogue)Jón Árnason. Íslenzkar þjóðsögur og æfintýri. Vol. 1; Simpson, Jacqueline. Icelandic Folktales and Legends (1972); Almqvist, Bo. 'Butterwitches and Buttercats' (2008)

Oral tradition recorded 19th century; beliefs attested 17th-19th century

A witch owns a cat-like familiar (smjörköttur, tilberi) that sneaks into neighbors' dairies, steals milk and butter, and brings it back, sometimes by vomiting butter. Farmers protect churns with crosses and iron. When the cat is injured, the witch shows corresponding wounds. Explains butter failure and milk loss; reinforces witchcraft accusations; apotropaic practices proliferate

The Milk-Hare (Mjölkhare) — Swedish and Norwegian Folklore (analogue)Tillhagen, Carl-Herman. Folklore collections; Kvideland, Reimund & Henning Sehmsdorf. Scandinavian Folk Belief and Legend (1988)

Early modern to 19th century oral tradition

A witch creates a milk-hare from butter, wool, and other materials, animates it to steal milk from others' cows. The hare is shot, and butter or milk leaks from the wound, revealing the witch. Shooting the hare with a silver button or blessed shot is a countermeasure. Narrative justifies shooting hares seen near farms; links dairy misfortune to witchcraft; legal proceedings in witch trials

The Butter Witch of Berkeley and English Dairy Witchcraft Trials (analogue)Ewen, C. L'Estrange. Witch Hunting and Witch Trials (1929); Sharpe, James. Instruments of Darkness (1996); Essex witch trial pamphlets 1645-1647

17th century pamphlet literature and trial records

Pamphlets and depositions describe witches who keep familiars that spoil or steal butter, prevent churning, or cause milk to be 'taken'. A familiar may appear as a cat, rat, or imp. Failure to churn butter is presented as maleficium evidence. Contributed to convictions; reflected economic anxiety over dairy production; later folklorists reinterpret as social scapegoating

Alchemical and Learned Magic: Lac Virginis and Butyrum Alchemy (analogue — learned tradition)Pseudo-Geber, Summa Perfectionis (13th-14th c.); Holmyard, E.J. Alchemy (1957); Principe, Lawrence. The Secrets of Alchemy (2013)

Medieval to early modern (13th-17th century)

Latin alchemical texts discuss lac virginis ('virgin's milk'), lac philosophorum, and butyrum antimonii ('butter of antimony' — antimony trichloride) as substances, not as animated constructs. The terms show that butyrum and lac had technical alchemical senses. No text animates them as a construct named as in Q108899145. Demonstrates that butyrum/lac vocabulary existed in learned magic without implying a dairy golem; modern readers sometimes misinterpret 'butter of antimony' as dairy magic
