Who It Is

The griffin (also spelled gryphon) is a majestic mythological creature with the body, tail, and back legs of a lion combined with the head, wings, and forelegs of an eagle. This hybrid combines the king of beasts and the king of birds, resulting in a creature of supreme power and majesty. Griffin imagery first appeared in the ancient Near East: Egyptian tomb paintings depict griffin-like creatures, and they featured prominently in Minoan frescoes at Knossos. The Greeks believed griffins guarded the gold deposits of the Hyperborean lands in Scythia, frequently battling the one-eyed Arimaspians who tried to steal the treasure. The Roman author Pliny the Elder described griffins as creatures with 'hooked beaks and wings' whose nests contained gold. In medieval Europe, the griffin inherited this role as a guardian of treasure and became a prominent heraldic symbol representing courage, vigilance, and strength. Christian symbolism adopted the griffin as representing the dual nature of Christ (divine as the eagle, human as the lion), most famously in Dante's Divine Comedy where a griffin draws the chariot of the Church. The griffin's reputation for monogamy (supposedly mating for life) made it a symbol of fidelity, while its fierceness made it a popular charge on coats of arms. In modern fantasy, griffins appear as noble mounts in works ranging from the Chronicles of Narnia to World of Warcraft, often serving as aerial cavalry for heroic factions.

Form

The canonical griffin is a composite creature: the body, hindquarters, and tail of a lion; the head, wings, and forelegs/talons of an eagle. Aelian adds a crest of white and dark feathers on the neck, red breast feathers, and white feathers from neck to feet. Pliny emphasizes the hooked beak and hooked talons. Ctesias describes the Indian variant as having black back feathers, red chest feathers, and white wings — a tricolor scheme that may reflect the plumage of real raptors. Ancient Greek art (vase painting, relief sculpture, coins) consistently shows the lion-eagle blend, with the eagle head often bearing pointed ears (sometimes horse-like, adding a third animal element). The griffin is almost always shown with wings — the wingless variant is rare in classical art and mostly appears in very early Minoan frescoes. In medieval heraldry, the griffin is typically rampant (rearing up), with the eagle's head, forelegs with talons, and the lion's body and hind legs. Size varies enormously by source: Ctesias compares it to a wolf; Aelian says it can fight elephants; the griffins of the Arimaspea are implied to be large enough to carry out sustained combat with armed men.

Powers

Guardianship of gold The griffin's defining function in Greek literature: it guards gold deposits in remote mountains, fighting off the one-eyed Arimaspians who attempt to steal it. Herodotus' formula grypes chrysophylakes ('gold-guarding griffins') is the core epithet. Herodotus 3.116, 4.13-27; Pliny; Aelian; Philostratus The guardianship is territorial rather than intelligent: griffins do not collect or hoard gold like dragons; they simply inhabit gold-bearing regions and attack intruders. The Arimaspians, despite being mortal and one-eyed, steal the gold successfully enough to sustain a perpetual war — the griffin's guard is formidable but not absolute. Philostratus rationalizes the gold connection: griffins sharpen their beaks on gold-bearing rocks, leaving traces; the 'guard' is incidental to their habitat rather than purposeful hoarding. This conflicts with Pliny's statement that griffins actively dig gold from mines.

Superhuman strength and combat ability Aelian reports griffins fighting elephants and lions — the strongest land animals — and being hostile to horses. Pseudo-Callisthenes' Alexander Romance (3rd century CE) describes Alexander capturing two griffins and attempting to fly to heaven harnessed to them, which implies enormous physical strength. In art, griffins are repeatedly shown in combat with armed Arimaspian warriors, holding their own against human weapons. Aelian, NA 4.27; Pseudo-Callisthenes, Alexander Romance (later Greek recension); Greek vase painting The elephant-fighting claim is hyperbolic and from a late source (Aelian). No classical author reports a griffin actually killing an elephant. The Alexander Romance is fiction, not natural history. Ctesias describes griffins as wolf-sized — modest predators, not titanic beasts. The elephant-fighting griffin of Aelian represents either a different tradition or simple exaggeration over time.

Flight The griffin's wings give it mastery of the air — an ability no lion or eagle alone possesses in combination with the other's body. Flight enables the griffin to strike from above in combat and to reach remote mountain peaks. In art, griffins draw the airborne chariots of Apollo and Nemesis, implying sustained, powerful flight. All sources; iconography; Aeschylus, Prometheus Bound 802-806 (the griffins are encountered in a remote, high place approached via the 'griffin-breeding' steppe) Ctesias and Aelian treat griffins as primarily terrestrial (four-footed animals with feathers on the back), implying that their wings are for striking and display as much as sustained flight. No source describes the mechanics of griffin flight. The wingless Minoan griffins of the Knossos throne room pose a puzzle: if the earliest known Aegean griffins lack wings, the winged version may be a later development or a regional variant. Most scholars treat the wingless griffins as a Cretan artistic convention, not evidence of a separate tradition.

Eagle-keen senses (sight, hearing) As a composite of eagle and lion, the griffin is implied to possess the eagle's extraordinary visual acuity and the lion's acute hearing and sense of smell. This is not explicitly attributed in the sources but is implicit in its function as a guardian: a gold-guard in remote mountains must detect approaching thieves from a distance. Implicit in all guardian traditions; explicit in some bestiary descriptions (e.g., MS Bodley 764) The griffin is repeatedly robbed by the Arimaspians, suggesting that its senses, while sharp, can be evaded by cunning human thieves. No source provides specific feats of sensory detection. None significant.

Symbolic power: mastery of earth and air Beyond physical abilities, the griffin's composite form conveys a symbolic power: the lion (king of the earth) and the eagle (king of the air) combined into one creature that dominates both domains. In heraldry and medieval symbolism, this duality becomes theological: Christ's divine nature (eagle) and human nature (lion) united in one person. Medieval bestiaries; heraldic tradition; modern symbolic analysis This is interpretive/symbolic, not literal. Classical sources do not attribute symbolic meaning to the griffin's composite form — they treat it as a real animal with a physical description, not a symbol. The Christological reading is entirely absent from classical Greek and Roman sources; it is a medieval Christian appropriation of a pagan motif.

Accounts

Aristeas of Proconnesus, a poet said to have died and returned to life, composed the epic Arimaspea describing a journey to the far north inspired by Apollo. He reached the Issedones, a people beyond Scythia, and learned from them of the one-eyed Arimaspians further north, who fight griffins for the gold those creatures guard. Beyond the griffins live the Hyperboreans, a blessed people at the edge of the world. Aristeas himself went no further than the Issedones — all knowledge of griffins and Arimaspians was second-hand, which Herodotus duly notes. This account establishes the geographic chain (Issedones → Arimaspians → Griffins → Hyperboreans) that structures all later Greek accounts of the far north.

At the edge of the known world, a permanent war rages between two strange peoples: the griffins, who guard the gold of the Riphean Mountains, and the Arimaspians, a one-eyed tribe of Scythian horsemen who try to steal it. Herodotus makes the Arimaspians the aggressors — they 'snatch' (harpazousi) the gold. Aeschylus calls the griffins 'sharp-beaked hounds of Zeus who do not bark' and warns Io to avoid the gold-rich stream where 'the one-eyed Arimaspian host, horsemen' dwell. The war is never resolved in any surviving text; it is an eternal condition of the frontier — a mythic explanation for why gold comes from the dangerous margins of the world. The motif was a favorite subject of Greek vase painters, especially in the fourth century BCE on South Italian and Black Sea vases, where scenes of armed Arimaspians battling griffins are common.

In Greek art and cult, griffins are the sacred animals that draw the chariots of two deities: Apollo, the god of light, prophecy, and the Hyperboreans, and Nemesis, the goddess of divine retribution. Pausanias mentions 'the griffins that draw the chariot of Nemesis' in his description of the statue of Nemesis at Rhamnous. Pliny notes that griffins appear as a yoked pair in the temple art of Nemesis. The Apollo connection is more diffuse: griffins guard Apollo's gold among the Hyperboreans (Aristeas), Apollo is associated with swans and griffins in Hyperborean myth, and griffin protomes decorated the tripods and cauldrons at Delphi. The solar-Apolline griffin is often conflated with the griffin of Nemesis: both are winged, golden, swift, and associated with the inaccessible margins of the world.

In a late addition to the Alexander legend, the conqueror, having reached the end of the earth, wishes to explore the heavens. He captures two enormous griffins, starves them for three days, and then harnesses them to a chariot or throne. He places meat on spears above the griffins' heads; as they fly upward, straining to reach the meat, they carry Alexander into the sky. He ascends so high he can see the whole world spread beneath him like a threshing floor, and the ocean encircling the land like a serpent. Eventually divine power compels the griffins to descend, and Alexander returns to earth humbled. The story is not classical — no ancient Greek source connects Alexander to griffins — but it became one of the most widely reproduced griffin tales in the Middle Ages, appearing in illuminated manuscripts, stone carvings (e.g., the 'Flight of Alexander' on the façade of St. Mark's Basilica, Venice; at Otranto Cathedral), and vernacular romances.

In the procession of the Church Triumphant at the summit of Mount Purgatory, Dante sees a chariot (the Church) drawn by a griffin — a creature with the body of a lion and the head and wings of an eagle, but with the eagle parts golden and the lion parts white mixed with red (the colors of Christ's flesh and blood). The griffin is an explicit Christological symbol: the two natures (divine/eagle, human/lion) united in one person. Its wings reach beyond Dante's sight, and when it moves, only the lion parts stir the air (Christ's human nature affected the material world; his divine nature did not). The griffin is attended by the seven virtues and is beheld with awe by the poet. This is the most elaborate literary use of the griffin as a theological symbol, and it draws directly on the bestiary tradition.

Field Notes

❀ The griffin is one of the few mythological creatures that appears in art (c. 3000 BCE) a full 2,500 years before any surviving written description of it (Herodotus, c. 430 BCE).

❀ Bronze griffin-head protomes (cauldron attachments) were among the most numerous votive offerings at Delphi and Olympia — the griffin was literally guarding the gold and treasure of the gods.

❀ In British heraldry, a male griffin is depicted without wings but with rays or spikes on its body. This distinction is unique to British heraldry and has no classical precedent.

❀ The griffin's hatred of horses, reported by Aelian and preserved in medieval bestiaries, is the basis for Ariosto's joke in Orlando Furioso: the hippogriff is impossible because no griffin would ever mate with a mare.

❀ Adrienne Mayor's Protoceratops hypothesis — that griffin legends began with dinosaur fossils in the Gobi Desert — made the griffin a fixture of modern paleontology museum exhibits, including the American Museum of Natural History.

❀ The word 'griffin' is also the origin of 'gryffindor' (via French griffon d'or, 'golden griffin'), the Hogwarts house associated with courage and chivalry. Gryffindor's emblem is a lion — a partial griffin, the earth-half without the wings.

❀ Dante's griffin in Purgatorio is one of the very few creatures in the Divine Comedy that appears only in Paradise-adjacent territory — it is too sacred for Hell and too embodied for Heaven.

❀ The Minoan griffins flanking the Knossos throne are wingless — the only major example of wingless griffins in Aegean art. Archaeologists still debate whether this represents a separate Cretan variant or simply an artistic choice.

Cultural Significance

Heraldic symbol of courage, military leadership, and guardianship. Combines solar imagery (eagle) with terrestrial power (lion). Represents the union of intellect and strength.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of the griffin as the ancient world's answer to the question every culture asks: who guards the gold at the edge of the map? The Greeks placed their griffin in Scythia, the Romans with the Hyperboreans, the Indians in their own mountains — but the answer was always the same creature: the lion that rules the earth, combined with the eagle that rules the air, stationed at the frontier where the known world gives way to legend. The griffin is not a monster to be slain; it is a threshold to be crossed — or, more often, not crossed. The Arimaspians try and fail, generation after generation. Alexander tries and is sent back. Apollo and Nemesis alone command it, and even they keep it at the margins. The griffin is the guardian you don't fight; you pay it tribute, you honor it on your coins and your cauldrons, and you stay on your side of the mountain. Five thousand years of this, and we are still drawing it on coats of arms, still naming houses after it, still putting it in games as the mount you have to earn. Because what the griffin really guards is the idea that some things — gold, knowledge, the sacred, the summit — should be hard to reach.
