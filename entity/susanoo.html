Who It Is

A colossal, ethereal humanoid warrior made of chakra that surrounds the user, activated only by those who have awakened Mangekyō Sharingan in both eyes. Susano'o acts as a guardian spirit that manifests in stages, from a skeletal ribcage to a full armored titan wielding legendary weapons. Each user's Susano'o has unique coloration and weapons.

Form

In classical Japanese art, Susanoo is depicted as a powerful, often bearded male figure with a fierce expression, befitting his stormy nature. He is typically shown wearing traditional Japanese court or warrior attire, with a flowing beard that emphasizes his wild, untamed character. In the famous Yamata-no-Orochi slaying scene, he is shown wielding a sword (the Totsuka-no-Tsurugi, a ten-span sword) against the eight-headed dragon. Edo-period woodblock prints often portray him as a heroic warrior, sometimes with windswept hair and storm clouds around him. In some representations, he is associated with the color blue or indigo, reflecting his dominion over storms and the sea. In modern popular culture (video games like Ōkami, Shin Megami Tensei, and Persona), Susanoo's design varies widely — from a traditional samurai-like figure to a wild-haired, tattooed warrior radiating lightning and wind — reflecting his dual nature as both destroyer and protector.

Flight

Control Over Storms and the Sea As the kami of the sea and storms, Susanoo commands wind, rain, and ocean waters. His emotional states are reflected in the weather — his weeping causes mountains to wither and rivers to dry up, while his rage stirs typhoons and earthquakes. Kojiki, Nihon Shoki His power is tied to his emotional state; when calm, he is productive, but when enraged, his destruction is indiscriminate. Some variants in the Nihon Shoki assign sea dominion to other deities, suggesting Susanoo's control was contested in regional traditions.

Dragon-Slaying / Serpent-Slaying Susanoo's defining heroic feat: using eight vats of strong sake to intoxicate the eight-headed, eight-tailed serpent Yamata-no-Orochi, he then decapitates and dismembers it, discovering the Kusanagi sword in its tail. This establishes the archetypal Japanese dragon-slaying myth. Kojiki (primary), Nihon Shoki He relies on cunning (the sake trap) rather than raw strength alone; he could not defeat the serpent in a direct confrontation. The Nihon Shoki variants differ on details of the serpent's appearance and the exact method of slaying.

Shape-Shifting / Transformation Susanoo can transform into various forms: he transforms Kushinada-hime into a comb (kushi) to hide her in his hair during the battle with Orochi. In some traditions, he takes on animal forms. This power reflects his liminal, boundary-crossing nature. Kojiki The full extent of his shape-shifting is not elaborated in the primary texts; only specific transformations are recorded. None significant.

Generative / Civilizing Power After settling in Izumo, Susanoo demonstrates the power to name the land, establish dwellings, introduce useful trees (cedar, cypress, camphor), and prescribe agricultural practices. He fathers a divine lineage culminating in Ōkuninushi, the great earthly kami who completes the creation of Japan. Kojiki (the Izumo cycle), Nihon Shoki variant accounts His civilizing work is secondary to his violent exploits in most narrative accounts. Some traditions attribute the introduction of trees and agriculture to other kami.

Purification / Expulsion of Pollution Susanoo's very birth from Izanagi's purification ritual (misogi) makes him a product of cleansing. His later banishment from Takamagahara involves a ritual purification (harae) in which he must offer goods as compensation. This establishes the mythological basis for Shinto purification rituals. Kojiki He is more often the cause of pollution (through his destructive acts) than its cleanser. Some scholars argue his 'purification' association is a later reading imposed by ritual specialists.

Behavior & Habitat

Susanoo is defined by contradiction. He is simultaneously the most destructive and the most heroic of the major kami. His behavior in Takamagahara is petulant, violent, and transgressive — he destroys his sister's rice fields, defecates in her palace, and kills one of her weaving maidens with a flayed horse thrown through the roof. This behavior is not simple evil but rather the expression of an untamed, pre-socialized force. After his banishment, he transforms into a classic dragon-slaying hero, using cunning (the eight vats of sake, the transformation of Kushinada-hime into a comb) to defeat Yamata-no-Orochi. Throughout the Kojiki, he weeps for his mother Izanami, suggesting that his rampages are rooted in grief and the inability to accept death. His behavior establishes the pattern of the 'trickster hero' in Japanese mythology: destructive when unbound, productive when channeled toward a worthy goal.

Accounts

After Izanagi returns from Yomi (the underworld) having failed to retrieve his dead wife Izanami, he performs a purification ritual (misogi) in a river in Himuka (Hyūga). As he washes his left eye, Amaterasu (the sun goddess) is born; from his right eye, Tsukuyomi (the moon god) is born; from his nose, Susanoo is born. Izanagi assigns them domains: Amaterasu receives Takamagahara (the High Plain of Heaven), Tsukuyomi receives the night, and Susanoo receives the sea. Susanoo, however, refuses his domain, weeping and wailing for his mother Izanami, causing mountains to wither and rivers to dry up.

Before departing for Yomi to seek his mother, Susanoo ascends to Takamagahara to say farewell to Amaterasu. His violent ascent shakes the mountains and rivers, alarming Amaterasu who arms herself with a bow and arrows, suspecting invasion. Susanoo proposes a trial by oath (ukehi) to prove his sincerity: they each produce children from the other's sacred object. Amaterasu chews Susanoo's Totsuka sword and produces three goddesses; Susanoo chews Amaterasu's magatama jewels and produces five gods. Susanoo claims victory and celebrates by rampaging — breaking the ridges of Amaterasu's rice paddies, filling her irrigation ditches with excrement, and ultimately flinging a flayed heavenly piebald pony through the roof of her sacred weaving hall, killing one of her weaving maidens. Amaterasu, terrified and disgusted, hides in the Ama-no-Iwato (Heavenly Rock Cave), plunging the world into total darkness.

Banished from heaven, Susanoo descends to the headwaters of the Hi River in Izumo Province. He encounters an elderly couple, Ashinazuchi and Tenazuchi, weeping because the eight-headed, eight-tailed serpent Yamata-no-Orochi has devoured seven of their eight daughters. Their last daughter, Kushinada-hime, is next. Susanoo, in exchange for Kushinada-hime's hand in marriage, devises a plan. He has the couple brew eight vats of strong sake and place them behind a fence with eight gates. The serpent arrives, dips each head into a vat, drinks itself into a stupor, and falls asleep. Susanoo then decapitates and dismembers the serpent with his Totsuka sword. When he cuts the middle tail, his blade chips — inside he discovers the Kusanagi-no-Tsurugi (later the Grass-Cutting Sword), which he presents to Amaterasu as a peace offering. He then builds a palace in Izumo at Suga ('pure/purifying') and marries Kushinada-hime.

After establishing himself in Izumo, Susanoo eventually moves to Ne-no-Kuni (the Netherworld / Root Land), a shadowy underworld realm associated with his mother Izanami. His descendant Ōkuninushi, fleeing his eighty jealous brothers, descends to Ne-no-Kuni seeking Susanoo's aid. Susanoo subjects Ōkuninushi to a series of trials: sleeping in a room of snakes, then centipedes and wasps; having an arrow shot into a vast field and being ordered to retrieve it (Susanoo then sets the field on fire); and having a centipede removed from his hair. With the help of Susanoo's daughter Suseri-bime, who provides protective charms (a scarf to ward off snakes and centipedes, a mouse that retrieves the arrow), Ōkuninushi survives each ordeal. Finally, Ōkuninushi ties Susanoo's hair to the rafters, steals Susanoo's life-giving sword (Ikutachi), bow and arrows (Ikuyumiya), and heavenly speaking lute (Amenonorigoto), and flees with Suseri-bime. Susanoo, awakened by the sound of the lute, pursues them but stops at the border of Ne-no-Kuni, shouting advice to Ōkuninushi to use the weapons to defeat his brothers and become the ruler of the earthly realm.

Field Notes

۩ Susanoo's transformation of Kushinada-hime into a comb (kushi) is a pun: the word 'kushi' means both 'comb' and 'mysterious/wondrous' in Old Japanese, playing on the idea that he makes her both hidden and wondrous.

۩ The Yamata-no-Orochi's body, after being slain, is said to have become the source of Izumo's fertile soil — making the serpent a kind of primordial dragon whose death fertilizes the land.

۩ In the Kojiki, Susanoo's first words after descending to Izumo are a poem — one of the earliest recorded Japanese poems (a tanka), making him, bizarrely, a founding figure of Japanese poetry as well as storm god.

۩ The Gion Matsuri in Kyoto, one of Japan's most famous festivals, originally honored Susanoo (as Gozu Tennō) to ward off a plague in 869 CE. It has been held almost every year since.

۩ Susanoo's fingernails and toenails, pulled out during his banishment, were said to have become new deities — a detail so grotesque and generative that it perfectly captures his essence.

۩ In the video game Ōkami, Susanoo is initially depicted as a lazy, boastful fraud who takes credit for the player's actions — a comedic deconstruction of his mythological heroism that gradually becomes sincere.

What We Don't Know

The exact etymology of 'Susanoo' remains unresolved, with proposals ranging from 'impetuous male' to a place-name origin (Susa in Izumo). The precise relationship between the Izumo regional cult of Susanoo and the Yamato imperial mythology is incompletely understood — to what degree did the Yamato court appropriate, suppress, or genuinely integrate Izumo traditions? The identification of Susanoo with Gozu Tennō in the Gion cult merits further investigation into the Buddhist-Shinto syncretic process. The archaeological record for Susanoo worship in pre-Kojiki Izumo (particularly at sites like the Izumo Taisha) could provide evidence for the pre-textual cult. The variant Nihon Shoki accounts that present Susanoo more positively as a culture hero need more systematic comparison with the Kojiki version. The survival of Susanoo motifs in regional folklore outside the official chronicles (particularly in Kyushu and the Japan Sea coast) is underexplored.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Susanoo not as a god who 'becomes good,' but as raw, undomesticated power that finds its purpose. He is the storm you can't control — until you understand that the same wind that destroys your rice field also drives the ship that brings your harvest home. In the logic of Shinto, purity is not the absence of chaos but the proper channeling of it. Susanoo's arc — from celestial vandal to dragon-slaying culture hero to underworld ancestor — is the arc of civilization itself: the wild boy of heaven descends, learns to direct his violence against true threats rather than his own family, and becomes the ancestral power behind earthly rule. He is not Amaterasu's opposite; he is her shadow, the turbulence that proves the value of her order. And in his ultimate gift — the Kusanagi sword, offered freely to the sister he once terrorized — we see the final truth: the chaos principle, when mature, voluntarily submits to the order it once threatened, without losing its essential power. That is the miracle of Susanoo, and it's why he has outlasted every attempt to domesticate him.
