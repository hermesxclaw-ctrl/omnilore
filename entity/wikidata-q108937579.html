Who It Is

It is bright as a fresh wound and dark as old wine, it carries your last breath to your fingertips and your last meal back to your liver, and you have about five liters of it — a private ocean that never stops moving. Blood looks simple until you ask what it actually is: a tissue that behaves like a fluid, a river that makes its own banks, a symbol so powerful that religions drink it metaphorically and empires have killed for its purity.

Not a person, but behavior as fluid tissue: Exhibits non-Newtonian, shear-thinning flow — viscosity decreases at high shear rates (in large arteries) and increases in microcirculation due to erythrocyte aggregation (rouleaux). Maintains homeostasis via buffering (bicarbonate, hemoglobin, plasma proteins), coagulation cascade (intrinsic/extrinsic pathways converging on fibrin), and immune surveillance (leukocyte chemotaxis, phagocytosis, antibody circulation). Powers
Oxygen and Carbon Dioxide Transport

Hemoglobin in erythrocytes reversibly binds O2 in lungs (98.5% of blood O2 is hemoglobin-bound, 1.5% dissolved) and releases it in tissues; carries ~20-25% of CO2 as carbaminohemoglobin and buffers CO2 as bicarbonate via carbonic anhydrase. One gram hemoglobin binds ~1.34 mL O2; 15 g/dL yields ~20 mL O2/dL blood.

Limit: Limited by hemoglobin concentration (anemia reduces capacity), lung diffusion, cardiac output, and hemoglobin affinity (affected by pH, CO2, 2,3-BPG, temperature — Bohr effect). Carbon monoxide binds 200-250x more avidly, blocking transport. Fails at altitude without acclimatization.

Nutrient and Waste Shuttling

Plasma dissolves and plasma proteins (albumin, lipoproteins) carry glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, vitamins to cells; returns urea, creatinine, bilirubin, lactic acid to liver/kidney/lungs for excretion. Albumin maintains oncotic pressure (~25 mmHg) preventing edema.

Limit: Dependent on liver synthesis of albumin and lipoproteins; hypoalbuminemia causes edema. Cannot transport hydrophobic lipids without carriers; hyperlipidemia reflects overload.

Hemostasis and Coagulation

Platelets adhere to exposed collagen, aggregate (via ADP, thromboxane A2), and provide phospholipid surface for coagulation cascade (12+ factors) culminating in fibrin mesh stabilizing platelet plug. Fibrinolysis (plasmin) later dissolves clot.

Limit: Requires calcium, vitamin K-dependent factors (II, VII, IX, X), and intact endothelium. Deficiency (hemophilia A/B, von Willebrand disease) or anticoagulants (warfarin, heparin) prolong bleeding. Excessive clotting causes thrombosis, stroke, pulmonary embolism.

Immune Defense and Surveillance

Leukocytes (neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, basophils) circulate, extravasate, phagocytose pathogens, present antigens, and produce antibodies (immunoglobulins in plasma). Complement proteins lyse microbes. Adaptive immunity (jawed vertebrates) via T/B cells with recombination.

Limit: Self-tolerance can break (autoimmunity); immunodeficiency (e.g., leukopenia, HIV targeting CD4+ T cells) impairs defense; blood is also route for systemic spread of pathogens (bacteremia, viremia).

Hormonal and Signaling Transport (Messenger Function)

Endocrine hormones (insulin, cortisol, thyroxine, sex steroids bound to SHBG, etc.) travel in plasma to distant targets; cytokines, chemokines signal damage/infection; platelets release growth factors (PDGF, TGF-beta) for wound healing.

Limit: Hormone half-life short; bound vs free fraction determines activity; blood-brain barrier restricts access to CNS.

pH Buffering and Homeostatic Regulation

Bicarbonate (HCO3-/CO2), hemoglobin/histidine, and plasma proteins buffer pH to 7.35-7.45; hemoglobin also buffers H+ via histidine residues. Electrolytes (Na+ 135-145, K+ 3.5-5.0 mmol/L) regulated by kidney and hormones (aldosterone, ADH).

Limit: Fails in acidosis (<7.35) or alkalosis (>7.45); pH <6.9 or >7.8 usually lethal. Compensation via hyperventilation (respiratory) and renal excretion limited in speed.

Thermoregulation and Hydraulic Function

Blood redistributes heat from core to skin for dissipation (vasodilation) or conserves heat (vasoconstriction); high heat capacity of water in plasma. Hydraulic turgor supports erection of corpora cavernosa, glomerular filtration pressure, and muscle perfusion.

Limit: Heat stroke when dissipation fails; hydraulic failure in shock (hypovolemia, distributive shock) drops perfusion pressure.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Attested: Freshly oxygenated arterial blood is bright scarlet-red (oxyhemoglobin reflects red wavelengths, absorbs blue-green). Venous blood is dark maroon / brownish-red (deoxyhemoglobin), not blue — blue appearance of veins is an optical effect of skin scattering. Symbols — Cross-culturally attested as symbol of life, vitality, kinship, sacrifice, guilt, and pollution. Ancient Near East and Mediterranean: blood as seat of life/soul (Hebrew nefesh in Leviticus 17:11 'the life of the flesh is in the blood'; Greek haima as psyche). Christianity: Eucharistic wine as blood of Christ (transubstantiation vs. symbolism debated). Shinto: chi as kegare (ritual impurity) requiring purification. European heraldry: gul

Domains — Habitat is the closed circulatory system of vertebrates: heart (pump), arteries/arterioles (high pressure distribution), capillaries (exchange, ~10 billion, total surface ~500-700 m²), venules/veins (low pressure return with valves), plus lymphatic return. Volume: ~7% body weight, ~70 mL/kg in adult human (4.5-6 L). Production niche: red marrow of axial skeleton (vertebrae, sternum, ribs, pelvis, proximal femur/humerus) and, in fetus, liver/splee

Accounts I
William Harvey Proves Blood CirculatesHarvey, De Motu Cordis (1628); contemporary reactions in Riolan, Primrose

1628-1650s

Building on Fabricius' venous valves and his own vivisections of dogs, snakes, and human cadavers, Harvey argues blood does not ebb and flow or get consumed by tissues (Galen) but moves in a circle: left ventricle → aorta → arteries → tissues → veins → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary veins → left atrium. His quantitative proof — heart ejects ~2 oz per beat × 72 beats/min = ~540 pounds per hour, far more than body weight or ingested food — makes one-way consumption impossible. He predicts invisible porosities (capillaries) connecting arteries and veins. Foundational for modern physiology and experimental medicine; enables later transfusion, intravenous therapy, and hemodynamics. Malpighi's 1661 capillary observation confirms prediction.

The First Documented Blood Transfusions and the Death of Antoine MauroyJean-Baptiste Denis, Philosophical Transactions 1667-68; Henry Oldenburg correspondence; Denis trial records 1668

1665-1668

After Richard Lower's dog-to-dog transfusions (1665), French physician Jean-Baptiste Denis transfuses lamb blood into 15-year-old boy with fever (June 15, 1667) — boy survives. Second transfusion to laborer also survives. Third case: Antoine Mauroy, a 34-year-old man with violent mania, receives calf blood twice; after second transfusion he dies. Denis is accused of murder, acquitted when autopsy suggests arsenic poisoning by wife, but French Parlement bans transfusion without Faculty approval, effectively halting human transfusion for 150 years. Demonstrates both possibility and lethal risk of xenotransfusion (later understood as hemolysis due to ABO incompatibility and species antigens). Ban delays transfusion until James Blundell’s human-to-human transfusions for postpartum hemorrhage (1818-1829).

Karl Landsteiner Discovers ABO GroupsLandsteiner 1901 Wiener klinische Wochenschrift; Landsteiner & Wiener 1940 Rh factor; Decastello & Sturli 1902 AB group

1900-1901, extended 1907-1940

Landsteiner, working in Vienna, mixes sera and red cells from colleagues: some combinations agglutinate, others not. He defines groups A, B, and C (later O); colleagues Adriano Sturli and Alfred von Decastello add AB (1902). Czech Jan Janský (1907) and American William Moss (1910) independently classify but with numbering confusion. Ottenberg (1907) first suggests cross-matching before transfusion. Landsteiner later co-discovers Rh factor with Alexander Wiener (1940). Makes transfusion safe and routine; enables surgery, trauma care, and later organ transplantation. Landsteiner receives Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine 1930. ABO genetics becomes first human trait with clear Mendelian inheritance (Bernstein 1924).

Accounts II
Charles Drew, Blood Banks, and the Color LineDrew thesis 1940; Blood for Britain project 1940-41; American Red Cross records; Love 1996 biography

1938-1950

Surgeon Charles Drew (1904-1950), African American pioneer, refines techniques for large-scale collection, processing, and storage of plasma (and later whole blood), demonstrating that plasma can be dried and reconstituted and that blood can be preserved with citrate-glucose. As medical director of Blood for Britain and first director of American Red Cross Blood Bank, he organizes mass donation. When War Department orders blood segregated by race (claiming Black blood different), Drew publicly denounces the policy as scientifically baseless and resigns in 1941. Establishes modern blood banking logistics still used; exposes scientific racism in medicine. Drew's later death after car accident spawns false myth that he was denied transfusion due to race — actually he received aggressive care but injuries were unsurvivable.

'The Life of the Flesh Is in the Blood' — Blood in Leviticus and Its AfterlivesLeviticus 17:10-14; Genesis 9:4-6; Talmud Keritot; Hebrews 9:11-22; Qur'an 2:173, 5:3, 6:145

6th century BCE (Priestly source) to present

Priestly law prohibits consuming blood: 'for the life (nefesh) of the flesh is in the blood, and I have given it to you upon the altar to make atonement.' Blood of slaughtered animals must be drained or covered with earth; blood of sacrifices is dashed on altar. This underlies kashrut (salting/soaking meat), Christian debate over Eucharist (literal vs symbolic blood), and Islamic prohibition of damm (flowing blood) as impure. Levitical bloodguilt also frames Genesis 9's prohibition of murder: 'whoever sheds the blood of man, by man shall his blood be shed.' Shapes dietary law, ritual slaughter (shechita, dhabihah), concepts of atonement and martyrdom, and — distorted — the medieval blood libel that Jews used Christian blood (see dark history). Modern Jewish and Muslim bioethics debates over blood transfusion and blood products reference these texts.

The Contaminated Blood Scandals: HIV and Hepatitis in the Blood SupplyCDC MMWR 1982-85; Institute of Medicine 'HIV and the Blood Supply' 1995; UK Infected Blood Inquiry Report 2024; French 'sang contaminé' trials 1991-93

1981-1995 (with inquiries to 2024)

Before HIV was identified and heat-treatment of clotting factors available, tens of thousands of hemophiliacs and transfusion recipients were infected with HIV and hepatitis C via pooled factor VIII/IX concentrates and whole blood. In France, officials delayed screening and heat-treatment for financial reasons; ministers were tried for manslaughter. In UK, ~30,000 infected, ~3,000 dead, covered up for decades. In US, CDC warnings in 1982-83 were resisted by blood industry and FDA. Screening (ELISA 1985) and viral inactivation ended most transmission, but litigation and public trust damage persist. Reforms: donor deferral, nucleic acid testing, pathogen reduction, hemovigilance systems, and compensation schemes (UK £10bn+ announced 2024). Profound loss of trust, especially among hemophilia and LGBTQ communities, and precedent for precautionary principle in blood policy.

Blood is blue inside your veins and turns red when it hits air. — Blood is always red — bright scarlet when oxygenated, dark maroon when deoxygenated. Veins look blue through skin due to light scattering, and textbook diagrams color deoxygenated blood blue for contrast. Venipuncture shows dark red venous blood before any air exposure.

Humans have exactly 8 pints / 5 liters of blood, and you can lose a lot without consequence. — Average adult has ~4.5-6 L (~9.5-12.6 US pints), varying by size, sex, and weight (~70 mL/kg). Loss of >15% (~750 mL) causes tachycardia and hypotension; >30-40% is life-threatening hemorrhagic shock requiring transfusion. Even donation (470 mL) causes transient volume drop and requires recovery.

Bloodletting was just medieval ignorance and never helped anyone. — Within humoral theory it was rational, and for a few conditions it does help — hereditary hemochromatosis (iron overload), polycythemia vera, and pulmonary edema historically. For most fevers and infections it was ineffective or harmful, and its overuse caused deaths. Modern therapeutic phlebotomy is evidence-based for specific indications only.

Your blood type determines your personality, romantic compatibility, and what you should eat. — No peer-reviewed evidence supports ABO-personality or ABO-diet links. ABO does affect risk for some diseases (e.g., O vs non-O for clotting, malaria severity) and transfusion compatibility, but not temperament. The belief is culturally strong in Japan/Korea and in diet fads but is pseudoscience.

If you drink blood or eat bloody meat you will get strength or disease directly from the animal's traits. — Ingested blood is digested as protein/iron like other food; it does not transfer traits, courage, or directly cause infection via traits, though undercooked blood can transmit pathogens (e.g., hepatitis E, bacteria) and excessive iron can be harmful. Cultural beliefs about consuming blood for vitality are symbolic, not physiological.
