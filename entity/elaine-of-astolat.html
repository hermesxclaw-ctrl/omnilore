I am the Lily Maid of Astolat. I loved Sir Lancelot, and he could not love me back. Let my body float to Camelot, and let them see what love can do when it is given and not returned.

Who It Is

Elaine of Astolat is the 'Lily Maid,' the young noblewoman who dies of unrequited love for Sir Lancelot — one of the most purely tragic figures in Arthurian legend, immortalized by Tennyson in 'The Lady of Shalott' and 'Lancelot and Elaine' (from Idylls of the King). She is sometimes confused with Elaine of Corbenic (the mother of Galahad) and Elaine of Garlot (Arthur's half-sister), but the Astolat Elaine is distinct: she is defined solely by her love, her rejection, and her beautiful, terrible death.

Elaine's story, as told by Malory, begins when Lancelot stays at her father's castle in Astolat before a tournament. He borrows a shield and armor not his own, so he can compete unrecognized. Elaine nurses him when he is wounded and falls completely, hopelessly in love. She asks him to be her husband — or at least her lover. Lancelot gently refuses: his heart belongs to Guinevere, and he has sworn never to marry. Elaine, unable to accept this, makes a second offer: 'Then will you be my paramour?' Lancelot refuses again, appalled — he is not that kind of man. Elaine takes to her bed, refuses food and drink, and wastes away.

Before she dies, Elaine dictates a letter to be placed in her hand. Her body is placed in a richly adorned barge, with a lily in one hand and the letter in the other. The barge is set adrift on the Thames, steered by a mute servant, and floats all the way to Westminster, where Arthur's court is assembled. The court is stunned by the sight: a beautiful dead maiden, arrayed like a bride, floating down the river. Lancelot reads the letter, which asks that he and Guinevere pray for her soul and that she be buried as 'a maiden who died for love of Sir Lancelot du Lac.' Lancelot pays for a rich burial and grieves — not with the passion of a lover but with the sorrow of a man who recognizes his unintended cruelty.

Tennyson transformed Elaine into the Lady of Shalott — an artist, a weaver, who lives under a mysterious curse and can only view the world through a mirror. When she sees Lancelot's reflection and turns to look at him directly, the curse falls, and she floats to Camelot in a barge, singing her death song. This romantic elaboration makes Elaine a symbol of the isolated artist and the destructive power of unmediated vision — but it also removes her agency, making her a passive victim rather than the active, passionate woman of Malory's version who knows what she wants and dies when she cannot have it.

Elaine's death is a judgment on the courtly love system that produced her. Lancelot's courtesy — his refusal to love her, framed as honor — is also a form of cruelty. He cannot give her what she needs, but his very presence, his superlative knighthood and beauty, makes it impossible for her to want anything else. Elaine is the cost of Lancelot's devotion to Guinevere: the collateral damage of a love too absolute to admit any other.
