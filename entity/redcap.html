Archive Dossier — Identity

Redcap

Pronunciation /ˈrɛdˌkæp/

Known As

Etymology

From 'red cap', referring to the cap soaked in blood. 'Powrie' may derive from Scots 'pourie' or 'powrie' meaning a goblin or imp. 'Redcomb' perhaps from 'red' and 'comb' (crest) or a variant of 'cap'.

Cultural Ownership

Anglo-Scottish border region, especially ruined castles in Scotland and northern England. Part of broader British folklore of goblins and household spirits.

Disputed Identifications

Sometimes confused with other goblins like the hobgoblin Robin Roundcap of East Yorkshire, or with brownie-like beings called 'redcaps' in Dutch folklore. The term 'redcap' is also used generically for fairies wearing red caps in Cornwall.

Who It Is

The redcap is the most murderous of all British fairies — a squat old goblin who haunts the ruined fortresses of the Anglo-Scottish Border, exactly the castles with the bloodiest histories, and there is a reason for the address: the redcap dyes his cap in human blood, and the blood must be kept fresh. If it dries, he dies. So he waits in the broken towers for benighted travelers, and he does not curse or trick them — he drops boulders on them, or tears them with iron talons, and re-dyes his cap. He wears heavy iron boots yet outruns any man; he carries a pikestaff in a grip too strong to break. The redcap is the Border's history distilled into a monster: three centuries of raid, reprisal, and massacre soaked into the stones until the stones grew a resident. His one weakness is scripture — read a verse of the Bible aloud, or hold up a cross-handled sword, and he vanishes with a howl, leaving behind a single long tooth. The most famous redcap of all, Robin Redcap, was the familiar of the genuinely historical Lord Soulis of Hermitage Castle — a man so hated that legend insists his tenants boiled him alive in a brass cauldron.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

A short, thickset old elf with long prominent teeth, skinny fingers armed with talons like eagles, large eyes of fiery red colour, grisly hair streaming down his shoulders, iron boots, a pikestaff in his left hand, and a red cap on his head. The cap is soaked in the blood of victims, giving it a crimson hue.

Symbols & Iconography

The red cap itself is the defining symbol, representing his bloodthirsty nature. Iron boots and pikestaff are also characteristic. In some depictions, he leaves behind a large tooth when banished.

Behaviour & Personality

Malevolent and murderous, the redcap inhabits ruined castles and attacks travellers who take refuge. He flings stones at them, and if he kills them, he soaks his cap in their blood. He is unaffected by human strength but is repelled by scripture or a crucifix, vanishing in flames with a yell.

Domains & Habitats

Ruined castles along the Anglo-Scottish border, especially those associated with tyranny or wicked deeds. Examples include Hermitage Castle and Blackett Tower.

Version Differences

A Perthshire variant depicts a more benign redcap who brings good fortune. The Dutch kabouter (redcap) is akin to a brownie. The generic use in Cornwall refers to fairies with red caps. The term 'redcap' also applies to a ghostly 'Old Red Cap' at Blackett Tower.

Origins

Border folklore of the peel towers, collected in the era of Walter Scott. The redcap concentrates the region's raiding-age carnage into a single site-bound predator — the genius loci of a massacre.

A short, thickset old man with long white hair, fire-red eyes, protruding tusks, iron boots, a pikestaff, taloned hands — and the wet crimson cap that is his life and his ledger.

Powers

Boulder-dropping ambush, unnatural speed despite iron boots, talon combat, and the vitality of every murder stored in the cap's dye.

Weaknesses & Wards

Dried blood — his cap is an hourglass; holy words and the cross-hilted sword banish him instantly, tooth left behind as a receipt.

Archive Dossier — Archivist

Known Gaps

Primary sources are mostly literary retellings from the 19th century; earlier oral traditions are not well documented. The Perthshire variant is not detailed. The exact origin of the redcap is unclear. Modern adaptations are numerous but not exhaustively indexed. Some sources may be in Scots or other dialects not fully translated.

Research Scope

This dossier focuses on the redcap as a specific entity in Anglo-Scottish border folklore, distinguishing it from other uses of the term. It draws on 19th-century collections (Walter Scott), regional variants, and modern reception. Primary sources are limited; much is transmitted through literary retellings.

Cultural Significance

The redcap made the leap from Border tales into the global fantasy bestiary — Harry Potter, Dungeons and Dragons, and modern folklore-horror all keep him — always with the cap, always with the arithmetic of fresh blood.
