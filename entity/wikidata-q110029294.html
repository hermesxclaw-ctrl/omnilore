Who It Is

In the lightless World of Darkness that Mandaean scripture places beneath and before our own, there reigns a queen who never sees the sun. Her name is Qin — mother of Ruha, consort of Anathan, grandmother of the devouring dragon Ur. She does not rule by decree but by fecundity: from her womb the entire dark lineage that will war against the Lightworld pours forth. To understand Qin is to understand why Mandaeans pray facing north, toward the Light, with their backs firmly turned to her domain.

Qin is not a trickster or tempter in the Christian sense; she is a cosmogonic principle. Behavior attested in Ginza: a) She dwells passively in Darkness until emanation/crisis forces interaction with Lightworld. b) She mates with Anathan (Anatan), her brother/consort, producing Ruha and Zahreil. The union is incestuous and presented as characteristic of Darkness (vs. Lightworld's pure emanations without sexuality). c) She delegates agency to her daughter Ruha, who is far more active in narratives (Ruha creates Ur, attempts to seduce Lightworld figures, causes the Flood). Powers
Progenitrix of Darkness

Qin generates the entire dark lineage through sexual reproduction with Anathan. Offspring include Ruha (Spirit) and Zahreil (also called Ezlat). Through Ruha, she is grandmother of Ur (the great dragon/serpent) and mother-in-law of concepts like sin and defilement. This is cosmogonic fecundity, not creation ex nihilo; she can only produce within Darkness.

Limit: Cannot create Light or Living Waters; cannot produce uthras; her progeny are inherently flawed and rebellious even against her. Power is bounded to Darkworld.

Dominion over Black Waters and Darkness

Qin dwells in and rules the Black Waters (mia siawia), the aqueous substrate of Darkness. The waters obey her as habitat; she is not described as manipulating them magically but as enthroned within them. Darkness itself is her element and extension.

Limit: Cannot enter Lightworld; Light dissolves Darkness on contact. Mandaean baptism (masbuta) in Living Waters repels her influence.

Delegated Corruption via Ruha

Qin does not act directly on Earth but works through Ruha, who ascends from Darkness, seduces, deceives, and creates. Ruha's powers (shape-shifting, false prophecy, creation of Ur, causing Flood) are extensions of Qin's lineage. Prayers ward against 'Ruha and her mother Qin' as a pair, suggesting Qin empowers Ruha.

Limit: Requires Ruha as intermediary; Qin herself never leaves Darkworld in any attested narrative.

Endurance Until Eschaton

Qin is not killable within time; she endures until the end of days when Lightworld will purge Darkness with fire and Living Waters. Left Ginza describes final annihilation of Darkworld, implying Qin's destruction then, not before.

Limit: Not immortal in absolute sense; eschatology promises her annihilation. Cannot prevent eventual defeat.

No Attested Shapeshifting or Combat Magic

Unlike Ruha and Ur, Qin has no attested transformations, spells, or direct combat feats. She does not fight Hibil-Ziwa or Manda d-Haiyi; her children do. This absence is significant and distinguishes her from more active demons.

Limit: Cannot be credited with powers not in texts; do not merge Ruha's or Ur's abilities onto Qin.

Accounts I
The Brood in the Black Waters — Qin and Anathan Beget Ruha and ZahreilGinza Rabba, Right Ginza Book 3 (Lidzbarski p. 75-84; Drower 1937 p. 58-62)

Literary form 3rd-7th century CE; oral traditions earlier

In the World of Darkness that existed before and below the Lightworld, there were two primal beings: Anathan and his sister-consort Qin. In the black, stinking waters they coupled incestuously, as is the nature of Darkness. Qin conceived and bore Ruha (Spirit, Rūhā) and her sister Zahreil (also Ezlat). The text emphasizes the fetid, cold, lightless habitat and the contrast with Lightworld's pure emanations which occur without sexuality. This is not a love story but an etiology of evil's origin: from Qin's womb comes the lineage that will trouble Earth. Establishes the dark genealogy; Ruha will later ascend and become the primary antagonist of Lightworld and creator of Ur. Qin thereafter recedes as progenitor rather than actor.

Ruha's Ascent from Qin's WorldGinza Rabba, Right Ginza 5.1 (Lidzbarski p. 178-185; Buckley 2002 p. 48-50)

3rd-7th century CE compilation

Ruha, daughter of Qin, leaves her mother's black waters and ascends toward the boundary of Light. She is described as restless and ambitious, unlike her mother who remains enthroned. Ruha attempts to create a world of her own, imitating Lightworld creators but producing only flawed, dark creations. Qin does not accompany her; she remains below, but prayers later pair them as 'Ruha and her mother Qin' when warding. The story marks the transition from Qin's passive generation to Ruha's active antagonism. Ruha's ascent leads to her seduction of Abatur's son Ptahil and to the flawed creation of Earth (Tibil). Qin's influence becomes indirect.

Qin in the Qolasta — Liturgical WardingQolasta (Canonical Prayerbook) Prayers 9, 18, 170, 379 (Drower 1959)

Prayers in present form late antiquity to early Islamic period; manuscripts 16th century+

In daily and baptismal prayers, priests and laypeople invoke protection: 'We are held, we are not held by Ruha and her mother Qin and all her brood' and 'May Ruha and Qin and their sons be bound.' Qin appears not in narrative but in apotropaic lists, always paired with Ruha. The prayers are recited during masbuta (baptism) in Living Waters, which are the ritual antithesis of Qin's Black Waters. The story here is ritual rather than mythic: the community continuously re-enacts separation from Qin. Demonstrates Qin's ongoing theological presence in living practice, not just ancient myth. Shows she is warded, not worshipped.

Accounts II
The Defeat of Qin's Grandson Ur by Hibil-ZiwaGinza Rabba Right Ginza 5.3 and Diwan Abatur (Drower 1953; Buckley 2010)

3rd-7th century CE

Ur, the great dragon/serpent, son of Ruha and thus grandson of Qin, grows monstrous and threatens both Darkworld and Earth. He devours, coils around mountains, and embodies Qin's lineage at its most destructive. The Lightworld sends Hibil-Ziwa (Radiance) who descends, binds Ur with chains of light, and imprisons him. Qin herself is not present at the battle; her power is represented through Ur. The text makes clear that Light can bind but not yet annihilate Darkness; Ur is fettered until the end of days, implying Qin's world is similarly contained but not destroyed. Explains why evil is constrained but not eliminated; sets up eschatology where Qin's entire world will be purged.

Eschaton — The Annihilation of Qin's WorldGinza Rabba Left Ginza 1.4-1.5; Right Ginza 5.5 (Lidzbarski p. 429-444, 198-205)

3rd-7th century CE, with Islamic-period redaction

At the end of time, after the final judgment of souls, the Lightworld will send a great fire and Living Waters to purge the World of Darkness. The Black Waters will be dried, the dark mountain consumed, and Qin, Anathan, Ruha, Zahreil, Ur and all their brood will be annihilated or eternally bound. Left Ginza describes this as the 'end of Ruha and her mother Qin.' It is not a battle Qin fights; it is an unmaking. The Lightworld will then be sealed and perfected. This is the only 'death' of Qin attested. Provides Mandaean theodicy: evil is real, powerful, but finite and doomed. Qin's story ends in erasure, not redemption.

Homonym Story: The Historical Qin State Becomes Empire (for disambiguation)Sima Qian, Shiji Chapters 5-6; also Zuo Zhuan and Zhanguo Ce

Historical events 771-206 BCE; texts compiled 4th century BCE - 94 BCE

The polity named Qin (秦), originally a horse-breeding fief on the western frontier, grows through Legalist reforms of Shang Yang, conquests under King Zheng, and in 221 BCE unifies China as the Qin dynasty, first imperial dynasty. It standardizes script, currency, and measures, builds early Great Wall, and falls rapidly after Qin Shi Huang's death. This 'Qin' is a human historical state, not a mythical beast, but shares romanization and character with the Mandaean name by coincidence. Its story is included here to prevent conflation. Gives China its name (via 'Qin' → 'China' in many languages); creates imperial model. No connection to Mandaean Qin except lexical homography in English.

Qin was a Chinese dragon or beast that became the Qin dynasty's namesake. — Chinese Qin (秦) is a historical polity name of unknown etymology, not a beast. The dynasty was not named after a creature. Mandaean Qin is an unrelated Aramaic name. No Chinese text links a beast Qin to the state.

Qin is a goddess worshipped by Mandaeans. — Mandaeans do not worship Qin; they ward against her. She is a demon-queen of Darkness, the antagonist of their Lightworld religion. Prayers ask for protection from her, not devotion to her.

Qin and Ruha are the same figure. — Qin is mother, Ruha is daughter. They appear together in prayers as 'Ruha and her mother Qin' — two distinct figures. Ruha is far more active in narratives.

Images online of a Chinese-style dragon labeled 'Qin' are authentic depictions of the Mandaean demon. — No authentic Mandaean image of Qin exists. Mandaean art is largely textual/diagrammatic. Chinese dragon images belong to unrelated Chinese tradition; using them for Qin is cultural conflation and misattribution.

Qin appears as a powerful playable demon in Shin Megami Tensei or similar games. — No verified appearance of Mandaean Qin in major game franchises as of 2026. Some demon lists include her speculatively, but she is not in Shin Megami Tensei, Persona, or Fate/Grand Order rosters. Appearances of 'Qin' in games (e.g., Qin: Tomb of the Middle Kingdom) refer to Chinese Qin dynasty homonym.

The Guqin instrument is named after the Qin dynasty or the demon Qin. — Guqin (古琴) uses character 琴 (qin = zither), different from 秦 (Qin state). The instrument's name means 'ancient zither' and predates the dynasty's naming in that form; the demon Qin is unrelated Mandaic word.
