The Approach

In the celestial bureaucracy of Chinese mythology, a giant rooster stands ready to crow down demons—this is Maori Xingguan, the Sun Rooster of the Hairy Head, a star deity whose comb shines with the light of dawn and whose cry silences the scorpion's sting.

Content advisory. None.
Identity

Maori Xingguan

Pronunciation Mǎo Rì Xīng Guān (Mandarin)

Known As

昴日星官Sun Rooster of Hairy HeadHuang CangMao Ri Xing Guan

Etymology

The Chinese name 昴日星官 (Mǎo Rì Xīngguān) literally means 'Sun Rooster Star Official of the Hairy Head mansion'. 'Mao' (昴) refers to the Pleiades star cluster, one of the 28 lunar mansions in Chinese astronomy, and 'Ri' (日) means sun, 'Xingguan' (星官) means star official or star deity. The name thus ties the deity to the solar aspect of the Pleiades mansion.

Cultural Ownership

Chinese folk religion, Taoism, and classical Chinese literature; also appears in Chinese Buddhist-influenced narratives.

Disputed Identifications

Some modern sources conflate Maori Xingguan with the star god of the Pleiades in Western astrology, but this is anachronistic. In the Fengshen Yanyi, he is identified as Huang Cang, a giant rooster, but this origin is not mentioned in Journey to the West, where he is simply a star god and son of a bodhisattva.

Description

Appearance

In Journey to the West, Maori Xingguan is described as a star god who can transform into a giant rooster, six or seven feet tall, with two combs. In his divine form, he likely appears as a celestial official, but the text focuses on his rooster transformation.

Symbols & Iconography

Rooster, comb, sun, Pleiades (Hairy Head mansion), celestial palace (Guangming Palace).

Behaviour & Personality

In Journey to the West, he is a helpful and powerful deity who responds to Sun Wukong's plea. He is dutiful and uses his natural rooster form to counter the scorpion demoness. In Fengshen Yanyi, he is a devoted disciple of Tongtian Jiaozhu, but his personality is not deeply explored.

Domains & Habitats

He resides in the Guangming Palace (Palace of Light) in Heaven. As a star deity, he is associated with the Pleiades mansion and the sun's aspect.

Version Differences

In Fengshen Yanyi, he is a former giant rooster named Huang Cang who became immortal and was deified after death. In Journey to the West, he is a star god from birth, son of the bodhisattva Pilanpo, with no mention of a mortal past. The two versions are not harmonized in traditional sources.

Powers & Abilities

Rooster Transformation

Source Version Journey to the West

Description

Maori Xingguan can transform into a giant rooster, which is his natural form. This form is particularly effective against scorpions and centipedes, as roosters are natural predators of these creatures.

Limitations

The transformation is primarily useful against insect/arthropod demons; it may not be effective against other types of foes.

Contradictions

None noted.

Immortality and Cultivation

Source Version Fengshen Yanyi

Description

In Fengshen Yanyi, after thousands of years of cultivation as a rooster, he achieved immortality and became a disciple of Tongtian Jiaozhu.

Limitations

Despite his cultivation, he was killed in battle, showing that his immortality was not absolute.

Contradictions

The Fengshen Yanyi origin is not referenced in Journey to the West.

Divine Star Authority

Source Version Both

Description

As a star official, he has authority over his celestial mansion and can be called upon by other deities.

Limitations

Not elaborated in sources.

Stories & Myths

Deification in Fengshen Yanyi

Source Fengshen YanyiCulture ChinesePeriod Ming dynasty (16th century)

Summary

Huang Cang, the first giant rooster, cultivated for thousands of years and became immortal. He became a disciple of Tongtian Jiaozhu and his favorite. During the Battle of the Ten Thousand Immortals, he was killed by the Chan sect. After his death, Jiang Ziya deified him as the Sun Rooster of Hairy Head, one of the twenty-eight stars.

Source Journey to the WestCulture ChinesePeriod Ming dynasty (16th century)

Summary
