Kothar-wa-Khasis.

Who It Is

Kothar-wa-Khasis.

Kothar-wa-Khasis. Craftsmanship, metallurgy, architecture, weaponry, magical implements, and divine construction. He is also associated with incantations and spells, as his name suggests 'wise' in the sense of secret knowledge. He is the patron of builders, smiths, and artisans.

Form

No direct physical description survives in Ugaritic texts. He is a deity, so he is likely anthropomorphic, but no iconography has been securely identified. In later Greek sources (Philo of Byblos), Chusor is described as a mortal inventor, but this is a euhemeristic reinterpretation. Modern artists often depict him as a burly, hammer-wielding smith-god, but this is extrapolation from his role, not evidence.

Powers

Divine Craftsmanship Kothar-wa-Khasis can create any object, from weapons to palaces, with supernatural skill. He forges the two clubs for Baal, named Yagrush (Chaser) and Ayyamur (Driver), which are instrumental in defeating Yam. He also builds Baal's palace, a grand cedar-and-silver structure. Ugaritic Baal Cycle (KTU 1.4) His creations are not inherently magical; they are just exceptionally well-made. The clubs' power comes from their divine craftsmanship, not from enchantment. None noted.

Architecture and Palace Building He constructs the most magnificent buildings, including Baal's palace on Mount Zaphon. The palace is described as vast, with cedar, silver, and gold, and it is built in only a few days. Ugaritic Baal Cycle (KTU 1.4) He only builds for gods, not mortals, in the myths. He requires proper payment or invitation. In Philo of Byblos, Chusor is credited with building the first houses for mortals, which is a later euhemeristic twist.

Master of Incantations and Spells His name includes 'Khasis' (wise), which suggests he is a master of magical knowledge. He is invoked in rituals, and his expertise in crafting ritual objects (like the bow for Aqhat) implies he can imbue objects with efficacy. Ugaritic texts (Aqhat epic, ritual texts) His magic is not flashy; it is embedded in his craft. No direct spells are recorded, so his magical role is inferred.

Divine Counselor and Mediator He advises Baal and other gods on practical matters. He is not a council elder, but his opinion is respected. In the Baal Cycle, he even teases Baal about his fear of the house being built, showing his confidence. Ugaritic Baal Cycle His counsel is practical, not prophetic. None.

Divine craftsmanship and temple architecture Kothar-wa-Khasis is the archetypal divine architect-smith of the Ugaritic pantheon, the main deity of craftsmanship. He is portrayed as a divine architect responsible for the construction of temples for other gods. He builds Yam's palace at El's command, constructs Baal's palace on Mount Saphon, and is invoked in Egyptian incantations (Budapest Kothar Papyrus) to take part in the building of a shrine. His mastery extends to the ceremonial furnishing of palaces: the dais, throne with footstool, palanquin, table and bowl he fashions for Athirat are of a quality that visibly pleases her. Ugaritic (Baal Cycle KTU 1.1-1.4); Egyptian reception (Budapest Kothar Papyrus) He builds only what he is commissioned to build; his genius serves the wills of higher gods (El, then Baal) rather than autonomous ambition. None direct; his craftsman role is unanimously attested.

Divine weapon-smithing and the naming of weapons Kothar forges two divine clubs/maces for Baal's battle with Yam and charges them with power by *naming* them. The first is called Yagarrish ('may it drive'), the second Ayyamarri (from 'ay 'any' + mry 'expel', roughly 'anyone-it-may-expel'). Striking Yam with the first fails; striking him with the second defeats the sea-god. The act of naming a weapon is presented as a magical, world-altering act, marking the boundary between material craft and incantatory art. Ugaritic (Baal Cycle KTU 1.2) Baal must strike twice — the first, lesser weapon is insufficient against Yam; the decisive power resides in the correctly-named instrument. None; both weapon names and the two-strike outcome are consistently attested.

Metallurgy of gold and silver (Ugaritic) / iron-working (Phoenicianized) In the Bronze-Age Ugaritic texts Kothar works in gold and silver — the precious metals of his era — and is never credited with iron. The later, euhemerized Phoenician figure Chousor (per Philo of Byblos) is instead credited, alongside a nameless brother, with the *discovery of iron-working*, reflecting first-millennium technological reality. Together these attest a craftsman-god whose defining domain is metallurgical skill across two eras. Ugaritic (Baal Cycle) vs Phoenician (Philo of Byblos, Phoenician History) Iron is never assigned to the Ugaritic Kothar; gold/silver abilities are never assigned to the Greek-era Chousor. The skill sets are era-consistent and do not overlap. The iron-vs-gold/silver difference is a genuine dissonance between the Ugaritic and Phoenician frames, not a contradiction within either.

Magic, incantation, divination and prophecy Kothar is 'a craftsman and spell-caster'. He is associated with magic and called a diviner. In the Baal Cycle the closing hymn to Shapash either names him as her friend or, on a minority reading, recognizes him as an expert in magic. The later Chousor is credited by Philo with being the first to prepare magical formulas, incantations and prophecies, and he is present in Mochos' cosmogony (via Damascius) opening the world-egg — an act patterned on deeds commonly attributed to Egyptian Ptah. Ugaritic ritual/mythic texts; Phoenician cosmogony of Mochos (via Damascius, De principiis) His magic is deployed in support of other deities' projects; his power to 'expel' and 'drive out' Yam and sea-monsters in the Shapash hymn is a protective rather than generative act. The disputed translation of his role in the Shapash closing hymn (friend vs expert-in-magic) is an open scholarly question.

Music and song Kothar could 'variously play the roles of an architect, smith, musician or magician' and is classed among the Ugaritic music-and-singing gods (a category attested in his classification). His protégé/parallel figures include Kinnaru and Cinyras, culture-hero lyremakers of the Levant and Cyprus. Ugaritic classification as an artisan-musician; see also the Kinnaru/Cinyras/Kothar comparisons No Ugaritic myth dramatizes his musicianship as such; it is one attested professional valence rather than a performed narrative plot. None material.

Domain over the sea: defence against Yam and serpents; patronage of sailors Kothar's maritime dimension makes him appear as a protector of sailors and, with Shapshu, a figure tasked with expelling/driving out Yam and various sea-monsters (in the closing hymn of the Baal Cycle). In Horon and the Mare (KTU 1.100, 'Ridding the Land of Serpents') he is listed among the deities Shapash should summon against snake venom. His overseas dwellings (Memphis, Caphtor) and his forging of the subjugation-weapons connect him to the mastery of watery chaos. Ugaritic (Baal Cycle closing hymn; KTU 1.100); John Curtis Franklin's study of Kothar's maritime dimension His sea-domain is protective and collaborative (with Shapash), not a separate kingship over the sea, which belongs to Yam. The interpretation of the Shapash-hymn lines naming him is disputed (friend vs magic-expert), which affects how his sea-role is read.

Gift-crafting and diplomatic making Kothar fashions the gifts that win Athirat's support for Baal — the dais, throne, palanquin, table and bowl — an act of diplomatic craftsmanship whose quality literally buys divine alliance. His workshop is the engine-room of divine politics: weapons to win battles, gifts to win allies, palaces to cement kingship. Ugaritic (Baal Cycle KTU 1.3-1.4) His artifacts influence, but do not decide, the rivalries of the pantheon; El, Athirat and Baal take the actual political actions. None.

Behavior & Habitat

Kothar-wa-Khasis is depicted as a master craftsman, architect, and magician. He is practical, efficient, and somewhat irreverent toward the chief god El. In the Baal Cycle, he speaks with authority and even teases Baal. He is not a warrior, but his creations are essential to victory. He is also a mediator and a source of wise counsel, though his wisdom is practical rather than cosmic.

Accounts

At the opening of the cosmic conflict between the storm-god Baal and the sea-god Yam, Yahweh's candidate El summons Kothar-wa-Khasis to his dwelling at the 'confluence of the rivers and the two oceans'. The senior god orders the artisan to build a palace for Yam — a construction whose purpose is to legitimate Yam's kingship — and to do so quickly, lest Yam take hostile action. The passage is fragmentary but establishes Kothar as the divine architect whose buildings are signs of political legitimacy.

When Baal despairs against the overwhelming power of the sea and its fierce creatures, Kothar-wa-Khasis assures him he will be victorious and win 'a kingdom without end'. He fetches two divine clubs, gives each a magic name — Yagarrish, 'may-it-drive', and Ayyamarri, 'anyone-it-may-expel' — and strikes Yam the first two times himself. Baal then drags the sea-god out and finishes him; Ashtart proclaims Yam now their captive. The victory reverses El's earlier order making Baal Yam's captive and establishes Baal's claim to kingship.

Needing Athirat's support to persuade El to grant him a palace, Baal sends his messengers Gupan and Ugar to Kothar-wa-Khasis. The artisan immediately begins working at his bellows, fashioning a dais, a throne with footstool, a palanquin or couch, a table and a bowl or platter. When Athirat, doing her 'woman's work by the seashore', sees Baal and Anat approach she briefly fears they have come to kill her sons — but the quality of the gifts so pleases her that her anger subsides and she throws her support behind Baal. The gifts convert a potential enemy into an ally.

After El grants permission, Baal again enlists Kothar-wa-Khasis, inviting the artisan to a feast in evident friendship and respect. Baal outlines his request for a palace. Kothar proposes installing a window in it; Baal objects, perhaps fearing that a window would let Yam attack or kidnap his daughters Pidray and Tallay. Kothar, good-naturedly, predicts Baal will change his mind. Baal eventually reverses his decision and asks for the window. Kothar installs it, rejoicing, and Baal then sends his thunder and rain through the opening — likely reflecting a belief that Baal communicates with the world by sending atmospheric phenomena through breaks in the clouds.

Kothar-wa-Khasis visits the righteous king Danel ('El is judge') shortly after the king learns he will have a son, and presents a bow as a gift — the bow for the son Aqhat. The bow is so fine that Anat covets it and tries to persuade Aqhat to give it to her. He contemptuously tells her to ask the craftsman-god to make her one of her own. Anat's rage at this slight leads to Aqhat's death at the hands of her agent (the culture-hero is destroyed, his bow broken), an episode that drives the tragic remainder of the epic.

Kothar-wa-Khasis is listed among the deities invited to a reception organized by king Kirta to celebrate the arrival of his wife Huraya, alongside Baal, Yarikh, Resheph and Rahmay. The presence of the craftsman-god among the guests of a royal house underlines his standing at the high table of Ugaritic ritual-society.

In an incantation against snakebite, the god Horon (Ḥoranu), who settles the case of a mother whose children are endangered by serpents, gathers plants to defeat the snakes. Kothar-wa-Khasis is listed among the deities Shapash is to summon to deal with the serpent-venom threat, linking the craftsman-god to apotropaic protective magic.

In Philo's euhemerizing account, Chousor is a distant descendant of Hypsouranios, founder of Tyre. With a nameless brother he discovers iron-working. Acting alone he invents fishing implements (hook, bait, line, raft), which leads Philo to equate his cult-name with that of Zeus Meilichius (perhaps because both are benevolent givers of wealth). He is also the first to prepare magical formulas, incantations and prophecies. His nameless brothers are credited with inventing brickwork, echoing Kothar's architectural role. In Mochos' cosmogony, Chousor opens the world-egg — an act scholars pattern on the deeds of the Egyptian Ptah.

Content Notes

Kothar-wa-Khasis forges and names the two divine clubs with which Baal defeats and scatters the sea-god Yam, and in the closing hymn he is tasked — with Shapash — to 'expel and drive out' Yam and various sea-monsters. This is mythic cosmic-violence: a creator-craftsman arming a storm-god against a fellow deity and chthonic monsters. It is not historical slaughter but a theomachy register that some later readers mapped onto biblical 'chaoskampf' narratives.

Mark S. Smith and Aaron Tugendhaft caution against reading Yam as 'chaos' and Kothar's work as cosmogonic violence; Yam is a legitimate contender within an established hierarchy, not a force of disorder.

Baal Cycle, KTU 1.2, KTU 1.6 VI

Kothar's craft is the material engine of divine conflict: his weapons defeat Yam, his palace symbolizes Baal's kingship, and his gift-bow (in the Aqhat epic) becomes the object of Anat's envy that precipitates the hero Aqhat's murder. His benign, good-natured artisan role is entangled, through the uses others make of his work, in lethal outcomes — a 'neutral toolmaker' whose artifacts enable violence.

In the Aqhat epic, the tragedy is triggered by Aqhat's contemptuous refusal of Anat; scholars read Kothar as the unconscious source of the fatal artifact rather than as an agent of the killing.

Baal Cycle; Epic of Aqhat

In Philo of Byblos' Phoenician History, the god Kothar is reduced to a mortal craftsman-king, Chousor, 'discovering' iron-working, fishing, and magic — an ancient rationalizing act that strips the divine dimension. This euhemeristic distortion, preserved by a Christian apologist (Eusebius) to discredit paganism, is a documented case of source-controlled flattening of a deity, comparable to how other Ugaritic gods were reduced to 'deified men'.

Modern scholars (Eissfeldt, Baumgarten) accept that genuine second-millennium Phoenician material underlies the euhemerized text even wherever Philo's rationalizing frame is tendentious.

Philo of Byblos / Sanchuniathon, via Eusebius

Kothar's dwelling is located at Memphis (ḥqkpt, 'house of the ka of Ptah') and Kaphtor/Crete — the hubs of Late Bronze Age metal and craft trade. Reading his homes as reflecting the *routes through which crafts and resources traveled* links the deity to the imperial-commercial economy of Bronze Age Egypt and the Aegean in which Ugarit itself was a node. This is not a dark act by Kothar, but it situates his mythology within extractive trade networks and cultural appropriation of foreign craft expertise.

The Memphis/Caphtor placement is interpreted as marking Kothar as a foreigner among the gods, reflecting real Mediterranean trade routes rather than any specific historical event.

Ugaritic texts; John Curtis Franklin's maritime-trade analysis

Kothar has been appropriated in later comparative and speculative writing as an 'eastern Hephaestus' or a 'pre-Yahwist creator-god', and the hypothesis that his name underlies alleged biblical references (the Hebrew Bible passages Ezekiel 3:32, Proverbs 31:19, Judges 3:8.10) has been repeatedly proposed and rejected. Some Quran-vs-Kawthar and Kothar-Khidr links are modern scholarly or devotional speculation that should not be treated as attested fact.

Dennis Pardee rules out the biblical emendations; the Ezekiel reference is to Danel, not Kothar; the Al-Kawthar and Khidr connections (the latter in the main article) are flagged there as proposed, not proven.

Scholarly Debates
One god or two? (Kothar and Khasis)
kṯr w ḫss represents two closely-related gods, Kothar and Khasis, combined in the binomial. ⇌ It is one god with a doubled 'skilled and wise' epithet-name (hendiadys).
Evidence for A: The binomial formula parallels other Ugaritic double-deity names; early scholars split them.
Evidence for B: Khasis never occurs alone; the normal reading is a single divine craftsman; later forms (Chousor) are singular.
Is Ilish an alternate name, an attendant, or a distinct god?
Ilish is an alternate name or attendant of Kothar-wa-Khasis (Jean Nougayrol). ⇌ Ilish is a separate carpenter-deity or divine herald, independent though similar.
Evidence for A: Both words are written with ngr in Ugaritic.
Evidence for B: Distinct roles (herald vs craftsman); Ilish has his own carpenter-wives; modern reevaluation.
Is the Egyptian Keserty actually Kothar?
Keserty is an alternate Egyptian spelling/reading of Kothar's name (László Kákosy). ⇌ Keserty is a separate Egyptian deity whose iconography (gazelle-head crown) resembles Resheph.
Evidence for A: Name similarity; the Cairo stele names Keserty explicitly, allowing separation from Resheph.
Evidence for B: Iconography resembles Resheph, not any distinct craftsman type; the onomastic link is not airtight.
The Kothar-Ptah equation
Kothar is analogous to, even identified with, Ptah (William F. Albright). ⇌ The Memphis dwelling is a marker of Kothar's foreign craftsman identity, not evidence of identity with Ptah.
Evidence for A: Both are divine craftsmen; Kothar dwells in Ptah's city; Chousor's world-egg act mirrors Ptah.
Evidence for B: No cultic identification is documented; the dwellings are explicitly 'foreign'/'overseas'.
Kothar's role in the closing Shapash hymn: friend vs magic-expert
The hymn makes Kothar a friend/acquaintance of Shapash. ⇌ It recognizes him as an expert in magic.
Evidence for A: Majority translation of the relevant terms as friendship.
Evidence for B: Minority reading as a magical-epithet title.
What We Don't Know

Primary sources were drawn via the Wikipedia Direct API (Firecrawl billing exhausted), which provides English Wikipedia articles as secondary discovery leads; I did not access the Ugaritic cuneiform texts, Egyptian papyri, or Philo/Eusebius directly in the original languages, so all translations are mediated by the English encyclopedia. No verified modern film/game/anime appearance of Kothar-wa-Khasis could be confirmed, and I explicitly refrained from fabricating one. The interpretation of several lacunose passages (Yam's palace purpose, the window's motivation, Kothar's closing-hymn role) is reconstructed by scholars and flagged as such. The Keserty-vs-Kothar identity, the Ilish conflation, and the one-god-vs-two-gods question all remain genuinely open and are recorded as contradictions rather than resolved. A Ugaritic-language or Syriac/Hebrew primary-source review, plus the actual Budapest Kothar Papyrus text and the Cairo Keserty stele, would strengthen verification. Research cutoff: 2026-08-01.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Kothar-wa-Khasis as the divine version of a master engineer and R&D department combined. If the gods needed a weapon, a palace, or a magical artifact, they called him. He was the original 'maker' — the one who turned divine ideas into reality.
