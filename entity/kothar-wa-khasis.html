Who It Is

Before there was a storm-god's throne, there was the smith who built it. Kothar-wa-Khasis — 'skilled and wise' — is the hidden architect of the Ugaritic cosmos, the divine artisan who raised temples for the gods, forged the named weapons that sank the sea-god Yam, and so pleased the queen of heaven with a set of palace furnishings that he single-handedly turned the politics of the pantheon. He is the least self-aggrandizing of deities: a good-natured master of fire and naming who serves any god who commissions him, yet whose craft underwrites every throne in the Late Bronze Age sky. When the Greeks later met his Phoenician descendant Chousor, they remembered him not as a god but as the mortal king who invented iron and magic — the faint, flattened echo of a craftsman-god whose real workshops stood at Memphis and beyond the sea.

Kothar-wa-Khasis is depicted as good-natured, loyal, and oddly subordinate yet indispensable within the divine hierarchy. He serves any deity who commissions him — El, Yam, and finally Baal — without asserting kingship of his own; his power is exercised *for* others. He is cheerful and patient: in the palace scene he proposes a window, is rebuffed by Baal, and responds with what scholars read as a genial, good-humored claim that Baal will change his mind — which he does. Powers
Divine craftsmanship and temple architecture

Kothar-wa-Khasis is the archetypal divine architect-smith of the Ugaritic pantheon, the main deity of craftsmanship. He is portrayed as a divine architect responsible for the construction of temples for other gods. He builds Yam's palace at El's command, constructs Baal's palace on Mount Saphon, and is invoked in Egyptian incantations (Budapest Kothar Papyrus) to take part in the building of a shrine. His mastery extends to the ceremonial furnishing of palaces: the dais, throne with footstool, palanquin, table and bowl he fashions for Athirat are of a quality that visibly pleases her.

Limit: He builds only what he is commissioned to build; his genius serves the wills of higher gods (El, then Baal) rather than autonomous ambition.

Divine weapon-smithing and the naming of weapons

Kothar forges two divine clubs/maces for Baal's battle with Yam and charges them with power by *naming* them. The first is called Yagarrish ('may it drive'), the second Ayyamarri (from 'ay 'any' + mry 'expel', roughly 'anyone-it-may-expel'). Striking Yam with the first fails; striking him with the second defeats the sea-god. The act of naming a weapon is presented as a magical, world-altering act, marking the boundary between material craft and incantatory art.

Limit: Baal must strike twice — the first, lesser weapon is insufficient against Yam; the decisive power resides in the correctly-named instrument.

Metallurgy of gold and silver (Ugaritic) / iron-working (Phoenicianized)

In the Bronze-Age Ugaritic texts Kothar works in gold and silver — the precious metals of his era — and is never credited with iron. The later, euhemerized Phoenician figure Chousor (per Philo of Byblos) is instead credited, alongside a nameless brother, with the *discovery of iron-working*, reflecting first-millennium technological reality. Together these attest a craftsman-god whose defining domain is metallurgical skill across two eras.

Limit: Iron is never assigned to the Ugaritic Kothar; gold/silver abilities are never assigned to the Greek-era Chousor. The skill sets are era-consistent and do not overlap.

Magic, incantation, divination and prophecy

Kothar is 'a craftsman and spell-caster'. He is associated with magic and called a diviner. In the Baal Cycle the closing hymn to Shapash either names him as her friend or, on a minority reading, recognizes him as an expert in magic. The later Chousor is credited by Philo with being the first to prepare magical formulas, incantations and prophecies, and he is present in Mochos' cosmogony (via Damascius) opening the world-egg — an act patterned on deeds commonly attributed to Egyptian Ptah.

Limit: His magic is deployed in support of other deities' projects; his power to 'expel' and 'drive out' Yam and sea-monsters in the Shapash hymn is a protective rather than generative act.

Music and song

Kothar could 'variously play the roles of an architect, smith, musician or magician' and is classed among the Ugaritic music-and-singing gods (a category attested in his classification). His protégé/parallel figures include Kinnaru and Cinyras, culture-hero lyremakers of the Levant and Cyprus.

Limit: No Ugaritic myth dramatizes his musicianship as such; it is one attested professional valence rather than a performed narrative plot.

Domain over the sea: defence against Yam and serpents; patronage of sailors

Kothar's maritime dimension makes him appear as a protector of sailors and, with Shapshu, a figure tasked with expelling/driving out Yam and various sea-monsters (in the closing hymn of the Baal Cycle). In Horon and the Mare (KTU 1.100, 'Ridding the Land of Serpents') he is listed among the deities Shapash should summon against snake venom. His overseas dwellings (Memphis, Caphtor) and his forging of the subjugation-weapons connect him to the mastery of watery chaos.

Limit: His sea-domain is protective and collaborative (with Shapash), not a separate kingship over the sea, which belongs to Yam.

Gift-crafting and diplomatic making

Kothar fashions the gifts that win Athirat's support for Baal — the dais, throne, palanquin, table and bowl — an act of diplomatic craftsmanship whose quality literally buys divine alliance. His workshop is the engine-room of divine politics: weapons to win battles, gifts to win allies, palaces to cement kingship.

Limit: His artifacts influence, but do not decide, the rivalries of the pantheon; El, Athirat and Baal take the actual political actions.

Accounts I
The building of Yam's palaceBaal Cycle, KTU 1.1

c. 1300-1100 BCE

At the opening of the cosmic conflict between the storm-god Baal and the sea-god Yam, Yahweh's candidate El summons Kothar-wa-Khasis to his dwelling at the 'confluence of the rivers and the two oceans'. The senior god orders the artisan to build a palace for Yam — a construction whose purpose is to legitimate Yam's kingship — and to do so quickly, lest Yam take hostile action. The passage is fragmentary but establishes Kothar as the divine architect whose buildings are signs of political legitimacy. Yam's palace is commissioned; the stage is set for Yam's claim to kingship and the coming battle with Baal.

The forging of the two weapons and Baal's victory over YamBaal Cycle, KTU 1.2

c. 1300-1100 BCE

When Baal despairs against the overwhelming power of the sea and its fierce creatures, Kothar-wa-Khasis assures him he will be victorious and win 'a kingdom without end'. He fetches two divine clubs, gives each a magic name — Yagarrish, 'may-it-drive', and Ayyamarri, 'anyone-it-may-expel' — and strikes Yam the first two times himself. Baal then drags the sea-god out and finishes him; Ashtart proclaims Yam now their captive. The victory reverses El's earlier order making Baal Yam's captive and establishes Baal's claim to kingship. Yam is defeated, Baal accrues kingship, and Kothar's named weapons prove the decisive instrument — establishing the artisan as the power behind the storm-god's triumph.

The gifts for AthiratBaal Cycle, KTU 1.3-1.4

c. 1300-1100 BCE

Needing Athirat's support to persuade El to grant him a palace, Baal sends his messengers Gupan and Ugar to Kothar-wa-Khasis. The artisan immediately begins working at his bellows, fashioning a dais, a throne with footstool, a palanquin or couch, a table and a bowl or platter. When Athirat, doing her 'woman's work by the seashore', sees Baal and Anat approach she briefly fears they have come to kill her sons — but the quality of the gifts so pleases her that her anger subsides and she throws her support behind Baal. The gifts convert a potential enemy into an ally. Athirat backs Baal's bid; El reluctantly grants the palace; Kothar's craft proves the hinge of divine diplomacy.

The palace of Baal and the windowBaal Cycle, KTU 1.4

c. 1300-1100 BCE

After El grants permission, Baal again enlists Kothar-wa-Khasis, inviting the artisan to a feast in evident friendship and respect. Baal outlines his request for a palace. Kothar proposes installing a window in it; Baal objects, perhaps fearing that a window would let Yam attack or kidnap his daughters Pidray and Tallay. Kothar, good-naturedly, predicts Baal will change his mind. Baal eventually reverses his decision and asks for the window. Kothar installs it, rejoicing, and Baal then sends his thunder and rain through the opening — likely reflecting a belief that Baal communicates with the world by sending atmospheric phenomena through breaks in the clouds. Baal's palace is built, symbolizing his cosmic kingship; the window becomes the conduit for Baal's storm-phenomena, and Kothar's judgment is vindicated.

Accounts II
The bow of AqhatEpic of Aqhat / Tale of Aqhat, KTU 1.17-1.19

c. 14th century BCE

Kothar-wa-Khasis visits the righteous king Danel ('El is judge') shortly after the king learns he will have a son, and presents a bow as a gift — the bow for the son Aqhat. The bow is so fine that Anat covets it and tries to persuade Aqhat to give it to her. He contemptuously tells her to ask the craftsman-god to make her one of her own. Anat's rage at this slight leads to Aqhat's death at the hands of her agent (the culture-hero is destroyed, his bow broken), an episode that drives the tragic remainder of the epic. The gift-bow precipitates the hero's murder — Kothar's craft, meant to bless, becomes the indirect cause of Aqhat's destruction when it arouses divine envy.

The feast of KirtaEpic of Kirta, KTU 1.14-1.16

c. 14th century BCE

Kothar-wa-Khasis is listed among the deities invited to a reception organized by king Kirta to celebrate the arrival of his wife Huraya, alongside Baal, Yarikh, Resheph and Rahmay. The presence of the craftsman-god among the guests of a royal house underlines his standing at the high table of Ugaritic ritual-society. Confirms Kothar's place among the principal deities in the Ugaritic banquet liturgy.

The summoning against serpent venomHoron and the Mare / 'Ridding the Land of Serpents', KTU 1.100

c. 13th century BCE

In an incantation against snakebite, the god Horon (Ḥoranu), who settles the case of a mother whose children are endangered by serpents, gathers plants to defeat the snakes. Kothar-wa-Khasis is listed among the deities Shapash is to summon to deal with the serpent-venom threat, linking the craftsman-god to apotropaic protective magic. Places Kothar within the magical/incantatory stream of Ugaritic religion, alongside the sun-goddess as a healer-protector.

Chousor in the Phoenician History (euhemerized)Philo of Byblos, Phoenician History (via Eusebius); cosmogony of Mochos (via Damascius)

Greek compilation c. 1st-2nd century CE, drawing on purported ancient Phoenician tradition

In Philo's euhemerizing account, Chousor is a distant descendant of Hypsouranios, founder of Tyre. With a nameless brother he discovers iron-working. Acting alone he invents fishing implements (hook, bait, line, raft), which leads Philo to equate his cult-name with that of Zeus Meilichius (perhaps because both are benevolent givers of wealth). He is also the first to prepare magical formulas, incantations and prophecies. His nameless brothers are credited with inventing brickwork, echoing Kothar's architectural role. In Mochos' cosmogony, Chousor opens the world-egg — an act scholars pattern on the deeds of the Egyptian Ptah. The euhemerized Chousor demonstrates how an Ugaritic creator-craftsman survived, transformed, into Greek-era Phoenician culture-saga as a founding mortal-technologist.

Egyptian reception: the invoker of temple-building and KesertyBudapest Kothar Papyrus; a New-Kingdom stele in the Cairo Museum (the engraver Woše-seti praying to Keserty)

c. New Kingdom (no older than Eighteenth Dynasty)

In Egypt, Kothar-wa-Khasis was associated with the construction of temples, attested by the Budapest Kothar Papyrus, an incantation invoking him to take part in building a shrine. A possible later magical papyrus may also reference him. The god Keserty, known from a stele in which the 'engraver, Woše-seti' prays to a seated god, is argued by some (e.g. László Kákosy) to be an alternate Egyptian spelling of Kothar's name, though his iconography (gazelle-head crown) resembles Resheph — an identity that was only separable from Resheph because Keserty's name is spelled out. Establishes that the West Semitic craftsman-god was known, if not popular, in ancient Egypt, syncretized with the craftsman milieu of Memphis and Ptah.

The internet sometimes assumes 'Kothar-wa-Khasis' means he is two separate and independent gods named Kothar and Khasis. — The binomial is a hendiadys meaning 'skilled and wise' / 'wise craftsman'. Some scholars have argued it represents two gods combined, but the dominant reading is a doubled single name (parallel to Nikkal-wa-Ib, Shahar-and-Shalim patterns). The second element Khasis never occurs alone.

Kothar was an 'iron god' / the god of blacksmithing in iron. — The Ugaritic Kothar (Bronze Age) works only in gold and silver and is never credited with iron. Iron-working is assigned only to his later euhemerized Phoenician form Chousor (in Philo), reflecting a first-millennium world. Conflating the two erases an era-significant distinction.

Kothar is identical with the Egyptian Ptah. — Kothar dwells in Ptah's city (Memphis) and shares Ptah's craftsman role, and Albright proposed an equation, but no documented cultic identification exists. The Memphis placement is read as symbolic of Kothar's 'foreign craftsman' identity, and the Kothar-Ptah equation is an assumption, not a fact.

The Hebrew Bible contains passages emended to include Kothar's name (Ezekiel, Proverbs, Judges), proving he is a biblical deity. — Dennis Pardee judges these emendation proposals implausible — the conventional readings are satisfactory. The suggested 'Kothar' in Judges' Cushan-rishathaim is unlikely. Kothar is an Ugaritic/Phoenician deity, not a covert biblical one.

Kothar-wa-Khasis appears in modern games/films/anime. — No verified standalone appearance could be confirmed. Some sources may use 'Kothar' as a name for unrelated characters (homonyms), but I could not verify any direct adaptation of the deity. Asserting one would be fabrication.
