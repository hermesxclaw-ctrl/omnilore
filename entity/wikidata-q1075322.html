The Approach

In the misty annals of Irish myth, a king loses his arm in battle and with it his throne—only to reclaim both through magic, silver, and the skill of divine healers. Nuada Airgetlám, the silver-handed sovereign of the Tuatha Dé Danann, embodies the ancient Celtic ideal of kingship: physical perfection, martial prowess, and the wisdom to step aside for a greater leader.

Content advisory. Contains descriptions of battle violence, dismemberment, and beheading.

Etymology

The name Nuada/Nuadu is thought to derive from a Celtic stem *noudont- or *noudent-, possibly connected to a Germanic root meaning 'acquire, have the use of' or 'catch, entrap (as a hunter)'. J.R.R. Tolkien linked this to the 'magic hand of Nodens the Catcher'. Julius Pokorny proposed a Proto-Indo-European root *neu-d- meaning 'acquire, utilise, go fishing'. An alternative Proto-Celtic formation *nawito- ('need, compel') is considered difficult by linguist Matasovic.

Cultural Ownership

Irish mythology (Gaelic), specifically the mythological cycle of the Tuatha Dé Danann. Related to British and Gaulish deity Nodens and Welsh Nudd/Lludd.

Disputed Identifications

Nuada is sometimes identified with Nechtan and Elcmar, both described as husbands of Boann. The identification with Elcmar is debated; some scholars see them as separate figures.

Description

Appearance

Nuada is described as a king of the Tuatha Dé Danann, but no detailed physical description survives. He is noted for his beauty and physical perfection, which is why his loss of an arm is significant. After his injury, he receives a silver arm made by Dian Cécht and Creidhne, and later a flesh-and-blood arm by Miach.

Symbols & Iconography

Nuada's primary symbol is his silver hand/arm (Airgetlám). His sword is one of the Four Treasures of the Tuatha Dé Danann, symbolizing his kingship and martial power. In later tradition, he is associated with the plain of Maigh Nuad (Maynooth).

Behaviour & Personality

Nuada is portrayed as a noble and just king, willing to sacrifice his own position for the good of his people. He shows chivalry in allowing the Fir Bolg to inspect his forces before battle. He is also pragmatic, stepping down in favor of Lugh when he recognizes the youth's superior leadership.

Domains & Habitats

As king of the Tuatha Dé Danann, Nuada's domain is Ireland, particularly the region of Connacht after the Fir Bolg are given a quarter. His association with Maigh Nuad (Maynooth) in County Kildare suggests a territorial connection.

Version Differences

In some versions, Nuada loses his arm, in others his hand. The replacement is sometimes by Dian Cécht alone, sometimes with Creidhne, and later by Miach. The identification with Nechtan/Elcmar varies across sources.

Source Version Cath Maige Tuired

Nuada receives a functional silver arm from Dian Cécht and Creidhne, and later a flesh-and-blood arm from Miach, restoring his physical perfection.

Stories & Myths

The First Battle of Mag Tuired

Source Cath Maige Tuired (and related texts)Culture IrishPeriod Mythological, pre-Christian

Summary

Nuada leads the Tuatha Dé Danann to Ireland and demands half the island from the Fir Bolg. When rejected, war ensues. During the battle, Nuada loses his arm to Sreng. Despite this, the Tuatha Dé win, and Sreng is offered a quarter of Ireland (Connacht).

Battle violence and dismemberment

Source Cath Maige Tuired

Description

Nuada loses an arm in battle, and later is beheaded by Balor. The text describes these events without graphic detail but implies brutal combat.

Scholarly Context

Such violence is typical of heroic mythology, reflecting the harsh realities of Iron Age warfare.
