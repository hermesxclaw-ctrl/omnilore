Who It Is

In the dusty, silent halls of the Land of No Return, a queen sits on a lapis lazuli throne, her gaze as cold as the grave she rules. She is Ereshkigal, the Lady of the Great Place, the keeper of the dead, and the one deity even the gods of heaven fear to challenge. Her story is one of power, grief, and the unyielding laws of the underworld that no one, not even her own sister, can escape.

Ereshkigal is portrayed as a stern, unyielding, and terrifying ruler. She is the enforcer of the laws of the underworld, which are absolute. She is not inherently 'evil' but is a guardian of order in her realm. She is fiercely protective of her domain and her authority. The myth of Nergal shows her as capable of great anger, but also as lonely and desiring companionship. In the Descent of Inanna, she is shown as grieving and in pain, yet still adhering to the strict rules of her world, refusing to let her sister leave without a substitute.

Powers
Sovereignty over the Underworld

Ereshkigal has absolute authority over the Land of No Return. She determines the fate of all who enter, whether they are gods or mortals. She can imprison gods, as she does with Inanna, and can only release them under specific conditions. Her power is bound by the 'laws of the underworld,' which she enforces without exception.

Limit: She is bound by the rules of her own realm. She cannot unilaterally set a soul free without a substitute, as seen in the Descent of Inanna. The god Ea/Enki can circumvent her power through trickery, showing that her authority, while absolute within her domain, can be challenged by the wisdom of the gods.

Control over Demons and Disease

Ereshkigal can dispatch her demons, such as Namtar and the galla, to the mortal world to inflict disease, madness, and death. The galla are described as relentless, terrifying creatures who seize their victims. This power is a key threat in the Descent of Inanna.

Limit: The demons act as her agents but can be bargained with or appeased through ritual. The god Ea can also create countermeasures, as he does with Asushunamir in the Akkadian version.

The Water of Life

In the Akkadian Descent of Ištar, Ea creates the figure Asushunamir to trick Ereshkigal into swearing an oath by the gods, which forces her to grant the Water of Life that revives Ištar. This shows that she is the holder of a potent life-giving substance, a power usually associated with the gods of the upper world.

Limit: She is tricked into giving it up, suggesting it is not a power she wields freely.

Divine Authority and Judgment

As a queen, she holds the power of judgment. She presides over the fates of the dead and can pass sentences, as she does on Inanna. She is also a figure of dread, and her name can be used in curses and oaths to invoke divine punishment.

Limit: Her judgment is typically absolute, but she can be influenced by other gods, as in the Nergal myth where she is ultimately persuaded to share her power.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No detailed physical description survives in the texts. She is a queen, likely depicted in royal robes and crown in iconography, though a specific visual canon is not established. In the Descent of Inanna, she is described as being in a state of distress, 'naked' and 'bent' during the birth-pangs of her child, a rare moment of vulnerability. Symbols — Her primary symbols are the throne and the royal scepter, signifying her absolute rule. The lapis lazuli necklace in the Descent of Inanna may symbolize her power and the link between the heavens and the underworld. In some texts, the owl is associated with the underworld and may be her symbol, though this is not explicitly stated. The 'eye' and 'mouth' of the underworld are gateways she controls. Her messenger is the god Namt

Domains — Ereshkigal is the sole ruler of the underworld, known as Kur, Irkalla, or the Land of No Return. Her domain is the realm of the dead, a dark, dusty place where the dead exist as shadows. She holds the power of life and death over all who enter. Her realm is also the source of demons and diseases, which she can send forth to afflict the living, as seen in the Descent of Inanna when her demon Namtar is sent to collect a substitute.

Accounts I
The Descent of Inanna (Sumerian)Sumerian poem, Old Babylonian copies

c. 1800-1600 BCE

Inanna, queen of heaven, decides to descend to the underworld to attend the funeral of Gugalanna, the Bull of Heaven, and possibly to challenge her sister Ereshkigal's power. She dresses in her finest and takes seven divine powers (me). At each of the seven gates, she is stripped of one article of clothing or ornament, and she is forced to bow before Ereshkigal. Ereshkigal, who is in the throes of childbirth, is enraged by Inanna's arrogance and fixes the 'Eye of Death' upon her. Inanna is killed and her corpse is hung on a hook. After three days, Inanna's vizier Ninshubur, following her instructions, appeals to the gods Enlil, Nanna, and Enki for help. Enki alone acts, creating two genderless figures (the kurgarra and the galatura) who are sent to the underworld with the food and water of life. They find Ereshkigal in pain, and their empathetic moaning gains her favor. She grants them Inanna's corpse, which they revive. However, the galla demons demand a substitute. Accounts II
Nergal and EreshkigalAkkadian myth, Amarna and Sultantepe versions

c. 14th-7th century BCE

At a banquet of the gods, Nergal, a warrior god, refuses to stand when Ereshkigal's messenger, Namtar, enters to pay respects on her behalf. Ereshkigal is insulted and demands that Nergal be sent to the underworld to apologize. In the Amarna version, Nergal is warned by Ea not to sit, eat, drink, or have sex in the underworld. He does all but the last, but Ereshkigal is so taken with him that she offers him the throne. He refuses and leaves, but she sends Namtar to demand his return. In the Sultantepe version, Nergal is tricked into staying, and the story ends with him becoming her husband and co-ruler. The myth explains the presence of a male consort for the queen of the underworld and the integration of Nergal (a god of war, plague, and the sun's destructive heat) into the underworld pantheon. Nergal becomes Ereshkigal's consort, and the two share rule over the underworld. This myth solidifies the underworld's role as a place of both death and potential regeneration, as Nergal's warrior aspect brings a new dimension to the realm.

Gilgamesh, Enkidu, and the Nether World (Sumerian)Sumerian poem

Old Babylonian period

In this poem, Enkidu descends to the underworld to retrieve a drum (or pukku and mikku) that Gilgamesh has dropped. He is warned not to behave like a living person (no clean clothes, no oil, no weapons, no crying out). He fails to heed the warnings and is trapped. Gilgamesh then appeals to Enlil, Sin, and Ea for help. Only Ea helps, and he creates a being (the sun god Shamash in some versions) to bring Enkidu's spirit back. The poem includes a long section where Gilgamesh questions the spirit of Enkidu about the fate of the dead. Ereshkigal is not a direct participant but is the ruler of the realm, and the poem emphasizes her power over the dead, including the spirits of heroes. Reinforces the idea that the underworld is a place of no return for the living, and that even heroes are subject to Ereshkigal's rule. Provides a detailed, melancholy vision of the afterlife.
