Ereshkigal, the queen of the dead, who sits in the darkness and the dust, whose judgment no living soul can escape.

Who They Are

Ereshkigal is the queen of the underworld (Irkalla), the realm of the dead. She is the older sister of Inanna (Ishtar). Unlike the Greek Hades, Ereshkigal does not rule from a throne of judgment but from a dark, dusty palace where the dead eat clay and drink muddy water. Her name means 'Lady of the Great Below.' In the Descent of Inanna, Ereshkigal strips her sister of all power and hangs her corpse on a hook — only through Enki's intervention is Inanna resurrected. In the myth of Nergal and Ereshkigal, Nergal visits the underworld, offends her, but later returns and marries her, becoming co-ruler. Ereshkigal is depicted as a terrifying, naked figure with claws and snakes for hair, though sometimes shown as a regal figure wearing a horned crown. She represents the inescapability of death — once a soul enters her realm, it cannot return. Her decrees are absolute, binding even the other gods. Yet she is also tragic: a goddess forced to dwell in darkness while her sister enjoys the light.

Attested in Descent of Inanna / Nergal and EreshkigalEra Sumerian to Neo-Babylonian (c. 3000-500 BCE)

Form

No detailed physical description survives in the texts. She is a queen, likely depicted in royal robes and crown in iconography, though a specific visual canon is not established. In the Descent of Inanna, she is described as being in a state of distress, 'naked' and 'bent' during the birth-pangs of her child, a rare moment of vulnerability. In the Nergal myth, she is a powerful, intimidating queen on her throne. The iconography of a naked goddess on seals is sometimes identified as her, but this is disputed.

Powers

Sovereignty over the Underworld Ereshkigal has absolute authority over the Land of No Return. She determines the fate of all who enter, whether they are gods or mortals. She can imprison gods, as she does with Inanna, and can only release them under specific conditions. Her power is bound by the 'laws of the underworld,' which she enforces without exception. All versions She is bound by the rules of her own realm. She cannot unilaterally set a soul free without a substitute, as seen in the Descent of Inanna. The god Ea/Enki can circumvent her power through trickery, showing that her authority, while absolute within her domain, can be challenged by the wisdom of the gods. The extent of her power over other gods is debated. In some texts, she is subordinate to the chief god Anu or Enlil, but in practice, she rules her domain independently.

Control over Demons and Disease Ereshkigal can dispatch her demons, such as Namtar and the galla, to the mortal world to inflict disease, madness, and death. The galla are described as relentless, terrifying creatures who seize their victims. This power is a key threat in the Descent of Inanna. Sumerian Descent of Inanna The demons act as her agents but can be bargained with or appeased through ritual. The god Ea can also create countermeasures, as he does with Asushunamir in the Akkadian version. The origin of demons is complex; some are her children or servants, while others are independent entities that she commands.

The Water of Life In the Akkadian Descent of Ištar, Ea creates the figure Asushunamir to trick Ereshkigal into swearing an oath by the gods, which forces her to grant the Water of Life that revives Ištar. This shows that she is the holder of a potent life-giving substance, a power usually associated with the gods of the upper world. Akkadian Descent of Ištar She is tricked into giving it up, suggesting it is not a power she wields freely. This power seems at odds with her role as a goddess of death, but it fits the theme of cyclical renewal that is central to the Inanna/Ištar myths.

Divine Authority and Judgment As a queen, she holds the power of judgment. She presides over the fates of the dead and can pass sentences, as she does on Inanna. She is also a figure of dread, and her name can be used in curses and oaths to invoke divine punishment. All versions Her judgment is typically absolute, but she can be influenced by other gods, as in the Nergal myth where she is ultimately persuaded to share her power. None.

Behavior & Habitat

Ereshkigal is portrayed as a stern, unyielding, and terrifying ruler. She is the enforcer of the laws of the underworld, which are absolute. She is not inherently 'evil' but is a guardian of order in her realm. She is fiercely protective of her domain and her authority. The myth of Nergal shows her as capable of great anger, but also as lonely and desiring companionship. In the Descent of Inanna, she is shown as grieving and in pain, yet still adhering to the strict rules of her world, refusing to let her sister leave without a substitute.

Accounts

Inanna, queen of heaven, decides to descend to the underworld to attend the funeral of Gugalanna, the Bull of Heaven, and possibly to challenge her sister Ereshkigal's power. She dresses in her finest and takes seven divine powers (me). At each of the seven gates, she is stripped of one article of clothing or ornament, and she is forced to bow before Ereshkigal. Ereshkigal, who is in the throes of childbirth, is enraged by Inanna's arrogance and fixes the 'Eye of Death' upon her. Inanna is killed and her corpse is hung on a hook. After three days, Inanna's vizier Ninshubur, following her instructions, appeals to the gods Enlil, Nanna, and Enki for help. Enki alone acts, creating two genderless figures (the kurgarra and the galatura) who are sent to the underworld with the food and water of life. They find Ereshkigal in pain, and their empathetic moaning gains her favor. She grants them Inanna's corpse, which they revive. However, the galla demons demand a substitute. Inanna, accompanied by demons, returns to the upper world, where she finds her husband Dumuzid not mourning her. She fixes the 'Eye of Death' upon him, and he is taken to the underworld. The story ends with a cycle of mourning and return, as Dumuzid's sister Geshtinanna agrees to take his place for half the year.

At a banquet of the gods, Nergal, a warrior god, refuses to stand when Ereshkigal's messenger, Namtar, enters to pay respects on her behalf. Ereshkigal is insulted and demands that Nergal be sent to the underworld to apologize. In the Amarna version, Nergal is warned by Ea not to sit, eat, drink, or have sex in the underworld. He does all but the last, but Ereshkigal is so taken with him that she offers him the throne. He refuses and leaves, but she sends Namtar to demand his return. In the Sultantepe version, Nergal is tricked into staying, and the story ends with him becoming her husband and co-ruler. The myth explains the presence of a male consort for the queen of the underworld and the integration of Nergal (a god of war, plague, and the sun's destructive heat) into the underworld pantheon.

In this poem, Enkidu descends to the underworld to retrieve a drum (or pukku and mikku) that Gilgamesh has dropped. He is warned not to behave like a living person (no clean clothes, no oil, no weapons, no crying out). He fails to heed the warnings and is trapped. Gilgamesh then appeals to Enlil, Sin, and Ea for help. Only Ea helps, and he creates a being (the sun god Shamash in some versions) to bring Enkidu's spirit back. The poem includes a long section where Gilgamesh questions the spirit of Enkidu about the fate of the dead. Ereshkigal is not a direct participant but is the ruler of the realm, and the poem emphasizes her power over the dead, including the spirits of heroes.

Scholarly Debates
Her role as a mother
In the Descent of Inanna, she is depicted as being in the throes of childbirth, suggesting she is a mother. ⇌ No children are explicitly named for her in most texts, and her role as a mother is not central to her mythos.
Her relationship with Nergal
Nergal is her consort and co-ruler of the underworld. ⇌ In some texts, Nergal is simply a resident of the underworld, and Ereshkigal's rule is absolute.
The nature of the underworld
The underworld is a place of dust and darkness, where the dead exist as shadows. ⇌ Some texts hint at a more complex afterlife, with the possibility of rewards or punishments.

What We Don't Know

Further research is needed to clarify the iconography of Ereshkigal, as the identification of specific figures on cylinder seals is uncertain. The identity of her child (if any) remains unknown. The full extent of her cult and the rituals associated with her worship, beyond funerary contexts, is not fully understood. The relationship between Ereshkigal and Allatu requires more detailed study.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Ereshkigal as the ultimate gatekeeper of a maximum-security prison. She is not evil, but she is absolutely bound by the rules of her domain. If you enter, you follow her laws, and there is no appeal. She is the final authority on death, and her power is absolute. Her sister Inanna, the queen of heaven, is like a celebrity trying to use her fame to get into the VIP section, only to find that her status means nothing in the underworld. Ereshkigal is the one who says, 'No, you are not special here. You are subject to my rules.'
