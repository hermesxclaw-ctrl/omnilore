Who It Is

Goblins are D&D's quintessential low-level humanoid enemy — the first monster most adventuring parties ever fight, and the creature that teaches new players the basics of combat. Standing around 3 to 4 feet tall, with flat faces, broad noses, pointed ears, wide mouths full of sharp teeth, and skin ranging from yellow-green to dull orange, goblins are individually weak and cowardly. Their strength lies entirely in numbers and ambush tactics. A goblin alone will flee from a house cat; a goblin war band of fifty, striking from darkness with shortbows and rusty blades, can bring down knights.

Goblin society is brutal and hierarchical, governed by whoever is strongest and meanest at any given moment. Leadership changes frequently through assassination or coups, and goblin 'chieftains' rarely last more than a few months before being overthrown. Their lairs are squalid, reeking warrens strewn with bones, refuse, and the spoils of raids — goblins are compulsive hoarders of shiny objects, and their treasure piles tend toward worthless junk (broken mirrors, bent spoons, pretty rocks) interspersed with the occasional genuine treasure they don't understand the value of.

Goblins are the bottom rung of the goblinoid hierarchy, subservient to hobgoblins and bugbears, who bully them ruthlessly. Their patron deity is Maglubiyet, the Battle Lord, a tyrannical god who conquered the goblin pantheon by killing all the other goblin gods and conscripting their souls into his eternal army. Goblins fear Maglubiyet and serve him out of terror rather than devotion.

Recent editions of D&D have complicated the goblin's traditional role as 'designated cannon fodder.' Sourcebooks like Volo's Guide to Monsters (2016) and the 2024 revision explore goblin culture in greater depth, presenting them as tragic figures — creatures thrust into a hostile world, abused by their larger kin, too small to claim good land but too numerous to ignore. Playable goblin characters have become popular among players who enjoy roleplaying scrappy survivors with chips on their shoulders. The goblin's design has influenced virtually every fantasy RPG and video game since — the goblins of World of Warcraft, Pathfinder, Warhammer, and countless Japanese RPGs all trace their lineage to D&D's interpretation of the European folkloric goblin.
