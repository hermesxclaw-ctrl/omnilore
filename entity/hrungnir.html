Hrungnir, the stone-hearted giant, whose whetstone shattered against Mjölnir and whose leg pinned the thunder god.

Who It Is

Hrungnir is a powerful frost giant, renowned for his strength, his enormous stone weapons, and his three-chambered heart of stone. He is one of the few giants who dared challenge the gods directly. The myth begins with Odin racing the giant through the sky on his horse Sleipnir; Hrungnir boasts that he can outride any god, and in his arrogance, arrives drunk at Valhalla. In the mead hall, he threatens to destroy Asgard, kill all the gods (except Freyja and Sif, whom he intends to take), until the gods summon Thor. Hrungnir challenges Thor to a duel in Jötunheimr. The giant fights with a massive whetstone as his weapon and a shield of stone. Thor throws his hammer Mjölnir, which smashes the whetstone mid-air and crushes Hrungnir's skull. A piece of the whetstone becomes lodged in Thor's forehead, and Hrungnir's leg falls upon Thor, pinning him. None of the gods can lift the leg until Thor's son Magni (three years old at the time) lifts it. The piece of whetstone in Thor's forehead was never removed; it remained as a mark. Hrungnir's heart, made of stone with three points, became a symbol carved on runestones (the 'Hrungnir's heart' symbol).

The Approach

Imagine the strongest creature in the entire world of giants—a being with a heart of solid stone and a head to match—charging into the hall of the gods, drunk and bellowing threats to steal their goddesses and tear down their golden halls. That is Hrungnir, the mightiest of the Jötnar, and the only opponent to ever leave a permanent scar on the god of thunder himself. His story is not a subtle one; it is a brutal, head-on collision of raw, unyielding nature with the divine order of Asgard, a clash so violent that it shatters a whetstone in mid-air and leaves a piece of it embedded in Thor's skull for eternity.

Identity

Hrungnir

Pronunciation HROONG-neer (with a hard, guttural 'H' and a rolled 'r')

Known As

Etymology

The name Hrungnir (Old Norse) is generally considered to be related to the verb 'að hrunga' (to tumble, to fall over, to crash down), fitting for a giant who is felled and whose body crashes to the ground. It may also be connected to the word 'hrunginn' (swollen, bulky), a reference to his immense size and physical mass.

Cultural Ownership

Norse (Old Norse / Icelandic), specifically from the Poetic Edda (Skáldskaparmál) and the Prose Edda (Skáldskaparmál), where the story of his duel with Thor is preserved in detail. He is a Jötunn (giant), a being from Jötunheimr.

Disputed Identifications

There is no major scholarly dispute over Hrungnir's identity, but he is sometimes conflated in popular culture with other generic 'rock giants' or 'mountain giants' due to his stone-based anatomy. He is distinct from the more commonly known Jötnar like Surtr, Ymir, or Loki. He is also sometimes confused with the giant who built the walls of Asgard (who is unnamed in the sources).

Description

Appearance

Hrungnir is described as a colossal Jötunn of immense size and strength. The most distinctive feature is his head, which is said to be made of stone (or have a stone heart). The Prose Edda explicitly states he had a heart of stone, and his head was made of stone. This is not merely a metaphor; it's a physical attribute that makes him a kind of living rock elemental. He is described as being so massive that when he falls, a mountain-like form is left behind.

Behavior

Hrungnir is depicted as arrogant, boastful, and recklessly aggressive. He is a being of immense physical power but limited cunning. He is quick to anger and to accept challenges, and he is a fierce, straightforward fighter who prefers direct combat to trickery. His behavior is that of a classic 'strongman' archetype—prone to hubris and underestimating opponents who use wits over raw force.

Domains

He is a mountain-dwelling giant, associated with the harsh, rocky wilderness of Jötunheimr. His domain is the untamed, uncultivated, and physically dangerous landscape of the giants. He is a personification of the brute, unyielding forces of nature.

Symbols

His symbols are the stone head, the stone heart, and his great shield. The whetstone that he throws at Þórr is also a key symbol, representing his crude, earthy weaponry. His heart is often depicted as a three-pointed shape in later iconography, a direct result of the Prose Edda's description that it was 'hard as stone' and 'three-cornered'.

Powers & Abilities

Colossal Strength

Source Version Prose Edda (Skáldskaparmál)

Description

Hrungnir is explicitly called 'the strongest of the Jötnar' (or 'the mightiest of all giants') in the Prose Edda. He is strong enough to lift and throw a massive whetstone with enough force to shatter Þórr's hammer Mjölnir (though it only lodged in his head).

Limitations

His strength is purely physical; he has no magical abilities or shape-shifting powers. He relies on brute force, which makes him vulnerable to cunning and trickery.

Contradictions

None significant. His strength is consistently portrayed as immense but ultimately inferior to Þórr's divinely-empowered strength.

Stone Anatomy (Head and Heart)

Source Version Prose Edda (Skáldskaparmál)

Description

His head and heart are made of stone. This makes him extremely durable and resistant to physical damage. It is a key weakness, however, as it makes him heavy and slow, and his stone heart is specifically targeted by Þórr's hammer.

Limitations

His stone head is a weak point; Þórr's hammer blow crushes it. His stone heart is also vulnerable to a direct hit. The weight of stone limbs makes him slower and less agile than his divine opponents.

Contradictions

The Hákonarmál stanza suggests he was killed at the 'feet of the mountain', implying he was perhaps caught off-guard or in a specific location, not in a formal duel. This is a minor contradiction with the Prose Edda's detailed duel narrative.

Giant's Shield

Source Version Prose Edda (Skáldskaparmál)

Description

Hrungnir is described as using a great shield, so large it could cover his entire body. This is a standard, if oversized, piece of giant equipment.

Limitations

It is a purely physical defense, and it is outmaneuvered by Þórr's clever attack from above.

Contradictions

None.

Stories & Myths

The Wager with Óðinn and the Ride to Ásgarðr

Source Prose Edda (Skáldskaparmál)Culture NorsePeriod Mythic past (recorded c. 1220 CE)

Summary

Hrungnir, in his arrogance, brags that he will take Ásgarðr, kill all the gods, and keep Freyja and Sif for himself. He then wagers his head against Óðinn's that his horse, Gullfaxi (Golden Mane), is faster than Óðinn's eight-legged steed, Sleipnir. Óðinn accepts the wager, and they race. Óðinn wins, but Hrungnir, in a drunken rage, follows him into Ásgarðr. The gods are alarmed, but because he is unarmed, they offer him a feast. He gets drunk and becomes even more boastful, threatening to drink all the gods' ale and to carry Valhalla away.

Participants

Hrungnir

Óðinn

Sleipnir

The Æsir (implied)

Consequences

The gods, tired of his threats, summon Þórr, who is away. Þórr arrives and is furious. Hrungnir, now sober, realizes he is unarmed and in a precarious position. He agrees to a formal duel, to be held at the border of Jötunheimr.

Variants

The Prose Edda is the only source for this detailed narrative. The Hákonarmál reference is a brief mention of his death, not a full story.

The Duel with Þórr

Source Prose Edda (Skáldskaparmál)Culture NorsePeriod Mythic past (recorded c. 1220 CE)

Summary

The duel is set at a place called Grjótúnagarðar (Stone Enclosures). Hrungnir, aware of Þórr's power, arms himself with a huge whetstone and a massive shield. He stands with his shield in front of him, waiting for Þórr. Þórr, accompanied by his servant Þjálfi, approaches. Þjálfi, seeing Hrungnir's stance, tricks him by saying that Þórr is planning to attack him from underground. Hrungnir, believing this, places his shield on the ground and stands on it. Þórr then throws Mjölnir from above. Hrungnir throws his whetstone. The two projectiles collide in mid-air. The whetstone shatters, and a piece lodges in Þórr's head. Mjölnir, however, continues its flight and crushes Hrungnir's stone head. Hrungnir falls, and his immense body pins Þórr to the ground by the leg. Þjálfi, who has killed Hrungnir's clay companion, Mökkurkálfi, is unable to lift the giant's leg. Þórr is only freed when his son, Magni, arrives and, being only three days old, easily lifts the leg. Þórr, in gratitude, gives Magni Hrungnir's horse, Gullfaxi.

Participants

Hrungnir

Consequences

Hrungnir is killed, his body left to rot at Grjótúnagarðar. Þórr is permanently scarred by the whetstone fragment in his head, a constant source of irritation and a reminder of the duel. Magni gains Gullfaxi. The duel establishes Þórr's superiority over even the strongest of the Jötnar.

Variants

The Hákonarmál stanza offers a more cryptic and possibly older version, simply stating he was killed 'at the feet of the mountain'. The Prose Edda's version is the most detailed and is the canonical narrative.

The Creation of Mökkurkálfi

Source Prose Edda (Skáldskaparmál)Culture NorsePeriod Mythic past (recorded c. 1220 CE)

Summary

To help Hrungnir in the duel, the Jötnar create a massive clay giant named Mökkurkálfi (Mist-Calf). They give it a horse's heart, but it is cowardly and useless. Þjálfi easily dispatches it, highlighting that raw size without courage or a proper heart is worthless.

Participants

Mökkurkálfi

Þjálfi

The Jötnar (implied)

Consequences

The clay giant is destroyed, serving as a comic and symbolic foil to Hrungnir's own stone-heart. It reinforces the theme that true strength comes from a courageous heart, not just a large body.

Variants

This is only found in the Prose Edda.

Relationships

Þórr (Thor)

Type Rival / VictimSource Prose Edda (Skáldskaparmál)

Description

Hrungnir is Þórr's greatest single opponent in a duel. He is the only Jötunn to land a solid, lasting blow on Þórr (the whetstone fragment). Their duel is a classic contest of raw, untamed nature versus divine, ordered power. Þórr is the ultimate victor, but he is permanently marked by the encounter.

Version Note

The duel is the central event of Hrungnir's story.

Conflicting Accounts

None significant.

Óðinn (Odin)

Type Rival / Wager opponentSource Prose Edda (Skáldskaparmál)

Description

Hrungnir's arrogance leads him to challenge Óðinn's horse, Sleipnir, to a race. This act of hubris is what brings him to Ásgarðr and sets the stage for his downfall. Óðinn is the catalyst, not the executioner.

Version Note

The race is a prelude to the main story.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Sleipnir (Odin's Horse)

Type Rival (in a race)Source Prose Edda (Skáldskaparmál)

Description

Hrungnir's horse, Gullfaxi, is fast, but Sleipnir, the eight-legged steed of Óðinn, is faster. The race is a display of Óðinn's superior mount and a humbling experience for Hrungnir.

Version Note

The race is a wager, and Hrungnir loses his head (metaphorically, as a wager).

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Magni (Thor's Son)

Type Indirect opponent / BenefactorSource Prose Edda (Skáldskaparmál)

Description

Hrungnir's corpse, after his death, becomes a problem for Þórr. The giant's leg pins Þórr to the ground, and only Magni, Þórr's three-day-old son, is strong enough to lift it. This act of strength earns Magni the horse Gullfaxi. Hrungnir's body, in death, is a final challenge that Þórr cannot overcome alone.

Version Note

This event highlights the theme of generational strength.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Gullfaxi (Golden Mane)

Type OwnerSource Prose Edda (Skáldskaparmál)

Description

Hrungnir is the owner of this magnificent horse, which he bet on in the race against Óðinn. After his death, the horse is given to Magni as a reward for his strength.

Version Note

The horse is a symbol of Hrungnir's wealth and status, which is transferred to the next generation of gods.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Mökkurkálfi (Mist-Calf)

Type Ally / ChampionSource Prose Edda (Skáldskaparmál)

Description

A clay giant created by the Jötnar to fight alongside Hrungnir. It is a hollow, cowardly construct, easily destroyed by Þjálfi. It serves as a contrast to Hrungnir's own solid, if brutish, nature.

Version Note

The clay giant is a key part of the duel scene.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Freyja and Sif

Type Object of desire / BoastSource Prose Edda (Skáldskaparmál)

Description

In his drunken boasting, Hrungnir threatens to take Freyja for himself and to kill all the gods, including Þórr, and take Sif. This is a direct insult to Þórr and a major reason for the gods' anger.

Version Note

Contradictions & Disputes

Location of Death

Position A

Prose Edda: Hrungnir is killed in a formal duel at a place called Grjótúnagarðar.

Position A — evidence

Snorri's detailed narrative of the duel.

Position B

Hákonarmál (st. 20): Hrungnir is killed 'at the feet of the mountain' (und fjöturs fótum).

Position B — evidence

The older skaldic poem's cryptic phrasing.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars accept Snorri's version as the fuller narrative, but the Hákonarmál reference suggests an older, possibly different, version of the story where the duel was less formalized or took place at a different location.

Nature of the Duel

Position A

Prose Edda: The duel is a formal, pre-arranged combat between Hrungnir and Þórr.

Position A — evidence

Snorri's narrative of the wager and the formal agreement to fight.

Position B

Hákonarmál: The duel may have been a more spontaneous or chaotic event, with Hrungnir being killed in a less ritualistic manner.

Position B — evidence

The poem's vague and evocative language.

Scholarly Consensus

The Prose Edda's version is likely a later, more developed retelling of an older, more ambiguous tradition. The core event—Þórr killing Hrungnir—is consistent.

Primary Evidence

Prose Edda (Skáldskaparmál, ch. 17, 24, 25)

Type textDate c. 1220 CE (written by Snorri Sturluson)Locator Skáldskaparmál, sections 17, 24, 25Language Old Norse (Icelandic)

Translation

The primary narrative source. It recounts Hrungnir's wager with Óðinn, his visit to Ásgarðr, his boasting, and his fatal duel with Þórr, including the famous detail of the whetstone lodged in Þórr's head.

Poetic Edda (Hákonarmál, st. 20)

Type textDate c. 10th century CE (skaldic poem)Locator Stanza 20Language Old Norse

Translation

Provides an alternate, older reference to Hrungnir's death, stating that he was killed by Þórr at the 'feet of the mountain' (und fjöturs fótum), a detail that differs from the Prose Edda's narrative of a formal duel.

Skáldskaparmál (Húsdrápa, st. 2)

Type textDate c. 10th century CE (skaldic poem by Úlfr Uggason)Locator Stanza 2Language Old Norse

Translation

A fragmentary skaldic poem that describes a carving of the fight between Þórr and Hrungnir, confirming the story's antiquity and importance in early Norse visual culture.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

The primary gap is the lack of a fully surviving, independent version of Hrungnir's story outside of Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda. The Hákonarmál reference is too brief to offer a complete alternative narrative. The original context of the story in earlier oral tradition is lost. There is also no surviving visual representation from the Viking Age itself, only literary descriptions of such carvings.
