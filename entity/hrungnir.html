Who It Is

Imagine the strongest creature in the entire world of giants—a being with a heart of solid stone and a head to match—charging into the hall of the gods, drunk and bellowing threats to steal their goddesses and tear down their golden halls. That is Hrungnir, the mightiest of the Jötnar, and the only opponent to ever leave a permanent scar on the god of thunder himself. His story is not a subtle one; it is a brutal, head-on collision of raw, unyielding nature with the divine order of Asgard, a clash so violent that it shatters a whetstone in mid-air and leaves a piece of it embedded in Thor's skull for eternity.

Hrungnir is depicted as arrogant, boastful, and recklessly aggressive. He is a being of immense physical power but limited cunning. He is quick to anger and to accept challenges, and he is a fierce, straightforward fighter who prefers direct combat to trickery. His behavior is that of a classic 'strongman' archetype—prone to hubris and underestimating opponents who use wits over raw force.

Powers
Colossal Strength

Hrungnir is explicitly called 'the strongest of the Jötnar' (or 'the mightiest of all giants') in the Prose Edda. He is strong enough to lift and throw a massive whetstone with enough force to shatter Þórr's hammer Mjölnir (though it only lodged in his head).

Limit: His strength is purely physical; he has no magical abilities or shape-shifting powers. He relies on brute force, which makes him vulnerable to cunning and trickery.

Stone Anatomy (Head and Heart)

His head and heart are made of stone. This makes him extremely durable and resistant to physical damage. It is a key weakness, however, as it makes him heavy and slow, and his stone heart is specifically targeted by Þórr's hammer.

Limit: His stone head is a weak point; Þórr's hammer blow crushes it. His stone heart is also vulnerable to a direct hit. The weight of stone limbs makes him slower and less agile than his divine opponents.

Giant's Shield

Hrungnir is described as using a great shield, so large it could cover his entire body. This is a standard, if oversized, piece of giant equipment.

Limit: It is a purely physical defense, and it is outmaneuvered by Þórr's clever attack from above.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Hrungnir is described as a colossal Jötunn of immense size and strength. The most distinctive feature is his head, which is said to be made of stone (or have a stone heart). The Prose Edda explicitly states he had a heart of stone, and his head was made of stone. This is not merely a metaphor; it's a physical attribute that makes him a kind of living rock elemental. Symbols — His symbols are the stone head, the stone heart, and his great shield. The whetstone that he throws at Þórr is also a key symbol, representing his crude, earthy weaponry. His heart is often depicted as a three-pointed shape in later iconography, a direct result of the Prose Edda's description that it was 'hard as stone' and 'three-cornered'.

Domains — He is a mountain-dwelling giant, associated with the harsh, rocky wilderness of Jötunheimr. His domain is the untamed, uncultivated, and physically dangerous landscape of the giants. He is a personification of the brute, unyielding forces of nature.

Accounts I
The Wager with Óðinn and the Ride to ÁsgarðrProse Edda (Skáldskaparmál)

Mythic past (recorded c. 1220 CE)

Hrungnir, in his arrogance, brags that he will take Ásgarðr, kill all the gods, and keep Freyja and Sif for himself. He then wagers his head against Óðinn's that his horse, Gullfaxi (Golden Mane), is faster than Óðinn's eight-legged steed, Sleipnir. Óðinn accepts the wager, and they race. Óðinn wins, but Hrungnir, in a drunken rage, follows him into Ásgarðr. The gods are alarmed, but because he is unarmed, they offer him a feast. He gets drunk and becomes even more boastful, threatening to drink all the gods' ale and to carry Valhalla away. The gods, tired of his threats, summon Þórr, who is away. Þórr arrives and is furious. Hrungnir, now sober, realizes he is unarmed and in a precarious position. He agrees to a formal duel, to be held at the border of Jötunheimr.

Accounts II
The Duel with ÞórrProse Edda (Skáldskaparmál)

Mythic past (recorded c. 1220 CE)

The duel is set at a place called Grjótúnagarðar (Stone Enclosures). Hrungnir, aware of Þórr's power, arms himself with a huge whetstone and a massive shield. He stands with his shield in front of him, waiting for Þórr. Þórr, accompanied by his servant Þjálfi, approaches. Þjálfi, seeing Hrungnir's stance, tricks him by saying that Þórr is planning to attack him from underground. Hrungnir, believing this, places his shield on the ground and stands on it. Þórr then throws Mjölnir from above. Hrungnir throws his whetstone. The two projectiles collide in mid-air. The whetstone shatters, and a piece lodges in Þórr's head. Mjölnir, however, continues its flight and crushes Hrungnir's stone head. Hrungnir falls, and his immense body pins Þórr to the ground by the leg. Þjálfi, who has killed Hrungnir's clay companion, Mökkurkálfi, is unable to lift the giant's leg. Þórr is only freed when his son, Magni, arrives and, being only three days old, easily lifts the leg. The Creation of MökkurkálfiProse Edda (Skáldskaparmál)

Mythic past (recorded c. 1220 CE)

To help Hrungnir in the duel, the Jötnar create a massive clay giant named Mökkurkálfi (Mist-Calf). They give it a horse's heart, but it is cowardly and useless. Þjálfi easily dispatches it, highlighting that raw size without courage or a proper heart is worthless. The clay giant is destroyed, serving as a comic and symbolic foil to Hrungnir's own stone-heart. It reinforces the theme that true strength comes from a courageous heart, not just a large body.

Hrungnir is a 'rock giant' or 'mountain giant' in the same vein as a D&D Stone Giant. — While he has a stone head and heart, he is a Jötunn, a being of the same mythological class as Loki, Surtr, and Ymir. He is not a separate 'elemental' species. He is a giant among giants, distinguished by his strength and his stone anatomy.

Hrungnir is a stupid, mindless brute. — While arrogant and boastful, he is not stupid. He is cunning enough to realize he is unarmed in Ásgarðr and agrees to a fair duel. He also anticipates Þórr's attack and sets up a defensive position with his shield. His downfall comes from being outmaneuvered by Þjálfi's trick, not from a lack of intelligence.

Hrungnir is a 'minor' or 'one-off' villain in Norse mythology. — He is a major figure. He is the only opponent to ever permanently wound Þórr (with the whetstone in his head). His story is one of the most detailed and dramatic in the Prose Edda, and it is referenced in skaldic poetry, indicating it was a well-known and important myth.
