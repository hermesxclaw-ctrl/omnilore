Who It Is

Raven is the central figure in the mythology of the Pacific Northwest Coast peoples — simultaneously a trickster, a creator god, a culture hero, and a shape-shifter. Among the Tlingit, Haida, Tsimshian, and other coastal nations, Raven is the being who brought light to the world, released the first humans from a clamshell, and established many of the fundamental conditions of human existence — though he did so through theft, deception, and an insatiable appetite rather than divine benevolence.

Physically, Raven is described as his namesake bird — but he can transform into any form at will. In Tlingit and Haida art, Raven is depicted in the distinctive formline style of Northwest Coast art: a black bird with a straight, heavy beak, often shown with the sun held in his beak, representing his most famous exploit. Unlike the eagle, Raven's beak is straight rather than curved. In his human form, he is sometimes described as a small, dark man with a strange, knowing glint in his eye. His feathers may show hints of blue or purple iridescence — the remnants of his original celestial nature.

The most famous Raven story — shared with variations across the Northwest Coast — is 'Raven Steals the Light.' In the beginning, the world was in total darkness. An old chief kept the sun, moon, and stars locked in carved boxes in his house. Raven, tired of stumbling in the dark, transformed himself into a hemlock needle floating in a stream. The chief's daughter drank the water and swallowed the needle, becoming pregnant with Raven. Reborn as the chief's grandson, Raven cried incessantly until the chief, to pacify the child, let him play with the star box. Raven opened it, releasing the stars into the sky. He cried again for the moon box, releasing the moon. Finally, he demanded the sun box. When it was given to him, Raven transformed back into his bird form, seized the sun in his beak, and flew up through the smoke hole of the longhouse, escaping into the sky. The black soot of the smoke hole stained his feathers forever, and the world below was illuminated for the first time.

Raven is also the liberator of humanity. In Haida tradition, after bringing light to the world, Raven discovered the first humans trapped inside a giant clamshell on the beach. Curious about the sounds coming from inside, he pried the shell open and coaxed the timid beings out — thus becoming the father of the Haida people. This dual role as bringer of both light and life establishes Raven as arguably the most important figure in Northwest Coast cosmology.

Yet Raven is also unmistakably a trickster — greedy, lustful, vain, and often humiliated by his own schemes. In countless stories, his hunger leads him into trouble: he steals fish, is outwitted by other animals, and suffers comical punishments. These stories were told around winter fires for entertainment and moral instruction, with the audience laughing at Raven's foolishness even as they acknowledged his cosmic significance. Raven reminds us that creation and chaos are never far apart.
