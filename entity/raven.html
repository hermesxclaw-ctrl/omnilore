I stole the sun because the dark was boring. I freed the first people because the clamshell was noisy. I am not a hero — I am hungry. That is the same thing.

Who It Is

Raven is the central figure in the mythology of the Pacific Northwest Coast peoples — simultaneously a trickster, a creator god, a culture hero, and a shape-shifter. Among the Tlingit, Haida, Tsimshian, and other coastal nations, Raven is the being who brought light to the world, released the first humans from a clamshell, and established many of the fundamental conditions of human existence — though he did so through theft, deception, and an insatiable appetite rather than divine benevolence.

Physically, Raven is described as his namesake bird — but he can transform into any form at will. In Tlingit and Haida art, Raven is depicted in the distinctive formline style of Northwest Coast art: a black bird with a straight, heavy beak, often shown with the sun held in his beak, representing his most famous exploit. Unlike the eagle, Raven's beak is straight rather than curved. In his human form, he is sometimes described as a small, dark man with a strange, knowing glint in his eye. His feathers may show hints of blue or purple iridescence — the remnants of his original celestial nature.

The most famous Raven story — shared with variations across the Northwest Coast — is 'Raven Steals the Light.' In the beginning, the world was in total darkness. An old chief kept the sun, moon, and stars locked in carved boxes in his house. Raven, tired of stumbling in the dark, transformed himself into a hemlock needle floating in a stream. The chief's daughter drank the water and swallowed the needle, becoming pregnant with Raven. Reborn as the chief's grandson, Raven cried incessantly until the chief, to pacify the child, let him play with the star box. Raven opened it, releasing the stars into the sky. He cried again for the moon box, releasing the moon. Finally, he demanded the sun box. When it was given to him, Raven transformed back into his bird form, seized the sun in his beak, and flew up through the smoke hole of the longhouse, escaping into the sky. The black soot of the smoke hole stained his feathers forever, and the world below was illuminated for the first time.

Raven is also the liberator of humanity. In Haida tradition, after bringing light to the world, Raven discovered the first humans trapped inside a giant clamshell on the beach. Curious about the sounds coming from inside, he pried the shell open and coaxed the timid beings out — thus becoming the father of the Haida people. This dual role as bringer of both light and life establishes Raven as arguably the most important figure in Northwest Coast cosmology.

Yet Raven is also unmistakably a trickster — greedy, lustful, vain, and often humiliated by his own schemes. In countless stories, his hunger leads him into trouble: he steals fish, is outwitted by other animals, and suffers comical punishments. These stories were told around winter fires for entertainment and moral instruction, with the audience laughing at Raven's foolishness even as they acknowledged his cosmic significance. Raven reminds us that creation and chaos are never far apart.

Form

Raven is a shape-shifter par excellence. In his most common form, he appears as a literal raven — a large, black bird with a heavy beak, intelligent black eyes, and a voice capable of everything from a soft croak to a harsh, mocking caw. He is often distinguished from ordinary ravens by some marker of his supernatural nature: he may speak with a human voice, carry objects with human-like dexterity, or take actions (starting fires, opening boxes, wearing clothes) that no ordinary bird could perform. In his human form, Raven often appears as an old man, a traveler, or a beggar. In some stories, his transformation is incomplete or imperfect: he may have raven's feet, feathers showing through his clothing, or an irrepressible croak in his voice. In Northwest Coast formline art, Raven is depicted with a long, straight beak — the primary visual cue distinguishing him from Eagle (curved beak) and Hawk (short, hooked beak). He is often shown with his beak open, holding or releasing light, or transforming into or from human form.

Powers

Shape-shifting Raven can transform into any form at will: human, animal, plant, or inanimate object. His most common transformations are into a pine needle, a leaf, a baby, an old man, or a beautiful woman — all forms chosen to infiltrate the defenses of his targets. He can shift between raven form and human form mid-sentence, and he frequently uses this ability to escape the consequences of his actions. Pan-Northwest Coast oral tradition His transformations may be imperfect, with telltale raven features (feet, voice, feathers) remaining visible. He can be trapped in a form if tricked by a more powerful being. In some stories, his transformations require specific conditions or are limited in duration. The extent and mechanics of Raven's shape-shifting vary. In some stories, he is a raven who acts human; in others, a being who can become anything.

Creation and Transformation of the World Raven is responsible for bringing light to the world (stealing the sun, moon, and stars), creating or releasing fresh water, bringing salmon to the rivers, shaping the landscape, and creating or transforming human beings. In Haida tradition, he is the primary creator, though his creative acts are often accidental or motivated by self-interest rather than benevolence. Haida, Tlingit, and Tsimshian oral tradition Raven is not an omnipotent creator. He works with and transforms existing materials rather than creating ex nihilo. His creations are often incomplete or imperfect, reflecting his impulsive and messy methods. In Tlingit tradition, the higher entity Great Raven (Nas-ca'kí-yéil) is the primary creator, while the trickster Yéil acts as a transformer. In Haida tradition, these roles are combined in a single Raven figure.

Unlimited Appetite and Cunning Raven's primary 'power' is his insatiable hunger — for food, novelty, and knowledge — combined with an endlessly creative intelligence. He can devise a scheme to get anything he wants, no matter how impossible. His greed is the engine of his genius; he will go to extraordinary lengths for a single fish or an amusing prank. Pan-Northwest Coast oral tradition His greed frequently causes his schemes to fail, or to succeed in ways that harm him. His inability to control his appetite is his greatest weakness — he will abandon a successful plan to chase a meal, or ruin a perfect disguise by croaking in satisfaction. None.

Flight and Travel As a bird, Raven can fly over the entire world. His journeys take him from the bottom of the ocean to the sky, from the human world to the spirit world and back. His wings carry him across the boundaries that constrain other beings. Pan-Northwest Coast oral tradition He can be injured, trapped, or slowed. His flight does not make him invulnerable. None.

Theft of Light Raven's most famous specific act is the theft of light: he steals the sun, moon, and stars from the being who hoarded them and releases them into the sky. This is not a repeatable power but a defining, world-changing deed that establishes his role as the bringer of order (or at least, of the conditions that make human life possible). Haida and Tlingit oral tradition This is a one-time event. Raven does not have ongoing control over the celestial bodies, though he may continue to interact with them as characters in his stories. The identity of the being who hoarded the light varies: it is often a chief or a supernatural being who kept the sun, moon, and stars in a series of nested boxes. In Tsimshian versions, the light is sometimes held by the Sky Chief or another figure.

Behavior & Habitat

Raven is driven by an insatiable appetite — for food, for novelty, for power, for light, and for the sheer chaotic pleasure of causing disruption. He is endlessly curious, compulsively clever, and almost completely lacking in self-control. His schemes range from the cosmically significant (stealing the sun to bring light to the world) to the absurdly petty (stealing a fish from a gull because he's hungry now). He is a master of disguise and impersonation, frequently transforming into a leaf, a pine needle, a baby, or a beautiful woman to infiltrate a target's defenses. His schemes often succeed brilliantly — and then backfire spectacularly when his greed, lust, or simple inability to stop talking undoes him. He is not a moral being in any conventional sense: he does good things (bringing light, creating humans, defeating monsters) and terrible things (tricking, betraying, and sometimes killing others, often for trivial reasons) with equal creative energy. His stories are often hilarious, sometimes horrifying, and always unpredictable.

Accounts

In the beginning, the world was dark. An old chief (or supernatural being) hoarded the sun, moon, and stars in a series of nested cedar boxes, keeping all light for himself. Raven, tired of bumping into things in the dark, discovered the chief's village and learned that the chief had a daughter who guarded the boxes. Raven transformed himself into a pine needle floating in the daughter's drinking water. She swallowed him, became pregnant, and gave birth to a strange, dark-haired baby boy (Raven in infant form). The chief, doting on his grandson, indulged the baby's every wish. The baby first asked to play with the box of stars — when it was opened, the stars flew up through the smoke hole into the sky. Then Raven demanded the box of the moon, and the moon similarly escaped. Finally, he screamed and cried until the chief relented and let him play with the box of the sun. The moment the box was opened, Raven shed his baby disguise, transformed back into a bird, seized the sun in his beak, and flew out through the smoke hole — which is why ravens' feathers are now black (they were burned by the sun). He released the sun into the sky, bringing light to the world for the first time.

After Raven had stolen the light and shaped much of the world, he was walking along a beach on Haida Gwaii (then called the Islands of the People) when he heard voices coming from a giant clamshell on the shore. Curious, he approached and saw small, frightened creatures huddled inside the shell. They were the first humans, hiding from the vast, dark world. Raven landed on the clamshell and coaxed them out with his most charming voice, telling them how wonderful the world was and how much fun they would have. Slowly, the humans emerged — tentative, blinking in the light that Raven had stolen. They were naked and shivering, so Raven found cedar bark and showed them how to make clothing. He taught them where to find food, how to build shelter, and how to survive. In some versions, Raven was disappointed by the humans (they were not as clever or as entertaining as he had hoped), but he felt responsible for them. In other versions, he was delighted by his new creations and cawed with laughter as they stumbled into the world.

In the early world, salmon did not exist, and the people were hungry. Raven learned that a powerful being — sometimes the Salmon People, sometimes a single spirit — controlled all the salmon and kept them hidden. Raven transformed himself and infiltrated their domain. He tricked or seduced his way into the salmon's home and stole a salmon, which he threw into a river. The salmon multiplied and filled the rivers, providing food for the people. In some versions, Raven also taught the people how to catch, clean, and respect the salmon. In other versions, his theft was purely selfish — he wanted the salmon for himself — and the benefit to humanity was an accident.

In the beginning, the tide did not rise and fall; the sea was always at the same level, and the people could not access the shellfish and other foods in the intertidal zone. Raven went to the Tide Woman — an old woman who sat by the edge of the sea, controlling the tide — and asked her to let the tide go out so the people could gather food. She refused. Raven then tricked her, sometimes by tickling her until she laughed and let go of the tide, sometimes by distracting her, and sometimes by more aggressive means. Whatever the method, she released the tide, and it rushed out, exposing the beach. Raven then made her promise to let the tide go in and out regularly, so the people would always have access to the intertidal food sources.

Raven, in human form as an old man, was traveling and grew hungry. He came upon a fisherman and his beautiful daughter. Raven asked to marry the daughter, promising to provide for the family. The fisherman, skeptical, agreed — and Raven began catching fish in supernatural abundance, filling the family's stores. For a time, all was well. But Raven's nature could not be contained: he began eating the finest fish himself, sneaking off to caw at the moon, and generally behaving more and more like a raven and less like a husband. Eventually, the fisherman caught Raven in his true form — a black bird — gorging on the best fish. Furious, the fisherman chased Raven away. As he flew off, Raven laughed and shouted that he had at least eaten well while it lasted.

Field Notes

☵ In the Raven Steals the Light story, Raven was originally white. His feathers were permanently scorched black when he flew out of the smoke hole carrying the sun — a detail that simultaneously explains raven coloration and marks him as a being permanently transformed by his own greatest act.

☵ The real common raven (Corvus corax) is one of the most intelligent animals on Earth, capable of tool use, puzzle-solving, play, and deception — traits that the Northwest Coast peoples observed and wove into their mythic Raven millennia before modern science confirmed corvid intelligence.

☵ Bill Reid's monumental sculpture 'The Raven and the First Men' was featured on the Canadian $20 bill from 2004 to 2012 — one of the few instances of Indigenous art appearing directly on national currency.

☵ In Haida society, everyone belongs to either the Raven moiety or the Eagle moiety, and marriage must be between members of opposite moieties. This means that every Haida person carries a clan identity that directly references the mythic beings at the center of their oral tradition.

☵ Raven stories are traditionally told only in the winter months. Telling Raven stories in summer is considered disrespectful or even dangerous, as the stories have power and the summer is a time for work, not for summoning the trickster's attention.

Scholarly Debates
Raven as Creator vs. Transformer
In Haida tradition, Raven is the primary creator who made the world, brought light, and shaped humanity — he is effectively a demiurge. ⇌ In Tlingit tradition, Raven is a transformer who reshapes an already-existing world and is subordinate to Great Raven (Nas-ca'kí-yéil), the more remote creator.
Evidence for A: Haida oral tradition consistently attributes all major creative acts to a single Raven figure. Swanton's collected Haida
Evidence for B: Tlingit oral tradition distinguishes between Great Raven (a cosmic creator) and Yéil (the trickster-transformer). Swanto
Raven's Morality
Raven is a fundamentally positive figure — a culture hero who brought light, salmon, and knowledge to humanity. His trickery is in service of creation. ⇌ Raven is a morally chaotic figure whose selfish appetites cause suffering as often as they create benefit. His 'heroic' acts are accidental byproducts of his greed.
Evidence for A: Many stories emphasize the beneficial outcomes of Raven's actions: the world has light, humanity has food, natural cycle
Evidence for B: Many stories emphasize Raven's cruelty, greed, and the suffering he causes: he steals, betrays, and sometimes kills for
What We Don't Know

The full extent of the Raven cycle across all Pacific Northwest nations is not comprehensively documented in accessible English sources. Many stories remain untold to outsiders, either because they are sacred or because they are clan property. The relationship between the Pacific Northwest Raven figure and the Arctic/Inuit raven figure (Tulugaq) is not well-studied. The pre-contact visual iconography of Raven in archaeological contexts is fragmentary. Contemporary Indigenous reclamation and reinterpretation of Raven stories is an ongoing and evolving field that is not well-captured by published scholarship.

A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine the universe as a massive, dark, locked mansion, and every good thing — light, water, food, music, stories, humans — is locked in a room somewhere. Raven is the burglar who breaks in: not for any noble reason, but because he's bored, hungry, and curious about what's behind the next door. He picks the locks, pockets the treasure, and on his way out, accidentally drops half of it where everyone else can find it. That's creation as Raven does it: brilliant, greedy, chaotic, and ultimately generous by accident. He's not your savior — he's the friend who crashes on your couch, eats all your food, tells the best stories you've ever heard, and somehow, in the process, makes your life richer than it was before. Just keep an eye on your daughters and your fish supply.
