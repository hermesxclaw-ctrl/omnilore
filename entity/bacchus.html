Who It Is

Picture the most liberating and most terrifying god in the Greek pantheon arriving in a city as a beautiful, wine-stained stranger — doors unbarring of their own accord, chains falling from his worshippers, vines and ivy sprouting from the floorboards — and burning the one who asks to see him in his true form, that same god later carrying his own mother up from Hades and riding a tiger-drawn chariot home to a Roman triumph. Dionysus—Bacchus, Liber, the 'twice-born'—is the god who comes, the god who dissolves the self, and the god you cannot safely refuse, a lord of masks, madness and wine whose ecstatic gift and whose terrible justice are one and the same power.

Two faces in one god: the patient, liberating god of wine, masks, theatre, ecstatic freedom and joyful release; and the terrifying, punishing epiphany-god who drives the uninitiated and the impious mad. He is a boundary-crosser, permanently an 'arriving stranger' (the god who comes), at home and yet foreign. He dissolves the self and reverses civic, gender and species boundaries. Powers
Wine and the vine

Dionysus is the god of winemaking; wine is his earthly incarnation. He can call vines, ivy, and grape-clusters into being (his bed-chamber and the world 'laugh' with vines and flowers when Zeus begets him; a vine twines around and strangles the Spartan-like rebel Lycurgus in one variant). He grants the gift and curse of wine to mortals — pleasure, ease of suffering, freedom, and madness. He is patron of viniculture and the vintage and receives the first-fruit libations.

Limit: Wine-drinking is forbidden to some (Demeter refuses it as against themis after Persephone's abduction); uncontrolled wine destroys the drinker rather than frees them.

Divine madness and ecstasy (enthusiasm)

He induces bakkheia — ritual frenzy — and 'enthusiasm' (etymologically, to have the god within). His maenads run wild on the mountains, tear animals (and men) apart with bare hands, and are protected from blade-fire and harm by his presence. He drives skeptics insane as punishment (Pentheus, Lycurgus). He frees followers from self-conscious fear and subverts the restraints of the powerful.

Limit: The madness is a two-edged gift: liberating to the willing, deadly to those who reject or mock the god.

Shape-shifting and theriomorphism

In his epiphany and punishment myths he appears or transforms as a bull, lion, leopard, serpent, and goat, and as a bearded mature man or an effeminate youth. He turns mortal foes and captors into beasts (the Tyrrhenian pirates are changed into dolphins; the rebel Lycurgus is maddened and his own son cut down; the nymph Psalacantha is turned into a plant).

Limit: Transformations are retributive and part of the god's epiphany, not arbitrary.

Miraculous protection and release

The god frees mortals from bonds; chains fall away, locked doors open, and his worshippers are immune to normal harm. The chains bind him fall off; the prison opens. His followers are unharmed by fire in some accounts.

Limit: Protection is conditional on devotion; impious onlookers are destroyed.

Katabasis and resurrection

Dionysus descends to the underworld to retrieve his mother Semele and raise her to Olympus (Pausanias' Lerna tradition; Aristophanes' The Frogs inverts it for comedy). In Orphic theology he is himself a dying-and-rising god, dismembered by the Titans and reborn, the pattern taken up by later mystery cults.

Limit: His underworld descent is episodic and mostly celebratory rather than a permanent underworld-dweller role, though he shares chthonic epithets (Chthonios, Eubouleus) with Hades.

Lord of the mask and theatre

As the patron of the Dionysia and the source of tragic performance, Dionysus is the god of the mask, of role-taking, of 'borrowed identities' and of becoming-another. He is the inspiration of drama and the god whose priests and festivals staged tragedy and comedy.

Limit: His connection to a specific repertoire of plays is institutional; his 'custodian of borrowed identities' reading is a modern reconstruction.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Dionysus is the youngest of the Olympians and notoriously hard to pin down iconographically because he appears in many forms: a beautiful long-haired youth (notably the painted type of a beardless, curly-haired young god), an older bearded wine-master, and a diety who transforms in the myths into a bull, a lion, a leopard, a serpent, and a goat. Symbols — The thyrsus (fennel wand), the kantharos and krater (wine vessels), the grape-vine and ivy, the bull and the leopard/panther, the snake, the mask. As a theatre god he is also the visual image behind the mask of drama and the aniconic cult pillar (the 'masked god on a pillar'). His processional chariot, in Hellenistic and Roman art, is drawn by tigers and accompanied by maenads, satyrs and drunken followers — the iconography of his trium

Domains — Vineyards, wine-presses, orchards, wild mountains (notably Cithaeron and Nysa), the sea-crossing between islands, the theatre (Athens' Dionysia festival, the polis stage itself), the underworld in his katabasis myths, and, in his Romanised form, the banquet and the triumph. His mystery-cult met in groves (Stimula on the Aventine), and his 'caves' and mountain-vents at places like Delphi were sacred to him.

Accounts I
The double birth of the twice-born godHomeric Hymn 1 (fragments); Nonnus, Dionysiaca Books 7-9; Hyginus, Fabulae 167; Diodorus Siculus 4

Archaic through Late Antiquity

Semele, daughter of Cadmus and Harmonia of Thebes, is loved by Zeus. Hera, envious, disguises herself as the nurse Beroe and persuades the pregnant Semele to ask Zeus to appear in his full glory. Zeus, bound by his oath, reveals himself holding his lightning, and Semele is burnt to ash. Zeus rescues the unborn child and sews it into his own thigh; in due course the infant is born from Zeus' thigh, receiving the surname 'twice-born' (Dimetor). Nonnus renders the birth with a vivid, near-orphic lyricism: the rounded thigh in labour becomes female, and the boy is brought forth having passed from a mother's womb to a father's; he is born horned, crowned with ivy, and wrapped with horned snakes, and nursed on Mount Nysa by the Nysiad nymphs. Semele is deified (as the goddess Thyone) and later rescued from Hades by her son; Dionysus gains the epithet Dimetor/twice-born and the permanent mark of his dual divine-human origin, which underwrites his claim to be an Olympian.

Pentheus and the sparagmos at ThebesEuripides, The Bacchae (405 BC); Ovid, Metamorphoses Book 3; Nonnus

Classical Athens, 5th century BC and later retellings

The god, newly manifest, returns to his birthplace Thebes (then ruled by his cousin Pentheus) in human disguise. Pentheus, along with his mother Agave and aunts Ino and Autonoë, denies Dionysus' divinity. Despite the blind seer Tiresias' warning, Pentheus denounces the revels. Dionysus drives him mad and lures him to spy, disguised as a woman, on the maenads on Mount Cithaeron. The possessed women take him for a lion and, with his own mother Agave among them, rip him limb from limb. Agave fixes his head on her thyrsus and bears it in triumph to Cadmus, only to be brought to horrified recognition en route home. Thebes is devastated by the killing of its young king by his own mother; the episode becomes the canonical image of the god's terrible epiphany. Euripides' version of the sparagmos (the limb-by-limb dismemberment) is one of the most famous passages in Greek tragedy and a recurring motif in later art and scholarship.

Lycurgus the Thracian king punishedHomeric (Iliad 6.130-40 reference); Apollodorus; Nonnus' Dionysiaca; Lycurgus Cup iconography

Epic through Late Antiquity

In Thrace, King Lycurgus attacks the god's followers, imprisoning the maenads. Dionysus flees to Thetis' care, sends a drought over the land, and drives Lycurgus mad. Lycurgus, in a wine-induced frenzy, slices his own son into pieces with an axe believing he is a patch of ivy (a plant holy to the god). An oracle declares the land barren while Lycurgus lives, and his people have him drawn and quartered. In an alternative artistic version Lycurgus tries to kill Ambrosia, a follower, who is transformed into a vine that strangles him. The tribe is delivered; the land's fertility returns. The myth is the source of the famous fourth-century AD Lycurgus Cup, which shows the bound king taunted by the god and attacked by a satyr.

Accounts II
The Tyrrhenian pirates turned to dolphinsHomeric Hymn to Dionysus (7); Ovid, Metamorphoses Book 3; Pseudo-Hyginus; Servius

Archaic hymn through Roman commentary

The god (in Ovid, a beautiful young boy; in other sources, a man) hires a Tyrrhenian pirate ship to sail from Icaria to Naxos. The pirates set course instead for Asia Minor to sell him into slavery. The god reveals himself, turns mast and oars into serpents and fills the vessel with ivy and flute-music so the sailors go mad and leap into the sea, becoming dolphins. Only the helmsman Acoetes, who tried to protect him, is spared. In Pseudo-Hyginus and Servius the extra motive is that the pirates attempted to rape the god. The incident is, for many scholars, the final proof of the god's divinity and a prelude to his katabasis. The name 'Bacchus' arises in some readings from this shipboard bacchic frenzy.

Ariadne on Naxos and the katabasis to rescue SemeleOvid; Pausanias Description of Greece 2; Pseudo-Apollodorus; Aristophanes' The Frogs

Classical through Roman

When Theseus abandons Ariadne asleep on Naxos, Dionysus finds and marries her; they have a son, Oenopion ('wine-face'). In variants the god sets her crown in the heavens as the constellation Corona, or descends to Hades to restore her to the gods. In a parallel katabasis (Pausanias 2) Dionysus descends to the underworld to rescue his mother Semele, contended with Cerberus (restrained for him by Heracles) and emerged with her from the waters of the Lerna lagoon, a yearly secret nighttime festival commemorating the event. Aristophanes' The Frogs comically reinvents the katabasis for theatre: the god descends to fetch a great tragedian and, after a poetry-rhyming contest, brings back Aeschylus over Euripides. Ariadne becomes the god's consort (and in many readings symbolises the soul the god liberates); Semele is raised to Olympus as Thyone; the Frogs establishes Dionysus as the god of drama's own comedy of descent.

The Indian War and the returning conqueror (euhemerised legend)Nonnus, Dionysiaca Books 13-40; Diodorus Siculus 4; Pliny; Roman imperial cult

Hellenistic through Roman

The god campaigns eastward and conquers India, a massive legendary war populated by his thiasos and opposed armies; on his triumphant return he rides in a tiger-drawn chariot attended by maenads, satyrs and revellers. Roman sources interpreted this as the historical prototype of the Roman Triumph (Pliny), and the ancestor-legend made him patron and founding hero of cities including Leptis Magna, birthplace of the emperor Septimius Severus, who promoted the cult. The 'conquering Bacchus' legend fused the liberator-god with imperial triumph, giving the Roman celebration of victory a god to embody it, and granting dynasties (Ptolemies, Severans) a divine founder.

The Apollonian/Dionysian oppositionWestern culture treats Apollo-versus-Dionysus as an ancient and central Greek opposition.The opposition is a modern myth invented by Nietzsche and Winckelmann; it is not found in classical sources.
Euhemerised conqueror vs chthonic mystery godSome Roman and euhemerist readings make Bacchus a historical conqueror and founder of cities (patron of Leptis Magna; prototype of the Roman triumph).The god is at core a chthonic, mystery and theatre deity whose 'conquest' is mythic allegory.

Bacchus and Dionysus are simply the same person with two names. — Dionysus is the Greek theonym; Bacchus is originally a Greek epithet (Bakkhos, the frenzy he induces) that the Romans adopted alongside their own native Liber Pater. The Roman Bacchus/Liber was a distinct, politically shaped fusion; Cicero denied the identity.

The god is no more than the party-god of wine and drunken revelry. — Dionysus is equally a god of madness, death, resurrection, the underworld katabasis, the mask and theatre, and terrifying divine justice; his 'playful' face is only one facet.

The maenads' dismemberment of Pentheus proves ancient Bacchic worship practised real human sacrifice. — The sparagmos of Pentheus is a mythic/tragic narrative; there is no reliable evidence that Dionysiac cults ritually dismembered or sacrificed human beings, and the omophagia is largely literary.

The Apollonian/Dionysian split is an ancient Greek idea. — It is a modern philosophical/psychological construction (chiefly Nietzsche and Winckelmann), absent from classical sources.
