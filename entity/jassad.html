Who It Is

The Jassad (literally 'body, corpse, shell' in Arabic) is a supernatural entity from Islamic tradition — a demon who took the form of the Prophet-King Solomon (Sulayman) and usurped his throne. This legend derives from Quran 38:34: 'And We certainly tried Solomon and placed on his throne a jasad [body]; then he returned [to Us].' Islamic exegesis (tafsir) elaborates this verse extensively: the demon who stole Solomon's form was named Sakhr (or Asif, or Habiq in various traditions), and he stole Solomon's signet ring — the source of his divine authority over jinn, animals, and the wind — by tricking one of Solomon's wives or concubines (often named as Jarada). With the ring, Sakhr assumed Solomon's exact physical form, sat on his throne, and ruled for forty days (or forty years in some versions), during which time the real Solomon wandered as an unrecognised beggar, a punishment for a sin (idolatry in his household, or pride). The Jassad ruled capriciously, issuing unjust decrees and indulging the harem, while the true Solomon was tested by the loss of his kingdom. Eventually, Solomon recovered the ring (either the demon threw it into the sea and a fish returned it, or the demon fled and dropped it), and he returned to his throne, binding Sakhr in chains or imprisoning him in a bottle. This legend profoundly influenced later Islamic and Jewish demonology, the concept of the demon doppelgänger, and the narrative of the 'false king.' The Jassad/Sakhr also appears in the Testament of Solomon (a pseudepigraphical Greek text with significant Islamic and Jewish recensions) as a demon bound by Solomon. The Jassad represents the ultimate demonic threat: not destruction but usurpation — the replacement of the rightful ruler with a perfect copy, indistinguishable to all but God. It encodes anxieties about legitimacy, identity, and the fragility of divine favour.
