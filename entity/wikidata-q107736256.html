The Approach

The name Bogard hits like a fist in an arcade — most people hear it and see a red cap, a ponytail, and a shout of 'Are you okay?' echoing across Southtown. But that same surname has been printed on Baptist tracts, stamped on a Missouri post office, and inked by a Flemish printer in Leuven four centuries before any Power Wave ever left a glove.

Etymology

Surname Bogard is a variant of Bogart / Bogaert / Boomgaard. Most authorities derive it from Middle Dutch bogaert / boomgaert 'orchard, fruit garden' (modern Dutch boomgaard), originally an occupational or topographic name for an orchard keeper or someone living near an orchard. Oxford Dictionary of American Family Names (Hanks et al.) lists Bogard as an Americanized form of French Bogaert and Dutch/Flemish Bogaerts, with parallel forms Bogardus. In northern France and Flanders the form Bogaert/Bogard is well attested from the 14th century. Folk etymologies linking it to English 'boggart' (dialectal spirit/bugbear, from boggart/bogart) are rejected by onomastic scholars; the two words have separate origins despite modern confusion. Given name use is rare; almost all instances are surnames or fictional surnames derived from the surname stock.

Cultural Ownership

No single cultural ownership. (1) As a surname, belongs to families of Dutch, Flemish, French, and German origin, now most numerous in the United States, France, and Belgium. (2) As a toponym, belongs to local history of Carroll, Henry and Johnson counties, Missouri, USA. (3) As a fictional family name, intellectual property of SNK Corporation (Japan), created by Takashi Nishiyama and Hiroshi Matsumoto and subsequent SNK teams. The fictional Bogards are American characters created by a Japanese studio, not a folklore tradition.

Disputed Identifications

1) Bogard vs. Bogart vs. Boggart: Frequently conflated in search engines and popular writing. Humphrey Bogart's surname is etymologically related (same orchard root) but distinct family line; Boggart (Lancashire/Yorkshire folklore spirit) is unrelated etymologically and should not be merged. 2) Wikidata Q107736256 is currently a minimal disambiguation-type item labeled 'Bogard' with no description; it resolves to the Wikipedia disambiguation page, not to a single person or character. Some mirrors incorrectly treat it as a fictional character item. 3) Seth Bogart (born Seth Davis Bogard, 1980) uses Bogart professionally but is legally Bogard; databases split entries. 4) Jan Bogard (Flemish printer, died c.1634) is sometimes conflated with Jean Bogard (same person, French form) and with later Leuven printers of similar name.

Description

Appearance

As a surname/toponym, Bogard has no intrinsic appearance. For the fictional Bogards, appearance is extensively attested and version-dependent:

Terry Bogard (SNK): Attested since 1991. Signature look - red baseball cap with white star, white T-shirt or later brown/white varsity jacket, blue jeans, red/white sneakers, long blond ponytail, blue eyes, muscular build (~182cm, 70kg in official data). Early Fatal Fury art by Masami Ōbari shows sleeveless vest, fingerless gloves, and trucker cap. Later KOF XIV/XV redesigns add bomber jacket with 'Running Wild' patch, more detailed denim, and aged features. Andy Bogard: shorter blond hair, white shozoku-like sleeveless gi, red sash, fingerless gloves, more reserved posture. Jeff Bogard (their father/adoptive father): muscular older man with blond hair, beard, karate gi, depicted only in flashbacks and portraits; killed before playable appearance.

Historical bearers: Photographic record exists for Ben M. Bogard (bearded, early 20th-century clerical dress), Delia Bogard (1940s film stills, dancer costumes), Dick Bogard (baseball uniform, 1960s). Jan Bogard known only through printer's device, no portrait attested.

Symbols & Iconography

Fictional: Terry's cap star, his jacket back design (wolf motif), and his catchphrase 'Are you okay? Buster Wolf!' are iconic. His Power Wave ground projectile is visualized as a yellow energy wave; Burn Knuckle as a lunging flaming punch. The 'Hungry Wolf' (Garō) epithet is rendered with wolf kanji 狼. Andy associated with Kōga-ryū ninjutsu and shuriken, white gi and prayer beads. Jeff associated with Hakkyokuseiken scroll. Historical: Ben M. Bogard's iconography is Baptist polemical literature (sword of truth, pillars). Jan Bogard's printer's mark: Golden Bible (Bibliorum Aureorum) with motto. Toponymic: Bogard, Missouri has no official seal beyond Carroll County courthouse records; road signs and water tower lettering.

Behaviour & Personality

Fictional Bogards - Terry: Attested as easygoing, charismatic, orphan-raised street fighter with strong sense of justice, surrogate father to Rock Howard (Geese Howard's son) after Fatal Fury: Mark of the Wolves. Loner who travels America, loves fast food and basketball, dislikes octopus. Canonically refuses to hold grudges; spares Geese in multiple endings. Andy: stoic, disciplined, more traditionally Japanese-influenced despite being American, devoted to fiancée Mai Shiranui but emotionally reserved. Jeff: idealized martial arts master, benevolent father figure, practitioner of Hakkyokuseiken. Historical Ben M. Bogard: attested as combative debater, prolific writer, staunch Landmark Baptist, segregationist, anti-evolution and anti-Catholic polemicist (per secondary scholarship). Paul Bogard: contemporary nature writer and dark-sky advocate, reflective, activist tone. Seth Bogart/Bogard: queer punk/pop artist, camp and transgressive persona (Hunx and His Punx).

Domains & Habitats

Surname distribution: Highest density in United States (U.S. Census 2010: ~4,500 bearers, rank ~7,500), concentrated in Missouri, Texas, Arkansas, California; also in France (Nord-Pas-de-Calais) and Belgium. Toponymic habitat: Bogard, Missouri - small incorporated city (0.53 sq mi) in Bogard Township, Carroll County, north-central Missouri, agricultural prairie; population 167 (2020 census), down from 519 (1920). Bogard Creek flows through Henry and Johnson counties, tributary to Deepwater Creek. Fictional habitat: South Town (fictionalized American city based on Southtown, USA, analogous to New York/Houston), later Second South Town; Terry and Andy canonically orphans raised in South Town, trained in Hakkyokuseiken dojo; Terry later itinerant, then resides in South Town with Rock Howard.

Version Differences

Terry Bogard has three major design eras: (1) Fatal Fury 1-3 (1991-1995): young, sleeveless, cap, ponytail; (2) Real Bout / Garou: Mark of the Wolves (1995-1999): aged slightly, bomber jacket, jeans; (3) KOF XIV onward (2016-): aged 35+, more detailed, facial stubble, modern streetwear but retaining cap. KOF continuity is separate from Fatal Fury canon (KOF is dream match/non-canon). Super Smash Bros. Ultimate (2019) uses Fatal Fury 2 / KOF '98 moveset hybrid, with 100+ moves. Andy's design is more stable but his KOF moveset adds Cho Reppa Dan. Jeff Bogard never playable; only backstory, with conflicting accounts whether he is Terry/Andy's biological or adoptive father (early English manuals say adoptive; later Japanese sources say biological father who adopted them after their mother died - see contradictions). Historical Bogards show no version differences beyond spelling variants.

Powers & Abilities

Hakkyokuseiken (Eight Extremes Holy Fist) - Power Wave

Source VersionFatal Fury: King of Fighters (1991) - Terry and Andy movelist; canonically taught by Tung Fu Rue and Jeff Bogard

Terry and Jeff's signature chi projectile. Terry slams fist to ground, sending yellow energy wave along ground. Andy cannot use Power Wave (uses projectile Zanei Ken instead). Jeff was master of the style.

Limitations

Ground-traveling only in original version; can be jumped over; later variants include Power Geyser (upward eruption) and Buster Wolf. Requires contact with ground; ineffective airborne. Andy explicitly unable to perform it, showing style divergence.

Contradictions

Early English manual says Tung Fu Rue taught Terry and Andy after Jeff's death; Japanese Garou Densetsu mook says Jeff taught them fundamentals before death, Tung completed training. Both attested.

Burn Knuckle / Burning Knuckle

Source VersionFatal Fury (1991) through KOF XV (2022) and Smash Ultimate (2019)

Terry's rushing punch with extended arm engulfed in flame/energy, closing distance rapidly. Core special move across all appearances.

Limitations

Punishable on block; linear trajectory; Terry vulnerable during windup. In Smash, has armor on startup but not invincible.

Contradictions

None significant; consistently present.

Power Geyser / Power Dunk / Buster Wolf

Source VersionFatal Fury 2 (1992) introduced Power Geyser; Fatal Fury 3 introduced Power Dunk; Garou: Mark of the Wolves (1999) introduced Buster Wolf

Power Geyser: Terry punches ground causing massive vertical geyser of energy (Desperation Move). Power Dunk: rising knee into diving punch. Buster Wolf (Garou: Mark of the Wolves, 1999): Terry dashes, punches, shouts 'Are you okay? Buster Wolf!' - became his Smash Final Smash.

Limitations

Super moves require meter (Power gauge). Buster Wolf requires close range for second hit. In Smash, Buster Wolf is directional input special, not Final Smash (which is Triple Geyser).

Contradictions

Power Geyser originally Terry-only; later Rock Howard inherits it, showing hereditary/teaching transmission.

Kōga-ryū Ninjutsu and Shiranui-ryū influence (Andy Bogard)

Source VersionFatal Fury (1991) - Andy movelist; expanded in KOF series

Andy uses ninjutsu-derived techniques: Shoryu Dan (rising dragon bullet), Zanei Ken (vacuum hand slash), Kuhadan (air projectile), and shuriken. More speed-oriented, less raw power than Terry.

Limitations

Shorter range projectiles than Terry; requires precise spacing; physically less durable than Terry in lore (Terry wins most canonical brother vs brother bouts).

Contradictions

Andy is American but practices Japanese ninjutsu due to training in Japan under Hanzo Shiranui (Mai's grandfather) - cultural dissonance noted by critics.

Printing and publishing (Jan Bogard) - technical ability

Source VersionSTCV/USTC imprints 1607-1633

Operation of movable-type printing press, Latin and Dutch typesetting, woodcut device printing, university thesis and theological folio production for University of Leuven and Douai.

Limitations

Pre-industrial press, limited to ~1,000-1,500 sheets per day, dependent on paper supply and ecclesiastical censorship (Catholic Counter-Reformation context). No surviving press equipment; only books.

Contradictions

None; well documented via imprints.

Oratory, debate and denominational organization (Ben M. Bogard)

Source VersionBogard's own publications and ABA minutes; secondary biography by Dan Gilbert

Public debate (118+ formal debates attested), radio broadcasting (early 1930s, KTHS), authorship of 30+ books/pamphlets, founding of American Baptist Association (1924).

Limitations

Influence largely confined to Landmark Missionary Baptist movement; rejected by Southern Baptist Convention mainstream; radio reach limited to Arkansas/Texas. Debating style described as aggressive and polarizing.

Contradictions

Supporters claim 118 debates undefeated; critics note debates were judged by partisan moderators.

Dark-sky advocacy and nature writing (Paul Bogard)

Source VersionThe End of Night (2013), What If Night? (2020)

Authorial ability to synthesize science, memoir, and advocacy; led to International Dark-Sky Association advocacy, public speaking, university teaching (James Madison University).

Limitations

Not a scientific researcher; relies on interviewing astronomers and ecologists. Advocacy impact measurable but light pollution continues to increase.

Contradictions

None significant.

The Founding of Bogard, Missouri and Bogard Township

Source Carroll County Historical Society; History of Carroll County, Missouri (1881); U.S. Post Office records; GNISCulture American Midwest settlement historyPeriod 1880-1884 (town platted); township organized 1870s

Summary

Bogard Township was organized in Carroll County in the early 1870s, named for an early settler family (sources differ whether for John Bogard or Rev. Bogard). The city of Bogard was platted in 1884 along the Chicago, Burlington and Quincy Railroad extension, with a post office established 1884. It grew as an agricultural shipping point for corn, wheat and livestock. Population peaked at 519 in 1920, then declined steadily with railroad abandonment and farm consolidation to 167 in 2020. Bogard Creek, 20 miles south, was named for the same family or for a trapper named Bogard who camped there in the 1830s (per Missouri place-name scholar Robert L. Ramsay).

Participants

Bogard family settlers

Chicago, Burlington and Quincy Railroad

Carroll County commissioners

Consequences

Created persistent toponym that preserves surname in geography; Bogard School District and Bogard Baptist Church remain. Example of railroad-era prairie town boom-bust cycle.

Variants

Ramsay (1952) lists two conflicting etymologies for Bogard Creek: after a pioneer family vs. after a French trapper 'Bougard'. No primary diary confirms trapper story.

Ben M. Bogard's Landmark Baptist Crusade and the American Baptist Association

Source Bogard, Ben M. Pillars of Orthodoxy (1900); American Baptist Association minutes 1924; Dan Gilbert biography; secondary scholarship by James TullCulture American Southern religious historyPeriod 1890-1951 (peak 1905-1930)

Summary

Ben Marcus Bogard (1868-1951), born in Elizabethtown, Kentucky, became a Missionary Baptist preacher in Arkansas. Rejecting the Southern Baptist Convention's centralization, he championed Landmarkism - the belief in an unbroken succession of Baptist churches since John the Baptist. After the 1905 split, he led the formation of the American Baptist Association in Texarkana, Texas/Arkansas in 1924, serving as its most prominent polemicist. He claimed 118 public debates against Methodists, Campbellites, and others, edited The Baptist Commonwealth, and was an early radio preacher on KTHS Hot Springs. His writings defended strict congregational autonomy and opposed evolution, Catholicism, and later, desegregation.

Participants

Ben M. Bogard

J. Frank Norris (rival/fellow polemicist)

American Baptist Association

Southern Baptist Convention

Consequences

ABA grew to ~1,700 churches by 1950; Bogard's books remained in print in Landmark circles into the 21st century. His segregationist editorials in the 1940s-50s are now cited as example of religious justification for Jim Crow. His name remains on Bogard Press (Texarkana).

Variants

Supporters portray Bogard as defender of orthodoxy; mainstream Baptist historians portray him as schismatic. Number of debates (118) is self-reported and unverified.

Ben M. Bogard's segregationist and anti-Catholic polemic

Source Bogard, Ben M. editorials in The Orthodox Baptist Searchlight, 1940-1951; secondary analysis in Tull, James E. A Study of Southern Baptist Landmarkism (1960) and Leonard, Bill J. Baptist Ways (2003)

Description

Ben M. Bogard, the most historically prominent bearer of the surname, published editorials in the 1940s-1950s opposing racial desegregation and defending Jim Crow, arguing for segregation on biblical grounds. He also published sustained anti-Catholic and anti-evolution tracts (e.g., 'The Baptist Way-Book', debates with Catholics). His 118 debates often targeted other denominations in combative terms. These writings are still distributed by Bogard Press without contextual disclaimers.

Scholarly Context

Historians of American religion place Bogard within the fundamentalist-Landmark movement that provided theological cover for segregation. Modern American Baptist Association has distanced itself from some of Bogard's racial views but has not formally repudiated his writings; this remains a point of scholarly critique.

Erasure of women in Bogard narratives

Source Fatal Fury character roster analysis; Carroll County histories (1881) which list male landowners only

Description

Both fictional and historical Bogard narratives are male-dominated. Fatal Fury's Bogard saga centers on fathers, sons, and male rivals; female characters (Mai Shiranui, Blue Mary) exist primarily as love interests or support. Historical records for Bogard, Missouri and for Jan Bogard's workshop mention women only as widows continuing businesses (Jan's widow continued printing briefly after c.1634) or as unnamed pioneer wives, reflecting archival bias.

Scholarly Context

Feminist game studies critique Mai Shiranui's design as male-gaze object despite her canonical relationship to Andy Bogard; historians note lack of primary sources for women's roles in early Missouri settlement and early modern printing houses.

Contradictions & Disputes

Etymology of surname Bogard

Position A

Derived from Middle Dutch boomgaard / bogaert 'orchard' (occupational/topographic)

Position A — evidence

Hanks et al. Dictionary of American Family Names (2003, 2022), Dauzat Dictionnaire des noms de famille de France, and Dutch Familienamen database list Bogard/Bogaert under boomgaard root with early attestations in Flanders (Bogaert 1290) and France (Bogard 14th c.). Distribution matches Dutch/Flemish migration to US.

Position B

Derived from Old French bogard / Germanic personal name + -ard, or from English dialect boggart 'goblin'

Position B — evidence

Popular genealogy sites (Ancestry, House of Names) claim 'boggart' spirit origin or 'bogaert = bow-hard' without citation; no peer-reviewed onomastic work supports boggart derivation. Early English 'boggart' is northern dialect, not found in Low Countries where Bogard earliest records cluster.

Scholarly Consensus

Consensus supports orchard derivation (Position A). Boggart conflation is folk etymology. No attested medieval use of Bogard meaning goblin.

Jeff Bogard's relationship to Terry and Andy

Position A

Jeff is their biological father

Position A — evidence

Japanese Garou Densetsu mooks (1994), KOF 10th Anniversary Official Data (2004), and SNK's 2022 KOF XV profile state 'father Jeff Bogard' and mention their deceased mother; Fatal Fury 3 manual (JP) says Jeff raised them from birth.

Position B

Jeff is their adoptive father who took them in as orphans

Position B — evidence

English Fatal Fury: King of Fighters manual (1991) and US Neo Geo manual state 'adoptive sons Terry and Andy' and 'adoptive father Jeff Bogard, who took in the orphans'. Early US localization team may have added 'adoptive' to explain why brothers look different and have different fighting styles.

Scholarly Consensus

Japanese sources are considered primary canon; English 'adoptive' is localization divergence. Most fan wikis now note both with qualifier. SNK has not issued retcon statement, so both remain attested variants.

Whether Geese Howard died in Fatal Fury 1

Position A

Geese died falling from Geese Tower in Fatal Fury 1 (original intent)

Position A — evidence

Fatal Fury 1 ending shows Geese falling with no survival shown; anime adaptation treats fall as fatal; developers in 1991 interview said fall was meant to be fatal but popularity forced return.

Position B

Geese survived the fall and returned in Fatal Fury 2/Real Bout

Position B — evidence

Fatal Fury Special and Fatal Fury 2 explicitly feature living Geese as boss; Real Bout Fatal Fury (1997) finally kills him with a second fall where he refuses Terry's hand. SNK's official timeline lists Geese as survivor of first fall.

Scholarly Consensus

Retcon is accepted: Geese survived first fall (Position B) as official canon; Position A reflects original authorial intent but not final continuity. KOF timeline ignores both and keeps Geese alive indefinitely.

Origin of Bogard, Missouri name

Position A

Named for the Bogard family, early settlers

Position A — evidence

History of Carroll County, Missouri (1881) and GNIS attribute town to Bogard family settlers; 1870 census shows multiple Bogard households in Bogard Township.

Position B

Named for Bogard Creek, which was named for a French trapper named Bougard

Position B — evidence

Ramsay (1952) notes alternative tradition that creek was named for a trapper 'Bogard' who camped there c.1830s, and township/town then took creek's name. No primary trapper diary found; tradition is oral.

Scholarly Consensus

Both may be true: creek and town likely named for same pioneer family, with trapper story being folk elaboration. No definitive primary source resolves it; listed as unknown variant.

Terry Bogard's age and timeline

Position A

Terry is 24 in Fatal Fury 2, 35 in Mark of the Wolves (consistent aging)

Position A — evidence

Fatal Fury official timeline ages Terry year by year (Fatal Fury 1 age 18, Fatal Fury 2 age 24, Garou age 35). Art shows visible aging, stubble, and father-figure role.

Position B

Terry is ageless / reset in KOF, where he remains 24 forever

Position B — evidence

KOF series is dream match with no aging; Terry is listed as 24 in KOF '94 through KOF XV (2022) despite 28-year real-world gap. Smash Ultimate uses KOF design but calls him 'The Legendary Hungry Wolf' without age.

Scholarly Consensus

Two continuities: Fatal Fury/Garou timeline ages (Position A is canon there); KOF timeline is static (Position B). Fans and SNK acknowledge split continuity; neither is error.
