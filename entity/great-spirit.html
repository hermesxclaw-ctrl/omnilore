The Great Spirit is in all things: the air we breathe, the sun that warms us, the earth beneath our feet. We are not separate from the sacred — we are the sacred, walking.

Who It Is

The Great Spirit is the supreme divine reality in many Native American religious traditions — most explicitly formulated among the Lakota (as Wakan Tanka) and the Ojibwe/Anishinaabe (as Gitche Manitou), but conceptually present across hundreds of Indigenous nations. The term 'Great Spirit' is a colonial-era translation that, while simplified, captures the essential concept: a transcendent, all-pervading creative power that manifests through the natural world, human consciousness, and the sacred order of existence. Unlike the anthropomorphic gods of many Western traditions, the Great Spirit is often understood as a mystery (Wakan) that permeates all things rather than a personified deity.

In Lakota theology, Wakan Tanka ('Great Mystery' or 'Great Sacred') is not a single being but a complex unity of sixteen aspects — the four directions, the above and below, and multiple manifestations of divine power. The Lakota pray to Wakan Tanka through these manifestations: Skan (the Sky, the source of all motion and energy), Wi (the Sun), Maka (the Earth), Inyan (the Rock, the primordial ancestor), and others. Each aspect is both a distinct spiritual power and part of the unified Wakan Tanka. This theology represents a sophisticated synthesis of monotheism and polytheism — unity expressed through multiplicity.

In Ojibwe tradition, Gitche Manitou ('Great Spirit') is the creator and sustainer of all life, associated with the sky, light, and the positive forces of the universe. Gitche Manitou is opposed by a lesser, malevolent spirit (sometimes called Matchi Manitou), establishing a dualistic framework. The Midewiwin (Grand Medicine Society) teaches that Gitche Manitou bestowed the sacred rituals and healing knowledge that sustain Anishinaabe spiritual life. Yet Gitche Manitou is not typically approached directly in daily prayer — intermediary spirits (manitous) and the sacred pipe serve as conduits for communication.

The Great Spirit concept has profoundly shaped Indigenous-environmentalist philosophy and intertribal identity. Figures like Black Elk (Lakota holy man), whose teachings were recorded in 'Black Elk Speaks,' articulated a vision of Wakan Tanka as the unifying force of all creation — expressed in the famous prayer: 'Grandfather, Great Spirit, you have set the powers of the four quarters of the earth to cross each other. You have made me cross the good road and the road of difficulties, and where they cross, the place is holy.' This vision of sacred interconnection has influenced global environmental and spiritual movements.

It is important to note that the 'Great Spirit' was sometimes misrepresented by Christian missionaries as equivalent to the Christian God, leading to oversimplification. However, Indigenous theologians and spiritual leaders have reclaimed the concept, articulating it in ways that honor both the specificity of tribal traditions and the universal human search for connection to the sacred.

Form

The Great Spirit is generally considered to be formless and omnipresent, without a physical or anthropomorphic appearance. It is not depicted in traditional iconography. However, its presence is felt and symbolized through natural phenomena: the sun, the sky, the wind, the earth, and the boundless heavens. In some traditions, particularly in the Lakota concept of Wakan Tanka, it is a collective of sacred beings, each with its own symbolic form (e.g., Inyan as a rock, Wi as the sun).

Powers

Creator of the Universe In many traditions, the Great Spirit is the creator of the world, the heavens, the earth, and all living beings. Pan-Indigenous, with variations The act of creation is often described in partnership with other beings or forces, such as the Trickster (e.g., Nanabozho in Ojibwe tradition) or the Earth Diver (a common motif where a small animal brings up mud to create land). Some traditions see creation as a single act, while others see it as an ongoing process. Some see the Great Spirit as the sole creator, while others see it as a first among equals.

Source of All Life and Power The Great Spirit is the ultimate source of 'wakan' or 'manitou'—the sacred, mysterious power that animates all things. Individuals, objects, and places can be 'wakan' because they are infused with this power. Lakota (Wakan Tanka), Algonquian (Manitou) This power is not a 'superpower' to be wielded; it is a spiritual essence to be respected. Access to it is achieved through ritual purity, prayer, and the guidance of spiritual leaders. The nature of this power is complex. In Lakota thought, Wakan Tanka is a pantheon of sacred beings, not a single entity. This has led to debate over whether it is truly monotheistic or polytheistic.

Giver of Laws and Ceremonies The Great Spirit is believed to have given the people their sacred laws, rituals, and ceremonies, such as the Sun Dance, the vision quest, and the use of the sacred pipe. Lakota, as recounted by Black Elk These gifts are to be preserved and practiced with utmost sincerity. They are not to be profaned or performed without proper knowledge and preparation. Different bands and tribes have different ceremonies and laws, and the details of their origin stories vary.

Behavior & Habitat

The Great Spirit is not typically described as a personal, intervening deity in the way of the Greek or Norse gods. It is more often seen as the source of all life, the sustainer of the cosmic order, and the giver of laws and ceremonies. It is a presence to be communed with through prayer, vision quests, and ceremony, rather than a being with a distinct personality or emotional life. It is the ultimate reality, the 'Great Mystery' that is both immanent in all things and transcendent.

Accounts

Black Elk's account describes a complex creation process. In the beginning, there was Inyan (the Rock), who was the first being. He created Maka (the Earth), but in doing so, he was drained of his power and became hard and dry. To sustain Maka, other wakan beings were created: Skan (the Sky), Wi (the Sun), and Hanwi (the Moon). Together, these beings form the Great Spirit (Wakan Tanka). The story emphasizes the interconnectedness of all things and the sacred nature of the world.

In the Ojibwe tradition, the Great Spirit (Gitche Manitou) created the world. However, a great flood was sent to purify the earth (often due to the misdeeds of humans or the actions of the underwater panther, Mishipeshu). The culture hero Nanabozho survived the flood on a raft, and with the help of animals like the otter, beaver, and muskrat, he dove to the bottom of the waters to retrieve mud. From this mud, the earth was recreated. The muskrat succeeded, but died in the effort, giving its life for the new world.

Scholarly Debates
Monotheism vs. Polytheism
The Great Spirit is a single, supreme, all-powerful creator god, equivalent to the Christian God. ⇌ The Great Spirit is a collective of sacred beings or an impersonal, all-pervading mystery, not a single deity.
Personal vs. Impersonal
The Great Spirit is a personal being who can be prayed to and who intervenes in human affairs. ⇌ The Great Spirit is an impersonal force or principle, not a being with a personality or will.
The Great Spirit and the Christian God
The Great Spirit is the same being as the Christian God, just revealed to Native Americans in a different way. ⇌ The Great Spirit is a distinct concept that is fundamentally different from the Christian God.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of the 'Great Spirit' not as a single, monolithic god, but as a vast, interconnected ecosystem of the sacred. It's like the internet—you can't point to one server and say 'that's the internet.' It's the network, the data, the connections, the power, all working together. Wakan Tanka is the whole network, and the various wakan beings are like the powerful websites and servers that make it work. The Great Spirit isn't a king on a throne; it's the very fabric of reality, the 'Great Mystery' that you can feel in a thunderstorm, see in the sun, and touch in the earth.
