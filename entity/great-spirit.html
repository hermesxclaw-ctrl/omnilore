Who It Is

Imagine a god that isn't a man in the sky, but a rock, a sky, a sun, and a moon, all bound together in a sacred, breathing web of power. That's the 'Great Spirit'—a concept so vast and varied that it shatters the single word we use for it. It's the 'Wakan Tanka' of the Lakota, a pantheon of sixteen sacred beings, and the 'Gitchi Manitou' of the Ojibwe, a great mystery that pervades all things. It's a force you don't worship so much as commune with, through sacred pipes, vision quests, and the whisper of the wind. And yet, the version you've seen in movies and cartoons is often a colonial ghost, a Christian god in a feather headdress. The real story is far stranger, deeper, and more alive.

The Great Spirit is not typically described as a personal, intervening deity in the way of the Greek or Norse gods. It is more often seen as the source of all life, the sustainer of the cosmic order, and the giver of laws and ceremonies. It is a presence to be communed with through prayer, vision quests, and ceremony, rather than a being with a distinct personality or emotional life. It is the ultimate reality, the 'Great Mystery' that is both immanent in all things and transcendent.

Powers
Creator of the Universe

In many traditions, the Great Spirit is the creator of the world, the heavens, the earth, and all living beings.

Limit: The act of creation is often described in partnership with other beings or forces, such as the Trickster (e.g., Nanabozho in Ojibwe tradition) or the Earth Diver (a common motif where a small animal brings up mud to create land).

Source of All Life and Power

The Great Spirit is the ultimate source of 'wakan' or 'manitou'—the sacred, mysterious power that animates all things. Individuals, objects, and places can be 'wakan' because they are infused with this power.

Limit: This power is not a 'superpower' to be wielded; it is a spiritual essence to be respected. Access to it is achieved through ritual purity, prayer, and the guidance of spiritual leaders.

Giver of Laws and Ceremonies

The Great Spirit is believed to have given the people their sacred laws, rituals, and ceremonies, such as the Sun Dance, the vision quest, and the use of the sacred pipe.

Limit: These gifts are to be preserved and practiced with utmost sincerity. They are not to be profaned or performed without proper knowledge and preparation.

Form & Signs

However, its presence is felt and symbolized through natural phenomena: the sun, the sky, the wind, the earth, and the boundless heavens. Accounts I
The Lakota Creation Story (as told by Black Elk)Black Elk Speaks by John Neihardt (1932) and The Sacred Pipe by Joseph Epes Brown (1953)

Pre-contact, recorded in the 20th century

Black Elk's account describes a complex creation process. In the beginning, there was Inyan (the Rock), who was the first being. He created Maka (the Earth), but in doing so, he was drained of his power and became hard and dry. To sustain Maka, other wakan beings were created: Skan (the Sky), Wi (the Sun), and Hanwi (the Moon). Together, these beings form the Great Spirit (Wakan Tanka). The story emphasizes the interconnectedness of all things and the sacred nature of the world. This story establishes the sacred order of the cosmos and the foundational relationships between all beings. It explains why the earth is sacred and why the sun, sky, and moon are venerated.

Accounts II
The Ojibwe Creation Story and the Great FloodOjibwe oral tradition, as recorded by various ethnographers including William Warren

Pre-contact, recorded in the 19th century

In the Ojibwe tradition, the Great Spirit (Gitche Manitou) created the world. However, a great flood was sent to purify the earth (often due to the misdeeds of humans or the actions of the underwater panther, Mishipeshu). The culture hero Nanabozho survived the flood on a raft, and with the help of animals like the otter, beaver, and muskrat, he dove to the bottom of the waters to retrieve mud. From this mud, the earth was recreated. The muskrat succeeded, but died in the effort, giving its life for the new world. This story explains the origin of the land, the importance of sacrifice, and the relationship between the Great Spirit, the trickster/hero, and the animal world.

The Great Spirit is a single, all-powerful god worshipped by all Native Americans. — There is no single 'Native American religion.' The Great Spirit is a concept with distinct meanings in different tribes. Many tribes have no concept of a 'Great Spirit' at all, and instead focus on a complex web of spirits, ancestors, and natural forces.

The Great Spirit is the same as the Christian God. — While some individuals see a connection, the concepts are not identical. The Great Spirit is often impersonal, immanent, and part of a larger spiritual ecosystem, whereas the Christian God is typically personal, transcendent, and a singular being.

The term 'Great Spirit' is an authentic, universal Indigenous term. — It is a translation of various distinct terms (Wakan Tanka, Gitchi Manitou, etc.) that have different meanings in different languages. The English term is a simplification that erases this diversity.
