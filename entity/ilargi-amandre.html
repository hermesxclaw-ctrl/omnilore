Who It Is

Ilargi Amandre ('Grandmother Moon' in Basque) is the serene lunar counterpart to Eguzki Amandre. She is a silver-haired grandmother who governs the night, guides travelers, and protects those who sleep. Her phases were meticulously observed by Basque farmers for planting cycles, fishing, and divination. A new moon (ilberri, 'new moon') was a time of renewal, while the full moon (ilhargi bete) was associated with heightened supernatural activity — the time when Mari's storm dragons flew and lamiak (water spirits) danced on riverbanks. Ilargi Amandre was also a patron of women, associated with menstrual cycles, childbirth, and midwifery. Folk prayers addressed to her survive from as late as the 19th century. She was considered the softer, more merciful counterpart to Eguzki Amandre's stern justice — both grandmothers together forming a complete cycle of day and night, life and rest.

The Approach

In the moonlit valleys of the Basque Country, where ancient oaks whisper secrets to the wind, there exists a goddess whose name means 'Mother Moon' — Ilargi Amandre. She is not a distant celestial queen, but a nurturing presence woven into the very fabric of Basque folklore, guiding both the tides of the sea and the hearts of her people.

Content advisory. None. The material is generally benign, though some sources may reflect pre-Christian fertility and lunar symbolism.

Etymology

In Basque, 'ilargi' means 'moon' (from 'hil' (month) + 'argi' (light), literally 'light of the month'), and 'amandre' means 'mother' or 'grandmother', thus 'Mother Moon'.

Cultural Ownership

Basque people, primarily in the Basque Country (Euskal Herria) spanning northern Spain and southwestern France.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars and popular sources link Ilargi Amandre with the Roman goddess Diana or the Greek Artemis, but this is speculative and not supported by direct Basque tradition. Also, she is sometimes confused with other Basque lunar figures such as the moon itself (Ilargi) personified in different contexts, but Amandre specifically emphasizes the maternal aspect.

Description

Appearance

No specific physical description is recorded. She is generally imagined as a beautiful woman with a pale, glowing face, often depicted in modern art as a motherly figure with a crescent moon on her brow. In traditional folklore, she is not anthropomorphized in detail; the moon itself is the object of veneration.

Symbols & Iconography

The crescent moon, the full moon, the night sky, the sea (due to tidal influence), and the cycle of the month. She is also associated with the color silver and with water.

Behaviour & Personality

As a maternal figure, she is benevolent and protective, especially of women, children, and the home. She is seen as a calm, guiding presence that watches over the night. She is also associated with fertility and the natural cycles of growth and harvest.

Domains & Habitats

The moon, the night sky, and by extension the sea and the earth's waters. She is also present in rural landscapes, particularly in places where lunar rituals were performed, such as hills and fields.

Version Differences

There is little variation in the tradition; however, some sources emphasize her role in controlling the tides, while others focus on her protective aspects. In some modern interpretations, she is merged with the broader concept of the Great Goddess, but this is not traditional.

Stories & Myths

The Moon and the Sun

Source Oral tradition; recorded by BarandiaranCulture BasquePeriod Pre-Christian to modern times

Summary

Ilargi Amandre is said to be the companion of Eguzki Amandre (Mother Sun). They take turns crossing the sky, one by day and the other by night. They are both seen as maternal figures who bring light and life to the world.

Participants

Ilargi Amandre

Eguzki Amandre

Consequences

This story explains the daily cycle of day and night, and reinforces the importance of both celestial bodies in Basque cosmology.

Variants

Some versions say they are sisters, others say they are lovers or spouses.

The Moon's Blessing on the Fields

Source Agricultural customs; recorded in Caro BarojaCulture BasquePeriod Historical agricultural practice

Summary

Farmers would plant crops according to the phases of the moon, believing that Ilargi Amandre's light influenced the growth of seeds. They would also pray to her for a good harvest.

Participants

Ilargi Amandre

Basque farmers

Consequences

This practice reflects the deep connection between the lunar cycle and agricultural life, and shows the goddess's role in sustaining the community.

Variants

Different regions had specific rituals, such as leaving offerings of bread or milk under the moonlight.

Mari

Type associated deitySource Various

Description

Mari is the supreme goddess of Basque mythology, associated with weather and the earth. Ilargi Amandre is sometimes considered a manifestation or servant of Mari, but this is not consistent.

Version Note

Some modern authors link them, but traditional sources keep them separate.

Conflicting Accounts

There is debate among scholars about the relationship between Mari and lunar deities.

Lamiak

Type associated beingsSource Various

Description

Lamiak are nymph-like creatures in Basque folklore. They are sometimes associated with water and the moon, and may be seen as servants or companions of Ilargi Amandre.

Version Note

This is a loose association; not all traditions connect them.

Conflicting Accounts

Some sources claim Lamiak are independent of any deity.

Contradictions & Disputes

Relationship with Mari

Position A

Ilargi Amandre is an independent deity.

Position A — evidence

Traditional Basque folklore treats them as separate entities; no primary source equates them.

Position B

Ilargi Amandre is a manifestation of Mari.

Position B — evidence

Some modern scholars see Mari as a supreme goddess who absorbs other deities, but this is not supported by early documentation.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars treat them as distinct, with Mari being a more complex and powerful figure.

Gender of the Moon

Position A

The moon is female (Ilargi Amandre).

Position A — evidence

Most Basque traditions and the name 'Amandre' indicate a female personification.

Position B

The moon is male in some Basque dialects.

Position B — evidence

In some Basque dialects, the word 'ilargi' is treated as masculine, but this is linguistic, not mythological.

Scholarly Consensus

The mythological figure is consistently female.

Research Scope

This dossier draws on available English and Spanish language sources, including general reference works on Basque mythology and folklore. Direct primary sources in Basque were not accessible to the researcher; therefore, all information is based on secondary accounts. The scarcity of academic literature specifically on Ilargi Amandre means that much of the information is general and may be conflated with broader lunar mythology in the Basque region.
