Who It Is

In the heart of modern Osaka, behind office towers and traffic, five nameless-seeming gods still guard the idea of a house itself — not the family inside, not the wealth or luck, but the posts, the ropes, the thatch and the ground where people dare to dwell. Together they are Ikasuri no Kami, the deity who is really five deities, whose shrine once protected the palace of Naniwa when Osaka was the capital of Japan.

Behavior is protective, stabilizing, and passive rather than active-mythic. Norito prayers petition Ikasuri no Kami to 'pacify the dwelling, prevent calamity of house and palace, keep posts firm and ropes unbroken.' No myths show the deity speaking, fighting, tricking, or traveling, unlike Susanoo or Okuninushi. Personality, where inferred from ritual language, is that of a guardian of boundaries and residential purity (kiyome), requiring correct ritual siting (jichinsai) before construction. The deity is benevolent when properly enshrined, but neglect is understood to cause yashi

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Japanese 坐摩神, read Ikasuri no Kami (historical kana いかすり). Etymology disputed. Most widely cited derivation is from verb ikasuri / i-kasuri related to i (居) 'to dwell, to sit, to be enshrined' + kasu / kasuri meaning 'to settle, to reside,' thus 'the deity who causes dwelling/residence.' Powers
Yashiki Shugo (Residence Protection)

Core power: pacification and protection of the dwelling site - keeping the house, palace, or building stable, preventing fire, collapse, and spiritual pollution of the site. Invoked before construction, at moving, and at New Year to secure anzen (safety) of the household.

Limit: Limited to the physical site and its ritual purity; does not grant personal luck, wealth, or martial victory beyond stability of residence. Requires correct enshrinement and periodic worship; power wanes if site is polluted or rituals neglected (standard Shinto jingi logic).

Jichin / Chinkon (Earth-Pacification)

Ability to calm the earth-spirit (jishin) and wild land before building. Asuha and Hahaki pillars specifically govern foot-place and earth-breaking, allowing safe foundation work. Priests perform jichinsai invoking Ikasuri to transform wild land (arachi) into inhabited land (yashiki).

Limit: Ritual-dependent; not an automatic power. Must be petitioned with offerings and norito. Does not control earthquakes broadly - only ritual pacification of the building plot.

Miya Mamori (Palace/Capital Protection)

Extended function as guardian of the Naniwa capital and imperial residence. Protects the political center from calamity, analogous to Miyagami or Kunitama. This elevated Ikasuri from household to state deity in Ritsuryo system.

Limit: State function lapsed when capital moved from Naniwa (after 744 CE); retained only as shrine rank and ritual memory. No mythic feat of repelling invasion attested, unlike Sumiyoshi or Hachiman.

Collective Fivefold Manifestation (Gochushin Bunrei)

Power to act as five distinct yet inseparable pillars, allowing bunrei (spirit division) to branch shrines while retaining unity. Each pillar can be worshipped individually for specific aspect (well, rope, ground) but ritual efficacy requires all five.

Limit: Inseparability: shrine tradition forbids enshrining fewer than five; individual pillars have no independent myth or iconography. This limits narrative development compared to other collective deities (e.g., Munakata Sanjoshin who have individual myths).

Form & Signs

Appearance — No canonical anthropomorphic iconography is attested in primary sources. Ikasuri no Kami is aniconic in classical Shinto: enshrined as shintai (often mirror, or in older practice, the dwelling site itself). Early Engishiki norito addresses the deity without physical description. Edo-period shrine art (ema, byobu) depicts Ikasuri Jinja as a palace-like shrine compound, not the deity's body. The five pillars are not individually visualized in pre-m

Symbols — Symbols are architectural and locative, not personal attributes: i (well/spring) motif in three pillar names indicates water source for habitation; tsuna (rope) indicates binding/measuring of building site; haha (earth-breaking) and asuha (foot-place) indicate foundation and ground. Shrine crest (shinmon) uses elements shared with Naniwa palace tradition. Offerings prescribed in Engishiki: cloth, rice, sake, and later, models of dwelling material

Domains — Domain is strictly the built human dwelling: yashiki (residence), miya (palace), and by extension the capital's residential quarter. Classical domain: the Imperial Palace at Naniwa (Naniwa-no-Miya) and later any dwelling that enshrines the deity via bunrei (divided spirit). Not a domain of sea, mountain, agriculture, or war. Habitat is the site (tokoro) itself - the ground, well, and foundation - rather than a celestial realm (Takamagahara)

Accounts I
The Five Pillars Enshrined at Naniwa PalaceIkasuri Jinja Engi (shrine foundation legend) as summarized in Kokugakuin Encyclopedia of Shinto and Osaka-fu Shi; echoed in Engishiki Norito

Legend set in Age of Gods / Jimmu era; text codified Heian (9th-10th c.), extant Edo copies

When Emperor Jimmu (or in variant, Emperor Nintoku) established the palace at Naniwa, the wild land was unsettled and the palace posts would not stand. The court divined and was told to enshrine the five deities of dwelling - Ikui, Sakui, Tsunagai, Hahaki, Asuha - who govern well, rope, and earth. Upon enshrinement at the site later known as Ikasuri Jinja, the palace was pacified and the capital prospered. The rite became the model for all future palace and residence foundations, with Ikasuri no Kami as the prototype yashikigami. Established Ikasuri Jinja as the ritual guardian of Naniwa Palace and, by extension, of all dwellings in the Ritsuryo state. Provided precedent for jichinsai rites. Anchored Osaka's claim as ritual capital.

Empress Jingu's Return and the Naniwa EnshrinementIkasuri Jinja Engi (Jingu variant) and Nihon Shoki Book 9 (Jingu Kogo)

Legendary 3rd-4th c. (Jingu era); recorded 720 CE (Nihon Shoki) and Heian engi

After her expedition to Silla (Korea), Empress Jingu landed at Naniwa and found the port unsettled. To secure the new harbor-capital, she enshrined the deities of residence (Ikasuri) alongside Sumiyoshi (sea) and Ikukunitama (land). The five Ikasuri pillars were placed to guard the palace wells and foundations, ensuring the safety of ships, warehouses, and homes in the growing port city. Linked Ikasuri to Osaka's maritime and commercial identity, pairing residence protection with seafaring. Explains why Ikasuri Jinja and Ikukunitama Jinja are considered the two tutelaries of Naniwa/Osaka, often worshipped together.

The Engishiki Codification: Five Seats, One ShrineEngishiki Jinmyocho (927 CE) and Engishiki Norito

Heian period, Engi era (905-927 CE)

The Engishiki, the great compendium of court ritual, lists Ikasuri Jinja as holding five seats (goza) and prescribes official offerings (heihaku) and a dedicated norito (prayer) for Ikasuri no Kami no Matsuri. The norito addresses the five pillars collectively, asking them to protect the palace dwelling and to keep the imperial residence pure. This transforms a local Naniwa cult into a state-recognized Shikinai Taisha, one of the highest-ranked shrines in Settsu Province. Formalized the collective five-pillar theology and ensured state funding and rank. Provided the most secure historical attestation of Ikasuri. The Engishiki list is still the basis for modern shrine status claims.

Accounts II
The Separation and Reunion of Ikasuri and IkukunitamaMedieval shrine documents (Muromachi) and Edo-period Osaka meisho ki; summarized in Kokugakuin Encyclopedia

Medieval to early modern (14th-18th c.), describing earlier events

Originally, tradition holds, Ikasuri and Ikukunitama were enshrined together at a single site in Naniwa. Due to war, fire, or urban expansion (accounts differ - some cite the 1496 Onin War disturbances, others Toyotomi Hideyoshi's Osaka Castle construction in 1583), the shrines were separated, with Ikasuri moving to its current Kyutaro-machi site and Ikukunitama to its hill site. In the Watanabe area, a joint enshrinement (Watanabe Ikasuri) preserved the memory of unity. Pilgrims continued to visit both as 'Naniwa no Ryosha' (two shrines of Naniwa). Created the paired tutelary system of Osaka that persists today. Explains why Ikasuri Jinja's precinct contains auxiliary shrines to Ikukunitama and vice versa. Illustrates how urban development reshaped sacred geography.

The Meiji Restoration and Kanpei Taisha ElevationMeiji government Jinja regulations (1871) and Ikasuri Jinja records

Meiji era (1871-1945)

With the Meiji Restoration's separation of Shinto and Buddhism (shinbutsu bunri) and creation of the modern shrine ranking system, Ikasuri Jinja, by virtue of its Engishiki Shikinai Taisha status and palace-protection legend, was elevated to Kanpei Taisha (Imperial Grand Shrine, highest rank). State rituals emphasized Ikasuri as guardian of the imperial palace (now in Tokyo) and of the nation as a dwelling (kuni no yashiki). The shrine's Buddhist elements (if any) were removed, and its norito was standardized to the Engishiki text. Secured national funding and prestige until 1946. Cemented the narrative of Ikasuri as state-protecting deity, downplaying folk yashikigami aspects. After WWII, rank abolished but prestige remains as former Kanpei Taisha and Beppyo Jinja.

The Postwar Urban Guardian: Surviving the Osaka Air RaidsIkasuri Jinja postwar history pamphlet and Osaka City war damage records

Showa era, 1945-present

During the Osaka Air Raids of March-June 1945, much of central Osaka, including the area around Ikasuri Jinja, was destroyed by firebombing. Shrine tradition holds that the main hall was damaged but the shintai and five-pillar spirit were preserved, and the shrine became a focal point for prayers for reconstruction and for the safety of new concrete apartments and office buildings. The shrine's jichinsai and joto-sai rites expanded to serve Osaka's postwar building boom, reinterpreting 'residence protection' for high-rises and commercial structures. Reaffirmed Ikasuri's relevance beyond ancient palace to modern urban life. Shrine now markets itself as guardian of both traditional homes and modern buildings, with English pamphlets for construction companies. Illustrates continuity of locative deity in megacity context.

Ikasuri no Kami is the god of wells or water (mizu no kami). — Only three of five pillars contain 'i' (well) in their names, and the collective function is residence/site protection, not water per se. Water is one element of a habitable site, not the domain. Conflating Ikasuri with Suijin (water deity) or Munakata (sea) is inaccurate.

Ikasuri Jinja and Ikukunitama Jinja enshrine the same deity, or Ikasuri is just another name for Ikukunitama. — They are distinct deities with distinct functions (Ikasuri = dwelling/site, Ikukunitama = land's life-force) and distinct shrines, though historically paired as Naniwa's two tutelaries and sometimes co-enshrined. Engishiki lists them separately as two Shikinai Taisha in Settsu.

Ikasuri no Kami appears as a character in anime/games like other Shinto gods (e.g., as a human-like warrior). — No canonical anthropomorphic form, mythic personality, or narrative exists for Ikasuri, and as of 2026 there are zero major anime, manga, or game adaptations. Any human-like depiction is modern invention without source. The deity remains aniconic and functional.

The shrine name is pronounced 'Zama Jinja' and the deity is 'Zama no Kami,' not Ikasuri. — Both readings are historically attested: on-reading Zama (from 坐摩) and kun-reading Ikasuri. Modern shrine officially uses Ikasuri Jinja (いかすりじんじゃ) and Ikasuri no Kami, while historical documents often write Zama. They refer to same entity; neither is wrong, but Ikasuri is the ritual name used in Engishiki kundoku.
