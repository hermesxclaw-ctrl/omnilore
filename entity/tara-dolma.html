Who It Is

Tara (Tibetan: Dölma, meaning 'She Who Liberates') is the most beloved female deity in Tibetan Buddhism, a fully enlightened Buddha who manifests in the form of a female bodhisattva of compassion and protection. According to the most famous origin story, Tara was born from a tear of Avalokiteshvara, the Bodhisattva of Compassion. When she attained enlightenment, certain monks suggested she should be reborn as a male to reach full Buddhahood. She replied: 'There is no man and no woman — I will remain in female form until all beings reach liberation.' She is the 'Swift Liberator' — the deity who answers prayers faster than any other. Tara manifests in 21 primary forms, the most important being White Tara (Sitatara), who symbolizes purity, long life, and healing, and Green Tara (Khadiravani Tara / Shyam Tara), who symbolizes enlightened activity, protection, and swift assistance. The practice of Tara is widespread across all schools of Tibetan Buddhism. Her mantra, 'Om Tare Tuttare Ture Svaha,' is one of the most commonly recited in the Tibetan Buddhist world. Tara is particularly invoked for protection from the eight great fears (lions, elephants, fire, snakes, thieves, drowning, imprisonment, and demons). In Tibetan iconography, Green Tara sits with one leg extended, ready to rise quickly to help beings, while White Tara sits in meditation posture. Tara is often called 'the Mother of the Buddhas' for her role in nurturing the enlightened qualities in all beings.

The Approach

In the vast pantheon of Tibetan Buddhism, few figures embody compassion in action as vividly as Tara, the swift and luminous savioress who springs from a tear of Avalokiteshvara. Known in Tibetan as Dölma, she is the one who hears the cries of the world and responds with instantaneous grace, her green form poised in readiness to aid, her red form blazing with fierce protection. She is not a distant deity but an intimate presence, a mother whose watchfulness spans the cosmos, and whose twenty-one forms offer a spectrum of enlightened activity.

Etymology

The Sanskrit name Tārā derives from the root √tṝ, meaning 'to cross' or 'to traverse,' thus 'she who carries across' or 'she who saves.' The Tibetan name Dölma (sgrol ma) similarly means 'the Liberator' or 'the one who delivers.'

Cultural Ownership

Tara is a pan-Buddhist deity, but she holds a special place in Tibetan Buddhism, where she is considered the female embodiment of enlightened activity and the consort of Avalokiteshvara. She is also venerated in Nepal, Mongolia, and other Himalayan regions, and has gained popularity in the West.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars have speculated on connections between Tara and pre-Buddhist goddesses, but there is no clear evidence of direct lineage. Within Buddhism, Tara is sometimes identified with the goddess of the same name in Hinduism, but they are distinct figures with different roles and mythologies. The identification of Tara with the Tibetan protectress Palden Lhamo is not standard; they are separate deities.

Description

Appearance

Tara is typically depicted as a beautiful young woman, often green in color (Green Tara), symbolizing youthful vitality and enlightened activity. She sits in a posture of ease (lalitasana), with one leg extended, ready to rise and help. Her right hand is in the gesture of giving (varada mudra), and her left hand holds a blue lotus (utpala). She wears the ornaments of a bodhisattva, including a crown, earrings, necklaces, and a silk skirt. In her red form (Red Tara), she is often depicted as more wrathful, with a blazing halo, and may hold a vajra and a red lotus.

Symbols & Iconography

The green color symbolizes the air element and the activity of compassion. The blue lotus represents purity and the power of transformation. The gesture of giving indicates her generosity. She is often shown with a small image of Amitabha in her crown, linking her to the lotus family. The twenty-one forms of Tara have distinct colors, postures, and attributes, each representing a different aspect of her protective and liberating power.

Behaviour & Personality

Tara is known for her swiftness and efficiency in responding to prayers. She is said to be 'the mother of all Buddhas' because she embodies the wisdom that gives birth to enlightenment. She is compassionate, fearless, and actively engaged in the world, unlike some meditative deities who are seen as withdrawn. Her personality is one of nurturing care combined with fierce protection when needed.

Domains & Habitats

Tara is not confined to a specific realm; she is present in the pure land of Potala, the abode of Avalokiteshvara, and is accessible to practitioners anywhere. She is particularly associated with places of pilgrimage in Tibet, Nepal, and India, such as the Kathmandu Valley and the Jokhang in Lhasa. In ritual, she is invoked in times of danger, illness, and spiritual obstacles.

Version Differences

The most common forms are Green Tara and White Tara. Green Tara is the active, compassionate savioress, while White Tara is associated with longevity, health, and serenity. There are also Red Tara (associated with magnetizing and subjugating), Black Tara (fierce protection), and Yellow Tara (wealth and prosperity). The Twenty-One Taras are a canonical set, each with a specific mantra and function. In different traditions, the iconography may vary, and some forms are more prominent in certain lineages.

Powers & Abilities

Swift Liberation from Fear

Source Version Classical Buddhist texts and oral tradition

Description

Tara is said to protect from the eight great fears: lions, elephants, fire, snakes, bandits, water, demons, and imprisonment. She responds instantly to the recitation of her mantra.

Limitations

Her protection is said to be effective for those with faith and devotion; it is not a guarantee of physical safety but a spiritual aid.

Contradictions

Some modern interpretations see the fears as psychological, while traditional texts take them literally.

Granting of Blessings and Sidhis

Source Version Tara Tantra and related ritual texts

Description

Tara bestows worldly benefits (prosperity, health, success) and spiritual attainments (progress on the path to enlightenment).

Limitations

The efficacy is said to depend on the practitioner's merit and motivation.

Contradictions

None noted.

Subjugation of Obstacles and Demons

Source Version Vajrayana ritual traditions

Description

In her wrathful forms, Tara can subdue negative forces, including demons and hindrances to practice.

Limitations

This power is often ritualized and requires specific initiations.

Contradictions

Some see this as metaphorical, others as literal.

Stories & Myths

The Origin from Avalokiteshvara's Tear

Source Tibetan Buddhist legendCulture TibetanPeriod Unknown; likely medieval

Summary

According to a popular legend, Avalokiteshvara, the bodhisattva of compassion, looked upon the suffering of beings and wept. From his tears, a lotus arose, and from that lotus, Tara was born. She vowed to remain in samsara until all beings were freed, and to assist Avalokiteshvara in his mission.

Participants

Avalokiteshvara

Tara

Consequences

Tara became the female counterpart to Avalokiteshvara, sharing his compassionate mission.

Variants

Some versions say she was born from his gaze or from a ray of light. Another story says she was a princess who vowed to be reborn as a woman to help beings, and then became enlightened.

Tara as the Princess Yeshe Dawa

Source Tārā TantraCulture Indian/TibetanPeriod Unknown

Summary

In this narrative, Tara was once a princess named Yeshe Dawa (Moon of Wisdom) who, through countless eons of practice, realized emptiness. She then vowed to always appear as a female bodhisattva to help beings, and eventually became a Buddha.

Participants

Princess Yeshe Dawa

Monks who advised her to pray for a male rebirth

Consequences

Her story emphasizes the possibility of enlightenment in a female body and challenges gender biases.

Variants

Some versions say she was a queen or a nun.

Relationships

Avalokiteshvara

Type Spiritual consort and companionSource Tibetan Buddhist iconography and legend

Description

Tara is often depicted as the consort of Avalokiteshvara, sharing his compassionate activity. She is said to have been born from his tear and to work alongside him.

Version Note

In some traditions, she is considered his mother or his disciple.

Conflicting Accounts

The exact nature of their relationship varies: consort, daughter, or disciple.

Padmasambhava

Type Protector and teacherSource Tibetan Buddhist hagiographies

Description

Padmasambhava, the founder of Tibetan Buddhism, is said to have received teachings from Tara and to have been protected by her. She is often invoked in the Nyingma tradition.

Version Note

Tara appears as a guardian and source of inspiration for Padmasambhava.

Conflicting Accounts

None noted.

Palden Lhamo

Type Distinct deity, sometimes associatedSource Tibetan Buddhist pantheon

Description

Palden Lhamo is a fierce protector goddess in Tibetan Buddhism. While both are female deities, they are distinct. Tara is a bodhisattva of compassion, while Palden Lhamo is a Dharmapala (protector of the Dharma).

Version Note

They are not the same, but some practitioners may propitiate both.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Contradictions & Disputes

Tara's gender and enlightenment

Position A

Traditional view: Tara is a fully enlightened Buddha, often depicted as a female bodhisattva.

Position A — evidence

The story of Princess Yeshe Dawa and the fact that Tara is called 'Mother of all Buddhas'.

Position B

Some feminist interpretations: Tara's story is used to argue for the possibility of female enlightenment, challenging patriarchal structures.

Position B — evidence

Modern scholarship on Buddhist feminism, e.g., Miranda Shaw's 'Passionate Enlightenment'.

Scholarly Consensus

There is no consensus; the traditional view is that Tara is enlightened, but the interpretation of her gender significance varies.

Tara's origin

Position A

Born from a tear of Avalokiteshvara.

Position A — evidence

Popular Tibetan legend.

Position B

Born from a lotus in a lake, or from the compassionate gaze of a Buddha.

Position B — evidence

Other versions in the Tara Tantra and oral traditions.

Scholarly Consensus

Multiple origin stories coexist; no single canonical origin.
