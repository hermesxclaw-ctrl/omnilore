The bow of destiny brought me across the sea to a land ruled by the serpent. I did not conquer it — I married it, and Cambodia was born.

Who It Is

Preah Thong (known as Kaundinya in Indian sources and Chinese records) is the legendary Brahmin who founded the first Cambodian kingdom by marrying the naga princess Neang Kakey. According to the myth preserved in Khmer chronicles and corroborated by Chinese envoys who visited Funan in the 3rd century CE, Preah Thong was an Indian Brahmin who dreamed of a divine bow that would lead him to his destiny. He sailed east and encountered the Naga Princess on the coast of what is now Cambodia. The Naga King, her father, tested Preah Thong by swallowing the land and flooding the sea — when Preah Thong refused to flee, proving his courage, the Naga King withdrew the waters, creating the fertile Cambodian floodplains, and gave his daughter in marriage. Preah Thong is credited with introducing Indian concepts of kingship, writing, law, and statecraft to the Khmer people. The city he founded became the nucleus of the Funan kingdom, the first Indianized state in Southeast Asia. Preah Thong's story embodies the synthesis of Indian and indigenous traditions that defined Khmer civilization. The legend is memorialized in the bas-reliefs of Angkor Wat and is still taught to Cambodian schoolchildren as the origin of their nation.

The Approach

In the mist-shrouded dawn of Khmer legend, a foreign prince named Preah Thong stepped ashore on a land that would become Cambodia—and there he met a serpent princess whose love would bind a kingdom to the waters. Their union, half-human and half-naga, is the mythic foundation of a people, a story of love, betrayal, and the eternal dance between land and river.

Content advisory. No explicit violent or sexual content in the core myth, but themes of cultural encounter, marriage alliance, and supernatural transformation; some versions include deception and conflict with the naga world.
Identity

Preah Thong

Pronunciation PRAY-uh TONG (approximate; Khmer: ព្រះថោង)

Known As

Preah ThongPreah ThaongKaundinyaKaudinyaHun-tien

Etymology

Preah Thong (ព្រះថោង) is often interpreted as 'the great lord' or 'the noble one'; 'Thong' may relate to 'gold' or 'shining' in some interpretations, though this is uncertain. The name is sometimes linked to the Indian sage Kaundinya, whose legend parallels the Khmer myth.

Cultural Ownership

Cambodian (Khmer) culture; also shared with Mon-Khmer and Southeast Asian traditions, particularly the legend of the founding of the Funan kingdom.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars identify Preah Thong with the Indian Brahmin Kaundinya who, according to Chinese records, founded Funan in the 1st century CE. Others argue he is a purely local legendary figure, and the identification may be a later synthesis.

Description

Appearance

Preah Thong is typically depicted as a handsome young prince, often in royal attire, sometimes with a spear or sword. In some artistic representations, he is shown with the naga princess Neang Neak, with her in serpent form or as a beautiful woman.

Symbols & Iconography

The naga (serpent) is central; the spear he thrusts into the ground to claim the land is a symbol of his authority. The white cloth he spreads over the naga princess's tail is sometimes interpreted as a symbol of civilization or domestication of the wild.

Behaviour & Personality

He is portrayed as adventurous, brave, and respectful of the supernatural. He accepts the naga princess's hand and agrees to her father's conditions, showing diplomacy and adaptability.

Domains & Habitats

The story is set on the coast of Cambodia, near the sea and rivers; the naga kingdom is underwater. The land he claims becomes the kingdom of Funan, later associated with the Khmer Empire.

Version Differences

There are multiple versions: in some, Preah Thong is a prince from India; in others, he is a local hero. Some versions emphasize the naga father's demand that Preah Thong build a city; others focus on the marriage and the birth of a lineage. The Chinese records of Kaundinya and Liuye are a parallel but distinct account.

Powers & Abilities

Divine favor and legitimacy

As the founder of a royal lineage, Preah Thong is endowed with the favor of the naga deities, granting him the right to rule. His union with the naga princess gives him supernatural protection and connection to the waters.

Limitations

This is a mythical attribute, not a personal power; it legitimizes his descendants.

Contradictions

Some versions do not emphasize any special powers, merely his role as a culture hero.

Stories & Myths

The Meeting of Preah Thong and Neang Neak

Source Khmer oral tradition and royal chroniclesCulture Cambodian (Khmer)Period Legendary; possibly 1st century CE or earlier

Summary

Preah Thong, a prince from a foreign land (often said to be India), arrives on the coast of Cambodia. He encounters Neang Neak, the daughter of the naga king, who is bathing in the sea. They fall in love and marry. Her father, the naga king, gives his blessing and drinks the water of the land to transform it into a habitable country. He also demands that Preah Thong build a city, which becomes the capital of Funan. The couple's descendants become the Khmer kings.

Participants

Preah Thong

Neang Neak

Naga king (Phanya Naga)

Consequences

The founding of the kingdom of Funan, the establishment of the royal lineage, and the origin of the Cambodian people's connection to the naga.

Variants

In some versions, the naga king is hostile and must be appeased; in others, he is benevolent. Some versions include a prophecy that the Khmer people will always be protected by the naga.

The Parallel Legend of Kaundinya and Liuye

Source Chinese historical records (e.g., Book of Liang)Culture Chinese records of Southeast Asian historyPeriod 1st century CE (as recorded later)

Summary

A foreigner named Kaundinya (Hun-tien) receives a divine arrow in a dream and sails to Funan. He meets a local queen named Liuye (Willow Leaf), who initially attacks him but then submits and marries him. Kaundinya becomes king and establishes the kingdom.

Participants

Kaundinya

Liuye

Consequences

The founding of Funan according to Chinese sources.

Variants

This account is often considered the historical basis for the Preah Thong myth, though the details differ (e.g., no naga, but a queen).

Colonial and patriarchal dynamics

Source Scholarly interpretations of the Funan founding myth

Description

In the Chinese version, Kaundinya (Preah Thong) is a foreign man who marries a local queen after she submits. In some interpretations, this symbolizes the imposition of Indian culture on indigenous Southeast Asian societies. The story may also reinforce patriarchal norms, as the female figure is often passive or becomes submissive.

Scholarly Context

Historians and anthropologists have analyzed the myth as a reflection of cultural encounters and power relations.

Contradictions & Disputes

Identity as a historical figure

Position A

Preah Thong is a purely mythical figure with no historical basis.

Position A — evidence

Lack of contemporaneous Khmer records; the story is legendary in nature.

Position B

Preah Thong is based on the historical Kaundinya, a Brahmin who founded Funan.

Position B — evidence

Chinese records document a foreigner named Kaundinya founding Funan, and the myth parallels that account.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars accept a historical core, but the details are obscured by mythology.

The nature of Neang Neak

Position A

She is a supernatural naga princess.

Position A — evidence

The naga is a common motif in Khmer mythology and royal ideology.

Position B

She is a human queen (Liuye) who was later mythologized as a naga.

Position B — evidence

Chinese records describe a human queen, and the naga element may be a later addition.

Scholarly Consensus

The naga element is likely a local mythological overlay on a historical encounter.

Type historical textDate 6th-7th century CE (compilation of earlier records)Locator Accounts of Funan in Chinese dynastic historiesLanguage Chinese

Translation

These records describe a foreigner named Hun-tien who married a local princess named Liuye (Willow Leaf) and founded the kingdom of Funan, a story that parallels the Preah Thong legend.

Various Khmer inscriptions referencing the naga lineage of Khmer kings

Type inscriptionDate Various; from pre-Angkorian to Angkorian periods (6th-13th century CE)Locator Inscriptions such as those at Angkor and other temple sitesLanguage Sanskrit and Old Khmer

Translation

Inscriptions often claim that kings descended from the union of a sage and a naga princess, legitimizing royal power.
