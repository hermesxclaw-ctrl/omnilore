Who It Is

Imagine the universe's most powerful bouncer, a god with the head of an elephant, the belly of a contented gourmand, and the wisdom of a thousand sages. This is Ganesha, the remover of obstacles, who stands at the threshold of every beginning — from a child's first day of school to the launch of a billion-dollar rocket. He is at once a cosmic force and a mischievous child, a stern guardian and a lover of sweets. To understand Ganesha is to understand the Hindu art of starting well, of honoring the gate before you walk through it, and of finding wisdom in the most unexpected of forms.

Ganesha is characterized as a devoted son, a playful child, a wise scribe, and a benevolent but fearsome guardian. He is known for his intelligence, his love of sweets, his cleverness (he is said to have circumambulated his parents Shiva and Parvati, declaring them to be his universe), and his role as a gatekeeper who demands reverence. He is also a fierce protector who can create obstacles for the careless and remove them for the worthy. Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Ganesha' (गणेश) is a compound of 'gana' (गण), meaning 'group', 'multitude', or 'troop', and 'isha' (ईश), meaning 'lord' or 'master'. Thus, Ganesha is 'Lord of the Ganas' — the troops of Shiva's attendants. 'Ganapati' (गणपति) is a synonym, with 'pati' meaning 'lord' or 'husband'. 'Vinayaka'

Pronunciation — GUH-nay-shuh (गणेश). In Sanskrit, the 'G' is hard, the first 'a' is short (like 'u' in 'but'), and the 'e' is a long 'ay' sound. The final 'a' is often elided in modern pronunciation.

Aliases — Ganapati, Vinayaka, The Elephant God, Vighneshvara, Ekadanta, Gajanana

Powers
Vighnaharta / Vighneshvara (Remover/Creator of Obstacles)

Ganesha's primary power is his lordship over obstacles (vighna). He can remove obstacles from the path of his devotees, but he can also place obstacles in the path of those who are arrogant, careless, or disrespectful. This dual nature makes him the essential deity to propitiate before any undertaking.

Limit: His power is often portrayed as conditional: he removes obstacles for those who approach with devotion, but creates them for the heedless.

Buddhi (Wisdom and Intellect)

Ganesha is the god of wisdom, intelligence, and learning. He is the patron of students, scholars, and writers. He is said to be the scribe who wrote down the Mahabharata as dictated by the sage Vyasa.

Limit: None stated, but his wisdom is often portrayed as requiring the devotee to actively seek it.

Lordship over the Ganas

As 'Ganapati', he commands the ganas — the myriad attendants, goblins, and semi-divine beings that serve Shiva. This gives him immense authority in the celestial hierarchy and makes him a powerful intercessor.

Limit: His authority is subordinate to that of Shiva and Parvati, his parents.

Ankusha and Pasha (Goad and Noose)

These weapons/tools symbolize his power to control the mind and the world. The noose is used to bind ignorance and delusion; the goad is used to prod the devotee toward righteousness.

Limit: Symbolic powers, not usually depicted as weapons of war.

Ability to Circumambulate the Universe

In a famous story, Ganesha wins a race against his brother Kartikeya by circumambulating his parents, Shiva and Parvati, declaring that they are the universe.

Limit: This is a narrative feat, not a standard power.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Ganesha is most commonly depicted with a human body (often pot-bellied, seated or standing) and the head of a one-tusked elephant. He typically has four arms, though two- and six-armed forms exist. He is usually shown with a broken tusk (Ekadanta) held in one hand, a noose (pasha) in another, a goad (ankusha) in a third, and a bowl of sweet modaka cakes, which he is often eating, in the fourth. Domains — Ganesha presides over all beginnings, thresholds, doors, and transitions. He is the patron of learning, wisdom, and intelligence; the remover of obstacles (Vighneshvara); the lord of the Ganas (Shiva's attendants); and a granter of success and prosperity. He is invoked at the start of any new venture, ceremony, or journey. He is also associated with the root chakra (Muladhara) in yogic tradition.

Accounts I
The Birth of Ganesha and the BeheadingShiva Purana (Rudra Samhita), also found in the Padma Purana and Varaha Purana

Composed c. 7th-14th century CE, based on earlier oral traditions

Parvati, while bathing, creates a boy from the turmeric paste (or clay) she scrapes from her body. She breathes life into him and sets him as a guard at her door. When Shiva returns, the boy, who does not know him, refuses him entry. Enraged, Shiva sends his ganas to fight the boy, but the boy defeats them. Finally, Shiva himself beheads the boy with his trident. Parvati is devastated. To console her, Shiva sends the ganas to find the head of the first living being they find facing north, which happens to be an elephant. He fixes the elephant's head onto the boy's body, reviving him. He is declared Ganesha, the lord of the ganas. Ganesha is established as the son of Shiva and Parvati, and is given the role of lord of the ganas. The story explains his elephant head and his role as a threshold guardian.

The Broken Tusk (Ekadanta)Mudgala Purana, Brahmanda Purana, and various oral traditions

c. 12th-15th century CE

There are several stories explaining Ganesha's single tusk. The most famous: when the sage Vyasa was dictating the Mahabharata, Ganesha was his scribe. Vyasa dictated continuously, and Ganesha's quill broke. To avoid losing a word, Ganesha broke off his own tusk and used it as a pen. Another story: Ganesha threw his tusk at the moon after the moon laughed at him for falling off his mouse. Another: he broke his tusk to use as a weapon against the demon Ravana, who was trying to take Shiva's sacred mountain. His single tusk becomes a symbol of self-sacrifice for knowledge, of overcoming duality, and of his quick wit.

Accounts II
The Race Around the WorldShiva Purana, Tamil and other regional retellings

c. 7th-14th century CE

Shiva and Parvati challenge their two sons, Kartikeya and Ganesha, to a race around the world. The winner will receive a divine fruit (often a mango). Kartikeya, confident in his speed, mounts his peacock and flies off. Ganesha, slow and with a mouse for a mount, simply walks around his parents Shiva and Parvati, who are seated together. He declares that they are his universe, and that circumambulating them is circumambulating the world. He wins the race and the fruit. The story establishes Ganesha's wisdom and his devotion to his parents. It is a popular teaching tale about the superiority of devotion over mere physical prowess.

Ganesha Writes the MahabharataMahabharata (Anushasana Parva), Vyasa's narration

c. 400 BCE - 400 CE

The sage Vyasa, needing a scribe for his epic, asks Ganesha to write it down. Ganesha agrees, but on the condition that Vyasa never pauses in his dictation. Vyasa agrees, but adds his own condition: that Ganesha must understand every verse before writing it. Ganesha agrees. To give himself time to compose, Vyasa would occasionally recite a verse that was so complex that Ganesha would have to pause to think. This created the 'gaps' in the Mahabharata. Ganesha is established as the patron of scribes and the god of wisdom. The story also explains the complexity and structure of the Mahabharata.

The Origin of the Elephant HeadThe elephant head is a result of Shiva's rage (beheading the boy and replacing the head).Ganesha was born with an elephant head, or the head was given to him by another deity (e.g., Vishnu) as a blessing.

Ganesha's elephant head means he is a 'god of elephants'. — Ganesha is not a god of elephants. He is a god of wisdom, beginnings, and obstacle removal. The elephant head symbolizes these qualities (intelligence, strength, and the ability to clear paths).

Ganesha and the elephant-headed god of other cultures (e.g., the Egyptian god Set with his animal head) are the same. — Ganesha is a unique deity with a specific mythology and cultural context. He is not a variant of any other animal-headed god.

Ganesha's broken tusk means he was 'defeated' in battle. — The broken tusk is a symbol of self-sacrifice (he broke it to write the Mahabharata) or of overcoming duality. It is not a sign of defeat.

Worshipping Ganesha is a form of idolatry that is 'primitive'. — Ganesha worship is a sophisticated theological and philosophical system. The image is a focus for meditation on his qualities, not an idol in the pejorative sense.
