Who It Is

Ganesha (Gaṇeśa), the elephant-headed god, is one of the most universally beloved and widely recognized deities across the Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain worlds—the Remover of Obstacles, the Lord of Beginnings, the patron of arts and sciences, and the deity of intellect and wisdom. No Hindu ritual, journey, business venture, or writing project begins without an invocation to Ganesha. His origin myth is rich with symbolic meaning: Parvati created him from the turmeric paste of her own body, breathed life into him, and set him to guard her door while she bathed. When Shiva, her husband, returned and was denied entry by the unknown boy, a cosmic battle ensued. In the ensuing conflict, Shiva's followers were routed, the gods themselves could not defeat the boy, and finally Shiva decapitated him with his trident. Parvati's grief and fury threatened to destroy creation, so to appease her, Shiva ordered his attendants to bring the head of the first living being they found facing north—which happened to be an elephant. Shiva affixed the elephant's head to the boy's body, restored him to life, and declared him foremost among the gods. This myth encodes profound spiritual truths: the human body (mortality) married to the elephant head (the universal memory, wisdom, and the cosmic Aum), the destruction of the ego (the original head) for the birth of wisdom, and the reconciliation of opposites (Shiva and Parvati, destruction and creation) in a unified divine family. Ganesha's iconography is equally rich: his large ears hear all prayers; his small mouth speaks little but wisely; his trunk can uproot mountains or pick up a single blade of grass; his broken tusk (which he broke to serve as a pen to write the Mahabharata dictated by Vyasa) represents sacrifice for knowledge; the modaka (sweet dumpling) in his hand represents the reward of spiritual practice; and his vehicle, the tiny mouse (mushika), represents the ego that must be controlled and ridden. Ganesh Chaturthi, his birthday festival, is a massive celebration, especially in Maharashtra. His worship extends beyond India to Nepal, Sri Lanka, Thailand, Indonesia, Myanmar, Tibet, China, and Japan.

Form

Ganesha is most commonly depicted with a human body (often pot-bellied, seated or standing) and the head of a one-tusked elephant. He typically has four arms, though two- and six-armed forms exist. He is usually shown with a broken tusk (Ekadanta) held in one hand, a noose (pasha) in another, a goad (ankusha) in a third, and a bowl of sweet modaka cakes, which he is often eating, in the fourth. His belly is prominent (Lambodara, 'the pot-bellied one'). He is often accompanied by a mouse (the rat, Mushika), his vehicle (vahana). His complexion is usually red or golden, and he is frequently adorned with a serpent as a sacred thread (yajnopavita). He is often depicted with a single tusk, the other broken. His ears are large and fan-like.

Powers

Vighnaharta / Vighneshvara (Remover/Creator of Obstacles) Ganesha's primary power is his lordship over obstacles (vighna). He can remove obstacles from the path of his devotees, but he can also place obstacles in the path of those who are arrogant, careless, or disrespectful. This dual nature makes him the essential deity to propitiate before any undertaking. Pan-Indian, attested across all major Puranas and in daily practice. His power is often portrayed as conditional: he removes obstacles for those who approach with devotion, but creates them for the heedless. Some texts emphasize his role as a remover; others (like the Mudgala Purana) emphasize his role as a creator of obstacles as a form of divine testing.

Buddhi (Wisdom and Intellect) Ganesha is the god of wisdom, intelligence, and learning. He is the patron of students, scholars, and writers. He is said to be the scribe who wrote down the Mahabharata as dictated by the sage Vyasa. Pan-Indian, attested in the Mahabharata tradition and in daily worship. None stated, but his wisdom is often portrayed as requiring the devotee to actively seek it. None.

Lordship over the Ganas As 'Ganapati', he commands the ganas — the myriad attendants, goblins, and semi-divine beings that serve Shiva. This gives him immense authority in the celestial hierarchy and makes him a powerful intercessor. Attested in the Shiva Purana and early iconography. His authority is subordinate to that of Shiva and Parvati, his parents. In the Ganapatya tradition, he is supreme, and Shiva is seen as subordinate to him.

Ankusha and Pasha (Goad and Noose) These weapons/tools symbolize his power to control the mind and the world. The noose is used to bind ignorance and delusion; the goad is used to prod the devotee toward righteousness. Attested in iconography and Puranic descriptions. Symbolic powers, not usually depicted as weapons of war. None.

Ability to Circumambulate the Universe In a famous story, Ganesha wins a race against his brother Kartikeya by circumambulating his parents, Shiva and Parvati, declaring that they are the universe. Attested in the Shiva Purana and many regional retellings. This is a narrative feat, not a standard power. None.

Behavior & Habitat

Ganesha is characterized as a devoted son, a playful child, a wise scribe, and a benevolent but fearsome guardian. He is known for his intelligence, his love of sweets, his cleverness (he is said to have circumambulated his parents Shiva and Parvati, declaring them to be his universe), and his role as a gatekeeper who demands reverence. He is also a fierce protector who can create obstacles for the careless and remove them for the worthy. He is often portrayed as a being of paradoxes: the remover and creator of obstacles; the lord of beginnings who is also the lord of endings; the god of wisdom who is also a glutton.

Accounts

Parvati, while bathing, creates a boy from the turmeric paste (or clay) she scrapes from her body. She breathes life into him and sets him as a guard at her door. When Shiva returns, the boy, who does not know him, refuses him entry. Enraged, Shiva sends his ganas to fight the boy, but the boy defeats them. Finally, Shiva himself beheads the boy with his trident. Parvati is devastated. To console her, Shiva sends the ganas to find the head of the first living being they find facing north, which happens to be an elephant. He fixes the elephant's head onto the boy's body, reviving him. He is declared Ganesha, the lord of the ganas.

There are several stories explaining Ganesha's single tusk. The most famous: when the sage Vyasa was dictating the Mahabharata, Ganesha was his scribe. Vyasa dictated continuously, and Ganesha's quill broke. To avoid losing a word, Ganesha broke off his own tusk and used it as a pen. Another story: Ganesha threw his tusk at the moon after the moon laughed at him for falling off his mouse. Another: he broke his tusk to use as a weapon against the demon Ravana, who was trying to take Shiva's sacred mountain.

Shiva and Parvati challenge their two sons, Kartikeya and Ganesha, to a race around the world. The winner will receive a divine fruit (often a mango). Kartikeya, confident in his speed, mounts his peacock and flies off. Ganesha, slow and with a mouse for a mount, simply walks around his parents Shiva and Parvati, who are seated together. He declares that they are his universe, and that circumambulating them is circumambulating the world. He wins the race and the fruit.

The sage Vyasa, needing a scribe for his epic, asks Ganesha to write it down. Ganesha agrees, but on the condition that Vyasa never pauses in his dictation. Vyasa agrees, but adds his own condition: that Ganesha must understand every verse before writing it. Ganesha agrees. To give himself time to compose, Vyasa would occasionally recite a verse that was so complex that Ganesha would have to pause to think. This created the 'gaps' in the Mahabharata.

Content Notes

Shiva, in a fit of rage, beheads his own son. This is a foundational story, but it is violent. The narrative is resolved with Ganesha's revival, but the act of filicide is a stark element.

The story is often interpreted allegorically: the ego is beheaded to make way for the divine. It is a story of sacrifice and transformation, not just violence.

Shiva Purana

During the British Raj, Western caricaturists often depicted Ganesha in a mocking or grotesque manner, using his elephant head as a symbol of 'heathen' idolatry. This is a form of cultural mockery that must be acknowledged.

This is part of the broader history of colonial representation of Indian culture, which often sought to denigrate Hindu deities to justify colonial rule.

Scholarly Debates
Earliest Vedic 'Ganapati'
The 'Ganapati' of the Rigveda (2.23.1) is a form of Brihaspati (Jupiter), not the elephant-headed Ganesha. ⇌ Some devotional and nationalist interpretations see the Rigvedic Ganapati as an early reference to Ganesha himself.
Evidence for A: The Rigveda describes Ganapati as a sage and priest, with no mention of an elephant head. The connection is etymological
Evidence for B: Proponents point to the shared name 'Ganapati' and the later importance of Ganesha.
Ganesha's Marital Status
Ganesha is a celibate brahmachari (celibate). This is the dominant view in North India. ⇌ Ganesha is married to two consorts, Riddhi (Prosperity) and Siddhi (Accomplishment), and has two sons, Shubha and Labha. This is the dominant view in South India and in Southeast Asian traditions.
Evidence for A: The Shiva Purana and many North Indian traditions emphasize his celibacy.
Evidence for B: The Mudgala Purana and South Indian traditions, as well as Balinese Hindu practice, depict him as married.
The Origin of the Elephant Head
The elephant head is a result of Shiva's rage (beheading the boy and replacing the head). ⇌ Ganesha was born with an elephant head, or the head was given to him by another deity (e.g., Vishnu) as a blessing.
Evidence for A: The Shiva Purana and most Puranic accounts describe the beheading.
Evidence for B: Some regional traditions and the Ganesha Purana offer alternative origin stories.

A way to hold it in your mind

If Shiva is the cosmic destroyer and Parvati the nurturing mother, Ganesha is the project manager of the universe. He's the one who clears the road, removes the bureaucratic hurdles, and makes sure the paperwork is in order before the big launch. He's the god you call when you're about to start something new — a business, a journey, a thesis — and you want to make sure it doesn't fall apart. He's also the god of learning, so he's the patron saint of students everywhere, the one who helps you remember the answers on the exam. He's the wise, slightly rotund uncle of the gods, the one who knows the best route around the traffic jam of life.
