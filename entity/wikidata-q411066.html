Who It Is

In the dead of night, a voice boomed from nowhere, warning of an enemy that would bring Rome to its knees. The Romans, being sensible, ignored it. The next day, the Gauls sacked the city. This is the story of Aius Locutius, the god who was just a voice—and the god who was right.

The deity's sole recorded behavior is the utterance of a single, prophetic warning. It is a one-time occurrence, a singular act of divine speech. No other interactions, demands, or communications are recorded.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Latin 'aius' (a form of the verb 'aio', meaning 'I say' or 'I affirm') and 'locutius' (from 'loquor', meaning 'I speak'). The name translates roughly as 'The Speaking Declarer' or 'The Announcing Speaker'.

Pronunciation — AY-yus lo-KOO-tee-us

Aliases — Aius Loquens, Aius Locutus, The Speaking Voice, The Divine Voice

Ownership — Roman

Powers
Prophetic Warning

The ability to foretell a specific future event and communicate it audibly. In this case, the warning of the Gallic invasion of 390 BCE.

Limit: The warning was given only once, in a voice that was heard but whose source was not seen. It was not specific enough to be actionable, as it did not name the enemy or suggest a defense, a point Cicero famously criticizes.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No physical description exists. Aius Locutius is a disembodied voice, never depicted in art or described in humanoid form. The deity is purely auditory, a phenomenon rather than a being with a body.

Symbols — No symbols are recorded. The only 'symbol' might be the location itself, the area near the Temple of Vesta and the Via Nova, where the voice was heard.

Domains — The deity's domain is that of prophetic utterance, particularly warnings of imminent danger. It is a numen (divine will or power) that manifests through speech, rather than a god with a defined sphere of influence like war (Mars) or the sea (Neptune). It is closely tied to the safety of the Roman state (salus publica).

Accounts I
The Warning at the Foot of the PalatineCicero, Livy, Plutarch

c. 390 BCE (the event); c. 1st century BCE – 1st century CE (the literary accounts)

In the dead of night, shortly before the catastrophic Gallic invasion led by Brennus, a mysterious and supernatural voice was heard near the Temple of Vesta, at the foot of the Palatine Hill. The voice was loud and clear, but its source was invisible. It warned, in effect, 'The Gauls are coming.' The warning was completely ignored by the Romans, who dismissed it as a phantom or a figment of imagination. The very next day, the Gauls sacked Rome. After the Romans recovered and repelled the invaders, they were ashamed that they had ignored such a clear divine sign. On the advice of the haruspices (priests who interpreted divine will), the Senate ordered that a temple and an altar be dedicated to the unnamed voice. However, a dispute arose about where exactly the voice had been heard, between the Via Nova (a street) and the edge of the Palatine.
