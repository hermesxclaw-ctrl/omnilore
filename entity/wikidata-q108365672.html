Who It Is

In Athens, your worth was measured in grain and oil — and if you could harvest 300 medimnoi a year, the city did not hand you a medal, it handed you a horse. The hippeus, the 'horseman,' was not a fairy-tale knight but a census class turned cavalryman, a wealthy citizen expected to buy, feed, and ride his own warhorse into the dust of Boeotia and Sicily, to parade gleaming at the Panathenaia in peace and to decide, by his vote and his lance, who ruled the democracy.

Behavior is institutional, not personal, but sources ascribe ethos. Aristotle (Ath. Pol. 7) and Plutarch (Solon) present hippeis as second-tier timocratic elite: oligarchic-leaning but not as exclusive as pentakosiomedimnoi. Thucydides and Xenophon portray Athenian hippeis as politically conservative, often suspicious of radical democracy — many supported the oligarchic coups of 411 and 404 BC (Xen. Hell. 2.3). Powers
Timocratic Political Privilege

As second Solonian class, hippeis could hold lower archonships (and after 457 BC, the archonship itself), serve as hipparchoi/phylarchs, and claim higher honor (time) in courts. Not merely military — a legal status determining eligibility for office and military levy.

Limit: Below pentakosiomedimnoi (500 medimnoi) who alone could be treasurers; above zeugitai (200) and thetes. Property qualification had to be continually proven; loss of income meant demotion at census.

Mounted Warfare - Screening, Harassment, Pursuit

Pre-4th century Greek cavalry did not deliver shock charges; utility was screening army flanks, scouting, harassing light troops, cutting down fleeing hoplites, and rapid communication. Xenophon details wheeling, feigned retreat, and javelin skirmishing.

Limit: Low shock value due to heavy armor, no stirrups/saddle, no horseshoes limiting terrain, small horses, inability to break formed hoplite phalanx. Vulnerable to peltasts and rough ground. Heavy armor fatigued horse quickly.

Cavalry Charge (4th century evolution)

Third evolutionary stage: coordinated charge in wedge or rhomboid, developed by Thessalians and perfected by Philip II and Alexander. Macedonian hetairoi delivered decisive hammer-and-anvil blows (e.g., Chaeronea 338 BC, Granicus 334 BC).

Limit: Required professional training, larger horses (imported), and combined-arms coordination; Athenian citizen hippeis never achieved this level. Charge still risked horse injury without stirrups.

State-Subsidized Maintenance and Inspection

On enrollment, hippeus received katastasis (equipment grant) and annual sitos/subsidy for groom and two horses (one remount/baggage horse). Horses and gear inspected (dokimasia) by boule; unfit horses rejected, owners fined.

Limit: Subsidy (40 talents total annually) did not cover full cost; personal wealth still required. Boule could downgrade or expel members. During oligarchic periods subsidies lapsed.

Ceremonial and Processional Display

In peacetime hippeis drilled and performed in religious processions (Panathenaic parade, etc.) as spectacle of civic order and elite piety. Xenophon describes anthippasia (mock cavalry battles) for festivals.

Limit: Ceremonial duties did not confer religious authority; purely display. Performance failures could bring public shame.

Command Authority (Hipparchos/Phylarchos)

Two hipparchoi (commanders) elected from top two classes commanded whole corps; five phylarchs per hipparch commanded tribal phyla. Authority included levy, training, discipline, and leading in field by decree of ekklesia.

Limit: Command subject to boule oversight and assembly decree on number dispatched; no independent war-making power. Spartan hipparmostes different office.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Attested hippeus appearance is that of a wealthy Archaic-Classical Greek citizen-soldier, not a uniformed medieval knight. Vase paintings, the Parthenon frieze, and Xenophon (Peri Hippikes 12) describe: bronze helmet (often Boeotian type for cavalry, lighter than Corinthian), linen or bronze cuirass (thorax), sometimes a mail-fringed kilt (pteryges), cuisses to knee, leather leggings, no shield in battle (unlike hoplite), cloak (chlamys). Weapons

Symbols — Horse itself is the primary status symbol — ownership presupposes surplus land producing 300 medimnoi. On the Acropolis and in dedications, hippeis dedicated horse figurines and reliefs. In civic procession (pompe) at Panathenaia, Great Dionysia, and Anthesteria, hippeis rode in formation as living display of elite wealth and piety; Parthenon frieze is the canonical iconographic programme. Accounts I
Solon Creates the Hippeis - The Timocratic ConstitutionAristotle, Athenaion Politeia 7.3-4; Plutarch, Life of Solon 18; Solon fragments

Archaic, c. 594/593 BC

After stasis between rich and poor, lawgiver Solon abolishes debt-slavery and divides citizens into four property classes based on annual produce in medimnoi. Those yielding 300-499 medimnoi become hippeis, second class. The reform ties political rights to wealth not birth, allowing hippeis to hold lesser magistracies and serve as cavalry, while reserving top offices for pentakosiomedimnoi. The medimnos (approx. 52 liters) of barley, oil, or wine becomes the measure of citizenship. Foundation of timocracy; creates institutional identity of hippeus for next two centuries; later expanded by Cleisthenes and Ephialtes. Provides ideological justification for cavalry service as privilege of wealth.

The Creation of the Athenian Cavalry after the Persian WarsAndocides (?), Thucydides 2.13.8; Aristotle Ath. Pol. 49; Xenophon Hipparchikos

Classical, 477-432 BC (formation c. 452 BC, expanded c. 445 BC)

Sparta had mocked Athens as horseless. After Plataea (479 BC) and during the Pentekontaetia, Athens builds a citizen cavalry. Initially 300 hippeis, then enlarged to 1000 citizens plus 200 Scythian/Thracian hippotoxotai (mounted archers), later replaced by 200 prodromoi javelin cavalry (395-360 BC), totaling 1200. Pericles in 431 BC counts 1000 hippeis including 200 mounted archers as part of Athens' war resources. Hipparchoi levy and boule inspects. Athens gains mobile arm for Attic defense, raiding Megarid and Boeotia; cavalry becomes visible elite corps with state pay (40 talents). Establishes dokimasia and katastasis system described by Xenophon.

Battle of Delium and the Boeotian Cavalry Lesson - 424 BCThucydides 4.93-96; Diodorus Siculus 12.70

Peloponnesian War, 424 BC

Athenians under Hippocrates invade Boeotia and fortify Delium. Boeotians under Pagondas deploy superior cavalry on flanks. Athenian hippeis, though outnumbered, initially hold but are outflanked when Theban cavalry circles. The defeat teaches Sparta, which had neglected cavalry, to form a regular hippeis corps in same year (Thuc. 4.55). Socrates and Alcibiades fight as hoplites/cavalry in retreat. Sparta decrees that rich provide horses and those unfit for hoplite service be drafted as riders without drill. Demonstrates limits of Athenian citizen cavalry vs Boeotian/Thessalian traditions; triggers Spartan cavalry reform; cited by Xenophon as example of need for training. Spartan cavalry remains weak, contributing to later defeat at Leuctra 371 BC.

Accounts II
The Knights on Stage - Aristophanes' Hippeis Satirizes the HorsemenAristophanes, Hippeis (Knights) 424 BC; scholia

Classical, Lenaia 424 BC

Aristophanes makes the chorus the hippeis themselves — bearded, long-haired elite youths who claim to be the true defenders of Athens. They attack the demagogue Cleon (Paphlagonian slave) on behalf of Demos (the People), portraying cavalrymen as anti-demagogue conservatives. The play, winning first prize over Cratinus, preserves contemporary slang, horse-care jokes, and political posture of hippeis as self-styled moral aristocracy. It is our only literary work where hippeis speak as collective character. Invaluable evidence for hippeis' self-image, dialect, and oligarchic sympathies; shows cavalry class could be mobilized theatrically. Later cited by Aristotle and Plutarch as source for Cleon's unpopularity. Establishes 'knights' translation in English.

Sicilian Expedition and the Cost of No Cavalry - 415-413 BCThucydides 6.20-22, 6.43, 6.64, 6.70, 7.27; Plutarch Life of Nicias

Peloponnesian War, 415-413 BC

Nicias warns ekklesia that Athens lacks adequate cavalry for Sicily, where Syracusans and allies have strong horse. Assembly ignores warning, sends only limited hippeis. In Sicily, Athenian hoplites repeatedly win infantry engagements but cannot exploit victory because Syracusan cavalry controls plain, prevents foraging, and harasses retreat. Thucydides calls lack of cavalry a decisive cause of catastrophe. Survivors' horses slaughtered for food during siege of Syracuse. Destruction of Athenian expedition (40,000 men lost); demonstrates strategic value of cavalry in open terrain; prompts later Athenian investment in prodromoi. Becomes classic case study in Greek military thought (Xenophon, Polybius).

Xenophon's Manuals and the Trial of the Thirty - Hippeis in Oligarchy and RestorationXenophon, Hipparchikos, Hellenica 2.3-4; Lysias Against Philon 16; Aristotle Ath. Pol. 38

Late 5th-early 4th century BC, 404-395 BC

After defeat in 404 BC, the Thirty Tyrants rely on hippeis and the 300 'whips' to enforce rule; many hippeis support oligarchy. After restoration of democracy (403 BC), hippeis face scrutiny (dokimasia) and atimia proposals but are largely amnestied. Xenophon, himself a hippeus and Socratic, writes two technical treatises c. 360 BC instructing hipparchs on recruitment, training, religious processions, and humane horsemanship — the earliest extant cavalry manuals in Europe, detailing the boule's horse inspection and the anthippasia mock battles. Xenophon's works preserve complete doctrine of citizen cavalry; influence Roman and Renaissance horsemanship. Political episode cements reputation of hippeis as oligarchic, leading to democratic distrust but not abolition of corps.

From Chariot to Charge - The Three Evolutionary StagesArchaeology (Mycenaean chariot kraters c. 1400 BC); Homer Iliad; Polybius 18.12; Arrian Anabasis; Xenophon

c. 1400 BC - 330 BC

Stage 1 (Mycenaean c.1400-1100 BC): horse pulls war chariot to transport elite warriors (wanax) to battle and evacuate wounded — not true cavalry. Stage 2 (Archaic c. 700-500 BC): chariot abandoned, small mounted scouts screen army and pursue fugitives; Thessalians excel, but heavy armor limits shock. Stage 3 (Classical-Macedonian 4th century BC): Philip II creates heavy Companion cavalry (hetairoi) in wedge, light prodromoi with sarissa (14-16 ft lance), integrating cavalry as decisive arm — Alexander's hammer at Chaeronea (338 BC) and Granicus (334 BC) where hippeis form nearly one-sixth of army. Transforms Greek warfare from hoplite-dominated to combined arms; Athenian citizen model becomes obsolete. Polybius later codifies sarissa cavalry; Livy (35.28-29) notes post-Alexander Tarentine light cavalry with two horses each (192 BC).

Hippeis were medieval-style knights with chivalry, stirrups, and heavy shock charge — No stirrups, no horseshoes, no framed saddle until Middle Ages; Greek hippeis wore bronze helmets and linen cuirasses, carried no shield, fought as light skirmishers/pursuers until Macedonian reforms. No code of chivalry; status was census-based, not feudal oath.

All hippeis were cavalrymen and all cavalrymen were hippeis — Hippeus was first a property class (300 medimnoi); many hippeis served as cavalry but some served as hoplites or did not serve mounted. Conversely, hippotoxotai and prodromoi and mercenaries rode but were not hippeis class; Spartan hippeis before 424 BC were infantry.

Sparta's 300 hippeis were 300 horsemen at Thermopylae — At Thermopylae (480 BC) the 300 Spartans were hippeis in name but fought as heavy infantry royal guard; Sparta had no effective cavalry until 424 BC. Film '300' reinforces mounted image.

Athenian democracy meant hippeis were democratic heroes — Hippeis were the conservative elite; many supported oligarchic coups of 411 and 404 BC and served the Thirty Tyrants. Democracy relied on thetes as rowers, not hippeis, for its radical phase.

Hippeis rode large warhorses like medieval destriers — Greek horses were small (12-14 hands), light, unshod, and ridden with only a cloth pad. Xenophon advises small, agile horses for maneuver, not massive chargers. Large Nisean horses were rare imports for Macedonians.
