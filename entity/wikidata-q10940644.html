Who It Is

It was a feathered arrow that could fly from the reed plains to the High Plain of Heaven and back again — and when it returned, it carried death with it. In Japan's oldest chronicles, Ame no Habaya is not a sword that cleaves serpents but an arrow that enforces the will of the heavenly kami, and whose single shot unravels a marriage, a mission, and a god's life.

Object has no personality; behavior is trajectory and retributive agency. Attested: shot from Ashihara no Nakatsukuni (Central Land of Reed Plains) it flies to Takama-ga-hara, is recognized by its givers, and is hurled back to earth with lethal effect. This boomerang/curse-return motif is attested in Kojiki/Nihon Shoki. No autonomous movement otherwise. Disputed/hypothetical: some modern fiction gives it sentience or homing.

Powers
Heaven-Earth Transit Flight

When shot, the arrow traverses the boundary between earthly world and Takama-ga-hara, passing through the pheasant messenger and arriving in heaven. Attested in Kojiki and Nihon Shoki Wakahiko episode.

Limit: Single attested flight; no evidence of repeated use or controlled recall by shooter. Requires Ame no Kagoyumi bow to launch (paired set).

Divine Identification and Retributive Return

Heavenly kami (Takagi/Takamimusubi) recognize the arrow's heavenly feathering and blood on shaft, identify breach of mission, and hurl it back down to kill its shooter. Functions as evidence and execution instrument.

Limit: Lethality is conditional on divine judgment, not automatic. In Kojiki, Takagi's incantation determines outcome; arrow is conduit, not independent judge.

Lethal Penetration of Divine Beings

Kills both the pheasant Nakime (a heavenly messenger bird) and subsequently the kami Ame no Wakahiko, who is a child of Amatsu-kami. Demonstrates ability to kill kami, not merely mortals or animals.

Limit: Wakahiko is killed while sleeping, struck in chest; suggests vulnerability during sleep, not invulnerability. No evidence it can kill higher heavenly kami like Takamimusubi or Amaterasu.

Paired Efficacy with Ame no Kagoyumi

Only effective when used with its matching heavenly bow. The set together confers legitimacy as heavenly envoy. Wakahiko's misuse of the set signals rebellion.

Limit: Arrow without bow not described as usable; bow without arrow not shown in this episode. No independent power attested for arrow alone.

Accounts I
The Commission of Ame no Wakahiko and the Gift of the Heavenly Bow and ArrowKojiki (712), Nihon Shoki (720) Jindai

8th century textualization of earlier oral myth; narrative set in Age of the Gods (Kamiyo)

After Ame no Hohi fails to report back after three years in Ashihara no Nakatsukuni, the heavenly kami convene. Takagi no Kami (Takamimusubi) and Amaterasu Ōmikami select Ame no Wakahiko, son of Ame no Kunikatama, as next envoy to pacify and report on the Central Land. They bestow upon him Ame no Kagoyumi (Heavenly True-Deer Bow) and Ame no Hahakiya/Habaya (Heavenly Feathered Arrow) as tokens of authority and tools to subdue any disobedient earthly deities (kunitsukami). Establishes Wakahiko's legitimate mandate; the regalia later becomes evidence of his betrayal when misused. Sets up heaven-earth surveillance motif.

Wakahiko's Descent, Marriage, and DerelictionKojiki, Nihon Shoki

8th century

Wakahiko descends but does not fulfill his mission. He slays or expels the earthly deity Ōkuninushi's daughter Shitateru-hime's former husband? In Kojiki, he marries Shitateru-hime (daughter of Ōkuninushi), takes over the land, and plans to make it his own kingdom. For eight years he fails to report to heaven, ignoring his commission. His residence becomes a palace in the earthly realm. Creates narrative need for heavenly inquiry; explains why heaven sends a messenger bird to check on him.

The Pheasant Nakime and the Shot of the Heavenly ArrowKojiki, Nihon Shoki

8th century

Takagi and Amaterasu send the pheasant Nakime (also called Nakime, 'Crying Woman' pheasant) to observe Wakahiko. The bird perches on the katsura tree at Wakahiko's gate and delivers the heavenly question: 'Why hast thou not reported for eight years?' A heavenly spy woman Ame no Sagume (or in some texts, Wakahiko's wife) tells Wakahiko: 'A strange bird has come.' Wakahiko, prompted, takes his heavenly bow and arrow and shoots Nakime, the arrow passing through the bird's chest. The arrow, bloodied, flies to heaven, providing physical proof of Wakahiko's violence against a heavenly messenger and his rebellion.

Accounts II
The Arrow's Return and the Death of Ame no WakahikoKojiki, Nihon Shoki

8th century

The blood-stained arrow flies up to Takama-ga-hara and is presented to Takagi no Kami and Amaterasu. Takagi examines the feathers and blood, recognizes it as the arrow he gave Wakahiko, and declares a conditional curse: 'If Wakahiko shot this at evil, let it miss him; if his heart be evil, let it strike him.' He then hurls the arrow back down. While Wakahiko sleeps in his bed with Shitateru-hime, the arrow pierces his chest/heart, killing him instantly. In some telling, Wakahiko dies after a period of illness; in Kojiki, death is immediate. Divine punishment establishes principle that heavenly regalia cannot be turned against heaven. Wakahiko's death triggers funeral and subsequent Ajisukitakahikone episode. Arrow's boomerang curse becomes proverbial for retributive justice.

The Funeral, the Mistaken Identity, and the Mourning SongKojiki, Nihon Shoki

8th century

Wakahiko's family builds a mourning hut (moya) for his funeral. His close friend Ajisukitakahikone (Ajishikitakahikone), who resembles Wakahiko, comes to mourn. Wakahiko's father and wife mistake Ajisukitakahikone for the deceased, saying 'Our lord is not dead.' Insulted that he is mistaken for a corpse, Ajisukitakahikone draws his sword, cuts down the mourning hut, and flees, his shining face illuminating the valley. The episode explains place names and the origin of mourning songs (funeral dirges) recorded in Kojiki. Links Habaya story to etiology of funeral customs and toponyms (e.g., Moyama). Shows social consequences of Wakahiko's death. Arrow itself not active here, but story is inseparable continuation.

The Conflation with Susanoo's Sword Ame no Habakiri in Later TraditionMedieval commentaries, Edo kokugaku, modern popular culture; contrast with Kojiki/Nihon Shoki Susanoo-Yamata no Orochi episode

Heian to present (textual conflation visible from 14th century onward)

In the distinct Susanoo cycle, Susanoo uses a Totsuka-no-Tsurugi called Ame no Habakiri (Heavenly Wing-Cutter) to slay Yamata no Orochi and discovers Kusanagi no Tsurugi inside its tail. Because both objects share haba/haha element and are heavenly weapons, later war tales (Taiheiki), swordsmith traditions, and modern games conflate Habaya (arrow) and Habakiri (sword) as one 'Ame no Habakiri/Habaya' serpent-slaying sword. Primary sources keep them separate: Habakiri is sword, Habaya is arrow, different owners (Susanoo vs Wakahiko). Creates widespread modern misconception that Habaya is Susanoo's sword. Obscures arrow's distinct narrative and leads to misattribution in databases (including Wikidata type 'mythical object'). Scholarly correction requires separating the two.

Ame no Habaya is Susanoo's sword that killed Yamata no Orochi and contained Kusanagi. — Susanoo's serpent-slaying sword is Ame no Habakiri (or Totsuka-no-Tsurugi, Orochi no Aramasa), not Habaya. Habaya is the heavenly arrow used by Ame no Wakahiko to shoot the pheasant Nakime. Kusanagi (Ame no Murakumo) was found inside Orochi's tail after being cut with Habakiri, unrelated to Habaya.

Ame no Habaya and Kusanagi no Tsurugi are the same heavenly weapon. — Habaya is an arrow (with bow), Kusanagi/Ame no Murakumo is a sword found in Orochi's tail. Different type, owner, and narrative. Habaya kills Wakahiko; Kusanagi becomes imperial regalia.

The arrow had magical homing powers and could kill anyone at will. — Primary texts show one specific flight heaven-earth and a conditional curse spoken by Takagi; lethality depends on divine judgment, not autonomous homing. Wakahiko is killed while asleep, not in combat. No evidence of general homing or repeated use.
