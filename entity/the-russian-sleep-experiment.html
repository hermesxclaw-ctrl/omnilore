The Russian Sleep Experiment

Who It Is
Form

The Russian Sleep Experiment names an event rather than a being, and so has no canonical appearance of its own; what it has instead is a set of bodies in progressive states of unmaking, described with the flat precision of an autopsy report. The subjects as the text presents them are not monsters in any conventional visual sense but human beings who have been disassembled, largely by their own hands, and who remain alive and articulate throughout. The narrative specifies that all four survivors had large portions of muscle and skin torn from their bodies, that the exposed bone at their fingertips indicated the wounds were inflicted by hand rather than with teeth, and that the abdominal organs below the ribcage had been removed and arranged on the floor around the still-living bodies, with the digestive tracts visibly continuing to work as they processed the subjects' own consumed flesh. The heart, lungs, and diaphragm are described as remaining in place, the ribs stripped of overlying muscle so that the lungs are visible through the ribcage — an image that is anatomically specific enough to feel documentary and impossible enough to signal fiction to any reader with medical training. Against this deliberately clinical description stands the entirely separate visual tradition that the public actually associates with the story: a black-and-white photograph of a hunched, skeletal humanoid with sunken eyes, protruding brow and cheekbones, and oversized teeth forced forward into a rictus grin. That figure is not described anywhere in the text. It is a photograph of a 2005 Halloween animatronic, and its monochrome presentation is a significant part of its persuasive force, lending an apparent period authenticity that a colour image could not. The gap between the story's own body-horror and the public's mental image of it is one of the more instructive features of the entity: the text's horror is medical and procedural, while the meme's horror is monstrous and facial, and the two have circulated together for over a decade without ever quite matching.

Powers

Sustained wakefulness under stimulant gas The story's premise-level mechanism: an unnamed experimental gas-based stimulant, toxic at high concentration and therefore administered in a sealed chamber with monitored oxygen intake, that abolishes the need for sleep. It is never chemically identified, has no stated developer, and produces no side effects of the sort a real stimulant would; it functions purely as a narrative solvent that dissolves the boundary between waking and something else. Its efficacy is total and its withdrawal is intolerable.

Refusal of death by ordinary means The subjects sustain injuries that the text explicitly frames as incompatible with life and continue to act. One subject's heart is described as beating for a full two minutes after he has bled out to the point of having more air than blood in his vascular system, and he continues to scream, struggle, and repeat the word 'MORE' for three minutes after it stops. Another survives a six-hour abdominal reconstruction without anaesthetic. The surgeon is made to state repeatedly that it should not be medically possible for the patient to be alive — a line whose function is to pre-empt the reader's own objection.

Immunity to sedation and anaesthesia Chemical suppression fails comprehensively. One subject receives more than ten times a human dose of a morphine derivative and continues to fight, breaking a doctor's ribs and arm. Another proves effectively immune to pre-surgical sedative. A paralytic administered for surgery clears the subjects' systems in an abnormally short period. This ability is thematically precise rather than arbitrary: every drug that fails is one that induces unconsciousness, and the entity's single defining trait is that it cannot be made unconscious.

Anomalous strength and oxygen metabolism Oxygen consumption in the sealed chamber during the three silent days registers at the level of five people undertaking heavy strenuous exercise. A restrained subject tears most of the way through a four-inch leather strap while a two-hundred-pound soldier holds the same wrist. Post-mortem examination finds triple the normal level of oxygen in one subject's blood, and nine broken bones, most of them fractured by the force of the subject's own musculature acting against his skeleton.

Self-evisceration as deliberate technique The mutilation is presented not as frenzy but as method. Forensic detail is supplied to establish intent — the destruction of flesh and bone at the fingertips proves the wounds were made by hand rather than by teeth, and the angles indicate that most or all were self-inflicted. The organs are not torn out and discarded but removed intact and fanned out around the living body. The subjects consume their own excised flesh over a period of days while leaving untouched the food rations supplied to them. The purpose, as they later state, is to generate sufficient pain and stimulus to prevent sleep.

Fatal sleep The story's closing rule and its cleanest horror. Once the transformation is complete, sleep is death: a subject monitored on EEG shows normal brainwaves punctuated by inexplicable flatlines resembling repeated brain death, and at the instant his eyes close and his head touches the pillow his trace converts to deep sleep and then flatlines permanently as his heart stops. This retroactively reframes every prior act of self-harm as rational, and converts the subjects from monsters into something closer to the condemned.

Incident Log

At a covert Soviet facility, researchers seal five political prisoners, described as enemies of the state during the Second World War, into a chamber flooded with an experimental sleep-inhibiting gas, having falsely promised them freedom if they remain awake for thirty days. The first five days pass with little complaint, though the subjects' conversation turns steadily toward traumatic material from their pasts. After day five paranoia sets in, the subjects cease speaking to one another, and each begins whispering denunciations of the others into the microphones. On day nine the first subject begins screaming, running the length of the chamber and shouting for three hours until his vocal cords tear; the others ignore him entirely. When a second begins to scream, the two remaining subjects calmly dismantle the books provided to them, smear the pages with their own faeces, and paste them over the portholes. All sound then stops for three days, while oxygen consumption indicates five people in heavy exertion. On the fourteenth morning the researchers break their own protocol and use the intercom, threatening to shoot anyone not lying flat and offering one subject immediate freedom for compliance. A single calm voice replies: 'We no longer want to be freed.'

The chamber is flushed of stimulant gas and filled with fresh air, whereupon three voices immediately begin begging for the gas to be restored. Soldiers entering find one subject dead and four alive in a condition the text declines to call life. The rations after day five are untouched. Flesh from the dead subject's thighs and chest has been forced into the floor drain, blocking it and allowing four inches of fluid to pool. All four survivors have torn away large areas of their own muscle and skin by hand, and their abdominal organs have been removed intact and arranged around them while their digestive tracts continue to process their own consumed flesh. The subjects fight violently against removal: one soldier dies with his throat torn out, another is gravely injured, and five more soldiers die by suicide in the following weeks. One subject's spleen ruptures in the struggle and he bleeds out, unsedatable by ten times a human dose of morphine derivative, his heart continuing for two minutes past exsanguination and his voice for three minutes past that, repeating the single word 'MORE'.

Attempts to surgically restore the subjects' organs founder on their immunity to anaesthesia. The most severely injured subject fights the anaesthetic gas, tearing nearly through a four-inch leather restraint, and dies the instant he loses consciousness; his autopsy finds triple normal blood oxygen and nine bones broken by his own muscular force. The mute subject, unable to object, nods assent to surgery without anaesthetic and lies unreacting through six hours of abdominal reconstruction, with an assisting nurse reporting that his mouth curled into a smile whenever their eyes met. Afterwards he asks for pen and paper and writes two words: 'Keep cutting.' The remaining two undergo the same procedure without anaesthetic, requiring a paralytic only because the surgeon cannot operate through their continuous laughter. Asked why they mutilated themselves and why they want the gas, they give one answer: 'I must remain awake.'

The researchers, having failed the project's stated goals, consider euthanasia, but the commanding officer — a former KGB agent — overrules them and orders the subjects returned to the gas. All three cease struggling at the news and visibly labour to stay awake: one hums continuously, the mute subject strains his legs alternately against his bonds, and the third holds his head off the pillow and blinks rapidly while his EEG shows normal activity punctuated by inexplicable flatlines. A nurse alone sees his eyes close; his trace converts to deep sleep and then flatlines permanently as his heart stops. The commander orders the chamber sealed with the remaining subjects and three researchers inside. One of the named researchers draws his weapon, shoots the commander between the eyes, kills the mute subject, and turns the gun on the last survivor, demanding to know what he is. The subject smiles and answers: 'We are you. We are the madness that lurks within you all, begging to be free at every moment in your deepest animal mind. We are what you hide from in your beds every night. We are what you sedate into silence and paralysis when you go to the nocturnal haven where we cannot tread.' The researcher fires into his heart, and the EEG flatlines as the subject chokes out: 'So... nearly... free...'

Scholarly Debates
Date and authorship of first publication
The story was first posted to the Creepypasta Wiki on 10 August 2010 by a user with the handle 'OrangeSoda'. This is the attribution given by Snopes, news.com.au, LiveAbout, and English Wikipedia, and it is supported by a dated wiki page created on 16 August 2010. ⇌ The story predates that posting by a year, appearing on the Rip747 WordPress blog on 8 August 2009 and on the Bodybuilding.com Misc forum on 20 August 2009 under the handle 'Falconpunch'. If correct, OrangeSoda propagated rather than authored the text. The Rip747 post further claimed the story had been passed on by the poster's brother, which is either evidence of still earlier circulation or a conventional distancing device of the kind the genre routinely employs.
Historical period of the fictional events
The text states the experiment took place 'in the late 1940s', which places it after the end of the Second World War, in the early Cold War period of Soviet consolidation. ⇌ The subjects are described as political prisoners deemed enemies of the state 'during World War II', and Tosha R. Taylor's scholarly treatment describes the story as revealing 'a top-secret effort by Russian scientists in World War II'. Wikipedia categorises it under Works set in the 1940s while also tagging it as an Internet meme introduced in 2009 — itself in tension with the article's own 2010 attribution. The internal inconsistency is unresolved in the source text and has propagated into the secondary literature.
Whether the closing supernatural revelation belongs to the work
The final speech — 'We are you. We are the madness that lurks within you all' — is present in the standard circulating text on both the Creepypasta Wiki and Creepypasta.com, and constitutes the story's thematic resolution, converting a pharmacological horror into a statement about human nature. ⇌ A substantial body of retellings, including several high-view narrations, omit or truncate the passage, and reader commentary frequently identifies it as the story's weakest element on the grounds that it abandons the clinical register that made the preceding text persuasive. On this reading the ending is a late or inferior accretion and the story is stronger without explanation.
Genre classification: urban legend or authored fiction
The story is an urban legend. Wikipedia's opening sentence states it 'has become the basis of an urban legend'; Snopes, LiveAbout, and Dread Central all use the term, and it is justified by the story's circulation as claimed fact, its anonymous transmission, and the persistent public belief in its truth. ⇌ The story is authored short fiction misread as legend. Genuine urban legends mutate substantially in retelling, whereas this text is reproduced near-verbatim across a decade and thousands of reposts — a stability characteristic of a fixed authored work. Wikipedia's own category assignments reflect the tension, filing the story simultaneously under 'Horror short stories', '2010 short stories', 'Russian urban legends', and 'Fakelore'.
The evidentiary status of the associated photograph
The image is definitively a photograph of 'Spazm', an animatronic Halloween prop manufactured by Morbid Enterprises in 2005, as identified by The13thFloor in 2016 and noted directly in Wikipedia's image caption. It postdates neither the story nor depicts anything the story describes. ⇌ A persistent lay reading treats the photograph as documentary evidence of the experiment, with its monochrome presentation and apparent period quality taken as corroboration. This belief survives the debunking and, in practice, drives more of the story's circulation than the text does — an inversion in which the fabricated illustration authenticates the fiction it was never part of.
What We Don't Know

The authorship of the text remains genuinely unknown. Neither 'OrangeSoda' nor the 2009 posters 'Rip747' and 'Falconpunch' have been identified, and no one has ever claimed the work. Whether the 2009 postings represent the original composition or a still earlier source has not been settled by any published investigation. The chain of transmission between the disputed 2009 postings and the 2010 Creepypasta Wiki page has not been reconstructed with archival evidence. Most secondary sources simply repeat the 2010 attribution without addressing the earlier claims, and the Creepypasta.com origins account is the principal source for the 2009 dates and is itself unsourced. The precise moment at which the 'Spazm' photograph became attached to the text is not documented. The October 2013 IReadCreepyPastas narration is the earliest widely cited fusion, but earlier pairings on imageboards are plausible and have not been searched for. No systematic study of the story's non-Anglophone circulation exists. Wikipedia cites a Russian-language DRIVE2 community post, and the Indian stage adaptation and Netflix's Ghoul both indicate substantial South Asian reception, but there is no scholarship mapping how the story is received in Russia itself — where its geopolitical framing would necessarily read very differently. The scholarly literature is thin relative to the story's cultural footprint. Taylor's chapter and the Srivastav and Rai article are the only substantial academic treatments located, and neither takes the text as its primary subject. There appears to be no folkloristic study of the story's belief dynamics comparable to the extensive literature on Slender Man. The 2015 novella adaptation is out of print and its author was not identified in the available reporting; its content and relationship to the source text could not be assessed. No reliable data exists on the prevalence of belief in the story's truth. Assertions that 'many' readers believe it real are repeated across journalism and by Wikipedia but rest on impressionistic evidence — comment threads and search behaviour — rather than survey work.

A way to hold it in your mind

In August 2010 an anonymous user posted a story about five Soviet prisoners kept awake for fifteen days by an experimental gas. Three years later, Snopes felt obliged to publish a formal fact-check — because enough people believed it had actually happened. The photograph that convinced them was of a Halloween prop.
