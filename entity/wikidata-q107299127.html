The Approach

In the twilight of Amenhotep III's magnificent reign, a priest named Simut climbed the ranks of the most powerful religious institution in Egypt, from a humble porter of the god's shrine to the second-highest authority in the cult of Amun. His story, pieced together from scattered artifacts and a lost tomb, offers a rare glimpse into the intricate machinery of priestly power and the individuals who navigated it.

Etymology

Simut (or Samut) is an ancient Egyptian name meaning 'Son of Mut', referring to the goddess Mut, the consort of Amun.

Cultural Ownership

Ancient Egyptian; specifically associated with the priesthood of Amun at Karnak during the 18th Dynasty.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars have suggested that Simut might be identical with other officials named Simut or Samut from the same period, but this is not confirmed. The name is relatively common, and the specific individual is distinguished by his titles.

Description

Appearance

No physical description survives. Depictions in Ramose's tomb show him as a male mourner, but details are not recorded.

Symbols & Iconography

No specific iconography is attested for Simut. As a priest, he would have worn leopard skin and other priestly regalia, but no images confirm this.

Behaviour & Personality

No direct evidence of personality. His career progression suggests ambition and competence, as he rose from a low-ranking wab priest to the second-highest priestly office.

Domains & Habitats

His sphere of activity was the temple complex of Karnak and the wider Theban area. He likely resided in Thebes.

No significant version differences exist; the historical record is consistent, though sparse.

Career advancement under Amenhotep III

Source Inscriptions on objects and tomb chapelCulture Ancient EgyptianPeriod 18th Dynasty, reign of Amenhotep III (c. 1390-1352 BCE)

Summary

Simut began as a wab priest and porter of the god's processional shrine. By Year 20 he was Fourth Prophet of Amun. In Year 34 he was promoted to Second Prophet, likely after the death of Anen, the previous holder.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

The tomb chapel A.24 is lost; no detailed records of its contents exist. The son's name is unknown. No physical description of Simut survives. The exact dates of his appointment as Fourth and Second Prophet are based on regnal years but not exact dates. The relationship with Anen is inferred. Further research could explore museum collections for objects bearing his name.

The scope includes his identity, titles, career, family, tomb, and depictions in other monuments. Sources include academic publications, Egyptological databases, and museum records.
