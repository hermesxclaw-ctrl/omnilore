Aix's domain is the stormy mountain peak, the wild, untamed places where the winds howl and the thunder roars. She is a chthonic figure, associated with the raw, pre-Olympian forces of nature that Zeus must conquer to establish his order.

Form

Aix is described as a she-goat of immense size and terrifying aspect, with a hide that was 'aegis-like' — impenetrable and storm-tossed. Her hair was said to be golden, and her eyes flashed with the light of a storm. She is not depicted in surviving vase paintings, but her iconographic identity is tied to the constellation Capra (the Goat), which is located near the charioteer Auriga.

Powers

Superhuman Strength and Ferocity As the mother of Typhon, the most powerful monster in Greek myth, Aix is possessed of a proportionate level of raw physical power. She is able to physically grapple with Zeus, the king of the gods, and even wound him. All versions She is ultimately defeated by Zeus, who is more powerful. None; her power is implied by her action but not enumerated.

Hide of the Aegis Aix's hide, the 'Aegis', is an impenetrable, storm-blessed skin. When Zeus flays her, he uses the hide to create his iconic shield, the Aegis, which he uses to terrify his enemies and summon storms. This makes Aix the indirect source of one of the most powerful artifacts in Greek mythology. Scholia on the Iliad; later mythographers The power is transferred to Zeus; Aix herself does not wield it. Another myth claims the Aegis was made from the hide of the giant Pallas, not Aix. This is a separate, competing tradition.

Constellation (Capra) After her death (or, in some versions, her apotheosis), Zeus places Aix among the stars as the constellation Capra. This gives her a form of eternal, celestial immortality, a common reward for figures who played a crucial role in the gods' lives. Pseudo-Eratosthenes, Hyginus This is a passive form of existence; she does not actively intervene in mortal affairs. The constellation is also said to be the goat Amalthea, creating the same conflation as the identity issue.

Behavior & Habitat

Aix is a fierce, protective mother. She attacks Zeus not out of malice but out of a maternal instinct to defend her monstrous son, Typhon, during his cosmic battle. She is a creature of the wild, a chthonic force of nature, embodying the untamed power of the storm and the mountain.

Accounts

After the Titanomachy, Zeus battles the final and most terrible threat to his rule: the storm-giant Typhon, born from Gaia and Tartarus. In the scholia tradition, the mother of Typhon is the she-goat Aix. As Zeus fights Typhon, Aix, seeing her son in danger, charges at Zeus and bites the sinews of his hands and feet, crippling him. Typhon then seizes the disabled Zeus, cuts the sinews from his limbs, and hides him in a cave. The god is rescued by Hermes and Aegipan, who restore his sinews, and Zeus returns to defeat Typhon and imprison him under Mount Etna. In his rage, Zeus flays Aix and uses her invulnerable hide to craft the Aegis.

The Aegis is the iconic storm-shield of Zeus (and later Athena). It is described as a terrifying, tasseled, golden shield that produces a deafening roar and a blinding flash when shaken. The scholia and later mythographers explicitly state that this shield is made from the skin of Aix, the mother of Typhon, making her hide the very symbol of divine power and terror.

Content Notes

Zeus, in his rage after being crippled and humiliated by Typhon, flays Aix alive to use her hide as a shield. This is a visceral act of retribution, transforming the body of his enemy into a symbol of his own power.

Flaying is a recurring motif in Greek myth (e.g., Marsyas, Actaeon), often representing the ultimate punishment for hubris or the complete subjugation of a chthonic force by an Olympian one.

Scholarly Debates
Mother of Typhon
Gaia is the mother of Typhon. ⇌ Aix is the mother of Typhon.
Evidence for A: Hesiod, Theogony 306-307.
Evidence for B: Scholia on Homer's Iliad 21.195.
Identity with Amalthea
Aix and Amalthea are the same figure. ⇌ Aix and Amalthea are distinct figures.
Evidence for A: Hyginus, Astronomica 2.13, conflates the two.
Evidence for B: The earliest sources (Hesiod, Callimachus) treat them separately, with Amalthea as the nurturing goat of Zeus's infancy
Origin of the Aegis
The Aegis is made from the hide of Aix. ⇌ The Aegis is made from the hide of the giant Pallas.
Evidence for A: Scholia on Iliad, Catasterismi.
Evidence for B: Scholia on Euripides, Ion 995.
What We Don't Know

No primary vase paintings or archaeological artifacts directly depicting Aix have been identified. The full text of the Hesiodic scholia that detail her attack on Zeus is not fully available in English translation in the public domain; the summary is based on the partial scholia and the Catasterismi. The exact nature of her cult, if any, is unknown. The conflation with Amalthea requires further scholarly analysis to fully untangle the late-antique reception.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Aix as the mythological equivalent of a Grizzly Bear mother in a nature documentary — fiercely protective, utterly terrifying, and entirely willing to take on something much larger and more powerful than herself to defend her cub. But in this case, the 'cub' is a kaiju-sized, fire-breathing storm giant, and the 'hiker' she attacks is the all-powerful king of the gods. She's the tragic antagonist — you understand why she does it, but you also know she has to lose for the story to work. Her posthumous transformation into the Aegis is the ultimate 'if you can't beat them, wear them as a coat' power move.
