Who It Is

Look at her too long — not even at her, but at her reflection — and you are no longer a man; you are a small grey monument in a garden of monuments. The Gorgon is the one image in all of mythology that does not become more reassuring the longer you study it. Confront her at her oldest, on the mid-seventh-century pediment of the temple of Artemis at Corfu: a winged, kneeling, sleepless face turning its enormous glaring eyes straight out of the stone to meet yours, two snakes knotted at her waist, and you are meeting the very thing archaic Greece placed on its shields, its temple roofs, and its coinage to hold the world's evil at arm's length. She is simultaneously the enemy warded off and the ward itself. In the earliest stratum the Gorgons are simply dread monsters — 'hatred of mortal man' in Prometheus Bound — whose presence is lethal to the gaze. They pursue relentlessly (the chase of Perseus being their only full narrative) and make a terrible crying/bellowing sound. Modern reception has progressively recast them as tragic, sympathetic figures: the rape victim, the wronged woman, the guardian of feminine power. Powers
Petrifying gaze

Anyone who looks directly upon a Gorgon — or the severed head of Medusa — is turned to stone. In Hesiod and Apollodorus all three sisters possess the ability; Pherecydes specifies Medusa's face turns men to stone; Pindar calls her severed head 'stony death'. The head retains its power after decapitation.

Limit: The gaze is only lethal to those who actually look at it — Perseus defeats Medusa by viewing her reflection in his mirrored bronze shield. The two immortal sisters cannot be killed. The head's power works on those who meet its eyes (Perseus uses it to turn Polydectes and Atlas to stone).

Immortality of Stheno and Euryale

Of the three sisters, only Medusa is mortal; Stheno ('forceful') and Euryale ('far-roaming') do not grow old and cannot die. They pursue Perseus forever, unable to catch him while he wears Hades' cap of invisibility.

Limit: Immortality does not grant invulnerability to visibility or capture, only to death.

Gorgon cry

The Gorgons emit a shrill, terrible cry or bellow — connected to the noise-root of their name. In the Hesiodic Shield the shriek seems to emanate from Heracles' shield itself. Pindar credits this cry with spurring Athena to invent the flute ('many-voiced song of flutes') to imitate it; Nonnus has Perseus 'listening for no trumpet but Euryale's bellowing'.

Limit: A weapon of terror/awe rather than direct harm; its role is to instill fear and (in Pindar) to inspire music.

Gorgon blood

The blood of a Gorgon has dual, opposing powers: healing and poisoning/lethal harm. Euripides' Ion has Athena give Erichthonius two drops — one 'wards off diseases and nourishes life', the other 'kills, as it is poison from the Gorgon serpents'. Apollodorus attributes to Athena giving Asclepius blood that raises the dead when drawn from the right side but is poison from the left. In Ovid the blood spilled in Libya and the Red Sea generates vipers, the Amphisbaena (a horned dragon-like creature with a snake-headed tail), and the corals of the Red Sea.

Limit: Requires the head or body of a slain/severed Gorgon; the healing-vs-harming polarity is source-dependent (left side poisons, right side heals in Apollodorus).

The severed head as a mobile weapon (gorgoneion)

After Perseus beheads Medusa, the head — retaining its petrifying power — becomes a portable weapon. Perseus uses it to petrify Atlas and King Polydectes, then gives it to Athena, who mounts it on her aegis where it both protects her from any weapon and instills fear in enemies. The image becomes the apotropaic gorgoneion.

Limit: The head must be shown/used toward a viewer to take effect; it does not act autonomously.

Flight via golden wings

The Gorgons are winged and fly — 'winged with snakes for hair' in Prometheus Bound, with 'golden wings, by which they flew' in Apollodorus. Their flight enables the impossible-speed pursuit of Perseus.

Limit: In their one narrative they still cannot overtake Perseus, protected by Hades' invisible cap.

Accounts I
The Beheading of Medusa (the Perseus cycle)Apollodorus, Bibliotheca II.iv.2-3; Hesiod, Theogony; Pindar, Pythian 12; Ovid, Metamorphoses; Aeschylus, Prometheus Bound

c. 700 BC onward; fullest in Apollodorus

King Polydectes of Seriphus, wanting to marry Perseus' mother Danaë, sends the young hero to fetch the Gorgon Medusa's head — an impossible task meant to kill him. Guided by Hermes and Athena, Perseus first visits the Graeae, the Gorgons' sisters who share a single eye and single tooth; he steals both and forces them to reveal the way to certain nymphs. From the nymphs he receives winged sandals for flight and Hades' cap of invisibility; Hermes gives him an adamantine sickle (harpe). Finding the three Gorgons asleep near Oceanus, Perseus approaches Medusa — looking only at her reflection in his mirrored bronze shield so as not to be petrified — and beheads her. From her neck spring the winged horse Pegasus and the golden giant Chrysaor (her children by Poseidon). Stheno and Euryale, who were pregnant-awake and immortal, chase after him, but he escapes unseen under Hades' cap. Perseus uses the severed head to petrify the Titan Atlas and, returning to Seriphus, Polydectes; then he gives the head to Athena, who sets it on her aegis. The Pursuit of the Immortal SistersHesiodic Shield of Heracles 216-237; Pindar, Pythian 12; Apollodorus; Nonnus, Dionysiaca

c. late 7th-mid 6th century BC and later

After Perseus beheads Medusa, her two immortal sisters Stheno and Euryale give chase. The Hesiodic Shield of Heracles, describing images on Heracles' shield, renders the scene vividly: 'The Gorgons, dreadful and unspeakable, were rushing after him, eager to catch him; as they ran on the pallid adamant, the shield resounded sharply and piercingly with a loud noise. At their girdles, two serpents hung down... Upon the terrible heads of the Gorgons rioted great Fear.' They cannot see Perseus because Hades' cap makes him invisible, so the pursuit fails. Euryale's lamenting cry for her dead sister is singled out — Pindar says Athena invented the flute to imitate the shrill, anguished dirge of Euryale's jaws. The immortal Gorgons are confirmed as survivors; their grief-cry becomes, in Pindar at least, the ultimate origin of Greek wind-music (the aulos).

The Transformation of Medusa (Ovid's version)Ovid, Metamorphoses IV.770-803

c. 8 AD

Ovid supplies the origin that later ages made canonical: Medusa was once a beautiful mortal maiden. Neptune (Poseidon) mated with her in the temple of Minerva (Athena). Enraged at the desecration of her sanctuary, Minerva transformed Medusa's glorious hair into living snakes, making her a horror to behold. No earlier Greek source preserves this moralizing backstory; it is a Roman, Ovidian invention that reframes the monster as a punished, tragic victim. Conditions all later feminist and sympathy-driven retellings of Medusa as a rape victim; it becomes the basis of the modern 'victim-blaming' charge against Minerva/Athena.

Accounts II
The Gorgon and the Aegis (Gigantomachy variant)Euripides, Ion 1003-1023; 987ff

c. 412 BC

Euripides records an alternative, less common genealogy and aetiology: in this telling the Gorgon is an offspring of Gaia, brought forth as an ally for her children the Giants, and Athena slays her during the Gigantomachy, then makes her aegis (shield/cuirass) from the Gorgon's skin. The tragic chorus's character Creusa describes weaving 'like an aegis, bordered with serpents' with a 'Gorgon in the middle'. This strands the aegis-Gorgon connection in the divine war rather than the Perseus story. Provides a second, independent aetiology for the Gorgon image on Athena's aegis, distinct from Medusa.

The Petrifying Head as Wandering Weapon (Atlas, the Red Sea, Andromeda)Ovid, Metamorphoses IV; Apollonius Rhodius, Argonautica 4.1515; Lucan, Pharsalia 9.820

c. 8 AD; 3rd century BC; 1st century AD

With Medusa's head in hand, Perseus' journey becomes a tale of casual petrification. Flying past the Titan Atlas in northwest Africa, who tries to drive him away, Perseus shows him the head and turns him to stone — Ovid's aetiology for the Atlas Mountains. Laying the head beside the shore in Ethiopia on his way to rescue and wed Andromeda, Perseus' blood of Medusa drenches seaweed and forms the corals of the Red Sea; drops of the blood in the Sahara and Libya spawn the region's venomous vipers and the Amphisbaena (a two-headed serpent). Makes the severed head a world-shaping weapon and generates multiple natural aetiologies (Atlas Mountains, Red Sea corals, Saharan vipers, the Amphisbaena).

Medusa's Offspring: Pegasus and ChrysaorHesiod, Theogony; Apollodorus; Ovid

c. 700 BC onward

When Perseus beheaded Medusa, from her body sprang two children she had conceived with Poseidon: Pegasus, the winged horse, and Chrysaor, 'he who bears a golden sword', a giant. In Hesiod's Theogony they are born fully armed/adult from the severed neck. Chrysaor later fathers Geryon, the monstrous three-bodied giant whom Heracles slays. The birth-from-decapitation makes Medusa simultaneously a victim and a mother even in death. Links the Gorgon to the wider Greek heroic cosmos — Pegasus becomes the famous steed, Chrysaor generates Geryon for the Heracles cycle.

All three Gorgons were the same, interchangeable monster. — Ancient sources treat them as a distinct triad with a key asymmetry: only Medusa is mortal; Stheno ('forceful') and Euryale ('far-roaming') are immortal and share the petrifying gaze but have no individual narratives beyond the pursuit of Perseus.

Medusa has always been a beautiful woman turned into a monster by rape. — Ovid's rape-and-punishment backstory is a Roman, first-century-AD invention, absent from all earlier Greek sources, which present Medusa as simply a lethal horror. Beautiful depictions predate Ovid (Pindar's 'fair-cheeked Medusa', Polygnotus' sleeping maiden), but the 'she was once a beautiful maiden' moralization is not the original Greek myth.

Looking at Medusa turns you to stone. — The precise mechanic varies. In Apollodorus all three Gorgons petrify by gaze; in Pherecydes it is specifically Medusa's face. The danger is from looking directly — Perseus 'safely' views her via reflection. The severed head also petrifies (Polydectes, Atlas), so the power inheres in the head, not only the living woman.

Medusa was a snake-bodied creature. — No ancient source gives her a snake lower body; the canon is snake-HAIR plus (in Archaic art) winged, sometimes wasp-shaped bodies. The snake-lower-body is a modern invention, popularized by Ray Harryhausen's 1981 Clash of the Titans, which even the medieval/early-modern art does not use.

The Versace logo is just a fashion symbol. — It is a gorgoneion, the ancient apotropaic evil-averting Gorgon head, drawn from the 'Rondanini Medusa'-type beautiful-then-facing image — the same image that guarded Greek shields, temple pediments, and coins. Its fashion use is a (literal) logo-ization of a once-sacred protective device.

The Gorgons are purely Greek, with no outside origins. — The clearest scholarly consensus is that full-bodied Gorgon iconography borrows heavily from Mesopotamian apotropaic demons — above all Lamashtu, shown with animal head, Knielauf (kneeling-running) pose, and snakes — and that the Perseus-beheading tableau parallels Gilgamesh slaying Humbaba (Burkert's 'creative misunderstanding').
