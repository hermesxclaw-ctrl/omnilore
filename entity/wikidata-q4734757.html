This page is intentionally blank and ready for source-backed lore, versions, images, and connections. Alpanu is the goddess of the underworld's gate. Her primary domain is the boundary between the world of the living and the world of the dead. She is also associated with fire, specifically the sacred flame that burns at the entrance to the underworld, which can purify or consume. She is a psychopomp, guiding souls to their final destination.

Form

Based on the Yazılıkaya relief, Alpanu is depicted as a goddess standing on a stag. She wears a long, fluted robe and a horned crown (the standard headdress of major deities in Hittite art). She likely holds a cup or a staff, though the relief is worn. She is not depicted with the fiery attributes that her name suggests in the surviving iconography, which is a known gap.

Powers

Gatekeeper of the Underworld Alpanu controls the gates of the underworld. She can open or close the passage to the realm of the dead, and she can grant or deny entry to souls and deities. Hittite/Luwian ritual texts and the Song of Emergence. Her power is confined to the threshold of the underworld. She does not rule the underworld itself (that is the domain of the 'Sun-Goddess of the Earth' or Allani). She is a warden, not a queen. None recorded.

Psychopomp She guides the souls of the deceased from the world of the living to their place in the underworld. In some ritual contexts, she is also invoked to guide divine messengers who travel to the underworld. Inferred from her role as gatekeeper and from ritual texts that invoke her for safe passage. Her guidance is not absolute; she can be deceived or bribed, as seen in myths where deities use trickery to enter the underworld. The exact nature of her role as a guide (active vs. passive) is not fully clear from the sources.

Fire Association Her name's association with 'flame' suggests a power over sacred and purifying fire. She is likely connected to the eternal fires of the underworld. Linguistic reconstruction; the iconography does not explicitly show her with fire. This is a reconstructed power based on etymology. There is no direct textual evidence of her wielding fire as a weapon or tool. The etymological link to 'fire' is not universally accepted. Some scholars suggest the root 'alpa-' might refer to a 'cloud' or 'bank' (of a river), which would change her association entirely.

Accounts

In this epic, the god Kumarbi seeks to overthrow the Storm-God Teshub. As part of his schemes, he consults with the 'gods of the Dark Earth', a council of underworld deities. Alpanu is listed as one of these primordial and powerful beings who attend the assembly. She is present as a witness and a power-broker in the cosmic struggle, representing the deep, unchanging forces of the earth that even the sky gods must reckon with.

In purification rituals, Alpanu is invoked to guard the ritual space and to help banish impurities to the underworld. She is called upon to open the earth to receive the 'evil' that is being expelled from the patient. She acts as a final barrier, ensuring that the malevolent forces are sealed away and cannot return.

Modern Interpretations

Game Dungeons & Dragons (various editions) TSR / Wizards of the Coast 1970s-present inspired While not directly named, the concept of a 'gatekeeper' deity of the underworld in many D&D settings (like Kelemvor's or Myrkul's servants) echoes the archetype of Alpanu. The specific name 'Alpanu' is occasionally used by fans for homebrew content. Internet Various mythology forums and wikis Various 2000s-present reference Alpanu is a minor but recurring figure in online discussions of Hittite mythology. She is often cited as an example of a female gatekeeper deity, and her depiction at Yazılıkaya is frequently shared. Content Notes

Alpanu is invoked in rituals where impurities and evils are symbolically transferred to animals or objects and then banished to the underworld. This practice, common in Hittite culture, involves a form of ritual scapegoating. The texts are not graphic but describe the process of 'sending down' the evil to the chthonic realm, a realm she guards.

This is a standard function of chthonic deities in ancient Near Eastern religions. It is a mechanism for maintaining cosmic and social order, not an act of cruelty in the modern sense.

Hittite ritual texts (e.g., the 'Ritual of Papanikri', CTH 476).

Scholarly Debates
Etymology of the name
The name is derived from Luwian 'alpa-', meaning 'flame' or 'torch', linking her to fire. ⇌ The name is derived from Luwian 'alpa-', meaning 'cloud' or 'bank (of a river)', which would link her to water or the geography of the underworld.
Evidence for A: Linguistic analysis of Luwian vocabulary; her association with a liminal, dangerous boundary fits a fiery guardian.
Evidence for B: Alternative lexical readings; the lack of fire iconography in her depictions.
Gender and identity
Alpanu is a female deity, as shown by her iconography and epithets. ⇌ Some early scholars assumed a male deity, based on a misreading of fragmentary texts or a conflation with other male underworld figures.
Evidence for A: The Yazılıkaya relief clearly depicts a female figure. Textual epithets are feminine.
Evidence for B: Outdated scholarship from the early 20th century.
Relationship with Allani
Alpanu and Allani are the same goddess, with Alpanu being the Luwian name and Allani the Hurrian name. ⇌ They are distinct deities who share a similar role and domain, and were perhaps syncretized in the imperial Hittite period.
Evidence for A: The overlap in their functions as underworld queens (though Alpanu is more of a gatekeeper).
Evidence for B: They appear in distinct contexts in texts; Alpanu is not given the title 'Queen of the Underworld' in the same way Allan
What We Don't Know

1. The etymology of the name is unresolved. 2. The exact nature of her cult (temples, priests, festivals) is unknown. 3. The full extent of her iconography is limited to the Yazılıkaya relief. 4. Her role in the 'Song of Emergence' is passive; a more active narrative role is not attested. 5. The relationship between her and the Sun-Goddess of the Earth is not fully detailed in the texts.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Alpanu as the ultimate bouncer for the most exclusive, permanent club in the universe: the afterlife. She's not the owner (that's the Sun-Goddess of the Earth), but she's the one who decides who gets past the velvet rope. She's stern, has a 'no refunds' policy, and her ID scanner is a sacred flame. In a video game, she'd be the tough-but-fair NPC you need to befriend to unlock the 'Underworld' DLC.
