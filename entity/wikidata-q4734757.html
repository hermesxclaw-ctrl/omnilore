Who It Is

Before the Hittites ruled Anatolia, before their chariots thundered across the plateau, older gods whispered from the misty mountains of Hattiland — and among them moved Alpanu, a guardian so obscure that even the ancients left only fragments of its name. Imagine a deity invoked not in grand temples but in quiet rooms where someone lay sick, where a family gathered to cleanse their home of unseen evil. Alpanu was the divine bouncer, the celestial banishment spell, the name spoken when the world felt unsafe. Today, this forgotten protector survives in clay tablets that crumbled to dust millennia ago — yet its purpose echoes in every culture that ever invented a charm against the dark.

Alpanu appears in ritual contexts as a protective deity invoked during purification ceremonies. The deity's role seems to have been apotropaic — warding off evil, disease, and malevolent forces. In Hattian tradition, such deities were often called upon during times of crisis or personal distress.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Alpanu is of Hattian origin, possibly related to the Hattian word 'alpa' meaning 'cloud' or 'sky', though this etymology is contested. Some scholars connect it to the Hattian root 'al-' suggesting divine or celestial nature.

Pronunciation — AL-pah-noo

Aliases — Alpan, Ala, Alani, Alp, Alpas

Ownership — Hattian (pre-Hittite) Anatolian culture, later incorporated into Hittite religious pantheon

Powers
Protection and Apotropaic Power

Alpanu was invoked in rituals for protection against evil forces, disease, and misfortune. The deity's power was channeled through ritual specialists (physicians-priests) during purification ceremonies.

Limit: Power was activated through proper ritual performance; incorrect ritual could render the protection ineffective or even dangerous.

Purification Authority

Alpanu participated in cleansing rituals that removed ritual impurity (Hittite: papratar) from individuals, households, or communities.

Limit: Required the mediation of ritual specialists; not directly accessible to ordinary worshippers.

Possible Chthonic Jurisdiction

Some scholars have suggested Alpanu may have had connections to the underworld, given the name similarity with Hurrian Allani, but this is contested and likely a scholarly conflation.

Limit: This is a modern interpretation, not directly attested in ancient sources.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Alpanu is not clearly depicted in surviving art. As a Hattian deity absorbed into Hittite practice, Alpanu may have been represented in anthropomorphic form similar to other Hattian deities, possibly with attributes of a protective or chthonic figure. Some scholars suggest possible association with the bull, a common symbol of Anatolian male deities, though this remains speculative.

Symbols — No definitive symbols are attested. Given the possible etymological connection to 'cloud' or 'sky', celestial imagery may have been associated with Alpanu, but this is speculative. In Hittite ritual texts, the deity is invoked with standard divine determinatives (DINGIR) without specific iconographic attributes.

Domains — Protection, purification, possibly boundaries and thresholds. Some scholars suggest a chthonic aspect given the later conflation with underworld deities, but this is uncertain. The deity may have guarded sacred spaces or served as an intercessor between human and divine realms.

Accounts I
Ritual Invocation of AlpanuHattian-Hittite ritual texts (CTH 733-740)

c. 1650-1200 BCE

In purification rituals, Alpanu was invoked alongside other Hattian deities to cleanse individuals or spaces from ritual pollution. The deity's name appears in incantation lists, suggesting a fixed position in the Hattian divine hierarchy that was preserved when Hittites adopted these rituals. The ritual was believed to restore purity and divine favor, allowing the individual to re-enter normal social and religious life.
