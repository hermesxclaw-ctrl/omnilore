Who It Is

In the summer of 1914, as Japan mourned the death of Emperor Meiji, a young student received a thick letter from his mentor, Sensei. The letter, which began with the words 'By the time this letter reaches you, I'll be gone from this world,' would unravel a confession of betrayal, guilt, and the weight of a modernizing nation. Kokoro, Natsume Sōseki's masterwork, is not merely a novel about a man's secret past; it is a profound meditation on the heart of a generation caught between tradition and change.

Sensei is reclusive, intellectual, and tormented by guilt. He maintains emotional distance but is capable of deep affection. The narrator is earnest, impressionable, and idealistic. K is intense, driven by ideals, and ultimately fragile. The widow and her daughter are portrayed as kind but somewhat enigmatic.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The Japanese word 'kokoro' (心) literally means 'heart,' but also encompasses 'affection,' 'spirit,' 'resolve,' 'courage,' 'sentiment,' and 'the heart of things.' The title's ambiguity reflects the novel's exploration of the inner life of its characters.

Pronunciation — KOH-koh-roh (IPA: [ko̞ko̞ɾo̞])

Aliases — Kokoro: Sensei's Testament (original serial title), こゝろ (original hiragana rendering), こころ (modern hiragana), 心 (kanji)

Ownership — Japan; part of the canon of modern Japanese literature. Kokoro is widely studied in Japanese schools and is considered one of Sōseki's most important works.

Psychological Insight

The novel provides deep psychological insight into the characters, particularly Sensei's guilt and the narrator's coming-of-age. This is not a supernatural power but a narrative strength.

Limit: None; it is a literary technique.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The novel is a prose narrative divided into three parts. It does not contain illustrations. The physical book has varied covers across editions, but the text itself is the primary medium.

Symbols — The title 'Kokoro' itself is a symbol of the heart/mind. The sea at Kamakura represents the initial meeting and the vastness of emotion. The grave of K, which Sensei visits monthly, symbolizes guilt and memory. Emperor Meiji's death and General Nogi's junshi represent the end of an era and the weight of tradition.

Domains — The novel is set in early 20th-century Japan, primarily in Tokyo and Kamakura, with flashbacks to Sensei's rural hometown and his student lodgings. The settings reflect the transition from rural to urban, traditional to modern.

Accounts I
Sensei and IKokoro, Part I

Early 1910s

The narrator meets Sensei at a beach in Kamakura. They become friends, and the narrator visits Sensei in Tokyo, learning of Sensei's monthly visits to a grave. Sensei is reticent about his past but promises to reveal it eventually. Establishes the narrator's admiration and Sensei's mystery.

Accounts II
My Parents and IKokoro, Part II

1912-1913

The narrator returns home to his ill father. Emperor Meiji falls ill and dies, and General Nogi commits junshi. Sensei sends a letter that the narrator reads on the train to Tokyo, revealing that Sensei plans to die. The narrator rushes to Tokyo, and the letter's contents are revealed in Part III.

Sensei's TestamentKokoro, Part III

1910s

Sensei's letter recounts his youth: his parents' deaths, his uncle's betrayal, his friendship with K, their rivalry for the daughter's affection, K's confession, and Sensei's deliberate inaction that leads to K's suicide. Sensei marries the daughter but is consumed by guilt and eventually decides to kill himself, following General Nogi's example. Sensei's suicide, the revelation of his past to the narrator.
