Who It Is

She was called Beautiful Lady because women dropped her juice into their eyes to make their pupils widen into dark, doe-like pools — and died for the vanity. Belladonna, the deadly nightshade, is Europe's most storied poison: a glossy black berry that can kill a child with two fruits, a witches' flying ointment, a surgeon's anesthetic, and a modern eye-drop, all in one plant.

As a plant, behavior is ecological: shade-tolerant, calcicole, found in disturbed soils, woodland clearings, limestone quarries, and hedgerows across central and southern Europe, western Asia and North Africa, naturalized in Britain and North America. In folklore, personified as a beautiful but treacherous woman — alluring, silent, deadly. Herbalist tradition personifies Dwale as 'dwale' (stupor) — inducing sleep, delirium, and death. Not a sentient character in myth but anthropomorphized in modern fiction as seductress-assassin (e.g., X-Men's Bella Donna).

Powers
Anticholinergic toxicity (tropane alkaloids)

All parts contain L-atropine (racemic hyoscyamine), scopolamine (hyoscine) and atropamine. Blocks muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, causing classic toxidrome: 'blind as a bat, mad as a hatter, red as a beet, hot as a hare, dry as a bone' — mydriasis, cycloplegia, tachycardia, hyperthermia, flushed dry skin, urinary retention, delirium, hallucinations, coma.

Limit: Alkaloid concentration varies widely by season, part (roots > leaves > berries), ripeness and geography; not reliably doseable in folk preparations — frequent accidental fatalities. Effects antagonized by physostigmine. No true antidote beyond supportive care.

Mydriasis (pupil dilation)

Dilute juice or atropine instilled in eye paralyzes sphincter pupillae and ciliary muscle, causing prolonged pupil dilation (7-14 days) and loss of accommodation. Basis of cosmetic use and modern ophthalmic examination.

Limit: Causes photophobia and blurred near vision; prolonged use risks angle-closure glaucoma. Cosmetic dose still systemically absorbable.

Deliriant hallucinogenesis (flying ointment)

Scopolamine/hyoscyamine absorbed transdermally or via mucous membranes produces vivid, often erotic or flying sensations, sensation of metamorphosis into animals, and amnesia. Core ingredient of alleged medieval 'flying ointments' (unguentum) with henbane, mandrake and aconite in fat base.

Limit: Highly toxic, dysphoric, not reliably euphoric; many alleged recipes are speculative reconstructions, not attested continuous tradition. No evidence of actual levitation.

Analgesic / antispasmodic (historical medical)

In carefully dosed extracts, belladonna was used for colic, peptic ulcer, Parkinsonian tremor, bradycardia, and as pre-anesthetic to reduce secretions. Atropine remains on WHO Essential Medicines list for bradycardia and organophosphate poisoning.

Limit: Narrow therapeutic index; superseded by safer synthetics for most indications. Historical plasters and liniments often caused poisoning.

Fictional / metaphorical powers (namesake characters)

Characters named Belladonna/Bella Donna are ascribed unrelated superhuman powers by creators: e.g., Marvel's Bella Donna Boudreaux: astral projection, energy blasts, empathic link to voodoo loa; RWBY's Blake Belladonna: superhuman agility, shadow clones via Semblance, Faunus night vision.

Limit: No botanical basis; powers are franchise-specific and non-canonical to plant lore. Must not be merged into plant power set.

Accounts I
The Fate Who Cuts: Atropos and the Naming of AtropaLinnaeus, Species Plantarum (1753) referencing Hesiod, Theogony 904 and Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 1.3.1; Ovid, Metamorphoses

Archaic Greek (8th-5th c. BCE) to 1753

The three Moirai (Fates) — Clotho who spins, Lachesis who measures, and Atropos who cuts the thread of life — embody inevitability of death. Linnaeus, seeking a name for Europe's most inexorably lethal common herb, chose Atropa for the genus, invoking Atropos the 'Unturning' one. The specific epithet belladonna preserved the Italian vernacular. Thus the plant's scientific name literally means 'the unturning beautiful lady' — beauty that severs life. The story is taxonomic, not mythic: no Greek myth features belladonna itself, but the naming story is retold in every flora since 1753. Fixed the association between classical fate and botanical lethality in global nomenclature; cemented the plant's identity as 'deadly' in scientific Latin.

Venetian Eyes: The Cosmetic That Named a PoisonPietro Andrea Mattioli, Commentarii (1554) and later Tomaso Garzoni, La Piazza Universale (1585); modern analysis in Mann, Murder, Magic and Medicine (1992)

16th century, Venice and Florence

Mattioli records that Italian women instilled the juice of solanum furiosum (identified as belladonna) into their eyes to make the pupils appear larger, darker and more alluring — 'per far gli occhi più belli.' The dilated pupil was considered beautiful and seductive in low candlelight. The practice spread among courtesans and noblewomen, giving rise to the name bella donna. Physicians warned that prolonged use dimmed vision and caused blindness, and that children poisoned by berries were often mistaken for victims of witchcraft. Etymology entered all European languages; created enduring femme-fatale archetype linking beauty and poison; practice continued into 19th century until replaced by purified atropine eye drops (still used in ophthalmology).

The Flying Ointment of the WitchesGiambattista della Porta, Magia Naturalis (1558) Book VIII; inquisitorial records from Friuli (Ginzburg, The Night Battles, 1966) and Reginald Scot, Discoverie of Witchcraft (1584)

15th-17th century, Italy, Germany, France

Confessing 'benandanti' and accused witches described an ointment made from belladonna, henbane, mandrake and hemlock boiled into lard or soot, smeared on skin, broomsticks or forks, producing sensations of flying to the Sabbath, transformation into wolves or cats, and carnal congress with spirits. Della Porta, who claimed to test a recipe, reported a subject who fell into deep delirium and awoke claiming to have flown. Modern ethnobotanists argue tropane alkaloids absorbed through thin skin and vaginal mucosa caused the flying delirium, while historians note many confessions were extracted under torture and reflect inquisitors' demonological manuals, not folk practice. Belladonna became archetypal 'witch plant' alongside mandrake; imagery of witches flying on broomsticks is partly traced to belladonna ointment applicators; contributed to witch persecutions.

Accounts II
The Poisoned Beer of the Danes - Macbeth's Belladonna StratagemHector Boece, Historia Gentis Scotorum (1527) and Raphael Holinshed, Chronicles of England, Scotland and Ireland (1577) — the source for Shakespeare's Macbeth

Medieval chronicle (11th c. setting, 16th c. writing)

Boece recounts that during the reign of King Duncan (c. 1030), the Scots, facing invasion by Danes under Sweno, feigned retreat and left behind barrels of ale and bread laced with the juice of 'solanum somniferum' (identified by later commentators as belladonna/dwale). The Danes feasted, fell into deep stupor and madness, and were slaughtered in their sleep by Macbeth and Banquo. Holinshed repeats the tale, and it is the historical kernel Shakespeare adapted — though Shakespeare replaced the botanical trick with the witches' prophecy. 19th-century historians dismissed the story as fabrication, but it demonstrates belladonna's reputation as a tactical poison. Established belladonna as a weapon of war in British historiography; reinforced association with Scotland and with Macbeth lore; cited in toxicology histories as early biological warfare anecdote.

Dwale in the Herball: Gerard, Culpeper and English Folk PoisoningsJohn Gerard, Herball (1597); Nicholas Culpeper, Complete Herbal (1653); coroners' rolls and pamphlets of 17th-century England

16th-17th century England

Gerard warns that 'Dwale causeth sleep, yea even death' and recounts children allured by the sweet black berries in hedgerows who 'have died by eating thereof.' Culpeper, more sympathetic to folk use, lists dwale as cold in the fourth degree, useful for 'hot burning agues' and to 'procure sleep' but warns 'it is not to be taken inwardly without great caution.' Pamphlets report accidental deaths: in 1619, three children in Lincolnshire died after eating 'black cherries of the wood'; in 1670s London, a woman was tried for murdering her husband with belladonna in pottage, though acquitted for lack of chemical proof. The plant's English name dwale (from Old Norse dvala 'trance') captures its soporific danger. Codified English knowledge of belladonna toxicity; contributed to hedgerow folklore warning children away from 'devil's berries'; laid groundwork for 19th-century forensic toxicology.

Belladonna of Sadness: Jeanne and the Devil's Flower (Modern Mythopoesis)Jules Michelet, La Sorcière (1862) → Eiichi Yamamoto & Osamu Tezuka, Kanashimi no Belladonna (Belladonna of Sadness) anime film (1973, Mushi Production)

19th century text, 1973 film (set in medieval France)

Michelet's romantic history La Sorcière reimagines the medieval witch as a peasant woman who, oppressed by feudal lords and the Church, turns to nature and the Devil for empowerment, with belladonna and mandrake as her sacraments. Yamamoto's Belladonna of Sadness adapts this into the story of Jeanne, a newlywed peasant raped by her lord on her wedding night (droit du seigneur), who after being ostracized makes a pact with a phallic Devil spirit, gains erotic and economic power through witchcraft, and is ultimately burned as a witch — her spirit becoming a plague of belladonna-like flowers. The film uses watercolor psychedelia and explicit sexual violence to link belladonna's tropane delirium with female rage and liberation. Became a cult classic of erotic anime and feminist reinterpretation of witchcraft; cemented 'Belladonna' as title for tragic, beautiful, transgressive femininity in global pop culture; revived Michelet's witch archetype for 1970s counterculture.

Belladonna berries are safe if cooked or if you only eat a few — Heat does not destroy tropane alkaloids; even 2-5 raw or cooked berries can be fatal to a child, 10-20 to an adult. Alkaloid content varies unpredictably, so no safe folk dose exists. Modern poison control advises immediate emergency care for any ingestion.

Belladonna was the witches' favorite hallucinogen for pleasant trips — Tropane delirium is typically dysphoric, terrifying, and amnesic — 'mad as a hatter' — with dry mouth, blurred vision, and tachycardia, not euphoric psychedelia. Historical reports describe panic and confusion, not insight. Modern recreational use is extremely dangerous and rarely repeated.

The name Belladonna comes from women using it to poison their husbands undetectably — While belladonna was used in some historical poisonings, the primary etymology is cosmetic pupil dilation (Mattioli 1544), not spousal murder. The poisoner archetype comes from Hichens' 1909 novel Bella Donna and later femme-fatale fiction, retrojected onto etymology.

Atropa belladonna and Amaryllis belladonna are the same plant or close relatives — They are unrelated: Atropa is Solanaceae (nightshade family, Eurasian, toxic berries), Amaryllis is Amaryllidaceae (amaryllis family, South African, pink lily flowers). They share only the specific epithet belladonna (both called 'beautiful lady' for showy appearance).

Belladonna of Sadness is a historical documentary about a real witch named Belladonna — Belladonna of Sadness is a 1973 Japanese animated fiction film adapting Jules Michelet's 1862 romantic history La Sorcière, which itself invented a composite 'witch' archetype. Jeanne is fictional, not a historical person; the film is psychedelic art, not history.
