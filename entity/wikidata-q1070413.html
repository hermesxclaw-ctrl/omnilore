Who It Is

In the darkest hour of the night, when the sun god Ra sails through waters that would doom lesser vessels, a ram-headed figure stands at the helm, looking both forward and back. This is Aken, the ferryman of the Egyptian underworld — the cosmic navigator who ensures the sun can rise again. He is not a god of temples or grand myths, but a figure of essential, quiet power: the one who gets you where you need to go, if you know the right words to say.

Aken is portrayed as a dutiful and essential servant of the solar cycle. He is neither hostile nor benevolent but functions as a necessary component of the sun god's nightly journey. He does not speak in most texts but performs his function silently and reliably. In the Book of the Dead, when identified with the ferryman, he is depicted as somewhat demanding, requiring the deceased to prove their worthiness through knowledge of the boat's parts and the correct spells. Powers
Ferryman of the Underworld

Aken's primary power is his ability to transport the solar barque and worthy souls across the waters of the Duat. He navigates the dangerous waters of the underworld, ensuring the sun god completes his nightly journey and can be reborn at dawn. This power makes him essential to the continuation of the cosmos.

Limit: Aken only ferries those who are deemed worthy. The deceased must know the correct spells and the names of the boat's parts to gain passage. In some texts, he requires payment or proof of worthiness.

Seeing Both Ways

Aken is described as 'He who sees behind him,' suggesting an ability to perceive both the past and the future, the world of the living and the realm of the dead. This dual sight makes him a guardian of thresholds and a being who understands the full scope of existence.

Limit: This ability is described but not elaborated upon. It may be symbolic of his role rather than an actual supernatural power.

Guardian of the Solar Barque

Aken protects the solar barque and its divine passengers from the dangers of the underworld, including the serpent Apep (Apophis) who threatens to devour the sun. His presence ensures the safe passage of Ra through the most dangerous hour of the night.

Limit: Aken's protection is not absolute; the sun god still requires the assistance of other deities, including Set and the crew of the barque, to defeat Apep.

Accounts I
The Fifth Hour of the NightThe Amduat

New Kingdom (circa 1550-1295 BCE)

In the 5th hour of the night, the solar barque enters the most dangerous waters of the underworld. Aken, the ferryman, guides the barque through these waters, navigating past the caverns where the enemies of Ra are punished. The text describes Aken as 'He who sees behind him,' suggesting his ability to navigate both forward and backward through the underworld's treacherous currents. The barque passes through the realm where the damned are punished, and Aken ensures the sun god's safe passage. The successful navigation of the 5th hour ensures the sun can continue its journey and be reborn at dawn. This hour represents the deepest point of the sun's journey into darkness before its rebirth.

Accounts II
The Ferryman's TestThe Book of the Dead, Spell 99

New Kingdom through Ptolemaic period (circa 1550 BCE - 30 BCE)

The deceased approaches the waters of the underworld and must cross to reach the afterlife. The ferryman (identified in some versions as Aken, in others as Hraf-haf) demands that the deceased prove their worthiness. The deceased must know the names of every part of the ferry boat, from the mooring posts to the rudder. The spell provides the correct answers, allowing the deceased to cross. This text emphasizes the importance of knowledge and preparation for the afterlife journey. Those who pass the test cross safely to the afterlife. Those who fail are trapped on the shores of the underworld, unable to reach the fields of the blessed.

The Ferryman of the Book of GatesThe Book of Gates

New Kingdom (circa 1550-1295 BCE)

In the Book of Gates, Aken appears at the 5th gate, where the solar barque must pass through a series of gates guarded by various deities. Aken's role is to ferry the barque through the waters of the gate, ensuring the sun god can continue his journey. The gate scenes emphasize the dangers of the underworld and the need for divine protection. The successful passage through the gates ensures the sun's continued journey and the possibility of resurrection for the blessed dead.

Aken is the Egyptian equivalent of the Greek Charon. — While both are ferrymen of the underworld, the comparison is imprecise. Charon ferries the dead across the rivers of the Greek underworld, while Aken's primary role is to ferry the solar barque. The ferrying of the dead is a secondary role in Egyptian texts, and the ferryman of the dead is not consistently identified as Aken.

Aken is the same god as Aker. — While both are associated with the underworld and the solar journey, they are distinct deities in most scholarly interpretations. Aken is the ferryman with a ram's head, while Aker is the two-headed lion who guards the horizons.

Aken was a major god with his own cult and temples. — Aken was a minor deity who appears primarily in funerary texts. There is no evidence of a dedicated cult or temple to Aken. His importance lies in his role in the solar journey, not in widespread worship.

Aken ferries all souls to the afterlife. — In Egyptian texts, only the worthy dead are ferried to the afterlife. The unworthy are punished in the underworld or destroyed. The ferryman's role is selective, not universal.
