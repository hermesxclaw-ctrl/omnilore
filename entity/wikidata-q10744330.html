The Approach

In the heart of Vietnam, a divine eye watches over a faith that seeks to unite the world's religions into one cosmic symphony. Cao Đài, the 'Highest Power,' emerged in the 1920s as a syncretic revelation, promising a path to universal salvation through the convergence of Buddhism, Christianity, Taoism, and Confucianism. But who is this deity, and how did a small, colonial-era movement grow into a global faith with millions of followers?

Identity

Cao Đài

Pronunciation kow dai (approximately 'kow' as in 'cow' with a rising tone, 'dai' as in 'dye' with a falling-rising tone)

Known As

Etymology

The name 'Cao Đài' literally means 'high tower' or 'high palace' in Vietnamese, often interpreted as 'Highest Power' or 'Supreme Being.' It is also an epithet for God in the Cao Đài faith.

Cultural Ownership

Cao Đài is the supreme deity of the Cao Đài religion, founded in Vietnam in the 1920s. The faith is deeply rooted in Vietnamese culture and has since spread to other countries with Vietnamese diaspora.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars debate the relationship between Cao Đài and traditional Vietnamese folk beliefs, as well as the extent to which Cao Đài is a distinct religion or a syncretic movement. There is also discussion about the influence of French colonialism and theosophy on the faith's formation.

Description

Appearance

Cao Đài is often depicted as a large Divine Eye, representing God's omnipresence and omniscience. The eye is typically shown within a triangle, symbolizing the three teachings (Buddhism, Taoism, Confucianism) or the three realms (heaven, earth, and hell).

Symbols & Iconography

The Divine Eye is the central symbol of Cao Đài. It appears on altars, temples, and religious regalia. The color scheme often includes blue (representing the universe), red (representing humanity), and yellow (representing the divine). The left eye is used because the left is considered the side of the heart and spirit.

Behaviour & Personality

Cao Đài is described as a loving, merciful, and just deity who communicates with humanity through spirit mediums. He is believed to have revealed the Cao Đài doctrine to establish a new era of universal salvation, emphasizing moral living, vegetarianism, and the unity of all religions.

Domains & Habitats

Cao Đài is believed to reside in the highest heaven, beyond all other deities. The Holy See in Tây Ninh is considered His earthly sanctuary, where His presence is especially strong.

Version Differences

There are various branches of Cao Đài, each with slightly different interpretations of the deity's nature and messages. Some emphasize the role of Cao Đài as the Jade Emperor (Ngọc Hoàng), while others focus on His universal aspect.

Stories & Myths

The Foundation of Cao Đài

Source Cao Đài oral tradition and religious textsCulture VietnamesePeriod 1920s

Summary

In 1920, a group of Vietnamese civil servants began holding seances in Saigon. They received messages from a spirit identifying itself as Cao Đài, who revealed the new religion's doctrine. The first official revelation was received on Christmas Eve 1925, and the religion was formally established in 1926.

Participants

Ngô Văn Chiêu

Phạm Công Tắc

Cao Quỳnh Cư

and other early mediums

Consequences

The Cao Đài religion was founded, growing rapidly in southern Vietnam, and later became a significant political and social force.

Variants

Some accounts emphasize the role of Ngô Văn Chiêu, who later founded a separate branch, while others focus on the collective leadership.

The Divine Eye Revelation

Source Cao Đài religious texts and oral traditionCulture VietnamesePeriod 1920s-1930s

Summary

Cao Đài revealed Himself to the mediums as a Divine Eye, symbolizing His all-seeing nature. This symbol became the central icon of the faith, used in temples and personal worship.

Consequences

The Holy See Construction

Source Cao Đài history and temple recordsCulture VietnamesePeriod 1933-1955

Summary

The Holy See in Tây Ninh was built as the global center of Cao Đài. Its architecture blends Eastern and Western styles, with intricate carvings and a grand prayer hall. The construction was a collective effort of the faithful.

Participants

Cao Đài followers

Architects and artisans

Consequences

The Holy See became a pilgrimage site and the administrative headquarters of the Cao Đài Church.

Variants

Different branches have their own temples, but the Tây Ninh Holy See is the most prominent.

Political involvement and persecution

Source Historical accounts of Cao Đài and Vietnam

Description

During the 20th century, Cao Đài was involved in Vietnamese politics, sometimes clashing with the French colonial authorities and later with the South Vietnamese government. Some Cao Đài leaders cooperated with the Japanese during WWII, and the religion had its own army. After the Vietnam War, the communist government suppressed the religion, leading to persecution of its followers.

Scholarly Context

Scholars note the complex role of Cao Đài in Vietnamese nationalism and the conflicts that arose from its political ambitions.

Colonial and cultural appropriation

Source Academic critiques of Cao Đài

Description

Some critics argue that Cao Đài's syncretism was a reaction to French colonialism and theosophical influences, and that it appropriated elements from other religions without full understanding. However, this is a matter of interpretation.

Scholarly Context

This view is debated; many scholars see Cao Đài as an authentic Vietnamese response to modernity.

Contradictions & Disputes

Nature of Cao Đài

Position A

Cao Đài is a distinct, new deity revealed in the 20th century.

Position A — evidence

The unique name and specific revelations given to Cao Đài mediums.

Position B

Cao Đài is the same God worshipped in other religions, merely re-revealed.

Position B — evidence

Cao Đài texts state that He is the same God who sent Buddha, Jesus, etc.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars describe Cao Đài as a syncretic religion that incorporates elements from other faiths, but the deity is considered unique to Cao Đài.

Origin of the revelation

Position A

The seances were genuine divine communications.

Position A — evidence

The rapid growth and sincere belief of followers.

Position B

The seances were a product of human imagination or manipulation.

Position B — evidence

Lack of scientific evidence for supernatural communication.

Scholarly Consensus

Scholars typically treat the seances as religious experiences, not as objective events, and do not take a position on their supernatural validity.
