Who It Is

There is a woman at the bottom of the Arctic sea, and she is missing her fingers. The phalanges that once curled around a kayak's gunwale were chopped away, joint by joint, by the father who threw her overboard to save his own skin — and every severed finger sprouted a seal, a walrus, a whale, so that the meat that keeps the whole Inuit world alive literally grew from the body of a betrayed daughter. That is Sedna, the Sea Woman, a deity who is not gentle and was never meant to be. She is the reason every hunter checks his harpoon, the reason every mother teaches her child to respect the seal, and the reason that when a community starves, it is forced to ask not 'what did we do' but 'which of us broke the taboo.' Her myth is the Arctic's starkest lesson: that survival is not a gift from heaven but a debt owed to a wounded, fingerless, endlessly forgiving-because-she-never-forgets power b

Sedna is everything the Arctic abundance ethic demands of a sea-mistress: implacable, vengeful when insulted, and strictly transactional. She is not generous out of love; she releases the seals and whales when the taboos (tirigusuusiit, the things-to-avoid) are kept and the community confesses, and she withholds them into famine when they are broken. She is a figure of fear and respect rather than warmth. Powers
Sovereignty over Sea Mammals

Sedna is the mistress and arbiter of the marine food supply: ringed seals, bearded seals, walruses, whales, and (by extension) the fish and other sea creatures the Inuit depend on. She decides whether they offer themselves to hunters. When angry she withholds them, causing famine; when placated she releases them.

Limit: Her power is conditional and reciprocal, bound by human conduct. If the community obeys the taboos (tirigusuusiit / maligait / piqujait — things to avoid, to follow, to do) and confesses breaches, she will release the animals. She is susceptible to shamanic intercession and can be soothed, begged, or fought by a powerful angakkuq.

Rulership of the Underworld (Adlivun)

Sedna is the ruler of Adlivun, the underworld beneath the land and sea where the spirits of the departed reside. Souls are brought there by the psychopomps Pinga and Anguta, purified, and after roughly a year proceed to Quidlivun / Qudlivun in the Land of the Moon for eternal rest. She is described as imprisoning souls as part of this preparation.

Limit: She is not a moral judge of the dead in most accounts; she houses and prepares them. In some Greenlandic-variant texts Anguta (not Sedna) torments unworthy souls by pinching them in the land of the dead.

Command over Storms and Weather at Sea

When Sedna is offended, she can raise storms, turbulent seas, and dangerous ice that make hunting impossible and strand or sink travelers. In the myths, this is how the angry bird-husband's allies (or a summoned sea-spirit) attack the kayak bearing Sedna and her father.

Limit: The storms are a response to a specific transgression — usually Sedna's abduction, her father's betrayal, or a broken taboo — not random malice.

Origin of Sea Mammals (Creatrix from her own body)

Her severed finger joints — first the tips, then the second knuckle, then the last — fall into the sea and become the species the Inuit hunt: ringed seals, walruses, whales, and (variously) the different seal species. In the giant-variant she is likewise dismembered at sea to create the food animals.

Limit: The act is done to her (by her father or the community), not by her will — she is the sacrifice whose body becomes food, not an active creator-goddess.

Focus of the Shamanic Journey (Angakkuq intercession)

Sedna is the primary spiritual destination of Inuit shamanism. To relieve famine, the angakkuq travels in spirit down through a barrier (variously a bottomless pit, a whirling wheel of ice, a cauldron of boiling water) to her sea-floor home, combs and washes her louse-ridden hair, and either begs or fights with her for the release of the animals.

Limit: Only a strong angakkuq can survive the journey. The rite is communal: it ends in public confession of the taboos broken and a celebration. The shaman's personal spirit-guide (tuurngaq) protects them.

Accounts I
The Origin of the Sea Mammals (the standard myth)Collected by Franz Boas (Baffin Island), Knud Rasmussen (Igloolik), and others

Pre-contact; recorded late 19th / early 20th century

In the most widespread version, Sedna is a young woman who rejects the suitors her father finds. A mysterious man — usually a bird-spirit, most commonly a fulmar, petrel, or raven, though some versions make him a dog — wins her father's favour with a promise of plenty and carries her away. On arrival the 'land of plenty' proves to be a bleak, barren place and the husband cruel or poor. Hearing Sedna cry, her father sets out in his kayak to rescue her. The bird-husband (or, in an extended variant, the allies he summons through a sea-spirit) raises a great storm to destroy the kayak. In terror the father throws Sedna overboard; she clings to the gunwale; he chops off her fingers joint by joint, first the tips, then the second knuckle, then the last. Each severed joint becomes a sea creature — ringed seals, walruses, whales — and Sedna sinks to the bottom of the sea, where she rules the marine realm. Sedna the reluctant bride and the dog-husbandCollected by Franz Boas (eastern Arctic and Labrador)

Pre-contact; recorded late 19th century

In this variant Sedna is dissatisfied with every husband her father proposes and instead marries a dog (or a man who is a dog in disguise). Their children are mixed human/animal. The father is so enraged — the dog being an improper and possibly bestial match — that he throws Sedna into the sea; when she clings to the boat he severs her fingers, which become the first seals, and she becomes a mighty sea goddess. The offspring of the union are said in some tellings to be the ancestors of the Qallunaat (the pale-skinned others / Europeans) and of other peoples, an aetiology of human diversity. Explains the origin of different peoples (including Europeans) and of the first seals; grounds Sedna's sea-goddess status.

Accounts II
The shaman's descent to comb her hairRecorded by Diamond Jenness (Copper Inuit) and Knud Rasmussen (Igloolik); corroborated in modern Inuit-religion scholarship (Merkur, Kleivan & Sonne)

Pre-contact; documented early 20th century and in the annual rite

Faced with hunger, the community summons its angakkuq to descend to Adlivun beneath the sea. The journey demands passing terrifying barriers, and the shaman is aided by a tuurngaq (personal spirit-guide). Reaching Sedna's home, he must clean a matted tangle of hair and sea-filth — she lacks fingers and cannot do it herself — then plead with, apologise for, and sometimes physically contend with her until she agrees to free the animals. A successful descent is followed by communal confession of the taboo-breakers and a feast. Rasmussen and Jenness both record that a failed or feared journey meant starvation and could be fatal for the shaman. The rite restores cosmic equilibrium, reopens the hunt, and re-binds the community through shared confession; it is the central ritual act of Inuit shamanism.

Nuliayuk the mistreated orphan (Netsilik)Knud Rasmussen, Fifth Thule Expedition fieldnotes (1931); cited in the Inuit-religion scholarship (Rasmussen 1965)

Pre-contact; documented early 20th century

In the Netsilik telling, Nuliayuk begins not as a princess but as an unwanted orphan girl the community despises. Seeking to be rid of her, the people try to drown her, chopping off her fingertips as she clings on. The severed joints become seals and walruses. Eventually Nuliayuk marries a sculpin and takes up residence in the sea, where she commands all the sea mammals and must be placated like the other versions of the Sea Woman. This variant stresses communal cruelty (not a single father's act) and reframes Sedna as the voiceless victim of the group's callousness whose remembered power protects and now determines the hunt. Positions Sedna as a figure of communal accountability and resilience; explains the origin of marine mammals and the stakes of mistreating the vulnerable.

Sedna is a 'mother goddess' or fertility figure. — She is the mistress of sea mammals and the ruler of a marine underworld, but she is not a nurturing life-giver in a fertility-goddess sense; she is a strict, vengeful enforcer of taboo and necessity. Her 'mother' epithets concern the provision of food from her own severed flesh, not fecundity.

Sedna is a mermaid. — In the attested traditions Sedna is a woman who lives beneath the sea; the mermaid form (fish tail replacing legs) is a modern artistic and media convention (e.g. the 1988 Qaqaq Ashoona carving and encyclopaedia illustrations), not a ubiquitous traditional claim.

Sedna is a demon or the Inuit equivalent of the Christian devil. — This is a missionary-projection. Sedna is a powerful, sometimes wrathful spirit, but the pre-Christian tradition presents her as a regulator of the food supply and a ruler of the underworld, not an evil being. Christian-coloured early accounts misread her.

The Sedna story is a single, fixed tale. — It is a family of related stories with substantial regional and personal variation (husband's identity, father's fate, finger counts, orphan-vs-bride framing, giant-vs-woman form). The early ethnographic record sometimes fixed one version as 'the' myth.
