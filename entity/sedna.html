Sedna

My father cut off my fingers and threw me into the sea. Now every seal, every whale, every walrus is a piece of me. You want to eat? Then come down here and comb my hair.

Who It Is

Sedna is the Inuit goddess of the sea and marine animals — one of the most important and formidable deities in the Arctic spiritual world. She rules over all sea mammals (seals, walruses, whales) from her undersea domain, and her mood determines whether hunters find food or face starvation. Sedna is not a benevolent mother goddess in the conventional sense; she is a terrifying, often wrathful figure, reflecting the harsh reality of Arctic survival where the sea provides life but also deals death.

Physically, Sedna is depicted differently across Inuit regions but consistently with grotesque, tragic elements. In the most widespread version, she is a giant woman with long, tangled hair covering her face, living at the bottom of the sea. She has no fingers — they were severed in the tragedy that made her a goddess. Her fingerless state means she cannot comb her own hair, which grows ever more tangled and dirty, mirroring the accumulation of human transgressions. In some depictions, sea creatures emerge from her severed finger stumps, and her lower body is fish-like or seal-like, reflecting her underwater existence.

The myth of Sedna's origin varies across the Arctic, but the core narrative is consistent. Sedna was a beautiful Inuk maiden whose father tried to marry her off against her will. In one common version, a bird spirit (a fulmar or petrel) disguised as a handsome hunter wooed and married her, taking her to a barren, miserable island. When her father came to rescue her by kayak, the bird spirit pursued them, creating a terrible storm. To save himself, Sedna's father threw her overboard. She clung desperately to the kayak's edge, but her father chopped off her fingers, one joint at a time. As they fell into the sea, her fingers transformed into the first seals, walruses, and whales. Sedna sank to the bottom, where she became the goddess of the sea.

Sedna's role in Inuit spiritual life is complex and central. She is the keeper of the sea animals, and when humans violate taboos or act disrespectfully, their transgressions accumulate as filth in Sedna's hair. When her hair becomes too tangled, she withholds the sea animals, and the hunters find no game. The shaman (angakkuq) must then undertake a dangerous spiritual journey to Sedna's undersea realm to appease her — combing her hair, cleaning her, and confessing the community's transgressions. Only then does she release the animals, ensuring the community's survival.

Sedna's psychology is important: she is not an evil being but a profoundly wronged one, whose trauma and anger hold the community hostage. The shaman's journey to Sedna is both a spiritual and psychological exercise — an acknowledgment that the suffering of the marginalized (the daughter thrown overboard, the woman abused) must be addressed for the community to thrive. In this sense, Sedna represents the suppressed feminine rage that, when unacknowledged, chokes off life itself.

Sedna's worship has persisted through centuries of colonization and Christianization in the Arctic, though it has undergone transformation. The dwarf planet Sedna, discovered in 2003 and named after the goddess, honors the mythological figure with a celestial body — fitting for the cold, distant, mysterious realm that Sedna inhabits.

Form

In the attested traditions Sedna is primarily a giant or full-sized woman, most often imagined at the bottom of the sea. Her defining physical marker is the absence of fingers: the joints were severed by her father (or, in the Netsilik variant, by the community) and each fallen joint became a sea mammal, so she is fingerless and cannot comb her own hair. Invisible-to-laypeople in life, she is revealed to the angakkuq on the sea floor with matted, louse-ridden hair tangled with debris when she is angry; it is the shaman's task to comb and wash it. The mermaid-like image — a woman's head and torso tapering into a marine mammal's tail — is chiefly an ARTISTIC convention in carvings, illustrations, and modern media (e.g. the 1988 Qaqaq Ashoona figure and the folklore article's own description), not a consistently attested traditional claim; many accounts describe her simply as a woman living beneath the sea.

Powers

Sovereignty over Sea Mammals Sedna is the mistress and arbiter of the marine food supply: ringed seals, bearded seals, walruses, whales, and (by extension) the fish and other sea creatures the Inuit depend on. She decides whether they offer themselves to hunters. When angry she withholds them, causing famine; when placated she releases them. Pan-Arctic: northern Alaska, Canada, eastern Greenland Her power is conditional and reciprocal, bound by human conduct. If the community obeys the taboos (tirigusuusiit / maligait / piqujait — things to avoid, to follow, to do) and confesses breaches, she will release the animals. She is susceptible to shamanic intercession and can be soothed, begged, or fought by a powerful angakkuq. Accounts differ on the mechanism of withholding: some say she keeps the animals in the basin of her qulliq oil lamp; others that they are in her house, or that she sends them out from her own body. The Caribou Inuit deny the Sea Woman figure entirely and assign caribou control to Sila and Pinga.

Rulership of the Underworld (Adlivun) Sedna is the ruler of Adlivun, the underworld beneath the land and sea where the spirits of the departed reside. Souls are brought there by the psychopomps Pinga and Anguta, purified, and after roughly a year proceed to Quidlivun / Qudlivun in the Land of the Moon for eternal rest. She is described as imprisoning souls as part of this preparation. Central Arctic (Igloolik, Netsilik); Adlivun cosmology recorded by Boas and later ethnographers She is not a moral judge of the dead in most accounts; she houses and prepares them. In some Greenlandic-variant texts Anguta (not Sedna) torments unworthy souls by pinching them in the land of the dead. Early Christian-influenced accounts recast her as a demonic ruler of a hell-like realm; the pre-Christian picture is of a caretaker/gatekeeper rather than a punisher.

Command over Storms and Weather at Sea When Sedna is offended, she can raise storms, turbulent seas, and dangerous ice that make hunting impossible and strand or sink travelers. In the myths, this is how the angry bird-husband's allies (or a summoned sea-spirit) attack the kayak bearing Sedna and her father. Coastal Inuit (Labrador, Greenland), and the storm episode central to most versions of the myth The storms are a response to a specific transgression — usually Sedna's abduction, her father's betrayal, or a broken taboo — not random malice. In the Baffin Island variant her parents blame her for a storm and throw her overboard, inverting who 'causes' the weather.

Origin of Sea Mammals (Creatrix from her own body) Her severed finger joints — first the tips, then the second knuckle, then the last — fall into the sea and become the species the Inuit hunt: ringed seals, walruses, whales, and (variously) the different seal species. In the giant-variant she is likewise dismembered at sea to create the food animals. Pan-Arctic; the central aetiological act of the creation myth The act is done to her (by her father or the community), not by her will — she is the sacrifice whose body becomes food, not an active creator-goddess. Which fingers become which animal varies: some versions name only seals; others include walruses and whales. Some versions have her cut her own fingers off; most have a male relative cut them.

Focus of the Shamanic Journey (Angakkuq intercession) Sedna is the primary spiritual destination of Inuit shamanism. To relieve famine, the angakkuq travels in spirit down through a barrier (variously a bottomless pit, a whirling wheel of ice, a cauldron of boiling water) to her sea-floor home, combs and washes her louse-ridden hair, and either begs or fights with her for the release of the animals. Pan-Arctic; the annual Sedna ceremony of the Central Inuit recorded by Rasmussen and described in the modern Inuit religion scholarship Only a strong angakkuq can survive the journey. The rite is communal: it ends in public confession of the taboos broken and a celebration. The shaman's personal spirit-guide (tuurngaq) protects them. Descriptions of the obstacles, of whether the shaman who accompanies him fights a guardian figure (sometimes Sedna's father, sometimes a hostile spirit), and of whether the shaman combs or merely smooths the hair, vary by region.

Accounts

In the most widespread version, Sedna is a young woman who rejects the suitors her father finds. A mysterious man — usually a bird-spirit, most commonly a fulmar, petrel, or raven, though some versions make him a dog — wins her father's favour with a promise of plenty and carries her away. On arrival the 'land of plenty' proves to be a bleak, barren place and the husband cruel or poor. Hearing Sedna cry, her father sets out in his kayak to rescue her. The bird-husband (or, in an extended variant, the allies he summons through a sea-spirit) raises a great storm to destroy the kayak. In terror the father throws Sedna overboard; she clings to the gunwale; he chops off her fingers joint by joint, first the tips, then the second knuckle, then the last. Each severed joint becomes a sea creature — ringed seals, walruses, whales — and Sedna sinks to the bottom of the sea, where she rules the marine realm. In several versions the father drowns and becomes the ruler of the land of the dead or a servant of his daughter.

In this variant Sedna is dissatisfied with every husband her father proposes and instead marries a dog (or a man who is a dog in disguise). Their children are mixed human/animal. The father is so enraged — the dog being an improper and possibly bestial match — that he throws Sedna into the sea; when she clings to the boat he severs her fingers, which become the first seals, and she becomes a mighty sea goddess. The offspring of the union are said in some tellings to be the ancestors of the Qallunaat (the pale-skinned others / Europeans) and of other peoples, an aetiology of human diversity.

Faced with hunger, the community summons its angakkuq to descend to Adlivun beneath the sea. The journey demands passing terrifying barriers, and the shaman is aided by a tuurngaq (personal spirit-guide). Reaching Sedna's home, he must clean a matted tangle of hair and sea-filth — she lacks fingers and cannot do it herself — then plead with, apologise for, and sometimes physically contend with her until she agrees to free the animals. A successful descent is followed by communal confession of the taboo-breakers and a feast. Rasmussen and Jenness both record that a failed or feared journey meant starvation and could be fatal for the shaman.

In the Netsilik telling, Nuliayuk begins not as a princess but as an unwanted orphan girl the community despises. Seeking to be rid of her, the people try to drown her, chopping off her fingertips as she clings on. The severed joints become seals and walruses. Eventually Nuliayuk marries a sculpin and takes up residence in the sea, where she commands all the sea mammals and must be placated like the other versions of the Sea Woman. This variant stresses communal cruelty (not a single father's act) and reframes Sedna as the voiceless victim of the group's callousness whose remembered power protects and now determines the hunt.

Content Notes

At the core of the myth, Sedna's father (Anguta) throws his daughter overboard a kayak, cuts off her fingers joint by joint as she clings to survive, and lets her drown so the boat will not founder. The marriage that precipitates it is coercive: in one version the father trades her to the bird-man for fish and gives her a sleeping potion. The Netsilik orphan variant reframes the abusers as the whole community attempting to drown a despised child.

Read by anthropologists (e.g. Laugrand, Oosten) as an aetiology of the harsh, taboo-governed Arctic subsistence contract rather than as a literal historical event; it encodes the cost of survival and the consequences of violating kinship and respect norms.

Sedna (mythology); Boas; Rasmussen

The severed finger joints becoming seals, walruses, and whales is a body-horror foundation myth: the food supply literally grows from a mutilated daughter's flesh. The image is retained vividly in Inuit art (e.g. Qaqaq Ashoona's 1988 carving of her with cut hands and a fish tail).

Body-part-to-game is a known cosmogonic pattern cross-culturally (compare the dismemberment themes across Arctic and circumpolar traditions); it is not an idiosyncrasy.

Sedna (mythology); Inuit art tradition

European missionaries and explorers, reading Inuit religion through a Christian lens, characterised Sedna as a demon or the devil, and used such characterisations to justify missionising and colonial-era dismissal of Inuit cosmology. The earliest written accounts are therefore filtered through this bias, and the Inuit world's religion was substantially displaced by Christian conversion by the mid-20th century, with suppression of angakkuit and their rites.

Modern scholars treat the early record critically, recognising the missionary perspective that coloured observation; the 1970s onward saw Inuit-led cultural revitalisation.

Inuit religion article; historical ethnographic record

Sedna's role as an implacable enforcer who visits famine, storms, and death on communities that breach taboos frames the deity as one ruled by necessity and vengeance rather than benevolence — a theodicy of Arctic survival and environmental dependence.

Positions Sedna as a figure of accountability: the community's own misconduct (cruelty to the orphan, broken taboos, ritual pollution) returns as hunger. Patton treats her in the 'ritual pollution' framework of marine pollution displacement (2007).

The husband is a bird-spirit (fulmar / petrel / raven). ⇌ The husband is a dog.

Evidence for B: Boas's eastern-Arctic and Labrador accounts.

The father drowns and becomes the ruler of the land of the dead. ⇌ The father survives and lives as Sedna's servant at the bottom of the sea (or is a creator-supreme-being in Greenlandic myth).
Evidence for A: Some Boas-collected versions.
Evidence for B: Central Arctic versions and the Greenlandic Anguta tradition.
Sedna's physical form
Sedna is a giant woman. ⇌ Sedna is an ordinary-sized woman (or a giant in the Greenlandic 'hungry daughter' telling and normal in others).
Evidence for A: Some early accounts describe a giant who attacks her parents.
Evidence for B: Most accounts describe a normal woman; the mermaid tail is a later artistic (and encyclopaedic-illustration) convention.
Nature of her power toward humanity
Sedna is a benign provider who withholds animals only in response to broken taboos. ⇌ Sedna is a wrathful, vengeful deity who must be constantly propitiated.
Evidence for A: Accounts stressing her role as provider and the necessity of correct ritual.
Evidence for B: Accounts stressing her anger, storms, and the fear she inspires.
The Caribou Inuit absence of the Sea Woman
A Sea Woman / sea-mother figure is universal across Inuit groups. ⇌ The Caribou Inuit, living inland, have no Sea Woman at all and instead attribute caribou control to Sila and Pinga.
Evidence for A: Pan-Arctic records of Sedna under many names.
Evidence for B: Explicit statement in the Inuit-religion scholarship on the Caribou (Kleivan & Sonne).
What We Don't Know

Further research is needed on: (1) primary Inuktitut texts and community-authoritative retellings, which remain under-exploited in this English-language dossier; (2) the specific Alaskan (Iñupiat/Yup'ik) and Greenlandic regional variants, which are less fully documented here than the Canadian forms; (3) the Chukchi / Siberian correspondence suggested by the Russian-language Wikidata description 'spirit in Eskimo and Chukchi mythology', which requires Russian-language primary sources; (4) the role of Sedna in contemporary Inuit religious revitalisation and cultural sovereignty; (5) an ethical reading of the many modern commercial adaptations (film, games, fantasy literature) of a sacred figure, and whether any involve Inuit consultation; (6) the disputed Smite video-game appearance, which I could not verify and left unflagged-as-unconfirmed; and (7) the exact provenance of individual Inuit artworks featuring Sedna and their rights/licenses. A way to hold it in your mind

There is a woman at the bottom of the Arctic sea, and she is missing her fingers. The phalanges that once curled around a kayak's gunwale were chopped away, joint by joint, by the father who threw her overboard to save his own skin — and every severed finger sprouted a seal, a walrus, a whale, so that the meat that keeps the whole Inuit world alive literally grew from the body of a betrayed daughter. That is Sedna, the Sea Woman, a deity who is not gentle and was never meant to be. She is the reason every hunter checks his harpoon, the reason every mother teaches her child to respect the seal, and the reason that when a community starves, it is forced to ask not 'what did we do' but 'which of us broke the taboo.' Her myth is the Arctic's starkest lesson: that survival is not a gift from heaven but a debt owed to a wounded, fingerless, endlessly forgiving-because-she-never-forgets power beneath the ice. When she is angry the sea goes silent and empty; when she is soothed the seals offer themselves again. Everything depends on keeping her — and by extension the world — reconciled.
