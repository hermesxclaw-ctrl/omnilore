Who It Is

In the cobbled streets of Guatemala City, when the moon is high and the night is silent, a small, elegantly dressed man with an oversized black hat appears, strumming a silver guitar. He is El Sombrerón, the goblin of the braids, whose haunting melodies lure young women into a dance that leaves them entranced, braided with ribbons, and forever marked by his obsession. But his charm hides a darker purpose, and those who resist or cross him may find themselves cursed with sleeplessness, madness, or worse.

El Sombrerón is a trickster and a seducer. He appears at night, often to young women with long hair, and serenades them with his guitar. He is charming and persistent, but his intentions are possessive and obsessive. He braids the hair of his victims with ribbons, and if they resist or try to remove the braids, he becomes angry and punishes them. He is known to cause sleeplessness, nightmares, and even madness in those he targets. He is also said to be fond of horses, and he may braid the manes of horses or ride them at night. Powers
Musical enchantment

El Sombrerón's guitar playing is irresistibly beautiful and hypnotic. When he plays, his victims are drawn to him and become entranced, unable to resist his call. The music is said to be so sweet that it can be heard from far away, and it fills the listener with a longing that is both pleasurable and dangerous.

Limit: The enchantment seems to work primarily on young women with long hair, and it may be resisted by those who are strong-willed or who are protected by religious symbols or the intervention of others.

Hair braiding and cursing

El Sombrerón braids the hair of his victims with colorful ribbons, often while they sleep. This act marks them as his, and it is both a symbol of his claim and a curse. The braids are difficult to remove, and if a victim attempts to cut or undo them, they suffer consequences such as illness, sleeplessness, or madness.

Limit: The curse appears to be broken if the victim seeks help from a priest or a healer, or if they cut their hair entirely, though this may bring other consequences.

Supernatural stealth and mobility

El Sombrerón can appear and disappear at will, and he is able to move silently through the night. He is often said to ride a small horse or mule that can travel great distances quickly, and he may appear in multiple places in the same night.

Limit: No specific limitations are recorded, though he is generally confined to the night and to rural or semi-rural areas.

Punishment of pride and disobedience

In some versions, El Sombrerón specifically targets young women who are vain, disobedient, or who have rejected suitors. He punishes them by making them lose their appetite, causing them to waste away, or by driving them to madness. This aspect of his power aligns him with other folkloric figures who enforce social norms.

Limit: His power seems to be limited to those who have transgressed in some way, and he may be powerless against those who are humble or who have not attracted his attention.

Accounts I
The Braided MaidenOral tradition, collected in various Guatemalan folklore anthologies

20th century (recorded)

A beautiful young woman with long, flowing hair catches the attention of El Sombrerón. He appears at her window at night, playing his guitar and singing to her. She is enchanted and allows him to braid her hair with ribbons. The next morning, she finds her hair intricately braided with colorful ribbons that she cannot remove. She becomes obsessed with him, unable to eat or sleep, and wastes away. Her family calls a priest, who performs a ritual to break the spell. The priest confronts El Sombrerón and banishes him, and the woman is freed, though she remains weak and changed. The woman's health is restored, but she is forever marked by the experience. El Sombrerón is banished from the area, but he continues to appear elsewhere.

Accounts II
The Horse's ManeOral tradition, collected in rural Guatemala

20th century (recorded)

A farmer notices that his horse is exhausted and has tangled, braided manes every morning. He suspects El Sombrerón is riding the horse at night. He stays awake to catch the culprit and sees a small man in a big hat riding his horse, playing a guitar. The farmer tries to grab him, but El Sombrerón disappears, leaving only the sound of his laughter. The horse becomes sick and dies, and the farmer is haunted by nightmares. The farmer loses his horse and is plagued by bad luck. He eventually moves away.

The Defiant BeautyOral tradition, collected in Guatemala City

20th century (recorded)

A proud young woman scoffs at the legend of El Sombrerón, saying she would never be fooled by such a creature. That night, she hears beautiful music and sees a handsome man in a black hat outside her window. She dances with him until dawn, and he braids her hair. When she wakes, she cannot undo the braids, and she becomes obsessed with the man, refusing to eat or speak. Her family seeks the help of a curandera (healer), who performs a cleansing ritual and advises the woman to wear a red ribbon as protection. The obsession fades, but the woman is never quite the same. The woman's pride is humbled, and she becomes more cautious and humble. El Sombrerón is said to have moved on to another town.

El Sombrerón is a Mexican legend. — El Sombrerón is specifically Guatemalan, though similar figures exist in Mexico (e.g., El Charro Negro).

El Sombrerón only targets women who are vain or disobedient. — While some versions emphasize this, many others portray him as targeting any woman he finds attractive, regardless of her behavior.

El Sombrerón is a friendly spirit who only wants to play music. — His actions are harmful and possessive; he causes psychological and physical distress.

El Sombrerón is the same as the Cadejo. — They are distinct entities, though both are night spirits in Central American folklore.
