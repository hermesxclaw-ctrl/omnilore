The Approach

Forged by the trickster god Loki, this enigmatic weapon appears only once in the surviving Norse corpus, hidden in a poem's riddle-like verses. Its very name is a scholarly reconstruction, its form a matter of fierce debate—dart, sword, or magic wand? Whatever it is, it is the only thing that can kill the rooster Viðofnir, who sits atop the World Tree. But is it also the mistletoe that slew Baldr? The answer lies buried in a tangle of manuscript emendations and poetic kennings.

Identity

Lævateinn

Pronunciation Approximately /ˈlaɪvəteɪn/ or /ˈlɛːvəteɪn/ in modern English; Old Norse /ˈlɛːwaˌtɛinː/.

Known As

HævateinnHævateinLævateinWounding WandWand-of-DestructionDamage TwigEvil-cudgel

Etymology

The name is a scholarly emendation of the manuscript form Hævateinn. The first element is from Old Norse læ (genitive læva), meaning 'cunning, deception, injury, bane'. The second element teinn means 'twig, rod, wand, shaft'. Thus Lævateinn is often glossed as 'Wounding Wand' or 'Wand-of-Destruction'. The original Hævateinn may mean 'sure-striking dart' (from hæva 'to hit' and teinn).

Cultural Ownership

Norse mythology, specifically the Poetic Edda. The weapon is associated with the god Loki and appears in the poem Fjölsvinnsmál.

Disputed Identifications

The weapon's type is disputed: dart/arrow, sword, magic wand, cudgel. Some scholars identify it with the mistletoe that killed Baldr, or with Surtr's flaming sword. The emendation from Hævateinn to Lævateinn is not universally accepted.

Description

Appearance

The weapon's physical appearance is not described in the poem. Interpretations depend on the assumed type: if a dart, it is a projectile; if a sword, a bladed weapon; if a wand, a staff-like object. No visual attributes are attested.

Symbols & Iconography

No iconography exists from the medieval period. In modern art, it is rarely depicted, and when it is, it is often shown as a sword or a wand.

Behaviour & Personality

As an inanimate object, it has no personality. In the poem, it is said to be kept in an iron vessel (or 'in Lægjarn's vessel') and is the only weapon that can kill Viðofnir.

Domains & Habitats

It is located within the poem's narrative at the location of Mímameiðr (the World Tree), specifically in a vessel (possibly an iron chest) near the tree. It is not associated with a particular realm beyond that.

Version Differences

The manuscript has Hævateinn; Bugge's emendation gives Lævateinn. Different editions and translations vary in the name and the weapon type. Some interpret 'völr' in the stanza as 'rods' (divining rods) rather than plumage, affecting the context.

Stories & Myths

The Quest of Svipdagr (Fjölsvinnsmál)

Source Poetic Edda, FjölsvinnsmálCulture NorsePeriod Possibly 10th-13th century, preserved in 17th century manuscripts

Summary

In the poem, the hero Svipdagr (or the seeker) asks the giant Fjölsviðr how to kill the rooster Viðofnir, who sits atop the tree Mímameiðr. Fjölsviðr replies that the only weapon is Lævateinn, which was forged by Loki (referred to as Lopt) and is kept in an iron vessel. The poem does not narrate the actual use of the weapon.

Participants

Svipdagr

Fjölsviðr

Loki (as maker)

Viðofnir (target)

Consequences

The weapon's use is not depicted; the poem ends with Fjölsviðr's answer.

Variants

The poem is sometimes considered the second half of Svipdagsmál, with the first half being Grógaldr. The name of the weapon varies between Hævateinn and Lævateinn.

Conflicting Accounts

The manuscript reading may have 'Hæva' (a name meaning 'high') as the maker, but most scholars interpret this as a reference to Loki.

Contradictions & Disputes

Name: Hævateinn vs Lævateinn

Position A

The original manuscript reading is Hævateinn, meaning 'sure-striking dart'.

Position A — evidence

Manuscripts have Hævateinn; Árni Magnússon glossed it as 'spiculum'.

Position B

Sophus Bugge's emendation to Lævateinn, meaning 'wounding wand', is correct.

Position B — evidence

Bugge argued the context suggests Loki, and 'læ' fits Loki's character.

Scholarly Consensus

Most modern editions accept Lævateinn, but the emendation is not universally accepted.

Weapon type

Position A

It is a dart or arrow.

Position A — evidence

The name Hævateinn suggests a projectile; early translations.

Position B

It is a sword.

Position B — evidence

Some scholars see it as a kenning for sword; Schück identified it with Surtr's sword.

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus; the most common interpretation in modern scholarship is a wand or staff, but the matter is unresolved.

Position C

It is a magic wand or staff.

Evidence C

The word 'teinn' means twig/wand; Sturtevant and Simek support a magic wand.

It is not the mistletoe.

Primary Evidence

Poetic Edda, Fjölsvinnsmál, stanza 26 (manuscript reading)

Type textDate 13th century (manuscript); poem likely earlierLocator Stanza 26 (various editions)Language Old Norse

Translation

In the original manuscript: 'Hævateinn heitir hann; hann lét Hæva smíða' (or similar). The name Hævateinn appears in the Codex Regius? Actually, Fjölsvinnsmál is not in Codex Regius; it survives in later paper manuscripts. The reading is Hævateinn.

Type textDate 1867Locator Fjölsvinnsmál stanza 26Language Old Norse with Latin/commentary

Type textDate 1787Locator Manuscript notesLanguage Latin

A.S. Cottle's translation (1797)
