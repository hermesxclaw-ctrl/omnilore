Who It Is

Before the Devil had a name, before Hell had a king, there was a demon with the body of a man, the head of a lion, and four terrible wings. His name was Pazuzu, and he was the bringer of the evil wind — the scorching, pestilent blast that carried locusts, famine, and plague across the ancient lands of Mesopotamia. He was a creature of pure terror, feared by kings and commoners alike. Yet, the most astonishing thing about this King of the Wind Demons is not his power to destroy, but his role as a protector. In a world where demons lurked in every shadow, the ancient Assyrians and Babylonians made a desperate, brilliant bargain: they chose to fight a monster with a monster. They wore the terrifying face of Pazuzu to drive away the even more insidious demoness Lamashtu, the child-snatcher. He was the ancient world's ultimate weapon of fear — a demon you could point at a worse demon.

Pazuzu is a contradictory figure. As a wind demon, he brings destructive storms, drought, and locusts. He is malevolent and feared. However, he is also a protective spirit, a 'necessary evil.' He is the enemy of Lamashtu, the demoness who kills infants and mothers in childbirth. By invoking Pazuzu, one could drive Lamashtu away, as the greater evil is repelled by the lesser (but still formidable) one. He is a force of chaotic nature, but one that can be directed against a worse threat.

Powers
Wind Control

As a wind demon, Pazuzu commands the destructive winds: storms, sandstorms, and the 'evil wind' that brings disease and locusts. He can cause drought and famine.

Limit: He is a demon, not a god. His power is significant but not supreme. He is subordinate to the great gods like Enlil and Ereshkigal.

Apotropaic Protection

Pazuzu's primary function in ritual was to drive away the demoness Lamashtu. His terrifying presence was used to protect pregnant women and newborns. He was a 'demon against demons.'

Limit: He is a dangerous ally. His protection is a 'lesser evil' against a greater one. He is not a benevolent guardian; he is a weapon to be used.

Terrifying Presence

His appearance alone was enough to frighten away malevolent spirits. The grotesque heads and hybrid form were designed to be a visual deterrent.

Limit: This is a passive power, effective only if the opposing spirit is susceptible to fear.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Pazuzu is a terrifying hybrid. He has the body of a man, but the head of a lion or a dog with gaping jaws and bared teeth. His hands are eagle's talons, and he has four wings (two large, two small). He often stands in a 'menacing' posture, one hand raised, as if to strike or ward off evil. Symbols — His iconography is as distinctive as it is terrifying: the hybrid form, the four wings, the talons, the menacing scowl. He is often depicted with his right hand raised and left hand lowered, a gesture that may represent the warding off of evil. The 'Pazuzu head' amulet is the most common symbol, worn to protect against Lamashtu and other demons.

Domains — Pazuzu is a demon of the wind, particularly the hot, dry, desert wind from the west (the 'evil wind'). He is associated with the subterranean realm, the underworld, and the mountains. He dwells in the wild, uninhabited places, but his power is felt everywhere when the wind blows.

Accounts I
The Enemy of LamashtuMesopotamian incantation series against Lamashtu.

1st millennium BCE

Lamashtu, the daughter of the sky god Anu, descends from the heavens to terrorize humanity. She creeps into homes, snatches infants from their mothers' breasts, and poisons the milk of nursing women. She brings disease and death to mothers and children. To combat her, the ritual specialists invoke Pazuzu. His image is placed on amulets worn by pregnant women and hung in the birthing room. His terrifying presence drives Lamashtu back to the underworld, protecting the mother and child. The ritual use of Pazuzu amulets became a standard protective practice in Mesopotamian households.

Accounts II
The Amulet of Protection: Pazuzu and the Pregnant WomanMesopotamian ritual texts and archaeological evidence from Neo-Assyrian households

c. 900-600 BCE (Neo-Assyrian period)

Pazuzu amulets—particularly the distinctive bronze heads with suspension loops—have been found in domestic archaeological contexts throughout Assyria and Babylonia, often in rooms associated with women and childbirth. These amulets were worn by pregnant women and new mothers as protection against Lamashtu, the demoness who was believed to stalk childbirth chambers, strangling newborns and spreading disease to mothers. The logic of this protective magic was apotropaic: by wearing the image of the fearsome Pazuzu, the wearer deterred Lamashtu, who was terrified of him. Ritual texts describe the proper consecration of Pazuzu amulets, the incantations to be recited over them, and the specific placement of the amulet on the mother's body. This practice represents one of the earliest documented forms of demonic counter-magic in world religion—using one demon against another. The widespread distribution of Pazuzu amulets across Assyrian and Babylonian sites testifies to the commonality of this belief and the genuine terror that Lamashtu inspired in Mesopotamian families. Pazuzu is the same as the Devil or Satan. — Pazuzu is a Mesopotamian wind demon, a specific figure from a specific culture. He has no connection to the Christian concept of Satan, which developed centuries later. The association is purely a modern pop-culture invention, popularized by 'The Exorcist.'

Pazuzu is a purely evil entity, a demon of pure malice. — While malevolent, Pazuzu was also a protective figure. He was invoked to ward off the demoness Lamashtu. He was a 'necessary evil,' a weapon used for protection.

Pazuzu is a 'fallen angel' or a member of the Christian demonic hierarchy. — He is a Mesopotamian demon, a child of the god Hanpa. He has no place in Christian angelology or demonology.
