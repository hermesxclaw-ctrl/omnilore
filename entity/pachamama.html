Who It Is

According to traditional accounts, in the high Andes, farmers still bend to the soil before they sow, whispering to a mother who is also a mountain, a field, and the very weave of time. They pour chicha into the earth, bury coca leaves and tiny replicas of the goods they hope to receive, convinced that if Pachamama is not fed, she may withhold rain or twist fate. To call her simply "Mother Earth" is to miss the way she stretches beyond soil into space-time itself—a living totality that remembers colonial violence, fuels myths of future rebellion, and now finds her name inscribed in constitutions and climate manifestos. Pachamama is not just a goddess; she is a contested world-making presence whose afterlives reveal how fiercely people fight over what counts as life, justice, and the earth itself.

In indigenous accounts, Pachamama is benevolent but demanding. She nourishes humans, animals, and crops, providing fertility, health, and protection when properly respected and fed through offerings. She can also withhold rain, cause crop failure, illness, or accidents if offended or neglected. Her behavior is relational and reciprocal: humans must "pay" the earth (pago a la tierra) to maintain balance. Powers
Fertility and agricultural productivity

Pachamama is credited with the power to make soil fertile, ensure the growth of crops, and sustain herds. Ritual offerings of food, coca, alcohol, and symbolic objects are made to secure her favor for planting and harvest seasons. This power is attested in colonial chronicles describing telluric cults and in modern ethnographies of Andean farming rituals.

Limit: Fertility is conditional on proper ritual reciprocity and moral behavior. Neglecting offerings, disrespecting the land, or engaging in excessive extraction can lead to diminished yields, drought, or disease. These limitations are described in indigenous narratives and contemporary eco-spiritual discourse.

Sustaining life and cosmic totality

As an embodiment of pacha (space-time, world), Pachamama sustains the existence of beings in the cosmos, providing material and spiritual sustenance. She is described as omnipresent and creative, capable of maintaining life in the universe and connecting all beings through universal energy.

Limit: This power is conceptual rather than mechanistic; it does not imply omnipotence in a monotheistic sense. The extent to which Pachamama is a single cosmic principle versus one among many powerful beings varies by tradition.

Regenerative power in resistance myths (Inkarrí)

In the Inkarrí myth, the decapitated Inca king’s body is believed to grow back from his buried head thanks to the regenerative power of Pachamama. When his body is complete, he will rise to restore indigenous society. Pachamama’s earth-body nourishes and regenerates the dismembered Inca, symbolizing both resistance and future renewal.

Limit: This regenerative power is specific to the mythic narrative and functions as a political and spiritual symbol rather than a general rule. It is not described as a routinely accessible miracle.

Punitive capacity (earthquakes, misfortune)

Pachamama can cause misfortune, illness, accidents, and environmental disturbances when disrespected. Some accounts link earthquakes and landslides to her displeasure, though this is more broadly associated with earth beings and huacas than with a single centralized goddess.

Limit: Punitive actions are typically localized and tied to specific transgressions (e.g., failing to make offerings, polluting sacred sites). They are not framed as universal moral judgment in the Christian sense.

Legal and political subject of rights

In contemporary Bolivia and Ecuador, Pachamama/Madre Tierra is recognized in constitutional and statutory law as a subject of rights, including rights to life, regeneration, and restoration. This transforms her from a purely religious figure into a legal entity used to challenge extractivist policies.

Limit: Legal recognition is mediated by state institutions and may coexist with continued extractive practices. The translation of indigenous ontologies into rights language can constrain Pachamama within Western legal categories.

Accounts I
Pachamama as Mother of Sun and MoonInca mythological narratives summarized in modern secondary literature and colonial chronicles

Pre-Hispanic with colonial recording

In some Inca myths, Pachamama is described as the wife of Pachacámac, a powerful creator deity, and the mother of Inti (the sun god) and Mama Quilla (the moon goddess). As mother of these celestial bodies, she is linked to the origin of the four cosmological principles—Water, Earth, Sun, and Moon—and serves as the source of material and spiritual sustenance for humans. This narrative situates Pachamama within a divine family and emphasizes her generative role in cosmic order. The story reinforces the centrality of earth and space-time in Inca cosmology and legitimizes ritual offerings to Pachamama as part of maintaining cosmic balance. It also provides a mythic genealogy for key deities, integrating earth worship into state religion.

Agricultural rituals of pago a la tierra (payment to the earth)Colonial chronicles and modern ethnographies of Andean farming communities

Pre-Hispanic origins with continuity into colonial and contemporary periods

Before planting and during harvest, Andean farmers perform rituals known as pago a la tierra, offering food, coca leaves, alcohol, and symbolic miniatures to Pachamama. The offerings are placed in the soil, at apachetas, or in ritual mesas, accompanied by prayers acknowledging her as Mother Earth and requesting fertility, protection, and balance. The ritual expresses reciprocal exchange: humans feed Pachamama so that she will feed them. Successful rituals are believed to result in good harvests, healthy animals, and protection from misfortune. Neglecting or improperly performing the ritual can lead to crop failure or illness. The practice reinforces communal identity and ecological ethics.

Accounts II
Inkarrí and the regenerative power of PachamamaAndean oral tradition analyzed in Ginett Pineda’s dissertation and other ethnohistorical works

Colonial and postcolonial oral tradition

The Inkarrí myth recounts how the Inca king was captured and decapitated by Spaniards. His head was buried, and over time his body is said to grow back from the head thanks to the regenerative power of Pachamama. When his body is complete, Inkarrí will rise again to establish a new empire and restore indigenous society. Pachamama’s earth-body nourishes the dismembered Inca, symbolizing both the persistence of indigenous life and the possibility of future justice. The myth functions as a narrative of resistance and hope, framing colonial violence as temporary and reversible through Pachamama’s regenerative power. It supports political imaginaries of decolonization and indigenous resurgence.

Pachamama in contemporary rights-of-nature discourseBolivian political speeches and legal texts; feminist and political ontology analyses such as Miriam Tola’s article

21st century

In contemporary Bolivia, Pachamama/Madre Tierra is invoked in political speeches and legal frameworks as a subject of rights. At the World People’s Conference on Climate Change and the Rights of Mother Nature, Evo Morales declared that humanity faces a choice between Pachamama and death, calling for rights of Mother Earth and denouncing capitalism. Laws recognize Pachamama’s rights to life, regeneration, and restoration, framing environmental protection as defense of a living being. Pachamama becomes a central symbol in global climate politics and decolonial discourse, influencing constitutional reforms and international debates. However, extractivist economic policies continue, creating tension between symbolic recognition and material practice.
