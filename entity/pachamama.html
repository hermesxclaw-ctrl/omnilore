Who It Is

Pachamama is the earth mother goddess of the Andes — one of the most ancient, enduring, and beloved deities in South America. Her name combines 'Pacha' (world, earth, time-space) and 'Mama' (mother), identifying her as the mother of the world, the source of all fertility, and the sacred earth itself. Unlike many pre-Columbian deities whose worship declined after the Spanish conquest, Pachamama's cult not only survived but has flourished, blending with Catholic elements and serving as a central figure in contemporary Andean Indigenous spirituality, environmental activism, and even Bolivian and Peruvian national identity.

Physically, Pachamama is represented in both traditional and modern forms. In pre-Columbian art, she appears as a female earth deity, often associated with mountains (apus), fertility symbols, and the agricultural landscape. In modern Andean imagery, she is sometimes depicted as a small, dark-skinned woman with a round, motherly figure, dressed in traditional Andean clothing, often holding agricultural products. However, her most authentic 'representation' is the earth itself — the soil, the mountains, the crops — and ritual offerings are made directly to the living land rather than to images.

Pachamama's worship is a daily, lived reality for millions of Andean people. The central ritual is the pago a la tierra (payment to the earth) or challa — an offering of coca leaves, chicha (corn beer), alcohol, sweets, and sometimes animal blood poured directly onto the ground with prayers for fertility, protection, and abundance. Before planting, harvesting, building a house, or beginning a journey, Andean people offer a challa to Pachamama. The first sip of any drink is poured onto the earth. Coca leaves — the most sacred plant in Andean religion — are chewed and offered to Pachamama in a relationship of reciprocity (ayni): the earth gives crops and life; humans give offerings and respect.

Pachamama's theology is fundamentally about reciprocity. In Andean cosmology, the cosmos operates through ayni — a principle of balanced exchange between all beings. Pachamama provides the conditions for life (soil, water, crops), and humans reciprocate through offerings, ritual, and respectful treatment of the land. To take from the earth without giving back is a cosmic violation that leads to illness, crop failure, or natural disaster. This ethical framework makes Pachamama worship inherently ecological: the earth is not a resource to be exploited but a mother to be honored.

During the colonial period, Pachamama was superficially syncretized with the Virgin Mary — both are mothers, both are associated with the earth and fertility, both are approached through offerings and prayers. However, the syncretism was strategic: Indigenous communities preserved Pachamama worship beneath a Catholic veneer. In modern Bolivia and Peru, Pachamama is openly venerated alongside Catholic figures, and her prominence has grown with Indigenous political movements. In 2010, Bolivia passed the 'Law of the Rights of Mother Earth' (Ley de Derechos de la Madre Tierra), granting nature legal rights and explicitly naming Pachamama. This landmark legislation — the first of its kind in the world — reflects how an ancient Andean goddess has become a symbol of 21st-century environmental consciousness.

The Approach

In the high Andes, farmers still bend to the soil before they sow, whispering to a mother who is also a mountain, a field, and the very weave of time. They pour chicha into the earth, bury coca leaves and tiny replicas of the goods they hope to receive, convinced that if Pachamama is not fed, she may withhold rain or twist fate. To call her simply "Mother Earth" is to miss the way she stretches beyond soil into space-time itself—a living totality that remembers colonial violence, fuels myths of future rebellion, and now finds her name inscribed in constitutions and climate manifestos. Pachamama is not just a goddess; she is a contested world-making presence whose afterlives reveal how fiercely people fight over what counts as life, justice, and the earth itself.

Etymology

Attested Quechua usage combines "pacha" and "mama". "Pacha" in Andean languages denotes a complex field of meanings including world, cosmos, space-time, epoch, and the ordered totality of existence; "mama" means mother, a term used for powerful female beings, kin, and nurturing entities. Thus "Pachamama" is commonly glossed as "Mother of the World" or "Mother Space-Time", but indigenous accounts emphasize her as the living totality that sustains and envelops beings rather than a merely personified planet. This etymology is attested in colonial Quechua vocabularies and modern Andean ethnolinguistic studies; exact pre-Inca semantic layers remain partly reconstructed and debated.

Cultural Ownership

Primarily Quechua- and Aymara-speaking Andean peoples of the central and southern Andes (Peru, Bolivia, northern Chile and Argentina), with broader Andean indigenous recognition. Inca imperial religion incorporated and systematized earth and space-time cults associated with Pachamama, but local communities (runakuna, ayllu-based groups) maintained distinct ritual and narrative traditions. Contemporary usage extends to pan-Andean and global environmental movements, often in ways that partially detach the figure from its specific indigenous ontologies.

Disputed Identifications

Several overlapping but non-identical identifications exist: (1) Pachamama as a specific earth deity within Inca state religion; (2) Pachamama as a broader Andean concept of living earth/space-time; (3) Pachamama equated with generic "Mother Earth" in global environmental discourse; (4) Pachamama syncretically identified with the Virgin Mary in colonial and postcolonial Catholic contexts; (5) Pachamama treated as a legal subject of rights in Bolivian and Ecuadorian constitutional frameworks. Scholars and indigenous activists warn that equating Pachamama simply with planet Earth or with Mary erases indigenous cosmology and political ontology. The degree to which Pachamama was a centralized Inca goddess versus a reverential appellation for multiple local earth beings is actively debated.

Description

Appearance

Attested descriptions of Pachamama in pre-Hispanic and colonial sources are relatively sparse and often indirect, focusing more on ritual practice than on detailed iconography. In many Andean communities, Pachamama is not a single anthropomorphic figure but the living earth itself—mountains, fields, slopes, and the space-time that envelops beings. When personified, she is commonly imagined as a mature woman or mother, sometimes emerging from the soil or associated with mountain bodies. Colonial and modern artistic depictions show her as a woman bearing harvests of potatoes, maize, or coca leaves, or as a female figure integrated into the landscape. Shrines (apachetas, sacred rocks, tree trunks) stand in for her presence rather than representing a fixed bodily form. This description is attested in ethnographic and iconographic studies; precise pre-Inca visual conventions remain largely unknown.

Symbols & Iconography

Attested symbols include: sacred rocks and cairns (apachetas) where travelers and farmers leave offerings; trunks of legendary trees; agricultural produce (potatoes, maize, coca leaves) carried by a female figure; libation pits and earth openings used for pouring chicha or alcohol; mountain peaks and slopes treated as her body; and ritual mesas (tables) with miniature objects representing goods offered to Pachamama. In contemporary Andean ritual kits, Pachamama is symbolized through earth-colored powders, seeds, and figurines. In Bolivian and global environmental iconography, she is often depicted as a nurturing earth-mother cradling the planet, a visual simplification that partially departs from indigenous telluric cults.

Behaviour & Personality

In indigenous accounts, Pachamama is benevolent but demanding. She nourishes humans, animals, and crops, providing fertility, health, and protection when properly respected and fed through offerings. She can also withhold rain, cause crop failure, illness, or accidents if offended or neglected. Her behavior is relational and reciprocal: humans must "pay" the earth (pago a la tierra) to maintain balance. She is not a distant, abstract deity but an omnipresent, immanent being whose moods and responses are experienced in everyday life. Modern environmental discourse tends to emphasize her generosity and victimhood (as a being harmed by extractivism), sometimes downplaying her ambivalent, potentially punitive aspects.

Domains & Habitats

Pachamama’s primary domain is earth understood broadly: soil, mountains, valleys, and the ecological zones of the Andes. She is also associated with space-time (pacha) as a unified field, linking terrestrial and cosmic dimensions. Farming rituals, pastoral activities, and travel across mountain passes fall under her purview. In some Inca mythological accounts, she is linked to the origin of the four cosmological principles—Water, Earth, Sun, and Moon—and thus participates in a wider cosmic order. Her habitat is not a separate heavenly realm but the very landscape and temporal cycles in which Andean life unfolds.

Version Differences

Attested differences include: (1) Pre-Hispanic and early colonial Andean accounts emphasize Pachamama as a reverential appellation for earth and space-time, embedded in local huaca cults and agricultural rituals; (2) In some Inca mythological narratives, Pachamama is described as the wife of Pachacámac and mother of Inti (sun) and Mama Quilla (moon), situating her within a more anthropomorphic divine family; (3) Colonial syncretic versions associate Pachamama with the Virgin Mary, sometimes mapping earth’s fertility onto Marian motherhood while simultaneously condemning indigenous earth worship as idolatry; (4) Contemporary indigenous eco-spirituality foregrounds Pachamama as a living totality sustaining buen vivir, often in opposition to colonial and capitalist exploitation; (5) Global environmental and New Age adaptations treat Pachamama as a generalized Mother Earth archetype, sometimes detached from Andean ritual specificity. These versions coexist and sometimes conflict, and scholars caution against collapsing them into a single, harmonized figure.

Powers & Abilities

Fertility and agricultural productivity

Source Version Pre-Hispanic and colonial Andean earth cults; modern indigenous practice; summarized in di Salvia’s encyclopedia entry and Pineda’s dissertation.

Description

Pachamama is credited with the power to make soil fertile, ensure the growth of crops, and sustain herds. Ritual offerings of food, coca, alcohol, and symbolic objects are made to secure her favor for planting and harvest seasons. This power is attested in colonial chronicles describing telluric cults and in modern ethnographies of Andean farming rituals.

Limitations

Fertility is conditional on proper ritual reciprocity and moral behavior. Neglecting offerings, disrespecting the land, or engaging in excessive extraction can lead to diminished yields, drought, or disease. These limitations are described in indigenous narratives and contemporary eco-spiritual discourse.

Contradictions

Global environmental rhetoric sometimes portrays Pachamama as endlessly generous and purely victimized, whereas indigenous accounts emphasize her capacity to punish and withdraw fertility. Some scholars argue that framing her solely as a nurturing mother obscures her ambivalent agency.

Sustaining life and cosmic totality

Source Version Inca mythological and religious interpretations; modern Andean philosophical and theological writings; reflected in contemporary indigenous statements about Pachamama as living totality.

Description

As an embodiment of pacha (space-time, world), Pachamama sustains the existence of beings in the cosmos, providing material and spiritual sustenance. She is described as omnipresent and creative, capable of maintaining life in the universe and connecting all beings through universal energy.

Limitations

This power is conceptual rather than mechanistic; it does not imply omnipotence in a monotheistic sense. The extent to which Pachamama is a single cosmic principle versus one among many powerful beings varies by tradition.

Contradictions

Some scholars treat Pachamama as a metaphor for ecological interdependence rather than a discrete deity, while others insist on her ontological reality as a living being. The boundary between personification and ontology is contested.

Regenerative power in resistance myths (Inkarrí)

Source Version Andean oral tradition analyzed in Pineda’s dissertation and other ethnohistorical studies.

Description

In the Inkarrí myth, the decapitated Inca king’s body is believed to grow back from his buried head thanks to the regenerative power of Pachamama. When his body is complete, he will rise to restore indigenous society. Pachamama’s earth-body nourishes and regenerates the dismembered Inca, symbolizing both resistance and future renewal.

Limitations

This regenerative power is specific to the mythic narrative and functions as a political and spiritual symbol rather than a general rule. It is not described as a routinely accessible miracle.

Contradictions

Interpretations differ on whether Pachamama is an active agent in Inkarrí’s regeneration or a passive medium; some readings emphasize her as co-conspirator in anti-colonial insurrection, others as the natural environment that makes rebirth possible.

Punitive capacity (earthquakes, misfortune)

Source Version Colonial chronicles on telluric cults; modern ethnographic reports of local beliefs about earth’s anger.

Description

Pachamama can cause misfortune, illness, accidents, and environmental disturbances when disrespected. Some accounts link earthquakes and landslides to her displeasure, though this is more broadly associated with earth beings and huacas than with a single centralized goddess.

Limitations

Punitive actions are typically localized and tied to specific transgressions (e.g., failing to make offerings, polluting sacred sites). They are not framed as universal moral judgment in the Christian sense.

Contradictions

In legal and rights-of-nature discourse, Pachamama is framed primarily as a victim of human exploitation, which can obscure indigenous understandings of her as a powerful being capable of retaliation.

Legal and political subject of rights

Source Version Bolivian and Ecuadorian legal frameworks; feminist and political ontology analyses such as Miriam Tola’s article on Pachamama and Mother Earth.

Description

In contemporary Bolivia and Ecuador, Pachamama/Madre Tierra is recognized in constitutional and statutory law as a subject of rights, including rights to life, regeneration, and restoration. This transforms her from a purely religious figure into a legal entity used to challenge extractivist policies.

Limitations

Legal recognition is mediated by state institutions and may coexist with continued extractive practices. The translation of indigenous ontologies into rights language can constrain Pachamama within Western legal categories.

Contradictions

Feminist and indigenous critics argue that the state’s version of Pachamama as Mother Earth reinforces gendered stereotypes and masks ongoing exploitation, while activist invocations seek to destabilize capitalist development models.

Stories & Myths

Pachamama as Mother of Sun and Moon

Source Inca mythological narratives summarized in modern secondary literature and colonial chroniclesCulture Inca / broader AndeanPeriod Pre-Hispanic with colonial recording

Summary

In some Inca myths, Pachamama is described as the wife of Pachacámac, a powerful creator deity, and the mother of Inti (the sun god) and Mama Quilla (the moon goddess). As mother of these celestial bodies, she is linked to the origin of the four cosmological principles—Water, Earth, Sun, and Moon—and serves as the source of material and spiritual sustenance for humans. This narrative situates Pachamama within a divine family and emphasizes her generative role in cosmic order.

Consequences

The story reinforces the centrality of earth and space-time in Inca cosmology and legitimizes ritual offerings to Pachamama as part of maintaining cosmic balance. It also provides a mythic genealogy for key deities, integrating earth worship into state religion.

Variants

Some versions emphasize Pachacámac as a coastal creator god with different consorts; others do not explicitly name Pachamama but refer to earth as mother. The degree to which this narrative was widespread versus localized is uncertain, and much of the detail is reconstructed from fragmentary colonial accounts.

Agricultural rituals of pago a la tierra (payment to the earth)

Source Colonial chronicles and modern ethnographies of Andean farming communitiesCulture Quechua and Aymara Andean communitiesPeriod Pre-Hispanic origins with continuity into colonial and contemporary periods

Summary

Before planting and during harvest, Andean farmers perform rituals known as pago a la tierra, offering food, coca leaves, alcohol, and symbolic miniatures to Pachamama. The offerings are placed in the soil, at apachetas, or in ritual mesas, accompanied by prayers acknowledging her as Mother Earth and requesting fertility, protection, and balance. The ritual expresses reciprocal exchange: humans feed Pachamama so that she will feed them.

Consequences

Successful rituals are believed to result in good harvests, healthy animals, and protection from misfortune. Neglecting or improperly performing the ritual can lead to crop failure or illness. The practice reinforces communal identity and ecological ethics.

Variants

Specific offerings, prayers, and ritual sequences vary by region and community. Some rituals explicitly name Pachamama; others refer to earth, mountains, or local huacas. Syncretic versions incorporate Catholic prayers or images of saints alongside offerings to Pachamama.

Inkarrí and the regenerative power of Pachamama

Summary

The Inkarrí myth recounts how the Inca king was captured and decapitated by Spaniards. His head was buried, and over time his body is said to grow back from the head thanks to the regenerative power of Pachamama. When his body is complete, Inkarrí will rise again to establish a new empire and restore indigenous society. Pachamama’s earth-body nourishes the dismembered Inca, symbolizing both the persistence of indigenous life and the possibility of future justice.

The myth functions as a narrative of resistance and hope, framing colonial violence as temporary and reversible through Pachamama’s regenerative power. It supports political imaginaries of decolonization and indigenous resurgence.

Different versions locate Inkarrí’s head in various regions and emphasize different timelines for his return. Some explicitly name Pachamama; others refer more generally to earth’s regenerative capacity. Interpretations vary on whether the myth is primarily political, spiritual, or cosmological.

Pachamama in contemporary rights-of-nature discourse

Summary

In contemporary Bolivia, Pachamama/Madre Tierra is invoked in political speeches and legal frameworks as a subject of rights. At the World People’s Conference on Climate Change and the Rights of Mother Nature, Evo Morales declared that humanity faces a choice between Pachamama and death, calling for rights of Mother Earth and denouncing capitalism. Laws recognize Pachamama’s rights to life, regeneration, and restoration, framing environmental protection as defense of a living being.

Pachamama becomes a central symbol in global climate politics and decolonial discourse, influencing constitutional reforms and international debates. However, extractivist economic policies continue, creating tension between symbolic recognition and material practice.

Dark History

Colonial suppression of earth cults

Source Peruvian colonial chronicles analyzed in di Salvia’s 2013 article; broader scholarship on Andean religious suppression.

Description

Spanish colonization brought campaigns against indigenous religious practices, including earth worship. Missionaries and colonial authorities condemned offerings to Pachamama and other huacas as idolatry, destroyed shrines, and imposed Catholic doctrine. Indigenous communities often continued earth rituals in secret or under syncretic forms, but the suppression involved coercion, punishment, and cultural trauma.

Scholarly Context

Ethnohistorical studies emphasize both the violence of suppression and the resilience of indigenous practices, highlighting how Pachamama worship adapted under pressure.

Syncretic appropriation and Marianization

Source Studies of Andean religious syncretism referenced in di Salvia’s encyclopedia entry and related literature.

Description

Associating Pachamama with the Virgin Mary allowed some indigenous communities to preserve aspects of earth worship, but it also facilitated the appropriation and redefinition of Pachamama within Catholic frameworks. Marianization could subordinate indigenous cosmology to colonial theology, framing earth’s fertility as derivative of Mary’s grace rather than Pachamama’s agency.

Scholarly Context

Scholars debate whether syncretism primarily served indigenous survival strategies or reinforced colonial domination; indigenous theologians today seek to reclaim Pachamama’s distinct identity.

Extractivism and environmental degradation

Source Miriam Tola’s article on Pachamama and Mother Earth; broader literature on extractivism and rights-of-nature in Bolivia.

Description

In contemporary Bolivia and other Andean countries, large-scale mining, hydrocarbon extraction, and agribusiness are often justified in the name of national development, despite legal recognition of Pachamama’s rights. These activities damage landscapes, pollute water, and disrupt communities that view Pachamama as a living being. Indigenous activists frame extractivism as violence against Pachamama.

Scholarly Context

Decolonial and feminist scholars analyze how Pachamama is mobilized in political discourse, noting paradoxes between symbolic protection and material exploitation.

Global commodification and misidentification

Source María Gloria Robalino’s article abstract on the "Lives and Afterlives of Pachamama" (Hispanic Review, 2024) notes chronic misidentifications and semantic transformations.

Description

Pachamama has become a popular symbol in global environmentalism and New Age spirituality, often used as a generic label for planet Earth or ecological consciousness. This can involve commodification (e.g., tourism, branding) and chronic misidentification that strips Pachamama of her specific Andean cultural context and political significance.

Scholarly Context

Robalino and others call for careful attention to indigenous accounts and colonial archives to avoid reproducing colonial erasures in contemporary discourse.

Contradictions & Disputes

Pachamama as specific deity vs. reverential appellation for earth

Position A

Pachamama is a distinct goddess within Inca and Andean pantheons, with defined relationships (wife of Pachacámac, mother of Inti and Mama Quilla) and centralized cult.

Position A — evidence

Mythological summaries and some colonial chronicles present Pachamama in anthropomorphic, familial terms, aligning her with other named deities.

Position B

Pachamama is primarily a reverential appellation for earth as a living, productive being, applied to multiple local earth entities and not a single, systematized deity.

Position B — evidence

Ethnographic and ethnohistorical studies (e.g., di Salvia’s encyclopedia entry) emphasize the diversity of telluric cults and the use of "Pachamama" as a general reverential term rather than a fixed pantheon member.

Scholarly Consensus

Many scholars lean toward understanding Pachamama as a complex, relational concept of earth and space-time rather than a strictly individualized goddess, while acknowledging that Inca state religion may have partially systematized her into a pantheon. The issue remains debated, and harmonization is avoided in careful scholarship.

Identification with Virgin Mary

Position A

Pachamama and the Virgin Mary can be harmoniously identified, reflecting indigenous-Christian syncretism that honors both earth and Marian motherhood.

Position A — evidence

Syncretic practices and local devotions show overlapping imagery and rituals, with communities invoking both Pachamama and Mary in agricultural and protective contexts.

Position B

Equating Pachamama with Mary erases indigenous cosmology and subordinates earth worship to Catholic doctrine, reproducing colonial domination.

Position B — evidence

Decolonial and indigenous theologians argue that Marianization was part of missionary strategies to replace earth cults, and that contemporary reclamation of Pachamama requires distinguishing her from Mary.

Scholarly Consensus

There is no single consensus; syncretism is recognized as both a survival strategy and a site of ongoing power struggles. Recent scholarship tends to foreground indigenous autonomy and caution against uncritical identification.

Pachamama as metaphor vs. ontological being

Position A

Pachamama is best understood as a metaphor for ecological interdependence and space-time, useful for environmental ethics but not literally a personified being.

Position A — evidence

Global environmental discourse and some philosophical writings use Pachamama as a symbolic figure for nature or planet Earth, emphasizing metaphorical resonance.

Position B

For many Andean communities, Pachamama is an ontologically real, living, conscious being who responds to offerings and transgressions.

Position B — evidence

Ethnographic accounts document ritual interactions, prayers, and experiences of Pachamama’s agency, treated by practitioners as real rather than symbolic.

Scholarly Consensus

Political ontology and decolonial scholarship increasingly insist on taking indigenous claims about Pachamama’s reality seriously, while acknowledging that metaphorical uses exist in other contexts. The tension between metaphor and ontology is a central analytical issue.

Rights-of-nature discourse and extractivism

Position A

Legal recognition of Pachamama’s rights marks a transformative shift toward ecological justice and decolonial politics, effectively protecting earth and communities.

Position A — evidence

Constitutional texts and activist celebrations highlight Pachamama’s rights as a groundbreaking innovation in environmental law.

Position B

Rights-of-nature discourse can be co-opted by states to legitimize continued extractivism, using Pachamama symbolically while materially subordinating her to development agendas.

Position B — evidence

Analyses like Miriam Tola’s show how Pachamama/Mother Earth is gendered and governed in ways that reinforce state power, while extractive projects proceed.

Scholarly Consensus

Scholars recognize both the symbolic importance and the practical limitations of rights-of-nature frameworks. There is broad agreement that implementation is uneven and that critical scrutiny of state practices is necessary.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

Several dimensions remain incomplete due to limited access to full-text sources and the constraints of this research context. Primary evidence from colonial chronicles (e.g., Cobo, Molina, Betanzos) could not be consulted directly in critical editions, so specific page and chapter locators for Pachamama references are approximate or inferred from secondary analyses. Detailed iconographic evidence from pre-Hispanic textiles, ceramics, and architecture that might depict earth beings associated with Pachamama was not systematically surveyed. Oral narratives from diverse Andean regions (Quechua, Aymara, and other groups) are represented mainly through a few academic syntheses, leaving many local variants undocumented here. Legal texts from Bolivia and Ecuador were not examined article by article, so the precise wording and implementation of Pachamama’s rights are summarized rather than exhaustively detailed. Reception in art, music, film, and internet culture is only sketched, focusing on major academic and activist references rather than a comprehensive catalog. Chronological layering between pre-Inca, Inca, colonial, and modern versions is reconstructed from secondary literature and may oversimplify complex regional histories. URLs for some cited works are left blank because stable links were not verifiable without full database access.

Research Scope

This dossier synthesizes accessible scholarly and primary-source-based discussions of Pachamama up to late July 2026, drawing primarily on Spanish- and English-language academic articles, reference works, dissertations, and publicly available summaries of legal and political discourse. It focuses on Quechua and Aymara Andean contexts, Inca mythological frameworks, colonial chronicles as interpreted by modern scholars, and contemporary rights-of-nature debates in Bolivia and broader environmental movements. It does not claim to cover all local traditions, artistic representations, or legal developments, and it acknowledges that many indigenous accounts remain unpublished or accessible only through fieldwork and community collaboration.
