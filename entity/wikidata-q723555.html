Form

Aseity is not a physical attribute; it has no appearance. It is a metaphysical attribute describing God's mode of existence. In visual art, it is occasionally symbolized by light, a burning bush (Exodus 3), or geometric symbols like a circle or triangle (the divine triangle in Christian iconography).

Powers

Self-existence God exists by and from Himself; He is not caused, created, or sustained by another. This is the core of aseity. Classical theism (Aquinas, Anselm, Augustine) None — aseity is absolute. However, it is not 'self-causation' in the sense of producing oneself, which would involve a prior state. Process theologians (e.g., Charles Hartshorne) reject aseity, arguing God is in dynamic relationship with the world and therefore not wholly independent.

Necessity God exists necessarily; it is impossible for God not to exist. This is a corollary of aseity. Anselm, Aquinas, Leibniz None in classical theism. Some philosophers (e.g., J.L. Mackie) argue necessity is meaningless or incoherent.

Independence from creation God does not need the world; creation is free, not necessary. Classical theism God's independence does not mean He is indifferent; classical theism affirms God's love and providence. Pantheism and panentheism deny full independence.

Behavior & Habitat

Aseity is not a behavior but a mode of being. It describes how God exists: necessarily, independently, and without dependence on anything else. In theological discourse, aseity is often paired with simplicity, immutability, impassibility, and eternity — God is not acted upon, does not change, and does not depend on creation.

Accounts

Moses encounters a bush that burns but is not consumed. God speaks from the bush and reveals His name as 'I AM WHO I AM' (Ehyeh Asher Ehyeh). This is the foundational biblical text for aseity: God is the self-existent one.

Aristotle argues that all motion requires a first mover, which itself is unmoved. This unmoved mover is pure actuality (energeia) and thinks itself (noesis noeseos). It is the ultimate source of all being and motion, though it does not 'create' in the Christian sense.

Anselm defines God as 'that than which nothing greater can be conceived.' He argues that such a being must exist in reality, not just in the mind, because existing in reality is greater than existing only in the mind. This argument implies God's necessary existence, a corollary of aseity.

Scholarly Debates
Self-causation vs. uncaused existence
God is self-caused (causa sui) — He causes His own existence. ⇌ God is uncaused — He has no cause at all, including Himself.
Evidence for A: Some early modern philosophers (e.g., Spinoza) used 'causa sui' to describe God.
Evidence for B: Aquinas explicitly rejects self-causation, arguing nothing can cause itself (Summa Theologiae I, q. 2, a. 3).
Aseity and creation
God is wholly independent of creation; creation is unnecessary. ⇌ God's nature is relational; creation is necessary for God to be fully God.
Evidence for A: Aquinas, Anselm, and most classical theists.
Evidence for B: Process theology (Hartshorne), panentheism, and some feminist theologies.
Aseity and time
God is timeless; aseity implies eternity (no beginning, no end, no succession). ⇌ God is temporal; aseity means God has always existed but exists within time.
Evidence for A: Boethius, Aquinas, Anselm.
Evidence for B: Open theism (e.g., Clark Pinnock), process theology.

A way to hold it in your mind

If the universe is a house, aseity is the claim that the builder doesn't live in the house, doesn't need the house, and could exist perfectly well if the house were never built. The house depends on the builder; the builder depends on nothing.
