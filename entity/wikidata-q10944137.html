Who It Is

On a dark mountain path in Shikoku, you hear a baby crying where no baby should be. Compassion makes you lift it — and then it begins to get heavier, and heavier, until its weight pins you to the earth. When you finally look down, the infant is gone and an old man with a malicious grin is sitting on your chest. You have met Konaki-jijii, the Crybaby Old Man.

Folkloric behavior is simple and predatory-deceptive: it cries to elicit compassion, allows itself to be picked up, then increases its weight to immobilize, crush, or exhaust the victim. It clings tenaciously and does not speak. It is not described as conversing or negotiating; release comes only if the victim recites a protective phrase, shows fortitude, or in some variants, falls to the ground. In Mizuki's GeGeGe no Kitaro, personality is radically reinterpreted: Konaki-jijii becomes a benevolent, loyal elder - wise, gruff but kind, with a strong sense of duty, serving as one of Kita

Powers
Cry Mimicry / Lure

Emits the piteous crying of an abandoned infant (ko-naki) to attract compassionate passersby. The cry is described as indistinguishable from a real baby and is heard from off the path, in brush or darkness, where no infant should be.

Limit: Only effective on those who respond with compassion and physically pick it up. In folklore, those who ignore the cry are unaffected. In Mizuki's version, he can cry on command but rarely needs to.

Progressive Weight Increase (Omori)

Once held or carried on the back/chest, its mass increases continuously and supernaturally, from normal infant weight to hundreds of kilograms, immobilizing and potentially crushing the victim. Described as 'heavy as a stone/boulder.'

Limit: Requires physical contact - it must be picked up or cling. Folklore suggests weight increase stops if victim falls prostrate or recites a specific phrase; Mizuki version makes it voluntary and controllable, used as grappling attack. Cannot affect multiple people at once.

Adhesive Clinging / Immobilization

Clings tenaciously to the victim's chest, back, or neck and cannot be easily thrown off. In Mizuki's depiction, this is a deliberate combat technique: Konaki-jijii leaps onto an enemy's back and becomes dead weight, pinning them.

Limit: In folklore, dawn or the victim's collapse ends the clinging. In franchise, stronger yokai can resist or throw him off; he is vulnerable while clinging (exposed).

Transformation / True Form Reveal

Shapeshifts from infant appearance to wizened old man form. The transformation is the reveal of its true nature and is tied to the weight increase.

Limit: Only between these two forms; no broader shapeshifting into animals or other humans is attested.

Superhuman Durability (Franchise only)

In GeGeGe no Kitaro, as a yokai ally, survives being thrown, struck, and battles with other yokai despite small size, implying yokai resilience.

Limit: Not invulnerable; can be injured by powerful yokai or spiritual attacks. No such durability in folklore where it is more phenomenon than combatant.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In folklore accounts, Konaki-jijii has two sequential forms. First, it presents as an abandoned infant: heard but not seen, crying piteously on a mountain road or forest path at dusk or night. When the victim picks it up, it appears as a small baby in swaddling. Second, as its weight increases, its true form is revealed: a small, wizened old man with a large head, bald or sparsely haired, with an infant-like face but aged features, often shown wi

Symbols — Core symbols: the crying voice as lure (mimicry), increasing weight/stone motif, mountain road at night, abandonment. No traditional religious iconography (no shrine, no ofuda, no specific crest). In Mizuki-derived iconography: stone-like texture to suggest heaviness, baby's bib or diaper, old man's face on baby's body to signal deception. Often paired visually with Obariyon for contrast in yokai encyclopedias. On Mizuki Shigeru Ro

Domains — Habitat is liminal mountain space: mountain passes (toge), forest roads, and trails between villages in Shikoku, especially Tokushima. Time is twilight, night, or rainy weather when travel is dangerous. It is a michi-no-yokai (road yokai) and yama-no-yokai (mountain yokai), associated with the dangers of travel, isolation, and the taboo of abandoned children. It has no shrine, temple, or fixed lair; it is encountered, not worshipped. In franchise

Accounts I
The Crying Baby on the Tokushima Mountain PassTokushima Prefecture oral tradition, as summarized in Showa-era folklore collections and cited in Mizuki Shigeru's yokai encyclopedias and Foster 2015

Oral tradition of unknown age; first printed in 20th century (1950s-1970s collections)

A traveler crossing a mountain pass at dusk or night hears an infant crying from the roadside brush. Finding no parent, the traveler, moved by pity, picks up the baby. Immediately the baby begins to grow heavier in the arms. With each step the weight increases - like holding a stone that gains mass - until the traveler is pinned, unable to move or breathe, and must collapse to the ground. By morning, the baby is gone, and in its place is said to be an old man, or simply nothing, having vanished. Some tellings say the victim is found dead, crushed. Etiological warning against picking up abandoned children on roads and against traveling alone at night; reinforces taboo and compassion-risk paradox. Establishes the core motif of deceptive vulnerability turned to crushing weight.

The Hunter Who Tried to Take the Child HomeVariant recorded in Shikoku mountain village reports, retold in yokai compendia (e.g., Murakami Kenji, Yokai Jiten; Meyer synthesis)

Oral tradition, recorded late 20th century

A hunter or woodcutter hears crying near a Jizo shrine or stream and decides to take the child to the village. As he walks, the bundle grows so heavy he cannot continue and is forced to set it down. When he looks, the infant has become a small old man who grins and disappears into the forest, leaving the man exhausted and shaken. He returns to tell the village not to heed cries in the mountains. Reinforces yama-no-kami and yokai as guardians of mountain boundaries; discourages removing things from the mountain. Some tellings link to real practice of abandoning unwanted infants in mountains.

Konaki-jijii's Debut as Kitaro's AllyMizuki Shigeru, GeGeGe no Kitaro manga chapters and subsequent anime episodes (Toei Animation)

1966-1968 (manga's Kitaro Family formation; anime debut 1968)

In Mizuki's reimagining, Konaki-jijii is introduced not as a predator but as a wise, elderly yokai who lives in the Yokai Forest and serves as an advisor and babysitter to Kitaro. When Kitaro faces an enemy yokai, Konaki-jijii offers to help: he leaps onto the foe's back, reverts to his heavy form, and pins the enemy with his weight while Kitaro attacks. His loyalty and gruff affection make him a core member of the Kitaro Family alongside Medama-oyaji, Nezumi-otoko, Sunakake-baba, and Ittan-momen. Transforms Konaki-jijii from obscure regional bogey into nationally beloved character; establishes heroic elder archetype that dominates all later depictions; creates merchandising and statue presence.

Accounts II
The Weight Battle Against Gyuki and Other Heavy FoesGeGeGe no Kitaro anime episodes (notably 1985 series ep. 8 and 2007 series; manga parallels)

1985, 2007, 2018 (recurring set-piece)

In a recurring anime plot, a powerful enemy yokai that relies on size or strength (such as Gyuki or Daimakai) confronts Kitaro. Konaki-jijii volunteers as a counter: he allows himself to be picked up or jumps onto the enemy, then activates his weight ability to its maximum, causing the giant foe to stagger, sink into the ground, or collapse. The comedy comes from the tiny old man-baby outweighing a monster many times his size. He often shouts 'Omoku natta zo!' (I've gotten heavy!) as his attack line. Codifies his power as a controllable combat technique rather than curse; provides template for video game movesets; reinforces theme that wisdom and technique beat brute force.

The Mizuki Shigeru Road Encounter - Contemporary Pilgrimage LegendMunicipal folklore / tourism narrative, Sakaiminato City, Tottori; guidebooks and visitor accounts 1993-present

1993-present (Heisei-Reiwa)

Visitors to Mizuki Shigeru Road in Sakaiminato encounter the bronze statue of Konaki-jijii clinging to a post. Local guides retell the Tokushima legend in abbreviated form, and a new custom has emerged: rubbing the statue's head or belly for blessings related to safe child-rearing and protection from shoulder/back pain (katakori), playing on his weight-on-back motif. Children pose pretending to be crushed by him. The story is now told as heritage rather than warning. Demonstrates how a regional yokai becomes national heritage through Mizuki's mediation; creates new ritual practice with no historical precedent; sustains local economy.

Konaki-jijii and the Obariyon Confusion - The Two Heavy Yokai TaleYokai encyclopedias and fan discourse, comparing Mizuki's entries for Konaki-jijii and Obariyon

1970s-present (encyclopedia era)

Encyclopedia compilers noted that Shikoku actually has two distinct heavy yokai: Konaki-jijii (cries like baby, held in arms, becomes heavy) and Obariyon (jumps on back without crying, becomes heavy). Mizuki illustrated both, but popular retellings merged them into one 'baby that jumps on your back and gets heavy.' Modern yokai books must explicitly untangle them, often presenting them on facing pages to show the difference: Konaki-jijii = arms, Obariyon = back. The story of the confusion itself has become part of the lore. Highlights how yokai taxonomy is constructed and how franchise canon can both preserve and blur regional distinctions; scholarly caution about treating encyclopedia entries as folklore.

Konaki-jijii appears in Toriyama Sekien's Edo-period yokai scrolls. — It does not. Sekien's Gazu Hyakki Yagyo (1776) and sequels contain no Konaki-jijii. The claim arises from assuming all famous yokai are Edo-period. Its visual canon is entirely Mizuki (1960s).

Konaki-jijii is a kind, helpful grandfather yokai in Japanese folklore. — In folklore it is a frightening, potentially lethal trickster that exploits compassion. The kind grandfather is Mizuki's mid-20th-century reinterpretation for children's manga, which has overwritten the folkloric version in popular memory.

Konaki-jijii jumps on your back and gets heavy. — That is Obariyon's method. Konaki-jijii is traditionally picked up in the arms like a baby after hearing crying. The back-riding conflation is common because both are Shikoku heavy yokai and are often listed together.

The name means 'crying baby old man' as in an old man who is crying. — The grammar is 'child-crying old man' - an old man who cries like a child (or causes a child-like crying). The cry is the lure, not the old man's own weeping in self-pity. Japanese ko-naki = child crying.
