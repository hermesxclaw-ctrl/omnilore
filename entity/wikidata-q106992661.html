The Approach

In the heart of the Venezuelan forest, a girl with a voice like a bird's weaves through the emerald shadows, speaking a language that predates words. She is Rima, the Daughter of the Didi, a creature of myth and wilderness who has haunted the edges of civilization since W. H. Hudson first conjured her in 1904. Her story is one of loss, love, and the brutal collision between the wild and the modern—a tale that has been reshaped by film, comics, and cartoons, yet never fully tamed.

Content advisory. Contains depictions of violence, death by fire, and colonialist attitudes typical of early 20th-century literature. Some adaptations contain racial stereotypes and cultural appropriation.

Rima

Etymology

The name 'Rima' is possibly derived from the Spanish 'rima' (rhyme) or from the Venezuelan indigenous word for 'bird'. Hudson never explicitly states the origin.

Cultural Ownership

Originating in British literature (W. H. Hudson, Argentine-British), the character has been adapted into American film, comics, and animation. The novel is set in the tropical forests of South America, likely Venezuela, and draws on indigenous legends.

Disputed Identifications

Some have speculated that Rima was based on a real indigenous woman or a legend of a lost white tribe, but Hudson's inspiration is not definitively documented. The DC Comics version differs significantly from the novel's depiction, leading to confusion about her canonical appearance and abilities.

Description

Appearance

In the novel, Rima is described as small (4'6"), demure, dark-haired, and about 17 years old. She is of mysterious origin, possibly of white ancestry, but raised in the jungle. In the 1959 film, Audrey Hepburn portrayed her with a more conventional Hollywood look, with dark hair and delicate features. In DC Comics' 1974 series, she is depicted as a grown woman with ash-blonde hair, wearing a leopard-skin bikini, and is more physically powerful. In the 2010 First Wave series, she is portrayed as a South American native with piercings and tattoos, not speaking but communicating in bird-like whistles.

Symbols & Iconography

Rima is associated with birds, particularly the 'bell bird' whose song she imitates. She is often depicted with a bird perched on her shoulder or hand. The name 'Rima' itself is linked to the Spanish word for rhyme, suggesting a poetic connection. In visual adaptations, she is often shown in a jungle setting with lush vegetation and wildlife.

Behaviour & Personality

In the novel, Rima is shy, gentle, and speaks in a bird-like language, reflecting her closeness to nature. She is curious about Abel but fearful of other humans. She has a deep spiritual connection to the forest and its creatures. In the DC Comics version, she is more assertive and heroic, using her abilities to protect the jungle from threats. In First Wave, she is silent and mysterious, communicating only through whistles.

Domains & Habitats

Rima's domain is the tropical rainforest of South America, specifically the region that is now Venezuela. She lives in a secluded part of the forest, away from human settlements, and is deeply tied to the ecosystem. In adaptations, she is often shown as the protector of this environment.

Version Differences

The novel's Rima is a tragic figure who dies by fire, whereas the comic book version survives and continues to have adventures. The comic book Rima is more physically capable and less ethereal. The First Wave version is more grounded in indigenous culture, with a different visual design and communication style. The film adaptation deviated significantly from the novel, altering the plot and characterization.

Powers & Abilities

Communication with Birds

Source Version Novel, DC Comics, First Wave (via whistles)

Description

Rima can understand and speak the language of birds, and can communicate with them. She uses this ability to gather information and summon birds for assistance.

Limitations

Limited to birds; in the novel, her speech is described as bird-like, not a universal animal language.

Contradictions

In the novel, she speaks a 'bird-like language' but also speaks Spanish to Abel. In First Wave, she does not speak at all, only whistles.

Animal Friendship

Source Version DC Comics, Super Friends cartoon

Description

She can befriend and command animals, such as calling a bear to push down trees or summoning crocodiles to attack enemies.

Limitations

Seems to require mutual trust; animals respond to her commands but not always instantaneously.

Contradictions

In the novel, she is more passive and does not command animals in such an active manner.

Agility and Speed

Source Version Novel, DC Comics

Description

She is extremely agile and swift, able to move through the jungle with ease.

Limitations

Not superhuman, but exceptional for a human.

Contradictions

None noted.

Plucking Poison Darts from Air

Source Version DC Comics

Description

She can catch poison darts mid-air, showcasing her reflexes and speed.

Limitations

Requires quick reflexes; not a supernatural ability.

Contradictions

Not present in the novel.

Reputation for Magic

Source Version Novel, DC Comics

Description

Natives believe she has magical powers, which serves as a defense mechanism.

It is a reputation, not actual magic; her 'magic' is her natural abilities.

In the novel, the natives fear her as the Daughter of the Didi, an evil spirit, but they do not attribute specific powers to her.

Stories & Myths

Green Mansions

Source W. H. Hudson's novelCulture British/Argentine literaturePeriod 1904

Summary

Abel, a political fugitive from Venezuela, flees into the jungle and encounters Rima, a mysterious girl who lives among the birds. They develop a deep bond, and Abel falls in love with her. Rima reveals that she is of unknown origin, possibly from a lost tribe. The local natives fear her, and when they discover her hiding place, they set the forest ablaze, killing Rima. Abel is devastated and leaves the jungle.

Participants

Abel

Rima

Natives (the tribe of the Didi)

Consequences

Rima's death symbolizes the destruction of the wilderness. Abel's grief and departure highlight the theme of loss.

Variants

The 1959 film changes the ending, with Rima surviving and Abel staying with her. The DC Comics version also has her survive.

Rima the Jungle Girl (comic series)

Source DC ComicsCulture American comicsPeriod 1974-1975

Summary

Rima is depicted as a blonde, powerful jungle heroine who protects her domain from poachers, criminals, and supernatural threats. She is aided by her ability to communicate with animals and her agility. The series ran for seven issues, featuring various adventures.

Participants

Consequences

The series was cancelled after seven issues, but Rima remained a cult favorite.

Variants

Dark History
Content advisory. The novel contains the brutal death of Rima by fire, which is a form of violence. The story also reflects colonialist attitudes towards indigenous peoples, depicting them as superstitious and savage. The character of Rima is a 'noble savage' trope, which can be problematic.

Death by Fire

Source Green Mansions

Description

In the novel, Rima is burned alive by the native tribe, a violent and tragic end. This is a central plot point and reflects the destruction of nature.

Scholarly Context

This act can be interpreted as a metaphor for the destruction of the wilderness by civilization, but it also perpetuates negative stereotypes of indigenous people as violent and irrational.

Colonialist and Racist Stereotypes

Source Green Mansions, film adaptations

Description

The novel and early adaptations portray the native South Americans as superstitious and savage, while Rima, who is possibly of white descent, is seen as more noble. This reflects the colonialist mindset of the time.

Scholarly Context

Modern scholarship critiques these portrayals as racist and imperialistic. The 'noble savage' trope is problematic as it romanticizes indigenous people while still considering them inferior.

Cultural Appropriation

Source Green Mansions, DC Comics

Description

The character of Rima, created by a British author, draws on South American indigenous legends and culture without full understanding or representation. Later adaptations by American companies further appropriated the character.

Scholarly Context

This raises questions about who has the right to tell these stories and how indigenous cultures are represented.
