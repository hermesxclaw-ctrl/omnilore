Who It Is

King Arthur is the central figure of the Matter of Britain — the legendary king of the Britons whose court at Camelot became the idealized mirror of chivalry, whose Knights of the Round Table pursued the Holy Grail, and whose prophesied return makes him the 'Once and Future King.' The historical Arthur, if he existed, was likely a Romano-British war leader (dux bellorum) who led the native Britons against Saxon invaders in the late 5th and early 6th centuries. The earliest source, the 9th-century Historia Brittonum attributed to Nennius, lists twelve battles Arthur fought, culminating at Mount Badon where he single-handedly killed 960 men. Early Welsh poetry — particularly the Y Gododdin and the poems attributed to Taliesin — mentions Arthur as a peerless warrior. However, Arthur was transformed from regional warlord to world-historical monarch by Geoffrey of Monmouth's Historia Regum Britanniae (c. 1136), which provided the first full biography: Arthur's conception at Tintagel through Merlin's magical assistance (Uther Pendragon transformed into Gorlois's likeness); his coronation at fifteen; his conquest of Britain, Ireland, Iceland, Norway, and Gaul; his defeat of the Roman Empire; and his final betrayal by his nephew Mordred at the Battle of Camlann, where Arthur is mortally wounded and carried to the Isle of Avalon. Geoffrey's work, blending history, legend, and pure invention, launched the entire Arthurian literary tradition. Chrétien de Troyes (late 12th century) added Lancelot, the Grail quest, and the conventions of courtly love. Thomas Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur (1485) synthesized the entire cycle into its definitive English form, establishing the tragic arc from Arthur's illegitimate conception to the destruction of the Round Table. The Arthurian legend embodies the paradox of idealized kingship: Arthur creates the perfect chivalric order, but its very perfection contains the seeds of its destruction — through the adulterous love of Lancelot and Guinevere, the incestuous birth of Mordred, and the fatal flaw of Arthur's own judgment. The 'Once and Future King' prophecy — that Arthur sleeps in Avalon and will return in Britain's hour of greatest need — transforms a historical defeat into a messianic promise, making Arthur Britain's national myth.

Form

No fixed canonical appearance. In the earliest Welsh material Arthur is a ferocious warrior, laughing as he personally slaughters witches and giants; he carries Caledfwlch (the sword later Excalibur) and leads an Otherworld-connected warband. In later romance he becomes a wise, dignified, even-tempered, somewhat bland, occasionally feeble 'blessed king.' Medieval iconography and the romances give him a crown, sword, and shield (often the Pridwen/Whiting shield in Welsh sources); the Nine-Worthies art shows a bearded or beardless monarch. No portrait survives from before Geoffrey's era, and 'appearance' is therefore mostly reconstructed or adaptation-specific (e.g., Clive Owen's gritty warlord in the 2004 King Arthur film, the boy 'Wart' in T. H. White).

Powers

Superhuman martial prowess In the pre-Galfridian Welsh and Galfridian traditions Arthur is a peerless warrior who personally mows down enemies. The Historia Brittonum credits him with slaying 960 men single-handedly at the Battle of Badon. In Culhwch and Olwen and 'Pa gur' he laughs while hunting giants and witches. Y Gododdin uses him as the ceiling of martial valour: a warrior who slew 300 'was no Arthur.' Historia Brittonum; Culhwch and Olwen; 'Pa gur'; Y Gododdin (Welsh-layered tradition) In the continental romances he is largely sidelined; his personal martial power is de-emphasized in favour of the knights (Lancelot, Galahad, Gawain). The martial Arthur (Welsh/Geoffrey) vs. the passive roi fainéant (romance). Not a seamlessly merged power set.

Wielding Excalibur / Caledfwlch Arthur's sword. In the romance tradition he draws it either from a stone (the Sword in the Stone test legitimizing his right to the throne) or receives it from the Lady of the Lake. Its Welsh ancestor is Caledfwlch; Geoffrey and later tradition Latinize it as Caliburnus/Excalibur. Culhwch and Olwen (Caledfwlch); Geoffrey of Monmouth (Caliburnus); Vulgate/high medieval romance (Ladies of the Lake, the Sword in the Stone and the lake-sword) The 'sword in the stone' and the 'lake sword' are two separate traditions, sometimes treated as successive (Malory's Arthur breaks one sword and receives another from the lake). The two should not be silently merged. Sword-in-stone vs. lady-of-the-lake; whether Excalibur is one sword or two; the scabbard (Welsh Caledfwlch's sheath makes its wielder invulnerable — a detail the romances drop).

Command and the Round Table ideal Arthur's abiding power is his kingship and the chivalric order he institutes — the Round Table, where no one sits higher than another, harnesses Might for Right. In White's Once and Future King Merlyn creates it explicitly as an institution to redirect destructive might toward good. It becomes the engine of dozens of quests and the outward glory of Camelot. The prose romances and Malory (institution); explicitly thematized by T. H. White (The Queen of Air and Darkness) and Tennyson The Round Table's equality is undermined by the Lancelot-Guinevere adultery and Mordred's factionalism, which splinter the court. Romance texts are internally inconsistent about how many knights sit at the Table and whether Arthur (as the 'first among equals') is really bound by its rules.

The messianic return / Unfading kingship A central motif: Arthur did not die at Camlann but was taken to Avalon / sleeps in a remote cave, awaiting his return. 'Rex quondam rexque futurus' — the once and future king. First attested as a live popular belief by William of Malmesbury (1125) and Hériman of Tournai (c. 1145, events of 1113). Pre-Galfridian absence-of-grave tradition (Englynion y Beddau); William of Malmesbury; Hériman of Tournai; post-1191 folklore of sleeping kings in caves A believed-return, not an exercised power; it was a serious embarrassment to contemporary Christianity (a rival 'returning king' competing with the return of Jesus). The return belief vs. the 1191 'discovery' of Arthur's tomb at Glastonbury, which was designed to bury the belief.

Otherworld patronage / supernatural warband In the earliest Welsh material Arthur's warband includes former pagan gods, his wife is clearly Otherworldly, and he raids the Otherworld fortress of Annwn for treasure and prisoners (Preiddeu Annwn). This makes him a mediator between mortal Britain and the supernatural. Preiddeu Annwn; Welsh Triads; 'Pa gur'; Culhwch and Olwen This strand is largely drowned out after Geoffrey's historicization; the romance and modern Arthur is mostly a mortal king. Claims of Arthur-as-forgotten-Celtic-deity are a minority scholarly position (parallels to totemic Hengist/Horsa). Human historical warlord vs. supernatural Otherworld hero — the two are not reconciled in the sources.

Rule over an empire Geoffrey's Arthur conquers Ireland, Iceland, the Orkneys, Norway, Denmark and Gaul, defeating the Roman procurator Lucius Tiberius before turning back on Mordred's usurpation. Geoffrey of Monmouth, Historia Regum Britanniae The imperial conquests are Geoffrey's literary invention; 16th-century humanist Polydore Vergil demolished the claim that Arthur ruled a post-Roman empire. Geoffrey's continent-spanning emperor vs. the pre-Galfridian island-warrior vs. the modern consensus of his non-historicity.

Accounts

Uther Pendragon, king of the Britons, covets Ygerna (Igraine), wife of his rival Duke Gorlois of Cornwall. With Merlin's magic he is disguised as Gorlois and sleeps with Ygerna at Tintagel, conceiving Arthur. Gorlois is killed in the same conflict, and Uther marries Ygerna; Arthur is born, but his paternity is secret and he is raised apart by the foster-father Sir Ector.

On Uther's death the succession is in question. An anvil (or stone) holds a sword; whoever draws it is the rightful king. The young, unknown 'Wart' (orphaned/fostered boy) draws the sword seemingly by accident during a youthful errand for his foster-brother Kay, and is proclaimed king — the Round Table ideal of right-by-worth over right-by-blood.

Arthur fights twelve battles against the invading Anglo-Saxons across Britain, culminating at the Battle of Badon (Mons Badonicus), where he is said to have personally slain 960 men. The Annales Cambriae date Badon to 516-518. The victory is widely associated with halting Saxon expansion for a generation.

Arthur sails into the Welsh Otherworld, Annwn, in his ship Prydwen to raid its fortresses, seize its treasure (including the magic cauldron of the Head of Annwn), and free its prisoners. The expedition costs many of his men; only seven return. The motif ties Arthur to the Otherworld and to the Celtic cauldron-of-rebirth myth that later feeds the Grail theme.

Arthur's kinsman Culhwch sets out to win Olwen, daughter of the giant Ysbaddaden Chief-Giant, who refuses her unless impossible tasks are completed — including the hunt of the great semi-divine boar Twrch Trwyth. Arthur and his 200-man warband help fulfil the tasks, hunts the boar across Ireland and Wales, and finally the giant is brought down so the couple can marry.

The greatest knight, Lancelot, and Queen Guinevere carry on a long, passion-torn adulterous affair. Arthur — in the romance tradition already a roi fainéant — knows or half-knows but is slow to act. The affair, once exposed, destroys the balance of the Round Table, drives Lancelot and Guinevere apart, and precipitates the civil war that ends the kingdom.

While Arthur is abroad (defeating the Romans in Gaul, in Geoffrey), his nephew/half-son Mordred seizes the throne and marries the queen. Arthur returns and meets Mordred at the river Camlann; in the Annales Cambriae both fall/kill each other (537-539). In Malory the two armies annihilate each other and Arthur is mortally wounded as he kills Mordred.

Mortally wounded at Camlann, Arthur hands his crown to his kinsman Constantine and is taken by boat (with three queens, in Malory) to the island of Avalon to be healed — never to be seen again by mortal eyes. This seeds the 'once and future king' return motif and frames Arthur's end not as death but as translation to another realm.

Scholarly Debates
Historicity vs. pure legend
Arthur was a real Romano-British war-leader against the Saxons (Historia Brittonum, Annales Cambriae). ⇌ Arthur is entirely fictional, a historicized folklore/possibly half-forgotten Celtic deity (Dumville: 'no historical evidence about Arthur; we must reject him from our histories').
Arthur as king vs. dux bellorum
Arthur was a king of all Britain. ⇌ Neither the Historia nor the Annales calls him rex; they say dux bellorum ('leader of wars') and miles ('soldier').
Sword in the stone vs. Lady of the Lake swords
Excalibur is the sword pulled from the stone/anvil. ⇌ Excalibur is a sword given by the Lady of the Lake, distinct from the stone-sword (which in Malory is broken).
Mordred: usurper vs. neutral, nephew vs. son
Mordred is Arthur's traitorous nephew who seizes the throne and marries Guinevere. ⇌ In the earliest Welsh tradition Medraut is simply a warrior who dies with Arthur at Camlann — no treason. In the Vulgate he is Arthur's incestuous SON, not just nephew.
Guinevere's infidelity
Guinevere is unfaithful to Arthur with Lancelot, causing Camelot's fall. ⇌ In early Welsh tradition Gwenhwyfar is abducted/rescued and the famous Lancelot affair is a later French invention.

A way to hold it in your mind

On a wet ridge in the sub-Roman twilight of a collapsing Britain, a war-leader no one had ever crowned turns a tide of battle and vanishes into an island of mist — and for the next fourteen centuries, a whole Europe refuses to let him die. King Arthur is the most successful ghost in the history of storytelling: a Brythonic Celtic warlord who may never have existed at all, who by the twelfth century had been rewritten as an emperor who conquered Gaul, by the thirteenth as a cuckold whose best friend slept with his queen, by the nineteenth as the Victorians' doomed portrait of ideal manhood, and by the twenty-first as a meme, a franchise, and a name tattooed on everything from kingfisher uniforms to video-game quests. He is 'the once and future king' because the legend never lets him finish dying. What makes him uncanny is that every civilisation that retold him rewrote him in its own image — and the man himself stayed a blank slate, a dux bellorum (a leader of wars, never in the earliest attestation a king) onto which a millennium of anxiety about power, love, faith and failure has been projected. He was less a king than a mirror.
