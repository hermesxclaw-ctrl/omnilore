Who It Is

Bragi is the Norse god of poetry, eloquence, and the skaldic art — the divine poet who welcomes heroes into Valhalla. He is the son of Odin and the giantess Gunnlod (from whom Odin stole the mead of poetry). Bragi is depicted as a long-bearded, wise figure, often shown with a harp or rune-carved tongue. His wife is Idun, the goddess of youth and keeper of the golden apples. In Valhalla, Bragi recites poetry to the einherjar and greets newly arrived warriors. He is sometimes identified with the historical skald Bragi Boddason, a 9th-century Norwegian poet, suggesting the figure may be a deified poet rather than an original god. The phrase 'bragr' (poetry, the best) is etymologically related to his name. In the Gesta Danorum, Bragi is a legendary wise king. Bragi represents the divine origin of the skaldic tradition — the belief that poetry was a gift of the gods, intoxicating and inspired, drawn from the mead of poetry.

Origins

Bragi is the son of Odin, born after Odin seduced the giantess Gunnlod and drank the mead of poetry. In some sources, Bragi is simply a wise god who presided over poetry. His name became synonymous with the finest poetry in Old Norse — poems were called 'bragr.'

Attested in Poetic Edda / Prose Edda (Skáldskaparmál) / Skaldic Poetry TraditionEra Viking Age (c. 800-1100 CE), recorded 13th century

Form

Bragi's physical appearance is only very sparsely described, and much of what is claimed for him is inferred rather than vivid iconography. Snorri's kenning catalogue refers to him as the 'long-bearded Ás' (hinn langskeggi áss), and from that phrase derives the expression 'beard-bragi' applied to any man with a conspicuous beard — a beard-reading that treats the poet's beard as a marker of age, wisdom and gravitas. Otherwise the texts give him no fixed weapon, mount or distinctive garment. Artistic tradition (notably the Swedish National Romantic painter Nils Blommér in his 1846 'Brage sittande vid harpan', 'Brage seated with the harp') associates him with a harp or lyre and with attributes of song and wisdom, but this iconography is a nineteenth-century invention rather than a medieval attestation; the Old Norse texts do not give Bragi a harp.

Powers

Eloquence and mastery of language Bragi's core 'power' is extraordinary eloquence and command of language, for which Snorri says he is 'renowned ... especially'. He is 'knowledgeable about poetry', and from his name the noun bragr/'poetry' and the title brag ('chief' of eloquent men or women) are said to derive. In practice this manifests as the ability to name, praise, welcome and instruct — the poet-god's gift of ordered, life-affirming speech. Prose Edda (Gylfaginning); Skáldskaparmál It is emphatically not a martial power: in Lokasenna Bragi is mocked as 'the wariest of war and shyest of shooting' and 'the bench-ornament'. His eloquence is verbal and social, not physical or aggressive. Whether the eloquence is a god's intrinsic power or merely the literary mirror of a great dead poet's remembered skill is the crux of the whole God-vs-deified-skald debate.

Poetry-making / inspiration as god of bragr Bragi is the god to whom poetry (bragr) is attached. In the Skáldskaparmál he is the internal authority who recounts the origin of the mead of poetry — the drink made from the blood of Kvasir mixed with honey that turns whoever drinks it into a skilled poet or scholar. Bragi thus stands at the point where sacred utterance, knowledge and art converge. Skáldskaparmál (the Ægir/Bragi dialogue, chapters 56-57) Not all poets fall under his aegis: in the mead myth Odin — not Bragi — is the one who wins and shares the mead, and the rhyme-poet's share that Odin drops is explicitly low-grade. Bragi is the emblem/patron of skaldic art in Snorri's scheme, while Odin is the possessor and dispenser of the mead. Scholars differ on whether Bragi is a 'poetry god' in a narrow sense or better read (per Jan de Vries) as the personification of praise-poetry — the social institution by which poets confer honour or inflict shame.

Welcoming the dead / threshold-greeter of Valhalla In the skaldic encomia Eiríksmál and Hákonarmál, Bragi appears within Valhalla as the figure who greets and receives slain kings (Eiríkr Bloodaxe, Hákon the Good) who arrive at Odin's hall. Odin commands Hermóðr and Bragi to 'go to meet the prince'. This role — the eloquent welcome of the honoured dead — is Bragi's most concrete function in the early poetry. Eiríksmál (c. 954); Hákonarmál (c. 961) The same poems simultaneously call Hermóðr both a god and a hero, and the received criticism is that Bragi here could equally be a deceased poet or a god or a dead hero — the divine status is not explicit. It is exactly this ambiguity that grounds the deified-skald hypothesis: the greeter-of-kings may be the remembered court poet Bragi Boddason imagined welcoming his former patrons' souls.

Regulated speech against discord (the 'praise-poet' counterweight) Following Jan de Vries's structural reading of Indo-European praise-poetry, Bragi functions as the embodiment of regulated, life-affirming speech that maintains social order, prestige and royal authority — the counterweight to Logan-Loki's verbal hostility. In Lokasenna he is the first feasting god to challenge Loki and offers gifts to avert the quarrel. Lokasenna; de Vries's comparative Indo-European analysis This is an interpretive reconstruction, not an attested 'ability' — flagged as scholarly hypothesis rather than narrative fact. Heinz Klingenberg considers de Vries's larger life-rebirth/fertility conclusions speculative. The social-order reading is more widely accepted than the fertility-god reading.

Rune-splendour / the tongue as a surface for speech-runes In Sigrdrífumál, the catalogue of rune-carving surfaces lists 'on Bragi's tongue' as one of the places where runes are cut; these are then shaved off, mixed with mead and shared among Æsir, elves, Vanir and men as speech-runes, birth-runes, ale-runes and magic-runes. The coupling of Bragi's tongue with speech-runes reinforces his identity as the god of spoken utterance. Sigrdrífumál (Poetic Edda) The stanza's meaning is notoriously unclear, and Bragi appears here in a list of surfaces (the sun, a bear's paw, an eagle's beak, a wolf's claw) rather than as an agent. It is not a discrete 'power he wields' so much as a metonymic tribute to eloquent speech. Interpreters dispute whether 'Bragi' here is the god, the archetypal poet, or a kenning-figure.

Doubtful: fertility / life-renewal association Jan de Vries, linking bragr's poetic sense to Old Indic bráhmaṇ- and to Bragi's marriage to Iðunn (goddess of rejuvenating apples), proposed that Bragi may have deeper roots as a deity of renewed life and fertility, comparable to figures titled 'lord'/'prince' such as Freyr, where hero-cult, fertility-cult and life-creating speech converge. Jan de Vries's reconstruction (20th-century comparative philology) Heinz Klingenberg calls these conclusions speculative; the connection to Iðunn may simply encode the complementarity between Bragi's imperishable word and Iðunn's rejuvenation, without any ancient fertility cult. This power should be treated as a modern hypothesis, not attested myth. Directly contested between de Vries (fertility origins) and Klingenberg (literary complementarity only).

Accounts

At a feast hosted by the sea-giant/god Ægir, Loki twice gains entry after invoking an old oath of blood-brotherhood with Odin and is served ale by Víðarr. Before drinking, he toasts the gods but pointedly excludes Bragi. Bragi, seated with his wife Iðunn, first offers Loki a horse, a sword and a ring to buy peace, warning him not to repay the Æsir with hatred. Loki responds by calling Bragi the 'wariest of war and shyest of shooting' — a cowardly bench-ornament. Bragi retorts that were he outside the hall he would hold Loki's head in his hand; Loki taunts that brave people act, they do not threaten from their seats. Iðunn intervenes, begging Bragi to respect kin-ties and not blame Loki. This single scene is Bragi's most developed narrative appearance and the source of the enduring image of a peaceable, eloquent but unwarlike god.

The Skáldskaparmál is framed as a dialogue in which the sea-giant Ægir, visiting Asgard, sits beside Bragi and is told the origin of skaldic poetry. Bragi recounts how, at the end of the Æsir-Vanir War, the two factions sealed peace by spitting into a vat; from that saliva was born the wisest of men, Kvasir. The dwarves Fjalarr and Galarr murdered Kvasir, mixed his blood with honey in the vessels Boðn, Són and Óðrerir, and thereby brewed the mead of poetry — anyone who drinks it becomes a skald or a scholar. After further dwarfish murders (of the giant Gillingr and his wife) and the ransom offer to Suttungr, the mead was hidden in Hnitbjörg, guarded by Suttungr's daughter Gunnlöð. Odin, in disguise as Bölverkr, won and stole it, drinking all three vats in three sips after spending three nights with Gunnlöð, then flying off as an eagle and disgorging the mead into Asgard. This myth is the charter of skaldic art, and Bragi — the god of poetry — is the one who retells it. It is the single most important primary text for understanding his domain.

In the course of the Ægir-Bragi dialogue, Bragi recounts how the giant Þjazi, in eagle form, forced Loki to lure Iðunn — keeper of the apples that keep the gods young — out of Asgard. Iðunn is carried off to Þrymheimr; the gods begin to grow old and grey. Loki, threatened with death and torture, borrows Freyja's falcon-cloak, transforms Iðunn into a nut and flies back to Asgard, with Þjazi pursuing as an eagle. The Æsir light a fire that sends Þjazi crashing to his death through the flames. Bragi, telling this story, is the loving husband narrating the perilous abduction of his own wife — reinforcing his link to Iðunn and to the preservation of divine vitality.

In Eiríksmál, Odin hears the approach of the slain king Eiríkr Bloodaxe and his retinue and orders the heroes Sigmundr and Sinfjötli to rise and greet him; Bragi then speaks, asking how Odin can be sure the newcomer is Eiríkr and why such a king was allowed to fall. Odin replies that it is Eiríkr and that his arrival is certain. In Hákonarmál, Odin likewise tells Hermóðr and Bragi to go and meet the dead Hákon the Good. In both, Bragi functions as the eloquent guardian-greeter of Valhalla's threshold — a role consistent with the remembered great skald welcoming the kings he once praised in life.

Scholarly Debates
Etymology of the name: which bragr?
Bragi derives from bragr meaning 'the first, the noblest, the foremost' (cf. bragnar 'chiefs', bragningr 'king', OE brego 'lord'), so the god is fundamentally a 'chieftain/lord' figure. ⇌ Bragi derives from bragr meaning 'poetry' (cf. Icelandic bragur 'poem, verse'), making him intrinsically the poetry-god.
God, or deified poet?
Bragi is a posthumously deified human skald — the ninth-century Bragi Boddason 'the Old', traditional first skald, elevated after death to godhood. ⇌ Bragi is an independent pre-Christian deity (or a personification of poetry with older roots), not derived from any one poet.
Son of Odin — biological or adoptive?
Bragi is literally the son of Odin, as Snorri states and as skalds are instructed to call him. ⇌ Bragi was at most an adoptive/fostered son of Odin, or not Odin's son at all in the older sources.
Which Bragi does Grímnismál mean?
The 'Bragi of poets' superlative in Grímnismál refers to the god Bragi. ⇌ It refers to the historical skald Bragi Boddason, remembered as the archetypal poet.
Iðunn's brother — was Bragi his killer?
Bragi slew Iðunn's brother (implied in Lokasenna; the couple it leaves in an incest-and-kin-murder shadow). ⇌ No such brother or killing existed; Loki's insinuation is pure flyting slander.

What We Don't Know

Sparse primary evidence: Bragi survives only in sparse allusions, never as a fully dramatised divine hero outside the Lokasenna flyting and the framing of the Skáldskaparmál. No cult site, temple, votive inscription or pre-Christian ritual evidence for Bragi exists — his status as a 'worshipped' god is therefore unverifiable and likely negative. The exact sense of the bragr etymology remains unresolved and lacks first-order philological consensus I could cite precisely. I could not confirm which specific Johnson brother maps to Bragi in The Almighty Johnsons (the cast table in the extract was empty) — the show is verified as a Norse-gods adaptation but the Bragi-specific mapping is noted cautiously. A way to hold it in your mind

There may be no god in the Norse pantheon whose very existence is a poem — and no god whose divinity the scholars themselves are still arguing over. Bragi, the silver-tongued 'god of poets', is chiefly known to us by his nickname in an insult: Loki, the mischief-maker, calls him 'Bragi the bench-ornament'. And that single sneer points to the deepest mystery in Norse myth. If most modern researchers are right, Bragi was never a god who was worshipped in a temple. He was a man — a ninth-century Norwegian court poet named Bragi the Old, the first skald whose verses we still have — who was so beloved and so gifted that in the space of a few generations the poets who came after him remembered him straight out of a mortal grave and into the hall of Odin, where dead kings are welcomed by the very man who once praised them in life.
