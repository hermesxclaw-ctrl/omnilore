Who It Is

There may be no god in the Norse pantheon whose very existence is a poem — and no god whose divinity the scholars themselves are still arguing over. Bragi, the silver-tongued 'god of poets', is chiefly known to us by his nickname in an insult: Loki, the mischief-maker, calls him 'Bragi the bench-ornament'. And that single sneer points to the deepest mystery in Norse myth. If most modern researchers are right, Bragi was never a god who was worshipped in a temple. He was a man — a ninth-century Norwegian court poet named Bragi the Old, the first skald whose verses we still have — who was so beloved and so gifted that in the space of a few generations the poets who came after him remembered him straight out of a mortal grave and into the hall of Odin, where dead kings are welcomed by the very man who once praised them in life.

Across texts Bragi emerges as the god of measured, eloquent, life-affirming speech — the antithesis of Loki's corrosive flyting. He is wise and learned, 'renowned for wisdom and especially for eloquence and command of language' (Gylfaginning). In the Skáldskaparmál he is cast as the master-narrator, calmly explaining the mead of poetry and the kennings of the gods. In skaldic encomia he is the courteous greeter-of-arrivals in Valhalla, welcoming dead kings. Powers
Eloquence and mastery of language

Bragi's core 'power' is extraordinary eloquence and command of language, for which Snorri says he is 'renowned ... especially'. He is 'knowledgeable about poetry', and from his name the noun bragr/'poetry' and the title brag ('chief' of eloquent men or women) are said to derive. In practice this manifests as the ability to name, praise, welcome and instruct — the poet-god's gift of ordered, life-affirming speech.

Limit: It is emphatically not a martial power: in Lokasenna Bragi is mocked as 'the wariest of war and shyest of shooting' and 'the bench-ornament'. His eloquence is verbal and social, not physical or aggressive.

Poetry-making / inspiration as god of bragr

Bragi is the god to whom poetry (bragr) is attached. In the Skáldskaparmál he is the internal authority who recounts the origin of the mead of poetry — the drink made from the blood of Kvasir mixed with honey that turns whoever drinks it into a skilled poet or scholar. Bragi thus stands at the point where sacred utterance, knowledge and art converge.

Limit: Not all poets fall under his aegis: in the mead myth Odin — not Bragi — is the one who wins and shares the mead, and the rhyme-poet's share that Odin drops is explicitly low-grade. Bragi is the emblem/patron of skaldic art in Snorri's scheme, while Odin is the possessor and dispenser of the mead.

Welcoming the dead / threshold-greeter of Valhalla

In the skaldic encomia Eiríksmál and Hákonarmál, Bragi appears within Valhalla as the figure who greets and receives slain kings (Eiríkr Bloodaxe, Hákon the Good) who arrive at Odin's hall. Odin commands Hermóðr and Bragi to 'go to meet the prince'. This role — the eloquent welcome of the honoured dead — is Bragi's most concrete function in the early poetry.

Limit: The same poems simultaneously call Hermóðr both a god and a hero, and the received criticism is that Bragi here could equally be a deceased poet or a god or a dead hero — the divine status is not explicit.

Regulated speech against discord (the 'praise-poet' counterweight)

Following Jan de Vries's structural reading of Indo-European praise-poetry, Bragi functions as the embodiment of regulated, life-affirming speech that maintains social order, prestige and royal authority — the counterweight to Logan-Loki's verbal hostility. In Lokasenna he is the first feasting god to challenge Loki and offers gifts to avert the quarrel.

Limit: This is an interpretive reconstruction, not an attested 'ability' — flagged as scholarly hypothesis rather than narrative fact.

Rune-splendour / the tongue as a surface for speech-runes

In Sigrdrífumál, the catalogue of rune-carving surfaces lists 'on Bragi's tongue' as one of the places where runes are cut; these are then shaved off, mixed with mead and shared among Æsir, elves, Vanir and men as speech-runes, birth-runes, ale-runes and magic-runes. The coupling of Bragi's tongue with speech-runes reinforces his identity as the god of spoken utterance.

Limit: The stanza's meaning is notoriously unclear, and Bragi appears here in a list of surfaces (the sun, a bear's paw, an eagle's beak, a wolf's claw) rather than as an agent. It is not a discrete 'power he wields' so much as a metonymic tribute to eloquent speech.

Doubtful: fertility / life-renewal association

Jan de Vries, linking bragr's poetic sense to Old Indic bráhmaṇ- and to Bragi's marriage to Iðunn (goddess of rejuvenating apples), proposed that Bragi may have deeper roots as a deity of renewed life and fertility, comparable to figures titled 'lord'/'prince' such as Freyr, where hero-cult, fertility-cult and life-creating speech converge.

Limit: Heinz Klingenberg calls these conclusions speculative; the connection to Iðunn may simply encode the complementarity between Bragi's imperishable word and Iðunn's rejuvenation, without any ancient fertility cult. This power should be treated as a modern hypothesis, not attested myth.

Domains — Bragi's domain is poetry, eloquence, command of language, wisdom, and the social arts of praise and welcome. He is closely tied to Valhalla/Odin's hall, where he receives slain kings. He is named son of Odin in the Prose Edda. He is paired with Iðunn, whose apples of rejuvenation sit in Asgard; together they represent the imperishable word (Bragi) and imperishable life (Iðunn). Accounts I
Bragi at Ægir's feast — the Lokasenna flytingLokasenna (Poetic Edda), Codex Regius

c. 1000 CE (poem); recorded c. 1270

At a feast hosted by the sea-giant/god Ægir, Loki twice gains entry after invoking an old oath of blood-brotherhood with Odin and is served ale by Víðarr. Before drinking, he toasts the gods but pointedly excludes Bragi. Bragi, seated with his wife Iðunn, first offers Loki a horse, a sword and a ring to buy peace, warning him not to repay the Æsir with hatred. Loki responds by calling Bragi the 'wariest of war and shyest of shooting' — a cowardly bench-ornament. Bragi retorts that were he outside the hall he would hold Loki's head in his hand; Loki taunts that brave people act, they do not threaten from their seats. Iðunn intervenes, begging Bragi to respect kin-ties and not blame Loki. This single scene is Bragi's most developed narrative appearance and the source of the enduring image of a peaceable, eloquent but unwarlike god. Bragi is humiliated rhetorically by Loki but holds to the code of the hall; Iðunn is forced to restrain him. The exchange establishes Bragi's defining tension: verbal mastery without martial force.

The Mead of Poetry — Bragi as master-narratorSkáldskaparmál, Prose Edda (Bragaræður, 'Bragi's Discourses')

c. 1225

The Skáldskaparmál is framed as a dialogue in which the sea-giant Ægir, visiting Asgard, sits beside Bragi and is told the origin of skaldic poetry. Bragi recounts how, at the end of the Æsir-Vanir War, the two factions sealed peace by spitting into a vat; from that saliva was born the wisest of men, Kvasir. The dwarves Fjalarr and Galarr murdered Kvasir, mixed his blood with honey in the vessels Boðn, Són and Óðrerir, and thereby brewed the mead of poetry — anyone who drinks it becomes a skald or a scholar. After further dwarfish murders (of the giant Gillingr and his wife) and the ransom offer to Suttungr, the mead was hidden in Hnitbjörg, guarded by Suttungr's daughter Gunnlöð. Odin, in disguise as Bölverkr, won and stole it, drinking all three vats in three sips after spending three nights with Gunnlöð, then flying off as an eagle and disgorging the mead into Asgard. This myth is the charter of skaldic art, and Bragi — the god of poetry — is the one who retells it. It is the single most important primary text for understanding his domain. Accounts II
The abduction of Iðunn (as told by Bragi)Skáldskaparmál, Prose Edda; also Haustlöng

c. 1225 (Prose Edda); Haustlöng c. 900-960

In the course of the Ægir-Bragi dialogue, Bragi recounts how the giant Þjazi, in eagle form, forced Loki to lure Iðunn — keeper of the apples that keep the gods young — out of Asgard. Iðunn is carried off to Þrymheimr; the gods begin to grow old and grey. Loki, threatened with death and torture, borrows Freyja's falcon-cloak, transforms Iðunn into a nut and flies back to Asgard, with Þjazi pursuing as an eagle. The Æsir light a fire that sends Þjazi crashing to his death through the flames. Bragi, telling this story, is the loving husband narrating the perilous abduction of his own wife — reinforcing his link to Iðunn and to the preservation of divine vitality. Confirms Bragi's marriage to Iðunn and frames her as essential to the gods' immortality. The myth is echoed in the skaldic poem Haustlöng, which lacks Bragi as narrator.

Greeting the fallen kings in ValhallaEiríksmál (c. 954) and Hákonarmál (c. 961), skaldic encomia

mid-10th century

In Eiríksmál, Odin hears the approach of the slain king Eiríkr Bloodaxe and his retinue and orders the heroes Sigmundr and Sinfjötli to rise and greet him; Bragi then speaks, asking how Odin can be sure the newcomer is Eiríkr and why such a king was allowed to fall. Odin replies that it is Eiríkr and that his arrival is certain. In Hákonarmál, Odin likewise tells Hermóðr and Bragi to go and meet the dead Hákon the Good. In both, Bragi functions as the eloquent guardian-greeter of Valhalla's threshold — a role consistent with the remembered great skald welcoming the kings he once praised in life. Provides the clearest early evidence of Bragi as a Valhalla figure of welcome, and the foundation of the deified-skald interpretation.
