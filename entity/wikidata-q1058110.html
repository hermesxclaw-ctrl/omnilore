Who It Is

In the shadowy recesses of the Roman pantheon, where the thunder of Jupiter and the fury of Mars dominate the celestial stage, there lurks a god of contradiction: Vejovis, the youthful deity of healing and destruction, whose very name whispers of anti-Jove and whose sanctuary on the Capitoline Hill was a place of refuge for the desperate and the dying. His worship, steeped in Etruscan mystery, offers a glimpse into a Rome that feared and honored the forces that lay beyond the familiar Olympian order.

Vejovis is associated with healing and protection, but also with destruction and the underworld. He is a god of contradictions, perhaps representing the dual nature of divine power. His worship included the sacrifice of a goat, an unusual victim, suggesting a non-standard ritual.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Vēiovis is typically interpreted as 'anti-Jove' or 'not-Jove', from the Latin 've-' (privative prefix) and 'Iovis' (Jove). This suggests a deity opposite to or distinct from Jupiter. Some scholars propose a connection to the Etruscan god Vetis or Veivis, indicating Etruscan origins.

Pronunciation — VAY-oh-vis (Classical Latin: /ˈweː.i̯o.wis/)

Aliases — Veiovis, Vēiovis, Vēdiovis, Vēive, Vēdius, Vetis

Ownership — Roman, with Etruscan origins

Form & Signs

Appearance — Vejovis is typically depicted as a youthful god, beardless, with a head of hair. He is often shown holding arrows or a bundle of arrows, sometimes accompanied by a goat. A statue found in his temple portrays him as a young man with a goat beside him, possibly indicating a connection to fertility or vitality.

Symbols — Arrows, goats, and possibly a thunderbolt (as an anti-Jupiter). His temple on the Capitoline was located between the two peaks, in a depression, which may symbolize his chthonic nature.

Domains — Healing, destruction, underworld, youth, possibly springtime. His temple on the Capitoline Hill and another on the Tiber Island (later associated with Aesculapius) indicate his role in health and medicine.

Accounts I
The Founding of the Temple on the CapitolineOvid, Fasti 3.429-448; Livy 35.41.8

192 BC

The temple of Vejovis on the Capitoline was vowed by the praetor Lucius Furius Purpurio in 200 BC during the Battle of Cremona against the Gauls, and dedicated in 192 BC. The temple was located in the depression between the two peaks of the Capitoline, known as the Asylum. The temple became a center for the worship of Vejovis, and his statue was placed inside.

Accounts II
Vejovis and the Tiber IslandVarious

3rd century BC onwards

Vejovis had a temple on the Tiber Island, which was later dedicated to Aesculapius. This connection suggests a healing function for Vejovis, as the island became a medical center. The association with Aesculapius may have led to Vejovis being identified with the Greek god of healing.
