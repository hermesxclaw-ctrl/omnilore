Who It Is

In the Guarani cosmos, before the world was world, there was Tupã — not a distant watchmaker, but a thunder-voiced creator whose breath shaped mountains and whose lightning seeded the first human clay. He is the sound of the storm and the source of life, a deity who speaks in rain and fire.

Tupã is a creator and sustainer, but also a force of nature that can be destructive. He is generally benevolent, providing rain for crops, but his storms can also bring devastation. He is not a trickster or a moral judge; rather, he is a powerful being who maintains cosmic order. In some myths, he is remote and impersonal; in others, he interacts with humans, sometimes punishing those who break taboos.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Tupã is believed to derive from the Tupi-Guarani word 'tupã' meaning 'thunder' or 'lightning', connecting the deity directly with the storm. Some scholars suggest a possible link to the Guarani word 'tup' meaning 'to be' or 'to exist', but this is speculative.

Pronunciation — too-PAHN (approximate; nasal final vowel)

Aliases — Tupa, Tupá, Tupã, Tupan, Tumpã

Ownership — Indigenous Guarani peoples of Paraguay, Brazil, Argentina, and Bolivia; also present in Tupi and related traditions.

Names are cult layers, not one fixed label.

Powers
Creation

Tupã created the earth, the sky, the plants, the animals, and the first humans. In some myths, he shaped the first humans from clay and breathed life into them.

Limit: He is not typically credited with creating the entire universe ex nihilo; some elements may pre-exist or be created by other beings.

Weather Control

Tupã controls thunder, lightning, and rain. He sends storms to water the earth or to punish those who have offended him.

Limit: He does not control all weather; other spirits may be responsible for wind, drought, or other phenomena.

Voice of Thunder

The sound of thunder is said to be Tupã's voice. He may communicate with humans through storms, conveying warnings or approval.

Limit: This is more of a symbolic attribute than an active power; Tupã does not typically speak directly to individuals.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Tupã is not typically described in anthropomorphic terms in traditional accounts. He is often associated with thunder, lightning, and storms, and may be perceived as a powerful natural force rather than a being with a fixed form. In some artistic representations, he is depicted as a man with a beard, holding a club or lightning bolt, but these are modern interpretations influenced by European art.

Symbols — Thunder, lightning, rain, storms; the sound of thunder is considered his voice. Some sources associate him with the celestial realm, and he may be connected to the Milky Way or the constellation of the Southern Cross. In Guarani art, he is sometimes symbolized by a stylized lightning bolt or a thunderbird.

Domains — Tupã resides in the celestial realm, often associated with the sky and the heavens. He controls weather phenomena, particularly thunder and lightning. He is also a creator deity, responsible for the earth, plants, animals, and humans.

Accounts I
The Creation of the WorldGuarani oral tradition, recorded in various ethnographic works

Pre-colonial and colonial era (oral tradition)

In the beginning, there was only darkness and water. Tupã, with the help of other beings, created the earth from the mud at the bottom of the waters. He then created the plants, the animals, and finally the first humans, whom he shaped from clay and breathed life into. He gave them fire and taught them how to live. The world was established, and humans were placed on earth to live and multiply. Tupã remained in the sky, watching over creation.

Accounts II
Tupã and the FloodGuarani oral tradition, recorded by Nimuendajú

Oral tradition

After humans became wicked, Tupã sent a great flood to destroy them. Some people were saved by climbing to the tops of mountains or by taking refuge in trees. This flood myth shares similarities with other South American flood narratives. The world was cleansed, and a new generation of humans repopulated the earth.

The Origin of FireGuarani oral tradition

Oral tradition

Tupã gave fire to humans, either by teaching them how to make it or by sending a lightning bolt to ignite a tree. This fire was essential for cooking, warmth, and protection. Humans gained an essential tool for survival and civilization.

Tupã is the same as the Christian God. — While missionaries used the name Tupã for the Christian God, the original Guarani deity was not omnipotent or omnipresent and had a different mythological role.

Tupã is a single, anthropomorphic god. — Traditional depictions are often abstract, associated with natural forces, and not consistently anthropomorphic.

Tupã is the creator of the universe. — In many Guarani myths, the universe or primordial elements pre-exist, and Tupã shapes the world rather than creating everything from nothing.
