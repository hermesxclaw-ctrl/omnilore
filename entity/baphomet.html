Who It Is

You're standing in a library that shouldn't exist, deep beneath a city you thought you knew. In the center of the room is a statue: a winged, goat-headed figure, one arm raised to the sky, the other pointed to the earth. It is both male and female, both human and animal, both light and shadow. The inscription beneath it reads: 'Solve et Coagula' — dissolve and coagulate. You realize, with a vertigo that feels like falling upward, that this thing is not evil. It is the most honest depiction of the universe you have ever seen. And you are not sure what to do with that.

Baphomet is one of the most contested symbols in Western esotericism — a being who has been called a demon, a god, a principle, and a hoax. The name first appears in 1098 in a letter by a Crusader, describing a supposed Muslim idol. In 1307, the Knights Templar were accused by the French king and the Inquisition of worshipping 'Baphomet,' and under torture, some knights confessed to venerating a bearded head or a cat-like idol. The charges were almost certainly fabricated to justify seizing Templar wealth, but the name stuck. For centuries, 'Baphomet' was an obscure historical footnote.

It was Éliphas Lévi, a 19th-century French occultist, who gave Baphomet its definitive form and meaning. In his 1856 book 'Dogme et Rituel de la Haute Magie,' Lévi published the famous illustration: a winged, androgynous figure with a goat's head, a pentagram on its forehead, one arm pointing up (toward white magic and the divine), one pointing down (toward black magic and the material), with the Latin words 'Solve' (dissolve) and 'Coagula' (coagulate) on its arms. This was not a depiction of Satan. It was a symbol of the reconciliation of opposites — light and dark, male and female, spirit and matter, creation and destruction. Baphomet represented the alchemical process of spiritual transformation: break yourself down, then rebuild yourself in a higher form. The Church of Satan later adopted a version of Baphomet (the Sigil of Baphomet) as its official symbol, but the original Lévi Baphomet is far more complex than a simple devil-figure.

Origins & Fall

The name 'Baphomet' first appears in a letter from Anselm of Ribemont in 1098. It reappears during the Templar trials (1307-1314), where tortured knights gave wildly inconsistent confessions about worshipping an idol called Baphomet — some described it as a bearded head, some as a cat, some as a three-faced statue. The charges were politically motivated, and no physical idol was ever produced. The name was largely forgotten until the 19th century, when Joseph von Hammer-Purgstall proposed (incorrectly) that the Templars were secret Gnostics and that 'Baphomet' derived from the Greek words 'baphe' (baptism) and 'metis' (wisdom). Éliphas Lévi synthesized these threads into his 1856 illustration, creating the goat-headed androgyne that defines all modern images of Baphomet. Aleister Crowley later adopted Baphomet as a symbol of the magical will and incorporated the figure into Thelema.

Form

Lévi's Baphomet is a seated, winged figure with a goat's head, a pentagram on its forehead, a torch between its horns (representing universal intelligence), and human breasts and torso. One arm is raised toward a white crescent moon (Chesed, mercy), and one points down toward a black crescent moon (Geburah, severity). The arms bear the words 'SOLVE' and 'COAGULA.' Its lower body is covered in scales or a cloth, and it has cloven hooves or human feet (varying by depiction). It has an erect caduceus (the Hermetic staff of balance) in its lap. It is androgynous: the breasts are female, the phallic caduceus is male, the face is animal. The whole figure represents the union of all opposites — not a monster but a map of the universe. Later Satanist versions (adopted by Anton LaVey's Church of Satan) add a goat head within an inverted pentagram, Hebrew letters spelling 'Leviathan,' and a more explicitly demonic aesthetic.

Powers

Synthesis of opposites / perfect equilibrium Lévi's core claim: Baphomet is 'the absolute in symbolic form', the power to reconcile binary opposites — mercy and justice (white and black moons of Chesed/Geburah), male and female (the two hermaphrodite arms), spirit and matter (the torch-fillet 'flame of intelligence' above the animal head), good and evil. It functions as an emblem of the alchemical Summa and of occult totality rather than an operational supernatural power. Éliphas Lévi, Dogme et Rituel de la Haute Magie (1856) Symbolic, pedagogical; it has no independent volition in Lévi's system. Later theistic occultists and Christians treat the figure as an autonomous demon/idol with real power; LaVeyan Satanists insist it is a metaphor with no supernatural efficacy.

Light and illumination (the 'flame of intelligence') The torch between the horns symbolizes enlightenment rising above brute matter—the soul 'elevated above matter' while still attached to it. In the occult-natural scheme it is linked to Lévi's 'Astral Light', a Mesmer-derived magnetic/spiritual fluid that skilled magicians could manipulate. The figure thereby represents access to hidden knowledge and magic. Éliphas Lévi; 18th/19th-c. mesmeric and 'spiritualist magnetism' theory (Baron du Potet, Henri Delaage) For Lévi, the Astral Light was a natural force of the magus's imagination and will, not the devil's gift. Catholic anti-magnetist polemicists (de Mirville, Gougenot des Mousseaux) read the same force as demonic possession.

Union of spirit and matter; alchemical transmutation The image's layered anatomy (scales=water, semicircle=atmosphere, feathers=volatile, phallic caduceus=eternal life/generation) encodes an alchemical chain of elements. Baphomet thus represents the philosopher's-work of transformation and the generative/magical 'child' — for Crowley the 'hieroglyph of arcane perfection' symbolizing the spiritual nature of the spermatozoon and the union of Chaos and Babalon in sex-magic. Éliphas Lévi; Aleister Crowley, Magick (Book 4) Ritual/initiatory symbolism; the O.T.O. secret instruction is reserved to higher degrees. Crowley's sexual-occult reading conflicts with the purely moral-philosophical reading of earlier occultists and with the (unfounded) 'Goat of Mendes' sexual-fertility claim.

The pentagram/magical signature (Sigil of Baphomet) The inverted pentagram with a goat's head and Hebrew letters (Leviathan, plus originally Samael and Lilith) is the modern operational sigil of materiality and of the Church of Satan. The inversion encodes Lévi's invented doctrine that one-point-down = evil/materiality, one-point-up = good/spirit-ruling-matter. Stanislas de Guaita, La Clef de la Magie Noire (1897); adopted by Anton LaVey's Church of Satan (1968-1972), drawn by LaVey under the pseudonym 'Hugo Zorilla' Iconographic and organizational, not a functional magical power. Lévi himself contrast the inverted pentagram with the upright 'microcosmic man'; de Guaita's 1897 image, not Lévi's, is the actual root of the Sigil.

Fear and intimidation (the 'phantom of all terrors' register) In Western demonology, folk-horror, and modern pop-culture fiction, Baphomet is frequently vested with the classic powers of a horned demon-lord: sovereignty over a cult or underworld hierarchy, the ability to receive worship, inspiring terror, and (in grimoire-derived media) command over legions. This is an ascribed, narrative power-set with no primary historical attestation as actual cult. James Blish's The Day After Judgment; Clive Barker's Cabal/Nightbreed; the 'unholy trinity' in Jason Dark's Geisterjäger John Sinclair Confined to fiction and folklore; demonological registers are inconsistent (Baphomet is variously a name for the Devil, an independent demon, or a symbolic glyph). The Church of Satan explicitly denies Baphomet's existence as a being; Catholic-accusation and grimoire horror give it real diabolic force.

Behavior & Habitat

Baphomet does not 'behave' — it is a symbol. But as a mythological entity, Baphomet has been assigned various behaviors by different traditions. In Christian demonology, it was a demon of heresy and corruption. In modern Satanism, it represents the carnal, material self — the animal nature that Christianity represses. In esoteric traditions, it is a teacher, a revealer of hidden truths, a guide through the alchemical process of self-transformation. It does not attack, deceive, or tempt; it reveals. The horror of Baphomet is not in what it does but in what it represents: the collapse of comfortable dualities, the revelation that good and evil, male and female, divine and bestial are not opposites but poles of a single spectrum.

Weaknesses & Wards

As a symbol, Baphomet's only weakness is misinterpretation. It can be reduced to a simple devil-figure, stripped of its esoteric complexity. In traditions that treat it as a demonic entity, it is vulnerable to the usual methods: exorcism, holy water, sacred symbols, the authority of divine figures. The Church of Satan's Sigil of Baphomet was designed to provoke — the 'weakness' is that this provocative function overrides the deeper symbolic meaning for most viewers. Baphomet cannot be 'killed' because it never lived — it is an idea, and ideas are bulletproof.

Accounts

King Philip IV of France, deeply in debt to the Templars and locked in a struggle with the papacy, ordered the simultaneous arrest of all Templars in France at dawn on Friday 13 October 1307. Under torture, dozens of knights confessed to renouncing Christ, spitting and urinating on the cross, exchanging 'obscene kisses' (on the spine, navel, and mouth), and worshipping an idol variously described as a cat, a long-bearded head, or a three-faced head — the 'Baphomet', which one Florentine Templar reportedly told initiates to adore as 'your god and your Mahomet'. No such idol was ever produced. Pope Clement V, pressured by Philip, dissolved the order in 1312 (Vox in excelso), and on 18 March 1314 Grand Master Jacques de Molay and Geoffroi de Charney, recanting their coerced confessions, were burned as relapsed heretics in Paris. Historians (Malcolm Barber, Peter Partner, Helen Nicholson, Alain Demurger) treat the Baphomet charge as a legal fiction assembled from tropes then leveled at Cathars, Jews, and Philip's political enemies — though the Chinon Parchment (2001) and readings by Sean Martin and Michael Haag keep open the question of whether the Templars staged a ritual denial-of-Christ as an initiation simulation.

The Viennese Orientalist von Hammer-Purgstall argued that the Templars were secret Ophite/Gnostic serpent-worshippers and that Baphomet signified the 'Baptism of Metis' or Sophia Achamoth, a hermaphrodite wisdom-figure. He relied on 'Baphomet' statuettes (some clearly faked or pseudo-Egyptian curiosities) then held in Weimar, Vienna, and cabinets of antiquities. Contemporaries (F. J. M. Raynouard immediately rebutted it in the Journal des savants; Charles William King; later Peter Partner) demonstrated the objects were unfounded forgeries from 'Rosicrucian or alchemical quacks' and never tied to the Templars. Yet the essay gave lasting scholarly patronage to the idea of an esoteric Templar Baphomet.

Lévi, a former Saint-Sulpice seminarist turned ceremonial magician and (per modern historian Julian Strube) lifelong romantic-socialist, published his own drawing of a winged androgynous goat-headed god called 'Baphomet'/'The Sabbatic Goat'. His caption decoded every element — the point-up pentagram (light), the two hands making the sign of occultism above and below invoking mercy and justice, the male and female arms of Khunrath's androgyne, the torch of intelligence between the horns, scales/water, semicircle/atmosphere, feathers/volatility, the rod of eternal life in place of genitals, and humanity's two breasts. Lévi insisted the Templar masters worshipped this figure not as the devil but as the god Pan of the Alexandrian and Gnostic schools; the image, he claimed, was the frontispiece of the 'ritual' reproducing 'the exact figure of the terrible emperor of night'. This single engraving created Baphomet as the world knows it.

De Guaita published the first goat's head set inside a downward-pointing pentagram, with five Hebrew letters at the points spelling Leviathan, and the names Samael and Lilith. Building on Lévi's doctrine of inversion (matter ruling over spirit = 'evil'; spirit over matter = 'good'), this became the formal sigil later adopted by Anton LaVey, who copied it from Maurice Bessy's A Pictorial History of Magic and the Supernatural after removing the Samael/Lilith text, renaming it the 'Sigil of Baphomet' (named in The Satanic Rituals, 1972).

The excommunicated free-thinking hoaxer Léo Taxil pretended to convert to Catholicism and published lurid 'exposés' claiming Freemasons were Satanic Palladists under demons' orders, inventing the high-priestess 'Diana Vaughan'. In 1897 he called a press conference and confessed the whole affair was a joke meant to mock both Freemasonry and the Catholic Church's gullibility. The famous 'Luciferian doctrine' quote — 'Lucifer is God... Adonay is also god' — is not in Taxil's own writings but was published by Abel Clarin de la Rive, sourced to Taxil's fictional creation Vaughan. It nevertheless circulated for decades as a genuine Freemasonic confession, and Christian evangelist Jack T. Chick recycled the Taxil material claiming Freemasons worship Baphomet.

In 2012 Oklahoma Rep. Mike Ritze privately placed a Ten Commandments monument at the state capitol. The Satanic Temple (which does not believe in a literal Satan and casts Baphomet as 'a literary figure, not a deity — he stands for rationality, skepticism', per Time) crowdfunded an 8.5 ft, 3,000-lb bronze Baphomet statue by Mark Porter, unveiling it in Detroit on 25 July 2015. The Oklahoma Supreme Court ordered the Ten Commandments monument removed as an unconstitutional religious display, so the TST statue never stood there; it was later displayed in Arkansas (2018) and Iowa, where Iowa the display was destroyed by a Christian candidate, Michael Cassidy, in December 2023 — triggering a hate-crime charge and a $75,000+ legal-defense crowdfund. A likeness of the statue also appears in Netflix's Chilling Adventures of Sabrina, which the Satanic Temple sued over (settled 2018).

Cultural Significance

Baphomet is the central symbol of modern Satanism and a touchstone of Western occultism. It represents the rebellion against religious authority that defines modern secular culture. The Satanic Temple, a non-theistic religious organization, has adopted Baphomet as its primary icon — a seven-foot bronze Baphomet statue was proposed for display alongside the Ten Commandments on Oklahoma state capitol grounds as a statement about religious pluralism (and as satire). Baphomet has been used in moral panics about Satanism (the 1980s 'Satanic Panic'), heavy metal album covers (Venom, Slayer, countless others), and countercultural iconography. It is simultaneously one of the most recognized and most misunderstood symbols in the world.

Modern Interpretations

Baphomet is ubiquitous in counterculture. The Sigil of Baphomet is the official logo of the Church of Satan and appears on merchandise, album covers, and tattoos worldwide. The Satanic Temple's Baphomet monument has traveled across America as a political statement. In film, Baphomet appears in horror movies (often conflated with Satan) and occasionally in more nuanced roles ('As Above, So Below' references the solve et coagula concept). The Netflix series 'Sabrina' features Baphomet as a dark deity. Modern esoteric practitioners continue to work with the Lévi image as a meditative tool. Baphomet's enduring power lies in its ambiguity — a Rorschach test onto which viewers project their fears and hopes about religion, rebellion, and the nature of good and evil.

Content Notes

The Baphomet idol accusation was largely manufactured and extracted under serious torture. Of the 138 prisoners at the first Paris trial (19 October-24 November 1307), nearly all admitted some guilt; the reliability of these confessions is an open historical question. De Molay and Geoffroi de Charney recanted on 18 March 1314 and were burned alive as relapsed heretics; dozens more Templars were burned in 1310 after Philip's court blocked their (uncoerced) defense. Philip seized the order's assets, allegedly as 'compensation' for the trial costs.

Barber: historians find it hard to accept the entire affair rests on total fabrication; the Chinon Parchment (2001) shows the Church absolved 72 Templars in 1308, and later papal commissions in England/Cyprus acquitted nearly everyone. The charges rip off tropes applied to Cathars, Jews, and accused witches.

The very name 'Baphomet' likely descends from a Christian libel that Muslims worshipped Muhammad as an idol ('mammet'). Crusade-era Provençal poems, chansons de geste, and the Occitan De Bafomet use Bafomet/Bafumetz for Muhammad. This is an 'Orientalizing' anti-Muslim trope repurposed as an anti-heretical charge against the Templars.

Helen Nicholson: the Templars were accused of becoming 'fairy-tale Muslims'. Alain Demurger rejects Templar adoption of Islamic doctrine; the accusation itself is the artifact.

A way to hold it in your mind

No ancient temple ever raised an altar to it, no priest ever censed an image of it in worship, and no archaeological dig has ever turned up an idol that answers to its name — yet Baphomet is arguably the most famous 'god' nobody actually worshipped. It begins not as a deity but as a word: in July 1098 a Crusader wrote home to translate the enemy's prophet as 'Bafomet', and centuries later, in 1307, that same name was dragged into a torture chamber and made the pretext for the destruction of the Knights Templar. It was only in 1856, in a Paris engraving drawn by an ex-seminarist haunted by his own failed priesthood, that Baphomet finally got a body — a winged, androgynous, goat-headed figure of impossible balance, half male and half female, one hand raised to mercy and the other to justice, a flame of intelligence burning between its horns. From that single line drawing poured the Tarot's Devil, the Church of Satan's Sigil, a 3,000-pound bronze statue in the middle of an American First Amendment war, and a hoax that convinced the Vatican Freemasonry had been worshipping it for years. Baphomet is the rare myth that we can date to the decade of its invention — a phantom assembled from accusation, forgery, artistry, and outrage, wearing the face of an ancient mystery it never actually had.
