Who It Is

The 2nd spirit in Massenet's Cendrillon is perhaps the ultimate example of a character who does everything and is named by nobody: as one of La Fée's anonymous numbered attendants, this soprano voice participates in the opera's most magical moments—the midnight transformation, the enchanted forest reunion, the lovers' slumber beneath the fairies' oak—yet has never been the subject of a programme note, a scholarly article, or a Wikipedia entry. What does it mean to be a named character in a 125-year performance tradition without ever acquiring a name?

As a minor comprimario role, the 2nd spirit is not individuated psychologically in the libretto; its personality is subsumed into the collective character of the fairy attendants, who are benevolent supernatural agents of transformation and love. Their collective dramatic function is to mediate between the mortal protagonists (Cendrillon and Prince Charming) and the magical will of the Fairy Godmother, La Fée. Powers
Magical Transformation

As part of the fairy attendant ensemble serving La Fée (the Fairy Godmother), the 2nd spirit participates in the central transformation of Act 1, in which Cendrillon is clothed in magnificent garments appropriate for the royal ball. This transformation is the primary magical act of the opera and is collectively performed by La Fée and her assembled spirits; the 2nd spirit contributes to the ritual that changes Lucette/Cendrillon from a household drudge into a radiant beauty unrecognizable even to her own stepfamily.

Limit:

Enchantment and Slumber Induction

In the Act 3 enchanted-forest tableau, the spirits collectively induce an enchanted sleep upon Cendrillon and Prince Charming after they have recognized each other's voices and declared their love through the barrier of a magical arbour of flowers. The lovers fall into a slumber in each other's arms, surrounded by the spirits—a protective enchantment that suspends time and shields the couple until the opera's resolution in Act 4.

Limit:

Spatial and Perceptual Manipulation

The spirits, presiding over the enchanted forest, are implicitly responsible for the magical arbour of flowers that prevents the lovers from seeing each other even as they sing together. This spatial manipulation—creating an auditory channel while blocking visual recognition—is a subtle power of perceptual division that the spirits maintain and, at the Fairy Godmother's direction, ultimately dissolve to allow the lovers to see each other.

Limit:

Guidance and Summoning

The spirits draw Cendrillon and the Prince together in the enchanted forest after the midnight flight; the Wikipedia synopsis notes that 'the prince and Cendrillon are drawn together by the fairies' beneath the magic oak. This summoning or magnetic guidance function implies that the spirits can direct the movements of mortals through invisible supernatural attraction, channeling both protagonists to the same liminal space from different points of departure.

Limit:

Protective Warding

In the Act 1 transformation scene, La Fée specifies that Cendrillon's glass slippers will protect her from being recognized by her family at the ball—a protective enchantment that the spirit ensemble collectively underwrites. While the libretto assigns the spoken articulation of this ward to La Fée, the spirits as attendants are co-agents of the magical field sustaining the disguise throughout Act 2.

Form & Signs

Appearance — As a purely theatrical construct, the 2nd spirit has no fixed visual iconography prescribed by the libretto; each production designs the spirits according to the director's and costume designer's interpretation. In the tradition of nineteenth-century French féerie opera, spirits were typically costumed in diaphanous white, silver, or pale-blue gauze garments suggestive of luminous immateriality—wings, ethereal drapery, and glittering accessories

Symbols — The 2nd spirit, as part of the fairy attendant ensemble in Cendrillon, inherits the iconographic vocabulary of the supernatural helper in French theatrical tradition: the magic wand or baguette, the luminous aura, and association with transformation and love. The enchanted oak (le chêne des fées) under which the spirits preside in Act 3 is the primary symbolic landscape, evoking the ancient European tradition of sacred trees as sites of fairy act

Domains — The 2nd spirit's operatic domain is the enchanted forest of Act 3, specifically the tableau designated 'Le Chêne des Fées' (The Fairies' Oak). This liminal space, neither court nor hearth, represents the supernatural boundary between the mundane world of the stepfamily home and the magical resolution of the love plot. Accounts I
The Transformation at the Hearth (Act 1)Henri Caïn, libretto for Cendrillon (Paris: Heugel, 1899); Jules Massenet, score

1899 (opera composition 1893–1896)

After the stepfamily departs for the royal ball, Cendrillon (Lucette) completes her chores and falls asleep by the warm hearth. La Fée and her attendant spirits—including the 2nd spirit—arrive and transform Cendrillon into a magnificent beauty. The spirits collectively clothe her in resplendent attire and enchant her glass slippers to shield her identity. La Fée warns Cendrillon that the spell lasts only until midnight. Cendrillon promises to return in time and departs for the ball. The spirits' collective magical action in this scene is the hinge on which the entire plot turns. Cendrillon attends the ball, meets Prince Charming, and the love plot is initiated. The midnight deadline creates the dramatic tension of Act 2.

The Enchanted Forest: Lovers United Under the Fairies' Oak (Act 3, Second Tableau)Henri Caïn, libretto for Cendrillon; Massenet, score, Act 3 second tableau

1899

After Cendrillon flees into the night believing herself rejected and seeking to die alone, and after the Prince similarly searches desperately for the unknown beauty, La Fée and her spirits draw both protagonists to a magic oak tree in an enchanted forest. An arbour of flowers screens the lovers from each other's sight, but not from sound; they recognize each other's voices and sing of their love. The spirits surround them. The flowers part; the lovers see each other at last. Then they fall into an enchanted slumber in each other's arms, cradled by the protective presence of the spirit ensemble. The lovers' enchanted sleep bridges the gap between Act 3 and Act 4, during which Cendrillon is discovered by her father Pandolfe by a stream, apparently unconscious for months. The reunion in Act 4 completes the love arc.

Accounts II
The Midnight Flight and Glass Slipper (Act 2 into Act 3)Caïn / Massenet, Cendrillon, Acts 2–3; Charles Perrault, Cendrillon ou la Petite Pantoufle de verre (1697)

1697 (source); 1899 (operatic version)

At the stroke of midnight, the magical protections set by the fairy spirits begin to expire. Cendrillon, terrified of discovery, flees the royal ball; in her flight she loses one of her glass slippers—the enchanted footwear that was part of the spirits' transformative work in Act 1. The glass slipper becomes the plot mechanism for recognition and reunion in Act 4, when it fits Cendrillon's foot and the Prince identifies his beloved. The temporary nature of fairy magic—a clock-bound enchantment—is foregrounded. The slipper's survival allows the opera's final recognition scene.

The Reunion and Acclamation (Act 4, Second Tableau)Caïn / Massenet, Cendrillon, Act 4

1899

At the palace assembly of eligible princesses, Cendrillon—wearing the matching glass slipper—is recognized by the Prince among all the gathered women. The lovers are reunited and acclaimed by all present, including the formerly hostile stepmother Madame de la Haltière. The fairy spirits' earlier enchantments, including the glass slipper's protective and identifying functions, are fully vindicated. The opera ends with the cast breaking the fourth wall to address the audience, declaring that they have tried to convey them through 'les beaux pays bleus' (the beautiful blue countries)—the realm of fairy. Full romantic resolution; the stepfamily's opposition is dissolved; the lovers are united; the opera's meta-theatrical framing device closes the narrative circle.

Number and individuation of spirit roles in the scoreThe libretto and Wikidata entries treat the numbered spirits (including the 2nd spirit) as distinct operatic characters with individual vocal assignments, each separately catalogued as a 'fictional character' (Q30061299) appearing in Cendrillon (Q1371386).In practice, many productions and critical discussions treat the spirit ensemble as an undifferentiated choral mass designated collectively 'les esprits,' with no meaningful distinction between the 1st, 2nd, 3rd, or further spirits as individuals. The New Grove Dictionary article on Cendrillon does not discuss the numbered spirits as separate dramatic entities.
Voice type of the 2nd spiritWikidata claim P412 (voice type) for Q63486068 is set to Q30903 (soprano), indicating the 2nd spirit is a soprano role.Operatic practice in ensemble fairy scenes often distributes spirit lines across multiple soprano sub-types (lyric soprano, soubrette, coloratura) and sometimes extends to high mezzo-soprano. Some productions cast lighter mezzo-sopranos in spirit parts without scholarly objection, suggesting the soprano designation is an approximation rather than a strict casting requirement in modern performance.
Relationship between the operatic spirits and Perrault's fairy-tale sourceThe spirit ensemble is a direct operatic elaboration of the magical helper figure in Perrault's Cendrillon, and Caïn merely expanded the single fairy godmother into a chorus of spirits in keeping with French operatic convention—the spirits are thus culturally continuous with Perrault's narrative.The spirit ensemble has no textual basis in Perrault's prose; Perrault's godmother operates alone and transforms Cinderella through individual magical acts with a wand. The operatic spirits are a wholesale theatrical invention by Caïn, drawing from the féerie stage and ballet divertissement traditions that are independent of Perrault, and should be understood as belonging to a distinct theatrical genealogy.
Dramatic function: active agent vs. atmospheric decorationThe numbered spirits, including the 2nd spirit, are active dramatic agents: they perform the transformation in Act 1, guide the lovers in Act 3, and induce the protective enchanted sleep—each action has plot consequences without which the opera's resolution would fail.The spirit roles are essentially atmospheric decorative elements—part of the opera's spectacular staging apparatus—and their 'actions' are indistinguishable from the larger enchantment credited to La Fée. No spirit sings an independent aria or makes a solo decision; they are functionally equivalent to a stage effect managed by an ensemble, and their individuation as characters is a bureaucratic artefact of opera-house role assignment rather than a dramatic reality.
