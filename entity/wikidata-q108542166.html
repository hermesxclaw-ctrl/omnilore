Who It Is

Homer gave it a single line in the Catalogue of Ships — Σκάρφη — a Locrian town tucked between sea and mountain on the narrow road to Thermopylae. Two and a half millennia later, an earthquake lifted the sea and erased it, yet Scarphe refused to stay drowned. Romans marched through it, Achaeans died outside it, geographers kept mapping it long after its harbor had silted away, and today its stones still hide, tentatively, beneath the fields near Molos.

As a polis, Scarpheia's character is that of a second-tier Locrian town: autonomous enough to mint bronze coinage (a privilege shared in Epicnemidian Locris only with Thronium), but never a hegemonic power. Its behavior in history is passive in surviving sources — occupied, marched through, inundated, fought beside — rather than initiating policy. Locrian identity: Opuntian and Epicnemidian Locrians were regarded by other Greeks as semi-peripheral, noted for close kinship ties and for providing Locrian contingents in larger alliances (e.g., Persian Wars, Lamian War). Powers
Strategic road control

Position on the sole practicable overland route from central Greece (Elateia/Phocis) to Thermopylae and the Malian Gulf. Control or passage through Scarpheia meant control of north-south movement between Boeotia/Phocis and Thessaly. Livy's account of Flamininus using the Thronium-Scarpheia-Heraclea corridor in 191 BCE demonstrates its continued logistical importance during the Roman-Seleucid War.

Limit: No evidence of fortified pass or toll station; secondary to Thermopylae itself and to larger neighbors Thronium and Nicaea. Town never held independent military power to close the road against major armies.

Coinage autonomy

One of only two Epicnemidian Locrian cities to strike its own bronze coinage (with Thronium) in the Classical-Hellenistic period, indicating at least limited fiscal and political autonomy within the Locrian koinon or under Boeotian/Macedonian hegemony. Types: Apollo/Athena obverse, grapes/barley/shield reverse, legend ΣΚΑΡ(ΦΕΩΝ).

Limit: Only bronze, small denominations for local circulation; no silver or gold, indicating modest economy. Attribution of some ΣΚΑΡ coins disputed between Scarpheia and other mints.

Territorial absorption of Augeiae

According to Strabo, Scarpheia expanded to occupy the chora of Augeiae, a Homeric neighbor that had vanished by the Augustan period. This suggests ability to absorb abandoned land and increase agricultural base.

Limit: Mechanism unknown — synoecism, abandonment due to inundation, or gradual desertion. No epigraphic land-registry confirms.

Resilience/rebuilding after catastrophe

Despite reported total destruction by tsunami in 426 BCE, town was rebuilt and persisted for at least 700+ years thereafter, appearing in 2nd c. BCE battle, 1st c. BCE-2nd c. CE geographers, and 6th c. CE administrative lists.

Limit: No archaeological stratigraphy published to confirm destruction layer or rebuilding phase; continuity may be toponymic rather than urban.

Accounts I
Homeric Catalogue of Ships — Scarphe among the LocriansHomer, Iliad 2.527-535

Late Bronze Age traditions codified ca. 750-700 BCE; earliest papyri 3rd c. BCE

In the Catalogue, Ajax son of Oileus leads the Locrians: 'they that dwelt in Cynos and Opus and Calliarus and Bessa and Scarphe and Augeiae and Tarphe and Thronium round about Boagrius River.' Scarphe appears as one of eight Locrian settlements contributing forty ships. No narrative action; its function is geographic completeness, establishing Locrian presence in the Achaean coalition. The pairing with Augeiae is significant for later debate about territorial succession. Fixed Scarphe in the canonical Greek geographic imagination; all later geographers (Strabo, Stephanus) cite Homer as proof of antiquity and Locrian identity. Created the Augeiae-Scarphe linkage that Strabo later explains as succession.

The disappearance of Augeiae and Scarphe's territorial expansionStrabo, Geographica 9.4.5 (citing earlier sources and Homer)

Augustan (Strabo writing ca. 7 BCE-23 CE, describing conditions of his time vs. Homeric past)

Strabo notes that in his day Scarpheia 'occupies the territory of Augeiae, which has disappeared.' He places Scarpheia 10 stadia from the sea, 30 from Thronium, and slightly less from Nicaea (name lost in lacuna). This implies that between Homer and Strabo, Augeiae was abandoned or absorbed and Scarpheia expanded into its chora, becoming the dominant settlement on that stretch of coast. Explains why Homer lists two towns where Strabo sees one; provides evidence for settlement shift in Epicnemidian Locris, possibly due to coastal change, synoecism, or the 426 BCE disaster.

Earthquake and tsunami of 426 BCE — destruction of ScarpheiaThucydides 3.89.1-4; Diodorus Siculus 12.59.1-2; Strabo 1.3.20 and 9.4.4 (citing Demetrius of Callatis); Demetrius of Callatis via Strabo

Summer 426 BCE (archonship of Eucleides at Athens, 5th year of Peloponnesian War)

A great earthquake struck central Greece, affecting Euboea, Boeotia, Locris and Atalante. The sea retreated and returned as a tidal wave. Thucydides describes inundation at Orobiae (Euboea), Atalante island, and Peparethus, with damage to low-lying areas. Diodorus and Strabo (following Demetrius of Callatis) explicitly include Scarpheia among the Locrian/Euboean towns destroyed or heavily damaged, with Strabo stating Scarpheia was 'destroyed by the inundation of the sea caused by the earthquake' (κατακλυσθῆναι). The event was linked by contemporaries to the Peloponnesian War and interpreted as divine portent. Temporary abandonment or severe damage; Strabo's account suggests total destruction, but later attestation proves rebuilding. The 426 BCE earthquake became a canonical example in ancient seismology (cited by Strabo, Seneca QN 6). Modern geologists correlate with Malian Gulf fault activity.

Accounts II
March of Lucius Quinctius Flamininus through Scarpheia to Heraclea (191 BCE)Livy, Ab Urbe Condita 36.19.1-3; Appian, Syriaca 17 (parallel account of Roman-Seleucid War)

191 BCE, Roman-Seleucid War, campaign against Antiochus III

During the Roman advance against Antiochus III, who held Thermopylae, the Roman commander Lucius Quinctius Flamininus (brother of Titus) marched from Elateia (recently taken) 'per Thronium et Scarpheam ad Heracleam' — by way of Thronium and Scarpheia to Heraclea Trachinia, to secure the Locrian coast and outflank the Seleucid position at Thermopylae. Pausanias (10.1.2) independently confirms this was the direct road from Elateia to Thermopylae via those towns. The passage shows Scarpheia as a functioning road station and territorial marker in the early 2nd c. BCE, fully recovered from the 426 BCE disaster. Roman control of Epicnemidian Locris; prelude to Battle of Thermopylae (191 BCE) where Romans defeated Antiochus. Demonstrates Scarpheia's continued strategic relevance.

Battle of Scarpheia (146 BCE) — Achaean WarPausanias 7.15.3-4; Cassius Dio fr. 72 (via Zonaras); Livy Periochae 52; Strabo 9.4.4 (reference); modern scholarship: Gruen, Errington

146 BCE, Achaean War (Roman intervention against Achaean League)

Near Scarpheia, Roman forces under Quintus Caecilius Metellus Macedonicus decisively defeated an Achaean League army led by Critolaus. Pausanias reports that Critolaus, after initial setbacks, retreated toward Scarpheia where his army was overtaken and routed; Critolaus himself disappeared (variously said to have drowned in marshes, poisoned himself, or fled). The defeat broke Achaean resistance in central Greece, opening the way for Lucius Mummius's sack of Corinth and the dissolution of the Achaean League. The battle is routinely called 'Battle of Scarpheia' or 'Battle of Scarphe' in modern histories, though ancient sources give limited tactical detail. Collapse of Achaean War effort in central Greece; Roman annexation of Greece as province of Achaea (or Macedonia's sphere); Scarpheia's name preserved as battle toponym in historiography.

Scarpheia in the Roman and Byzantine geographers — from Pliny to RavennaPliny NH 4.27; Ptolemy Geog. 3.14.7; Lycophron Alexandra 1146 (scholia); Appian (reference); Hierocles Synecdemus 643.9; Stephanus of Byzantium s.v. Σκάρφεια; Geographer of Ravenna Cosmographia 4.10

1st c. CE (Pliny) through 7th c. CE (Ravenna)

After the Hellenistic period, Scarpheia ceases to appear in narrative history but persists as a toponym in every major geographic list: Pliny lists 'Scarphe' among Locrian towns; Ptolemy gives coordinates for Σκάρφεια; Lycophron's obscure Alexandra mentions Scarpheia (with scholia explaining it as Locrian); Appian references it in context of Roman campaigns; Hierocles lists it among Thessalian/Locrian cities under Justinian's administration; Stephanus defines it as 'polis of Locris'; Ravenna copies it into medieval cosmography. This unbroken lexicographic survival suggests either continuous habitation as a village/kome or, more likely, fossilization of a classical toponym in administrative tradition. Preserved the name for Renaissance rediscovery; provided William Smith and later Barrington Atlas with evidence for tentative localization near Molos.

Scarphe was a mythological figure, hero, or monster. — Scarphe is not a person or creature but a polis/town of the Epicnemidian Locrians. Confusion arises from databases tagging it as 'fictional character' or 'heroic-legend' due to automated metadata. No myth, genealogy, or cult attaches to a person named Scarphe.

Scarpheia was permanently destroyed by the 426 BCE tsunami and never reinhabited. — Strabo says it was destroyed, but Livy (191 BCE), Pausanias, Pliny, Ptolemy, Hierocles and Stephanus all list it afterwards, and it gave its name to the Battle of Scarpheia in 146 BCE. It was rebuilt and persisted for centuries, though possibly on a slightly shifted site.

Scarphe and Scarpheia are two different towns. — They are the same settlement with orthographic variation: Homer uses Σκάρφη (Scarphe), later authors predominantly use Σκάρφεια (Scarpheia) — a common -eia suffix expansion. Stephanus and Strabo treat them as identical.

The site of Scarphe has been excavated and is a visible archaeological park near Molos. — No confirmed excavation or displayed remains exist. The identification near Molos is tentative, based on literary distances and road logic, marked with a question mark in Barrington Atlas and Pleiades. Surface finds have been noted but no published stratigraphic excavation or inscription confirms the identification.

Scarpheia minted silver coins and was a major economic power. — Only small bronze coins for local use are attributed to Scarpheia; no silver or gold. It was one of only two Epicnemidian Locrian mints, which is notable for a small town, but output was modest and circulation local. It was never a major economic center like Opus or Lamia.
