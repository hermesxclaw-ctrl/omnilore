She controls all sea mammals, including seals, walruses, whales, and fish. She is also associated with the underworld (Adlivun) and the souls of the dead, who may reside near her dwelling. Form

Arnakuagsak is not described in physical, anthropomorphic detail in most sources. She is primarily an unseen, omnipotent force residing at the bottom of the sea. When she is described, it is often as a giant woman, sometimes with only one eye, or with dirty, tangled hair. This unkempt hair is a crucial detail, as it symbolizes the pollution of her domain by human transgression. Some accounts describe her as having a dog-like or walrus-like form, or as a hybrid being, reflecting her connection to the animal world.

Powers

Control over Sea Mammals Arnakuagsak holds absolute dominion over seals, walruses, whales, and other marine life. She can withhold them from hunters, causing starvation, or release them, bringing prosperity. Attested in all major ethnographic accounts of Greenlandic Inuit religion. Her power is contingent on human behavior. She can be appeased through ritual action (shamanic intervention) and communal confession of taboo violations. None, this is her core, universally attested power.

Control over Weather Her displeasure can manifest as violent storms, fog, and treacherous ice conditions, directly endangering hunters and communities. Attested in accounts linking her anger to poor hunting conditions. Her weather control appears to be a secondary effect of her primary power over animals, rather than an independent ability. Some accounts attribute storms to other spirits, but Arnakuagsak's wrath is a common cause cited.

Sovereignty over the Dead She resides in the underworld, Adlivun, and is associated with the souls of the dead. The dead are said to dwell in her house or in a land nearby, and she is sometimes seen as their ruler or caretaker. Attested in descriptions of the afterlife in Greenlandic Inuit cosmology. Her role as a psychopomp or ruler of the dead is less prominent than her role as animal guardian, but it is a consistent part of her domain. The specifics of the afterlife vary widely by region. Some accounts place the dead in a land of plenty, others in a cold, dark place, with Arnakuagsak's role in either scenario being ambiguous.

Cursing and Retribution She can inflict illness, misfortune, and death upon individuals or communities who persistently violate taboos and ignore her authority. Implicit in many stories where sickness follows a breach of taboo, requiring shamanic intervention to placate her. Her retribution is not arbitrary; it is a direct consequence of human actions that pollute her domain. The specific nature of the punishment (e.g., sickness vs. bad luck) is not systematically codified in the sources.

Behavior & Habitat

Arnakuagsak is a matriarchal figure who jealously guards the sea mammals. She is generally benevolent but becomes wrathful when humans violate taboos—particularly those surrounding menstruation, childbirth, death, or the treatment of animals. Her anger manifests as storms, poor hunting, and the withdrawal of seals, whales, and fish. She is not a trickster but a strict enforcer of cosmic and social order, demanding ritual cleanliness and humility from her followers.

Accounts

A girl refuses all suitors. Her father, in anger, marries her to a dog. The girl gives birth to a litter of puppies, which become the ancestors of various peoples (often Europeans, Inuit, and others). The father, ashamed and enraged, throws the girl and her dog-husband into the sea. He cuts off her fingers as she clings to the side of his kayak. As her severed fingers fall into the water, they transform into seals, walruses, and whales. The girl herself sinks to the bottom of the sea and becomes Arnakuagsak, the ruler of the animals born from her own flesh.

When a community faces famine or poor hunting, the angakok (shaman) undertakes a perilous spiritual journey. He (or she) descends through the depths of the ocean, passing through dangerous realms, to reach Arnakuagsak's house. He must overcome various obstacles, including a slippery, blood-slicked floor and a fierce guardian (often a large dog or a rolling sealskin). Upon reaching her, he must comb her hair, which is matted with dirt and filth—the accumulated spiritual pollution from human taboo violations. As he combs, he soothes her anger and asks her why she withholds the animals. She reveals the specific sins of the people. The shaman returns to the community and announces these sins, compelling the offenders to confess, which restores cosmic balance and allows Arnakuagsak to release the animals again.

What We Don't Know

The primary sources are ethnographic accounts from the 19th and early 20th centuries, which may not fully capture the nuances of pre-Christian Inuit theology. The perspective of contemporary Greenlandic Inuit on Arnakuagsak's role in their cultural heritage is not fully explored in the current research. The specific rituals performed in her honor beyond the shamanic descent are not comprehensively documented. The relationship between her and other local spirits, such as the tuurngaq, requires further analysis.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Arnakuagsak not as a 'goddess' but as the living, breathing soul of the Arctic Ocean itself. She is the ecosystem personified — a powerful, sensitive, and easily offended force that rewards those who treat it with respect and punishes those who take it for granted. Her myth is an ancient, brilliant piece of environmental ethics, encoded in a story about a wronged woman, long before the term 'ecosystem' even existed.
