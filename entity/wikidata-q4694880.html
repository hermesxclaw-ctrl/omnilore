This page is intentionally blank and ready for source-backed lore, versions, images, and connections. Ah Peku Patera is located on Io, the innermost of Jupiter's four large Galilean moons. Its domain is the volcanic plains of Io, a world of extreme geological activity. The patera itself is a domain of molten rock and extreme heat, a stark contrast to the surrounding frozen, sulfurous landscape. It exists within Jupiter's intense radiation belts and is subject to immense tidal forces from the giant planet, which are the primary driver of Io's volcanism.

Form

Ah Peku Patera is a patera, a type of irregular, saucer-shaped volcanic crater. It is a depression, typically several kilometers across, with a scalloped or irregular rim. The floor of the patera is likely covered with dark, silicate lava flows and possibly sulfur dioxide frost. It resembles a large, dark, and relatively flat-floored caldera, similar to the volcanic craters found on Earth, but without the central peak or cone often seen in terrestrial calderas. Its appearance is that of a dark, roughly circular to irregular scar on Io's brightly colored, sulfurous surface.

Powers

High-Temperature Silicate Volcanism The patera is capable of erupting silicate lava at temperatures exceeding 1,000°C (1,800°F), far hotter than any active lava on Earth today. This is evidenced by the detection of thermal emission at short infrared wavelengths. Modern planetary science (Galileo, New Horizons, Earth-based infrared observations) The eruption is not continuous; it likely occurs in pulses or episodes. The exact frequency and duration of eruptions are not fully constrained. No major contradictions, but the exact composition of the lava (e.g., mafic vs. ultramafic) is a subject of ongoing research and debate.

Volcanic Plume Generation Like many of Io's paterae, Ah Peku may generate volcanic plumes, which are tall, umbrella-shaped columns of gas and dust (primarily sulfur dioxide) that can rise hundreds of kilometers above the surface. Inferred from observations of other similar paterae and global plume monitoring No specific plume has been definitively attributed to Ah Peku Patera, but its morphology is consistent with being a source of such activity. None.

Surface Resurfacing Through its lava flows, the patera is able to resurface its own floor and the surrounding plains, creating fresh, dark deposits of silicate rock that contrast with Io's brighter, sulfurous surface. Inferred from the appearance of fresh dark material associated with paterae across Io The rate of resurfacing is unknown for this specific patera. None.

Behavior & Habitat

As a volcanic feature, Ah Peku Patera's 'behavior' is defined by its eruptive activity. It is a site of active volcanism, characterized by high-temperature silicate lava eruptions. These eruptions produce intense thermal emission, detectable in infrared wavelengths. The patera may experience periodic or sporadic eruptions, with lava fountains and flows that resurface its floor. It may also be a source of volcanic plumes, which can deposit sulfur and sulfur dioxide across the surrounding terrain. Its behavior is dynamic, changing over timescales of months to years.

Accounts

The mythological Ah Peku is a god of thunder and storms. He is a powerful, potentially destructive force, associated with the noise of thunder and the coming of rain. He is one of many deities in the Maya pantheon responsible for the natural forces that shaped life, agriculture, and the world. He is not one of the major, well-documented gods like Itzamna or Kukulkan, but rather a more specific, possibly local, deity linked to a particular aspect of the sky's power. His role was to bring the thunder, a sign of the storm's approach, and he was likely propitiated to ensure beneficial, rather than destructive, storms for the crops.

In 2006, the IAU officially named a volcanic depression on Jupiter's moon Io after the Maya thunder god. This act was part of a systematic effort to map the solar system, using names from world mythologies to label and organize new worlds. The feature was first observed in detail by the Galileo spacecraft, which revealed its volcanic nature. Later, the New Horizons spacecraft and powerful Earth-based telescopes continued to monitor it. The 'story' of Ah Peku Patera is now a scientific one: it is a window into the most volcanically active world known, a place where the ancient power of a thunder god is mirrored by the real, measured power of tidal forces and molten rock.

What We Don't Know

Research on the geological feature is limited by the lack of a dedicated, high-resolution orbital mission to Io after Galileo. The exact eruption style, frequency, and composition of lava at Ah Peku Patera are still poorly constrained. Research on the mythological deity Ah Peku is severely limited by the scarcity of primary sources. Most knowledge is derived from colonial-era dictionaries and brief mentions in other texts. A comprehensive study of his role and worship in Maya society is a major gap in the scholarly record.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Ah Peku Patera not as a mountain, but as a scar — a dark, irregular, saucer-shaped depression on the face of a world that is literally turning itself inside out. It is a window into the fiery heart of Io, a place where the ancient story of a thunder god has been reborn as a modern scientific discovery. It's a reminder that the universe is not a static, silent place, but a dynamic, violent, and awe-inspiring realm where even the ground beneath your feet is in constant, molten motion.
