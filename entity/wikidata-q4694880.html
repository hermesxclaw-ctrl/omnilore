Who It Is

Imagine a god of thunder so powerful that his name was given to a lake of fire on another world. On Jupiter's moon Io, a world wracked by the gravitational pull of a giant planet, lies Ah Peku Patera — a volcanic depression named after a Yucatec Maya deity of storms. It is a place where the ancient power of a forgotten god finds a new, scientific expression in the most volcanically active body in our solar system. This is the story of a name that bridges the gap between myth and molten rock.

As a volcanic feature, Ah Peku Patera's 'behavior' is defined by its eruptive activity. It is a site of active volcanism, characterized by high-temperature silicate lava eruptions. These eruptions produce intense thermal emission, detectable in infrared wavelengths. The patera may experience periodic or sporadic eruptions, with lava fountains and flows that resurface its floor. It may also be a source of volcanic plumes, which can deposit sulfur and sulfur dioxide across the surrounding terrain. Its behavior is dynamic, changing over timescales of months to years.

High-Temperature Silicate Volcanism

The patera is capable of erupting silicate lava at temperatures exceeding 1,000°C (1,800°F), far hotter than any active lava on Earth today. This is evidenced by the detection of thermal emission at short infrared wavelengths.

Limit: The eruption is not continuous; it likely occurs in pulses or episodes. The exact frequency and duration of eruptions are not fully constrained.

Volcanic Plume Generation

Like many of Io's paterae, Ah Peku may generate volcanic plumes, which are tall, umbrella-shaped columns of gas and dust (primarily sulfur dioxide) that can rise hundreds of kilometers above the surface.

Limit: No specific plume has been definitively attributed to Ah Peku Patera, but its morphology is consistent with being a source of such activity.

Surface Resurfacing

Through its lava flows, the patera is able to resurface its own floor and the surrounding plains, creating fresh, dark deposits of silicate rock that contrast with Io's brighter, sulfurous surface.

Limit: The rate of resurfacing is unknown for this specific patera.

Symbols — In a scientific context, Ah Peku Patera is a symbol of Io's volcanic nature and the power of tidal heating. It symbolizes the dynamic, ever-changing nature of the solar system, a world where the ground itself is in constant flux.

Accounts I
The Story of Ah Peku (Maya Thunder God)Yucatec Maya mythology (pre-Columbian and colonial-era texts)

Pre-Columbian to Colonial period (16th century)

The mythological Ah Peku is a god of thunder and storms. He is a powerful, potentially destructive force, associated with the noise of thunder and the coming of rain. He is one of many deities in the Maya pantheon responsible for the natural forces that shaped life, agriculture, and the world. He is not one of the major, well-documented gods like Itzamna or Kukulkan, but rather a more specific, possibly local, deity linked to a particular aspect of the sky's power. His role was to bring the thunder, a sign of the storm's approach, and he was likely propitiated to ensure beneficial, rather than destructive, storms for the crops. His worship was part of the complex ritual calendar of the Maya, ensuring the cycle of rain and drought remained balanced for agricultural survival.

Accounts II
The Modern Story of Ah Peku PateraNASA, JPL, IAU, and the planetary science community

2006-present (naming and observation)

In 2006, the IAU officially named a volcanic depression on Jupiter's moon Io after the Maya thunder god. This act was part of a systematic effort to map the solar system, using names from world mythologies to label and organize new worlds. The feature was first observed in detail by the Galileo spacecraft, which revealed its volcanic nature. Later, the New Horizons spacecraft and powerful Earth-based telescopes continued to monitor it. The 'story' of Ah Peku Patera is now a scientific one: it is a window into the most volcanically active world known, a place where the ancient power of a thunder god is mirrored by the real, measured power of tidal forces and molten rock. The name connects ancient Maya culture to the cutting edge of space exploration, creating a permanent, cross-cultural landmark in the solar system. It also highlights the ongoing process of discovery and mapping of our cosmic neighborhood.

Ah Peku Patera is a volcano like Mount Vesuvius or Kilauea. — It is a patera, a type of irregular, flat-floored volcanic depression. It is a volcanic feature, but its morphology is distinct from a classic stratovolcano or shield volcano. It is more akin to a large caldera.

The Maya god Ah Peku was a major, well-documented deity like Zeus or Odin. — Ah Peku is a relatively obscure figure in Maya mythology. Our knowledge is limited to his name and his association with thunder. He was not a central figure in the Maya pantheon.

The patera is named because the Maya believed the god lived on Jupiter's moon. — The name is a modern scientific convention. The IAU chose the name to follow a systematic naming scheme for Io's features, drawing from world mythologies. There is no ancient Maya knowledge of Jupiter's moons.
