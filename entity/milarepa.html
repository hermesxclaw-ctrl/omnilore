Archive Dossier — Identity

Milarepa

Pronunciation MEE-lah-REP-ah (Tibetan: མི་ལ་རས་པ)

Known As

Jetsun MilarepaMilarepa Shepa DorjeMila ThöpagaThe Cotton-CladThe Great Yogi

Etymology

The name Milarepa (mi la ras pa) is Tibetan, meaning 'Mila, the cotton-clad one', referring to his practice of wearing only a thin cotton cloth even in winter, symbolizing his mastery of inner heat (tummo). He was born with the name Thöpaga (thos pa dga'), meaning 'delightful to hear'.

Cultural Ownership

Tibetan Buddhism, specifically the Kagyu school. His life is a cornerstone of Tibetan religious culture and is known throughout the Himalayan world, including Bhutan, Nepal, and among Tibetan diaspora communities.

Disputed Identifications

There is no serious dispute about his historical existence, but the details of his biography are heavily hagiographical. Some scholars question the accuracy of traditional dates and events, seeing them as symbolic rather than factual. The story of his early black magic may be a literary device to emphasize his later transformation.

Archive Dossier — Approach

In the frozen caves of the Himalayas, a man who had once killed dozens through sorcery sat naked in the snow, singing songs of enlightenment. Milarepa — Tibet's most beloved poet-saint — transformed from a black-magic avenger into a master of the Vajrayana path, proving that even the darkest karma can be burned away by relentless practice. His life story, a blend of history and hagiography, has inspired Buddhists for nearly a millennium and continues to challenge our notions of sin, redemption, and the power of the human mind.

Born with a black knot, I died with a white one. I killed many with black magic, but through the Dharma I saved countless more. No sin is beyond redemption.

Who It Is

Milarepa (Jetsün Milarepa) is one of the most beloved figures in Tibetan Buddhism — a poet, mystic, and enlightened yogi whose life story is a dramatic tale of sin, suffering, and redemption. Born to a wealthy family, young Milarepa's father died, and his aunt and uncle seized the family's wealth, forcing Milarepa, his mother, and his sister into poverty. His mother sent him to learn black magic, which he used to take vengeance by collapsing his relatives' house during a wedding feast, killing 35 people. Consumed by guilt, Milarepa sought a teacher who could purify his karma. He found the great translator Marpa Lotsawa, who subjected him to extreme trials — including building and demolishing stone towers repeatedly — to burn off his negative karma. After years of arduous practice in mountain caves, subsisting on nettle soup (which turned his skin greenish), Milarepa attained enlightenment. He spent the rest of his life wandering the Himalayas, teaching through spontaneous songs (doha) of realization. His Hundred Thousand Songs is a classic of Tibetan literature. Milarepa is depicted as a thin, green-skinned yogi wearing only a white cotton cloth (hence his name: Mila 'the cotton-clad'), seated in a cave with a hand to his ear, listening to the inner sound of reality. His story is a powerful testament to the possibility of spiritual transformation no matter how evil one's past actions.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

Traditional depictions show Milarepa as an ascetic with a thin, gaunt frame, often with a greenish or greyish complexion due to his diet of nettles. He is typically shown wearing a white cotton cloth and sometimes a meditation belt. His hair is often tied in a topknot, and he may have a hand cupped to his ear, listening to the sounds of the universe.

Symbols & Iconography

Common symbols include the cotton cloth (ras), the skull cup (kapala), the tiger-skin loincloth (in some depictions), the trident (khatvanga), and the hand-to-ear gesture (karana mudra). He is often seated on a deerskin or in a cave. His images convey his mastery of yogic powers and his role as a poet.

Behaviour & Personality

Milarepa is portrayed as fiercely determined, single-minded, and initially vengeful, but after his conversion he becomes humble, compassionate, and devoted to practice. He is known for his ascetic endurance, his skill in debate and teaching, and his spontaneous, often humorous songs. He shows a deep empathy for suffering beings and a willingness to challenge orthodoxies.

Domains & Habitats

He lived primarily in remote caves and hermitages in the Himalayas, including Drakpa Tashi Cave, Chuwar, Lachi Snow Range, and other locations in Tibet and Nepal. He is associated with mountains and wilderness, where he meditated for years.

Version Differences

The traditional hagiography emphasizes his early sins and dramatic conversion. Some versions place more emphasis on his magical powers. Modern scholarly accounts tend to interpret the biography as symbolic, focusing on his poetry and teachings. There are also variations in the details of his encounters with disciples, such as Rechungpa.

Archive Dossier — Powers

Tummo (Inner Heat)

Source Version Traditional hagiography, Kagyu lineage

Description

The ability to generate intense internal heat through yogic practice, allowing him to survive extreme cold in thin cotton clothing.

Limitations

Requires long-term practice and mastery of specific yogic techniques; not a supernatural power but a result of advanced meditation.

Contradictions

Some accounts exaggerate this as miraculous, while others see it as a natural result of yoga.

Black Magic (early life)

Source Version Traditional hagiography

Description

Before his conversion, Milarepa used tantric sorcery to kill enemies of his family, reportedly causing hailstorms and destruction.

Limitations

He later repudiated these powers and sought purification.

Contradictions

The extent of his involvement is debated; some see it as a didactic story to highlight his later redemption.

Miraculous Powers (Siddhis)

Source Version Hagiography and oral tradition

Description

After enlightenment, he is said to have performed various miracles: flying, passing through solid objects, and appearing in multiple places.

Limitations

These are typically described as displays of realization, not magic for personal gain.

Contradictions

Skeptical scholars view these as legendary embellishments.

Poetic and Teaching Ability

Source Version The Hundred Thousand Songs

Description

He composed spontaneous songs (mgur) that conveyed profound Buddhist teachings in accessible, poetic language.

Limitations

None; this is his most enduring legacy.

Contradictions

Some songs may have been composed by later disciples, but the core is attributed to him.
