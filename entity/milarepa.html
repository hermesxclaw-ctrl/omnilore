Who It Is

In the frozen caves of the Himalayas, a man who had once killed dozens through sorcery sat naked in the snow, singing songs of enlightenment. Milarepa — Tibet's most beloved poet-saint — transformed from a black-magic avenger into a master of the Vajrayana path, proving that even the darkest karma can be burned away by relentless practice. His life story, a blend of history and hagiography, has inspired Buddhists for nearly a millennium and continues to challenge our notions of sin, redemption, and the power of the human mind.

Milarepa is portrayed as fiercely determined, single-minded, and initially vengeful, but after his conversion he becomes humble, compassionate, and devoted to practice. He is known for his ascetic endurance, his skill in debate and teaching, and his spontaneous, often humorous songs. He shows a deep empathy for suffering beings and a willingness to challenge orthodoxies.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Milarepa (mi la ras pa) is Tibetan, meaning 'Mila, the cotton-clad one', referring to his practice of wearing only a thin cotton cloth even in winter, symbolizing his mastery of inner heat (tummo). He was born with the name Thöpaga (thos pa dga'), meaning 'delightful to hear'.

Pronunciation — MEE-lah-REP-ah (Tibetan: མི་ལ་རས་པ)

Aliases — Jetsun Milarepa, Milarepa Shepa Dorje, Mila Thöpaga, The Cotton-Clad, The Great Yogi

Powers
Tummo (Inner Heat)

The ability to generate intense internal heat through yogic practice, allowing him to survive extreme cold in thin cotton clothing.

Limit: Requires long-term practice and mastery of specific yogic techniques; not a supernatural power but a result of advanced meditation.

Black Magic (early life)

Before his conversion, Milarepa used tantric sorcery to kill enemies of his family, reportedly causing hailstorms and destruction.

Limit: He later repudiated these powers and sought purification.

Miraculous Powers (Siddhis)

After enlightenment, he is said to have performed various miracles: flying, passing through solid objects, and appearing in multiple places.

Limit: These are typically described as displays of realization, not magic for personal gain.

Poetic and Teaching Ability

He composed spontaneous songs (mgur) that conveyed profound Buddhist teachings in accessible, poetic language.

Limit: None; this is his most enduring legacy.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Traditional depictions show Milarepa as an ascetic with a thin, gaunt frame, often with a greenish or greyish complexion due to his diet of nettles. He is typically shown wearing a white cotton cloth and sometimes a meditation belt. His hair is often tied in a topknot, and he may have a hand cupped to his ear, listening to the sounds of the universe.

Symbols — Common symbols include the cotton cloth (ras), the skull cup (kapala), the tiger-skin loincloth (in some depictions), the trident (khatvanga), and the hand-to-ear gesture (karana mudra). He is often seated on a deerskin or in a cave. His images convey his mastery of yogic powers and his role as a poet.

Domains — He lived primarily in remote caves and hermitages in the Himalayas, including Drakpa Tashi Cave, Chuwar, Lachi Snow Range, and other locations in Tibet and Nepal. He is associated with mountains and wilderness, where he meditated for years.

Accounts I
The Early Life and RevengeThe Life of Milarepa

11th century (traditional dates: 1052-1135)

Milarepa's father died when he was young, and his relatives seized the family wealth. His mother, driven to desperation, sent him to learn black magic to take revenge. He killed many of his enemies by unleashing hailstorms and causing a building to collapse. Later, he felt deep remorse and sought a teacher to purify his negative karma. His revenge caused great suffering, leading to his intense remorse and eventual conversion.

Trials under MarpaThe Life of Milarepa

11th century

Milarepa sought out Marpa Lotsawa, a great translator and master. Marpa subjected him to severe trials, including building and demolishing towers repeatedly, to purify his negative karma. After years of hardship, Marpa finally accepted him as a disciple and gave him teachings. Milarepa's karma was purified, and he received the lineage teachings that led to his enlightenment.

Accounts II
The Hermit Years and EnlightenmentThe Life of Milarepa

11th century

After receiving teachings, Milarepa meditated in caves for many years, often subsisting on nettles. He attained enlightenment in a single lifetime, becoming a fully realized master. His experiences are recounted in his songs. He became a renowned teacher and attracted many disciples, including Rechungpa and Gampopa.

The Song of the Yak HornThe Hundred Thousand Songs

11th century

In a famous story, Milarepa demonstrated his miraculous powers by entering a yak horn that was too small for him, confounding a skeptical scholar. He used this to teach the illusory nature of reality. The scholar became a disciple.
