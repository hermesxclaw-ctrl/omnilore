Güestia

The Night Host, Asturian Procession of the Dead

also known asLa GüestiaLa GüestaLa GüesteLa Santa CompañaThe Night HostAsturian Procession of the Dead
TypeGhost Procession / Death Omen
CultureSpanish
Who It Is
Form

The Güestia is described as a procession of souls or ghosts that wander the night paths of rural Asturias. The figures are often depicted as shrouded in white or grey, with candles or torches that emit a faint, eerie light. Some accounts describe them as carrying a coffin, which is a sign that a death will occur in the house of the person who sees them. The procession is led by a living person, known as the 'cargado' or 'bearer', who is forced to carry a cross or a candle until another living person takes their place. The souls themselves are often faceless or have hollow eyes, and their feet do not touch the ground, floating slightly above the earth. The procession moves in silence, but the sound of footsteps and the rustling of robes can be heard, along with the faint murmur of prayers or lamentations. The appearance varies by region: in some versions, the souls are dressed in black, while in others they wear white; in some, they carry torches, in others, they carry bones or skulls.

Powers

Death Omen The Güestia has the ability to foretell death. Anyone who sees or hears the procession is marked for death, usually within a year. The appearance of the procession at a house is a certain sign that someone in that household will die. This power is not malevolent but is a supernatural warning, and it is believed that the souls are fulfilling a divine or cosmic duty.

Spectral Manifestation The Güestia can manifest as a ghostly procession that is visible to some but not to others. It can also be heard by those who cannot see it, as the sound of footsteps, whispers, or the ringing of a bell. The procession is said to be able to pass through solid objects, such as walls, and to move without touching the ground.

Burden Transfer The 'cargado' (bearer) of the Güestia has the ability to transfer their burden to another living person. This is done by handing over the cross or candle to an unsuspecting person, who then becomes the new 'cargado'. This transfer is a curse, and the new bearer is doomed to lead the procession until they can pass it on. This power is a key element of the myth, as it creates a cycle of fear and avoidance.

Accounts

A man named Xuan from a small village in western Asturias was walking home late one night when he encountered the Güestia. He saw a procession of hooded figures carrying candles, led by a woman he recognized as his neighbor, María. María was the 'cargada', carrying a large wooden cross. She looked at Xuan with pleading eyes and tried to hand him the cross, but Xuan, knowing the danger, fled. He ran to the church and hid inside, but the next day he learned that María had died. Xuan was haunted by the memory and later became a hermit, fearing that the Güestia would return for him.

A farmer named Pelayo was returning from a market in a nearby town when he reached a crossroads at midnight. He saw the Güestia approaching, led by a tall figure in a black cloak, unlike the usual white-robed souls. The figure carried a coffin on its shoulder, and behind it followed a crowd of souls with hollow eyes. Pelayo hid behind a tree, but the procession stopped at the crossroads, and the leader placed the coffin on the ground. Pelayo heard a voice whisper his name, and he felt a cold hand on his shoulder. He turned and saw his own reflection in the coffin, but aged and dead. He fled, but from that night on, he was plagued by nightmares and died within a month.

A young woman named Covadonga was walking to a neighbor's house to help with a birth when she encountered the Güestia. The 'cargado' was a man she knew, a shepherd named Ramón, who was carrying a candle and a cross. Ramón tried to hand her the cross, but Covadonga refused, saying 'God save me'. The procession passed, but Covadonga felt a chill and heard a voice say 'You will be the next'. She returned home and fell ill, but she was saved by a priest who performed an exorcism. The priest told her that she had been marked, but the exorcism broke the curse. She never spoke of the incident again.

Scholarly Debates
Nature of the Güestia
The Güestia is a purely malevolent force that brings death and misfortune. ⇌ The Güestia is a neutral or even benevolent force, serving as a warning to allow people to prepare for death, and the souls are in purgatory seeking prayers.
Leadership of the procession
The Güestia is always led by a living person (the 'cargado') carrying a cross or candle. ⇌ In some versions, the Güestia is led by a supernatural figure, such as a ghostly warrior (the Estantigua) or a hooded figure with a coffin, and the 'cargado' is not always present.
Visibility
The Güestia is visible to everyone who encounters it, and seeing it is a death omen. ⇌ The Güestia is only visible to those who are destined to die or to the 'cargado', and others may only hear it or feel its presence.
What We Don't Know

There is limited scholarly research on the Güestia specifically, as it is often subsumed under the broader study of the Santa Compaña. The oral tradition is rich, but many accounts have not been systematically collected or analyzed. The relationship between the Güestia and pre-Christian beliefs (e.g., Celtic or Roman) is underexplored. Additionally, the modern reception of the Güestia in urban areas and among younger generations is not well documented. The role of the 'cargado' and the psychological impact of the belief on individuals is a potential area for further study.

A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine walking alone on a moonlit path in the mountains of Asturias, when you see a line of hooded figures approaching, their candles flickering in the darkness. They carry a coffin, and you feel a chill that has nothing to do with the cold. This is the Güestia, a procession of the dead that has haunted the imagination of northern Spain for centuries.
