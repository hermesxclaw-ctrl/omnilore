Who It Is

The Güestia is the Asturian variant of the pan-Iberian ghost procession, akin to the Galician Santa Compaña. The word 'güestia' derives from Latin 'hostis' (enemy, but also 'host' in the sacred sense), suggesting a host or army of the dead. The Güestia appears as a procession of souls wrapped in white shrouds, each carrying a lit candle, marching silently through the Asturian countryside at night. The air grows cold, and dogs howl at their passing. The living person leading the procession bears a cross and is cursed to walk until they can pass their burden to another. The Güestia is most commonly encountered at crossroads — liminal spaces where the world of the dead bleeds through. Those who see the Güestia should never look directly at the candles, should make the sign of the cross, and should leave offerings (or sometimes, throw a rock in the opposite direction to distract the dead). Like its Galician cousin, the Güestia visits the homes of the dying and circles them three times — the number carries into the waiting period before the death occurs. Asturias, with its misty mountains, deep gorges, and isolation from the Moorish conquest (the region was never fully Islamized), preserved a uniquely dense body of pre-Christian folk beliefs. The Güestia reflects the Celtic substratum of northern Iberian folklore — connecting to the broader Atlantic European tradition of the 'company of the dead' found from Ireland to Galicia to Brittany.
