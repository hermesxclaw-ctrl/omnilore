Who It Is

Imagine walking alone on a moonlit path in the mountains of Asturias, when you see a line of hooded figures approaching, their candles flickering in the darkness. They carry a coffin, and you feel a chill that has nothing to do with the cold. This is the Güestia, a procession of the dead that has haunted the imagination of northern Spain for centuries. But is it a curse, a warning, or a glimpse into the afterlife? Step into the world of Asturian mythology and discover the secrets of the Night Host.

The Güestia is not a single entity but a collective of souls, and its behavior is ritualistic and purposeful. It appears only at night, usually between midnight and dawn, and follows a fixed route that passes by houses, crossroads, and cemeteries. The procession does not speak, but its presence is felt as a heavy, oppressive atmosphere, and it is said to bring a chill to the air. The primary purpose of the Güestia is to announce an imminent death: whoever sees it, or hears it, will die within a short time, unless they are the 'cargado' who must carry the cross. Powers
Death Omen

The Güestia has the ability to foretell death. Anyone who sees or hears the procession is marked for death, usually within a year. The appearance of the procession at a house is a certain sign that someone in that household will die. This power is not malevolent but is a supernatural warning, and it is believed that the souls are fulfilling a divine or cosmic duty.

Limit:

Spectral Manifestation

The Güestia can manifest as a ghostly procession that is visible to some but not to others. It can also be heard by those who cannot see it, as the sound of footsteps, whispers, or the ringing of a bell. The procession is said to be able to pass through solid objects, such as walls, and to move without touching the ground.

Limit:

Burden Transfer

The 'cargado' (bearer) of the Güestia has the ability to transfer their burden to another living person. This is done by handing over the cross or candle to an unsuspecting person, who then becomes the new 'cargado'. This transfer is a curse, and the new bearer is doomed to lead the procession until they can pass it on. This power is a key element of the myth, as it creates a cycle of fear and avoidance.

Accounts I
The Cargado of the GüestiaOral tradition, recorded in 'Mitos y supersticiones asturianas' by Fundación Municipal de Cultura de Oviedo

19th century

A man named Xuan from a small village in western Asturias was walking home late one night when he encountered the Güestia. He saw a procession of hooded figures carrying candles, led by a woman he recognized as his neighbor, María. María was the 'cargada', carrying a large wooden cross. She looked at Xuan with pleading eyes and tried to hand him the cross, but Xuan, knowing the danger, fled. He ran to the church and hid inside, but the next day he learned that María had died. Xuan was haunted by the memory and later became a hermit, fearing that the Güestia would return for him. María died, and Xuan lived in fear, eventually becoming a recluse. The story serves as a warning to never accept the cross from the Güestia.

Accounts II
The Crossroads of the DeadOral tradition, recorded in 'La Güestia. Personaje Mitológico de Asturias' (local folklore collection)

20th century

A farmer named Pelayo was returning from a market in a nearby town when he reached a crossroads at midnight. He saw the Güestia approaching, led by a tall figure in a black cloak, unlike the usual white-robed souls. The figure carried a coffin on its shoulder, and behind it followed a crowd of souls with hollow eyes. Pelayo hid behind a tree, but the procession stopped at the crossroads, and the leader placed the coffin on the ground. Pelayo heard a voice whisper his name, and he felt a cold hand on his shoulder. He turned and saw his own reflection in the coffin, but aged and dead. He fled, but from that night on, he was plagued by nightmares and died within a month. Pelayo died within a month, as foretold by the vision. The story emphasizes the danger of encountering the Güestia at a crossroads, a liminal space.

The Passing of the CrossOral tradition, recorded in 'Asturian Mythology: The Xanas, the Nuberu and the Güestia' (YouTube documentary)

20th century

A young woman named Covadonga was walking to a neighbor's house to help with a birth when she encountered the Güestia. The 'cargado' was a man she knew, a shepherd named Ramón, who was carrying a candle and a cross. Ramón tried to hand her the cross, but Covadonga refused, saying 'God save me'. The procession passed, but Covadonga felt a chill and heard a voice say 'You will be the next'. She returned home and fell ill, but she was saved by a priest who performed an exorcism. The priest told her that she had been marked, but the exorcism broke the curse. She never spoke of the incident again. Covadonga was marked for death but was saved by an exorcism. The story shows the role of the Church in countering the curse.
