Who It Is

In the beginning, when the world was still a thick, impassable forest, it was Ogun who hacked the first path with his machete, clearing the way for the other orishas to descend to Earth. He is the primordial blacksmith, the god of iron and war, whose temper is as sharp as his blade—yet he is also the guardian of drivers and the patron of all who work with metal. His story is one of creation and destruction, of divine service and tragic fall, and his worship spans continents, from the sacred groves of Yorubaland to the altars of Havana, Rio, and Port-au-Prince.

Ogun is characterized by aggression, courage, directness, and a 'doglike' personality—loyal but fierce. He is impetuous, authoritarian, hardworking, and sometimes selfish. He is associated with transformation, mediation, and the clearing of paths. He is a warrior and a craftsman, and he is known to enjoy alcohol (especially rum) and the company of women.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Ogun' (Yoruba: Ògún) is believed to derive from the Yoruba word for 'iron' or 'metal', reflecting his association with metallurgy. In Dahomean and Ewe traditions, the equivalent deity is called 'Gu' or 'Egu'.

Pronunciation — OH-gun (Yoruba: Ò-gún)

Aliases — Ògún, Ogou, Ogum, Gu, Egu, Osin Imole

Iron mastery

Ogun is the master of iron and metallurgy, granting him power over all iron tools and weapons.

Ogun is a judge who punishes those who break oaths sworn on iron.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Ogun is typically depicted as a powerful, muscular man dressed in red and black, often carrying a machete, cutlass, or other iron tools. In some traditions, he wears a cap adorned with iron implements. In Haitian Vodou, Ogou is often represented as a military figure with a red sash and an iron saber.

Symbols — Iron is Ogun's primary emblem. His symbols include the dog, palm frond, machete, cutlass, anvil, hammer, and other blacksmith tools. In ritual, his altars display iron objects, and his devotees wear chains of iron implements. In Candomblé, his symbols include a sword, broadsword, and iron chain.

Domains — Ogun is the deity of war, iron, hunting, metalworking, metallurgy, blacksmiths, technology, innovation, and divine judgment. He is also associated with rum and rum-making. His domain includes roads and vehicles, making him the patron of drivers. He is believed to have disappeared into the earth at Ire-Ekiti, and he is celebrated in Ogun, Ekiti, Oyo, and Ondo States in Nigeria.

Accounts I
Ogun's path-clearingYoruba oral tradition

Primordial

In the beginning, the Earth was a dense forest. Ogun, with his machete and the help of a dog, cleared a path for the other orishas to descend. This act earned him the title 'Osin Imole' (first of the primordial orishas to come to Earth). Ogun became the pioneer and guardian of the crossroads.

Accounts II
Ogun and the throne of IfeYoruba oral tradition

Historical/mythical

After the death of Obatala, who reigned twice, Ogun attempted to seize the throne of the Ife Empire. He was ousted by Obalufon Ogbogbodirin and sent into exile. This event is commemorated in the Olójó Festival. Ogun's exile led to his wandering and eventual disappearance at Ire-Ekiti.

Ogun's disappearance at IreYoruba oral tradition

Mythical

As the first king of Ire, Ogun killed some of his subjects who failed to show respect, then killed himself with his own sword. However, his followers believe he did not die but disappeared into the earth at Ire-Ekiti, promising to help those who call on his name. Ogun became known as Onire (owner of Ire) and is worshipped as a protector.

Ogun is the same as the Christian devil. — Ogun is a deity in Yoruba religion, not a devil figure. This misconception arises from colonial misunderstandings of African religions.

Ogun is only a god of war. — Ogun is also the god of iron, technology, innovation, and craftsmanship. He is the patron of blacksmiths and drivers.

Animal sacrifice in Ogun worship is cruel and meaningless. — Animal sacrifice is a sacred ritual with specific meanings and purposes in Yoruba and diaspora religions, often for atonement or offering.
