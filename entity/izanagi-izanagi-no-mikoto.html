Who It Is

Izanagi is the primal creator kami in Japanese mythology who, together with his sister-wife Izanami, created the Japanese archipelago and the first generation of kami. He is one of the seven generations of heavenly kami, the seventh and final generation of the Kamiyonanayo. With Izanami, standing on the Floating Bridge of Heaven (Ama-no-Ukihashi), they stirred the brine with the jeweled spear (Ame-no-Nuboko) and formed the island of Onogoro. They descended there and married. Using the spear, they created the islands of Japan. After Izanami died giving birth to the fire kami Kagutsuchi, Izanagi visited Yomi (the underworld) to retrieve her, only to find her rotting and transformed into a corpse-goddess. He fled, sealing the entrance to Yomi with a massive boulder, and performed purification (misogi) from which Amaterasu, Tsukuyomi, and Susanoo were born. Izanagi is a foundational figure — his purification created the three most important kami, and his boulder sealing Yomi established the boundary between life and death. After his purifications, he withdrew to a permanent dwelling and never acted directly in myth again.

Origins

Izanagi emerged as the seventh generation of heavenly kami. With Izanami, he created the Japanese islands by stirring the sea with the jeweled spear. After Izanami's death and his journey to Yomi, he purified himself, and from his left eye was born Amaterasu, from his right eye Tsukuyomi, and from his nose Susanoo.

The Approach

Before the islands of Japan rose from the sea, before the sun goddess took her place in the heavens, there was a divine brother and sister who stood at the edge of chaos, armed with a jeweled spear and a question: what would they create together? Their answer shaped a world, but their grief would tear it apart—and from that rupture, the very forces of life, death, and renewal were born.

Content advisory. Contains descriptions of death, decay, and divine violence (including the killing of a child). No explicit sexual content, but the narrative includes a divine marriage and procreation.

Etymology

The name is traditionally interpreted as derived from the verb 'izanau' (to invite), with the suffix '-gi' indicating a male. Thus, Izanagi means 'Male-who-invites'. An alternative theory by Shiratori Kurakichi suggests the root 'isa-' from 'isao' (achievement/merit).

Cultural Ownership

Shinto (Japanese indigenous religion)

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars have compared Izanagi to other creator deities like Pangu in Chinese mythology, but these are speculative parallels and not direct identifications.

Description

Appearance

Izanagi is typically depicted as a male deity, but no specific physical description is given in the primary sources. In later artistic representations, he is shown as a regal figure, often with a beard, wearing traditional Japanese court attire.

Symbols & Iconography

Associated symbols include the jeweled spear (Ame-no-nuboko), the heavenly pillar, and the peach (used to repel the hags of Yomi). In art, he is sometimes shown with a spear or sword.

Behaviour & Personality

Izanagi is portrayed as a proactive and decisive creator, but also impulsive and emotional. He defies the gods' command to look at Izanami, kills his son in grief, and boldly confronts the underworld. He is also depicted as a strict father, expelling Susanoo for his disruptive behavior.

Domains & Habitats

As a creator deity, Izanagi is associated with the heavenly realm (Takamagahara) and the earthly realm. He descends to Yomi (the underworld) and later purifies himself at a river on Earth.

Version Differences

In the Kojiki, Izanagi and Izanami are the seventh generation of gods, while the Nihon Shoki includes variants where they are descendants of earlier deities. The order and number of islands created also differ between the two texts.

Stories & Myths

Creation of the Land

Source Kojiki, Nihon ShokiCulture JapanesePeriod Mythic age (before recorded history)

Summary

Izanagi and Izanami, commanded by the other gods, churn the primordial sea with a jeweled spear. The brine congeals into the island of Onogoro. They descend, erect a heavenly pillar, and perform a marriage ritual. After a mistake, they repeat it correctly and give birth to the islands of Japan and many deities.

Participants

Izanagi

Izanami

The other gods (Kotoamatsukami)

Consequences

The Japanese archipelago is formed, and the divine lineage leading to the imperial family begins.

Variants

The Nihon Shoki provides a different order and number of islands, and includes variants on the origin of the couple.

Descent into Yomi

Source Kojiki, Nihon ShokiCulture JapanesePeriod Mythic age

Summary

After Izanami dies giving birth to the fire god, Izanagi descends to Yomi to bring her back. He finds her rotting, and in fear, flees. Izanami sends hags and thunder gods after him. He uses grapes, bamboo shoots, and peaches to distract them, and seals the entrance with a boulder. Izanami curses him, vowing to kill 1000 people a day; he responds that he will create 1500.

Participants

Izanagi

Izanami

Yakusanoikazuchi (thunder gods)

Yomotsu-shikome (hags)

Consequences

Death and life become intertwined; Izanagi is polluted by his journey, leading to the need for purification.

Variants

The Nihon Shoki has similar but slightly different details, such as the exact words of the curse.

Purification at Awagihara

Source KojikiCulture JapanesePeriod Mythic age

Summary

Izanagi bathes in a river to cleanse himself of the pollution of Yomi. As he removes his clothing and washes, various deities are born. From his left eye, right eye, and nose, the sun goddess Amaterasu, the moon god Tsukuyomi, and the storm god Susanoo are born.

Participants

Izanagi

Consequences

The Three Precious Children are born, and the world is divided among them.

Variants

The Nihon Shoki also describes the birth of these deities, but with some differences in the details.

Exile of Susanoo

Source Kojiki, Nihon ShokiCulture JapanesePeriod Mythic age

Summary

Susanoo, given dominion over the seas, weeps and causes devastation. He asks to go to the land of his mother, and Izanagi, enraged, expels him.

Participants

Izanagi

Susanoo

Consequences

Susanoo becomes a wandering deity, leading to further myths.

Variants

The Nihon Shoki has similar accounts, but the details of Susanoo's misbehavior vary.

Infanticide

Source Kojiki, Nihon Shoki

Description

Izanagi kills his son Kagutsuchi with a sword, an act of grief and rage after Izanami's death.

Scholarly Context

This act is often interpreted as a metaphor for the cycle of creation and destruction, and the necessity of death for life.

Desecration of the dead

Source Kojiki, Nihon Shoki

Description

Izanagi breaks his promise not to look at Izanami in Yomi, and sees her decayed body.

Scholarly Context

This story highlights the taboo against looking at the dead and the concept of pollution (kegare) in Shinto.
