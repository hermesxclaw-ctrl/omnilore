Who It Is

Imagine a god so vital to a people that when an empire stole his statue, it was like stealing their very sky. Atarsamain—whose name literally means 'Athtar of the Heavens'—was a deity of the morning star, a celestial warrior whose idol was a prize worth conquering for. In the 8th century BCE, the mighty Assyrian king Tiglath-Pileser III did exactly that, ripping the god from his worshippers and carting him off to Mesopotamia as a trophy. But who was this heavenly power, and what did it mean to lose the sky itself?

Atarsamain's behavior is inferred from his role as a divine patron of Arabian tribal confederations. He was invoked for military victory, protection of caravans, and the success of kings. As a Venus deity, he likely had a dual aspect—a fierce warrior in his morning-star manifestation and a more benign or protective figure in other contexts.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Atarsamain is a compound of the divine name Athtar (or Attar) and the West Semitic word for 'heaven' or 'sky' (shamayin/shamem). The name likely means 'Athtar of the Heavens' or 'Venus of the Sky', linking the deity to the planet Venus in its morning-star aspect.

Pronunciation — ah-TAR-sah-mayn

Aliases — Athtar-shamayin, Attar-Shamem, Atarshamin, Athtar of the Heavens

Ownership — Ancient South Arabian (Sabaean) culture, with parallels across the broader West Semitic world. The deity is attested in the kingdom of Saba (modern-day Yemen) and was also venerate

Powers
Celestial Authority

As 'Athtar of the Heavens,' Atarsamain held sway over the sky and the movements of Venus. This granted him a cosmic perspective and authority over celestial omens, which were crucial for timing wars, trade, and agriculture.

Limit: No specific limitations are recorded.

Divine Patronage of Kings

Atarsamain was invoked as a protector of tribal kings and their military campaigns. The Assyrian capture of his image suggests that possession of the idol was seen as a way to secure the deity's favor for one's own side.

Limit: His power was tied to the physical idol in some traditions, as the Assyrians believed capturing the image transferred his allegiance.

Venusian Dual Aspect

Like many Venus deities (Ishtar, Aphrodite), Atarsamain may have embodied both a warlike aspect (as the fierce morning star) and a protective or nurturing aspect (as the evening star). This duality is inferred from comparative mythology rather than direct evidence.

Limit: This is a scholarly inference, not a documented belief.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No direct visual depictions of Atarsamain survive. As a celestial deity of the morning star, he was likely symbolized by astral imagery—a star or a radiant disc—similar to how other West Semitic Venus deities were represented. The deity's name emphasizes the sky, suggesting an association with the open heavens rather than a specific natural feature.

Symbols — The morning star (Venus), a radiant disc or star symbol, possibly a spear or sword as a warrior deity. The name itself—'of the Heavens'—serves as a verbal symbol of his celestial domain.

Domains — The sky, celestial phenomena (especially the morning star/Venus), warfare, tribal protection, kingship, and possibly rain and fertility (by extension from his identification with Athtar).

Accounts I
The Assyrian Capture of AtarsamainAnnals of Tiglath-Pileser III (c. 732 BCE)

8th century BCE

During his campaign against the Arab tribes of the northern Arabian desert, the Assyrian king Tiglath-Pileser III captured the divine image of Atarsamain, along with other Arabian gods. The idol was taken to Assyria as a trophy, demonstrating the subjugation of the Arab tribes and the transfer of divine favor to the Assyrian king. The capture was a major propaganda victory for Assyria and a religious humiliation for the Arabs. It also provides the only definitive historical event featuring Atarsamain.

Accounts II
The Sabaean Dedicatory TraditionSabaean inscriptions from Sirwah and other sites

c. 8th–7th century BCE

In the kingdom of Saba, Atarsamain was invoked in dedicatory inscriptions, often alongside Almaqah, the national god. These inscriptions typically commemorate military victories, royal building projects, or the construction of temples, indicating Atarsamain's role as a patron of the Sabaean state and its rulers. The inscriptions establish Atarsamain as a significant, though secondary, deity in the Sabaean pantheon, with a particular focus on his celestial and martial aspects.

Atarsamain is the same as the Greek god Apollo. — There is no evidence for this identification. Apollo was a Greek deity, and while some Greek writers equated Arabian gods with Greek ones (interpretatio graeca), Atarsamain's specific attributes do not match Apollo's.

Atarsamain was the chief god of Saba. — The chief god of Saba was Almaqah. Atarsamain was a significant deity, but he was secondary to Almaqah in the state pantheon.
