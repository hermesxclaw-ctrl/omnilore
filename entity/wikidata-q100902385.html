The Approach

At the edge of the living world, where the last light of day fades into an impenetrable gloom, lies a slope that leads to the land of the dead—Yomotsu Hirasaka. It is a threshold not merely of earth and stone, but of life and death, myth and reality. Here, the god Izanagi fled from the decayed embrace of his beloved, sealing the way with a boulder that echoes through millennia. This is the pass that shaped the Japanese cosmos, and its story is as much about love and loss as it is about the boundary that defines our mortality.

Content advisory. Contains themes of death, decay, and the underworld. The myth includes graphic descriptions of a decomposing body.
Identity

Yomotsu Hirasaka

Pronunciation yo-mo-tsu hi-ra-sa-ka

Etymology

The name is Japanese. 'Yomotsu' (黄泉津) relates to Yomi, the Japanese underworld, and 'Hirasaka' (平坂) means 'flat slope' or 'pass'. Thus the name can be interpreted as 'the flat slope of the underworld' or 'the pass to Yomi'.

Cultural Ownership

Japanese mythology and folklore; also appears in modern Japanese popular culture (anime, manga, games) as a location or concept.

Disputed Identifications

Some sources conflate Yomotsu Hirasaka with the more famous Yomotsu Hirasaka (黄泉比良坂) mentioned in the Kojiki, but the exact nature and location are subject to scholarly debate. In modern fiction, it may be a specific character or place, but no canonical definition is established.

Description

Appearance

In mythological texts, Yomotsu Hirasaka is described as a slope or pass leading to the underworld, often depicted as a dark, forbidding place. In modern fiction, it may be visualized as a boundary between the living and dead, sometimes with a boulder or gate.

Symbols & Iconography

The boulder (千引石, Chibiki no iwa) used by Izanagi to block the pass is a key symbol. In art, it may be represented as a threshold or torii gate.

Behaviour & Personality

As a location, it has no personality. In modern personifications, it might be portrayed as a guardian or a mysterious entity.

Domains & Habitats

The underworld (Yomi) and the boundary between life and death.

Version Differences

In the Kojiki, the name is 'Yomotsu Hirasaka' (黄泉比良坂), while in the Nihon Shoki it may be written differently. Modern adaptations vary widely: sometimes it is a literal place, sometimes a metaphor, and sometimes a character's name.

Powers & Abilities

Boundary between worlds

Source Version Kojiki, Nihon Shoki

Description

It serves as the threshold between the living world and Yomi, the underworld. Crossing it may have consequences.

Limitations

In mythology, crossing is possible but returning is difficult. The boulder blocks the way after Izanagi's escape.

Contradictions

Some versions suggest it is simply a place, not an entity with powers.

Stories & Myths

Izanagi's Descent to Yomi

Source Kojiki, Nihon ShokiCulture JapanesePeriod Ancient (712 CE and 720 CE)

Summary

After his wife Izanami dies, Izanagi descends to Yomi to bring her back. He finds her decayed and is chased by demons. He escapes through Yomotsu Hirasaka and blocks it with a boulder, severing the connection between the worlds.

Participants

Izanagi

Izanami

demons of Yomi

Consequences

The separation of the living and dead; Izanagi undergoes purification, leading to the birth of other deities.

Variants

The Nihon Shoki has a similar account but with some differences in details.

Death and decay

Source Kojiki, Nihon Shoki

Description

The myth graphically describes Izanami's decomposing body, which is a central element of the story.

Scholarly Context

Scholars interpret this as a metaphor for the inevitability of death and the pollution associated with it in Shinto.

Primary Evidence

Kojiki (古事記), the oldest extant chronicle in Japan, compiled by Ō no Yasumaro in 712 CE

Type textDate 712 CELocator Book 1, section on Izanagi's descent to YomiLanguage Classical Chinese with Japanese readings

Translation

The term 'Yomotsu Hirasaka' appears as the slope or pass that leads to Yomi, the land of the dead. The text describes Izanagi blocking it with a boulder after fleeing from Yomi.

Nihon Shoki (日本書紀), the second oldest chronicle, completed in 720 CE

Type textDate 720 CELocator Book 1, similar account of Izanagi's underworld journeyLanguage Classical Chinese

Translation

A parallel account of the underworld pass, though the name may be spelled differently.
