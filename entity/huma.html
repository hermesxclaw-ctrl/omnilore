Who It Is

In the shimmering heat of Persian legend, a bird of grace circles unseen above the thrones of kings — its shadow a promise of crowns, its flight an omen of fate. The Huma, more spirit than flesh, bestows sovereignty with a single touch, yet remains forever beyond mortal grasp.

The Huma is benevolent and elusive, never alighting on the ground. It is said to be compassionate, and its shadow is believed to bestow kingship upon anyone it falls upon. It is also associated with wisdom and guidance, often appearing to those destined for greatness.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Huma' is often connected to the Avestan 'Vərəθraγna' or 'Haoma', but more plausibly derives from Middle Persian 'humāy' meaning 'fortunate, auspicious'. Some scholars link it to the Avestan 'hvāthwa' or 'hū' (good) and 'mā' (mother), though this is speculative.

Pronunciation — HOO-mah (Persian: هوما)

Aliases — Homa, Humay, Humai, Hüma, Simurgh (often conflated), Anqa (Arabic counterpart)

Ownership — Persianate world, particularly Iran; also found in Turkish, Urdu, and other Islamic cultures influenced by Persian literature.

Names are cult layers, not one fixed label.

Powers
Shadow of Kingship

The shadow of the Huma falling upon a person is said to mark them as a future king or bestow good fortune.

Limit: The bird must choose the individual; it cannot be summoned or forced.

Flight Without Landing

The Huma is said to fly perpetually, never touching the ground, and is often depicted without legs.

Limit: It never rests, symbolizing constant spiritual striving.

Bestowal of Fortune

The Huma brings general good luck and prosperity to those it favors.

Limit: It is impartial and cannot be influenced by wealth or power.

Wisdom and Guidance

The Huma is a source of divine wisdom and often guides heroes or kings in their quests.

Limit: It appears only to the pure of heart.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The Huma is typically described as a large bird, often with brilliant plumage, sometimes depicted with the head of a bird and the body of a lion or with peacock-like features. It is said to be so radiant that its feathers shimmer like gold. Some accounts describe it as having no visible legs, flying eternally without perching.

Symbols — The Huma symbolizes fortune, kingship, and spiritual ascension. In Persian art, it appears on royal insignia, carpets, and miniatures. It is also a common motif in heraldry, representing nobility and power.

Domains — The Huma is believed to dwell in the heavens, often associated with the highest peaks or the realm of the sun. It is a celestial bird, not bound to earthly habitats.

Accounts I
The Huma and the ShahVarious Persian courtly texts

Medieval Islamic period

A king or prince is resting when a Huma flies overhead, and its shadow falls upon him, signifying his divine right to rule. This motif is used to legitimize dynasties and heroic figures. The individual becomes king or is confirmed in his kingship.

Accounts II
The Huma in Attar's Conference of the BirdsAttar's Mantiq al-Tayr

12th century

The Huma is among the birds that decline the journey to find the Simurgh, arguing that it already enjoys royal favor and has no need for further spiritual quest. This is used as a critique of pride and complacency. The Huma misses the chance for spiritual enlightenment.

The Huma and the PhoenixLater Persian and Turkish literature

16th-18th centuries

The Huma is sometimes equated with the Phoenix (Anqa), with stories of its self-immolation and rebirth, though this is a later conflation. Confusion in later iconography and literature.
