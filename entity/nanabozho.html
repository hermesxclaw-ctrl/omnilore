Nanabozho

Great Hare, Manabozho, The Trickster Creator

Who It Is

Nanabozho (also spelled Nanabush, Manabozho, or Wenabozho) is the central culture hero and trickster figure of the Ojibwe and related Anishinaabe peoples of the Great Lakes region. He is simultaneously a creator who helped shape the world after the great flood, a teacher who gave humanity essential knowledge and technologies, and a trickster whose foolish schemes often backfire comically. His dual nature — profound and profane, sacred and silly — makes him one of the most complex and beloved figures in Algonquian mythology.

Physically, Nanabozho is most commonly depicted as a great hare or rabbit (from which his name derives), sometimes walking on two legs, sometimes as a human with rabbit-like characteristics. In other traditions, he is more human in appearance, though always with a connection to the spirit world. He is associated with the west wind, his father being the West Wind spirit, and his grandmother (Nokomis) being the Earth. His twin brother is often a wolf or a stone spirit by whom he is sometimes accompanied or against whom he competes.

Nanabozho's most significant mythological role is as a post-flood creator. In Anishinaabe tradition, the world was destroyed by a great flood sent by the Great Spirit. Nanabozho, surviving on a raft with various animals, sent them diving one by one for a piece of earth from the bottom of the waters. Muskrat eventually succeeded, bringing up a tiny piece of mud. Nanabozho placed this mud on the back of a turtle and breathed life into it, causing it to expand into the land upon which all living things now dwell — hence the name 'Turtle Island' for North America. This flood narrative is one of the most important and widely told stories in Anishinaabe tradition.

As a culture hero, Nanabozho gave humanity essential gifts. He taught the Anishinaabe how to make fire, how to hunt and fish, how to use medicinal plants, and how to construct birchbark canoes. He wrestled with the spirit of corn and, upon defeating it, was instructed to bury its body — from which the first corn plants grew. He fought and defeated the cannibal spirit Windigo, protecting human communities from its predation. He is also the creator of the sacred Midewiwin (Grand Medicine Society), one of the most important religious institutions in Anishinaabe culture.

Yet Nanabozho is unmistakably a trickster. Countless stories tell of his greed getting him into trouble — he tries to hoard food and loses it, attempts to imitate other beings with disastrous results, and is repeatedly humiliated by his own vanity and appetite. In one story, he tries to fly with geese by tying their feathers to himself; predictably, the feathers fall off and he plummets into a lake. These humorous tales were traditionally told during winter evenings, when Nanabozho stories were permitted, and they served both as entertainment and as vehicles for transmitting cultural values.

Nanabozho continues to be a vital figure in contemporary Anishinaabe culture. His stories are told by elders, adapted by Indigenous authors (notably in the works of Gerald Vizenor and Louise Erdrich), and serve as a symbol of Anishinaabe identity and resilience.
