Who It Is

Nanabozho (also spelled Nanabush, Manabozho, or Wenabozho) is the central culture hero and trickster figure of the Ojibwe and related Anishinaabe peoples of the Great Lakes region. He is simultaneously a creator who helped shape the world after the great flood, a teacher who gave humanity essential knowledge and technologies, and a trickster whose foolish schemes often backfire comically. His dual nature — profound and profane, sacred and silly — makes him one of the most complex and beloved figures in Algonquian mythology.

Physically, Nanabozho is most commonly depicted as a great hare or rabbit (from which his name derives), sometimes walking on two legs, sometimes as a human with rabbit-like characteristics. In other traditions, he is more human in appearance, though always with a connection to the spirit world. He is associated with the west wind, his father being the West Wind spirit, and his grandmother (Nokomis) being the Earth. His twin brother is often a wolf or a stone spirit by whom he is sometimes accompanied or against whom he competes.

Nanabozho's most significant mythological role is as a post-flood creator. In Anishinaabe tradition, the world was destroyed by a great flood sent by the Great Spirit. Nanabozho, surviving on a raft with various animals, sent them diving one by one for a piece of earth from the bottom of the waters. Muskrat eventually succeeded, bringing up a tiny piece of mud. Nanabozho placed this mud on the back of a turtle and breathed life into it, causing it to expand into the land upon which all living things now dwell — hence the name 'Turtle Island' for North America. This flood narrative is one of the most important and widely told stories in Anishinaabe tradition.

As a culture hero, Nanabozho gave humanity essential gifts. He taught the Anishinaabe how to make fire, how to hunt and fish, how to use medicinal plants, and how to construct birchbark canoes. He wrestled with the spirit of corn and, upon defeating it, was instructed to bury its body — from which the first corn plants grew. He fought and defeated the cannibal spirit Windigo, protecting human communities from its predation. He is also the creator of the sacred Midewiwin (Grand Medicine Society), one of the most important religious institutions in Anishinaabe culture.

Yet Nanabozho is unmistakably a trickster. Countless stories tell of his greed getting him into trouble — he tries to hoard food and loses it, attempts to imitate other beings with disastrous results, and is repeatedly humiliated by his own vanity and appetite. In one story, he tries to fly with geese by tying their feathers to himself; predictably, the feathers fall off and he plummets into a lake. These humorous tales were traditionally told during winter evenings, when Nanabozho stories were permitted, and they served both as entertainment and as vehicles for transmitting cultural values.

Nanabozho continues to be a vital figure in contemporary Anishinaabe culture. His stories are told by elders, adapted by Indigenous authors (notably in the works of Gerald Vizenor and Louise Erdrich), and serve as a symbol of Anishinaabe identity and resilience.

Form

Nanabozho is a shape-shifter, but his primary form is that of a giant hare or rabbit, or of a human with hare-like features. In stories where he takes hare form, he is described as having long ears, powerful hind legs, and a quick, nervous energy. In human form, he often appears as a tall man, sometimes with rabbit-like ears or other markers of his dual nature. He may be depicted as a wanderer, dressed simply, carrying a medicine bag or a bow. In Ojibwe art and storytelling, his depiction varies widely: some traditions emphasize his hare form (connecting him to the Great Hare, Michabo), while others depict him as a human culture hero. In Midewiwin birch bark scrolls and ceremonial art, he may appear as a stylized rabbit or human figure with symbolic attributes related to the medicine society. In modern depictions, he is often shown in hare form — a nod to the widespread Algonquian identification of the trickster with the rabbit.

Powers

Shape-shifting Nanabozho can change his form at will, most commonly between a giant hare and a human, but also into other animals, plants, or objects as needed. He uses this ability to escape danger, to infiltrate his enemies' defenses, and — frequently — to play pranks. Pan-Anishinaabe oral tradition His transformations are not always perfect or complete. He may retain telltale signs of his true nature. He can sometimes be trapped in a form if tricked by a more powerful or clever being. The extent of his shape-shifting varies by story. Some narratives treat him as a hare who acts human; others treat him as a being who can choose any form.

Transformation of the World Nanabozho is responsible for shaping the landscape of the Great Lakes region. He created or modified rivers, lakes, islands, and rock formations. He defeated monsters — often giant creatures that threatened humanity — and transformed their bodies into useful features of the landscape. He also established customs, invented technologies, and taught the people how to live. Pan-Anishinaabe oral tradition His transformative power is not unlimited or all-at-once. He works piece by piece, encounter by encounter. Some of his transformations are accidental — the result of his misadventures rather than deliberate creation. In some traditions, Nanabozho is the primary shaper of the world. In others, he works under the authority of Gitche Manitou (the Great Spirit) or in concert with other manitous.

Superhuman Strength and Size Nanabozho is often described as a giant — in both hare and human form — with strength sufficient to move mountains, redirect rivers, and wrestle monsters. His physical prowess is one of his most straightforward 'heroic' attributes, contrasting with his cunning and trickery. Pan-Anishinaabe oral tradition His strength is not infinite. He can be overwhelmed by sufficiently powerful opponents, trapped, or injured. His physical power is less reliable than his cleverness. In some stories, Nanabozho is not particularly large or strong, relying entirely on trickery. The 'giant' characterization appears more prominently in stories of world-shaping and monster-fighting.

Master of Deception and Cunning Like all tricksters, Nanabozho's primary weapon is his intelligence. He can devise schemes of remarkable complexity, impersonate others, lie convincingly, and manipulate even powerful beings. He is a trickster whose cleverness can accomplish what strength cannot. Pan-Anishinaabe oral tradition His overconfidence is his greatest weakness. His schemes frequently collapse when he underestimates his opponent, gets greedy, or cannot resist gloating. He is out-tricked roughly as often as he succeeds. None.

Founder of the Midewiwin In Midewiwin tradition, Nanabozho founded the medicine society after receiving sacred knowledge from Gitche Manitou or from other manitous. He taught the people the ceremonies, songs, and healing practices of the Midewiwin, and established the order of the medicine lodges. This is his most significant sacred role, and it elevates him from comic trickster to serious religious founder. Midewiwin tradition, as documented by Basil Johnston and other scholars This is a one-time foundational act, not a repeatable power. Nanabozho is the founder, not the ongoing administrator, of the Midewiwin. Some Anishinaabe traditions attribute the founding of the Midewiwin to other figures or to a collection of manitous rather than to Nanabozho alone.

Behavior & Habitat

Nanabozho embodies the full spectrum of trickster behavior, from the cosmically creative to the absurdly foolish. He is a transformer who reshapes the world — creating rivers, establishing customs, defeating monsters — but he is also driven by an insatiable appetite, a boundless curiosity, and a remarkable talent for self-destruction through overconfidence. He schemes, he lies, he cheats, and he frequently gets caught — often in humiliating and scatological ways that make him the butt of his own joke. Yet he is also a culture hero who taught the Anishinaabe people essential skills: how to make fire, how to hunt, how to build canoes, and how to practice the ceremonies of the Midewiwin. He is a paradox: the wisest fool and the most foolish wise man, capable of saving the world in one story and drowning in his own excrement in the next. His stories are often told for entertainment, but they also carry moral, practical, and cosmological lessons.

Accounts

Nanabozho's birth story is one of tragedy, heroism, and monstrous origin. His mother, a human woman (sometimes named Winonah or Nokomis's daughter), was pregnant with him when she was killed by evil spirits or by a monster. Some versions say she was carried away by the West Wind, or that she was killed by jealous spirits who feared the child she carried. Nanabozho was born from her body after her death — in some versions, he emerged as a fully-formed being; in others, his grandmother Nokomis rescued him as an infant and raised him. He grew up knowing he was destined to avenge his mother and protect humanity from the monsters that threatened them. His father, in most traditions, is the West Wind or a spirit being, making Nanabozho a semi-divine figure.

The evil underwater manitous (or serpent beings) caused a great flood to destroy the world. Nanabozho, warned by the manitous or by his grandmother, built a great raft and gathered the surviving animals. As the waters rose and covered the entire world, Nanabozho sent animal after animal to dive to the bottom and bring back a piece of earth. The loon tried and failed, the otter tried and failed, the beaver tried and failed. Finally, the tiny muskrat volunteered. The other animals mocked him, but he dove deep — so deep that he nearly drowned — and surfaced dead, but with a tiny bit of mud clutched in his paw. Nanabozho placed the mud on a turtle's back and breathed on it, causing it to grow and expand until it became the entire earth — Turtle Island, as the Anishinaabe still call North America.

The Serpent People — underwater manitous who took the form of giant horned serpents — terrorized the Anishinaabe, devouring people and poisoning the waters. Nanabozho, whose mother had been killed by these or similar beings, prepared for war. He crafted weapons, purified himself through ceremony, and sought the aid of the thunder beings (thunderbirds, traditional enemies of the underwater serpents). In an epic battle, Nanabozho and the thunder beings fought the Serpent People. Nanabozho used his cunning to lure the serpents into a trap, and together with the thunderbirds, he destroyed them — or in some versions, drove them deep underground, where they remain to this day. Their chief was killed, and his bones became the rock formations visible along certain lakeshores.

Nanabozho was walking through the forest, hungry as always, when he came across some delicious-looking food growing on a bush. He ate it — and immediately felt a burning, urgent need to defecate. He squatted, and the burning substance (sometimes identified as a powerful laxative plant) caused him to produce a massive pile of excrement. Enraged at his own body, he turned around and scolded his anus: 'How dare you make such a mess! I'll teach you to behave!' He then sat on a fire to punish his anus — and in the resulting pain and confusion, he fell into his own excrement. The story ends with Nanabozho, the great trickster and culture hero, covered in his own filth, having been defeated by his own rear end. It is a quintessential trickster-tricked tale, told with great relish and crude humor.

Fire was hoarded by an old man (or a spirit being) who kept it for himself, leaving the Anishinaabe cold and hungry. Nanabozho, seeing his people suffer, decided to steal the fire. He transformed himself into a rabbit — or, in some versions, a leaf or a piece of bark — and approached the old man's lodge. Gaining the old man's trust (often by appearing as a helpless creature or a charming stranger), Nanabozho waited for his moment, seized a burning brand in his mouth or paws, and fled. The old man gave chase, but Nanabozho — sometimes with the help of other animals in a relay — carried the fire to the people. In getting the fire, however, his fur was singed or marked, which is why hares (or ravens, or whichever form he took) have the markings they do.

Field Notes

☵ Nanabozho is the reason North America is called 'Turtle Island' by many Indigenous peoples. After the great flood, he placed a bit of mud — brought up from the depths by the muskrat — on a turtle's back, and it grew into the entire continent.

☵ Longfellow's 'The Song of Hiawatha' was the best-selling long poem in American literary history — and its central character is Nanabozho, wearing the wrong name. Every single Hiawatha-themed summer camp, canoe brand, and product tie-in is built on a case of mistaken identity.

☵ In one widely told comic story, Nanabozho gets into an argument with his own body parts — his anus, his testicles, his feet — and loses. These 'body part rebellion' tales are a standard feature of trickster cycles across Native North America.

☵ The Woodland School of Anishinaabe painting, pioneered by Norval Morrisseau in the 1960s, frequently depicts Nanabozho in vivid, spiritually charged images that blend traditional symbolism with modern artistic expression.

☵ Nanabozho's grandmother Nokomis is one of the few figures from Indigenous oral tradition to have a crater on the Moon named after her — though, fittingly, it's part of the broader Hiawatha confusion, as the name was popularized by Longfellow's poem.

What We Don't Know

The relationship between pre-Christian Anishinaabe concepts of Nanabozho and the figure as documented after centuries of missionary influence is difficult to reconstruct definitively. Many Nanabozho stories remain untranslated or are considered sacred and not suitable for public dissemination. The full extent of the Midewiwin tradition's teachings about Nanabozho is not publicly accessible, as much of it is esoteric ceremonial knowledge. The relationship between Nanabozho and cognate Algonquian figures (Wisakedjak, Napi, Glooskap) has not been comprehensively mapped. The contemporary reception of Nanabozho in Anishinaabe literature and art is an ongoing and evolving field.

A way to hold it in your mind

Nanabozho is the god of 'it seemed like a good idea at the time.' He's the voice in your head that says 'I bet I could jump that' right before you break your ankle. But he's also the voice that says 'we need to try something different' when the world is ending. He's the spirit of creative risk — the willingness to look stupid, fail spectacularly, and somehow, in the process of failing, accidentally save the world. He is proof that you don't have to be wise to be important, and you don't have to be good to be useful. The most profound truths, in the Anishinaabe tradition, sometimes come from the mouth of a giant rabbit who just set his own tail on fire.
