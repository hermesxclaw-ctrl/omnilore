Form

Based on Lucian's description, Adikia is a strikingly beautiful young woman. She is 'soft and delicate' in frame, suggesting fragility or perhaps a lack of the hardiness associated with virtue. Her most distinguishing feature is her movement and gaze: she does not walk soberly or in a straight line, but sways, turns her head from side to side, and casts her eyes about restlessly. This is the body language of the seductress and the shifty, untrustworthy person. She wears a 'bright and flower-wrought robe' — a garment of vibrant color and floral decoration, symbolizing pleasure, allure, and the ephemeral beauty of the world. This is a direct contrast to the likely plain, sturdy, or even severe clothing of Dike, who is associated with order and law.

Powers

Seductive Allure Adikia's primary 'power' is her beauty and charm. She is able to make injustice appear attractive, pleasant, and desirable. She does not compel, but tempts. Her power is the power of temptation itself. Lucian, Hermotimus 82 Her allure is a deception. The path she represents leads to ruin, not to true happiness. She cannot compel a truly virtuous soul, but can only tempt it. None recorded.

Crooked Path Manifestation In Lucian's allegory, Adikia is associated with the easy, pleasant path that is actually the wrong path. She has the power to make the wrong choice seem like the right one, to make the crooked road look straight. Lucian, Hermotimus 82 Her path is ultimately a dead end or leads to a fall. The 'pleasant' life she represents is hollow and unsatisfying. None recorded.

Personification of a Concept As a personified abstraction, Adikia 'exists' wherever injustice exists. She is not a localized deity but a universal moral force, albeit a negative one. General Greek personification tradition She has no independent agency beyond the actions of humans who embody injustice. She is a symptom and a symbol, not a prime mover. Some philosophical traditions might argue that a personification of a negative concept has no real existence at all, being merely a poetic figure.

Accounts

In a satirical dialogue about philosophers, the character Lycinus recounts a vision of two paths leading to two different lives. One path is steep, rocky, and difficult, leading to Virtue (Arete). The other is smooth, easy, and pleasant, leading to the life of Pleasure (Hedone). On this pleasant path stands Adikia, described as a beautiful, soft, and delicate woman in a bright, flower-wrought robe, who sways and casts her eyes about. She represents the seductive ease of the unjust life. Lycinus uses this allegory to critique the false promises of philosophers who claim to have found an easy road to virtue.

Pausanias describes a carved scene on the famous Chest of Cypselus at Olympia. The scene shows a beautiful woman striking another woman with a staff. The inscription, according to Pausanias's reading, named the beater as 'Dike' (Justice) and the beaten woman as 'Adikia' (Injustice). This would be the earliest attestation of Adikia as a visual figure. However, the reading is not certain, and some scholars have suggested the names might have been different.

Modern Interpretations

literature The Faerie Queene (Book V, 'The Legend of Artegall') Edmund Spenser 1596 inspired Spenser's personification of 'Munera' (bribery) in the House of Talus and the general allegorical framework of Justice vs. Injustice in Book V is thematically and visually indebted to the classical personification tradition that includes Adikia. The character of 'Adicia' (a name directly derived from Adikia) appears in Book V, Canto ix, as the wife of the tyrant Grantorto, embodying blind fury and injustice. This is the most direct literary reception of Adikia in the English Renaissance. art Renaissance and Baroque Allegories of Justice and Injustice Various (e.g., Giotto, Ambrogio Lorenzetti, Hans Baldung Grien) 14th-17th centuries inspired The visual tradition of depicting Injustice as a corrupt, blindfolded, or brutal figure, often contrasted with a serene Justice, is a direct descendant of the classical personification. Lorenzetti's 'Allegory of Good and Bad Government' in Siena is a prime example, where 'Bad Government' (Tyranny) is a demonic figure, a far cry from Lucian's beautiful Adikia, but conceptually her heir. philosophy Nietzsche's critique of slave morality Friedrich Nietzsche 1887 inspired Nietzsche's argument that 'justice' is often a mask for ressentiment (the revenge of the weak) can be seen as a modern, philosophical re-evaluation of the Adikia/Dike opposition. A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Adikia as the mythological personification of 'the easy way out'. She is the voice that says 'just this once', 'everyone does it', 'you deserve it'. She is the beautiful, alluring path of rationalized selfishness that leads away from the difficult, rocky path of integrity. She is not a villain who attacks you; she is the temptation that whispers sweetly. In modern terms, she is the embodiment of every 'get rich quick' scheme, every 'one little white lie', every 'cutting corners' decision that feels good in the moment and costs you everything later. She is the 'chaotic evil' alignment, the Joker's smile, the Siren's song — not because she is loud or violent, but because she is so, so pleasant to look at.
