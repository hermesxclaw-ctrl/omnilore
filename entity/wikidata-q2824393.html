Who It Is

Imagine the most beautiful woman you have ever seen, dressed in a robe of brilliant, flower-woven silk, swaying as she walks down a path that looks smooth, easy, and utterly delightful. She is not a monster. She is not ugly. She is the Greek personification of Injustice itself — Adikia — and her only power is making the wrong road look like the only road worth taking. She is the original 'pretty face of evil', the mythological ancestor of every charming villain, seductive tempter, and 'one bad decision' you've ever encountered. She has no temples, no worshippers, and no epic poems — just one satirical description from a second-century CE wit that has haunted Western culture ever since.

Adikia is the personification of the seductive, crooked path. She is not depicted as a raging monster or a tyrant, but as a charming, alluring figure whose beauty and demeanor entice people into unjust actions. Her wandering gaze suggests a lack of focus, a disregard for the straight path of law, and a constant search for new opportunities or targets. She embodies the psychology of the wrongdoer who rationalizes their actions, finding pleasure and beauty in what is crooked. Powers
Seductive Allure

Adikia's primary 'power' is her beauty and charm. She is able to make injustice appear attractive, pleasant, and desirable. She does not compel, but tempts. Her power is the power of temptation itself.

Limit: Her allure is a deception. The path she represents leads to ruin, not to true happiness. She cannot compel a truly virtuous soul, but can only tempt it.

Crooked Path Manifestation

In Lucian's allegory, Adikia is associated with the easy, pleasant path that is actually the wrong path. She has the power to make the wrong choice seem like the right one, to make the crooked road look straight.

Limit: Her path is ultimately a dead end or leads to a fall. The 'pleasant' life she represents is hollow and unsatisfying.

Personification of a Concept

As a personified abstraction, Adikia 'exists' wherever injustice exists. She is not a localized deity but a universal moral force, albeit a negative one.

Limit: She has no independent agency beyond the actions of humans who embody injustice. She is a symptom and a symbol, not a prime mover.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Based on Lucian's description, Adikia is a strikingly beautiful young woman. She is 'soft and delicate' in frame, suggesting fragility or perhaps a lack of the hardiness associated with virtue. Her most distinguishing feature is her movement and gaze: she does not walk soberly or in a straight line, but sways, turns her head from side to side, and casts her eyes about restlessly. Accounts I
The Two Paths (Lucian's Allegory)Lucian of Samosata, Hermotimus 82

c. 165-175 CE

In a satirical dialogue about philosophers, the character Lycinus recounts a vision of two paths leading to two different lives. One path is steep, rocky, and difficult, leading to Virtue (Arete). The other is smooth, easy, and pleasant, leading to the life of Pleasure (Hedone). On this pleasant path stands Adikia, described as a beautiful, soft, and delicate woman in a bright, flower-wrought robe, who sways and casts her eyes about. She represents the seductive ease of the unjust life. Lycinus uses this allegory to critique the false promises of philosophers who claim to have found an easy road to virtue. The allegory serves as a warning to Hermotimus and the reader: the beautiful path of Adikia is a trap. It reinforces the central theme of the dialogue, which is the difficulty of attaining true philosophical wisdom and the ease with which one can be deceived by appearances.

Accounts II
The Chest of Cypselus (Possible Depiction)Pausanias, Description of Greece 5.18.2

Chest dated to c. 7th century BCE; Pausanias's description from c. 175 CE

Pausanias describes a carved scene on the famous Chest of Cypselus at Olympia. The scene shows a beautiful woman striking another woman with a staff. The inscription, according to Pausanias's reading, named the beater as 'Dike' (Justice) and the beaten woman as 'Adikia' (Injustice). This would be the earliest attestation of Adikia as a visual figure. However, the reading is not certain, and some scholars have suggested the names might have been different. If accurate, this scene establishes the iconographic trope of Justice physically punishing Injustice, a motif that would persist in Western art for millennia. It also suggests that the personification of Injustice was known in the archaic period, even if her name was not always fixed.

Adikia was a major goddess in the Greek pantheon with temples and worshippers. — There is zero evidence of any cult, temple, or ritual dedicated to Adikia. She is a personified abstraction, a literary and artistic device, not a worshipped deity. She is more like a character in a moral fable than a goddess like Athena or Hera.

Adikia was a monstrous, ugly figure. — The only detailed ancient description (Lucian) explicitly describes her as 'beautiful in appearance and very pleasant to look upon'. Her power lies precisely in her beauty and allure, not in ugliness or terror. She is the 'pretty face' of evil.

Adikia is the same as Nemesis or Adrasteia. — Nemesis is the goddess of retribution, who punishes hubris and arrogance. Adrasteia is another name for Nemesis or an inescapable force of justice. Adikia is the personification of the unjust act itself, not the punishment for it. They are opposing concepts: Adikia is the crime, Nemesis is the consequence.

Adikia is a character in a major Greek epic like the Iliad or Odyssey. — Adikia does not appear in either Homeric epic. Her single most important literary appearance is in a satirical dialogue by the second-century CE writer Lucian. She is a figure of later, more philosophically-minded literature, not the early epic tradition.
