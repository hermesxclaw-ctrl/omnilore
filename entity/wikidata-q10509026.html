The Approach

In the age when the Roman emperor Augustus proclaimed peace on earth, a king named Fróði ruled Denmark, and his realm enjoyed such tranquility that a golden ring could lie untouched on the moor of Jelling. But this golden age was bought at the price of two enslaved giantesses, Fenja and Menja, who were forced to turn a magic millstone that could grind out anything its owner desired—gold, peace, or even an army. Their song of toil and revenge would ultimately shatter the peace, drown a king, and set the sea forever turning salt.

Content advisory. Depictions of slavery, forced labor, violence, and revenge. The poem includes descriptions of harsh working conditions and the brutal overthrow of a king.
Identity

Grótti

Pronunciation //ɡroːtːi/

Known As

GrottiGrǫttiThe Mill of FróðiThe Magic MillThe Mill of the Sea

Etymology

The name 'Grótti' is derived from the Old Norse verb 'grǽða' meaning 'to grind' or 'to make something by grinding', thus 'grótti' literally means 'the grinder' or 'that which grinds'.

Cultural Ownership

Norse mythology, specifically Danish and Icelandic tradition; later part of broader Scandinavian folklore.

Disputed Identifications

There is no major dispute over the identity of Grótti itself, but its mythological function and the symbolism of the mill have been interpreted differently by scholars, ranging from a symbol of cosmic order to a representation of oppressive labor.

Description

Appearance

Grótti is described as a pair of large millstones, so massive that no ordinary man could turn them. The stones are magical, capable of grinding out whatever the user wishes. In the poem, the mill is set up in a hall, with the mill-frame and handles that the two giantesses turn.

Symbols & Iconography

The mill symbolizes abundance, prosperity, and the power to create or destroy. It is also a symbol of oppression and exploitation, as the enslaved giantesses are forced to work it. In folklore, the mill becomes the source of salt in the sea, explaining the ocean's salinity.

Behaviour & Personality

Grótti itself is an inanimate object, but it is imbued with magical properties. It responds to the will of its users, producing whatever they command, from gold and peace to salt and an army. It does not have a personality but is a tool of great power.

Domains & Habitats

Grótti is associated with the royal hall of King Fróði in Denmark (Lejre), where it is used to grind gold and peace. Later, it is taken aboard a ship by Mysing, where it grinds salt until it sinks, creating the whirlpool that turns the sea salty.

Version Differences

In the Poetic Edda, the mill is given to Fróði by Hengikjopt, and the giantesses are bought from King Fjölnir of Sweden. In the Prose Edda, the mill is also given by Hengikjopt, and the giantesses are Fenja and Menja. The poem emphasizes the giantesses' past as warriors and their revenge, while the Prose Edda provides a more detailed narrative of Fróði's peace and the mill's eventual fate. In later folklore, the mill is placed at the bottom of the sea, grinding salt forever, and the whirlpool is called the Swelkie.

Powers & Abilities

Wish-Grinding

Source Version Both Poetic Edda and Prose Edda

Description

Grótti can grind out anything the user asks for, such as gold, peace, happiness, or even an army.

Limitations

The mill requires someone strong enough to turn it; only the giantesses Fenja and Menja are able to operate it. The mill is not inherently sentient but follows the commands of its operator.

Contradictions

None noted.

Creation of Salt

Source Version Prose Edda and later folklore

Description

In the Prose Edda and folklore, when Mysing commands the mill to grind salt, it produces salt endlessly, eventually sinking the ship and creating the sea's salinity.

Limitations

The mill continues to grind until it is stopped or breaks, but in the story, it is not stopped in time.

Contradictions

None noted.

Stories & Myths

The Song of Grótti (Grottasǫngr)

Source Poetic EddaCulture Old Norse / IcelandicPeriod Medieval (13th century manuscript, but possibly older oral tradition)

Summary

King Fróði of Denmark buys two enslaved giantesses, Fenja and Menja, and forces them to grind gold and peace for him. They sing a work song, but Fróði refuses to give them rest. In their song, they reveal their past as mighty warriors and declare that they are now grinding an army to overthrow Fróði. They grind so hard that the mill breaks, and the army, led by Mysing, attacks and kills Fróði.

Participants

Fróði

Fenja

Menja

Mysing

Consequences

Fróði is killed, his kingdom is overthrown, and the mill is taken by Mysing.

Variants

The Prose Edda provides a prose version with additional details, such as the origin of the mill and the fate of the mill after Mysing takes it.

Why the Sea Is Salt

Source Norwegian Folktale (Asbjørnsen and Moe)Culture NorwegianPeriod 19th century (collected)

Summary

A poor man obtains a magic mill that grinds anything he wishes. He becomes rich, but a greedy brother steals it. The brother asks the mill to grind salt, but he does not know how to stop it. The mill grinds salt endlessly, filling the ship and sinking it. At the bottom of the sea, the mill continues to grind salt, making the sea salty.

Participants

A poor man

His greedy brother

Consequences

The mill sinks to the bottom of the sea and continues to grind salt, explaining the ocean's saltiness.

Variants

This folktale is a clear adaptation of the Grótti myth, with the mill's ability to grind anything and the disastrous result of grinding salt.

The Swelkie

Source Orcadian and Shetlandic FolkloreCulture Scottish (Orkney and Shetland)Period 19th century (collected)

Summary

In the Pentland Firth, there is a whirlpool called the Swelkie, caused by two witches named Grotti Finnie and Grotti Minnie who grind salt at the bottom of the sea, creating the whirlpool.

Participants

Grotti Finnie

Grotti Minnie

Consequences

The whirlpool is a constant hazard for sailors.

Variants

This is a direct folkloric continuation of the Grótti myth, with the giantesses replaced by witches and the mill still grinding salt.

Relationships

King Fróði

Type owner/wielderSource Grottasǫngr, Prose Edda

Description

Fróði acquires the mill and uses it to grind gold, peace, and happiness for his kingdom. He is ultimately killed because of the mill's power, as the giantesses grind an army that destroys him.

Version Note

In the poem, Fróði is the king who buys the giantesses; in the Prose Edda, he is described as a great king who brought peace to the land.

Conflicting Accounts

None noted.

Fenja and Menja

Type enslaved operatorsSource Grottasǫngr, Prose Edda

Description

The two giantesses are forced to turn the mill. They are the ones who sing the song and ultimately grind out the army that destroys Fróði.

Version Note

In the poem, they are described as strong and mighty, descendants of mountain giants; in the Prose Edda, they are called gýgjar (female giants).

Conflicting Accounts

None noted.

Dark History
Content advisory. Slavery, forced labor, violence, murder, and destruction.
Warning: Slavery, forced labor, violence, murder, and destruction. —

Enslavement of Fenja and Menja

Source Grottasǫngr, Prose Edda

Description

The giantesses are bought as slaves and forced to work the mill without rest, showing the brutal treatment of enslaved people in the mythological context.

Scholarly Context

The poem reflects the realities of slavery in the Norse world, where captives could be forced into hard labor.

Violent overthrow of Fróði

Source Grottasǫngr

Description

The giantesses grind an army that attacks and kills Fróði, leading to the end of his peace. The poem describes the army burning the settlement and overthrowing his throne.

Scholarly Context

The story may reflect historical conflicts and the fragility of peace.

Destruction caused by the mill

Source Prose Edda

Description

In the Prose Edda, Mysing's ship sinks due to the endless grinding of salt, creating a whirlpool that endangers sailors.

Scholarly Context

This serves as an etiological myth for the saltiness of the sea and the dangers of the maelstrom.

Contradictions & Disputes

The fate of the mill after sinking

Position A

The mill continues to grind salt at the bottom of the sea, creating the sea's saltiness.

Position A — evidence

Prose Edda and folklore stories say the mill is still grinding.

Position B

The mill might have been broken or stopped.

Position B — evidence

No direct evidence for this; it is a logical inference.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars accept the etiological explanation that the mill is still grinding salt.

The identity of the giantesses

Position A

Fenja and Menja are giantesses (gýgjar).

Position A — evidence

The Prose Edda calls them gýgjar; the poem describes them as descendants of mountain-risar.

Position B

They might be valkyries or other supernatural beings.

Position B — evidence

Their warrior past and ability to grind an army could suggest valkyrie-like attributes.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars accept them as giantesses, but their warrior aspect is notable.
