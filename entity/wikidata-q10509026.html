Who It Is

In the age when the Roman emperor Augustus proclaimed peace on earth, a king named Fróði ruled Denmark, and his realm enjoyed such tranquility that a golden ring could lie untouched on the moor of Jelling. But this golden age was bought at the price of two enslaved giantesses, Fenja and Menja, who were forced to turn a magic millstone that could grind out anything its owner desired—gold, peace, or even an army. Their song of toil and revenge would ultimately shatter the peace, drown a king, and set the sea forever turning salt.

Grótti itself is an inanimate object, but it is imbued with magical properties. It responds to the will of its users, producing whatever they command, from gold and peace to salt and an army. It does not have a personality but is a tool of great power.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Grótti' is derived from the Old Norse verb 'grǽða' meaning 'to grind' or 'to make something by grinding', thus 'grótti' literally means 'the grinder' or 'that which grinds'.

Pronunciation — //ɡroːtːi/

Aliases — Grotti, Grǫtti, The Mill of Fróði, The Magic Mill, The Mill of the Sea

Ownership — Norse mythology, specifically Danish and Icelandic tradition; later part of broader Scandinavian folklore.

Powers
Wish-Grinding

Grótti can grind out anything the user asks for, such as gold, peace, happiness, or even an army.

Limit: The mill requires someone strong enough to turn it; only the giantesses Fenja and Menja are able to operate it. The mill is not inherently sentient but follows the commands of its operator.

Creation of Salt

In the Prose Edda and folklore, when Mysing commands the mill to grind salt, it produces salt endlessly, eventually sinking the ship and creating the sea's salinity.

Limit: The mill continues to grind until it is stopped or breaks, but in the story, it is not stopped in time.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Grótti is described as a pair of large millstones, so massive that no ordinary man could turn them. The stones are magical, capable of grinding out whatever the user wishes. In the poem, the mill is set up in a hall, with the mill-frame and handles that the two giantesses turn.

Symbols — The mill symbolizes abundance, prosperity, and the power to create or destroy. It is also a symbol of oppression and exploitation, as the enslaved giantesses are forced to work it. In folklore, the mill becomes the source of salt in the sea, explaining the ocean's salinity.

Domains — Grótti is associated with the royal hall of King Fróði in Denmark (Lejre), where it is used to grind gold and peace. Later, it is taken aboard a ship by Mysing, where it grinds salt until it sinks, creating the whirlpool that turns the sea salty.

Accounts I
The Song of Grótti (Grottasǫngr)Poetic Edda

Medieval (13th century manuscript, but possibly older oral tradition)

King Fróði of Denmark buys two enslaved giantesses, Fenja and Menja, and forces them to grind gold and peace for him. They sing a work song, but Fróði refuses to give them rest. In their song, they reveal their past as mighty warriors and declare that they are now grinding an army to overthrow Fróði. They grind so hard that the mill breaks, and the army, led by Mysing, attacks and kills Fróði. Fróði is killed, his kingdom is overthrown, and the mill is taken by Mysing.

Accounts II
Why the Sea Is SaltNorwegian Folktale (Asbjørnsen and Moe)

19th century (collected)

A poor man obtains a magic mill that grinds anything he wishes. He becomes rich, but a greedy brother steals it. The brother asks the mill to grind salt, but he does not know how to stop it. The mill grinds salt endlessly, filling the ship and sinking it. At the bottom of the sea, the mill continues to grind salt, making the sea salty. The mill sinks to the bottom of the sea and continues to grind salt, explaining the ocean's saltiness.

The SwelkieOrcadian and Shetlandic Folklore

19th century (collected)

In the Pentland Firth, there is a whirlpool called the Swelkie, caused by two witches named Grotti Finnie and Grotti Minnie who grind salt at the bottom of the sea, creating the whirlpool. The whirlpool is a constant hazard for sailors.
