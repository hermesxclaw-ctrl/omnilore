Etymology

The term 'Luus' derives from the Tibetan 'klu' (gLu), which itself comes from the Sanskrit 'nāga'. In Mongolian, it refers to a class of serpentine spirits associated with water and underground realms. The spelling varies: 'Luus' is common in academic transliteration, while 'Lus' appears in some sources.

Cultural Ownership

Mongolian, Buryat, and other Central Asian cultures influenced by Tibetan Buddhism. The Luus are integrated into Mongolian shamanic and Buddhist practices.

Disputed Identifications

The Luus are often conflated with the Tibetan klu and Indian nāga. While they share similarities, the Mongolian Luus have distinct characteristics, such as their association with specific local landscapes and their role in Mongolian folk religion. Some scholars debate whether the Luus are indigenous or introduced via Buddhism.

Archive Dossier — Approach

In the windswept steppes of Mongolia, where the sky meets the earth in an unbroken line, the Luus—serpentine dragons of water and earth—rule the unseen currents beneath the land. They are the guardians of springs, rivers, and mountains, capricious spirits whose favor can bring abundance or ruin. To the herders who share their domain, the Luus are not distant deities but immediate presences, acknowledged in daily rituals and offerings. Their world is one of shifting alliances, where a single misstep can turn a life-giving stream into a source of sickness and misfortune.

Every spring is the home of a Lus, every river the body of a dragon king. Do not pollute the water, do not break the earth without asking — the Lus remember.

Who It Is

Lus (singular and plural; also known as Luus) are a class of powerful water and earth spirits in Mongolian shamanic cosmology, closely related to the Tibetan Buddhist nagas but with distinct indigenous features. They dwell in lakes, rivers, springs, wells, and underground caves, and are the guardians of the water sources that sustain life on the steppe. The Lus are organized under a hierarchy of dragon-kings (Lus Khans), each ruling a specific body of water or region. In Mongolian belief, the Lus must be treated with extreme respect — polluting a spring, washing in a river at inappropriate times, or building near a sacred well can offend them. Offended Lus cause illness (particularly skin diseases and tuberculosis), drought, and the failure of wells. In Mongolian Buddhist practice, lamas perform rituals (Lus Sang) to appease the Lus, offering milk, rice, incense, and precious objects. The offering of 'lus bum' (vases filled with sacred substances) buried at water sources is a common practice. The Lus were believed to be the original inhabitants of the land before humans arrived, and their rights must be respected. The cult of the Lus is one of the most persistent elements of Mongolian folk religion — even urban Mongolians today will seek a lama's help if they suspect a Lus has been disturbed. The Lus represent the interface between human civilization and the natural world, demanding careful, reverent stewardship of water resources.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

The Luus are typically described as serpentine dragons, often with a snake-like body, sometimes with multiple heads or horns. They are associated with water, so they may appear as snakes, fish, or amphibious creatures. In some accounts, they can take human form, often as beautiful or majestic beings. They are said to be white or blue in color, symbolizing water and sky.

Symbols & Iconography

In Mongolian and Tibetan art, Luus are depicted as coiled serpents or dragons, often beneath bodies of water or in the earth. They are associated with the color white and blue, and with jewels (nāga pearls) that symbolize wealth and wisdom. Ritual objects include offerings of milk, tea, and incense.

Behaviour & Personality

Luus are capricious and easily offended. They are protectors of their territory, and any pollution of water or disrespect to sacred places can provoke their wrath. They are believed to control weather, especially rain, and can cause droughts or floods. They are also associated with fertility and wealth, and can grant blessings to those who honor them.

Domains & Habitats

Luus dwell in rivers, lakes, springs, wells, and underground watercourses. They also inhabit mountains and caves, particularly those with water sources. In Mongolian cosmology, they are part of the lower world, but their influence reaches the surface.

Version Differences

In Buryat tradition, the Lus (or Lusan) are similar but may have different names and specific local variations. Some sources distinguish between water-dwelling and earth-dwelling Luus. In shamanic practice, they are often considered subordinate to the sky deity Tengri, but they are also independent agents.

Archive Dossier — Powers

Weather Control

Source Version General Mongolian and Tibetan belief

Description

Luus can cause rain, hail, storms, and droughts. They are often invoked in rituals for rain.

Limitations

Their power is limited to their domain; they cannot affect areas outside their jurisdiction.

Contradictions

Some sources attribute all weather to Tengri, while others give Luus a role.

Disease and Healing

Source Version Mongolian folk medicine and shamanic practice

Description

Luus can inflict diseases, especially skin and internal ailments, when offended. They can also cure if appeased.

Limitations

Their healing often requires ritual appeasement.

Contradictions

Diseases are also attributed to other spirits or demons.

Shape-shifting

Source Version Folktales

Description

Luus can take human form, often to interact with humans, sometimes seducing or testing them.

Limitations

They may be bound by rules, such as not being able to hide their true nature from certain people.

Contradictions

Not all accounts mention shape-shifting.

Bestowing Wealth and Fertility

Source Version Mongolian belief

Description

Luus can grant prosperity, children, and good fortune to those who respect them.

Limitations

Requires proper offerings and behavior.

Contradictions

Wealth is also attributed to other deities.

Tsagaan Ubgen

Type associatedSource Mongolian folklore

Description

Tsagaan Ubgen (White Old Man) is a spirit of longevity and the earth, often associated with mountains and waters. He may be seen as a master of animals and sometimes linked to the Luus.

Version Note

Both are nature spirits, but their relationship is not clearly defined.

Conflicting Accounts

Some see them as separate, others as aspects of the same earth spirit.
