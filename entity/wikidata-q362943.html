Form

No direct visual depiction of Artaius survives. By analogy with Gallo-Roman interpretatio romana, he was likely depicted in the anthropomorphic style of Mercury — a mature, bearded or youthful male figure — possibly with bear attributes such as a bearskin cloak or a bear companion. This reconstruction is hypothetical, based on the common practice of equating native deities with Roman counterparts while retaining native iconographic elements.

Powers

Bear-strength and ferocity Inferred from the bear etymology, Artaius likely embodied the raw power, ferocity, and protective instincts of the bear — a creature of immense physical strength and terrifying defense of its young. Reconstructed from name evidence and Celtic bear-symbolism Not directly attested; a reasonable inference from comparative Celtic bear-cults (Artio, Bear-Goddess of Bern) None direct; the inference is scholarly, not epigraphic

Mercury's powers (via syncretism) As Artaius Mercurius, he would have been invoked for safe travel, successful commerce, eloquence, and protection at boundaries and crossroads. Attested via the Latinized name 'Mercurius' in votive inscriptions The Roman Mercury's powers are well-documented; the degree to which they were transferred to Artaius is unknown The syncretism may have been shallow — a Roman veneer over a native cult with different functions

Kingship and sovereignty The bear was a royal animal in Celtic tradition (cf. the bear-king Arthur, the bear as emblem of the Uí Néill dynasty in Ireland). Artaius may have been invoked as a guarantor of royal authority and territorial sovereignty. Reconstructed from comparative Celtic royal symbolism Highly speculative; no direct evidence connects Artaius to kingship The Roman equation with Mercury suggests a more 'civic' function; the royal-bear aspect may be a later or parallel development

Accounts

No narrative myths of Artaius survive. The cult existed in the territory of the Helvetii, a Celtic people who had been forcibly resettled by the Romans after their disastrous migration attempt of 58 BCE. The bear-god may have represented a native counterpoint to Roman authority — a wild, untamable power that the Romans sought to domesticate by equating him with their own Mercury. The votive altars suggest a personal, devotional cult rather than a state-sponsored one.

The name 'Arthur' is traditionally derived from the same Proto-Celtic root *arto- ('bear'), and some scholars have proposed a direct line of descent from a bear-god like Artaius to the legendary king. However, the etymology is sound while the mythic continuity is not — Arthur's name may simply mean 'Bear-Man', a heroic epithet, without implying a direct cultic descent from Artaius. Nevertheless, the bear-king archetype — a ruler who embodies the strength and ferocity of the bear — resonates across Celtic cultures.

Scholarly Debates
Artaius as a bear-god
Artaius is a bear-deity, as his name derives from *arto- ('bear'). ⇌ The name may be a personal name or toponym, not a divine epithet; the 'bear' meaning is not conclusive.
Evidence for A: Etymology; parallel bear-deities (Artio) in the same region.
Evidence for B: The inscriptions are formulaic and give no iconographic or mythological detail.
Connection to Arthur
Arthur's name derives from the same root, implying a continuous mythic tradition. ⇌ The etymology is coincidental; Arthur is a medieval literary figure with no direct cultic ancestry.
Evidence for A: Etymology; bear-king archetype in Celtic tradition.
Evidence for B: No medieval Welsh source connects Arthur to a bear-god; the name may be a Latinized form of a different name.
Artaius and Artio as a pair
Artaius and Artio are a divine pair (god and goddess of bears). ⇌ They are separate, unrelated deities or regional variants of the same concept.
Evidence for A: Proximity of attestation (Helvetian territory); parallel names.
Evidence for B: No inscription links them; they may be independent manifestations.
What We Don't Know

Only two inscriptions attest to Artaius, and both are formulaic votive texts. No iconography, mythology, or native-language evidence survives. The relationship with Artio is speculative. The Arthurian connection is etymological only. Modern reception is limited to scholarly reference and niche reconstructionist practice. Research is limited by the paucity of evidence.

A way to hold it in your mind

Artaius is a whisper, not a shout — a god known from two stones, a name, and a root. Think of him as a ghost in the archives: a bear-shaped shadow at the edge of Roman Switzerland, a native power half-erased by empire but stubbornly present in the etymology of a legend. He's the 'lost episode' of Celtic mythology — a tantalizing fragment that hints at a richer, wilder story we'll never fully recover.
