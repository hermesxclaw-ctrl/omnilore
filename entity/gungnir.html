Who It Is

Before the hammer there was the spear. Norse tradition gives Odin a weapon that fits in a single sentence — a spear called Gungnir that never came to rest in its throw — and then refuses to describe it: no length, no metal, no carving. What survives instead is a trail of moments. A spear flies over an enemy host and the first war in the world begins. A reed that only looks harmless turns into a spear in a king's chest, and a voice says: now I give you to Odin. A god hangs nine nights on the windy tree, wounded with a spear, given to himself. And on the last day, Odin rides out first — golden helm, fair byrnie, and his spear, called Gungnir — straight at the wolf that will swallow him. Gungnir is not a described object; it is a repeated gesture. The Allfather's arm extends, something sharp leaves his hand, and it is already decided.

Attested behavior is minimal and perfect: Gungnir never came to rest in its throw (Skaldskaparmal). It is the god's instrument of initiation — the weapon that opens wars (Voluspa 24) and closes them (Gylfaginning 51) — and of dedication, since Odin's spear is what marks a victim or a host as his (Gautreks saga; the ritual reading of Voluspa 24). In the kenning-tradition it is simply 'what Hroptr goes with'. Personality is not the spear's; the spear is the god's gesture made solid: unreasoning, unstoppable, already decided.

Powers
Never misses its target

Skaldskaparmal states the property in one clause: 'at geirrinn nam aldri stadar i lagi' — the spear never came to rest in its thrust, i.e. it always struck home. The wager-scene presents this as Loki's selling-point when he hands the spear to Odin before the assembled gods.

Limit: No counter-example or failure is recorded anywhere in the corpus; equally, the property is asserted, not demonstrated — no Eddic scene shows Gungnir being thrown at a named target and hitting. It is a guarantee without a test.

Rune-carried point

Sigrdrifumal 17 lists Gungnir's point among the surfaces on which runes were carved before the runes were shaved off, dissolved into the sacred mead, and dispersed through the worlds. The spear is thus a rune-bearing object in the oldest surviving magical catalogue of the Poetic Edda.

Limit: The stanza does not say which runes, what they did, or that they caused the spear's accuracy. The causal link is a modern inference.

Spear of dedication and sacrifice

Voluspa 24 has Odin cast a spear over the Vanir host to open the first war — the gesture scholars read as consecrating the enemy to the god. In Gautreks saga, Odin's gift-spear (a 'reed' that becomes a spear) dedicates King Vikarr: 'Nu gef ek thik Odni' — 'Now I give you to Odin.' Havamal 138 binds the same formula to Odin's own body: wounded with a spear, given to Odin, himself to himself.

Limit: None of these passages names Gungnir. The connection is contextual and widely accepted in scholarship, but it is a reconstruction, not a quotation.

Odinforce conduit and Asgardian royal sceptre (Marvel)

In Marvel Comics, Gungnir is Odin's uru-forged spear, 'The Spear of Heaven', able to channel the Odinforce and match Mjolnir in battle without it. In the Marvel Cinematic Universe it functions as the sceptre of Asgard's throne.

Limit: Adaptation-only powers; nothing in the Norse corpus supports uru, the Odinforce, or throne-sceptre use.

Piece of Eden (Assassin's Creed)

In Assassin's Creed Valhalla, Gungnir is a Spear of Eden — one of the Isu 'Pieces of Eden' — with force-field and reach powers, created by Brokkr and Sindri, wielded by Odin, lost after Ragnarok, and recovered by Eivor in the Goinnhellir cavern in 877 CE.

Limit: Adaptation-only.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No surviving source describes what Gungnir looked like. The Eddas call it a geirr — a spear — and stop there: no length, no metal, no carving, no glow. That silence is itself a fact worth honoring: Gungnir is the only major Norse divine weapon with no described appearance at all, beside Mjolnir's famously short handle and Freyr's sword, which is described by its absence. Symbols — The spear is Odin's signature attribute in art as in verse: the one-eyed god with a spear, ravens, and wolves. Domains — Asgard and Odin's hand; battlefields and the threshold of war; Valhalla's hall (per the G 181 reading); and the last battlefield of all, where Odin rides out with Gungnir against Fenrir. In Assassin's Creed Valhalla (adaptation) it sleeps in the Goinnhellir cavern near Yggdrasil's chamber until Eivor retrieves it. It has no shrine, cult, or dwelling of its own: Gungnir is a royal possession, not a place.

Accounts I
Loki's wager and the forging of the treasuresProse Edda, Skaldskaparmal (the Sons-of-Ivaldi section)

Written c. 1220; story older in oral tradition

Loki, for pure mischief, shaves off all of Sif's hair. Thor catches him and is about to break every bone in his body when Loki swears he will have the dark-elf dwarves make replacement hair of gold that grows like real hair. He goes to the dwarves called the Sons of Ivaldi, and they produce three treasures: the golden hair, the ship Skidbladnir, and the spear that Odin owned, which is called Gungnir. Puffed up, Loki then wagers his own head with the dwarf Brokkr that Brokkr's brother Sindri cannot make three treasures as good. Sindri works the forge while Brokkr pumps the bellows; a fly — unnamed in the text, though later retellings whisper it is Loki — bites Brokkr on the hand, then the neck, then between the eyes until blood blinds him, and the last piece is pulled from the fire slightly flawed: Mjolnir, whose handle came out short. The gods sit in judgment — Odin, Thor, and Freyr — and Loki presents his gifts: the spear to Odin, the hair to Thor for Sif, the ship to Freyr. The spear-cast that opened the first warPoetic Edda, Voluspa 24

Poem recorded c. 1270; content traditionally older

After the threefold burning of Gullveig/Heid, the gods take counsel, and then: 'Odin hurled [his spear] and shot into the host; that was yet the first war in the world.' The Aesir's shield-wall breaks and the Vanir — dangerous with war-magic — tread the field. The stanza does not name the spear, but the gesture is the spear-cast of dedication: the enemy host is given to Odin before a blade is crossed. The Aesir–Vanir war, ended by hostage-exchange and the famous truce that some scholars read as the origin of the shared cult.

Ragnarok: the spear against the wolfProse Edda, Gylfaginning 51

c. 1220

When Heimdall's horn sounds, Odin rides first onto the field — golden helm, fair byrnie, and his spear, which is called Gungnir — and steers straight at the Fenris-wolf. Thor fights the Midgard Serpent beside him and cannot help; Freyr falls to Surtr for want of his given-away sword; and the wolf swallows Odin. Gungnir's last attested moment in the mythology is being carried by its master into the fight he loses. Odin dies; Vidarr avenges him by tearing the wolf's jaws apart. The spear itself vanishes from the record — no source says what becomes of Gungnir after Ragnarok.

Accounts II
Runes on the spear's pointPoetic Edda, Sigrdrifumal 17

Codex Regius, c. 1270

The valkyrie Sigrdrifa teaches Sigurd the lore of runes and where they were carved: on shields and sword-blades, on the wolf's claw and the eagle's beak, on glass and gold — and on Gungnir's point and Grani's breast, on the Norn's nail and the owl's beak. The runes were then shaved off, stirred into the holy mead, and sent out across the worlds: some with the Aesir, some with the elves, some with the wise Vanir, some with mortal men. Gungnir is situated inside the magical economy of the runes: an object whose point once carried writing of power, alongside the Norns' own nail.

The reed that became a spear: the death of King VikarrGautreks saga, ch. 7; parallel in Saxo Grammaticus, Gesta Danorum

Saga c. 13th–14th century, built around older verse

Odin, disguised as the one-eyed Hrosshars-Grani, owes Starkadr a favor and demands King Vikarr as payment. When a mock-sacrifice is staged to lift a bad omen, Starkadr works the fake noose and the fake branch — and the spear Odin pressed into his hand, 'which would seem a reed-stalk', turns real: 'Now I give you to Odin,' Starkadr says, and the reed pierces the king through. The calf-gut noose hardens to a strong withe, the branch springs up and hangs the king, and the place is called Vikarshólmar ever after. The story is the clearest narrative of what Odin's spear means: a gift that looks like nothing, a death that looks like a game, and a king given away to the god. Vikarr dies a sacrificial death — hanged and spear-wounded, the same double death Odin describes himself suffering in Havamal 138. Starkadr is cursed with the deed and driven from Hordaland.

Odin's own hangingPoetic Edda, Havamal 138–139

Codex Regius, c. 1270

The High One speaks: he hung on the windy tree nine whole nights, wounded with a spear, given to Odin, himself given to himself; no bread was offered him, no horn of drink; he looked down, caught up the runes, screaming, and fell back. The spear is unnamed, but the stanza is the hinge of the whole Gungnir tradition: the god's own body is the sacrifice, and a spear is the instrument — the same instrument, by strong implication, that dedicated Vikarr and the Vanir host. Odin wins the runes and their wisdom; the nine-night ordeal becomes the model of his sacrificial kingship.

Brokkr and Eitri (Sindri) forged Gungnir. — Skaldskaparmal is explicit: the Sons of Ivaldi made Gungnir, Sif's hair, and Skidbladnir. Brokkr and Sindri forged the rival set — Draupnir, Gullinbursti, Mjolnir — in a contest Loki lost. Assassin's Creed Valhalla canonizes the wrong attribution.

Odin hung himself on Yggdrasil pierced by Gungnir. — Havamal 138 says 'wounded with a spear' and names no spear. The identification is inference, repeated so often it now reads as fact.

Gungnir returns to Odin's hand like Mjolnir. — The return-to-hand property is attested only for Mjolnir. Gungnir's attested property is that it never stops short in its throw. No source has it flying back.

The runes carved on Gungnir's point are what make it never miss. — Sigrdrifumal 17 attests runes on the point; Skaldskaparmal attests the never-miss property. No text connects the two causally.

Oaths were sworn on Gungnir. — No primary source attests oath-swearing on Gungnir. The attested Norse oath-object is the ring; the wager story involves a head-wager, not a spear-oath. (The OmniLore tasking notes carried this claim; research found no textual support.)

Gungnir is made of uru / is a Piece of Eden / channels the Odinforce. — All three are adaptation-specific: Marvel's uru-and-Odinforce spear, Assassin's Creed's Isu artifact. The Norse corpus says nothing of materials.

Wagner's Wotan carries Gungnir. — Wagner's spear is an unnamed treaty-spear of world-ash; the name Gungnir appears nowhere in the Ring.
