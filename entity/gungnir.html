Who It Is

Gungnir is the spear of Odin in Norse mythology: forged by the dwarf-smiths called the Sons of Ivaldi at Loki's commission, given to Odin before the assembled gods, and guaranteed — in the only description the Eddas bother to give it — never to come to rest in its throw. It is the weapon of the god's decisions: the spear-cast that opens the first war in the world (Voluspa 24, unnamed), the spear Odin carries first onto the field at Ragnarok against the Fenris-wolf (Gylfaginning 51), the spear whose point once carried runes (Sigrdrifumal 17), and the model for the sacrificial spear that dedicates King Vikarr to Odin and wounds the god himself on the world-tree (Gautreks saga; Havamal 138, both unnamed). The name already appears in 10th-century skaldic verse. No source describes its appearance. Modern reception is enormous: Marvel's uru-and-Odinforce spear, the MCU films, Wagner's unnamed treaty-spear, a PSP tactical RPG, Assassin's Creed Valhalla's 'Spear of Eden', Fire Emblem's holy lance, and the Saab RBS 15 anti-ship missile that carries the name into the 21st century.

Gungnir occupies a strange place among the great weapons of myth: it is the most famous spear in the northern tradition and almost entirely undescribed. The Eddas tell where it came from — the workshop of the Sons of Ivaldi, alongside Sif's golden hair and the ship Skidbladnir — and what it does — it never comes to rest in its throw — and then fall silent. There is no story of Gungnir being used in single combat, no scene of it missing or striking, no description of its shaft or head. What the sources give instead is context: the spear as the extension of Odin's will, the instrument of dedication and sacrifice, the token of the god who gives away kings and armies and himself. Scholarship and popular culture have both been busy filling the silence. The Eddas' unnamed spears (Voluspa 24, Havamal 138, Gautreks saga) are routinely identified as Gungnir; Marvel forged it of uru and wired it to the Odinforce; video games have made it everything from a demon-summoning lance to an Isu Piece of Eden. The honest core remains the medieval one: a spear that never stops short, in the hand of a god who never turns back.

Provenance

In Skaldskaparmal (the Prose Edda), Loki cuts off all of Sif's hair for mischief. Thor seizes him and is about to break every bone in his body when Loki swears he will have the dwarves make replacement hair of gold. He goes to the dwarves called the Sons of Ivaldi, who produce three treasures: the golden hair, the ship Skidbladnir, and 'the spear that Odin owned, which is called Gungnir'. Loki then wagers his own head with the dwarf Brokkr that Brokkr's brother Sindri cannot make three treasures as good. Sindri forges Gullinbursti, Draupnir, and Mjolnir while a fly — unnamed in the text — torments Brokkr at the bellows, and Mjolnir's handle comes out short. The gods Odin, Thor, and Freyr judge the treasures; Loki gives Gungnir to Odin and explains that it never came to rest in its thrust. The hammer is judged the greatest treasure, Loki loses his head-wager, escapes beheading by arguing the wager covered only his head and not his neck, and Brokkr sews his lips shut with an awl — the strap remembered as Vartari.

Form

Unattested. No medieval source describes Gungnir's length, metal, or decoration; the honest baseline is an ordinary Viking-Age spear — iron head on an ash shaft. The one textual hint of marking is Sigrdrifumal 17, where runes are said to have been carved on its point. Modern depictions (golden, rune-scored, glowing, sceptre-like in the Marvel films) are inventions.

Powers

Never misses its target Skaldskaparmal states the property in one clause: 'at geirrinn nam aldri stadar i lagi' — the spear never came to rest in its thrust, i.e. it always struck home. The wager-scene presents this as Loki's selling-point when he hands the spear to Odin before the assembled gods. Prose Edda, Skaldskaparmal, the Sons-of-Ivaldi section (attested). No counter-example or failure is recorded anywhere in the corpus; equally, the property is asserted, not demonstrated — no Eddic scene shows Gungnir being thrown at a named target and hitting. It is a guarantee without a test. Modern summaries phrase it as 'always hits regardless of the attacker's skill' (the Wikipedia summary's wording). That is a fair paraphrase of 'never stops short', but the Old Norse does not actually say anything about the thrower's skill — the spear is described as perfect, not as a skill-canceller.

Rune-carried point Sigrdrifumal 17 lists Gungnir's point among the surfaces on which runes were carved before the runes were shaved off, dissolved into the sacred mead, and dispersed through the worlds. The spear is thus a rune-bearing object in the oldest surviving magical catalogue of the Poetic Edda. Poetic Edda, Sigrdrifumal 17 (attested). The stanza does not say which runes, what they did, or that they caused the spear's accuracy. The causal link is a modern inference. Popular retellings routinely fuse the two passages into 'runes make Gungnir never miss'. No medieval text says this.

Spear of dedication and sacrifice Voluspa 24 has Odin cast a spear over the Vanir host to open the first war — the gesture scholars read as consecrating the enemy to the god. In Gautreks saga, Odin's gift-spear (a 'reed' that becomes a spear) dedicates King Vikarr: 'Nu gef ek thik Odni' — 'Now I give you to Odin.' Havamal 138 binds the same formula to Odin's own body: wounded with a spear, given to Odin, himself to himself. Voluspa 24; Gautreks saga ch. 7; Havamal 138 (all attested; the spear is named only in none of the three — see contradictions). None of these passages names Gungnir. The connection is contextual and widely accepted in scholarship, but it is a reconstruction, not a quotation. Whether the unnamed spears of Voluspa, Havamal, and Gautreks saga are 'the same spear' is a matter of interpretation; some scholars treat the spear-cast as a ritual motif that outlives any single weapon.

Odinforce conduit and Asgardian royal sceptre (Marvel) In Marvel Comics, Gungnir is Odin's uru-forged spear, 'The Spear of Heaven', able to channel the Odinforce and match Mjolnir in battle without it. In the Marvel Cinematic Universe it functions as the sceptre of Asgard's throne. Marvel Comics (Thor line, 1960s–present); MCU films Thor (2011), Thor: The Dark World (2013), Thor: Ragnarok (2017). Adaptation-specific. Adaptation-only powers; nothing in the Norse corpus supports uru, the Odinforce, or throne-sceptre use. Piece of Eden (Assassin's Creed) In Assassin's Creed Valhalla, Gungnir is a Spear of Eden — one of the Isu 'Pieces of Eden' — with force-field and reach powers, created by Brokkr and Sindri, wielded by Odin, lost after Ragnarok, and recovered by Eivor in the Goinnhellir cavern in 877 CE. Behavior & Habitat

As an artifact, Gungnir is the god's gesture made solid: unreasoning, unstoppable, already decided. It is never depicted as having a will of its own, a voice, or a temper — unlike Mjolnir, which returns to the hand, Gungnir is simply perfect and silent.

Weaknesses & Wards

None attested. The spear has no recorded failure, no counter, and no flaw — the myth gives it none, and equally gives it no feats: its perfection is asserted, never demonstrated. At Ragnarok, its master is killed despite it (Gylfaginning 51), which is the closest the corpus comes to a limitation.

Accounts

Loki, for pure mischief, shaves off all of Sif's hair. Thor catches him and is about to break every bone in his body when Loki swears he will have the dark-elf dwarves make replacement hair of gold that grows like real hair. He goes to the dwarves called the Sons of Ivaldi, and they produce three treasures: the golden hair, the ship Skidbladnir, and the spear that Odin owned, which is called Gungnir. Puffed up, Loki then wagers his own head with the dwarf Brokkr that Brokkr's brother Sindri cannot make three treasures as good. Sindri works the forge while Brokkr pumps the bellows; a fly — unnamed in the text, though later retellings whisper it is Loki — bites Brokkr on the hand, then the neck, then between the eyes until blood blinds him, and the last piece is pulled from the fire slightly flawed: Mjolnir, whose handle came out short. The gods sit in judgment — Odin, Thor, and Freyr — and Loki presents his gifts: the spear to Odin, the hair to Thor for Sif, the ship to Freyr. Of the spear he says only that it never came to rest in its thrust. Brokkr presents Draupnir (eight rings drip from it every ninth night), Gullinbursti (the golden boar that runs through air and water and lights the dark), and Mjolnir (which never misses, returns to the hand, and can shrink to pocket size). The verdict: the hammer is best, the greatest defence against the frost-giants, and the dwarf has won the wager. Loki offers to buy back his head; Brokkr refuses. Loki flees on shoes that run through air and water; Thor catches him. Brokkr moves to cut, and Loki points out that the wager covered his head but not his neck. The dwarf settles for piercing Loki's lips and sewing them shut with an awl — the strap is remembered by name: Vartari.

After the threefold burning of Gullveig/Heid, the gods take counsel, and then: 'Odin hurled [his spear] and shot into the host; that was yet the first war in the world.' The Aesir's shield-wall breaks and the Vanir — dangerous with war-magic — tread the field. The stanza does not name the spear, but the gesture is the spear-cast of dedication: the enemy host is given to Odin before a blade is crossed.

When Heimdall's horn sounds, Odin rides first onto the field — golden helm, fair byrnie, and his spear, which is called Gungnir — and steers straight at the Fenris-wolf. Thor fights the Midgard Serpent beside him and cannot help; Freyr falls to Surtr for want of his given-away sword; and the wolf swallows Odin. Gungnir's last attested moment in the mythology is being carried by its master into the fight he loses.

The valkyrie Sigrdrifa teaches Sigurd the lore of runes and where they were carved: on shields and sword-blades, on the wolf's claw and the eagle's beak, on glass and gold — and on Gungnir's point and Grani's breast, on the Norn's nail and the owl's beak. The runes were then shaved off, stirred into the holy mead, and sent out across the worlds: some with the Aesir, some with the elves, some with the wise Vanir, some with mortal men.

Odin, disguised as the one-eyed Hrosshars-Grani, owes Starkadr a favor and demands King Vikarr as payment. When a mock-sacrifice is staged to lift a bad omen, Starkadr works the fake noose and the fake branch — and the spear Odin pressed into his hand, 'which would seem a reed-stalk', turns real: 'Now I give you to Odin,' Starkadr says, and the reed pierces the king through. The calf-gut noose hardens to a strong withe, the branch springs up and hangs the king, and the place is called Vikarshólmar ever after. The story is the clearest narrative of what Odin's spear means: a gift that looks like nothing, a death that looks like a game, and a king given away to the god.

The High One speaks: he hung on the windy tree nine whole nights, wounded with a spear, given to Odin, himself given to himself; no bread was offered him, no horn of drink; he looked down, caught up the runes, screaming, and fell back. The spear is unnamed, but the stanza is the hinge of the whole Gungnir tradition: the god's own body is the sacrifice, and a spear is the instrument — the same instrument, by strong implication, that dedicated Vikarr and the Vanir host.

Field Notes

Cultural Significance

Gungnir anchors Odin's identity as the god of war, wisdom, and sacrifice: the spear is his signature in kennings and art, and the spear-cast is the gesture that dedicates hosts and victims to him. Viking-Age iconography — Vendel helmet plates, the Boksta runestone (U 855), the Gotland stone G 181 — plausibly shows Odin with his spear, though none of those images names it. The etymology ('the swaying one', from gunga, 'to sway') captures the trembling flight of a thrown spear.

Modern Interpretations

Marvel Comics made Gungnir an uru spear channeling the Odinforce, and the MCU films made it the sceptre of Asgard's throne (Thor 2011; Loki on the throne in Thor: The Dark World 2013; shattered by Hela in Thor: Ragnarok 2017). Wagner's Wotan carries an unnamed treaty-spear of world-ash that commentators call Gungnir. Games: Gungnir: Inferno of the Demon Lance (Sting/Atlus, 2011), Final Fantasy XI/XIV, Fire Emblem: Genealogy of the Holy War's holy lance, Smite's Odin, God of War Ragnarok's unnamed Odin-spear, and Assassin's Creed Valhalla's Spear of Eden. The Saab RBS 15 Mk IV anti-ship missile is marketed as 'Gungnir'. Gaiman's Norse Mythology (2017) retells its forging. Popular accounts routinely misattribute the forging to Brokkr and Eitri, and assert the Hávamal spear and the Voluspa spear are Gungnir — both claims the texts leave unstated.

Content Notes

Gautreks saga's Vikarr episode is the clearest narrative of a human being killed as a dedication to Odin: hanged and pierced with the spear that 'seemed a reed'. Scholars (e.g., H.R. Ellis Davidson; Neil Price) connect such stories with the double death — hanging plus spear-wound — attested in Havamal 138 and with early accounts of sacrifices to Odin. The spear is the instrument that makes the killing a giving.

The historicity of specific sacrificial practices is debated; the sagas are literature, not eyewitness records. What is certain is that the motif — death by Odin's spear as a gift to the god — was thinkable and was told.

Gautreks saga ch. 7; Havamal 138; scholarly discussion in Ellis Davidson (1964) and Price (2019)

Voluspa 24's spear-cast over the Vanir host opens a war; the gesture is read as dedicating the slain to Odin. The same logic makes Odin a god who harvests battlefields, and the spear his sickle.

The ritual reading is reconstruction from the stanza plus comparative material (the custom of casting a spear over an enemy host is reported in saga-age and scholarly discussions; the precise attestations are catalogued in the secondary literature rather than in one surviving law-text).

Voluspa 24; secondary literature on the spear-cast ritual

Havamal 138 describes Odin wounding himself with a spear and hanging nine nights 'given to Odin, myself given to myself'. The spear is the tool of divine self-harm in pursuit of the runes. Modern retellings that put Gungnir in this scene are adding a name the text refuses — a small but instructive case of popular tradition sharpening a vague image into a weapon.

The stanza is among the most studied in the corpus; the unnamed spear is generally agreed to be a deliberate withholding, whatever the 'real' referent.

Was the Voluspa spear Gungnir?
The spear Odin casts over the Vanir host in Voluspa 24 is Gungnir. ⇌ The stanza names no weapon; the spear-cast may be a ritual motif rather than a specific artifact.
Evidence for A: The gesture matches Gungnir's attested role as Odin's war-spear; most retellings and scholars assume the identification.

Was the Havamal spear Gungnir?
Gungnir is the spear that wounded Odin on the world-tree (common in modern retellings and games). ⇌ Havamal 138 says only 'wounded with a spear'; the name is withheld.
Evidence for A: Thematic fit: the god dedicated to himself with his own weapon; several popular retellings assert it outright.
Evidence for B: The stanza text; no medieval gloss connects the two.

Gungnir 'always hits the target regardless of the attacker's skill' (modern summary phrasing). ⇌ The Old Norse says the spear 'never came to rest in its thrust' ('nam aldri stadar i lagi') — a statement about the spear's flight, not the thrower's ability.
Evidence for A: Wikipedia's summary; most popular accounts.

A way to hold it in your mind

If Mjolnir is a tool — a hammer that comes home to the hand that throws it — Gungnir is a verdict. It has no handle to shorten and no return flight; it simply does not stop short. The stories around it are never about fights, only about decisions: a war declared with one cast, a king given away with one thrust, a god wounding himself with one spear. Gungnir is what it looks like when a god commits — and in Norse myth, the gods' commitments always run toward their own death.
