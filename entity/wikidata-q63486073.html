Who It Is

The 4th spirit of Cendrillon is, paradoxically, one of the most anonymous entries in OmniLore: a mezzo-soprano voice in a group of six, unnamed beyond an ordinal, with no solo aria, no individual personality, and no life outside the enchanted forest scene where she helps two lovers fall asleep under a magic oak. Yet her very anonymity is historically significant — she represents the unbroken chain from Lully's seventeenth-century court spirit-ensembles to Massenet's modernized fairy tale, the persistence of the supernatural retinue as the sonic and visual language of the enchanted in French opera. When you hear six mezzo-sopranos encircling the lovers in that blue-lit forest, you are hearing two centuries of French operatic convention made briefly, beautifully audible.

The 4th spirit, as an ensemble role within a group of six, has no individually scripted personality in the libretto or score. The spirits as a collective are characterized by benevolent supernatural service to la Fee (the Fairy Godmother) and to the lovers Cendrillon and the Prince. Their function in the narrative is to inhabit and animate the enchanted forest, to draw the lovers together through magical means, and to preside over their enchanted sleep in each other's arms. Powers
Magical transformation assistance

As attendants of la Fee (the Fairy Godmother), the spirits collectively participate in the transformation of Cendrillon from her domestic state into her ball-gown appearance in Act 1. The 4th spirit, as part of this ensemble, contributes to the magical operation that produces Cendrillon's magnificent ball costume, the glass slippers, and the enchanted carriage. The transformation is presented in the opera as a collective magical act of the fairy retinue rather than the sole power of la Fee, suggesting the spirits function as amplifiers or channels of fairy magical power.

Limit:

Enchanted forest habitation and animation

The spirits inhabit the magical oak forest of Act 3, Scene 2, and their presence is what makes the space enchanted. They animate the supernatural arbour of flowers that appears to separate the lovers and then disappears to reveal them to each other. Their collective singing and choreographic movement in this scene constitutes a magical performance that draws Cendrillon and the Prince together across the enchanted space. The spirits thus have domain over the physical properties of the enchanted forest setting.

Limit:

Magical slumber induction

At the climax of Act 3, Scene 2, the lovers fall into a magical slumber in each other's arms while 'surrounded by the spirits.' The spirits' encircling presence is the proximate cause of the enchanted sleep, which suspends the lovers in a timeless fairy-tale state and allows the narrative to jump forward to Act 4 where Cendrillon awakens believing the events were a dream. This power of enchanted sleep induction is a collective property of the spirit ensemble and places them in a lineage of sleep-inducing supernatural figures in European fairy-tale tradition.

Limit:

Liminal traversal (movement between realms)

The spirits demonstrate the ability to move freely between the enchanted fairy realm and the human domestic world, as shown by their appearance in Cendrillon's house in Act 1 accompanying la Fee. This liminal traversal ability — crossing from the fairy world into mundane human space — is presented as natural for the spirit retinue and marks them as supernatural beings not bound by the ordinary separation between the fairy and human worlds that governs the main characters. This ability aligns them with similar fairy-retinue figures in European folklore and opera tradition.

Limit:

Collective choral voice (supernatural music)

As mezzo-soprano ensemble roles, the spirits possess a collective musical voice that functions within the opera as a sonic marker of the supernatural. Massenet's score, as noted by critics, achieves its 'spectral ballet under a bluish light' in Act 3 partly through the distinctive timbre of the spirit ensemble contrasted with the solo voices of the principal characters. The collective mezzo-soprano sound of all six spirits creates a homogeneous, otherworldly vocal texture. Individual spirits (including the 4th) may have distinct melodic lines within the ensemble passages, but these are subordinate to the collective sonic effect.

Accounts I
The Fairy Transformation (Act 1, Scene 2)Cendrillon libretto by Henri Cain, score by Jules Massenet

1899 (operatic version); 1697 (Perrault source)

After the household departs for the royal ball and Cendrillon is left alone by the warm chimney hearth, the Fairy Godmother (la Fee) and her attendants — including the spirit ensemble — enter the house. La Fee, singing the aria 'Ah! douce enfant,' awakens and transforms Cendrillon: her plain domestic clothes are replaced by magnificent ball attire, she receives the famous glass slippers which will protect her from being recognized by her family, and she is warned that the spell will last only until midnight. The spirit ensemble participates in the transformation scene, their presence marking the incursion of the fairy world into the human domestic sphere. The scene establishes the spirit retinue as la Fee's instrumental agents of magical change. Cendrillon attends the ball and meets the Prince, setting in motion the central romantic plot. The glass slippers serve as the recognition device that ultimately reunites the lovers in Act 4.

The Fairies' Oak (Act 3, Scene 2 — Les Fees)Cendrillon libretto by Henri Cain, score by Jules Massenet

1899

Under a magic oak tree in an enchanted forest, the prince and Cendrillon are independently drawn together by the fairies. An enchanted arbour of flowers mysteriously appears and blocks their view of each other, yet they recognize each other's voice and sing of their love across the magical barrier. The prince offers his heart to see his beloved. The flowers then disappear and the lovers, surrounded by the spirits who encircle them in a choreographed fairy ring, fall into a mutual enchanted slumber in each other's arms. The scene is the emotional and musical heart of the opera: it is the moment where the lovers' connection is confirmed as real, not illusory, even while surrounded by the ambiguous magic of the fairy world. The spectral ballet under blue lighting, featuring the spirit ensemble in full choreographic display, is the opera's most visually and musically elaborate supernatural passage. Accounts II
The Midnight Flight (Act 2 → Act 3 transition)Cendrillon libretto by Henri Cain, score by Jules Massenet

1899

When the clock strikes midnight during the royal ball, the fairy enchantment begins to expire. Cendrillon flees the palace in her transformation clothes, losing one of her glass slippers in her flight as the stunned Prince looks on. The spirit ensemble's collective power — the time-limited spell cast in Act 1 with their assistance — proves unable to be extended beyond the midnight boundary set by la Fee. The glass slipper left behind becomes the recognition token that drives the Act 4 reunion plot. The spirits are not visible in this scene but their earlier magical work determines its outcome entirely. The lost glass slipper enables the Act 4 reunion: the royal herald summons all eligible princesses to try on the slipper, and the Prince recognizes Cendrillon among them.

The Grand Finale: Recognition and Reunion (Act 4, Scene 2)Cendrillon libretto by Henri Cain, score by Jules Massenet

1899

Back at the royal palace, the Prince recognizes Cendrillon among the assembled princesses attempting to try on the glass slipper. The lovers are publicly reunited and acclaimed by all present, including the chastened Madame de la Haltiere. The entire cast then breaks the fourth wall in the opera's epilogue, turning to the audience and singing out of character that the piece is over and they have done their best to carry the audience through 'les beaux pays bleus' (the beautiful blue countries) — the same phrase used in the suppressed prologue. The spirit ensemble participates in this celebratory finale as part of the fairy-world contingent that has guided the narrative to its happy resolution. The moment where all the magical forces of the fairy world are vindicated in the restoration of the proper order of love over cruelty. The opera's moral is affirmed: fairy-tale magic and true love triumph over domestic cruelty and social pretension. The epilogue's direct address to the audience frames the entire opera as a therapeutic fairy-tale journey.

Wikidata classification as 'ghost in a work of fiction'Wikidata (Q63486073) classifies the 4th spirit under Q30061299 ('ghost in a work of fiction') alongside the primary classification of 'operatic character'. This implies the spirits are understood by Wikidata contributors as belonging to a category of supernatural deceased-spirit beings.The libretto and score of Cendrillon present the spirit attendants as living fairy beings — members of la Fee's supernatural retinue, not deceased souls or ghosts. The French operatic term 'esprit' in this context means an animated supernatural being, not a ghost in the thanatological sense. The 'ghost' classification is a Wikidata taxonomic artefact resulting from the difficulty of classifying supernatural fictional entities that do not fit neatly into either 'fairy' or 'human' categories. No production history, critical commentary, or libretto annotation supports reading the Cendrillon spirits as ghosts.
Whether the numbered spirits are individually distinct dramatic characters or a single ensemble entityWikidata creates six separate entity records (for 1st through 6th spirit), and production credits sometimes list individual singers for each numbered spirit role, implying that each spirit is a distinct character with its own identity, voice part, and stage position sufficient to warrant individual credit.The libretto and score treat the six spirits as an indistinguishable ensemble with no individual names, personality traits, solo arias, or distinct narrative functions. Critics and musicologists discussing Cendrillon invariably refer to 'the spirits' as a collective unit. The numbering is a stage-management convention (like 'First Lady' and 'Second Lady' in Die Zauberflöte) rather than a signal of genuine dramatic individuation. Creating six separate Wikidata entities for what is essentially one undifferentiated ensemble role reflects the database's bias toward enumerable entities rather than the opera's dramatic reality.
The opera's place in the Cinderella operatic canon relative to Rossini's La CenerentolaMassenet's Cendrillon, with its supernatural fairy machinery including the spirit ensemble, is a faithful and artistically superior treatment of the Perrault source. Critics including the New Grove Dictionary authors praise its perfectly proportioned score and its wit and humor. The supernatural element including the spirits is essential to the opera's identity as a 'conte de fées' (fairy tale), and the opera's 2018 Metropolitan Opera premiere after 119 years confirmed its enduring value.Rossini's La Cenerentola (1817), which eliminates the supernatural fairy machinery entirely in favor of a humanist reinterpretation, is performed far more frequently in the modern repertoire and is generally considered the dominant Cinderella opera. From this perspective, Massenet's spirit ensemble represents a nostalgic and dramatically weaker choice — the supernatural machinery requiring elaborate production resources while adding little dramatic depth compared to Rossini's human drama. The spirits in this reading are evidence of an operatic convention that Rossini wisely discarded.
The spirits' agency as magical actors versus passive atmospheric decorationThe synopsis passage 'the prince and Cendrillon are drawn together by the fairies' and 'the lovers, surrounded by the spirits, fall into a slumber' attributes active causal agency to the spirit ensemble: they draw the lovers together and induce the enchanted sleep. This makes them genuine dramatic participants with magical powers that drive the plot.In practice in performance and in the score's musical structure, the spirit ensemble functions as atmospheric decoration rather than active dramatic agents. They do not sing independently of la Fee, have no dialogue, take no independent actions, and are staged as choreographic backdrop. The 'agency' attributed to them in the synopsis is more properly understood as belonging to la Fee and the fairy world collectively, with the spirits as the embodied visible form of that magical environment rather than its operators.
