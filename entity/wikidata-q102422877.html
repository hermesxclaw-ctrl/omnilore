The Approach

Before Dracula, before the modern vampire's suave menace, there was Carmilla: a beautiful, enigmatic young woman who insinuates herself into the life of a lonely heiress in a remote Styrian castle, blurring the lines between romantic devotion and predatory hunger. Sheridan Le Fanu's 1872 novella remains a cornerstone of vampire fiction, a tale that is at once a chilling Gothic horror and a subversive exploration of forbidden desire.

Content advisory. Contains themes of vampirism, predation, implied sexual violence, and coercion. The relationship between Carmilla and Laura involves manipulation and a power imbalance, with Carmilla's advances being both seductive and threatening. The story includes scenes of blood, illness, and death. Some modern adaptations may add explicit content.

Etymology

The name 'Carmilla' is likely a variant of 'Carmella' or 'Carmen', possibly derived from the Hebrew 'Karmel' (garden) or Latin 'carmen' (song). The alias 'Mircalla' is an anagram of 'Carmilla'. 'Millarca' is another anagram. The surname 'Karnstein' evokes 'carn' (flesh) and 'stein' (stone), hinting at the vampire's nature.

Cultural Ownership

Irish Gothic literature, English-language vampire fiction. Created by Joseph Sheridan Le Fanu, an Irish writer. The story is set in Styria, Austria, within the Austro-Hungarian Empire.

Disputed Identifications

Carmilla is sometimes conflated with other literary vampires, such as the Brides of Dracula or Elizabeth Báthory, but these are distinct figures. Some scholars have debated the extent of Le Fanu's originality, noting possible influences from folklore and earlier vampire tales.

Description

Appearance

Carmilla is described as a young woman of striking beauty, with dark hair, pale skin, and a languid, graceful demeanor. She often appears in a state of delicate health, with a 'sweet' and 'innocent' face that belies her true nature. She wears fashionable clothing of the period, but her most distinctive feature is a small mole on her neck, which matches a portrait of her ancestor.

Symbols & Iconography

The vampire is associated with the color black, as seen in the 'black creature' that attacks Bertha. The mole on Carmilla's neck is a physical marker of her identity and vulnerability. The ruined chapel and tomb of Karnstein symbolize the decay of aristocratic power and the persistence of evil. The portrait of Mircalla Karnstein underscores the theme of doubling and ancestral recurrence.

Behaviour & Personality

Carmilla is charming, intelligent, and seductive, but also secretive, manipulative, and predatory. She is affectionate towards Laura, yet her advances are tinged with menace. She sleeps during the day, avoids food, and has an aversion to religious practices. She is also shown to be cunning, as she conceals her true identity and movements. Her personality is a mix of vulnerability and dominance, often leaving Laura confused and conflicted.

Domains & Habitats

Carmilla's primary domain is the remote castle in Styria, where she stays with Laura. Her ancestral home is the ruined village of Karnstein, where she is eventually destroyed. She is a creature of the night, associated with darkness and the supernatural. She moves freely through locked doors and can transform into a large black cat-like creature.

Version Differences

In the original serialization, Carmilla's character is depicted somewhat differently than in the book edition, as the illustrations by David Henry Friston and Michael Fitzgerald show inconsistencies. The 2019 edition edited by Carmen Maria Machado adds a framing story and footnotes that humanize Carmilla, presenting her as a tragic figure rather than a pure villain. Some adaptations, such as the web series, portray her as a more sympathetic and romantic character.

Powers & Abilities

Vampirism

Source Version Original novella

Description

Carmilla is a vampire, able to sustain herself by drinking blood, typically from young women. She has a hypnotic influence over her victims, making them susceptible to her will. She can also appear in dreams and cause a gradual weakening in her victims.

Limitations

She is vulnerable to religious symbols and rites, and can be destroyed by decapitation and burning. She must return to her tomb during the day. She is bound to her grave and cannot stray far from it for extended periods.

Contradictions

The novella does not specify all traditional vampire weaknesses, such as sunlight or garlic. Some adaptations add these elements.

Stories & Myths

The Story of Carmilla

Source Le Fanu, Joseph Sheridan. 'Carmilla'.Culture Irish Gothic, Victorian England, Austrian settingPeriod 1872

Summary

Laura, a young Englishwoman living in a remote Styrian castle, is visited by the enigmatic Carmilla after a carriage accident. The two become close, but Carmilla's strange behavior and romantic advances unsettle Laura. Laura falls ill, and it is revealed that Carmilla is a vampire, the Countess Mircalla Karnstein. With the help of General Spielsdorf, whose niece was a previous victim, they track Carmilla to her tomb and destroy her.

Consequences

Carmilla is destroyed, and Laura survives, but the trauma of the experience lingers. The story serves as a warning about the dangers of unchecked desire and the supernatural.

Variants

The serialized version lacks the Hesselius prologue. Modern adaptations often change the ending or the nature of the relationship.

The Story of Bertha Rheinfeldt

Source Le Fanu, Joseph Sheridan. 'Carmilla'.Culture Victorian GothicPeriod 1872

Summary

General Spielsdorf recounts how his niece Bertha was seduced and killed by a vampire calling herself Millarca. She stayed with them under the pretense of a family connection, and Bertha fell ill. Spielsdorf discovered her true nature and attempted to kill her, but she escaped.

Predatory sexuality

Source Original novella

Description

Carmilla's advances towards Laura are both seductive and threatening, representing a predatory form of homosexuality. She feeds on Laura while professing love, blurring the lines between affection and abuse.

Scholarly Context

Scholars have analyzed this as a representation of Victorian anxieties about female sexuality and homosexuality.

Contradictions & Disputes

Nature of Carmilla's relationship with Laura

Position A

Carmilla genuinely loves Laura and their relationship is a tragic romance.

Position A — evidence

Carmilla's passionate speeches and apparent reluctance to harm Laura.

Position B

Carmilla is merely a predator who uses affection to manipulate her victim.

Position B — evidence

Carmilla's actions are consistently self-serving, and she feeds on Laura despite professing love.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars view the relationship as predatory, with any romantic elements being a tool of manipulation.

Carmilla's origin

Position A

Carmilla is the vampire Countess Mircalla Karnstein, who lived in the 17th century.

Position A — evidence

The portrait resemblance and the discovery of her tomb.

Position B

Carmilla is a separate entity who merely adopts the identity of Mircalla.

Position B — evidence

The story does not explicitly state that Carmilla is Mircalla, only that she resembles her.

Scholarly Consensus

The text strongly implies they are the same, and most interpretations accept this.

The role of the Hesselius prologue

Position A

The prologue adds a layer of authenticity and frames the story as a case study.

Position A — evidence

It connects the story to Le Fanu's other works and provides a rationalizing frame.

Position B

The prologue is unnecessary and detracts from the narrative.

Position B — evidence

Some readers find it intrusive and prefer the original serialization.

Scholarly Consensus

There is no consensus; it is a matter of taste.
