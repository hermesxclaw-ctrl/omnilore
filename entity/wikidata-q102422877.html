Who It Is

Before Dracula, before the modern vampire's suave menace, there was Carmilla: a beautiful, enigmatic young woman who insinuates herself into the life of a lonely heiress in a remote Styrian castle, blurring the lines between romantic devotion and predatory hunger. Sheridan Le Fanu's 1872 novella remains a cornerstone of vampire fiction, a tale that is at once a chilling Gothic horror and a subversive exploration of forbidden desire.

Carmilla is charming, intelligent, and seductive, but also secretive, manipulative, and predatory. She is affectionate towards Laura, yet her advances are tinged with menace. She sleeps during the day, avoids food, and has an aversion to religious practices. She is also shown to be cunning, as she conceals her true identity and movements. Her personality is a mix of vulnerability and dominance, often leaving Laura confused and conflicted.

Powers
Vampirism

Carmilla is a vampire, able to sustain herself by drinking blood, typically from young women. She has a hypnotic influence over her victims, making them susceptible to her will. She can also appear in dreams and cause a gradual weakening in her victims.

Limit: She is vulnerable to religious symbols and rites, and can be destroyed by decapitation and burning. She must return to her tomb during the day. She is bound to her grave and cannot stray far from it for extended periods.

Shapeshifting

Carmilla can transform into a large black cat-like beast, as seen when she attacks her victims. This form allows her to move silently and evade detection.

Limit: The transformation is not fully explained, and she may be vulnerable in this form, as General Spielsdorf attacks her while she is in this state.

Superhuman agility and strength

She can move through locked doors and escape attacks with ease, suggesting supernatural physical abilities.

Limit: Not explicitly stated, but implied.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Carmilla is described as a young woman of striking beauty, with dark hair, pale skin, and a languid, graceful demeanor. She often appears in a state of delicate health, with a 'sweet' and 'innocent' face that belies her true nature. She wears fashionable clothing of the period, but her most distinctive feature is a small mole on her neck, which matches a portrait of her ancestor.

Symbols — The vampire is associated with the color black, as seen in the 'black creature' that attacks Bertha. The mole on Carmilla's neck is a physical marker of her identity and vulnerability. The ruined chapel and tomb of Karnstein symbolize the decay of aristocratic power and the persistence of evil. The portrait of Mircalla Karnstein underscores the theme of doubling and ancestral recurrence.

Domains — Carmilla's primary domain is the remote castle in Styria, where she stays with Laura. Her ancestral home is the ruined village of Karnstein, where she is eventually destroyed. She is a creature of the night, associated with darkness and the supernatural. She moves freely through locked doors and can transform into a large black cat-like creature.

Accounts I
The Story of CarmillaLe Fanu, Joseph Sheridan. 'Carmilla'.

1872

Laura, a young Englishwoman living in a remote Styrian castle, is visited by the enigmatic Carmilla after a carriage accident. The two become close, but Carmilla's strange behavior and romantic advances unsettle Laura. Laura falls ill, and it is revealed that Carmilla is a vampire, the Countess Mircalla Karnstein. With the help of General Spielsdorf, whose niece was a previous victim, they track Carmilla to her tomb and destroy her. Carmilla is destroyed, and Laura survives, but the trauma of the experience lingers. The story serves as a warning about the dangers of unchecked desire and the supernatural.

Accounts II
The Story of Bertha RheinfeldtLe Fanu, Joseph Sheridan. 'Carmilla'.

1872

General Spielsdorf recounts how his niece Bertha was seduced and killed by a vampire calling herself Millarca. She stayed with them under the pretense of a family connection, and Bertha fell ill. Spielsdorf discovered her true nature and attempted to kill her, but she escaped. Bertha dies, and Spielsdorf becomes determined to avenge her, leading to Carmilla's destruction.

Carmilla is a lesbian vampire who preys on women because of her sexual orientation. — Carmilla's sexuality is not explicitly defined; her vampirism is a metaphor for forbidden desire and predation. The story reflects Victorian anxieties about female sexuality, but it does not present a positive portrayal of lesbianism.

Carmilla is the first vampire in literature. — Carmilla is a foundational vampire story, but not the first. Vampires appeared in earlier works like John Polidori's 'The Vampyre' (1819) and in folklore.

Carmilla is a direct influence on Dracula. — Carmilla did influence Bram Stoker's Dracula, but Stoker also drew from other sources. The influence is significant but not exclusive.
