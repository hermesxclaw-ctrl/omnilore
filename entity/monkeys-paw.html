Who It Is

A dried, mummified monkey's paw sits in a modest English parlor, its fingers curled as if clutching a secret. It promises three wishes, but the price is paid in blood and sorrow. This is the story of how a simple relic became one of the most enduring warnings against tampering with fate.

The paw is inanimate but is described as having a 'twisted' and 'snake-like' movement when it grants a wish. It seems to have a malevolent will of its own, though this is ambiguous. It does not speak or act independently beyond the granting of wishes.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name is literal: the object is a mummified paw of a monkey, and the story revolves around its magical properties. 'Monkey's paw' has become a proverbial phrase for a wish-granting object with dire consequences.

Pronunciation — MUN-keez paw

Aliases — The Paw, Monkey's Paw

Ownership — English literary tradition, specifically the horror and supernatural genre. The story is a foundational text in the 'cursed object' subgenre and has entered global popular culture.

Powers
Wish Granting

The paw grants three wishes to its owner. The wishes are fulfilled, but in a way that brings misery and tragedy, as the paw punishes interference with fate.

Limit: Only three wishes are granted. The paw cannot be used by more than one person? (In the story, Mr. White makes all three wishes, but it is implied that the paw could be passed on). The wishes are granted in a twisted manner, often with unintended consequences.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The paw is described as 'dried' and 'mummified', with 'a little hairy paw' that resembles a monkey's. It is not described in great detail, leaving much to the reader's imagination. It is small enough to be held in one hand and has a 'sinister' quality.

Symbols — The paw symbolizes the dangerous allure of wish fulfillment and the folly of interfering with fate. It is often depicted in illustrations and adaptations as a dark, withered hand with curled fingers. It is associated with the concept of the 'deal with the devil' and the 'cursed object' trope.

Domains — The paw originates in India, where it was enchanted by a fakir. It travels to England with Sergeant-Major Morris and ends up in the White household. It is a domestic object, but its origins are exotic and mysterious.

Accounts I
The Monkey's PawW. W. Jacobs, Harper's Monthly, 1902

Early 20th century (set in contemporary England, but with colonial overtones from India)

Mr. and Mrs. White and their son Herbert receive a mummified monkey's paw from Sergeant-Major Morris, a friend who served in India. The paw grants three wishes, but with terrible consequences. Mr. White wishes for £200 to pay off the mortgage. The next day, Herbert is killed in an industrial accident, and the company pays the family £200 in compensation. A week later, Mrs. White, mad with grief, forces her husband to wish Herbert back to life. That night, a knocking is heard at the door. Mr. White, terrified of what might be outside, uses his third wish. The knocking stops, and when Mrs. White opens the door, no one is there. The White family is destroyed: Herbert dies, Mrs. White descends into madness, and Mr. White is left with the guilt of his wishes. The final outcome is ambiguous: the third wish may have undone the second, or it may have prevented a horror from entering the house.

The paw grants three wishes to each owner. — The original story states that the paw grants three wishes total, not per owner. Morris had his three wishes, and the paw was passed to Mr. White.

The paw is a real historical artifact. — The Monkey's Paw is a fictional creation of W. W. Jacobs.

The story is based on an Indian legend. — There is no known Indian legend of a monkey's paw with these properties. The story is Jacobs's invention, though it draws on Orientalist tropes.
