Who It Is

A little mummified monkey's paw, brought back from India by a sergeant-major who throws it on the fire and warns his hosts to let it burn: it grants three wishes to three separate owners, enchanted by a fakir who wanted to prove that fate rules life and that those who interfere with fate meet sorrow. The Whites — comfortable, ordinary, mildly skeptical — fish it out. Their first wish is modest, almost embarrassed: two hundred pounds, to clear the mortgage. The next day their son Herbert is killed in the machinery at his factory, and the firm, disclaiming all liability, sends compensation: two hundred pounds. What the story does next made it immortal. The grieving mother, days after the funeral, remembers the paw's two remaining wishes — and demands her husband wish their son alive again. He does. And then, in the dark, far down the lane: a knock. Then another. Then a steady hammering at the door, while the old woman fights the bolts and the old man, who alone saw the body and knows what ten days and the machinery mean, scrabbles on the floor for the paw. She throws the door wide on his third wish — to an empty lane, a quiet street, a lamp flickering on nothing. Jacobs never shows what knocked. That restraint — horror as invoice, the monster kept entirely offstage — made a nine-page magazine story the most anthologized dark-wish tale in English, and its moral formula (be careful what you wish for; fate bills at market rates, cruelly itemized) has become the standing template every deal-with-consequences story since is measured against.

Provenance

Published September 1902 in Harper's by W. W. Jacobs, a comic writer whose one horror story outlived all his comedies; built on the older three-wishes folk pattern, industrialized: the wish economy meets factory machinery and liability law.

Attested in W. W. Jacobs' short story The Monkey's PawEra 1902

Form

A small, dried, leathery monkey's paw, unpleasant to hold, ordinary enough to sit on a mantel between the candlesticks — malice disguised as bric-a-brac.

Powers

Three wishes per owner, granted with perfect literalism and maximum collateral cruelty; the paw twists in the hand at the moment of wishing — the only warning it ever gives.

Weaknesses & Wards

Fire would have ended it on page two; human hope retrieved it. Its own third wish is the sole undo mechanism, spendable exactly once, in the dark, at the door.

Field Notes

⚱ Jacobs was famous as a humorist — his masterpiece of dread was a genre day-trip.

⚱ The two-hundred-pound compensation equals the mortgage: the paw pays debts precisely.

⚱ 'What knocked' has no answer in the text — the most productive locked door in horror literature.

Cultural Significance

The definitive modern cursed object and the template of wish-horror: The Simpsons, The X-Files, Stephen King's Pet Sematary (which King calls his monkey's-paw book) all pay it tribute.
