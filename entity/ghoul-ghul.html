Who It Is

Everything you picture when you hear the word 'ghoul' — the hunched grave-robber, the cemetery, the corpse-eating — was added by a Frenchman in 1704. The creature the Bedouin actually feared was something far stranger: a shapeshifter that came to you on the desert road wearing the face of a beautiful woman or a long-lost aunt, that whistled from ahead of you and then from behind to unmoor your sense of direction, and that you could identify only by looking down at its feet, because whatever else it could change, it could never change its cloven hooves. And it talked. In the founding hadith a ghoul is caught stealing dates, pleads that she has children to feed, is released, steals again, is caught again — and finally buys her freedom by teaching her captor the Throne Verse, the very scripture Muslims recite to this day to keep her kind out of the house. The Arabic ghūl is a predator whose weapon is not strength but social intelligence, and this makes it a fundamentally different order of monster from the Western creature that bears its name. It hunts by exploiting the obligations of desert hospitality and kinship: it appears on the road at night, in the ruins and abandoned places off the traffic routes, and offers the traveller precisely the relationship he cannot refuse. The Oxford English Dictionary and most Arabist lexicography favour the 'seize/s

Pronunciation — Classical Arabic [ɣuːl] (غُول) — a voiced uvular fricative followed by a long back vowel, closer to 'ghool' than to English 'goul'. Feminine غُولة [ˈɣuː.la]; plural غِيلان [ɣiːˈlaːn]. English /ɡuːl/ (rhyming with 'cool'); French goule [ɡul]; German Ghul [ɡuːl]; Japanese グール [ɡɯːɾɯ]; Persian [ɣul]. Powers
Metamorphosis (taghawwul / تغوّل)

The ghoul's cardinal ability, encoded in the reflexive verb taghawwalat, which means simultaneously 'the ghouls changed their shapes' and 'they led travellers astray into bewilderment' — the transformation and the disorientation are one act in Arabic. The creature changes both colour and form at will, producing the proverbial Arabic similes 'he changes colour like a ghoul' and 'as capricious as a ghoul changing its colours'. Preferred forms are the beautiful young woman (against male travellers), the unassuming old woman or long-lost female relative (in the tales told to girls, per Dwight Reynolds), the traveller's own trusted friend, and the hyena. The transformation is never complete: the feet remain cloven or asinine in every major transmission — French (les pieds fourchus, le seul élément constant), German (nur seine Füße bleiben stets Eselshufe), Russian (ослиные копыта) — which makes footwear the single most important item in the ghoul's wardrobe and foot-inspection the traveller's first defence.

Limit:

Anthropophagy and necrophagy

The ghoul eats human beings. In the Arabian register this is predation on the living — it kills travellers and consumes them, and al-Dimashqi records that it preys on beasts as well. Corpse-eating and grave-violation are, per Finnish comparative scholarship and Al-Rawi's central thesis, a Western accretion introduced through Galland, not an Arabian trait; the Central Asian variant, however, independently developed a corpse-dependency, subsisting on graves in forests, swamps, disused wells and battlefields. The Lovecraftian ghoul makes necrophagy causal rather than incidental: its ghouls were once human and were transformed into doglike bestial humanoids by a diet consisting exclusively of the dead. Tokyo Ghoul makes the appetite a metabolic prison — its ghouls can digest nothing but human flesh, water and coffee, and find all other food nauseating.

Limit:

The one-blow rule (invulnerability to the second strike)

The most distinctive and most specific power in the entire Arabic corpus, attested in both the Arabic and Russian traditions: a ghoul struck once with a sword dies, but if struck a second time it revives. This is not resilience but a formal narrative law, and it functions in the tales as a test of the hero's discipline — Ta'abbata Sharra's celebrated ghoul-slaying turns on his refusal to strike twice. It inverts the usual folkloric arithmetic in which persistence defeats the monster, making restraint the condition of victory.

Limit:

Acoustic disorientation and pathfinding sabotage

The ghoul hunts by sound. Arabic sources describe it whistling a devilish whistle from ahead of the traveller and then from behind him, systematically destroying his sense of direction and drawing him off the road. This is why the prophetic tradition attaches the danger to the roadbed rather than to travel itself, and why the countermeasure is also acoustic: 'when the ghouls shapeshift upon you, hasten to make the call to prayer' (fa-asriʿū al-adhān). The ghoul's weapon is the corruption of the traveller's audio-spatial map, and the adhan is a competing sound that restores it.

Limit:

Nocturnality and photophobia

Al-Dimashqi describes ghouls as cave-dwelling animals that emerge only at night and shun the light of the sun. The restriction is absolute enough in most traditions to make daylight a reliable sanctuary, and it converges with the prophetic recommendation of night travel (the earth being 'folded up at night') to produce a specific danger window: the traveller is advised to move at night, precisely when the ghoul is abroad, and is therefore given verbal countermeasures rather than a curfew.

Limit:

Seduction and exploitation of hospitality obligation

The ghoul's most effective power is social rather than physical. It offers travellers exactly the relationship they are culturally forbidden to refuse — hospitality, kinship, sexual welcome — and Reynolds documents the gendered split in its approach: seductive young woman for men, familiar female relative for women and girls. In the Umm Ghula tale-type the men are taken in because they are greedy or flattered while the women of the household see through the glamour and are disbelieved, making the ghoul a device for a specific social critique rather than a generic predator.

Limit:

Speech, intelligence and social organisation

Unlike almost every monster in the modern Western catalogue, the Arabic ghoul talks, and Arabic folktales contain extended dialogue with ghouls who reason, bargain, swear oaths, break them, and offer moral instruction. As a jinn it possesses moral agency: it is accountable, it can be argued with and bound. Lovecraft is the one major Western author to preserve this, describing his ghouls as gregarious and capable of intelligent conversation; Fallout preserves it too, with non-feral ghouls holding civic and commercial roles across the wasteland. D&D, Romero's revenants and most gaming derivatives strip it entirely.

Limit:

Theft, grave-robbery and petty predation

A cluster of distinctly unheroic appetites: the ghoul steals coins, robs the dead, filches dates from a storeroom (Abu Ayyub al-Ansari's ghoul), and preys on unattended small children. Japanese comparative treatments read this cluster as the monsterisation of the criminal classes — the highway bandit, the impersonator, the grave-robber — and note that some modern ulama accordingly interpret Umar's ghoul-slaying as the killing of a highwayman.

Limit:

Susceptibility to divine names, the adhan and Ayat al-Kursi

The ghoul's power set is defined as much by its exposed flanks as by its capacities. Invoking the name of God (Bismillah) strips the disguise and forces the true form; the adhan drives the creature off; and Ayat al-Kursi, the Throne Verse of Surat al-Baqara, is the definitive permanent ward — surrendered, in the founding hadith, by a captured ghoul herself as ransom. Japanese-transmitted lore adds iron as an apotropaic. That the strongest countermeasure comes from the monster's own mouth as a negotiated settlement is theologically remarkable and has no parallel in the Western tradition.

Limit:

Conversion and moral reformation

As a jinn the ghoul is subject to the same religious law as humanity and can accept Islam, whereupon it abandons its savage nature and may become well-disposed toward human beings. This is the single sharpest divergence between the Arabic creature and its Western descendants: the ghoul is not ontologically evil but morally unreformed, and its monstrousness is a state that can be exited.

Limit:

Paralytic touch (gaming lineage)

The Dungeons & Dragons ghoul — undead rather than jinn, reeking of carrion — paralyses living creatures with its claws and bite, with elves traditionally immune; the ghast is an escalated variant adding a nauseating stench, and the abyssal ghoul a further demonic escalation. This ability appears nowhere in the Arabic sources but has become the ghoul's default power in gaming, templating Magic: The Gathering and the great majority of subsequent fantasy franchises.

Limit:

Radiological necrosis and near-immortality (Fallout lineage)

The Fallout ghoul is produced by massive radiation exposure interacting with the Forced Evolutionary Virus, causing extreme necrosis of the skin and tissue while dramatically extending lifespan and conferring healing from further radiation rather than harm. The trade-off is neurological: continued degradation of the brain can eventually destroy higher cognition, producing 'ferals'. This is a wholly modern rebuild that nonetheless reproduces the original structure — a near-human being reduced by an environmental hazard at the edge of civilisation to a predator on those who pass through it.

Limit:

Kagune and kakugan (Tokyo Ghoul lineage)

Sui Ishida's ghouls possess the kagune, a retractable predatory organ of crystallised Rc cells used as both weapon and armour, and the kakugan, a red-on-black eye transformation that betrays the ghoul's nature under stress or hunger. They are physically far stronger than humans and outwardly indistinguishable from them — the concealment being the point, since the franchise uses the species as a sustained allegory of minority existence under a hostile majority. The disguise capacity is a direct structural inheritance from the Arabic shapeshifter, rebuilt as permanent passing rather than momentary illusion.

Limit:

Blood-bond servitude (World of Darkness inversion)

White Wolf's World of Darkness reverses the term entirely: a 'ghoul' is a living human or animal bound into servitude by ingesting vampire blood, gaining extended life and a share of the vampire's supernatural strength while becoming addicted to and dominated by its master. The Tzimisce clan maintains hereditary ghoul families. The inversion is instructive — the ghoul here is the victim of a predator rather than the predator, which is arguably closer to Al-Rawi's sociological reading of the original than any of the horror-genre versions.

Accounts I
Ta'abbata Sharra's Night Combat with the GhulOde preserved in the Mufaddaliyat (8th c.) and the Hamasah of Abu Tammam (9th c.); narrative frame in al-Isfahani, Kitab al-Aghani

late 6th – early 7th century CE

The su'luk (brigand) poet Thabit ibn Jabir al-Fahmi encounters a ghul in the waterless waste of Raha Bitan on a night of moonless dark, fights her hand to hand until dawn, and kills her with a single stroke of his Yemeni blade. He then sits upon her corpse until morning to examine what he has killed, and describes what he sees: a monstrous head, a body like a cat's or a hyena's, cloven feet, a hairy leg, the eyes of a cat set in a hideous face, and a split tongue. He carries the corpse away as proof. The poem is presented as first-person testimony rather than legend, and functions within the su'luk aesthetic as a boast of self-sufficiency: the outcast who lives beyond tribal protection defeats the very creature that preys on those who leave the road. The poem is the earliest substantial description of the ghoul in any language and established the creature's canonical physical inventory — cloven feet, hyena affinity, feline eyes, split tongue — for the whole subsequent tradition. It also supplied the poet's own laqab in one etymological legend, in which he carried a ram under his arm

Abu Ayyub al-Ansari and the Ghoul Who Stole DatesSunan al-Tirmidhi, Kitab Fada'il al-Qur'an; commentary in al-Mubarakfuri, Tuhfat al-Ahwadhi

7th century CE narrative; transmitted from the 9th century

Abu Ayyub al-Ansari, the Prophet's host in Medina, finds dates disappearing nightly from his storeroom (sahwa). He lies in wait and catches a ghoul in the shape of a creature resembling a young girl or a cat. She begs release, pleading that she has a family and dependants; he frees her. She returns and steals again. He catches her a second time and threatens to take her to the Messenger of God. To buy her freedom she teaches him a countermeasure: recite Ayat al-Kursi, the Throne Verse, on entering the house, and no devil will approach until morning. He releases her, and when he reports the exchange to the Prophet, Muhammad confirms 'She has told you the truth, though she is a habitual liar.' This is the foundational text for the ghoul's theological status in Islam. It establishes the creature as a negotiating agent rather than a beast, capable of speech, deceit, familial obligation, oath-breaking and bargaining; and it makes Ayat al-Kursi the standard household apotropaic in Muslim practice down to the present day — a devotional habit of enormous scale derived from a monster's ransom

Umar ibn al-Khattab Slays a Ghoul on the Road to Syriaal-Mas'udi, Muruj al-Dhahab (The Meadows of Gold), c. 947 CE

7th century narrative; recorded 10th century

Travelling to Syria before his caliphate, Umar ibn al-Khattab encounters a ghoul on the road and kills it with his sword. Al-Mas'udi reports the event as history, without hedging, in a work of serious universal historiography, and the episode became the standard proof-text for the ghoul's objective existence in later Islamic scholarship. The story gave ghoul-belief the endorsement of the second Rashidun caliph, making denial of the creature theologically awkward for traditionists and forcing the Mu'tazilite denial into a reinterpretive rather than dismissive posture. It also established the sword as an effective weapon, against the more common tradition in which only verbal and religious countermeasures work.

The Prophet's Instruction on Night Travel and the AdhanHadith preserved in the lexicographical and zoological tradition; cited in Lisan al-Arab s.v. غول and al-Damiri, Hayat al-hayawan al-kubra

7th century narrative; classical transmission through the 14th century

The Prophet instructs his followers: 'Travel by night, for the earth is folded up at night' — and adds that when the ghouls shapeshift upon them (idha taghawwalat lakum al-ghilan) they should hasten to make the call to prayer. A companion prohibition forbids camping or praying on the roadbed itself, 'for it is the lair of snakes and beasts of prey'. The instruction is practical rather than mythological in register: it treats the ghoul as one hazard among several in a taxonomy of road dangers that also includes serpents and predators. Established the adhan as the ghoul's countermeasure and, more broadly, embedded the creature permanently in the practical piety of travel. Al-Damiri's inclusion of the tradition in the first systematic Arabic zoological encyclopedia (c. 1371) formalised the ghoul as a describable order of being within natural history rather than only within demonology.

The King's Son and the She-GhoulAlf Layla wa-Layla (One Thousand and One Nights), core Arabic stratum

compiled c. 9th–15th centuries; Galland's French edition 1704–1717

A prince is lured aside by a woman who claims to be in distress; she is a she-ghoul, and she conducts him toward her dwelling and her waiting offspring, intending to feed him to them. He escapes by invoking God. The tale is the only story in the core Arabic corpus of the Nights to feature a ghoul, and Galland's French rendering presents the creature essentially as an ogre — a substitution that domesticated the figure for a readership already equipped with Perrault. Because it is the sole core-stratum ghoul tale, this story bore the entire weight of the Arabic ghoul's transmission into European letters, and Galland's ogre-assimilation set the terms of the Western misreading. The invocation of God as the escape mechanism preserved the theological structure even as the ogre-framing erased it.

Accounts II
The Story of Sidi NoumanAntoine Galland, Les Mille et Une Nuits — an 'orphan tale' from the Galland stratum, associated with the Syrian narrator Hanna Diyab

narrated to Galland 1709; published in the Mille et Une Nuits 1704–1717

Sidi Nouman marries the beautiful Amine and is baffled that she eats almost nothing, taking her rice grain by grain with a bodkin. He follows her one night and finds her in a cemetery, feasting on a fresh corpse in the company of a ghoul. Confronted, she transforms him into a dog by sorcery; he is eventually restored and takes his revenge. The tale is the source of the enduringly reproduced Victorian engraving 'Amine Discovered with the Goule'. This was, in the assessment repeated across the comparative literature, the West's first conscious introduction to the ghoul, and it is the specific point of entry for graveyard habitation and corpse-eating — traits which Al-Rawi and the Finnish comparative tradition agree are absent from the Arabian creature. Every subsequent Western ghoul, from Beckford through Poe to Lovecraft, D&D and Fallout, descends from the image fixed in this tale. The Arabian ghoul was a desert road-predator; the Western ghoul is a cemetery scavenger because of Sidi Nouman.

Umm Ghula: The Ghoul as False KinOral tale-type documented by Dwight F. Reynolds and collected across the Arabic-speaking world; reported in the standard folkloric literature

attested in modern collection; of indeterminate antiquity

A ghoul appears to women and girls not as a seductress but as a long-lost female relative or an unassuming old woman, and is welcomed into the household under the obligations of kinship and hospitality. She is variously called Umm Ghula ('Mother Ghoul') or 'Our Aunt Ghoul'. The tale-type's engine is disbelief: the women or children of the household detect the imposture — often by the feet — and are not believed by the men, who are deceived through greed, arrogance or flattery. Survival depends on whether the warning is heeded. Reynolds's analysis established that the ghoul is a gendered narrative instrument rather than a generic predator, encoding a specific critique about whose testimony is credited within the household. The tale-type is also the primary vehicle by which the ghoul survives as a living figure in Arabic childhood — in North Africa occupying roughly the position of the European big bad wolf.

Pickman's ModelH. P. Lovecraft, 'Pickman's Model', Weird Tales, October 1927 (written September 1926)

1926–1927

The narrator Thurber recounts why he broke with the Boston painter Richard Upton Pickman, whose canvases of ghouls were unbearably convincing. In a cellar studio in the North End, above an ancient well connecting to forgotten tunnels, Pickman shows him a painting of an enormous red-eyed, canine-faced ghoul chewing on a human victim. Thurber flees; later he finds that the curled paper he snatched from the canvas was not a sketch but a photograph. The ghouls in the tale abduct human infants and leave changelings, and the transformation from human to ghoul proceeds through an exclusive diet of the dead. Fixed the Anglophone visual image of the ghoul — doglike face, rubbery hide, hunched loping frame, red eyes — more decisively than any Arabic source, and supplied the template for the Dungeons & Dragons ghoul and thence for the entire modern gaming tradition. Lovecraft's ghouls retain one genuine Arabic trait that most Western versions discard: they are gregarious and can hold intelligent conversation.

Vathek and the Ghouls of the Palace of Underground FireWilliam Beckford, Vathek (composed in French 1782; Henley's English translation published 1786 as An Arabian Tale, From an Unpublished Manuscript)

1782–1786

The caliph Vathek, driven by forbidden knowledge and appetite, descends by degrees into damnation, culminating in the subterranean Palace of Underground Fire ruled by Eblis, where the damned wander with their hearts eternally aflame. Ghouls haunt the tale's cemeteries and waste places, and Henley's translation gives the word its first appearance in English. The 1786 English text is the first attestation of 'ghoul' in the language and installed the creature in the Gothic imagination as an emblem of transgressive appetite and desecration. Beckford's ghouls are Galland's cemetery-dwellers rather than Arabia's road-predators, so the English word entered the language already carrying the translator's error.

Night of the Living Dead and the Renaming of the GhoulGeorge A. Romero (dir.), Night of the Living Dead, Image Ten, 1968

1968

Reanimated corpses besiege a Pennsylvania farmhouse and consume the living. The film's dialogue, radio broadcasts and Romero's own account call them 'ghouls'; the word 'zombie' does not appear. Romero drew on Richard Matheson's I Am Legend and later stated flatly that he never called his creatures zombies. Critics writing in Cahiers du Cinéma applied the Haitian term retroactively, and it stuck. The single most consequential act of monster-misnaming in modern popular culture. The global zombie — the shambling, flesh-eating, virally propagated revenant that now anchors an entire genre across film, television, games and literature — is by direct descent a ghoul that was relabelled by reviewers. The Arabic root gh-w-l therefore stands, at one remove, behind The Walking Dead, Resident Evil and 28 Days Later.

Whether ghouls exist at all — the medieval Islamic reality debateTraditionist position: ghouls are real beings within the created order. Hadith preserved in Sunan al-Tirmidhi and elsewhere prescribe specific countermeasures (the adhan, Ayat al-Kursi, the invocation of God's name), which would be incoherent against a non-existent threat; al-Mas'udi reports Umar ibn al-Khattab's slaying of a ghoul as plain history in the Muruj al-Dhahab; al-Damiri gives the creature an entry in the first systematic Arabic zoological encyclopedia (c. 1371); al-Dimashqi describes its cave ecology; and the earliest layer of Arabic poetry, above all Ta'abbata Sharra's ode, presents first-person eyewitness testimony.Rationalist position: ghouls do not exist and belief in them is pre-Islamic superstition. The Qur'an never mentions the creature. The Mu'tazila denied it categorically, and al-Jahiz — himself Mu'tazilite — specifically ridiculed Ta'abbata Sharra's boast as a fabrication. A countervailing hadith tradition has the Prophet declaring 'la ghula' (there is no ghoul), which the Polish transmission records as an outright condemnation of the belief. Most contemporary ulama follow this line and reinterpret Umar's encounter metaphorically as the killing of a highway bandit.
Origin of the word and the creature — native Arabic or Mesopotamian importNative Arabic formation: the word derives transparently from the productive triliteral root غ-و-ل, whose verb ghala means 'to seize, snatch away, destroy', generating a coherent semantic family (ightiyal, 'assassination'; gha'ila, 'hidden calamity'; taghawwul, 'shapeshifting/leading astray'). Ibn Sida's definition — 'everything that snatches a human away and destroys him is a ghul' — is a definition by root, not by imported referent, and the creature's whole profile is derived from the specific hazards of Arabian desert travel.Mesopotamian derivation: Ahmed Al-Rawi argues the ghoul descends from the Akkadian and Sumerian gallu, underworld demons who seize victims and drag them below, transmitted via early Bedouin–Akkadian trade contact. Turkish comparative scholarship extends this to the whole Sumerian '-hul' demon series (Utuk Hul, Gidim Hul, Ala Hul, Maskim Hul), and the Turkic Gulyabani complex shows the phonetic and functional continuity such a derivation predicts. On this reading the Arabic root supplied a convenient home for a borrowed being.
Whether grave-robbing and corpse-eating are Arabian traitsWestern accretion: the Arabian ghul preys on living travellers on the desert road; it is a hazard of movement, not of burial grounds. The Finnish comparative treatment states explicitly that grave-robbing is a vice of the Western ghoul which the Arabian creature lacks, and Al-Rawi's 2009 Folklore study identifies Galland's Mille et Une Nuits as the precise point at which cemetery habitation and corpse-feasting were introduced, with 'The Story of Sidi Nouman' — an orphan tale with no pre-Galland Arabic manuscript — as the vector.Genuinely attested, at least regionally: the Central Asian and Kazakh transmissions describe a ghoul that subsists on graves in forests, swamps, disused wells and battlefields, arriving in the company of ravens — a corpse-dependency documented independently of any European contact. The hyena association, present in the earliest Arabic and Hebrew sources, carries grave-robbing as one of its constituent traits. On this view Galland amplified and relocated an existing minor motif rather than inventing it.
The ghoul's sex and its relationship to si'latOriginally and essentially feminine: in the earliest Arabic strata the noun is grammatically and conceptually feminine, and the Italian treatment holds that ghul are exclusively female against jinn of both sexes. The living oral tradition documented by Reynolds is overwhelmingly female — Umm Ghula, 'Our Aunt Ghoul' — and the tale-type's whole social logic depends on the creature approaching as a woman.A masculine base term with derived feminine: later Arabic settles ghul as masculine with ghula supplied as the feminine, and si'lat as a distinct female counterpart. A competing lexicographic scheme makes si'lat female and qutrub male beneath ghul as a superordinate category, while a fourth position holds si'lat to be categorically distinct — defined by an invariable fixed form, the exact inverse of the ghoul's protean nature. No single scheme commands consensus across the lexicographers.
What the ghoul encodes sociallyA rationalised account of real desert hazards and real criminality: the ghoul is the monsterisation of the highway bandit who ambushes travellers, the confidence trickster who impersonates a friend, and the grave-robber. Japanese comparative treatments make this the primary reading, and it is reinforced by the mainstream modern ulama interpretation of Umar's ghoul as a highwayman, and by the 1667 Mecca incident in which a hyena attack was reported as a ghoul's work.A mechanism of social exclusion: Al-Rawi argues that ghoul-belief was sustained by the ostracism of congenitally deformed desert-dwellers and their mothers, conditions he attributes to endogamy and malnutrition, with the Catalan transmission preserving this reading most explicitly. Japanese material adds that ghoul-accusation functioned as a witch-hunt mechanism against individuals, particularly women. On this reading the ghoul is not a description of a danger but an instrument for producing outcasts.
Whether the modern zombie is a ghoulYes, by direct descent: Romero's Night of the Living Dead (1968) calls its revenants 'ghouls' in dialogue and radio broadcast, the word 'zombie' appears nowhere in the film, and Romero stated plainly that he never thought of them as zombies. Critics at Cahiers du Cinema applied the Haitian term retroactively. The entire global zombie genre is therefore terminologically a ghoul lineage that has forgotten its own name.No, they are distinct traditions that briefly shared a label: the zombie's substantive content — reanimated corpse, contagion, loss of self, mass horde — derives from Haitian Vodou and from Richard Matheson's I Am Legend, not from Arabic folklore. Romero's usage was a loose English horror-word for 'flesh-eating monster', already detached from Arabia by two centuries of Gothic drift. The genre's actual genealogy runs through Vodou and Cold War contagion anxiety, and the 1968 word choice is an accident of vocabulary rather than a line of descent.
Whether Algol was named for observed variabilityThe name records observation: ra's al-ghul, 'the head of the ghoul', is uniquely demonic among Arabic star names, and the star's regular winking — a drop of more than a magnitude every 2.87 days, visible to the unaided eye — is the obvious reason a culture of night travellers would single it out and name it after a shapeshifter.The name records position, not behaviour: al-Sufi's Book of Fixed Stars (c. 964), the most careful Arabic stellar catalogue, says nothing whatever about Algol's variability, and the star occupies in the Greek tradition the position of the Gorgon's severed head in Perseus's grip — a demonic name inherited from Greek iconography rather than derived from Arabic observation. The eclipsing nature was first noted by Montanari in 1667 and explained by Goodricke in 1783.
