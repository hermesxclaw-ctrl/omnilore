Who It Is

Before there were books, before there was any script at all, there was a god who measured the moon's light in fractions and gambled for the fate of days. Thoth — ibis-headed, baboon-bodied, the scribe who uses the universe as his page — is the deity who holds the pen that writes reality. He is not the warrior-kings of Olympus nor the storm-gods of the north; he is patient, calculating, utterly precise. His domain is the quiet space where numbers become magic and words become law. To understand the Egyptian cosmos is to understand that it was not only created but written — and Thoth is the one who never stops writing.

Thoth is depicted as calm, rational, precise, and measured — the archetypal scribe and counselor. He is never hasty or impulsive, preferring deliberation and calculation. Unlike the warrior gods (Set, Horus) or the generative gods (Osiris, Geb), Thoth operates through words, numbers, and hidden knowledge. He is described as 'self-begotten' and 'self-produced' — an autodidactic creator god who knows all things. In the Pyramid Texts, he is 'the powerful one of the Ennead.' Powers
Divine Scribe / Inscription of Reality

Thoth is the recorder and author of all things. He writes the decrees of the gods, records the deeds of all beings, and inscribes the outcomes of the Weighing of the Heart. His written word is performative — what he inscribes becomes reality. The Book of Thoth (multiple texts attributed to him) contains the spells that control the universe, the names of all beings, and the secrets of creation.

Limit: He does not create ex nihilo in most accounts — he records and orders preexisting reality. His writings require the existence of Ma'at (truth/order) as their foundation.

Lunar Manipulation / Calendrical Control

Thoth gambled with the Moon for 1/72nd of its light (360/72 = 5 days) and won, adding five days to the 360-day year so his mother Nut could bear children. He controls the lunar cycle, regulating the month and measuring time itself. His lunar disk and crescent are the guarantors of calendar accuracy.

Limit: The gambling myth appears first in Plutarch (Greek period); the earlier Egyptian tradition likely portrayed Thoth's calendrical role differently.

Omniscience / Universal Knowledge

Thoth knows all things — the names of the gods, the spells of power, the hidden mechanisms of the cosmos, the medical properties of plants, the movements of the stars, the secrets of the soul. He is 'the Lord of Books' and 'the Reckoner of the Universe.' He taught humanity hieroglyphs, mathematics, astronomy, medicine, and all civilized arts.

Limit: His knowledge is not always directly accessible to humans — it must be sought through study, initiation, or divine revelation.

Weighing of the Heart / Psychopomp Judgement

In the Hall of Two Truths, Thoth records the outcome of the Weighing of the Heart ceremony. He places a feather of Ma'at on one side of the scale and the deceased's heart on the other. If the scale balances, the soul passes; if not, Ammit devours it. However, Thoth has the authority to replace an imperilled heart with a new one if the deceased understands the correct spells (Book of the Dead, Chapter 30B).

Limit: He does not judge — he records. Judgment belongs to Osiris and the Forty-Two Assessors of Ma'at. Thoth's role is scribal, not adjudicative.

Mastery of Magic (Heka)

Thoth is credited with knowledge of the heka — the magical force that underlies the universe. He taught Isis the secret words to resurrect Osiris. The Book of Thoth contains spells that raise the dead, transform matter, and command the gods. In the Hermetic tradition, this becomes the basis for theurgy and alchemy.

Limit: Magical knowledge must be used in accordance with Ma'at. Misuse causes chaos.

Mediation / Divine Arbitration

Thoth serves as the mediator and counselor among the gods. In the Contendings of Horus and Set, he advises Ra and writes letters of reconciliation. He stands on Ra's solar barque as his counsellor. His measured, rational temperament makes him an ideal arbiter of godly disputes.

Limit: His counsel can be ignored (as when Set continues his destructive rampage despite advice).

Self-Creation / Self-Begotten

In the Hermopolitan creation myth, Thoth is self-begotten — he came into existence through his own power, alongside the Ogdoad. In some traditions, he was born from the lips of Ra (emerging as speech from the sun god's mouth). In the Book of the Dead, the scribe proclaims 'I am the writing palette of Thoth.'

Limit: Self-creation is contested: he exists from the beginning in some accounts but is a product of Ra in others.

Equilibrium / Balance (as A'an)

In his baboon form A'an (or Aani), Thoth personifies equilibrium. He sits atop the scales in the Hall of Two Truths, ensuring just measure. He holds up the crescent moon as a symbol of cosmic balance. The baboon's posture (sitting with raised arms) was read by Egyptians as mirroring the gesture of a balance.

Limit: This aspect is primarily eschatological — it operates in the afterlife rather than in daily life.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Thoth is most commonly depicted as a man with the head of an African sacred ibis (Threskiornis aethiopicus), identifiable by its curved black beak, white body plumage, and black wingtips. In this form, he is shown wearing a headdress consisting of a lunar disk perched atop a crescent moon, symbolizing his lunar nature and his role as the reckoner of times and seasons. His sacred colors are white (ibis plumage, lunar light), green (the green ibis was a specific sacred type), and silver (associated with the moon). Accounts I
The Birth of the Five Epagomenal DaysPlutarch, De Iside et Osiride, c. 100 CE

Ptolemaic-Egyptian (recorded in 1st-2nd c. CE)

The sky goddess Nut was cursed by Ra to remain barren on all 360 days of the year. Thoth gambled with the Moon (Khonsu in some versions, Iah in others) for 1/72nd of its light — that is, five seventytwoths of 360 degrees, equalling five days. Thoth won, adding five intercalary days (the epagomenal days) to the calendar. During these five 'extra' days, Nut gave birth successively to Osiris, Horus the Elder, Set, Isis, and Nephthys. This story explains both the five gods' existence and the origin of the 365-day Egyptian civil year. Establishes Thoth as the regulator of time, the creator of the calendar, and the indirect cause of the central Osiris-myth generation. The last of the five days, the birthday of Nephthys, was considered particularly unlucky.

Thoth and the Weighing of the HeartBook of the Dead, Chapter 125 (Papyrus of Ani, c. 1250 BCE)

New Kingdom (18th-19th Dynasty)

In the Hall of Two Truths, the deceased stands before Osiris. Anubis adjusts the scales: the heart of the deceased on one side, the feather of Ma'at on the other. Thoth, in his baboon form Aani, records the measurement. If the scale balances, Thoth announces the result to the gods, and the soul proceeds to the Field of Reeds. In Chapter 30B of the Book of the Dead, Thoth instructs the deceased on a magical formula that allows him to retrieve his heart if it is found wanting — essentially granting an appeal. The formula says: 'O my heart of my mother, O my heart of my mother, O my heart of my transformations... Do not stand as a witness against me, do not oppose me in the judgment.' Establishes Thoth as the critical intermediary between death and paradise. The Weighing of the Heart scene is the most frequently reproduced image of Thoth in funerary art. The formula for heart retrieval became one of the most popular spells in the Book of the Dead.

Thoth Restores Isis and Resurrects OsirisPyramid Texts, Coffin Texts, and Plutarch's De Iside et Osiride

Old Kingdom (Pyramid Texts) through Ptolemaic (Plutarch)

After Set murdered Osiris and scattered his body, Isis searched for the fourteen (or sixteen) pieces. Thoth, moved by Isis's grief and devotion, gave her the 'words of power' (the secret magical formulae) needed to reconstruct and resurrect Osiris's body, and to conceive Horus. Thoth also oversaw the process of judgment that ultimately vindicated Osiris as king of the underworld. In some versions, Thoth wrote the Opening of the Mouth ritual that allowed Osiris to speak and breathe again. This sequence establishes Thoth's mastery over death magic and his alliance with the Osirian cycle. Thoth's gift of the resurrection formula makes him the patron of all Egyptian mortuary magic. It establishes the template for the resurrection hope in Egyptian religion and cements Thoth's role as a benevolent force opposed to Set's chaos.

Accounts II
Thoth Mediates the Contendings of Horus and SetChester Beatty Papyrus I (c. 1185 BCE, 20th Dynasty)

New Kingdom, Ramesside period

Horus and Set wage an eighty-year legal battle before the Ennead for the throne of Egypt. The goddesses and gods take sides — Ra favors Set; the Ennead favors Horus. Thoth acts as the primary scribe and legal advisor, writing letters of inquiry, recording testimony, and offering measured counsel. He proposes multiple compromise solutions, including settling the dispute by consulting the Neith oracle. Ultimately, Thoth writes the final decree awarding the throne to Horus. Throughout the tale, Thoth is the voice of reason, opposing Set's violence and Ra's indecision with patient scribal authority. Establishes Thoth as the constitutional mediator of the divine realm. The story reinforces the principle that law and writing triumph over violence and chaos. Thoth's role as arbitrator becomes the template for his later syncretic identification with Hermes, god of communication and negotiation.

Thoth and King Thamus: Plato's Critique of WritingPlato, Phaedrus 274c-275b (c. 370 BCE)

Classical Greek

In Plato's dialogue, Socrates recounts a myth: The god Theuth (Thoth) came to King Thamus of Egyptian Thebes and demonstrated his inventions — number, calculation, geometry, astronomy, draughts, dice, and especially writing. 'This invention, O king,' said Theuth, 'will make the Egyptians wiser and will improve their memories, for it is an elixir of memory and wisdom.' Thamus replied: 'You have not discovered an elixir of memory, but of reminding. This invention will produce forgetfulness in the souls of those who use it, because they will not practice their memory. You offer your students the appearance of wisdom, not true wisdom.' This dialogue established a long-standing philosophical debate about the nature of writing, memory, and wisdom that continues into modern media theory. It directly inverts Thoth's Egyptian role — rather than being the divine bestower of knowledge, his invention becomes suspect. The passage is still widely cited in philosophy, media ecology, and literacy studies.

The Ancient Egyptian Book of Thoth (Demotic Dialogue)Multiple fragmentary papyri, Ptolemaic period; critical edition by Jasnow and Zauzich (2005)

c. 300-30 BCE

A long Demotic text, preserved in over forty fragmentary copies, presents a dialogue between a human called 'The-one-who-loves-knowledge' (mr-rḫ, mer-ekh) and a divine entity addressed as 'The-one-who-praises-knowledge' (Ḥsr-rḫ, Heser-ekh), understood to be Thoth himself. The dialogue covers the nature of the cosmos, the structure of temples, the names and functions of gods, the hierarchies of the universe, the judgment of the dead, and the esoteric knowledge required for priestly initiation. The text represents the most sustained Egyptian philosophical treatise in Demotic and demonstrates that native Egyptian theological speculation continued at a sophisticated level into the Ptolemaic period, despite Greek cultural dominance. This text revolutionized modern understanding of Late Period Egyptian theology, showing that the Hermetic tradition may have genuinely Egyptian roots rather than being purely Greek invention. It confirms Thoth's continued importance as a teacher-god in the first millennium BCE.
