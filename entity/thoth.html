Thoth, the lord of writing, who knows the words of power and records the deeds of all beings in his eternal scroll.

Who They Are

Thoth is the ibis-headed god of writing, knowledge, magic, and the moon. He serves as the divine scribe, recording the decrees of the gods, measuring time, and recording the results of the Weighing of the Heart ceremony. He is the inventor of hieroglyphs, mathematics, astronomy, and all sciences — the 'Lord of Books' and the 'Reckoner of Time.' Thoth is self-created (in some traditions, born from the lips of Ra) and acts as mediator between gods, counselor to Ra, and defender of Osiris in his judgment. He is depicted as a man with the head of an ibis, holding a writing palette and reed pen, or as a baboon (his sacred animal). His cult center was at Hermopolis (Khmunu). In the Greek period, Thoth was identified with Hermes Trismegistus, the founder of Hermetic philosophy. He replaces the heart of a deceased person if it is found wanting at judgment, ensuring the soul's passage. Thoth's wisdom was considered so vast that he knew the secrets of the universe.

Attested in Pyramid Texts / Book of the Dead / Hermetic CorpusEra Old Kingdom to Roman Egypt (c. 2600 BCE-400 CE)

Form

Thoth is most commonly depicted as a man with the head of an African sacred ibis (Threskiornis aethiopicus), identifiable by its curved black beak, white body plumage, and black wingtips. In this form, he is shown wearing a headdress consisting of a lunar disk perched atop a crescent moon, symbolizing his lunar nature and his role as the reckoner of times and seasons. He often carries an ankh (symbol of life) in one hand and a writing palette with a reed pen in the other. He may also be shown directly as a full ibis, particularly in amuletic or votive form. Alternatively, Thoth appears as a dog-faced or hamadryas baboon (Papio hamadryas), especially in his role as A'an, the god of equilibrium, where he holds up a crescent moon. As Iah-Djehuty (a lunar aspect), he takes a more fully human form. On rare occasions, he is depicted wearing the Atef crown or the double crown of Upper and Lower Egypt. In the Papyrus of Ani, he is shown with a scribal palette and reed pen, recording the judgment scene. In the Greco-Roman period, he is sometimes depicted as Hermes Trismegistus — a fully human bearded philosopher figure holding a caduceus and a scroll.

Powers

Divine Scribe / Inscription of Reality Thoth is the recorder and author of all things. He writes the decrees of the gods, records the deeds of all beings, and inscribes the outcomes of the Weighing of the Heart. His written word is performative — what he inscribes becomes reality. The Book of Thoth (multiple texts attributed to him) contains the spells that control the universe, the names of all beings, and the secrets of creation. Pyramid Texts through Ptolemaic; Hermetic corpus He does not create ex nihilo in most accounts — he records and orders preexisting reality. His writings require the existence of Ma'at (truth/order) as their foundation. Some traditions present Thoth as the creator of writing and thus of recorded history itself; others (Plato's Phaedrus) present Thoth as merely the inventor of letters, not of memory or wisdom.

Lunar Manipulation / Calendrical Control Thoth gambled with the Moon for 1/72nd of its light (360/72 = 5 days) and won, adding five days to the 360-day year so his mother Nut could bear children. He controls the lunar cycle, regulating the month and measuring time itself. His lunar disk and crescent are the guarantors of calendar accuracy. Plutarch, De Iside et Osiride (c. 100 CE); Pyramid Texts (lunar aspects) The gambling myth appears first in Plutarch (Greek period); the earlier Egyptian tradition likely portrayed Thoth's calendrical role differently. The 360→365 calendar story is a Greek-era rationalization. Earlier Egyptian evidence shows the civil calendar of 365 days was in use by the Old Kingdom.

Omniscience / Universal Knowledge Thoth knows all things — the names of the gods, the spells of power, the hidden mechanisms of the cosmos, the medical properties of plants, the movements of the stars, the secrets of the soul. He is 'the Lord of Books' and 'the Reckoner of the Universe.' He taught humanity hieroglyphs, mathematics, astronomy, medicine, and all civilized arts. Book of the Dead, Hermetic corpus, Greek interpretations His knowledge is not always directly accessible to humans — it must be sought through study, initiation, or divine revelation. The extent of Thoth's omniscience varies: some texts imply he knows everything; others suggest even he cannot change some fated outcomes.

Weighing of the Heart / Psychopomp Judgement In the Hall of Two Truths, Thoth records the outcome of the Weighing of the Heart ceremony. He places a feather of Ma'at on one side of the scale and the deceased's heart on the other. If the scale balances, the soul passes; if not, Ammit devours it. However, Thoth has the authority to replace an imperilled heart with a new one if the deceased understands the correct spells (Book of the Dead, Chapter 30B). Book of the Dead, Chapters 30B, 125 — attested from New Kingdom onward He does not judge — he records. Judgment belongs to Osiris and the Forty-Two Assessors of Ma'at. Thoth's role is scribal, not adjudicative. Some vignettes show Thoth actively participating in judgment; others show him as a neutral recorder. The distinction may reflect different theological schools.

Mastery of Magic (Heka) Thoth is credited with knowledge of the heka — the magical force that underlies the universe. He taught Isis the secret words to resurrect Osiris. The Book of Thoth contains spells that raise the dead, transform matter, and command the gods. In the Hermetic tradition, this becomes the basis for theurgy and alchemy. Osiris myth (Pyramid Texts onward); Book of Thoth; Hermetica Magical knowledge must be used in accordance with Ma'at. Misuse causes chaos. Whether Thoth invented magic or simply knows it is disputed across sources.

Mediation / Divine Arbitration Thoth serves as the mediator and counselor among the gods. In the Contendings of Horus and Set, he advises Ra and writes letters of reconciliation. He stands on Ra's solar barque as his counsellor. His measured, rational temperament makes him an ideal arbiter of godly disputes. Chester Beatty Papyrus I (Contendings of Horus and Set, c. 1185 BCE); Pyramid Texts His counsel can be ignored (as when Set continues his destructive rampage despite advice). In some versions, Thoth acts decisively; in others, he merely records the decisions of others.

Self-Creation / Self-Begotten In the Hermopolitan creation myth, Thoth is self-begotten — he came into existence through his own power, alongside the Ogdoad. In some traditions, he was born from the lips of Ra (emerging as speech from the sun god's mouth). In the Book of the Dead, the scribe proclaims 'I am the writing palette of Thoth.' Hermopolitan theology; Book of the Dead; Pyramid Texts Self-creation is contested: he exists from the beginning in some accounts but is a product of Ra in others. The Hermopolitan Ogdoad tradition makes Thoth co-eternal with the primordial waters. The Heliopolitan Ennead tradition subordinates him to Ra-Atum.

Equilibrium / Balance (as A'an) In his baboon form A'an (or Aani), Thoth personifies equilibrium. He sits atop the scales in the Hall of Two Truths, ensuring just measure. He holds up the crescent moon as a symbol of cosmic balance. The baboon's posture (sitting with raised arms) was read by Egyptians as mirroring the gesture of a balance. Book of the Dead; temple reliefs at Edfu and Dendera This aspect is primarily eschatological — it operates in the afterlife rather than in daily life. Some Egyptologists see A'an as a distinct god later absorbed by Thoth; others treat it as a title of Thoth himself.

Accounts

The sky goddess Nut was cursed by Ra to remain barren on all 360 days of the year. Thoth gambled with the Moon (Khonsu in some versions, Iah in others) for 1/72nd of its light — that is, five seventytwoths of 360 degrees, equalling five days. Thoth won, adding five intercalary days (the epagomenal days) to the calendar. During these five 'extra' days, Nut gave birth successively to Osiris, Horus the Elder, Set, Isis, and Nephthys. This story explains both the five gods' existence and the origin of the 365-day Egyptian civil year.

In the Hall of Two Truths, the deceased stands before Osiris. Anubis adjusts the scales: the heart of the deceased on one side, the feather of Ma'at on the other. Thoth, in his baboon form Aani, records the measurement. If the scale balances, Thoth announces the result to the gods, and the soul proceeds to the Field of Reeds. In Chapter 30B of the Book of the Dead, Thoth instructs the deceased on a magical formula that allows him to retrieve his heart if it is found wanting — essentially granting an appeal. The formula says: 'O my heart of my mother, O my heart of my mother, O my heart of my transformations... Do not stand as a witness against me, do not oppose me in the judgment.'

After Set murdered Osiris and scattered his body, Isis searched for the fourteen (or sixteen) pieces. Thoth, moved by Isis's grief and devotion, gave her the 'words of power' (the secret magical formulae) needed to reconstruct and resurrect Osiris's body, and to conceive Horus. Thoth also oversaw the process of judgment that ultimately vindicated Osiris as king of the underworld. In some versions, Thoth wrote the Opening of the Mouth ritual that allowed Osiris to speak and breathe again. This sequence establishes Thoth's mastery over death magic and his alliance with the Osirian cycle.

Horus and Set wage an eighty-year legal battle before the Ennead for the throne of Egypt. The goddesses and gods take sides — Ra favors Set; the Ennead favors Horus. Thoth acts as the primary scribe and legal advisor, writing letters of inquiry, recording testimony, and offering measured counsel. He proposes multiple compromise solutions, including settling the dispute by consulting the Neith oracle. Ultimately, Thoth writes the final decree awarding the throne to Horus. Throughout the tale, Thoth is the voice of reason, opposing Set's violence and Ra's indecision with patient scribal authority.

In Plato's dialogue, Socrates recounts a myth: The god Theuth (Thoth) came to King Thamus of Egyptian Thebes and demonstrated his inventions — number, calculation, geometry, astronomy, draughts, dice, and especially writing. 'This invention, O king,' said Theuth, 'will make the Egyptians wiser and will improve their memories, for it is an elixir of memory and wisdom.' Thamus replied: 'You have not discovered an elixir of memory, but of reminding. This invention will produce forgetfulness in the souls of those who use it, because they will not practice their memory. You offer your students the appearance of wisdom, not true wisdom.'

A long Demotic text, preserved in over forty fragmentary copies, presents a dialogue between a human called 'The-one-who-loves-knowledge' (mr-rḫ, mer-ekh) and a divine entity addressed as 'The-one-who-praises-knowledge' (Ḥsr-rḫ, Heser-ekh), understood to be Thoth himself. The dialogue covers the nature of the cosmos, the structure of temples, the names and functions of gods, the hierarchies of the universe, the judgment of the dead, and the esoteric knowledge required for priestly initiation. The text represents the most sustained Egyptian philosophical treatise in Demotic and demonstrates that native Egyptian theological speculation continued at a sophisticated level into the Ptolemaic period, despite Greek cultural dominance.

Content Notes

During the Late and Ptolemaic Periods (c. 664-30 BCE), the cult of Thoth at Hermopolis and Tuna el-Gebel engaged in the mass mummification of ibises. Millions of the birds were bred, killed, and mummified as votive offerings to Thoth. The ibis cemeteries at Tuna el-Gebel and Saqqara contain vast deposits of mummified ibises, packed into ceramic jars or individually wrapped. The scale implies industrial-scale breeding and slaughter facilities. The practice was economically significant but represents systematic animal sacrifice on an enormous scale.

Archaeological excavations at Tuna el-Gebel and Saqqara; Herodotus, Histories II.67 (regarding animal mummification)

Egyptological studies (Ikram 1995, 2004) have shown that many mummified ibises died through neck-breaking, starvation, or asphyxiation — methods consistent with sacrificial euthanasia rather than natural death. While animal mummification was framed as reverence for the god's sacred animal, the actual process involved mass killing.

Ikram, S. (2004) 'Beloved Beasts: Animal Mummies from Ancient Egypt'; Hourigan, E. (2011) 'Mummified Ibis: A Multi-Analytical Investigation'

Thoth's gifts — writing, mathematics, science — were strictly controlled by the priestly and scribal elite. Literacy in pharaonic Egypt was likely never more than 1-5% of the population, making Thoth's 'knowledge for all humanity' a privilege of a narrow scribal class. The hieroglyphic writing system itself was described by its practitioners as 'the words of the gods' (mdw nṯr, medu netjer), deliberately arcane.

Baines, J. (1983) 'Literacy and Ancient Egyptian Society'; numerous scribal texts emphasizing the exclusivity of Thoth's knowledge

From the Renaissance Hermetic revival (Ficino's translation of the Corpus Hermeticum, 1463) through Aleister Crowley's Thoth Tarot (1944) and contemporary New Age practices, Thoth has been extensively appropriated by Western esoteric traditions. These traditions often strip Thoth of his Egyptian cultural context, reframing him as a universal 'wisdom figure' or 'Ascended Master' in the Theosophical tradition. Mme Blavatsky's Theosophy identified Thoth as a 'Master of the Ancient Wisdom' alongside other appropriated non-European deities. This pattern of extraction extracts religious meaning from its cultural origins for Western spiritual consumption.

Hornung, E. (2001) The Secret Lore of Egypt: Its Impact on the West; Hanegraaff, W. (1996) New Age Religion and Western Culture

As a god of magic, Thoth was sometimes invoked in protective and also destructive magical contexts. Some texts associate him with the sending of plagues or scorpion stings as divine punishment, though this role is far less developed than the plague associations of Sekhmet or Resheph. The Metternich Stela (c. 360 BCE) depicts Horus healed of scorpion venom through Thoth's magical intercession — framing Thoth as primarily a healer rather than a plague-sender.

Metternich Stela (CGC 9401); Borghouts, J.F. (1978) Ancient Egyptian Magical Texts

What We Don't Know

1. Pronunciation reconstruction: the exact vocalization of ḏḥwty remains uncertain despite multiple proposed reconstructions. Disagreement persists about the pharyngeal stops. 2. The gambling-with-the-moon narrative: known only from Plutarch's Greek account. No pre-Greek Egyptian source for this specific story has been identified. Further research needed in Ptolemaic temple inscriptions at Dendera and Edfu. 3. Demotic Book of Thoth: approximately 60% of known fragments remain untranslated or only partially published. The complete text may be substantially longer than the Jasnow-Zauzich reconstruction. 4. Western esoteric reception: the full influence network of Thoth-Hermes in alchemical, Rosicrucian, and Theosophical traditions is beyond the scope of a single dossier. A companion dossier on Hermes Trismegistus would be desirable. 5. Artistic representations: limited access to museum collections (British Museum, Louvre, Egyptian Museum Cairo) for first-hand iconographic verification. The descriptions rely on published catalogues and secondary sources. 6. Temple of Thoth at Hermopolis: the structure was demolished for sugar-factory fill in the mid-19th century. Very little archaeological documentation survives. Foundation deposits and loose blocks may remain unexcavated. 7. No Arabic-language or modern Egyptian sources were consulted — Thoth's cultural afterlife in Egyptian Islam and Coptic Christianity is a gap.

A way to hold it in your mind

Before there were books, before there was any script at all, there was a god who measured the moon's light in fractions and gambled for the fate of days. Thoth — ibis-headed, baboon-bodied, the scribe who uses the universe as his page — is the deity who holds the pen that writes reality. He is not the warrior-kings of Olympus nor the storm-gods of the north; he is patient, calculating, utterly precise. His domain is the quiet space where numbers become magic and words become law. To understand the Egyptian cosmos is to understand that it was not only created but written — and Thoth is the one who never stops writing.
