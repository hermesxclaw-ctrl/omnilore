Who It Is

Inti is not merely a sun god -- he is the axis upon which the entire Inca Empire turned. Every political institution, agricultural calendar, and royal ceremony was organized around his radiance. Understanding Inti means understanding how a civilization without writing, money, or draft animals built the largest empire in pre-Columbian America: by harnessing the one force that no Andean community could do without -- sunlight.

Inti governs the daily movement of the sun across the sky, rising in the east and setting in the west. He is associated with warmth, growth, and agricultural fertility. During the Inti Raymi festival, his presence is invoked through elaborate ceremonies involving chicha (corn beer) libations, animal sacrifices (especially llamas and guinea pigs), and the burning of coca leaves. The Sapa Inca would publicly drink chicha with Inti as a ritual act of communion. Accounts I
Manco Cápac and Mama Ocllo: The Founding of CuscoGarcilaso de la Vega, Comentarios Reales (1609); Sarmiento de Gamboa, History of the Incas (1572)

According to the most widely known Inca origin myth, Inti and Mama Quilla sent their children Manco Cápac and Mama Ocllo (Mama Uqllu) from Lake Titicaca to civilize the peoples of the Andes. Inti gave Manco Cápac a golden staff (tupayachi) and instructed the siblings to travel until the staff sank into the earth — the signal that they had found the site of their future capital. At what is now Cusco, the staff sank, and Manco Cápac and Mama Ocllo established the city, taught the people agriculture, weaving, and religion, and founded the Inca dynasty. In an alternative variant, Manco Cápac was raised from Lake Titicaca directly by Inti, not born of Inti and Quilla.

Pachacuti and the Expansion of the Sun CultMartín de Murúa, Historia general del Piru; Rowe, Ann Pollard, 'Inca Policies and Institutions'

The ninth Sapa Inca, Pachacuti (ruled 1438–1471), is credited with elevating Inti from a regional solar deity to the imperial patron of Tawantinsuyu. According to chroniclers, Pachacuti reformed state religion, built and expanded the Coricancha in Cusco, and institutionalized the Inti Raymi festival on a pan-imperial scale. He reorganized the priesthood of Inti under the Willaq Umu (High Priest of the Sun), creating a parallel administrative hierarchy to the secular government. Pachacuti's reforms linked Inti worship directly to the imperial mita labor system, ensuring that every province contributed to the sun temple's maintenance.

Accounts II
The Ayllu and the Panaca: Kinship under IntiMcEwan, Gordon Francis, The Incas: New Perspectives (2006); Guaman Poma de Ayala, Nueva corónica (1615)

Inca society was organized into ayllu (kin groups) and panaka (royal descent groups). Each Sapa Inca founded a panaka to maintain his mummy (mallqui) and estate, with the group responsible for continuing his worship — which was fundamentally worship of Inti embodied in the deceased ruler. Upon death, the Sapa Inca was believed to join Inti in the sky, and his mummy was consulted on state matters. This created a continuous living institution where Inti's solar authority mediated between the dead and the living, and between the royal lineages and the common people. The ayllu system distributed land from Inti's storehouses (qullqa) based on family size and labor contribution, reinforcing the sun god's role as economic guarantor.

The Spanish Extirpation and Survival of Inti WorshipCobo, Bernabé, Historia del Nuevo Mundo (1653); Millones, Luis, El retoño de la oscuridad; scattered colonial tribunal records

After the Spanish conquest began in 1532, Catholic authorities systematically targeted Inti worship. The Coricancha was looted for its gold plating and converted into the Church of Santo Domingo. The Inti Raymi festival was banned as idolatrous, and the Spanish destroyed solar shrines across the empire. Yet Inti worship proved remarkably resilient. In remote Andean communities, solar rituals continued under the guise of Catholic saints — the Virgin of the Sun (Virgen del Sol) absorbed many of Inti's attributes. The Quechua term inti was retained in colonial religious syncretism, and the Pleiades cycle (Qullqa) continued to govern agricultural calendars in rural Peru and Bolivia. Modern Andean spirituality still invokes Inti as a living force.

The Disappearance of Manco Cápac and the Division of the SunMolina, Cristóbal de, Relación de las fábulas y ritos de los Incas (1575); Arriaga, Pablo José de, La extirpación de la idolatría del Piru (1621)

In one myth variant, Manco Cápac — son of Inti — disappeared into the earth after completing his civilizing mission, splitting into multiple huacas (sacred objects) that continued Inti's presence on earth. This myth explains the Inca practice of dividing Inti's identity into multiple solar aspects: Apu Inti (the sun as mountain spirit), Churi Inti (the son-sun), and Inti Raymi (the festival-sun). The division allowed the Inca to worship Inti through multiple institutional channels simultaneously — the state cult at Coricancha, the royal panaka mummies, and the agricultural calendar rites — without contradiction.
