Inti

I rise every morning not for myself but for the corn, the potato, the children of the sun. The Sapa Inca is my son. The empire is my garden. The gold of Cusco is the color of my skin.

Who It Is

Inti is the Inca sun god — the divine ancestor of the Inca royal dynasty and the focus of the state-sponsored religion that legitimized Inca imperial rule. While Viracocha was the transcendent creator, Inti was the immanent, visible, daily source of life, warmth, and agricultural fertility. Every Inca emperor (Sapa Inca) claimed direct descent from Inti, making the sun god not merely a deity to be worshipped but the source of political authority itself. Inti's worship was centered at the Coricancha (Temple of the Sun) in Cusco, whose walls were once covered in sheets of gold to reflect the sun's radiance.

Physically, Inti was represented in Inca art as a golden disc with a human face, from which rays of light emanated. The most sacred representation — the Punchao (a golden disc image of the sun) — was kept in the Coricancha and brought out for major ceremonies. After the Spanish conquest, the Punchao was seized and reportedly sent to Spain, though it has since been lost. In earlier periods, Inti was associated with the condor (the great Andean bird that soars closest to the sun) and was sometimes depicted in textiles and metalwork as a radiant, anthropomorphic figure.

Inti's relationship with the Inca royal dynasty was central to imperial ideology. According to the origin myth, Inti took pity on humanity living in darkness and ignorance. He sent his son Manco Capac and his daughter Mama Ocllo to emerge from the waters of Lake Titicaca (or from a cave at Pacaritambo in another version) and travel until a golden staff they carried sank into the earth. At Cusco, the staff sank, and there they founded the Inca capital. Manco Capac became the first Sapa Inca, and all subsequent Inca rulers traced their lineage directly to Inti through him. This divine descent justified Inca rule: the Sapa Inca was Inti's representative on earth, and resistance to Inca authority was resistance to the sun god himself.

Inti's worship was the most elaborate in the Inca religious calendar. The Inti Raymi (Festival of the Sun) — celebrated on the winter solstice (June in the Southern Hemisphere) — was the year's most important ceremony. The Sapa Inca, nobles, priests, and thousands of subjects gathered in Cusco's main plaza. The mummies of previous Inca emperors were brought out and seated in a semicircle. Llama sacrifices were offered, and the Sapa Inca poured a ceremonial libation of chicha (corn beer) to Inti from a golden vessel. The festival lasted nine days and concluded with the kindling of the new sacred fire, using a parabolic mirror to focus the sun's rays — a technology that impressed the Spanish chroniclers. The modern Inti Raymi, revived in Cusco in 1944, is now a major cultural event drawing thousands of visitors.

Inti's domain extended beyond mere light to encompass the entire agricultural cycle. The sun's position determined the planting and harvest calendar, and Inti's warmth was understood as the sustaining force that made crops grow. The Inca built numerous solar observatories (such as the ‘Hitching Post of the Sun' at Machu Picchu, a carved stone aligned with the solstices) to track Inti's movements and plan agricultural activities. The sun's daily journey — rising in the east, crossing the sky, setting in the west, and passing through the underworld at night — was understood as Inti's eternal labor on behalf of humanity.
