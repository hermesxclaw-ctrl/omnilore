Who It Is

Before 1896, no one in English went on a date. Young people called, courted, or kept company — always under a parent's eye in the parlor. Then working-class couples slipped out to dance halls and cheap restaurants, a cashier kept a little Date Book of her suitors, and a throwaway slang word quietly rewired how half the planet finds love.

Attested behavior: intentional, planned, and evaluative. Dating is framed as low-commitment exploration with romantic or intimate possibility, distinct from committed relationship or friendship, though it may lead to either. Norms govern initiation (historically male-initiated and male-paid in American script; increasingly negotiated), self-presentation (impression management, selective disclosure), communication (flirtation, ambient intimacy via likes/comments/mutuals), and exit (ghosting, debriefing with friends). Powers
Evaluative Partner Assessment

Provides a low-commitment frame to observe, converse with, and assess a potential partner's values, compatibility, attraction and life goals through shared experience. Functions as a sorting mechanism before higher-commitment stages.

Limit: Assessment is noisy: impression management, selective self-presentation, and brief encounters limit predictive validity for long-term compatibility. Algorithms can amplify bias.

Identity and Personal Growth

Expands self-understanding through emotional exchange, perspective-taking, and narrative reflection. Psychological research frames dating as developmental even when it does not lead to relationship.

Limit: Growth effects depend on reflection and safety; negative dating experiences can reduce self-esteem and increase anxiety.

Social Network Bridging

Connects individuals beyond immediate kin, school or community networks, especially via online dating and social media mutuals, increasing heterogamy and geographic mobility.

Limit: Bridging is stratified by digital access, literacy, and platform design; can reproduce segregation via filters.

Intimacy Regulation and Scripting

Offers culturally legible scripts for initiating, pacing, and ending romantic/sexual interaction — who asks, who pays, how to signal interest, how to exit. Reduces ambiguity compared to unstructured interaction.

Limit: Scripts encode gender, class and heteronormative assumptions; mismatch between partners' script expectations is a common source of conflict and coercion risk.

Ambient and Synchronous Digital Intimacy

Enables sustained low-intensity connection (likes, story replies, shared memes) and real-time co-presence at distance (video, voice, co-watching) that maintains bonds between meetings or across geography.

Limit: Requires consistent access and emotional labor; can blur boundaries and create surveillance or burnout.

Prototypically: two people (dyadic) meet by prior agreement at a defined time and place, engage in conversation and shared activity (meal, walk, film, dance, video call), present selves through dress, grooming and self-disclosure, and evaluate compatibility. Accounts I
The Date Book: How a Slang Word Left the ParlorGeorge Ade, Chicago Record (1896) and Fables in Slang (1899); OED; Bailey 1988

1890s-1900s, Victorian to Progressive Era

In middle- and upper-class homes, courtship meant calling: a young man visited a young woman in her family's parlor under supervision. Working-class couples, crowded into small tenements without parlors, met instead in public — restaurants, theatres, dance halls. Columnist George Ade captured this shift by using 'date' for a public, prearranged meeting outside the home, and 'Date Book' for a cashier's ledger of suitors. The term spread as public leisure, department stores, and streetcars made unsupervised mixed-gender sociability ordinary. Dating was at first a class-marked practice, even slightly disreputable, before colleges and mass culture normalized it. Established the core modern definition: a planned, dyadic, public encounter with romantic possibility. Created a linguistic marker for the transition from family-mediated to individual and commercial courtship.

From Front Porch to Back Seat: The Automobile and the 1920s Dating RevolutionBailey 1988; Weigel 2016; contemporary college newspapers and advice columns

1920s-1930s

As automobiles, cinemas, and coeducational universities spread, dating moved decisively out of the home and into commercial leisure. The man was expected to ask, plan, pay and provide transport; the woman to be asked and to manage reputation. Rating-and-dating culture on campuses linked popularity to dating frequency. This 'dating system' replaced calling by the 1930s and tied romance to consumer spending — tickets, dinners, fuel — embedding courtship in the emerging consumer economy. Codified gendered scripts (male initiation/payment) that persisted for decades; made dating a public performance of status and desirability; linked love to consumption, a theme later critiqued by Weigel and Illouz.

Going Steady: Stability After WarBailey 1988; W. Waller, 'The Rating and Dating Complex' (1937); popular magazines 1940s-1950s

1940s-1960s

After World War II, amidst early marriage and suburbanization, 'going steady' — repeated dating with one person, often marked by wearing a letterman jacket or class ring — became the ideal. Dating was now explicitly a path to engagement and marriage, with high social pressure to pair off. At the same time, sexologists and magazines debated petting, necking, and the boundaries of respectable intimacy, while queer dating remained largely hidden due to criminalization and stigma. Normalized serial monogamy as a life stage; reinforced heteronormative, marriage-directed dating while marginalizing alternatives; set up 1960s-70s countercultural challenges (sexual revolution, feminist critique).

Accounts II
From Omiai to Deeto: Japan's Negotiation of Love MarriageSilverberg 2006; Edwards 1989; Hendry 1981; Japanese women's magazines 1920s

1912-1970s (Taishō to postwar)

Traditional omiai involved formal introductions via a matchmaker (nakōdo) with both families present to negotiate family-to-family alliance. During the Taishō era, Western-influenced ideas of ren'ai (romantic love) and individual choice entered urban discourse, and the loanword deeto (デート) appeared for unsupervised, couple-centered outings. Postwar, love marriages (ren'ai kekkon) overtook arranged marriages in number by the 1960s, but omiai persisted and later hybridized (e.g., modern marriage-hunting konkatsu). Deeto retained connotations of Western-style, consumer-leisure romance — cafés, department stores, cinemas — distinct from the formal, family-mediated omiai. Illustrates how dating vocabulary and practice were borrowed and localized, not simply imported; created a durable binary (omiai vs deeto) that still structures Japanese discourse on how couples meet.

The Swipe: How Online Dating Disintermediated MeetingFinkel et al. 2012; Rosenfeld, Thomas & Hausen PNAS 2019; Pew Research Center 2013-2023; corporate histories of Match.com (1995), OkCupid (2004), Tinder (2012)

1995-2019

Early websites digitized newspaper personals; mobile apps in the 2010s added geolocation, algorithmic recommendation, and the swipe interface. Stigma faded as use became mainstream — about 30% of Americans reported using a dating site/app by the late 2010s, higher among younger adults. Social media added a second pathway: flirtation via mutuals, likes, and story replies (ambient intimacy) rather than explicit matching. Rosenfeld et al. showed that by 2017, meeting online had surpassed meeting through friends and family for heterosexual couples in the US. Research also noted benefits for socially anxious users and risks of impression management and choice overload. Dramatically expanded pool beyond immediate networks; increased interracial and same-sex meeting; normalized algorithmic mediation of intimacy; raised new concerns about data privacy, harassment, and discrimination by design.

Love in Lockdown: Virtual Dating and the Pandemic HomePew Research Center 2020-2022; journal articles on COVID and intimacy (e.g., Journal of Social and Personal Relationships 2021-2023); app feature announcements (Tinder Passport, Bumble Video)

2020-2023

COVID-19 closed bars, campuses and restaurants and restricted travel. Dating apps rapidly added in-app video and voice; users shifted early encounters from public spaces to private homes via Zoom, FaceTime, WhatsApp and Google Hangouts — a practice labeled virtual dating. Long-distance dating research was extended to co-located couples forced apart. Studies found deliberate self-disclosure could sustain satisfaction at distance, but also noted Zoom fatigue and blurred boundaries. Many practices — video first dates, explicit health and boundary negotiation — persisted after restrictions lifted, blending synchronous digital tools with eventual in-person meetings. Legitimized video as a screening stage; made health, cohabitation and risk tolerance explicit dating topics; demonstrated resilience and limits of digitally maintained intimacy.

Dating is ancient and universal — people have always dated. — Planned, dyadic, unsupervised leisure dating with individual choice is historically recent (1890s America) and culturally specific. Earlier and many contemporary societies used family-supervised calling, arranged introductions, or community-mediated courtship. The word and practice were borrowed into Chinese, Japanese and Arabic as new concepts, not translations of existing ones.

Traditional dating was always male-initiated and male-paid because of natural gender roles. — The male-ask, male-pay script was codified in the 1920s-1950s American dating system tied to men's greater access to wages, cars and public space. Earlier calling was female-hosted in the home, and contemporary norms are increasingly negotiated or egalitarian. The script reflects historical political economy, not biology.

Online dating is superficial and only for hookups; no one finds serious relationships online. — Large-sample studies show online dating is now the most common way heterosexual couples in the US meet (Rosenfeld et al. 2019) and that many users seek committed relationships. Pew 2023 found 12% of American adults in committed relationships met online. Hookups occur but are not the majority use case.

Long-distance dating inevitably fails, and virtual dating is not real dating. — Meta-analyses find long-distance partners report comparable satisfaction and stability to geographically close couples, mediated by deliberate communication and idealization, though reunion is a vulnerable transition (about one-third break up within three months of reunification). Virtual dating during COVID demonstrated that synchronous video interaction can sustain assessment and intimacy before meeting.

Dating apps use science to find your perfect match. — Finkel et al.'s 2012 comprehensive review found no published evidence that proprietary matching algorithms outperform chance or simple similarity at predicting long-term compatibility. Apps excel at expanding access and filtering on stated preferences, not at scientifically guaranteeing compatibility.
