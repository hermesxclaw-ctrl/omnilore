Who It Is

Every scar is a compromise — the body choosing speed over perfection. Where once there was basketwoven skin, hair, sweat glands and elastic give, there is now a pale, aligned wall of collagen: tougher in one direction, weaker in every other, a living record that you were injured and survived.

Scar tissue is biologically inert but mechanically active: fibroblasts and myofibroblasts continuously remodel and contract the collagen matrix. It does not grow, learn, or act, but it behaves differently from normal tissue: less extensible, less resistant to UV, less able to thermoregulate (no sweat glands), more prone to shear due to loss of rete pegs, and in myocardium, electrically inert and non-contractile. Pruritus and pain occur in keloids and hypertrophic scars. Psychosocial behavior is human-mediated: scars affect self-image, stigma, and identity.

Powers
Wound closure and tensile restoration

Rapid deposition of provisional matrix then dense crosslinked collagen restores continuity after injury, preventing infection and hemorrhage. Provides early strength.

Limit: Inferior quality: ~70-80% tensile strength of normal skin at best; no restoration of adnexal structures; prolonged collagen synthesis creates stiff, inelastic wall that blocks cell communication and regeneration.

Contraction via myofibroblasts

Myofibroblasts (fibroblasts expressing alpha-smooth muscle actin) contract the collagen matrix, drawing wound edges together.

Limit: Excess contraction causes contracture scars over joints, limiting mobility; requires mechanical stress shielding to limit.

Barrier function (incomplete)

Scar epidermis can re-establish a barrier, but dermal functions are incomplete.

Limit: No sweat glands → impaired thermoregulation; no hair follicles; reduced UV resistance; loss of rete pegs → easier shear injury; elastic fibers absent early.

Pathological overgrowth (keloid)

Keloid fibroblasts produce excessive, stiff bundled collagen indefinitely beyond wound margins, forming tumor-like but benign masses, most common on shoulders and chest.

Limit: Benign (not cancerous) but can be painful/itchy; surgical excision alone often worsens recurrence; higher prevalence with high melanin and African or Asian ancestry.

Internal fibrosis (myocardial scar)

After myocardial infarction, cardiac muscle is replaced by collagen scar, preventing rupture but

Limit: Scar is non-contractile and electrically inert → loss of muscular power, risk of heart failure and arrhythmia; unlike skin, functional loss is life-threatening.

Form & Signs

Appearance — A mature cutaneous scar is typically a flat or raised, pale whitish or hyperpigmented patch with a smooth, shiny surface and linear or irregular borders. Early scars are red/pink due to vascularity, fading over months to years. Hypertrophic scars are raised, red (lighter skin) or dark brown (darker skin), confined to wound borders, appearing 4-8 weeks after injury. Keloids are raised, firm, often shiny, purplish to brown, expanding beyond origina

Symbols — In medicine, scar is iconography of healing and of failure to regenerate. In cultural practice, intentional scars (cicatrisation) encode adulthood, lineage, beauty, mourning, or valor depending on community — e.g., Tabwa and Luba raised keloid patterns, Sepik River crocodile scarification for manhood, Mursi and Karo patterns. In Western visual culture, facial scar often codes villainy or survived violence (film noir, pirate, dueling scar). Lightn

Skin dermis is the classic site, but fibrosis/scar forms in myocardium after infarction, liver (cirrhosis), lung (pulmonary fibrosis), kidney, cornea, and after surgery in peritoneum and other organs. Accounts I
The Myocardial Scar After Heart AttackCardiology and pathology literature; clinical reviews of myocardial infarction healing

Modern (20th-21st century, described since early 1900s)

After coronary occlusion, cardiac myocytes die and are replaced by a collagen scar. The scar prevents ventricular wall rupture but cannot contract or conduct normally. Over weeks, fibroblasts lay dense aligned collagen; the resulting whitish patch is visible at autopsy. The heart's pumping power diminishes proportionally to scar size. Reduced ejection fraction, heart failure, arrhythmia substrate; basis for late gadolinium enhancement on cardiac MRI as diagnostic marker; informs revascularization and device therapy.

The Burn That Heals With Hair LossDermatologic surgery and burn literature; Wolfram et al. 2009; Gauglitz et al. 2011

Modern, with historical burn care dating to antiquity

Deep second-degree burns destroy dermis and adnexa. If healed within two weeks with new epithelium, minimal scar forms. If prolonged inflammation and fibroblast proliferation occur, dense collagen wall forms, rete pegs are lost, and hair follicles and sweat glands never return. Small wounds under 2 mm can heal scar-free; larger dermal wounds cannot. Permanent alopecia in burned area, impaired sweating and temperature regulation, hypertrophic scarring and contracture requiring pressure garments and stress-shielding.

The Keloid That Outgrows Its WoundClinical case literature; Bayat et al. 2003; Gauglitz et al. 2011

Described since 1806 (Alibert's 'cheloide') to present

After surgery, acne, or even a piercing, some individuals develop a keloid: a firm, itchy, raised mass that keeps growing beyond the original cut. Histology shows thick, hyalinized collagen bundles. Unlike hypertrophic scars that stay within borders and may flatten, keloids invade surrounding skin and often recur if excised. Cosmetic distress, pain, pruritus; treatment challenges (intralesional steroids, pressure, radiotherapy, not excision alone); illustrates genetic and melanin-associated risk.

Accounts II
The Pitted Face: Atrophic Acne ScarsDermatology textbooks; acne scar classification literature

Modern (acne vulgaris as common disease)

When severe acne or varicella destroys fat and muscle support beneath skin, collagen overexpression still blocks regeneration but does not overextend tissue, leaving sunken, pitted scars — icepick, boxcar, rolling types. Staphylococcus infection and genetic connective tissue disorders like Ehlers-Danlos can produce similar atrophic scars. Permanent textural change; psychosocial impact; basis for subcision, laser, and filler therapies; example that not all scars are raised.

The Navel: The Scar Everyone HasAnatomy and embryology texts; placental mammal biology

Lifelong, from birth

After the umbilical cord is cut, the stump dries and separates, leaving the umbilical scar — the belly button or navel. It is a true scar, composed of fibrous tissue where cord vessels closed. In egg-laying animals, a similar umbilical scar may fade within days or persist for life depending on species. Universal human landmark; surgically significant for laparoscopy access; cultural focus for adornment and body modification.

Stretch Marks: Scars of Rapid GrowthEndocrinology and dermatology literature on striae distensae

Modern, associated with pregnancy, puberty, weight gain, corticosteroid excess

When skin is stretched rapidly — pregnancy, adolescent growth spurt, weight gain, or tension near joints during healing — or under elevated corticosteroids, dermal collagen and elastin rupture and heal as linear atrophic scars. Initially red-purple, they fade to silvery-white over years. Some authorities classify them as scars, others as distinct lesions. Common, benign, improves in appearance but never fully disappears; illustrates tension and hormonal modulation of scarring.

Scar tissue is stronger than normal skin. — Scar tissue is weaker and less functional: ~70-80% tensile strength at best, less elastic, no hair follicles or sweat glands, less UV resistance, more prone to shear due to loss of rete pegs.

Scars are just on skin; internal organs don't scar. — Internal organs scar extensively — myocardial scar after heart attack, liver cirrhosis, pulmonary fibrosis — same collagen biology, termed fibrosis, with serious functional loss.

Keloids are cancerous tumors and should be cut out. — Keloids are benign inert masses of collagen, not cancer, but surgical removal alone often exacerbates them and increases recurrence; multimodal therapy is required.

Dark skin scars worse because of poor healing. — High melanin and African or Asian ancestry associated with higher risk of keloid/hypertrophic scarring due to fibroblast biology and genetics, not poor care; adverse scarring is more noticeable due to pigmentation contrast, but risk is biological.

Scars fade completely if you use creams or oils. — Mature scars may improve in color and texture over months to years, but collagen architecture is permanent; no topical can restore hair follicles, sweat glands, or basketweave collagen. Some treatments (silicone, pressure, steroids, laser) can modulate but not erase scars.
