Who It Is

Triphylus never slays a monster or steals fire — his power is quieter and more political: his name was stamped onto a whole borderland. In the western Peloponnese, where Arcadia bleeds into Elis, the district called Triphylia — 'Land of Three Tribes' — claimed descent from a single Arcadian prince, a bastard son of the culture-hero Arcas. He appears only in genealogies and dedications, yet his shadow explains why that contested strip of hills and coast kept insisting it was not quite Arcadian, not quite Elean, but something older and triple-rooted.

No myths ascribe deeds, speech, personality traits, motives, or moral character to Triphylus. He is a genealogical link — son of Arcas, father of Erasus — and an eponym. Pausanias calls him nothos (bastard/illegitimate son), which implies a social status but not a character judgment; the term distinguishes inheritance rights, not personality. No behavior can be reconstructed beyond his function as ancestor. Status: unknown.

Eponymous Ancestor Status (No Supernatural Powers Attested)

Triphylus is not credited with any divine power, metamorphosis, superhuman feat, magic, or heroic deed in surviving sources. His function is purely genealogical-political: to provide a mythic ancestor for the Triphylians and to connect Triphylia to the prestigious Arcas lineage. Unlike his father Arcas (culture-hero who taught agriculture), Triphylus has no technē or aretē assigned.

Limit: No powers to limit; the figure's lack of deeds means he cannot be invoked for protection, prophecy, or cult. His bastard status explicitly limits inheritance: Pausanias 8.4.1-2 shows Arcadia divided among the three legitimate sons (Azan, Apheidas, Elatus) with no share for Triphylus, explaining why his eponymy lies outside Arcadia proper.

Accounts I
The Eponymy of Triphylia — Land of Three TribesPausanias 5.5.3; Strabo Geographica 8.3.12-15; Stephanus of Byzantium Ethnica s.v. Triphylia

Mythic aetiology, discussed by authors 7 BCE - 6th c. CE

Both Pausanias and Strabo explain the toponym Triphylia as 'named after Triphylus' (apo Triphylou). Strabo adds ethnographic detail: Triphylia was so called because it was inhabited by three tribes (treis phylai) — either Minyans, Epeians, and Eleans, or Arcadians, Minyans, and Eleans — and the eponym's name conveniently matches the folk etymology (tri- + phylē). Stephanus epitomizes the tradition: 'Triphylia ... apo Triphylou tou Arkados, hōs Pausanias'. The story is not a narrative adventure but an aetiological charter myth legitimizing Triphylia's distinct identity between Elis and Arcadia. Provides mythic justification for Triphylia's autonomy and mixed population. In historical times Triphylia was contested between Elis and Arcadia (and later Sparta); having an Arcasid eponym allowed Triphylians to claim Arcadian kinship while maintaining separateness. The 'three tribes' etymology persists in modern scholarship as example of Greek eponymous invention.

The Division of Arcadia Among the Sons of ArcasPausanias 8.4.1-3; Apollodorus Bibliotheca 3.9.1 (parallel)

Mythic time, recorded 1st-2nd c. CE (Apollodorus) and 2nd c. CE (Pausanias)

After Arcas's reign, Arcadia was partitioned among his legitimate sons. Pausanias 8.4.1-2: Azan received Azania (northern Arcadia), Apheidas received Tegea and surroundings, Elatus received Mount Cyllene and later migrated to Phocis. Triphylus, as nothos, receives no share in Arcadia proper — his portion is displaced outward to Triphylia, outside the core. Apollodorus gives the same tripartition without mentioning Triphylus, confirming the pattern of exclusion. The story explains both Arcadian internal geography and why Triphylia is culturally Arcadian yet politically peripheral. Aetiology for Arcadian sub-regions (Azania, Tegeatis, Cyllene) and for Triphylia's liminal status. Reinforces Greek inheritance norms where nothoi could be acknowledged but not equal heirs. Later Arcadian dedications at Delphi include all four sons, symbolically reuniting the partitioned house.

Accounts II
The Father of Erasus and the Arcadian Dedication at DelphiPausanias 10.9.5-6

Historical dedication (early 4th century BCE?) described in 2nd c. CE

Pausanias describes a group dedication by the Arcadians at Delphi, near the entrance: statues of Arcas, Elatus, Apheidas, Azan, Triphylus, and Erasus son of Triphylus, plus other Arcadian heroes. The inclusion of Triphylus and his son Erasus alongside the legitimate line shows that despite his bastard status, Triphylus was publicly honored as part of the Arcasid house in a panhellenic sanctuary. Erasus is otherwise unknown except as 'son of Triphylus', indicating the genealogy was extended one generation to anchor contemporary Triphylian or Arcadian families to the dedication. Only attestation of Triphylus's offspring; proves his role as genealogical bridge. The dedication's existence (confirmed by Pausanias autopsy) shows Triphylus had sufficient status for monumental representation at Delphi, despite literary obscurity. Erasus remains a genealogical dead-end with no further myths.

The Mother-Name Variant: Laodamia, Leanira, Meganira, ChrysopeleiaPausanias 10.9.5 (Laodameia); Apollodorus 3.9.1 (Leanira/Meganira/Chrysopeleia); scholia and Frazer notes

Classical to Imperial mythography

The identity of Arcas's wife/mistress and mother of his sons varies systematically. Pausanias (10.9.5) is precise: Laodameia daughter of Amyclas is mother of Triphylus (and by implication not of the legitimate three, or at least distinguished). Apollodorus (3.9.1) reports three alternative traditions for Arcas's partner: Leanira daughter of Amyclas, Meganira daughter of Crocon, or the nymph Chrysopeleia, with sons Elatus, Apheidas, Azan. Leanira and Laodameia are likely scribal variants of same name (Leaneira/Laodameia), both daughters of Amyclas, suggesting a single Spartan princess tradition that split in transmission. Meganira and Chrysopeleia represent alternative local genealogies. Illustrates fluidity of mythographic genealogy and how bastard vs legitimate lines could be reassigned to different mothers. For Triphylus, it means his maternal Spartan ancestry is stable (daughter of Amyclas) even if her personal name fluctuates. Modern editors (Frazer, Wagner) treat Leanira as corruption of Laodamia.

Triphylus was a king of Arcadia who ruled alongside his brothers — Pausanias explicitly calls Triphylus nothos and excludes him from the division of Arcadia (8.4.1-2). He received no Arcadian share; his eponymy is displaced to Triphylia, outside Arcadia proper. He is never called king (basileus) in any source.

Triphylus had magical powers or heroic feats like his father Arcas — No source ascribes any deed, power, or adventure to Triphylus. He is purely genealogical. His father Arcas has culture-hero deeds (teaching agriculture), but these are not transferred to the son. Any powers assigned are modern invention.

Triphylia was named after the three sons of Arcas — Triphylia is named after Triphylus alone (Paus. 5.5.3, Stephanus), and its folk etymology is 'three tribes' (treis phylai) referring to its mixed population (Minyans/Epeians/Eleans), not three brothers. The three legitimate sons give their names to Azania, Tegeatis, and Cyllene, not Triphylia.
