The Approach

Before the first wrestler ever stepped into a dohyō, before the first sword was forged for an emperor's conquest, there was Takemikazuchi—the thunder god who sat atop an inverted blade on a wave-battered shore and crushed his rival's hand like a reed. He is the god who pins the great catfish of earthquakes beneath a stone, the deity whose sword fell from heaven through a storehouse roof to guide the first emperor of Japan. His story is a saga of divine conquest, martial might, and the birth of sumo itself.

Content advisory. Contains depictions of divine violence, including decapitation (in the context of the creation myth), crushing of limbs, and military conquest. Some episodes involve coercion and subjugation of local deities. Readers should be aware that these myths originate from a specific cultural and historical context and may reflect the ideologies of the Yamato state.

Etymology

The name Takemikazuchi is typically parsed as 'brave' (take) + 'mighty thunder' (mikazuchi), but the exact etymology is debated. The alternative names Takefutsu and Toyofutsu incorporate 'futsu,' which is associated with the sound of cutting or the sword's edge. The name 'Kashima' is derived from the shrine's location.

Cultural Ownership

Japanese

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars have suggested that Takemikazuchi and Futsunushi may be the same deity or have overlapping origins, but they are generally considered distinct in the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki. The Nihon Shoki also mentions a deity named Takehazuchi as the vanquisher of the star god Amatsu-Mikaboshi, which may be a variant name for Takemikazuchi.

Description

Appearance

Takemikazuchi is not described in detail in the primary texts. In art, he is often depicted as a fierce warrior in armor, sometimes holding a sword or a lightning bolt. In the namazu-e prints, he is shown as a powerful figure pressing down on a giant catfish with a stone or a sword.

Symbols & Iconography

His primary symbol is the sword, particularly the Totsuka-no-Tsurugi (ten-fist sword) and the Futsu-no-mitama. He is also associated with thunder, lightning, and the 'pinning rock' (kaname-ishi) that restrains the earthquake catfish.

Behaviour & Personality

Takemikazuchi is portrayed as a decisive, authoritative, and powerful deity. He is direct in his dealings, as seen when he demands the surrender of Izumo. He is also shown to be physically overwhelming, as in his wrestling match with Takeminakata. He is a martial deity, embodying the might of the heavenly gods.

Domains & Habitats

His primary domain is Kashima (modern Ibaraki Prefecture), where the Kashima Shrine is located. He is also associated with the sea, as he and his companions land on a shore and sit on a sword atop a wave. He is a god of thunder, war, and the imperial house.

Version Differences

In the Kojiki, Takemikazuchi is the son of the sword Itsu-no-ohabari and is accompanied by Ame-no-torifune. In the Nihon Shoki, he is accompanied by Futsunushi, and the story differs in details: the beach is called Itasa, the Izumo deity is named Ōanamuchi, and the wrestling match with Takeminakata is absent. The Nihon Shoki also mentions the star god Amatsu-Mikaboshi, who is subdued by a deity possibly identified as Takemikazuchi or Takehazuchi.

Limitations

None stated.

Contradictions

Sword Mastery and Divine Weapon

Source Version Kojiki and Nihon Shoki

Description

Takemikazuchi is closely associated with swords. He uses the Totsuka-no-Tsurugi as a seat and later sends his sword, Futsu-no-mitama, to aid Emperor Jimmu. The sword is said to have the power to fell evil deities spontaneously.

Limitations

The sword's power is only demonstrated in the Jimmu episode.

Contradictions

The sword is also attributed to Futsunushi in some contexts.

Subduing Earthly Deities

Source Version Kojiki

Description

Takemikazuchi is sent by the heavenly gods to subjugate the terrestrial deities of the Middle Country. He successfully persuades Kotoshironushi to yield and defeats Takeminakata in combat.

Limitations

He does not subdue all deities himself; some yield without direct conflict.

Contradictions

The Nihon Shoki gives a different account where Ōanamuchi yields after being promised a palace.

Stories & Myths

Birth from Izanagi's Sword

Source Kojiki, Nihon ShokiCulture JapanesePeriod Age of the Gods (mythological)

Summary

After Izanagi returns from the underworld, he beheads the fire god Kagu-tsuchi. The blood from his ten-fist sword gives birth to several deities, including Takemikazuchi, who emerges from the blood near the base of the blade.

Participants

Izanagi

Kagu-tsuchi

Takemikazuchi

Consequences

Takemikazuchi is born as a powerful deity associated with the sword and thunder.

Variants

The Nihon Shoki is less specific about Takemikazuchi's birth, and the name of the sword is given as Ame-no-ohabari.

Quelling of the Middle Country

Source Kojiki, Nihon ShokiCulture JapanesePeriod Age of the Gods (mythological)

Summary

Amaterasu and Takamusubi decide to send Takemikazuchi (or his father) to conquer the terrestrial world. He and Ame-no-torifune land at Izumo, sit on an inverted sword, and demand that Ōkuninushi surrender. After Ōkuninushi consults his sons, Kotoshironushi yields, but Takeminakata challenges Takemikazuchi to a strength contest and is defeated. Takeminakata is exiled to Suwa.

Participants

Takemikazuchi

Ame-no-torifune

Ōkuninushi

Kotoshironushi

Takeminakata

Consequences

The Middle Country is subjugated by the heavenly gods, and Takeminakata becomes the deity of Suwa Grand Shrine. This episode is considered the mythical origin of sumo wrestling.

Variants

In the Nihon Shoki, Takemikazuchi is accompanied by Futsunushi, the Izumo deity is called Ōanamuchi, and there is no wrestling match. Instead, Ōanamuchi yields after being promised a palace.

Assisting Emperor Jimmu

Source Kojiki, Nihon ShokiCulture JapanesePeriod Mythological imperial era

Summary

During Jimmu's eastern expedition, he and his troops are incapacitated by a bear or poison fumes. Takakuraji presents a sword that Jimmu uses to subdue the evil deities. Takakuraji explains that in a dream, Amaterasu and Takamusubi commanded Takemikazuchi to descend, but he said it was enough to send his sword, which he deposited through the roof of Takakuraji's storehouse.

Participants

Emperor Jimmu

Takakuraji

Takemikazuchi

Amaterasu

Takamusubi

Consequences

Jimmu is able to continue his conquest, and the sword, known as Futsu-no-mitama, becomes the main object of worship at Isonokami Shrine.

Variants

The Kojiki and Nihon Shoki differ on the cause of the incapacitation (bear vs. poison fumes).

Dark History

Violent Conquest

Source Kojiki, Nihon Shoki

Description

Takemikazuchi is a war deity who forcibly subjugates other deities, using intimidation and physical force. This reflects the historical expansion of the Yamato state.

Scholarly Context

Scholars view these myths as legitimizing the imperial dynasty's rule over other clans and regions.

Divine Violence in Creation

Source Kojiki, Nihon Shoki

Description

Takemikazuchi's birth occurs from the blood of the decapitated fire god Kagu-tsuchi, which is itself a violent act by Izanagi.

Scholarly Context

This reflects the cycle of creation and destruction in Shinto mythology.

Scholarly Consensus

The discrepancy is attributed to different textual traditions. Futsunushi is likely a local deity of Katori who was incorporated into the myth.

Birth of Takemikazuchi

Position A

Takemikazuchi is born from the blood of Izanagi's sword.

Position A — evidence

Kojiki (birth episode)

Position B

Takemikazuchi is the son of the sword Itsu-no-ohabari.

Position B — evidence

Kojiki (conquest episode)

Scholarly Consensus

The Kojiki itself presents both ideas, suggesting a conflation of traditions. The sword is personified as a deity in the later episode.

Subjugation of Izumo

Position A

Position A — evidence

Kojiki

No wrestling match occurs; Ōanamuchi yields after being promised a palace.

Position B — evidence

Nihon Shoki

Scholarly Consensus

The wrestling match is likely a later addition to explain the origin of sumo. The Nihon Shoki's account is more diplomatic.

Research Scope

This dossier focuses on Takemikazuchi as portrayed in the Kojiki (712 CE) and Nihon Shoki (720 CE), the two primary chronicles of Japanese mythology. It also covers his role as the deity of Kashima Shrine, his association with the namazu-e earthquake prints of the Edo period, and his appearances in modern popular culture. The research draws on English translations by Basil Hall Chamberlain and W.G. Aston, as well as scholarly commentary. The research cutoff is August 2026.
