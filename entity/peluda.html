Archive Dossier — Identity

Peluda

Pronunciation Peluda: peh-LOO-dah (Spanish); La Velue: lah vuh-LOO (French)

Known As

La VelueThe Shaggy Beast of La Ferté-BernardThe Hairy Beast of La Ferté-BernardThe Hairy One

Etymology

The French name 'La Velue' derives from the Old French 'velu' meaning 'hairy' or 'shaggy', referring to its fur-covered body. The Spanish name 'Peluda' has the same meaning, introduced by Jorge Luis Borges. The English translations 'Shaggy Beast' or 'Hairy Beast' follow this etymology.

Cultural Ownership

French folklore, specifically the region of Sarthe (La Ferté-Bernard, Connerré, Yvré-l'Évêque, Tuffé) along the River Huisne. The creature is a local legend, not part of broader French mythology.

Disputed Identifications

Some commentators, including Paul Cordonnier-Détrie, suggest the Peluda belongs to the same family as the Tarasque of Tarascon and Beaucaire, and both are often lumped together as 'dragons'. However, this is a modern interpretive comparison, not an ancient identification.

Archive Dossier — Approach

In the green valleys of Sarthe, France, there once lurked a beast so terrible that even the floodwaters of Noah's Ark could not wash it away. The Peluda, or 'La Velue'—the Hairy One—was a serpent-tailed monster covered in long green fur and deadly poisoned spines, a terror that stalked the banks of the River Huisne and the streets of La Ferté-Bernard. It devoured livestock, snatched children and maidens, and withered crops with its fiery breath, until a young woman's fiancé struck its one vulnerable spot: the tail. This is the story of a creature that survived the Deluge and met its end at a bridge, but whose legend still lingers in fountains, games, and the annals of imaginary beasts.

No sword could pierce its shaggy hide, and every limb that was severed grew back instantly. Only a single spot beneath the belly was vulnerable to the blade.

Who It Is

The Peluda (also known as La Peluda, meaning 'the hairy one') is a dragon-like monster from the folklore of La Ferté-Bernard in the Sarthe region of northwestern France. According to legend, the Peluda was one of a kind — a monstrous creature resembling a giant serpent covered not in scales but in thick, shaggy hair, with a head like a dragon and a long, sinuous body that could crush trees and houses. It possessed a deadly venomous stinger on the tip of its tail like a scorpion, and its breath could wither crops and poison the air for miles. The beast emerged from the Huisne River, where it lived, to devastate the countryside, devouring livestock and destroying villages. Like the Hydra of Greek myth, any of the Peluda's body parts that were cut off would regrow, making it seemingly invulnerable to conventional weapons. The hero to defeat it varied by telling: some versions credit a courageous knight who discovered its one weak spot — its soft underbelly — while others attribute its death to a local peasant who had been blessed by a saint. The story is sometimes linked to the legend of Saint Margaret (or Saint Martha in certain tellings). The Peluda belongs to the family of French guivre and is closely related in type to the Tarasque and the Graoully, all representing the serpent-dragon archetype in Gallic folklore.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

The Peluda is described as serpent-headed and serpent-tailed, with an egg-shaped body the size of an ox. Its body is covered in long green fur, from which emerge sharp spikes tipped with deadly poison. It has broad, tortoise-like feet. The beast's fur and spikes are its most distinctive features, giving it a shaggy, fearsome appearance.

Symbols & Iconography

In iconography, the Peluda is depicted as a dragon-like creature with fur and spikes. A drawing appears on the cover of the 1889 pamphlet. Another illustration is in Jean Paul Ronecker's book on dragons (2004). The terra cotta dragon at Tuffé is claimed to represent a velue, though this is uncertain. The 2007 fountain in Tuffé likely depicts the creature in a stylized form.

Behaviour & Personality

The Peluda is a malevolent beast that terrorized the countryside and town. It devoured livestock, especially sheep, and snatched human victims, particularly children and young maidens. It used its tail to strike dead humans and animals, and when chased, it retreated to the River Huisne and caused floods that ruined crops, leading to famine. Some accounts say it shot fire from its mouth to wither crops. It was relentless and seemingly invulnerable except for one weak spot on its tail.

Domains & Habitats

The Peluda lived on the banks of the River Huisne in the Sarthe region of France. It ranged from the river to the city of La Ferté-Bernard and the surrounding countryside, including areas near Connerré and Yvré-l'Évêque. It was active during the High Middle Ages, perhaps more specifically the 15th century.

Version Differences

The core description is consistent across sources, but there are variations: some accounts emphasize its ability to cause floods, while others (like Borges and Claud Roy) mention fire-breathing. The method of defeat is consistent: a sword strike to the tail. The location of its death is traditionally at the bridge of Yvré-l'Évêque. The name and introduction vary: originally French 'La Velue', later Spanish 'Peluda' via Borges.

Tail strike

Source Version Folklore accounts

Description

The beast can strike with its serpent-like tail to kill humans and animals instantly.

Contradictions

None noted.

Flood causation

Source Version Folklore accounts

Description

When chased, the Peluda retreats into the River Huisne and causes a flood, ruining crops and bringing famine.

Limitations

Requires access to the river.

Contradictions

Some sources instead say it shoots fire from its mouth to wither crops; both are mentioned.

Fire-breathing

Source Version Borges (1957), Claud Roy (as cited)

Description

Borges and poet Claud Roy describe the beast as shooting flames from its mouth that wither or set crops on fire.

Limitations

Not mentioned in all sources; may be an embellishment.

Contradictions

Contradicts or supplements the flood ability; some sources do not mention fire.

Invulnerability

The beast is generally invulnerable to conventional attacks, except for a single vulnerable spot on its tail.

Limitations

Vulnerable only on the tail.
