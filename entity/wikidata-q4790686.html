Who It Is

Imagine a god so evil that his very name was a weapon, a being of pure destruction who was nevertheless worshipped in the heart of the Roman Empire. This is the paradox of Arimanius: a demon from the East who may have become a god of time and fate in the West, a figure of absolute darkness who might have held the key to the cosmos itself.

As Ahriman, he is the personification of destruction, chaos, deceit, and death — the antithesis of all that is good and creative. He is the source of all evil, disease, and suffering. In Mithraic context, if the lion-headed figure is indeed Arimanius, he appears to have been worshipped or venerated in a ritual context, possibly as a chthonic or liminal deity who needed to be propitiated. This is a radical departure from the Zoroastrian view of him as an enemy to be ultimately destroyed.

Powers
Creation of Evil

Ahriman is credited with creating all that is harmful, venomous, and destructive in the world: disease, pests, demons, and moral failings.

Limit: His power is not absolute; he is ultimately a created being who will be defeated and destroyed at the end of time (Frashokereti).

Corruption and Deceit

He is the master of lies and deception, capable of tempting and misleading humans and even divine beings.

Limit: He cannot corrupt the faithful who adhere to Asha (truth and righteousness).

Chthonic Authority (Mithraic)

If the lion-headed figure is Arimanius, he is depicted as having authority over the underworld and the passage of time, holding the key and scepter.

Limit: Unknown; the figure is often shown bound by the serpent, suggesting a cyclical or constrained power.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In Zoroastrian tradition, Ahriman is not described with a physical form — he is a purely spiritual, malevolent force. However, in Greco-Roman Mithraism, Arimanius is often iconographically associated with a striking, syncretic figure: a nude, winged, lion-headed deity, entwined by a serpent, holding a key in one hand and a scepter in the other. This figure is found in several Mithraea. It is crucial to note that this identification is not univers

Symbols — In Zoroastrianism: darkness, the serpent (as a symbol of his evil creation), the fly (a creature he created), and the north. In Mithraism (if lion-headed figure): the lion (representing fire, the sun, and possibly destruction), the serpent (time, renewal, or evil), the key (to the underworld), and the scepter (authority).

Domains — In Zoroastrianism: the domain of darkness, evil, falsehood, death, and the underworld. He rules the demonic hosts (Daevas) and the northern region of darkness. In Mithraism: if the identification holds, he may have been associated with the underworld, the passage of time, and the cycle of seasons, possibly representing the necessary, if destructive, force of cosmic renewal.

Accounts I
The Creation of the World (Zoroastrian Cosmogony)Bundahishn (Zoroastrian text)

Composed in its current form in the 9th century CE, but based on older Avestan traditions

In the beginning, there were two primal spirits: Spenta Mainyu (the Beneficent Spirit) and Angra Mainyu (the Destructive Spirit). Ahriman, dwelling in darkness, attacked the good creation of Ahura Mazda, bringing chaos, death, and evil into the world. He is said to have created a host of demons to counter each of the good creations. The world is now a battleground between good and evil, a state that will last until the final renovation of the world, when Ahriman is destroyed.

Accounts II
The Final Battle and Destruction (Frashokereti)Zoroastrian eschatological texts (Bundahishn, Denkard)

Sasanian era (3rd-7th century CE) and later

At the end of time, a savior (Saoshyant) will come, the dead will be resurrected, and the forces of good will wage a final battle against Ahriman and his demons. Ahriman will be defeated, rendered powerless, and cast into the darkness from which he came, or melted by a stream of molten metal. The world is purified, evil is eradicated, and creation is restored to its original perfect state.

The Temptation of ZoroasterDenkard and other Pahlavi texts

Late Sasanian / Early Islamic era (7th-9th century CE)

Ahriman attempts to tempt the prophet Zoroaster with worldly power and riches, but Zoroaster resists all his offers, remaining steadfast in his devotion to Ahura Mazda. This story is a classic example of the cosmic struggle between good and evil playing out in the life of a single individual. Zoroaster's resistance proves the power of free will and faith, and becomes a model for all humanity.

Arimanius is simply the Roman name for the Devil (Satan). — While Ahriman is a personification of evil, he is not the same as the Christian Satan. Ahriman is a cosmic principle of destruction and falsehood, not a fallen angel. The theological frameworks are fundamentally different.

The lion-headed figure in Mithraic temples is definitively Ahriman. — This is a highly debated identification. While the name Arimanius appears in Mithraic inscriptions, the iconographic link to the lion-headed figure is not proven and is rejected by many scholars.

Zoroastrianism is a simple 'good vs. evil' dualism with two equally powerful gods. — While dualism is central, Zoroastrianism is more accurately described as a monotheistic or henotheistic religion. Ahura Mazda is the supreme, uncreated creator, and Ahriman, though powerful, is a lesser, created being who will ultimately be defeated.
