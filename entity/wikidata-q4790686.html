Form

In Zoroastrian tradition, Ahriman is not described with a physical form — he is a purely spiritual, malevolent force. However, in Greco-Roman Mithraism, Arimanius is often iconographically associated with a striking, syncretic figure: a nude, winged, lion-headed deity, entwined by a serpent, holding a key in one hand and a scepter in the other. This figure is found in several Mithraea. It is crucial to note that this identification is not universally accepted — the lion-headed figure may instead represent a time deity (Aion) or a composite god of the cosmic cycle.

Powers

Creation of Evil Ahriman is credited with creating all that is harmful, venomous, and destructive in the world: disease, pests, demons, and moral failings. Zoroastrian (Avestan texts) His power is not absolute; he is ultimately a created being who will be defeated and destroyed at the end of time (Frashokereti). None significant.

Corruption and Deceit He is the master of lies and deception, capable of tempting and misleading humans and even divine beings. Zoroastrian (Avestan texts) He cannot corrupt the faithful who adhere to Asha (truth and righteousness). None significant.

Chthonic Authority (Mithraic) If the lion-headed figure is Arimanius, he is depicted as having authority over the underworld and the passage of time, holding the key and scepter. Greco-Roman Mithraism (iconographic, disputed) Unknown; the figure is often shown bound by the serpent, suggesting a cyclical or constrained power. The identification is disputed; this power may belong to a different deity (e.g., Aion).

Behavior & Habitat

As Ahriman, he is the personification of destruction, chaos, deceit, and death — the antithesis of all that is good and creative. He is the source of all evil, disease, and suffering. In Mithraic context, if the lion-headed figure is indeed Arimanius, he appears to have been worshipped or venerated in a ritual context, possibly as a chthonic or liminal deity who needed to be propitiated. This is a radical departure from the Zoroastrian view of him as an enemy to be ultimately destroyed.

Accounts

In the beginning, there were two primal spirits: Spenta Mainyu (the Beneficent Spirit) and Angra Mainyu (the Destructive Spirit). Ahriman, dwelling in darkness, attacked the good creation of Ahura Mazda, bringing chaos, death, and evil into the world. He is said to have created a host of demons to counter each of the good creations.

At the end of time, a savior (Saoshyant) will come, the dead will be resurrected, and the forces of good will wage a final battle against Ahriman and his demons. Ahriman will be defeated, rendered powerless, and cast into the darkness from which he came, or melted by a stream of molten metal.

Ahriman attempts to tempt the prophet Zoroaster with worldly power and riches, but Zoroaster resists all his offers, remaining steadfast in his devotion to Ahura Mazda. This story is a classic example of the cosmic struggle between good and evil playing out in the life of a single individual.

What We Don't Know

The primary gap is the unresolved debate over the Mithraic lion-headed figure. Without definitive epigraphic or textual evidence linking the name 'Arimanius' to this specific iconography, any description of the Mithraic Arimanius must be heavily qualified. Further research is needed on the specific Parthian and Persian intermediary traditions that transmitted the figure of Ahriman to the Greco-Roman world. The full range of Mithraic inscriptions mentioning Arimanius (CIMRM) should be systematically re-examined in light of modern scholarship.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Arimanius as a cosmic 'negative image.' In his original Zoroastrian context, he is the force of entropy, decay, and falsehood — the necessary opposite that gives meaning to truth and creation. In the Roman mystery cults, he may have been reinterpreted as a necessary, if terrifying, part of the cosmic machine — the shadow that turns the wheel of time. He is the ultimate 'villain,' but also a mirror reflecting the anxieties and dualities of the cultures that feared and perhaps even venerated him.
