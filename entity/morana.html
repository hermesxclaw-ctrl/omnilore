Who It Is

Each spring, in villages and schoolyards across Central Europe, a straw woman in a white dress is carried to the water’s edge. Children laugh and sing, adults watch with a mixture of nostalgia and unease. At the river, the songs grow louder. The figure is set on fire or simply hurled into the cold current. No one looks back. Only when the effigy has vanished beneath the surface do they turn toward home, carrying green branches and the fragile hope of spring. The woman they have just killed is Morana—Marzanna—the Slavic goddess of winter and death, a shadowy presence whose ritual drowning has outlived kings, synods, and empires. To follow her trail is to walk the boundary between folklore and faith, between the comfort of seasonal celebration and the stark memory of hunger, plague, and cold. Her “behavior” is the seasonal progression of cold and decay. Powers
Rule over winter and the dark half of the year

Morana is widely understood as the goddess or personification of winter, presiding over the cold, barren season when vegetation dies back and the land lies dormant. Her “power” is the ability to bring frost, snow, and the cessation of growth, effectively suspending the fertility granted by spring and summer deities. In mythographic terms, she governs the dark half of the year, from autumn to spring, during which her presence is felt in long nights, icy winds, and frozen fields.

Limit: Her rule is cyclical and temporary: each year, the ritual drowning or burning of her effigy marks the end of her dominance and the return of spring. She cannot permanently prevent the renewal of life; her power is bounded by the seasonal cycle.

Bringer of death, plague, and disease

Etymological evidence and folk associations link Morana to death, plague, and pestilence. The Proto‑Slavic root *morъ and related forms in Slavic languages denote deadly disease and mass mortality. In this sense, Morana’s power includes the ability to bring death to humans and animals, particularly through the hardships of winter—cold, hunger, and illness. The ritual expulsion of her effigy is explicitly framed as driving away death and disease from the community.

Limit: Morana’s association with plague and disease is primarily symbolic; there is no detailed myth in which she personally inflicts specific illnesses. Her power is diffuse, tied to environmental conditions rather than targeted acts. Christian sources may exaggerate her malevolence to justify suppression of the ritual.

Nightmare and oppressive dream influence

The linguistic connection between Morana/Marzanna and mara/zmora—nightmare spirits that sit on sleepers’ chests—suggests an extended domain over oppressive dreams and nocturnal fear. In some traditions, mara is a separate entity, but the shared root and overlapping imagery (a weight on the chest, suffocation, paralysis) allow scholars and modern practitioners to associate Morana with nightmares, sleep paralysis, and the psychological experience of being crushed by darkness.

Limit: This association is largely reconstructed and not explicitly stated in medieval chronicles. Folk practice around Marzanna’s effigy focuses on seasonal change rather than dreams. Thus, Morana’s nightmare power is more a conceptual extension than a firmly attested attribute.

Participation in cyclical death‑and‑rebirth of nature

Morana’s most prominent role is as a participant in the cyclical death and rebirth of nature. Her ritual destruction—drowning, burning, or tearing apart the effigy—symbolizes the death of winter and the release of spring. In some reconstructed mythic cycles, Morana’s death is paired with the emergence of spring deities such as Jarilo, Vesna, or Kostroma, forming a narrative of seasonal succession. Her power is thus paradoxical: she brings death, but her own ritual death enables rebirth.

Limit: The exact mythic structure of this cycle is not consistently attested across all Slavic regions. Some communities perform the ritual without explicit narrative about Morana’s relationship to specific spring deities. The death‑and‑rebirth framework is partly a scholarly reconstruction based on comparative mythology.

Protective and purifying function through ritual expulsion

Although Morana herself is a destructive figure, the ritual surrounding her effigy has a protective and purifying function for the community. By constructing, parading, and then destroying the effigy, participants symbolically remove death, misfortune, and winter’s hardships from their environment. Specific ritual rules—such as ensuring that all parts of the effigy enter the water, not looking back after throwing it, and carrying green juniper or branches—are believed to prevent illness from following the participants home and to secure the transition to spring.

Limit: The protective power resides in the ritual, not in Morana’s benevolence. She is not a guardian goddess who chooses to defend the community; rather, the community defends itself by symbolically killing or expelling her. The efficacy of the ritual is a matter of belief and tradition, not empirically verifiable power.

The Drowning of Marzanna in Polish Village TraditionJan Długosz, Annales; Maciej Stryjkowski’s chronicle; Oskar Kolberg’s ethnographic records

Medieval origins, documented in the 15th–19th centuries, continuing into the modern era

In Polish village tradition, the fourth Sunday of Lent or the spring equinox is marked by a ritual in which the community constructs a straw effigy called Marzanna, dressing it in white garments and sometimes bridal attire. Young people carry the effigy through the village, singing songs that bid farewell to winter and death. At the edge of a river, pond, or marsh, they throw Marzanna into the water; in some variants, they first set her on fire, so that she burns and then sinks. The participants then turn away and walk back to the village without looking behind them, believing that if they look back, illness or misfortune will follow. On the return journey, they carry a green branch (gaik) decorated with ribbons and flowers, symbolizing the arrival of spring. The ritual is both lament and celebration: the community mourns the hardships of winter while rejoicing in the promise of warmth and growth. Jarilo and Morana: A Reconstructed Slavic Seasonal MythComparative folklore and mythological reconstruction (East Slavic songs, modern scholarship)

Folk song evidence from early modern periods; scholarly reconstruction in the 19th–20th centuries

In a reconstructed mythic cycle, Jarilo and Morana are siblings, both children of the thunder god Perun. As infants, they are separated: Jarilo is stolen by Veles and raised in the underworld (Nav), while Morana remains in the world above. Each spring, Jarilo returns from the land of the dead, bringing fertility, warmth, and the renewal of vegetation. He marries Morana, uniting life and death, spring and winter. Their union sustains the world through the growing season. However, at the end of the year, Jarilo betrays Morana or fails to uphold his vows, and in response, Morana kills him, returning the world to winter and death. Jarilo’s death marks the end of the fertile season, and Morana’s rule resumes until he is reborn and returns again the following spring. The cycle explains the annual progression of seasons: spring’s arrival with Jarilo, the flourishing of life during his union with Morana, and the return of winter when Morana kills him. Morana and the Effigy Called ‘Death’ in Christian PolemicSynod of Poznań decree; later ecclesiastical commentary

Early 15th century and subsequent centuries of church regulation

The Synod of Poznań in 1420 issued a decree instructing clergy not to allow a ‘superstitious Sunday custom’ in which people carried an effigy called ‘Death’ and drowned it in puddles. Although the text does not name Morana or Marzanna, later scholars connect this effigy with the Marzanna ritual. From the church’s perspective, the custom was a dangerous relic of pagan worship, incompatible with Christian doctrine. Clergy were urged to suppress it, but in practice, the ritual persisted, often shifting from overt religious practice to a more folkloric, seasonal celebration. Over time, Christian communities sometimes reinterpreted the effigy as a symbolic representation of sin or misfortune rather than a goddess, but the underlying structure—procession, destruction, and return—remained. The decree illustrates the tension between official Christian doctrine and popular seasonal customs. It shows that the ritual was widespread enough to attract ecclesiastical attention and that the church perceived it as spiritually harmful. Modern School and Community Rituals of Drowning MarzannaContemporary ethnographic reports, local news, and cultural documentation

20th–21st centuries

In contemporary Central and Eastern Europe, the drowning or burning of Marzanna/Morana has become a widely recognized cultural event, often organized by schools, folklore groups, or municipal authorities. Children build straw dolls, dress them in simple clothing, and carry them to nearby rivers or ponds. Teachers explain the custom as a way of saying goodbye to winter and welcoming spring. Songs and rhymes are still used, though their content may be simplified or modernized. The effigy is thrown into the water or burned, and participants cheer. In some places, environmental concerns have led to the use of biodegradable materials or symbolic burning without actual drowning. The ritual is framed as heritage rather than religion, and participants may not consciously associate the effigy with a goddess. The modern ritual preserves elements of the traditional practice while reframing it as cultural heritage.
