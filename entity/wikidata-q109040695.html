Who It Is

In the farmhouses of Gascony and Languedoc, you do not buy luck — you steal it, in the shape of a cat. Fed on the first spoonful of every meal and carried home without a backward glance, the mistigri — sometimes written matagot or mandagot — purrs wealth into the house for as long as you honor the bargain, and takes it away the moment you forget.

Attested behavior: The mistigri is a transactional household spirit. If properly acquired (stolen from a neighbor, lured with food, or found and carried home without looking back) and properly fed (first bite of every meal, fresh milk, sometimes a chicken), it brings prosperity — money appears, livestock thrives, chores are aided. If neglected, insulted, or if the owner looks back while carrying it, it withdraws favor and may bring ruin, illness, or simply leave. Powers
Fortune-bringing / Prosperity induction

Core attested power: while housed and fed correctly, the mistigri causes increase in household wealth — coins found, crops/livestock prosper, debts ease. Mechanism is never explained; it is presented as luck, not conjuration.

Limit: Conditional on strict hospitality: must receive first bite/morsel of every meal, fresh food, and respect. If condition is broken, fortune reverses. Cannot be commanded to produce specific sums or to harm enemies on demand. No attested limit on amount, but narratives imply modest peasant prosperity, not kingly riches.

Homing / Return and Tenacity

If expelled, abandoned, or sold, the mistigri returns to its chosen house, or cannot be killed or permanently removed. In some tellings it must be passed to another person before the owner can die.

Limit: Can be transferred if voluntarily given or stolen by another (theft is the legitimate transfer method in Gascony). Looking back while carrying it breaks the bond. Christian exorcism is said to be able to expel it in late clerical variants, but folk tellers treat exorcism as unreliable.

Shapeshifting (limited)

Attested in a minority of variants: the spirit can appear as a black dog, rat, or small dark humanoid, but cat is its true/preferred form. Not a full were-creature transformation.

Limit: Only between small domestic/dark forms; no attested transformation into inanimate objects or large monsters. Many informants deny shapeshifting entirely and insist it is always a cat.

Sentience and Speech Comprehension

Understands human language, recognizes its owner, responds to calls and to the chicken-lure ritual. Does not speak human language in most attestations, but communicates through behavior (purring, leading to coins).

Limit: No attested human speech, prophecy, or spell-casting. Intelligence is cat-like cunning, not human reasoning.

Accounts I
The Stolen Matagot of ArmagnacJean-François Bladé - Contes populaires de la Gascogne (1886), Vol. 3

Oral tradition collected 1860s-1880s; published 1886

A poor farmer is told by an old woman that his neighbor's black matagot brings that neighbor wealth. To obtain it, he must steal it without being seen, tuck it under his arm, and carry it home without ever looking back, no matter what voices or noises follow. He does so, feeding it the first spoonful of every meal. His farm prospers — his hens lay, his debts lessen. When he once forgets to feed it first, the cat disappears and his luck turns. The story ends with the moral that stolen luck must be constantly honored. Explains both the acquisition taboo (theft + no looking back) and the maintenance taboo (first morsel). Establishes the mistigri as transferable property whose theft is socially sanctioned in narrative.

The Chicken Lure of LanguedocClaude Seignolle - Le Folklore du Languedoc (1960), chap. on household spirits; also Contes, récits et légendes des pays de France (1997)

Oral tradition collected 1930s-1950s; published 1960/1997

A housewife who wants prosperity is instructed to roast a chicken, go to a crossroads at midnight, and call 'Mistigri! Mistigri!' A black cat appears and follows the smell. She must pick it up and carry it home without looking back, even if it scratches. At home she must give it the first bite of every dish. While honored, the house fills with coins found in odd places. When her husband mocks the cat and withholds food, the cat vanishes and the family is poorer than before. Codifies the food-offering contract and the danger of skepticism. Shows the mistigri as sensitive to respect, not merely to food.

Accounts II
The Cat That Would Not Let Its Master DieCharles Joisten - Contes populaires du Dauphiné and Êtres fantastiques : patrimoine narratif de l'Isère (2005)

Collected 1960s-1970s; published 1971-2005

An old man who has kept a mistigri for decades falls gravely ill but cannot die. The priest says it is because he harbors a familiar spirit that must be passed on. The family tries to give the black cat away, but it returns each night. Only when a neighbor agrees to accept it — stealing it in the traditional manner — can the old man finally expire. The neighbor then prospers. Introduces the deathbed-transfer motif common to familiars and house spirits across Europe; Christian reinterpretation frames the spirit as impediment to a good death.

The Mistigri as Witch's Familiar at the SabbathClaude Seignolle - Le Folklore de la Provence (1937) and later synthesis; Van Gennep - Manuel de folklore français contemporain, Vol. 1

19th-early 20th century folklore with early modern witchcraft overlay (16th-18th century demonological background)

Inquisitorial and later clerical accounts reinterpret the household mistigri as a demon given by the Devil at the sabbat. The witch feeds it with her own food or blood, and it does her bidding — curdling milk, bringing coins. When the witch is arrested, the cat disappears or is found to be an ordinary cat. Folklorists note this is an elite demonization of a previously neutral house spirit, not an independent folk narrative. Shows the Christianization and criminalization of the belief; explains why later informants are reluctant to admit keeping a mistigri and why some stories end with exorcism.

The Mistigri of the Card Table — Le PouilleuxFrench popular culture: rules for the card game 'Mistigri' (also called Pouilleux, Old Maid), attested in print from the 19th century; e.g., Méry - L'Arbitre des jeux (1847), later Larousse des jeux

19th century to present

In the game Mistigri, the Jack of Clubs (le Mistigri) is the unpairable card that no one wants to be left holding. The loser who ends with it is 'le mistigri'. The name transfers the cat's unlucky/trickster aura to the card. This is not a narrative about a creature, but it explains the English label 'Jack mistigri' — Jack (the card) + Mistigri (the cat) — and why image searches mix cats and playing cards. Some modern children's books and internet posts have retrofitted a 'monster Jack Mistigri' from the card name, without folk basis. Demonstrates how a game name creates a homonym that contaminates folklore databases (including Wikidata). No creature lore originates here, but the homonym generates modern pseudo-lore.

Jack mistigri is an ancient, distinct French monster with its own mythology separate from the matagot. — No pre-19th-century source attests a distinct 'Jack mistigri' monster. The form appears to be a modern anglicization of French 'mistigri' (cat / Jack of Clubs) conflated with the well-documented matagot/mistigri house spirit. All narrative motifs attributed to 'Jack mistigri' are matagot motifs.

The mistigri grants wishes or finds buried treasure on command. — Oral sources limit it to incremental household prosperity (coins found, livestock thriving) while it is fed correctly. It does not grant wishes, speak, or hunt treasure on command. Wish-granting is a modern fantasy embellishment.

Mistigri is a ferocious beast or cryptid that attacks people. — It is consistently described as a normal-sized domestic cat, not a large predator. No attacks are attested; the only harm is withdrawal of luck or inability to die if not passed on. The Wikidata 'beast' archetype is misleading.

The name comes from ancient Egyptian or from 'mist' + 'gris' (grey mist). — Etymology is from French cat-call 'mistigri' and Occitan matagot; no Egyptian or 'mist' connection. Folk etymologies inventing 'mist' are modern internet inventions.
