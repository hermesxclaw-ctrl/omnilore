Who It Is

In the shadow of Anubis, the great jackal who presides over the mummification of the dead, stands a quieter figure — a jackal-headed goddess who guards the threshold of the embalming house, knife in hand, eyes fixed on the horizon of the afterlife. This is Anput, the 'female Anubis,' a deity who has been worshipped for over four thousand years, yet remains one of the most overlooked figures in the Egyptian pantheon. She is the protector of the dead, the mistress of the sacred land, and the mother of the goddess who brings fresh water to the thirsty souls of the underworld. In the modern world, she has become a cult favorite on internet forums, a symbol of the 'forgotten' goddess, and a subject of memes that imagine her as the long-suffering wife of Anubis, doing the real work of mummification while her husband gets all the credit. But who was Anput, really? Was she a distinct goddess wit

Anput is a protective and nurturing deity, but with a fierce, guardian aspect. She is the 'Mistress of the Sacred Land' (the necropolis), overseeing the embalming process and guarding the deceased from harm. In funerary texts, she is invoked to purify the body, to ward off evil spirits, and to guide the soul through the Duat (underworld). She is not a trickster or a chaotic force; she is a steady, maternal, and terrifyingly loyal protector. In later syncretic texts, she is sometimes identified with Isis or Nephthys as a mourner and protector of the dead.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Anput (also Input or Inpwt) is the feminine form of Anubis (Inpw). In ancient Egyptian, the suffix -t denotes the feminine gender, so Anput literally means 'the female Anubis' or 'the feminine counterpart of Anubis.' The name is often written with the jackal standard and a feminine ending,

Pronunciation — AN-put (with a short 'a' as in 'cat' and a clipped 'put'; the 't' is articulated, unlike in French). In reconstructed Egyptian it may have been pronounced something like 'In-pu-t' or 'An-pu-t.'

Aliases — Input, Inpwt, Anupet, Anputet, Anubises (rare, Greek-influenced), The Female Anubis

Powers
Guardianship of the Dead

Anput protects the mummy and the soul of the deceased from malevolent forces, demons, and the dangers of the underworld. She is often depicted with a knife, ready to strike down any threat.

Limit: Her protection is limited to those who have been properly mummified and buried with the correct rituals. The unburied or those who failed the judgment of the dead may not receive her aid.

Mummification and Embalming

She presides over the mummification process, ensuring the body is preserved and the organs are protected. She is the female counterpart to Anubis, who is the patron of embalmers.

Limit: Her power is ritual rather than magical; it requires the correct performance of the rites by priests.

Purification

She purifies the deceased with water and natron, cleansing them of sin and impurity before they enter the afterlife.

Limit: Purification is only effective for those who have lived a righteous life and passed the Weighing of the Heart.

Guidance through the Duat

She guides the soul through the underworld, helping it navigate the dangerous pathways and avoid the pitfalls that lead to annihilation.

Limit: She cannot protect those who have been judged unworthy; they are devoured by Ammit.

Motherhood and Nurturing

As the mother of Kebechet (the goddess of purification, who pours sacred water for the dead), Anput is a nurturing, maternal figure who cares for the deceased as her own child.

Limit: Her nurturing aspect is often overshadowed by her protective role; it is not a 'power' in the traditional sense.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Anput is almost always depicted as a woman with the head of a jackal (or sometimes a full jackal), mirroring Anubis. She wears a long, tight-fitting sheath dress, often with a wesekh collar (broad necklace) and a headdress that may include a jackal standard or a simple modius (a flat-topped crown). In some representations, she holds a was-scepter (power) and an ankh (life), or a knife to protect the deceased. Her skin is typically black or dark b

Symbols — Jackal (her animal form), the was-scepter, the ankh, the knife (for protection), the embalming bed, the mummy wrapping, the canopic jars (especially the jackal-headed one for the stomach, though that is usually associated with Duamutef), and the 'Sacred Land' (the necropolis). Her color is black, and she is often shown with the red sun disk (as a solar eye) in late periods.

Domains — Necropolis, mummification, embalming, the afterlife, protection of the dead, purification, the sacred land (cemetery), and the ritual of opening the mouth. She is also associated with the 'Place of Embalming' (the wabet) and the 'Beautiful House' (the per-nefer, the mummification workshop). In Cynopolis, she was the local goddess of the dead.

Accounts I
The Guardian of the Embalming HouseCoffin Texts, Spell 154 (Middle Kingdom)

c. 2055–1650 BCE

In this spell, Anput is invoked to stand at the door of the embalming house, warding off any spirit or demon that would disturb the body of the deceased. She is described as 'the one who is in the place of embalming, the mistress of the sacred land.' The deceased asks her to 'open the way' and to 'let the soul ascend to the heavens.' The spell ensures the safe passage of the soul to the afterlife, and the body remains preserved and undisturbed.

The Weighing of the Heart (Vignette)Book of the Dead, Papyrus of Ani (c. 1250 BCE)

New Kingdom, 19th Dynasty

In the famous vignette of the Weighing of the Heart, Anput is depicted standing behind the scales, sometimes as a jackal-headed goddess, sometimes as a full jackal. She watches as the heart of the scribe Ani is weighed against the feather of Ma'at. Her presence is a silent, stern reminder that the dead are under her protection and judgment. Ani passes the judgment and is granted eternal life. Anput's presence ensures that the ritual is conducted without interference.

Accounts II
The Lament of the Two JackalsPlutarch, De Iside et Osiride (c. 100 CE), and earlier Egyptian laments

c. 100 CE

Plutarch, in his allegorical interpretation of the Osiris myth, mentions that the Egyptians mourned the death of Osiris with 'the two jackals' — Anubis and his female counterpart (Anput). They are said to have howled at the tomb, their cries echoing the grief of Isis and Nephthys. This is a late, Hellenized account, but it reflects the enduring association of Anput with mourning and the funerary cult. The lamentation is part of the ritual mourning that ensures Osiris's resurrection, and by extension, the resurrection of all the dead.

The Eye of Re (Syncretic Myth)Temple inscriptions at Dendera and Edfu (Ptolemaic period)

c. 305–30 BCE

In the Ptolemaic period, Anput is sometimes identified as an aspect of the Eye of Re, the fierce solar goddess (like Sekhmet or Hathor) who is the daughter of the sun god. In this role, she is a fiery protector who destroys the enemies of Re, but she is also the one who brings the life-giving flood and heals the sick. Her jackal form is retained, but she is now shown with a sun disk. This syncretism elevates Anput from a purely funerary deity to a cosmic protector, linking her to the cycle of the sun and the annual Nile flood.

Anput is a 'goddess of death' or a 'dark goddess' who rules the underworld. — Anput is a goddess of mummification, protection, and the afterlife. She does not 'rule' the underworld; she guards and protects the dead within it. She is not a malevolent or 'dark' deity.

Anput is the wife of Anubis and is always depicted with him. — She is often depicted with Anubis, but not always. She can appear alone in funerary scenes, and her role as 'wife' is not consistent across all texts.

Anput is a 'forgotten' goddess who was erased from history. — Anput was a minor goddess, but she was not 'erased.' She appears in texts and reliefs from the Old Kingdom to the Roman period. Her cult was simply never as widespread as that of Anubis.

Anput has the head of a dog, not a jackal. — In Egyptian art, Anput is depicted with the head of a jackal (or a full jackal), not a domestic dog. The distinction is important in Egyptian symbolism.
