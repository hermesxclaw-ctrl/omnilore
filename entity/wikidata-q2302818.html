Form

Anput is almost always depicted as a woman with the head of a jackal (or sometimes a full jackal), mirroring Anubis. She wears a long, tight-fitting sheath dress, often with a wesekh collar (broad necklace) and a headdress that may include a jackal standard or a simple modius (a flat-topped crown). In some representations, she holds a was-scepter (power) and an ankh (life), or a knife to protect the deceased. Her skin is typically black or dark brown, symbolizing fertility, rebirth, and the fertile Nile silt, as well as the color of mummified flesh.

Powers

Guardianship of the Dead Anput protects the mummy and the soul of the deceased from malevolent forces, demons, and the dangers of the underworld. She is often depicted with a knife, ready to strike down any threat. Book of the Dead, Chapter 151 (New Kingdom) Her protection is limited to those who have been properly mummified and buried with the correct rituals. The unburied or those who failed the judgment of the dead may not receive her aid. None noted; her role as guardian is consistent.

Mummification and Embalming She presides over the mummification process, ensuring the body is preserved and the organs are protected. She is the female counterpart to Anubis, who is the patron of embalmers. Coffin Texts, Spell 154 (Middle Kingdom) Her power is ritual rather than magical; it requires the correct performance of the rites by priests. Some texts give this role to Anubis alone, with Anput merely as a witness or companion.

Purification She purifies the deceased with water and natron, cleansing them of sin and impurity before they enter the afterlife. Pyramid Texts, Utterance 535 (Old Kingdom) Purification is only effective for those who have lived a righteous life and passed the Weighing of the Heart. In some spells, purification is performed by Nephthys or Isis instead.

Guidance through the Duat She guides the soul through the underworld, helping it navigate the dangerous pathways and avoid the pitfalls that lead to annihilation. Book of the Dead, Chapter 144 and related spells (New Kingdom) She cannot protect those who have been judged unworthy; they are devoured by Ammit. In many texts, this role is given to Anubis or Thoth; Anput's guidance is less emphasized.

Motherhood and Nurturing As the mother of Kebechet (the goddess of purification, who pours sacred water for the dead), Anput is a nurturing, maternal figure who cares for the deceased as her own child. Various funerary texts and genealogical lists (New Kingdom onward) Her nurturing aspect is often overshadowed by her protective role; it is not a 'power' in the traditional sense. Some traditions make Kebechet the daughter of Anubis alone, without a named mother.

Behavior & Habitat

Anput is a protective and nurturing deity, but with a fierce, guardian aspect. She is the 'Mistress of the Sacred Land' (the necropolis), overseeing the embalming process and guarding the deceased from harm. In funerary texts, she is invoked to purify the body, to ward off evil spirits, and to guide the soul through the Duat (underworld). She is not a trickster or a chaotic force; she is a steady, maternal, and terrifyingly loyal protector. In later syncretic texts, she is sometimes identified with Isis or Nephthys as a mourner and protector of the dead.

Accounts

In this spell, Anput is invoked to stand at the door of the embalming house, warding off any spirit or demon that would disturb the body of the deceased. She is described as 'the one who is in the place of embalming, the mistress of the sacred land.' The deceased asks her to 'open the way' and to 'let the soul ascend to the heavens.'

In the famous vignette of the Weighing of the Heart, Anput is depicted standing behind the scales, sometimes as a jackal-headed goddess, sometimes as a full jackal. She watches as the heart of the scribe Ani is weighed against the feather of Ma'at. Her presence is a silent, stern reminder that the dead are under her protection and judgment.

Plutarch, in his allegorical interpretation of the Osiris myth, mentions that the Egyptians mourned the death of Osiris with 'the two jackals' — Anubis and his female counterpart (Anput). They are said to have howled at the tomb, their cries echoing the grief of Isis and Nephthys. This is a late, Hellenized account, but it reflects the enduring association of Anput with mourning and the funerary cult.

In the Ptolemaic period, Anput is sometimes identified as an aspect of the Eye of Re, the fierce solar goddess (like Sekhmet or Hathor) who is the daughter of the sun god. In this role, she is a fiery protector who destroys the enemies of Re, but she is also the one who brings the life-giving flood and heals the sick. Her jackal form is retained, but she is now shown with a sun disk.

Scholarly Debates
Independent Goddess vs. Feminine Aspect
Anput is a distinct goddess, the wife of Anubis, with her own cult and mythology. ⇌ Anput is simply the grammatical feminine form of Anubis, not a separate deity with independent worship.
Evidence for A: Her name appears in Pyramid Texts and Coffin Texts as an invocable entity, and she has a title, 'Mistress of the Sacred
Evidence for B: There is no archaeological evidence of a temple dedicated solely to Anput, and she is always mentioned in tandem with An
Mother of Kebechet
Anput is the mother of Kebechet, the goddess of purification. ⇌ Kebechet is the daughter of Anubis alone, or of Nephthys.
Evidence for A: Genealogical lists from the New Kingdom name Anput as Kebechet's mother.
Evidence for B: Other sources do not mention a mother for Kebechet, or attribute her to Nephthys.
Role in the Weighing of the Heart
Anput is present at the Weighing of the Heart as a guardian. ⇌ Anput is not a standard participant; her appearance in the vignette is a scribal choice, not a canonical requirement.
Evidence for A: The Papyrus of Ani shows her standing behind the scales.
Evidence for B: Many other Book of the Dead papyri do not include her, only Anubis and Thoth.
Syncretism with the Eye of Re
Anput is an aspect of the Eye of Re, a solar goddess. ⇌ Anput is a funerary goddess, not a solar one; the syncretism is a late Ptolemaic invention.
Evidence for A: Temple inscriptions at Dendera and Edfu call her 'the Eye of Re.'
Evidence for B: No pre-Ptolemaic source connects her to the sun god.
What We Don't Know

1. No dedicated archaeological report on the cult of Anput at Cynopolis has been synthesized; primary excavation reports (e.g., from the Petrie Museum) were not fully reviewed. 2. The exact iconography of Anput in the Old Kingdom is not fully catalogued; many reliefs are damaged or unpublished. 3. The relationship between Anput and the goddess Nephthys is underexplored; some scholars suggest they may have been conflated in the New Kingdom, but this is not confirmed. 4. Modern Kemetic (neopagan) practices involving Anput are not well-documented in academic sources; only internet forums and personal blogs were surveyed. 5. The Ptolemaic syncretism of Anput with the Eye of Re requires deeper study of the Dendera and Edfu temple texts, which are voluminous and only partially translated. 6. No comprehensive study exists on Anput's reception in modern media; my survey is based on general knowledge and internet searches. 7. The exact pronunciation of her name in ancient Egyptian is uncertain; Egyptological conventions vary.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Anput as the 'co-founder' of a funeral home. Anubis is the face of the business — the iconic jackal on the logo, the one everyone recognizes. But Anput is the one who actually runs the embalming room, who makes sure the body is properly preserved, who guards the door against anyone who would disturb the dead, and who holds the knife to protect the deceased from harm. She's the quiet, competent partner who doesn't seek the spotlight, but without whom the entire operation would fall apart. In the modern world, she's the 'work-from-home' goddess, the one who does the essential, unglamorous work of caring for the dead, while her more famous counterpart gets all the attention. And like many overlooked figures, she has found a new audience in the internet age, where she has become a symbol of the unsung heroes of mythology.
