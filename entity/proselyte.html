Who It Is

Before the word 'convert' carried its modern weight, the ancient Mediterranean had a quieter term for those who crossed the threshold of a people without being born into it: proselyte. In the synagogues of the Roman world, these newcomers stood at the edges of worship, bound by some laws, freed from others, and forever negotiating what it meant to belong. Their story is not one of simple conversion, but of a spectrum of belonging that shaped both Judaism and early Christianity.

Proselytes were expected to adopt Jewish customs and laws to varying degrees. Righteous proselytes fully converted, while gate proselytes observed the Seven Noahide Laws and some Jewish practices. They were often noted for their piety and devotion.

Etymology — From Greek προσήλυτος (prosēlutos), meaning 'stranger' or 'newcomer', itself a translation of Hebrew גֵּר תּוֹשָׁב (ger toshav), 'resident alien'.

Pronunciation — PROS-uh-lyte

Aliases — prosēlutos, ger toshav, resident alien, God-fearer, devout man, worshipper of God

Conversion to Judaism

Righteous proselytes underwent full conversion, including circumcision for males and immersion in a mikveh, becoming fully Jewish.

Limit: Requires commitment to all commandments; not reversible in traditional Judaism.

Resident Alien Status

Gate proselytes lived among Jews, observing the Seven Noahide Laws and certain customs, but were not full members of the covenant.

Limit: Not required to follow all Torah laws; not considered Jewish for all purposes.

Participation in Synagogue Life

Proselytes could attend synagogue and participate in worship, though with some restrictions depending on their status.

Limit: Full participation limited to righteous proselytes.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No distinctive physical appearance is described in the sources. Proselytes would have dressed according to the customs of the Jewish community they joined.

Symbols — No specific symbols are associated with proselytes. In art, they might be depicted in scenes of conversion or synagogue life.

Domains — Proselytes lived in the Land of Israel or in Jewish diaspora communities. They were integrated into the social and religious life of the synagogue.

Accounts I
The Ethiopian EunuchActs of the Apostles 8:26-40

1st century CE

Philip the evangelist meets an Ethiopian eunuch, a court official of the queen of Ethiopia, who is reading Isaiah. The eunuch is a 'God-fearer' or proselyte, having come to Jerusalem to worship. Philip explains the scripture, and the eunuch is baptized. The eunuch becomes one of the first Gentile converts to Christianity, highlighting the spread of the gospel beyond Jewish communities.

Accounts II
The Proselytes of Acts 2Acts of the Apostles 2:5-11

1st century CE

On Pentecost, devout Jews from every nation, including 'proselytes,' are present in Jerusalem and hear the apostles speaking in their own languages. The event marks the birth of the Christian church and the inclusion of proselytes in the new movement.
