The Approach

In the shadowy mountains of Arcadia, fifty brothers once dared to test the king of the gods—and paid for it with their lives. Among them was Eumon, a name that survives only as a whisper in an ancient catalogue of impiety.

Content advisory. This page discusses child murder and cannibalism in the context of Greek mythology.
Identity

Eumon

Pronunciation /ˈjuːmɒn/ (YOO-mon) in English; in reconstructed Ancient Greek: [eǔmona]

Etymology

The name Eumon (Εὔμονα) may derive from Greek elements meaning 'good' (εὖ) and possibly 'mind' or 'spirit' (μένος), but ancient sources do not provide an explicit etymology.

Cultural Ownership

Greek mythology, specifically Arcadian regional tradition

Disputed Identifications

Eumon is sometimes confused with other sons of Lycaon due to fragmentary lists; no separate figure of the same name is attested in Greek mythology.

Description

Appearance

No specific physical description of Eumon survives. As a son of Lycaon, he would have been a mortal prince of Arcadia, but his appearance is unattested.

Symbols & Iconography

No known symbols or iconography specific to Eumon. In ancient art, sons of Lycaon are rarely depicted individually; they are often shown collectively in scenes of Zeus's punishment.

Behaviour & Personality

Eumon is described as 'nefarious and carefree' (in the source material) along with his siblings, indicating a lack of moral restraint. Their impiety led to Zeus's test and their destruction.

Domains & Habitats

Eumon would have resided in Lycosura or other Arcadian settlements, but no specific domain is recorded.

Version Differences

The list of Lycaon's sons varies among sources. Apollodorus provides the fullest list, while Pausanias and others give different names. Eumon appears only in Apollodorus's list.

Stories & Myths

The Wickedness of Lycaon's Sons

Source Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.8.1; Pausanias, Description of Greece 8.2.4-6Culture Greek (Arcadian)Period Mythic (pre-Trojan War)

Summary

Lycaon, king of Arcadia, had fifty sons, including Eumon. They were notorious for their impiety and wickedness. To test them, Zeus visited in the guise of a peasant. The brothers killed a child (often identified as Nyctimus, Lycaon's own son or a hostage) and mixed its entrails into the meal offered to Zeus. The god, disgusted, overturned the table and struck the brothers and their father with lightning, killing them all. Lycaon was transformed into a wolf, but his sons were simply slain.

Consequences

Death of Eumon and his brothers; Lycaon's transformation into a wolf (in some versions); establishment of Zeus Lycaeus worship in Arcadia.

Variants

Pausanias gives a different account where Lycaon himself is transformed into a wolf, and the sons are not all killed; some versions mention that only Lycaon was punished, while others say all sons died. The child killed is sometimes Nyctimus, who is later restored to life.

Dark History

Child sacrifice and cannibalism

Source Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.8.1

Description

Eumon and his brothers killed a child and served its entrails to Zeus as a test. This act is a central element of the myth and reflects ancient anxieties about impiety and human sacrifice.

Scholarly Context

Scholars interpret this myth as an aetiological tale explaining the origins of the cult of Zeus Lycaeus, possibly involving historical human sacrifice or as a warning against impiety.

Contradictions & Disputes

Identity of the child killed

Position A

The child was Nyctimus, Lycaon's own son (according to some scholia).

Position A — evidence

Scholia on Lycophron and some late sources.

Position B

The child was an anonymous hostage or a local boy (as implied by Apollodorus).

Position B — evidence

Apollodorus does not specify the child's identity.

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus; the identity remains uncertain.

Fate of the sons

Position A

All fifty sons were killed by Zeus's lightning.

Position A — evidence

Apollodorus states all were killed.

Position B

Some sons were spared or transformed.

Position B — evidence

Pausanias mentions that only Lycaon was punished, and some sons may have survived.

Scholarly Consensus

The myth is inconsistent; Apollodorus is the main source for the wholesale destruction.
