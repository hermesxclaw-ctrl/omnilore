Who It Is

Phrasimus never speaks, fights, or performs a miracle in surviving Greek myth — and that is precisely why he matters. He is a hinge in the Athenian family tree, the mortal son-in-law of a river-god whose daughter would marry a king and whose granddaughters would be sacrificed to save Athens itself.

No behavior, speech, motive, or personality trait is narrated for Phrasimus. He does not act, speak, or make decisions in surviving myth. His sole function is to be the husband of Diogenia and father of Praxithea, thereby linking the Cephisus river lineage to the Athenian royal house. Any character traits would be invention. Reconstructed: he must have been considered a respectable Athenian (worthy to marry a river-god's daughter and father a queen), but this is a social inference, not an attested trait. Disputed/unknown: all personality dimensions.

Powers
Mortal status — no supernatural powers attested

Phrasimus is presented as a mortal Athenian man. No power, skill, transformation, divine gift, weapon, or supernatural ability is attributed to him in any source. He is not described as a seer, warrior, craftsman, or magician. His significance is purely genealogical.

Limit: As a mortal, he would be subject to normal human limitations, but no vulnerability, death story, or feat is narrated.

Genealogical linkage (social/kinship function)

His only 'ability' in narrative terms is to connect two important lineages: the river-god Cephisus via Diogenia to the Erechtheid kings of Athens via Praxithea. This kinship function legitimizes Praxithea's status as both river-granddaughter and queen, and gives her children (the Erechtheids) both autochthonous and riverine ancestry.

Limit: This is a narrative role, not a diegetic power. He does not mediate between gods and mortals in any active sense.

Accounts I
The Marriage of Phrasimus and Diogenia, Daughter of CephisusPseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.15.1

Compilation 1st-2nd century CE, reflecting earlier Athenian genealogical traditions (Classical-Hellenistic)

In the catalogue of Athenian families, Apollodorus records that Diogenia, daughter of the river-god Cephisus, married Phrasimus, an Athenian man. No courtship, dowry, or wedding narrative is given; the marriage is stated as fact to establish the parentage of their daughter Praxithea. This is the sole direct narrative involving Phrasimus as an actor (husband). The pairing unites a river-god lineage (Cephisus, one of the major rivers of Attica) with the mortal Athenian elite in the generation before the kingship of Erechtheus. Produces Praxithea, who will marry Erechtheus and become queen of Athens; establishes river-ancestry for the Erechtheid children. No other consequence is narrated.

The Birth of PraxitheaPseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.15.1

1st-2nd century CE compilation

Phrasimus and Diogenia have a daughter, Praxithea (Πραξιθέα). Apollodorus names her as their only attested child. Her name, meaning 'active goddess' or 'she who is active among the gods' (from prasso + thea), is appropriate for her later decisive role in the sacrifice narrative. No birth story, infancy, or upbringing is described for Praxithea in this source; she appears next as the wife of Erechtheus. Praxithea becomes the crucial link between Cephisid and Erechtheid lines. Her later consent to sacrifice her daughters for Athens becomes a foundational Athenian patriotic exemplum.

The Marriage of Praxithea to Erechtheus, King of AthensPseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.15.1; Euripides, Erechtheus fragments (for Praxithea's identity as queen)

Euripides ca. 422 BCE; Apollodorus 1st-2nd century CE

Praxithea, daughter of Phrasimus and Diogenia, marries Erechtheus, the powerful early king of Athens (son of Pandion and Zeuxippe, or in older tradition autochthonous son of Hephaestus and Ge). Apollodorus states this marriage directly after introducing Phrasimus. Euripides' tragedy Erechtheus dramatizes Praxithea as queen, delivering a famous speech on civic sacrifice. Through this marriage, Phrasimus becomes father-in-law to the king and maternal grandfather to the next generation of Athenian royalty. Legitimizes Erechtheus's children as descendants of Cephisus. Establishes the Erechtheid dynasty that includes Cecrops II, Pandorus, Metion, and the daughters Protogeneia, Pandora, Procris, Creusa, Oreithyia, and Chthonia. Phrasimus's lineage is now royal.

Accounts II
The Sacrifice of the Erechtheids (Hyacinthides) — Phrasimus as Grandfather of the VictimsEuripides, Erechtheus fr. 360 Kannicht (via Lycurgus, Against Leocrates 100); Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.15.4; Lycurgus, Against Leocrates 98-101; Plutarch, Parallela Minora; Hyginus, Fabulae 46

5th century BCE (Euripides) through 1st-2nd century CE (Apollodorus, Plutarch, Hyginus)

During the war between Athens and Eleusis (led by Eumolpus, son of Poseidon), the oracle demands the sacrifice of one of Erechtheus's daughters to secure victory. In Euripides' version, Praxithea — daughter of Phrasimus — voluntarily offers her daughter (in some accounts, all three daughters die, either sacrificed or by suicide after one is sacrificed). Apollodorus (3.15.4) says Erechtheus sacrificed his youngest daughter Chthonia, and her sisters Protogeneia and Pandora killed themselves. The daughters are sometimes called Hyacinthides. Phrasimus is not named in the sacrifice scene, but as maternal grandfather he is the ancestor of the victims, and his river-god connection via Diogenia/Cephisus adds pathos to the autochthonous sacrifice. Athens wins the war; Erechtheus is subsequently killed by Poseidon (or Zeus at Poseidon's request) for killing Eumolpus. The daughters receive heroic cult as the Hyacinthides. The War of Erechtheus against Eleusis and the Death of ErechtheusPseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.15.4-5; Pausanias 1.5.2, 1.27.4; Euripides, Erechtheus fragments; Thucydides 2.15 (brief mention)

5th century BCE to 2nd century CE

Erechtheus, son-in-law of Phrasimus, leads Athens against Eleusis, whose champion is Eumolpus, son of Poseidon (and Chione). After the sacrifice of his daughters, Erechtheus kills Eumolpus in battle. Poseidon, enraged at his son's death, asks Zeus to strike Erechtheus down, or kills him himself with his trident. Apollodorus says Poseidon destroyed Erechtheus and his house. Pausanias notes Erechtheus's tomb and his association with Poseidon-Erechtheus in the Erechtheion. Phrasimus's grandson(s) survive to continue the dynasty (Cecrops II becomes king). The war explains the cultic association of Erechtheus with Poseidon on the Acropolis. End of Erechtheus's reign; succession passes to Cecrops II (eldest surviving son of Praxithea and Erechtheus, thus grandson of Phrasimus). Establishes etiology for the Erechtheion and the rivalry between Athena and Poseidon for Attica. Phrasimus's descendants now are the sole Athenian royal house.

Cephisus the River-God and His Athenian DescendantsPseudo-Apollodorus 3.15.1; Pausanias 1.19.2, 9.24.1; Ovid, Metamorphoses 7.349 (Cephisus); Strabo 9.1.11

Classical to Imperial

Cephisus (Κηφισός), the river flowing through Boeotia and Attica, is personified as a river-god, son of Oceanus and Tethys. He fathers Diogenia (by unknown mother), who marries Phrasimus. Through this marriage, the river's lineage enters Athenian royalty. Cephisus is also father of Narcissus (by Liriope) in Boeotian tradition, and of other nymphs. The Attic Cephisus was distinct from the Boeotian Cephisus but mythographically conflated. Phrasimus's marriage to Diogenia thus represents the common mythic pattern of river-god daughters marrying mortal kings/heroes to legitimize territorial claims. Gives Athenian kings a river-god ancestor, reinforcing autochthony and control of Attic waters. Explains why Praxithea's children are sometimes called Cephisides in poetic genealogy. No independent story of Cephisus interacting with Phrasimus beyond the marriage alliance is preserved.

Phrasimus was a king, hero, or god with powers and exploits. — No ancient source gives Phrasimus any title, power, deed, or story beyond being husband of Diogenia and father of Praxithea. He is not called king (basileus), hero, seer, or god. His son-in-law Erechtheus is the king; his father-in-law Cephisus is the god. Phrasimus is a mortal link between them.

Phrasimus appears in many myths and has multiple stories. — He appears in exactly one ancient passage (Apollodorus 3.15.1) and is otherwise absent from myth, tragedy, epic, vase painting, and cult. All other 'stories' involving him are contextual — the stories of his daughter, son-in-law, and grandchildren — in which he is not an actor.

Praxithea was directly the daughter of the river-god Cephisus, so Phrasimus is irrelevant or a mistake. — Apollodorus explicitly makes Praxithea daughter of Phrasimus and Diogenia (who is daughter of Cephisus), making her granddaughter of Cephisus. Calling her 'daughter of Cephisus' is a telescoping simplification found in some modern summaries, not the precise ancient genealogy. Phrasimus is not a scribal error; the reading is stable across manuscripts.
