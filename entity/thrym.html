Thrym, the king of Jötunheimr, who stole the thunder god's hammer and demanded the marriage of a goddess for its return.

Who It Is

Thrym is the king of the frost giants, best known as the antagonist of the most famous comic poem in the Poetic Edda: Þrymskviða (The Lay of Thrym). The story begins when Thrym steals Thor's hammer Mjölnir and demands the goddess Freyja as his bride in exchange for its return. The gods despair until Heimdall suggests a plan: Thor must dress as Freyja, wear bridal linen and a veil, and go to Jötunheimr posing as the goddess. Thor is deeply reluctant but goes, accompanied by Loki disguised as the bridesmaid. At the wedding feast, Thor (as 'Freyja') eats an entire ox, eight salmon, and three barrels of mead — alarming the giants. Loki explains that 'Freyja' hasn't eaten for eight days in anticipation. When Thrym lifts the veil to kiss his bride, he recoils at Thor's burning eyes; Loki explains that 'she' hasn't slept for eight nights. Finally, the hammer is brought out to sanctify the marriage — and Thor seizes it, kills Thrym and all the giants and giantesses at the wedding. Thrym represents the arrogant, greedy giant who overreaches and whose schemes crumble in the face of divine power.

Form

Thrym is described in 'Þrymskviða' as a powerful jötunn, a giant of immense stature, though the poem gives few physical details. He is wealthy, as evidenced by his boasts of having 'gold-horned cattle' and 'black oxen' in abundance, and he possesses treasured items like his dog and his horse. He is likely depicted with the typical attributes of a frost giant: massive size, rough features, and a commanding presence. In artistic depictions, he is often shown as a bearded, burly figure, sometimes with icy blue skin or frost-covered hair, though these are later artistic interpretations.

Powers

Theft and Concealment Thrym is able to steal Thor's hammer, the most powerful weapon of the gods, and hide it deep underground. This demonstrates his ability to act with stealth and cunning against the gods, a rare feat for a giant. Þrymskviða, Poetic Edda His theft is discovered immediately, and he is unable to use the hammer himself, as it is a divine weapon attuned to Thor. He only uses it as a bargaining chip. None, though the mechanics of how he stole it are not described.

Command over the Frost Giants As 'king of the frost giants', Thrym has authority over his people, as seen when he orders his giants to prepare the wedding feast and when he demands the presence of 'the bride' and her attendants. Þrymskviða, Poetic Edda His authority is limited to his own realm and does not extend to other giant clans or the gods. None.

Superhuman Strength and Vitality (Giant Physiology) Like all jötnar, Thrym possesses immense physical strength and endurance, typical of his kind. His ability to consume an entire ox, eight salmon, and three barrels of mead at the wedding feast highlights his prodigious appetite, which is a hallmark of giant nature. Þrymskviða, Poetic Edda Despite his strength, he is no match for Thor in direct combat, as evidenced by his swift demise when Thor retrieves his hammer. None.

Behavior & Habitat

Thrym is bold, arrogant, and confident in his power. He steals Thor's hammer, Mjölnir, and then demands Freyja as his bride in exchange for its return, demonstrating both his audacity and his desire for legitimacy through marriage to a goddess. He is also somewhat gullible and easily fooled by Loki's ruse, failing to see through Thor's disguise as Freyja. His behavior at the 'wedding' — eating an entire ox and eight salmon, drinking three barrels of mead, and glaring with 'burning' eyes — reveals his boisterous, gluttonous nature, which contrasts with the expected decorum of a bride. He is ultimately undone by his own desires and his failure to recognize his enemy.

Accounts

Thrym, the king of the frost giants, steals Thor's hammer, Mjölnir, and hides it eight leagues underground. He then demands Freyja, the goddess of love and fertility, as his bride in exchange for its return. Freyja is furious and refuses, causing the gods to convene. Loki suggests that Thor himself should dress as Freyja, wearing a bridal veil and necklace, to travel to Jötunheimr and retrieve the hammer. Thor reluctantly agrees, and Loki accompanies him as his 'maid'. Upon arrival, Thrym welcomes his 'bride' and holds a grand feast. Thor, disguised, eats an entire ox, eight salmon, and three barrels of mead, and glares with terrifying eyes. Thrym is amazed but is reassured by Loki that Freyja simply missed her husband and was eager for the wedding. When Thrym brings out Mjölnir to consecrate the wedding, Thor seizes it, throws off his disguise, and slays Thrym and all the giants in the hall.

After Thrym's death at the wedding feast, Thor's rampage through the giant's hall is described with grim humor. He strikes down not only Thrym but the entire wedding party, including Thrym's aged sister who had come to bless the marriage. The poem ends with Thor retrieving Mjölnir and a triumphant return. However, skaldic poetry and Saxo Grammaticus hint at lingering tensions: the theft of Mjölnir was a humiliation for the gods that required deception to resolve, playing on anxieties about the giants' growing boldness in the lead-up to Ragnarök. Some interpretations view the poem as a fertility myth—Thor, dressing as the fertility goddess Freyja, symbolically reclaims both masculine power (the hammer) and productive order.

Field Notes

☘ The poem 'Þrymskviða' is one of the most frequently adapted Norse myths, appearing in everything from opera to Marvel comics.

☘ Thor's disguise is so effective that he eats an entire ox, eight salmon, and three barrels of mead, and still manages to pass as Freyja, thanks to Loki's quick thinking.

☘ In the poem, Heimdall, the watchman of the gods, is the one who suggests the idea of dressing Thor as a bride, making him an unlikely fashion consultant.

☘ The story has been interpreted as a satire of Viking wedding customs, as well as a commentary on gender roles and the fluidity of identity.

☘ Thrym's demand for Freyja is so outrageous that it causes the goddess herself to 'snort' with rage, and her necklace, the Brísingamen, breaks in her fury.

Scholarly Debates
Thrym's role in the mythos
Thrym is a significant figure, representing the archetypal giant who challenges the gods' authority. ⇌ Thrym is a minor character, appearing only in a single poem and possibly being a literary invention.
Evidence for A: His story is one of the most famous and widely retold Norse myths, suggesting his importance.
Evidence for B: He is not mentioned in any other surviving source, casting doubt on his prominence in the broader tradition.
The nature of the poem 'Þrymskviða'
The poem is a serious myth about the theft of a divine weapon and the restoration of order. ⇌ The poem is a parody or comedy, mocking the gods and gender roles.
Evidence for A: The poem's structure and language are consistent with other eddic poems.
Evidence for B: The absurd premise of Thor dressing as a bride and the exaggerated feasting suggest a humorous intent.
What We Don't Know

No archaeological evidence directly related to Thrym has been found. The poem is the sole source. Further research could explore the poem's relationship to other eddic poems and its place in the oral tradition. The exact nature of Thrym's 'kingdom' and his relationship to other giant clans is not detailed.

A way to hold it in your mind

Thrym is like a schoolyard bully who steals the quarterback's football and demands the prom queen in exchange. He's confident, brash, and completely out of his depth when the quarterback shows up to the negotiation in a prom dress and a wig. The story is a perfect blend of high-stakes drama and slapstick comedy, reminding us that even the mightiest gods can be brought low by their own pride — and that sometimes, the best way to defeat a giant is with a little bit of wit and a whole lot of disguise.
