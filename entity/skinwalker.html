Who It Is

Imagine a figure that is not a monster from the deep, but a neighbor, a healer, a trusted elder who has secretly chosen the darkest path. The Yee Naaldlooshii, or 'skinwalker', is a witch from Navajo tradition who wears the skin of a predator to become it, a being of profound betrayal who walks the line between the human world and the wild, between healing and harm. It is a legend so potent that it is not told lightly, even today, and its power has leaked into the modern world as a symbol of the ultimate fear of the 'other' within our own community.

Skinwalkers are believed to be motivated by a desire for power, wealth, and dominance over others. Their actions are malevolent and destructive: causing illness, death, poverty, and misfortune. They are said to travel at night, sometimes at supernatural speeds, and may knock on doors to test their power or lure victims out. They are known to be extremely secretive and will do anything to protect their identity, as being exposed is believed to strip them of their power.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The Navajo term 'Yee Naaldlooshii' translates roughly to 'with it, he goes on all fours' or 'he who walks on all fours with it' (the 'it' referring to the animal skin). The English term 'skinwalker' is a direct translation of the concept of wearing an animal's skin to transform.

Powers
Animal Transformation

The ability to transform into any animal by wearing its skin. The most common forms are predatory animals like coyotes, wolves, and bears. They are said to retain their human intelligence while in animal form.

Limit: The transformation requires the skin of the animal. It is believed that if the skin is damaged or hidden, the skinwalker can be trapped in animal form or killed. Some accounts say they move with an unnatural gait or are unable to speak perfectly in animal form.

Cursing / 'Shooting' Illness

The ability to cause illness, injury, or death by 'shooting' a physical object (such as a bone, a piece of ash, or a pebble) into a victim's body, or by performing a ritual over a personal item or footprint.

Limit: The curse is not absolute; it can be countered by a powerful medicine man (Hataałii) performing the appropriate anti-witchcraft ceremony (like the 'Evilway' or 'Lifeway' chants). The victim's own spiritual strength and the skill of the medicine man are factors.

Supernatural Speed and Stealth

They are said to be able to move at incredible speeds, faster than any human or animal, and to be able to move silently and unseen through the night.

Limit: Not well-defined. Some stories suggest they tire or can be outrun by a determined victim in a vehicle.

Mental Influence and Illusion

Some accounts suggest they can read minds, know your secrets, and use this knowledge to manipulate or terrify you. They are also said to be able to create illusions to lure victims.

Limit: Not well-defined. It is often implied that their power is limited by the victim's knowledge of them and their own spiritual strength.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In human form, a skinwalker is believed to be an ordinary-looking Navajo person, often a medicine man or woman who has chosen the path of witchcraft (ántʼįįhnii). They are not inherently marked by appearance. When transformed, they are said to be able to take the form of any animal—coyote, wolf, fox, bear, bird, or even a domestic animal like a horse or dog. Some accounts describe them as imperfect or uncanny in animal form, moving with a strange

Symbols — The primary symbol is the animal pelt, particularly of a predator like a coyote or wolf. The act of wearing the skin is the central transformative act. The night itself, and the number four (sacred to the Navajo), are associated with their rituals. They are also associated with the color black, representing the dark path they have chosen.

Domains — The Yee Naaldlooshii belongs to the liminal spaces of the Navajo homeland (Dinétah) in the Four Corners region of the Southwestern United States. They are active in the deserts, canyons, and mesas, but are also believed to operate within the community, at night, and at the edges of civilization. They are creatures of the in-between: between human and animal, life and death, and the physical and spiritual worlds.

Accounts I
The Generic Warning TaleNavajo Oral Tradition

Unknown, pre-contact to present

A person is driving or walking alone at night and sees an animal—a coyote or wolf—acting strangely. It runs alongside the car at impossible speed, or it stands on its hind legs, or its eyes gleam with an unnatural intelligence. The person realizes it is not a normal animal but a Yee Naaldlooshii. The encounter is a warning, a test, or a prelude to an attack. The person must escape and seek the help of a medicine man to cleanse themselves of the encounter. The traveler is terrified and must undergo a purification ceremony to remove the spiritual contamination of the encounter.

Accounts II
The Exposed SkinwalkerNavajo Oral Tradition

Unknown

A person suspects a neighbor or a local medicine man of being a skinwalker. They set a trap: they hide the skin of an animal that the suspected witch is known to use, or they find the skin and damage it. Without their skin, the skinwalker is trapped in human form or is forced to reveal themselves. Once exposed, their power is broken, and they may be killed or driven out of the community. The skinwalker loses their power and is dealt with by the community. The person who exposed them is seen as brave and has protected the community.

The Medicine Man's HealingsNavajo Oral Tradition and Ethnographic Records (e.g., Kluckhohn)

19th-20th century

A family is suffering from a string of misfortunes: illness, death of livestock, bad luck. They consult a Hataałii (medicine man), who diagnoses the problem as the work of a skinwalker. The medicine man performs a complex ceremony, such as the 'Evilway' chant, to identify the witch, draw out the cursed object, and restore balance and harmony (hózhǫ́) to the family. The ceremony is a public and community event, reinforcing social bonds against the threat of the antisocial witch. The family is healed, and the community's spiritual and social order is restored. The identity of the skinwalker may or may not be revealed.

Skinwalkers are the same as wendigos. — They are completely different figures from different cultures. The wendigo is an Algonquian spirit of winter and cannibalism, while the skinwalker is a Navajo witch who wears animal skins. The conflation is a modern internet and media invention.

All Native American tribes have a 'skinwalker' legend. — The Yee Naaldlooshii is specific to the Navajo (Diné) people. Other tribes have their own shapeshifter or witch figures, but they are distinct and should not be lumped together.

Skinwalkers are a type of werewolf. — While both involve human-to-animal transformation, the skinwalker is a witch who intentionally and ritually transforms by wearing an animal's skin, not a person afflicted by a curse or a lunar cycle. The transformation is a deliberate act of dark magic, not an involuntary affliction.

Skinwalker Ranch is a sacred Navajo site or is directly connected to the legend. — The 'Skinwalker Ranch' in Utah is a privately owned property. The name was applied in the 1990s by its then-owner, Robert Bigelow, who was interested in paranormal phenomena. There is no evidence that the ranch holds any special significance in Navajo tradition, and its connection to the skinwalker legend is a modern marketing and media creation.

It is appropriate to discuss skinwalkers openly and casually. — For many Navajo people, the skinwalker is a serious and taboo subject. Discussing it openly, especially with outsiders, is considered disrespectful and even dangerous. The silence is a form of respect and self-protection.
