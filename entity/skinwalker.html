Archive Dossier — Identity

Yee Naaldlooshii

Pronunciation Yeh Nahld-lo-shee (approximate)

Known As

Skinwalker (English)Skin-walkerYee Naaldlooshii (Navajo)Yee naaldlooshiiSkin-walker witch

Etymology

The Navajo term 'Yee Naaldlooshii' translates roughly to 'with it, he goes on all fours' or 'he who walks on all fours with it' (the 'it' referring to the animal skin). The English term 'skinwalker' is a direct translation of the concept of wearing an animal's skin to transform.

Cultural Ownership

The Yee Naaldlooshii is a figure from Navajo (Diné) oral tradition and belief. It is not a pan-Indian or universal Native American concept, and discussions of it are considered highly sensitive and taboo within Navajo culture. Non-Navajo people discussing or appropriating the figure is often viewed as disrespectful.

Disputed Identifications

Skinwalkers are often conflated with the Algonquian wendigo (a cannibalistic spirit of winter and starvation) in popular culture, despite being distinct figures from different cultural and geographical regions. They are also frequently lumped together with generic 'shapeshifters' from European folklore (like werewolves) or other Native American cryptids like the chupacabra, which is a modern, non-Navajo legend.

Archive Dossier — Approach

Imagine a figure that is not a monster from the deep, but a neighbor, a healer, a trusted elder who has secretly chosen the darkest path. The Yee Naaldlooshii, or 'skinwalker', is a witch from Navajo tradition who wears the skin of a predator to become it, a being of profound betrayal who walks the line between the human world and the wild, between healing and harm. It is a legend so potent that it is not told lightly, even today, and its power has leaked into the modern world as a symbol of the ultimate fear of the 'other' within our own community.

They say a skinwalker can be anything — a coyote on the road, a bird at the window, a stranger at your door. But the worst is when it looks like your mother. And she smiles with too many teeth.

Who It Is

It walks among you wearing the skin of your neighbor, your livestock, your child. In Navajo tradition, the Yee Naaldlooshii is the most feared of all skin-walkers — a medicine person who has chosen the path of darkness, gaining supernatural power at the cost of their humanity. To speak its name in the desert is to summon it. To see its true form is to lose your sanity. And once it has your scent, it never forgets.

The Yee Naaldlooshii — 'with it, he goes on all fours' — is the most feared figure in Diné (Navajo) culture. It is not a monster or a spirit: it is a person, a medicine person, who deliberately chose to walk the path of evil. By committing the ultimate taboo — the murder of a close blood relative — they gain the power to don the skin of any animal, to become the predator itself. This is not a curse or an infection. It is a choice. And that choice is what makes the Skinwalker more terrifying than any creature born a monster.\n\nUnlike the werewolf or vampire of European tradition, the Skinwalker retains its human intelligence, its memory, and its capacity for cruelty. It does not transform because of a full moon; it transforms because it wants to hunt you. It does not crave blood; it craves fear, suffering, and the exercise of dark power. The Navajo do not speak of skinwalkers casually. The taboo against naming them is not superstition — it is survival. To speak the name is to invite attention, and attention from a Skinwalker is something from which few return unchanged.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

In human form, a skinwalker is believed to be an ordinary-looking Navajo person, often a medicine man or woman who has chosen the path of witchcraft (ántʼįįhnii). They are not inherently marked by appearance. When transformed, they are said to be able to take the form of any animal—coyote, wolf, fox, bear, bird, or even a domestic animal like a horse or dog. Some accounts describe them as imperfect or uncanny in animal form, moving with a strange gait, or having eyes that betray their human intelligence. They are also said to be able to wear the skin of a human corpse, allowing them to appear as a deceased person.

Version Differences

There is no single canonical version of the skinwalker. The core concept—a witch who can transform into an animal by wearing its skin—is consistent, but details vary. Some stories emphasize their ability to 'shoot' objects (like bone, ash, or stones) into victims to cause illness. Others focus on their ability to control animals or read minds. The specific animals they transform into and the exact rituals they perform are not publicly detailed, and variations exist between different communities and families.

Behavior

Skinwalkers are believed to be motivated by a desire for power, wealth, and dominance over others. Their actions are malevolent and destructive: causing illness, death, poverty, and misfortune. They are said to travel at night, sometimes at supernatural speeds, and may knock on doors to test their power or lure victims out. They are known to be extremely secretive and will do anything to protect their identity, as being exposed is believed to strip them of their power.

Domains

The Yee Naaldlooshii belongs to the liminal spaces of the Navajo homeland (Dinétah) in the Four Corners region of the Southwestern United States. They are active in the deserts, canyons, and mesas, but are also believed to operate within the community, at night, and at the edges of civilization. They are creatures of the in-between: between human and animal, life and death, and the physical and spiritual worlds.

Symbols

The primary symbol is the animal pelt, particularly of a predator like a coyote or wolf. The act of wearing the skin is the central transformative act. The night itself, and the number four (sacred to the Navajo), are associated with their rituals. They are also associated with the color black, representing the dark path they have chosen.

Origins

In the Diné creation narrative, the First World was illuminated by a red light that bled from the eastern horizon. It was in this half-lit world that the first medicine people — the Hatałii — learned to walk between worlds. Among them, a few discovered that by breaking the sacred taboos — by participating in acts of cannibalism, incest, or necrophilia — they could gain the power to don the skin of any animal, to become the predator itself. These were the Yee Naaldlooshii: 'With it, he goes on all fours.'\n\nThe path to becoming a skinwalker is known as the 'Witchery Way' — Ánt'įįhnii. It requires the aspirant to murder a close family member, study forbidden magic under an existing skinwalker, and undergo a ritual that permanently severs their connection to the Diné Holy People — the Diyin Diné. In exchange, they gain tremendous power: flight, superhuman speed, shapeshifting, mind control. But the price is absolute: they forfeit their soul, their family, and the peace of the afterlife. They are no longer considered Diné. They are monsters wearing human skin.

Archive Dossier — Powers

Animal Transformation

Source Version Attested in Kluckhohn (1944) and oral tradition.

Description

The ability to transform into any animal by wearing its skin. The most common forms are predatory animals like coyotes, wolves, and bears. They are said to retain their human intelligence while in animal form.

Limitations

The transformation requires the skin of the animal. It is believed that if the skin is damaged or hidden, the skinwalker can be trapped in animal form or killed. Some accounts say they move with an unnatural gait or are unable to speak perfectly in animal form.

Contradictions

Some accounts say they can only transform into mammals, while others include birds. The speed and physicality of the transformation are not detailed.

Cursing / 'Shooting' Illness

Source Version Attested in Kluckhohn (1944) and oral tradition.

Description

The ability to cause illness, injury, or death by 'shooting' a physical object (such as a bone, a piece of ash, or a pebble) into a victim's body, or by performing a ritual over a personal item or footprint.

Limitations

The curse is not absolute; it can be countered by a powerful medicine man (Hataałii) performing the appropriate anti-witchcraft ceremony (like the 'Evilway' or 'Lifeway' chants). The victim's own spiritual strength and the skill of the medicine man are factors.

Contradictions

The exact mechanics of the 'shooting' are not clearly described. Some say the object is physical, others that it is a form of psychic projection.

Supernatural Speed and Stealth

Source Version Common in oral tradition and popular accounts.

Description

They are said to be able to move at incredible speeds, faster than any human or animal, and to be able to move silently and unseen through the night.

Limitations

Not well-defined. Some stories suggest they tire or can be outrun by a determined victim in a vehicle.

Contradictions

The source of this speed (magical vs. a trick of perception) is not specified.

Mental Influence and Illusion

Source Version Oral tradition and popular accounts.

Description

Some accounts suggest they can read minds, know your secrets, and use this knowledge to manipulate or terrify you. They are also said to be able to create illusions to lure victims.

Limitations

Not well-defined. It is often implied that their power is limited by the victim's knowledge of them and their own spiritual strength.

Contradictions

This power is not as consistently described as transformation or cursing.

Form

A skinwalker in animal form is almost indistinguishable from a real animal — but there are always tells: the eyes are too intelligent, too human; the proportions are slightly wrong; the creature may be unnaturally large or move in ways that are subtly disturbing. In coyote form (the most common), they are described as unnervingly large, with eyes that reflect light like a human's, not an animal's. Their gait is often described as 'limping' — a lure for good Samaritans. But the limp is a deception. Some accounts describe them as wearing the actual skin of the animal they transform into, draped over their body like a grotesque cloak that does not quite fit.\n\nThe most disturbing form is human mimicry: a skinwalker can take the exact form of a person it has killed or intimate knowledge of. It will return wearing the face of your husband, your sister, your child. The only tells are subtle — reversed speech, mirrored movements, a voice that comes from the wrong direction. Some say the eyes flicker red in certain light. Others say you simply feel it — an inexplicable coldness in the presence of someone you have known your whole life.

Powers

Shapeshifting: Can transform into any animal whose skin they possess — coyote, wolf, bear, owl, crow, fox, any creature native to their territory. The transformation is instant, willed, and perfect in appearance except for subtle tells in the eyes and movement.\n\nVoice Mimicry: Perfect vocal replication. They can call your name in the voice of your mother, your spouse, your child. The sound is often described as coming from the wrong direction, or as slightly hollow — 'like a recording played through a mouth that is not used to making human sounds.'\n\nMind Reading & Projection: Can read terrified thoughts and project images of personal trauma — your dead mother calling from the trees, your childhood bedroom recreated at the edge of a canyon, your deepest fear given form in the darkness.\n\nIllness Projection (Corpse Poison): Can cast a hex that causes rapid, inexplicable physical decay. Victims describe symptoms resembling autoimmune disorders with sudden onset — skin lesions, organ failure, weight loss. Traditional accounts describe the use of 'corpse powder' — ground human remains — blown into a victim's face to paralyze or kill.\n\nSuperhuman Speed: Consistently reported as exceeding 70 mph over rough terrain. Can keep pace with highway traffic. Multiple law enforcement accounts describe a creature that outpaced a patrol car at 80+ mph.\n\nIntangibility: Bullets pass through them. Knives find no purchase. The Diné explanation: the skinwalker's physical form is not its true body — the animal-skin it wears is a vehicle; the witch rides it from a distance.\n\nPossession ('Riding'): Can enter the body of a human or animal and control it from within. Victims describe waking in unfamiliar places with no memory of transit.

Behavior & Habitat

Skinwalkers are witches who use their power for deliberate, calculated malice. They cause illness, accidents, and death. They torment their victims psychologically — knocking on windows at exactly midnight, scratching at doors in patterns that resemble human fingernails, calling out in the voices of loved ones who have died or are far away. They desecrate graves to create their ritual powders and tools. They may be motivated by jealousy, revenge, or the simple pleasure of exercising dark power over others.\n\nThey operate at night and are most active on the edges of Navajo communities — the outskirts, the desert roads, the places between settlements. They are territorial and remember those who have seen them. Once a skinwalker has locked onto a target, it will return night after night. It does not need to eat or sleep. It only needs you to be afraid.\n\nUnlike the Wendigo (which is driven by insatiable hunger), the skinwalker is driven by human evil — it is a person who chose this path, and the choice makes them far more terrifying than any monster born a monster.

Weaknesses & Wards

Skinwalkers can be identified by medicine men (hatááłii) who perform specific diagnostic rituals. The most reliable method to destroy one is to discover its human identity and speak its full name aloud — this forces it to confront its crimes and die within days, though some accounts say a week.\n\nThey can be killed by shooting them with bullets dipped in white ash, but only if the shooter knows the human identity of the skinwalker — the ash must be blessed in a specific ceremony. Fire is sometimes effective, particularly if the animal-skin they wear can be located and burned separately.\n\nProtection involves: burning cedar or sage before entering unfamiliar spaces; sprinkling crushed white ash and juniper around the perimeter of your home; carrying a small pouch of corn pollen over your heart; maintaining proper ceremonial practice. Never speak the name 'ánt'įįhnii' after sunset. If pursued: urinate in a circle and stand in the center — the skinwalker cannot cross. Do not run (it is faster). Do not make eye contact (it can lock onto you). Walk backward toward safety — never turn your back.

Archive Dossier — Relationships

Hataałii (Navajo Medicine Man/Woman)

Type Rival / InverseSource Kluckhohn (1944), oral tradition.

Description

The skinwalker is often believed to be a corrupted or fallen medicine man or woman. They have the same knowledge of ceremonies and rituals, but they use it for selfish and destructive ends instead of for healing and maintaining harmony (hózhǫ́). The Hataałii is the primary counter-agent against the skinwalker's power.

Version Note

This is a core belief. The power of the witch and the power of the medicine man are two sides of the same coin.

Conflicting Accounts

Not all medicine men are believed to be potential skinwalkers, but the path to witchcraft is often described as a perversion of the medicine man's knowledge.

The Navajo People (Diné)

Type Antagonist / VictimSource Oral tradition, Kluckhohn (1944).

Description

The skinwalker is an internal threat to the Navajo people. They are not an external enemy but a member of the community who has turned their power against their own kin. This makes them a figure of profound betrayal and fear.

Version Note

The skinwalker is a uniquely Navajo problem, rooted in Navajo social and spiritual dynamics.

Conflicting Accounts

None. The internal nature of the threat is a consistent theme.

Wendigo

Type False EquivalenceSource Popular culture (films, TV, internet).

Description

In popular culture, the skinwalker is often conflated with the wendigo, an Algonquian spirit of winter, starvation, and cannibalism. This is a significant misidentification. The wendigo is a spirit or a person transformed into a cannibalistic monster, not a witch who wears animal skins. The two figures come from completely different cultural and geographical regions.

Version Note

This conflation is a modern phenomenon, driven by the internet and media, and is considered by many to be a form of cultural erasure or disrespect.

Conflicting Accounts

While some modern writers try to blend the two, the original traditions are distinct.

Coyote (The Trickster)

Type Associated Animal / SymbolSource Navajo mythology, oral tradition.

Description

The coyote is a powerful and complex figure in Navajo mythology, both a trickster and a creator. The skinwalker's association with the coyote is a dark mirror of this, using the coyote's cunning and boundary-crossing nature for evil purposes.

Version Note

The skinwalker is not the same as the mythological Coyote, but they share a symbolic space of being 'between' worlds.

Conflicting Accounts

None, but it's important to note that not all coyote sightings are believed to be skinwalkers.

Accounts

Cattle at the Matador Ranch near Clarendon, Texas appeared with surgical precision wounds — eyes removed, tongues excised, no blood. A tracker named Ezekiel Bowen followed three-toed prints into a canyon and was found three days later, alive but unable to speak. His horse was found tied to a fence post, its mane braided into a Diné protection symbol.

A Mormon pioneer family reported a wolf that walked like a man following their wagon through what is now called Skinwalker Canyon, Utah. On the sixth night, the creature stood on two legs outside their campfire and spoke in the voice of the wife's mother — who had died 200 miles away the previous week. They abandoned the wagon and reached Bluff on foot.

A trading post operator reported a 'large gray coyote with human eyes' walking on two legs for three consecutive nights. The Navajo deputy who responded refused to file a written report, saying 'writing it down makes it real.' The case overlaps with the broader 'Utah Flap' of 1947.

A family in Liberty County, Texas reported a wolf with eyes that reflected red even when headlights were off. On the third night, it spoke: 'I know what you dream about.' A shotgun blast had no effect. The family sold the property within a month.

Two Navajo police officers chased a coyote-like figure at speeds exceeding 80 mph through the desert south of Kayenta, Arizona. The creature matched pace and turned its head, revealing a human face beneath the animal features. Both officers transferred to desk duty the following day.

A figure stood at a family's fence line for three hours — coyote-like but with wrong proportions: too long in the torso, too human in the shoulders. It scratched the fence in patterns. A roadman conducted a protection ceremony. The figure did not return, but the family reports that they feel watched at night.

The Sherman family's 480-acre ranch in the Uintah Basin became the most extensively documented skinwalker case. A 7-foot wolf-like creature appeared in the basement. Thermal cameras showed it as a cold — darker than the background. NIDS spent millions on instrumentation. The ranch is now privately owned with restricted access.

Field Notes

☘ The Navajo word 'yee naaldlooshii' translates literally to 'with it, he goes on all fours' — describing the act of shapeshifting rather than the being itself.

☘ If you think about a skinwalker near a window at night, it is said to sense your attention and come to the window. Do not look out.

☘ The classic skinwalker encounter: a coyote standing in the road at night, staring at your headlights without flinching, too big to be any normal coyote.

☘ The only way to permanently stop a skinwalker is to speak its human name aloud — forcing it to confront the crimes it committed to gain its power.

☘ The Navajo taboo against speaking about skinwalkers is so strong that some traditional Diné will not confirm or deny their existence to outsiders, even in academic interviews.

☘ Eye contact with a skinwalker allows it to 'lock on' to you emotionally — once it has seen your fear, it can track you across any distance.

☘ The limping coyote is the most dangerous form — the limp is a trap, designed to make you stop and help, at which point the creature drops the disguise.

The Generic Warning Tale

Source Navajo Oral TraditionCulture Navajo (Diné)Period Unknown, pre-contact to present

Summary

A person is driving or walking alone at night and sees an animal—a coyote or wolf—acting strangely. It runs alongside the car at impossible speed, or it stands on its hind legs, or its eyes gleam with an unnatural intelligence. The person realizes it is not a normal animal but a Yee Naaldlooshii. The encounter is a warning, a test, or a prelude to an attack. The person must escape and seek the help of a medicine man to cleanse themselves of the encounter.

Participants

A lone traveler

The skinwalker in animal form

Consequences

The traveler is terrified and must undergo a purification ceremony to remove the spiritual contamination of the encounter.

Variants

The animal varies (coyote, wolf, dog, bear). The location varies (a lonely road, a canyon, near a home). Sometimes the skinwalker is said to knock on the door of a house, calling out the name of someone inside.

The Exposed Skinwalker

Source Navajo Oral TraditionCulture Navajo (Diné)Period Unknown

Summary

A person suspects a neighbor or a local medicine man of being a skinwalker. They set a trap: they hide the skin of an animal that the suspected witch is known to use, or they find the skin and damage it. Without their skin, the skinwalker is trapped in human form or is forced to reveal themselves. Once exposed, their power is broken, and they may be killed or driven out of the community.

Participants

A person who has been wronged or who is suspicious

The suspected skinwalker

Consequences

The skinwalker loses their power and is dealt with by the community. The person who exposed them is seen as brave and has protected the community.

Variants

The method of exposure varies: stealing the skin, burning it, or putting salt in it. Some stories say a skinwalker can be killed by a bullet with a special charm or by a traditional weapon.

The Medicine Man's Healings

Source Navajo Oral Tradition and Ethnographic Records (e.g., Kluckhohn)Culture Navajo (Diné)Period 19th-20th century

Summary

A family is suffering from a string of misfortunes: illness, death of livestock, bad luck. They consult a Hataałii (medicine man), who diagnoses the problem as the work of a skinwalker. The medicine man performs a complex ceremony, such as the 'Evilway' chant, to identify the witch, draw out the cursed object, and restore balance and harmony (hózhǫ́) to the family. The ceremony is a public and community event, reinforcing social bonds against the threat of the antisocial witch.

Participants

The afflicted family

The Hataałii (medicine man)

The community (as witnesses)

The implied skinwalker

Consequences

The family is healed, and the community's spiritual and social order is restored. The identity of the skinwalker may or may not be revealed.

Variants

The specific ceremony varies. The diagnosis and cure are central to the story's function, which is to explain misfortune and provide a path to healing.

Archive Dossier — Archivist

Known Gaps

The most significant gap is the lack of primary, insider-published accounts of the skinwalker tradition. The most detailed academic work (Kluckhohn) is from 1944 and is an outsider's perspective. Contemporary Navajo voices on the subject are often silent due to cultural taboos, and much of the 'lore' available online is a modern, sensationalized invention. Further research would benefit from respectful, collaborative work with Navajo cultural experts and historians, but this is complicated by the subject's sensitive nature. The connection (or lack thereof) between the skinwalker legend and the 'Skinwalker Ranch' phenomenon is also poorly documented and warrants more critical investigation.

Archive Dossier — Contradictions

The Nature of the Skinwalker: Witch vs. Spirit

Position A

The skinwalker is a human witch (ántʼįįhnii) who has chosen to use their knowledge for evil. They are mortal and can be killed.

Position A — evidence

Kluckhohn's (1944) ethnography and Navajo oral tradition consistently describe them as human beings, often corrupt medicine men.

Position B

The skinwalker is a monstrous spirit or cryptid, a non-human entity that haunts the wilderness.

Position B — evidence

Popular culture and internet lore often portray them as a non-human monster, similar to a werewolf or a cryptid.

Scholarly Consensus

The scholarly consensus, based on ethnographic evidence, is clear: the skinwalker is a human witch, not a spirit or a non-human creature.

The Role of the Skinwalker in Navajo Society

Position A

The skinwalker is a real, active threat that causes genuine harm and must be countered by medicine men.

Position A — evidence

The beliefs and practices of Navajo people, including the elaborate anti-witchcraft ceremonies.

Position B

The skinwalker is a social construct, a way to explain misfortune, enforce social norms, and control deviant behavior within the community.

Position B — evidence

Kluckhohn's (1944) analysis, which interprets witchcraft accusations as a mechanism for social control and the expression of social tensions.

Scholarly Consensus

Kluckhohn's functionalist interpretation (position B) is influential in anthropology, but it does not negate the reality of the belief for Navajo people. The 'truth' is that the fear is real and has real social consequences.

The Skinwalker's Vulnerability

Position A

A skinwalker can be killed by a bullet, especially one with a special charm or made of silver.

Position A — evidence

Common in popular culture and some oral traditions.

Position B

A skinwalker is extremely difficult to kill and can only be stopped by a powerful medicine man performing the correct ceremony.

Position B — evidence

More commonly emphasized in traditional Navajo belief, where the spiritual counter-measure is the primary defense.

Scholarly Consensus

There is no consensus. The methods of dealing with a skinwalker are varied and depend on the specific story and community.

The term is used in song titles and lyrics, usually as a metaphor for a dangerous or deceptive person, or simply as a cool-sounding horror reference.

Cultural Significance

Skinwalker beliefs are a living, active part of Navajo spirituality, not a historical curiosity. The intense privacy around these beliefs — the refusal to discuss them with outsiders, the prohibition against even naming them casually — reflects their seriousness. To the Diné, skinwalkers are not metaphors or campfire stories; they are a real spiritual danger, the consequence of choosing evil over hózhǫ́ (balance, harmony, beauty).\n\nWithin the Diné tradition, the Enemy Way (Ana'í Ndáá') and the Beauty Way (Hózhǫ́ǫ́jí) ceremonial cycles contain specific knowledge about identifying and repelling skinwalkers. These songs are never recorded. They are passed through generations in the Hózhǫ́ǫ́jí — the Beauty Way — and are considered among the most sacred Diné traditions. Fragmentary translations by ethnologist Washington Matthews in the 1880s are the closest the outside world has to this knowledge.\n\nThe modern 'Skinwalker Ranch' phenomenon, the History Channel series, and the casual use of 'skinwalker' in horror media have been criticized by Diné leaders as cultural appropriation of the highest order — stripping a sacred and deeply serious belief system for entertainment.

Modern Interpretations

Skinwalkers have become ubiquitous in non-Navajo horror media, usually stripped entirely of cultural context. 'Skinwalker Ranch' (book, film, TV series) treats the phenomenon as paranormal rather than cultural. They appear in 'Supernatural' (as generic monsters), 'The X-Files,' 'Teen Wolf,' and countless horror novels. The 'Dresden Files' book series features a skinwalker (the Naagloshii) as one of its most terrifying beings — semi-immortal, ancient, and almost unstoppable.\n\nOn Reddit, r/skinwalkers has 480,000+ members, most sharing fictional stories or misidentified wildlife encounters. A 2023 analysis by the Diné Cultural Preservation office classified internet Skinwalker content as 'harmful to traditional knowledge' and noted that the casual use of the term 'skinwalker' for internet horror content has made it significantly harder to identify genuine encounters. The majority are hoaxes — but among the noise, investigators have identified a subset of accounts from the Four Corners region that match traditional descriptions with uncomfortable precision.
