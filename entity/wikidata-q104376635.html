Who It Is

In the shadow of an enchanted castle, where the screams of captive knights and ladies echoed through stone corridors, there dwelt a giant whose name struck terror into the heart of Cornwall: Galligantua. He was no mere brute of the hills, but a sorcerer's pawn, wielding dark magic that could twist a duke's daughter into a white doe. Only one man dared to face him—Jack, the Giant Killer, armed with a cloak of invisibility and a sword of sharpest edge. Their confrontation would decide the fate of a hundred souls and break a spell that had held a noble lady in animal form for years.

Galligantua is depicted as a powerful and cruel giant who holds many knights and ladies captive. He is associated with a sorcerer, indicating a cunning and malevolent nature. Unlike other giants who are merely gluttonous, Galligantua uses magic to oppress his victims.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Galligantua' is likely derived from the French 'Gargantua', the giant created by François Rabelais, though the connection is speculative. It may be a corruption or adaptation of that name, reflecting the influence of French fairy tales on English chapbooks.

Pronunciation — gal-ee-GAN-too-uh (approximate)

Powers
Superhuman strength

As a giant, Galligantua possesses immense physical power, capable of overpowering ordinary men.

Limit: None specified, but he is ultimately defeated by Jack's cunning and magical items.

Control over captives

He holds many knights and ladies prisoner in his castle, indicating some form of authority or magical restraint.

Limit: He relies on the sorcerer's magic to maintain the transformation of the duke's daughter.

Association with sorcery

Galligantua is aided by a sorcerer who can transform humans into animals. It is unclear if Galligantua himself possesses magical abilities, but he benefits from them.

Limit: The sorcerer flees when Galligantua is beheaded, suggesting the magic is tied to the sorcerer, not the giant.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Galligantua is described as a giant, but no specific physical description is given in the standard texts. He is likely imagined as large and fearsome, consistent with other giants in the tale. The enchanted castle setting suggests he may be more civilized than other giants, but still monstrous.

Symbols — The enchanted castle and the white doe are key symbols. The castle represents imprisonment and dark magic, while the white doe symbolizes the transformed duke's daughter, innocence and nobility under a curse.

Domains — He dwells in an enchanted castle, which is described as being in a remote location, likely in Wales or the West Country of England, as Jack travels there after his adventures in Wales.

Accounts I
Jack's Final Adventure: The Defeat of GalligantuaThe History of Jack the Giant-Killer (chapbook, c. 1711)

Early modern (18th century)

After a series of adventures, Jack, now a knight of the Round Table, ventures forth alone. He meets an elderly man who directs him to an enchanted castle belonging to the giant Galligantus. The giant holds captive many knights and ladies, and a duke's daughter who has been transformed into a white doe by a sorcerer. Jack, using his cloak of invisibility, sword, and other magical items, enters the castle, beheads the giant, and the sorcerer flees. The spell is broken, the duke's daughter is restored to her human form, and the captives are freed. Jack then marries the duke's daughter at King Arthur's court, and they live happily ever after. The giant is killed, the sorcerer escapes, the captives are freed, and Jack marries the duke's daughter, receiving an estate from King Arthur.
