The Approach

High in the craggy mountains of ancient Arcadia, where the waters of the Styx were said to trickle from a sheer cliff, lay the forgotten city of Nonacris—a place so steeped in myth that its very name conjured the primordial world of Lycaon and the gods. Though Pausanias found only ruins, its legacy endures in the verses of Ovid and the geographies of Pliny, a ghost of a town that once stood at the threshold of the underworld.

Identity

Nonacris

Pronunciation non-AK-ris (Ancient Greek: Νώνακρις)

Known As

NonakrisNonacridaNonakrida

Etymology

Named after Nonacris, the wife of Lycaon, the mythical king of Arcadia. The name may derive from Greek roots meaning 'nine' (ennea) and 'top' (akris), but this is uncertain.

Cultural Ownership

Ancient Greek (Arcadia)

Disputed Identifications

Ancient writers place it in Arcadia, but modern scholars debate the exact site. Some suggest Kerpini, others near Solos. The identification is not definitively confirmed.

Description

Appearance

The town was situated in a mountainous region, near a cliff from which water believed to be the Styx trickled. By the time of Pausanias, it was in ruins.

Symbols & Iconography

The town is associated with the water of the Styx, which was said to be deadly. It may have been a cult site for the worship of Styx or related chthonic deities.

Behaviour & Personality

The town was a small polis in Arcadia, part of the state of Pheneus. It likely had a temple and other civic buildings, as indicated by archaeological finds near Kerpini.

Domains & Habitats

Located in the region of Pheneatis, northwest of Pheneus, in what is now Achaea, southern Greece. The area is mountainous, near the river Styx (now Mavroneri).

Version Differences

Ancient sources consistently place it in Arcadia, but the exact location is debated. Some modern scholars suggest Kerpini, others Solos.

Stories & Myths

Foundation by Lycaon

Source Pausanias, Description of Greece 8.17.6Culture Ancient GreekPeriod Mythical

Summary

The town was named after Nonacris, the wife of Lycaon, the mythical king of Arcadia. Lycaon was known for his impiety and was transformed into a wolf by Zeus.

Participants

Lycaon

Nonacris

Consequences

The town became a mythical site associated with the water of the Styx.

Variants

Some sources may attribute the name to a nymph, but the primary tradition is the wife of Lycaon.

The Water of the Styx

Source Herodotus, Histories 6.74; Pausanias 8.17.6Culture Ancient GreekPeriod Archaic to Roman

Summary

Near Nonacris, a stream of water trickled from a cliff, believed to be the water of the Styx. This water was said to be deadly, capable of dissolving any vessel except a horse's hoof. It was used as a test of death in oaths.

Participants

The Styx

Travelers

Consequences

The site became a curiosity and a place of pilgrimage for those wishing to see the deadly water.

Variants

Herodotus describes the water as coming from a rock, while Pausanias says it trickles from a cliff.
