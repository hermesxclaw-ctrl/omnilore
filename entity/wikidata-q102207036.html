The Approach

A name that echoes through centuries, Asterius belongs to many—a legendary king, a feared monster, and a forgotten saint. But who was the real Asterius? The answer may be as elusive as the labyrinth he once guarded.

Content advisory. Contains references to mythological violence, including human sacrifice and the killing of a child.
Identity

Asterius

Pronunciation /əˈstɪəriəs/ (uh-STEER-ee-uhs)

Known As

AsterionAsterius (Minotaur)Asterius of Crete

Etymology

From Greek ἀστέριος (asterios), meaning 'starry' or 'of the stars', derived from ἀστήρ (astēr), 'star'.

Cultural Ownership

Primarily Greek mythology; also appears in Roman adaptations and later Christian hagiography.

Disputed Identifications

The name Asterius/Asterion is applied to multiple distinct figures, including the Minotaur, a king of Crete, a giant, and several early Christian saints. Some ancient sources conflate these figures, leading to confusion.

Description

Appearance

The Minotaur Asterius is typically depicted with the body of a man and the head of a bull, though some ancient art shows a fully human figure with a bull's head or a bull's body with a human head. The king Asterion of Crete is described as a handsome mortal, while the giant Asterion is said to be a son of the river god or a giant with a hundred arms. Christian saints named Asterius have no specific physical descriptions.

Symbols & Iconography

The labyrinth is the primary symbol associated with the Minotaur. In art, the Minotaur is often shown in combat with Theseus. The name 'Asterius' itself evokes stars, but no specific star-related iconography is attached to the mythological figures.

Behaviour & Personality

The Minotaur is depicted as a savage, man-eating creature, confined to the labyrinth. The king Asterion is a wise ruler, while the giant Asterion is a fierce opponent. Christian saints are portrayed as pious and martyred.

Domains & Habitats

The Minotaur dwells in the labyrinth of Crete, built by Daedalus. King Asterion rules Crete. The giant Asterion is associated with various mythic locales. Saints are associated with their respective cities.

Version Differences

The Minotaur's origin varies: some say he was born from Pasiphaë and a bull, others say he was the son of Pasiphaë and Minos, or that he was a human prince named Asterion who was transformed. The name Asterius is also given to a son of Minos, which may be a confusion with the Minotaur.

Stories & Myths

The Birth of the Minotaur

Source Bibliotheca (Pseudo-Apollodorus), Metamorphoses (Ovid)Culture GreekPeriod Classical antiquity

Summary

King Minos of Crete prayed to Poseidon for a bull to sacrifice, but when a magnificent bull emerged from the sea, Minos kept it. As punishment, Poseidon made Pasiphaë, Minos's wife, fall in love with the bull. She coupled with it, giving birth to the Minotaur, a creature with a human body and a bull's head. Minos, ashamed, had Daedalus build the labyrinth to contain him.

Participants

Minos

Pasiphaë

Poseidon

Daedalus

The Minotaur

Consequences

The Minotaur was imprisoned, and Athens was forced to send seven youths and seven maidens every nine years as tribute to be devoured.

Variants

Some versions say the Minotaur was originally a human prince named Asterion, who was cursed or transformed. Others say he was the son of Minos and Pasiphaë, not of the bull.

Theseus and the Minotaur

Source Various (Plutarch, Ovid, etc.)Culture GreekPeriod Classical antiquity

Summary

Theseus, son of King Aegeus of Athens, volunteered to be among the tribute to Crete. With the help of Minos's daughter Ariadne, who gave him a ball of thread, Theseus entered the labyrinth, slew the Minotaur, and found his way out.

Participants

Theseus

Ariadne

The Minotaur

Minos

Consequences

Theseus escaped with Ariadne, but later abandoned her on Naxos. He forgot to change his ship's sails from black to white, causing his father Aegeus to commit suicide.

Variants

Some versions have Ariadne giving Theseus a sword or a wreath. Others say the Minotaur was killed in a different manner.

The Martyrdom of Asterius of Ostia

Source Butler's Lives of the SaintsCulture ChristianPeriod 3rd century CE

Summary

Asterius of Ostia was a Christian martyr who was put to death during the persecution of Emperor Alexander Severus. He was killed for his faith, and his relics were venerated.

Participants

Asterius

Consequences

He was recognized as a saint and martyr.

Variants

Details are sparse and may be legendary.

Contradictions & Disputes

Identity of the Minotaur

Position A

The Minotaur is a monster, half-man half-bull.

Position A — evidence

Most ancient sources describe him as a hybrid creature.

Position B

The Minotaur is a human prince named Asterion, cursed to have a bull's head.

Position B — evidence

Some late sources, like the mythographer Palaephatus, rationalize the myth, suggesting he was a human.

Scholarly Consensus

The hybrid monster is the standard version; the human version is a later rationalization.

Paternity of the Minotaur

Position A

The father is the Cretan bull.

Position A — evidence

Most sources say Pasiphaë coupled with the bull.

Position B

The father is Minos.

Position B — evidence

A few sources say Minos was the father, and the bull aspect was a punishment.

Scholarly Consensus

The bull is the more common father, but there is ambiguity.

The name Asterius/Asterion

Position A

Asterius is the original name of the Minotaur.

Position A — evidence

Some scholia and later texts name the Minotaur 'Asterius'.

Position B

Asterius is a separate figure, a king of Crete.

Position B — evidence

There is a distinct king Asterion in mythology.

Scholarly Consensus

The name is shared, but they are generally considered separate figures.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

Limited primary sources for the Christian saints named Asterius; many hagiographical details are legendary. The exact origin of the name 'Asterius' for the Minotaur is unclear. Scholarly debate exists on the rationalization of the myth. Modern reception is vast, but this dossier covers only a sample.

Research Scope

This dossier focuses on the mythological figures named Asterius, particularly the Minotaur of Greek legend, while also documenting other historical and legendary bearers of the name. Due to the fragmented nature of ancient sources, some information is reconstructed or disputed.
