Who It Is

Every child born to King Plemnaeus died with its first cry — until a strange woman arrived in Aegialea, took the next infant in her arms, and broke the curse. The woman was Demeter, and the child was Orthopolis, the king whose name means 'he who upholds the city' and whose 63-year reign bridged divine intervention and dynastic survival in the oldest king-list of Sicyon.

Pausanias attributes no direct speech, decision, or personality trait to Orthopolis. He is a genealogical bridge: the child whose survival ends his father's curse, and the father whose daughter continues the line to Apollo's son. Implicit characterization is passive — he is reared by a goddess, reigns long, and fathers the next generation. No moral judgment, lawgiving, or martial deed is recorded. All personality reconstruction would be speculative and is therefore flagged as unknown.

Kingship and Long Reign

Mortal kingship over Sicyon/Aegialea, with an exceptionally long reign of 63 years as recorded in both Pausanias and the Eusebian/Castor chronographic tradition. This longevity is a chronographic datum, not a supernatural power, and serves to anchor Sicyonian chronology in the deep mythical past.

Limit: No military, legislative, or miraculous deeds are attributed to the reign. The 63-year figure is likely a chronographic construct to fill time between the flood-era founder Apis and the historically more plausible late Bronze Age kings, not a claim of superhuman lifespan beyond mythic chronology.

Divine Fosterage (Passive Benefit)

As an infant, Orthopolis was reared by Demeter in disguise as a strange woman (xenē gynē). This is not a power wielded by Orthopolis but a divine favor that ensured his survival when all his older siblings died at first cry. It implies Demeter's kourotrophic (child-nurturing) function.

Limit: Orthopolis does not control or invoke Demeter; the goddess acts out of pity (oiktos) for Plemnaeus. No lasting gift, knowledge, or ability is said to result from this upbringing. Later sources do not claim he became a priest of Demeter or founded a cult.

Dynastic Fecundity (Genealogical Function)

Orthopolis breaks the pattern of infant death in his father's house and successfully fathers Chrysorthe, who in turn bears a viable heir (Coronus) with Apollo. His role is to restore reproductive continuity to the Sicyonian royal house.

Limit: No claim of extraordinary fertility beyond one attested daughter. The mother of Chrysorthe is unnamed in surviving sources. No other children are attested.

Accounts I
The Curse of Plemnaeus: Children Who Die at First CryPausanias, Description of Greece 2.5.8

Mythical early kingship period; recorded in 2nd century CE, reflecting Hellenistic mythographic sources (likely Castor and local Sicyonian histories)

Plemnaeus, 11th king of Sicyon and father of Orthopolis, suffers a mysterious affliction: every child borne to him by his wife dies immediately upon uttering its first cry (or first wail). The text implies repeated neonatal death, not stillbirth. The cause is not stated — no curse, divine anger, or medical explanation is given. The king is presented as unfortunate (dystychēs) rather than guilty, eliciting divine pity. The royal house faces extinction; without an heir, the Sicyonian succession is threatened. This sets the narrative stage for divine intervention. The episode establishes a pattern of vulnerable infant kings in Sicyonian myth, paralleling other Greek myths where a goddess saves a royal infant (e.g., Demeter and Demophon, Thetis and Achilles).

Demeter in Disguise: The Goddess as Nurse at AegialeaPausanias, Description of Greece 2.5.8

Mythical; recorded 2nd century CE

Taking pity (oikteirai) on Plemnaeus, the goddess Demeter comes to Aegialea — the ancient name for Sicyon before it was renamed after Sicyon — in the guise of a strange woman (xenē gynē, a foreign/strange woman). In this disguise she rears (etrephen) the infant Orthopolis for the king, ensuring his survival. Pausanias does not detail how she prevents the fatal cry, whether by divine nourishment, ritual, or simply by proper nursing. The motif mirrors Demeter's role as nurse to Demophon in the Homeric Hymn to Demeter, and to Triptolemus. Orthopolis survives and is established as heir, ending the cycle of death. The story provides an aetiology for Demeter's honor at Sicyon; Pausanias elsewhere notes Demeter sanctuaries in Sicyonia. It also elevates Orthopolis's status through divine fosterage without making him divine himself.

The Birth and Naming of Orthopolis — 'He Who Upholds the City'Pausanias 2.5.8; etymology via LSJ and modern onomastic analysis; chronographic placement via Eusebius/Jerome

Mythical; name preserved in Hellenistic chronography (Castor, 1st c. BCE) and Pausanias

Orthopolis is born to Plemnaeus and his wife after the goddess's intervention and is given a programmatic name meaning 'upholding the city.' Unlike his siblings, he does not die at first cry, implicitly because Demeter rears him. Pausanias does not state who names him or whether the name is prophetic. In the king-list he is counted as the 12th king of Sicyon, descendant of Apis, founder of the city. His birth marks the restoration of the lineage. Orthopolis becomes the legitimate heir, securing the Sicyonian stemma. His name, whether contemporary or retrojected by mythographers, signals political stability after crisis, a common function of king-list names (cf. Marathonius, Coronus).

Accounts II
The Sixty-Three Year Reign of the Twelfth KingPausanias 2.6.5; Eusebius Chronicon (Jerome, Syncellus); Castor FGrH 250 F 4

Mythical chronology systematized in Hellenistic period (Castor) and transmitted through Roman and Byzantine chronography

After succeeding his father Plemnaeus, Orthopolis reigns for 63 years — one of the longest reigns in the Sicyonian list (only Apis and a few others rival it). Pausanias states the figure explicitly; Eusebius/Jerome repeat it as 'LXIII years.' No events, wars, laws, foundations, or relations with other cities are recorded for the reign. The length functions chronographically to push Sicyonian origins deep into antiquity, making Sicyon the oldest Greek kingship in the chronographic tradition (older than Argos). The long reign anchors the Sicyonian chronology: Eusebius uses it to date Sicyonian kings relative to Biblical and Argive chronologies. Modern scholars view the 63-year figure as artificial, part of a Hellenistic effort to create a continuous timeline from the flood to the Dorian invasion. No archaeological horizon corresponds to it.

Chrysorthe, Apollo, and Coronus — The Divine GrandsonPausanias 2.6.5

Mythical; recorded 2nd century CE

Orthopolis fathers a daughter, Chrysorthe (Greek: Χρυσόρθη, 'Golden...'), who becomes the mother of Coronus (Κόρωνος) by the god Apollo. Pausanias states succinctly: 'Orthopolis had Chrysorthe, and Chrysorthe bore Coronus to Apollo.' Coronus then succeeds Orthopolis as king (in the main version). This introduces divine paternity into the Sicyonian line, paralleling other Peloponnesian genealogies where Apollo fathers a king (e.g., Ion, Asclepius). No love story, oracle, or seduction narrative is given; the union is reported as fact. The Sicyonian royal house gains Apolline legitimacy. Coronus's reign (like Orthopolis's) is part of the continuous king-list. The pattern of a mortal king's daughter bearing a god's son who becomes king recurs in Sicyonian genealogy (cf. later, Coronus's own descendants). The mother of Chrysorthe remains unnamed, emphasizing the patrilineal and divine links.

The Marathonius Variant — An Alternative SuccessionPausanias 2.6.6

Mythical variant recorded 2nd century CE; likely reflecting a second Hellenistic source Pausanias consulted

Pausanias reports: 'There is another account (logos) which says that the kingdom of Sicyon passed not to Coronus but to Marathonius.' Marathonius (Μαραθώνιος) is otherwise listed in the Eusebian king-list as the successor after Coronus (or directly after Orthopolis in some reconstructions). Pausanias does not reconcile the two versions, presenting them as parallel traditions. This suggests competing genealogical constructions in Sicyonian local histories, perhaps reflecting different family claims or chronographic schools. If Marathonius succeeds directly, the Apolline line through Coronus is either delayed or represents a collateral branch. Eusebian lists generally show: Orthopolis (63 yrs) → Marathonius (30 yrs) → Marathus → Echyreus → Corax → Epopeus → Lamedon → etc., but the order varies by manuscript. The variant shows the fluidity of mythical king-lists before Castor standardized them.

Orthopolis was a god or hero with supernatural powers who protected cities. — Orthopolis is explicitly a mortal king in all ancient sources. His name means 'upholding the city' but he is not a deity, and no cult, sacrifice, or divine power is attributed to him. The confusion arises from the etymology (orthos + polis) resembling epithets of Athena Polias or Zeus Polieus.

Orthopolis founded a city called Orthopolis. — No ancient source says Orthopolis founded a city. He ruled Aegialea/Sicyon, which was founded by Apis/Aegialeus long before him. There is a separate toponym Orthopolis/Orthos in other regions (e.g., Thessaly), unrelated to this king.

Orthopolis's story is fully preserved in Hesiod or Homer. — Orthopolis does not appear in Hesiod, Homer, or any Archaic poetry. He is known only from Pausanias (2nd c. CE) and the Hellenistic chronographic tradition (Castor via Eusebius). His absence from early sources is itself evidence of his late, local, systematized nature.

Demeter was Orthopolis's mother. — Demeter is his nurse/foster-mother in disguise, not his biological mother. Pausanias is explicit: she 'reared for him his son Orthopolis' (etrephen) for Plemnaeus; the biological mother is Plemnaeus's unnamed wife.

Orthopolis appears as a major character in modern games, films, or Percy Jackson-style fiction. — No verified modern fictional adaptation features Orthopolis as a named character. Searches of major game databases (e.g., Hades, Assassin's Creed Odyssey, Age of Mythology), film/TV databases, and Rick Riordan's works show no appearance. His reception is confined to scholarly handbooks and online myth encyclopedias that summarize Pausanias.
