Who It Is

You find a journal in your grandmother's attic, written in a language you don't recognize. You translate one line: 'She was made of the same dust as Adam, and she refused to lie beneath him.' The next page is a drawing — a woman with owl's wings and hair of serpents, her eyes black as void, her mouth open in a scream or a laugh. In the margin, in your grandmother's handwriting: 'She visits me in my dreams. She tells me I am not property. She tells me I am not less. She is terrifying. I love her.'

Lilith is one of the most complex, contested, and powerful female figures in mythology — a woman who appears in Mesopotamian demon texts, Jewish folklore, Christian mysticism, and modern feminist theology. In her most famous incarnation — the medieval Jewish text 'The Alphabet of Ben Sira' (c. 800 CE) — Lilith is not a demon born in hell but Adam's first wife, created from the same earth at the same time, his absolute equal. When Adam demanded she lie beneath him during sex, she refused. 'Why must I lie beneath you?' she asked. 'We are equal, for we were both created from dust.' When Adam tried to force her, she spoke the ineffable name of God, grew wings, and flew away from Eden — abandoning paradise rather than accept subordination.

God sent three angels — Senoy, Sansenoy, and Semangelof — to bring her back. They found her at the Red Sea, coupling with demons and birthing a hundred demon children a day. She refused to return and was cursed: a hundred of her children would die each day. In revenge, Lilith became the killer of infants and the seducer of sleeping men, a night-demon who causes wet dreams and steals the life from newborns. But even this curse is ambiguous — she will spare any child protected by an amulet bearing the names of the three angels. Lilith is not pure evil; she negotiates. She has agency. She made a choice, paid the price, and found a way to survive. In Mesopotamian texts, she predates Judaism entirely — the 'lilitu' were wind-demons, female spirits of the night associated with owls, desolation, and the death of children. The Burney Relief (c. 1800 BCE) depicts a winged, talon-footed goddess who may be Lilith or Ishtar or Ereshkigal — her image is ancient beyond any single tradition.

Name & Tongue

Pronunciation

Akkadian lilītu — /ˈlɪliːtuː/ (li-LEE-too). Feminine member of the Mesopotamian lilû/lilītu class; built on the Sumerian logogram LÍL, glossed “air, wind, spirit,” extended to restless wandering spirits.

Hebrew לִילִית (lîlît) — /liˈliθ/ in scholarly vocalization; Modern Hebrew לילית /liˈlit/. The single biblical occurrence is Isaiah 34:14.

English “Lilith” — /ˈlɪlɪθ/ (LIL-ith). Canonical archive display form.

Aramaic (incantation bowls) — לילית /lilithā'/, often plural, individually named (e.g., “Lilith Hablas, granddaughter of Lilith Zarnai”).

Arabic — ليليث (Līlīth), a later folk adaptation; distinct from Jewish liturgical usage.

Transliteration variants — Lilit, Lilīt, Lilit(h), Lillith, Lilitu, Lillu, Lilu — search aliases, not separate entities.

One homophone trap: the flower-name “lilac” shares a root field with nothing here; “Lilith” is not etymologically “lily” (that folk etymology is modern and unsupported).

The Medieval Rupture · Alphabet of Ben Sira, laid bare5 rows

The first surviving explicit story in which a woman named Lilith is created from earth alongside Adam, demands equality, pronounces the divine name, leaves him, and bargains with three angels is the medieval Alphabet of Ben Sira (commonly dated c. 9th–10th c. CE). It is not Genesis, not the Talmud, and is widely treated as satirical or parodic literature. Its enormous later influence does not change its genre or date.

Protection Technology · bowls, amulets, and lived fear

Liliths were not merely literary abstractions. Late antique bowls could address them in the plural, draw them bound, name particular liliths, and expel them through legalistic divorce formulas. One published bowl issues a “writ of divorce” to Lilith Hablas, granddaughter of Lilith Zarnai, described as a smiter and burner of children and fetuses — evidence for individually named lilith-demons in ritual corpora, not guaranteed aliases of the later singular first wife.

  • Penn Museum B9013 (Nippur): protects a man and wife; uses a demon image in its design.
  • British Museum Mandaic bowl (W 1980-0415-28): names several liliths; binding and expulsion formulas.
  • Childbirth amulets: the names Senoy, Sansenoy, and Semangelof ward infants; documented from Kochi and 19th-century Europe into the 20th century.
  • Book of Raziel tradition: protective amulets for pregnant people and newborns (Metropolitan Museum Bulletin documents the tradition).
  • “Adam and Eve, out Lilith” formulas: phrases and angel-name seals belong to material religion — the people who used them were historical actors, not props in a demon story.

The Archive presents this material with reverence for the families who used it: this is the history of real fear around real cribs, not a horror garnish.

Accounts I

Ancient 24

I · Mesopotamian Strata · 8 accounts

Mesopotamian · 1The Huluppu Tree and the Phantom Maid. Inana plants a halub tree in Uruk, but a serpent settles at its roots, the Anzud bird in its branches, and a female figure in its trunk. Gilgamesh kills the serpent; the bird and the female figure flee. The current ETCSL translation calls her “the phantom maid” — no Adam, no Eden, no infant pact. Kramer's influential 1938 reconstruction popularized the proper name “Lilith”; modern philology keeps the question open.

Mesopotamian · 2The Class of the lilû. Akkadian texts know male members of a supernatural class, associated in scholarship with ghosts whose human life cycle was never completed. They are categories, not a biography with a proper name.

Mesopotamian · 3lilītu, the Female Wind-Spirit. The feminine counterpart of lilû, built on the Sumerian logogram LÍL (“air, wind, spirit”). The morphological link to the later Hebrew Lilith is real; the claim that every later Jewish detail already existed here is not.

Mesopotamian · 4ardat-lilî, the Maiden Who Turns to Young Men. A young-woman ghost denied ordinary sexual and familial fulfillment, threatening the young men she cannot have. This resemblance is the engine of later comparisons — and a class term, not a proper-name story.

Mesopotamian · 5etel lilî, the Princely Spirit. A rarer member of the same restless-ghost taxonomy, preserved in lexical and magical corpora.

Mesopotamian · 6Lamashtu's Amulets (comparative account). Lamashtu, daughter of Anu, with a stable hybrid iconography, threatens pregnancy and infants; protective plaques invoke Pazuzu against her. The functional overlap with later Lilith traditions supports comparison — Lamashtu is not Lilith, and the Archive keeps them separate.

Mesopotamian · 7The Queen of the Night Plaque (contested account). A nude, winged, taloned figure stands on lions between owls, holding rod-and-ring emblems. The British Museum calls the title modern and the identity uncertain; its own materials say the figure is widely considered Ishtar. “Lilith” is one historical proposal, not an object label.

Mesopotamian · 8The Arslan Tash Inscription. A protective text addressing a night-flying female — “Flyer in a dark chamber” — within an incantation context. Scholarship disputes the authenticity and date of the Arslan Tash plaques; treat as contested.

II · Biblical & Second Temple · 6 accounts

Biblical · 9Isaiah 34:14, the Single Verse. In the oracle against Edom, Hebrew lîlît appears once, among desert creatures in a ruined landscape. The verse supplies place, atmosphere, and dangerous liminality — it does not narrate Adam's first wife.

Biblical · 10Rashi's Gloss. The medieval commentator reads lîlît as the name of a female demon. A commentary layer, not a biblical statement.

Biblical · 114Q510, Songs of the Sage. Qumran apotropaic material lists Lilith with destroying angels, bastard spirits, and demons — Jewish knowledge of a dangerous being well before the medieval narrative.

Biblical · 124Q511, the Fragment. One occurrence preserves little more than the name in a damaged context. Responsible reconstruction stays modest.

Biblical · 13The Translation Wars. English versions render lîlît as “screech owl,” “night creature,” “night monster,” or simply transliterate. Rendering it as an owl is a translation choice, not proof of an ancient owl emblem.

Biblical · 14The Absence in Genesis. No named Lilith appears in Genesis. Later readers connect the two creation accounts and Adam's separation traditions to the medieval first-wife story — that synthesis is later, and the Archive labels it as such.

III · Rabbinic Fragments · 10 accounts

Rabbinic · 15The Hair of Lilith (Eruvin 100b). The Talmud compares long female hair to Lilith. It specifies no modern color, texture, or style — a legal comparison, not a portrait commission.

Rabbinic · 16The Solitary Sleeper (Shabbat 151b). A warning: a person sleeping alone may be seized by Lilith. The terse warning later grows into the full succubus apparatus.

Rabbinic · 17The Winged Likeness (Niddah 24b). A fetal form resembling Lilith; Rashi explains a demoness with a human face and wings.

Rabbinic · 18Hormin, Son of Lilith (Bava Batra 73a–b). A tall-tale narrative names Lilith's son Hormin/Hurmin. Do not merge him automatically with later demon genealogies.

Rabbinic · 19Adam's 130 Years (Eruvin 18). Separated from Eve, Adam generates spirits, demons, or female demons. The passage does not say the singular Lilith was his first wife.

Rabbinic · 20Genesis Rabbah 23:6. Another separation tradition — demons from Adam's seed — that later synthesis would borrow. Inserting “Lilith” here is interpretation, not quotation.

Rabbinic · 21The lilin, a Plural Class. Rabbinic texts know lilins as a class of demonic beings; plurality is the rule, singularity the later story.

Rabbinic · 22The Hair Weapon. From the long-hair comparison, folklore develops hair that binds, strangles, and entangles victims — a motif Goethe and Rossetti would later eroticize.

Rabbinic · 23The Succubus Frame. Nocturnal-emission traditions attach to Lilith: she steals seed to spawn demons. Systematized later; the rabbinic texts are terse.

Rabbinic · 24Fragments, Not One Biography. The rabbinic layer establishes a recognizable supernatural type — hair, wings, night danger, demon offspring — but never tells Ben Sira's single plot. The Archive refuses to smooth the gaps.

IV · Bowls & Amulets · 8 accounts

Bowls · 25Penn Museum B9013, Nippur. An incantation bowl protecting a man and wife, deploying a demon image in its own design — ritual technology, not literature.

Bowls · 26The Mandaic Bowl (British Museum). Names several liliths and expels them through binding formulas — plurality and particularity in the ritual record.

Bowls · 27The Divorce Writ. One published bowl issues a “writ of divorce” to Lilith Hablas, granddaughter of Lilith Zarnai, “smiter and burner of children and fetuses.” Named lilith-demons with a genealogy of their own.

Bowls · 28Bound and Chained. Bowls draw liliths chained or bound — the image of restraint is itself part of the technology.

Amulets · 29The Kochi Amulet (Magnes Collection). A childbirth amulet from Jewish Kerala — protection practice far from the Babylonian heartland.

Amulets · 30Senoy, Sansenoy, Semangelof. The three angel names, written on cribs and walls, become the standard ward against Lilith — the covenant materialized.

Amulets · 31Nineteenth-Century Europe. Museum collections document amulets for pregnant people and newborns into the 20th century: Lilith was a real, present fear for new parents.

Amulets · 32The Book of Raziel Tradition. Protective texts for childbirth carry the tradition forward; the Metropolitan Museum's Bulletin documents the practice with care.

V · Ben Sira & the Medieval Rupture · 10 accounts

Ben Sira · 33The Creation of the First Wife. Adam complains of being alone; God forms a woman from the same earth as Adam and calls her Lilith. The story is medieval, anonymous, and commonly dated to the ninth–tenth centuries.

Ben Sira · 34The Quarrel over Position. Adam demands to lie above; Lilith refuses: “Why must I lie beneath you? We are equal, for we were both created from dust.”

Ben Sira · 35The Divine Name. Lilith pronounces the ineffable name of God — the story's hinge — and grows wings. The power to speak the name is the power to leave.

Ben Sira · 36Flight from Eden. She abandons paradise rather than accept subordination. In the satire's frame, Eden becomes the thing refused.

Ben Sira · 37At the Red Sea. The angels find her coupling with demons, birthing a hundred demon children a day. The wilderness is already populated with her line.

Ben Sira · 38The Three Angels. Senoy, Sansenoy, and Semangelof pursue her on God's errand and demand her return.

Ben Sira · 39The Curse of the Hundred. Refusing to return, she is cursed: a hundred of her children will die each day. The price of her freedom is her motherhood.

Ben Sira · 40The Covenant over Infants. She will spare any child protected by an amulet bearing the three angels' names. She negotiates — agency, contract, and survival in one clause.

Ben Sira · 41The Satirical Frame. The Alphabet of Ben Sira is widely treated as satire or parody. Its genre matters: the story is transgressive literature, not scripture, not Talmud, not a lost chapter of Genesis.

Ben Sira · 42The Amulet Practice That Followed. Whatever its genre, the story's covenant powered centuries of real protective practice — the literary rupture became lived religion.

VI · Kabbalah & Mysticism · 8 accounts

Kabbalah · 43The Left Emanation. Isaac ha-Kohen's thirteenth-century Treatise on the Left Emanation pairs Samael and Lilith as central powers of the evil side — a decisive systematization, not a timeless doctrine.

Kabbalah · 44Zohar, Bereshit. A creeping creature is identified as Lilith — one passage among the Zohar's dense symbolic fields.

Kabbalah · 45Zohar, Mishpatim. Sexual and nocturnal danger attach to Lilith in passages on the “other side.”

Kabbalah · 46The Maidservant (Zohar, Introduction). A “maidservant” figure who can carry away children under her power — one of the Zohar's most cited Lilith roles.

Kabbalah · 47Greater and Lesser Lilith. Later Kabbalistic taxonomy splits Lilith into aspects — a developed hierarchy with its own literature; passage-level citations pending in this Archive.

Kabbalah · 48Naamah and the Four Queens. Later texts place Lilith among the queens of demons — with Naamah, Agrat bat Mahlat, Eisheth Zenunim, and Machlath — a demonic court assembled in the mystical literature.

Kabbalah · 49The Qlippotic Mapping. In later Kabbalah, Lilith is mapped onto the “shells” (qlippot) of the sefirotic tree — evil as husk and residue. Specialist citation pending.

Kabbalah · 50Emek ha-Melekh and Later Works. Seventeenth-century and later compilations multiply Lilith lore; each claim needs passage-level citation before inclusion, and the Archive says so out loud.

VII · Literary Afterlives · 7 accounts

Literature · 51Goethe, Walpurgis Night (1808). Mephistopheles introduces Lilith as Adam's first wife and warns Faust about her ensnaring hair. The medieval story enters European high literature.

Literature · 52Browning, “Adam, Lilith, and Eve” (1883). The Victorian poem reframes the trio as a parable of a wife's riddle — Lilith the first, Eve the second.

Literature · 53Rossetti, “Body's Beauty” (1881). The sonnet accompanying Lady Lilith: “the rose and poppy are her flowers,” the hair a net. Pre-Raphaelite reception made flesh.

Literature · 54MacDonald, Lilith: A Romance (1895). A theological fantasy casts Lilith as a dark princess in an allegorical cosmology — public domain, endlessly reprinted.

Literature · 55Sterling, “The Lilith” (1919). The American poet makes Lilith the mother of all — a “Lilith” as universal ancestress, late-romantic and dark.

Literature · 56Plaskow, “The Coming of Lilith” (1972). The Jewish feminist counter-midrash: Lilith returns to Eden, she and Eve trade experiences, and their friendship challenges Adam and the divine order. Explicitly modern — that is its power.

Literature · 57Ramlochan, Everyone Knows I Am a Haunting (2017). Postcolonial poetry uses Lilith's haunting as a witness-figure — scholarship reads her as a voice for the erased.

VIII · Modern Screens & Stages · 5 accounts

Modern · 58Lilith Fair (1997–1999; 2010). An all-women-led touring festival challenged industry assumptions about scheduling women performers together — the name as reclamation.

Modern · 59Diablo IV's Daughter of Hatred (2023). Lilith is Mephisto's daughter and Inarius's partner in creating Sanctuary — a radical franchise cosmology built on the refusal story's skeleton.

Modern · 60Supernatural's Lilith (2008–2009). The demon who breaks the first seal — a white-eyed antagonist whose mythology is invented for the show.

Modern · 61True Blood's Vampire Progenitor. The series builds a vampire religion around Lilith as its foundational figure — transformative reuse.

Modern · 62Sabrina's Madam Satan. In Chilling Adventures of Sabrina, Lilith is revealed as the true power behind the infernal throne — feminist-tinged, scorned, scheming, and finally sovereign.

Witness Quotations · short, language-paired, cited

Isaiah 34:14 (Hebrew Bible; public-domain JPS 1917 rendering). The sole biblical occurrence: “…the wild-cats shall meet with the jackals, and the satyr shall cry to his fellow; yea, the night-monster shall repose there, and shall find her a place of rest.” Hebrew לִילִית is transliterated in other versions; the “owl” and “night-monster” readings are translation history, not ancient iconography. SRC-011 · SRC-012

Alphabet of Ben Sira (medieval narrative, c. 9th–10th c.). The hinge of the first-wife story, quoted in translation: “Why must I lie beneath you? We are equal, for we were both created from dust.” The work is medieval satire — not Genesis, not Talmud. SRC-026 · SRC-027

Goethe, Faust I, Walpurgis Night (1808; public-domain English translation). Mephistopheles introduces her: “…Lilith, Adam's first wife… beware her fair hair: she excels in magic wiles.” The hair-snare enters European high literature here. SRC-037

Incantation-bowl divorce writ (Aramaic corpus, paraphrased). A bowl issues a writ of divorce to “Lilith Hablas, granddaughter of Lilith Zarnai,” smiter and burner of children — named, plural, and legally expelled; not the singular first wife. SRC-024 · SRC-025

Zohar, Mishpatim (paraphrase with passage citation). Lilith appears in the “other side” symbolism with sexual and nocturnal danger; each Zoharic claim is cited passage by passage rather than summarized as one doctrine. SRC-031

Plaskow, “The Coming of Lilith” (1972; summarized, not quoted — copyrighted). Lilith returns to Eden, she and Eve exchange experiences, and their friendship challenges Adam and the divine order. An explicit modern counter-midrash — that is its power, and its date. SRC-033

Misidentification Chamber · the four classic conflations4 rows

The Archive's core information architecture: four images circulate as “Lilith” that are not her — or not securely her. Each is kept in its own labeled cell.

What circulatesWhat the evidence says
The Queen of the Night relief as “the portrait of Lilith.”The British Museum calls the identity uncertain and widely considers the figure Ishtar; the object title is modern. It is reproduced everywhere as Lilith — that caption is the problem, not the plaque.
Lamashtu as “the earlier Lilith.”A distinct Mesopotamian demoness, daughter of Anu, with her own iconography and her own counter-demon (Pazuzu). Functional overlap with infant danger supports comparison — not identity.
The ⚸ glyph as “the ancient Lilith sigil.”The glyph belongs to modern astrological Black Moon Lilith — a calculated lunar-apogee point with mean, true, and interpolated variants. No ancient ritual corpus uses it as a Lilith seal.
Franchise Liliths (Diablo, Marvel, DC, Borderlands) as “the mythological Lilith.”Each is a distinct character with its own continuity — Mephisto's daughter, a Dracula child, a Titan superhero, a Siren. Diablo art captions on Jewish-text posts are cross-continuity bleed, not scholarship.
