Who It Is

Every empire, lottery, stock-market crash, and surprise meeting that ever upended a life has had a name: Fortuna. There is no more democratic, and no more terrifying, idea in all of religion — a goddess who hands a throne to a slave one morning and tumbles a prince to the gutter by nightfall, blind to merit, deaf to prayer, spinning a wheel that obeys nothing but its own momentum. The Romans did not love her so much as they could not stop building for her; they raised her the greatest sanctuary in Italy to bribe the universe into mercy, and when it still turned against them they called her insane, blind, and stupid. She is the slave who became a king, the general who called his mass-murder 'good luck,' and the philosopher in a death cell who learned to call her God's servant. You are on her wheel right now — you always have been.

Capricious, fickle, blind to merit, and morally indifferent. She bestows and revokes gifts without warning and cannot be reliably propitiated, though prayer, sacrifice, and votive vows were nonetheless offered. In the Latin tragic and satiric tradition she is 'insane, blind and stupid' (Pacuvius), 'cruel and fickle' (Juvenal), and equally capable of the devastating 'cruel Fortune' face (Atrox Fortuna) that claimed lives. In the medieval Boethian frame she is an instrument of God's hidden will rather than an independent power.

Pronunciation — for-TOO-nah (Classical Latin /forˈtuː.na/; voiced 'foor-too-nah' in Ecclesiastical use)

Aliases — Fortūna (Latin), Fortune (anglicized), Fors Fortuna, Lady Fortune, Lady Luck, Automatia (epithet, 'she who does what she will')

Powers
Granting and withholding of luck

The core power: Fortuna bestows good or bad fortune in any endeavor — agriculture, commerce, marriage, war, health, politics — without regard for merit. Whether a crop fails, a venture thrives, or a gambler wins is her domain. Romans sought her favor through prayer, vow, and votive, but her gifts were never guaranteed and could be revoked at any moment.

Limit: Her gifts are fickle and impermanent; she can empty the cornucopia as easily as fill it. She rewards neither virtue nor piety reliably.

Steering the course of fate (gubernaculum)

Via her ship's rudder, Fortuna steers the direction of individual lives and the destiny of cities and nations. The Greek Tyche, her counterpart, is likewise described as ruling over ships: 'At thy bidding, swift ships are steered upon the sea' (Pindar).

Limit: The steering is chaotic; a rudder implies direction but the destination is often catastrophic or unknown.

Bestowal of abundance (cornucopia)

Through the horn of plenty she grants material prosperity, agricultural plenty, wealth, and the grain supply (Fortuna Annonaria). The cornucopia in myth was torn from the river-god Acheloüs by Hercules and in at least one version granted to the abundance-goddess Abundantia/Copia, whom Romans closely associated with Fortuna.

Limit: The abundance is not permanent; the same horn can be emptied, and her plenty can be hoarded only with virtue (virtus).

Civic, military, and imperial fortune

As Fortuna Populi Romani, Publica, Romana, Equestris, and Augusta, she was the guardian of the Roman people's destiny, granting victory in war, stability to the state, safe return of emperors from campaign (Fortuna Redux), and prosperity of the empire. Generals and emperors from Sulla (Felix) to Augustus built their public claims on her special favor.

Limit: Her favor could be lost through hubris (superbia) or neglect of ritual; national 'luck' was a contested political claim.

Turning the Wheel of Fortune

Her defining late-ancient and medieval power: spinning the rota fortunae to raise and dash down individuals and kingdoms. First named by Cicero (In Pisonem, rota fortunae), made a topos by Boethius, and elaborated into the four-stage medieval Wheel. The wheel can elevate a slave to a throne (Servius Tullius) and cast an emperor to ruin.

Limit: In the Boethian frame her wheel is ultimately subordinate to God's providence; it 'ceases to be the course of chance' if it stops.

The sortes oracle at Praeneste

At the sanctuary of Fortuna Primigenia, Fortuna could answer questions through lots (sortes): a small boy drew one of many inscribed oak rods to foretell the future. The oracle was consulted by Romans down to the Christian era, when Constantine and Theodosius I suppressed it.

Limit: Skeptics (Cicero) ridiculed the lot-drawing; the oracle was closed by the Christian emperors.

Accounts I
Servius Tullius, the slave-king whom Fortuna raisedLivy, Ab Urbe Condita; Ovid Fasti; Dionysius of Halicarnassus; Plutarch

Legendary 6th c. BCE (reign c. 578-535 BCE)

Fortuna's great rags-to-throne story. Servius Tullius, sixth king of Rome, was born of Ocrisia, a captive Latin princess made a slave, and was said to be divinely fathered by a phallic Lar (household spirit) rising from the hearth. He rose to the kingship 'as if from nowhere' and is remembered as Rome's second founder, the popular reformer who brought the plebs into the franchise, instituted the census and the comitia centuriata, and built temples to Fortuna and Diana. In the tradition recorded by Plutarch, Fortuna herself was said to have passed through the palace 'window gate' (Porta Fenestella) to become Servius's consort — he was literally Fortuna's beloved. His eventual murder by his daughter Tullia and son-in-law Tarquinius Superbus, with Tullia driving her chariot over his body at the Vicus Sceleratus, marks the paradoxical 'tragic crime' that Fortuna could bless and then abandon. Fortuna's foundational association with Rome's most beloved 'self-made' king made her the goddess of the low-born and the socially mobile; his rise is the archetype of the Wheel's ascent.

The founding of the cult at Praeneste and the sortes oracleCicero, De Divinatione; Virgilian and local tradition; sanctuary archaeology

Legendary origin; sanctuary monumentalized late 2nd c. BCE

The great sanctuary of Fortuna Primigenia at Praeneste (Palestrina), 'first-born' Fortuna, was the most magnificent of all Fortuna's shrines — six vast terraces, porticoes, a theatre-cavea, and a circular temple with a gilded warrior-statue of the goddess, visible across Latium and from the sea. Its foundation myth centered on Caeculus, son of Vulcan and eponymous founder of Praeneste. The oracle drew lots to answer pilgrims; Cicero tells how the Praenestine nobleman Numerius Suffucius, following the dream-given instruction to strike a rock, found the sortes carved on oak inside, establishing the lot-divination. Parents brought their firstborn children to Fortuna Primigenia to secure their survival. The oracle flourished until Constantine and Theodosius I forbade the practice and closed the temple. Fortuna Primigenia's sanctuary became a major Italic and Roman pilgrimage site; the sortes model of divination spread and persisted into Christian times.

Accounts II
Sulla the Fortunate, or how a dictator married his own luckPlutarch, Life of Sulla; Appian; the epigraphic record

1st c. BCE

The dictator Lucius Cornelius Sulla, after a career of startling reversals and victories in the civil wars, adopted the title Felix ('the fortunate'), claiming his success as the result of a special, personal alliance with Fortuna. He named his twins Faustus and Fausta ('lucky'), and wielded his divine good luck as political legitimation — a precedent every later strongman and emperor would follow. His enemies saw the same 'luck' as pure brutality, tyranny, and murder. His fellow-commander Lutatius Catulus built the temple of Fortuna Huiusce Diei ('Today's Fortune') at the Battle of Vercellae in 101 BCE and linked his own self-presentation to the 'lucky' dictator. Fortuna became a weapon of autocratic legitimation; the 'luck of the general' shaped the late Republic and empire.

Boethius and Lady Fortune's wheelBoethius, Consolation of Philosophy (Bk II)

c. 524 CE

The statesman-philosopher Boethius, imprisoned and awaiting execution, imagined a dialogue with Lady Fortune. She defends her turning wheel as her very essence: 'I know the manifold deceits of that monstrous lady... this is my nature, this is my constant game. I turn my wheel that spins.' Boethius reframed her as a servant of God's providence — even the apparently ruinous turns of fortune are part of a hidden, just divine plan. This text, 'required reading' for medieval scholars, became the primary conduit through which the pagan goddess survived into the Christian Middle Ages, and the source of the ubiquitous medieval Wheel of Fortune image (regnabo-regno-regnavi-sum sine regno) in manuscripts, cathedral windows (Amiens), and morality plays (Everyman). Fortuna was Christianized and made the emblem of worldly mutability, humility, and the fall of the proud; her Wheel dominated medieval and Renaissance art and literature.

The mad, blind, stupid goddess of the tragediansPacuvius (fragment); Seneca, Agamemnon; Juvenal, Satires

2nd c. BCE - 2nd c. CE

A counter-tradition portrays Fortuna not as a helpful goddess but as a cruel, insane, blind power. The tragedian Pacuvius has 'Philosophers say that Fortune is insane and blind and stupid... she stands on a rolling, spherical rock.' Seneca's Agamemnon chorus thunders, 'Whatever Fortune has raised on high, she lifts but to bring low.' Juvenal laments that she is 'cross to all'; Ovid, in exile, ruefully writes of 'the goddess who admits by her unsteady wheel her own fickleness.' This darker view — Fortuna as the cause of sudden catastrophe, the breaker of kings, the enemy of the lowly — coexisted with her votive cult and fed every tragic use of fortune in later literature. The tragic/cruel face of Fortuna (Atrox Fortuna, credited with taking Augustus's heirs Gaius and Lucius) shaped Western tragedy's obsession with sudden falls.

Fortuna is the same as the Fates (Parcae/Moirai). — The Fates spin, measure, and cut the thread of each being's inescapable lifespan. Fortuna governs chance and luck, which alter circumstances but not the fundamental course of living. Romans believed Fortuna could be influenced (via prayer and rite) and might reward virtue, while the Fates could not be changed.

The Wheel of Fortune is an unbroken pagan Roman symbol. — The wheel image is ancient — Cicero first names the 'rota fortunae' in 55 BCE — but the four-stage medieval Wheel (regnabo-regno-regnavi-sum sine regno) was codified in the Middle Ages chiefly through Boethius's Christianized Consolation. It became a moral allegory about the fall of the proud, not a direct, continuous pagan artifact.

Fortuna was a major goddess with a rich mythology of adventures and legends. — Fortuna had a vast cult and many temples but almost no independent mythology. She was a force to be worshipped and feared, not a character in adventure stories. Her 'stories' are about the founding of her cults and her effect on historical figures (Servius Tullius, Sulla, Boethius), not her own exploits.

Fortuna was a goddess of the rich and powerful only. — Fortuna was especially popular among slaves, freedmen, the plebs, and the lower classes, for whom 'luck' offered the only hope of advancement in a rigid society. The epigraphic record is thick with dedications by the humble, and the Servius Tullius story made her the patron of the socially mobile and low-born.

The blindfolded Fortuna is the same figure as blindfolded Lady Justice. — Both are blindfolded women, but their attributes differ decisively: Fortuna holds a wheel/rudder/horn and carries no scales; Justice holds scales (and adds the sword). Fortuna's blindness signals the arbitrariness of luck; Justice's signals impartiality before the law. The popular confusion is understandable but wrong.
