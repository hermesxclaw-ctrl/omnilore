Who It Is

Stand on the north bank of the Luo where the sun never leaves the water, and you stand in Rakuyo — Luoyang — the city that has died and been rebuilt more times than any other capital in China. For three thousand years emperors moved heaven itself to live here, monks carved 100,000 Buddhas into its cliffs, and armies burned it to ash, only for the next dynasty to lay its streets again over the bones.

In transmitted literature Luoyang behaves as the ritual barometer of the realm: when the capital is ordered and populated, the dynasty is legitimate; when it burns, the Mandate is lost. Histories describe it as cosmopolitan, scholarly and devout in prosperity — site of the Imperial University, translation of Buddhist sutras, and Sogdian merchant quarters — and as catastrophic in collapse, with repeated depopulation and forced migration. Yang Xuanzhi's elegiac tone portrays Northern Wei Luoyang as magnificent but impermanent. Tang poets (Bai Juyi, who retired and was buried at Longmen)

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Chinese Luò (洛, the Luo River 洛河) + yáng (陽, 'sunny side' / yang principle). In traditional Chinese toponymy, yang denotes the north bank of a river (or south slope of a mountain) because the river flows west-east and the sun to the south illuminates the north bank. Attested as Luoyi in Western Zhou

Pronunciation — Mandarin Luòyáng /lwɔ̂.jǎŋ/; Middle Chinese *lak-yang; Japanese on-reading Rakuyō [ɾakɯꜜjoː]; Korean Nagyang; Vietnamese Lạc Dương

Aliases — Luoyang, Luòyáng, Rakuyō, Chengzhou (成周), Wangcheng (王城), Luoyi (洛邑)

Ownership — Han Chinese and broader Chinese civilization (Huaxia). Specifically associated with Zhou, Han, Wei-Jin, Northern Wei (Xianbei-led), Sui, Tang and subsequent central-plain dynasties

Ritual-Cosmic Legitimation

As Chengzhou/Luoyi, possession of the Nine Tripod Cauldrons and performance of suburban sacrifices (jiao) at the Altar of Heaven and Altar of Soils and Grains conferred Mandate of Heaven. Control of Luoyang allowed a ruler to claim centrality (zhongguo) even when military power lay elsewhere.

Limit: Symbolic only; did not prevent sacks in 189-311 AD. Cauldrons' historicity and location are unverified archaeologically — possibly mythic. Requires scholarly interpretation, not literal supernatural power.

Strategic Confluence Control

Command of the Luo-Yellow River confluence, the Yang Canal (linking Luo to Yellow in 48 AD), and the eastern terminus of the Tong Pass corridor. Who held Luoyang controlled grain transport from the east and access to both Guanzhong (Chang'an) and the Central Plain. Population capacity 600,000-1,000,000 at peaks indicates logistical centrality.

Limit: Flood-prone; Luo and Yellow River shifts required constant hydraulic work. Vulnerable to northern steppe incursions via Mangshan gap — repeatedly captured (311, 328, 422, 534).

Buddhist Patronage and Cave-Temple Creation

Under Emperor Xiaowen, Luoyang became the engine of Chinese Buddhism: imperial sponsorship of Longmen Grottoes (30,000+ Northern Wei statues, 100,000+ total), translation activity, and construction of Yongning Temple pagoda — claimed tallest in China (c.137m with mast, per Luoyang Qielan Ji). Shaolin Temple foundation (495) under Luoyang's orbit created Chan (Zen) lineage.

Limit: Yongning pagoda burned in 534, never rebuilt to same height; height figures may be exaggerated. Longmen carving continued for 400 years, not a single imperial act.

Scholarly and Literary Amplification

Seat of the Imperial University (Taixue, restored 29 AD), Imperial Observatory (Spiritual Terrace, 56 AD) with armillary sphere, and later examination culture. Tang Luoyang produced and attracted poets (Bai Juyi, Liu Yuxi) and became the archetype of the 'eastern capital' in poetry — its peonies, ruins and Longmen scenery amplify cultural memory far beyond physical remains.

Limit: Institutions repeatedly destroyed; continuity was ideological rather than physical. Observatory instruments lost.

Multiethnic Commercial and Religious Conduit

Tang Shendu hosted Sogdian (Hu) merchants, Nestorian Christian communities (evidenced by 815-827 epitaphs showing Han-Sogdian Christian intermarriage and mixed-race sons becoming civil/military officials), and Zoroastrian presence. The city's markets and monasteries functioned as translators between steppe, oasis and central plain cultures.

Limit: Communities were minorities; Nestorian presence declined after 845 Huichang persecution of Buddhism (which also affected Christians). Epigraphic sample is small (dozens of stones).

Accounts I
The Duke of Zhou Builds Chengzhou for the Shang RemnantsShiji (Shiji, Zhou Benji), Book of Documents (Shangshu - Luogao, Shaogao), He zun bronze inscription

c. 1036 BC (traditional); Western Zhou early

After King Wu conquered the Shang, his brother the Duke of Zhou, acting as regent for King Cheng, constructed a second capital at Chengzhou (later Luoyi) to house the potentially rebellious Shang nobility under close surveillance and to move the Nine Tripod Cauldrons — the bronze vessels symbolizing dominion over the Nine Provinces — from Haojing to the new eastern center. A second enclosure, Wangcheng, was built 15km west. The He zun vessel, cast by a Zhou officer, records King Cheng's command to build Chengzhou, corroborating the textual tradition. Established the 'two capitals' system and the ideological centrality of the Luo area. Created the ritual precedent that controlling Luoyang meant controlling legitimacy. Modern Luoyang claims this as its founding moment; Wangcheng Park preserves the rammed-earth platform identified as Wangcheng.

Luoyang Becomes Capital of Eastern Han — The Rectangular City of Palaces and AltarsHou Hanshu, Annals of Guangwu; Hanshu; archaeological plan of Han-Wei Luoyang

25-220 AD

On 27 November 25 AD, Emperor Guangwu (Liu Xiu) declared Luoyang capital of the restored Han, abandoning war-torn Chang'an. Over three decades the court built a rigorously rectangular walled city (4 x 2.5 km), the Southern and Northern Palaces linked by a Covered Way, the Altar of Soils and Grains and Altar of Heaven (26 AD), the restored Imperial University (29 AD), the Yang Canal linking the Luo to the capital (48 AD), and the Spiritual Terrace observatory (56 AD). In 166 AD a maritime mission claiming to represent Marcus Aurelius (Andun, king of Da Qin) arrived via Rinan (central Vietnam) and reached Luoyang, the first recorded Roman embassy. Luoyang became the model Confucian capital for four centuries; its grid, palace duality and ritual altars were copied by Wei, Jin and Northern Wei. The Roman mission, whether genuine or merchants masquerading, entered Chinese historiography as proof of Luoyang's world-city status.

Burning of Luoyang — Dong Zhuo's Sack and the Disaster of YongjiaHou Hanshu, Sanguozhi, Zizhi Tongjian juan 59-60; Jinshu

24 September 189 AD (burning after Yellow Turban wars); 4 April 190 AD (Dong Zhuo's systematic sack); 311 AD (Yongjia sack)

After the Yellow Turban Rebellion (184 AD) weakened the Han, the capital burned on 24 Sept 189 during eunuch-general factional slaughter. Warlord Dong Zhuo seized the child emperor, then, facing a coalition of regional lords, ordered Luoyang ransacked, pillaged and razed on 4 April 190 as he moved the court to Chang'an — palaces, temples and tombs were stripped. Cao Cao held the emperor at Xuchang (196-220) before his son Cao Pi restored Luoyang as Wei capital in 220. Recovery was shattered in 311 when Xiongnu-led Han-Zhao forces under Liu Cong sacked Luoyang, massacred the Jin court and burned the city in the Disaster of Yongjia (永嘉之亂), followed by a second sack in 328 during Han-Zhao vs Later Zhao war. Ended Luoyang's continuous Han-Wei centrality; population scattered, texts lost. The Yongjia sack inaugurated the Upheaval of the Five Barbarians and the north-south division. Luoyang remained contested for two centuries, changing hands between Liu Yu's Jin expedition (416), Northern Wei (422), Liu Song (430) and finally Wei (460s).

Accounts II
Emperor Xiaowen Moves the Northern Wei Capital and Carves LongmenWeishu, Luoyang Qielan Ji, Longmen epigraphy

493-534 AD

As part of a deliberate sinicization program, Emperor Xiaowen (元宏) moved the Northern Wei capital from Datong (Pingcheng) to Luoyang in 493, forcibly relocating over 150,000 Xianbei and Han households by 495, ordering adoption of Han surnames, language and dress. He commissioned the Longmen Grottoes, carving Buddhist chapels into limestone cliffs along the Yi River, and built the Shaolin Temple on Mount Song for the Indian monk Batuo (跋陀). The Yongning Temple's nine-storey wooden pagoda, described by Yang Xuanzhi as the tallest in China with a golden mast visible 50 li away, dominated the skyline. At its height the city again reached c.600,000. In 534 warlord Gao Huan sacked Luoyang and moved its population to Ye, ending its Northern Wei florescence. Created the Buddhist art of Longmen — 30,000 Northern Wei statues, later expanded to 100,000+ — now UNESCO World Heritage. Shaolin became the cradle of Chan Buddhism and martial traditions. The forced migration accelerated Xianbei-Han fusion but also provoked backlash leading to the Six Garrisons Revolt and Wei split.

Sui-Tang Dongdu and Wu Zetian's Divine Capital ShenduJiu Tangshu, Xin Tangshu, Zizhi Tongjian, Sui-Tang Luoyang archaeology reports

604-907 AD (Sui foundation 604; Tang Dongdu; Wu Zhou 690-705)

Emperor Yang of Sui rebuilt Luoyang in 604 on a new, larger grid inspired by his father Wen's Chang'an, making it the eastern capital. Under the Tang, Luoyang as Dongdu (東都) reached c.1 million inhabitants, second only to Chang'an. When Wu Zetian, China's only female emperor, founded her Zhou dynasty (690-705), she made Luoyang her primary capital and renamed it Shendu (神都, Divine Capital), constructing the Mingtang (Hall of Light) and Tiantang (Heavenly Hall) — colossal timber ritual palaces where she performed imperial rites. The An Lushan Rebellion (755-763) damaged the city. Tang epitaphs from Luoyang reveal a cosmopolitan Christian community: Lady An, a Sogdian Nestorian (d.821), and her Han Christian husband Hua Xian (d.827), and an earlier An-family epitaph (815) showing Han-Sogdian intermarriage and mixed-race sons who became Tang officials while openly supporting monasteries. Luoyang's Sui-Tang plan defined the modern city's underground footprint; Mingtang/Tiantang postholes excavated since 1986 anchor heritage parks. From Henanfu to Modern Luoyang — The Peony Capital RememberedSong Shi, Ming-Qing local gazetteers, modern census and UNESCO dossier

960 AD - present (renamed Henanfu under Song-Yuan-Ming-Qing; restored to Luoyang 1912)

After the Tang, Luoyang lost capital primacy: the Song called it Xijing, the Jurchen Jin and Yuan administered it as Henanfu, and Ming-Qing kept the name Henanfu. Its symbolic capital was preserved in poetry, painting and peony cultivation rather than politics. In 1912 the Republic restored the name Luoyang. Modern archaeology (1950s onward) revealed the layered cities beneath the modern one. In 2000 Longmen Grottoes were inscribed as UNESCO World Heritage (criteria i, ii, iii). Today Luoyang is a prefecture-level city (7 districts, 7 counties, 1 development zone, 7.079 million permanent residents in 2022; metro 2.75 million in 2018) that markets itself as cradle of Chinese civilization and peony culture, while heavy industry and tourism coexist over the buried palaces. The 'Four Great Ancient Capitals' narrative (with Beijing, Nanjing, Xi'an) cements Luoyang's heritage status. Wangcheng Park and Sui-Tang City Ruins Park make the buried cities visible. Luoyang has been capital for 13 dynasties continuously, making it the longest-serving capital. — The '13 dynasties' count depends on definition; it includes brief warlord regimes (e.g., Later Liang, Later Tang) and counts Eastern Zhou, Eastern Han, Wei, Western Jin, Northern Wei, Sui, Tang (as eastern capital) etc. Some lists count 9, others 13. Luoyang was often secondary capital (Dongdu/Xijing) or one of several, and was completely abandoned for decades after sacks.

Rakuyo is only a Bloodborne weapon and has no historical meaning. — Rakuyo is the Japanese on-reading of Luoyang (洛陽). The weapon in Bloodborne: The Old Hunters is named after the city, evoking classical elegance; the city itself is a 3,000-year-old historical capital with extensive textual and archaeological record.

The Longmen Grottoes were built all at once by one emperor. — Longmen was carved over ~400 years from 493 AD (Northern Wei) through Tang, with distinct phases: Guyang Cave (Northern Wei), Binyang Caves (Sui), Fengxian Vairocana (Tang, 672-675). Over 2,345 caves and 100,000 statues represent successive donor generations, not a single project.

Luoyang's peony fame is ancient and continuous since Zhou. — Peony cultivation as civic symbol is Tang-Song onward, celebrated by Tang poets like Bai Juyi and Liu Yuxi. Earlier periods prized other plants; the modern Peony Festival dates to 1983.

Sogdian 'Hu' women in Tang Luoyang were only entertainers and had no social mobility. — Epitaphs from Luoyang (815, 821/827) show Sogdian Christian women marrying Han Chinese Christian men, and their mixed-race sons becoming Tang civil and military officials who openly patronized Christian monasteries. 'Hu' stereotype as barmaids/dancers existed but was not the whole reality.
