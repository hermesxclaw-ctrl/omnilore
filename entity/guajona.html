Guajona

Known As

LumiaGuajaGuaxa

Etymology

Guajona is an augmentative of Guaja, also present in Asturian mythology as Guaxa. The origin may be classical Arabic وحش (wahsh), meaning 'beast'.

Cultural Ownership

Cantabrian and Asturian folklore (northern Spain)

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars associate the Guajona with the broader European witch or hag archetype, but her vampiric traits distinguish her from typical Spanish witches. The name 'Lumia' may be a regional variant or a separate but related entity.

Archive Dossier — Approach

In the dark of a Cantabrian night, a cloaked figure with a single razor-sharp tooth slips through the cracks of a sleeping home, drawn not by gold or revenge, but by the warm blood of children. This is the Guajona, a vampire-hag whose silent visits leave the young pale and exhausted, a terror whispered in the mountains of northern Spain.

In the dark of the moon, when the mist rolls down from the peaks, La Guajona emerges from her cave — lipless mouth grinning, iron teeth gleaming — and those who smell her foul breath pray they are not the one she hunts.

Who It Is

La Guajona is a terrifying witch-like creature from Cantabrian folklore in northern Spain. She is described as an old, haggard woman with long, tangled white hair, pale skin, and a third eye in the middle of her forehead — but her most distinctive and horrifying feature is her mouth: she has no lips, revealing a constant, ghastly grin of sharp, blackened or iron teeth. She also lacks a nose, replaced by two gaping holes. La Guajona lives in caves and emerges at night to wander remote mountain paths, hunting for lone travelers, especially children. According to legend, she has a particular obsession with blood — she attacks victims to drink their blood, and she is associated with the vampire-like 'guajona' tradition of northern Spain. Parents used the Guajona to frighten children into obedience: 'La Guajona will come for you if you wander into the mountains at night!' In some accounts, the Guajona can be repelled by iron, garlic, or a cross. She is also said to have a keen sense of smell, able to detect fear from a great distance. The creature shares features with the Greek Lamia, the Slavic Baba Yaga (in her child-eating aspect), and the Romanian Strigoi. Unlike many Cantabrian creatures (the benevolent Anjana, the helpful Trenti), the Guajona is purely malevolent, representing the dark, dangerous aspect of the Cantabrian mountains. The legend of La Guajona likely has roots in pre-Christian beliefs about mountain spirits and was reinforced by medieval fears of witches and vampires. Her name may derive from 'guaja' (a term for witch) or from 'ojáncana' (the female cyclops of Cantabrian myth), indicating a shared etymological root with the Ojanco/Ojáncanu family of Cantabrian monsters.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

The Guajona resembles a disfigured human female, covered from head to toe in an old, thin black cloak. Her hands and feet are gnarled bird legs. Her face is yellow and covered with rough, hairy warts. Her eyes are tiny and bright as stars. She has an aquiline nose and a mouth containing a single black, razor-sharp tooth that extends long enough to reach under her chin.

Symbols & Iconography

The dominant symbol is the single black tooth used for bloodsucking. The black cloak and bird-like extremities evoke a witch or hag archetype. No known iconographic representations exist in traditional art; modern depictions are rare.

Behaviour & Personality

The Guajona is nocturnal, appearing only at night. She invades homes silently and walks toward healthy young children to suck their blood while they sleep, using her tooth to pierce their veins. She does not kill, but leaves victims almost bloodless, causing them to wake tired, pale, and discolored. She also attacks adults. Her daytime whereabouts are unknown, but it is suspected she hides underground.

Domains & Habitats

The Guajona inhabits the folklore of Cantabria and Asturias, rural northern Spain. She is associated with the night and is believed to hide underground during the day.

Version Differences

The Asturian counterpart Guaxa may have similar traits, but details vary. The name 'Lumia' might be a regional variant with distinct characteristics. No major divergent versions are recorded.
