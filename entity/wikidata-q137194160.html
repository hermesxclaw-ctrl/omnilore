Who It Is

Before the ninja were a brand, they were a covenant — and the covenant had a god. On a wooded mountain in southern Shiga called Aburahi, the Kōka warrior clans who would become history's most famous spies swore themselves to a flame. A kami had descended there, they said, in a light like burning oil, and from that apparition the hill, the shrine, and the deity all took the same name. For three centuries the Kōka samurai-shū funded, guarded, and worshipped what they called the general shrine of their whole district, their 勝軍神 — their god who grants victory in war. Their 1475 donations were scratched into documents that survive to this day. The same deity that once blessed assassins and guerrillas now protects the nation's oil refineries. Few gods have lived so strange a double life: fire-bearer to a ninja army, then quiet patron of an industry.

As the Kōka confederacy's patron, Aburahi no Ōkami was understood as a martial, protective deity who granted victory (勝軍神) and shielded its warrior devotees. In its popular guise as a fire-oil-god, it is protective and beneficent to industry yet carries the latent danger of uncontrolled fire — a duality typical of kami whose element is flame. Sources do not attribute it a personal mythology of temper, romance, or retribution beyond its martial patronage.

Powers
Granting victory in war (勝軍神 / shōgun-gami, kachigami)

The deity's foremost medieval power: giving martial success, protection, and good fortune to warriors who invoked it. As the patron of the Kōka confederacy, it is the reason Kōka ninja and samurai clans treated the shrine as their 総社 (sōsha, general shrine of the district) and gathered in its precincts.

Limit: Victory-granting was conditional on the warriors' collective fidelity and offerings; the god was a communal patron rather than a personal tutelary, and its martial power operated through the confederacy's own skill and fortune rather than miraculous interventions in any recorded narrative.

Lordship over oil and flame (油の火の神)

By folk etymology from the shrine name (abura 'oil' + hi 'fire'), the deity became a god of oil and fire, invoked for protection, prosperity, and safe handling of oil and fire-related trades. This gave rise to lasting popular devotion from the oil industry nationwide, which continues to the present.

Limit: A devotional-economic association rather than a scriptural power; it reflects wordplay-based folk religion and has no mythological action attached to it.

Mountain-kami numen (sacred summit)

As a kami whose shintai is Mount Aburahi, the deity possesses the sacred mountain as its dwelling, with an inner-shrine summit (Gakujinja) paired with the lower shrine. The numinous force animates the whole mountain and blessed the region's ascetic and warrior culture.

Limit: A descriptive sacred site attribute, not a discrete power with named feats.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The deity has no fixed anthropomorphic visual form in surviving records; like most Shinto kami it is worshiped chiefly through the numinous site (Mount Aburahi as shintai), the shrine's architecture, and the kami-symbols (神紋) of a quince flower (木瓜) with twin horizontal bands (二ツ引). Its destructive-or-generative dual nature as a fire-oil power is expressed metaphorically — the 'oil flame' of its descent — rather than through iconographic likeness

Symbols — The shrine crest is a Quince (木瓜 mokko) with the 'two horizontal bars' device (二ツ引). Ritual arts include the nationally-designated Aburahi taiko dance (油日の太鼓踊) and the nuburi (奴振) procession dance. Accounts I
The Descent of the Oil-Flame KamiShrine tradition (油日神社 official account, per Japanese Wikipedia)

Uncertain; mythic-heroic founder age (traditionally Emperor Yōmei or Emperor Tenmu era)

High on Mount Aburahi, a kami descended to the earth expressed as a light like the flame of burning oil. Because of this flame-like descent, the place and the god took the name Aburahi ('oil-fire'). The legend grounds both the shintai mountain and the realm of flame in a single founding vision. The name, the mountain cult, and the fire/oil association are all explained by this founding event; it licenses the later industry-veneration.

Prince Shōtoku Builds the ShrineShrine tradition (per Japanese Wikipedia)

circa late 6th-early 7th century (traditional)

Prince Shōtoku (574-622), the great imperial patron of Buddhism, is traditionally said to have erected the shrine hall and enshrined Aburahi as 油日大明神 (Aburahi Daimyōjin). The claim links the shrine to Japan's most revered semi-legendary imperial founder of temples, anchoring the deity in national rather than merely local prestige. Prince Shōtoku figures prominently in the region's syncretic religion; the Kōka warriors themselves later venerated Shōtoku as a war-god. Establishes the shrine's early state and fuses the martial-warrior and Buddhist-royal strands of the cult; a silk painting of Saint Shōtoku among the shrine's cultural treasures sustains the link.

Accounts II
Patron of the Kōka Ninja ConfederacyKōka ikki historical record; 1475 donation document; shrine account of '甲賀の総社'

15th-16th century (Sengoku era)

The Kōka confederacy — the jizamurai and shinobi families famous as the warrior-ninja of Kōga-ryū — made Aburahi Daimyōjin their patron deity. A 1475 document records donations to the god by members of the samurai-shū, and the warriors collectively venerated the shrine as the general shrine (総社) of Kōka district. Successive gifts — a 1566 tower gate and cloisters, a 100-koku perpetual stipend donated by the Kōka Chūsō in 1586, a 1620 bell — show the confederacy's sustained martial piety across generations. The deity became the religious anchor of Kōka's famous ninja identity; the shrine's surviving architecture was built by these warriors, and the martial devotion is a core part of the region's cultural heritage. The association persisted into the Edo era when surviving Kōka ninja served the Tokugawa shogunate.

Ieyasu's Escape through AburahiTokugawa Ieyasu and Honnō-ji Incident articles

1582

After Oda Nobunaga was killed in the Honnō-ji Incident (1582), Tokugawa Ieyasu fled from Sakai across the mountains toward home. One reconstruction of his route has him cross Kōka — passing through Aburahi before entering Tsuge in Iga. The detail links the deity's home turf to one of the most famous escape episodes in samurai history, through the very terrorist-infested (and ninja-defended) corridor the Kōka warriors controlled. Signals the strategic and historical importance of the Aburahi crossroads in the Sengoku era; though the specific route is argued by historians, it keeps Aburahi in circulation in wider samurai history.
