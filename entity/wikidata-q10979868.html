Who It Is

She does not arrive with thunder like Zeus or beauty like Aphrodite. Poine comes afterward — when the blood has already been spilled and the debt is unpaid. In Greek thought she is the price itself made animate: the Recompense that snatches children in Argos, the shadow at the doorway of the Underworld, and the quiet attendant who stands behind Nemesis until punishment can no longer be deferred.

Not a personality with motives like Olympians, but a function: inexorable, unappeasable retribution until satisfaction is paid. In tragedy she is invoked as the inevitable 'requital' for blood guilt — silent, patient, collective. In Argive myth she acts as Apollo's instrument: punitive, indiscriminate (takes any Argive child for the king's crime), nocturnal, predatory. In Roman moralizing (Seneca, Cicero) Poena is internalized as conscience and fear of punishment. She does not speak in surviving texts; she acts or presides. Powers
Retributive Exaction (Poinē as Blood-Price)

Embodies and enforces the requirement that wrongdoing be paid for with suffering, death, or compensation. In Aeschylus, poinē is the 'price' exacted for murder (e.g., Clytemnestra's murder demands Orestes' poinē). Not magical but juridical-cosmic: she ensures no crime goes unpaid.

Limit: Cannot act until crime occurs; bound to proportionality in early law (wergild) but in myth acts excessively (collective punishment). Can be delayed but not denied.

Child-Snatching and Infanticide (Argive Poine)

Enters houses by night and seizes infants/children from mothers' breasts, killing or carrying them off. Causes terror and depopulation of Argos.

Limit: Corporeal and killable: Coroebus kills her with sword (Pausanias) or spear (Statius). Her power ends with her death, but Apollo then substitutes plague.

Plague-Bringing (Secondary Divine Punishment)

Her death does not end punishment; Apollo sends loimos (plague) on Argos until atonement. In some readings Poine herself spreads contagion.

Limit: Requires divine authorization (Apollo). Averted by tripod dedication at Delphi or heroic self-sacrifice (Coroebus offers himself).

Underworld Guardianship and Torment

As plural Poenae, they sit at the threshold of Orcus and assist Tisiphone and the Furies in punishing the damned (Tartarus). They embody eternal penalty.

Limit: Chthonic; cannot leave Underworld except when summoned by gods (Apollo, Juno). Subordinate to Dike, Nemesis, or Minos/Rhadamanthus.

Psychological Persecution (Conscience and Fear)

In Roman philosophy, Poena is internalized as metus poenae — fear of punishment that haunts the guilty, sleeplessness, madness.

Limit: Non-corporeal; affects only the guilty. Can be resisted by Stoic virtue in Seneca's account.

Accounts I
The Argive Poine: Apollo's Vengeance for Linus and PsamathePausanias 1.43.7-8; Conon Diegeseis 19 (via Photius); Statius Thebaid 1.557-672

Hellenistic legend recorded 1st c. BCE - 2nd c. CE; poetic peak c. 90 CE (Statius)

King Crotopus of Argos has daughter Psamathe who bears Apollo's son Linus in secret. She exposes the infant, who is raised by shepherds but torn apart by shepherd dogs. Apollo, enraged at his son's death and Psamathe's execution by her father, sends Poine — a child-snatching demon — to Argos. Poine enters homes at night and steals infants from their mothers, devouring or carrying them off, until the hero Coroebus (Korobos), a young Argive, confronts and kills her. Apollo, angered again at the killing of his agent, sends a plague (loimos) upon Argos that will not cease until Coroebus atones. Coroebus goes to Delphi, is instructed by the Pythia never to return home but to carry a tripod until it falls and found a city there (Tripodiskos on Mount Geraneia). Variant: Psamathe is killed by Crotopus before Poine arrives; Coroebus's self-exile ends the plague. Aetiology for Argive ritual lament for Linus (ailinon song), for the city Tripodiskos, and for apotropaic rites against child-stealing demons. Poine in the House of Atreus: The Price of BloodAeschylus Agamemnon 58-62, 146-155, 812; Choephori 382-383, 946-949; Eumenides 177, 232, 476

458 BCE

Aeschylus uses poinē as both noun and daimon throughout the Oresteia. Agamemnon's sacrifice of Iphigenia and Paris's violation of xenia require poinē; the Chorus warns that 'Poinē stays, not forgetting'. After Clytemnestra murders Agamemnon, Orestes is told he must exact poinē. In Choephori 382 the Chorus sings 'for blood, blood must be paid (poinē)'. At Choephori 946 Orestes declares that 'Poinē has come' with the Furies. The Erinyes themselves are called poinai in Eumenides, blurring the terms. Here Poine is not a separate character but the principle of requital that drives the trilogy toward the Areopagus trial where Dike replaces vendetta. Establishes poinē as central tragic concept: justice as payment. Influences later philosophical use (Plato) and Roman poena. Shows early personification without fixed myth.

The Poenae at the Vestibule of OrcusVirgil Aeneid 6.273-281, 570-572; Ovid Metamorphoses 4.453 (poenae); Seneca Hercules Furens 500-503

29-19 BCE (Virgil), 1st c. CE (Seneca)

When Aeneas descends to the Underworld with the Sibyl, at the very entrance (vestibulum Orci) he sees a crowd of personified horrors: Grief, Cares, Diseases, Old Age, Fear, Hunger, Death, and 'ultrices Poenae' — avenging Punishments — seated on the threshold, alongside the Furies and Discord. Deeper in Tartarus, Tisiphone guards the damned with her Poenae. The image systematizes Poenae as permanent chthonic officials who ensure eternal punishment for the judged. Seneca repeats them as jailers of Hades. Becomes the standard Roman iconography for Poenae; copied by Dante (Inferno 3), Spenser, Milton, and modern fantasy Underworlds. Fixes plural, infernal, non-narrative version.

Accounts II
Attendant of Nemesis at RhamnusPausanias 1.33.2-3; Ammianus Marcellinus 14.11.25; Mesomedes Hymn to Nemesis (2nd c. CE)

2nd c. CE (Pausanias), 4th c. CE (Ammianus)

Pausanias describes the great statue of Nemesis at Rhamnus (by Agoracritus, pupil of Phidias) and the local belief in Nemesis as distributor of due portion. Later authors systematize her retinue: Nemesis/Adrasteia is accompanied by Dike, Aidos, and Poine/Poena as executors. Ammianus, describing the fall of Gallus Caesar, says 'Poena, minister of Justice and avenger of wrongdoing, followed Adrasteia'. The Hymn to Nemesis invokes her as queen of justice with retribution at hand. Here Poine is not independent but the hand of Nemesis. Explains epithet 'Adrasteia' (inescapable) and links Poine to late antique syncretic justice theology. Influences Renaissance emblem books where Nemesis holds bridle and Poena holds whip.

Poine as Follower of Dike in Philosophical AllegoryHesiod Works and Days 256-262 (Dike and retribution); Plato Laws 728c, Gorgias 525b; Plutarch De sera numinis vindicta 564e-565a

c. 700 BCE (Hesiod) to c. 100 CE (Plutarch)

Hesiod says Dike (Justice) weeps when violated and Zeus sends poinē. Plato in Laws 728c writes that 'Dike follows, and Poine follows Dike, avenging transgression' — making Poine the inevitable second follower after Justice. Plutarch in his essay On the Delays of Divine Vengeance describes Poine, Dike, and Erinyes as daimones who attend upon souls after death, with Poine correcting the curable and Erinyes destroying the incurable. This moralizes Poine into a purgative, almost penitential force. Transforms mythic demon into ethical principle; influences Christian poena as purgatorial punishment and later legal philosophy (poena as penalty). Cited by Neoplatonists and Church Fathers.

Coroebus's Atonement and the Founding of TripodiskosPausanias 1.43.7-8; Statius Thebaid 1.649-672; Conon 19 (Photius)

Hellenistic to Imperial

After killing Poine, Coroebus finds Argos still afflicted by plague. The Delphic oracle tells him to take a tripod from the sanctuary and carry it until it falls, and there settle. He wanders into Megaris where the tripod slips on Mount Geraneia; he founds the village Tripodiskos (Little Tripod). Some versions say he offered himself as sacrifice to Apollo and was spared by the god's pity. The story explains the name Tripodiskos and provides a model of expiation: killing punishment itself is insufficient; only voluntary exile and dedication end divine wrath. Aetiology for Megarian settlement; paradigm for pharmakos and scapegoat motifs. Pausanias claims to have seen Coroebus's tomb in Megara.

Poena is a major Olympian goddess of pain with temples and worship — No temple, sanctuary, votive, or cult calendar attests Poine/Poena. She is a poetic personification and local demon, not an Olympian. Worship is directed to Nemesis, Dike, or the Erinyes; Poine is their instrument. No evidence of prayer to Poine alone.

English word 'pain' comes directly from Greek poinē meaning pain — English pain comes via Old French peine from Latin poena 'punishment, penalty'. Latin poena is borrowed from Greek poinē, but Greek poinē means 'penalty, recompense, blood-price', not physical pain. Semantic shift to 'physical suffering' happened in Latin and French.

Poena and the Erinyes/Furies are completely separate with different appearances — Ancient poets treat them as overlapping categories; Statius gives Poine Fury-like features (snakes, torch) precisely because she is modeled on Erinyes. Distinction is modern systematization.

Poena is the Roman name for Greek Poine and they are identical — Roman Poenae develop distinct infernal, plural, doorway-guardian role (Virgil) that Greek singular Poine does not have. Roman poets expand and demonize the concept; Greek usage remains more abstract/legal.

There are famous ancient statues or vase paintings of Poena — No securely identified ancient image of Poine survives. The Nemesis of Rhamnus statue is Nemesis, not Poine. Any modern image labeled Poine is conjectural or 19th-century invention.
