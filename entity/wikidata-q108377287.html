Who It Is

He commands ten ships in the greatest catalogue in Western literature — and then almost vanishes. Thalpius, son of Eurytus, stands shoulder to shoulder with three other princes of Elis on the beach at Aulis, leader of the Epeians bound for Troy, named by Homer and then left to the margins of myth where even his grandfather's name is disputed.

No speeches, decisions, or character traits are attributed to Thalpius in the Iliad. He is named only in the Catalogue and not given individual aristeia, dialogue, or moral characterization. By function he is a cooperative co-commander: Homer presents the Epeian force as led jointly by four men (Amphimachus, Thalpius, Diores, Polyxenus) sharing command of 40 ships total (10 each). This implies collegial leadership rather than single kingship, but personality must be marked as unknown. Hyginus and Pausanias add no behavioral detail. Powers
Co-command of the Epeian Contingent

Joint military leadership of 10 ships and their crews from Elis/Buprasion, as part of a four-man command structure over 40 ships total. In epic terms this implies authority to muster, provision, and lead men in battle and council.

Limit: Shared command — not sole king of the Epeians. Subordinate to Agamemnon as overall commander of the Achaean expedition. No independent strategic decisions are attributed to him. Ship number fixed at 10; no larger fleet or territorial rule beyond his contingent is claimed.

Epic Warrior Status (Implied)

As a named basileus in the Catalogue, Thalpius is by convention a bronze-armed warrior capable of fighting in the front ranks. No specific feat, duel, kill, wound, or aristeia is narrated for him, unlike major heroes.

Limit: No attested kills, no divine protection, no special weapon or armor described. He does not appear in battle narratives in Iliad 4-22, nor in funeral games in Iliad 23. Any assumption of prowess is reconstructed from status, not documented.

Accounts I
The Catalogue of Ships — The Epeians Muster for TroyHomer, Iliad Book 2

ca. 760-710 BCE (archaic epic)

In the great muster at Aulis, Homer enumerates the Achaean forces. For Elis and Buprasion, he names four leaders: Amphimachus and Thalpius, sons of Cteatus and Eurytus respectively, and Diores son of Amarynceus, and Polyxenus son of Agasthenes. Each leads ten ships, making forty Epeian ships in total. The passage defines the Epeian contingent's geographic origin — Buprasion and Elis where the rock Hyrmine and Myrsinos and the Olenian rock stand — and establishes Thalpius as one of the legitimate hereditary leaders, son of Eurytus of the Actorione/Molionid line. This is the sole Homeric appearance of Thalpius; he is not given a speech or individual action. Establishes the canonical roster of Achaean leaders for all later mythography; fixes Thalpius' status as an Elean leader and his ship number. Later authors (Hyginus, Apollodorus epitomators, Pausanias) copy this roster verbatim. No further Iliadic consequence is narrated for him specifically.

The Molionidae Lineage — Sons of ActorPausanias, Description of Greece 5.3.3-4; Eustathius ad Il. 2.620; Hesiodic and Elean genealogical tradition

Archaic genealogical poetry through 2nd century CE antiquarian compilation

Pausanias, explaining the antiquities of Elis, traces the Epeian kingship through Actor, whose sons Eurytus and Cteatus — the Moliones or Actorione, sometimes described as conjoined twins or double-bodied warriors fathered by Poseidon — marry into Elean royalty and produce Amphimachus and Thalpius. Thalpius is thus grandson of Actor (and of Molione) and great-grandson of Poseidon in some accounts where the Molionidae are Poseidon's sons. This genealogy explains why two cousins share command: they inherit a dual kingship. Homer does not name Actor in the Catalogue passage, but later genealogists supply him to systematize Elean succession. Integrates Thalpius into the broader Peloponnesian heroic genealogy and explains Elean political structure. Makes Thalpius part of the Poseidonian lineage, though he himself has no divine powers. No cult or hero worship is recorded as consequence.

The Epeian Contingent at Troy — A Silent Presence in the WarHomer, Iliad (books 4-22) by absence; Quintus Smyrnaeus, Posthomerica; Dictys Cretensis, Ephemeris belli Troiani

8th century BCE (Homer) through 3rd-4th century CE (Quintus, Dictys)

After the Catalogue, the Epeians as a group fight in the general battles, but Thalpius is never singled out for a kill, wound, or speech. In Iliad 13, the Epeians are mentioned in the fighting around the ships, but Thalpius is not named. Quintus Smyrnaeus (Posthomerica), which continues the war after Hector's death, does not give Thalpius an aristeia or death scene; he remains among the unnamed leaders. Dictys Cretensis (a fictional 'journal' of the war, 3rd/4th c. CE) lists the Elean leaders in his catalogue (2.10) copying Homer, but provides no individual deeds. The story here is one of narrative silence: Thalpius is present at Troy by definition, but the tradition preserves no deeds for him, unlike Polyxenus or Amphimachus who receive slightly more notice. Demonstrates the hierarchy of epic memory: catalogue heroes without subsequent narrative tend to fade. Thalpius becomes a name on a list, illustrating how the Epic Cycle preserved names even when stories were lost. No death or return (nostos) is recorded for him.

Accounts II
Hyginus' Latin Catalogue — Transmission to RomeHyginus, Fabulae 97

Late 1st century BCE - 2nd century CE (Augustan to Antonine)

Hyginus, in his handbook of myths, provides a Latin prose list of all Achaean leaders and ship numbers for Roman readers. Under the Epeians he writes: 'Amphimachus Cteati filius et Thalpius Eurytionis [Euryti] filius, Diores Amaryncei filius, Polyxenus Agasthenis filius — naves XL.' He preserves the Homeric division (10 ships each, 40 total) and the filiation, but Latinizes the names and introduces a textual corruption where Eurytus becomes Eurytion. This entry is not a new story but the mechanism by which Thalpius survived in medieval manuscripts and early printed mythologies, becoming the source for Renaissance dictionaries. Ensured Thalpius' name remained in the Western mythographic canon through the Middle Ages and Renaissance, even without narrative. All early modern dictionaries (Boccaccio, Natale Conti, Smith) derive their entry from this line.

Pausanias in Elis — Antiquarian Geography of the EpeiansPausanias, Description of Greece 5.1-5.5

ca. 150-180 CE

Visiting Elis, Pausanias describes the land of the Epeians, its rivers (Alpheios, Peneios), towns (Buprasion, Ephyra, Olenos), and its heroic past. He explains that the Epeians were the old name for Eleans, and that their leaders to Troy — including Thalpius and Amphimachus — were descendants of the Molionidae who had ruled Elis jointly. He notes local traditions about Augeas, Actor, and Amarynceus, and attempts to reconcile Homeric geography with the Elis of his own day, where Buprasion was already a ruin. Thalpius appears here not as an actor in a story but as a genealogical marker anchoring Homeric epic to Elean landscape. Provides the only ancient attempt to locate Thalpius in real geography and to connect him to cult and landscape. No hero shrine for Thalpius is mentioned, implying he had no active cult in Roman Elis, unlike Pelops or Oxylus.

The Absent Nostos — No Return StoryAbsence in Odyssey, Nostoi (lost Epic Cycle), Apollodorus Epitome 6, Hyginus Fabulae 114

7th century BCE - 2nd century CE

Unlike Nestor, Diomedes, or even Polyxenus (who in some traditions founds a city in Italy), Thalpius has no surviving nostos (return) story. The lost epic Nostoi, Apollodorus' Epitome, and Hyginus' list of survivors do not name him among those who died, were lost, or founded colonies. The Odyssey never mentions him. This silence is itself a story about epic survival: minor catalogue figures often lacked return traditions because their home regions (Elis) had less interest in Trojan War foundation myths than Aetolia or Crete. Modern scholars note that we cannot assume he returned safely; we simply have no tradition. Leaves Thalpius' ultimate fate unknown in antiquity, contributing to his obscurity. Later creative works are free to invent a return, but no ancient authority supports any version. Flagged as unknown.

Thalpius was killed by Hector at Troy. — No ancient source records Thalpius' death. Hector kills Amphimachus son of Cteatus (Thalpius' cousin and co-leader) at Iliad 13.185-188, and kills Diores' companion, but Thalpius is not mentioned as a casualty. The confusion arises because Amphimachus and Thalpius are named together in the Catalogue and readers conflate their fates. Thalpius' fate after the war is unattested — unknown, not dead at Troy.

Thalpius was a major hero with superhuman powers or a divine weapon. — No powers, divine weapons, or superhuman feats are attested for Thalpius. He is a purely human catalogue figure with no aristeia, no divine patron, and no special ability. Any attribution of powers comes from generic assumptions about epic heroes or from conflation with his ancestors the Molionidae (who were said to be double-bodied and very strong). The Molionidae's strength does not transfer to Thalpius in any source.

Thalpius founded a city in Italy or Crete after the Trojan War. — No ancient nostos or foundation tradition exists for Thalpius. Polyxenus (his co-leader) is sometimes said to have settled in Italy, and other Epeians went to Cyprus, but Thalpius has no such tradition. Claims of a Thalpian foundation are modern inventions without ancient source.
