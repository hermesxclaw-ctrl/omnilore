Who It Is

He has no famous deed, no monstrous form, no divine parent — only a name on a death list. Calydoneus sailed from the island of Dulichium to win Penelope, ate his host's cattle for years without invitation, and died on the floor of Odysseus' hall with an arrow in his chest, one of dozens whose only shared biography is how they overreached and were slaughtered.

Attested only through collective behavior: Homer characterizes all suitors as hybristai — arrogant, parasitic, violating xenia (guest-host law) by indefinitely occupying Odysseus' oikos, consuming his livestock and wine, harassing Penelope, plotting to murder Telemachus (Odyssey 4.660-672, 16.383-392), and abusing servants including the maids. Apollodorus adds no individual personality. Calydoneus must be understood as complicit in these acts by membership, but no individual speech, decision, or trait is preserved. Powers
Aristocratic Status and Martial Competence (Collective)

As a suitor, Calydoneus would possess the expected abilities of a late Bronze Age / early Iron Age Greek basileus-class youth: spear and bow handling, feasting etiquette, ship travel. No superhuman power, magic, or divine gift is attributed. His 'power' is social: eligibility to claim Penelope and thereby the kingship of Ithaca.

Limit: Explicitly inferior to Odysseus in cunning, archery, and divine favor. Unable to string Odysseus' bow (Od. 21.118-185, where Leodes, Antinous, Eurymachus fail; Dulichians fail offstage). Mortal and killable; no resistance to weapons. Violates xenia, thus incurs divine nemesis (Athena, Zeus).

Xenia Violation as Narrative Liability

Not a power but a mythic function: by participating in the prolonged occupation, Calydoneus incurs miasma and justifies his eventual killing under Greek religious law. This is his only 'supernatural' dimension — being marked for divinely sanctioned retribution.

Limit: No ability to avert fate; Athena actively supports Odysseus. No ritual purification attempted.

Accounts I
The Catalogue of the Suitors — Arrival from DulichiumHomer, Odyssey 16.247-253; Pseudo-Apollodorus, Epitome 7.26-27

Archaic (Homer) and Imperial Roman (Apollodorus)

After Odysseus fails to return from Troy, 108 (Homer) or 136+ (Apollodorus including servants) young nobles converge on Ithaca to court Penelope. Telemachus recites the census to his father in disguise: 52 from Dulichium, 24 from Same, 20 from Zacynthus, 12 from Ithaca. Apollodorus, systematizing later, expands Dulichium to 57 and supplies names, including Calydoneus, alongside others like Amphinomus, Ctesippus, and Agelaus. Calydoneus thus leaves his home island — whose exact location was already debated in antiquity — to take residence in a rival kingdom, staking his lineage and wealth on a marriage contest. Establishes the scale of the crisis in Ithaca and the pool of victims for the eventual Mnesterophonia. For Calydoneus, it fixes his identity as a Dulichian and his legal status as a mnestēr, making him liable to Odysseus' revenge. The numerical discrepancy becomes a lasting philological problem.

The Occupation of Odysseus' Hall — Years of ParasitismHomer, Odyssey Books 1, 2, 17, 19, 20

8th-7th century BCE

For three to four years the suitors, including Calydoneus, live in Odysseus' palace, 'wasting his substance' (Od. 1.160, 2.123). They slaughter his cattle, sheep, and goats, drink his wine, force his servants to serve them, and pressure Penelope to choose one. They reject Telemachus' demand to leave (Od. 1.368-380, 2.130-145) and ignore omens (e.g., eagle omen at 2.146-156, Theoclymenus' prophecy at 20.351-357). Penelope delays them with the stratagem of weaving and unweaving Laertes' shroud (Od. 2.93-110, 19.137-156). Calydoneus is never singled out but is present for these collective acts; by remaining, he shares culpability for violating xenia, the most serious social-religious offense in Homeric society. Creates the moral justification for mass killing; Athena and Zeus endorse retribution. Economically ruins the oikos and politically destabilizes Ithaca. Sets up the test of the bow as a device to isolate the suitors.

Accounts II
The Plot Against TelemachusHomer, Odyssey 4.660-672, 16.383-392, 16.411-412

8th-7th century BCE

Learning that Telemachus has sailed to Pylos and Sparta to seek news of Odysseus, Antinous proposes ambushing and murdering him on his return, at the strait between Ithaca and Same. The suitors collectively agree; Amphinomus alone dissents mildly but does not prevent the plan. A ship is manned and set to lie in wait. The plot fails because Athena warns Penelope (4.787-841) and Telemachus evades the ambush with Athena's help (15.28-42, 16.351-353). As a Dulichian, Calydoneus is implicated in the conspiracy by Homer's formula 'all the suitors approved' (16.394) unless explicitly excepted — and he is not excepted. Escalates suitors' guilt from parasitism to attempted kin-murder, removing any remaining sympathy and confirming their worthiness of death in epic justice. Telemachus' survival allows him to fight alongside Odysseus.

The Contest of the Bow — Failure to Win PenelopeHomer, Odyssey Book 21

8th-7th century BCE

Penelope, prompted by Athena and Eurymachus, brings out Odysseus' great bow and announces she will marry whoever strings it and shoots through twelve axe heads (21.1-79). One by one the suitors try and fail. Leodes fails first; Antinous and Eurymachus struggle and fail despite heating and greasing the bow (21.118-185). The Dulichian contingent, including Calydoneus, would have taken their turn in the sequence but Homer compresses their attempts: 'all tried, and none could string it' (21.185-186). Telemachus nearly succeeds but is stopped by Odysseus' signal (21.128-129). The bow is then given to Odysseus, still disguised as a beggar, over the suitors' protests. This is the last lawful chance for Calydoneus to claim Penelope. Demonstrates divine favor rests with Odysseus alone; failure proves suitors' illegitimacy as kings. Directly precipitates the slaughter, as Odysseus now has weapon in hand and doors secured.

The Mnesterophonia — Slaughter in the MegaronHomer, Odyssey Book 22; Pseudo-Apollodorus, Epitome 7.33

8th-7th century BCE / 1st-2nd century CE

With the bow in hand, Odysseus shoots Antinous through the throat (22.8-21), reveals himself, and with Telemachus, Eumaeus, and Philoetius — who have locked the doors and secured weapons — systematically kills the suitors. Athena, disguised as Mentor, deflects suitor spears and terrifies them (22.205-256). The suitors, including Calydoneus, attempt to fight with swords and tables but are slaughtered; none of the Dulichians are spared. Homer describes the floor 'running with blood' (22.309) and compares the dying suitors to fish gasping on a beach (22.384-388). Apollodorus summarizes: 'Odysseus... killed all the suitors with the help of Telemachus and Eumaeus and Philoetius.' Calydoneus dies here, without individual aristeia or lament. Restores Odysseus' household and kingship; demonstrates restoration of order (dike) after hybris. Corpses are later removed and house purified with sulfur (22.435-494). Calydoneus was a hero with distinct powers, appearance, or heroic deed like other Greek heroes. — He has no individual myth, power, or iconography. He is a name on a list, attested only in Apollodorus' catalogue, with no aristeia, metamorphosis, or divine parentage. His only 'deed' is collective parasitism and death. Popular AI-generated lists that assign him weapons or abilities are fabrications.

Homer names Calydoneus among the suitors in the Odyssey. — Homer never names Calydoneus. Odyssey 16.247-253 gives only numbers for Dulichians; only Amphinomus is named individually from Dulichium in Homer. The name Calydoneus appears first in Pseudo-Apollodorus, Epitome 7.26, centuries later.

The suitors were 108 in all sources and Calydoneus was one of 108. — 108 is the Homeric total (52+24+20+12). Apollodorus' total is larger (57+23+20+12 = 112 plus servants), and Hyginus gives yet other totals. Calydoneus belongs to the Apollodoran 57-count, not the Homeric 52-count in a strict sense; the '108 suitors' formula is Homer-specific.

Calydoneus survived the slaughter or was spared like Phemius and Medon. — No source spares him. Phemius (bard) and Medon (herald) are explicitly spared by Odysseus at Telemachus' intercession (Od. 22.330-380). All suitors, including all Dulichians, are killed; Apollodorus 7.33 confirms 'he killed all the suitors.' No variant tradition of survival exists for Calydoneus.
