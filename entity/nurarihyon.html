Who It Is

Imagine coming home at dusk, the busiest time of the evening, and finding a strange, bald-headed old man in your favorite chair, calmly sipping your tea as if he's always lived there. He doesn't threaten you, he doesn't steal from you—he simply acts like he owns the place, and you're too confused and intimidated to stop him. This is Nurarihyon, the 'Slippery Old Man' of Japanese folklore, a yokai whose greatest power is not magic or strength, but an unshakable, infuriating sense of entitlement. He is the god of crashing, the master of the power move, and in the modern age, he's been crowned the Supreme Commander of all yokai—a promotion he definitely didn't ask for, but absolutely deserves.

His defining behavior is the act of 'slipping into' a wealthy or busy home at dusk, when the owners are distracted. He does not steal or cause chaos. Instead, he sits down in the main room, helps himself to tea and food, and acts as if he is the master of the house. He is so nonchalant and appears so authoritative that the human residents, upon finding him, are often too confused or intimidated to throw him out. He leaves when he is good and ready, without a trace.

Powers
Slipping (Intrusion)

The ability to enter any home, especially the homes of the wealthy and powerful, without being noticed or stopped. This is not a physical invisibility but a form of social or psychological illusion. He appears so composed and authoritative that he is not challenged.

Limit: His intrusion is limited to the dusk-to-night timeframe. He does not appear to be able to enter places that are actively guarded or where the owner is particularly vigilant.

Absolute Authority (Yokai Sodaisho)

In modern interpretations, he has the authority to command all other yokai. He is the general of the Night Parade and a mediator in yokai disputes. This power is not physical strength but a form of immense spiritual and social prestige.

Limit: This authority is not absolute in all stories; some yokai are shown to be rebellious. It is a title of respect, not a magical compulsion.

Immortality

As a yokai, he is effectively ageless and immortal, having lived for centuries.

Limit: None stated.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Nurarihyon is traditionally depicted as an elderly man with a highly distinctive, elongated, gourd-shaped bald head. He is often dressed in the robes of a high-ranking Buddhist monk or a nobleman, complete with a small cap or hood. He has a serene, almost smug expression, with thin, wispy facial hair. He is never shown with weapons or in a threatening pose; his power is implied through his demeanor and clothing.

Symbols — The gourd (a symbol of both prosperity and the uncanny), the tea bowl, the monk's robes (symbolizing authority and detachment), and the act of sitting, which represents his unspoken claim to territory.

Domains — Other people's houses at dusk; the Yokai Night Parade (Hyakki Yagyo). He is a creature of the liminal space between day and night, and between the human world and the spirit world.

Accounts I
The Slippery GuestEdo-period folklore

Edo period (17th-19th century)

A wealthy merchant's family is preparing for the evening meal when they find an unfamiliar old man with a strange, elongated head sitting at the head of the table, calmly sipping their tea. He asks for more food and acts as if he owns the place. The family, confused and intimidated by his serene demeanor, does not dare to confront him. He stays for hours, and then simply leaves, never to be seen again. The family is left wondering if they imagined the whole thing. The family is left uneasy and confused. The story serves as a cautionary tale about the dangers of the liminal time of dusk and the importance of being watchful.

Accounts II
The Commander of the Night ParadeModern folklore (Shigeru Mizuki)

20th century

On certain nights, the Hyakki Yagyo (Night Parade of 100 Demons) takes place. Nurarihyon, as the Supreme Commander, is said to lead the procession, riding in a grand palanquin or walking at the front. He is the calm, wise general at the heart of the chaos, directing the flow of the parade and ensuring the yokai do not get out of hand. This version establishes him as a king-like figure. This story solidifies his role as the leader of the yokai world in modern popular culture.

The Test of HospitalityAnecdotal folklore

Various

A story about a young, brave householder who, upon finding Nurarihyon in his home, treats him with perfect courtesy, offering him the best seat and the finest tea. Impressed by this, Nurarihyon leaves a small gift of gold or a blessing of good fortune for the household. This variant suggests that the creature is a test of character and can be a bringer of fortune to those who show him respect. The household is blessed with prosperity.

Nurarihyon is a demon (Oni). — He is a yokai, a broad category of supernatural beings. He is not an Oni, which are typically depicted as ogre-like, horned, and violent. Nurarihyon is a trickster and a spirit of intrusion.

He is a violent or evil spirit. — Traditional lore shows him as a non-violent, mischievous intruder. He does not steal or harm, he simply occupies space and acts with authority.

His role as 'Supreme Commander' is ancient lore. — This title is a 20th-century creation by Shigeru Mizuki. It is not found in any pre-modern source.

He is a single, unique entity. — Like many yokai, 'Nurarihyon' is a category or a type of phenomenon. There are many stories of similar 'slippery old men' entering homes. He is a recurring archetype, not a single individual.
