Form

In the Tower of the Winds relief, Apeliotes is depicted as a youthful, beardless man. He carries a fold of his garment filled with ripened grain, fruits, and flowers — a visual metaphor for the east wind's role in ripening crops. The figure is shown with a gentle, beneficent expression, in contrast to the more turbulent depictions of other winds. He wears a light tunic, suggesting the mildness of the east wind.

Powers

Wind Control (Southeast/East) Apeliotes commands the southeast-to-east wind. He can summon gentle breezes that ripen crops and bring favorable sailing conditions. The wind is described as warm, moist, and beneficial. Greek, Tower of the Winds His power is limited to the southeast quadrant; he cannot command the other winds' domains. His wind is gentle — he cannot summon destructive storms. Some sources attribute stormy qualities to the east wind in general, conflicting with Apeliotes's gentle characterization.

Harvest Blessing The east wind's warmth ripens grain, fruits, and flowers. Apeliotes's association with harvest abundance is his defining attribute. Greek, Tower of the Winds iconography His blessing is seasonal and dependent on other weather conditions. None recorded.

Accounts

The Tower of the Winds, built by Andronikos of Cyrrhus, is an octagonal marble structure in Athens. Each of its eight faces depicts a wind god in relief, with Apeliotes on the southeast face. He is shown as a young man holding a fold of his garment filled with grain and fruits, symbolizing the east wind's role in ripening crops. The tower also functioned as a weather vane, with a bronze Triton at the top indicating the direction of the wind.

In the Odyssey, Aeolus gives Odysseus a bag containing all the winds, except the west wind (Zephyrus) which is to carry him home. The east wind is not individually named in this episode, but the framework of winds as controlled by Aeolus establishes the mythological context for wind gods like Apeliotes.

Aristotle provides a systematic classification of the winds, describing their directions and qualities. He notes that the east wind (which he calls 'Apeliotes' or 'Eurus' depending on the passage) is dry and warm, bringing fair weather. His classification influenced later wind systems, including the Tower of the Winds.

What We Don't Know

Limited literary sources specifically naming Apeliotes. Most evidence comes from the Tower of the Winds and later compilations. The distinction between Apeliotes and Eurus requires further clarification. No dedicated scholarly monograph on Apeliotes exists; he is treated within broader works on the Anemoi. Modern reception beyond the Tower of the Winds is minimal.

A way to hold it in your mind

If the Greek wind gods were a pantheon of weather celebrities, Boreas would be the rock star, Zephyrus the romantic lead, and Notus the moody artist. Apeliotes? He's the reliable farmer's friend — the one who shows up every harvest season, does his job, and never makes headlines. He's the wind that dries the grain, ripens the figs, and fills the sails of ships heading home. In a mythology full of drama, Apeliotes is the quiet professional.
