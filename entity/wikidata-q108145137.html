Who It Is

Before there were islands, there were birds with seeds in their bellies. Myrsine is the quiet proof — a genus of tough, leathery-leaved trees and shrubs that rode those birds across the entire tropical world, from the Azores to the Seychelles to Hawaiʻi, and then, stranded on each island, learned to become something new. You have probably brushed past one without knowing it: the colicwood of Florida scrub, the mapou of New Zealand bush, the kōlea of Hawaiian cloud forest. Naked branches flowering after leaf-fall, purple-black drupes that ripen a year late — Myrsine does not show off, it endures.

Long-lived, slow-growing, shade-tolerant understory to canopy trees in tropical and subtropical forest, scrub, and montane forest to subalpine shrubland (Hawaiian species to ~2000 m, New Zealand to ~1000 m). Pollination primarily by small generalist insects (flies, bees, thrips); dioecy promotes outcrossing. Dispersal is overwhelmingly ornithochorous — drupes eaten by pigeons, thrushes, white-eyes, and in Hawaiʻi by ʻōmaʻo and historically by honeycreepers. Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Ancient Greek μυρσίνη (myrsinē) meaning 'myrtle' (Myrtus), applied by Linnaeus for the myrtle-like leathery evergreen leaves and globose fruits. Linnaeus explicitly referenced Bauhin's pre-Linnaean Myrsine africana. Not related to Myrica or Myrtus phylogenetically.

Pronunciation — /ˈmɜːr.saɪ.niː/ (mur-SY-nee), anglicized also /mɜːrˈsiːn/; classical Greek myr-SEE-nee

Powers
Ornithochorous dispersal via fleshy drupe

Thin-fleshed, sugar- and lipid-rich drupes attract frugivorous birds; single hard seed passes intact through gut, enabling long-distance colonization. Explains pantropical distribution and island radiations.

Limit: Dependent on intact native bird fauna; decline of native frugivores (e.g., Hawaiian honeycreepers) reduces recruitment. Seeds desiccate quickly if not ingested; no wind dispersal.

Embelin (2,5-dihydroxy-3-undecyl-1,4-benzoquinone) production — anthelmintic and antimicrobial

Fruits and leaves of M. africana (and related M. melanophloeos, Embelia) accumulate embelin, a benzoquinone with documented anthelmintic activity against tapeworms and nematodes, plus antibacterial and antioxidant effects in vitro.

Limit: Efficacy in humans based on traditional use and limited clinical trials; dose-dependent toxicity (nausea, purgation) at high doses. Concentration varies by provenance and season. Not demonstrated for all 280+ species.

Sclerophyllous evergreen persistence

Leathery, gland-dotted leaves with thick cuticle allow year-round photosynthesis, drought tolerance, and resistance to herbivory in nutrient-poor or exposed sites.

Limit: Not frost-hardy for most tropical species; temperate species (M. australis, M. divaricata) tolerate light frost only. Leaves still palatable to introduced browsers (possums, goats).

Ornamental and hedging utility

Dense, compact habit, small glossy leaves, and tolerance of clipping make M. africana (African boxwood) and M. australis widely grown as hedges, topiary, and coastal windbreaks. Slow growth reduces maintenance.

Limit: Slow establishment; dioecious plants require both sexes for fruit set (often not needed ornamentally). In Florida, native colicwood (M. cubana) not widely cultivated due to limited nursery stock.

Wood and ethnobotanical material uses

Hard, fine-grained wood used historically for tool handles, walking sticks, and in Hawaiʻi for house posts, spears, and kapa beaters (kōlea). Bark and leaves used in rongoā Māori for skin ailments (limited documentation) and in East Africa for anthelmintic decoctions.

Limit: Wood small-dimensioned, not timber-scale. Medicinal uses not validated for all species; traditional knowledge is regionally specific and often held by cultural practitioners.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Evergreen shrubs to small-medium trees, typically 1-15 m, rarely to 20 m (e.g., M. melanophloeos in Africa). Bark smooth to fissured, often grey-brown. Leaves coriaceous (leathery), simple, alternate, exstipulate, elliptic to obovate or linear, 2-15 cm, margins entire or shallowly toothed/crenate, often with translucent gland-dots and marginal glands; petiole short. Plants dioecious or polygamo-dioecious in many species. Inflorescences fascicled

Symbols — No ancient deity iconography; modern cultural markers: In New Zealand, mapou/matipo leaves used in rongoā Māori and as forest indicator species; wood formerly used for tool handles. In Hawaiʻi, kōlea wood valued for house posts and kapa beaters; trees are kinolau-associated forest indicators of healthy mesic/wet forest. In East Africa/India, M. africana branches and fruits sold in markets as medicine, not symbolic art. No verified traditional pet

Domains — Pantropical and subtropical, nearly worldwide between ~35°N (Azores, southern USA - Florida colicwood M. cubana/M. floridana complex) and ~50°S (southern New Zealand). Centers of diversity: Southeast Asia-Malesia, Pacific Islands (French Polynesia, Hawaiʻi with ~20 endemic species), New Zealand (11-12 species), Australia (eastern), Africa (sub-Saharan, Madagascar, Seychelles), Neotropics (Andes to Central America). Habitats: lowland tropical rain

Accounts I
The Linnaean Founding — Myrsine africana and the Birth of the GenusLinnaeus, Species Plantarum (1753) and Genera Plantarum ed. 5 (1754)

1753 (18th century)

Linnaeus erected Myrsine based on material from the Cape of Good Hope and pre-Linnaean polynomial Myrsine africana, describing its myrtle-like leaves and single-seeded drupe. The protologue distinguished it from Myrtus by floral structure (5-merous, stamens opposite petals). Type later lectotypified on M. africana L., collected in Africa and the Azores. This anchored the genus to an Afro-Asian distribution, long before Pacific diversity was known. Established nomenclatural priority for Myrsine over later Rapanea (1775) and Suttonia (1832), which would later be sunk as synonyms. Defined the morphological baseline (leathery leaves, persistent calyx, globose drupe) still used in floras.

Rapanea vs. Myrsine — The Pacific Split and ReunionMez, Das Pflanzenreich IV.236 (1902); Hosaka, Occasional Papers of the Bishop Museum (1940); Jackes, Austrobaileya 7(1) (2005)

1902-2005 (20th-21st century)

Mez formalized Rapanea for New World and Pacific species lacking a style and with fully adnate stamens, keeping African-Asian Myrsine s.s. separate. Hosaka described 11 Hawaiian Myrsine (as Suttonia/Rapanea) from Kauaʻi and Oʻahu. Late 20th-century cladistic and molecular work showed Rapanea is polyphyletic and nested within Myrsine. Jackes (2005) formally transferred all Australian Rapanea to Myrsine, a move adopted by Plants of the World Online and most modern floras, though New Zealand's early electronic Flora retained Rapanea until updated by Ngā Tipu Aotearoa. Reduced two large genera to one pantropical Myrsine s.l. with >280 species; simplified biogeographic interpretation as single long-distance dispersal radiation rather than two parallel genera. Created numerous new combinations (e.g., Myrsine howittiana, M. variabilis).

Kōlea — The Hawaiian Radiation on Isolated VolcanoesWagner, Herbst & Sohmer, Manual of the Flowering Plants of Hawaiʻi (1990, rev. 1999); Appelhans et al., Frontiers in Plant Science (2020) on Hawaiian Myrsinaceae phylogeny

Colonization estimated 2-5 Ma to present; taxonomic description 1910s-1940s

A single colonization event gave rise to ~20 endemic Hawaiian Myrsine species (kōlea), diversifying across islands and elevations: M. lessertiana (kōlea lau nui, large leaves, mesic forest, all major islands), M. sandwicensis (now M. lessertiana complex), M. lanaiensis, M. juddii (critically endangered, Oʻahu Waiʻanae range), M. fosbergii, M. knudsenii (Kauaʻi). Leaves range from narrow linear (M. linearifolia) to broad elliptic; divaricating juvenile forms absent unlike NZ. Fruits feed native birds ʻōmaʻo, ʻalauahio. Radiation parallels lobelioids and silverswords as example of adaptive diversification on chronosequence of islands. Hawaiʻi became a model for island biogeography of Myrsine; many single-island endemics now federally listed as endangered due to habitat loss, pigs, and invasive plants. Traditional uses of kōlea wood for kapa beaters and house posts documented ethnographically.

Accounts II
Mapou and Matipo — The New Zealand Story and the Black Matipo ConfusionAllan, Flora of New Zealand Vol. 1 (1961); Dawson & Lucas, Field Guide to New Zealand Native Trees; Ngā Tipu Aotearoa database (Manaaki Whenua, continuously updated)

Pre-European to present; botanical description 1786 (Forster) onward

New Zealand hosts ~11 Myrsine species, most called mapou or matipo by Māori: M. australis (red matipo, mapou — common lowland tree to 6 m, red branchlets), M. divaricata (weeping matipo, divaricating shrub), M. salicina (toro), M. oliveri. Māori used leaves and bark in rongoā, and wood for implements. European settlers confused Myrsine matipo with Pittosporum tenuifolium ('black matipo' or 'kōhūhū'), a different family (Pittosporaceae) with similar Māori name matipo but unrelated — a confusion persisting in horticultural trade. Myrsine fruits ripen naked on branches after leaf fall, a phenology noted by Cockayne as distinctive. Clarified ethnobotanical record: true mapou is Myrsine, while black matipo is Pittosporum. Ngā Tipu Aotearoa now lists all NZ species under Myrsine, with Rapanea as synonym. Conservation: several NZ Myrsine are regionally uncommon but not globally threatened, unlike Pacific island endemics.

From Myrsinaceae to Primulaceae — The Molecular MergerAnderberg, Ståhl & Källersjö, Plant Systematics and Evolution (2000); Källersjö et al., American Journal of Botany (2000); APG II (2003), APG III (2009), APG IV (2016)

1998-2016

Traditional Cronquist and Takhtajan systems placed Myrsine in Myrsinaceae (order Primulales) with Ardisia, Embelia, and Primulaceae separate. DNA sequences (rbcL, ndhF, matK) showed Myrsinaceae is nested within a paraphyletic Primulaceae s.s. To maintain monophyly, APG expanded Primulaceae s.l. to include Myrsinaceae, Theophrastaceae, and Primulaceae s.s. as subfamilies. Myrsine thus moved to Primulaceae subfamily Myrsinoideae (formerly Myrsinaceae). Morphological synapomorphies (free-central placentation, gland-dotted leaves) support the clade. All modern floras, herbaria (Kew, Missouri Botanical Garden), and databases (POWO, WFO, Tropicos) list Myrsine under Primulaceae. Teaching and field guides had to update family keys; horticultural labels lagged. Explains shared chemistry (saponins, benzoquinones) across expanded family.

Colicwood and the Azores Outlier — A Plant That Crossed an OceanPress & Short, Flora of Madeira (1994); Schäfer, Flora of the Azores (2002, 2005); POWO distribution for Myrsine africana and Myrsine cubana/M. floridana

19th century discovery to present

Myrsine africana occurs disjunctly in the Azores — the only Myrsine in Macaronesia — 4,000 km from its African mainland range, interpreted as long-distance bird dispersal. In the southeastern USA, the colicwood complex (Myrsine cubana A.DC. / M. floridana / Rapanea punctata auct.) inhabits Florida scrub, hammocks, and coastal thickets, extending to the Bahamas and Cuba. Early Florida botanists (Chapman, Small) called it Rapanea guianensis, later corrected. The fruits were noted as purgative/colic remedy in folk medicine, giving the common name colicwood, though efficacy unproven. Both outliers illustrate the genus's capacity for transoceanic establishment via bird gut. Highlighted Myrsine as biogeographic model for long-distance dispersal; Azores populations are of conservation interest as isolated gene pools. In Florida, colicwood is indicator of intact maritime hammock and listed as conservation concern in some counties.

Myrsine is still in the family Myrsinaceae, separate from primroses. — Since APG II (2003) and APG IV (2016), Myrsinaceae has been merged into an expanded Primulaceae as subfamily Myrsinoideae based on DNA phylogeny. Myrsine is now Primulaceae, sister to Primula and Ardisia, though many garden labels and older books still say Myrsinaceae.

Black matipo is a Myrsine. — In New Zealand, 'black matipo' is Pittosporum tenuifolium (Pittosporaceae), not Myrsine. True matipo/mapou is Myrsine australis/divaricata. The shared Māori name matipo and similar small-leaved habit cause confusion, but fruits are completely different (capsule vs. drupe) and families unrelated.

Myrsine fruits are edible berries like blueberries and can be eaten freely. — Fruits are thin-fleshed drupes with a single hard seed; while birds eat them, human edibility is limited. Myrsine africana fruits contain embelin and saponins and are used as a purgative/vermifuge in small traditional doses; larger quantities cause vomiting and diarrhea. Most species have not been tested for edibility.

All Myrsine species look the same — small glossy myrtle-like shrubs. — Genus is morphologically diverse: from divaricating wiry shrubs (M. divaricata) to 15 m trees (M. melanophloeos), linear-leaved cliff dwellers (M. linearifolia on Kauaʻi) to broad-leaved cloud-forest trees (M. lessertiana). Leaf margins range from entire to sharply toothed, and sexual systems vary (dioecious vs. polygamous).
