Who They Are

Sué is the Muisca sun god, the primary male deity of the Muisca pantheon and the husband of Chía (the moon goddess). He governs the day, the warmth that makes crops grow, and the solar year that regulates Muisca agriculture. His temple at Suamox (modern Sogamoso) was the most sacred site in the Muisca Confederation — the 'Rome of the Muisca' — and was guarded by the most powerful priests in the realm.

The Approach

In the high cold plains of the Eastern Andes, where the Muisca people watched the sun cross a sky that seemed closer than anywhere else on Earth, the Sun was not a distant ball of fire but a living, married god. His name was Sué, and his light was not merely light: it was the breath of creation, the source of all life, the father of crops, and the giver of every person's own personal shadow-spirit.

Content advisory. Contains descriptions of ritual sacrifice and alcohol consumption in Muisca religious practice. Some accounts of violence by Spanish conquistadors against Muisca temples.

Cultural Ownership

Muisca (Chibcha) people of the Altiplano Cundiboyacense, present-day Colombia

Disputed Identifications

Some sources identify Sué with the Sun itself, while others distinguish the god as a personification. The name 'Sua' may also be used as a general term for 'sun'.

Description

Appearance

No direct descriptions of Sué's physical appearance survive. Likely anthropomorphic, possibly depicted with rays or a golden visage, but this is speculative. The Sun itself was his manifestation.

Symbols & Iconography

The Sun itself was the primary symbol. Temples were oriented to the solstices. Gold offerings (tunjos) were common. The 'City of the Sun' (Sogamoso) was sacred to him.

Behaviour & Personality

As a creator deity, Sué was benevolent, providing light and warmth essential for agriculture. He was a husband to Chía and a ruler over the northern zaques. He gave each person a personal shadow-spirit, indicating a personal, guiding role.

Domains & Habitats

Sky, particularly the sun's path. Temples at Sogamoso, Bacatá, and Guatavita. He was associated with the northern Muisca territory (zaques' domain).

Version Differences

Some sources describe Sué and Chía as siblings, others as spouses. The relationship between Sué and the supreme creator Chiminigagua varies: Chiminigagua created Sué, but Sué was also a principal deity. The association with specific caciques (Ramiriquí) is mentioned in some sources but not all.

Stories & Myths

Creation of the Sun and Moon

Source Muisca oral tradition, recorded by Spanish chroniclersCulture MuiscaPeriod Pre-Columbian

Summary

After Chiminigagua created light and the world, he created Chía (the Moon) and Sué (the Sun) to illuminate the earth and govern the cycles of time. They were placed in the sky as a married couple.

Participants

Chiminigagua

Chía

Sué

Consequences

The sun and moon began their eternal cycle, bringing day and night, seasons, and agricultural fertility.

Variants

Some versions say Sué and Chía were siblings, not spouses.

The Summer Solstice Festival

Source Spanish chroniclers, e.g., Pedro SimónCulture MuiscaPeriod Pre-Columbian

Summary

On the summer solstice, Muisca nobles led a procession to the Sun Temple at Sogamoso. They made sacrifices to ask for bountiful harvests. The people painted their bodies, drank chicha, and worshipped their shadows, believing each shadow was a personal god given by Sué.

Participants

Muisca nobles

Muisca people

Sué

Consequences

The festival reinforced social cohesion and religious devotion, ensuring agricultural prosperity.

Variants

Different temples were used in different regions.

Destruction of the Sun Temple

Source Pedro Simón, 'Noticias historiales'Culture Spanish colonialPeriod 16th century

Summary

Spanish conquerors, seeking to eradicate indigenous religion, set fire to the Sun Temple at Sogamoso. The temple, which was a major religious center, was destroyed, and its treasures were looted.

Participants

Spanish conquerors

Muisca priests

Consequences

The loss of the temple was a severe blow to Muisca religion and cultural identity.

Variants

Some accounts say the fire was accidental, others deliberate.

Ritual sacrifice

Source Spanish chroniclers; archaeological evidence

Description

During the summer solstice, sacrifices were made to Sué to ensure good harvests. These may have included animal and possibly human sacrifice, though the evidence for human sacrifice is not definitive.

Scholarly Context

Human sacrifice among the Muisca is debated; some scholars argue it was rare or non-existent, while others point to colonial accounts.

Alcohol consumption

Source Spanish chroniclers

Description

Festivals involved drinking chicha, a fermented maize beverage, to the point of intoxication, as part of religious celebration.

Scholarly Context

Chicha was central to Muisca ritual and social life.

Destruction of the Sun Temple

Source Pedro Simón

Description

The Spanish conquerors burned the Sun Temple at Sogamoso, destroying a sacred site and its associated religious objects.

Scholarly Context

This was part of the broader Spanish campaign to suppress indigenous religions.

Cultural Significance

The Temple of the Sun at Sogamoso was the most important religious structure in the Muisca world — a national sanctuary that united the various Muisca chiefdoms under a single cult. When the Spanish conquistadors under Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada arrived in 1537, they burned the temple to the ground, destroying the golden statue of Sué and scattering the sacred relics. The site remains one of the most important Muisca archaeological zones in Colombia. The Muisca solar calendar, organized around Sué's annual cycle, divided the year into 20 months of roughly 18 days each — an intricate system that governed planting, harvesting, and festival dates.
