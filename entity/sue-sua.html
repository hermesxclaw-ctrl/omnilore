Who It Is

In the high cold plains of the Eastern Andes, where the Muisca people watched the sun cross a sky that seemed closer than anywhere else on Earth, the Sun was not a distant ball of fire but a living, married god. His name was Sué, and his light was not merely light: it was the breath of creation, the source of all life, the father of crops, and the giver of every person's own personal shadow-spirit.

As a creator deity, Sué was benevolent, providing light and warmth essential for agriculture. He was a husband to Chía and a ruler over the northern zaques. He gave each person a personal shadow-spirit, indicating a personal, guiding role.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No direct descriptions of Sué's physical appearance survive. Likely anthropomorphic, possibly depicted with rays or a golden visage, but this is speculative. The Sun itself was his manifestation.

Symbols — The Sun itself was the primary symbol. Temples were oriented to the solstices. Gold offerings (tunjos) were common. The 'City of the Sun' (Sogamoso) was sacred to him.

Domains — Sky, particularly the sun's path. Temples at Sogamoso, Bacatá, and Guatavita. He was associated with the northern Muisca territory (zaques' domain).

Accounts I
Creation of the Sun and MoonMuisca oral tradition, recorded by Spanish chroniclers

Pre-Columbian

After Chiminigagua created light and the world, he created Chía (the Moon) and Sué (the Sun) to illuminate the earth and govern the cycles of time. They were placed in the sky as a married couple. The sun and moon began their eternal cycle, bringing day and night, seasons, and agricultural fertility.

Accounts II
The Summer Solstice FestivalSpanish chroniclers, e.g., Pedro Simón

Pre-Columbian

On the summer solstice, Muisca nobles led a procession to the Sun Temple at Sogamoso. They made sacrifices to ask for bountiful harvests. The people painted their bodies, drank chicha, and worshipped their shadows, believing each shadow was a personal god given by Sué. The festival reinforced social cohesion and religious devotion, ensuring agricultural prosperity.

Destruction of the Sun TemplePedro Simón, 'Noticias historiales'

16th century

Spanish conquerors, seeking to eradicate indigenous religion, set fire to the Sun Temple at Sogamoso. The temple, which was a major religious center, was destroyed, and its treasures were looted. The loss of the temple was a severe blow to Muisca religion and cultural identity.
