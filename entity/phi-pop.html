Who It Is

In the moonlit villages of Isan, a whisper passes: the Pop has come. It is not a ghost that haunts houses or wails in the dark—it is a hunger that lives inside a neighbor, a relative, a friend. By day, they seem ordinary; by night, their spirit slips out, leaving the body to feast on the living. The Pop does not kill with fangs or claws; it crawls into your sleeping form and eats you from within, savoring the liver, the intestines, the very life that makes you human. To be accused of being a Pop is to be cast out, to be feared, to be watched as you waste away. To be haunted by one is to know that death comes not from outside, but from the hidden craving within your own community.

The Pop is cunning, deceitful, and gluttonous. It craves human viscera, particularly intestines and liver. It operates by possessing a host, often a woman, and leaving the body at night to feed. It is patient and can hide its presence, but it is also greedy and may take risks to satisfy its hunger.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The Thai word 'ปอบ' (Pop) is of uncertain etymology. It may be related to the Khmer word 'ប៉ប' (pâb) meaning 'to eat' or 'to devour', reflecting its cannibalistic nature. Some scholars suggest a connection to the Mon-Khmer linguistic substrate in the region.

Pronunciation — [pɔːp̚]

Aliases — Pob, ปอบ, Phi Pop, Phi Pob

Ownership — Thai folklore, particularly associated with the Isan (Northeast Thailand) region and Laos. It is a malevolent spirit within the broader category of 'phi' (ghosts) in Thai animistic

Powers
Possession

The Pop can enter and control a human body, often while the host sleeps. The host may be unaware or unable to resist.

Limit: The Pop must leave the host body to feed, and during this time the host appears asleep or in a trance. Some traditions say the Pop can be driven out by certain rituals.

Cannibalistic Feeding

The Pop feeds on human viscera, particularly intestines and liver, often while the victim sleeps. It may also cause the victim to waste away.

Limit: The Pop must have access to the victim, often requiring proximity. It may be deterred by certain protective rituals or objects.

Shapeshifting

Pop can take the form of animals, commonly a black dog, and Ha Gom can shapeshift into human or animal forms at will.

Limit: Ordinary Pop may be limited to specific animal forms; Ha Gom has no such limitation.

Immunity to Sacred Objects

Ha Gom is unaffected by sacred objects, monks, or holy amulets.

Limit: Only a sorcerer (mor phi) with equal or greater spiritual power can subdue it.

Transmission

The Pop can be transmitted to another person through saliva, often from an old sorceress before her death, or inherited through bloodlines.

Limit: The transmission often requires a willing or unaware recipient.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Pop is typically invisible or incorporeal, but it can manifest as a black dog or other animals. When possessing a human, the host may appear normal, but in some traditions, the possessed person's eyes may appear sunken or have a strange gleam. The Pop itself is often described as a shadowy, formless entity that can enter and leave the body at will.

Symbols — The Pop is associated with darkness, night, and the consumption of raw flesh. It is often depicted in modern media as a shadowy figure or a black dog. In folklore, it is linked to sorceresses and hereditary curses.

Domains — Pop is believed to inhabit rural villages, particularly in Isan and Laos. It can live inside a human host, often a sorceress, and is passed down through families. It is nocturnal and prefers dark places.

Accounts I
The Tale of the Prince and the ServantTraditional legend

Unknown, likely ancient

A prince learns magic words that allow him to enter animal bodies. His servant overhears and uses them to enter the prince's body, impersonating him. The real prince, now in a bird's body, informs his wife, who destroys the servant's original body. The servant is then trapped in an animal body and becomes a revenant that eats intestines. The servant becomes the first Pop, doomed to possess bodies and consume viscera.

The Ta Puang IncidentVajrayana Viset

1892

An old Laotian man of Phuan descent, Ta Puang, arrives in Uttaradit and settles near Wat Pak Fang. Despite his age, his skin is smooth and radiant. Shortly after, three young women die mysteriously. Rumors spread that Ta Puang is a Pop in disguise. The incident is recorded in a periodical for the educated elite, contributing to the Pop's notoriety.

The Ban Songyae ExorcismLocal history and Catholic mission records

1908

At Ban Songyae, Yasothon, Pop possessed members of five families. Two Catholic priests, Father Desaval and Father Ambrosio, performed an exorcism that brought the situation under control. Some villagers later converted to Christianity. The successful exorcism led to conversions and left a lasting impression on the community.

The Yai Sao AccusationThai Rath

1996

An elderly woman named Yai Sao, suffering from chronic illness and weight loss, was accused by neighbors of being a Pop after she revived from apparent death and showed a preference for raw blood dishes. Thai Rath investigated and found her to be a frail, elderly woman who wept upon hearing the accusation. The accusation was shown to be unfounded, highlighting the social dangers of Pop accusations.

The 2002 Pop PairKom Chad Luek

2002

A pair of Pop, appearing as a monk with a dog and a nun with a cat, were reported in the suburbs of Bangkok. Frightened residents reported them to police, and the price of nard (a protective plant) skyrocketed. The abbot of Wat Suan Kaew dismissed the rumors as nonsense. The incident caused panic and economic impact on the nard market.

Accounts II
The 2007 Kalasin ExorcismNews reports

2007

After four sudden deaths in Kalasin, about 1000 residents raised funds for an exorcism of Pop allegedly dwelling in two women. The exorcism was performed, but the outcome is not documented.

The 2012 Pakse DeathsNews reports

2012

Ten males died suddenly in Pakse, Laos, and people believed Pop was responsible. The deaths were attributed to Pop by the community.

The 2024 TikTok WarningTikTok

2024

A pregnant woman shared a dream where an old woman bit into her womb, interpreting it as a Pop trying to devour her unborn child. The video went viral, spreading awareness and fear.

The 2025 Na Di IncidentNews reports

2025

A woman named Somkid reported strange lights and animal deaths in her village, attributing them to a Pop Laek Na. Her father had been a shaman. The community became fearful, and the incident was reported in the media.

The 2026 Mukdahan CaseNews reports

2026

An elderly woman asked a villager named Duangporn for food. The next day, Duangporn developed severe abdominal pain and died. The cause was reportedly severe cardiac [arrest?]. The incident drew public attention. The case was widely discussed, though the cause of death was cardiac.
