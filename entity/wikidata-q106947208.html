Who It Is

In the cold, rushing waters of a sacred waterfall, a lone figure stands chanting ancient words, inviting the kami to wash away the impurities of body and mind. This is misogi, a Shinto practice of ritual purification that has endured for centuries, blending physical endurance with spiritual awakening.

The practice involves physical endurance, mental focus, and spiritual intent. Participants often undergo preliminary purification such as fasting or prayer. The ritual is performed with reverence and discipline.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The term 'misogi' (禊) is derived from the Japanese verb 'misogu' (禊ぐ), meaning 'to purify oneself by washing'. It is often written with the kanji 禊, which combines the radical for 'spirit' or 'ritual' with 'water'.

Pronunciation — MEE-soh-gee

Aliases — Misogiharae, Misogi no gyōji

Ownership — Japan; Shinto tradition

Ritual Purification

Misogi is believed to cleanse the body and spirit of impurities (kegare), restoring harmony with the kami and the natural world.

Limit: Requires sincere intent and proper ritual procedure.

Spiritual Awakening

Through the practice, participants may become aware of their own spirit and unify with the kami.

Limit: Depends on individual devotion and practice.

Mental and Physical Discipline

In martial arts, particularly aikido, misogi is used to prepare the mind and develop the dantian (center).

Limit: Requires regular practice and guidance.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Practitioners typically wear white clothing: women wear a white kimono, men wear a fundoshi (loincloth), and both wear headbands. The ritual often takes place at waterfalls, rivers, lakes, or in the sea, especially in cold weather.

Symbols — Water is the central symbol of purification. The waterfall is considered a sacred site where kami reside. The color white symbolizes purity. The phrase 'harai tamae kiyome tamae rokkon shōjō' is a key incantation.

Domains — Sacred natural water sources: waterfalls, rivers, lakes, and the sea. Notable locations include Mount Ontake, the Kii mountain range, Mount Yoshino, and the Otowa no taki waterfall at Kiyomizu Temple in Kyoto. In the US, the Tsubaki Grand Shrine of America had a waterfall for misogi.

Accounts I
Purification of IzanagiKojiki and Nihon Shoki

Ancient (8th century CE)

After returning from the underworld, the god Izanagi purified himself in a river, and from his washing emerged various deities, including Amaterasu (sun goddess), Tsukuyomi (moon god), and Susanoo (storm god). This myth is foundational to Shinto purification practices. Established the importance of ritual purification in Shinto; led to the creation of major deities.

Misogi vs. HaraeSome sources use the terms interchangeably.Scholars distinguish misogi as a specific water purification, while harae is a broader purification ritual.

Misogi is a form of meditation only. — It is a ritual purification practice involving physical action and spiritual intent.

Misogi is only performed at waterfalls. — It can be performed at any natural water source, including rivers, lakes, and the sea.
