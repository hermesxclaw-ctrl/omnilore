The Approach

In the cold, rushing waters of a sacred waterfall, a lone figure stands chanting ancient words, inviting the kami to wash away the impurities of body and mind. This is misogi, a Shinto practice of ritual purification that has endured for centuries, blending physical endurance with spiritual awakening.

Identity

Misogi

Pronunciation MEE-soh-gee

Known As

MisogiharaeMisogi no gyōji

Etymology

The term 'misogi' (禊) is derived from the Japanese verb 'misogu' (禊ぐ), meaning 'to purify oneself by washing'. It is often written with the kanji 禊, which combines the radical for 'spirit' or 'ritual' with 'water'.

Cultural Ownership

Japan; Shinto tradition

Disputed Identifications

Some Western sources conflate misogi with harae, but they are distinct practices, though closely related.

Description

Appearance

Practitioners typically wear white clothing: women wear a white kimono, men wear a fundoshi (loincloth), and both wear headbands. The ritual often takes place at waterfalls, rivers, lakes, or in the sea, especially in cold weather.

Symbols & Iconography

Water is the central symbol of purification. The waterfall is considered a sacred site where kami reside. The color white symbolizes purity. The phrase 'harai tamae kiyome tamae rokkon shōjō' is a key incantation.

Behaviour & Personality

The practice involves physical endurance, mental focus, and spiritual intent. Participants often undergo preliminary purification such as fasting or prayer. The ritual is performed with reverence and discipline.

Domains & Habitats

Sacred natural water sources: waterfalls, rivers, lakes, and the sea. Notable locations include Mount Ontake, the Kii mountain range, Mount Yoshino, and the Otowa no taki waterfall at Kiyomizu Temple in Kyoto. In the US, the Tsubaki Grand Shrine of America had a waterfall for misogi.

Version Differences

Practices vary among groups. Some incorporate specific exercises like furitama (spirit shaking) and tori-fune (bird boat rowing). Some groups use salt, sake, or specific counting and shouting. The duration and intensity also vary.

Stories & Myths

Purification of Izanagi

Source Kojiki and Nihon ShokiCulture JapanesePeriod Ancient (8th century CE)

Summary

After returning from the underworld, the god Izanagi purified himself in a river, and from his washing emerged various deities, including Amaterasu (sun goddess), Tsukuyomi (moon god), and Susanoo (storm god). This myth is foundational to Shinto purification practices.

Participants

Izanagi

Consequences

Established the importance of ritual purification in Shinto; led to the creation of major deities.

Variants

Slight variations in the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki.

Contradictions & Disputes

Misogi vs. Harae

Position A

Some sources use the terms interchangeably.

Position A — evidence

Popular usage and some English-language descriptions.

Position B

Scholars distinguish misogi as a specific water purification, while harae is a broader purification ritual.

Position B — evidence

Academic Shinto studies and Japanese ritual manuals.

Scholarly Consensus

They are related but distinct; misogi is a form of harae.
