Who It Is

Imagine a night road in old Kyoto where the air goes suddenly hot — and then a single burning wheel, taller than a man, rolls toward you with a tonsured priest's face grinning from its hub. If you are unlucky enough to look at it, Sekien's 1779 bestiary warns, the thing steals your soul; if you are clever, you have already nailed to your door a scrap of paper reading 'This place is the village of Shōbo' — the one charm the wheel-monk dares not approach. Wanyūdō is a yōkai whose whole horror lives in a single glance and a single scrap of text: a flaming wheel and a monk's head, rolling across the boundary between the living world and the gates of Hell, and in your peripheral vision during an ordinary walk home. Wanyūdō is furtive and malicious but rule-bound. It steals the souls of those who simply see its form ('those who see this lose their souls'), yet it is repelled by a specific written charm pasted on a door — a very talisman-oriented, controllable weakness. In the Kyoto legend, it vocally taunts a woman onlooker ('Instead of looking at me, have a look at your own child!') before harming her infant, revealing a cruel, sadistic sense of play. Powers
Soul-seizure by gaze

Anyone who simply sees Wanyūdō's form 'loses their soul' — Sekien's own caption states it plainly: 'Those who see this lose their souls.' The seeing is the danger; mere eyewitness is sufficient to trigger the soul-loss. It steals souls and carries them to Hell to be judged.

Limit: Contingent on being seen; it cannot approach a home if the protective charm 'This place is the village of Shōbo' is pasted on the door.

Rolling fiery-wheel locomotion

The ox-cart wheel wrapped in flame turns and moves around on its own, rolling through towns and along roads. Its independence from a cart or oxen marks it as a living, animate yōkai rather than an inert vehicle.

Limit: Travels along roads and open ground; the written charm bars it from houses.

Fiery / infernal attack

Wrapped in flames, it threatens targets with fire. In game adaptations this becomes a breath or wheel of fire; folk descriptions emphasize the burning wheel itself as the weapon.

Limit: Repelled by the paper charm.

Hell-ferryman / dragging souls to judgment

Some folklore casts Wanyūdō as the gate-guard of Hell who 'gets great joy from dragging people to hell with its wheel to be judged.' It shuttles between the mortal world and the underworld, ferrying condemned souls.

Limit: Depicted as voluntary/compelled; it 'works a lot to pay off the sins of its past' when returned to earth, suggesting a purgatorial arc.

Regeneration-free physicality / immortality of the damned

As a restless soul bound to a wheel, it persists across the boundary of death, returning to earth cyclically to atone — a form of the yōkai immortality shared with other vengeful or condemned spirits.

Limit: None documented.

Written-charm vulnerability (invocation of Shōbo village)

Its single well-documented countermeasure is apotropaic: pasting a notice reading 'This place is the village of Shōbo' (此所勝母の里) on the house door. The charm works because Wanyūdō 'will not dare to approach.' The phrase derives from the Shiji anecdote of Confucius' disciple Zengzi refusing to enter a village called 'Defeating Mother' (Shengmu), because the name implied overcoming one's mother.

Limit: The charm is a verbal/written ward, effective indoors; does not stop it on open roads.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Sekien depicts Wanyūdō as a male face — that of a tonsured Buddhist priest (入道, nyūdō) — attached directly to the center of a wheel of an ox-cart (kuruma). The wheel is wrapped in fire and appears to roll or spin on its own. The exact Sekien plate is preserved at the Kyushu University Library (hdl 2324/422771). Symbols — The burning ox-cart wheel is the defining symbol. Fire = the yōkai's destructive/hellish nature and its ties to the underworld. The wheel = motion, cyclical return, and the cart (kuruma) associated with conveying the dead or damned to hell — directly paralleling the Kasha 'fire cart' (also from 火車, hi no kuruma). The tonsured monk's head marks him as an 'nyūdō' figure: a priest who has 'entered the Way' of

Domains — Roads between the mortal world and the underworld; the gates of Hell; towns such as Kyoto (the legend set in the city); burning or sparsely frequented paths where lonely travellers or gawkers might be caught. yokai.com places it among the many sōra, or roadside haunters, and describes it roaming to scare townsfolk and seize those who linger near it, dragging them down to Hell to be judged.

Accounts I
The Wheel-Monk of Sekien's BestiaryKonjaku Gazu Zoku Hyakki (1779), Sekien's plate and caption

1779 (Edo period)

In the second volume of the Gazu Hyakki Yagyō tetralogy, Sekien depicts Wanyūdō as a large priest's head set in the hub of a burning, self-turning ox-cart wheel. The single-sentence caption is at once a description and a ward: those who see the wheel-monk lose their souls, but the creature will not approach any home bearing the 'village of Shōbo' charm. Became the canonical image and protective rite that almost all later Wanyūdō lore and representation descends from; established his status as 'a relatively well-known yōkai.'

Wanyūdō and the Baby's Legs (Kyoto legend)yokai.com/wanyuudou; retold in the Wikipedia Wanyūdō article

Modern collected folklore (precise origin unattested)

As Wanyūdō rolled through a Kyoto street, a woman leaned out her window to watch it pass. The demon called to her: 'Instead of looking at me, have a look at your own child!' She turned to the baby laid on the floor and found him lying in a pool of blood with his legs missing. When she looked back at the demon, she saw her baby's legs in its mouth as it ate them. The tale is one of the more visceral in the modern Wanyūdō canon, existing to warn against idle curiosity at night. Codifies Wanyūdō's cruel taunting and cannibalistic predation; the story is widely reproduced on yōkai databases as 'one of the most famous legends.'

Accounts II
The Daimyō Reincarnated as a WheelSecondary folklore summarized in the Wikipedia Wanyūdō article and yokai.com

Late/undated tradition

Various folklore purports Wanyūdō to be the condemned soul of a tyrannical daimyō (feudal lord) who in life was notorious for having victims dragged behind an ox-cart (kuruwa-zeme style torture). As punishment, his soul is bound to the wheel itself, now tasked with guarding the gates of Hell and wandering the road between this world and the underworld, stealing the souls of anyone too near and dragging them to Hell — while also 'working to pay off the sins of its past' whenever it returns to earth. Provides a moral-justice origin absent from Sekien's caption; seeds the hell-ferryman characterization.

The Origins of Katawaguruma (Side-Wheel) at Higashinotoin, KyotoShokoku Hyakumonogatari (1677), 'The Matter of the Katawaguruma of Higashinotoin, Kyoto'

1677 (Enpō 5)

The anonymous Shokoku Hyakumonogatari (One Hundred Tales from Various Provinces) records the 'Katawaguruma' — a wheel yōkai manifesting at Higashinotoin in Kyoto — which scholars (via Murakami 2000) treat as the literary source from which Sekien derived Wanyūdō. The 1677 account is ungendered; the female reading arises only in 1743's Shokoku Rijindan. Thus the same basic wheel legend is argued to have branched into the male Wanyūdō and the female Katawaguruma. Established the pre-Sekien existence of the wheel yōkai and anchors the identity dispute between the two renderings.
