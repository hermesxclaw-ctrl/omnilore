Who It Is

On the edge of the Flemish Ardennes, where the language border cuts like a fault line through Belgium, Ronse — Renaix in French — has burned, been pillaged, rebuilt, prayed over and woven back together so many times that its very stones seem to remember. Beneath its basilica lies a 13th-century crypt built to house the bones of a Roman martyr believed to cure madness, and every Trinity Sunday those bones still travel 32 kilometers around the hills in a procession older than the city charter itself.

As a community, Ronse is characterized in sources as resilient and industrially adaptive — repeatedly rebuilding after fires (880, 1559, 1719), sackings (1478), plague (1635-36) and textile deindustrialization (1960s). Culturally liminal: officially Flemish but with deep French-Picard history and a persistent French-speaking minority (approx. 25-30% francophone in late 20th century), producing a distinctive border-town identity, sometimes tense during Belgian language conflicts of the 1960s-80s. Powers
Pilgrimage Economy (Medieval)

Ability to attract long-distance pilgrims seeking cure of mental illness via relics of Saint Hermes. Generated market, hospitality and votive income from 11th century onward; sustained town through Norman destructions.

Limit: Dependent on perceived miraculous efficacy; vulnerable to Reformation iconoclasm and Enlightenment skepticism. Relics hidden/fled during Viking raids (880, 940 recovery).

Linen and Textile Manufacturing

From late medieval linen preparation and weaving to 19th-20th century mechanized cotton, wool and carpet production. Associated Weavers (founded 1964 in Ronse) became one of Europe's largest carpet manufacturers. At peak (1840) >55% of population derived living from textiles.

Limit: Highly vulnerable to mechanization, competition from Northern France (Lille-Roubaix-Tourcoing), and post-WWII deindustrialization; acute crisis 1840s and 1960s led to mass out-migration and unemployment.

Strategic Hill Terrain and Cycling Prestige

Hills (Oude Kruisberg, Kanarieberg, Hotond) provide natural defense and later sporting capital. Ronse is a fixed point on the Tour of Flanders (Ronde van Vlaanderen) and cyclo-cross GP Mario De Clercq, drawing tourism and media.

Limit: Hilly terrain limits large-scale industrial expansion and agriculture; cycling prestige is seasonal and dependent on race organizers.

Juridical Autonomy (Ronse Franchise)

The Chapter of Saint Hermes administered an enclave (Franchise) with complete juridical and fiscal independence and its own justice system, separate from the baronial town, until 1794.

Limit: Abolished by French revolutionary legislation 1796; created dual administration and frequent jurisdictional disputes.

Art Nouveau Architectural Heritage

Villa Carpentier (1899) by Victor Horta represents high-style Art Nouveau domestic architecture, contributing to heritage tourism and protected status.

Limit: Single major Horta work in Ronse; not a cluster like Brussels; requires conservation resources.

Accounts I
Monastic Foundation and the Gift to KornelimünsterCartulary of Kornelimünster (Inde Monastery) via Sanderus, Flandria Illustrata; Delghust (1936)

7th century (foundation tradition) - 9th century (donation c. 830-840)

Hills around Ronse show Paleolithic and Neolithic occupation, with Roman building fragments indicating a villa or settlement. Urban nucleus forms in the 7th century when Saint Amand (Amandus of Maastricht, apostle of Flanders, d. c. 675) or a successor founds a church and monastery dedicated to Saints Peter and Paul. In the 9th century Louis the Pious grants Ronse and its monastery to the Abbey of the Inde at Kornelimünster near Aachen, placing it under imperial ecclesiastical control. Around the same time the relics of Saint Hermes, a Roman martyr (d. c. 120), are translated to Ronse, establishing its future pilgrimage identity. Creates dual lordship (abbey as distant proprietor, local lord as advocate) and pilgrimage focus that will define medieval Ronse. Establishes Latin name Rothnacum.

Viking Raids, Burning and Recovery of the RelicsAnnals of monastic chronicles summarized in Delghust; church tradition of Saint Hermes

880 - 940 - 1083 - 1129

During Viking (Norman) incursions, monks flee Ronse repeatedly. In 880 the monastery is burnt by Normans. Relics of Saint Hermes are evacuated and only recovered in 940. To secure them, a Romanesque crypt is built and the relics housed there in 1083; the upper collegiate church of Saint Hermes is consecrated in 1129. The saint becomes renowned as healer of mental illness, generating the French proverb: 'Saint Hermes guérit les fous du pays mais garde ceux de Renaix' (Saint Hermes cures the madmen of the region but keeps those of Ronse). Pilgrimage sustains the local economy through the High Middle Ages. Romanesque crypt becomes oldest surviving fabric and spiritual core; pilgrimage economy institutionalized; annual Fiertel procession originates in this cult.

City Charter and the Ronse FranchiseCharter of Gerard de Wautripont, 1240; Sanderus; municipal archives

1240 - 1549 - 1794

Gerard de Wautripont, lord of Ronse and advocate (voogd/avoué) of the Inde Monastery, grants Ronse all privileges of a city in 1240 — market rights, aldermen, walls. Economy flourishes on linen. A few years later the Inde Monastery sells all its Ronse possessions, ending distant abbey lordship. The seigneury becomes a barony in 1549. Uniquely, the Chapter of Saint Hermes retains the Ronse Franchise, an enclave with complete juridical and fiscal independence and its own court, coexisting with the baronial town until the French Revolution abolishes both in 1794-1796. Dual jurisdiction shapes urban politics for 500 years; Ronse ranks as a small but privileged textile city in Flanders.

Accounts II
Fire, Calvinism and Exile: The 16th-Century CatastrophesGadeyne (1976); Delghust; Reformation histories of the Spanish Netherlands

1478 - 1559 - c. 1560-1585

On 26 March 1478 French troops pillage and burn Ronse during Burgundian-Habsburg wars, but the linen economy enables rapid recovery. By mid-16th century Ronse becomes an important Calvinist center in the Spanish Netherlands. The Duke of Alba's Council of Blood (1567-1573) represses Protestantism ruthlessly. Combined with economic pressures, large numbers of weavers and fullers flee permanently to Holland, England and Germany, transferring Flemish textile expertise abroad. On 21 July 1559 a catastrophic fire ruins the entire city, compounding the crisis. Demographic and skill drain; temporary collapse of textile dominance; later Counter-Reformation re-Catholicization under archdukes Ferdinand and Isabella after 1600.

Baroque Splendor, Plague and French AnnexationsSanderus (updated editions); Delghust; castle records

1629 - 1635-1636 - 1680-1700 - 1719

Under the relatively peaceful Archdukes, Ronse recovers. In 1629 Count Jean de Nassau-Siegen becomes baron of Ronse and builds the Château de Renaix, considered one of the most beautiful castles of the Southern Netherlands (demolished 1823). Plague in 1635-1636 nearly empties the city. Despite Spanish opposition, Louis XIV annexes Ronse to France from 1680 to 1700 (Chambres de Réunion). Under Austrian rule, on 31 March 1719 a gigantic fire again turns most of the city to ashes. Inhabitants rebuild, and commerce remains textile-based. Castle becomes symbol of baroque lordship but is lost in 19th century; fires enforce brick rebuilding and new urban plan; French periods introduce French administrative influence that persists linguistically.

Revolution, Farmers' War and Industrial Boom and BustFrench administrative records 1794-1815; Gadeyne (1976); municipal census 1799, 1840

1794 - 1798 - 1815 - 1840-1960s

The French victory at Fleurus (26 June 1794) leads to annexation of the Austrian Netherlands. Ronse suffers heavy expropriations (church property) and financial difficulty; old administration dissolved 1796, French municipal law imposed. The Boerenkrijg/Guerre des Paysans (Farmers' War) in 1798 — a peasant revolt against French conscription — costs many lives in the region. In 1799 Ronse counts ~10,000 inhabitants but a third live in poverty. After union with the Netherlands (1815) and Belgian independence (1830), textiles revive: by 1840 >55% live from textiles, but mechanization triggers deep crisis, driving migration to Lille-Roubaix-Tourcoing and agricultural work in Somme/Oise. From the 1870s mechanized industry prospers despite WWI slowdown; decline begins after WWII and becomes acute in the 1960s with factory closures. Today Ronse is a commercial and touristic center. End of Ancien Régime dual jurisdiction; modern municipality created; industrial heritage defines 19th-20th century identity; post-industrial transition to services, retail, cycling tourism.

Ronse is a small village that was merged in 1977 like most Belgian municipalities. — Ronse is one of few Belgian municipalities that was NOT merged in 1977; it remains a single-city municipality (34.7 km²) encompassing only the city proper, unlike surrounding merged communes.

Saint Hermes is a local Flemish folk saint invented in the Middle Ages. — Hermes is a Roman martyr (d. c. 120 CE under Hadrian), venerated in the early Church; his relics were translated to Ronse in the 9th century. The folk belief that he cures mental illness is a medieval specialization of his cult, not his origin.

The saying 'Saint Hermes cures the mad but keeps those of Ronse' means Ronse people are actually mad. — It is a humorous French proverb (Saint Hermes guérit les fous du pays mais garde ceux de Renaix) playing on the town's reputation as pilgrimage center for mental illness — implying so many mad pilgrims came that locals seemed mad by association, or self-deprecating local humor. Not a medical claim.

Ronse's textile industry collapsed because of World War I destruction. — WWI caused a temporary slowdown, but the industry prospered from the 1870s to the 1950s. The acute decline began after WWII and especially in the 1960s due to global competition and mechanization, not war damage.

Renaix and Ronse are two different towns. — They are the same city: Ronse is the Dutch official name, Renaix the French name. Both refer to the single municipality in East Flanders.
