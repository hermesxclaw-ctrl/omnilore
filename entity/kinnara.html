Who It Is

Kinnaras are the celestial musicians of the Himalayan paradise — beings half human, half bird, whose form draws the line between heaven and earth straight through their own bodies. The male kinnara and female kinnari live in Himavanta, the mythic forest below Mount Meru, where they sing and dance with such devotion that they became the pan-Asian emblem of two things at once: transcendent art and faithful love. The Mahabharata gives them their creed in their own voice: We are everlasting lover and beloved. We never separate. The most beloved kinnari story is Manora: a kinnari princess captured by a hunter while bathing, married to a human prince, betrayed at court, who flies away home to the Himalayas — and is won back only when the prince endures a seven-year journey to the kingdom of the bird-people to prove himself. Danced in the courts of Thailand and Cambodia for centuries, Manora made the kinnari the region's figure of grace: the wife who can fly, and the love that has to earn her staying.

Origins

Sanskrit kimnara — literally, what sort of man? — named in the epics among the divine races of Kubera's mountain court; Buddhism carried the bird-bodied form across Southeast Asia.

Attested in Mahabharata, Jataka tales, Thai and Khmer artEra Classical antiquity to present

Form

Human head and torso rising from a bird's wings and legs — jeweled, crowned, mid-song; the gilded, swan-tailed kinnari of Thai art is among the most reproduced figures of Southeast Asian aesthetics.

Powers

The male plays the lute (vina), the female drums. Their song is renowned; the Jatakas credit them with fluting and soft-bodied dance. Pan-Hindu/Buddhist; strongest in early stupa art and East Asian renderings Musicians of the court, not warriors; their art is for delight and worship. None attested — musicianship is the one universal kinnara trait.Flight between the human and mystical worlds The bird-bodied form grants flight. Thai kinnaree are said to 'fly between the human and the mystical worlds'; the Sudhana/Manohara cycle has the kinnari depart by flying. East Asian art depicts them airborne with flowing garments. Southeast Asian and East Asian Buddhist tradition In the Manohara versions, flight is sometimes enabled by a magical girdle the heroine must wear — when she is captured or the girdle is removed she cannot fly. Weaknesses & Wards

Hunters, nets, and courts — the Manora cycle turns on the capturable, betrayable vulnerability of grace among humans.

Accounts

Asked about their nature, the kinnaras answer in their own voice: 'We are everlasting lover and beloved. We never separate. We are eternally husband and wife; never do we become mother and father. No offspring is seen in our lap. We are lover and beloved ever-embracing. In between us we do not permit any third creature demanding affection. Our life is a life of perpetual pleasures.' This is the canonical statement of kinnara romantic-monogamous identity — they are lovers, never parents, eternally paired.

A kinnara couple live devotedly in the forest. When the husband is wounded (in some tellings by a hunter or a stray arrow), his wife's extraordinary devotion and lamentation move Indra, lord of the gods, to descend and heal the wounded husband. The story is told to illustrate the virtue of devotion and of mutually faithful love.

Manohara, youngest of the seven daughters of the Kinnara King on Mount Kailash, travels to the human realm and is caught by a hunter (with a magic noose in some versions) who gives her to Prince Sudhana, the renowned archer-heir of the Panchala kingdom. They fall in love and marry. Years later, while Sudhana is away at war, the royal counselor accuses Manohara of bringing bad luck to the city and she is threatened with death. She flies back to the Kinnara kingdom, leaving her ring and directions. Sudhana learns the language of animals from a hermit, undertakes a seven-years-seven-months-seven-days journey past a yaksha-ogre, a river of flames, and a gigantic tree, and reaches the Kinnara king, who sets three tests: lifting a stone bench, skill with bow and arrow, and — the crucial third — identifying which of seven identical women is Manohara. Sudhana recognizes her by the ring. The king consents, and the couple returns to Panchala.

Despite their gentle, harmless nature, kinnaras are described as being 'caught, put into cages, and thus presented to kings for their delight.' Their bird-like grace and beauty made them prized living curiosities of the royal court.

In Shaolin lore the Kinnara King (Jinnaluo Wang, 紧那羅王) — a manifestation of Vajrapani and one of the divinities of the Guanyin/Dharmasala protective host — is credited as the progenitor of the monks' staff and bare-hand fighting. Historical and hagiographic sources describe the Bodhisattva Vajrapani's Kinnara King form instructing the defenders of the monastery.

Burmese Buddhists believe that out of the 136 past animal lives of the Buddha, four were kinnaras. The kinnari is also one of the 108 symbols on the footprint of the Buddha, giving the gentle musician a place in the very genealogy of the enlightened one.

Field Notes

❀ Thailand's highest royal decoration for women bears the kinnari's image.

❀ In the Lotus Sutra assemblies, kinnara kings attend the Buddha's sermons.

❀ The half-horse kinnaras of India and half-bird kinnaris of Southeast Asia are the same word wearing two bodies — a mythological pun three thousand years long.

Scholarly Debates
Anatomy: half-bird vs half-horse
The kinnara is half-man/half-horse (centaur-like). ⇌ The kinnara is half-man/half-bird.
Kinnara vs Kalaviṅka (Karyōbinga)
They are the same or interchangeable half-human/half-bird Buddhist creature. ⇌ They are distinct and unrelated.
The Mahabharata's Kinnara Kingdom: real people or mythic hybrid beings?
The Kinnaras are a real Himalayan people whose kingdom is described in the epic's geography. ⇌ They are purely mythological hybrid beings.
The Kinnara King / Vajrapani identification
The Kinnara King of Shaolin is Vajrapani (one of his protective manifestations). ⇌ The Kinnara King is a quasi-independent figure (a bird-musician protector) in his own right.
Gentle beloved vs hunted quarry
Kinnaras are harmless, tender, protective spirits. ⇌ Kinnaras are hunted, caged, and presented to kings as collected curiosities.

A way to hold it in your mind

Ask 'what kind of man is that?' — kiṃ-nara — and the ancient world of the Himalayas answers with a being that is neither man nor bird but the perfect union of both, whose only calling is music and whose only creed is eternal, childless devotion. The kinnara is the celestial love-bird of Indra's court, the gentlest sight in all of Buddhist art: half swan, half singer, fully gentle. Yet this same creature that nurses abandoned human babies in one Jataka is, in the next, caught in a cage and presented to a king 'for his delight.' And the name now travels on a stranger road still — from the Mount Kailash of a mythic bird-princess Manohara, through the golden wings of a pre-colonial Philippine idol, down to the wooden throb of a Los Angeles Buddhist taiko drum, and across to the dignified self-naming of India's Kinnar community. Few beings in world mythology claim such a spread: a single word gathering under one breath a Himalayan dream of love, an Angkorian dance, a Burmese film trophy, Shaolin steel, and the living soul of a people.
