Who It Is

Rangi-nui (Great Sky) is the primordial sky father in Māori cosmology, who lay in an eternal embrace with his wife Papa-tū-ā-nuku (Earth Mother), their children living in the darkness between them. He is one of the two primal parents from whom all gods and living things descend. In the Māori creation story, Rangi and Papa's union was so tight that no light could enter the world. Their children — Tāne, Tangaroa, Rongo, Haumia, Tāwhiri, and Tūmatauenga — lived in cramped darkness and debated how to survive. After many attempts, Tāne pushed Rangi upward, separating the parents. Rangi wept for his separation from Papa, and his tears became rain, dew, and mist. His sighs became the winds. Tāwhiri, who sided with Rangi, became the storm god who attacks his brothers. Rangi is the source of all things celestial and the father of all gods. In Hawaiian tradition, Rangi is Wākea, who similarly married Papa (Earth Mother). Rangi represents the masculine, celestial principle — distant, powerful, and ever-weeping for his lost love.

Origins

Rangi and Papa emerged from the primeval void of Te Kore and Te Pō. They embraced so closely that their children were born into darkness. After Tāne separated them, Rangi's tears of longing became the rain. Tāwhiri, Rangi's loyal son, became the storm god to avenge his father's separation.
