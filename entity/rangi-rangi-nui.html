Who It Is

Before the world was, there was only darkness and the tight embrace of two primordial parents: Rangi-nui, the vast Sky Father, and Papa-tū-ā-nuku, the Earth Mother. Their union was so close that their children lived in the cramped space between them, yearning for light and room to move. The story of how these children forced their parents apart is not just a creation myth—it is the foundational narrative of Māori cosmology, explaining the origins of the sky, the earth, and all that exists between.

In the myths, Rangi is portrayed as a loving but passive father. He clings to Papa, preventing light from entering the world. He does not act to change his situation; rather, his children decide to separate him from Papa. His weeping is said to cause rain, and his sighs create the mist. He is generally benevolent but his inaction leads to conflict.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Rangi' is derived from Proto-Polynesian *langi, meaning 'sky'. 'Nui' means 'great' or 'large', so Rangi-nui translates as 'Great Sky' or 'Sky Father'. The extended name 'Rangi-nui-e-tū-nei' means 'the great sky standing here'.

Pronunciation — In Māori, 'Rangi' is pronounced approximately as /ˈɾaŋi/, with a tapped 'r' and a nasal 'ng' sound. 'Nui' is pronounced /nu.i/.

Aliases — Rangi, Rangi-nui, Ranginui, Rangi-a-tamainu, Rangi-nui-e-tū-nei

Ownership — Rangi-nui is a central figure in Māori mythology, belonging to the indigenous Māori people of Aotearoa (New Zealand). He is a primordial deity (atua) and is revered as the ancestor

Powers
Sky Control

As the sky father, Rangi controls the sky, weather, and celestial bodies. He can cause rain (his tears), wind (his breath), and storms. He is the source of light (after separation) and the abode of the stars.

Limit: His powers are passive; he does not actively wield them but rather his state affects the world. After separation, he is fixed above the earth and cannot descend.

Progenitor

Rangi is the father of many deities and all living things. His union with Papa produced the gods of the forest, sea, wind, and more.

Limit: His creative power is expressed through his offspring, not directly.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Rangi-nui is typically personified as a male deity whose body is the sky itself. He is often described as lying prone upon the earth, with his arms and legs reaching down to embrace Papa. In some representations, he is seen as a giant, with his head in the east and his feet in the west. His body is adorned with stars, and his breath is the wind.

Symbols — The sky is the primary symbol of Rangi. In Māori art, he may be represented by the patterns of the heavens, such as the stars and constellations. The separation of Rangi and Papa is often depicted in carvings as the moment when the children push them apart, with Rangi above and Papa below. The colors of the sky at dawn and dusk are sometimes associated with Rangi's emotions.

Domains — Rangi's domain is the sky, the heavens, and the celestial realm. He is the father of all things, and his realm is the dwelling place of many atua (deities) and the stars. In Māori cosmology, the sky is not just a physical space but also a spiritual one, where the ancestors reside.

Accounts I
The Separation of Rangi and PapaGeorge Grey's 'Polynesian Mythology' and other oral traditions

Pre-contact (oral tradition), recorded in the 19th century

In the beginning, Rangi (sky) and Papa (earth) lay in a close embrace, and their children were born into the darkness between them. The children grew weary of the cramped, dark world and discussed separating their parents. After various proposals, Tāne, the god of the forest, stood on his head and pushed with his feet, forcing Rangi upward and Papa downward. Thus, light entered the world, and the sky and earth were separated. Rangi's tears fell as rain, and Papa's sighs became mist. The separation created the world as we know it: the sky above and the earth below. Tāwhirimātea, the god of winds, was angry at the separation and fought against his brothers, causing storms and hurricanes. The other children went on to populate the world and establish their domains.

Rangi and Papa are analogous to the Greek Ouranos and Gaia. — While there are similarities, Māori mythology is distinct and should not be conflated with Greek mythology. The cultural context, symbolism, and narratives are unique.

Rangi is a sky god like Zeus. — Rangi is not a ruling god; he is a primordial personification of the sky, and his role is passive. He does not intervene in human affairs as Zeus does.
