Archive Dossier — Identity

Rangi-nui

Pronunciation In Māori, 'Rangi' is pronounced approximately as /ˈɾaŋi/, with a tapped 'r' and a nasal 'ng' sound. 'Nui' is pronounced /nu.i/.

Known As

RangiRangi-nuiRanginuiRangi-a-tamainuRangi-nui-e-tū-nei

Etymology

The name 'Rangi' is derived from Proto-Polynesian *langi, meaning 'sky'. 'Nui' means 'great' or 'large', so Rangi-nui translates as 'Great Sky' or 'Sky Father'. The extended name 'Rangi-nui-e-tū-nei' means 'the great sky standing here'.

Cultural Ownership

Rangi-nui is a central figure in Māori mythology, belonging to the indigenous Māori people of Aotearoa (New Zealand). He is a primordial deity (atua) and is revered as the ancestor of many iwi (tribes) and all living things. The stories of Rangi and Papa are considered taonga (treasures) and are part of the oral tradition passed down through generations.

Disputed Identifications

There is some scholarly debate about whether Rangi-nui is the same as the sky god of other Polynesian cultures, such as Ranginui in the Cook Islands, or Atea in other traditions. While these figures share similarities, they are distinct within their own cultural contexts. Additionally, in some Māori traditions, Rangi is personified as a male deity, but in others, the sky is more abstract. These variations are regional and should be noted.

Archive Dossier — Approach

Before the world was, there was only darkness and the tight embrace of two primordial parents: Rangi-nui, the vast Sky Father, and Papa-tū-ā-nuku, the Earth Mother. Their union was so close that their children lived in the cramped space between them, yearning for light and room to move. The story of how these children forced their parents apart is not just a creation myth—it is the foundational narrative of Māori cosmology, explaining the origins of the sky, the earth, and all that exists between.

Rangi-nui (Great Sky) is the primordial sky father in Māori cosmology, who lay in an eternal embrace with his wife Papa-tū-ā-nuku (Earth Mother), their children living in the darkness between them. He is one of the two primal parents from whom all gods and living things descend.

Who It Is

Rangi-nui (Great Sky) is the primordial sky father in Māori cosmology, who lay in an eternal embrace with his wife Papa-tū-ā-nuku (Earth Mother), their children living in the darkness between them. He is one of the two primal parents from whom all gods and living things descend. In the Māori creation story, Rangi and Papa's union was so tight that no light could enter the world. Their children — Tāne, Tangaroa, Rongo, Haumia, Tāwhiri, and Tūmatauenga — lived in cramped darkness and debated how to survive. After many attempts, Tāne pushed Rangi upward, separating the parents. Rangi wept for his separation from Papa, and his tears became rain, dew, and mist. His sighs became the winds. Tāwhiri, who sided with Rangi, became the storm god who attacks his brothers. Rangi is the source of all things celestial and the father of all gods. In Hawaiian tradition, Rangi is Wākea, who similarly married Papa (Earth Mother). Rangi represents the masculine, celestial principle — distant, powerful, and ever-weeping for his lost love.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

Rangi-nui is typically personified as a male deity whose body is the sky itself. He is often described as lying prone upon the earth, with his arms and legs reaching down to embrace Papa. In some representations, he is seen as a giant, with his head in the east and his feet in the west. His body is adorned with stars, and his breath is the wind.

Symbols & Iconography

The sky is the primary symbol of Rangi. In Māori art, he may be represented by the patterns of the heavens, such as the stars and constellations. The separation of Rangi and Papa is often depicted in carvings as the moment when the children push them apart, with Rangi above and Papa below. The colors of the sky at dawn and dusk are sometimes associated with Rangi's emotions.

Behaviour & Personality

In the myths, Rangi is portrayed as a loving but passive father. He clings to Papa, preventing light from entering the world. He does not act to change his situation; rather, his children decide to separate him from Papa. His weeping is said to cause rain, and his sighs create the mist. He is generally benevolent but his inaction leads to conflict.

Domains & Habitats

Rangi's domain is the sky, the heavens, and the celestial realm. He is the father of all things, and his realm is the dwelling place of many atua (deities) and the stars. In Māori cosmology, the sky is not just a physical space but also a spiritual one, where the ancestors reside.

Version Differences

There are regional variations in the story. In some versions, Rangi and Papa are not originally parents but are created separately. In others, the number of children varies, and the method of separation differs (e.g., some say they were pushed apart, others say they were cut). In some traditions, Rangi is not a personified deity but rather a personification of the sky itself, with less anthropomorphic traits.

Origins

Rangi and Papa emerged from the primeval void of Te Kore and Te Pō. They embraced so closely that their children were born into darkness. After Tāne separated them, Rangi's tears of longing became the rain. Tāwhiri, Rangi's loyal son, became the storm god to avenge his father's separation.

Archive Dossier — Powers

Sky Control

Source Version General Māori mythology

Description

As the sky father, Rangi controls the sky, weather, and celestial bodies. He can cause rain (his tears), wind (his breath), and storms. He is the source of light (after separation) and the abode of the stars.

Limitations

His powers are passive; he does not actively wield them but rather his state affects the world. After separation, he is fixed above the earth and cannot descend.

Contradictions

None noted.

Progenitor

Source Version General Māori mythology

Description

Rangi is the father of many deities and all living things. His union with Papa produced the gods of the forest, sea, wind, and more.

Limitations

His creative power is expressed through his offspring, not directly.

Contradictions

Some traditions list different children or different parentage.
