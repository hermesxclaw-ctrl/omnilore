Who It Is

Imagine a creature so feared that its very name is a warning: the Man-Eater. It has the face of a man, the body of a lion, and a tail that can kill from a distance. It does not merely attack its prey — it devours them whole, leaving nothing but silence. This is the manticore, a monster born in the Persian imagination, adopted by Greek naturalists, moralized by medieval Christians, and reborn in the digital battlefields of modern gaming. It is a creature that has survived for two and a half thousand years by being the perfect predator — and the perfect metaphor for everything we fear in the wild, in ourselves, and in the 'other' beyond the edges of the map.

The manticore is a man-eater by nature — its very name means 'man-eater.' It hunts humans, stalking them and devouring them whole, leaving no bones behind. It is said to be extremely swift and powerful. Some accounts describe it as preferring human flesh over all other prey. It may use its voice to lure victims, though this is a later embellishment. It shoots its tail spines like arrows when threatened or hunting, able to strike from a distance.

Powers
Venomous Tail Spines

The manticore's tail is covered in venomous spines, often described as similar to porcupine quills. Some accounts say it can shoot these spines like arrows, striking enemies from a distance. The venom is lethal.

Limit: The number of spines may be limited — some accounts suggest it can only shoot a certain number before being depleted.

Triple Row of Teeth

The manticore has three rows of teeth in its jaw, like a shark. This allows it to devour its victims whole, leaving no trace.

Limit: None attested.

Superhuman Speed and Strength

The manticore is described as being exceptionally swift and powerful, able to overpower human prey with ease.

Limit: None attested.

Human Voice

The manticore's voice is described as a strange blend of a human voice and a trumpet's blast. Some later accounts suggest it may use this to lure victims.

Limit: The luring aspect is a later embellishment, not in the earliest accounts.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The manticore is consistently described with the head and face of a human (typically male), the body of a lion, and the tail of a scorpion. The tail may be covered in venomous spines, sometimes compared to porcupine quills. Its mouth has three rows of teeth, like a shark's. The color is variously given as red, tawny, or lion-colored. Some accounts describe the eyes as blue or grey, and the voice as a mixture of a trumpet's blast and a human's voi

Symbols — The manticore symbolizes danger, deception, and the untamed wild. In Christian bestiaries, it represents the devil or the hypocrite who speaks pleasantly but devours souls. In heraldry, it appears rarely but symbolizes ferocity and terror. In modern culture, it has become a symbol of the monstrous hybrid, often used to represent a creature that is both intelligent and savage.

Domains — Ancient accounts place the manticore in India, specifically in the forests and wastelands of the Indian subcontinent. Later European accounts place it in 'the East' more vaguely. In modern fantasy (Dungeons & Dragons, Pathfinder), it is found in temperate and tropical mountains, hills, and badlands, often lairing in caves or rocky terrain.

Accounts I
Ctesias's Account of the MartichorasCtesias of Cnidus, 'Indica' (via Photius)

c. 400 BCE

Ctesias, a Greek physician at the Persian court, describes the martichoras as a creature of India. It has a human face, a lion's body, and a tail with a scorpion's sting. It can shoot spines from its tail like arrows. It has three rows of teeth and devours humans whole, leaving no bones. Its name means 'man-eater.' This account becomes the foundation for all later descriptions of the manticore in Greek and Roman literature.

Pliny's Natural History EntryPliny the Elder, 'Naturalis Historia'

c. 77 CE

Pliny repeats Ctesias's description, adding that the manticore's voice is like a trumpet. He expresses some skepticism but includes it in his natural history as a real creature of India. Pliny's authority ensures the manticore's place in Western natural history and bestiary traditions for over a thousand years.

Accounts II
Pausanias's SkepticismPausanias, 'Description of Greece'

c. 150 CE

Pausanias dismisses the manticore as a fable. He suggests that the Indian tiger, with its powerful jaws, may have been misinterpreted as having three rows of teeth. He also notes that no one has actually seen a manticore. Pausanias's skepticism offers an early rationalist interpretation, suggesting the manticore may be a distorted account of a real animal (the tiger).

The Bestiary MoralizationVarious medieval bestiaries (e.g., Aberdeen Bestiary, Ashmole Bestiary)

12th–15th centuries

Medieval bestiaries repeat Pliny's description, adding a Christian moralization: the manticore represents the devil or the hypocrite who speaks with a pleasant voice but devours souls. It becomes a symbol of deception and spiritual danger. The manticore becomes a fixed part of medieval iconography, appearing in bestiaries, church carvings, and heraldry.

The Manticore in Dungeons & DragonsDungeons & Dragons (TSR/Wizards of the Coast)

1970s–present

The manticore is a staple monster in Dungeons & Dragons, appearing in the original Monster Manual (1977) and every edition since. It is a magical beast with a human head, lion body, and dragon-like wings, and it can shoot spikes from its tail. It is often encountered as a territorial predator. The D&D manticore becomes the most influential modern version, shaping the creature's appearance in countless video games, novels, and other media.

The manticore is a Greek creature. — The manticore originates in Persian mythology. It was adopted into Greek and Roman literature by Ctesias and Pliny, who placed it in India.

The manticore has wings. — Classical accounts do not describe wings. Wings are a modern addition, popularized by Dungeons & Dragons and subsequent fantasy media.

The manticore breathes fire. — There is no classical source describing fire-breathing. This is a conflation with the chimera, which does breathe fire. Some modern fantasy has given the manticore fire breath, but it is not part of the traditional lore.

The manticore is a sphinx. — While both have human heads and lion bodies, the manticore is male, lacks wings (in classical accounts), and is a man-eater. The sphinx is often female, has wings, and is associated with riddles.

The manticore's spines are always venomous. — Some classical sources describe the tail as a scorpion's stinger (which is venomous), while others describe porcupine-like quills. The venomous aspect is not universal in all accounts.
