Who It Is

A fearsome predator with the body of a lion, the face of a man, and the wings of a dragon. Its most distinctive feature is its tail, which is covered in iron-hard spikes that it can fire like crossbow bolts. Manticores are gluttonous and cruel, preferring human prey.

Form

The manticore is classically described as having the body of a lion (typically red or tawny), the face of a human (often with blue eyes and a ruddy complexion), and a tail that is either a scorpion's sting or covered in venomous spines like a porcupine's quills. The most famous feature is its three rows of teeth, set in its upper and lower jaws, interlocking like a saw. Some accounts describe the spines as being able to be launched like arrows, while others describe them as merely venomous to the touch. The creature's voice is often described as a blend of a trumpet and a flute, though some accounts say it can mimic human speech.

Powers

Spine Shooting The manticore's tail is covered in venomous spines that it can shoot like arrows at its prey, either in a single volley or one at a time. The spines are said to be as thick as a reed and a foot long. This ability is attested in Ctesias, Aelian, and later medieval bestiaries, but is notably ABSENT from Pliny's account, who only mentions the scorpion sting. Ctesias, Aelian, medieval bestiaries Some accounts say the manticore can only shoot a limited number of spines before they regrow. The range is not specified, but implied to be short-to-medium. Pliny does not mention the spine-shooting ability, only the scorpion-like tail. This may be a later embellishment or a conflation with the porcupine.

Three Rows of Teeth The manticore has three rows of teeth in both its upper and lower jaws, which interlock like a saw. This allows it to tear flesh and crush bones, enabling it to consume its prey whole, leaving no trace. All ancient sources (Ctesias, Aristotle, Pliny, Aelian) None noted, but the teeth are a feature of its anatomy, not a separate 'power'. None.

This contradicts the spine-shooting version of the tail, though some later accounts combine both, giving the tail a sting AND spines.

Behavior & Habitat

The manticore is a voracious predator of human flesh, consuming its victims whole, leaving no bones behind. It is said to be extremely swift, capable of outrunning any human. It hunts either alone or in small groups, and its favorite prey is humans, though it will also eat other animals. In some accounts, it is said to prefer the flesh of young men. It is fearless and will attack even armed opponents.

Accounts

Ctesias, a Greek physician at the Persian court, describes the martichoras as a creature of India. It has a human face, a lion's body, and a tail with a scorpion's sting. It can shoot spines from its tail like arrows. It has three rows of teeth and devours humans whole, leaving no bones. Its name means 'man-eater.'

Pliny repeats Ctesias's description, adding that the manticore's voice is like a trumpet. He expresses some skepticism but includes it in his natural history as a real creature of India.

Pausanias dismisses the manticore as a fable. He suggests that the Indian tiger, with its powerful jaws, may have been misinterpreted as having three rows of teeth. He also notes that no one has actually seen a manticore.

Medieval bestiaries repeat Pliny's description, adding a Christian moralization: the manticore represents the devil or the hypocrite who speaks with a pleasant voice but devours souls. It becomes a symbol of deception and spiritual danger.

The manticore is a staple monster in Dungeons & Dragons, appearing in the original Monster Manual (1977) and every edition since. It is a magical beast with a human head, lion body, and dragon-like wings, and it can shoot spikes from its tail. It is often encountered as a territorial predator.

Field Notes

⛧ The manticore's name literally means 'man-eater' in Old Persian, making it one of the few mythological creatures whose name is a direct description of its behavior.

⛧ The manticore is one of the few monsters from 'natural history' (as opposed to pure mythology) that made the leap to medieval bestiaries and then to modern fantasy gaming.

⛧ In the *Aberdeen Bestiary*, the manticore is described as having a 'trumpet-like' voice, which is a far cry from the terrifying roar one might expect.

⛧ The manticore is a 'Pathfinder Iconic Monster,' meaning it is one of the signature creatures of the Pathfinder RPG, alongside the goblin and the dragon.

⛧ The manticore's three rows of teeth have been interpreted by some scholars as a misunderstanding of the tiger's carnassial teeth, which are indeed formidable.

Scholarly Debates
Tail: Scorpion stinger vs. Porcupine quills vs. Both
The tail ends in a scorpion's stinger. ⇌ The tail is covered in porcupine-like quills that can be shot.
Evidence for A: Ctesias's original description (via Photius) mentions a scorpion's sting.
Evidence for B: Pliny and later bestiaries describe the tail as having spines like a porcupine's, which can be shot.
Wings or no wings
The classical manticore has no wings. ⇌ Modern fantasy (D&D, Pathfinder) depicts it with wings.
Evidence for A: Ctesias, Pliny, and bestiaries do not mention wings.
Evidence for B: The D&D Monster Manual (1977) and subsequent games add wings.
Reality vs. Myth
The manticore is a real creature (as believed by Pliny and medieval bestiaries). ⇌ The manticore is a mythical creature, possibly based on a distorted account of a tiger.
Evidence for A: Pliny and bestiaries treat it as a real animal of India.
Evidence for B: Pausanias suggests it is a fable; modern scholars generally accept it as mythical.
Voice: Trumpet vs. Human
The manticore's voice is like a trumpet's blast. ⇌ The manticore's voice is like a human voice, possibly used to lure victims.
Evidence for A: Pliny describes the voice as 'like a trumpet.'
Evidence for B: Some later bestiaries and modern fiction describe a human-like voice.
What We Don't Know

The original Persian sources for the manticore are lost; the earliest surviving accounts are Greek (Ctesias) and Roman (Pliny). The exact nature of the tail (scorpion vs. quill) is ambiguous in the earliest sources. The creature's role in pre-Islamic Persian mythology is not well-documented outside of the Greek transmission. Modern popular culture's version (with wings) is well-documented but represents a significant departure from classical lore. The manticore's appearance in medieval heraldry is under-researched.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of the manticore as the ancient world's 'serial killer' of the animal kingdom: a predator so efficient and so terrifying that it was described in the language of natural history, not myth. It's the creature that made the 'man-eater' label literal. In modern fantasy, it's the monster that bridges the gap between the 'real' world of ancient travelers and the 'unreal' world of dungeons and dragons.
