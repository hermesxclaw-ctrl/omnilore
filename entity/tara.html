Tara

Who It Is
Form

In her most common form, Green Tārā (śyāmatārā) is depicted as a young sixteen-year-old girlish woman with dark green or blue-green skin, associated with awakened activity. Because dark green is seen as a combination of all colors, Green Tārā is considered the source of all beneficial activities. She is shown seated or standing with one leg extended in readiness to aid beings. Her right hand makes the varada mudrā (gesture of granting boons), and her left hand holds a blue lotus (utpala) that releases its fragrance with the appearance of the moon—linking her to lunar and nocturnal symbolism. She wears ornaments and a crown; Amitābha Buddha is often depicted on her crown, affirming her membership in the Lotus Buddha family. White Tārā (Sitatārā) is shown with seven eyes: two on the face, one on the forehead, and one on each palm and foot. She sits on a white lotus and is associated with maternal compassion, healing, longevity, and purification. Red Tārā is associated with power, magnetizing (vaśīkaraṇa), and transforming desire into compassion. Blue Tārā (Ekajaṭā) appears as a ferocious protector. Ferocious Ugrā Tārā is dark/black, stands on a corpse, holds a sword, cutter, blue lotus, and skullcup, with Aksobhya Buddha on her head.

Powers

Savioress of Saṃsāra Tārā's primary power is rescuing beings from the ocean of saṃsāra. She is invoked by the root mantra Oṃ tāre tuttāre ture svāhā, where 'tuttāre' means 'the one who helps beings to cross' and 'ture' means 'swift'. She saves from all eight outer dangers and their inner psychological equivalents.

Emanation of Compassion According to origin myth, Tārā arose from a single tear of Avalokiteśvara that fell to the earth and became a lotus, from which she emerged. She is therefore considered the personification of his karuṇā (compassion). She also manifests as Yeshe Tsogyal, Padmasambhava's consort.

Mother of All Buddhas The Tārā Tantra calls her 'a mother who gives birth to the buddhas of the three times'. In Vajrayāna she is regarded as a Buddha who transcends saṃsāra and nirvāṇa, capable of manifesting supreme bodhi in many world systems.

Protector from Eight Dangers The Āryatārāṣṭaghoratāraṇī Sūtra teaches that Tārā protects from lions (pride), wild elephants (delusion), fire (anger), snakes (jealousy), bandits (wrong views), bondage/waters (attachment/desire), infectious diseases, and demons/doubts.

Twenty-One Emanations Green Tārā emanates twenty-one distinct forms, each with specialized functions: pacifying (śānti), increasing (pauṣṭika), enthralling (vaśīkaraṇa), and wrathful (abhicāra). Each has its own mantra, color, implements, and activity.

Pure Land Abider Tārā presides over her own buddhafield, Arrayed in Turquoise Petals (Yurlod Kurpa), said to be contained within Sukhāvatī, the pure land of Amitābha. She is part of the Lotus Buddha family.

Fertility and Nature Goddess As Khadiravaṇī-Tārā ('dweller in the Khadira forest'), she is associated with plant life, flowers, acacia trees, and the wind element. She functions as a healing goddess (especially White Tārā), a nurturing mother, and a fertility deity.

Tantric Deity Yoga As a yidam, Tārā is the latent aspect of the practitioner's own mind in Vajrayāna deity yoga. Through visualization, mantra recitation, and completion-stage practice, the adept develops her awakened qualities, ultimately realizing emptiness and the inseparability of compassion and wisdom.

Behavior & Habitat

Tārā is characterized by swift, compassionate responsiveness to the cries of beings in saṃsāra. She embodies motherhood, playfulness, and fierce protective energy. According to tradition, she vowed to be reborn always as a woman until saṃsāra ended, making her a feminist icon in Buddhist history. She is invoked for protection from eight outer dangers (lions, elephants, fire, snakes, robbers, waters, infectious diseases, demons) and their inner psychological analogues (pride, delusion, anger, jealousy, wrong views, avarice, desire, doubt). In Vajrayāna practice she functions as a yidam—an emanation of the adept's own mind—simultaneously regarded as an independently existing Buddha. She is associated with the wind element (vāyu), making her swift in responding to calls for aid. Her playful, girlish nature manifests when practitioners take the spiritual path too seriously; she laughs at self-righteousness and plays pranks on those who lack reverence for the feminine.

Accounts

The most widely known origin myth tells that Avalokiteśvara, the Bodhisattva of Compassion, wept on seeing the boundless suffering of beings in saṃsāra. His tears fell to earth, became a lotus, and from it arose Tārā. The Indian master Sūryagupta records: 'Arya-Lokesvara...saw that however many migrating beings he removed from samsara, they grew no fewer, and He wept. Tara sprang from the opening filaments of his face—of an utpala (blue lotus) that grew in the water of His tears.' This establishes Tārā as the direct personification of karuṇā.

In another origin story, millions of years ago, a princess named Jñanachandra (Yeshe Dawa, 'Moon of Primordial Awareness') made offerings to Buddha Tonyo Drupa for countless aeons and received bodhicitta instruction. Monks suggested she pray to be reborn as a male to advance further. She replied that only 'weak-minded worldlings' see gender as a barrier to enlightenment, and resolved always to be reborn as a woman until saṃsāra ends. She meditated in a palace for ten million years, releasing tens of millions of beings from suffering. Tonyo Drupa prophesied she would henceforth manifest as Goddess Tārā in many world systems. The 14th Dalai Lama has cited this as a true feminist movement in Buddhism.

In Tibetan tradition, Green Tārā manifested as the Nepalese Princess Bhrikuti (also called Bhr̥kuṭī), who married the Tibetan king Songtsen Gampo in the 7th century. She is credited with bringing Buddhism to Tibet alongside Princess Wencheng (White Tārā's manifestation). This historical conflation grounds the deity in Tibetan political and religious history.

The Indian mahasiddha Padmasambhava gave a Red Tārā sadhana to Yeshe Tsogyal in the 8th century, instructing her to hide it as a treasure (terma). The teaching remained concealed until the 20th century, when Nyingma lama Apong Terton rediscovered it. It was later retransmitted to Sakya Trizin and Chagdud Tulku Rinpoche, who released it to western students.

Atīśa Dīpaṃkaraśrījñāna (982–1054 CE), the Bengali master who founded the Kadampa school of Tibetan Buddhism, was a devotee of Tārā. He composed a praise to her and three Tārā sadhanas. His Sādhana of the Twenty-One Tārās became one of the most popular systems in Tibetan Buddhism. Each of the twenty-one forms has its own mantra, used to invoke specific activities—Swift Heroic Tārā for subduing hindrances, White Tārā for healing, Golden Tārā for increasing, etc.

The Tārā Tantra describes a mandala of nine Tārās: Green Tārā at center, surrounded by dark blue, gold, red, and green Taras in the four quarters, plus Hook, Lasso, Shackles, and Bell Taras at the four gates. All are depicted as young women covered in jewels. This mandala system organizes the deity's multiplicity into a systematic ritual framework.

What We Don't Know

Exact dating of the earliest Tārā image at Kānherī cave 90 requires epigraphic confirmation. The relationship between the Buddhist Tārā and the Hindu Tārā Mahāvidyā remains debated—whether the Hindu form derived from Buddhist sources or developed independently. The Ugrā Tārā cross-pollination timeline into Hindu tantra needs clearer dating. Specific film, anime, or game appearances of Tārā in modern popular culture require broader search beyond Wikipedia. The exact location and dating of the Agusan image needs archaeological verification.

A way to hold it in your mind

Tārā is not a passive icon of compassion—she is the goddess who looked at a universe of suffering and said: I will not leave until every being is free. She is the only major Buddhist deity who made her gender the foundation of a cosmic vow, emerging from tears of mercy and refusing to ascend to Buddhahood in a male body. Her twenty-one faces, her mantra chanted by children and lamas alike, her forest-green skin and her seven-eyed white form: she is the most complete map of feminine awakened power in the Buddhist pantheon.
