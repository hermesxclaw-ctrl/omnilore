Who It Is

Tara (Tārā, 'star' or 'she who ferries across') is one of the most beloved and widely worshipped female deities in Vajrayana Buddhism, particularly in the Tibetan tradition, where she is known as Jetsun Drolma ('Venerable Savioress'). Her most widely known origin myth tells that she was a princess named Yeshe Dawa ('Moon of Wisdom') who, through aeons of practice, became a bodhisattva. When monks suggested she should pray to be reborn as a male to attain Buddhahood, she responded that gender is an illusion of the unenlightened mind and vowed to attain enlightenment in female form—and to continue manifesting in female form to liberate all beings until samsara is emptied. From her tears of compassion, the two most famous forms of Tara emerged: Green Tara (Syama Tara), who represents active compassion and protection from the eight great fears (lions, elephants, fire, snakes, robbers, imprisonment, drowning, and demons), and White Tara (Sita Tara), associated with healing, long life, and serenity. Green Tara is typically depicted seated in the royal ease posture (lalitasana), with her right leg extended, ready to spring into action, holding a blue lotus (utpala). White Tara sits in full lotus posture with seven eyes (the third eye of wisdom plus eyes on her palms and soles), symbolizing her all-seeing compassion. In Tibetan Buddhism, Tara is considered the female counterpart and consort of Avalokiteshvara, born from his compassionate tear. The famous 21 Praises of Tara (Homage to the 21 Taras) is one of the most frequently recited liturgies in Tibetan Buddhism, invoking her protection against every conceivable danger and obstacle. Tara practice is central to all four major schools of Tibetan Buddhism and is particularly associated with the Gelug and Kadam lineages. She is also widely worshipped in Nepal, Mongolia, and throughout the Himalayan region.
