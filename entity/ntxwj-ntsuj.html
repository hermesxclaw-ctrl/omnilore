Who It Is

In the highlands of Southeast Asia, where mist clings to the mountains and the spirit world hums just beneath the everyday, the Hmong speak of ntxwj and ntsuj—two words that drift between breath and soul, shadow and self. To understand a Hmong person is to understand that they carry more than one life within them, and that those lives can wander, flee, or be stolen.

The soul is believed to be fragile and prone to wandering, especially during sleep, fright, or illness. It can be frightened away by loud noises, sudden shocks, or traumatic events. A person can lose one or more of their souls, leading to physical or mental illness. The soul is also thought to have its own will and may leave the body if it is unhappy or if it is stolen by malevolent spirits.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — In the Hmong language, ntsuj is often translated as 'soul' or 'spirit,' and ntxwj is a related term that can mean 'shade' or 'ghostly double.' The pairing ntxwj ntsuj forms a compound that refers to the multiple soul components believed to reside in a person. The exact etymological roots are debated

Pronunciation — ntxwj is pronounced approximately as 'n-tshwee' (with a voiceless nasal and a retroflex affricate), and ntsuj as 'n-tsu' (with a breathy tone). Tone contours are essential in Hmong; the exact pitch may vary by dialect.

Aliases — ntsuj plig, ntxwj ntsuj, plig, soul, spirit

Powers
Soul flight

The soul can leave the body during sleep or trance, allowing the person to experience dreams or for the shaman to travel to the spirit world.

Limit: If the soul is away too long or is captured, the person becomes ill or dies.

Soul multiplicity

A person has multiple souls, each with a specific function. Loss of one soul may cause specific symptoms, such as weakness or mental illness.

Limit: The number of souls and their functions are not universally agreed upon.

Soul calling

Through rituals, the shaman can call back a lost soul, using offerings, chants, and sometimes animal sacrifice to entice the soul to return.

Limit: The ritual must be performed correctly; if the soul has been captured by a powerful spirit, more elaborate ceremonies are needed.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Ntxwj and ntsuj are invisible, incorporeal entities. They are not depicted in physical form in traditional Hmong art, but are sometimes described metaphorically as a shadow or a breath. In shamanic rituals, the shaman may see them as small lights or as bird-like shapes.

Symbols — In ritual contexts, the soul is symbolized by a small piece of cloth or a string tied around the wrist (khau nrig) during soul-calling ceremonies. The shaman's rattles, drums, and headdress are symbolic of the journey to retrieve a lost soul.

Domains — The soul resides within the human body, but it can travel during dreams and trance states. The spirit world, where souls go after death or during soul loss, is a parallel realm inhabited by ancestors and various spirits. The shaman navigates this realm to find and retrieve lost souls.

Unknown; traditional

A young child becomes listless and ill after being frightened by a dog. The family calls a shaman, who discovers that the child's ntsuj has been snatched by a malevolent forest spirit. The shaman journeys to the spirit world, negotiates with the spirit, and retrieves the soul, restoring the child to health. The child recovers, and the family offers a pig to the shaman as payment.

20th century

A man dreams that he is walking in a beautiful valley. When he wakes, he feels weak and cannot work. His wife calls a shaman, who explains that his ntxwj has been attracted to the spirit world and does not want to return. The shaman performs a soul-calling ceremony, offering a chicken and tying a string around the man's wrist to bind the soul back to his body. The man regains his strength and returns to work.
