Who It Is

The name Phorbas means 'he who gives pasture' — and in Greek myth it is given to at least fifteen different men, from a dragon-slaying boxer who became king of Rhodes to the shepherd who pulled a pierced infant off Mount Cithaeron and, without knowing it, saved Oedipus.

Varies by identity: Rhodian Phorbas is violent, competitive, and civic-benefactor turned tyrannical boxer who challenges travelers (like Antaeus); Argive/Thessalian kings are genealogical placeholders with little character; Trojan Phorbas is pious/hospitable (favored by Hermes); shepherd Phorbas is compassionate and disobedient to a royal order to expose the infant — he pities and saves rather than kills; Acarnanian Phorbas is a loyal ally-warrior; Pan Phorbas is rustic, revelrous. No single personality can be harmonized.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Ancient Greek Φόρβας, from φέρβω / φορβή 'to feed, give pasture, nourish' + -ας agent suffix. Literally 'feeder, giver of pasture, nourisher.' Genitive Φόρβαντος. LSJ and Beekes connect to pastoral vocabulary; the name is transparently auspicious for herdsmen and kings. Not to be confused with Φόβος

Powers
Serpent-slaying and pest-clearance

Rhodian Phorbas ridded Rhodes of a plague of great serpents that infested the island, killing them and restoring habitability. Rewarded with kingship.

Limit: Mortal feat, not magical; requires physical combat. Later Rhodians expelled him after he became violent.

Pankration / Boxing supremacy

Phorbas son of Lapithes/ Triopas was an unbeatable boxer who challenged and killed travelers on the road, similar to Antaeus or Cercyon.

Limit: Mortal athletic skill; ultimately killed by Apollo (in boxing myth) according to some Rhodian accounts where Apollo defeats him for his hubris.

Hermes' favor and pastoral wealth

Phorbas of Troy was 'loved by Hermes' (Hermei philos) who made him rich in herds and property. Divine patronage grants prosperity, not combat power.

Limit: Wealth does not protect his son Ilioneus from death at Troy; favor is not invulnerability.

Shepherding, exposure, and rescue

Phorbas the botēr disobeys Laius' order to expose Oedipus on Cithaeron, instead gives the infant to a Corinthian shepherd (or Polybus' herdsman) ensuring survival. Skills: mountain navigation, infant care, oath-keeping.

Limit: No supernatural power; moral choice with catastrophic unintended consequence (fulfillment of oracle).

Warrior combat (Trojan and Persean wars)

Phorbas son of Metion fights with Phineus against Perseus at the banquet massacre; Phorbas of Acarnania fights with Eumolpus against Erechtheus at Eleusis. Both are spearmen in heroic battle.

Limit: Both are killed — by Perseus (turned to stone or slain) and by Erechtheus respectively — showing mortal vulnerability.

Pan nature (rustic divinity)

As one of the twelve younger Panes, Phorbas has goat legs, horns, pan-pipes, superhuman agility in mountains, and participation in Dionysus' retinue.

Limit: Minor Pan, not the great god Pan; powers limited to rustic music, panic, and scouting.

Charioteering

Phorbas as charioteer (heniochos) of Theseus, skilled in horse management and racing.

Limit: Mortal techne; no divine speed.

Accounts I
Phorbas son of Lapithes, the Rhodian dragon-slayer and boxer-kingDiodorus Siculus 5.58; Pseudo-Apollodorus 1.7.3; Hyginus Fab. 28

Mythic pre-Trojan generation; recorded 1st c. BCE - 2nd c. CE

Phorbas, son of Lapithes (eponym of the Lapiths) and Orsinome, brother of Periphas, is invited by the Rhodians who are plagued by countless serpents. He clears the island, killing the beasts, and is made king. He later becomes a brutal boxing champion who challenges passers-by, until Apollo intervenes and kills him, or in another version he reigns justly and founds a dynasty. His son Triopas? Or his brother Triopas? Genealogies diverge. The Rhodians heroized him. Aetiology for Rhodian cult of Phorbas and for the island's habitability; explains Rhodian kingship before the Heliadae; provides moral about hubris in athletics.

Phorbas son of Triopas and Hiscilla, prince of ThessalyPseudo-Apollodorus 1.7.4; Diodorus 5.61; scholia to Apollonius Rhodius

Mythic Deucalionid stemma; Hellenistic compilation

Triopas, son of Poseidon and Canace (or Lapithes), marries Hiscilla daughter of Myrmidon and fathers Iphimedeia, Erysichthon (the impious hunger-sufferer), and Phorbas. This Phorbas is a genealogical link, fatherless in narrative but important for connecting Thessalian houses to Rhodes and Argos. Some traditions make this Phorbas the same as the Rhodian hero, merging the two. Anchors Thessalian genealogy; explains kinship between Triopas' line and Myrmidons; variant parentage creates duplicate Phorbases.

Phorbas, king of Argos and father of TriopasPausanias 2.16.1; Pseudo-Apollodorus 2.1.1-2; Hyginus Fab. 124

Mythic Argive genealogy; recorded 2nd c. CE

In the Argive succession, Phorbas succeeds Criasus (or Argus) as king of Argos. He fathers Triopas (a different Triopas from the Thessalian) who succeeds him, and Arestor (father of Argus Panoptes in some). This establishes the Inachid line leading to Io. He is a culture-hero king with no surviving deeds beyond reigning. Fills Argive king-list gap between Inachus and Crotopus; links Argos to Thessalian Triopas via name duplication, causing later confusion.

Phorbas the Shepherd who saves OedipusSophocles Oedipus Tyrannus 1021-1185 (unnamed shepherd); Seneca Oedipus 811-816 (named Phorbas); Pseudo-Apollodorus 3.5.7; Hyginus Fab. 66

5th c. BCE (Sophocles) to 1st c. CE (Seneca) naming

King Laius orders his herdsman to expose his infant son on Mount Cithaeron with ankles pierced to thwart the oracle that the son will kill him. The herdsman — called Phorbas in Latin and later Greek tradition, but left unnamed by Sophocles — pities the child, spares him, and hands him to a Corinthian shepherd of King Polybus. The child becomes Oedipus, later kills Laius and marries Jocasta, fulfilling the oracle precisely because he was saved. When plague strikes Thebes, the shepherd is summoned and under interrogation reveals the truth, destroying Oedipus. Renaissance sculpture memorializes the moment of rescue. Central pivot of the Oedipus myth; demonstrates theme of fate vs compassion; Phorbas' mercy enables tragedy. His testimony is the anagnorisis trigger.

Accounts II
Phorbas of Troy, beloved of Hermes, and his son IlioneusHomer Iliad 14.490-505; Dictys Cretensis 2.??; Tzetzes ad Lycophron

Archaic epic (8th c. BCE)

Phorbas of Troy, a wealthy herdsman favored by Hermes who made him rich, has a son Ilioneus, an only child. At Troy, during Poseidon's intervention on the Greek side, Peneleos the Boeotian leader spears Ilioneus under the brow, the spear driving through the eye and out the nape, killing him. Phorbas' line ends; his wealth cannot save his son. The pathos emphasizes war's indiscriminate slaughter. Mini-tragedy within Iliad aristeia; illustrates Hermes' favor is not hereditary protection; Ilioneus' death is one of the Iliad's most graphic.

Phorbas son of Metion of Syene at the battle of Perseus vs PhineusOvid Metamorphoses 5.74-106

Augustan (8 CE)

At the banquet where Phineus claims Andromeda and attacks Perseus, a melee erupts. Among Phineus' Ethiopian/Egyptian allies is Phorbas son of Metion from Syene (Aswan), described as fighting alongside other exotic warriors. Perseus, overwhelmed, uses the Gorgon's head to petrify or slays them. Phorbas is killed in the carnage — Ovid's catalogue lists his death among many to show Perseus' superhuman defense. Demonstrates Perseus' Gorgon weapon; Ovid's ethnographic catalogue displays Roman fascination with Egypt; Phorbas is a name-fill with geographic epithet.

Phorbas of Acarnania, son of Poseidon, ally of Eumolpus against ErechtheusPausanias 1.38.3; Apollodorus 3.15.4; Statius Thebaid 7.254 (scholia)

Mythic Athenian-Eleusinian war; recorded 2nd c. CE

Eumolpus, son of Poseidon and Chione, leads Thracians/Eleusinians against King Erechtheus of Athens. With him comes Phorbas of Acarnania, also a son of Poseidon, as ally. In the battle at Eleusis, Erechtheus kills Phorbas (and Eumolpus). Poseidon avenges his sons by killing Erechtheus with his trident or asking Zeus to strike him. The war establishes the Eleusinian Mysteries' aetiology. Aetiology for the truce between Athens and Eleusis and for the priesthoods of Eumolpidae and Kerykes; explains Erechtheus' death.

Phorbas among the twelve younger Panes and father of Ambracia and Tiresias variantsNonnus Dionysiaca 14.88-96 (Panes); Stephanus of Byzantium s.v. Ambracia; Suda s.v. Tiresias

5th c. CE (Nonnus); 6th-10th c. CE (Stephanus/Suda)

Nonnus lists Phorbas as one of the twelve younger Panes, goat-legged sons of Pan who join Dionysus' Indian campaign. Separately, Stephanus records Phorbas son of Helios as father of Ambracia, eponym of the city Ambracia in Epirus (daughter Ambracia). The Cretans, according to a scholion preserved in the Suda, call Phorbas father of the seer Tiresias (instead of Everes and Chariclo), making Tiresias Cretan. Another fragment makes Phorbas father of Dexithea who by Aeneas mothers Romulus and Remus — a highly aberrant genealogy. Shows late mythographic proliferation: same name reused for eponyms and divine retinue; Cretan Tiresias variant challenges mainstream Theban genealogy; Dexithea variant is an outlier attempting to link Greek hero to Roman founders.

Phorbas is a single mythological character with a continuous biography. — Phorbas is a common heroic name reused for at least 12 distinct mortals (plus a Pan) across different regions and generations. No ancient source gives him a unified life story.

Sophocles' shepherd who saves Oedipus is named Phorbas in the original play. — Sophocles never names him; he is 'the herdsman' (botēr). The name Phorbas for this shepherd appears first in Seneca's Latin Oedipus and is then retrojected onto Sophocles by later translators and sculptors.

Phorbas was a god or had supernatural powers. — All Phorbases except the late Pan are mortal men — kings, herdsmen, warriors, athletes. Their 'powers' are human skills (boxing, shepherding) or Hermes-granted wealth, not divinity. The Pan is a minor rustic daimon, not Olympian.

Phorbas fathered Romulus and Remus. — One extremely aberrant late variant makes Phorbas father of Dexithea who by Aeneas bears Romulus and Remus. The canonical Roman tradition makes them sons of Mars and Rhea Silvia (or Ilia), with Aeneas generations earlier. The Phorbas-Dexithea stemma is isolated and contradictory chronologically.
