Who It Is

Before stomach acid, before enzymes, before any nutrient can reach your blood, your jaws must do violence. Chewing — mastication — is the body's first demolition site: teeth like hammers and shears, tongue and cheeks like careful hands, saliva already rewriting starch into sugar while you are still tasting. It is unconscious until it isn't, ancient enough to predate mammals, and so ordinary you do it thousands of times a day without ever learning how you learned it.

Chewing is primarily an unconscious (semi-autonomic) central pattern generator (CPG) behavior housed in the brainstem, modulated by higher conscious input. Characteristics: rhythmic, bilaterally coordinated but typically unilateral per sequence (preferred chewing side), adaptable duration and force based on food texture (hard nuts vs soft bread), integrated with salivation, swallowing, and respiration. Powers
Mechanical Comminution and Surface Area Amplification

Teeth crush and grind food into smaller particles, exponentially increasing surface area for digestive enzymes and bile. Incisors cut, canines grip, premolars and molars shear and grind with transverse power strokes. This is the foundational power of chewing; without it, large or fibrous foods resist enzymatic attack.

Limit: Limited by dental status, occlusal force, pain, and food texture. Edentulous individuals or those with malocclusion show reduced comminution efficiency and larger swallowed particle size. Extremely hard or fibrous foods may remain incompletely fragmented even with prolonged chewing.

Salivary Enzymatic Initiation and Bolus Formation

Chewing stimulates mechanoreceptors that trigger salivary flow from parotid, submandibular, and sublingual glands. Saliva containing amylase and lingual lipase begins carbohydrate and lipid hydrolysis, moistens and binds particles with mucins into a cohesive, warm bolus ready for safe swallowing and esophageal peristalsis.

Limit: Enzymatic contribution is modest compared to pancreatic phase; amylase is inactivated by gastric acid. Xerostomia (dry mouth), Sjögren's syndrome, or anticholinergic drugs impair bolus formation and increase choking risk.

Nutrient Bioaccessibility and Glycemic Modulation

Thorough chewing ruptures plant cell walls and protein matrices (e.g., almond parenchyma, meat fibers) releasing encapsulated lipids and micronutrients; simultaneously, chewing frequency modulates gastric emptying, cephalic-phase insulin release, GLP-1 secretion, and postprandial glycemia. More chews per bite increase diet-induced thermogenesis via sympathetic activation and enhance satiety signaling.

Limit: Effect is food-specific: increased chewing of nuts/meat increases nutrient extraction, while fewer chews of rapidly digestible starch (white bread, rice) may blunt glycemic spikes by delaying absorption. Overgeneralizing 'more chewing is always better' is inaccurate. Individual dentition, age, and metabolic status modify outcomes.

Sensorimotor Learning and Adaptive Motor Program

The masticatory central pattern generator in the brainstem generates rhythmic jaw movements, continuously adapted by proprioceptive feedback from periodontal ligaments and TMJ, and by cortical input. This allows real-time adjustment of force, cycle duration, and lateral excursion to food hardness and to changes in occlusion (tooth loss, restorations, dentures).

Limit: Adaptation requires learning and can be incomplete in elderly or neurologically impaired individuals, increasing aspiration risk. Excessive input from myofascial pain or occlusal imbalance can destabilize the program, contributing to temporomandibular disorders.

Hippocampal and Cognitive Stimulation

Mastication increases cerebral blood flow and sensory input, shown to stimulate hippocampal neurogenesis and help maintain hippocampal function in animal models and associated with better cognitive performance and reduced stress markers in humans. Tooth loss and soft-diet rearing in mice correlate with hippocampal deficits.

Limit: Causality in humans is correlational; confounding by nutrition, socioeconomic status, and general health. Chewing gum studies show acute attention benefits but long-term neuroprotective claims remain hypothetical.

Non-Nutritive and Industrial Mastication (Analogous Power)

By analogy, mechanical 'chewing' is applied to forestry masticators/mulchers that grind brush and small timber into mulch to create firelines, and to premastication where a caregiver pre-chews food for an infant, externally performing comminution the infant cannot yet achieve.

Limit: Forestry mastication does not involve enzymatic digestion; premastication carries pathogen transmission risk (e.g., cariogenic bacteria, viruses) and is culturally variable and now discouraged in many clinical guidelines.

Accounts I
The Mammalian Invention: From Synapsid Shearing to Precise OcclusionCrompton, Hylander, and Kielan-Jaworowska on cynodont and early mammal jaw evolution; fossil dentitions from Permian to Jurassic

Late Permian (~260 Ma) to Early Jurassic (~200 Ma); synthesized in 20th-21st century literature

Mastication in the strict sense appears with early synapsids. Non-mammalian vertebrates generally capture and swallow without transverse grinding. Cynodont therapsids evolved heterodont dentition and increasingly precise occlusion, culminating in mammals with a dentary-squamosal jaw joint, diphyodonty, and unilateral chewing with a transverse power stroke. This allowed mammals to exploit seeds, insects, and fibrous plants by mechanically breaking them before fermentation, a key adaptive advantage over contemporaneous reptiles. Enabled mammalian diversification into herbivorous, omnivorous, and insectivorous niches; set the anatomical template for human mastication; established the necessity of dental occlusion and its vulnerability to disorder.

The Duck-Bills' Answer: Hadrosaurid Dental BatteriesWilliams et al. 2009; Erickson et al.; Ostrom and Norman on ornithopod feeding

Cretaceous (c. 100–66 Ma); described in modern literature

While sauropods relied on massive gut fermentation and gastroliths, ornithopods — especially hadrosaurids (duck-billed dinosaurs) — convergently evolved chewing analogs. They developed dental batteries containing hundreds of stacked teeth (up to 1,400 per individual) with continuous replacement, and a pleurokinetic hinge allowing transverse grinding similar to cows. Microwear shows grinding of tough conifers, horsetails, and angiosperms, extracting more nutrients without a mammalian jaw joint. Provided competitive advantage in Late Cretaceous ecosystems; demonstrated that 'chewing' can evolve convergently without mammalian anatomy; informs debates on dinosaur metabolism and niche partitioning.

Cud and Night: Rumination as Externalized ChewingAnimal science literature on ruminant digestion; recent ethology studies on circadian rumination patterns (e.g., 2020s studies on dairy cattle nocturnal rumination)

Eocene origin of Ruminantia (~50 Ma); modern husbandry observations 19th century–present

Cattle, sheep, goats, deer and other ruminants chew food more than once. After initial ingestion, food enters the rumen and reticulum for microbial fermentation, is regurgitated as cud, re-chewed thoroughly to reduce particle size, and reswallowed for further digestion. Researchers have shown rumination intensifies at night, with more intense and longer chewing bouts during nocturnal recumbency, optimizing nutrient extraction and reducing predation risk while resting. Allows ruminants to thrive on cellulose-rich forage indigestible to monogastric mammals; underpins human pastoral economies, dairy and meat production; provides model for understanding chewing efficiency and methane production.

Accounts II
The Parent's Mouth: Premastication Across SpeciesAnthropological and primatological literature; e.g., Pelto et al. on premastication, Hinde & Milligan on primate infant feeding; historical medical texts

Prehistoric to present; documented ethnographically 19th–21st centuries

When infants lack teeth and coordinated chewing, caregivers in many societies — and some other mammals and birds — premasticate: food is chewed to a soft bolus in the adult mouth and transferred to the infant. This provides appropriately textured, enzymatically pre-treated food and, historically, may have transmitted oral microbiota. While once universal, the practice has declined where commercial baby foods are available and is now clinically discouraged in some settings due to pathogen transmission (e.g., Streptococcus mutans, HIV, HTLV). Enabled infant survival before modern food processing; shaped weaning foods and cultural taste transmission; modern tension between cultural normalcy and biomedical risk messaging.

Fletcherism: The Great Masticator and the 32-Chew DoctrineHorace Fletcher 'The New Glutton or Epicure' (1903), 'Fletcherism: What It Is' (1913); contemporary press coverage and medical responses; Whorton 1982 history of dietetics

1890s–1920s (peak), with lasting influence

American businessman Horace Fletcher (1849–1919), after regaining health through obsessive thorough chewing, promoted 'Fletcherizing' — chewing each bite until liquefied, often 32 times or until tasteless, and rejecting food that could not be thus processed. Endorsed by John Harvey Kellogg, William James, and even the U.S. Army briefly, Fletcherism became a fad promising weight control, moral discipline, and digestive health. Medical establishment ultimately dismissed its extreme claims, but it seeded modern interest in eating rate, satiety, and mindful eating. Popularized the idea that chewing rate affects health and that thorough mastication could cure disease; contributed to vocabulary ('Fletcherize'); left a cautionary example of extrapolating personal anecdote into universal prescription; anticipated later controlled trials on chewing and energy intake.

Nine Thousand Years of Gum: Birch Tar Chewing in Mesolithic ScandinaviaLarsson et al. 2019 Antiquity; Jensen et al. 2019 Nature Communications (ancient DNA from chewed tar); archaeological reports from Huseby Klev

c. 7000 BCE (Early Mesolithic); excavated 1980s–2010s, analyzed 2019

Lumps of birch bark tar, chewed and bearing clear tooth imprints from children and adults, show that northern Europeans chewed tar 9,000 years ago. Functions debated: adhesive for hafting tools (tar softens when chewed and heated), possible medicinal/antiseptic use, or simply as a chewing gum-like activity. In 2019, DNA extracted from a chewed tar lump at Huseby Klev yielded a complete human genome of a Scandinavian hunter-gatherer woman, plus oral microbiome, demonstrating that chewing residues can become archaeological archives. Pushes evidence for non-nutritive chewing deep into prehistory; provides direct biological data from chewing artifacts; blurs line between tool production and oral habit.

The Soft Diet Trap: When Chewing Is LostClinical nutrition literature on edentulism, dysphagia, and soft diets; hospital dietetics guidelines; studies on fruit/vegetable intake in edentulous elderly

20th–21st century

When chewing is impossible due to tooth loss, pain, or medical prescription, patients are placed on soft or pureed diets that require no chewing. While necessary short-term, long-term soft diets correlate with reduced fruit and vegetable intake, inadequate fiber and micronutrients, and increased risk of malnutrition, particularly in elderly and post-surgical populations. Rehabilitation focuses on dental restoration, texture-modified foods that preserve nutrition, and masticatory training, underscoring that chewing is not merely mechanical but nutritional gatekeeping. Highlights chewing as determinant of dietary quality and health equity; drives innovation in palatable soft foods and implant dentistry; informs public health campaigns for oral health preservation.

Does more chewing always improve health and weight control?More chews per bite universally reduces intake, increases satiety hormones (GLP-1), increases diet-induced thermogenesis, and aids weight loss.Effects are food-specific and modest; more chewing increases nutrient extraction from some foods (nuts) and may increase glycemic load from starches if it accelerates amylase action; long-term weight loss evidence is weak.
Is chewing's cognitive benefit causal or correlational?Mastication directly stimulates hippocampal neurogenesis and prevents cognitive decline via increased cerebral blood flow and sensory input.Association between tooth loss/reduced chewing and cognitive decline is confounded by nutrition, socioeconomic status, and general health; chewing is a marker, not a mechanism.
What defines 'true' chewing in evolution?Only mammals chew in the strict sense (precise occlusion, transverse power stroke, unilateral chewing). Dinosaur and fish grinding are analogous but not homologous.Hadrosaurids and some fish exhibit true chewing with comparable functional outcomes (particle size reduction, increased digestive efficiency) and should be considered chewing sensu lato.
Is premastication adaptive or hazardous?Premastication is a traditional, adaptive infant feeding strategy that provides appropriate texture, enzymes, and microbiota when alternatives are lacking.Premastication is a hazardous behavior that transmits pathogens and should be universally discouraged.

You must chew each bite 32 times for health (one chew per tooth). — No evidence supports a universal 32-chew rule. Optimal chew number varies by food texture, dentition, and individual. Studies use protocols like 25 vs 40 chews for almonds to test extremes, but guidelines emphasize eating slowly and chewing until food is appropriately textured, not counting to 32. The number comes from Fletcher's mnemonic, not physiology.

Chewing gum has existed in its modern form for 9,000 years. — Archaeological birch bark tar lumps from 7000 BCE show non-nutritive chewing, but they were likely adhesive for tool hafting or medicinal, not confectionery gum. Modern chewing gum (chicle, synthetic polymers, sweeteners) dates to the 19th century (e.g., Spruce gum, Wrigley's 1891). Conflating tar chewing with Wrigley's is anachronistic.

Carnivores don't chew at all. — Carnivores chew little compared to herbivores and often shear or gulp, but they do use carnassial teeth to shear meat and some (e.g., wolves, hyenas) crack bone with powerful chewing. 'Wolfing it down' describes rapid ingestion, not absence of mastication.

If you chew more, you will automatically lose weight because you burn calories chewing. — Diet-induced thermogenesis does increase slightly with more chews via sympathetic activation, but the calorie cost of chewing itself is trivial (a few calories per meal). Appetite and intake effects are mediated by satiety hormones and eating rate, not by chewing as exercise. Weight loss requires sustained energy deficit.

Birds, reptiles, and amphibians chew like mammals. — No living birds, amphibians, or reptiles exhibit mammalian mastication with precise occlusion and transverse grinding. Birds use beaks and gizzards (with grit) to grind food internally; reptiles and amphibians generally capture and swallow whole or with simple puncture-crushing. Some fish have pharyngeal jaws that grind, but this is not homologous to mammalian chewing.
