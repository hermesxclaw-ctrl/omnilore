Who It Is

Before there was Cupid with his chubby cheeks and Valentine's Day arrows, there was Eros—a being so fundamental that the universe itself could not begin without him. In the oldest Greek telling, he is the fourth thing to exist, after Chaos, Earth, and the Abyss: a force of cosmic attraction that draws all things together. But by the time of the Roman Empire, this primordial power had shrunk into a mischievous boy who shoots gods and mortals alike with irresistible desire—a force that could make Medea betray her family, drive Dionysus to rape, or unite a mortal princess with a god. The story of how a cosmic creator became a chubby cherub is one of the strangest transformations in all of mythology—and the modern Valentine's Day icon is only the last, most sanitized chapter.

Eros is capricious, mischievous, and unpredictable. He delights in making gods and mortals fall in love, often with ironic or cruel results. He is loyal to his mother Aphrodite but also rebellious, as seen in the Psyche myth where he defies her orders. He is depicted as playful and sometimes petulant, especially in his child form. In his primordial aspect, he is a cosmic force of attraction and creation, more abstract and less personal.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The Greek ἔρως (éros) means 'desire' or 'love', derived from the verb ἔραμαι (éramai) 'to desire, love'. R. S. P. Beekes speculates a Pre-Greek origin, making the word's deeper roots uncertain. The Latin 'Cupid' derives from cupere, 'to desire'. The name is the root of the English word 'erotic'.

Pronunciation — AIR-os (UK), ER-os (US); Ancient Greek: É-rōs with a long 'o'

Powers
Love Arrows

Eros's signature weapon. Gold-tipped arrows cause the target to fall deeply in love with the first person they see. Lead-tipped arrows cause aversion or repulsion. The arrows work on both mortals and immortal gods, making Eros one of the few beings who can override divine will.

Limit: The effect is indiscriminate—Eros cannot control who the victim falls for. He himself is not immune to love's effects, as shown in the Psyche myth where he is wounded by his own arrow.

Cosmic Creation (as Protogonos/Phanes)

As the primordial first-born god, Eros/Phanes is the creator of all other beings. He bore Nyx (Night) as his daughter and consort, and through her created Gaia (Earth) and Ouranos (Sky). He holds the scepter of universal power and passes it to Nyx, then to Ouranos.

Limit: His power is ultimately passed on—he does not remain the supreme ruler. Zeus later swallows him to absorb his creative power.

Flight

Eros can fly with his wings, moving swiftly between Olympus, earth, and the underworld. He is described as 'swift as the whirlwinds of the tempest' in Aristophanes.

Limit: None documented

Emotional Manipulation

Beyond arrows, Eros can directly inspire passion, madness, or obsession. He 'smites maids' breasts with unknown heat' and drives gods mad with desire, as with Dionysus and Aura.

Limit: His influence creates overwhelming passion but not lasting love—that requires mutual trust and effort, as the Psyche myth demonstrates.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Eros appears in two distinct forms across Greek art and literature. In early Greek poetry and vase painting, he is a handsome young adult male, athletic and beardless, embodying sexual power and youthful virility. He is winged, with large powerful wings, and carries his signature bow and quiver of arrows. Symbols — Bow and arrows (gold-tipped arrows cause love, lead-tipped cause aversion), wings, torch, blindfold (in later art), butterfly (associated with Psyche), egg (in Orphic tradition), scepter (as primordial ruler)

Domains — Love, desire, sex, attraction, procreation, creation. As Protogonos/Phanes, he is the first-born god and creator of all beings. As Aphrodite's son, he governs romantic and sexual desire between individuals. He shares the fourth day of each month as sacred with Herakles, Hermes, and Aphrodite in Athens.

Accounts I
The Birth of Eros (Hesiodic)Hesiod, Theogony

c. 700 BC

Eros emerges as the fourth primordial being, after Chaos, Gaia, and Tartarus. He is the force that draws beings together, enabling creation and procreation. He is described as 'the most beautiful among the immortal gods'. Eros's presence enables the cosmic order to proceed—without him, nothing could come together to create.

The Orphic CreationOrphic fragments

c. 6th century BC onwards

Chronos (Time) creates the cosmic egg, from which Eros/Phanes emerges as the first ruler of the universe. He is called Protogonos ('first-born') and creates all other beings. He bears Nyx as his daughter and takes her as consort. Nyx bears Gaia and Ouranos. The scepter of power passes from Eros to Nyx to Ouranos. Zeus later swallows Phanes, absorbing his creative power and remaking the world. Eros is the original creator and ruler, but his power is ultimately absorbed by Zeus and then passed to Dionysus, who is an incarnation of the primordial Eros.

Accounts II
Eros and PsycheApuleius, The Golden Ass (fullest version); earlier folktale tradition

c. 160-180 AD (written), older oral tradition

Psyche, a mortal princess of breathtaking beauty, attracts worship that rivals Aphrodite. Jealous, Aphrodite orders Eros to make Psyche fall in love with the ugliest creature on earth. Instead, Eros accidentally wounds himself with his own arrow and falls in love with Psyche. He spirits her away to a magical palace, visiting her only at night in darkness, forbidding her to look at his face. Psyche's jealous sisters convince her to light a lamp and look—she sees the beautiful god, but he flees, wounded and betrayed. Psyche wanders the earth seeking him, completing impossible tasks set by Aphrodite (sorting grains, fetching golden fleece, collecting water from the Styx, descending to the underworld for Persephone's beauty). She fails the last task, falling asleep from a box of underworld sleep, but Eros rescues her. Zeus grants Psyche immortality, and she becomes the goddess of the soul. Their daughter is Voluptas/Hedone (Pleasure). The union of Love and Soul produces Pleasure. The myth becomes a central allegory for the soul's journey toward divine love in later Neoplatonic and Christ

Eros and AuraNonnus, Dionysiaca

5th century AD

Eros shoots Dionysus with an arrow, driving the god mad with desire for the virgin huntress Aura. Dionysus rapes Aura, who becomes pregnant and gives birth to twins. Aura, driven mad, tries to kill the infants but is stopped. One child, Iacchus, becomes a companion of Dionysus. A tragic story showing Eros's arrows can cause immense suffering, not just joyful love. Aura's name means 'breeze', and her story is one of violation and madness.

Eros and MedeaApollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica

3rd century BC

At Hera's request, Aphrodite persuades Eros to shoot Medea with his arrow so she falls in love with Jason. Eros, playing dice, is bribed with a golden ball. Medea is struck with overwhelming passion for Jason, which drives her to betray her family and help him steal the Golden Fleece. Medea's forced love leads to tragedy—she kills her brother, betrays her homeland, and later murders her children when Jason abandons her. Eros's intervention has catastrophic ripple effects.

Eros/Cupid is a cute, harmless baby who makes people fall in love. — The chubby cherub is a late Hellenistic/Roman and Renaissance invention. The original Greek Eros was a powerful, sometimes dangerous young god whose arrows caused obsession, rape, and tragedy. He is 'cute' only in the most sanitized modern depictions.

Eros and Cupid are the same god in all respects. — They are closely related but not identical. Greek Eros is more complex—primordial creator in some traditions, powerful force of desire in others. Roman Cupid is more consistently the mischievous son of Venus, often depicted as a child.

Eros's arrows always create true, lasting love. — His arrows create overwhelming desire and obsession, which is not the same as love. The Psyche myth explicitly shows that lasting love requires trust, effort, and facing challenges—not just being shot by an arrow.

Eros is the son of Aphrodite in all Greek sources. — In the earliest sources, Eros has no parents—he is a primordial being. The parentage with Aphrodite is a later development, first attested in Simonides (6th-5th century BC).
