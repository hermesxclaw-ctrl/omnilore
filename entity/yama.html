Who It Is

Yama is the Hindu god of death, the lord of the ancestors (pitrs), and the judge of the souls of the deceased. He holds a uniquely important and grimly dignified position in the Hindu pantheon. His name derives from the Sanskrit root 'yam,' meaning to restrain or to control, and Yama restrains the living from the realm of death and the dead from the realm of the living. In the Rigveda, Yama is the first mortal to have died and discovered the path to the afterlife, thereby becoming the king of the dead and the guide for all who follow. He is the son of the sun god Surya (or Vivasvat) and his twin sister is Yamuna, the sacred river. Yama is typically depicted as a dark or green-skinned figure of terrifying yet majestic appearance, riding a black buffalo. He carries two primary implements: the danda (a mace or staff of justice) and the pasha (a noose with which he captures the soul at the moment of death). He is accompanied by his two fierce, four-eyed dogs, Shyama and Shabala, who guard the path to his realm and bring souls before him. In his court, the scribe Chitragupta reads from the Akashic records (Agrasandhani), an exhaustive account of every deed the soul has committed in life. Based on this, Yama pronounces judgment: the virtuous are guided to the realm of the ancestors (Pitrloka) or to higher heavens, while the wicked are consigned to various hells (Narakas) appropriate to their sins. Yama appears prominently in the Katha Upanishad, where the young seeker Nachiketa visits him and receives profound teachings on the nature of the self, death, and liberation. This philosophical encounter transforms Yama from a purely fearsome figure into a teacher of the deepest spiritual truths. He is the guardian of the southern direction (dikpala) and the embodiment of dharma as applied through inevitable justice.
