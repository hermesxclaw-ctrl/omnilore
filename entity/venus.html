Who They Are

Venus was the Roman goddess of love, beauty, desire, sex, fertility, prosperity, and victory. She was syncretized with the Greek Aphrodite but held a far more elevated and politically significant position in Roman religion. Through her son Aeneas — the Trojan hero who fled the burning city and whose descendants founded Rome — Venus was venerated as the divine ancestress of the Roman people. Julius Caesar's gens Iulia claimed direct descent from Venus through Aeneas's son Iulus (Ascanius), making her patronage central to the legitimacy of the Julio-Claudian dynasty.

In Roman art, Venus was portrayed with extraordinary sensuality and grace. The most famous type, the Venus Pudica ('Modest Venus'), shows her emerging from the sea or rising from her bath, one hand covering her breasts and the other her groin in a gesture that simultaneously conceals and draws attention to her nudity. Other representations show her as Venus Victrix ('Venus the Victorious'), armed and triumphant, or as Venus Genetrix ('Venus the Ancestress'), draped in the stola and palla of a respectable Roman matron. Her attributes included the rose, the myrtle, the dove, the swan, and the scallop shell.

Venus's Roman cult was more explicitly tied to state power and civic virtue than her Greek counterpart's. The temple of Venus Erycina on the Capitoline Hill, dedicated in 215 BCE during the dark days of the Second Punic War, was built at the direction of the Sibylline Books to secure divine favor. Venus Verticordia ('Changer of Hearts') was invoked to turn the hearts of Roman women away from licentiousness toward chastity. Caesar's temple of Venus Genetrix in his new forum (46 BCE) established her as the patron of the Julian family and, by extension, of Rome itself. The poet Lucretius opened his philosophical epic De Rerum Natura with a hymn to Venus as the generative force of the universe — the goddess through whom all living things are conceived and born.

Venus's mythological narrative includes her adulterous affair with Mars — exposed by her husband Vulcan before the assembled gods — her love for the mortal Adonis, and her role in the Trojan War as the protector of Aeneas and his people. Her girdle (cestus) possessed the power to inspire irresistible desire in whomever she chose. In Apuleius's tale of Cupid and Psyche, Venus appears as a jealous and vengeful mother-in-law, subjecting Psyche to impossible trials — a rare negative portrayal that highlights the complexity of her character.
