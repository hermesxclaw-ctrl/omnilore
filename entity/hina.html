I climbed the rainbow to escape weariness and found my home in the moon. There I beat my tapa cloth forever — the patterns you see in the lunar face are the work of my hands. The tides obey my rhythm; the women know my name.

Who They Are

Hina is one of the most widespread and multifaceted goddesses in Polynesian mythology — a lunar deity associated with the moon, women's arts (particularly tapa/barkcloth making), coral reefs, and the ocean. Her name and attributes vary across the Pacific: she is the mother of Maui in many traditions, the goddess who lives in the moon in Hawai'i and Tahiti, and a figure associated with femininity, creativity, and the rhythmic cycles of time. Hina embodies the feminine principle that complements the masculine solar and warrior deities of Polynesian religion.

Physically, Hina is associated with the moon itself. In Hawaiian tradition, she is the woman in the moon (a motif common to many cultures) — Hina-i-ka-malama (Hina in the Moon). She is depicted as a beautiful woman who dwells on the lunar surface, often accompanied by her implements of tapa-making: the anvil (kua) and beater (i'e). In some traditions, she holds a calabash and rainbow. Her lunar association connects her to the cycles of the month, menstruation, tides, and the rhythms that govern women's lives and ocean navigation.

Hina's most famous Hawaiian myth tells of her ascent to the moon. Tired of her life on earth — weary of her husband's cruelty or the endless labor of tapa-making — Hina climbed a rainbow to reach the sun. Finding the sun too hot, she climbed higher to the moon, where the climate was cooler and more pleasant. She took her tapa-making tools with her and now lives eternally on the moon, where she continues to beat tapa. The marks visible on the moon's surface are said to be the patterns of her tapa cloth or the banyan tree under which she works.

In Māori and broader Eastern Polynesian tradition, Hina (sometimes called Hine, Sina, or Ina) is closely associated with Maui. In some traditions, she is Maui's mother — the goddess Taranga who wrapped the infant Maui in her topknot and cast him into the sea. In others, she is Maui's wife or sister. In the Marquesas, Hina is the mother of Maui-tikitiki and is deeply associated with the ocean. Across these variations, the connection between Hina and Maui forms a constellation of myths about the trickster-hero and his relationship to the feminine divine.

Hina is also associated with feminine arts and industries. In Tahiti, she is the patroness of tapa-making — the art of beating mulberry bark into cloth, an essential women's craft across Polynesia. The sounds of tapa beaters are said to echo Hina's own creative work. She is connected to coral reefs — the living structures built by tiny creatures over millennia, mirroring the patient, cumulative nature of women's work.

In modern Polynesian culture, Hina has experienced a revival as a symbol of feminine power and Indigenous cultural identity. She is increasingly invoked alongside Pele and other female deities in cultural reclamation movements, Hawaiian sovereignty discourse, and artistic expressions that celebrate traditional women's arts and knowledge. The Hōkūle'a — the famous Polynesian voyaging canoe that has sailed across the Pacific using traditional navigation techniques — regularly invokes Hina for guidance during voyages, acknowledging the moon's crucial role in oceanic navigation.

Form

Hina is associated with the moon itself. In Hawaiian tradition, she is 'the woman in the moon' — Hina-i-ka-malama — a beautiful woman who dwells on the lunar surface, visible in the dark markings of the full moon. She is depicted as a graceful, luminous figure, often shown with her implements of tapa (barkcloth) making: the anvil (kua) and beater (i'e). In some visual traditions, she holds a calabash (water gourd) and a rainbow (ānuenue), which she used to climb to the moon. Her hair is long and flowing, sometimes associated with the moonbeams themselves. In Tahitian and Māori tradition, she is a woman of great beauty who can transform or be transformed — her appearance shifting between human form and the luminous presence of moonlight on water. Her lunar association connects her to the cycles of the month, menstruation, tides, and rhythms governing women's lives and ocean navigation.

Powers

Lunar Embodiment and Celestial Dwelling Hina's defining power is her connection to the moon — she is the woman in the moon, who climbed a rainbow to escape her earthly life and now dwells eternally on the lunar surface. Her presence in the moon connects her to the rhythms of the month, the female menstrual cycle, the tides, and the patterns of planting and fishing that follow the lunar calendar. In some traditions, Hina is not just in the moon but is the moon — the celestial body is her physical form, and its phases represent her moods and activities. Hawaiian and Tahitian traditions Hina's lunar home is permanent — she cannot return to the earth. Her power is tied to the moon's cycles; during the new moon, her influence is at its lowest. In some traditions, Hina lives on the moon; in others, she governs the moon from elsewhere; in others still, the moon is merely her symbol rather than her dwelling.

Mastery of Women's Arts (Tapa-making, Weaving, Crafts) Hina is the divine patron of tapa (barkcloth) making, weaving, and all feminine creative crafts. She took her tapa-making tools with her to the moon and continues to beat tapa there eternally — the patterns visible on the lunar surface are said to be the marks of her work. In Hawaiian and Tahitian tradition, women invoke Hina when making tapa, asking for her blessing on their work. She is also associated with childbirth, healing, and the secret knowledge passed between women through generations. Hawaiian, Tahitian, and wider Polynesian traditions Hina's craft patronage is specifically feminine — in traditional Polynesian gender divisions, men's crafts (carving, canoe-making) are under the patronage of male gods. The specific crafts associated with Hina vary by island culture — tapa is primary in Hawai'i and Tahiti, while weaving and mat-making are more emphasized in other traditions.

Control of Tides and Ocean Rhythms As a lunar deity, Hina governs the tides — the daily and monthly rhythms of the ocean that are driven by the moon's gravitational pull. Polynesian navigators, fishermen, and coastal communities have always understood the deep connection between Hina (the moon), the tides, and ocean life. Hina's oceanic aspect extends to coral reefs (sometimes called 'the gardens of Hina'), the creatures that live in the shallows, and the rhythms of fishing that follow the lunar calendar. Polynesian maritime and navigational knowledge Hina's oceanic power is primarily in the intertidal zone and shallow waters — the deep ocean is more associated with Tangaroa (or Kanaloa in Hawaiian tradition). The division between Hina's lunar-oceanic domain and Tangaroa's deep-sea domain is not rigid — in some traditions, Hina and Tangaroa are connected or even consorts.

Shape-shifting and Transformation Hina is associated with transformation — she climbed a rainbow, a physical manifestation of light and water, to reach the moon; she can appear as a beautiful woman, a luminous figure, or the moon itself. In some traditions, Hina transforms into various sea creatures or natural phenomena. Her connection to tapa-making — the transformation of raw bark into beautiful cloth — is itself a form of shape-shifting: the metamorphosis of raw nature into culture. Various Polynesian traditions Hina's transformations are generally between human form, lunar presence, and natural phenomena — she does not typically shapeshift into animals in the manner of trickster gods like Maui. The extent and nature of Hina's transformative abilities vary widely across traditions.

Maternal and Fertility Power In many traditions, Hina is the mother of Maui — the demigod trickster and culture hero. She wrapped the infant Maui in her topknot and cast him into the sea, where he survived and grew to become the great hero. Her maternal power is paradoxical: she both abandons and protects, casting Maui into danger and yet ensuring his survival. In her aspect as Hina-te-iwaiwa, she is a goddess of childbirth, fertility, and the protection of mothers and infants. Māori and wider Polynesian traditions Hina's maternal power does not prevent Maui's eventual death — she cannot grant immortality or protect her children from fate. In some traditions, Hina is not Maui's mother but his wife, his sister, or unrelated. The relationship varies dramatically across cultures.

Behavior & Habitat

Hina embodies the feminine creative principle — not the passive femininity of some traditions but an active, transformative creativity. In her most famous myth, she chooses to leave an unsatisfying earthly life, climbing a rainbow to reach the moon and making a new home for herself in the sky. This is not victimhood but agency — Hina decides her own fate. Her behavior is characterized by industrious craft (endless tapa-making on the moon), connection to the rhythms of nature (tides, lunar cycles), and a certain restlessness or yearning for something beyond the mundane. In her role as Maui's mother, she is protective and nurturing; as a figure associated with the ocean, she can be dangerous and transformative — the sea that gives and takes. Her multiplicity across Polynesian cultures reflects a goddess comfortable with transformation and able to manifest in many forms.

Accounts

Hina lived on earth with her husband, but she was deeply unhappy. Her husband treated her cruelly, and the endless labor of tapa-making — beating barkcloth from morning to night — wore her down. One day, tired of her suffering, Hina looked up at the rainbow arching across the sky and decided to leave. Carrying her tapa-making tools — her anvil (kua) and beater (i'e) — she climbed the rainbow. She first tried to reach the sun, but found it too hot and blinding. She climbed higher, to the moon, where the light was cool and silver and pleasant. There she made her home. She continues to beat tapa on the moon to this day — the dark markings visible on the full moon's face are the patterns of her barkcloth or the banyan tree under which she works. Hina-i-ka-malama — Hina in the Moon — is a symbol of escape from suffering, creative industry, and the choice to seek a better life.

In Māori tradition, Hina (sometimes called Taranga) gave birth to Maui prematurely. Believing him stillborn, she wrapped the infant in her topknot (tikitiki) and cast him into the sea. But Maui did not die — the ocean spirits and his divine ancestors protected him, and he floated safely to shore. He was found and raised by his ancestor Tama-nui-ki-te-rangi (or, in some versions, by the gods). When Maui grew up, he sought out his mother Hina and was reunited with her. The topknot in which he was wrapped gave him one of his epithets: Māui-tikitiki-a-Taranga — Maui of the Topknot of Taranga (Hina). Hina's casting of Maui into the sea — an apparent abandonment that was actually a protection — is a defining moment in both their myths.

In Samoan tradition, Sina (cognate of Hina) was a beautiful young woman who befriended an eel. The eel fell in love with Sina, but she was frightened by his advances. When the eel was dying, he asked Sina to bury his head. From the buried head grew the first coconut tree. The coconut itself bears Sina's likeness: the three 'eyes' of the coconut are said to be Sina's face — two eyes and a mouth. When you drink from a coconut, you are kissing Sina. This story connects Hina/Sina to the origin of one of the most important food and resource plants in the Pacific, linking her lunar and feminine aspects to agricultural fertility.

In some traditions, Hina was the first to make tapa (barkcloth). She discovered the process of stripping bark from the paper mulberry tree, soaking it in water, and beating it with wooden mallets until it became soft, pliable cloth. She taught this art to human women, passing on the secrets of pattern-making, dyeing, and the sacred songs (mele) that accompany tapa production. Hina's tapa-making on the moon is the eternal continuation of this first creative act. The patterns on the moon — visible to all — are her ongoing gift to humanity: a reminder that the moon's light is also the light of feminine creativity and craft.

Scholarly Debates
One Hina or Many?
There is a single pan-Polynesian Hina goddess whose attributes vary locally but who represents a fundamental lunar-creative feminine archetype common to all Polynesian cultures. ⇌ 'Hina' is a name applied to multiple distinct goddesses across Polynesia who happen to share a name but represent different divine figures with different origin stories, relationships, and attributes.

What We Don't Know

The specific rituals and ceremonies associated with Hina worship across Polynesia — lunar ceremonies, tapa-making rituals, and women's healing practices — are incompletely documented due to colonial disruption and the gender of early ethnographers (mostly male, with limited access to women's sacred spaces and knowledge). The relationship between the multiple Hina figures across Polynesia requires further comparative study, particularly the historical development of the Hina archetype from Proto-Polynesian origins. The oral nature of the traditions means significant local knowledge — especially women's knowledge — was never recorded and may be lost. The revival of tapa-making and women's traditional arts in contemporary Polynesia is reconnecting with Hina's legacy in ways that are not yet comprehensively documented. Direct archaeological evidence for Hina worship is minimal.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Hina as the ultimate example of 'leaving to find yourself.' Her life on earth was unbearable — cruel partner, endless work, no appreciation. She didn't fight. She didn't negotiate. She looked at a rainbow and thought: 'That's my exit.' She packed her tools (she was always practical), climbed into the sky, and set up shop on the moon. Now she's up there, doing what she loves — creating, making, beating tapa in eternal, beautiful rhythm. The patterns on the moon aren't scars or craters — they're her artwork, visible to everyone on Earth. She's not hiding; she's thriving. And she's not alone — she's the patron of every woman who has ever made something beautiful from raw materials, every person who has ever climbed out of a bad situation, every creative soul who works through the night while the rest of the world sleeps. Hina is proof that sometimes the best thing you can do with a bad situation is leave it behind — and make something more beautiful with what you carry.
