Who It Is

Hina is not one goddess but a family of them — a name that rippled across the Pacific as Polynesian voyagers carried it from island to island, reshaping it into new forms with every landfall. In Aotearoa, she becomes the terrified maiden Hine-tītama who flees her father-husband into the underworld and emerges as Hine-nui-te-pō, the implacable goddess of night and death, her obsidian teeth the last thing Māui ever saw when he tried to conquer mortality itself. In Samoa, she is Sina, the beautiful woman whose lost love becomes the first coconut tree, its three eyes watching across centuries. In Hawaii, she is Hina-puku-iʻa, provider of seafood, and Hina-ʻopu-hala-koʻa, mother of reef life. Everywhere, she is powerful, generative, and dangerous — a goddess who does not fit into one slot but keeps escaping the categories modern comparativists try to impose on her.

Hina's personality shifts dramatically across her mythic transformations. As Hine-tītama, she is loving and unsuspecting, marrying Tāne Mahuta without knowing he is her father. Upon discovering the truth, she flees to the underworld in shame and wrath, becoming the cold, implacable goddess Hine-nui-te-pō — she who shepherds souls into death and refuses to release them. In the Māui death episode, she is sleeping but not vulnerable; her destruction of Māui is an act of preservation (she prevents a mortal from violating the natural order). Powers
Goddess of Night and Death (Hine-nui-te-pō)

In her transformed identity, Hine-nui-te-pō receives the spirits of the dead (wairua) into the first level of Rarohenga, the Māori underworld, preparing them for their next stage of journey. She holds authority over the boundary between life and death and determines the fate of souls.

Limit: Hine-nui-te-pō does not grant immortality or release souls back to the living world. Her domain is one-way; Māui's attempt to reverse death fails.

Lunar Association

Across multiple Polynesian cultures, Hina is linked to the moon. In Hawaiian tradition, Hina is sometimes identified with the moon goddess (Hanaiakamalama). In Māori, the connection is implicit in her pale luminosity and her association with night. The moon governs tides, seasons, menstruation, and planting cycles — all of which Hina's various identities touch.

Limit: The lunar association is not uniformly attested across all Hina traditions; some iwi or island groups emphasize other domains.

Magic Chant and Incantation (Hinauri)

In the Hinauri narrative, she chants incantations (karakia) that kill Tinirau's other wives who attempt to murder her. This establishes Hina as a figure with knowledge of lethal ritual speech — a power associated with priestly or chiefly women in Māori tradition.

Limit: The specific chants are not recorded in the surviving sources; only their effects are described.

Origin Power (Sina)

In Samoan mythology, Sina's encounter with an eel (Tuna) results in the birth of the first coconut tree. This is not a combat power but a generative one: her sadness and the eel's dying request produce a plant that becomes central to Samoan and broader Polynesian subsistence, architecture, and ritual.

Limit: The origin of the coconut is only one version of a larger set of Samoan plant-origin myths.

Accounts I
The Transformation of Hine-tītama into Hine-nui-te-pōMāori oral tradition; recorded by Grey 1856/1956; Orbell 1998

Pre-European oral; first written record 1856

Hine-tītama, a beautiful maiden, marries Tāne Mahuta, the god of forests, without knowing he is her father. When she discovers the truth, she is overwhelmed by shame and rage. She flees the world of light and descends into the underworld, Rarohenga. There she transforms into Hine-nui-te-pō — 'the great woman of the night' — and assumes her new role as goddess of death and guardian of souls. Tāne pursues her, but she rebukes him: 'Go back, Tāne, and care for our children in the world of light. I will stay here and receive those who come to the darkness.' This is the mythic origin of death as a permanent separation between the living and the dead, and it explains why human beings must die rather than live forever. Death enters the human condition as an irreversible boundary. Hine-nui-te-pō becomes the unavoidable endpoint of every human life, and the underworld Rarohenga becomes the destination of all wairua (souls). The myth also establishes gendered roles in the cosmos: Tāne remains in the world of light and forest; Hine-nui-te-pō governs the night and the dead.

The Death of Māui in Hine-nui-te-pō's VaginaMāori oral tradition; Grey 1856/1956; Orbell 1998

Pre-European oral

After Māui achieves his great exploits — fishing up the North Island, slowing the sun, and giving fire to humanity — he learns that Hine-nui-te-pō holds the secret of human immortality. Determined to reverse death, Māui transforms himself into a worm and crawls into Hine-nui-te-pō's vagina while she sleeps, planning to exit through her mouth and thus 'kill death' by reversing the direction of birth. His companion, the small bird Tītīwai (or in some versions, small birds who laugh at him), warns him to turn back, but Māui insists. As he emerges, Hine-nui-te-pō awakens, and in her rage she crushes him with her obsidian (or pounamu) vaginal teeth. Māui dies, and humanity remains mortal. Some versions add that his head escapes and becomes the first salmon or the first to enter the underworld. Mortality is permanently established. Māui's death is the price of his attempt to overstep human limits. The episode is often cited as the origin of death's inevitability in Māori thought.

Accounts II
Sina and the Eel: The Origin of the CoconutSamoan oral tradition; collected by Westervelt, Beckwith, and others

Pre-European oral

Sina, a beautiful woman, goes to bathe in a pool. An eel (Tuna) admires her and follows her. In some versions, the eel is a god or a transformed man who loves Sina. She rejects him or, in some variants, befriends him. When he dies or is killed, his head is buried, and from it grows the first coconut tree. The coconut's three eyes (the two circular scars and the sprouting eye) represent the eel's face: the two eyes are his watching eyes, and the sprouting eye is his mouth. This myth explains the coconut as a living memorial to a supernatural love. The legend is known throughout Polynesia, including in Māori versions where Sina is conflated with Hina. The coconut tree becomes a foundational resource for food, drink, oil, rope, and construction across the Pacific. The myth encodes the relationship between humans, animals, and plants in Polynesian ecological thought.

Hinauri and the Dog-IrawaruMāori oral tradition; Grey 1856/1956

Pre-European oral

Hinauri, a sister of Māui, is married to Irawaru. Māui becomes annoyed with Irawaru and uses his magical powers to stretch his limbs, turning him into a dog. When Hinauri asks Māui if he has seen her husband, Māui tells her to call 'Moi! Moi!' — the dog's new name. The dog runs to her, and Hinauri realizes what Māui has done. Overwhelmed with grief and rage, she throws herself into the sea. She drifts until she washes ashore at Wairarawa, where the brothers Ihuatamai and Ihuwareware find her and restore her. She becomes their wife and conceives a child. Later, she is taken by the chief Tinirau but mistreated by his other wives, who attempt to kill her, so she chants incantations which kill them. Her brother Māuimua, also known as Rupe, comes searching for her and finds her on the day she gives birth, whereupon he flies her and her baby up to heaven to live with Rehua, the star-god. Hinauri's transformation from victim to powerful ritual practitioner shows the theological role of women's karakia in Māori tradition. Hina is the Māori equivalent of the Greek Selene or Roman Diana. — While Hina has lunar associations, reducing her to a 'moon goddess' obscures her broader roles as ancestress, provider, goddess of death, and originator of the coconut in different Polynesian cultures. She is not a single-purpose deity and should not be flattened into a comparative-mythology slot.

Hina, Sina, and Ina are different goddesses. — They are cognate name-forms of the same Proto-Polynesian deity root. The variations are primarily orthographic and dialectal, not evidence of entirely separate figures.

Māui killed Hine-nui-te-pō and brought back immortality. — In the standard Māori tradition, Māui dies in Hine-nui-te-pō's vagina and immortality is NOT brought back. A few rare variants give Māui a partial success, but the dominant narrative is that death remains irreversible.
