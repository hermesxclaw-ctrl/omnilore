Who It Is

In the vast casualty lists of the Trojan War, Homer pauses for a single breath to name two fallen warriors — Aesepus and Pedasus — and in that breath, a mother emerges from the fog of myth: Alexirrhoe, 'she who wards off the stream.' She has no grand temples, no surviving hymns, no epic of her own. Her entire existence in the ancient record is a footnote to a war story, a genealogical whisper preserved in the margins of scholarship. Yet her name carries a promise of protection against the flood — a defensive power that, as far as we know, she never got to use.

No specific behaviors or personality traits are recorded. Her sole mythological role is genealogical: she is the mother of two heroes, Aesepus and Pedasus, who appear in Homer's Iliad as Trojan allies. Her name suggests a protective or defensive function, possibly as a local tutelary deity of a river or spring.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Greek ἀλέξω (alexō, 'to ward off, defend') and ῥοή (rhoē, 'flow, stream'). The name likely means 'she who wards off the flow' or 'defender against the stream' — possibly a reference to stemming a flood or, more figuratively, averting a disaster or plague.

Pronunciation — ah-LEX-ir-oh-ee (English approximation); a-lek-SIR-ro-ay (reconstructed Ancient Greek)

Aliases — Alexirhoe, Alexirrhoë

Ownership — Greek mythology / ancient Greek religion; obscure figure likely from regional cult or genealogical tradition

Warding/Protection (inferred from name)

The element 'alex-' in her name suggests an apotropaic function — the ability to ward off evil, disease, or disaster. However, this is etymological inference, not attested myth.

Limit: No myth survives demonstrating this power in action.

Motherhood of Heroes

As the mother of Aesepus and Pedasus, she is part of the heroic genealogical network that connects the Trojan War narrative to local river traditions.

Limit: Her sons are minor figures in the Iliad, killed by Euryalus and Patroclus respectively.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No physical description survives in any ancient source. As a nymph or heroine associated with rivers, she would conventionally be imagined as a beautiful young woman, possibly with watery attributes — flowing hair, damp robes, a water jug — but this is pure artistic convention, not attested detail.

Symbols — No symbols are attested. By convention, nymphs of rivers might be associated with water vessels, reeds, or fish, but nothing specific to Alexirrhoe survives.

Domains — Likely a Naiad (freshwater nymph) or local heroine associated with a river in the Troad (northwestern Anatolia) or the Peloponnese. The river god Abas (or the river Aesepus itself) is given as her consort, placing her firmly in the realm of freshwater sources.

Accounts I
The Genealogy of Two Fallen HeroesPseudo-Plutarch, De Fluviis; Scholia on Iliad

Recorded in Roman-era paradoxography; reflecting earlier Hellenistic scholarship

Alexirrhoe, a nymph, unites with the river god Abas (or the river Aesepus) and bears two sons: Aesepus and Pedasus. These sons grow to become warriors on the Trojan side in the Trojan War. In the Iliad, Aesepus is killed by Euryalus (son of Mecisteus), and Pedasus is killed by Patroclus (or in some readings, by Euryalus as well). Their deaths are brief, poignant moments in the vast catalogue of war casualties — names that Homer lists to evoke the human cost of the conflict. The brief mention of these heroes serves to root the Trojan War in local Anatolian geography, connecting the epic narrative to specific rivers and regions. Alexirrhoe herself disappears from the narrative after her genealogical function is fulfilled.

Accounts II
The Genealogy of the Trojan River-HeroesPseudo-Plutarch, De Fluviis; Scholia on Homer's Iliad; Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca

Hellenistic to Roman Imperial period

According to the genealogical tradition preserved in the paradoxographical work De Fluviis and in the Homeric scholia, Alexirrhoe was the mother of twin sons—Aesepus and Pedasus—who fought and died at Troy. Their father was either the river-god Abas or, in a more complex reading, the river-god Aesepus himself, making Alexirrhoe the grandmother of warriors who bore her husband's name. This genealogical tangle is characteristic of Greek mythography, where nymphs and river-gods were endlessly combined and recombined to generate heroic lineages. The twins Aesepus and Pedasus appear in Book 6 of the Iliad, where they are slain by the Greek hero Euryalus. Homer himself does not name their mother, leaving that detail to the later mythographic tradition that connected Alexirrhoe to the Troad. The scholarly debate centers on whether Alexirrhoe was ever an independently worshipped figure or whether she was invented by Hellenistic mythographers precisely to fill the genealogical gap left by Homer's silence.
