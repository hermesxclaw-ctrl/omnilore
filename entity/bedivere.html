Who It Is

Sir Bedivere (Bedwyr in Welsh) is one of the earliest and most enduring of Arthur's knights — a companion who appears in the earliest Welsh sources and remains at Arthur's side through the final battle of Camlann. In the Welsh tale Culhwch and Olwen (c. 1100), Bedwyr is described as the handsomest man in Britain (except for Arthur) and the fastest runner, wielding a magic lance whose strike is deadly. His name means something like 'Birch-man' (bedw = birch), connecting him to one of the sacred trees of Celtic tradition.

Bedivere's defining role in the Arthurian canon is as the knight who returns Excalibur to the Lady of the Lake. In Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur — and in Tennyson's iconic poetic treatment — the mortally wounded Arthur commands Bedivere to take Excalibur and throw it into the lake. Bedivere, awed by the sword's beauty (its pommel and hilt blazing with jewels) and reasoning that it should be preserved as a relic, twice lies to Arthur, claiming he threw the sword while hiding it. Arthur, who can see through the deception, rebukes him harshly: 'Traitor! Twice you have failed me!' On the third attempt, Bedivere throws Excalibur into the water, and an arm 'clothed in white samite' rises to catch it, brandishes it three times, and sinks back into the lake.

After Excalibur is returned, Bedivere carries the dying Arthur to the waterside, where a barge appears carrying three queens (including Morgan le Fay) and other ladies in black hoods. Arthur is placed in the barge and borne away to Avalon. Bedivere is left alone on the shore, the last knight of the Round Table, watching his king disappear into the mist. He later finds a hermitage where a freshly dug grave holds a body — whether Arthur's or another's, Malory leaves ambiguous.

Bedivere's loyalty is his defining trait. Unlike Lancelot, he has no grand love affair; unlike Gawain, he has no solar power or famous adventure; unlike Galahad or Percival, he is not a Grail knight. He is the reliable one, the steady one, the knight who is simply there at the end. His survival of Camlann — and his role as the sole witness to Arthur's departure — gives him a unique position in the legend. He is the keeper of the story, the one who can say: 'I was there. I saw the arm catch the sword. I saw the barge take the king.'

In the Welsh tradition, Bedwyr's death is recorded in the Englynion y Beddau ('Stanzas of the Graves'): he is buried at Tryfan, a mountain in Snowdonia. The Welsh Bedwyr is missing a hand ('Bedwyr of the Perfect Sinew, though he was one-handed, no three warriors drew blood in the same field faster than he'), a detail that connects him to the Irish king Nuada of the Silver Hand and suggests a deep mythological substratum beneath the chivalric surface.
