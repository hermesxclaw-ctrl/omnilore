Who It Is

On a rain-soaked roadside in the Japanese countryside, where the myth of the gentle monk folds into the dark history of a nation's infant dead, stands a small stone figure in a red bib — Jizō, the bodhisattva who swore he would not become a Buddha until every last soul in hell was freed. He is at once the softest and the most terrifying figure in East Asian religion: the kindly saviour who gathers aborted fetuses and dead children into his robes away from the demons that would torment them, and the eternal guardian whose vaulted vow chains him to the underworld until the end of time. From a Brahmin maiden weeping for a slandering mother to the Chinese prince whose incorrupt body sits on a sacred mountain, from the video-game power-up of a million Mario players to the silent statue that watches a lost child in My Neighbor Totoro, Jizō walks the line between salvation and a grief so total

Kṣitigarbha is defined by his vows: to teach all beings in the six worlds between the parinirvāṇa of Śākyamuni Buddha and the arising of Maitreya, and, most famously, not to attain Buddhahood until all hells are emptied. He is the bodhisattva of hell-beings, saviour of the damned, protector of children (including deceased and unborn), guardians of travellers, pilgrims and fire-fighters. In the Sutra he embodies boundless filial piety and compassionate endurance: as the Brahmin maiden Sacred Girl he is deeply troubled by his mother's slander of the Three Jewels and works to redeem her.

Powers
Empty-the-Hells Vow of salvation

The defining Kṣitigarbha power/vow: he refuses Buddhahood until every hell is emptied. He can enter all the hells at will, force open their gates with his staff, and retrieve or save beings condemned there, guiding them through the Six Paths of rebirth.

Limit: The vow itself is the limit — he remains a bodhisattva (not a Buddha) and continues in saṃsāra until the hells are empty, at which point he would attain Buddhahood.

Gate-forcing shakujō (ringed staff)

His monk's staff, topped with rings, forces open the gates of hell and — as the attribute of an itinerant monk — alerts insects and small animals of his approach so he does not accidentally crush them, embodying his universal compassion.

Limit: Portrayed iconographically rather than as a narrative force; its hell-opening power is symbolic and doctrinal.

Cintāmaṇi wish-granting jewel

His tear-shaped jewel (nyoi hōju / rúyì bǎozhū) grants wishes and lights up the darkness, symbolising enlightenment: it illuminates the dark hell-realms and grants the mundane and spiritual wishes of devotees (a core reason the Lotus Sutra was copied in Jizō worship).

Limit: Symbolises and grants enlightenment/devotion-driven wishes, not literal omnipotence.

Incarnation & awakened body (illustrated by Kim Gyo-gak)

The historical Silla prince Kim Gyo-gak, who became the monk Dizang on Mount Jiuhua, meditated for decades, survived the scorpion-sting of the mountain goddess, expanded his kasaya (monastic robe) supernaturally to cover the entire mountain when flung into the air, and after his death at 99 his body was found not to have decayed — evidence taken as proof he was an incarnation (nirmāṇakāya) of Dizang. The preserved, dehydrated body is still venerated at the Shrine of the Living Buddha on Mount Jiuhua.

Limit: Legendary or hagiographic in the face, not narrated in the sutra; the incorrupt-body detail is a mark of sanctity in the Chinese tradition.

Rokudō (Six Ways) manifestation & blue-lotus radiance

As 'Lord of the Six Ways' he manifests six rays/beams to each of the Six Paths, and in the Vajrayāna and contemplation texts he emits a 'blue lotus light illuminating the ten directions' — a radiant salvific luminosity associated with the vajra family.

Limit: Symbolism of the desire-realm cosmology.

Saviourship of dead children (mizuko & Sai no Kawara)

Jizō is believed to protect and rescue the souls of dead children, who at Sai no Kawara on the Sanzu River are condemned to endlessly stack stone piles that demons (oni) knock down. In Lafcadio Hearn's later version the oni torment the children, who hide refuge in Jizō's robes; Jizō also shortens the children's suffering, and parents pile stones or leave toys at his statues to lessen their children's time in the underworld.

Limit: A folk/vernacular development (children's limbo appears in the Muromachi period), layered onto the canonical bodhisattva rather than in the sutra.

Accounts I
The Brahmin Maiden Sacred GirlKṣitigarbha Bodhisattva Pūrvapraṇidhāna Sūtra

Translated 695-700 CE; origin debated

In a distant past eon, Kṣitigarbha was a Brahmin-caste maiden named Sacred Girl, who was deeply troubled by the death of her mother — a woman who had repeatedly slandered the Three Jewels. Moved by filial grief and fear for her mother's fate, the maiden made a great vow to save all sentient beings. Her compassion was so vast that she vowed not to rest until every being in the six worlds was delivered, and her vow became the basis of Kṣitigarbha's eternal mission. The Buddha recounts this to the beings of the Trāyastriṃśa Heaven as an act of gratitude for his own mother Māyā. Establishes the vow-to-empty-the-hells and the filial-piety frame that defines Kṣitigarbha's cult.

Kim Gyo-gak and Mount JiuhuaJiuhua shan Huacheng si ji (Fei Guanqing, 9th c.) and Song gaoseng zhuan (Zanning)

Tang dynasty (8th century CE for the events); 9th-10th c. records

A former prince of Silla (Korea), Kim Gyo-gak, came to Tang China, took the monastic name Dizang ('Kṣitigarbha'), and climbed Mount Jiuhua in Anhui where he built a hut and cultivated the dharma in deep seclusion. The goddess of the mountain, testing him, transformed into a scorpion and stung him; he remained unmoved and continued his practice. Touched by his perseverance, she manifested and apologised, giving him an antidote and spring water. Later the nobleman Zhuge, discovering the starving, long-haired monk, had a temple built for him with the land donated by the wealthy Minrang — land that, when Jijang flung his kasaya into the air, supernaturally expanded to cover the whole mountain. Minrang renounced the mountain as Jijang's protector; his son became a monk. Jijang lived on Jiuhua 75 years and died aged 99; three years later his body was found incorrupt, and devotees concluded he was an incarnation of Dizang. Established Mount Jiuhua as one of the Four Sacred Mountains of Chinese Buddhism and made it Dizang's bodhimaṇḍa, where the incorrupt-body shrine is still venerated.

Accounts II
Fujiwara Hirotari's descent and returnRyōki (c. 822)

Early Heian (c. 822)

After Fujiwara Hirotari dies, he catches sight of his wife suffering in the Land After Death. With the aid of Jizō he is able to return to life and, by copying the Lotus Sutra, lifts the burden of suffering from his wife. The tale, in the earliest known setsuwa collection, anchors Jizō's early Japanese role as a hell-realm rescuer whose devotees' Lotus-Sutra recitation delivers relief to the dead. One of the foundational Japanese miracle-tales tying Jizō to the Lotus Sutra and post-mortem release.

The Agonashi Jizō (Jizō without a Jaw)local Oki Island legend; Gabi Greve's documenting tradition

Event dated to 1870 (temple transfer); legend older

A man in Kanawa in Omiya town on Oki Island suffered a terrible toothache; after three days of agony he pulled out his own jaw, threw it away, and died — but was reborn as a bodhisattva. The pious islanders carved a wooden statue of a Jizō without a chin (Agonashi Jizō, 'Jizō without a Jaw'; also Shitsu Heiyu, 'Jizō who Heals Toothaches'), reportedly made by the Heian poet and scholar Ono no Takamura (802-853). Sufferers of toothache pray before the statue; in gratitude they place a nashi (pear) into a nearby river, lake, or the sea — a pun on nashi meaning both 'pear' and 'without' (i.e., 'to not have a toothache'). The statue, transferred to Hagi Temple in Osaka in 1870 when its original temple closed, is a hibutsu (secret) image shown publicly only once a year. An exemplar of Jizō's folk-assimilative healing powers, blending bodily-affliction relief with wordplay-ritual.

The Sai no Kawara children's limbo (wasan and pilgrimage)Fuji no hitoana no sōshi (c. 1600); Jizō wasan; Lafcadio Hearn; folk tradition

Concept attributed to priest Kūya (10th c.); connected to wasan by the 17th c.; limbo tale in Otogizōshi from Muromachi

Dead children are gathered at Sai no Kawara, the dry riverbed-bank of the Sanzu River, where they are condemned to stack piles of small stones to build merit. Each time they near completion, the piles are knocked down — in the earliest form by winds and flames that also reduce the children to cremated bones (from which Jizō revives them), and in Lafcadio Hearn's later version by tormenting demons, with the children taking refuge in Jizō's robes. Jizō is the children's protector at the limbo, and this-worldly sites — above all Osorezan in Aomori — were identified as the 'Sai no Kawara', where pilgrims come to commune with dead children. Created the emotional core of Jizō's child-protector cult and the mizuko-kyō's Sai-no-Kawara geography, massively shaping modern parental mourning practice.
