Shapash.

Who It Is

Shapash.

Form

No surviving Ugaritic depiction of Shapash has been definitively identified. She is described in texts as 'the lamp of the gods' (nrt ilm), suggesting a radiant, illuminating presence. She is not described in anthropomorphic detail in the surviving texts; her iconography may have been solar (a winged sun disc, a radiant orb), but this is reconstructed, not attested. In modern artistic imagination, she is often depicted as a winged woman bearing a sun-disc or a torch, but this is a modern interpretation, not an ancient attestation.

Powers

Solar illumination (Light of the World) Shapash is the sun itself, the 'lamp of the gods' (nrt ilm). She illuminates the earth, the heavens, and the underworld. Her light is not merely physical but also metaphorical: she reveals truth, exposes wrongdoing, and brings clarity. Ugaritic (Baal Cycle, ritual texts) She must descend into the underworld each night, suggesting her light is not constant in the mortal realm. None within the Ugaritic corpus.

Psychopomp (Guide of the Dead) Shapash travels daily to the underworld and returns. She knows the paths of the dead and can guide souls. In the Baal Cycle, she descends to the underworld to search for Baal's body and later to confront Mot. She is the one who can move between the world of the living and the dead, making her a crucial intermediary. Ugaritic (Baal Cycle, KTU 1.6) She does not have power over the dead themselves (that is Mot's domain), but she can navigate the underworld and intercede. Her role as a psychopomp is sometimes conflated with the Mesopotamian sun god Shamash's role as a judge of the dead, but the Ugaritic texts do not give her a judicial role in the underworld.

Oath-Witness and Punisher of Perjury Shapash is invoked as a witness to oaths. In the Epic of Aqhat, she is called upon to curse the murderer Yatpan, and her curse is effective. She is a guarantor of cosmic and social order. Ugaritic (Epic of Aqhat, KTU 1.19 IV) Her curse is potent but not instantaneous; she is a witness and enforcer, not a direct agent of vengeance. None attested.

Healing In the Epic of Kirta, Shapash is invoked or involved in the healing of the ailing king Kirta. She is associated with restoration and well-being. Ugaritic (Epic of Kirta, KTU 1.16) The exact mechanics of her healing are not detailed; she appears as an intercessor or a source of restorative power. None attested.

Divine Messenger and Mediator In the Baal Cycle, Shapash acts as a messenger and mediator between the gods, carrying messages and interceding on behalf of Baal. She is the one who ultimately convinces Mot to release Baal, restoring cosmic order. Ugaritic (Baal Cycle, KTU 1.6 V–VI) She is not the supreme deity; she acts within the divine hierarchy, deferring to El (the chief god). None attested.

Solar illumination of both worlds Shapshu lights the living world by day and the underworld at night. The closing hymn of the Baal Cycle situates the gods and the rpum (semi-divine ancestral figures) 'under' Shapshu, likely while she illuminates the world of the living and the realm of the dead in turn. Anat asks her to burn bright (ʾišḫn) to illuminate the underworld while they retrieve Baal's corpse. Ugaritic Baal Cycle, KTU 1.6; closing hymn KTU 1.6 iv 45–54 Her illumination of the dead realm is tied to her daily circuit; she is a passive luminary more than an active combatant. No text attributes offensive power to her. None attested; the boundary-crossing is the consistent, central feature.

Messenger and mediator of the high god El Shapshu is called the 'royal messenger' of El, her probable father. She carries El's decisions across the pantheon — announcing Yam's accession to Athtar (KTU 1.2 iii), searching for Baal at El's command (KTU 1.6 iii), and warning Mot that El supports Baal's kingship, ending the final conflict (KTU 1.6 vi). Ugaritic Baal Cycle, esp. KTU 1.2 iii, KTU 1.6 iii and vi She transmits El's authority rather than legislating herself, though Mot's fear of 'offending Shapshu' shows her own standing commands respect. Baal Cycle's character list labels her 'the sun god' (masculine) in some translations while the narrative and most scholarship treat her as goddess — a translation/gender inconsistency rather than a doctrine.

Jurisdiction over the boundary of life and death Because her daily path crosses into the underworld, Shapshu uniquely mediates between the living and the dead. She helps Anat recover and bury the slain Baal, and El dispatches her to restore the missing god. She is invoked in funerary/ancestor contexts (the pgr epithet, the royal-accession rite KTU 1.161). Baal Cycle KTU 1.6; ritual KTU 1.161 She does not resurrect or resurrect autonomously; she locates, restores, and bridges. Baal's return depends on El's will and the cosmic order, not on Shapshu's direct power. Some scholars (Wiggins) have proposed she might be the wet-nurse of Keret's son in KTU 1.15 II 27, a maternal-solar reading that is a restoration and not certain.

Arbiter in protective magic / enchanter In incantation texts Shapshu acts as an arbitrator who summons and coordinates other gods against snakebite. In KTU 1.100 she is the one who visits ten deities in turn until Ḥoranu acts; in KTU 1.107 she inquires after a snake-bitten boy and is called to remove the venom; in KTU 1.82 she, with Baal and Anat, protects a victim; in 'Horon and the Mare' Kothar-wa-Khasis is among those she should summon to deal with snake venom. KTU 1.100, KTU 1.82, KTU 1.107; Pardee's 'Horon and the Mare' Her role is mobilising others (notably Ḥoranu, who gathers the healing plants) more than wielding the remedy herself; Ḥoranu is the active healer. None; her divinatory-oracular role (KTU 1.78) is related but separately attested.

Sovereign 'Eternal' solar queen Epithets rbt špš ('Great Lady Shapshu') and špš ʿlm ('Eternal Shapshu', Šapši ʿālami) mark her as a supreme, perpetual royal figure. In a letter to the king of Ugarit (KTU 2.42) she is named second only behind Baal in the invoked pantheon. Her name occurs in 66 Ugaritic personal names, the fourth most common divine element after Baal, El, and Resheph — the most popular of any goddess. KTU 2.42; offertory and onomastic corpora of Ugarit No temple of Shapshu is attested at Ugarit (unlike Baal and Dagon, which had temples), and she has no known consort — her worship was global/royal rather than shrine-anchored. Her lack of a temple and consort makes the extent of her cult hard to reconstruct; the onomastic evidence nonetheless proves genuine popularity.

Accounts

When El decides to give the kingship to the sea-god Yam, Shapshu brings the news to the star-god Athtar. She warns him that opposition to El's decision is useless and tries to prevent him from contesting the throne, acting as a voice of moderation (Page reads her role this way). When Athtar protests that he has no court and fears defeat, Shapshu suggests the reason may be his youth and want of a wife.

After Mot kills Baal, Anat finds her brother's body at the entrance to the underworld. Shapshu — who traverses that boundary on her nightly journey — helps Anat lift the corpse onto her shoulder so it can be carried to Mount Sapan for burial. Earlier, when Baal still lived, his messengers were told to reach Mot in the netherworld by joining Shapshu on her journey there. Baal, as he goes to meet Mot, is advised (in a speech likely given by Shapshu) to take a substitute in his image who will be slain in his stead, and she promises to bury his body.

El dreams that Baal lives and, through Anat, commands Shapshu to go and search for the god. Shapshu reassures Anat that she will find him and receives a blessing. When Baal and Mot later lock in a final, exhausting single combat on Mount Sapan, neither can prevail — until Shapshu intervenes, warning Mot that El has designated Baal as monarch. Mot, terrified at opposing El's will or offending Shapshu, concedes the kingship to Baal, ending the cycle. The text closes with a hymn honouring Shapshu.

A figure styled 'the mother of the stallion and the mare' (ủm.pḥl.pḥlt) calls on Shapshu — addressing her as 'mother', possibly honorifically — for help, because her children are in danger from snakebite. Shapshu acts as an arbitrator, visiting ten different deities in their dwellings until she reaches the fortress of the god Ḥoranu, who is the first to act: he gathers plants and defeats the serpents, saving the offspring. The incantation ends with a marriage between Ḥoranu and the mother, apparently on condition that he share the healing knowledge. A note on the tablet's edge calls it a 'spell against the bite of a snake'.

Scholarly Debates
Gender of the Ugaritic sun: goddess vs. 'sun god'
Shapshu is a female sun goddess (the overwhelming scholarly and primary-source position; epithets rbt, nrt ỉlm). ⇌ The Baal Cycle's character list and some translations refer to 'the sun god', and the Akkadian-equation Shamash/Utu is male.
Identification with Asherah/Athirat
Shapshu and Athirat may be the same deity, given shared rbt title, motherhood-of-gods role, and the new 'day' reading of ˀaṯrt ym. ⇌ They are separate goddesses; Athirat is the sea-queen and El's consort, Shapshu the solar messenger with no attested consort.
Shapshu vs. the Mesopotamian/South Arabian sun: which solar gender is original?
The female sun goddess is a Northwest Semitic feature, and Mesopotamia's male Shamash superseded an earlier female-sun tradition (Old Akkadian names suggest this). ⇌ The male sun is the Mesopotamian norm; the western female sun is a regional divergence with no claim to greater antiquity.
The closing hymn: what exactly is Shapshu's bond with Kothar-wa-Khasis?
Kothar is Shapshu's companion/friend. ⇌ Kothar-and-Hasis is her 'spellcaster/diviner' (a title), or the 'pilot' of her solar barque.
Attribution of Baal's substitute-advisers to Shapshu
The speaker who advises Baal to take a substitute and promises to bury him is Shapshu. ⇌ The damaged text may attribute the speech to another deity.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Shapash as the ultimate cosmic commuter. Every day, she clocks in, lights up the world, and then takes the night shift in the underworld, guiding the dead and keeping the forces of chaos in check. She's the divine equivalent of a lighthouse keeper, a search-and-rescue worker, and a supreme court justice all rolled into one. She doesn't smash mountains or slay sea monsters like Baal, but without her, Baal would have stayed dead, Mot would have won, and the world would have been plunged into eternal darkness. She's the unsung hero of the Ugaritic pantheon — the one who makes sure the lights stay on and the dead stay put.
