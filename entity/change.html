Who It Is

She is the most famous woman in the Chinese sky, a goddess of breathtaking beauty who has been a thief, a toad, a lonely empress, and a symbol of national pride. Her name is on the most ambitious spacecraft ever sent to the far side of the Moon, yet her story is one of the most heartbroken in all of mythology. She is Chang'e, and she drank the potion of eternal life, only to find that forever is a very long time to be alone.

Chang'e's behavior is primarily defined by her isolation and eternal solitude. She is a figure of profound loneliness, separated from her husband and all of humanity. She is often portrayed as a silent, melancholic, and contemplative figure, gazing down at the world she left behind. Her personality is ambiguous: she can be seen as a tragic heroine, a selfish thief, or a victim of fate. Powers
Immortality

The central power of her myth. After consuming the elixir of immortality, she gains eternal life and becomes a celestial being. This is not a natural state; it is a stolen power with a heavy price (eternal separation from her mortal life).

Limit: The immortality is permanent and cannot be undone. It confines her to the moon, effectively a beautiful prison. She is eternally separated from her husband and the mortal world.

Lunar Sovereignty

As the Moon Goddess, she has dominion over the moon and its inhabitants (the Jade Rabbit, Wu Gang, the Cassia Tree). She is the spirit of the moon itself in some interpretations.

Limit: Her power is largely passive. Her domain is a place of solitude, not a seat of active power. She is more of a presiding spirit than an active ruler.

Flight / Levitation

She is able to fly, as she fled to the moon on a moonbeam or by the power of the elixir. In art, she is often depicted floating or soaring through the sky.

Limit: This ability is tied to her consumption of the elixir and is not a general power she can bestow. It is a one-way trip.

Transformation

In some traditions, she is able to transform into a toad, or is transformed into one as a punishment.

Limit: This is often a curse or a consequence of her actions, not a freely used ability. It is a state of degradation.

Accounts I
The Theft of the ElixirGuicang (fragment), Huainanzi

Warring States to Han dynasty (c. 4th-1st century BCE)

The hero Houyi, a master archer, saved the world by shooting down nine of the ten suns that were scorching the earth. As a reward, he was given a potion of immortality by the Queen Mother of the West (Xi Wangmu). He did not drink it immediately, either because he wanted to stay with his wife, Chang'e, or because he was not yet worthy. Chang'e, for reasons that are not explained in the earliest sources, drank the potion herself and fled to the moon, where she has remained ever since. Chang'e is eternally separated from her husband and becomes the Moon Goddess. Houyi is left alone on earth. The myth explains the presence of a beautiful woman on the moon and provides a moral lesson about ambition, theft, and the consequences of seeking immortality.

Accounts II
The Lonely Moon PalaceYouyang Zazu (酉阳杂俎) and later folklore

Tang dynasty (618-907 CE) and later

This is the 'aftermath' story. Chang'e now lives in the Guanghan Gong, the 'Wide-Cold Palace,' on the moon. She is accompanied by the Jade Rabbit (Yutu), who endlessly pounds the elixir of immortality in a mortar. A man named Wu Gang, who was punished for his laziness or his desire for immortality, is forced to chop down a giant cassia tree that heals itself with every blow. Chang'e is surrounded by beauty but is utterly alone, gazing down at the world she has lost. This story establishes the moon as a place of eternal, melancholic labor and solitude. It creates the iconic imagery of the Moon Palace and its inhabitants, which dominates all later depictions of Chang'e.

The Mid-Autumn FestivalFolk tradition, documented from the Tang dynasty onwards

Tang dynasty (618-907 CE) to present

The myth of Chang'e is the central story of the Mid-Autumn Festival (Zhongqiu Jie). On the 15th day of the 8th lunar month, when the moon is at its fullest and brightest, families gather to admire the moon, eat mooncakes, and share Chang'e's story. The roundness of the moon and the mooncake symbolizes family reunion and completeness, which stands in poignant contrast to Chang'e's own eternal separation from her family. In some versions of the story, the mooncakes were used to hide secret messages to coordinate a rebellion against the Mongols during the Yuan dynasty, adding a historical layer to the myth. The myth becomes a living part of Chinese culture, celebrated annually. It transforms Chang'e from a mythic figure into a cultural icon of longing, reunion, and the bittersweet nature of beauty and immortality.

Chang'e was always a benevolent, loving goddess. — The earliest sources depict her as a thief who stole immortality from her husband. Her benevolent and heroic portrayal is a later, more modern development, popularized by the Mid-Autumn Festival and modern media.

The Jade Rabbit is a cute, magical pet. — The Jade Rabbit's role is to endlessly pound the elixir of immortality, a task that is often seen as a form of penance or eternal labor. It is a symbol of self-sacrifice and devotion, not just a cute companion.

The moon is a beautiful, peaceful place. — In the myth, the moon is a place of bitter, eternal cold (the 'Wide-Cold Palace') and endless, futile labor (Wu Gang chopping the cassia tree). It is a prison of solitude, not a paradise.

Chang'e is a unique, singular figure. — Her story is part of a larger complex of Chinese moon myths, and she may be connected to earlier goddesses like Chang Xi. The moon itself has multiple inhabitants and stories, including the toad and the cassia tree.
