Who It Is

Peteus never ruled Athens, and that is precisely why his family did. Chased out of Attica by King Aegeus, the grandson of the earth-born Erechtheus vanished into the hills of Phocis with a band of followers from Stiria — only for his son Menestheus to be carried back by the twin gods themselves to steal the throne from Theseus.

Characterization is minimal and inferred from actions. Pausanias and Plutarch present Peteus as a victim of dynastic persecution who flees rather than fights Aegeus, suggesting political weakness or prudence. His ability to lead a substantial group of Stirian companions into exile implies status as a deme leader/patron. He is not ascribed speeches, moral judgments, or personality traits. Later tradition treats him as the wronged father whose lineage is vindicated by the Dioscuri. No source calls him impious, tyrannical, or heroic in battle. Erechtheid Legitimacy (Blood Right)

As son of Orneus and grandson of Erechtheus, Peteus carries the autochthonous Erechtheid bloodline, the primary qualification for Athenian kingship in myth. This legitimacy passes to his son Menestheus, who is presented as more legitimate than Theseus (who is an outsider via Aegeus/Pittheus).

Limit: Legitimacy alone does not confer kingship; Peteus never reigns. Aegeus's power blocks his claim, forcing exile.

Deme Leadership and Retinue Mobilization

Peteus is able to lead 'the majority of his companions' from the deme Stiria into exile in Phocis, implying patronage or chieftaincy over that deme and capacity to transplant a community.

Limit: No military feat is ascribed; the retinue is not described as an army. He does not wage war to reclaim Athens.

Exile Survival and Foundation Association

Survives persecution by Aegeus and establishes residence in Phocis, with etiological link to foundation of Stiris. This is a standard heroic exile-and-foundation ability, not supernatural.

Limit: No city-foundation is directly credited to Peteus himself; Stiris is said to be founded by his companions or by later settlers from his group.

Paternal Legacy (Dynastic Vindication via Son)

Through his son Menestheus, Peteus's line reclaims Athens when the Dioscuri install Menestheus as regent/king to spite Theseus. Peteus's posthumous vindication is his only 'power' in narrative terms.

Limit: Peteus himself is dead or absent by this point in most chronologies; he does not act. Menestheus's reign ends with his death at Troy or on return.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No physical description of Peteus survives in any primary source. No vase painting, sculpture, or relief has been securely identified as Peteus. As a mortal Erechtheid prince, he would be imagined in standard heroic/mortal iconography of Attic genealogy (bearded or youthful elite male), but this is reconstruction, not attestation. Status: unknown. No attributes, clothing, or distinctive marks are assigned to him, unlike his grandfather Erechtheus

Symbols — No personal symbols, animals, objects, or cult emblems are attested for Peteus. His symbolic function is genealogical and political: he embodies the displaced legitimate Erechtheid line that returns via his son. The deme Stiria (Steiria) and Phocian Stiris function as his geographic symbols in etiological tradition. No coinage, seal, or sanctuary is linked to him. Accounts I
The Erechtheid Stemma: Birth of Peteus to OrneusPausanias 2.25.6; Pseudo-Apollodorus Bibliotheca 3.15.1-8

Archaic genealogy systematized in Classical-Hellenistic sources, attested in Imperial texts

Erechtheus, the earth-born king of Athens reared by Athena, fathers Orneus. Orneus fathers Peteus (Peteos/Petes). This places Peteus in the senior autochthonous line, senior to the Pandion-Aegeus-Theseus line that actually holds the throne. The genealogy is given to explain why Menestheus, not a son of Theseus, is the legitimate heir and leader of the Athenians at Troy in the Iliad. Establishes the dynastic rivalry that drives Peteus's exile and his son's later usurpation. Provides Iliadic authority for Menestheus's command (Iliad 2.552-554 calls Menestheus son of Peteus).

The Egyptian Petes VariantUncertain late source summarized in modern handbooks; possibly derived from chronographic or lexicographic tradition (no secure Pausanian/Plutarchian parallel)

Late Antiquity to modern reception; attested in Wikipedia/Wikidata summaries citing unspecified 'some versions'

In some versions, Petes was originally an Egyptian who later obtained Athenian citizenship. This would make Peteus a naturalized citizen rather than an autochthonous Erechtheid, aligning him with other Egyptian-founder motifs in Athenian myth (Cecrops as Egyptian in some late accounts). The story, if ancient, would explain his lack of power in Athens and his need to earn citizenship before his son could claim kingship. If accepted, it undermines the Erechtheid legitimacy narrative and makes Menestheus's claim rest on naturalization, not blood. Most scholars reject or ignore this variant due to lack of primary citation.

Persecution by Aegeus and Flight to PhocisPlutarch Theseus 32 (context); Stephanus of Byzantium s.v. Stiris; Pausanias 10.35.8 (geographic corroboration)

Mythical time: generation before Theseus (Aegeus's reign); narrated in Imperial sources drawing on Atthides

Peteus, as a rival Erechtheid claimant, is pursued (διωχθείς) by King Aegeus of Athens. To escape, he flees Attica for Phocis, taking with him the majority of his companions who came from the deme of Stiria (Steiria). In Phocis he settles in the area that becomes Stiris, which retains the name of his Attic deme. The flight explains both his absence from Athenian politics and the toponymic doublet Stiria/Stiris. Removes the rival line from Athens, allowing Aegeus and then Theseus to rule unopposed; creates a Phocian-Athenian link; sets up Menestheus's status as an exile who must be recalled.

Accounts II
The Deme Etiology: Stiria and StirisStephanus of Byzantium s.v. Steiria and Stiris; Pausanias 10.35.8; Strabo 9.2.13 (on Phocian towns)

Classical deme system retrojected into myth; preserved in 2nd-6th century CE sources

The Attic deme Steiria (tribe Pandionis, on the east coast near Brauron) is said to have supplied Peteus's followers. When they followed him to Phocis, they founded or settled Stiris, a town in Phocis near Chaeronea. The name similarity is explained as intentional commemoration. This is a typical Greek toponymic myth linking deme history to heroic exile. Provides mythic charter for kinship between Attica and Phocis; explains why Menestheus, though Athenian king, has Phocian connections; used by Pausanias as geographic curiosity.

Fatherhood of Menestheus by Polyxene or MnesimacheScholia and mythographic handbooks; summarized in modern compendia (Smith, Graves, Grimal) citing lost Atthidographic source

Mythical generation before Trojan War; recorded in Hellenistic-Roman mythography

By his wife Polyxene or, in a variant, Mnesimache, Peteus becomes father of Menestheus. Menestheus will become the Athenian leader at Troy (Iliad 2.552) and the man who replaces Theseus. The mother's name varies by manuscript tradition, indicating a poorly preserved female line. No narrative of courtship or marriage survives; the union is purely genealogical. Produces the next Athenian king and Trojan War commander; completes the Erechtheid stemma from Erechtheus to the Iliadic generation.

The Dioscuri's Revenge: Menestheus Restored to Athens to Spite TheseusPlutarch Theseus 32-35; Diodorus Siculus 4.62-63; Pausanias 1.17.5-6; Apollodorus Epitome 1.23

Mythical time: Theseus's abduction of Helen and his absence (imprisonment with Pirithous in Hades); narrated in late Hellenistic/Imperial sources

Theseus, with Pirithous, abducts the young Helen and hides her at Aphidnae. Her brothers the Dioscuri (Castor and Pollux) invade Attica to recover her. The Athenians, hating Theseus for his tyranny or fearing the Dioscuri, welcome the twins. To punish Theseus, the Dioscuri send for Menestheus, son of Peteus, who is in exile in Phocis (or on the borders), and make him regent or king of Athens. Menestheus, as an Erechtheid, is presented as a legitimate alternative. Theseus, upon return from Hades, finds he cannot retake the city and goes into exile himself, eventually dying on Scyros. Vindicates Peteus's line: the exiled family returns as kings. Explains the interregnum between Theseus and Menestheus in the Athenian king-list (Eusebius gives Menestheus 23 years). Sets up Menestheus as the Athenian commander at Troy, not Theseus's sons Acamas and Demophon (who serve as exiles). Peteus himself is presumably dead by this point and does not return.

Menestheus at Troy: Fulfillment of Peteus's LineHomer Iliad 2.552-554, 4.327-331, 12.331-363; Apollodorus Epitome 3.11; Pausanias 2.25.6

Late Bronze Age setting; Iliad c. 750 BCE; mythographic elaboration 5th century BCE - 2nd century CE

Menestheus, son of Peteus, leads the Athenian contingent of 50 ships to Troy. Homer calls him 'son of Peteus, whom ... no man on earth could equal in marshalling chariots and shield-bearing warriors' (Iliad 2.552-554), with Nestor as the only exception. He fights bravely at Troy, survives, and according to some traditions dies on the return voyage at Melos or reigns until Theseus's sons return. His success retroactively justifies Peteus's lineage as the true Athenian royal house during the heroic age. Makes Peteus the grandfather of one of the major Iliadic leaders; ensures his name survives in the Catalogue of Ships; cements the Erechtheid-Peteid line in Panhellenic epic, not just Atthidography.

Peteus was king of Athens. — Peteus never reigns in any primary source. He is a prince/exile; his son Menestheus becomes king after Theseus. The confusion arises because his father Orneus and grandfather Erechtheus were kings, and his son was king, so he is assumed to have been.

Peteus is the same as Pittheus, grandfather of Theseus. — Distinct figures: Peteus son of Orneus son of Erechtheus (father of Menestheus) vs Pittheus son of Pelops, king of Troezen, father of Aethra and grandfather of Theseus. Names are similar but genealogies and roles are opposite (Peteus is rival to Theseus's line; Pittheus is Theseus's maternal grandfather).

Peteus has a known appearance, symbols, or cult. — No ancient source describes his appearance, attributes, or worship. He has no iconography, no sanctuary, no festival. He is a genealogical link, not a cult hero like Erechtheus or Theseus.

The Egyptian origin is the standard version of Peteus's birth. — The Egyptian Petes story is a weakly attested variant reported without primary citation in modern handbooks; the dominant, source-backed tradition makes him fully Athenian and autochthonous via Erechtheus. The Egyptian claim should be flagged as disputed, not canonical.

Peteus led an army against Athens to put his son on the throne. — Peteus does not wage war on Athens; his son Menestheus is installed by the Dioscuri's invasion to recover Helen, not by Peteus's army. Peteus is already in exile and likely dead by that time; his companions settle in Phocis and do not march on Athens.
