Who It Is

On the battlefield of Mag Tuired, a single stroke of Sreng’s sword does more than sever Nuada’s arm—it fractures the very idea of kingship. In a world where a blemish on the ruler’s body can sicken the land, Nuada Airgetlám stands at the fault line between wholeness and justice, his silver hand glinting like a promise that broken things can be remade—but never without cost. To follow Nuada through loss, restoration, abdication, and death is to watch Ireland’s earliest myths wrestle with a question that still haunts us: what does it mean for power to be both lawful and human?

Nuada is consistently characterized as a just, lawful, and honorable ruler. In Cath Maige Tuired and related narratives, he abides by the legal principle that no blemished man may be king, voluntarily relinquishing his throne after losing his hand, even though he remains capable and respected. This adherence to law, even when personally disadvantageous, marks him as a paradigmatic figure of righteous kingship. Powers
Silver Hand (Airgetlám)

After losing his hand in battle against Sreng at the First Battle of Mag Tuired, Nuada receives a silver hand crafted by Dian Cécht (physician) and Credne (metalworker). The hand is described as moving as well as any natural hand, effectively restoring his physical functionality and symbolizing a fusion of divine craft, healing, and kingship. This prosthetic allows Nuada to regain his throne temporarily, though the legal blemish remains an issue until Miach later replaces it with a flesh hand.

Limit: Despite its full mobility, the silver hand marks Nuada as physically blemished under the legal norms of kingship, initially disqualifying him from rule. The hand’s existence depends on the skill and favor of Dian Cécht and Credne, indicating reliance on specialist divine craftsmen. It does not confer explicit magical offensive powers beyond restoration.

Flesh Hand Restoration

Miach, son of Dian Cécht, later replaces Nuada’s silver hand with a living, flesh hand, healing him completely and removing the legal blemish that barred him from kingship. This act demonstrates a higher level of healing power than Dian Cécht’s prosthetic solution and underscores the importance of bodily integrity in sacral kingship.

Limit: The restoration is a one-time, extraordinary intervention; no broader healing miracles by Nuada himself are described. The act provokes jealousy and violence from Dian Cécht, who kills Miach, indicating social and divine tensions around surpassing established craft.

Sword of Nuada (One of the Four Treasures)

Nuada possesses a sword that is counted among the Four Treasures of the Tuatha Dé Danann. It is described as a weapon from which no one could escape or defend once it was drawn, symbolizing irresistible royal authority and martial supremacy. The sword functions as both a literal weapon and a mythic emblem of kingship and justice.

Limit: The sword’s use is not extensively narrated in specific battle scenes; its powers are largely described in general terms. It appears as a treasure associated with Nuada rather than a constantly wielded artifact in detailed episodes.

Kingship and Sovereignty

Nuada’s primary power is sacral kingship over the Tuatha Dé Danann and, by extension, over Ireland. His rule is portrayed as just, lawful, and beneficial to the land. The health of the realm is implicitly tied to his bodily integrity, reflecting a broader Indo-European motif in which the king’s physical wholeness mirrors the fertility and order of the territory.

Limit: Nuada’s kingship is constrained by legal norms: any physical blemish disqualifies him, leading to his deposition after losing his hand. His sovereignty is also subject to political necessity, as he yields the throne to Lugh when a more suitable war-leader is required.

Martial Prowess and Leadership

Nuada is a capable warrior and commander, leading the Tuatha Dé Danann in their conflicts with the Fir Bolg and Fomorians. He engages in single combat with Sreng and participates in the strategic planning of battles, embodying the martial aspect of kingship.

Limit: Nuada’s martial feats are significant but not portrayed as superhuman in the sense of invulnerability; he suffers serious injury and ultimately dies in battle. His leadership is shared with other champions (Dagda, Ogma, Lugh), indicating a distributed martial hierarchy.

Hunting and Fishing (Reconstructed)

Comparative evidence from Nodens at Lydney Park and linguistic analysis of Nuada’s name suggest an original association with hunting, fishing, and possibly the sea. In this reconstructed profile, Nuada would have been a provider deity linked to acquisition of food and wealth through skillful pursuit.

Limit: Irish narrative sources rarely, if ever, explicitly depict Nuada as a hunter or fisher; this domain is inferred rather than directly attested. It should therefore be treated as a plausible but hypothetical aspect of his earlier pan-Celtic identity.

Accounts I
The First Battle of Mag Tuired (Moytura)Lebor Gabála Érenn; related invasion narratives; summarized in modern secondary sources

Narrative tradition likely early medieval; manuscripts 11th–12th centuries

Nuada leads the Tuatha Dé Danann to Ireland, where the Fir Bolg hold the land. He sends envoys offering the Fir Bolg half of Ireland in exchange for peace; they refuse, and both sides prepare for war. In a gesture of chivalry, the armies exchange weapons to inspect each other’s arms. The battle at Mag Tuired lasts several days. On the final day, Nuada faces the Fir Bolg champion Sreng in single combat. Sreng’s broad-bladed sword cuts through Nuada’s shield and severs his right arm at the shoulder. Despite Nuada’s grievous wound, the Tuatha Dé Danann win the battle, and the surviving Fir Bolg are granted the province of Connacht. Nuada has won Ireland but lost his right to rule, as a blemished king cannot reign. Nuada’s loss of his hand disqualifies him from kingship under the legal norms of the Tuatha Dé Danann. The victory secures territory for the Tuatha Dé Danann but sets in motion a crisis of sovereignty that will lead to Bres’s tyrannical rule and the later Second Battle of Mag Tuired.

The Silver Hand and the Tyranny of BresCath Maige Tuired (Second Battle of Mag Tuired); Lebor Gabála Érenn; modern summaries

Early medieval narrative; extant in later manuscripts

After the First Battle of Mag Tuired, Nuada’s missing hand renders him ineligible for kingship. The Tuatha Dé Danann choose Bres mac Elatha, a physically perfect but half-Fomorian man, as king. Bres proves a disastrous ruler: he favors his Fomorian kin, imposes heavy tribute on the Tuatha Dé Danann, and fails to uphold the generosity expected of a king. Dagda and Ogma are humiliated with menial labor, and poets and guests are poorly treated. Meanwhile, Dian Cécht and Credne craft a silver hand for Nuada that moves as well as a natural hand, partially restoring his status. Later, Miach replaces the silver hand with a flesh hand, fully healing Nuada. A satirical poem (glam dícenn) by the poet Cairbre exposes Bres’s misrule, leading to his deposition. Nuada is restored as king, but the Fomorian threat remains, setting the stage for the Second Battle of Mag Tuired. Nuada’s silver and later flesh hand restore his bodily integrity and kingship, but the period of Bres’s tyranny leaves the Tuatha Dé Danann weakened and subject to Fomorian tribute. Accounts II
The Second Battle of Mag TuiredCath Maige Tuired (The Second Battle of Mag Tuired)

Early medieval narrative; preserved in later manuscripts

With Bres deposed and Nuada restored as king, the Tuatha Dé Danann face escalating oppression from the Fomorians, who demand heavy tribute. Lugh, a young hero of many skills and Nuada’s great-nephew, arrives at Tara and is tested by the court. Recognizing Lugh’s exceptional abilities, Nuada eventually yields the kingship to him, acknowledging that a more versatile champion is needed for the coming war. In the Second Battle of Mag Tuired, the Tuatha Dé Danann, led by Lugh and supported by Nuada and other champions, confront the Fomorians. Nuada fights bravely but is ultimately slain by Balor of the Evil Eye. Lugh kills Balor and secures victory, freeing the Tuatha Dé Danann from Fomorian tyranny. Nuada’s death marks the end of his earthly kingship but cements his role as a sacrificial ruler whose loss contributes to the liberation of his people. The Fomorians are defeated, and the Tuatha Dé Danann regain their freedom and sovereignty. Nuada’s death underscores the cost of kingship and the sacrificial dimension of leadership. Nuada at Brú na Bóinne and the Loss of His HomeDindshenchas traditions; summarized in modern secondary sources

Medieval; precise dating varies by text

Nuada is said to reside at Brú na Bóinne, a major ritual complex on the River Boyne. In one tradition, the Dagda uses legal wordplay and cunning to gain both Brú na Bóinne and Nuada’s wife Boann, effectively displacing Nuada from his home. The episode illustrates the importance of legal language, hospitality, and cunning in the politics of the Tuatha Dé Danann, as well as the fluidity of property and marital relationships among the gods. Nuada loses his residence and, in some versions, his wife, highlighting the vulnerability of even a just king to legal manipulation. The Dagda’s acquisition of Brú na Bóinne strengthens his own position and deepens his association with the Boyne complex.

Nuada and the Place-Name Maynooth (Nuada’s Mound)Dindshenchas and topographical lore; modern place-name studies

Medieval origins; ongoing place-name usage

The town of Maynooth in County Kildare is often etymologized as "Magh Nuadhad" or "Nuada’s Mound/Plain", suggesting a mythic association with Nuada. While detailed narratives are sparse, the place-name indicates that Nuada’s memory was embedded in the landscape, with local traditions likely connecting him to the area as a figure of kingship or ritual significance. Nuada’s name becomes part of the enduring geography of Ireland, reinforcing his status as a foundational figure whose presence is felt not only in texts but also in place-names.
