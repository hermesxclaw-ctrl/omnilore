Nuada of the silver hand, the just king who lost and regained his throne, who led the gods to battle with his invincible sword.

Who They Are

Nuada (or Nuada Airgetlám, 'Nuada of the Silver Hand') is the king of the Tuatha Dé Danann and a god of war, kingship, and justice. In the First Battle of Mag Tuired, Nuada's hand was cut off by the Fomorian champion Sreng. Because a king must be physically unblemished, Nuada was forced to abdicate in favor of the half-Fomorian Bres. The physician Dian Cecht crafted a functioning silver hand (or arm) for Nuada, and later his son Miach flesh-covered it, allowing Nuada to be restored as king. Nuada's silver hand was considered one of the great wonders of the Tuatha Dé Danann. After his restoration, Nuada led the Tuatha Dé Danann against the Fomorians in the Second Battle of Mag Tuired. He was killed in the battle by Balor's evil eye. Nuada is associated with the ancient Irish kingship of Tara. His weapon is a sword (Claímh Solais, the Sword of Light), one of the four great treasures of the Tuatha Dé Danann — it was said to be invincible in battle. Nuada is often identified with the British god Nodens and the Gaulish god Nodons. He represents the ideal of the just and powerful king who sacrifices and suffers for his people.

Attested in Lebor Gabála Érenn / Cath Maige TuiredEra Medieval Irish (recorded 11th-12th c.), reflecting earlier oral tradition

Form

Attested descriptions of Nuada are sparse and largely functional rather than detailed. He is consistently portrayed as a king and warrior of the Tuatha Dé Danann, equipped with weapons and armor appropriate to a high champion. The most distinctive and repeatedly emphasized feature is his silver hand or arm (Airgetlám), crafted by Dian Cécht and Credne after he loses his flesh hand in battle. In Cath Maige Tuired, the silver hand is described as moving as well as any natural hand, implying a seamless integration of metal and flesh and an early literary image of prosthetic restoration. Later, Miach replaces the silver hand with a living, flesh hand, restoring Nuada’s bodily wholeness. Iconographic reconstructions often depict Nuada as a tall, regal figure with a gleaming silver arm, a sword in hand, and royal garments, but these visual details are largely modern artistic extrapolations rather than directly attested in the medieval texts.

Powers

Silver Hand (Airgetlám) After losing his hand in battle against Sreng at the First Battle of Mag Tuired, Nuada receives a silver hand crafted by Dian Cécht (physician) and Credne (metalworker). The hand is described as moving as well as any natural hand, effectively restoring his physical functionality and symbolizing a fusion of divine craft, healing, and kingship. This prosthetic allows Nuada to regain his throne temporarily, though the legal blemish remains an issue until Miach later replaces it with a flesh hand. Cath Maige Tuired (Second Battle of Mag Tuired); Lebor Gabála Érenn; later retellings Despite its full mobility, the silver hand marks Nuada as physically blemished under the legal norms of kingship, initially disqualifying him from rule. The hand’s existence depends on the skill and favor of Dian Cécht and Credne, indicating reliance on specialist divine craftsmen. It does not confer explicit magical offensive powers beyond restoration. Some versions imply that the silver hand fully restores Nuada’s eligibility for kingship, while others maintain that only the later flesh hand provided by Miach removes the blemish. Modern interpretations sometimes treat the silver hand as a purely positive symbol of wholeness, whereas medieval legal concepts emphasize its problematic status.

Flesh Hand Restoration Miach, son of Dian Cécht, later replaces Nuada’s silver hand with a living, flesh hand, healing him completely and removing the legal blemish that barred him from kingship. This act demonstrates a higher level of healing power than Dian Cécht’s prosthetic solution and underscores the importance of bodily integrity in sacral kingship. Cath Maige Tuired (Second Battle of Mag Tuired) The restoration is a one-time, extraordinary intervention; no broader healing miracles by Nuada himself are described. The act provokes jealousy and violence from Dian Cécht, who kills Miach, indicating social and divine tensions around surpassing established craft. Some summaries minimize Miach’s role or omit the flesh-hand episode, focusing instead on the silver hand as Nuada’s defining feature. This creates differing emphases on whether Nuada’s kingship is restored through prosthesis or complete organic healing.

Sword of Nuada (One of the Four Treasures) Nuada possesses a sword that is counted among the Four Treasures of the Tuatha Dé Danann. It is described as a weapon from which no one could escape or defend once it was drawn, symbolizing irresistible royal authority and martial supremacy. The sword functions as both a literal weapon and a mythic emblem of kingship and justice. Lebor Gabála Érenn; later mythological summaries and secondary sources The sword’s use is not extensively narrated in specific battle scenes; its powers are largely described in general terms. It appears as a treasure associated with Nuada rather than a constantly wielded artifact in detailed episodes. Some modern retellings conflate Nuada’s sword with other legendary Irish weapons or elaborate its powers beyond what is attested, adding attributes such as light or fire that are not clearly present in the medieval texts.

Kingship and Sovereignty Nuada’s primary power is sacral kingship over the Tuatha Dé Danann and, by extension, over Ireland. His rule is portrayed as just, lawful, and beneficial to the land. The health of the realm is implicitly tied to his bodily integrity, reflecting a broader Indo-European motif in which the king’s physical wholeness mirrors the fertility and order of the territory. Lebor Gabála Érenn; Cath Maige Tuired; dindshenchas traditions Nuada’s kingship is constrained by legal norms: any physical blemish disqualifies him, leading to his deposition after losing his hand. His sovereignty is also subject to political necessity, as he yields the throne to Lugh when a more suitable war-leader is required. Some sources emphasize Nuada’s kingship as primarily historical and downplay its sacral dimension, while others foreground the mythic and ritual aspects. Modern neopagan interpretations may treat his kingship as an archetype of ethical leadership, extending beyond the specific legal framework of medieval Ireland.

Martial Prowess and Leadership Nuada is a capable warrior and commander, leading the Tuatha Dé Danann in their conflicts with the Fir Bolg and Fomorians. He engages in single combat with Sreng and participates in the strategic planning of battles, embodying the martial aspect of kingship. Cath Maige Tuired; Lebor Gabála Érenn; Mythopedia synthesis Nuada’s martial feats are significant but not portrayed as superhuman in the sense of invulnerability; he suffers serious injury and ultimately dies in battle. His leadership is shared with other champions (Dagda, Ogma, Lugh), indicating a distributed martial hierarchy. Later romanticized retellings sometimes exaggerate Nuada’s combat abilities or present him as an almost invincible hero, whereas the primary texts emphasize both his courage and his vulnerability.

Hunting and Fishing (Reconstructed) Comparative evidence from Nodens at Lydney Park and linguistic analysis of Nuada’s name suggest an original association with hunting, fishing, and possibly the sea. In this reconstructed profile, Nuada would have been a provider deity linked to acquisition of food and wealth through skillful pursuit. Comparative mythology (Nodens inscriptions; Tolkien’s "The Name Nodens"; etymological studies by Pokorny and others) Irish narrative sources rarely, if ever, explicitly depict Nuada as a hunter or fisher; this domain is inferred rather than directly attested. It should therefore be treated as a plausible but hypothetical aspect of his earlier pan-Celtic identity. Some scholars reject or downplay the hunting/fishing association, arguing that the Irish material supports a primarily kingly and martial profile. Others see the hunting aspect as central to understanding the name’s etymology and the deity’s original functions.

Accounts

Nuada leads the Tuatha Dé Danann to Ireland, where the Fir Bolg hold the land. He sends envoys offering the Fir Bolg half of Ireland in exchange for peace; they refuse, and both sides prepare for war. In a gesture of chivalry, the armies exchange weapons to inspect each other’s arms. The battle at Mag Tuired lasts several days. On the final day, Nuada faces the Fir Bolg champion Sreng in single combat. Sreng’s broad-bladed sword cuts through Nuada’s shield and severs his right arm at the shoulder. Despite Nuada’s grievous wound, the Tuatha Dé Danann win the battle, and the surviving Fir Bolg are granted the province of Connacht. Nuada has won Ireland but lost his right to rule, as a blemished king cannot reign.

After the First Battle of Mag Tuired, Nuada’s missing hand renders him ineligible for kingship. The Tuatha Dé Danann choose Bres mac Elatha, a physically perfect but half-Fomorian man, as king. Bres proves a disastrous ruler: he favors his Fomorian kin, imposes heavy tribute on the Tuatha Dé Danann, and fails to uphold the generosity expected of a king. Dagda and Ogma are humiliated with menial labor, and poets and guests are poorly treated. Meanwhile, Dian Cécht and Credne craft a silver hand for Nuada that moves as well as a natural hand, partially restoring his status. Later, Miach replaces the silver hand with a flesh hand, fully healing Nuada. A satirical poem (glam dícenn) by the poet Cairbre exposes Bres’s misrule, leading to his deposition. Nuada is restored as king, but the Fomorian threat remains, setting the stage for the Second Battle of Mag Tuired.

With Bres deposed and Nuada restored as king, the Tuatha Dé Danann face escalating oppression from the Fomorians, who demand heavy tribute. Lugh, a young hero of many skills and Nuada’s great-nephew, arrives at Tara and is tested by the court. Recognizing Lugh’s exceptional abilities, Nuada eventually yields the kingship to him, acknowledging that a more versatile champion is needed for the coming war. In the Second Battle of Mag Tuired, the Tuatha Dé Danann, led by Lugh and supported by Nuada and other champions, confront the Fomorians. Nuada fights bravely but is ultimately slain by Balor of the Evil Eye. Lugh kills Balor and secures victory, freeing the Tuatha Dé Danann from Fomorian tyranny. Nuada’s death marks the end of his earthly kingship but cements his role as a sacrificial ruler whose loss contributes to the liberation of his people.

Nuada is said to reside at Brú na Bóinne, a major ritual complex on the River Boyne. In one tradition, the Dagda uses legal wordplay and cunning to gain both Brú na Bóinne and Nuada’s wife Boann, effectively displacing Nuada from his home. The episode illustrates the importance of legal language, hospitality, and cunning in the politics of the Tuatha Dé Danann, as well as the fluidity of property and marital relationships among the gods.

The town of Maynooth in County Kildare is often etymologized as "Magh Nuadhad" or "Nuada’s Mound/Plain", suggesting a mythic association with Nuada. While detailed narratives are sparse, the place-name indicates that Nuada’s memory was embedded in the landscape, with local traditions likely connecting him to the area as a figure of kingship or ritual significance.

Content Notes

Nuada’s arm is severed at the shoulder by Sreng’s sword during the First Battle of Mag Tuired, leaving him permanently injured. Under the legal norms of kingship, this bodily blemish disqualifies him from rule, illustrating a harsh linkage between physical wholeness and political legitimacy. The narrative foregrounds the vulnerability of the king’s body and the social consequences of injury.

Scholars interpret this episode within broader Indo-European patterns of sacral kingship, where the king’s body symbolizes the land’s fertility and order. The legal exclusion of blemished kings reflects a system that stigmatizes disability and enforces strict bodily norms.

Lebor Gabála Érenn; Cath Maige Tuired; modern summaries

After Nuada’s deposition, Bres becomes king and imposes severe tribute on the Tuatha Dé Danann, favoring his Fomorian kin. Dagda and Ogma are forced into humiliating labor, and guests and poets are denied proper hospitality. This period of misrule constitutes political oppression and economic exploitation, with the Tuatha Dé Danann effectively subjected to a colonial-style domination by the Fomorians.

The tyranny of Bres is often read as a critique of unjust kingship and a reflection of historical experiences of foreign rule and tribute. The glam dícenn (satirical curse) used to depose Bres highlights the role of poets as agents of social justice and resistance.

Cath Maige Tuired; modern summaries

After Miach successfully restores Nuada’s flesh hand, surpassing Dian Cécht’s silver prosthesis, Dian Cécht kills his son in a fit of jealousy. This intra-divine violence reveals the darker side of professional rivalry and the cost of innovation within the divine community.

Miach’s death is frequently discussed in studies of Irish myth as an example of how excellence and healing can provoke destructive envy, complicating the image of the gods as purely benevolent. It also underscores the precariousness of those who challenge established hierarchies.

Cath Maige Tuired

Nuada ultimately dies in battle against the Fomorians, killed by Balor. His death is part of a larger war that involves mass slaughter and the overthrow of oppressive rulers. Nuada’s willingness to fight and die for his people embodies a sacrificial dimension of kingship, but also highlights the brutal realities of mythic warfare.

Nuada’s death is interpreted as a mythic representation of the king’s sacrifice for the community, resonant with broader Indo-European motifs of dying and resurrected or replaced rulers. It also serves as a narrative pivot that elevates Lugh while acknowledging the cost of liberation.

Cath Maige Tuired

In the Brú na Bóinne episode, the Dagda uses legal wordplay to gain Nuada’s residence and, in some versions, his wife Boann. This manipulation results in Nuada’s displacement and loss of property, illustrating how law and cunning can be used to dispossess even powerful figures.

This episode is often analyzed as a commentary on the power of legal language and the potential for abuse within systems of law. It also reflects tensions between different divine figures and the fragility of status and ownership.

What We Don't Know

Several dimensions remain incomplete or uncertain: (1) Detailed physical descriptions of Nuada beyond his silver/flesh hand are largely absent from primary texts, leaving modern visualizations speculative. (2) Genealogical relationships (e.g., Nuada as brother to Dian Cécht and Goibniu, husband to Boann) vary between sources and may reflect later systematizations; a comprehensive survey of all manuscript variants was not possible within this scope. (3) The exact etymological development of Nuada’s name and its relationship to Nodens and Lludd Llaw Eraint remains debated; this dossier relies on a limited selection of linguistic studies. (4) Archaeological evidence directly linked to Nuada in Ireland is minimal; comparative use of Nodens at Lydney Park is inferential rather than definitive. (5) Modern neopagan practices involving Nuada are diverse and often undocumented in academic literature; this dossier references them in general terms without exhaustive ethnographic detail. (6) Some online sources (blogs, databases) synthesize material without full citation of primary texts; while useful, they require cautious use and further verification. (7) Access to all relevant Irish-language manuscripts and critical editions was limited to widely available digital resources, leaving potential nuances in lesser-known texts unexplored.

A way to hold it in your mind

On the battlefield of Mag Tuired, a single stroke of Sreng’s sword does more than sever Nuada’s arm—it fractures the very idea of kingship. In a world where a blemish on the ruler’s body can sicken the land, Nuada Airgetlám stands at the fault line between wholeness and justice, his silver hand glinting like a promise that broken things can be remade—but never without cost. To follow Nuada through loss, restoration, abdication, and death is to watch Ireland’s earliest myths wrestle with a question that still haunts us: what does it mean for power to be both lawful and human?
