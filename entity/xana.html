Archive Dossier — Identity

Xana

Pronunciation Asturian: /ˈʃana/ or [ˈɕa.na]

Known As

Anjana (Eastern Galicia, Cantabria)

Etymology

The origin is unclear; some scholars derive it from the Latin 'Diana', the Roman goddess of the hunt and the moon.

Cultural Ownership

Asturias (Spain), also found in Eastern Galicia and Cantabria (as Anjana).

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars connect the xana to the Roman Diana, but this is not universally accepted. The relationship to the Galician-Portuguese 'Enchanted Moura' and the Romanian 'Zână' is debated; they may share a common Indo-European root or be independent developments.

Archive Dossier — Approach

In the green hills of Asturias, where streams run cold and clear, there are whispers of a woman of unearthly beauty who combs her golden hair by the water's edge. She is the xana, a fairy of the springs, who can reward the pure-hearted with love and gold—or drive the wicked to madness with her song. But beware: she may steal your child and leave a changeling in its place, a creature that ages in months and speaks of centuries. To unmask it, you must place eggshells by the fire and listen for the telltale cry: 'I was born one hundred years ago...'

By the spring of clear water, at the hour when the sun touches the mountain peaks, the Xana combs her golden hair with a comb of pure gold, and her song is the sound of lost time.

Who It Is

La Xana is a fairy-like being from the mythology of Asturias in northern Spain, one of the most cherished figures in Asturian folklore. Descended from the Celtic nymph cults of the pre-Roman Astures tribe, Xanas are described as extraordinarily beautiful young women with long, flowing blonde or red hair, pale skin, and eyes that can be either blue, green, or golden. They are very small — rarely more than half a meter tall — and are often found near crystalline springs, rivers, and caves. Xanas are guardians of natural treasures, including gold, hidden beneath the waters, and they emerge at dawn, dusk, and full moon to comb their hair with combs of pure gold while singing hypnotic songs. While generally benevolent, Xanas are dangerous when their trust is broken. A Xana may grant wishes, reveal treasure locations, or weave magical fabrics for humans who show them kindness. However, stealing a Xana's golden comb or spying on her bathing means instant misfortune or death. Xanas are said to sometimes fall in love with mortal men, producing half-fairy children, but such unions always end tragically. A Xana's cave or spring is called a 'xineu' and is considered sacred — polluting or damaging a xineu brings a curse. The Xana figure likely descends from the Celtic water goddess cults of northern Iberia and shares characteristics with the French Dames Blanches, the Goidelic Irish banshee, and the Breton korrigan.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

Always female, of extraordinary beauty. Often depicted with long hair, sometimes combing it. Associated with pure water sources.

Symbols & Iconography

Fountains, rivers, waterfalls, caves, forested regions with pure water. Toponyms referencing xanas are common in Asturias.

Behaviour & Personality

Dual nature: can be beneficial or malicious. Offers 'love water' and rewards to the worthy; hypnotic voices heard on spring and summer nights. Pure souls feel peace and love; impure souls feel suffocation and may go insane. Some attack people and steal food.

Domains & Habitats

Fountains, rivers, waterfalls, caves, forests with pure water.

Version Differences

In Eastern Galicia and Cantabria, the equivalent is the Anjana, which may have distinct traits. The xana's portrayal varies across individual folktales: some are mothers, some are enchanted, some are treasure guardians, some are malicious.
