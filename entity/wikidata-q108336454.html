Who It Is

On the marshy edge of Lake Hyria in Aetolia, a mother abandoned her newborn because his divine father made him too beautiful to keep — and when that child later threw himself into the same water to become a swan, the Greeks said the lake itself remembered her name. Hyria is not a goddess with temples, but a mortal woman whose grief etiologically stained a whole landscape, and whose town-name echoes from Boeotia to Salento to Cilicia.

In Antoninus Liberalis 12, Hyrie is characterized by two actions: 1) a sexual union with Apollo (no resistance or consent language preserved; presented as liaison that produces Cycnus), and 2) abandonment/exposure of the infant Cycnus, either from shame, fear, or paternal pressure — the motive is not elaborated by Antoninus, who focuses on the child's fate. Powers
No attested supernatural powers (mortal status)

Hyrie/Hyria is consistently presented as a mortal woman, daughter of the mortal Amphinomus. No source attributes to her divinity, magic, shapeshifting, prophecy, or combat abilities. Her only narrative power is genealogical: as mother of Cycnus by Apollo, she provides the semi-divine lineage that explains Cycnus's extraordinary beauty and his eventual metamorphosis (which is enacted by Apollo/Zeus, not by her).

Limit: Mortal, vulnerable to shame, grief, and social pressure; unable to prevent son's exposure or metamorphosis; no control over divine transformations.

Eponymic/etiological function (narrative, not supernatural)

Her name provides the etiology for Lake Hyria and, by extension, the towns named Hyria. In Greek mythic logic, the landscape is said to 'remember' her or her son. This is a narrative function, not an ability she wields.

Limit: Passive; she does not create or control the lake, the lake is named after her or after the swan-son event.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No physical description of Hyrie survives in any primary source. Antoninus Liberalis and Ovid give no details of hair, dress, stature, or attributes. As a mortal princess, daughter of Amphinomus, she is presented without iconographic markers, divine symbols, or cult dress. No vase painting, sculpture, or mosaic has been securely identified as Hyrie. Renaissance and modern imagined portraits are inventions without ancient basis. Status: unknown /

Symbols — No attested symbols, animals, plants, or cult objects are associated directly with Hyrie. Indirect associations via her son Cycnus: swans (κύκνος), lakes/marshes, and the Lake Hyria/Conope waters. The swan is the symbol of the son, not the mother. No votive, coin, or sanctuary evidence links Hyrie to a specific iconographic program. The toponym Hyria itself functioned as geographic marker in Boeotia and Aetolia, not as religious symbol.

Domains — Hyrie's domain is domestic/genealogical, not divine. She belongs to no divine sphere (not a nymph, not a goddess). Her habitats in myth are: 1) the household of Amphinomus (unlocated, presumably Boeotian or Aetolian elite), 2) association with Lake Hyria near Conope in Aetolia (10.2.6 Strabo) where her son's metamorphosis occurs. Accounts I
Hyrie and Apollo: The Birth of CycnusAntoninus Liberalis, Metamorphoses 12 (citing Nicander and Boios)

Hellenistic original (2nd c. BCE) preserved in 2nd-3rd c. CE

Hyrie, daughter of Amphinomus, is loved by Apollo and bears him a son, Cycnus. Out of shame or fear, she abandons the infant. The child is not killed but exposed; he survives, raised either by shepherds or, in some retellings, among swans, and grows into a youth of extraordinary beauty. This establishes the semi-divine genealogy that will explain Cycnus's later metamorphosis. Cycnus's survival sets up his later career as a beautiful but arrogant youth who rejects lovers; Hyrie disappears from the narrative after the birth, reappearing only as genealogical marker. The story provides an etiology for Cycnus's beauty and divine favor.

The Exposure and Upbringing of CycnusAntoninus Liberalis, Metamorphoses 12

Hellenistic/Imperial

After Hyrie exposes Cycnus, the infant is found and raised. Antoninus says he was left in a field or marsh and was tended, growing up to be exceptionally handsome. His beauty attracts many suitors, but he is haughty and dismissive. This childhood of abandonment explains his later distrust and cruelty toward lovers. Cycnus's character as proud and unyielding is rooted in his origin story; his later demand for impossible labors from his lover Phylius stems from this arrogance.

Cycnus, Phylius, and the Impossible LaborsAntoninus Liberalis, Metamorphoses 12 (detailed); Ovid, Metamorphoses 7.371-383 (compressed)

Hellenistic original, Roman adaptation

As a youth, Cycnus is loved by Phylius (or Phylleus/Phylius). Cycnus, being arrogant, sets Phylius three impossible tasks to prove his love: to kill a lion without weapons, to capture man-eating vultures alive, and to drag a wild bull to an altar by hand. With Heracles' or Zeus's covert help, Phylius succeeds. Cycnus, shamed or still contemptuous, refuses to honor the bargain. Phylius, insulted, refuses further. Cycnus, in despair or anger, throws himself into Lake Conope (also called Lake Hyria). Cycnus's leap leads to his metamorphosis into a swan (κύκνος), giving the bird its name and etiology. Phylius's story becomes a moral about erastes/eromenos obligations and hubris. The lake is thereafter called Hyria/Conope.

Accounts II
The Metamorphosis into the Swan and the Naming of Lake HyriaAntoninus Liberalis 12; Ovid Met. 7.371-383; Strabo 10.2.6

Hellenistic to Augustan

As Cycnus falls into the lake, Apollo (or in some tellings, Zeus) takes pity and transforms him mid-air or upon hitting the water into a swan, whose white plumage and mournful cry recall the youth's beauty and lament. The lake near Conope in Aetolia, previously called Conope, is thereafter also called Lake Hyria, after Hyrie (his mother) or after the swan-event itself. Strabo notes Lake Hyria lies between Pleuron and Calydon, near the Achelous plain, confirming the geographic anchor. Etiology for swan species, for lake name, and for proverbial 'swan song'. The transformation is catalogued among Ornithogonia (bird-origin myths). The lake name persists in Strabo and Stephanus as geographic reality.

Hyria in the Catalogue of Ships: The Boeotian TownHomer, Iliad 2.496; Strabo 9.2.12; Pausanias 9.19.4 (Tanagra region); Stephanus s.v. Hyria

Late Bronze Age tradition codified c. 750 BCE; Classical-Hellenistic geography

In the Boeotian contingent of the Catalogue of Ships, Homer lists 'Hyria and rocky Aulis, Schoenus, Scolus...' as towns contributing ships to Troy. This Hyria is a Boeotian settlement near Aulis and Tanagra, on the Euripus strait. By Strabo's time (Augustan), Hyria was deserted/uninhabited (οὐκέτι οἰκεῖται), its territory absorbed by Tanagra. Its eponym in local myth is Hyrieus, not Hyrie, showing the toponym's independent heroic genealogy. Establishes Hyria as a genuine Bronze Age toponym with continuity into historical geography; provides anchor for Hyrieus-Orion myth cycle (Hyrieus hosts Zeus, Poseidon, Hermes who create Orion from a bull-hide). Distinguishes Boeotian Hyria from Aetolian Lake Hyria.

Hyrieus, King of Hyria, and the Birth of Orion (Male Eponym Parallel)Antoninus Liberalis 25 (via Hellanicus?); Ovid, Fasti 5.537-538; Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 1.4.3; Pausanias 9.20.3; Strabo 9.2.12 scholia

Classical to Imperial

Hyrieus, son of Poseidon and Alcyone (daughter of Atlas), son or grandson of Ogygus, is king of Hyria in Boeotia. Childless, he hosts Zeus, Poseidon, and Hermes, who urinate on a bull-hide buried in earth and after ten months produce Orion (etymologized as from ouron, urine). Hyrieus thus gives his name to Hyria, paralleling Hyrie's eponymy for the lake. Some traditions make Hyrieus father of Nycteus and Lycus, or of Crinacus. This male eponym story runs alongside but separate from Hyrie daughter of Amphinomus. Explains Orion's Boeotian birth and Hyria's fame beyond Homer; creates confusion in modern sources that merge Hyrie (female) and Hyrieus (male) into one figure. The Orion birth story becomes the most famous myth attached to the toponym Hyria, eclipsing Hyrie's story.

Hyria was a Greek goddess or water nymph who ruled Lake Hyria. — No primary source calls Hyrie a goddess or nymph. She is a mortal woman, daughter of Amphinomus, defined solely by her role as mother of Cycnus. She has no cult, no divine domain, and no powers. The lake is named after her (or her son's story), not ruled by her.

Hyria and Hyrieus are the same person, or Hyrie is the daughter of Hyrieus. — Hyrie (female, daughter of Amphinomus, mother of Cycnus) and Hyrieus (male, son of Poseidon and Alcyone, king of Hyria, host of Orion's birth) are distinct figures from different traditions (Aetolian Ornithogonia vs. Boeotian foundation myth). No ancient source makes them related.

Hyrie transformed into a swan or into Lake Hyria. — Only her son Cycnus transforms into a swan (Antoninus 12, Ovid). Hyrie does not transform in any critical text; the lake is a geographic feature that receives its name from the story, not from her metamorphosed body. The 'Hyrie became the lake' variant appears only in late, uncritical paraphrases.

The Finnish Hyria school and the Italian town Oria are named after the mythological Hyria. — Finnish Hyria is an acronym/abbreviation of Hyvinkää-Riihimäki, with no mythological reference. Oria's ancient name Hyria/Uria is Messapian, likely pre-Greek substrate, and its foundation myths involve Messapians/Cretans, not Hyrie daughter of Amphinomus. The shared spelling is coincidental homonymy.
