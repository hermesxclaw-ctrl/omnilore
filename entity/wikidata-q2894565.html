Who It Is

In the shadow of the Hebrew Bible's condemnation, a goddess flickers in the margins of history — a name without a face, a cult without a temple, a deity reduced to a pun. Ashima was worshipped by the men of Hamath, resettled in Samaria by Assyrian conquerors, and her name was twisted by the prophet Amos into an accusation: those who swore by her were literally swearing by 'guilt.' But was she a goddess of fate, as her name might suggest, or a 'name' itself, a divine presence so abstract she was known only by the act of being called? The evidence is maddeningly thin — a few verses, a disputed papyrus, a rabbinic joke about a bald sheep. Yet she endures in the record, a ghost of a faith that was erased by conquest and polemic, waiting for someone to ask not just what she was, but who she was to the people who once bowed before her.

No narrative texts describe Ashima's behavior, personality, or actions. She is known only as a name in biblical texts and a possible theophoric element in personal names. Her silence in the historical record is notable — she appears as a passive object of worship, not an actor in any surviving myth. This makes her a 'name without a story,' a deity known to have been worshipped but whose mythology, if it ever existed in written form, has been entirely lost. Divine Patronage

As a national or civic deity, Ashima would have been understood as a patron and protector of her worshippers, offering divine favor, protection, and blessing to the community that maintained her cult.

Limit: This is an inference from comparative religion, not a documented power. No texts describe Ashima actively protecting or blessing anyone.

Oracular or Fated Pronouncement

If the etymology connecting her name to 'fate' (shimtu) is correct, Ashima may have been understood as a deity who decreed or revealed destiny. This would make her an oracular goddess, consulted for knowledge of the future.

Limit: The etymology is debated. Even if correct, the connection to actual cult practice is speculative.

Cultic Presence

The worship of Ashima, attested in the Hebrew Bible and possibly at Elephantine, implies a belief in her ability to receive offerings, hear prayers, and respond to devotion. Her cult had enough substance to be noticed and condemned by biblical authors.

Limit: The nature of her cultic presence — whether she was believed to inhabit a statue, a temple, or a natural location — is unknown.

Form & Signs

The archaeological record of Ammonite religion is sparse, and no definitive iconographic representation of Ashima has been identified. The Talmudic tradition describes her cult image as a 'bald sheep' or 'shorn lamb,' but this is a deliberate mockery by later Jewish commentators, not a reliable description of her actual cult statue. Accounts I
The Export of Ashima to Samaria (2 Kings 17)Hebrew Bible, 2 Kings 17:24-34

After the Assyrian conquest of the Northern Kingdom (c. 722 BCE)

After the Assyrian Empire conquered the Kingdom of Israel, it deported the Israelite population and resettled Samaria with peoples from Babylon, Cuthah, Avva, Hamath, and Sepharvaim. These settlers brought their own deities with them. The men of Hamath made Ashima. When lions attacked the settlers, they attributed the disaster to their failure to worship the local god (YHWH) properly, and an Israelite priest was sent to teach them the proper worship. However, the settlers continued to worship their own gods alongside YHWH, creating a syncretistic religious landscape. The text lists Ashima among these imported deities, making her one of the few named foreign gods in the biblical account of this period. The passage establishes Ashima as a foreign deity whose worship was considered illegitimate by biblical authors. It also provides the historical context for the later prophetic condemnation in Amos.

Accounts II
The Oath by Ashima (Amos 8)Hebrew Bible, Book of Amos

8th century BCE (prophetic activity); text likely edited in later centuries

The prophet Amos, denouncing the sins of Israel, includes a curse on those who 'swear by the sin of Samaria.' The Hebrew word translated as 'sin' (ashmah) is almost certainly a deliberate pun on the name Ashima. The oath is addressed to the god of Dan and the 'way' or 'pilgrimage' of Beersheba, indicating that the people were swearing by their local deities. Amos's pun transforms the goddess's name into an accusation: those who worship Ashima are literally swearing by 'guilt' or 'sin' itself. The passage is a powerful example of prophetic rhetoric using wordplay to condemn foreign worship. The passage provides the only clear biblical evidence of actual oath-taking in Ashima's name, suggesting her cult was active in the northern kingdom before the Assyrian conquest.

The Elephantine Cult of Ashim-BethelElephantine Papyri (Aramaic documents)

5th century BCE

The Jewish community at Elephantine, a military colony on the Nile, left behind a rich archive of Aramaic documents. Among the deities they worshipped, alongside YHWH, were several compound deities, including 'Ashim-Bethel.' The name suggests a syncretistic fusion of Ashima (or an 'Ashim' figure) with Bethel, a deity associated with sacred stones or the 'house of God.' The community's religious practice was clearly diverse and did not conform to the later strict monotheism of the Jerusalem temple. The presence of 'Ashim-Bethel' in personal names and legal documents indicates that this deity was an active part of daily religious life. The Elephantine evidence complicates our understanding of Ashima, suggesting either that her cult spread beyond Ammon and Samaria, or that 'Ashim' is a separate deity entirely. The compound nature of the name suggests theological creativity and syncretism.
