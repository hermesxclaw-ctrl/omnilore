Angrboða, the sorrow-bringer, mother of the wolf, the serpent, and the queen of the dead — the womb of Midgard's doom.

Who It Is

Angrboða is a giantess who lives in Járnviðr (Ironwood) and is the consort of Loki. Her name means 'Sorrow-Bringer' or 'She Who Causes Anguish.' She is the mother of three of the most monstrous and significant beings in Norse mythology: the wolf Fenrir, the Midgard Serpent Jörmungandr, and Hel, the ruler of the underworld. The gods, learning of these monstrous children through prophecy, took action: Odin cast Jörmungandr into the sea, banished Hel to the underworld, and had the gods bind Fenrir (which cost Týr his hand). Angrboða is also the ancestress of all wolves, according to the eddic poem Hyndluljóð. She is a dark and shadowy figure — little else is known of her beyond her motherhood of monsters, making her one of the most significant yet least detailed figures in Norse myth. She represents the raw, chaotic, untamed female power of the giant world that constantly threatens the ordered realm of the gods. In modern contexts, she appears in Neil Gaiman's American Gods and as a character in God of War: Ragnarök.

Attested in Prose Edda (Gylfaginning) / Poetic Edda (Hyndluljóð)Era Viking Age (c. 800-1100 CE), recorded 13th century

Form

Attested textual sources provide almost no physical description of Angrboða herself. She is identified as a jötunn (giantess) and as an ‘old woman’ or ‘witch’ dwelling in Ironwood, east of Midgard. From this, scholars reconstruct her as a female giant associated with wild, liminal forest spaces and with wolf-shaped offspring. Later artistic and popular depictions often portray her as tall, gaunt, and feral, sometimes with wolfish features or surrounded by wolves, but these are adaptation-specific and not grounded in the medieval texts. Her age is implied by the epithet ‘old woman’ (gýgr or gamla), suggesting a crone-like figure rather than a youthful goddess.

Powers

Monstrous motherhood and breeding of wolf-shaped giants Angrboða’s primary ‘power’ in the sources is generative: she bears and nurtures monstrous offspring who threaten the gods and the cosmic order. With Loki she is mother of Fenrir, Jörmungandr, and Hel, and in Ironwood she breeds wolf-shaped giants, including the moon-devouring Mánagarmr and the brood of Fenrir. This reproductive capacity is framed as a source of future catastrophe rather than a neutral maternal role. Attested in Völuspá hin skamma (Fenrir’s mother), Prose Edda Gylfaginning (mother of Fenrir, Jörmungandr, Hel), and Völuspá/Gylfaginning Ironwood passages (old woman/witch breeding Fenrir’s brood and Mánagarmr). Her generative role is described but not mechanistically detailed; there is no explicit magical technique beyond being a witch and giantess. The texts do not attribute to her direct control over her offspring once born, nor do they show her intervening in events of Ragnarök. Some sources restrict her motherhood to Fenrir alone (Poetic Edda), while Snorri’s Prose Edda extends it to Jörmungandr and Hel. The identification of the Ironwood witch with Angrboða is widely accepted but not explicit in the texts, so her role as breeder of Mánagarmr and other wolves is interpretive rather than firmly attested.

Witchcraft and troll-wife status Angrboða is described as a witch (seiðkona-like figure) and as a troll-wife dwelling in Ironwood. This implies knowledge of magic, prophecy, or seiðr, and a capacity to participate in the darker, liminal forms of Norse sorcery associated with giants and outcasts. Prose Edda Gylfaginning (Ironwood witch, Ironwood-women), Poetic Edda Völuspá (old woman in Ironwood), later scholarly glosses identifying Angrboða with these figures. The Eddas do not narrate specific spells, rituals, or feats performed by Angrboða. Her witchcraft is implied by her designation and by her role in breeding monstrous wolves, but no explicit magical acts are described. Some scholars caution against over-reading the witch designation, noting that ‘witch’ in Snorri’s paraphrase may be a generic term for a dangerous female giant rather than a technical seiðr practitioner. Others emphasize the seiðr connotations and link her to broader traditions of giantesses practicing magic. The lack of detailed narrative leaves the question open.

Indirect cosmic destruction through offspring Angrboða’s most consequential ‘ability’ is indirect: through her children, she becomes a source of cosmic destruction. Fenrir devours Odin and contributes to the ruin of the gods; Jörmungandr poisons Thor and encircles the world; Hel rules the dead and receives Baldr, whose death precipitates Ragnarök; Mánagarmr devours the moon and reddens the heavens with blood. The gods’ fear of her ‘nature’ reflects this anticipated impact. Prose Edda Gylfaginning (prophecies of mischief and disaster from her children), Poetic Edda Völuspá (Fenrir’s brood, Mánagarmr, cosmic bloodshed). Angrboða herself is not depicted as acting directly in Ragnarök; her role is genealogical and anticipatory. The texts do not show her making strategic decisions or commanding her offspring. Some modern retellings elevate her into an active antagonist or mastermind of Ragnarök, but this is adaptation-specific and not supported by the medieval sources, which keep her largely offstage.

Accounts

In a brief stanza, the poem notes that Loki sired the wolf (Fenrir) on Angrboða and begot Sleipnir on the stallion Svaðilfari. The stanza characterizes Angrboða as a witch and as particularly evil, emphasizing the ominous nature of the union that produced Fenrir. The narrative context is genealogical rather than fully story-driven: the poem lists monstrous and significant beings and their parentage.

Völuspá describes an old woman sitting in Ironwood, east of Midgard, nurturing the offspring of Fenrir. Among them is one in monstrous guise who will snatch the moon, feed on the flesh of the dead, and redden the gods’ halls with gore, causing the sun to darken and storms to rage. Snorri paraphrases this in Gylfaginning, describing a witch in Ironwood who bears many wolf-shaped giants, from whom the mighty Moon-Hound (Mánagarmr) will arise to devour the moon and be filled with the flesh of dead men.

In Gylfaginning, Snorri Sturluson presents Angrboða as a giantess in Jötunheimr with whom Loki fathers three children: the wolf Fenrir, the Midgard serpent Jörmungandr, and Hel, ruler of the dead. The gods learn of prophecies that great mischief and disaster will arise from these siblings. Fearing their nature and their parents’ nature, the gods take measures: they cast Jörmungandr into the sea, send Hel to rule over Niflheim, and attempt to bind Fenrir. Angrboða herself remains offstage, but her role as mother of the brood is central to the narrative’s explanation of the monsters’ origins.

In Baldrs draumar, Odin rides to the underworld to consult a dead seeress about Baldr’s fate. At one point he accuses her: ‘No wise-woman art thou, nor wisdom hast; of giants three the mother art thou.’ Some scholars suggest this may allude to Angrboða as mother of three giant children (Fenrir, Jörmungandr, Hel), though the poem does not name her. The seeress’s identity remains ambiguous, and the line may instead refer to another giantess or function as a generic insult.

Content Notes

Angrboða’s children are central agents of violence in Norse eschatology. Fenrir devours Odin; Jörmungandr poisons Thor; Mánagarmr devours the moon and is described as feeding on the flesh of dead men, reddening the gods’ halls with gore. These acts are framed as inevitable outcomes of prophecies tied to Angrboða’s brood.

Scholars such as John Lindow and Carolyne Larrington discuss the apocalyptic imagery of Völuspá and the role of monstrous offspring in Ragnarök, noting how Angrboða’s lineage embodies the destructive forces that overturn the gods’ order.

Prose Edda Gylfaginning (Fenrir, Jörmungandr, Hel, Mánagarmr); Poetic Edda Völuspá (Ironwood and moon-snatcher).

Angrboða is characterized as a witch and as ‘most evil’, with the gods expecting evil from her ‘nature’ as a giantess. Her role as mother of monsters contributes to a pattern in Norse myth where female figures associated with giants, witchcraft, and liminal spaces are framed as sources of danger and chaos.

Feminist and gender-focused readings of Norse myth highlight how giantesses and witches are often depicted as threatening, with their reproductive and magical capacities linked to disorder. Angrboða exemplifies this pattern, though the sparse textual record leaves much interpretive space.

Poetic Edda Völuspá hin skamma (witch alone seemed most evil); Prose Edda Gylfaginning (evil expected from her nature).

Through her daughter Hel, Angrboða is indirectly associated with the realm of the dead. Hel’s reception of Baldr and rule over those who die of sickness or old age situate Angrboða’s lineage within the management of death and the boundary between life and afterlife.

Studies of Hel and the Norse underworld note the significance of her parentage by Loki and Angrboða, framing their union as a mythic explanation for the existence of a realm of death outside Valhalla and the heroic afterlife.

Prose Edda Gylfaginning (Hel’s parentage and domain); Poetic Edda Baldrs draumar (Baldr’s descent to Hel).

What We Don't Know

The surviving medieval sources provide very limited information about Angrboða’s personality, motives, and direct actions; she appears primarily as a genealogical node. No securely identified archaeological or iconographic representations of Angrboða are known, leaving her visual depiction largely a matter of modern artistic imagination. The identification of the Ironwood witch and the seeress in Baldrs draumar with Angrboða remains speculative, and the original scope of her motherhood (Fenrir alone vs. three monsters) cannot be definitively reconstructed beyond Snorri’s systematization. Many modern expansions of her role—additional children, detailed backstory, active participation in Ragnarök—are adaptation-specific and not attested in the medieval corpus. Rights and access limitations mean that some critical editions and specialized articles could not be consulted directly here, and non-English scholarship may contain further nuance not reflected in this dossier.

A way to hold it in your mind

In the shadowed forest called Ironwood, east of the human world, an old giantess sits among wolves. She does not swing a sword or hurl thunderbolts; instead, she quietly breeds monsters. From her union with Loki come the wolf that will devour Odin, the serpent that girdles the world, and the pale ruler of the dead. Her name is Angrboða—‘she who brings grief’—and though the surviving texts grant her only a handful of lines, those lines anchor the genealogy of the apocalypse. To follow her trail is to watch Norse myth build the end of the world out of a single, ominous mother.
