Who It Is

In the shadowed forest called Ironwood, east of the human world, an old giantess sits among wolves. She does not swing a sword or hurl thunderbolts; instead, she quietly breeds monsters. From her union with Loki come the wolf that will devour Odin, the serpent that girdles the world, and the pale ruler of the dead. Her name is Angrboða—‘she who brings grief’—and though the surviving texts grant her only a handful of lines, those lines anchor the genealogy of the apocalypse. To follow her trail is to watch Norse myth build the end of the world out of a single, ominous mother.

Attested: the texts emphasize her role as mother of beings prophesied to bring ‘great mischief and disaster’ to the gods. The gods expect evil from her ‘nature’ as a giantess and from Loki’s nature as a trickster. She is described as a witch (seiðkona-like figure) who breeds wolf-shaped giants in Ironwood. Her behavior is thus framed as generative and ominous: she nurtures the brood of Fenrir and his kin, enabling the rise of cosmic predators. Powers
Monstrous motherhood and breeding of wolf-shaped giants

Angrboða’s primary ‘power’ in the sources is generative: she bears and nurtures monstrous offspring who threaten the gods and the cosmic order. With Loki she is mother of Fenrir, Jörmungandr, and Hel, and in Ironwood she breeds wolf-shaped giants, including the moon-devouring Mánagarmr and the brood of Fenrir. This reproductive capacity is framed as a source of future catastrophe rather than a neutral maternal role.

Limit: Her generative role is described but not mechanistically detailed; there is no explicit magical technique beyond being a witch and giantess. The texts do not attribute to her direct control over her offspring once born, nor do they show her intervening in events of Ragnarök.

Witchcraft and troll-wife status

Angrboða is described as a witch (seiðkona-like figure) and as a troll-wife dwelling in Ironwood. This implies knowledge of magic, prophecy, or seiðr, and a capacity to participate in the darker, liminal forms of Norse sorcery associated with giants and outcasts.

Limit: The Eddas do not narrate specific spells, rituals, or feats performed by Angrboða. Her witchcraft is implied by her designation and by her role in breeding monstrous wolves, but no explicit magical acts are described.

Indirect cosmic destruction through offspring

Angrboða’s most consequential ‘ability’ is indirect: through her children, she becomes a source of cosmic destruction. Fenrir devours Odin and contributes to the ruin of the gods; Jörmungandr poisons Thor and encircles the world; Hel rules the dead and receives Baldr, whose death precipitates Ragnarök; Mánagarmr devours the moon and reddens the heavens with blood. The gods’ fear of her ‘nature’ reflects this anticipated impact.

Limit: Angrboða herself is not depicted as acting directly in Ragnarök; her role is genealogical and anticipatory. The texts do not show her making strategic decisions or commanding her offspring.

Accounts I
Loki and Angrboða: birth of FenrirPoetic Edda, Völuspá hin skamma (Short Völuspá, within Hyndluljóð)

13th century compilation, drawing on earlier oral myth

In a brief stanza, the poem notes that Loki sired the wolf (Fenrir) on Angrboða and begot Sleipnir on the stallion Svaðilfari. The stanza characterizes Angrboða as a witch and as particularly evil, emphasizing the ominous nature of the union that produced Fenrir. The narrative context is genealogical rather than fully story-driven: the poem lists monstrous and significant beings and their parentage. The birth of Fenrir sets in motion the chain of events that will culminate in Ragnarök, as the gods later bind the wolf and fear his prophesied role in Odin’s death. Angrboða’s reputation as a witch and mother of the wolf contributes to the gods’ expectation of evil from her offspring.

The giantess in Ironwood and the brood of FenrirPoetic Edda, Völuspá; Prose Edda, Gylfaginning (paraphrase)

13th century compilation and prose redaction

Völuspá describes an old woman sitting in Ironwood, east of Midgard, nurturing the offspring of Fenrir. Among them is one in monstrous guise who will snatch the moon, feed on the flesh of the dead, and redden the gods’ halls with gore, causing the sun to darken and storms to rage. Snorri paraphrases this in Gylfaginning, describing a witch in Ironwood who bears many wolf-shaped giants, from whom the mighty Moon-Hound (Mánagarmr) will arise to devour the moon and be filled with the flesh of dead men. The Ironwood brood, especially Mánagarmr, contributes to the cosmic upheaval of Ragnarök by devouring celestial bodies and spreading bloodshed. The passage reinforces the idea that the monstrous lineage stemming from Fenrir—and by extension from Angrboða—is central to the world’s end.

Accounts II
Angrboða as mother of three monstersProse Edda, Gylfaginning

Early 13th century

In Gylfaginning, Snorri Sturluson presents Angrboða as a giantess in Jötunheimr with whom Loki fathers three children: the wolf Fenrir, the Midgard serpent Jörmungandr, and Hel, ruler of the dead. The gods learn of prophecies that great mischief and disaster will arise from these siblings. Fearing their nature and their parents’ nature, the gods take measures: they cast Jörmungandr into the sea, send Hel to rule over Niflheim, and attempt to bind Fenrir. Angrboða herself remains offstage, but her role as mother of the brood is central to the narrative’s explanation of the monsters’ origins. The gods’ actions toward Angrboða’s children shape the cosmological landscape: Jörmungandr encircles the world, Hel rules the dead, and Fenrir’s binding leads to Tyr’s maiming and Odin’s eventual death. Angrboða’s motherhood thus becomes a key explanatory node for the forces that will destroy the gods.

Possible allusion in Baldrs draumarPoetic Edda, Baldrs draumar

13th century compilation

In Baldrs draumar, Odin rides to the underworld to consult a dead seeress about Baldr’s fate. At one point he accuses her: ‘No wise-woman art thou, nor wisdom hast; of giants three the mother art thou.’ Some scholars suggest this may allude to Angrboða as mother of three giant children (Fenrir, Jörmungandr, Hel), though the poem does not name her. The seeress’s identity remains ambiguous, and the line may instead refer to another giantess or function as a generic insult. If the line does refer to Angrboða, it would place her in the underworld and link her to prophetic knowledge about Baldr’s death and Ragnarök. However, the ambiguity means this cannot be treated as securely attested narrative about her.
