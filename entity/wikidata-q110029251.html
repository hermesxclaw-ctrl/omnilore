Who It Is

Before there were dynasties, before the Xia poured the first bronze, there was Yao — the ruler so virtuous that later philosophers made him the measure of what a sovereign should be. He watched the sun and stars to give farmers a calendar, turned away his own flawed son to give the throne to a farmer named Shun, and ordered a flawed minister to hold back a flood that was drowning the world. Whether he was a man, a memory, or a moral argument is still debated.

Attested personality is entirely ethical-exemplary: Yaodian opens 'Yao was reverent, intelligent, accomplished, sincere, and mild' (qinming wensiyou). Core traits: filial piety, diligence, humility, receptivity to counsel, self-abnegation (yielding throne to worth over blood), concern for the people's suffering during the flood. He tests candidates for succession rather than imposing his son. He listens to the Four Mountains (Siyue) and commoners. In Mencius he is the model of benevolent government (renzheng). Powers
Sagely Virtue and Moral Resonance (De)

Yao's primary 'power' is de - charismatic virtue that pacifies peoples and attracts worthies. Yaodian says his virtue 'shone to the four quarters and reached heaven and earth.' This is not magic but a political cosmology where virtue harmonizes nature.

Limit: Virtue does not prevent natural disasters (flood persists); requires correct ministers to implement. Cannot reform his son Danzhu's character.

Astronomical and Calendrical Regulation

Commands Xi and He (Xihe) clans to observe celestial phenomena: sun's rising/setting, star culminations (Niao, Huo, Xu, Mao), intercalary month to fix 366-day year. Establishes agricultural calendar.

Limit: Requires specialist lineages; Yao delegates rather than observes himself. Calendar still drifts without intercalation.

Discernment and Personnel Selection

Ability to recognize worth and unworthiness: rejects Danzhu and Gun despite recommendations, identifies Shun among commoners through testing (marrying his daughters, observing filial piety).

Limit: Initial reliance on counsel of Four Mountains who recommend Danzhu and Gun; Yao must be persuaded and tests over years/decades. Not omniscient.

Sovereign Authority: Banishment and Execution

Power to exile or execute: banishes Gun to Feather Mountain (Yushan) for failing flood control; exiles Danzhu to Danshui; punishes the Four Evils in some traditions (though often attributed to Shun).

Limit: Punishment is post-failure and debated as just vs. harsh; Yao's authority shared with council (Siyue).

Cultural Invention Patronage (Go/Weiqi)

Tradition credits Yao with creating the game of Go to improve his stupid son Danzhu's intellect. Not in earliest classics; appears in Zhang Hua's Bowuzhi (3rd c. CE) and later encyclopedias.

Limit: Adaptation-specific/folkloric; no Warring States attestation. Danzhu remains unimproved in most versions.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No contemporary portrait exists. Transmitted texts describe Yao not by physical features but by moral physiognomy. Shiji and Yaodian call him 'like the sun' and 'divinely intelligent' (qinming). Later portraiture (Han murals, Ming/Qing popular prints, temple statues at Yao Temple in Linfen) shows Yao as a mature bearded sovereign in imperial mianfu (twelve-tasseled crown, dark robes), often with a benevolent, aged face, sometimes holding a sceptr

Symbols — Symbols: sun and calendrical instruments (gui sundial, astronomical observation terrace); the Tang polity; the act of abdication (rang). Iconography links Yao to astronomy - commissioning Xi and He to observe sun, moon, stars and regulate seasons. Associated with the invention/patronage of the board game Go (weiqi) made to teach his son Danzhu, and with drums and bells for remonstrance (Yaos drum for criticism). Color association: yellow/earth vi

Domains — Domain: ideal kingship, astronomy/calendar, flood management, moral legitimacy, abdication system (shanrang). Habitat: capital at Tang (identified traditionally with modern Linfen/Taosi, Shanxi, or alternatively Pingyang), later at Tao? Yao's realm is 'All Under Heaven' (tianxia) centered on the Central Plain. Cosmology: Yao rules by virtue that harmonizes Heaven and humanity; his astronomical reforms align human time with celestia

Accounts I
The Canon of Yao: Ordering the Heavens and the CalendarShangshu Yaodian

Warring States textual core, claiming high antiquity

Yao, residing at Tang, commands the brothers Xi and He to dwell at four directional extremes to observe the sun, stars, and seasons. Xi Zhong at Yuyi observes the sunrise and the Bird Star to fix spring equinox (day and night equal, stars Niao). Xi Shu at Nanjiao observes summer solstice and the Fire Star. He Zhong at Meidu observes autumn equinox and the Void Star. He Shu at Yudu observes winter solstice and the Mao Star. He fixes the year at 366 days with an intercalary month to correct the four seasons and enable agriculture. The narrative establishes Yao as the founder of Chinese calendrical science and agrarian order. Provides ideological charter for imperial astronomy (later Imperial Astronomical Bureau). Becomes model for ruler harmonizing heaven and humanity. Basis for later claim that Taosi observatory is Yao's.

Seeking a Successor and Testing ShunShangshu Yaodian, Shiji Wudi Benji, Mencius 5A5-6

Warring States to Western Han

Aged Yao seeks one who can succeed him and manage the flood. The Four Mountains (Siyue) recommend Danzhu, Yao's son, but Yao rejects him as 'insincere and quarrelsome.' They then recommend Gun, whom Yao doubts but appoints under pressure. After Gun fails for nine years, Siyue recommends Shun, a commoner renowned for filial piety despite a cruel father (Gusou) and brother (Xiang) who tried to kill him. Yao tests Shun by marrying his two daughters Ehuang and Nüying to him and observing his conduct, entrusting him with administration for 20-30 years before abdicating. Establishes the shanrang (abdication) ideal: throne goes to worth, not blood. Central to Confucian political philosophy and later debates about legitimacy. Shun becomes the model successor.

Gun's Failure and the Great FloodYaodian, Shiji, Shanhaijing Haiwai Beijin

Warring States to Han

During Yao's reign, 'the waters assailed the heavens, encircling mountains and overtopping hills.' Yao asks who can control it. Siyue recommends Gun (father of Yu the Great). Yao hesitates, saying Gun disobeys orders and ruins his clan, but appoints him. For nine years Gun uses damming and diking (blocking) and fails. Yao then removes him; Shun later executes or banishes Gun to Feather Mountain (Yushan) where he dies or transforms into a yellow bear/turtle. Yao then tasks Yu, Gun's son, who succeeds by dredging. Explains transition from Yao to Yu via flood control; legitimizes Yu's later dynasty (Xia) through merit. Establishes contrasting methods: blocking vs. channeling.

Accounts II
The Rejection and Exile of DanzhuShiji Wudi Benji, Bamboo Annals, Mencius, Bowuzhi

Warring States to Jin

Yao's son Danzhu (Zhu) is described as obstinate, lacking virtue, fond of quarreling and games. Yao refuses to pass the throne to him, instead yielding to Shun. In Shiji, Yao educates Danzhu but finds him unworthy. In Bamboo Annals, Yao banishes Danzhu to Danshui (south) and enfeoffs him as lord of Tang or Fang. Folklore adds Yao invented Go to teach Danzhu, who still failed. After Yao's death, some say Danzhu briefly contested Shun's succession with help of dissatisfied lineages, but was defeated. Moral tale against hereditary privilege; justifies meritocracy. Explains why Yao's line did not found a dynasty. Provides etiology for Go.

Xu You and the Refusal of the WorldZhuangzi, Shiji, Lunheng

Warring States (Zhuangzi) to Han

Yao, seeking a worthy successor, offers the throne to the hermit Xu You, who lives at Mount Ji and washes his ears after hearing the offer, declaring he has no need for the world, as a wren needs only a twig. In Zhuangzi's 'Rang Wang' (Yielding the Throne) chapter, Xu You refuses, saying the world is already governed and he will not replace a skilled cook. Yao respects his integrity and instead finds Shun. The story contrasts Confucian rulership with Daoist reclusion. Creates archetype of the eremitic sage who refuses power; influences Chinese literati ideals of reclusion. Used to critique ambition.

Death, Burial, and the Contested SuccessionShiji, Bamboo Annals, Shanhaijing, Liji

Warring States to Han

Yao reigns for 70-100 years (70 in Yaodian test period, 98-100 in Shiji, 58 in Bamboo Annals Ancient version). He dies at 118 (Shiji) and is buried at Guilin? Traditions place his tomb at Yaoling on Mount Gu (Shanxi) or at Guiji. After his death, Shun observes three years mourning and yields nominally to Danzhu, but the people follow Shun, confirming Heaven's choice. Bamboo Annals' darker variant: Shun imprisoned Yao at Taoyang and seized power, and Danzhu was exiled, suggesting not peaceful abdication but coup. Funerary cult at Yaomiao (Yao Temple) in Linfen persists. Succession narrative becomes template for dynastic founding myths and later debates about legitimacy of usurpation vs. abdication.

Yao is a proven historical emperor who ruled China in 2356-2255 BCE with exact dates. — Dates come from Han chronology (Shiji, Bamboo Annals) retrocalculated centuries later. No contemporary inscription confirms Yao; modern historians treat dates as traditional, not verified. Taosi association is hypothesis, not proof.

Yao invented the game of Go (weiqi) historically. — Go invention is a late etiological legend first attested in 3rd-century CE Bowuzhi, not in Warring States classics. Yaodian and Shiji never mention it. It was created to moralize Danzhu's stupidity.

Yao, the Yao people (Mien), and the Yao language are all named after Emperor Yao. — No etymological link. 'Yao' for the Mien people comes from a different Chinese character (瑶/瑤, later 傜) meaning 'precious' or earlier ethnonym, unrelated to 堯 (Emperor Yao). The African Yao people/language and Trinidad Yao language are entirely unrelated homonyms via romanization.

Yao was a god with supernatural powers like controlling weather. — In earliest texts Yao is a sage-king whose 'powers' are moral and administrative (virtue, calendar, personnel). Supernatural birth (dragon conception, 14-month pregnancy) and deification are Han apocryphal embellishments, not core Yaodian portrayal. He delegates flood control and astronomy to ministers.

The French film 'Yao' (2018) and Disney's character Yao are adaptations of Emperor Yao. — Both are unrelated homonyms: the film is about a Senegalese boy named Yao, the Disney character is a Chinese soldier whose name is a common modern given name. Neither references the sage-emperor.
