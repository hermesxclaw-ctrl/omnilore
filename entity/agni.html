The Approach

In the beginning, there was neither day nor night, only the god Prajapati. From his forehead sprang Agni, the first fire, and with him came light, and with light came the division of time itself. Agni is not merely a god of fire; he is the mouth of the gods, the messenger who carries human offerings to the divine, the witness to sacred vows, and the transformative power that burns away ignorance to reveal knowledge. His presence is felt in the crackling hearth, the lightning's flash, and the sun's life-giving rays, a tripartite existence that binds the cosmos together.

Content advisory. Contains descriptions of ritual sacrifice, including animal sacrifice in ancient Vedic practices. Discusses the consumption of offerings by fire and the cremation of the dead. No graphic violence or sexual content.

Etymology

Sanskrit अग्नि (Agni) derives from the Proto-Indo-European *h₁n̥gʷnis, meaning 'fire', with cognates in Latin (ignis), Lithuanian (ugnis), and Slavic languages. Ancient Indian grammarians offered alternative derivations: from the root 'aj' (to drive) meaning 'nimble, agile'; from 'agri' (first) referring to the first in the universe to arise; or from a combination of roots meaning 'to go', 'to shine/burn', and 'to lead'.

Cultural Ownership

Hinduism, with significant presence in Buddhism and Jainism; part of the broader Indo-European fire deity complex.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars connect Agni to the Albanian fire god Enji and other Indo-European fire deities, but this is a reconstruction, not a direct historical link. In later Hinduism, Agni is sometimes identified with Rudra or Śiva in his destructive aspect, but this is a theological interpretation, not a universal equivalence.

Description

Appearance

Agni is typically depicted as a red or ruddy man with two faces, three legs, seven arms, and seven tongues of flame. He rides a ram or a chariot drawn by red horses. His hair, eyes, and beard are fiery, and he carries a flaming spear or a sacrificial ladle. In some depictions, he has a pot-belly and is accompanied by his consort Svāhā.

Symbols & Iconography

Fire, the sacrificial altar (vedi), the sacred ladle (sruva), the flaming spear, the ram, the color red, the southeast direction, the number seven (seven tongues, seven flames).

Behaviour & Personality

Agni is characterized as a voracious consumer, a messenger, a purifier, and a witness. He is described as youthful, radiant, and swift. He is also seen as a mediator between gods and humans, and a protector of households. He is known to hide and play tricks, but also to be generous with his blessings.

Domains & Habitats

He resides in the southeast quarter, in the fire altar, in the home hearth, in the cremation ground, in lightning, in the sun, and in the waters (where he hides). As a guardian of the southeast, he is often represented in that corner of Hindu temples.

Version Differences

In the Vedas, Agni is a prominent cosmic deity and messenger. In the post-Vedic period, his importance declines as he is internalized as a metaphor for transformative energy and knowledge. In Buddhism, he appears as Aggi in texts and debates. In Jainism, he is associated with fire-bodied beings and the Tejas class of deities.

Agni Hides in the Waters

Source Shatapatha Brahmana 1.2.3.1Culture HinduPeriod Vedic

Summary

Three previous Agnis died while serving as Hotri priests. The fourth Agni, fearing a similar fate, hid in the waters. The deities discovered him and brought him back, making him the fire of the present age.

Participants

Agni

The deities

Consequences

Agni becomes the current fire god, and his hiding explains why fire can be found in water (as latent heat).

Animal Sacrifice

Source Vedic texts, e.g., Shatapatha Brahmana

Description

In the Vedic period, Agni was the recipient of animal sacrifices, including cattle and other animals. The fire consumed the flesh, and the smoke carried the offering to the gods.

Scholarly Context

Animal sacrifice was a central part of Vedic religion, but it declined in later periods, and modern Hindu practice generally avoids it.

Cremation

Source Grihya Sutras, later texts

Description

Agni is the fire of cremation, consuming the dead body. This is a necessary rite in Hindu tradition, releasing the soul from the physical form.

Scholarly Context

Cremation is a standard Hindu practice, and Agni's role is essential but not considered dark in the tradition.

Contradictions & Disputes

Origin of Agni

Position A

Agni was born from Prajapati's forehead (Kaṭhaka Saṃhitā).

Position A — evidence

Kaṭhaka Saṃhitā 6.1

Position B

Agni is self-born or born from the kindling sticks (Rigveda).

Position B — evidence

Rigveda hymns describing Agni as 'born of the kindling sticks'

Scholarly Consensus

Multiple origin myths coexist; no single canonical origin.

Agni's Identity with Other Deities

Position A

Agni is a distinct deity.

Position A — evidence

Vedic texts treat Agni as a separate god.

Position B

Agni is identified with Rudra/Śiva or other gods in some texts.

Position B — evidence

Later texts, e.g., some Puranas, equate Agni with Śiva.

Scholarly Consensus

Agni is a distinct deity in the Vedic period, but later traditions sometimes identify him with other gods.

Agni's Role in the Trinity

Position A

Agni is one of the three cosmic rulers (with Indra and Sūrya).

Position A — evidence

Vedic texts assign Agni dominion over the earth.

Position B

Agni is subordinate to the Trimurti (Brahmā, Viṣṇu, Śiva).

Position B — evidence

Post-Vedic texts relegate Agni to a minor role.

Scholarly Consensus

Agni's importance declines in the post-Vedic period.
