Who It Is

At six-foot-seven, Thor Henry Anderson towered over most men of his era — a Burns, Oregon farm kid who graduated high school at sixteen, played for the Oregon Ducks, survived World War II as an Army Air Forces officer, and then quietly built the modern Gonzaga Bulldogs from a cramped gym into a Division I Big Sky program that would one day become a national power.

Contemporary newspaper accounts (Spokesman-Review, Spokane Daily Chronicle) portray Anderson as low-key, steady, and institutionally loyal — 21 seasons at Gonzaga through losing and rebuilding years, doubling as athletic director from 1953. Described as a builder/administrator rather than a flamboyant tactician. Surprise departure to conference rival Montana State in 1972 suggests ambition or institutional friction, but no primary source explains motive in detail. Later administrative work at NAU (nearly a decade) and service on Fiesta Bowl board indicate reputation for facility management

Accounts I
Burns to Eugene: The Sixteen-Year-Old Graduate and the Tall ForwardEastern Oregon Normal School and University of Oregon athletic/academic records; Burns High School graduation records

1937-1941

Born Thor Henry Anderson December 5, 1920 in Milton-Freewater, eastern Oregon, Anderson graduated from Burns High School in Burns at age 16 in 1937. He played two seasons at Eastern Oregon Normal School in La Grande (now Eastern Oregon University), then transferred to the University of Oregon in Eugene where he played forward at 6 ft 7 in for the Ducks under head coach Howard Hobson, who had led Oregon to the first NCAA championship in 1939. Anderson earned his bachelor's degree in 1941 at age 20 while in graduate school. Established Anderson's credentials as both student and tall forward in Hobson's system; degree and playing experience led directly to first coaching opportunity at age 20.

Baker, War, and the Road to Grants Pass: Building a Prep PowerhouseOregon high-school athletic records; U.S. Army Air Forces service record; Spokane newspaper retrospective (1972 hiring coverage)

1941-1951

While in graduate school at Oregon, Anderson accepted his first head coaching job at Baker High School in eastern Oregon in October 1941. His early coaching career was interrupted by service as an officer in the U.S. Army Air Forces in World War II. He returned to Baker in 1945, then moved west to Medford in 1946 and Grants Pass in 1947. At Grants Pass his 1950 team finished as Oregon state runner-up. Over his prep career he compiled 167-43 (.795) before Gonzaga hired him in April 1951 at age thirty. Prep success (.795) made Anderson an attractive college candidate despite no prior college coaching experience; Gonzaga hired him to replace L. T. Underwood after an 8-22 season.

Twenty-One Years in Spokane: Rebuilding GonzagaGonzaga University Athletic Department records; Sports Reference coaching logs; Spokesman-Review and Spokane Daily Chronicle game coverage; Big Sky Conference history

1951-1972

Gonzaga's previous coach L. T. Underwood resigned after going 8-22 (.267) in 1951 after two seasons. Anderson's first team in 1951-52 went 19-16 (.543), immediate improvement. In 1953 he added athletic director duties. Under Anderson, Gonzaga elevated to NCAA University Division (Division I) in 1958, became a charter member of the Big Sky Conference in 1963, and opened the on-campus Kennedy Pavilion in 1965, replacing off-campus facilities. He was named Big Sky Coach of the Year in 1966. Over 21 seasons he went 290-275 (.513) at Gonzaga, the longest tenure in program history at that time. He stepped down as AD in 1972. Stabilized Gonzaga basketball through its transition to major-college status and conference affiliation; built infrastructure (Kennedy Pavilion) that served until McCarthey Athletic Center (2004). Did not achieve NCAA tournament berth; winning percentage near .500 reflects mid-major building phase before later Gonzaga national prominence under Dan Fitzgerald, Dan Monson, and Mark Few.

Accounts II
The Surprise Move to Bozeman: Two Seasons as a RivalMontana State University Bobcats media guides 1972-1974; Big Sky Conference standings; Spokane newspaper coverage June 1972

1972-1974

Weeks after stepping down as Gonzaga AD in spring 1972, Anderson surprisingly left Gonzaga entirely to become head coach at Montana State University in Bozeman — a Big Sky rival. Contemporary press described the move as surprising given his 21-year identification with Gonzaga. In two seasons with the Bobcats he went 28-24 (.538), a winning record but without a conference title or postseason breakthrough, before departing in 1974. Ended Anderson's head coaching career at 318-299 (.515) overall; opened path for Gonzaga to hire new leadership; demonstrated mobility within the Big Sky coaching fraternity.

Flagstaff and the Dome: Athletic Director in the PinesNorthern Arizona University Athletics history; Walkup Skydome dedication records 1977; Fiesta Bowl board records

1974-1983

In 1974 Anderson left coaching to become athletic director at Northern Arizona University in Flagstaff, another Big Sky school. Over nearly a decade he oversaw the planning and building of the Walkup Skydome (originally Walkup Skydome, now J. Lawrence Walkup Skydome), which opened in 1977 as the world's largest clear-span timber dome and an all-weather home for NAU football and basketball. He also served on the board of directors of the Fiesta Bowl in Tempe during the bowl's growth years. He retired from NAU at the end of 1983 at age 63. Walkup Skydome remains NAU's signature facility and a landmark of 1970s stadium engineering; Anderson's tenure is remembered administratively rather than competitively.

Las Vegas and Gig Harbor: Final InningsLas Vegas Stars (Pacific Coast League) records 1984; Gonzaga baseball history (Larry Koentopp); Gig Harbor obituaries September 2005

1984-2005

After retiring from college athletics, Anderson moved to Las Vegas in 1984 to work in minor-league baseball for the Las Vegas Stars of the Pacific Coast League. The Stars had been the Spokane Indians from 1973 to 1982 and were headed by Larry Koentopp — the former Gonzaga baseball coach Anderson had hired in 1969 and who had succeeded him as Gonzaga AD in 1972. Anderson and his wife Betty, married in 1943, later retired to Gig Harbor, Washington. He died there September 5, 2005 at age 84 of an aortic aneurysm. Closed the circle on a career that linked Spokane, Las Vegas, and the Pacific Northwest; obituaries in Spokane and Tacoma-area papers summarized his role as Gonzaga's stabilizing builder before the program's later national rise.

Hank Anderson was the coach who made Gonzaga a national powerhouse and took them to the NCAA tournament — Anderson stabilized Gonzaga through its transition to Division I (1958) and Big Sky membership (1963) and built Kennedy Pavilion (1965), but went 290-275 (.513) with no NCAA tournament bids. Gonzaga's national emergence came later under Dan Fitzgerald (late 1970s-1990s), Dan Monson, and especially Mark Few (1999-present). Anderson's era was a building phase, not a tournament era.

Hank Anderson is only a fictional character from the video game Detroit: Become Human — The most internet-visible 'Hank Anderson' today is indeed the fictional Detroit police lieutenant (2018), voiced by Clancy Brown, who trends in fan wikis and search results. However, Thor Henry 'Hank' Anderson (1920-2005) was a real, well-documented college coach and AD for over 30 years before the game existed. The shared name causes search-engine conflation.

Anderson had a losing career record — His Gonzaga record was near .500 (290-275, .513) and his Montana State record was winning (28-24, .538), for a career winning record of 318-299 (.515). He was not a sub-.500 coach overall, though his Gonzaga winning percentage was modest by modern Gonzaga standards.
