Who It Is

In the moonlit valleys of the Basque Country, where ancient oaks stand sentinel over myths older than any written word, there shines a goddess whose name itself means 'the light of the dead.' Ilargi, also called Ile-kete, is the lunar deity of a people who saw in the moon's phases the eternal rhythm of life, death, and rebirth. Yet her story is a mosaic of fragments—some attested in medieval Christian condemnations, others whispered in folk charms and place-names—leaving modern seekers to piece together a figure who may be the oldest face of the divine feminine in the Pyrenees.

As a celestial deity, Ilargi's behavior is inferred from the moon's cycles. She is benign or neutral, associated with fertility, growth, and the rhythm of time. In some folk tales, the moon is a protective presence, but also capricious, causing madness or influencing human behavior.

Powers
Lunar influence

As the moon, Ilargi controls the phases of the moon, which in Basque folk belief affect tides, crops, and human health. She is said to have the power to influence growth, particularly of plants and livestock.

Limit: Not explicitly defined; the moon's influence is natural rather than miraculous.

Illumination of the night

Ilargi provides light in the darkness, a protective and guiding force for travelers and night workers.

Limit: Only during the night, and wanes with the lunar cycle.

Fertility and regeneration

The moon's cycle is linked to fertility, both of women and the earth. Ilargi is thus a goddess of fertility and rebirth, as the moon dies and is reborn each month.

Limit: No direct ancient evidence; this is inferred from comparative mythology.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No direct description of Ilargi's appearance survives. She is implicitly the moon itself, a luminous disk in the night sky. Some modern interpretations depict her as a beautiful woman with long hair, but this is not attested in any ancient source.

Symbols — The moon, particularly the full moon and its phases. The crescent moon may be associated. No specific symbols are documented beyond the moon itself.

Domains — The sky, specifically the moon. Her domain is the night sky, and she is associated with the sea (tides), agriculture (planting cycles), and human life (menstrual cycles, birth).

Accounts I
The Moon and the SunReconstructed from Basque proverbs and folk sayings

Unknown; likely ancient

Ilargi and Ekhi (the sun) are siblings or spouses, and they alternate in the sky. The moon often hides from the sun, and their relationship explains day and night. In some versions, the moon is female and the sun is male, in others the reverse. The alternation of day and night is established.

Accounts II
The Moon's Dark SideFolk tales collected by Barandiarán

20th century

The moon is sometimes seen as a dangerous force that can cause madness or misfortune, especially when full. People avoid looking at it directly or sleeping under its light. Fear of the full moon persists in some rural areas.

The Moon and the DeadEtymology and folk beliefs

Unknown

The name 'light of the dead' suggests a connection to the afterlife. The moon may guide souls to the other world or illuminate the path of the dead. The moon is associated with funerary rites and mourning.

Ilargi is a major goddess in Basque mythology with a rich mythology. — Our knowledge of Ilargi is extremely fragmentary; no extensive myths survive. She is primarily known from etymology, place-names, and folk customs.

Ilargi is the same as the Greek Selene or Roman Luna. — Ilargi is a Basque deity with unique characteristics; equating her with Greco-Roman goddesses is a superficial comparison.

The Basque moon was always female. — While the word is feminine, some folk traditions may have depicted the moon as male; the evidence is not conclusive.
