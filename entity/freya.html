Freya

also known asFreyaFreyiaFrejaFreaGefnHörn
Typemythology
CultureNorse
Who It Is
Form

Freyja is described as the most glorious of the goddesses. She rides a chariot drawn by two blue-grey (or 'grey') cats, a gift from Thor according to some traditions. She owns a feathered cloak of falcon feathers (hamr) that allows the wearer to transform into a falcon. Her most famous possession is the glittering golden necklace Brísingamen, forged by four dwarven smiths. She is accompanied by the boar Hildisvíni ('Battle-swine'). When weeping for her absent husband Óðr, her tears turn to red gold on land and amber in the sea. She is frequently depicted with a broad necklace (the archaeological record suggests this was a status symbol worn by elite Iron Age women). In the Schleswig Cathedral fresco (12th century), a cloaked but nude woman riding a large cat appears beside a similar figure riding a distaff, theorized as Freyja and Frigg.

Powers

Seiðr mastery Freyja is the primary divine practitioner of seiðr, a form of magic for seeing and influencing the future. According to Heimskringla, she introduced seiðr to the Æsir after the Æsir-Vanir War, teaching it to Odin himself. Seiðr involves visionary journeys and was considered a feminine art; male practitioners (seiðmenn) risked social stigma (ergi). Freyja's association with this art makes her the patroness of witchcraft and fate-weaving. Heimskringla Ynglinga saga ch. 4; Prose Edda Seiðr is described as having been 'only practiced by the Vanir' before Freyja taught it to the Æsir, implying a cultural boundary. Male practitioners risked accusations of ergi (unmanliness). While Heimskringla credits Freyja with introducing seiðr to the Æsir, some scholars debate whether seiðr was originally Vanir-specific or a broader Norse magical practice.

War-goddess and receiver of the slain Freyja rules Fólkvangr and receives half of all warriors slain in battle ('whenever she rides into battle she gets half the slain'). Her hall Sessrúmnir receives these warriors. This makes her a parallel to Odin's Valhalla function, and scholars connect her role to the valkyries ('choosers of the slain'). Grímnismál, Gylfaginning ch. 24 The exact division mechanism (how half goes to Freyja vs Odin) is not detailed in surviving sources. Some scholars debate whether Fólkvangr represents an earlier warrior cult distinct from the Odin-centric Valhalla tradition, or whether the two represent complementary afterlife functions.

Falcon shape (hamr) Freyja possesses a cloak of falcon feathers that enables her to transform into a falcon (hamr). She lends this cloak to Loki in Þrymskviða and to Loki again in Skáldskaparmál ch. 56 to rescue Iðunn. Heimdallr also uses it in the Brísingamen recovery myth. Þrymskviða; Prose Edda Skáldskaparmál The mechanics of transformation are not described in detail. The falcon cloak appears in multiple myths with different borrowers; whether Freyja herself transforms regularly is not explicitly stated beyond the hamr concept.

Gold-weeping Freyja cries tears of red gold for her absent husband Óðr. This mythological topos makes gold itself a metonym for Freyja: skalds use 'Freyja's weeping' and 'Freyja's tears' to mean gold. The tears turn to amber in the sea. Prose Edda Gylfaginning ch. 29; Skáldskaparmál ch. 32, 36, 49 Only attested in literary sources from the 13th century onward. None significant; the motif is consistent across sources.

Ruler of Fólkvangr and Sessrúmnir Freyja's heavenly field Fólkvangr ('field of the host') and hall Sessrúmnir ('filled with many seats') serve as her afterlife domain. Scholarly theory posits Sessrúmnir as functionally equivalent to Valhalla, reflecting a separate warrior-initiation tradition. Grímnismál, Gylfaginning ch. 24 Only brief stanzaic attestations survive; the full cosmology of Fólkvangr is not preserved. Why there are two heroic paradises (Fólkvangr and Valhalla) is debated: possibly different forms of warrior initiation, or separate cultural strata.

Accounts

Freyja passes by dwarfs smithying a golden necklace and desires it. The dwarfs stipulate that she must spend a night with each of them. She agrees. Loki, under Odin's service, discovers the affair and tells Odin. Odin commands Loki to steal the necklace. Loki transforms into a fly, infiltrates Freyja's locked bower by squeezing through a gable hole, and bites her cheek as a flea to keep her asleep while he removes Brísingamen. The next morning Freyja discovers the theft and confronts Odin. He refuses to return it unless she engineers an eternal battle between two kings of forty each, where the slain repeatedly rise to fight, until a Christian smites them. She accepts, and the necklace is returned.

Thor wakes to find Mjöllnir missing. Loki and Thor discover that the jötunn Þrymr has hidden it and demands Freyja as his bride. When they tell Freyja, she reacts with fury: 'I would be the most man-mad of women if I journeyed with thee to the home of the giants.' The halls shake, Brísingamen falls from her neck. The gods hold a thing and Heimdallr proposes dressing Thor as Freyja, with Loki as his bridesmaid. Thor objects but is overruled. Dressed as Freyja, Thor consumes an ox and eight salmon and drinks three casks of mead, alarming Þrymr. When Þrymr tries to kiss 'Freyja' beneath the veil, he finds terrifying eyes. Loki makes excuses. Mjöllnir is produced for the 'wedding blessing,' Thor grabs it, and slaughters the jötnar.

In Völuspá, a figure named Gullveig ('Gold-drink') enters the hall of Hár (Odin) and is speared by the Æsir, burnt three times, and thrice reborn. After her third rebirth she is called Heiðr and begins practicing seiðr. Starting with Gabriel Turville-Petre, scholars including Simek, Orchard, and Lindow have argued that Gullveig/Heiðr is Freyja, and that her mistreatment by the Æsir precipitated the Æsir-Vanir War. This theory connects Freyja's introduction of seiðr to the Vanir as the spark of cosmic conflict.

Freyja rides her cat-chariot to the home of the jötunn Hyndla to help her faithful servant Óttar trace his ancestry. She transforms Óttar into her boar Hildisvíni for the journey. By means of flattery and threats of death by fire, Freyja compels Hyndla to reveal Óttar's genealogy so he may claim his inheritance. The poem praises Óttar for building a hörgr (stone altar) and making blót (sacrifices) to Freyja, illustrating reciprocal divine-human relationships in Norse religion.

Freyja is married to Óðr, a god of desire and inspiration whose name is etymologically linked to Odin ('mind, wit, soul'). Óðr goes on long travels and is frequently absent. Freyja stays behind weeping, shedding tears of red gold. She searches for him 'among strange peoples' and adopts numerous names during her wanderings: Gefn, Hörn, Mardöll, Sýr, Vanadís. Heimskringla reports that after Freyr's death, Freyja is the last survivor among the Æsir and Vanir, keeping up sacrifices and becoming famous. Her name becomes the honorific for all women of rank (frúvor, húsfreyja).

Under Christianization, Freyja's erotic qualities made her a target for missionaries: 'she is called a whore and a harlot by the holy men.' Her fertility and vegetation functions were transferred to the Virgin Mary, and plants once named for her were renamed. Yet she persisted: in 18th-century Iceland, she was invoked via magical staves; in 19th-century Småland, Sweden, farmers observed her in sheet lightning checking rye ripeness. In Värend, she shook apple trees at Christmas for good harvests. As late as 1880, rural Swedes left apples for her and warned against leaving ploughs outside because she might sit on them and ruin them. Modern Asatru practitioners honor her as goddess of fertility, abundance, beauty, and protection.

Content Notes

Under Christianization, Freyja's erotic and fertile aspects were systematically demonized. Missionaries called her 'a whore' and 'a harlot,' contrasting her with the Christian ideal of an asexual Virgin Mary. Her functions in vegetation protection and childbirth assistance were transferred to Mary, while her sexual autonomy was condemned as licentiousness. This represents a deliberate theological strategy to suppress female religious authority.

Näsström documents how Freyja became 'a particular target under Christianization' precisely because her erotic power conflicted with Christian sexual norms. Plant renaming (Freyja's tears → Virgin Mary's herb) is evidence of this cultural overlay.

Näsström 1995, Britt-Mari; article text citing missionary rhetoric

In the late euhemerized Sörla þáttr, Freyja is Odin's concubine who barters sex with four dwarfs to obtain Brísingamen. While framed as a consensual bargain ('whether she liked it better or worse, Freyja agreed'), the narrative treats her sexuality as a commodity to be traded for power. The Christian redactor's moral agenda frames this as corrupting, justifying Loki's theft and Odin's withholding of the necklace.

The text is a late euhemerized Christian-era composition; the sexual transaction may reflect Christian moralizing about pagan goddesses rather than authentic pre-Christian tradition. Earlier sources do not narrate Brísingamen's origin this way.

Sörla þáttr in Flateyjarbók (c. 1387-1394 CE)

Freyja is repeatedly targeted for forced marriage by powerful jötnar. In Gylfaginning ch. 42, the jötnar builder demands Freyja as his bride (along with the sun and moon) as payment for fortifying Asgard. In Þrymskviða, Þrymr demands Freyja as his wife to return Mjöllnir. In Lokasenna, Loki accuses her of promiscuity with all gods and elves. The pattern of Freyja as an unwilling sexual prize reflects cultural anxieties about female autonomy and mixed unions.

The jötnar-as-rapist trope is common in Norse myth; Freyja's fierce refusals ('the halls of the gods shake') make her one of the few goddesses who actively resists these demands rather than passively enduring them.

Gylfaginning ch. 42; Þrymskviða; Lokasenna

Plants named for Freyja across Scandinavia—Freyja's tears, Freyja's hair (Polygala vulgaris), Freyja's distaff (Orion)—were systematically renamed for the Virgin Mary during Christianization. This botanical censorship erased visible pagan devotion from the landscape.

Place-name evidence (Frøihov, Frövi, Härnevi) preserves underground devotion despite surface-level Christian renaming.

Article 'Eponyms' section; Näsström

What We Don't Know

Óðr's independent mythology is almost entirely lost; we only know him through his relationship to Freyja. The original form of the Brísingamen myth (pre-Sörla þáttr) is unattested. The visual appearance of Freyja in pre-Christian art is extremely scarce; the archaeological identifications (Hagebyhöga pendant, Oseberg wagon, Eschwege phalera) are interpretations, not confirmed depictions. Pre-Christian ritual practices specific to Freyja are poorly documented. The exact nature and mechanics of seiðr are debated. Cross-cultural parallels beyond Scandinavia are weak or speculative. Modern Heathenry practices vary widely and cannot be used to reconstruct ancient cult.

A way to hold it in your mind

Freyja is the Norse goddess who defies containment. She is at once the most approachable deity for love and fertility and the most terrifying chooser of the slain, riding a cat-drawn chariot into battle to claim half of all warriors dead on the field. She weeps gold for a husband who vanishes into wanderings, wields magic so potent that Odin himself had to learn it from her, and bartered her body to dwarfs to win the necklace Brísingamen—only to have it stolen by Loki in flea-form while she slept. Christian missionaries tried to erase her by renaming her sacred plants for the Virgin Mary, but 19th-century Swedish farmers still blamed her for lightning and thanked her for apple harvests. She is the lady who became an honorific for noblewomen, the witch who taught sorcery to the king of the gods, and the war-goddess whose field Fólkvangr may once have rivaled Valhalla itself.
