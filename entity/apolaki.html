Who It Is

Before the Spanish burned the scrolls, the Philippines had a sun god who could blind his own sister with a bamboo club and then teach his people to forge swords from the same mountain where he lived. Apolaki is not a distant celestial figure — he is a culture hero who walked down from Mount Arayat to show humanity how to smelt metal, grow rice, and wage war. The scar on the moon's face is his signature.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Apolaki derives from the Filipino root 'apo' (meaning elder, ancestor, or lord) combined with 'laki' (meaning great or large). It thus translates approximately to 'Great Lord' or 'Grand Ancestor.' In Pangasinan tradition the name appears as Anagaoley or Ama-Gaoley, meaning 'Supreme Father.'

Aliases — Apolake, Apo Init, Apolaki Caldera

Form & Signs

Appearance — Apolaki is depicted as a towering, radiant figure wreathed in solar fire. In Kapampangan oral tradition, he is described as a warrior-king bearing a spear and shield, his skin shimmering like molten bronze. He carries the Zabbors — the traditional bamboo clubs used in Kapampangan martial combat. Accounts I
The Quarrel of Apolaki and MayariMayari (Wikipedia) — Kapampangan mythology section; Clark, Jordan (2017), 'Kapampangan Origin of Day & Night – Apolaki', The Aswang Project

In Kapampangan mythology, after Bathala departed the world without a will, his children Apolaki and Mayari quarreled over who would rule the earth alone. They fought with bamboo clubs (Zabbors) until Apolaki struck Mayari in the face, blinding one eye. When he saw his sister stricken, Apolaki took pity and agreed to share rule — Apolaki governing the day with bright, full light, Mayari governing the night with dimmer light due to her scarred eye. This myth explains why the sun is brighter than the moon, and why the moon appears scarred in some phases.

Accounts II
The Chase of Arao and Buan (Sun and Moon)Tala (goddess) (Wikipedia); Philippine Mythology (Wikipedia)

In a related Tagalog narrative, the sun god Arao (widely identified as Apolaki) and the moon goddess Buan (identified as Mayari) both had large families of stars. Buan believed her stars could not survive the heat of Arao, so they agreed to destroy their stars. Arao devoured his, but Buan hid hers in the clouds. Upon discovering this betrayal, Arao is eternally in pursuit of Buan, trying to destroy her. Eclipses are explained by Arao getting close enough to bite her. At dawn Buan hides the stars and brings them forth only when her eldest daughter Tala (the evening and morning star) signals that the sun is too far away to pursue them. This myth accounts for eclipses, the phases of the moon, and the origin of the Tagalog words for bright star (tala), sun (araw), and moon (buwan).

The Cultural Hero: Apolaki Teaches HumanityList of Solar Deities (Wikipedia); List of War Deities (Wikipedia); Mount Arayat (Wikipedia)

In Kapampangan tradition, Apolaki is not merely a celestial sovereign but a culture-bringer. He descended from Mount Arayat to teach early Kapampangan inhabitants metallurgy (the forging of weapons and tools), woodcutting, rice cultivation, and the art of warfare. This transformer-deity role positions Apolaki as the originator of Kapampangan material civilization — a pattern common in Austronesian mythology where high gods double as culture heroes. His association with Mount Arayat (a real stratovolcano in Central Luzon) roots the myth in a tangible landscape feature that Kapampangan communities have inhabited for millennia.
