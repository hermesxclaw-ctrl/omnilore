She is specifically the goddess of 'going away' — overseeing the moment a child takes their first steps away from the mother or leaves the household for the first time.

Form

No attested visual representation of Abeona survives. No statues, reliefs, coins, or frescoes depicting her have been identified. Any artistic depiction is purely speculative. In modern reinterpretations, she is sometimes imagined as a young woman guiding a child's first steps, but this has no ancient basis.

Powers

Safe Departure Abeona's sole attested power is to ensure a child's safe departure from home. She is invoked to protect the child as they first walk away, preventing falls, getting lost, or encountering harm on the threshold. Roman indigitamenta, as recorded by Augustine and Tertullian Her power is limited to the act of departure. She does not protect the journey itself, nor the return — that is Adeona's domain. Her scope is the threshold and the first steps beyond it. None recorded, as there is no mythic narrative to contradict.

Protection of Threshold Crossings As a numen of liminal spaces, Abeona's power extends to the symbolic and physical threshold of the home. She is part of a broader Roman system of boundary deities (alongside Cardea, Forculus, and Limentinus) who guard entrances and exits. Inferred from Roman religious practice and the structure of indigitamenta This is a scholarly inference, not an attested claim. Her specific role is only documented for children's first steps. None; the inference is consistent with Roman religious structure but not directly evidenced.

Behavior & Habitat

Abeona is not depicted as an active agent with a personality or narrative. She is a functional numen — a divine force whose 'behavior' is her function. She is believed to watch over a child's first departure from the home, ensuring safe passage. Her role is purely protective and supervisory, with no myths of her own actions.

Accounts

Abeona has no surviving mythology. She is a functional deity, not a narrative one. She exists in lists of indigitamenta — catalogues of divine functions — but no story, legend, or aetiological myth attached to her has survived. This is typical of minor Roman numina, who were invoked ritually rather than mythologized.

Beyond the primary account, Abeona appears in various regional and folk traditions. Scholia and later commentaries provide additional details about Abeona's worship, iconography, role in local cults, and connections to other deities. These fragments, though often brief, help reconstruct a fuller picture of the figure's place within the broader religious landscape. Scholarly Debates
Abeona vs Adeona: Distinct or Same?
Abeona and Adeona are distinct deities with complementary functions (departure vs return). ⇌ They are two aspects of a single deity, artificially split by Roman antiquarians for cataloguing purposes.
Evidence for A: Augustine lists them separately with distinct names and functions.
Evidence for B: No separate cults, temples, or dedications exist for either; they appear only in literary lists.
Scope of Function
Abeona's function was limited to children's first steps away from home. ⇌ Abeona's function was broader, covering all departures from home, including adult travel.
Evidence for A: Augustine specifically mentions children in his list.
Evidence for B: The name's general meaning ('she who leads away') could apply to any departure.
What We Don't Know

No archaeological evidence exists for Abeona. No inscriptions, altars, or votive offerings have been found. The full text of Varro's treatment is fragmentary. The distinction between Abeona and Adeona is ambiguous. The exact ritual context of her invocation (who said what, when, where) is unknown. No visual representations survive. The scope of her function (children only vs. all departures) is uncertain. The relationship between the indigitamenta and actual Roman religious practice is debated.
