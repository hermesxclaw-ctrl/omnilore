Form

There is no iconographic canon for Ame-no-Ohabari as a separately depicted figure; like most primordial kami it is never described by physical form in the Kojiki. As a weapon it is a totsuka-no-tsurugi, a 'ten-handbreadth' straight sword of archaic double-edged type — the Totsuka-no-Tsurugi article glosses it specifically as a blade with edges 'on both sides of the tip'. The alternation between a plain sword and a personified deity means later illustrated mythologies (and pop-culture versions) usually draw a long, straight, double-edged Chinese-influenced bronze/iron sword or occasionally a sweeping 'heavenly' blade with an ornamental guard; none of this is attested. What is attested is only the class: an unusually long war-sword, longer than a man's two extended hands.

Powers

Basis of divine decapitation As a totsuka (ten-handbreadth) blade, Ame-no-Ohabari is capable of cutting off the head of even a kami of Kagutsuchi's catastrophic power — fire strong enough that its birth burned the genitals of the creator goddess Izanami to death. It severs the immortal fire-god in a single stroke of the grieving Izanagi. Kojiki, Kamiumi section The power is wielded wholly by Izanagi; the sword itself is not an independent agent in this scene and never acts of its own volition while a mere weapon. None within the Kojiki, but the sword's own agency is the later point of dispute (see Motoori-vs-Chamberlain).

Blood as divine seed (therogenesis from the blade) Whatever supernatural property the blade carries is expressed most spectacularly through the blood it spills. From the blood of Kagutsuchi that drips from the sword's tip, its blade, and its handle, nine additional deities spontaneously come into being: from the tip, Iwasaku, Nesaku and Iwa-tsutsu-no-o; from the mid-blade, Mika-haya-hi, Hi-haya-hi and Takemikazuchi (given as Take-mi-kagu-tsuchi-no-o, Takefutsu or Toyofutsu); from the handle, Kuraokami and Kuramitsuha. The blade is thus a kind of divine forge, converting one slain god's body into a pantheon. Kojiki, Kamiumi section (blood lists) The power is conditional on the victim being a slain divine being; the blood, not the steel, does the generative work. There is no attestation of the sword creating gods from an ordinary kill. The Nihon Shoki gives a vaguer blood-genealogy and the exact triads differ slightly between sources; some deity lists (e.g. Futsunushi vs Takemikazuchi's alternate Takefutsu/Toyofutsu identities) are internally redundant.

Personification / sentience By the Conquest-of-Izumo (kuni-yuzuri) episode, the same entity that was a sword has acquired 'the mind and speech of a sentient god'. It does not merely exist as a weapon; it holds a place in the heavenly polity, is named in Amaterasu's war-council, reasons about the divine succession, and makes policy decisions. Kojiki, Conquest of Izumo chapter; Motoori Norinaga's Kojiki-den The degree to which this sentience is original vs. acquired is fiercely contested (Motoori: it is the sword's spirit; Chamberlain: the text supplies no warrant). The sentient deity never uses the sword-blade's destructive power; it acts only through delegation. See Disputed Identifications in identity — this is the single most disputed aspect of the figure.

Genesis of a martial successor (the 'father-sword' role) Ame-no-Ohabari's defining narrative power is genealogical: it is the father of Takemikazuchi, the thunder and sword deity who becomes the chief god of Kashima, the imperial Yamato kingship's pre-eminent war-god, patron of the 'first sumo match' (the Takeminakata bout), and one of the two martial gods whose Futsunomitama sword is enshrined at Isonokami Jingū. Through this fatherhood the sword indirectly underwrites the state-cult of Kashima Shrine and the Nakatomi/Fujiwara clan traditions. Kojiki postscript (Takemikazuchi 'referred ... as the child of Itsu-no-Ohabari') The paternity is asserted in the Kojiki's phrasing but is arguably aetiologised — invented backwards from the war-god's prominence to give him an august heavenly father. Takemikazuchi's own folk-origin (Iwao Ōwa's theory that he began as a maritime clan god of the Ō house, later claimed by the Nakatomi) sits alongside the mythic divine paternity without reconciliation.

Accounts

After the creator couple Izanagi and Izanami give birth to the islands and the various kami, Izanami bears the fire-deity Kagutsuchi (Hi-no-Kagutsuchi/Homusubi). The child's birth-blessing is a catastrophe: the fire burns the mother's genitals and wounds her mortally, and she dies. Overcome by grief and then rage, Izanagi takes his ten-grasp sword — postscripturally named Ame-no-Ohabari, 'Heavenly-Blade-Extended', here also Itsu-no-Ohabari — and beheads his own son. He cuts the corpse into eight pieces, which become eight volcanoes, and from the sword-spilled blood and the severed body further gods swarm into being (see Powers). The slaying marks the entry of death into the world that began with the archipelago's creation, and it is the primal act of divine patricide in the Japanese canon.

The heavenly gods (Amaterasu and Takamusubi, with the counsel of Omoikane) decide to subdue the terrestrial Middle Country (Ashihara no Nakatsukuni) and hand it to Amaterasu's heirs. Takemikazuchi or his father Itsu-no-ohabari is the proposed envoy. The narrative explicitly notes that the sword-deity, which 'previously appeared as a ten-fist sword', 'here has the mind and speech of a sentient god'. Chosen for the task, Ame-no-Ohabari does not go himself: he volunteers his young son Takemikazuchi, who descends with the deity-bird-boat Ame-no-torifune to the shore of Izumo (Inasa beach). There Takemikazuchi plants his own totsuka sword point-down on the wave-crest, perches on it, and demands that Ōkuninushi cede the land. After the local gods' resistance — Kotoshironushi's easy capitulation and Takeminakata's failed strength-bout (the mythic origin of sumo) — Ōkuninushi yields, and the transfer founds the imperial claim on Japan.

Although not itself enshrined, Ame-no-Ohabari stands at the head of the martial divine lineage that later anchors two of Japan's most important shrines: Takemikazuchi at Kashima and (via the sword-born Futsunushi, brother) the associated Katori faith. In the imperial campaigns into the Kantō and Tōhoku, Yamato armies prayed to the Kashima and Katori deities for victory, and the Nakatomi/Fujiwara priestly house claimed Takemikazuchi as its ujigami (clan deity), elevating veneration into Kasuga-taisha at Nara. The sword of Takemikazuchi, Futsunomitama-no-Tsurugi (布都御魂), is enshrined as the central object at Isonokami Jingū — a physical heir to the totsuka-class blade Ame-no-Ohabari represents.

Content Notes

The sword's founding act is Izanagi beheading his own son Kagutsuchi in grief and rage. This is not sanitised in the myth: the creator god, having lost his wife to the fire-child, turns the blade of creation onto his offspring and hacks the body into eight volcano-pieces. The sword is the instrument of a god killing his child.

Scholars read this as the mythic marking of death's entry into the world and the transition from creation to a mortal cosmos; the violence is structural, tied to cosmic order (fire must be bounded), not gratuitous. Motoori Norinaga frames the sword's role reverentially; modern retellings either foreground or bowdlerise the filicide.

Kojiki, Kamiumi section

Izanami burns to death giving birth to the fire-god; her dying act in one variant births the water-goddess to contain the fire. The sword's own act is a response to this — but the myth inextricably couples woman's death-in-childbirth with the creation of a weapon of vengeance.

Feminist readings of the Kamiumi cycle note how the cosmogony repeatedly sublimes female suffering into divine instrument making; Ame-no-Ohabari inherits that charge as the 'revenge' the patriarch forges from a sacrifice.

Kojiki + Engishiki variant

The eight cut parts of Kagutsuchi's corpse become the eight volcanoes of Japan — a sacred landscape born of mutilation. The sword is the tool of that transformation, making it, in a sense, the instrument that organises the country's volcanic geography.

Comparative mythologists note parallels with the world-egg/dismemberment cosmogonies of other cultures; the specific 'land from a slain god's body' motif recurs across Indo-European and East-Asian traditions.

Kagutsuchi/Hi-no-Kagutsuchi article; Kojiki

Ame-no-Ohabari delegates his own son Takemikazuchi to coerce the earthly gods into ceding Japan, and the resulting myth later becomes the legitimation of the Yamato kingship's military expansion into the Kantō/Tōhoku (armies pray to Kashima/Katori). A mythological parent using a child as a conqueror is woven into real dynastic warfare.

Iwao Ōwa (1989) argues the myth was retroactively fitted to priestly-clan politics; the 'first sumo' framing and the coercive land-grab encode an ideology of heavenly-right-to-rule over prior inhabitants.

Scholarly Debates
Is Ame-no-Ohabari a sword or a sentient kami?
It is one being — the sword whose 'spirit' (tamashii) later manifests as the sentient god (Motoori Norinaga, Kojiki-den). ⇌ There is no textual warrant for the identity; the Kojiki only narrates an instrument that later, in a different episode, appears as a god with mind and speech (Basil Hall Chamberlain).
Evidence for A: Norinaga's influential nativist commentary reads the whole weapon-kami complex as ensouled.
Evidence for B: Chamberlain's translation apparatus: 'there is nothing in the text warranting this interpretation.'
Itsu-no-Ohabari vs Ame-no-Ohabari: two names, one entity?
Same weapon/deity under two epithets (Izanagi's sword is 'given postscripturally as Ame-no-ohabari, otherwise known as Itsu-no-ohabari'). ⇌ The Nihon Shoki and some variants treat the sword-kami's roles so dissimilarly that some compilers effectively treat them as separate narrative functions.
Evidence for A: Kojiki postscript explicitly equates the names; Takemikazuchi article treats them as one.
Evidence for B: Divergent treatment across chronicles and medieval texts.
Paternity of Takemikazuchi
Takemikazuchi is the son of Ame-no-Ohabari (Kojiki postscript; Conquest of Izumo sends 'his son'). ⇌ Takemikazuchi was originally a local maritime clan-god (of the Ō house), and the heavenly-son paternity is an aetiological rewrite claimed later by the Nakatomi/Fujiwara priestly lineage (Iwao Ōwa, 1989).
Evidence for A: Kojiki text.
Evidence for B: Clan-taboo and priestly-succession analysis in Jinja to kodai ōken saishi.
Which sword did Izanagi wield in the Kagutsuchi beheading?
Ame-no-Ohabari (the totsuka sword named postscripturally). ⇌ Merely a generic 'ten-grasp sabre' (totsuka-no-tsurugi) not yet named as Ame-no-Ohabari; the naming is retrospective.
Evidence for A: Kamiumi text names the ten-grasp sword Ame-no-Ohabari.
Evidence for B: The name is applied 'postscripturally', i.e. after the act, and totsuka is a class not a proper name.
The sword-kami's role in the Conquest of Izumo (does it fight at all?)
Ame-no-Ohabari never fights; it delegates to its son and remains in heaven. ⇌ Because it 'has the mind and speech of a sentient god' and belongs to the martial lineage, some retellings show it actively campaigning alongside its son.
Evidence for A: Kojiki text — the father does not descend.
Evidence for B: Pop-culture and illustrated retellings that compress the delegation into a joint war-god campaign.

A way to hold it in your mind

There is a sword in Japanese myth that ends one world and starts another — and then, without ever being drawn again, quietly becomes the father of Japan's most feared war-god. It is the blade Izanagi pulls, weeping, when the fire his wife bore burns her to death: Ame-no-Ohabari, the 'Heavenly Point-Blade-Extended'. In a single stroke it cuts off the head of the god of fire, Kagutsuchi, and from the blood that spills a new host of thunder, storm and water deities is born — among them Takemikazuchi, the divine athlete who would later demand the surrender of the land of Izumo from the tip of his own sword. The strangest thing about Ame-no-Ohabari is that this is not its end but its beginning. When the heavens hold a war-council and search for a champion to subdue the earthly realm, the sword reappears — now with a mind and a voice — and instead of descending itself, it hands the mission to its son. A weapon that slew a god becomes, in its second act, a father who chooses not to fight; and down that line runs the martial charisma that would one day bless the imperial armies of the Yamato kingship.
