Who It Is

There is a sword in Japanese myth that ends one world and starts another — and then, without ever being drawn again, quietly becomes the father of Japan's most feared war-god. It is the blade Izanagi pulls, weeping, when the fire his wife bore burns her to death: Ame-no-Ohabari, the 'Heavenly Point-Blade-Extended'. In a single stroke it cuts off the head of the god of fire, Kagutsuchi, and from the blood that spills a new host of thunder, storm and water deities is born — among them Takemikazuchi, the divine athlete who would later demand the surrender of the land of Izumo from the tip of his own sword. The strangest thing about Ame-no-Ohabari is that this is not its end but its beginning. When it becomes a person, Ame-no-Ohabari is characterised by quiet martial competence and a revealing self-deprecation: summoned to descend and conquer the earthly realm, it elects instead to send its young son Takemikazuchi. This is read by commentators as humility, filial delegation, or a mark that the sword's 'power' belongs properly to its offspring — the myth gives the sword the mind and speech of a kami, so it reasons, weighs the divine mandate, and chooses the next generation. Powers
Basis of divine decapitation

As a totsuka (ten-handbreadth) blade, Ame-no-Ohabari is capable of cutting off the head of even a kami of Kagutsuchi's catastrophic power — fire strong enough that its birth burned the genitals of the creator goddess Izanami to death. It severs the immortal fire-god in a single stroke of the grieving Izanagi.

Limit: The power is wielded wholly by Izanagi; the sword itself is not an independent agent in this scene and never acts of its own volition while a mere weapon.

Blood as divine seed (therogenesis from the blade)

Whatever supernatural property the blade carries is expressed most spectacularly through the blood it spills. From the blood of Kagutsuchi that drips from the sword's tip, its blade, and its handle, nine additional deities spontaneously come into being: from the tip, Iwasaku, Nesaku and Iwa-tsutsu-no-o; from the mid-blade, Mika-haya-hi, Hi-haya-hi and Takemikazuchi (given as Take-mi-kagu-tsuchi-no-o, Takefutsu or Toyofutsu); from the handle, Kuraokami and Kuramitsuha. The blade is thus a kind of divine forge, converting one slain god's body into a pantheon.

Limit: The power is conditional on the victim being a slain divine being; the blood, not the steel, does the generative work. There is no attestation of the sword creating gods from an ordinary kill.

Personification / sentience

By the Conquest-of-Izumo (kuni-yuzuri) episode, the same entity that was a sword has acquired 'the mind and speech of a sentient god'. It does not merely exist as a weapon; it holds a place in the heavenly polity, is named in Amaterasu's war-council, reasons about the divine succession, and makes policy decisions.

Limit: The degree to which this sentience is original vs. acquired is fiercely contested (Motoori: it is the sword's spirit; Chamberlain: the text supplies no warrant). The sentient deity never uses the sword-blade's destructive power; it acts only through delegation.

Genesis of a martial successor (the 'father-sword' role)

Ame-no-Ohabari's defining narrative power is genealogical: it is the father of Takemikazuchi, the thunder and sword deity who becomes the chief god of Kashima, the imperial Yamato kingship's pre-eminent war-god, patron of the 'first sumo match' (the Takeminakata bout), and one of the two martial gods whose Futsunomitama sword is enshrined at Isonokami Jingū. Through this fatherhood the sword indirectly underwrites the state-cult of Kashima Shrine and the Nakatomi/Fujiwara clan traditions.

Limit: The paternity is asserted in the Kojiki's phrasing but is arguably aetiologised — invented backwards from the war-god's prominence to give him an august heavenly father.

Accounts I
The Slaying of Kagutsuchi, the Fire GodKojiki, Kamiumi section

Age of the Gods (mythic time, recorded c. 712 CE)

After the creator couple Izanagi and Izanami give birth to the islands and the various kami, Izanami bears the fire-deity Kagutsuchi (Hi-no-Kagutsuchi/Homusubi). The child's birth-blessing is a catastrophe: the fire burns the mother's genitals and wounds her mortally, and she dies. Overcome by grief and then rage, Izanagi takes his ten-grasp sword — postscripturally named Ame-no-Ohabari, 'Heavenly-Blade-Extended', here also Itsu-no-Ohabari — and beheads his own son. He cuts the corpse into eight pieces, which become eight volcanoes, and from the sword-spilled blood and the severed body further gods swarm into being (see Powers). The slaying marks the entry of death into the world that began with the archipelago's creation, and it is the primal act of divine patricide in the Japanese canon. Death enters the cosmos; the fire-god's corpse becomes volcanoes; a generation of storm/thunder/water deities is born from the sword-blood; Izanagi then descends to Yomi seeking Izanami, and the sword is not wielded again in person until the Conquest of Izumo.

Accounts II
The Conquest of Izumo: a Father Delegates His ThroneKojiki, Conquest of Izumo (kuni-yuzuri) chapter

Age of the Gods (mythic time, recorded c. 712 CE)

The heavenly gods (Amaterasu and Takamusubi, with the counsel of Omoikane) decide to subdue the terrestrial Middle Country (Ashihara no Nakatsukuni) and hand it to Amaterasu's heirs. Takemikazuchi or his father Itsu-no-ohabari is the proposed envoy. The narrative explicitly notes that the sword-deity, which 'previously appeared as a ten-fist sword', 'here has the mind and speech of a sentient god'. Chosen for the task, Ame-no-Ohabari does not go himself: he volunteers his young son Takemikazuchi, who descends with the deity-bird-boat Ame-no-torifune to the shore of Izumo (Inasa beach). There Takemikazuchi plants his own totsuka sword point-down on the wave-crest, perches on it, and demands that Ōkuninushi cede the land. After the local gods' resistance — Kotoshironushi's easy capitulation and Takeminakata's failed strength-bout (the mythic origin of sumo) — Ōkuninushi yields, and the transfer founds the imperial claim on Japan. Heian and earlier (historicised myth)

Although not itself enshrined, Ame-no-Ohabari stands at the head of the martial divine lineage that later anchors two of Japan's most important shrines: Takemikazuchi at Kashima and (via the sword-born Futsunushi, brother) the associated Katori faith. In the imperial campaigns into the Kantō and Tōhoku, Yamato armies prayed to the Kashima and Katori deities for victory, and the Nakatomi/Fujiwara priestly house claimed Takemikazuchi as its ujigami (clan deity), elevating veneration into Kasuga-taisha at Nara. The sword of Takemikazuchi, Futsunomitama-no-Tsurugi (布都御魂), is enshrined as the central object at Isonokami Jingū — a physical heir to the totsuka-class blade Ame-no-Ohabari represents. A mythological sword becomes the genealogical origin of a real, historically operative state religion of war; the deity's martial charisma is repurposed as dynastic-sanction for military expansion.

Ame-no-Ohabari is the Kusanagi sword or one of the Three Imperial Regalia. — It is not. Kusanagi-no-Tsurugi is a distinct totsuka-family blade that Susanoo found in the tail of Yamata no Orochi and that later became one of the Three Sacred Treasures. Ame-no-Ohabari is Izanagi's Kagutsuchi-slaying sword — older, and never part of the imperial regalia.

The sword is definitely a living god because the Kojiki says so. — The Kojiki itself never states the sword is ensouled or worshipped as the deity's body; that is Motoori Norinaga's influential reading, which Chamberlain explicitly rejected. The text merely shows an object in one episode and a god with mind/speech in another.

Ame-no-Ohabari has an active shrine cult of its own. — There is no independent, well-attested shrine devoted to Ame-no-Ohabari as a standalone object-kami. Its worship-context is entirely through its descendants (Kashima's Takemikazuchi, Katori's Futsunushi, Isonokami's Futsunomitama). The sword proper has no attested dedicated cult center.

Izanagi beheaded Kagutsuchi in ordinary anger and the sword is therefore a simple 'sword of vengeance'. — The act is cosmologically structured: it marks the entry of death into creation and, via the blade-blood, births the storm/thunder deities. It is a sacrificial-ordering act of a dying world, not mere emotional violence, and modern scholars read it structurally.
