Who It Is

In the twilight of the Boyne, where the winter sun stands still, a god is conceived who will never grow old. He is Aengus Óg, the Young Son of the Dagda, a being of love, dreams, and stolen immortality, whose kisses turn into birds and whose very name means 'true vigour' or 'one desire.' His story is a tapestry of clever tricks, tragic loves, and the eternal cycle of the seasons.

He is a trickster and a clever negotiator, using wordplay to gain the Brú forever. He is passionate in love, willing to go to great lengths to win his dream bride Caer. He is also protective and generous, fostering Diarmuid and giving him powerful weapons. He is capable of violence, killing his foster mother and a poet who slandered his brother. In Scottish tales, he is sometimes portrayed as light-headed and rebellious against his mother Beira.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Óengus is derived from Proto-Celtic *oino- (one) and *gustu- (choice, vigour), meaning 'true vigour' or 'lone strength'. The medieval Dindsenchas also offers a folk etymology: 'one desire', because Boann's union with the Dagda was her only wish. The epithet Óg means 'young'.

Pronunciation — Old Irish: /ˈoːi̯ŋɡus/; Modern Irish: Aonghus /ˈeːnˠəsˠ/ or /ˈn̪ˠiːsˠ/ (depending on dialect).

Aliases — Aengus, Óengus, Oíngus, Áengus, Aonghus, Macan Óc

Ownership — Irish and Scottish Gaelic mythology; part of the Tuatha Dé Danann. Later associated with fairy lore.

Powers
Shape-shifting

He can transform himself and others into swans. He turns himself and Caer into swans to fly away together. He also transforms Étaín into a fly and back into human form.

Limit: Not explicitly stated, but transformations are often temporary or conditional.

Control over time

He can make the sun stand still, as his father did, to hide a pregnancy or to gain ownership of the Brú. This is used to trick Elcmar.

Limit: Requires a spell or incantation; may have unintended consequences.

Resurrection

He can repair broken bodies and bring the dead back to life. He takes Diarmuid's body to the Brú and breathes life into it when he wishes to speak with him.

Limit: Not fully explained; may be temporary or limited.

Musical magic

His swan song puts all listeners to sleep for three days and nights. His harp music attracts maidens and youths.

Limit: Not specified.

Kisses into birds

He can shape his kisses into invisible birds that follow lovers home, singing love songs and whispering memories. In the Dindsenchas, he uses this to torment Cairbre.

Limit: Birds can be detained by a magical tree.

Ownership of magical weapons

He owns swords Moralltach and Beagalltach, and spears Gáe Buide and Gáe Derg, which he gives to Diarmuid.

Limit: Weapons are powerful but not unique to Aengus.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In Irish texts, he is described as 'red-armed', 'mighty and stern', and an expert in arms, horseman, and sailor. In a Scottish folktale, he is described as riding a white steed, clad in shining gold raiment with a royal crimson robe that streams in the wind. He is often depicted as youthful and handsome.

Symbols — Swans, white horses, golden harp with silver strings, invisible birds made from kisses. The Brú na Bóinne (Newgrange) is his dwelling. He is associated with the winter solstice.

Domains — He resides at Brú na Bóinne (Newgrange) on the River Boyne. In Scottish lore, he lives in Tír na nÓg during winter and returns to the human world in spring. He is associated with youth, love, summer, and poetic inspiration.

Accounts I
Conception and Birth of AengusTochmarc Étaíne, Dindsenchas

Medieval (8th-12th century)

The Dagda desires Boann, wife of Elcmar. He sends Elcmar on a one-day errand, but makes the sun stand still so that many months pass. Boann becomes pregnant with Aengus. To hide the pregnancy, the Dagda casts a spell on Elcmar so he does not notice the passage of time. Aengus is born, and later learns his true parentage. Aengus eventually claims the Brú na Bóinne from Elcmar using a trick of ambiguous wording.

The Wooing of ÉtaínTochmarc Étaíne

8th-9th century

Midir, Aengus's foster-brother, falls in love with Étaín. Aengus helps him win her. Midir's jealous wife Fuamnach turns Étaín into a fly. Aengus partially lifts the spell, making her human at night, and takes her as his lover. When Fuamnach discovers, she drives Étaín away. Aengus kills Fuamnach in retaliation. Étaín is eventually restored to human form and remarries, but the events lead to complex entanglements.

The Wooing of AengusTochmarc Aengusa

12th century

Aengus dreams of a beautiful girl and falls in love. His mother Boann and father the Dagda search for her for years. Finally, King Bodb Derg finds her: Caer Ibormeith. She and 149 other girls are chained in pairs at the lake of the Dragon's Mouth. Every second Samhain, they transform into swans for a year. Aengus is told he can marry Caer if he identifies her in swan form. He does, transforms himself into a swan, and they fly away together, singing beautiful music that puts all listeners to sleep for three days. Aengus and Caer are united, and their singing causes a deep sleep.

The Fosterage of the House of the Two PailsMedieval Irish text

Medieval

Manannán mac Lir, as High King of the Tuath Dé, convinces Aengus to cast a spell on his foster-father Elcmar, forcing him from the Brú until 'ogham and pillar, heaven and earth, and the sun and the moon have been blended together'. Elcmar laments that he would have given the Brú if asked, and foretells woe for Aengus's people. Aengus expresses remorse. Elcmar is banished, and Aengus takes possession of the Brú.

Accounts II
Aengus and DiarmuidFianna Cycle, especially Tóraigheacht Dhiarmada agus Ghráinne

12th-15th century

Aengus is the foster-father and protector of Diarmuid Ua Duibhne. When Diarmuid elopes with Gráinne, Aengus helps them escape the Fianna. After Diarmuid is killed by a boar, Aengus takes his body to the Brú na Bóinne and breathes life into it whenever he wishes to speak with him. Diarmuid dies, but Aengus preserves his body.

Aengus and the PoetDeath Tales of the Tuatha de Danann

Medieval

Aengus kills the poet of Lugh Lámhfhada because the poet lied about Aengus's brother Ogma an Cermait having an affair with one of Lugh's wives. The poet is killed, and Aengus's action is not shown to have further consequences.

Aengus and Elcmar's DeathDeath Tales of the Tuatha de Danann

Medieval

Aengus kills his stepfather Elcmar in retaliation for Elcmar killing Midir. Elcmar is dead.

Scottish Folktale: Angus and BrigidScottish folklore, collected by John Gregorson Campbell and others

19th century

Angus, son of Beira, the Fairy Queen of Winter, lives in Tír na nÓg. He dreams of Brigid, who is held captive by the Fairy Queen. Angus borrows three days from August and searches for her. He finds her in Beira's underground palace on the first day of spring. Their meeting causes flowers to bloom. They marry, but Beira disrupts the feast. Eventually, Beira weakens, and Angus and Brigid become King and Queen of Summer. Angus and Brigid rule over summer.

Aengus is a god of love like the Greek Eros. — He is associated with youth, love, and poetic inspiration, but he is not a straightforward love god. He is also a trickster and warrior.

Aengus is the same as the Welsh Mabon. — They are cognates, but they are distinct figures in their respective mythologies.

Aengus's story of Caer is a simple fairy tale. — It is a complex myth with seasonal symbolism, involving transformation and quest.

Aengus is a purely benevolent figure. — He kills people, including his foster mother, and uses deception.
