Aengus Óg

The Young Son; God of Love and Youth

Who It Is
Form

Aengus is often depicted as a handsome young man of fine physical proportions, with an attractive nature. He is sometimes shown with four swans circling around him.

Powers

Love and Youth Aengus is associated with the power of love and youth, and is said to have the ability to inspire poetic and musical creativity.

Accounts

Aengus falls in love with Étain, a mortal woman, and uses his powers to win her hand in marriage.

Aengus is tasked with finding a magical boar that has been stolen from the Otherworld.

The Approach

In the twilight of the Boyne, where the winter sun stands still, a god is conceived who will never grow old. He is Aengus Óg, the Young Son of the Dagda, a being of love, dreams, and stolen immortality, whose kisses turn into birds and whose very name means 'true vigour' or 'one desire.' His story is a tapestry of clever tricks, tragic loves, and the eternal cycle of the seasons.

Content advisory. Contains themes of deception, betrayal, and killing, including the murder of a poet and a foster mother. Also includes a story of a woman transformed against her will and kept as a lover. No explicit sexual content, but themes are mature.

Etymology

The name Óengus is derived from Proto-Celtic *oino- (one) and *gustu- (choice, vigour), meaning 'true vigour' or 'lone strength'. The medieval Dindsenchas also offers a folk etymology: 'one desire', because Boann's union with the Dagda was her only wish. The epithet Óg means 'young'.

Cultural Ownership

Irish and Scottish Gaelic mythology; part of the Tuatha Dé Danann. Later associated with fairy lore.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars equate him with the Welsh Mabon and the Gaulish Maponos, but this is debated. The name is also cognate with Pictish Onuist/Unuist and Old Welsh Unust, but these may be separate figures. In Scottish folklore, he is sometimes conflated with the fairy king or the son of Beira.

Description

Appearance

In Irish texts, he is described as 'red-armed', 'mighty and stern', and an expert in arms, horseman, and sailor. In a Scottish folktale, he is described as riding a white steed, clad in shining gold raiment with a royal crimson robe that streams in the wind. He is often depicted as youthful and handsome.

Symbols & Iconography

Swans, white horses, golden harp with silver strings, invisible birds made from kisses. The Brú na Bóinne (Newgrange) is his dwelling. He is associated with the winter solstice.

Behaviour & Personality

He is a trickster and a clever negotiator, using wordplay to gain the Brú forever. He is passionate in love, willing to go to great lengths to win his dream bride Caer. He is also protective and generous, fostering Diarmuid and giving him powerful weapons. He is capable of violence, killing his foster mother and a poet who slandered his brother. In Scottish tales, he is sometimes portrayed as light-headed and rebellious against his mother Beira.

Domains & Habitats

He resides at Brú na Bóinne (Newgrange) on the River Boyne. In Scottish lore, he lives in Tír na nÓg during winter and returns to the human world in spring. He is associated with youth, love, summer, and poetic inspiration.

Version Differences

In Irish mythology, he is the son of the Dagda and Boann. In Scottish folklore, he is the son of Beira (the Cailleach) and the husband of Brigid. The stories of his birth and his takeover of the Brú differ in details between 'The Wooing of Étaín' and 'The Fosterage of the House of the Two Pails'. The latter has Manannán mac Lir as the instigator of the spell, and Aengus expresses remorse.

Stories & Myths

Conception and Birth of Aengus

Source Tochmarc Étaíne, DindsenchasCulture IrishPeriod Medieval (8th-12th century)

Summary

The Dagda desires Boann, wife of Elcmar. He sends Elcmar on a one-day errand, but makes the sun stand still so that many months pass. Boann becomes pregnant with Aengus. To hide the pregnancy, the Dagda casts a spell on Elcmar so he does not notice the passage of time. Aengus is born, and later learns his true parentage.

The Wooing of Étaín

Source Tochmarc ÉtaíneCulture IrishPeriod 8th-9th century

Summary

Aengus partially lifts the spell, making her human at night, and takes her as his lover. When Fuamnach discovers, she drives Étaín away. Aengus kills Fuamnach in retaliation.

The Wooing of Aengus

Source Tochmarc AengusaCulture IrishPeriod 12th century

Summary

Aengus dreams of a beautiful girl and falls in love. His mother Boann and father the Dagda search for her for years. Finally, King Bodb Derg finds her: Caer Ibormeith. She and 149 other girls are chained in pairs at the lake of the Dragon's Mouth. Every second Samhain, they transform into swans for a year. Aengus is told he can marry Caer if he identifies her in swan form. He does, transforms himself into a swan, and they fly away together, singing beautiful music that puts all listeners to sleep for three days.

The Fosterage of the House of the Two Pails

Source Medieval Irish textCulture IrishPeriod Medieval

Summary

Manannán mac Lir, as High King of the Tuath Dé, convinces Aengus to cast a spell on his foster-father Elcmar, forcing him from the Brú until 'ogham and pillar, heaven and earth, and the sun and the moon have been blended together'. Elcmar laments that he would have given the Brú if asked, and foretells woe for Aengus's people. Aengus expresses remorse.

Scottish Folktale: Angus and Brigid

Source Scottish folklore, collected by John Gregorson Campbell and othersCulture ScottishPeriod 19th century

Summary

Angus, son of Beira, the Fairy Queen of Winter, lives in Tír na nÓg. He dreams of Brigid, who is held captive by the Fairy Queen. Angus borrows three days from August and searches for her. He finds her in Beira's underground palace on the first day of spring. Their meeting causes flowers to bloom. They marry, but Beira disrupts the feast. Eventually, Beira weakens, and Angus and Brigid become King and Queen of Summer.

Participants

Consequences

Angus and Brigid rule over summer.

Variants

Another tale says Angus is trapped in rocks as punishment for contradicting his mother, becoming an echo.

Murder of Fuamnach

Source Tochmarc Étaíne

Description

Aengus kills his foster mother Fuamnach for turning Étaín into a fly and driving her away. This is an act of vengeance but also a killing of a family member.

Scholarly Context

In medieval Irish literature, such acts of vengeance are common among the Tuatha Dé Danann, reflecting a heroic code.

Murder of Elcmar

Source Death Tales of the Tuatha de Danann

Description

Aengus kills his stepfather Elcmar in retaliation for Elcmar killing Midir. This is another act of vengeance.

Scholarly Context

This is part of a cycle of violence among the gods.

Murder of Lugh's poet

Source Death Tales of the Tuatha de Danann

Description

Aengus kills a poet for spreading a false rumor about his brother. This shows a willingness to use lethal force to defend honor.

Scholarly Context

Poets had high status, and killing one was a serious offense, but Aengus's action is not condemned in the text.

Coercive relationship with Étaín

Source Tochmarc Étaíne

Description

Aengus makes Étaín his lover at night while she is in a partially transformed state. Some interpretations may view this as non-consensual, though the text does not explicitly say.

Scholarly Context

In medieval Irish literature, consent is often not addressed in the same way as modern standards. Aengus's actions are presented as helping her, not harming her.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

The primary sources are medieval and often fragmentary. The exact nature of Aengus's powers and his role in pre-Christian religion is speculative. Scottish folklore is later and may be influenced by Christianization. The relationship between Aengus and Mabon/Maponos is debated. There is no comprehensive modern scholarly monograph solely on Aengus; information is scattered. Some texts are not fully translated into English.

Research Scope

This dossier covers Aengus Óg as depicted in Irish and Scottish folklore and mythology, focusing on primary medieval texts (e.g., Tochmarc Étaíne, Dindsenchas, Tochmarc Aengusa) and later folkloric collections. It includes analysis of his name, family, roles, powers, stories, and reception. It does not cover modern fantasy adaptations in exhaustive detail, but notes major ones. Research cutoff: 2026-08-01.
