Who It Is

Kamapua'a is the pig demigod of Hawaiian mythology — a shape-shifting, lustful, violent, and ultimately sympathetic trickster figure whose tumultuous relationship with the volcano goddess Pele is one of the most famous love-hate stories in Polynesian myth. Kamapua'a's name means 'hog-child,' and he is the son of Hina and a chiefly lineage. He can transform between a handsome man, a monstrous hog, a fish, a plant, or a piece of lava. In his most famous myth cycle, Kamapua'a traveled to Kīlauea and confronted Pele, insulting her, mocking her fires, and challenging her power. Pele responded with rivers of lava, but Kamapua'a — associated with water and the lush, wet forests of the windward side — called down torrential rains that cooled her flames. The battle between them is an ancient Hawaiian explanation for the climatic and geological struggle between the wet and dry sides of the islands. Ultimately, a truce was negotiated: Pele would rule the dry leeward side (Kona) and the volcanic fire, while Kamapua'a would rule the wet windward side (Hilo) and the lush forests. Their relationship, however, was also a love affair — passionate, violent, and ultimately impossible to sustain. Kamapua'a embodies the vital, messy, fertile power of the forest: vigorous plant growth, the rooting of pigs, the sudden downpour. He is not worshipped in temples but celebrated in chants that acknowledge the chaotic creative force of nature, desire, and defiance.
