Tikoloshe

The Little Terror; Demon of the Waters; Evil Dwarf Spirit

also known asTokolosheTikoloshTonkoloshTonkolosiTokolotsheThokolosi
TypeMalevolent Spirit / Trickster
CultureZulu
Who It Is
Form

The Tikoloshe is typically described as a dwarf-like creature, standing only a few feet tall, with a disproportionately large head and a wiry, emaciated body. Its skin is often depicted as dark or black, sometimes with a bluish or greenish tint, reflecting its aquatic nature. The creature has large, bulging eyes that are said to glow in the dark, and its teeth are sharp and jagged, reminiscent of a predator. It is often portrayed as being covered in short, coarse hair, though some accounts describe it as having a smooth, slippery skin like that of a frog. The Tikoloshe is said to be able to change its appearance at will, sometimes taking on the form of a small child or an animal, but its true form is that of a grotesque dwarf. In some traditions, it is depicted as having only one leg, or with feet that point backwards, adding to its eerie and unnatural demeanor.

Powers

Invisibility The Tikoloshe can become invisible by drinking water or swallowing a stone. This ability allows it to move unseen among humans, making it a formidable and terrifying presence. It is said that the creature uses this power to stalk its victims, appearing suddenly to cause fright or harm.

Shape-shifting In some accounts, the Tikoloshe can change its form, taking on the appearance of a child, an animal, or even a shadow. This ability adds to its deceptive nature, allowing it to approach victims without suspicion.

Causing illness and death The Tikoloshe is believed to have the power to inflict sickness or even death upon those it targets. This is often done at the behest of a witch or sorcerer, but the spirit may also act independently if provoked or offended.

Sexual assault The Tikoloshe is said to be capable of sexually assaulting individuals, particularly women, during their sleep. This is a deeply disturbing aspect of the legend, and it is often cited as a reason for the fear and caution surrounding the spirit.

Accounts

A woman in a rural village begins to suffer from nightmares and a feeling of being watched at night. She consults a sangoma, who reveals that a tikoloshe has been sent by a jealous rival to torment her. The sangoma advises her to raise her bed on bricks and to place a charm under her pillow. The woman follows the advice, and the tikoloshe is unable to reach her, eventually leaving her in peace. The story serves as a cautionary tale about the dangers of envy and the importance of seeking spiritual protection.

A chief becomes greedy and begins to hoard the village's cattle, ignoring the needs of his people. A mysterious dwarf appears, claiming to be a water spirit, and offers to help the chief increase his wealth in exchange for a sacrifice. The chief agrees, but the dwarf, who is actually a tikoloshe, tricks him into giving up his own son. The chief's son is taken to the river, and the tikoloshe disappears, leaving the chief with nothing. The story is a moral lesson about greed and the dangers of making deals with malevolent spirits.

A young man in a modern township discovers that his neighbor is a witch who keeps a tikoloshe as a familiar. The tikoloshe is used to steal from other homes and to curse those who offend the witch. The young man, with the help of a pastor, manages to banish the tikoloshe by reciting prayers and burning protective herbs. The witch is exposed and driven out of the community. This story reflects the ongoing belief in the tikoloshe in urban settings and the blending of traditional and Christian practices.

Scholarly Debates
Origin
The Tikoloshe is a Zulu spirit, as commonly stated in popular sources. ⇌ Some sources, such as the Tumblr post, claim that the origin lies in Xhosa mythology, a related Nguni group. This discrepancy reflects the shared cultural heritage and the difficulty of pinpointing a single origin.
Method of invisibility
The Tikoloshe becomes invisible by drinking water. ⇌ Other accounts say it swallows a stone to achieve invisibility. Both methods are mentioned in the source text, indicating a lack of consensus in the folklore.
Nature of the spirit
The Tikoloshe is inherently evil and malevolent, as described in most sources. ⇌ In some Xhosa traditions, the 'Hili' is considered a more benign water spirit, capable of mischief but not inherently evil. This suggests a spectrum of interpretations across different communities.
Physical appearance
The Tikoloshe is a dwarf-like creature with a large head and dark skin. ⇌ Some descriptions depict it as having a single leg, a tail, or the ability to change shape, indicating variability in physical attributes across accounts.
What We Don't Know

Further research is needed to document the full range of regional variations in the Tikoloshe legend, particularly in less-studied Nguni communities. The historical evolution of the spirit's depiction from pre-colonial times to the present is not well-documented, and the impact of urbanization and Christianity on belief in the Tikoloshe remains under-explored. Additionally, the role of the Tikoloshe in contemporary media and its reception by modern audiences, especially within South Africa, warrants deeper analysis. The relationship between the Tikoloshe and other water spirits in African mythology is also a promising area for comparative study.

A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine a creature so small it can hide under a bed, yet so feared that entire communities raise their sleeping platforms to keep it at bay. The Tikoloshe is not just a myth; it is a living presence in the minds of many South Africans, a reminder of the unseen forces that shape our fears and beliefs.
