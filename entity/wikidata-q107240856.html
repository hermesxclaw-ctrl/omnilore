The Approach

In the shadowed highlands of Arcadia, where the oaks whispered secrets of the age of heroes, there lived a prince whose very name echoed the rolling hills that would bear his legacy. Tricolonus, son of the infamous Lycaon, stands as a figure both founder and victim—his story woven into the fabric of Greek myth, yet obscured by the mists of time.

Content advisory. Mythological themes include patricide, divine punishment, and violent death of suitors.
Identity

Tricolonus

Pronunciation tri-KOL-o-nus

Known As

TrikolonosTricolonos

Etymology

From Greek τρικόλωνος (trikolonos), meaning 'three-hilled' or 'three-membered', likely referring to the topography of the town he founded.

Cultural Ownership

Ancient Greek, specifically Arcadian mythology

Disputed Identifications

There is no major dispute over his identity, but some scholars question whether the two Tricoloni mentioned in sources are the same person or distinct individuals.

Description

Appearance

No physical description of Tricolonus survives in ancient sources. As a mythical Arcadian prince, he would be imagined as a heroic figure of the heroic age, but specific attributes are unattested.

Symbols & Iconography

No known symbols or iconographic representations are associated with Tricolonus.

Behaviour & Personality

Little is known about his personality. He is depicted as a progenitor and founder, which suggests a role of leadership and colonization.

Domains & Habitats

He is associated with the region of Arcadia in the Peloponnese, specifically the town of Tricoloni, which he reputedly founded.

Version Differences

There are two figures named Tricolonus in Greek mythology: one is a son of Lycaon, the other a descendant (likely grandson) who was a suitor of Hippodamia. Some sources may conflate them, but they are typically treated as distinct.

Stories & Myths

Tricolonus, Son of Lycaon

Source Pausanias, Description of Greece 8.3.4Culture GreekPeriod Mythical heroic age

Summary

Tricolonus was one of the fifty sons of Lycaon, the impious king of Arcadia. He became the father of Zoeteus and Paroreus, and was said to be the eponymous founder of the town of Tricoloni.

Participants

Tricolonus

Lycaon

Zoeteus

Paroreus

Consequences

He established a lineage and a city, contributing to the mythological geography of Arcadia.

Variants

No significant variants are recorded.

Tricolonus, Suitor of Hippodamia

Source Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Roman Antiquities 1.13.1Culture GreekPeriod Mythical heroic age

Summary

A later Tricolonus, a descendant of the first, was one of the suitors of Hippodamia, daughter of King Oenomaus of Pisa. Like other suitors, he was defeated and killed by Oenomaus in the chariot race that was the condition of marriage.

Participants

Tricolonus

Hippodamia

Oenomaus

Consequences

His death served as a warning to other suitors and highlighted the cruelty of Oenomaus.

Variants

The list of suitors varies among sources; Tricolonus appears only in Dionysius of Halicarnassus.

Lycaon's impiety and punishment

Source Various Greek myths

Description

Lycaon, father of Tricolonus, was famously punished by Zeus for serving the god a meal of human flesh. This dark background may have implications for his sons, though Tricolonus himself is not implicated.

Scholarly Context

Scholars debate the historicity of Lycaon and the symbolic meaning of the myth.

Death of suitors

Source Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Roman Antiquities 1.13.1

Description

The suitors of Hippodamia, including Tricolonus (the descendant), were killed by Oenomaus after losing a chariot race. This reflects the violent nature of heroic age contests.

Scholarly Context

The myth may reflect historical practices of bride contests or the dangers of challenging a king.
