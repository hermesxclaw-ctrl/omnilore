Etymology

In Dungeons & Dragons lore, “illithid” is presented as an Undercommon term glossed as “mind flayer” or “mind ruler,” emphasizing psychic domination and mental predation rather than a direct real‑world linguistic origin. The English phrase “mind flayer” is a descriptive compound coined in early TSR materials to evoke the image of a creature that tears, strips, or ‘flays’ the mind, paralleling terms like ‘skin‑flayer’ in horror fiction. Later fan and scholarly commentary often connects the name’s affective resonance to Lovecraftian and Lumleyan cosmic‑horror traditions, but no formal etymology beyond in‑world glosses is attested.

Cultural Ownership

Illithids/mind flayers are proprietary intellectual property of TSR, Inc. and later Wizards of the Coast, created within the Dungeons & Dragons tabletop role‑playing game. They are considered “Product Identity” under the Wizards of the Coast Open Game License and are not released as open content. Within fan culture they function as a shared icon of D&D’s horror‑fantasy tradition, but legal and creative control remains with Wizards of the Coast.

Disputed Identifications

Illithids are sometimes informally described as ‘Lovecraftian’ or equated with Cthulhu Mythos entities (especially Brian Lumley’s Chthonians) because of their tentacled faces, psionic powers, and cosmic‑horror themes. Primary sources, however, indicate that Gary Gygax drew inspiration from the cover of Lumley’s “The Burrowers Beneath” and from a tentacled miniature, not from a specific Mythos creature, and illithids are not canonically part of the Cthulhu Mythos. In‑setting, their origin is variously attributed to the Far Realm, to a time‑traveling empire from the distant future, or left deliberately obscure; these competing accounts are explicitly contradictory rather than different names for the same entity.

Archive Dossier — Approach

In the deepest caverns of the Underdark, where light never reaches and the stone itself seems to listen, a robed figure waits. Its eyes are blank white, its skin slick with slime, and from its face hang four patient tentacles, flexing with quiet hunger. It does not raise a sword or chant a spell. Instead, a wave of invisible pressure rolls out from its mind, and the thoughts of those nearby shatter like glass. This is the mind flayer—illithid—one of Dungeons & Dragons’ most unsettling creations, a creature that does not merely kill its victims but devours their very selves. To understand it is to walk a line between fascination and revulsion, tracing a history of empires built on stolen minds and horrors that feel disturbingly close to home.

Who It Is

The Mind Flayer (also known by their species name, Illithids) is one of the most sophisticated and terrifying creations in the Dungeons & Dragons bestiary — a race of psionic aberrations who view all other intelligent species as cattle. Their appearance is instantly iconic: a tall, thin humanoid with mauve or purplish skin, clad in flowing robes, with a head resembling an octopus — four facial tentacles surrounding a lamprey-like mouth, and blank, pupil-less white eyes. These tentacles are their primary weapon: a Mind Flayer grapples a victim, attaches its tentacles to their head, and in a process called 'extraction,' bores through the skull to devour the brain. The victim remains conscious through much of this — the Mind Flayer's psionic powers ensure this. The Illithid lifecycle is one of TTRPG's most disturbing pieces of body horror. Mind Flayers reproduce through tadpole-like larvae that swim in the brine pools of an Elder Brain — a bioengineered gestalt intelligence composed of the merged brains of deceased Mind Flayers, serving as a living library, deity, and hive mind nexus for the colony. To create a new Mind Flayer, a tadpole is inserted through the eye socket of a humanoid victim in a process called ceremorphosis: over several days, the tadpole consumes and replaces the host's brain, transforming the body into a new Illithid. Some personality fragments of the host may survive, but the new being is fundamentally a Mind Flayer. This process means that Illithids do not reproduce biologically — they parasitize other species to perpetuate themselves. In D&D lore, the Illithids are not native to the current multiverse. They are refugees from the far future — the end of time itself — who traveled back to escape the inevitable heat death of their original universe. Their vast, nautiloid-shaped spelljamming ships sail the Astral Plane and the space between worlds, raiding prime material worlds for slaves and hosts. This temporal origin gives them a cosmic, almost existential quality among D&D's monsters: they are a reminder that the multiverse has an expiration date. Societally, Mind Flayers are a totalitarian hive mind under the psychic domination of the Elder Brain, which dictates every aspect of colony life. Individual Illithids have some autonomy but are ultimately extensions of the Elder Brain's will. This changes with very rare 'renegade' Illithids who develop arcane magic (Illithids normally despise arcane magic and its practitioners) — these individuals, called Alhoons or Illithiliches, pursue lichdom to escape the Elder Brain's control, becoming one of the most dangerous beings in the multiverse. The Mind Flayer's psionic abilities include Mind Blast (a cone of psychic energy that stuns), Detect Thoughts, Levitation, Plane Shift, and Dominate Monster — making an elder colony one of the most difficult challenges in the game. The Mind Flayer has achieved mainstream recognition through Baldur's Gate 3 (2023), where the central plot revolves around the party being infected with Illithid tadpoles and facing a rogue Elder Brain that has enslaved even other Mind Flayers.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

Attested descriptions across editions depict mind flayers as tall, robed, humanoid figures roughly human height (about 5'4"–6'2"), with narrow, almost frail builds. Their heads resemble an octopus: a smooth, bulbous cranium, pale or mauve skin, and four long, prehensile tentacles surrounding a small circular mouth lined with rows of teeth. The tentacles can extend to several feet and are capable of fine manipulation, but their most iconic use is to grasp a victim’s head and burrow into the skull to extract the brain. Their skin is described as soft, damp, and coated in a glistening slime, often in shades of mauve, lavender, or sickly greenish‑purple. Eyes are solid white, lacking visible pupils, contributing to an alien, unreadable gaze. Hands have three long fingers and a thumb, with soft cartilaginous nails; feet have two webbed toes. Despite lacking a conventional jaw, mind flayers can speak, often in hissing or wetly articulated tones. They typically wear dark, high‑collared robes, sometimes adorned with skulls, arcane sigils, or armored plates, emphasizing their status as sinister, intellectual overlords rather than brute warriors.

Symbols & Iconography

Illithid iconography centers on tentacles, brains, and stylized Elder Brain motifs. In art and game materials, mind flayers are frequently shown cradling or consuming a humanoid brain, or standing before a massive brain‑like entity in a brine pool. The Elder Brain itself—an enormous, pulsating brain in a stone basin—is a central symbol of illithid society, representing collective consciousness and absolute domination. Qualith, their tactile script, is depicted as raised, four‑dimensional patterns on stone or metal surfaces, readable by touch and psionic perception rather than sight; this script functions as both a practical writing system and a cultural emblem of their alien cognition. Nautiloid ships—organic, shell‑like vessels used in Spelljammer and other settings—serve as visual shorthand for illithid cosmic reach. In many illustrations, mind flayers are robed in garments bearing tentacular motifs or brain‑shaped brooches, reinforcing their identity as psychic predators. Adaptation‑specific iconography (e.g., video games, miniatures) often exaggerates the cephalopod features, making the tentacles longer and more numerous to heighten horror.

Behaviour & Personality

Attested lore consistently portrays mind flayers as highly intelligent, calculating, and ruthlessly self‑interested. They believe themselves to be the rightful dominant species of the multiverse, viewing other sentient beings as livestock, tools, or experimental material. Their behavior is characterized by cold, clinical cruelty: they enslave, experiment upon, and consume other creatures without remorse, guided by a combination of racial arrogance and collective Elder Brain directives. Individual illithids are often described as patient schemers, preferring manipulation, psionic domination, and long‑term plots over direct confrontation. They value knowledge—especially about minds, magic, and planar phenomena—and maintain extensive archives, often encoded in Qualith or stored within the Elder Brain’s memory. Personality traits vary slightly by edition and variant: ulitharids are portrayed as even more ambitious and domineering; alhoons (illithid liches) are obsessed with personal immortality and arcane power, sometimes at odds with Elder Brain authority. While most sources emphasize Lawful Evil alignment, some later materials allow for rare deviations, including renegade or morally ambiguous illithids, but these are explicitly exceptional. Their social interactions are heavily mediated by psionic communication, making their emotional life difficult for non‑illithids to interpret; reconstructed accounts suggest a mix of fear, reverence, and competition within their communities.

Domains & Habitats

Mind flayers are most strongly associated with the Underdark—vast subterranean networks beneath fantasy worlds such as the Forgotten Realms—where they build moist, cavernous cities and lairs near underground lakes or brine pools. These settlements are typically centered on an Elder Brain’s pool, with organic architecture, psionically shaped stone, and labyrinthine tunnels designed to confuse intruders and channel thralls. In Spelljammer and related settings, illithids also inhabit nautiloid ships and extraplanar bases, extending their reach into wildspace and other planes. Origin theories place them either in the Far Realm (a dimension of madness and alien geometry) or in a distant future from which they traveled back in time; in both cases, their ‘natural’ domain is portrayed as fundamentally alien to standard material planes. They prefer environments that can be saturated with brine and psychic resonance, often modifying local ecosystems to support their tadpoles and thralls. In game terms, their favored terrain is underground, but narrative sources emphasize their ability to infiltrate surface societies through hidden enclaves, urban sewers, and secret cults. Domains of influence include mental domination, slavery, and clandestine rule over cities via controlled leaders.

Version Differences

Across editions and settings, core features—tentacled face, brain consumption, psionic powers, underground lairs, Elder Brain hierarchy—remain stable and attested. Differences arise in origin, cosmology, and mechanical expression. Early D&D (Strategic Review, Eldritch Wizardry, 1E Monster Manual) presents mind flayers primarily as psionic monsters with a powerful Mind Blast and brain‑eating tentacles, with minimal backstory. 2E and Dragon’s “Ecology of the Mind Flayer” introduce detailed biology, ceremorphosis (tadpoles implanted into hosts), and Elder Brain‑centered communities. The Illithiad and Lords of Madness expand this into a full civilization, adding Far Realm connections, nautiloid ships, and multiple origin theories. 3E/3.5E codify illithids as aberrations with complex psionic rules, while 4E reclassifies them as ‘aberrant humanoids’ and integrates them into a broader cosmology of the Far Realm, sometimes altering alignment descriptors. 5E simplifies mechanics but retains Lawful Evil alignment and emphasizes their role as masterminds with lair actions and regional effects. Setting‑specific lore (Forgotten Realms, Eberron, Ravenloft, Spelljammer) introduces local variations—e.g., different historical empires, unique Elder Brains, or altered relationships with githyanki/githzerai—but these are framed as regional manifestations rather than wholly separate species. Fan and narrative adaptations occasionally depict sympathetic or rebellious mind flayers, but official rules and core lore treat these as rare exceptions.

Archive Dossier — Powers

Mind Blast

Source Version Strategic Review #1; Eldritch Wizardry; AD&D Monster Manual; The Illithiad; Lords of Madness; D&D 3E/3.5E; D&D 5E Monster Manual

Description

A cone or radius of psionic energy that overwhelms the minds of nearby creatures. In early descriptions (Strategic Review, Eldritch Wizardry), Mind Blast is a 5‑foot radius wave of ‘Psi force’ that affects opponents differently based on intelligence, causing permanent insanity, rage, confusion, coma, or death. Later editions standardize it as a cone or area attack that can stun, incapacitate, or render victims unconscious, often with saving throws or resistance mechanics. Narratively, Mind Blast is the illithid’s primary battlefield weapon, allowing it to disable groups of foes before closing in to extract brains.

Limitations

Mechanically limited by range, area, and saving throws; many editions restrict its use per round or per day. Creatures with high mental ability scores, psionic defenses, or specific resistances (e.g., mind blank, protection from evil, certain racial traits) can resist or negate its effects. In some settings, Mind Blast is less effective against constructs, undead, or creatures without conventional brains.

Contradictions

Early rules emphasize extreme, often permanent effects (insanity, death), while later editions tone these down to stunned or incapacitated conditions for balance. Some sources treat Mind Blast as purely psionic; others allow it to be affected by magic‑resistance mechanics, creating ambiguity about whether it is ‘magical’ or strictly psionic.

Brain Extraction and Consumption

Source Version Strategic Review #1; AD&D Monster Manual; Dragon #78 “Ecology of the Mind Flayer”; The Illithiad; Lords of Madness; Forgotten Realms Wiki; D&D 5E Monster Manual

Description

Illithids use their tentacles to grasp a victim’s head, burrow through the skull, and extract the brain, which they then consume. This is both a feeding mechanism and a ritualized act of domination: consuming a brain is said to absorb aspects of the victim’s memories and personality, though the degree of retention varies by source. The act is graphically described in multiple texts as a slow, horrifying process, often performed while the victim is still alive.

Limitations

Requires the victim to be immobilized or sufficiently restrained; many rules specify that the illithid must maintain a grapple for several rounds before extraction. Creatures without brains, with nonstandard anatomy, or protected by certain spells or psionic defenses may be immune or resistant. Brain extraction is typically lethal and cannot be performed on already dead creatures in a way that yields full nourishment.

Contradictions

Some sources claim that illithids gain detailed memories from consumed brains; others suggest only vague impressions or emotional residue. The degree to which brain consumption contributes to Elder Brain knowledge versus individual illithid experience is also variably described.

Psionic Domination and Thrall Creation

Source Version The Illithiad; Lords of Madness; Dragon #78; Forgotten Realms Wiki; D&D 3E/3.5E psionics rules; D&D 5E Monster Manual

Description

Mind flayers possess powerful telepathic abilities that allow them to charm, dominate, and enslave other creatures. They can impose mental control, compel obedience, and overwrite free will, creating thralls who serve as guards, laborers, or infiltrators. In many texts, illithid communities are surrounded by rings of dominated slaves drawn from multiple species, including humans, dwarves, drow, and others.

Limitations

Domination typically requires line of sight, range, and concentration; thralls can sometimes break free through strong will, magical protection, or external intervention. High‑level spells (e.g., protection from evil, mind blank, greater restoration) can disrupt or remove domination. Some creatures (e.g., certain undead, constructs, or mindless beings) are immune.

Contradictions

Different editions treat domination as either a spell‑like ability, a psionic power, or a unique illithid effect, leading to rules differences in how it interacts with magic resistance and anti‑magic fields. Narrative sources sometimes imply near‑absolute control, while game mechanics allow for saving throws and resistance.

Telepathy and Qualith Script

Source Version The Illithiad; Lords of Madness; Forgotten Realms Wiki; D&D Lore Wiki; D&D 3E/3.5E; D&D 5E

Description

Illithids communicate primarily via telepathy, sharing thoughts, emotions, and complex concepts without spoken language. They also use Qualith, a tactile, psionically‑read script inscribed on surfaces in raised patterns. Telepathy allows them to coordinate silently, issue commands to thralls, and interface with the Elder Brain’s collective consciousness.

Limitations

Telepathy is usually limited by range (often 60–120 feet for individual illithids, much greater for Elder Brains) and may be blocked by certain materials, wards, or planar boundaries. Qualith requires specialized training or illithid physiology to read; non‑illithids generally cannot interpret it without magical or psionic assistance.

Contradictions

Some sources suggest that telepathy allows illithids to communicate across vast distances via Elder Brain relays; others restrict it to local ranges. The exact nature of Qualith (purely tactile vs. multi‑sensory) varies between descriptions.

Ceremorphosis (Tadpole Implantation)

Source Version Dragon #78 “Ecology of the Mind Flayer”; The Illithiad; Lords of Madness; Forgotten Realms Wiki; D&D Lore Wiki

Description

Ceremorphosis is the process by which illithids reproduce: they implant a tadpole from the Elder Brain’s brine pool into the brain of a humanoid host. Over days or weeks, the tadpole consumes and replaces the host’s brain, transforming the body into a new illithid. This process is described as agonizing and invariably fatal to the host’s original personality.

Limitations

Ceremorphosis requires suitable hosts—typically humanoids of specific size and physiology. Some hosts produce variant illithids or failed transformations. The process can be interrupted by magical or psionic intervention, though success is rare. Elder Brains control tadpole production and implantation, limiting reproduction to community needs.

Contradictions

Origin theories differ on whether ceremorphosis is the original illithid reproductive method or a later adaptation. Some sources hint at alternative or experimental methods (e.g., cloning, arcane rituals), but these are not consistently attested.

Elder Brain Collective Consciousness

Source Version Dragon #78; The Illithiad; Lords of Madness; Forgotten Realms Wiki; D&D 5E Monster Manual (Elder Brain entry)

Description

Each illithid community is centered on an Elder Brain—a massive brain in a brine pool that houses the memories and personalities of deceased illithids. Living mind flayers are psychically linked to the Elder Brain, which issues commands, stores knowledge, and coordinates community strategy. Elder Brains possess immense psionic power, including long‑range telepathy, mental surveillance, and defensive abilities.

Limitations

Elder Brains are physically immobile and dependent on their brine pools and lairs. They can be attacked and destroyed, though this is extremely difficult and usually requires high‑level adventurers. Their influence is strongest within their lairs and diminishes with distance, though some sources grant them extended planar reach.

Contradictions

Some texts treat Elder Brains as purely collective entities with no individual will; others portray them as distinct personalities with their own ambitions, sometimes at odds with individual illithids. The exact mechanism by which deceased illithids’ minds are absorbed is variably described.

Psionic and Magical Variants (Ulitharids, Alhoons, Vampiric Illithids)

Source Version The Illithiad; Lords of Madness; Menzoberranzan boxed set; Card Game Database Wiki summary; Forgotten Realms Wiki

Description

Illithid variants include ulitharids (larger, more powerful mind flayers with additional tentacles and higher status), alhoons or illithiliches (illithids who become liches to achieve personal immortality), and vampiric illithids (hybrid entities with vampiric traits). These variants possess enhanced psionic and/or magical abilities, such as stronger Mind Blasts, spellcasting, and unique lair powers.

Limitations

Ulitharids are rare and often closely monitored by Elder Brains; alhoons are typically outcasts, hunted by their own kind for defying Elder Brain authority. Vampiric illithids are unstable and may suffer from conflicting biological drives. All variants retain core illithid vulnerabilities to anti‑psionic measures and certain magical protections.

Contradictions

Different sources disagree on whether ulitharids inevitably become Elder Brains or simply serve as elite leaders. The status of alhoons within illithid society varies: some texts depict them as universally reviled; others allow for secret alliances. Vampiric illithids appear in limited, setting‑specific materials and are not universally recognized.

Weaknesses and Vulnerabilities

Source Version D&D Lore Wiki; Lords of Madness; D&D 3E/3.5E rules; D&D 5E Monster Manual

Description

Illithids are physically weaker than many martial foes, relying on psionics and thralls. They are vulnerable to direct physical attacks if deprived of their powers or caught off guard. Anti‑magic fields, psionic dampening, and certain spells (e.g., mind blank, protection from evil, dispel magic) can reduce or negate their abilities. Creatures without minds or brains—constructs, some undead, oozes—are poor targets for their signature attacks.

Limitations

These weaknesses are situational; illithids typically design lairs and strategies to minimize exposure, using thralls and traps to keep enemies at a distance. Their reliance on psionics means that environments or artifacts that disrupt mental powers can significantly reduce their threat.

Contradictions

Some narrative sources portray illithids as nearly unstoppable masterminds, while game mechanics emphasize their moderate physical stats and the availability of countermeasures. The degree to which anti‑magic affects psionics varies by edition and setting.

Archive Dossier — Relationships

Elder Brain

Type family|political|ritualSource Dragon #78; The Illithiad; Lords of Madness; Forgotten Realms Wiki; D&D 5E Monster Manual

Description

The Elder Brain functions as both progenitor and ruler of an illithid community. It houses the minds of deceased illithids, issues commands, and controls tadpole production. Individual mind flayers are psychically linked to the Elder Brain, experiencing it as a combination of parent, god, and absolute sovereign.

Version Note

Consistent across 2E–5E and major sourcebooks; early editions mention communal brains less explicitly.

Conflicting Accounts

Some sources emphasize Elder Brains as purely collective entities; others grant them distinct personalities and ambitions. The degree of individual autonomy illithids retain varies between descriptions.

Githyanki

Type hostileSource The Illithiad; Lords of Madness; various campaign settings; Forgotten Realms Wiki

Description

Githyanki are former illithid slaves who rebelled and now wage eternal war against mind flayers. They hunt illithids, destroy Elder Brains, and raid nautiloid ships, viewing illithid extermination as a sacred duty.

Version Note

Stable across settings: githyanki hatred of illithids is a defining trait.

Conflicting Accounts

Minor variations exist in the scale of githyanki campaigns and their success; some settings depict them as major threats to illithid survival, others as sporadic raiders.

Githzerai

Type hostileSource The Illithiad; Lords of Madness; Planescape materials; Forgotten Realms Wiki

Description

Githzerai, like githyanki, descend from illithid slaves but chose a path of asceticism and chaos‑aligned philosophy. They oppose mind flayers through targeted strikes and ideological rejection of domination, often clashing with illithid enclaves in Limbo and the Underdark.

Version Note

Consistent portrayal as enemies of illithids, though less militaristic than githyanki.

Conflicting Accounts

Some narratives emphasize githzerai as covert saboteurs; others depict them as more distant, focusing on philosophical opposition rather than direct warfare.

Ilsensine

Type worshipper|religiousSource The Illithiad; Lords of Madness; Forgotten Realms Wiki; D&D deity references

Description

Ilsensine is the primary deity of illithids, portrayed as a massive brain with tentacles extending into infinity. Mind flayers revere Ilsensine as the embodiment of perfect intellect and psionic power, seeking to emulate its cold, all‑consuming knowledge.

Version Note

Widely attested in 2E–3.5E and setting materials; 5E references are more limited but consistent.

Conflicting Accounts

Some sources treat Ilsensine as a true god; others frame it as a powerful quasi‑deity or conceptual entity. The exact nature of its interaction with Elder Brains is debated.

Maanzecorian

Type worshipper|religiousSource The Illithiad; Lords of Madness; Forgotten Realms Wiki

Description

Maanzecorian is an illithid deity associated with knowledge and records, particularly of the dead. Illithids who venerate Maanzecorian focus on cataloging and preserving information, including the memories of consumed brains.

Version Note

Prominent in 2E–3E lore; later materials sometimes downplay or omit Maanzecorian.

Conflicting Accounts

Some sources state that Maanzecorian was destroyed or diminished in planar conflicts, leading to shifts in illithid religion; others leave its status ambiguous.

Thralls (Enslaved Humanoids)

Type ownership|exploitative|victimSource Dragon #78; The Illithiad; Lords of Madness; D&D Lore Wiki; Forgotten Realms Wiki; Baldur’s Gate 3

Description

Illithids maintain large populations of thralls—humanoids whose minds are dominated or altered to serve as laborers, guards, and experimental subjects. These relationships are explicitly exploitative: thralls are denied autonomy, subjected to torture and experimentation, and often consumed when no longer useful.

Version Note

Thrall‑keeping is a constant feature across editions and settings.

Conflicting Accounts

Some narratives allow for rare thralls who partially resist or escape domination; others treat thrall status as effectively irreversible without powerful intervention.

Alhoons (Illithid Liches)

Type hostile|political|creativeSource Menzoberranzan boxed set; The Illithiad; Lords of Madness; Forgotten Realms Wiki

Description

Alhoons are illithids who pursue lichdom, rejecting Elder Brain absorption in favor of personal immortality. This places them in conflict with mainstream illithid society, which views them as heretics and hunts them. Alhoons often form small cabals, experimenting with necromancy and arcane lore.

Version Note

Introduced in 2E and elaborated in later sourcebooks.

Conflicting Accounts

Some sources depict occasional pragmatic alliances between Elder Brains and alhoons; others insist on absolute enmity.

Ulitharids

Type family|politicalSource The Illithiad; Lords of Madness; Forgotten Realms Wiki

Description

Ulitharids are elite illithids with additional tentacles and greater psionic power. They often serve as high‑ranking leaders within communities and are sometimes destined to become new Elder Brains.

Version Note

Consistent portrayal as rare, powerful variants.

Conflicting Accounts

Whether all ulitharids eventually transform into Elder Brains or only some is disputed; different texts offer different models of succession.

Far Realm

Type cosmologicalSource Lords of Madness; D&D 3E/4E cosmology; D&D Lore Wiki

Description

Many modern sources link illithids to the Far Realm, a dimension of madness and alien geometry. In this view, mind flayers are either native to the Far Realm or heavily influenced by it, explaining their aberrant biology and psionic powers.

Version Note

Far Realm origin is emphasized in 3E–4E; earlier editions lack this framework.

Conflicting Accounts

Competes with the time‑traveling empire origin; some settings attempt to reconcile the two, others treat them as mutually exclusive theories.

Cultural Significance

Alongside the Beholder, the definitive D&D original monster. The ceremorphosis lifecycle is one of the most horrific concepts in TTRPG. The time-travel origin and hive mind society raise profound questions about free will and identity. Product Identity protected by Wizards of the Coast. Baldur's Gate 3 brought Illithid lore to millions of mainstream gamers.
