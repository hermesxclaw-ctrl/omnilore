Form

The narrative describes Alatangana as an anthropomorphic being with a physical body, as he is able to walk, touch, and interact with the world. His specific physical features (height, skin color, facial features) are not described in the surviving records. He is a tangible, embodied deity, not a formless spirit.

Powers

World Creation Alatangana transformed the primordial, uninhabitable earth into a habitable world. The exact nature of this transformation is not detailed in the summary, but it established him as the creator of the physical environment. Kono tradition His creative power was not ex nihilo (from nothing). He worked with an existing, but useless, earth. He is not an omnipotent creator. None documented.

Bestowal of the World He gave the world to his children (the first humans) as a gift, establishing them as its inhabitants and stewards. Kono tradition This act of generosity was conditional, as he later cursed them with death when they offended him. None documented.

Cursing (Death) In response to his children's disrespect, Alatangana cursed them with death, introducing mortality into the human condition. This is his most significant act of power, as it defines the human experience. Kono tradition This is a curse, not a direct act of destruction. It is a punishment that shapes the future of humanity. None documented.

Physical Embodiment and Interaction He is able to physically walk the earth, interact with his creation, and communicate directly with his children. He is an embodied deity. Kono tradition His physical form is not described, and his power is not tied to a specific weapon or artifact. None documented.

Accounts

In the beginning, the earth was a sterile, uninhabitable place. Alatangana, the creator, was lonely and sought companionship. He went to the house of Sa, the personification of Death (or a being associated with death), who lived with his wife and daughter. Alatangana asked Sa for his daughter's hand in marriage. Sa refused. Undeterred, Alatangana and the daughter eloped, fleeing to a distant land. Alatangana then used his power to transform the earth, making it fertile and habitable. He and his wife had many children. Alatangana, proud of his family, decided to visit Sa and show him his grandchildren. He took the children to Sa's house. When they arrived, the children, not knowing who Sa was, mocked him, laughing at his appearance. Alatangana was horrified and tried to silence them, but it was too late. Sa was deeply offended. As punishment for this disrespect, Sa cursed the children, declaring that they would all eventually die. This curse introduced death into the world. Alatangana, in turn, cursed Sa, stating that Sa would have no power over the world that Alatangana had made.

Having created the world and set humanity upon the earth, the creator Alatangana resolved to bestow on humankind a choice concerning the afterlife, to distinguish those who would die permanently from those who would live again. In the Kono telling, a messenger or intermediary was sent bearing the two ways, and through a lapse akin to those found in many creation myths the decree reached humanity only imperfectly, so that the way of death rather than the way of returning came to prevail for ordinary mortals. The story thus accounts for both the generosity of the high god and the sorrow of mortality, framing Alatangana as a benevolent but now-distant ruler over the fates of his creatures.

Content Notes

The central theme of the myth is the origin of death. Death is not a natural phenomenon but a punishment inflicted on humanity for its disrespect towards Sa. This is a classic 'origin of death' myth, explaining why humans are mortal.

This myth is part of a widespread genre of African 'origin of death' stories, which often feature a messenger, a trickster, or a failed test as the cause of mortality. The Alatangana myth is unique in that the curse is a direct response to a specific act of disrespect.

What We Don't Know

The primary gap is the lack of a confirmed, detailed primary source. The Wikipedia lead cites R.S. Rattray's 'Some Folk-Lore Stories and Songs in Chinyanja' (1907), but this title suggests a Bantu language context, not Kono. The exact ethnographic work that first recorded the Alatangana myth needs to be identified and consulted. Other gaps include: (1) The specific Kono-language text of the myth is not available. (2) The role of Alatangana in contemporary Kono religious practice is not documented. (3) There are no known artistic or iconographic representations of Alatangana. (4) The myth's relationship to other Mande creation stories is not explored in the available sources. (5) The specific cultural context of the myth (e.g., its role in initiation rituals or social structure) is not documented.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Alatangana as a cosmic 'Dad' who tries to do his best. He creates a world for his family, but he can't control everything. His kids mess up, and he has to live with the consequences. He's not a perfect, all-powerful being; he's a powerful being with human-like flaws. Sa, on the other hand, is the ultimate 'father-in-law from hell' — he's ancient, powerful, and you do NOT want to get on his bad side. The myth is a reminder that even gods have family drama, and that death is often the price we pay for the mistakes of our ancestors.
