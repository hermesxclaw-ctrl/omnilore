Who It Is

Somewhere in the card catalog of a great library — Yale's, if the story is true — there is a card for a book that does not exist. It was smuggled in by a student, decades ago, and librarians have quietly removed and re-filed it ever since, because people keep asking for the Necronomicon: the grimoire of the mad Arab Abdul Alhazred, banned by popes, burned by patriarchs, chained in the stacks of Arkham. The joke is that the card is exactly as real as the book — and the book, which began as the daydream of a five-year-old boy in Providence, Rhode Island, playing at being an Arab after reading the Arabian Nights, has ended up more real than most books that exist. It has been published, banned, cited in a murder trial, bound in fake human skin, and demanded of the Vatican. The most successful fictional book ever written is the one its author never wrote.

The book behaves like a contagion of knowledge. Reading leads, in Lovecraft's own summary, to 'terrible consequences'; the authorities of most countries and 'all branches of organised ecclesiasticism' suppress it; scholars who consult it end mad, dead, or vanished. Its personality is that of a threshold: it knows the Old Ones, it knows the gate (Yog-Sothoth), and it records the rites by which the gate is opened. Powers
Forbidden knowledge of the Old Ones

Within the fiction, the book contains an account of the Old Ones — their history, their nature, and the means of summoning them — including the claim that 'man is neither the oldest nor the last of earth's masters.'

Limit: Lovecraft never wrote the book's contents; only fragments exist in his fiction. Any complete text is a hoax or adaptation.

Summoning rites

In 'The Dunwich Horror,' Wilbur Whateley needs a specific rite from the unabridged Necronomicon (page 751 of the Dee translation) to open the gate for his brother; the book's rites 'howled through at their Seasons' are what the Old Ones wait for.

Limit: The rite is never printed; the copy Whateley needs is held at Miskatonic University, and he dies trying to steal it.

Corruption of readers

Lovecraft wrote that 'reading leads to terrible consequences' and the 'History' records that the Greek text 'impelled certain experimenters to terrible attempts.' Scholars who consult it in his stories meet madness, death, or disappearance.

Limit: The mechanism is never specified; the effect is atmospheric and deferred — the book's power is its reputation.

Prophecy (the couplet)

The book's most famous passage — 'That is not dead which can eternal lie, / And with strange aeons even death may die' — functions in-story as a prophecy of the Old Ones' return.

Limit: No other prophetic content is attested in Lovecraft.

Real-world agency (hoax editions and belief)

Outside the fiction, the book's power is sociological: readers have searched libraries for it, pranksters have catalogued it (including the Yale University Library card incident), booksellers and the Vatican Library receive requests for it, and several publishers have profited from 'real' editions.

Limit: No genuine manuscript exists; every purported copy is a hoax, an homage, or a work of fiction using the name.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Lovecraft deliberately refused to describe the book. He wrote that 'if anyone were to try to write the Necronomicon, it would disappoint all those who have shuddered at cryptic references to it.' Across his fiction only fragments of its physicality appear: black-letter Latin editions (the fifteenth-century German printing), a copy disguised under the title Qanoon-e-Islam, and unspecified 'hoary and mouldy' volumes shelved beside real occult books

Symbols — In Lovecraft's fiction the Necronomicon has no fixed iconography; it functions as a name and a rumor. Its symbols are textual: the black-letter German printing, the Wormius colophon, the page numbers of the Dee translation (751), and the Arabic word azif itself. Accounts I
The life and death of Abdul Alhazred'History of the Necronomicon' (1927/1938)

In-fiction c. 700-738 A.D.; text written 1927

Alhazred, a half-crazed poet of Sanaá in Yemen, wanders the ruins of Babylon, the subterranean secrets of Memphis, and the Empty Quarter of Arabia, where he claims to have found the annals of a race older than mankind beneath a nameless desert town and to have seen fabulous Irem, the City of Pillars. In his last years he settles in Damascus, where he writes Al Azif. In 738 A.D., according to the twelfth-century biographer Ebn Khallikan, he is seized by an invisible monster in broad daylight and devoured before a crowd of fright-frozen witnesses. The Azif survives him, gains a surreptitious circulation among philosophers, and begins its long career of translation, suppression, and burning.

The Hound and the Leng amulet'The Hound' (1922/1924)

1920s

Two decadent collectors rob a grave and find a jade amulet they recognize from 'the forbidden Necronomicon of the mad Arab Abdul Alhazred' — the soul-symbol of the corpse-eating cult of Leng. Their reading of Alhazred's book does not save them; the Hound of the title hunts them down. Death of both collectors; the book is implicated as the source of the knowledge that drew them to the amulet.

The Dunwich Horror and the 751st page'The Dunwich Horror' (1928/1929)

1928

Wilbur Whateley, the hybrid son of a human woman and the entity Yog-Sothoth, visits the library of Miskatonic University to consult the unabridged Necronomicon for the rite that would let his monstrous twin brother through the gate. The rite would have appeared on page 751 of the complete Dee translation; Wilbur's inherited copy is defective. He is killed by a guard dog before he can steal the book, and the twin is destroyed at the story's end. The longest excerpt of the Necronomicon in Lovecraft appears here; the book is established as the key to the Old Ones' return.

Accounts II
The 1938 publication — the 'hoax' begins'History of the Necronomicon' (Rebel Press chapbook, 1938; also Leaves, issue 2, 1938)

1938

A year after Lovecraft's death, R. H. Barlow's Rebel Press printed Lovecraft's mock history as a chapbook of eighty copies, presented with a straight face as a factual account. Written in 1927 as 'a mock-erudite synopsis of Abdul's life,' the text purports to be non-fiction — it even credits the rumor that Robert W. Chambers derived The King in Yellow from the book. This 1938 publication is the hinge on which the Necronomicon swung from private literary joke to public legend: from it, the tradition of treating the book as real — and of faking copies of it — descends. Readers began writing to libraries and dealers for the book; by the 1940s a student had smuggled a card for it into the Yale University Library catalog; commercial hoax editions followed in the 1970s.

The commercial hoaxes: Scithers, Simon, Hay, TysonWikipedia article 'Necronomicon' (lead, cross-checked against HowStuffWorks and the Simon Necronomicon article)

1973-2004

In 1973 George H. Scithers commercially published a hoax Necronomicon through Owlswick, with an introduction by L. Sprague de Camp detailing a fictional history of translators who died mysteriously; the text was repetitive nonsense glyphs in a fabricated language. The most famous hoax followed in 1977: the Simon Necronomicon, by a pseudonymous editor, which blends Mesopotamian myth with Lovecraftian allusion and claims descent from an ancient manuscript no one has ever produced; its publisher and framing made it a bestseller, and it is now itself a piece of occult culture (Owen Davies calls it 'a well-constructed hoax' while noting that grimoires have always been stitched together this way). George Hay's 1978 anthology, with an introduction by Colin Wilson, was assembled (as David Langford described) from a supposed computer analysis of a John Dee cipher text, actually written by Robert Turner. The Evil Dead: the Necronomicon Ex-MortisThe Evil Dead (1981), Evil Dead II (1987), Army of Darkness (1992), dir. Sam Raimi; HowStuffWorks, 'How the Necronomicon Works'

1981-1992

Sam Raimi's cabin-in-the-woods horror classic gives the Necronomicon its most famous cinematic form: a book bound in human skin and written in blood, whose recitation summons demons that possess the film's protagonists. The 1992 sequel Army of Darkness sends Ash Williams to the Middle Ages with the book's binding incantation, 'Klaatu barada nikto,' and a heroic battle to keep the book out of the dead's hands. The franchise's 'Book of the Dead' — an adaptation-specific object with almost nothing in common with Lovecraft's vague original — has become the default image of the Necronomicon worldwide. The franchise fixed the Necronomicon's modern iconography (flesh-bound, face-covered, screaming when opened) and carried the name into the mainstream; the 2002 'Book of the Dead' DVD edition packaged the film in a latex replica of Sullivan's prop.

Meaning of the nameThe title means 'an image of the law of the dead' (Lovecraft's own gloss: nekros + nomos + eikon).The Greek doesn't support that reading; -ikon is a neuter adjectival suffix, and the title is better rendered 'Book considering (or classifying) the dead' (Joshi) or 'Book of dead names' (popular).
When did the 'real' Necronomicon hoax begin?The 1938 posthumous publication of the 'History of the Necronomicon' (Rebel Press chapbook; Leaves) is the origin of the hoax tradition — a straight-faced pseudo-history that launched the legend.The first deliberate real-world hoaxes came later: the Yale library card (1940s), fanzine-era catalog listings (1950s), and the first commercial hoax edition (Scithers, 1973).
Is the book a spellbook?The Necronomicon is a book of spells and summoning rituals.Lovecraft's original is a forbidden history of the Old Ones; spells were added by later authors, the hoax editions, and the films.
Was Lovecraft telling the truth when he denied it was real?The Necronomicon is a real (or astral) book that Lovecraft accessed in dreams; his denials were part of the game.The book is 100% fiction; Lovecraft's letters to enquirers are unambiguous confessions, and he was 'frankly confessing the fakery.'
Authorship of the Simon Necronomicon'Simon' is the pseudonym of Peter Levenda, the registered copyright holder of several of the books.Levenda denies authorship; 'Simon' is a separate person or persona.
The anachronism of Olaus WormiusThe 1228 Latin translation by Olaus Wormius is part of the book's history.The real Olaus Wormius lived 1588-1654; the 1228 translation is impossible.

The Necronomicon is a real, ancient suppressed grimoire. — It was invented by H. P. Lovecraft, who spent years telling enquirers exactly that: 'there is no Necronomicon of the mad Arab Abdul Alhazred' (letter to Robert Bloch, 1933); '100% fiction' (letter to William Frederick Anger, 1934).

The 'Simon' Necronomicon (1977) is the authentic text, predating Lovecraft. — No manuscript exists; the book is a pastiche of Mesopotamian myth and occult sources (R. C. Thompson's Devils and Evil Spirits of Babylonia; Pritchard's Ancient Near Eastern Texts) with Lovecraftian allusion; Owen Davies calls it 'a well-constructed hoax.'

Lovecraft wrote the Necronomicon. — He wrote a one-page mock history and a few quoted fragments. He said writing the whole book 'would disappoint all those who have shuddered at cryptic references to it' and joked about an 'abridged Necronomicon' (letter to Robert E. Howard, 1932).

The Necronomicon is the Egyptian Book of the Dead. — The Egyptian Book of the Dead is a genuine ancient funerary text, unrelated to Lovecraft. 'Book of the Dead' is only a nickname for the Necronomicon in the Evil Dead franchise.

Reading the Necronomicon drives people insane. — This is an in-fiction trope of the horror stories and the RPG; no text exists to test it. In Lovecraft's fiction, the madness comes from the cosmic knowledge described, not from the paper.

Olaus Wormius was a medieval monk who translated the book in 1228. — The real Olaus Wormius was a seventeenth-century Danish physician (1588-1654) with no connection to any such book; the 1228 translation is fiction.

'Abdul Alhazred' is a plausible Arabic author name. — It is grammatically impossible in Arabic (the definite article is doubled), which is consistent with its actual origin: a five-year-old Lovecraft's self-chosen play name, possibly influenced by the pun 'all-has-read.'
