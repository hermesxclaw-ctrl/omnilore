Who It Is

The Necronomicon is the most famous fictional book in horror literature: a grimoire of eldritch lore written by the 'mad Arab' Abdul Alhazred in Damascus around 730 CE, containing the true names and summoning rituals of the Great Old Ones — entities that predate humanity, exist outside the known laws of physics, and regard human consciousness as a brief, irrelevant flicker. To read the Necronomicon is to understand, in full detail, the insignificance of all human endeavor. Lovecraft invented the book as a literary device to lend an air of documentary authenticity to his stories, but it grew beyond its origin: the Necronomicon is referenced by nearly every writer in the Cthulhu Mythos. August Derleth published supposed 'excerpts.' Real-world bookstores have sold 'authentic' editions as novelties. The book is said to consist of scripture-like chapters describing Azathoth (the blind idiot god), Yog-Sothoth (the gate and the key), Cthulhu (the sleeper in R'lyeh), Nyarlathotep (the crawling chaos), and the means of summoning them. Lovecraft claimed he dreamed the title. The book carries the most recognizable epigraph in weird fiction: 'That is not dead which can eternal lie, And with strange aeons even death may die.'

Provenance

First mentioned in Lovecraft's 1924 story 'The Hound' and developed through 'The Call of Cthulhu' (1928), 'The Dunwich Horror' (1929), and 'The Shadow out of Time' (1936). Lovecraft constructed a fake history: written by Abdul Alhazred (a mad Yemeni poet who wandered the ruins of Babylon before being devoured by an invisible monster), translated into Greek by Theodorus Philetas (950 CE), and into Latin by Olaus Wormius (1228 CE). The book was placed in the Miskatonic University Library, the British Museum, the Bibliothèque Nationale, and the Widener Library. Lovecraft invented every detail — no medieval grimoire called the Necronomicon exists — but his invention is so elaborate that the U.S. Library of Congress has a catalog entry for it.

Attested in H. P. Lovecraft's Weird Tales (1920s-30s) / Expanded Mythos / Pop CultureEra Compiled c. 730 CE (Yemen / Damascus), translated into Greek 950 CE, Latin 1228 CE; suppressed by Pope Gregory IX 1232 CE.

Form

Bound in skin — human skin, according to most accounts — with clasps of an unidentified black metal. The pages are parchment, yellowed with age, and the text is written in an angular script interleaved with diagrams that are physically painful to look at — geometries that should not exist, angles that violate Euclidean space.

Powers

The Necronomicon contains the only known complete account of how to open gates to other dimensions, summon the Great Old Ones, and read the history of the universe before humanity. Its reading causes madness, nightmares, and physical decay. It is said to be 'alive' — different readers see different texts; the book chooses what you may read.Forbidden knowledge of the Old Ones Within the fiction, the book contains an account of the Old Ones — their history, their nature, and the means of summoning them — including the claim that 'man is neither the oldest nor the last of earth's masters.' Lovecraft's own stories ('The Dunwich Horror,' 'The Call of Cthulhu,' 'At the Mountains of Madness') and the 'History of the Necronomicon.' Lovecraft never wrote the book's contents; only fragments exist in his fiction. Any complete text is a hoax or adaptation. Later authors and adaptations treat the book as a compendium of spells; HowStuffWorks notes this 'reputation as a book of spells' was added after Lovecraft, whose original conception was closer to a forbidden history.Summoning rites In 'The Dunwich Horror,' Wilbur Whateley needs a specific rite from the unabridged Necronomicon (page 751 of the Dee translation) to open the gate for his brother; the book's rites 'howled through at their Seasons' are what the Old Ones wait for. 'The Dunwich Horror' (1928/1929). The rite is never printed; the copy Whateley needs is held at Miskatonic University, and he dies trying to steal it. The Simon Necronomicon (1977) supplies elaborate rituals and claims real efficacy; the film adaptations give the book a binding incantation ('Klaatu barada nikto' in Army of Darkness). None of these derive from Lovecraft.Corruption of readers Lovecraft wrote that 'reading leads to terrible consequences' and the 'History' records that the Greek text 'impelled certain experimenters to terrible attempts.' Scholars who consult it in his stories meet madness, death, or disappearance. 'History of the Necronomicon'; 'The Festival'; 'The Case of Charles Dexter Ward'; 'The Diary of Alonzo Typer.' The mechanism is never specified; the effect is atmospheric and deferred — the book's power is its reputation. In the Evil Dead films the book's power is immediate and physical (it summons demons when read aloud); this is adaptation-specific.Prophecy (the couplet) The book's most famous passage — 'That is not dead which can eternal lie, / And with strange aeons even death may die' — functions in-story as a prophecy of the Old Ones' return. 'The Nameless City' (1921), attributed to Alhazred; identified as from the Necronomicon in 'The Call of Cthulhu' (1928); quoted by later Mythos writers including Brian Lumley in 'The Burrowers Beneath.' No other prophetic content is attested in Lovecraft. None of substance; the couplet is the single most stable element across all versions of the Mythos.Real-world agency (hoax editions and belief) Outside the fiction, the book's power is sociological: readers have searched libraries for it, pranksters have catalogued it (including the Yale University Library card incident), booksellers and the Vatican Library receive requests for it, and several publishers have profited from 'real' editions. Wikipedia article 'Necronomicon' (lead); Edward Guimont, 'The Necronomicon Yalensis and Lovecraft in Connecticut' (Lovecraftian Proceedings No. 4, 2022); Joseph P. Laycock, 'How the Necronomicon Became Real' (2019). No genuine manuscript exists; every purported copy is a hoax, an homage, or a work of fiction using the name. Occultists such as Kenneth Grant (The Magical Revival, 1972) argue the book exists astrally in the Akashic records; skeptics such as Donovan K. Loucks (The H. P. Lovecraft Archive) respond that such claims are unfalsifiable opinion.

Weaknesses & Wards

It cannot be burned — accounts suggest the book resists ordinary destruction. The only defense is not to read it. Lovecraft's scholars who succeed in using it to summon entities invariably die horribly or are absorbed into the being they summoned.

Accounts

Alhazred, a half-crazed poet of Sanaá in Yemen, wanders the ruins of Babylon, the subterranean secrets of Memphis, and the Empty Quarter of Arabia, where he claims to have found the annals of a race older than mankind beneath a nameless desert town and to have seen fabulous Irem, the City of Pillars. In his last years he settles in Damascus, where he writes Al Azif. In 738 A.D., according to the twelfth-century biographer Ebn Khallikan, he is seized by an invisible monster in broad daylight and devoured before a crowd of fright-frozen witnesses.

Two decadent collectors rob a grave and find a jade amulet they recognize from 'the forbidden Necronomicon of the mad Arab Abdul Alhazred' — the soul-symbol of the corpse-eating cult of Leng. Their reading of Alhazred's book does not save them; the Hound of the title hunts them down.

Wilbur Whateley, the hybrid son of a human woman and the entity Yog-Sothoth, visits the library of Miskatonic University to consult the unabridged Necronomicon for the rite that would let his monstrous twin brother through the gate. The rite would have appeared on page 751 of the complete Dee translation; Wilbur's inherited copy is defective. He is killed by a guard dog before he can steal the book, and the twin is destroyed at the story's end.

A year after Lovecraft's death, R. H. Barlow's Rebel Press printed Lovecraft's mock history as a chapbook of eighty copies, presented with a straight face as a factual account. Written in 1927 as 'a mock-erudite synopsis of Abdul's life,' the text purports to be non-fiction — it even credits the rumor that Robert W. Chambers derived The King in Yellow from the book. This 1938 publication is the hinge on which the Necronomicon swung from private literary joke to public legend: from it, the tradition of treating the book as real — and of faking copies of it — descends.

In 1973 George H. Scithers commercially published a hoax Necronomicon through Owlswick, with an introduction by L. Sprague de Camp detailing a fictional history of translators who died mysteriously; the text was repetitive nonsense glyphs in a fabricated language. The most famous hoax followed in 1977: the Simon Necronomicon, by a pseudonymous editor, which blends Mesopotamian myth with Lovecraftian allusion and claims descent from an ancient manuscript no one has ever produced; its publisher and framing made it a bestseller, and it is now itself a piece of occult culture (Owen Davies calls it 'a well-constructed hoax' while noting that grimoires have always been stitched together this way). George Hay's 1978 anthology, with an introduction by Colin Wilson, was assembled (as David Langford described) from a supposed computer analysis of a John Dee cipher text, actually written by Robert Turner. Donald Tyson's 2004 reconstruction, Necronomicon: The Wanderings of Alhazred, is openly a fictional reconstruction by an author who states the book is not real.

Sam Raimi's cabin-in-the-woods horror classic gives the Necronomicon its most famous cinematic form: a book bound in human skin and written in blood, whose recitation summons demons that possess the film's protagonists. The 1992 sequel Army of Darkness sends Ash Williams to the Middle Ages with the book's binding incantation, 'Klaatu barada nikto,' and a heroic battle to keep the book out of the dead's hands. The franchise's 'Book of the Dead' — an adaptation-specific object with almost nothing in common with Lovecraft's vague original — has become the default image of the Necronomicon worldwide.

Cultural Significance

The Necronomicon is the template for every 'forbidden book' in modern horror: The Evil Dead's Book of the Dead is explicitly based on it; Hellboy's occult tomes; every librarian's-trope about the book that must not be read. It has been cited in real court cases, listed in real-world library catalogs, and inspired actual scholarly inquiries into its (non-)existence. Its cultural reach extends from heavy metal album covers to video game quest items (Elder Scrolls, Bloodborne).

A way to hold it in your mind

The Necronomicon is the only book in history whose author never wrote it, whose publishers never saw it, and whose readers have been reading it for a century. It works the way a black hole works: Lovecraft built the event horizon — the name, the rumors, the couplet, the bibliography of bans and burnings — and every later writer, hoaxer, filmmaker, and fan has been matter falling in. The book's 'power' is that it is a vacuum of content with a gravity well of reputation, which is exactly what Lovecraft intended when he said that writing it would only disappoint everyone who shuddered at the references.
