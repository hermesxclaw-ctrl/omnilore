Who It Is

In the shadow of the famous siege where Vercingetorix threw down his arms at Caesar's feet, a quieter monument stands: a stone, carved in the victor's language, bearing the name of a god the Romans didn't conquer, but chose to borrow. His name is Alisanos, 'He of the Alder.' He is a ghost of a god — no myths, no face, no stories — just a whisper of a name, a tree, and a war. He is the ultimate proof that history is written by the survivors, but even the vanquished leave their fingerprints on the stones.

Unknown. As a local deity, his behavioral traits are not recorded. The Roman interpretation suggests he was a god of war, perhaps a protector of the tribe, but this is an inference from the single Mars epithet. His original, pre-Roman character is likely to have been tied to the natural world (the alder grove) and possibly to tribal sovereignty or healing, common traits among Celtic gods, but these are speculative.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Alisanos is generally connected to the Gaulish (Celtic) root *alisā, meaning 'alder tree' or 'alder grove' (compare Old Irish *ailes* or *fern* and the Gaulish *alisia*). The suffix -anos/-aunos is a common Gaulish theonymic marker, often indicating association with a place or dedicated san

Pronunciation — ah-LEE-sah-nos (Latinized) / ah-lee-SAH-nos (Gaulish approximate). The 'a' is short in the first two syllables, with stress falling on the second syllable in Latin reading.

Aliases — Alisanus, Alisano, Alisaunus

Powers
Divine Protection (Martial)

As a god identified with Mars, Alisanos would have been invoked for protection in battle and for the martial strength of the community. This is the only 'power' explicitly suggested by the epigraphic evidence.

Limit: This power is inferred from the Mars identification, not from any specific mythological story. It is a Roman interpretation of a local god's function.

Chthonic/Tree Association

Based on the etymology of his name, Alisanos may have been a god of the alder tree, possibly associated with the underworld, regeneration, and the natural cycle. Alder wood is water-resistant and was used for making piles and foundations, linking it to protection and stability.

Limit: This is a linguistic and comparative inference, not an attested belief. No myth or ritual connects Alisanos to the alder tree beyond his name.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No direct visual representation of Alisanos survives. No statues, reliefs, or coins depict him. His appearance is completely unknown and unattested. Any description of him would be pure invention. The only 'image' we have is the name itself, which evokes the alder tree, and his Roman identification with Mars, which suggests he may have been depicted in Roman style if any statue existed, but none has been found.

Symbols — The alder tree is the only symbol directly associated with his name. In Celtic cultures, the alder was a sacred tree, often associated with strength, protection, and the Otherworld. The Roman Mars connection would conventionally bring symbols like the spear, shield, and wolf, but these are not attested for Alisanos specifically.

Domains — Epigraphically attested: War (via the Mars identification). Inferred from etymology: the alder tree and possibly the forest or grove. Inferred from context: local/tribal god of the Lingones, likely a protector of the community and its territory.

No Surviving MythologyN/A

N/A

There are no myths, legends, or stories about Alisanos. He is a name on a stone, nothing more. The only 'story' is the historical one: a local Celtic god was given a Roman name and placed within the Roman pantheon, a common act of religious syncretism during the Roman Empire. His identity was partially preserved by being equated with a major Roman deity, ensuring his name survived in inscriptions, but his original mythology was lost.

Alisanos was a major, widely-worshipped god of the Roman Empire. — He was a highly local deity of the Lingones tribe, with evidence found only at Alésia. He was not a major pan-Roman or pan-Celtic god.
