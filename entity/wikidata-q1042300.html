The Approach

When a scientist speaks of 'fauna,' they are not merely listing animals—they are invoking a Roman goddess of earth and fertility, a divine name that Carl Linnaeus borrowed in 1745 to catalogue the wildlife of Sweden. From the frozen realms of cryofauna to the hidden aquifers of stygofauna, the term has expanded to cover every conceivable habitat, including the theoretical alien analogues of xenofauna. This is the story of a word that became a scientific cornerstone, yet retains echoes of ancient mythology.

Etymology

From the name of Fauna, a Roman goddess of earth and fertility, related to the god Faunus and the forest spirits called fauns. All cognates with the Greek god Pan; Modern Greek equivalent is 'panis' or 'panida' (πανίς, πανίδα).

Cultural Ownership

Western scientific tradition, originally Latin/Roman, adopted globally in biology.

Disputed Identifications

The term is sometimes used loosely to mean 'animals' in general, but technically refers to the animal life of a specific region or time. There is potential confusion with the mythological figure Fauna, but the scientific usage is distinct.

Domains & Habitats

The term is used to describe animal life in various habitats, leading to subdivisions like cryofauna (cold regions), cryptofauna (hidden microhabitats), epifauna (on seafloor surface), infauna (within seafloor sediments), limnofauna (freshwater), macrofauna (large benthic/soil organisms), megafauna (large animals), meiofauna (small benthic invertebrates), mesofauna (macroscopic soil animals), microfauna (microscopic animals), stygofauna (groundwater), troglofauna (caves), and xenofauna (theoretical alien analogues).

Version Differences

The term has evolved from a simple catalogue of animals to a comprehensive ecological classification system. Different studies may use varying sieve sizes to define macrofauna and meiofauna, leading to slight variations in usage.

Stories & Myths

The Naming of Fauna

Source The provided source material and historical knowledgeCulture Western scientificPeriod 18th century

Summary

Carl Linnaeus, the Swedish botanist and zoologist, coined the term 'fauna' in 1745 for his book 'Fauna Suecica', a catalogue of Swedish animals. He derived it from the name of the Roman goddess Fauna, thus linking the scientific study of animals to ancient mythology.

Participants

Carl Linnaeus

Consequences

The term became standard in biology, leading to the creation of similar terms for plants ('flora') and fungi ('funga').

Variants

The term was later extended to include various subdivisions based on habitat and size.
