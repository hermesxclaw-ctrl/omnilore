Who It Is

You are walking home on a night road so empty that your own footsteps are the only sound — and then there is a second pair. Beto-beto. Beto-beto. It matches your pace exactly: speed up and it quickens, stop and it stops, and when you turn, the road behind you holds nothing at all. This is Betobeto-san, the Japanese yōkai of the second set of footsteps, and the entire Japanese folklore tradition's answer to it is not an amulet, a blade, or an exorcist — it is manners. Step to the side of the road, bow slightly to the invisible sound at your shoulder, and say, gently, 'Betobeto-san, after you — please go ahead.' And the footsteps will walk past you, and fade up the road, and trouble you no more. In the whole global catalogue of monsters, there may be no other being that is defeated, completely and permanently, by a polite request. That is Betobeto-san's strange and quietly profound place i

Betobeto-san walks behind people on empty night roads, matching the victim's pace exactly — accelerating when they accelerate, stopping when they stop, never closing the gap nor falling behind, so that the sound always remains 'one pace behind.' It causes no recorded physical harm in any classical telling. Its defining behavioral trait is absolute, almost courteous compliance: if the walker steps to the side of the road and addresses it politely — 'Betobeto-san, after you / please go ahead' — the footsteps halt, move past, and fade away up the road. Name & Tongue

Etymology — Pure onomatopoeia. 'beto-beto' (ベトベト) is the Japanese mimetic word for the sticky, soft, flat slap-slap of footsteps — commonly of geta or zōri sandals on a night road — reduplicated to evoke an unbroken, rhythmic sequence. Pronunciation — be-to-be-to-san — approximately 'BEH-toh-BEH-toh-sahn,' with even stress on each of the four open syllables of the base word and a soft, unaspirated final nasal. The repeated 'beto' is trochaic and rhythmic, mirroring the footsteps it names. In English adaptation it is almost always rendered 'Betobeto-san' with the French/Anglicised '-san' honorific.

Powers
Perfect pace-matching pursuit

Betobeto-san generates a rhythmic second set of footsteps that exactly track the walker's gait — accelerating, slowing, stopping, and resuming in lockstep, always remaining approximately one pace behind. The sound cannot be shaken by walking faster: it simply keeps time. This is its only 'active' power, and it is purely acoustic.

Limit: It does not physically intercept, attack, or block a fleeing walker; it only follows. Running is reported to be futile but non-essential to escape, because the simpler dismissal exists.

Complete invisibility

In the oldest traditions Betobeto-san has no visible form at all — turning around reveals an empty road. The footstep sound exists independently of any visual referent, making it a purely acoustic entity.

Limit: None escape by seeing it, because there is nothing to see; the power is total in the folk record.

Dread generation without violence

Betobeto-san produces terror through rhythm and anticipation alone — the universal night-walk dread of an unpaced, unplaceable second presence. Notable in that it accomplishes this without any recorded threat, wound, theft, or curse.

Limit: The dread is transient and fully curable by a single courtesy phrase.

Dismissal by courtesy ('after you')

Betobeto-san's single defining and most remarkable 'power' is its instant, total compliance with a polite spoken request. On hearing 'Betobeto-san, osaki ni okoshi / dozo o-saki ni' ('after you, Betobeto-san, please go ahead'), the footsteps halt and pass, then fade. This is functionally an invulnerability-to-etiquette weakness-as-power: the creature is disarmingly obedient to manners.

Limit: Works only if the speaker is genuinely addressing it by name and with courtesy. The Nara version specifies stepping to the side of the road and voicing the polite phrase; the Shizuoka version has the speaker likewise announce 'go ahead.'

Regional audio-shape plasticity

The entity manifests through region-specific onomatopoeia: 'beto-beto' (sticky slap, Nara/standard), 'pisha-bisha / bisha-bisha' wetter splashing footsteps (Shizuoka variant 'pishagatsuku'/'bishagatsuku'), and the related 'Bishakutsu' of Fukui's Sakai District. The same class of creature re-skins its soundtrack by region.

Limit: Regional variants are treated as related but possibly distinct entities; the 'power' is really an observation about transmission and naming rather than an active ability.

Accounts I
The Nara night-road followerSōgō Nihon Minzoku Goi (综合日本民俗語彙, 1955); repeatedly cited in national yōkai encyclopedias

Twentieth-century folk record; older rural oral roots

A person walking alone on a dark night road in Uda District hears a rhythmic beto-beto close behind them. They walk faster; the sound speeds up. They stop; the sound stops. Turning, they see nothing. The traditional solution, taught as practical wisdom rather than exorcism, is to step to the side of the road and say, courteously, 'Betobeto-san, please go ahead' (after you). The footsteps then move past and disappear up the road, and the walker is free. The story is notable for containing neither a monster's attack nor a hero's weapon — only the correct social script. The walker is unharmed and the encounter dissolves; the tale functions as an etiquette lesson embedded in folklore.

The Shizuoka mountain-descent encounterSōgō Nihon Minzoku Goi (1955) via the English-language Betobeto-san article; Shizuoka folk record

Twentieth-century folk record

In Shizuoka it is said that one will encounter Betobeto-san when descending from a small mountain (小山から下りてくる時). The walker hears the same matched footsteps behind them on the downhill path. The recorded remedy uses a slightly different verbal formula — the speaker announces 'coming' and requests the creature to 'go ahead' — after which the follower departs. The mountain-descent setting emphasizes the liminal threshold between the wild high ground and inhabited lowlands, the classic territory of the acoustic yōkai. Unharmed; the footsteps yield to the spoken courtesy and recede.

Accounts II
Shigeru Mizuki's alleged encounterShigeru Mizuki's own account, reported in the Betobeto-san reference literature and cited in the English-language article

Twentieth century

Manga artist and yōkai folklorist Shigeru Mizuki stated that he had encountered something appearing to be 'akin to' this yōkai — a second set of footsteps following him in the night that, on his own account, he gave visual form in his work. The anecdote is inseparable from his art-philosophy: he described yōkai as manifesting first as an ambient 'kehai' (presence/atmosphere) and 'wanting to take shape,' and explained that he 'gives form to kehai.' Betobeto-san, the footsteps-only being, is arguably the purest case in his canon of a yōkai that is all sensation until an artist shapes it. The account became a cornerstone of Betobeto-san's modern fame and, via Mizuki's hugely popular yōkai encyclopedias and GeGeGe no Kitarō, the mechanism by which the sound became a nationally beloved character.

The loneliness-void of the modern retellingMy Neighbor Yokai (となりの妖怪さん, Tonari no Yōkai-san), manga by noho (2017–), anime by Liden Films (2024)

2017–2024

In the slice-of-life supernatural series set in the village of Fuchigamori, the character Benmaru Kobayashi / Betobeto-san is a former 'void of loneliness that preyed on people' who has since taken a humanoid form and become a gentle, alcohol-loving member of the local found-family of yōkai and humans. This is the single most significant re-characterization of the entity: it transforms the benign-but-alien footstep-follower of folklore into a sympathetic, embodied creature, and for the first time in mainstream fiction explicitly attaches a malevolent 'preyed on people' past to Betobeto-san. Betobeto-san is normalized as a beloved archetype of the quietly lonely yōkai who needs company — a warm inversion of its folk role. The 'void of loneliness' backstory gives modern audiences the darker emotional reading absent from classical sources.
