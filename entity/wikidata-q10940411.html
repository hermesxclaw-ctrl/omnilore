Who It Is

Before Emperor Jimmu ever raised a bow in Yamato, another child of heaven had already come down — a god whose name means 'Light of the Fire' or 'Light of the Rice Ears,' remembered as the eldest son of Ninigi and the ancestor of the lords of Owari. His name is Hoakari, and whether he is one god or two has puzzled Japanese mythologists for thirteen centuries.

Attested: almost no narrative personality. Unlike his brother Hoori (Yamasachihiko) who has extensive adventure, Hoakari is a genealogical figure - his role is to be an ancestor, not an actor. Kujiki portrays him as obedient heavenly descender who settles and rules in Owari/Yamato. Disputed: whether his lack of stories indicates he was originally a local Owari deity later grafted into Imperial genealogy to legitimize the Owari clan's status. Unknown: motives, temperament, speech - no dialogues preserved.

Powers
Amatsukami Descent and Heavenly Authority (Tenson Korin lineage)

As son of Ninigi (grandson of Amaterasu), Hoakari inherits heavenly legitimacy to descend and rule on earth. In Kujiki Book 7, as Nigihayahi, he descends with heavenly regalia and retinue to Yamato/Owari, establishing authority over local kunitsukami.

Limit: Authority is genealogical-political, not magical; he does not control elements or wage war with supernatural powers in surviving myths. Subordinate to Amaterasu and Takamimusubi in heavenly hierarchy.

Ancestral / Generative Power (Ujigami function)

Primary attested 'power' is to beget the Owari clan and related lineages (Owari no Muraji, Tsumori, Amabe). Through him, descendants claim ritual authority over Atsuta Shrine and custody of Kusanagi.

Limit: No miraculous feats; power is lineage continuity and ritual privilege, not personal superhuman ability.

Fire / Light Association (Name-based domain)

By etymology (ho=fire, akari=light), associated with fire, sunlight, or shining rice ears. Some modern popular sources ascribe fire control or solar brilliance, but no ancient text describes him wielding fire as an ability.

Limit: No attested feats of creating or controlling fire; mother Konohanasakuya's fire ordeal is her feat, not his. Attribution of fire powers is reconstructed/inferred, not attested.

Accounts I
The Fire Ordeal Birth - Ninigi Tests Konohanasakuya-himeKojiki Vol.1, Nihon Shoki Book II (Age of Gods)

Yamato period, codified 712-720 CE (mythic time: Age of Gods, between Tenson Korin and Jimmu)

After Ninigi descends to earth, he weds Konohanasakuya-hime (daughter of Oyamatsumi). She becomes pregnant after a single night, and Ninigi suspects infidelity, saying a heavenly child should not be conceived so quickly. To prove her fidelity and the child's heavenly paternity, Konohanasakuya enters a parturition hut, seals it, and sets it ablaze, declaring that if the children are truly Ninigi's they will be born safely. She bears three sons amid the flames. In the main Kojiki/Nihon Shoki account these are Hoderi, Hosusori, and Hoori (Hikohohodemi). Variant traditions (Nihon Shoki issho, Kujiki) name the eldest as Hoakari/Hikohoakari, linking the fire ordeal directly to his name 'Fire Light.' Establishes heavenly paternity and legitimacy of Ninigi's line; explains why Ninigi's descendants have both heavenly and earthly natures; provides etiology for Hoakari's name and fire association; sets up later rivalry between sea (Hoderi) and mountain (Hoori) brothers. For Hoakari specifically, being born in fire makes him the 'child proven by fire.'

The Eldest Son and Ancestor of the Owari ClanSendai Kuji Hongi (Kujiki) Books 3 and 7; Shinsen Shojiroku (815)

Heian period codification (9th century), claiming to record Age of Gods / pre-Jimmu period

In the Kujiki's Tenson Hongi, Ninigi and Konohanasakuya have a son Hikohoakari (Hoakari), the eldest, who does not participate in the Hoderi-Hoori fishing-hook tale. Instead, he is granted or assumes authority over the Owari region. He becomes the ancestor (oyagami) of the Owari no Muraji, the powerful kuni no miyatsuko of Owari, and through them of related clans (Tsumori, Amabe). The Shinsen Shojiroku lists over a dozen clans as 'descendants of Hoakari no Mikoto,' cementing his role as a clan ujigami. Legitimizes Owari clan's high status and their hereditary role as priests of Atsuta Jingu and custodians of the sword Kusanagi; creates a parallel heavenly lineage alongside the Imperial line (which descends from Hoori); explains why Owari is called Owari (traditionally 'tail' - the last settled).

Hoakari as Nigihayahi - The Prior Descent to YamatoNihon Shoki Book II variant; Sendai Kuji Hongi Book 7; Nihon Shoki Jinmu chronicle

Mythic time: before Emperor Jimmu's eastern expedition; textual codification 720 CE and 9th-10th century

Nigihayahi no Mikoto, a heavenly deity bearing heavenly regalia, descends to Yamato (atop Mt. Ikaruga or in Kawachi) before Jimmu's conquest, marries local chief's daughter (Mikai? Tomibiko's sister), and rules the Yamato basin. When Jimmu arrives, Nigihayahi's son Umashimaji submits to Jimmu, proving Nigihayahi's heavenly origin. Kujiki explicitly states: 'Nigihayahi is Hoakari, also called Ame no Nigihayahi, also called Hikohoakari.' Thus Hoakari's descent is backdated to be earlier and independent of Ninigi's descent, making him the first Tenson ruler of central Japan. Explains why Yamato was already ruled by a heavenly descendant before Jimmu; resolves political need to integrate Mononobe and Owari lineages into Imperial legitimacy without making them rebels; makes Hoakari/Nigihayahi ancestor of Mononobe, Owari, and related military clans. After submission, his line becomes loyal supporters of the Imperial house.

Accounts II
Settlement in Owari and the Foundation of Masumida ShrineEngishiki Jinmyocho (927); Masumida Jinja engi (shrine legends, medieval); Owari no Kuni Fudoki fragments

Heian to medieval (10th-14th century shrine legends, claiming Age of Gods origin)

After his descent, Hoakari (as Ame no Hoakari or Masumida Myojin) settles in Owari and is enshrined at Masumida Jinja (modern Inazawa City), the Ichinomiya (chief shrine) of Owari Province. Engishiki lists Masumida as a Myojin Taisha. Medieval engi claim the shrine was founded where Hoakari's heavenly radiance first illuminated the Owari plain, or where he planted rice. The Owari clan as hereditary kannushi served the shrine and nearby Atsuta Jingu, linking Hoakari to the Kusanagi sword enshrined at Atsuta. Establishes Owari's religious center and the Owari clan's ritual authority; ties Hoakari to agricultural fertility of the Nobi Plain; explains why Masumida is ranked above other Owari shrines; provides living worship continuity to present day.

The Genealogical Bridge to the Imperial LineKujiki Book 7; Kojiki genealogies; Nihon Shoki Jimmu lineage

Mythic genealogy, codified 712-927 CE

Through Hoakari's descendants, the Owari line reconnects to the Imperial line. Kujiki traces Hoakari -> ... -> Owari clan daughters who marry into the early Imperial family. Notably, Hoakari's descendant Tamanooya or descendants of the Owari provide wives for early emperors (e.g., Emperor Jimmu's consort Himetataraisuzu-hime is sometimes linked via collateral lines in extended genealogies, though main Kojiki makes her daughter of Kotoshironushi). The broader point is that Hoakari's line and Hoori's Imperial line are presented as two branches of Ninigi's sons that reunite through marriage, ensuring Owari loyalty. Politically, makes Owari not outsiders but close kin to the throne; justifies their role as maternal relatives (gai seki) and shrine custodians; completes the triad of Ninigi's sons as founders of sea, mountain, and plain lineages.

Hoakari in the Shinsen Shojiroku Clan Register - The Proliferation of Descendant ClansShinsen Shojiroku (815), Kaibetsu and Shinsen sections

Early Heian (815 CE)

The Shinsen Shojiroku, commissioned to sort true vs. false clan claims, lists numerous clans as descendants of 'Hoakari no Mikoto' or 'Ame no Hoakari' or 'Hikohoakari.' Examples include Owari no Muraji, Tsumori no Muraji (Sumiyoshi priests), Amabe, and others in Yamato, Kawachi, and Owari. Each entry gives a brief origin: 'Descended from Hoakari no Mikoto, son of Ninigi, grandson of Amaterasu.' This is not a narrative story but a bureaucratic myth-making that solidifies Hoakari's status as a major ujigami on par with other Tenson ancestors. Institutionalizes Hoakari worship and gives legal privileges to claiming clans; preserves Hoakari's name when Kojiki had omitted him; provides modern scholars with evidence for Heian-period political use of mythology.

Hoakari is a major god with elaborate myths like Amaterasu or Susanoo. — Hoakari has almost no narrative myths in Kojiki/Nihon Shoki; he is primarily a genealogical ancestor figure. His 'stories' are clan genealogies and shrine legends, not adventure tales. His fame comes from ancestry, not deeds.

Hoakari is the god of fire who controls flames. — No ancient text describes Hoakari wielding or controlling fire. His name suggests fire/light, and his mother survived fire, but he has no attested fire powers. Attributing pyrokinesis is modern inference, not source-based.

Hoakari and Nigihayahi are definitely the same god, proven by ancient texts. — Identification is only explicit in the Sendai Kuji Hongi (9th-10th c., regarded as apocryphal) and later shrine tradition. Kojiki and Nihon Shoki main texts do not equate them. Modern scholarship treats it as disputed Heian syncretism, not settled fact.

There are ancient statues and images showing what Hoakari looked like. — No Kofun or Heian-period image securely identified as Hoakari exists. Current shrine statues are Edo-modern artistic reconstructions in court dress, not ancient iconography. Engishiki and earlier texts give no appearance.
