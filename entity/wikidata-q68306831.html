Who It Is

Imagine a family tree so potent it takes the form of a man. Not a diagram on paper, but a towering, dark-wood ancestor whose entire body is a writhing mass of tiny, carved descendants, each one a link in a chain that stretches back to the beginning of time. This is A'a, a deified ancestor from the Marquesas Islands, a masterpiece of Polynesian art that has been called 'the most important work of art in the British Museum' — and whose very existence is a testament to the power of lineage, the trauma of colonial loss, and the enduring spirit of a people.

As a deified ancestor, A'a was not believed to 'behave' in a narrative sense like a character in a myth. Instead, he was an object of veneration and a conduit for spiritual power (mana). Ritual behavior centered on the figure would have included offerings, prayers, and the recitation of genealogies that linked the living community to A'a. The figure was likely kept in a sacred meeting house or a dedicated shrine, and its presence was believed to protect the community, ensure fertility, and provide guidance. Powers
Ancestral Mana

As a deified ancestor, A'a was believed to be a vessel of immense spiritual power, or mana. This power was not magical in a fictional sense, but a real, culturally-acknowledged force that could be harnessed by his descendants. His presence was believed to sanctify a space and provide protection from malevolent spirits and enemies.

Limit: Mana was not unlimited; it required proper ritual maintenance, offerings, and the correct performance of genealogical recitations to be activated. The power was also tied to the physical figure—if the figure was desecrated or removed, the community's access to that mana was severed.

Genealogical Continuity

The figure's body, covered with small human figures, was a physical manifestation of the community's lineage. It was a tool for remembering and validating genealogy, which was crucial for social and political power in Marquesan society. A'a embodied the entire line of descent, making him the ultimate source of social identity.

Limit: This power was symbolic and social, not a physical or supernatural ability. Its effectiveness depended on the community's adherence to the genealogical system.

Protection and Intercession

The figure was likely invoked for protection in warfare and for the community's general well-being. As a powerful ancestor, A'a could intercede with the spirit world on behalf of the living, warding off danger and ensuring prosperity.

Limit: This power was conditional on the community's ritual purity and devotion. If the community failed in its duties, the ancestor's protection could be withdrawn.

Form & Signs

Appearance — A'a is a powerful, imposing wooden figure carved from a single block of dense hardwood. It stands roughly 1.17 meters tall, making it a substantial presence. The figure is anthropomorphic, with a broad, sturdy torso and a large, commanding head. The face is characterized by a prominent, open mouth, likely originally inlaid with shell or bone teeth (now lost), and large, circular eyes that would have been similarly inlaid with shell or pearl. Accounts I
The Genealogy of A'aMarquesan oral tradition (partially recorded by early missionaries and later anthropologists)

Pre-European contact (before 1790s)

The central narrative associated with A'a is not a story of epic deeds, but the genealogy he embodies. He was the primordial ancestor of a specific tribe or community on the island of Ahuahu. The small figures on his body are his descendants, each one representing a generation or a specific lineage. The narrative is one of creation and continuity: the community exists because A'a existed, and his power flows through the generations to the present day. The figure was a physical mnemonic for this vital story, recited and re-enacted in rituals. The figure legitimized the social hierarchy and land rights of the community. It was a focal point for communal identity and a source of spiritual strength.

Accounts II
The Loss of A'aMissionary accounts (John Williams) and subsequent historical records

1823-1824

The story of A'a's removal from his community is a painful and pivotal one. The London Missionary Society missionary John Williams visited Ahuahu in the early 1820s and, through a combination of persuasion, exchange, and the rapidly changing power dynamics brought by European contact, obtained the figure. The local people, likely under pressure and seeing the growing power of the Europeans and their God, relinquished the figure. Williams took it to England, where it was eventually donated to the British Museum in 1887. The removal severed the community's physical link to their ancestral power, a loss that resonates to this day. The figure became a famous museum artifact in the West, but its removal contributed to the cultural and spiritual disruption of the Marquesan community. In 2016, the British Museum lent the figure to the Marquesas Islands for a temporary exhibition, marking the first time it had returned to its homeland in nearly 200 years. This visit was an emotionally powerful event for the Marquesan people, allowing them to reconnect with a powerful symbol of their heritage

A'a in the Western ImaginationArt historical and literary reception

Late 19th - 21st century

Once in the British Museum, A'a captured the Western imagination. His striking form and the intricate detail of his carving made him a celebrated example of 'primitive art.' He was featured in numerous exhibitions and books on Oceanic art. The British poet and art critic Herbert Read famously described him as 'the most important work of art in the British Museum,' a statement that has been widely quoted and debated, cementing A'a's status as a masterpiece of world art. A'a became a symbol of the aesthetic value of Polynesian art. The quote from Read elevated his status from an ethnographic curiosity to a world-class masterpiece, influencing how Oceanic art was perceived and valued in the West.
