Who It Is

Before there was an emperor, before there was a dynasty, before there was even a written word, there were the Three Lords — a trio of culture heroes who pulled humanity out of the mud and taught us to cook our food, plant our crops, and marry our spouses. But here's the twist: nobody can agree on who they actually were. Was it Fuxi the serpent-sage with his carpenter's square, Nüwa the clay-sculptor of humanity, Shennong the divine farmer who tasted a hundred herbs and sometimes died doing it, or Suiren the fire-driller who showed us how to make flame? The ancient Chinese sources offer at least five different lineups, and that ambiguity is the point. The Three Lords aren't a pantheon — they're a placeholder for the moment when we stopped being animals and started being us.

As a collective, the Three Lords are characterized as the first civilizers of humanity. They are not typically depicted as warlike or dramatic figures; their heroism is civilizational. They teach, innovate, and establish order. Each Lord brings a specific gift of civilization: Suiren brings fire, Fuxi brings hunting, fishing, and the eight trigrams (or, in some versions, marriage and writing), Shennong brings agriculture and herbal medicine. They are depicted as wise, benevolent, and patient teachers who guide humanity from a state of nature toward organized society.

Civilizational Innovation

Each Lord introduces a foundational technology or practice that transforms human existence. Suiren discovers how to create fire by drilling wood (and sometimes is said to have been born from fire itself); Fuxi teaches hunting, fishing, animal domestication, and establishes the institution of marriage; Shennong introduces agriculture and discovers the medicinal properties of plants.

Limit: These are not supernatural powers in the modern sense; they are practical innovations presented as world-historical events. The Lords are not depicted as magicians but as teachers and inventors.

Cosmic Longevity

The Three Lords are said to have ruled for extraordinarily long periods. Some traditions give each Lord a reign of 18,000 years; other traditions multiply this. The Huainanzi and related texts describe them as inhabiting a primordial time when the normal rules of human life did not yet apply.

Limit: These are not personal powers but attributes of the primordial age they inhabit. In some rationalizing traditions, the long reigns are interpreted as the reigns of dynasties or lineages rather than of individual persons.

Cosmic Ordering

In the more abstract versions of the tradition, the Three Lords (as Heavenly Lord, Earthly Lord, and Human Lord) are responsible for establishing the fundamental structure of the cosmos. The Heavenly Lord creates the heavens, the Earthly Lord creates the earth, and the Human Lord creates humanity and its institutions.

Limit: This version is more mythological and less 'practical' than the culture-hero version. It is not the dominant tradition in popular culture, which tends to favor the Fuxi-Nüwa-Shennong triad.

Transmission of Esoteric Knowledge

In Daoist tradition, the Three Lords are associated with the transmission of esoteric knowledge, including the eight trigrams, the calendar, and the principles of yin and yang. Fuxi in particular is credited with creating the eight trigrams, which form the basis of the I Ching (Book of Changes).

Limit: This is a later development of the tradition, reflecting Daoist interest in Fuxi's trigrams as a cosmic code.

Symbols — Fire (Suiren), the carpenter's square and serpent body (Fuxi), the plow and herbs (Shennong), the eight trigrams (Fuxi), the compass (Nüwa), the cosmic triad of Heaven, Earth, and Humanity (the abstract version of the Three Lords). The number three itself is symbolically significant, representing the three realms of Heaven, Earth, and Humanity, and the three powers of the cosmos.

Domains — The Three Lords collectively preside over the transition from primordial chaos to civilization. Their domains include: (1) the cosmic order (establishing the patterns that govern the world); (2) the arts of subsistence (fire, hunting, agriculture, herbal medicine); (3) the social order (marriage, kinship, governance); (4) the intellectual order (writing, divination, the trigrams). Accounts I
The Three Lords Establish CivilizationComposite account drawn from Huainanzi, Fengsu Tongyi, and later encyclopedias

Warring States through Han dynasty (5th century BCE – 3rd century CE), with later elaborations

In the beginning, humanity lived like animals: naked, eating raw food, without marriage, without writing, without agriculture. The Three Lords appear at this primordial moment and each brings a gift. Suiren shows humanity how to drill wood to make fire, transforming the diet and providing warmth and protection. Fuxi teaches hunting, fishing, and the domestication of animals; he establishes the institution of marriage, preventing the chaos of unregulated unions. Shennong teaches agriculture, showing humanity how to cultivate the five grains, and tastes hundreds of herbs to discover their medicinal properties, establishing the foundations of Chinese medicine. Each innovation builds on the previous one, moving humanity from subsistence to society to civilization. The Three Lords are thus the bridge between the natural world and the human world. Humanity emerges from the state of nature and enters the state of civilization. Accounts II
Fuxi and Nüwa: The Serpent SiblingsVarious texts and Han dynasty pictorial art

Warring States through Han dynasty and beyond

In the most popular version of the tradition, Fuxi and Nüwa are depicted as siblings (and in some versions, spouses) with human upper bodies and serpent lower bodies. Their intertwined serpent tails appear in Han dynasty stone reliefs, often with Fuxi holding a carpenter's square and Nüwa holding a compass — symbols of cosmic order. In some versions of the flood myth, Fuxi and Nüwa are the only survivors of a great deluge, and they repopulate the earth. In other versions, Nüwa is the creator of humanity, fashioning the first people from yellow clay. The relationship between the two figures is complex and varies across texts: they are sometimes siblings, sometimes spouses, sometimes both. Their serpent bodies mark them as belonging to the primordial, pre-human world, even as they establish the institutions of human society. The pairing of Fuxi and Nüwa establishes the template for the Chinese origin myth. Shennong Tastes the Hundred HerbsVarious texts including the Shennong Bencao Jing (The Divine Farmer's Classic of Materia Medica) and later herbal traditions

Han dynasty attribution, with much later elaboration

Shennong, the Divine Farmer, is said to have tasted hundreds of herbs to discover their medicinal properties. In some versions, he has a transparent stomach, allowing him (and his assistants) to observe the effects of each plant as it is digested. This mythic account establishes the foundation of Chinese herbal medicine. The Shennong Bencao Jing, a Han dynasty text attributed to Shennong, catalogs 365 medicinal substances — one for each day of the year — organized into three categories: superior (non-toxic, life-prolonging), medium (tonic, some toxic), and inferior (strongly acting, often toxic). The text became the foundational pharmacopoeia of Chinese medicine. Shennong becomes the patron deity of agriculture and medicine. His 'tasting of the hundred herbs' is one of the most famous episodes in Chinese mythology, and the Shennong Bencao Jing remains a foundational text in Chinese medical tradition. The transparent stomach motif is a memorable and vivid image that has persisted in popular culture.

The Three Lords are always Fuxi, Nüwa, and Shennong — This is the most popular version, but there is no single canonical list. Different texts give different triads, and the composition varied across time and region.

The Three Lords are historical figures who actually lived — There is no historical evidence for the existence of the Three Lords. They are mythological figures who represent the transition from nature to civilization. The long reigns and miraculous abilities attributed to them are mythological, not historical.

The Three Lords are equivalent to the Greek gods or other pantheons — The Three Lords are not gods in the Greek or Norse sense. They do not live on a separate divine plane, do not intervene in human affairs as the Greek gods do, and are not primarily objects of worship. They are culture heroes and cosmic ancestors, closer to figures like Prometheus (who also brought fire to humanity) than to Zeus or Odin.
