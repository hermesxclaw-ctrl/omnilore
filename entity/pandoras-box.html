Who It Is

The most famous 'object' in Greek mythology never existed in Greece. When Hesiod told the story around 700 BCE, there was no box — there was a pithos, a great clay storage jar as tall as a person, half-buried in the earth, the same kind of jar the Greeks used to hold wine, olive oil, and occasionally the bodies of the dead. The familiar little lidded box of paintings and movies is a translation error that has outlived the truth it obscured: in 1508 the scholar Erasmus rendered Hesiod's pithos as pyxis, 'box,' and a 2,700-year-old story about a woman who uncorks a jar of plagues quietly became a story about opening a box of trouble. And locked inside that jar sat the one thing that would not fly free — a word whose meaning has launched a thousand arguments: elpis, hope, expectation, or some uneasy blur of all three. The box itself is inert; it is a receptacle whose menace lies in its contents and in the act of opening. In extended usage the idiom personifies an action that, once taken, unleashes a cascade of unforeseen and uncontrollable problems. Its 'personality' is a modern projection: curiosity lures the opener, and the consequences cannot be re-contained ('once opened, cannot be closed'). Powers
Contains and dispenses all evils (Hesiodic jar)

The pithos holds 'countless plagues' — sickness, death and other unspecified evils — which Pandora releases into the world when the lid is removed. Once outside, they afflict earth and sea without cease. This is the jar's primary mythic power: it is the reservoir from which all human evil was decanted into mortal existence.

Limit: One thing does not escape — elpis (hope or expectation) — because Pandora sealed the lid in time. The jar's power to dispense is not absolute; it retains a single occupant.

The un-closeable/irreversible aftermath (idiomatic power)

As an idiom, 'opening Pandora's box' names a one-way irreversible action: whatever the box releases cannot be put back. The property is that the consequences are permanently beyond the opener's control once the act is done.

Limit: This is a moral/allegorical property, not a literal magic; it describes human action and its fallout rather than an object's durable physical power.

Holds hope (elpis) as a sole remaining occupant

The box is the prison that keeps elpis from fully escaping. Depending on interpretation, this preserves hope for humanity (a comfort) or withholds the true antidote to misery; it may also keep humanity free of a false, idle hope that makes men lazy (Hesiod, Works and Days 498, 500).

Limit: Elpis is ambiguous: it may mean 'hope,' 'expectation,' or 'expectation of evil'; its function hangs on whether the jar is a prison (keeping hope from people) or a pantry (storing hope for people).

Symbolic identity with Pandora the earthen woman

Many scholars see a close symbolic analogy between Pandora, who was made from clay, and the clay jar that dispenses evils — both are earthen receptacles. In pre-Hesiodic strata Pandora/Anesidora was herself a chthonic earth-goddess who 'sends up gifts' (the blessings of the soil), a power Hesiod's myth inverts into a dispenser of evils.

Limit: The 'all-giving' goddess reading is a scholarly reconstruction from vase iconography and name etymology, not the explicit Hesiodic text; Hesiod himself shows no awareness of it (per the standard English commentary noted by Phipps).

Domains — A heavenly wedding gift brought from the gods to the household of Epimetheus (Works and Days). Mythically it belongs to the threshold between the divine and mortal worlds. Accounts I
The Gift of Fire and the Jar of Evils (Hesiod, Works and Days)Hesiod, Works and Days 60-105

Archaic, c. 700 BCE

After the Titan Prometheus steals fire from heaven and gives it to humanity, the king of the gods Zeus takes vengeance. He has Hephaestus mould the first woman, Pandora, from earth and water, and every god bestows a gift upon her (Athena teaches her weaving and clothing; Aphrodite sheds grace, longing and care; Hermes gives her a shameless mind, a deceitful nature and the power of lying speech; the Graces and Hours adorn her). Hermes names her Pandora, 'because all they who dwelt on Olympus gave each a gift, a plague to men who eat bread.' Prometheus warns his brother Epimetheus not to accept any gift from Zeus, but Epimetheus ('Afterthought') ignores the warning and takes Pandora as his bride. She brings with her a great jar into which the 'countless plagues' are sealed. Driven by curiosity, Pandora removes the lid, and all the evils — sickness, death, misery — fly out into the world. She hastens to shut the jar, but only one thing remains within: elpis, hope (or expectation). The Jar of Blessings (Aesopic fable, recorded by Babrius)Babrius, Fables (after Aesop), Perry Index 312

c. 2nd century CE recording an older tradition; related hint in Theognis of Megara (6th c. BCE)

In an alternative aetiology the gods send the jar to humans not as a punishment but as a gift of all good things. Rather than a named woman, a generic 'foolish man' (akratēs anthrōpos) opens the jar out of curiosity, and the blessings escape and fly back to heaven. Once the lid is replaced, only hope remains behind, 'promising that she will bestow on each of us the good things that have gone away.' Here the jar contains the blessings mortals lack, and hope is a true, earth-bound divine gift. Offers a more optimistic reading in which humanity's only retained good (hope) is preserved precisely because the good things fled. It reframes the whole myth from theodicy-of-evil to aetiology-of-hope.

Accounts II
Epimetheus as the Opener (Renaissance tradition)Erasmus, Adagia; Andrea Alciato's Emblemata (1534); Gabriele Faerno's Fabulae Centum (1563); Giulio Bonasone engraving; art of Farinati, Le Clerc

16th-17th centuries

Renaissance retellings shifted responsibility for the opening from Pandora to her husband Epimetheus ('Afterthought,' 'he whom mistakes made wise'). Erasmus' Latin Adagia opened the box, in his telling, by Epimetheus. Alciato depicted Hope seated on a jar declaring 'I alone stayed behind at home when evils fluttered all around.' Faerno's poem made hope the survivor of 'universal blessings' (bona universa): 'Of all good things that mortals lack, / Hope in the soul alone stays back.' Bonasone's engraving shows Epimetheus holding the lid of a jar from which the female personifications of the Roman virtues fly up, Hope delayed on the lip; Le Clerc's etching shows the lid plainly in Epimetheus' hand. Refocused the myth onto the loss of virtues and of hope rather than the release of disease; made Epimetheus (not Pandora) the tragic opener, softening the misogynist framing of the fault.

Pandora and the Five Ages (Hesiod's structural problem)Hesiod, Works and Days (the 'another story' of the Five Ages, Gold through Iron)

Archaic, c. 700 BCE

Immediately after telling the Pandora myth, Hesiod relates the descent of humanity through five ages (Gold, Silver, Bronze, Heroic, Iron). Scholars note the two stories sit uneasily together: if Pandora is the world's first woman, then only men existed in the Golden Age, and the world declines from good to evil 'within the Pandora myth' while in the Five Ages the decline happens 'between the ages' (Stephanie Nelson). Helen Van Noorden reads the Five Ages as an 'alternative' to the Pandora story rather than a free-standing continuation. Raises the question of whether Pandora's jar or the succession of ages is Hesiod's primary explanation for human misery; Geoffrey Kirk notes the world's first-woman implies a pre-woman Golden Age.

The box in the original myth was a container or box. — It was a pithos, a large clay storage or funerary jar. The 'box' is a 16th-century mistranslation by Erasmus (pithos → pyxis). The jar's use as a burial vessel (from which souls escaped) gives it a chthonic resonance a simple box lacks.

Pandora herself opened the box and this is the fixed, single story. — In Hesiod she opens the jar, but Renaissance art and humanist retellings frequently have Epimetheus open it, and an Aesopic tradition has a generic 'foolish man' open a jar of blessings. The blame for opening was never stable.

The box is a fixed small ornate object as shown in art/movies. — Its form has changed radically: jar (antiquity), box (Erasmus), casket (Rossetti), oaken chest (Longfellow), straight-sided box (19th c. art). There is no single canonical visual form.

Hope was clearly the good thing that remained, a comfort to humanity. — Whether hope is a benefit, a withheld blessing, or simply ambiguous 'expectation' (possibly expectation of evil) is one of the most contested cruxes in classical scholarship. Nietzsche called hope 'the most evil of evils because it prolongs man's torment.'

'Opening Pandora's box' as a harmless idiom has no ideological baggage. — The myth originated as an institutionalised misogynist theodicy — woman as 'a plague to men' — and was later fused with Eve in Christian typology to justify female subordination, and deployed in imperial/orientalist contexts. The idiom inherited this loaded origin.
