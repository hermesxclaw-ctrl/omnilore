Who It Is

It was a jar, first — a pithos, the man-high clay vessel Greeks stored grain and wine in; the 'box' is a Renaissance mistranslation that stuck. It arrives as a wedding gift with a bride built for the purpose: after Prometheus stole fire for humanity, Zeus ordered the gods to craft Pandora — the name means All-Gifted — beautiful, curious, and delivered to Prometheus's slower brother Epimetheus with the jar she was never told not to wonder about. What follows is the oldest engineered accident in literature. She opens it. Out flies every evil the human condition would ever know — disease, old age, toil, envy, war — scattering irretrievably into the world, and Pandora slams the lid in time to trap exactly one thing: Elpis. Hope. And there the myth turns razor-sharp and refuses to resolve, because Hesiod does not say whether hope remaining in the jar means hope was saved for us — or kept from us; whether hope is the one mercy among the evils, or the subtlest evil of all, the thing that keeps mortals toiling through the other misfortunes. Twenty-seven centuries of readers have opened that question the way Pandora opened the jar, and it has never gone back in. The relic's modern career is unmatched: 'opening Pandora's box' is the world's standing idiom for irreversible curiosity — the experiment, the weapon, the technology that cannot be un-released.

Provenance

Hesiod's Works and Days (c. 700 BCE), with a variant in his Theogony; Erasmus's 16th-century Latin turned pithos into pyxis — jar into box — and the smaller container proved more portable for art and idiom.

Attested in Hesiod's Works and DaysEra c. 700 BCE

Form

A great storage jar of fired clay, sealed — the most domestic object in Greek myth, which is the horror of it: catastrophe shelved in the pantry.

Powers

Containment of all evils (past tense); single-use irreversibility; and the permanent detention of Hope, on ambiguous charges.Contains and dispenses all evils (Hesiodic jar) The pithos holds 'countless plagues' — sickness, death and other unspecified evils — which Pandora releases into the world when the lid is removed. Once outside, they afflict earth and sea without cease. This is the jar's primary mythic power: it is the reservoir from which all human evil was decanted into mortal existence. Hesiod, Works and Days 94-101 (the canonical version) One thing does not escape — elpis (hope or expectation) — because Pandora sealed the lid in time. The jar's power to dispense is not absolute; it retains a single occupant. In the Aesopic/Babrius and Theognis tradition the jar instead contains blessings (the things humans lack), and it is the accidental opening by a foolish man that lets them flee, leaving only hope. The direction of the container's cargo is thus directly contradicted across sources.The un-closeable/irreversible aftermath (idiomatic power) As an idiom, 'opening Pandora's box' names a one-way irreversible action: whatever the box releases cannot be put back. The property is that the consequences are permanently beyond the opener's control once the act is done. Modern idiomatic usage (from the Renaissance on) This is a moral/allegorical property, not a literal magic; it describes human action and its fallout rather than an object's durable physical power. In Hesiod the box is partially re-closeable — Pandora does shut it, which is why hope remains. The modern idiom's claim that nothing can be re-contained is stronger than the original myth, where one good thing was in fact saved by the act of closing.Holds hope (elpis) as a sole remaining occupant The box is the prison that keeps elpis from fully escaping. Depending on interpretation, this preserves hope for humanity (a comfort) or withholds the true antidote to misery; it may also keep humanity free of a false, idle hope that makes men lazy (Hesiod, Works and Days 498, 500). Hesiod, Works and Days; debated by West, Griffith, Verdenius, Leinieks and others Elpis is ambiguous: it may mean 'hope,' 'expectation,' or 'expectation of evil'; its function hangs on whether the jar is a prison (keeping hope from people) or a pantry (storing hope for people). Nietzsche (Human, All Too Human) calls hope 'the most evil of evils because it prolongs man's torment,' an inversion of the comforting reading.Symbolic identity with Pandora the earthen woman Many scholars see a close symbolic analogy between Pandora, who was made from clay, and the clay jar that dispenses evils — both are earthen receptacles. In pre-Hesiodic strata Pandora/Anesidora was herself a chthonic earth-goddess who 'sends up gifts' (the blessings of the soil), a power Hesiod's myth inverts into a dispenser of evils. Pre-Hesiodic/parallel vase-painting tradition noted by Harrison, Phipps, Gantz; Hesiod's Works and Days The 'all-giving' goddess reading is a scholarly reconstruction from vase iconography and name etymology, not the explicit Hesiodic text; Hesiod himself shows no awareness of it (per the standard English commentary noted by Phipps). Whether Pandora is 'all-gifted' (receptacle of divine gifts, Hesiod's stated etymology) or 'all-giving' (an earth-goddess, the older sense) is a genuine interpretive split.

Weaknesses & Wards

One lid, one curious owner — the jar's security model assumed no one would ever look, the assumption that has never once held.

Accounts

After the Titan Prometheus steals fire from heaven and gives it to humanity, the king of the gods Zeus takes vengeance. He has Hephaestus mould the first woman, Pandora, from earth and water, and every god bestows a gift upon her (Athena teaches her weaving and clothing; Aphrodite sheds grace, longing and care; Hermes gives her a shameless mind, a deceitful nature and the power of lying speech; the Graces and Hours adorn her). Hermes names her Pandora, 'because all they who dwelt on Olympus gave each a gift, a plague to men who eat bread.' Prometheus warns his brother Epimetheus not to accept any gift from Zeus, but Epimetheus ('Afterthought') ignores the warning and takes Pandora as his bride. She brings with her a great jar into which the 'countless plagues' are sealed. Driven by curiosity, Pandora removes the lid, and all the evils — sickness, death, misery — fly out into the world. She hastens to shut the jar, but only one thing remains within: elpis, hope (or expectation). Hesiod closes with the aetiological moral that the earth and sea are now 'full of evils,' and that 'there is no way to escape the will of Zeus' (105).

In an alternative aetiology the gods send the jar to humans not as a punishment but as a gift of all good things. Rather than a named woman, a generic 'foolish man' (akratēs anthrōpos) opens the jar out of curiosity, and the blessings escape and fly back to heaven. Once the lid is replaced, only hope remains behind, 'promising that she will bestow on each of us the good things that have gone away.' Here the jar contains the blessings mortals lack, and hope is a true, earth-bound divine gift.

Renaissance retellings shifted responsibility for the opening from Pandora to her husband Epimetheus ('Afterthought,' 'he whom mistakes made wise'). Erasmus' Latin Adagia opened the box, in his telling, by Epimetheus. Alciato depicted Hope seated on a jar declaring 'I alone stayed behind at home when evils fluttered all around.' Faerno's poem made hope the survivor of 'universal blessings' (bona universa): 'Of all good things that mortals lack, / Hope in the soul alone stays back.' Bonasone's engraving shows Epimetheus holding the lid of a jar from which the female personifications of the Roman virtues fly up, Hope delayed on the lip; Le Clerc's etching shows the lid plainly in Epimetheus' hand.

Immediately after telling the Pandora myth, Hesiod relates the descent of humanity through five ages (Gold, Silver, Bronze, Heroic, Iron). Scholars note the two stories sit uneasily together: if Pandora is the world's first woman, then only men existed in the Golden Age, and the world declines from good to evil 'within the Pandora myth' while in the Five Ages the decline happens 'between the ages' (Stephanie Nelson). Helen Van Noorden reads the Five Ages as an 'alternative' to the Pandora story rather than a free-standing continuation.

Cultural Significance

The Greek counterpart to Eve and the apple — woman, curiosity, and the origin of suffering in one story — and modernity's favorite name for irreversible technological release, from the atom onward.

Scholarly Debates
Jar of evils vs jar of blessings
Hesiod's jar contains countless evils released on humanity (Works and Days 94-101). ⇌ An Aesopic fable (Babrius, Perry 312) and a hint in Theognis have the jar contain blessings that flee, leaving hope behind.
Is elpis hope, expectation, or expectation-of-evil — and is it good for humanity?
West: elpis is comforting hope preserved as an antidote; 'it would be absurd to represent the presence of ills by their confinement in a jar or the presence of hope by its escape from one.' ⇌ Griffith: elpis is a blessing withheld so 'life should be the more dreary and depressing'; or elpis may be simply 'expectation' — possibly of evil.
Who opens the container — Pandora, Epimetheus, or a generic man?
Pandora opens the jar (Hesiod; nearly all modern versions). ⇌ Epimetheus opens it (Erasmus, Alciato, Faerno, Bonasone, Le Clerc, Farinati); or a generic 'foolish man' (Babrius' Aesopic version).
Pandora: 'all-gifted' human woman vs 'all-giving' earth goddess
Hesiod's stated etymology: Pandora = 'all-gifted' (every god gave her a gift); she is the first mortal woman. ⇌ An earlier goddess Pandora/Anesidora = 'all-giving'/'she who sends up gifts,' a chthonic earth-goddess; Hesiod inverted the name's meaning (Phipps, Harrison).
The 'box' itself: pithos vs pyxis vs casket
The original container is a pithos, a large storage/funerary jar. ⇌ Erasmus' pyxis ('box') is a translation error; later 19th-century art and literature re-naturalised it as a chest/coffer or ornate box.

A way to hold it in your mind

The most famous 'object' in Greek mythology never existed in Greece. When Hesiod told the story around 700 BCE, there was no box — there was a pithos, a great clay storage jar as tall as a person, half-buried in the earth, the same kind of jar the Greeks used to hold wine, olive oil, and occasionally the bodies of the dead. The familiar little lidded box of paintings and movies is a translation error that has outlived the truth it obscured: in 1508 the scholar Erasmus rendered Hesiod's pithos as pyxis, 'box,' and a 2,700-year-old story about a woman who uncorks a jar of plagues quietly became a story about opening a box of trouble. And locked inside that jar sat the one thing that would not fly free — a word whose meaning has launched a thousand arguments: elpis, hope, expectation, or some uneasy blur of all three. Whether that last tenant is humanity's comfort or its most cruel deception is the question no two scholars answer the same way, and it is the question that turns a clay pot into a living emblem of every irreversible choice.
