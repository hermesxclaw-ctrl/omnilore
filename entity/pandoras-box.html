Who It Is

It was a jar, first — a pithos, the man-high clay vessel Greeks stored grain and wine in; the 'box' is a Renaissance mistranslation that stuck. It arrives as a wedding gift with a bride built for the purpose: after Prometheus stole fire for humanity, Zeus ordered the gods to craft Pandora — the name means All-Gifted — beautiful, curious, and delivered to Prometheus's slower brother Epimetheus with the jar she was never told not to wonder about. What follows is the oldest engineered accident in literature. She opens it. Out flies every evil the human condition would ever know — disease, old age, toil, envy, war — scattering irretrievably into the world, and Pandora slams the lid in time to trap exactly one thing: Elpis. Hope. And there the myth turns razor-sharp and refuses to resolve, because Hesiod does not say whether hope remaining in the jar means hope was saved for us — or kept from us; whether hope is the one mercy among the evils, or the subtlest evil of all, the thing that keeps mortals toiling through the other misfortunes. Twenty-seven centuries of readers have opened that question the way Pandora opened the jar, and it has never gone back in. The relic's modern career is unmatched: 'opening Pandora's box' is the world's standing idiom for irreversible curiosity — the experiment, the weapon, the technology that cannot be un-released.

Provenance

Hesiod's Works and Days (c. 700 BCE), with a variant in his Theogony; Erasmus's 16th-century Latin turned pithos into pyxis — jar into box — and the smaller container proved more portable for art and idiom.

Attested in Hesiod's Works and DaysEra c. 700 BCE

Form

A great storage jar of fired clay, sealed — the most domestic object in Greek myth, which is the horror of it: catastrophe shelved in the pantry.

Contains and dispenses all evils (Hesiodic jar) The pithos holds 'countless plagues' — sickness, death and other unspecified evils — which Pandora releases into the world when the lid is removed. Once outside, they afflict earth and sea without cease. This is the jar's primary mythic power: it is the reservoir from which all human evil was decanted into mortal existence. Hesiod, Works and Days 94-101 (the canonical version) One thing does not escape — elpis (hope or expectation) — because Pandora sealed the lid in time. The jar's power to dispense is not absolute; it retains a single occupant. In the Aesopic/Babrius and Theognis tradition the jar instead contains blessings (the things humans lack), and it is the accidental opening by a foolish man that lets them flee, leaving only hope. The direction of the container's cargo is thus directly contradicted across sources.

The un-closeable/irreversible aftermath (idiomatic power) As an idiom, 'opening Pandora's box' names a one-way irreversible action: whatever the box releases cannot be put back. The property is that the consequences are permanently beyond the opener's control once the act is done. Modern idiomatic usage (from the Renaissance on) This is a moral/allegorical property, not a literal magic; it describes human action and its fallout rather than an object's durable physical power. In Hesiod the box is partially re-closeable — Pandora does shut it, which is why hope remains. The modern idiom's claim that nothing can be re-contained is stronger than the original myth, where one good thing was in fact saved by the act of closing.

Holds hope (elpis) as a sole remaining occupant The box is the prison that keeps elpis from fully escaping. Depending on interpretation, this preserves hope for humanity (a comfort) or withholds the true antidote to misery; it may also keep humanity free of a false, idle hope that makes men lazy (Hesiod, Works and Days 498, 500). Hesiod, Works and Days; debated by West, Griffith, Verdenius, Leinieks and others Elpis is ambiguous: it may mean 'hope,' 'expectation,' or 'expectation of evil'; its function hangs on whether the jar is a prison (keeping hope from people) or a pantry (storing hope for people). Nietzsche (Human, All Too Human) calls hope 'the most evil of evils because it prolongs man's torment,' an inversion of the comforting reading.

Symbolic identity with Pandora the earthen woman Many scholars see a close symbolic analogy between Pandora, who was made from clay, and the clay jar that dispenses evils — both are earthen receptacles. In pre-Hesiodic strata Pandora/Anesidora was herself a chthonic earth-goddess who 'sends up gifts' (the blessings of the soil), a power Hesiod's myth inverts into a dispenser of evils. Pre-Hesiodic/parallel vase-painting tradition noted by Harrison, Phipps, Gantz; Hesiod's Works and Days The 'all-giving' goddess reading is a scholarly reconstruction from vase iconography and name etymology, not the explicit Hesiodic text; Hesiod himself shows no awareness of it (per the standard English commentary noted by Phipps). Whether Pandora is 'all-gifted' (receptacle of divine gifts, Hesiod's stated etymology) or 'all-giving' (an earth-goddess, the older sense) is a genuine interpretive split.

Weaknesses & Wards

One lid, one curious owner — the jar's security model assumed no one would ever look, the assumption that has never once held.

Accounts

After the Titan Prometheus steals fire from heaven and gives it to humanity, the king of the gods Zeus takes vengeance. He has Hephaestus mould the first woman, Pandora, from earth and water, and every god bestows a gift upon her (Athena teaches her weaving and clothing; Aphrodite sheds grace, longing and care; Hermes gives her a shameless mind, a deceitful nature and the power of lying speech; the Graces and Hours adorn her). Hermes names her Pandora, 'because all they who dwelt on Olympus gave each a gift, a plague to men who eat bread.' Prometheus warns his brother Epimetheus not to accept any gift from Zeus, but Epimetheus ('Afterthought') ignores the warning and takes Pandora as his bride. She brings with her a great jar into which the 'countless plagues' are sealed. Driven by curiosity, Pandora removes the lid, and all the evils — sickness, death, misery — fly out into the world. She hastens to shut the jar, but only one thing remains within: elpis, hope (or expectation). Hesiod closes with the aetiological moral that the earth and sea are now 'full of evils,' and that 'there is no way to escape the will of Zeus' (105).

In an alternative aetiology the gods send the jar to humans not as a punishment but as a gift of all good things. Rather than a named woman, a generic 'foolish man' (akratēs anthrōpos) opens the jar out of curiosity, and the blessings escape and fly back to heaven. Once the lid is replaced, only hope remains behind, 'promising that she will bestow on each of us the good things that have gone away.' Here the jar contains the blessings mortals lack, and hope is a true, earth-bound divine gift.

Renaissance retellings shifted responsibility for the opening from Pandora to her husband Epimetheus ('Afterthought,' 'he whom mistakes made wise'). Erasmus' Latin Adagia opened the box, in his telling, by Epimetheus. Alciato depicted Hope seated on a jar declaring 'I alone stayed behind at home when evils fluttered all around.' Faerno's poem made hope the survivor of 'universal blessings' (bona universa): 'Of all good things that mortals lack, / Hope in the soul alone stays back.' Bonasone's engraving shows Epimetheus holding the lid of a jar from which the female personifications of the Roman virtues fly up, Hope delayed on the lip; Le Clerc's etching shows the lid plainly in Epimetheus' hand.

Immediately after telling the Pandora myth, Hesiod relates the descent of humanity through five ages (Gold, Silver, Bronze, Heroic, Iron). Scholars note the two stories sit uneasily together: if Pandora is the world's first woman, then only men existed in the Golden Age, and the world declines from good to evil 'within the Pandora myth' while in the Five Ages the decline happens 'between the ages' (Stephanie Nelson). Helen Van Noorden reads the Five Ages as an 'alternative' to the Pandora story rather than a free-standing continuation.

Cultural Significance

The Greek counterpart to Eve and the apple — woman, curiosity, and the origin of suffering in one story — and modernity's favorite name for irreversible technological release, from the atom onward.

Content Notes

In Hesiod's Works and Days, Zeus engineers a woman expressly as 'a plague to men who eat bread' — the gods each bestow a gift, and the aggregate is a punishment. Pandora is functionally a weaponised gift whose opening inflicts sickness, death and toil on all humanity. The myth is, in effect, an aetiologised misogyny: women are the divine vehicle of evil.

Robert Graves judged the episode 'not a genuine myth but an anti-feminist fable, probably of his own invention.' H. J. Rose found it 'decidedly more illiberal than epic' in making woman the origin of man's woes. Jane Ellen Harrison read the Hesiodic story as evidence of a shift from matriarchy to patriarchy: the life-bringing goddess Pandora is eclipsed by the death-bringing human Pandora.

Hesiod, Works and Days 60-105; Theogony 560-612 ('a beautiful evil')

Hesiod laments that the race of women is 'the deadly race and tribe of women who live amongst mortal men to their great trouble, no helpmeets in hateful poverty, but only in wealth,' and insists that men who avoid women by avoiding marriage fare no better. The myth weaves a pessimistic, patriarchal anthropology of women.

Hesiod is described by commentators as 'wary of women' and 'a misogynist of the same calibre as the later poet Semonides' (see the Hesiod article's characterisation). Froma Zeitlin's 'the case of Hesiod's Pandora' (in Playing the Other) analyses how the myth inscribes gender difference and female otherness into Greek cosmology.

Hesiod, Theogony 590-609; Works and Days

From the early Church Fathers, Pandora was made a type of Eve, and it has been argued that through the Hellenisation of Western Asia Hesiod's misogyny came to influence Jewish and Christian interpretation, reinforcing doctrinal anxiety about women into the Renaissance. Bishop Jean Olivier's Latin poem Pandora (1541) used both the classical and Biblical accounts 'to demonstrate that woman is the means of drawing men to sin'; Jean Cousin the Elder's Eva Prima Pandora (1550) fused the two explicitly.

Commentators note the 'doctrinal bias against women so initiated then continued into the Renaissance.' The conflating of a pagan myth with scripture to justify female subordination is a documented instance of ideology weaponising myth.

Patristic typology; Olivier, Pandora (1541); Cousin the Elder, Eva Prima Pandora (1550); Horozco, Emblemas morales (1589)

The jar is Hesiod's explanation for why the human condition is one of toil, sickness, and death: they are sent by Zeus as punishment, and 'there is no way to escape the will of Zeus.' The myth justifies divine tyranny and an aristocratic/producerist work ethic (the Works and Days is a farmer's almanac preaching honest labour) as the natural order, with collective human misery the price of disobedience.

The myth sits in a didactic poem that couples hard work with divine justice; the connection of the Pandora punishment to the agrarian work-ethic programme of Works and Days is well recognised.

Hesiod, Works and Days (general frame and conclusion at 105); Works and Days labour passages

In the 19th and early 20th centuries, imperial and colonial culture used 'Pandora's box' as a figure for the dangers of encountering 'savage' or 'primitive' peoples and of unchecked colonial curiosity — a trope that projected Western guilt and projection onto non-Western peoples as the 'evils' that erupt when one opens the 'box' of empire or race. This is an orientalist instrumentalisation of a Greek myth to rationalise colonial anxiety.

The metaphorical export of 'Pandora's box' into colonial and racial discourse is a documented pattern of classical reception as ideology; scholars of reception (e.g. in Hellenism and Empire studies) note how Greek mythology was deployed to legitimise colonial hierarchies.

Imperial-era English usage; literary and journalistic idiom of the British Empire era

Voltaire's Pandore (1740) makes Jupiter (Zeus) fall in love with the female statue and then, in revenge for Earth's revolt, send Destiny to tempt 'this new Eve' into opening a box of curses; the figure of Pandora is reduced to an object of divine desire and a scapegoat. Longfellow's Masque of Pandora (1876) has Pandora 'collapse in despair' and beg Epimetheus to kill her, collapsing her into shame and self-blame when the box (an 'oaken chest') is opened against expectation of blessing.

The persistent tendency to make the woman-holder bear the guilt while gods/patriarchal structure go unexamined is a recurrent pattern across the myth's reception history.

Voltaire, Pandore (1740); Longfellow, The Masque of Pandora (1876); Pabst's Die Büchse der Pandora via Wedekind's Lulu (see reception)

Scholarly Debates
Jar of evils vs jar of blessings
Hesiod's jar contains countless evils released on humanity (Works and Days 94-101). ⇌ An Aesopic fable (Babrius, Perry 312) and a hint in Theognis have the jar contain blessings that flee, leaving hope behind.
Evidence for A: Works and Days text; the dominant reading.
Evidence for B: Babrius fable 58; Theognis fragment; M. L. West's argument for a pre-Hesiodic myth of a good-filled jar.
Is elpis hope, expectation, or expectation-of-evil — and is it good for humanity?
West: elpis is comforting hope preserved as an antidote; 'it would be absurd to represent the presence of ills by their confinement in a jar or the presence of hope by its escape from one.' ⇌ Griffith: elpis is a blessing withheld so 'life should be the more dreary and depressing'; or elpis may be simply 'expectation' — possibly of evil.
Evidence for A: Statistical analysis that 'expectation of good' outnumbers 'expectation of bad' 5:1 in extant Greek; the jar-as-pantry r
Evidence for B: Hesiod elsewhere calls hope 'empty' (498) and 'no good' (500), making men lazy; Pindar calls elpis 'sweet'; Sophocles ma
Who opens the container — Pandora, Epimetheus, or a generic man?
Pandora opens the jar (Hesiod; nearly all modern versions). ⇌ Epimetheus opens it (Erasmus, Alciato, Faerno, Bonasone, Le Clerc, Farinati); or a generic 'foolish man' (Babrius' Aesopic version).
Evidence for A: Works and Days text.
Evidence for B: Renaissance art and humanist retellings; Babrius.
Pandora: 'all-gifted' human woman vs 'all-giving' earth goddess
Hesiod's stated etymology: Pandora = 'all-gifted' (every god gave her a gift); she is the first mortal woman. ⇌ An earlier goddess Pandora/Anesidora = 'all-giving'/'she who sends up gifts,' a chthonic earth-goddess; Hesiod inverted the name's meaning (Phipps, Harrison).
Evidence for A: Works and Days 81-2.
Evidence for B: 5th-century vase paintings showing Pandora/Anesidora emerging from the earth as a chthonic giver; the Ashmolean amphora
The 'box' itself: pithos vs pyxis vs casket
The original container is a pithos, a large storage/funerary jar. ⇌ Erasmus' pyxis ('box') is a translation error; later 19th-century art and literature re-naturalised it as a chest/coffer or ornate box.
Evidence for A: Hesiod's text; linguistic analysis.
Evidence for B: Erasmus, Adagia; Rossetti's casket; Longfellow's 'oaken chest.'

A way to hold it in your mind

The most famous 'object' in Greek mythology never existed in Greece. When Hesiod told the story around 700 BCE, there was no box — there was a pithos, a great clay storage jar as tall as a person, half-buried in the earth, the same kind of jar the Greeks used to hold wine, olive oil, and occasionally the bodies of the dead. The familiar little lidded box of paintings and movies is a translation error that has outlived the truth it obscured: in 1508 the scholar Erasmus rendered Hesiod's pithos as pyxis, 'box,' and a 2,700-year-old story about a woman who uncorks a jar of plagues quietly became a story about opening a box of trouble. And locked inside that jar sat the one thing that would not fly free — a word whose meaning has launched a thousand arguments: elpis, hope, expectation, or some uneasy blur of all three. Whether that last tenant is humanity's comfort or its most cruel deception is the question no two scholars answer the same way, and it is the question that turns a clay pot into a living emblem of every irreversible choice.
