Who It Is

In the misty forests of Hokkaido, the great owl watches. To the Ainu, Cikap-kamuy is not just a bird but the guardian of the land, a deity whose piercing gaze sees all human deeds. He is the bringer of prosperity, but also the punisher of disrespect—a god who once killed two messengers for falling asleep and taught humanity the rituals that keep the world in balance. His story is a reminder that respect for nature is not optional, but essential for survival.

Cikap-kamuy is a stern but benevolent deity. He observes human behavior and ensures that rituals are performed correctly. He is quick to anger when disrespect is shown, as seen in his killing of Crow and Mountain Jay, but he is also merciful and teaches humans the proper rituals to restore harmony.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Cikap means 'bird' and kamuy means 'deity' or 'spirit' in the Ainu language. Kotan-kor-kamuy means 'deity possessing the village'.

Pronunciation — CHEE-kap KAH-moo-ee

Aliases — Kotan-kor-kamuy, Kamui-Chikap, Fukuro (in Doodle Champion Island Games)

Ownership — Ainu people of Hokkaido, Japan, and surrounding regions

Oversight of human and kamuy behavior

Cikap-kamuy observes the actions of humans and other deities, ensuring that proper respect and rituals are maintained.

Limit: He relies on messengers to communicate with the heavens, and his power to enforce behavior is indirect.

Control over material success

He is considered a deity of material success, bringing prosperity to those who honor him.

Limit: His blessings are conditional on proper ritual observance.

Tears of gold and silver

In some areas, his tears are said to be gold and silver, symbolizing wealth.

Limit: Not universally attested.

Communication with heaven

He can send messages to the heavens, though he requires a messenger to carry them.

Limit: His lengthy messages are difficult to deliver, leading to the death of inattentive messengers.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Cikap-kamuy is depicted as a great owl (Blakiston's fish owl), characterized by its large size and prominent ear tufts. In contrast, smaller owls like the little horned owl are associated with evil spirits. In some regional traditions, his tears are said to be gold and silver, symbolizing wealth and abundance.

Symbols — The great owl itself is the primary symbol. In Ainu art, owls are often carved in wood as 'ikayop' (prayer sticks) or depicted in patterns on ceremonial robes. The owl's image represents watchfulness, wisdom, and the guardian of the land.

Domains — He is the master of the land (mosir) and villages (kotan). He resides in the heavens but descends to the earthly realm to watch over humans. His primary domain is the terrestrial world, overseeing the behavior of both humans and other kamuy.

Accounts I
The Famine and the MessengersAinu oral tradition, recorded by Etter and others

Pre-modern (oral tradition)

A famine struck the land and humans were starving. Cikap-kamuy wished to send a message to heaven to inquire about the cause. He asked Crow to be his messenger, but Crow fell asleep on the third day and was killed. Then he asked Mountain Jay, who fell asleep on the fourth day and was also killed. Finally, Dipper Bird listened respectfully for six days and delivered the message. The kamuy of fish and game were angry because humans had stopped showing proper respect. Cikap-kamuy then taught humans the correct rituals, and the famine ceased. Crow and Mountain Jay became birds of ill omen, while Dipper became a sign of good fortune. Humans learned the proper rituals for hunting and fishing.

Accounts II
The Marriage of Cikap-kamuy's SisterAinu mythology, recorded in various sources

Pre-modern

In another myth, Cikap-kamuy has a sister who is forced to marry Okikurmi, a culture hero, after he defeats the owl god in a fight. This myth establishes a connection between the owl deity and the culture hero, and explains certain ritual or familial relationships.

Cikap-kamuy is the same as Kotan-kar-kamuy. — Kotan-kar-kamuy is a creator deity, while Cikap-kamuy is an owl deity of the land. They are distinct in many traditions.

All owls are considered sacred in Ainu culture. — Only the great owl (Cikap-kamuy) is sacred; smaller owls are associated with evil spirits.

Cikap-kamuy is a benevolent god who never punishes. — He punishes disrespect, as seen in the killing of Crow and Mountain Jay.
