Who It Is

It is not a poison you swallow. It is a weapon you are given, through fang, spine, stinger, or spur — a cocktail brewed in a gland and driven through skin to rewrite your nerves, blood, and muscle in minutes. Venom is one of evolution's most repeated inventions, built from ordinary proteins turned lethal, and it kills tens of thousands of people every year while also holding some of modern medicine's most promising drugs.

Venom is a behaviorally deployed tool, not a passive trait. Predatory use: snakes, cone snails, spiders, centipedes, and box jellyfish inject to immobilize and pre-digest prey; cone snails can switch between defensive and predatory venom cocktails. Defensive use: bees, wasps, stingrays, stonefish, platypus (male-male combat during breeding season), and Lonomia caterpillars deploy when threatened. Powers
Neurotoxicity - ion channel and receptor blockade

Neurotoxins disrupt nervous signaling by blocking or modulating ion channels (Na+, K+, Ca2+) or acetylcholine receptors. Result: paralysis, respiratory failure, sensory disruption. Found in mambas, cone snails (conotoxins), scorpions, black widow spiders (latrotoxins), blue-ringed octopus (tetrodotoxin via symbionts), and box jellyfish.

Limit: Highly prey-specific; ion channel isoforms differ between taxa, so potency varies by victim. Antivenom and receptor variation can confer resistance (e.g., mongoose acetylcholine receptor mutations, honey badger resistance). Dose-dependent; dry bites deliver none.

Hemotoxicity and coagulopathy

Haemotoxins disrupt blood clotting via procoagulant or anticoagulant proteins, platelet aggregation inhibitors, and hemorrhagins. Viper venoms often cause consumptive coagulopathy, hemorrhage, and hypotension. Includes serine proteases, metalloproteases, and C-type lectins.

Limit: Effects delayed (minutes to hours) compared to neurotoxic paralysis; dependent on victim's clotting cascade. Can be countered by antivenom and clotting factor replacement if administered early.

Myotoxicity - muscle destruction

Myotoxins bind muscle receptors and disrupt sarcolemma, causing rhabdomyolysis, muscle necrosis, and secondary kidney failure from myoglobin release. Small basic peptides in rattlesnake and sea snake venoms; also sea anemone and some lizard venoms.

Limit: Often requires relatively high local concentration; systemic myotoxicity less common than local. Antivenom reduces but does not reverse established muscle death.

Cytotoxicity and necrotoxicity

Cytotoxins kill individual cells; necrotoxins cause broader tissue death (necrosis). Phospholipases A2 hydrolyze membrane phospholipids; also found in bee apitoxin (melittin, phospholipase A2) and black widow venom. Viper and bee venoms are classic necrotic agents causing pain, swelling, blistering, and tissue loss.

Limit: Local action; systemic spread limited unless large dose. Necrosis is often due to secondary inflammation and ischemia, not toxin alone.

Prey-specific ion channel specialization

Venoms evolve to be maximally efficient on predator's standard diet by targeting precise prey ion channel subtypes. Cone snail fish-hunters vs worm-hunters have non-overlapping conopeptide sets; spider venoms tuned to insect vs vertebrate channels.

Limit: Specialization reduces efficacy on non-prey taxa; explains why many venoms are medically significant to humans only incidentally.

Pharmacological repurposing - therapeutic toxins

Isolated venom components become drugs at controlled doses: captopril (from Bothrops jararaca bradykinin-potentiating peptide) for hypertension; ziconotide (from Conus magus) for chronic pain; exenatide (from Heloderma suspectum exendin-4) for type 2 diabetes; batroxobin and eptifibatide (from snake venoms) as antithrombotics; solenopsins from fire ant venom investigated for cancer and psoriasis.

Limit: Native venom is not medicine; therapeutic window is narrow and requires purification, modification, and delivery control. Whole venom remains toxic.

Symbols — Venomous animals dominate iconography of danger, protection, and medicine. Accounts I
The Toxicofera Hypothesis - One Venom System at the Base of Lizards and SnakesFry et al. 2006 Nature; subsequent phylogenomic tests 2015-2022

2006-present (proposed origin ~170 million years ago, Late Jurassic)

Bryan Fry's team proposed that all venomous lizards and snakes share a single early origin of venom from duplicated salivary genes, uniting Serpentes, Iguania, Anguidae, Helodermatidae and Varanidae in clade Toxicofera. They showed protein types (e.g., CRISP, kallikrein) shared across these groups and argued Komodo dragon and bearded dragon possess incipient venom glands. The hypothesis implied snake venom did not evolve independently but was inherited and elaborated. Spurred decade of venom gland transcriptomics and mass spectrometry. Komodo dragon venom complexity was shown to rival snake venom, supporting functional venom in varanids. However, later phylogenomic studies questioned Toxicofera monophyly, suggesting venom may have evolved convergently multiple times within lizards.

Platypus Spur - Sweat Glands Turned WeaponWhittington et al. 2008 Genome Research; Wong et al. 2013 Toxicon; historical Aboriginal knowledge

1800 (European description) to present; platypus lineage ~120 Ma

Male platypuses (Ornithorhynchus anatinus) possess hollow tarsal spurs on hind limbs connected to crural glands that produce venom seasonally during breeding competition. Unlike reptile venom derived from salivary glands, platypus venom evolved from modified sweat glands, and gene duplication played an initial but not ongoing dominant role. Envenomation in humans causes immediate excruciating pain, swelling, and prolonged hyperalgesia unresponsive to morphine, though not lethal. Fossil tarsal spurs in non-therian Mammaliaformes suggest venom may have been ancestral in early mammals. Demonstrated independent origin of mammalian venom distinct from reptiles, supporting homoplasy model. Venom peptides (defensin-like) are studied for novel analgesics. Highlighted seasonal regulation and male-male combat function rather than predation.

Cone Snails - From Lethal Harpoon to Pain DrugOlivera et al. 1985-1990 Science; FDA approval record for ziconotide 2004

1980s discovery to 2004 drug approval

Cone snails (Conus spp., >800 species) fire a harpoon-like radular tooth to inject a cocktail of 50-200 conopeptides, each 10-30 amino acids, targeting specific ion channels. Fish-hunting Conus geographus can kill humans within hours (dozens of recorded fatalities). Baldomero Olivera's lab at University of Utah isolated omega-conotoxin MVIIA from Conus magus, which blocks N-type calcium channels. Synthesized as ziconotide (Prialt), it became an FDA-approved intrathecal analgesic for severe chronic pain, 1000x more potent than morphine without opioid tolerance. Established cone snail venom as premier library for neuroscience tools and drug leads. Created venomics pipeline now screening thousands of conopeptides for epilepsy, cardiac and cancer applications. Demonstrated venom's dual nature as lethal and curative.

Accounts II
Gila Monster Saliva and the Diabetes DrugEng et al. 1992 Journal of Biological Chemistry; FDA approval of exenatide 2005

1992 peptide discovery to present clinical use

Gila monster (Heloderma suspectum) and Mexican beaded lizard (H. horridum) deliver venom via grooved teeth and chewing, not hollow fangs. In 1992, endocrinologist John Eng isolated exendin-4 from Gila monster venom, a peptide 53% homologous to human GLP-1 but resistant to DPP-4 degradation. Developed as exenatide (Byetta/Bydureon), it became the first GLP-1 receptor agonist for type 2 diabetes, now a multi-billion dollar drug class (including semaglutide descendants). Mass spectrometry later showed Heloderma venom complexity equals that of many snakes. Proved lizard venom, once thought primitive, holds pharmaceutical complexity. Opened reptile venom to systematic drug screening. GLP-1 agonists now treat diabetes and obesity globally.

Lonomia Caterpillars - When Hairs KillArocha-Pinango et al. 1967-1992; Brazilian Ministry of Health surveillance

1960s first clinical recognition to present; Lonomia lineage ancient

Caterpillars of the saturniid moth Lonomia obliqua and L. achelous bear dense urticating bristles connected to venom glands. Unlike irritating hairs of most caterpillars, Lonomia venom contains potent procoagulant and anticoagulant toxins causing disseminated intravascular coagulation and hemorrhage. Contact, often from leaning against a tree trunk where caterpillars aggregate, can cause fatal bleeding syndrome days later. Brazil recorded hundreds of severe cases and dozens of deaths, leading to production of specific antivenom (antilonomic serum) by Instituto Butantan in São Paulo, the only caterpillar antivenom in the world. Forced recognition that Lepidoptera can be lethally venomous, not merely allergenic. Led to public health campaigns, tree marking, and unique antivenom production. Venom procoagulants are studied for thrombosis research.

Box Jellyfish and the Race for AntivenomBarnes 1964 description of Chironex fleckeri; Tibballs 2006 review; Australian venom research

1950s clinical recognition to present

Box jellyfish (Chironex fleckeri, Carukia barnesi causing Irukandji syndrome) trail up to 60 tentacles each bearing millions of nematocysts that inject porins and cytolysins causing dermonecrosis, cardiac collapse and, for Irukandji, catecholamine storm. Chironex is considered among the most venomous marine animals, with deaths occurring within minutes from cardiac arrest. Commonwealth Serum Laboratories developed box jellyfish antivenom in the 1970s, but efficacy remains debated and supportive care is primary. Recent cryo-EM work has mapped pore-forming toxins to guide small-molecule inhibitors. Drove beach safety infrastructure in northern Australia (stinger nets, vinegar stations), venom research funding, and public understanding of Cnidarian envenomation. No reliable field antidote beyond vinegar to prevent further nematocyst discharge and CPR.

Venomous and poisonous mean the same thing. — In modern toxinology they are distinct functional categories: venom is actively injected through a wound (fang, stinger, spine), poison is passively delivered when touched or eaten. A poison dart frog is poisonous, not venomous; a rattlesnake is venomous, not poisonous. Many animals are both or neither, and some (like spitting cobras that spray) blur lines, but the distinction matters for medical treatment.

You can suck, cut, or tourniquet a snakebite to remove venom. — Incision, oral suction, tourniquets, and electric shock worsen tissue damage, increase infection, and do not remove significant venom. WHO and Red Cross guidelines recommend immobilizing the limb, keeping victim calm and supine, removing tight jewelry, and transporting rapidly to medical facility for antivenom. Pressure immobilization is recommended only for neurotoxic elapid bites in Australia, not for viper bites where it increases necrosis.

Baby snakes are more dangerous because they can't control venom dose. — Studies of Crotalus and Bothrops show juveniles can meter venom and often inject less total venom than adults due to smaller glands, though venom composition may differ. Adult snakes deliver larger, more dangerous doses on average. Dry bites (no venom injected) occur in 20-50% of defensive strikes at any age.

All venom is deadly to humans and has no medical value. — Most venomous species cannot kill humans; only ~30-50 spider species, a few dozen snakes, and a handful of marine animals are medically significant. At controlled doses, venom components are approved drugs: captopril (hypertension from Bothrops), ziconotide (pain from Conus magus), exenatide (diabetes from Heloderma), and eptifibatide/tirofiban (antiplatelet from snake venoms). Venomics has >5,000 papers exploring further uses for thrombosis, arthritis, and cancer.

Venom (Marvel character) has something to do with real venom biology. — Marvel's Venom is an alien symbiote named for metaphorical toxicity, not biological venom. It has no venom gland, fang delivery, or toxinology basis. Search engines frequently mix the two because the fictional character dominates pop-culture results for the word 'Venom'.
