A berry gave me my child, a lingonberry made me a mother; the forest is his father, the sauna bench his cradle.

Who It Is

Marjatta is the final and most enigmatic figure of the Kalevala — the virgin who gives birth to a son destined to replace Väinämöinen himself. Her story, in the 50th and final runo of the 1849 expanded Kalevala, is a transparent and deliberate Christian allegory. Marjatta (whose name echoes the Finnish word marja, 'berry') is a chaste shepherdess who becomes pregnant after eating a lingonberry — a miraculous conception that parallels the Annunciation. The parallels are explicit: she gives birth in a stable (a sauna in the stable), her child disappears and is found (like the infant Jesus in the Temple), and he is proclaimed the true king by an elder figure.

Marjatta is banished from her family for her unexplained pregnancy and must give birth alone. She seeks a place in the village sauna — the traditional Finnish birthplace — but is refused at every house. Finally, she finds shelter in a forest sauna, where the horses steam in their stalls — the Finnish equivalent of the Bethlehem stable. She gives birth to a son on her knees, warms him by the sauna stove, and names him herself (with no father to perform the naming ritual). The child mysteriously vanishes and is found in a swamp, hiding from those who would harm him.

When the child is brought before the elder Väinämöinen for judgment — traditional law says a fatherless child must be killed — the infant speaks with supernatural authority, rebuking the great sage and pointing out his own sins (the Aino tragedy, among others). In a stunning reversal of the entire epic's power structure, the old hero Väinämöinen is displaced by this infant king. The child is baptized and crowned king of Karelia. Väinämöinen, recognizing that his time has passed, departs in his copper boat, sailing beyond the horizon — but leaving his kantele and his songs to the Finnish people.

Marjatta's story is Lönnrot's most overt Christian interpolation into the Kalevala. The virgin birth, the flight from persecution, the stable, the child who speaks wisdom beyond his years — these are unmistakable Gospel parallels. Yet Marjatta also echoes earlier Finnish folklore about miraculous births from berries, and her story's emotional core — a young woman rejected by her community, giving birth in solitude — resonates beyond its allegorical frame. She represents the new age displacing the old, Christianity supplanting paganism, but also the eternal theme of renewal: the young supplanting the old, the future rising from the rejection of the past. Her son's baptism marks the official end of the Kalevala's mythic age and the beginning of historical time.
