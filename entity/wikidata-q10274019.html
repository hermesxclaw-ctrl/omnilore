The Approach

In the quiet laboratories of Cambridge and London, Ernest Baldwin mapped the chemical machinery of life across the animal kingdom, turning the obscure field of comparative biochemistry into a discipline that would influence generations. His textbooks, translated worldwide, carried the torch of Frederick Gowland Hopkins into the post-war era, yet his own story remains a footnote in the annals of science—a dedicated educator, a tireless organizer, and a man who faced a debilitating illness with quiet perseverance.

Content advisory. Contains references to terminal illness (myotonic muscular dystrophy) and death by congestive heart failure. No other sensitive material.

From Crypt School to Cambridge

Source Wikipedia articleCulture EnglishPeriod Early 20th century

Summary

Born in Gloucester, Baldwin attended the Crypt Grammar School and then St. John's College, Cambridge, where he studied natural sciences and specialized in biochemistry. He won an 1851 Exhibition scholarship and remained at Cambridge for postgraduate study, influenced by Frederick Gowland Hopkins and the Needhams.

Publishing the Textbook

Source Wikipedia articleCulture EnglishPeriod 1937

Summary

Inspired by his mentors, Baldwin published 'An Introduction to Comparative Biochemistry' in 1937, which became a standard text. He later published 'Dynamic Aspects of Biochemistry' in 1947, which won the 1952 European Cortina-Ulisse Prize.

Participants

War Work and Research

Source Wikipedia articleCulture EnglishPeriod 1940-1949

Summary

During World War II, Baldwin served as an Air Warden. After the war, he continued research at Cambridge on the roundworm Ascaris lumbricoides and studied phosphagens at the Marine Biological Laboratory. He also helped organize the First International Congress of Biochemistry in 1949.

Building UCL Biochemistry

Source Wikipedia articleCulture EnglishPeriod 1950-1969

Summary

In 1950, Baldwin became chair of biochemistry at University College London. He developed the curriculum, managed new laboratories, and collaborated with M. B. Donald to create a joint diploma in biochemical engineering. He held visiting professorships in the US and mentored students, including Frederick Sanger.
