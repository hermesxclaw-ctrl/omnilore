The Approach

Before weaving, before spinning, there was felting—a cloth born not from looms but from pressure, moisture, and time. For millennia, nomadic peoples of the Central Asian steppes have transformed the fleece of sheep and camels into the very walls of their homes, the blankets on their saddles, and the amulets they carried for protection. Felt is one of humanity's oldest textiles, a material that shaped the lives of entire cultures, and it remains a vibrant medium for artists and craftspeople today.

Content advisory. This entry discusses historical manufacturing processes that involved toxic mercury compounds (carroting), which may be of concern to some readers. It also covers cultural practices and traditions of nomadic peoples; readers should approach with respect and awareness of cultural appropriation issues.
Identity

Felt

Pronunciation /fɛlt/

Etymology

The word 'felt' comes from the Old English 'felt', likely derived from the Proto-Germanic '*feltaz', meaning 'something beaten or compressed'. It is related to the Old Norse 'felt' and Middle Dutch 'vilt'.

Cultural Ownership

Felt is a global textile with deep roots in Central Asian nomadic cultures, particularly among Turkic and Mongolian peoples. It is also significant in Scandinavian and other European traditions.

Disputed Identifications

Some sources suggest feltmaking originated independently in multiple regions, but the strongest evidence points to Central Asia. There is no major dispute over the identity of felt as a textile.

Description

Appearance

Felt is a dense, non-woven fabric with a matted surface. It can be soft or firm depending on fiber and processing. Natural wool felt is typically off-white or cream, but it can be dyed in a wide range of colors. It is often used in layers, and decorative patterns can be created with colored pre-felts or embroidery.

Symbols & Iconography

In Central Asian traditions, felt patterns often incorporate abstract designs derived from plants, animals, and symbolic motifs. These patterns carry religious and protective meanings. In modern contexts, felt is associated with craft, coziness, and eco-friendly materials.

Behaviour & Personality

Felt is not a living entity; it is a material. Its 'behavior' is characterized by its properties: fire-retardant, sound-absorbing, and able to hold fluids without feeling wet. It is durable and insulating, making it ideal for housing and clothing.

Domains & Habitats

Felt is found in a wide range of environments, from nomadic yurts on the Central Asian steppes to contemporary art studios. It is used in industrial applications, fashion, and home decor.

Version Differences

Felt can be made from natural fibers (wool, fur) or synthetic fibers (acrylic, rayon). Natural fiber felt has unique properties like fire retardancy, while synthetic felt is often cheaper and more uniform. Manufacturing methods include wet felting, needle felting, and carroting (historical).

Summary

According to legend, Saints Clement and Christopher packed their sandals with wool to prevent blisters while fleeing persecution. The movement and sweat during their journey turned the wool into felt socks.

The Origin of Felt in Central Asia

Source Archaeological and historical evidenceCulture Turkic-Mongolian nomadic culturesPeriod Ancient to present

Summary

Feltmaking likely originated in East Asia, with evidence from Siberia and Mongolia. Nomadic peoples used felt extensively for housing, clothing, and household items, and developed intricate decorative techniques.

Participants

Feltmakers

Consequences

Felt became integral to the nomadic lifestyle and spread across Central Asia.

The Ahi Guilds of Turkey

Source Ottoman historical recordsCulture TurkishPeriod Ottoman Empire

Summary

Feltmaking in Turkey was organized into craft guilds called 'ahi', which registered members and protected the knowledge of felting. In Istanbul, there were 1,000 felters working in 400 workshops.

Participants

Consequences

The guild system preserved and regulated the craft.

Mercury poisoning in hat making

Source Historical accounts, e.g., 'Alice in Wonderland' imagery

Description

The carroting process used mercuric nitrate, which caused mercury poisoning in felters, leading to the phrase 'mad as a hatter'.

Scholarly Context

The link between hatters and mercury poisoning is well-documented; the exact origin of the phrase is debated.

Origin of feltmaking

Position A

Felt originated in Central Asia (Siberia/Mongolia)

Position A — evidence

Archaeological evidence from Siberia and Mongolia dates back to 7th century BCE and earlier.

Felt may have originated independently in multiple regions

Most scholars support Central Asian origin, but the Çatal Hüyük evidence is debated.
