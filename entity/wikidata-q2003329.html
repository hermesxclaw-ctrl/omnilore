Form

No physical representation of Achamán is attested in any source. The Guanches likely did not create images of him, as their worship was centered on natural elements and celestial bodies. He was conceived as an invisible, omnipresent force residing in the sky, rather than an anthropomorphic figure.

Powers

Creation Achamán was credited with the creation of the world, the heavens, and the Guanches themselves. He was the ultimate source of all life and existence. Post-conquest chronicles, primarily Galindo and Torriani The creation narrative is not detailed in surviving sources, so the exact scope and method of his creation are unknown. None recorded, though some scholars suggest his role as creator may have been influenced by Christian concepts introduced after the conquest.

Providence and Judgment He was believed to intervene in human affairs, providing rain, fertility, and protection. He also acted as a moral judge, rewarding the virtuous and punishing the wicked, both in life and in the afterlife. Post-conquest chronicles and Guanche oral traditions as recorded by early historians The mechanisms of his judgment and the criteria for reward/punishment are not detailed in the sources. The nature of the afterlife associated with him is ambiguous, with some sources suggesting a happy hunting ground and others a more abstract spiritual realm.

Antagonism with Guayota Achamán was locked in a cosmic struggle with Guayota, an evil being associated with fire and the underworld. Achamán eventually triumphed, imprisoning Guayota within the volcano Teide. Post-conquest folklore and chronicles, particularly the legend of the imprisonment of Guayota This narrative is fragmentary and likely contains later Christian-influenced elements of a devil figure. The myth is not fully elaborated in early sources and may represent a later syncretic development.

Behavior & Habitat

Achamán was believed to be a benevolent creator and protector, who provided for the Guanches and maintained the cosmic order. He was invoked in times of need, particularly during droughts or conflicts. He was also a moral authority, rewarding the righteous and punishing the wicked, though the specifics of this judgment are not well-documented.

Accounts

Guayota, a malevolent being, was said to have stolen the sun (Magec), plunging the world into darkness. The Guanches prayed to Achamán for help. Achamán confronted Guayota, defeated him, and imprisoned him inside the volcano Teide. He then restored the sun to its proper place in the sky.

Achamán was credited with the creation of the first Guanches. The exact details of this creation are lost, but he was seen as their divine progenitor and protector.

Scholarly Debates
Nature of Achamán
Achamán was a single, supreme, anthropomorphic deity. ⇌ Achamán was an abstract concept of the sky or heavens, not a personal god.
Evidence for A: Post-conquest chronicles often describe him in personal terms, as a creator and judge.
Evidence for B: The lack of any physical representation and the focus on natural elements in Guanche worship suggest a more abstract con
Relationship with Magec
Magec was a separate, subordinate deity. ⇌ Magec was an aspect or manifestation of Achamán.
Evidence for A: Some sources list Magec as a distinct entity.
Evidence for B: The myth of Guayota implies that Achamán controls the sun, suggesting a closer connection.
Influence of Christianity
The belief in Achamán was a pre-Christian, indigenous tradition. ⇌ The accounts of Achamán were heavily influenced or even invented by Christian chroniclers.
Evidence for A: The core elements of the belief are consistent across multiple early sources.
Evidence for B: The parallels between Achamán and the Christian God, particularly in his role as creator and judge, are striking.
What We Don't Know

The most significant gap is the loss of the Guanche language and the majority of their oral tradition. All surviving accounts were recorded by Spanish conquerors and chroniclers, who may have misinterpreted or altered the original beliefs. The exact nature of Achamán's creation myth, his role in the afterlife, and his relationship with other deities remain unclear. More research into archaeological sites and comparative Berber mythology could provide further insights.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Achamán as the 'sky father' of the Guanche people, similar to Zeus or Jupiter, but with a more abstract and less anthropomorphic character. He was the ultimate authority, the source of all order, and the protector of his people. His conflict with Guayota, the fire demon, is a classic myth of order versus chaos, light versus darkness, and the triumph of good over evil. He was the embodiment of the sky itself, a powerful and ever-present force in the lives of the Guanches.
