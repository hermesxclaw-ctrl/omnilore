The Approach

He gave his eye for a drink from a well, and his life for a glimpse of the runes. He is the god who hangs himself on the World Tree, who whispers secrets to the dead, and who will be devoured by a wolf at the end of the world. He is not a hero. He is not a villain. He is Odin, and he is the price of wisdom.

Identity

Odin

Pronunciation OH-din (English); OH-thin (approximate Old Norse, with the 'ð' as in 'the')

Known As

Etymology

From Proto-Germanic *Wōðanaz, meaning 'lord of frenzy' or 'leader of the possessed'. The Old Norse Óðinn is derived from this, relating to óðr, meaning 'mind', 'inspiration', 'fury', or 'poetry'.

Cultural Ownership

Norse and broader Germanic paganism; attested across Scandinavia, Iceland, Germany, and Anglo-Saxon England. A central figure in the reconstructed Proto-Germanic religion.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars link Odin to the Roman god Mercury (interpretatio romana) due to shared attributes of wisdom, travel, and the dead. The relationship to Freya's husband Óðr is debated—whether they are the same figure or separate but related entities. His exact origins (Proto-Indo-European heritage versus later Germanic development) are disputed.

Description

Appearance

Depicted as a tall, old man with a long beard, often wearing a wide-brimmed hat and a cloak. He is famously one-eyed, having sacrificed his other eye for wisdom. Often shown carrying his spear Gungnir and wearing a ring or arm-ring (Draupnir).

Behavior

Odin is a complex and contradictory figure: a wise seeker of knowledge, a wily trickster, a patron of warriors and poets, a god of the dead, and a instigator of conflict. He is known for his cunning, his willingness to deceive and betray even his own champions, and his relentless pursuit of wisdom and power to stave off Ragnarök.

Domains

Wisdom, poetry, war, death, magic (seiðr), prophecy, kingship, and the hunt. He is the leader of the Æsir gods and the ruler of Asgard.

Symbols

His spear Gungnir, his ravens Huginn (Thought) and Muninn (Memory), his wolves Geri and Freki, his eight-legged horse Sleipnir, and his self-made runes. The Valknut symbol is often associated with him in modern times, though its historical connection is debated.

Powers & Abilities

Wisdom and Prophecy

Source VersionPoetic Edda (Völuspá, Hávamál), Prose Edda (Gylfaginning)

Odin sacrifices his eye at Mimir's well for wisdom and hangs himself on Yggdrasil for nine days to learn the runes. He can see the future but is often powerless to change it.

Limitations

His knowledge is hard-won; he remains vulnerable to fate and his own prophecies.

Contradictions

He is all-wise but still seeks more knowledge, suggesting his wisdom is not absolute.

Shapeshifting

Source VersionProse Edda, various sagas

Odin can change his form at will, transforming into animals or other beings.

Limitations

Not explicitly stated, but presumably limited by his own will.

Contradictions

None explicitly stated.

Seiðr Magic

Source VersionYnglinga saga, Lokasenna (accusation by Loki)

A form of Norse magic associated with fate-weaving and prophecy, typically practiced by women (völvas). Odin's practice of it is sometimes seen as unmanly (ergi) by other gods.

Limitations

Practice is considered taboo for men in the society depicted.

Contradictions

He is the chief god yet practices a form of magic considered shameful, showing his disregard for social norms in pursuit of power.

Command over the Dead

Source VersionPoetic Edda (Völuspá, Grímnismál)

He can raise the dead (as with the völva in Völuspá) and receives the souls of slain warriors (einherjar) in Valhöll.

Limitations

His power over the dead is not absolute; he must use spells to compel them to speak.

Contradictions

None explicitly stated.

Summary

Odin and his brothers Vili and Vé slay the primordial giant Ymir and fashion the world from his body. They later find two logs on a beach and create the first humans, Ask and Embla, giving them spirit, reason, and senses.

The Mead of Poetry

Source Prose Edda (Skáldskaparmál)Culture NorsePeriod Mythic past

Summary

Odin steals the Mead of Poetry, a magical drink that grants wisdom and poetic skill. He seduces the giantess Gunnlöð to get it, then escapes in the form of an eagle, regurgitating the mead into vats for the gods.

The Binding of Fenrir

Source Prose Edda (Gylfaginning)Culture NorsePeriod Mythic past

Summary

The gods, fearing the wolf Fenrir's growth, decide to bind him. They trick him with a magical ribbon (Gleipnir) after two failed attempts. Fenrir agrees to be bound only if one god places a hand in his mouth as a pledge. The war-god Týr does so, and Fenrir bites off his hand.

The Death of Baldr

Source Prose Edda (Gylfaginning)Culture NorsePeriod Mythic past

Summary

Odin's son Baldr is killed by a mistletoe spear thrown by the blind god Höðr, tricked by Loki. Odin, grief-stricken, sends his other son Hermóðr to Hel to try to bring Baldr back. The attempt fails, and Baldr remains in the underworld.

Human Sacrifice

Source Gesta Hammaburgensis (Adam of Bremen), Risala (Ibn Fadlan), Hávamál (Odin's self-sacrifice)

Description

Early accounts, such as those by Adam of Bremen (11th c.) and Ibn Fadlan (10th c.), describe human sacrifices at temples like Uppsala and in Viking funerals. Odin is specifically associated with sacrifices, often by hanging or stabbing, as a means to gain favor or knowledge.

Scholarly Context

The practice is well-attested in the archaeological record (e.g., bog bodies, hanging victims) and in medieval texts. It was a religious act, not seen as 'evil' in its own context.

Warning: Sexual Coercion — Show unsanitized · Archive policy & legal

Sexual Coercion

Source Prose Edda (Skáldskaparmál), Saxo Grammaticus' Gesta Danorum

Description

In the story of the Mead of Poetry, Odin seduces the giantess Gunnlöð to obtain the mead, and in another version, he sleeps with the giantess Rindr to beget a son, Váli, to avenge Baldr. The latter is sometimes depicted as rape.

Scholarly Context

These stories highlight Odin's ruthless pursuit of his goals, even at the expense of others' autonomy. They reflect a culture where such actions by a king/god were sometimes framed as necessary or heroic.

Warning: Instigator of Conflict — Show unsanitized · Archive policy & legal

Instigator of Conflict

Source Various sagas, Poetic Edda (e.g., Eiríksmál)

Description

Odin is often depicted as a god who stirs up war and discord, not just as a warrior but as a cunning strategist who pits his own champions against each other to collect the best warriors for Ragnarök.

Scholarly Context

This 'treacherous' aspect of Odin is well-documented. He is a 'god of the slain' who cares more about building his army for the final battle than about the lives of his followers.

Use of Seiðr

Source Ynglinga saga, Lokasenna

Description

Odin's practice of seiðr, a form of magic often seen as unmanly, is an example of his transgressive nature. He will break any taboo to gain power.

Scholarly Context

This is a key aspect of his character: he is a god who operates outside the norms of his own society to achieve his ends.

Contradictions & Disputes

Odin's role as a 'good' god

Position A

Odin is a benevolent Allfather, a wise and just ruler who protects humanity and warriors.

Position A — evidence

His creation of the world, his gift of wisdom and poetry, his patronage of kings.

Position B

Odin is a capricious, treacherous god who sows discord and betrays his own followers for his own ends (i.e., preparing for Ragnarök).

Position B — evidence

His sacrifice of warriors, his use of deception and betrayal in myths (e.g., the Mead of Poetry, the binding of Fenrir), his instigation of wars.

Scholarly Consensus

The latter view is more supported by the primary sources. Odin is a complex, morally ambiguous figure, not a simple 'good' god.

Odin's relationship to Óðr

Position A

Odin and Óðr are the same figure, with 'Óðr' being an alternate name or aspect of Odin.

Position A — evidence

The etymological similarity of the names, and the fact that Freyja is sometimes called 'Óðs mey' (Óðr's maiden).

Position B

They are separate, though related, figures. Óðr is the husband of Freyja, and their names share an etymological root.

Position B — evidence

No myth explicitly states they are the same. Freyja is not married to Odin.

Scholarly Consensus

The relationship is uncertain and debated. Some see Óðr as a hypostasis of Odin, others as a separate, possibly older, deity.

Odin's origins

Position A

Odin is a late development, emerging in the late Iron Age or Migration Period, possibly derived from a god of the dead or a shamanic figure.

Position A — evidence

The lack of clear evidence for him before the Migration Period; his complex, contradictory nature might be a result of syncretism.

Position B

Odin has deep Proto-Indo-European roots, perhaps related to figures like the Vedic Rudra or the Celtic Lugus.

Position B — evidence

Linguistic and comparative mythological studies that suggest a common 'sovereignty god' archetype.

Scholarly Consensus

There is no consensus. The debate is ongoing, with many scholars leaning toward a development in the late Iron Age but with possible older roots.
