Who They Are

An old man with one eye knocks on your door in the middle of a storm. He doesn't ask for shelter — he asks what you would sacrifice to know the truth about your own death. His voice is soft, but the question burns. When you hesitate, he smiles. He already knows your answer. He knew it before you were born.

Odin is the chief god of the Norse pantheon, but he is nothing like a comfortable throne-sitter. He is the god of wisdom, war, death, poetry, magic, and the runes — and he paid for every scrap of that knowledge with pain. He hanged himself from the World Tree Yggdrasil for nine days and nights, pierced by his own spear, to learn the secrets of the runes. He gouged out his own eye and dropped it into Mimir's Well for a single drink of cosmic wisdom. This is not a god who inherited power; this is a god who tore it from the universe with his bare hands.

Odin is driven by a single, desperate obsession: he knows Ragnarök is coming, and he is doing everything in his power to prepare. He gathers the greatest slain warriors in Valhalla, sends his ravens Huginn and Muninn across the worlds to gather intelligence, and constantly seeks prophecies and forbidden knowledge — even when the prophecies tell him he will be devoured by the wolf Fenrir. He is not a king waiting to be overthrown; he is a general who has already read the last page of the book and is fighting anyway.

Origins

Odin is the son of Borr and the giantess Bestla. Along with his brothers Vili and Vé, he slew the primordial giant Ymir and fashioned the cosmos from his body — his blood became the oceans, his bones the mountains, his skull the sky. After creating the world, Odin and his brothers created the first humans, Ask and Embla, from two trees. Odin's reign is defined not by a founding myth but by the relentless accumulation of wisdom throughout his existence, culminating in his foreknowledge of Ragnarök.

Attested in Oral TraditionEra Antiquity

Form

A tall, gaunt old man with a long grey beard, cloaked in a dark blue or grey hooded robe and wearing a wide-brimmed traveler's hat pulled low over his missing eye. He carries the spear Gungnir, which never misses its mark. Two ravens — Thought (Huginn) and Memory (Muninn) — perch on his shoulders. Two wolves, Geri and Freki, lie at his feet. He often disguises himself as a wandering beggar or a weathered traveler to test mortals' hospitality.

Wisdom and Prophecy Odin is the wisest of all beings, having sacrificed his eye at Mimir's Well to drink from its waters and gain cosmic knowledge. He also hanged himself on Yggdrasil for nine days and nights to learn the secrets of the runes. He consults the severed head of the wise being Mimir for counsel and can see the future, though he is often bound by fate. Attested in Poetic Edda (Völuspá, Hávamál) and Prose Edda (Gylfaginning). His knowledge does not grant him power over fate; even he is doomed to fall at Ragnarök. None significant.

Seidr (Magic) Odin is a master of seidr, a form of Norse magic associated with fate, prophecy, and shapeshifting. This practice was traditionally considered feminine (ergi), and Odin's mastery of it is a sign of his willingness to break taboos for power. Attested in Ynglinga Saga and Lokasenna, where Loki taunts him for practicing it. Performing seidr was seen as unmanly, a point of contention and mockery from other gods. The exact nature and mechanics of seidr are not fully understood by modern scholars.

Runic Magic Odin is the god of the runes, having discovered them through his self-sacrifice on Yggdrasil. He can use them for a variety of purposes, including binding, healing, and cursing. Attested in Hávamál (Rúnatals þáttr). The specific powers of each rune are not fully detailed in surviving sources. None significant.

Shape-shifting Odin can change his form at will, taking the shape of animals or other beings. He is said to travel as an eagle, a snake, and many other forms. Attested in Ynglinga Saga and various myths. No specific limitations are given, but he often uses this ability for deception. None significant.

God of War and the Slain Odin is the god of war, but he is not a god of honorable combat. He is a god of strategy, cunning, and the chaos of battle. He sends his valkyries to choose which warriors will die and be brought to Valhöll to fight for him at Ragnarök. Attested throughout Poetic and Prose Edda. His role as a war-god is to sow discord and choose the fallen, not to ensure victory for any particular side. His methods are often seen as treacherous, even by his own followers.

Behavior & Habitat

Odin is manipulative, calculating, and profoundly lonely in his knowledge. He frequently tests mortals with cruel trials disguised as hospitality — the wandering stranger you turn away might be the All-Father, and the consequences are legendary. He favors cunning over brute force, poetry over proclamations. He is capable of immense cruelty (sacrificing his worshippers in battle to harvest the best warriors for Valhalla) and immense tenderness (weeping over the death of his son Baldr). He values intelligence above all else and treats stupidity as a moral failing.

Weaknesses & Wards

His obsession with preventing Ragnarök blinds him to other concerns and sometimes accelerates the doom he fears. He is bound by fate — he knows he will die to Fenrir and cannot change it. His missing eye gives him wisdom but literal blind spots. His need for knowledge makes him vulnerable to manipulation by those who can offer it. The death of Baldr, which he could not prevent despite all his power, shattered something fundamental in him.

Accounts

Odin and his brothers, Vili and Vé, slew the primordial giant Ymir and used his body to create the world. From his flesh they made the earth, from his blood the seas, from his bones the mountains, and from his skull the sky. They then created the first humans, Ask and Embla, from tree trunks, and Odin gave them spirit and life.

Odin desired the Mead of Poetry, a magical brew that grants the gift of skaldic verse. He tricked the giant Suttung and his daughter Gunnlod to obtain it. He slept with Gunnlod for three nights and then drank all the mead, escaping by shape-shifting into an eagle.

Odin hanged himself from the world-tree Yggdrasil for nine days and nine nights, wounded by his own spear, as a sacrifice to himself, in order to gain the knowledge of the runes. He received no food or drink, and at the end of his ordeal, he saw the runes and seized them, screaming.

Odin's son Baldr is killed by a spear of mistletoe, guided by the trickster Loki. Odin, grieving, sends his other son Hermóðr to Hel to try and bargain for Baldr's return, but the attempt fails. Odin then impregnates the giantess Rindr, and their son, Váli, is born to avenge Baldr.

Odin and his companions Loki and Hœnir are forced to pay a ransom for killing an otter, which is actually the shape-shifted son of the dwarf Hreidmar. Loki is sent to get the ransom, and he steals a hoard of gold, including a magical ring called Andvaranaut, from the dwarf Andvari. Odin is forced to give up the ring, which carries a curse.

Cultural Significance

Odin was the patron of kings, warriors, poets, and outcasts — a god for those who lived on the edge of society. Wednesday is named after him (Woden's Day). His myth of self-sacrifice on Yggdrasil has drawn comparisons to the crucifixion by scholars of comparative religion. The Viking Age was shaped by Odinic values of courage in the face of certain doom and the pursuit of fame that would outlast death.

Modern Interpretations

Odin appears prominently in Marvel comics and films as a stern but benevolent father figure — a significant sanitization of the mythological original. Neil Gaiman's 'American Gods' presents him as Mr. Wednesday, a con-man god surviving in America through grift and manipulation. The 'God of War' (2018) game features him as a cruel, paranoid figure whose foresight of Ragnarök has made him a monster. He remains the archetype of the wise wanderer, the mentor who tests you before helping you.

Odin and Óðr are the same deity, a split from a single original figure. ⇌ They are two distinct gods, with Óðr being a lesser-known or possibly older figure.
Evidence for A: The names are etymologically identical (Óðr is the noun from which Óðinn is derived).

What We Don't Know

The exact nature of Odin's pre-Viking Age cult is not fully understood. The relationship between Odin and Óðr, and the potential link to the Celtic god Lugus, remains speculative. The full extent of his role in everyday religious practice (as opposed to elite, warrior-focused worship) is unclear. The specific rituals and practices of his cult, beyond the famous human sacrifices described by Adam of Bremen, are not well-documented.
