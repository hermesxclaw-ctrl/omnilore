Who It Is

He gave his eye for a drink from a well, and his life for a glimpse of the runes. He is the god who hangs himself on the World Tree, who whispers secrets to the dead, and who will be devoured by a wolf at the end of the world. He is not a hero. He is not a villain. He is Odin, and he is the price of wisdom.

Odin is a complex and contradictory figure: a wise seeker of knowledge, a wily trickster, a patron of warriors and poets, a god of the dead, and a instigator of conflict. He is known for his cunning, his willingness to deceive and betray even his own champions, and his relentless pursuit of wisdom and power to stave off Ragnarök.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Proto-Germanic *Wōðanaz, meaning 'lord of frenzy' or 'leader of the possessed'. The Old Norse Óðinn is derived from this, relating to óðr, meaning 'mind', 'inspiration', 'fury', or 'poetry'.

Pronunciation — OH-din (English); OH-thin (approximate Old Norse, with the 'ð' as in 'the')

Aliases — Óðinn, Wōden, Wotan, Wōðanaz, Allfather, Alföðr

Ownership — Norse and broader Germanic paganism; attested across Scandinavia, Iceland, Germany, and Anglo-Saxon England. A central figure in the reconstructed Proto-Germanic religion.

Powers
Wisdom and Prophecy

Odin sacrifices his eye at Mimir's well for wisdom and hangs himself on Yggdrasil for nine days to learn the runes. He can see the future but is often powerless to change it.

Limit: His knowledge is hard-won; he remains vulnerable to fate and his own prophecies.

Shapeshifting

Odin can change his form at will, transforming into animals or other beings.

Limit: Not explicitly stated, but presumably limited by his own will.

Seiðr Magic

A form of Norse magic associated with fate-weaving and prophecy, typically practiced by women (völvas). Odin's practice of it is sometimes seen as unmanly (ergi) by other gods.

Limit: Practice is considered taboo for men in the society depicted.

Command over the Dead

He can raise the dead (as with the völva in Völuspá) and receives the souls of slain warriors (einherjar) in Valhöll.

Limit: His power over the dead is not absolute; he must use spells to compel them to speak.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Depicted as a tall, old man with a long beard, often wearing a wide-brimmed hat and a cloak. He is famously one-eyed, having sacrificed his other eye for wisdom. Often shown carrying his spear Gungnir and wearing a ring or arm-ring (Draupnir).

Symbols — His spear Gungnir, his ravens Huginn (Thought) and Muninn (Memory), his wolves Geri and Freki, his eight-legged horse Sleipnir, and his self-made runes. The Valknut symbol is often associated with him in modern times, though its historical connection is debated.

Domains — Wisdom, poetry, war, death, magic (seiðr), prophecy, kingship, and the hunt. He is the leader of the Æsir gods and the ruler of Asgard.

Accounts I
The Creation of the World and HumanityProse Edda (Gylfaginning), Poetic Edda (Völuspá)

Mythic past

Odin and his brothers Vili and Vé slay the primordial giant Ymir and fashion the world from his body. They later find two logs on a beach and create the first humans, Ask and Embla, giving them spirit, reason, and senses. The world is created, and humanity is established, with Odin as a paternal figure.

The Mead of PoetryProse Edda (Skáldskaparmál)

Mythic past

Odin steals the Mead of Poetry, a magical drink that grants wisdom and poetic skill. He seduces the giantess Gunnlöð to get it, then escapes in the form of an eagle, regurgitating the mead into vats for the gods. Odin gains the mead, which he distributes to poets and scholars, becoming their patron.

Accounts II
The Binding of FenrirProse Edda (Gylfaginning)

Mythic past

The gods, fearing the wolf Fenrir's growth, decide to bind him. They trick him with a magical ribbon (Gleipnir) after two failed attempts. Fenrir agrees to be bound only if one god places a hand in his mouth as a pledge. The war-god Týr does so, and Fenrir bites off his hand. Fenrir is bound until Ragnarök, and Týr loses his hand. Odin is fated to be devoured by Fenrir at Ragnarök.

The Death of BaldrProse Edda (Gylfaginning)

Mythic past

Odin's son Baldr is killed by a mistletoe spear thrown by the blind god Höðr, tricked by Loki. Odin, grief-stricken, sends his other son Hermóðr to Hel to try to bring Baldr back. The attempt fails, and Baldr remains in the underworld. Baldr's death is a major omen of Ragnarök. Odin impregnates the giantess Rindr to produce a son, Váli, who avenges Baldr by killing Höðr.

RagnarökPoetic Edda (Völuspá), Prose Edda (Gylfaginning)

Mythic future

Odin leads the einherjar and the gods against the forces of Surtr, Loki, and the giants. He fights the wolf Fenrir and is swallowed whole. His son Víðarr avenges him by tearing Fenrir's jaw apart. The world is destroyed in fire and flood, but a new world rises from the sea, and some gods survive.

Odin's role as a 'good' godOdin is a benevolent Allfather, a wise and just ruler who protects humanity and warriors.Odin is a capricious, treacherous god who sows discord and betrays his own followers for his own ends (i.e., preparing for Ragnarök).

Odin's originsOdin is a late development, emerging in the late Iron Age or Migration Period, possibly derived from a god of the dead or a shamanic figure.Odin has deep Proto-Indo-European roots, perhaps related to figures like the Vedic Rudra or the Celtic Lugus.

Odin is the 'king' of the Norse gods in a human monarchy sense. — He is the leader of the Æsir, but his rule is not absolute. He is bound by fate and often acts through cunning rather than command. Other gods, like Thor, are more popular with the common people.

Odin is a purely benevolent 'Allfather' figure. — He is a complex, morally grey god of war, death, and wisdom. He is just as likely to betray his followers as to protect them, all in service of his own goals.

Vikings all worshipped Odin as their primary god. — While Odin was a major god, evidence suggests Thor was more widely worshipped by the common people, especially farmers and sailors. Odin was more the god of kings, warriors, and poets.

Odin is a 'sky father' or creator god in the monotheistic sense. — He is a creator in that he (with his brothers) shaped the world from Ymir's body, but he did not create the universe ex nihilo. He is not omnipotent or omniscient, and he is subject to fate.
