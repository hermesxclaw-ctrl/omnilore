Who It Is

In the vast, star-strewn darkness of the South African Karoo, a hunter huddles by his fire, listening. The wind picks up, a spiraling column of dust dances at the edge of the firelight, and he knows—with a cold certainty older than any god—that //Gauwa is out there, the taker of hearts, the lord of the cold and the dark. He is the shadow that falls when the Mantis's light flickers, the end of every story, the adversary who makes creation itself meaningful. He is death, and he is hungry.

Gauna is a malevolent being who actively seeks to harm, kill, and devour. He is associated with death, disease, and misfortune. He is a predator, stalking his victims in the wild. He is also a thief, stealing the life-force or the 'heart' of his victims. Unlike /Kaggen, who is a bumbling but ultimately creative trickster, Gauna is a cunning and dangerous adversary. He is not easily tricked and is a formidable opponent.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Gauna (also rendered //Gauwa or //Gaunab) is from the ǀXam (San) language of southern Africa. The precise linguistic root is debated, but it is associated with the concept of opposition to /Kaggen, the creator-trickster figure. Some scholars suggest the name relates to notions of the 'other

Pronunciation — The leading '//' represents a dental click, a characteristic sound of Khoisan languages. The name is roughly pronounced as '//Gau-na', with the click produced by the tongue against the teeth.

Aliases — //Gauwa, //Gaunab, //Gaua

Ownership — San / Xam mythology. The ǀXam San people, an indigenous hunter-gatherer people of the northern Cape region of South Africa. Their oral traditions were recorded in the late 19th cen

Powers
Death Manipulation

Gauna is the primary agent of death in ǀXam mythology. He kills people, often by shooting them with invisible arrows or by stealing their hearts. He is the lord of the underworld, where the souls of the dead reside.

Limit: Gauna is not omnipotent. He can be tricked, fought, and even defeated by clever heroes like /Kaggen. He is also bound by certain rules, such as the need to be invited or to find a victim's 'scent'.

Shape-shifting

Gauna can transform his appearance, taking on forms that are terrifying or deceptive. He may appear as a whirlwind, an animal, or a shadowy figure.

Limit: His transformations are often described as being incomplete or monstrous, revealing his true nature.

Supernatural Strength and Ferocity

Gauna is a formidable physical opponent, capable of overpowering and killing humans with ease. He is a creature of immense power and aggression.

Limit: He can be overpowered by /Kaggen when the trickster uses his wits and magical abilities.

Mastery over the Hunt (Negative Aspect)

Gauna can blight the hunt, making it unsuccessful or dangerous. He scares away game or leads hunters astray.

Limit: His influence can be countered by rituals and the power of the shamans.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Gauna is not described with a fixed physical form in the ǀXam narratives. He is a shape-shifter and a disembodied presence, often associated with darkness, the bush at night, and the wind. He can manifest as a whirlwind, a shadow, or a monstrous figure. In some stories, he appears as a giant or a being with animalistic features, but his essence is more about his terrifying presence than a fixed appearance.

Symbols — Gauna is symbolically associated with darkness, the night, the cold south wind, and the wild bush. He is also linked to death and the process of dying. His name itself is a symbol of the 'other', the dangerous unknown that lies beyond the boundaries of human society and the creative order of /Kaggen.

Domains — Gauna's primary domain is the underworld, the realm of the dead. He is also the master of the bush at night, a place of danger and chaos outside the safety of the camp. He is associated with the cold, the dark, and the wild. He is the antithesis of the domestic, life-giving space of the campfire and the community.

Accounts I
The Story of the Mantis and the Dying ManBleek-Lloyd Collection; told by ||kabbo.

Pre-colonial (recorded 1870s)

A man is dying. His family calls for /Kaggen (the Mantis) to save him. /Kaggen comes and sees that the man's heart has been taken by Gauna. /Kaggen, using his trickster abilities, travels to the underworld or confronts Gauna to retrieve the heart. The story details the struggle between the life-giving power of the creator and the death-dealing force of Gauna. The outcome varies, but the story establishes the fundamental conflict between life and death, order and chaos. It also shows that even the creator can struggle against the power of death.

Accounts II
Gauna and the WhirlwindBleek-Lloyd Collection; various informants.

Pre-colonial (recorded 1870s)

Gauna is associated with the whirlwind, a powerful and dangerous natural phenomenon. The whirlwind is seen as a manifestation of his presence, a force that can pick up dust, debris, and even people, carrying them away to their doom. People are warned to be careful when a whirlwind appears, as it may be Gauna seeking a victim. This story establishes Gauna's connection to the wild and dangerous forces of nature, reinforcing his role as a being to be feared.

The Theft of the HeartBleek-Lloyd Collection; various informants.

Pre-colonial (recorded 1870s)

A common theme is Gauna's method of killing. He steals the heart of his victim, which is the seat of life and vitality. Once the heart is taken, the person begins to sicken and die. A shaman or /Kaggen must then intervene to track down Gauna and recover the stolen heart. This narrative explains the cause of illness and death, framing it as a cosmic theft by a malevolent being.

Gauna is the 'Devil' of the San people. — This is a Christian-influenced interpretation. The ǀXam did not have a dualistic cosmology with a single 'evil god' opposing a 'good god'. Gauna is a dangerous, death-dealing being, but he is not the equivalent of Satan. He is a part of the natural and spiritual world, a force to be feared and countered, not a tempter or a symbol of moral evil.

The San people worshipped Gauna. — There is no evidence that the ǀXam 'worshipped' Gauna. They feared him, told stories about him, and used rituals and shamans to protect themselves from him. Worship implies a form of devotion or supplication, which is not attested in the sources. He was a malevolent force to be managed, not a deity to be revered.

Gauna is just a personification of the fear of the dark. — While Gauna is associated with the night and the wild, he is a complex mythological figure with a specific role in the cosmos. He is the agent of death, the lord of the underworld, and the adversary of the creator /Kaggen. He is not simply a metaphor for fear, but a being with a narrative existence and a defined role in the ǀXam worldview.
