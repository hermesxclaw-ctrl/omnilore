Who It Is

Gauna (also //Gauwa or !Gauwa) is the San deity of death, sickness, and darkness — Kaggen's opposite and adversary. While Kaggen creates and sustains life, Gauna consumes it. He is described as a shadowy figure who lurks in the dark places of the bush, sending disease, misfortune, and death to humans. The San believe that illness is caused by Gauna's invisible arrows, which strike the body and can only be removed by a trance healer (n/om k'xao) who battles Gauna's influence in the spirit realm during the healing dance. Unlike Kaggen, who is identified with the light and life of day, Gauna is associated with night, the cold, and the grave. He is sometimes conflated with the spirits of the angry dead who have not been properly buried or remembered. In San cosmology, the relationship between Kaggen and Gauna is not a simple good-versus-evil duality: Gauna's existence is accepted as a necessary part of the cosmic order — death is what gives life its urgency and meaning. Still, he is actively resisted through ritual, dance, and the community's collective spiritual energy. The ethnographic records of Wilhelm Bleek's interviews with the Xam man //Kabbo provide the most detailed surviving account of Gauna's mythology, preserved from a culture that faced near-extermination during the colonial era.

The Approach

In the San night, where the bush becomes a threshold and graves are avoided like open mouths in the earth, one name surfaces in modern retellings: Gauna, the one said to rule the tormented dead and send them back to trouble the living. He is cast as the enemy of Cagn, the mantis‑creator who dreamed the world into being—a dark counterpoint to creation, dwelling in an underworld that pulls at the edges of the hunting ground. Yet when we turn to the fragile notebooks of /Xam storytellers, Gauna’s name is almost a ghost, hinted at in later summaries but elusive in the original texts. This tension between a vividly described death‑lord and his near‑absence from primary sources is where the real story begins.

Content advisory. This dossier discusses death, haunting, torment of the dead, fear of graves, and coercive spiritual power. Readers who are grieving or sensitive to depictions of death and the afterlife may find some sections emotionally difficult.
Identity

Gauna

Pronunciation Interpretive: likely /ˈɡaʊ.nə/ or /ˈɡɑː.nə/ in English usage. No phonetic transcription from /Xam or other San languages was found for this specific name.

Etymology

Unknown. No securely attested etymology for "Gauna" in the /Xam or broader San linguistic record could be located. Some later popular sources implicitly associate the name with San concepts of stars or spirits (e.g., !Gaunu in Bleek & Lloyd), but this linkage is speculative and not supported by explicit primary-text evidence.

Cultural Ownership

Attributed to the Bushpeople/San of southern Africa, often specifically linked in secondary literature to the /Xam or closely related groups. However, the figure "Gauna" appears primarily in modern mythographic summaries rather than in the 19th‑century /Xam textual corpus itself, so his status as an emic, traditionally named deity is uncertain and may reflect later systematizing by outsiders.

Disputed Identifications

Gauna is widely described in modern reference works as a death or underworld deity, enemy of the creator Cagn (/Kaggen). Yet the major primary corpus for /Xam myth—the Bleek & Lloyd notebooks and Specimens of Bushman Folklore—does not clearly attest a figure named "Gauna" in this role. Instead, related names such as !Gaunu and !Gaunu‑tsaxau occur in other mythic contexts (stars, children of the Mantis). As a result, some scholars question whether "Gauna" is (1) a distinct, traditional San deity of death, (2) a systematizing construct created by later compilers to provide a dualistic counterpart to Cagn, or (3) a misreading or re‑spelling of other /Xam names. The available evidence does not allow a definitive resolution.

Description

Appearance

Attested (secondary): Gauna is described in modern mythographic summaries as a male spirit‑being who lives in the underworld and rules the tormented ghosts of the dead. No detailed physical description, animal form, or iconography is provided in the available sources. Unlike /Kaggen, who is explicitly mantis‑ and eland‑shaped, Gauna’s form is left vague, suggesting a more abstract or generalized personification of death and the underworld rather than a zoomorphic figure.
Reconstructed: Given San cosmology’s emphasis on animal‑human transformations and nocturnal landscapes, some scholars infer that Gauna may be imagined as a presence in the bush at night or near graves and waterholes, but this remains interpretive and is not directly stated in primary texts.
Unknown: No explicit description of Gauna’s body, clothing, or sensory qualities (voice, smell, etc.) has been located in the Bleek & Lloyd corpus or other primary /Xam sources.

Symbols & Iconography

Attested (secondary): Gauna is symbolically associated with the underworld, graves, and the spirits of the dead. The Encyclopedia.com summary notes that Bushpeople avoid graves for fear that the dead will pull them down into the underworld, a taboo linked to Gauna’s power over the dead.
Reconstructed: The "bush at night" realm mentioned in some metadata and interpretive discussions likely reflects the San experience of nocturnal danger, unseen presences, and liminal spaces where the living and the dead intersect. Gauna, as leader of the dead, can be seen as a personification of these dangerous zones.
Unknown: No specific rock‑art motif, animal emblem, or ritual object is explicitly identified as Gauna’s symbol in the sources consulted.

Behaviour & Personality

Attested (secondary): Gauna is portrayed as hostile to the living world created by Cagn. He hates the world and attempts to interfere with the affairs of humans and animals. He commands the spirits of the dead to haunt their living relatives and regularly visits the earth to seize people and imprison them in the underworld. He is powerful enough to cause trouble but explicitly described as less powerful than Cagn.
Interpretive: This characterization aligns Gauna with a chaotic, destructive principle in San cosmology, contrasting with Cagn’s creative, trickster‑creator role. Gauna’s behavior is not playful tricksterism but persistent, malevolent interference—more akin to a death‑lord or underworld ruler than to a morally ambiguous trickster.
Unknown: No detailed personality traits (humor, cunning, cowardice, etc.) are recorded, and there is no evidence of Gauna acting as a culture hero or benefactor.

Domains & Habitats

Attested (secondary): Gauna lives in the underworld, ruling the tormented ghosts of the dead. From this realm he sends spirits to haunt the living and personally visits the earth to capture people. The underworld is conceptualized as a place of imprisonment and torment rather than a neutral land of the dead.
Interpretive: The "bush at night" realm, mentioned in some modern metadata, likely reflects the San perception of nocturnal bush as a liminal, dangerous space where spirits and death‑powers are active. Gauna’s domain thus spans both the subterranean underworld and the dark, peripheral spaces of the hunting ground.
Unknown: The cosmological structure of Gauna’s realm (layers, entrances, specific locations) is not described in the available sources.

Version Differences

Attested (secondary): In the Encyclopedia.com/Myths and Legends of the World entry, Gauna is clearly framed as (1) leader of the spirits of the dead, (2) enemy of Cagn, and (3) ruler of an underworld of tormented ghosts. Ritual taboos (avoiding graves) are explicitly linked to fear of Gauna’s power.
Reconstructed/disputed: In the Bleek & Lloyd corpus and related scholarship on /Kaggen, death and the origin of death are explained through other myths (e.g., Kaggen as the moon, the hare’s miscommunication). These narratives do not mention Gauna. Some scholars therefore argue that the dualistic pairing "Cagn vs. Gauna" may be a later interpretive construction, influenced by comparative mythology (e.g., creator vs. death‑god) rather than a fully emic San system.
Unknown: No variant San narrative directly contrasting Gauna and Cagn in a mythic story (e.g., a battle, negotiation, or cosmic conflict) has been located in primary texts. The relationship is described in summary form only.

Gauna is described as the leader of the spirits of the dead, ruling the tormented ghosts in the underworld and directing them to haunt their living relatives.

Limitations

His authority is confined to the realm of the dead and those he can seize or influence; he is explicitly said to be less powerful than Cagn, who created the world and established protective rituals and taboos.

Contradictions

No primary /Xam text has yet been found that explicitly names Gauna in this role, raising questions about whether this leadership is a traditional San belief or a later mythographic synthesis.

Rule of the underworld

Source Version Myths and Legends of the World, "Gauna" entry

Description

Gauna lives in the underworld and rules the tormented ghosts imprisoned there. He can imprison living humans in this realm when he seizes them from the earth.

Limitations

Gauna’s underworld is portrayed as a place of torment and confinement, but the extent of his jurisdiction (all dead vs. specific categories) is not specified.

Contradictions

San cosmology in Bleek & Lloyd emphasizes complex relationships between humans, animals, and spirits across the landscape; an explicitly centralized underworld ruler is not clearly attested in those texts, leading some scholars to question the universality of this depiction.

Haunting and coercion via spirits

The specific mechanisms of haunting (illness, bad luck, nightmares, accidents) are not detailed; the description remains general.

Contradictions

Other San traditions attribute misfortune and illness to a range of causes, including sorcery, animal‑spirit anger, and trance‑dance dynamics. The degree to which Gauna is singled out as the primary source of haunting varies across modern summaries and is not clearly anchored in primary texts.

Seizing the living and imprisoning them in the underworld

Source Version Myths and Legends of the World, "Gauna" entry

Gauna regularly visits the earth to seize people and imprison them in the underworld, effectively causing their death or spiritual capture.

Contradictions

San narratives about death often emphasize miscommunication (the hare and the moon) and the inevitability of mortality rather than a personalized death‑god who actively abducts people. This difference in emphasis is a key point of scholarly debate.

Gauna is explicitly described as the enemy of Cagn, the creator god. He hates the world Cagn created and tries to interfere with the affairs of the living.

Gauna is said to be less powerful than Cagn, implying that Cagn’s rituals and taboos can limit Gauna’s influence.

Contradictions

Primary /Xam texts present /Kaggen as a complex trickster‑creator with many adversaries and conflicts, but do not clearly narrate a cosmic opposition with a named death‑god Gauna. The dualistic framing may be a later interpretive overlay.

Stories & Myths

Gauna, Leader of the Spirits of the Dead

Source Myths and Legends of the World, "Gauna" entry (Encyclopedia.com)Culture San/Bushpeople of southern Africa (as described in modern mythographic literature)Period Modern mythographic summary based on 20th‑century scholarship; original traditional period not specified

Summary

The Bushpeople believe that Gauna is the leader of the spirits of the dead and the enemy of Cagn, the creator god. Gauna lives in the underworld, ruling the tormented ghosts of the dead. At his command, these spirits haunt their living relatives. Gauna regularly visits the earth to seize people and imprison them in the underworld, expressing his hatred for the world Cagn created. To protect themselves from Gauna’s interference, the Bushpeople follow rituals and taboos taught by Cagn, including avoiding graves so that the dead cannot pull them down into the underworld.

Participants

Gauna (leader of the spirits of the dead)

Cagn (/Kaggen), creator god

Spirits of the dead

Living Bushpeople who observe taboos

Consequences

Gauna’s hostility and power over the dead establish a cosmological tension between creation and death. Human communities respond by adhering to ritual taboos and practices that maintain distance from graves and the underworld, reinforcing social norms around burial and sacred spaces.

Variants

No detailed narrative variants are provided in the Encyclopedia.com entry. Other San myths about death (e.g., Kaggen as the moon and the hare’s miscommunication) explain the origin of death without mentioning Gauna, suggesting parallel or alternative traditions rather than direct variants of the same story.

Description

Gauna is described as the enemy of Cagn, the creator god. He hates the world Cagn created and attempts to interfere with the affairs of the living, primarily through his control of the dead and their spirits.

Version Note

This relationship is clearly stated in modern mythographic summaries but not directly attested in the Bleek & Lloyd /Xam texts as a named opposition between "Gauna" and /Kaggen.

Conflicting Accounts

Primary /Xam narratives portray /Kaggen in conflict with various beings (baboons, hyenas, etc.) but do not explicitly name Gauna as his cosmic adversary. Some scholars therefore regard the Cagn–Gauna opposition as a later interpretive construct.

Spirits of the dead

Gauna is said to rule the tormented ghosts of the dead in the underworld and to command them to haunt their living relatives.

Version Note

The relationship is presented as hierarchical and coercive: Gauna directs the dead rather than simply coexisting with them.

Conflicting Accounts

Other San traditions emphasize complex, reciprocal relationships between the living and the dead, mediated by trance dances and healers, without necessarily positing a single ruler of the dead. The prominence of Gauna in this role may vary across communities and sources.

Bushpeople/San communities

Type hostile/ritualSource Myths and Legends of the World, "Gauna" entry

Description

Gauna regularly visits the earth to seize people and imprison them in the underworld. Bushpeople respond by following rituals and taboos taught by Cagn to avoid Gauna’s interference, such as staying away from graves.

Version Note

The relationship is characterized by fear and avoidance rather than worship or propitiation. Gauna is not described as receiving offerings or prayers in the available sources.

Conflicting Accounts

San religious practice documented in ethnography focuses heavily on trance healing, animal‑spirit relations, and rainmaking. Gauna’s role in actual ritual practice is not well documented, and some scholars question whether he is a central figure in lived San religion.

Dark History
Content advisory. Gauna’s mythology involves death, torment of the dead, haunting of the living, spiritual coercion, and fear of graves and the underworld. These themes may be distressing, especially in contexts of bereavement or anxiety about death.

Torment of the dead in the underworld

Source Myths and Legends of the World, "Gauna" entry (Encyclopedia.com)

Description

Gauna is said to rule the tormented ghosts of the dead in the underworld. Rather than a peaceful land of the ancestors, his realm is depicted as a place of suffering and imprisonment, where the dead are subject to his authority and used as instruments to trouble the living.

Scholarly Context

This depiction reflects a darker, punitive conception of the afterlife than some other African traditions that emphasize ancestor veneration. It may also be influenced by comparative mythological frameworks that pair creator gods with malevolent underworld rulers.

Haunting and coercion of the living

Source Myths and Legends of the World, "Gauna" entry

Description

At Gauna’s command, the spirits of the dead haunt their living relatives, causing fear and misfortune. The living must avoid graves and adhere to taboos to prevent being pulled into the underworld.

Scholarly Context

San ethnography documents strong emotional and ritual responses to death, including avoidance of certain places and objects. The figure of Gauna may crystallize these fears into a personalized agent of death and haunting, though direct primary evidence is limited.

Seizure and imprisonment of humans

Source Myths and Legends of the World, "Gauna" entry

Description

Gauna regularly visits the earth to seize people and imprison them in the underworld, effectively enacting violent, coercive death.

Scholarly Context

This motif resonates with broader African and global mythic patterns in which death or underworld beings abduct humans. In the San context, it may intersect with experiences of sudden death, illness, and colonial violence, though such connections are interpretive and not explicitly stated in the sources.

Contradictions & Disputes

Existence of Gauna as a distinct traditional San deity

Position A

Gauna is a genuine, traditional San death deity, leader of the spirits of the dead and enemy of Cagn.

Position A — evidence

Myths and Legends of the World and its Encyclopedia.com reproduction explicitly describe Gauna in this role, presenting him as part of Bushpeople belief.

Position B

Gauna is a later mythographic construct or misreading, not clearly attested in primary /Xam texts; death and the underworld in San cosmology are explained through other figures and narratives.

Position B — evidence

The Bleek & Lloyd corpus and Specimens of Bushman Folklore, which are the primary sources for /Xam mythology, do not clearly attest a figure named "Gauna" as leader of the dead. Instead, they present complex narratives about /Kaggen, stars, animals, and the origin of death (e.g., the moon and hare story) without naming Gauna.

Scholarly Consensus

There is no clear consensus. Many popular and introductory works on African mythology repeat the Gauna–Cagn dualism, while more specialized San scholarship focuses on /Kaggen and other figures without emphasizing Gauna. The lack of direct primary evidence suggests caution in treating Gauna as a fully established traditional deity.

Nature of the underworld and the afterlife in San cosmology

Position A

The underworld ruled by Gauna is a place of torment and imprisonment, with the dead used to haunt the living.

Position A — evidence

The Gauna entry describes the underworld as a realm of tormented ghosts under Gauna’s control, emphasizing fear and avoidance of graves.

Position B

San conceptions of the afterlife are more diffuse, involving ancestors, spirits, and landscape features without a single centralized ruler; Gauna’s underworld may be an oversimplification.

Position B — evidence

Ethnographic and textual studies of San spirituality highlight trance healing, animal‑spirit relations, and sacred landscapes, with multiple spirit beings and complex interactions rather than a single underworld monarch. The origin‑of‑death myth involving Kaggen as the moon and the hare focuses on miscommunication rather than punitive afterlife.

Scholarly Consensus

Specialist scholarship tends to emphasize the complexity and multiplicity of San spirit worlds. The Gauna‑centered underworld may represent one interpretive strand or an external systematization rather than a universally held San doctrine.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

Major gaps remain in the documentation of Gauna. No primary /Xam or other San text has yet been located that explicitly names Gauna as leader of the dead and enemy of Cagn; the figure appears primarily in modern mythographic summaries. The linguistic form of "Gauna" in San languages is uncertain, and its relationship (if any) to names like !Gaunu and !Gaunu‑tsaxau is not clarified in the sources. Detailed descriptions of Gauna’s appearance, specific myths involving him, and his role in actual San ritual practice are lacking. Access to the full Bleek & Lloyd archive and to more recent San oral histories in original languages would be necessary to resolve whether Gauna is an emic deity, a regional variant, or a later interpretive construct. This dossier is therefore constrained by reliance on English‑language secondary sources available online as of 2026‑07‑30.

Research Scope

Research focused on English‑language materials accessible online as of 2026‑07‑30, including the Encyclopedia.com reproduction of Myths and Legends of the World, the sacred‑texts.com edition of Bleek & Lloyd’s Specimens of Bushman Folklore, and the Digital Bleek & Lloyd archive metadata. No direct access to unpublished field notes, contemporary San oral histories, or specialist monographs beyond available snippets was possible. The dossier prioritizes distinguishing attested primary evidence from later mythographic synthesis and flags uncertainties where Gauna’s status as a traditional deity is not firmly supported.
