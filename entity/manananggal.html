Who It Is

The manananggal is a terrifying witch-like creature in Philippine folklore that can sever its upper body from its lower half, sprout bat-like wings, and fly into the night to prey on sleeping humans. By day, she appears as a normal woman — often beautiful, married, and living in the community. By night, she rubs a special oil on her body that allows the upper torso to detach, leaving the lower half standing in place. She flies to the homes of pregnant women, extending an impossibly long, hollow tongue to suck the blood of fetuses or the hearts of the sick. The manananggal can be defeated by finding her lower body and sprinkling salt, garlic, or ashes on it — preventing her from rejoining and trapping her at dawn, when she dies in the sunlight. The legend is deeply rooted in rural Philippine communities and continues to appear in Filipino horror films, comics, and television.

The Approach

In the moonlit provinces of the Visayas, a woman's torso rises from her sleeping body, wings unfurling, entrails glistening in the dark. She is the manananggal, the 'self-segmenter'—a creature that separates itself to hunt, leaving its lower half behind like a discarded garment. For centuries, this terrifying figure has haunted Philippine folklore, a nightmare that preys on the most vulnerable: pregnant women, newborns, and lovers. Her legend is not merely a ghost story but a complex tapestry of fear, social taboo, and cultural identity, surviving from pre-colonial times to modern cinema, where she still flies through the collective imagination of the Philippines and beyond.

Content advisory. Depictions of violence against pregnant women and infants, body horror, and sexualized portrayals of a female monster in some adaptations.
Identity

Manananggal

Pronunciation mah-nah-nahng-GAHL

Known As

MagtatangalThe Self-Segmenter

Etymology

From Tagalog 'tanggál' meaning 'to remove' or 'to separate', translating to 'remover' or 'separator', interpreted as 'one who separates itself'.

Cultural Ownership

Philippines, particularly Visayan regions (Capiz, Iloilo, Bohol, Antique)

Disputed Identifications

Sometimes conflated with other Philippine creatures like aswang, but distinct in its ability to separate its upper torso. Some scholars see parallels with Southeast Asian entities like the Thai Krasue or Indonesian Penanggalan, but these are separate beings with different traits.

Description

Appearance

Typically depicted as a female creature with a grotesque appearance. It can detach its upper torso from its lower half, exposing its entrails. It grows large, bat-like wings for nocturnal flight. It possesses fangs, giving a vampire-like appearance. Some accounts describe an elongated, proboscis-like tongue used to extract fetuses or draw blood.

Symbols & Iconography

In modern media, the manananggal is often shown with a distinct upper torso, trailing entrails, and leathery wings. The lower torso is often shown standing or seated, as if alive. The creature is associated with darkness, night, and the moon.

Behaviour & Personality

The manananggal is a nocturnal predator, seeking out sleeping pregnant women to feed on fetuses or blood. It also targets newlyweds, lovers, and newborn children. It is cunning and malicious, but has a vulnerability: its severed lower torso. It must rejoin its body before sunrise or it will die. It is repelled by garlic, salt, holy water, daggers, light, vinegar, spices, and the tail of a stingray.

Domains & Habitats

Rural areas in the Visayas, particularly Capiz, Iloilo, Bohol, and Antique. It is said to dwell in trees, churches, or other dark places. It often targets households where there are pregnant women or newlyweds.

Version Differences

Descriptions vary: some say it has a proboscis, others a long tongue; some fly with wings, others are said to fly without them. In some accounts, the manananggal is an ordinary woman by day, and only transforms at night. In others, it is a separate creature. The creature is sometimes male (as in the historical account), but most modern portrayals are female.

Stories & Myths

The Manananggal and the Pregnant Woman

Source Philippine folklore (oral tradition)Culture FilipinoPeriod Pre-colonial to present

Summary

A manananggal flies to a house where a pregnant woman sleeps. Using its long proboscis, it inserts into the woman's womb to suck out the fetus or drink her blood. The woman may not wake, or if she does, she may be paralyzed. The creature's lower torso is left hidden, often under a tree or in a dark corner. If discovered, the lower half can be destroyed with salt, garlic, ash, or fire, preventing the creature from reuniting and causing its death at sunrise.

Participants

Manananggal

Pregnant woman

Potential witnesses

Consequences

If the lower half is not destroyed, the manananggal returns before dawn and reattaches, continuing its attacks. If destroyed, the creature dies.

Variants

In some versions, the manananggal targets newlyweds or lovers, and may also attack grooms who were abandoned before marriage.

The Aswang in Catanduanes

Source Spanish colonial account (as quoted in source)Culture Filipino (colonial era)Period 17th-18th century

Summary

A Spanish account describes a 'magtatangal' who shows himself at night without his head or entrails, carrying his head to different places and returning it in the morning. The author dismisses it as a fable, but notes that the natives affirm they have seen it, attributing it to the devil's deception.

Preying on pregnant women and infants

Source Philippine folklore

Description

The manananggal is said to extract fetuses from pregnant women and drink their blood, and to target newborn children. This is a core aspect of the folklore.

Scholarly Context

Some scholars interpret this as a manifestation of fears surrounding childbirth and maternal mortality in pre-modern societies.

Colonial documentation and demonization

Source Spanish colonial accounts

Description

Spanish colonial accounts describe the creature as a 'devil' and a 'fable', reflecting the colonial perspective that indigenous beliefs were superstitious and demonic.

Scholarly Context

These accounts are primary evidence of how colonial powers viewed and recorded indigenous folklore, often distorting it.

Sexualized portrayals in modern media

Source Modern media

Description

Some modern adaptations, such as the erotic novel 'Melania: Devourer of Men', sexualize the manananggal, depicting it as a seductive and dangerous female monster.

Scholarly Context

This reflects contemporary cultural trends, but may not be rooted in traditional folklore.

Contradictions & Disputes

Gender of the manananggal

Position A

Most modern portrayals depict the manananggal as female.

Position A — evidence

Contemporary films and literature almost always show a female creature.

Position B

Historical accounts, such as the Spanish colonial description, mention a male 'magtatangal'.

Position B — evidence

The 17th-18th century account describes a male figure.

Scholarly Consensus

The original folklore may have included both genders, but the female form has become dominant in modern culture, possibly due to associations with fertility and childbirth.

Method of feeding

Position A

The manananggal uses a proboscis-like tongue to extract fetuses or blood.

Position A — evidence

Common in modern folklore and media.

Position B

Some accounts describe it as eating human flesh.

Position B — evidence

The Spanish account says it 'eats human flesh'.

Scholarly Consensus

There is variation; the proboscis may be a later addition, while flesh-eating is mentioned in earlier accounts.

Relationship to aswang

Position A

The manananggal is a type of aswang.

Position A — evidence

Many sources group them together, and they share weaknesses.

Position B

The manananggal is a distinct creature, separate from aswang.

Position B — evidence

Some scholars argue they have different origins and characteristics.

Scholarly Consensus

There is no consensus; it depends on the source and region.
