Who It Is

In the moonlit provinces of the Visayas, a woman's torso rises from her sleeping body, wings unfurling, entrails glistening in the dark. She is the manananggal, the 'self-segmenter'—a creature that separates itself to hunt, leaving its lower half behind like a discarded garment. For centuries, this terrifying figure has haunted Philippine folklore, a nightmare that preys on the most vulnerable: pregnant women, newborns, and lovers. Her legend is not merely a ghost story but a complex tapestry of fear, social taboo, and cultural identity, surviving from pre-colonial times to modern cinema, where she still flies through the collective imagination of the Philippines and beyond.

The manananggal is a nocturnal predator, seeking out sleeping pregnant women to feed on fetuses or blood. It also targets newlyweds, lovers, and newborn children. It is cunning and malicious, but has a vulnerability: its severed lower torso. It must rejoin its body before sunrise or it will die. It is repelled by garlic, salt, holy water, daggers, light, vinegar, spices, and the tail of a stingray.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Typically depicted as a female creature with a grotesque appearance. It can detach its upper torso from its lower half, exposing its entrails. It grows large, bat-like wings for nocturnal flight. It possesses fangs, giving a vampire-like appearance. Some accounts describe an elongated, proboscis-like tongue used to extract fetuses or draw blood.

Symbols — In modern media, the manananggal is often shown with a distinct upper torso, trailing entrails, and leathery wings. The lower torso is often shown standing or seated, as if alive. The creature is associated with darkness, night, and the moon.

Domains — Rural areas in the Visayas, particularly Capiz, Iloilo, Bohol, and Antique. It is said to dwell in trees, churches, or other dark places. It often targets households where there are pregnant women or newlyweds.

Accounts I
The Manananggal and the Pregnant WomanPhilippine folklore (oral tradition)

Pre-colonial to present

A manananggal flies to a house where a pregnant woman sleeps. Using its long proboscis, it inserts into the woman's womb to suck out the fetus or drink her blood. The woman may not wake, or if she does, she may be paralyzed. The creature's lower torso is left hidden, often under a tree or in a dark corner. If discovered, the lower half can be destroyed with salt, garlic, ash, or fire, preventing the creature from reuniting and causing its death at sunrise. If the lower half is not destroyed, the manananggal returns before dawn and reattaches, continuing its attacks. If destroyed, the creature dies.

Accounts II
The Aswang in CatanduanesSpanish colonial account (as quoted in source)

17th-18th century

A Spanish account describes a 'magtatangal' who shows himself at night without his head or entrails, carrying his head to different places and returning it in the morning. The author dismisses it as a fable, but notes that the natives affirm they have seen it, attributing it to the devil's deception. The account illustrates early colonial documentation of the creature and the Spanish perception of indigenous beliefs.

The Manananggal and the Stingray WhipPhilippine folklore (oral tradition)

Unknown

A manananggal is said to avoid the tail of a stingray, which can be fashioned into a whip. This suggests a specific method of defense or attack against the creature. The stingray tail is a known apotropaic item.
