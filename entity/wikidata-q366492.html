Form

There is no attested description of Anamelech's appearance. No iconography, cult statue, or visual representation survives. As a result, any depiction in modern media is anachronistic and purely speculative. The only indirect clue is the name's association with the god Anu, who was not typically depicted in anthropomorphic form but was symbolized by a horned cap or a star.

Powers

Behavior & Habitat

The only behavior attributed to Anamelech in the primary source is the acceptance of child sacrifice. The biblical text states that the Sepharvites 'burned their children in the fire as sacrifices to Adrammelech and Anamelech'. This is a cultic action performed by worshippers, not a narrative of divine behavior. The deity's own will, desires, or actions are not described.

Accounts

After the Assyrian conquest of the Northern Kingdom of Israel, the Assyrian king (likely Sargon II) resettled the land of Samaria with peoples from various conquered territories, including Babylon, Cuthah, Avva, Hamath, and Sepharvaim. The Sepharvites brought with them their own gods, Adrammelech and Anamelech, and practiced child sacrifice in their honor. The biblical narrative frames this as a failure to worship YHWH, leading to divine punishment. The text does not provide a narrative of Anamelech's own deeds, only the cultic practice of his worshippers.

The biblical writer pairs Anamelech with Adrammelech as the two gods of the Sepharvites. Because the name 'Anamelech' clearly invokes Anu (the great sky-god of Mesopotamian cosmology) while 'Adrammelech' resembles 'Adar-' or 'Addu-' (storm/king elements), generations of scholars have tried to ground the pair in the real cult of the named city of Sepharvaim. Sepharvaim is widely identified with the Babylonian twin city Sippar, which had two famous temples: Ebabbar, the sanctuary of the sun-god Shamash, and E-ulmash, sacred to the warrior-goddess Anunit. On this reading, Adrammelech and Anamelech have been variously explained as the deified sun and moon, or as local forms of Adad and Anu. None of these identifications is conclusive, and the pair may instead preserve a garbled memory of two genuine Babylonian deities whose precise names the biblical writer and later readers no longer recognized.

What We Don't Know

1. No extra-biblical evidence for Anamelech exists. 2. The exact nature of the deity (distinct god vs. epithet) remains uncertain. 3. The historicity of the child sacrifice practice is debated. 4. No iconography or cultic objects have been identified. 5. The relationship between Anamelech and Adrammelech is unknown. 6. The specific Sepharvite theology and mythology of Anamelech is entirely lost.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Anamelech as a god known to us only through a 'wanted poster' written by his enemies. The Bible is the prosecution's exhibit, not a neutral biography. He's a historical figure whose entire identity is filtered through a single, terrifying accusation. He's the ultimate 'lost' god — a name, a cult, a practice, and a mystery, all wrapped in the smoke of a fire we can no longer see. If you want to understand him, you have to read between the lines of a text that wants to erase him.
