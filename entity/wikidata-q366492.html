Who It Is

In the shadow of the Assyrian empire, a people uprooted from their home in the city of Sippar carried their most terrifying god across the desert to a new land. They called him Anamelech — 'Anu is King' — and they believed he demanded the ultimate price: the burning of their own children. We know this dark figure only through the hostile words of his enemies, a single verse in a biblical book that condemns him to infamy. He is a god of smoke and ashes, a deity whose entire cult was reduced to a warning, a name whispered in horror across millennia. Anamelech is the ghost of a faith we can never fully recover, a divine king whose throne is a funeral pyre.

The only behavior attributed to Anamelech in the primary source is the acceptance of child sacrifice. The biblical text states that the Sepharvites 'burned their children in the fire as sacrifices to Adrammelech and Anamelech'. This is a cultic action performed by worshippers, not a narrative of divine behavior. The deity's own will, desires, or actions are not described.

Powers
Acceptance of Sacrifice

The primary 'power' attributed to Anamelech is the reception of child sacrifice. The Sepharvites believed that burning their children in fire would appease or gain favor from this deity. This is the only attested power.

Limit: Unknown. The text does not describe any limits to the deity's power.

Astral Association (Saturn)

In later rabbinic interpretation (Targum Jonathan), Anamelech was identified with the planet Saturn. This may imply astral powers, but this is a post-biblical reinterpretation.

Limit: This is an interpretive gloss, not an attested contemporary belief.

Form & Signs

Appearance — There is no attested description of Anamelech's appearance. No iconography, cult statue, or visual representation survives. As a result, any depiction in modern media is anachronistic and purely speculative. The only indirect clue is the name's association with the god Anu, who was not typically depicted in anthropomorphic form but was symbolized by a horned cap or a star.

Symbols — No symbols are securely attested. The name itself, invoking Anu, might imply the symbol of the horned crown or the star of Ishtar, but this is speculative. The later rabbinic association with Saturn may have carried astral symbolism, but again, this is not contemporary.

Domains — Anamelech is explicitly identified as a god of the city of Sepharvaim (Sippar). The domain is therefore civic and protective. The association with the planet Saturn (in Targum Jonathan) suggests an astral domain, though this is a later interpretive layer, not a contemporary attestation.

Accounts I
The Resettlement of Samaria and the Worship of Sepharvaim2 Kings 17:24-41

c. 720 BCE (events); c. 6th century BCE (composition)

After the Assyrian conquest of the Northern Kingdom of Israel, the Assyrian king (likely Sargon II) resettled the land of Samaria with peoples from various conquered territories, including Babylon, Cuthah, Avva, Hamath, and Sepharvaim. The Sepharvites brought with them their own gods, Adrammelech and Anamelech, and practiced child sacrifice in their honor. The biblical narrative frames this as a failure to worship YHWH, leading to divine punishment. The text does not provide a narrative of Anamelech's own deeds, only the cultic practice of his worshippers. The biblical text states that the new settlers were attacked by lions as divine punishment, leading them to request a priest of YHWH to teach them the proper worship of the land's god. The narrative serves as an etiological explanation for the syncretic religious practices of the Samaritans.

Accounts II
The Two Gods of Sepharvaim: Sun and Moon Over Sippar2 Kings 17:31; scholarly identifications debated by Assyriologists (e.g., connections to Sippar's twin sanctuaries of Shamash and Anunit)

Events c. 720 BCE; identification scholarship ranging from antiquity to the 20th century

The biblical writer pairs Anamelech with Adrammelech as the two gods of the Sepharvites. Because the name 'Anamelech' clearly invokes Anu (the great sky-god of Mesopotamian cosmology) while 'Adrammelech' resembles 'Adar-' or 'Addu-' (storm/king elements), generations of scholars have tried to ground the pair in the real cult of the named city of Sepharvaim. Sepharvaim is widely identified with the Babylonian twin city Sippar, which had two famous temples: Ebabbar, the sanctuary of the sun-god Shamash, and E-ulmash, sacred to the warrior-goddess Anunit. On this reading, Adrammelech and Anamelech have been variously explained as the deified sun and moon, or as local forms of Adad and Anu. None of these identifications is conclusive, and the pair may instead preserve a garbled memory of two genuine Babylonian deities whose precise names the biblical writer and later readers no longer recognized. Anamelech is a demon. — In the primary source (2 Kings), Anamelech is a god worshipped by the Sepharvites. The demonic classification is a later Christian reinterpretation.

Anamelech is the planet Saturn. — This is a rabbinic interpretation (Targum Jonathan), not a contemporary ancient Near Eastern belief. The etymology suggests a connection to the god Anu, not Saturn.

Anamelech and Adrammelech are the same deity. — They are clearly listed as two distinct gods of Sepharvaim in 2 Kings 17:31.

Anamelech is a Canaanite god. — The biblical text specifically associates Anamelech with Sepharvaim, a city in Mesopotamia (Sippar). While there may be West Semitic influences (the 'Melech' element), the primary cultural context is Syro-Mesopotamian.
