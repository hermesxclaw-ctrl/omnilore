The Approach

In the beginning, from the very body of the creator god Sanamahi, a divine horse burst forth—not to serve, but to challenge the serpent lord Pakhangba. This is Samaton, the winged steed of Meitei mythology, whose hoofbeats echo through the rituals of the Lai Haraoba and whose lineage is said to run in the veins of every Manipuri pony. Here is his story, as told in ancient chronicles and sung in living tradition.

Content advisory. None
Identity

Samaton

Pronunciation /saa-ma-ton/ or /saa-ma-don/

Known As

SamadonShamadon AyangbaShamaton Ayangpa

Etymology

The name Samaton (also Samadon) likely derives from Meitei roots, but its precise meaning is not clearly established. The variant 'Ayangba' or 'Ayangpa' may relate to a specific epithet or quality, possibly indicating 'swift' or 'unbroken'.

Cultural Ownership

Meitei (Manipuri) culture, Northeast India

Disputed Identifications

Some sources may confuse Samaton with other divine horses in South Asian mythology; however, he is distinctly a Meitei figure. The relationship to the Manipuri pony is legendary, not scientifically confirmed.

Description

Appearance

Samaton is described as a winged divine horse, though specific physical details (color, size, wing structure) are not provided in available sources. He is likely depicted as majestic and supernatural.

Symbols & Iconography

Samaton is a symbol of royalty and divine power. He is associated with the Manipuri pony, and his image may be used in heraldry or royal insignia, though specific examples are not documented in this dossier.

Behaviour & Personality

Initially created to attack Pakhangba, Samaton is depicted as a powerful and formidable creature. Later, he becomes the mount of God Marjing, indicating a shift to a more noble and servile role. He is revered as an ancestor of the Manipuri pony, implying a protective or generative nature.

Domains & Habitats

Samaton is a celestial or divine being, associated with the cosmos in Meitei cosmology. He is not tied to a specific earthly habitat, but his descendants are the Manipuri ponies of the Manipur region.

Version Differences

The name varies between Samaton and Samadon, and the epithet Ayangba/Ayangpa, but these are considered free variations in the Meitei language. No major narrative differences are noted in available sources.
