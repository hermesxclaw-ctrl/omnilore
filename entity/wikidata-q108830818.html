Who It Is

Karma is not a cosmic scoreboard waiting to punish you — it is an ancient Indian theory that every intentional act plants a seed inside you, and that seed will fruit, perhaps today, perhaps in a life you have not yet lived. From Upanishadic sages to Jain philosophers who imagined karma as invisible dust clinging to the soul, to Buddhists who located it entirely in intention, the idea has shaped how a third of humanity understands responsibility, suffering, and freedom.

Described as autonomous, regular, and non-theistic in most classical formulations: 'the law of karma operates independent of any deity or divine judgment' (Reichenbach). It is not vengeful or merciful; it is causal like a natural law, but ethical — intentional good acts tend to produce pleasant results, intentional bad acts unpleasant results, for the actor. It is cumulative, habitual, and self-reinforcing: Potter and Coward note karma seeds habits (vāsanā) which shape perception and future choices. Powers
Ethical Causation and Phala Generation

Core power: intentional actions produce corresponding pleasant or unpleasant fruits (phala) for the agent, either visibly in this life or invisibly across lives. Good intent and good deeds yield punya (merit) and sukha; bad intent/deeds yield pāpa (demerit) and duḥkha.

Limit: Not mechanical one-to-one; fruition depends on conditions, intensity, repetition, and counteracting karma. Unintentional or disinterested acts have reduced or neutral effect (Buddhism). Does not guarantee immediate result; may ripen lifetimes later.

Saṃskāra and Vāsanā Formation (Habit and Perception Shaping)

Every karma leaves an invisible residue (saṃskāra) and latent tendency (vāsanā) that transforms the actor's dispositions, perceptual habits, and character. Potter and Coward describe this as psychology of habit: repeated karma creates nature.

Limit: Residues can be weakened by contrary practice, insight, or asceticism; not indelible. Requires sustained effort to break.

Rebirth and Gati Determination

Karma determines the nature, realm, and circumstances of future births — human, animal, hungry ghost, hell-being, or deity — and the quality of that life (longevity, health, status in classical texts).

Limit: In traditions that decouple karma and rebirth (some early Vedic and modern reinterpretations, and some scriptures noted as having no link), this function is denied. Even where affirmed, other factors like divine grace (Sikhism) or prārabdha exhaustion limit its sole determinacy.

Jain Material Obstruction - Eightfold Karmic Veiling

In Jainism karma as matter directly obstructs soul's qualities: knowledge-obscuring (jñānāvaraṇa), perception-obscuring (darśanāvaraṇa), feeling-producing (vedanīya), deluding (mohanīya), lifespan-determining (āyu), body-determining (nāma), status-determining (gotra), and power-obstructing (antarāya).

Limit: Only shed through saṃvara (stoppage) and nirjarā (shedding) via ethical restraint, austerity, and meditation; cannot be removed by ritual alone.

Merit Transfer and Ritual Efficacy (Puṇya-Pariṇāmanā)

Generated merit (puṇya) can be dedicated or transferred to others — ancestors, all beings, or deities — influencing their karmic condition. Underlies śrāddha, dāna, and Mahayana bodhisattva ideal of transferring merit for universal liberation.

Limit: Theravada restricts transfer to rejoicing in merit; not all schools accept efficacy. Cannot transfer demerit unwillingly.

Liberation Conditioning (Mokṣa/Nirvāṇa Gate)

Karma both binds and, when understood and transcended, provides the path to liberation. Niṣkāma karma (action without attachment to fruit) and cessation of craving (tṛṣṇā) stop new karma formation, allowing exhaustion of old karma and exit from saṃsāra.

Limit: Requires sustained ethical, meditative, and wisdom training; mere intellectual understanding does not exhaust prārabdha. Jainism requires literal shedding of all particles.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Karma has no anthropomorphic form in classical doctrine; it is an impersonal principle. When visualized, traditions use metaphors rather than iconography: Vedic and Upanishadic texts speak of seed (bīja) and fruit (phala); Hindu dharmaśāstra speaks of an unseen residue (adṛṣṭa) and ledger; Buddhism speaks of cetanā (volition) and saṃskāra (formations) as mental imprints; Jainism uniquely describes karma as extremely subtle matter — sticky, dusty

Symbols — Core symbols: (1) Bīja-phala (seed-fruit) — most pan-Indian; (2) Cakra / Saṃsāra-cakra (wheel of rebirth) — Buddhist Bhavacakra shows karma as the driving force between lives; (3) Ledger/account book (lekha, bahī) — prominent in Sikh and North Indian folk imagery with Chitragupta as scribe; (4) Threads/bonds (bandha) and dust/particles — Jain painting; (5) Vāsanā/saṃskāra as furrows or perfume residue — psychological metaphor in Vedānta and Yoga.

Domains — The domain of karma is saṃsāra — the cycle of birth, death and rebirth across human, animal, divine, hellish and other gatis. Its field includes the mental continuum (citta-santāna), the subtle body (liṅga-śarīra) or jīva in Jainism, and the social-moral world. Accounts I
Yājñavalkya Teaches Artabhāga That Karma Determines Death and RebirthBṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 3.2.13 and 4.4.1-6

c. 700-600 BCE

At King Janaka's court, sage Yājñavalkya is questioned by Ārtabhāga: what happens when a person dies and speech, breath and mind dissolve? Yājñavalkya takes him aside and whispers that it is karma. What remains is karman: 'a person becomes good by good action, bad by bad action.' He then elaborates the famous sequence — desire leads to resolve, resolve to action, action to attainment — establishing karma as the continuity between lives. The esoteric whisper signals that karma was once secret knowledge. Earliest surviving Indian articulation of ethicized karma and rebirth linkage; foundation for all later Hindu, Buddhist and Jain theories. Marks shift from ritual-action karma to moral-intentional karma.

Kṛṣṇa's Instruction on Niṣkāma Karma to Arjuna on KurukṣetraBhagavad Gītā, Chapters 2-3, 18

c. 200 BCE - 200 CE (textual fixation)

Arjuna collapses on the battlefield, unwilling to kill his kin. Kṛṣṇa reveals that action is unavoidable — even inaction is action — but bondage comes not from action itself but from attachment to its fruit. He teaches niṣkāma karma: 'Your right is to the work alone, never to its fruits' (2.47). By acting in accordance with dharma without craving, one generates no binding karma. Kṛṣṇa also classifies karma as deep and mysterious (gahanā karmaṇo gatiḥ) and distinguishes karma, vikarma and akarma. Canonical solution to the tension between duty and liberation; legitimizes active life in the world; systematized later into the fourfold karma taxonomy (sañcita/prārabdha/āgāmi/kriyamāṇa) in Vedānta commentaries.

Aṅgulimāla — The Garland of Fingers Whose Karma Is Cut ShortMajjhima Nikāya 86 Aṅgulimāla Sutta; also Dhammapada commentary

c. 3rd century BCE - 1st century CE oral tradition

Aṅgulimāla, a Brahmin student turned serial killer who wears a necklace of fingers, is about to kill his mother when the Buddha intercepts him. Unable to outrun the calmly walking Buddha, he converts, becomes a monk, and endures villagers' stones and abuse as the ripening of his vast bad karma, but because his intention has transformed, he does not generate new karma and attains arahantship in this life. The Buddha tells him he is experiencing in this life the hell-result that would have ripened for eons. Illustrates Buddhist principles that (1) karma is intention (cetanā), (2) present transformation can alter fruition, (3) even heinous karma is not fatalistic, and (4) arahants experience residual phala without new bondage. Frequently cited against victim-blaming readings.

Accounts II
Mahāvīra Endures the Ripening of Past Karma Through Ascetic EnduranceKalpa Sūtra, Bhagavatī Sūtra (Viyāhapaṇṇatti), Ācārāṅga Sūtra

c. 3rd century BCE - 5th century CE compilation; events set c. 6th century BCE

Mahāvīra, the 24th Tīrthaṅkara, voluntarily undertakes twelve years of extreme asceticism before omniscience. Jain narratives stress he is not suffering arbitrarily but deliberately allowing (udīraṇā) and burning off (nirjarā) dense, long-accumulated karma particles through fasting, silence, exposure, and non-retaliation when beaten and insulted. His endurance demonstrates saṃvara (stopping influx) and nirjarā (shedding) — the Jain technology for dismantling material karma. Exemplifies Jain material karma theory and the path to kevala-jñāna (omniscience) and mokṣa; establishes asceticism as karmic physics, not mere self-punishment. Contrasts with Buddhist middle way.

Vessantara Jātaka — The Prince Who Gives Away Everything to Perfect GenerosityVessantara Jātaka (Jātaka 547), Pali Jātaka collection; also Cariyāpiṭaka

c. 3rd century BCE - 5th century CE (verses early, prose later)

In his penultimate birth before becoming the Buddha, Prince Vessantara practices the perfection of giving (dāna-pāramitā) by giving away his kingdom's auspicious white elephant, then his children and finally his wife. Each gift generates immense punya but also brings exile and suffering, testing whether generosity is tainted by attachment. The narrative shows karma as merit-making that ripens as future Buddhahood, while also warning that even meritorious acts have complex fruits. Most popular Jātaka in Southeast Asia; performed ritually to generate merit; illustrates Buddhist karma as intentional perfection leading across lifetimes to awakening; raises ethical debate about using family as gifts.

Guru Nānak and the Ledger of Karma Before the Divine CourtGuru Granth Sahib - Japji Sahib, Āsā kī Vār, Sukhmani Sahib; Janamsākhī hagiographies

15th-17th century compositions; Janamsākhīs 16th-18th century

Guru Nānak teaches that every being carries a lekha (account) of karam written by divine order (hukam), read in Dharam Khand by Dharam Rāja. Yet he subverts fatalism: 'karamī āvai kapṛā nadarī mokh duār' — by karma the body is obtained, but by grace (nadar) the gate of liberation opens. In Janamsākhī stories, Nānak confronts ritualists who believe pilgrimage automatically washes karma, insisting that inner remembrance of the Name (nām) and compassionate action, not mere ritual, transform the account. Karma is real but not sovereign; grace is sovereign. Establishes Sikh distinctiveness: accepts karma/saṃsāra but rejects caste-based karmic determinism and ritual expiation alone; centers ethics and devotion. Influences Sikh social reform against caste discrimination.

Karma means fate, destiny, or predetermination — that everything is predetermined and you cannot change it. — Classical karma is not fatalism. Prārabdha is only the ripening portion of past karma that conditions present circumstances; kriyamāṇa/āgāmi karma is present agency that shapes future. Texts like Yoga Vāsiṣṭha and Bhagavad Gītā explicitly contrast daiva (fate) with puruṣakāra (human effort) and prioritize effort. Karma explains conditions, not that you lack choice in responding.

Karma is instant cosmic payback — if you do something bad, the universe will immediately punish you, and if you do good, you will be immediately rewarded. — Classical theories stress delay and conditionality: phala may ripen in this life, next life, or much later, depending on intensity, repetition, and counteracting factors. 'Instant karma' is a modern pop phrase (popularized by John Lennon 1970) with no classical basis. The Buddha lists karma as only one of many conditions (niyāmas).

Karma is a system of divine punishment and reward enforced by God or the universe keeping score. — Most classical formulations describe karma as an impersonal, autonomous causal regularity, not a deity's judgment. Even theistic schools that posit Īśvara as regulator distinguish karma as law from divine whim. Halbfass and Reichenbach emphasize karma operates independent of divine judgment.

Bad things happening to someone prove they deserved it due to bad karma, so compassion or help is unnecessary. — This victim-blaming reading is explicitly countered in canonical sources: the Buddha taught that not all suffering is karmic (SN 36.21 lists eight causes), and compassion (karuṇā) is obligatory regardless of cause. Jain and Hindu ethics also enjoin aid irrespective of karmic origin. Using karma to justify indifference is a folk misuse, critiqued by Ambedkar and engaged Buddhists.

Karma is exclusively Buddhist or exclusively Hindu, or is the same in all Eastern religions. — Karma is pan-Indian with distinct systematizations: Hindu (ātman carrier, fourfold taxonomy), Buddhist (no-self, intention-only), Jain (material particles, eight types), Sikh (karma subordinate to grace), Taoist/Chinese (merged with ganying). Treating them as identical erases major doctrinal differences.

You can erase all bad karma by a single ritual, donation, or pilgrimage, or that good karma automatically cancels bad karma like a bank balance. — Texts are nuanced: some rituals generate punya, but they do not simply delete pāpa one-to-one; Jainism requires actual shedding through asceticism and cessation; Buddhism requires transformation of intention and wisdom; Vedānta debates whether knowledge burns sañcita but prārabdha must still be lived out. Merit transfer is contested and not a simple arithmetic offset.
