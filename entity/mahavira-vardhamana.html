Who It Is

In the swirling dust of ancient India, a prince walked away from his kingdom, tore off his silks, and began a twelve-year silence so profound that legends say even the gods leaned in to listen. His name was Vardhamāna, but the world would come to know him as Mahāvīra — the Great Hero — the last ford-maker of this age, whose radical insistence on non-violence would outlast empires.

Traditional accounts describe Mahāvīra as extraordinarily disciplined, silent for long periods, indifferent to physical discomfort, and deeply compassionate towards all living beings. He was known for his rigorous fasting and his practice of 'vīrya' (heroic effort) in enduring hardships. He was also a skilled teacher, able to explain complex philosophical points with clarity.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Mahāvīra is Sanskrit for 'Great Hero'. Vardhamāna means 'increasing' or 'prospering', likely a birth name. Jñātṛputra means 'son of the Jñātṛs' (the clan). Nigaṇṭha Nātaputta (Pali) means 'the fetterless one of the Nāya clan'.

Pronunciation — mə-HAH-vee-ruh (Sanskrit: [mɐˈɦaːʋiːɾɐ])

Aliases — Vardhamāna, Vesālie, Jñātṛputra, Niganṭha Nātaputta, Sanmatī, Mahāvīra

Ownership — Jainism (both Śvetāmbara and Digambara sects); also referenced in Buddhist and Hindu texts as a contemporary of the Buddha.

Powers
Kevala Jñāna (Omniscience)

The state of absolute, unlimited knowledge of all substances and their modifications, past, present, and future.

Limit: Attained only after 12.5 years of intense asceticism; cannot be communicated fully in words.

Anekāntavāda (Many-sidedness)

The philosophical doctrine that reality is complex and can be viewed from multiple perspectives; this is not a supernatural power but a cognitive approach.

Limit: Requires rigorous logical training.

Miraculous powers (ṛddhis)

Some hagiographies attribute to him the ability to fly, read minds, and perform other supernormal feats, but these are often downplayed in favor of his teachings.

Limit: These powers are not central to his path; they arise from spiritual purity.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In iconography, Mahāvīra is typically depicted as a serene ascetic, either seated in meditation (padmāsana) or standing in the kāyotsarga posture (abandonment of the body), with a lion emblem beneath him. He is shown with a shaved head, no clothing (in Digambara tradition), and often with a golden or dark complexion depending on the tradition. His eyes are half-closed in deep contemplation. In some depictions, he has a halo and a serene smile.

Symbols — The lion is his distinguishing symbol. His attendant spirits (yakṣa and yakṣī) are Matanga and Siddhayikā (Śvetāmbara) or Gomedha and Ambikā (Digambara). His complexion is often golden in Śvetāmbara art, and his posture is usually meditative.

Domains — He wandered the Gangetic plains of ancient India, particularly in the regions of modern Bihar and Uttar Pradesh. He is associated with royal courts and ascetic groves, and his sermons were delivered in village squares and royal assemblies.

Accounts I
The Birth and Early LifeKalpa Sūtra, Ācārāṅga Sūtra

Traditional date: 599 BCE

Mahāvīra was born to King Siddhārtha and Queen Triśalā (also called Videhadattā) in Kundagrama. His birth was preceded by auspicious dreams. He was named Vardhamāna because the kingdom prospered after his conception. As a prince, he lived a luxurious life, married (in Śvetāmbara tradition) and had a daughter, but at age 30 he renounced the world. His renunciation set the stage for his ascetic career.

The Twelve and a Half Years of AsceticismĀcārāṅga Sūtra, Kalpa Sūtra

c. 569–557 BCE (traditional)

He wandered naked and silent, enduring abuse from villagers, sleeping on rough ground, fasting for months, and meditating in cemeteries. He bore all hardships without attachment. He attained Kevala Jñāna under a Sāla tree on the bank of the Rijupālika river.

Accounts II
The First Sermon and the Founding of the SanghaJain Agamas

After enlightenment

His first sermon was delivered at Pāvā to the eleven Brahmin scholars who became his chief disciples (gaṇadharas), led by Indrabhūti Gautama. They accepted his teachings and established the fourfold order of monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen. The Jain community was formally organized.

The Nirvana at PāvāKalpa Sūtra, Uttarapurāṇa

Traditional: 527 BCE or 510 BCE

At age 72, after 30 years of teaching, Mahāvīra attained moksha (liberation) at Pāvāpurī. His soul is said to have ascended to the abode of the liberated souls (siddhaloka). His death is commemorated as Diwali by Jains.

Mahāvīra was the founder of Jainism. — He was the 24th Tirthankara and revived an existing tradition; Pārśvanātha and others preceded him.

Mahāvīra and the Buddha were contemporaries and often debated. — They were contemporaries, but there is no historical record of a direct meeting.

Jainism is an offshoot of Hinduism. — Jainism is an independent religion with ancient roots, though it shares some cultural context.

Mahāvīra was a prince who gave up a life of luxury. — This is true in traditional accounts, but some scholars question the royal status, suggesting he was a clan leader.
