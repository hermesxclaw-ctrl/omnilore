Etymology

Mahāvīra is Sanskrit for 'Great Hero'. Vardhamāna means 'increasing' or 'prospering', likely a birth name. Jñātṛputra means 'son of the Jñātṛs' (the clan). Nigaṇṭha Nātaputta (Pali) means 'the fetterless one of the Nāya clan'.

Cultural Ownership

Jainism (both Śvetāmbara and Digambara sects); also referenced in Buddhist and Hindu texts as a contemporary of the Buddha.

Disputed Identifications

Some early scholars conflated Mahāvīra with the Buddha or with a mythical figure; but modern scholarship accepts his historicity. The exact dates of his life are disputed, with traditional dates (599–527 BCE) vs. scholarly revisions (c. 549–477 BCE or c. 499–427 BCE).

Archive Dossier — Approach

In the swirling dust of ancient India, a prince walked away from his kingdom, tore off his silks, and began a twelve-year silence so profound that legends say even the gods leaned in to listen. His name was Vardhamāna, but the world would come to know him as Mahāvīra — the Great Hero — the last ford-maker of this age, whose radical insistence on non-violence would outlast empires.

Who It Is

Mahāvīra ('Great Hero') was the 24th and last Tīrthaṅkara ('Ford-Maker') of Jainism, the historical founder-figure of the Jain tradition as it exists today. He was born Prince Vardhamāna to King Siddhartha and Queen Trishala in Kundagrama (near modern Vaishali, Bihar). Renouncing the world at age 30, he undertook 12 years of extreme asceticism, meditation, and silence, enduring abuse from villagers and wild animals alike, until he attained kevala jñāna (omniscience) under a Sala tree on the banks of the Rijupaluka River. For the next 30 years, he preached the path of asceticism, non-violence (ahiṃsā), non-attachment, and the principle of multiple viewpoints (anekāntavāda). He organized his followers into a fourfold order of monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen. Mahāvīra's teachings form the core of Jain philosophy: the eternal nature of the soul (jīva), the doctrine of karma as a physical substance that clings to the soul, the necessity of severe asceticism to burn off karma, and the goal of liberation (moksha) through the three jewels of right faith, right knowledge, and right conduct. He attained nirvana (final liberation) at Pavapuri at age 72. Unlike the Buddha, Mahāvīra was not the founder of a new tradition but the last in a long lineage of Tīrthaṅkaras stretching back to Ādinātha (Rishabhanatha).

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

In iconography, Mahāvīra is typically depicted as a serene ascetic, either seated in meditation (padmāsana) or standing in the kāyotsarga posture (abandonment of the body), with a lion emblem beneath him. He is shown with a shaved head, no clothing (in Digambara tradition), and often with a golden or dark complexion depending on the tradition. His eyes are half-closed in deep contemplation. In some depictions, he has a halo and a serene smile.

Symbols & Iconography

The lion is his distinguishing symbol. His attendant spirits (yakṣa and yakṣī) are Matanga and Siddhayikā (Śvetāmbara) or Gomedha and Ambikā (Digambara). His complexion is often golden in Śvetāmbara art, and his posture is usually meditative.

Behaviour & Personality

Traditional accounts describe Mahāvīra as extraordinarily disciplined, silent for long periods, indifferent to physical discomfort, and deeply compassionate towards all living beings. He was known for his rigorous fasting and his practice of 'vīrya' (heroic effort) in enduring hardships. He was also a skilled teacher, able to explain complex philosophical points with clarity.

Domains & Habitats

He wandered the Gangetic plains of ancient India, particularly in the regions of modern Bihar and Uttar Pradesh. He is associated with royal courts and ascetic groves, and his sermons were delivered in village squares and royal assemblies.

Version Differences

Śvetāmbara tradition holds that he was born to a royal family and was married with a daughter; Digambara tradition asserts he never married and renounced the world at age 30. Śvetāmbara iconography often shows him with a simple loincloth, while Digambara images show him nude. The dates of his nirvana differ: 527 BCE (Śvetāmbara) vs. 510 BCE (Digambara). The location of his early life also differs: Kundagrama is identified by some as Basu Kund, by others as a suburb of Vaishali.

Origins

Mahāvīra was born as the son of King Siddhartha and Queen Trishala. His mother had 14 auspicious dreams before his birth, foretelling the birth of a Tīrthaṅkara. He was married to Princess Yashoda and had a daughter (whose name varies in texts). At 30, he renounced the world, tore out his hair in five handfuls (a ritual renunciation), and began his ascetic journey.

Archive Dossier — Powers

Kevala Jñāna (Omniscience)

Source Version All Jain traditions

Description

The state of absolute, unlimited knowledge of all substances and their modifications, past, present, and future.

Limitations

Attained only after 12.5 years of intense asceticism; cannot be communicated fully in words.

Contradictions

None; universally accepted in Jainism.

Anekāntavāda (Many-sidedness)

Source Version All Jain traditions

Description

The philosophical doctrine that reality is complex and can be viewed from multiple perspectives; this is not a supernatural power but a cognitive approach.

Limitations

Requires rigorous logical training.

Contradictions

None.

Miraculous powers (ṛddhis)

Source Version Later hagiographical texts

Description

Some hagiographies attribute to him the ability to fly, read minds, and perform other supernormal feats, but these are often downplayed in favor of his teachings.

Limitations

These powers are not central to his path; they arise from spiritual purity.

Contradictions

Not emphasized in early texts like Ācārāṅga Sūtra.

Counterparts Across Cultures

Beings of the same nature, imagined by other peoples.

Archive Dossier — Relationships

Pārśvanātha

Type predecessorSource Jain tradition

Description

The 23rd Tirthankara, whose teachings Mahāvīra is said to have revived and reformed.

Version Note

Jains believe Pārśvanātha lived 273 years before Mahāvīra, but some scholars suggest they were closer in time.

Conflicting Accounts

Scholarly debate on the historical gap between them.

Indrabhūti Gautama

Type chief discipleSource Jain tradition

Description

The first gaṇadhara, who compiled Mahāvīra's teachings into the Agamas.

Version Note

Gautama is said to have attained Kevala Jñāna on the night of Mahāvīra's nirvana.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

King Bimbisāra

Type contemporary rulerSource Buddhist and Jain texts

Description

King of Magadha, who is said to have met Mahāvīra and the Buddha.

Version Note

Bimbisāra is mentioned as a patron of both.

Conflicting Accounts

Some texts place Mahāvīra in his court, but details vary.

Gautama Buddha

Type contemporarySource Buddhist texts

Description

Founder of Buddhism, who lived in the same region and era.

Version Note

Buddhist texts mention Nigaṇṭha Nātaputta as a rival teacher.

Conflicting Accounts

There is no record of a direct meeting; some later texts invent debates.

Triśalā

Queen, who had auspicious dreams before his birth.

Version Note

Also called Videhadattā.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Siddhārtha

King of Kundagrama, of the Nāya clan.
