Who It Is

In the dry heart of Kamilaroi Country, where riverbeds can lie bare for months and the horizon shimmers with heat, people once watched the sky for a particular kind of cloud—a sign that the great eagle-woman was passing. Birrahgnooloo, they said, was the reason the land still lived. Baiame had shaped the world and withdrawn to his camp in the sky, but without her rain the rivers would shrink to dust and the grasslands would die. When storms rolled in and waterholes brimmed, it was her wings that had gathered the clouds, her body that flowed in the newly filled channels. She was not a distant goddess carved in stone; she was the living marshes of the Macquarie, the fertility of women, the quiet assurance that life would continue if people kept faith with Country. Powers
Gift of rain

Birrahgnooloo is repeatedly described as the being who brings rain to the dry Kamilaroi country. In narratives recorded from the Narran Lake region, she takes the form of a wedge-tailed eagle and sends storms that fill waterholes and turn brown earth green. Academic dictionary entries note that she can send floods if properly asked, implying a ritual relationship in which human communities petition her for water in times of drought. This power is not merely meteorological; it is cosmological, linking the moral and ritual order of the people to the life-sustaining cycles of rainfall and river flow.

Limit: Her gift of rain is conditioned by proper ritual respect and by the broader law established by Baiame. The phrase “if properly asked” suggests that floods or abundant rain are not automatic but depend on correct ceremonial practice and moral behavior. There is no evidence that she can arbitrarily control weather anywhere on earth; her power is localized to her Country and its climatic patterns. Colonial disruption of ceremonial life likely affected the continuity of such ritual relationships, but this is a historical inference rather than a directly attested limitation.

Fertility of women and giver of children

Birrahgnooloo is widely characterized as “The Mother of All” and “The Giver of Children.” She makes women fertile, ensures conception, and sustains the continuity of human communities. In this role she is not merely a symbolic mother; she is an active ancestral power whose presence is invoked in relation to pregnancy, childbirth, and the health of families. Her association with rivers and waterholes reinforces the link between flowing water and reproductive fluids, between the filling of the land and the filling of the womb.

Limit: Her fertility power is framed within the social and ceremonial structures of Aboriginal communities. It is not described as a transactional, individualistic “granting of children” in exchange for offerings, as in some other mythologies; rather, it is embedded in the ongoing relationship between people and Country. There is no detailed ritual manual specifying how individuals petition her for children, and any such practices may be restricted knowledge. Modern medical and social changes have altered how fertility is understood, but these changes do not negate her cosmological role.

Embodiment of rivers, marshes, and earth

Birrahgnooloo is closely associated with the Macquarie Marshes and the rivers of central New South Wales, which are described in modern summaries as part of her body. This embodiment means that she is not simply a spirit who controls water; she is the living form of the waterways themselves. The filling and drying of rivers, the seasonal expansion and contraction of marshes, and the fertility of the surrounding earth are all expressions of her being. In this sense, she functions as a localized earth-and-water deity whose body is the landscape.

Limit: Her embodiment is geographically specific: it applies to particular rivers and marshes within Kamilaroi and Wiradjuri Country, not to all water everywhere. While comparative scholars sometimes speak of “Mother Earth” figures, Birrahgnooloo’s embodiment should not be generalized beyond her region. Furthermore, the concept of embodiment is part of the Dreaming, where time and space are non-linear; it should not be reduced to a simple metaphor or to a literalist claim that every physical change in the marshes is directly caused by her conscious decision.

Participation in mythic danger and sanctification of sacred sites

In a narrative recorded by K. Langloh Parker from the Narran Lake traditions, Birrahgnooloo and Baiame’s second wife go fishing during Baiame’s absence. A Kurrea, a great crocodile-like creature, rises from the depths of the lake and swallows both women. When Baiame returns and finds them gone, he tracks the Kurrea to Narran Lake, kills it with his power, and splits its body open to free his wives. The lake where the Kurrea dies becomes sacred ground. In this story, Birrahgnooloo’s power is not expressed through direct combat but through her participation in a dangerous event that leads to the sanctification of a place. Her vulnerability and rescue contribute to the moral and spatial ordering of Country.

Limit: This narrative does not present Birrahgnooloo as a warrior or monster-slayer; her role is primarily as victim and rescued wife. Her power here is relational and cosmological rather than martial. The story is specific to Narran Lake and should not be generalized as a universal pattern for all sacred sites.

Birrahgnooloo as rain-bringer of Kamilaroi CountryNarratives recorded by K. Langloh Parker among Kamilaroi (Gamilaraay) people in the late nineteenth century, as summarized in modern mythological compilations.

Late nineteenth-century recording of older oral traditions; the myth itself belongs to the Dreaming, which is considered timeless and ongoing.

In semi-arid northern New South Wales, the Kamilaroi people tell of Birrahgnooloo, wife of Baiame and mother of Daramulum, who takes the form of a wedge-tailed eagle. After Baiame has shaped the world and withdrawn to his sky camp, the land he leaves behind would wither without rain. Birrahgnooloo flies over the dry plains, her wings gathering clouds and her passage calling storms. Where she passes, rivers fill, waterholes brim, and brown earth turns green. People understand that without her, the world Baiame created would die. Her gift of rain is not random: it responds to the moral and ceremonial life of the people, who know that proper respect and ritual are necessary to sustain the relationship. In this story, Birrahgnooloo is not a distant goddess but the reason the land still lives, a presence felt whenever rain breaks a long drought. The story establishes Birrahgnooloo as the indispensable complement to Baiame’s creative act: without her rain, his world would wither. The Kurrea of Narran LakeMyth recorded by K. Langloh Parker from Narran Lake traditions and summarized in modern mythological compilations.

Late nineteenth-century recording of older oral traditions; the myth itself belongs to the Dreaming.

In the Narran Lake region, a story tells how Baiame’s wives, including Birrahgnooloo, went fishing while Baiame was away. As they fished, a Kurrea—a great crocodile-like creature dwelling in the depths of the lake—rose up and swallowed both women whole. When Baiame returned and found them missing, he searched and discovered that the Kurrea had taken them. Using his power, he tracked the creature to Narran Lake, confronted it, and killed it. He split open its body and freed his wives, restoring them to life. The place where the Kurrea died became sacred ground, marked by the memory of danger, rescue, and the presence of ancestral beings. Birrahgnooloo’s ordeal and deliverance are part of the story that explains why Narran Lake is a site of spiritual significance. The story sacralizes Narran Lake as a site where ancestral beings suffered danger and were rescued. It reinforces Baiame’s power as protector and establishes Birrahgnooloo’s vulnerability and importance within the family of ancestral beings. Birrahgnooloo as mother of DaramulumMythological genealogies recorded in ethnographic and linguistic works on Kamilaroi and related groups, including references in academic dictionaries and secondary summaries.

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century ethnographic documentation of older oral traditions; the genealogy itself belongs to the Dreaming.

In several southeastern Aboriginal traditions, Baiame, the sky father and creator, has two wives, one of whom is Birrahgnooloo. With her he has a son, Daramulum (Dharramalan), whose name means “one-legged” in some interpretations. Daramulum is an important ancestral being associated with male initiation sites (boras) and with rock engravings in the Sydney region, where he is often depicted in profile. As mother of Daramulum, Birrahgnooloo stands at the intersection of cosmic creation, social law, and ritual practice: her son participates in the initiation of boys into manhood, while she embodies the fertility of women and the continuity of families. The genealogy situates her within a family of high beings whose relationships structure the Dreaming. The story establishes Birrahgnooloo’s status as a high-ranking ancestral mother whose offspring play central roles in ritual and cosmology. It links female fertility to male initiation, suggesting a complementary relationship between the powers that sustain life and the ceremonies that shape social adulthood.
