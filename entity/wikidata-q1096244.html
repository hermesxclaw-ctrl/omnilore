Who It Is

Imagine a damp plain where reeds hiss in the wind as far as the eye can see — not yet Japan, but the raw, unfinished country between a bright heaven above and a polluted darkness below. That is Ashihara no Nakatsukuni, the Central Land of Reed Plains: the world we live in, as ancient Japanese myth saw it, a middle realm that had to be bargained for, bullied for, and finally descended into by the gods themselves.

As a place, Ashihara possesses agency in mythic terms: it is unruly, noisy, and violent before pacification. Kojiki says after Susanoo's descent, the land's earthly deities (kunitsukami) were 'riotous, making rocks, tree stumps and leaves able to speak' (Nihon Shoki variant). Ōkuninushi must master it through suffering and magic. Amaterasu's heavenly deities view it as noisy (sawagashiki kuni) and in need of ordering. Cosmological Mediation (Middle Position)

As the middle realm, Ashihara functions as the only place where heavenly (amatsukami) and earthly (kunitsukami) deities can meet, intermarry, and contend. It is the ritual center where heavenly will is enacted. This liminality makes it the locus of legitimacy: rule over Ashihara confers rule over the visible world.

Limit: Cannot ascend to Takamagahara without heavenly aid (Ame no Hashidate, Ame no Iwafune); remains vulnerable to pollution from Yomi and to heavenly coercion. Its fertility depends on continued correct ritual from the heavenly line.

Generative Fertility (Reed Plain / Rice Plain)

Originally wild reed beds, after heavenly ordering it becomes Toyoashihara no Mizuho no Kuni — abundant with reeds and rice. Ninigi brings heavenly rice (from Takamagahara's fields) to plant in Ashihara, establishing agriculture as divine gift. The land's abundance is thus both natural and bestowed.

Limit: Fertility is conditional on ritual purity and obedience to heavenly kami; myths of famine or disorder follow ritual failure. Reeds indicate uncultivated state that must be transformed.

Political Legitimation

Possession and pacification of Ashihara legitimates kingship. The Kuniyuzuri narrative explicitly transfers sovereignty from Ōkuninushi (kunitsukami) to Amaterasu's lineage (amatsukami), providing mythic charter for Yamato rule over Izumo and other polities.

Limit: Transfer is contested within texts: requires negotiation, deception (Amaterasu's messengers), and finally military threat by Takemikazuchi and Futsunushi. Ōkuninushi's consent is conditional on a grand shrine (Izumo Taisha).

Permeability to Other Worlds

Ashihara is porous: heavenly deities descend (Tenson Kōrin) and ascend, dead travel to Yomi via Yomotsu Hirasaka, and Susanoo moves between all three realms. Sacred sites act as portals (Ama no Iwato, Himuka, Izumo). This permeability allows divine intervention but also pollution and chaos.

Limit: Passage is restricted and dangerous; Izanagi must block Yomi's entrance with a boulder; mortals who enter Yomi cannot return unpolluted.

Accounts I
Kuniumi — The Birth of the Reed PlainKojiki Kamitsumaki; Nihon Shoki Book 1 (Kamiyo)

Mythic time, codified 712-720 CE, reflecting Yayoi-Kofun oral traditions

Izanagi and Izanami, standing on Ame no Ukihashi, stir the brine with the jeweled spear Ame no Nuboko. Droplets form Onogoro Island. They descend, perform marriage rites, and beget the islands: Awaji, Iyo (Shikoku), Oki, Tsukushi (Kyushu), Iki, Tsushima, Sado, and Yamato (Honshu). These eight are Ōyashima. They then beget deities of wind, trees, mountains, and finally Kagutsuchi (fire), whose birth kills Izanami. The reed plain thus emerges as a moist, jelly-like floating mass that coalesces into islands. Nihon Shoki variants add that the land drifted like a jellyfish and that other deities stabilized it. Establishes Ashihara no Nakatsukuni as the physical world, distinct from Takamagahara. Provides charter for territorial claims; each island's birth explains toponyms. Izanami's death introduces death pollution, requiring Izanagi's purification which births Amaterasu, Tsukuyomi, and Susanoo — the rulers of the three realms.

The Expulsion of Susanoo and His Descent to AshiharaKojiki Kamitsumaki; Nihon Shoki Book 1

Mythic time, codified 712-720

Susanoo, assigned to rule Ne no Kuni or the sea, weeps for his mother Izanami and refuses duty, causing destruction. Amaterasu and the heavenly kami expel him from Takamagahara after his violent outrages (destroying rice fields, flaying a horse, causing Amaterasu to hide in Ame no Iwato). Susanoo descends to Ashihara, first to Torikami Peak in Izumo at the Hi River. There he encounters the elderly couple Ashinazuchi and Tenazuchi mourning their daughter Kushinada-hime, who is to be devoured by the eight-headed serpent Yamata no Orochi. Susanoo slays Orochi with sake, finds the sword Kusanagi in its tail, and marries Kushinada, building a palace at Suga in Izumo. Introduces Susanoo as culture hero of Ashihara's Izumo region and explains the origin of the imperial sword Kusanagi, later part of the Sanshu no Jingi. Establishes Izumo as Susanoo's base and the lineage leading to Ōkuninushi (Susanoo's descendant). Demonstrates Ashihara's initial chaos (monsters) and need for heroic ordering.

Ōkuninushi (Ōnamuchi) and the Trials — Lord of the Reed PlainKojiki Kamitsumaki; Nihon Shoki Book 1

Mythic time, codified 712-720

Ōnamuchi, descendant of Susanoo (sixth generation in Kojiki), is the chief kunitsukami ruling Ashihara. Early tales: he aids the White Hare of Inaba (Shiro Usagi) flayed by crocodile-sharks (wani), revealing his compassion vs. his cruel 80 brothers (Yasogami). The brothers repeatedly kill him (crushing with hot boulder, trapping in split tree), but his mother and Susanoo revive him. He flees to Ne no Kuni where Susanoo subjects him to ordeals: sleeping in a snake room, centipede/wasp room, and retrieving an arrow from a burning field (aided by a mouse), and retrieving Susanoo's hair ornaments from his head while Susanoo sleeps (aided by Kushinada's daughter Suseri-bime). He elopes with Suseri-bime, steals Susanoo's sword, bow, and koto, and Susanoo, pursuing, forgives him and names him Ōkuninushi (Great Land Master), commanding him to rule Ashihara. Establishes Ōkuninushi as the legitimate indigenous ruler of Ashihara before heavenly takeover. Explains Izumo's prominence and the moral contrast between heavenly and earthly deities. Accounts II
Kuniyuzuri — The Transfer of the Land (Land-Ceding Myth)Kojiki Kamitsumaki; Nihon Shoki Book 2 (Kamiyo) — central political myth

Codified 712-720, reflecting late 7th-century Yamato consolidation over Izumo and Kyushu polities

Amaterasu, seeing Ashihara in turmoil under Ōkuninushi, declares it should be ruled by her child. She sends successive envoys: Ame no Hohi (who defects to Ōkuninushi, ancestor of Izumo priests), Ame no Wakahiko (who also defects and is killed by a heavenly arrow), and finally Takemikazuchi (with Futsunushi in Nihon Shoki) bearing heavenly swords. Takemikazuchi arrives at Inasa Beach in Izumo, plants his sword tip-up, and demands Ōkuninushi cede the land. Ōkuninushi defers to his sons: Kotoshironushi (who fishing, agrees) and Takeminakata (who wrestles Takemikazuchi, loses, and flees to Suwa). Ōkuninushi agrees to surrender the visible land (aragoto) if he retains the invisible/spirit world (kakuregoto) and receives a grand shrine (Izumo Taisha) where he will be worshipped. The kunitsukami withdraw. Mythic charter for Yamato supremacy: heavenly lineage legitimately inherits Ashihara. Explains dual sovereignty: emperor rules visible realm, Ōkuninushi rules hidden/spirit realm. Justifies Izumo Taisha's scale and the Suwa shrine (Takeminakata's exile). Historical reading: reflects Yamato

Tenson Kōrin — Descent of the Heavenly Grandson NinigiKojiki Kamitsumaki; Nihon Shoki Book 2

Mythic time, codified 712-720

After Kuniyuzuri, Amaterasu sends her grandson Ninigi no Mikoto (son of Ame no Oshihomimi) to rule Ashihara. She gives him the Sanshu no Jingi (mirror, magatama, Kusanagi sword), the heavenly rice ears, and attendants including Ame no Uzume, Tajikarao, and Sarutahiko (earthly deity who guides the descent). Ninigi descends via Ame no Ukihashi to Mt. Takachiho (Himuka, Kyushu). He encounters Konohanasakuya-hime (daughter of mountain deity Ōyamatsumi), marries her; her hasty pregnancy causes his doubt, she proves fidelity by bearing children in a burning hut. Their descendants include Hoderi, Hosuseri, and Hoori (Yamasachi). Establishes direct heavenly rule over Ashihara; begins the imperial lineage (Ninigi → Hoori → Ugayafukiaezu → Jimmu). Transfers heavenly agriculture to earth. Takachiho remains sacred descent point; Himuka's importance contested between Miyazaki and Kirishima traditions.

Jimmu Tōsei — Pacification and Founding of Yamato on the Reed PlainKojiki Nakatsumaki; Nihon Shoki Books 3-4

Mythic time set c. 660 BCE in texts, codified 712-720; reflects Kofun period memory

Ninigi's great-grandson Kamuyamato Iwarebiko (Emperor Jimmu) leads an eastern expedition (Tōsei) from Himuka to conquer and unify Ashihara. Guided by the three-legged crow Yatagarasu sent by Amaterasu, and aided by earthly deities who submit, he defeats the chieftain Nagasunehiko in Yamato after a long campaign via the Seto Inland Sea. He establishes his palace at Kashihara in Yamato, becoming first human emperor (tennō) to rule Toyoashihara no Mizuho no Kuni. The narrative marks transition from divine to human rule over the reed plain. Completes the mythic history of Ashihara: from divine creation to heavenly descent to human imperial rule. Provides foundation date (traditionally 660 BCE, 11 Feb 660) still celebrated as National Foundation Day (Kenkoku Kinen no Hi). Transforms cosmological term into historical Japan.

Ashihara no Nakatsukuni was created by Susanoo after he was expelled from heaven. — Primary texts (Kojiki 712, Nihon Shoki 720) show Ashihara already existed, created by Izanagi and Izanami during Kuniumi, before Susanoo's birth. Susanoo descends to it, slays Orochi, and fathers the lineage that rules it, but does not create it.

Ashihara no Nakatsukuni is simply the ancient name for Japan as a geographic country. — In 8th-century texts it is a cosmological realm — the middle world between heaven and underworld — that includes but is not limited to the Japanese islands. Only from the Man'yōshū period onward does Toyoashihara become a poetic synonym for Japan, and modern use as 'Japan' is a secondary, political reinterpretation.

Ashihara no Nakatsukuni is the Japanese equivalent of Norse Midgard and therefore exactly parallel. — While both are 'middle earths' between heaven and underworld, Ashihara's vertical tripartite cosmology, its specific association with reed beds/rice, and its political function (legitimating Yamato rule via Kuniyuzuri) have no direct Norse parallel. The comparison is a useful heuristic introduced by Aston (1896) but not an identity.

The term Toyoashihara no Nakatsukuni and Ashihara no Nakatsukuni refer to different places. — They are variant names for the same cosmological realm; Toyo- is an honorific prefix meaning 'abundant/luxuriant' added for eulogy (as in Toyoashihara no Mizuho no Kuni). Texts use them interchangeably, with the longer form in formal norito and imperial edicts.

Yomi, Ne no Kuni, and Ashihara are all the same underworld. — Kojiki distinguishes them: Ashihara is the living middle world; Yomi is the polluted land of the dead (Izanami's realm); Ne no Kuni is Susanoo's root land/sea realm, sometimes conflated but textually distinct; Tokoyo no Kuni is a distant eternal land. Conflation obscures the tripartite structure.
