Who It Is

Before the gods of Olympus, before the Titans themselves, there was only the sky — a vast, bronze dome that covered the earth and fathered the first dynasty of the cosmos. This is the story of Uranus, the primordial sky god whose violent overthrow set the stage for Greek mythology's most epic conflicts.

Uranus is a primordial, impersonal force. In myth, he is a harsh father who hates his children and hides them in the earth, provoking his own downfall. After his castration, he recedes from active involvement, though he later warns Cronus and advises Zeus.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Most linguists derive the name from Proto-Greek *Worsanós, from the Indo-European root *ṷérs- 'to rain, moisten', making Uranus the 'rain-maker' or 'lord of rain'. A less likely etymology connects it to *ṷérso- 'height, top', meaning 'the one standing on high'.

Pronunciation — YOOR-ə-nəs or yoo-RAY-nəs (English); [uːranós] in Ancient Greek.

Aliases — Ouranos, Caelus (Roman), Sky, Heaven

Ownership — Greek mythology, later adopted into Roman mythology as Caelus.

Powers
Personification of the Sky

Uranus embodies the sky itself, covering the earth and serving as the dwelling place of the gods.

Limit: After his castration, he no longer covers the earth at night; he is detached from the earth.

Progenitor of Gods and Beings

Uranus fathered the Titans, Cyclopes, Hecatoncheires, and, through his blood, the Erinyes, Giants, Meliae, and Aphrodite.

Limit: His generative power is violent and often unwanted, leading to his downfall.

Divine Authority

Uranus was the first ruler of the cosmos, a role he held until his son Cronus overthrew him.

Limit: He was overthrown and lost his power.

Prophecy and Counsel

After his castration, Uranus, along with Gaia, warned Cronus about his fate and advised Zeus on how to avoid being overthrown.

Limit: He does not act directly, only through prophecy and advice.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Uranus is typically depicted as a naked, bearded man, sometimes winged, holding a scepter or a sickle. In art, he is often shown as an elderly figure, sometimes hovering over Gaia. The Pergamon Altar shows him winged and fighting with a sword. He is rarely anthropomorphized in literature; he is primarily the sky itself, conceived as a bronze dome.

Symbols — The sky, the sickle (in the castration myth), the dome or vault of heaven. In Roman depictions, he may be shown as a starry sky or a celestial globe.

Domains — The sky, the heavens, the celestial sphere. He is the personification of the sky itself, which covers the earth.

Accounts I
The Castration of UranusHesiod, Theogony, lines 154-210

Archaic

Gaia, angered by Uranus's hatred of their children and his hiding them within her, creates a sickle and asks her children to punish him. Only Cronus agrees. When Uranus comes to lie with Gaia, Cronus reaches out and castrates him, throwing the severed genitals into the sea. From the blood that falls on the earth spring the Erinyes, the Giants, and the Meliae. From the foam around the genitals in the sea, Aphrodite is born. Uranus is overthrown, the Titans gain power, and Aphrodite is born. Uranus calls his sons 'Titans' (Strainers) and prophesies vengeance.

Accounts II
Uranus's Role in the Succession MythHesiod, Theogony, lines 453-506, 886-900

Archaic

After his castration, Uranus and Gaia warn Cronus that he is destined to be overthrown by one of his children. They advise Rhea to go to Crete to give birth to Zeus, and later advise Zeus to swallow Metis to prevent the same fate. Zeus is saved and eventually becomes the ruler of the cosmos.

Uranus was a widely worshipped god. — No cult addressed directly to Uranus survived into classical times.

Uranus is the same as the Roman Caelus in all details. — Caelus has a different genealogy and mythology, though he is often equated.

Uranus's castration was a unique myth. — Similar myths exist in other cultures, such as the Hurrian creation myth involving Anu and Kumarbi.

Uranus was a benevolent father. — He hated his children and hid them, leading to his overthrow.
