Who It Is

L'Om Badaluk (from Latin Orcus — the underworld god who became a demon and then a monster) is the Italian ogre, a man-eating giant or monstrous being that terrorizes the folkloric landscapes of the Italian peninsula. Unlike the dim-witted ogres of French tradition (typified by Perrault's 'Le Petit Poucet'), the Italian Orco is often cunning, shape-shifting, and associated with specific locations such as ruined towers, deep caves, or solitary mills. The name appears throughout Italy in various forms: Orco in Tuscany, Uerru in Sardinia, and as a generalized bogeyman figure ('l'uomo nero') used to frighten children into obedience. In the fairy tales of Giambattista Basile (Lo Cunto de li Cunti, 1634), the Orco appears as a half-animal, half-human creature with an insatiable appetite for human flesh, but one who can be outwitted by clever peasants. In some versions, the Orco is a shape-shifter who can become a wolf, a bull, or a serpent. The creature is etymologically related to the English 'ogre' itself, which entered French and English via Giambattista Basile's Neapolitan fairy tales. The Italian 'Orco' also has strong ties to the underworld: the Roman god Orcus was a punisher of broken oaths, and the transitional sense from 'god of the dead' to 'man-eating monster' reflects the Christianization of pagan underworld figures. The term 'ogre' in modern fantasy derives directly from this Italian folkloric root.
