Who It Is

Before the world was finished, before the rules were set, there was a scruffy, hungry, and impossibly clever being who walked the earth. He was a thief, a liar, a glutton, and a lecher. He was also a creator, a hero, and the one who brought fire to humanity. His name was Coyote, and in his stories, you can hear the world being made—and unmade—by his boundless appetites and brilliant, foolish schemes. He is the original chaos agent, the one who breaks the rules to see what happens, and in doing so, he creates the world we live in.

Coyote is the embodiment of the id. He is driven by hunger, lust, and a boundless, often misdirected curiosity. He is gluttonous, lecherous, and lazy, preferring to trick others into doing his work. He is a master of disguise and deceit, but his schemes frequently backfire, leading to his own humiliation. He is also a creature of immense creativity and stubbornness, often using his wits (and occasionally his foolishness) to accomplish great things, even if by accident. He is a boundary-crosser, a rule-breaker, and a catalyst for change, both good and bad.

Powers
Shapeshifting

Coyote can transform between his animal and human forms at will, and sometimes into other objects or beings.

Limit: He can be trapped in a form or lose his powers if he breaks a taboo or is tricked by a more powerful being.

Immortality / Resurrection

Coyote often survives death, being chopped to pieces, burned, or drowned, only to put himself back together or be revived.

Limit: This is not always a power he controls; he may be revived by others or through his own cleverness. Sometimes he stays dead until a specific condition is met.

Transformation of the World

As a transformer, Coyote can alter the landscape, create animals and people, and institute customs and taboos. He often does this by accident or as a punishment.

Limit: His power is often reactive, not intentional. He is not an omnipotent creator but a powerful being who shapes things through his actions.

Master of Deception

His primary 'power' is his cunning. He can trick animals, humans, and even gods into doing his bidding.

Limit: His greed and arrogance often blind him, leading his schemes to fail spectacularly.

Stealing Fire

In many traditions, Coyote is the fire-bringer, who stole fire from the Fire Beings or the Sky People to give to humans.

Limit: The theft is usually a one-time event, and he often requires help from other animals.

Power over Life and Death

In some stories, Coyote is responsible for the origin of death. He either fails to bring back the dead or actively chooses to let death remain.

Limit: This is often a result of his foolishness or a deliberate choice to prevent overpopulation. He is not a god of the underworld, but a being who set the rule.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Coyote is a shapeshifter. He can appear as a literal coyote, a man, or a being with features of both. In his animal form, he is described as a scraggly, clever-looking dog with a bushy tail, yellow eyes, and a perpetual expression of mischief or hunger. Symbols — The coyote animal itself is his primary symbol. Other symbols include fire, the sun, the moon, stars, the bow and arrow, and the trickster's own insatiable stomach. In some traditions, his scat is a symbol of his transformative, often crude, creative power. He is also associated with the wind, which carries his howl.

Domains — Coyote is a wanderer, at home in every terrestrial landscape: the desert, the plains, the mountains, the forests, and the edges of human settlements. He is a master of the wild, but also a frequent visitor to the human world, often causing chaos there. He is associated with fire (which he stole or discovered), with death (which he brought into the world), with the stars (which he scattered), and with the creation of human beings and their customs

Accounts I
Coyote and the Origin of DeathVarious (e.g., Navajo, Salishan, Kootenai)

Mythic time

In one common story, Coyote's son is killed by a monster. The Creator (or another powerful being) tells Coyote that if he can place a specific object (like a burning stick or a rock) in a ceremonial way, his son will be revived. Coyote, in his grief and impatience, fails the test, and death becomes permanent in the world. In other versions, he chooses death, arguing that a world without death would become too crowded and that people would not value life. Death becomes a permanent part of the human condition. This story explains the existence of mortality and is often told with a mix of tragedy and dark humor.

Coyote Steals FireVarious (e.g., Kalapuya, Klamath, Shasta, Miwok)

Mythic time

In the time before fire, humans were cold and ate raw food. Coyote, seeing their suffering, devises a plan to steal fire from the Fire Beings who guard it. He often does this by transforming into a leaf or a piece of wood to float down a river, or by distracting the guards with a game. He then races away, passing the fire from animal to animal (e.g., from his tail to the trees) until it is safely delivered to the people. Humans gain fire for warmth, cooking, and protection. Coyote is celebrated as a culture hero for this act, even if his motives were not purely altruistic.

Accounts II
Coyote and the Monster (Slaying of the Devourer)Various (e.g., Nez Perce, Crow, Shoshone)

Mythic time

A giant monster is devouring all the animals and people. Coyote, using his wits, allows himself to be swallowed. Once inside the monster's belly, he uses his knife to cut the heart out, killing it. He then frees the people and animals from the monster's carcass, sometimes by cutting an opening in its side. He then distributes the body parts of the monster to become various things, or uses the blood to create different peoples. The world is saved from the devourer. This story establishes Coyote as a hero and transformer, and often explains the origins of various tribes or natural features.

Coyote and the Ducks (The Eye-Juggler)Various (e.g., Klamath, Modoc, Nez Perce)

Mythic time

Coyote, hungry, sees a flock of ducks. He tricks them by pretending to be dead or by singing a song that makes them dance with their eyes closed. He then grabs them one by one. However, he is often tricked in return by a clever duck who tells him to throw his eyes up into the air and catch them. Coyote does so, but fails to catch them, and is left blind. He is forced to use substitute eyes (often from a juniper berry or a piece of pitch) to see again. Coyote gets a meal, but often loses his eyes in the process, demonstrating the theme of the trickster being tricked. This story is a classic example of his foolishness and his ability to recover from his own mistakes.

Coyote and the Rock (The Rock that Grew)Various (e.g., Paiute, Shoshone, Ute)

Mythic time

Coyote insults or kicks a large rock. The rock, angered, begins to chase him. Coyote runs, but the rock grows larger and faster. He tries to hide, but the rock finds him and crushes him, often flattening him. He is eventually freed by some other animal (like the ants) who chew him loose, or he manages to escape, but he has learned a lesson about respecting even inanimate things. Coyote is humiliated and physically harmed. The story serves as a warning against arrogance and disrespect.

Coyote is merely a 'trickster' in the same vein as Loki or Eshu. — While he is a trickster, Coyote is also a culture hero, a transformer, and sometimes a creator. He is a sacred figure in many traditions, and his stories are not just entertainment but also teach moral, social, and cosmological lessons. The trickster aspect is only one part of his complex character.

All Coyote stories are the same across tribes. — There is no single 'Coyote myth.' The stories, character traits, and even his name vary dramatically between tribes. A Crow story about Old Man Coyote is very different from a Navajo story about the bungling Coyote.

Coyote is a 'god' in the Greek or Roman sense. — Coyote is not a deity with a fixed domain, a temple, or a formal cult. He is a mythic being, a character in stories, who can be powerful but is not omnipotent or omniscient. He is more akin to a culture hero or a transformer than a god.

The Wile E. Coyote cartoon is an accurate representation of the mythic Coyote. — Wile E. Coyote is a literal, non-supernatural coyote whose sole motivation is catching a bird. He is a comedic figure, not a mythic one. While he captures the trickster's persistence and tendency for schemes to backfire, he lacks the creative, sacred, and transformative aspects of the mythic Coyote.

Coyote is always a male figure. — While Coyote is most often depicted as male, there are stories where he takes on female form or where the trickster role is played by a female figure. The gender of the trickster is not fixed.
