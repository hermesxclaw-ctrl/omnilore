Who It Is

Coyote is one of the most widespread and iconic figures in Native American mythology, appearing in the oral traditions of dozens of tribes from the Great Basin to the Great Plains, the Southwest to the Pacific Northwest. He is the archetypal trickster — a figure who appears in tales of creation, heroism, foolishness, and moral instruction in equal measure. Coyote is neither purely good nor purely evil; he is selfish, clever, foolish, wise, and always hungry — a mirror of human nature in its most honest form.

Physically, Coyote is described as the animal himself — the four-legged wild dog of the Americas — though he often walks on two legs, speaks, and behaves like a human when the story demands it. In some tales, he shape-shifts into human form, appearing as a scraggly old man with yellow eyes who is always looking for his next meal. His appearance as an actual coyote emphasizes his wildness — he cannot be domesticated, predicted, or controlled by the rules of polite society.

Coyote's role in cosmology varies across cultures. Among many tribes, he is a creator figure who helped shape the world. In some Paiute and Shoshone stories, Coyote was present at creation and contributed essential elements to the human world. He stole fire from the gods and brought it to humanity (a story with clear parallels to Prometheus). He placed the stars in the sky — though carelessly, throwing them up by the handful rather than arranging them carefully, explaining why the night sky appears chaotic rather than ordered. Among some California tribes, Coyote created death by insisting that people should die permanently rather than being temporarily dead and returning, believing that without death, the world would become overcrowded.

However, Coyote's trickster nature means his schemes often backfire. In a classic tale, Coyote tries to imitate the Creator's work but produces only flawed, comical results. He greedily hoards food only to lose it. He falls for his own tricks. He is sexually voracious and often suffers humiliating consequences for his lust. These stories serve as both entertainment and moral instruction: listeners laugh at Coyote's foolishness while recognizing their own greed, pride, and shortsightedness reflected in him.

Coyote is also a figure of profound metaphysical significance in some traditions. In Navajo (Diné) belief, Coyote is associated with chaos, transformation, and the boundary between order and disorder. He is sometimes called 'First Angry' or 'First Scolder,' representing the disruptive forces that must be acknowledged and integrated rather than denied. In the Diné creation story, Coyote's theft of the Water Monster's baby causes the great flood that forces the people to emerge into the current world — his mischief inadvertently driving cosmic progress.

Coyote survived colonization in remarkable ways. In many reservation communities, Coyote stories are still told as moral instruction and entertainment, and Coyote has become a symbol of Indigenous survival and adaptability — the trickster who refuses to be erased.

Form

Coyote is most often described as a coyote, the animal, but he is also a man. He can shift between forms at will. In some stories, he is a man with coyote features; in others, he is a full animal. His appearance is often used to comic effect: he may be scruffy, scrawny, or have a perpetually hungry look. He is rarely described as majestic or beautiful; his power lies in his cunning, not his looks.

Powers

Shape-shifting Coyote can change his form from coyote to man and back, and can sometimes take other forms as well. Virtually universal across all Coyote traditions. His transformations are often imperfect or temporary. He may be caught mid-transformation, or he may be unable to change back. He is not a master of disguise; his identity often gives him away. None known; shape-shifting is a core trait.

Immortality / Supernatural Resilience Coyote is often killed in stories, but he always comes back. He may be dismembered, drowned, or burned, but he survives. Universal. Death is a recurring theme in Coyote stories, but it is rarely permanent. His immortality is not always a blessing; he may be trapped in a painful state, or he may be forced to experience the consequences of his actions. In some stories, Coyote is specifically mortal, and his death is a real event. The inconsistency is a feature of the oral tradition, not a bug.

Cunning / Deception Coyote's primary 'power' is his intellect, which he uses for both good and ill. He can outwit monsters, chiefs, and even gods. Universal. His cunning often fails him because of his own greed, lust, or impatience. He is a brilliant strategist who is undone by his own appetites. None.

Creation / Transformation In many traditions, Coyote is a transformer or culture hero who shapes the world. He may create the landscape, the animals, or the stars. Common but not universal. In some traditions, he is a secondary figure who works alongside another creator (e.g., the Maidu's 'Earth-Maker'). His creations are often flawed or comedic. He may create something good by accident, or he may create something bad on purpose. His role as a creator is sometimes in tension with his role as a trickster. He can be both the one who makes the world and the one who messes it up.

Shapeshifting Coyote can transform between his animal and human forms at will, and sometimes into other objects or beings. Pan-tribal He can be trapped in a form or lose his powers if he breaks a taboo or is tricked by a more powerful being. In some stories, he is a coyote who acts like a human, in others a man who can become a coyote; the mechanics are rarely explained.

Immortality / Resurrection Coyote often survives death, being chopped to pieces, burned, or drowned, only to put himself back together or be revived. Pan-tribal This is not always a power he controls; he may be revived by others or through his own cleverness. Sometimes he stays dead until a specific condition is met. In some stories, his death is permanent, serving as a lesson. The final fate of Coyote varies: he may be waiting in the west, or he may have been killed by his own creations.

Transformation of the World As a transformer, Coyote can alter the landscape, create animals and people, and institute customs and taboos. He often does this by accident or as a punishment. Pan-tribal (especially prominent in creation cycles) His power is often reactive, not intentional. He is not an omnipotent creator but a powerful being who shapes things through his actions. In some traditions, he is the sole creator; in others, he works alongside or is subordinate to a higher power like the Sun or a 'Great Spirit.'

Master of Deception His primary 'power' is his cunning. He can trick animals, humans, and even gods into doing his bidding. Pan-tribal His greed and arrogance often blind him, leading his schemes to fail spectacularly. None.

Stealing Fire In many traditions, Coyote is the fire-bringer, who stole fire from the Fire Beings or the Sky People to give to humans. California, Plateau, and other traditions The theft is usually a one-time event, and he often requires help from other animals. In some versions, he is the one who loses fire, and another hero (like Raven) recovers it.

Power over Life and Death In some stories, Coyote is responsible for the origin of death. He either fails to bring back the dead or actively chooses to let death remain. Pan-tribal (origin of death myths) This is often a result of his foolishness or a deliberate choice to prevent overpopulation. He is not a god of the underworld, but a being who set the rule. In some traditions, he is seen as a hero for choosing death, as a world without death would become overcrowded and stagnant.

Behavior & Habitat

Coyote is characterized by his insatiable appetites: for food, for sex, for power, and for attention. He is a glutton, a lecher, a liar, and a braggart. He is also, paradoxically, a culture hero. He is the one who stole fire from the fire beings, who released the buffalo from a cave, who placed the stars in the sky, and who made the world safe for humans. He is a creature of pure id, but his actions often have unintended, world-shaping consequences. He is a bungler who succeeds by accident, and a schemer who fails by his own greed.

Accounts

In one common story, Coyote's son is killed by a monster. The Creator (or another powerful being) tells Coyote that if he can place a specific object (like a burning stick or a rock) in a ceremonial way, his son will be revived. Coyote, in his grief and impatience, fails the test, and death becomes permanent in the world. In other versions, he chooses death, arguing that a world without death would become too crowded and that people would not value life.

In the time before fire, humans were cold and ate raw food. Coyote, seeing their suffering, devises a plan to steal fire from the Fire Beings who guard it. He often does this by transforming into a leaf or a piece of wood to float down a river, or by distracting the guards with a game. He then races away, passing the fire from animal to animal (e.g., from his tail to the trees) until it is safely delivered to the people.

A giant monster is devouring all the animals and people. Coyote, using his wits, allows himself to be swallowed. Once inside the monster's belly, he uses his knife to cut the heart out, killing it. He then frees the people and animals from the monster's carcass, sometimes by cutting an opening in its side. He then distributes the body parts of the monster to become various things, or uses the blood to create different peoples.

Coyote, hungry, sees a flock of ducks. He tricks them by pretending to be dead or by singing a song that makes them dance with their eyes closed. He then grabs them one by one. However, he is often tricked in return by a clever duck who tells him to throw his eyes up into the air and catch them. Coyote does so, but fails to catch them, and is left blind. He is forced to use substitute eyes (often from a juniper berry or a piece of pitch) to see again.

Coyote insults or kicks a large rock. The rock, angered, begins to chase him. Coyote runs, but the rock grows larger and faster. He tries to hide, but the rock finds him and crushes him, often flattening him. He is eventually freed by some other animal (like the ants) who chew him loose, or he manages to escape, but he has learned a lesson about respecting even inanimate things.

Scholarly Debates
Coyote as Creator vs. Fool
In many Plains traditions (e.g., Crow, Cheyenne), Coyote is 'Old Man Coyote,' a powerful creator who shapes the world and establishes laws. ⇌ In many Southwestern traditions (e.g., Navajo, Hopi), Coyote is a foolish, often dangerous being who causes problems through his greed and stupidity.
Evidence for A: Crow and Cheyenne creation stories feature Coyote as the primary transformer.
Evidence for B: Navajo and Hopi stories often feature Coyote as a bungler who is punished for his actions.
The Origin of Death
Coyote is responsible for death because he failed a test or was too impatient. ⇌ Coyote deliberately chose death, arguing that a world without it would be unsustainable.
Evidence for A: In many versions, he is grieving and makes a mistake.
Evidence for B: In other versions, he makes a logical, if harsh, argument for the necessity of death.
Coyote's Relationship to the Supreme Being

What We Don't Know

A complete, comparative analysis of all Coyote stories across every tribe is a monumental task that is not yet complete. Many stories remain untranslated or are considered sacred and not meant for public dissemination. The specific ceremonial context of many Coyote stories is not well-documented in public sources. The visual iconography of Coyote in pre-contact art is difficult to definitively identify. The reception of Coyote in modern Indigenous literature and art is a rich field that could be explored in much greater depth.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Coyote not as a single character, but as a concept. He is the embodiment of chaos, of the untamed, of the parts of ourselves that are greedy, lustful, and impulsive. He is also the embodiment of creativity, of adaptability, and of the cunning needed to survive in a harsh world. He is the id, the ego, and the superego all rolled into one, and he is always, always hungry.
