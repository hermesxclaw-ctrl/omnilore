The Approach

El Cóndor Pasa is a name that soars across two centuries and two continents—from the Quechua-speaking highlands of the Andes to the global pop charts—yet its true identity remains elusive. Is it a folk song, a theatrical production, a political anthem, or a cinematic image? The answer depends on whom you ask, and the journey through its many incarnations reveals a story of cultural exchange, artistic reinvention, and the enduring power of a simple melody.

Content advisory. No dark material of a violent or explicit nature is known. However, the work's history involves cultural appropriation debates, colonial-era romanticization of indigenous peoples, and political symbolism that may be sensitive to some audiences.
Identity

El Cóndor Pasa

Pronunciation el KON-dor PAH-sah

Known As

El Cóndor Pasa (If I Could)The Condor PassesEl Cóndor Pasa (song)El Cóndor Pasa (zarzuela)

Etymology

Spanish for 'The Condor Passes' or 'The Condor Flies By'. The condor is a large Andean bird, symbolizing freedom and the Andean spirit.

Cultural Ownership

Peruvian, with Quechua and Andean roots; later globalized through international adaptations.

Disputed Identifications

Some claim it is an ancient Inca melody, but this is disputed; most scholars attribute the melody to Daniel Alomía Robles, who composed it for the 1913 zarzuela. The song has been misattributed to traditional folk in some sources.

Description

Appearance

As a musical composition, 'El Cóndor Pasa' has no visual appearance. As a theatrical work, the zarzuela features characters in Andean attire, with the condor as a central symbolic figure. The song's melody is characterized by a distinctive pentatonic scale, evoking Andean folk music.

Symbols & Iconography

The condor is a powerful symbol in Andean culture, representing freedom, the heavens, and the connection between the earthly and spiritual realms. In the context of the song, it has come to symbolize longing, hope, and the Andean identity.

Behaviour & Personality

The song's mood is melancholic yet uplifting, with a sense of yearning and aspiration. It has been described as a lament for lost freedom or a celebration of the Andean spirit.

Domains & Habitats

The zarzuela is set in the Peruvian Andes, specifically in a mining community. The song is often associated with the highlands of Peru and the broader Andean region.

Version Differences

The original zarzuela version is a full theatrical piece with multiple songs and scenes. The instrumental version, popularized by Los Incas, is a condensed arrangement of the main theme. Simon & Garfunkel's version adds English lyrics and a folk-rock arrangement, changing the song's character from theatrical to popular music. Other versions include orchestral, choral, and electronic adaptations.

Stories & Myths

The Zarzuela 'El Cóndor Pasa'

Source Original libretto by Julio Baudouin, music by Daniel Alomía RoblesCulture PeruvianPeriod 1913

Summary

The zarzuela tells the story of a young indigenous miner named José, who dreams of escaping his oppressive life in the mines. He is inspired by the flight of a condor, which symbolizes freedom. The plot involves a love triangle and themes of social injustice and indigenous rights. The music incorporates Andean melodies and rhythms.

Participants

José (protagonist)

María (love interest)

Mining overseer (antagonist)

Chorus of miners

Consequences

The zarzuela was well-received in Peru and helped popularize Andean music in urban theaters. It was later revived and adapted into various forms.

Variants

The zarzuela is rarely performed in full today; the song 'El Cóndor Pasa' is the most famous excerpt.

The International Popularization

Source Los Incas, Simon & GarfunkelCulture InternationalPeriod 1960s-1970s

Summary

In 1963, the Argentine band Los Incas recorded an instrumental version of the song, which caught the attention of Paul Simon. Simon wrote English lyrics and, with Art Garfunkel, released 'El Cóndor Pasa (If I Could)' in 1970. The song became a global hit, introducing the melody to millions and sparking interest in Andean music.

Participants

Los Incas

Paul Simon

Art Garfunkel

Consequences

The song became one of Simon & Garfunkel's most famous recordings, and it brought international fame to the original composition. It also led to a legal settlement with Robles' son, who received songwriting credits.

Variants

Many cover versions exist, including by Plácido Domingo, Sarah Brightman, and various folk groups.

Cultural Heritage Recognition

Source Peruvian governmentCulture PeruvianPeriod 2004

Summary

In 2004, the Peruvian government declared 'El Cóndor Pasa' part of the nation's cultural heritage, recognizing its importance to Peruvian identity and its global impact.

Participants

Consequences

The declaration helped protect the song's legacy and affirmed its Peruvian origin.

Contradictions & Disputes

Authorship of the melody

Position A

Daniel Alomía Robles composed the melody in 1913.

Position A — evidence

Original score and copyright registrations by Robles.

Position B

The melody is based on traditional Andean folk music, possibly pre-Columbian.

Position B — evidence

The pentatonic scale and style resemble traditional Andean music; some claim Robles merely arranged a folk tune.

Scholarly Consensus

Most musicologists credit Robles as the composer, though he may have incorporated folk elements.

Cultural ownership

Position A

The song is Peruvian and should be recognized as such.

Position A — evidence

Peruvian government declaration and Robles' nationality.

Position B

The song belongs to the broader Andean culture, not just Peru.

Position B — evidence

The melody and themes are shared across Andean countries like Bolivia, Ecuador, and Chile.

Scholarly Consensus

The song is often seen as a symbol of the Andes as a whole, but Peru claims it as national heritage.

Authenticity of Simon & Garfunkel's version

Position A

It is a respectful adaptation that brought international attention to the original.

Position A — evidence

The duo credited Robles and paid royalties to his estate.

Position B

It is cultural appropriation that exploited Andean culture for commercial gain.

Position B — evidence

Paul Simon initially claimed the melody was a traditional folk song, and the English lyrics have no connection to the original themes.

Scholarly Consensus

The adaptation is generally seen as a positive introduction, but the debate continues.
