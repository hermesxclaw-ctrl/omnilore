Who It Is

Imagine a god who wears the skin of his own sacrifice like a raincoat, his body painted red underneath, his hands dangling from the wrists of a dead man. He is not a god of death, but of spring—a terrifying, visceral reminder that life is only possible through the shedding of the old. Xipe Totec, 'Our Lord the Flayed One,' is the Aztec deity who turned agricultural renewal into a state-sponsored ritual of blood and rebirth, a figure so potent that his image still haunts video games, horror films, and the nightmares of the Spanish conquistadors who first documented his cult. His is a story of maize, gold, and the brutal poetry of transformation.

As a god of renewal, his behavior is dualistic: he is both a violent, martial deity and a life-giving force. The act of flaying himself to feed humanity is his defining myth, paralleling the shedding of a snake's skin or the outer husk of maize seed. He is a liberator, freeing people from the constraints of the old, and a bringer of spring after the dry season. He is also a punishing deity, associated with skin diseases, eye ailments, and plague, reflecting the consequences of the cycle of decay and rebirth.

Powers
Flaying and Renewal

Xipe Totec's core power is the ability to shed his skin (or have it shed) to be reborn, symbolizing agricultural renewal. He flayed himself to provide food for humanity, and his act was reenacted through human sacrifice.

Limit: This power is cyclical, tied to the seasons. He can only renew by first undergoing the death/decay phase.

Warfare and Liberation

He was believed to have invented war, and his insignia (cap and rattle staff) were the war attire of the Mexica emperor. He is a liberator, freeing people from the 'old skin' of their previous state, whether on the battlefield or in the cycle of life.

Limit: His martial power is tied to the ritual of sacrifice; war is a means to feed the gods, not an end in itself.

Patronage of Disease and Healing

He is strongly associated with skin diseases (pimples, blisters, scabs), inflammation, eye diseases, and possibly plague. Offerings were made to him to cure these ailments, but his association also implies he could inflict them.

Limit: His power over disease is a direct consequence of his flaying nature; he can both cause and cure, depending on his favor.

Mastery over Goldsmithing

He is the patron of goldsmiths and silversmiths. This connection likely stems from the association of gold with the sun, the east (his direction), and the idea of transformation through fire—a parallel to his own transformative nature.

Limit: This is a specific patronage, not a general power over creation.

Accounts I
The Flaying of Xipe TotecReconstructed from colonial codices and anthropological interpretation (e.g., Sahagún, Durán).

Postclassic (c. 1200-1521 AD)

To provide food for humanity, Xipe Totec flayed his own skin, offering it as a covering for the earth. This act is seen as the divine prototype for the agricultural cycle: the maize seed must shed its outer husk to germinate, and the earth must be 'flayed' by the planting stick to yield food. His flaying is also a metaphor for the snake shedding its skin, and for the transition from the dry, dead season to the green, living one. Established the cycle of death and rebirth in nature and justified the ritual of human sacrifice (Tlacaxipehualiztli) as a reenactment of his act.

Accounts II
The Festival of TlacaxipehualiztliFlorentine Codex (Sahagún), Codex Telleriano-Remensis, Durán's History.

Pre-conquest, observed during the month of Tlacaxipehualiztli (roughly March-April, our spring)

This was the central festival of Xipe Totec. It was a month-long ritual of war, sacrifice, and renewal. Captives were sacrificed to the god, and their bodies were flayed. The skins were then worn by priests and other individuals for up to twenty days. These 'flayed ones' (ximilli) were treated as living embodiments of the god, and they would process through the city, collecting offerings and blessing people. The skins were often painted yellow or red, and some were decorated to resemble the god's own regalia. At the end of the festival, the skins were removed and placed in a special container, where they would decay, symbolizing the death of the old year and the promise of new life. Reinforced the state's military power through ritual, renewed the agricultural cycle, and provided a visceral, communal experience of the god's power.

Xipe Totec is a 'god of flaying' as a form of torture. — His flaying is a symbol of agricultural renewal (the seed shedding its husk) and rebirth. The human sacrifice was a reenactment of his own self-sacrifice to feed humanity, not an act of sadistic cruelty for its own sake, though it was brutal.

The Aztecs invented the cult of Xipe Totec. — The cult was widespread across Mesoamerica and likely originated on the Gulf Coast. The Aztecs adopted it after their conquests in the 15th century.

Xipe Totec is the same as the Maya god of the east. — While there are similarities (the east, the color red), there is no conclusive evidence that they are the same deity. The Maya had their own complex pantheon.

The flayed skin was worn for the entire year. — The skins were worn only during the festival of Tlacaxipehualiztli, which lasted for about 20 days. After that, they were placed in a special container to decay.
