Xipe Totec

Our Lord the Flayed One, The Red Tezcatlipoca, God of Spring and Renewal

TypeDeity
CultureAztec (Mexica)
RealmSpring / Agriculture / Renewal / Goldworking

Xipe Totec as depicted in the Codex Borgia, pre-Columbian Aztec manuscript, c. 1500. Public domain.

I wore my own skin first. Now I wear yours. When the husk rots and falls away, what remains is spring — and gold. Death feeds the seed. The seed feeds you.

Who They Are

Xipe Totec ('Our Lord the Flayed One') is the Aztec god of spring, vegetation, agricultural renewal, and goldsmiths — a deity whose worship involved one of the most visually striking and violent rituals in Mesoamerican religion: the flaying of sacrificial victims, whose skins were then worn by priests for twenty days to enact the god's central myth of renewal through death. Despite the visceral horror of this rite, Xipe Totec is fundamentally a god of life — the divine force that causes seeds to germinate, crops to grow, and the earth to renew itself each spring.

Physically, Xipe Totec is depicted wearing the flayed skin of a sacrificial victim — the skin yellowed and stitched up the back, with the hands of the victim's skin hanging loosely from the god's wrists. In Aztec sculpture, the face of the living god peers out through the mouth opening of the dead skin, creating a haunting image of life contained within death. Beneath the flayed skin, Xipe Totec's body is often painted red or gold — the color of the living flesh beneath the dead husk, the color of spring beneath winter's death. He carries a chicahuaztli (a rattle-staff that mimics the sound of rain on leaves) and a shield with concentric circles representing the sun.

Xipe Totec's central myth explains his flayed appearance. To provide food for humanity, Xipe Totec flayed himself alive, offering his own skin as the covering of the newly sprouting earth. Just as a seed must shed its husk to germinate, Xipe Totec shed his skin so that the earth could be covered in new vegetation. The ritual reenactment of this myth — the festival of Tlacaxipehualiztli ('The Flaying of Men') during the second month of the Aztec calendar (roughly March) — involved captured warriors being sacrificed, their hearts removed, and their skins carefully peeled off. Priests representing Xipe Totec wore these skins for twenty days, during which they would beg throughout Tenochtitlan, receiving offerings that they blessed in return. After twenty days, the skins (now rotting and falling apart) were removed and deposited in caves at the base of the temple — symbolizing the old husk giving way to new life.

Xipe Totec's association with goldsmiths is significant. The process of transforming raw ore into gleaming gold — shedding the dross to reveal the precious metal within — mirrors Xipe Totec's myth of shedding the outer skin to reveal new life. Goldsmiths worshipped Xipe Totec as their patron, and his festivals involved the display of goldwork.

While the Spanish chroniclers described Xipe Totec's rites with revulsion, understanding the theology behind them reveals a profound meditation on the relationship between death and renewal. In an agricultural society, the death of the seed (its decomposition in the soil) is the precondition for new life. Xipe Totec's flayed skin represents the inevitable death that precedes all growth — the cosmic truth that nothing new can emerge until the old is shed. His worship acknowledged the terrifying but necessary role of death in the cycle of life.
