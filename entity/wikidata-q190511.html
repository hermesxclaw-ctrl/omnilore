Who It Is

Imagine a serpent so vast it could encircle the world, a creature of pure chaos that tries to swallow the sun every single night. In ancient Egypt, this wasn't a fantasy—it was the reason the sun rose each morning. Meet Apophis, the Great Serpent, the Enemy of Ra, the embodiment of everything that threatens order, and the most persistent villain in human history.

Apophis's behavior is singular: he seeks to disrupt the cosmic order (Ma'at) by attacking the solar barque of Ra during its nightly journey through the underworld. He is described as a roaring, hissing, chaotic force that creates storms, darkness, and earthquakes. He is not a cunning or intelligent villain in the modern sense, but a primordial force of destruction that acts on instinct. Powers
Immense Size and Physical Strength

Apophis is described as a colossal serpent, 450 cubits long. His physical strength is enough to threaten the solar barque and to cause earthquakes and storms.

Limit: Despite his size, he is consistently defeated or repelled by the gods and the pharaoh, suggesting his power is not absolute.

Control over Darkness and Chaos

Apophis is the embodiment of 'Isfet' (chaos) and the forces of darkness. He can create darkness, storms, and eclipses by attacking the sun.

Limit: His power is limited to the night and the underworld; he cannot act in the light of day.

Hypnotic Gaze and Roar

Apophis is described as having a 'roar' that can cause chaos and a 'gaze' that can petrify or overwhelm his enemies.

Limit: These powers can be countered by the magic of the gods and by reciting specific spells.

Serpentine Venom

As a serpent, Apophis is presumably venomous, though this is not explicitly described. His bite is likely fatal.

Limit: Not directly attested.

Ability to Swallow the Sun

In some texts, Apophis is said to swallow the sun, causing a solar eclipse. This is a temporary state, as the sun is always eventually freed or passes through him.

Limit: The sun is always freed, either by the gods or by Ra's own power.

Resilience and Regeneration

Apophis is extremely difficult to destroy. He is often shown being hacked into pieces, but he is able to regenerate or be resurrected, which is why the battle against him is a nightly occurrence.

Limit: His regeneration is not absolute; he can be temporarily defeated and bound.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Apophis is consistently depicted as a colossal serpent, often described as being 450 cubits long (approximately 230 meters or 750 feet). He is usually shown as a giant snake with a thick, coiled body, sometimes with multiple coils. In some depictions, he has a serpent's head, but in others, he may have a human head or the head of a different creature. He is often shown being pierced with spears, bound with ropes, or hacked with knives by the gods

Symbols — Apophis is symbolized by the serpent, the Uraeus (the cobra on the pharaoh's crown, though this is a protective symbol, not one of Apophis), and by darkness, water, and the night. He is associated with the number 7 and with the constellation of the 'Serpent' (Hydra). In ritual, he is represented by a wax figure of a serpent, which is to be destroyed. His name is often written with a knife or a serpent determinative, indicating his

Domains — Apophis is the lord of the Duat (the underworld), specifically the chaotic waters of Nun and the dark, dangerous regions of the afterlife. He is the master of darkness, storms, earthquakes, and all forms of destructive chaos. He is associated with the night, with the hours when the sun is hidden, and with the primordial abyss that existed before creation. He is not a god of a specific city or cult, but a universal enemy of the world order.

Accounts I
The Nightly Journey of Ra and the Battle with ApophisAmduat, Book of Gates, Book of the Dead

New Kingdom (c. 1550-1070 BCE) and later

Each night, the sun god Ra travels through the underworld (Duat) in his solar barque. As he enters the darkest hours, the great serpent Apophis, who lies in the waters of Nun, attacks the barque. The gods who accompany Ra, particularly Set, Horus, and the goddess Serqet, defend the barque with spears, nets, and magic. The pharaoh, as the earthly representative of Ra, also participates in the battle. The battle is a cosmic struggle between order (Ma'at) and chaos (Isfet). If Apophis were to win, the world would be plunged into darkness and chaos. The gods and the deceased who have been judged worthy (the 'justified dead') help to repel the serpent. The battle is depicted in detail in the Amduat, where Apophis is bound, hacked, and speared, but never permanently destroyed. The sun rises again each morning, signifying the victory of order over chaos. The battle is renewed every night, making it a cyclical, never-ending conflict.

The Creation of ApophisThe Bremner-Rhind Papyrus (Book of Overthrowing Apep)

Late Period (c. 664-332 BCE)

According to the Book of Overthrowing Apep, Apophis was born from the saliva of the goddess Neith, who spat into the primordial waters of Nun during the act of creation. This makes him a primordial being, created from the chaos that existed before the world. He is not a created god but a byproduct of the creative process, an inherent flaw or chaotic element in the universe. This origin story explains why he is so difficult to destroy: he is part of the very fabric of pre-creation. Apophis becomes the eternal enemy of the sun god and the cosmic order.

Accounts II
The Ritual of Overthrowing ApepThe Bremner-Rhind Papyrus (Book of Overthrowing Apep)

Late Period (c. 664-332 BCE)

To ensure the sun's victory over Apophis, the Egyptians performed a daily ritual. Priests would create a wax figure of a serpent, sometimes with a human head, and then destroy it. The figure was burned, mutilated, spat upon, and trampled. The ritual was accompanied by the recitation of spells that described the destruction of Apophis and the victory of Ra. The ritual was performed in the temple of Ra, and it was believed that by destroying the wax figure, the priests were helping to destroy the real Apophis in the underworld. The ritual was so important that it was performed multiple times a day, at dawn, noon, and dusk. The ritual was believed to ensure the continued existence of the world and the daily rising of the sun.

The Battle of the Great CatOstracon from Deir el-Medina, Book of the Dead

New Kingdom (c. 1550-1070 BCE)

In some depictions, Apophis is defeated by the 'Great Cat' (Mau), an aspect of the sun god Ra. The cat is shown cutting off the head of the serpent with a knife beneath a sacred tree (often a persea or ished tree). This is a more direct and violent depiction of the defeat of Apophis, and it may have been a popular story among the common people. The cat is a symbol of the sun god, and its ability to kill snakes makes it a natural enemy of Apophis. The sun rises, and the world is safe for another day.

Apophis is a demon or a fallen angel. — Apophis is a primordial force of chaos, not a demon in the Christian or Islamic sense. He is a god-like being, but he is not worshipped and is the enemy of the gods.

Apophis is a god of snakes. — Apophis is not a god of snakes, but a giant serpent who embodies chaos. He is not worshipped and has no cult.

Apophis is the same as Set. — While they are sometimes associated, Apophis and Set are distinct entities. Set is a complex deity who is sometimes a defender of Ra, while Apophis is the eternal enemy.

The asteroid 99942 Apophis is named after the Egyptian god because it is a threat to Earth. — The asteroid is named after the Egyptian god because it was initially thought to have a small chance of impacting Earth. The name is a reference to the god of chaos and destruction, not to any actual threat from the god.
