Who It Is

On the rooftops of old Lithuanian farmsteads, two small horse figures stand facing each other, silent guardians against the storm. These are the Ašvieniai, the divine twins who once pulled the Sun across the sky—and who still watch over the homes of the living.

Divine helpers, guardians of the home, and escorts of the Sun. They are benevolent but not extensively characterized in surviving myths.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Old Lithuanian 'ašva' (mare), from Proto-Indo-European *h₁éḱwos (horse). Cognate with Sanskrit 'aśva' and Latin 'equus'.

Pronunciation — ASH-vee-en-ay (approximate)

Aliases — Dieva dēli (Latvian), Divine Twins, Žirgeliai (little horses), Ašvieniai (Lithuanian), Usins (one of the twins, Latvian)

Ownership — Baltic (Lithuanian and Latvian)

Powers
Solar Chariot Pulling

They pull the carriage of Saulė across the sky, ensuring the sun's daily journey.

Limit: Bound to the sun's path; not independent agents in this role.

Protection of the Home

When depicted on rooftops, they are believed to protect the house from harm, especially fire and lightning.

Limit: Protective function is apotropaic, not active intervention.

Divine Twinship

As Divine Twins, they share a special bond and often act in pairs, aiding mortals and gods.

Limit: In Baltic sources, their individual powers are not elaborated.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Typically depicted as two horses, often white, or as twin male deities. In folk art, they appear as small horse figures (žirgeliai) on rooftops.

Symbols — Horses, solar chariot, rooftops, beehives, household objects. They are associated with the Sun (Saulė) and the dawn.

Domains — Sky, associated with the solar journey; also domestic spaces as protectors.

Pre-Christian and folkloric

Ašvieniai pull the Sun's carriage across the sky, bringing daylight to the world. They are often depicted as horses or horse-like beings. The sun rises and sets daily, maintaining cosmic order.
