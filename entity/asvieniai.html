Who It Is

Ašvieniai are the divine twin horsemen of Baltic mythology, the sons of Dievs (the sky god), who drive the sun-chariot of Saulė across the sky each day. Their name is cognate with the Vedic Aśvins and Greek Castor and Polydeuces — making them part of a very ancient Indo-European tradition of divine twin horsemen who rescue, heal, and guide the sun. In Latvian tradition, they are called Dieva dēli (God's sons) and are depicted riding silver and golden horses. They harness Saulė's horses at dawn and unharness them at sunset, crossing the cosmic ocean (the sea) in a silver boat each night. Ašvieniai are associated with healing, especially of wounds and blindness; with rescuing those in distress at sea; and with ensuring the fertility of horses. They are usually depicted on or above rooftops in Lithuanian folk art — the iconic wooden sculptures of twin horses on gable peaks (the 'horse on the roof') are visual representations of the Ašvieniai protecting the household. Their cult was so pervasive that Christian authorities tolerated it, and the horse-gable tradition survives today as a symbol of Lithuanian folk architecture. The twins are also associated with the dawn and dusk battles of light against darkness, representing the eternal cycle of day and night.

The Approach

On the rooftops of old Lithuanian farmsteads, two small horse figures stand facing each other, silent guardians against the storm. These are the Ašvieniai, the divine twins who once pulled the Sun across the sky—and who still watch over the homes of the living.

Etymology

From Old Lithuanian 'ašva' (mare), from Proto-Indo-European *h₁éḱwos (horse). Cognate with Sanskrit 'aśva' and Latin 'equus'.

Cultural Ownership

Baltic (Lithuanian and Latvian)

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars identify Ašvieniai with the Vedic Ashvins and Greek Dioscuri as reflexes of the Indo-European Divine Twins. The Latvian Ūsiņš is sometimes considered one of the twins, but this is debated.

Description

Appearance

Typically depicted as two horses, often white, or as twin male deities. In folk art, they appear as small horse figures (žirgeliai) on rooftops.

Symbols & Iconography

Horses, solar chariot, rooftops, beehives, household objects. They are associated with the Sun (Saulė) and the dawn.

Behaviour & Personality

Divine helpers, guardians of the home, and escorts of the Sun. They are benevolent but not extensively characterized in surviving myths.

Domains & Habitats

Sky, associated with the solar journey; also domestic spaces as protectors.

Version Differences

Latvian tradition emphasizes them as sons of Dievs (the sky god) and includes Ūsiņš as a horse god, sometimes one of the twins. Lithuanian tradition focuses on their role as the Sun's chariot pullers.

Contradictions & Disputes

Ūsiņš as one of the Divine Twins

Position A

Ūsiņš is one of the Ašvieniai, based on his role as horse god and solar charioteer.

Position A — evidence

Latvian dainas sometimes mention Ūsiņš in connection with Dieva dēli.

Position B

Ūsiņš is a separate deity, not one of the twins, as his cult has independent features.

Position B — evidence

Ūsiņš is also a fertility god of bees and horses, which may be a distinct function.

Scholarly Consensus

Unresolved; most scholars treat Ūsiņš as a related but distinct figure.

Exact nature of the twins: gods or horses?

Position A

They are divine beings who can take horse form.

Position A — evidence

Folk art often shows them as horses, but they are called 'sons of God' in Latvian.

Position B

They are purely horse symbols, not anthropomorphic deities.

Position B — evidence

In some songs, they are explicitly horses pulling the sun.

Scholarly Consensus

They are likely both: deities with horse forms.
