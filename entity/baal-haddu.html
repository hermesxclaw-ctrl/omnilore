Who It Is

Before the Hebrew prophets ever cursed it, before the goat-headed Bael of the grimoires, before Diablo II made him a 'Lord of Destruction', the name Baʿal was the everyday Canaanite word for lord — and it belonged to a storm-bull who fought the Sea, was eaten by Death, and came back when the autumn rains arrived. In a six-tablet epic written on clay at Ugarit around 1300 BCE, Baʿal loses his throne, wins it back in single combat against the ocean, badgers the high god for a palace, gets killed by the god of death, is buried by his sister and the sun, and returns to rule the rains again. He is the young, ambitious, anxious king of the gods — not inherited, but earned — and his seasonal death-and-return is the quiet engine of the eastern Mediterranean's whole agricultural year. In the Ugaritic cycle Baʿal is a young, ambitious, anxious warrior-king — a deity who wins his throne by hard single combat rather than inheriting it, and who relies on allies (his sister Anat, the craftsman Kothar, then Athirat) to secure his house. Unlike the aloof high god El, Baʿal intervenes actively in human affairs and is called upon in battle. He is shown as sometimes fearful (he dreads Mot and even considers eternal servitude to him), as mercurial, and as demanding recognition of his sovereignty, sending messengers to summon Mot to his feast and acknowledge his kingship. Powers
Storm mastery — rain, lightning, wind, thunder

Baʿal 'rides the clouds' (rkb ʿrpt), controls lightning, wind, thunder and rain, and is the source of the life-giving rains on which Levantine rain-fed agriculture depends. His return in autumn causes the storms that revive the land; his absence in summer dries it. He can make his thunder flash and his cloud thunder by command.

Limit: His climatic power is cyclical — locked to the seasonal pattern of his death/recovery; he cannot override the drought while Mot holds him.

Divine combat and kingship

Baʿal is the victorious warrior-king who defeats the sea god Yam, the 'Twisted Serpent' Tannin, 'Lotan the Fugitive Serpent' (biblical Leviathan), and the 'Mighty One with Seven Heads', and who later contends with Mot. He is 'Victorious Baʿal' and the one 'without equal'; he attains kingship (malik) over the gods.

Limit: Unlike Marduk or Yahweh, his kingship is 'limited, attained by difficult single combat and with the help of other gods', and his victories are not permanent — Mot returns.

Magic weapons (the two clubs)

The divine craftsman Kothar-wa-Khasis fashions two clubs with magical names, gives them to Baʿal, and strikes Yam himself the first two blows; Baʿal then drags Yam out and finishes him before scattering him.

Limit: The clubs require the craftsman-god's naming/crafting; Baʿal needs Kothar's aid.

Making the palace a reality (thunder-out windows)

Baʿal insists his palace be built without windows out of fear, then later opens windows by 'thundering them out'. The palace-building sequence is read by many as reflecting an autumnal festival (booths representing Ba'al's palace) essential to renewing the rains — possibly a precursor tradition to Sukkot.

Limit: Requires El's permission, Athirat's advocacy, and Kothar's craftsmanship.

Procreative power / substitute-child device

To cheat Mot, Baʿal finds a heifer in the fields, conceives a child with it, dresses the child in his robes, and offers it as a gift/doppelganger to Mot, who slays it believing it is Baʿal. This deceives death and allows Baʿal's 'resurrection'.

Limit: The substitute does not actually die in Baʿal's place in every reading; text is damaged and the sequence is partly reconstructed.

Resurrection / dying-and-rising

Baʿal dies at Mot's hands (or through the substitute), is buried by Shapash and Anat on Mount Zaphon, and returns; Anat then destroys Mot. This is the paradigmatic Near Eastern dying-and-rising/death-rebirth cycle cited by Paola Corrente as a concrete Near Eastern example of death and resurrection.

Limit: The whole 'dying-and-rising god' category is contested (see contradictions); Baʿal is often classified as a 'dying' god whose return is to the same life, or whose 'resurrection' is seasonal/rather than a triumph over death-as-final.

Leadership of the Rephaim (Baʿal Rāpiʾuma)

As the vanquisher of Mot (death), Baʿal is called Baʿal Rāpiʾuma and regarded as leader of the Rephaim (Rpum), the ancestral spirits, especially of ruling dynasties — a chthonic/ancestral-cult role alongside his storm-god identity.

Limit: The chthonic register coexists with, and is somewhat separate from, his storm-god kingship.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Baʿal was associated with the bull, the symbol of strength and fertility in Ugaritic texts (as was El), and with the thunderbolt and cloud as the storm-king. He 'rides the clouds' (rkb ʿrpt). Symbols — The storm-bull (male virility and fertility) and the lightning/cloud ('rider of the clouds'); his mace and the two magic clubs that Kothar names for him; Mount Ṣapānu/Jebel Aqra as his mountain throne; the (later) ram and two horns for Baʿal Hammon/Qarnaim. As vanquisher of the sea he was patron of sailors and sea-merchants. As vanquisher of Mot he was 'Baʿal Rāpiʾuma', leader of the Rephaim ancestral spirits.

Domains — Sky, rain, lightning, wind, storms, fertility of crops and livestock, kingship, and war. His abode is the palace on Mount Zaphon (Ṣapānu; later classical Mons Casius = Jebel Aqra, near Ugarit on the Syrian coast). He resides in El's and Athirat's household when he lacks his own palace. Accounts I
The Battle with Yam (Baʿal vs the Sea)Ugaritic Baal Cycle, tablets 1–2 (KTU 1.1–1.2)

c. 1300–1100 BCE

The sea-river god Yam (Yaw) demands kingship over the gods. El, who favors him, orders Kothar to build Yam a palace and declares Yam 'darling of El', instructing him to drive Baʿal from his throne. Yam sends envoys to demand Baʿal's surrender, whereupon Baʿal attacks them, held back by Anat and Athtart. Baʿal begins to despair at Yam's power and his 'fierce sea-creatures', but Kothar-wa-Khasis assures him of victory and fashions two magic clubs, striking Yam twice before Baʿal drags him out and kills him. Athtart tells Baʿal to scatter the enemy; he cries that Yam is dead and that he is king. Establishes Baʿal as vanquisher of the sea, patron of sailors and merchants, and heir-apparent to kingship; regarded as the prototype of the sea-battle vision in Daniel 7.

The Building of Baʿal's Palace (cosmic kingship)Ugaritic Baal Cycle, tablets 3–4 (KTU 1.3–1.4)

c. 1300–1100 BCE

Baʿal, lacking a house and court and forced to dwell in El's and Athirat's household, complains to his sister Anat. She threatens El — vowing to make his grey hair run with blood — and the earthquake of her rage exposes the high god. When this fails, Baʿal has Kothar craft gifts for Athirat, the queen-mother of the gods, to win her support. Athirat, initially wary that Baʿal has come to kill her sons, is won over by the gifts, and successfully pleads his case before El, who reluctantly grants Baʿal a house. Kothar builds his palace of cedar, brick and precious metals. Baʿal first insists it have no windows — lest his daughters escape or Yam return — then opens one by thundering it out. Enthroned, he declares that any who resist his power will be sent to Mot, and sends messengers inviting Death to his feast. Secures Baʿal's cosmic kingship and his palace on Mount Zaphon; the palace ritual is tied by scholars to a Canaanite autumnal festival (booths) perhaps precursor to Sukkot.

Accounts II
The Death and Resurrection of Baʿal (vs Mot)Ugaritic Baal Cycle, tablets 5–6 (KTU 1.5–1.6)

c. 1300–1100 BCE

Mot ('Death'), offended by Baʿal's feast invitation, threatens to break Baʿal into pieces and eat him. Baʿal confesses his fear of Mot and declares he will be Mot's servant eternally. Advised by Shapash (the sun-goddess) to find a substitute in his image, Baʿal conceives a child with a heifer and offers it as a doppelganger; Mot slays it (believing it Baʿal). Gupan and Ugar report Baʿal dead; El mourns, strewing dust on his head; Anat dresses in sackcloth, and she and Shapash bury Baʿal on Mount Zaphon. El, in a dream, learns Baʿal lives. Anat then finds Mot, seizes him, splits, winnows, burns, grinds and scatters him to the birds. Baʿal returns and reigns again. Mot recovers after seven years to demand satisfaction; the two gods fight on Mount Zaphon until Shapash warns Mot that El now favors Baʿal and will overturn his throne; Mot concedes and Baʿal rules. Makes Baʿal the paradigmatic dying-and-rising deity of the Bronze Age Near East (though the category is contested); his seasonal death/resurrection models the Levantine rain cycle.

Elijah's Contest on Mount CarmelHebrew Bible, 1 Kings 18

Traditionally 9th century BCE, redacted later

The prophet Elijah challenges the priests of Baʿal (traditionally tied to Jezebel's Phoenician worship, whether Melqart of Tyre or Baʿal Shamem) to a sacrifice contest. Baʿal's 450 prophets call on him all day to light their bull-sacrifice, to no avail; Elijah, after soaking his altar with water, calls on Yahweh, whose heavenly fire consumes the offering. The people, convinced, follow Elijah's order to slay Baʿal's prophets, after which rain comes — showing Yahweh's command over the weather, not Baʿal's. In the biblical theology, proves Baʿal is a powerless false god and Yahweh is the true lord of storm and rain; foundational text for Baʿal's negative reputation in Judaism, Christianity and Islam.

The rkb ʿrpt 'Rider of the Clouds' epithet and cross-tradition storm-god idiomUgaritic texts and biblical parallels

2nd millennium BCE (Ugaritic); biblical parallels later

Baʿal's signature epithet 'rider/mounter of the clouds' (rkb ʿrpt, cf. rkb bʿrbt in Ps 68:4) is part of a shared Northwest-Semitic and wider Near Eastern storm-god idiom, related to Zeus the 'cloud-gatherer' and Yahweh 'the rider of the heavens'. The term rkb has both equine and sexual uses. Demonstrates both the cultural kinship of storm deities across the ancient Near East and the way biblical monotheism absorbed storm-god attributes into Yahweh.

Baal is a single specific demon in Hell. — The grimoire 'Bael'/'Baal' is a late medieval/early-modern demonological corruption of the ancient Canaanite storm-god's name. Ancient Baʿal was a respected supreme deity, not a demon.

'Baal' and Moloch/Molech are the same god, and both demanded child sacrifice as a generic Canaanite practice. — Moloch/Molech is a separate sacrificial figure in the Hebrew Bible, distinct from Baʿal as storm-god; the child-sacrifice attribution is tied mainly to the disputed Carthaginian Baʿal Hammon evidence, not to the Ugaritic Baʿal.

Baal was always 'rejected' in ancient Israel; his worship was a foreign import. — The title baʿal (Lord) appears to have been among the earliest Israelite usages for the deity (theophoric names like Eshbaʿal, and possibly Yahweh-as-Baʿal), and worship of Baʿal elements coexisted with Yahwism for centuries before the prophets' rejection, in some southern traditions as compatible-or-identical with Yahweh.

The 'dying-and-rising god' Baal is direct ancient evidence for the Christian resurrection. — Modern scholarship rejects the Frazerian universal category; Baʿal's death/return is seasonal, culturally specific, and textually ambiguous, and it is not straightforward evidence for any later religion's resurrection claims.
