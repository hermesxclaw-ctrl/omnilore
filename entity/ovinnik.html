Who It Is

Somewhere behind the village, in a low wooden shed separate from every house, an old demon keeps the winter's grain — and the village's worst fear. The ovin, the East Slavic drying-barn where sheaves are toasted before they are threshed, was the one building nobody threshed on a holy day, nobody entered without crossing himself, and nobody worked past the sunset into the wind. Because the being who lived in its dark corner could set the whole settlement's store of bread alight with a thought. The Slavs called him Ovinnik — 'the barn one', Grandfather of the ovin, brother to Domovoy and Bannik — and they fed him roosters and bliny and, in the older tales, a black cock's blood, just to keep the flame in his red eye banked. If you were brave enough at New Year, or a girl courting a fortune, you put your bare hand through his window at midnight and let him touch it: warm, and he promised you

Depicted as the most aggressive and feared of the Slavic farmstead spirits. His defining behaviour is arson-by-spirit: he sets fire to the grain in the drying-house (and by extension can burn the wooden village) when the farmer threshes on a holy day, threshes at night without permission, works during high wind, fails to make the sign of the cross before entering, mocks him openly, or labours past sunset. He is exacting about the taboos of the ovin. When placated, he is a lesser guardian of stored grain; when offended, he is a destroyer — a dualistic fire-spirit whose hostility is triggered

Powers
Spontaneous arson / fire-starting (the defining power)

The Ovinnik can ignite the grain and structural wood of the ovin without a visible flame-source, burning the drying-house and — in the village tradition — the surrounding wooden settlement. The en-Wikipedia stub states he is 'prone to burning down the threshing houses by setting fire to the grain.' In the fuller ethnographic reading this is not random destruction but punitive and conditional: it happens when a farmer violates the ovin's taboos (threshing on holy days, at night without asking, during high wind, working past sunset, failing to cross oneself, mocking the spirit).

Limit: The arson is generally presented as a response to ritual transgression rather than constant hostility; a placated Ovinnik watches the grain rather than burning it. Fire was the real, constant hazard of wooden Slavic villages, and the folk belief ran parallel to the practical rule that grain-drying barns be kept outside the dwelling and not worked on rest days.

The touch-divination / fortune-telling hand

The Ovinnik determines a person's fortune by touching them. The en-Wikipedia stub records the New Year's Eve version: a warm touch means good luck and fortune, a cold touch means unhappiness. The OmniLore/folk-custom record adds the marriage-omen version: a girl would thrust her hand through the barn window at midnight and the feel of the Ovinnik's hand (warm and furry = a good husband; cold or scaly = a bad one) would predict her marital future. This is structurally the same divination-by-touch as the Bannik's (which strokes the back gently for good fortune or strikes with claws for ill).

Limit: Divination by touch is confined to set ritual moments (New Year's Eve, midnight marriage omen) and venues (the open doorway/window of the barn or bathhouse); it is not a casual, repeatable power.

Shapeshifting / animal apparition

The Ovinnik may appear as a huge black cat, a shaggy dog, or a man covered in dark fur with glowing red eyes (per the fuller folk tradition preserved in the OmniLore record). This aligns him with the household-spirit family's chthonic/animal forms — the domovoy also appears as cat, dog, bear, or a departed ancestor, often with tail and horns; the Bannik as a small naked old man or a stone/coal.

Limit: As an apparition he is generally seen only at liminal times (deep night, holy days), in or at the threshold of the ovin.

Grain-guardianship (the benevolent face)

When respected, the Ovinnik guards the stored grain of the ovin against rot, theft, and rivals, ensuring the farmstead's winter supply survives. Like the domovoy who protects 'the well-being of a kin in any of its aspects', the Ovinnik is the custodial spirit of the khleba (grain) in its drying/storage dwelling.

Limit: The guardianship becomes punitive retribution the moment the taboos are broken; the good face is conditional on correct ritual conduct.

Accounts I
The barn-burner: threshing on a holy dayEast Slavic oral tradition (bylichki); en-WP stub; Ivanits 1992

Pre-Christian to 19th-century folk belief (recorded primarily in the 19th century)

A farmer who threshes grain in the ovin on a forbidden day (a holy day, or at night without the Ovinnik's permission, or in high wind) finds the drying-house suddenly ablaze with no earthly source; the fire spreads and can consume the village's wooden buildings. The Ovinnik is understood to have set the grain alight as punishment for breaking the ovin's calendar and boundary. Loss of the year's stored grain and, in the worst readings, village-wide conflagration — the central terror the spirit personifies.

The New Year's Eve touchen-Wikipedia 'Ovinnik' stub (directly attested)

19th-century folk custom

On New Year's Eve, a household member would present themselves at the ovin and let the Ovinnik touch them. A warm touch meant good luck and fortune for the coming year; a cold touch meant unhappiness and ill fortune. The omen guided the family's expectations and ritual precautions for the year.

Accounts II
The midnight marriage omenEast Slavic (Russian-regional) folk custom; preserved in the OmniLore entity record

19th century

A girl seeking to learn what kind of husband she would receive would thrust her bare hand through the ovin's window at midnight and feel the Ovinnik's touch in the dark. Warm and furry presaged a good, prosperous husband; cold or scaly presaged a bad one. The omen shaped the girl's (and family's) expectations and was part of the divination-side of Slavic agricultural-courtship custom.

The placation feast: roosters, bliny and the black cocken-WP stub; OmniLore entity record (archaic black-cock variant)

19th-century folk ritual

Because the Ovinnik was feared as an arsonist-spirit, peasants actively worked to keep him friendly: they offered him roosters and bliny (pancakes) at the ovin, left bread at the threshold, and — in more archaic recorded tradition — offered a black cock's blood or a rooster's head. On his nameday (linked to the Feast of the Elevation of the Cross, 27 September), farmers brought special offerings and refrained from threshing. Ritual food-offerings and the nameday rest-day kept the grain safe and the ovin from burning; this is the practical, agricultural-seremony face that balanced the terror.

The Ovinnik is just another name for the Domovoy (house spirit). — They are separate beings of different spaces: the domovoy rules the dwelling, the Ovinnik the free-standing ovin (grain-drying barn). The Ovinnik is more dangerous, fire-linked, and rarely appears in the house.

The Ovinnik is a random arsonist with no rules. — His burning is conditionally triggered by specific taboo-breaks (holy-day or night threshing, high wind, missing the sign of the cross, mockery, working past sunset) and he can be an active grain-protector when appeased.

His touch-omen is strictly a New Year's Eve fortune-telling. — The touch-oracle has multiple ritual slots, including the midnight marriage-omen (hand through the barn window), and is structurally shared with the Bannik.

Every 'Ovinnik' in fiction refers to the folk spirit. — A Marvel Comics 'Gennedy Ovinnik' is a human Iron-Man-style character who coincidentally shares the surname; Slavic-spirit-named monsters in franchises (cf. The Witcher's kikimora) are frequently unrelated to the folklore.

The Ovinnik's appearance (black cat / furred man) is directly attested in the English literature. — The primary English stub gives no appearance; the black animal/ember-eye image comes from the broader Russian/Ukrainian folk-belief scholarship and must be read as the reconstructed-rich portrait, not a stub-confirmed fact.
