Who It Is

Before the sun goddess Amaterasu's descendants ever set foot on the islands of Japan, there was another ruler — a wandering, murdered, resurrected god who was tricked, tested, and ultimately given the world. His name was Ōkuninushi, the 'Great Land Master,' and his story is a brutal, romantic, and deeply human epic that forms the hidden foundation of Japanese mythology. He is the god who was betrayed by his brothers, who outwitted a storm god in the underworld, and who won a kingdom not by force alone, but by his compassion for a suffering white hare. He is the reason the most powerful shrine in Japan is built on a remote coast, and why a jolly, rotund god of fortune sits in kitchens and shops today. He is the god who lost the world, and in doing so, gained everything.

Ōkuninushi is portrayed as a complex figure: initially a victim of his brothers' jealousy, he is passive and suffering. He endures betrayal, murder, and humiliation. However, he is also resourceful, brave, and compassionate. He wins the love of the goddess Suseri-hime through his cleverness. His defining trait is his role as a 'culture hero' who pacifies the land, establishing order and agriculture. He is a god of healing, marriage, and good fortune, reflecting his later popular role as a benevolent, approachable deity.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Ōkuninushi (大国主) literally means 'Great Land Master' or 'Master of the Great Land.' His earlier name, Ōnamuchi (大己貴), is more obscure but is often interpreted as 'Great Name Possessor' or 'Great August Name.' The name Yachihoko (八千矛) means 'Eight Thousand Spears,' a reference to his legendary spear

Pronunciation — OH-koo-nee-NOO-shee. The 'Ō' is a long vowel, held for two beats. In Japanese: おおくにぬし.

Aliases — Ōnamuchi, Ōa-na-muchi, Ōkuni-nushi-no-kami, Ōnamuchi-no-kami, Yachihoko-no-kami, Ōkuni-toko-tachi-no-kami

Powers
Land-Pacification (Kuni-zukuri)

His primary power is the ability to subdue and pacify the land, making it habitable and productive. He does this not through brute force alone, but through a combination of physical strength, magical knowledge, and cleverness. He is the archetypal 'culture hero' who transforms a wild land into a civilized one.

Limit: He is not a supreme deity; he is ultimately subordinate to the heavenly deities (amatsukami), who order him to cede the land. His power is limited to the earthly realm.

Flight and Escape

He possesses the ability to escape from dangerous situations, including the underworld of Yomi. He flees Susanoo's court, and when Susanoo pursues him, he uses a magical item (a peach or a rock) to create a barrier. His ability to escape is tied to his cleverness and his access to magical objects.

Limit: He is not invulnerable; he is killed by his brothers and must be resurrected by his mother.

Magical Knowledge and Sorcery

He gains magical knowledge and power from his time in Yomi, learning from Susanoo. This includes the use of a magical sword and the ability to command the forces of nature. His power is often associated with the hidden, esoteric arts.

Limit: The exact nature of his sorcery is not detailed in the myths.

Divine Authority and Blessing

As the ruler of Izumo, he has the power to bless his worshippers with prosperity, good harvests, and marital happiness. He is a god of healing and is invoked for cures. In his Daikoku form, he grants wealth and good fortune.

Limit: His blessings are not absolute; they are mediated by the will of the heavenly deities.

Shapeshifting (implied)

While not explicitly described as a shapeshifter, his ability to escape and his association with the unseen world suggest a fluidity of form. In some local legends, he appears in the form of a white hare or other animals.

Limit: Not a central part of his myth.

Accounts I
The White Hare of InabaKojiki, Book 1, Section 22

Mythic

Ōkuninushi, then called Ōnamuchi, is traveling with his eighty brothers. They encounter a naked, skinned white hare on the shore. The brothers cruelly trick the hare, telling it to wash in salt water and dry in the wind, causing it immense pain. Ōnamuchi, seeing the hare's suffering, tells it to wash in fresh water and roll in cattail pollen. The hare is healed and reveals itself to be the deity of Inaba, prophesying that Ōnamuchi will win the hand of the princess Yagami-hime, even though his brothers are also pursuing her. The hare's prophecy comes true. This story establishes Ōkuninushi's compassion and sets him apart from his cruel brothers. It is also the etiological myth for the place name 'Inaba.'

The Murder and Resurrection of ŌnamuchiKojiki, Book 1, Section 23

Mythic

Driven by jealousy, Ōnamuchi's eighty brothers plot to kill him. They lead him into the mountains and, at a red-clay hill, they split open a boar, roast it, and trick him into rolling it down the hill, crushing him to death. His mother, the goddess Suseri-hime (in some versions, his mother is named as a different deity), sends the goddess Kisagai-hime and the goddess Umugi-hime to resurrect him. They scrape the shell of a clam and use the powder to restore him to life. Ōnamuchi is resurrected but is forced to flee to the land of Yomi to escape his brothers' wrath.

Accounts II
The Descent to Yomi and the Trials at Susanoo's CourtKojiki, Book 1, Section 24

Mythic

Fleeing his brothers, Ōnamuchi travels to the land of Yomi (the underworld) to seek the aid of the great deity Susanoo. At Susanoo's palace, he falls in love with Susanoo's daughter, Suseri-hime. Susanoo, testing him, sets him a series of impossible tasks: sleeping in a snake-filled room, a centipede-and-bee-filled room, and shooting an arrow into a vast plain. With Suseri-hime's secret help, he survives each trial. Finally, Susanoo sends him to retrieve an arrow, and when he does, Susanoo sets the field on fire. Ōnamuchi escapes by hiding in a mouse hole. He then steals Susanoo's sword, bow, and arrows, and flees back to the surface world with Suseri-hime. Ōnamuchi gains Susanoo's blessing (after being pursued and negotiating a truce) and returns to the surface world with the magical weapons and Suseri-hime. He is now empowered to defeat his brothers and claim his sovereignty.

The Conquest of Izumo and the Kuni-yuzuri (Transfer of the Land)Kojiki, Book 1, Sections 25-27; Nihon Shoki, Book 1

Mythic

Returning from Yomi, Ōnamuchi defeats his brothers, using a magical wind or a stone to crush them. He then establishes his rule over Izumo. He later marries several other goddesses, including Suseri-hime and Yagami-hime. The narrative then shifts to the heavenly deities. They decide that the earthly realm must be governed by their descendant, Ninigi. They send messengers to demand that Ōkuninushi cede the land. After a series of failed negotiations (in the Kojiki, the god Takemikazuchi arrives and threatens Ōkuninushi with his sword), Ōkuninushi agrees to yield the visible world, but asks for a magnificent palace to be built for him in Izumo, where he will remain as a hidden deity, ruling the unseen world of spirits and magic. This myth establishes the divine mandate for the Yamato imperial line (descendants of Ninigi) to rule Japan. It also explains the unique status of Izumo Taisha and Ōkuninushi's role as a hidden, spiritual ruler. He is not defeated; he is negotiated with and given a prestigious retreat.

Ōkuninushi is a 'minor' or 'regional' god who was easily defeated by the heavenly deities. — He is a major deity who was the sovereign of the earthly realm. The kuni-yuzuri is a negotiated transfer, not a defeat. He is given a magnificent palace and remains a powerful, hidden ruler. His worship at Izumo Taisha is one of the most important in Japan.

The White Hare of Inaba is just a cute animal story. — The story is a foundational myth that establishes Ōkuninushi's compassion, his separation from his brothers, and his destiny as a ruler. It is also an etiological myth for the place name Inaba.

Ōkuninushi is the same as Daikokuten. — They are syncretized, but they are not the same. Daikokuten is a Buddhist deity (Mahākāla) who was identified with Ōkuninushi in the medieval period. The fusion created a new, popular god of wealth, but the ancient myth of Ōkuninushi is distinct.

He is a god of war and conquest. — He is a 'pacifier' of the land, but his methods are not purely martial. He is also a god of magic, healing, and marriage. His later form as Daikoku is a god of wealth and household prosperity.
