Okuninushi, the great master of the land, who rules the unseen arts of medicine and weaves the bonds of love.

Who It Is

Okuninushi ('Great Land Master') is the kami of medicine, sorcery, and terrestrial dominion who ruled the land before the heavenly kami (Amaterasu's line) took over. He is the central figure of the Izumo cycle of myths, a distinct mythological tradition from the Yamato tradition centered on Amaterasu. Son of Susanoo (or, in some versions, of other kami), Okuninushi demonstrated compassion and wisdom from childhood. In the Hare of Inaba myth, he helped a wounded white hare (actually a deity in disguise) while his eighty brothers (the Yasogami) mistreated it — the hare prophesied that Okuninushi would marry the princess Yakami-hime. He survived multiple attempts on his life by his jealous brothers by receiving help from Susanoo's daughter Suseri-hime and by passing Susanoo's tests. With the help of the dwarf deity Sukunabikona, Okuninushi developed medicine, agriculture, and magic. When the heavenly kami (Amaterasu) sent messengers (first the ancestors of the Izumo clan, then Ninigi) to demand that Okuninushi hand over the land, he eventually agreed in exchange for a great shrine (Izumo Taisha) built in his honor. He remains the lord of the unseen world (medicine and magic) and presides over marriage (the kami of en-musubi, the binding of relationships).

Attested in Kojiki / Nihon Shoki / Izumo mythologyEra Asuka to Heian Period (c. 600-800 CE)

Form

No canonical physical description exists in the Kojiki or Nihon Shoki; he is a god, and his physical form is not described. In later art, he is often depicted as Daikoku, a jovial, rotund figure seated on bales of rice, holding a mallet (uchide no kozuchi) that grants wishes and a large sack of treasures. He is sometimes shown with a mouse or rats, who are his messengers. In the myths, he is a wandering, suffering hero, but his physical appearance is left to the imagination.

Powers

Land-Pacification (Kuni-zukuri) His primary power is the ability to subdue and pacify the land, making it habitable and productive. He does this not through brute force alone, but through a combination of physical strength, magical knowledge, and cleverness. He is the archetypal 'culture hero' who transforms a wild land into a civilized one. Attested in both the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki. He is not a supreme deity; he is ultimately subordinate to the heavenly deities (amatsukami), who order him to cede the land. His power is limited to the earthly realm. None significant.

Flight and Escape He possesses the ability to escape from dangerous situations, including the underworld of Yomi. He flees Susanoo's court, and when Susanoo pursues him, he uses a magical item (a peach or a rock) to create a barrier. His ability to escape is tied to his cleverness and his access to magical objects. Attested in the Kojiki. He is not invulnerable; he is killed by his brothers and must be resurrected by his mother. None significant.

Magical Knowledge and Sorcery He gains magical knowledge and power from his time in Yomi, learning from Susanoo. This includes the use of a magical sword and the ability to command the forces of nature. His power is often associated with the hidden, esoteric arts. Attested in the Kojiki. The exact nature of his sorcery is not detailed in the myths. None significant.

Divine Authority and Blessing As the ruler of Izumo, he has the power to bless his worshippers with prosperity, good harvests, and marital happiness. He is a god of healing and is invoked for cures. In his Daikoku form, he grants wealth and good fortune. Attested in later worship and syncretic beliefs. His blessings are not absolute; they are mediated by the will of the heavenly deities. None significant.

Shapeshifting (implied) While not explicitly described as a shapeshifter, his ability to escape and his association with the unseen world suggest a fluidity of form. In some local legends, he appears in the form of a white hare or other animals. Implied in local folklore and later traditions. Not a central part of his myth. None significant.

Accounts

Ōkuninushi, then called Ōnamuchi, is traveling with his eighty brothers. They encounter a naked, skinned white hare on the shore. The brothers cruelly trick the hare, telling it to wash in salt water and dry in the wind, causing it immense pain. Ōnamuchi, seeing the hare's suffering, tells it to wash in fresh water and roll in cattail pollen. The hare is healed and reveals itself to be the deity of Inaba, prophesying that Ōnamuchi will win the hand of the princess Yagami-hime, even though his brothers are also pursuing her.

Driven by jealousy, Ōnamuchi's eighty brothers plot to kill him. They lead him into the mountains and, at a red-clay hill, they split open a boar, roast it, and trick him into rolling it down the hill, crushing him to death. His mother, the goddess Suseri-hime (in some versions, his mother is named as a different deity), sends the goddess Kisagai-hime and the goddess Umugi-hime to resurrect him. They scrape the shell of a clam and use the powder to restore him to life.

Fleeing his brothers, Ōnamuchi travels to the land of Yomi (the underworld) to seek the aid of the great deity Susanoo. At Susanoo's palace, he falls in love with Susanoo's daughter, Suseri-hime. Susanoo, testing him, sets him a series of impossible tasks: sleeping in a snake-filled room, a centipede-and-bee-filled room, and shooting an arrow into a vast plain. With Suseri-hime's secret help, he survives each trial. Finally, Susanoo sends him to retrieve an arrow, and when he does, Susanoo sets the field on fire. Ōnamuchi escapes by hiding in a mouse hole. He then steals Susanoo's sword, bow, and arrows, and flees back to the surface world with Suseri-hime.

Returning from Yomi, Ōnamuchi defeats his brothers, using a magical wind or a stone to crush them. He then establishes his rule over Izumo. He later marries several other goddesses, including Suseri-hime and Yagami-hime. The narrative then shifts to the heavenly deities. They decide that the earthly realm must be governed by their descendant, Ninigi. They send messengers to demand that Ōkuninushi cede the land. After a series of failed negotiations (in the Kojiki, the god Takemikazuchi arrives and threatens Ōkuninushi with his sword), Ōkuninushi agrees to yield the visible world, but asks for a magnificent palace to be built for him in Izumo, where he will remain as a hidden deity, ruling the unseen world of spirits and magic.

Field Notes

☘ The story of the White Hare of Inaba is one of the most famous and beloved myths in Japan, and the hare is a popular symbol of kindness and compassion.

☘ Izumo Taisha's main hall (honden) is said to have once been the tallest wooden structure in Japan, its massive pillars dwarfing even the great temples of Nara.

☘ Every year in the tenth month of the lunar calendar, all the gods of Japan are said to gather at Izumo Taisha for the Kamiari-sai (Festival of the Gods' Presence). During this month, the rest of Japan is said to have no gods.

☘ The mallet of Daikoku, the uchide no kozuchi, is a symbol of good fortune and is said to be able to grant wishes.

☘ Ōkuninushi is sometimes called the 'god of the invisible world' (kakuriyo), ruling over spirits, magic, and the unseen forces of nature.

Content Notes

The central plot of his early life is the repeated, murderous jealousy of his brothers. They lead him into the mountains, trick him into rolling a boulder down a hill onto himself, and crush him to death. This is a graphic and brutal act of sibling violence.

This motif is often interpreted as a mythic reflection of the violent struggles for power among early Japanese clans. It is a foundational narrative of suffering and resurrection.

Kojiki, Book 1, Section 23

The White Hare of Inaba is skinned alive by a crocodile (or shark) and is then cruelly tricked by Ōkuninushi's brothers into bathing in salt water, which causes him immense, searing pain. The story is told with a level of vivid detail about the creature's agony.

The hare's suffering is a key element of the narrative, highlighting the cruelty of the brothers and the compassion of Ōkuninushi. It is not gratuitous; it serves a moral purpose.

Kojiki, Book 1, Section 22

Scholarly Debates
Parentage
Ōkuninushi is the son of Susanoo (Kojiki). ⇌ Ōkuninushi is a descendant of Susanoo, but not his direct son (some Nihon Shoki variants).
Evidence for A: Kojiki, Book 1, Section 22.
Evidence for B: Nihon Shoki, Book 1, various passages.
The Nature of the Kuni-yuzuri
Ōkuninushi is forced to cede the land through intimidation (Kojiki). ⇌ Ōkuninushi willingly cedes the land, having fulfilled his role (Nihon Shoki variants).
Evidence for A: The dramatic confrontation with Takemikazuchi in the Kojiki.
Evidence for B: Nihon Shoki accounts where he departs peacefully.
His Role as a 'Suffering God'
He is a genuinely tragic figure, a victim of fate and his brothers' cruelty. ⇌ His suffering is a narrative device to highlight his eventual triumph and his strength of character.
Evidence for A: The graphic details of his murder and the trials in Yomi.
Evidence for B: His cleverness and resourcefulness in overcoming each trial, which suggests he is an active agent in his own salvation.
What We Don't Know

The Nihon Shoki's variant accounts need to be read in full to detail every difference. The specific iconography of Daikoku and his development in the medieval period requires a dedicated study. The local legends and rituals at Izumo Taisha, including the Kamiari-sai (the festival when all gods gather at Izumo), are not fully detailed here. A deeper look at the 'suffering god' comparative mythology (e.g., parallels with figures like Odin or Christ) would be valuable for a comparative section. The role of his other wives and children is underdeveloped.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Ōkuninushi as the 'CEO of the Visible World' who is forced into a hostile takeover by the 'Board of Heavenly Directors.' He doesn't fight the inevitable; he negotiates a golden parachute — a massive, beautiful palace in Izumo where he remains the hidden, spiritual ruler. He's the god who lost the battle for the surface world but won the war for the soul of Japan. His journey from a naive, suffering youth to a wise, powerful, and compassionate ruler is the archetypal 'hero's journey' of Japanese myth.
