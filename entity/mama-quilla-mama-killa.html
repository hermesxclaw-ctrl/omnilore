Who It Is

In the moonlit highlands of the Andes, the Inca saw not a dead rock but the face of a queen: Mama Quilla, the Mother Moon, whose silver tears fell as gentle rain and whose jealous embrace left dark marks on her luminous body. To the Inca, she was the third power of the cosmos, the guardian of women and the keeper of time, a goddess whose temple gleamed with a silver disc the size of a wall. But when the moon dimmed in eclipse, they feared a beast was devouring her, and the world itself trembled on the edge of eternal night.

Protective of women, guardian of marriage and menstrual cycles. Cried tears of silver. In myth, she squeezed a fox against her, producing dark spots on the moon, indicating a jealous or possessive nature.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Quechua: 'Mama' (mother) + 'Killa' (moon). The term 'quilla' is possibly a borrowing from the Puquina language, explaining similar words for moon in Quechua, Aymara, and Mapuche.

Pronunciation — [ˈmama ˈkiʎa]

Aliases — Mama Killa, Mother Moon

Ownership — Inca Empire (pre-Columbian South America), also venerated by coastal communities such as the Chimú.

Lunar control

She is the goddess of the moon, controlling its phases and eclipses. Lunar eclipses were seen as her being attacked by an animal.

Limit: Vulnerable to animal attacks during eclipses, requiring human intervention to scare away the beast.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Envisaged as a beautiful human woman. In iconography, represented by a silver disc covering an entire wall in her temple, symbolizing the moon's light.

Symbols — Silver disc, the moon itself, tears of silver (possibly symbolizing rain or dew).

Domains — The moon, the night sky, the lunar calendar, marriage, women's cycles, protector of women.

Accounts I
The Fox and the Moon SpotsOral tradition, recorded in colonial chronicles

Pre-Columbian

A fox fell in love with Mama Quilla because of her beauty. He rose into the sky to be with her, but she squeezed him against her, producing the dark patches on the moon. The dark spots on the moon are explained.

Accounts II
Lunar Eclipse RitualBernabé Cobo, Historia del Nuevo Mundo

Pre-Columbian and post-conquest

During a lunar eclipse, the Inca believed that an animal (mountain lion or serpent) was attacking Mama Quilla. To scare it away, they threw weapons, gestured, and made as much noise as possible. If the animal succeeded, the world would be left in darkness. The ritual was performed to save the moon. After the Spanish conquest, the natives continued the tradition, and the Spanish used their ability to predict eclipses to gain respect.
