Who They Are

Mama Quilla is the Inca moon goddess, the wife of Inti and mother of the stars. She governed the calendar, the passage of time, and the cycles of women. Her temple (Mama Quilla Huasi) at Coricancha was covered in silver — the metal associated with her — and her waxing and waning phases determined the timing of Inca festivals.

The Approach

In the moonlit highlands of the Andes, the Inca saw not a dead rock but the face of a queen: Mama Quilla, the Mother Moon, whose silver tears fell as gentle rain and whose jealous embrace left dark marks on her luminous body. To the Inca, she was the third power of the cosmos, the guardian of women and the keeper of time, a goddess whose temple gleamed with a silver disc the size of a wall. But when the moon dimmed in eclipse, they feared a beast was devouring her, and the world itself trembled on the edge of eternal night.

Content advisory. Contains descriptions of lunar eclipse rituals involving noise-making and weapon-throwing, and references to Spanish colonial exploitation of indigenous beliefs.
Identity

Mama Quilla (Mama Killa)

Pronunciation [ˈmama ˈkiʎa]

Known As

Mama KillaMother Moon

Etymology

Quechua: 'Mama' (mother) + 'Killa' (moon). The term 'quilla' is possibly a borrowing from the Puquina language, explaining similar words for moon in Quechua, Aymara, and Mapuche.

Cultural Ownership

Inca Empire (pre-Columbian South America), also venerated by coastal communities such as the Chimú.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars propose a connection with the Puquina-speaking elite of the Inca court. The exact nature of her relationship to other lunar deities in the Andes (e.g., Cuyen) is debated.

Description

Appearance

Envisaged as a beautiful human woman. In iconography, represented by a silver disc covering an entire wall in her temple, symbolizing the moon's light.

Symbols & Iconography

Silver disc, the moon itself, tears of silver (possibly symbolizing rain or dew).

Behaviour & Personality

Protective of women, guardian of marriage and menstrual cycles. Cried tears of silver. In myth, she squeezed a fox against her, producing dark spots on the moon, indicating a jealous or possessive nature.

Domains & Habitats

The moon, the night sky, the lunar calendar, marriage, women's cycles, protector of women.

Version Differences

Some coastal communities, including the Chimú, viewed her as more important than Inti. After the Ichma joined the Inca empire, she became the mother of their deity Pacha Kamaq, an adaptation of local beliefs.

Lunar Eclipse Ritual

Source Bernabé Cobo, Historia del Nuevo MundoCulture IncaPeriod Pre-Columbian and post-conquest

Summary

During a lunar eclipse, the Inca believed that an animal (mountain lion or serpent) was attacking Mama Quilla. To scare it away, they threw weapons, gestured, and made as much noise as possible. If the animal succeeded, the world would be left in darkness.

Participants

Mama Quilla

Inca people

Animal (mountain lion or serpent)

Consequences

The ritual was performed to save the moon. After the Spanish conquest, the natives continued the tradition, and the Spanish used their ability to predict eclipses to gain respect.

Variants

The attacking animal is sometimes identified as a serpent.

Eclipse rituals

Source Bernabé Cobo, Historia del Nuevo Mundo

Description

The Inca would throw weapons and make noise during lunar eclipses to scare away the animal attacking Mama Quilla. This could be seen as a form of ritual aggression, though directed at a supernatural beast.

Scholarly Context

This practice was recorded by Spanish chroniclers and was part of a broader Andean tradition of ritual responses to celestial phenomena.

Spanish exploitation

Source Source material (Wikipedia), based on colonial accounts

Description

After the Inca converted to Catholicism, they continued the eclipse rituals. The Spanish used their ability to predict eclipses to gain great respect from the natives, which they exploited to strengthen their control.

Scholarly Context

This illustrates the colonial dynamics where indigenous beliefs were manipulated for political ends.

Cultural Significance

Mama Quilla was the most important female deity in the Inca state pantheon. The moon was understood as the regulator of all natural cycles, and the Inca calendar (which combined lunar and solar calculations) was maintained by the priests of Mama Quilla. The Coya (empress, the Sapa Inca's principal wife) was considered the living representative of Mama Quilla on earth, just as the Sapa Inca represented Inti. The most important temple of Mama Quilla outside Cusco was on the Island of the Moon (Coati) in Lake Titicaca, where a convent of 'Chosen Women' (Aclla) served the goddess.
