Bachué is the mother goddess of the Muisca religion, the primordial woman who emerged from the sacred lagoon of Iguaque holding a three-year-old child by the hand. She is the ancestor of all humanity, the teacher of civilization, and the divine mother who returned to the waters after completing her earthly mission — transforming into a serpent.

The Approach

From the dark waters of Lake Iguaque, a woman emerged carrying an infant—the first mother of humanity, whose story still echoes in the mountains of Colombia.

Content advisory. Contains themes of incest (mother-son union) and transformation into serpents, as recorded in Muisca mythology.
Identity

Bachué

Pronunciation bah-CHWAY (approximate Spanish pronunciation)

Known As

FurachoguaBachueBachué (Chibcha: 'the one with the naked breast')

Etymology

The name Bachué is derived from the Chibcha language, meaning 'the one with the naked breast' or 'the woman with the bare breast.' The alternative name Furachogua means 'the good woman.'

Cultural Ownership

Muisca (Chibcha) culture, indigenous people of the Altiplano Cundiboyacense in present-day Colombia.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars have compared Bachué to other earth/mother goddesses in Mesoamerican and Andean traditions, but these are speculative and not directly attested. There is no evidence of a direct equivalent in other Chibchan groups.

Description

Appearance

Bachué is described as a beautiful woman, often depicted with bare breasts, symbolizing motherhood and fertility. In some artistic representations, she is shown with a child in her arms. No detailed physical description survives in primary sources.

Symbols & Iconography

Associated with water, lakes, serpents, fertility, and motherhood. The serpent is a key symbol, as she transforms into a serpent at the end of her earthly life. In modern Colombian art, she is often depicted as a maternal figure with indigenous features.

Behaviour & Personality

Bachué is a nurturing and generative figure, the mother of humanity. She is also a guide, sometimes returning from the underworld to advise her people. Her actions are purposeful, focused on populating the earth and establishing social order.

Domains & Habitats

Lake Iguaque (in present-day Boyacá, Colombia) is her primary sacred site. She is also associated with the underworld (the realm of the dead) and with the earth itself as a mother goddess.

Version Differences

Primary chronicler Simón provides the most detailed account. Later retellings vary in details, such as the name of her husband (sometimes called a 'parrot god') and the exact nature of her return. Some modern interpretations emphasize her role as a fertility goddess, while others highlight her connection to the underworld.

Powers & Abilities

Creation of humanity

Source Version Pedro Simón's account

Description

Bachué gave birth to the human race, populating the earth. She emerged from the lake with a child who became her husband, and together they had many children who spread across the land.

Limitations

None recorded; this is a foundational myth.

Contradictions

No direct contradictions in primary sources, but the exact number of children and the process of population spread are not detailed.

Transformation into serpents

Source Version Pedro Simón's account

Description

After fulfilling her role, Bachué and her husband transformed into serpents and returned to the sacred lagoon, symbolizing the cycle of life and death.

Limitations

This transformation is a one-time event, not a recurring power.

Contradictions

Some versions say they became snakes, others say they submerged into the lake without transformation; the serpent motif is consistent.

Guidance from the underworld

Source Version Pedro Simón's account

Description

Bachué is believed to sometimes return from the underworld to guide her people, acting as a protector and counselor.

Limitations

Not a constant presence; she appears only when needed.

Contradictions

The nature of her guidance is not elaborated in primary sources.

Stories & Myths

The Emergence of Bachué and the Origin of Humanity

Source Pedro Simón, Noticias Historiales (1626)Culture Muisca (Chibcha)Period Pre-Columbian, recorded in early 17th century

Summary

Bachué emerged from the waters of Lake Iguaque holding a baby boy. The child grew to become her husband, and together they had many children, who populated the earth. After achieving this, they transformed into serpents and returned to the lake, where they continue to reside. The Muisca believed Bachué sometimes returned to guide them.

Participants

Bachué

Her husband (sometimes called the parrot god)

Consequences

The establishment of human society and the sacredness of Lake Iguaque as a pilgrimage site.

Variants

Some versions emphasize the incestuous nature of the union, while others downplay it. The identity of the husband varies; some sources call him a parrot god, others simply 'her son.'

Dark History
Content advisory. The myth involves incest between mother and son, which may be disturbing to some readers. This is presented as a mythological element, not a historical practice.

Incestuous union

Source Pedro Simón's account

Description

Bachué's son becomes her husband, implying an incestuous relationship. This is a common motif in creation myths, but it is a sensitive topic.

Scholarly Context

Scholars interpret this as a symbolic representation of primordial unity and the necessity of reproduction, not as a reflection of actual Muisca social practices.

Cultural Significance

Bachué is the most beloved figure in Muisca mythology — the divine mother who gave birth to the people and taught them everything they needed to know. The lagoon of Iguaque was second only to the sacred lagoon of Guatavita (where the El Dorado ceremony took place) in Muisca religious importance. Bachué's transformation into a serpent at the end of her life connects her to the earth, the water, and the cycle of life and death. In modern Colombia, Bachué is a symbol of indigenous identity and the sacred connection between the Muisca people and their ancestral lands. Her myth shares striking parallels with other South American creation stories involving a primordial couple emerging from water.
