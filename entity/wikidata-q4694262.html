Form

No physical description survives. As a war deity, she would likely have been depicted in Roman-Celtic syncretic style — possibly armed, perhaps with a spear or shield, or in the guise of a warrior goddess. Roman-period depictions of Celtic war deities often borrowed from Minerva or Bellona imagery, but no confirmed depiction of Agrona exists.

Powers

Battle-prowess and combat As a war goddess, she would have been invoked for victory in battle, martial skill, and possibly the protection of warriors in combat. Reconstructed from name etymology and Romano-Celtic comparative religion No direct attestation of specific powers; all are inferred from her name and cultural context. None — evidence too sparse for contradiction.

Bestowal of victory or defeat War deities in Celtic tradition often determined the outcome of battles, granting victory to one side and slaughter to the other. Comparative — parallels with Mórrígan, Nantosuelta, Andraste Inferred, not directly attested for Agrona. None.

Behavior & Habitat

Unknown. By etymology, she is a goddess of battle and slaughter — likely a deity who presided over the chaos and violence of warfare, possibly with tutelary functions for warriors. Her role may have included protection of a specific tribe or warband, though the inscription gives no cultic detail.

Accounts

An unnamed devotee (likely a soldier or officer stationed at the frontier fort) erected an altar to Agrona, fulfilling a vow. The circumstances — whether in thanks for survival in battle, recovery from illness, or some other petition — are lost to history. The inscription is the sole surviving trace of her cult.

In the Fourth Branch, Agrona is named as the mother of Gwydion and Gilfaethwy, two central figures in the complex tale of Math, King of Gwynedd. She plays no active role in the narrative — she is a genealogical anchor, a name in a lineage. Whether this Agrona is the same as the Romano-British goddess is debated; the name's rarity makes a connection tantalizing but unproven.

Scholarly Debates
Identity: Romano-British goddess vs. Welsh literary figure
Agrona of the inscription and Agrona of the Mabinogion are the same deity, surviving across centuries of tradition. ⇌ The two are unrelated — the name coincidence is either accidental or the Welsh figure is a literary invention drawing on a folk memory of a name, not a living goddess.
Evidence for A: The name is extremely rare; etymological consistency; Celtic tradition of genealogical survival of deities as ancestors.
Evidence for B: Chronological gap of over 800 years; no intermediate attestations; the Mabinogion's genealogical figures are often purel
What We Don't Know

The single most significant gap is the absence of any second attestation of Agrona — no other inscriptions, no coin evidence, no literary reference in classical sources. The dedicator's name and the specific reason for the vow are lost. The connection to the Mabinogion figure remains unresolved. No archaeological context for the altar's original placement is firmly established (it was found in a secondary context). No image or depiction is known. Her cult practices, priesthood, and regional scope are entirely unknown. Modern reception is sparse and largely confined to niche reconstructionist circles and occasional fantasy name-drops.

A way to hold it in your mind

Agrona is the archaeological equivalent of a single photograph in an empty archive. Imagine finding one business card on a battlefield — no other trace of the person, no records, no letters, just a name and a title. That's Agrona. She's not a fully fleshed-out goddess with myths and temples; she's a whisper from a Roman frontier post, a name carved in stone by someone who believed she could tip the scales of battle. She's the patron saint of fragmentary evidence — a reminder that history is full of figures who exist in a single line, a single stone, a single moment of devotion. For every well-documented deity like Jupiter or Mars, there are hundreds of Agronas — names preserved by accident, gods who almost didn't survive at all.
