Who It Is

One stone. A name. A goddess of slaughter hiding in plain sight on the Roman frontier. Agrona exists in the archaeological record as a single fragment of carved limestone — a vow made by a soldier on the edge of the empire, a prayer for victory or survival that outlived its maker by eighteen centuries. She is a ghost of the Romano-British borderlands: a Celtic deity wearing a Latin inscription, a war-goddess whose name means 'battle' and whose story is almost entirely a matter of what we cannot see. In an age of information, she is a goddess of absence — and that absence is precisely what makes her fascinating.

Unknown. By etymology, she is a goddess of battle and slaughter — likely a deity who presided over the chaos and violence of warfare, possibly with tutelary functions for warriors. Her role may have included protection of a specific tribe or warband, though the inscription gives no cultic detail.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No physical description survives. As a war deity, she would likely have been depicted in Roman-Celtic syncretic style — possibly armed, perhaps with a spear or shield, or in the guise of a warrior goddess. Roman-period depictions of Celtic war deities often borrowed from Minerva or Bellona imagery, but no confirmed depiction of Agrona exists.

Symbols — No attested symbols. By cultural context, spears, shields, swords, and possibly severed heads (a common Celtic war motif) would be plausible, but none are attested for Agrona specifically.

Domains — Warfare, battle, slaughter, possibly sovereignty-through-violence. The name itself is her primary domain.

Accounts I
The Vindolanda VowRIB 2044 — dedicatory inscription

2nd-3rd century CE

An unnamed devotee (likely a soldier or officer stationed at the frontier fort) erected an altar to Agrona, fulfilling a vow. The circumstances — whether in thanks for survival in battle, recovery from illness, or some other petition — are lost to history. The inscription is the sole surviving trace of her cult. The altar survived to become the only evidence of her worship, preserved in the archaeological record of Roman Britain.

Accounts II
The Mabinogion GenealogyMath fab Mathonwy, Fourth Branch of the Mabinogion

13th-14th century manuscripts; earlier oral tradition

In the Fourth Branch, Agrona is named as the mother of Gwydion and Gilfaethwy, two central figures in the complex tale of Math, King of Gwynedd. She plays no active role in the narrative — she is a genealogical anchor, a name in a lineage. Whether this Agrona is the same as the Romano-British goddess is debated; the name's rarity makes a connection tantalizing but unproven. Provides a potential — though contested — link between the Romano-British deity and Welsh mythological tradition.

Identity: Romano-British goddess vs. Welsh literary figureAgrona of the inscription and Agrona of the Mabinogion are the same deity, surviving across centuries of tradition.The two are unrelated — the name coincidence is either accidental or the Welsh figure is a literary invention drawing on a folk memory of a name, not a living goddess.

Agrona was a major Celtic goddess with a widespread cult. — She is attested by exactly ONE inscription. She may have been a local or tribal deity of modest scope, or a name for a broader war-goddess concept. We cannot reconstruct a 'major cult' from a single stone.

Agrona is the same as the Irish Mórrígan. — They share a thematic domain (war, slaughter) and a possible Indo-European etymological root, but they are from different Celtic branches (Brittonic vs. Goidelic) with no evidence of shared cult or identity.

The Mabinogion's Agrona proves a continuous Welsh worship of the goddess. — The Mabinogion reference is genealogical and passive. It demonstrates name survival, not cultic continuity. A name in a genealogy is not evidence of ongoing worship.
