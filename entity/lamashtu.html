Who It Is

In the dim rooms where ancient mothers labored and infants slept, one name carried a terror that no locked door could keep out: Lamashtu, the lion-headed daughter of the sky god Anu, who—according to the clay tablets of Mesopotamia—could reach into the womb itself, steal a child from the breast, and drink its blood. Far from a mere shadow in the night, she was a demon-goddess with her own will, a figure so feared that families carved her image into amulets and invoked another terrifying spirit, Pazuzu, just to hold her back.

Lamashtu is characterized as malevolent, autonomous, and relentless. Unlike many Mesopotamian demons who act on divine command, she is said to act "of her own accord" in doing evil. Incantations describe her as slaying children, unborn and newborn infants, harming mothers and pregnant women, eating men and drinking their blood, disturbing sleep, bringing nightmares, killing foliage, infesting rivers and lakes, and spreading disease, sickness, and death. Powers
Attack on pregnancy and unborn children

Lamashtu is repeatedly described as reaching into the womb, causing miscarriages, and slaying unborn children. She threatens women during pregnancy and childbirth, making her a central figure in magico-medical concerns about reproductive vulnerability.

Limit: Her attacks are believed to be countered by properly performed incantations, ritual actions (figurines, offerings, canal and mountain imagery), and the protective power of Pazuzu and other invoked deities.

Killing and consuming infants and children

She is said to kidnap children while they are breastfeeding, gnaw on their bones, and suck their blood. This predatory behavior extends to newborns and young children, making her a personification of sudden infant death and unexplained childhood illness.

Limit: Protective amulets, especially those invoking Pazuzu, are believed to prevent her from approaching the child; ritual placement of her figurine and offerings aim to redirect or neutralize her aggression.

Causing disease, fever, and pestilence

Lamashtu is associated with lasting fever, sickness, and general pestilence. Incantations against her are glossed as rituals to dispel fever and her presence, indicating that she embodies or carries disease.

Limit: Her influence can be mitigated through specific magico-medical rituals, including figurine manipulation, offerings, and repeated recitation of incantations.

Disturbing sleep and bringing nightmares

She is said to disturb sleep and fill the night with terrible dreams, linking her to nocturnal anxiety and dream-related afflictions.

Limit: Protective rituals and amulets placed near the bed, especially for children, are intended to keep her away during the night.

Blighting vegetation and polluting waters

Lamashtu is described as killing foliage and infesting rivers and lakes, suggesting a capacity to harm crops and water sources, thereby contributing to famine and environmental distress.

Limit: Ritual imagery of digging canals and raising mountains, and invoking multiple gods, aims to redirect or contain her influence over the landscape.

Autonomous malevolence

Unlike many demons who act on divine command, Lamashtu is said to act in malevolence of her own accord. Her name is written with the divine determinative, indicating that she is a goddess or demigoddess with independent agency.

Limit: Her autonomy makes her harder to control through appeals to higher gods, but rituals still attempt to enlist other deities (and Pazuzu) to oppose her.

Travel on the river of the underworld

She is believed to ride in her boat on the river of the underworld, linking her to chthonic waters and the realm of the dead.

Limit: This is more a cosmological motif than a discrete power; rituals symbolically block or divert her path.

Accounts I
Lamaštu Daughter of Anu (canonical incantation series)Canonical Lamaštu incantation tablets as edited by Walter Farber, Lamaštu, 2014.

Second–First millennia BCE (with primary surviving manuscripts from the first millennium)

The series is not a narrative myth in the modern sense but a structured set of incantations and ritual instructions centered on Lamashtu. The texts describe her genealogy as the daughter of Anu, list her seven names, and catalog her evil deeds: attacking pregnant women, killing unborn and newborn children, drinking blood, causing disease, and disturbing sleep. The incantations call upon various gods and especially Pazuzu to drive her away. Ritual actions include the making of a figurine, offerings of bread and water, symbolic canal-digging and mountain-raising, and the placement of the figurine near the afflicted child. The series constructs a ritual drama in which Lamashtu is conjured, named, and expelled. The intended consequence is the removal of Lamashtu’s influence, the healing of sickness, and the protection of mothers and infants. The series also codifies her demonological profile for later tradition.

Accounts II
Incantation to dispel lasting fever and LamashtuAkkadian incantation and ritual edited in TUAT 2 (1988).

First millennium BCE

This incantation presents Lamashtu as a cause of persistent fever in a child. The ritual prescribes crafting a figurine of Lamashtu, placing bread and water before it, having a black dog carry the figurine, and then placing it near the child’s head with the heart of a piglet in its mouth. The incantation is recited three times daily for three days. The text describes Lamashtu’s harmful nature and calls upon divine powers to remove her from the child. The ritual aims to transfer the illness and Lamashtu’s presence to the figurine and its animal carriers, thereby curing the child. It reinforces the association between Lamashtu and fever.

Protective invocation of Pazuzu against LamashtuAmulets and small statues bearing Pazuzu’s image and inscriptions referencing Lamashtu.

First millennium BCE

While not a continuous story, the combination of Pazuzu amulets and references to Lamashtu creates a narrative frame in which Pazuzu, himself a dangerous figure associated with famine and drought, is enlisted as a protector against Lamashtu. Inscriptions and iconography show Pazuzu confronting or warding off her, and families place his image near mothers and infants to keep Lamashtu away. This ritualized opposition establishes a demonological rivalry and shapes later perceptions of both figures: Pazuzu as a paradoxical protector, Lamashtu as a primary threat to childbirth.
