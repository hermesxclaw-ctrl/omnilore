Lamashtu

She Who Terrifies, Child-Snatcher, Daughter of Anu

Lamashtu, the daughter of Anu, whose touch brings death to infants and whose gaze curses the womb with barrenness.

Who It Is

Lamashtu is a fearsome demoness who preys on pregnant women and newborn infants. She is the daughter of the sky god Anu, making her uniquely powerful among demons. Unlike other malevolent spirits, Lamashtu was not created from the underworld — she descended from heaven of her own accord to pursue her malignant purposes. She is depicted as a hybrid monster: a lion's head, donkey's teeth and ears, a hairy body, bird-like talons, and her fingers and toes tipped with bronze talons. She nurses a pig and a dog at her breasts (corrupting the maternal act) and holds snakes in both hands. Lamashtu attacks pregnant women in their seventh month, snatches infants from their cradles, drinks their blood, and gnaws on their bones. She also causes nightmares and brings disease. To ward her off, Mesopotamians used Pazuzu amulets (her sworn enemy), Lamashtu plaques showing her being driven away, and elaborate exorcism rituals. Pregnant women wore metal pendants of Lamashtu precisely because even depicting her was believed to limit her power. She personifies the very real ancient fear of maternal and infant mortality.

Attested in Akkadian incantations / Amulets / Exorcism textsEra Neo-Assyrian to Neo-Babylonian (c. 1000-500 BCE)

Form

Attested descriptions portray Lamashtu as a hybrid female figure with the head of a lioness, donkey-like teeth and ears, naked breasts, a hairy body, long fingers and fingernails, and the feet or talons of a bird (often associated with the monstrous bird Anzû). She is frequently shown standing or kneeling on a donkey, nursing a pig and a dog, and holding snakes in her hands. Her hands are sometimes described as stained with blood, emphasizing her predatory relationship to infants. This composite anatomy marks her as a liminal, dangerous being combining predatory animals, domestic beasts, and chthonic symbols.

Powers

Attack on pregnancy and unborn children Lamashtu is repeatedly described as reaching into the womb, causing miscarriages, and slaying unborn children. She threatens women during pregnancy and childbirth, making her a central figure in magico-medical concerns about reproductive vulnerability. Canonical Lamaštu incantation series; first-millennium Akkadian incantations and rituals. Her attacks are believed to be countered by properly performed incantations, ritual actions (figurines, offerings, canal and mountain imagery), and the protective power of Pazuzu and other invoked deities. No major contradictions about this power; all sources agree on her role as a threat to pregnancy, though some earlier Sumerian references may present Dimme in less explicitly malevolent terms.

Killing and consuming infants and children She is said to kidnap children while they are breastfeeding, gnaw on their bones, and suck their blood. This predatory behavior extends to newborns and young children, making her a personification of sudden infant death and unexplained childhood illness. Akkadian demonological descriptions; Academic Dictionaries summary based on incantation texts; Farber’s edition. Protective amulets, especially those invoking Pazuzu, are believed to prevent her from approaching the child; ritual placement of her figurine and offerings aim to redirect or neutralize her aggression. No significant contradictions; the exact imagery (gnawing bones vs. drinking blood) varies slightly between texts but reinforces the same predatory theme.

Causing disease, fever, and pestilence Lamashtu is associated with lasting fever, sickness, and general pestilence. Incantations against her are glossed as rituals to dispel fever and her presence, indicating that she embodies or carries disease. TUAT 2 incantation "to dispel lasting fever and Lamashtu"; Lamaštu incantation series. Her influence can be mitigated through specific magico-medical rituals, including figurine manipulation, offerings, and repeated recitation of incantations. Some texts emphasize fever, others broader sickness; these are complementary rather than contradictory emphases.

Disturbing sleep and bringing nightmares She is said to disturb sleep and fill the night with terrible dreams, linking her to nocturnal anxiety and dream-related afflictions. First-millennium Akkadian descriptions of her evil deeds; demonological summaries. Protective rituals and amulets placed near the bed, especially for children, are intended to keep her away during the night. No major contradictions; some texts mention nightmares explicitly, others only disturbed sleep.

Blighting vegetation and polluting waters Lamashtu is described as killing foliage and infesting rivers and lakes, suggesting a capacity to harm crops and water sources, thereby contributing to famine and environmental distress. Akkadian demonological lists and incantation descriptions; Academic Dictionaries summary based on Farber. Ritual imagery of digging canals and raising mountains, and invoking multiple gods, aims to redirect or contain her influence over the landscape. Her environmental impact is less consistently attested than her role in childbirth; some texts omit these aspects, but there is no explicit denial.

Autonomous malevolence Unlike many demons who act on divine command, Lamashtu is said to act in malevolence of her own accord. Her name is written with the divine determinative, indicating that she is a goddess or demigoddess with independent agency. First-millennium Akkadian texts; Farber’s analysis; demonological summaries. Her autonomy makes her harder to control through appeals to higher gods, but rituals still attempt to enlist other deities (and Pazuzu) to oppose her. Scholars debate whether this autonomy should classify her as a goddess rather than a demon; ancient texts themselves present both divine determinative and demonological context.

Travel on the river of the underworld She is believed to ride in her boat on the river of the underworld, linking her to chthonic waters and the realm of the dead. Later descriptive summaries based on incantation imagery; Wikipedia and derivative sources drawing on Farber. This is more a cosmological motif than a discrete power; rituals symbolically block or divert her path. The specific river and details of her journey are not consistently described; this motif may be reconstructed from fragmentary texts rather than fully attested in a single narrative.

Accounts

The series is not a narrative myth in the modern sense but a structured set of incantations and ritual instructions centered on Lamashtu. The texts describe her genealogy as the daughter of Anu, list her seven names, and catalog her evil deeds: attacking pregnant women, killing unborn and newborn children, drinking blood, causing disease, and disturbing sleep. The incantations call upon various gods and especially Pazuzu to drive her away. Ritual actions include the making of a figurine, offerings of bread and water, symbolic canal-digging and mountain-raising, and the placement of the figurine near the afflicted child. The series constructs a ritual drama in which Lamashtu is conjured, named, and expelled.

This incantation presents Lamashtu as a cause of persistent fever in a child. The ritual prescribes crafting a figurine of Lamashtu, placing bread and water before it, having a black dog carry the figurine, and then placing it near the child’s head with the heart of a piglet in its mouth. The incantation is recited three times daily for three days. The text describes Lamashtu’s harmful nature and calls upon divine powers to remove her from the child.

While not a continuous story, the combination of Pazuzu amulets and references to Lamashtu creates a narrative frame in which Pazuzu, himself a dangerous figure associated with famine and drought, is enlisted as a protector against Lamashtu. Inscriptions and iconography show Pazuzu confronting or warding off her, and families place his image near mothers and infants to keep Lamashtu away.

Content Notes

Lamashtu is described as kidnapping infants while they are breastfeeding, gnawing on their bones, and sucking their blood. These images reflect ancient attempts to personify and ritualize responses to sudden infant death and unexplained childhood illness, but they are graphically violent.

Scholars interpret these motifs within the broader Mesopotamian demonological system, where malevolent beings embody specific threats and are addressed through ritual rather than moral narrative.

Akkadian demonological descriptions; Academic Dictionaries summary based on Lamaštu incantations.

Texts attribute miscarriages, stillbirths, and complications in childbirth to Lamashtu’s attacks on the womb and pregnant women. This demonological attribution externalizes reproductive risk but also embeds fear and blame in a personalized figure.

Studies of Mesopotamian medicine and religion note that such demonological explanations coexist with empirical observations and practical remedies, forming a complex system of care and meaning-making around pregnancy.

Lamaštu incantation series; magico-medical texts.

Lamashtu is associated with lasting fever, general sickness, killing foliage, and infesting rivers and lakes. These attributions link her to broader suffering, including famine and ecological distress.

Researchers see these motifs as part of a pattern in which demons personify multi-dimensional crises—health, agriculture, and environment—allowing ritual specialists to address them collectively.

TUAT 2 incantation; demonological descriptions; Farber’s edition.

Rituals against Lamashtu involve figurines, a black dog carrying the figurine, and placing a piglet’s heart in the figurine’s mouth. These practices include symbolic violence against animals and the transfer of illness to effigies.

Scholars of ancient magic analyze these rituals as controlled, symbolic violence aimed at redirecting harm away from humans; they also note the ethical and cosmological frameworks that made such actions acceptable within ancient societies.

TUAT 2 incantation; Lamaštu ritual tablets.

What We Don't Know

The surviving evidence for Lamashtu is heavily weighted toward first-millennium BCE incantation and ritual texts; earlier Sumerian material on Lamme/Dimme is fragmentary and not fully accessible in translation, limiting firm conclusions about her earliest roles. Visual evidence is dispersed across museum collections, and not all plaques and amulets have been comprehensively cataloged or published. Many details of her cosmology (e.g., the specifics of her journey on the river of the underworld) are reconstructed from partial texts rather than fully attested narratives. Comparative discussions with figures like Lilith rely on thematic parallels rather than direct textual links. Reception in modern popular culture beyond well-known role-playing adaptations has not been exhaustively surveyed here, and non-English scholarship may contain additional nuances not captured in this dossier.

A way to hold it in your mind

In the dim rooms where ancient mothers labored and infants slept, one name carried a terror that no locked door could keep out: Lamashtu, the lion-headed daughter of the sky god Anu, who—according to the clay tablets of Mesopotamia—could reach into the womb itself, steal a child from the breast, and drink its blood. Far from a mere shadow in the night, she was a demon-goddess with her own will, a figure so feared that families carved her image into amulets and invoked another terrifying spirit, Pazuzu, just to hold her back.
