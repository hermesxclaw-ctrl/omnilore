Who It Is

Geb is the rare god you've been standing on your whole life and never once thanked: the earth itself, according to the oldest theology of Heliopolis. In a mythology that overwhelmingly pairs a sky-FATHER with an earth-MOTHER, Geb inverts the entire pattern — a male earth beneath an arched, star-dusted sky-goddess, still in eternal embrace until the air god could prise them apart. His laughter is an earthquake; barley grows literally from his ribs; he is the common father of every serpent in the world. And yet this god who founded the royal line of Egypt, from whom king after king claimed descent, held no temple, no cult center, no altar of his own. In death he becomes a warden with a jackal-headed staff to bind the damned, in Rome a bearded Cronus in crocodile-skin vestments, and in a 2016 blockbuster a CGI colossus at the foot of Pantheon. He is earth, and like the ground beneath a dyna

Geb is characterized in surviving myth as a chthonic, emotionally volatile father-king. His laughter shakes the earth (earthquakes). He both harbors and punishes the dead: the earth opens its jaws to receive them, and his stave imprisons the unworthy. He is the archetypal aging divine king, displaced from power, whose lineage (Osiris, then Horus) carries kingship forward.

Powers
Personification of the Earth / autochthonic fatherhood

Geb is the earth itself in the Heliopolitan cosmology. As the common father of all snakes, he is called 'father of snakes' — one Egyptian name for snake was sȝ-tȝ, 'son of the earth'. Barley grows on his ribs and vegetation springs from his body.

Limit: Passive personification — he embodies, rather than actively summons, the earth.

Seismogenesis — the earth laughs

Earthquakes were explained as Geb's laughter shaking the ground.

Limit: Attested as aetiological explanation, not a deliberate power.

Lordship and transfer of divine kingship

Geb was the primeval divine king of Egypt, a role he passed to his son Osiris after the conflicts with Set; Osiris in turn passed rule to Horus. He thereby anchors the legitimate royal succession of the pharaohs.

Limit: His kingship is always already superseded by his sons.

Judge of the dead / Warden of the underworld

Geb's chthonic aspect governs the fate of the dead: 'Geb opening his jaws' metaphorically releases the worthy from their tombs to the fertile Field of Reeds, while his stave 'Mighty One' binds the unworthy in the earth.

Limit: Attested in funerary metaphor; his judgement is delegated within the wider tribunal.

Father of serpents and chthonic animals

As father of snakes, he sires the mythological serpent Nehebkau of primeval times (Coffin Texts) and is the ancestor of the serpent realm. He may also manifest as a crocodile or bull.

Limit: Predominantly a genealogical/theological claim.

Support of the created cosmos

Together with Nut, Geb formed the permanent boundary between the primeval waters (Nun) and the newly created world, separate but inextricably joined as earth and sky.

Limit: Cosmological role rather than an active power.

Accounts I
The Separation of Geb and NutHeliopolitan creation myth (alluded to in Pyramid/Coffin Texts; elaborated in later sources)

Old Kingdom onward

Out of the primordial waters Nun rose the self-begotten god Atum, who produced Shu (air/emptiness) and Tefnut (moisture). These twin siblings in turn produced Geb (the earth) and Nut (the sky). Geb and Nut lay locked in continuous embrace and intercourse, their union spanning the cosmos. The god Shu, compelled to separate them, thrust himself between the two — lifting Nut into the sky as a star-covered arch over Geb, who reclined below. From this separation came the ordered universe, with Geb and Nut serving as the permanent boundary between the primeval waters and the created world. The younger interpretation reads the separation in human terms of hostility and sexual jealousy, and Geb is said to have challenged his father Shu in revenge. Established the structure of the cosmos and enabled the birth of the four great gods (Osiris, Set, Isis, Nephthys) to Geb and Nut. Possibly Set's birthday was considered unlucky.

Geb's Revolt, Tefnut, and the Disrupted Divine OrderScholarly reconstruction from Coffin Texts and related allusions

Middle Kingdom textual roots

After Shu had forcibly separated Geb from his sister-wife Nut, Geb challenged his father's leadership and caused Shu to withdraw from the world. In an act of deliberate recombination of the divine order, Geb — who was in love with his mother Tefnut — took her as his chief queen-consort, separating Shu from his sister-wife just as Shu had earlier separated Geb from Nut. The episode exposes the underlying hostility, sexual jealousy, and father-son rivalry within the Heliopolitan royal family, reframing the cosmological separation in human dynastic terms. Established a parallel order of reproductive pairings alongside the original creation; Shu withdraws from active rule. Raises the question of whether Shu or Geb was the first god-king.

Accounts II
The Murder of Osiris and the Contest for the ThroneOsiris myth; 'Contendings of Horus and Seth'; Plutarch

attested New Kingdom, elaborated Greco-Roman

Osiris, Geb's son and heir, was killed by his brother Set, who dismembered him and scattered the pieces so he could not be resurrected. Isis (Osiris's sister-wife) and Nephthys gathered the pieces, and through Isis's magic Osiris was revived just long enough to father Horus. The kingship devolved to the dead Osiris then had to be settled between Horus and Set. This contest was presented as a legal judgment before the assembled Ennead, in which the presiding judge could be Geb — the father of both Osiris and Set who had himself once held the throne — or, alternatively, the creator gods Ra or Atum. Horus ultimately triumphed, establishing the pharaonic succession of Horus, and Set was cast out. Anchored royal legitimacy in the line Geb–Osiris–Horus; established judgement of the dead and the tribunal model; cast Set as the archetypal usurper while Osiris became lord of the underworld.

The Fall and Geb's Heirship of RaBook of the Heavenly Cow

New Kingdom (possible Amarna-period origin)

After humanity rebelled against the sun god Ra, Ra destroyed them through the violent Eye of Ra (Hathor), tricking her into intoxication with blood-colored beer after survivors remained. Suffering and death entered the world, and the creator withdrew from direct cohabitation with humanity. Ra ascended into the sky on the back of Nut as the heavenly cow, and in the reordered cosmos the text implies that Geb was appointed as the heir of the departing sun god; rule over humankind was handed to Osiris, with Thoth governing the night sky as his deputy. Explains the imperfection of the world, established the separation of gods from humanity, and grounds Geb's role as a transitional divine king who passes the throne to Osiris.
