[PARALLEL, NOT ATTESTED FOR TAROIMO] Momotarō — The Peach Boy (Type 95)Seki Keigo, Nihon Mukashibanashi Taisei (1950-1958), Type 95; English translation: Seki Keigo, Folktales of Japan (1963) trans. Robert J. Adams; also Iwaya Sazanami, Nihon Otogibanashi (1894-1896)

Oral tradition pre-Edo; first literary attestation late Muromachi/early Edo (e.g., Momotarō references in 17th c. chapbooks); standardized in Meiji textbooks

An old childless couple finds a giant peach floating down a river; a boy, Momotarō, is born from the peach. He grows supernaturally fast and strong, leaves home with millet dumplings (kibidango), befriends a dog, monkey, and pheasant, sails to Onigashima (Demon Island), defeats the oni, and returns with treasure to support his parents. This is the canonical 'crop-born hero' template. Taroimo's name suggests a direct structural parallel — a child born from a taro corm instead of a peach — but no text attests that Taroimo actually follows this plot. Included here as the closest attested narrative type to contextualize what a Taroimo story WOULD look like if verified, and to show what Seki's index does NOT contain. Established the 'Tarō' heroic-legend template that Taroimo's name evokes; became national moral tale in Meiji education. Demonstrates that taro as birth vessel would be a logical but unattested variant.

Accounts II
[PARALLEL] Ta-no-Kami and the Field Child — Agricultural Deity TalesYanagita Kunio, Tōno Monogatari (1910) Nos. 17, 26, 31; Yanagita Kunio, Senkyō Shō (1946); Harada Toshiaki, Ta-no-Kami Shinkō (1958)

Oral traditions collected 1910-1950s; beliefs attested from medieval to Showa era

Across rural Japan, the field deity (ta-no-kami) is said to dwell in the paddies in growing season and retreat to the mountains in winter (yama-no-kami). Offerings of taro, rice, and sake are made at field shrines. Several tales describe a mysterious child found in a field or storehouse who is revealed to be the deity's offspring or incarnation, bringing good harvest if treated well and famine if offended. Taro, as a stored corm, is specifically associated with winter survival and thus with the deity's blessing. No tale in these collections names the child 'Taroimo,' but the motif of a taro-associated miraculous child is attested as a pattern. This is the folkloric habitat where a character named Taroimo could plausically have originated, if he is not a modern invention. Explains cultural logic for a heroic-legend figure tied to taro; provides ritual context (imo matsuri, harvest festivals). No direct link to Taroimo established.

Heian to Edo (literary attestation 12th c.; popularization Edo period)

Kintarō, child of a mountain witch (Yama-uba) and a dragon or noble, raised on Mount Ashigara, displays superhuman strength (wrestling bears, uprooting trees), befriends animals, and later becomes the samurai Sakata no Kintoki serving Minamoto no Yorimitsu. Like Momotarō, he is a 'Tarō' hero with wilderness upbringing and animal companions. Included as another attested 'Tarō' hero to show the naming convention and heroic-legend archetype that Q108593230's 'heroic-legend' tag evokes. No source links Kintarō to Taroimo.
