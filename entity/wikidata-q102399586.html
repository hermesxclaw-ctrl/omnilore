The Approach

Before Shakespeare gave him the name of a mischievous fairy, Puck was something far stranger: a shape-shifting hobgoblin of English folklore, a night-riding terror who could sour the milk, mislead the traveler, and drag the unwary through brambles. He was the household spirit you never quite saw, the whispered name that made children shiver by the fire. And in one of the most remarkable transformations in literary history, this goblin of the hedgerows stepped onto the Elizabethan stage and became the immortal trickster of A Midsummer Night's Dream. But the stage Puck is only one face of a creature who has haunted English imagination for over a thousand years.

Identity

Puck

Pronunciation Puck: /pʌk/ (rhymes with 'luck'). Robin Goodfellow: /ˈrɒbɪn ˈɡʊdfɛloʊ/.

Etymology

The name 'Puck' is of uncertain origin. It is likely derived from Old English 'puca' (a mischievous spirit), related to Old Norse 'púki', Old Swedish 'puke', and Middle English 'pouke'. The Welsh 'pwca' and Irish 'púca' are cognates. The meaning is generally 'a mischievous spirit' or 'goblin'. The alternative name 'Robin Goodfellow' combines the common name 'Robin' with 'Goodfellow', a euphemistic epithet meant to appease the spirit.

Cultural Ownership

English folklore, with broader Celtic and Germanic parallels. The figure is especially associated with the English countryside, particularly the Midlands and southern England, but similar spirits appear across the British Isles and Scandinavia.

Disputed Identifications

There is scholarly debate over the relationship between Puck and other household spirits such as the brownie, the hob, and the goblin. Some scholars view them as distinct, while others see them as regional variations of a single type. The identification of Puck with the Irish púca or Welsh pwca is generally accepted but not universally agreed. Additionally, the folkloric Puck is often conflated with Shakespeare's fairy, though the latter is a literary creation that draws on but significantly alters the folk tradition.

Description

Appearance

Puck's appearance is not consistently described in folklore. In some accounts, he is a shape-shifter, able to take the form of a horse, dog, or other animal. In others, he appears as a hairy, dark-skinned creature, or as a small, impish figure. Shakespeare's Puck is a 'merry wanderer of the night' with a 'hobgoblin' name, but his physical description is vague. The 1628 pamphlet 'Robin Goodfellow: His Mad Pranks and Merry Jests' describes him as a 'merry companion' who can change his shape at will.

Symbols & Iconography

Puck is often associated with the night, the forest, and domestic spaces. He is sometimes depicted with a lantern or a broom, and in later visual art, he is shown as a small, winged fairy. The name 'Robin Goodfellow' evokes the robin bird, and he is sometimes associated with that bird. In Shakespeare, he is linked to the moon and to the fairy court.

Behaviour & Personality

Puck is a trickster, known for his mischievous and sometimes malicious pranks. He is said to lead travelers astray, to spoil milk, to pinch servants, and to cause general domestic chaos. However, he can also be helpful, performing household chores in exchange for small gifts of food or drink. He is capricious and unpredictable, and he demands to be treated with respect.

Domains & Habitats

Puck is associated with the English countryside, particularly with forests, meadows, and crossroads. He is also a household spirit, said to haunt farmhouses and cottages. In Shakespeare, he is a fairy of the forest, serving the fairy king Oberon.

Version Differences

The folkloric Puck is a solitary, often malevolent spirit, while Shakespeare's Puck is a playful, loyal servant of Oberon. The 1628 pamphlet presents a more benign, jocular Puck who is a 'merry companion' and a servant of the fairy queen. Later literary adaptations often blend these versions. Regional variants, such as the Irish púca, are more dangerous and can be prophetic.

Household help

In some traditions, Puck (as Robin Goodfellow) will perform household chores such as threshing, churning butter, or cleaning, in exchange for a bowl of cream or bread.

He may leave if offended or if his payment is not given.

Contradictions

Some sources describe him as purely mischievous, while others emphasize his helpful side.

Magic and enchantment

Source Version Shakespeare's A Midsummer Night's Dream

Description

In Shakespeare, Puck uses a magical flower ('love-in-idleness') to cast love spells. He is also able to fly, travel around the world quickly, and assume the appearance of other characters.

Limitations

He must obey Oberon's commands; his magic can have unintended consequences.

Contradictions

This is a literary invention, not attested in folklore.

Stories & Myths

Puck's Pranks in Reginald Scot's Discoverie of Witchcraft

Source Reginald Scot, The Discoverie of Witchcraft (1584)Culture EnglishPeriod 16th century

Summary

Scot describes Robin Goodfellow as a spirit that performs household tasks, such as grinding malt, but also causes mischief. He notes that people believe in him and tell tales of his exploits.

Participants

Robin Goodfellow

Householders

Consequences

Scot uses these tales as evidence of superstition and the foolishness of believing in spirits.

Variants

Scot's account is one of the earliest detailed descriptions of Robin Goodfellow.

Robin Goodfellow: His Mad Pranks and Merry Jests

Source Anonymous pamphlet (1628)Culture EnglishPeriod 17th century

Summary

This pamphlet tells of Robin Goodfellow's origins and his various pranks and helpful deeds. He is depicted as a merry spirit who serves the fairy queen and plays tricks on mortals, but also helps them if they are kind to him.

Participants

Robin Goodfellow

Fairy Queen

Various mortals

Consequences

The pamphlet helped to solidify the image of Puck as a more playful, less malevolent figure.

Variants

This is a literary work that draws on folklore but also adds new elements.

Shakespeare's A Midsummer Night's Dream

Source William Shakespeare (c. 1595)Culture EnglishPeriod Elizabethan

Summary

Puck, also called Robin Goodfellow, is a fairy servant to Oberon. He is sent to fetch a magical flower and to apply its juice to the eyes of a young man, but he mistakenly applies it to the wrong person, causing a chaotic series of love entanglements. At the end, he delivers a famous epilogue asking the audience to forgive the play's faults.

Consequences

The play is one of Shakespeare's most popular comedies and has shaped the modern perception of Puck as a mischievous but ultimately benign fairy.

Variants

Shakespeare's Puck is a literary creation, though he draws on folkloric elements.

Source John Milton, 'L'Allegro' (1645)Culture EnglishPeriod 17th century

Summary

Milton mentions 'the fairy Mab' and 'the drudging goblin' (Robin Goodfellow) who works at night to help farmers, but leaves if he is given a reward. This shows the persistence of the folkloric tradition.

Contradictions & Disputes

Nature of Puck: malevolent vs. benevolent

Position A

Puck is a malicious goblin who causes harm and terror.

Position A — evidence

Early folklore and Scot's description emphasize his mischievous and frightening side.

Position B

Puck is a helpful household spirit who aids humans.

Position B — evidence

The 1628 pamphlet and later traditions emphasize his helpfulness.

Scholarly Consensus

Scholars generally agree that Puck is a trickster who can be both helpful and harmful, depending on the context and how he is treated.

Relationship between Puck and Robin Goodfellow

Position A

Puck and Robin Goodfellow are the same entity, with Robin Goodfellow being an alias.

Position A — evidence

Shakespeare uses both names interchangeably; the 1628 pamphlet does the same.

Position B

They are originally distinct spirits that were later conflated.

Position B — evidence

Some scholars argue that 'Puck' is a general term for a goblin, while 'Robin Goodfellow' is a specific household spirit.

Scholarly Consensus

The majority view is that they are the same figure, but the question remains open.

Position A

Position A — evidence

Position B

Puck is a large, hairy goblin.

Position B — evidence

Some folk descriptions and the etymology of 'hobgoblin'.

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus; appearance varies by source.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

The research is limited by the availability of primary sources; many medieval references to Puck are fragmentary. The exact origins of the name and the original nature of the spirit are uncertain. The dossier does not cover all regional variants or all modern adaptations; it focuses on the most significant. Further research could explore the Puck's relationship to other goblin figures, the evolution of his image in art, and the full range of appearances in popular culture.

Research Scope

This dossier covers the folklore figure of Puck (also known as Robin Goodfellow), focusing on attested traditions from medieval and early modern England, including literary references, and the figure's reception in later culture. It does not cover the hockey puck, the moon, the magazine, or other unrelated subjects that share the name. The research draws on primary texts such as Shakespeare's A Midsummer Night's Dream, Reginald Scot's The Discoverie of Witchcraft, and Robert Burton's The Anatomy of Melancholy, as well as scholarly interpretations. Modern adaptations are surveyed selectively, with emphasis on direct representations of the folkloric or Shakespearean Puck.
