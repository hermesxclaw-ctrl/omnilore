Who It Is

Before Shakespeare gave him the name of a mischievous fairy, Puck was something far stranger: a shape-shifting hobgoblin of English folklore, a night-riding terror who could sour the milk, mislead the traveler, and drag the unwary through brambles. He was the household spirit you never quite saw, the whispered name that made children shiver by the fire. And in one of the most remarkable transformations in literary history, this goblin of the hedgerows stepped onto the Elizabethan stage and became the immortal trickster of A Midsummer Night's Dream. But the stage Puck is only one face of a creature who has haunted English imagination for over a thousand years.

Puck is a trickster, known for his mischievous and sometimes malicious pranks. He is said to lead travelers astray, to spoil milk, to pinch servants, and to cause general domestic chaos. However, he can also be helpful, performing household chores in exchange for small gifts of food or drink. He is capricious and unpredictable, and he demands to be treated with respect.

Powers
Shape-shifting

Puck can change his form, taking the appearance of various animals or objects. In folklore, he is said to appear as a horse, a dog, or a will-o'-the-wisp. In Shakespeare, he can imitate the voices of others.

Limit: Not specified; he appears to be able to change at will.

Leading travelers astray

Puck is known to mislead travelers, causing them to lose their way. This is a common motif in folklore.

Limit: Not specified.

Domestic mischief

Puck can sour milk, cause objects to disappear, pinch people, and create general chaos in households.

Limit: Not specified.

Household help

In some traditions, Puck (as Robin Goodfellow) will perform household chores such as threshing, churning butter, or cleaning, in exchange for a bowl of cream or bread.

Limit: He may leave if offended or if his payment is not given.

Magic and enchantment

In Shakespeare, Puck uses a magical flower ('love-in-idleness') to cast love spells. He is also able to fly, travel around the world quickly, and assume the appearance of other characters.

Limit: He must obey Oberon's commands; his magic can have unintended consequences.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Puck's appearance is not consistently described in folklore. In some accounts, he is a shape-shifter, able to take the form of a horse, dog, or other animal. In others, he appears as a hairy, dark-skinned creature, or as a small, impish figure. Shakespeare's Puck is a 'merry wanderer of the night' with a 'hobgoblin' name, but his physical description is vague. Symbols — Puck is often associated with the night, the forest, and domestic spaces. He is sometimes depicted with a lantern or a broom, and in later visual art, he is shown as a small, winged fairy. The name 'Robin Goodfellow' evokes the robin bird, and he is sometimes associated with that bird. In Shakespeare, he is linked to the moon and to the fairy court.

Domains — Puck is associated with the English countryside, particularly with forests, meadows, and crossroads. He is also a household spirit, said to haunt farmhouses and cottages. In Shakespeare, he is a fairy of the forest, serving the fairy king Oberon.

Accounts I
Puck's Pranks in Reginald Scot's Discoverie of WitchcraftReginald Scot, The Discoverie of Witchcraft (1584)

16th century

Scot describes Robin Goodfellow as a spirit that performs household tasks, such as grinding malt, but also causes mischief. He notes that people believe in him and tell tales of his exploits. Scot uses these tales as evidence of superstition and the foolishness of believing in spirits.

Robin Goodfellow: His Mad Pranks and Merry JestsAnonymous pamphlet (1628)

17th century

This pamphlet tells of Robin Goodfellow's origins and his various pranks and helpful deeds. He is depicted as a merry spirit who serves the fairy queen and plays tricks on mortals, but also helps them if they are kind to him. The pamphlet helped to solidify the image of Puck as a more playful, less malevolent figure.

Accounts II
Shakespeare's A Midsummer Night's DreamWilliam Shakespeare (c. 1595)

Elizabethan

Puck, also called Robin Goodfellow, is a fairy servant to Oberon. He is sent to fetch a magical flower and to apply its juice to the eyes of a young man, but he mistakenly applies it to the wrong person, causing a chaotic series of love entanglements. At the end, he delivers a famous epilogue asking the audience to forgive the play's faults. The play is one of Shakespeare's most popular comedies and has shaped the modern perception of Puck as a mischievous but ultimately benign fairy.

Milton's 'L'Allegro'John Milton, 'L'Allegro' (1645)

17th century

Milton mentions 'the fairy Mab' and 'the drudging goblin' (Robin Goodfellow) who works at night to help farmers, but leaves if he is given a reward. This shows the persistence of the folkloric tradition. Milton's poem reinforces the image of Puck as a helpful household spirit.
