Who It Is

In the quiet of a Roman-era shrine, a Gaulish worshipper scratched a name onto a stone: Alaunus. Not a god of grand epics or thunderous battles, but a local power of the land, a healer of the spring and the river. He is a whisper from a lost world, a deity who was powerful enough to be equated with Apollo, yet obscure enough to have left us only a handful of stones. He is the patron saint of the forgotten, the god of the small places that history almost erased.

As a healing and solar deity, his behavior is inferred from his cult. He would have been a benevolent, protective figure, associated with light, health, and perhaps prophecy. The lack of mythological narratives means his personality is unknown.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No direct visual representation of Alaunus has been found. As a syncretized deity with Apollo, he would have been depicted in the classical style of Apollo — a youthful, beardless man, often with a lyre, laurel wreath, or bow. However, without specific iconography, his appearance remains unverified and likely varied across regions.

Symbols — No unique symbols are attested. In his Apollo syncretism, symbol would include the lyre, laurel, and possibly a raven or griffin. In a local context, he may have been associated with a specific spring, healing well, or river.

Domains — Healing, light, the sun, possibly prophecy and arts (via Apollo). His association with rivers (via the name Alaunus) suggests a domain over waterways, which were often considered sacred healing sites in Celtic tradition.

Roman period

There are no surviving myths, legends, or stories about Alaunus. His existence is known only through epigraphic evidence. This is common for many local Celtic deities, whose oral traditions were not written down and whose Roman-era cults were focused on votive practice rather than narrative. The lack of narrative makes it difficult to reconstruct his personality, his specific deeds, or his relationships with other deities.
