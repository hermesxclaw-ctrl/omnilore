Who It Is

The Valkyries (Old Norse valkyrja, 'chooser of the slain') are female spirit-warriors who serve Odin, riding through battlefields on winged horses or wolves, deciding which warriors will live and which will die, and then carrying the souls of the bravest fallen to Valhalla. There, the slain warriors (Einherjar) feast and train for Ragnarök. The Valkyries are not bringers of death but judges of worth — they select only those who deserve a place in Odin's hall. They are described as armor-clad, spear-wielding, shining figures, often associated with swans or ravens. The most famous Valkyries are named: Brynhildr, Sigrdrífa, Gunnr, Skögul, Hlökk, Herfjötur, Göll, Hrist, and Mist. Some Valkyries in the heroic sagas are mortal women who have been chosen for this role — they can fall in love, marry, and even lose their Valkyrie status (as Brynhildr famously does). The Valkyries appear at their most prominent in the Völsunga saga and the Poetic Edda, where Brynhildr's forbidden love for Sigurðr creates one of the great tragedies of Norse literature. In the afterlife they are the cup-bearers and companions of the Einherjar. The Valkyries embody the Norse martial ideal: beautiful, fearless, and merciless — sword-maidens who transform the chaos of battle into a divinely ordered selection of the worthy.

Form

The sources describe Valkyries as female figures, often riding horses and bearing spears or shields. They are typically imagined as beautiful and radiant, sometimes described as 'shining' or 'glowing.' They wear armor, though the specifics (helmets, byrnies, etc.) are mentioned in passing. They are often associated with light and the sky. In the 'Helgakviða Hundingsbana I', they are described as 'warrior maidens' riding through the sky, their armor glinting like fire. They are also associated with ravens (Odin's birds) and wolves, creatures of the battlefield.

Powers

Chooser of the Slain The Valkyries' core power is to determine the outcome of battles and decide which warriors die and which are taken to Valhalla. They are agents of fate, and their choice is absolute. Attested in both Poetic and Prose Edda; a fundamental aspect of their nature. They act under Odin's command; they are not independent arbiters of all death, only of those in battle. Some sources (e.g., Völuspá) suggest they are more like Norns, weaving fate, while others (Snorri) see them more as Odin's military enforcers.

Ride the Sky They can ride horses through the air and over the sea, traveling between Midgard and Asgard. Their passage is often marked by light or supernatural phenomena. Attested in Helgakviða Hundingsbana I, where their arrival is described as a brilliant light. None stated. None.

Shape-shifting (Swan-maiden aspect) Some Valkyries, particularly in the Helgi poems, are described as having the ability to change into swans. This is a common motif in Germanic folklore. Attested in the Helgi poems (Völsunga cycle), where Valkyries are sometimes called 'swan-maidens'. In the stories, if a man steals their swan-skin, they are trapped in human form. This aspect is not prominent in Snorri's account, which focuses on their martial role. It may be a separate folkloric tradition.

Supernatural Combat Prowess They are formidable warriors, bearing spears and shields. They are not just passive choosers; they participate in the battle's chaos. Implied in their depiction as 'warrior maidens' and their martial iconography. They are not invincible; e.g., Brynhildr is punished and put to sleep by Odin for disobedience. Their role as combatants vs. choosers is debated; some see them as more spectral, deciding fates, while others see them as active fighters.

Serve the Einherjar In Valhalla, they serve mead and ale to the fallen warriors, ensuring the eternal feast continues. Attested in Grímnismál and Gylfaginning. This is a service role, not a power. None.

Accounts

The Valkyrie Brynhildr (Sigrdrífa) disobeys Odin by granting victory to a warrior he had doomed. As punishment, Odin pricks her with a sleep-thorn, casts her into a deep sleep, and surrounds her with a wall of fire. The hero Sigurd (Siegfried) rides through the flames, awakens her, and they fall in love. She teaches him runes and wisdom before their tragic fate unfolds.

Hildr, a Valkyrie, is kidnapped by the warrior Hedin. Her father Högni pursues them. Hildr, rather than stopping the conflict, appears to resurrect the slain warriors each night so the battle can continue forever. This eternal battle is known as the 'Hjaðningavíg'.

The Valkyrie Sigrún (in one version) protects and aids the hero Helgi. She rides through the sky with her sisters, and her love for Helgi is central to the story. She defies her family to be with him, leading to tragedy.

Content Notes

Modern popular culture often romanticizes the Valkyries as beautiful, glamorous warrior-women, obscuring their grim function: they choose who dies in battle. The sources present them as agents of death, not as symbols of empowerment divorced from violence.

Scholars like Hilda Ellis Davidson have noted the Valkyries' connection to the harsh realities of Viking-age warfare and the cultural importance of dying in battle.

All primary sources

The Valkyrie imagery and the 'Walküre' motif were appropriated by Nazi Germany, particularly through Richard Wagner's operas and the promotion of a 'Nordic' warrior ideal. This has tainted the figure's reception and requires careful contextualization.

The use of 'Operation Valkyrie' as the name for the July 20, 1944, assassination attempt on Hitler is a direct, if ironic, modern reference.

Modern historical analysis of Nazi iconography

In some modern media, Valkyries are reduced to hyper-sexualized 'shield maidens' or 'warrior babes,' stripping them of their mythological power and agency. This is a form of cultural misogyny.

This is a common issue in the reception of female mythological figures in fantasy and gaming.

Scholarly Debates
Nature of the Valkyries: Supernatural vs. Mortal
They are purely supernatural beings, Odin's divine agents (Snorri's Prose Edda, Grímnismál). ⇌ They are, or can be, mortal women who take on the role (Völsunga saga, Helgi poems).
Evidence for A: Snorri's systematic description; their role as cup-bearers in Valhalla.
Evidence for B: Brynhildr's story, where she is a human princess in some versions; the 'swan-maiden' motif.
Role in Battle: Choosers vs. Combatants
They are primarily choosers, deciding who lives and dies, but not active fighters. ⇌ They are active participants in the battle, fighting alongside or against the warriors.
Evidence for A: Their name, 'Chooser of the Slain'; Snorri's description of them 'determining' outcomes.
Evidence for B: Their martial iconography (spears, shields) and their presence in the chaos of battle in skaldic poetry.

They are beautiful, shining women, as described in the Helgi poems. ⇌ They are terrifying, corpse-like beings, associated with carrion and decay.

What We Don't Know

The exact nature of the Valkyries' original form (supernatural vs. mortal) is debated. The archaeological evidence for their depiction is inconclusive. The relationship between the 'mythological' and 'heroic' Valkyries is a complex literary issue. The full extent of their role in pre-Christian religious practice (if any) is unknown. The figure of the 'swan-maiden' is a folkloric motif that is not fully integrated into the main mythological accounts.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of the Valkyries not as a 'girl gang' of warriors, but as a celestial SWAT team sent by the CEO of the gods (Odin) to perform a specific, high-stakes task: talent acquisition for his eternal army. They are the ultimate headhunters, scouting the world's battlefields for the most promising candidates. Their choice is final, their methods are violent, and their workplace is a bloody field. The mead they serve in Valhalla is not just a drink; it's the signing bonus for a job that never ends.
