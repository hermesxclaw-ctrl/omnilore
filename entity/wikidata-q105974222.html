The Approach

In the shadowed hills of ancient Arcadia, where myth and memory intertwine, there lived a prince whose name echoed in the stones of a forgotten city. Thocnus, son of the infamous Lycaon, founded a city that bore his name—yet today, only fragments of his story survive, whispered through the texts of ancient historians.

Content advisory. Contains references to mythological impiety and human sacrifice (associated with the Lycaon myth).
Identity

Thocnus

Pronunciation THOCK-nus (English approximation); in Ancient Greek: /tʰɔ̂ːknos/.

Etymology

The name Θῶκνος (Thôknos) is of uncertain etymology. It may be related to the Greek word θωκός (thōkos), meaning 'seat' or 'dwelling', possibly reflecting his role as founder of a city. However, this is speculative.

Cultural Ownership

Greek mythology, specifically Arcadian regional myth.

Disputed Identifications

No known disputes; Thocnus is a minor figure rarely mentioned in surviving sources.

Description

Appearance

No physical description survives; as a mythological prince, he may be imagined in the heroic tradition, but this is not attested.

Symbols & Iconography

No known symbols or iconography; his only symbol is the city he founded, Thocnia.

Behaviour & Personality

No specific traits are recorded. As a son of Lycaon, he may share in the impiety of his father, but this is not explicitly stated.

Domains & Habitats

He was associated with the region of Arcadia, specifically the city of Thocnia, which he founded on a hill.

Version Differences

The only known version is from Pausanias, who mentions Thocnus as the founder of Thocnia. Other sources may list different sons of Lycaon, but Thocnus is not consistently included.

Stories & Myths

Founding of Thocnia

Source Pausanias, Description of GreeceCulture GreekPeriod Classical/Hellenistic (mythological time)

Summary

Thocnus, one of the fifty sons of Lycaon, was the reputed eponymous founder of the Arcadian city of Thocnia, which was situated on a hill. The city likely served as a local center, but no further narrative survives.

Participants

Thocnus

Consequences

The city of Thocnia was established and named after him, preserving his name in the landscape.

Variants

No other known variants; the story is brief and purely etiological.

Relationships

Lycaon

Type fatherSource Pausanias, Description of Greece; Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Roman Antiquities

Description

Thocnus was one of the fifty sons of Lycaon, the impious king of Arcadia who was transformed into a wolf by Zeus as punishment for his cruelty and impiety.

Version Note

Lycaon is consistently named as the father, but the mother varies: the naiad Cyllene, Nonacris, or an unknown woman.

Conflicting Accounts

The identity of Thocnus's mother is uncertain; different sources may name different mothers for Lycaon's sons.

Lycaon's impiety and punishment

Source Pausanias, Description of Greece; other mythographers

Description

Lycaon, the father of Thocnus, was known for his impiety and cruelty. In some versions, he served human flesh to Zeus to test his divinity, leading to his transformation into a wolf.

Scholarly Context

This is a well-known myth explaining the origin of lycanthropy and the punishment of impiety.
