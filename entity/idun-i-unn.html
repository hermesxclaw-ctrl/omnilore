Who It Is

Of all the Norse gods, Iðunn is the one the immortals literally cannot live without — and the one who spends her most famous adventure as a nut clutched in a falcon's claws. The Æsir can survive the loss of a hammer or a wolf, but snatch away the goddess who keeps the apples of youth and they fray into grey, withered elders at their own assembly, 'rather ugly in form.' Everything about Iðunn is a paradox of guardianship: the keeper the gods 'depend greatly upon' is the one who is lured out of Asgard by a promise of fairer apples, and whose rescue hinges on her being shrunk to a seed and flown home ahead of a burning eagle. Iðunn's character is thin in the sources. In Lokasenna she defends her husband Bragi, calling Loki's insults 'evil', and bids Loki remember that she and Bragi once did him a kindness at a drinking-meeting — implying (though unattested elsewhere) a past of peace-making. She is trusting to the point of vulnerability: in the abduction narrative she is lured out of Asgard by Loki's claim of fairer apples. Powers
Apples of Eternal Youth

Iðunn keeps the apples that, when eaten by the gods as they begin to grow old, restore them to youth. The Prose Edda (Gylfaginning ch. 26) states this occurs 'until Ragnarök' — i.e. the apples are a renewable but not eternal fix against the gods' eventual doom. In Haustlöng the rejuvenating substance is referred to obliquely as 'the gods' old-age medicine' / 'the Æsir's age-old cure'. Because she is the sole custodian, the gods are wholly dependent on her; without her, as in the abduction narrative, they 'grow old and grey' and their assembly becomes 'rather ugly in form'.

Limit: The apples work only while the gods are alive and only until Ragnarök; the 'eternal youth' does not prevent Baldr's death or the doom war. The apples are finite and her custody is fragile — the entire myth turns on them being able to be stolen/lured away. Iðunn herself does not wield the rejuvenation actively; she merely stores and distributes the apples.

The eski (ash-wood casket) of apples

Iðunn carries her apples in an eski, a wooden box made of ash-wood traditionally used for personal possessions. The casket is her defining material attribute and is what Loki's lure targets and what she must bring for comparison.

Limit: Not a magical tool in itself; the magic resides in the apples contained within.

Transformation (as subject, not agent)

In the rescue, Loki transforms Iðunn into a nut so he can carry her back to Asgard. She does not transform herself; she is acted upon. The nut form links her to fertility symbolism (Davidson notes nuts as fertility totems) and to potential hidden-agency readings.

Limit: Undergoes the transformation unwillingly; she has no feats of self-transformation in any source.

Keeper of the gods' youth / life-sustaining custodian

Beyond the apples, Iðunn's very presence (and faithful custody) is what sustains the gods' youth into Ragnarök. Her name's 'ever young' sense, per Lindow, may allow her to perform this function even apart from the fruit, and North sees her as 'an aspect of Freyja' on whom the gods rely for youth and beauty.

Limit: Structural rather than active power; she is a keeper, not a combatant.

Domains — Iðunn's home is Asgard, where she dwells as Bragi's wife; she is numbered among the ásynjur (goddesses) seated on thrones at Ægir's banquets (Skáldskaparmál ch. 55). Accounts I
The Abduction of Iðunn by ÞjaziProse Edda (Skáldskaparmál ch. 56); oldest variant in Haustlöng (Þjóðólfr of Hvinir)

Redacted c. 1220; poem composed c. mid-10th century

Odin, Loki and Hœnir are traveling and slaughter an ox, which will not cook. Þjazi, in eagle form, offers to make the fire work in exchange for a share, then eats so much that the enraged Loki strikes at him with a staff — which sticks fast to the eagle and carries Loki up into the sky, his legs banging against stones and trees. Þjazi frees Loki only on condition that Loki swear to lure Iðunn and her apples of youth out of Asgard. Later Loki tells Iðunn he has found apples fairer than her own and that she should bring hers to compare; she follows him into a wood, where Þjazi, in eagle form, seizes her and bears her off to Þrymheimr in Jötunheimr. Deprived of her apples, the gods grow old and grey; they arrest Loki and threaten him with torture and death until he promises to retrieve her. Borrowing Freyja's falcon-skin coat, Loki flies to Jötunheimr, finds Iðunn alone while Þjazi is out at sea, transforms her into a nut, and carries her back. Lokasenna (The Flying of Loki) — 'Flyting' exchangePoetic Edda, Lokasenna stanzas 16–18 (with prose framework)

Compiled c. 1270; verse older

At Ægir's feast, after Loki has insulted Bragi, Iðunn speaks in her husband's defense, calling Loki's words 'evil' (stanza 16: '... words all evil / Loki amongst the gods'). Loki retorts that of all men she is madly in love with the one who killed her own brother. Iðunn pleads with Loki to remember the kindness she and Bragi once showed him at a drinking-meeting — kindness that, given the fragmentary record, is never detailed. Loki then turns to Gefjon. The 'killer of her brother' accusation exists nowhere else; neither the brother nor the killer is named in any surviving source. Establishes Iðunn's loyalty to Bragi and her history with Loki; creates the unsolvable 'brother's killer' riddle.

Accounts II
Iðunn among the elves / dís (Hrafnagaldr Óðins)Poetic Edda (late poem Hrafnagaldr Óðins, in some editions)

Late medieval / early modern composition

In a passage unattested anywhere else, Iðunn is identified as descending from elves, as one of 'Ivaldi's elder children', and as a dís who dwells in the dales. This genealogical aside is the source the Wikimedia data-schema's P22 (father: Ivaldi) property draws on, though the identification is contested and likely late. Roots Iðunn in an elfin/dís lineage and links her to the Ivaldi family (elsewhere famed for crafting, incl. the ship Skíðblaðnir), but the source is late and the genealogy is not harmonized with other attestations.

The Rerir apple (parallel motif, not a direct Iðunn myth)Völsunga saga ch. 2; also cited in Apples in mythology

13th century, compiling older tradition

King Rerir and his wife have no heir; they pray to Odin and Frigg. In response a wish-maiden (in the guise of a crow) drops a magical golden apple onto Rerir's lap. He shares it with the queen, who conceives and endures an extraordinarily long six-year pregnancy; Rerir dies campaigning, and the queen orders her baby cut from her, dying in the process. The surviving child is Völsung, ancestor of the Völsungar heroes. Davidson reads this as further evidence that apples were a symbol of fertility/immortality in Norse tradition, parallel to Iðunn's role. Völsung's birth founds the Völsung line; the apple motif is a fertility/animation device.

The father of Iðunn (and her genealogy)Hrafnagaldr Óðins makes her a child of Ivaldi ('one of Ivaldi's elder children').Wikidata P22 also records Óðr as a purported father; mainstream Eddic sources give her no father at all.
The 'killer of her brother' accusationLokasenna implies Iðunn had a brother and slept with his killer.No brother or killer is named in any other source, and the accusation is a flyting slur.
Iðunn as 'an aspect of Freyja'Richard North: Iðunn is 'probably ... an aspect of Freyja, a goddess whom the gods rely on for their youth and beauty'.They are separate named goddesses with distinct relations (Iðunn alone married to Bragi, keeper of apples; Freyja the Vanir love/war goddess who lends the falcon skin).
