Idun is the goddess of youth and spring, the keeper of the golden apples that the gods must eat to maintain their eternal youth. Without her apples, the Æsir would age and wither.

Who It Is

Idun is the goddess of youth and spring, the keeper of the golden apples that the gods must eat to maintain their eternal youth. Without her apples, the Æsir would age and wither. She is the wife of Bragi, the god of poetry. Her single major myth is one of the most dramatic in Norse mythology: the giant Thjazi forced Loki to lure Idun out of Asgard by promising her apples from a magical tree. Loki tricked her into leaving, and Thjazi, in eagle form, snatched her away. Without Idun and her apples, the gods began to grey, wrinkle, and age. They forced Loki to retrieve her, and by changing Freyja's falcon cloak, Loki flew to Thjazi's home, turned Idun into a nut, and flew back. Thjazi pursued but was killed by the gods in a fire they built at the walls of Asgard. Idun represents the necessity of renewal and the fragility of immortality — even gods depend on her. Her name may mean 'the rejuvenating one.'

Origins

Idun is described as the daughter of the dwarf Ivaldi (in some accounts) or of unknown parentage. She was brought to Asgard as one of the goddesses serving the æsir. She married Bragi and gave both the gods and herself eternal youth through her apples.

Attested in Poetic Edda (Lokasenna) / Prose Edda (Gylfaginning, Skáldskaparmál)Era Viking Age (c. 800-1100 CE), recorded 13th century

Form

Iðunn is not physically described in the sources; no detail of her body, hair, or dress survives. The Prose Edda and Haustlöng situate her carrying the apples of youth in an eski, a small box of ash-wood used for carrying personal possessions. The 19th-century Romantic-era painters who made her famous (Freund, Blommér, Qvarnström, J. Doyle Penrose, John Bauer, B. E. Ward, E. Doepler, Carl Larsson's 'Brita as Iduna') consistently depict her as a beautiful, youthful woman bearing a basket or casket of apples, often golden, in a natural/idyllic Asgard setting — but this iconography is entirely post-medieval artistic invention, not an attested pagan description.

Powers

Apples of Eternal Youth Iðunn keeps the apples that, when eaten by the gods as they begin to grow old, restore them to youth. The Prose Edda (Gylfaginning ch. 26) states this occurs 'until Ragnarök' — i.e. the apples are a renewable but not eternal fix against the gods' eventual doom. In Haustlöng the rejuvenating substance is referred to obliquely as 'the gods' old-age medicine' / 'the Æsir's age-old cure'. Because she is the sole custodian, the gods are wholly dependent on her; without her, as in the abduction narrative, they 'grow old and grey' and their assembly becomes 'rather ugly in form'. Poetic Edda (Lokasenna), Prose Edda (Gylfaginning 26, Skáldskaparmál), Haustlöng (10th-c. skaldic) The apples work only while the gods are alive and only until Ragnarök; the 'eternal youth' does not prevent Baldr's death or the doom war. The apples are finite and her custody is fragile — the entire myth turns on them being able to be stolen/lured away. Iðunn herself does not wield the rejuvenation actively; she merely stores and distributes the apples. Lindow argues that the etymological sense 'ever young' would let Iðunn grant youth 'without her apples', while Haustlöng's non-mention of apples supports this reading; the Prose Edda, by contrast, makes the apples indispensable. These two framings (the appled vs. the apple-less Iðunn) coexist uneasily.

The eski (ash-wood casket) of apples Iðunn carries her apples in an eski, a wooden box made of ash-wood traditionally used for personal possessions. The casket is her defining material attribute and is what Loki's lure targets and what she must bring for comparison. Prose Edda, Gylfaginning 26 and Skáldskaparmál 56 Not a magical tool in itself; the magic resides in the apples contained within. None significant.

Transformation (as subject, not agent) In the rescue, Loki transforms Iðunn into a nut so he can carry her back to Asgard. She does not transform herself; she is acted upon. The nut form links her to fertility symbolism (Davidson notes nuts as fertility totems) and to potential hidden-agency readings. Prose Edda, Skáldskaparmál 56; also Apples in mythology Undergoes the transformation unwillingly; she has no feats of self-transformation in any source. None.

Keeper of the gods' youth / life-sustaining custodian Beyond the apples, Iðunn's very presence (and faithful custody) is what sustains the gods' youth into Ragnarök. Her name's 'ever young' sense, per Lindow, may allow her to perform this function even apart from the fruit, and North sees her as 'an aspect of Freyja' on whom the gods rely for youth and beauty. Prose Edda; analysis by Lindow, Richard North Structural rather than active power; she is a keeper, not a combatant. The aspect-of-Freyja thesis is scholarly conjecture, not attested myth.

Accounts

Odin, Loki and Hœnir are traveling and slaughter an ox, which will not cook. Þjazi, in eagle form, offers to make the fire work in exchange for a share, then eats so much that the enraged Loki strikes at him with a staff — which sticks fast to the eagle and carries Loki up into the sky, his legs banging against stones and trees. Þjazi frees Loki only on condition that Loki swear to lure Iðunn and her apples of youth out of Asgard. Later Loki tells Iðunn he has found apples fairer than her own and that she should bring hers to compare; she follows him into a wood, where Þjazi, in eagle form, seizes her and bears her off to Þrymheimr in Jötunheimr. Deprived of her apples, the gods grow old and grey; they arrest Loki and threaten him with torture and death until he promises to retrieve her. Borrowing Freyja's falcon-skin coat, Loki flies to Jötunheimr, finds Iðunn alone while Þjazi is out at sea, transforms her into a nut, and carries her back. Þjazi gives chase in eagle form; the gods pile wood in Asgard's courtyard and set it alight; Loki drops Iðunn behind the wall, and the scorched eagle overshoots, plunges through the fire, and falls, whereupon the gods kill him — 'and this killing is greatly renowned.'

At Ægir's feast, after Loki has insulted Bragi, Iðunn speaks in her husband's defense, calling Loki's words 'evil' (stanza 16: '... words all evil / Loki amongst the gods'). Loki retorts that of all men she is madly in love with the one who killed her own brother. Iðunn pleads with Loki to remember the kindness she and Bragi once showed him at a drinking-meeting — kindness that, given the fragmentary record, is never detailed. Loki then turns to Gefjon. The 'killer of her brother' accusation exists nowhere else; neither the brother nor the killer is named in any surviving source.

In a passage unattested anywhere else, Iðunn is identified as descending from elves, as one of 'Ivaldi's elder children', and as a dís who dwells in the dales. This genealogical aside is the source the Wikimedia data-schema's P22 (father: Ivaldi) property draws on, though the identification is contested and likely late.

King Rerir and his wife have no heir; they pray to Odin and Frigg. In response a wish-maiden (in the guise of a crow) drops a magical golden apple onto Rerir's lap. He shares it with the queen, who conceives and endures an extraordinarily long six-year pregnancy; Rerir dies campaigning, and the queen orders her baby cut from her, dying in the process. The surviving child is Völsung, ancestor of the Völsungar heroes. Davidson reads this as further evidence that apples were a symbol of fertility/immortality in Norse tradition, parallel to Iðunn's role.

Content Notes

The central myth is the forcible abduction of a goddess. Iðunn is lured from safety by a trusted figure (Loki acting on oath to Þjazi) with a false promise, then seized and carried bodily against her will to a male giant's remote hall (Þrymheimr). The rescue likewise treats her as cargo: she is transformed into a nut and carried off in a falcon's claws. Neither the abduction nor the rescue frames Iðunn as consenting or as an agent; she is the prize/object of the conflict.

Shows a pattern of abducting female figures into/from the divine sphere; Lindow notes the general movement of 'female jötnar to the gods' is reversed in her abduction, one of the most dangerous moments for the gods.

Prose Edda (Skáldskaparmál 56); Haustlöng

Skáldskaparmál lists 'Þjazi's booty' among her kennings, reducing a goddess to the property of her abductor. In the rescue she is literally shrunk to a nut and clutched in a bird's claws — an object rather than a person.

Highlights the non-individuated, structural role goddesses often play as plot-objects in the Norse myth corpus.

Prose Edda (Skáldskaparmál); narrative of the rescue

The myth's stakes are that without Iðunn's apples the gods 'grow old and grey' and their assembly turns 'ugly'. The immortals are literally dependent on a single keeper's custody, making the gods' youth fragile and hostage to one figure's abduction. This is a structural anxiety about divine mortality.

Lindow reads the abduction as 'one of the most dangerous moments' for the gods.

Prose Edda; Haustlöng

The rescue ends in a group killing: the gods set a pyre in Asgard's courtyard, the eagle-bearded Þjazi plunges through the flames, and the Æsir set upon and kill him. The death is celebrated ('this killing is greatly renowned'), framing lethal vigilante vengeance by the pantheon.

Punishment violence against a jötunn abductor; the same frame underlies Skaði's later atonement.

Prose Edda; Haustlöng

In Lokasenna, Loki accuses Iðunn of having slept with the man who killed her own brother. Neither the brother nor the killer is named anywhere in the surviving corpus, so the charge is unverifiable and its sexual register is a cultural weapon in the flyting exchange.

Hollander reads all Lokasenna slurs as improvisatory insults 'easy to make and difficult to disprove', not trusted lore; but the accusation has a gendered, shaming force.

Poetic Edda, Lokasenna 16–18

In 11th-century verse by Þórbjörn Brúnason (in Heiðarvíga saga), a woman wishes her husband dead and to 'give apples of Hel' — reversing Iðunn's life-giving apples into a poison/death gift. This dark inversion suggests the apple symbol carried a deathly under-image (the food of the dead) alongside its life-giving face.

Davidson reads this as implying the apple was 'the food of the dead' in some strands, a subversion of Iðunn's rejuvenation.

Þórbjörn Brúnason, cited via Davidson / Apples in mythology

Scholarly Debates
Etymology: 'ever young' vs 'rejuvenator' vs yew-tree
'Ever young' (Old Norse ið 'again/ever' + spatial/adjectival sense) — the traditional gloss. ⇌ 'Rejuvenator'/'the rejuvenating one' (from iðja 'to renew') and the contested evergreen-yew (ýr) derivation, linking her to chthonic perennialness.
Evidence for A: Standard dictionary tradition and Snorri's framing of the apple-keeper's name.
Evidence for B: Proposals in etymological literature; Grimm's connection to the idisi ('with the original form idis the goddess Idunn ma
Apples vs 'old-age medicine' as the objective of the myth
The Prose Edda and Apples in mythology make physical apples in an eski the instrument of immortality. ⇌ Haustlöng (10th c., the oldest attestation) never mentions apples — Iðunn is 'the maid who knew the Æsir's old age cure', suggesting the veiled substance may not originally have been apples.
Evidence for A: Gylfaginning 26 and Skáldskaparmál; the eski of apples.
Evidence for B: Haustlöng lines; Lindow's note that the 'ever young' name would let her grant youth without apples.
The father of Iðunn (and her genealogy)
Hrafnagaldr Óðins makes her a child of Ivaldi ('one of Ivaldi's elder children'). ⇌ Wikidata P22 also records Óðr as a purported father; mainstream Eddic sources give her no father at all.
Evidence for A: Hrafnagaldr Óðins st. 6; the daughter-of-Ivaldi framing.
Evidence for B: Absence in the Eddas; Óðr's association with Freyja makes a paternity claim awkward.
The 'killer of her brother' accusation
Lokasenna implies Iðunn had a brother and slept with his killer. ⇌ No brother or killer is named in any other source, and the accusation is a flyting slur.
Evidence for A: Lokasenna 16–18.
Evidence for B: Absence everywhere else; Hollander's reading of the poem as humorous improvisation.
Iðunn as 'an aspect of Freyja'
Richard North: Iðunn is 'probably ... an aspect of Freyja, a goddess whom the gods rely on for their youth and beauty'. ⇌ They are separate named goddesses with distinct relations (Iðunn alone married to Bragi, keeper of apples; Freyja the Vanir love/war goddess who lends the falcon skin).
Evidence for A: North's close reading of Haustlöng and the shared youth/beauty function.
Evidence for B: Independent attestations, distinct spouses, no textual fusion in the Eddas.

A way to hold it in your mind

Of all the Norse gods, Iðunn is the one the immortals literally cannot live without — and the one who spends her most famous adventure as a nut clutched in a falcon's claws. The Æsir can survive the loss of a hammer or a wolf, but snatch away the goddess who keeps the apples of youth and they fray into grey, withered elders at their own assembly, 'rather ugly in form.' Everything about Iðunn is a paradox of guardianship: the keeper the gods 'depend greatly upon' is the one who is lured out of Asgard by a promise of fairer apples, and whose rescue hinges on her being shrunk to a seed and flown home ahead of a burning eagle. She is at once the god of the pantry of immortality and the most fragile hostage the pantheon ever had — a goddess whose only 'power' is faithfully holding the cure her masters need, and whose theft is recorded, in the oldest surviving witness, as 'one of the most dangerous moments' the gods ever faced.
