The Approach

In the annals of feudal Japan, few swordsmen loom as large—or as enigmatically—as Yagyū Jūbē Mitsuyoshi. A master of the blade who vanished from the shogun's court for over a decade, his life is a tapestry of documented service and tantalizing legend, where the historical record fades into the realm of myth.

Content advisory. Contains depictions of violence and assassination in historical and fictional contexts.
Identity

Yagyū Jūbē Mitsuyoshi

Pronunciation YAH-gyoo JOO-bay MIT-soo-yo-shee

Known As

Yagyū JūbēJūbē MitsuyoshiShichirō (birth name)

Etymology

The name Yagyū derives from the clan's ancestral village in Nara. 'Jūbē' is an honorific title, and 'Mitsuyoshi' is his given name. The character '厳' (Mitsu) can mean 'strict' or 'severe'.

Cultural Ownership

Japan (historical and popular culture)

Disputed Identifications

There is scholarly debate over the details of his life, particularly his disappearance and death, with legends conflicting with sparse historical records.

Description

Appearance

Historical records do not provide a detailed physical description. Contemporary portraits show him with both eyes. The popular image of Jūbē with an eyepatch is a later legend, not supported by primary evidence.

Symbols & Iconography

In modern fiction, he is often associated with the eyepatch and a single-bladed sword style. The Yagyū clan's association with the Shinkage-ryū school of swordsmanship is a key symbol.

Behaviour & Personality

Historical records suggest he was bold and brash, leading to his dismissal from the shogun's court. He was a skilled swordsman and author of a martial treatise. Legends portray him as a wandering swordsman perfecting his art.

Domains & Habitats

He lived primarily in Edo (Tokyo) and his ancestral village of Yagyū in Nara. His travels during his twelve-year absence are unknown but likely involved warrior pilgrimages across Japan.

Version Differences

The historical Jūbē differs significantly from the fictionalized versions. The eyepatch, various exploits, and dramatic death scenarios are largely fictional additions. Fictional portrayals vary in the cause of his eye loss (e.g., sword, arrow, training accident) and the circumstances of his death.

Stories & Myths

Life at the Shogun's Court

Source Historical recordsCulture JapanesePeriod Edo period

Summary

In 1616, Jūbē became an attendant to Shogun Tokugawa Hidetada and later served as a sword instructor to Tokugawa Iemitsu. He was dismissed in 1631, possibly due to his boldness or his decision to go on a warrior pilgrimage.

Participants

Yagyū Jūbē

Tokugawa Hidetada

Tokugawa Iemitsu

Consequences

His dismissal led to a twelve-year absence from official records.

Variants

The reason for his dismissal is uncertain.

The Twelve-Year Disappearance

Source Yagyū clan chronicles and Tsuki no ShōCulture JapanesePeriod Edo period

Summary

After his dismissal, Jūbē disappeared from official records for twelve years. His own treatise suggests he traveled the countryside perfecting his swordsmanship.

Participants

Yagyū Jūbē

Consequences

He reappeared at age 36 at a swordsmanship demonstration and was reinstated as a government inspector.

Variants

The exact details of his whereabouts are unknown.

Death and Burial

Source Historical recordsCulture JapanesePeriod Edo period

Summary

Jūbē died in 1650 under uncertain circumstances. Various accounts suggest heart attack, falcon hunting accident, fishing accident, or assassination by his half-brother's attendants. He was buried in his home village.

Participants

Yagyū Jūbē

Yagyū Tomonori (half-brother)

Consequences

He was succeeded by his brother Yagyū Munefuyu.

Variants

Multiple conflicting accounts of his death exist.
