Who It Is

In the oldest Chinese dictionary, nature is sorted not by what is normal, but by what is strange: a softshell turtle with three legs is called nai, a hardshell turtle with three legs is called fen — not a species, but a category for the anomalous, the ominous, and the memorable.

No personality, speech, or agency is attributed in classical sources. The Erya and bencao entries treat fen/nai as passive natural anomalies to be observed and interpreted, not as characters with motives. Zhiguai (anomaly tale) collections do not give them dialogue or intentional action; when mentioned, they appear as omens found in rivers, ponds, or presented to a ruler, after which diviners interpret the meaning. Name & Tongue

Etymology — Modern names are descriptive: san 三 'three' + zu 足 'foot/leg' + gui 龜/龟 'hardshell turtle/tortoise' and bie 鱉/鳖 'softshell turtle' (family Trionychidae). Classical terms are distinct: Fen 賁 (OC *pər, MC bjun) originally means 'adorned, vigorous, large' and is used only in Erya as the technical name

Pronunciation — Mandarin: Sānzúguī [san˥ tsu˧˥ kwei˥], Sānzúbiē [san˥ tsu˧˥ pie˥], Fén [fən˧˥] (also read Bēn/Bì in other contexts; Erya commentaries gloss as fen), Nái [nai˧˥]. Middle Chinese (Baxter): fen < bjun, nai < nəng. Japanese on-reading: sansokuki, sansokubetsu, fun/hon, dai/nou.

Aliases — Sanzugui 三足龜 / 三足龟, Sanzubie 三足鱉 / 三足鳖, Fen 賁 / 贲 (hardshell three-legged turtle), Nai 能 (softshell three-legged turtle), Three-footed turtle, Tripod turtle

Ownership — Han Chinese classical textual tradition. Attested from Warring States to Han lexicography (Erya) and continuously transmitted through Sinitic scholarly culture in China, Korea, Jap

Powers
Omen / portent (zaiyi 災異)

Primary 'power' is semiotic: appearance is read as a Heaven-sent warning of political or natural disorder, flood, or misrule. Not an active ability of the turtle itself but a meaning projected by correlative cosmology. Taiping Yulan places it among anomalies that 'when seen, the ruler should examine himself.'

Limit: Interpretation depends entirely on human diviners; no consistent specific prediction (some texts associate turtle anomalies with water disasters, others with general yin excess). No ability to cause the disaster itself.

Longevity association (by extension from turtles generally)

Normal turtles (gui/bie) are symbols of longevity (shou 壽) and endurance, living 100+ years in lore, supporting the world (Ao). The three-legged variant inherits this association only weakly; bencao notes anomalous turtles are NOT to be used for longevity elixirs, inverting the normal turtle's positive longevity power.

Limit: Explicitly stated as '不可食' — not edible/medicinal — so longevity benefit is negated. No claim of extended lifespan beyond normal turtle.

Divination substrate (potential, but negated)

Normal turtle plastrons (gui ban 龜板) are the classic pyromantic material for Shang oracle bones and later divination. A three-legged turtle's plastron would theoretically be usable but is considered flawed/inauspicious and thus rejected for divination, according to bencao cautions.

Limit: No text describes actually using a three-legged plastron for successful divination; all sources advise against use.

Aquatic adaptation (softshell nai)

As a bie-type softshell, nai would share softshell abilities: highly aquatic, fast swimming, burying in mud, snorkel breathing, aggressive bite. These are natural-history traits, not supernatural powers.

Limit: Three-legged condition would impair swimming and burying; Guo Pu's observation implies reduced mobility, not enhanced.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Classical texts give no detailed iconography beyond leg count and shell type. Fen is defined as a gui-type turtle — implying domed, hard, bony carapace with scutes, beaked head, four limbs in normal form but three in this variant, short tail. Nai is defined as a bie-type softshell — implying flat, leathery, cartilaginous carapace without scutes, snorkel-like snout, webbed limbs, also with three legs. No color, size, or ornament is specified. Guo

Unlike the three-legged crow (solar symbol) or three-legged toad (wealth symbol), the three-legged turtle has no temple image, no bronze motif, and no star-asterism. Its symbolism is entirely omenological: in Han and later correlative thought, anomalous animals (zaiyi 災異) signal disorder in the human or natural realm. Accounts I
The Erya Definition — Naming the AnomalyErya 爾雅, Shi Yu 釋魚

c. 3rd century BCE – 1st century CE (text stabilized in Western Han)

In the oldest surviving Chinese dictionary, the world is classified by definition. Among fish, turtles and reptiles, the text states simply: 'A bie with three feet is called nai, a gui with three feet is called fen.' No narrative, no myth, no origin story — just a name for a thing that should have four legs but has three. The entry establishes that Chinese natural taxonomy had a dedicated slot for the anomalous turtle, treating it as a recognizable category alongside normal turtles, not as a unique monster. Created the canonical terminology used for the next two millennia; every later bencao, leishu and dictionary quotes this line verbatim. Established the gui/bie distinction as central to Chinese turtle classification.

Guo Pu's Commentary — Sometimes Three-LeggedGuo Pu 郭璞, Erya Zhu 爾雅注

Jin dynasty, c. 310 CE

Guo Pu, famed commentator of Shanhaijing and Erya and himself a diviner, annotates the Erya entry with a terse empirical note: turtles and softshells 'sometimes' (huo shi 或時) have three feet. He does not describe a mythic encounter or a specific turtle; he normalizes the anomaly as an occasional natural occurrence. This brief gloss shifts the entry from pure lexicography toward natural history and teratology, implying Guo Pu had seen or heard of real three-legged turtles. Provided the dominant interpretation for later scholars: fen/nai are rare natural variants, not separate supernatural species. Cited by all subsequent leishu and bencao.

Bencao Gangmu — Do Not Eat the Strange TurtleLi Shizhen 李時珍, Bencao Gangmu 本草綱目, Scaly Animals Section

Ming dynasty, 1578–1596 (published 1596)

Li Shizhen, compiling the great materia medica, includes gui and bie as medicinal items (plastron, flesh, blood). Quoting Erya, he notes the three-legged variants fen and nai and explicitly warns: both are anomalous things (yiwu 異物) and should not be eaten or used medicinally. He does not claim they are poisonous in a pharmacological sense, but inauspicious and potentially harmful. The entry is not a story of a particular turtle but a prescriptive note that reflects centuries of omenological caution: what is anomalous should not be consumed to gain longevity. Codified medical rejection of anomalous turtles; repeated verbatim in Qing bencao and Japanese honzōgaku (materia medica) texts. Reinforced association between physical anomaly and ritual impurity.

Accounts II
Leishu Encyclopedias — Among the OmensTaiping Yulan 太平御覽 juan 880 and Taiping Guangji 太平廣記, citing earlier zhiguai

Song dynasty, 978–983 CE, compiling earlier Six Dynasties and Tang anomaly tales

The Song imperial encyclopedias, massive state-sponsored compendia, copy the Erya definition into their chapters on scaly and shelled creatures, placing the three-legged turtle among lists of prodigies: white crows, two-headed snakes, six-legged beasts. No single narrative of a three-legged turtle's adventure is given; instead it appears as an entry in catalogues of zaiyi (disasters and anomalies) that officials should report to the throne. One Taiping Guangji citation (quoting a lost Six Dynasties zhiguai) mentions a three-legged softshell presented to a prefect as an auspicious/inauspicious omen, after which diviners debated its meaning — the turtle itself does nothing. Institutionalized the three-legged turtle as part of bureaucratic omen reporting, linking natural anomaly to political cosmology. No independent mythic cycle developed.

Wakan Sansai Zue — The Turtle Crosses to JapanTerajima Ryōan 寺島良安, Wakan Sansai Zue 和漢三才圖會

Edo period, 1712

Terajima, compiling the Japanese analogue to the Chinese Sancai Tuhui, includes an entry for the three-legged turtle under reptiles, copying the Bencao Gangmu definition and providing a woodcut illustration: a turtle viewed from above with three legs (one foreleg missing). The text gives Japanese readings fun and nou and notes 'this is a strange turtle, not used.' The entry shows how the Chinese lexicographic category was transmitted intact to Japan, where it was classified among 'strange beasts' rather than native Japanese yokai. No Japanese folklore tale is attached; the creature remains a Chinese textual import. Demonstrates Sinosphere transmission; provided the visual template copied in later Japanese popular encyclopedias and modern yōkai dictionaries that list the three-legged turtle as a Chinese yōkai.

Modern Teratology — The Three-Legged Turtle Is BornContemporary Chinese popular science and teratology reports (e.g., Shanghai Natural History Museum displays; news reports 2010–2023)

21st century

Modern Chinese news and museum reports periodically describe real turtles hatched with three legs due to congenital limb malformation (ectromelia), often attributed to incubation temperature, genetics or pollution. Articles frequently invoke the ancient terms fen/nai and the Erya definition to frame the modern birth as a 'living three-legged turtle from legend.' Scientists explain the condition as developmental anomaly, not a species. These reports constitute the only 'stories' where a specific three-legged turtle is an individual protagonist — e.g., a three-legged softshell found in Jiangsu in 2015 that survived in a zoo, or a pet red-eared slider with three legs kept by a hobbyist. The narrative is demythologized: anomaly as biology, not omen. Reframes classical omen category as teratological reality; provides material specimens that anchor ancient text in observable biology. Frequently cited online as proof that 'the Erya described a real mutation.'

The three-legged turtle is the same as the three-legged crow (Sanzuwu) or three-legged toad (Jin Chan), just a different animal form of the same 'three-legged auspicious beast.' — All three are distinct: Sanzuwu is a solar crow deity with extensive mythology (ten suns, Hou Yi myth, Han iconography); Jin Chan is a wealth toad (Liu Hai legend); fen/nai are lexicographic anomaly categories for turtles with no deity status, no solar/lunar association, and no wealth symbolism. They share only the descriptor 'three-legged' (sanzu).

Ancient Chinese believed three-legged turtles were a common mythical species that lived on Penglai or other immortal islands. — No classical text places fen/nai on Penglai, Kunlun, or any mythic realm, and no narrative describes a population of three-legged turtles. Erya and all later citations treat them as rare individual anomalies found in ordinary rivers/ponds, not as inhabitants of a mythic land. The immortal-island turtle is Ao, a giant normal turtle.

The Erya entry proves ancient Chinese had scientific knowledge of genetics and deliberately bred three-legged turtles. — Erya is a dictionary, not a genetics text; it names an observed or hypothetical anomaly without explaining cause. No breeding, genetic theory, or experimental account is given. Guo Pu and Li Shizhen treat it as occasional natural occurrence or omen, not as bred variety.
