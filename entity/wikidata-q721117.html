Form

No direct visual depictions of Asaruludu have been identified with certainty. In textual descriptions, he is associated with radiance and light, often described as carrying a torch or lamp. In the Surpu incantations, his 'shining face' is said to repel evil spirits. Given his association with purification, he may have been depicted with water vessels or purification implements, though this remains speculative.

Powers

Purification Asaruludu's primary power is the ability to cleanse spiritual and physical impurity. He removes the effects of broken oaths, curses, and taboo violations. In the Surpu series, his invocation is the central ritual act for absolving the afflicted. Sumerian and Akkadian traditions Requires proper ritual procedure; the afflicted must also confess and make restitution. None noted in available sources.

Light Manipulation Asaruludu wields light as a weapon against demons and evil spirits. His 'shining face' and 'burning torch' drive away malevolent forces. Akkadian incantation tradition The light is specifically anti-demonic; it does not appear to have offensive applications against mortals. None noted in available sources.

Oath Enforcement Asaruludu punishes oath-breakers with skin diseases (leprosy, scabies) and other afflictions. This makes him a terrifying witness to treaties and legal agreements. Kudurru inscriptions, Neo-Babylonian period Punishments are inflicted on the oath-breaker and their descendants; no evidence of mercy or revocation. This punitive role seems at odds with his healing function, though both are consistent with his role as a god of boundaries between pure and impure.

Exorcism Asaruludu is invoked in exorcism rituals to expel demons from possessed individuals and haunted spaces. Akkadian incantation series Requires a trained exorcist (āšipu) to perform the ritual correctly. None noted in available sources.

Behavior & Habitat

Asaruludu is consistently portrayed as a purifier and protector. He acts against demons, witches, and malevolent forces, cleansing both individuals and sacred spaces. In the Surpu series, he is invoked to break curses and undo ritual impurity. His behavior is that of a divine healer-priest, wielding light as a weapon against darkness.

Accounts

After Marduk's victory over Tiamat and his creation of the cosmos, the great gods bestow fifty names upon him, each representing an aspect of his power. Asaruludu is the forty-eighth name, given to honor Marduk's role as the purifier who 'carries the shining torch' and 'restores the gods to their shrines.' This text effectively absorbs the older deity Asaruludu into Marduk's identity, making him a manifestation of Marduk's purifying power.

The Surpu series addresses the problem of 'mamitu' - a curse or oath that has been inadvertently broken, causing suffering and misfortune. Asaruludu is invoked as the divine purifier who can break these curses. The ritual involves the burning of symbolic materials (garlic, dates, wool) while the exorcist recites incantations calling on Asaruludu to 'shine forth' and 'drive away the evil.' The afflicted person is then cleansed with water and declared pure.

Kudurrus (boundary stones) were carved with the images and names of gods who were invoked to protect land grants and legal agreements. Asaruludu is frequently listed among these divine witnesses. The inscriptions warn that any who violates the boundary or breaks the agreement will be struck with 'leprosy, scabies, and incurable skin diseases' by Asaruludu. This made him a terrifying guarantor of property rights.

Scholarly Debates
Independent deity vs. aspect of Marduk
Asaruludu was an independent deity of the Eridu region, later absorbed into Marduk's cult. ⇌ Asaruludu was always an aspect of Marduk, representing his purifying power.
Evidence for A: Early Sumerian god lists and temple hymns treat Asaruludu as a distinct deity with his own cult.
Evidence for B: The Enuma Elish explicitly includes Asaruludu among Marduk's fifty names, and no clear evidence of an independent cult s
Relationship to Asalluhi
Asaruludu and Asalluhi are the same deity, with the names being variants. ⇌ They are separate deities with overlapping functions.
Evidence for A: Both names appear in similar contexts (incantations, purification rituals) and have similar meanings.
Evidence for B: Both names appear in the same god lists, suggesting they were considered distinct.
What We Don't Know

The archaeological evidence for Asaruludu's cult is sparse. No temple dedicated to him has been identified with certainty. The exact meaning of his name remains debated. His relationship to Asalluhi requires further study. The full range of his epithets and their development over time has not been comprehensively catalogued. Access to primary sources (cuneiform tablets) is limited to specialized scholars.
