Who It Is

On a weathered stone at Besançon, a Syrian woman paid to rebuild a ruined temple for a god most Romans would have simply called Mercury — but she called him Mercurius Cissonius, using a surname that appears nowhere in myth, only in the hard Latin of frontier dedications from the Rhine to the Swiss plateau. Who was Cissonius, and why did merchants, carters, and perhaps dreamers along the roads of Germania Superior invoke him by a name the poets never recorded?

No myth or narrative characterizes Cissonius's personality (unknown). As an aspect of Mercury, his behavior is inferred from Mercury's Gallo-Roman profile: patron of commerce, roads, travel, messengers, and boundary-crossing. Van Andringa emphasizes that native surnames like Cissonius could be descriptive, topographic, or communal, not necessarily personality traits. The Vesontio inscription shows the god as recipient of euergetism and temple maintenance, implying a civic, protective role. Powers
Mercurial patronage of commerce and travel (interpretatio)

As Mercurius Cissonius, the god shares Mercury's attested powers: protection of merchants, travellers, roads, and transactions. The pairing with Negotiator at Nida and the concentration along Rhine trade corridors supports this as the core functional domain.

Limit: No Cissonius-specific miracle or feat is narrated; powers are inferred from Mercury, not independently attested for Cissonius. Cannot claim distinct commercial technique beyond Mercury.

Guardianship of carters/wagons (disputed, Hatt etymology)

If derived from Gaulish *cissum 'carriage, wagon', Cissonius would be Mercury as protector of carters, wagon-drivers, and overland transport, a specialization of his travel domain.

Limit: Etymology is disputed and marked uncertain by Delamarre and de Vries; no inscription mentions carts, wagons, or transport; no iconographic cart appears with Cissonius stones.

Dream-bringing / oneiric mediation (highly tentative, Delamarre hypothesis)

If from *cit-souno- 'bringing dreams' (cit- intensive + sounos 'sleep/dream'), Cissonius would have power to send or govern dreams, aligning with Mercury's role as messenger and psychopomp who moves between worlds.

Limit: Delamarre himself marks derivation uncertain; no epigraphic or literary text links Cissonius to dreams, sleep, or oracles; purely linguistic speculation.

Autonomous local divine identity (deus Cissonius)

In several dedications the god is invoked as deus Cissonius without Mercury, suggesting capacity to act as an independent local deity, receiving vows (votum) and temple cult on his own name, while still being interpretable as Mercury.

Limit: Autonomy may be epigraphic shorthand rather than theology; no separate mythology or distinct powers accompany the standalone name; iconography remains Mercurial.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No distinct iconography is attested for Cissonius alone (attested). All visual evidence falls under Gallo-Roman Mercury. In Gaul, Mercury is the most frequently depicted god, typically as a beardless youth with winged hat (petasos), winged sandals, caduceus (kerykeion), purse (marsupium), and often a rooster, tortoise, or goat. At the Altbachtal and Rhineland sites where Cissonius is named, the associated Mercury images follow this standard type.

Symbols — Attested symbols are those of Mercury: caduceus, purse, petasos, rooster. If Hatt's *cissum 'cart' etymology were correct, a cart/wagon emblem would be expected but none is documented in association with Cissonius stones (disputed). Accounts I
Dubitatia Castula Rebuilds the Temple at VesontioCIL XIII 5425 (Besançon, Musée des Beaux-Arts et d'Archéologie); discussed by Hatt, Van Andringa, and EDCS

Imperial Roman, 2nd century CE (undated, imperial lettering)

Dubitatia Castula, who identifies herself as natione Sura (Syrian by origin), records that the temple and portico of Mercurius Cissonius at Vesontio had collapsed through age (vetustate conlapsum) and that she restored it from the ground at her own expense (de suo restituit). The text is a building dedication, not a myth, but it narrates a concrete cult history: an established sanctuary fell into ruin and was revived by private euergetism from a woman of eastern origin living in Gaul. Demonstrates that Cissonius had a templum with porticus (architectural sanctuary, not just altar) at the civitas capital; shows geographic mobility and female patronage in provincial religion; provides the only extended narrative text for Cissonius beyond short votive formulae.

The Altbachtal Precinct: Multiple Mercuries at Augusta TreverorumAltbachtal sanctuary excavations (Gose 1955; Wightman 1970); CIL XIII 3638-3665 series; fragment CIL XIII 3653 (Cissonius)

Flavian (late 1st c.) to late 3rd century CE (destruction c. 275 CE)

At Augusta Treverorum, capital of the Treveri, a vast extra-mural sacred area (Altbachtal) contained some 70 small temples. The central Mercury temple is dedicated without surname, but two fragmentary inscriptions from the same precinct name Mercury as Cissonius and as Visucius. The story is not a myth but a cult-topographic narrative: within one sanctuary complex, the Treveri worshipped Mercury under multiple native surnames side by side, suggesting either distinct chapels or successive dedications by different groups. Shows that Cissonius was not exclusive at Trier and coexisted with Visucius; supports interpretation of surnames as community or functional subdivisions rather than separate gods; helps define Cissonius's westernmost core distribution.

Mercury Negotiator and Mercury Cissonius at Nida-HeddernheimCIL XIII 7353 and Heddernheim vicus inscriptions; discussed by William Van Andringa (2002)

2nd century CE

At the vicus of Nida (Heddernheim), a civilian settlement near the limes, Mercury is invoked in one dedication as Negotiator (the Trader) and in another as Cissonius. Van Andringa uses this juxtaposition to argue that if Hatt's carter etymology is right, Cissonius would be the native counterpart to the Latin functional title Negotiator - both framing Mercury as god of commerce and transport. The narrative is epigraphic: two ways of naming the same mercantile function, one Latin, one indigenous. Provides the strongest contextual argument for Hatt's mercantile interpretation; links Cissonius to economic life on the frontier; shows bilingual naming strategies in Gallo-Roman religion.

Accounts II
The Rhineland Cluster: Private Vows along the Rhine RoadCIL XIII 6326 (Mühlburg), CIL XIII 5303 (Riegel), CIL XIII 7584-7585 (Cologne), EDCS records for Ehl, Ladenburg (Lopodunum)

1st-3rd century CE (peak 2nd c.)

Dozens of small altars from vici and roadside settlements along the Upper Rhine (Mühlburg/Karlsruhe, Riegel, Ehl in Alsace, Ladenburg) bear the short formula Mercurio Cissonio VSLM (votum solvit libens merito). Each is a micro-narrative: an individual, often with Celtic or Latin name, makes a vow in crisis or gratitude and fulfills it with a stone. The repetition of the same formula across sites creates the collective story of Cissonius as a road-god of the Rhine corridor, invoked by travellers and locals for safe passage or successful business. Defines the core distribution (Germania Superior/Swiss plateau) and the social base (private, not state cult); absence of imperial or military dedications suggests a civilian, possibly guild-based cult; supports Van Andringa's view of Mercury as most popular god in Gaul.

The Name Without Mercury: Deus Cissonius as Independent InvocationSeveral fragmentary altars listed as Deus Cissonius (e.g., Trier fragment, some Rhineland stones) cited by MacKillop and EDCS; CIL XIII 3653 variant readings

2nd-3rd century CE

In a minority of texts the dedicant omits Mercury entirely and addresses the god simply as Deus Cissonius (or Cissonius alone). The narrative here is theological: a worshipper felt the native name sufficient, implying Cissonius could stand alone as a divine persona. This mirrors practice for other Mercury surnames (Visucius, Arvernus) where the native name sometimes eclipses the Roman one. Fuels scholarly debate on whether Cissonius was originally an independent Celtic deity later identified with Mercury (interpretatio) or always an epithet; MacKillop includes him among surnames suggesting distinct identity; more cautious scholars see it as abbreviation.

Modern Rediscovery: From CIL to Digital CorpusCorpus Inscriptionum Latinarum XIII (1901-1943); L'Année épigraphique (AE); Epigraphik-Datenbank Clauss-Slaby (EDCS); recent syntheses by Delamarre, Van Andringa, Maier

Discovery 19th century to present (stones found 19th-early 20th c., digitized 1990s-2020s)

Cissonius has no medieval or early modern afterlife; his story after antiquity is one of epigraphic rediscovery. Stones were collected in 19th-century corpora (CIL XIII), re-edited in AE, and digitized in EDCS. Each republication reframes the god: de Vries (1961) catalogues him among obscure Mercury names, Hatt proposes the cart etymology, Delamarre (2003) re-etymologizes as dream-bringer, Van Andringa (2002) re-contextualizes him in Gallo-Roman religion, and dictionaries (Green, MacKillop, Maier) popularize him for Anglophone audiences. The modern narrative is thus historiographic rather than mythological. Keeps Cissonius visible despite absence from literary sources; shows how a god known only from stones survives through scholarly transmission; highlights ongoing uncertainty as new readings of Gaulish lexicon shift interpretation.

Cissonius was the Celtic god of wagons/carts and protected all travellers with his wagon. — This is Hatt's hypothesis based on *cissum 'cart', not an attested function. No inscription mentions wagons, carts, or travel, and no image shows a wagon. The meaning is disputed and the cart etymology is one of several uncertain proposals.

Cissonius was a major pan-Celtic god worshipped across Gaul and Britain, with myths and festivals. — Cissonius is attested only in a tight cluster of Latin votive inscriptions (Germania Superior core, outliers to Cologne and Raetia), with no literary myth, festival, or British attestation. He is a regional Gallo-Roman epithet of Mercury, not a pan-Celtic deity.

Cissonius and Visucius are two names for the same god, or Cissonius is just another spelling of Cisonius as a different god. — Cissonius/Cisonius are orthographic variants of one name (gemination variation). Visucius is a distinct epithet with separate distribution, though both are Mercury surnames and co-occur at Trier. They are not synonyms and should not be merged.

We know what Cissonius looked like - he is depicted with a wagon or with dream symbols. — No statue, relief, or description is inscribed as Cissonius with distinctive attributes. All associated imagery is standard Gallo-Roman Mercury (petasos, caduceus, purse, rooster). Any wagon or dream iconography is modern reconstruction from disputed etymology, not evidence.
