Who They Are

The world is dark. Not nighttime dark — absolute, soul-crushing dark that has lasted for days. You can hear millions of kami screaming in the blackness. Outside a cave, the gods are throwing a party, dancing, laughing, anything to coax her out. And then, through the crack in the stone, a single thread of light. You burst into tears. You didn't realize you'd forgotten what hope felt like.

Amaterasu Ōmikami is the supreme deity of Shinto, the goddess of the sun and the universe, and the divine ancestress of the Japanese imperial family. She is not a wrathful sun god who scorches the earth but a nurturing, radiant presence whose light makes life possible. Her name means 'Great Divinity Illuminating Heaven,' and in Shinto belief, her light is not merely physical — it is the illumination of truth, order, and harmony itself.

But Amaterasu is not passive or meek. When her brother Susanoo, the storm god, went on a rampage that destroyed her rice fields, flung a flayed horse into her weaving hall, and killed one of her attendants, she did not fight him. She retreated into the Heavenly Rock Cave and sealed it behind her, plunging the entire cosmos into darkness. This was not surrender — it was the ultimate protest, a divine strike that forced every other god in heaven to confront what a world without her light actually meant.

“The world is dark.”
Origins

Amaterasu was born from the left eye of Izanagi, the primordial creator god, as he purified himself after escaping the underworld Yomi. Her brother Tsukuyomi, the moon god, was born from his right eye, and Susanoo from his nose. Izanagi gave Amaterasu dominion over the High Celestial Plain (Takamagahara), making her the ruler of the heavenly realm. The myth of her retreat into the cave — Ama-no-Iwato — is the central crisis of Shinto cosmology, resolved when the goddess Ame-no-Uzume performed a bawdy dance that made the assembled gods laugh so loudly Amaterasu peeked out, and the god Ame-no-Tajikarao pulled her forth.

Form

A woman of sublime, radiant beauty cloaked in light so brilliant it is difficult to look at directly. She wears flowing white and crimson robes of imperial style, adorned with magatama (curved jewels) and the sacred mirror Yata no Kagami. Her hair is long and black, often depicted flowing around her like a solar corona. In some representations, she is surrounded by an eight-fold halo of light rays. She carries the divine regalia — mirror, jewel, and sword — that she passed to her grandson Ninigi and through him to the Japanese emperors.

Powers

Solar illumination and control of daylight Amaterasu embodies and governs the sun’s light, bringing day and visibility to the heavens and the earth. Her withdrawal into the rock cave causes universal darkness, and her re-emergence restores light and order. This power is not described as a discrete technique but as her essential nature as sun kami. Kojiki (Ama-no-Iwato episode) and Nihon Shoki variants describing her as Hi no Kami (sun kami). [[0]](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9440) [[1]](https://kugehio.info/article/amaterasu-japanese-sun-goddess-shinto-mythology) Her light can be withdrawn when she retreats, suggesting that her presence is contingent on her willingness to engage with the world. The myths do not describe external constraints on her solar power, but they imply that social and ritual conditions—such as the behavior of other kami—affect whether she continues to shine upon the world. No major contradictions about her solar nature exist within classical texts, though modern scholars debate whether the cave myth should be read literally, symbolically, or politically.

Sovereignty over Takamagahara Amaterasu is entrusted by Izanagi (and in some Nihon Shoki variants by both Izanagi and Izanami) with rule over the Plain of High Heaven, making her the highest kami in the celestial hierarchy. She exercises authority by commissioning other kami, judging Susanoo’s behavior, and participating in decisions about the transfer of rule to the earthly realm. Kojiki (post-purification commissioning of Amaterasu) and Nihon Shoki scrolls 1–2. [[0]](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9440) [[2]](https://en.senkohome.com/myths-religions-origins-japanese/) Her sovereignty is framed within a cooperative relationship with other high kami such as Takamimusuhi; decisions about sending Ninigi and negotiating with earthly kami are shared. The texts do not depict her as omnipotent; rather, she is a supreme ruler within a network of divine actors. Some Nihon Shoki variants emphasize Takamimusuhi’s role more strongly, leading to scholarly debate about whether Amaterasu or Takamimusuhi should be considered the primary sovereign of heaven.

Creative power through ukei (trial by pledge) In the contest with Susanoo, Amaterasu participates in a ritual of mutual oath-taking and object transformation (ukei), resulting in the birth of five male and three female kami from the regalia and ornaments they exchange. She claims the male kami as her own children, asserting genealogical and political precedence. Kojiki ukei episode; Nihon Shoki parallel accounts. [[0]](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9440) The creative power is context-specific, arising from ritual exchange and divine objects rather than a general ability to create life at will. Interpretations differ on whether the ukei is primarily a test of purity, a political contest, or a cosmogonic act; the texts themselves present it as a trial of sincerity with creative consequences.

Bestowal and mediation of imperial legitimacy Through the myth of Ninigi’s descent and the transmission of the Three Imperial Regalia, Amaterasu confers divine authority on the imperial line. The mirror, sword, and jewel function as tangible signs of her mandate, and the emperor is presented as her descendant. Kojiki (descent of Ninigi and regalia), Nihon Shoki (imperial genealogies), Engishiki norito, and Ise Jingū ritual tradition. [[4]](https://theoriesofanything.com/research/the-kojiki-japan-s-record-of-ancient-matters) [[2]](https://en.senkohome.com/myths-religions-origins-japanese/) This power operates within mythic and ritual frameworks; its political application has varied across historical periods and has been contested in modern constitutional debates. Modern historians question the historicity of an unbroken divine lineage and critique the use of Amaterasu’s myth to justify imperial absolutism, especially in the prewar era.

Withdrawal and restoration of cosmic order By retreating into the rock cave, Amaterasu disrupts the cosmic order, and by emerging she restores it. This capacity to suspend and resume the world’s normal functioning underscores her centrality to the cosmos. Kojiki Ama-no-Iwato episode; Nihon Shoki variants. [[1]](https://kugehio.info/article/amaterasu-japanese-sun-goddess-shinto-mythology) [[0]](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9440) Her withdrawal is triggered by emotional and moral offense rather than arbitrary whim, suggesting that cosmic order depends on ethical relations among kami. Some modern interpretations read the cave myth as an allegory for solar eclipses or seasonal cycles, while others emphasize its ritual and political dimensions; these are interpretive contradictions rather than textual ones.

Behavior & Habitat

Amaterasu is generally serene, benevolent, and orderly. She values harmony (wa) above all else and governs through quiet authority rather than displays of power. However, she has a limit: when her brother Susanoo's chaos became intolerable, her response was dramatic and world-altering. She is curious (peeking out of the cave to see the party) and can be moved by joy and community. She favors peaceful resolution over conflict but is not afraid to use her power as leverage — her withdrawal of light was the most effective negotiation tactic in mythological history.

Weaknesses & Wards

Her commitment to harmony can make her avoidant of necessary confrontation. She is susceptible to curiosity and trickery — the gods lured her out of the cave by exploiting her desire to see what was causing the laughter. Her power is tied to her presence; when she hides, everything suffers. Her relationship with Susanoo is a persistent source of cosmic instability.

Accounts

After the death of Izanami and his journey to Yomi, Izanagi performs a purification (misogi) in a river. As he washes his left eye, Amaterasu is born, shining with radiant light. From his right eye comes Tsukuyomi, and from his nose Susanoo. Izanagi assigns Amaterasu to rule Takamagahara, Tsukuyomi to govern the night, and Susanoo to rule the seas. Nihon Shoki preserves alternate versions in which Amaterasu is jointly produced by Izanagi and Izanami as Hi no Kami or emerges from a white copper mirror held in Izanagi’s left hand.

Susanoo ascends to Takamagahara after being assigned rule over the seas, prompting Amaterasu to suspect his intentions. To test his sincerity, they engage in a ukei (trial by pledge), exchanging regalia and ornaments. From Amaterasu’s jewel necklace, Susanoo produces three female kami; from Susanoo’s sword, Amaterasu produces five male kami. Amaterasu claims the male kami as her own children, asserting that they arose from her ‘seed’ (monozane). Susanoo, confident in his vindication, subsequently behaves outrageously, desecrating Amaterasu’s fields and sacred spaces.

After the ukei, Susanoo’s behavior becomes destructive: he destroys rice fields, scatters excrement in the palace, and flays a sacred horse, throwing its body into Amaterasu’s weaving hall, causing the death of one of her attendants. Horrified and offended, Amaterasu retreats into the Heavenly Rock Cave, sealing herself inside and plunging the world into darkness. The other kami gather to devise a plan to lure her out. Ame-no-Uzume performs a bawdy, ecstatic dance on an upturned tub, causing the assembled kami to laugh uproariously. Curious about the laughter despite the darkness, Amaterasu opens the cave door slightly. The strong kami Ame-no-Tajikarao pulls her out, while a mirror placed before the cave reflects her own light back to her. The cave is sealed to prevent her return, and light is restored to the world.

Having established her supremacy in Takamagahara, Amaterasu, together with Takamimusuhi, decides to entrust rule of the earthly realm to her descendants. She first commissions Ameno-Oshihomimi, but after consultations and negotiations with the earthly kami (kunitsukami), the mission is ultimately given to Ninigi-no-Mikoto, her grandson. Ninigi descends to earth with the Three Imperial Regalia—the mirror, sword, and jewel—as tokens of Amaterasu’s authority. This descent establishes the divine origin of the imperial line, which traces its ancestry back to Amaterasu through Ninigi.

Cultural Significance

Amaterasu is the central deity of Shinto, Japan's indigenous religion. The Ise Grand Shrine, rebuilt every twenty years in an ancient cycle of renewal, has housed her sacred mirror for nearly two millennia and is the holiest site in Japan. The Japanese imperial family traces its unbroken lineage to her, making her not just a god but the wellspring of Japanese sovereignty. Her cave myth is one of the world's great stories of depression, withdrawal, and the healing power of community and joy.

Modern Interpretations

Amaterasu appears as a divine white wolf in the acclaimed video game 'Ōkami,' where she fights to restore color and life to a cursed world. In anime and manga, she is a recurring figure of serene, ultimate power — from 'Naruto' (as the Mangekyo Sharingan technique) to 'Noragami.' Modern Japanese media often explores the tension between her harmonious nature and the necessary exercise of power, reflecting Japan's post-war identity struggles.

Content Notes

During his rampage, Susanoo flays a sacred horse and throws its body into Amaterasu’s weaving hall, causing the death of one of her attendants. This act combines sacrilege, violence against animals, and the disruption of women’s ritual labor. Amaterasu’s subsequent withdrawal into the cave is framed as a response to this violation.

Feminist and ritual studies scholars have highlighted the gendered dimension of this scene, noting that the weaving hall represents women’s sacred work and that its desecration reflects broader patterns of violence against female spaces in myth. [[1]](https://kugehio.info/article/amaterasu-japanese-sun-goddess-shinto-mythology) [[0]](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9440)

Kojiki Ama-no-Iwato episode; Nihon Shoki variants.

Susanoo’s actions include destroying rice fields, scattering excrement in the palace, and violating boundaries between pure and impure spaces. These acts create ritual pollution that affects Amaterasu and the heavenly realm.

Shinto ritual theory interprets these episodes as dramatizations of kegare (pollution) and the necessity of purification and social order. Amaterasu’s withdrawal underscores the consequences of unchecked transgression.

Kojiki; Nihon Shoki.

Amaterasu’s status as imperial ancestress was central to state Shinto in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. The myth of her divine mandate was used to justify the emperor’s sacral authority, support militaristic expansion, and frame Japan’s colonial projects as divinely sanctioned.

Historians and religious studies scholars have critiqued the politicization of Amaterasu’s myth, emphasizing its role in wartime propaganda and the suppression of alternative religious expressions. Postwar reforms explicitly rejected the emperor’s status as a living deity, reshaping the public understanding of Amaterasu.

Modern historical scholarship on Meiji and prewar Japan; analyses of state Shinto and imperial ideology.

The elevation of Amaterasu as a uniquely supreme sun goddess has sometimes been used to assert Japanese cultural superiority, marginalizing other traditions and justifying nationalist narratives.

Postcolonial and comparative religion scholars warn against reading Amaterasu’s myth as evidence of inherent national destiny, highlighting how myth can be mobilized for exclusionary and oppressive purposes.

Modern critical scholarship on nationalism and religion in Japan.

A way to hold it in your mind

Amaterasu-Ōmikami is the sun that thinks—radiant, offended, retreating into stone when the world violates her order, then returning in a burst of laughter and mirrored light to restart the cosmos. Her stories sit at the hinge of Japanese myth and history: a goddess born from a god’s tear-washed eye, a sovereign whose absence plunges heaven into chaos, and an ancestress whose name has justified emperors, shrines, and wars. To follow Amaterasu is to trace how a single beam of mythic sunlight can illuminate ritual, politics, and identity across more than a millennium.
