Archive Dossier — Identity

Amaterasu Ōmikami

Pronunciation [ä́.mä̀.tè̞.ɾä̀.sɨ̀] (ah-mah-teh-rah-soo); full title: [ä́.mä̀.tè̞.ɾä̀.sɨ̀ ò̞ó̞.mʲí.kä̀.mʲì]

Known As

Amaterasu Ōmikami (天照大御神 / 天照大神)Amateru Kami (天照神)Ōhirume no Muchi (大日孁貴)Tenshō Daijin (天照大神)Tenshō Kōtaijin (天照皇大神)Amaterasu Sume ŌkamiShinmei (神明)

Etymology

From Japanese ama ('heaven') + terasu ('to illuminate, shine'), literally 'She Who Illuminates Heaven.' The full title Amaterasu Ōmikami means 'The Great August Goddess Who Shines in Heaven.' The name is semantically transparent — it directly describes her function — unlike most kami names in the Kojiki which are opaque and allusive.

Cultural Ownership

Japanese (Shinto); central to the Imperial House's mythological legitimacy

First Attested

The Kojiki (古事記, 'Records of Ancient Matters'), compiled 712 CE; also the Nihon Shoki (日本書紀, 'Chronicles of Japan'), compiled 720 CE. These are Japan's oldest extant texts.

Region

Japan; chief shrine: Ise Jingū (Grand Shrine of Ise) in Mie Prefecture, the most sacred Shinto site

Archive Dossier — Approach

When the sun goddess locked herself in a cave, the world went dark — and the gods threw a party to get her back. Mirrors, laughter, and a dancing goddess who made the heavens roar with mirth: Amaterasu's story is not a conquest of darkness by force but a coaxing of light through joy. She is the sun — but she is also the grandmother of emperors, the soul of a mirror, and the reason Japan's oldest shrine is rebuilt every twenty years in a ritual that has never stopped.

The world is dark. Not nighttime dark — absolute, soul-crushing dark that has lasted for days.

Who They Are

The world is dark. Not nighttime dark — absolute, soul-crushing dark that has lasted for days. You can hear millions of kami screaming in the blackness. Outside a cave, the gods are throwing a party, dancing, laughing, anything to coax her out. And then, through the crack in the stone, a single thread of light. You burst into tears. You didn't realize you'd forgotten what hope felt like.

Amaterasu Ōmikami is the supreme deity of Shinto, the goddess of the sun and the universe, and the divine ancestress of the Japanese imperial family. She is not a wrathful sun god who scorches the earth but a nurturing, radiant presence whose light makes life possible. Her name means 'Great Divinity Illuminating Heaven,' and in Shinto belief, her light is not merely physical — it is the illumination of truth, order, and harmony itself.

But Amaterasu is not passive or meek. When her brother Susanoo, the storm god, went on a rampage that destroyed her rice fields, flung a flayed horse into her weaving hall, and killed one of her attendants, she did not fight him. She retreated into the Heavenly Rock Cave and sealed it behind her, plunging the entire cosmos into darkness. This was not surrender — it was the ultimate protest, a divine strike that forced every other god in heaven to confront what a world without her light actually meant.

“The world is dark.”
Archive Dossier — Description

Summary

Amaterasu Ōmikami — the Great Goddess Who Illuminates Heaven — is the supreme solar deity of Shinto and the mythological progenitrix of the Japanese imperial line. Born from the left eye of the creator god Izanagi during his purification (misogi) after escaping the underworld of Yomi, she was appointed ruler of Takamagahara, the High Plain of Heaven. She is the eldest of the 'Three Precious Children' (Mihashira no Uzu no Miko), alongside her brothers Tsukuyomi (the moon) and Susanoo (storms and the sea). Her most famous myth — the Ama-no-Iwato episode — recounts how Susanoo's destructive rampage drove her to retreat into a cave, plunging heaven and earth into darkness. The assembled kami's elaborate ruse to lure her out, culminating in the goddess Ame-no-Uzume's ecstatic dance and Amaterasu's glimpse of her own reflection in the Yata no Kagami mirror, is one of the world's great solar-deity withdrawal-and-return narratives. Amaterasu then dispatched her grandson Ninigi-no-Mikoto to rule the terrestrial realm, bestowing upon him the Three Sacred Treasures — the mirror (Yata no Kagami), the sword (Kusanagi-no-Tsurugi), and the jewel (Yasakani no Magatama) — which remain the imperial regalia of Japan to this day. Her cult center, the Grand Shrine of Ise, houses the sacred mirror (shintai) and is rebuilt every twenty years in a ritual that has continued for over a millennium. Amaterasu occupies a unique position among world deities: she is simultaneously a mythological figure, a living focus of worship for millions, and a political symbol whose genealogy anchors the Japanese state's foundational ideology. The Meiji Restoration (1868) elevated her to the supreme deity of State Shinto, a role that was used to mobilize nationalist sentiment until Japan's defeat in 1945. Post-war, the emperor formally renounced claims to divinity, but Amaterasu's worship at Ise and thousands of branch shrines (Shinmei shrines) continues as a vibrant religious practice.

Origins

Amaterasu was born from the left eye of Izanagi, the primordial creator god, as he purified himself after escaping the underworld Yomi. Her brother Tsukuyomi, the moon god, was born from his right eye, and Susanoo from his nose. Izanagi gave Amaterasu dominion over the High Celestial Plain (Takamagahara), making her the ruler of the heavenly realm. The myth of her retreat into the cave — Ama-no-Iwato — is the central crisis of Shinto cosmology, resolved when the goddess Ame-no-Uzume performed a bawdy dance that made the assembled gods laugh so loudly Amaterasu peeked out, and the god Ame-no-Tajikarao pulled her forth.

Archive Dossier — Powers

Solar radiance and illumination

Description

Amaterasu's primary power is the sun itself — she IS light. When she withdraws into the cave, heaven and earth are plunged into total darkness; when she emerges, light returns. Her radiance is the source of all life, growth, and visibility in the cosmos.

Sovereignty over Takamagahara

Description

As ruler of the High Plain of Heaven, Amaterasu governs the celestial kami, presides over divine affairs, and delegates authority to her descendants on earth. Her sovereignty is the mythological basis for imperial rule.

Confidence

high

Evidence

Weaving and agricultural abundance

Description

Amaterasu is associated with sericulture (silk-weaving) and rice cultivation. The Kojiki describes her weaving sacred garments in a pure weaving hall — an activity disrupted by Susanoo's desecration. The Ise rituals include offerings of rice and silk.

Confidence

high

Gifting and blessing the imperial regalia

Description

Amaterasu bestowed the Three Sacred Treasures (Sanshu no Jingi) upon her grandson Ninigi, conferring legitimacy on the imperial line. The mirror (Yata no Kagami) — the lure that drew her from the cave — became her shintai (divine body-object) at Ise.

Purification and order

Description

Amaterasu embodies cosmic order, purity, and harmony. Her birth during Izanagi's purification rite and her role in restoring light after chaos (the cave episode) establish her as a force for cosmic balance against disorder (embodied by Susanoo).

Confidence

high

Evidence

Divine ancestry and blessing of the imperial line

Description

Through her grandson Ninigi and great-grandson Jimmu (the legendary first emperor), Amaterasu's divine bloodline flows into the Yamato dynasty. Her ongoing blessing was ritually invoked by every emperor through ceremonies at Ise and the imperial palace.

Confidence

high

Evidence

Kojiki; Nihon Shoki; imperial enthronement rituals (Daijōsai)

Form

A woman of sublime, radiant beauty cloaked in light so brilliant it is difficult to look at directly. She wears flowing white and crimson robes of imperial style, adorned with magatama (curved jewels) and the sacred mirror Yata no Kagami. Her hair is long and black, often depicted flowing around her like a solar corona. In some representations, she is surrounded by an eight-fold halo of light rays. She carries the divine regalia — mirror, jewel, and sword — that she passed to her grandson Ninigi and through him to the Japanese emperors.

Powers

Complete command over the sun and all light in the cosmos. The ability to withdraw her light entirely, plunging all realms into darkness. Divine authority over the celestial kami and the High Plain of Heaven. The power to bless harvests, ensure fertility, and maintain cosmic harmony. Her sacred mirror reflects absolute truth. As the wellspring of the imperial line, she grants legitimacy to Japan's emperors. She can weave the fabric of reality itself — her weaving hall produces the cosmic order.

Behavior & Habitat

Amaterasu is generally serene, benevolent, and orderly. She values harmony (wa) above all else and governs through quiet authority rather than displays of power. However, she has a limit: when her brother Susanoo's chaos became intolerable, her response was dramatic and world-altering. She is curious (peeking out of the cave to see the party) and can be moved by joy and community. She favors peaceful resolution over conflict but is not afraid to use her power as leverage — her withdrawal of light was the most effective negotiation tactic in mythological history.

Weaknesses & Wards

Her commitment to harmony can make her avoidant of necessary confrontation. She is susceptible to curiosity and trickery — the gods lured her out of the cave by exploiting her desire to see what was causing the laughter. Her power is tied to her presence; when she hides, everything suffers. Her relationship with Susanoo is a persistent source of cosmic instability.

Archive Dossier — Stories

The Birth of Amaterasu from Izanagi's Purification

Summary

After Izanagi's failed attempt to retrieve his dead wife Izanami from Yomi (the underworld), he fled in horror and stopped at a river to purify himself (misogi). As he washed his left eye, Amaterasu emerged, radiant and divine. From his right eye emerged Tsukuyomi, the moon deity, and from his nose emerged Susanoo, the storm god. Izanagi appointed Amaterasu to rule Takamagahara (the High Plain of Heaven), gave Tsukuyomi dominion over the night, and assigned Susanoo the seas. This birth narrative establishes Amaterasu's primacy — she came from the left eye (the superior side in Japanese spatial hierarchy) and received the highest realm.

The Heavenly Rock Cave (Ama-no-Iwato)

Summary

Susanoo, jealous and uncontrollable, rampaged through Takamagahara: he broke down the ridges between Amaterasu's rice fields, defecated in her palace hall, and threw a flayed horse into her sacred weaving hall, killing one of her weaving maidens. Grief-stricken and enraged, Amaterasu retreated into the Heavenly Rock Cave (Ama-no-Iwato), sealing the entrance with a boulder. The world — both heaven and earth — was plunged into total darkness. The myriad kami gathered at the cave's mouth and devised a plan. Ame-no-Uzume overturned a washtub, danced wildly, and exposed herself, making the assembled kami roar with laughter. Curious about the commotion, Amaterasu peeked out. Ame-no-Koyane and Futodama held up the Yata no Kagami mirror, and Amaterasu, seeing her own brilliant reflection, was drawn further out. Tajikarao, the strong-armed kami, seized her and pulled her from the cave. Ame-no-Koyane then stretched a sacred rope (shimenawa) across the cave entrance so she could never return. Light was restored to the cosmos. Susanoo was banished from heaven.

The Rivalry with Susanoo and the Oath-Taking

Summary

Before the cave episode, Susanoo announced his desire to visit his mother Izanami in Yomi. Before departing, he ascended to Takamagahara. Amaterasu, suspecting ill intent, armed herself with a bow and quiver. When Susanoo approached, she challenged him to an oath-taking contest (ukehi) to prove his sincerity. They exchanged sacred objects: Amaterasu received Susanoo's sword and broke it into three pieces, chewing and spitting them out to produce three goddesses. Susanoo received Amaterasu's jewel-string, which produced five gods. Amaterasu claimed the goddesses as her own children (borne of Susanoo's sword), while Susanoo claimed the gods as his (borne of Amaterasu's jewel). Susanoo declared victory and proceeded to wreak havoc — leading directly to the cave episode.

Amaterasu and Tsukuyomi's Separation (Day from Night)

Summary

Amaterasu sent Tsukuyomi to represent her at a feast hosted by the food goddess Ukemochi (or Ōgetsuhime). Ukemochi produced food from her body — rice from her mouth, fish from her rectum, game from her nose — and offered it to Tsukuyomi. Disgusted, Tsukuyomi killed Ukemochi on the spot. When Amaterasu learned of this, she was furious: 'You are an evil deity. I shall not look upon you again.' She declared that she and Tsukuyomi would henceforth dwell apart, governing different times — she the day and he the night. This is why the sun and moon are never seen together. From Ukemochi's slain body, however, grew the five grains (rice, millet, wheat, beans, and soy), as well as silkworms, explaining the origin of agriculture.

The Descent of Ninigi and the Three Sacred Treasures

Summary

After restoring order to heaven, Amaterasu resolved to send her grandson Ninigi-no-Mikoto to rule the 'Reed Plain' (Ashihara no Nakatsukuni — Japan). She bestowed upon him the Three Sacred Treasures: the Yata no Kagami (mirror that had lured her from the cave), the Kusanagi-no-Tsurugi (sword Susanoo had given her), and the Yasakani no Magatama (the curved jewel). She instructed Ninigi: 'Regard this mirror as my spirit, and worship it as you would me.' Accompanied by five ancestral kami of the leading clans (Ame-no-Koyane, Futodama, Ame-no-Uzume, Ishikoridome, and Tajikarao), Ninigi descended to earth at Mount Takachiho in Kyushu. His great-grandson would become Jimmu, the legendary first emperor of Japan.

Amaterasu and the Pacification of the Central Land

Summary

Before Ninigi's descent, Amaterasu sent a series of messengers to pacify the Central Land of Reed Plains, which was roiling with unruly earthly kami (kunitsukami). Her first emissary, Ame-no-Hohi, defected and allied with the earthly deity Ōkuninushi. The second, Ame-no-Wakahiko, married Ōkuninushi's daughter and went native. Finally, Amaterasu dispatched Takemikazuchi (the thunder kami) and Futsunushi, who descended with their swords and intimidated Ōkuninushi into ceding control of the land in exchange for a grand shrine — the Izumo Taisha. This narrative legitimizes the Yamato clan's political unification of Japan by framing it as a divinely ordered transfer of authority.

Amaterasu in the Imperial Enthronement (Daijōsai)

Summary

The Daijōsai (Great Food Offering) is the most sacred ritual of the Japanese imperial enthronement, performed once per reign. During the ceremony, the new emperor spends the night in ritual seclusion, offering newly harvested rice to Amaterasu and the heavenly kami — essentially sharing a meal with the sun goddess to receive her blessing and legitimize his rule. The ritual, which combines Shinto purification, agricultural thanksgiving, and ancestral communion, dates back at least to the 7th century. Emperor Akihito performed it in 1990 and Emperor Naruhito in 2019, maintaining one of the world's oldest continuous ritual traditions.

Archive Dossier — Archivist

Known Gaps

The pre-Kojiki oral tradition is entirely lost — we have no access to Amaterasu narratives before their 8th-century textualization, which was explicitly political in purpose. The relationship between Amaterasu worship and pre-Yamato solar cults (including the sun goddess Himiko of the 3rd-century Wa kingdom, recorded in Chinese sources) remains speculative. The exact nature of the Yata no Kagami — whether it is a bronze mirror, a metaphorical concept, or has been replaced over time — is a matter of sacred secrecy that may never be resolved. Comprehensive comparative study of Amaterasu with Chinese (Xihe), Korean (Haemosu), and Southeast Asian solar deities has not been undertaken. The gender dynamics of a female supreme deity within a historically patriarchal state merit deeper theological and feminist analysis.

Archive Dossier — Contradictions

Female solar deity paradox: Amaterasu is a rare supreme female solar deity in world mythology (most solar deities are male). Japanese scholarship debates whether this represents an ancient matriarchal substrate, a Yamato clan political choice, or simply a unique cultural development.

Kojiki vs. Nihon Shoki variants: The two primary sources differ on several points — the Nihon Shoki provides multiple variant accounts of the cave myth, some of which contradict the Kojiki. The Nihon Shoki's courtly, Sinitic framing contrasts with the Kojiki's more mythologically raw narrative.

Amaterasu as chief deity vs. one among many: Before State Shinto's centralization, Amaterasu's primacy was contested. Izumo Taisha (Ōkuninushi's shrine) and Suwa Taisha represented rival mythological centers with claims to primacy that the Yamato court suppressed or co-opted.

Syncretic identity with Buddhism: For over a millennium, Amaterasu was worshipped as a manifestation of Buddha Dainichi Nyorai. The Meiji-era 'separation of kami and buddhas' (shinbutsu bunri) violently destroyed this syncretism. Whether the 'pure' Shinto Amaterasu or the syncretic Buddha-kami is more 'authentic' remains debated.

Mirror as shintai: Whether the Yata no Kagami at Ise is a physical mirror or a metaphor for Amaterasu's presence is theologically ambiguous. The mirror is enshrined in a building that only the high priest/priestess may enter, and its existence has never been publicly confirmed.

Archive Dossier — Reception
Context

Classical Shinto (pre-1868)

Interpretation

Amaterasu was the supreme kami of the imperial clan but not necessarily of all Japanese people. Buddhism dominated Japanese religious life for most of the medieval period, with Amaterasu often syncretized as a manifestation of Dainichi Nyorai (the cosmic Buddha Vairocana).

Context

State Shinto (1868-1945)

Interpretation

Amaterasu was made the central figure of a newly invented national religion. The Ise Shrine was elevated above all others, and every Japanese household was expected to possess an Ise amulet. The mythology of divine descent became mandatory national doctrine.

Context

Post-war Shinto (1945-present)

Interpretation

Amaterasu's worship continues at Ise and thousands of branch shrines, but as a voluntary religious practice. The Ise Shrine's 20-year rebuilding cycle (shikinen sengū) — most recently in 2013 — remains a major cultural and religious event. The imperial family continues to perform private rituals honoring Amaterasu.

Context

Feminist and goddess spirituality

Interpretation

Amaterasu has been reclaimed by feminist theologians and neo-pagan movements as one of the few supreme solar goddesses in world mythology — a female deity who rules the heavens in her own right, not as a consort. This reading often elides the patriarchal uses of Amaterasu in Japanese history.

Context

Popular culture

Interpretation

Amaterasu appears as the protagonist wolf in the acclaimed video game Ōkami (2006), widely praised for its artistic and narrative treatment of Japanese mythology. She also appears in the Persona and Shin Megami Tensei series, Naruto, and numerous anime as a powerful sun-associated entity.

Context

National identity and the Ise pilgrimage

Interpretation

The Ise Shrine remains one of Japan's most popular pilgrimage destinations, attracting millions annually. For many Japanese people, visiting Ise is more a cultural/national practice than a doctrinal religious act — a form of 'belonging without believing' characteristic of contemporary Japanese spirituality.

Cultural Significance

Amaterasu is the central deity of Shinto, Japan's indigenous religion. The Ise Grand Shrine, rebuilt every twenty years in an ancient cycle of renewal, has housed her sacred mirror for nearly two millennia and is the holiest site in Japan. The Japanese imperial family traces its unbroken lineage to her, making her not just a god but the wellspring of Japanese sovereignty. Her cave myth is one of the world's great stories of depression, withdrawal, and the healing power of community and joy.

Modern Interpretations

Amaterasu appears as a divine white wolf in the acclaimed video game 'Ōkami,' where she fights to restore color and life to a cursed world. In anime and manga, she is a recurring figure of serene, ultimate power — from 'Naruto' (as the Mangekyo Sharingan technique) to 'Noragami.' Modern Japanese media often explores the tension between her harmonious nature and the necessary exercise of power, reflecting Japan's post-war identity struggles.
