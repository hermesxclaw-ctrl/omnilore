Who It Is

You have just found a spell that does not flash, bang, or scar — it buzzes. Whispered as Muffliato, it fills the ears of everyone nearby with a soft, unplaceable humming, turning a crowded Gryffindor dormitory into a private room. It was not handed down by Merlin or taught by Flitwick, but scribbled in the margins of a battered second-hand Potions textbook by a lonely teenage Severus Snape — and it is one of the few spells in Harry Potter that was invented on-page, in living memory.

As an invented teenage spell, Muffliato reflects Snape's preoccupations: privacy, secrecy, defence against eavesdropping and bullying (Snape was himself bullied by the Marauders). It is defensive/utility, not aggressive. Harry's use is pragmatic and slightly mischievous — to hide conversations from dorm-mates and to gossip about Malfoy. Hermione's later adoption is cautious and protective (camp security). The spell has no sentience or personality.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Unattested in official Rowling etymology notes. Scholarly and fan consensus derives it from English verb 'to muffle' (to deaden or suppress sound, from Middle French moufle 'mitten, muffle') + pseudo-Latin ending -iato, common to Rowling spell coinages (cf. Levicorpus, Sectumsempra, Expelliarmus). T

Aliases — Muffliato Charm, Muffliato Spell, Muffliato Hex (colloquial mislabel)

Powers
Auditory Privacy Field (Buzzing Ear Fill)

Upon casting, fills the ears of all persons in proximity to the caster (except the caster and chosen conversants) with a low, unidentifiable buzzing/humming that renders nearby speech unintelligible to them. Allows caster to hold a normal-volume conversation without being overheard. Effect is area-of-effect centered on caster or target area, not a projectile.

Limit: Range limited to earshot (~room-sized); does not silence caster's voice to intended listener; does not block magical eavesdropping methods such as Extendable Ears (Weasleys' invention) if outside buzzing range or if counter-charmed? Not shown to work through walls at distance; requires wand and spoken incantation; presumably can be detected by a skilled wizard noticing the buzzing; no evidence it blocks Legilimency or other mind-reading; duration appears to be minutes, requiring recasting for prolonged privacy; not shown to affect portraits, ghosts, or house-elves consistently.

Invented Spell Status (Half-Blood Prince Origin)

One of a small set of spells invented by Severus Snape as a student (c. 1971-1978), alongside Levicorpus, Liberacorpus, Langlock, and Sectumsempra. Demonstrates that spell creation is possible for gifted students, not only ancient founders.

Limit: Requires discovery via marginalia; not taught by professors, so knowledge is restricted to those who read the Prince's copy. Harry initially does not know its effects and tests it experimentally.

Non-Lethal, Non-Combative Utility

Classified as a Charm (utility), not Dark Arts. No damage, no physical transformation, no compulsion. Can be used repeatedly without apparent magical exhaustion or cost beyond standard spellcasting fatigue.

Limit: Offers no offensive or defensive combat advantage; does not protect against physical attack, Veritaserum, or truth compulsion; cannot be used to silence an opponent's spellcasting.

Accounts I
The Discovery in Advanced Potion-MakingHarry Potter and the Half-Blood Prince, Chapter 12 'Silver and Opals' and Chapter 9 'The Half-Blood Prince'

2005 publication; in-universe 1996-1997 (Harry's sixth year)

Harry obtains a battered second-hand copy of Advanced Potion-Making inscribed 'This Book is the Property of the Half-Blood Prince.' In its margins he finds handwritten spells including Muffliato. Curious, he tries it in the Gryffindor boys' dormitory to have a private conversation with Ron about Hermione and about Draco Malfoy. Ron and Hermione notice the buzzing filling the ears of nearby students (Dean, Seamus, Neville), confirming the spell works. Harry realizes the Prince was an inventive spell-maker. Establishes Muffliato as Harry's go-to privacy tool for the rest of the year; deepens mystery of the Prince's identity; foreshadows Snape's talent and moral ambiguity; Harry becomes increasingly reliant on Prince's notes, leading to later use of Sectumsempra.

Gossip About Malfoy — Library and Common Room ConferencesHarry Potter and the Half-Blood Prince, Chapters 14 'Felix Felicis' and 15 'The Unbreakable Vow' (and recurring uses through Chapters 18-20)

2005; in-universe 1996-1997

Convinced Draco Malfoy has become a Death Eater, Harry repeatedly uses Muffliato to discuss his suspicions with Ron and Hermione without being overheard by other Gryffindors or by eavesdropping portraits. Hermione is skeptical and chastises Harry for trusting the Prince's spells, but acknowledges Muffliato's usefulness. The trio's private huddles become a running motif, with the buzzing described each time. Allows Harry to pursue his investigation without alerting teachers or Malfoy; highlights Hermione's ambivalence about Prince's magic (useful but untrustworthy); builds tension before Harry's later misuse of Sectumsempra.

Snape's Invention — The Teenage Spell-CreatorHarry Potter and the Half-Blood Prince, Chapter 28 'Flight of the Prince' + WizardingWorld.com 'Severus Snape' biography; inferred from Half-Blood Prince's copy dating to Snape's Hogwarts years (c. 1971-1978)

2005 (revelation); in-universe c. 1970s

After Dumbledore's death, Harry learns the Half-Blood Prince was Severus Snape, whose mother was Eileen Prince. As a poor, bullied, brilliant student fascinated by the Dark Arts and spell creation, Snape invented Muffliato alongside Levicorpus (used against James Potter), Langlock, and Sectumsempra. Muffliato likely originated from Snape's need for privacy from the Marauders (James Potter, Sirius Black, Remus Lupin, Peter Pettigrew) who bullied and humiliated him, and from his desire to hide conversations with Lily Evans. Recontextualizes Muffliato as a defensive, almost poignant invention rather than a prank; explains why Snape's spells are a mix of utility and cruelty; deepens Snape's character as innovator; retroactively explains why no professor taught Muffliato.

Accounts II
The Burrow Wedding — Security Under the Fallen MinistryHarry Potter and the Deathly Hallows, Chapter 8 'The Wedding'

2007; in-universe August 1997

At Bill and Fleur's wedding at the Burrow, with the Ministry infiltrated by Death Eaters, Hermione — having learned Muffliato from Harry the previous year — casts it to allow the trio to discuss the looming danger and their plan to leave, without being overheard by wedding guests or potential Ministry informants. The buzzing again fills nearby ears, demonstrating Hermione's adoption of the spell. Shows transmission of Prince's magic beyond Harry; establishes Muffliato as part of Hermione's protective repertoire during the war; signals that even 'Prince' magic can be repurposed for good.

On the Run — The Tent in the WildernessHarry Potter and the Deathly Hallows, Chapters 14 'The Thief' and 15 'The Goblin's Revenge' (and recurring during camping sequence Chapters 12-19)

2007; in-universe Autumn 1997

While Horcrux-hunting and camping in a magically extended tent, Hermione routinely casts Muffliato (alongside protective enchantments like Protego and Muggle-Repelling Charms) to prevent their conversations from being overheard by anyone — or anything — outside the tent, including possible Snatchers and magical creatures. The spell becomes part of a layered security routine. Illustrates Muffliato's wartime utility as low-level operational security; contrasts with more powerful protective enchantments; shows Hermione's thoroughness; no failure of the spell is depicted, but it is insufficient against larger magical surveillance (Taboo curse, Trace).

The Prince's Book Withheld — Slughorn and Snape's ClassroomHarry Potter and the Half-Blood Prince, Chapters 9, 18, 24; corroborated by WizardingWorld.com

2005; in-universe 1996-1997

Professor Slughorn, unaware of the annotations, praises Harry's Potions excellence derived from the Prince's copy, while Snape (as Potions/Defence professor) remains unaware Harry possesses his old book until the Sectumsempra incident. Muffliato's presence in the margins, alongside instructions for better potion-making, shows Snape's dual genius in Potions and Charms, and his habit of annotating textbooks rather than publishing formally. Explains why Muffliato never entered the Hogwarts curriculum; remains 'underground' knowledge; Harry's eventual surrender of the book to Snape removes Muffliato from his immediate access, but Hermione retains it.

Muffliato makes the caster or the conversation silent/inaudible, like a silencing charm. — It does the opposite: it does not silence the speakers at all. It fills the listeners' ears with buzzing so they cannot make out the speech. The speakers talk at normal volume.

Hermione invented Muffliato or it was taught by Flitwick in Charms class. — Invented solely by Severus Snape as a student. Never taught in class; Harry discovers it in the Prince's annotated textbook and Hermione learns it from Harry.

Muffliato is a Dark spell / Dark Arts because it was invented by the Half-Blood Prince alongside Sectumsempra. — Official classification is Charm (non-Dark, utility). Snape invented both benign and Dark spells; Muffliato itself causes no harm and is used by heroes for protection.

Muffliato appeared in early books (e.g., Chamber of Secrets or Prisoner of Azkaban) and was used by Snape as a teacher. — First appearance is Half-Blood Prince (2005), sixth book. No earlier mention exists; Snape never casts it on-page as a teacher.

The buzzing can be heard by the caster and makes it hard for the caster to hear as well. — Caster and intended conversant hear each other normally; only bystanders experience buzzing. Text is explicit that Harry and Ron converse clearly while others hear buzzing.

Muffliato is the same as Langlock or Levicorpus, or its incantation is 'Muffliate'. — Three distinct Prince spells: Muffliato (buzzing privacy), Langlock (glues tongue to roof of mouth), Levicorpus (hoists by ankle). Correct incantation is Muffliato, attested in UK/US first editions.
