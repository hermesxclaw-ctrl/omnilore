Who It Is

Far beneath the waves off the coast of Kyushu, where light thins to green and silence becomes weight, there is a palace that does not decay. Its gates are guarded by fishes in courtly robes, its tides are kept in two glittering jewels, and its master can appear as a venerable old man, a vast dragon coiled around the sea itself, or three brothers who divide the ocean into upper, middle and abyss. He is Watatsumi — the Great Deity of the Sea — and every Japanese story that touches the water must eventually answer to him.

In primary sources Watatsumi is benevolent, hospitable, and judicial rather than wrathful. He shelters the exiled Hoori for three years, arranges his marriage to his daughter Toyotama-hime (Otohime), recovers the lost fishhook through a census of fishes, and dispenses measured justice: he teaches Hoori a spell and the use of tide jewels to punish his arrogant brother while also teaching him to spare him when he submits. Powers
Sovereignty over Sea Life

Commands all fishes and sea creatures; can summon them for census and interrogation. In Kojiki, he gathers 'all the fishes of the sea, great and small' and questions them to locate Hoderi's lost hook.

Limit: Authority appears limited to sea; cannot directly control land deities or heavenly kami without mediation. Requires cooperation of creatures — the tai must be examined to retrieve hook.

Tide Manipulation via Tide Jewels

Possesses and bestows the Kanju (Tide-Ebbing Jewel, shio-hiru-tama) and Manju (Tide-Flowing Jewel, shio-mitsu-tama) which can make the sea instantly flood or recede. Watatsumi instructs Hoori in their incantation: to drown Hoderi if he attacks, and to save him when he repents.

Limit: Jewels are transferable artifacts; once given to Hoori, Watatsumi no longer directly wields them in narrative. Effect requires spoken spell (norito-like). Overuse would destroy the recipient as well.

Shape-shifting and Wani Command

Can dispatch wani (shark/crocodile/sea-dragon) as messengers and mounts; his daughters Toyotama-hime and Tamayori-hime can assume wani form, implying inherited transformation. Watatsumi himself is later depicted as dragon capable of human form.

Limit: Transformation of daughters is involuntary during childbirth and must not be witnessed; violation breaks marriage and forces return to sea. Watatsumi's own transformations are not described in 8th-century texts — inferred from later Ryūjin iconography.

Palatial Timelessness and Hospitality

Maintains Ryūgū-jō, an undersea palace where time flows differently and mortal needs are suspended. Hoori dwells there three years without aging or hunger, feasted and married into the sea lineage.

Limit: Hospitality is conditional on taboo observance (not looking at true form). Mortal cannot remain permanently without becoming homesick; return to land is permitted but severs direct residence.

Storm Raising and Placation

Can raise sudden storms and rough seas to block passage, as at Hashirimizu where the sea god's will tosses Prince Yamato Takeru's ship. Requires propitiation, in that narrative by human self-sacrifice.

Limit: Storm ceases immediately when offering is accepted, indicating bargain-like theology. Not portrayed as indiscriminate destruction — targeted at those who boast over the sea ('this is but a little sea').

Accounts I
Birth of Ōwatatsumi in the Kamiumi (Birth of the Gods)Kojiki, Ch. 11-12; Nihon Shoki Book 1

712-720 CE textualization of earlier oral tradition (Yayoi-Kofun mythic layer)

After creating the Japanese islands (kuniumi), Izanagi and Izanami beget deities (kamiumi). Among them is the Sea-Deity Ōwatatsumi no Kami (Great-Ocean-Possessor), born alongside deities of wind, mountains, and water-gates. His birth establishes the sea as a divine territory parallel to Ōyamatsumi's mountains, ordering the cosmos into sea/mountain/field domains. Establishes Watatsumi's primordial seniority as a direct child of the creator pair, legitimizing Azumi clan claims to sea stewardship. Provides cosmological pairing that recurs in later rituals separating sea and mountain offerings.

Birth of the Watatsumi Sanjin from Izanagi's MisogiKojiki Ch. 17; Nihon Shoki Book 1, misogi purification episode

712-720 CE

After Izanami dies bearing Kagutsuchi and Izanagi fails to retrieve her from Yomi, he returns polluted by death. At the mouth of the Tachibana River in Himuka (Hyuga), he performs misogi purification. As he washes, deities are born from his discarded garments and from washing his body. From washing at different depths, three Watatsumi deities are born: Sokotsu Watatsumi (Bottom), Nakatsu Watatsumi (Middle), and Uwatsu Watatsumi (Upper/Surface), who rule the stratified seas. They are accompanied by three Sumiyoshi deities born alongside them. Explains ritual purity: sea deities themselves are born from purification, so seawater is purifying. Provides mythic charter for Sumiyoshi and Shikaumi shrines and for misogi rites still performed at seashores. Stratification explains tidal zones and fishing grounds.

Hoderi and Hoori (Umisachi Yamasachi) — Hoori's Sojourn in Ryūgū-jōKojiki Ch. 37-42; Nihon Shoki Book 2, variant accounts

712-720 CE; oral antecedents likely Kofun period

Brothers Hoderi (Umisachi, Luck of the Sea) and Hoori (Yamasachi, Luck of the Mountain) quarrel after Hoori loses Hoderi's fishhook. Advised by Shiotsuchi no Oji, Hoori travels to the sea bottom and finds Watatsumi's palace. He is welcomed, marries Watatsumi's daughter Toyotama-hime (called Otohime or Luxuriant-Jewel-Princess), and lives three years in the palace. When Hoori sighs from homesickness, Watatsumi summons all fishes, finds the hook in a sea bream's throat, washes it, and returns it with instructions and the two tide jewels. He sends the couple back on a wani. On land, Hoori uses the jewels to subdue Hoderi, who submits and becomes his servant. Foundational myth for imperial lineage: Hoori and Toyotama-hime's son Ugayafukiaezu fathers Emperor Jimmu. Establishes tide jewels as regalia-like treasures, explains Azumi maritime authority, and encodes taboo of seeing wife's true wani form during childbirth (which Toyotama-hime later violates, causing her to abandon Hoori).

Accounts II
Oto-tachibana-hime and the Hashirimizu CrossingNihon Shoki Book 5, Emperor Keikō, Yamato Takeru cycle

720 CE textualization; legend set in early Yamato expansion (trad. 4th century)

Prince Yamato Takeru (Yamato-dake) marches to Sagami and boasts the sea to Kazusa is 'but a little sea, one might jump over it.' Mid-crossing, Watatsumi (海神) raises a violent storm tossing the ship. His concubine Oto-tachibana-hime offers herself as substitute sacrifice: 'Let my person be given to redeem the Prince.' She plunges into the waves; the storm immediately ceases and the ship reaches shore. The strait is thereafter called Hashirimizu (Running Waters). Aetiology for place name Hashirimizu (Kannonzaki, Yokosuka). Demonstrates sea god as wrathful enforcer of humility and recipient of human sacrifice (hitobashira-like). Provides precedent for appeasing Watatsumi before sea crossings, reflected in later maritime rites.

Genealogy of Emperor Jimmu — Daughter of the Sea GodNihon Shoki Book 3, Emperor Jimmu genealogy; Kojiki Ch. 42-44

712-720 CE

Toyotama-hime, daughter of Watatsumi, bears Hoori a son Ugayafukiaezu but flees back to the sea after Hoori breaks the taboo and watches her wani form during childbirth. Her younger sister Tamayori-hime (Jewel-Good-Princess) raises the child and later marries him. Their fourth child is Hikohohodemi, posthumously Emperor Jimmu (Kami Yamato Ihare-biko), first emperor. Thus Jimmu is described as 'son of Tamayori-hime, daughter of the Sea-God.' Legitimizes imperial house as descendant of both heavenly (Amaterasu via Ninigi) and sea deities, uniting mountain/sea and heaven/earth lines. Explains why Jimmu is said to be 'of clear intelligence and resolute will' from birth — sea god blood. Reinforces Shikaumi/Sumiyoshi shrines as imperial ancestral shrines.

Azumi Clan and Shikaumi Shrine FoundationShikaumi Shrine engi, Sumiyoshi engi, and Kojiki genealogies; Engishiki Jinmyōchō

Engi traditions compiled Heian period, reflecting Kofun-Yayoi Azumi maritime dominance

The Azumi, ancient seafaring people of northern Kyushu, claim descent from Watatsumi. Their ancestral shrine on Shika Island enshrines the Watatsumi Sanjin. Legend holds that Empress Jingū, after her Korean expedition, enshrined Watatsumi there and deposited offerings. The shrine's treasures (ancient armor, swords) attest to its role as guardian of sea routes through the Genkai Sea and Inland Sea. Institutionalizes Watatsumi worship in state Shinto; Shikaumi becomes Ichinomiya of Chikuzen and Myōjin Taisha. Maritime rites for safe voyage, fishing, and naval warfare are directed to Watatsumi. Archaeology shows continuous votive deposition from Yayoi onward.

Watatsumi and Ryūjin are exactly the same deity with two names — Kojiki and Nihon Shoki never use the name Ryūjin; Watatsumi is the 8th-century sea deity, while Ryūjin (Dragon God) is a later medieval syncretism blending Watatsumi with Chinese Longwang and Buddhist nāga lore. Shrines may equate them today, but early texts keep them distinct — Watatsumi's palace has fish retainers and wani, not the turtle/jellyfish court of later Ryūjin tales.

Watatsumi is always depicted as a giant dragon — Primary sources describe him as an old man and palace lord who commands dragons/wani, not as a dragon himself. Dragon iconography is medieval onward. The wani that carry Hoori are his servants, and his daughter's wani form is a specific transformation during childbirth, not his default.

The tide jewels are Watatsumi's personal magic that he uses directly — In Kojiki, Watatsumi possesses the jewels but gives them to Hoori with explicit spells; he does not use them himself in narrative. They are transferable regalia, and their power depends on correct incantation. Later folklore sometimes shows Ryūjin wielding them directly.

Watatsumi lives in Ryūgū-jō with Otohime as in Urashima Tarō — Urashima Tarō's Otohime and timeless Dragon Palace is a Heian-period and later folk development that merges Watatsumi's palace with Buddhist otherworld motifs. Kojiki's Otohime is Toyotama-hime, and her palace sojourn ends with return and taboo, not Urashima's tragic time-loss. The names overlap but stories are distinct layers.
