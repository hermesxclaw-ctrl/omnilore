The Approach

In the tangled genealogies of Greek myth, few figures are as elusive as Pleisthenes—a name that appears in the shadows of the House of Atreus, sometimes as the father of Agamemnon and Menelaus, sometimes as a son of Atreus, and sometimes as a victim of his own family's curse. His story, pieced together from scholia and fragments, reveals a man whose very existence is a matter of scholarly debate, a figure who dies young and is remembered only through the lives of others.

Content advisory. Contains themes of familial murder, cannibalism (in the banquet of Thyestes), and betrayal. No explicit sexual violence.
Identity

Pleisthenes

Pronunciation PLAY-sthen-eez (English), Πλεισθένης (Ancient Greek: pleːstʰénɛːs)

Known As

Plisthenes

Etymology

The name is derived from the Greek 'πλεῖστος' (pleistos, 'most') and 'σθένος' (sthenos, 'strength'), thus meaning 'very strong' or 'greatest strength'.

Cultural Ownership

Ancient Greek mythology, specifically the Argive/Mycenaean saga cycle.

Disputed Identifications

The name is shared by several members of the House of Tantalus, and the identification of the father of Agamemnon and Menelaus is confused. Some scholars equate the son of Pelops with the father of Agamemnon, while others treat them as distinct.

Description

Appearance

No direct physical description survives. As a mythological figure, he is not described in any surviving text or artistic representation.

Symbols & Iconography

No known iconography. He is not depicted in ancient art.

Behaviour & Personality

In the tradition where he is the father of Agamemnon and Menelaus, he is said to have 'done nothing of note' and died young. In Hyginus's version of the plot for Euripides' play, he is an unwitting victim, killed by his own father. No personality traits are recorded.

Domains & Habitats

Mycenae or Argos, as a member of the royal house.

Version Differences

He is variously: a son of Atreus and Aerope; a son of Pelops (or a bastard son); the father of Agamemnon, Menelaus, and Anaxibia by Aerope, Cleolla, or Eriphyle; a son of Thyestes and Aerope (one of the children killed by Atreus); and a son of Menelaus and Helen.

Stories & Myths

Pleisthenes as Father of Agamemnon and Menelaus

Source Hesiod, Aeschylus, Porphyry, Tzetzes, scholium on Euripides' OrestesCulture Ancient GreekPeriod Archaic to Classical

Summary

In this tradition, Pleisthenes is the father of Agamemnon and Menelaus (and sometimes Anaxibia), but he dies young, and his sons are raised by their grandfather Atreus. This explains why Homer calls them Atreids. The mother is variously Aerope, Cleolla, or Eriphyle. In the version where Aerope is the mother, she is given to Pleisthenes by Nauplius after being exiled by her father Catreus.

Consequences

Agamemnon and Menelaus become the leaders of the Greek forces at Troy; their patrimony is ambiguous.

Variants

Tzetzes says Aerope is his mother, not wife; the scholium on Orestes says Cleolla is his mother and Eriphyle his wife.

Pleisthenes as Son of Pelops

Source Scholia to Pindar's Olympian 1Culture Ancient GreekPeriod Classical or later

Summary

A son (or bastard son) of Pelops is named Pleisthenes. Some scholars identify this figure with the father of Agamemnon and Menelaus, creating a genealogy where Pelops is the grandfather of the Atreides.

Participants

Pelops

Pleisthenes

Consequences

If identified with the father of Agamemnon, it places him one generation earlier than the usual genealogy.

Variants

The scholia may be confused; the identification is modern.

Pleisthenes as Victim of Atreus's Revenge

Source Hyginus, Fabulae 86 (possible plot of Euripides' 'Pleisthenes')Culture Roman (based on Greek sources)Period 1st century CE (or later)

Summary

Thyestes, after being banished for adultery with Aerope, sends Atreus's son, Pleisthenes (whom he had raised as his own), to Atreus to be killed. Atreus, believing the boy is Thyestes' son, kills him unknowingly.

Participants

Thyestes

Atreus

Pleisthenes

Consequences

Atreus unknowingly kills his own son, adding to the family's curse.

Variants

This is a late account, and the plot may not reflect the actual Euripides play.

Pleisthenes as Son of Thyestes and Aerope

Source Inferred from other sources (e.g., Aeschylus's 'Agamemnon' mentions the banquet of Thyestes)Culture Ancient GreekPeriod Archaic

Summary

Pleisthenes is one of the infant sons of Thyestes and Aerope whom Atreus kills and serves to Thyestes at a banquet.

Participants

Consequences

This act of cannibalism is part of the curse on the House of Atreus.

Variants

The names of the children vary; sometimes they are Tantalus and Pleisthenes.

Pleisthenes as Son of Menelaus and Helen

Source Euripides, Helen and Iphigenia in Tauris; possibly other sourcesCulture Ancient GreekPeriod 5th century BCE

Summary

A son of Menelaus and Helen, named Pleisthenes, travels with a son of Helen and Paris to Cyprus. The timing is usually placed during Helen's stay in Troy, but some scholars, like Jonathan Burgess, suggest it may have occurred after the war.

In the plot from Hyginus, Atreus kills his own son, believing him to be Thyestes' son.

Contradictions & Disputes

Parentage of Agamemnon and Menelaus

Position A

Homer: Atreus is their father.

Position A — evidence

Iliad and Odyssey consistently call them Atreids.

Position B

Hesiod, Aeschylus, and others: Pleisthenes is their father.

Position B — evidence

Scholia to the Iliad and other sources.

Scholarly Consensus

It is generally accepted that the tradition of Pleisthenes as father is a later attempt to reconcile conflicting genealogies or to explain the epithet 'Atreides'.

Identity of the father of Agamemnon

Position A

Pleisthenes is a son of Atreus.

Position A — evidence

Tzetzes and the scholium on Orestes.

Position B

Pleisthenes is a son of Pelops.

Position B — evidence

Scholium to Pindar's Olympian 1.

Scholarly Consensus

The evidence is ambiguous; some scholars accept the identification, others do not.

Relationship of Aerope to Pleisthenes

Position A

Aerope is his wife.

Position A — evidence

Apollodorus and the Iliad scholium.

Position B

Aerope is his mother.

Position B — evidence

Tzetzes.

Scholarly Consensus

The majority of sources make Aerope his wife; Tzetzes is a late source and may be confused.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

The exact genealogy of the various figures named Pleisthenes is unresolved. The lost play of Euripides is known only through fragments and a possible plot in Hyginus, which may not be reliable. The identity of the son of Pelops is uncertain. There is no artistic representation of any of the figures named Pleisthenes. The research is based on the provided source material and the cited references; no additional sources were consulted beyond those listed.

This dossier focuses on the mythological figure(s) named Pleisthenes within the House of Tantalus, as attested in ancient Greek and Roman sources. It covers the primary traditions, genealogical variations, and modern scholarly interpretations.
