Who It Is

The kukri, a curved blade that has become synonymous with the Gurkhas of Nepal, is more than a weapon—it is a cultural emblem, a tool of daily life, and a symbol of martial honor. Its distinctive shape, with a blade that widens toward the tip and angles downward, sets it apart from other knives and has earned it a place in both history and popular imagination.

As a weapon, it is designed for chopping and slashing. In Gurkha culture, it is considered a living entity with a soul; it is believed that once drawn, it must taste blood before being sheathed again.

Etymology — The word 'kukri' is derived from the Nepali word 'खुकुरी' (khukurī), which is of uncertain origin. Some suggest it may be related to the Sanskrit word 'kṣurikā' meaning 'knife'.

Powers
Chopping

The kukri is highly effective for chopping due to its weight and curved shape, allowing powerful blows.

Limit: Not designed for thrusting; the curve limits stabbing capability.

Slashing

The blade's curve allows for efficient slashing motions.

Limit: Requires space for the swing.

Utility

Used as a tool for cutting wood, clearing brush, and even preparing food.

Limit: Not a multi-tool; specialized for chopping.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The kukri is a curved knife with a blade that broadens towards the tip, which angles downward. The blade is typically 16-18 inches long, and the handle is often made of wood or horn. The sheath is usually wood covered in leather or metal.

Symbols — The kukri is a symbol of the Gurkha soldiers and Nepal. It appears on the cap badge of the Gurkha regiments and is used in the Gurkha emblem. Its distinctive shape is iconic.

Domains — The kukri originates from Nepal and is used by the Gurkha people. It is used in combat, as a tool for cutting wood and vegetation, and in religious ceremonies.

Accounts I
Gurkha Legend of the KukriOral tradition

Unknown, likely ancient

There is a legend that the kukri was created to be a weapon that could not be used for evil because it was so distinctive and required skill to use. Another story says that the kukri was used in ancient times to sacrifice animals, and its shape was designed to ensure a quick and humane kill. The kukri became a symbol of Gurkha identity and martial prowess.
