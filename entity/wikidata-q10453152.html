The Approach

In the shadowed forests of ancient India, where serpents coiled in the roots of the world, there rose a goddess whose very name whispered protection against the venomous bite of fate. Jāṅgulī, the Snake Goddess, was not merely a deity of the wild; she was the divine antidote, the healer who turned poison into grace, and her cult spread from the subcontinent to the distant courts of Tibet and beyond, leaving a trail of mantra and ritual that still echoes in the tantric traditions of Asia.

Content advisory. No graphic or disturbing content is inherent to the subject. However, the subject involves serpents and poison, which may be unsettling to some. The historical and religious contexts are treated with respect; no violence or explicit material is described.
Identity

Jāṅgulī

Pronunciation JAHN-goo-lee (Sanskrit: जाङ्गुली). In Tibetan contexts, the pronunciation is often 'Jang-gul-ee'.

Known As

JanguliJangulyaJāṅgulī DevīVidyādevīBuddhist JāṅgulīHindu Jāṅgulī

Etymology

The name Jāṅgulī is derived from the Sanskrit root 'jaṅgula', which means 'poison' or 'venom'. Thus, Jāṅgulī literally means 'she who is related to poison' or 'the poison goddess'. The name is also associated with the word 'jaṅgala', meaning 'forest' or 'wilderness', linking her to her sylvan abode. In Tibetan, she is known as 'Dug sel ma' (Duk Selma), meaning 'the Remover of Poison'.

Cultural Ownership

Jāṅgulī is a goddess shared between Hindu and Buddhist traditions, primarily in South Asia and the Himalayas. In Hinduism, she is a manifestation of Durgā or Pārvatī, while in Buddhism, she is a tantric deity associated with the Vajrayāna pantheon, particularly in Tibet and Nepal. Her worship is historically documented from at least the early medieval period (c. 7th-8th century CE) in India, and she remains a significant figure in contemporary Buddhist rituals for protection against snakebite and poison.

Disputed Identifications

There is scholarly debate about the exact origin of Jāṅgulī. Some scholars argue she is originally a Hindu folk goddess who was later adopted into Buddhism, while others suggest she may have been a Buddhist invention that absorbed Hindu elements. Her identification with the Buddhist deity Parnashavari (the 'Leaf-clad goddess') is sometimes proposed, but this is not universally accepted. In some texts, Jāṅgulī is equated with the goddess Manasā, the serpent goddess of Bengal, but this is a modern conflation; historically they are distinct. The relationship between Jāṅgulī and the nāgas (serpent deities) is also debated, with some seeing her as a nāgī herself, but this is not attested in early sources.

Description

Appearance

Jāṅgulī is typically depicted as a beautiful goddess with a dark blue or green complexion, symbolizing her connection to the forest and poison. She has two or four arms. In her right hands, she holds a snake and a vajra (thunderbolt) or a trident; in her left hands, she holds a snake and a lotus or a skull cup. She is adorned with serpents as ornaments, including a serpent as a sacred thread and a crown of serpents. Her lower garment is often made of tiger skin, and she stands in a graceful posture (ālīḍha) on a corpse or a lotus. In some depictions, she is surrounded by serpents and peacocks, the latter being natural enemies of snakes.

Symbols & Iconography

Key symbols: serpents (nāgas) representing both danger and protection; the lotus symbolizing purity; the vajra representing indestructible wisdom; the trident symbolizing the three realms; the skull cup representing the transformation of death. Her iconography emphasizes her role as the conqueror of poison and the protector of devotees from snakebite and envenomation.

Behaviour & Personality

Jāṅgulī is portrayed as a fierce yet compassionate deity. She is protective and swift to aid those who call upon her, especially in times of danger from snakes or poison. She is also associated with healing and the destruction of evil forces. In Buddhist contexts, she is a bodhisattva-like figure who uses her power to liberate beings from suffering, particularly the suffering caused by poison, both literal and metaphorical (e.g., ignorance and negative emotions).

Domains & Habitats

Jāṅgulī is associated with forests, jungles, and wilderness areas where snakes are common. She is also believed to reside in the underworld or in the realm of the nāgas. In ritual practice, she is invoked in domestic and temple settings for protection against snakebite and for curing poison-related ailments. Her cult is particularly strong in rural areas of India, Nepal, and Tibet, where snakebite is a real danger.

Version Differences

In Hindu tradition, Jāṅgulī is often considered a form of Durgā or Pārvatī, and she is worshipped with offerings of milk, flowers, and incense. In Buddhist tradition, she is a tantric deity with elaborate sādhana rituals, visualized in union with her consort or as a solitary deity. The iconography varies: Hindu depictions may show her with a lion mount, while Buddhist depictions often show her without a mount or with a peacock. The number of arms also varies from two to four. In some later texts, she is merged with the goddess Parnashavari, who is also associated with forest and healing.

Jāṅgulī is immune to all poisons, including snake venom, and can neutralize any poison with her glance or touch.

Stories & Myths

The Origin of Jāṅgulī in the Devī Māhātmya

Source Devī Māhātmya (Glory of the Goddess), part of the Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa (c. 5th-6th century CE)Culture HinduPeriod Classical period (c. 400-600 CE)

Summary

The Devī Māhātmya narrates the goddess Durgā's battles against demons. In one episode, she creates the goddess Jāṅgulī to combat the demon of poison, who was tormenting the world. Jāṅgulī, emanating from Durgā's third eye, takes the form of a serpent-wreathed deity and drinks the poison, rendering it harmless. This story establishes Jāṅgulī as a manifestation of the supreme goddess.

Participants

Durgā

Jāṅgulī

Demon of Poison (sometimes named as Jālaṅdhara or a demon named Viṣa)

Consequences

Jāṅgulī becomes a protector of the universe, and her worship is established to ward off poison and snakebite.

Variants

In some regional versions, Jāṅgulī is created by Śiva to protect his devotees, or she is born from the sweat of Pārvatī.

Buddhist Legend of Jāṅgulī as a Protector of the Dharma

Source Various Buddhist tantric texts, including the MañjuśrīmūlakalpaCulture Buddhist (Mahayana and Vajrayana)Period Early medieval period (c. 7th-9th century CE)

Summary

In Buddhist legend, Jāṅgulī is a goddess who vowed to protect the Buddha's teachings from the dangers of poison, both literal and metaphorical. She is said to have been a powerful nāgī queen who converted to Buddhism and became a guardian of the faith. She is often depicted as a bodhisattva in feminine form, using her powers to heal and protect sentient beings.

Participants

Jāṅgulī

The Buddha

Nāga kings

Consequences

Jāṅgulī is incorporated into the Buddhist pantheon as a protective deity, and her rituals are used for healing and exorcism.

Variants

In Tibetan Buddhism, she is sometimes identified with the goddess Parnashavari, who is also a protector against disease.

Jāṅgulī and the Peacock

Source Folk tradition and iconographic manualsCulture Hindu and BuddhistPeriod Medieval period (c. 10th-15th century CE)

Summary

A popular folk story explains why Jāṅgulī is often depicted with a peacock. According to the legend, a peacock once saved a sage from a deadly snakebite by eating the snake. The sage, grateful, blessed the peacock, and from then on, peacocks were considered sacred to Jāṅgulī. This story is not found in classical texts but is common in oral tradition and is reflected in iconography.

Participants

Jāṅgulī

A peacock

A sage

Consequences

Peacocks are associated with Jāṅgulī, and they are considered auspicious in her worship.

Variants

In some versions, the peacock is a mount of Jāṅgulī, though this is not standard.

Animal sacrifice

Source Anthropological studies of folk religion in South Asia

Description

In some rural folk traditions, Jāṅgulī is worshipped with offerings of animal sacrifice, typically goats or chickens, to appease her and seek protection from snakes. This practice is not attested in classical texts and is declining.

Scholarly Context

Animal sacrifice is a common feature of folk Hindu goddess worship, and it is often interpreted as a means of establishing a reciprocal relationship with the deity.

Fear of snakebite

Source Historical and anthropological scholarship on folk medicine

Description

The worship of Jāṅgulī is rooted in the very real danger of snakebite in rural South Asia. Her cult likely arose as a coping mechanism for the high mortality rate from snakebites. This is a dark historical context, as it reflects the vulnerability of agricultural communities.

Scholarly Context

The fear of snakes is a universal human trait, and many cultures have deities dedicated to protection from them. Jāṅgulī's worship is a rational response to a real threat.

Contradictions & Disputes

Origin: Hindu vs Buddhist

Position A

Jāṅgulī originated as a Hindu folk goddess and was later adopted into Buddhism.

Position A — evidence

The Devī Māhātmya (c. 5th-6th century) mentions her, which predates the earliest Buddhist tantric texts.

Position B

Jāṅgulī was a Buddhist creation that absorbed Hindu elements.

Position B — evidence

The earliest Buddhist texts on Jāṅgulī (Mañjuśrīmūlakalpa) are dated to the 8th century, but they may reflect earlier oral traditions. Some scholars argue that the goddess is a Buddhist invention because no pre-Buddhist Hindu texts mention her.

Scholarly Consensus

The consensus is that she is a syncretic deity, with both Hindu and Buddhist elements, and her exact origin is uncertain.

Identification with Parnashavari

Position A

Jāṅgulī and Parnashavari are the same goddess.

Position A — evidence

Both are associated with forests and healing, and some Tibetan texts use the names interchangeably.

Position B

They are distinct deities.

Position B — evidence

Their iconography and specific functions differ: Jāṅgulī is specifically for poison, while Parnashavari is for epidemics.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars treat them as distinct but related deities, with some overlap in later traditions.

Number of arms

Position A

Jāṅgulī is typically depicted with two arms.

Position A — evidence

Early sculptures and some textual descriptions show two arms.

Position B

She is depicted with four arms.

Position B — evidence

Later tantric texts and Tibetan thangkas often show four arms.

Scholarly Consensus

Both forms are attested; the variation is due to regional and chronological differences.

Primary Evidence

Sādhanamālā (Garland of Practices), a collection of Buddhist tantric sādhanas (c. 11th century CE)

Type textDate c. 11th century CELocator Sādhana No. 160 (as cited in Bhattacharyya, 'The Indian Buddhist Iconography')Language Sanskrit

Translation

The Sādhanamālā describes Jāṅgulī as a goddess with a dark blue complexion, adorned with serpents, and holding a snake in her right hand and a trident in her left. She is seated on a lotus and surrounded by serpents. This text provides a canonical iconographic description.

Mañjuśrīmūlakalpa, a Buddhist tantric text (c. 8th century CE)

Type textDate c. 8th century CELocator Chapter 53 (as cited in scholarly discussions)Language Sanskrit

Translation

This text includes a mantra and ritual for Jāṅgulī, used for protection against snakebite and poison. It is one of the earliest textual references to her in a Buddhist context.

Stone sculptures of Jāṅgulī from Bihar and Bengal, India (c. 9th-12th century CE)

Type archaeologicalDate c. 9th-12th century CELocator Various museum collections, e.g., Indian Museum, Kolkata; Patna MuseumLanguage N/A

Translation

These sculptures depict Jāṅgulī with multiple arms, holding serpents and ritual implements, often with a serpent canopy. They confirm her popularity in the Pala period.

Tibetan Buddhist canon (Tengyur), including ritual texts on Jāṅgulī

Type textDate c. 13th century CE and laterLocator Various volumes, e.g., 'Dug sel ma'i sgrub thabs' (Sādhana of Jāṅgulī)Language Tibetan

Translation

The Tibetan canon contains numerous sādhanas and ritual texts dedicated to Jāṅgulī, indicating her integration into Tibetan Buddhism as a protective deity against poison and disease.

Aṣṭasāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā, a Mahayana Buddhist sutra (c. 1st century CE)

Type textDate c. 1st century CELocator Chapter on the Goddess (as cited in some studies)Language Sanskrit

Translation

Some scholars suggest that Jāṅgulī appears in this text as a protector of the dharma, but this is speculative and not universally accepted.
