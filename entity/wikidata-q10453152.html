Who It Is

In the shadowed forests of ancient India, where serpents coiled in the roots of the world, there rose a goddess whose very name whispered protection against the venomous bite of fate. Jāṅgulī, the Snake Goddess, was not merely a deity of the wild; she was the divine antidote, the healer who turned poison into grace, and her cult spread from the subcontinent to the distant courts of Tibet and beyond, leaving a trail of mantra and ritual that still echoes in the tantric traditions of Asia.

Jāṅgulī is portrayed as a fierce yet compassionate deity. She is protective and swift to aid those who call upon her, especially in times of danger from snakes or poison. She is also associated with healing and the destruction of evil forces. In Buddhist contexts, she is a bodhisattva-like figure who uses her power to liberate beings from suffering, particularly the suffering caused by poison, both literal and metaphorical (e.g., ignorance and negative emotions).

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Jāṅgulī is derived from the Sanskrit root 'jaṅgula', which means 'poison' or 'venom'. Thus, Jāṅgulī literally means 'she who is related to poison' or 'the poison goddess'. The name is also associated with the word 'jaṅgala', meaning 'forest' or 'wilderness', linking her to her sylvan abode.

Pronunciation — JAHN-goo-lee (Sanskrit: जाङ्गुली). In Tibetan contexts, the pronunciation is often 'Jang-gul-ee'.

Aliases — Janguli, Jangulya, Jāṅgulī Devī, Vidyādevī, Buddhist Jāṅgulī, Hindu Jāṅgulī

Powers
Immunity to poison

Jāṅgulī is immune to all poisons, including snake venom, and can neutralize any poison with her glance or touch.

Limit: No limitations are recorded, but her power is believed to be effective only for those with faith in her.

Curing snakebite and other poisonings

She can cure victims of snakebite and other venomous bites or stings, often through the recitation of her mantras or the application of consecrated water.

Limit: The efficacy is believed to depend on the purity of the practitioner and the devotion of the patient.

Control over serpents

She has absolute control over snakes and other reptiles, commanding them to harm or spare as she wishes.

Limit: She uses this power primarily for protective purposes.

Granting protection from wild animals

As a forest goddess, she protects her devotees from wild animals, particularly snakes, tigers, and other predators.

Limit: Not mentioned in classical texts.

Bestowing wisdom and spiritual power

In Buddhist contexts, she is a bestower of wisdom (prajñā) and spiritual accomplishments (siddhi).

Limit: This power is realized through tantric practice and initiation.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Jāṅgulī is typically depicted as a beautiful goddess with a dark blue or green complexion, symbolizing her connection to the forest and poison. She has two or four arms. In her right hands, she holds a snake and a vajra (thunderbolt) or a trident; in her left hands, she holds a snake and a lotus or a skull cup. She is adorned with serpents as ornaments, including a serpent as a sacred thread and a crown of serpents. Her lower garment is often mad

Symbols — Key symbols: serpents (nāgas) representing both danger and protection; the lotus symbolizing purity; the vajra representing indestructible wisdom; the trident symbolizing the three realms; the skull cup representing the transformation of death. Her iconography emphasizes her role as the conqueror of poison and the protector of devotees from snakebite and envenomation.

Domains — Jāṅgulī is associated with forests, jungles, and wilderness areas where snakes are common. She is also believed to reside in the underworld or in the realm of the nāgas. In ritual practice, she is invoked in domestic and temple settings for protection against snakebite and for curing poison-related ailments. Her cult is particularly strong in rural areas of India, Nepal, and Tibet, where snakebite is a real danger.

Accounts I
The Origin of Jāṅgulī in the Devī MāhātmyaDevī Māhātmya (Glory of the Goddess), part of the Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa (c. 5th-6th century CE)

Classical period (c. 400-600 CE)

The Devī Māhātmya narrates the goddess Durgā's battles against demons. In one episode, she creates the goddess Jāṅgulī to combat the demon of poison, who was tormenting the world. Jāṅgulī, emanating from Durgā's third eye, takes the form of a serpent-wreathed deity and drinks the poison, rendering it harmless. This story establishes Jāṅgulī as a manifestation of the supreme goddess. Jāṅgulī becomes a protector of the universe, and her worship is established to ward off poison and snakebite.

Accounts II
Buddhist Legend of Jāṅgulī as a Protector of the DharmaVarious Buddhist tantric texts, including the Mañjuśrīmūlakalpa

Early medieval period (c. 7th-9th century CE)

In Buddhist legend, Jāṅgulī is a goddess who vowed to protect the Buddha's teachings from the dangers of poison, both literal and metaphorical. She is said to have been a powerful nāgī queen who converted to Buddhism and became a guardian of the faith. She is often depicted as a bodhisattva in feminine form, using her powers to heal and protect sentient beings. Jāṅgulī is incorporated into the Buddhist pantheon as a protective deity, and her rituals are used for healing and exorcism.

Jāṅgulī and the PeacockFolk tradition and iconographic manuals

Medieval period (c. 10th-15th century CE)

A popular folk story explains why Jāṅgulī is often depicted with a peacock. According to the legend, a peacock once saved a sage from a deadly snakebite by eating the snake. The sage, grateful, blessed the peacock, and from then on, peacocks were considered sacred to Jāṅgulī. This story is not found in classical texts but is common in oral tradition and is reflected in iconography. Peacocks are associated with Jāṅgulī, and they are considered auspicious in her worship.

Jāṅgulī is the same as Manasā, the Bengali snake goddess. — Jāṅgulī and Manasā are historically distinct deities, though both are associated with snakes. Manasā is primarily a folk goddess of Bengal, while Jāṅgulī has a wider pan-Indian and Buddhist presence.

Jāṅgulī is a purely Buddhist goddess. — Jāṅgulī is also a Hindu goddess, appearing in the Devī Māhātmya and other Puranic texts.

Jāṅgulī is a demon or a nāgī (serpent being). — Jāṅgulī is a benevolent goddess, not a demon. While she is associated with serpents, she is not a nāgī herself; she is a divine being who controls them.
