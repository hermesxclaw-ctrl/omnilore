Who It Is

Before Partholón ever ploughed a furrow in Ireland, before houses, ale or cattle, a fleet of strangers was already there — 800 Fomorians living on fish and wild birds for two centuries under a leader whose very nickname no one can now translate. His name was Cichol Gricenchos, the first Fomorian ever named in Irish pseudo-history, and his defeat at Mag Itha marks the first battle ever said to have been fought on Irish soil.

Characterization is minimal and functional. Cichol is presented as a leader of a pioneer population who sustains his people for 200 years by fishing and fowling, implying resilience and maritime subsistence skill, but also technological inferiority compared to Partholón's people who introduce agriculture, houses, and brewing. He is a military leader who engages and loses the Battle of Mag Itha. Name & Tongue

Etymology — Personal name Cichol / Cíocal is of uncertain etymology, possibly non-Goidelic or pre-Goidelic in literary invention. Epithet Gricenchos / Grigenchosach is obscure and not securely translated. R.A.S. Macalister renders it as 'clapperleg' (suggesting withered or noisy/clattering leg/feet), David Comy

Pronunciation — Middle Irish approx. /ˈkʲiːxol ɡʲrʲikʲenxos/; Modern Irish rendering Cíocal Gricenchos approx. /ˈciːkəl ˈɡrʲɪcənˠxəsˠ/; Anglicized 'Cih-kol Gri-ken-khos' with guttural ch as in loch

Aliases — Cíocal Gricenchos, Cichol Gricenchos, Cíccal Grigenchosach, Cichol Grigenchosach, Cichol Claeperleg (anglicized gloss), Cicul Gricenchos

Ownership — Medieval Irish learned (pseudo-historical) tradition. Not a figure of living folk religion or pre-Christian cult; belongs to the synthetic history constructed by Christian scribes

Powers
Leadership of the Fomorians

Attested as earliest-mentioned leader (tóisech) of the Fomorians in Ireland. Commands a migration/settlement group of 800 people. Authority is political/military, not supernatural.

Limit: No supernatural powers attributed. Leadership ends with defeat at Mag Itha; fate of Cichol after battle is not stated — whether killed, expelled, or died out is unspecified.

Maritime Migration and Subsistence Survival

Brings his people to Ireland by sea and sustains them for 200 years by fishing and fowling (iacach ocus enach), implying navigation and foraging expertise in a pre-agricultural landscape.

Limit: Explicitly limited: they do NOT practice animal husbandry, ploughing, house-building, or brewing — those are introduced only by Partholón. This frames Cichol's group as technologically limited within the synthetic history's progress narrative.

Warfare — Battle of Mag Itha

Leads Fomorians in the first battle of Ireland against Partholón. This is presented as a conventional battle, not a magical contest.

Limit: Defeated decisively by Partholón after ten years of coexistence. No feats, weapons, or tactics described; defeat is total and his people disappear from narrative thereafter.

Accounts I
The Arrival of Cichol Gricenchos — The First Fomorian SettlementLebor Gabála Érenn, §§ 159-160 (Macalister Vol.2)

11th century compilation; manuscripts 12th-15th century

311 years after the Flood, Cichol Gricenchos comes to Ireland with 200 men and 600 women. They are identified as Fomorians (Fomoire), the first people to reach Ireland after the Flood in this scheme. No origin beyond the sea is given in the earliest recension; later glosses make them descendants of Ham or of earlier biblical genealogies. Their arrival establishes a pre-Partholón occupation of an otherwise empty, uncultivated island. Establishes the Fomorians as autochthonous predecessors whom successive invaders will displace; provides chronological anchor (311 years post-Flood) for all later invasions; sets up technological contrast with Partholón.

Two Hundred Years of Fishing and FowlingLebor Gabála Érenn §160

11th century

For 200 years Cichol's people subsist solely by fishing and fowling. The text explicitly notes they have no cattle, no plough, no houses, no ale. This long, static period emphasizes the island's virgin state and the Fomorians' limited culture — they survive but do not transform the land. The duration allows scribes to fill the gap between Flood and Partholón while keeping Ireland inhabited. Creates narrative justification for Partholón's later cultural innovations as true civilizing acts; reinforces Fomorians as hunter-gatherer precursors. The 200-year figure becomes a chronological fixed point for later annalists.

Accounts II
The Coming of Partholón — First Cultivator of IrelandLebor Gabála Érenn §§ 161-162; Foras Feasa ar Éirinn I.6

11th century (LGE); 17th century (Keating)

In the 311th year after the Flood (or by AFM dating AM 2520), Partholón son of Sera son of Sru arrives in Ireland with his followers, the first to bring cattle, plough, dwelling-houses, and brewing. For ten years the two peoples coexist on the island — Cichol's Fomorians and Partholón's cultivators — until tension leads to conflict. The coexistence period is unique to this invasion pair. Introduces agriculture and civilization to Ireland in the synthetic history; sets stage for first battle; establishes pattern of successive invasions displacing predecessors that structures entire LGE.

The Battle of Mag Itha — First Battle Ever Fought in IrelandLebor Gabála Érenn §162; Annals of the Four Masters M2520; Foras Feasa ar Éirinn I.6

11th century core; annalistic reception 17th century

Ten years after Partholón's arrival, Partholón defeats Cichol Gricenchos and the Fomorians at Mag Itha. The text calls this the first battle (cet cath) ever fought in Ireland. No details of combat, casualties, or weapons are given beyond the outcome: Partholón is victorious and the Fomorians are defeated and thereafter disappear from this era of the narrative. The site Mag Itha is traditionally located in the barony of Raphoe, Donegal, though other identifications exist. Removes Fomorians from Ireland until their later return in the Nemed period; establishes Partholón's people as sole inhabitants until their plague-death; Mag Itha becomes archetypal first battlefield in Irish historiography.

The Historiographical Afterlife — Recensions, Annals, and Modern ScholarshipMacalister edition apparatus; CELT corpus; scholarly commentary by John Carey, Mark Williams

12th-21st century transmission

Cichol's episode is preserved with minor variants across all LGE recensions and is copied into Early Modern histories (Keating, Ó Cléirigh). 19th-20th century translators (O'Donovan, Comyn, Macalister) debate the epithet's meaning and note the episode's artificial chronology. Modern Celticists (Carey, Williams, Ó hÓgáin) treat Cichol as a learned invention to fill pre-Partholón time, not a reflex of pre-Christian myth, and note absence of any independent folklore or cult. Cichol remains the earliest named Fomorian but is recognized as historiographical rather than mythological in the strict sense; his story influences modern dictionaries and encyclopedias but generates almost no independent literary adaptation.

Cichol Gricenchos was a Fomorian god or demon king with magical powers — In primary sources he is a mortal leader of a migrating people with no magical powers, weapons, or divine attributes described. Supernatural Fomorian traits belong to later figures like Balor and Conand in different eras of the synthetic history.

Cichol's epithet definitely means 'clapperleg' or 'withered feet' and describes his appearance — Both translations are conjectural guesses by 19th-20th century translators; the Old/Middle Irish is obscure and possibly corrupt. No narrative describes his legs or feet, and O'Donovan left it untranslated as unintelligible.

Cichol was killed at the Battle of Mag Itha — LGE says Partholón defeated (ro bris) Cichol and the Fomorians at Mag Itha but does not explicitly state Cichol was killed. His fate after defeat is unstated; later retellings add a death to provide closure.

Cichol and the Fomorians are pre-Celtic aboriginal inhabitants preserved in folk memory — The episode is a 11th-century learned invention to fill chronological gaps between Flood and Partholón, synchronized with biblical history. No independent folklore, archaeology, or pre-11th century attestation supports historicity.

There are many stories about Cichol in Irish myth — Only one narrative cluster exists — arrival, 200-year subsistence, and defeat at Mag Itha — preserved in LGE and its derivatives (Keating, AFM). He does not appear in Ulster Cycle, Fenian Cycle, or other mythological tales.
