Who It Is

Imagine a mountain that decides to stand up and walk. That's a bergrisi — a rock giant of Norse legend, carved from the living stone of Jötunheimr's peaks, with a temper as hard as granite and a grudge against the gods that's older than the mountains themselves. When Thor loses his hammer, a bergrisi is the one who took it. When the gods need a cauldron big enough to brew ale for all of Valhalla, a bergrisi owns it. These are the beings that make avalanches look like polite warnings.

Bergrisar are territorial, reclusive, and hostile to outsiders. They guard mountain passes, hoard treasure (especially gold and precious stones), and are known to hurl boulders at intruders. Unlike their more politically ambitious cousins (like Surtr or Þrymr), bergrisar rarely venture into Asgard or Midgard — they are content to rule their rocky domains. They are depicted as slow to anger but devastating when provoked, and are known to kidnap human women, steal livestock, and destroy settlements that encroach on their territory.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Old Norse 'berg' (mountain/rock) + 'risi' (giant). Literally 'mountain-riser' or 'rock-giant.' The word 'risi' itself is related to the Proto-Germanic *wrisjon, suggesting a being that rises or towers.

Pronunciation — BAIR-gree-see (approximate Old Norse: /ˈberɡˌriːsi/)

Aliases — Bergrisar (plural), Mountain Giant, Rock Giant

Ownership — Norse/Germanic mythology, specifically the jötunn (giant) class. Bergrisar are a subset of the risi/jötunn family associated with mountains and rocky terrain.

Names are cult layers, not one fixed label.

Powers
Superhuman Strength

Bergrisar possess strength that allows them to lift mountains, hurl boulders the size of houses, and crush buildings with their bare hands.

Limit: Their strength is physical rather than magical; they can be outwitted by cleverer opponents.

Stone Manipulation

Some sources suggest bergrisar can shape rock and stone as easily as a human shapes clay, creating caves, tunnels, and fortresses within mountains.

Limit: This ability is implied rather than explicitly stated in surviving sources.

Weather Manipulation (Limited)

Bergrisar are sometimes associated with avalanches, rockfalls, and localized earthquakes, suggesting a limited ability to command the mountain's fury.

Limit: This is likely a naturalistic explanation of geological events rather than intentional magic.

Immortality (or Extreme Longevity)

Like all jötnar, bergrisar are extremely long-lived, surviving for centuries or millennia. They age incredibly slowly.

Limit: They are not truly immortal; they can be killed by physical violence.

Shapeshifting

Some bergrisar, like other jötnar, possess the ability to shapeshift, though this is less commonly attributed to mountain giants than to their more magically-inclined cousins.

Limit: This may be a conflation with troll traditions.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Bergrisar are described as enormous beings of mountainous scale, their bodies seemingly fused with the rock itself. In skaldic poetry, they are depicted with stone-gray skin, craggy features, and strength that rivals tectonic forces. Their size varies by source — some are merely 'hill-sized' while others are described as spanning valleys. They are often depicted as wearing crude animal hides or being entirely naked, their bodies covered in moss a

Symbols — Mountains themselves, boulders, avalanches, earthquakes. In skaldic poetry, bergrisar are used in kennings for 'mountain' and 'stone.' Their association with stone makes them symbols of permanence, stubbornness, and the untamable wildness of the natural world.

Domains — Mountains, rocky highlands, caves, glacial valleys, and mountain passes. Bergrisar are specifically associated with the jagged peaks of Jötunheimr, but also inhabit the mountain ranges of Midgard (especially in Norway and Iceland). They are the geological aristocracy of the giant world — while sea giants (hafstrambar) rule the waves and frost giants (hrímþursar) claim the ice, bergrisar claim the high ground.

Accounts I
The Theft of MjölnirÞrymskviða (Poetic Edda)

c. 10th-11th century (composition), 13th century (recorded)

Thor wakes to find his hammer Mjölnir missing. Loki borrows Freya's falcon cloak and flies to Jötunheimr, where he discovers that Þrymr, a bergrisi of immense power, has stolen the hammer and buried it eight leagues deep. Þrymr demands Freya as his bride in exchange for the hammer's return. When Freya refuses, the gods devise a plan: Thor will dress as the bride and Loki as his handmaiden. At the wedding feast, Thor's monstrous appetite (eating an entire ox and drinking three barrels of mead) nearly gives him away, but Loki covers by claiming the bride was so eager for the wedding that she hadn't eaten for eight days. When Þrymr lifts the bride's veil to kiss her, he recoils at her burning eyes — again, Loki explains she hadn't slept for eight nights. Finally, when Þrymr brings out the hammer to consecrate the marriage, Thor seizes it and slaughters the entire wedding party. Thor reclaims Mjölnir and annihilates the bergrisi and his entire clan. The story demonstrates that even the mightiest mountain giant is no match for Thor's divine strength once he has his hammer.

Hymir's CauldronHymiskviða (Poetic Edda)

c. 10th-11th century (composition), 13th century (recorded)

The gods need an enormous cauldron to brew ale for a feast. Tyr reveals that his father, Hymir, a bergrisi of colossal size, owns a cauldron a mile deep. Thor and Tyr journey to Hymir's hall, where they face a series of trials: Thor eats two oxen at dinner, Hymir tests Thor's strength by having him break a crystal goblet (Thor eventually succeeds by throwing it at Hymir's head), and together they go fishing. On the fishing trip, Thor nearly brings up the World Serpent Jörmungandr, and Hymir is so terrified he cuts the line. The next day, Hymir refuses to give up the cauldron, and Thor smashes it over Hymir's head, killing the giant and taking the cauldron. The gods get their cauldron, Hymir is slain, and Thor once again proves that bergrisar, for all their size, are no match for him.

Accounts II
Grettir and the Mountain GiantsGrettis saga

c. 14th century

The outlaw Grettir Ásmundarson, while wandering the Icelandic wilderness, encounters various supernatural beings in the mountains — trolls, undead, and mountain giants. These encounters are episodic, with Grettir using his legendary strength and cunning to defeat beings that would crush ordinary men. The saga treats these bergrisar as real threats to travelers, suggesting a folk belief in mountain giants persisting into the Christian era of Iceland. Grettir's victories over mountain giants cement his reputation as the strongest man in Iceland, though his outlaw status means his deeds go unrewarded by society.

The Bergrisar of LandnámabókLandnámabók

c. 12th-13th century

The Book of Settlements records multiple encounters between Norse settlers and bergrisar in the Icelandic landscape. These accounts treat mountain giants as part of the natural environment — landmarks are named after them, valleys are avoided because of them, and some settlers reportedly drove them off with Christian symbols or clever tricks. The bergrisar of Landnámabók are less cosmic and more local, functioning as territorial rivals to human expansion. The bergrisar are displaced or killed as Iceland is settled, symbolizing the triumph of human civilization over wild nature.

Bergrisar are the same as Frost Giants (hrímþursar). — Frost giants are specifically associated with ice and cold, while bergrisar are associated with rock and mountains. They are related but distinct categories.

All Norse giants are evil and stupid. — Giants in Norse mythology are complex beings. Some are wise (Mimir), some are beautiful (Gerd), and some are tragic. The bergrisar of the sagas are often cunning opponents, not mindless brutes.

Bergrisar are the same as the Jötunn of Jötunheimr. — Jötunheimr is the realm of the jötnar, but not all jötnar live there, and not all inhabitants of Jötunheimr are bergrisar. Bergrisar specifically dwell in mountains.

The term 'bergrisi' appears frequently in Norse sources. — The term is actually relatively rare in surviving texts. It appears in a handful of Eddic poems and sagas, and its meaning is sometimes ambiguous.
