Who It Is

The Bergrisi (literally 'mountain-giant' in Old Norse) is a specific type of Jötunn (giant) associated with mountains, rocks, and stone. In the Prose Edda and skaldic poetry, the Bergrisar are distinguished from other giant-types like the Hrímþursar (frost-giants) and Eldjötnar (fire-giants). They are the personifications of the Scandinavian mountain landscape — immense, ancient, and immovable. Bergrisar are described as being of such size that their footsteps cause earthquakes, their sneezes create avalanches, and their quarrels hurl boulders across fjords (explaining glacial erratics — enormous rocks left in unlikely places by Ice Age glaciers). They are hostile to the gods (Æsir) and to humans, but they are not uniformly evil: they represent the primordial, anti-cosmic forces of nature that exist before and beyond divine order. The most famous Bergrisi encounter is Thor's journey to Jötunheimr, where he and his companions meet the giant Skrýmir, whose snores cause earthquakes and whose glove is a hall large enough for all of them to sleep in (Þórsdrápa and Gylfaginning). The Bergrisi also connects to the Icelandic landscape imagination — the country's volcanic, mountainous terrain is constantly described as the bodies, bones, and cast-off rocks of giants. The concept survived into Scandinavian folk belief as the 'rise' (from risi = giant) who lives in local mountains and throws rocks at church builders (a common legend explaining ruined or incomplete medieval churches). The Bergrisi is the Norse expression of the universal giant archetype — primordial, chthonic, affiliated with stone — transformed by the specific geology of Scandinavia.

Form

Bergrisar are described as enormous beings of mountainous scale, their bodies seemingly fused with the rock itself. In skaldic poetry, they are depicted with stone-gray skin, craggy features, and strength that rivals tectonic forces. Their size varies by source — some are merely 'hill-sized' while others are described as spanning valleys. They are often depicted as wearing crude animal hides or being entirely naked, their bodies covered in moss and lichen, as if the mountain has claimed them.

Powers

Superhuman Strength Bergrisar possess strength that allows them to lift mountains, hurl boulders the size of houses, and crush buildings with their bare hands. Attested across multiple sources including Þrymskviða and various sagas. Their strength is physical rather than magical; they can be outwitted by cleverer opponents. None significant.

Stone Manipulation Some sources suggest bergrisar can shape rock and stone as easily as a human shapes clay, creating caves, tunnels, and fortresses within mountains. Reconstructed from saga descriptions of giant-built structures and cave systems. This ability is implied rather than explicitly stated in surviving sources. Some scholars argue this is a later romantic embellishment rather than an original Norse belief.

Weather Manipulation (Limited) Bergrisar are sometimes associated with avalanches, rockfalls, and localized earthquakes, suggesting a limited ability to command the mountain's fury. Attested in saga accounts of giants causing landslides and rockfalls. This is likely a naturalistic explanation of geological events rather than intentional magic. None.

Immortality (or Extreme Longevity) Like all jötnar, bergrisar are extremely long-lived, surviving for centuries or millennia. They age incredibly slowly. Implied across the mythological corpus — giants predate the gods and will survive Ragnarök. They are not truly immortal; they can be killed by physical violence. None.

Shapeshifting Some bergrisar, like other jötnar, possess the ability to shapeshift, though this is less commonly attributed to mountain giants than to their more magically-inclined cousins. Rarely attested; appears in later saga material rather than the Eddas. This may be a conflation with troll traditions. Disputed — some scholars see shapeshifting as a universal jötunn trait, others as limited to specific individuals.

Behavior & Habitat

Bergrisar are territorial, reclusive, and hostile to outsiders. They guard mountain passes, hoard treasure (especially gold and precious stones), and are known to hurl boulders at intruders. Unlike their more politically ambitious cousins (like Surtr or Þrymr), bergrisar rarely venture into Asgard or Midgard — they are content to rule their rocky domains. They are depicted as slow to anger but devastating when provoked, and are known to kidnap human women, steal livestock, and destroy settlements that encroach on their territory.

Accounts

Thor wakes to find his hammer Mjölnir missing. Loki borrows Freya's falcon cloak and flies to Jötunheimr, where he discovers that Þrymr, a bergrisi of immense power, has stolen the hammer and buried it eight leagues deep. Þrymr demands Freya as his bride in exchange for the hammer's return. When Freya refuses, the gods devise a plan: Thor will dress as the bride and Loki as his handmaiden. At the wedding feast, Thor's monstrous appetite (eating an entire ox and drinking three barrels of mead) nearly gives him away, but Loki covers by claiming the bride was so eager for the wedding that she hadn't eaten for eight days. When Þrymr lifts the bride's veil to kiss her, he recoils at her burning eyes — again, Loki explains she hadn't slept for eight nights. Finally, when Þrymr brings out the hammer to consecrate the marriage, Thor seizes it and slaughters the entire wedding party.

The gods need an enormous cauldron to brew ale for a feast. Tyr reveals that his father, Hymir, a bergrisi of colossal size, owns a cauldron a mile deep. Thor and Tyr journey to Hymir's hall, where they face a series of trials: Thor eats two oxen at dinner, Hymir tests Thor's strength by having him break a crystal goblet (Thor eventually succeeds by throwing it at Hymir's head), and together they go fishing. On the fishing trip, Thor nearly brings up the World Serpent Jörmungandr, and Hymir is so terrified he cuts the line. The next day, Hymir refuses to give up the cauldron, and Thor smashes it over Hymir's head, killing the giant and taking the cauldron.

The outlaw Grettir Ásmundarson, while wandering the Icelandic wilderness, encounters various supernatural beings in the mountains — trolls, undead, and mountain giants. These encounters are episodic, with Grettir using his legendary strength and cunning to defeat beings that would crush ordinary men. The saga treats these bergrisar as real threats to travelers, suggesting a folk belief in mountain giants persisting into the Christian era of Iceland.

The Book of Settlements records multiple encounters between Norse settlers and bergrisar in the Icelandic landscape. These accounts treat mountain giants as part of the natural environment — landmarks are named after them, valleys are avoided because of them, and some settlers reportedly drove them off with Christian symbols or clever tricks. The bergrisar of Landnámabók are less cosmic and more local, functioning as territorial rivals to human expansion.

Field Notes

☘ The god Tyr is the son of a bergrisi named Hymir, making the god of war literally half mountain-giant.

☘ In Hymiskviða, Thor breaks a crystal goblet by throwing it at the bergrisi's head — the only thing hard enough to shatter it.

☘ Bergrisar are so tied to the landscape that Icelandic place names still preserve their legendary presence.

Scholarly Debates
Classification: Distinct class or descriptive epithet?
Bergrisar are a distinct class of giants, separate from frost giants (hrímþursar) and sea giants. ⇌ Bergrisar is simply a descriptive term for any giant that lives in mountains, not a separate category.
Evidence for A: The term appears consistently in association with mountain-dwelling giants in skaldic poetry.
Evidence for B: Snorri Sturluson uses 'risi' and 'jötunn' interchangeably, suggesting the categories are fluid.
Relationship to trolls
Bergrisar are essentially the same as trolls in later Norse folklore. ⇌ Bergrisar and trolls are distinct beings with different origins and characteristics.
Evidence for A: Later Icelandic sagas use 'bergrisi' and 'troll' interchangeably.
Evidence for B: The Eddas treat trolls as a separate, lesser category of being.
Size and scale
Bergrisar are truly colossal, mountain-sized beings. ⇌ Bergrisar are merely 'large humans' — bigger than normal people but not landscape-scale.
Evidence for A: Skaldic poetry describes them in cosmic terms, and Þrymr's hammer is large enough that Thor needs special handling.
Evidence for B: In sagas, bergrisar interact with humans on a personal scale — they can be fought, captured, and killed by heroic humans
What We Don't Know

Limited direct attestation of the specific term 'bergrisi' in primary sources. Most evidence is inferred from context or from the broader category of jötunn. The relationship between bergrisar and trolls in later folklore requires more detailed study. The exact appearance of bergrisar is not described in surviving sources in detail — modern depictions are largely speculative.

A way to hold it in your mind

Bergrisar are the Norse equivalent of what you'd get if you crossed a geological formation with a territorial landlord. They own the high ground, they don't like visitors, and they have a tendency to throw boulders at anyone who doesn't pay proper respect. In modern terms, think of them as the original 'get off my lawn' — except the lawn is a mountain range and the 'get off' is backed by enough strength to level a village.
