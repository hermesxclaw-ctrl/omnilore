Etymology

The term 'auspicious symbol' translates to 'xiángruì' (祥瑞) or 'jíxiáng fúhào' (吉祥符号). 'Jixiang' combines 'ji' (吉, lucky) and 'xiang' (祥, auspicious), while 'furhao' means symbol or sign.

The Legend of the Four Auspicious Beasts

Source Chinese mythology and folklore (e.g., 'Classic of Mountains and Seas' (Shan Hai Jing), though specific stories vary)Culture ChinesePeriod Ancient to modern

Summary

The dragon, phoenix, qilin, and tortoise are among the most auspicious creatures, each symbolizing different virtues: the dragon for power and yang energy, the phoenix for rebirth and yin energy, the qilin for benevolence and wisdom, and the tortoise for longevity and stability. Their appearances are often said to herald peace and prosperity.

The Story of the 'Fu' Character

Source Folk traditionCulture ChinesePeriod Ming and Qing dynasties to present

Summary

The character 'fu' (福) meaning blessing or good fortune is often displayed upside down during the New Year, as the word for 'upside down' (dao) sounds like 'arrive' (dao), so 'fu dao' means 'fortune has arrived.' This practice is said to have originated when a prince placed the character upside down to prevent a servant's mistake, and the emperor, amused, declared it auspicious.

Participants

A prince (possibly from the Ming or Qing dynasty)

The emperor

Consequences

The upside-down 'fu' became a standard New Year decoration, symbolizing the arrival of good fortune.

Variants

Different regions may use different characters or styles.
