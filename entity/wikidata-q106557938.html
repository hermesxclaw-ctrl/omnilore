Who It Is

Before it became the brightly wrapped indulgence of childhood, candy was a luxury of the wealthy, a medieval digestive aid, and even a vehicle for poison. Its history is a story of sugar, empire, and the relentless pursuit of sweetness—a tale that spans continents and centuries, transforming from a rare medicinal treat to a global industry that shapes our cravings and our health.

Candy is typically eaten casually, with fingers, as a snack between meals. It is not considered a formal dessert in many cultures, but rather an informal treat.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The word 'candy' entered English from Old French 'çucre candi' (meaning 'sugar candy'), which may have earlier roots in Arabic 'qandi', Persian 'qand', and Sanskrit 'khanda', all words for sugar.

Pronunciation — /ˈkændi/

Aliases — Sweets, Sugar confectionery, Confections, Sweetmeats

Ownership — Global, with distinct traditions in many cultures; historically associated with India, Persia, the Middle East, Europe, and the Americas.

Medicinal use

Before the Industrial Revolution, candy was used to calm digestion or soothe sore throats.

Limit: This use has largely disappeared.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Candy comes in a wide variety of shapes, sizes, colors, and textures, from hard, glossy boiled sweets to soft, chewy caramels, fluffy marshmallows, and creamy chocolates. It is often brightly colored and packaged in wrappers.

Symbols — Candy is often associated with sweetness, indulgence, childhood, holidays (especially Halloween), and reward. Iconic symbols include the candy cane, lollipop, and wrapped chocolate.

Domains — Candy is found in homes, stores, vending machines, and at celebrations. It is produced in factories and artisanal confectioneries.

Accounts I
The Origin of Sugar CandyProvided source material

6th to 4th century BCE

Sugarcane is indigenous to South and Southeast Asia. Ancient Indians boiled sugarcane juice to produce pieces of sugar called 'khanda'. Persians and Greeks discovered these 'reeds that produce honey without bees' and spread sugar cultivation. Sugar and sugarcane agriculture spread to the West, eventually leading to the development of candy as we know it.

Candy as Medicine and LuxuryProvided source material

Middle Ages

Candy was initially a medicinal aid for digestion, made from spices and sugar. It was served at banquets for the wealthy. One such candy, 'chamber spice', contained cloves, ginger, aniseed, juniper berries, almonds, and pine kernels dipped in melted sugar. Candy became associated with wealth and status.

Accounts II
The Industrial Revolution and Penny CandyProvided source material

1830s onward

Technological advances and sugar availability made candy affordable for the working class and children. Penny candies became the first material good children spent their own money on, and candy stores relied on child customers. Candy became a mass-market commodity and a staple of childhood.

The Adulteration CrisisProvided source material

Late 19th to early 20th century

Unregulated manufacturers added hazardous substances to candy for color: green (chromium(III) oxide, copper acetate), red (lead(II,IV) oxide, mercury sulfide), yellow (lead chromate), and white (chalk, arsenic trioxide). Public pressure led to the Pure Food and Drug Act of 1906. Regulation of food additives began, improving safety.
