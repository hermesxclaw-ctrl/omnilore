Who It Is

Salt stings the lip before the lime hits — sharp, cold, and bright enough to make you wince. Tequila burns clean underneath, rounded by orange liqueur that smells like sun-warmed peel. The margarita is not subtle. It is a border cocktail, born where dust meets ice, where American tourists and Mexican bartenders negotiated pleasure in a salt-rimmed glass, and it has been arguing about its own birthday ever since.

Flavor profile: attested as strongly sour (fresh lime), moderately sweet (orange liqueur and optional agave/simple syrup), saline, and agave-forward with tequila's vegetal, peppery, sometimes smoky notes. Texture is cold and diluting — shaken versions are crisp and bracing; frozen versions are smooth, sweet, and palate-numbing. Effect is intoxicating; standard IBA spec is ~1.7 standard drinks. Social role is convivial, informal, associated with celebration, vacation, and Tex-Mex dining. Not a contemplative sipping cocktail; designed for immediate refreshment in heat.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Spanish margarita = 'daisy', from Latin margarita, from Greek margarites (μαργαρίτης) 'pearl'. In cocktail taxonomy, 'Daisy' is a 19th-century drink family: base spirit + orange liqueur/cordial + citrus + sweetener, often with soda. Margarita is the direct Spanish translation of Daisy. Powers
Balanced Sour-Sweet-Salt Intoxication

Delivers ethanol via tequila (typically 35-40% ABV base, often 100% blue agave) modulated by citrus acidity and orange liqueur sweetness. The salt rim suppresses bitterness and amplifies sweet/sour perception.

Limit: Potency limited by dilution (shake/blend) and ice melt; sweetness can mask alcohol, increasing overconsumption risk. Acidity can irritate gastric lining.

Modular Flavor Carrier

Base template readily accepts fruit purees, vegetable juices (cucumber, beet, carrot, celery), coconut, spices, and alternative sweeteners without losing margarita identity, provided lime + tequila remain.

Limit: When orange liqueur is omitted (e.g., Tommy's) or replaced with soda/sports drink, purists dispute whether it remains a margarita; IBA definition requires orange liqueur.

Thermal Refreshment (Frozen Mechanization)

Frozen version provides physical cooling via slush ice, served at ~ -2 to 0°C, using blender (1947 Hernandez) or dedicated soft-serve machine (1971 Martinez) that prevents solid freezing via continuous churn.

Limit: Requires equipment; texture degrades quickly as ice melts; high sugar content increases caloric load.

Rim-Enhanced Palatability

Salt or Tajín rim creates contrast enhancement: salt reduces perceived sourness and bitterness, heightens lime aroma, and provides tactile ritual (lick rim, sip, bite lime).

Limit: Sodium load; not suitable for sodium-restricted diets; Tajín adds chili heat that may overwhelm delicate tequilas.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Attested: Served in three canonical forms — on the rocks (shaken with ice, strained over fresh ice), straight up (shaken, strained without ice), or frozen (blended to a slush). Most bars use a stepped-diameter margarita glass (variant of champagne coupe) or a rocks glass. Color is pale straw to light lime-green, cloudy from lime pulp and agitation. Accounts I
The Daisy Lineage and the 1936 Tijuana Proto-MargaritaDavid Wondrich cocktail history; James Graham account in Iowa newspaper; Cafe Royal Cocktail Book 1937

1936-1937

Cocktail historian David Wondrich traces the margarita to the Daisy family (spirit + orange liqueur + citrus). Iowa newspaper editor James Graham reported finding a tequila daisy in Tijuana in 1936, years before personal-name myths. The 1937 Cafe Royal Cocktail Book's 'Picador' — tequila, triple sec, lime in margarita proportions — shows the formula was already in print in London, suggesting informal border practice had been codified without the name Margarita. Establishes that the drink's structure predates all 1938+ invention anecdotes; reframes margarita as translation (daisy → margarita) rather than eponym.

Carlos 'Danny' Herrera at Rancho La Gloria for Marjorie King (1938)Herrera's own testimony; Albert Hernandez retelling; San Diego Reader 1992 investigation

1938

Herrera claimed he invented the margarita at his restaurant Rancho La Gloria, halfway between Tijuana and Rosarito, for customer Marjorie King, a former Ziegfeld dancer allergic to all spirits except tequila. He combined tequila, triple sec, and lime and, unable to recall the name story consistently, allegedly named it after her. Bartender Albert Hernandez popularized a version in La Jolla after 1947 and repeated the tale. Became one of the most repeated origin myths; widely cited in tourism literature before being debunked by the San Diego Reader in 1992 for inconsistencies in dates and King's identity.

Don Carlos Orozco at Hussong's Cantina for Margarita Henkel Cesena (1941)Hussong's Cantina house history; Baja tourism press

1941

Hussong's Cantina — established 1892, Ensenada's oldest bar — claims bartender Don Carlos Orozco created the drink for frequent patron Margarita Henkel Cesena, a ranch operator of Mexican-German heritage, sometimes described as daughter of a German ambassador (disputed). He is said to have experimented with tequila, Damiana liqueur, and lime before settling on the classic trio. Hussong's markets itself as 'home of the original margarita' with strong tourist draw; claim disputed due to lack of contemporary documentation and ambassador-daughter embellishment.

Accounts II
Francisco 'Pancho' Morales at Tommy's Place, Juárez (July 4, 1942)Notimex (Mexican official news agency); Morales family testimony; cocktail expert consensus

1942

Francisco 'Pancho' Morales claimed he first mixed the margarita at Tommy's Place Bar in Juárez on July 4, 1942, for a customer who requested a Magnolia-like drink. He later left bartending, moved to the U.S., and worked as a milkman for 25 years before his claim was publicized. Notimex and many modern experts cite Morales as having the strongest documentary support among personal-name claims, though no 1942 menu survives. Morales's claim gained posthumous credibility; Juárez holds civic pride events; still competes with earlier Daisy evidence that suggests no single inventor.

Margarita Sames at Acapulco and the Hilton Pipeline (1948)Margarita Sames interviews; Tommy Hilton anecdote; Jose Cuervo 1945 ads as counter-evidence

1948

Dallas socialite Margarita Sames said she concocted the drink for guests at her Acapulco vacation home in 1948, using tequila, Cointreau, and lime. Attendee Tommy Hilton (of Hilton Hotels) allegedly brought the recipe back to the Hilton chain, spreading it nationally. However, Jose Cuervo had already advertised 'Margarita: It's more than a girl's name' in 1945, undermining a 1948 invention date. Illustrates how postwar American elite tourism in Acapulco helped nationalize the drink via hotel distribution, even if invention claim is chronologically impossible as an origin.

Santos Cruz at the Balinese Room for Peggy Lee (1948)Balinese Room house lore, Galveston, Texas

1948

Head bartender Santos Cruz at the legendary Balinese Room (a pier nightclub over the Gulf) claimed he created the margarita for singer Peggy Lee, naming it after the Spanish form of her name (Peggy → Margaret → Margarita). The Balinese Room was a major entertainment venue, and the story ties the drink to American popular music glamour. Adds Texas to the origin map and reflects practice of naming drinks for female performers; no contemporary 1948 press corroboration found.

Mechanizing the Freeze: Hernandez Blender (1947) and Martinez Machine (1971)Family testimony of Albert Hernandez Sr.; Mariano Martinez interviews; Dallas Morning News; Smithsonian Food History

1947-1971

Albert Hernandez Sr. in La Jolla stopped using crushed ice and began blending margarita ingredients in a kitchen blender in 1947 to improve consistency. Two decades later, Dallas restaurateur Mariano Martinez, overwhelmed by demand and inconsistent blender results, adapted a soft-serve ice cream machine in 1971 into the world's first frozen margarita machine — a cooled horizontal cylinder with rotating impeller that dispenses thick slush. Martinez's machine was later accessioned by the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History. Frozen margarita became a mass-market phenomenon, defining Tex-Mex chain service and enabling high-volume, consistent slush; cemented Dallas as frozen margarita capital.

The margarita was invented by Jimmy Buffett or named after his song Margaritaville. — Buffett's 1977 song celebrated an already-ubiquitous drink; the margarita was in print by 1953 and advertised by 1945, decades before the song.

A margarita must be frozen or served in a giant fishbowl. — Classic margarita is shaken and served on the rocks or straight up; frozen slush is a 1947/1971 variant, not the original form.

Margarita mix and sweet-and-sour is authentic; fresh lime is optional. — Craft and IBA standards require fresh lime juice; mixes with high-fructose corn syrup and artificial lime are modern shortcuts criticized for muting agave character.

The margarita is ancient Mexican tradition. — It is a 20th-century border hybrid, likely 1930s-1940s, tied to U.S. tourism and tequila export, not pre-Hispanic or 19th-century Mexican practice.

Any tequila + lime drink is a margarita, including Paloma. — Paloma (tequila + grapefruit soda + lime) is a distinct cocktail with its own history (1950s); margarita requires orange liqueur (or agave nectar in Tommy's variant) and is not interchangeable.
