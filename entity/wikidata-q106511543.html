Who It Is

In the shadowed halls of medieval England, a name whispered through the ages carries the weight of prophecy and rebellion. Ulrica, a figure of fiery resolve and tragic destiny, emerges from the pages of Walter Scott's Ivanhoe not merely as a character but as a symbol of defiance against tyranny. Across centuries and genres, from opera stages to Borges' labyrinthine fictions, Ulrica persists—a name that echoes with the power to enchant, to curse, and to endure.

In Scott's work, Ulrica is vengeful, cunning, and fiercely independent, seeking retribution for wrongs done to her family. Verdi's Ulrica is mysterious, ominous, and powerful, wielding influence through her prophecies. Sister Ulrica in Tenko is likely compassionate yet strong, adapting to harsh circumstances. Borges' Ulrica is enigmatic, alluring, and intellectual.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Germanic origin, derived from the elements 'uodal' (heritage, fortune) and 'ric' (ruler, powerful). Thus Ulrica means 'prosperous ruler' or 'powerful heritage'.

Pronunciation — ul-REE-kah (English), UWL-ree-kah (Swedish)

Aliases — Ulrika, Ulrike, Ulrikke, Ullie, Ulla

Ownership — Primarily Germanic and Scandinavian cultures, particularly Sweden, Germany, and Denmark.

Powers
Prophecy (fictional)

In Verdi's opera, Ulrica is a fortune-teller capable of predicting the future, as seen when she foretells the death of King Gustavus III.

Limit: Her prophecies are ambiguous and open to interpretation; she cannot prevent the events she foresees.

Vengeance and Manipulation (fictional)

In Ivanhoe, Ulrica uses her knowledge of Torquilstone's secrets to aid the besiegers, enabling the castle's fall and achieving revenge against Front-de-Bœuf.

Limit: Her actions are limited by her age and physical frailty; she ultimately perishes in the fire.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Varies by portrayal. In Scott's Ivanhoe, Ulrica is described as an aged woman with a wild and fierce aspect, dressed in a dark cloak. In Verdi's opera, she is a fortune-teller, often portrayed as a gypsy or sorceress with a dark presence. In Tenko, Sister Ulrica is a nun, likely in habit. In Borges' story, Ulrica is a young woman of striking beauty.

Symbols — In opera, Ulrica is associated with dark arts, prophecy, and the supernatural. In Ivanhoe, she is linked to vengeance and the downfall of her oppressors. The name may evoke Nordic or Germanic heritage.

Domains — Fictional settings range from medieval England (Ivanhoe) to 18th-century Sweden (Un ballo in maschera), a Japanese POW camp (Tenko), and a modern European city (Borges). Historical Ulricas lived primarily in Sweden, often in court or artistic circles.

Accounts I
Ulrica in IvanhoeWalter Scott's novel

Medieval period (12th century) as depicted in 19th-century novel

Ulrica is the daughter of a Saxon thane whose family was dispossessed by the Norman Front-de-Bœuf. She becomes the mistress of Front-de-Bœuf's father, and later lives in the castle of Torquilstone, nursing a burning desire for revenge. During the siege, she aids the outlaws by revealing a secret passage, leading to the castle's capture. In the end, she sets the castle ablaze and dies in the flames, triumphant in her vengeance. The fall of Torquilstone and the death of Front-de-Bœuf; Ulrica's own death.

Ulrica in Un ballo in mascheraVerdi's opera

18th century (set in Sweden or Boston depending on version)

Ulrica is a fortune-teller who is consulted by King Gustavus III (or Riccardo in the Boston version) in disguise. She predicts that he will be killed by the next person who shakes his hand, which turns out to be his friend and conspirator Anckarström. Her prophecy sets the tragic events in motion. The king's assassination, as foretold.

Accounts II
Sister Ulrica in TenkoBBC TV series

World War II (1940s)

Sister Ulrica is a nun who is interned in a Japanese prisoner-of-war camp in Singapore. She is a minor character who provides support and spiritual comfort to the other women. Her presence contributes to the group's morale and survival.

Ulrica in Borges' storyJorge Luis Borges, 'Ulrica'

1970s

The narrator, a Colombian professor, meets a Norwegian woman named Ulrica in a hotel bar. They have a brief, intense affair, but she leaves him, and he never sees her again. The story is a meditation on love, memory, and the fleeting nature of encounters. The narrator is left with a profound sense of loss.
