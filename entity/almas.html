She was not a human woman, but she was not an ape. She was covered in dark hair, immensely strong, and could outrun a horse. The villagers called her Zana. She was an Almas.

Who It Is

The Almas (Mongolian for 'wild man') is the Central Asian equivalent of Bigfoot -- a cryptid hominid reported across the vast expanses of Mongolia, Kazakhstan, the Caucasus, and surrounding regions. Unlike the towering Sasquatch, the Almas is described as a more human-scale creature: 5 to 6 feet tall, powerfully built, covered in reddish-brown hair, with a sloping forehead, prominent brow ridges, a flat nose, and a receding chin. It is said to walk with a distinctive bow-legged gait and to communicate through grunts and whistles. The Almas is unique among cryptid hominids in the depth and detail of its documentation. The Russian Imperial Academy of Sciences received reports from explorers in the 19th century. In the early 20th century, zoologist Khaklov collected extensive eyewitness accounts across Central Asia. Soviet researcher Boris Porshnev made the Almas his life's work, compiling a dossier of over 500 reported encounters and proposing the creature represented a surviving population of Neanderthals or Homo erectus. In 1925, Red Army soldiers under General Mikhail Topilsky allegedly shot and killed a male Almas in the Pamir Mountains and examined its body, describing it as a hairy, human-like creature with distinct simian features. The most famous ongoing case involves a woman named Zana, captured in the Caucasus in the mid-19th century, who was described as an Almas -- a hairy, powerfully built woman who could not learn to speak but had children with local men; her descendants lived into the 20th century and were documented by researchers. DNA testing on Zana's alleged descendants has produced conflicting results, with some studies claiming West African ancestry and others supporting a local, possibly archaic hominin origin. The Almas remains one of cryptozoology's most scientifically intriguing cases.
