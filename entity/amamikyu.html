Who It Is

Amamikyu is the sun goddess and ancestral mother of the Ryukyuan people in Ryukyuan mythology. According to the founding myth of the Ryukyu Kingdom, Amamikyu was sent by the supreme deity Izaiho-no-Mikoto to govern the newly created Ryukyu Islands. She descended onto Kudaka Island (the most sacred site in Ryukyuan religion) and brought with her rice seeds, crops, and the arts of civilization. Amamikyu is associated with the weaving tradition of the Ryukyus — she taught the women of the islands to weave, and her own weaving produced the sacred garments used in religious ceremonies. She is also the source of the noro (priestess) tradition. According to one of the most famous myths, Amamikyu lived alone on Kudaka Island until the sun god (or the supreme god) took pity on her loneliness and sent four seasons of plants and animals, and eventually a companion. Amamikyu's festival, the Amamikyu Matsuri, was traditionally held at the Kudaka Island shrine, where the kaminchu (priestesses) would conduct rituals to ensure the sun's return and the fertility of the rice fields. Unlike the Japanese Amaterasu (with whom she is sometimes syncretized), Amamikyu is a distinctly Ryukyuan goddess — more agricultural, more shamanic, and more directly tied to the island's sacred geography. The goddess remains a powerful symbol of Ryukyuan cultural identity and the matriarchal traditions of the Okinawan people.

The Approach

In the beginning, there were no islands in the vast blue expanse—only the Heavenly Emperor above and the empty sea below. It was Amamikyu, the divine weaver of land and life, who descended from the celestial castle to breathe existence into the Ryukyu archipelago, setting in motion a lineage of kings and priestesses that would endure for centuries.

Etymology

The name 'Amamikyu' is a Japanese reading of Chinese characters 阿摩美久 or 阿摩彌姑, likely written ad hoc for the Okinawan pronunciation. It may be related to the Amami Islands. Other kanji spellings include 天御子 and 天美子 (Amamiko).

Cultural Ownership

Ryukyuan (Okinawan) religion and culture; specifically associated with the Ryukyu Kingdom and the indigenous belief system of the Ryukyu Islands.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars have drawn parallels with other creation deities in Japanese and broader East Asian mythology, but direct identifications remain speculative. The name's connection to the Amami Islands is plausible but not definitively proven.

Description

Appearance

No explicit physical description is provided in the sources. She is a goddess, likely depicted in human form in later art, but no canonical iconography exists.

Symbols & Iconography

Associated with sacred sites: Sefa-utaki, Kudaka Island, Tamagusuku Castle, Chinen Castle, Minton Castle. As a creation goddess, symbols of fertility and creation may be implied but not explicitly documented.

Behaviour & Personality

Portrayed as obedient to the Heavenly Emperor, resourceful in requesting materials, and proactive in creating islands and communities. She becomes pregnant without sexual intercourse, indicating a divine and miraculous nature.

Domains & Habitats

Heavenly Gusuku (heavenly castle) as origin; descended to Kudaka Island, then Okinawa Island; final home at Minton Castle. Associated with sacred groves (utaki) and royal authority.

Version Differences

The name and reading vary widely across sources and regions. The creation myth is consistent in Chūzan Seikan, but later retellings may emphasize different aspects. No major conflicting versions are recorded.

Stories & Myths

Creation of the Ryukyu Islands

Source Chūzan SeikanCulture RyukyuanPeriod 1650 (compiled)

Summary

The Heavenly Emperor, residing in the Heavenly Gusuku, saw no islands in the world and ordered Amamikyu to create the Ryukyu Islands. She requested materials, and Shinerikyu brought grasses, trees, and stones. She descended to Kudaka Island, then landed at Sefa-utaki on Okinawa, building castles and communities. She asked for materials to make people, but other gods refused to descend. She became pregnant by the divine spirit and populated the islands. Later, a heavenly grandchild named Tentei was born, whose three sons and two daughters became the ancestors of the five social classes: the first son became the first king (Tenson), the second became the first feudal lord (Aji), the third became the first farmer, the first daughter became the first royal noro priestess, and the second daughter became the first village noro priestess.
