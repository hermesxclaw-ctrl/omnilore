Who It Is

On the south Munster coast a single wave still bears her name. When Tonn Chlíodhna breaks over Glandore Harbour, fishermen once said Clíodhna herself was breathing — the banshee queen who left the Land of Promise for love, was stolen back by music and sea, and never quite left Ireland at all. From a rock pile five miles from Mallow to the bird-song that heals the sick, her story moves between goddess, fairy queen, and warning.

Behavior varies sharply by version and cannot be harmonized. In Acallam she is a lovesick otherworld woman who chooses exile for love with Ciabhan, passive victim of Manannán's people who put her to sleep with music and drown her with a wave. In Book of Lismore she is a knowledgeable, hospitable otherworld host who aids Teigue with guidance, birds, and a magical cup, speaking with authority about her own legend. Powers
Queenship over the Sídhe of South Munster and Banshee Host

Rules as queen over the sióga (fairies) and banshees of Desmond. Commands other sídhe and can send or withhold the death-cry for allied families. Munster peasantry considered her their benefactor and protector.

Limit: Authority is territorially limited to South Munster; not a national banshee queen. Cannot override fate but can warn of it. In literary sources her power is more personal than institutional.

Tonn Chlíodhna - Wave Mastery and Omen

Embodies or commands a great wave at Glandore that bears her name. The wave's roar was an omen of death for kings and its surge can sweep away even otherworld beings. In some tellings she was drowned by it; in others she became it.

Limit: Wave is localized to Glandore; in Acallam it is summoned by Manannán's people against her, suggesting she does not fully control it. Some versions say she is its victim, not its master.

Otherworld Music and Enchantment Sleep

Possesses or is subject to music that causes supernatural sleep. Her three birds sing a song that heals sickness, induces sleep, and can guide travelers. Conversely, the music played by Manannán's followers puts her to sleep so the wave can take her.

Limit: Birds are a gift she bestows, not permanently retained; their song heals but also can lull into vulnerability. Sleep music can be used against her.

Shapeshifting and Transformation

Can transform creatures to any form she pleases. In O'Keeffe legend she transforms her sister Aoibhell into a beautiful white cat and transforms a cave at Castlecor into a palace. Title queen of the fairies reflects general glamour and illusion.

Limit: Requires a wand (slat draíochta); when she loses her precious wand she cannot reverse the transformation. Enchantment can be broken if one comes who loves Aoibhell more than her treasures. Transformation is limited to one week in human form at midsummer for the victim.

Abduction and Love-Charm

Can abduct mortals she desires, enveloping them in a dark cloud and carrying them to her unseen hall. Also described as goddess of love and beauty who inspires love in mortals. FitzGerald and O'Keeffe traditions show her as lover who takes or wins chieftains.

Limit: Abducted mortals can be sought and sometimes recovered with help of otherworld-aware persons (Kerry wizard). Her love is often unrequited or rivaled, leading to conflict.

Healing and Prophecy via Birds and Cup

Three birds whose sweet song heals sickness; an emerald cup that transforms water into wine and is tied to life - if parted from it, the owner will soon die. She imparts prophetic warning about the cup to Teigue.

Limit: Cup's power is conditional and dangerous; loss foretells death. Birds guide but do not fight. Healing is through song, not touch.

Accounts I
Tonn Chlíodhna - Cleena's Wave (Acallam na Senórach)Acallam na Senórach (Colloquy of the Ancients)

Middle Irish, c. 1200, manuscripts 15th century

Ciabán (Ciabham) of the Curling Hair, son of the King of Ulster, leaves Ireland by currach and is nearly drowned by mountainous white waves. He is rescued by a rider on a dark grey horse with a golden bridle who brings him to Tír Tairngire, the Land of Promise, ruled by King Manannán. At a feast the chief druid Gebann's daughter, Cleena of the Fair Hair, sees Ciabán and falls in love. They elope, sailing back to Ireland and landing at Teite's Strand. Ciabán goes ashore to hunt, leaving Cleena in the boat. The people of Manannán pursue them in ships; one plays enchanted music that puts Cleena to sleep. A great wave then sweeps her away. The place is thereafter called Tonn Chlíodhna, Clíodhna's Wave, near Glandore Harbour. Aetiology for the dangerous wave and reef at Glandore; explains a coastal hazard as otherworld vengeance. Establishes Clíodhna's association with sea, music, and tragic love. Variant endings determine whether she drowns or becomes a sídhe queen of the wave.

The Voyage of Teigue, Son of Cian (Eachtra Thaidhg mhic Céin)Book of Lismore (Leabhar Mhic Cárthaigh Riabhaigh)

Middle Irish tale, manuscript c. 1480-1500, tale perhaps 14th century

Teigue, son and heir of Cian, king of West Munster, sets out with warriors to rescue kin abducted and enslaved by raiders from a far land. After rowing nine weeks they reach Inis Derglocha (Red Loch Island), where they encounter luminaries from Ireland's mythical past. The last person they meet introduces herself: 'I am Cleena Fairhead, daughter of Genann mac Treon of the Tuatha Dé Danann, sweetheart of Eochaid Redweapon's son Ciabhan of the curling locks; for now some time I am in this island, and from me Cleena's Wave in the borders of Munster is denominated.' She gives them three birds to guide them and provide music, and an emerald cup that turns water into wine, warning Teigue that if he is parted from the cup he will soon die. Teigue rescues his kinsmen and returns home victorious, the birds and cup aiding him. Provides Clíodhna's own first-person account of her exile and naming of the wave, linking the wave legend to a voyage tale. The Legend of O'Keeffe, the Druid and his Two Daughters (The White Cat of Castlecor)19th-century literary folklore, Fermoy/Castlecor tradition recorded in Croker-influenced collections and local histories

Set in early 8th century in the legend; recorded 19th century

In Fermoy lived Draoi Ruadh, the last of the Druids, made Prince of Fermoy by the King of Munster for summoning a storm to drive back enemies. He had two beautiful accomplished daughters: elder Cleena, who could transform creatures to any form she pleased and was titled queen of the fairies, and younger Aoibhell (Aoivil), her inseparable companion. Prince Caiov (O'Keeffe) of a neighboring territory often visited and excelled at sports, winning the druid's admiration. Both sisters fell in love with him. Cleena declared her love and marriage was discussed with her father. Aoibhell secretly won Caiov's love. When Cleena discovered this, in jealousy she called on her old nurse to curse Aoibhell, who sickened and lost her beauty. Cleena then pretended to help with a powerful sleep draught that caused apparent death. Aoibhell was coffined and entombed under the palace. By a secret entrance Cleena and the nurse moved the unconscious Aoibhell to a cave at Castlecor, 5 miles northeast of Kanturk, telling her on waking she would be imprisoned until she forgot Caiov. The Legend of Ellen O'Brien and FitzGeraldMunster folk legend, Shannon region, recorded in 19th-century collections (Croker tradition, later retellings)

Folk legend with no medieval attestation; recorded 19th century, set in unspecified chieftain era

FitzGerald was a youthful chieftain by the Shannon, brave, hospitable, generous and an accomplished harpist. Ellen O'Brien, only daughter of a chief whose power had crumbled under Saxon pressure, loved him and was won by his harp. Clíodhna also desired FitzGerald and resolved to bring him to her unseen hall. On a festival day a dark cloud descended, enveloped FitzGerald and carried him away; no trace could be found and it was believed he was taken by enchantment. Ellen, stricken, sought a wizard in Kerry who communed with the otherworld. The wizard told her FitzGerald had been taken to Clíodhna's fairy palace (Carrigcleena or Glandore) and instructed her how to seek him, involving music and otherworld negotiation. In fuller variants Ellen's fidelity and harp-playing rival Clíodhna's enchantment, and FitzGerald is eventually recovered or remains with Clíodhna depending on telling. Illustrates Clíodhna's role as abductor of desirable mortals, parallel to other sídhe lover-abductor tales. Demonstrates her rivalry with mortal women and the need for druidic/wizardly mediation. Clíodhna's Birds and the Otherworld Apple TreeSynthesis of Book of Lismore (birds), folk tradition, and modern summaries of goddess of love and beauty motif

Motif attested medieval (birds) and elaborated in 19th-20th century folklore

Clíodhna is said to have three brightly coloured birds who eat apples from an otherworldly tree (often identified as the apple tree of Emain Ablach or similar). Their sweet song heals sickness and can lull listeners to sleep. In the Book of Lismore she gives three such birds to Teigue to guide him; in folk retellings the birds remain with her at Carrigcleena, and their singing is heard over the rocks. Modern popular accounts expand this into her being a goddess of love and beauty whose birds represent love, healing, and enchantment. The apple itself is a standard otherworld symbol of immortality and sovereignty. Provides Clíodhna's most recognizable iconography beyond the wave; used in modern art, jewelry, and neopagan devotion. Connects her to broader Irish otherworld bird and apple symbolism. Reinforces healing vs harming duality.

Clíodhna as Tutelary of the Uí Fidgenti and Desmond FamiliesGenealogical and poetic tradition, bardic poetry associated with O'Donovans, O'Collins, MacCarthys, O'Keeffes, FitzGeralds

Tradition spanning 373-977 AD Uí Fidgenti period through late medieval Desmond, recorded in early modern poetry and 19th-century antiquarian notes

Clíodhna is especially associated with old Irish families of Munster. She is observed to be linked to lands that were territory of the Uí Fidgenti (O'Donovans and O'Collins) during their period of influence (c. 373-977), and later to those who occupied former Uí Fidgenti territory (MacCarthys and FitzGeralds). Surviving narrative and poetry associate her with the O'Keeffes (Ó Caoimh) and O'Donovans, the latter originally from Uí Fidgenti in North Munster where their original goddess was Mongfind, later moving south to Carbery on Cuan Dor (Glandore), Clíodhna's abode, after which she features in poetry to them. The MacCarthy dynasty of Desmond adopted her as their fairy woman, as did the O'Keeffes and FitzGeralds, with whom she is said to have had amorous affairs. Michael Collins, descended from Ó Coileáins of Uí Chonaill Gabra (also Uí Fidgenti), knew stories of her at Rosscarbery school and visited Clíodhna's Rock on Sundays. Clíodhna is the ancient Irish goddess of love, equivalent to Aphrodite/Venus — No medieval source calls her goddess of love and beauty. That epithet is a modern summary, popularized in 19th-20th century popular writing and neopaganism. Medieval texts present her as a high-status sídhe woman of the Tuatha Dé Danann with otherworld music and sea associations; her love affairs are narrative events, not a divine domain.

Clíodhna is just a banshee who wails at death — She is described as Queen of the Banshees of the Tuatha Dé Danann, not a single keening banshee. Her folklore emphasizes queenship, territorial patronage, healing birds, and wave omens; the classic old hag wailing at a window is a different banshee type. She is depicted as young and beautiful, not hag-like.

Clíodhna and Aoibhell are the same figure or interchangeable Munster fairy queens — They are distinct figures with separate territories (Clíodhna: South Munster/Carrigcleena/Glandore; Aoibhell: Craglea, County Clare) and separate traditions. Only the late O'Keeffe legend makes them sisters; most sources keep them separate.

Tonn Chlíodhna is a purely mythical wave with no real location — Tonn Chlíodhna is a real, named reef and wave at Glandore Harbour, County Cork, with a documented maritime hazard and folk omen tradition. The Dindsenchas and Acallam provide aetiology for an existing place, not an invented otherworld location.

The name is pronounced Clee-OH-na and spelled Cliona in Irish — Irish spelling is Clíodhna (with síneadh fada and dh), pronounced approximately KLEE-uh-nuh [ˈclʲiːnˠə]. Cliona is an anglicized simplification; Cleena is an older transliteration. Pronunciation varies by dialect but is not three syllables with stress on OH.
