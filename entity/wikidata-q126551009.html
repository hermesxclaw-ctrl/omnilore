Who It Is

Imagine a king so hungry for a glimpse of the divine that he takes the advice of a seer and rounds up every sick person in his kingdom, sending them to die in the quarries. That's the story of Amenothes—a man whose name means 'Amun is satisfied,' but whose legend is a brutal, twisted memory of Egypt's most chaotic eras. He is the shadowy monarch who, in a single act of misguided piety, unleashed a rebellion that nearly tore Egypt apart. Was he a real pharaoh, a fictional priest, or a scapegoat for a national trauma? The sands of history have swallowed the truth, leaving only a Greek historian's bitter tale.

The only behavioral trait attested is a desire to see the gods (as reported by Josephus), which frames him as a theurgist, a seeker of divine vision, or a king with priestly ambitions. This is a common trope in Greco-Egyptian literature, where kings or sages seek direct communion with the divine.

Powers
Divine Vision (Desire and Ability)

The central power attributed to Amenothes in the Manethonian narrative is the ability or the desire to see the gods. In the Egyptian tradition, this was a privilege of the highest priests and the king, often achieved through ritual purification and temple rites.

Limit: The act of seeing the gods is presented as a dangerous and portentous event, leading to a series of calamities in the narrative.

Accounts I
The Counsel of Amenothes (The 'Lepers' Narrative)Manetho via Josephus, Contra Apionem

Hellenistic (3rd century BCE) / Roman (1st century CE)

According to the account preserved by Josephus, a king named Amenothes desired to see the gods, as had his predecessor. He was counseled by a wise man (often identified as a seer or prophet) that he could achieve this vision if he cleared the land of all lepers and other unclean persons. The king gathered these individuals and sent them to work in the stone quarries. The outcasts, led by a priest named Osarseph, later rebelled, allied with the Hyksos, and wrought havoc on Egypt, leading to a period of chaos. The narrative is used by Josephus to counter anti-Jewish claims by linking the expulsion of the 'lepers' to the Exodus story. For Amenothes, the consequence is the loss of his kingdom to chaos, and his eventual restoration after a long war.
