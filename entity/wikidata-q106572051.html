Who It Is

War is a chameleon of human catastrophe: a constant across millennia, yet forever changing its shape. From prehistoric raids to cyberattacks, it is the organized application of violence for political ends, a force that has built empires, toppled states, and shaped the very nature of human society.

War is characterized by organized violence, destruction, and mortality. It involves strategic planning, tactical maneuvers, and the application of force to achieve political, economic, or territorial objectives. War often escalates, with participants adopting increasingly extreme measures. It can be total, involving entire societies, or limited, restricted to specific military targets.

Powers
Destruction

War's primary ability is to cause widespread destruction of life, property, and infrastructure. This can be achieved through direct combat, siege, bombardment, or deliberate targeting of civilian areas.

Limit: Destruction is limited by the capabilities of the belligerents, the terrain, and the laws of war (where applicable).

Political Change

War can bring about political change, such as regime change, territorial annexation, or the creation of new states. It can topple governments, redraw borders, and alter the balance of power.

Limit: Political outcomes are often unpredictable and may not align with the original war aims. Wars can also lead to unintended consequences, such as the rise of new powers or prolonged instability.

Social Transformation

War can transform societies, leading to changes in social structures, gender roles, technology, and culture. It can accelerate technological innovation, centralize state power, and alter demographic patterns.

Limit: Social transformation is often slow and uneven, and may not be intentional. The long-term effects of war on society are complex and contested.

Form & Signs

Appearance — War has no physical form; it manifests through the actions of armies, weapons, and the destruction of landscapes and cities. Its visual iconography includes military uniforms, flags, artillery, tanks, aircraft, and the aftermath of battle: ruined buildings, refugee columns, and mass graves.

Symbols — Common symbols of war include crossed swords, the god Mars, the goddess Bellona, the dove with an olive branch (as a symbol of peace opposing war), military medals, poppies (symbolizing remembrance of war dead), and the peace sign. War memorials and cenotaphs are physical symbols of war's impact.

Domains — War occurs in all environments: land, sea, air, space, and cyberspace. It can be fought in cities, jungles, deserts, mountains, and oceans. It is a social phenomenon, arising within and between states, empires, and other organized groups.

Accounts I
Prehistoric WarfareArchaeological evidence and anthropological studies (e.g., Keeley, Kelly)

Paleolithic to Neolithic

The origins of war are debated. Some scholars argue that early humans were largely peaceful, while others point to evidence of violent conflict, such as massacres and weapon injuries, in prehistoric remains. Keeley claims that 90-95% of known societies engaged in warfare, with forms ranging from small raids to massacres. Kelly suggests that before 400,000 years ago, conflict resembled chimpanzee clashes, but later, peaceful relations became more common in some societies. Prehistoric warfare likely influenced social organization, territorial boundaries, and population dynamics. It may have led to the development of defensive structures and weapons.

Accounts II
War and State FormationCharles Tilly, 'Coercion, Capital, and European States, AD 990–1992'

990–1992

Tilly argued that 'war made the state, and the state made war.' In Europe, the need to wage war led to the consolidation of states, which extracted resources and built administrative structures to support military campaigns. In turn, these states perpetuated war to maintain their power and expand their territories. The modern nation-state system in Europe was largely shaped by centuries of warfare. This process also led to the development of standing armies, taxation systems, and centralized bureaucracies.

The Evolution of Modern WarfareGeneral historical knowledge

19th–21st centuries

Since the 19th century, warfare has been transformed by industrialization, technological innovation, and globalization. The World Wars of the 20th century exemplified total war, involving entire societies and causing unprecedented destruction. After 1945, the threat of nuclear war led to a decline in great power conflicts, but civil wars and insurgencies have proliferated, often involving asymmetric warfare and non-state actors. Modern warfare has led to the development of international humanitarian law, the rise of peacekeeping operations, and the ongoing debate about the decline of war. However, civil wars and humanitarian crises continue to cause immense suffering.
