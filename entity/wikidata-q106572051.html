The Approach

War is a chameleon of human catastrophe: a constant across millennia, yet forever changing its shape. From prehistoric raids to cyberattacks, it is the organized application of violence for political ends, a force that has built empires, toppled states, and shaped the very nature of human society.

Content advisory. This dossier discusses war, including violence, death, destruction, and other distressing topics. It includes historical and modern examples of armed conflict and does not shy away from the grim realities of warfare.

Etymology

The English word 'war' derives from the 11th-century Old English words 'wyrre' and 'werre', from Old French 'werre' (modern French 'guerre'), in turn from the Frankish '*werra', ultimately deriving from the Proto-Germanic '*werzō' meaning 'mixture, confusion'. The word is related to Old Saxon 'werran', Old High German 'werran', and modern German 'verwirren', meaning 'to confuse, to perplex, to bring into confusion'.

Cultural Ownership

Universal; war is a human phenomenon found across all cultures and historical periods.

Disputed Identifications

There is scholarly debate about whether war is an innate human behavior or a cultural invention. Some argue that prehistoric hunter-gatherer societies were largely peaceful, while others contend that warfare has been common throughout human history. The definition of war itself is also contested, with some scholars including all organized violence and others restricting it to state-based conflicts.

Description

Appearance

War has no physical form; it manifests through the actions of armies, weapons, and the destruction of landscapes and cities. Its visual iconography includes military uniforms, flags, artillery, tanks, aircraft, and the aftermath of battle: ruined buildings, refugee columns, and mass graves.

Symbols & Iconography

Common symbols of war include crossed swords, the god Mars, the goddess Bellona, the dove with an olive branch (as a symbol of peace opposing war), military medals, poppies (symbolizing remembrance of war dead), and the peace sign. War memorials and cenotaphs are physical symbols of war's impact.

Behaviour & Personality

War is characterized by organized violence, destruction, and mortality. It involves strategic planning, tactical maneuvers, and the application of force to achieve political, economic, or territorial objectives. War often escalates, with participants adopting increasingly extreme measures. It can be total, involving entire societies, or limited, restricted to specific military targets.

Domains & Habitats

War occurs in all environments: land, sea, air, space, and cyberspace. It can be fought in cities, jungles, deserts, mountains, and oceans. It is a social phenomenon, arising within and between states, empires, and other organized groups.

Version Differences

War has evolved significantly over time. Prehistoric warfare likely involved small-scale raids and ambushes. With the rise of states, warfare became more organized and large-scale. The invention of gunpowder and industrialization led to mass conscription and total war. In the modern era, nuclear weapons have created the possibility of mutually assured destruction, and cyberwarfare and information warfare have added new dimensions. Since 1945, great power wars have declined, but civil wars and insurgencies have increased.

Powers & Abilities

Destruction

Source Version All forms of war

Description

War's primary ability is to cause widespread destruction of life, property, and infrastructure. This can be achieved through direct combat, siege, bombardment, or deliberate targeting of civilian areas.

Limitations

Destruction is limited by the capabilities of the belligerents, the terrain, and the laws of war (where applicable).

Contradictions

Some argue that war can be 'clean' or 'surgical' with precision weapons, but evidence shows that even modern warfare causes significant collateral damage.

Political Change

Source Version Throughout history

Description

War can bring about political change, such as regime change, territorial annexation, or the creation of new states. It can topple governments, redraw borders, and alter the balance of power.

Limitations

Political outcomes are often unpredictable and may not align with the original war aims. Wars can also lead to unintended consequences, such as the rise of new powers or prolonged instability.

Contradictions

Some wars have achieved their stated aims, while others have resulted in stalemate or defeat. The outcome depends on numerous factors, including military strategy, economic resources, and international support.

Social Transformation

Source Version Modern total wars

Description

War can transform societies, leading to changes in social structures, gender roles, technology, and culture. It can accelerate technological innovation, centralize state power, and alter demographic patterns.

Limitations

Social transformation is often slow and uneven, and may not be intentional. The long-term effects of war on society are complex and contested.

Contradictions

While some argue that war has driven technological progress, others emphasize its devastating human and social costs.

Stories & Myths

Prehistoric Warfare

Source Archaeological evidence and anthropological studies (e.g., Keeley, Kelly)Culture Various hunter-gatherer and early agricultural societiesPeriod Paleolithic to Neolithic

Summary

The origins of war are debated. Some scholars argue that early humans were largely peaceful, while others point to evidence of violent conflict, such as massacres and weapon injuries, in prehistoric remains. Keeley claims that 90-95% of known societies engaged in warfare, with forms ranging from small raids to massacres. Kelly suggests that before 400,000 years ago, conflict resembled chimpanzee clashes, but later, peaceful relations became more common in some societies.

Participants

Early human groups

Tribes

Clans

Consequences

Prehistoric warfare likely influenced social organization, territorial boundaries, and population dynamics. It may have led to the development of defensive structures and weapons.

Variants

Different regions and time periods show varying levels of conflict. Some societies were more warlike than others.

War and State Formation

Source Charles Tilly, 'Coercion, Capital, and European States, AD 990–1992'Culture European statesPeriod 990–1992

Summary

Tilly argued that 'war made the state, and the state made war.' In Europe, the need to wage war led to the consolidation of states, which extracted resources and built administrative structures to support military campaigns. In turn, these states perpetuated war to maintain their power and expand their territories.

Participants

European monarchs

Nobles

Armies

Taxpayers

Consequences

The modern nation-state system in Europe was largely shaped by centuries of warfare. This process also led to the development of standing armies, taxation systems, and centralized bureaucracies.

Variants

Tilly's theory is dominant but not universally accepted. Some scholars emphasize other factors, such as economic development and social change.

The Evolution of Modern Warfare

Source General historical knowledgeCulture GlobalPeriod 19th–21st centuries

Summary

Since the 19th century, warfare has been transformed by industrialization, technological innovation, and globalization. The World Wars of the 20th century exemplified total war, involving entire societies and causing unprecedented destruction. After 1945, the threat of nuclear war led to a decline in great power conflicts, but civil wars and insurgencies have proliferated, often involving asymmetric warfare and non-state actors.

Participants

Nation-states

Non-state actors

Insurgents

Civilians

Consequences

Modern warfare has led to the development of international humanitarian law, the rise of peacekeeping operations, and the ongoing debate about the decline of war. However, civil wars and humanitarian crises continue to cause immense suffering.

Variants

Warfare varies across regions, with different strategies, technologies, and legal frameworks.

Relationships

State

Type creatorSource Tilly (1990)

Description

The state is the primary entity that wages war, using its military forces to pursue political objectives. War has also been a key factor in the formation of states, as argued by Tilly.

Version Note

In modern times, non-state actors also engage in warfare, but states remain the dominant actors in international armed conflict.

Conflicting Accounts

Some scholars argue that states are the sole legitimate actors in war, while others recognize the role of insurgent groups and militias.

Peace

Type oppositeSource General philosophical and political thought

Description

War is typically defined in opposition to peace. Peace is often seen as the absence of war, but it can also be a positive condition of justice and stability. Wars often aim to establish a particular peace, such as a 'peace of the strong' or a 'just peace'.

Version Note

The relationship between war and peace is complex; some argue that 'peace' can be maintained through deterrence (e.g., nuclear deterrence), which is a form of war preparation.

Conflicting Accounts

Some pacifists argue that peace is the only moral goal and that war is never justified. Others, such as just war theorists, argue that some wars can be just and lead to a better peace.

Military

Type instrumentSource General

Description

The military is the organized force that conducts war. It includes armies, navies, air forces, and other branches. The military is a key institution of the state, and its size, technology, and strategy shape the nature of war.

Version Note

The military has evolved from small professional armies to large conscripted forces and now to professional, technologically advanced militaries in many countries.

Conflicting Accounts

There is debate about the role of the military in society, including issues of civilian control, militarism, and the use of private military contractors.

Civilians

Type victimSource General historical and legal sources

Description

Civilians are often the primary victims of war, suffering casualties, displacement, and the destruction of their homes and livelihoods. Modern warfare has increasingly blurred the line between combatants and civilians, leading to high civilian casualty rates in many conflicts.

Version Note

International humanitarian law seeks to protect civilians, but violations are common. In total war, civilians are often deliberately targeted.

Conflicting Accounts

Some argue that civilian casualties are an unfortunate but unavoidable aspect of war, while others condemn them as war crimes. The distinction between combatants and civilians is often contested.

Atrocities and War Crimes

Source Historical records, international criminal tribunals, and human rights organizations

Description

War has often involved the deliberate killing of civilians, prisoners of war, and other non-combatants. Examples include the Mongol invasions, the Thirty Years' War, the World Wars, and numerous recent conflicts. War crimes such as genocide, ethnic cleansing, and sexual violence have been documented in many conflicts.

Scholarly Context

International humanitarian law (IHL) prohibits such acts, but enforcement remains a challenge. Scholars debate the causes of atrocities, including ideology, dehumanization, and military necessity.

Colonial Warfare

Source Historical accounts and postcolonial studies

Description

Colonial powers often waged wars of conquest against indigenous peoples, resulting in massive loss of life, displacement, and cultural destruction. These wars were frequently justified by racist ideologies and involved brutal tactics, including the deliberate destruction of food supplies and the use of concentration camps.

Scholarly Context

Scholars have documented the devastating impact of colonial warfare on indigenous populations and its lasting effects on postcolonial states.

Civilian Suffering in Modern War

Source United Nations reports, non-governmental organizations, and media coverage

Description

Since 1945, civil wars and insurgencies have caused immense civilian suffering. Tactics such as siege warfare, the use of landmines and cluster munitions, and the deliberate targeting of civilians have led to high casualty rates and humanitarian crises.

Scholarly Context

The study of civilian victimization in war has become a major field, with scholars examining the dynamics of violence against civilians and the effectiveness of humanitarian interventions.

Contradictions & Disputes

Is war declining?

Position A

War is in decline. Scholars like Joshua Goldstein, John Mueller, and Steven Pinker argue that the frequency and deadliness of war have decreased over time, especially since 1945.

Position A — evidence

Statistical data showing a decline in battle deaths, great power wars, and territorial conquests since 1945. Pinker's 'Better Angels of Our Nature' documents these trends.

Position B

War is not in decline. Bear F. Braumoeller and others challenge this view, pointing to the persistence of civil wars and the possibility of new great power conflicts.

Position B — evidence

Civil wars have increased in absolute terms since 1945. Braumoeller's research argues that the decline is not statistically robust and that the post-WWII period may be an anomaly.

Scholarly Consensus

The debate is ongoing. Many scholars accept that certain types of war (e.g., great power wars) have declined, but there is disagreement about the overall trend and its causes.

Were prehistoric societies peaceful?

Position A

Prehistoric societies were largely peaceful. Some anthropologists argue that early hunter-gatherers lived in egalitarian bands and rarely engaged in organized violence.

Position A — evidence

Sparse archaeological remains and ethnographic studies of modern hunter-gatherers that show peaceful relations between groups.

Position B

Prehistoric societies were warlike. Keeley and others argue that archaeological evidence shows high rates of violent death and that warfare was common even among hunter-gatherers.

Position B — evidence

Keeley's analysis of archaeological sites and ethnographic records suggests that 90-95% of societies engaged in warfare. Evidence includes massacres, weapon injuries, and fortifications.

Scholarly Consensus

There is no consensus. The debate is complicated by the difficulty of interpreting archaeological evidence and the diversity of human societies.

Is war innate or cultural?

Position A

War is an innate human behavior, rooted in evolutionary biology and psychology. This view is held by some evolutionary psychologists and sociobiologists.

Position A — evidence

Evidence of violence in chimpanzees, our closest relatives, and the ubiquity of war across cultures suggests a biological basis.

Position B

War is a cultural invention, not an innate drive. Many anthropologists and sociologists argue that war is a social phenomenon that emerges under specific conditions, such as the rise of states and agriculture.

Position B — evidence

The existence of peaceful societies and the fact that warfare has changed dramatically over time suggest that culture plays a dominant role. Kelly's work on warless societies supports this.

Scholarly Consensus

This is a long-standing debate. Most scholars now see war as a complex interplay of both biological and cultural factors, but the relative importance is still disputed.
