Who It Is

Diwata is a term used across the Philippines (derived from Sanskrit devata, meaning 'divine being') for a class of nature spirits that inhabit forests, mountains, rivers, seas, and caves. Unlike the supreme gods (like Bathala), diwata are localized spirits who serve as guardians of specific natural places. Each mountain, river, or ancient tree is believed to have its own diwata. They are generally benevolent but easily offended — they bless respectful visitors with good fortune but punish those who pollute, deforest, or desecrate their domains with illness, disorientation, or accidents. Diwata are described as beautiful, ageless beings, sometimes indistinguishable from humans, other times translucent, luminous, or able to transform into animals. Offerings of food, betel nut, and tobacco are commonly left at the entrances to forests or on riverbanks to appease them. The tradition of asking permission ('ba'ya' or 'tabi po') before entering forests or crossing rivers survives in rural Philippines today. The Spanish colonial adaptation transformed 'diwata' into a general term for any pagan deity, but its original meaning as a localized nature spirit remains the most authentic.
