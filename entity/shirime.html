Who It Is

The shirime is a one-joke yokai, and the joke is immortal. A samurai walking a road outside Kyoto at night is stopped by a man in a kimono who calls out: excuse me, sir — a moment, please. The samurai turns. The stranger drops his robe, bends over, and presents his bare backside — where, in the place least expected, a single enormous eye opens and shines like lightning. The samurai flees; the shirime, presumably, is satisfied; no other behavior, motive, habitat, or capability is recorded anywhere in the tradition. That is the entire file — one incident, one witness, one eye — and its thinness is exactly why it is beloved. The shirime (the name is a pun: shiri, buttocks; me, eye; and shirime also means a backward glance) was preserved and possibly invented by the haiku master and painter Yosa Buson, whose yokai scrolls delighted in absurd spirits, and it has been passed down ever since as the tradition's purest specimen of a crucial yokai truth: not everything in the dark wants to eat you. Some of it just wants to see your face when it drops its robe. The shirime asks for a moment of your time, delivers the single most committed practical joke in world folklore, and disappears from the record forever — the monster as one perfect punchline, still landing after two and a half centuries.

Origins

An Edo-period anecdote preserved through Yosa Buson's yokai paintings (the Buson yokai emaki) — a literary-artistic yokai, likely born as much from a painter's wit as from any village report.

Attested in Edo-period anecdote; Buson yokai paintingsEra Edo period

Form

An ordinary robed figure on a dark road until the reveal: bare buttocks parting around one huge, brilliant, lightning-bright eye — the tradition's most reliably described anatomical feature, for obvious reasons.

Anus-eye manifestation The defining power: Shirime can cause a large, glittering, luminous eye to open in the place of its anus. In Buson's emaki the eye is 'glowing like lightning.' The eye's purpose is to shock and frighten an observer, not to see or gather information in any attested narrative. Attested in Buson yōkai emaki (c. 1754–1757) and repeated in Mizuki's shirime + the English Wikipedia anecdote. No narrative shows the eye serving as a second organ of sight; its function is purely spectatorial-fright. The creature does not use it to harm or curse anyone. Whether she eye 'glows like lightning' (Buson reading) or 'glitters' (Wikipedia summary) is a wording difference across secondary accounts, not a substantive variant.

Faceless human disguise Shirime presents as a normal human being at a distance — 'when close enough, however, it appears that it is a human' yet 'it has no facial features.' It can strip off its clothes to bare its posterior in a staged confrontation, blending the body-erasure gift of the noppera-bō family with the scatological reveal. Attested (Wikipedia 'Shirime'; Noppera-bō article the nupperibōzu lineage). The disguise only works at a distance; on close approach the absence of facial features and the anus-eye are revealed. Some readings classify Shirime purely as a subclass of the noppera-bō (Yumoto), while others (and the distinctive anus-eye) argue it is its own creature — see contradictions.

Scare-based joy (harmless prankster) Described as deriving its happiness from frightening people without intending harm. It stages a low-brow, voyeuristic prank rather than a predatory attack. This is an absence-of-power-as-feature: Shirime has no appetite for victims, no curse, no theft, no possession. Attested (English Wikipedia 'Shirime': 'It does not mean to harm people. Its joy comes from scaring people.'). The harmless register means Shirime cannot be weaponized in any attested story; its only tool is psychological shock. The shock it produces is described as terror, but no source records lasting harm, possession, or death — consistent across accounts.

Weaknesses & Wards

None required. It attacks nothing, takes nothing, and wants nothing but the reaction — walking on without turning around defeats it completely, and has the side effect of being the only dignified outcome available.

Accounts

Long ago, a samurai was walking at night down the road to Kyōto when he heard someone calling out for him to wait. 'Who's there?!' he asked nervously, and turned around to find a man stripping off his clothes and pointing his bare buttocks at the flabbergasted traveler. A huge glittering eye then opened up where the strange man's anus should have been. The traveller is shocked (the folklore leaves the aftermath ambiguous — the fright itself is the point) and the creature, satisfied, presumably withdraws.

In a set of yōkai paintings by the great haiku poet and painter Yosa Buson, dated c. 1754–1757, a figure called the nupperibōzu is depicted allegedly appearing in the Katabira-suji ('Chainmail alley') area of Kyoto City. The figure is faceless — part of the noppera-bō (flat-faced) family — but is shown with a distinctive trait: an eyeball in the anus, glowing like lightning. Buson 'included it in many of his yōkai paintings,' per the Shirime article, indicating an artistic fascination with the creature.

Because the faceless nupperibōzu variant sported the distinctive eye-in-anus, the manga artist and yōkai scholar Shigeru Mizuki gave it the name shirime ('butt-eye'). His embellished cartoon depicts the shirime stripping naked while carrying its clothes in order to spook a samurai. This is the single most influential act in the creature's modern identity: Mizuki's naming and cartoon are largely why 'Shirime' is today a recognized named yōkai, distinct from the broad noppera-bō family.

On April 1, 2023, an independently-made PC game titled 'Shirime: The Curse of Butt-Eye' was released, based on the legend and starring Shirime as the protagonist. Its April 1 release date may be a deliberate joke aligned with the creature's prankster character, though this is inference. The game is the only documented standalone modern adaptation found in citation search.

Cultural Significance

The shirime is the yokai canon's designated absurdist — cited whenever scholars argue that the tradition holds humor and horror in the same hand — and a fixture of every irreverent yokai compendium since Buson put brush to it.

A way to hold it in your mind

On a dark road to Kyōto, a lone samurai is hailed from behind by a stranger's voice — 'wait!' — and turns to find a man shrugging off his clothes and presenting his naked rear, whereupon a vast, glittering eye blinks open where an anus should be. It is one of the strangest sights in all of Japanese folklore, and it is a joke. The creature that pulls it is called Shirime — literally the 'buttocks-eye' — a yōkai whose entire being is a groaner of a prank: faceless, harmless, delighting only in fright. Yet behind the schoolyard gag crouches a surprisingly rich and sharply contested history: a Kyoto alley immortalized by the great poet-painter Yosa Buson, and a name coined barely a century ago by the manga master Shigeru Mizuki, who turned an obscure faceless monk into the most famous posterior in the supernatural canon.
