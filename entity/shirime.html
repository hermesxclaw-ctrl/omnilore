Who It Is

On a dark road to Kyōto, a lone samurai is hailed from behind by a stranger's voice — 'wait!' — and turns to find a man shrugging off his clothes and presenting his naked rear, whereupon a vast, glittering eye blinks open where an anus should be. It is one of the strangest sights in all of Japanese folklore, and it is a joke. The creature that pulls it is called Shirime — literally the 'buttocks-eye' — a yōkai whose entire being is a groaner of a prank: faceless, harmless, delighting only in fright. Yet behind the schoolyard gag crouches a surprisingly rich and sharply contested history: a Kyoto alley immortalized by the great poet-painter Yosa Buson, and a name coined barely a century ago by the manga master Shigeru Mizuki, who turned an obscure faceless monk into the most famous posterior in the supernatural canon.

Shirime is a prankster, not a predator. The folklore explicitly states it 'does not mean to harm people' and that 'its joy comes from scaring people.' It stages a low, vulgar practical joke: accosting a passerby, stripping, and revealing the glowing eye in its anus to provoke shock, laughter, and — in the traveler's case — terror. The humor is scatological (buttocks/anus), a decidedly coarse register within the yōkai bestiary. It has no appetite for victims, no curse, no theft; it is one of the most benign and overtly comic creatures in the tradition.

Powers
Anus-eye manifestation

The defining power: Shirime can cause a large, glittering, luminous eye to open in the place of its anus. In Buson's emaki the eye is 'glowing like lightning.' The eye's purpose is to shock and frighten an observer, not to see or gather information in any attested narrative.

Limit: No narrative shows the eye serving as a second organ of sight; its function is purely spectatorial-fright. The creature does not use it to harm or curse anyone.

Faceless human disguise

Shirime presents as a normal human being at a distance — 'when close enough, however, it appears that it is a human' yet 'it has no facial features.' It can strip off its clothes to bare its posterior in a staged confrontation, blending the body-erasure gift of the noppera-bō family with the scatological reveal.

Limit: The disguise only works at a distance; on close approach the absence of facial features and the anus-eye are revealed.

Scare-based joy (harmless prankster)

Described as deriving its happiness from frightening people without intending harm. It stages a low-brow, voyeuristic prank rather than a predatory attack. This is an absence-of-power-as-feature: Shirime has no appetite for victims, no curse, no theft, no possession.

Limit: The harmless register means Shirime cannot be weaponized in any attested story; its only tool is psychological shock.

Accounts I
The Samurai on the Road to KyotoWikipedia 'Shirime' (story summarised from Japanese yōkai anecdote/tradition); also Mizuki's canonical strip

Oral/anecdotal tradition; popularized in the modern era by Shigeru Mizuki

Long ago, a samurai was walking at night down the road to Kyōto when he heard someone calling out for him to wait. 'Who's there?!' he asked nervously, and turned around to find a man stripping off his clothes and pointing his bare buttocks at the flabbergasted traveler. A huge glittering eye then opened up where the strange man's anus should have been. The traveller is shocked (the folklore leaves the aftermath ambiguous — the fright itself is the point) and the creature, satisfied, presumably withdraws. The sole 'harm' is the traveler's shock; the story functions as a comic-grotesque scare rather than a moral or cautionary tale.

The Buson Emaki: The Nupperibōzu of Katabira-sujiWikipedia 'Noppera-bō', citing the 'Buson yōkai emaki' (c. 1754–1757)

c. 1754–1757

In a set of yōkai paintings by the great haiku poet and painter Yosa Buson, dated c. 1754–1757, a figure called the nupperibōzu is depicted allegedly appearing in the Katabira-suji ('Chainmail alley') area of Kyoto City. The figure is faceless — part of the noppera-bō (flat-faced) family — but is shown with a distinctive trait: an eyeball in the anus, glowing like lightning. Buson 'included it in many of his yōkai paintings,' per the Shirime article, indicating an artistic fascination with the creature. The emaki is the earliest documented anchor of the creature. Folklorist Kōichi Yumoto later judged the figure 'just a noppera-bō,' while the eye-in-anus trait is what would distinguish it and eventually earn it the name shirime.

Accounts II
Mizuki Names and Embellishes the ShirimeWikipedia 'Noppera-bō'; Shigeru Mizuki's yōkai essays and comic strip

Late 20th century (Shigeru Mizuki, 1922–2015)

Because the faceless nupperibōzu variant sported the distinctive eye-in-anus, the manga artist and yōkai scholar Shigeru Mizuki gave it the name shirime ('butt-eye'). His embellished cartoon depicts the shirime stripping naked while carrying its clothes in order to spook a samurai. This is the single most influential act in the creature's modern identity: Mizuki's naming and cartoon are largely why 'Shirime' is today a recognized named yōkai, distinct from the broad noppera-bō family. Established 'shirime' as a discrete named yōkai in modern popular consciousness and English-language yōkai sources; anchors the entry in the Mizuki canon (GeGeGe no Kitarō-adjacent yōkai scholarship).

Shirime: The Curse of Butt-Eye (2023 video game)PC indie game 'Shirime: The Curse of Butt-Eye', released April 1, 2023

2023

On April 1, 2023, an independently-made PC game titled 'Shirime: The Curse of Butt-Eye' was released, based on the legend and starring Shirime as the protagonist. Its April 1 release date may be a deliberate joke aligned with the creature's prankster character, though this is inference. The game is the only documented standalone modern adaptation found in citation search. Demonstrates the persistence of the creature as a niche but recognized yōkai in the global indie-gaming space.
