Who It Is

Everyone wants it, everyone talks about it, and no one can agree what it is. Is happiness the bright flash of joy you feel here and now — the laugh yesterday that Gallup counts in Paraguay — or the quiet verdict you give your whole life from the top of a ladder in Finland? From Aristotle's flourishing to Bhutan's constitution to Pharrell's chorus, happiness slips between feeling and judgment, luck and virtue, private smile and national dashboard.

Described in psychology as comprising two separable components: (1) Affective/experiential: frequent positive emotions (joy, contentment, amusement, love), infrequent negative emotions, high positive-to-negative affect ratio (PANAS). (2) Cognitive/evaluative: global life satisfaction judgment (SWLS, Cantril ladder). Behaviors associated: prosociality, approach motivation, broadened attention (Fredrickson's broaden-and-build theory, 2001), greater sociability and generosity in experimental games. Powers
Association with Health and Longevity (Correlational)

Observational studies show individuals reporting higher happiness/SWB have better self-rated health, stronger social relationships, and greater resilience. Some longitudinal studies find association with lower cardiovascular risk and longer lifespan, but causality is not established.

Limit: As of 2016, no evidence that happiness itself causes improved physical health; confounds include socioeconomic status, baseline health, and reverse causation (healthier people report higher happiness). Experimental mood induction shows short-term immune and pain tolerance effects, but long-term causal claims are unsupported.

Broaden-and-Build Cognitive Effects

Positive affect broadens attentional scope, increases creativity (e.g., improved performance on Duncker candle problem), enhances cognitive flexibility and openness to information, and builds durable personal resources (social bonds, skills).

Limit: Effects are moderate, context-dependent, and measured in lab with induced mood; does not imply permanent intellectual enhancement. Negative affect can also enhance analytical accuracy.

Social and Prosocial Potentiation

Happier individuals are more likely to help others, volunteer, and be judged as more likable and cooperative. Happiness is associated with stronger marriage and friendship stability in longitudinal surveys.

Limit: Directionality unclear; social connection is both predictor and outcome. Cultural variation: collectivist cultures may prioritize relational harmony over individual happiness reporting.

Resilience and Stress Buffering (Psychological)

Higher SWB and positive affect predict faster recovery from stress, lower rumination, and better coping after adversity (e.g., bereavement, unemployment). Reported resilience in face of adversity.

Limit: Resilience is multiply determined; happiness is one factor among many (social support, economic resources). Peak-end rule and affective forecasting biases mean people mispredict their resilience.

Economic and Policy Utility (Governance Tool)

When aggregated, happiness/SWB scores are used as policy dashboards to complement GDP: Bhutan's GNH Index (4 pillars, 9 domains, 33 indicators), UK ONS Well-being Dashboard (2012), OECD Better Life Index, World Happiness Report rankings influencing policy discourse.

Limit: Self-report biases, cultural response styles, translation issues, and Goodhart's Law (metric becomes target) limit utility. Happiness economists acknowledge philosophical and methodological issues but continue using questionnaires for population averages.

Form & Signs

Appearance — As an abstract mental state, happiness has no canonical physical appearance. Iconographic representations are culture-bound and modern: the yellow smiley face (Harvey Ball, 1963), the emoticon :) and emoji 🙂/😊/😁 (Unicode 6.0, 2010), upward-curved mouth, bright colors (yellow), light, and expansive posture in psychological coding (Duchenne smile: orbicularis oculi + zygomaticus major). Historical art used allegorical female figures: Felicitas/Abun

Symbols — Primary modern symbols: smiley face, sun, light, open hands, laughter. Traditional symbols: cornucopia (abundance), laurel wreath (fulfillment), lotus (Buddhist sukha), four-leaf clover (hap/luck etymology). In Bhutan, the dragon and Gross National Happiness mandala with four pillars. In positive psychology, the PERMA model (Seligman) uses five pillars as diagrammatic symbol. Color psychology associates yellow and warm hues with happiness in West

Domains — Domains: individual subjective experience, interpersonal relationships, work, health, community, governance. Habitats in research: laboratory, daily life (experience sampling via Day Reconstruction Method, Kahneman), national populations (Gallup World Poll, ~150 countries), workplace and schools (positive education). Cosmology: In Aristotelian ethics, happiness (eudaimonia) is the telos (final end) of human life within the polis. In utilitarianis

Accounts I
Aristotle's Eudaimonia: Happiness as Activity According to VirtueAristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Books I and X

Classical Greece, c. 350 BCE

Aristotle opens by asking what the highest human good (telos) is. He rejects that it is pleasure, honor, or wealth, arguing that eudaimonia — often translated as happiness but better as flourishing — is activity of the soul in accordance with virtue over a complete life. It requires external goods, habituation, practical wisdom (phronesis), and contemplation (theoria). A single swallow does not make a summer; happiness is not a momentary feeling but a quality of a whole life well-lived. This account establishes the enduring eudaimonic tradition distinct from hedonic feeling. Founded Western eudaimonic ethics; influenced Stoic, Thomistic, and modern virtue ethics and contemporary eudaimonic psychology (Ryff, Deci & Ryan, Seligman). Created the hedonia/eudaimonia distinction still central to happiness research.

Bentham's Felicific Calculus: Happiness as Measurable UtilityJeremy Bentham, An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789), and 1780 manuscripts on measuring happiness

Late 18th century

Bentham proposes the principle of utility: an action is right insofar as it produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number. He argues happiness (pleasure minus pain) can and should be measured along seven dimensions: intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, and extent. Government performance should be judged by its ability to maximize this aggregate happiness. This is the first systematic attempt to quantify happiness for public policy. Launched utilitarian ethics and happiness economics; influenced welfare economics, cost-benefit analysis, and modern SWB measurement. John Stuart Mill later amends Bentham to distinguish higher (mental) from lower (bodily) pleasures, responding to 'pig satisfied' objection.

Bhutan's Gross National Happiness: Happiness as Constitutional MandateConstitution of Bhutan (2008), Article 9; Centre for Bhutan Studies GNH Index (2008, 2010, 2015); Karma Ura et al.

1972 (King Jigme Singye Wangchuck's declaration) to present

Fourth King Jigme Singye Wangchuck declares Gross National Happiness more important than Gross Domestic Product. The state operationalizes this as four pillars (sustainable development, cultural preservation, environmental conservation, good governance) and nine domains (psychological well-being, health, education, time use, cultural diversity, good governance, community vitality, ecological diversity, living standards) measured via a 33-indicator survey. The 2008 Constitution mandates the state to promote GNH. Bhutan becomes the first nation to formally measure happiness as development goal; inspires UK, UAE, New Zealand well-being budgets and OECD frameworks. GNH screening tool reviews all policies for happiness impact.

Accounts II
The Positive Psychology Turn: Seligman and the Science of HappinessMartin Seligman, Authentic Happiness (2002), Flourish (2011); Sonja Lyubomirsky, The How of Happiness (2007); Ed Diener's SWB program

1998-present (Seligman's APA presidency)

In 1998, Martin Seligman as APA president calls for a shift from disease model to studying positive emotion, engagement, relationships, meaning, and accomplishment (PERMA). Ed Diener systematizes Subjective Well-Being research; Lyubomirsky estimates a happiness pie chart (50% genetics, 10% circumstances, 40% intentional activities) and catalogs 12 intentional activities. Barbara Fredrickson, Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi (flow), and others create an empirical science of happiness with interventions (gratitude letters, three good things). Creates academic journals (Journal of Happiness Studies, 2000), university courses, and a self-help industry. Provides evidence base for World Happiness Report. Critiqued for individualism and WEIRD sample bias.

The World Happiness Report and the Global Happiness RankingWorld Happiness Report, eds. Helliwell, Layard, Sachs (2012-present), data from Gallup World Poll

2012-present

First published in 2012 at UN High Level Meeting on Well-being and Happiness, the report ranks ~150 countries on Cantril ladder life evaluations plus six explanatory variables (GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom, generosity, corruption). Finland, Denmark, and Nordic countries consistently top evaluative rankings, while Gallup Positive Experience rankings (did you smile/laugh yesterday?) are topped by South American countries (Paraguay, Panama 85/100 in 2018). The report demonstrates that social support and freedom explain more variance than income after a threshold. Makes national happiness a media event each March 20 (International Day of Happiness). Influences policy in UAE (Minister of Happiness), New Zealand Wellbeing Budget (2019), and OECD dashboards. Popularizes Cantril ladder as public shorthand for national success.

The Measurement Debates: Kahneman's Experienced vs. Remembered HappinessDaniel Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011); Kahneman & Krueger, Developments in the Measurement of Subjective Well-Being (2006); Day Reconstruction Method (2004)

1990s-2010s

Kahneman distinguishes the experiencing self (moment-to-moment affect, 'what I experience here and now') from the remembering self (retrospective life evaluation, subject to peak-end rule and duration neglect). Using the Day Reconstruction Method and experience sampling, he shows people are poor affective forecasters and that remembered happiness diverges from experienced happiness. This challenges reliance on single life-satisfaction questions and reveals that how happiness is measured — now vs. later — changes the result, explaining why Nordic countries win on memory-based SWB while Latin American countries win on current affect. Forces happiness research to use multiple measures (PANAS, SWLS, Cantril, DRM, Positive Experience). Underpins critique that self-report surveys are prone to cognitive biases and that happiness is stable over time yet increases with age (young adults least happy).

What happiness is: hedonic feeling vs. eudaimonic flourishingHappiness is positive affect and life satisfaction (hedonic SWB) — what you feel here and now plus how you evaluate your life (Kahneman, Diener, Veenhoven).Happiness is eudaimonia — living virtuously, meaningfully, and fully functioning, not merely feeling good (Aristotle, Ryff, Seligman in Flourish, Landes). Feeling happy while living badly is not true happiness.
How much happiness is determined: set-point vs. intentional activityLyubomirsky's 50% genetic set-point, 10% circumstances, 40% intentional activity suggests large self-control.Behavioral geneticists and critics argue 50-10-40 is an unreplicated heuristic, misreads twin studies, and overstates controllability; circumstances (poverty, war, discrimination) matter far more than 10%.
Can happiness be validly measured via self-reportSelf-report surveys (SHS, PANAS, SWLS, Cantril) are valid and useful; people can use 'happy' appropriately in verbal contexts for both current feeling and life evaluation; national averages are meaningful (happiness economists).Self-reports are prone to cognitive biases (peak-end rule, affective forecasting errors), cultural response styles, translation issues, and memory inaccuracies; they should be complemented by objective dashboards.
Does happiness cause healthHigher happiness is associated with better physical and mental health, stronger relationships, and resilience.No causal evidence that happiness improves physical health; association is correlational and confounded.

Happiness means being happy all the time; happy people never feel negative emotions. — Evidence shows even very happy people (Diener & Seligman 2002) experience negative affect; SWB is defined as frequent positive and infrequent — not absent — negative affect. Psychological health requires emotional diversity; suppressing negative emotions reduces well-being.

Money can't buy happiness at all, or conversely, more money always makes you happier. — World Happiness Report data show income (GDP per capita) correlates with life evaluation up to a threshold, but with diminishing returns; social support, freedom, and health explain more variance after basic needs met. Kahneman & Deaton (2010) found emotional well-being plateaus ~$75k US (later revised), while life evaluation continues rising. Neither extreme claim is accurate.

Happiness is 50% genetic, 10% circumstances, 40% under your control — so you can choose to be 40% happier. — The 50-10-40 pie chart is Lyubomirsky's heuristic estimate from limited studies, not a fixed law. Heritability does not mean immutability, and circumstances (poverty, health, community) matter far more than 10% in many contexts. Intentional activities help modestly but not deterministically.

Nordic countries are the happiest in every sense, so everyone should live like them. — Nordics top evaluative life satisfaction (Cantril ladder) but South American countries (Paraguay, Panama, Guatemala) top affective Positive Experience (smiling, enjoyment yesterday) in Gallup 2018. Different measures give different winners; cultural response styles and question wording matter. Happiness is fuzzy and measure-dependent.

Happiness is the same as joy, pleasure, or contentment. — Scholarly distinctions: joy is momentary and process-bound (Summa), pleasure is sensory, contentment is low-arousal satisfaction, happiness (philosophically) can refer to a whole life evaluation even if the person was often miserable, while SWB combines affect + evaluation. Collapsing them obscures important differences.
