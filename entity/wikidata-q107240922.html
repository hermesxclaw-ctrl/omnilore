The Approach

In the shadowed mountains of Arcadia, where the ancient oaks whispered secrets of a golden age, there lived a prince whose name would be forever etched in the annals of divine vengeance. Socleus, son of the impious Lycaon, stood at the precipice of a fate that would consume his entire lineage in a single, cataclysmic moment. His story, though brief, is a chilling testament to the consequences of hubris and the unforgiving justice of Zeus.

Content advisory. This entry contains mythological depictions of child sacrifice, divine punishment, and mass death. Reader discretion is advised.
Identity

Socleus

Pronunciation SO-klee-us (English), so-KLEH-as (reconstructed Ancient Greek)

Etymology

The name Socleus (Σωκλέα) is of uncertain etymology. It may be related to the Greek word σῶς (sōs, 'safe, sound') or σώζω (sōzō, 'to save'), suggesting a possible meaning 'savior' or 'safe one', though this is speculative.

None known. Socleus is a minor figure, and no conflicting identifications have been found in ancient sources.

Description

Appearance

No specific physical description of Socleus survives in ancient sources. As a son of Lycaon, he would be of princely bearing, but no details are attested.

Symbols & Iconography

None known. Socleus does not appear in any surviving artistic depictions from antiquity.

Behaviour & Personality

According to Apollodorus, Socleus and his brothers were 'the most nefarious and carefree of all people' (ἀσεβέστατοι καὶ ἀφροντιστότατοι). This suggests a character marked by impiety and reckless disregard for divine law.

Domains & Habitats

Socleus was an Arcadian prince, likely residing in the region of Lycosura or on Mount Lycaeum, the traditional seat of his father Lycaon.

Version Differences

There are no significant variations in the account of Socleus across ancient sources. The myth of Lycaon's sons is consistent, though other authors (e.g., Pausanias) provide additional details about Lycaon's punishment but do not name all the sons.

Stories & Myths

The Punishment of Lycaon and His Sons

Source Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.8.1Culture GreekPeriod Mythical (Archaic period, likely Bronze Age setting)

Summary

Lycaon, king of Arcadia, had fifty sons, including Socleus. They were notorious for their impiety and arrogance. To test their wickedness, Zeus visited them disguised as a peasant. The brothers, in a shocking display of sacrilege, killed a child and mixed its entrails into the meal offered to the god. Zeus, enraged, overturned the table (an act that was said to have given the city of Trapezus its name, from 'trapeza', meaning table) and struck the entire family with lightning, killing Socleus, his brothers, and their father. Lycaon was transformed into a wolf, according to some accounts.

Consequences

The entire line of Lycaon was destroyed, and the myth explains the origin of the wolf cult of Zeus Lycaeus and the name of the city Trapezus. It also serves as an etiological tale for the punishment of impiety.

Variants

Pausanias (8.2.3) recounts a similar story but does not name the sons. Some versions say Lycaon was turned into a wolf immediately, while others say he was killed by lightning. The number of sons varies from 22 to 50 depending on the source.

Child sacrifice and cannibalism

Source Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.8.1

Description

Socleus and his brothers slaughtered a child and served its entrails to Zeus, an act that constitutes both human sacrifice and attempted cannibalism.

Scholarly Context

Scholars interpret this myth as reflecting ancient fears of human sacrifice and the breakdown of xenia (guest-host relations). It also serves as an etiological tale for the cult of Zeus Lycaeus, which may have involved human sacrifice in archaic times.

Scholarly Consensus

The number of sons is likely symbolic, representing the many tribes of Arcadia. The exact count varies by source, and Socleus is only named in Apollodorus.

Primary Evidence

Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.8.1

Type textDate 1st-2nd century CELocator Book 3, Chapter 8, Section 1Language Ancient Greek

Translation

Socleus is listed among the fifty sons of Lycaon. The text recounts that Zeus, angered by their impiety, visited them in disguise and was served a meal containing the entrails of a child. In wrath, he overturned the table and struck the brothers and their father with lightning.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

The primary source for Socleus is a single passage in Apollodorus. No other ancient text names him. His mother is not specified in the surviving sources, though other sons of Lycaon are attributed to various mothers. No archaeological or artistic evidence directly references Socleus. Modern scholarly discussion of Socleus as an individual is virtually nonexistent; he is only mentioned in lists of Lycaon's sons. Further research might explore regional Arcadian traditions, but the available evidence is extremely limited.
