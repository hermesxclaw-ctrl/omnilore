Who It Is

In the shadowed mountains of Arcadia, where the ancient oaks whispered secrets of a golden age, there lived a prince whose name would be forever etched in the annals of divine vengeance. Socleus, son of the impious Lycaon, stood at the precipice of a fate that would consume his entire lineage in a single, cataclysmic moment. His story, though brief, is a chilling testament to the consequences of hubris and the unforgiving justice of Zeus.

According to Apollodorus, Socleus and his brothers were 'the most nefarious and carefree of all people' (ἀσεβέστατοι καὶ ἀφροντιστότατοι). This suggests a character marked by impiety and reckless disregard for divine law.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Socleus (Σωκλέα) is of uncertain etymology. It may be related to the Greek word σῶς (sōs, 'safe, sound') or σώζω (sōzō, 'to save'), suggesting a possible meaning 'savior' or 'safe one', though this is speculative.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No specific physical description of Socleus survives in ancient sources. As a son of Lycaon, he would be of princely bearing, but no details are attested.

Symbols — None known. Socleus does not appear in any surviving artistic depictions from antiquity.

Domains — Socleus was an Arcadian prince, likely residing in the region of Lycosura or on Mount Lycaeum, the traditional seat of his father Lycaon.

Accounts I
The Punishment of Lycaon and His SonsPseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.8.1

Mythical (Archaic period, likely Bronze Age setting)

Lycaon, king of Arcadia, had fifty sons, including Socleus. They were notorious for their impiety and arrogance. To test their wickedness, Zeus visited them disguised as a peasant. The brothers, in a shocking display of sacrilege, killed a child and mixed its entrails into the meal offered to the god. Zeus, enraged, overturned the table (an act that was said to have given the city of Trapezus its name, from 'trapeza', meaning table) and struck the entire family with lightning, killing Socleus, his brothers, and their father. Lycaon was transformed into a wolf, according to some accounts. The entire line of Lycaon was destroyed, and the myth explains the origin of the wolf cult of Zeus Lycaeus and the name of the city Trapezus. It also serves as an etiological tale for the punishment of impiety.

Number of sons of LycaonApollodorus lists fifty sons, including Socleus.Pausanias does not give a specific number, and other sources mention varying counts (e.g., 22).

Socleus was a villain who personally committed heinous acts. — Socleus is not individually characterized in ancient sources; the act of impiety is collective, attributed to all the brothers together.

The myth is about cannibalism. — The myth involves the serving of a child's entrails to Zeus, which is an act of extreme sacrilege and human sacrifice, but it is not necessarily cannibalism in the sense of consumption by humans; it is an offering to a god.
