Who It Is

In the beginning, there was a void, and then there was a sky. Not a sky as we know it — a vast, divine being named Anu, who was the heavens themselves. He was the first god, the father of all others, and the ultimate authority in the cosmos. But unlike the passionate, scheming gods of later mythologies, Anu was a distant, silent king. He was the sky you look up to, the authority you appeal to, and the power you hope never notices you. He was the original 'sky daddy,' sitting on his throne above the world, dispensing justice and destruction with the same indifferent hand.

Anu is a remote, aloof, and transcendent figure. He is the ultimate source of authority, but he rarely intervenes directly in the affairs of gods or humans. He is a 'laissez-faire' king of the gods, delegating power to Enlil (the god of wind and storms) and Ea/Enki (the god of wisdom and waters). He is a father figure, often approving the requests of other gods (especially his daughter Ishtar) but also acting as a moral arbiter. He is not a trickster or a warrior; he is a cosmic judge and the ultimate guarantor of order.

Powers
Kingship over the Gods

Anu is the supreme authority in the divine assembly. His word is final, and his decrees are absolute. He can grant kingship to other gods (as he does to Marduk in the Enuma Elish) and to human kings.

Limit: He rarely exercises this power directly, preferring to delegate.

Creator of Heaven and Earth

In Sumerian tradition, Anu is one of the first beings, born of the primordial sea (Nammu). He is the personification of the sky, and his union with Ki (the earth) produces the air god Enlil, who separates them.

Limit: He is not a craftsman-creator like Ea/Enki; his creation is more of a primordial emanation.

The 'Me' (Divine Decrees)

Anu holds the 'Me', the divine decrees that are the fundamental principles of civilization, kingship, and ritual. He is the guardian of these cosmic laws.

Limit: These decrees can be stolen (as Inanna does) or transferred.

Granting the Bull of Heaven

Anu gives the Bull of Heaven to Ishtar to use against Gilgamesh, demonstrating his ability to unleash cosmic forces at the request of his children.

Limit: The Bull is a destructive force that is ultimately killed by Gilgamesh and Enkidu, showing that even Anu's gifts are not invincible.

Creating Rescuers

In the Descent of Ishtar, Anu creates two sexless beings (kurgarru and galaturu) to rescue Ishtar from the underworld, showing his power to create life for specific purposes.

Limit: He does not go to the underworld himself; he uses agents.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Anu is not described with a detailed physical form. He is primarily conceptualized as the sky itself, the vast, overarching dome of heaven. In art, he is often represented by a horned crown (the symbol of divinity), a star symbol, or a seated figure on a throne. He is rarely depicted anthropomorphically in a way that gives him a distinctive body; his essence is the heavens.

Symbols — The primary symbol of Anu is the horned crown (tiered tiara), which is the standard symbol of divinity in Mesopotamian art. He is also associated with the star (the cuneiform sign DIĜIR), the number 60 (the highest in the Sumerian sexagesimal system), and the planet Uranus (which the Babylonians associated with Anu).

Domains — Anu's domain is the highest heaven, the 'heaven of Anu.' This celestial realm is the highest of the three heavens in Mesopotamian cosmology (the other two being the heavens of the stars and the heavens of the earth). He is the god of the sky, the heavens, and the celestial sphere. Accounts I
The Separation of Heaven and EarthSumerian creation myth (various texts)

3rd millennium BCE

In the beginning, An (sky) and Ki (earth) were joined as one. Their son, Enlil, the god of wind, separated them, lifting the sky (An) up and keeping the earth (Ki) below. This separation created the space for life to exist. Anu then became the god of the heavens, while Ki/Earth became the domain of other gods. Established the cosmic order of the universe, with Anu as the supreme god of the upper realm.

Anu and the Bull of HeavenEpic of Gilgamesh

2nd millennium BCE

After Gilgamesh rejects Ishtar's advances, she flies to her father Anu in heaven and demands the Bull of Heaven to punish Gilgamesh. Anu warns her that the Bull will bring seven years of famine, but she provides for the people. Anu gives her the Bull, which Gilgamesh and Enkidu slay, leading to the gods' decision that Enkidu must die. The death of Enkidu, which is the turning point of the epic, is a direct result of Anu's decision to grant Ishtar's request.

Accounts II
The Flood (Atra-Hasis)Atra-Hasis

17th century BCE

When humanity becomes too noisy and populous, Enlil decides to destroy them. Anu, as the supreme god, is consulted and ultimately approves the decision to send a flood. However, Ea/Enki warns Atra-Hasis to build a boat, saving humanity. After the flood, Anu and the other gods realize they are dependent on human offerings and establish a new order. The establishment of the new cosmic order, with human mortality and population control as a permanent condition.

The Descent of Ishtar to the UnderworldAkkadian version

2nd millennium BCE

Ishtar descends to the underworld, and while she is gone, all sexual activity ceases on earth. The gods, led by Ea, create a plan to rescue her. Anu, as her father, creates two sexless beings (kurgarru and galaturu) who use the water of life to revive her. Ishtar is rescued, and the cycle of life and death is restored.

The Theogony of Dunnu (Hurrian influence)Akkadian text from the 1st millennium BCE

1st millennium BCE

In this text, a different succession myth is presented. Anu is overthrown by his son Kumarbi (the Hurrian grain god), who bites off Anu's genitals and becomes pregnant with the storm god Teshub. This is a separate tradition from the standard Mesopotamian theology. This narrative shows the syncretism between Mesopotamian and Hurrian mythologies.

Anu is the same as the Greek god Zeus. — Anu and Zeus share the 'sky father' archetype, but they are distinct gods with different myths, cults, and cultural contexts. Anu is more remote and less anthropomorphic than Zeus.

Anu is a creator god who made the world from nothing. — Anu is a personification of the sky, not a craftsman-creator. In Sumerian myth, he is born from the primordial sea (Nammu), and he is one of many primordial beings. The actual act of creation is often attributed to Ea/Enki or Marduk.

Anu is a 'good' god who is kind to humanity. — Anu is not a moral exemplar. He is a cosmic force who can sanction the destruction of humanity (flood myth) and unleash devastating beasts (Bull of Heaven). He is not malevolent, but he is not benevolent in a human sense.

The planet Uranus is named after the Greek god, not Anu. — This is true. The planet is named after the Greek sky god Uranus. However, the Babylonians associated the planet with Anu, and the Greek name Uranus itself likely derives from the Semitic word for 'sky' (Anu), showing a linguistic and conceptual connection.
