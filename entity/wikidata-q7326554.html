Form

Anu is not described with a detailed physical form. He is primarily conceptualized as the sky itself, the vast, overarching dome of heaven. In art, he is often represented by a horned crown (the symbol of divinity), a star symbol, or a seated figure on a throne. He is rarely depicted anthropomorphically in a way that gives him a distinctive body; his essence is the heavens.

Powers

Kingship over the Gods Anu is the supreme authority in the divine assembly. His word is final, and his decrees are absolute. He can grant kingship to other gods (as he does to Marduk in the Enuma Elish) and to human kings. All periods He rarely exercises this power directly, preferring to delegate. None. This is the most consistent aspect of his character.

Creator of Heaven and Earth In Sumerian tradition, Anu is one of the first beings, born of the primordial sea (Nammu). He is the personification of the sky, and his union with Ki (the earth) produces the air god Enlil, who separates them. Sumerian creation myths He is not a craftsman-creator like Ea/Enki; his creation is more of a primordial emanation. In the Enuma Elish, Anu is a second-generation god, born from Anshar and Kishar, not a primordial being.

The 'Me' (Divine Decrees) Anu holds the 'Me', the divine decrees that are the fundamental principles of civilization, kingship, and ritual. He is the guardian of these cosmic laws. Sumerian period These decrees can be stolen (as Inanna does) or transferred. None.

Granting the Bull of Heaven Anu gives the Bull of Heaven to Ishtar to use against Gilgamesh, demonstrating his ability to unleash cosmic forces at the request of his children. Epic of Gilgamesh The Bull is a destructive force that is ultimately killed by Gilgamesh and Enkidu, showing that even Anu's gifts are not invincible. None.

Creating Rescuers In the Descent of Ishtar, Anu creates two sexless beings (kurgarru and galaturu) to rescue Ishtar from the underworld, showing his power to create life for specific purposes. Akkadian version of the Descent of Ishtar He does not go to the underworld himself; he uses agents. None.

Accounts

In the beginning, An (sky) and Ki (earth) were joined as one. Their son, Enlil, the god of wind, separated them, lifting the sky (An) up and keeping the earth (Ki) below. This separation created the space for life to exist. Anu then became the god of the heavens, while Ki/Earth became the domain of other gods.

After Gilgamesh rejects Ishtar's advances, she flies to her father Anu in heaven and demands the Bull of Heaven to punish Gilgamesh. Anu warns her that the Bull will bring seven years of famine, but she provides for the people. Anu gives her the Bull, which Gilgamesh and Enkidu slay, leading to the gods' decision that Enkidu must die.

When humanity becomes too noisy and populous, Enlil decides to destroy them. Anu, as the supreme god, is consulted and ultimately approves the decision to send a flood. However, Ea/Enki warns Atra-Hasis to build a boat, saving humanity. After the flood, Anu and the other gods realize they are dependent on human offerings and establish a new order.

Ishtar descends to the underworld, and while she is gone, all sexual activity ceases on earth. The gods, led by Ea, create a plan to rescue her. Anu, as her father, creates two sexless beings (kurgarru and galaturu) who use the water of life to revive her.

In this text, a different succession myth is presented. Anu is overthrown by his son Kumarbi (the Hurrian grain god), who bites off Anu's genitals and becomes pregnant with the storm god Teshub. This is a separate tradition from the standard Mesopotamian theology.

Content Notes

Anu, while often aloof, is complicit in the destruction of humanity in the flood myth. He approves Enlil's plan to wipe out humanity, demonstrating that the supreme god can sanction genocide as a solution to a problem.

This reflects the Mesopotamian view of a fragile human existence subject to the whims of the gods.

Atra-Hasis

In the Theogony of Dunnu, Anu is violently overthrown by his son Kumarbi, who bites off Anu's genitals. This is a myth of violent succession, not a historical event, but it involves graphic bodily harm.

This myth is a variant of the 'succession myth' found in Greek mythology (Uranus and Cronus) and Hurrian mythology. It reflects political anxieties about the transfer of power.

Theogony of Dunnu

Anu grants Ishtar the Bull of Heaven, knowing it will cause seven years of famine and destruction. He is willing to unleash cosmic devastation to satisfy his daughter's wrath.

This shows that the gods are not benevolent protectors; they are forces of nature that can be dangerous and unpredictable.

Scholarly Debates
Primordial vs. Generated Being
Anu is a primordial being, born from the primordial sea (Nammu) and co-eternal with the earth (Ki). ⇌ Anu is a second-generation god, born from Anshar and Kishar, who are themselves born from the primordial pair Apsu and Tiamat.
Evidence for A: Sumerian creation myths (Eridu Genesis).
Evidence for B: Enuma Elish (Babylonian creation epic).
Active vs. Passive Ruler
Anu is an active and involved ruler who directly intervenes in the affairs of gods and humans. ⇌ Anu is a remote, passive figure who delegates all active power to Enlil and Ea.
Evidence for A: The Descent of Ishtar (he creates rescuers), Epic of Gilgamesh (he gives the Bull of Heaven).
Evidence for B: Sumerian creation myths (he is separated from earth by Enlil), Atra-Hasis (he approves but does not execute the flood).
Consort
Anu's consort is Ki (Earth). ⇌ Anu's consort is Antu, Urash, or Nammu.
Evidence for A: Sumerian creation myth.
Evidence for B: Various Akkadian texts and god lists.
Parentage of Ishtar/Inanna
Ishtar/Inanna is the daughter of Anu. ⇌ Ishtar/Inanna is the daughter of Sin (the moon god) and Ningal.
Evidence for A: Epic of Gilgamesh, Descent of Ishtar.
Evidence for B: Some Sumerian god lists and the tradition of Ur.
What We Don't Know

The research is based on widely available translations and scholarly summaries of the primary texts. A deeper dive into the original cuneiform tablets, specific temple records from Uruk, and the most recent archaeological findings (e.g., from the ongoing excavations at Uruk) would add further detail. The Hurrian syncretism is noted but not fully explored. The specific iconography of Anu in art (e.g., the horned crown) could be more thoroughly documented with visual examples.

A way to hold it in your mind

Anu is the CEO of the universe. He doesn't do the work, doesn't get his hands dirty, and rarely shows up to the office. But he signs the checks, owns the company, and can fire (or flood) anyone he wants. He is the ultimate authority figure — distant, powerful, and terrifying in his indifference. If Zeus is the dramatic, hands-on manager, Anu is the reclusive founder who never leaves the penthouse.
