Who It Is

In the night sky of old Dahomey, the moon was not a cold, distant stone—it was Gleti, a goddess whose face carried the soft light of the world and whose countless children glittered as stars around her. When an eclipse darkened her glow, the Fon said it was the shadow of her husband crossing her, a brief, intimate touch written across the heavens. Gleti’s story is thinly traced in surviving texts, yet in those few lines she holds an entire cosmos of relationships: moon and sun, mother and children, shadow and light.

Attested: Gleti’s behavior is inferred from cosmological explanations rather than narrative characterization. She is a stable, cyclical presence governing the night sky and the stars, with eclipses interpreted as moments of interaction with her husband. She is not portrayed as capricious or violent; her role is primarily cosmic and explanatory. Reconstructed: Modern sources emphasize her as gentle, nurturing, and associated with fertility, cycles, and transition, projecting a personality of patient, rhythmic guidance. Powers
Lunar governance

Gleti embodies and governs the moon in Fon/Dahomean cosmology. She is responsible for the lunar cycles and the presence of the moon in the night sky, serving as the personified force behind its phases and light.

Limit: Her role is limited to the lunar and astral domain; there is no clear evidence that she governs all aspects of weather, fate, or human affairs in the way that Mawu-Lisa or other major Vodun spirits do.

Motherhood of the stars

Gleti is described as the mother of all the stars, sometimes conceptualized as numerous minor astral deities or "star-children" who populate the night sky.

Limit: This motherhood is cosmological rather than narrative; specific names and stories of individual star-children are not preserved in accessible sources.

Eclipse interaction with her husband

In Dahomean mythology, an eclipse is explained as the shadow of Gleti’s husband crossing her face. This frames eclipses as moments of intimate celestial interaction between the moon goddess and her consort.

Limit: The myth provides an etiological explanation for eclipses but does not elaborate on ritual responses or detailed narrative episodes. The husband’s identity is not consistently named in primary sources.

Fertility and cyclical power (modern devotional interpretation)

Contemporary goddess guides and blogs attribute to Gleti powers over fertility, cycles of time, and transitions, emphasizing her role in human reproduction, growth, and spiritual transformation.

Limit: These powers are extrapolated from general lunar symbolism and are not clearly documented in early ethnographic accounts of Fon religion. They should be treated as adaptation-specific and modern rather than historically attested.

Creator or rain deity (disputed, syncretic)

One modern blog claims Gleti is one of three primary rain deities of Zaire, a creator goddess who gave life to humanity and passed duties to Bunzi after her mother’s death.

Limit: These claims conflict with established regional and cultural attributions (Zaire/Congo vs. Dahomey/Benin) and appear to conflate multiple African mythologies. No supporting evidence is found in standard academic references on Fon or Dahomean religion.

Appearance — No ethnographic or iconographic description of Gleti’s physical appearance survives in widely accessible academic literature. She is primarily conceptualized as the moon itself rather than as a detailed anthropomorphic figure. Secondary sources describe her symbolically as luminous, associated with silver or white colors and the phases of the moon, but these are modern interpretive correspondences rather than attested traditional depictions.

The stars are described as her children, so a sky filled with stars functions as an implicit symbol of her fecundity and astral motherhood. Accounts I
The eclipse and the shadow of Gleti’s husbandSummarized in Auset 2009; Coulter & Turner 2013; Wikipedia/DBpedia.

Recorded in modern reference works based on earlier oral traditions; precise dating of the oral myth is unknown.

In Dahomean cosmology, when an eclipse occurs, it is said that the shadow of the moon’s husband crosses the face of Gleti. The event is interpreted not as a catastrophe but as a moment of contact between the lunar goddess and her consort, explaining the temporary darkening of the moon or sun in relational terms rather than purely physical ones. The myth provides an etiological explanation for eclipses and reinforces the idea of relational balance between celestial bodies. It situates eclipses within a framework of cosmic intimacy rather than hostility.

Accounts II
Gleti as mother of the starsAuset 2009; Coulter & Turner 2013; Wikipedia/DBpedia.

Traditional, but only indirectly attested through modern compilations.

Gleti is described as the mother of all the stars. The stars are sometimes conceptualized as her numerous children, filling the night sky as a sign of her fecundity and creative power. The story is more a cosmological assertion than a detailed narrative, explaining the presence of countless stars as the offspring of the moon goddess. The motif reinforces her role as a generative force in the heavens and provides a familial structure to the astral realm.
