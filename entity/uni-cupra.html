Who It Is

Before Rome's gods were named Jupiter and Juno, the Etruscan city of Veii honored a queen of heaven whose name was spoken as Uni—a goddess of marriage, fertility, and sovereignty who would later be folded into the worship of Juno. Yet Uni was no mere copy of a Greek or Roman deity; she was a distinct and powerful figure in her own right, whose cult stretched across Etruria and even found a home in a sanctuary at Pyrgi, where she was identified with the Phoenician goddess Astarte.

As the supreme goddess of the Etruscan pantheon, Uni was likely a powerful and protective deity, overseeing marriage, fertility, and the welfare of the state. She was also a goddess of sovereignty, and her favor was sought by rulers, as seen in the Pyrgi dedication.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The etymology of 'Uni' is uncertain. Some scholars have suggested a connection with the Latin 'iun-', as in Juno, or with the Etruscan word for 'youth' or 'vital force', but no consensus exists.

Pronunciation — OO-nee

Aliases — Cupra, Juno, Astarte, Hera

Sovereignty

Uni was a goddess of royal power and state authority. The Pyrgi Tablets record a dedication by a ruler, suggesting that her favor was essential for legitimate rule.

Limit: None specifically attested.

Marriage and Fertility

She was a patroness of marriage and childbirth, similar to Hera and Juno. She likely protected women and oversaw the continuation of the family and the community.

Limit: None specifically attested.

Divine Favor and Protection

Uni could grant her protection to individuals or cities. The dedication at Pyrgi suggests that she was seen as a source of divine favor for the ruler Thefarie Velianas.

Limit: None specifically attested.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No direct textual description of Uni's appearance survives. Iconographic evidence from Etruscan mirrors suggests she was depicted as a mature woman, often wearing a crown or a diadem, and sometimes holding a staff or a patera (offering dish).

Symbols — Common symbols associated with Uni include the goat (especially in her cult at Veii), the peacock, and the patera. She is also depicted with a crown or a veil, emphasizing her status as a queen and a bride.

Domains — Uni was worshipped throughout Etruria, with major sanctuaries at Veii, Pyrgi, and possibly at other sites like Orvieto and Tarquinia. Her temples were often located on city heights or in prominent positions, reflecting her high status.

Accounts I
The Pyrgi DedicationPyrgi Tablets

c. 500 BCE

Thefarie Velianas, ruler of Caere, dedicated a sacred area and statues to Uni-Astarte in the third year of his rule. The inscription records this act of piety and suggests a close relationship between the Etruscan and Phoenician communities at Pyrgi. The dedication demonstrates the syncretic nature of Etruscan religion and the political importance of Uni's cult.

Accounts II
The Cult at VeiiRoman historians (e.g., Livy)

5th-4th century BCE

Uni, under the name Juno, had a famous temple at Veii. When Veii was captured by the Romans, the statue of Juno was ritually 'evoked' to Rome, where it was installed in a temple on the Aventine Hill. This story reflects the transfer of divine favor from the Etruscan city to Rome. The evocation of Juno from Veii was a symbol of Roman dominance and the absorption of Etruscan religious traditions.
