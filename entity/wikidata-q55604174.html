Who It Is

Imagine a goddess known only by a single sentence from a Greek historian—a divine name whispered across the steppe, now a ghost of a ghost. Argimpasa, the Scythian 'Aphrodite of the Heavens,' is a deity we know exists, but whose true face is lost to the wind. She is a name on a list, a footnote in history, yet she represents an entire world of belief that was deliberately erased by time and the silence of a non-literate culture.

As a goddess, her behavior is not described in any surviving narrative. Her domain, as inferred from Herodotus's identification with Aphrodite Urania, likely included aspects of love, sexuality, and possibly celestial or chthonic realms. The Scythian practice of the 'enarees' (androgynous soothsayers) is sometimes linked to Aphrodite Urania and thus possibly to Argimpasa, but this connection is not directly attested.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No physical description of Argimpasa survives in any source. She is not depicted in any known Scythian artifact that can be definitively identified with her, as the Scythians did not leave behind a systematic iconography of their deities that can be matched to the names recorded by Herodotus. Any depiction is speculative.

Symbols — No specific symbols are known for Argimpasa. The Scythians used animal-style art, but no specific animal or object is recorded as her attribute. The lack of a direct iconographic record makes any symbolic association conjectural.

Domains — Likely domains, based on the Greek parallel, include: love, sexual desire, fertility, the heavens, and possibly a chthonic aspect (as Aphrodite Urania was sometimes associated with the celestial realm, but also had connections to the underworld in some traditions). The Scythian context suggests she may have been a more encompassing mother or earth-sky goddess, but this is speculative.

Accounts I
Herodotus's Account of Scythian ReligionHerodotus, The Histories, Book 4

5th century BCE

Herodotus provides the only surviving written account of the Scythian pantheon. He lists the gods worshipped by the Scythians and gives their Scythian names. Argimpasa is identified as the Scythian equivalent of Aphrodite Urania. No further narrative is provided. The account is a Greek interpretation of Scythian religion, filtered through the lens of interpretatio graeca. This account is the primary source for all modern knowledge of Scythian religion. It has shaped all subsequent scholarship, but its reliability and the accuracy of the Greek identifications are debated.
