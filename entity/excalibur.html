Who It Is

A blade pulled from stone by a hidden king, later cast back into the depths of a lake by a dying monarch: Excalibur is more than a weapon—it is the symbol of rightful sovereignty itself, a legend that has been reforged in every retelling from medieval chronicles to modern cinema.

As an inanimate object, Excalibur does not have personality, but it is often attributed with magical properties, such as cutting through iron and being unbreakable. It is loyal to its rightful wielder and may be returned to the lake when Arthur dies.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Excalibur derives from the Welsh Caledfwlch, a compound of caled 'hard' and bwlch 'breach, cleft'. Geoffrey of Monmouth Latinised this as Caliburnus, possibly influenced by the Latin chalybs 'steel'. The name evolved through various spellings in French romance to the familiar Excalibur.

Pronunciation — /ɛkˈskælɪbər/

Aliases — Caliburn, Caliburnus, Caledfwlch (Welsh), Kaledvoulc'h (Breton), Calesvol (Middle Cornish), Escalibor

Ownership — Arthurian legend, shared across Welsh, Breton, Cornish, French, and English traditions; widely adopted in global popular culture.

Powers
Cutting through iron

The sword can slice through iron as if it were wood, as stated in Chrétien de Troyes and later works.

Limit: None specified

Rightful sovereignty

Only the true king can pull the sword from the stone, proving his lineage and divine right to rule.

Scabbard's protection

The scabbard, in some versions, prevents the wearer from losing blood, offering protection in battle.

Limit: The scabbard is stolen in some tales, leaving Arthur vulnerable.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In The Dream of Rhonabwy, the sword has a golden hilt with two chimeras, and when unsheathed, flames like fire issue from the chimeras' mouths. Other sources do not describe its appearance in detail, but it is often depicted as a magnificent, ornate weapon.

Symbols — Excalibur symbolizes rightful sovereignty, divine appointment, and the chivalric ideal. It is often associated with the Lady of the Lake, Avalon, and the magical nature of Arthur's reign.

Domains — Excalibur is associated with the British Isles, particularly with Avalon and the lake from which it is given. It is a royal weapon, kept in Arthur's court or carried by him in battle.

Accounts I
The Sword in the StoneRobert de Boron's Merlin, Vulgate Cycle, Malory

c. 1200–15th century

A sword is embedded in an anvil atop a stone in a churchyard. It is prophesied that whoever pulls it out is the true king of Britain. Many nobles fail, but Arthur, unaware of the contest, succeeds effortlessly. He later repeats the feat publicly, and Merlin reveals his parentage. Arthur becomes king, fulfilling the prophecy.

Accounts II
The Lady of the LakePost-Vulgate Cycle, Malory

13th–15th century

After Arthur's sword from the stone breaks in a duel with King Pellinore, Merlin leads him to a lake where a hand holding a sword rises from the water. The Lady of the Lake gives Arthur the sword Excalibur in exchange for a boon, which she later claims as the head of Balin. Arthur gains a new, magical sword; the boon leads to Balin's tragic fate.

The Return of ExcaliburVulgate Mort Artu, Post-Vulgate Mort Artu, Malory

13th–15th century

At the Battle of Camlann, Arthur is mortally wounded. He orders a knight (Griflet in Vulgate, Bedivere in later versions) to cast Excalibur into a nearby lake. After two deceptions, the knight complies, and a hand rises from the water to catch the sword. Morgan then takes Arthur to Avalon. Excalibur is returned to the lake, marking the end of Arthur's reign.

Excalibur is always the sword in the stone. — In many early and some later versions, the sword in the stone is not Excalibur; Excalibur is given by the Lady of the Lake after the stone sword breaks.

The sword was named Excalibur from the beginning. — The name evolved from Caledfwlch to Caliburnus to Excalibur over centuries.

Excalibur is a historical artifact. — There is no archaeological evidence for Excalibur; it is a legendary weapon.
