The Approach

In the moonlit meadows of Michael Drayton's fairy realm, a tiny knight rides forth on a grasshopper, his lance aimed at the heart of a queen's favor—and at the giant Pigwiggin, whose very name sounds like a jest but whose deeds are the stuff of chivalric parody.

Content advisory. None
Identity

Pigwiggin

Pronunciation PIG-wig-in

Etymology

The name 'Pigwiggin' likely derives from 'pig' and 'wiggin' (a variant of 'wig'), possibly suggesting a small, pig-like or comical appearance, or a nonsense name typical of fairy lore.

Cultural Ownership

English literature, specifically the fairy poetry of the Renaissance.

Disputed Identifications

None known. Pigwiggin is unique to Drayton's Nimphidia.

Description

Appearance

Pigwiggin is described as a fairy knight, tiny in stature, armed with a 'bee's sting' for a spear and a 'butterfly's wing' for a shield. He rides a grasshopper. His appearance is comical and diminutive, fitting the mock-heroic tone.

Symbols & Iconography

His armor and weapons are drawn from the natural world: a 'nut-shell' helmet, a 'spider's web' for a scarf, and a 'lady-bird' for a horse (in some readings). These symbols emphasize the miniature scale and whimsical nature of fairy chivalry.

Behaviour & Personality

Pigwiggin is a lover, a knight-errant who pursues Queen Mab with amorous intent. He is bold, amorous, and somewhat absurd, engaging in a duel with Oberon over Mab's affections. His behavior parodies the conventions of courtly love and chivalric romance.

Domains & Habitats

He inhabits the fairy court, which in Drayton's poem is set in a pastoral, English landscape, possibly near the River Ankor in Warwickshire, echoing Drayton's own 'Idea'.

Version Differences

Pigwiggin appears only in Nimphidia. No other versions of the character exist. Later adaptations of fairy lore (e.g., Shakespeare's A Midsummer Night's Dream) do not include him.

Powers & Abilities

Diminutive size and fairy nature

Source Version Nimphidia (1627)

Description

As a fairy, Pigwiggin is tiny and possesses magical abilities inherent to fairies, such as the ability to ride a grasshopper and wield a bee's sting as a weapon.

Limitations

His size makes him vulnerable to larger creatures and environmental hazards.

Contradictions

None.

Chivalric combat

Source Version Nimphidia (1627)

Description

He engages in a duel with Oberon, demonstrating skill with fairy weapons.

Limitations

The duel is more comedic than heroic, and he is ultimately defeated.

Contradictions

Stories & Myths

The Duel of Oberon and Pigwiggin

Source Nimphidia (1627)Culture English RenaissancePeriod 1627

Summary

Queen Mab, having quarreled with her husband Oberon, flees to the fairy court. Pigwiggin, smitten with Mab, pursues her. Oberon, jealous, challenges Pigwiggin to a duel. They fight with comical weapons (bee stings, butterfly wings) until Puck intervenes, and the two are reconciled.

Contradictions & Disputes

Pigwiggin's identity as a fairy knight

Position A

He is a parody of chivalric heroes.

Position A — evidence

His comical weapons and the mock-heroic tone of the poem.

Position B

He is a genuine fairy knight.

Position B — evidence

He is described with chivalric language and engages in a formal duel.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars view him as a parody, but the character also participates in the fairy tradition seriously.
