Who It Is

For three weeks in August 2021, the world believed the map of the far north had grown by thirty metres of gravel — a speck of Greenlandic soil 780 metres beyond any land previously recorded, only to learn a year later that the speck was not land at all, but the mud-capped crown of a grounded iceberg pretending to be an island.

Inanimate geological feature. Behavior is geophysical: grounding on shallow seabed, accumulation and retention of moraine veneer, vulnerability to storm erosion, drift or disintegration. Described by discoverers as 'short-lived islet' that 'could vanish as soon as a powerful new storm hits.' Ephemeral dynamics typical of north Greenland gravel banks, which appear and disappear on decadal scales with sea-ice and iceberg movement.

Powers
Moraine Mantle Camouflage

Ability to mimic a terrestrial island through a veneer of soil, pebbles, mud and glacial till deposited on flat iceberg surface, fooling field observers and initial GPS-based identification.

Limit: Veneer is thin, unconsolidated, and not lithified; does not constitute bedrock. Easily eroded or washed away.

Grounded Stability (Temporary Anchoring)

As a grounded iceberg, remains stationary by resting on shallow seabed, allowing it to persist in place long enough to be mapped and landed upon, unlike drifting icebergs.

Limit: Grounding is shallow and unstable; strong storms, sea-ice pressure, or basal melting can refloat or destroy it. No long-term permanence.

Ephemeral Topography

Maintains ~60x30 m emergent surface at ~3 m elevation, sufficient to be above sea level and meet cartographic island size thresholds for a brief period.

Limit: Dimensions based on single-visit estimate; no repeated survey confirms persistence. Likely already altered or submerged by 2022-2023.

Storm-Driven Formation

Hypothesized genesis during a violent storm that piled seabed mud and moraine onto a grounded iceberg or pushed iceberg onto shoal, creating the observed bank relatively recently.

Limit: Hypothetical; no direct observation of formation event. Alternative is slower glacial transport and grounding.

Accounts I
Oodaaq: The Predecessor and the GPS ErrorUffe Petersen et al. discovery 1978; Morten Rasch expedition 2021

1978-2021

Oodaaq (also Oodap Qeqertaa) was discovered in 1978 by a Danish survey team led by Uffe Petersen and for decades was considered the northernmost land candidate, a 15x8 m gravel bank at ~83°40'N. In July-August 2021, Morten Rasch and colleagues from University of Copenhagen set out to revisit Oodaaq to document its persistence. Due to a handheld GPS error, they navigated to a point 780 m northwest of Oodaaq and landed on an unrecorded mass, initially believing it was Oodaaq itself. Only after posting photos did comparison with helicopter GPS and DTU data reveal they had been on a different feature entirely. Revealed existence of Qeqertaq Avannarleq as a separate feature and highlighted navigational challenges in high Arctic where magnetic and GPS anomalies and featureless ice make precise positioning difficult. Prompted re-evaluation of all northernmost candidates.

The Three-Week Island: Announcement of the World's Northernmost IslandUniversity of Copenhagen press release 27 August 2021; interviews with Morten Rasch and René Forsberg

27 August 2021

University of Copenhagen announced discovery of what was thought to be the world's northernmost island, proposing the Greenlandic name Qeqertaq Avannarleq. Rasch stated: 'Together with DTU, we realized that my GPS had erred, leading us to believe that we were on Oodaaq. In fact, we had just discovered a new island further north, a discovery that ever so slightly expands the Kingdom.' René Forsberg, head of geodynamics at DTU Space, was quoted that it 'meets the criteria of an island' while cautioning 'these small islands come and go.' Dimensions 60x30 m, elevation 3 m, were widely reproduced. Global media coverage (BBC, Guardian, CNN, Smithsonian) declared a new northernmost point of land. Greenlandic name entered public use despite unofficial status. Expectations of territorial expansion and Guinness-type records were raised.

The Island Hunters: Hobbyists Force a Second LookReports of 'island hunters' (island-hunting enthusiast community) response to social media photos, August-September 2021

2021

When expedition photos were posted to social media, a loose international group of hobbyist adventurers known as island hunters - enthusiasts who systematically visit and verify extreme islands - flagged inconsistencies. They noted the feature's appearance and position relative to known satellite imagery and urged professional verification. Rasch contacted DTU expert René Forsberg, leading to joint analysis of helicopter GPS logs versus handheld GPS and satellite data, confirming the new position northwest of Oodaaq. Demonstrated role of citizen observers in Arctic geography; triggered the rigorous re-analysis that would later overturn the island classification. Also illustrates how social media dissemination accelerates scientific correction.

Accounts II
Unequivocally Not an Island: The 2022 ReclassificationDTU Space / National Space Institute announcement September 2022, via René Forsberg

September 2022

After a year of remote-sensing analysis, Forsberg announced that Qeqertaq Avannarleq 'unequivocally' is not an island but the top of a grounded iceberg covered by a layer of soil, pebbles and mud, likely deposited by glaciers. The conclusion rested on elevation models, lack of bedrock signature, and comparison with similar ephemeral banks. The feature was therefore removed from consideration as northernmost land. Danish media reported the reversal with headlines noting the island 'was never an island.' Qeqertaq Avannarleq lost candidate status for northernmost point; Kaffeklubben Island (1900) retained status as northernmost undisputed permanent land. Highlighted need for substrate verification for all gravel banks north of Greenland. No formal retraction of name, but name now refers to iceberg feature.

The Contest for 83° North: Kaffeklubben and the Ephemeral BanksHistorical and cartographic record 1900-2022

1900-present

Kaffeklubben Island, discovered by Robert Peary's expedition in 1900 and named by Lauge Koch in 1921, at 83°39'45"N, 700 m north of Cape Morris Jesup, is the northernmost undisputed permanent island. North of it, a succession of ephemeral candidates have been reported: Oodaaq (1978), ATOW1996, Stray Dog West, 83-42, and Qeqertaq Avannarleq (2021). All are small gravel/moraine banks on or near grounded ice, appearing and disappearing with storms and ice movement. Danish Geodata Agency and DTU Space maintain that only Kaffeklubben qualifies as permanent land; others are classified as short-lived or non-land. Qeqertaq Avannarleq's story reinforces the scientific consensus that the northernmost point of land is not a stable prize but a shifting category dependent on definition of 'island' (bedrock vs ice, permanence vs ephemerality).

Formation Theory: Storm-Born Moraine on IceResearcher commentary 2021-2022 (Rasch, Forsberg interviews)

2021-2022 (hypothesis)

One theory holds Qeqertaq Avannarleq formed relatively recently during a violent storm that either thrust seabed mud and moraine onto a grounded iceberg's surface or drove an already debris-covered iceberg onto a shallow shoal where it grounded. The unconsolidated, unsorted nature of the surface material (till) supports glacial transport rather than in-situ bedrock weathering. No direct observation exists; theory inferred from sediment character and ephemeral context. Alternative slower accumulation via successive glacial advances cannot be ruled out. If correct, implies feature age is years to decades, not millennia, and explains why it was not seen on earlier satellite passes. Underscores vulnerability to the next major storm.

Qeqertaq Avannarleq is the confirmed northernmost island in the world and permanently extends Denmark's territory. — It was a candidate for only about a year (Aug 2021-Sep 2022) and was then reclassified as a grounded iceberg, not land. It does not permanently extend territory, and Kaffeklubben Island remains the northernmost undisputed island. The name itself remains unofficial.

The island was discovered because scientists were looking for new land; the GPS error story is a minor footnote. — The GPS error is central: the expedition intended to visit Oodaaq and only realized they were on a different feature after comparing handheld GPS with helicopter GPS and DTU data, prompted by hobbyist island hunters on social media.

Qeqertaq Avannarleq is made of solid rock like Kaffeklubben Island. — Its surface is unconsolidated soil, pebbles, mud and moraine (glacial till and seabed sediment) over ice, with no bedrock observed. Kaffeklubben has bedrock; Qeqertaq Avannarleq does not.

The name Qeqertaq Avannarleq is the official approved Greenlandic place name. — The name was proposed by the discoverers as a descriptive, unofficial name. It has not been approved by Greenland's Place Names Committee (Nunat Aqqinik Aalajangiisartut) and remains unofficial per University of Copenhagen and DTU communications.
