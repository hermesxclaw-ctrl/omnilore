Who It Is

Ghostface is the masked identity assumed by the killers in the 'Scream' franchise, and — uniquely among major slasher icons — the identity is not a single supernatural entity or individual but a persona adopted by multiple different murderers across the series. This radical concept was Wes Craven's and screenwriter Kevin Williamson's self-aware deconstruction of the slasher genre: by making Ghostface a role anyone could play (driven by different motives — revenge, fame, peer pressure, fan obsession), the franchise critiqued the slasher formula while simultaneously executing it masterfully. The costume — a hooded black robe with jagged sleeves and a white rubber mask based on Edvard Munch's 'The Scream' painting, featuring an elongated, mournful face — was discovered almost by accident. Craven and producer Marianne Maddalena found the 'Ghost-Face' mask (later renamed for copyright) in a location-scouting house, and Craven's request to use it led to the film's iconic look. A crucial subversion is that Ghostface is more physically vulnerable than traditional slashers: the killers trip, stumble, get thrown through windows, and are frequently fought off by their victims — making them more realistic and heightening the tension in a different way from an invulnerable supernatural killer. Each Ghostface has a different motive and identity: in the first film, Billy Loomis and Stu Macher orchestrate the killings out of revenge and 'peer pressure' nihilism; in Scream 2, it's Mrs. Loomis and a film student seeking fame; in Scream 3, it's Sidney's half-brother Roman, a Hollywood director who masterminded the entire series' events; in Scream 4, it's Jill Roberts, Sidney's fame-obsessed cousin; and in the 2022 'requel' Scream and its 2023 sequel Scream VI, new killers emerge from toxic fandom of the 'Stab' film-within-a-film franchise. The voice — provided by Roger L. Jackson across all films — has become inseparable from the character: the taunting phone calls ('What's your favorite scary movie?') establish psychological terror before physical violence. Ghostface's rule-based games (horror movie trivia quizzes, 'the rules of surviving a horror movie') make the killings a meta-commentary on the genre itself, forcing victims to participate in their own horror movie before being murdered in it. This self-aware dimension, combined with the genuinely tense kill scenes and whodunit mystery structure, makes Ghostface the most intellectually engaged slasher archetype.

Cultural Significance

Deconstructed the slasher genre by making the monster's identity fluid rather than fixed. 'What's your favorite scary movie?' and the rules of horror became embedded in pop culture lexicon. Revitalized the slasher genre in the 1990s. The meta-horror approach influenced an entire generation of self-aware genre fiction.
