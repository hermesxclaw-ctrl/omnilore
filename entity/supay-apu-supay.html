Who It Is

In the high Andes, where the mountains themselves are alive with spirits, there rules a figure whose name has been twisted by conquest into a synonym for the devil—yet whose true nature is far older and stranger. He is Supay, the lord of the underworld, the keeper of the dead, the master of demons and earthquakes, a being of both fear and reverence, whose image was reshaped by Spanish colonizers into the Christian Satan, but whose roots lie deep in the Inca cosmos.

Supay is typically described as a fearsome ruler of the dead, who receives souls and presides over the underworld. He is not inherently evil in the Christian sense but is a powerful, sometimes capricious force of nature. In some accounts, he is a punisher of wrongdoers, while in others he is simply a guardian of the dead. Modern Andean beliefs sometimes portray him as a trickster or a being to be appeased.

Powers
Ruler of the Underworld

Supay governs Uku Pacha, the realm of the dead, and decides the fate of souls after death.

Limit: His power is confined to the underworld, though he can cause earthquakes on the surface.

Master of Demons and Malevolent Spirits

Supay commands legions of demons or lesser spirits (also called supay) that can harm the living.

Limit: These spirits are generally bound to the underworld or to places like mines and caves.

Cause of Earthquakes

Supay is said to shake the earth, causing earthquakes, when he moves or is angered.

Limit: No known way to prevent this, though rituals and offerings may appease him.

Control over Mineral Wealth

Supay is associated with mines and the treasures of the earth, and can grant or withhold mineral riches.

Limit: Those who seek wealth must make offerings to him; failure leads to accidents or death.

Shape-shifting or Illusion

In some modern legends, Supay can appear in various forms, including as a handsome man or an animal, to deceive people.

Limit: Not attested in early colonial sources.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No definitive pre-Columbian iconography of Supay has been identified. Colonial-era illustrations by Guaman Poma show a figure resembling the European Devil, with horns, tail, and dark skin. In modern Andean folk art, Supay is often depicted as a red or black demon with fangs and claws. Some scholars suggest that originally Supay may have been formless or represented by abstract symbols such as the 'chakana' (Andean cross) or as a jaguar or serpen

Symbols — Possible symbols include the underworld (Uku Pacha), caves, mines, and earthquakes. In syncretic traditions, Supay is associated with the devil mask used in festivals like the Diablada. The 'chakana' cross may represent the three worlds, with the underworld as Supay's domain.

Domains — Supay dwells in Uku Pacha, the underworld or inner world of Andean cosmology, which is also associated with the roots of plants, caves, and the realm of the dead. He is also linked to mines and mineral wealth, as these are extracted from the earth. Earthquakes are said to be caused by his movements.

Accounts I
The Origin of SupayNo single origin myth is recorded. Inca cosmology likely placed him as a primordial being of the underworld.

Pre-conquest

Supay existed as the lord of Uku Pacha from the beginning of time, alongside Viracocha the creator and other deities. He was not created but was an inherent part of the cosmos. His existence explained the realm of the dead and the dangers of earthquakes.

Supay and the Spanish ConquestColonial chronicles and modern scholarship

16th-17th centuries

Spanish missionaries equated Supay with the Christian Devil, using the term 'supay' to translate 'demon' in catechisms. This led to the demonization of Supay in official religion, while indigenous people continued to honor him in secret or syncretized him with Catholic saints. Supay became a symbol of resistance and of the devil in Andean Christianity. His image was merged with European devil iconography.

Accounts II
Supay in the Mines of PotosíColonial and modern accounts of mining in Potosí, Bolivia

Colonial era to present

Miners believe that Supay (or 'El Tío') rules the underground world and controls the mineral wealth. They make offerings of coca leaves, alcohol, and cigarettes to him to ensure safety and good fortune. If they fail, he may cause cave-ins or accidents. The figure of El Tío, a horned devil, is still venerated in Bolivian mines today, a direct continuation of Supay worship.

Supay in Modern FestivalsEthnographic studies of Andean festivals

20th-21st centuries

In the Diablada dance of Oruro, Bolivia, dancers wear elaborate devil masks representing Supay and other demons. The dance reenacts the struggle between good and evil, with the Archangel Michael defeating the devils. This syncretic festival blends Catholic and indigenous beliefs. The festival is a major cultural event and a UNESCO Masterpiece of Oral and Intangible Heritage.

Supay is the Inca equivalent of the Devil. — This is a colonial construction. The Inca had no concept of a single evil being opposing a good god. Supay was a lord of the underworld, not a tempter or enemy of humanity.

Supay is the god of death, similar to Hades or Anubis. — While he is associated with the dead, the Inca did not have a personified 'god of death' in the same way. Supay is more accurately the ruler of the underworld realm, not a psychopomp.

Supay is purely evil and malevolent. — In pre-conquest belief, Supay was a powerful but not purely evil being. He was a force of nature, to be respected and appeased, not worshipped as a devil.

The Diablada dance is purely a Christian celebration. — It is a syncretic festival that blends Catholic and indigenous elements. The devil figure (Supay) is not just the Christian Satan but also the Andean underworld lord.
