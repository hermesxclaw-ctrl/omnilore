Who They Are

Supay is the Inca god of death and the ruler of the underworld (Uku Pacha). He presides over the realm of the dead, where souls travel after death to await eventual transformation. In the syncretic Christian-Andean tradition, Supay was assimilated with the Devil, becoming a figure of fear and temptation in contemporary Andean folklore.

The Approach

In the high Andes, where the mountains themselves are alive with spirits, there rules a figure whose name has been twisted by conquest into a synonym for the devil—yet whose true nature is far older and stranger. He is Supay, the lord of the underworld, the keeper of the dead, the master of demons and earthquakes, a being of both fear and reverence, whose image was reshaped by Spanish colonizers into the Christian Satan, but whose roots lie deep in the Inca cosmos.

Content advisory. Content includes descriptions of death, the underworld, and demonic associations. It also discusses colonial-era religious syncretism and the demonization of indigenous deities. No graphic violence or explicit material.

Etymology

The etymology of 'Supay' is uncertain. Some scholars suggest it derives from the Quechua word 'supay' meaning 'demon' or 'spirit of the dead,' while others propose connections to the Aymara word 'supaya' or to a pre-Inca root. The term may have originally denoted any spirit or shadow, later narrowed to the lord of the underworld.

Cultural Ownership

Supay is a deity of the Inca Empire and pre-Inca Andean cultures, particularly associated with the Quechua and Aymara peoples. After the Spanish conquest, his figure was syncretized with the Christian Devil in many Andean communities.

Disputed Identifications

Some colonial sources identify Supay with the Christian Devil, but this is a product of missionary interpretation. Modern scholars debate whether Supay was originally a single deity or a class of spirits. Some Andean traditions today use 'Supay' to refer to any malevolent spirit, while others retain a more specific underworld lord.

Description

Appearance

No definitive pre-Columbian iconography of Supay has been identified. Colonial-era illustrations by Guaman Poma show a figure resembling the European Devil, with horns, tail, and dark skin. In modern Andean folk art, Supay is often depicted as a red or black demon with fangs and claws. Some scholars suggest that originally Supay may have been formless or represented by abstract symbols such as the 'chakana' (Andean cross) or as a jaguar or serpent.

Symbols & Iconography

Possible symbols include the underworld (Uku Pacha), caves, mines, and earthquakes. In syncretic traditions, Supay is associated with the devil mask used in festivals like the Diablada. The 'chakana' cross may represent the three worlds, with the underworld as Supay's domain.

Behaviour & Personality

Supay is typically described as a fearsome ruler of the dead, who receives souls and presides over the underworld. He is not inherently evil in the Christian sense but is a powerful, sometimes capricious force of nature. In some accounts, he is a punisher of wrongdoers, while in others he is simply a guardian of the dead. Modern Andean beliefs sometimes portray him as a trickster or a being to be appeased.

Domains & Habitats

Supay dwells in Uku Pacha, the underworld or inner world of Andean cosmology, which is also associated with the roots of plants, caves, and the realm of the dead. He is also linked to mines and mineral wealth, as these are extracted from the earth. Earthquakes are said to be caused by his movements.

Version Differences

Pre-conquest Inca beliefs likely viewed Supay as a neutral or ambivalent deity of the dead, not a moral evil. Colonial chroniclers, influenced by Christianity, equated him with the Devil, leading to a demonized version. In modern Andean Catholicism, 'supay' can mean the Christian Devil, but in some rural traditions, a distinct underworld lord remains, separate from Satan. The Diablada dance of Bolivia and Peru features a character called 'Supay' that blends both.

Ruler of the Underworld

Supay governs Uku Pacha, the realm of the dead, and decides the fate of souls after death.

His power is confined to the underworld, though he can cause earthquakes on the surface.

Master of Demons and Malevolent Spirits

Description

Supay commands legions of demons or lesser spirits (also called supay) that can harm the living.

Limitations

These spirits are generally bound to the underworld or to places like mines and caves.

Contradictions

The distinction between Supay and his demonic minions is often blurred.

Cause of Earthquakes

Source Version Attested in Garcilaso de la Vega and other chroniclers.

Description

Supay is said to shake the earth, causing earthquakes, when he moves or is angered.

Limitations

No known way to prevent this, though rituals and offerings may appease him.

Contradictions

Some accounts attribute earthquakes to other deities or to natural forces.

Control over Mineral Wealth

Source Version Post-conquest mining folklore, especially in Potosí.

Description

Supay is associated with mines and the treasures of the earth, and can grant or withhold mineral riches.

Limitations

Those who seek wealth must make offerings to him; failure leads to accidents or death.

Contradictions

This aspect is more prominent in colonial and modern traditions than in pre-conquest sources.

Shape-shifting or Illusion

Source Version Contemporary oral tradition in Andean communities.

Description

In some modern legends, Supay can appear in various forms, including as a handsome man or an animal, to deceive people.

Limitations

Not attested in early colonial sources.

Contradictions

This may be a later folkloric development influenced by European devil lore.

Supay and the Spanish Conquest

Source Colonial chronicles and modern scholarshipCulture Andean (colonial period)Period 16th-17th centuries

Summary

Spanish missionaries equated Supay with the Christian Devil, using the term 'supay' to translate 'demon' in catechisms. This led to the demonization of Supay in official religion, while indigenous people continued to honor him in secret or syncretized him with Catholic saints.

Participants

Consequences

Supay became a symbol of resistance and of the devil in Andean Christianity. His image was merged with European devil iconography.

Miners believe that Supay (or 'El Tío') rules the underground world and controls the mineral wealth. They make offerings of coca leaves, alcohol, and cigarettes to him to ensure safety and good fortune. If they fail, he may cause cave-ins or accidents.

Supay in Modern Festivals

Source Ethnographic studies of Andean festivalsCulture Bolivia, PeruPeriod 20th-21st centuries

Summary

In the Diablada dance of Oruro, Bolivia, dancers wear elaborate devil masks representing Supay and other demons. The dance reenacts the struggle between good and evil, with the Archangel Michael defeating the devils. This syncretic festival blends Catholic and indigenous beliefs.

Supay rules over the souls of the dead in Uku Pacha. The Inca believed that the dead resided in the underworld and that Supay was their lord.

Some sources say the dead go to Supay regardless, others say only those who lived bad lives.

Demonization by Spanish missionaries

Source Colonial chronicles and religious texts

Description

Spanish missionaries systematically equated Supay with the Christian Devil, using the term 'supay' to translate 'demon' in catechisms and sermons. This was part of the broader campaign to eradicate indigenous religions.

Scholarly Context

This was a deliberate strategy to facilitate conversion by aligning native deities with evil.

Suppression of indigenous worship

Source Colonial records of extirpación de idolatrías

Description

Worship of Supay and other deities was prohibited, and practitioners were punished. Many sacred sites were destroyed or repurposed.

Scholarly Context

The Spanish Inquisition in Peru actively sought out 'idolaters' and punished them.

Syncretic violence in festivals

Source Modern ethnographic studies

Description

The Diablada dance reenacts the defeat of the devils by Christian forces, symbolically reinforcing the subjugation of indigenous beliefs.

Scholarly Context

While the festival is now celebrated with pride, it originated as a tool of religious instruction and control.

Contradictions & Disputes

Nature of Supay: benevolent or malevolent?

Position A

Supay was a malevolent demon, equivalent to the Christian Devil.

Position A — evidence

Colonial chronicles and missionary texts consistently demonize him.

Position B

Supay was a neutral or even protective deity of the underworld, not evil.

Position B — evidence

Modern ethnographic research and reexamination of Inca cosmology suggest a more complex figure.

Scholarly Consensus

Most modern scholars reject the simplistic equation with the Devil and see Supay as an ambivalent underworld lord.

Supay as a single deity vs. a class of spirits

Position A

Supay is a specific named deity, the lord of the underworld.

Position A — evidence

Colonial sources refer to 'Supay' as a proper name.

Position B

'Supay' is a generic term for any spirit of the dead or malevolent entity.

Position B — evidence

Modern Quechua usage often treats 'supay' as a common noun.

Scholarly Consensus

The term likely had both meanings, with a supreme Supay over lesser supays.

Origin of the name 'Supay'

Position A

Derived from Quechua root meaning 'demon' or 'shadow.'

Position A — evidence

Quechua dictionaries from colonial era list 'supay' as 'devil.'

Position B

Borrowed from Aymara or another language.

Position B — evidence

Aymara has similar terms like 'supaya.'

Scholarly Consensus

Unresolved; likely a shared Andean root.

Supay's role in the afterlife

Position A

Supay punishes sinners in a hell-like underworld.

Position A — evidence

Some colonial accounts describe torment in the afterlife.

Position B

Supay simply receives all dead, without judgment.

Position B — evidence

Inca concepts of afterlife were not primarily punitive; the underworld was a place of rest.

Scholarly Consensus

The punitive aspect is likely a Christian influence.

Research Scope

This dossier synthesizes available scholarly and historical sources on Supay (Apu Supay) as known from Inca religion, colonial chronicles, and modern Andean traditions. Due to the scarcity of primary sources and the oral nature of Inca religion, much of the information is reconstructed from colonial-era accounts and ethnographic studies. The research covers identity, origins, attributes, stories, relationships, dark history, reception, contradictions, and misconceptions. Gaps are noted where verification was impossible.

Cultural Significance

Supay's transformation from underworld ruler to Christian Devil is one of the clearest examples of religious syncretism in the Andes. The Diablada (Dance of the Devils) of Oruro, Bolivia — a UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage — features elaborately costumed dancers representing Supay and his demons, now integrated into the Catholic festival of the Virgin of Socavón. In mining communities across the Andes, 'El Tío' (the Uncle) — a figure derived from Supay — is worshipped as the lord of the mines, with offerings of coca, cigarettes, and alcohol left at his underground shrines.
