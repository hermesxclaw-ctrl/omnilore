Who It Is

In the misty hills of Ganzhou, villagers still leave incense before a stone figure of an old man holding a bronze compass — not a god of thunder or wealth, but the deified geomancer who claimed he could read the veins of dragons sleeping under the earth. That is Yangong, the Lord Yang who turned the art of choosing graves into a religion.

Hagiographic personality (reconstructed from late sources, not contemporary attestation): erudite, austere, compassionate epithet Jiupin ('Save the Poor') because he reputedly refused payment from poor families and sited graves to benefit commoners rather than only elites. Said to be secretive, transmitting art only to selected disciples after tests of character. Wrathful toward greedy officials who misused geomancy. Name & Tongue

Etymology — Chinese 楊公 / 杨公 (Yáng Gōng). Yang (楊) is a common Han surname; Gong (公) is an honorific meaning 'Duke,' 'Lord,' or 'Venerable Elder.' Together 'Lord Yang' or 'Venerable Yang.' Jiupin (救貧, 'Save the Poor') is a later epithet. Junsong (筠松) is the personal name attested in late hagiography.

Pronunciation — Mandarin: Yánggōng (IPA [jɑŋ˧˥ kʊŋ˥]), Yáng Jùnsōng; Cantonese (Jyutping): Joeng4 Gung1; Hakka (Sixian): Yongˇ Gungˊ; Middle Chinese reconstruction: *Yang Kuwng (per Baxter-Sagart)

Aliases — Yang Junsong, Yang Jiupin, Yang Jiupin Xianshi, Yang Guoshi, Yang Xianshi, Yang Gong Xianshi

Powers
Reading Dragon Veins (Xunlong Dianxue)

Ability to perceive mountains as living dragons, trace their veins (longmai) and locate the precise lair (xue) where qi gathers, determining auspicious burial or dwelling sites.

Limit: Requires correct topography: true dragon must have embracing hills (sha), winding water, and bright hall (mingtang). Useless on flat or desecrated land. Later texts warn that misreading brings family extinction. Power is interpretive skill, not supernatural creation.

Averting Calamity and Bringing Fortune via Siting

Proper siting reputed to secure descendants' official success, wealth, and health; improper siting causes illness, childlessness, or ruin. As deity, petitioned to retroactively mitigate bad siting.

Limit: Efficacy is delayed (affects descendants, not client), conditional on descendants' morality, and contested by Confucian rationalists. No claim of immediate miracle in classical texts.

Authorship of Geomantic Classics and Compass Innovation

Traditionally credited with composing core Jiangxi school classics and refining the luopan compass with added rings.

Limit: Textual scholarship (Bennett 1978, Bruun 2003) shows classics are composite, post-Tang compilations; compass evolution was gradual, not single invention. Attribution is honorific.

Apotropaic and Hydrological Miracles (Folk Deification Layer)

In temple lore, causes springs to appear during drought, stops landslides, exposes false geomancers, and appears in dreams to guide disciples to correct sites.

Limit: Miracle tales are posthumous, localized, and formulaic; not claimed as powers during lifetime. Require sincere worship and moral conduct.

Transmission and Lineage Blessing

Power to authorize legitimate transmission: only disciples who receive his method (via teacher-student chain from Zeng and Liao) are considered orthodox; his blessing validates a geomancer's practice.

Limit: Blessing is social legitimacy, not supernatural empowerment; rival lineages claim competing transmissions (e.g., Fujian Min school).

Form & Signs

Appearance — Attested iconography (Ming-Qing temple statues, modern prints, luopan-maker votives): an elderly Han literatus-official with long white or grey beard, wearing Tang-style official hat (putou or jinxianguan) and long robe, often holding or standing beside a luopan (fengshui compass) or scroll. Sometimes shown with a staff, pointing at hills. No Tang-period portrait survives; all images are retrospective hagiographic creations. In some Hakka lineage

Symbols — Core symbols: 1) Luopan - circular geomantic compass with concentric rings marking directions, heavenly stems, earthly branches; emblem of his authority over siting. 2) Long (dragon) - undulating mountain ranges called 'dragon veins' (longmai) that he is said to read. 3) Scroll/book - attributed classics (Hanlong Jing). 4) Mountains/water (shanshui) - idealized landscape of protective hills and embracing water. Color association: earthy

Domains — Domains: fengshui (dilixue, kanyu), specifically xingshi (forms) school of Jiangxi (Jiangxi pai / Gan pai); siting of graves (yinyangzhai) and later yangzhai (dwellings); dragon veins, xue (lairs), sha (harmful influences), shui (water mouths). Habitat in lore: the granite hills and rivers of southern Jiangxi (Ganzhou, Yudu, Ningdu), where he wandered after fleeing Chang'an. Accounts I
The Court Geomancer of Emperor XizongLate hagiography synthesized in Ganzhou Fuzhi and prefaces to Hanlong Jing; no contemporary Tang dynastic history attestation

Claimed late 9th century (Tang, 873-888 CE); texts recording story Ming-Qing

Yang Junsong serves as imperial geomancer (or Jinzi Guanglu Daifu) under Emperor Xizong in Chang'an, in charge of imperial tombs and palace siting. He masters the secret books of Qingtang and the art of Kanyu, gaining reputation for finding auspicious sites that ensure dynastic longevity. Establishes his prestige as court expert, explaining later claim that he possessed imperial secret texts. Provides narrative justification for his flight with stolen books.

Flight from Huang Chao and the Theft of Secret BooksGanzhou Fuzhi, lineage genealogies, oral tradition summarized in Bruun 2003

Claimed 880-885 CE (Huang Chao Rebellion); recorded Ming-Qing

When rebel Huang Chao sacks Chang'an (880), the Tang court flees. Yangong, foreseeing dynastic collapse, seizes the imperial library's geomantic classics (or copies them) and escapes south, eventually reaching the mountainous Ganzhou region in Jiangxi, beyond rebel reach. He lives as a wandering master among the Hakka migrants. Explains how forbidden imperial knowledge entered popular circulation and why Jiangxi became the cradle of fengshui. Frames Yangong as transmitter who saved the poor by democratizing elite knowledge.

Teaching Zeng Wenshan and Liao Yu in the Ganzhou HillsZeng and Liao family genealogies; Hanlong Jing prefaces; ethnographic accounts in Feuchtwang 2001

Claimed late 9th - early 10th century; genealogies compiled Song-Ming

In Ganzhou, Yangong tests and then accepts two disciples: Zeng Wenshan (Zeng Wenchuan) and Liao Yu (Liao Jinjing). He teaches them to read dragon forms by walking the hills for years, instructing them to observe how water embraces and winds coil. He imparts the formulas of the three he (heaven, earth, man) and then disappears, leaving them as founders of the Jiangxi school. Creates the orthodox lineage: Yang-Zeng-Liao. All later Jiangxi geomancers claim descent from this transmission. Zeng and Liao tombs and temples become secondary cult sites.

Accounts II
Jiupin - Saving the Poor with Auspicious GravesFolk etymology and temple miracle tales; explanation of epithet Yang Jiupin

Folk tales collected Qing to present; epithet attested in Qing texts

Yangong refuses lucrative commissions from greedy landlords and instead seeks out poor but virtuous families, siting their ancestors' graves for free at hidden true lairs. Those families later produce scholars and officials, proving that virtue plus correct siting overcomes poverty. Hence he is called Yang Jiupin (Yang Who Saves the Poor). Moralizes geomancy as ethical practice; explains his popularity among commoners and Hakka migrants. Reinforces belief that fengshui rewards morality.

The Death and Apotheosis on Mount Yang XianTemple legend at Yang Xian Ling, Yudu County; local gazetteer

Claimed death c. 906 CE; apotheosis stories Ming-Qing to present

After years of wandering, Yangong dies (or transcends as xian) on a mountain in Yudu, Jiangxi, which is thereafter called Yang Xian Ling (Peak of Immortal Yang). His grave is said to be hidden or empty because he achieved immortality. Geomancers report his spirit appears in dreams to correct their compass readings, and pilgrims report incense spontaneously igniting. Site becomes pilgrimage center and cult focus; annual rites on his death anniversary. Establishes him as ling (efficacious) deity rather than mere historical teacher.

Exposing the False GeomancerQing anecdotal collections (biji) and contemporary oral tales recorded by ethnographers

Qing (1644-1912) to contemporary oral tradition

A charlatan claims Yang Gong transmission and extorts a family for an expensive but inauspicious site. The family's son prays at Yang Gong Temple; that night Yangong appears in a dream, reveals the true lair nearby, and causes the charlatan's compass needle to spin wildly at the false site. The family is saved and the charlatan flees. Reinforces orthodoxy and warns against commercial fraud; used by lineage masters to assert authenticity. Reflects historical anxieties about unregulated geomancers.

Birthplace and Death PlaceBorn in Douzhou (modern Guangdong) or Jiangxi; died and buried at Yang Xian Ling, Yudu, Ganzhou.Born in Shandong or Henan as northern court official; multiple competing grave sites in Fujian and Guangdong claim authenticity.

Yangong is the same as Erlang Shen (Yang Jian) or a martial god of war. — Distinct figure. Yang Jian/Erlang Shen is a martial, demon-quelling deity with a third eye and hound; Yangong is a deified geomancer with a compass. Confusion arises from shared surname Yang and honorific Gong.

Yangong invented the fengshui compass (luopan) from scratch. — The south-pointing spoon (sinan) predates Tang; the luopan evolved gradually from Han to Song with added rings. Later tradition credits Yang with refining or adding layers, not inventing the instrument.

Worshipping Yangong guarantees wealth and lottery success. — Traditional petitions concern safe burial, family continuity, and averting misfortune from bad siting, not immediate wealth. Wealth claims are modern commercial exaggeration.

Yang Gong Feng Shui is an ancient, unchanged Tang system practiced identically today. — What is taught today as 'Yang Gong' is a Ming-Qing synthesis with modern innovations; early Tang practice, if it existed, is not recoverable as a single fixed system.
