A'im's primary domains are fire, destruction (specifically arson and the burning of fortified places), warfare, and the granting of wit or intelligence. It also has a role as a revealer of hidden truths, particularly concerning private matters and the location of treasures. Its association with serpents and vipers also connects it to the chthonic and the underworld.

Form

A'im is consistently described as a Grand Duke (or Prince) of Hell who manifests with three distinct heads: the first is that of a serpent, the second is that of a man (often described as handsome or noble), and the third is that of a cat. The entity is said to ride upon a viper or a large serpent, which serves as its mount. In its hand, A'im carries a blazing firebrand or torch, which it uses to ignite cities, castles, and other large structures. The overall image is one of fiery destruction combined with a tripartite, hybridized form that symbolizes its dominion over different aspects of the natural and human world.

Powers

Pyrokinesis / Arson A'im is able to ignite cities, castles, and other large structures using its firebrand. This is its most prominent and destructive ability. Attested in all primary sources (Weyer, Goetia, Dictionnaire Infernal). The texts do not specify any limitations on this power, other than it being subject to the summoner's control during a ritual. None noted in the sources.

Truth-Saying / Divination A'im provides 'true answers' concerning private matters, warfare, and hidden treasures. Attested in Weyer and the Goetia. The texts do not specify any conditions under which its answers might be false or misleading. Some later occultists have suggested that demons are inherently deceptive, but this is not present in the primary source material.

Granting Wit / Intelligence A'im can 'make men witty in all manner of ways', suggesting it can enhance a person's intelligence, cunning, or eloquence. Attested in the Goetia. The exact nature and extent of this wit-granting ability is not detailed. None noted.

Command over Legions A'im rules over 26 legions of spirits (in the Goetia) or is a prince of the order of Powers (in Weyer). Attested in both primary sources. The nature of these legions is not described. The number of legions is consistent (26), but the rank (Prince vs. Duke) varies between sources.

Behavior & Habitat

A'im is described as a 'strong' and 'great' prince who is willing to serve a summoner, provided the proper rituals are followed. It is said to give 'true answers' concerning private matters, warfare, and the location of treasures. It is also noted for its ability to 'make men witty in all manner of ways', suggesting a role as a bestower of cunning, intelligence, or eloquence. Unlike some demons described as inherently malicious or deceptive, A'im is portrayed in the grimoires as a relatively straightforward and reliable source of information, though its methods of destruction (burning cities) are clearly destructive in nature.

Accounts

Johann Weyer, a Dutch physician and demonologist, compiled a comprehensive list of demons he believed were falsely worshipped as gods. He included A'im under the name 'Haborym', describing its appearance and powers. This work was intended as a refutation of witchcraft beliefs, arguing that these entities were not real gods but rather fallen angels or demons who could be controlled.

The anonymous compiler(s) of the 'Ars Goetia' incorporated Weyer's list, renaming 'Haborym' as 'Aim' (or A'im). The description was expanded slightly, adding the detail about making men witty and clarifying its rank as a Duke ruling 26 legions. This version became the standard reference for later ceremonial magicians.

Collin de Plancy, a French writer, included A'im (as 'Aim' or 'Haborym') in his encyclopedic dictionary of demons. This work popularized demonology for a wider audience and included an illustration (in the 1863 edition) of the demon, which has become a widely reproduced image. De Plancy's work is a synthesis of earlier grimoires, not an original source.

What We Don't Know

The primary gap is the lack of a definitive etymology for the name 'A'im'. The relationship between A'im and Haborym is well-understood but not universally accepted. There is also a lack of detailed scholarly work on the reception of A'im in modern media, particularly in video games. Further research could examine the specific iconographic choices in the Dictionnaire Infernal illustration and the entity's role in the broader context of the Goetia's hierarchy.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of A'im not as a character in a story, but as a piece of intellectual technology from the 16th century. It was a tool designed to explain misfortune (why did my city burn?) and to provide a sense of control over a chaotic world through ritual. It's a 'demon' in the same way a hurricane is a 'natural disaster' — a powerful, personified force of nature that early modern Europeans tried to bargain with. Its survival in modern video games and occultism is a testament to the enduring power of that idea: the desire to command the forces that can destroy us, and perhaps, to gain a little wit along the way.
