The Approach

Before the flood, before the Gaels, before even the first wave of invaders, a woman named Cessair sailed to the western edge of the world with fifty women and three men. Their story, told in the medieval Irish Lebor Gabála Érenn, is a strange blend of Biblical legend and native myth, a tale of survival, desire, and transformation that ends with only one man escaping the rising waters by becoming a salmon. Who was this 'sorrowful' leader, and what does her myth reveal about Ireland's ancient past?

Behaviour & Personality

In the Lebor Gabála, Cessair is depicted as a leader who follows divine instruction (either from Noah or an idol) and leads her people to Ireland. She is not characterized in depth.

Domains & Habitats

Ireland, specifically the western seaboard; her death is associated with Cúil Ceasra(ch) in Connacht.

Version Differences

The number of women varies: 50 in the standard version, 150 in Mallory's version. The details of the landing and the fate of the men also vary.

Stories & Myths

The Flight from the Flood

Source Lebor Gabála Érenn; Keating; Annals of the Four MastersCulture Medieval Irish ChristianPeriod Said to be before the Biblical flood (c. 2300 BC in Christian chronology)

Summary

Cessair, daughter of Bith (a son of Noah not on the Ark), is told by Noah to flee the coming flood, or in some versions, after being denied a place on the Ark, an idol advises her to sail to Ireland. She sets out with three ships and fifty women (or 150 in one version). Two ships are lost, leaving only Cessair, forty-nine other women, and three men: Fintan, Bith, and Ladra. They land at Dún na mBarc on Bantry Bay forty days before the flood. The women are divided among the men, but Bith and Ladra die, leaving Fintan with all the women. Overwhelmed, Fintan flees to a cave and survives the flood by shape-shifting into a salmon, then an eagle, then a hawk. He lives for 5,500 years and later recounts Irish history to the High King Diarmait mac Cerbaill.

Consequences

Cessair dies and is buried under a cairn; Fintan becomes a legendary survivor and historian.

Variants

In the version cited by J.P. Mallory, there are 150 women and three men; the first man dies, the second dies of exhaustion, and the third escapes by turning into a salmon. In the earlier Cín Dromma Snechtai, the first woman is Banba, not Cessair.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

The exact location of Carn Ceasra(ch) is unknown. The earlier Cín Dromma Snechtai text is lost, so the Banba version is only known through quotations. No detailed description of Cessair's appearance or personality survives. The relationship between the Cessair myth and the later Gallia legend is unclear. Further research could explore the iconography of Fintan as a salmon and its connection to the Salmon of Knowledge.

Research Scope

This dossier covers the figure of Cessair as presented in the Lebor Gabála Érenn, the Annals of the Four Masters, Geoffrey Keating's Foras Feasa ar Éirinn, and related medieval Irish sources. It includes analysis of her possible mythological origins, the variant traditions, and scholarly interpretations. It does not cover the later legend of Cessair, daughter of the King of Gallia, in detail.
