Who It Is

Before the flood, before the Gaels, before even the first wave of invaders, a woman named Cessair sailed to the western edge of the world with fifty women and three men. Their story, told in the medieval Irish Lebor Gabála Érenn, is a strange blend of Biblical legend and native myth, a tale of survival, desire, and transformation that ends with only one man escaping the rising waters by becoming a salmon. Who was this 'sorrowful' leader, and what does her myth reveal about Ireland's ancient past?

In the Lebor Gabála, Cessair is depicted as a leader who follows divine instruction (either from Noah or an idol) and leads her people to Ireland. She is not characterized in depth.

Accounts I
The Flight from the FloodLebor Gabála Érenn; Keating; Annals of the Four Masters

Said to be before the Biblical flood (c. 2300 BC in Christian chronology)

Cessair, daughter of Bith (a son of Noah not on the Ark), is told by Noah to flee the coming flood, or in some versions, after being denied a place on the Ark, an idol advises her to sail to Ireland. She sets out with three ships and fifty women (or 150 in one version). Two ships are lost, leaving only Cessair, forty-nine other women, and three men: Fintan, Bith, and Ladra. They land at Dún na mBarc on Bantry Bay forty days before the flood. The women are divided among the men, but Bith and Ladra die, leaving Fintan with all the women. Overwhelmed, Fintan flees to a cave and survives the flood by shape-shifting into a salmon, then an eagle, then a hawk. He lives for 5,500 years and later recounts Irish history to the High King Diarmait mac Cerbaill. Cessair dies and is buried under a cairn; Fintan becomes a legendary survivor and historian.

Accounts II
Cessair, Daughter of the King of GalliaKeating's Foras Feasa ar Éirinn

5th or 6th century BC (according to Keating's chronology)

A later legend identifies a different Cessair as the daughter of the King of Gallia who marries the High King Úgaine Mór. She becomes a queen of Ireland, but this is a separate tradition.
