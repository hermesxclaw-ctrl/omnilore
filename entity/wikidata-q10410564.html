The Approach

On a dark, drizzly evening in the Korean countryside, a traveler might glimpse a hunched figure hopping along the muddy path—a single leg propelling it with uncanny speed, a conical hat shadowing two glowing eyes. This is the dokkakgwi, the one-legged ghost of Korean folklore, a creature that is less a malevolent spirit and more a mischievous, unpredictable force of nature. It challenges strong men to wrestling matches, rewards the kind with magical gifts, and punishes the greedy with cruel irony. Its story weaves together ancient Chinese mountain spirits, Korean shamanistic beliefs, and a rich tradition of morality tales, offering a window into a world where the supernatural is never far from the everyday.

Content advisory. Some traditional tales include themes of deception, greed, and physical punishment. No graphic violence or sexual content is present in the source material.
Identity

Dokkakgwi

Pronunciation DOK-kahk-gwee

Known As

Dujiaogui (Chinese)Dok-kak-gwiOne-Legged GhostOne-Legged Demon

Etymology

The name 'dokkakgwi' (독각귀) is composed of the Korean words 'dok' (독) meaning 'one' or 'single', 'gak' (각) meaning 'leg', and 'gwi' (귀) meaning 'ghost' or 'spirit'. It literally translates to 'one-legged ghost' or 'one-legged demon'. The Chinese equivalent, 'Dujiaogui' (独脚鬼), has the same meaning.

Cultural Ownership

The dokkakgwi is primarily a creature of Korean folklore, but its origins are linked to Chinese mythology, specifically the one-legged mountain spirits like the Kui and Wutong Shen. It is a subtype of the broader Korean dokkaebi.

Disputed Identifications

There is scholarly debate about the exact relationship between the dokkakgwi and other one-legged beings in East Asian folklore, such as the Chinese Kui (夔) and the Japanese Sansei. Some scholars see a direct lineage, while others argue for independent development or later cross-cultural borrowing.

Description

Appearance

The dokkakgwi is typically described as a grotesque, hairy humanoid with a single powerful leg, allowing it to move with remarkable speed. It is often depicted wearing a satgat (conical bamboo hat) and a dorongi (straw raincoat), appearing on dark, drizzly days. Two glowing eyes peer from beneath the brim of its hat. In some variations, it has only one glaring eye and a fearsome expression.

Symbols & Iconography

The single leg is the most distinctive symbol. The satgat and dorongi associate it with rural, agricultural settings and rainy weather. The dokkaebi gamtu (invisibility cap) and bangmangi (magical club) are common symbols of its magical nature.

Behaviour & Personality

Dokkakgwi are mischievous, amoral, and unpredictable. They are not purely evil but are known for playing pranks, leading travelers astray, and hiding belongings. They famously challenge humans to wrestling matches (ssireum), which they almost always win due to immense strength. They can be generous to those who please them and punitive to those who annoy them.

Domains & Habitats

They are associated with mountains, rural villages, and wilderness areas. They appear on dark, drizzly days, and are often encountered at night or in secluded places.

Version Differences

Traditional depictions emphasize the single leg, satgat, and dorongi. Modern interpretations of dokkaebi, influenced by Japanese oni, often show two legs, horns, and a stocky build. The dokkakgwi specifically retains the one-legged trait, distinguishing it from other dokkaebi.

Stories & Myths

The Bump on the Chin

Source Korean folktaleCulture KoreanPeriod Traditional (oral tradition, collected in modern era)

Summary

A kind old man with a large bump on his chin stumbles upon a dokkaebi celebration. He joins in their singing, and the delighted dokkaebi remove his bump as a reward. A greedy neighbor with a similar bump tries to imitate him, but his unpleasant singing irritates the dokkaebi, who punish him by attaching the first man's bump to his own, leaving him with two.

Participants

Kind old man

Greedy neighbor

Dokkaebi (including dokkakgwi)

Consequences

The kind man is rewarded and healed; the greedy man is punished with an additional bump.

Variants

Different versions may have different rewards or punishments, but the theme of rewarding kindness and punishing greed is consistent.

The Man Who Defeated a Dokkaebi

Source Korean folktaleCulture KoreanPeriod Traditional

Summary

A man is persistently challenged to a wrestling match by a dokkaebi. Learning that the creature's weakness lies in its single leg (or left side), he manages to defeat it. Depending on the version, the dokkaebi either flees in humiliation, leaving behind its magical club, or befriends the man and uses its powers to bring him wealth.

Participants

Man

Dokkaebi (dokkakgwi)

Consequences

The man gains wealth or a magical club, and the dokkaebi is either defeated or becomes an ally.

Variants

The outcome varies: the dokkaebi may flee, befriend, or be killed. The weakness may be the leg or the left side.

Contradictions & Disputes

Origin of the one-legged trait

Position A

The dokkakgwi is directly descended from Chinese one-legged spirits like the Kui and Wutong Shen.

Position A — evidence

The Classic of Mountains and Seas describes one-legged spirits, and cultural exchange between China and Korea is well-documented.

Position B

The dokkakgwi developed independently in Korea, with the one-legged trait arising from local beliefs.

Position B — evidence

Korean dokkaebi have unique characteristics not found in Chinese spirits, suggesting indigenous development.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars lean toward a Chinese influence, but the exact nature and timing of the transmission is uncertain.

Appearance: one eye vs. two eyes

Position A

Dokkakgwi have two glowing eyes.

Position A — evidence

Classical descriptions mention 'two glowing eyes'.

Position B

Some variations describe a single glaring eye.

Position B — evidence

Some regional tales or artistic depictions show a single eye.

Scholarly Consensus

Both versions exist in folklore; there is no single canonical appearance.

Weakness: single leg vs. left side

Position A

The dokkakgwi's weakness is its single leg.

Position A — evidence

In 'The Man Who Defeated a Dokkaebi', the man learns the weakness is the single leg.

Position B

The weakness is its left side.

Position B — evidence

Some versions specify the left side as the vulnerable spot.

Scholarly Consensus

The weakness is generally associated with the leg, but the exact location varies by telling.
