The Approach

Before the word 'shark' ever sliced through the English language, mariners spoke of 'sea dogs'—creatures of tooth and fin that haunted the world's oceans. These ancient predators, whose ancestors swam the Devonian seas over 400 million years ago, have evolved into more than 500 species, from the palm-sized dwarf lanternshark to the colossal whale shark, a gentle giant that filters the sea for plankton. But sharks are more than mere survivors; they are apex architects of marine ecosystems, and their story is one of both awe and peril, as human hands now threaten to erase them from the waters they have ruled for eons.

Identity

Shark

Pronunciation /ʃɑːrk/

Etymology

The word 'shark' is of uncertain origin. The most likely etymology is from the Dutch 'schurk', meaning 'villain, scoundrel', which was applied to the fish due to its predatory behavior. Alternatively, it may derive from the Yucatec Maya word 'xoc' or 'xooc' (pronounced [ʃoːk]), meaning 'shark'.

Cultural Ownership

Global; sharks are found in all seas and have been part of human culture worldwide, from ancient mariners' tales to modern media.

Disputed Identifications

The term 'shark' is sometimes informally extended to include extinct shark-like chondrichthyans such as hybodonts, but this is not a formal taxonomic grouping. Additionally, there is debate over whether sharks form a monophyletic group; current molecular studies support monophyly, but some earlier morphological studies suggested polyphyly.

Description

Appearance

Sharks are cartilaginous fishes with a ribless endoskeleton, dermal denticles (placoid scales), five to seven gill slits on each side, and pectoral fins not fused to the head. They have numerous sets of replaceable teeth embedded in their gums. Sizes range from the dwarf lanternshark (17 cm) to the whale shark (up to 18.8 m).

Symbols & Iconography

In human culture, sharks are often depicted as fearsome predators, symbolized by their dorsal fin cutting through water. They appear in art, literature, and film as emblems of danger and power.

Behaviour & Personality

Sharks exhibit a range of behaviors: many are apex predators, while some are filter-feeders. They have complex sensory systems and are highly adapted to their environments. Some species migrate long distances, and many are solitary, though some form schools.

Domains & Habitats

Sharks inhabit all seas, from surface waters to depths of 2,000 meters. Most live in saltwater, but a few exceptions like the bull shark and river sharks can tolerate freshwater, and the Ganges shark lives exclusively in freshwater.

Version Differences

There are over 500 species across thirteen orders, each with distinct morphologies and ecological niches. For example, hammerhead sharks have unique head shapes, sawsharks have elongated toothed snouts, and angel sharks are flattened like rays.

Evolutionary History

Source Provided source materialCulture ScientificPeriod Devonian to present

Summary

Sharks first appeared in the Devonian Period (419–359 million years ago) as shark-like chondrichthyans. The earliest confirmed modern sharks (Selachii) date to the Early Jurassic (~200 million years ago). They diversified greatly during the Jurassic and largely replaced hybodonts.

Source Provided source material

Description

Shark finning is the practice of catching sharks, removing their fins, and discarding the body at sea. This is a major threat to shark populations.
