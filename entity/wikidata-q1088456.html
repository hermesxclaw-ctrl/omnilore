Who It Is

He was born from a beheading. When Perseus severed Medusa's head, her blood hit the air and did not simply spill — it delivered two sons: the white-winged Pegasus, and beside him a young man already gripping a sword of gold. His name was Chrysaor, and though his brother would carry heroes into the sky, Chrysaor would father monsters who guarded the edges of the world.

No personality, speeches, or deeds are attributed to Chrysaor himself in surviving archaic or classical sources. He is a genealogical bridge: born miraculously, then acts solely as husband to Callirhoe and father to Geryon (and in some accounts Echidna). He does not speak, fight, rule, or quest in extant narratives. Later mythographers treat him as a passive progenitor of monstrous lineages at the world's edge. Any ascription of temperament (noble, warlike, cruel) is modern invention. Status: unknown / unattested.

Powers
Golden Sword (Khruseon Aor)

Born holding a golden sword, from which his name derives. Hesiod states 'he held a golden blade in his hands' at birth. No further description of its forging, powers, or use in combat survives.

Limit: No attested feats performed with the sword; no evidence it is magical, unbreakable, or divine beyond its material (gold). Never described as used to kill or defend. Later authors do not mention it again.

Monstrous Progenitor / Generative Power

Capacity to father powerful, often multi-bodied or serpentine offspring with the Oceanid Callirhoe, producing Geryon (three-bodied giant) and, in some accounts, Echidna (half-nymph, half-serpent).

Limit: Not a personal combat power; his offspring are his mythic function. No evidence he controls or commands them after birth.

Divine Birth / Viability after Maternal Decapitation

Miraculous emergence alive and fully formed from the blood/neck of the beheaded Medusa, shared with Pegasus. Implies divine vitality inherited from Poseidon (immortal father) and Medusa (mortal Gorgon but of divine lineage).

Limit: One-time birth miracle, not a repeatable ability. Does not confer immortality — no source states Chrysaor is immortal or invulnerable; his death is never described, but he disappears from narrative after fathering children.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No extended physical description survives. Hesiod says only that Chrysaor was 'great' (megas) and held a golden sword (khruseon aor) in his hands at birth (Theogony 283). Pseudo-Apollodorus repeats the birth without adding appearance. Archaic art on the Corfu pediment shows him as a small, youthful anthropomorphic male standing beside his mother Medusa, mirroring Pegasus on the opposite side — not as a horse, giant, or monster. Symbols — Primary symbol is the golden sword / aor itself, which is etymological and likely not a separate magical artifact but an attribute explaining his name. He belongs to the generation of divine-monstrous beings inhabiting the liminal edges of the cosmos, not Olympus or the human polis. Accounts I
The Birth from MedusaHesiod, Theogony 280-288; Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.3.2; Ovid, Metamorphoses 4.791-802

Archaic (Hesiod c. 700 BCE) through Roman (Ovid c. 8 CE)

Poseidon lay with Medusa in a soft meadow among spring flowers (Hesiod) or in Athena's temple (Ovid's later version). When Perseus, aided by Athena and Hermes, beheaded the mortal Gorgon Medusa, two offspring conceived by Poseidon sprang from her neck as her blood poured out: the winged horse Pegasus, named for the springs (pegai) of Ocean, and Chrysaor, named for the golden sword he clutched. Perseus then fled with Medusa's head. Establishes Chrysaor's divine parentage and twin-like bond with Pegasus; explains his name; positions him as part of the Perseus cycle without further direct involvement. Pegasus goes on to serve Bellerophon and become a constellation; Chrysaor recedes to genealogical role.

Father of Geryon, Lord of ErytheiaHesiod, Theogony 979-983; Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.5.10; Diodorus Siculus, Bibliotheca historica 4.17.1-2; Stesichorus, Geryoneis (fragmentary, 6th c. BCE)

Archaic to Classical

Chrysaor married Callirhoe (Callirrhoe), daughter of the Titan Oceanus. Together they produced Geryon (Geryones), a giant with three bodies joined at the waist, who dwelt on the island Erytheia beyond Oceanus and herded magnificent red cattle. Geryon was later killed by Heracles as his tenth labour when Heracles crossed Oceanus, slew the herdsman Eurytion and two-headed dog Orthrus, and drove the cattle to Tiryns. Makes Chrysaor ancestor of one of Heracles' most famous adversaries; links his lineage to the far western edge of the world and to themes of cattle-raid and liminal monstrosity. Geryon's death is one of the canonical Labours.

Possible Father of Echidna, Mother of MonstersHesiod, Theogony 295-305 (ambiguous); Pausanias 8.18.2 (citing Hesiod); Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.1.2 (variant)

Archaic

In the passage immediately following the Geryon genealogy, Hesiod introduces Echidna, 'half fair-cheeked nymph, half monstrous snake,' who dwells in a cave. The manuscript tradition is ambiguous whether the lines 'Callirhoe bore... Echidna' continue the Chrysaor-Callirhoe union or begin a new genealogy. Many modern editors (West, Most) read Echidna as daughter of Chrysaor and Callirhoe, making her Geryon's sister; others read her as daughter of Phorcys and Ceto or Tartarus and Gaia per later authors. If accepted, Chrysaor becomes grandfather (through Echidna and Typhon) of most of Greek mythology's greatest monsters, vastly expanding his genealogical importance.

Accounts II
The Corfu Pediment: Chrysaor in StoneTemple of Artemis, Corfu, west pediment; archaeological reports (Rodenwaldt 1939, Archaeological Museum of Corfu)

Early Archaic, c. 580-570 BCE

The massive limestone pediment (c. 17m wide) centers on Medusa in Knielauf pose, flanked by her children: Pegasus to one side and Chrysaor to the other, both shown as diminutive figures beside the towering Gorgon, with panthers flanking the group. It is the earliest and most monumental depiction of Chrysaor, emphasizing his status as Medusa's immediate offspring and his pairing with Pegasus as a balanced dyad (horse/human, animal/anthropomorphic). Demonstrates that Chrysaor was visually recognized by early 6th century BCE and considered important enough for major temple decoration; confirms literary birth tradition was already canonical. No inscription labels him, identification rests on pairing with Pegasus and Medusa.

The Carian Ancestor: Chrysaor Son of GlaucusStephanus of Byzantium, Ethnica s.v. Mylasa; cf. scholia on Apollonius Rhodius

Late Antiquity (6th c. CE, drawing on earlier Hellenistic sources)

In a wholly separate genealogy, Chrysaor is son of Glaucus (son of Sisyphus, king of Corinth) and grandson of Sisyphus. His son Mylasus founds and gives his name to Mylasa, chief city of Caria (in modern Turkey). This Chrysaor has no connection to Medusa, Poseidon, or Pegasus, and functions as a foundation hero linking Carian cities to Corinthian heroic ancestry. Provides Mylasa with a prestigious Greek pedigree; explains the name Mylasa. Creates a doublet: both Chrysaor and Bellerophon are grandsons of Sisyphus via Glaucus, both linked to Pegasus in different ways (Bellerophon tames Pegasus). Scholars view this as mythographic invention or homonymy.

Heracles' Tenth Labour and the Erasure of Chrysaor's LineHesiod, Theogony 979-983; Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.5.10; Diodorus Siculus 4.17-18; Stesichorus, Geryoneis fragments

Archaic to Hellenistic

Though Chrysaor himself does not appear in the action, his lineage is the target of Heracles' tenth labour. Heracles, ordered by Eurystheus to fetch Geryon's cattle, journeys to Erytheia, kills Orthrus and Eurytion, then kills three-bodied Geryon despite his formidable strength, and drives the cattle across Oceanus back to Greece. The narrative is the only extended story in which Chrysaor's descendants act, and it ends in their annihilation. Ends the direct male line of Chrysaor-Callirhoe in the west; cattle become part of Heracles' pan-Mediterranean journey; Erytheia becomes a byword for the far west. Chrysaor is remembered only as 'father of Geryon' thereafter.

Chrysaor was a winged horse like Pegasus, or a giant warrior who fought heroes. — Chrysaor was a human-appearing young man whose only attribute was a golden sword; he has no attested fights, wings, or giant size. Pegasus alone is the winged horse.

Chrysaor ruled as king of Iberia or guarded Geryon's cattle himself. — Chrysaor never appears as ruler or herdsman; his son Geryon ruled/owned the cattle on Erytheia. Chrysaor's role ends at paternity.

Medusa was always a monster and Chrysaor's conception was consensual or irrelevant. — In Hesiod she is a mortal Gorgon from birth; Ovid's later version makes her a maiden raped by Poseidon and punished by Athena. The rape element is Ovidian, not Hesiodic.

Chrysaor is a major god with cult worship and temples. — No cult, temple, festival, or votive inscription for Chrysaor is attested. He is a minor genealogical figure.
