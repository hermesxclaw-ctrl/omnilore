The Approach

In the shadowed highlands of Arcadia, where gods once walked disguised among mortals, there lived a prince whose name survives only as a footnote to a feast of horrors. Polichus, son of the impious Lycaon, was one of fifty brothers whose collective crime was so vile that it drew the thunderbolt of Zeus himself—and erased their names from most of history.

Content advisory. Contains references to child sacrifice, cannibalism (implied), divine punishment, and mass death.
Identity

Polichus

Pronunciation PO-li-kus (English approximation); in Ancient Greek: /pó.li.kʰon/.

Etymology

The name Polichus (Greek: Πόλιχον) is likely derived from the Greek word 'polis' (city) with a diminutive suffix, possibly meaning 'little city' or 'of the city'. However, this is speculative; ancient sources do not provide an etymology.

Cultural Ownership

Ancient Greek mythology, specifically Arcadian regional tradition. The myth of Lycaon and his sons is part of the broader Greek mythological corpus, with roots in Arcadia.

Disputed Identifications

Polichus is not clearly identified in other sources. Some scholars have suggested that the names of Lycaon's sons may have been invented or compiled from various local traditions, and there is no independent attestation of Polichus outside the list in Apollodorus.

Description

Appearance

No physical description of Polichus survives. As an Arcadian prince, he would have been depicted as a young man of noble bearing, but no ancient source provides details.

Symbols & Iconography

None specific to Polichus. In artistic depictions of the Lycaon myth, the sons are often shown as a group, but Polichus is not individually identified.

Behaviour & Personality

Apollodorus describes Lycaon's sons as 'the most nefarious and carefree of all people' (πονηρότατοι καὶ ἀφροντιστότατοι). This suggests they were wicked, reckless, and impious, but individual personalities are not recorded.

Domains & Habitats

Arcadia, a mountainous region in the central Peloponnese. The sons of Lycaon were associated with the city of Lycosura, which was said to be the oldest city in the world, founded by Lycaon.

Version Differences

Different sources give varying lists of Lycaon's sons. Apollodorus provides a list of fifty names, including Polichus, but other authors such as Pausanias give different names or omit some. The number of sons also varies; some traditions say there were fifty, others twenty-two or fewer.

Powers & Abilities

None

Source Version All sources

Description

Polichus is not attributed any special powers or abilities. He is a mortal prince, notable only for his impiety and his role in the crime against Zeus.

Limitations

As a mortal, he is subject to death, as demonstrated by his being killed by Zeus's lightning bolt.

Contradictions

None, as no powers are attributed.

Stories & Myths

The Crime of Lycaon and His Sons

Source Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.8.1Culture Ancient Greek (Arcadia)Period Mythological age, before the flood of Deucalion (roughly the Bronze Age in Greek myth)

Summary

Lycaon, king of Arcadia, had fifty sons by various wives or nymphs. They were the most wicked and carefree of men. To test Zeus, they mixed the entrails of a child into a meal served to the god, who had come disguised as a peasant. Zeus, enraged, overturned the table and struck Lycaon and all his sons with lightning bolts, killing them. Only the youngest son, Nyctimus, was spared (according to some versions), or the flood that Zeus sent to punish humanity was a consequence of this crime.

Participants

Zeus

Lycaon

Polichus

49 other sons (e.g., Minaeus, Oenotrus, etc.)

A child (unnamed, possibly Nyctimus or another)

Consequences

The deaths of Lycaon and his sons. In some versions, Zeus then sent the Great Flood to wipe out humanity, sparing only Deucalion and Pyrrha. The site of the crime was said to be on Mount Lycaeum, where a sanctuary of Zeus Lycaeus was later established.

Variants

Pausanias (8.2) gives a different account: Lycaon sacrificed a child on the altar of Zeus Lycaeus, and was turned into a wolf. He does not mention the sons being killed. Other versions say that only Lycaon was punished, and the sons were spared or had varying fates. Some lists of sons are longer or shorter, and some names differ.

Relationships

Description

Polichus was one of the fifty sons of Lycaon, king of Arcadia. Lycaon was known for his impiety and was punished by Zeus.

Version Note

In Apollodorus, Lycaon is the father, but the mother(s) are not specified for Polichus. Some sources say Lycaon's wife was the naiad Cyllene, Nonacris, or another woman.

Conflicting Accounts

Pausanias does not mention the sons in his account of Lycaon's punishment, so the relationship is less emphasized.

Child sacrifice and cannibalism

Source Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.8.1

Description

Lycaon and his sons mixed the entrails of a child into a meal offered to Zeus. This is an act of extreme impiety and violence, involving the murder and dismemberment of a child.

Scholarly Context

Scholars interpret this myth as an aetiological tale explaining the origins of the cult of Zeus Lycaeus at Mount Lycaeum, where human sacrifice may have been practiced in historical times. The story also serves as a warning against impiety and the testing of the gods.

Contradictions & Disputes

Punishment of Lycaon's sons

Position A

All fifty sons were killed by Zeus's lightning bolt (Apollodorus).

Position A — evidence

Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.8.1.

Position B

Only Lycaon was punished; the sons' fates are not mentioned (Pausanias).

Position B — evidence

Pausanias, Description of Greece 8.2.1-3.

Scholarly Consensus

Scholars generally accept that there were multiple traditions. The version with the sons' deaths may be a later elaboration to emphasize the extent of Lycaon's impiety.

Number of sons

Position A

There were fifty sons (Apollodorus).

Position A — evidence

Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.8.1.

Position B

There were twenty-two or fewer (other sources, e.g., some scholia).

Position B — evidence

Various scholia and lexicographers.

Scholarly Consensus

The number fifty is likely symbolic (fifty months of an Olympiad?) or derived from the number of districts in Arcadia. The exact count is not historically reliable.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

No primary sources outside Apollodorus explicitly name Polichus. The etymology is uncertain. No artistic depictions or archaeological evidence specifically for Polichus. The full list of sons varies; further research into scholia and local Arcadian traditions might yield additional references, but they are likely lost. The exact mother of Polichus is unknown.

Research Scope

This dossier covers the mythological figure Polichus as attested in ancient Greek sources, primarily the Bibliotheca (Pseudo-Apollodorus). It examines his identity, the Lycaon myth cycle, his role among the fifty sons, the crime and punishment narrative, and the limited reception in later literature. The research is based on the provided source material and standard reference works.
