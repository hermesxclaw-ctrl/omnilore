Who It Is

In the shadowed highlands of Arcadia, where gods once walked disguised among mortals, there lived a prince whose name survives only as a footnote to a feast of horrors. Polichus, son of the impious Lycaon, was one of fifty brothers whose collective crime was so vile that it drew the thunderbolt of Zeus himself—and erased their names from most of history.

Apollodorus describes Lycaon's sons as 'the most nefarious and carefree of all people' (πονηρότατοι καὶ ἀφροντιστότατοι). This suggests they were wicked, reckless, and impious, but individual personalities are not recorded.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Polichus (Greek: Πόλιχον) is likely derived from the Greek word 'polis' (city) with a diminutive suffix, possibly meaning 'little city' or 'of the city'. However, this is speculative; ancient sources do not provide an etymology.

Pronunciation — PO-li-kus (English approximation); in Ancient Greek: /pó.li.kʰon/.

Aliases — Polichus

Ownership — Ancient Greek mythology, specifically Arcadian regional tradition. The myth of Lycaon and his sons is part of the broader Greek mythological corpus, with roots in Arcadia.

Powers

Polichus is not attributed any special powers or abilities. He is a mortal prince, notable only for his impiety and his role in the crime against Zeus.

Limit: As a mortal, he is subject to death, as demonstrated by his being killed by Zeus's lightning bolt.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No physical description of Polichus survives. As an Arcadian prince, he would have been depicted as a young man of noble bearing, but no ancient source provides details.

Symbols — None specific to Polichus. In artistic depictions of the Lycaon myth, the sons are often shown as a group, but Polichus is not individually identified.

Domains — Arcadia, a mountainous region in the central Peloponnese. The sons of Lycaon were associated with the city of Lycosura, which was said to be the oldest city in the world, founded by Lycaon.

Accounts I
The Crime of Lycaon and His SonsApollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.8.1

Mythological age, before the flood of Deucalion (roughly the Bronze Age in Greek myth)

Lycaon, king of Arcadia, had fifty sons by various wives or nymphs. They were the most wicked and carefree of men. To test Zeus, they mixed the entrails of a child into a meal served to the god, who had come disguised as a peasant. Zeus, enraged, overturned the table and struck Lycaon and all his sons with lightning bolts, killing them. Only the youngest son, Nyctimus, was spared (according to some versions), or the flood that Zeus sent to punish humanity was a consequence of this crime. The deaths of Lycaon and his sons. In some versions, Zeus then sent the Great Flood to wipe out humanity, sparing only Deucalion and Pyrrha. The site of the crime was said to be on Mount Lycaeum, where a sanctuary of Zeus Lycaeus was later established.
