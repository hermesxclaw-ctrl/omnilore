The Approach

In the sprawling family trees of Greek myth, one name appears again and again—Clymenus—worn by kings, heroes, and even the god of the dead himself. But behind this single name lies a web of distinct lives, each with its own story, from a father's incestuous tragedy to a king's dying curse that sparked a war.

Content advisory. Contains themes of incest, rape, cannibalism, suicide, and violence.
Identity

Clymenus

Pronunciation /ˈklaɪmənəs/ (KLY-mə-nəs)

Known As

PericlymenusKlumenosClymenos

Etymology

From Ancient Greek Κλύμενος (Klúmenos), meaning 'famous' or 'infamous', derived from κλέος (kléos) 'fame' and μένος (ménos) 'might, force'.

Cultural Ownership

Ancient Greek mythology

Disputed Identifications

The name is applied to multiple distinct mythological figures, leading to potential confusion. Some sources conflate the various Clymeni, especially the son of Helios and the king of Orchomenus. The identification of Clymenus as a title of Hades is attested but its relationship to the other figures is unclear.

Description

Appearance

No specific physical description is given for any of the figures named Clymenus in ancient sources. As a mortal king, he would be depicted in regal attire; as a son of Helios, he might have a radiant appearance, but this is not attested.

Symbols & Iconography

No distinctive symbols or iconographic attributes are associated with the name Clymenus. In art, he might be shown in scenes of his myths (e.g., the incest with Harpalyce), but no specific vase paintings or sculptures are identified.

Behaviour & Personality

The various Clymeni exhibit different traits: the son of Phoroneus is a religious founder; the king of Orchomenus is vengeful; the Arcadian king is incestuous and cruel; the suitor is a typical suitor. No unified personality can be reconstructed.

Domains & Habitats

Clymenus is associated with several Greek regions: Boeotia (Orchomenus, Onchestus), Arcadia, Elis (Olympia, Phrixa), Calydon, Dulichium, and Pylos (as father of Eurydice). As a title of Hades, he is a chthonic deity.

Version Differences

The most significant discrepancy is between the genealogies: some sources make Clymenus the son of Helios and father of Phaethon, while others make him the son of Phoroneus, or a king of Orchomenus, or a Calydonian prince. These are distinct individuals sharing a name, not variants of one figure.

Incest and cannibalism (Clymenus of Arcadia)

Source Parthenius, Love Romances 13; Antoninus Liberalis, Metamorphoses 13

Description

Clymenus, king of Arcadia, committed incest with his daughter Harpalyce, resulting in a son. He later killed the son and served him as food to Harpalyce, who then killed the child and was transformed into a bird. Clymenus committed suicide.

Scholarly Context

This myth is part of a group of stories about incest and cannibalism in Greek mythology, often used to explain the origins of certain birds or to illustrate the consequences of extreme impiety. Modern scholars interpret it as a cautionary tale about violating fundamental social taboos.

Rape of Nyctimene

Source Hyginus, Fabulae 204

Description

In one version, Clymenus raped his daughter Nyctimene, who was then transformed into an owl by Athena.

Scholarly Context

This is a variant of the myth of Nyctimene, typically attributed to Epopeus. The transformation into an owl is a common motif for victims of sexual violence in Greek myth.

Contradictions & Disputes

Parentage of Phaethon

Position A

Helios is the father of Phaethon by Clymene.

Position A — evidence

Most sources, including Ovid and Apollodorus.

Position B

Clymenus is the father of Phaethon by Merope.

Position B — evidence

Hyginus, Fabulae 154.

Scholarly Consensus

Hyginus's version is considered a mistake or a variant; the standard genealogy is Helios and Clymene.

Father of Nyctimene

Position A

Epopeus is the father of Nyctimene.

Position A — evidence

Ovid, Hyginus (in other versions).

Position B

Clymenus is the father of Nyctimene.

Position B — evidence

Hyginus, Fabulae 204.

Scholarly Consensus

The myth is more commonly associated with Epopeus; Clymenus is a variant.

Clymenus as a single figure or multiple

Position A

The name refers to multiple distinct mythological figures.

Position A — evidence

Different genealogies and stories are attributed to different Clymeni.

Position B

Some sources may conflate them.

Position B — evidence

The name is common, and ancient sources do not always distinguish clearly.

Scholarly Consensus

Modern scholars treat them as separate figures, though confusion exists in ancient texts.
