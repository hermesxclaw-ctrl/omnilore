The Approach

In the moonlit sky of Japanese myth, a god watches from afar, separated from his sister by an act of disgust that split day from night forever. Tsukuyomi, the moon reader, remains one of Shinto's most enigmatic figures.

Content advisory. Mythological violence.

Pronunciation [t͡sɨ̥.kɯ̈́.jó̞.mʲí] or [t͡sɨ̥.k̟ʲí.jó̞.mʲí]

Known As

Etymology

The name 'Tsukuyomi' is a compound of Old Japanese 'tsuku' (moon, month) and 'yomi' (reading, counting). An alternative interpretation combines 'tsukiyo' (moonlit night) and 'mi' (looking, watching). The Nihon Shoki also spells it 'Tsukuyumi' (moon bow), possibly a variant pronunciation. The honorific '-no-Mikoto' is akin to 'the Great'.

Cultural Ownership

Japanese mythology and Shinto religion

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars argue Tsukuyomi and Susanoo may be the same deity due to overlapping stories. Also, some sources conflate Ukemochi with Ōgetsuhime.

Description

Appearance

Not described in detail in ancient texts. He is a moon deity, often depicted in art as a male figure, sometimes with a bow or mirror. No canonical physical description exists.

Symbols & Iconography

Moon, night, bow (from 'Tsukuyumi'), mirror (white bronze mirror in some accounts). In art, he may be shown with a crescent moon or as a hunter.

Behaviour & Personality

In mythology, he is calm and quiet, but also capable of disgust and violence, as shown by his killing of Ukemochi. He is often portrayed as a solitary figure, separated from Amaterasu.

Domains & Habitats

Moon, night, and in some accounts the sea ('eightfold tides of the sea plain') or 'Yoru no Oshikuni' (Realm of Night Food). In the Nihon Shoki, he is to assist the sun and manage heavenly affairs.

Version Differences

In the Kojiki, he is born from Izanagi's right eye and rules the night. In the Nihon Shoki, he is born from a mirror or as a child of Izanagi and Izanami, and is assigned to rule the sea or assist the sun. His role is more prominent in the Nihon Shoki, where he kills Ukemochi.

Stories & Myths

Birth from Izanagi's right eye

Source Kojiki; Nihon Shoki (variant 6)Culture JapanesePeriod Mythic age

Summary

After Izanagi escapes from Yomi and performs purification, he washes his right eye, and Tsukuyomi is born. He is one of the three noble children, with Amaterasu from the left eye and Susanoo from the nose.

Amaterasu

Variants

The Killing of Ukemochi

Source Nihon Shoki (variant 11)Culture JapanesePeriod Mythic age

Summary

Amaterasu sends Tsukuyomi to represent her at a feast given by Ukemochi. Ukemochi produces food by spitting out fish, game, and rice. Disgusted, Tsukuyomi kills her.

Participants

Tsukuyomi

Ukemochi

Amaterasu

Consequences

Amaterasu is so angry she refuses to look at Tsukuyomi again, separating day and night forever.

Variants

In the Kojiki, Susanoo kills Ōgetsuhime similarly.

Killing of Ukemochi

Source Nihon Shoki, Volume 1, variant 11

Description

Tsukuyomi kills Ukemochi in disgust at her method of producing food. This act is seen as the reason for the separation of day and night.

Scholarly Context

Some scholars interpret this as a myth explaining the origin of food and the separation of celestial bodies, or as a reflection of ritual practices.

Primary Evidence

Kojiki (古事記)

Type textDate 712 CELocator Book 1, section on the birth of the three noble childrenLanguage Classical Japanese

Translation

Tsukuyomi born from Izanagi's right eye during purification after escaping Yomi.

Nihon Shoki (日本書紀)

Type textDate 720 CELocator Volume 1, variants 1, 6, and 11Language Classical Chinese and Japanese

Multiple accounts: born from a white bronze mirror in Izanagi's right hand, or from his right eye; also as son of Izanagi and Izanami. In variant 11, he kills Ukemochi.
