Who They Are

Tsukuyomi is the Shinto god of the moon, born from the right eye of the primordial creator Izanagi as he washed away the pollution of the underworld. He is one of the three noble children alongside his sister Amaterasu, the sun goddess, and his brother Susanoo, the storm god. Unlike the moon deities of most world mythologies, Tsukuyomi is male, austere, and defined less by what he does than by a single catastrophic act of violence: at a banquet, the food goddess Uke Mochi produced a feast from her own mouth and body, and Tsukuyomi, disgusted by what he saw as defilement, killed her on the spot. Amaterasu was so horrified by her brother's act that she refused ever to look at him again — which is why, the myth explains, the sun and moon occupy the sky at different times, eternally estranged. He remains one of Shinto's most enigmatic major deities, worshipped at shrines including Tsukuyomi-no-miya at Ise, yet appearing in strikingly few surviving myths — a king of the night remembered chiefly for one unforgivable dinner.

Tsukuyomi is the kami of the moon, the night, and (by extension) the measurement of time through lunar phases. His domain is also the realm of indirect light — reflection, illusion, dreams, and the hidden or obscured. In Shinto practice, he is associated with the lunar calendar and with the tides (important for fishing and maritime communities). Unlike Amaterasu's domain of daylight agriculture and imperial rule, Tsukuyomi's domain is the night world of the sea, dreams, and the unconscious. His association with death (through the 'yomi' element of his name) is speculative but persistent — the moon as a guide for souls traveling to the afterlife.

Origins

Born from Izanagi's right eye during the purification ritual (misogi) after his escape from Yomi, the land of the dead. The Nihon Shoki gives variant accounts: in one, he is born from a white-copper mirror held in Izanagi's right hand. His assignment to rule the night came in the same breath as Amaterasu's dominion over the sun — a sibling division of the sky that his own violence would make permanent estrangement.

Attested in Kojiki (712 CE), Nihon Shoki (720 CE)Era Classical Japan

Form

Rarely described directly in classical sources — an absence that became part of his mystique. Later art renders him as a pale, elegant courtier in flowing robes, often with a crescent moon motif, cold and beautiful as moonlight on water.

Powers

Dominion over the moon, the night, the tides, and the measurement of time — his name is often read as moon-reading, tying him to the lunar calendar that governed agricultural and ritual life in classical Japan.

Lunar Dominion / Night Rule Tsukuyomi rules the night and governs the moon's phases. His power is the regulation of lunar cycles — the orderly progression from new moon to full moon and back — which in ancient Japan governed the calendar, agricultural timing, and religious festivals. Kojiki, Nihon Shoki His rule is defined by separation from Amaterasu — he can never appear in the same sky as her, enforcing the sun-moon separation that makes day and night distinct. The extent to which Tsukuyomi actively 'governs' the moon versus merely being associated with it is unclear from the sparse sources.

Purity Judgment / Sudden Violence In his only mythic action, Tsukuyomi kills Ukemochi upon seeing her vomit food from her mouth. This demonstrates a power of violent judgment against perceived impurity or incorrect form. Unlike Susanoo's chaotic destructiveness, Tsukuyomi's violence is principled (if extreme) — a response to violated purity taboos. Nihon Shoki This power is reactive, not proactive, and results in his permanent exile. He does not demonstrate ongoing violent capability. The Kojiki does not contain this myth, raising the possibility that it was a later addition or a variant tradition.

Tidal Control As the moon kami, Tsukuyomi is associated with control over the tides, an attribution based on the observable relationship between lunar phases and ocean tides. This makes him important for maritime communities, fishermen, and sailors. Regional worship traditions (Iki Island, maritime communities) Tidal control is more an attribute of the moon's natural influence than an actively wielded power in the myths. Some traditions assign tidal control to Watatsumi or other sea kami rather than Tsukuyomi.

Indirect Sight / Hidden Knowledge The moon reflects the sun's light rather than generating its own — Tsukuyomi's power is thus the power of reflection, mediation, and second-hand knowledge. He sees what is hidden in darkness, governs dreams and illusions, and represents knowledge gained indirectly — through reflection, measurement, and observation rather than direct illumination. Interpretive tradition, implied by his symbolic attributes This is a symbolic/interpretive power rather than one directly demonstrated in the myths. None significant; this is an interpretive reading.

Food Generation (via Ukemochi's Death) Though Tsukuyomi kills Ukemochi, his act unintentionally generates all food crops — rice, millet, beans, wheat, and silkworms — that spring from her dead body. This is a paradoxical power: destruction that generates sustenance, death that produces life. Nihon Shoki The food generation is not Tsukuyomi's intentional power but the consequence of his violence — the myth distributes agency between Tsukuyomi (who kills) and Ukemochi (whose body transforms). A version of this myth in the Kojiki attributes the food goddess's death to Susanoo instead of Tsukuyomi — a significant variant that may reflect competing traditions about which sibling was responsible.

Behavior & Habitat

Tsukuyomi is characterized by distance, silence, and a kind of cold purity. His single mythic act — killing Ukemochi — is driven by disgust at the 'impure' method of food production (vomiting). This act reveals a rigid, almost obsessive concern with purity and proper form — a profoundly different ethos than Amaterasu's nurturing order or Susanoo's chaotic energy. After his banishment, Tsukuyomi accepts his sentence without protest or further action. Unlike Susanoo, who rages against banishment and goes on to new adventures, Tsukuyomi simply disappears into the night, maintaining his assigned domain in eternal, silent distance from his sister. This passivity is either the mark of perfect restraint or profound alienation, depending on interpretation. He is the kami who does not interact — who observes, measures, and judges from a distance, and who acts only when his sense of purity is offended.

Accounts

After escaping Yomi and purifying himself in the river, Izanagi washes his right eye. From this act, Tsukuyomi-no-Mikoto is born — the second of the Three Precious Children. Izanagi rejoices and assigns Tsukuyomi the domain of the night. The right eye (as opposed to the left eye, which produced Amaterasu) has subtle symbolic implications in Japanese tradition — the right side being associated with the secondary, the reflected, the yin principle. Tsukuyomi is given no further narrative in the Kojiki; he simply receives his domain and vanishes from the story.

Amaterasu, reigning in Takamagahara, sends Tsukuyomi to visit Ukemochi-no-Kami (the food goddess) in the earthly realm. Ukemochi, wanting to honor her divine guest, prepares a feast in a peculiar manner: turning to face the land, she vomits boiled rice from her mouth; turning to the sea, she vomits various fish; turning to the mountains, she vomits game animals. She presents this food to Tsukuyomi, who is revolted by the method of its production. 'How filthy and vile!' he cries out, drawing his sword and killing Ukemochi on the spot. He then returns to Takamagahara and reports his actions to Amaterasu. Amaterasu is furious. 'You are a wicked god,' she declares. 'I can no longer look upon you. From this day, we shall live apart.' And so, the sun and moon were separated — Amaterasu rules the day, and Tsukuyomi the night, never appearing together in the same sky. From Ukemochi's dead body, the food crops arise: from her head, cows and horses; from her forehead, millet; from her eyebrows, silkworms; from her eyes, panic grass; from her belly, rice; from her genitals, wheat, beans, and adzuki beans. The great kami K Musubi-no-Kami takes these seeds and cultivates them, giving agriculture to the world.

Cultural Significance

His estrangement from Amaterasu is the Shinto explanation for the day and night cycle. His killing of Uke Mochi also functions as an origin myth for agriculture: from her body came rice, millet, beans, and silkworms — abundance born of an act the heavens never forgave.

What We Don't Know

Tsukuyomi is arguably the most under-researched major kami in Japanese mythology. The near-total absence of myths about him in the Kojiki demands explanation but has no scholarly consensus. The variant assignment of the Ukemochi myth (to Susanoo in the Kojiki, to Tsukuyomi in the Nihon Shoki) requires more systematic analysis of the relationship between these two texts and their compilers. The survival of Tsukuyomi worship at isolated shrines (particularly Iki Island) suggests regional lunar cults that were never fully incorporated into the Yamato synthesis — comparative study of these local traditions could reveal an alternative Tsukuyomi mythology. The 'yomi' element in his name and its possible connection to the underworld (Yomi-no-Kuni) is intriguing but entirely speculative without more evidence. His identification with Buddhist lunar figures (Gakko Bosatsu) in the medieval syncretic tradition is poorly documented in English-language scholarship. The gender ambiguity of Tsukuyomi — and its implications for understanding Shinto concepts of gender, divinity, and cosmology — is an underexplored field.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Tsukuyomi as the part of the divine that cannot coexist with the everyday. He is not evil, not monstrous, not even particularly angry — he is simply incompatible. The same impulse that makes him kill Ukemochi — the insistence on purity, on proper form, on the right way of doing things — is what makes him incapable of sharing the sky with Amaterasu. His purity is a purity of distance, of separation, of the cold, clear light that illuminates without warming. He is the principle of reflection — the mirror that shows you truth but never touches you. In a mythology dominated by the warm, nurturing, agricultural light of the sun goddess, Tsukuyomi is the necessary counterpoint: the moon that governs tides and calendars, the night that allows the day to have meaning, the silence that makes speech possible. He is not the villain of Japanese mythology — he is its most profound absence, the figure who defines himself by what he is not (Amaterasu) and by what he can never have (reunion, reconciliation, warmth). In this, he is arguably the most modern of the kami: a figure of alienation, of principled solitude, of the coldness that sometimes passes for clarity. He is the god you pray to when you need to see something clearly, without the comfort of the sun's warmth — when you need the truth, even if it's cold.
