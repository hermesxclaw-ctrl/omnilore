Who It Is

In the summer of 1764, the rugged mountains of Gévaudan in southern France became the stage for a reign of terror that would grip a nation and baffle the king himself. A creature — part wolf, part nightmare — began to hunt humans, tearing the throats of shepherds and children in the fields. It was as if the forest itself had turned predator, and no trap, no army, no royal huntsman could stop it. The Beast of Gévaudan was not just an animal; it was a crisis of faith, a challenge to royal authority, and a mystery that has haunted the French imagination for over two centuries.

The Beast was described as unusually bold and cunning. It attacked livestock and humans, often targeting the head and throat. It was said to be remarkably fast and agile, leaping over walls and hedges. It seemed to avoid traps and ambushes with uncanny intelligence, leading many to believe it was not a natural animal. Some accounts describe it as having a particular taste for human flesh, while others say it killed more than it ate.

Powers
Unnatural Size and Strength

Witnesses consistently reported a creature far larger than a normal wolf, with the strength to kill adult cattle and drag away sheep or even human victims.

Limit: No documented physical limitations, though some accounts suggest it was wounded on multiple occasions.

Cunning and Trap Avoidance

The Beast reportedly avoided traps, poisoned baits, and ambushes, and seemed to understand the tactics of the hunters sent against it.

Limit: This could be attributed to natural wolf behavior rather than supernatural intelligence.

Supernatural Invulnerability (Disputed)

Some accounts claim that bullets did not stop the Beast, and that it survived numerous direct hits. This led to the belief that it was a sorcerer's familiar or a demonic entity.

Limit: This is a disputed, supernatural claim. The failure of firearms could be due to poor shot placement, the Beast's speed, or the use of inadequate weapons.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Attested descriptions vary wildly. Most witnesses described a wolf-like creature, but larger than a normal wolf, with a massive head, a broad chest, and a long, bushy tail. Some said the tail was tipped with black. Others described reddish or dark grey fur, and some claimed it had a black stripe along its back. Symbols — The Beast has become a symbol of the wild, untamed nature of the Margeride, and a source of regional pride. It is also a symbol of the fear of the unknown and the failure of authority to protect the people. In modern times, it represents the enduring power of folklore to capture the imagination.

Domains — The Beast was active in the Gévaudan region, particularly in the Margeride Mountains, a rugged, forested, and sparsely populated area of the Massif Central. It also roamed the surrounding plateaus and valleys, attacking isolated farms and villages.

Accounts I
The First AttacksParish records and contemporary accounts

1764

In the summer of 1764, a series of attacks on livestock and people began in the south of Gévaudan. The first recorded human victim was a young woman, Jeanne Boulet, killed in July 1764 near the village of Les Hubacs. The attacks quickly escalated, and the Beast became the terror of the region. The attacks created widespread panic and led to the mobilization of local militias.

Accounts II
The Royal HuntOfficial correspondence and eyewitness accounts

1765

King Louis XV sent his chief huntsman, François Antoine, to Gévaudan with a team of professional wolf hunters. After months of hunting, Antoine presented a large wolf to the court, claiming it was the Beast. The wolf was killed in September 1765, and Antoine was celebrated as a hero. However, attacks resumed shortly after, proving the Beast was still at large. The failure of the royal hunt deepened the crisis and led to further speculation about the Beast's nature.

The Chastel Family and the Final KillLocal tradition and later accounts

1767

After the attacks resumed, a local nobleman, the Marquis d'Apcher, organized another hunt. On June 19, 1767, a hunter named Jean Chastel allegedly shot the Beast at the Sogne d'Auvers. The animal was reportedly a strange wolf-like creature with a massive head and reddish fur. Some accounts claim Chastel used a blessed silver bullet, a detail that has become iconic. The attacks ceased after this kill. The Beast's body was reportedly buried or sent to the king, but its fate is disputed.
