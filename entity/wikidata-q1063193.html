The Approach

In the shadow of Delphi's cliffs, where the Castalian spring still whispers against stone, there once stood a rite so strange and somber that even the Greeks themselves struggled to explain it. Each year, the Delphians would set a table with figs and cakes, then strike a young girl with a rod—and in that blow, they recalled a famine, a king's cruelty, and a maiden's desperate leap. This is the story of Charila, the girl whose death became a festival, and whose name survives only in fragments of myth and ritual.

Content advisory. This entry discusses ritualized violence against a child, including a reenactment of a fatal blow, as part of an ancient religious festival. The material is presented for scholarly and cultural context and may be disturbing to some readers.
Identity

Charila

Pronunciation In English, typically /kəˈrɪlə/ (kuh-RIL-uh). In reconstructed Ancient Greek, Χαρίλα is pronounced [kʰa.rí.laː] with a rough breathing on the initial chi and a long final alpha.

Etymology

The name Charila (Χαρίλα) is likely derived from the Greek root χάρις (charis), meaning 'grace,' 'favor,' or 'beauty.' The name may thus signify 'graceful one' or 'one who brings favor.' The etymology is uncertain, and no ancient source provides an explicit derivation.

Cultural Ownership

Charila is a figure from ancient Greek religion, specifically associated with the city of Delphi and the cult of Apollo. She is not a pan-Hellenic myth but a local Delphic tradition, and her story is known primarily from a single literary source (Plutarch).

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars have speculated that Charila may be an epithet or local form of a goddess, such as Artemis or Persephone, but this is not supported by ancient evidence. The identification is speculative and not widely accepted.

Description

Appearance

No ancient description of Charila's appearance survives. She is typically imagined as a young girl, but there is no textual evidence for her physical characteristics. In the ritual, she may have been represented by a puppet or by a real girl, but the sources are ambiguous.

Symbols & Iconography

No ancient iconography of Charila is known. No vase paintings, reliefs, or other artistic representations have been identified. The only symbolic object associated with her is the shoe used to strike her and the figs and cakes offered at her grave.

Behaviour & Personality

In the myth, Charila is a passive victim: she is struck by the king and subsequently hangs herself. No personality traits are described. As a ritual figure, she is the focus of the ceremony but does not act.

Domains & Habitats

Charila is associated with Delphi, specifically with the area near the tomb of the king (possibly a heroön) and the sanctuary of Apollo. She is a chthonic figure, connected to the earth and the dead.

Version Differences

Only one version of the myth is known, from Plutarch. There are no known variants. The ritual, however, may have varied over time, but no such variations are recorded.

Stories & Myths

The Myth of Charila

Source Plutarch, Quaestiones Graecae 12Culture Ancient Greek (Delphic)Period Unknown; the myth is aetiological, explaining a ritual that may date to the Archaic or Classical period. The story itself is set in an unspecified time of famine.

Summary

During a time of famine, the king of Delphi distributed grain to the citizens, but when an orphan girl named Charila came to him, he struck her with his shoe. Charila, in despair, hanged herself. As a result, a plague or famine returned. The Delphians consulted the oracle, which told them to propitiate the girl's ghost. They then performed a ritual in which a stone or puppet (or possibly a real girl) was given figs and cakes and then struck with a shoe, after which it was taken to a ravine and buried. This ritual was repeated annually at the festival of the 'Charila'.

Child abuse and suicide

Source Plutarch, Quaestiones Graecae 12

Description

The myth of Charila describes a king striking an orphan girl with his shoe, causing her to hang herself. The ritual commemorates this event by striking a representation of the girl.

Scholarly Context

Scholars have interpreted this as a scapegoat ritual or a vegetation rite, but the violence is central to the narrative. It reflects ancient Greek attitudes towards orphans and the power of the dead.

Contradictions & Disputes

Whether Charila was a real girl or a puppet in the ritual

Position A

Plutarch's account suggests that the ritual involved a real girl who was struck with a shoe and given figs and cakes, then led to a ravine.

Position A — evidence

Plutarch's text says 'they strike a girl with a shoe' and 'they give her figs and cakes,' but the Greek is ambiguous.

Position B

Some scholars argue that the ritual used a puppet or an effigy, not a real girl, because the story describes a statue or image.

Position B — evidence

Comparative rituals often use puppets, and the later part of the ritual involves burying the figure, which is easier with an effigy.

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus. The text is ambiguous, and the question remains open.

The identity of the king

Position A

The king is unnamed in Plutarch.

Position A — evidence

Plutarch does not name him.

Position B

Some scholars have speculated he might be a historical or legendary king of Delphi, but no evidence exists.

Position B — evidence

No other source provides a name.

The king remains anonymous.

Primary Evidence

Type textDate c. 100 CELocator Moralia 293C-DLanguage Ancient Greek

Translation

Plutarch recounts the story of Charila, a girl who was struck by the king of Delphi during a famine, and who hanged herself. A subsequent plague led the Delphians to perform a ritual involving a puppet or girl named Charila, who was struck with a shoe and then given figs and cakes.
