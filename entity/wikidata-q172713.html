Who It Is

What does a god give up to save the world? For Týr, the answer was literally a piece of himself. When the monstrous wolf Fenrir demanded a token of trust before allowing the gods to bind him, only one god stepped forward. Týr placed his right hand between the wolf's jaws, knowing exactly what would happen — and when Fenrir bit down, Týr didn't flinch. In a pantheon of thunder-hurlers and tricksters, Týr is the god of the hard choice: the courage to lose so that others might win. His name survives in every Tuesday that passes, a quiet monument to the one-handed god who held the line.

Týr embodies courage, self-sacrifice, and honor in their purest forms. Unlike Odin's cunning intelligence or Thor's overwhelming brute force, Týr's defining act is one of deliberate, knowing sacrifice: he places his right hand in Fenrir's mouth fully aware he will lose it, because it is the only way the gods can bind the wolf and prevent greater catastrophe. He does not hesitate. When the wolf bites down, Týr does not flinch. Powers
Courage and Sacrifice

Týr's defining power is moral rather than magical: the courage to make the ultimate sacrifice for the cosmic good, placing his hand in Fenrir's jaws as the only token the wolf would trust.

Limit:

Victory in Battle

Snorri states Týr 'has great power over victory in battles.' Warriors invoked him for success, and the Tiwaz rune was carved on weapons as a victory charm.

Limit:

Judicial Authority

As Mars Thincsus ('Mars of the Thing'), Týr presided over or sanctified the Germanic legal assembly, making him a god of law, justice, and the social order.

Limit:

Rune Mastery

The Tiwaz rune bears his name and carries his martial and judicial powers. The Sigrdrífumál instructs warriors to carve 'victory runes' on their swords and call twice on Týr.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Týr is most famously depicted in the scene of binding Fenrir — shown with his right hand in the wolf's mouth at the moment of sacrifice. John Bauer's 1911 illustration is the iconic representation: a bearded warrior facing the monstrous wolf with grim determination. Other depictions emphasize his martial aspect, showing him as an armed, one-handed warrior. An 18th-century Icelandic manuscript (ÍB 299 4to) shows Týr equated with Mars in Roman-styl

Symbols — The Tiwaz rune (ᛏ) — the T-rune of the Elder Futhark — is his primary symbol, used as a magical sign on weapons and in inscriptions. Swords were sometimes marked with the Tiwaz rune to invoke victory. His missing right hand is itself symbolic of sacrifice for the greater good. The thing (assembly) is associated with him, and he may have been invoked to sanctify legal proceedings.

Domains — War, justice, law, the thing (legal assembly), oaths, and sacrificial courage. His name survives in Scandinavian toponyms: Tislund ('Týr's grove') is frequent in Denmark, suggesting sacred groves dedicated to his worship. Accounts I
The Binding of FenrirGylfaginning (Prose Edda), chapter 34

13th century (recording earlier oral tradition)

The gods learn that the wolf Fenrir will devour Odin at Ragnarök. They attempt to bind him with increasingly strong chains, but Fenrir breaks free each time. Finally, the dwarves forge Gleipnir — an unbreakable ribbon made from impossible things (the sound of a cat's footfall, a woman's beard, mountain roots, bear sinews, fish breath, bird spittle). Fenrir, suspicious, agrees to be bound only if one god places a hand in his mouth as a pledge of good faith. Only Týr volunteers. When Fenrir realizes he cannot break Gleipnir, he bites off Týr's right hand at the wrist. The gods laugh — except Týr. Fenrir remains bound until Ragnarök. Týr becomes the one-handed god. His sacrifice ensures cosmic safety but marks him permanently.

Thor and Týr Visit HymirHymiskviða (Poetic Edda)

13th century

The gods need a giant cauldron large enough to brew ale for all of them. Týr reveals that his father, the jötunn Hymir, owns such a cauldron. Týr and Thor travel to Hymir's hall. There they encounter Týr's grandmother, a hideous nine-hundred-headed giantess, and his beautiful gold-adorned mother. Hymir returns from hunting, and Thor performs feats of strength — eating two of Hymir's oxen and later going fishing with the giant, catching the Midgard Serpent. The gods ultimately steal the cauldron and flee, with Hymir and other giants in pursuit. Thor kills the pursuers with Mjölnir. The gods obtain the brewing cauldron. The story establishes Týr's jötunn parentage in this tradition.

Týr at RagnarökGylfaginning and Völuspá (Poetic Edda)

13th century

At Ragnarök, Týr faces the monstrous hound Garmr who guards the entrance to Hel. They fight and kill each other — Týr slays the beast but dies of his wounds. This mutual destruction mirrors the fates of other major gods: Odin vs. Fenrir, Thor vs. Jörmungandr. Týr's death in single combat with a guardian beast reinforces his identity as the god who faces danger without hesitation. Týr dies at Ragnarök, taking Garmr with him. His fate is sealed and inevitable, like all the Æsir.

Accounts II
Týr in LokasennaLokasenna (Poetic Edda)

13th century

During Loki's flyting (insult contest) with the gods at Ægir's feast, Loki taunts Týr about his missing hand and his unnamed wife. Loki claims that Týr's wife bore Loki a son, implying cuckoldry. Týr defends himself, but the exchange reveals Loki's talent for finding each god's vulnerability. Unlike Thor, who threatens violence, Týr's response is more measured, befitting his judicial temperament. Reveals personal details about Týr's family life otherwise lost from the surviving corpus. Hints at a consort possibly reflected in the continental Germanic record (see Zisa).

Tacitus's Mars and the SemnonesTacitus, Germania (98 CE)

Roman Iron Age

Tacitus describes the Semnones, a Germanic tribe, who worship a supreme god in a sacred grove. Anyone entering the grove must be bound with a fetter, acknowledging the god's power. This ritual binding and the martial aspect suggest identification with *Tīwaz, the god of binding and war. The human sacrifice mentioned by Tacitus — 'Hercules and Mars they appease by animal offerings of the permitted kind' — aligns with later Scandinavian evidence of Týr's worship. Provides the earliest historical attestation of *Tīwaz worship through Roman ethnography, predating Norse sources by over a millennium.

Sigrdrífumál — Calling Twice on TýrSigrdrífumál (Poetic Edda)

13th century

The valkyrie Sigrdrífa instructs the hero Sigurðr in rune magic. She teaches that victory runes should be carved on the sword's hilt and blade, and one should 'call twice on Týr.' This brief but significant reference confirms Týr's role as a god invoked for martial success and associates him directly with runic magic. Demonstrates Týr's practical role in warrior magic. The 'call twice' formula suggests ritual practice now lost.
