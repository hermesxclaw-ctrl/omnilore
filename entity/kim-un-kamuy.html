Who It Is

In the snowy forests of Hokkaido, the Ainu people speak of a god who walks on four legs and wears a fur coat—the bear god, Kim-un-kamuy, whose return to the heavens each spring is celebrated with song, wine, and the sacred arrow.

Benevolent and protective, but can be wrathful if offended. He is generous, rewarding human piety with gifts of game and prosperity. He is a family-oriented deity, caring for his wife and cub.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Kim-un-kamuy (キムンカムイ) means 'god of the mountains' in Ainu: kim (mountain) + un (in/of) + kamuy (deity). Metotush Kamuy may relate to 'bear' or 'mountain bear'; Nuparikor Kamuy likely means 'he who owns the mountains'.

Pronunciation — KEEM-oon KAH-moo-ee (approximate; Ainu pronunciation is not fully known)

Aliases — Metotush Kamuy, Nuparikor Kamuy

Ownership — Ainu people of Hokkaido, Japan (also historically Sakhalin and Kuril Islands)

Powers
Divine authority over bears and mountains

Kim-un-kamuy is the master of bears and the mountains; he can grant or withhold game.

Limit: Not clearly defined; he is subject to the will of other high deities.

Shape-shifting

He can appear as a human or a bear, as implied in the myth where he visits the human village.

Limit: Not explicitly stated, but his ability to interact with humans suggests transformation.

Blessing of the bear-sending ritual

He accepts the spirit of the sacrificed bear and rewards humans with prosperity.

Limit: Requires proper ritual performance.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Kim-un-kamuy is envisioned as a great bear, often white or with a divine radiance. He lives in the mountains, which are his realm.

Symbols — The bear, the mountain, the inau (sacred rods), wine and millet dumplings as offerings.

Domains — Mountains of Hokkaido, especially deep forests; his home is in the heavens, but he descends to the mountains.

Accounts I
The Bear God and His WifeWikipedia summary (likely based on Ainu oral traditions)

Pre-modern (oral tradition, recorded in 19th-20th century)

Kim-un-kamuy's wife descends to a human village and is delayed. He goes to find her, taking their cub. In the village, he meets the hunt goddess Kamuy Paseguru and the hearth goddess Kamuy Fuchi. A fox bewitches him, and Kamuy Paseguru knocks him out. He wakes in a tree, sees an old bear's body and a cub, and watches humans perform a bear ceremony with offerings. He realizes the dead bear is his wife's disguise? Actually, the myth says he sees the body of an old bear, and later finds his wife alive in the village. The cub is his child. The humans treat the cub well and offer gifts. He and his wife feast with Kamuy Fuchi, then return home. Later, the cub returns with more gifts. Explains the origin of the bear-sending ritual: bears are voluntary sacrifices that return to the heavens and bring gifts.

Accounts II
The Bear-Sending Ceremony (Iomante)Ainu ethnography (Etter, Munro)

Historical practice, suppressed in the 20th century

A bear cub is captured and raised in the village for a year. Then it is killed in a ceremony, its flesh eaten, and its spirit sent back to Kim-un-kamuy's home. The spirit tells of the humans' kindness, ensuring future blessings. Maintains the cycle of reciprocity between humans and the bear god.
