As a daughter of a river god, her domain was the river itself and the surrounding land. In the Argive tradition, she was one of the nymphs who presided over the sacred springs near the Heraion of Argos.

Form

No ancient author provides a physical description of Anchiroe. As a Naiad (a water nymph), she would conventionally have been imagined as a beautiful young woman, often depicted in Greek vase painting and later art as either nude or lightly draped, sometimes with a water jug (hydria) or a reed crown. Her iconography would have been indistinguishable from other river nymphs.

Powers

Naiad immortality As a Naiad, Anchiroe was a long-lived (or immortal) spirit bound to her freshwater source. She did not age or die as mortals do, so long as her river or spring endured. All versions If her river dried up or was diverted, her power and life force would wane. This is a standard Naiad limitation attested across Greek sources. None known — this is inferred from standard Naiad lore rather than explicitly stated for Anchiroe.

Nurturing / protective blessing In the Argive tradition, Anchiroe (with her sisters Euboea and Prosymna) nursed and protected the infant Hera. This implies a protective, sustaining power over the young and the sacred. Argive tradition (Pausanias 2.17.1) None stated. The power is inferred from the myth's role as an aetiological story explaining the sacred springs at the Heraion. None known.

Maternal lineage (heroic genealogy) In one tradition, Anchiroe was the mother of the hero Diomedes, one of the greatest Greek warriors at Troy. This is not a 'power' per se, but a genealogical function that gave her a heroic legacy. Argive tradition (Pausanias 2.25.9) None stated. Other traditions make Diomedes the son of Deipyle, daughter of King Adrastus of Argos. The two genealogies are mutually exclusive and were never harmonized by ancient authors.

Behavior & Habitat

Anchiroe's behavior is not described in any surviving source. As a Naiad, she would have been a tutelary spirit of a specific spring or river — a figure who could bless, heal, or inspire, but also who could be dangerous if her waters were polluted or if she was offended. The single myth in which she acts — nursing the infant Hera — casts her as a nurturing, protective figure.

Accounts

When the goddess Hera was born, her mother Rhea, seeking to protect her from the wrath of her father Cronus, entrusted the infant to the daughters of the river Asterion. The three nymphs — Euboea, Prosymna, and Anchiroe — carried the baby to a sacred grove and washed her in the waters of the Asterion. In gratitude, Hera later granted the river and its nymphs eternal honor, and the place where she was bathed was marked by a sanctuary. The river Asterion was said to have three springs, one for each nymph, and the waters were considered sacred to Hera.

In one Argive genealogical tradition, the hero Diomedes — the 'tamer of horses' who fought at Troy and wounded the gods Ares and Aphrodite — was the son of Hipponous and the nymph Anchiroe, daughter of the river Erasinus. This lineage gave Diomedes a divine, watery ancestry that connected him to the land of Argos itself.

A third Anchiroe, daughter of the river Chremetes in North Africa, was said in a scholium to be the mother of Psyllus, an eponymous ancestor of the Psylli, a Libyan people known for their alleged immunity to snake venom. The river Chremetes is likely the modern Wadi al-Farigh or a similar watercourse in Cyrenaica.

Scholarly Debates
Who is Anchiroe?
Anchiroe is a single figure, daughter of the river Erasinus and mother of Diomedes (Pausanias 2.25.9). ⇌ Anchiroe is a single figure, daughter of the river Asterion and nurse of Hera (Pausanias 2.17.1).
Evidence for A: Pausanias 2.25.9 explicitly names Anchiroe as the mother of Diomedes.
Evidence for B: Pausanias 2.17.1 explicitly names Anchiroe as one of the three nymphs who nursed Hera.
Who is Diomedes' mother?
Diomedes is the son of Tydeus and Deipyle (Homer, Apollodorus). ⇌ Diomedes is the son of Hipponous and Anchiroe (Pausanias 2.25.9).
Evidence for A: Homer's Iliad and Apollodorus' Library both give the Tydeus/Deipyle genealogy, and it is the standard version.
Evidence for B: Pausanias records a local Argive tradition that makes Diomedes the son of Hipponous and Anchiroe.
What We Don't Know

1. The Wikidata entity Q126684991 does not specify which Anchiroe it represents, so this dossier treats all three attested figures as a composite. If the entity is intended to refer to a specific Anchiroe, the dossier should be revised accordingly. 2. No ancient visual depiction of Anchiroe has been securely identified. 3. The scholia on Pindar that mention the Libyan Anchiroe have not been consulted in the original Greek; the translation and interpretation rely on secondary references. 4. The relationship between the Argive Anchiroe (daughter of Asterion) and the local cult of Hera at the Heraion has not been fully explored in modern scholarship; more research is needed on the ritual significance of the three springs. 5. No modern scholarly monograph or article focuses specifically on Anchiroe; all references are embedded in broader studies of Greek nymphs or Argive mythology.

A way to hold it in your mind

Anchiroe is the mythological equivalent of a 'character actor' — a figure who appears briefly, plays a crucial supporting role (nursing a goddess, bearing a hero), and then vanishes. She is also a reminder that in Greek mythology, the same name could be worn by multiple figures, like a shared stage name in a repertory company. If you're looking for a goddess with a grand epic, Anchiroe is not your figure. But if you're interested in the texture of local Greek religion — the sacred springs, the minor nymphs, the alternate genealogies that coexisted for centuries — she is a perfect window into that world.
