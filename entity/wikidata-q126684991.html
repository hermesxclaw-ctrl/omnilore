Who It Is

In the shadow of one of the most powerful sanctuaries in the ancient Greek world — the great temple of Hera at Argos — three sisters once bathed a goddess. Their names were Euboea, Prosymna, and Anchiroe, daughters of a river, and their small act of nurture became the reason a sacred spring flowed for centuries. But Anchiroe is a name with a twist: it was shared by at least three different nymphs, each with her own river, her own story, and her own claim to a hero's bloodline. She is a figure who slips through the cracks of Greek mythology — a water spirit who appears in a handful of lines, yet connects to the Trojan War, the infancy of a queen of the gods, and a snake-charming people in the sands of North Africa.

Anchiroe's behavior is not described in any surviving source. As a Naiad, she would have been a tutelary spirit of a specific spring or river — a figure who could bless, heal, or inspire, but also who could be dangerous if her waters were polluted or if she was offended. The single myth in which she acts — nursing the infant Hera — casts her as a nurturing, protective figure.

Powers
Naiad immortality

As a Naiad, Anchiroe was a long-lived (or immortal) spirit bound to her freshwater source. She did not age or die as mortals do, so long as her river or spring endured.

Limit: If her river dried up or was diverted, her power and life force would wane. This is a standard Naiad limitation attested across Greek sources.

Nurturing / protective blessing

In the Argive tradition, Anchiroe (with her sisters Euboea and Prosymna) nursed and protected the infant Hera. This implies a protective, sustaining power over the young and the sacred.

Limit: None stated. The power is inferred from the myth's role as an aetiological story explaining the sacred springs at the Heraion.

Maternal lineage (heroic genealogy)

In one tradition, Anchiroe was the mother of the hero Diomedes, one of the greatest Greek warriors at Troy. This is not a 'power' per se, but a genealogical function that gave her a heroic legacy.

Limit: None stated.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No ancient author provides a physical description of Anchiroe. As a Naiad (a water nymph), she would conventionally have been imagined as a beautiful young woman, often depicted in Greek vase painting and later art as either nude or lightly draped, sometimes with a water jug (hydria) or a reed crown. Her iconography would have been indistinguishable from other river nymphs.

Symbols — Water jug (hydria), reeds, springs, rivers. In the context of the Hera-nursing myth, her symbol is the nurturing spring itself — the waters of the Asterion river that were sacred to Hera.

Domains — Fresh water — specifically rivers and springs. As a daughter of a river god, her domain was the river itself and the surrounding land. In the Argive tradition, she was one of the nymphs who presided over the sacred springs near the Heraion of Argos.

Accounts I
The Nursing of Hera (Argive tradition)Pausanias, Description of Greece 2.17.1

Classical–Roman era (tradition likely much older)

When the goddess Hera was born, her mother Rhea, seeking to protect her from the wrath of her father Cronus, entrusted the infant to the daughters of the river Asterion. The three nymphs — Euboea, Prosymna, and Anchiroe — carried the baby to a sacred grove and washed her in the waters of the Asterion. In gratitude, Hera later granted the river and its nymphs eternal honor, and the place where she was bathed was marked by a sanctuary. The river Asterion was said to have three springs, one for each nymph, and the waters were considered sacred to Hera. The myth served as an aetiological explanation for the sacred springs at the Heraion of Argos, one of the most important religious sanctuaries in the Greek world. It also established a ritual connection between the nymphs and the goddess Hera, who was worshipped at Argos with exceptional reverence.

Accounts II
Mother of Diomedes (Argive tradition)Pausanias, Description of Greece 2.25.9; also attested in the scholia to Homer's Iliad

Classical–Roman era

In one Argive genealogical tradition, the hero Diomedes — the 'tamer of horses' who fought at Troy and wounded the gods Ares and Aphrodite — was the son of Hipponous and the nymph Anchiroe, daughter of the river Erasinus. This lineage gave Diomedes a divine, watery ancestry that connected him to the land of Argos itself. This genealogy gave Diomedes a semi-divine status and tied him to the river Erasinus, which flowed through the Argive plain. It is one of two competing genealogies for Diomedes, the other making him the son of Tydeus and Deipyle.

Mother of Psyllus (Libyan tradition, late and obscure)Scholia on Pindar, Olympian 6.80

Hellenistic or later (the scholia are late but preserve older material)

A third Anchiroe, daughter of the river Chremetes in North Africa, was said in a scholium to be the mother of Psyllus, an eponymous ancestor of the Psylli, a Libyan people known for their alleged immunity to snake venom. The river Chremetes is likely the modern Wadi al-Farigh or a similar watercourse in Cyrenaica. This version extends the Anchiroe name into the Greek colonial world of North Africa, where local river nymphs were adopted into Greek genealogical frameworks.

Anchiroe is a single, well-defined goddess with a consistent mythology. — Anchiroe is a name shared by at least three distinct nymphs in Greek mythology, each with a different genealogy and role. She is not a major goddess but a minor water nymph whose name appears in scattered local traditions.

Anchiroe is the mother of Diomedes, the Trojan War hero. — This is true only in one local Argive tradition. The dominant, pan-Hellenic tradition makes Diomedes the son of Tydeus and Deipyle.

Anchiroe is an obscure figure with no meaningful mythological role. — While obscure, the Anchiroe who nursed Hera played a role in one of the most important aetiological myths of the Argive Heraion, one of the most significant sanctuaries in the Greek world.
