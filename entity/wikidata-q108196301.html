Who It Is

At sixteen he inherited the world, at thirty he fled it — barefoot through vineyards, hiding in a freedman's villa while the Senate declared him a public enemy. Between those two moments, Nero sang on the stage of Greece while Rome burned, murdered his mother and his wives, built a palace of gold where the fire had been, and convinced half the empire that he was a monster and the other half that he was a god who would return.

Ancient sources present two-layer personality: Early reign (54-59) guided by Seneca and Burrus - described by Tacitus (Ann. 13.4) as promising clemency and Augustan restraint, with genuine interest in administration, Hellenic culture, and popular entertainment. Later reign (59-68) after Burrus' death (AD 62) and Seneca's retirement, sources depict increasing autocracy, theatrical self-identification, and cruelty. Modern historiography (Griffin 1984, Champlin 2003) argues sources conflate aristocratic disdain for philhellenism and populism with tyranny. Attested behavior: obsessive

Powers
Imperium and Tribunician Power

As princeps, held proconsular imperium maius, tribunicia potestas, pontifex maximus, and pater patriae. Could command armies, convene Senate, veto legislation, control treasury, appoint governors, and sentence citizens. Early reign exercised through Senate cooperation; later through direct edict and prefects.

Limit: Constitutionally bound by Augustan fiction of Republic; required Senatorial ratification and Praetorian Guard loyalty. Lost legitimacy when Senate declared hostis (public enemy) in June 68.

Artistic Performance (Citharody, Tragic Acting, Poetry)

Trained citharode (singer to lyre) under Terpnus, performed publicly in Naples (AD 64), Greece (AD 66-67) winning 1,808 crowns according to Dio 63.10, and Rome. Composed poetry (now lost except fragments), including Troica halosis (Sack of Troy). Acted in tragedies as Oedipus, Orestes, Hercules.

Limit: Elite considered performance infamia; legally disqualified aristocrats from stage. Performances required Greek context to be socially acceptable. Voice described as 'feeble and husky' (Suetonius 20) requiring voice trainer.

Architectural Patronage and Urban Planning

Commissioned Domus Aurea with architects Severus and Celer, featuring revolving dining room (cenatio rotunda), extensive frescoes by Fabullus, and innovative concrete vaulting. Rebuilt Rome post-fire with wider streets, porticoes, and fire codes (Tacitus Ann. 15.43). Built Gymnasium, Baths of Nero (Thermae Neronis), and amphitheaters.

Limit: Costs exhausted treasury and required heavy taxation and confiscations; project unfinished at death and largely demolished/built over by Flavians (Colosseum over stagnum).

Diplomatic Settlement - Corbulo's Armenian Compromise

Delegated war to general Gnaeus Domitius Corbulo in Parthian War (58-63), resulting in compromise: Parthian prince Tiridates received Armenia as Roman client, crowned by Nero in Rome AD 66 in elaborate ceremony (Dio 63.1-7). Demonstrated ability to stage diplomacy as theater.

Limit: Success depended entirely on Corbulo's generalship; Nero later forced Corbulo to suicide (AD 67) out of jealousy/fear.

Charioteering

Drove chariots in Greek games at Olympia (even when thrown from chariot, awarded victory) and in Circus Maximus. Favored green faction. Built private circus in Vatican valley.

Limit: Considered disgraceful for emperor; Suetonius notes he was thrown and had to be put back in chariot at Olympia.

Accounts I
The Adoption and Accession (AD 49-54)Tacitus Ann. 12.25-26, 12.64-69; Suetonius Nero 7-8; Cassius Dio 61.1-7

AD 49-54, Julio-Claudian

After Agrippina married Claudius (AD 49), she secured Nero's adoption over Claudius' natural son Britannicus (AD 50), his marriage to Claudia Octavia (AD 53), and control of Praetorian Guard via Burrus. When Claudius died suddenly on 13 October AD 54 after eating mushrooms (Tacitus, Suetonius, Dio attribute to Agrippina via Locusta; modern scholars note natural causes possible), Burrus and Seneca presented 16-year-old Nero to the Guard, who acclaimed him. Senate ratified. Britannicus was sidelined. Nero's first speech, written by Seneca, promised to rule by Augustan precedent and respect the Senate. Established Julio-Claudian succession through adoption rather than blood; set precedent for Praetorian role in succession; began quinquennium Neronis (five good years) under Seneca-Burrus guidance.

The Murder of Agrippina (AD 59)Tacitus Ann. 14.1-13; Suetonius Nero 34; Dio 62.12-14

AD 59

Power struggle escalated as Nero, influenced by mistress Poppaea Sabina and seeking independence, resented Agrippina's domination (she had once struck a coin with jugate portraits). After failed poisoning, Nero's freedman Anicetus, prefect of Misenum fleet, devised a collapsible boat to drown her in Baiae. The boat failed - Agrippina swam ashore. Nero then sent soldiers under Anicetus who murdered her in her villa, staging it as suicide after she allegedly sent a freedman with a dagger. Her last words in Tacitus: 'Ventrem feri' ('Strike the womb' that bore Nero). Removed last restraint; Seneca wrote justificatory letter to Senate claiming Agrippina plotted against Nero; public unease; matricide became central to Nero's black legend and Christian Antichrist typology.

The Great Fire of Rome and the Christians (AD 64)Tacitus Ann. 15.38-44; Suetonius Nero 38; Dio 62.16-18; Pliny NH 17.1.5

19-26 July AD 64

Fire began in shops near Circus Maximus, burned for six days, destroyed 10 of 14 regions. Nero was at Antium, returned and organized relief (opening public buildings, lowering grain prices) and then rebuilt with fire codes (wider streets, stone porticoes, limited building height). Rumor spread that Nero had sung of Troy during the fire or ordered it to clear land for Domus Aurea. To quash rumor, Tacitus reports Nero scapegoated Christians (Chrestiani), followers of Christus executed under Pontius Pilate, arresting them and executing with elaborate cruelty: covered in animal skins and torn by dogs, crucified, and burned as torches in Vatican gardens. Urban reform and Domus Aurea construction; first attested Roman persecution of Christians (historic debated); enduring legend 'Nero fiddled while Rome burned' (anachronistic - fiddle did not exist; Suetonius says he sang on stage, not fiddled).

Accounts II
The Greek Tour and the Coronation of Tiridates (AD 66-67)Suetonius Nero 19-25; Dio 63.1-21; Tacitus Ann. 15.29-31 (background)

AD 66-67

After settling Parthian War, Nero invited Tiridates I of Armenia to Rome (AD 66) for coronation, staging a spectacular ceremony where Tiridates knelt and Nero placed diadem, proclaiming him king as client of Rome - theater as diplomacy. Then Nero embarked on unprecedented imperial tour of Greece (AD 66-67), competing in all four Panhellenic games (Olympia, Pythia, Isthmia, Nemea), winning every contest (even when falling from chariot at Olympia). He declared Greece free at Isthmus (echoing Flamininus), and performed as citharode and tragedian to ecstatic Greek crowds. Meanwhile freedman Helius governed Rome harshly. Alienated Roman elite and army by prolonged absence and Hellenic self-presentation; drained treasury with largesse; demonstrated philhellenism that endeared him to eastern provinces and fueled Nero Redivivus legend.

The Pisonian Conspiracy and the Deaths of Seneca and Lucan (AD 65)Tacitus Ann. 15.48-74; Suetonius Nero 36; Dio 62.24-27

April AD 65

Senator Gaius Calpurnius Piso led conspiracy of senators, knights, officers and poets (including Lucan) to assassinate Nero at Baiae and replace him. Betrayed by freedman Milichus, conspiracy was uncovered. Nero forced suicides and executions of at least 19, including Seneca (ordered to open veins in bath, dictated to scribes as he died), his nephew Lucan, Petronius (author of Satyricon, forced suicide with parody will), and prefect Faenius Rufus. Tigellinus led purge. Eliminated Stoic opposition and literary elite; cemented reign of terror narrative; Seneca's death became Stoic martyrdom icon; increased dependence on Tigellinus.

Revolt, Declaration as Hostis, and Suicide (AD 68)Suetonius Nero 40-50; Dio 63.22-29; Tacitus Hist. 1.4-8; Plutarch Galba 2-7

March-June AD 68

Governor of Gallia Lugdunensis Gaius Julius Vindex revolted (March 68), supported by Servius Sulpicius Galba, governor of Hispania Tarraconensis. Nero initially dismissed revolt, then panicked, planning to kill senators and burn Rome. Praetorian prefect Nymphidius Sabinus bribed Guard to desert Nero; Senate declared him hostis (public enemy). Nero fled Rome at night with freedman Phaon, Sporus and Epaphroditus to Phaon's villa 4 miles north. When Senate's executioners approached, he ordered secretary Epaphroditus to help him stab his throat, quoting Greek tragedy: 'What an artist dies in me! Qualis artifex pereo!' Died 9 June AD 68, aged 30. Buried in Domitii family tomb. End of Julio-Claudian dynasty; triggered Year of Four Emperors (Galba, Otho, Vitellius, Vespasian); Domus Aurea unfinished and later buried; legend that Nero had not died but fled to Parthia began immediately.

Nero fiddled while Rome burned — The violin/fiddle did not exist in AD 64. Suetonius says Nero sang 'The Sack of Ilium' from a tower in stage costume; Tacitus says he was at Antium when fire started and returned to organize relief. The fiddle is medieval anachronism from later translations/legends.

Nero was the first to persecute Christians empire-wide and killed Peter and Paul himself — Tacitus describes punishment of Christians in Rome AD 64, not empire-wide edict. Peter and Paul's martyrdoms under Nero are later Christian tradition (Eusebius, 4th c.) with no contemporary evidence linking Nero directly to their deaths; historicity of Peter in Rome debated.

Nero was universally hated and Rome rejoiced at his death — Senate and elite hated him, but plebs and eastern provinces mourned him. Suetonius (Nero 57) notes common people decorated his tomb with flowers; three pseudo-Nerones exploited popular longing; Otho and Vitellius honored his memory to gain support.

Nero burned Christians as human torches to light his gardens as a regular practice — Tacitus describes one episode in Vatican gardens after the fire as punishment for arson, not routine illumination. The image became exaggerated in later art (Henryk Siemiradzki's 'Nero's Torches' 1876) into emblem of continuous cruelty.

Nero's name means 'black' and he was called that for cruelty — Latin 'niger' means black, but 'Nero' is Sabine for 'strong' (Suetonius Tib. 1). No ancient source connects name to color or cruelty; folk etymology is modern invention.
