Who It Is

Frankenstein's Monster (often incorrectly called 'Frankenstein,' which is the name of his creator, not the creature) is one of the most profound creations in Gothic literature — an artificial being constructed from assembled body parts and animated by scientific means. Mary Shelley conceived the story during the 'Year Without a Summer' (1816) while staying at Lake Geneva with Percy Bysshe Shelley, Lord Byron, and others. In the novel, the ambitious young scientist Victor Frankenstein assembles a creature from cadaver parts and brings it to life through an unspecified scientific process (not lightning, as in most adaptations; Shelley deliberately kept the method vague). Horrified by his creation's appearance, Frankenstein abandons it immediately, setting in motion a tragic chain of events. The Monster, possessing intelligence, sensitivity, and an innate desire for companionship, is repeatedly rejected by every human it encounters due to its grotesque appearance. It secretly educates itself by observing a family through a cottage wall, learning to speak, read (from Paradise Lost, Plutarch's Lives, and The Sorrows of Young Werther), and understand human society — making it one of literature's great autodidacts. The Monster's demand that Frankenstein create a female companion, and Frankenstein's refusal (born from fear of creating a race of monsters), leads to the Monster's vengeful killing of Frankenstein's loved ones one by one. The novel ends with both creator and creation pursuing each other to the Arctic, where Frankenstein dies and the Monster, mourning his creator, drifts away on an ice floe to die. Unlike the grunting, bolt-necked figure of Universal Pictures films, Shelley's Monster is eloquent, philosophical, and deeply tragic — a being made monstrous not by nature but by universal rejection. The novel pioneered science fiction and explored themes of creation without responsibility, the nature of humanity, the dangers of unchecked ambition, and whether evil is innate or socially constructed. The Monster has become a cultural shorthand for scientific hubris and the creator's responsibility toward their creation — themes that resonate more powerfully with each advance in AI, genetic engineering, and biotechnology.

Cultural Significance

The foundational text of science fiction. Created the 'created being rebels against creator' archetype. Central metaphor for scientific ethics, the definition of humanity, and social construction of monstrosity. The subtitle 'The Modern Prometheus' frames scientific ambition as both heroic and transgressive.
