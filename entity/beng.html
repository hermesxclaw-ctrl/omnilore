Who It Is

Imagine a world being born while a shadow in the depths tries to steal the very soil of creation—that shadow, in Romani stories, is Beng, the Devil whose cunning never quite matches the God he opposes. To follow Beng through cosmogonic myths, roadside warnings, and centuries of misrepresentation is to watch evil take on a portable, shifting shape, carried in a single word across continents and into the moral heart of a people without a homeland.

Beng’s personality is consistently characterized as malevolent, but his behavior often has a trickster dimension. In cosmogonic tales, Beng attempts to outwit Devel, stealing or hiding the earth used for creation, or trying to drown the creator, only to be thwarted by his own greed or foolishness. In moral stories, Beng tempts humans into breaking taboos, betraying kin, or violating rules of purity (mahrime), sometimes through clever speech or deceptive promises. Powers
Cosmic opposition to Devel (God)

In Romani cosmogonic myths analyzed by Muscianisi and others, Beng functions as the cosmic adversary of Devel, embodying the principle of evil in a dualistic structure reminiscent of Avestan (Zoroastrian) thought. Beng attempts to thwart or corrupt creation, sometimes by manipulating the material used to form the earth or by trying to drown Devel. His "power" here is not a detailed magical technique but a metaphysical role: he represents the active force of chaos, destruction, and moral negation, set against Devel’s creative goodness.

Limit: Beng is consistently defeated or constrained by Devel’s superior power and wisdom. His attempts to sabotage creation fail, and the world comes into being despite his interference. This limitation underscores the dualistic but asymmetrical nature of the cosmology: evil is powerful but ultimately subordinate to good.

Temptation and moral corruption

Beng’s most pervasive ability is to tempt humans into wrongdoing. He lures people into breaking taboos, betraying kin, or engaging in impure acts, often through promises of wealth, pleasure, or power. This aligns Beng with the broader Devil archetype in Christian and Islamic traditions, but in Romani contexts the focus is frequently on violations of marime (ritual impurity) and social obligations, reflecting the centrality of cleanliness and family in Romani life.

Limit: Beng’s success depends on human consent and weakness. Those who remain vigilant, adhere to community norms, and seek Devel’s protection can resist his temptations. In many stories, Beng is outwitted or exposed, reinforcing the possibility of moral victory.

Trickery and shape-shifting

As a trickster spirit, Beng can appear in different guises—often as a human stranger, a helpful figure, or a seemingly harmless presence—before revealing his true intentions. This shape-shifting is more narrative than systematic: tales describe Beng changing form or hiding his identity to deceive, but there is no detailed catalogue of transformations as in some other mythologies.

Limit: Beng’s disguises are ultimately penetrable. Characters who pay attention to moral cues, ritual rules, or divine warnings can recognize and expose him. His trickery often backfires, leading to comic or instructive outcomes.

Association with illness and misfortune

In some strands of Romani belief, misfortune, illness, or accidents may be attributed to the influence of Beng or to devilish forces. This is less a specific power than a causal attribution: Beng is invoked to explain why bad things happen, especially when linked to moral or ritual failings.

Limit: Not all misfortune is blamed on Beng; natural causes, ancestral issues, and other spirits (such as the mullo, the restless dead) also play roles. Beng’s association with misfortune is strongest where moral transgression is involved.

Accounts I
The creation of the world and the contest between Devel and BengThree Romani cosmogonic myth variants analyzed by Domenico Giuseppe Muscianisi (2022) and earlier folkloristic collections.

Recorded in the late 19th and 20th centuries; narrative motifs likely older.

In these cosmogonic tales, Devel (God) and Beng (Devil) participate in the creation of the world. Devel seeks to form the earth from primordial waters or mud, while Beng, representing evil, attempts to sabotage the process—stealing the earth, hiding it, or trying to drown Devel. Despite Beng’s interference, the world is created, often with imperfections or dangerous elements attributed to his involvement. The narrative dramatizes a dualistic tension between good and evil, with Beng as the cosmic adversary whose cunning cannot overcome Devel’s ultimate authority. The world’s existence and its mixture of good and evil are explained through Beng’s failed sabotage. The story establishes Beng’s role as the embodiment of evil and sets the stage for ongoing moral struggle in human life.

Accounts II
Beng’s bargains with humansNineteenth-century collections of Romani tales (e.g., Wlislocki) and later retellings in folklore anthologies.

Recorded in the late 19th century; narrative motifs likely older.

In various tales, Beng appears to humans offering wealth, success, or protection in exchange for their souls, loyalty, or transgression of community norms. The human protagonist, often poor or desperate, is tempted but ultimately finds a way to escape the bargain—sometimes through clever wording, sometimes through divine intervention by Devel. These stories mirror broader European "deal with the Devil" motifs but are framed within Romani concerns about family, purity, and survival on the road. The tales reinforce the dangers of making deals with evil and the importance of maintaining moral integrity. They also highlight the possibility of outwitting Beng, emphasizing cunning aligned with righteousness rather than with malice.

Beng as a tempter of impurityGeneral Romani moral discourse and scattered narrative references in ethnographic and folkloric literature.

Modern and contemporary oral tradition.

In everyday stories told within families, Beng is invoked as the figure who encourages people to break rules of cleanliness and social order—urging someone to neglect washing, to cross forbidden boundaries, or to disrespect elders. These narratives are often short, anecdotal, and didactic, serving to teach children about marime and the consequences of ignoring community norms. The stories reinforce the centrality of ritual purity and respect in Romani life. Beng’s role is to personify the pull toward disorder and disrespect, making abstract moral concepts vivid and memorable.
