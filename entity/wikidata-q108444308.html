Who It Is

He was born into a house that had already survived monsters — the Chimera slain, the Amazons routed — only to be cut down not by a beast but by a god on a Lycian battlefield. Isander, eldest son of the great Bellerophon, barely earns three lines in Homer, yet his abrupt death marks the moment divine favor turned to divine accounting.

Homer characterizes Isander only by his death in war, implying martial valor: he fought the 'glorious Solymi' (agauoi Solymoi) until slain by Ares. The scholia and later mythographers frame his death as part of divine compensation for Bellerophon's earlier successes, suggesting a pious or at least doomed filial role rather than personal hubris. No speeches, motives, or personality traits are preserved. All character reconstruction is hypothetical.

Princely Martial Prowess

As son and heir of Bellerophon, Isander is presumed to have been trained as a Lycian aristocratic warrior. Homer's phrase that he 'fought against the glorious Solymi' (machesthai Solymoisin) implies command or front-line combat status. No specific feat, weapon, or technique is named.

Limit: Mortal; no divine parentage, no invulnerability, no attested divine gift. Killed in conventional warfare despite status.

Royal Succession Status

Eldest (?) son of Bellerophon and Philonoe, positioned as heir to the Lycian throne of Iobates. His brother Hippolochus later succeeds in the line leading to Glaucus, suggesting Isander's death removed the primary heir.

Limit: Succession never realized due to death in battle; no reign attested.

Vulnerability to Divine Hostility

Explicitly slain by Ares, god of war, 'insatiate of battle' (atos polemoio). This indicates not merely death in battle but direct divine agency, often interpreted as divine retribution for Bellerophon's later hubris or as compensation for excessive good fortune.

Limit: No resistance or countermeasure attested; death is immediate and absolute.

Accounts I
Bellerophon's Exile, Trials, and Marriage into LyciaHomer, Iliad 6.155-195; Apollodorus Bibliotheca 2.3.1-2; Pindar Olympian 13

Archaic epic (8th c. BCE) with Classical elaboration

Bellerophon, grandson of Sisyphus, is falsely accused by Anteia (Stheneboea) after rejecting her advances and sent by King Proetus to King Iobates of Lycia with a deadly letter. Iobates sets him impossible tasks: slay the Chimera, fight the Solymi and Amazons. With the winged horse Pegasus (gift of Athena/Poseidon), Bellerophon succeeds. Impressed and recognizing divine favor, Iobates gives him half his kingdom and his daughter Philonoe in marriage. This union produces Isander, Hippolochus, and Laodamia, establishing a new Lycian royal house. Creates the legitimacy and territory that Isander inherits; establishes the Solymi as hereditary enemies of the house; sets up the later divine reversal of fortune.

The Birth and Naming of the Lycian PrincesHomer Iliad 6.196-197; Apollodorus Bibliotheca 2.3.2; Strabo Geographica 12.8.5

Archaic to Hellenistic

After his marriage, Bellerophon fathers three children by Philonoe: Isander (Isandros/Peisandros), Hippolochus, and daughter Laodamia. The names are programmatic: Isander 'equal to man' signals heroic expectation; Hippolochus 'ambush of horses' links to equestrian father; Laodamia 'tamer of the people' foreshadows her union with Zeus. The family resides in Lycia as part of Iobates' dynasty, bridging Argive and Anatolian lineages. Establishes the genealogical bridge to the Trojan War generation: Hippolochus fathers Glaucus (Lycian leader at Troy), Laodamia mothers Sarpedon by Zeus.

The War Against the Glorious Solymi and the Death of IsanderHomer Iliad 6.203-205 (primary); Strabo Geographica 13.4.16 on Solymi identity; Apollodorus epitome

Archaic epic

In the only narrative action directly attested for Isander, he marches against the Solymi, described by Homer as 'glorious' or 'renowned' (agauoi) and by Strabo as a warlike mountain people north of Lycia (later identified with Pisidians or Termilae). There, 'Ares, insatiate of war, slew Isander as he fought the Solymi.' The phrasing suggests either that Ares intervened personally on the battlefield or, in formulaic epic diction, that he fell in fierce combat. The scholia add that his misfortune was compensation (tisis) for his father's earlier excessive good fortune. Eliminates the senior Bellerophontid heir; leaves Hippolochus as successor; demonstrates the turning of divine favor against the house; prefigures Bellerophon's own fall.

Accounts II
The Divine Reversal: Bellerophon's Hubris and the Ruin of His HouseHomer Iliad 6.200-202; Pindar Isthmian 7.44-48; Apollodorus 2.3.2; Euripides Bellerophon (fragmentary)

Archaic to Classical (Homer to 5th c. tragedy)

After his victories, Bellerophon becomes hated by the gods (theoi echthros). Homer says he wandered alone on the Aleian plain, avoiding the paths of men, his soul devoured by grief. Pindar and later sources elaborate that he attempted to fly Pegasus to Olympus and was thrown by Zeus. In this context, the deaths of his children — Isander by Ares, Laodamia by Artemis' wrath — are presented as part of the divine punishment that erases his lineage's prosperity. Homer explicitly links Isander's death to this reversal: the father who was once most fortunate now loses his son. Explains Isander's death not as random casualty but as theological compensation; marks the end of Bellerophon's heroic arc and transition to tragic wanderer motif.

The Survivors: Hippolochus and Laodamia Carry the Line to TroyHomer Iliad 2.876-877, 6.197-199, 6.206-211, 16.328-332; Apollodorus Bibliotheca 3.1.1; Diodorus 5.79

Archaic epic

After Isander's death, his siblings survive to continue the bloodline. Hippolochus fathers Glaucus, who leads the Lycians at Troy and famously exchanges armor with Diomedes, recounting Bellerophon's story (Iliad 6.144-211). Laodamia (also called Deidamia/Hippodamia) is loved by Zeus and bears Sarpedon, the great Lycian king and son of Zeus who dies at Troy by Patroclus' hand. Thus Isander's death leaves him as the childless, truncated branch, while his siblings become ancestors of Troy's most important Lycian heroes. Artemis later kills Laodamia (Homer 6.205), further decimating the house. Isander becomes genealogically sterile; his memory preserved only as Glaucus' uncle in the famous Glaukos-Diomedes episode, which is the main vehicle for transmission of his name.

The Solymi in Lycian EthnographyHerodotus Histories 1.173; Strabo Geographica 14.3.9; Pliny Natural History 5.27

Classical to Roman

Beyond myth, the Solymi were remembered as the pre-Greek inhabitants of Lycia, later displaced by Cretan colonists under Sarpedon. Herodotus says the Lycians were formerly called Solymi and Termilae. Strabo identifies the Solymi with the Pisidian highlanders. In this euhemerized reading, Bellerophon's and Isander's wars against the Solymi reflect Bronze Age conflicts between Greek-settled coastal Lycia and indigenous mountain tribes — giving Isander's death a pseudo-historical geographic anchor. Transforms Isander's mythic death into an aetiology for Lycian-Pisidian enmity; used by later geographers to explain place names and tribal boundaries.

Isander was the hero who tamed Pegasus and killed the Chimera. — Those deeds belong to his father Bellerophon. Isander has no attested monster-slaying or Pegasus association; his only attested action is dying in the Solymi war.

Isander survived and became king of Lycia after Bellerophon. — Homer and Apollodorus agree Isander predeceased his father, killed by Ares. The succession passed through his younger brother Hippolochus to Glaucus.

Isander and Peisander are two different sons of Bellerophon. — They are variant spellings of the same person in manuscript tradition. Bellerophon had two sons: Isander/Peisander and Hippolochus, not three sons.

Isander appears as a playable character or boss in God of War or similar games. — No major video game, film, or TV series features Isander as a distinct character. He is at most mentioned in genealogical lore entries (e.g., Assassin's Creed Discovery Mode). Bellerophon appears more often.
