Who It Is

Every year at the end of Buddhist Lent, on a stretch of the Mekong River that separates Thailand and Laos, thousands of people gather on the banks to watch pale red fireballs drift up from the dark water and vanish into the night sky. Whether they are the breath of a giant serpent or an unsolved physics problem depends entirely on whom you ask — but the serpent imagined there, the Phaya Nāk, is the same being that once coiled its seven great hoods over a meditating Buddha to shelter him from a storm, that was stretched as a rope to churn the ocean of milk for the nectar of immortality, and that nearly lost its entire race to a king's revenge-furnace in the Mahabharata. Half-human and half-serpent, jewel-hooded, master of poison and rain and buried treasure, the nāga has for two thousand years been the great threshold-dweller of South and Southeast Asia — the being that guards the boundar

Nāgas are proudly territorial, numinously powerful, water-obsessed, and morally ambivalent — benevolent when honored, lethal when crossed. They guard treasures, bodies of water, and esoteric (often bound) knowledge. Their mood is defined by treatment: the same nāga race that shelters a meditating Buddha can demand a human bride, hoard gold at the bottom of a lake, or kill a king by snakebite. Powers
Shape-shifting (anthropomorphic transformation)

Nāgas can assume fully human, partially human, or fully serpentine form at will. The Buddha tells one nāga that ordination is impossible in its animal body, but that it could earn rebirth as a human and become a monk. Mucalinda takes the form of a young Brahmin to pay homage to the Buddha; the nāga princess Soma of Funan appears as a human woman. The serpent-maiden lovers (Ulupi, Nagini) use this power to enter human society and marry heroes.

Limit: Form is not always freely controlled — an animal-form nāga cannot ordain (it needs rebirth); Nagini's curse in Harry Potter renders her snake form eventually permanent.

Elemental control — water, rain, storms, earth

Nāgas are rain-bringers and storm-wielders. In Thailand they are 'patrons of water' and command rain; the Nak hai nam ('nāga granting water') oracle forecasts rainfall annually. The main article states all four great races of nāgas 'can set off storms, rain, tempest and create lands from the sea.' Paravataksha's sword causes earthquakes and his roar thunder. Creatures like Karkotaka and Takshaka control venom and weather.

Limit: Rain-giving is often seasonal and conditional on proper worship and clean environment.

Potent venom (Kālakēṭu / nāga poison)

The nāga's venom is cosmically dangerous. Kaliya's poison boils the Yamuna for four leagues and kills all birds and beasts near the bank. Takshaka's venom reduces a banyan tree to ash on challenge. In the Samudra Manthana, Vasuki's churning exudes halāhala, a poison so potent it threatens all creation; Shiva drinks it, which turns his throat blue (the epithet Nīlakaṇṭha). This is the theological root of nāga astrology and snakebite cures centered on the goddess Manasa (Viṣahari, 'destroyer of poison').

Limit: Poison can be rendered benign or cured by Manasa worship; Krishna burns off Kaliya's poison by dancing on his heads.

Guardianship of treasure and secret knowledge

Nāgas guard gems, gold and 'spiritual' or 'elementally-encoded' treasure in the underworld and under the seas. In Tibetan Buddhism they are guardians/owners of submerged treasure — both monetary wealth and spiritual terma encoded by adepts. The Prajñāpāramitā sutras were, by tradition, entrusted to a great nāga who guarded them in the sea until Nāgārjuna recovered them — the charter myth of Nagarjuna's name.

Limit: Treating the treasure with disrespect incurs wrath; disturbing a nāga's water is harmful.

Accounts I
The Churning of the Ocean of Milk (Samudra Manthana)Vishnu Purana, Mahabharata, Kamba Ramayana

Epic/Puranic

After the sage Durvasa curses Indra and the devas to lose their fortune, the devas are defeated by the asuras and the gods lose the three worlds. Advised by Vishnu, the devas and asuras agree to churn the Ocean of Milk to obtain amrita, the nectar of immortality. Mount Mandara is uprooted as the churning rod, and Vasuki — the great nāga king who coils on Shiva's neck — agrees to serve as the churning rope after being promised a share. When the mountain sinks, Vishnu's Kurma (turtle) avatar supports it. The nāga's body produces the lethal halāhala poison as the churning grinds on; Shiva drinks it to save creation, his throat turning blue (Nīlakaṇṭha). From the ocean emerge the fourteen ratnas (gems, goddesses like Lakshmi, the apsaras, and others). The nāga is thus the pivot on which the entire cosmic churning turns — the rope that grinds out both the poison and the nectar of immortality. Vasuki becomes a lasting emblem of Shiva (worn coiled on his neck); the episode blesses the nāgas with renown and gives the serpent race a share of the myth's glory.

The Sarpa Satra (Snake Sacrifice) and Astika's InterventionMahabharata, Adi Parva

Epic

King Parikshit of the Kuru dynasty, cursed to die of snakebite for killing a snake and mocking a meditating sage, is slain by Takshaka, the nāga king of the Khandava forest. His son Janamejaya vows to annihilate the entire serpent race and begins a great Sarpa Satra — a fire-sacrifice that draws countless nāgas into the flames. As the race is nearly wiped out, Vasuki sorrows for his kin and appeals to his nephew Astika, the young sage born of the Brahmin Jaratkaru and the nāga princess Manasa (Jaratkaru). Astika enters the sacrificial ground and, having earned a boon from the king for his erudition, halts the sacrifice — his command 'Stay, stay, stay' overrides the priests' mantras and holds Takshaka suspended above the fire. Janamejaya grants the boon, the sacrifice is stopped, and peace is brokered between the Kurus and the serpents. Astika also decrees that anyone reciting his story is immune to snakebite. Krishna Dances Upon Kaliya (Kaliya Mardan)Bhagavata Purana, Canto 10, Chapter 16

Puranic (c. 8th–10th century CE)

The venomous nāga Kaliya, driven from his home island of Ramanaka by fear of the serpent-devouring Garuda (who cannot enter Vrindavan due to a sage's curse), takes refuge in the Yamuna river, poisoning its waters for four leagues. Krishna, troubled by the serpent's terrorizing of Radha and the people, enters the river; Kaliya coils around him and drags him to the bottom. Krishna expands himself free, then springs onto Kaliya's hundred hoods, dancing with the weight of the whole universe and beating the venom out of the serpent. As Kaliya vomits blood and nears death, his wives pray to Krishna for mercy. Kaliya surrenders, promising never to harass anyone again, and Krishna, after a final dance, banishes him to Ramanaka (or per regional legend, to the deeps pushed into Patala, where he resides to this day). The event is celebrated as the Naga Nathaiyā/Naga Nritya dance. The Yamuna is made safe and pure; Kaliya is humbled not destroyed, and the episode becomes one of the most beloved images of Hindu art and dance (Kaliya Mardana).

Accounts II
Mucalinda Shelters the BuddhaMucalinda Sutta (Pali Canon), Vinaya Sutra I.3, Lalitavistara Sutra

Texts of varying date; the Buddha's enlightenment conventionally 6th–5th century BCE

Six (or five) weeks after enlightenment, as the Buddha meditates under the Bodhi Tree, a great storm rises and the heavens darken for seven days. King Mucalinda, the nāga king, comes up from beneath the earth, coils seven times around the Buddha's body, and spreads his great hood over him as a shelter of seven heads. When the storm clears, Mucalinda assumes the form of a young Brahmin and pays homage, circumambulating the Buddha three times before returning joyfully to his palace. The image of the Buddha sheltered under the nāga's hood is among the most iconic in Buddhist art, especially in Laos, and the first surviving depiction dates to a 2nd-century BC stupa at Pauni, Maharashtra. The nāga becomes an emblem of protective devotion and of the natural world's reverence for the awakened one — a counterweight to the Hindu nāga's more menacing side.

The Founding of Funan: Kaundinya and the Nāga Princess SomaKhmer legend, reported by Chinese envoys Kang Tai and Zhu Ying; stories of Preah Thong and Neang Neak

Funan era (1st century CE and later)

According to Khmer tradition, an Indian Brahmin prince named Kaundinya I dreams that he must take a magic bow from a temple and defeat Soma (Liuye, 'Willow Leaf'), the nāga princess and daughter of the nāga king. In the battle that follows he and Soma fall in love and marry, establishing the royal lineage of the Funan dynasty; Kaundinya builds the capital city Vyadhapura, and the kingdom becomes Kambujadeśa ('Cambodia'). The love story of Preah Thong and Neang Neak is the foundation for standard Khmer wedding practice, and many Khmer regard themselves as descendants of the nāgas who will one day return to restore prosperity. The marriage legitimizes the Cambodian royalty as nāga-descended and cements the nāga as a national ancestor-symbol whose seven-headed forms adorn Angkorian temple balustrades.

Karkotaka and King NalaMahabharata

Epic

The nāga king Karkotaka, one of the sons of Kashyapa and Kadru, deceived the sage Narada in a game of chance and was cursed to remain stationary in a forest until saved by King Nala. During a forest fire, Nala rescues the thumb-sized Karkotaka, freeing him from the curse. In return the serpent stings Nala — transforming him into an ugly shape — to expel the asura Kali that has possessed the king, and assures Nala he will be undefeatable while the poison remains in his body. Karkotaka sends Nala to King Rituparna as the charioteer Bahuka, arranging the exchange of mantras that ultimately reunites Nala with his bride Damayanti. Karkotaka becomes a savior-figure who rescues a king from possession; the story shows the nāga as a morally complex agent who curses, tests, and ultimately redeems.

Antaboga and the Creation of the World TurtleJavanese/Sundanese/Balinese tradition, Wawacan Sulanjana

Pre-Islamic, transmitted in wayang

In Indonesian tradition, at the beginning of time only the great serpent Antaboga existed. Antaboga meditated and co-created (or engendered) the world-turtle Bedawang, upon whose back the entire world rests. The serpent also produced two offspring, Bambang Naga Tatmala and Dewi Nagagini, and in Sundanese myth is responsible for the birth of Dewi Sri, the rice goddess, who emerges from an egg formed from Antaboga's tears. The myth is preserved in wayang performances that blend Indic philosophy with Javanese animism. Antaboga becomes the Indonesian nāga/world-serpent par excellence, its image carved on keris, temple stairs, and gong decorations — a symbol of the Javanese 'dragon' and of the underworld that holds up the earth.

A 'naga' is always a dragon. — The nāga is a specific half-human/half-serpent water-and-treasure deity of Hindu-Buddhist Asia, usually a cobra; it is distinct from the airborne Chinese/Japanese dragon (lóng/ryū), despite modern glosses and frs. franchise designs. Many media 'dragon' references to nagas are loose.

There is one 'Naga king.' — Multiple nāgarājas — Shesha/Ananta, Vasuki, Takshaka, Karkotaka, Mucalinda, Antaboga — each with distinct roles and mythologies. Conflating them into a single figure loses the structure of the myth.

The Warcraft Nagas faithfully represent the mythic nāga. — Blizzard's Nagas are ancient elves transformed by the Old Gods into sea-creatures — a franchise invention. The name is borrowed; the origin, motivation and design are original to the game.

The Sarpa Satra was a historical event. — It is a mythic-charter narrative in the Mahabharata (Adi Parva), not a recorded historical massacre; there is no independent historical record of Janamejaya's sacrifice.

Nagini in Harry Potter is just a pet snake. — Rowling's Nagini is a Maledictus (person permanently transforming into a serpent) — an echo of the nāgī whose snake form becomes fixed, a genuine (if fictionalized) nod to the nāga's form-limitation theme. It is a name use with thematic resonance, not a faithful adaptation.
