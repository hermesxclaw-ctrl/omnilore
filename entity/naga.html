Who It Is

The Nagas are a race of semi-divine serpentine beings that occupy a central place in the mythologies of South and Southeast Asia. They are typically depicted as great snakes, often with multiple heads (most famously as seven-headed cobras), or as beings capable of assuming human form. The term 'Naga' applies equally to individual serpent deities, most notably the great Naga kings—Shesha (Ananta), Vasuki, Takshaka, and Mucalinda—and to the entire class of serpent beings. In Hindu cosmology, the Nagas dwell in the subterranean realm of Patala (Naga Loka), a world of unimaginable beauty and wealth illuminated by the jewels on the Nagas' hoods. Shesha, the thousand-headed serpent king, serves as the couch upon which Vishnu reclines, his infinite hoods forming a canopy over the god. Vasuki was used as the churning rope during the Churning of the Ocean of Milk (Samudra Manthan). In the Mahabharata, the enmity between the Nagas and humans (particularly the Kuru dynasty) drives significant narrative arcs, most notably the snake sacrifice (Sarpa Satra) of King Janamejaya. In Buddhist tradition, the Naga king Mucalinda protected the meditating Buddha from a storm by spreading his hood over him—one of the most iconic images in Buddhist art. Across Southeast Asia, Nagas are deeply integrated into local belief systems: in Thailand and Laos, they are called Phaya Nak, believed to inhabit the Mekong River, and the mysterious Naga fireballs (bang fai phaya nak) that rise from the river during the end of Buddhist Lent are attributed to them. In Cambodia, Nagas are architectural guardians, their multi-headed forms adorning temple balustrades at Angkor Wat and other Khmer monuments. They are simultaneously water deities (associated with rain, rivers, and fertility), underworld guardians (protecting treasures and esoteric knowledge), and symbols of the bridge between the human and divine worlds.

Form

Nāgas are classically depicted in three principal forms: (1) entirely human with a halo of cobra hoods rising behind the head and snakes draped on the head and neck; (2) as common serpents, usually the Indian cobra (Naja naja); or (3) as half-human, half-serpent beings with a human torso and upper body joined to a serpentine lower body, common in Hindu and Buddhist iconography. The great naga kings are shown with multiple heads — most famously five, seven, or as many as a thousand (Shesha), each head often wearing an ornate crown with a jewel. In Southeast Asia (Java and Bali) they are rendered as crowned, giant magical serpents, sometimes winged, and in Bali are carved as the endless stair-railings of temples and the Ubud monkey forest. The female nāgī/nagini is the serpent-maiden, frequently the beautiful half-maiden, half-serpent bride of human heroes. A nāga's hood is studded with a luminescent gem (nāga-maṇi); the Brahma Purana describes the naga king illuminating the quarters by the thousand jewels on his hoods.

Powers

Shape-shifting (anthropomorphic transformation) Nāgas can assume fully human, partially human, or fully serpentine form at will. The Buddha tells one nāga that ordination is impossible in its animal body, but that it could earn rebirth as a human and become a monk. Mucalinda takes the form of a young Brahmin to pay homage to the Buddha; the nāga princess Soma of Funan appears as a human woman. The serpent-maiden lovers (Ulupi, Nagini) use this power to enter human society and marry heroes. Hindu epics & Buddhist sutras Form is not always freely controlled — an animal-form nāga cannot ordain (it needs rebirth); Nagini's curse in Harry Potter renders her snake form eventually permanent. Whether full humanity is a permanent transformation or a temporary disguise varies by source and by the individual nāga.

Elemental control — water, rain, storms, earth Nāgas are rain-bringers and storm-wielders. In Thailand they are 'patrons of water' and command rain; the Nak hai nam ('nāga granting water') oracle forecasts rainfall annually. The main article states all four great races of nāgas 'can set off storms, rain, tempest and create lands from the sea.' Paravataksha's sword causes earthquakes and his roar thunder. Creatures like Karkotaka and Takshaka control venom and weather. Pan-Hindu/Buddhist/folk Rain-giving is often seasonal and conditional on proper worship and clean environment. Water affinity vs. subterranean (Patala) dwelling — the nāga spans both realms.

Potent venom (Kālakēṭu / nāga poison) The nāga's venom is cosmically dangerous. Kaliya's poison boils the Yamuna for four leagues and kills all birds and beasts near the bank. Takshaka's venom reduces a banyan tree to ash on challenge. In the Samudra Manthana, Vasuki's churning exudes halāhala, a poison so potent it threatens all creation; Shiva drinks it, which turns his throat blue (the epithet Nīlakaṇṭha). This is the theological root of nāga astrology and snakebite cures centered on the goddess Manasa (Viṣahari, 'destroyer of poison'). Mahabharata, Bhagavata Purana, Puranas Poison can be rendered benign or cured by Manasa worship; Krishna burns off Kaliya's poison by dancing on his heads. Whether halāhala emerged from Vasuki's mouth or from the churned ocean varies across recensions.

Guardianship of treasure and secret knowledge Nāgas guard gems, gold and 'spiritual' or 'elementally-encoded' treasure in the underworld and under the seas. In Tibetan Buddhism they are guardians/owners of submerged treasure — both monetary wealth and spiritual terma encoded by adepts. The Prajñāpāramitā sutras were, by tradition, entrusted to a great nāga who guarded them in the sea until Nāgārjuna recovered them — the charter myth of Nagarjuna's name. Buddhist tradition, Tibetan Buddhism Treating the treasure with disrespect incurs wrath; disturbing a nāga's water is harmful. The relationship between the nāga's material treasure-hoarding and its esoteric patronage is not fixed across schools.

Accounts

After the sage Durvasa curses Indra and the devas to lose their fortune, the devas are defeated by the asuras and the gods lose the three worlds. Advised by Vishnu, the devas and asuras agree to churn the Ocean of Milk to obtain amrita, the nectar of immortality. Mount Mandara is uprooted as the churning rod, and Vasuki — the great nāga king who coils on Shiva's neck — agrees to serve as the churning rope after being promised a share. When the mountain sinks, Vishnu's Kurma (turtle) avatar supports it. The nāga's body produces the lethal halāhala poison as the churning grinds on; Shiva drinks it to save creation, his throat turning blue (Nīlakaṇṭha). From the ocean emerge the fourteen ratnas (gems, goddesses like Lakshmi, the apsaras, and others). The nāga is thus the pivot on which the entire cosmic churning turns — the rope that grinds out both the poison and the nectar of immortality.

King Parikshit of the Kuru dynasty, cursed to die of snakebite for killing a snake and mocking a meditating sage, is slain by Takshaka, the nāga king of the Khandava forest. His son Janamejaya vows to annihilate the entire serpent race and begins a great Sarpa Satra — a fire-sacrifice that draws countless nāgas into the flames. As the race is nearly wiped out, Vasuki sorrows for his kin and appeals to his nephew Astika, the young sage born of the Brahmin Jaratkaru and the nāga princess Manasa (Jaratkaru). Astika enters the sacrificial ground and, having earned a boon from the king for his erudition, halts the sacrifice — his command 'Stay, stay, stay' overrides the priests' mantras and holds Takshaka suspended above the fire. Janamejaya grants the boon, the sacrifice is stopped, and peace is brokered between the Kurus and the serpents. Astika also decrees that anyone reciting his story is immune to snakebite.

The venomous nāga Kaliya, driven from his home island of Ramanaka by fear of the serpent-devouring Garuda (who cannot enter Vrindavan due to a sage's curse), takes refuge in the Yamuna river, poisoning its waters for four leagues. Krishna, troubled by the serpent's terrorizing of Radha and the people, enters the river; Kaliya coils around him and drags him to the bottom. Krishna expands himself free, then springs onto Kaliya's hundred hoods, dancing with the weight of the whole universe and beating the venom out of the serpent. As Kaliya vomits blood and nears death, his wives pray to Krishna for mercy. Kaliya surrenders, promising never to harass anyone again, and Krishna, after a final dance, banishes him to Ramanaka (or per regional legend, to the deeps pushed into Patala, where he resides to this day). The event is celebrated as the Naga Nathaiyā/Naga Nritya dance.

Six (or five) weeks after enlightenment, as the Buddha meditates under the Bodhi Tree, a great storm rises and the heavens darken for seven days. King Mucalinda, the nāga king, comes up from beneath the earth, coils seven times around the Buddha's body, and spreads his great hood over him as a shelter of seven heads. When the storm clears, Mucalinda assumes the form of a young Brahmin and pays homage, circumambulating the Buddha three times before returning joyfully to his palace. The image of the Buddha sheltered under the nāga's hood is among the most iconic in Buddhist art, especially in Laos, and the first surviving depiction dates to a 2nd-century BC stupa at Pauni, Maharashtra.

According to Khmer tradition, an Indian Brahmin prince named Kaundinya I dreams that he must take a magic bow from a temple and defeat Soma (Liuye, 'Willow Leaf'), the nāga princess and daughter of the nāga king. In the battle that follows he and Soma fall in love and marry, establishing the royal lineage of the Funan dynasty; Kaundinya builds the capital city Vyadhapura, and the kingdom becomes Kambujadeśa ('Cambodia'). The love story of Preah Thong and Neang Neak is the foundation for standard Khmer wedding practice, and many Khmer regard themselves as descendants of the nāgas who will one day return to restore prosperity.

The nāga king Karkotaka, one of the sons of Kashyapa and Kadru, deceived the sage Narada in a game of chance and was cursed to remain stationary in a forest until saved by King Nala. During a forest fire, Nala rescues the thumb-sized Karkotaka, freeing him from the curse. In return the serpent stings Nala — transforming him into an ugly shape — to expel the asura Kali that has possessed the king, and assures Nala he will be undefeatable while the poison remains in his body. Karkotaka sends Nala to King Rituparna as the charioteer Bahuka, arranging the exchange of mantras that ultimately reunites Nala with his bride Damayanti.

In Indonesian tradition, at the beginning of time only the great serpent Antaboga existed. Antaboga meditated and co-created (or engendered) the world-turtle Bedawang, upon whose back the entire world rests. The serpent also produced two offspring, Bambang Naga Tatmala and Dewi Nagagini, and in Sundanese myth is responsible for the birth of Dewi Sri, the rice goddess, who emerges from an egg formed from Antaboga's tears. The myth is preserved in wayang performances that blend Indic philosophy with Javanese animism.

A way to hold it in your mind

Every year at the end of Buddhist Lent, on a stretch of the Mekong River that separates Thailand and Laos, thousands of people gather on the banks to watch pale red fireballs drift up from the dark water and vanish into the night sky. Whether they are the breath of a giant serpent or an unsolved physics problem depends entirely on whom you ask — but the serpent imagined there, the Phaya Nāk, is the same being that once coiled its seven great hoods over a meditating Buddha to shelter him from a storm, that was stretched as a rope to churn the ocean of milk for the nectar of immortality, and that nearly lost its entire race to a king's revenge-furnace in the Mahabharata. Half-human and half-serpent, jewel-hooded, master of poison and rain and buried treasure, the nāga has for two thousand years been the great threshold-dweller of South and Southeast Asia — the being that guards the boundary between water and land, human and divine, this world and the glittering underworld of Patala. It is remembered as both the savior of the cosmos and the near-victim of genocide, as a demon and as a god. That doubleness is the whole point of the nāga: no creature in Asian myth is more at home in the murky place where one thing becomes another.
