Form

Aphroditus is depicted as a figure with male genitalia and female secondary sexual characteristics (breasts), wearing female clothing. The most common visual representation shows the figure lifting or opening the robe to reveal the male genitals (anasyrma gesture). Terracotta figurines from Cyprus and Athens show this exact pose. Macrobius's testimony mentions a bearded statue in Cyprus, though this is the only source for a beard.

Powers

Fertility bestowal Like Aphrodite, Aphroditus was likely a fertility deity. The anasyrma gesture, revealing the genitals, was a ritual act believed to promote fertility and ward off evil. The combination of male and female elements may have represented a totality of generative power. Inferred from figurine iconography and comparative ritual practice; not explicitly stated in surviving texts. No explicit textual attestation of this power beyond the ritual context. Some scholars see this as a general fertility function; others see it as specifically apotropaic (evil-averting) rather than generative.

Apotropaic protection The anasyrma gesture was widely used in Greek and Near Eastern culture to avert evil, the evil eye, and misfortune. Aphroditus's statues may have served this protective function. Inferred from comparative iconography and ritual practice; not stated in surviving texts. No direct textual evidence. None documented.

Behavior & Habitat

As a cult figure, Aphroditus was associated with rituals involving gender-crossing. The anasyrma gesture — lifting the skirt to reveal genitals — is the key ritual act, connected to fertility and apotropaic (evil-averting) magic. In some Athenian rituals, women may have cross-dressed in connection with Aphroditus's cult.

Accounts

In Cyprus, particularly at Amathus, a deity called Aphroditus was worshipped. The figure had male genitals but wore female clothing. Macrobius records that sacrifices were offered to this god. The cult appears to have been a local Cypriot variation of Aphrodite worship, incorporating gender ambiguity into the goddess's sphere.

In Athens, the cult of Aphroditus was connected to the festival of the Adonia or, more likely, to a separate ritual where women cross-dressed. The connection between Aphroditus and the Adonia is debated; some scholars see Aphroditus as a figure in the Adonis cycle, while others see him as a separate deity. The anasyrma gesture appears in Athenian vase painting, suggesting ritual use.

Pseudo-Lucian describes a statue of Aphrodite in Cnidus that had male genitals. This statue is sometimes connected to Aphroditus, though the text calls it Aphrodite. The statue's mixed-gender presentation was noted as remarkable and was a tourist attraction.

Scholarly Debates
Independent deity vs. aspect of Aphrodite
Aphroditus was an independent Cypriot deity, a male god of fertility. ⇌ Aphroditus was a form or aspect of Aphrodite, the goddess worshipped in male guise.
Evidence for A: Macrobius treats him as a distinct god; Hesychius's gloss 'male Aphrodite' could imply a separate being.
Evidence for B: The name is a masculine form of Aphrodite; the cult is geographically tied to Aphrodite's major sanctuary at Paphos.
Relationship to Hermaphroditus
Aphroditus and Hermaphroditus are the same figure, with 'Aphroditus' being the earlier Cypriot form. ⇌ They are distinct figures: Aphroditus is a cultic deity; Hermaphroditus is a mythological character.

What We Don't Know

1. The exact nature of the cult ritual is unknown — we have only the figurine iconography and brief textual references. 2. The relationship between Aphroditus and Aphrodite is unresolved. 3. The connection to Athens is fragmentary. 4. No primary Cypriot-language sources survive. 5. The bearded statue mentioned by Macrobius is not archaeologically attested. 6. The gender of the worshippers and the specifics of the cross-dressing ritual are unclear.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Aphroditus as the ancient Greek answer to a question nobody asked: what if Aphrodite, the goddess of love and beauty, were also a man? The result is a deity who embodies the idea that the divine transcends human categories. If you've ever seen a statue of a figure who is clearly both male and female and wondered what that meant, Aphroditus is the god for you. He (she?) is a reminder that the gods are not bound by our rules.
