Who It Is

Imagine a god who lifts her skirt to show you his genitals — not as a joke, but as a sacred act of protection and fertility. That is Aphroditus: a Cypriot deity who was Aphrodite and not-Aphrodite, male and female, a figure who tore a hole in the tidy gender binary of ancient Greek religion and left scholars arguing about it for two thousand years. In the terracotta figurines, the deity stands with robe lifted, revealing male genitals beneath female clothing — a gesture called anasyrma, the same gesture Greek women used to ward off evil. This is not a myth about a hero's quest or a god's wrath; it is a cult of pure, radical ambiguity, a god who existed in the space between categories.

As a cult figure, Aphroditus was associated with rituals involving gender-crossing. The anasyrma gesture — lifting the skirt to reveal genitals — is the key ritual act, connected to fertility and apotropaic (evil-averting) magic. In some Athenian rituals, women may have cross-dressed in connection with Aphroditus's cult.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Masculine form of Aphrodite, the Greek goddess of love and beauty. The name is a grammatical gender-shift of the goddess's name, indicating a male or androgynous aspect.

Pronunciation — /æfroʊˈdaɪtəs/ (af-roh-DYE-təs)

Aliases — Aphroditos, Aphroditos of Cyprus, Aphrodite with the male genitals

Ownership — Ancient Greek; specifically associated with Cyprus (Amathus) and attested in Athens. The figure represents a cultic intersection of gender, fertility, and Aphrodite worship.

Powers
Fertility bestowal

Like Aphrodite, Aphroditus was likely a fertility deity. The anasyrma gesture, revealing the genitals, was a ritual act believed to promote fertility and ward off evil. The combination of male and female elements may have represented a totality of generative power.

Limit: No explicit textual attestation of this power beyond the ritual context.

Apotropaic protection

The anasyrma gesture was widely used in Greek and Near Eastern culture to avert evil, the evil eye, and misfortune. Aphroditus's statues may have served this protective function.

Limit: No direct textual evidence.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Aphroditus is depicted as a figure with male genitalia and female secondary sexual characteristics (breasts), wearing female clothing. The most common visual representation shows the figure lifting or opening the robe to reveal the male genitals (anasyrma gesture). Terracotta figurines from Cyprus and Athens show this exact pose. Macrobius's testimony mentions a bearded statue in Cyprus, though this is the only source for a beard.

Symbols — Male genitals (revealed via anasyrma), female clothing, robe, Cyprus (the island itself). The gesture of lifting the robe is the primary symbolic act.

Domains — Fertility, sexuality, gender ambiguity, Cyprus, Aphrodite worship. The figure is primarily associated with Cyprus (Amathus) and Athens.

Accounts I
The Cyprian CultMacrobius, Saturnalia 3.8; Hesychius; Etymologicum Magnum

Attested in Hellenistic and Roman sources; likely older in origin

In Cyprus, particularly at Amathus, a deity called Aphroditus was worshipped. The figure had male genitals but wore female clothing. Macrobius records that sacrifices were offered to this god. The cult appears to have been a local Cypriot variation of Aphrodite worship, incorporating gender ambiguity into the goddess's sphere. The cult influenced Athenian practice, where a connection to Aphroditus is attested. The figure's existence complicates modern understanding of gender in ancient Greek religion.

Accounts II
The Athenian ConnectionPhilostratus, Imagines; modern scholarly reconstruction

Classical to Roman periods

In Athens, the cult of Aphroditus was connected to the festival of the Adonia or, more likely, to a separate ritual where women cross-dressed. The connection between Aphroditus and the Adonia is debated; some scholars see Aphroditus as a figure in the Adonis cycle, while others see him as a separate deity. The anasyrma gesture appears in Athenian vase painting, suggesting ritual use. The Athenian cult may have been a space for exploring gender roles within a religious context.

The Cnidian StatuePseudo-Lucian, Amores 15-16

2nd-3rd century CE

Pseudo-Lucian describes a statue of Aphrodite in Cnidus that had male genitals. This statue is sometimes connected to Aphroditus, though the text calls it Aphrodite. The statue's mixed-gender presentation was noted as remarkable and was a tourist attraction. The statue's existence shows that gender-ambiguous Aphrodite imagery was known in the broader Greek world.

Aphroditus is the same as Hermaphroditus. — They are likely distinct figures. Aphroditus is a Cypriot cultic deity; Hermaphroditus is a mythological character from Caria. The equation is a late conflation.

Aphroditus was a transgender goddess. — Modern categories of gender identity do not map neatly onto ancient cultic figures. Aphroditus is best understood as a deity embodying gender ambiguity within the context of Aphrodite worship.

Aphroditus was a major pan-Hellenic deity. — Aphroditus is attested primarily in Cyprus and Athens, and the evidence is fragmentary. The figure was likely a local or regional cult.
