Who It Is

When the ocean of milk is churned in Hindu myth, it is not only nectar that rises from its depths but a radiant woman seated on a lotus—Lakṣmī, Fortune herself, whose choice of Viṣṇu reshapes the fate of gods and demons. To follow her through Vedic hymns, epic battles, tantric dialogues, and domestic rituals is to watch prosperity become a person, a power, and a moral force that can bless a household or abandon a king.

Purāṇic and Vaiṣṇava theological texts portray Lakṣmī as compassionate, gracious, and mediating. In Śrīvaiṣṇava thought she is puruṣakāra—the one who intercedes for devotees, softening Viṣṇu’s justice with mercy. She embodies auspiciousness (maṅgala) and is said to favor the righteous, aligning prosperity with dharma. At the same time, some narratives warn that fortune is fickle, and Śrī may depart from the arrogant or adharmic, as in stories where royal fortune leaves unjust kings. Her personality is thus both nurturing and morally discerning. These traits are attested in Purāṇic narrative

Powers
Bestowal of wealth and material prosperity

Lakṣmī is widely attested as the giver of wealth, success, and material abundance. The Śrī Sūkta and Saubhāgya Lakṣmī Upaniṣad invoke her to grant gold, cattle, grain, and prosperity. Purāṇic narratives and ritual texts associate her worship with flourishing households and kingdoms.

Limit: Prosperity is conditioned on dharma and proper worship; texts warn that Lakṣmī may depart from the unjust, arrogant, or negligent. Wealth without righteousness is portrayed as unstable.

Embodiment of royal fortune (Śrī) and legitimacy

In epic and Purāṇic literature, Lakṣmī/Śrī personifies royal fortune and sovereignty. Her presence with a king signifies legitimate rule; her departure signals decline. She is invoked in coronation rituals and royal hymns.

Limit: Royal fortune is contingent on adherence to dharma; kings who violate moral order risk losing Śrī.

Cosmic creative, sustaining, and dissolving power (Śakti)

The Lakṣmī Tantra and related Pāñcarātra texts present Lakṣmī as the supreme Śakti of Nārāyaṇa, the source of pure creation (śuddha-sattva), maintenance, and dissolution. She is the dynamic power through which the static divine manifests the universe.

Limit: In many Vaiṣṇava systems, her power is inseparable from Viṣṇu and does not operate independently in a dualistic sense, though some tantric readings emphasize her relative autonomy.

Mediatrix and intercessor (puruṣakāra)

Śrīvaiṣṇava theology assigns Lakṣmī the role of mediatrix between devotees and Viṣṇu. She is said to intercede, making the Lord accessible and tempering his justice with mercy, particularly in doctrines of surrender (śaraṇāgati).

Limit: Her mediating role is doctrinally emphasized in specific schools and is not universal across all Hindu traditions.

Granting auspiciousness, fertility, and beauty

Lakṣmī is invoked for auspicious beginnings, marital harmony, fertility, and aesthetic grace. Hymns praise her as beautiful and fertile, and domestic rituals seek her presence for harmonious households.

Limit: Texts often link auspiciousness to ethical conduct and ritual purity.

Withdrawal of fortune and participation in cosmic cycles

Narratives imply that Lakṣmī can withdraw fortune from those who misuse power, contributing to cycles of rise and fall in cosmic and historical time. Her emergence from and potential return to the ocean of milk symbolize the cyclical nature of prosperity.

Limit: This is more an interpretive theological motif than an explicit enumerated power; texts suggest it through narrative rather than doctrinal formulae.

Accounts I
The Churning of the Ocean (Samudra-manthana) and the Emergence of LakṣmīVishnu Purana I.9; Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.8–8.9; other Purāṇas

Classical and early medieval (c. 4th–10th century CE)

The gods, weakened by Durvāsas’ curse and oppressed by the Dānavas, seek Viṣṇu’s aid. He instructs them to churn the ocean of milk with the demons to obtain amṛta (nectar). During the churning, various treasures and beings arise, including Lakṣmī, radiant and lotus-born. Praised by the gods, she chooses Viṣṇu as her consort, becoming Śrī, Fortune, and the ornament of the universe. Her emergence marks the restoration of divine order and prosperity. Lakṣmī’s union with Viṣṇu reestablishes cosmic balance and prosperity; the gods regain strength and eventually defeat the demons. The myth grounds Lakṣmī’s identity as goddess of wealth and auspiciousness and explains her close association with Viṣṇu.

Durvāsas’ Garland and the Loss of FortuneVishnu Purana I.9 (Legend of Lakṣmī)

Classical (c. 4th–6th century CE)

The sage Durvāsas, in a state of religious frenzy, gives a divine garland to Indra, who carelessly places it on his elephant. The elephant throws the garland to the ground, disrespecting the gift that housed Fortune (Śrī). Durvāsas curses Indra, causing the gods to lose their power and fortune, leading to their oppression by the demons and setting the stage for the churning of the ocean. The curse results in the gods’ loss of Śrī/Lakṣmī, symbolizing the departure of fortune due to arrogance and disrespect. This narrative underscores the moral dimension of prosperity and the need to honor divine gifts.

Accounts II
Lakṣmī as Sītā in the RāmāyaṇaValmiki Rāmāyaṇa (various passages)

Classical (c. 3rd century BCE–2nd century CE)

Sītā, wife of Rāma (an avatāra of Viṣṇu), is repeatedly likened to or identified with Lakṣmī. Her beauty, virtue, and suffering are framed as the earthly manifestation of the goddess of fortune accompanying Viṣṇu’s incarnation. This identification reinforces the pattern that Lakṣmī descends with Viṣṇu in his avatāras. The identification sacralizes Sītā’s story, linking human marital and ethical drama to cosmic patterns of divine consortship and fortune. It shapes later devotional and theological readings of both Rāma and Lakṣmī.

Lakṣmī as Rukmiṇī and Other Consorts in the Mahābhārata and PurāṇasMahābhārata; various Purāṇas

Classical and medieval

In narratives of Kṛṣṇa and other avatāras of Viṣṇu, consorts such as Rukmiṇī are identified with Lakṣmī. These stories portray Lakṣmī accompanying Viṣṇu in his incarnations, maintaining the pattern of divine partnership and fortune. The identification reinforces Lakṣmī’s role as inseparable from Viṣṇu and as the embodiment of fortune in different cosmic and historical contexts.

Domestic Myths of Lakṣmī’s Visit on Dīpāvalī and KojāgariRitual manuals and vernacular folklore

Medieval to modern

Household stories narrate that Lakṣmī visits clean, lamp-lit homes on Dīpāvalī night, bestowing prosperity on those who remain awake and worship her. Kojāgari myths similarly describe her favoring those who keep vigil and honor her with offerings. These stories shape domestic practice, encouraging cleanliness, light, and wakefulness as signs of devotion and readiness to receive fortune.
