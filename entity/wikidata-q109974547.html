Who It Is

At the edge of the World of Light a river flows that is not water. It is called Piriawis, the great heavenly Yardna, and no soul can cross it without being seen. On its banks stand two watchers who are never named apart: Shilmai and Nidbai, the uthras who guard the living water itself.

Behavior is consistently guardian and liturgical, not narrative-dramatic. Nidbai, with Shilmai, watches (naṭar) over Piriawis, maintains its purity, and witnesses rituals performed in its earthly counterpart. In Qulasta prayers, they are invoked as present and listening: they guard the Yardna so that baptism is efficacious and so that souls ascending after death are not detained. They are benevolent, vigilant, and obedient to the Great Life (Mana Rba). They do not act as tricksters, judges, or warriors; they do not fall, rebel, or punish. Powers
Guardianship of Piriawis (Naṭrutha d-Piriawis)

Joint watch over the heavenly Yardna, preventing pollution, intrusion by Ruha and her offspring, and unauthorized crossing. The river's purity guarantees the efficacy of earthly baptism and the ascent of souls. Invocation of their names seals the Yardna as ritually fit.

Limit: Power is custodial, not creative or destructive. They do not create Piriawis, control its flow, or judge souls themselves; they guard what the Great Life established. Cannot act outside Piriawis domain.

Witness and Validation of Ritual Purity

Presence as witnesses (sahdia) that a baptism or masiqta performed in the Yardna is valid and reaches the World of Light. Priests invoke Shilmai and Nidbai at the opening of every maṣbuta so that the living water is connected to Piriawis.

Limit: Efficacy depends on proper priesthood, flowing water, and northward orientation; guardians do not validate defective ritual alone. They witness, they do not forgive sins independently.

Protection of Ascending Souls at the Water Crossing

In eschatological ascent literature, the soul (nishmata) after death must cross Piriawis and pass mataraṭa (watch-houses). Nidbai and Shilmai ensure the river is passable for the righteous and impassable for the unworthy without the proper kushta (truth) and seal.

Limit: They are not judges like Abatur or gatekeepers like Adathan and Yadathan at the Gate of Mercies; they do not weigh deeds. Their role is to keep the crossing itself secure.

Invocatory Intercession (Burkta)

When named in prayers, they are asked to bring blessing, to remember the petitioner before the Great Life, and to keep the Yardna pure for the community. Not worshipped themselves but venerated as intermediaries.

Limit: Mandaeism is strictly monotheistic toward Mana Rba / Great Life; uthras are not independent gods and cannot grant salvation alone. Intercession is always 'by the word of Life.'

Apotropaic Protection via Name Inscription

Inscription of the names Shilmai and Nidbai on lead amulets (qirasa), bowls, and manuscript colophons for protection of individuals, especially children and priests, and for healing. Name itself is considered protective.

Limit: Apotropaic use is secondary and not described in Ginza cosmology; efficacy is folk-religious, not doctrinal. No attested offensive magic.

Accounts I
The Two Guardians Set Over PiriawisGinza Rabba, Right Ginza Book 3 and Book 15.5

Late antiquity, codified by 7th c. CE; manuscripts 16th c. onward

In the cosmogonic-cosmological sections of the Ginza, after the emanation of the Lightworlds and the establishment of Piriawis as the great Yardna of the World of Light, the Great Life (Mana Rba) appoints Shilmai and Nidbai as uthria naṭria - the two guardian uthras - over its banks. The text describes Piriawis as flowing from the House of Perfection, its waters white and radiant, and states that it is guarded so that no defilement from the lower worlds (Earth, Ruha's realm) can pollute it. The pair are named together in a list of uthras who hold stations in the Lightworld, immediately after Yushamin and Abatur. No separate creation story for Nidbai is given; his origin is as an emanated uthra of Light, not born or created by Ptahil. Establishes the theological basis for why every earthly Yardna must be guarded and invoked before baptism; Piriawis becomes the archetype of all baptismal water. The pairing becomes formulaic for all subsequent liturgy.

Invocation at the Opening of the QulastaQulasta (Canonical Prayerbook) Prayers 1 and 3 - Daily Prayers and Yardna Consecration

Liturgical core late antiquity; Drower edition 1959 from 17th-19th c. manuscripts

Every Mandaean daily office and every baptism begins with the priest standing at the Yardna facing north, reciting: 'In the name of the Great Life, from the World of Light that is above all works... Shilmai and Nidbai, uthras who guard Piriawis, the great Yardna of the Life, are established.' The prayer continues to ask that the earthly Yardna be joined to Piriawis, that its waters be living, and that the guardians witness the act. Nidbai is not addressed alone but always with Shilmai, and the congregation responds with 'and praised be the Life.' This is not a mythic narrative but a performative story - the ritual re-enacts the heavenly guardianship on earth. Makes Nidbai a daily presence in living Mandaean religion; his name is among the first learned by children. Liturgical repetition ensures textual preservation.

The Crossing of the Soul Over PiriawisLeft Ginza Book 3 (Book of Souls), chapters on the ascent; Diwan Abatur

Late antiquity; Left Ginza compilation 6th-7th c.

After death, the soul (nishmata) rises northward, accompanied by a lightbeing (often Hibil Ziwa or Anush Uthra), and comes to Piriawis. The text describes the soul's fear at the wide, radiant water. Shilmai and Nidbai are stationed there; they do not interrogate the soul about sins (that is done at the mataraṭa), but they ensure the river is calm for those bearing the seal (kušṭa), the alms (zidqa), and the baptismal name. The soul is given to drink from Piriawis or is carried across. In the Diwan Abatur scroll, this is depicted visually: a river band with two labeled guardians, and the soul in a boat or on a bridge of light. The unworthy without kushta cannot cross and are turned back to the watch-houses. Defines Nidbai's eschatological role as facilitator of salvation, not judge. Reinforces Mandaean emphasis on baptism as passport for ascent.

Accounts II
The Baptismal Liturgy: Making the Yardna One with PiriawisQulasta Prayers 15-35 (Maṣbuta liturgy) and The Mandaean Book of John, chapter on John the Baptist at the Yardna

Liturgical, continuous use from late antiquity to present

When Yahya Yuhana (John the Baptist) baptizes in the earthly Yardna, the liturgy states that Shilmai and Nidbai are present invisibly, holding the heavenly Yardna open so that the living water flows between worlds. The priest says, 'Shilmai and Nidbai guard Piriawis, and Piriawis comes and mingles with this Yardna.' The story is both etiological (why baptism works) and ongoing (every baptism repeats John's archetypal baptism). In the Book of John, John questions the Lightbeings about the Yardna, and is told of its guardians. Links Nidbai directly to John the Baptist's authority and to the central sacrament of Mandaeism. Explains why Mandaeans must live near flowing water.

The Watch at the North: Nidbai Among the Stations of LightDiwan Abatur and Ginza Rabba Right Ginza Book 5.3 (Description of Lightworld stations)

Medieval scroll tradition based on late antique cosmology

In the elaborate Lightworld geography, each uthra has a škinta (dwelling) or station. Shilmai and Nidbai's station is at the bank of Piriawis, at the threshold between the higher Lightworlds (House of Perfection, Abatur's house) and the lower creations. Travelers such as Hibil Ziwa on his descent to the underworld, and later the ascending souls, pass their post. The Diwan lists them among the 'uthras of the north' who never leave their watch. Unlike Yushamin who rebels or Ptahil who creates imperfectly, Shilmai and Nidbai are examples of perfect obedience - they remain at their post eternally. Provides moral exemplar of steadfastness; used in priestly instruction (tarmida initiation) about duty. Contrasts faithful guardians with fallen or flawed creators.

The Masiqta: Guardians Witness the Ascent MealQulasta Prayers 49-77 (Masiqta - Death Mass) and Haran Gawaita (Scroll of Great Revelation)

Masiqta liturgy late antiquity; Haran Gawaita 7th-10th c.

During the masiqta, the ritual meal for the dead that helps the soul ascend, priests recite a long litany of Lightworld beings who are invited to the ritual table. Shilmai and Nidbai are invoked as guardians of Piriawis to open the way so that the food (pihta, mambuha) and prayers can reach the soul in the Lightworld. The Haran Gawaita, a historical narrative of Mandaean migrations, mentions that even during persecution and exile, priests carried the names of Shilmai and Nidbai as part of the portable Yardna blessing, allowing ritual continuity without a fixed temple. Shows Nidbai's role extends beyond baptism to death rites; ensures community continuity in diaspora. Haran Gawaita link explains survival of invocation outside Mesopotamia.

Nidbai and Shilmai are a single combined angel named 'Shilmai-Nidbai' — They are two distinct uthras who are always paired liturgically, like Adathan and Yadathan. Mandaean texts list them as 'Shilmai and Nidbai, the two guardians.' Counting them as one collapses a deliberate dyad structure central to Mandaean cosmology.

Nidbai is a fallen angel or has a dark/evil aspect — No Mandaean source depicts Nidbai as fallen, rebellious, or punitive. Unlike Yushamin who rebels or Ptahil who errs, Nidbai is an exemplar of perfect obedience and remains at his post. He is benevolent and protective.

Nidbai appears in the Bible, Quran, or Manichaean scriptures — Nidbai is attested only in Mandaean literature (Ginza, Qulasta, Diwan Abatur, Book of John). He does not appear in Jewish, Christian, Islamic, or Manichaean canonical texts. Manichaeism has a structural parallel (Xroshtag and Padvaxtag) but not the same figure.

Nidbai is a major deity or object of worship for Mandaeans — Mandaeans worship only the Great Life (Mana Rba). Uthras like Nidbai are venerated, invoked as witnesses and guardians, and asked for blessing, but not worshipped as gods. Prayer is always 'in the name of the Great Life.'

The name Nidbai means 'guardian of the river' in Mandaic — No published Mandaic dictionary derives Nidbai from a root meaning guardian or river. The word for guardian is naṭra; river is yardna/Piriawis. Etymology of Nidbai is unknown/uncertain; Drower & Macuch list it as a proper name without etymology.
