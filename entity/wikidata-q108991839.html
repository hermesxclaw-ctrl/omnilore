Who It Is

You are not built from a blueprint. You are built from a library of 20,000 recipes written in a four-letter alphabet, copied, edited, spliced and silenced by a committee of chemical editors — and we have spent 160 years arguing about what counts as a single recipe. That recipe is the gene.

Genes do not have personality or intent. Their 'behavior' is chemical: they are transcribed by RNA polymerase into RNA, regulated by transcription factors, chromatin state (methylation, histone modification), and non-coding RNAs. Expression is tissue-specific, developmental stage-specific, and environmentally responsive. Metaphorical language ('selfish gene', 'controlling', 'blueprint') is anthropomorphism; Dawkins' 'selfish' refers to selection dynamics, not motive. Powers
Protein coding (mRNA transcription and translation)

Transcribed by RNA polymerase II into pre-mRNA, 5'-capped, polyadenylated, spliced, exported and translated by ribosomes into a polypeptide that folds into a functional protein (enzyme, structural protein, signaling molecule).

Limit: Requires promoter, enhancers, splice signals, ribosome binding; subject to nonsense-mediated decay if premature stop codon; not all transcripts are translated; pseudogenes may be transcribed but non-functional.

Non-coding RNA production

Genes encoding functional RNAs that are not translated: ribosomal RNA (rRNA), transfer RNA (tRNA), microRNA (miRNA), small nuclear RNA (snRNA), long non-coding RNA (lncRNA), etc., which perform catalysis, scaffolding, regulation.

Limit: Function must be demonstrated; many transcripts are transcriptional noise/junk RNA without biological function and do not qualify as genes under function-based definitions (Kampourakis 2017).

Regulation of other genes

Genes encoding transcription factors, repressors, and regulatory RNAs that bind gene-associated regulatory regions to activate or silence other genes (e.g., lac repressor, HOX genes, TP53).

Limit: Regulatory effect is indirect via product diffusion; regulatory DNA sequences themselves are not part of the gene but are gene-associated regions.

Inheritance and variation (Mendelian function)

Segregates as discrete unit during meiosis; alleles at a locus produce phenotypic variation; recombination, mutation, and drift change allele frequencies, driving evolution.

Limit: Linkage, incomplete penetrance, polygenic traits, epigenetic inheritance, and environmental effects mean single genes rarely determine traits alone; most traits are quantitative.

Mutability and evolvability

Can acquire point mutations, indels, copy-number variation, transposition; creates new alleles, alternative splicing patterns, or de novo genes from non-coding sequence.

Limit: Most mutations are neutral or deleterious; de novo gene birth requires ORF plus transcription plus functional validation per strict criteria (Van Oss & Carvunis 2019).

Form & Signs

Appearance — A gene has no visible appearance at human scale. At molecular scale, it is a linear sequence of nucleotides — adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G), cytosine (C) in DNA (uracil U replaces T in RNA transcripts) — arranged as a double helix ~2 nm diameter. A typical human protein-coding gene's transcribed region averages ~62,000 base pairs (including introns), but coding sequence averages only ~1,346 bp; the longest, TTN (titin), spans 304,814 bp;

Symbols — Standard icon is the DNA double helix, often stylized as a twisted ladder, or a chromosome ideogram with a highlighted band. In textbooks, genes are depicted as arrows or boxes on a DNA line, with exons as filled boxes and introns as thin lines, promoter as bent arrow. HGNC symbol: italicized uppercase (e.g., BRCA1) for human genes, with protein non-italicized. No traditional cultural symbol; modern symbolic shorthand includes 'GATC' le

Domains — Genes inhabit genomes in all cellular life and many viruses. In eukaryotes: primarily in nuclear chromosomes (linear, chromatin-packaged), plus small circular genomes in mitochondria (37 genes in humans) and plastids in plants. In prokaryotes: single circular chromosome plus plasmids, often organized in operons (multiple genes co-transcribed as polycistronic mRNA). Accounts I
Mendel's Peas and the Discrete Factor (1856-1866)Mendel, G. Versuche über Pflanzen-Hybriden, Verhandlungen des naturforschenden Vereines in Brünn

1856-1866; rediscovered 1900

Gregor Mendel, Augustinian friar in Brno, crosses 8,000 pea plants (Pisum sativum) tracking seven traits (seed shape, color, etc.). He finds 3:1 ratio in F2 and 9:3:3:1 for dihybrids, inferring invisible paired 'Elemente' that segregate and assort independently. Work ignored until 1900 when de Vries, Correns, and Tschermak independently rediscover it. Johannsen later renames Elemente as 'genes'. Foundation of genetics; establishes particulate inheritance vs. blending inheritance; enables Johannsen's genotype/phenotype distinction and later chromosome theory.

The White-Eyed Fly and Genes on Chromosomes (1910-1915)Morgan, T.H. et al. The Mechanism of Mendelian Heredity (1915); Morgan 1910 Science paper on white-eyed Drosophila

1910-1928

Thomas Hunt Morgan, initially skeptical of Mendel, finds white-eyed male Drosophila melanogaster mutant. Crosses show sex-linked inheritance, mapping gene for white (w) to X chromosome. With Sturtevant, Bridges, and Muller, constructs first genetic linkage maps, proves genes are linear arrays on chromosomes, and discovers crossing over, nondisjunction, and position effects. Chromosome theory of inheritance; gene = physical locus; enables mapping and later molecular identification.

One Gene, One Enzyme (1941)Beadle & Tatum, PNAS 27:499-506; follow-up work with Neurospora crassa

1941-1959

George Beadle and Edward Tatum irradiate bread mold Neurospora, creating nutritional mutants each requiring a specific amino acid. Each mutant corresponds to a defective enzyme in a metabolic pathway, leading to hypothesis: one gene specifies one enzyme (later one polypeptide). Supported by Garrod's inborn errors of metabolism (1902). Connects genes to biochemistry; earns 1958 Nobel Prize; later modified to one gene-one polypeptide and one gene-one transcript due to non-coding genes and multimeric proteins.

Accounts II
The Double Helix and the Cracking of the Code (1953-1966)Watson & Crick Nature 1953; Crick et al. Nature 1961; Nirenberg & Matthaei 1961; Human genome sequencing

1953-1966

Watson and Crick model DNA's double helix, revealing how genes copy. Crick's central dogma (1957): DNA → RNA → protein. Nirenberg, Matthaei, Khorana and Holley decipher 64 codons. Discovery of mRNA (Brenner, Jacob, Meselson 1961), tRNA, and introns (Roberts & Sharp 1977, Nobel 1993) shows genes are split and regulated. Regulatory genes proposed by Jacob & Monod 1961. Molecular definition of gene solidified as transcribed DNA; enables genetic engineering, sequencing, and modern genomics.

The Human Genome Project and the Gene Count Collapse (1990-2003)International Human Genome Sequencing Consortium Nature 2001; Venter et al. Science 2001

1990-2003 (draft 2000, complete 2003, finished T2T 2022)

13-year, $3 billion effort to sequence 3.2 billion base pairs of Homo sapiens. Pre-project estimates predicted 60,000-100,000 genes; early draft found ~30,000-40,000; refined annotation now ~19,900-21,000 protein-coding genes plus ~17,000-20,000 non-coding RNA genes. Revealed that protein-coding genes occupy only ~1.2% of genome, transcribed regions ~35-40%, and vast non-coding regulatory and repetitive DNA. Revised view of human complexity not from gene number but regulation and splicing; enabled GWAS, personalized medicine, and ENCODE follow-up; showed gene density far lower than expected.

ENCODE, Junk DNA, and the Definition War (2012-present)ENCODE Project Consortium Nature 2012; Graur et al. Genome Biology and Evolution 2013 critique; Gerstein et al. Genome Research 2007 definition

2012-present

ENCODE claimed 80.4% of human genome has biochemical function (transcribed, bound, etc.), implying many more genes. Critics (Dan Graur, Michael Eisen) argued biochemical activity ≠ selected function, and evolutionary conservation suggests only 5-10% functional. Debate reignited what counts as a gene: Gerstein's operational 'union of overlapping functional products' vs. Kampourakis' functional product definition requiring proven biological role. Parallel debate over de novo genes and pervasive transcription noise. No single gene definition satisfies all cases (overlapping genes, alternative splicing, trans-splicing, RNA viruses); textbooks now list multiple definitions; highlights that gene is a conceptual tool, not a fixed physical object.

One gene-one trait / one gene-one protein?Popular and early scientific view: each gene controls one trait or makes one protein (Beadle & Tatum one gene-one enzyme).One gene can produce many products via alternative splicing, RNA editing, and post-translational modification; most traits are polygenic and influenced by environment.

You have a gene 'for' blue eyes, height, intelligence, or criminality. — Most traits are polygenic (hundreds of genes) plus environment. Even eye color involves ~16 genes (HERC2, OCA2 major). Genes encode proteins/RNAs, not traits directly; alleles influence probability, rarely determine outcome alone.

Genes are destiny — your genome determines who you will be. — Penetrance and expressivity vary; epigenetics, development, environment, chance, and culture shape phenotype. Identical twins (same genes) diverge in health and personality. Regulatory networks modulate expression.

Humans have ~100,000 genes and most DNA is genes. — Humans have ~19,900-21,000 protein-coding genes (GENCODE v44: 19,370). Protein-coding sequence is ~1.2% of genome; transcribed regions ~35-40%; ~98% is non-coding, including regulatory, structural, and repetitive DNA.

A gene is always a stretch of DNA that makes a protein. — Since the 1960s, genes include non-coding RNA genes (rRNA, tRNA, miRNA, lncRNA) that never make protein but are functional. Strict protein-only definition is outdated but persists in some textbooks and articles (e.g., 2021 American Scientist piece critiqued).

If we sequence your genes we can predict everything about you, and CRISPR can easily edit any trait. — Sequencing predicts monogenic risks well, but polygenic prediction is probabilistic (polygenic scores). CRISPR editing is precise at DNA level but off-target effects, delivery, and polygenic architecture make trait editing complex and ethically fraught.

Junk DNA is useless and ENCODE proved it all has function. — 'Junk' originally meant non-coding, not useless; some non-coding is regulatory, but much is transposons, pseudogenes, and transcriptional noise without selected function. ENCODE's biochemical activity ≠ biological function under selection.
