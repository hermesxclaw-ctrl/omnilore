The Approach

In the early morning of August 6, 1945, a B-29 bomber named Enola Gay released a single bomb over Hiroshima. It was called 'Little Boy,' a name that belied its devastating power, marking the first use of a nuclear weapon in warfare and changing the course of history forever.

Content advisory. This dossier discusses the atomic bombing of Hiroshima, including mass casualties, destruction, and the long-term effects of radiation. It also touches on the moral and ethical debates surrounding the use of nuclear weapons.
Identity

Little Boy

Pronunciation LIT-ul BOY

Known As

Mark IL-11Thin ManSkinny One

Etymology

The name 'Little Boy' is believed to be an allusion to the 'Thin Man' design, as it was a smaller, shorter version. The exact origin is disputed; some attribute it to Robert Serber, while others suggest it was coined by Air Force representatives for security reasons.

Cultural Ownership

United States (Manhattan Project), now part of world historical heritage

Disputed Identifications

There are conflicting accounts of how the bomb was named. Robert Serber claimed he named the designs based on shapes, but Norman Ramsey's history suggests the names originated from Air Force representatives. The reconciliation is that Serber coined the names and the Air Force adopted them for their own security purposes.

Description

Appearance

Little Boy was a gun-type fission bomb, approximately 10 feet long and 28 inches in diameter, weighing about 9,700 pounds. It had a cylindrical shape with a tail section for stability during free fall.

Symbols & Iconography

The bomb was often depicted in wartime propaganda and later in historical memorials. It was painted with a simple exterior, and some crew members wrote messages on it before the mission.

Behaviour & Personality

As an inanimate object, it had no behavior. However, its design was optimized for a specific explosive yield and delivery method.

Domains & Habitats

It was designed to be carried by a B-29 Superfortress and was stored and assembled on Tinian Island before the mission.

Version Differences

Little Boy was the Mark I design, distinct from the Mark II (Thin Man, abandoned) and Mark III (Fat Man). It used a gun-type mechanism with uranium-235, unlike the implosion-type Fat Man which used plutonium.

Powers & Abilities

Nuclear Explosion

Source Version All sources agree on the yield.

Description

Little Boy produced a yield of approximately 15 kilotons of TNT (63 TJ), creating a massive explosion, intense heat, and radiation.

Limitations

The design was inefficient; less than 1 kg of the 64 kg of uranium underwent fission.

Contradictions

Some sources may vary slightly on the exact yield, but 15 kilotons is widely accepted.

Gun-type Fission Mechanism

Source Version This is the documented design.

Description

The bomb fired a hollow cylinder of enriched uranium (the 'bullet') down a barrel into a solid cylinder (the 'target') to achieve critical mass.

Limitations

The mechanism was simpler but less efficient than implosion; it was never tested before use.

Contradictions

None found.

Stories & Myths

The Bombing of Hiroshima

Source Numerous historical accounts, including the official Manhattan Project history and memoirs of crew members.Culture American and JapanesePeriod World War II, 1945

Summary

On August 6, 1945, the B-29 Enola Gay, piloted by Colonel Paul Tibbets, dropped Little Boy on Hiroshima. The bomb exploded at an altitude of about 1,900 feet, causing massive destruction and an estimated 70,000-80,000 immediate deaths, with many more from radiation and injuries in the following months.

Participants

Enola Gay crew

Hiroshima residents

Japanese military and government

Consequences

The bombing led to Japan's surrender and the end of World War II. It also initiated the nuclear age and ongoing debates about the ethics of nuclear weapons.

Variants

There are differing accounts of the exact death toll and the decision-making process, but the event itself is well-documented.

Development and Testing

Source Manhattan Project records, including the MHD and personal accounts.Culture AmericanPeriod 1942-1945

Summary

Little Boy was developed as a simpler alternative to the plutonium gun-type design, which was abandoned due to predetonation concerns. The uranium gun design was never tested before use, as it was considered almost certain to work.

Participants

Francis Birch

William Parsons

Robert Oppenheimer

Emilio Segrè

Consequences

The untested design proved effective, but its inefficiency was noted. The success of Little Boy led to the development of more efficient weapons.

Variants

Some accounts emphasize the confidence in the design, while others note the risk taken in using an untested weapon.

Mass casualties

Source Hiroshima Peace Memorial Museum and historical studies

Description

The explosion and subsequent radiation killed an estimated 70,000-80,000 people immediately, with total deaths by the end of 1945 reaching perhaps 140,000.

Scholarly Context

Historians debate the exact figures, but the scale of human suffering is undisputed.

Radiation effects

Source Medical studies and survivor testimonies

Description

Survivors (hibakusha) suffered from acute radiation syndrome, increased cancer rates, and social stigma.

Scholarly Context

The long-term health effects have been extensively studied.

Ethical controversy

Source Historical and ethical analyses

Description

The decision to use the bomb has been criticized as a war crime by some, while defended as necessary to end the war quickly and save lives.

Scholarly Context

This remains a deeply debated topic in history and ethics.
