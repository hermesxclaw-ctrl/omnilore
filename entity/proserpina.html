Who It Is

Imagine the earth splitting open beneath your feet, not as a metaphor, but as a literal event. The god of the dead, in a chariot pulled by black horses, seizes you and drags you down into a realm of shadows and silence. Your mother, the goddess of all that grows, hears your scream and lets the world wither in her grief. This is the story of Proserpina, a goddess who is both the bride of death and the promise of spring, a figure whose annual return from the underworld is the reason flowers bloom and grain rises from the soil. She is the original 'persephone' of myth, a woman who was taken, who ate of the underworld's fruit, and who became a queen of two realms, forever caught between the darkness and the light.

Proserpina embodies a dual nature. As the maiden (Kore), she is playful, innocent, and associated with the carefree act of gathering flowers. As the Queen of the Underworld, she is solemn, powerful, and just. She is not a cruel goddess, but a necessary one, presiding over the realm of the dead with an unyielding, though not malicious, authority. Her annual return to the upper world is marked by a joyous, life-giving energy, making her a symbol of cyclical renewal.

Powers
Queen of the Underworld

Proserpina rules the underworld alongside Pluto. She has authority over the dead and can grant or withhold favors to souls. In Roman literature, she is often invoked in oaths and curses, as her power is absolute in her realm.

Limit: She is bound to the underworld for a portion of the year (typically autumn and winter), a fate imposed by her eating of the pomegranate seeds. She cannot leave during this time, unless Pluto grants an exception.

Spring and Grain Bringer

Her annual return to the upper world marks the start of spring and the growth of grain. She is a vegetation goddess, essential for agricultural fertility. Her power is not just to make things grow, but to signal the renewal of the cycle of life.

Limit: Her power is cyclical and tied to the seasons. She is not a goddess of constant summer, but one of return and renewal. Her absence brings winter and barrenness.

Invoked in Curses and Magic

Because of her authority over the dead, Proserpina is a common figure in Roman curse tablets (defixiones) and magical papyri. She is called upon to ensure that a curse is fulfilled by the spirits of the dead.

Limit: This power is invoked through ritual, not directly. She does not appear to act on her own to harm the living, but rather enforces the will of those who properly petition her.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In Roman art, Proserpina is typically depicted as a beautiful young woman, often in a flowing robe (stola). She is shown either as a maiden holding flowers or grain, or as a solemn queen seated on a throne in the underworld, often holding a scepter or a torch. Symbols — Her primary symbols are the narcissus flower, the torch, the sheaf of grain, the pomegranate (a symbol of her underworld bond), and the scepter. In Roman art, she is often shown with a torch, which is a symbol of her mother's search and her own power. The pomegranate is the most potent symbol of her fate, representing both the sweetness of life and the binding nature of the underworld.

Domains — Proserpina's domains are twofold: the Underworld (as queen) and Spring/Grain (as a vegetation goddess). This duality is the core of her myth and cult. She is a 'psychopomp' of sorts, not leading souls to death, but reigning over them. Her springtime return signifies rebirth, not just of plants, but of the promise of life after the symbolic death of winter.

Accounts I
The Abduction of Proserpina (The Rape of Proserpina)Ovid, Metamorphoses V; Claudian, De Raptu Proserpinae; Hyginus, Fabulae 146

Augustan and Late Antique

While Proserpina is gathering flowers in the fields of Henna (Sicily), the earth splits open and Pluto (Dis), god of the underworld, emerges in his chariot and carries her off to be his bride. Her mother, Ceres, searches the world for her in grief, causing the earth to become barren. In her search, Ceres learns from a nymph (or the Sirens) that Proserpina was taken, and eventually, with the intervention of Jupiter, a deal is struck: Proserpina may return to the world above, but only if she has not eaten in the underworld. She has eaten a pomegranate seed (or, in some versions, a few seeds), binding her to the underworld for a portion of the year. The compromise is that she spends two-thirds (or half) of the year with her mother and the rest with her husband. The myth explains the cycle of the seasons: winter is Ceres's mourning, spring is Proserpina's return. It also establishes the sanctity of marriage (even a violent one) and the irrevocable nature of a pact made in the underworld.

Accounts II
The Search of Ceres and the Founding of the Cult at EnnaCicero, In Verrem II 4.106-110; Ovid, Fasti IV

1st century BCE - 1st century CE

Ceres's search for her daughter is a central part of the story. In Sicily, she is said to have lit torches from the fires of Mount Etna to aid her search. The sanctuary at Enna, where the abduction supposedly took place, became a major cult center. Cicero's account emphasizes the importance of this sanctuary and the theft of its sacred statue by the corrupt governor Verres, a crime he uses to demonstrate Verres's impiety. Established the cult of Ceres and Proserpina as a major state cult in Sicily and Rome. The story of the search is a testament to a mother's love and the power of grief.

The Secular Games (Ludi Saeculares)Livy, Ab Urbe Condita 29.14

First held 249 BCE (historically recorded)

A solemn, once-in-a-century festival held to honor Pluto and Proserpina. The games were intended to secure the safety and prosperity of the Roman state. The rituals involved offerings of black animals (appropriate for underworld deities) at an altar set up in the Campus Martius (Tarentum). Reinforced the state's connection to the underworld deities and was seen as a way to ensure Rome's continued existence. The games were later revived by Augustus in 17 BCE, but re-dedicated to Apollo and Diana, showing a shift in religious focus.

Proserpina is just a copy of Persephone. — While fully syncretized with the Greek Persephone, the Roman Proserpina had a distinct cult and identity, particularly as the agricultural goddess Libera. Her Roman rituals (e.g., the Secular Games) were not simply Greek imports.

The 'Rape of Proserpina' is a romantic love story. — The Latin 'raptus' means 'seizure' or 'abduction', not 'rape' in the modern sense of sexual assault. However, the story is still one of violent, non-consensual seizure. It is not a romantic tale; it is a myth about the harsh realities of marriage in a patriarchal society and the cycle of life and death.

Proserpina is a 'goddess of spring' who is simply happy to return. — Her return is a relief for her mother and the earth, but she is also the Queen of the Underworld. She is a complex figure who rules over the dead. Her return is not a simple 'happy ending' but a negotiated compromise between two realms.
