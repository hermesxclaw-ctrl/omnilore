Who It Is

Before the Greeks called themselves Hellenes, they imagined someone else had already been there — the Pelasgians, a shadow-people who farmed the plains of Thessaly, tended the oak of Zeus at Dodona, and built walls on the Acropolis before being written out of their own land. They never left a word in their own language, yet every Classical author claimed to know who they were.

Behavior is defined by narrative function: autochthonous (earth-born), agricultural, pious toward Dodonian Zeus, skilled spearmen (Homer Iliad 2.843: Pelasgoi egcheimoroi), and as builders. Herodotus portrays them as barbarian-speaking (barbarophonoi) but teachable — they give names to Greek gods and adopt Hellenic language over time (2.50-52). Athenian tradition portrays them as useful laborers then threatening neighbors who must be expelled (Herodotus 6.137). No individual psychology beyond eponym Pelasgus as ancestral king.

Powers
Autochthony (Earth-Born Origin)

Foundational ability to be born directly from earth without human parents, conferring legitimate claim to land. Asius fr. 7 Bernabé: 'godlike Pelasgus whom the black earth gave up'; Aeschylus Suppl. 250: Pelasgus as child of Palaichthon whom earth brought forth. Basis for Arcadian and Argive autochthony ideology.

Limit: Not a personal power but a genealogical status; only eponym Pelasgus possesses it, not all Pelasgians. Competing genealogy makes Pelasgus son of Zeus and Niobe (Acusilaus FGrHist 2 F 25) — thus not earth-born.

Guardianship of Dodona and Pelasgic Zeus Cult

Pelasgians as original custodians of the oracle of Zeus at Dodona, interpreting rustling of sacred oak and doves. Homer Iliad 16.233 calls Zeus 'Pelasgic, dwelling afar, ruling over wintry Dodona'; Hesiod fr. 130 calls Dodona 'seat of Pelasgians'. Herodotus 2.52 says Pelasgians first sacrificed to unnamed gods at Dodona and received divine names from Egypt via oracle.

Limit: Cult later Hellenized; by Classical period Dodona served Molossians/Thesprotians, not Pelasgians. Herodotus admits Pelasgians did not know names of gods.

Megalithic / Cyclopean Building (Pelasgic Wall)

Attributed construction of massive fortification walls, especially the Pelasgikon teichos on the Acropolis (Herodotus 5.64, Thucydides 2.17, Pausanias 1.28.3). Later authors extend to walls at Tiryns and other Bronze Age remains now known to be Mycenaean.

Limit: Archaeology identifies these walls as Mycenaean (Late Helladic), not built by historical Pelasgians. Attribution is etiological.

Barbarian Speech and Linguistic Substrate

Possession of a non-Greek language or languages called barbaros. Herodotus 1.57 claims Pelasgians of Creston speak non-Greek, those of Placia likewise, and infers all Pelasgians did. Modern linguists associate Pelasgian with Pre-Greek substrate toponyms (-ss-, -nth-, -tt-) and Lemnian/Etruscan if Tyrrhenian equation holds.

Limit: No Pelasgian text survives; Lemnian stele language is not labeled Pelasgian in situ. Herodotus admits he does not know what language Pelasgians spoke (1.57). Substrate words may be Minoan, Anatolian, or multiple layers.

Spearmanship and Fertile Land Husbandry

Homeric epithet for Pelasgians as spear-famed (egcheimoroi) and holders of fertile Larisa (eribolos). Implies martial and agricultural competence in Thessalian plain.

Limit: Generic heroic epithet, not unique ability. Larisa toponym recurs (Thessalian, Argive, Cretan Larisa), causing geographic confusion.

Accounts I
The Pelasgians at Troy — Hippothous and Pylaeus from LarisaHomer, Iliad 2.840-843, 10.429, 17.288

Late Bronze Age setting, 8th c. BC composition

In the Catalogue of Trojans, the Pelasgians are listed among Priam's allies, between Hellespontine cities and Thracians. They come from fertile Larisa, led by Hippothous and Pylaeus, sons of Lethus son of Teutamides, and are renowned spearmen. In Book 10 they camp toward the sea beside Carians, Paeonians, Leleges and Caucones. In Book 17, Hippothous is killed by Ajax while trying to drag off Patroclus's body, showing Pelasgians fighting in the front line. This is the earliest attestation and treats Pelasgians as Anatolian/Thracian-border allies of Troy, not mainland Greeks. Establishes Pelasgians as martial Anatolian people distinct from Achaeans; later authors struggle to reconcile Trojan Pelasgians with mainland Pelasgian Argos and attempt to locate Larisa (three cities of that name).

Pelasgic Zeus of Dodona and the Oak OracleHomer Iliad 16.233-235; Hesiod fr. 130 MW via Strabo 7.7.10; Herodotus 2.52-57

Archaic to Classical

Achilles prays: 'Lord Zeus, Dodonian, Pelasgian, dwelling afar, ruling over wintry Dodona, where your interpreters the Selloi sleep on the ground with unwashed feet.' Hesiod explains Dodona as 'seat of Pelasgians' where the oak speaks. Herodotus rationalizes: Pelasgians originally sacrificed to nameless gods, consulted Dodona, and only later learned divine names from Egypt via Phoenicians; the oracle's doves were originally Egyptian priestesses. The epithet Pelasgikos thus preserves memory of Pelasgian custodianship before Hellenes. Links Pelasgians to oldest Greek oracle; explains why Homer calls Zeus Pelasgic not Dodonian. Becomes proof text for Pelasgians as pre-Greek religious population.

Pelasgus the Earth-Born — First Man of Arcadia and Father of LycaonHesiod fr. 160 MW; Asius of Samos fr. 7 Bernabé via Pausanias 8.1.4; Acusilaus FGrHist 2 F 25; Pausanias 8.1-2

Archaic (Hesiod, Asius ca. 600 BC) to Imperial (Pausanias 2nd c. AD)

Pelasgus is the eponymous ancestor. Hesiod makes him father of King Lycaon. Asius, quoted by Pausanias, calls him the first man: 'godlike Pelasgus whom the black earth gave up, that the race of mortals might exist.' His son Lycaon founds Lycosura, the oldest city, and institutes Zeus Lykaios cult, later infamous for human sacrifice and werewolf transformation. Alternative genealogy (Acusilaus) makes Pelasgus son of Zeus and Niobe, daughter of Phoroneus, thus Hellenized and not autochthonous. Arcadians use Pelasgus to claim they were never immigrants but sprang from the land itself, older than the moon (proselenoi). Creates enduring Arcadian identity as autochthonous Pelasgians; Lycaon story becomes aetiology for Zeus Lykaios ritual and werewolf lore. Competing genealogies reflect tension between autochthony and Hellenic kinship.

Accounts II
King Pelasgus of Argos and the Danaids — The Suppliant KingAeschylus, Suppliants (Hiketides) 250-330, 605-624; Strabo 8.6.9 citing Aeschylus

Classical Athens, ca. 463 BC

King Pelasgus rules a vast Pelasgian Argos stretching from the Strymon River in Thrace, including Perrhaebia, Dodona and Pindus in the west, to the sea in the east — essentially all eastern Greece. He is son of Palaichthon ('ancient earth'). Fifty Danaids, dark-skinned descendants of Io fleeing forced marriage to their cousins the Aegyptiads, land at Argos and beg asylum, speaking a karbana audan (barbarian/foreign speech, or specifically Carian). They claim Argive ancestry through Io. Pelasgus must choose between war with Egypt and violating suppliant right; he puts the decision to the Argive assembly, which votes to protect them, precipitating war with Egypt. Strabo notes Aeschylus located the original Pelasgian homeland around Mycenae. Dramatizes Pelasgian kingship as pan-Hellenic and autochthonous yet confronted with foreignness; introduces Danaid-Pelasgian kinship that Sophocles and Euripides later affirm. Establishes Pelasgian Argos as political entity.

The Pelasgians of Crete — Language Mixing Side by SideHomer Odyssey 19.172-177

8th c. BC composition

Odysseus, disguised as a Cretan, tells Penelope that Crete has ninety cities and many languages: 'There are Achaeans, great-hearted Eteocretans, Cydonians, Dorians divided in three tribes, and noble Pelasgians.' The phrase 'language mixing with language side by side' (glossa memigmenē) suggests Crete as multiethnic. Pelasgians are listed last, distinguished from true Cretans, implying they are immigrants or remnant substrate, yet noble (diioi). No further Homeric detail. Evidence for Pelasgians as one of several Cretan ethnē; used by later authors to argue Pelasgians were widespread sea peoples. Modern scholars link to Minoan substrate or Bronze Age migrations.

The Pelasgic Wall and Expulsion from Attica to LemnosHerodotus 6.137-140, 5.64; Hecataeus FGrHist 1 F 127; Thucydides 2.17; Pausanias 1.28.3

Classical (Herodotus ca. 430 BC) recalling Bronze Age/Archaic

Pelasgians, invited to Athens, build the wall around the Acropolis (Pelasgikon/Pelasgic Wall) and receive land at the foot of Hymettus as reward. They become hubristic, assault Athenian maidens at Brauron, and are expelled by Athenians. They sail to Lemnos, Imbros and Samothrace, where they become the Pelasgians who later abduct Athenian women from Brauron again, carry them to Lemnos, and beget children who kill their Pelasgian fathers — an aetiology for Lemnian misandry. Herodotus presents this as Athenian justification for Miltiades' later conquest of Lemnos (ca. 500 BC). Hecataeus gives variant where Pelasgians are simply driven out for being barbarians. Explains Acropolis fortification and Athenian claim to Lemnos; portrays Pelasgians as both builders and abductors. Becomes key text for Pelasgian-Tyrrhenian/Lemnian equation. Archaeology shows Lemnos inhabited by non-Greek speakers until 5th c. BC, lending partial support.

Pelasgians Become Hellenes — Herodotus' Linguistic TheoryHerodotus 1.56-58, 2.50-52, 8.44

Classical

Herodotus argues that Hellenes were once Pelasgians who changed language: Athenians were Pelasgian before being Hellenized; Pelasgians spoke a barbarian language (barbaros), as still heard at Creston and Placia. When Hellenes separated, they were few and grew by absorbing Pelasgians and others. He claims Pelasgians taught Greeks the names of gods and religious rites, learned via Dodona from Egypt. Thus Pelasgian is not a foreign invasion but the substrate from which Greek identity emerged. This theory allows Athenians to claim both autochthony (as Pelasgians) and Hellenicity. Most influential ancient theory of Pelasgian-Hellenic continuity; shapes all later antiquarian synthesis and modern substrate hypotheses. Contradicted by Thucydides who treats Pelasgian as pre-Hellenic name that faded.

Pelasgians were a unified empire or kingdom that ruled all Greece before the Greeks. — Ancient sources describe scattered enclaves and retrojected kingdoms (Aeschylus's vast Pelasgian Argos is tragic invention), not a centralized state. Homer shows two separate Pelasgian groups on opposite sides of Aegean; Herodotus and Thucydides list small surviving pockets (Creston, Placia, Athos). Archaeology shows no uniform Pelasgian material culture; 'Pelasgian' walls are Mycenaean.

The Pelasgian language is attested and deciphered, proving Pelasgians were Illyrian/Albanian or Slavic. — No text self-identified as Pelasgian survives. The Lemnian Stele is Lemnian, not labeled Pelasgian; its link to Pelasgians is via Herodotus's equation. Substrate toponyms are not a deciphered language. Albanian-Pelasgian continuity theories (Kollias, D'Angély) cherry-pick Herodotus and ignore linguistics; mainstream Albanology derives Albanian from Illyrian/Thracian, not Pelasgian.

Pelasgians were a distinct race with identifiable art, religion, and matriarchal goddess culture destroyed by patriarchal Greeks. — No distinct Pelasgian art or religion is archaeologically identifiable; Dodona and Acropolis walls are attributed to them etiologically. Matriarchal Pelasgian goddess theory derives from Graves (1955) and Gimbutas, not ancient sources, which portray Pelasgians as patriarchal (King Pelasgus, Lycaon). Ancient religion attributed to them (nameless gods at Dodona) is Herodotus's speculation.

Greeks unanimously considered Pelasgians as barbarians and foreigners. — Usage was fluid and strategic: Herodotus calls them barbarian-speaking, but also says Athenians were Pelasgians who became Greeks; Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides treat Pelasgus as Greek king; Dionysius insists they were Greeks. The term could mean ancestor, neighbor, or barbarian depending on author's argument about Hellenic identity.
