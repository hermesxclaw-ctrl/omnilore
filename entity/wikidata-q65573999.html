Who It Is

Central figure of Christianity, revered as the Son of God and savior. Historical first-century Jewish preacher from Nazareth whose life, crucifixion, and reported resurrection form the foundation of the world's largest religion. Also recognized as a prophet in Islam (Isa).

Jesus Christ is the central figure of Christianity.

The name Jesus is the English transliteration, through Latin Iesus, of Ancient Greek Iesous, itself the Greek rendering of the Hebrew Yehoshua/Yeshua, meaning 'Yahweh saves' or 'Yahweh is salvation' — from yasha (to save) and the divine name. 'Christ' is not a name but a title: from Greek Christos, a calque of Hebrew Mashiach ('Messiah,' 'the anointed one'), referring to the ancient practice of anointing priests and kings with sacred oil. The double name thus encodes a claim: Jesus is the anointed savior. The Latin inscription 'Iesus Nazarenus Rex Iudaeorum' (INRI, 'Jesus of Nazareth, King of the Jews') was affixed to the cross per the Gospel of John (19:19-22).

Christianity (all major branches: Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant, Oriental, and Restorationist traditions); also revered as a prophet and messianic figure in Islam (Isa ibn Maryam), the Baha'i Faith, and Druze tradition. The figure of Jesus is simultaneously a historical first-century Galilean Jewish preacher, the central object of Christian worship, and a figure of global cultural significance beyond religion. The historical Jesus is studied academically as a first-century Jew of the Second Temple period; the Christ of faith is the second person of the Trinity incarnate.

Form

appearance: The Gospels give no physical description of Jesus whatsoever — a striking silence that later tradition filled. The earliest visual depictions (Roman catacombs, 3rd-4th c.) show him as a beardless, short-haired young man in the Good Shepherd type, indistinguishable from contemporary pagan figures. The bearded, long-haired 'Pantocrator' image emerged in Byzantine art from the 4th-6th centuries and became canonical. The Shroud of Turin (first securely attested 1350s) purports to show a crucified man's image but its authenticity is scientifically disputed; radiocarbon dating in 1988 suggested medieval origin, though the methodology remains contested. The Book of Isaiah 53:2 ('no beauty that we should desire him') was read by early Christians as prophecy of an unremarkable appearance. Medieval and Renaissance art overwhelmingly depicted a European Jesus, a culturally contingent choice that modern scholarship and art increasingly complicate.

symbols_iconography: The cross (the central Christian symbol, transformed from an instrument of Roman execution into the sign of salvation); the ichthys (fish, from IXTHYS — Iesous Christos Theou Yios Soter, 'Jesus Christ, Son of God, Savior'); the Chi-Rho (XP, the first two letters of Christos, adopted by Constantine); the lamb (Agnus Dei); the Good Shepherd; the Alpha and Omega; the sacred heart (Catholic devotion); the crown of thorns; the five wounds; INRI inscription; the empty tomb; the nimbus/halo with a cross; the Book of Life; the vine and branches (John 15); the bread and wine of the Eucharist.

behavior_personality: The Gospel portrait is layered and at times paradoxical: a teacher who blesses the poor and meek (Sermon on the Mount, Matthew 5-7) yet drives moneychangers from the Temple with a whip (John 2:15); a healer who touches lepers and speaks with Samaritans and women (against social convention) yet calls a Canaanite woman a 'dog' before healing her daughter (Matthew 15:26); one who commands love of enemies yet pronounces woe upon whole cities (Matthew 11:21-24) and calls Peter 'Satan' (Mark 8:33). He prays alone in the wilderness, weeps at Lazarus's tomb (John 11:35), and cries out in apparent abandonment on the cross ('My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?' — Mark 15:34, quoting Psalm 22). He teaches in parables deliberately opaque to outsiders (Mark 4:11-12), associates with tax collectors and sinners, and repeatedly predicts his own suffering and death. The portrait resists harmonization: the meek teacher and the apocalyptic preacher, the healer and the judge, coexist in the texts.

domains_habitats: First-century Galilee and Judea under Roman occupation: Nazareth (childhood), Capernaum (ministry base on the Sea of Galilee), the wilderness (temptation), Jerusalem (final week, crucifixion, resurrection). Cosmic domain: seated at the right hand of God the Father, interceding for humanity, Lord of the Church, and future judge of the living and the dead. The Incarnation grounds the theological claim that the creator entered creation: 'The Word became flesh and dwelt among us' (John 1:14).

Accounts

In Luke, the angel Gabriel announces to the virgin Mary that she will conceive by the Holy Spirit; she responds with the Magnificat ('My soul magnifies the Lord'). Mary and Joseph travel to Bethlehem for a Roman census, where Jesus is born in a stable and laid in a manger; shepherds are told by angels and come to worship. In Matthew, magi from the East follow a star to Bethlehem bearing gold, frankincense, and myrrh, while King Herod, threatened, slaughters the infants of Bethlehem (the Massacre of the Innocents); the family flees to Egypt and returns after Herod's death to settle in Nazareth. The genealogies trace Jesus's descent from David and Abraham (Matthew) and from Adam (Luke).

Jesus is baptized in the Jordan by John the Baptist, despite John's protest ('I need to be baptized by you'). The heavens open, the Spirit descends like a dove, and a voice declares, 'You are my beloved Son; with you I am well pleased.' Jesus then fasts forty days in the Judean wilderness, where Satan tempts him three times: to turn stones to bread, to throw himself from the Temple pinnacle, and to worship Satan for all the kingdoms of the world. Jesus answers each with scripture ('Man shall not live by bread alone'; 'You shall not put the Lord your God to the test'; 'Worship the Lord your God, and him only shall you serve').

The most famous ethical discourse in history: the Beatitudes ('Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven'), the command to be salt and light, the intensification of the Law ('You have heard it was said... but I say to you'), the Lord's Prayer, the Golden Rule, the command to love enemies, and the parables of the wise and foolish builders. It concludes with the crowds astonished 'because he taught them as one who had authority, and not as their scribes.'

Jesus's signature teaching form: the sower, the weeds among the wheat, the mustard seed, the leaven, the hidden treasure, the pearl of great price, the net, the good Samaritan (Luke 10:25-37), the prodigal son (Luke 15:11-32), the rich man and Lazarus, the talents, and the workers in the vineyard. The parables are deceptively simple stories that overturn expectations — enemies become neighbors, the lost are celebrated, the last are first.

At the Last Supper, Jesus institutes the Eucharist ('This is my body... this is my blood of the covenant'), washes the disciples' feet, and predicts Peter's denial and Judas's betrayal. In Gethsemane he prays in agony ('not my will, but yours be done') while the disciples sleep. Judas betrays him with a kiss; he is arrested, tried before the Sanhedrin (charged with blasphemy for claiming to be the Messiah/Son of God), and handed to Pontius Pilate. Pilate finds no case but yields to the crowd's demand for crucifixion (releasing Barabbas instead), symbolically washing his hands. Jesus is scourged, crowned with thorns, forced to carry the cross, and crucified at Golgotha between two thieves, with the inscription INRI. He speaks seven 'words' from the cross, cries out in Aramaic 'Eloi, Eloi, lema sabachthani?' ('My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?'), and dies at the ninth hour. A centurion declares, 'Truly this man was the Son of God.'

On the first day of the week, women (Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James, Salome) find the tomb empty; angels announce, 'He is not here; he has risen.' The risen Jesus appears to the disciples over forty days, offering peace, eating with them, and commissioning them: 'Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit' (the Great Commission, Matthew 28:19). The earliest creedal summary, 1 Corinthians 15:3-8, lists appearances to Cephas, the Twelve, over five hundred at once, James, and Paul.

A way to hold it in your mind

In the Roman province of Judea, around the year 30 of the Common Era, an itinerant Galilean preacher with no army, no wealth, and no published writings was executed on a cross — the empire's instrument for the slow, public humiliation of slaves and rebels. Within three centuries, the sign of that execution would be carved above the throne of the emperor who legalized his cult. Within two millennia, his birth year would become the zero point of the world's calendar, his face would be painted on a billion walls, and his name would be invoked to justify both the most radical ethic of enemy-love in human history and some of its most appalling violence. Whatever else he was — teacher, prophet, failed apocalypticist, or the incarnate God of the universe — Jesus of Nazareth is the most consequential human figure in recorded history, and the archive records that he remains, two thousand years later, both the most loved and the most argued-over.
