Who It Is

Hanako-san is a classic Japanese school ghost story -- the spirit of a young girl who haunts the girls' bathroom, most commonly the third stall on a specific floor. The legend, widely known among Japanese schoolchildren, states that if you knock three times on the third stall door and call 'Hanako-san, are you there?' she will answer 'Yes, I'm here.' What happens next varies: some versions say she opens the door and drags you into the toilet to drown you; others say a small, bloody hand reaches under the stall; still others claim she simply plays with you if you're kind. Hanako-san is typically depicted as a young girl with a bob haircut wearing a red skirt (or sometimes an old-fashioned school uniform), with pale, ghostly skin. Her origin story varies: the most common version says she was a student who died in a bathroom stall -- perhaps hiding from bullies, killed during an air raid in WWII, or murdered by a parent. Other versions say she committed suicide in the school bathroom. The legend serves multiple purposes in Japanese school culture: as a scary story to frighten younger students, as a test of courage, and as a subtle warning against lingering in bathrooms. Hanako-san is not always malevolent; some regional variants depict her as a playful or lonely spirit. The legend has been adapted extensively in anime, manga, and Japanese horror films, and is arguably the most well-known Japanese school ghost story.

Form

Attested descriptions consistently portray Hanako-san as a young school-age girl, usually in elementary school, with a straight bob haircut and simple clothing: a white blouse or shirt paired with a red skirt, red pleated skirt, or red overalls. The red garment is sometimes interpreted as a visual echo of blood or wartime fire, but this is an interpretive layer rather than an explicit traditional statement. Her face is generally human and intact—unlike more grotesque yokai such as kuchisake-onna—and she is imagined as occupying a single toilet stall rather than drifting through corridors. In some variants, only a pale hand or a disembodied voice is perceived, leaving her full body unseen.

Powers

Summoned presence via ritual knocking Hanako-san appears or responds only when a specific summoning protocol is followed: going to the designated girls’ bathroom, knocking three times on the third stall, and calling her name. This ritualized access differentiates her from random hauntings and turns her into a test of courage among children. Modern school kaidan versions recorded from the late 1960s onward; codified in Tsunemitsu’s "Gakkō no Kaidan" and summarized in YOKAI.JP and later analyses. Her presence is generally confined to the specified stall and bathroom; failure to follow the ritual (wrong stall, wrong number of knocks, wrong wording) may result in no response. Some accounts treat her as omnipresent in any school restroom, while others insist on a precise location and procedure.

Vocal response from within the stall In many tellings, Hanako-san’s primary manifestation is a disembodied voice answering, "Yes, I am here" from inside the closed stall, without necessarily opening the door or revealing her body. Standardized urban legend form as described in YOKAI.JP and children’s magazine features of the 1970s–1980s. The voice may be the only manifestation; the summoner can still flee without physical harm in some versions. Other variants replace the voice with a visible hand or full-bodied apparition, suggesting different local imaginations of her presence.

Physical attack and dragging into the toilet Darker variants describe Hanako-san opening the stall door, grabbing the summoner, and pulling them into the toilet, resulting in death or disappearance. This power aligns her with more lethal yokai and functions as a cautionary tale. Narratives collected in oral folklore (e.g., USC Digital Folklore Archives entry) and analyzed in Druga’s article on toilet ghosts. This outcome is conditional: it often depends on Hanako-san’s ‘mood’ or on the summoner’s behavior (mockery, disrespect, or failure to flee). Other versions explicitly deny lethal consequences, presenting her as a non-lethal scare or game partner.

Appearance of a white hand from below or above In early and regional variants, a pale hand emerges from beneath the stall door or from the toilet itself, sometimes calling itself "Third Hanako-san." The hand may simply frighten the viewer or symbolically mark the boundary between worlds. Sawauchi Village variant recorded by Matsutani; echoed in later retellings documented by YOKAI.JP. The hand’s action is usually limited to reaching out; explicit harm is not always described. Later standardized versions replace the hand with a full-bodied ghost or a purely vocal presence.

Playful interaction and games Some contemporary retellings, especially those aimed at younger children, depict Hanako-san as playing games or engaging in harmless interaction once summoned, turning the encounter into a test of bravery rather than a deadly threat. USC Digital Folklore Archives interview and modern children’s media adaptations. This benign behavior appears in specific, often sanitized contexts (podcasts, children’s books) and may not reflect older, more frightening versions. Contrasts sharply with lethal variants; scholars treat this as an adaptation to changing attitudes toward children’s horror.

Summoned presence Hanako-san appears or responds only when a specific ritual is performed—typically knocking three times on the designated stall and calling her name. This makes her a "summoned" ghost whose manifestation depends on human action. Modern call-and-response versions documented from the late 1960s onward and popularized in 1970s–1980s children’s magazines and Tsunemitsu’s "School Ghost Stories." If the ritual is not performed correctly (wrong stall, wrong number of knocks, incorrect wording), some versions state that she will not appear at all. Some accounts claim she can appear spontaneously at night to haunt restroom users, without being summoned, while others insist she is strictly ritual-bound.

Vocal reply from within the stall Upon being called, Hanako-san answers from inside the stall, usually with a quiet "Yes, I am here," confirming the presence of an unseen entity. YOKAI.JP entry; Nippon.com discussion of Hanako in the Toilet; multiple oral accounts. In many tellings, the voice alone is the extent of the encounter, and the summoner can flee without physical harm. Other variants treat the vocal reply as a prelude to physical attack, making the voice itself a trap.

Physical manifestation and attack In more frightening versions, Hanako-san opens the stall door or extends a ghostly hand, dragging the victim into the toilet or killing them within the cubicle. Sawauchi "Third Hanako-san" variant (white hand); USC folklore archive narrative; Druga’s analysis of toilet ghosts. The attack is usually contingent on the summoner remaining in place after the reply; leaving immediately can avert harm in some tellings. Gentler variants deny any lethal outcome, framing the encounter as a scare or a game rather than a fatal attack.

Game or challenge A minority of versions describe Hanako-san as inviting the summoner to play a game, turning the haunting into a test of bravery or a playful interaction. USC Digital Folklore Archives interview, which notes versions where Hanako plays games and the ending is "good." Details of the game are rarely specified; this element appears to be a later softening of the legend for younger audiences. This playful depiction conflicts with narratives that emphasize her as a vengeful or deadly ghost.

Guardian or gatekeeper function (interpretive) Some scholars and commentators interpret Hanako-san as a modern echo of toilet deities, acting as a guardian or gatekeeper of liminal spaces rather than a random predator. The Lore Archive article; comparative references to Kawaya-gami and other latrine spirits. This is an interpretive reconstruction rather than an explicit power described in children’s stories. Traditional toilet deities are often protective, whereas Hanako-san’s stories emphasize fear and danger; the connection remains debated.

Behavior & Habitat

In the most representative modern version, Hanako-san is an interactive ghost who responds when summoned. The ritual requires going to the girls’ bathroom (often specified as the third floor), knocking three times on the door of the third stall, and calling, "Hanako-san, are you there?" A faint reply—"Yes, I am here"—signals her presence. Depending on the variant, she may simply answer and vanish, reach out a white hand from below, appear above the stall door, or violently drag the summoner into the toilet to their death. Other tellings soften her behavior: she plays games with children, tests their courage, or merely scares them without physical harm. Scholars note that these behavioral differences reflect local school cultures and the storyteller’s intent—whether to enforce caution, provide a thrill, or explore darker fears.

Accounts

In Sawauchi Village, children tell of a ghost in the gymnasium bathroom: if someone enters the third stall from the back, a white hand reaches out from below and a voice calls, "The Third Hanako-san." The story emphasizes the shock of the sudden hand and the uncanny naming of the stall, rather than a detailed origin or outcome.

Children dare one another to go to the girls’ restroom—often on the third floor—knock three times on the third stall, and ask, "Hanako-san, are you there?" A voice replies, "Yes, I am here." In some tellings, the child flees in terror; in others, the stall door opens and Hanako-san appears, sometimes dragging the victim into the toilet.

One popular origin narrative claims Hanako-san was a young girl who hid in a school restroom during an air raid in World War II. The building was bombed, and she died in the flames, her red skirt symbolizing the fire and blood. Her ghost remains in the toilet, answering when called and sometimes punishing those who disturb her.

Another origin story describes Hanako-san as a student who was bullied and fled to the restroom to hide, where she died—either by suicide, accident, or at the hands of a stranger who entered the school. Her ghost remains in the stall, targeting those who repeat patterns of cruelty or simply anyone who enters her space.

Commentators propose that Hanako-san may be a secularized descendant of traditional toilet deities, such as Kawaya-gami, who once protected latrines and demanded respect. In this reading, the school restroom becomes a new sacred space, and Hanako-san enforces boundaries through fear rather than ritual offerings.

Field Notes

⍟ Hanako-san’s summoning ritual—third floor, third stall, three knocks—reads like a child-friendly command-line interface to the supernatural.

⍟ Despite being a toilet ghost, Hanako-san is often portrayed more neatly dressed and less grotesque than many other yokai, emphasizing her status as an ordinary schoolgirl turned extraordinary.

⍟ School librarians in the 1990s reportedly had shelves where Hanako-san, kuchisake-onna, and other school kaidan shared space, effectively turning the library into a curated haunted archive.

⍟ In some modern retellings, children negotiate with Hanako-san by promising not to bully others, turning the legend into an impromptu moral contract.

⍟ The legend’s fixation on the third stall mirrors broader folkloric fascination with ‘third’ thresholds—neither first nor last, but the uncanny middle.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Hanako-san as the school system’s unofficial ghost administrator: she lives in the one place where institutional order loosens, where children are alone with their bodies and their fears. Every knock on her stall door is a tiny rebellion against adult control and a rehearsal for facing the things that scare us but can’t be put on a timetable—war stories, bullying, growing up, and the knowledge that even the safest spaces have backdoors to the unknown.
