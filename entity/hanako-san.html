Who It Is

According to traditional accounts, if you grew up in a Japanese school in the late twentieth century, courage wasn’t just measured by test scores or sports day—it was measured by whether you dared to knock on the third stall door and ask, "Hanako-san, are you there?" The answer, whispered from the other side of the thin partition, turned an ordinary restroom into a gateway between childhood and the uncanny.

Hanako-san is an interactive ghost: she does not usually appear unprovoked but responds to a specific summoning ritual. The most widely attested pattern instructs the summoner to go to the girls’ restroom—often on the third floor—knock three times on the door of the third stall, and ask, "Hanako-san, are you there?" A soft reply, "Yes, I am here," signals her presence. Name & Tongue

Etymology — “Hanako” (はなこ／花子) is a common Japanese girl’s given name; the honorific “-san” (さん) marks her as a familiar yet respectfully distant figure. The compound “Toire no Hanako-san” literally means “Hanako of the toilet,” situating her identity in the restroom space rather than in a family line or village

Pronunciation — [to.i.ɾe no ha.na.ko saɴ] (Japanese), commonly rendered in English as “HAH-nah-koh sahn.”

Aliases — Hanako-san, Hanako of the Toilet, Hanako of the Third Stall, Toilet Hanako

Powers
Summoned presence

Hanako-san appears or responds only when a specific ritual is performed—typically knocking three times on the designated stall and calling her name. This makes her a "summoned" ghost whose manifestation depends on human action.

Limit: If the ritual is not performed correctly (wrong stall, wrong number of knocks, incorrect wording), some versions state that she will not appear at all.

Vocal reply from within the stall

Upon being called, Hanako-san answers from inside the stall, usually with a quiet "Yes, I am here," confirming the presence of an unseen entity.

Limit: In many tellings, the voice alone is the extent of the encounter, and the summoner can flee without physical harm.

Physical manifestation and attack

In more frightening versions, Hanako-san opens the stall door or extends a ghostly hand, dragging the victim into the toilet or killing them within the cubicle.

Limit: The attack is usually contingent on the summoner remaining in place after the reply; leaving immediately can avert harm in some tellings.

Game or challenge

A minority of versions describe Hanako-san as inviting the summoner to play a game, turning the haunting into a test of bravery or a playful interaction.

Limit: Details of the game are rarely specified; this element appears to be a later softening of the legend for younger audiences.

Guardian or gatekeeper function (interpretive)

Some scholars and commentators interpret Hanako-san as a modern echo of toilet deities, acting as a guardian or gatekeeper of liminal spaces rather than a random predator.

Limit: This is an interpretive reconstruction rather than an explicit power described in children’s stories.

In some versions she is simply a disembodied voice or a pale hand emerging from beneath the stall door; in others she appears fully, sometimes bloodied or ghostly pale. Accounts I
The Sawauchi "Third Hanako-san"Miyoko Matsutani, Modern Folklore Vol. 7; later analyses by Hirota & ASIOS and YOKAI.JP.

Late 1940s (story), recorded mid-20th century

In Sawauchi Village, children tell of a ghost in the gymnasium bathroom: if someone enters the third stall from the back, a white hand reaches out from below and a voice calls, "The Third Hanako-san." The story emphasizes the shock of the sudden hand and the uncanny naming of the stall, rather than a detailed origin or outcome. The tale marks the third stall as a taboo space, teaching children to avoid it and reinforcing the idea that certain places in school are haunted.

The standard summoning ritualTsunemitsu’s "School Ghost Stories"; YOKAI.JP; Nippon.com; multiple oral accounts.

Late 1960s onward; popularized in 1970s–1980s and codified in 1990s.

Children dare one another to go to the girls’ restroom—often on the third floor—knock three times on the third stall, and ask, "Hanako-san, are you there?" A voice replies, "Yes, I am here." In some tellings, the child flees in terror; in others, the stall door opens and Hanako-san appears, sometimes dragging the victim into the toilet. The ritual becomes a rite of passage, testing courage and reinforcing group bonds. It also spreads the legend as children recount their experiences, whether real or imagined.

Accounts II
The war orphan originUSC Digital Folklore Archives; The Lore Archive; Druga’s article (interpretive context).

Legend circulating from the 1950s; origin story retroactively attached.

One popular origin narrative claims Hanako-san was a young girl who hid in a school restroom during an air raid in World War II. The building was bombed, and she died in the flames, her red skirt symbolizing the fire and blood. Her ghost remains in the toilet, answering when called and sometimes punishing those who disturb her. This version ties the legend to collective wartime trauma and the memory of children killed in bombings, giving the ghost a tragic, sympathetic dimension.

The bullied child and the intruderUSC Digital Folklore Archives; The Lore Archive.

Late 20th century onward.

Another origin story describes Hanako-san as a student who was bullied and fled to the restroom to hide, where she died—either by suicide, accident, or at the hands of a stranger who entered the school. Her ghost remains in the stall, targeting those who repeat patterns of cruelty or simply anyone who enters her space. This narrative foregrounds school bullying and the fear that the supposedly safe school environment can harbor lethal danger.

Hanako-san as modern latrine spiritThe Lore Archive; comparative folklore discussions referencing Kawaya-gami.

21st-century scholarly and fan analyses.

Commentators propose that Hanako-san may be a secularized descendant of traditional toilet deities, such as Kawaya-gami, who once protected latrines and demanded respect. In this reading, the school restroom becomes a new sacred space, and Hanako-san enforces boundaries through fear rather than ritual offerings. This interpretive story reframes Hanako-san as a guardian of liminal spaces, suggesting continuity between premodern folk religion and modern urban legends.
