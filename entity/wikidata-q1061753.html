The Approach

In the quiet hills of western Galilee, the village of Iqrit once stood as a living testament to centuries of Christian and Muslim coexistence. Its stone houses, olive groves, and the echoing bell of its Melkite church told a story of continuity and faith. But in the winter of 1948, that story was violently interrupted. Expelled from their homes with a promise of return, the people of Iqrit have spent over seven decades fighting to reclaim a village that was razed on Christmas Day 1951—a wound that remains open in the heart of Palestinian and Israeli history.

Content advisory. This dossier contains accounts of forced displacement, destruction of property, and legal battles. It deals with the ongoing Israeli-Palestinian conflict and may be emotionally challenging. It is presented with scholarly context and sensitivity.
Identity

Iqrit

Pronunciation IK-rit (Arabic: إقرت or إقرث, Iqrith)

Known As

IqrithIkritIkretAcrefAkrithYoqeretYokereth

Etymology

The name Iqrit is of Arabic origin, possibly derived from the Canaanite or Aramaic root meaning 'to uproot' or 'to be uprooted', reflecting the village's tragic fate. The Hebrew name Yoqeret (יוקרת) appears in Talmudic sources.

Cultural Ownership

Palestinian Christian (Melkite Greek Catholic and Greek Orthodox), with a Muslim minority; historically part of the Galilee region.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars identify Iqrit with the Talmudic Jewish village of Yoqeret, but this is not universally accepted. The Crusader name Acref may be a corruption of the Arabic name.

Description

Appearance

Iqrit was a typical Galilean village with stone houses clustered on a tell, surrounded by olive, fig, oak, laurel, and carob trees. It had a large Melkite church, a private school, natural springs, wells, cisterns, and threshing floors. The village was known for its fig groves covering the hill of al-Bayad.

Symbols & Iconography

The Melkite church and cemetery are the most significant symbols of Iqrit's Christian heritage. The village's ruins and the church stand as symbols of the Nakba and the ongoing struggle for return.

Behaviour & Personality

The villagers were primarily agricultural, cultivating figs, olives, lentils, and tobacco. They had a strong community life centered around the church and school. They were known for their peaceful coexistence between Christians and Muslims.

Domains & Habitats

The village was located in the western Galilee, 25 km northeast of Acre. The landscape included hills, arable land, and natural water sources.

Version Differences

Historical descriptions vary: Guérin noted a 'considerable' village; the PEF described a smaller population. The 1945 census recorded 490 residents, but by 1948 the number had grown to 491, with 432 Melkites and 59 Muslims.

Stories & Myths

The 1948 Expulsion

Source Benny Morris, 'The Birth of the Palestinian Refugee Problem Revisited'Culture Palestinian ArabPeriod 1948

Summary

After surrendering to Israeli forces on 31 October 1948, the villagers were ordered to leave on 5 November, with promises of return in two weeks. They were trucked to Rameh, while some fled to Lebanon. The village was later destroyed.

Participants

Israeli Army (Oded Brigade)

Villagers of Iqrit

Consequences

Displacement of all villagers; village destroyed on Christmas Day 1951.

Variants

Some accounts say the expulsion was part of a policy to create an 'Arabless border strip'.

The Supreme Court Ruling and Destruction

Source Israel Supreme Court records; Washington Report on Middle East AffairsCulture Israeli legal systemPeriod 1951

Summary

In July 1951, the Supreme Court ruled in favor of the villagers' right to return. However, an emergency decree was issued, and on Christmas Day 1951, the IDF demolished the village, forcing the village chief to watch.

Participants

Iqrit villagers

Muhammad Nimr al-Hawari

Israel Supreme Court

IDF

Consequences

The village was razed, and the villagers remained displaced.

Variants

The IDF claimed military necessity, but the court had not authorized destruction.

The Sit-In Movement

Source Media reports; Benvenisti's writingsCulture Palestinian-Israeli solidarityPeriod 1970s

Summary

Villagers conducted sit-ins in the church for six years, drawing public empathy and support from Israeli cultural figures. The authorities recognized the right of return in principle but refused to implement it.

Participants

Iqrit villagers

Israeli public and artists

Government of Israel

Consequences

No return; the issue remains unresolved.

Variants

Some argue that allowing return would set a precedent for other displaced villages.

Expulsion and destruction

Source Benny Morris; Washington Report

Description

The villagers were expelled in 1948 and their village destroyed in 1951 despite a Supreme Court ruling in their favor. The IDF forced the village chief to watch the demolition.

Scholarly Context

This is part of the broader Nakba, the Palestinian catastrophe.

Expropriation

Source Zehava Galon

Description

In 1955, 16,000 dunams were expropriated for Jewish settlements, justified by the claim that no one had lived there for two years.

Scholarly Context

This reflects the Israeli policy of land confiscation from absentees.
