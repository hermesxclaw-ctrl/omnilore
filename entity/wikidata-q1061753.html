Who It Is

In the quiet hills of western Galilee, the village of Iqrit once stood as a living testament to centuries of Christian and Muslim coexistence. Its stone houses, olive groves, and the echoing bell of its Melkite church told a story of continuity and faith. But in the winter of 1948, that story was violently interrupted. Expelled from their homes with a promise of return, the people of Iqrit have spent over seven decades fighting to reclaim a village that was razed on Christmas Day 1951—a wound that remains open in the heart of Palestinian and Israeli history.

The villagers were primarily agricultural, cultivating figs, olives, lentils, and tobacco. They had a strong community life centered around the church and school. They were known for their peaceful coexistence between Christians and Muslims.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Iqrit is of Arabic origin, possibly derived from the Canaanite or Aramaic root meaning 'to uproot' or 'to be uprooted', reflecting the village's tragic fate. The Hebrew name Yoqeret (יוקרת) appears in Talmudic sources.

Pronunciation — IK-rit (Arabic: إقرت or إقرث, Iqrith)

Aliases — Iqrith, Ikrit, Ikret, Acref, Akrith, Yoqeret

Ownership — Palestinian Christian (Melkite Greek Catholic and Greek Orthodox), with a Muslim minority; historically part of the Galilee region.

Legal rights recognition

In 1951, the Israel Supreme Court recognized the villagers' right to return to their land, a rare legal victory for displaced Palestinians.

Limit: The ruling was undermined by an emergency decree and subsequent expropriation.

Community resilience

The villagers maintained a continuous presence at the church, conducting sit-ins for six years in the 1970s and continuing to bury their dead in the village cemetery.

Limit: Israeli authorities have prevented cultivation of lands and permanent residence.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Iqrit was a typical Galilean village with stone houses clustered on a tell, surrounded by olive, fig, oak, laurel, and carob trees. It had a large Melkite church, a private school, natural springs, wells, cisterns, and threshing floors. The village was known for its fig groves covering the hill of al-Bayad.

Symbols — The Melkite church and cemetery are the most significant symbols of Iqrit's Christian heritage. The village's ruins and the church stand as symbols of the Nakba and the ongoing struggle for return.

Domains — The village was located in the western Galilee, 25 km northeast of Acre. The landscape included hills, arable land, and natural water sources.

Accounts I
The 1948 ExpulsionBenny Morris, 'The Birth of the Palestinian Refugee Problem Revisited'

1948

After surrendering to Israeli forces on 31 October 1948, the villagers were ordered to leave on 5 November, with promises of return in two weeks. They were trucked to Rameh, while some fled to Lebanon. The village was later destroyed. Displacement of all villagers; village destroyed on Christmas Day 1951.

Accounts II
The Supreme Court Ruling and DestructionIsrael Supreme Court records; Washington Report on Middle East Affairs

1951

In July 1951, the Supreme Court ruled in favor of the villagers' right to return. However, an emergency decree was issued, and on Christmas Day 1951, the IDF demolished the village, forcing the village chief to watch. The village was razed, and the villagers remained displaced.

The Sit-In MovementMedia reports; Benvenisti's writings

1970s

Villagers conducted sit-ins in the church for six years, drawing public empathy and support from Israeli cultural figures. The authorities recognized the right of return in principle but refused to implement it. No return; the issue remains unresolved.
