I opened my eyes, and the world began. Where my gaze fell, life awoke — the kangaroo, the emu, the flowering wattle. I do not make; I wake. And when I close my eyes each night, remember: I will open them again.

Who It Is

Yhi is the Aboriginal Australian goddess of light, creation, and the sun — a powerful female creator figure whose mythology is particularly associated with the Kamilaroi (Gamilaraay) people of New South Wales. In the Dreamtime, Yhi was the great light that broke the primordial darkness, and her warmth and radiance awakened the sleeping spirits of creation. She is one of the most complete solar goddess figures in world mythology — a female creator whose light births life into being.

In the beginning, according to Kamilaroi tradition, the world lay in complete darkness and silence. Everything slept — the land, the potential spirits of animals and plants, even time itself. Yhi, the Sun Woman, had not yet awakened. When she finally opened her eyes, her light flooded the world for the first time. The warmth of her gaze fell upon the sleeping earth, and wherever her light touched, life stirred. She traveled across the land, and her passage created the first day. The spirits of plants, animals, and features of the landscape awoke under her luminous gaze and took their places in the world.

Yhi's creative act was not a single event but a process of awakening. She did not make the animals from nothing; rather, her light awakened latent spirits that had been sleeping in the formless darkness. As she moved across the land, grass sprouted, flowers opened, insects stirred, and the first birds took flight. Each living thing owes its existence to the moment Yhi's light fell upon its sleeping spirit and called it into being. This concept of creation-as-awakening is distinctive in world mythology — Yhi does not craft or command; she illuminates, and illumination is creation.

Yhi's most famous myth concerns her pursuit by the Spirit of Darkness. After Yhi had awakened the world and brought light, a great darkness-spirit — sometimes conceived as a malevolent being that resented her light — pursued her across the sky. This pursuit is what causes sunset and sunrise. Each day, Yhi rises in the east and travels across the sky, bringing light and warmth. Each night, the darkness-spirit chases her below the western horizon, and the world is plunged into darkness. But Yhi cannot be permanently overcome. She travels through the underworld at night and rises again each morning, eternally reawakening the world.

Yhi is also associated with the creation of human mortality — or rather, the origin of death as transition rather than ending. In one tradition, after awakening the world, Yhi realized that if living things did not die, the world would become overcrowded. She created death as a transformation — a return to the spirit world from which the sleeping spirits had originally been awakened. Death is thus not an enemy but a necessary part of the cycle Yhi established.

Yhi's mythology bears interesting parallels to other solar creators in world religion — particularly the Egyptian Ra and the Japanese Amaterasu — though Yhi's status as a specifically female solar creator distinguishes her. Female solar deities are relatively rare in world mythology (most sun gods are male), making Yhi significant for comparative religious studies. Her association with the Kamilaroi people's annual cycles and the importance of sunlight in an often-arid environment grounds her celestial power in practical, life-giving necessity.

Form

Yhi is described as a radiant, benevolent feminine presence who emerges from the darkness of the primordial void. She is not described in physical terms like a humanoid figure; rather, she is the sun itself, a source of warmth and light. Her appearance is one of pure incandescence, a blinding brightness that brings life to the dormant earth.

Powers

Solar Radiance Yhi embodies the sun's light and warmth. Her primary power is her ability to bring life and activity to the world through her radiance. This is her defining attribute. Gamilaraay tradition as recorded by Parker Her power is tied to the cycle of day and night; she must travel across the sky, and her absence brings darkness and dormancy. None recorded in the available sources.

Life Awakening Yhi's light awakens the dormant life-forms of the earth. Each plant and animal that stirs is a direct result of her touch. She is the catalyst for the world's vitality. Gamilaraay tradition as recorded by Parker She awakens what is already there; she does not create life from nothing, but rather rouses it from its primordial sleep. None recorded in the available sources.

Cosmic Journey Yhi's daily journey across the sky is a cosmic act of creation, as her light re-awakens the world each morning. This journey is a fundamental part of her nature and power. Gamilaraay tradition as recorded by Parker Her journey is cyclical and predictable, not a free-willed act of travel. None recorded in the available sources.

Behavior & Habitat

Yhi is a creator and life-giver. She acts with deliberate, loving intent, waking the sleeping life-forms of the earth with her warmth and light. She is a patient, nurturing figure who does not force life but rather invites it to stir and grow. Her journey across the sky is a daily act of re-creation, a cycle of awakening that sustains all living things.

Accounts

In the beginning, the earth was a cold, dark, and lifeless void. Yhi, the Sun Mother, slept in this darkness. When she awoke, she opened her eyes, and her light flooded the world. She traveled across the sky, and wherever her light touched the ground, life began to stir. Plants sprouted, animals woke, and the world became vibrant and full of activity. Yhi's journey across the sky became the daily cycle of day and night, and her warmth became the source of all life.

In the Dreamtime, the world lay in darkness, cold, silent, and still. Yhi, the Sun Goddess, slept beneath the earth in deep dreaming. The other spirit beings grew tired of the eternal darkness and called out to her, urging her to wake and bring light to the world. Stirred by their calls, Yhi opened her eyes for the first time, and light flooded across the land. She rose from her underground resting place and began to walk across the sky, her radiance transforming everything it touched. Where her light fell, dormant seeds sprouted, flowers opened their petals, and animals frozen in the darkness stretched and began to move. Yhi walked across the entire sky, bringing warmth, growth, and life to every corner of the world. At the end of her journey, she descended again beneath the earth and darkness returned, but now the world knew light, and all living things waited through the night for her return. This cycle established the rhythm of day and night, the pattern of waking and sleeping, and the seasons of growth and rest that govern all life. The story explains not only the origin of sunlight but also the fundamental order of the natural world that Aboriginal peoples observed and celebrated in their ceremonial life.

What We Don't Know

The primary gap is the lack of direct, primary-source material from Gamilaraay Elders. The available record is mediated through a non-Indigenous collector from the colonial era. Further research should seek out contemporary Gamilaraay community sources, cultural centers, or publications that present the story from within the culture. The specific language of the original story is also not available; only Parker's English rendering is accessible.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Yhi not as a deity who builds a world like a carpenter, but as the first ray of sunlight that hits a frozen, snow-covered landscape. She doesn't construct the mountains or carve the rivers; she simply brings the warmth that makes everything else possible. She is the spark that ignites the engine of life, the ultimate 'morning person' whose good mood literally wakes up the planet. If the world were a computer in sleep mode, Yhi is the power button.
