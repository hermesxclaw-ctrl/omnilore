Who It Is

In the first year of kingship, when humans still lived without writing or cities, something rose from the Persian Gulf at sunrise. Its body was a fish, but beneath the fish-head was a man's head, and beneath the tail were human feet and a human voice. It did not come to rule or to devour. It came to teach. The Babylonians called him Uanna. The Greeks, who heard his story three centuries before Christ from a Babylonian priest named Berossus, called him Oannes.

In Berossus, Oannes is benevolent, didactic, and amphibious: he spends daylight hours among humans teaching without eating, and at sunset dives back into the sea to spend the night in the water, being amphibious. He teaches comprehensively: letters, sciences, crafts, city-building, temple foundation, law, geometry, agriculture, and how to gather seeds and fruits. He writes a book on origins and civil polity and gives it to humanity. Powers
Comprehensive Civilizational Instruction

Teaches humanity writing, mathematics, geometry, architecture (city and temple building), law, agriculture, seed gathering, and tool use. Berossus says he taught 'all things that make for civilization' and that nothing was added after him.

Limit: Does not rule or coerce; instruction is offered, not imposed. Does not provide military or magical domination. Knowledge is cultural, not omnipotence.

Amphibious Existence

Able to live both in sea/Apsu and on land, breathing in both, returning to water at night. Greek text says 'amphibious' (amphibion).

Limit: Must return to water at night; not fully terrestrial. No flight or teleportation attested.

Authorship of Primordial Book

Writes a book concerning the origin of the world, kingship, and polity (peri geneseos kai politeias) and gives it to humans, serving as source for Berossus' own history.

Limit: Book does not survive except as claimed source; content known only via Berossus summary (cosmogony with Bel/Marduk, etc.).

Apotropaic and Purification Power

As apkallu, wards off evil, disease, and demons; figurines of fish-sages buried in foundations protect buildings. Holds banduddu (bucket) and mullilu (cone) for aspersion.

Limit: Protective, not destructive; requires ritual deployment by humans (figurines, incantations). Power is delegated from Ea.

Superhuman Wisdom (nemequ / hasisatu)

Possesses divine wisdom from Ea, 'completer of plans of heaven and earth', endowed with comprehensive intelligence surpassing humans.

Limit: Wisdom does not include immortality; Adapa tradition shows sage refused immortality via Ea's advice. Uanna remains mortal/created being, not god.

Articulate Human Speech Despite Fish Form

Speaks with human voice and language, intelligible to humans, despite fish body.

Limit: Speech is didactic; no record of prophecy beyond teaching cosmogony.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No cuneiform text describes Uanna's physical appearance directly; the description comes from Berossus in Greek: a composite being with the body of a fish, but underneath the fish's head a second, human head, and human feet attached beneath the tail, with a human voice. Domains — Domain is wisdom (Akkadian nemequ, hasisatu), civilization, and apotropaic protection. Accounts I
Oannes Emerges from the Erythraean Sea to Civilize MankindBerossus, Babyloniaca F1 via Alexander Polyhistor apud Eusebius and Syncellus

c. 290 BCE original; preserved in 4th-9th c. CE chronographers

In the first year of Alorus, first king of Babylon (Sumerian Alulim), an amphibious rational animal named Oannes rose from the Erythraean Sea (Persian Gulf) where it borders Babylonia. By day he conversed with men, teaching them letters, sciences, crafts, city and temple building, law, geometry, and agriculture; he required no food and at sunset returned to the sea to spend the night. He wrote a book on the origin of the world and kingship and gave it to men. Nothing was added to his teachings afterward. Berossus presents this as the source of Babylonian civilization and chronology. Foundation of Babylonian civilization, kingship, and writing; legitimizes Berossus' history as derived from Oannes' book; establishes antediluvian chronology of 10 kings and 432,000 years before Flood. Becomes locus classicus for fish-sage motif in Western tradition.

Uanna, Sage of Ayalu in the Uruk List of Kings and SagesUruk List of Kings and Sages (W 20030,7) SpTU 1, 27

Seleucid copy c. 165 BCE; tradition older

Succession list pairs each antediluvian king with his apkallu sage. First entry: King Ayalu (Alulim, king of Eridu) with sage Uanna. Subsequent pairs: Alalgar with Uandugga, Enmenluanna with Enmedugga, etc., totaling seven antediluvian apkallu, followed by postdiluvian human ummanu. No narrative, only pairing, establishing Uanna as archetypal first sage. Systematizes apkallu tradition; shows Uanna as primus inter pares; later texts use list to claim scholarly lineage. Basis for Berossus' Alorus-Oannes pairing.

The Seven Apkallu Sent from the Apsu by EaBit Meseri and related incantations (KAR 298, ABRT 1,56); Erra Epic I:37; Gilgamesh I:9

Early first millennium BCE (Neo-Assyrian/Neo-Babylonian manuscripts)

Ea (Enki), lord of the Apsu, creates/fashions seven apkallu, sages of great wisdom, who bring the arts of civilization to humanity before the Flood. They are described as purifiers who completed the plans of heaven and earth, endowed with comprehensive understanding. After the Flood, they return to the Apsu. Ritual texts invoke them for protection: figurines of the seven sages are made and buried in houses to repel evil. Uanna is chief among them. Explains origin of culture as divine gift via intermediaries; provides ritual mechanism for ongoing protection; distinguishes antediluvian apkallu from postdiluvian human scholars.

Accounts II
Adapa and the South Wind - The Wise Man Who Refused Immortality (Parallel/Conflated Tradition)Adapa Myth (Fragment A: El-Amarna EA 356; Fragment B: Assur; Fragment D: Nineveh) - cuneiform

Old Babylonian to Neo-Assyrian copies (c. 1800-700 BCE); earliest fragments 14th c. BCE Amarna

Adapa, sage of Eridu and priest of Ea, breaks the wing of the South Wind (Shutu) who capsized his boat. Summoned to heaven to Anu, Ea advises him to wear sackcloth, not to eat the food of immortality, and to refuse Anu's offer of eternal life. Adapa follows advice, is clothed and sent back to earth as wise man but mortal, given priestly privileges but not divinity. Later tradition (Uruk List, Berossus) sometimes identifies Adapa with Utuabzu, seventh apkallu, and Hellenistic authors conflate Adapa with Oannes. Explains why wisdom does not equal immortality; establishes model of sage as intermediary; creates confusion between Uanna/Oannes and Adapa in later sources. If conflated, gives Oannes a heavenly journey narrative; if distinct, shows two separate sage archetypes.

The Four Annedoti Before the Flood (Berossus via Apollodorus and Abydenus)Apollodorus of Athens (?) apud Syncellus p. 30-31; Abydenus apud Eusebius; Berossus FGrHist 680 F 4-5

c. 200 BCE - 2nd c. BCE epitomes

After Oannes, three more amphibious Annedoti (Greek for apkallu) appear at intervals before the Flood: second Annedotus Idotion?/Annedotos after Alorus, third Odakon after Ammenon, fourth after Amegalaros etc. Apollodorus lists Oannes, Annedotus, Eneugamos, Eneuboulos, Anementos, Odakon. Each teaches something. This expands single Oannes into series of seven, aligning Greek Annedoti with cuneiform seven apkallu, though numbers and names diverge. Shows Hellenistic systematization of apkallu tradition into chronological series; attempts to harmonize Berossus with cuneiform seven; source for later Christian chronology of pre-Flood patriarchs.

Oannes' Cosmogony - The Book of Origins Dictated to MankindBerossus F1 cosmogonic fragment via Eusebius and Syncellus; Damascius De principiis 125

c. 290 BCE

Oannes narrates Babylonian cosmogony: originally darkness and water, monstrous composite beings; Bel (Marduk) cuts the female leader Omoroca (Tiamat) in half, making heaven and earth, and cuts off his own head, mixing blood with earth to create humans, who therefore have divine intelligence. Oannes' book is Berossus' claimed source for this cosmogony, paralleling Enuma Elish but with Hellenistic details (e.g., Omoroca = Ummu-Hubur). Preserves Babylonian creation epic in Greek; provides parallel to Genesis 1 for Christian apologists; demonstrates Oannes as revealer of cosmic knowledge, not just crafts.

Oannes was a god, specifically the Philistine god Dagon or the Babylonian god Ea. — Oannes/Uanna is an apkallu, a sage/servant of Ea, not Ea himself. Dagon is a separate Syrian grain/sea deity (Dagān). The conflation comes from early modern writers and Lovecraft's 'Dagon' who misread Berossus; cuneiform texts clearly subordinate apkallu to Ea.

Oannes was an alien from Sirius who taught humanity. — No cuneiform or classical source suggests extraterrestrial origin. The claim originates with Robert Temple's The Sirius Mystery (1976) and Zecharia Sitchin's ancient astronaut books, which mistranslate and fabricate sources. Oannes is a Mesopotamian mythic sage from the Apsu (freshwater abyss), not outer space.

Oannes is just the Greek name for the biblical Noah/Utnapishtim. — Oannes appears before the Flood as teacher; Noah/Utnapishtim (Akkadian Atrahasis, Sumerian Ziusudra, Berossus Xisuthros) is the Flood survivor who builds the ark. They are distinct roles in same antediluvian chronology: Oannes is first sage under Alulim, Xisuthros is last king under whom Flood occurs.

The fish-man description proves ancient people saw real mermen or knew about evolution. — The description is mythic/ritual: fish cloak symbolizes connection to Ea and the Apsu. Neo-Assyrian art shows it as a costume worn by priests/exorcists. No evidence of observed marine cryptid; not a proto-evolutionary concept.

Oannes taught only writing and nothing else. — Berossus lists comprehensive curriculum: letters, sciences, crafts, city and temple building, law, geometry, agriculture, botany. Cuneiform epithet 'who completed plans of heaven and earth' implies cosmological/architectural wisdom, not just literacy.
