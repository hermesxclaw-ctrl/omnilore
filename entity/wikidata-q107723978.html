Who It Is

No bigger than a pigeon and perched on legs so short its breast almost brushes the ground, the Chabo carries its enormous upright tail like a folded fan held high above its head — a living ornament bred for centuries to be beautiful, not useful, and to die if its defining gene doubles.

Described consistently across fancier literature as docile, tame, and highly broody. Hens are noted as excellent broodies and mothers despite small size, often used as foster mothers for pheasant or quail eggs in the fancy — a practical trait noted by Tegetmeier (1867) and all subsequent standards. Cocks are reported as less aggressive than Shamo or other game-derived breeds, with a relatively low crow volume, making them suitable for urban keeping where permitted. Birds are active, upright in carriage, and tolerant of confinement but require dry, sheltered housing due to low ground clearan

Powers
Creeper chondrodystrophy (Cp) - extreme leg shortening

Heterozygous Cp/cp genotype produces severe shortening of long bones (tibiotarsus, tarsometatarsus) resulting in shanks 1/3 to 1/2 normal length. This is the breed-defining trait and is autosomal incompletely dominant with recessive lethality.

Limit: Homozygous Cp/Cp is embryonic lethal (death at ~18-21 days incubation, 25% of eggs from Cp/cp x Cp/cp matings). Homozygous cp/cp birds have normal leg length and are disqualified from showing but are necessary for breeding to reduce lethality. Short legs impair walking on rough ground, increase breast feather soiling and bumblefoot risk, and reduce ability to escape predators.

Upright tail carriage and low wing carriage (breed type)

Selection for very short back and altered vertebral/ tail angle produces tail held 60-90 degrees above horizontal, often above head height, with wings held low and angled down. Creates distinctive 'U' silhouette when viewed from side.

Limit: Extreme tail angle can impede mating (requires assisted mating or feather trimming in some lines). Low wings drag and soil in wet conditions.

Broodiness and foster-mother ability

Hens exhibit strong and persistent broodiness, will incubate and rear chicks of other breeds and species. Historically used to hatch pheasant, partridge and quail eggs.

Limit: Small body size limits number of eggs covered (6-8 bantam eggs). Broodiness reduces egg production (Chabo lays ~60-80 small tinted/cream eggs per year, not a layer breed).

Fibromelanosis in Shinguro type (hyperpigmentation)

Shinguro Chabo exhibits dermal and connective tissue melanism (black skin, comb, wattles, shanks, meat, periosteum) due to fibromelanosis (Fm) duplication involving EDN3, as in Silkie and Ayam Cemani.

Limit: Not recognised outside Japan; black comb is disqualification in Western standards. Associated with slightly reduced hatchability in some fibromelanotic lines.

Frizzle, Silkie-feathered and hen-feathered plumage variants

Frizzle (F) causes feathers to curl outward; Silkie (h) causes lack of hamuli giving hair-like plumage; hen-feathering (Hf) causes males to grow rounded female-type hackle and saddle feathers due to aromatase upregulation.

Limit: Frizzle homozygous (F/F) produces brittle feathers and is avoided; Silkie-feathered birds have poor wet-weather tolerance and cannot fly; hen-feathered cocks may have reduced fertility signaling.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The Chabo is a true bantam (no large counterpart) with extreme chondrodystrophy producing shanks typically 2.0-3.5 cm long in heterozygous Cp/cp birds. The body is compact, broad and low, with a very short back and a large single comb (five to six points, ideally straight in males, may flop in females) and large wattles and earlobes (red comb/wattles, red or white earlobes depending on variety; Shinguro type has black pigmentation). Accounts I
Derivation from Shamo fighting chickens via selective breedingKomiyama et al. Gene 2003/2004; Oka et al. Animal Genetics 2007; Japanese Avian Bioresource literature

Edo period (17th-19th century), reconstructed via 21st-century mtDNA

Mitochondrial D-loop sequencing of all major Japanese native breeds shows Chabo haplotypes nest within the Shamo (Japanese game fowl) clade, not with Red Junglefowl directly or with Chinese bantams. This indicates Chabo was created in Japan by dwarfing selection from the already domesticated Shamo fighting stock, likely by selecting the smallest, shortest-legged offspring over many generations. Historical trade records suggest Southeast Asian small chickens may have contributed, but maternal lineage is Shamo-derived. The creeper mutation was then fixed in heterozygous state. Explains why Chabo shares game-bird body traits (broad chest, large comb, upright posture) despite ornamental use; refutes earlier European assumption that Chabo was imported directly from Java as a wild species. Supports classification as true bantam developed in Japan.

The Jan Steen chicken - earliest European image c.1660Jan Steen, Portrait of Jacoba Maria van Wassenaer; discussed in Roberts, British Poultry Standards (2008) and Ekarius (2007)

c.1660 (early Edo, Kanbun era in Japan)

In Jan Steen's portrait of the young Dutch noblewoman Jacoba Maria van Wassenaer, a small white chicken with conspicuously short legs and a tall upright tail appears at the sitter's feet. Poultry historians identify this as a Chabo, predating the Meiji export era by 200 years. It suggests that occasional Chabo reached Europe via Dutch East India Company (VOC) trade through Dejima, Nagasaki, as curiosities for the elite, even during sakoku (closed country) when Japan restricted foreign trade to the Dutch and Chinese. Provides terminus ante quem for the existence of the Chabo phenotype by mid-17th century; used as evidence that the breed was already stabilized in Japan before 1636 sakoku edicts.

Sakoku and the Meiji opening - first documented exports 1860sTegetmeier 1867; Lewis Wright 1880; FAO DAD-IS history

1636-1868 (sakoku) to 1860s-1870s (Meiji Restoration)

Under Tokugawa sakoku, Japan was closed to foreign trade except via Nagasaki. The Chabo was kept as an ornamental of daimyo and wealthy townspeople and not exported. After the 1854 Perry Expedition and 1868 Meiji Restoration, Japan opened. The first documented exports of Chabo to Europe and the United States occurred in the 1860s. Tegetmeier notes the breed 'apparently reached the United Kingdom in the 1860s' but was too new to be included in his 1865 first British Poultry Standard; he described it fully in The Poultry Book (1867) from imported birds. American imports followed via Yokohama. Established Chabo as an exhibition breed in the West; led to rapid multiplication of colour varieties through outcrossing in Europe and America, diverging from Japanese type.

Accounts II
The Crystal Palace and the Japanese Bantam Club 1912Poultry Club of Great Britain archives; Roberts 2008

1912

At the Crystal Palace Poultry Show in London — the world's largest poultry show at the time — British Chabo enthusiasts formed the Japanese Bantam Club (later Japanese Bantam Club of Great Britain). The club standardized the Western type, promoted the breed, and maintained the breed standard separate from the Poultry Club's general standard. This institutionalization marked the transition from imported curiosity to established exhibition breed with formal judging criteria. Creation of a dedicated breed club ensured preservation and promotion; Western colour varieties proliferated under club auspices. The club remains active today.

International Chabo Bantam Club - Switzerland 1937International Chabo Bantam Club records; EE archives

1937

At a meeting in Switzerland in 1937, fanciers from several European countries founded the International Chabo Bantam Club (Internationaler Chabo Bantam Club). This reflected the breed's pan-European popularity and the need to harmonize standards across German, French, Dutch and British variants. The club coordinated the EE standard and facilitated exchange of breeding stock across borders in the interwar period. Established international governance for the breed; helped preserve bloodlines through World War II disruptions. The club continues to publish the international standard and holds European shows.

Japanese native types - Higo, Okina and Shinguro divergenceJapanese Poultry Association (Nihon Kei Hozonkai) standards; Okada and other Japanese breed monographs

Edo to Showa (18th-20th century), codified late 20th century

Within Japan, regional selection produced distinct Chabo types: In Higo (Kumamoto) on Kyushu, breeders selected for an enormous, deeply serrated comb, creating Higo-Chabo, with two tail variants — Daruma (short, rounded tail not exceeding head height, named for the daruma doll) and Taikan (normal upright Chabo tail). On Okinawa, the Okina Chabo (also called bearded Chabo) was selected for a beard and muffs (feathering under beak). The Shinguro (or Kuro Chabo) was selected for complete fibromelanosis, producing a wholly black bird with black comb, face and legs, valued for its rarity. These types are maintained as varieties of Chabo in Japan but are often unknown or not recognised in the West. Demonstrates that 'Chabo' in Japan is a breed group with internal diversity, not a single Western show type; conservation efforts now treat these as genetic resources. Western frizzle/Silkie Chabo have no counterpart in this Japanese typology.

The Japanese Bantam is simply a miniaturized version of a large chicken breed — It is a true bantam with no large counterpart. It was not created by shrinking a large fowl but by dwarfing Shamo game stock and fixing the creeper mutation; there is no large Chabo.

Short legs are a healthy, harmless cute trait — Short legs are caused by a chondrodystrophy (Cp) that is lethal in homozygous form, causing 25% embryo death in standard matings and associated leg/foot problems. It is a deleterious mutation maintained for aesthetics.

Chabo are wild or jungle fowl from Japan — They are fully domestic (Gallus gallus domesticus), developed through selective breeding in captivity, with no wild population. Their ancestors are domestic Shamo, themselves domesticated from Red Junglefowl.

All Japanese Bantams have frizzled or Silkie feathers — Frizzle, Silkie-feathered and hen-feathered Chabo are Western-developed variants introgressed into Chabo; traditional Japanese Chabo has normal hard feathers. These variants are not recognised in Japan.

The breed is ancient, dating to time immemorial in Japan — Earliest secure depiction is c.1660 and breed likely stabilized in early Edo (17th century). It is not attested in Heian or medieval sources, and genetic evidence points to relatively recent derivation from Shamo.
