Who It Is

On the vast, wind-scoured steppes of Mongolia, where the sky meets the earth in an unbroken line, there is a figure who embodies the harsh mercy of winter. Tsagaan Übgen, the White Old Man, is not a distant god of thunder or war, but a presence felt in the biting cold, the first snowfall, and the quiet wisdom of elders. He is the guardian of animals, the protector of the weak, and the keeper of the balance between humanity and the wild—a deity who asks not for worship, but for respect.

He is wise, benevolent, and protective, especially of children, animals, and the poor. He enforces moral codes, rewarding good deeds and punishing cruelty. He is known to be approachable and kind, but can be stern when necessary.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Mongolian: 'Tsagaan' (white) + 'Öbgen' (old man). The name literally means 'White Old Man', reflecting his snow-white beard, hair, and clothing, and his association with winter and age.

Pronunciation — /tsɑːˈɡɑːn ˈʊbɡən/ (approx. tsah-GAHN OOB-gen)

Aliases — Sagaan Ubgen, White Old Man, Tsagan Ebugen, Tsagaan Ebugen, Sagan Ubgen, White Elder

Powers
Control over weather and winter

He can bring snow, frost, and cold, and is believed to have power over the winter season.

Limit: Not clearly defined; may be limited by the will of higher gods (e.g., Tengri)

Protection of animals

He is the patron of wild animals, especially deer, and punishes hunters who kill without need.

Limit: His protection may be conditional on respect and ritual offerings.

Granting longevity and fertility

He can grant long life, health, and fertility to people and animals.

Limit: Often requires prayers or offerings.

Wisdom and prophecy

He is considered wise and can reveal hidden knowledge or foretell the future.

Limit: He may only speak through dreams or visions.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Tsagaan Übgen is typically depicted as a white-bearded, white-haired old man dressed in white clothing, often holding a long staff and sometimes accompanied by animals. He may appear as a hermit or a wandering elder. In some depictions, he carries a rosary or a book.

Symbols — His white color symbolizes purity, winter, and old age. His staff represents authority and support. Animals, especially deer and birds, are his companions. In Buddhist art, he may be shown with a halo or other divine attributes.

Domains — He resides in mountains, forests, and remote places, but also appears in human settlements, especially during winter festivals. He is associated with the cold season, snow, and the wilderness.

Accounts I
The White Old Man and the HunterMongolian folktale

Unknown (traditional)

A hunter encounters Tsagaan Übgen in the forest. The old man warns him not to kill more animals than needed. The hunter ignores the warning and slaughters a whole herd. Tsagaan Übgen then curses him to become a beggar, and the hunter loses all his wealth and health until he repents and makes offerings. The hunter learns respect for nature and becomes a protector of animals.

Accounts II
The White Old Man and the ChildrenBuryat legend

Unknown

During a harsh winter, two children are lost. Tsagaan Übgen appears as an old man, gives them shelter and food, and leads them back to their village. He asks for nothing in return but tells them to be kind to animals. The children grow up to be respected elders, and the village builds a shrine to the White Old Man.

The Contest with the DevilKalmyk legend

Unknown

A devil challenges Tsagaan Übgen to a contest of magic. The devil tries to freeze the world with his breath, but Tsagaan Übgen melts it with his warmth. The devil then tries to kill him with a lightning bolt, but Tsagaan Übgen catches it and turns it into a staff. The devil is defeated and flees, and Tsagaan Übgen becomes the guardian of the winter solstice.
