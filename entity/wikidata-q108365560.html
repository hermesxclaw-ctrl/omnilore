Who It Is

Erasippus never gets a statue, a speech, or even a single line of dialogue in surviving myth — he is one of fifty sons born in a bewildering week when Heracles, guest of King Thespius, fathered a child on each of the king's daughters while hunting a lion on Mount Cithaeron. His name, 'lover of horses,' survives only in lists, yet his fate — drafted into a colonial expedition to Sardinia under Iolaus — ties him to one of the earliest Greek myths of state-sponsored colonization.

No personality, speech, moral character, or behavior is attested for Erasippus as an individual. He does not speak or act independently in any surviving narrative. His only actions are collective: being born, being counted among the fifty, and being dispatched as part of the forty to Sardinia. Disputed/hypothetical: later mythographers imply obedience to Heracles' colonial instructions, but this is a collective fate, not individual characterization.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Ancient Greek Ἐράσιππος (Erasippos). Conventionally derived from eramai / eraō (ἐράω, 'to love, desire') + hippos (ἵππος, 'horse'), thus 'lover of horses' or 'desiring horses.' The -ippos element is common in Greek heroic names (e.g., Xanthippos, Melanippos). No ancient etymology is explicitly given

Pronunciation — Ancient Greek: /e.rá.si.pːos/ (eh-RAH-sip-pos), Modern English: eh-ruh-SIP-uss. Stress on second syllable in Greek; anglicized Er-a-SIP-pus.

Aliases — Erasippos, Ἐράσιππος

Powers
Heraclid Lineage (No Specific Superhuman Power Attested)

As son of Heracles, Erasippus possesses heroic descent but no source attributes to him any of Heracles' superhuman strength, divine parentage benefits, or specific powers. The Thespiadae as a group are not credited with individual feats comparable to their father.

Limit: Mortal; no invulnerability, no divine protection, no magical ability described. Subject to Heracles' paternal authority regarding colonial dispatch.

Colonial Founder Status (Collective)

As one of the forty sent to Sardinia with Iolaus, Erasippus participates in the mythic colonization — founding cities, introducing Greek customs and the cult of Iolaus/Heracles to the island. Diodorus describes the colonists as seizing the best land and establishing lasting settlements.

Limit: Achievement is collective; no individual foundation is credited to Erasippus by name. Later Sardinian archaeology shows Nuragic culture continuity, so mythic colonization is etiological, not historical fact.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No ancient source describes Erasippus' physical appearance, height, hair, clothing, or distinguishing marks. As a son of Heracles and a Thespian princess, he would be implicitly understood as Heraclid — of heroic stature — but this is inference, not attested description. No vase painting, sculpture, or coin has been identified as depicting Erasippus individually. Attested: male, human, mortal (though Heraclid lineage). Unknown: all visual details

Accounts I
Heracles and the Cithaeronian LionApollodorus Bibliotheca 2.4.9-10; Diodorus Siculus 4.29.2

Mythic heroic age, narrated in Hellenistic/Roman mythography; lion hunt as youthful Heracles labor before canonical Twelve Labors

At age eighteen, Heracles, dwelling with Amphitryon, hunts the lion that preyed on the herds of Amphitryon and Thespius on Mount Cithaeron. King Thespius of Thespiae hosts Heracles for an extended period during the hunt. The lion is eventually slain (later Heracles wears its skin in some traditions, though the Nemean lion later supersedes it). The hospitality sets the stage for the liaison with the Thespiades. Establishes Heracles' early heroic reputation and creates debt of hospitality that Thespius exploits to obtain Heraclean grandchildren; provides etiology for Thespian Heracles cult.

The Night(s) with the Daughters of ThespiusApollodorus 2.4.10; Athenaeus 13.556F citing Herodorus; Diodorus 4.29.2; Tzetzes Chiliades 2.221

Mythic heroic age; variant chronologies preserved in Classical to Byzantine sources

Wishing to have grandchildren by Heracles, Thespius sends his fifty daughters to Heracles' bed, one per night (or all in one night, according to Herodorus). Heracles, thinking each is the same woman or unaware due to darkness/wine, lies with each. Lysippe, daughter of Thespius (mother variously listed), conceives Erasippus. In one variant, one daughter refuses and Heracles condemns her to virgin priesthood. Conception of fifty sons including Erasippus; establishes the Heracleidae-Thespiadae lineage; later used to explain Thespian aristocratic claims of Heraclean descent.

Accounts II
Birth and Naming of the Fifty SonsApollodorus 2.4.10 (list of names and mothers); Tzetzes Chiliades 2.221-227

Hellenistic mythographic compilation preserving earlier Boeotian genealogy

Each daughter bears a son. Apollodorus provides the only complete surviving roster linking each mother to son: Lysippe bears Erasippus. The list functions as genealogical charter rather than narrative. No individual birth story or omen is given for Erasippus. Creates a cohort of Heraclids who will later be divided for political purposes; provides Thespiae with a surplus of royal heirs.

Heracles' Instructions for the SonsApollodorus 2.7.6; Diodorus Siculus 4.29.4

Mythic, recounted in Hellenistic mythography as Heracles' later command before his apotheosis

Years later, Heracles sends a message to Thespius from abroad (or before his death) instructing him how to dispose of the sons: keep seven, send three to Thebes, and send forty under Iolaus to found a colony in Sardinia. This tripartite division reflects archaic Greek colonization practice and Theban-Thespian relations. Erasippus is assigned to the Sardinian contingent (the forty). The seven retained become Thespian aristocrats; the three sent to Thebes integrate into Theban Heracleidae.

The Sardinian Colony of Iolaus and the ThespiadaeDiodorus Siculus 4.29.5-4.30.1; Pausanias 9.27.7, 10.17.5; Apollodorus 2.7.6

Mythic colonization narrative, possibly reflecting 8th-6th century BCE Greek knowledge of Sardinia

Joined by Iolaus, the forty sons including Erasippus sail to Sardinia. There they subdue the native inhabitants, seize the fertile plains, found cities, and introduce Greek institutions. Iolaus is heroized in Sardinia with a cult and tombs (the Iolaia). The sons become ancestors of Sardinian peoples; Diodorus claims they built works called Iolaia and that Sardinia was called Iolaon after Iolaus. Etiology for Greek presence and Heraclean cult in Sardinia; explains Thespian-Sardinian kinship claims; Erasippus disappears from narrative after arrival — no further individual deeds, death, or descendants are named for him specifically.

Erasippus was a famous hero with his own labors or cult like his father Heracles. — No ancient source gives Erasippus any individual deed, power, cult, or story beyond being one of the fifty. He is a genealogical name in a list, not an active hero. Only the collective 'Thespiadae' has significance, and only Iolaus among the Sardinian group received cult.

Heracles spent a single romantic night with Thespius' daughters by mutual consent. — Ancient sources frame the episode as Thespius' scheme, with Heracles possibly deceived (thinking it was the same woman) and daughters' consent not discussed. One variant explicitly punishes a daughter for refusing. Modern sanitized retellings omit coercive/deceptive elements.

The Sardinian colony is historical fact proving Greek settlement of Sardinia by Heracles' sons. — The Sardinian expedition is mythic etiology. Archaeology shows continuous Nuragic culture in Sardinia with limited early Greek presence; Diodorus and Pausanias are explaining the Iolaus cult and place-names (Iolaia), not recording history. Erasippus has no historical footprint.

Erasippus' mother is interchangeable with any Thespian daughter. — Apollodorus specifically pairs Lysippe with Erasippus. While Diodorus does not name mothers individually, the Apollodoran pairing is the only precise attestation and should not be arbitrarily reassigned.
