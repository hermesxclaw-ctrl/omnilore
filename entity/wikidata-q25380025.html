Form

No textual description of Ammamma's appearance survives. No iconographic representations have been securely identified. The cult inventory texts from Taḫurpa mention a cult statue, but its form is not described in the surviving fragments.

Powers

Tutelary Protection As the dLAMMA of Taḫurpa, she was believed to protect the city, its walls, its people, and its livestock from harm, both physical and supernatural. Hittite cult inventory texts Her power was likely geographically limited to her city and its immediate environs. None recorded.

Oath Enforcement Invoked in treaties, she was believed to have the power to curse and punish those who broke their oaths, bringing divine retribution upon them and their families. Hittite state treaties (CTH 42, CTH 62) Her power was activated by the oath; it was a conditional power. None recorded.

Behavior & Habitat

As a tutelary deity (dLAMMA), Ammamma was believed to protect her city, Taḫurpa, and its inhabitants. She was a divine witness to treaties, implying a role as an enforcer of oaths and a punisher of perjury. Her character was likely that of a local guardian goddess, fierce in protection and just in judgment.

Accounts

The city of Taḫurpa, located in the vicinity of the Hittite capital Ḫattusa, maintained a temple and cult for its tutelary goddess Ammamma. The cult inventory texts, commissioned by King Tudḫaliya IV, record the goddess's temple equipment, the provisions for her offerings (bread, beer, meat), and the festivals held in her honor. She was a central figure in the local religious life, a divine patron whose favor was essential for the city's well-being.

When Hittite kings made treaties with foreign rulers, they invoked a vast pantheon of deities to serve as witnesses. Ammamma was consistently included in these lists. Her role was to observe the oath-taking and to bring divine punishment upon the party that broke the agreement. The treaties are formulaic, but the inclusion of a local Hattian goddess like Ammamma highlights her integration into the Hittite imperial religious framework.

Scholarly Debates
Ammamma vs. Mamma
Ammamma and Mamma are the same deity, with 'Am-' being a prosthetic vowel or a variant spelling. ⇌ Ammamma and Mamma are distinct deities, possibly with different origins or cult centers.
Evidence for A: The names appear in similar contexts, and the variation is consistent with Hattian scribal practice.
Evidence for B: The names are not always used interchangeably; 'Mamma' might refer to a different, less-attested figure.
Connection to Mother Goddesses
Ammamma should be understood as a mother-goddess figure, given the reduplicative 'ma' structure and her tutelary role. ⇌ There is no evidence for a maternal aspect. Her role is strictly that of a local tutelary deity, and comparisons to mother goddesses are anachronistic.
Evidence for A: Tutelary deities in Anatolia often had maternal or protective aspects. The name structure is similar to other divine nam
Evidence for B: No text describes her as a mother. Her cult is focused on city protection, not fertility or childbirth.
What We Don't Know

The primary gap is the lack of any narrative mythology for Ammamma. We have no myths, epics, or stories about her. Her character is known only through cultic and legal texts. The appearance of her cult statue is unknown. The relationship between Ammamma and Mamma is unresolved. Her role in non-state, popular religion is unknown. The Hattian-language sources are poorly understood, limiting our ability to fully interpret her name and nature. Research is limited to the surviving Hittite and Hattian cuneiform corpus from Boğazköy (Ḫattusa) and other sites.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Ammamma as the patron saint of a small town — like a local guardian saint in medieval Europe. She wasn't a big-name, state-wide deity like the Storm God, but in her town, she was everything. Her 'power' was the power of a community's faith and the fear of divine retribution that kept people honest. She's the divine equivalent of a small-town mayor who is also the judge, jury, and town hero, all rolled into one.
