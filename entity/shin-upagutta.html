Who It Is

In the shimmering heat of a Burmese summer, when the rains came late and the people prayed for deliverance, they turned not to a distant god but to a gentle, smiling monk who had, according to legend, bound the great serpent king himself. This is Shin Upagutta, the guardian saint of Myanmar, a figure of immense power and profound humility, whose story weaves through centuries of Buddhist tradition and the living faith of millions.

Shin Upagutta is known for his compassion, humility, and protective nature. He is believed to be ever-watchful, guarding the faithful from dangers both physical and spiritual. He is also known for his wit and cleverness, as seen in his dealings with the naga king. Despite his power, he remains a humble monk, demonstrating the Buddhist ideal of non-attachment.

Powers
Supernatural powers (iddhi)

As an arahat, Shin Upagutta possesses various supernatural abilities, including telepathy, levitation, and the ability to create illusions. He uses these powers to protect the faithful and to preach the Dharma.

Limit: His powers are bound by the Buddhist precepts; he does not use them for selfish purposes.

Binding of the Naga King

His most famous feat is his binding of the naga king, who was causing storms and flooding. Shin Upagutta used his powers to capture and subdue the naga, forcing it to cease its destructive behavior and to aid in the construction of a stupa.

Limit: The naga was bound only after the naga's own pride led it to challenge the monk; Upagutta acted with compassion, not violence.

Guardianship of the faithful

Shin Upagutta is believed to protect people from dangers, especially during the rainy season and at sea. He is invoked for safety and for protection from wild animals and evil spirits.

Limit: His protection is conditional on the faith and morality of the person.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Shin Upagutta is typically depicted as a Buddhist monk, often seated in meditation or standing with a serene expression. He may be shown holding a staff or a begging bowl. In some representations, he wears the traditional yellow or saffron robes of a Theravada monk. He is sometimes portrayed with a small naga (serpent) coiled nearby, symbolizing his conquest of the naga king.

Symbols — Key symbols include the lotus (purity), the alms bowl (renunciation), and the naga (serpent) as a subdued force. In Burmese art, he may be shown with a halo or aura, indicating his enlightened status.

Domains — Shin Upagutta is associated with the human realm, particularly Myanmar, where he is a guardian of the land and its people. He is also linked to the naga world, as he is said to have subdued the naga king. In some traditions, he resides in a hidden monastery or on a mythical mountain, from where he watches over the faithful.

Accounts I
The Binding of the Naga KingBurmese and Thai legend

Unknown; likely ancient

Once, the naga king, angered by an insult or a challenge, created storms and floods that threatened the people and the construction of a great stupa (often attributed to King Asoka). The people prayed for help, and Shin Upagutta, a monk of great power, came to their aid. He used his supernatural abilities to create a beautiful chariot or a golden cage, which the proud naga entered out of curiosity or vanity. Once inside, Upagutta bound the naga with a spell, rendering it powerless. The naga, humbled, begged for mercy. Upagutta released it on the condition that it cease its destructive ways and help in the building of the stupa. The naga agreed and became a protector of the faith. The naga became a protector of Buddhism, and Shin Upagutta became revered as a guardian saint. The story reinforces the power of the Dharma over worldly forces.

Accounts II
Protection of the Buddha's RelicsBurmese tradition

Unknown

Shin Upagutta is said to have protected the relics of the Buddha from being taken by evil forces. He used his powers to hide or guard the relics, ensuring they remained safe for the faithful. His actions ensured the continued presence of the Buddha's teachings in the world.
