Who It Is

In the boiling black pitch of the fifth pit, where corrupt politicians bob like dumplings in tar, a demon with an evil tail steps forward and offers Dante a deal — a guide, a shortcut, and a precise timestamp for the death of Christ. His name is Malacoda, the only major devil in Hell whom Dante invented from scratch, and every word he says is a test of whether a pilgrim can tell courtesy from con artistry in a place built on fraud.

Attested behavior in text: Malacoda is authoritative, verbally adept, and duplicitous. He halts his subordinates when they threaten Virgil and Dante (XXI.79-84), questions Virgil's purpose, accepts Virgil's claim of divine mandate ('vuolsi così colà dove si puote'), and grants safe conduct. He then volunteers information about the broken bridge and offers an armed escort of ten demons, framing it as helpful. First attested in Dante's Inferno, Canto XXI, line 76: 'Allor si volse a noi e disse: "..." Malacoda'. The name follows Dante's systematic onomastic invention for the ten named Malebra

Powers
Command of the Malebranche

As 'capo' or leader, Malacoda can order, restrain, and dispatch the other Malebranche. He stops Barbariccia and others from attacking Dante and Virgil with a word, and orders ten demons to escort the poets. His signal (cenno) is instantly obeyed, even when obscene.

Limit: Authority appears limited to the twelve assigned to Bolgia Five; he cannot command demons of other bolgias. His subordinates are unruly and brawl among themselves, suggesting imperfect control.

Knowledge of Hell's Topography and History

Malacoda knows the layout of Malebolge, the location of ridges, crags, and the fact that the bridges over Bolgia Six collapsed. He provides a precise chronological calculation: 1,266 years since the road was broken, five hours from now on the previous day, linking to the earthquake at Christ's death/Harrowing of Hell.

Limit: Knowledge is either flawed or deliberately falsified: he claims a nearby crag yields passage to the next bolgia, but Virgil later discovers all bridges over Bolgia Six are broken and they must climb via ruins. Whether error or lie is debated.

Deception and Fraudulent Persuasion

Malacoda's primary power is verbal fraud - offering safe conduct and guidance that is technically helpful but contains a false premise (that another bridge exists intact). This mirrors the sin punished in his bolgia and exemplifies fraud as intellectual sin.

Limit: Deception is constrained by divine providence: he cannot directly harm Dante due to Virgil's invocation of divine will, and cannot leave his bolgia to pursue. His lie is transparent to Virgil upon inspection.

Limited Jurisdiction by Divine Providence

Not a power but a divinely imposed boundary that defines his existence: he and his fiends are ministers of divine will within Bolgia Five and are simultaneously denied the power to exit it. This is both empowerment and imprisonment.

Limit: Cannot pursue Dante and Virgil beyond the pitch; cannot enter Bolgia Six; cannot act outside Malebolge. This limitation enables the poets' escape by sliding down the embankment into the next bolgia where devils cannot follow.

Obscene Signal / Parodic Military Command

Malacoda communicates via a 'cenno' (sign) that is answered by a subordinate making a trumpet of his anus - a thunderous fart. This functions as a military trumpet call in Hell's parodic army, demonstrating command through grotesque inversion of chivalric/courtly signals.

Limit: Purely communicative and comic; no supernatural effect beyond signaling. Highlights Dante's use of low bodily humor to degrade the demonic.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Attested: Dante gives no direct physical description of Malacoda distinct from the generic Malebranche. No height, color, wings, horns, or tail are described in the text itself, despite the name implying a tail. The Malebranche collectively are described as black, pitch-covered, winged, and armed with grappling hooks (uncini, raffi) and they fly or run along the dikes. The Malebranche's hooks (uncini) symbolize the grafters' own hooked, grasping hands. Accounts I
The Barrators' Pit and the Threat to the PilgrimsDante Alighieri, Inferno, Canto XXI, lines 1-75 (setup) and 76-90 (Malacoda's intervention)

1308-1321

Dante and Virgil arrive at the fifth bolgia to find barrators submerged in boiling pitch, guarded by demons with grappling hooks who fish them out like meat from a cauldron. As the poets watch, a demon arrives carrying a newly arrived barrator from Lucca. Virgil tells Dante to hide while he negotiates. The Malebranche threaten Virgil, and Barbariccia and others advance with hooks. Malacoda, their leader, intervenes, halting the attack and demanding to know Virgil's business. Establishes Malacoda's authority and the rule that divine mandate overrides demonic violence. Sets up the negotiation that allows the narrative to continue through Malebolge.

The Negotiation for Safe ConductDante Alighieri, Inferno, Canto XXI, lines 90-105

1308-1321

Virgil explains to Malacoda that their journey is willed in Heaven ('vuolsi così colà dove si puote ciò che si vuole'), a formula previously used to pass the guardian devils at the gates of Dis. Malacoda accepts this, his wrath falling ('l'ira sua cadde'), and grants them safe passage. He then volunteers strategic intelligence about the terrain ahead, presenting himself as helpful guide rather than jailer. Virgil calls Dante forth from hiding; the poets are now under Malacoda's ostensible protection. Demonstrates the legalistic nature of Hell - even demons must obey higher providential permission. Foreshadows Malacoda's deceit.

The Lie of the Bridges and the Dating of the HarrowingDante Alighieri, Inferno, Canto XXI, lines 106-126

1308-1321

Malacoda tells the poets they cannot continue along the ridge because the sixth arch (bridge over Bolgia Six) lies broken at the bottom. He directs them to a nearby crag where another passage yields. To prove his knowledge, he gives a precise timestamp: 'Yesterday, five hours from now, it was 1,266 years since the road was broken here.' This dates the earthquake that shattered Hell's bridges to the death of Christ (traditionally 34 AD at noon, per Luke 23:44), placing Dante's journey on Holy Saturday morning, 9 April 1300, about 7 a.m. He then says he is sending men along that way to see if anyone is taking the air, and offers them as escort. Provides one of the Commedia's key chronological anchors, allowing scholars to date the fictional journey. Narratively, it sends the poets forward with false confidence. Virgil later discovers (Canto XXIII.133-141) that Malacoda lied - all bridges over Bolgia Six are broken, not just one. The lie forces the poets to scramble down the collapsed ruins, leading to the encounter with the Hypocrites.

Accounts II
The Dispatch of the Ten and the Obscene TrumpetDante Alighieri, Inferno, Canto XXI, lines 118-139 and Canto XXII, lines 1-12

1308-1321

Malacoda selects ten Malebranche - Barbariccia as decurion, plus Alichino, Calcabrina, Cagnazzo, Libicocco, Draghignazzo, Ciriatto, Graffiacane, Farfarello, and Rubicante - to escort the poets to the next crag. He warns them not to harm Dante. As final command, he gives a signal (cenno), and another demon responds by making a trumpet of his anus, producing a thunderous fart that Dante compares to a trombone. The troop sets off, with Virgil and Dante following warily. Creates the famous 'devil escort' episode, a parody of a military detachment. The scatological signal establishes the Malebranche as grotesquely comic, undermining their menace. Malacoda himself does not join the escort; he remains in Bolgia Five, as later clarified.

The Brawl in the Pitch and the Failed PursuitDante Alighieri, Inferno, Canto XXII, lines 13-151 and Canto XXIII, lines 13-58

1308-1321

While escorted, Dante watches the demons torment barrators. A Navarrese barrator is fished out and interrogated; he tricks Alichino and Calcabrina by promising to call other grafters, then dives back into pitch. Alichino pursues but fails to catch him; Calcabrina, in mutual recrimination, grapples Alichino and both tumble into the boiling pitch, requiring the others to disentangle them. Seizing the chaos, Virgil grabs Dante and slides down the steep embankment into Bolgia Six, fleeing the escort. The demons attempt pursuit but are bound by providential law: they cannot leave the fifth pit. Dante hears them chasing like hounds but they cannot follow beyond the dike. Virgil later confirms Malacoda's lie when they find no intact bridge. Demonstrates the limits of Malacoda's power and the anarchy of his troops. The poets' narrow escape transitions the narrative from Bolgia Five to Bolgia Six (Hypocrites). Malacoda is not seen again; his final fate is to remain governing Bolgia Five eternally. The episode proves that demonic promises in fraud's realm are inherently untrustworthy.

The Providential Boundary - Ministers of the Fifth PitDante Alighieri, Inferno, Canto XXI, lines 127-135

1308-1321

Immediately after dispatching the escort, Malacoda (or the narrator clarifying his status) explains the theological rule governing the Malebranche: the Providence that gave them the fifth pit to govern as ministers of its will takes from their souls the power to leave it. This gloss explains why Malacoda can offer an escort only along the ridge within his jurisdiction and why the poets can escape by leaving the bolgia. Establishes the paradox of demonic authority as both empowered and imprisoned. Provides narrative justification for the poets' survival and for Malacoda's disappearance after Canto XXI. Frequently cited in theological readings of Dante's demonology as example of evil being instrumentalized by divine justice.

Malacoda is a demon from medieval folklore, demonology, or classical mythology like Geryon or Cerberus. — Malacoda is entirely Dante's invention, first attested in Inferno XXI (c.1314-1319). Unlike Geryon (classical), Cerberus (Greco-Roman), or even Malebranche subordinates with possible folkloric echoes, Malacoda has no pre-Dantean source. Early commentators explicitly note he is 'fictus a poeta.'

Malacoda can leave his bolgia and chase Dante into the next ditch, or appears later in Inferno. — Malacoda is bound by Providence to Bolgia Five and cannot leave it. He disappears from narrative after dispatching the escort in Canto XXI and is not present in the chase (Canto XXIII) or later. His power is strictly jurisdictional.

Malacoda told the truth about the bridges and was genuinely helpful. — Virgil discovers in Canto XXIII that Malacoda's claim that 'nearby's another crag that yields a passage' over Bolgia Six is false - all bridges over that bolgia are broken. Whether intentional lie or error, the information is false and forces a difficult climb. Dante uses this to illustrate that fraud cannot be trusted even when polite.

Malacoda's name means 'bad head' or is related to 'Malachor' or Hebrew mal'akh. — It is Italian mala + coda ('tail'), not capo ('head') and has no Hebrew etymology. The confusion arises from similar-sounding demon names in occult literature (e.g., Malphas, Malacoda-like invented grimoire names).

The fart trumpet was a crude addition by translators, not in Dante's original. — The line 'ed elli avea del cul fatto trombetta' (XXI.139) is in all manuscripts and critical editions. Dante deliberately wrote scatological comedy; it is not translator embellishment. Some Victorian translations euphemized it, creating impression it was added later.
