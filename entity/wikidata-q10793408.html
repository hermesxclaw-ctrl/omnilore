The Approach

In the boiling black pitch of the fifth pit, where corrupt politicians bob like dumplings in tar, a demon with an evil tail steps forward and offers Dante a deal — a guide, a shortcut, and a precise timestamp for the death of Christ. His name is Malacoda, the only major devil in Hell whom Dante invented from scratch, and every word he says is a test of whether a pilgrim can tell courtesy from con artistry in a place built on fraud.

Content advisory. Contains discussion of eternal torture (immersion in boiling pitch, grappling-hook mutilation), grotesque scatological humor (farts as military signals), deception, and medieval Christian cosmology of Hell. No sexual violence attested for this figure.
Identity

Malacoda

Pronunciation Italian: [ˌmalaˈkoːda] (mah-lah-KOH-dah), stress on penultimate syllable. English adaptation: /ˌmæləˈkoʊdə/ (MAL-uh-KOH-duh). In Tuscan 14th-century pronunciation, similar but with purer vowels and dental /t/. Dante's hendecasyllable requires four syllables: Ma-la-co-da.

Known As

Malacoda of the MalebrancheLeader of the MalebrancheIl Duca dei Malebranche (the Duke/Chief of the Evil Claws) - descriptive epithet in commentary tradition

Etymology

Italian mala + coda = 'evil tail' or 'bad tail'. From Latin mala (feminine of malus, 'bad, evil') + cauda ('tail'). First attested in Dante's Inferno, Canto XXI, line 76: 'Allor si volse a noi e disse: "..." Malacoda'. The name follows Dante's systematic onomastic invention for the ten named Malebranche (Barbariccia, Alichino, Calcabrina, etc.), most of which combine pejorative or comic elements with physical grotesquerie. Unlike Geryon or Malebranche subordinates with possible folkloric echoes, Malacoda has no pre-Dantean attestation and is universally regarded by philologists as a Dantean coinage. The -a ending is feminine grammatically but applied to a masculine demon, consistent with coda being feminine in Italian.

Cultural Ownership

Italian literary creation within the Tuscan Christian visionary epic tradition. Created by Dante Alighieri (Florence, c.1265-1321) for the Commedia, specifically Inferno. Belongs to world literature via Dante's authorship; no living folk community claims ownership. Revered as part of Italy's national literary heritage and Catholic imaginative tradition, but in public domain as 14th-century text.

Disputed Identifications

No serious scholarly dispute that Malacoda is a distinct fictional character. False identifications to correct: 1) Not a pre-existing demon from demonology, hagiography, or classical myth - early commentators (Jacopo Alighieri, Guido da Pisa) already note he is 'fictus a poeta'. 2) Not to be conflated with Malacoda as modern reuse - e.g., Malacoda (American doom metal band, 2014), Malacoda the demon in the mobile game 'Obey Me!' or fanon - these are name-only homonyms. 3) Not identical to the collective 'Malebranche' - he is their leader, not the group. 4) Occasionally confused in popular summaries with Barbariccia (the sergeant who actually leads the escort of ten); Malacoda remains behind and commands.

Description

Appearance

Attested: Dante gives no direct physical description of Malacoda distinct from the generic Malebranche. No height, color, wings, horns, or tail are described in the text itself, despite the name implying a tail. The Malebranche collectively are described as black, pitch-covered, winged, and armed with grappling hooks (uncini, raffi) and they fly or run along the dikes. Malacoda is differentiated only by speech, authority, and action, not physiognomy. Reconstructed: Illustrators from Botticelli (c.1485-1495) to Gustave Doré (1861) and William Blake (1824-1827) supply visual invention: Doré shows Malacoda as a muscular, bat-winged, horned devil with a long barbed tail, often larger than his subordinates, gesturing commandingly; Blake shows a more humanoid, flame-like demon. These are adaptation-specific inventions, not textual. Unknown: Whether Dante imagined Malacoda's 'evil tail' as literal morphological feature or as moral nickname remains unstated.

Symbols & Iconography

Name-symbolism: The 'evil tail' evokes bestiality, treachery (tail as behind, hidden part), and heraldic cowardice in medieval bestiaries. The Malebranche's hooks (uncini) symbolize the grafters' own hooked, grasping hands. Iconographically, Malacoda is associated with: 1) The boiling pitch (pece) of Bolgia Five as mirror-punishment for sticky-fingered corruption; 2) The broken bridges of Bolgia Six as sign of the Harrowing of Hell and Christ's power over fraud; 3) The obscene trumpet/fart as parody of military and liturgical signals, emblematic of fraud's inversion of order. In visual tradition, he is often shown with a baton or pointing gesture, and surrounded by ten subordinates, emphasizing hierarchy.

Behaviour & Personality

Attested behavior in text: Malacoda is authoritative, verbally adept, and duplicitous. He halts his subordinates when they threaten Virgil and Dante (XXI.79-84), questions Virgil's purpose, accepts Virgil's claim of divine mandate ('vuolsi così colà dove si puote'), and grants safe conduct. He then volunteers information about the broken bridge and offers an armed escort of ten demons, framing it as helpful. His speech is courteous on surface ('O tu che siedi', formal address) but contains a calculated lie. He signals with a deliberately vulgar gesture (the 'cenno' followed by anal trumpet), showing command through grotesque comedy. Personality interpretation (scholarly): Commentators from Benvenuto to Singleton read him as the epitome of fraudulent intelligence - polite, bureaucratic, legalistic, yet fundamentally untrustworthy, mirroring the barrators he guards. Unlike the bestial Alichino or Calcabrina who brawl impulsively, Malacoda exercises cold, managerial deceit. Disputed: Whether his lie is malicious trap or merely the natural inability of devils to speak truth (see Contradictions).

Domains & Habitats

Domain: Fifth Bolgia (Bolgia Quinta) of Malebolge, the Eighth Circle of Hell, where barrators (barattieri) - those guilty of graft, bribery, and corrupt exchange of public office for money - are punished. The bolgia is a single ditch filled with boiling pitch, traversed by dikes (argini) and bridges (ponti). Malacoda's jurisdiction is explicitly limited: 'la provvidenza... a governar la quinta bolgia... toglie da lor poder di partirs' (XXI.127-129) - Providence grants them power to govern the fifth pit but takes away power to leave it. Thus he cannot leave Bolgia Five. Habitat details: He operates from the ridge (scoglio) between Bolgia Five and Six, near the collapsed bridge. He commands twelve Malebranche total, though he dispatches ten on escort. He cannot cross into Bolgia Six, which explains why Dante and Virgil must find another route after discovering the lie.

Version Differences

Textual versions: No significant variant in Malacoda's lines across manuscripts; Petrocchi vs Sanguineti editions differ only in punctuation. Translation differences affect tone: Singleton renders his speech more formal-bureaucratic; Ciardi and Mandelbaum render it more colloquial-cunning; Hollander preserves legalistic register. Adaptation versions: In Doré, he is visually dominant; in the 2010 video game Dante's Inferno he is absent (replaced by generic demons); in Peter Greenaway's film A TV Dante (1989) he is voiced with sarcastic bureaucratic menace; in modern Italian children's adaptations he is often sanitized, with the fart joke omitted or euphemized. No folklore variants exist because he is purely literary.

Powers & Abilities

Command of the Malebranche

Source VersionInferno XXI.76-90, 118-126; confirmed by early commentaries

As 'capo' or leader, Malacoda can order, restrain, and dispatch the other Malebranche. He stops Barbariccia and others from attacking Dante and Virgil with a word, and orders ten demons to escort the poets. His signal (cenno) is instantly obeyed, even when obscene.

Limitations

Authority appears limited to the twelve assigned to Bolgia Five; he cannot command demons of other bolgias. His subordinates are unruly and brawl among themselves, suggesting imperfect control.

Contradictions

None on existence of command; degree of discipline disputed - some readings emphasize his control, others note the immediate chaos after his departure shows weak control.

Knowledge of Hell's Topography and History

Source VersionInferno XXI.106-117

Malacoda knows the layout of Malebolge, the location of ridges, crags, and the fact that the bridges over Bolgia Six collapsed. He provides a precise chronological calculation: 1,266 years since the road was broken, five hours from now on the previous day, linking to the earthquake at Christ's death/Harrowing of Hell.

Limitations

Knowledge is either flawed or deliberately falsified: he claims a nearby crag yields passage to the next bolgia, but Virgil later discovers all bridges over Bolgia Six are broken and they must climb via ruins. Whether error or lie is debated.

Contradictions

If lying, his knowledge is accurate but weaponized; if mistaken, his knowledge is imperfect. See Contradictions register.

Deception and Fraudulent Persuasion

Source VersionInferno XXI.106-126; Benvenuto da Imola commentary; modern critical consensus (Barolini, Singleton)

Malacoda's primary power is verbal fraud - offering safe conduct and guidance that is technically helpful but contains a false premise (that another bridge exists intact). This mirrors the sin punished in his bolgia and exemplifies fraud as intellectual sin.

Limitations

Deception is constrained by divine providence: he cannot directly harm Dante due to Virgil's invocation of divine will, and cannot leave his bolgia to pursue. His lie is transparent to Virgil upon inspection.

Contradictions

Some older readings (e.g., 19th-century moralizing commentaries) treat his offer as genuinely helpful and the bridge error as honest mistake; majority modern view treats it as intentional deceit consistent with demonic nature.

Limited Jurisdiction by Divine Providence

Source VersionInferno XXI.127-129: 'la provvidenza... toglie da lor poder di partirs' i'

Not a power but a divinely imposed boundary that defines his existence: he and his fiends are ministers of divine will within Bolgia Five and are simultaneously denied the power to exit it. This is both empowerment and imprisonment.

Limitations

Cannot pursue Dante and Virgil beyond the pitch; cannot enter Bolgia Six; cannot act outside Malebolge. This limitation enables the poets' escape by sliding down the embankment into the next bolgia where devils cannot follow.

Contradictions

None; consistently attested. Some popular retellings incorrectly show Malacoda chasing Dante into Bolgia Six.

Obscene Signal / Parodic Military Command

Source VersionInferno XXI.136-139, XXII.1-12

Malacoda communicates via a 'cenno' (sign) that is answered by a subordinate making a trumpet of his anus - a thunderous fart. This functions as a military trumpet call in Hell's parodic army, demonstrating command through grotesque inversion of chivalric/courtly signals.

Limitations

Purely communicative and comic; no supernatural effect beyond signaling. Highlights Dante's use of low bodily humor to degrade the demonic.

Contradictions

None; universally read as satirical. Censored in some bowdlerized translations (e.g., Longfellow footnotes it delicately).

Stories & Myths

The Barrators' Pit and the Threat to the Pilgrims

Source Dante Alighieri, Inferno, Canto XXI, lines 1-75 (setup) and 76-90 (Malacoda's intervention)Culture Tuscan Italian, medieval Christian visionary epicPeriod 1308-1321

Summary

Dante and Virgil arrive at the fifth bolgia to find barrators submerged in boiling pitch, guarded by demons with grappling hooks who fish them out like meat from a cauldron. As the poets watch, a demon arrives carrying a newly arrived barrator from Lucca. Virgil tells Dante to hide while he negotiates. The Malebranche threaten Virgil, and Barbariccia and others advance with hooks. Malacoda, their leader, intervenes, halting the attack and demanding to know Virgil's business.

The Negotiation for Safe Conduct

Source Dante Alighieri, Inferno, Canto XXI, lines 90-105Culture Tuscan ItalianPeriod 1308-1321

Summary

Virgil explains to Malacoda that their journey is willed in Heaven ('vuolsi così colà dove si puote ciò che si vuole'), a formula previously used to pass the guardian devils at the gates of Dis. Malacoda accepts this, his wrath falling ('l'ira sua cadde'), and grants them safe passage. He then volunteers strategic intelligence about the terrain ahead, presenting himself as helpful guide rather than jailer.

Participants

Malacoda

Virgil

Dante (hidden, then called forth)

Consequences

Virgil calls Dante forth from hiding; the poets are now under Malacoda's ostensible protection. Demonstrates the legalistic nature of Hell - even demons must obey higher providential permission. Foreshadows Malacoda's deceit.

Variants

Some translations render Malacoda's acceptance as grudging vs. feigned courtesy; critical debate on whether his fall of anger is genuine or tactical.

The Lie of the Bridges and the Dating of the Harrowing

Source Dante Alighieri, Inferno, Canto XXI, lines 106-126Culture Tuscan Italian, medieval Christian chronologyPeriod 1308-1321

Summary

Malacoda tells the poets they cannot continue along the ridge because the sixth arch (bridge over Bolgia Six) lies broken at the bottom. He directs them to a nearby crag where another passage yields. To prove his knowledge, he gives a precise timestamp: 'Yesterday, five hours from now, it was 1,266 years since the road was broken here.' This dates the earthquake that shattered Hell's bridges to the death of Christ (traditionally 34 AD at noon, per Luke 23:44), placing Dante's journey on Holy Saturday morning, 9 April 1300, about 7 a.m. He then says he is sending men along that way to see if anyone is taking the air, and offers them as escort.

Participants

Malacoda

Virgil

Dante

Jesus Christ (referenced historically)

Consequences

Provides one of the Commedia's key chronological anchors, allowing scholars to date the fictional journey. Narratively, it sends the poets forward with false confidence. Virgil later discovers (Canto XXIII.133-141) that Malacoda lied - all bridges over Bolgia Six are broken, not just one. The lie forces the poets to scramble down the collapsed ruins, leading to the encounter with the Hypocrites.

Variants

Chronological calculation disputed: Dante follows the tradition that Christ died in 34 AD, not 33; 'five hours from now' interpreted as 7 a.m. Holy Saturday. Some commentators argue Malacoda's dating is accurate and only the bridge claim is false; others suggest both are part of deception.

The Dispatch of the Ten and the Obscene Trumpet

Source Dante Alighieri, Inferno, Canto XXI, lines 118-139 and Canto XXII, lines 1-12Culture Tuscan ItalianPeriod 1308-1321

Summary

Malacoda selects ten Malebranche - Barbariccia as decurion, plus Alichino, Calcabrina, Cagnazzo, Libicocco, Draghignazzo, Ciriatto, Graffiacane, Farfarello, and Rubicante - to escort the poets to the next crag. He warns them not to harm Dante. As final command, he gives a signal (cenno), and another demon responds by making a trumpet of his anus, producing a thunderous fart that Dante compares to a trombone. The troop sets off, with Virgil and Dante following warily.

Consequences

Creates the famous 'devil escort' episode, a parody of a military detachment. The scatological signal establishes the Malebranche as grotesquely comic, undermining their menace. Malacoda himself does not join the escort; he remains in Bolgia Five, as later clarified.

Variants

Order and spelling of demon names vary slightly in manuscripts and translations; some early prints omit one name. The identity of which demon makes the trumpet is unspecified - 'un diavolo' - but popular art often assigns it to Barbariccia or Farfarello.

The Brawl in the Pitch and the Failed Pursuit

Source Dante Alighieri, Inferno, Canto XXII, lines 13-151 and Canto XXIII, lines 13-58Culture Tuscan ItalianPeriod 1308-1321

Summary

While escorted, Dante watches the demons torment barrators. A Navarrese barrator is fished out and interrogated; he tricks Alichino and Calcabrina by promising to call other grafters, then dives back into pitch. Alichino pursues but fails to catch him; Calcabrina, in mutual recrimination, grapples Alichino and both tumble into the boiling pitch, requiring the others to disentangle them. Seizing the chaos, Virgil grabs Dante and slides down the steep embankment into Bolgia Six, fleeing the escort. The demons attempt pursuit but are bound by providential law: they cannot leave the fifth pit. Dante hears them chasing like hounds but they cannot follow beyond the dike. Virgil later confirms Malacoda's lie when they find no intact bridge.

Participants

Malacoda (absent but legally responsible; his escort fails)

Barbariccia

Alichino

Calcabrina

Navarrese barrator (Ciampolo, per commentators)

Virgil

Dante

Consequences

Demonstrates the limits of Malacoda's power and the anarchy of his troops. The poets' narrow escape transitions the narrative from Bolgia Five to Bolgia Six (Hypocrites). Malacoda is not seen again; his final fate is to remain governing Bolgia Five eternally. The episode proves that demonic promises in fraud's realm are inherently untrustworthy.

Variants

Whether Malacoda himself joins the chase is explicitly denied by the text (XXIII.54-57: the demons cannot leave), but some popular summaries and game adaptations incorrectly show Malacoda leading the chase. Early commentators debate whether the Navarrese's trick reflects Malacoda's own fraudulent nature infecting his prisoners.

The Providential Boundary - Ministers of the Fifth Pit

Source Dante Alighieri, Inferno, Canto XXI, lines 127-135Culture Tuscan ItalianPeriod 1308-1321

Summary

Immediately after dispatching the escort, Malacoda (or the narrator clarifying his status) explains the theological rule governing the Malebranche: the Providence that gave them the fifth pit to govern as ministers of its will takes from their souls the power to leave it. This gloss explains why Malacoda can offer an escort only along the ridge within his jurisdiction and why the poets can escape by leaving the bolgia.

Participants

Malacoda

Malebranche collective

Divine Providence (as actor)

Consequences

Establishes the paradox of demonic authority as both empowered and imprisoned. Provides narrative justification for the poets' survival and for Malacoda's disappearance after Canto XXI. Frequently cited in theological readings of Dante's demonology as example of evil being instrumentalized by divine justice.

Variants

Attribution of these lines varies: some editors attribute them to Malacoda speaking, others to Dante the narrator. Hollander attributes to Malacoda; Singleton to narrator. Meaning unchanged.

Relationships
Malacoda→Barbaricciaally

Malacoda's sergeant and de facto field commander. Barbariccia ('Curly Beard') is named first among the ten dispatched and is called 'decurio' (leader of ten). Malacoda delegates escort leadership to him, indicating trust and hierarchical structure. Barbariccia is the one who physically restrains his comrades at Malacoda's order.

Malacoda→Alichino and Calcabrinaally

Two of the ten subordinates whose brawl and fall into pitch precipitates the collapse of Malacoda's plan. Alichino (from harlequin) and Calcabrina (frost-trampler) embody the impulsive, quarrelsome nature of the Malebranche that Malacoda must manage. Their failure reflects on Malacoda's imperfect control.

Malacoda→The Malebranche (collective: Cagnazzo, Libicocco, Draghignazzo, Ciriatto, Graffiacane, Farfarello, Rubicante, Scarmiglione)ally

The twelve demons of Bolgia Five, of whom Malacoda is chief. The name Malebranche means 'Evil Claws.' They are his ministers and enforcers, armed with hooks to torment barrators. Malacoda commands all twelve but dispatches ten, keeping two (including Scarmiglione, mentioned separately) in reserve. Their grotesque names and behavior parody civic militias.

Malacoda→Virgil (Publius Vergilius Maro)hostile

Malacoda's interlocutor and temporary truce partner. Virgil invokes divine authority to neutralize Malacoda's threat, forcing Malacoda to grant passage. Malacoda responds with feigned helpfulness and a lie about bridges, attempting to mislead Virgil. Virgil is initially trusting but later recognizes the deceit (XXIII.140-141: 'he who...'), showing his superior discernment.

Malacoda→Dante the Pilgrimhostile

The living human intruder whom Malacoda first threatens, then offers to guide, then attempts to trap via misdirection. Dante is terrified throughout, hiding behind Virgil and later clinging to him during flight. Malacoda's lie directly endangers Dante, forcing a dangerous slide down the embankment.

Malacoda→The Barrators (Barattieri)victim

The damned souls Malacoda eternally tortures - corrupt public officials who made money from office. They are submerged in boiling pitch; Malacoda's demons hook and rend them when they surface for air, mirroring how they grasped for illicit profit. Malacoda is their jailer and tormentor, not their ally.

Malacoda→Divine Providence / Godcreator

The ultimate authority that assigns Malacoda his post and limits his power. The text explicitly states Providence gave them the fifth pit to govern and took away power to leave. Malacoda is thus a minister (ministro) of divine justice, an unwilling instrument of order, not an autonomous rebel like Satan.

Malacoda→Satan (Lucifer / Dis)ally

Ultimate sovereign of Hell, though Malacoda never interacts with him directly in text. As leader of a bolgia guard, Malacoda is part of Satan's hierarchical infernal bureaucracy, but his immediate governance is delegated via Providence. Unlike Satan who is frozen in Judecca, Malacoda is active in Malebolge.

Torture of Barrators in Boiling Pitch

Source Inferno XXI.7-75, XXII.13-90; Singleton commentary on contrapasso

Description

Malacoda presides over a punishment explicitly designed as contrapasso for corruption: souls who made 'sticky' deals are made sticky, submerged in boiling pitch (pece) that burns and conceals. When they surface to ease pain, Malacoda's demons hook them with raffi and uncini, tearing flesh like cooks turning meat. Dante compares them to dolphins arching and to frogs in a ditch. The torture is eternal, without relief, and Malacoda is its chief administrator.

Scholarly Context

Scholars (Freccero, Barolini) read this as Dante's political rage against Florentine and Lucchese corruption; the pitch also evokes the caulking of ships and the biblical pitch of Noah, perverting a protective substance into torment. No sanitization - Dante lingers on screams and tearing.

Deception as Fraudulent Violence

Source Inferno XXI.106-126, XXIII.139-144; Aquinas, Summa Theologica on fraud; Benvenuto da Imola

Description

Malacoda's lie about the bridges is not mere mischief but an act of fraud, the very sin punished in the Eighth Circle. By offering false guidance to pilgrims on a divinely sanctioned journey, he attempts to waste their time, expose them to his unruly troops, and potentially trap them in a bolgia with no exit. This reflects medieval demonology where devils cannot speak truth and where fraud is considered worse than force.

Scholarly Context

Modern critics (Hollander, Durling) debate intentionality, but consensus views the lie as exemplary of Hell's nature: even helpful-seeming devils deceive. Content advisory: portrays manipulation and abuse of trust by authority figure.

Grotesque Scatology and Degradation

Source Inferno XXI.136-139; Bakhtin on carnivalesque; Auerbach on Dante's realism

Description

The episode culminates in Malacoda's signal being answered by a demon making a trumpet of his anus, producing a loud fart. Dante explicitly likens it to a trombone. This is not incidental humor but theological degradation: the demonic parody of angelic trumpets and military signals through the lowest bodily function, showing Hell as inversion of Heaven. Medieval audiences would recognize this as both comic and profoundly debasing.

Scholarly Context

Scholars note Dante's deliberate use of low style (comico-realistico) in Malebolge to match the baseness of fraud. Modern bowdlerized editions sometimes euphemize or footnote this, sanitizing Dante's intentional obscenity. Preserved here without euphemism, with advisory for scatological content.

Contradictions & Disputes

Intentional Lie vs. Honest Mistake about Bridges

Position A

Malacoda deliberately lies to trap the pilgrims - consistent with demonic nature and fraud's essence.

Position A — evidence

Virgil explicitly says 'he who... deceives' (XXIII.140-144: '...malizioso...'); Benvenuto da Imola and most modern critics (Singleton, Hollander, Durling) note devils cannot tell truth, and the lie mirrors barratry's deceit; Malacoda's courteous tone is classic fraud.

Position B

Malacoda is mistaken or speaking in good faith; devils can be ignorant of Hell's post-earthquake topography, or he genuinely believes a crag exists.

Position B — evidence

Text never states Malacoda knows all bridges are broken; he says 'the sixth arch lies broken' (singular), which is true, and 'nearby's another crag' - could be error not malice; some 19th-century commentators (Scartazzini) argue demons, as ministers of justice, would not needlessly lie when Providence already limits them.

Scholarly Consensus

Majority modern consensus favors intentional deceit (Position A), but acknowledges Dante leaves ambiguity to force reader's hermeneutic suspicion - the point is pilgrims must not trust devils regardless of intent.

Who Speaks the Providence Limitation (XXI.127-129)

Position A

Malacoda himself explains the providential limit on his power.

Position A — evidence

Some manuscripts and early prints lack quotation marks (which are editorial); Hollander's edition attributes lines to Malacoda as continuation of his speech, fitting his bureaucratic self-justification.

Position B

Dante the narrator interjects to explain the rule to the reader.

Position B — evidence

Petrocchi and Singleton attribute lines to narrator, arguing Malacoda would not theologically explicate Providence; the shift to third-person 'takes from their souls' suggests authorial gloss.

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus; editorial choice. Meaning unaffected, but attribution affects characterization of Malacoda as self-aware vs. merely described.

Etymology and Meaning of 'Malacoda'

Position A

'Evil tail' - straightforward pejorative + physical feature.

Position A — evidence

Mala + coda is transparent Italian; all early commentators gloss as 'mala cauda' (evil tail); parallels other Malebranche names that combine mal- + body part/behavior (Malacoda, Malebranche).

Position B

'Bad tail' as heraldic or moral term meaning 'cowardly end' or 'treacherous follower' (coda as rear guard).

Position B — evidence

Some philologists (e.g., Barbi) note coda in medieval Italian could mean 'retinue' or 'consequence', suggesting 'evil retinue' or 'evil outcome', but this is minority.

Scholarly Consensus

Position A is overwhelmingly accepted; Position B noted as speculative.

Does Malacoda Pursue Dante After the Brawl?

Position A

Malacoda remains in Bolgia Five and does not pursue; only the ten escorts chase.

Position A — evidence

Text states demons cannot leave the fifth pit (XXI.127-129, XXIII.54-57); Malacoda dispatched ten and stayed behind; Canto XXIII describes pursuers as 'those ten' without naming Malacoda; no line shows Malacoda sliding down bank.

Position B

Malacoda leads the pursuit along with his troops.

Position B — evidence

Popular summaries and some illustrations show Malacoda at head of chase for dramatic effect; confusion arises because 'Malebranche' pursuit is sometimes loosely attributed to their leader.

Scholarly Consensus

Position A is textually correct; Position B is popular error.

Primary Evidence

Dante Alighieri, Inferno, Canto XXI, lines 29-139 and Canto XXII, lines 13-151

Type textDate 1308-1321 (composition); earliest manuscripts 1330sLocator Canto XXI 76-139 (introduction, negotiation, lie of the bridges, dispatch of escort); Canto XXII 13-15, 94-151 (reference to pursuit); critical edition Petrocchi lines 106-126 for chronological passageLanguage Tuscan Italian (Florentine volgare)

Translation

Hollander & Hollander: 'You cannot go further along this ridge... Yesterday, five hours later than this hour, it was one thousand two hundred sixty-six years since the road was broken here.'; Singleton: 'Yesterday, five hours beyond this hour, a thousand two hundred and sixty-six years were complete since the way was broken here.' Original: 'Ieri, cinque ore più oltre che quest'otta, mille dugent sessantasei anni compié che qui la via fu rotta.'

Manuscripts of the Commedia preserving Inferno XXI-XXII - e.g., Codex Trivulziano 1080 (1337, copied by Francesco di ser Nardo), Codex Laurentianus Pl. 90 sup. 125, Codex Egerton 2628 (British Library)

Type manuscriptDate 1337-1350s for earliest witnessesLocator Folio containing Inf. XXI.76 where name appears as 'Malacoda'; variant spellings 'Malacoda' stable across tradition, no significant variant 'Malachoda'Language Tuscan Italian

Translation

No translation needed; diplomatic transcriptions show consistent spelling. Petrocchi's stemma uses Trivulziano as base.

Early Dante commentaries (Ottimo Commento, Jacopo Alighieri, Guido da Pisa, Lana, Benvenuto da Imola) on Inferno XXI

Type textDate 1322 (Jacopo Alighieri) to 1375-1380 (Benvenuto)Locator Jacopo Alighieri Chiose to Inf. XXI.76: 'Malacoda è uno demonio capo delli altri'; Guido da Pisa Expositiones, c.1335-1340, glosses Malacoda as 'capitaneus Malebranche'; Benvenuto da Imola Comentum, ad XXI.76-139, discusses his mendacityLanguage Latin and Italian

Translation

Benvenuto: 'This Malacoda is the leader of those devils, cunning and deceitful, as befits fraud.' Early commentators already debate whether his lie about bridges is intentional deceit or error.

Dante Alighieri, Inferno, Canto XXI, lines 136-139 - scatological signal passage

Type textDate 1308-1321Locator Inf. XXI.136-139: 'ed elli avea del cul fatto trombetta' (and he had made a trumpet of his ass)Language Tuscan Italian

Translation

Mandelbaum: 'and he had made a trumpet of his ass'; Hollander: 'and he had made a trumpet of his arse'; Durling: 'he made a trumpet of his asshole'. The preceding line: Malacoda gives 'un cenno' (a signal) and the demon responds with 'un peto' likened to tromba. Establishes Malacoda's command via obscene military parody.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

No detailed physical description in primary text - all visual attributes are later artistic invention; cannot verify tail morphology. No manuscript autograph survives - earliest witnesses are 1337+, so authorial spelling nuances lost. Limited scholarly monograph solely on Malacoda - most analysis treats him as part of Malebranche collective, so individual psychology is reconstructed from brief speech. No archaeological or liturgical source - purely literary. Translation nuances of 'cenno' and 'trombetta' debated but not resolvable without authorial gloss. No evidence of oral folklore - gaps flagged as unknown rather than unattested.

Research Scope

Exhaustive dossier on Malacoda as literary invention in Dante Alighieri's Inferno (1308-1321), focusing on Cantos XXI-XXIII. Covers philology of the name, manuscript and commentary tradition, narrative function, relationship to the Malebranche, and modern reception. Does not treat Malacoda as folklore or mythological figure; distinguishes attested text from critical reconstruction. Research cutoff 2026-08-09. No archival manuscript autopsy; relies on critical editions (Petrocchi, Sanguineti, Singleton, Durling-Martinez, Hollander) and published commentaries.
