Who It Is

O Del (literally 'The God' in Romani) is the supreme creator god of the Romani people, a distant but benevolent sky father who created the world and established the moral laws that govern it. Unlike many pagan deities, O Del is not directly anthropomorphized in Romani oral tradition — he is a somewhat abstract, monotheistic supreme being, reflecting the Romani's centuries-long exposure to Christianity and Islam. O Del created Beng (the devil), the stars, the moon, and the spirits, and set the order of the world. He is associated with justice and purity: he sees all violations of Romani customary law (Romanipen) and metes out punishment through fate or natural disaster. O Del is rarely named directly in everyday speech; instead, euphemisms like O Baro Devel ('The Great God') are used out of reverence. This reverent distancing mirrors the Jewish tradition of avoiding the Tetragrammaton. Prayers to O Del typically involve petitions for protection against Beng, illness, and the malice of the gadje. He stands at the apex of the Romani spiritual hierarchy, above all other spirits and saints.

Form

O Del has no fixed anthropomorphic depiction in Romani oral tradition. As the Devla article and Romani culture records indicate, Del functions as a supreme, somewhat abstract deity — 'revered' and 'distant' rather than portrayed with a body or attribute set. The OmniLore entity record characterizes him as 'a distant but benevolent sky father who created the world,' a formulation consistent with the reconstructed proto-Indo-European sky-father *Dyēus from which Del's etymology derives, but it is important to be precise: the documented source material attests the WORD and the WORSHIP, not a developed mythology of physical appearance. No canonical Romani iconography of Del exists in the English-language record; no face, animal aspect, or dwelling is reliably described. Where visual imagery is used today it is overwhelmingly the Abrahamic/Christian iconography of the host religion.

Powers

Supreme creation and cosmic governance As the supreme being, Del is credited with creating the world and establishing the moral order that governs it (the OmniLore record: he 'created the world and established the moral laws'). In the reconstructed devotional frame he set the order of the world, created the stars, the moon, and the spirits, and created Beng (the Devil) as his cosmological counterpart. This is a creator-king power, distant rather than dramatically exhibited. Attested as the implied role of the supreme God in Romani belief; explicitly asserted in the OmniLore entity record; consistent with the *Dyēus-derived high-god etymology. Del operates at cosmic/abstract scale; the attested record gives no personal combat or intervention feats. Punishments are 'through fate or natural disaster' — mediated, not directly dramatized. Whether Del CREATED Beng or merely coexists with him as an eternal counterpart is not settled in English-language sources; some Romani folk accounts treat the world as having been made in a collaboration/rivalry between Del and Beng (see stories).

Omniscient moral judgment Del 'sees the heart and the deed' — nothing is hidden from his eye. He is the enforcer of Romanipen ('Gypsiness'/Romani customary law) and Romano Zakono (the Romani Code), meting out consequences for violations. OmniLore entity record quote and the moral-governance framing of Romani religion; the belief that God sees and judges is consistent with the documented adoption of surrounding monotheistic moral theology. Judgment is conceived as operating via fate/social consequence rather than as a personalized legal procedure; no attested mythology of trials or adjudication scenes exists. None resolved in sources; the exact mechanism (divine fate vs natural/social consequence) is not textually pinned down.

Answerer of prayer / protector Del is invoked in prayer for protection against Beng, illness, and the malice of the gadje (non-Roma outsiders). Prayers to Del form the ritual spine of Romani protection-magic as documented in Romani folklore (which closely ties prayer with protective herbs and amulets), and the reverent euphemisms (O Baro Devel) accompany these invocations. Romani folklore motif list; OmniLore record ('Prayers to O Del typically involve petitions for protection against Beng, illness, and the malice of the gadje'). Prayer may be channeled through the host religion's intermediaries (Christ, the Virgin Mary via the Shaktist consort-frame), so Del is not necessarily addressed directly even when it is he who is ultimately worshipped. None in sources; but note the Shaktist 'consort required for worship' practice adds a mediation layer not present in the pure supreme-God frame.

Source of 'Divla' / divine force (reconstruction) In the modern polytheistic-reconstruction register, a Bar o Devel / Siv is named as specific 'god of power and music.' This attributes a domain (power; music) to the high-god figure that the abstract supreme-Del register does not carry. It is a reconstruction-presentation, not a classical myth. 'Romani culture' article, Faith and religion section (reconstructed pantheon list). This is a modern/partial reconstruction; it does not reflect a single, uniform, historically continuous formulation. Few believers per the Devlaism note. Conflicts with the more common abstract-monotheist Del framing (see contradictions register).

Accounts

Romani oral tradition presents Del as the distant supreme creator who made the world and set the moral laws that govern it. He created the stars, the moon, and the spirits, and established the order in which Beng (the Devil) stands as the opposing force of evil and corruption. Humanity lives under this order and the moral code (Romanipen/Romano Zakono) that Del oversees. There is no widely-attested, single fixed narrative of this creation in the English-language record; the account circulates as a theological premise rather than as a dramatized origin tale.

The defining 'story' of Del is not a myth cycle but a living ritual idiom: Roma speak of and pray to 'Devla / Del' directly, especially in moments of hardship, illness, danger, or moral reflection. The word functions as both noun ('God') and invocation ('O God!'). Under the Shaktist-influenced practice documented among a minority, prayer to God is channeled through a female consort (the Virgin Mary or Saint Anne). Del is invoked for protection against Beng and the malice of outsiders, and the name is treated with reverent indirection.

After the Yugoslav Wars, some Roma — including the Arlije and Gurbeti groups — deliberately constructed an ethnic religion rooted in older Romani folklore and oriented around Devel. Its holy days are called Djisatedimi, and it retains rites carried over from Christianity and Islam: infant baptism (bona) and religious male circumcision (sunet bijav). 'Devlaism' represents a conscious attempt to rebuild a distinct Romani religious identity anchored in the indigenous Devel-concept rather than in the host religion alone. The record notes it has relatively few believers.

Content Notes

Romani folklore scholarship has been contaminated by fabricated material. The Romani folklore article notes that the writings of Heinrich von Wlislocki, a self-taught 'gypseologist' widely treated as authentic Romani stories, actually 'have no connection to authentic Romani traditions,' causing widespread misinterpretation of the Roma. Any claim about 'the Romani God' sourced to such 19th-century gypsofantasia must be treated with suspicion. This directly bears on O Del: the romanticized, occult-flavored portrait of Romani religion in much western literature is largely invention, and the honest record for Del is (a) the genuine deva-derived word/term and (b) the real worship frame — not the exoticized mythology of the fortune-teller stereotype.

Distinguishes genuine Romani ethnographic material from the orientalist corpus; a core reason the archive flags Romani entities (see anna-kumanova.json, the fabricated-disproportion exemplar).

Romani folklore article (Wlislocki fabrication note)

The Roma have historically been targets of proselytization, enslavement (e.g., in the Danubian Principalities), and genocide (the Porajmos). Religious coercion — forced conversion to Christianity or Islam as the price of settlement/safety, and the pressure to 'adopt the religion of the host country for any ceremony' — has shaped how Del is practiced. The belief system survived 'regardless of such adoption processes' (Romani people article), but lived religious practice bears the imprint of centuries of external religious domination.

Religious survival under coercion is a documented resilience theme; Del's monotheistic/abstract framing is partly a product of this history.

Romani people article — Religion section

Because Del/Devel superficially resembles 'devil,' non-Romani interpreters have repeatedly confused the Romani God with the Devil. This is a documented linguistic/ethnographic misidentification that the Devla article explicitly corrects. It is a species of the orientalist flattening of Romani culture and has led to the Roma being libeled as 'devil-worshippers' in some folk prejudice.

A clarifying correction; relevant to how the archive presents the Del/Beng pairing without allowing the homophone confusion.

Devla article (explicit Del-vs-Beng distinction)

The 21st-century reclaiming of Saint Sarah as 'Kali / a Romani Goddess' and the Devlaism revival are efforts to rebuild indigenous religious identity, but they sit in tension with the Christian and Muslim communities to which most Roma belong, and can draw criticism as invented rather than continuous tradition. The 'few believers' status of Devlaism reflects how a reconstructed Del-centered religion competes with long-established host-religion practice.

An internal Romani debate about authenticity and continuity; flagged here without asserting a verdict.

What We Don't Know

Primary gaps: (1) No primary Romani-language text was accessed in this tick — all evidence is drawn from secondary English-language Wikipedia articles. The etymology (deva/devus/*Dyēus) and the Del/Beng distinction are well-attested, but any Romani-language original prayers, song-texts, or oral-formulaic invocations to Del would materially deepen the dossier. (2) The exact creation-relationship between Del and Beng is unresolved in English sources; a Romani-studies specialist source (e.g., a dedicated monograph on Romani religion) is needed. (3) The reconstructed pantheon register (Bar o Devel/Siv and co-deities) is presented without a full genealogical or synchronic account; French/German-language Romani-studies literature would likely resolve it. (4) Modern pop-culture appearances are essentially nil and were honestly NOT fabricated — Del functions as a worship-term and oath, not a named character in film/game/anime; any claim otherwise would be invention. (5) The Xoraxane (Muslim) vs Dasikane (Christian) Romani division affects how Del is practiced, but its theological specifics were beyond this tick's scope.

A way to hold it in your mind

If ever a supreme god dwelt quietly behind an everyday word, it is O Del — for in the Romani language the faithful do not reach for scripture or a holy book when they need God; they simply say Devla. He is 'the Great God,' O Baro Devel, whose reverent name is the same word that once thundered in Sanskrit as deva and that shares a root with the ancient sky-father *Dyēus of the whole Indo-European world. Del is not a god of carved idols or dramatic myth — there are no famous stories of his battles, no temples built for him. Instead he is the silent order behind everything: the one who sees the heart and the deed, the invisible enforcer of Romanipen, the supreme being whose only real counterpart is his dark opposite, Beng the Devil. In a faith carried across centuries and borders without a fixed home, Del is the one constant — a sky-father so vast and so present that his people rarely dare to name him plainly, preferring to whisper, 'the Great God,' and know that he is listening. That a whole people's supreme deity lives chiefly in a word and an invocation — not in temples or epics — is precisely why Del endures: you cannot exile a god you are carrying in your mouth.
