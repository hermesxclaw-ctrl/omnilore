Form

Never described in surviving texts. As an Oceanid, standard iconographic convention would depict her as a beautiful young woman, often with water-associated attributes such as a jug, a crown of reeds, or a watery veil. Some later vase paintings of nymphs suggest flowing garments and a serene, ethereal presence. In the absence of direct evidence, her appearance is reconstructed from the generic nymph type.

Powers

Nymphic immortality As a divine nymph, Amphiro is immortal and ageless, though not invulnerable. Nymphs in Greek tradition can suffer, be transformed, or even fade from memory, but they do not die of natural causes. Pan-Hellenic tradition No individual feats or powers are attested. Her divinity is passive — she embodies her domain rather than actively intervening in mortal affairs. None recorded.

Water domain As an Oceanid, she presides over fresh water sources. The Oceanids collectively were invoked in rituals for clean water, fertility, and safe travel across seas. Pan-Hellenic tradition Her individual domain is not specified beyond her name's implication of flowing water. Unlike Peitho or Metis, she has no distinct divine function beyond her aquatic association. None recorded.

Behavior & Habitat

No individual behavior is recorded. As a catalog figure, her existence is primarily genealogical. The Oceanids collectively are associated with springs, fountains, and the fresh waters of the earth, embodying the life-giving and ever-moving nature of streams.

Accounts

Hesiod names the eldest Oceanids — Perseis, Clymene, Styx, and others — as the daughters of Oceanus and Tethys. Amphiro appears in this catalog as one of the senior nymphs, listed among a group that includes Dione, Nemea, and others. The catalog serves a genealogical and cosmological purpose: it maps the divine family tree and establishes the water nymphs as the connective tissue of the mythological cosmos.

A fragmentary poem that also lists Amphiro among the Oceanids. This parallel catalog tradition suggests that her name was standard enough to be included in multiple early genealogical works.

Scholarly Debates
Spelling of the name
Amphiro (Ἀμφιρῴ) — the standard Hesiodic form ⇌ Amphirho (Ἀμφιρῥώ) — a variant transliteration emphasizing the rho
Evidence for A: Most critical editions of the Theogony print Ἀμφιρῴ
Evidence for B: Some manuscripts and lexica show Ἀμφιρῥώ
Inclusion in later catalogs
Amphiro is a standard Oceanid name in the Hesiodic tradition ⇌ Some later catalogs (e.g., Hyginus) omit her in favor of other names
Evidence for A: Hesiod, Theogony 360; Hesiodic fragment 7
Evidence for B: Hyginus, Fabulae, Preface
What We Don't Know

No dedicated scholarly monograph exists on Amphiro. All information is derived from the catalog tradition of Greek mythology. Future research could examine manuscript variants of the Theogony for additional attestations of her name. Archaeological evidence for her worship is non-existent — she was a literary figure, not a cult figure. Her presence in modern media is minimal and unverified.
