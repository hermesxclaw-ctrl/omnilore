Who It Is

Before the sky existed, before the earth was named, there was only a single, silent, infinite ocean of fresh water. It had no shores, no surface, no bottom. It simply was. This was Abzu — the cosmic deep, the source of all rivers and wells, the quiet, patient foundation upon which the entire universe would be built. But this primordial peace was not to last. For when the first gods were born from its waters, their noise and dancing would shatter the silence, and the Abzu, the very source of all life, would become the first casualty of creation. This is the story of the god who was the world, and who was murdered to make a new one.

In the earliest Sumerian sources, Abzu is not an active agent; he is a place and a state of being. He is the source of all fresh water — rivers, springs, wells — and thus the ultimate source of agricultural and urban life. He is passive and inert. In the Babylonian Enūma Eliš, he is given a personality: he is disturbed by the chaotic din of the younger gods and is persuaded by his vizier Mummu to destroy them. This is his only major narrative action, and it is a destructive one, driven by irritation and a desire for quiet.

The Sumerian term is often written as 'engur' or 'engurra,' which also refers to the subterranean waters. In Akkadian, the name becomes 'Apsû' (𒀊𒍪𒍪). Powers
Primordial Existence

Abzu is one of the two primordial entities (with Tiamat) that existed before the creation of the universe. He is the source of all fresh water, and thus a fundamental constituent of reality.

Limit: He is passive and easily disturbed; he does not actively shape the world.

Generative Power

He is the 'begetter' of the early generations of gods. In the Enūma Eliš, he and Tiamat 'mingled their waters' to produce the first divine pair (Lahmu and Lahamu).

Limit: His generative power is spent after the first generation; he does not create again.

Control over Fresh Water

As the personification of the underground freshwater ocean, he is the ultimate source of all earthly fresh water. His domain feeds the Tigris and Euphrates.

Limit: In practice, this power is delegated to his son Enki, who is the active administrator of the Abzu.

Plan to Destroy the Younger Gods

In the Enūma Eliš, Abzu plots to destroy the younger gods who are disturbing his rest. This is his only active 'power play,' and it fails.

Limit: He is outwitted and captured by the god Ea (Enki), who casts a spell on him and puts him to sleep. Abzu is then killed, and Ea builds his home upon his corpse.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Abzu is not typically depicted anthropomorphically. He IS the cosmic body of water itself — the vast, dark, silent, underground ocean of fresh water that lies beneath the earth's crust. When personified, he is imagined as a primordial, horned being (often with a water-stream or fish-like attributes), but the primary 'appearance' is of a boundless, formless, life-giving yet terrifyingly passive entity.

Symbols — Water, the deep, the abyss, a horned crown (when personified), a flowing stream, the temple of Eridu. The 'Abzu' symbol in cuneiform is a combination of the signs for 'water' and 'to know' (or 'deep').

Domains — The cosmic underground ocean of fresh water. The subterranean realm. The source of all springs, rivers, and wells. The domain of the god Enki/Ea, who resides within it. The temple of E-abzu at Eridu is its earthly manifestation.

Accounts I
The Birth of the Gods (Enūma Eliš)Enūma Eliš, Tablet I

c. 12th century BCE (text), older oral tradition

In the beginning, there was only Apsû (Abzu), the fresh water, and Tiamat, the salt water. Their waters mingled, and from this union the first gods were born: Lahmu and Lahamu, then Anshar and Kishar, and eventually the great gods Anu and Ea. The younger gods were loud and boisterous, their dancing and noise disturbing Apsû's peace. Unable to sleep, Apsû consulted his vizier Mummu, who advised him to destroy them. Tiamat was horrified and refused to harm her children, but Apsû resolved to carry out the plan. The younger gods learned of this and were terrified, but the wise Ea devised a counter-spell. Ea recited a powerful incantation that cast Apsû into a deep sleep, then slew him. Ea then built his magnificent home, the E-abzu ('House of the Deep'), upon the corpse of Apsû, and there he dwelt with his wife Damkina. It is here that their son, the great god Marduk, is born. The murder of Apsû is the inciting incident of the entire epic. Accounts II
The Abzu as the Home of EnkiNumerous Sumerian texts: 'Enki and the World Order', 'Enki and Ninmah', 'The Eridu Genesis', 'Inanna and Enki'

c. 3rd millennium BCE

In the Sumerian tradition, the Abzu is not a person but a place — the subterranean freshwater ocean. This realm is the domain of the god Enki (later Ea). Enki resides in his magnificent palace at the heart of the Abzu, surrounded by his attendants, the Seven Sages (Abgal), and creatures like the fierce Urmah (a lion-headed monster). From here, Enki controls the me — the divine decrees that govern civilization — and the waters that bring life to the land. The Abzu is the source of the Tigris and Euphrates, and its waters are channeled to the surface to irrigate the fields and fill the sacred temple pools. This establishes the Abzu as a central, sacred location in Sumerian cosmology, the source of all life-giving water and the seat of wisdom and civilization.

The Temple of E-abzu at EriduGudea Cylinders, other royal inscriptions

c. 2100 BCE

The temple of the god Enki at Eridu was known as the E-abzu, the 'House of the Abzu.' It was believed to be built directly upon the primeval freshwater ocean. The temple was a physical portal to the cosmic deep, and its sacred pool (the abzu-pool) was a miniature representation of the cosmic ocean. Kings like Gudea of Lagash describe rebuilding and purifying this temple, emphasizing its role as the center of the world and the source of divine wisdom. The E-abzu became the most important religious center in southern Mesopotamia, and its name became synonymous with the concept of the cosmic deep itself.

Abzu is the same as Tiamat. — Abzu is the male principle of fresh water (underground), while Tiamat is the female principle of salt water (the ocean). They are consorts, not the same entity.

Abzu is an evil god. — Abzu is not inherently 'evil.' In Sumerian texts, he is a passive, generative source of life. In the Babylonian epic, he is a disturbed and irritable primordial force, but his actions are not 'evil' in a moral sense; they are a reaction to being disturbed. He is a force of cosmic inertia, not malice.

Abzu is a sea monster or a dragon. — Abzu is not a dragon or a monster. He is a cosmic body of water. When personified, he is a deity, not a beast. The 'monster' role in the Enūma Eliš is taken by Tiamat and her brood (like Kingu), not Abzu.
