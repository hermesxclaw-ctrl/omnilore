Who It Is

Heat has no substance and no home. You cannot bottle it, weigh it, or own it — you can only watch it move. For two centuries natural philosophers tried to trap it as an invisible fluid called caloric, only to discover it is not a thing at all but a happening: energy in transit, born when temperature differences demand equilibrium and vanishing the instant that transit stops.

Behavior is strictly processual: heat exists only during transfer driven by temperature difference or non-conservative dissipative processes. It flows spontaneously from hotter to colder (Clausius statement of Second Law) unless work is expended. It is not conserved: can be generated by friction/dissipation but not destroyed except by conversion to other energy forms. It is path-dependent, not a state function; integral of δQ around a cycle ≠ 0. Magnitude depends on process path, not just endpoints.

Powers
Thermal Conduction

Transfer of energy through direct contact without bulk matter movement, via molecular collisions and lattice vibrations (phonons) and electron diffusion. Fourier's law q'' = -k∇T quantifies rate.

Limit: Requires material medium and temperature gradient; rate limited by thermal conductivity k; no net matter transfer; negligible in vacuum

Thermal Radiation (Electromagnetic)

Transfer via electromagnetic waves, especially infrared, without medium. Stefan-Boltzmann law and Planck's law describe emission. Only mode that operates through vacuum.

Limit: Requires emissivity/absorptivity; net flow depends on T^4 difference; blocked by opaque shields; not dependent on contact

Frictional / Dissipative Generation

Mechanical work degraded into internal energy appears as heat transferred to bodies in contact. Joule showed work can be quantitatively converted to heat at rate 4.186 J/cal. Heat is not conserved here but generated.

Limit: Irreversible; cannot be fully recovered as work; amount depends on dissipative path; second law limits conversion efficiency

Phase-Change and Latent Transfer

Heat absorbed or released during isothermal phase changes (melting, vaporization) without temperature change, altering internal structure. Measured by ice calorimetry (Lavoisier-Laplace).

Limit: Temperature remains constant during pure phase change at fixed pressure; quantity depends on mass and latent enthalpy; not sensible as temperature rise

Calorimetric Measurement (Operational Ability)

Quantity of heat inferred by effect on reference body: temperature change (Q = mcΔT) or mass of ice melted (Lavoisier-Laplace ice calorimeter). SI unit joule defined mechanically.

Limit: Indirect; requires known specific heat or latent heat; assumes no work or matter transfer during measurement; precision limited by heat leaks

Its effects are observable: expansion of thermometric liquids, phase changes (melting, boiling), incandescence, infrared radiation detectable by thermopile or skin as warmth. Accounts I
Joseph Black Discovers Specific and Latent HeatJoseph Black lectures and experiments, reported by John Robison in Lectures on the Elements of Chemistry (1803); contemporary letters to William Cullen

1756-1762

Black, professor at Glasgow and Edinburgh, mixed equal masses of different substances (water, mercury, glass) at different temperatures and found temperature changes were not equal. He coined 'capacity for heat' (later specific heat). He then showed ice at 32°F absorbs large quantity of heat without warming while melting, and boiling water absorbs heat without temperature rise. He called this 'latent heat'. He demonstrated with ice calorimetry that heat gained by cold body equals heat lost by hot body when work is excluded. First quantitative distinction between heat and temperature; enabled calorimetry; provided empirical foundation for Lavoisier and later thermodynamics; established that thermometers measure temperature, not heat quantity

Lavoisier and Laplace Build the Ice Calorimeter and Codify CaloricAntoine Lavoisier and Pierre-Simon Laplace - Mémoire sur la chaleur (1783)

1780-1783

Lavoisier, rejecting phlogiston, proposed caloric as an imponderable, conserved fluid that flows from hot to cold and combines with matter to cause expansion and phase change. With Laplace he built the ice calorimeter: heat released by a guinea pig or chemical reaction melts ice, mass of melt measures heat. They measured specific heats and heats of combustion, showing respiration is slow combustion. Caloric provided elegant quantitative framework despite being ultimately wrong. Caloric theory became dominant quantitative theory for 40 years; introduced precise calorimetry; linked heat to chemistry and physiology; experiments remain valid data even after theory replaced

Count Rumford's Cannon Boring and the Indefinite Generation of HeatBenjamin Thompson, Count Rumford - Philosophical Transactions 1798

1798

While supervising cannon boring at the Munich arsenal, Rumford noticed enormous heat produced by blunt borer. He immersed a cannon barrel in water and showed friction could boil water indefinitely as long as mechanical work continued, without exhausting any caloric reservoir. He argued heat cannot be a finite material substance but must be motion. Though not immediately accepted, experiment became classic anti-caloric evidence. Serious empirical challenge to caloric conservation; inspired Humphry Davy (1799) to melt ice by friction in vacuum; paved way for mechanical theory

Accounts II
Sadi Carnot's Reflections and the Ideal Heat EngineSadi Carnot - Réflexions sur la puissance motrice du feu (1824)

1824

Young engineer Carnot asked what limits work obtainable from heat. Using caloric theory (heat conserved, falling from hot to cold like water over a wheel), he described a reversible cycle of isothermal and adiabatic steps (now Carnot cycle) and proved maximum efficiency depends only on temperatures of hot and cold reservoirs, not working substance. Though premise (caloric conservation) was false, cycle and efficiency theorem survive with reinterpretation where heat is not conserved. Founded thermodynamics as science of heat-work conversion; Carnot efficiency η = 1 - Tc/Th remains central; later corrected by Clausius and Kelvin who retained cycle but abandoned caloric conservation

Joule Measures the Mechanical Equivalent of HeatJames Prescott Joule - Papers in Philosophical Magazine and Philosophical Transactions 1843-1850

1843-1850

Joule performed series of precise experiments: electrical heating, compression of gases, and most famously paddle-wheel agitation of water by falling weights. He showed a fixed amount of mechanical work always produces same quantity of heat, measuring ~772 foot-pounds per BTU (modern 4.186 J/cal). He concluded heat is not substance but 'a state of vibration' and that work and heat are interconvertible forms of energy. Provided quantitative mechanical equivalent; convinced Thomson and helped Clausius formulate First Law (energy conservation); ended caloric theory; established joule as unit

Clausius Defines Heat as Process and States the First and Second LawsRudolf Clausius - Annalen der Physik 1850, and Die mechanische Wärmetheorie (1864)

1850-1865

Clausius reconciled Carnot and Joule: energy is conserved (First Law: ΔU = Q - W), but heat is not conserved and not a state function. He defined heat strictly as energy transferred other than by work or matter transfer, introduced internal energy U and entropy S, and stated heat flows spontaneously hot→cold (Clausius statement). He introduced symbol Q and clarified sign convention (Q positive when absorbed). This definition remains canonical. Modern thermodynamic definition of heat established; distinction between heat (process) and internal energy/thermal energy (state) clarified; enabled quantitative thermodynamics and later statistical mechanics

Heat and temperature are the same thing; a hotter object always contains more heat. — Temperature is intensive (K) measuring average kinetic energy per degree of freedom; heat is extensive process energy (J) transferred due to temperature difference. A large lukewarm lake contains more internal thermal energy than a small hot nail, but no heat until transfer occurs. Two bodies at same temperature exchange no net heat.

Objects contain heat; heat is a substance that flows like a fluid. — Bodies contain internal energy, not heat. Heat exists only during transfer. Once transfer stops, energy is internal energy. The fluid metaphor is legacy of caloric theory, abandoned after Joule and Clausius.

Cold is a separate substance or 'cold energy' that flows into warm objects. — Cold is absence of thermal energy; net heat flows hot→cold spontaneously. 'Cold flow' is just negative heat flow in accounting.

Friction destroys energy or creates heat that is conserved afterward. — Friction converts ordered mechanical work into disordered internal energy; heat can be generated (not conserved) and then transferred. Total energy conserved (First Law), but heat itself not conserved.

The calorie and BTU are modern SI units for heat. — SI unit is joule (J); calorie (thermochemical cal = 4.184 J) and BTU (≈1055 J) are legacy non-SI units retained in nutrition and HVAC but deprecated in science.
