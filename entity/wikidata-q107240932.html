The Approach

In the rugged highlands of Arcadia, a prince named Coretho met his end not in battle, but at the thunderous hand of Zeus himself—his crime, a feast so profane it still echoes through myth.

Content advisory. Contains themes of cannibalism and divine punishment.
Identity

Coretho

Pronunciation /kɒˈrɛθoʊ/ (English approximation); in Ancient Greek: Κορεθων (Korethōn), genitive Κορεθοντος.

Known As

KorethosCorythus (possibly)

Etymology

The name Coretho (Κορέθοντα) is of uncertain etymology. It may be related to Greek words for 'helmet' (korys) or 'maiden' (korē), but no definitive derivation is attested.

Cultural Ownership

Greek mythology, specifically Arcadian myth; part of the Lycaonid dynasty.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars suggest Coretho may be identical to Corythus, an Italian king and father of Dardanus by Electra, but this is speculative and not generally accepted.

Description

Appearance

No detailed physical description of Coretho survives. As one of the fifty sons of Lycaon, he would have been a young Arcadian prince, but specific features are unknown.

Symbols & Iconography

No distinctive symbols or iconography are associated with Coretho. He appears only in literary lists of Lycaon's sons.

Behaviour & Personality

Coretho is described as one of the most nefarious and carefree of Lycaon's sons, participating in the impious act of serving human flesh to Zeus.

Domains & Habitats

Coretho was from Arcadia, a mountainous region in the Peloponnese. No specific domain or habitat is assigned to him.

Version Differences

The list of Lycaon's sons varies among sources; some lists include Coretho, others do not. The identification with Corythus is a modern hypothesis.

Powers & Abilities

None

Source Version All sources

Description

Coretho is not attributed any special powers or abilities in the myths. He is a mortal prince.

Limitations

Mortal and vulnerable to divine punishment.

Contradictions

None.

Stories & Myths

The Test of Zeus and the Punishment of Lycaon's Sons

Source Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.8.1Culture GreekPeriod Mythical (Archaic period)

Summary

Coretho and his brothers were the most impious of all men. To test them, Zeus visited them disguised as a peasant. They mixed the entrails of a child into the god's meal. In rage, Zeus overthrew the table and killed Coretho and his brothers with lightning bolts.

Participants

Coretho

His 49 brothers

Lycaon (father)

Zeus

Consequences

The death of Coretho and his brothers; the founding of the city of Trapezus (possibly) from the overturned table.

Variants

Other versions say Lycaon himself was turned into a wolf, while his sons were killed; some lists name different sons.

Dark History

Cannibalism

Source Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.8.1

Description

Coretho and his brothers served the entrails of a child to Zeus, an act of extreme impiety and cannibalism.

Scholarly Context

This act is a common motif in myths used to illustrate the depravity of certain figures; it also serves as an aetiological explanation for the founding of Trapezus (from 'trapeza', table).

Divine punishment

Source Pseudo-Apollodorus

Description

Zeus killed Coretho and his brothers with lightning bolts, a violent punishment for their crimes.

Scholarly Context

Lightning is a typical weapon of Zeus for punishing hubris and impiety.

Identification with Corythus

Position A

Similarity of names and the fact that Corythus is also a son of Lycaon in some traditions? (Not attested)

Lack of direct evidence; different geographical associations (Arcadia vs. Italy).

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars treat them as distinct; the identification is speculative.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

Primary sources are scarce; only Apollodorus explicitly names Coretho. No archaeological or epigraphic evidence. The identification with Corythus is unproven. Further research could explore later mythographic works (e.g., Hyginus) and scholia.
