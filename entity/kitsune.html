Who It Is

Somewhere in the mountains of Mino, in the ninth century, a farmer's wife hears the family dog growl — and the woman he has loved for years, the mother of his child, is suddenly a fox at the door, her human face crumbling like a paper lantern in rain. She runs; he keeps the child; and Japanese literature, which had never seen such a scene before, spends the next twelve hundred years trying to decide whether that moment is tragedy, comedy, miracle, or warning. The answer it keeps arriving at is: all of them at once. The kitsune is a fox that puts on a woman's face and a kami's authority and a demon's appetite, and the culture that made it never quite resolved which of those is the disguise. It guards the rice granary and eats the chickens. It marries you and loves you and leaves you the moment the dog barks. It walks through your walls as firelight, slips into your body as possession, and

Two temperaments organize the whole tradition. The zenko ('good fox') serves Inari, guards fields, rewards piety, and carries messages between the divine and human worlds; the nogitsune ('wild fox') is a trickster who punishes arrogant priests, greedy merchants, and boastful drunkards with humiliations of exquisite precision — phantom landscapes, leaf-money, false baths. Name & Tongue

Etymology — The word kitsune is of uncertain origin; no accepted derivation exists in historical linguistics. Folk etymologies are ancient and numerous. A popular one reads the name as kitsu-ne ('come and sleep'), from the phrase a fox is said to whisper to reluctant guests; another as ki-tsu-ne ('always come')

Pronunciation — kee-TSU-neh; Japanese [kitsɯne], two syllables in native prosody (ki-tsu-ne), often rendered 'kit-soo-nay' in English media.

Aliases — kitsune (狐, fox), kyūbi no kitsune (九尾の狐, nine-tailed fox), nogitsune (野狐, wild/field fox), zenko (善狐, good fox), myōbu (命婦, court-lady fox), tenko (天狐, celestial fox)

Powers
Shapeshifting (bakeru)

Transformation into human form — beautiful women, monks, warriors, specific individuals — and also into objects, other animals, giants, and monsters. The proverb kitsune shichi bake, tanuki ya bake ('the fox changes seven times, the tanuki eight') treats fox shapeshifting as a limited repertoire compared to the raccoon dog's. Transformation is imperfect: tails, fur, shadows, reflections, and scent betray the fox, and the disguise fails when the creature is startled, drunk, or careless (Yokai.com).

Limit: Social exposure collapses the disguise; dogs see through it; mirrors and water reveal it; some versions require props (a skull, leaves, the jewel) or conditions (night) — other versions make transformation effortless

Bewitchment and illusion (bakasu)

Kitsune deceive the senses wholesale: travelers walk in circles, see houses and baths that are not there, hold leaves that look like coins, hear phantom voices. Itō Ryōhei (2023) documents the 'disorienting deity' (madowashigami) type, in which a person lost on a mountain road attributes their confusion to a fox — the fox sometimes appears only in the interpretation, not the event. A folk remedy instructs the bewildered to 'sit down and think.'

Limit: Illusions break at dawn, under religious counter-ritual, or when the victim's nerve holds; the interpretive-device scholarship suggests much 'bewitchment' is attribution, not agency

Fox possession (kitsunetsuki)

The fox enters a human body and speaks, acts, and suffers through it; victims — disproportionately women in the historical record — were said to crave abura-age, eat ravenously, and behave erratically. Possession could be cured by exorcism (shugendō priests, miko), and was also cultivated: hereditary 'fox-owning' families (kitsune-mochi) in the Izumo region were believed to command familiars — the osaki, or the tiny kuda-gitsune said to live inside a bamboo tube — and to unleash them on enemies.

Limit: Exorcism, negotiation, and religious intervention reverse possession; the possessed could also become a benevolent guardian, as in an 11th-century tale of a 'hungry fox'

Fox-fire (kitsunebi)

Kitsune emit or command small flames and drifting lights — kitsunebi — seen along roads, in fields, and at cemeteries at night; the lights lure, mislead, or simply ornament the fox's passage, and appear as standard elements of pictorial depiction, often beside a white fox.

Limit: Usually harmless; naturalistic explanations (phosphorescence, marsh gas) are offered by skeptics and recorded by folklorists without endorsing them

The fox jewel (hoshi no tama / tama)

A small luminous sphere — 'star jewel' in Miyagi, 'priest jewel' on the Oki Islands — that the fox carries, guards obsessively, and in many versions must keep to transform or to work magic; some traditions identify it as the fox's soul or life-force. A human who seizes or hides the jewel can compel or disable the fox until it is returned.

Limit: The fox is powerless, or bound, without it

Longevity, wisdom, and the tails

Kitsune live for hundreds or thousands of years, growing a new tail with age — some folktales specify a tail per century — to a maximum of nine, at which point fur turns gold or white and the creature approaches omniscience and near-immortality. One, five, seven, and nine are the commonest tail counts in tales. The nine-tailed fox is, however, 'derived straight from Chinese classics' (the celestial fox of 1,000 years, tian hu), and the strict one-tail-per-century rule is a modern rationalization, not an ancient doctrine.

Limit: Age thresholds vary wildly by source; some traditions never mention tails at all

Blessing, cursing, and divine service

As Inari's messengers, kitsune bring prosperity, good harvests, and protection; they reward virtue and punish vice. The same power set, wielded by a nogitsune or an offended fox, delivers ruin: sickness, madness, humiliation, and death. Foxes of the Inari cult also guard granaries in a quite literal sense — they eat the rats that eat the rice, an ecology that Britannica credits as the origin of their sacred status.

Limit: Moral conditionality: the fox's blessing is tied to human conduct; shrine foxes remain wild animals and are not reliably tame

Divination and inherited magic

Old foxes possess knowledge of hidden things and the future; the onmyōji Abe no Seimei's legendary powers are attributed to his fox mother's blood, making fox heritage a channel for esoteric arts. Celestial foxes (tenko) are bodiless spirits of vast knowledge.

Limit: Tied to specific lineages and individuals; human society treats fox-derived knowledge with suspicion

Accounts I
The Fox-Wife of Mino (Nihon Ryōiki I:2)Nihon Ryōiki (Kyōkai, early 9th c.); English translations by Nakamura (1997, pp. 103–104) and Watson (2013, pp. 14–15)

c. 787–822

The earliest fox-wife story in Japanese literature. A man of Mino Province takes a woman as wife, not knowing she is a fox; she bears him a son. The household dog barks at her and will not stop; her true form is exposed, and she flees. The tale is told as a Buddhist miracle — the fox-wife and her hybrid child are framed by Kyōkai as an instance of the strange workings of karma in the world. Establishes the template of every later fox-wife story: domestic happiness, canine exposure, flight, and a liminal child. The dog as revealer recurs in Chinese fox tales (the Tang tale of Miss Ren) and throughout the Japanese tradition.

Kuzunoha and the Birth of Abe no SeimeiAbe no Seimei monogatari (1662); puppet plays Shinodazuma (1678) and Ashiya Dōman ōuchi kagami (1734); ukiyo-e by Kuniyoshi

Earliest known written form 1662; material older in oral tradition

Abe no Yasuna rescues a white fox; in gratitude it assumes the form of his widowed sister-in-law Kuzunoha, becomes his wife, and bears a son, the future onmyōji Abe no Seimei. When the child sees her true form — her fox shadow thrown on the paper door — she writes a farewell poem, leaves, and vanishes into Shinoda Forest. Seimei grows into the greatest yin-yang master of legend, his power implicitly inherited from his mother. Gives Japanese culture its model of the fox-mother: loving, gifted, doomed to leave. The historical Seimei (10th century) never had a fox mother; the legend attached itself to him in the early modern period and made him, in the popular imagination, the scion of fox-kind.

Tamamo-no-mae and the Sessho-sekiOtogizōshi cycle (established by the 14th century); Hokusai's paintings; local legend at Nasu

Written form by 14th century; elaborated in Edo and modern retellings

The brilliant and beautiful court lady Tamamo-no-mae — in truth a nine-tailed golden fox — charms the emperor and his court, sows illness and discord, and is unmasked by diviners. Hunted down, she flees to Nasu plain, is slain, and her dying spirit enters a stone: the Sessho-seki, 'killing stone,' which poisons all who touch it. The stone is eventually broken or pacified by a Buddhist priest, and the fox-spirit, in some versions, achieves release and even reverence. The story links feminine charm, foreign influence, and political catastrophe; it is the most famous Japanese instance of the nine-tailed fox and the hinge connecting Japanese fox lore to the Chinese Daji story (Shang dynasty) — the same vixen, in the later legend, returning after two millennia. The Sessho-seki remains a tourist site at Nasu, and reported cracks in the stone are interpreted through the myth.

The Fox Wedding (kitsune no yomeiri)Weather lore and folktale; folktale-type scholarship (Seki Keigo's Types of Japanese Folktales)

Early modern to present

When rain falls while the sun shines — a sunshower — the foxes are holding their wedding procession: a train of lights and figures crossing the fields, visible to the spiritually acute, sometimes to everyone. Humans who witness it may be blessed, warned, or merely bewildered; interfering with the procession invites fox displeasure. Makes the kitsune a weather deity of sorts and cements its association with thresholds and transitions; the image of the lantern-lit fox wedding in a sunshower became a staple of ukiyo-e and remains one of the most reproduced kitsune motifs.

Accounts II
The Trickster's RepertoireKonjaku monogatari shū; Lafcadio Hearn's Glimpses of Unfamiliar Japan (1894); Casal (1959); Nozaki (1961)

Medieval to modern

A family of short tales in which foxes punish the proud and the greedy: a boastful drinker is shown a phantom feast and left biting grass; a merchant accepts leaves that look like gold and is ruined; a traveler is led in circles all night by a 'disorienting deity'; a priest is humiliated before his congregation. Hearn recorded the folk explanation — foxes 'bewitch' — used by Izumo farmers for every unexplained mishap. Yokai.com sums up the target list: 'wicked priests, greedy merchants, and boastful drunkards.' The tales function as folk justice and as cautionary comedy; they also trained generations of Japanese to read ordinary accidents as fox-work — the interpretive habit Itō Ryōhei documents in modern fieldwork.

Tsurigitsune (The Fox-Trapping)Kyogen repertoire

Muromachi period onward

A fox, to rescue its whelp from a trapper, takes the form of a monk and visits the trapper's hut to wheedle the trap away. The trapper is at first deceived, then suspicious; he challenges the 'monk,' and the fox, rattled, reverts to animal form and flees — but saves its cub. The play is a comic masterpiece of disguise, bluff, and the limits of fox cunning. Keeps the kitsune on stage as a sympathetic trickster and demonstrates that the disguise fails under pressure — the same vulnerability the fox-wife stories treat tragically.

Kokaji (The Fox of the Sword-Making)Noh repertoire

Muromachi period

At the imperial command to forge a great sword, the smith Munechika prays at the Inari shrine; a white fox — Inari's messenger, in the form of a court lady (myōbu) — appears and assists at the forge, and the sword is completed. The fox is honored at the court; the play dramatizes the kitsune's holy service in its most benign register. Institutionalizes in high art the link between foxes, Inari, and craft blessings — the white fox as divine collaborator rather than deceiver.

The Bewitched and the Bewitchers (madowashigami tales)Konjaku monogatari shū 27:42; Itō Ryōhei's fieldwork (Kokugakuin Japan Studies 4, 2023)

11th–12th century and 20th–21st century fieldwork

Kuni-no-toshinobu, walking in familiar Kyōto, finds himself going round and round the same place; he concludes a 'disorienting deity' has possessed him from Kujō — 'was it a fox or something similar?' Neither deity nor fox appears in the story; the fox exists only in the interpretation. Itō Ryōhei shows the same structure alive in modern Fukushima fieldwork: people who lose their way in the mountains say 'a fox bewitched me,' and the folk remedy is to sit down and think. The story is born when the fox is invoked, and dies — or is replaced by another explanation — when it is not. Demonstrates that the kitsune is not only a character in Japanese folklore but a shared explanatory technology — a way of making sense of disorientation, illness, and misfortune — which is why the creature is simultaneously beloved, feared, and ubiquitous.

Kitsune are always evil or always good — The tradition is structurally ambivalent: the same being serves Inari and possesses the unwary, loves truly and deceives utterly. The 'good fox / evil fox' split is a modern taxonomic convenience, not an ancient law.

A fox gains one tail every 100 years, maxing at nine — Attested folktales give one, five, seven, and nine tails without a consistent schedule; the century rule is a modern rationalization, and the nine-tailed form is Chinese-derived.

All kitsune are Inari's messengers — Inari's foxes (zenko, myōbu) are one branch; nogitsune ('wild foxes') have no shrine allegiance and are the protagonists of most trickster and possession tales.

Kitsune, huli jing, and kumiho are the same creature — They share an ancient East Asian fox-spirit complex and specific borrowings (nine tails, Daji), but each national tradition has its own moral register, lore, and cult. Japanese kitsune are more domestic and shrine-bound; Korean kumiho are more predatory; Chinese huli jing more varied and older.

Kitsunetsuki was an 'official medical diagnosis' until the early 20th century — Fox possession was widely believed and documented, and early psychiatrists discussed it as a culture-bound syndrome — but it was never a formal diagnostic category in modern medicine. The belief caused real suffering and stigma; the 'official diagnosis' phrasing inflates its institutional status.

The kitsune is a modern anime invention (or 'Pokémon made it up') — Foxes as omens appear in the Nihon Shoki (720 CE); the fox-wife story is a 9th-century Buddhist miracle tale; the nine-tailed vixen is 14th-century at latest. Anime and games are the latest layer of a 1,300-year tradition.

Holo from Spice & Wolf is a kitsune — Holo is a wolf deity (ōkami), not a fox; the confusion stems from her being an animal-eared harvest spirit with a similar romantic structure. The wolf and fox traditions are distinct in Japanese folklore.
