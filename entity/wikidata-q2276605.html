Form

No definitive depictions of Angitia survive. She was likely depicted in anthropomorphic form, possibly with attributes of healing or snakes. Votive offerings from her sanctuary include bronze figures of snakes, but these are more likely offerings than representations of the goddess herself. Later Roman-era depictions, if any, would likely have shown her in the guise of a Roman matron or a goddess of healing.

Powers

Curing Snake Bites Her principal and most well-attested power. She was believed to possess the knowledge to heal the venomous bites of the snakes of central Italy. This was likely a practical skill passed down through generations of Marsian healers, personified in the goddess. Attested in Pliny the Elder and the votive offerings of anatomical body parts from her sanctuary. Her power was specific to snake venom and associated ailments; she was not a general panacea goddess. None directly, but the mechanism of the cure (magical vs. herbal) is not specified in the sources.

Snake Charming As the divine patroness of the Marsi, she granted her people the ability to handle, charm, and control venomous snakes without being harmed. This was a defining ethnic trait of the Marsi in Roman literature. Attested in Silius Italicus and Pliny the Elder. This power was a gift to her people, not something she performed herself in the surviving myths. The exact nature of this skill—whether it was a magical ritual, a form of herbal protection, or a physical technique—was a subject of both ancient wonder and skepticism.

Sorcery and Herbalism Angitia was a goddess of magic, a mistress of potent herbs and remedies. This power is a logical extension of her healing role and is strongly implied by her conflation with the sorceress Medea. Implied by her identification with Medea in Pliny and other sources, and by the general association of snake-charming with magical arts in the ancient world. This power is more inferred than explicitly described. The specific nature of her magic is unknown. The Roman conflation of Angitia with Medea may have over-emphasized the 'sorcery' aspect of a goddess who was primarily a practical, local healer.

Behavior & Habitat

As a goddess, her behavior is inferred from her cult and functions. She was a benevolent, healing deity who protected her people from the dangers of the venomous snakes that infested the Fucine Lake region. She was also a goddess of magic and sorcery, a mistress of potent, natural remedies that could both harm and heal.

Accounts

According to this etiological myth, Angitia was not a native goddess but was Medea herself, the Colchian princess and sorceress. After her infamous deeds in Greece (the murder of her children, the poisoning of her rival Glauce), Medea fled in her dragon-drawn chariot. She crossed the Adriatic and landed in the territory of the Marsi in central Italy. There, she taught the local people the arts of healing and snake-charming, which they had previously lacked. She was then venerated as a goddess under the name Angitia.

This is not a myth about Angitia herself, but a 'story' of her cultural legacy. The Marsi were famous throughout the Roman world as 'Marsorum gens' (the Marsian race) who could charm snakes. They would handle venomous vipers, spit on them to render them harmless (according to Pliny), and use them in rituals. This ability was seen as a direct gift and inheritance from their goddess Angitia. During the Second Punic War, Marsian soldiers in Hannibal's army were noted for their ability to use snakes as weapons or to terrify their enemies.

What We Don't Know

The primary gap is the lack of any definitive iconographic representation of Angitia herself. We have her sanctuary, her inscriptions, and her votive offerings, but no certain image of the goddess. The exact nature of the Marsian snake-charming ritual is also lost. Furthermore, the specific rituals and festivals of her cult are not described in any surviving source. The relationship between her cult and the later Roman imperial period is also under-documented.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Angitia as the patron saint of a remote, dangerous mountain town. She's not a grand Olympian; she's the local hero-deity. Her 'church' is a sacred grove by a lake. Her 'miracles' are practical, life-saving cures for a very specific and terrifying problem: snake bites. Her 'congregation' is a tough, independent people known for their strange and amazing skill. She's the goddess you pray to when you hear a rattle in the grass — a blend of ancient doctor, wise-woman, and the fierce protectress of her people.
