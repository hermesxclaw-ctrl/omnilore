Who It Is

Before Rome was an empire, before the legions marched, the mountain people of central Italy had a secret. They walked among vipers, whispered to them, and were not bitten. Their power came from a goddess of the groves and the venom — a healer and a sorceress named Angitia, a deity so potent that the Romans would later claim she was Medea herself, fled from the tragedy of Greek myth to find a new home among the Marsian hills.

As a goddess, her behavior is inferred from her cult and functions. She was a benevolent, healing deity who protected her people from the dangers of the venomous snakes that infested the Fucine Lake region. She was also a goddess of magic and sorcery, a mistress of potent, natural remedies that could both harm and heal.

Powers
Curing Snake Bites

Her principal and most well-attested power. She was believed to possess the knowledge to heal the venomous bites of the snakes of central Italy. This was likely a practical skill passed down through generations of Marsian healers, personified in the goddess.

Limit: Her power was specific to snake venom and associated ailments; she was not a general panacea goddess.

Snake Charming

As the divine patroness of the Marsi, she granted her people the ability to handle, charm, and control venomous snakes without being harmed. This was a defining ethnic trait of the Marsi in Roman literature.

Limit: This power was a gift to her people, not something she performed herself in the surviving myths.

Sorcery and Herbalism

Angitia was a goddess of magic, a mistress of potent herbs and remedies. This power is a logical extension of her healing role and is strongly implied by her conflation with the sorceress Medea.

Limit: This power is more inferred than explicitly described. The specific nature of her magic is unknown.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No definitive depictions of Angitia survive. She was likely depicted in anthropomorphic form, possibly with attributes of healing or snakes. Votive offerings from her sanctuary include bronze figures of snakes, but these are more likely offerings than representations of the goddess herself. Later Roman-era depictions, if any, would likely have shown her in the guise of a Roman matron or a goddess of healing.

Symbols — The snake is her primary symbol. Other likely symbols include healing implements, herbs, and possibly a staff or vessel associated with her magic. The sanctuary itself, the Lucus Angitiae, was a sacred grove, a symbol of her connection to the wild, natural world.

Domains — Snakes and serpent-charming, healing and medicine (particularly of snake bites), magic and sorcery, herbalism and pharmacology, protection of her people (the Marsi and Paeligni).

Accounts I
The Myth of Medea's Flight to ItalyPliny the Elder (Naturalis Historia), drawing on earlier Greek and Roman sources.

Late Republic / Early Empire (1st century CE).

According to this etiological myth, Angitia was not a native goddess but was Medea herself, the Colchian princess and sorceress. After her infamous deeds in Greece (the murder of her children, the poisoning of her rival Glauce), Medea fled in her dragon-drawn chariot. She crossed the Adriatic and landed in the territory of the Marsi in central Italy. There, she taught the local people the arts of healing and snake-charming, which they had previously lacked. She was then venerated as a goddess under the name Angitia. This myth served to 'Hellenize' and explain the origin of a powerful local deity. It created a link between the Marsi and the famous Greek mythological world, elevating their status. It also provided a neat explanation for the Marsians' unusual and formidable skill with snakes.

Accounts II
The Marsian Snake-CharmersSilius Italicus (Punica), Pliny the Elder (Naturalis Historia), and various other Roman authors.

1st century CE (describing events of the 3rd century BCE).

This is not a myth about Angitia herself, but a 'story' of her cultural legacy. The Marsi were famous throughout the Roman world as 'Marsorum gens' (the Marsian race) who could charm snakes. They would handle venomous vipers, spit on them to render them harmless (according to Pliny), and use them in rituals. This ability was seen as a direct gift and inheritance from their goddess Angitia. During the Second Punic War, Marsian soldiers in Hannibal's army were noted for their ability to use snakes as weapons or to terrify their enemies. The Marsian snake-charming tradition became a famous, almost legendary, ethnic trait. It cemented Angitia's role as the national goddess of the Marsi and contributed to their fearsome reputation as a hardy, magical mountain people.

Angitia was simply the Roman name for Medea. — Angitia was an indigenous Italic goddess of the Marsi. The identification with Medea was a late, literary conflation by Roman authors, not a native belief. It is a classic example of 'interpretatio Romana' (the Roman practice of equating foreign gods with their own).

The Marsi were immune to snake venom. — The Marsi were highly skilled at handling and charming snakes, and likely used herbal remedies to neutralize or protect against venom. They were not biologically immune, a fact that was a source of wonder and exaggeration in Roman sources.

Angitia was a major goddess of the Roman pantheon. — Angitia was a local, regional goddess. She was never a part of the official Roman state religion and had no major temple or cult in Rome itself. Her importance was confined to the Marsian and Paelignian territories.
