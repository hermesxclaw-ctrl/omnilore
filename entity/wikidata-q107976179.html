Who It Is

Koza is not one thing but a chorus of goats, ghosts, and silk merchants answering to the same name — a Slavic trickster-goat who barges into winter kitchens demanding song, a bellowing Polish bagpipe stitched from an actual goatskin, a 15th-century caravanserai in Bursa where the Ottoman silk road was weighed in cocoons, and a vanished town on the edge of Wakayama that still whistles as a train station. Say the word and you summon all of them at once.

Attested (folklore): Ritual Koza is boisterous, lascivious, gluttonous, and comic — it dances, butts people with horns, bleats, pretends to die (falls down after being 'shot' or 'beaten'), then is miraculously revived by music, a doctor figure, or vodka, to the delight of hosts who then give gifts (food, money). Tale Koza-Dereza is a classic trickster-abuser: she lies to her master that she was starved, causing the master to expel his children or wife, then occupies the hare's (or other animal's) house until expelled by a bee, crayfish, or rooster. Powers
Ritual death and resurrection (fertility magic)

During kolędowanie, the Koza performer collapses as if dead after symbolic shooting/beating, then revives when music plays or a 'doctor' administers a cure. The revival is believed to transfer vitality to household, ensuring crop and herd fertility for the year. Attested in Kolberg and contemporary ethnographic film.

Limit: Power is performative and communal belief-based, not literal; requires correct ritual sequence, music, and host offerings. No attested ability outside ritual context.

Trickster deception and house usurpation (tale type)

In Koza-Dereza tales (ATU 212C / 43), the goat lies convincingly to her owner about mistreatment, manipulates humans, and forcibly occupies another animal's dwelling, resisting eviction by wolf, bear, and fox through intimidation ('I am Koza-Dereza, half side flayed...'). Only a small creature (bee, crayfish, rooster) defeats her.

Limit: Power fails against smallest opponent; goat is ultimately expelled, beaten, or killed. No supernatural invulnerability — relies on bluff.

Apotropaic noise and horn-butting

Clacking wooden jaw, bleating, bell-ringing, and mock butting are described as driving away evil spirits, winter demons, and misfortune from the house. Horns are used to poke audience playfully.

Limit: Effect is symbolic/sonic within folk belief; no attested physical protection beyond social function.

Musical voice (instrument Koza)

The instrument Koza produces a loud, reedy, continuous drone with chanter melody, capable of leading dance music for hours, outdoors audible over large crowds. Traditional repertoire includes weselne (wedding) marches, polkas, and obertas.

Limit: Requires skilled reed maintenance, constant bag pressure, limited chromatic range (typically one octave plus). Goatskin bag sensitive to humidity and temperature.

Commercial sanctuary (architectural Koza Han)

As a caravanserai, Koza Han provided secure storage, lodging, and market for silk cocoons, with guarded courtyard and stone vaults. Historically granted tax and legal protections under vakıf endowment.

Limit: Function ceased as silk road declined; now retail and café space. No supernatural ability.

Koza-Dereza (The Flayed Goat-Braggart)Afanasyev, Narodnye russkie skazki No. 23; Ukrainian oral variants recorded by Kolberg and later by Hrinchenko

Oral tradition pre-19th century, first published 1855-1863

A man buys a goat and sends her to graze with his children. Each time he asks the goat if she has eaten, she replies she has had nothing but a blade of grass and a drop of water, though she is full. Enraged, the man drives away his children (or wives in variants). The goat then finds the hare's hut while the hare is away, occupies it, and frightens away all who try to evict her — wolf, bear, fox — by chanting 'I am Koza-Dereza, half my side flayed, I will gore you with horns, trample with feet.' Finally a bee (or crayfish, or rooster in Polish variants) stings her or pinches her, and she flees. The family is reunited. In some variants the goat is killed. Etiological tale explaining distrust of braggarts; moral about lying and ingratitude; tale type ATU 212C remains a core vertep puppet play. Later reinterpreted in Soviet children's books as comic story, sanitizing the expulsion violence.

The Christmas Koza House Visit (Kolędowanie z Kozą)Kolberg, Lud; ethnographic films by Polish National Film Archive (1950s-1970s); contemporary documentation by Muzeum Wsi Polskiej

Attested 19th century to present (living tradition)

On Christmas Eve or New Year's Eve, a troupe of carolers (kolędnicy) including Koza, a Herod, a Doctor, a Jew, a Bear, and musicians goes house to house. Koza dances wildly, demands treats, pretends to die after being 'shot' by a hunter figure, falls to floor. The Doctor examines her, musicians play a lively tune, Koza leaps up revived, dances more vigorously, and the hosts give pierogi, vodka, or money. The house is blessed for the year. In some villages, if hosts are stingy, Koza butts them and musicians play a mocking tune. Ritual ensures symbolic fertility and community bonding; now performed at folk festivals (e.g., Kazimierz Dolny) and in diaspora communities in US and Brazil. UNESCO intangible heritage nomination discussions reference it.

The Goatskin Bagpipe's Wedding ServiceSobieska, Jadwiga and Sobieski, Marian - field recordings 1950-1970, Polish Radio Folk Archive; Museum of Folk Musical Instruments in Szydłowiec catalog

Instrument attested 18th century to present; repertoire documented 19th-20th century

The Koza (kozioł weselny) was the indispensable wedding instrument in western Poland. The koziarz (piper) led the wedding procession from church to inn, played for the oczepiny (capping ceremony), and played through the night. Legend says a Koza made from a stubborn goat that ate the farmer's wedding wreath will never stay in tune unless the piper drinks with it. The instrument's death is also ritualized: when the bag finally rots, it is buried with honors. Repertoire preserved in post-war folk revival; instrument nearly extinct by 1960s, revived by makers like Tomasz Kiciński. Now taught at Academy of Music in Poznań.

Accounts II
The Founding of Koza HanVakıf deed of Bayezid II (896 AH / 1490-91 CE); Evliya Çelebi, Seyahatname (17th century) description of Bursa; Goodwin, History of Ottoman Architecture

1490-1491 construction, active silk market 15th-19th centuries

Sultan Bayezid II endows a new han in Bursa to capture the Persian silk trade. Built of stone and brick with 95 cells, it becomes the official cocoon (koza) auction market where silk producers from the Bursa plain bring cocoons to be weighed, taxed, and sold to weavers. The han's courtyard hosts the annual cocoon festival. Evliya Çelebi later describes it as full of Persian and Armenian merchants, with the scent of mulberry leaves. After the 19th-century silk blight and the rise of factory silk, the han declines, survives the 1855 Bursa earthquake, and is restored in the 20th century as a bazaar for silk scarves and cafés. Koza Han remains the symbol of Bursa's silk heritage, now UNESCO World Heritage Site component (Bursa and Cumalıkızık, inscribed 2014). Continues as commercial and tourist center.

Koza, Wakayama: From Town to StationWakayama Prefecture municipal history; Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications Heisei mergers; JR West station records

Town of Koza (Koza-chō) existed 1889-2005; Koza Station opened 1936

The town of Koza on the Kii Peninsula, named for the Koza River (Koza-gawa), thrives on forestry and fishing. The Kisei Main Line brings Koza Station in 1936, connecting the remote coast. Population declines from ~4,500 (1960) to ~1,800 (2000). Under the Heisei municipal mergers, Koza-chō merges with Kushimoto-chō on April 1, 2005, ceasing to exist as an independent municipality. The name survives only in Koza Station, Koza River, and Koza Junior High. Similarly, Kōza District in Kanagawa shrinks as its towns become cities (Zama, Ebina, Ayase), leaving only Samukawa Town today. Case study in rural depopulation and Heisei mergers; toponymic survival in infrastructure. Koza Station remains a stop for Limited Express Kuroshio.

Koza (2015) — The Boxer's Last FightFilm Koza, directed by Ivan Ostrochovský, screenplay by Marek Leščák & Ivan Ostrochovský; Berlinale Forum catalogue 2015; Slovak Film Institute

2015 (contemporary story, filmed 2013-2014)

Peter 'Koza' Baláž, a former Olympic boxer from Slovakia (real-life bronze medalist, Atlanta 1996 qualification), lives in poverty in a Roma settlement, his boxing career over, his girlfriend Miša pregnant. Nicknamed Koza (Goat) for his stubbornness, he goes on a bizarre odyssey through Slovakia and Germany with his manager, taking low-paying freak-show fights to earn money for an abortion Miša does not want, while wrestling with his own inability to provide. Shot in stark black-and-white documentary style with non-actors, the film ends ambiguously on the road, with Koza still fighting. Won awards at Vilnius and GoEast, selected as Slovak Oscar entry (not nominated). Brought attention to Roma poverty and post-athletic precarity in Slovakia. Title's goat metaphor discussed in reviews as stubbornness and scapegoating.

Is Koza a deity or a ritual character?Popular internet and neo-pagan sources claim Koza was a Slavic goddess or god of fertility, sometimes equated with Veles.Academic Slavistics (e.g., Moszyński, Ivanits) finds no primary source for a theonym Koza; it is a folk ritual mask and tale type, not a deity. Theonymic lists (Helmold, Thietmar) never mention Koza.
Organological identity: Is Koza the same as Dudy?Popular and some folk-festival usage treats 'koza' as generic word for any Polish bagpipe.Organologists (Sobieska, Muzeum Szydłowiec) distinguish Koza / Kozioł (Wielkopolska, large, horn chanter, one drone) from Dudy (Podhale, Gajdy) by construction, reed type, and repertoire.
Etymology of Japanese Koza (Okinawa) — goat or Goza?Some English travel blogs claim Okinawa's Koza comes from Japanese 'koza' meaning goat or 'small seat'.Okinawan historiography traces Koza to Okinawan place name Goza (呉座), altered under US administration to Koza for Camp Koza; unrelated to mainland Kōza/Koza or to animal.

Koza is a single fictional heroic-legend character with a unified biography and powers. — Koza is a disambiguation term covering at least six independent referents (folkloric goat, tale trickster, bagpipe, caravanserai, Japanese toponyms, film/surname). No single fictional continuity exists; the Wikidata QID is a disambiguation page, not a character.

The Koza bagpipe is made from a goat that is killed specifically for the instrument in a ritual sacrifice. — Historically the skin came from farm slaughter for meat/skin, not ritual sacrifice; modern makers use legally sourced goatskin or synthetic bags. No evidence of dedicated sacrificial killing for instrument construction in primary organological literature.

Koza Han means 'Goat Inn' in Turkish. — Turkish koza means 'cocoon' (silkworm cocoon), not goat (which is keçi). Koza Han is 'Cocoon Inn', named for the silk cocoon market it housed. The goat meaning is Slavic, not Turkish.
