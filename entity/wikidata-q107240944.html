The Approach

In the shadow of Mount Lykaion, where the ancient Greeks told tales of a king who dared to test the gods, there lived a prince whose name meant 'begetter'—Genetor, one of fifty sons born to the impious Lycaon. His story, though scant, is a chilling fragment of a mythic cycle that warned of the consequences of mortal arrogance and the terrible price of impiety.

Content advisory. Mythological themes include infanticide, cannibalism (implied), and divine punishment by lightning.
Identity

Genetor

Pronunciation /ˈdʒɛnɪtɔːr/ (approximate English pronunciation: JEN-i-tor)

Etymology

The name 'Genetor' (Greek: Γενήτωρ) derives from the Greek verb 'genesthai' (to be born, to become) and is typically translated as 'begetter' or 'ancestor'. It is related to the concept of generation and lineage, suggesting a figure associated with origins or fatherhood.

Cultural Ownership

Ancient Greek mythology, specifically the Arcadian tradition. The myth of Lycaon and his sons is set in Arcadia, a region in the central Peloponnese, and is part of the broader mythological complex surrounding Zeus Lykaios.

Disputed Identifications

Genetor is a minor figure, and there is no known dispute over his identification. However, the list of Lycaon's sons varies among ancient sources, and it is possible that some names are variants or local traditions. No other figure named Genetor is attested in Greek mythology.

Description

Appearance

No ancient source describes Genetor's physical appearance. As a prince of Arcadia, he would have been depicted as a young man of noble bearing in later artistic representations, but no such depictions are known.

Symbols & Iconography

No specific symbols or iconographic attributes are associated with Genetor. He is not known to have been depicted in ancient art. In modern times, he is a name in a list, without distinct imagery.

Behaviour & Personality

The only characterization of Genetor comes from Apollodorus, who describes the fifty sons of Lycaon as 'the most nefarious and carefree of all people.' This suggests that Genetor shared in the impiety and arrogance of his brothers, but no individual traits are recorded.

Domains & Habitats

As an Arcadian prince, Genetor would have lived in the region of Arcadia, likely in the city of Lycosura or on Mount Lykaion, the mythical seat of his father. However, no specific domain or habitat is attributed to him individually.

Version Differences

The list of Lycaon's sons varies among ancient sources. Apollodorus provides a list of fifty names, but other authors, such as Pausanias, do not enumerate all of them. There are also variations in the names of the mothers: Apollodorus says the sons were born to different women, while some scholia suggest that Lycaon's wife was the naiad Cyllene or Nonacris. These variations do not specifically affect Genetor's role.

Stories & Myths

The Impiety of Lycaon and His Sons

Source Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.8.1; Pausanias, Description of Greece 8.2; Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Roman Antiquities 1.13Culture Ancient Greek (Arcadia)Period Mythical age (before the Trojan War, likely in the Bronze Age)

Summary

Lycaon, king of Arcadia, had fifty sons, including Genetor. They were the most impious and reckless of men. To test their wickedness, Zeus visited them disguised as a peasant. The brothers killed a child and mixed its entrails into the meal served to the god. Zeus, enraged, overturned the table (or in some versions, struck the house with lightning) and killed Lycaon and his sons with lightning bolts. In some versions, Lycaon was turned into a wolf.

Participants

Zeus

Lycaon

Genetor and his brothers (including names like Callisto, Nyctimus, etc.)

Consequences

The death of Lycaon and his sons. In some versions, the youngest son, Nyctimus, was spared to continue the lineage. The myth explains the origins of the cult of Zeus Lykaios on Mount Lykaion and may be associated with human sacrifice in primitive times.

Variants

Pausanias says that Lycaon was turned into a wolf, and that his sons were killed by Zeus. He also mentions that Nyctimus was restored to life. Other sources, such as the scholia to Lycophron, may provide different lists of sons or alternative details, but these do not specifically alter Genetor's role.

Infanticide and Cannibalism

Source Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.8.1

Description

The sons of Lycaon killed a child and mixed its entrails into a meal served to Zeus. This act is the primary cause of their punishment.

Scholarly Context

Scholars interpret this myth as an aetiological tale explaining the origins of the cult of Zeus Lykaios, which may have involved human sacrifice. The story serves to distance the historical cult from such practices by attributing them to a mythical impious king and his sons.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

There is very little information about Genetor beyond his name and his inclusion in the list of Lycaon's sons. No ancient art or inscriptions depict him. His mother is unknown. The exact role he plays in the myth is identical to that of his brothers. Further research could explore the full list of Lycaon's sons in various sources and any regional variations, but it is unlikely that any additional information about Genetor specifically exists.

Research Scope

This dossier focuses on the figure of Genetor as attested in ancient Greek mythology, primarily through the Bibliotheca of Apollodorus and related sources. It covers his identity, mythological context, and the story of his demise, while acknowledging the scarcity of direct references and the challenges of reconstructing his role within the larger Lycaon myth.
