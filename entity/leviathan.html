Leviathan

The Twisting Serpent, King of the Sea, Primeval Chaos Dragon, The Great Whore-Be

also known asLivyatanThe Twisting Serpent
TypePrimordial Monster / Chaos Entity
CultureAbrahamic
Who It Is
Form

Leviathan has no single canonical physical description, but ancient sources converge on a multi-headed sea serpent or dragon of immense size. The Ugaritic Lôtān may have had seven heads. In Jewish midrash, Leviathan is 300 miles long with eyes that shine like the morning (Job 41:18), capable of making the deep waters boil with its breath. Its scales are impenetrable (Job 41:15–17); it breathes fire and smoke (Job 41:18–21). The Talmudic description gives it a fearsome visage but notes it fears the tiny worm 'kilbit.' In medieval Christian art, Leviathan became the Hellmouth—a monstrous jaw into which the damned disappear at the Last Judgment, appearing in Anglo-Saxon art from c. 800 onward. In Gnostic Ophite diagrams, Leviathan appears as an ouroboros—a serpent biting its own tail, encircling the material world. Hobbes' frontispiece shows Leviathan as a crowned giant made of human bodies, wielding a sword and crozier. The Satanic Bible's Sigil of Baphomet inscribes the Hebrew name לויתן counter-clockwise around a pentagram.

Powers

Indomitability and Invulnerability Leviathan's scales are impenetrable (Job 41:15–17); no weapon can pierce it. Its strength is such that it terrifies the mighty and makes the deep boil with its heat. In the ancient Near Eastern Chaoskampf tradition, this invulnerability represents the self-asserting power of primordial chaos that even the gods must contend with. Only God can ultimately defeat it (Isaiah 27:1). Job 41:1–34; Isaiah 27:1 Leviathan is ultimately subject to God's sovereignty. Its invulnerability is temporary—eschatologically it will be slain. It fears the tiny worm 'kilbit' (Baba Bathra 77b), suggesting even the mightiest chaos-force has an Achille's heel. Some interpreters (RSV footnote) suggest Job's Leviathan is merely a crocodile, not a cosmic monster—this naturalizing reading reduces its mythological significance.

Fire-Breathing and Boiling the Deep When Leviathan is hungry, it sends forth heat that makes all the waters of the deep boil (Baba Bathra 75a). Its breath kindles coals, and a flame goes out of its mouth (Job 41:19–21). Its eyes are like the eyelids of the morning, shining with illuminating power (Job 41:18). These features mark it as a fire-breathing dragon of the highest mythological order. Job 41:18–21; Baba Bathra 75a The fire-breathing motif is not present in the Ugaritic Lôtān texts; it may be a later Jewish expansion. Some modern exegetes interpret 'smoke' and 'flame' in Job 41 as poetic hyperbole for a crocodile's physical effects rather than literal dragon-breath.

Chaos Embodiment Leviathan represents the untamed, chaotic aspect of creation—the sea as a threatening, devouring force. In Canaanite mythology, Lôtān serves Yammu (Sea god) as a rebellious force against Baal Hadad's legitimate kingship. In the Hebrew Bible, this is re-theologized: God creates and controls Leviathan, using it to demonstrate divine superiority over chaos (Psalm 104:26). In Gnosticism, Leviathan becomes the boundary of the material world, actively preventing souls from reaching the Pleroma. Psalm 104:26; Ugaritic Baal Cycle; Gnostic texts The precise theological function of Leviathan as 'chaos' depends heavily on genre: in Job it is theophanic display; in Isaiah it is eschatological enemy; in Psalms it is God's creature. The 'chaos' reading faces tension with Psalm 104:26, where Leviathan is simply one of God's creatures playing in the sea, not an adversarial force.

Multi-Headed Dominion The Ugaritic Lôtān may be described as 'the mighty one with seven heads' (šlyṭ d.šbʿt rašm). This multi-headed motif transfers to the seven-headed dragon of Revelation 12–13, which gives power to the Beast. The seven heads represent complete dominion over the cosmos—each head a realm of chaos under a single controlling intelligence. In comparative mythology, multi-headed serpents appear across cultures: the Hydra (Greek), the seven-headed serpent defeated by Ninurta (Sumerian), the nine-headed Bakunawa (Philippine). Ugaritic Baal Cycle; Revelation 12–13; Sumerian iconography The Ugaritic text has lacunae, making it unclear whether 'seven heads' describes Lôtān or another monster. The Revelation dragon's seven heads are explicitly linked to Babylon/Rome, not directly to Leviathan. Christianity shifts the multi-headed motif from Leviathan to the Beast/Rome, weakening the direct identification.

Material-World Encapsulation (Gnostic) In Ophite Gnosticism, Leviathan is an ouroboros that encircles the material world like a dragon-shaped archon, biting its own tail. It generates the intrinsic evil of the universe and separates the Kenoma (lower world, governed by Archons) from the realm of Monad (God). After death, a soul must pass through the seven celestial spheres; if it does not succeed, it will be swallowed by Leviathan, who holds the world captive and returns the soul into an animal body. In Mandaeism, Leviathan is regarded as being coessential with a demon called Ur. In Manichaeism, Leviathan is killed by the sons of the fallen angel Shemyaza—an act portrayed as foolish, symbolizing the greatest triumphs as transient. Gnostic texts; Ophite Diagrams; Mandaean Ginza; Manichaean sources (Tardieu 2008) Ophite sources are fragmentary; whether the biblical Leviathan was actually identified with the Eden serpent is debated (Rasimus 2009). Mainstream Christianity rejects the Gnostic identification entirely, treating Leviathan as either a natural creature or a demon, not a cosmic boundary.

Accounts

In the Ugaritic Baal Cycle, the sea god Yammu sends his messenger to demand that the high god El surrender Baal Hadad. Yammu's agents include Lôtān, 'the fugitive serpent,' possibly 'the mighty one with seven heads.' Baal Hadad, with the support of the craftsman god Kothar-wa-Khasis, defeats Lôtān—either by spear or by divine decree. This victory establishes Baal's kingship over the cosmos. The narrative encodes the seasonal cycle: the storm god's defeat of the sea serpent ensures rain, fertility, and agricultural abundance.

In the divine speeches of Job, God responds to Job's complaints by describing two primordial monsters: Behemoth (land) and Leviathan (sea). Leviathan is portrayed as the ultimate example of untamable creation—its scales are impenetrable, it breathes fire and smoke, it terrifies the mighty. God asks Job: 'Can you put a rope through its nose? Can you pierce its jaw with a hook?' The rhetorical point is clear: if Job cannot control Leviathan, he has no ground to question God's governance of the cosmos. Leviathan here is not evil but a manifestation of divine power—a creature so formidable that only its Creator can master it.

Rabbinic tradition holds that God created Leviathan male and female on the fifth day, but slew the female to prevent the species from multiplying and destroying the world. Her flesh is salted and reserved for the eschatological banquet given to the righteous when the Messiah arrives. Leviathan's skin will be stretched over the tent of the feast, illuminating Jerusalem with its brightness. The Sukkot prayer asks to merit dwelling 'in the sukkah of the skin of Leviathan.' The Akdamut poem (11th century) describes God slaughtering Leviathan and serving it to all righteous in heaven. In 1 Enoch, Leviathan (female, watery) and Behemoth (male, desert) are the two great monsters of the fifth day, destined to feed the elect.

In Ophite Gnostic systems, Leviathan appears as a dragon-shaped archon who encircles the material world like an ouroboros—biting his own tail. This serpent generates the intrinsic evil of the universe and separates the Kenoma (lower, material realm) from the Pleroma (divine fullness). After death, a soul must traverse the seven celestial spheres; if it fails, Leviathan swallows it and returns it to an animal body (reincarnation). The Ophites were accused by Origen of venerating the biblical serpent of Eden, which they may have identified with Leviathan. In Mandaeism, Leviathan is coessential with Ur, the demon of darkness. In Manichaeism, Leviathan is killed by the sons of the fallen angel Shemyaza in an act symbolizing the transience of earthly triumph.

Thomas Hobbes' Leviathan is the most influential secular appropriation of the biblical monster's name. Hobbes uses it as a metaphor for the sovereign state: an artificial person whose body is composed of all citizens, wielding absolute power to maintain peace and prevent the war of all against all. The famous frontispiece by Abraham Bosse shows a crowned giant made of human faces, holding a sword (temporal power) and a crozier (spiritual power). Hobbes argues that this Leviathan-state 'feeds' on its citizens through education, ideology, and conditioning, imprinting conscience mechanically so that power reproduces itself across generations. The work became foundational for social contract theory.

Leviathan has been one of the most adaptable mythological names in modern culture. Herman Melville's Moby-Dick (1851) explicitly invokes the Leviathan tradition—the white whale as the inscrutable, indomitable sea monster. Mastodon's 2004 concept album Leviathan retells the Moby-Dick story through progressive metal. In the TV series Supernatural (season 7, 2011–2012), Leviathans are ancient monsters released from Purgatory, shape-shifting predators who infiltrate human society. In Shin Megami Tensei (1992–present), Leviathan is a recurring high-level demon. In Dungeons & Dragons, Leviathan is a powerful elemental water creature. The name has also been applied to extinct megafauna (Livyatan melvillei, a Miocene sperm whale) and cryovolcanoes on Neptune's moon Triton.

Content Notes

Leviathan's transformation into the Hellmouth—a monstrous jaw devouring the damned at the Last Judgment—was one of the most terrifying images of medieval Christianity. From Anglo-Saxon art c. 800 onward, the Hellmouth appeared in illuminated manuscripts, cathedral sculpture, and mystery plays across Europe. The damned were depicted being dragged into Leviathan's jaws by demons, their screams visible. This imagery served a clear pastoral function: fear of being swallowed by Leviathan motivated repentance. But it also exploited primal terror—the fear of being eaten alive—as a tool of religious control. The Hellmouth persisted through the Reformation and into baroque art (Rossignolo's Last Judgment fresco, c. 1555).

The Hellmouth motif is a striking example of how a biblical chaos-monster was repurposed as an instrument of terror in Christian pastoral practice. The transition from 'God's creature' (Psalm 104) to 'devouring jaw of hell' (medieval art) represents a theological escalation with pastoral and psychological consequences.

Link 1995; Hofmann 2008; medieval art history

Peter Binsfeld's 1589 classification of Leviathan as the demon of envy (one of the Seven Princes of Hell) cemented the identification of the biblical sea monster with a specific sin. This demonological mapping was part of a broader early modern project of assigning named demons to each vice, creating a structured infernal bureaucracy that mirrored heavenly hierarchies. Leviathan-as-envy meant that the vast, uncontrollable sea creature became a personal tempter, whispering jealousy and covetousness into human hearts.

The demonization of Leviathan reflects early modern anxieties about hierarchy, order, and sin. By assigning specific demons to specific sins, theologians created a manageable infernal map—but also projected the monster's chaotic vastness into the most intimate human failures (envy, desire, pride).

Binsfeld 1589; Catholic Encyclopedia (1911)

The Church of Satan (1969) inscribed the Hebrew word לויתן (Leviathan) counter-clockwise around the Sigil of Baphomet's pentagram, making Leviathan the literal center of Satanic iconography. This was not a neutral mythological reference but an intentional provocation: reclaiming a name from the Bible's most fearsome monster for a religion that celebrates rebellion against Christian morality. The Sigil became one of the most recognizable religious symbols of the 20th century, appearing on album covers, tattoos, and internet culture—often divorced from its actual theological context.

The appropriation of Leviathan by Satanism represents the final stage in the monster's theological journey: from Canaanite deity-servant to biblical creature to Christian devil to Gnostic archon to Satanic prince of hell. Each transformation stripped away one layer of original meaning and added a new layer of oppositional identity.

Church of Satan website; LaVey 1969

While Hobbes intended his Leviathan as a solution to anarchy, subsequent history revealed the terrifying potential of his metaphor. The absolute sovereign state—'the mortal god' that contains chaos through terror—became the template for absolutist monarchy, totalitarianism, and police states. The idea that the state must 'feed' on its citizens, imprint their conscience mechanically, and maintain itself through terror, reads very differently after Stalin, Hitler, and modern surveillance states.

Hobbes' Leviathan is one of the most ambivalent texts in political philosophy. It offers a genuine solution to civil war but also provides intellectual cover for absolutism. The metaphor of the state as devouring monster remains disturbingly apt for analyzing modern state violence.

Hobbes 1651; Goff 2022

Scholarly Debates
Is Leviathan a real creature or a mythological symbol?
Leviathan is a mythological construct derived from Canaanite and Mesopotamian Chaoskampf traditions. It never existed as a physical animal. The 19th-century identification with the crocodile (RSV footnote) or great whales is a naturalizing misreading that strips the text of its theological and mythological significance. ⇌ Leviathan refers to large aquatic animals known to the ancient Israelites—possibly the crocodile (Gesenius), the whale (modern Hebrew לויתן), or a now-extinct megafauna. The detailed description in Job 41 reflects observation of real creatures, albeit rendered in hyperbolic poetic language.

What We Don't Know

The Ugaritic Baal Cycle text has lacunae that make it uncertain whether 'seven heads' refers to Lôtān or another monster. The lost ending of the Assumption of Moses (if it contained Leviathan material) is inaccessible. Islamic sources do not contain a direct Leviathan figure; any connection must be reconstructed from hadith references to great sea creatures. Pre-Christian Jewish art or inscriptions depicting Leviathan are extremely scarce. The Ophite Gnostic sources are fragmentary and their identification of Leviathan with the Eden serpent is debated. The exact Canaanite-Hebrew transmission history of Lotan to Leviathan requires more detailed linguistic analysis than current scope permits. Non-Abrahamic traditions (Hindu, Buddhist, Shinto) have no identified Leviathan parallels beyond generic world-serpent motifs.

A way to hold it in your mind

Leviathan is not just a sea monster. It is the name humanity gives to whatever it cannot control—the ocean that swallows ships, the empire that swallows nations, the state that swallows its citizens, the desire that swallows the soul, and the chaos that preceded creation. From the Canaanite Lôtān defeated by Baal Hadad to Hobbes' sovereign state devouring its own people, from the Gnostic serpent encircling the material world to Mastodon's crushing guitar riffs about the white whale, Leviathan has been reimagined in every age as the boundary between order and chaos—but each reimagining reveals more about the age doing the imagining than about the ancient monster itself. The biblical text's most profound move is not to make Leviathan evil but to make it God's creature: the ultimate proof that even chaos serves the Creator. That theological insight—that the thing we most fear is, in the grand scheme, merely a plaything in the sea—is what has kept Leviathan relevant for three thousand years.
