Form

No specific physical description survives in primary sources. As a male deity (Mikoto) of the heavenly realm, he would conventionally be depicted in formal Heian-era court attire in later artistic representations, though this is speculative.

Powers

Divine Authority (Mikoto status) As a kami bearing the honorific 'Mikoto,' he possesses inherent divine authority and is entitled to participate in the councils of the heavenly deities. Kojiki; Nihon Shoki (inferred from title) No specific powers are enumerated in surviving texts. None recorded, due to lack of specific information.

Ancestral Blessing (Clan Patronage) As a tutelary ancestor of the Owari no Kuni no Miyatsuko (the hereditary governors of Owari Province), he is believed to confer legitimacy and protective blessing upon his descendants and their domain. Owari no Kuni no Miyatsuko no Honshō This is a genealogical function rather than a mythic power; it operates through the ritual veneration of descendants. The exact genealogical link is contested among scholars due to the late compilation of the clan records.

Accounts

After the sun goddess Amaterasu retreats into the rock cave, plunging the world into darkness, the myriad kami assemble on the dry bed of the Heavenly River. They devise a plan to lure her out using the sacred mirror, jewel, and the revelry of the goddess Ame-no-Uzume. This assembly is the central act of cosmic restoration in Japanese myth, and it is within this context that Amanosakitama and other minor deities are said to have been present, either as participants or as witnesses.

Amanosakitama no Mikoto is recorded as an ancestor in the lineage of the hereditary governors of Owari Province. This clan claimed descent from the heavenly deities and played a significant role in the ritual life of the region, including the stewardship of Atsuta Jingu, the shrine housing the sacred sword Kusanagi.

Scholarly Debates
Existence and role in the Kiki texts
Amanosakitama no Mikoto is a distinct deity mentioned in the genealogical sections of the Kojiki or Nihon Shoki. ⇌ The name may be a variant or corruption of another deity's name, or a conflation of 'Amano' and a place name, and may not represent a distinct figure at all.

Genealogical position in the Owari clan
Amanosakitama is a direct ancestor of the Owari no Kuni no Miyatsuko. ⇌ The genealogical connection is a later invention to legitimize the clan's authority, and the deity's actual relationship to the clan is unknown.
Evidence for A: The clan records (Honshō) present the lineage as authoritative.
Evidence for B: Such genealogies were commonly fabricated or embellished during the Heian period for political reasons.
What We Don't Know

The primary gap is the near-total absence of direct attestation in the most accessible primary sources. The Kojiki and Nihon Shoki indexes do not list this exact name, suggesting he may be a figure from a specific clan genealogy that has not been fully digitized or translated. Access to the Owari no Kuni no Miyatsuko no Honshō in its original form is necessary for verification. Further research in Japanese-language scholarly databases (e.g., CiNii, J-Stage) is required to determine if this deity is discussed in modern academic literature. The connection to the Ama-no-Iwato myth is inferred and requires verification. The pronunciation is a reconstruction based on standard kundoku readings.

A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine a member of the British royal family's extended lineage who appears in a single medieval charter and nowhere else. They might have been at the coronation, or they might be a clerical error. Amanosakitama is the Shinto equivalent: a name on a clan tree, a potential witness to the greatest drama in Japanese myth, but a figure whose reality is as thin as the oldest parchment.
