Who It Is

In the shadow of the underworld, where the flames of hell lick at the edges of the living world, stands a bodhisattva who refuses to enter paradise until every last being is saved. Kṣitigarbha, known as Dizang in China and Jizō in Japan, is the guardian of the forgotten, the lost, and the damned—a figure whose vow of compassion echoes through millennia of Buddhist practice.

Kṣitigarbha is characterized by his boundless compassion and unwavering commitment to his vow. He is said to work tirelessly in the darkest realms, comforting the suffering and guiding them toward liberation. He is patient, gentle, and approachable, often described as a father or mother figure. Unlike many bodhisattvas who reside in pure lands, he chooses to dwell in the hells and intermediate states, embodying the ideal of selfless service.

Etymology — The Sanskrit name Kṣitigarbha (क्षितिगर्भ) is composed of 'kṣiti' (earth) and 'garbha' (womb, embryo, or storehouse). It is often translated as 'Earth Treasury' or 'Earth Store'.

Powers
Vow to save all beings

Kṣitigarbha has vowed not to attain Buddhahood until the hells are emptied. This vow gives him immense merit and power, making him a powerful intercessor for beings in suffering.

Limit: The vow is ongoing; he cannot achieve final liberation until all beings are saved, which is effectively infinite.

Ability to open the gates of hell

His staff can strike the ground and open the gates of hell, allowing him to enter and rescue beings.

Limit: He uses this power only for liberation, not for personal gain.

Wish-fulfilling jewel (cintāmaṇi)

The jewel he holds can grant wishes and emit light that illuminates the darkness of the underworld, providing comfort and guidance.

Limit: The jewel is a symbol of his compassion, not a universal wish-granter; it works in accordance with karma.

Manifestation in all six realms

Kṣitigarbha can appear in any of the six realms of existence to teach and save beings, adapting his form to their needs.

Limit: His manifestations are limited by the karma of beings; he cannot force liberation.

Protection of children and travelers

In Japanese folk belief, Jizō is invoked to protect children, especially those who die young, and to guide travelers safely.

Limit: This is a folk development not explicitly found in canonical sutras.

He holds a staff (khakkhara) in his right hand, which has six rings that jingle to warn insects and small animals, and a wish-fulfilling jewel (cintāmaṇi) in his left hand. Accounts I
The Vow of the Earth Store BodhisattvaKṣitigarbha-praṇidhāna Sūtra

Composed in India or Central Asia, translated into Chinese in the 7th century

In a past life, Kṣitigarbha was a Brahmin woman who, out of filial piety, made offerings to a Buddha to save her mother from hell. She then vowed to save all beings from suffering. In another life, he was a king who vowed to delay his own enlightenment until all beings were liberated. The vow established Kṣitigarbha as the bodhisattva of the underworld, and the sutra became a popular text for funerary rituals.

The Descent into HellKṣitigarbha-praṇidhāna Sūtra

Tang dynasty (618-907 CE)

Kṣitigarbha descends into the hells to teach the suffering beings, using his staff to open the gates and his jewel to light the darkness. He comforts the damned and helps them generate merit for rebirth. His presence in hell became a central theme in Chinese and Japanese art and ritual.

Accounts II
The Legend of Mount JiuhuaLocal Chinese hagiography

Tang dynasty onward

A Korean monk named Kim Qiaoque (also known as Jijang) traveled to China and chose Mount Jiuhua as his retreat. He practiced austerities so severe that his face was said to have become black. After his death, his body was preserved, and he was identified as a manifestation of Kṣitigarbha. The mountain became a major pilgrimage site. Mount Jiuhua became the bodhimaṇḍa of Kṣitigarbha, and his cult flourished in China.

Jizō and the Children of Sai no KawaraJapanese folk legend

Kamakura period (1185-1333 CE) and later

Children who die before their parents are sent to the river of death (Sai no Kawara) in the underworld, where they must pile stones to build a tower for their parents' salvation. Demons (oni) scatter the stones, causing the children to suffer. Jizō appears to protect the children, hiding them in his sleeves and comforting them. This legend made Jizō the patron of children, especially those who die young, and led to the practice of placing bibs and caps on Jizō statues.

Jizō is the Buddhist equivalent of the Grim Reaper. — Jizō is not a death god; he is a compassionate savior who helps beings in the afterlife.

Jizō statues are always for aborted children. — While Jizō is associated with mizuko kuyō, his statues also honor deceased children, travelers, and all beings in the underworld.

Kṣitigarbha is the same as the Earth goddess. — Despite the name 'Earth Store,' Kṣitigarbha is not a goddess of the earth. He is a male bodhisattva whose name metaphorically refers to his role as a 'storehouse' of compassion.

The Kṣitigarbha Sūtra is a translation of a Sanskrit original. — The so-called 'Kṣitigarbha-praṇidhāna Sūtra' is widely considered by scholars to be a Chinese apocryphal text, composed in China, not a direct translation from Sanskrit.
