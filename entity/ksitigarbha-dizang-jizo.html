Who It Is

Kṣitigarbha is the bodhisattva who vows not to attain Buddhahood until all hell-beings are liberated — the savior of those suffering in the hell realms. His name means 'Earth Treasury' or 'Earth Womb,' referring to the hidden potential of the mind (like a seed in the earth). He is depicted as a simple monk with a shaven head, holding a staff in his right hand (to open the gates of hell) and a wish-fulfilling jewel in his left hand (to light the darkness and grant the wishes of sentient beings). Kṣitigarbha made a unique vow: to work specifically in the hell realms, where suffering is greatest and other bodhisattvas rarely go. He vowed, 'If I do not go to hell to help them, who else will?' In East Asia, Jizo (Japan) and Dizang (China) became especially beloved as the protector of children, travellers, pregnant women, and those who died prematurely. In Japan, Jizo statues are often found in cemeteries and along roadsides, frequently dressed in red bibs and caps placed by grieving parents who lost children. Jizo is said to hide children from demons in his sleeves and help them cross to the Pure Land. Kṣitigarbha's vow of 'Not until the hells are empty will I become a Buddha' represents the ultimate expression of bodhisattva compassion.

Origins

According to the Kṣitigarbha Sūtra, he was once a Brahmin woman who saved her mother from hell through her filial piety and vows. She vowed to liberate all hell-beings. In another life, he was a princess who made similar vows. He achieved immense merit over countless eons before becoming a bodhisattva.

Attested in Kṣitigarbha Sūtra / Mahayana Buddhist CanonEra c. 3rd-5th centuries CE

The Approach

In the shadow of the underworld, where the flames of hell lick at the edges of the living world, stands a bodhisattva who refuses to enter paradise until every last being is saved. Kṣitigarbha, known as Dizang in China and Jizō in Japan, is the guardian of the forgotten, the lost, and the damned—a figure whose vow of compassion echoes through millennia of Buddhist practice.

Content advisory. This dossier discusses themes of death, hell, suffering, and salvation as depicted in Buddhist cosmology. Some descriptions of hell realms and punishments may be disturbing to sensitive readers. The material is presented with scholarly context and is not intended to sensationalize or endorse any particular religious belief.

Etymology

The Sanskrit name Kṣitigarbha (क्षितिगर्भ) is composed of 'kṣiti' (earth) and 'garbha' (womb, embryo, or storehouse). It is often translated as 'Earth Treasury' or 'Earth Store'.

Cultural Ownership

Kṣitigarbha is a Mahāyāna Buddhist bodhisattva venerated across East Asia. While originating in India, his cult became particularly prominent in China, Japan, and Korea, where he developed distinct local characteristics.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars have suggested a possible connection with the Vedic deity Pṛthivī (Earth goddess), but this is speculative. In Japan, Jizō is sometimes syncretized with local deities such as Koyasu Kannon or with the folk figure of the protector of children, but these identifications are not canonical.

Description

Appearance

Kṣitigarbha is typically depicted as a monk with a shaven head, wearing simple monastic robes. He holds a staff (khakkhara) in his right hand, which has six rings that jingle to warn insects and small animals, and a wish-fulfilling jewel (cintāmaṇi) in his left hand. In East Asian art, he may appear as a serene figure with a halo, sometimes standing on a lotus. In Japan, Jizō is often shown as a childlike figure, sometimes with a baby or child in his arms, reflecting his role as protector of children.

Symbols & Iconography

The staff (khakkhara) symbolizes his role as a wandering monk and his ability to open the gates of hell. The six rings represent the six realms of rebirth (gods, demigods, humans, animals, hungry ghosts, hell beings). The wish-fulfilling jewel (cintāmaṇi) represents his power to grant wishes and illuminate the darkness of the underworld. In Chinese iconography, he may wear a crown with the five Dhyāni Buddhas, indicating his cosmic authority. In Japanese, he is often depicted with a red bib and a child's cap, placed on statues by devotees to protect deceased children.

Behaviour & Personality

Kṣitigarbha is characterized by his boundless compassion and unwavering commitment to his vow. He is said to work tirelessly in the darkest realms, comforting the suffering and guiding them toward liberation. He is patient, gentle, and approachable, often described as a father or mother figure. Unlike many bodhisattvas who reside in pure lands, he chooses to dwell in the hells and intermediate states, embodying the ideal of selfless service.

Domains & Habitats

Kṣitigarbha is primarily associated with the underworld (naraka), the realm of hungry ghosts (preta), and the intermediate state between death and rebirth (bardo). He is also considered a protector of travelers, children, and pregnant women. In Japanese folk religion, he is venerated at roadside shrines, often as a guardian of the dead. In Chinese Buddhism, he is the lord of the underworld, but he is also invoked for worldly benefits such as healing and longevity.

Version Differences

In Indian texts, Kṣitigarbha is a relatively minor figure, mentioned in lists of bodhisattvas. In China, he gained prominence as a savior of the damned, with a rich hagiography and a specific mountain (Mount Jiuhua) as his bodhimaṇḍa. In Japan, Jizō evolved into a popular folk deity, especially associated with children and travelers, with numerous localized forms such as 'Koyasu Jizō' (child-rearing Jizō) and 'Sai no Kawara Jizō' (Jizō of the river of death). In Korea, Jijang is venerated as a guardian of the underworld and is the subject of the 'Jijang Sipryun' (Ten Kings of the Underworld) cult.

Powers & Abilities

Vow to save all beings

Source Version Kṣitigarbha-praṇidhāna Sūtra

Description

Kṣitigarbha has vowed not to attain Buddhahood until the hells are emptied. This vow gives him immense merit and power, making him a powerful intercessor for beings in suffering.

Limitations

The vow is ongoing; he cannot achieve final liberation until all beings are saved, which is effectively infinite.

Contradictions

Some traditions hold that he will eventually become a Buddha, while others maintain he will remain a bodhisattva forever.

Ability to open the gates of hell

Source Version Kṣitigarbha-praṇidhāna Sūtra

Description

His staff can strike the ground and open the gates of hell, allowing him to enter and rescue beings.

Limitations

He uses this power only for liberation, not for personal gain.

Contradictions

None recorded.

Wish-fulfilling jewel (cintāmaṇi)

Source Version Various sutras and iconographic traditions

Description

The jewel he holds can grant wishes and emit light that illuminates the darkness of the underworld, providing comfort and guidance.

Limitations

The jewel is a symbol of his compassion, not a universal wish-granter; it works in accordance with karma.

Contradictions

Some texts describe the jewel as a physical relic, others as a metaphor.

Manifestation in all six realms

Source Version Kṣitigarbha-praṇidhāna Sūtra

Description

Kṣitigarbha can appear in any of the six realms of existence to teach and save beings, adapting his form to their needs.

Limitations

His manifestations are limited by the karma of beings; he cannot force liberation.

Contradictions

None recorded.

Protection of children and travelers

Source Version Japanese folk religion

Description

In Japanese folk belief, Jizō is invoked to protect children, especially those who die young, and to guide travelers safely.

Limitations

This is a folk development not explicitly found in canonical sutras.

Contradictions

Some orthodox Buddhist teachers downplay these folk attributes as superstitious.

Stories & Myths

The Vow of the Earth Store Bodhisattva

Source Kṣitigarbha-praṇidhāna SūtraCulture Mahāyāna BuddhismPeriod Composed in India or Central Asia, translated into Chinese in the 7th century

Summary

In a past life, Kṣitigarbha was a Brahmin woman who, out of filial piety, made offerings to a Buddha to save her mother from hell. She then vowed to save all beings from suffering. In another life, he was a king who vowed to delay his own enlightenment until all beings were liberated.

Participants

Kṣitigarbha

Buddha Śākyamuni

Various past-life figures

Consequences

The vow established Kṣitigarbha as the bodhisattva of the underworld, and the sutra became a popular text for funerary rituals.

Variants

The sutra contains multiple past-life stories, each emphasizing a different aspect of his compassion.

The Descent into Hell

Source Kṣitigarbha-praṇidhāna SūtraCulture Chinese BuddhismPeriod Tang dynasty (618-907 CE)

Summary

Kṣitigarbha descends into the hells to teach the suffering beings, using his staff to open the gates and his jewel to light the darkness. He comforts the damned and helps them generate merit for rebirth.

Participants

Kṣitigarbha

Hell beings

Yama, the king of the underworld

Consequences

His presence in hell became a central theme in Chinese and Japanese art and ritual.

Variants

In some versions, he is depicted as a judge who mitigates the punishments of the dead.

The Legend of Mount Jiuhua

Source Local Chinese hagiographyCulture Chinese BuddhismPeriod Tang dynasty onward

Summary

A Korean monk named Kim Qiaoque (also known as Jijang) traveled to China and chose Mount Jiuhua as his retreat. He practiced austerities so severe that his face was said to have become black. After his death, his body was preserved, and he was identified as a manifestation of Kṣitigarbha. The mountain became a major pilgrimage site.

Participants

Kim Qiaoque

Local people

Monks

Consequences

Mount Jiuhua became the bodhimaṇḍa of Kṣitigarbha, and his cult flourished in China.

Variants

Some accounts say Kim Qiaoque was a prince of Silla, while others say he was a commoner.

Jizō and the Children of Sai no Kawara

Source Japanese folk legendCulture Japanese BuddhismPeriod Kamakura period (1185-1333 CE) and later

Summary

Children who die before their parents are sent to the river of death (Sai no Kawara) in the underworld, where they must pile stones to build a tower for their parents' salvation. Demons (oni) scatter the stones, causing the children to suffer. Jizō appears to protect the children, hiding them in his sleeves and comforting them.

Participants

Jizō

Children

Oni (demons)

Sai no Kawara

Consequences

This legend made Jizō the patron of children, especially those who die young, and led to the practice of placing bibs and caps on Jizō statues.

Variants

In some versions, Jizō himself builds the towers for the children.

Relationships

Buddha Śākyamuni

Type student/teacherSource Kṣitigarbha-praṇidhāna Sūtra

Description

Kṣitigarbha is a disciple of the Buddha and receives his teachings. The Buddha entrusts him with the care of beings in the period after his own nirvāṇa.

Version Note

In the sutra, the Buddha praises Kṣitigarbha's vow and asks him to protect beings until Maitreya arrives.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Maitreya

Type successorSource Kṣitigarbha-praṇidhāna Sūtra

Description

Kṣitigarbha is to remain in the world until Maitreya becomes the next Buddha, after which he can finally rest.

Version Note

This emphasizes the temporal scope of his vow.

Conflicting Accounts

Some traditions say he will become a Buddha before Maitreya, while others say he will never become a Buddha.

Yama (Yanluo Wang)

Type collaborator/rivalSource Chinese Buddhist texts

Description

Yama is the king of the underworld who judges the dead. Kṣitigarbha works within Yama's realm to save beings, often interceding on their behalf.

Version Note

In some accounts, Kṣitigarbha is subordinate to Yama; in others, he overrides Yama's judgments.

Conflicting Accounts

Some depictions show Kṣitigarbha as one of the Ten Kings of the underworld, while others place him above them.

Avalokiteśvara

Type fellow bodhisattvaSource Mahāyāna pantheon

Description

Avalokiteśvara and Kṣitigarbha are both bodhisattvas of compassion, but Avalokiteśvara focuses on saving beings from suffering in general, while Kṣitigarbha specializes in the underworld and intermediate states.

Version Note

They are often depicted together in temple art.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Manjuśrī

Type fellow bodhisattvaSource Mahāyāna pantheon

Description

Manjuśrī is the bodhisattva of wisdom, and Kṣitigarbha is the bodhisattva of compassion for the suffering. They represent complementary aspects of enlightenment.

Version Note

Both are often included in the 'Three Saints' or 'Four Great Bodhisattvas' groupings.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Pregnant women and children

Type protectorSource Japanese folk religion

Description

In Japanese folk belief, Jizō protects pregnant women, children, and travelers. He is especially invoked for children who die young, as they are considered to have unresolved karma.

Version Note

This role is not explicit in early Indian texts but developed in Japan.

Conflicting Accounts

Some orthodox Buddhist teachers discourage these folk practices as uncanonical.

Hell imagery

Source Various sutras and commentaries

Description

Kṣitigarbha's association with hell involves graphic descriptions of tortures and punishments in Buddhist texts, which can be disturbing. These are intended to motivate moral behavior and compassion.

Scholarly Context

Hell imagery in Buddhism serves as a pedagogical tool, not a literal description of an eternal afterlife.

Children's suffering

Source Japanese folk religion

Description

The legend of Sai no Kawara depicts dead children suffering in the underworld, which reflects historical anxieties about infant mortality and the fate of unbaptized or untended children. This has led to practices like mizuko kuyō (memorial services for aborted or miscarried children).

Scholarly Context

Scholars note that the practice of mizuko kuyō is a modern development, and the association with Jizō is complex, involving both comfort and potential guilt.

Colonial and cultural appropriation

Source Contemporary observations

Description

In some Western contexts, Jizō statues have been adopted as garden ornaments without understanding their religious significance, leading to cultural insensitivity.

Scholarly Context

This is a form of cultural appropriation that can be offensive to practitioners.

Contradictions & Disputes

Kṣitigarbha's eventual enlightenment

Position A

Kṣitigarbha will eventually become a Buddha after saving all beings.

Position A — evidence

Some commentarial traditions suggest that his vow is finite and will be fulfilled.

Position B

Kṣitigarbha will never become a Buddha because he has vowed to remain a bodhisattva forever.

Position B — evidence

Other traditions emphasize the infinite nature of his vow, making his enlightenment impossible.

Scholarly Consensus

The majority of Mahāyāna texts treat his vow as ongoing, and his status as a bodhisattva is emphasized over his future Buddhahood.

Role in the underworld

Position A

Kṣitigarbha is a judge of the dead, similar to Yama.

Position A — evidence

Some Chinese texts depict him as one of the Ten Kings of the underworld.

Position B

Kṣitigarbha is a savior who rescues beings from hell, not a judge.

Position B — evidence

The Kṣitigarbha Sūtra emphasizes his role as a rescuer, not a judge.

Scholarly Consensus

The savior role is more prominent in canonical texts; the judge role is a later folk development.

Origin of the cult

Position A

Kṣitigarbha originated in India and was later adopted in East Asia.

Position A — evidence

Mentions in early Mahāyāna sutras and Gandhāran art suggest an Indian origin.

Position B

Kṣitigarbha is a purely East Asian creation with no Indian precedent.

Position B — evidence

No Indian texts solely dedicated to Kṣitigarbha have been found, and his iconography as a monk is atypical for Indian bodhisattvas.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars accept an Indian origin, but the lack of dedicated Indian texts leaves room for debate.
