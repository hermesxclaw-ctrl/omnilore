Form

No direct physical description of Auxesia survives in ancient texts. She is presumed to have been depicted as a young, matronly woman, similar to other agricultural deities. The cult statues (xoana) were made of olive wood, as detailed by Herodotus. A key feature was the clothing: the statues were dressed in a peplos made of natural, undyed wool, a detail that became a central part of the cult's mythology and ritual.

Powers

Bestowal of Agricultural Growth As a personification of growth, Auxesia's primary power is to ensure the fertility of the land, promoting the growth of crops and the increase of livestock. This power is directly tied to the proper observance of her cult. Attested in the general nature of her cult and the myth of the stolen statues (Herodotus). Her power is conditional on proper worship. When the Athenians stole the statues, she withdrew her favor, causing famine and infertility in Aegina. None directly, but her power is more inferred from her nature and cult than explicitly described in any single source.

Withholding of Growth (Punishment) Auxesia can also withhold her blessings, causing crop failure, famine, and general infertility. This punitive aspect is demonstrated in the Aeginetan myth when she brought a famine upon the island after the statues were stolen. Attested in Herodotus's account of the Aeginetan famine. This power is a direct consequence of neglect or sacrilege, acting as a divine enforcement mechanism for her cult. None, though the specific mechanism of the famine (direct divine action vs. natural consequence) is not detailed.

Accounts

The Athenians, following an oracle, sent for the statues of Auxesia and Hegemone from Epidaurus. When the Epidaurians refused, the Athenians stole them. A plague and famine struck Aegina, which had been entrusted with the statues by Epidaurus. The Aeginetans, following an oracle, made new statues and established a cult to appease the goddesses. The Athenians, in a subsequent attempt to seize the statues, were met with supernatural resistance: the statues fell to their knees, a sign of divine anger. The Aeginetans then cut the cables of the Athenian ship, leaving the Athenians stranded and the statues in Aegina.

In Athens, Auxesia (or Auxo) was worshipped alongside Hegemone as one of the two Horae, goddesses of the seasons. They were integral to the state religion, with a sanctuary near the Acropolis. The ephebes, young men undergoing military training, participated in a procession to their sanctuary, integrating the goddesses into the civic and educational life of the city.

Content Notes

The myth explicitly describes a plague and famine sent by the goddesses as punishment for the theft of their statues. This results in widespread suffering and death, serving as a grim reminder of the consequences of impiety.

This is a common motif in Greek religion, where deities are quick to punish hubris and sacrilege. The famine serves as a divine sanction for the political order, reinforcing the power of the cult.

Herodotus, Histories 5.82-88

The myth of the statue theft is deeply intertwined with the historical rivalry between Athens and Aegina. It is used to explain and justify the enmity between the two states, framing it as a divinely sanctioned conflict.

Herodotus himself notes the political motivations behind the myth, showing how religious narratives are often shaped by political realities. The story serves as a charter myth for the Aeginetan identity and its opposition to Athens.

Herodotus, Histories 5.82-88

Scholarly Debates
Identity as a Hora vs. a Separate Goddess
Auxesia is a distinct goddess of growth, worshipped primarily in Aegina and Epidaurus, with her own unique mythology. ⇌ Auxesia is the same as Auxo, one of the two Horae worshipped in Athens, and should be understood primarily as a seasonal deity.
Evidence for A: Herodotus's account of the Aeginetan cult does not mention the Horae, focusing instead on her role as a local protector.
Evidence for B: Pausanias and inscriptions from Athens clearly associate Auxo with the Horae and the state cult.
The Origin of the Cult Statues
The statues were made by the legendary craftsman Daedalus. ⇌ The statues were made by unknown, local craftsmen.
Evidence for A: This claim is mentioned by some later sources, who attribute the xoana to Daedalus to enhance their prestige.
Evidence for B: Pausanias explicitly states that the statues were not the work of Daedalus, attributing them to a local tradition.
What We Don't Know

The exact nature of the pre-Athenian cult of Auxesia on Aegina and Epidaurus is poorly understood. The precise location of her sanctuary on Aegina is unknown. The relationship between her cult and that of Aphaea on Aegina is unclear. The full extent of her powers and the specific rituals of her worship, beyond the detail of the undyed wool, are not well-documented. A full survey of modern scholarship on her cult is needed.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Auxesia as the ancient Greek equivalent of a local patron saint or a guardian spirit of the harvest — but with a much more demanding temperament. She's not a distant Olympian; she's the goddess of your town's crops, your city's prosperity. Her power is directly tied to the land she protects, and if you wrong her or her people, she doesn't just get angry — she makes the earth itself turn against you. She's the divine embodiment of 'you reap what you sow,' both literally and figuratively.
