Who It Is

In the smoke of the North Sea, a Dunkirk captain who could not legally hold a commission because he was not noble broke through a Dutch blockade with six small vessels, burned his enemies' prizes rather than lose them, and once escaped an English prison in a rowboat — then saved Paris from starvation by stealing an entire Dutch grain fleet. That was Jan Baert, whom Versailles would rename Jean Bart.

Attested: blunt, uncultivated (in court terms), courageous to recklessness, skilled small-squadron tactician, loyal to Dunkirk crew and to Louis XIV as patron. Forbin and Saint-Simon portray him as ill-at-ease at Versailles, speaking Flemish-accented French, preferring sea to court. Louis XIV reportedly valued his effectiveness over birth. Modern historiography (Villiers, Vergé-Franceschi) stresses professionalism: mastery of North Sea tides, sandbanks, and convoy warfare rather than mere daring. Powers
Commerce raiding and prize capture (guerre de course)

Command of fast privateer frigates and later ships of the line to intercept enemy merchantmen, force surrender, and bring prizes to Dunkirk. Credited in tradition with 386 prizes taken and many more sunk or burned.

Limit: Dependent on Letters of Marque and royal tolerance; prizes subject to adjudication and sharing with armateurs; vulnerable to superior English/Dutch squadrons; not piracy — state-sanctioned.

Blockade running and coastal navigation

Exploitation of Dunkirk Banks, tides, and shallow channels to slip through Anglo-Dutch blockades, notably with grain convoys. Demonstrated 1670s and 1691 when he slipped blockade to raid allied commerce.

Limit: Required local pilots, favorable winds, and small squadrons; larger ships like Le Glorieux could not always use shallow routes.

Small-squadron tactics and boarding

Aggressive close action: breaking through blockading lines with 6 vessels, forcing larger Dutch merchantmen aground near Faro (1693), and capturing escorted convoys by boarding.

Limit: Inferior in formal line-of-battle against first-rate fleets; relied on surprise and coastal geography.

Convoy escort and grain protection

Most strategically significant: 1694 rescue of French grain convoy from Dutch capture at Texel, preventing famine in Paris during Nine Years' War food crisis.

Limit: One-off strategic success; could not solve systemic French supply shortages.

Leadership and crew loyalty

Ability to recruit and retain Dunkirk Flemish crews, including escape from Plymouth with 20 sailors in a rowboat, indicating personal authority beyond rank.

Limit: Authority initially informal due to non-noble birth; regular commission only from 1679, full nobility 1694.

Form & Signs

The only physical datum is Dr. Louis Lemaire's 1928 osteological estimate of 1.90 m tall, unusually tall for the period, based on bones attributed to Bart in Saint-Éloi. Accounts I
Apprenticeship under Michiel de Ruyter and defection to France (c.1666-1672)Dutch muster rolls (fragmentary) and French Marine enlistment records; Henri Malo and Patrick Villiers syntheses

1666-1672 (Second and Third Anglo-Dutch Wars)

Born into a Dunkirk family that had served Spain, Bart as a teenager served in the Dutch navy under Admiral Michiel de Ruyter, the dominant naval tactician of the age. When Louis XIV's France invaded the United Provinces in 1672, Dunkirk — now French — recalled its sailors. Bart entered French service not as an officer (barred by birth) but as captain of a Dunkirk privateer (corsaire) with an irregular commission. Gave Bart Dutch naval doctrine and North Sea expertise that later made him a terror to Dutch commerce; established pattern of Dunkirk sailors switching sovereigns as the port changed hands.

Capture and rowboat escape from Plymouth with Claude de Forbin (1689)Forbin, Mémoires (1730); English Admiralty prisoner records, Plymouth

1689

At the war's outset, Bart and fellow Dunkirk captain Claude de Forbin were captured by an English warship and taken to Plymouth as prisoners of war. After three days, they overpowered or evaded guards and escaped with 20 other French sailors in a small rowboat, crossing the Channel to Brittany. The feat became central to Bart's legend of daring. Immediate celebrity at Versailles; Louis XIV gave Bart special Mediterranean mission. Cemented lifelong association with Forbin, later vice-admiral and memoirist.

Slipping the blockade and Scottish raid (1691)SHD Marine B4 14; Scottish local records and French dispatches

1691

Bart slipped through the Anglo-Dutch blockade of Dunkirk, then terrorized the allied merchant fleet in the North Sea. He raided the Scottish coast, burning a castle and four villages — a typical commerce-war diversion to force enemy to detach warships to coastal defense. Demonstrated Dunkirk's continued threat despite blockade; provoked English redeployment. Added to his reputation for ruthlessness in British sources.

Accounts II
Lagos, the Smyrna convoy and the Faro action (1693)Tourville's fleet dispatches; SHD Marine B4 16; Dutch Admiralty reports

June 1693

Bart commanded the 62-gun Le Glorieux in Marshal de Tourville's fleet that crushed the Anglo-Dutch Smyrna convoy at the Battle of Lagos (off Portugal). After the victory, Tourville detached Bart. Near Faro, Bart intercepted six richly laden Dutch merchantmen of 24-50 guns, forced them aground, and burned them when he could not bring them off. Showed transition from privateer to fleet officer; secured prize wealth but also demonstrated limits of prize-taking under fleet discipline.

Battle of Texel and the grain convoy — saving Paris (29 June 1694)Letters patent 4 Aug 1694; intendants' famine correspondence; Dutch States General records; SHD Marine B4 17

1694

France faced famine after poor harvests. A huge convoy of 100+ Norwegian/Dutch grain ships chartered to feed France was captured by a Dutch squadron off Texel. Bart, with a squadron including Le Glorieux and smaller vessels, attacked the Dutch escort, recaptured the grain ships, and escorted them to Dunkirk and onward to Paris. The action averted starvation in the capital. Greatest strategic success; Louis XIV ennobled Bart on 4 August 1694 with peerage, making him Chevalier Bart. Became national hero and propaganda symbol of royal protection against famine.

Battle of Dogger Bank and the end of active service (17 June 1696)SHD Marine B4 18; Dutch and English gazettes

1696-1697

Bart struck again at Dogger Bank, intercepting a Dutch convoy of 112 merchantmen escorted by five warships. He captured or destroyed many, though the Dutch escort fought hard. It was his last major action; the Peace of Ryswick (30 Oct 1697) ended the Nine Years' War and his corsair warfare. He died of pleurisy in Dunkirk on 27 April 1702, aged 51, and was buried in Saint-Éloi. Confirmed reputation as most successful French commerce raider of the war; peace removed legal basis for privateering. Death in 1702 prevented participation in War of Spanish Succession.

Jean Bart was a pirate — He was a state-sanctioned privateer (corsaire) and later commissioned naval officer operating under Letters of Marque and royal orders. Piracy is unauthorized; Bart's prizes were adjudicated in prize courts.

He was always a French naval officer — Barred from officer corps until 1679 due to non-noble birth, he held an irregular corsair commission. Only after proven success was he made lieutenant, then captain, then ennobled in 1694.

The Azur Lane and One Piece characters accurately depict Jean Bart — Both are inspired-by homages using only name and maritime association. One Piece's Jean Bart is a former slave pirate; Azur Lane's is an anthropomorphized battleship as a woman. Neither reflects biography, appearance, or personality.

Jean Bart single-handedly saved France from famine — He recaptured a large Baltic grain convoy at Texel (1694) that relieved Paris, but France still suffered severe famine mortality in 1693-1694, especially in rural areas. It was a major but partial relief.

He was monolingual French — Almost certainly native Dutch/Flemish speaker who used French professionally. Signed 'Jan Baert' in 1689 and spoke French with Flemish accent noted by contemporaries.
