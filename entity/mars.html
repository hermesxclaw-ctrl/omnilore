Who It Is

Before he was a planet, before he was a god of war, Mars was a farmer's god. He was the one who kept the wolf from the flock and the blight from the field, a protector of boundaries and a guardian of the hearth. Then Rome grew, and it needed a god of conquest, so Mars grew with it, becoming the divine father of the most powerful military machine the ancient world had ever seen. He is the god who fathered Rome through an act of divine violence, and he is the god who watched over its fields and its legions with the same stern, paternal eye. He is not the chaotic, blood-drunk Ares of the Greeks; he is the disciplined, relentless, and deeply Roman god of the march.

Mars is the embodiment of Roman martial virtue: discipline, courage, and unwavering protection. He is not a berserker; he is the god of the organized, strategic, and divinely sanctioned war that expands and protects the Roman state. He is also a paternal figure, guarding the fields and the family, and a chthonic force, warding off evil from the boundaries of the city.

Powers
Divine Patronage of War

Mars grants victory to the Roman legions. His favor is sought before, during, and after battles. He is the divine commander-in-chief.

Limit: His favor is not guaranteed; it must be earned through proper ritual and virtuous conduct. He is not a god of personal combat but of the collective army.

Agricultural Fertility

Mars protects fields, herds, and harvests from blight, pests, and evil spirits. The suovetaurilia sacrifice is performed to purify the land and secure his blessing.

Limit: This power is tied to the land and its boundaries, not to personal wealth.

Paternal Protection (Pater Patriae)

As the father of Romulus and Remus, Mars is the divine ancestor of the Roman people. He protects the state as a father protects his family.

Limit: His protection is for the community as a whole, not for individual citizens.

Chthonic Boundary Guardian

As Mars Gradivus, he marches before the army, but he also guards the boundaries of the city. The Salian priests performed his ritual dances to activate his protective power at the city's borders.

Limit: His protective power is strongest at the liminal spaces of the Roman world.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In Roman art, Mars is depicted as a mature, bearded man in full military regalia: a crested helmet, a bronze cuirass, a spear, and a shield. Unlike the often youthful and naked Ares, Roman Mars is a dignified, armored commander. He is often shown with the wolf and the woodpecker, his sacred animals. Later, imperial propaganda often showed him as a heroic, semi-nude figure in the Greek manner, but the core Roman image was that of a disciplined sol

Symbols — The spear, the shield, the crested helmet, the wolf, the woodpecker (picus), the horse, the sacrificial pig/sheep/bull (suovetaurilia), and the planet named after him. The Campus Martius (Field of Mars) was a vast area outside the sacred boundary of the city used for military training and political assemblies.

Domains — War (specifically the Roman military), agriculture (the protection of crops and livestock), the fatherhood of the Roman people, and the boundary of the city (as Mars Gradivus, the one who marches before the army). He is also a god of the calendar, giving his name to March (the first month of the old Roman year) and to the planet Mars.

Accounts I
The Birth of Romulus and RemusLivy, Ab Urbe Condita; Plutarch, Life of Romulus; Ovid, Fasti

Mythic founding era

Mars, in his aspect as a virile, aggressive deity, impregnated the Vestal Virgin Rhea Silvia, who was sworn to chastity. When her uncle, King Amulius, discovered her pregnancy, he imprisoned her and ordered her twins to be thrown into the Tiber River. The basket carrying the twins washed ashore at the foot of the Palatine Hill, where a she-wolf (lupa) suckled them. The twins were later found and raised by a shepherd, Faustulus. They grew to be natural leaders, and after discovering their true lineage, they overthrew Amulius and founded a new city on the site of their salvation. The founding of Rome. Mars becomes the divine father of the Roman race, and his martial character is seen as the source of Roman destiny and power.

The Rape of the Sabine WomenLivy, Ab Urbe Condita; Plutarch, Life of Romulus

Mythic founding era

To provide wives for the newly founded city of Rome, Romulus invited the neighboring Sabines to a festival in honor of Consus. At a pre-arranged signal, the Roman men seized the unmarried Sabine women. The Sabine king, Titus Tatius, declared war. The women, now Roman wives and mothers, intervened and begged their fathers and husbands to make peace, leading to the unification of the Romans and Sabines. The growth of Rome's population and the integration of the Sabine people into the Roman state. This story is often cited as a foundational myth of Roman militarism and patriarchy.

Accounts II
The Vengeance of Mars UltorAugustus, Res Gestae; Ovid, Fasti; Suetonius, Life of Augustus

1st century BCE - 1st century CE

After the assassination of Julius Caesar, his adopted son Octavian (later Augustus) vowed to build a temple to Mars the Avenger if he won the ensuing civil war. He defeated the assassins at the Battle of Philippi (42 BCE) and later fulfilled his vow, building a magnificent temple in the new Forum of Augustus. The temple housed the standards lost to the Parthians at the Battle of Carrhae, which Augustus had diplomatically recovered. Mars Ultor became the symbol of a new, secure, and divinely sanctioned imperial order. The temple became the center of imperial military propaganda and a site for state rituals. Mars was transformed from a god of the Republic to a god of the Imperial family.

The Rites of the SaliiOvid, Fasti; Livy, Ab Urbe Condita; Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Roman Antiquities

Archaic and Republican

Every March, the Salian priests, dressed in ancient armor and carrying the sacred shields (ancilia), performed a ritual dance through the streets of Rome, beating their weapons and singing the Carmen Saliare. The dance was meant to wake the god and activate his protective power for the coming campaign season. It was a public spectacle that linked the city's well-being to Mars's martial energy. The ritual marked the start of the military year and reinforced the sacred bond between Mars and the Roman state. The shields were said to have been a divine gift, a shield that fell from the sky, with 11 copies made to protect the original from theft.

Mars is just the Roman name for the Greek god Ares. — Mars is a native Italic god with a distinct, older character. He was a protector of agriculture and the community before he was a god of war. The Romans equated him with Ares to fit him into the Greek mythological framework, but his religious cult remained uniquely Roman.

Mars was a bloodthirsty, chaotic god of war like Ares. — Mars was a disciplined, paternal god who protected Rome and its lands. He was a divine commander, not a berserker. His war was a righteous, state-sanctioned war, not personal violence.

The planet Mars was named after the god because of its red color, representing blood. — This is true. The Romans named the red planet after their god of war. However, the association with blood is a later, more 'Greek' interpretation. The archaic Roman Mars was also a god of agriculture, and the red planet may have been associated with the redemptive power of spring and the return of life.

Mars was a minor god compared to Jupiter. — While Jupiter was the king of the gods, Mars was a central and vital deity for the Roman state. He was the father of the Roman people and the divine patron of their military. His importance was second only to Jupiter, and arguably more immediate in the daily life of the state.
