Mars, father of the Roman people, who watches over the walls of Romulus's city and guides our legions to victory.

Who They Are

Mars was the Roman god of war and, uniquely among Indo-European war gods, also a deity of agriculture and fertility. Unlike the Greek Ares — a figure of bloodlust and chaotic violence whom the Greeks considered brutish and untrustworthy — Mars was revered as a dignified, fatherly protector of the Roman people. He was second only to Jupiter in the Roman pantheon and was believed to be the divine father of Romulus and Remus through his union with the Vestal Virgin Rhea Silvia, making him the literal progenitor of Rome.

In artistic representation, Mars embodied the idealized Roman warrior: a tall, muscular man in the prime of life, typically depicted nude or wearing only a helmet and military cloak (paludamentum) to display his martial physique. He carried a spear, sword, and shield, and was frequently shown with his sacred animals — the wolf (commemorating the she-wolf that nursed Romulus and Remus) and the woodpecker (picus), which also served as oracular birds. The finest surviving portrait is the Mars Ultor ('Mars the Avenger') from the Forum of Augustus, which shows him as a mature, bearded warrior-king, majestic rather than fearsome.

Mars's role in Roman religion was far more complex than simple war patronage. His month, March (Martius), opened the Roman year and the agricultural season. His priesthood — the Salii ('Leapers'), twelve young patricians who performed armed dances through the streets — sang the Carmen Saliare, an archaic hymn invoking Mars as the protector of crops and fields. The sacrifice of the October Horse (Equus October), in which the right-hand horse of the winning chariot team was killed and its tail brought to the Regia, was an ancient fertility ritual as much as a military one. His sacred spears (hastae Martiae), kept in the Regia, were believed to tremble of their own accord when war or disaster threatened.

As Mars Ultor, he was the divine guarantor of vengeance — Augustus built a magnificent temple to this aspect of Mars in his new forum, fulfilling a vow made before the Battle of Philippi where he avenged Julius Caesar's assassination. As Mars Gradivus ('He Who Marches'), he was the god of the advancing army. His Campus Martius (Field of Mars) was the training ground for Roman soldiers and the site of the centuriate assembly, making him the patron of both military might and republican governance. Mars's cult was served by the Flamen Martialis and the college of Salii, and his festivals — especially the Feriae Marti — were among the oldest and most important in the Roman calendar.

His mythological biography, fleshed out by Greek influence, included his affair with Venus (the Roman adaptation of the Ares-Aphrodite myth), producing the children Harmonia/Concordia, and the terrifying twins Phobos and Deimos (Fear and Terror) who accompanied him into battle.

Form

In Roman art, Mars is depicted as a mature, bearded man in full military regalia: a crested helmet, a bronze cuirass, a spear, and a shield. Unlike the often youthful and naked Ares, Roman Mars is a dignified, armored commander. He is often shown with the wolf and the woodpecker, his sacred animals. Later, imperial propaganda often showed him as a heroic, semi-nude figure in the Greek manner, but the core Roman image was that of a disciplined soldier.

Powers

Divine Patronage of War Mars grants victory to the Roman legions. His favor is sought before, during, and after battles. He is the divine commander-in-chief. All versions His favor is not guaranteed; it must be earned through proper ritual and virtuous conduct. He is not a god of personal combat but of the collective army. None, this is his core power.

Agricultural Fertility Mars protects fields, herds, and harvests from blight, pests, and evil spirits. The suovetaurilia sacrifice is performed to purify the land and secure his blessing. Archaic Italic version This power is tied to the land and its boundaries, not to personal wealth. This agrarian aspect is often overshadowed by his military role in modern reception, but it was a primary function for early Romans.

Paternal Protection (Pater Patriae) As the father of Romulus and Remus, Mars is the divine ancestor of the Roman people. He protects the state as a father protects his family. Roman state religion His protection is for the community as a whole, not for individual citizens. This paternal role is a distinctly Roman addition, absent from the Greek Ares.

Chthonic Boundary Guardian As Mars Gradivus, he marches before the army, but he also guards the boundaries of the city. The Salian priests performed his ritual dances to activate his protective power at the city's borders. Archaic Roman His protective power is strongest at the liminal spaces of the Roman world. This role is often confused with Terminus, the god of boundaries, but Mars's role is more active, a martial patrol against intruders, both human and spiritual.

Behavior & Habitat

Mars is the embodiment of Roman martial virtue: discipline, courage, and unwavering protection. He is not a berserker; he is the god of the organized, strategic, and divinely sanctioned war that expands and protects the Roman state. He is also a paternal figure, guarding the fields and the family, and a chthonic force, warding off evil from the boundaries of the city.

Accounts

Mars, in his aspect as a virile, aggressive deity, impregnated the Vestal Virgin Rhea Silvia, who was sworn to chastity. When her uncle, King Amulius, discovered her pregnancy, he imprisoned her and ordered her twins to be thrown into the Tiber River. The basket carrying the twins washed ashore at the foot of the Palatine Hill, where a she-wolf (lupa) suckled them. The twins were later found and raised by a shepherd, Faustulus. They grew to be natural leaders, and after discovering their true lineage, they overthrew Amulius and founded a new city on the site of their salvation.

To provide wives for the newly founded city of Rome, Romulus invited the neighboring Sabines to a festival in honor of Consus. At a pre-arranged signal, the Roman men seized the unmarried Sabine women. The Sabine king, Titus Tatius, declared war. The women, now Roman wives and mothers, intervened and begged their fathers and husbands to make peace, leading to the unification of the Romans and Sabines.

After the assassination of Julius Caesar, his adopted son Octavian (later Augustus) vowed to build a temple to Mars the Avenger if he won the ensuing civil war. He defeated the assassins at the Battle of Philippi (42 BCE) and later fulfilled his vow, building a magnificent temple in the new Forum of Augustus. The temple housed the standards lost to the Parthians at the Battle of Carrhae, which Augustus had diplomatically recovered. Mars Ultor became the symbol of a new, secure, and divinely sanctioned imperial order.

Every March, the Salian priests, dressed in ancient armor and carrying the sacred shields (ancilia), performed a ritual dance through the streets of Rome, beating their weapons and singing the Carmen Saliare. The dance was meant to wake the god and activate his protective power for the coming campaign season. It was a public spectacle that linked the city's well-being to Mars's martial energy.

Content Notes

Mars's rape of a Vestal Virgin is a foundational act of violence. It is not presented as a crime but as a divine prerogative, an assertion of power that creates the Roman people. The victim is punished for the god's actions.

This myth has been analyzed by feminist scholars as a justification for patriarchal power and the subordination of women's bodies to the state. The Vestal's punishment for a crime she did not willingly commit highlights the double standard of Roman morality.

Livy, Ab Urbe Condita; Plutarch, Life of Romulus

Romulus kills his twin brother Remus after Remus jumps over the sacred boundary wall of the new city. This act of murder is the price of Rome's foundation, establishing a pattern of violence and civil conflict that would haunt the Republic and Empire.

The killing of Remus is a central problem in Roman myth. It was seen as a necessary act to establish a single, unified power, but it also foreshadows the civil wars that ended the Republic. Some scholars see it as a mythologization of Rome's violent origins.

Livy, Ab Urbe Condita

Mars was the divine patron of the Roman legions. His cult was central to the military machinery that conquered the Mediterranean world and beyond. The Temple of Mars Ultor was a monument to Augustus's military victory and his 'avenging' of Caesar, a political act of great violence.

The worship of Mars was not a personal devotion but a state-sponsored ideology of conquest. The god's favor was sought for the destruction of enemies and the expansion of Roman power, which involved immense suffering for conquered peoples.

What We Don't Know

The exact etymology of the name 'Mars' remains uncertain. The full text of the Carmen Saliare is lost. The precise nature of the archaic, pre-Greek-influenced Mars is reconstructed from fragments and later sources. The relationship between Mars and the chthonic god of the Lapis Niger is not fully understood. The full extent of his cult in the provinces is not documented here.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Mars not as a reckless soldier, but as a stern, armored general and a protective father. He is the god of the Roman army, but also the god of the Roman farm. He is the divine force that both defends the homeland and expands its borders. He is the masculine principle of order, discipline, and protection, the power that makes civilization possible by holding the wild at bay.
