Who They Are

Chía is the Muisca moon goddess, the most powerful and most complex female deity in the Muisca pantheon. She is both a benevolent giver of light and life and a dangerous force of chaos and flooding. Her waxing and waning governs the Muisca calendar, and her temple at the town of Chía (named after her) was one of the most important religious centers in the Muisca Confederation. She is the wife of Sué (the sun god) and the mother of the Muisca people under Bachué.

The Approach

In the high Andean world of the Muisca, where the sun and moon were woven into the fabric of daily life, Chía was the goddess of the Moon—a luminous presence whose cycles guided the rhythms of night, fertility, and time. Her temple, perched on a hill near what is now Bogotá, was a place of pilgrimage, where the faithful sought her favor and the caciques paid homage. Yet, like so many deities of the Americas, her story was nearly lost to conquest and time, surviving only in fragments of colonial chronicles and the enduring memory of a people.

Content advisory. The account of Chía includes references to human sacrifice, which was part of Muisca religious practice. This material is presented with historical context and is not intended to sensationalize or endorse such practices.

Etymology

The name 'Chía' is derived from the Muisca language (Chibcha), and is also the name of the town and sacred site associated with the goddess. The exact linguistic root is not fully documented, but it is often linked to the moon.

Cultural Ownership

Muisca (Chibcha) culture, indigenous people of the Altiplano Cundiboyacense in present-day Colombia.

Disputed Identifications

Some colonial sources and modern scholars have equated Chía with the goddess of the moon in other Chibchan groups, but this is not universally accepted. There is also debate about whether Chía was a distinct deity or a manifestation of a broader lunar deity.

Description

Appearance

No specific physical description of Chía survives in colonial sources. As a moon goddess, she was likely associated with the moon itself, and possibly represented in iconography as a lunar disc or a female figure with lunar symbols. Reconstructions based on Muisca art suggest anthropomorphic representations of deities, but none have been securely identified as Chía.

Symbols & Iconography

The moon is the primary symbol. Colonial sources mention that Chía's temple was associated with a sacred hill, and that the moon's phases were linked to her actions. Some modern interpretations associate her with the crescent moon and with water, as the moon was thought to influence rivers and lakes.

Behaviour & Personality

Chía is described in myth as the wife of Sué, the Sun god, but their relationship was troubled. She is said to have been responsible for the creation of the night and the stars, and her movements across the sky were linked to the lunar cycle. She was also associated with fertility and the protection of women, though specific myths are scarce.

Domains & Habitats

Chía's primary domain was the moon and the night sky. She was also connected to water and fertility, as the moon was believed to influence the tides and the growth of crops. Her main cult center was the town of Chía, near modern Bogotá, where her temple stood on a hill.

Version Differences

Colonial sources vary in the degree of detail they provide. Simón emphasizes the temple and rituals, while Piedrahita provides a more elaborate myth. Modern retellings often blend these sources and add interpretations that may not be explicitly attested. There is no evidence for a separate 'Chía' in other Chibchan groups, but some scholars have drawn parallels with other lunar deities.

Stories & Myths

The Myth of Chía and Sué

Source Lucas Fernández de Piedrahita, 'Historia general' (1688)Culture MuiscaPeriod Pre-Columbian (recorded in 17th century)

Summary

Chía was the wife of Sué, the Sun god. They lived together in the sky, but Chía was often overshadowed by her husband's brilliance. In a fit of jealousy or disagreement, Chía left the Sun and retreated to the night, taking the moon with her. She then created the stars to accompany her. This separation explained the cycle of day and night, and the phases of the moon.

Participants

Chía

Sué (Sun god)

Consequences

The moon and sun became separate celestial bodies, and the lunar cycle was established. The separation also symbolized the duality of light and darkness in Muisca cosmology.

Variants

Some versions say that Chía and Sué had a child, who became the rainbow. Others suggest that the separation was due to a transgression by Chía. The exact details are not consistent across sources.

The Temple of Chía and the Cacique of Guatavita

Source Pedro Simón, 'Noticias historiales' (1626)Culture MuiscaPeriod Pre-Columbian (recorded in 17th century)

Summary

The temple of Chía was located on a hill in the town of Chía. It was a place of pilgrimage and ritual. The cacique of Guatavita, a neighboring ruler, was responsible for the ceremonies dedicated to Chía, which included offerings and possibly human sacrifice. The temple was a center of political and religious power.

Participants

Consequences

The temple served as a focal point for lunar worship and reinforced the alliance between the communities of Chía and Guatavita.

Variants

Other sources mention that the temple was also used for astronomical observations.

Dark History
Content advisory. The worship of Chía may have involved human sacrifice, as reported by Spanish chroniclers. This is a sensitive topic that should be presented with cultural context and respect for the descendants.

Human sacrifice

Source Pedro Simón (1626) and other chroniclers

Description

Some colonial accounts indicate that the Muisca practiced human sacrifice, possibly including in the worship of Chía, particularly during times of crisis or major ceremonies. The victims were often prisoners of war or slaves.

Scholarly Context

The extent and nature of Muisca human sacrifice is debated. Some scholars question the reliability of Spanish accounts, which may have exaggerated such practices to justify conquest. Modern archaeology has not confirmed widespread human sacrifice, but some evidence exists.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

The exact nature of Chía's iconography is unknown; no surviving Muisca artifacts have been definitively identified as representations of her. The extent of her worship outside the town of Chía is unclear. The role of human sacrifice in her cult is debated. Modern oral traditions have been heavily influenced by colonial and contemporary reinterpretations, making it difficult to separate pre-Columbian elements. The pronunciation of her name in the original Muisca language is uncertain. Further research in colonial archives and archaeology is needed.

Cultural Significance

Chía is the source of the name of the town of Chía (now a suburb of Bogotá), one of the oldest continuously inhabited settlements in Colombia. Her dual nature — both life-giver and destroyer — reflects the Muisca understanding of the universe as a place of opposing forces that must be balanced. The El Dorado ceremony at Lake Guatavita, which involved the Zipa covering himself in gold dust and diving into the lake as an offering, was performed in honor of both Chía and the waters she governed. Chía's cult was one of the most persistent and difficult for Spanish missionaries to suppress.
