Who It Is

In the high Andean world of the Muisca, where the sun and moon were woven into the fabric of daily life, Chía was the goddess of the Moon—a luminous presence whose cycles guided the rhythms of night, fertility, and time. Her temple, perched on a hill near what is now Bogotá, was a place of pilgrimage, where the faithful sought her favor and the caciques paid homage. Yet, like so many deities of the Americas, her story was nearly lost to conquest and time, surviving only in fragments of colonial chronicles and the enduring memory of a people.

Chía is described in myth as the wife of Sué, the Sun god, but their relationship was troubled. She is said to have been responsible for the creation of the night and the stars, and her movements across the sky were linked to the lunar cycle. She was also associated with fertility and the protection of women, though specific myths are scarce.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Chía' is derived from the Muisca language (Chibcha), and is also the name of the town and sacred site associated with the goddess. The exact linguistic root is not fully documented, but it is often linked to the moon.

Pronunciation — CHEE-ah (approximate, based on Spanish transcription; original Chibcha pronunciation is uncertain)

Aliases — Chie, Chía (Moon goddess), Goddess of the Moon

Ownership — Muisca (Chibcha) culture, indigenous people of the Altiplano Cundiboyacense in present-day Colombia.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No specific physical description of Chía survives in colonial sources. As a moon goddess, she was likely associated with the moon itself, and possibly represented in iconography as a lunar disc or a female figure with lunar symbols. Reconstructions based on Muisca art suggest anthropomorphic representations of deities, but none have been securely identified as Chía.

Symbols — The moon is the primary symbol. Colonial sources mention that Chía's temple was associated with a sacred hill, and that the moon's phases were linked to her actions. Some modern interpretations associate her with the crescent moon and with water, as the moon was thought to influence rivers and lakes.

Domains — Chía's primary domain was the moon and the night sky. She was also connected to water and fertility, as the moon was believed to influence the tides and the growth of crops. Her main cult center was the town of Chía, near modern Bogotá, where her temple stood on a hill.

Accounts I
The Myth of Chía and SuéLucas Fernández de Piedrahita, 'Historia general' (1688)

Pre-Columbian (recorded in 17th century)

Chía was the wife of Sué, the Sun god. They lived together in the sky, but Chía was often overshadowed by her husband's brilliance. In a fit of jealousy or disagreement, Chía left the Sun and retreated to the night, taking the moon with her. She then created the stars to accompany her. This separation explained the cycle of day and night, and the phases of the moon. The moon and sun became separate celestial bodies, and the lunar cycle was established. The separation also symbolized the duality of light and darkness in Muisca cosmology.

Accounts II
The Temple of Chía and the Cacique of GuatavitaPedro Simón, 'Noticias historiales' (1626)

Pre-Columbian (recorded in 17th century)

The temple of Chía was located on a hill in the town of Chía. It was a place of pilgrimage and ritual. The cacique of Guatavita, a neighboring ruler, was responsible for the ceremonies dedicated to Chía, which included offerings and possibly human sacrifice. The temple was a center of political and religious power. The temple served as a focal point for lunar worship and reinforced the alliance between the communities of Chía and Guatavita.
