This page is intentionally blank and ready for source-backed lore, versions, images, and connections. Ashinazuchi is a deity of the earth, agriculture, and the human community. He represents the suffering of mortal parents and the fragility of human life in the face of divine and monstrous forces. He is also a deity of the Izumo region, the ancient heartland of Japanese mythology.

Form

Ashinazuchi is described in the Kojiki as an old man (翁, okina) with a bent back, white hair, and a long beard. He is depicted as a humble farmer or mountain dweller, wearing simple clothing. He is often shown with his wife, Tenazuchi, who is similarly aged and dressed. In art, they are typically portrayed as a pair of elderly peasants, sometimes kneeling or bowing before Susanoo.

Powers

Divine Parentage and Blessing Ashinazuchi is a kami (deity), but his power is not martial or cosmic. His power lies in his role as a progenitor and a source of divine blessing. By giving his daughter in marriage to Susanoo, he secures a divine alliance that ensures the prosperity of his lineage and the Izumo region. Kojiki and Nihon Shoki He is powerless against the monster Yamata no Orochi, requiring the intervention of a more powerful deity. None.

Hospitality and Ritual Knowledge He possesses the knowledge of proper ritual hospitality (饗応, kyōō), offering Susanoo sake and a place to rest. This act of piety is what allows the storm god to formulate his plan to defeat the serpent. Kojiki His knowledge is domestic, not martial. None.

Behavior & Habitat

Ashinazuchi is depicted as a grieving, desperate father. When Susanoo encounters him, he is weeping, having already lost seven of his eight daughters to the monstrous eight-headed serpent Yamata no Orochi. He is respectful and submissive to the storm god, immediately offering him hospitality and explaining his plight. He is also trusting, agreeing to give his last daughter's hand in marriage to Susanoo after the god promises to slay the beast.

Accounts

Susanoo, exiled from the High Plain of Heaven, descends to the land of Izumo. He comes upon an old man and an old woman weeping around a young girl. The old man, Ashinazuchi, explains that he and his wife had eight daughters, but the eight-headed serpent Yamata no Orochi has devoured seven of them, and it is now time for the eighth, Kushinada-hime, to be taken. Susanoo offers to slay the serpent in exchange for the girl's hand in marriage. Ashinazuchi agrees. Susanoo transforms Kushinada-hime into a comb and places her in his hair. He then instructs the couple to brew strong sake and build a fence with eight gates. At each gate, a vat of sake is placed. The serpent, attracted by the sake, drinks from each vat and falls into a drunken sleep. Susanoo then slices the serpent into pieces. In its tail, he finds the magnificent sword Kusanagi-no-Tsurugi. He gives the sword to the sun goddess Amaterasu, and marries Kushinada-hime. Ashinazuchi and Tenazuchi are given a place in the new household and are treated with respect.

After the storm-god Susanoo killed the eight-headed serpent Yamata no Orochi, he did not merely save the girl: he took her as his bride. Ashinazuchi and his wife Tenazuchi, overjoyed and bound by their promise, offered their daughter Kushinada-hime to Susanoo, and the marriage consecrated the ground of Izumo. Susanoo sought a place to dwell with his new wife and raised a palace at Suga, composing a verse that modern readers often take as the first Japanese song. Ashinazuchi, the aging earth-father of the Izumo cycle, thus becomes the ancestor-in-law of the great gods and the link between the mortal mountain-folk of Izumo and the divine storm-king.

Scholarly Debates
Paternity of Kushinada-hime
The Kojiki states that Ashinazuchi and Tenazuchi are the parents of Kushinada-hime. ⇌ Some variants in the Nihon Shoki present Ashinazuchi and Tenazuchi as local deities who are not the biological parents, but rather guardians or custodians of the maiden.
Evidence for A: Kojiki, Book 1, Section 18.
Evidence for B: Nihon Shoki, Book 1, Section on Susanoo.
Meaning of the name
The name means 'Foot-Comb' (足名椎), referring to a comb for the feet, a tool of grooming and preparation. ⇌ The name is a phonetic spelling of 'Inazuchi' (雷, thunder), linking him to storm deities and the power of Susanoo.
Evidence for A: The paired name with his wife, Tenazuchi ('Hand-Comb'), suggests a complementary, domestic meaning.
Evidence for B: Some scholars note the phonetic similarity and the association with Susanoo, a storm god.
What We Don't Know

The specific local cults dedicated to Ashinazuchi and Tenazuchi are not well-documented in English-language sources. A deeper investigation into regional shrine records and Japanese-language scholarship would be needed to fully understand their worship. The exact iconography in Japanese art is also not thoroughly indexed.

A way to hold it in your mind

Ashinazuchi is the 'grieving father' archetype in Japanese mythology. He's not the hero, but the catalyst. Think of him as the desperate villager in a monster movie who gives the hero the information and the motivation needed to fight. His pain is the emotional core that makes the story about more than just a god slaying a beast.
