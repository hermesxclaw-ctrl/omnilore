Who It Is

Walk the edge of a Romani settlement in Serbia at the start of the movable Easter fast and you may see an old tree hung with a comb, a mirror, and a baby's clothes — gifts to a grandmother who is not there, but who must not be forgotten. Her name is Bibi, and 'bibi' is simply Romani for 'aunt'. The aunt protects or she punishes: to the family that keeps her feast she is the warm, tireless healer who travelled village to village curing sick children, so each village keeps its own day; to the family that lets its well run foul, its dead go unburied properly, or its rules lapse, she is the red-veiled old woman at the crossroads whose anger is cholera. Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Romani bibi, meaning 'aunt' (a kinship term for a paternal or maternal aunt). The diminutive form is bibiori (little aunt). The feminine noun belongs to the core Romani kinship lexicon, which, like the rest of the language, derives ultimately from an Indic source — Romani bibi is cognate with t

Pronunciation — Approximately ['bi.bi] in Romani (two short, even syllables; initial stress in most Balkan dialects). The Serbian rendering Tetkica Bibija is ['tɛt.ki.tsa 'bi.bi.ja]. In literature the name is usually Latin-scripted as Bibi.

Aliases — Auntie Bibi, Bibija, Tetkica Bibija, Healer Bibi, Bibiyaku, Bibijako Day / Bibijako Djive / Bibijako Dive (the holiday named for her)

Ownership — The figure and the feast named for her (Bibijako Djive, 'Auntie Day') belong to the ritual and devotional life of Balkan Romani communities and their diaspora, especially in Serbia

Powers
Healing and protection of children and the family

In her attested 'Auntie Bibi' register, Bibi is the healer and protector of the family and of children's health. Her primary power is prophylactic and curative: held to shield children from illness and to be invoked for the well-being of the household. The Slava-style feast (Bibijako Djive) is the principal act through which her protection is mobilized — the family honours her collectively under the holy tree, and the acclamation 'Bibijako sostipe!' ('To the health of Bibi/aunt!') is shouted precisely as a blessing over the collective. The gifts hung for a 'Bibia' (a comb, a mirror, children's clothes) symbolise the domestic care that she is asked to complete.

Limit: Her help is conditional on the family's keeping the feast and, in the disease register, on maintaining purity and rectitude. Neglect — failing to clean, improper burial rites, moral transgression — is the antecedent of her withdrawal of protection (and, in the darker register, of her wrath). Among Orthodox Roma, a priest is obligatory at the celebration, indicating her power is exercised within an Eastern Christian liturgical frame even as she remains a non-canonized saint.

Sending and withholding epidemic disease (the cholera / 'Red Lady' register)

In the older and popular disease-goddess register emphasised in the entities.json entry, Bibi is a feared bringer of epidemic illness — above all cholera — who emerges from contaminated water sources or stands at crossroads and the settlement's edge. Her anger is triggered by violations of hygiene and social/moral taboo (uncleanliness, improper burial, transgression). Because the figure is unwinnable by force, the community must placate her with prophylactic offerings: red cloth, wine, and food left at the settlement boundary, sometimes with the sacrifice of a red rooster.

Limit: She can be appeased; the offering-ritual is understood to avert or end her scourge. Her plague power is anthropologically tied to the real threat of cholera in 19th–20th century Balkan Romani settlements.

Threshold and boundary guardianship

Bibi is a liminal guardian of the community's boundaries — the threshold, the crossroads, the wellhead, the settlement's edge. She stands at the limit between the safe, ordered domestic interior and the dangerous outside from which disease and impurity come. In the disease register she must be met at these boundaries with gifts; in the benign register the holy tree and the feast-ground function as the consecrated threshold where she is received. This slots her into a well-known class of female household/liminal guardian spirits found across Southeastern European and Indic tradition.

Limit: Her guardianship is directional and conditional rather than absolute; it protects the kin-group that honours her and punishes the one that does not.

Crossing calendar / healing-journey mobility

According to the origin-legend, Bibi travelled to different places on different dates and performed healings; each community accordingly keeps its own date for the feast, tied to the movable Easter fasting cycle rather than a fixed calendar day. Her 'power' thus has a spatial-and-temporal distribution built into it: she is simultaneously one figure and locally many, honouring each settlement at the date she once visited it.

Limit: This explains rather than empowers; the practical effect is that the celebration dates are mobile and only loosely synchronised across communities.

Healing identity with Shashthi (typological inheritance)

The Romani folk-religion frame presents Bibi as 'the Roma version of the Hindu goddess Shashthi'. Typologically this is real: Shashthi (Sanskrit Ṣaṣṭhī, 'sixth') is the Hindu goddess of childbirth, child-protection and healing from childhood disease, venerated in India and Nepal, who (critically) evolved from a feared child-devouring illness goddess into a benevolent saviour-and-bestower of children — an evolution that mirrors Bibi's disease-goddess-to-healing-saint trajectory. The identification is made explicit in the attested article and is consistent with the deep Indic ancestry of Romani religion carried out of the Indian subcontinent during the medieval diaspora.

Limit: It is a typological/religious-historical comparison, not an assertion that Bibi is literally Shashthi or a direct 1:1 transfer; direct text-critical evidence of a continuous cultic lineage between the two is not available in the sources consulted.

Accounts I
The healing-journey legend (why the feast dates differ)Wikipedia 'Bibi (Romani cult)'; retold in Serbian ethnology

Traditional oral account, recorded in the encyclopedic source

Bibi is said to have travelled to different places and performed healings on different dates. Each settlement she visited preserves the memory of her visit, and for this reason each region celebrates Bibijako Djive on its own day, all of them falling within the variable Easter period (and thus moving from year to year). The feast is therefore not fixed on a saint's day but floats with the liturgical calendar, matching the per-settlement geography of her generosity. Establishes the mobility and regional plurality of the holiday's date; gives each community a proprietary claim on the saint.

The Slava and the handing-over of the cakeWikipedia 'Bibi (Romani cult)'; Romani adaptation of the Serbian Slava tradition

Documented contemporary practice

Bibi belongs to the small set of Romani Slava household deities. On the day of the feast, after the Orthodox service, the Romani families gather at a holy place — usually a tree or a cross — bringing a celebratory cake and fast dishes, and in some places hang gifts for a 'Bibia' (a comb, a mirror, and children's clothing) on a holy tree. They process with the cake and candles, shouting 'Bibijako sostipe!' ('To the health of Bibi!'). Under the family's pear or walnut tree, the host of the next 'glory' (slava) is determined and the slava cake is handed over. The day is marked with songs, dancing, and sweets given to children. Annual renewal of the family's bond with Bibi, continuation of the feast cycle, and the rotation of household hosting duty.

Accounts II
The Muslim Roma observance (Djisatedimi)Wikipedia 'Bibi (Romani cult)'; Dragoljub Acković's 2004 and 2010 monographs; ethnographer Aleksandar Petrović

Contemporary observance; documented in the 2000s

Muslim Roma also keep a Bibi feast, under the name Djisatedimi ('the holiday' in the Muslim Roma vernacular). According to ethnographer Aleksandar Petrović, Muslim Roma in Kruševac (Serbia) adopted the Bibijako Djive feast from Christian Roma and celebrate it on the same day, but at night: after fasting for several days, the celebration begins at sundown. Acković's two monographs on Bibi report that the ethnographic literature contains no earlier record of Muslim Roma celebrating Bibijako Djive; however, his 2010 monograph compiles accounts gathered among Muslim Roma from Prizren, Uroševac (Ferizaj) in Kosovo, and Belgrade indicating that these communities kept the feast for the sake of protecting their children from disease, with some keeping it for an entire month, from 31 January to 1 March. Demonstrates Bibi's reach across the Christian/Muslim religious divide in Romani society, and her hardening into a children-from-disease protector even among Muslim Roma.

The Red Lady at the wellhead (cholera register)Romani oral tradition as summarised in the OmniLore entities.json entry (AAT-2882)

Pre-modern through contemporary folklore

In the disease-goddess register, Bibi appears as an old woman in red, sometimes with a red veil, standing at a wellhead or crossroads or at the settlement's edge. When cholera struck a Romani settlement it was said that Bibi had been angered by uncleanliness, improper burial rites, or moral transgression. Pacification rituals left offerings of red cloth, wine, and food at the settlement boundary, occasionally with the sacrifice of a red rooster, to recall her mercy. An etiological and moral account of epidemic disease that also enforces community purity and hygiene norms; the memory of historical cholera outbreaks is encoded as divine punishment and appeasement.
