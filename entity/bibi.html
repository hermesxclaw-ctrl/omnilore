Who It Is

Bibi (literally 'aunt' or 'grandmother' in Romani) is a feared goddess of disease — particularly cholera — in Romani folklore. She is described as an old woman dressed in red, sometimes with a red veil, who emerges from contaminated water sources or stands at crossroads to spread plague. Her appearance is not wholly malevolent: she is a tutelary goddess who punishes violations of social and hygienic taboos within the community. When cholera struck a Romani settlement, it was believed that Bibi had been angered by uncleanliness, improper burial rites, or moral transgressions. Pacification rituals involved leaving offerings of red cloth, wine, and food at the edge of a settlement, sometimes accompanied by the sacrifice of a red rooster. In some traditions, Bibi is identified with the Virgin Mary under a red veil, representing a syncretic blending of Romani goddess beliefs with Christian iconography. She continues to be remembered in Balkan Romani communities as both a warning and a protective figure — a terrible grandmother who punishes neglect but also guards the boundaries of community health.

Attested in Romani oral tradition (Balkan Roma, especially Gurbet and Arlije groups)Era Pre-modern through contemporary Romani folklore

Form

Attestations describe Bibi not through fixed iconography but through the costume and setting of her feast and through association with a female elder figure. She is conventionally imagined as an old woman — a grandmother or great-aunt figure — dressed in red (the register is strongest in the popular 'Red Lady' disease-veil portrayals), sometimes veiled in red. In the modern healing-saint register she is the tender, protective aunt of the household rather than a depicted deity; she has no standardised sculptural or painted image in the Romani tradition, and where visual representation does occur it is typically through the ordinary women (the grandmothers and aunts who keep the feast) who 'stand in' for her rather than through a cult image. (Interpretive synthesis: the appearance is inferred from the name 'aunt', the red garment in the cholera register, and the ritual props — combs, mirrors, and children's clothes — that stand for domestic, female care.)

Powers

Healing and protection of children and the family In her attested 'Auntie Bibi' register, Bibi is the healer and protector of the family and of children's health. Her primary power is prophylactic and curative: held to shield children from illness and to be invoked for the well-being of the household. The Slava-style feast (Bibijako Djive) is the principal act through which her protection is mobilized — the family honours her collectively under the holy tree, and the acclamation 'Bibijako sostipe!' ('To the health of Bibi/aunt!') is shouted precisely as a blessing over the collective. The gifts hung for a 'Bibia' (a comb, a mirror, children's clothes) symbolise the domestic care that she is asked to complete. Attested — encyclopedic (Wikipedia 'Bibi (Romani cult)'), Museum of Roma Culture, 2019 heritage register. Her help is conditional on the family's keeping the feast and, in the disease register, on maintaining purity and rectitude. Neglect — failing to clean, improper burial rites, moral transgression — is the antecedent of her withdrawal of protection (and, in the darker register, of her wrath). Among Orthodox Roma, a priest is obligatory at the celebration, indicating her power is exercised within an Eastern Christian liturgical frame even as she remains a non-canonized saint. Whether she actively heals or merely refrains from harming is left ambiguous; the fear register implies she withholds healing from those who offend her.

Sending and withholding epidemic disease (the cholera / 'Red Lady' register) In the older and popular disease-goddess register emphasised in the entities.json entry, Bibi is a feared bringer of epidemic illness — above all cholera — who emerges from contaminated water sources or stands at crossroads and the settlement's edge. Her anger is triggered by violations of hygiene and social/moral taboo (uncleanliness, improper burial, transgression). Because the figure is unwinnable by force, the community must placate her with prophylactic offerings: red cloth, wine, and food left at the settlement boundary, sometimes with the sacrifice of a red rooster. Popular/ethno-medical register (as summarised in the OmniLore entities.json entry for this entity); the encyclopedic sources foreground the healing face and do not dwell on the plague-sending face. She can be appeased; the offering-ritual is understood to avert or end her scourge. Her plague power is anthropologically tied to the real threat of cholera in 19th–20th century Balkan Romani settlements. This register is the central contested identification (see contradictions). Some sources treat it as a distinct figure (an 'Olabibi' goddess of illness and healing) rather than an aspect of Auntie Bibi.

Threshold and boundary guardianship Bibi is a liminal guardian of the community's boundaries — the threshold, the crossroads, the wellhead, the settlement's edge. She stands at the limit between the safe, ordered domestic interior and the dangerous outside from which disease and impurity come. In the disease register she must be met at these boundaries with gifts; in the benign register the holy tree and the feast-ground function as the consecrated threshold where she is received. This slots her into a well-known class of female household/liminal guardian spirits found across Southeastern European and Indic tradition. Reconstructed from the distribution of attested practices (settlement-edge offerings; the tree-and-cross feast site) and comparative demonology. Her guardianship is directional and conditional rather than absolute; it protects the kin-group that honours her and punishes the one that does not. The boundary is also where the Red Lady appears to attack, so she guards and threatens the same line.

Crossing calendar / healing-journey mobility According to the origin-legend, Bibi travelled to different places on different dates and performed healings; each community accordingly keeps its own date for the feast, tied to the movable Easter fasting cycle rather than a fixed calendar day. Her 'power' thus has a spatial-and-temporal distribution built into it: she is simultaneously one figure and locally many, honouring each settlement at the date she once visited it. Attested legend recounted in Wikipedia 'Bibi (Romani cult)'. This explains rather than empowers; the practical effect is that the celebration dates are mobile and only loosely synchronised across communities. The same legend is used to rationalise the absence of a single fixed feast day — itself a point of variation among communities.

Healing identity with Shashthi (typological inheritance) The Romani folk-religion frame presents Bibi as 'the Roma version of the Hindu goddess Shashthi'. Typologically this is real: Shashthi (Sanskrit Ṣaṣṭhī, 'sixth') is the Hindu goddess of childbirth, child-protection and healing from childhood disease, venerated in India and Nepal, who (critically) evolved from a feared child-devouring illness goddess into a benevolent saviour-and-bestower of children — an evolution that mirrors Bibi's disease-goddess-to-healing-saint trajectory. The identification is made explicit in the attested article and is consistent with the deep Indic ancestry of Romani religion carried out of the Indian subcontinent during the medieval diaspora. Encyclopedic claim (Wikipedia 'Bibi (Romani cult)') + corroborating Wikipedia 'Shashthi' article. It is a typological/religious-historical comparison, not an assertion that Bibi is literally Shashthi or a direct 1:1 transfer; direct text-critical evidence of a continuous cultic lineage between the two is not available in the sources consulted. Some scholars of Romani religion caution against over-reading Indic survivals, given that Romani religious life is overwhelmingly shaped by centuries of Christian (and Muslim) host-society conversion; the Shashthi link may be revivalist reconstruction as much as unbroken survival.

Accounts

Bibi is said to have travelled to different places and performed healings on different dates. Each settlement she visited preserves the memory of her visit, and for this reason each region celebrates Bibijako Djive on its own day, all of them falling within the variable Easter period (and thus moving from year to year). The feast is therefore not fixed on a saint's day but floats with the liturgical calendar, matching the per-settlement geography of her generosity.

Bibi belongs to the small set of Romani Slava household deities. On the day of the feast, after the Orthodox service, the Romani families gather at a holy place — usually a tree or a cross — bringing a celebratory cake and fast dishes, and in some places hang gifts for a 'Bibia' (a comb, a mirror, and children's clothing) on a holy tree. They process with the cake and candles, shouting 'Bibijako sostipe!' ('To the health of Bibi!'). Under the family's pear or walnut tree, the host of the next 'glory' (slava) is determined and the slava cake is handed over. The day is marked with songs, dancing, and sweets given to children.

Muslim Roma also keep a Bibi feast, under the name Djisatedimi ('the holiday' in the Muslim Roma vernacular). According to ethnographer Aleksandar Petrović, Muslim Roma in Kruševac (Serbia) adopted the Bibijako Djive feast from Christian Roma and celebrate it on the same day, but at night: after fasting for several days, the celebration begins at sundown. Acković's two monographs on Bibi report that the ethnographic literature contains no earlier record of Muslim Roma celebrating Bibijako Djive; however, his 2010 monograph compiles accounts gathered among Muslim Roma from Prizren, Uroševac (Ferizaj) in Kosovo, and Belgrade indicating that these communities kept the feast for the sake of protecting their children from disease, with some keeping it for an entire month, from 31 January to 1 March.

In the disease-goddess register, Bibi appears as an old woman in red, sometimes with a red veil, standing at a wellhead or crossroads or at the settlement's edge. When cholera struck a Romani settlement it was said that Bibi had been angered by uncleanliness, improper burial rites, or moral transgression. Pacification rituals left offerings of red cloth, wine, and food at the settlement boundary, occasionally with the sacrifice of a red rooster, to recall her mercy.

Content Notes

The darkest attested face of Bibi is her identification as a bringer of cholera and plague, an old woman in red who emerges from contaminated water or stands at the crossroads and spreads disease when the community is thought to be unclean or morally transgressive, or to have committed improper burials. This frames historically devastating epidemics — which fall overwhelmingly on poor, marginalised Romani settlements — as divine punishment for a community's own failings, saddling victims with guilt for their suffering. The pacification ritual (red cloth, wine, food, sometimes a red rooster) is a response to a real, terrifying mortality threat.

Anthropologically this is a classic disease-spirit complex: an epidemic agent personified as a wrathful, puritanical female who polices purity ('mahrime') norms, mirroring the structural function of the Hindu Shashthi, who in early texts 'causes diseases in mother and child' and 'steals the born' and must be propitiated.

Popular/ethno-medical Romani tradition (as summarised in the OmniLore entities.json entry); the feared face is underplayed in the encyclopedic register.

Bibi's punishment-frame encodes and reinforces a recurring external stigma against Romani communities as 'unclean'. Whether agency lies in an internal purity code or an internalised response to external prejudice is genuinely difficult to separate; the goddess-polices-purity structure can double as a justification for excluding those deemed impure.

Romani purity ('mahrime'/'marime') has been studied as both an internal social-regulatory system and a protective response to persecution; scholars such as Ian Hancock emphasise that impurity codes regulate social relations rather than describing filth.

Comparative Romani-studies framing (cf. the 'beng' dossier's treatment of purity/mahrime).

Much of what a general reader 'knows' about a formal Romani pantheon of goddesses (Bibi among them, with Olabibi, Devleski Day, etc.) derives from late-19th-century non-Romani collectors such as Heinrich von Wlislocki and Charles Godfrey Leland, whose systematisations modern Romani scholars regard as partially invented or heavily embroidered. This risks presenting an outsider-constructed 'mythology' as authentic Romani belief.

The decline of Wlislocki's credibility is an established position in Romani studies; the 'pantheon' tables found in popular sources mix attested cults (Bibi is attested) with reconstructed lists.

Romani folklore overview (Wlislocki warning); the Romani folklore Wikipedia article cautions that Wlislocki's 'myths have no connection to authentic Romani traditions'.

Bibi is venerated as a de facto saint by Orthodox Roma yet is not canonised by any Church, and her celebration nevertheless requires an Orthodox priest. This liminal status—venerating a non-canonised figure inside a liturgy—reflects and sometimes sharpens the cultural friction Romani communities navigate between Indigenous/Indic-tradition religion and the formal Christianity of host societies. It has also fuelled suspicion that Romani observance is 'inauthentic' Christianity.

The tension between folk veneration and official canonisation is a well-known pattern for popular saints and household deities across the Orthodox world, not unique to the Roma.

A way to hold it in your mind

Walk the edge of a Romani settlement in Serbia at the start of the movable Easter fast and you may see an old tree hung with a comb, a mirror, and a baby's clothes — gifts to a grandmother who is not there, but who must not be forgotten. Her name is Bibi, and 'bibi' is simply Romani for 'aunt'. The aunt protects or she punishes: to the family that keeps her feast she is the warm, tireless healer who travelled village to village curing sick children, so each village keeps its own day; to the family that lets its well run foul, its dead go unburied properly, or its rules lapse, she is the red-veiled old woman at the crossroads whose anger is cholera. In one telling she is the Roma heir of the Hindu goddess Shashthi — the sixth-day protector of newborns who, in a deeper past, was the one who stole the born — and so the Roma carried her, along with their language and their kin-words, out of India a thousand years and more ago. Bibi is not a remote sky-god; she is the punitive, devoted matriarch of every Romani hearth, and the whole community, Christian and Muslim, Orthodox priest and night-fasting Roma alike, tries to stay on the right side of her memory.
