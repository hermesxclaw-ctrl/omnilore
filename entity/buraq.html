Who It Is

Al-Buraq (Arabic: 'The Lightning') is the fabulous winged steed that carried the Prophet Muhammad from Mecca to Jerusalem (the Isra') and then through the seven heavens to the presence of Allah (the Mi'raj) in a single night. According to hadith, Buraq is described as a white, long-bodied creature, smaller than a mule but larger than a donkey, with wings on its thighs. It has the face of a woman, the body of a horse, and the tail of a peacock — a composite creature of surpassing beauty. Each step Buraq takes carries its rider as far as the eye can see. During the Night Journey, Buraq was brought to Muhammad by the angel Jibril (Gabriel), and the Prophet encountered other prophets along the way — Adam, John, Jesus, Joseph, Idris, Aaron, and Moses — at each level of heaven. Buraq also carried the Prophet's companion, the archangel Jibril himself, and is linked to other prophets in Islamic tradition: Abraham is said to have ridden Buraq to Mecca. In popular Islamic imagery, Buraq is depicted as a winged horse with a human face and a peacock's tail, symbolizing the transcendence of the Prophet's journey beyond the physical world into the spiritual realm. Buraq represents the bridge between the earthly and the divine, the vehicle of revelation, and the unique honor of Muhammad as the Prophet who traversed the boundaries of creation and stood where no created being had stood before.

The Approach

In the luminous night journey of the Prophet Muhammad, a creature smaller than a mule yet larger than a donkey carried him from Mecca to Jerusalem and through the heavens—a celestial steed whose name, Buraq, evokes lightning itself. This is the story of a mount that transcends the mundane, a being of light and speed, whose image has galloped through centuries of Islamic art and imagination.

Content advisory. Religious subject; depictions of the Buraq and the Prophet Muhammad are sensitive in some Islamic traditions; no graphic or violent content.

Etymology

Derived from Arabic root b-r-q (برق), meaning 'to flash' or 'lightning', reflecting the creature's incredible speed.

Cultural Ownership

Islamic culture and tradition, particularly within the narrative of the Prophet Muhammad's Night Journey (Isra and Mi'raj).

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars have speculated a connection to the Greek Pegasus or the Hindu Uchchaihshravas, but these are unsubstantiated and not accepted in Islamic tradition.

Description

Appearance

The Buraq is described as a white, radiant creature, smaller than a mule but larger than a donkey. It has wings, and its step reaches the farthest point of sight. Some later traditions depict it with a human face, but this is not in the earliest sources. Its beauty is said to be dazzling, and it emits light.

Symbols & Iconography

In Islamic art, the Buraq is often depicted with a horse's body, a human head, wings, and a peacock's tail, though this is a later Persian and Mughal convention. It symbolizes swiftness, spiritual ascension, and divine favor.

Behaviour & Personality

The Buraq is docile and obedient to God's command. It is gentle with the Prophet, but when the Prophet's sandal strap was loose, it shied away until the Prophet secured it, showing its sensitivity to proper conduct. It is swift beyond comprehension, covering vast distances in a single stride.

Domains & Habitats

The Buraq is a heavenly creature, residing in paradise, but it descends to earth to serve the prophets. It is not bound to any earthly habitat.

Version Differences

Early hadith describe it simply as a white animal with wings. Later Persian and Turkish traditions elaborate its appearance, adding a human face, peacock tail, and jeweled trappings. Some accounts say it was ridden by prophets before Muhammad, including Abraham and Jesus, but this is not in the canonical hadith.

Stories & Myths

The Night Journey and Ascension (Isra and Mi'raj)

Source Quran, Hadith (Bukhari, Muslim), TafsirCulture IslamicPeriod 7th century CE

Summary

In a single night, the angel Gabriel brought the Buraq to the Prophet Muhammad in Mecca. The Prophet rode the Buraq to Jerusalem, where he led other prophets in prayer at the Temple Mount. Then the Buraq ascended with him through the heavens, where he met Adam, Jesus, Moses, Abraham, and others, and received the command of daily prayers.

Participants

Prophet Muhammad

Angel Gabriel

Buraq

Prophets of the past

Consequences

The journey established Jerusalem as a holy site in Islam and instituted the five daily prayers. It also affirmed Muhammad's status as the seal of the prophets.

Variants

Different hadiths vary in the details of the celestial encounters. Some versions say the Buraq was tied to a ring at the Western Wall (al-Buraq Wall), which is now a sacred site.
