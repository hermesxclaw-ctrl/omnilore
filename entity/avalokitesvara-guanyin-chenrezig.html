Who It Is

In the vast pantheon of Buddhist compassion, one figure stands as the embodiment of mercy itself: Avalokiteśvara, the Bodhisattva of Infinite Compassion, whose name means 'the Lord who looks down upon the world with pity.' Revered across Asia from Sri Lanka to Japan, he appears in myriad forms—as the thousand-armed savior in Tibetan Buddhism, the gentle Guanyin in China, and the ever-watchful Chenrezig in the Himalayas. His story is one of divine vows, cosmic sacrifice, and the promise that no being is beyond the reach of compassion.

Avalokiteśvara is the embodiment of compassion (karuṇā). He is said to hear the cries of all beings and respond with appropriate means (upaya). He is patient, gentle, and self-sacrificing, as shown in the legend where his head and arms shatter from the effort of trying to free all beings from samsara. He is also a teacher, appearing in whatever form is needed to guide beings toward enlightenment.

Powers
Karuṇā (Compassion)

The power to perceive the suffering of all beings and to respond with boundless compassion. This is his primary 'power' and the source of all his other abilities.

Limit: None; it is an all-encompassing quality, but its efficacy depends on the faith of the devotee.

Saving from perils

In the Lotus Sutra, he can protect beings from fire, floods, demons, weapons, imprisonment, and other dangers when they call upon him with faith.

Limit: Requires the devotee to invoke his name sincerely.

Manifestation in various forms

He can appear in 33 forms (in the Lotus Sutra) or 108 forms (in some later texts) to teach beings according to their capacities—as a monk, a layperson, a celestial being, a woman, a child, etc.

Limit: He only assumes forms that are beneficial to the devotee's spiritual progress.

Six-Syllable Mantra (Om mani padme hum)

The mantra associated with Chenrezig is believed to invoke his compassion and bring liberation. It is recited by millions daily.

Limit: No formal limitations; its power is dependent on the practitioner's faith and repetition.

Thousand arms and thousand eyes

In the Mahakarunika form, he has a thousand arms and a thousand eyes, enabling him to see and help all beings simultaneously. This form is said to have arisen from his head and arms shattering in grief.

Limit: None; this is a symbolic representation of his limitless compassion.

Power over rebirth

As a bodhisattva, he can choose his rebirth and is said to be able to liberate beings from the hells, as in the legend of his descent to hell to save souls.

Limit: He does not force liberation; beings must have some merit or karmic potential.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Avalokiteśvara is typically depicted as a male bodhisattva in Indian and Tibetan art: youthful, with a serene expression, adorned with jewels and fine robes. He often holds a lotus (padma) and a mala (rosary), and sometimes a water flask. In his headdress, a small image of his spiritual father, Amitabha, is often shown. Symbols — Lotus (purity), rosary (compassion in action), water flask (the dew of compassion), willow branch (flexibility and healing), peacock feather (in some forms, wisdom), the image of Amitabha in the crown (his teacher and spiritual father), and the six-syllable mantra 'Om mani padme hum' (the essence of his compassion). In Chinese iconography, Guanyin is often shown with a child, symbolizing fertility and protection.

Domains — Avalokiteśvara resides in the Pure Land of Sukhavati as an attendant of Amitabha, but he is also present in all realms of samsara, especially in the human realm and the hells. He is often associated with Mount Potalaka (in Indian mythology, a mountain south of India; in Chinese Buddhism, Mount Putuo in Zhejiang is considered his earthly abode). In Tibetan Buddhism, his main seat is the Potala Palace in Lhasa, which is named after his mountain.

Accounts I
The Universal Gate (Lotus Sutra)Lotus Sutra, Chapter 25

Circa 1st-2nd century CE

The Buddha praises Avalokiteśvara's compassion and describes how calling his name can save beings from seven perils, three poisons (greed, hatred, delusion), and one calamity (lust). He also explains that Avalokiteśvara manifests in various forms to teach beings. Establishes Avalokiteśvara as a major savior figure in Mahayana Buddhism.

The Shattering of Heads and ArmsKarandavyuha Sutra

Circa 4th-5th century CE

Avalokiteśvara, seeing the countless beings suffering in samsara, vows to free them all. After many eons, he becomes discouraged and his head and arms shatter into pieces. Amitabha comes to his aid, reassembles him with eleven heads and a thousand arms, and encourages him to continue. This story explains the iconography of the thousand-armed Avalokiteśvara and emphasizes the importance of perseverance in compassion.

Guanyin and the FishermanChinese folklore

Tang dynasty (618-907 CE) and later

In a common story, Guanyin appears as a fisherman or a beautiful woman to teach a cruel fisherman compassion. She shows him the suffering of fish and persuades him to give up fishing. The fisherman converts to Buddhism and becomes a monk.

Accounts II
The Legend of Miao ShanChinese Buddhist and folk tradition

Circa 11th-12th century CE

Miao Shan, a princess, defies her father's orders to marry and instead becomes a nun. Her father persecutes her, but she remains steadfast. She later sacrifices her eyes and arms to save her dying father, who is cured and converts to Buddhism. She is then revealed to be a manifestation of Guanyin. This legend explains Guanyin's female form and her association with filial piety, and it became the basis for the most popular Chinese narrative of Guanyin.

Chenrezig and the Six SyllablesTibetan Buddhist tradition

Circa 11th century CE and later

The Buddha Amitabha sends Chenrezig to Tibet to civilize the people. Chenrezig takes the form of a monkey and mates with a rock demoness, producing the first Tibetans. He also teaches them the six-syllable mantra to liberate them. This story establishes Chenrezig as the patron deity of Tibet and the Dalai Lama as his incarnation.

The Descent into HellChinese Buddhist folklore

Ming dynasty (1368-1644 CE) and later

Guanyin descends into the underworld to save the souls of the damned. She is confronted by the King of Hell, but she overcomes him and releases many souls. The King complains to the Jade Emperor, but Guanyin is ultimately victorious. This story reinforces Guanyin's role as a savior of all beings, even in the darkest realms.

Guanyin is a separate goddess unrelated to Avalokiteśvara. — Guanyin is the Chinese name for Avalokiteśvara, the same bodhisattva.

Avalokiteśvara is only a male deity. — In East Asia, he is often depicted as female, especially in Chinese and Japanese traditions.

The mantra 'Om mani padme hum' translates to 'Hail to the jewel in the lotus' and refers to a specific deity. — The mantra's meaning is debated; it is often interpreted as invoking the compassionate one, but the exact translation is not universally agreed upon.

Guanyin is only a folk deity, not a canonical Buddhist figure. — Guanyin is a canonical bodhisattva in Mahayana Buddhism, appearing in major sutras like the Lotus Sutra.
