Who It Is

Imagine the moment a battle turns. The front line holds, spears locked, shields pressed together. Then, a single soldier breaks. He turns and runs, and in that instant, the fear spreads like a contagion. The line collapses, the army becomes a mob, and the rout begins. The Greeks knew this moment so well that they gave it a name and a face: Phobos, the son of Ares, the god of panic. He is not a god of courage or strategy, but of the primal, irrational terror that undoes armies and turns heroes into fugitives. He is the reason we have the word 'phobia'.

Phobos is not a character with a personality in the way that Zeus or Athena are. He is an embodiment of the panic that seizes soldiers in battle. He is described as the force that causes armies to break and flee, the sudden, irrational terror that turns a disciplined phalanx into a mob of fugitives. He is a companion of Ares, and his presence on the battlefield is a sign of impending disaster for one side or the other.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Phobos derives from the Ancient Greek φόβος (phóbos), meaning 'fear', 'panic', 'flight', or 'terror'. It is the root of the modern English word 'phobia'. In Homeric Greek, the word specifically denotes the panic that causes an army to rout and flee, distinct from a general sense of anxiety.

Pronunciation — Ancient Greek: [pʰóbos]; Modern English: /ˈfoʊbɒs/ (FOH-boss)

Aliases — Phobus, Phobos (Greek: Φόβος), The son of Ares, The embodiment of panic

Powers
Personification of Panic

Phobos is the embodiment of the sudden, overwhelming fear that seizes soldiers in battle. His presence can cause an army to break and flee, turning a potential victory into a rout. He is not a god who fights with weapons, but a force that undermines courage and discipline.

Limit: Phobos's power is limited to the psychological realm. He cannot physically harm a foe, but he can make them vulnerable to harm by causing them to flee or freeze. He is also a companion of Ares, not a commander; he acts in support of the war god, not independently.

Aura of Terror

Phobos is described as emitting an aura of terror that can affect those around him. On the Shield of Heracles, he is described as having fire flashing from his eyes and a great noise emanating from his presence, causing even the bravest to tremble.

Limit: This power is not described in detail, and its effects are not systematically defined. It is more of a poetic description of his terrifying nature than a specific ability.

Domains — Phobos's domain is the battlefield, specifically the moment of rout and panic. He is the god of fear, panic, and the flight that follows. He is also associated with the psychological state of terror in general, and his name is the root of the modern word 'phobia'. His realm is not a physical place but a psychological state, the moment when courage fails and reason gives way to primal fear.

Accounts I
The Birth of Phobos and DeimosHesiod, Theogony, line 934; Homer, Iliad, Book 15, lines 119-120

Archaic (8th-7th century BCE)

Phobos and his brother Deimos (Terror) are the sons of Ares, the god of war, and Aphrodite, the goddess of love. This parentage is a clear example of the Greeks' understanding of the close relationship between love, war, and the emotions they generate. The children of this union are not heroes or gods of the Olympian court, but personified forces that accompany their father on the battlefield. Phobos and Deimos become the constant companions of Ares, driving his chariot and spreading panic and terror among the ranks of soldiers. They are the psychological weapons of the war god, the forces that break the spirit of the enemy.

Accounts II
Phobos on the Shield of HeraclesPseudo-Hesiodic, Shield of Heracles, lines 139-144

Archaic (c. 6th century BCE)

The shield of Heracles, described in the pseudo-Hesiodic poem, is adorned with a series of terrifying images, including Phobos and Deimos. The presence of these figures on the shield is meant to terrify the enemy, serving as a psychological weapon before the physical battle even begins. The image of Phobos, with fire flashing from his eyes, is a graphic representation of the power of fear in warfare. The shield becomes a symbol of Heracles' power and the terror he inspires in his foes. The inclusion of Phobos and Deimos on the shield reinforces the idea that fear is a weapon in its own right.

Phobos on the Throne of Zeus at OlympiaPausanias, Description of Greece, Book 5, Chapter 11, Section 8

Classical (5th century BCE); recorded by Pausanias in the 2nd century CE

Pausanias records that the statue of Zeus at Olympia, one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, had Phobos and Deimos painted on the footrest of the throne. This placement, at the feet of the king of the gods, suggests that even Zeus, the supreme deity, was associated with the power of fear. The image of Phobos and Deimos on the throne of Zeus was a reminder that the gods themselves were not immune to the power of terror, and that fear was a fundamental aspect of the divine order. The inclusion of Phobos and Deimos on the throne of Zeus elevates them from minor daimones to figures of cosmic significance. It also reinforces the idea that fear is a tool used by the gods to maintain order and control.

Phobos and Deimos are the horses of Ares. — In some Homeric passages, Phobos and Deimos are described as the horses of Ares, but this is a poetic conflation of the personified deities with the animals that pull the war god's chariot. In most sources, they are his sons.
