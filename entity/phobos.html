Phobos, the son of Ares, who sends cold terror into the hearts of men and makes them flee before the enemy.

Who They Are

Phobos is the god of fear, panic, and terror, especially on the battlefield. He is the son of Ares (War) and Aphrodite (Love), twin brother of Deimos (Terror). Along with his sister, he accompanied his father into battle, spreading fear and confusion among opposing armies. In the Iliad, Phobos and Deimos harness Ares's horses and follow him into combat. Phobos carries a shield carved with the head of a lion or Gorgon to terrify enemies. His name gives us the modern word 'phobia.' He represents not just ordinary fear but the paralyzing, irrational terror that makes soldiers drop their weapons and flee. The Romans identified him as Timor or Pavor. Ares's sacred shield was called the 'Shield of Phobos.' In art, he appears as a young, grim-faced warrior. His influence extends beyond war — he personifies the universal experience of dread that seizes mortals in dangerous moments.

Attested in Hesiod's Theogony / Homer's IliadEra Archaic Greece (c. 700 BCE)

Form

Phobos is rarely described in physical detail in surviving texts. He is more often depicted as a personified force or emotion than a fully realized anthropomorphic deity. In art, he is typically shown as a young, beardless warrior, often alongside his brother Deimos, attending to their father Ares. On the Shield of Heracles, he is described as a figure of terror, with fire flashing from his eyes and a great noise emanating from his presence. He is often depicted with a spear or shield, ready for battle.

Powers

Personification of Panic Phobos is the embodiment of the sudden, overwhelming fear that seizes soldiers in battle. His presence can cause an army to break and flee, turning a potential victory into a rout. He is not a god who fights with weapons, but a force that undermines courage and discipline. Homeric and Hesiodic traditions Phobos's power is limited to the psychological realm. He cannot physically harm a foe, but he can make them vulnerable to harm by causing them to flee or freeze. He is also a companion of Ares, not a commander; he acts in support of the war god, not independently. Some sources describe Phobos as a son of Ares and Aphrodite, while others imply he is a more abstract force, born of Ares alone or even a personification of a natural phenomenon. The line between a personal god and an impersonal force is often blurred in Greek religion.

Aura of Terror Phobos is described as emitting an aura of terror that can affect those around him. On the Shield of Heracles, he is described as having fire flashing from his eyes and a great noise emanating from his presence, causing even the bravest to tremble. Pseudo-Hesiodic Shield of Heracles This power is not described in detail, and its effects are not systematically defined. It is more of a poetic description of his terrifying nature than a specific ability. None noted.

Behavior & Habitat

Phobos is not a character with a personality in the way that Zeus or Athena are. He is an embodiment of the panic that seizes soldiers in battle. He is described as the force that causes armies to break and flee, the sudden, irrational terror that turns a disciplined phalanx into a mob of fugitives. He is a companion of Ares, and his presence on the battlefield is a sign of impending disaster for one side or the other.

Accounts

Phobos and his brother Deimos (Terror) are the sons of Ares, the god of war, and Aphrodite, the goddess of love. This parentage is a clear example of the Greeks' understanding of the close relationship between love, war, and the emotions they generate. The children of this union are not heroes or gods of the Olympian court, but personified forces that accompany their father on the battlefield.

The shield of Heracles, described in the pseudo-Hesiodic poem, is adorned with a series of terrifying images, including Phobos and Deimos. The presence of these figures on the shield is meant to terrify the enemy, serving as a psychological weapon before the physical battle even begins. The image of Phobos, with fire flashing from his eyes, is a graphic representation of the power of fear in warfare.

Pausanias records that the statue of Zeus at Olympia, one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, had Phobos and Deimos painted on the footrest of the throne. This placement, at the feet of the king of the gods, suggests that even Zeus, the supreme deity, was associated with the power of fear. The image of Phobos and Deimos on the throne of Zeus was a reminder that the gods themselves were not immune to the power of terror, and that fear was a fundamental aspect of the divine order.

Modern Interpretations

Astronomy Mars Moon Phobos Asaph Hall (discoverer) 1877 namesake The larger of the two moons of Mars is named Phobos, after the Greek god of fear. The naming was suggested by Henry Madan, based on a suggestion from the Iliad, where Phobos accompanies Ares (Mars) into battle. The moon is a small, irregularly shaped body that orbits Mars at a very close distance, and it is slowly spiraling inward, predicted to eventually crash into the planet or break apart into a ring. Literature Phobos in modern fiction Various 20th-21st century inspired Phobos, as the god of fear, is a common figure in modern fantasy and science fiction. He appears as a character in the Percy Jackson series by Rick Riordan, where he is a minor antagonist. He also appears in the video game Hades by Supergiant Games, where he is a minor character. The name 'Phobos' is also used in various science fiction settings as a name for a spaceship, a planet, or a weapon. Psychology Phobia Modern psychology 19th-21st century reference The English word 'phobia', meaning an irrational fear of a specific object or situation, is derived from the name of Phobos. The term was first used in the late 18th century, and it has since become a standard term in psychology and psychiatry. The connection between the ancient god of panic and the modern clinical term for anxiety is a direct linguistic inheritance. Scholarly Debates
Sons of Ares and Aphrodite vs. Abstract Forces
Phobos and Deimos are the sons of Ares and Aphrodite, as stated by Hesiod in the Theogony. ⇌ Homer does not explicitly name their mother, and in some contexts, they appear as abstract forces or even as the horses of Ares, suggesting a less personal, more impersonal origin.
Evidence for A: Hesiod, Theogony, line 934: 'And Ares, the golden-helmed, sired Deimos and Phobos, terrible gods...'
Evidence for B: Homer, Iliad, Book 15, lines 119-120: Phobos and Deimos are described as the horses of Ares, not as his sons.
Personification vs. Daimon
Phobos is a personal deity, a son of Ares, who can be invoked and worshipped. ⇌ Phobos is an impersonal force, a personification of a psychological state, rather than a fully realized god with a personality and a cult.
Evidence for A: Sophocles, Oedipus Rex, line 190, where the Chorus invokes Phobos to be driven from the land.
Evidence for B: The lack of any known cult or temple dedicated to Phobos, suggesting that he was not worshipped as a personal god.
What We Don't Know

The primary gap in the research is the lack of any surviving cult or temple dedicated to Phobos, which limits our understanding of how he was worshipped, if at all. Additionally, the exact nature of his relationship to Ares — whether he is a son, a horse, or an abstract attribute — is ambiguous in the sources. The visual depictions of Phobos in ancient art are also limited, and it is difficult to determine if he had a standardized iconography.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Phobos not as a god with a personality and a cult, but as the personification of a psychological state: the sudden, overwhelming panic that seizes soldiers in battle. He is the fear that breaks an army, the terror that turns a disciplined phalanx into a mob of fugitives. He is the reason we have the word 'phobia'.
