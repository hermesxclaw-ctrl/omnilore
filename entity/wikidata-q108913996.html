Who It Is

In the Meitei cosmos, even the smallest teeth have a queen. Shapi Leima can call every rat, mouse and burrowing creature in the world to gather at her feet with a single thought — and yet, in legend, this mistress of rodents chose to leave heaven to share one humble mortal husband with her two sisters.

Oral tradition characterizes Shapi Leima and her sisters as dutiful yet willful daughters of heaven who choose exogamy with a mortal against celestial expectation, suggesting agency and affection over obedience. As youngest, she is often portrayed as most gentle or most devoted in popular tellings, but primary summaries do not assign distinct personality traits versus her sisters — differentiation appears to be modern elaboration. Name & Tongue

Etymology — Meiteilon (Manipuri language): 'Shapi' (ꯁꯄꯤ / সপি) = rodents, specifically mice, rats and allied small burrowing mammals; in some transcriptions 'Sapi/Sabi' reflects dialectal and transliteration variation of the same root. 'Leima' (ꯂꯩꯃ / লৈমা) = queen, lady, mistress, goddess; literally 'chief quee

Pronunciation — Meiteilon: /ʃa.pi ləi.ma/ (SHAH-pee LAY-ma); variant Sabi /sa.bi ləi.ma/. Meitei Mayek: ꯁꯄꯤ ꯂꯩꯃ. Bengali-Assamese script: সপি লৈমা / শাপি লৈমা.

Aliases — Shabi Leima, Sapi Leima, Sabi Leima, Shapi Leima

Powers
Absolute Summoning and Command of Rodents

Can summon every rodent in the world to any location at will, and command their actions — to gather, disperse, infest, or withdraw. Rodents obey as subjects to a queen.

Limit: No attested limit stated, but implicitly bound to rodents only — not other animals (birds/fish belong to sisters). No evidence she can create rodents ex nihilo, only summon existing ones. Requires her will/intent; not passive aura.

Celestial Origin and Longevity

As daughter of Salailen (sky god), she is divine (Lai), not mortal; possesses divine longevity, beauty, and ability to move between sky and earth realms.

Limit: While divine, she chooses mortal marriage and appears to live mortal-adjacent life, suggesting voluntary limitation — not omnipotent. No attested immortality feats or combat powers.

Blessing and Blighting of Harvest (Indirect)

Through control of rodents, she can indirectly protect or devastate grain stores and fields. Propitiation of Shapi Leima is folk-logically linked to preventing rodent plagues; anger may manifest as infestation.

Limit: Not a grain goddess herself — Phouoibi holds that portfolio. Shapi Leima's agricultural effect is mediated solely via rodents, not via direct crop fertility.

Shapeshifting / Celestial Descent (Implied)

Ability to leave celestial realm and dwell as human wife, implying transformation or veil of mortal form. Sisters are described as descending to earth and living as human women.

Limit: No detailed transformation description; mechanism unknown. Not described as animal shapeshifting.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No canonical, textually attested iconography for Shapi Leima survives in published primary sources. Unlike pan-Indian goddesses with shastra-prescribed forms, Meitei Leimas in oral tradition are described anthropomorphically as supremely beautiful celestial women (like her sisters), without distinctive rodent features themselves. Rodents are her subjects/vehicles, not her body. Modern devotional art (post-1990s Sanamahi revival) typically depicts

Symbols — Primary symbol: rodents collectively — rats, mice, and burrowing mammals (Meitei 'shapi'). Unlike Ganesha's single mushika, Shapi Leima commands the entire class. No attested yantra, mantra, or fixed emblem in Laihui. In Lai Haraoba ritual, Leimas generally are represented aniconically or through priestess embodiment rather than fixed icons. Modern symbols invented for cultural posters include sheaves of grain with gnawed kernels a

Domains — Domain: all rodents of the world (Meitei cosmology). Habitat: dual — celestial realm of Salailen (sky/heaven, Meitei Nong) as birthplace, and earthly mortal household after marriage. Unlike Ngaleima (water) or Phouoibi (fields), Shapi Leima's earthly habitat is liminal: granaries, fields, household stores, burrows, and village margins where humans and rodents intersect. Cosmology: Meitei universe divided into Nong (sky), Malem (earth), and u

Accounts I
The Birth of the Three Leima Sisters to Salailen SidabaSanamahi Laihui / Salailen genealogy as summarized in Yumlembam Gopi Devi and Wahengbam Ibohal Singh

Mythic time; manuscript tradition codified by 18th-19th century, reflecting earlier oral cosmology

In the Meitei creation cosmology, Salailen Sidaba (also called Soraren), the sky god and progenitor of celestial beings, fathers three daughters who will become the Leima triad: Khunu Leima, Nganu Leima, and Shapi Leima, the youngest. They are raised in the celestial realm (Nong) as divine princesses. Their birth establishes the tripartite division of familiar animals under goddess queens — pigeons/doves, ducks/waterfowl, and rodents — reflecting the Meitei classification of the lived environment. The sisters are inseparable in heaven, foreshadowing their joint fate on earth. Establishes divine kinship structure and explains why these three animal groups have presiding goddesses. Sets narrative premise for descent to earth. Reinforces Salailen's role as father of Lais.

The Descent to Earth — The Goddesses Choose MortalityLukhrabi Macha Fungawari Singbul (oral Phungawari) as transcribed by B. Jayantakumar Sharma (e-pao.net)

Folktale, undated oral antiquity; transcription 2000s

Having heard of or seen the world of humans, the three celestial sisters express desire to experience mortal life. With or without Salailen's explicit blessing (versions differ), they descend from the sky to the earth (Malem). They arrive in the human realm as exquisitely beautiful women, concealing or gently revealing their divine origin. Their descent is not punitive exile but voluntary exploration, a common Meitei motif where Lais test or join humanity. Moves goddesses from celestial to earthly narrative space; enables marriage plot. Illustrates Meitei theme of porous boundaries between Lai and human worlds.

Accounts II
The Marriage to the Same Mortal Man — The Widow's SonLukhrabi Macha Fungawari Singbul (e-pao.net transcription); summarized also in Glimpses of Manipuri Culture

Folktale; reflects pre-modern village society

On earth, the three sisters encounter the same mortal man — the son of a widow (Lukhrabi Macha), a poor but virtuous youth. Despite their celestial status, all three fall in love with or choose to marry him, and he accepts all three as wives. They enter into a polyandrous-in-reverse (polygynous) household: three divine co-wives sharing one human husband. The marriage is presented not as rivalry but as sisterly solidarity; they live together as a joint family. The tale emphasizes the husband's good fortune and the sisters' devotion, while also testing how divinity adapts to mortal poverty and domestic labor. Central charter myth for Shapi Leima's earthly life. Explains her transition from sky goddess to household goddess. Provides etiology for human-rodent cohabitation (through her presence). Establishes moral of female agency and sisterly unity.

Shapi Leima in the Mortal Household — The Mistress of Rodents at WorkDomain-logic episodes inferred from 'mistress of all rodents' epithet and comparative Phungawari motifs; fragmentary references in Yumlembam Gopi Devi and oral commentary

Folkloric, no fixed date

Living as a human wife, Shapi Leima does not abandon her divine portfolio. At will, she demonstrates her power: when the household needs to prove her identity, or when slighted, she summons swarms of rats and mice to fill the granary, courtyard, or fields, then dismisses them. In benevolent tellings, she restrains rodents from eating the family's grain, showing her protective aspect; in cautionary tellings, her displeasure results in sudden infestation. Villagers learn that the presence of rodents is not random but under the governance of a goddess who now lives among them. This episode, though not always narrated as a separate tale, is the functional core of her cult: propitiate Shapi Leima to keep rodents at bay. Etiological explanation for rodent plagues and their control. Justifies folk practices of offering to Shapi Leima before harvest or when storing grain. Differentiates her from Phouoibi (who gives grain) — Shapi can take it away via her subjects.

The Return, Separation, or Quiet Remembrance — Why the Sisters Are Remembered TogetherSynthesized from multiple Phungawari endings and Lai Haraoba oral commentary; no single definitive ending in published transcription

Folkloric

Endings diverge. In one common pattern, after a period of mortal life, the sisters' divine nature becomes untenable — either they are recalled to heaven by Salailen, or they choose to return, leaving the mortal husband bereft but blessed. In another, they remain on earth, their divinity fading into ancestral memory, becoming guardian Lais of their respective animal domains. For Shapi Leima, the outcome is that she withdraws from daily human sight but remains present wherever rodents gather; thus every rustle in the granary recalls her. The triad is thereafter worshipped or remembered together in storytelling, not as isolated goddesses, explaining why Meitei folklore rarely invokes Shapi Leima alone. Provides closure to sky-earth marriage motif and explains current status: goddesses are not dead but withdrawn, accessible through story and ritual. Reinforces triad worship and the idea that rodents, pigeons, and ducks remain under divine queenship.
