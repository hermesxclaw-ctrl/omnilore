Who It Is

Mordred (Medraut, Modred, Medrawt) is the archetypal traitor of Arthurian legend — Arthur's illegitimate son (or nephew, in earlier sources), the usurper who seizes the throne while Arthur fights Lancelot in France, and the knight who dies battling his father at the apocalyptic Battle of Camlann. He is, in Tennyson's words, 'the man they loved the most, who proved to be the traitor.' His role is not merely that of a villain but of a tragic necessity — the agent of the Round Table's inevitable dissolution, the consequence of Arthur's own sins (particularly his incestuous liaison with his half-sister Morgause, which produced Mordred).

Mordred's earliest appearance is in the Welsh Annales Cambriae, which records for the year 537: 'The Battle of Camlann, in which Arthur and Medraut fell.' The terse entry does not specify whether they were enemies or allies — some scholars argue that the earliest Welsh tradition may have had Medraut dying alongside Arthur rather than against him. In Geoffrey of Monmouth's Historia Regum Britanniae (c. 1136), Mordred is Arthur's nephew, left as regent while Arthur campaigns in Gaul. Mordred seizes the crown and queen (Guinevere), and Arthur returns to fight him. By the time of the French Vulgate Cycle and Malory, Mordred is explicitly Arthur's son by incest, a discovery that leads Arthur to order the May Day Massacre — the slaughter of all infants born on May Day in an attempt to kill Mordred. This Herod-like atrocity creates the very enemy it seeks to destroy.

Mordred's character ranges from purely villainous to tragically sympathetic, depending on the version. In Malory, he is a schemer and a rapist (he tries to force Guinevere to marry him). In T.H. White's The Once and Future King, he is a figure of profound tragedy — a child denied love, raised in hatred, who becomes the instrument of his father's destruction not through innate evil but through damage. In modern retellings (Marion Zimmer Bradley, Bernard Cornwell), Mordred is sometimes cast as a religious traditionalist fighting against Christian usurpation of Britain's pagan heritage.

The Battle of Camlann is the end of the Arthurian world. Arthur and Mordred meet in single combat. Arthur runs Mordred through with his spear, but Mordred, in his death-throes, strikes Arthur a mortal blow to the head. 'Thus,' Malory writes, 'they fought until they were both dead.' Arthur is carried to Avalon, and the Round Table — along with the dream of a unified Britain — dissolves into legend. Mordred is the dark twin, the shadow-self: he is what Arthur made, what Arthur refuses to acknowledge, and what Arthur cannot escape.
