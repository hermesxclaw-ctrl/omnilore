Who It Is

She has no temple, no statue, no hymn sung to her alone — and that is exactly how she works. Pseudea is the lie that does not need to shout, the quiet daughter of Strife herself, born not from love but from Night's cold parthenogenesis, one of a brood of miseries Hesiod thought necessary to explain why human words so often fail to match the world.

Attested: As a child of Eris (Strife), Pseudea embodies the lies and false discourses that arise from and perpetuate discord. Hesiod's catalogue implies a passive, generative existence — she is what strife produces, not an agent with independent myths. Disputed: Whether the Pseudologoi are active deceivers or merely the resulting falsehoods themselves; the Greek plural suggests reified utterances rather than a conscious liar. Powers
Personification of Falsehood (Pseudologia)

To embody and generate lying words and false discourses. Not a power she wields but what she is; where she is present, statements diverge from truth and oaths become unreliable.

Limit: No active agency described; she does not choose victims or cast spells. She is a result of Eris, not a cause independent of strife. Cannot be propitiated or warded off by ritual (no cult attested).

Amplification of Strife through Amphillogiai (Disputes)

Closely paired with her sister Amphillogiai (Ambiguous Disputes/Quarrels). The lies she embodies produce endless verbal contention; Hesiod places them consecutively to show lies → disputes → lawlessness (Dysnomia) → ruin (Ate).

Limit: Requires pre-existing Eris/strife; does not create conflict ex nihilo in Hesiodic logic. No direct combat power.

Kinship with Horkos (Oath) - Corruption of Oaths

Her brother Horkos (Oath) punishes perjury; Pseudea's lies are the human act that triggers Horkos's wrath. In Works and Days 282-285, Hesiod warns that Horkos runs alongside crooked judgements. Pseudea thus indirectly brings divine punishment.

Limit: No evidence she can nullify oaths herself; Horkos is the enforcer. Modern conflation with perjury goddess is inaccurate.

No Attested Metamorphic or Martial Power

Unlike Eris, who flies over battlefields, shouts to rouse armies, and appears on shields, Pseudea has no martial, shapeshifting, or divine intervention power attested. She is non-combatant, non-oracular.

Limit: Cannot be invoked for protection, prophecy, or transformation. Any modern game/statblock giving her combat stats is adaptation-specific invention.

Accounts I
The Birth of Night's Grandchildren: How Strife Became a MotherHesiod, Theogony 211-232

c. 700 BCE

After Chaos and Gaia generate the cosmos, Nyx (Night) bears a host of loathsome children without a father, including Eris (Strife). Eris, uniquely among Nyx's children, then bears her own brood without a father: Ponos (Toil), Lethe (Forgetfulness), Limos (Famine), Algea (Pains), Hysminai (Battles), Machai (Wars), Phonoi (Murders), Androktasiai (Manslaughters), Neikea (Quarrels), Pseudologoi/Pseudea (Lies), Amphillogiai (Disputes), Dysnomia (Lawlessness), Ate (Ruin), and Horkos (Oath). The passage is a theogonic catalogue, not a narrative with dialogue, explaining the genealogy of human miseries. Pseudea's birth is listed without episode or exploit — her existence is the story, showing that lies are the offspring of strife. Establishes the Hesiodic etiology of falsehood: lies are not primordial but secondary, born from discord. This genealogy underpins Greek moral thought that truth (Aletheia) is opposed not just by error but by a divine lineage of strife. No further mythic consequences are narrated for Pseudea herself.

The Two Strifes: Which Eris Bears Lies?Hesiod, Works and Days 11-26

c. 700 BCE

Hesiod corrects himself: there are two Erides, not one. The blameworthy Eris fosters evil war and conflict and is loved by no mortal, though men honor her by necessity of the gods. The praiseworthy Eris, placed by Zeus in the roots of the earth, spurs even the idle man to work through healthy competition (potter envies potter). This dichotomy retroactively clarifies that Pseudea belongs to the blameworthy lineage — the Eris born of Night — not the beneficial one. The poet uses this to warn Perses against quarreling in the agora where lies and disputes (Pseudea/Amphillogiai) thrive. Moral-philosophical consequence: Greek ethics distinguishes competitive emulation from deceitful strife. Pseudea is firmly placed on the negative side, explaining why lies are never praised in Hesiod. Scholarly consequence: debate over whether Hesiod invented the second Eris to resolve contradiction.

The Apple of Discord: The Mother's Most Famous CrimeCypria (Epic Cycle, lost, summarized in Proclus Chrestomathy) ; Hyginus Fabulae 92; Lucian Dialogues of the Gods 20

Cypria c. 650 BCE (lost); Proclus summary 2nd c. CE; Hyginus 1st c. BCE-2nd c. CE; Lucian 2nd c. CE

At the wedding of Peleus and Thetis, all gods are invited except Eris. Enraged at being refused entry, she throws a golden apple inscribed 'to the fairest' (tēi kallistēi) among Hera, Athena, and Aphrodite. Zeus orders them to be judged by Paris on Mount Ida; Paris chooses Aphrodite for the bribe of Helen, precipitating the Trojan War. Homer alludes to the Judgement without naming Eris or the apple (Iliad 24.25-30); the Cypria first links Eris to Zeus and Themis's plan to cause war. Pseudea does not appear in the episode, but the story demonstrates the exact strife-environment that, in Hesiodic logic, breeds lies, disputes, and perjured oaths — her domain. The apple itself becomes the archetypal symbol of discord-born deception. Mythic: Trojan War, death of heroes, fall of Troy. Theological: Establishes Eris as active divine agent, unlike her passive children. For Pseudea: illustrates the narrative world where lies flourish — Paris's judgement involves deceptive promises and disputed beauty claims, the precise sphere of Pseudologoi.

Accounts II
Eris on the Battlefield: The Roarer Who Never FightsHomer, Iliad 4.440-445, 5.518-519, 11.3-14, 11.73-74; Hesiod, Shield of Heracles 148-150

c. 750-650 BCE

In the Iliad, Eris is not a combatant like Apollo or Athena but the 'rouser of armies' (4.445: 'Strife who rages incessantly'). She is depicted on Athena's aegis with Phobos, Alke, Ioke, and on Achilles' shield with Kydoimos and Ker, and on Heracles' shield flying over Phobos's head, 'preparing for the battle-rout of men — cruel one, she took away the mind and sense of any men who waged open war.' Zeus sends her to the Achaean ships to shout a terrible war-cry that makes war sweeter than homecoming. She rejoices watching slaughter and is last to leave the field. This establishes the martial strife that, in Hesiod's system, gives birth to Phonoi, Androktasiai, and — in the verbal aftermath — Pseudologoi: the false boasts, deceptive truces, and lying heralds of war. Literary: Codifies Eris as atmospheric, not tactical, deity. For Pseudea: explains why Lies are listed among war-children (Hysminai, Machai) — battlefield lies (false reports, broken truces) are a recognized epic motif (e.g., Dolon, Pandaros's broken oath in Iliad 4).

Horkos and the Punishment of Lies: The Sister Who Avenges Her BrotherHesiod, Works and Days 219-224, 282-285, 804; Theogony 231-232

c. 700 BCE

The last child of Eris is Horkos (Oath), who 'most troubles men upon earth when anyone swears a false oath willingly.' Hesiod warns that when judges give crooked verdicts, Dike (Justice) weeps and Horkos runs beside them, while 30,000 watchers of Zeus note perjury. Though Pseudea is not named in Works and Days, the thematic link is explicit: lies (Pseudea) produce false oaths, which Horkos punishes with ruin (Ate). In Theogony, Horkos is the final, most dreaded child, implying the catalogue culminates in the ultimate consequence of strife-born lies — divine retribution for perjury. This is the closest Hesiod comes to giving Pseudea narrative consequence. Moral: Establishes archaic Greek legal-religious thought where falsehood is not mere error but a theologically punishable offspring of strife. Later law (e.g., Solon) echoes this. No mythic story of Pseudea being punished; she is the condition for Horkos's action.

Apate and Dolos: The Aunt and Cousin of LiesHesiod, Theogony 224 (Apate), 211-232; Nonnus Dionysiaca 8.110-150 (Apate's role); Hyginus Fabulae Prefatio (Dolus)

Hesiod c. 700 BCE; Nonnus 5th c. CE

To understand Pseudea's narrow domain, contrast her with kin: Apate (Deceit), daughter of Nyx and sister of Eris (thus aunt of Pseudea), is the more active trickster who deceives gods and mortals (Nonnus makes her Hera's tool to deceive Semele). Dolos (Trick), son of Erebus or Eris in later sources, is crafty deception. Pseudea/Pseudologoi are blunter: not cunning ruses but plain false statements. No surviving story shows Pseudea deceiving anyone; Apate does. This negative story — the absence of narrative — is itself evidence: Hesiodic personifications of this type rarely get myths, unlike Apate who later gains them. Clarifies taxonomy of falsehood in Greek thought and prevents conflation. Shows Pseudea's obscurity is typical, not exceptional, for Eris's children (most have no myths).

Pseudea is a major Olympian goddess with temples and myths like Athena or Aphrodite. — She is an obscure Hesiodic personification with a single catalogue attestation, no cult, no temple, no independent myth, and no iconography. She is not Olympian but a daimon of the Nyx-Eris lineage.

Pseudea threw the Apple of Discord or caused the Trojan War. — Her mother Eris threw the apple (according to Cypria/Hyginus/Lucian). Pseudea is Eris's daughter and has no role in the Judgement of Paris; the apple story does not mention her.

Pseudea is the same as Apate or Dolos, just a different name for the goddess of lies. — Hesiod distinguishes them genealogically and semantically: Apate (Deceit, daughter of Nyx) is cunning trickery; Dolos (Trick) is craft; Pseudologoi/Pseudea (Lies) are false words, daughters of Eris. They are aunt/niece/cousin, not synonyms.

The Romans worshipped Pseudea as Mendacia. — No Roman cult of Mendacium/Falsum as daughter of Discordia is attested. Discordia is Eris's equivalent, but her children are not systematically mapped in Roman sources. Any Roman name is modern invention.
