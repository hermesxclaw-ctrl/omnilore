Who It Is

Imagine a god who never appears in the stories, who has no face, no epic battles, and no love affairs. His power is not in what he does, but in what he says. He is the divine voice in the darkness of a temple, the final word on matters of life, death, and law. This was Anbay, the oracle god of a lost Arabian kingdom, a deity whose 'myth' is not a tale told, but a decree issued. He is the god of the final verdict, the celestial judge whose silence is more powerful than any thunderbolt.

Anbay is described in inscriptions as a deity who acts, primarily through speech and decree. He is a god who 'commands,' 'proclaims,' and 'reveals.' He is also a protector and healer, invoked for 'well-being' and 'security.' His behavior is that of a divine judge and oracle-giver, a mediator between the divine and human realms, but one who acts with authority and finality.

Powers
Oracular Proclamation

Anbay's primary power was to speak as the mouthpiece of the gods. He could reveal hidden knowledge, divine will, and future events through oracles. This made him a crucial deity for kings and commoners alike, who sought his guidance for important decisions.

Limit: His pronouncements were considered final; there is no evidence of him being biddable or capable of being persuaded to change a decree.

Divine Justice and Decree

He had the power to issue binding decrees, both for individuals and for the state. He could establish laws, settle disputes, and legitimize the rule of kings. His justice was seen as absolute and derived from the highest divine authority.

Limit: His justice was dispensed through his oracles or by the king acting in his name; there is no evidence of him personally intervening in human affairs.

Protection and Healing

Anbay was a protective deity. He was invoked to safeguard people from illness, evil spirits, and misfortune. He was also called upon to protect temples, cities, and trade caravans, essentially serving as a divine guardian for his worshippers.

Limit: His protection was conditional on the piety and proper ritual observance of his devotees.

Form & Signs

Appearance — There are no known anthropomorphic or theriomorphic (animal-form) depictions of Anbay that have survived or been definitively identified. He is not represented in the known corpus of South Arabian art, a common situation for many deities in this region, who were often worshipped in aniconic forms or symbolized by sacred objects, animals, or abstract symbols. This lack of a physical representation makes him a purely textual and symbolic entity.

Symbols — Due to the lack of iconography, his symbols are inferred from texts and context. They may have included: 1) The Staff or Scepter: A symbol of authority and decree. 2) A specific animal, possibly a bull or a snake, which are common symbols of power and fertility in South Arabian religion, though this is speculative. 3) The very concept of 'Proclamation' itself, as his name is believed to be tied to the act of naming or calling.

Accounts I
The Great Oracle of TimnaReconstructed from multiple Qatabanian inscriptions

c. 5th-1st century BCE

In the capital city of Timna, the temple of Anbay was the spiritual and judicial heart of the kingdom. Here, the king would come to seek Anbay's counsel before wars, treaties, and major building projects. Priests would interpret the god's will, which was revealed through various means, possibly including the casting of lots, the observation of sacred animals, or the interpretation of dreams. The king's authority was seen as derived directly from Anbay, and his decrees were considered to have the force of divine law. This story is not a single narrative but a composite picture of his central role in the state religion. Anbay's oracles legitimized the rule of the Qatabanian kings and reinforced the social and political order of the kingdom. His cult was a central pillar of the state.

Accounts II
The Divine ConsortInscriptions invoking the divine pair

c. 1st millennium BCE

Anbay was the consort of Dhat-Badan, the great sun goddess. This union was a central feature of the Qatabanian pantheon. Together, they represented a complementary divine pair: she was the nurturing, life-giving force of the sun, and he was the authoritative, justice-dispensing power of the divine word. Their union was invoked in oaths and dedications, and they were seen as the ultimate divine parents of the kingdom and its people. This divine marriage created a powerful theological framework that legitimized the social hierarchy and the unity of the kingdom.

Anbay is a demon or a minor evil spirit. — Anbay was a major, benevolent god in the South Arabian pantheon, specifically the consort of the sun goddess and a god of oracles and justice. He was a protector and a source of divine guidance.

Anbay is a form of the Egyptian god Anubis. — Anubis is an Egyptian god of mummification and the afterlife. Anbay is a South Arabian god of oracles and justice. They are completely unrelated, with different origins, roles, and cultural contexts. The similarity in their names is coincidental.

We have detailed myths and stories about Anbay. — We do not. Our knowledge of Anbay is almost entirely derived from dedicatory and legal inscriptions. No narrative myths, such as creation stories or heroic tales, have survived. His 'stories' are reconstructed from the context of his worship.
