Who It Is

There is one god in the Greek pantheon whose very existence is a question about you: whether the love you feel will come back. Anteros — the counter-love, the love returned — is Eros' younger twin and his completion, the winged boy who only prospers when desire is answered, and who sharpens his club against those who answer it with cruelty. The Greeks set his story where law and passion meet: his only Athenian altar was raised by resident foreigners after two lovers died leaping from the same rock — one for being scorned, the other for scorning. His presence is the reason the statue in Piccadilly Circus that London calls 'Eros' is secretly his, and why Plato taught that your beloved only calls it friendship while their soul faithfully mirrors every ache you feel.

His double nature is the crux. In the sovereignty reading he is benevolent: he answers love with love, and (per Porphyry/Themis) his mere proximity makes Eros grow — i.e., returned love sustains and matures desire, so the relationship only thrives when both parties love. In the punitive reading he is severe: he avenges those who spurn or mock genuine love, punishing 'those who scorn love and the advances of others' (the metics' altar honours his role in vindicating the spurned Timagoras). Powers
Reciprocation of love (ant-erōs induction)

Anteros causes love to be returned. When a lover genuinely loves, Anteros 'answers' the erōs by making the beloved fall in love in return — the beloved's soul is filled with love in reply (Plato's Phaedrus 255 account of counter-love). The deity is the personification of this mutual dynamic rather than a wielder of a discrete magic: his mere presence is love's answer.

Limit: Reciprocity only operates on genuine, beauty-inspired love; Plato stresses the beloved's return is misnamed 'friendship'. It does not compel one-sided infatuation.

Growth-aura (the Porphyry/Themis mechanic)

Per Porphyrius, whenever Anteros is near his brother Eros, Eros grows; whenever Anteros is away, Eros shrinks back toward his juvenile size. This encodes the theological claim that love only reaches maturity when it is answered — unreturned love (Eros alone, without Anteros) remains stunted.

Limit: The motif only concerns Eros' growth-state; it does not grant Anteros independent power over other gods.

Punitive chastisement of scorners

Anteros avenges unrequited or scorned love. The god punishes those who mock, reject, or take cruel advantage of sincere advances. The metics' altar at Athens was raised to him precisely in this capacity, commemorating how the spurned Timagoras was vindicated after the callous Meles met the same fate.

Limit: Attested chiefly through the single Timagoras/Meles narrative and the god's standard definition; no independent catalogue of punishments survives.

Counter-love / mirror-echo (psychological-metaphoric)

In the philosophical register Anteros is not a physical agent but the name of a psychic process: the beloved's answering affection that mirrors the lover's. Plato describes it as the pain-and-relief mirror-image of the lover's state — 'counter-love'. This reading largely de-mythologises the deity into an abstract principle.

Limit: Purely conceptual; no cult or iconographic props.

Iconographic differentiation (butterfly wings, lead arrows, golden club)

Artistically, Anteros is distinguished from Eros by plumed butterfly wings, long hair, and armament of a golden club or lead arrows. This is less a 'power' than an identity-marking attribute set, but it is his distinct iconographic signature in the Erotes.

Limit: Artistic licence makes the attributes inconsistent across periods; lead vs golden arrows are sometimes swapped.

Accounts I
The birth of counter-love (Ares and Aphrodite)Standard mythographic tradition (Hesiodic-inherited Erotes family; consolidated in later handbooks)

Archaic-to-Classical (attested in consolidated mythographic form by the Hellenistic/Imperial era)

Anteros is born to the illegitimate Olympian lovers Ares and Aphrodite, given as a playmate to his lonely brother Eros. The rationale: love must be answered if it is to prosper — a lonely Eros (unanswered desire) is incomplete. He is also catalogued alongside the other children of that divine marriage, including Harmonia, Phobos, Deimos, and (in some lists) Adrestia and the Erotes. Establishes Anteros as part of the Aphrodite/Eros family and anchors his function as the answer to Eros.

The Themis advice and Eros' growth (Porphyrius)Porphyrius (Porphyry), Neoplatonic allegory, reported in the mythographic tradition

3rd century AD

Aphrodite complained to Themis that Eros remained a perpetual child. Themis advised her to give him a brother. Aphrodite then gave birth to Anteros, and from then on Eros grew whenever Anteros was present, but shrank back to a smaller size whenever he was absent. The lesson is allegorical: love only matures, only 'grows up', when it is reciprocated — unreturned desire languishes in a childlike state. Provides the canonical explanation for why Eros is depicted as a child and why requited love is seen as love's fully-grown form.

Accounts II
The origin from Poseidon and NeritesAelian, On the Characteristics of Animals (De Natura Animalium)

2nd–3rd century AD

In Aelian's account, Nerites — the stunningly beautiful only-brother of the Nereids, son of Nereus and Doris — was loved by the sea-god Poseidon and answered that love. Their mutual love was the very origin of Anteros (mutual/'counter' love). Poseidon made Nerites his charioteer, and the boy drove astonishingly fast; the sun-god Helios, resentful (Aelian conjectures rivalry either in charioteering or in love), transformed Nerites into a sea-snail/shellfish. This is the sole surviving attestation of an alternative, sea-born genealogy for Anteros. Gives Anteros a marine, homoerotic primordial origin distinct from the Ares/Aphrodite birth — an important variant for LGBTQ readings.

The Altar of the Metics: Timagoras and MelesAthenian mythographic tradition (the metics' altar of Anteros)

Classical Athenian (antiquarian record; the altar stood in Athens)

The metics (resident foreigners) of Athens raised an altar to Anteros commemorating the spurned love of Timagoras. Timagoras, a metic, declared his love to the Athenian youth Meles, who mockingly ordered him to throw himself from the top of a tall rock. Timagoras obeyed and died. Conscience-stricken, Meles repented and threw himself from the same rock. The altar hallowed the counter-love as vindication/justice — Anteros as the force that avenges a rejected, deeply sincere love. The single concrete cult act for Anteros and the foundation-myth for his punitive/vindicating function.

The counter-love of the beloved (Phaedrus psychology)Plato, Phaedrus

c. 370 BC

Plato names the mirroring affection of the beloved 'anteros' (counter-love). Inspired by beauty, the lover is filled with divine love and fills the beloved's soul with love in return; the beloved then falls back in love with the lover, though convention calls it only friendship. When apart they suffer, together they are relieved — the exact mirror of the lover's state. This is the philosophical heart of Anteros: reciprocity as the completion of love. Establishes Anteros as a key concept in Platonic erotic theory and in the Neoplatonic tradition, far beyond his cult identity.
