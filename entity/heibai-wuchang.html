Who They Are

Heibai Wuchang — the Black and White Impermanence — are death's messengers in Chinese folk religion: two spectral constables, one robed in white, one in black, who walk the world collecting souls the moment their allotted span runs out. Their origin story is one of the most affecting in any underworld mythology. In life they were Xie Bian and Fan Wujiu, two constables and sworn friends. Caught in a storm, Xie told Fan to wait under a bridge while he fetched an umbrella. The river rose. Fan, refusing to break his word by leaving the meeting place, drowned holding the bridge post; Xie returned, found his friend dead because of his promise, and hanged himself in grief — which is why the White Impermanence is depicted with a long protruding tongue. The underworld, moved by a loyalty stronger than death, commissioned them both. The tall white one wears a hat reading You Too Shall Come; he is oddly benevolent, and gamblers pray to him for luck. The short black one carries the chains. Together they are impermanence itself given two faces: the gentle reminder and the hard arrest.

Origins

Song-era folk religion personified the Buddhist concept of impermanence (wuchang) into paired soul-collectors, then attached the bridge-drowning legend of the two loyal constables — grief and fidelity promoted into cosmic office.

Attested in Taoist-Buddhist folk religionEra Song dynasty onward

Form

White Impermanence: tall, deathly pale, long tongue, mourning-white robes, the fateful inscription on his tall hat. Black Impermanence: short, dark-faced, scowling, chains and warrant in hand.

Soul escort and psychopomp function Attested across textual, ritual, and iconographic sources, Heibai Wuchang’s primary ability is to escort the souls of the dead from the world of the living to the underworld. Bai Wuchang is often said to appear at the moment a person’s allotted lifespan ends, gently guiding virtuous souls and ensuring a peaceful transition. Hei Wuchang apprehends souls who resist, as well as those of criminals and wandering ghosts, using chains and shackles to bind them. Together, they function as psychopomps who cannot be bribed or evaded, embodying the inevitability of death and the moral order of the afterlife. Chinese folk religion and Daoist underworld systems; documented in Wang, Asian Religions at UTK article, and ethnographic studies of Ba Jiajiang and City God Temples. Their authority is bounded by underworld bureaucracy: they act on orders from City Gods, Hell Kings, or fate registers. They do not independently judge souls or alter karmic outcomes. In some stories, exceptionally powerful immortals or deities (such as Sun Wukong in Journey to the West) can resist or evade them, indicating that their power is not absolute over all beings. Some modern popular accounts portray them as omnipotent ‘grim reapers’ who control death itself, but traditional sources emphasize their role as messengers and constables rather than sovereigns of life and death.

Life-span calculation Bai Wuchang is associated with calculating the length of human lives, symbolized by the abacus he carries in Southeast Asian Hokkien practice. He is said to consult registers of fate to determine when a person’s time has come, then issue orders for soul collection. This ability is more symbolic than mechanically described, but it reinforces his role as the administrator of impermanence. Singapore and Malaysia tang-ki traditions; RFIG Deity Lens blog; ethnographic accounts of Toa Ah Pek. This function is conceptual; no attested text describes the exact mechanism of calculation. It is constrained by higher cosmic laws and cannot be altered by personal pleading, though good deeds may influence post-mortem treatment rather than the timing of death. Some narratives attribute life-span calculation to other deities (such as the God of Longevity or the Kitchen God), creating overlapping responsibilities. Scholars generally interpret these overlaps as the result of syncretic folk systems rather than strict doctrinal conflict.

Moral discrimination and punishment Hei Wuchang’s inscriptions and tools indicate a power to distinguish good from evil and to punish wrongdoers. He is said to track down criminals, corrupt officials, and malicious ghosts, binding them and delivering them to hell courts. His presence in Ba Jiajiang troupes often involves exorcistic actions, symbolically beating or capturing malevolent forces. Temple inscriptions, Sutton’s iconographic analysis, Ba Jiajiang ethnography, RFIG Deity Lens blog. Hei Wuchang does not decide the final sentence; he delivers souls to judges. His punitive actions are limited to apprehension and preliminary restraint. In some stories, he cannot act against those protected by higher deities or powerful talismans. Popular tales sometimes exaggerate his cruelty, depicting him as personally torturing souls. Scholarly and ritual sources emphasize his role as an officer rather than executioner, suggesting that such depictions are sensationalized.

Bestowal of fortune and protection Despite their association with death, Heibai Wuchang are worshipped as fortune deities in some regions. Bai Wuchang’s hat inscriptions promising wealth and great fortune, and his gentle demeanor, support the belief that he can grant prosperity and protection to devotees who live ethically. Hei Wuchang, though fearsome, is also invoked to ward off evil spirits and criminal influences, protecting communities through his strict enforcement. City God Temple cults, RFIG Deity Lens blog, Wang’s analysis of their moral symbolism. Their fortune-bestowing role is conditional on moral behavior and ritual propriety. They are not generic wealth gods; their blessings are tied to ethical conduct and respect for underworld order. Some sources emphasize only their death-related functions, downplaying or ignoring their fortune aspect. This creates a tension between their role as feared messengers and as sought-after protectors, which scholars interpret as a reflection of the ambivalence of death in human imagination.

Exorcism and ghost control In Ba Jiajiang troupes and ghost festival rituals, Heibai Wuchang participate in exorcistic performances that symbolically capture, bind, and remove malevolent spirits from the community. Hei Wuchang’s chains and Bai Wuchang’s banners are used to ‘round up’ ghosts, restoring social and spiritual order. Their presence in processions is believed to deter wandering spirits and prevent them from harming the living. Taiwanese Ba Jiajiang ethnography; Asian Religions at UTK article; temple festival documentation. These powers are ritual and symbolic; their effectiveness depends on communal belief and proper performance. They are not described in canonical texts as universal ghost controllers, and other deities (such as City Gods and Marshal spirits) share similar exorcistic roles. Modern skeptics may interpret these rituals as theatrical rather than efficacious, while devotees insist on their spiritual effectiveness. Scholarly accounts generally refrain from adjudicating efficacy, focusing instead on social and symbolic functions.

Weaknesses & Wards

Like all of Diyu's officers, they serve the ledger and can be confounded by its errors; temple lore also softens them with incense, spirit money, and respect.

Accounts

Two court bailiffs, Xie Bi’an and Fan Wujiu, are tasked with recapturing an escaped convict. As they pursue the fugitive, a sudden storm breaks out. Xie tells Fan to wait under Nantai Bridge while he goes to fetch help or an umbrella, depending on the version. Fan, loyal to his friend’s instruction, remains under the bridge despite rising waters. The river floods, and he drowns. When Xie returns and discovers Fan’s death, he is overcome with grief and guilt, believing he has caused his friend’s demise by asking him to wait. Unable to bear this burden, Xie hangs himself. Their unwavering loyalty to each other, even unto death, impresses the underworld authorities. As a result, they are apotheosized as Heibai Wuchang, with Xie becoming Bai Wuchang (White Impermanence) and Fan becoming Hei Wuchang (Black Impermanence).

Early sources mention Wuchang Gui, the Impermanence Ghost, as a singular figure associated with the Buddhist concept of impermanence. This ghost appears in tales where he suddenly arrives to escort the dying, embodying the unpredictability of death. Over time, particularly by late imperial China, this singular ghost is reimagined as a pair: Heibai Wuchang. Bai Wuchang is sometimes identified as the original Wuchang Gui, with Hei Wuchang added to create a yin–yang complementarity and to differentiate functions of reward and punishment. The paired structure aligns with broader Chinese tendencies to personify abstract concepts through complementary figures (e.g., Wen and Wu officials, yin and yang spirits).

In Journey to the West, underworld officials attempt to summon Sun Wukong, the Monkey King, to hell for judgment. Among these officials are figures identified with Heibai Wuchang or similar death messengers. They arrive with warrants and chains, intending to escort Sun Wukong’s soul. However, Sun Wukong resists, fights them, and eventually invades the underworld, erasing his name from the registers of death. This episode dramatizes the tension between cosmic bureaucracy and extraordinary spiritual power, showing that even death messengers can be defied by a sufficiently powerful being.

In many City God Temples, Heibai Wuchang are not only feared as death messengers but also revered as fortune deities. Devotees offer incense, paper money, and food to their statues, asking for protection, prosperity, and successful business. Bai Wuchang’s hat inscriptions promising wealth and great fortune are interpreted literally: seeing him or honoring him is believed to bring luck. Hei Wuchang, though stern, is invoked to ward off thieves, fraudsters, and malevolent spirits. Stories circulate of people who, after sincerely worshipping Heibai Wuchang and reforming their behavior, receive unexpected financial windfalls or escape misfortune, attributed to the deities’ intervention.

Cultural Significance

Their statues flank city-god temples across the Chinese world, and performers walk as the towering white figure in Ghost Festival processions. You Too Shall Come, the hat reads — folk religion's most economical memento mori.

A way to hold it in your mind

Two figures walk the invisible roads of the dying—one tall and pale in mourning white, tongue hanging from a death noose, hat promising fortune to those who meet him; the other short and dark, chains clinking, eyes bulging like a drowned man risen from the river. Together they are Heibai Wuchang, Black and White Impermanence, the constables of China’s underworld bureaucracy. They do not decide who lives or dies; they arrive when the decision has already been written, enforcing a law older than any emperor. Yet their story begins not in hell, but under a bridge in a storm, with a promise kept too faithfully and a friendship that refused to survive alone.
