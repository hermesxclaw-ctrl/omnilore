Who It Is

Two figures walk the invisible roads of the dying—one tall and pale in mourning white, tongue hanging from a death noose, hat promising fortune to those who meet him; the other short and dark, chains clinking, eyes bulging like a drowned man risen from the river. Together they are Heibai Wuchang, Black and White Impermanence, the constables of China’s underworld bureaucracy. They do not decide who lives or dies; they arrive when the decision has already been written, enforcing a law older than any emperor. Yet their story begins not in hell, but under a bridge in a storm, with a promise kept too faithfully and a friendship that refused to survive alone.

In narrative and ritual sources, Heibai Wuchang behave as underworld constables whose personalities reflect their respective functions but remain bound by law. Bai Wuchang is often described as calm, courteous, and even benevolent. He approaches the dying with a kind of resigned compassion, guiding virtuous souls gently to the underworld. His name ‘certainly at peace’ encodes this demeanor. In some stories, he rewards those who perform good deeds, granting them fortune or leniency in the afterlife.

Powers
Soul escort and psychopomp function

Attested across textual, ritual, and iconographic sources, Heibai Wuchang’s primary ability is to escort the souls of the dead from the world of the living to the underworld. Bai Wuchang is often said to appear at the moment a person’s allotted lifespan ends, gently guiding virtuous souls and ensuring a peaceful transition. Hei Wuchang apprehends souls who resist, as well as those of criminals and wandering ghosts, using chains and shackles to bind them. Together, they function as psychopomps who cannot be bribed or evaded, embodying the inevitability of death and the moral order of the afterlife.

Limit: Their authority is bounded by underworld bureaucracy: they act on orders from City Gods, Hell Kings, or fate registers. They do not independently judge souls or alter karmic outcomes. In some stories, exceptionally powerful immortals or deities (such as Sun Wukong in Journey to the West) can resist or evade them, indicating that their power is not absolute over all beings.

Life-span calculation

Bai Wuchang is associated with calculating the length of human lives, symbolized by the abacus he carries in Southeast Asian Hokkien practice. He is said to consult registers of fate to determine when a person’s time has come, then issue orders for soul collection. This ability is more symbolic than mechanically described, but it reinforces his role as the administrator of impermanence.

Limit: This function is conceptual; no attested text describes the exact mechanism of calculation. It is constrained by higher cosmic laws and cannot be altered by personal pleading, though good deeds may influence post-mortem treatment rather than the timing of death.

Moral discrimination and punishment

Hei Wuchang’s inscriptions and tools indicate a power to distinguish good from evil and to punish wrongdoers. He is said to track down criminals, corrupt officials, and malicious ghosts, binding them and delivering them to hell courts. His presence in Ba Jiajiang troupes often involves exorcistic actions, symbolically beating or capturing malevolent forces.

Limit: Hei Wuchang does not decide the final sentence; he delivers souls to judges. His punitive actions are limited to apprehension and preliminary restraint. In some stories, he cannot act against those protected by higher deities or powerful talismans.

Bestowal of fortune and protection

Despite their association with death, Heibai Wuchang are worshipped as fortune deities in some regions. Bai Wuchang’s hat inscriptions promising wealth and great fortune, and his gentle demeanor, support the belief that he can grant prosperity and protection to devotees who live ethically. Hei Wuchang, though fearsome, is also invoked to ward off evil spirits and criminal influences, protecting communities through his strict enforcement.

Limit: Their fortune-bestowing role is conditional on moral behavior and ritual propriety. They are not generic wealth gods; their blessings are tied to ethical conduct and respect for underworld order.

Exorcism and ghost control

In Ba Jiajiang troupes and ghost festival rituals, Heibai Wuchang participate in exorcistic performances that symbolically capture, bind, and remove malevolent spirits from the community. Hei Wuchang’s chains and Bai Wuchang’s banners are used to ‘round up’ ghosts, restoring social and spiritual order. Their presence in processions is believed to deter wandering spirits and prevent them from harming the living.

Limit: These powers are ritual and symbolic; their effectiveness depends on communal belief and proper performance. They are not described in canonical texts as universal ghost controllers, and other deities (such as City Gods and Marshal spirits) share similar exorcistic roles.

Accounts I
Legend of the Nantai BridgeRegional Fuzhou legend; summarized in Lim, Wang, and Ōya

Late imperial (exact dating uncertain; likely Ming–Qing transmission)

Two court bailiffs, Xie Bi’an and Fan Wujiu, are tasked with recapturing an escaped convict. As they pursue the fugitive, a sudden storm breaks out. Xie tells Fan to wait under Nantai Bridge while he goes to fetch help or an umbrella, depending on the version. Fan, loyal to his friend’s instruction, remains under the bridge despite rising waters. The river floods, and he drowns. When Xie returns and discovers Fan’s death, he is overcome with grief and guilt, believing he has caused his friend’s demise by asking him to wait. Unable to bear this burden, Xie hangs himself. Their unwavering loyalty to each other, even unto death, impresses the underworld authorities. As a result, they are apotheosized as Heibai Wuchang, with Xie becoming Bai Wuchang (White Impermanence) and Fan becoming Hei Wuchang (Black Impermanence). The deaths of Xie and Fan establish their moral character as loyal and self-sacrificing. Wuchang Gui and the evolution into Heibai WuchangŌya’s analysis of Song-dynasty materials and later folk religion

Song dynasty onward

Early sources mention Wuchang Gui, the Impermanence Ghost, as a singular figure associated with the Buddhist concept of impermanence. This ghost appears in tales where he suddenly arrives to escort the dying, embodying the unpredictability of death. Over time, particularly by late imperial China, this singular ghost is reimagined as a pair: Heibai Wuchang. Bai Wuchang is sometimes identified as the original Wuchang Gui, with Hei Wuchang added to create a yin–yang complementarity and to differentiate functions of reward and punishment. The paired structure aligns with broader Chinese tendencies to personify abstract concepts through complementary figures (e.g., Wen and Wu officials, yin and yang spirits). The transformation from a singular ghost to a pair allows for richer narrative and ritual elaboration. Heibai Wuchang become more visually striking and morally nuanced, capable of representing both gentle guidance and stern enforcement. This evolution also facilitates their integration into Daoist underworld bureaucracy, where paired officials are common.

Accounts II
Heibai Wuchang in Journey to the West16th-century novel ‘Journey to the West’ (Xiyou Ji)

Ming dynasty

In Journey to the West, underworld officials attempt to summon Sun Wukong, the Monkey King, to hell for judgment. Among these officials are figures identified with Heibai Wuchang or similar death messengers. They arrive with warrants and chains, intending to escort Sun Wukong’s soul. However, Sun Wukong resists, fights them, and eventually invades the underworld, erasing his name from the registers of death. This episode dramatizes the tension between cosmic bureaucracy and extraordinary spiritual power, showing that even death messengers can be defied by a sufficiently powerful being. The story reinforces Heibai Wuchang’s status as mid-level officials: they have authority over ordinary beings but can be overpowered by exceptional immortals. It also popularizes the image of underworld constables with chains and warrants, contributing to later visualizations of Heibai Wuchang.

Heibai Wuchang as fortune deitiesCity God Temple cult narratives and modern religious practice

Late imperial to contemporary

In many City God Temples, Heibai Wuchang are not only feared as death messengers but also revered as fortune deities. Devotees offer incense, paper money, and food to their statues, asking for protection, prosperity, and successful business. Bai Wuchang’s hat inscriptions promising wealth and great fortune are interpreted literally: seeing him or honoring him is believed to bring luck. Hei Wuchang, though stern, is invoked to ward off thieves, fraudsters, and malevolent spirits. Stories circulate of people who, after sincerely worshipping Heibai Wuchang and reforming their behavior, receive unexpected financial windfalls or escape misfortune, attributed to the deities’ intervention. These narratives expand Heibai Wuchang’s role beyond death, integrating them into everyday concerns of livelihood and safety. They reinforce the moral message that ethical living attracts divine favor, while wrongdoing invites punishment. The dual nature of the pair—fortune and fear—makes them powerful tools for social regulation.
