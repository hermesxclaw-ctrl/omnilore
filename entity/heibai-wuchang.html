Etymology

The collective name 黑白無常 (Hēibái Wúcháng) combines 黑 (hēi, black) and 白 (bái, white) with 無常 (wúcháng), a term that entered Chinese largely through Buddhist discourse as the translation of Sanskrit anitya, ‘impermanence’. In Buddhist teaching, wuchang denotes the transient, conditioned nature of all phenomena; in folk religion this abstraction is personified as a ghostly official who enforces the inevitability of death. Over time, the singular ‘Impermanence Ghost’ (無常鬼, Wuchang Gui) diversified into a paired configuration, Black and White Impermanence, whose colors evoke yin–yang polarity, funerary symbolism (white as mourning color in Chinese culture), and bureaucratic differentiation of roles (reward vs punishment). The personal names 謝必安 (Xiè Bì’ān) and 范無救 (Fàn Wújiù) are themselves theological: ‘certainly at peace’ and ‘no rescue’, encoding acceptance of fate and the impossibility of escaping death.

Cultural Ownership

Heibai Wuchang belong primarily to the sphere of Chinese folk religion and Daoist ritual culture, with strong Buddhist conceptual influence. Their cult is especially prominent in southern China (Fujian, Guangdong), Taiwan, and among Hokkien and Teochew diaspora communities in Southeast Asia, where they appear in temple processions, Ba Jiajiang (Eight Generals) troupes, and ghost festival rituals. They are integrated into the bureaucratic underworld systems of Daoism and popular religion, serving under City Gods (城隍, Chenghuang), the Ten Kings of Hell, and sometimes the Fengdu Emperor. While rooted in Han Chinese religious imagination, local variants in Taiwan, Singapore, and Malaysia have developed distinctive iconography and ritual practices, indicating shared but regionally adapted ownership.

Disputed Identifications

Scholarly and folk traditions disagree on whether Heibai Wuchang originated as a single ghostly figure (Wuchang Gui) later split into two, or as a pair from the outset. Some sources identify Wuchang Gui specifically with Bai Wuchang, suggesting that Black Impermanence was a later addition; others treat Wuchang Gui as a generic category of impermanence ghosts. There is also variation regarding their mortal origins: some legends present Xie Bi’an and Fan Wujiu as court bailiffs from Fuzhou; others as local constables or even generals. In modern popular culture, Heibai Wuchang are sometimes conflated with other psychopomp figures such as Ox-Head and Horse-Face (Niutou Mamian), but in ritual and scholarly descriptions they remain distinct officials with different ranks and functions.

Archive Dossier — Approach

Two figures walk the invisible roads of the dying—one tall and pale in mourning white, tongue hanging from a death noose, hat promising fortune to those who meet him; the other short and dark, chains clinking, eyes bulging like a drowned man risen from the river. Together they are Heibai Wuchang, Black and White Impermanence, the constables of China’s underworld bureaucracy. They do not decide who lives or dies; they arrive when the decision has already been written, enforcing a law older than any emperor. Yet their story begins not in hell, but under a bridge in a storm, with a promise kept too faithfully and a friendship that refused to survive alone.

Who They Are

Heibai Wuchang — the Black and White Impermanence — are death's messengers in Chinese folk religion: two spectral constables, one robed in white, one in black, who walk the world collecting souls the moment their allotted span runs out. Their origin story is one of the most affecting in any underworld mythology. In life they were Xie Bian and Fan Wujiu, two constables and sworn friends. Caught in a storm, Xie told Fan to wait under a bridge while he fetched an umbrella. The river rose. Fan, refusing to break his word by leaving the meeting place, drowned holding the bridge post; Xie returned, found his friend dead because of his promise, and hanged himself in grief — which is why the White Impermanence is depicted with a long protruding tongue. The underworld, moved by a loyalty stronger than death, commissioned them both. The tall white one wears a hat reading You Too Shall Come; he is oddly benevolent, and gamblers pray to him for luck. The short black one carries the chains. Together they are impermanence itself given two faces: the gentle reminder and the hard arrest.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

Attested descriptions portray Heibai Wuchang as a strikingly asymmetrical pair of male underworld officials. Bai Wuchang (White Impermanence, Xie Bi’an, Seventh Lord) is typically depicted as extraordinarily tall and thin, clad in flowing white robes that emphasize his spectral stature. His face is pale, sometimes corpse-like, with a long tongue protruding from his mouth—a visual reference to death by hanging. He wears a tall, narrow hat, often of white paper or cloth, inscribed with auspicious phrases such as ‘一見大吉’ (Great fortune upon seeing me) or ‘一見生財’ (Wealth comes upon seeing me). In Southeast Asian Hokkien contexts, the tang-ki embodying Toa Ah Pek may stand on stilts to exaggerate his height and carry an abacus, symbolizing his role in calculating the length of human lives.

Hei Wuchang (Black Impermanence, Fan Wujiu, Eighth Lord) contrasts sharply: he is short, stocky, and sometimes depicted as bloated, with dark or blackened skin and bulging eyes. His appearance evokes drowning and the discoloration of a waterlogged corpse. He wears black robes and a squarer hat or headpiece, sometimes bearing inscriptions such as ‘天下太平’ (Peace under heaven) or moral slogans about distinguishing good from evil. He often carries chains, shackles, or a wooden board inscribed with ‘分明善惡’ (I distinguish good and bad), emphasizing his punitive function. In processions, Ji Ah Pek may be portrayed by a medium with heavy makeup, iron chains, and a fierce expression.

Both figures are usually shown together, flanking a doorway, altar, or City God, reinforcing their inseparability. Their bodily exaggerations—height vs shortness, pallor vs darkness, tongue vs bloatedness—are attested in temple art, ethnographic descriptions, and scholarly analyses. Some modern adaptations simplify their features for popular media, but the core contrast remains. Where depictions diverge (for example, omitting the tongue or changing inscriptions), these are adaptation-specific and not universally attested.

Symbols & Iconography

Heibai Wuchang’s iconography is dense with symbolic elements. Bai Wuchang’s white robe and mourning color align him with funerary rites and the gentle escort of souls; his tall hat, inscribed with auspicious phrases, signals his capacity to bring fortune to the virtuous. The abacus carried by Toa Ah Pek in Singapore and Malaysia is a localized symbol of life calculation, suggesting that he tallies the allotted lifespan of each person. His long tongue is both a sign of his own death by hanging and a reminder of the thin line between life and death.

Hei Wuchang’s black robe and dark complexion embody severity, punishment, and the shadow side of impermanence. Chains, shackles, and iron implements in his hands mark him as an enforcer who apprehends evil souls and wandering ghosts. The inscription ‘Peace under heaven’ on his hat can be read as a promise that strict enforcement of moral order ultimately produces social harmony. The board with ‘I distinguish good and bad’ explicitly states his role as a moral discriminator.

Together, the pair visually enact yin–yang complementarity: white/yang/right side vs black/yin/left side, as noted in temple arrangements where Bai stands on the right (yang) and Hei on the left (yin). Their duality also symbolizes the two possible encounters with death: a peaceful transition for those who have lived well, and a terrifying arrest for those who have committed serious wrongs. In Ba Jiajiang troupes, their costumes are among the most recognizable, with exaggerated hats, painted faces, and props that dramatize their roles as soul escorts and exorcists.

Attested iconography consistently emphasizes their bureaucratic status: they wear official-style robes, sometimes with badges or insignia, and stand in hierarchical relation to City Gods and Hell Kings. Hypothetical reconstructions that portray them as purely monstrous or animalistic lack support in ritual and artistic evidence.

Behaviour & Personality

In narrative and ritual sources, Heibai Wuchang behave as underworld constables whose personalities reflect their respective functions but remain bound by law. Bai Wuchang is often described as calm, courteous, and even benevolent. He approaches the dying with a kind of resigned compassion, guiding virtuous souls gently to the underworld. His name ‘certainly at peace’ encodes this demeanor. In some stories, he rewards those who perform good deeds, granting them fortune or leniency in the afterlife.

Hei Wuchang, by contrast, is stern, intimidating, and uncompromising. He hunts down evil-doers, criminals, and unruly ghosts, using chains and shackles to bind them. His name ‘no rescue’ underscores the inevitability of punishment for those who have crossed moral boundaries. Yet he is not portrayed as sadistic; rather, he is a strict enforcer of cosmic law. In some Southeast Asian accounts, devotees fear his appearance but also respect him as a necessary guardian who keeps dangerous spirits in check.

As a pair, they embody loyalty and mutual devotion. Their origin legend emphasizes their willingness to keep promises even unto death, and their posthumous appointment as underworld officials is framed as a reward for this loyalty. In temple narratives, they are said to work in concert: Bai calculates and summons, Hei apprehends and escorts. They do not act capriciously; their behavior is constrained by registers of fate and orders from higher authorities such as City Gods and Hell Kings.

Adaptation-specific portrayals in modern media sometimes exaggerate their cruelty or turn them into comic figures, but these are creative reinterpretations rather than attested traditional behavior. Where sources differ on their emotional tone—gentle vs frightening—this reflects the dual nature of death itself and the moral status of the person encountering them.

Domains & Habitats

Heibai Wuchang operate at the boundary between the world of the living and the underworld. Their primary domain is the roads of the dying and the bureaucratic corridors of Diyu, the Chinese hell or underworld conceived as a complex administrative system. They are frequently described as patrolling city streets at night, appearing at deathbeds, and traveling along invisible routes that connect households to underworld courts.

Within Diyu, they are subordinate officials under City Gods (Chenghuang), the Ten Kings of Hell, and sometimes the Fengdu Emperor. Their ‘habitat’ includes courtrooms where souls are judged, holding cells where ghosts await sentencing, and liminal spaces such as bridges and riverbanks that symbolize transitions. The Nantai Bridge in their origin legend is itself a liminal site, marking the crossing between safety and danger, life and death.

In ritual practice, their presence is localized in City God Temples, underworld shrines, and Ba Jiajiang performance spaces. They are invoked during ghost festivals (such as the Hungry Ghost Festival) to guide wandering souls, maintain order among ghosts, and protect communities from spiritual harm. Their domain is thus both cosmic and communal: they belong to the grand architecture of the afterlife and to the everyday religious life of neighborhoods.

Attested sources do not describe them as rulers of specific hell levels; rather, they are transit officers whose jurisdiction is movement and capture. Hypothetical claims that they preside over particular torture chambers or reincarnation wheels lack clear textual support and should be treated as modern extrapolations.

Version Differences

Across regions and periods, several version differences emerge. Historically, Wuchang Gui appears as a singular figure in Song-dynasty contexts, likely associated with Bai Wuchang. Later, especially in late imperial and modern folk religion, the paired Heibai Wuchang become standard. Some traditions emphasize their mortal identities as bailiffs from Fuzhou; others generalize them as anonymous constables.

In Taiwan and southern China, they are integrated into Ba Jiajiang troupes with elaborate costumes and specific choreographies; in Singapore and Malaysia, Toa Ah Pek and Ji Ah Pek are embodied by tang-ki mediums who perform possession rituals, with localized props such as the abacus. Mainland Chinese depictions may omit the abacus and focus more on their roles in City God Temples.

The inscriptions on their hats and boards vary: ‘Great fortune whenever I appear’ vs ‘Wealth comes upon seeing me’ for Bai; ‘Peace under heaven’ vs ‘I distinguish good and bad’ for Hei. These differences reflect regional creativity rather than doctrinal contradiction. Some modern sources conflate them with Ox-Head and Horse-Face or treat them as generic ‘grim reapers’, but scholarly analyses maintain their distinct identity.

There is also variation in how strongly they are associated with Daoism vs Buddhism. Some scholars emphasize their Buddhist conceptual origin (impermanence), while others highlight their integration into Daoist underworld bureaucracy. Popular religion tends to blur these boundaries, treating them simply as underworld officials. Where sources disagree on their exact rank—under City God vs under Fengdu Emperor vs under Ten Kings—this reflects the fluidity of Chinese religious systems rather than clear hierarchy.

Origins

Song-era folk religion personified the Buddhist concept of impermanence (wuchang) into paired soul-collectors, then attached the bridge-drowning legend of the two loyal constables — grief and fidelity promoted into cosmic office.

Archive Dossier — Powers

Soul escort and psychopomp function

Source Version Chinese folk religion and Daoist underworld systems; documented in Wang, Asian Religions at UTK article, and ethnographic studies of Ba Jiajiang and City God Temples.

Description

Attested across textual, ritual, and iconographic sources, Heibai Wuchang’s primary ability is to escort the souls of the dead from the world of the living to the underworld. Bai Wuchang is often said to appear at the moment a person’s allotted lifespan ends, gently guiding virtuous souls and ensuring a peaceful transition. Hei Wuchang apprehends souls who resist, as well as those of criminals and wandering ghosts, using chains and shackles to bind them. Together, they function as psychopomps who cannot be bribed or evaded, embodying the inevitability of death and the moral order of the afterlife.

Limitations

Their authority is bounded by underworld bureaucracy: they act on orders from City Gods, Hell Kings, or fate registers. They do not independently judge souls or alter karmic outcomes. In some stories, exceptionally powerful immortals or deities (such as Sun Wukong in Journey to the West) can resist or evade them, indicating that their power is not absolute over all beings.

Contradictions

Some modern popular accounts portray them as omnipotent ‘grim reapers’ who control death itself, but traditional sources emphasize their role as messengers and constables rather than sovereigns of life and death.

Life-span calculation

Source Version Singapore and Malaysia tang-ki traditions; RFIG Deity Lens blog; ethnographic accounts of Toa Ah Pek.

Description

Bai Wuchang is associated with calculating the length of human lives, symbolized by the abacus he carries in Southeast Asian Hokkien practice. He is said to consult registers of fate to determine when a person’s time has come, then issue orders for soul collection. This ability is more symbolic than mechanically described, but it reinforces his role as the administrator of impermanence.

Limitations

This function is conceptual; no attested text describes the exact mechanism of calculation. It is constrained by higher cosmic laws and cannot be altered by personal pleading, though good deeds may influence post-mortem treatment rather than the timing of death.

Contradictions

Some narratives attribute life-span calculation to other deities (such as the God of Longevity or the Kitchen God), creating overlapping responsibilities. Scholars generally interpret these overlaps as the result of syncretic folk systems rather than strict doctrinal conflict.

Moral discrimination and punishment

Source Version Temple inscriptions, Sutton’s iconographic analysis, Ba Jiajiang ethnography, RFIG Deity Lens blog.

Description

Hei Wuchang’s inscriptions and tools indicate a power to distinguish good from evil and to punish wrongdoers. He is said to track down criminals, corrupt officials, and malicious ghosts, binding them and delivering them to hell courts. His presence in Ba Jiajiang troupes often involves exorcistic actions, symbolically beating or capturing malevolent forces.

Limitations

Hei Wuchang does not decide the final sentence; he delivers souls to judges. His punitive actions are limited to apprehension and preliminary restraint. In some stories, he cannot act against those protected by higher deities or powerful talismans.

Contradictions

Popular tales sometimes exaggerate his cruelty, depicting him as personally torturing souls. Scholarly and ritual sources emphasize his role as an officer rather than executioner, suggesting that such depictions are sensationalized.

Bestowal of fortune and protection

Source Version City God Temple cults, RFIG Deity Lens blog, Wang’s analysis of their moral symbolism.

Description

Despite their association with death, Heibai Wuchang are worshipped as fortune deities in some regions. Bai Wuchang’s hat inscriptions promising wealth and great fortune, and his gentle demeanor, support the belief that he can grant prosperity and protection to devotees who live ethically. Hei Wuchang, though fearsome, is also invoked to ward off evil spirits and criminal influences, protecting communities through his strict enforcement.

Limitations

Their fortune-bestowing role is conditional on moral behavior and ritual propriety. They are not generic wealth gods; their blessings are tied to ethical conduct and respect for underworld order.

Contradictions

Some sources emphasize only their death-related functions, downplaying or ignoring their fortune aspect. This creates a tension between their role as feared messengers and as sought-after protectors, which scholars interpret as a reflection of the ambivalence of death in human imagination.

Exorcism and ghost control

Source Version Taiwanese Ba Jiajiang ethnography; Asian Religions at UTK article; temple festival documentation.

Description

In Ba Jiajiang troupes and ghost festival rituals, Heibai Wuchang participate in exorcistic performances that symbolically capture, bind, and remove malevolent spirits from the community. Hei Wuchang’s chains and Bai Wuchang’s banners are used to ‘round up’ ghosts, restoring social and spiritual order. Their presence in processions is believed to deter wandering spirits and prevent them from harming the living.

Limitations

These powers are ritual and symbolic; their effectiveness depends on communal belief and proper performance. They are not described in canonical texts as universal ghost controllers, and other deities (such as City Gods and Marshal spirits) share similar exorcistic roles.

Contradictions

Modern skeptics may interpret these rituals as theatrical rather than efficacious, while devotees insist on their spiritual effectiveness. Scholarly accounts generally refrain from adjudicating efficacy, focusing instead on social and symbolic functions.

Form

White Impermanence: tall, deathly pale, long tongue, mourning-white robes, the fateful inscription on his tall hat. Black Impermanence: short, dark-faced, scowling, chains and warrant in hand.

Powers

Soul-collection at the ledgered hour; reward and punishment in miniature — the white one bestows sudden wealth on the kind, the black one drags the cruel.

Weaknesses & Wards

Like all of Diyu's officers, they serve the ledger and can be confounded by its errors; temple lore also softens them with incense, spirit money, and respect.

Type creatorSource Legend of the Nantai Bridge; Wang; Sutton; Asian Religions at UTK article.

Fan Wujiu is the mortal identity of Hei Wuchang. In the Nantai Bridge legend, he drowns while faithfully waiting for Xie under the bridge. His death by drowning shapes his later iconography as Black Impermanence, with bloated body and dark skin. His name ‘no rescue’ underscores the inevitability of death and the impossibility of saving those whose time has come.

A minority of tales reverse the deaths, suggesting Xie drowned and Fan hanged himself, but these are not mainstream and may reflect local storytelling variations.

Heibai Wuchang serve under the City God in many local religious systems. They are described as his constables or officers, tasked with apprehending souls and ghosts within the city’s jurisdiction and delivering them to the City God’s court. In temple iconography, they often flank the City God or appear in side shrines within City God Temples.

Version Note

Attested in southern Chinese and Taiwanese contexts; not universal in all regions.

Conflicting Accounts

Some systems place them directly under the Ten Kings of Hell or the Fengdu Emperor, bypassing the City God. Scholars interpret these differences as reflecting local pantheon structures.

In Daoist and popular underworld cosmology, the Ten Kings of Hell judge the fate of souls. Heibai Wuchang are described as their messengers, bringing souls to their courts for sentencing. They do not participate in judgment but ensure that no soul escapes the process.

Some Buddhist-leaning accounts focus more on Yama (Yanluo Wang) as the primary judge, with Heibai Wuchang serving under him rather than under a full set of ten kings.

Some more orthodox Daoist or Buddhist communities may view tang-ki possession with suspicion, preferring scriptural rituals. This creates tension over the proper mode of engaging Heibai Wuchang.

Suicide and self-sacrifice

Source Legend of the Nantai Bridge; Wang; Lim; Ōya.

Description

The origin legend of Heibai Wuchang centers on Fan Wujiu’s drowning and Xie Bi’an’s suicide by hanging. While framed as acts of loyalty and guilt, these deaths involve self-destruction under emotional duress. The narrative has been used to valorize self-sacrifice and unwavering obedience, which can be problematic when interpreted as endorsing extreme self-harm for the sake of duty.

Scholarly Context

Religious studies scholars note that such legends reflect traditional values of loyalty and righteousness but must be read critically in modern contexts where suicide is recognized as a serious mental health issue. The story’s function is to explain apotheosis and moral character, not to prescribe behavior, yet its emotional power can influence cultural attitudes toward self-sacrifice.

Underworld punishment and torture imagery

Source Daoist and folk hell descriptions; temple art surveys; Journey to the West episodes.

Description

Heibai Wuchang operate within a hell system that includes graphic punishments for sinners: torture chambers, dismemberment, burning, and other forms of suffering. While they are not the primary executioners, their role in escorting souls to these punishments implicates them in a violent cosmology. Temple murals and popular literature often depict them leading bound souls past scenes of torment.

Scholarly Context

Scholars interpret these images as didactic tools designed to deter wrongdoing by vividly portraying consequences. They also note that such imagery can reinforce fear-based religious adherence and may be used to control social behavior through threats of post-mortem violence.

Coercive moral enforcement

Source Folk tales; temple sermons; ethnographic accounts of moral instruction using underworld imagery.

Description

Heibai Wuchang’s dual role as fortune-bringers and punishers creates a system where religious devotion and moral behavior are enforced through fear of death and punishment. Stories of them capturing corrupt officials or punishing those who neglect ritual obligations can be read as reinforcing hierarchical and patriarchal social orders, where obedience to authority is paramount.

Scholarly Context

Critical analyses highlight how underworld officials like Heibai Wuchang are used in sermons and community narratives to legitimize existing power structures, including patriarchal family systems and state authority. While they also protect the vulnerable by punishing criminals, the same imagery can be mobilized to suppress dissent or nonconformity.

Colonial and modern distortions

Source Missionary reports; early ethnographic writings; modern horror films and games.

Description

In colonial and modern contexts, Western observers sometimes exoticized Heibai Wuchang as ‘Chinese grim reapers’, reducing complex religious figures to caricatures of death. Missionary literature occasionally portrayed them as demonic or superstitious, contributing to colonial narratives that denigrated Chinese religious practices. Modern media adaptations may further distort their roles, emphasizing horror elements while ignoring their moral and protective functions.

Scholarly Context

Postcolonial scholarship critiques these distortions, arguing that they reflect broader patterns of Orientalism and cultural misunderstanding. Contemporary Chinese and diaspora communities often respond by reasserting the ethical and communal significance of Heibai Wuchang, resisting purely negative portrayals.

Archive Dossier — Stories

Legend of the Nantai Bridge

Source Regional Fuzhou legend; summarized in Lim, Wang, and ŌyaCulture Chinese (Fuzhou, Fujian)Period Late imperial (exact dating uncertain; likely Ming–Qing transmission)

Summary

Two court bailiffs, Xie Bi’an and Fan Wujiu, are tasked with recapturing an escaped convict. As they pursue the fugitive, a sudden storm breaks out. Xie tells Fan to wait under Nantai Bridge while he goes to fetch help or an umbrella, depending on the version. Fan, loyal to his friend’s instruction, remains under the bridge despite rising waters. The river floods, and he drowns. When Xie returns and discovers Fan’s death, he is overcome with grief and guilt, believing he has caused his friend’s demise by asking him to wait. Unable to bear this burden, Xie hangs himself. Their unwavering loyalty to each other, even unto death, impresses the underworld authorities. As a result, they are apotheosized as Heibai Wuchang, with Xie becoming Bai Wuchang (White Impermanence) and Fan becoming Hei Wuchang (Black Impermanence).

Participants

Xie Bi’an (later Bai Wuchang)

Fan Wujiu (later Hei Wuchang)

Escaped convict (minor figure)

Underworld authorities (implicit)

Consequences

The deaths of Xie and Fan establish their moral character as loyal and self-sacrificing. Their apotheosis into underworld officials provides a narrative foundation for their cult, framing them as exemplars of friendship and duty. The story also sacralizes Nantai Bridge as a site of tragic loyalty and liminality. In religious imagination, their origin explains why they are entrusted with escorting souls: they themselves crossed the boundary between life and death through acts of fidelity.

Variants

Some versions emphasize Xie’s guilt and present his hanging as an act of atonement; others stress Fan’s obedience and depict him as a martyr to loyalty. The details of the storm and the escaped convict vary. In certain retellings, Xie goes to fetch an umbrella; in others, he seeks reinforcements. The core elements—waiting under the bridge, drowning, hanging, and apotheosis—remain consistent. Scholars note that the legend likely crystallized from earlier, more diffuse stories about loyal officials rewarded after death.

Wuchang Gui and the evolution into Heibai Wuchang

Source Ōya’s analysis of Song-dynasty materials and later folk religionCulture Chinese (Buddhist-Daoist syncretic)Period Song dynasty onward

Summary

Early sources mention Wuchang Gui, the Impermanence Ghost, as a singular figure associated with the Buddhist concept of impermanence. This ghost appears in tales where he suddenly arrives to escort the dying, embodying the unpredictability of death. Over time, particularly by late imperial China, this singular ghost is reimagined as a pair: Heibai Wuchang. Bai Wuchang is sometimes identified as the original Wuchang Gui, with Hei Wuchang added to create a yin–yang complementarity and to differentiate functions of reward and punishment. The paired structure aligns with broader Chinese tendencies to personify abstract concepts through complementary figures (e.g., Wen and Wu officials, yin and yang spirits).

Consequences

The transformation from a singular ghost to a pair allows for richer narrative and ritual elaboration. Heibai Wuchang become more visually striking and morally nuanced, capable of representing both gentle guidance and stern enforcement. This evolution also facilitates their integration into Daoist underworld bureaucracy, where paired officials are common.

Variants

Some traditions retain Wuchang Gui as a separate figure, while others subsume him entirely into Bai Wuchang. The timing and mechanism of the transition are not precisely documented, and scholars reconstruct it from scattered references, making this a partially hypothetical narrative based on attested patterns.

Heibai Wuchang in Journey to the West

Source 16th-century novel ‘Journey to the West’ (Xiyou Ji)Culture Chinese literaryPeriod Ming dynasty

Summary

In Journey to the West, underworld officials attempt to summon Sun Wukong, the Monkey King, to hell for judgment. Among these officials are figures identified with Heibai Wuchang or similar death messengers. They arrive with warrants and chains, intending to escort Sun Wukong’s soul. However, Sun Wukong resists, fights them, and eventually invades the underworld, erasing his name from the registers of death. This episode dramatizes the tension between cosmic bureaucracy and extraordinary spiritual power, showing that even death messengers can be defied by a sufficiently powerful being.

Consequences

The story reinforces Heibai Wuchang’s status as mid-level officials: they have authority over ordinary beings but can be overpowered by exceptional immortals. It also popularizes the image of underworld constables with chains and warrants, contributing to later visualizations of Heibai Wuchang.

Variants

Different editions and adaptations of Journey to the West vary in how explicitly they name Heibai Wuchang. Some later retellings in opera and television explicitly label the messengers as Black and White Impermanence, while the original text may use more generic terms. Scholars caution against retrojecting modern names onto the Ming novel without textual confirmation.

Heibai Wuchang as fortune deities

Source City God Temple cult narratives and modern religious practiceCulture Chinese folk religion (southern China, Taiwan, Southeast Asia)Period Late imperial to contemporary

Summary

In many City God Temples, Heibai Wuchang are not only feared as death messengers but also revered as fortune deities. Devotees offer incense, paper money, and food to their statues, asking for protection, prosperity, and successful business. Bai Wuchang’s hat inscriptions promising wealth and great fortune are interpreted literally: seeing him or honoring him is believed to bring luck. Hei Wuchang, though stern, is invoked to ward off thieves, fraudsters, and malevolent spirits. Stories circulate of people who, after sincerely worshipping Heibai Wuchang and reforming their behavior, receive unexpected financial windfalls or escape misfortune, attributed to the deities’ intervention.

Consequences

These narratives expand Heibai Wuchang’s role beyond death, integrating them into everyday concerns of livelihood and safety. They reinforce the moral message that ethical living attracts divine favor, while wrongdoing invites punishment. The dual nature of the pair—fortune and fear—makes them powerful tools for social regulation.

Variants

Some communities emphasize their fortune aspect more strongly, decorating their altars with wealth symbols; others focus on their punitive role, using their images in exorcistic rites. Scholarly accounts note that this dual function is typical of Chinese tutelary deities, who often combine protective and disciplinary roles.

The evolution from Wuchang Gui to Heibai Wuchang is reconstructed from scattered references and may oversimplify a complex process. Regional variations in hat inscriptions, props, and ritual roles are only partially covered; a truly exhaustive account would require fieldwork in multiple communities. Archive Dossier — Contradictions

Origin: singular Wuchang Gui vs paired Heibai Wuchang

Position A

Heibai Wuchang originated as a singular Impermanence Ghost (Wuchang Gui), likely associated with Bai Wuchang, and later developed into a pair with the addition of Hei Wuchang.

Position A — evidence

Ōya’s analysis of Song-dynasty references to Wuchang Gui; Asian Religions at UTK article noting Bai Wuchang’s earlier prominence; regional legends that focus on a single figure before introducing a partner.

Position B

Heibai Wuchang have always been conceptualized as a pair of black and white officials, with Wuchang Gui representing a generic category rather than a specific precursor.

Position B — evidence

Modern folk practice and temple iconography overwhelmingly depict Heibai Wuchang as a pair, with little awareness of a singular precursor; some narratives treat Wuchang Gui as a generic term for impermanence ghosts rather than a specific deity.

Scholarly Consensus

Scholars tend to support position A, viewing the pair as an evolution from a singular figure, but acknowledge that evidence is fragmentary and reconstructed. Position B reflects contemporary practice rather than historical origin.

Mortal identities and offices

Position A

Xie Bi’an and Fan Wujiu were court bailiffs from Fuzhou, whose official duties and loyalty led to their apotheosis.

Position A — evidence

Legend of the Nantai Bridge as cited in regional gazetteers and modern scholarship; Wang’s description of them as court bailiffs.

Position B

They were generals, constables, or anonymous commoners whose precise offices are not historically verifiable.

Position B — evidence

Alternative folk tales that describe them as generals or simply ‘two friends’; lack of archival records confirming specific offices; variation in titles across regions.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars accept the bailiff identification as the dominant narrative but note that it is legendary rather than historically documented. The exact historical status of Xie and Fan remains uncertain.

Hierarchical placement in the underworld

Position A

Heibai Wuchang serve under City Gods (Chenghuang) as their constables, operating primarily within city jurisdictions.

Position A — evidence

Wang’s classification; temple arrangements where they flank City Gods; local explanations that describe them as ‘City God’s policemen’.

Position B

They serve directly under the Ten Kings of Hell or the Fengdu Emperor, functioning as central underworld messengers rather than local officers.

Position B — evidence

Taoist underworld texts and modern explanations that place them under Hell Kings or Fengdu Emperor; depictions of them in hell courts without City Gods present.

Scholarly Consensus

There is no single consensus; scholars emphasize that Chinese religious systems are plural and locally variable. Both positions are valid within their respective traditions, and attempts to harmonize them into a single hierarchy risk oversimplification.

Fortune-bringing vs purely death-related role

Position A

Heibai Wuchang are primarily death messengers and should not be regarded as fortune deities; their hat inscriptions are symbolic rather than literal promises of wealth.

Position A — evidence

Descriptions of them as underworld constables whose main duty is soul escort; emphasis on impermanence and moral judgment in scholarly analyses.

Position B

They function as fortune deities in many communities, actively bestowing prosperity and protection on devotees who worship them.

Position B — evidence

City God Temple practices where devotees seek wealth and protection from Heibai Wuchang; hat inscriptions explicitly mentioning fortune; testimonies of devotees attributing financial success to their favor.

Scholarly Consensus

Scholars recognize both roles, interpreting the fortune aspect as a later or localized development that complements rather than replaces their death-related function. The tension reflects the broader ambivalence of death deities in folk religion.

Identification in Journey to the West

Position A

The underworld messengers in Journey to the West are Heibai Wuchang, providing an early literary attestation of the pair.

Position A — evidence

Later opera and TV adaptations explicitly name Black and White Impermanence in scenes derived from Journey to the West; visual similarities between the novel’s descriptions and Heibai Wuchang iconography.

Position B

The messengers are generic death officials, and identifying them specifically as Heibai Wuchang is a later interpretive move by readers and adapters.

Position B — evidence

Textual analysis of the original novel shows generic terms for underworld messengers without explicit use of the names Bai Wuchang or Hei Wuchang; lack of clear identification in early commentaries.

Scholarly Consensus

Many scholars lean toward position B, cautioning against retroactive naming. However, they acknowledge that the novel contributed to the broader image of underworld messengers that later coalesced around Heibai Wuchang.
