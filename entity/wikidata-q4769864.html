Who It Is

Imagine a woman so beautiful that she could make the sun itself feel plain. Now imagine that same woman, driven by whispers of a trickster spider, trying to steal the sun's throne—and being cursed to wear two faces for eternity, one of beauty and one of horror. This is Anog Ite, the Double-Faced Woman of the Lakota, a figure whose story is not just a myth of pride and punishment, but the very origin of a sacred, terrifying dream that could grant a woman unimaginable skill—or mark her for a life of madness and wandering.

Anog Ite is characterized by insatiable ambition, pride, and vanity. She is never satisfied with what she has and constantly desires more power, status, and recognition. She is manipulative and uses her beauty and influence to achieve her goals, even when it means defying the cosmic order. Her behavior serves as a cautionary tale about the dangers of unchecked ambition and pride.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Anog Ite translates from Lakota as 'Double-Faced Woman' or 'Two-Faced Woman'. The name derives from 'anog' (double or two) and 'ite' (face). This refers to her physical manifestation after her punishment, where one side of her face was made hideous.

Powers
Shape-shifting/Dual Visage

After her punishment, Anog Ite possesses two faces: one beautiful and one hideous. This is not a power she controls but a permanent mark of her transgression. It symbolizes her dual nature and the consequences of her actions.

Limit: She cannot change or hide this dual visage. It is a permanent reminder of her fall.

Influence over Dreams (Double Woman)

Anog Ite is the spirit behind the Double Woman dream. In these dreams, she appears to women and offers them great skill in quillwork and other domestic arts, but the gift comes with a price: the dreamer is often marked as different, potentially becoming a 'contrary' or experiencing social isolation, madness, or a life of wandering.

Limit: The power is a bargain, not a free gift. The woman who dreams of Anog Ite is forever changed and often burdened by her gift.

Mother of the Buffalo Gods

As the wife of Tate, she is the mother of the four directional winds, who are also the buffalo gods: Okaga (South Wind), Iyan (East Wind), Yata (North Wind), and Eya (West Wind). This makes her a powerful matriarchal figure in the Lakota cosmos.

Limit: After her fall, she is separated from her sons, and her relationship with them is strained. She loses her status as their honored mother.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Anog Ite was originally a beautiful woman, the wife of Tate (the Wind). After her punishment by the Great Spirit (Wakan Tanka), one side of her face was made grotesque and hideous, while the other remained beautiful. This dual visage is her defining characteristic. She is often depicted with two faces, one pleasant and one monstrous.

Symbols — The double face is her primary symbol. She is also associated with the buffalo, as she was the mother of the four buffalo gods. The swirling wind and the whirlwind are also linked to her, as she was the wife of the Wind. The Badlands are considered her domain, a place of upheaval and geological chaos, mirroring her own internal chaos.

Domains — Anog Ite is associated with the home, particularly the tipi and the domestic sphere, but her story has a cosmic dimension. After her punishment, she is associated with the earth, specifically the Badlands of South Dakota. She is also connected to the Double Woman dream cult, where she acts as a spirit guide granting special skills, particularly in quillwork and other women's arts, but at a great personal cost.

Accounts I
The Fall of Anog IteLakota oral tradition, recorded by J.R. Walker (1917)

Mythic time (pre-creation of the current world order)

Anog Ite was the beautiful wife of Tate, the Wind god. She lived in the heavens with her husband and their four sons. However, she was vain and ambitious. The trickster figure Iktomi (the Spider) saw her dissatisfaction and flattered her, telling her she was more beautiful than the wife of the Sun (Wi) and that she deserved a higher station. He suggested she should try to take the Sun's place. Anog Ite, blinded by pride, followed Iktomi's advice and attempted to usurp the Sun's position. When the Great Spirit (Wakan Tanka) discovered her transgression, he punished her. He decreed that she would be cast down from the heavens and that from that moment on, one side of her face would be hideous and monstrous, while the other would remain beautiful. She was banished to the earth, where she became a figure of caution and a spirit of the Double Woman dream. Anog Ite's fall explains the origin of the Double Woman dream cult, the creation of the Badlands (as the place she was cast down), and serves as a moral lesson about the dangers of pride, vanity, and insatiable ambition. Anog Ite is a 'goddess of vanity' or a 'dark goddess' to be worshipped or invoked. — Anog Ite is a cautionary figure in Lakota mythology, not a deity to be worshipped. Her story is a moral lesson about the consequences of pride and insatiable desire. She is a spirit in the Double Woman dream cult, but the bargain she offers is dangerous and costly.

Anog Ite is a creator goddess. — She is not a creator goddess. She is a figure within the existing cosmic order, and her story explains the origin of certain cultural practices and natural features, not the creation of the world.

Her story is a simple tale of 'pride before a fall.' — While pride is a central theme, the story is more complex. It also explores the nature of ambition, the role of temptation, the consequences of defying cosmic order, and the sacred, yet dangerous, nature of certain gifts (like the Double Woman dream).
