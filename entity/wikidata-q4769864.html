Form

Anog Ite is described as having two faces: the front face is beautiful, radiant, and alluring, while the back of her head bears a second face that is hideous, twisted, and terrifying. The transformation from a single-faced to a double-faced being is the central physical marker of her punishment for defying the Sun (Wi).

Powers

Shapeshifting / Dual Form Her defining power is her double-faced nature. She can present her beautiful face to charm and deceive, or her terrifying face to intimidate and punish. This is both a power and a curse, as she can never hide her true nature. Walker (Lakota Myth) She cannot fully conceal her second face; it is always present, even if hidden from view. This makes her unable to maintain deception indefinitely. None recorded.

Influence over Dreams and Visions Anog Ite is associated with 'Double Woman' dreams, which are believed to grant artistic or visionary powers (especially to women) but at the cost of social alienation or madness. She can appear in dreams to offer gifts of skill or knowledge. General Lakota tradition (Deloria, Densmore) The gifts she offers are double-edged; they may bring talent but also personal turmoil. The dreamer must be strong enough to bear the vision. Some sources treat these dreams as positive shamanic gifts, while others frame them as curses.

Curses and Discord She can sow discord among individuals and communities, turning friends into enemies and families against each other. Her curse is not overtly magical but psychological, exploiting existing tensions. Walker Her influence is limited to those who are already vulnerable to vanity, jealousy, or ambition. None recorded.

Accounts

Anog Ite was originally a beautiful and ambitious woman, the wife of Tate (the Wind) and the mother of four sons (the Four Winds). She grew resentful of her subordinate status among the gods and coveted the power and attention of the Sun (Wi). She conspired with Iktomi (the trickster spider) to seduce the Sun and usurp his place. She succeeded in attracting Wi's attention and even sat on his throne, but her actions violated the cosmic order. Skan (the Sky, the judge of the gods) intervened. As punishment, Wi struck her with his power, splitting her face in two — one side remained beautiful, the other became hideous. Skan then cast her down from the sky to the earth, where she became a wanderer, forever cursed to see the consequences of her pride. She was renamed Anog Ite, the Double-Faced Woman, and her story became a warning against vanity and ambition.

Anog Ite appears to individuals — often women — in dreams, offering them a vision of artistic or ceremonial power. The dreamer may be given a gift of quillwork, beadwork, or song. However, the dream is a test: the dreamer must choose between the beautiful and the ugly face. Choosing the beautiful face brings talent but also isolation and a life on the margins of society. Choosing the ugly face brings a harder path but a more grounded life.

Content Notes

Anog Ite's face is split in two by the Sun, making her permanently disfigured. This is a harsh, physical punishment for a crime of pride, not malice. The narrative does not glorify the violence but presents it as a necessary consequence of breaking cosmic law.

The punishment is a common motif in world mythology (e.g., the splitting of figures like the Norse Loki's binding or the Greek Prometheus's torture) — a physical mark of a spiritual transgression.

Walker

Anog Ite is cast out from the celestial realm and forced to wander the earth. She becomes an 'other,' a figure of fear and caution. The 'Double Woman' dream similarly isolates the dreamer, marking them as different from their community.

This reflects a broader pattern in many cultures where those with spiritual or artistic gifts are both revered and feared, set apart from the social mainstream.

Deloria, Densmore

Early European-American ethnographers, including Walker, may have unconsciously shaped the narrative to fit Christian moral frameworks (e.g., the 'fall from grace' narrative). This is not a distortion of the core story but a potential framing bias in how it was recorded and presented.

Post-colonial scholarship on Lakota religion (e.g., the work of Raymond DeMallie) has highlighted the need to read these texts critically, recognizing the influence of the recorder's worldview.

Walker (as recorded)

Scholarly Debates
Role of Iktomi
Iktomi is the primary instigator, manipulating Anog Ite's ambition for his own amusement. ⇌ Anog Ite is solely responsible for her actions; Iktomi is a minor or absent figure.
Evidence for A: Walker's recorded version gives Iktomi a significant role as a tempter.
Evidence for B: Some oral traditions and fragmentary accounts omit Iktomi entirely, focusing on Anog Ite's personal ambition.
Nature of the 'Double Woman' Dream
The dream is a positive, shamanic gift that grants artistic or spiritual power. ⇌ The dream is a curse that leads to madness, social alienation, or death.
Evidence for A: Some accounts describe the dream as a source of sacred songs and quillwork patterns.
Evidence for B: Other accounts emphasize the dreamer's isolation and the danger of the vision.

A way to hold it in your mind

Anog Ite is the original 'Icarus' of the Great Plains — a figure who flew too close to the sun and was permanently marked by the fall. She is also the mythological ancestor of every cautionary tale about the price of vanity, from the Evil Queen's mirror in Snow White to the tragic ambition of characters like Walter White. She is not evil; she is a warning.
