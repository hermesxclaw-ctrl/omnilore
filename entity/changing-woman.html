Changing Woman

Who It Is

Changing Woman (Asdzą́ą́ Nádleehé in Diné) is the most important female deity in Navajo (Diné) religion — the personification of the earth, the seasons, fertility, and the eternal cycle of renewal. Her name reflects her essential nature: she grows old in winter and becomes young again in spring, perpetually renewing herself without the need for a mate or external force. She represents the life-giving power of the earth itself — constant in its cycles, endlessly regenerative, and fundamentally feminine.

Physically, Changing Woman is associated with turquoise — the sacred stone of the Diné — and she is sometimes called 'Turquoise Woman.' She is depicted as a woman of extraordinary beauty who grows from infancy to old age and back again with the seasons. In Navajo sand paintings used in healing ceremonies, she is represented with symbols of fertility: corn, pollen, rain, and the four sacred mountains that bound Diné territory. She is associated with the color white (representing dawn and the east) and with corn pollen, the most sacred substance in Diné ritual.

The creation of Changing Woman is a central event in Diné cosmology. According to the emergence narrative, after the Diné people emerged into the current (Fourth) World, First Man and First Woman discovered a baby girl on top of Gobernador Knob (Ch'óol'į́'í), a sacred mountain in present-day New Mexico. This infant was Changing Woman, born of the earth and sky through the union of a piece of turquoise and a white shell. She grew rapidly, aging four seasons in four days — and then reversed, becoming young again.

Changing Woman's most important role is as the mother of the Hero Twins (Monster Slayer and Born for Water), the great culture heroes who rid the world of the monsters that threatened humanity. Changing Woman was impregnated by the Sun (Jóhonaa'éí) and gave birth to the twins, who grew up to undertake their epic quest. As their mother, she gifted them with the weapons, prayers, and spiritual protection they needed to succeed. After the monsters were slain, Changing Woman created the first human beings from the corn and other sacred substances she scraped from her own body, establishing the clans of the Diné people.

Changing Woman is central to the Kinaaldá, the Navajo girls' puberty ceremony — one of the most important and enduring ceremonies in Diné culture. During a girl's first menstruation, a four-day ceremony is held in which the initiate ritually embodies Changing Woman: she runs toward the east at dawn (representing Changing Woman's vitality), grinds corn for a sacred cake, and receives the blessings of becoming a woman. This ceremony connects each Diné girl directly to Changing Woman, making the deity's renewal not just a cosmic event but a lived, embodied experience.

Unlike many other figures in world mythology, Changing Woman does not have a single fixed myth cycle — she is understood to be ever-changing, ever-renewing, impossible to fully capture in story. Her existence is celebrated in the Blessingway ceremony, the foundational Diné ceremonial that invokes beauty, harmony, and balance (hózhó). She embodies hózhó itself: the state of beauty, harmony, health, and positive well-being that is the goal of Diné spiritual practice.

Form

Changing Woman is associated with extraordinary beauty that perpetually renews itself. She is described as a woman who grows from infancy to old age and back again with the seasons — in winter she is aged and wrinkled; in spring she is a young girl; in summer a woman in her prime; in autumn she begins to age — and then the cycle reverses. She is associated with turquoise (the sacred blue-green stone) and white shell, which are both her creative substances and her adornments. In visual representation (sandpaintings, contemporary Diné art), she is shown as a woman of dignity and beauty surrounded by symbols of fertility: corn, pollen, rain clouds, and the four sacred mountains (Sis Naajiní/Blanca Peak to the east, Tsoodził/Mount Taylor to the south, Dook'o'oosłííd/San Francisco Peaks to the west, Dibé Nitsaa/Hesperus Peak to the north). She is associated with the color white (dawn, the east) and with corn pollen (tádídíín), the most sacred substance in Diné ritual. She is never depicted as old in a negative sense — her aging is part of her cyclical nature, and she embodies the beauty of each life stage equally.

Powers

Perpetual Self-Renewal Changing Woman's defining power is her ability to renew herself perpetually without external assistance. She ages through the seasons — old in winter, young in spring — and the cycle repeats endlessly. She does not need a mate, medicine, or ritual to accomplish this; it is her inherent nature. This self-renewal is not immortality in the static Western sense (being frozen at one age forever) but a dynamic, cyclical process. She is the embodiment of the earth's regenerative power, and her renewal is the template for all natural cycles. Attested across all Diné traditions; central to Blessingway narrative Her self-renewal is seasonal, not instantaneous — she must pass through the full cycle. She cannot remain permanently young or permanently old. Her power is the cycle itself, not the ability to escape it. Some versions emphasize the seasonal cycle (aging and renewing over a year); others emphasize the four-day cycle (aging through four stages in four days).

Mother of the Hero Twins / Empowerment of Warriors Changing Woman gave birth to the Hero Twins (Monster Slayer / Naayéé' Neizghání and Born for Water / Tóbájíshchíní) after being impregnated by the Sun. She equipped them with the weapons, prayers, and ceremonial knowledge they needed to confront the monsters (Naayéé') that threatened humanity. Her role as the twins' mother makes her the source of the power that rid the world of evil — the Hero Twins succeed because of their mother's gifts. She is thus a warrior-mother figure: her generative power produces the warriors who save the world. Blessingway narrative; Matthews (1897); Haile/Wyman (1970) She does not fight the monsters herself — her power operates through her sons. She is the source of the power but not its direct wielder. Some versions emphasize the twins' own agency more than their mother's empowerment. The role of the Sun as father varies in significance.

Creation of the Diné Clans Changing Woman created the first Diné clans (the original kinship groups) by rubbing skin from various parts of her body — her chest, her back, her arms — and shaping the scraps into people. Each scrap became the founder of a different clan. This makes her the literal mother of all Diné people: every Navajo can trace their clan identity, ultimately, to Changing Woman's body. This is a profoundly intimate creation — not molding from clay or speaking into being, but producing people from her own physical substance. Blessingway narrative; attested across Diné ceremonial traditions The number and identity of the original clans created by Changing Woman vary across traditions. Contemporary Diné clan system includes many clans that post-date the original creation. Some traditions attribute the creation of certain clans to other Holy People rather than to Changing Woman.

Origin and Guardian of the Kinaaldá Ceremony Changing Woman performed the first Kinaaldá (girls' puberty ceremony) at her own menarche, establishing the template for all subsequent Kinaaldá ceremonies. The four-day ceremony — running toward the east, grinding corn, baking the alkąąd cake, receiving teachings — is a reenactment of Changing Woman's original rite. Through the Kinaaldá, every Diné girl becomes Changing Woman: the ceremony transforms the girl into a woman and connects her directly to the generative power of the earth. Changing Woman's presence is ritually invoked in every Kinaaldá. Blessingway narrative; ongoing Diné ceremonial practice The Kinaaldá's efficacy depends on proper ceremonial performance — the prayers, songs, and ritual actions must be correct. Changing Woman's power flows through the ceremony but requires human participation. The exact form of the first Kinaaldá varies in different ceremonial versions — some emphasize running, others the corn cake, others the teachings.

Gift of the Blessingway (Hózhóójí) Changing Woman gave the Blessingway ceremony to the Diné people. The Blessingway is the foundational ceremonial complex of Navajo religion — the 'mother ceremony' from which all other Navajo ceremonies derive. It is performed for all major life transitions: birth, puberty, marriage, healing from illness, and death. The ceremony invokes hózhó (beauty, harmony, balance, well-being) and reconnects the patient to the pattern of creation. Changing Woman's gift of the Blessingway is her greatest cultural legacy — every Navajo ceremony, in some sense, traces back to her. Blessingway narrative; Wyman (1970); ongoing ceremonial practice The Blessingway requires trained ceremonial practitioners (hataałii) to perform correctly. The ceremony is complex, and Changing Woman's gift must be maintained through proper transmission of ceremonial knowledge. Some traditions attribute elements of the Blessingway to other Holy People in addition to Changing Woman. The exact contents of the ceremony vary by region and practitioner.

Accounts

After the Diné people emerged into the Fourth World (the current world), First Man (Áłtsé Hastiin) and First Woman (Áłtsé Asdzą́ą́) were traveling through the newly-formed landscape when they came to Gobernador Knob (Ch'óol'į́'í), a sacred mountain in present-day New Mexico. There, on the summit, they found a baby girl lying on the ground — she had been created from a piece of turquoise and a white shell, or she had simply appeared, a child of the earth and sky. This was Changing Woman. They took her in and raised her. She grew with supernatural speed — aging four seasons in four days. On the fourth day, she reached menarche and performed the first Kinaaldá ceremony, running toward the dawn, grinding corn, and receiving the teachings that would become the template for all Diné girls' puberty ceremonies. Her growth then reversed: she grew young again, establishing the perpetual cycle of self-renewal that defines her nature.

As Changing Woman reached womanhood, the Sun (Jóhonaa'éí) saw her beauty and desired her. He came to her, and she was impregnated — by his direct presence, by a beam of sunlight, or by water in which his image was reflected. She gave birth to twin sons: Monster Slayer (Naayéé' Neizghání) and Born for Water (Tóbájíshchíní). The world at that time was infested with Naayéé' — monsters who devoured humans. The twins, when they came of age, learned of their father's identity and undertook a journey to the Sun's house to obtain the weapons and power needed to confront the monsters. Changing Woman equipped them with prayers, protective songs, and her blessing. After their epic quest, the Hero Twins slew most of the monsters — but Changing Woman, in her wisdom, advised them to spare some (Old Age, Poverty, Hunger, Cold) because without these, humans would have no motivation to strive, to be generous, or to appreciate warmth and plenty.

After the monsters were slain and the world was safe, Changing Woman decided to create the Diné people. She rubbed her skin — from her chest, her back, her arms, her legs — and shaped the pieces into people. Each body part became a different clan (the origin of the Diné clan system). She taught them how to live in hózhó, how to perform the Blessingway, how to plant corn, and how to organize themselves as a people. Then she departed to the west, to her house on an island in the Pacific Ocean, made of turquoise and white shell. But before she left, she gave the Diné the ceremonies and teachings that would sustain them in her absence. Some versions say she will return when the world is in greatest need.

Content Notes

In many versions, the Sun impregnates Changing Woman without her explicit consent — he sees her beauty, desires her, and acts. While Diné tradition does not frame this as assault (the Sun is a Holy Person, and Changing Woman is herself divine), modern readers may find the power dynamics troubling. Some versions describe her as willing; others are ambiguous. This element raises questions about divine sexuality, agency, and how Indigenous traditions navigate the relationship between female deities and male divine power.

Diné scholars have cautioned against imposing Western feminist frameworks on Diné narratives without understanding the cultural context. The relationship between Changing Woman and the Sun is a cosmological principle (the union of earth and sky), not a human relationship.

Matthews (1897); various Blessingway versions

During the U.S. boarding school era (late 19th through mid-20th century), Diné children were forcibly removed from their families and forbidden from practicing their culture and religion. The Kinaaldá ceremony — the puberty rite that connects Diné girls to Changing Woman — was suppressed, and Diné girls were subjected to Western assimilationist practices instead. This was a form of cultural genocide that specifically targeted the female ceremonial tradition Changing Woman embodies. The trauma of this suppression continues to affect Diné communities, and the revitalization of the Kinaaldá is an act of cultural healing.

The boarding school era is increasingly recognized as a form of cultural genocide. The specific impact on Diné women's ceremonial life is an important area of study.

What We Don't Know

The relationship between Changing Woman and White Shell Woman — whether they are one or two, and what the ceremonial implications are — is one of the most significant unresolved questions in Diné religious studies and should ideally be addressed by Diné scholars working within the tradition. The impact of the boarding school era on the transmission of Kinaaldá knowledge is an important area of cultural recovery that deserves more documentation. The contemporary practice of Kinaaldá — how many Diné girls undergo the ceremony today, how the ceremony has adapted to modern life, what challenges face its continuation — is an active area that would benefit from community-based research. The relationship between Changing Woman's mythology and Diné concepts of gender and sexuality is underexplored in scholarly literature, partly because of the sensitive nature of ceremonial knowledge.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Changing Woman as the ultimate regenerative battery. She doesn't need to be recharged — she recharges herself. She's not a goddess who was young once and then got old, or who froze herself at a particular age, or who needs ambrosia or golden apples or a magic fountain to stay beautiful. She is the cycle itself. She ages, and then she doesn't. Winter comes, and she's old. Spring comes, and she's a girl again. That's not a superpower — it's a natural law. And because she IS the cycle, everything that cycles through life — corn, seasons, women's bodies, the earth itself — is connected to her. The Kinaaldá ceremony isn't a ritual ABOUT Changing Woman; it IS Changing Woman. The girl who runs toward the dawn isn't pretending to be Changing Woman — she IS Changing Woman, right then, in that moment. And that means every Diné woman who has undergone Kinaaldá carries Changing Woman within her. She's not a myth — she's a living presence in the bodies and lives of Diné women. That's what makes her the most powerful female figure in Indigenous American religion: she's not supernatural. She's hyper-natural. She's what nature does.
