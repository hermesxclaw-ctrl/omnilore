Who It Is

Before the Navajo people walked the fifth world, a woman walked between ages — shedding her old skin to become young again, weaving clans from her own flesh. Changing Woman is not just a creator; she is the first Navajo woman, the mother of the Hero Twins, and the living heartbeat of the kinaalda rite that still transforms girls into women today.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The Navajo name Asdzą́ą́ Nádleehé translates literally as 'the woman who changes.' Powers
Clan creation through self-skin-shedding

Changing Woman created the four original Navajo clans by taking strips of her own shed skin, placing them in her mountain soil bundle, and singing them into four ancestral couples. Each couple was linked to one of the four sacred mountains and its associated color (white shell, turquoise, abalone, jet). This generative act established the matrilineal clan system that remains the foundation of Navajo social identity, marriage rules, and ceremonial obligation.

Cyclical age-shifting

She can change her age at will — from radiant youth to weathered elder and back again. This power is the literal meaning of her name and is ritually re-enacted in the kinaalda puberty ceremony, where the girl being initiated runs toward the sunrise each morning, symbolically absorbing Changing Woman's renewal.

Limit:

Sky and earth formation

During the emergence narrative, Changing Woman assisted in shaping the physical sky and earth. She is sometimes credited with fixing the cardinal directions and the four sacred mountains in place.

Limit:

Fertility and life-force transmission

The kinaalda replicates Changing Woman's generative power. Girls who complete the four-day rite are believed to absorb her strength, beauty, and capacity for renewal, ensuring the continuation of the Navajo people.

Blessing and longevity

The Blessing Way ceremony invokes Changing Woman to bestow long life, good fortune, and hózhǫ́ (balance/beauty) on participants. Her songs and pollen-path symbolism are central to this protective framework.

Accounts I
The Creation of the Clans

During the emergence of the Navajo into the present world (the fifth world), Changing Woman took strips of her own old skin and placed them in her mountain soil bundle. She sang over them and they became four couples — the ancestors of the four original Navajo clans. Each couple was associated with one sacred mountain and its corresponding color and direction. This act of self-skin-shedding echoes the reptile-like renewal motif found across North American creation narratives, but its social consequence is uniquely Navajo: the clan system that governs marriage, kinship, and identity to this day.

The Birth of the Hero Twins

Changing Woman conceived twin sons after the Sun (Jóhonaaʼéí) shone sunlight into her yázhí (a ceremonial basket or womb-space). The twins, Nayenezgani (Monster Slayer) and Tobadzischini (Born for Water), grew with extraordinary speed and became the culture heroes who rid the world of the Anaye — monstrous beings who preyed on the Navajo people. Their births fulfilled a prophecy that a divine child would save the people, and Changing Woman's role as mother of the saviors elevated her from creator to the progenitor of Navajo protection.

Accounts II
The Kinaalda Rite

When a Navajo girl reaches puberty, she undergoes the kinaalda — a four-day ceremony that re-enacts Changing Woman's journey from girlhood through the physical and spiritual trials that mark her transition. The girl grinds corn (a task associated with Changing Woman's own labor), runs a daily race toward the sunrise (echoing Changing Woman's association with the Sun), and prepares a large cake for the community. Her family and community witness her transformation into a woman, mirroring how Changing Woman's aging and renewal sustain the Navajo people. The kinaalda is one of the most important surviving Navajo ceremonies and is still practiced today.

The Blessing Way and Long Life

The Blessing Way (Hózhǫ́ǫ́jí) is a prayer ceremony that invokes Changing Woman's power to bring fortune, long life, and harmony. According to Diné Bahaneʼ, Changing Woman asked the Holy People to create a ceremony that would allow the Navajo people to live long and prosperous lives. The Blessing Way uses her songs, prayers, and symbolic imagery (the four sacred mountains, the four colors, the pollen path) to restore balance (hózhǫ́) to individuals and the community. It is the most frequently performed Navajo ceremony and remains central to Diné spiritual practice.
