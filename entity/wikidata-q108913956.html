Who It Is

When a flock of pigeons wheels suddenly over a field in the Manipur valley and settles as one, elders once said Khunu Leima had called them. Not a distant sky-queen, but a sister — one of three divine daughters who left heaven, chose a mortal husband, and divided the living world between them: waterfowl, wild grain-thieves, and the grey-white doves that still nest in the eaves of Meitei homes.

Attested in oral narratives as gentle, nurturing, and obedient among the three sisters, yet capable of decisive action when summoning her birds. She is described as the ruler/mistress who can call all pigeons to any place at will, implying command and protective authority rather than trickster or warrior traits. In the sisters' marriage story, she participates in the collective decision to marry a mortal, suggesting agency within kinship constraints. Powers
Sovereignty over pigeons and doves

Absolute command over all pigeons and doves (khunu). She can summon every pigeon to a single location instantly, disperse them, or direct their flight as messengers or omens.

Limit: Limited to Columbidae; no attested control over other birds (those belong to Nganu Leima for waterfowl, or other lais). No evidence of control over human minds or weather beyond bird-mediated signs.

Messenger and omen mediation

Pigeons under her command function as intermediaries between sky (Salailen) and earth, carrying messages, warnings, or blessings. Their cooing, arrival, or nesting in a house is read as her favour.

Limit: Interpretive power resides in human reading of omens; no attested direct speech through birds. Requires ritual attention to be effective.

Protection and blessing of households

Houses where pigeons nest unharmed are considered under her protection; harming pigeons is taboo and believed to bring misfortune, infertility of granary, or loss of peace.

Limit: Protective power is apotropaic and conditional on respect for birds; not a martial shield or healing power attested in texts.

Sky-lineage divinity and shapeshifting (implied)

As a daughter of Salailen (sky god), she possesses lai nature — ability to move between celestial and earthly realms and to appear in human or bird form. Not detailed as transformation feats but inherent to lais descending to earth.

Limit: No specific shapeshifting story attested for Khunu Leima alone (unlike Sanamahi or Pakhangba who have serpent/human forms). Do not invent a pigeon-transformation episode.

Accounts I
The Birth of the Sky DaughtersWakoklon Heelel Thilel Salai Amailon Pukok Puya and Sanamahi Laihui

Mythic time; manuscripts 15th-18th century, tradition older

In the beginning, Salailen Sidaba (also called Soraren, the sky father) and Leimarel Sidabi (earth mother) generate the cosmos. Among their offspring are the lais who will govern earthly domains. Salailen fathers a group of daughters — including Nganu Leima, Shapi Leima, Khunu Leima, and Ngaleima — each assigned to a class of living beings. Khunu Leima is given dominion over khunu (pigeons and doves), creatures of the air that dwell close to humans. The assignment establishes the tripartite division that will structure later earthly stories. Establishes Khunu Leima's divine rank as sky-descended lairembi and her specific ecological niche; legitimizes later taboos and rituals concerning pigeons. Provides cosmological charter for Meitei bird lore.

The Three Sisters Descend to EarthMoirang Kanglei oral tradition; Lai Haraoba Laishol chants

Folkloric time; oral tradition transcribed 19th-20th century

Nganu Leima (mistress of ducks and waterfowl), Shapi Leima (mistress of rodents and grain-pests), and Khunu Leima leave the sky realm to live on earth. They wander the wetlands, fields, and villages of Kangleipak, observing human life. Each is drawn to the creatures of her domain: Nganu to the lakes, Shapi to the granaries, Khunu to the rooftops and courtyards where pigeons nest. Their descent explains why these animals are not merely wild but have divine mistresses. Explains distribution of animals and human-animal taboos; sets stage for marriage to a mortal, a common motif where lais test or join humanity.

The Marriage to the Same Mortal ManMoirang Kanglei folklore; summaries in modern Meitei myth compilations and e-pao.net folklore articles

Folkloric time; documented 20th century from oral sources

On earth, the three sisters meet a mortal man — described in different tellings as a handsome farmer, hunter, or orphan of Moirang — and all three agree to marry him, living together as co-wives. The unusual sororal polyandry/polygyny (three sisters to one husband) is presented not as scandal but as a way to keep the sisters united while binding their animal domains to a single human household. Khunu Leima becomes the wife associated with domestic peace and the pigeons that nest in the marital home. The marriage binds human domesticity to divine stewardship of animals. Charters the co-existence of waterfowl, rodents, and pigeons around human settlements as a kinship arrangement; explains why harming any of these animals offends a divine wife. Provides narrative basis for household rituals seeking harmony among co-wives and for taboos against killing pigeons.

Accounts II
The Summoning of the PigeonsLiving folk belief summarized in encyclopedic entries and maibi oral teaching; ethnographic field notes

Ongoing folk belief, recorded 1908 (Hodson) to present

When Khunu Leima wishes to show her presence or to convey a message, she summons all pigeons to a place at once — a sudden flocking that cannot be explained by feeding alone. Villagers interpret a sudden gathering of pigeons on a roof, or their simultaneous departure, as her doing. Conversely, she can keep pigeons away from a place that has offended her. The story is less a single narrative than a recurring demonstration of her core power, told as anecdotes: a farmer who spared a nesting pair was rewarded with a flock that cleaned his field of pests; a hunter who shot a dove found his house thereafter empty of birds. Reinforces taboo against killing or disturbing pigeons; provides etiology for pigeon-related omens (yengba) in Meitei divination.

The Division of Domains and ReturnSynthesized from puya genealogies and Lai Haraoba cosmology; comparative Meitei scholarship (Singh 1988, Parratt 1980)

Mythic-folkloric synthesis

After a period living among humans, the sisters' domains are fully established: Nganu Leima remains with the waterfowl of Loktak Lake and wetlands, Shapi Leima with the rodents of fields and granaries, Khunu Leima with the pigeons of rooftops and temples. Some tellings have the sisters return to the sky or become guardian lairemas of their respective habitats, still accessible through prayer and Lai Haraoba invocation. Khunu Leima's continued presence is felt whenever pigeons coo at dawn — her voice in the birds. Explains current distribution of cult attention: Khunu Leima is invoked for domestic harmony and safe messages, not for lake or field fertility. Establishes her as a laibung (shrine)-associated deity in villages where pigeons are protected.

Pigeon Taboo and the Burning of the Puyas (Historical Overlay)Cheitharol Kumbaba and oral history of 18th-century Hinduization

1729-1732 CE (reign of Garibniwaz Pamheiba)

Under King Garibniwaz's adoption of Gaudiya Vaishnavism, many Meitei puyas were burned and Sanamahi practices suppressed. Knowledge of deities like Khunu Leima survived primarily through oral transmission by maibas/maibis and hidden manuscripts. The taboo against harming pigeons persisted even among Hinduized Meiteis, a living trace of Khunu Leima's cult when textual records were lost. Elders recall that pigeons continued to be fed at Kangla and at village shrines despite official discouragement of 'old' lais. Explains gaps in written sources for Khunu Leima; highlights resilience of animal-associated taboos as archive when texts were destroyed. Context for modern revival efforts to reconstruct her lore from fragments.

Khunu Leima is the same as Ngaleima or a generic 'pigeon goddess' interchangeable with any bird goddess. — Khunu Leima is specifically pigeons/doves (khunu); Ngaleima is fish (nga), Nganu Leima is waterfowl (nganu). Meitei taxonomy is precise — each lairembi governs a distinct class. Collapsing them erases indigenous classification.

Khunu Leima is a Hindu goddess or an avatar of Lakshmi/Saraswati. — She is a Sanamahi (Kanglei) deity predating Vaishnavite Hinduization of Manipur (18th century). Later syncretic retellings sometimes Hinduize her, but puya sources place her in the Salailen lineage, not the Hindu pantheon.

She has elaborate powers like weather control or war magic. — Attested powers are narrowly focused: summoning/commanding pigeons, mediating omens, protecting households that respect pigeons. No puya or oral source gives her weather, war, or creation powers beyond her sky lineage inheritance.

Pigeons are sacrificed to Khunu Leima. — The taboo is the opposite: pigeons are protected, not sacrificed. Harming them is believed to offend her. Offerings are rice, flowers, and incense at shrines, not bird sacrifice.
