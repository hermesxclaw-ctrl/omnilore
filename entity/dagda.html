Who It Is

Of all the gods the Tuath Dé Danann carried out of prehistoric Ireland into literature, the one their medieval scribes named simply 'In Dagda' — the Good God, the Great God — is the most unmistakably physical: a bearded giant in a hooded cloak who rules the fortress of the Boyne, yet stoops to the porridge-pit of his enemies and eats his own humiliation. He is the only deity who can kill nine men with one end of his staff and resurrect the slain with the other; the only one whose cauldron never runs dry and whose harp, called by the names of the oak and the four-angled music, flies to his hand and lulls a hostile army to sleep. He is the all-father and the buffoon, the wizard and the adulterer, the sovereign who freezes the sun itself to hide the birth of his son in the passage-tomb of Newgrange — a myth, many now believe, of the winter solstice sunbeam entering the womb of the mound. The Dagda is the archetypal strong-man king-druid: full-bodied, greedy in appetite and sexual appetite, yet wise and magically supreme. He is simultaneously the buffoon-giant of coarse tradition (the same tales that dignify him as 'good god' show him stuffed with porridge, belaboured, sexually predatory) and the 'horseman/all-father' of sovereignty. He is a dispenser of plenty to his people and a protector of the land, but his dealings with the women of the Otherworld — the Morrígan and Boann — involve coercion and deception. Powers
The un-dry cauldron (coire ansic)

An inexhaustible cauldron of plenty: 'an assembly used not to go unsatisfied from it', symbolising plenty and generosity. It is one of the Four Treasures of the Tuatha Dé Danann, brought by the tribe from Murias (one of the four northern cities).

Limit: Symbolic of hospitality rather than a weapon; no limit to its bounty stated

The staff-club of life and death (lorg mór)

A great staff, club or mace whose one (smooth) end kills instantly — 'could kill nine men with one blow' — while the opposite (rough) end restores the slain to life. The Dagda used the smooth end to resurrect his own son Cermait Milbél. Mesca Ulad calls it the 'terrible iron staff' (lorg aduathmar iarnaidi).

Limit: Dual-ended; the power belongs to the staff, not to the Dagda alone

The magic harp Uaithne

A richly ornamented harp made of oak. It will not play unless summoned by its two bynames, 'Oak of Two Meadows' (Daur Dá Bláo) and 'the Four Angled Music' (Cóir Cetharchair); when called it leaps off the wall and flies to its master. When the Dagda plays it he commands the 'Three Strains' — joy, sorrow, and sleep — and by the sleep-strain he immobilised the Fomorians and escaped. In one register his harp-playing 'put the seasons in their correct order'.

Limit: Requires the two bynames to be spoken; the name Uaithne may be that of the harper rather than the instrument

Control of time, seasons, weather and crops

The Dagda can 'control life and death... the weather and crops, as well as time and the seasons'. He made 'the sun stand still' so that Elcmar would not notice the passage of time while Boann was pregnant with Aengus. As an earth-/fertility god his blessing governs the fertility of the land.

Limit: The 'sun standing still' is attributed to a spell rather than a standing personal ability; seasonal control is variously assigned to the harp

Shape-shifting and colour-change (clóca/cloak)

Via his magical cloak (lumman) and general Tuath Dé power, the Dagda could change shape and colour — a standard supernatural attribute of his people, who could cloak themselves in the féth fíada ('magic mist').

Limit: Attested mainly through the cloak in the Yellow Book of Lecan list

Horsemanship and the sovereignty horse

The epithet Eochu/Eochaid ('horseman') marks him as a master of the horse and a wielder of horse-associated sovereignty — the royal horse that links the king to the land.

Limit: Attested by epithet and tribal name (Uí Echach 'descendants of the horse') rather than by a single surviving horse-miracle narrative

Resurrection of the dead

Beyond the magical staff's life-giving end, the Dagda is credited in some traditions with the power to return the dead to life — he resuscitated Cermait, and the fertility/rebirth complex connects his cauldron with plenty and renewal.

Limit: Bounded by context — he does not raise whole armies, only specific figures

Rhetorical/prophetic battle-speech

When asked by Lug what he will do against the Fomorians, the Dagda vows: 'I will take the side of the men of Erin both in mutual smiting and destruction and wizardry. Their bones under my club will be as many as hailstones under the feet of herds of horses'. The speech is a boast of devastating power rather than a list.

Limit: Rhetorical flourish

The two ever-present pigs and the black-maned heifer

He keeps one pig that is always growing and another that is always roasting — an endless food supply — and ever-laden fruit trees. His black-maned heifer, given for his labours before the Second Battle, lows and calls all of Ireland's cattle drawn by Fomorian tribute to graze.

Limit: Attested as possessions/provisions rather than powers per se

Form & Signs

Appearance — The Dagda is consistently described as a large bearded man or giant, dressed in a hooded cloak and carrying a massive club or staff. The medieval sources emphasise his corpulent, powerful build — he is a father-figure of immense stature rather than a lithe young hero. In Mesca Ulad he wields a 'terrible iron staff' (lorg aduathmar iarnaidi). Coir Anmann calls him 'a beautiful god of the heathens' and 'an earth-god' of great magical power. Compare

Accounts I
The Dagda and the Fomorian porridge / the building of the fortCath Maige Tuired (Second Battle)

compiled 12th c. from 9th-c. material

Under Fomorian oppression, Bres forces the Dagda to build the fortress Dún Brese, and the Fomorian kings Elatha, Indech and Tethra compel him to throw up raths (earthen forts). In one of the most famous comic episodes, the Dagda arrives at the Fomorian camp to negotiate, is fed a huge cauldron of porridge meant to bury him (a pit is dug, gruel poured in, and 'his grave' dug beside it), yet he survives by eating it all, licking the bottom, and walking away — a parody of the giant's greed that underlines both his coarse power and his role as the buffoon-giant of tradition. The Dagda's humiliation-and-survival establishes the comic register of his power; the raths are Fomorian tribute-labour on the Tuath Dé

The Samhain tryst with the MorríganCath Maige Tuired

compiled 12th c.

On Samhain, before the Second Battle of Mag Tuired, the Dagda meets the war-goddess Morrígan washing herself with one foot on either bank of the river Unius near Riverstown, Co. Sligo (in some sources she created the river). They have sex, and she promises to summon Ireland's magicians to cast spells for the Tuath Dé and to destroy the Fomorian king Indech, 'taking from him the blood of his heart and the kidneys of his valour'. She later deposits two handfuls of his blood in the river. It is a sovereignty compact sealed by sexual union at the festival of the dead that opens the year. The alliance binds the war-goddess to the Tuath Dé; Indech's death is attributed to her curse (though the text also credits Ogma with killing him)

The affair with Boann and the birth of AengusTochmarc Étaíne; Cath Maige Tuired; Dindsenchas; Aislinge Óenguso

medieval

Boann, goddess of the River Boyne, lives at Brú na Bóinne with her husband Elcmar. The Dagda desires her, so he sends Elcmar on a one-day errand, then lies with Boann; to hide the pregnancy, he casts a spell that 'makes the sun stand still', freezing time so Elcmar notices nothing. Boann gives birth to Aengus (Maccán Óg, 'the young son'). When Aengus learns the Dagda is his father, he asks for land; in the 'day and a night' trick (perhaps from Tochmarc Étaíne) he spirits the Brú from Elcmar — or, in other versions, from the Dagda himself. Aengus becomes the ruler of Brú na Bóinne; the tale is the charter for Aengus's rule of Newgrange

Accounts II
Retrieving the harp Uaithne from the FomoriansCath Maige Tuired (Second Battle)

compiled 12th c.

After the Second Battle of Mag Tuired, the retreating Fomorians carry off the Dagda's harp Uaithne, together with the harper Uaithne who had been abducted. The Dagda, with Lugh and Ogma, pursues them to the enemy hall. There the harp hangs mocking on the wall. The Dagda calls it by its two bynames — 'Come Daur Dá Bláo / Come Cóir Cetharchair / Come summer, come winter' — and the harp leaps off the wall, flies to him, and slays nine Fomorians on the way. He plays the Three Strains (joy, sorrow, sleep); the sleep-strain puts the Fomorian host to sleep, and the Tuath Dé escape. The harper and harp are restored; the battle-victory is sealed by the musical-capture episode

The banishing of the octopus at Mag MuirthemneDindsenchas (poem on Mag Muirthemne)

medieval

The Dagda drives away an octopus with his 'mace of wrath' on the plain of Mag Muirthemne, commanding: 'Turn thy hollow head! Turn thy ravening body! Turn thy resorbent forehead! Avaunt! Begone!' The sea recedes with the creature, and the plain of Mag Muirthemne is left behind — an exorcising of a chthonic marine monster from the land. The plain is cleared of the sea-creature as the water withdraws

The Dagda's death from Cethlenn's woundLebor Gabála Érenn / Irish Annals and king-list tradition

medieval

The Dagda reigned as High King of the Tuatha Dé Danann for seventy or eighty years (sources differ), dwelling at Brú na Bóinne. He died there, finally succumbing to a wound inflicted by Cethlenn (the wife of Balor) during the battle of Mag Tuired. Though the 'Ever-living one' is subject to violent death, his death is thus located at the great battle against the Fomorians, underscoring that the gods can die. His reign ends; he is buried/withdrawn at the Brú, which passes to Aengus
