The Approach

Astolfo of England, a knight who rode a hippogriff to the Moon to recover a lost witt, remains one of the most delightfully improbable heroes in all of Renaissance epic poetry. In Ludovico Ariosto's Orlando Furioso, this irrepressible paladin turns disaster into adventure, blending valor, folly, and a touch of the absurd.

Content advisory. Contains medieval and Renaissance depictions of battle, violence, and religious conflict; includes brief, non-explicit references to romantic entanglements and enchantment.
Identity

Astolfo

Pronunciation /əˈstɒlfoʊ/ (English), /aˈstɔlfo/ (Italian)

Known As

AstolphoAstolpheAstolfo d'Inghilterra

Etymology

The name's origin is uncertain; possibly derived from Germanic elements meaning 'noble wolf' or a variant of 'Adolf' with influence from Italian 'stolto' (foolish), which suits his comic role.

Cultural Ownership

Italian Renaissance literature, specifically the chivalric romances of Boiardo and Ariosto; later European literature and opera.

Disputed Identifications

Some readers have identified Astolfo with the historical Anglo-Saxon saint or noble of similar name, but there is no evidence of a direct connection; the character is a fictional creation of the romance tradition.

Description

Appearance

Astolfo is described as a handsome, young English knight with golden hair and a fair complexion. He is often depicted in armor, sometimes with a crest of a golden eagle or a winged horse. In Ariosto's illustrations, he is shown riding a hippogriff or a flying horse.

Symbols & Iconography

The hippogriff (a mythical creature, half eagle, half horse) is his most iconic mount. He also carries a magical horn that can terrify enemies, and later a book and a vial of oil given to him by the Apostle John. His coat of arms is often depicted with a golden eagle.

Behaviour & Personality

Astolfo is impulsive, boastful, and somewhat naive, yet also brave, loyal, and resourceful. He often acts without thinking, leading to both comic mishaps and unexpected successes. He is generous and kind-hearted, but also prideful and eager for glory. His folly is a recurring theme, but he is never malicious.

Domains & Habitats

As a knight of Charlemagne's court, he is based in France, but his adventures take him across Europe, Africa, Asia, and even to the Moon. He is associated with the earthly paradise of the Moon, where he retrieves Orlando's lost wits.

Version Differences

In Boiardo's Orlando Innamorato, Astolfo is a secondary character who joins the paladins in their quests, often providing comic relief. In Ariosto's Orlando Furioso, he becomes a central figure in the latter half, with the Moon episode and the recovery of Orlando's sanity. Later adaptations, such as operas by Lully and Handel, simplify his role, often focusing on his romantic exploits or his comic nature.

Powers & Abilities

Magical Horn

Source Version Orlando Furioso, Canto 15

Description

A horn given to him by the enchantress Logistilla, which when blown causes all enemies to flee in terror or fall from their horses.

Limitations

The horn's power is said to be ineffective against those who are pure of heart or who are not afraid; it is primarily used against human armies and monsters.

Contradictions

Some retellings omit the horn or change its power.

Hippogriff Riding

Source Version Orlando Furioso, Canto 15

Description

Astolfo tames and rides the hippogriff, a creature that can fly faster than any horse and is intelligent and loyal.

Limitations

The hippogriff is not invincible and can be injured; Astolfo must dismount for certain tasks.

Contradictions

In Boiardo's work, Astolfo does not ride a hippogriff; that is introduced by Ariosto.

Flight to the Moon

Source Version Orlando Furioso, Canto 34

Description

Astolfo travels to the Moon on Elijah's chariot, where he finds the lost wits of Orlando and other mortals in vials.

Limitations

This journey is only possible with divine aid and the magical chariot; Astolfo cannot do this alone.

Contradictions

The Moon journey is unique to Ariosto; Boiardo does not include it.

Book and Vial of Oil

Source Version Orlando Furioso, Canto 34

Description

Given by the Apostle John on the Moon, the book allows Astolfo to undo enchantments, and the oil restores life to the dead.

Limitations

The book's power is limited to breaking spells, and the oil can only revive those who died without sin.

Contradictions

These items are not mentioned in Boiardo's work.

Stories & Myths

The Hippogriff and the Castle of the Enchantress

Source Orlando Furioso, Canto 15Culture Italian RenaissancePeriod 16th century

Summary

Astolfo, separated from the main group, encounters the hippogriff and learns to ride it. He then flies to the castle of the enchantress Logistilla, where he is given the magical horn and instructions on how to use it.

Participants

Astolfo

Logistilla

Hippogriff

Consequences

He gains the horn and the hippogriff, which enable his later adventures.

Variants

Some versions shorten this episode or omit Logistilla.

The Journey to the Moon

Source Orlando Furioso, Canto 34Culture Italian RenaissancePeriod 16th century

Summary

Astolfo, aided by the Apostle John, rides Elijah's chariot to the Moon, where he finds the Valley of Lost Things, including the phial containing Orlando's wits. He brings it back to Earth and restores Orlando's sanity.

Participants

Astolfo

Apostle John

Orlando

Consequences

Orlando is cured of his madness and resumes his heroic role.

Variants

This is the most famous episode and has been adapted in many works.

The Battle with the Caliph of Africa

Summary

Astolfo uses his magical horn to rout the Saracen army, and with the help of the revived warriors, he captures the city of Biserta.

Violence in battle

Source Orlando Furioso

Description

Astolfo participates in battles where many enemies are killed, sometimes with the aid of his magical horn causing panic and trampling.

Scholarly Context

This is typical of Renaissance epic poetry, reflecting the martial culture of the time.

Religious conflict

Source Orlando Furioso

Description

The narrative frames the conflict between Charlemagne's Christian knights and the Saracen forces as a holy war, with Astolfo fighting against Muslims.

Scholarly Context

The poem reflects the Crusading ideology and the contemporary fear of Ottoman expansion.

Enchantment and seduction

Source Orlando Furioso

Description

Astolfo encounters enchanted women and temptations, but he is not sexually aggressive; the episodes are more comedic than dark.

Scholarly Context

These episodes are part of the romance genre's exploration of love and magic.

Contradictions & Disputes

Astolfo's role in Orlando's madness

Position A

In Ariosto, Astolfo is the key figure who recovers Orlando's wits.

Position A — evidence

Orlando Furioso, Canto 34

Position B

In Boiardo, Orlando's madness is not a major plot point, and Astolfo has no such role.

Position B — evidence

Orlando Innamorato, where Orlando's love for Angelica drives the plot but he does not go mad.

Scholarly Consensus

Ariosto invented the madness and the Moon episode; it is a major innovation.

Astolfo's character: fool or wise?

Position A

Some readers see Astolfo as a foolish, comic figure.

Position A — evidence

His impulsive actions and boastful nature.

Position B

Others interpret him as a wise, resourceful hero who uses unconventional means.

Position B — evidence

His successful quests and the trust placed in him by divine figures.

Scholarly Consensus

Modern critics often see him as a complex blend of folly and wisdom, embodying the theme of 'foolishness' that leads to truth.

Astolfo's historical existence

Position A

Some popular sources claim Astolfo was a real English knight.

Position A — evidence

None; the claim appears in unsourced internet content.

Position B

There is no historical evidence for his existence.

Position B — evidence

He appears only in fictional romances from the 15th century onward.

Scholarly Consensus

He is purely fictional.
