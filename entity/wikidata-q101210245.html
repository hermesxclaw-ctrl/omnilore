Who It Is

Astolfo of England, a knight who rode a hippogriff to the Moon to recover a lost witt, remains one of the most delightfully improbable heroes in all of Renaissance epic poetry. In Ludovico Ariosto's Orlando Furioso, this irrepressible paladin turns disaster into adventure, blending valor, folly, and a touch of the absurd.

Astolfo is impulsive, boastful, and somewhat naive, yet also brave, loyal, and resourceful. He often acts without thinking, leading to both comic mishaps and unexpected successes. He is generous and kind-hearted, but also prideful and eager for glory. His folly is a recurring theme, but he is never malicious.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name's origin is uncertain; possibly derived from Germanic elements meaning 'noble wolf' or a variant of 'Adolf' with influence from Italian 'stolto' (foolish), which suits his comic role.

Aliases — Astolpho, Astolphe, Astolfo d'Inghilterra

Ownership — Italian Renaissance literature, specifically the chivalric romances of Boiardo and Ariosto; later European literature and opera.

Powers
Magical Horn

A horn given to him by the enchantress Logistilla, which when blown causes all enemies to flee in terror or fall from their horses.

Limit: The horn's power is said to be ineffective against those who are pure of heart or who are not afraid; it is primarily used against human armies and monsters.

Hippogriff Riding

Astolfo tames and rides the hippogriff, a creature that can fly faster than any horse and is intelligent and loyal.

Limit: The hippogriff is not invincible and can be injured; Astolfo must dismount for certain tasks.

Flight to the Moon

Astolfo travels to the Moon on Elijah's chariot, where he finds the lost wits of Orlando and other mortals in vials.

Limit: This journey is only possible with divine aid and the magical chariot; Astolfo cannot do this alone.

Book and Vial of Oil

Given by the Apostle John on the Moon, the book allows Astolfo to undo enchantments, and the oil restores life to the dead.

Limit: The book's power is limited to breaking spells, and the oil can only revive those who died without sin.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Astolfo is described as a handsome, young English knight with golden hair and a fair complexion. He is often depicted in armor, sometimes with a crest of a golden eagle or a winged horse. In Ariosto's illustrations, he is shown riding a hippogriff or a flying horse.

Symbols — The hippogriff (a mythical creature, half eagle, half horse) is his most iconic mount. He also carries a magical horn that can terrify enemies, and later a book and a vial of oil given to him by the Apostle John. His coat of arms is often depicted with a golden eagle.

Domains — As a knight of Charlemagne's court, he is based in France, but his adventures take him across Europe, Africa, Asia, and even to the Moon. He is associated with the earthly paradise of the Moon, where he retrieves Orlando's lost wits.

Accounts I
The Hippogriff and the Castle of the EnchantressOrlando Furioso, Canto 15

16th century

Astolfo, separated from the main group, encounters the hippogriff and learns to ride it. He then flies to the castle of the enchantress Logistilla, where he is given the magical horn and instructions on how to use it. He gains the horn and the hippogriff, which enable his later adventures.

Accounts II
The Journey to the MoonOrlando Furioso, Canto 34

16th century

Astolfo, aided by the Apostle John, rides Elijah's chariot to the Moon, where he finds the Valley of Lost Things, including the phial containing Orlando's wits. He brings it back to Earth and restores Orlando's sanity. Orlando is cured of his madness and resumes his heroic role.

The Battle with the Caliph of AfricaOrlando Furioso, Cantos 38-39

16th century

Astolfo uses his magical horn to rout the Saracen army, and with the help of the revived warriors, he captures the city of Biserta. The Christian forces gain a decisive victory.
