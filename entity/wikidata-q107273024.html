The Approach

Before zero, the void was a philosophical puzzle, a grammatical absence, a blank space on the counting board. Then a humble dot in India began to move, to calculate, to hold place, and in doing so, it transformed mathematics from a tool of counting into a language of the infinite. This is the story of the most revolutionary number ever conceived—a number that represents nothing, yet makes everything possible.

Etymology

The English word 'zero' comes from French 'zéro' and Italian 'zero', a contraction of Venetian 'zevero', from Italian 'zefiro', borrowed from Arabic 'ṣifr' (صفر), meaning 'empty'. The Arabic word was used to translate the Sanskrit 'śūnya' (शून्य), meaning 'empty' or 'void'. The Italian mathematician Fibonacci used the term 'zephyrum', which became 'zefiro' and then 'zero'.

Cultural Ownership

The concept of zero emerged independently in multiple cultures: ancient Babylonians used a placeholder symbol, ancient Mayans developed a zero concept, and ancient Indians developed the full concept of zero as a number with its own properties. The modern numeral system was transmitted from India through Islamic mathematicians to Europe.

Disputed Identifications

There is scholarly debate about whether the ancient Babylonians truly had a concept of zero as a number or only as a placeholder. Similarly, the exact origin of the zero symbol in India is uncertain, with various possible dates and locations. The Maya independently developed a zero concept, but its full mathematical implications are debated.

Description

Appearance

As a numeral, zero is typically written as a circle or an oval (0). In many typefaces, it is distinguished from the letter O by being narrower or having a slash. In ancient India, it was often a dot (bindu). The Mayan zero was a shell-like glyph. In Babylonian cuneiform, it was represented by a double wedge or a slanted wedge.

Symbols & Iconography

The symbol '0' is universal in modern mathematics. The concept of zero is often associated with emptiness, void, nothingness, and the circle as a symbol of eternity or wholeness. In some cultures, zero is symbolized by a dot or a shell.

Behaviour & Personality

As a mathematical entity, zero behaves according to specific rules: it is the additive identity (adding zero to any number leaves it unchanged), the absorbing element for multiplication (multiplying any number by zero yields zero), and it has no multiplicative inverse (division by zero is undefined). It is neither positive nor negative, and it is an even number. It is the smallest non-negative integer and the largest non-positive integer.

Domains & Habitats

Zero exists in the realm of mathematics, specifically in number systems such as integers, rationals, reals, and complex numbers. It is the origin on number lines and coordinate systems. It appears in algebra, calculus, set theory, and many other branches.

Version Differences

Different cultures have represented zero differently: as a placeholder in Babylonian sexagesimal system, as a dot in Indian mathematics, as a shell in Mayan numerals, and as a circle in modern Western notation. The concept also evolved from a mere placeholder to a full-fledged number with arithmetic properties.

Division by Zero (Undefined)

Source Version Standard arithmetic and calculus

Description

Division by zero is undefined in standard arithmetic because zero has no multiplicative inverse. The expression x/0 has no meaning, and 0/0 is an indeterminate form in calculus.

Limitations

In some contexts, such as extended real numbers or wheel theory, division by zero is defined differently, but these are non-standard.

Contradictions

Some alternative mathematical systems (e.g., Riemann sphere, wheel theory) define division by zero in specific ways, leading to different results.

Exponentiation Rules

Source Version Elementary algebra

Description

Any nonzero number raised to the power of zero equals one: x^0 = 1. Zero raised to a positive power is zero: 0^x = 0. The expression 0^0 is indeterminate or defined as 1 in some contexts.

Limitations

0^0 is undefined in some contexts, but often defined as 1 in combinatorics and set theory.

Contradictions

The value of 0^0 is disputed; some define it as 1, others as undefined.

Stories & Myths

The Invention of Zero in India

Source Historical accounts, BrāhmasphuṭasiddhāntaCulture IndianPeriod c. 5th-7th century CE

Summary

Indian mathematicians developed the concept of zero as a number with its own arithmetic rules. Brahmagupta in 628 CE provided rules for operations with zero, including that zero plus a positive number is that number, zero minus a positive number is the negative of that number, and zero times any number is zero. This was a crucial step in the development of the decimal place-value system.

Participants

Brahmagupta

Indian mathematicians

Consequences

The concept of zero as a number enabled the development of algebra and calculus, and the place-value system revolutionized arithmetic.

Variants

The exact timeline and inventors are debated; some attribute earlier developments to other scholars like Aryabhata.

Transmission to the Islamic World and Europe

Source Al-Khwarizmi's works, Fibonacci's Liber AbaciCulture Islamic and EuropeanPeriod 9th-13th century CE

Summary

Islamic scholars like Al-Khwarizmi adopted Indian numerals, including zero, and wrote treatises that spread the system. In the 13th century, Fibonacci introduced these numerals to Europe in his book Liber Abaci, which was influential in replacing Roman numerals.

Participants

Al-Khwarizmi

Fibonacci

Islamic scholars

Consequences

The Hindu-Arabic numeral system, with zero, became standard in Europe, facilitating commerce, science, and mathematics.

Variants

The transmission was gradual and faced resistance; some European cities banned the use of Arabic numerals initially.

Mayan Independent Invention of Zero

Source Mayan inscriptions and codicesCulture MayanPeriod c. 36 BCE onward

Summary

The Maya independently developed a concept of zero, using a shell glyph for zero in their vigesimal (base-20) numeral system. They used it in their calendar and astronomical calculations.

Participants

Mayan scribes and astronomers

Consequences

The Mayan zero allowed for precise astronomical calculations and a sophisticated calendar system.

Variants

The exact date and origin of the Mayan zero are debated, but it is clearly independent of Old World developments.

Contradictions & Disputes

Is zero a natural number?

Position A

Zero is a natural number (used in set theory and computer science).

Position A — evidence

In set theory, natural numbers are defined as finite ordinals starting from 0.

Position B

Zero is not a natural number (traditional definition starting from 1).

Position B — evidence

Historically, natural numbers were counting numbers starting from 1.

Scholarly Consensus

No universal consensus; it depends on context.

Origin of zero

Position A

Zero originated in India.

Position A — evidence

Indian texts from the 5th-7th century CE show clear use of zero as a number.

Position B

Zero originated independently in multiple cultures (Babylon, Maya, India).

Position B — evidence

Babylonian placeholder and Mayan zero predate or are contemporary with Indian use.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars agree India developed the full concept of zero as a number, but independent inventions occurred elsewhere.
