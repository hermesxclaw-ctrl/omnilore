The Approach

In the deep forests of Mount Ashigara, a boy with skin like a red ogre wrestled bears, spoke to animals, and grew into one of Japan's most beloved folk heroes. His name was Kintaro — 'Golden Boy' — and his legend has echoed through centuries of art, theater, and story, from woodblock prints to modern anime.

Content advisory. Contains mention of violence against animals and humans in a legendary context; no explicit graphic content.
Identity

Kintarō (金太郎)

Pronunciation KEEN-tah-roh (approximately)

Known As

KintaroGolden BoyKintokiSakata no KintokiKaidōmaruOnishōji

Etymology

Kintarō (金太郎) literally means 'Golden Boy' (kin = gold, tarō = eldest son or boy). His later name Sakata no Kintoki (坂田金時) is a formal samurai name, with 'Sakata' being a clan name and 'Kintoki' a variant reading of the same characters.

Cultural Ownership

Japan; specifically associated with the regions of Kyoto, Kanagawa (Mount Ashigara), and Tokyo. The legend is a national folk hero story, widely known across Japan.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars have suggested a historical basis for Kintaro, possibly a warrior named Sakata no Kintoki who served Minamoto no Yorimitsu in the Heian period. However, the folk hero and the historical figure are often conflated; the exact relationship is uncertain. The legend has also been localized to different mountains, and some versions identify him as the son of a mountain witch or a divine being.

Description

Appearance

Kintaro is typically depicted as a young boy with red or ruddy skin, wearing a bib with the character 'kin' (金) and carrying a large axe (ono). He is often shown wrestling with a bear or riding a bear, and sometimes accompanied by a deer and a monkey. In adulthood, he is depicted as a strong samurai.

Symbols & Iconography

The axe (ono), the bib with '金', the bear, the mountain setting, and the red skin. These are iconic in Japanese art.

Behaviour & Personality

Kintaro is depicted as a wild, fearless, and superhumanly strong child who lives in nature, communicates with animals, and performs astonishing feats of strength. He is kind-hearted, loyal, and has a sense of justice, but also has a mischievous side.

Domains & Habitats

Mount Ashigara (in the Hakone region), though some versions place him on Mount Kintoki (in Kanagawa Prefecture) or other mountains. He is a mountain-dweller.

Version Differences

In many versions, he is the son of a human noblewoman and a mountain deity or spirit. In others, he is the son of a mountain witch (yama-uba). The story of how he meets Minamoto no Yorimitsu varies: sometimes he is discovered by Yorimitsu's retainer, Watanabe no Tsuna, who tests his strength; sometimes he is recruited directly. His later life as a samurai is less commonly told but exists in some versions.

Stories & Myths

The Birth of Kintaro

Source Otogizōshi and various folktalesCulture JapanesePeriod Muromachi period (14th-16th centuries) for literary record; older oral tradition

Summary

Kintaro is born to a noblewoman (often identified as the daughter of a wealthy family) who, due to circumstances, flees to Mount Ashigara. There, she raises her son in the wilderness. Some versions say his father is a mountain god or a dragon, giving him divine strength. The mother is sometimes depicted as a yama-uba (mountain witch), but this is a later conflation.

Participants

Kintaro

his mother

Consequences

Kintaro grows up in the wild, developing his extraordinary strength and kinship with animals.

Variants

The identity of the mother varies: sometimes she is a princess, sometimes a mountain witch, sometimes a human who was exiled.

The Meeting with Minamoto no Yorimitsu

Source Otogizōshi, later military chroniclesCulture JapanesePeriod Heian period (794-1185) for the historical setting; literary record in Muromachi period

Summary

Minamoto no Yorimitsu (also known as Raikō), a famous general, is traveling through the area. His retainer Watanabe no Tsuna encounters Kintaro and tests his strength, perhaps by challenging him to a wrestling match or asking him to lift a heavy object. Impressed, Yorimitsu recruits Kintaro as a retainer, giving him the name Sakata no Kintoki.

Participants

Kintaro

Minamoto no Yorimitsu

Watanabe no Tsuna

Consequences

Kintaro leaves the mountain and becomes a samurai, serving under Yorimitsu and participating in his exploits, including the famous subjugation of the demon Shuten-dōji.

Variants

Some versions have Yorimitsu himself finding Kintaro; others have Tsuna. The test of strength varies.

Kintaro and the Demon Shuten-dōji

Source Otogizōshi, military chroniclesCulture JapanesePeriod Heian period (historical setting), literary record in Muromachi period

Summary

As one of Yorimitsu's 'Four Heavenly Kings' (Shitennō), Kintoki participates in the raid on the demon Shuten-dōji, who terrorizes Kyoto. The warriors disguise themselves as mountain ascetics and defeat the demon.

Violence in the legend

Source Otogizōshi

Description

Kintaro's feats include wrestling and presumably killing bears and other wild animals. As an adult, he participates in the killing of the demon Shuten-dōji, which involves violence and possibly decapitation.

Scholarly Context

In the context of Japanese folklore, such violence is typical of hero tales and is not meant to be realistic. It symbolizes the triumph of order over chaos.

Contradictions & Disputes

Historical existence

Position A

Kintaro is a purely fictional folk hero.

Position A — evidence

No contemporary records mention him; the earliest literary sources are from centuries later.

Position B

Kintaro is based on a real person, Sakata no Kintoki, who served Minamoto no Yorimitsu.

Position B — evidence

Some later chronicles list him as a retainer of Yorimitsu, and there is a tradition that he was a historical figure.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars treat him as a legendary figure, but the possibility of a historical basis is not entirely dismissed.

Mother's identity

Position A

His mother is a yama-uba (mountain witch).

Position A — evidence

Some versions of the story, especially later ones, depict her as a yama-uba.

Position B

His mother is a noblewoman.

Position B — evidence

Earlier versions often depict her as a noblewoman, and the yama-uba connection may be a later conflation.

Scholarly Consensus

The yama-uba connection is likely a later addition; the original mother was probably human.
