Who It Is

In the deep forests of Mount Ashigara, a boy with skin like a red ogre wrestled bears, spoke to animals, and grew into one of Japan's most beloved folk heroes. His name was Kintaro — 'Golden Boy' — and his legend has echoed through centuries of art, theater, and story, from woodblock prints to modern anime.

Kintaro is depicted as a wild, fearless, and superhumanly strong child who lives in nature, communicates with animals, and performs astonishing feats of strength. He is kind-hearted, loyal, and has a sense of justice, but also has a mischievous side.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Kintarō (金太郎) literally means 'Golden Boy' (kin = gold, tarō = eldest son or boy). His later name Sakata no Kintoki (坂田金時) is a formal samurai name, with 'Sakata' being a clan name and 'Kintoki' a variant reading of the same characters.

Pronunciation — KEEN-tah-roh (approximately)

Aliases — Kintaro, Golden Boy, Kintoki, Sakata no Kintoki, Kaidōmaru, Onishōji

Ownership — Japan; specifically associated with the regions of Kyoto, Kanagawa (Mount Ashigara), and Tokyo. The legend is a national folk hero story, widely known across Japan.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Kintaro is typically depicted as a young boy with red or ruddy skin, wearing a bib with the character 'kin' (金) and carrying a large axe (ono). He is often shown wrestling with a bear or riding a bear, and sometimes accompanied by a deer and a monkey. In adulthood, he is depicted as a strong samurai.

Symbols — The axe (ono), the bib with '金', the bear, the mountain setting, and the red skin. These are iconic in Japanese art.

Domains — Mount Ashigara (in the Hakone region), though some versions place him on Mount Kintoki (in Kanagawa Prefecture) or other mountains. He is a mountain-dweller.

Accounts I
The Birth of KintaroOtogizōshi and various folktales

Muromachi period (14th-16th centuries) for literary record; older oral tradition

Kintaro is born to a noblewoman (often identified as the daughter of a wealthy family) who, due to circumstances, flees to Mount Ashigara. There, she raises her son in the wilderness. Some versions say his father is a mountain god or a dragon, giving him divine strength. The mother is sometimes depicted as a yama-uba (mountain witch), but this is a later conflation. Kintaro grows up in the wild, developing his extraordinary strength and kinship with animals.

Kintaro's Childhood FeatsOtogizōshi and folktales

Same as above

Kintaro wrestles a bear, uproots trees, and performs other feats of strength. He befriends a bear, a deer, a monkey, and other animals, who become his companions and serve him. His reputation as a wonder child spreads.

Accounts II
The Meeting with Minamoto no YorimitsuOtogizōshi, later military chronicles

Heian period (794-1185) for the historical setting; literary record in Muromachi period

Minamoto no Yorimitsu (also known as Raikō), a famous general, is traveling through the area. His retainer Watanabe no Tsuna encounters Kintaro and tests his strength, perhaps by challenging him to a wrestling match or asking him to lift a heavy object. Impressed, Yorimitsu recruits Kintaro as a retainer, giving him the name Sakata no Kintoki. Kintaro leaves the mountain and becomes a samurai, serving under Yorimitsu and participating in his exploits, including the famous subjugation of the demon Shuten-dōji.

Kintaro and the Demon Shuten-dōjiOtogizōshi, military chronicles

Heian period (historical setting), literary record in Muromachi period

As one of Yorimitsu's 'Four Heavenly Kings' (Shitennō), Kintoki participates in the raid on the demon Shuten-dōji, who terrorizes Kyoto. The warriors disguise themselves as mountain ascetics and defeat the demon. The demon is slain, and Kintoki's legend grows.
