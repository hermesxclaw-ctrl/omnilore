Who It Is

It smelled of pine forests and plague doctors' masks, tasted like Protestant rebellion, and was once blamed for stopping London from growing at all — gin began as a monk's cure-all and became the cheapest, most demonised, most inventive spirit on earth.

As beverage, gin's 'personality' is defined by juniper-forward piney/resinous character balanced by supporting botanicals. Unlike neutral vodka, gin is assertive and aromatic; unlike whisky, it is typically unaged and designed for mixing. Powers
Juniper-Predominant Flavour and Aroma

Primary organoleptic power: piney, resinous, slightly peppery flavour from Juniperus communis berries (actually cone scales) plus supporting botanicals: coriander seed (citrus-spice), angelica root (earthy fixative), orris root (violet), citrus peel, cardamom, anise, caraway, liquorice. Steam-infused or macerated then redistilled.

Limit: Juniper must predominate; over-extraction yields bitter, turpentine notes. Historical turpentine adulteration mimicked juniper cheaply but was toxic. Modern flavour stability limited; botanical oils can louche with tonic.

High Ethanol Intoxication and Preservation

37.5-50% ABV provides intoxication, analgesia, preservation of botanicals, and shelf stability. Historically valued as aqua vitae (water of life) for medicinal preservation and as safer alternative to contaminated water when mixed (though gin itself not safer than water).

Limit: Dose-dependent toxicity: acute alcohol poisoning, addiction, liver disease. Historical gin was often low-proof, contaminated, or cut with water and acids.

Medicinal and Diuretic Reputation

Juniper has documented diuretic, carminative and antiseptic properties; apothecaries prescribed juniper tonic wines for coughs, colds, pains, ruptures, cramps, kidney ailments, gallstones. Plague doctors used juniper in beak masks as miasma prophylactic.

Limit: Efficacy limited; no antibiotic or antiviral effect against plague (Yersinia pestis). Modern gin not medicinal; juniper oil can be nephrotoxic in excess.

Cocktail Base Versatility

Neutral-dry profile makes gin ideal cocktail base: martini (gin + vermouth), gin and tonic (gin + quinine tonic), Tom Collins, Negroni, Gimlet, French 75, Aviation, Bramble. Botanical complexity pairs with citrus, bitter, sweet and herbal modifiers.

Limit: Strong juniper can clash with delicate ingredients; not interchangeable with vodka/whisky without recipe adjustment. Sweetened Old Tom vs London Dry behave differently in cocktails.

Quinine Masking and Anti-Malarial Vehicle

In tropical British colonies, gin masked bitter quinine (dissolved in carbonated water as tonic) - the only effective anti-malarial until 20th century. Combination created gin and tonic as prophylactic ritual for officers and administrators.

Limit: Modern tonic contains trace quinine only as flavouring (83 ppm limit in US), no prophylactic effect. Gin itself has no anti-malarial property.

Infusion and Liqueur Platform

High-proof gin extracts flavours from fruits and botanicals for liqueurs: sloe gin (sloes + sugar), damson gin, Pimm's No.1 Cup (gin + citrus/spices, 25% ABV fruit cup). Enables seasonal preservation of hedgerow fruits.

Limit: Legally not gin if sugar >100g/L or ABV <37.5% (EU); must be labelled liqueur/fruit cup. Infusion requires months; risk of over-extraction/bitterness.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Modern gin is a clear, colourless distilled spirit (typically 37.5-50% ABV; EU minimum 37.5%, US minimum 40%) unless aged or infused (e.g., barrel-aged gin, sloe gin which is red-purple). Viscosity is light; legs in glass reflect alcohol and occasional sugar (Old Tom). Botanical charge is invisible but aromatic: juniper must be predominant by EU/US law for product labelled gin. Historical jenever (oude) can be slightly straw-tinted from malt wine

Accounts I
Monastic Aqua Vitae and the Juniper PanaceaDer Naturen Bloeme (Jacob van Maerlant, c.1266-1269) and Een Constelijck Distileerboec (Philippus Hermanni, Antwerp 1552)

13th-16th century

Across medieval Europe monks and alchemists distilled sharp, fiery aqua vitae from wine, infusing juniper berries for medicinal tonics. Juniper was already a core doctor's kit herb; plague doctors stuffed beaks with juniper to ward off miasma during the Black Death. Apothecaries dispensed juniper tonic wines for coughs, colds, pains, strains, ruptures and cramps as a cure-all. The 1552 Antwerp distilling manual provides the earliest printed jenever recipe: wine distillate redistilled with juniper and other herbs. These were medicines first, but contemporaries noted they were consumed with suspicious enthusiasm, blurring medicine and recreation. Established juniper distillate as legitimate medicine and created technical knowledge (distillation, botanical maceration) that Flemish and Dutch commercial distillers would industrialise in the 17th century. Provided lexical and recipe foundation for jenever/gin.

Dutch Courage at Antwerp and the Eighty Years' WarEnglish military accounts 1585; States General tax ordinance 1606; Philip Massinger The Duke of Milan (1623)

1585-1623

During the Eighty Years' War, English soldiers sent to support Antwerp against Spanish forces in 1585 were observed drinking jenever for calming effects before battle, giving rise to the English idiom 'Dutch courage'. By 1606 the Dutch States General was taxing 'distilled anise, gin or fennel water sold as alcoholic drinks', proving commercial circulation. By 1623 Massinger's play mentions geneva/jenever in London, and by mid-17th century numerous small Dutch and Flemish distillers were redistilling malted barley spirit (malt wine) with juniper, anise, caraway and coriander, sold in pharmacies for kidney ailments, lumbago, gout and gallstones. Jenever became an object of commerce in the spirits industry, sold beyond pharmacies. The 'Dutch courage' narrative helped anglicise jenever and prepared English palate for later gin adoption. Tax records show state fiscal interest in spirits early.

The Glorious Revolution and the Protestant DrinkParliamentary import restrictions 1689-1690; historian Angela McShane; William III and Mary II co-sovereignty

1688-1695

Gin had existed in England in varying forms since early 17th century with a brief Stuart Restoration resurgence, often crude and flavoured with turpentine. After William of Orange (William III) and Mary II took the throne in the Glorious Revolution (1688), government imposed heavy duties on imported French brandy to fund wars against Catholic France and to favour Protestant allies. This created a vacuum for domestic spirits. Gin, described by historian Angela McShane as a 'Protestant drink' because its rise was fuelled by a Protestant king and his armies fighting Catholic Irish and French, became the cheap alternative to brandy. Unlicensed production was permitted while imported spirits were penalised. Created large market for poor-quality barley unfit for brewing. Laid legal and economic groundwork for the Gin Craze. Established gin as English national spirit in opposition to French brandy.

Accounts II
The Gin Craze: Drunk for a Penny, Dead Drunk for Two PenceParliamentary records 1695-1735; William Hogarth Beer Street and Gin Lane (1751); contemporary pamphlets and magistrate reports

1695-1735 (peak 1720s-1730s)

After government allowed unlicensed gin production and taxed imports, thousands of gin shops sprang up: of 15,000 drinking establishments in London (excluding coffee and chocolate houses), over half were gin shops. Gin was cheaper than beer and spirits, consumed regularly by the poor. Beer retained healthy reputation because brewed ale was safer than unclean water; gin was blamed for social collapse, higher death rates that stabilised London's population, neglect, crime and infant mortality. William Hogarth's paired engravings Beer Street (prosperous, healthy beer drinkers) and Gin Lane (emaciated, gin-soaked despair, mother dropping child) became arguably the most potent anti-drug poster ever conceived (BBC). The epithet 'Mother's Ruin' entered language, reflecting concern over women's drinking and child welfare. Much gin was distilled legally in residential houses (1,500 residential stills in 1726) and often flavoured with turpentine for resinous woody notes. The Gin Acts: Riots, Regulation and the Birth of Licensed RetailGin Act 1736 (9 Geo. 2 c. 23); Gin Act 1751 (24 Geo. 2 c. 40)

1736-1751

The Gin Act 1736 imposed prohibitive taxes: £50 annual licence for retailers and high duties, aiming to suppress consumption. It failed catastrophically, leading to street riots, illicit stills, informer attacks and widespread evasion; duty was gradually reduced and abolished in 1742. The more successful Gin Act 1751 forced distillers to sell only to licensed retailers and brought gin shops under local magistrates' jurisdiction, raising quality and price, reducing outlets. Gin in this era was still pot-still, maltier than modern London gin. Another common variation involved distilling in presence of sulphuric acid, which creates diethyl ether via dehydration of ethanol, imparting sweet ether aroma and possible additional analgesic/intoxicating effects (Paracelsian tradition). Dutch/Belgian jenever (oude style) remained distinct, produced in Schiedam and Hasselt, referred to in American pre-Prohibition guides as Holland or Geneva gin. Column Stills, Empire and the Invention of London Dry and Gin & TonicRobert Stein patent 1826; Aeneas Coffey patent 1831; Erasmus Bond tonic water 1858; Jerry Thomas Bartender's Guide 1862

1826-1900

Invention of the continuous column still made neutral spirit (high-purity ethanol) practical and cheap, enabling the 'London dry' style to evolve later in 19th century: clean, juniper-forward, dry, without sugar or malt wine. Simultaneously, in tropical colonies, quinine dissolved in carbonated water (tonic water) was the only effective anti-malarial; its intense bitterness was masked with gin, creating gin and tonic as officer-class ritual. Gin became common base for mixed drinks including the martini. Old Tom faded by early 20th century. Sloe gin tradition (sloes infused in gin with sugar) and fruit cups like Pimm's No.1 Cup (1840s, 25% ABV) flourished. Oude jenever remained popular through 19th century, exported as Holland gin to America. London dry became global standard for gin, displacing maltier styles. Gin and tonic became enduring cocktail and cultural symbol of empire. Cocktail culture codified gin's role as base spirit.

Prohibition Bathtub Gin and the 21st-Century RenaissanceUS National Prohibition Act (Volstead Act) 1920-1933; contemporary speakeasy records; market data 2013-present

1920-1933; 2013-present

During US Prohibition, relatively simple gin production enabled illicit 'bathtub gin' compounded in homes, speakeasies and blind pigs: neutral spirit flavoured with juniper oil and botanicals, often dangerously adulterated with methanol or glycerine. Despite quality issues, it sustained cocktail culture (e.g., Bee's Knees to mask harshness). After repeal, gin remained staple but declined in fashion by late 20th century against vodka and whisky. Since 2013 gin has been in global ascendancy: many new brands and producers, strong growth, innovation with local botanicals (e.g., Hendrick's cucumber/rose, Japanese gin with yuzu, Australian gin with native botanicals), barrel-aged and pink gins, and premiumisation. National Jenever Museums in Hasselt (Belgium) and Schiedam (Netherlands) institutionalise heritage. Modern craft gin often returns to Old Tom and jenever inspiration while using column-still precision. Gin was invented by Dr Franciscus Sylvius in 1650 as a kidney medicine. — Jenever-type spirit was taxed in 1606 and mentioned in English literature in 1623, when Sylvius was a child. Sylvius worked on distillation but did not invent gin; the spirit evolved from medieval monastic juniper aqua vitae and 16th-century Flemish/Dutch commercial distilling.

Gin is just flavoured vodka - neutral spirit with juniper added after distillation. — EU/US law requires gin (especially distilled gin and London dry) to be redistilled with juniper and botanicals, not merely compounded. Jenever contains malt wine. Compound gin (infused without redistillation) exists but cannot be labelled distilled gin in EU.

Gin and tonic was invented as a clever cocktail by British officers in India who discovered quinine. — Quinine tonic water was developed separately (commercialised 1858) as anti-malarial; mixing with gin to mask bitterness evolved gradually as colonial social practice, not single invention moment. Modern tonic has only trace quinine and no prophylactic effect.

The Gin Craze proves gin is uniquely deadly compared to other spirits. — 18th-century gin was often low-quality, adulterated and consumed in context of extreme poverty, malnutrition and disease. Beer was considered healthy because brewing made water safer, not because alcohol was safe. Mortality correlation is not proof gin alone caused deaths.

'Mother's Ruin' means gin was marketed to mothers or causes ruin only for women. — Epithet reflects 18th-century gendered moral panic about women's drinking and child neglect (Hogarth's Gin Lane mother), not marketing. Gin was consumed by all genders and classes, but stigma was disproportionately applied to poor women.

London Dry Gin must be made in London. — London dry is a technical style (neutral spirit redistilled with botanicals, no added sugar/colour, juniper predominant), not a geographical indication. It can be made anywhere. Plymouth Gin is the geographically protected one.
