This page is intentionally blank and ready for source-backed lore, versions, images, and connections. Ame no Uzume is the goddess of dance, theater, entertainment, and the arts. She is also associated with divine mediation, joy, and the dispelling of darkness and despair. She is a patron of performing artists, especially dancers and actors.

Attested in Wikidata public knowledge graph — Q462803

Form

Ame no Uzume is typically depicted as a beautiful, youthful goddess with flowing black hair, often wearing the traditional attire of a Shinto priestess (a white kimono with a red hakama). In artistic depictions of the cave scene, she is shown with a spear or a sakaki branch in hand, and sometimes with a fan. Her expression is often lively and mischievous, capturing her role as the divine entertainer.

Powers

Divine Dance (Kagura) Ame no Uzume's most famous power. Her dance is not merely entertainment; it is a ritual act capable of altering the emotional and spiritual state of the heavens. Her dance is said to be the origin of kagura, the sacred dance performed at Shinto shrines. It is a power of joy, laughter, and transformation. Kojiki and Nihon Shoki (both) The dance is most effective when performed in a state of genuine abandon and joy. A forced or insincere performance would lack its divine potency. None

Divine Persuasion / Mediation Ame no Uzume is a master of persuasion, able to influence even the most stubborn of deities. She uses her wit, charm, and performance to change the course of events. This is most clearly seen in the cave episode, where she convinces Amaterasu to emerge. Kojiki and Nihon Shoki (both) Her persuasion is indirect; she works through emotional and psychological means rather than direct confrontation or force. None

Divine Voice / Chant In the Nihon Shoki, Ame no Uzume is said to have chanted a ritual invocation as part of her dance. This suggests a power of spoken or sung words to influence the divine realm. Nihon Shoki The exact nature of this power is not detailed in the text. None

Kagura Invocation Ame-no-Uzume can summon, sustain, and direct the ecstatic dance that opens communication between realms. Kagura, the ritual dance she originated, is believed to attract kami, seal covenants, and purify space through rhythmic movement and music. It is both entertainment and technology-of-the-divine, allowing humans and gods to speak across thresholds. Kojiki (712 CE); kagura transmission lineages Kagura is a communal, embodied practice—Uzume cannot compel a god or spirit through dance alone if the recipient is actively unwilling; Amaterasu was ready to emerge once curiosity overcame her isolation. Some scholars argue kagura evolved from multiple shamanic streams, not a single founder; others maintain Uzume is the mythological eponym of the art.

Great Persuasion Known as The Great Persuader, Uzume can reframe social and cosmic standoffs through humor, spectacle, and theatrical provocation. Her cave strategy converted despair into collective laughter and curiosity. She does not argue theology; she engineers a mood so potent that gods change their minds. Kojiki, Nihon Shoki Her persuasion works through emotion rather than logic; it is most effective on crowds, not isolated individuals. Nihon Shoki attributes the cave persuasion partly to Ame-no-Koyane and Futodama, not Uzume alone.

Realm-Walking She traverses the floating bridge between heaven and earth, accompanies divine descent missions, and mediates at liminal thresholds. This is less a teleportation power than a ritual status: she is at home wherever performance crosses worlds. Kojiki descent-to-earth episode No explicit cosmology limits her range, but she is consistently shown moving at order of higher kami (e.g., Amaterasu). None.

Animal Speech and Cutting In one variant she cuts the mouth of the sea cucumber, rendering it permanently silent. This displays a dangerous, surgical power over living creatures and operates as an etiological explanation rather than a display of cruelty. Nihon Shoki / kagura etiology Only attested in a single brief episode; scope is unclear. Some regional kagura narratives omit the sea cucumber episode entirely.

Behavior & Habitat

Ame no Uzume is characterized by her boldness, wit, and uninhibited nature. She is the only deity in the Kojiki who dares to challenge the despair of the other gods with laughter and dance. She is a trickster figure, but her tricks are always in service of a greater good. She is also a mediator, using her performance to draw Amaterasu out of her cave and restore light to the world.

Accounts

After Susanoo's violence against Amaterasu's loom and the killing of one of her maidens, the sun goddess retreats into the Amano-Iwato cave, plunging the world into darkness. The assembled kami try every diplomatic and magical tactic and fail. Uzume then overturns a tub, stamps on it in a lewd dance, strips off her garments, and makes the gods laugh so heartily that Amaterasu, curious about the commotion, peeks out. A mirror and jewel are placed to draw her further. When she steps out, Ame-no-Tajikarawo blocks the cave entrance so she cannot retreat. Light returns to the earth. Uzume's dance is credited as the origin of kagura.

When Amaterasu sends her grandson Ninigi to rule the central land, the divine embassy reaches the floating bridge of heaven and is blocked by Sarutahiko, the pathfinder kami. Uzume is sent forward. In one version she flirts with Sarutahiko; in another she negotiates with charm and wit. Sarutahiko is won over and allows passage, then marries Uzume. Together they found the Sarume clan, a lineage of female miko (shrine mediums) and performers.

During the descent to earth, Uzume uses her dagger to cut the mouth of the sea cucumber (namako), rendering it permanently silent. This is an etiological tale explaining why sea cucumbers do not cry out when caught or cooked, and it is reenacted in some kagura movements.

Beyond the single cave incident, Uzume is credited as the mythic source of kagura, which in turn absorbed three shamanic ritual traditions: Omoto Kagura, Hana Matsuri, and Hayachine Kagura. Each preserves choreographic elements interpreted as shamanic communication with kami rather than mere entertainment. Uzume's dance therefore survives not only in myth but in living bodies.

On October 28, 1920, Tomokiyo Yoshisane, founder of the new religion Shintō Tenkōkyo, received a divine revelation from Ame-no-Uzume during a kishin session (spirit possession). She taught him the ritual mikiyome tamashizume, a body-purification and soul-strengthening rite. This positions Uzume as an active, post-Meiji religious teacher, not only an archaic mythological figure.

At the Tori-no-Ichi (Rooster Market) festivals held in November at shrines dedicated to Uzume and Sarutahiko, vendors sell masks and pictures of the divine couple. The festival has become a popular occasion for merchants and entertainers to pray for commercial success and artistic skill. The masks literally bring Uzume's face into the marketplace.

Content Notes

Uzume's cave dance involves exposing her breasts and genitals. In ancient Japanese ritual, such exposure was a deliberate act of boundary-crossing meant to shock the collective into laughter and break taboos. It is not described as sexual violence or humiliation in the texts, but modern readers may misinterpret it as crude or degrading.

Scholars such as Yoshie Akio have interpreted the exposure as a shamanic 'reversal of order' that reconfigures cosmic hierarchy through comedy rather than shame.

Kojiki (712 CE)

Uzume cuts the mouth of the sea cucumber, silencing it permanently. This minor violence is presented as etiological humor, not cruelty, but it shows her willingness to mutilate to enforce silence or mark an oath.

Read as a mythic justification for the silence of certain animals, or as a lesson about keeping secrets in ritual contexts.

Scholarly Debates
Who lured Amaterasu from the cave?
Uzume alone, through her dance and the mirror/jewel trick. ⇌ A collaborative effort including Ame-no-Koyane, Futodama, and Tajikarao, with Uzume's dance as one element.
Evidence for A: Kojiki emphasizes Uzume's solo initiative.
Evidence for B: Nihon Shoki distributes roles across multiple kami; some passages omit her entirely.
Is Uzume an Inari kami?
Yes, she is identical with Ōmiyanome-no-Ōkami and therefore part of the Inari network. ⇌ No, she is a distinct heavenly kami; the Inari connection is a later Edo-period synthesis based on her marriage to Sarutahiko.
Evidence for A: Some shrine genealogies list her as Inari; her name Ōmiyanome links her to rice/abundance.
Evidence for B: Kojiki and Nihon Shoki do not identify her with Inari; Inari is not mentioned in the cave myth.
Directional association
Uzume is associated with the east, like the dawn she personifies. ⇌ No explicit cardinal direction is assigned to her in primary texts; east is inferred from dawn symbolism.
Evidence for A: Comparative Indo-European dawn goddesses (Ushas, Eos) are eastern.
Evidence for B: Kojiki and Nihon Shoki do not state her direction.
What We Don't Know

The exact liturgical choreography of the first kagura is not recoverable from surviving texts. Regional kagura lineages preserve secret transmissions that have not been fully documented in open scholarship. The relationship between Ame-no-Uzume and Ōmiyanome-no-Ōkami remains unresolved because Edo-period shrine genealogies conflate them without explicit proof. The proposed Indo-European parallel via Witzel's Laurasian hypothesis is controversial and not widely accepted by Japanese scholars. Primary-source translations of the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki cave episodes vary in their rendering of Uzume's exposure; no consensus English edition exists that captures the ritual register without modernizing or sanitizing it.

A way to hold it in your mind

If the Shinto pantheon were a rock band, Ame no Uzume would be the lead singer and frontwoman — the one who knows how to work a crowd, turn a disaster into a celebration, and get the whole world dancing again. She's the ultimate proof that sometimes the most powerful force in the universe is a well-timed joke.
