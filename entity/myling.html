Who It Is

Imagine walking home through a dark Scandinavian forest. The snow is deep, the wind is cold, and you are alone. Then you hear it — a child crying. Not a baby's cry, but the thin, desperate wail of a small child who has been left alone in the dark. You stop. You listen. The crying continues, but you see nothing. You walk faster. The crying follows you. You break into a run. The crying keeps pace, and now you feel it — a weight on your back, small but impossibly heavy, like a child climbing onto your shoulders. You are not alone. The myling has found you.

The myling's defining behavior is its relentless pursuit of the living — specifically, it seeks out travelers, hunters, and woodcutters, and will follow them for hours or days, crying and wailing. It is described as tireless and unshakeable. The myling does not attack directly in most accounts; instead, it torments its victim through its constant, unbearable crying. Its goal is not to kill but to force its victim to acknowledge it and, crucially, to 'give it a name' — to recognize it as a human being. Powers
Tireless Pursuit

The myling can follow a victim for days without rest, never tiring, never giving up. It is described as being able to match any pace, including that of a running horse or a fast-moving sled.

Limit: The myling is said to lose track of its victim if they cross running water (in some accounts) or if they can make it to a church or other consecrated ground.

Weight Manipulation

The myling can climb onto a person's back, sled, or horse and add immense weight — making movement nearly impossible. This is described as a physical weight, not just a psychological burden.

Limit: The weight is lifted if the victim can 'name' the myling or perform a specific ritual (such as throwing a piece of steel over it).

Supernatural Wailing

The myling's cry is described as piercing, constant, and impossible to ignore. It can be heard from a great distance and does not fade with distance. The sound is said to cause deep psychological distress in those who hear it.

Limit: The wailing stops if the myling is 'released' (given a proper burial or acknowledgment).

Invisibility / Partial Invisibility

In many accounts, the myling can choose to be invisible, revealing itself only when it wants to be seen. It is often heard before it is seen, and some victims report feeling its presence or hearing its cry without ever seeing it.

Limit: The myling seems to become visible when it wants to be acknowledged, or when it is actively pursuing a victim.

Shape-shifting (minor)

In a few accounts, the myling is described as being able to take the form of a small animal — most commonly a bird or a cat — though this is less common than the standard child form.

Limit: The shape-shifting appears to be limited to small forms and does not seem to be used for deception — the myling always reveals its true nature eventually.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The myling is described in most accounts as appearing as a small, naked child — often described as grey-skinned or pale, with hollow eyes and a thin, hungry face. Some accounts describe it as having a disproportionately large head on a tiny body. It is frequently described as being wet or dripping with water (particularly when encountered near bogs or streams), and its footprints are said to leave no trace or to be reversed (toes pointing backwar

Symbols — The myling is symbolically associated with: the forest itself (as a place of abandonment and death); the crossroads (where it was sometimes buried, and where it is believed to linger); the unborn and unbaptized child (in Lutheran Scandinavia, unbaptized children were believed to be denied proper afterlife, making them vulnerable to becoming mylingar); the cry or wail (as a symbol of unacknowledged grief and injustice); and the weight of the dead

Domains — Forests, marshes, bogs, lonely roads, crossroads, the edges of settlements. The myling is a liminal creature — it occupies the boundary between the human world and the wild, between life and death. It is most commonly encountered in the deep forest, far from human habitation, but it is also associated with the 'utmark' (the outlying land beyond cultivated fields) — a space that was both economically important and spiritually dangerous i

Accounts I
The Woodcutter's BurdenCollected in Hyltén-Cavallius 'Wärend och Wirdarne' (1863)

19th century

A woodcutter working deep in the forest hears a crying sound. He ignores it, but the crying follows him as he works. As he prepares to leave, he finds a small, grey child standing at the edge of the clearing. The child climbs onto his back, and he feels an immense weight pressing down on him. He struggles to walk, the child's thin arms around his neck, its crying constant in his ear. He finally manages to reach the edge of the forest, where he encounters an old woman who recognizes what is happening. She tells him to throw a piece of steel (a knife, a nail) over his shoulder. He does, and the myling falls away, its cry fading into the distance. The woodcutter survives but is marked by the experience — he is said to have been 'haunted' by the memory for years, and he never returns to that part of the forest.

Accounts II
The Sled RideULMA folklore archive, collected 1920s

Early 20th century

A farmer is driving his horse-drawn sled home through the forest at night. He hears a child crying by the roadside. He stops, but sees nothing. As he continues, he hears the crying again — this time, the sound seems to be coming from the sled behind him. He looks back and sees a small, grey shape huddled in the back of the sled. He tries to drive faster, but the horse becomes agitated and refuses to move. The crying grows louder. The farmer, desperate, throws his mittens at the shape — nothing. He throws his whip — nothing. Finally, he remembers what his grandmother told him: he calls out a name — any name — and says 'I name you.' The crying stops. The shape is gone. The horse moves again, and the farmer drives home, shaken. The farmer reports the experience to a folklore collector decades later, noting that he never traveled that road at night again.

The Unbaptized ChildLandstad, 'Norske Folkeviser' (1853)

19th century (ballad likely older)

A young woman conceives a child out of wedlock. To hide her shame, she gives birth in secret and smothers the infant, burying it in the forest. She marries and has a family, but her new life is haunted by the sound of a crying child. The crying follows her everywhere — in her home, in the fields, in church. She grows thin and pale, unable to sleep. Finally, she confesses to a priest, who tells her that the child's soul cannot rest until it is acknowledged and given a proper burial. She returns to the forest, finds the spot where she buried the child, and gives it a name. The crying stops. She builds a small grave marker and lives the rest of her life in penance. The woman's confession and the child's burial release the spirit. The story serves as a moral warning against infanticide and the shame that drove it.

Nature of the myling: vengeful vs. pitifulThe myling is a vengeful spirit seeking to punish the living for its death — it pursues travelers and mothers alike, causing suffering.The myling is a pitiful creature seeking only acknowledgment and release — its crying is a plea, not a threat, and it can be released through naming or burial.
Origin of the belief: pre-Christian vs. ChristianThe myling belief predates Christianity and is rooted in pre-Christian Scandinavian concepts of the dead and the afterlife.The myling belief is a product of Christian (specifically Lutheran) theology, particularly the doctrine of baptism and the fate of unbaptized souls.
The myling's physical formThe myling is always visible as a small, grey child.The myling is usually invisible, heard but not seen.

The myling is a 'monster' that attacks and kills its victims. — In traditional folklore, the myling almost never directly attacks or kills. It torments through its crying and its weight, but its goal is acknowledgment and release, not murder. The 'monster' interpretation is a modern pop-culture invention.

The myling is the same as the 'changeling'. — The changeling is a different tradition — a fairy child left in place of a human child. The myling is the ghost of a dead child, not a substitute. The confusion may arise from both involving children and the supernatural.

The myling only haunts its mother. — While the mother is a primary target in many narratives, the myling also haunts travelers, woodcutters, hunters, and anyone who encounters it in the forest. It is a general 'forest ghost' as much as a personal haunting.

The myling is a uniquely Scandinavian belief with no parallels elsewhere. — The 'undead child' motif appears across Indo-European traditions — the Slavic poroniec, the Balkan drekavac, the Baltic vilkas, and similar figures in Celtic and Germanic folklore. The Scandinavian myling is a regional variant of a broader pattern.
