Myling

The Unbaptized Child Ghost, Murdered Infant's Wraith

I cry in the forest where she left me. Pick me up — I become heavier with every step. You cannot reach the churchyard until you speak her name. Say it. Say my mother's name, and bury me in the graveyard.

Who It Is

The Myling (also Myrding, Utburd in Norwegian) is the ghost of an unbaptized infant who was murdered by its mother and buried secretly — usually in a forest, marsh, or under a pile of stones. In a time when infanticide was the desperate recourse of unmarried or impoverished mothers, the Myling legend provided a supernatural framework for the horror and guilt. The Myling manifests as a crying baby heard at night in desolate places. It grows heavier and heavier if someone picks it up or tries to help, and it demands to be carried to consecrated ground for proper burial. The tragedy of the Myling is that the act of carrying it becomes supernatural labour: it grows impossibly heavy, the ground turns to marsh, the path lengthens endlessly. The only way to lay the Myling to rest is to give it a name and bury it in the churchyard with a proper Christian rite — and to speak the mother's name, acknowledging the crime. Some variants call the Myling 'Utburd' (literally 'that which is carried out' — i.e., the infant left to die of exposure in the forest, a historical practice in Scandinavia before Christianity and intermittently afterward). The Myling is related to the broader European 'unbaptized infant ghost' complex (Drekavac in the Balkans, Poroniec in Poland) but is distinct in its physicality — the ghost is not a wail alone but a pressing, crushing weight that demands physical labour as expiation. The haunting is a demand for acknowledgment: name the crime, bury the body, speak the mother's guilt, and the child can rest.

Form

The myling is described in most accounts as appearing as a small, naked child — often described as grey-skinned or pale, with hollow eyes and a thin, hungry face. Some accounts describe it as having a disproportionately large head on a tiny body. It is frequently described as being wet or dripping with water (particularly when encountered near bogs or streams), and its footprints are said to leave no trace or to be reversed (toes pointing backward). In some accounts, the myling appears as a ball of light or a small, indistinct grey shape that resolves into child form when approached. It is almost never described as wearing clothing — the nakedness is part of the horror, emphasizing its vulnerability and abandonment. The myling's most distinctive feature in many accounts is its voice: a thin, high-pitched wail that is described as both childlike and profoundly unsettling.

Powers

Tireless Pursuit The myling can follow a victim for days without rest, never tiring, never giving up. It is described as being able to match any pace, including that of a running horse or a fast-moving sled. Consistent across Swedish and Norwegian accounts The myling is said to lose track of its victim if they cross running water (in some accounts) or if they can make it to a church or other consecrated ground. Some accounts say the myling cannot cross water at all; others say it can follow across any terrain. The church-ground limitation is more consistent.

Weight Manipulation The myling can climb onto a person's back, sled, or horse and add immense weight — making movement nearly impossible. This is described as a physical weight, not just a psychological burden. Common in Swedish accounts from the 19th century The weight is lifted if the victim can 'name' the myling or perform a specific ritual (such as throwing a piece of steel over it). Some accounts describe the weight as purely psychological — a feeling of being weighed down by guilt.

Supernatural Wailing The myling's cry is described as piercing, constant, and impossible to ignore. It can be heard from a great distance and does not fade with distance. The sound is said to cause deep psychological distress in those who hear it. Universal across all accounts The wailing stops if the myling is 'released' (given a proper burial or acknowledgment). None significant — the wailing is the myling's most consistent trait.

Invisibility / Partial Invisibility In many accounts, the myling can choose to be invisible, revealing itself only when it wants to be seen. It is often heard before it is seen, and some victims report feeling its presence or hearing its cry without ever seeing it. Common in accounts where the myling is encountered at night The myling seems to become visible when it wants to be acknowledged, or when it is actively pursuing a victim. Some accounts describe the myling as always visible (a grey shape in the forest); others describe it as only audible.

Shape-shifting (minor) In a few accounts, the myling is described as being able to take the form of a small animal — most commonly a bird or a cat — though this is less common than the standard child form. Rare; appears in some Swedish dialect accounts The shape-shifting appears to be limited to small forms and does not seem to be used for deception — the myling always reveals its true nature eventually. This is a minority tradition; most accounts describe the myling as always appearing as a child.

Behavior & Habitat

The myling's defining behavior is its relentless pursuit of the living — specifically, it seeks out travelers, hunters, and woodcutters, and will follow them for hours or days, crying and wailing. It is described as tireless and unshakeable. The myling does not attack directly in most accounts; instead, it torments its victim through its constant, unbearable crying. Its goal is not to kill but to force its victim to acknowledge it and, crucially, to 'give it a name' — to recognize it as a human being. In some accounts, the myling will climb onto a traveler's back or sled, adding its weight and making the journey nearly impossible. It is particularly active at night, in forests, and near bogs and marshes. In some versions, the myling will only appear to those who are alone — it is said to avoid groups of people. The myling is also associated with specific locations: the site of its death, crossroads, and the homes of its parents (where it may return to haunt them).

Accounts

A woodcutter working deep in the forest hears a crying sound. He ignores it, but the crying follows him as he works. As he prepares to leave, he finds a small, grey child standing at the edge of the clearing. The child climbs onto his back, and he feels an immense weight pressing down on him. He struggles to walk, the child's thin arms around his neck, its crying constant in his ear. He finally manages to reach the edge of the forest, where he encounters an old woman who recognizes what is happening. She tells him to throw a piece of steel (a knife, a nail) over his shoulder. He does, and the myling falls away, its cry fading into the distance.

A farmer is driving his horse-drawn sled home through the forest at night. He hears a child crying by the roadside. He stops, but sees nothing. As he continues, he hears the crying again — this time, the sound seems to be coming from the sled behind him. He looks back and sees a small, grey shape huddled in the back of the sled. He tries to drive faster, but the horse becomes agitated and refuses to move. The crying grows louder. The farmer, desperate, throws his mittens at the shape — nothing. He throws his whip — nothing. Finally, he remembers what his grandmother told him: he calls out a name — any name — and says 'I name you.' The crying stops. The shape is gone. The horse moves again, and the farmer drives home, shaken.

A young woman conceives a child out of wedlock. To hide her shame, she gives birth in secret and smothers the infant, burying it in the forest. She marries and has a family, but her new life is haunted by the sound of a crying child. The crying follows her everywhere — in her home, in the fields, in church. She grows thin and pale, unable to sleep. Finally, she confesses to a priest, who tells her that the child's soul cannot rest until it is acknowledged and given a proper burial. She returns to the forest, finds the spot where she buried the child, and gives it a name. The crying stops. She builds a small grave marker and lives the rest of her life in penance.

Field Notes

† The myling is one of the few Scandinavian folk creatures that is almost never described as malevolent in its original folklore — it is a victim, not a monster, and its 'attacks' are really cries for acknowledgment.

† The 'naming' ritual — giving the myling a name to release it — is a direct parallel to Christian baptism, suggesting that the folk tradition was deeply shaped by Lutheran theology.

† In some Swedish accounts, throwing a piece of steel (a knife, a nail, a needle) over the myling is enough to banish it — steel was believed to repel supernatural creatures across Scandinavian folklore.

† The myling is often described as leaving no footprints in snow or mud — a detail that marks it as definitively dead, since the living always leave traces.

† The myling tradition persisted into the early 20th century — folklore collectors in the 1920s and 1930s recorded first-hand accounts from elderly informants who claimed to have encountered the myling in their youth.

What We Don't Know

1. Primary source access: The Hyltén-Cavallius and Landstad texts are available but not fully digitized in accessible form; I have relied on secondary summaries and quoted extracts. 2. Regional variation: The myling tradition varies significantly between Sweden, Norway, and Denmark, and even between regions within each country; a comprehensive regional mapping requires access to the full ULMA archive, which is not fully digitized. 3. Archaeological evidence: The connection between the myling belief and actual infant burial practices is suggestive but not definitively established; more work is needed on the archaeology of infant burials in rural Scandinavia. 4. Modern reception: The myling's presence in modern pop culture (games, films, internet folklore) is under-documented; a comprehensive survey of its appearances would require a separate research pass. 5. Language: I have not been able to access several key Norwegian and Danish sources in their original language, relying instead on English and Swedish-language summaries.

A way to hold it in your mind

The myling is the Scandinavian forest's conscience. It is not a monster that hunts for prey — it is a cry that refuses to be silenced. It is the ghost of every child who was never acknowledged, never named, never given a place in the world of the living. To encounter the myling is to be confronted with a question: what do we owe to those we have abandoned? The myling's power is not in its claws or its teeth — it has none — but in its voice, which carries the weight of a community's guilt and a mother's grief. It is the sound of a story that was never allowed to be told, demanding to be heard.
