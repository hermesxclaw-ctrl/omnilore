The Approach

In the smoky clubs of 1940s New York, a new sound emerged that would forever change jazz—bebop. It was fast, complex, and unapologetically intellectual, a 'musician's music' that demanded close listening. But where did it come from, and why did it ignite such a revolution?

Identity

Bebop

Pronunciation /ˈbiːbɒp/ (bee-bop)

Known As

BopRebop

Etymology

Derived from nonsense syllables (vocables) used in scat singing. First known use in McKinney's Cotton Pickers' 'Four or Five Times' (1928), again in Jack Teagarden's 'I'se a Muggin'' (1936), and 'rebop' in 1939 recordings. Thelonious Monk claimed the original title 'Bip Bop' for his composition '52nd Street Theme' was the origin. Dizzy Gillespie stated audiences coined the name after hearing him scat the then-nameless compositions. Another theory suggests derivation from 'Arriba! Arriba!' used by Latin American bandleaders.

Cultural Ownership

African American jazz musicians in the United States, primarily in New York City, in the early to mid-1940s.

Disputed Identifications

Some researchers speculate the term was used by Charlie Christian because it sounded like something he hummed. The exact origin remains uncertain; multiple theories exist.

Description

Appearance

Not applicable; bebop is a musical style, not a visual entity.

Symbols & Iconography

Associated with the small combo format: saxophone, trumpet, piano, double bass, drums. Often depicted in art and photography with berets, horn-rimmed glasses, and goatees, though these are stereotypical.

Behaviour & Personality

Characterized by fast tempos, complex chord progressions, rapid chord changes, angular melodies, substitute chords, and virtuosic improvisation. It was intended as 'musician's music' for close listening, not dancing.

Domains & Habitats

Developed in the United States, particularly in New York City clubs like Minton's Playhouse and Monroe's Uptown House. It spread internationally in the late 1940s and 1950s.

Version Differences

Late bop moved towards extended forms, departing from pop and show compositions. Rebop differed from bebop with its more impressionistic use of discordant chords.

Stories & Myths

The Birth of Bebop

Source Various oral histories and scholarly accountsCulture African American jazz culturePeriod Early to mid-1940s

Summary

Younger jazz musicians, including Charlie Parker, Dizzy Gillespie, Thelonious Monk, and Kenny Clarke, expanded the creative possibilities of jazz beyond swing, creating a new 'musician's music' that was not danceable and demanded close listening. They explored advanced harmonies, complex syncopation, and intricate melodies, often using contrafacts based on popular swing-era chord progressions.

Participants

Charlie Parker

Dizzy Gillespie

Thelonious Monk

Kenny Clarke

Charlie Christian

Bud Powell

Max Roach

Art Blakey

Consequences

Bebop became the foundation of modern jazz, influencing subsequent styles such as cool jazz, hard bop, and modal jazz.

Variants

Some accounts emphasize the role of Minton's Playhouse and Monroe's Uptown House as incubators. Others highlight the influence of Latin music and the 'Arriba!' theory.

Parker's 'Cherokee' Revelation

Source Charlie Parker, quoted in multiple sourcesCulture African American jazz culturePeriod 1942

Summary

Parker described a transcendent moment while performing 'Cherokee' at Clark Monroe's Uptown House, where he discovered that using higher intervals of a chord as a melody line with appropriately related changes allowed him to play what he had been hearing.

Participants

Charlie Parker

Consequences

This breakthrough led to the development of bebop harmony, including chord substitutions and use of upper intervals.

Variants

Some scholars, like Gerhard Kubik, argue that this harmonic development sprang from African tonal sensibilities and the blues, not Western art music.
