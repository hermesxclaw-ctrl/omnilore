Who It Is

In the quiet Roman town of Godmanchester, someone once carved a stone to a god who exists for us only as a name on that altar. Abandinus—spirit of the River Great Ouse, guardian of a single community—has no myths, no temples, no statues. Yet his altar outlived the empire that produced it, a two-thousand-year-old whisper from a person whose name is lost, to a god whose only biography is a stone.

As a localized divine figure, Abandinus was likely venerated as a protective spirit associated with the River Great Ouse and its surrounding territory. The votive altar suggests a personal or communal act of devotion, possibly seeking healing, protection, or prosperity.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Abandinus is believed to derive from the River Great Ouse, which was known in antiquity as the Abus or Abona. The theonym likely means 'of the river Abus' or 'he who belongs to the Abus', linking the deity intrinsically to the watercourse that defined the local landscape.

Pronunciation — ah-BAN-din-us

Aliases — Abandinus

Ownership — Romano-British; a syncretic deity venerated in Roman Britain, likely by a local Celtic population under Roman occupation.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No depiction, statue, or iconographic representation of Abandinus survives. The deity is known solely from a single inscribed altar; any physical appearance is entirely unattested and should not be conjectured.

Symbols — No symbols are attested. The only associated object is the inscribed stone altar itself.

Domains — Rivers, local waterways, territorial protection, possibly healing or prosperity, Romano-British religious life.

Accounts I
The lone votiveArchaeological record (RIB 2516)

2nd–3rd century CE

A single altar dedicated to Abandinus was erected at the Roman settlement of Godmanchester (Durovigutum). The dedicator's name is lost. The act itself—carving a stone altar and setting it up—is the only 'story' we have; no myths, legends, or narratives about Abandinus survive. The altar survived into the modern era, providing the sole evidence for this deity's existence.
