Hunah Ku is a supreme creator god in the Maya tradition recorded by Spanish sources, meaning 'The Only God' or 'One God.' Described by Bishop Diego de Landa in the 16th century as the supreme being worshipped by the Yucatec Maya, Hunah Ku was an invisible, incorporeal creator who existed before all other gods. His status in the classic Maya pantheon is debated among scholars.

The Approach

In the deep well of Maya cosmology, before the first dawn broke over the maize fields, there was a name whispered in the dark: Hunah Ku, the One God, the single heartbeat of creation. But was this a primordial deity of the ancient Maya, or a colonial invention—a native echo of the Spanish God forced onto the stones of Chichen Itza? The answer, like the god himself, remains half in shadow, half in light.

Content advisory. None required. Contains discussions of colonial-era religious syncretism and the potential misinterpretation of ancient Maya texts.
Identity

Hunah Ku

Pronunciation Approximately 'hoo-NAH koo' (Yucatec Maya: /huˈnaʔ ku/), with 'Hunab' sometimes pronounced 'hoo-NAHB'.

Known As

Hunab Ku

Etymology

The name is typically parsed from Yucatec Maya as 'hun' (one) and 'ku' (god), yielding 'One God' or 'Only God.' Alternative interpretations suggest 'hunab' may relate to 'hun' (one) and 'ab' (measure or unit), possibly meaning 'the measure of the cosmos' or 'the single source.'

Cultural Ownership

Yucatec Maya culture, particularly the colonial-period Maya of the Yucatán Peninsula. The term appears in the Chilam Balam books and in the writings of Diego de Landa. It is not attested in Classic-period Maya inscriptions.

Disputed Identifications

Scholars debate whether Hunah Ku was a genuine pre-Columbian deity or a colonial-era construct. Some equate him with Itzamna, the aged creator god, while others see him as a Christianized interpretation of a remote creator. No ancient depiction or glyph has been securely identified as Hunah Ku.

Description

Appearance

No described appearance. Hunah Ku is generally considered a remote, abstract deity without iconography or physical form. No known depictions exist.

Symbols & Iconography

None known. Unlike other Maya gods, Hunah Ku is not associated with any animal, object, or glyph.

Behaviour & Personality

Described as a supreme creator, distinct from the many other gods. In colonial sources, he is portrayed as a single, all-powerful deity, but his personality is not elaborated. He is more a theological concept than a character.

Domains & Habitats

Heavenly or celestial realm; not tied to any earthly location. He is a creator god, but his specific domains (e.g., creation, sky) are not clearly delineated.

Version Differences

The earliest colonial sources (Landa) present Hunab Ku as a monotheistic creator. Later Maya texts, like the Chilam Balam, use the term more loosely, possibly as a general expression of divinity. Modern New Age interpretations have reinvented him as a cosmic creator, often conflating him with other gods.

Powers & Abilities

Creation of the universe

Source Version Colonial Yucatec (Landa)

Description

According to Diego de Landa, Hunab Ku was the creator of all things. This is the only power explicitly attributed to him in colonial sources.

Limitations

No specific limitations are recorded. The power is only mentioned in passing.

Contradictions

No other Maya sources attribute creation to Hunah Ku; the Popol Vuh attributes creation to Tepeu and Gucumatz.

Supreme deity status

Source Version Colonial Yucatec (Landa)

Description

Landa describes him as 'the one living and true God,' implying omnipotence and omniscience, but these attributes are not elaborated.

Limitations

This may be a projection of Christian concepts onto Maya beliefs.

Contradictions

Maya religion was polytheistic; the concept of a single supreme god is unusual and likely influenced by Christianity.

Colonial religious suppression

Description

The Spanish conquest and subsequent missionary activities led to the destruction of Maya religious practices and texts. The concept of Hunah Ku may have been altered or invented during this period as missionaries sought to find parallels to Christianity.

Scholarly Context

Modern scholars view colonial sources with caution, recognizing the potential for bias and misrepresentation.

Contradictions & Disputes

Pre-Columbian vs. Colonial origin

Position A

Hunah Ku was a genuine pre-Columbian Maya deity, possibly equivalent to Itzamna.

Position A — evidence

The term 'hunab ku' appears in colonial Maya texts like the Chilam Balam, which may preserve pre-Columbian concepts. Some scholars see linguistic and conceptual parallels with other Maya creator gods.

Position B

Hunah Ku was a colonial invention, created by missionaries or Maya converts under Christian influence.

Position B — evidence

The first and most detailed description comes from Diego de Landa, a missionary who actively suppressed Maya religion. The concept of a single creator god is atypical of Maya polytheism and suspiciously similar to Christianity. No Classic-period inscriptions or iconography reference Hunah Ku.

Scholarly Consensus

Most contemporary scholars lean toward the colonial-invention hypothesis, but the debate remains open due to the scarcity of evidence.

Relationship to Itzamna

Position A

Hunah Ku is synonymous with Itzamna.

Position A — evidence

Itzamna is a creator god in Yucatec Maya religion, and Landa mentions both Hunab Ku and Itzamna, possibly as separate entities.

Position B

Hunah Ku is a distinct deity or a purely abstract concept.

Position B — evidence

Landa lists Hunab Ku and Itzamna separately, suggesting they were distinct. Also, Itzamna has a rich mythology and iconography, while Hunah Ku has none.

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus; the identification is speculative.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

No archaeological or epigraphic evidence for Hunah Ku exists; the term is only known from colonial texts. The original pronunciation and meaning are uncertain. The relationship between Hunah Ku and other Maya deities is unclear. Modern New Age interpretations have further muddied the waters. Further research into colonial Maya dictionaries and unpublished manuscripts may shed light, but access is limited. The research cutoff date is August 1, 2026.

This dossier covers the historical and scholarly understanding of Hunah Ku (Hunab Ku) in Maya studies, from early colonial sources to modern academic debates. Cultural Significance

Hunah Ku's significance lies in the ongoing scholarly debate about whether Maya religion was genuinely polytheistic or contained monotheistic elements. The concept of a supreme, invisible creator god appears in many cultures alongside the worship of lesser, more accessible deities, and Maya religion may have followed this pattern. De Landa's description has influenced popular understanding of Maya religion, though modern scholarship tends to emphasize the primacy of Itzamna or the creator gods of the Popol Vuh as the more authentically Maya supreme beings.
