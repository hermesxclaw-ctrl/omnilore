Who It Is

Two small boys are left at the altar of Hera on the hill above Corinth — either to save them, or to hide a murder. By morning they are dead, and for the next two thousand years everyone will blame their mother.

In Euripides they have no speaking lines; their behaviour is that of innocent, trusting children who love their mother and are used as pawns to deliver the poisoned robe to Glauce. The paidagogos describes them as playful and obedient. No source attributes personality traits, speech, or agency to Mermerus individually; Pheres is even less individualized. Later moralizing sources treat them purely as victims.

Powers
Mortal Child Status (No Supernatural Powers)

Mermerus and Pheres are consistently presented as ordinary mortal infants/boys. No source attributes divine parentage effects, magic, shapeshifting, prophecy, or heroic strength. Their only 'power' is narrative: as sons of a grandson of Helios (via Aeëtes) and of Jason, they carry heroic lineage but do not manifest it.

Limit: Completely vulnerable to adult violence; unable to resist Medea, Corinthians, or wild animals. In Euripides they cry out offstage but cannot escape.

Hero-Cult After Death (Posthumous Timai)

After death they receive heroic honours at Corinth: tomb before the city, annual sacrifices, and rites performed by Corinthian children (hair offering, mourning dress) as atonement. Euripides has Medea institute the rite; Pausanias describes it as still visible in his day.

Limit: Cult is propitiatory and expiatory, not empowering; does not grant them agency or return. Pausanias notes the Romans abolished the child-mourning custom by his time.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No ancient source gives a physical description of Mermerus or Pheres. As nepia / paidia (infants / small boys) in Euripides they are pre-ephebic children, carried on stage as mute extras, wearing ordinary Corinthian children's clothing. Later art (Roman sarcophagi, Pompeian frescoes, Corinthian votives) shows them as small boys in short chitons, often clinging to Medea's robe or at an altar. No distinctive attributes, symbols, or iconography atta

Symbols — No personal symbols. Their tomb (mnema) and the sanctuary of Hera Akraia on Acrocorinth are their topographic markers per Pausanias 2.3.7. Annual rites described by Euripides (1378-1383) and Pausanias — sacrifices by Corinthian children, hair-cutting and black garments — are associated with their cult as heroized dead (heroon). In visual art they are identified only by context with Medea, Jason, or the paidagogos (tutor).

Domains — Mortal children, no divine domain. Habitat: Corinth, in the house of Jason and then as suppliants at the sanctuary of Hera Akraia (Heraion) on Acrocorinth according to Corinthian version; or in exile context after Jason's marriage to Creon's daughter (Euripidean version). No cosmology or afterlife domain except hero-cult at Corinth.

Accounts I
Euripides' Medea — The Mother's MurderEuripides, Medea

431 BCE, Classical Athens

After Jason abandons Medea to marry Glauce (Creon's daughter), Medea feigns reconciliation and sends her two young sons with the poisoned peplos and stephane that kills Glauce and Creon. To deny Jason any offspring and to save the boys from Corinthian vengeance (and, in her own words, to wound Jason beyond cure), Medea kills both sons inside the house. Their cries are heard offstage; Jason discovers the bodies on Medea's dragon-drawn chariot provided by Helios. She forbids Jason burial and announces she will bury them herself in Hera Akraia's precinct and found a cult. Establishes the dominant Western version of infanticidal Medea; explains Corinthian hero-cult aetiology; Medea escapes to Athens to Aegeus. Euripides' innovation (or canonization) makes filicide central to Medea's myth.

The Corinthian Stoning — Eumelus and Pausanias' Local AetiologyEumelus of Corinth, Korinthiaka fr. 3 West via Pausanias 2.3.6-7 and Scholia to Euripides Medea 9

Archaic epic (c. 730-600 BCE) preserved in 2nd century CE periegesis

Medea, as queen in Corinth, attempts to make her children immortal by hiding them in the sanctuary of Hera Akraia, or leaves them as suppliants at the altar when she flees after Jason's betrayal. The Corinthians, hating Medea as a barbarian witch or fearing retribution, drag the boys from the altar and stone them to death. A plague and infant mortality follow until the oracle orders yearly sacrifices and honours. Pausanias saw their tomb (a mound with a statue of Medea) and the Heraion where the crime occurred. Provides aetiology for the historical Corinthian ritual where seven boys and seven girls from noble families served Hera Akraia for a year in black, with shorn hair. Explains why Corinthians, not Medea, bear blood-guilt. Euripides is accused by ancient scholia of taking a bribe from Corinthians to shift blame to Medea.

Diodorus' Rationalized History — Conspiracy in CorinthDiodorus Siculus, Bibliotheca Historica 4.54.1-4.55.4

c. 60-30 BCE, using earlier sources (Dionysius Scytobrachion?)

Jason and Medea rule Corinth after Creon's death. Medea bears sons. When her foreignness and sorcery make her hated, Corinthians conspire. Medea, learning of the plot, flees to Athens, leaving her sons as suppliants at Hera's altar trusting the sanctuary will protect them. Corinthians violate asylum and kill them, then spread the story that Medea killed them. Diodorus presents this as the 'true' historical kernel behind the tragic myth. Euhemeristic attempt to exculpate Medea partially and explain dual traditions. Influential for later mythographers who want to reconcile Euripides with local cult.

Accounts II
Hyginus' Compact Mythography — Flight to AthensHyginus, Fabulae 25

1st-2nd century CE

Medea, daughter of Aeëtes, wife of Jason, had two sons Mermerus and Pheres. When Jason married Creusa (Glauce), Medea killed Creusa with poison, killed her own sons Mermerus and Pheres, and fled to Athens to King Aegeus. Hyginus gives the canonical Roman school-text version: names fixed, maternal filicide affirmed, no Corinthian stoning mentioned. Fixes the names Mermerus and Pheres in the Latin West; becomes source for medieval and Renaissance handbooks. No cult aetiology.

The Lioness on the Hunt — The Surviving Mermerus VariantPausanias 2.3.11 (with scholia) and late mythographic summaries; summarized in modern handbooks (Grimal, Tripp) as 'Mermerus killed by a lioness while hunting'

2nd century CE and later compilations

In a rare divergent account, not all of Medea's children die together. Mermerus survives infancy, grows to youth, and is killed while hunting, mauled by a lioness (or lion). This separates his fate from Pheres and removes maternal filicide for at least one son. The story is often conflated with the other Mermerus (son of Pheres, king of Thesprotia) who also dies violently on a hunt, suggesting contamination between homonyms. Pausanias mentions Thesprotian Mermerus' death in hunt context, which later epitomators transferred to the Corinthian Mermerus. Creates a non-filicide death for Mermerus, used by some ancient apologists to mitigate Medea's crime. Modern scholars treat it as a late conflation or doublet rather than independent Corinthian tradition.

The Many Children — Expanded Corinthian FamilyPseudo-Apollodorus Bibliotheca 1.9.28; Scholia to Euripides Medea 264; Ptolemy Chennus via Photius; Pausanias 2.3.7-8

1st century BCE - 2nd century CE compilations

Alternative genealogies give Medea many more children: Apollodorus lists Mermerus and Pheres as two sons, but notes variant that Medea bore seven sons and seven daughters who were all killed by Corinthians; another variant says she bore Thessalus (who survives to become eponym of Thessaly), Alcimenes and Tisander (who die). The 'many children' version explains why Pausanias saw multiple tombs and why the yearly ritual required fourteen noble children. Mermerus and Pheres remain the canonical pair, but are embedded in a larger brood. Shows fluidity of mythic number; allows a survivor (Thessalus) to continue Jason's line and found Thessalian dynasty, solving genealogical problem of Jason's heirs. Demonstrates that 'two sons' is Athenian tragic reduction.

Euripides' Medea killed her children in the original, ancient myth and the Corinthian stoning is a later cover-up. — The Corinthian stoning at Hera Akraia is attested in Eumelus' Archaic epic, older than Euripides (431 BCE). Euripides either invented or, more likely, canonized maternal filicide for tragedy. Antiquity already debated which was older; scholia accuse Euripides of taking Corinthian bribes to shift blame.

Mermerus and Pheres had magical powers or heroic destinies like their mother or father. — No ancient source gives them any powers, prophecy, or deeds. They are presented as ordinary, helpless small children whose sole mythic function is to be victims whose death founds a cult and defines Medea and Jason. Any powers are modern fanon or conflation with Medea's sorcery.

Mermerus was killed by a lioness and Pheres by Medea — a standard split fate. — Joint death (both killed together, by same agent) is the overwhelmingly dominant tradition. The lioness-hunting death is a rare, late, weakly attested variant that most scholars see as contamination with a different Mermerus (son of Pheres of Thesprotia). It is not a canonical split.

The names Mermerus and Pheres appear in Euripides' Medea. — Euripides never names the boys; they are called paidia, tekna, sons. The names Mermerus and Pheres first appear in later Hellenistic and Roman mythographers (Hyginus, Apollodorus) retroactively applied to Euripides' anonymous children.

Medea's children have surviving tombs that prove the murder was historical. — Pausanias saw a heroon/mnema in Corinth and a sanctuary of Hera Akraia, but these are cult sites aetiologized by myth, not historical forensic evidence. No archaeological burial of named individuals has been identified; the 'tomb' is a hero shrine.
