Who It Is

Imagine the goddess of fortune has a sister — and she is everything her sibling is not. Ugly where Lakshmi is beautiful, dark where she is radiant, riding a donkey where she sits on a lotus. This is Alakshmi, the anti-Lakshmi, the goddess of misfortune who emerges from the churning ocean before her younger sister, and who is ritually banished from every Hindu home during Diwali with a string of lime and green chilies hung at the door. She is the sting of poverty that makes wealth sweet, the shadow that defines the light, and the cosmic goad that pushes the lazy toward prosperity. In the West, we have Lady Luck; in India, she has a dark twin who is just as real.

Alakshmi is not malevolent in the sense of a demon seeking destruction; rather, she is a bringer of misfortune, poverty, and grief. She is said to enter households where there is quarreling, laziness, or ill-doing, bringing jealousy and malice in her trail. She is believed to be pleased by sour and spicy offerings, and can be warded off by the smell of lime and chilies. She chastises the irresponsible with her 'infamous blessings' — which are actually curses of misfortune — to goad them toward right conduct.

Powers
Bringer of Misfortune

Alakshmi has the power to bring poverty, grief, and inauspiciousness to individuals and households. She is said to cause brothers to fall out, families to be ruined, and lineages (kula) to be destroyed.

Limit: Her power is most effective in homes where there is ill-doing, laziness, or irresponsibility. She is repelled by rituals, cleanliness, and right conduct.

Ward-Off Vulnerability

Alakshmi is repelled by the smell of lime and green chilies hung at the doorstep. She is also banished through the chanting of the Alakshmi Nashana Mantra, which destroys her influence and invites Lakshmi.

Limit: The ward-off is only effective if the household is otherwise clean and virtuous; otherwise, she may simply wait at the threshold.

Pedagogical Chastisement

Alakshmi is believed to 'bless' the lazy and irresponsible with misfortune, poverty, and grief in order to remind them to work hard and pave the way for Lakshmi's arrival. Her curses are thus a form of tough love.

Limit: This interpretation is not universal; many traditions simply see her as a force to be avoided.

Accounts I
The Churning of the Ocean (Samudra Manthana)Padma Purana

Puranic (c. 4th–15th century CE)

During the great churning of the cosmic ocean by the gods and demons, many beings and objects emerged. The Padma Purana states that the inauspicious and bad emerges first, and so Alakshmi, the goddess of misfortune, emerged from the churning ocean before her sister Lakshmi, the goddess of fortune. The gods, seeing her, sent her to dwell among pernicious persons, giving them poverty and grief. Only after this did Lakshmi emerge, bringing auspiciousness and joy. Alakshmi became the Asura of inauspiciousness and grief, the opposite of Lakshmi. Her emergence established the cosmic duality of fortune and misfortune, and the need for humans to ward her off while inviting her sister.

Accounts II
The Banishment from Diwali HomesNorth Indian folk tradition; Puranic ritual texts

Contemporary practice with ancient roots

During Deepavali (Diwali), the festival of lights dedicated to Lakshmi, Hindus perform rituals to ceremonially banish Alakshmi from the home. This often involves cleaning the house thoroughly, lighting lamps to guide Lakshmi, and hanging lime and green chilies at the doorstep to either repel Alakshmi or offer her her share so she does not enter. The goddess of misfortune is thus acknowledged and sent away, making space for her younger sister's blessings. The ritual reinforces the domestic boundary between auspiciousness and inauspiciousness, and the belief that prosperity requires both effort (to banish Alakshmi) and devotion (to invite Lakshmi).

The Shadow of LakshmiPuranic and folk traditions

Puranic to contemporary

Alakshmi is often described as the 'shadow' of Lakshmi — her dark, inverted reflection. Where Lakshmi is beautiful, Alakshmi is ugly; where Lakshmi brings wealth, Alakshmi brings poverty; where Lakshmi is worshipped, Alakshmi is warded off. This shadow metaphor emphasizes that fortune and misfortune are two sides of the same cosmic coin, and that one cannot exist without the other. The shadow metaphor has led to theological interpretations of Alakshmi as a necessary counterbalance in the cosmos, and as a form of the great Mahadevi who goads humans toward right action.

Alakshmi is a demoness from the Vedas. — Alakshmi is not mentioned by name in the Vedas. She appears in Puranic literature, particularly the Padma Purana. Her attributes match the Vedic goddess Nirṛti, but she is a distinct Puranic development.

Alakshmi is purely evil and hates humans. — Alakshmi is a bringer of misfortune, but she is not a demon of malice. She is part of the cosmic order, sent by the gods to dwell among the wicked. Some traditions view her as a divine chastiser who helps humans grow.

Worshipping Alakshmi brings bad luck. — Alakshmi is not typically 'worshipped' in the positive sense. She is acknowledged and warded off. However, some traditions believe that acknowledging her with offerings (lime and chilies) prevents her from entering the home and causing harm.

Alakshmi is the same as Daridra (personified poverty). — Alakshmi is associated with poverty, but she is a distinct goddess. Daridra is a personification of poverty itself, while Alakshmi is a goddess who brings misfortune, which may include poverty.
