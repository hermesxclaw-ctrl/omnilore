Form

Alakshmi is described with deliberately repulsive features that invert the beauty of her sister Lakshmi. She is said to be 'cow-repelling, antelope-footed, and bull-toothed.' Other descriptions include a dry, shriveled body, sunken cheeks, thick lips, and beady eyes. She is often depicted riding a donkey, a humble and inauspicious mount, in contrast to Lakshmi's lotus and owl. Her complexion is described as the blackest (krishna varna), and she wears black clothes.

Powers

Bringer of Misfortune Alakshmi has the power to bring poverty, grief, and inauspiciousness to individuals and households. She is said to cause brothers to fall out, families to be ruined, and lineages (kula) to be destroyed. Padma Purana; Chakrabarty's account Her power is most effective in homes where there is ill-doing, laziness, or irresponsibility. She is repelled by rituals, cleanliness, and right conduct. Some traditions say she is an autonomous force of misfortune; others say she is a divine goad sent to correct behavior, implying her power is conditional and pedagogical.

Ward-Off Vulnerability Alakshmi is repelled by the smell of lime and green chilies hung at the doorstep. She is also banished through the chanting of the Alakshmi Nashana Mantra, which destroys her influence and invites Lakshmi. North Indian folk tradition; Puranic ritual texts The ward-off is only effective if the household is otherwise clean and virtuous; otherwise, she may simply wait at the threshold. Some believe the lime and chilies are an offering to please her, not a repellent. The two interpretations coexist in different regions.

Pedagogical Chastisement Alakshmi is believed to 'bless' the lazy and irresponsible with misfortune, poverty, and grief in order to remind them to work hard and pave the way for Lakshmi's arrival. Her curses are thus a form of tough love. Modern devotional interpretation; folk tradition This interpretation is not universal; many traditions simply see her as a force to be avoided. The idea that Alakshmi is a benevolent chastiser is a later, more theological interpretation. The Puranic texts themselves present her as an Asura of inauspiciousness to be banished.

Behavior & Habitat

Alakshmi is not malevolent in the sense of a demon seeking destruction; rather, she is a bringer of misfortune, poverty, and grief. She is said to enter households where there is quarreling, laziness, or ill-doing, bringing jealousy and malice in her trail. She is believed to be pleased by sour and spicy offerings, and can be warded off by the smell of lime and chilies. She chastises the irresponsible with her 'infamous blessings' — which are actually curses of misfortune — to goad them toward right conduct.

Accounts

During the great churning of the cosmic ocean by the gods and demons, many beings and objects emerged. The Padma Purana states that the inauspicious and bad emerges first, and so Alakshmi, the goddess of misfortune, emerged from the churning ocean before her sister Lakshmi, the goddess of fortune. The gods, seeing her, sent her to dwell among pernicious persons, giving them poverty and grief. Only after this did Lakshmi emerge, bringing auspiciousness and joy.

During Deepavali (Diwali), the festival of lights dedicated to Lakshmi, Hindus perform rituals to ceremonially banish Alakshmi from the home. This often involves cleaning the house thoroughly, lighting lamps to guide Lakshmi, and hanging lime and green chilies at the doorstep to either repel Alakshmi or offer her her share so she does not enter. The goddess of misfortune is thus acknowledged and sent away, making space for her younger sister's blessings.

Alakshmi is often described as the 'shadow' of Lakshmi — her dark, inverted reflection. Where Lakshmi is beautiful, Alakshmi is ugly; where Lakshmi brings wealth, Alakshmi brings poverty; where Lakshmi is worshipped, Alakshmi is warded off. This shadow metaphor emphasizes that fortune and misfortune are two sides of the same cosmic coin, and that one cannot exist without the other.

Alakshmi as malevolent vs. pedagogical
Alakshmi is a malevolent force that brings undeserved misfortune and must be warded off at all costs. ⇌ Alakshmi is a divine chastiser who brings misfortune only to the lazy and irresponsible, to goad them toward right conduct.
Evidence for A: Puranic texts describe her as the granter of pain and suffering, sent to dwell among the wicked.
Evidence for B: The 'elder sister' metaphor and the belief that she 'chastises with her infamous blessings' suggest a pedagogical role.
Lime and chilies: repellent vs. offering
The sourness of lime and pungency of chilies create a smell that Alakshmi cannot tolerate, thus warding her off. ⇌ Alakshmi is pleased by sour and spicy offerings, and the lime and chilies are her share, left at the door so she does not enter the home.
Evidence for A: Common North Indian folk belief.
Evidence for B: Alternative folk belief, also common in North India.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Alakshmi as the cosmic version of a strict elder sister who gives you a hard time so you get your life together. She's the bad grade that makes you study, the empty wallet that makes you work, the quarrel that makes you value peace. She doesn't hate you — she's just the universe's way of saying 'do better.' And when you finally succeed, she steps aside, and her younger sister Lakshmi walks in with the rewards.
