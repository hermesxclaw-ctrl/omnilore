Form

As a star-nymph, Asterope is imagined as a radiant, ethereal young woman, one of seven sisters. In the only surviving monumental depiction, she appears as a solemn, draped female figure, her pose suggesting grief or foreboding at the impending doom of her husband Oenomaus.

Powers

Stellar Divinity As a Pleiad, Asterope is a celestial nymph, a star-personification. Her nature is tied to the rising and setting of the Pleiades cluster, which marked agricultural seasons in the ancient Greek world. All sources She has no independent agency or mythic deeds; her power is purely symbolic and genealogical. None.

Divine Consort Her union with Ares, the god of war, grants her offspring (Oenomaus) a semi-divine status and a violent, martial nature. Pseudo-Apollodorus, Hyginus This is a passive ability—she does not wield Ares' power herself. Some traditions name Ares as the father of Oenomaus, others suggest a mortal father, making Asterope's role as a divine consort a contested point.

Behavior & Habitat

In myth, Asterope is a passive figure, defined by her relationships—daughter of Atlas, lover of Ares, wife of Oenomaus, mother of Hippodamia. She does not act independently in surviving narratives; her role is genealogical and symbolic, her name linking her to the celestial realm.

Accounts

Asterope and her six sisters, daughters of Atlas, were pursued by the giant hunter Orion. Zeus placed them in the sky as the Pleiades star cluster, where they are still pursued by Orion's constellation. In another version, they killed themselves out of grief for their father's burden.

Asterope's daughter, Hippodamia, was sought by many suitors. Her father, King Oenomaus of Pisa, challenged each suitor to a chariot race, with the loser being killed. He had divine horses from Ares and always won. Pelops, with the help of Myrtilus, sabotaged Oenomaus' chariot (replacing the bronze linchpins with wax), causing him to crash and die. Pelops married Hippodamia. Asterope is depicted as a silent, tragic witness to this drama on the Temple of Zeus at Olympia.

Scholarly Debates
Mother of Oenomaus
Asterope is the mother of Oenomaus by Ares (Apollodorus, Hyginus). ⇌ Oenomaus is the son of Ares and a different mother, or of a mortal father (various lost sources, implied by some scholia).
Evidence for A: Bibliotheca 3.10.1; Fabulae 84.
Evidence for B: Scholia to Homer's Iliad, which cite other genealogical traditions.
Wife vs. Daughter-in-Law of Oenomaus
Asterope is the mother of Hippodamia, and thus the wife of Oenomaus. ⇌ Some sources imply Asterope is a separate figure, possibly a wife of Oenomaus but not the mother of Hippodamia.
Evidence for A: Pausanias (who describes the Olympia pediment, where she stands with Oenomaus).
Evidence for B: The ambiguity in the genealogical sources.
The 'Lost' Pleiad
Merope is the lost star, hiding in shame for marrying a mortal (Sisyphus). ⇌ Electra is the lost star, hiding in grief for the destruction of Troy.
Evidence for A: Hyginus, Astronomica.
Evidence for B: Various scholia and later mythographers.
What We Don't Know

The exact nature of Asterope's role in the Olympia pediment is open to interpretation; she may be a wife, a daughter, or a symbolic figure of grief. Her individual cult is unattested. The full corpus of scholia and fragmentary genealogies has not been exhaustively surveyed for this dossier.

A way to hold it in your mind

Asterope is the 'supporting character' of Greek mythology—she has no solo adventure, no epic quest, no dramatic speech. She is a name on a family tree, a face in a crowd scene, a star in a constellation. But her presence is felt everywhere: in the myth of the Olympic Games, in the curse of the House of Atreus, in the night sky. She is a reminder that even the quietest figures in a story can be essential to its plot.
