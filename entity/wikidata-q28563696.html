Who It Is

In the shadow of a temple built to honor Zeus, a woman carved in marble watches a chariot race she knows will end in death. She is Asterope, a star who fell to earth, married a king whose cruelty was a legend, and became the silent witness to the myth that birthed the Olympic Games. She is not a heroine. She is not a villain. She is a footnote to a tragedy—and yet, her name is written in the sky.

In myth, Asterope is a passive figure, defined by her relationships—daughter of Atlas, lover of Ares, wife of Oenomaus, mother of Hippodamia. She does not act independently in surviving narratives; her role is genealogical and symbolic, her name linking her to the celestial realm.

Powers
Stellar Divinity

As a Pleiad, Asterope is a celestial nymph, a star-personification. Her nature is tied to the rising and setting of the Pleiades cluster, which marked agricultural seasons in the ancient Greek world.

Limit: She has no independent agency or mythic deeds; her power is purely symbolic and genealogical.

Divine Consort

Her union with Ares, the god of war, grants her offspring (Oenomaus) a semi-divine status and a violent, martial nature.

Limit: This is a passive ability—she does not wield Ares' power herself.

Form & Signs

Appearance — As a star-nymph, Asterope is imagined as a radiant, ethereal young woman, one of seven sisters. In the only surviving monumental depiction, she appears as a solemn, draped female figure, her pose suggesting grief or foreboding at the impending doom of her husband Oenomaus.

Symbols — The star (as a member of the Pleiades), the chariot (as wife of Oenomaus), the veil (as a bride, in some interpretations).

Domains — The night sky (specifically the Pleiades cluster), the star itself, and by extension, fate and the perilous transition of marriage.

Accounts I
The Pleiades and the HunterHesiod, Works and Days; Aratus, Phaenomena

8th-3rd century BCE

Asterope and her six sisters, daughters of Atlas, were pursued by the giant hunter Orion. Zeus placed them in the sky as the Pleiades star cluster, where they are still pursued by Orion's constellation. In another version, they killed themselves out of grief for their father's burden. The sisters become the Pleiades, a prominent star cluster used for navigation and agricultural timing. The 'lost' seventh star is sometimes identified as Merope (hiding in shame for marrying a mortal) or Electra (hiding in grief for Troy).

Accounts II
The Doomed Chariot Race of PelopsPseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca; Pausanias; Sophocles (fragments)

5th century BCE (depicted at Olympia c. 460 BCE)

Asterope's daughter, Hippodamia, was sought by many suitors. Her father, King Oenomaus of Pisa, challenged each suitor to a chariot race, with the loser being killed. He had divine horses from Ares and always won. Pelops, with the help of Myrtilus, sabotaged Oenomaus' chariot (replacing the bronze linchpins with wax), causing him to crash and die. Pelops married Hippodamia. Asterope is depicted as a silent, tragic witness to this drama on the Temple of Zeus at Olympia. Pelops becomes king of Pisa, founds the Olympic Games in honor of his victory, and the Peloponnese is named after him. The curse of Myrtilus (whom Pelops kills) haunts the House of Atreus for generations.

Asterope is a major, active goddess with her own myths. — She is a minor figure, a star-nymph whose primary importance is genealogical and symbolic. She has no independent myths or deeds.

The Pleiades are always depicted as a group of seven identical sisters. — Each sister has a distinct identity, genealogy, and mythic role. Only six stars are clearly visible to the naked eye, leading to the myth of the 'lost' Pleiad.

The Olympic Games were founded by Heracles. — While Heracles is credited with founding them in some traditions, the most prominent mythic origin is the chariot race of Pelops, in which Asterope's husband dies.
