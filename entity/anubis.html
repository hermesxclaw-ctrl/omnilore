Who It Is

On the desert edge where tombs cut into the earth and jackals once prowled, the Egyptians imagined a black canid who did not tear open graves but sealed them, who did not feast on the dead but prepared them for a second life. That figure—Anubis—became the quiet technician of eternity: embalmer, guardian, and operator of the scales on which every heart was weighed. His story is not told in sweeping myths but in the precise language of spells and the stillness of statues watching over the dead, a god whose power lies in procedures rather than spectacle.

Attested: Texts present Anubis as diligent, procedural, and protective rather than emotionally characterized. He embalms, guards, guides, judges, and performs ritual services such as burning incense and forming the deceased’s new body. He is described as "lord of burial" and "guardian" who prevents tomb violation and ensures proper rites. Reconstructed: Later interpretive traditions portray him as a stern but fair psychopomp, reflecting the impartiality implied by his role in weighing the heart. Powers
Embalming and mummification

Attested: Anubis is repeatedly described in Coffin Texts and later sources as the embalmer who mummifies the deceased "with his best embalming" and as "lord of the place of embalming." He is credited with inventing or presiding over mummification rites and transforming the corpse into a viable spiritual entity.

Limit: Attested: His embalming power operates within the framework of proper ritual performance; texts imply that without correct rites, the deceased cannot be fully transformed. Unknown: No explicit ancient statement describes Anubis failing at embalming, but improper human practice is portrayed as a risk.

Protection of graves and necropoleis

Attested: Anubis is called "lord of burial" and "he who is upon his mountain" overseeing the necropolis. He protects tombs from desecration and is invoked to guard the deceased against physical and spiritual harm.

Limit: Attested: Protection is contingent on proper offerings and ritual observance; texts warn against neglecting tomb maintenance. Unknown: Specific mythic episodes of Anubis punishing tomb robbers are not preserved in detail.

Psychopomp and guide through the underworld

Attested: Anubis guides the deceased through the dangerous journey of the underworld, assisting them with spells and ritual knowledge. He is associated with leading souls to the judgment hall and sometimes with opening paths.

Limit: Attested: Guidance is mediated through spells and ritual objects; without these, the deceased may be lost or vulnerable. Unknown: No detailed itinerary of Anubis’s guidance beyond general statements is preserved.

Judgment and weighing of the heart

Attested: In New Kingdom Book of the Dead scenes, Anubis operates the scales that weigh the heart of the deceased against the feather of Ma’at in the Hall of Two Truths. He assesses the balance and determines whether the deceased is justified or condemned to annihilation by Ammit.

Limit: Attested: Anubis’s role is procedural; ultimate verdicts are often pronounced by Osiris or the assembled gods. The weighing requires truthful confession and ritual purity from the deceased.

Topographical and local patronage

Attested: Anubis bears epithets linking him to specific places, such as "Lord of Asyut" and "He who presides over the Sacred Land." He functions as a local patron of cemeteries and associated cults.

Limit: Attested: His authority is spatially bounded to necropoleis and ritual precincts; he is not a universal sky or creation god.

Ritual support: incense, formation, and transformation

Attested: Coffin Texts describe Anubis burning incense for the deceased, "forming" them so they may exist on earth through him, and enabling bestial or divine transformations (e.g., crocodile imagery and the epithet "Bull of his Side").

Limit: Attested: These transformations are symbolic and ritual, not physical metamorphoses in the modern fantasy sense.

Form & Signs

The black color symbolizes the fertile Nile silt and the discoloration of a corpse after embalming, linking him visually to regeneration and funerary transformation. Accounts I
Royal funerary protection in the Pyramid TextsPyramid Texts (Old Kingdom royal tombs)

Old Kingdom (c. 24th–23rd century BCE)

Attested: In the Pyramid Texts, Anubis appears not in continuous narrative but in ritual utterances that collectively tell a story of the king’s safe passage to the afterlife. Anubis guards the burial, oversees the embalming, and ensures that the king’s body is properly prepared. He is invoked to protect the tomb and to guide the king’s spirit to join the gods. The "story" is embedded in liturgical formulae rather than prose myth. The king becomes an effective spirit (akh) and joins the divine realm, with Anubis’s protection preventing decay and disruption of the burial.

Anubis and the embalming of OsirisCoffin Texts; later Egyptian funerary tradition; summarized in modern scholarship

Middle Kingdom onward

Attested (ritual template): Coffin Texts repeatedly fold the deceased into the pattern of Osiris’s embalming, with Anubis as the operative embalmer. The deceased is addressed as Osiris, and Anubis’s actions on Osiris become the model for all mummification. Reconstructed narrative: Later tradition infers a myth in which Anubis embalms Osiris after his murder and dismemberment, preparing him for resurrection. The explicit narrative is not preserved in a single Egyptian mythic text but is reconstructed from ritual allusions. Osiris becomes lord of the underworld, and the deceased, by undergoing analogous rites, gains access to postmortem life.

Accounts II
The weighing of the heart in the Hall of Two TruthsBook of the Dead Spell 125 and associated iconography

New Kingdom and later

Attested: The deceased stands before a tribunal of gods in the Hall of Two Truths. Anubis weighs the heart of the deceased against the feather of Ma’at on a balance. Thoth records the result, and Osiris presides over the court. If the heart is lighter or equal to the feather, the deceased is declared justified and admitted to the blessed afterlife; if heavier, the heart is devoured by the monster Ammit, annihilating the soul. The judgment determines eternal fate—either integration into the ordered cosmos or destruction. Anubis’s role is central to the mechanical and symbolic operation of the scales.

Plutarch’s philosophical myth of AnubisPlutarch, *De Iside et Osiride*

1st–2nd century CE

Attested: Plutarch recounts a version of the Osiris myth in which Anubis is the son of Osiris and Nephthys, conceived in secret and raised by Isis. He interprets Anubis as a mediator between the visible and invisible, or between heaven and earth, using Greek philosophical categories. This narrative reflects Greek attempts to rationalize Egyptian religion rather than Egyptian priestly tradition. Anubis becomes a symbol of mediation and mixed nature in Plutarch’s philosophical system, influencing later Greco-Roman and European perceptions of the god.
