Etymology

The Egyptian name is conventionally transcribed as Inpw/Jnpw. Its etymology is disputed: many Egyptologists connect it with the root *inp* meaning "to decay" or "to put on" (as in wrapping), while others regard the name as opaque and possibly derived from an older substrate term for canids or necropolis deities. The Greek form Ἄνουβις (Anoubis) reflects Hellenistic transmission of the Egyptian name into the Greek phonological system. No single etymology commands universal scholarly consensus; most modern works treat the name as formally Egyptian but semantically uncertain.

Cultural Ownership

Anubis belongs primarily to the religious and funerary traditions of ancient Egypt from the Early Dynastic Period through the Roman era. His cult and imagery are rooted in Nile Valley mortuary landscapes, especially necropoleis such as those at Asyut and the Theban region. Later appropriations in Greco-Roman, European, and global popular culture do not share custodial authority over his ancient religious meaning, which remains closely tied to Egyptian priestly, textual, and archaeological contexts.

Disputed Identifications

Scholars debate the precise zoological identification of Anubis’s animal form: older literature calls him a jackal, while more recent zooarchaeological and iconographic studies argue that the depicted canid is a generic desert dog or a stylized composite rather than a specific species. Anubis is sometimes conflated with or distinguished from other canid deities such as Wepwawet and Duamutef; some regional cults may have merged these figures, while priestly texts maintain separate names and functions. In Greco-Roman syncretism, Anubis is occasionally equated with Hermes or associated with Hades, but these identifications are interpretive overlays rather than Egyptian self-definitions.

Archive Dossier — Approach

On the desert edge where tombs cut into the earth and jackals once prowled, the Egyptians imagined a black canid who did not tear open graves but sealed them, who did not feast on the dead but prepared them for a second life. That figure—Anubis—became the quiet technician of eternity: embalmer, guardian, and operator of the scales on which every heart was weighed. His story is not told in sweeping myths but in the precise language of spells and the stillness of statues watching over the dead, a god whose power lies in procedures rather than spectacle.

Who They Are

You're in a vast, silent hall. Before you is a scale so enormous it disappears into the darkness above. A jackal-headed figure places your heart on one side. On the other side, a single white feather. The scale begins to tip. You don't breathe. You can't. The jackal's eyes — ancient, patient, utterly without malice or mercy — meet yours. This is not judgment. This is simply truth. And you're about to learn exactly what you weigh.

Anubis is the Egyptian god of embalming, mummification, the afterlife, and the guardian of tombs. More than any other Egyptian deity, he is the one you meet when you die. In the Hall of Two Truths (Maat), he takes your heart — the seat of your soul, your memories, your every deed — and places it on the Scale of Justice against the feather of Ma'at, the goddess of truth and cosmic order. If your heart is lighter than the feather, you enter the paradise of the Field of Reeds. If it is heavier… Anubis feeds it to Ammit, the Devourer, and your soul is obliterated completely.

Despite this terrifying responsibility, Anubis is not evil. He is neutral in the truest sense — a cosmic functionary who performs his duty with absolute precision and without favoritism. Before Osiris took over as lord of the dead, Anubis was the primary god of the underworld. Even after, he remained the psychopomp, the guide who takes your hand and leads you through the darkness to your final accounting. He is the god who wraps your body in linen, who protects your tomb from grave robbers, who ensures your ka recognizes your body so you can live again.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

Attested: Anubis is most commonly depicted either as a recumbent or standing black canid—traditionally called a jackal but more accurately a stylized desert dog—or as a man with a black canid head. The black color symbolizes the fertile Nile silt and the discoloration of a corpse after embalming, linking him visually to regeneration and funerary transformation. He often appears with a collar and sometimes a flail or scepter, and in judgment scenes he stands beside scales weighing the heart of the deceased. Reconstructed: Some scholars infer that the choice of a nocturnal canid reflects Egyptian observation of scavengers around cemeteries, transformed from a threat into a protective divine guardian. [[2]](https://hellanicus.lib.aegean.gr/items/982fe0ab-d081-4111-a170-27119e3d5335)

Symbols & Iconography

Attested: Key symbols include the black canid, the embalming table, the scales of judgment, and the epithet "He who is upon his mountain" indicating a necropolis hill overlooking tombs. Anubis is associated with the "Sacred Land" (the necropolis), incense burners, mummy wrappings, and the heart scarab in Book of the Dead contexts. In some depictions he appears with a headless animal skin tied to a pole, a ritual emblem linked to funerary processions. Adaptation-specific: Modern art and media often exaggerate his musculature, armor, or weaponry, which are not attested in Pharaonic sources. [[3]](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/religion-and-philosophy/anubis-deity) [[1]](https://www.spurlock.illinois.edu/exhibits/online/mummification/sources.html)

Behaviour & Personality

Attested: Texts present Anubis as diligent, procedural, and protective rather than emotionally characterized. He embalms, guards, guides, judges, and performs ritual services such as burning incense and forming the deceased’s new body. He is described as "lord of burial" and "guardian" who prevents tomb violation and ensures proper rites. Reconstructed: Later interpretive traditions portray him as a stern but fair psychopomp, reflecting the impartiality implied by his role in weighing the heart. Unknown: Egyptian sources do not provide detailed personality narratives or dialogues for Anubis; any modern characterization beyond his ritual functions is speculative.

Domains & Habitats

Attested: Anubis’s primary domain is the necropolis—the "Sacred Land" of tombs and burial grounds. He is linked to specific sites such as Asyut and to the desert margins where cemeteries were located. His cosmic role centers on the underworld (Duat) and the liminal space between the living and the dead, especially embalming chambers and judgment halls. Reconstructed: Some scholars see him as a deity of thresholds and transitions, mediating between worlds rather than ruling a distinct realm, especially after Osiris becomes the principal lord of the underworld. [[0]](https://loregraph.org/sources/coffin-texts-anubis-egyptian-jackal-god) [[2]](https://hellanicus.lib.aegean.gr/items/982fe0ab-d081-4111-a170-27119e3d5335)

Version Differences

Attested: In Old Kingdom Pyramid Texts, Anubis appears as a major funerary guardian for the king. In Middle Kingdom Coffin Texts, his roles expand and are elaborated through numerous epithets as embalmer, guide, and lord of specific necropoleis. By the New Kingdom, Osiris dominates the underworld, and Anubis’s role shifts toward specialist functions—embalming, guiding, and judging—rather than sovereign rule. Greco-Roman sources reinterpret him philosophically and syncretically, emphasizing his mediating nature and sometimes altering his parentage. Modern popular culture further transforms him into a generalized "death god" or warrior figure, which diverges significantly from ancient ritual portrayals. [[3]](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/religion-and-philosophy/anubis-deity) [[4]](https://figshare.mq.edu.au/articles/thesis/The_significance_of_Anubis_as_seen_in_coffin_texts/19442297) [[2]](https://hellanicus.lib.aegean.gr/items/982fe0ab-d081-4111-a170-27119e3d5335)

Origins

Anubis's parentage varies across Egyptian history. In early myths, he is the son of Ra and the cow goddess Hesat. In later traditions — particularly after Osiris rose to prominence — he is the illegitimate son of Osiris and Nephthys (Set's wife), conceived when Nephthys disguised herself as Isis. Abandoned at birth and raised by Isis, Anubis became Osiris's loyal protector. When Osiris was murdered and dismembered by Set, Anubis invented the process of mummification to preserve his father's body — the first embalming in history. He then guarded the tomb against Set's attempts to further desecrate the corpse.

Archive Dossier — Powers

Embalming and mummification

Source Version Coffin Texts (Middle Kingdom); EBSCO Research Starter summary of funerary roles; later ritual manuals.

Description

Attested: Anubis is repeatedly described in Coffin Texts and later sources as the embalmer who mummifies the deceased "with his best embalming" and as "lord of the place of embalming." He is credited with inventing or presiding over mummification rites and transforming the corpse into a viable spiritual entity.

Limitations

Attested: His embalming power operates within the framework of proper ritual performance; texts imply that without correct rites, the deceased cannot be fully transformed. Unknown: No explicit ancient statement describes Anubis failing at embalming, but improper human practice is portrayed as a risk.

Contradictions

Some later narratives attribute the embalming of Osiris to other gods or to collective action, but Coffin Texts emphasize Anubis’s central role. Scholars differ on whether Anubis is the sole inventor of mummification or one among several funerary deities involved. [[0]](https://loregraph.org/sources/coffin-texts-anubis-egyptian-jackal-god) [[3]](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/religion-and-philosophy/anubis-deity)

Protection of graves and necropoleis

Source Version Pyramid Texts; Coffin Texts; iconography of recumbent canid statues near sarcophagi.

Description

Attested: Anubis is called "lord of burial" and "he who is upon his mountain" overseeing the necropolis. He protects tombs from desecration and is invoked to guard the deceased against physical and spiritual harm.

Limitations

Attested: Protection is contingent on proper offerings and ritual observance; texts warn against neglecting tomb maintenance. Unknown: Specific mythic episodes of Anubis punishing tomb robbers are not preserved in detail.

Contradictions

Later Egyptian and modern narratives sometimes exaggerate punitive aspects (e.g., Anubis as executioner of robbers), but these are not clearly attested in canonical texts.

Psychopomp and guide through the underworld

Source Version Book of the Dead; Coffin Texts; museum educational summaries of underworld journeys.

Description

Attested: Anubis guides the deceased through the dangerous journey of the underworld, assisting them with spells and ritual knowledge. He is associated with leading souls to the judgment hall and sometimes with opening paths.

Limitations

Attested: Guidance is mediated through spells and ritual objects; without these, the deceased may be lost or vulnerable. Unknown: No detailed itinerary of Anubis’s guidance beyond general statements is preserved.

Contradictions

Other deities (e.g., Thoth, Horus) also appear as guides in some texts, leading to scholarly debate about whether Anubis is the primary psychopomp or one of several cooperating figures. [[1]](https://www.spurlock.illinois.edu/exhibits/online/mummification/sources.html) [[0]](https://loregraph.org/sources/coffin-texts-anubis-egyptian-jackal-god)

Judgment and weighing of the heart

Source Version Book of the Dead Spell 125; visual depictions in papyri and tombs; Spurlock Museum exhibit.

Description

Attested: In New Kingdom Book of the Dead scenes, Anubis operates the scales that weigh the heart of the deceased against the feather of Ma’at in the Hall of Two Truths. He assesses the balance and determines whether the deceased is justified or condemned to annihilation by Ammit.

Limitations

Attested: Anubis’s role is procedural; ultimate verdicts are often pronounced by Osiris or the assembled gods. The weighing requires truthful confession and ritual purity from the deceased.

Contradictions

Some interpretations emphasize Thoth as the primary judge, with Anubis as technician of the scales; others treat Anubis as a judge in his own right. Scholarly consensus sees a shared judicial function among several deities. [[1]](https://www.spurlock.illinois.edu/exhibits/online/mummification/sources.html) [[3]](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/religion-and-philosophy/anubis-deity)

Topographical and local patronage

Source Version Coffin Texts; Middle Kingdom epithets analyzed in specialized theses.

Description

Attested: Anubis bears epithets linking him to specific places, such as "Lord of Asyut" and "He who presides over the Sacred Land." He functions as a local patron of cemeteries and associated cults.

Limitations

Attested: His authority is spatially bounded to necropoleis and ritual precincts; he is not a universal sky or creation god.

Contradictions

Some regional cults may have emphasized different local titles or merged Anubis with other canid deities, leading to variation in his perceived scope. [[0]](https://loregraph.org/sources/coffin-texts-anubis-egyptian-jackal-god) [[4]](https://figshare.mq.edu.au/articles/thesis/The_significance_of_Anubis_as_seen_in_coffin_texts/19442297) [[2]](https://hellanicus.lib.aegean.gr/items/982fe0ab-d081-4111-a170-27119e3d5335)

Ritual support: incense, formation, and transformation

Source Version Coffin Texts spells (e.g., CT 456, CT 480, CT 657, CT 723) as summarized in modern scholarship.

Description

Attested: Coffin Texts describe Anubis burning incense for the deceased, "forming" them so they may exist on earth through him, and enabling bestial or divine transformations (e.g., crocodile imagery and the epithet "Bull of his Side").

Limitations

Attested: These transformations are symbolic and ritual, not physical metamorphoses in the modern fantasy sense.

Contradictions

Interpretations differ on whether these images reflect actual beliefs in postmortem shape-shifting or metaphorical expressions of power and status. [[0]](https://loregraph.org/sources/coffin-texts-anubis-egyptian-jackal-god) [[4]](https://figshare.mq.edu.au/articles/thesis/The_significance_of_Anubis_as_seen_in_coffin_texts/19442297)

Form

A man with the head of a black jackal — the color black representing both the fertile soil of the Nile and the mystery of death and rebirth. He is often depicted as powerfully built, wearing a linen kilt and sometimes holding the ankh (symbol of life) and a was-scepter. His ears are tall, pointed, and eternally alert. In full animal form, he is a recumbent black jackal atop a shrine, the classic 'Anubis fetish' found guarding tomb entrances. His eyes are piercing gold or amber — the color of the Egyptian desert at sunset, never blinking.

Powers

Mastery over all rites of death and preservation — he invented mummification and knows every spell in the Book of the Dead. The ability to guide souls safely through the Duat (underworld) to the Hall of Judgment. Absolute authority in the weighing of hearts ceremony; his reading of the scale is final and cannot be appealed. Protection of tombs and graves against both physical and spiritual defilement. The power to recognize and reunite a soul with its preserved body. Command over embalming priests and the ability to teach mortals his sacred arts.

Behavior & Habitat

Anubis is silent, patient, and utterly professional. He does not rage, he does not gloat, he does not show favor. When you die, he appears beside you — not to comfort, not to frighten, but to guide. His voice (when ancient texts imagine him speaking) is calm and measured. He treats pharaoh and peasant alike; the scales do not care about your earthly station. He is protective — fiercely so — of the dead, punishing grave robbers with a cold, methodical fury. He loves his mother Isis and remains steadfastly loyal to Osiris. He has no known consort and few myths depict him in emotional extremes.

Weaknesses & Wards

Anubis is a functionary, not a supreme power — he serves the cosmic order and cannot override the will of greater gods like Ra or Osiris. His authority is limited to the realm of the dead; he has little influence in the world of the living. In some myths, Set successfully tricks or overpowers him temporarily. His rigid adherence to ritual and procedure could theoretically be exploited by someone who understood the rules better than he does.

Death, embalming, and annihilation

Source Book of the Dead; Coffin Texts; museum educational materials.

Description

Attested: Anubis’s domain is death and the treatment of corpses. Mummification involves invasive procedures—removal of organs, desiccation, wrapping—that modern readers may find disturbing. In the judgment scene, failure to meet moral standards results in the heart being devoured by Ammit, annihilating the person’s existence. Anubis’s role in weighing the heart participates in this potential destruction.

Scholarly Context

Egyptologists emphasize that these practices were framed as acts of care and cosmic order rather than cruelty, but they nonetheless involve stark images of bodily manipulation and existential risk. [[1]](https://www.spurlock.illinois.edu/exhibits/online/mummification/sources.html) [[3]](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/religion-and-philosophy/anubis-deity)

Association with scavenging animals and cemetery fears

Source Iconographic and zooarchaeological studies; theses on Anubis’s origin.

Description

Reconstructed: Anubis’s canid form likely derives from observation of jackals or dogs that scavenged near cemeteries, animals that could disturb graves. The transformation of such creatures into a protective god reflects an attempt to ritually control and invert a source of fear.

Scholarly Context

Scholars note that this process parallels other ancient strategies of turning dangerous animals into divine guardians, but direct textual statements about this origin are lacking, so the reconstruction remains interpretive. [[2]](https://hellanicus.lib.aegean.gr/items/982fe0ab-d081-4111-a170-27119e3d5335)

Colonial and orientalist distortions

Source Histories of Egyptology and museum practice; modern critical studies.

Description

Attested: European and later colonial scholarship often exoticized Anubis as a "primitive" or "monstrous" dog-headed god, using him as a symbol of an imagined, irrational "Orient." Early museum displays and popular literature sometimes stripped him of ritual context and emphasized sensational aspects of mummification.

Scholarly Context

Recent work in the history of scholarship critiques these portrayals and seeks to restore Anubis’s role within Egyptian religious thought, highlighting how colonial frameworks shaped modern perceptions of Egyptian deities.

Religious conflict and censorship

Source Late antique Christian polemics; archaeological evidence of temple closures.

Description

Attested: In late antiquity, Egyptian temples and cults, including those associated with Anubis, faced suppression under Roman and later Christian authorities. Dog-headed gods were sometimes mocked or condemned in polemical texts, contributing to the marginalization of Anubis’s worship.

Scholarly Context

Scholars of late antiquity document the gradual decline of traditional Egyptian cults and the re-framing of their gods as idols, noting that Anubis’s image became part of broader debates about paganism and idolatry.

Royal funerary protection in the Pyramid Texts

Source Pyramid Texts (Old Kingdom royal tombs)Culture Ancient EgyptianPeriod Old Kingdom (c. 24th–23rd century BCE)

Summary

Attested: In the Pyramid Texts, Anubis appears not in continuous narrative but in ritual utterances that collectively tell a story of the king’s safe passage to the afterlife. Anubis guards the burial, oversees the embalming, and ensures that the king’s body is properly prepared. He is invoked to protect the tomb and to guide the king’s spirit to join the gods. The "story" is embedded in liturgical formulae rather than prose myth.

Participants

Anubis

Deceased king

Osiris (in some later recensions)

Other funerary deities

Consequences

The king becomes an effective spirit (akh) and joins the divine realm, with Anubis’s protection preventing decay and disruption of the burial.

Variants

Different pyramids and recensions emphasize various epithets and roles; some later versions introduce Osiris more prominently, shifting Anubis’s role toward specialist functions.

The weighing of the heart in the Hall of Two Truths

Source Book of the Dead Spell 125 and associated iconographyCulture Ancient EgyptianPeriod New Kingdom and later

Summary

Attested: The deceased stands before a tribunal of gods in the Hall of Two Truths. Anubis weighs the heart of the deceased against the feather of Ma’at on a balance. Thoth records the result, and Osiris presides over the court. If the heart is lighter or equal to the feather, the deceased is declared justified and admitted to the blessed afterlife; if heavier, the heart is devoured by the monster Ammit, annihilating the soul.

Consequences

The judgment determines eternal fate—either integration into the ordered cosmos or destruction. Anubis’s role is central to the mechanical and symbolic operation of the scales.

Variants

Different papyri and tomb scenes vary in the exact positioning of Anubis and other deities; some emphasize Thoth’s judicial speech, others highlight Osiris’s authority. Plutarch’s philosophical myth of Anubis

Source Plutarch, *De Iside et Osiride*Culture Greco-Egyptian (Hellenistic Roman)Period 1st–2nd century CE

Summary

Attested: Plutarch recounts a version of the Osiris myth in which Anubis is the son of Osiris and Nephthys, conceived in secret and raised by Isis. He interprets Anubis as a mediator between the visible and invisible, or between heaven and earth, using Greek philosophical categories. This narrative reflects Greek attempts to rationalize Egyptian religion rather than Egyptian priestly tradition.

Participants

Anubis

Osiris

Isis

Nephthys

Consequences

Anubis becomes a symbol of mediation and mixed nature in Plutarch’s philosophical system, influencing later Greco-Roman and European perceptions of the god.

Variants

Other classical authors mention Anubis in passing, sometimes equating him with Hermes or describing him as a dog-headed god of the Egyptians; these accounts vary in accuracy and detail.

Archive Dossier — Archivist

Known Gaps

Primary Egyptian texts are preserved in hieroglyphic and hieratic scripts; this dossier relies on published translations and summaries rather than fresh philological work. Access to some theses and articles is limited to abstracts or repository metadata, constraining detailed engagement with their arguments. Many specific spell numbers and epithets for Anubis exist beyond those cited here; a truly exhaustive catalogue would require systematic reading of all Pyramid Texts, Coffin Texts, Book of the Dead manuscripts, and related ritual corpora. The origin of Anubis’s name and precise zoological identification of his animal form remain debated, with no definitive resolution. Narrative myths centered on Anubis are sparse; much of his "story" must be reconstructed from ritual texts, which introduces interpretive uncertainty. Late antique polemics and archaeological data on the suppression of Anubis’s cult are uneven and regionally variable. This research is limited to sources accessible in English and to secondary syntheses available as of July 2026.

Research Scope

This dossier synthesizes accessible scholarly and reference materials on Anubis up to 30 July 2026, focusing on English-language publications and widely cited translations of Egyptian funerary texts. It prioritizes primary textual corpora (Pyramid Texts, Coffin Texts, Book of the Dead) and iconographic evidence, supplemented by modern theses, research articles, and museum exhibits. Interpretive statements are flagged as reconstructed or debated where direct textual support is limited. The scope does not claim to exhaust all regional cult practices or unpublished archaeological data.

Archive Dossier — Primary Evidence

Pyramid Texts (Old Kingdom royal tomb inscriptions)

Type textDate c. 24th–23rd century BCELocator Spells such as PT 219, PT 220, PT 532 (Anubis as "he who is upon his mountain" and "lord of the necropolis")Language Middle Egyptian

Translation

Modern translations by R. O. Faulkner and others present Anubis as a guardian of the burial, presiding over the embalming and protecting the king’s corpse. These attestations are among the earliest textual evidence for the god as a funerary protector.

Coffin Texts (Middle Kingdom non-royal funerary inscriptions)

Type textDate c. 2055–1650 BCELocator Spells including CT 60, CT 73, CT 205, CT 398, CT 456, CT 480, CT 530, CT 531, CT 571, CT 657, CT 723 (Anubis as embalmer, "Bull of his Side," lord of Asyut, presider over the Sacred Land)Language Middle Egyptian

Translation

R. O. Faulkner, *The Ancient Egyptian Coffin Texts* (1973–1978). These spells depict Anubis actively embalming, guarding, burning incense, and forming the deceased, and they emphasize his local cult titles such as "Lord of Asyut" and "He who is on his mountain." [[0]](https://loregraph.org/sources/coffin-texts-anubis-egyptian-jackal-god)

Book of the Dead (New Kingdom and later funerary papyri)

Type textDate c. 1550–50 BCELocator Spell 30B (heart scarab), Spell 125 (judgment in the Hall of Two Truths, weighing of the heart)Language Middle Egyptian (later stages)

Translation

Standard translations (e.g., T. G. Allen, R. O. Faulkner) show Anubis weighing the heart of the deceased against the feather of Ma’at and overseeing the judgment scene, often depicted visually with Anubis at the scales. [[1]](https://www.spurlock.illinois.edu/exhibits/online/mummification/sources.html)

Plutarch, *De Iside et Osiride*

Type textDate late 1st–early 2nd century CELocator Sections 13–14 (Anubis as son of Osiris and Nephthys, mediator between gods and humans)Language Greek

Translation

Plutarch presents a Hellenistic philosophical interpretation of Egyptian myths, describing Anubis as a hybrid being and a guardian, and explicitly identifying him as the child of Osiris and Nephthys. This is a secondary, interpretive source rather than an Egyptian priestly text.

Canid-headed deity statues and reliefs from tombs and temples (e.g., Saqqara, Thebes, Asyut)

Type archaeologicalDate c. 3rd millennium BCE–Roman periodLocator Recumbent black canid statues placed near sarcophagi; reliefs of a man with a black canid head in judgment scenes and embalming chambersLanguage Visual/iconographic

Translation

Iconography consistently shows Anubis as a black canid or canid-headed man associated with embalming tables, tomb entrances, and judgment scenes, reinforcing his funerary and protective roles. [[2]](https://hellanicus.lib.aegean.gr/items/982fe0ab-d081-4111-a170-27119e3d5335)

Middle Kingdom and later ritual texts describing the Opening of the Mouth ceremony

Type textDate c. 2055 BCE onwardLocator Temple and tomb inscriptions; later compilations of ritual manualsLanguage Middle Egyptian

Translation

Priests wearing jackal masks invoke Anubis during the Opening of the Mouth ritual to restore the senses of the deceased, enabling them to eat, breathe, and speak in the afterlife. [[3]](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/religion-and-philosophy/anubis-deity)

Archive Dossier — Contradictions

Parentage of Anubis

Position A

Anubis is the son of Osiris and Nephthys, raised by Isis (Greek interpretive tradition).

Position A — evidence

Plutarch, *De Iside et Osiride*, explicitly names Osiris and Nephthys as Anubis’s parents and describes Isis as his foster mother.

Position B

Anubis’s parentage is unknown or variably assigned (e.g., to Ra) in Egyptian and later sources; some traditions avoid specifying it.

Position B — evidence

Egyptian funerary texts focus on Anubis’s functions and epithets without clear genealogical statements; modern reference works note alternative or absent parentage traditions.

Scholarly Consensus

Most Egyptologists treat Plutarch’s account as a Hellenistic interpretation rather than a direct reflection of Egyptian priestly doctrine. Parentage is considered fluid and secondary to Anubis’s ritual role. [[3]](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/religion-and-philosophy/anubis-deity) [[2]](https://hellanicus.lib.aegean.gr/items/982fe0ab-d081-4111-a170-27119e3d5335)

Species of Anubis’s animal form

Position A

Anubis is specifically a jackal, reflecting desert scavengers around cemeteries.

Position A — evidence

Traditional Egyptological literature and popular works refer to Anubis as a jackal god, based on the long-snouted, upright-eared depiction and association with desert necropoleis.

Position B

Anubis represents a generic canid or composite desert dog, not a precise zoological species.

Position B — evidence

Recent iconographic and zooarchaeological studies argue that the depicted animal does not match any single species exactly and that Egyptian art often stylizes animals for symbolic purposes.

Scholarly Consensus

There is growing recognition that "jackal" is a convenient label rather than a precise identification; many scholars now prefer "canid" or "dog/jackal" to acknowledge ambiguity. [[2]](https://hellanicus.lib.aegean.gr/items/982fe0ab-d081-4111-a170-27119e3d5335)

Status as lord of the underworld

Position A

Anubis was originally the primary god of the dead and lord of the underworld.

Position A — evidence

Some reconstructions of early Egyptian religion suggest that Anubis’s prominence in Old Kingdom funerary texts indicates an earlier central role in the realm of the dead.

Position B

Anubis has always been primarily a funerary specialist (embalmer, guide, judge), with Osiris as the main ruler of the underworld in most periods.

Position B — evidence

By the Middle Kingdom and later, textual and iconographic evidence overwhelmingly present Osiris as lord of the underworld, with Anubis serving in specialized capacities; EBSCO and thesis literature emphasize this shift.

Scholarly Consensus

Scholars agree that Anubis’s relative prominence changed over time, but direct evidence for a pre-Osirian underworld kingship is limited. The safer conclusion is that Anubis was always central to funerary practice, while Osiris came to dominate underworld sovereignty. [[4]](https://figshare.mq.edu.au/articles/thesis/The_significance_of_Anubis_as_seen_in_coffin_texts/19442297) [[3]](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/religion-and-philosophy/anubis-deity)

Degree of syncretism with Wepwawet

Position A

Anubis and Wepwawet were largely merged into a single canid funerary god in some periods.

Position A — evidence

Shared iconography and occasional joint mentions in texts suggest functional convergence; some modern summaries state that historians assume eventual combination.

Position B

Anubis and Wepwawet remained distinct deities with overlapping but separable functions and epithets.

Position B — evidence

Detailed epigraphic studies show consistent use of separate names and specific roles (Wepwawet as opener of ways, Anubis as embalmer and guardian).

Scholarly Consensus

The relationship is understood as partial syncretism: in some local or late contexts, the gods may blur, but priestly texts generally maintain distinctions. [[0]](https://loregraph.org/sources/coffin-texts-anubis-egyptian-jackal-god) [[2]](https://hellanicus.lib.aegean.gr/items/982fe0ab-d081-4111-a170-27119e3d5335)

Cultural Significance

Anubis was one of ancient Egypt's most widely worshipped gods for over 3,000 years. Embalmers wore jackal masks during mummification to channel his power. His image guarded every major tomb, from the Valley of the Kings to humble family graves. The 'Weighing of the Heart' scene from the Book of the Dead — carved on countless tomb walls and painted on papyrus scrolls — is one of the most recognizable images from antiquity. Anubis represents the Egyptian conviction that death is not an end but a transition, and that every action in life has consequences.

Modern Interpretations

Anubis appears in countless films (The Mummy series), games (Assassin's Creed Origins, Smite, Age of Mythology), and books (The Kane Chronicles by Rick Riordan). He is frequently depicted as a brooding, gothic figure — a significant departure from his clinical, dispassionate mythological character. Modern works often romanticize him as a 'dark guardian' or death-romantic figure, pairing him with mortal love interests. The image of the jackal-headed figure with scales remains the universal symbol of death's judgment.
