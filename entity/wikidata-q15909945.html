Who It Is

In the misty mountains of central Taiwan, where the Tsou people have lived for centuries, there once stood a deity whose name whispers of cultural collision: A-li̍p-chó͘. Half-remembered, its story nearly erased by colonial rule and cultural change, this ancestral spirit survives today primarily as a name — a Hokkien-sounding title for what may have been a far older, indigenous power. Its existence raises a haunting question: how many gods have been lost to history, their names preserved but their stories scattered like morning mist over the Alishan peaks?

The deity's personality and behavior are not documented in available sources. As a divine figure, it was likely believed to influence natural phenomena, harvests, or community affairs, but specific myths or stories about its actions are not recorded in the accessible literature.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'A-li̍p-chó͘' is a Hokkien (Minnan) transliteration. 'A' is a common Hokkien prefix for names, 'li̍p' (立) means 'to stand' or 'establish', and 'chó͘' (祖) means 'ancestor' or 'patriarch'. The full name can be read as 'Ancestor Who Stands' or 'The Established Ancestor'.

Pronunciation — Ah-lip-tsoh (Hokkien)

Aliases — A-lip-cho, A-li̍p-chó·, Ah-Lip-Cho
