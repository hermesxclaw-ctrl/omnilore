Who They Are

Hebe is the goddess of youth and the cupbearer of the Olympian gods. Daughter of Zeus and Hera, she served nectar and ambrosia at divine feasts before being replaced by Ganymede. She married Heracles upon his apotheosis, reconciling Hera to her stepson. Her Roman counterpart is Juventas. Hebe personifies the bloom of eternal youth — vigor, beauty, and the untainted energy of adolescence. She also assisted Hera in preparing her chariot and attending to her mother's needs. Cult worship of Hebe was centered at Phlius and Sicyon, where she was honored as the goddess who preserves young people in their prime.

Attested in Hesiod's Theogony / Homeric HymnsEra Archaic Greece (c. 700 BCE)

Form

In ancient art Hebe is always the virgin wearer of a sleeveless dress, shown with either or both parents, at her own wedding, or with Aphrodite as a beauty-and-youth pair. In black-figure and red-figure vase painting she is a modest, richly dressed bride-figure; on the Archaic Sophilos dion she appears alone among the goddesses without the usual shoulder-cloak, in an elaborate animal-and-geometric patterned gown with earrings and hair bound in three braids. She is occasionally winged, which has caused persistent scholarly confusion with Iris and Nike. Unlike the other major goddesses she has no animal of her own, but Zeus's eagle is her emblem when she pours him drink, and in the marriage myth a pair of doves with human voices was her father's wedding gift. In later art she becomes the eternally girlish cupbearer holding out a golden cup of immortality.

Powers

Granting and Restoring Youth (Rejuvenation) Hebe's signature power is control over eternal youth and the ability to restore the prime of life to mortals — a power that appears exclusive to her among the gods. In Ovid's Metamorphoses (Book 9), when the goddesses and gods lament their inability to restore youth to favoured mortals, only Hebe can act. She restores the aged Iolaus, nephew/charioteer of Heracles, to vigorous young manhood so he can take the field against Eurystheus. Philostratus the Elder states she is the reason the other gods remain eternally young, and Bacchylides credits her (as the princess, basileia) with responsibility for immortality itself. Homeric/Hesiodic core; Ovid (Latin, 8 CE); Euripides' Heracleidae; Bacchylides; Philostratus the Elder The power is exercised on behalf of others and, in the surviving stories, only at the intercession of Heracles or the will of the divine assembly — there is no myth of Hebe using it on her own behalf or against a mortal's will. Immortality and eternal youth are treated as distinct: the Tithonus myth (who received immortality but not eternal youth) is cited as the very contrast her marriage to Heracles resolves — she guarantees both. The exclusive nature is asserted by Ovid but ancient religion had other rejuvenation lore (Medea restores Aeson with a boiling-potion, though that is squarely magic not deity). 'Exclusive to Hebe' refers strictly to the Olympian goddess class in Ovid's framing.

Serving Nectar and Ambrosia (Cupbearer of the Gods) Hebe pours the nectar and serves the ambrosia — the immortal food and drink of Olympus — to the assembled gods. Her role is more than service: because ambrosia/nectar are the substances of immortality, the cupbearer is functionally the one who sustains the gods' deathlessness. The word 'ambrosia' is linked by some linguists to a PIE root associated with immortality, undying, and lifeforce, dovetailing with her youth-bringing power. Iliad and Odyssey (Homer); Hymn to Hermes; Classical vase painting Her tenure is superseded by the mortal Ganymede (in later tradition) as Zeus's personal cupbearer, often framed as ending at her marriage. Some scholars read the Hebe-to-Ganymede shift as an ancient aition for the eromenos/receiver-of-youth institution of Greek pederasty. The Iliad already has Ganymede as Zeus's personal cupbearer alongside Hebe's general service; whether Hebe was ever 'dismissed' is a post-Classical moralizing gloss (see dark history) rather than a Classical event.

Domains of Forgiveness, Pardons, and Mercy (at Sicyon) Uniquely, Hebe was worshipped at Sicyon and Phlius as a goddess of forgiveness and mercy — the remover of guilt and the pardoner of suppliants. Freed prisoners hung their fetters in her sacred grove at Phlius; all who sought sanctuary in her temple received full forgiveness. This legal-ethical domain of absolution is a striking counterpoint to her youth-bringing power and gives her a civic, juridical character rare among the younger Olympians. Pausanias, Description of Greece 2.13.3-4 (cult at Phlius/Phliasia) The forgiveness is institutional (suppliant sanctuary, release of prisoners) rather than a private moral absolution the way later theology frames grace. The same goddess as both spring-and-youth and pardoner is not modernly reconciled; the two roles may reflect the merging of two local goddesses ('Ganymeda' at Phlius and 'Hebe' the Homeric cupbearer) that Pausanias himself records.

Powers of the Domestic/Patron-of-Brides Sphere As daughter of Hera (goddess of marriage) and bride of Heracles, Hebe presides over weddings, brides, and the ideals of youthful, legally solemnized marriage — a power of social/ritual order: her union models the correct, fertile, monogamous divine marriage. In the Theocritean Encomium, the feasting couple of Ptolemy II and Arsinoe are framed against Heracles' lawful retirement to Hebe's chambers. Edmund Spenser's Epithalamion; vase paintings (Ricci Hydria c. 525 BCE); Theocritus; Catullus Poem 68; Propertius It is a patronal/symbolic power, not a physical feat; Hebe does no battle-heroics in myth. Catullus uses her legal marriage to Heracles as an anti-type of adulterous love, while Propertius (see stories) embellishes a pre-apotheosis passion — two rival readings of the same marriage.

Accounts

In the standard myth Hebe is the daughter of Zeus and Hera, born after Ares and Eileithyia. A reconstructed (and openly flagged as speculative, not an original-audience narrative) Orphic strand gives Hera a means of conceiving without Zeus: journeying to the realm of Oceanus and Tethys at the end of the world, she enters the garden of Flora, touches a solitary, nameless plant from the land of Olene, and conceives Ares; returning later, she eats lettuce and conceives Hebe. The lettuce motif is loaded: in ancient Greece lettuce was linked to sexual impotency (Plutarch records women should never eat the heart of a lettuce), to death (Aphrodite laid the dying Adonis in a lettuce patch), and yet also to menstruation and lactation, associating the plant with motherhood. Thoreau later wittily calls Hebe the daughter of Juno and wild lettuce in Walden.

Hera holds a feast to celebrate the seventh day after her daughter Hebe's birth. The gods fall into a friendly contest over who will give the best gift: Poseidon, Athena, and Hephaestus present toys, while Apollo offers a song. Callimachus, writing a poem for the seventh-day celebration of his friend Leon's daughter, uses Apollo's divine gift of song as the prototype for his own literary offering. The story injects a rare festive, tender register into Hebe's otherwise quiet mythology.

After Heracles' apotheosis — when, in the standard account, he burns on Mount Oeta and is received onto Olympus — Hebe is granted to him as his divine bride, the reconciliation of the hero with his tormentor Hera sealed through her daughter. Vase paintings (notably the Ricci Hydria c. 525 BCE) depict Hebe herself driving the chariot that carries Heracles from Earth to Olympus, a role usually played by Athena. The marriage is presented as the paradigm of a lawful, joyful, fertile divine union: Theocritus has the feasting couple of Ptolemy II and Arsinoe mirrored by Heracles retiring to Hebe's chambers; Catullus (Poem 68) invokes the couple as the anti-type of his own secret adultery; a lost comedy by Epicharmus of Kos, 'The Marriage of Hebe' (Hebes Gamos), dramatized the wedding feast. Hebe bore Heracles two sons, the little gods Alexiares and Anicetus. Propertius offers a striking variant: that Heracles blazed with love for Hebe at the moment of his death on Oeta, before his divinity was complete.

Iolaus, nephew and charioteer of Heracles and once a famed warrior, has grown so old he cannot fight. To honor the dying wish of Heracles and to allow the old hero to aid the Heraclidae against the tyrant Eurystheus, Hebe and Heracles together pray to the gods, and Hebe restores Iolaus to full young manhood. He takes the field and captures Eurystheus, securing the Heraclidae's safety. In Ovid the episode becomes the showcase for Hebe's unique power: the other gods, whose favoured mortals are aging, admit they cannot reverse it — only Hebe holds the power of returning youth, making her the most prized and most potent of domestic goddesses.

Hebe's most iconic image is pouring drink for Zeus as his eagle — or in some scenes Zeus himself momentarily in eagle form — perches beside her, a cup extended to her father. In Servius's tradition her father Zeus presented her with the gift of two doves with human voices, one of which flew to the future site of the oracle at Dodona. The eagle-and-cup motif is so standard that it becomes the defining image of her in the 1750-1880 European vogue for 'en Hébé' portraits.

Scholarly Debates
Hebe's paternity: Zeus or Hera alone?
Hebe is the daughter of Zeus and Hera (Hesiod, Theogony; the mainstream tradition). ⇌ Orphic/reconstructed and the lettuce-parthenogenesis strand makes her Hera's child alone, conceived from eating lettuce in the garden of Flora without Zeus.
Hebe as cupbearer vs Ganymede: dismissal or coexistence?
Hebe was the original cupbearer who was later (post-Classically) 'dismissed', often after the invented clothing story. ⇌ The earliest Iliad already shows both Hebe serving the gods (and Hephaestus at one point) while Ganymede is Zeus's personal cupbearer; Cicero implies either could serve. Classical sources tie any departure simply to her marriage.
The winged attendant: Hebe, Iris, or Nike?
Winged female figures attending gods are often labeled Hebe. ⇌ Such figures are frequently Iris (messenger) or Nike (victory), and only one, on the Sosias cup (marked with an eta Η), is certain Hebe.
Same goddess or two? 'Ganymeda' at Phlius vs 'Hebe' the cupbearer
Ganymeda (at Phlius) and Hebe are the same goddess, as Pausanias reports the Phliasians themselves came to believe. ⇌ The pardon-granting, suppliant-protecting Phliasian goddess has a distinct juridical character that may reflect an older, separate local power later absorbed.
Immortality vs eternal youth: what exactly does Hebe grant?
Hebe (per Bacchylides and Philostratus) is responsible for the gods' immortality itself. ⇌ Hebe specifically controls youth/vigour, while immortality is a more general Olympian property (as the Tithonus myth shows they are conceptually distinct).

What We Don't Know

The existing published record on Hebe's regional cults (especially the Sicyon/Phliasia forgiveness-cult and the Argive Heraion side-by-side statues, now lost) relies heavily on Pausanias as a single witness; an epigraphic survey of Argive/Phliasian inscriptions would strengthen it. The Ganymeda/Ganymede etymological and cultic relationship is unresolved and needs a dedicated comparative study of the two names. The full provenance and iconology of the American Hebe fountains (J. L. Mott production runs, the casting molds, individual civic archives) has not been exhaustively compiled. Modern screen/appearance claims (Disney's unnamed Olympian, comics cameos) could not be verified by name in any reliable source and are flagged as inference. A way to hold it in your mind

At the top of Olympus, where the gods are served from the very cup that keeps them deathless, stands the youngest and most overlooked divinity of all — a goddess whose entire being is the refusal of an ending. Hebe pours the nectar, Hebe keeps the immortals young, Hebe is the reason the flame of Olympus never guttering into age. Yet this girl with the cup, the eternal daughter of Zeus and Hera, is the rarest thing in Greek myth: a deity with no wrath, no revenge, no dark deed — only the quiet, absolute power to say 'not yet' to time. And if that makes her sound too gentle to matter, remember the one thing she can do that no other god can: she can hand you back your youth, freely, and watch your best self walk again beside the heroes.
