Who It Is

Imagine a battlefield after the fighting is done: the groans of the wounded, the silence of the dead, the air thick with the smell of blood. The Greeks had a name for the force that made this happen — not a god who chose to intervene, but a presence that simply was: the Androktasiai, the Manslaughters. They are not characters in a story. They are a cosmic fact. Born from Strife herself, they are the reason that when humans clash, they kill. In Hesiod's great catalogue of evils, they stand shoulder to shoulder with Murder, Famine, and Ruin — a grim reminder that in the ancient Greek imagination, the slaughter of men was not a tragedy that happened to the world. It was woven into the world's very fabric, as inevitable as night itself.

As personified abstractions, the Androktasiai do not 'act' in any narrative sense. They are not characters in myths. They exist as a category of divine force: the manifestation of manslaughter as a cosmic phenomenon. In Hesiod's genealogical system, they function as an explanation for why violence and death are woven into the fabric of the world — they are the divine offspring of Strife, and thus part of the inherent disorder of existence.

Etymology — From the Greek ἀνδροκτασία (androktasia), meaning 'manslaughter' or 'slaughter of men.' Compound of ἀνήρ (anēr, 'man') and κτείνω (kteinō, 'to kill' or 'slay'). The name is essentially an abstract noun personified into a divine being.

Personification of Manslaughter

The Androktasiai are the divine embodiment of the act of killing in battle. Their existence makes slaughter a cosmic principle — not merely a human action, but a force that exists independently of any individual killer. In Hesiod's system, they are the reason war is cyclical and inescapable.

Limit: They have no independent agency, no myths, no cult, and no individual identity. They are a collective abstraction, not a character.

Influence on Battle

As children of Eris (Strife), the Androktasiai are part of the machinery of war. Wherever there is conflict, the Androktasiai are present — not as combatants, but as the very essence of the killing that occurs. Their presence makes battlefields sacred and accursed simultaneously.

Limit: No direct intervention in human affairs is attested. They are not invoked in prayers or rituals. They are a theological concept, not a practical deity.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No physical description survives. The Androktasiai are never depicted in ancient art with any certainty. By analogy with the Keres (who are described as dark, grim-eyed, gnashing their teeth, with claws), we might imagine them as grim female spirits, but this is reconstruction, not evidence. They are best understood as invisible forces — the numinous presence of slaughter itself, made into a divine entity.

Symbols — No attested symbols. By extension from their domain, they might be associated with weapons, blood, and battlefields, but this is interpretive. In the Hesiodic catalogue, they are listed alongside Phonoi (Murders) and Hysminai (Combats), placing them squarely in the realm of martial violence.

Domains — War, battle, slaughter, manslaughter, violent death, the aftermath of combat. They are the personification of the act of killing itself, distinct from the Keres (who are the spirits of death) and the Phonoi (who are murders, often with a connotation of blood-guilt).

Accounts I
The Catalogue of Eris's OffspringHesiod, Theogony, lines 226–232

c. 8th–7th century BCE

Hesiod lists the children of Eris (Strife), the goddess of discord. She bears, without any father (parthenogenetically, like her mother Nyx), a brood of evils: Ponos (Toil), Lethe (Forgetfulness), Limos (Famine), the Algea (Pains), the Hysminai (Combats), the Makhai (Battles), the Phonoi (Murders), the Androktasiai (Manslaughters), the Neikea (Quarrels), the Pseudea (Lies), the Amphillogiai (Disputes), Dysnomia (Lawlessness), and Ate (Ruin). The Androktasiai are thus part of a comprehensive catalogue of the miseries that plague human existence. The catalogue establishes the Androktasiai as fundamental forces in the Hesiodic cosmos. They are not incidental — they are genealogically necessary, born from the primordial principle of Strife. Their existence explains why war, murder, and slaughter are inescapable features of the human condition.

The Androktasiai are the same as the Keres. — The Androktasiai and the Keres are distinct entities in Hesiod's Theogony. They have different mothers (Eris vs. Nyx) and are listed separately. The Keres are death-spirits who actively seize the dying; the Androktasiai are personifications of the act of slaughter. They are related and functionally similar, but not identical.

The Androktasiai are important deities with cults and myths. — The Androktasiai have no cult, no myths, no temples, and no independent narratives. They appear only in Hesiod's catalogue of Eris's children. They are theological abstractions, not active deities.

The Androktasiai are beautiful or heroic figures. — There is no description of them at all. By analogy with the Keres (who are described as grim, bloody, and terrifying), the Androktasiai would likely be fearsome if depicted, but there is no evidence. They are not heroic — they are the personification of the horror of war.
