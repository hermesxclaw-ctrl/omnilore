Who It Is

You find a brass lamp in a market stall in Marrakech. The seller warns you not to rub it. You laugh — you've heard the stories. Of course you rub it. Smoke pours from the spout, thick and fragrant, filling the room. When it clears, there is a figure standing before you: neither man nor woman, hairless and ageless, its eyes burning like twin coals, its form shifting subtly at the edges as if it can't quite remember how to be solid. It does not say 'Your wish is my command.' It says: 'You have no idea what you have done. I have been waiting for three thousand years. And I am very, very angry.' Whisper the first wish carefully — in the oldest stories, the djinn always finds a way to make it hurt.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Djinn are not fixed in form and are generally invisible to humans. When they do appear, they can take almost any shape — a human, an animal (especially snakes and scorpions), a wind, or a pillar of fire. In the West they are usually imagined as the blue-skinned spirits of Disney's Aladdin, but in the Arabian tradition their 'true form' is often described as wispy or fiery, capable of billowing smoke and shapeshifting. Accounts I
The Fisherman and the Jinni (Arabian Nights)One Thousand and One Nights (The Arabian Nights)

Medieval collection, 8th-15th century

A poor fisherman hauls up a sealed brass bottle. Broken open by his knife, the cork releases a pillar of smoke that coalesces into a vast, terrible jinni. The jinni reveals he was imprisoned by King Solomon for defying the great king. In a fury after centuries of waiting, he vows to kill whoever frees him. The humble fisherman — no warrior, outwits the jinni with a simple riddle: 'By what god do you swear you were truly inside that bottle?' Doubting himself, the jinni shrinks back into the flask to prove it, and the fisherman hastily recorks it, trapping him again — turning the legend of the trapped genie into one of the world's most famous stories of cleverness over raw power.

Accounts II
Aladdin and the Wonderful LampOne Thousand and One Nights (Aladdin and the Wonderful Lamp)

Medieval collection; tale circulated from the Arabic tradition

The poor tailor's son Aladdin is tricked by a sorcerer into retrieving an old lamp from a magic cave. When he rubs it, a mighty djinn of the lamp appears and announces itself as his slave, bound to obey the lamp's master. Aladdin quickly learns the power — and the danger — of wish-granting. The lamp's djinn builds him palaces, brings him wealth, and helps him win the princess, but a rival sorcerer steals the lamp and turns its power against him. The tale popularized the matchless 'genie in the lamp' image that defines how the world imagines a djinn bound to servitude — though this is a literary device, not a Quranic one.

Surah Al-Jinn — The Qur'anic PortraitThe Quran, Surah Al-Jinn (Chapter 72)

7th century CE

The Quran devotes an entire chapter to the djinn. It describes how a company of djinn overheard the Quran being recited and were deeply moved by it, acknowledging its truth and turning to Islamic belief. The surah establishes the central Islamic view of djinn: they are created from smokeless fire, possess reason and free will, and are morally accountable beings who may be righteous or evil, Muslim or not. This is the theological core that separates the Islamic djinn — an invisible parallel race — from the later, more magical 'genie who grants wishes' of Westernized folklore.
