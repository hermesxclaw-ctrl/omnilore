Who It Is

In the fledgling colony of Jamestown, where survival hung by a thread, Samuel Jordan carved a plantation out of the Virginia wilderness—only to see it become a fortress of refuge when war erupted. His story is one of early American ambition, resilience, and a legacy that outlived him in the most unexpected way: a scandalous breach-of-promise lawsuit that made his widow the talk of the colony.

Documented actions suggest he was a capable and enterprising settler, as he patented land, served as a Burgess, and his plantation became a refuge during the 1622 attack, indicating he was trusted and possibly a leader in his community.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The surname 'Jordan' is of English origin, derived from the Middle English personal name 'Jordan,' which was brought back by Crusaders and given to children baptized with water from the River Jordan. The given name 'Samuel' is of Hebrew origin, meaning 'name of God' or 'God has heard.'

Accounts I
Early settlement and patentVirginia Company records

1610-1620

Samuel Jordan arrived in Virginia around 1610 and became an Ancient Planter. In 1620, he received a patent for 450 acres, including 200 acres for himself and his wife Cecily as ancient planters and 250 acres for headrights of five indentured servants. Established Jordan's Journey, a plantation that later became a refuge.

First legislative assemblyVirginia Assembly records

1619

Jordan served as Burgess for Charles City in the first representative assembly in North America, and served on the committee of readers for the Great Charter. Contributed to the establishment of representative government in Virginia.

Accounts II
Survival of the 1622 attackVirginia Company records

1622

During the Powhatan surprise attack of 1622, Jordan's Journey was not listed as having any killed, and it became a fortified refuge for settlers from other settlements. Jordan's Journey grew as a safe haven; population increased from 42 to 56 in a year.

Death and widow's lawsuitVirginia Council records

1623-1625

Samuel Jordan died in early 1623. His widow Cecily became the subject of the first breach-of-promise lawsuit in North America, filed by Rev. Greville Pooley. Cecily won the case and later married William Farrar. The lawsuit determined Cecily's marriage and control over Jordan's property; Farrar eventually acquired his own patent.
