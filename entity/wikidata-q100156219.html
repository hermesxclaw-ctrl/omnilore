The Approach

In the fledgling colony of Jamestown, where survival hung by a thread, Samuel Jordan carved a plantation out of the Virginia wilderness—only to see it become a fortress of refuge when war erupted. His story is one of early American ambition, resilience, and a legacy that outlived him in the most unexpected way: a scandalous breach-of-promise lawsuit that made his widow the talk of the colony.

Identity

Samuel Jordan

Pronunciation SAM-yool JOR-dan

Etymology

The surname 'Jordan' is of English origin, derived from the Middle English personal name 'Jordan,' which was brought back by Crusaders and given to children baptized with water from the River Jordan. The given name 'Samuel' is of Hebrew origin, meaning 'name of God' or 'God has heard.'

Cultural Ownership

English colonial America, specifically the Virginia Colony

Disputed Identifications

There is uncertainty regarding his early life, including whether he had children from a previous marriage. Genealogist John Dorman acknowledges the possibility that Thomas Jordan, who arrived in Virginia in 1619, could be Samuel's son, but no conclusive evidence exists.

Behaviour & Personality

Documented actions suggest he was a capable and enterprising settler, as he patented land, served as a Burgess, and his plantation became a refuge during the 1622 attack, indicating he was trusted and possibly a leader in his community.

Domains & Habitats

He lived in the Virginia Colony, specifically at his plantation 'Beggar's Bush' (later 'Jordan's Journey') near today's Jordan Point, Virginia.

Stories & Myths

Early settlement and patent

Source Virginia Company recordsCulture English colonialPeriod 1610-1620

Summary

Samuel Jordan arrived in Virginia around 1610 and became an Ancient Planter. In 1620, he received a patent for 450 acres, including 200 acres for himself and his wife Cecily as ancient planters and 250 acres for headrights of five indentured servants.

Survival of the 1622 attack

Source Virginia Company recordsCulture English colonialPeriod 1622

Summary

During the Powhatan surprise attack of 1622, Jordan's Journey was not listed as having any killed, and it became a fortified refuge for settlers from other settlements.

Death and widow's lawsuit

Source Virginia Council recordsCulture English colonialPeriod 1623-1625

Summary

Samuel Jordan died in early 1623. His widow Cecily became the subject of the first breach-of-promise lawsuit in North America, filed by Rev. Greville Pooley. Cecily won the case and later married William Farrar.

Participants

Samuel Jordan

Greville Pooley

Consequences

The lawsuit determined Cecily's marriage and control over Jordan's property; Farrar eventually acquired his own patent.
