Who It Is

Vayu (Vāyu) is the Hindu god of wind and air—the restless, invisible, and immensely powerful force that pervades the atmosphere and sustains life through breath (prana). In the Rigveda, Vayu is described as a swift, beautiful deity who rides a chariot drawn by a thousand horses (or by gazelles), roaring across the sky. He is the first deity to receive the sacrificial offering of Soma, and he is often invoked alongside Indra, with whom he shares a close association—riding in the same chariot and partaking of the sacred drink together. Vayu is literally the breath of the gods; all living beings depend on him, for without air there is no life. In the Puranas and epics, Vayu's most famous role is as the father of Hanuman, the monkey god of the Ramayana. When the wind ceased to blow while Hanuman was an infant (after he had been struck by Indra's thunderbolt for trying to eat the sun), all the gods had to appease Vayu by restoring and blessing his son, demonstrating Vayu's essential cosmic function and his fierce protectiveness. Vayu also fathered Bhima, the mightiest of the Pandava brothers in the Mahabharata. As one of the eight dikpalas, Vayu is the guardian of the northwest direction. In philosophical terms, Vayu is closely associated with prana, the vital breath or life-force that animates all existence. The five pranas (prana, apana, vyana, udana, samana) are all manifestations of Vayu at the microcosmic level. His temples are less common than those of major deities like Shiva or Vishnu, but Vayu worship is integrated into the daily ritual of pranayama (breath control) and the recognition of breath as the most immediate connection between the individual self (atman) and the universal life-force.

Form

Vayu is typically depicted as a handsome, youthful deity riding a swift antelope (or a chariot drawn by antelopes or horses). He is often shown with a white or pale complexion, carrying a white banner or flag that flutters in the wind. In some depictions, he holds a staff or a goad. His body is sometimes shown as translucent or airy, suggesting his insubstantial nature. In Vedic art, he is often depicted as a strong, muscular man with a wind-blown appearance, his hair and garments flowing as if caught in a constant breeze.

Powers

Control over Wind and Atmosphere Vayu has absolute control over all winds, from gentle breezes to devastating hurricanes. He can create, direct, and still any wind, and can command the atmosphere itself. All versions His power over the atmosphere is limited by the cosmic order (rita) and the will of the supreme gods (Vishnu or Shiva). Some texts suggest he is independent, while others place him under the command of Indra or Vishnu.

Giver of Life (Prana) Vayu is the breath of life (prana). He sustains all living beings by providing the air they breathe. His absence means death. Vedic, Upanishadic His life-giving power is dependent on the recipient's karma and the divine plan. While he is the 'breath of life', he is not the creator of life itself; he is the sustainer.

Superhuman Speed Vayu is the fastest of all beings, able to travel anywhere in an instant. His speed is unmatched, and he is often sent as a messenger by the gods. All versions His speed is not limited by physical distance, but he is bound by the cosmic law. None significant.

Physical Strength (Granting) Vayu can grant immense physical strength to his chosen devotees or sons. Hanuman and Bhima both inherit his superhuman strength. Ramayana, Mahabharata The strength he grants is proportional to the devotee's merit and purpose. None significant.

Invisibility and Intangibility As the wind, Vayu can be invisible and intangible, passing through solid objects and becoming undetectable. Vedic, Puranic He can be detected by those with spiritual insight or by the sound of his passage. Some texts describe him as having a visible form, while others emphasize his invisibility.

Destructive Power (Storms) Vayu can unleash devastating storms, tornadoes, and hurricanes, capable of leveling mountains and uprooting forests. This destructive aspect is feared and respected. Vedic, Puranic His destructive power is checked by the cosmic order and the other gods. He is both a destroyer and a preserver, reflecting the dual nature of the wind.

Behavior & Habitat

Vayu is characterized by his speed, restlessness, and power. He is a bringer of life (through breath), but also a force of destruction (through storms and hurricanes). He is described as a loyal servant of the gods, especially Indra and Vishnu, and as a father figure to Hanuman and Bhima. He is generous to his devotees but can be temperamental, prone to sudden outbursts of power when angered or disrespected.

Accounts

Vayu, the wind god, carries the divine energy of Shiva to Anjana, a celestial nymph cursed to be born as a monkey. Through Vayu's agency, Hanuman is born, inheriting his father's speed, strength, and ability to fly. Vayu becomes Hanuman's spiritual and physical father, and Hanuman is often called 'Pavanputra' (son of the wind).

Kunti, the mother of the Pandavas, invokes Vayu to obtain a son. Vayu grants her Bhima, who is born with immense strength and a voracious appetite. Bhima's strength is directly attributed to Vayu, and he is often called 'Vayusuta' (son of Vayu).

Vayu plays a supporting role in the churning of the cosmic ocean by the gods and demons. He fans the flames of the serpent Vasuki, which is used as the churning rope, keeping the process going. His winds also help to spread the various treasures and ambrosia that emerge from the ocean.

In a fit of pride, Vayu attempted to blow down the Mount Meru, the cosmic axis. However, Garuda, the eagle mount of Vishnu, intervened and defeated Vayu, humbling him. This episode teaches the lesson of humility and the limits of even divine power.

The Avestan Vayu is invoked as a powerful deity who spans the 'space between' the material and spiritual worlds. He is described as having both a good and an evil aspect, representing the dualistic nature of existence. He is a protector of the righteous and a punisher of the wicked.

Scholarly Debates
Paternity of Hanuman
Vayu is the father of Hanuman, as stated in the Valmiki Ramayana. ⇌ Shiva is the father, with Vayu acting as a carrier of Shiva's essence.
Evidence for A: Valmiki Ramayana, Book 1, Sarga 66, where Anjana is said to have been blessed by Vayu.
Evidence for B: Regional South Indian traditions and some later Puranas, which emphasize Shiva's role.
Vayu's status relative to Indra
Vayu is equal to Indra, a co-equal deity of the storm. ⇌ Vayu is subordinate to Indra, the king of the gods.
Evidence for A: The Rigveda often pairs them as 'Vayu-Indra', suggesting equality.
Evidence for B: Later Puranic texts place Indra as the king of the gods, with Vayu as a directional guardian.
Vayu's role in Jainism
Vayu is a personal deity, a god of the wind. ⇌ Vayu is a class of air-bodied beings, not a single deity.
Evidence for A: Hindu texts and popular worship.
Evidence for B: Jain Agamas, which describe vayukayika beings.
Vayu's destructive aspect
Vayu is a benevolent, life-giving deity. ⇌ Vayu is a destructive, fearsome force.
Evidence for A: His role as the giver of breath and life.
Evidence for B: His ability to create devastating storms and his episode of attacking Mount Meru.
What We Don't Know

There is limited archaeological evidence directly linking Vayu to specific artifacts outside of textual descriptions. The exact nature of the Vedic ritual practices dedicated to Vayu is not fully understood. The regional variations in his mythology, particularly in South India, require further study. The relationship between the Hindu Vayu and the Zoroastrian Vayu is a complex topic that requires more in-depth comparative analysis. The reception of Vayu in modern Indian popular culture, beyond the epics, is an area that could be explored further.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Vayu as the divine personification of the atmosphere itself. He's not just the god of wind; he's the god of the space between, the breath that connects all living things. If Indra is the king of the sky, Vayu is the sky's very substance. He's the force that carries messages, moves ships, and spreads seeds. In modern terms, he's the original 'cloud' — not the digital one, but the real one that brings rain and life.
