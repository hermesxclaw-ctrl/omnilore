Who It Is

He is the invisible force that fills your lungs and whips the leaves into a frenzy. He is the first breath of a newborn, and the last gasp of the dying. He is Vayu, the god of wind, the swiftest of the gods, and the father of the mighty Hanuman. In a universe where gods are often distant and abstract, Vayu is the one you feel on your skin with every gust of wind.

Vayu is characterized by his speed, restlessness, and power. He is a bringer of life (through breath), but also a force of destruction (through storms and hurricanes). He is described as a loyal servant of the gods, especially Indra and Vishnu, and as a father figure to Hanuman and Bhima. He is generous to his devotees but can be temperamental, prone to sudden outbursts of power when angered or disrespected.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From the Sanskrit root 'va' (to blow or move). The word 'Vayu' literally means 'that which flows' or 'the blower'. In the Rigveda, he is the god of the wind, the breath of the universe.

Pronunciation — VAH-yoo (Sanskrit: वायु)

Aliases — Vata, Vayudeva, Pavana, Anila, Marut, Matan

Ownership — Hinduism (Pan-Indian), with significant presence in Jainism and Buddhism. Also recognized in Zoroastrianism as 'Vayu' or 'Vata', a wind deity.

Powers
Control over Wind and Atmosphere

Vayu has absolute control over all winds, from gentle breezes to devastating hurricanes. He can create, direct, and still any wind, and can command the atmosphere itself.

Limit: His power over the atmosphere is limited by the cosmic order (rita) and the will of the supreme gods (Vishnu or Shiva).

Giver of Life (Prana)

Vayu is the breath of life (prana). He sustains all living beings by providing the air they breathe. His absence means death.

Limit: His life-giving power is dependent on the recipient's karma and the divine plan.

Superhuman Speed

Vayu is the fastest of all beings, able to travel anywhere in an instant. His speed is unmatched, and he is often sent as a messenger by the gods.

Limit: His speed is not limited by physical distance, but he is bound by the cosmic law.

Physical Strength (Granting)

Vayu can grant immense physical strength to his chosen devotees or sons. Hanuman and Bhima both inherit his superhuman strength.

Limit: The strength he grants is proportional to the devotee's merit and purpose.

Invisibility and Intangibility

As the wind, Vayu can be invisible and intangible, passing through solid objects and becoming undetectable.

Limit: He can be detected by those with spiritual insight or by the sound of his passage.

Destructive Power (Storms)

Vayu can unleash devastating storms, tornadoes, and hurricanes, capable of leveling mountains and uprooting forests. This destructive aspect is feared and respected.

Limit: His destructive power is checked by the cosmic order and the other gods.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Vayu is typically depicted as a handsome, youthful deity riding a swift antelope (or a chariot drawn by antelopes or horses). He is often shown with a white or pale complexion, carrying a white banner or flag that flutters in the wind. In some depictions, he holds a staff or a goad. His body is sometimes shown as translucent or airy, suggesting his insubstantial nature. In Vedic art, he is often depicted as a strong, muscular man with a wind-blow

Symbols — His primary symbol is the white banner or flag, representing the wind's visibility. He is also associated with the antelope (swiftness), the bow and arrow (speed and reach), and the conch shell (the sound of the wind). His vahana (vehicle) is the antelope, and his weapon is the goad or staff.

Domains — Vayu is the god of the wind, the atmosphere (antariksha), the breath of life (prana), and the northwest direction. He is also associated with the vital airs within the body (prana, apana, vyana, udana, samana). He is the guardian of the northwest (Vayavya) quarter and is one of the eight directional guardians (ashtadikpalas).

Accounts I
The Birth of HanumanRamayana (Valmiki)

c. 500 BCE - 100 CE

Vayu, the wind god, carries the divine energy of Shiva to Anjana, a celestial nymph cursed to be born as a monkey. Through Vayu's agency, Hanuman is born, inheriting his father's speed, strength, and ability to fly. Vayu becomes Hanuman's spiritual and physical father, and Hanuman is often called 'Pavanputra' (son of the wind). Hanuman becomes the greatest devotee of Rama, using his wind-given powers to serve the divine cause. Vayu's role as father establishes a lineage of strength and devotion.

The Birth of BhimaMahabharata (Vyasa)

c. 400 BCE - 400 CE

Kunti, the mother of the Pandavas, invokes Vayu to obtain a son. Vayu grants her Bhima, who is born with immense strength and a voracious appetite. Bhima's strength is directly attributed to Vayu, and he is often called 'Vayusuta' (son of Vayu). Bhima uses his wind-given strength to protect his family and defeat their enemies, including the Kauravas. His strength is a key factor in the Pandavas' victory in the Kurukshetra War.

Accounts II
The Churning of the Ocean (Samudra Manthan)Vishnu Purana, Mahabharata

c. 400 BCE - 1000 CE

Vayu plays a supporting role in the churning of the cosmic ocean by the gods and demons. He fans the flames of the serpent Vasuki, which is used as the churning rope, keeping the process going. His winds also help to spread the various treasures and ambrosia that emerge from the ocean. The churning produces the nectar of immortality (amrita), but also the deadly poison (halahala) that Shiva consumes. Vayu's role, while minor, is essential to the process.

Vayu's Destruction of the Meru Peak (Puranic episode)Shiva Purana, Bhagavata Purana

c. 300-1000 CE

In a fit of pride, Vayu attempted to blow down the Mount Meru, the cosmic axis. However, Garuda, the eagle mount of Vishnu, intervened and defeated Vayu, humbling him. This episode teaches the lesson of humility and the limits of even divine power. Vayu is humbled and learns to respect the supreme gods. This episode reinforces the hierarchy of the divine pantheon.

Vayu and the Avestan YashtAvesta (Yasht 15)

c. 1000-600 BCE

The Avestan Vayu is invoked as a powerful deity who spans the 'space between' the material and spiritual worlds. He is described as having both a good and an evil aspect, representing the dualistic nature of existence. He is a protector of the righteous and a punisher of the wicked. Vayu is a key figure in Zoroastrian cosmology, embodying the cosmic struggle between good and evil. He is a mediator between the two realms.

The Hindu Vayu and the Zoroastrian Vayu are the same deity. — They share a common Indo-Iranian root, but they are distinct deities with different mythologies and roles. The Hindu Vayu is a personalized god, while the Zoroastrian Vayu is a more abstract, dual-aspect deity.

Vayu is just a minor god, a personification of the wind. — Vayu is a powerful deity who is the father of Hanuman and Bhima, the chief of the Maruts, and the guardian of the northwest. He is also the breath of life (prana), making him essential to all existence.

Vayu is the same as the Greek god Aeolus. — While both are wind gods, they are from completely different mythological traditions with different attributes and stories. Aeolus is a mortal king in some myths, while Vayu is a primordial deity.

Vayu is a purely benevolent deity. — Vayu has a destructive aspect. He can unleash devastating storms and has been known to act out of pride and anger, as seen in his attack on Mount Meru.
