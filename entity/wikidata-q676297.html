Who It Is

In the shadow of a Bronze Age mountain, a king bows before a god so tall that the relief carved into the living rock still looms over travelers today. The god holds a cluster of grapes in one hand and a sheaf of wheat in the other — a promise of abundance carved in stone. His name is Arma, the moon-god of the Hittites and Luwians, a deity who swore kings to their oaths, healed the sick, and lit the night sky over a forgotten empire. He is not Zeus, not Ra, not a name you heard in school — and that is exactly why he matters.

Arma is primarily a benevolent, protective deity — a guarantor of oaths, a bestower of kingship, and a source of agricultural fertility. There is no surviving mythology describing him as a trickster, warrior, or capricious figure. He appears in prayers as a healer and a protector of the weak, particularly those who appeal to him at night.

Powers
Lunar illumination and night protection

As a moon-god, Arma provides light in darkness, guiding travelers and protecting those who move at night. In prayers, he is invoked to 'shine upon' the supplicant.

Limit: Naturally, his power wanes during the new moon; no text explicitly discusses this, but the lunar cycle was certainly observed in ritual contexts.

Oath enforcement

Arma is called upon in treaties and oaths to witness agreements and to punish those who break them. This is a common role for sun and moon gods in ancient Near Eastern cultures (cf. Šamaš, Sin).

Limit: His punishment is typically implied (sickness, misfortune) rather than described in detail.

Healing

In Luwian and Hittite ritual texts, Arma is invoked to cure illnesses, particularly those thought to be caused by divine anger or sorcery. He is associated with 'purification' rituals.

Limit: His healing is often mediated by ritual practitioners (the 'old women' or 'physicians' of the texts).

Agricultural fertility

In Neo-Hittite iconography (İvriz), Arma holds grapes and wheat, symbolizing his role in ensuring bountiful harvests. This is a later, more localized development of his cult.

Limit: This role is not explicitly attested in the earlier Hittite texts.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In Neo-Hittite reliefs (e.g., İvriz), Arma is depicted as a large, bearded male figure wearing a horned crown (the standard divine headdress in Anatolian art), a tasseled kilt, and sometimes a crescent-moon emblem on his crown or held in his hand. He often holds a curved staff or a lituus (augural staff). In earlier Hittite art, he is rarely depicted anthropomorphically; the crescent moon itself served as his symbol.

Symbols — The crescent moon (often worn on the crown or shown on a standard), a lituus (curved staff), a cluster of grapes, wheat ears, a horned crown, a long robe with tassels. In hieroglyphic Luwian, his name is written with the logogram for 'moon' (LUNA).

Domains — Moon, night sky, oaths and treaties, kingship, healing, agricultural fertility. His cult was centered on the cities of Kaššu (identified with modern Kaman-Kalehöyük) and later Tuwana (modern Bor/Kemerhisar), but he was worshipped throughout Hittite and Luwian territories.

Accounts I
The Treaty WitnessTreaty between Muršili II of Ḫatti and Targašnalli of Ḫapalla (CTH 67)

c. 1340 BCE

Arma is invoked as one of a thousand gods who witness the treaty, ensuring that both parties keep their word. The treaty lists him among the 'lords of the oath' — a standard formula that places the moon-god among the highest divine authorities. The treaty established Ḫapalla as a vassal state of Ḫatti. Arma's role as witness made him a guarantor of the political order.

Accounts II
The İvriz ReliefRock relief at İvriz, Turkey

c. 8th century BCE

King Warpalawas of Tuwana stands in prayer before a colossal figure of Arma (if the identification is correct). The god, towering over the king, holds grapes and wheat, blessing the land with fertility. The accompanying inscription records Warpalawas's piety and his dedication of the monument. The relief served as a public declaration of the king's divine favor and a permanent prayer for agricultural abundance.

The Prayer of Kantuzzili (attributed)Hittite prayer texts (CTH 372)

c. 14th century BCE

In a famous Hittite prayer, the speaker (possibly king Kantuzzili) appeals to the Sun-god, but the prayer's structure is paralleled in hymns to other deities, including Arma. The supplicant pleads for justice, healing, and deliverance from suffering, framing the god as a compassionate judge. The prayer illustrates the personal, emotional relationship between worshippers and deities in Hittite religion, a shift from purely state-oriented cult.

Arma is the same as the Greek god Ares (because the names are similar). — Ares is a Greek war-god; Arma is an Anatolian moon-god. The names are completely unrelated etymologically. 'Arma' in Latin means 'weapons', which may cause confusion, but the Anatolian Arma has no connection to war.

Arma was a 'minor' deity. — Arma was a major deity, invoked in treaties, prayers, and festivals across the Hittite Empire. His cult was state-sponsored and widespread.

The Hittites were a 'lost' civilization with no connection to the modern world. — The Hittites were a major Bronze Age power with extensive diplomatic and trade connections to Egypt, Babylon, and the Aegean. Their language was deciphered in the early 20th century, and their culture is well-documented in thousands of cuneiform tablets.
