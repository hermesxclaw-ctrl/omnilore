Who It Is

He did not march into Yamato — he sailed down from heaven in a boat made of rock. When Emperor Jimmu's army fought its way into the Yamato plain, they found it already ruled by a sun-descended god who had arrived before them: Nigihayahi, the heavenly prince in the Ame-no-Iwafune, whose loyalties would decide who truly spoke for the sun goddess.

Attested behavior: loyal to heavenly order (amatsukami legitimacy), willing to enforce hierarchy even against kin. In Jimmu narrative, he orders Nagasunehiko to submit to Jimmu as the true lineal descendant of Amaterasu/Takamimusubi, demonstrating deference to senior heavenly mandate over personal alliance. When disobeyed, he kills Nagasunehiko himself and surrenders to Jimmu, citing moral obligation (chūgi) and loyalty. This is presented as righteous kin-slaying for heavenly legitimacy. No other personality traits (humor, cruelty, generosity) are narrated. Reconstructed: as ancestor-deity

Powers
Heavenly Descent via Ame-no-Iwafune

Ability to descend from Takamagahara to earth in the Ame-no-Iwafune (Heavenly Rock Boat), a celestial vessel. This marks him as a Tenson deity with legitimate heavenly mandate, parallel to Ninigi's descent via Ame-no-Ukihashi.

Limit: One-way descent in narrative; no attested ability to return alive. Return only as corpse via wind god in Kujiki. Vessel itself not described as weapon or reusable.

Sun-Lineage Legitimacy and Authority

Status as descendant of Amaterasu (via Takamimusubi/Ame-no-oshihomimi line) granting authority to rule Yamato and to judge Nagasunehiko's loyalty. He can command submission and be recognized by Jimmu as fellow heavenly kin.

Limit: Authority is subordinate to Jimmu's senior line (Jimmu as great-grandson of Ninigi). Nigihayahi must submit once Jimmu's legitimacy is confirmed; cannot claim emperorship.

Martial Command and Kin-Slaying

Commands Nagasunehiko and Yamato forces; personally kills Nagasunehiko when he disobeys the order to submit. Demonstrates martial prowess and authority over life/death within his household.

Limit: No supernatural weapon described; killing is by unspecified means (later commentaries assume sword). Not invulnerable; he dies (Kujiki) before his son's birth.

Ancestral Progenitor Power

As ujigami, he begets Umashimaji (Umashimade), ancestor of Mononobe and Hozumi clans, transmitting heavenly lineage and clan occupation (military/ritual). His 25 accompanying deities become clan ancestors.

Limit: Progenitor status is genealogical/ritual, not active miracle-working. No fertility miracles described beyond normal birth.

Posthumous Heavenly Translation (Kujiki only)

After death, his corpse is transported back to heaven by the whirlwind god (Shinatsuhiko no Mikoto / Shinatsuhiko) at Takamimusubi's command for a heavenly funeral.

Limit: Applies only to corpse, not living ascent; requires intervention of Takamimusubi and wind deity. Considered disputed due to Kujiki's forgery status.

Accounts I
Descent from Heaven in the Ame-no-IwafuneNihon Shoki Book 3; Sendai Kuji Hongi Book 4; Kojiki Book 2 (brief)

As narrated: Age of Gods (Kamiyo); as text: 712-720 CE (Nara period compilation)

After the Tenson Korin (Ninigi's descent), a second heavenly descent occurs. Nigihayahi, son (or brother) of the heavenly line, descends from Takamagahara to Yamato in the Ame-no-Iwafune, the Heavenly Rock Boat. In Kujiki he is accompanied by 25 heavenly retainer deities who will become ancestors of Mononobe lineages, carrying heavenly treasures. He lands in Yamato (at Kawachi/Yamato border, near Tomi) and establishes rule before Emperor Jimmu's arrival, representing a prior heavenly mandate in the central land. Establishes Nigihayahi as legitimate heavenly ruler of Yamato prior to Jimmu, creating the ideological problem of two sun-lineages in the same land that the Jimmu narrative must resolve. Legitimizes Mononobe clan as heavenly, not earthly, in origin.

Marriage to the Tomi PrincessKojiki Book 2; Nihon Shoki Book 3, variant notes; Kujiki Book 4

Age of Gods / Early Yamato; text Nara period

After settling in Yamato, Nigihayahi marries a local chieftain's daughter/sister. In Kojiki she is Tomibime (Tomiyahime), daughter of the Tomi chief (often read as Nagasunehiko's daughter or kin). In Nihon Shoki and Kujiki she is Mikashigiyahime (Mika-shigiya-hime), younger sister of Nagasunehiko. The marriage alliance binds the heavenly newcomer to the indigenous Yamato power structure represented by Nagasunehiko's family, making Nagasunehiko both brother-in-law and military lieutenant. Produces the heir Umashimaji (Umashimade), future Mononobe ancestor. Creates kinship obligation that makes the later killing of Nagasunehiko a fraught act of kin-slaying for loyalty to heaven.

Rule over Yamato with NagasunehikoSendai Kuji Hongi Book 5; implied in Nihon Shoki Jimmu narrative

Pre-Jimmu Yamato; text Heian (Kujiki)

Kujiki expands the brief Kiki notice into a reign: Nigihayahi rules Yamato with Nagasunehiko as his general/administrator. They control the Yamato plain from a stronghold at Tomi (Tomiyama), commanding local kuni-tsukami and resisting outside incursion. This is presented as legitimate heavenly governance, not rebellion, until Jimmu's eastern expedition arrives from the west (Kyunshu via Osaka). Sets up the confrontation with Jimmu as a conflict between two legitimate heavenly lines, not simply heaven vs earth. Provides Mononobe with a pre-imperial golden age to claim.

Accounts II
Jimmu's Eastern Expedition: The Standoff at Osaka and the Proof of Heavenly TreasuresKojiki Book 2, Ch. 44-48; Nihon Shoki Book 3, Jimmu 1-4

Legendary Jimmu period (trad. 660 BCE); text Nara period

Jimmu (Kamuyamato Iwarehiko) and his brother Itsuse lead an expedition eastward to conquer Yamato. At Osaka (Naniwa), they meet Nagasunehiko, who defeats them, killing Itsuse. Nagasunehiko declares he serves Nigihayahi, the true heavenly descendant, and sends a message to Jimmu accusing Jimmu of being an impostor. Both sides present heavenly treasures (ama-no-takara) as proof of sun-lineage descent: Jimmu shows his, Nagasunehiko shows Nigihayahi's. Despite proof on both sides, Nagasunehiko refuses to cease hostilities, insisting only his lord is legitimate. Creates stalemate of legitimacy; forces Nigihayahi to choose between kin/loyal retainer and heavenly hierarchy. Itsuse's death necessitates Jimmu's detour via Kii and Kumano.

The Killing of Nagasunehiko and Submission to JimmuNihon Shoki Book 3; Kojiki Book 2; Kujiki Book 5

Jimmu period; text Nara/Heian

Recognizing Jimmu as the senior lineal descendant of Amaterasu (Jimmu being great-grandson of Ninigi, while Nigihayahi is from a collateral heavenly line), Nigihayahi orders Nagasunehiko to submit and serve Jimmu. Nagasunehiko disobeys, continuing to fight. Nigihayahi then kills Nagasunehiko himself — in Nihon Shoki he slays him, in some readings he has him executed — citing moral obligation, loyalty (chū), and the heavenly order. He then submits to Jimmu, presenting his heavenly treasures and his people, and is pardoned and enrolled as a servant of the new emperor. Jimmu accepts his surrender, confirming Mononobe loyalty to the throne. Resolves dual-legitimacy crisis; establishes precedent that collateral heavenly lines must yield to the main imperial line. Secures Mononobe clan's position as loyal imperial military house (Mononobe no Muraji) and preserves Nigihayahi's lineage through Umashimaji, who formally presents the heavenly swords/spears to Jimmu.

Death Before His Son's Birth and Heavenly FuneralSendai Kuji Hongi (Kujiki) Book 5

Post-Jimmu; text Heian, disputed

According to Kujiki only, Nigihayahi dies before his son Umashimaji is born — his wife Mikashigiyahime is pregnant at his death. Takamimusubi, his heavenly father/patron, sends the whirlwind god Shinatsuhiko (Shinatsuhiko no Mikoto) to carry Nigihayahi's corpse back up to heaven in the wind for a heavenly funeral. Umashimaji is then born posthumously and later serves Jimmu, presenting his father's heavenly treasures. Gives Nigihayahi a heavenly burial equal to his heavenly descent, elevating Mononobe ancestor to full amatsukami status even in death. Explains why Umashimaji, not Nigihayahi himself, becomes the active Mononobe founder under Jimmu.

Nigihayahi is the same as Ninigi, or just another name for the imperial ancestor. — They are distinct Tenson figures in Kiki. Ninigi is the grandson of Amaterasu sent to rule earth (ancestor of Jimmu); Nigihayahi is a collateral heavenly descendant who ruled Yamato before Jimmu and then submitted. Conflating them erases the dual-legitimacy conflict central to the Jimmu narrative.

Nigihayahi was a villain who opposed Jimmu and was defeated. — In primary sources he does not oppose Jimmu; his retainer Nagasunehiko does. Nigihayahi himself orders submission, kills his own retainer for disobedience, and surrenders voluntarily. He is portrayed as loyal to heavenly hierarchy, not as an enemy.

Ghost of Tsushima features Nigihayahi as a character or boss. — Ghost of Tsushima contains only a minor charm item named 'Charm of Nigihayahi' (and similar kami charms); the deity does not appear as a character, story element, or boss. It is a name-only reference.

The Kujiki is an ancient, equally authoritative source proving Nigihayahi = Amenohoakari and his heavenly funeral. — Sendai Kuji Hongi is dated to early Heian (9th-10th century), centuries after Kojiki/Nihon Shoki, and is considered by modern scholarship to be a forgery/synthesis that copies and elaborates Kiki material. Its equation and wind-god funeral story are not corroborated in earlier texts.

Nigihayahi's myth comes from Korea and he was a Korean prince. — Some scholars (notably Egami Namio's 'horse-rider' hypothesis) have proposed that Tenson myths reflect Korean immigrant lineages, but this is a historiographical hypothesis about the sociological background of Yamato myths, not an attested identity of Nigihayahi as Korean. No primary source calls him Korean.
