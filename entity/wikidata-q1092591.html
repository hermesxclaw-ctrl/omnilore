Who It Is

In the dry months before the rains, the Mexica did not pray to maize in the abstract — they carried it like a newborn. Five perfect cobs, swaddled in blankets and set in a basket to sleep until the next planting, were Centeōtl himself: a yellow-skinned youth with maize tassels in his headdress and a black stripe cutting from eyebrow to jaw, whose birth on the day 1 Xōchitl tied the fate of every kernel to the calendar, the Pleiades, and the uneasy bargain between human blood and human food.

Attested personality is limited; colonial sources treat Centeōtl less as a narrative actor with dialogue and more as an embodiment of maize's life cycle and a patron to be petitioned. He is described as benevolent provider when honored, vulnerable when neglected — his 'resting' in granaries between harvests is treated as a literal dormancy requiring respect. Sahagún's informants attribute to him modesty and youthfulness, contrasting with the more overtly sexual and penitential character of his mother Tlazōlteōtl. Powers
Maize Embodiment and Germination

Centeōtl IS the mature maize ear; his presence causes seed to sprout, stalks to tassel, and cobs to fill. Ritual handling of his effigy (five cobs) is believed to ensure germination of the next planting. Not a spell-caster but an immanent vegetative force.

Limit: Dependent on human ritual care, rain (Tlaloc), solar warmth, and proper calendar timing. Without offerings, planting at wrong Pleiades position, or neglect of seed cobs, he withers and famine follows. Cannot act outside agricultural season.

Calendrical and Divinatory Patronage

As Lord of the Day 7 and Fourth Lord of the Night, Centeōtl governs the tōnalli (day-fortune) of those born on his days. Diviners read his influence as favorable for farmers and merchants but requiring penitence.

Limit: Only operative within the 260-day count; does not override other patrons (e.g., Tezcatlipoca as First Lord of Night). Fortune is advisory, not deterministic, and can be mitigated by ritual.

Subsistence and Abundance Provision

Guarantees tlaolli abundance — full granaries, successful storage, and freedom from famine. Chicomecōātl aspect specifically governs the 'seven serpents' abundance and the filling of cuexcomatl granaries.

Limit: Requires reciprocal offering: first fruits, five-cob bundles, fasting, sexual abstinence before planting. Broken reciprocity results in cocoliztli (famine/disease).

Seed Dormancy and Regeneration

Power to 'sleep' as stored seed cobs from harvest to next planting and reawaken. The five cobs kept in the home are explicitly called his 'resting' form, protected until sowing.

Limit: Vulnerable to mold, pests, and ritual pollution (e.g., adultery in household). If seed is eaten or sold, regeneration fails.

Association with Pleiades and Seasonal Timing

Linked to Tianquiztli (Pleiades); his annual return is timed to their appearance, signaling when to plant. In myth, Quetzalcoatl's retrieval of maize is astronomically timed to Pleiades.

Limit: Not a stellar deity himself; association is calendrical marker, not rulership of stars (which belong to Citlalicue etc.).

Form & Signs

Appearance — Most colonial pictorial manuscripts and Sahagún's Indigenous artists portray Centeōtl as a youthful male, often adolescent to young adult, with distinctive yellow or golden body paint/skin — the color of ripe, dried maize. The Florentine Codex and Primeros Memoriales describe him as telpochtli (young man). He wears a headdress that incorporates actual or stylized maize ears (cintli), often with green foliage or tassels (miahuatl) sprouting upward

Symbols — Core symbols: cintli (mature maize ear on cob), miahuatl (maize tassel), xilotl (tender ear), elotl (green cob), and tlaolli (shelled kernels) — Centeōtl is specifically cintli, the storable staple. Associated with day-sign Xōchitl (Flower, 1 Flower birth day), the number 7, the Pleiades (Tianquiztli, 'marketplace' stars) which signal planting time, and the colors yellow/gold and green. Regalia includes paper ornaments (amatl), quetzal

Domains — Domain: maize in its storable, cultivated form (cintli), and by extension subsistence, agricultural fertility, seed preservation, and human sustenance. Broader association with tlaolli and centla (maize fields, milpas), granaries (cuezcomatl), and the household hearth where maize is nixtamalized. Cosmologically linked to the earth (tlalticpac), the sun's warmth that ripens grain, and the Pleiades whose heliacal rising marked the agricultural

Accounts I
Birth of Centeōtl to Tlazōlteōtl and Piltzintecuhtli on 1 XōchitlFlorentine Codex, Book 1, Chapter 13; also summarized in Thelma Sullivan's Primeros Memoriales translation

Late Postclassic, recorded c. 1558-1577

Sahagún's Nahua informants state that Centeōtl was born to the earth and filth/purification goddess Tlazōlteōtl and the solar youth Piltzintecuhtli (identified with the planet Mercury and with Xochipilli). His day-sign is 1 Xōchitl (1 Flower), placing him in the Xochiquetzal family complex of beauty, flowers, and fertility. The genealogy legitimizes maize as child of earth and sun — the two forces that make it grow. Establishes Centeōtl's calendrical identity and explains his youthfulness and floral associations; links maize to both terrestrial fertility and solar heat. Used by diviners to interpret 1 Flower births.

Centeōtl as Son of XōchiquetzalCodex Vaticanus A (Codex Ríos) and oral traditions reported by Alva Ixtlilxochitl; referenced in Nicholson's synthesis

Late Postclassic, recorded early colonial c. 1560-1600

In this variant, Xōchiquetzal, patron of flowers, weaving, and young mothers, bears Centeōtl. The story emphasizes maize as a beautiful, delicate child needing protection, paralleling how young maize (xilotl) is called 'the pretty goddess' and must be guarded from wind and frost. No father is named in some tellings, emphasizing parthenogenetic floral birth. Reinforces association of maize with feminine beauty and vulnerability; explains why young maize was symbolized by a goddess (Chicomecōātl as Princess of Unripe Maize) before hardening into male Centeōtl.

Quetzalcōātl Brings Maize to Humanity and Reveals CenteōtlLeyenda de los Soles (Legend of the Suns, Codex Chimalpopoca, fol. 78-79); Historia de los Mexicanos por sus pinturas

Pre-conquest myth recorded c. 1558

After the creation of humans, the gods ask what humans will eat. Quetzalcōātl transforms into a black ant (or follows a red ant) to enter Tonacatepetl (Mountain of Sustenance) where maize is hidden. He carries a kernel back to Tamoanchan, the gods grind it and place it in human mouths. That maize IS Centeōtl — the substance and spirit are one. In some tellings Nanahuatzin or the Tlaloque break the mountain open to release all maize varieties. Explains why maize is sacred and why Centeōtl must be thanked before eating; establishes Pleiades timing (maize revealed when Pleiades rise) and the debt of human sustenance to Quetzalcoatl's effort. Ritual reenactment in planting festivals.

Accounts II
Huey Tozoztli — The Five Cobs Carried Like NewbornsFlorentine Codex Book 2, chapters on Huey Tozoztli (Great Vigil) and Toxcatl; Durán, Book of the Gods and Rites, ch. 9; Codex Borbonicus pp. 23-24

Late Postclassic, observed annually in April/May (fourth month)

At Huey Tozoztli, the great feast honoring Centeōtl-Chicomecōātl, elder women select five newly ripened maize cobs — the largest and most perfect. Each is wrapped in red paper and cloth, carried on the back like an infant, and placed in a special basket (chiquihuitl) in the home granary where they remain as honored guests until the next harvest. They are called Centeōtl's embodiment and also identified with Lady Chicomecōātl Seven Serpents. Offerings of maize dough, incense, and blood are made before them. Ritual ensures seed viability and household abundance; failure to house the cobs properly predicts famine. Provides the most detailed attested worship practice for Centeōtl and shows inseparability from Chicomecōātl in domestic religion.

The Bare-Breasted Dances in the Milpa and the Pollen BattlesFlorentine Codex Book 2, Huey Tozoztli; Torquemada, Monarquía Indiana (1615) citing earlier sources; contemporary ethnography by Alan Sandstrom (1991)

Late Postclassic to present (continuous tradition)

When young maize sprouts, Nahua women loosen their hair and huipils and dance bare-breasted through the milpa to thank Centeōtl and Mother Earth. They sing, pick five ears, and process back to the village. The festival culminates in joyous battles where participants throw flower petals, scented maize flour (pinolli), and pollen at each other and at the maize-bearers, soaking them. The erotic display is explicitly linked to human fertility mirroring maize fertility. Reinforces reciprocal fertility: human female bodies bless the field. Colonial friars condemned the nudity as lascivious, leading to suppression or clothing modifications after 1521. The pollen battle survives in some Huey Atlixcayotl festivals today.

Centeōtl as Lord of the Day 7 and Fourth Lord of the Night in the TōnalpōhualliFlorentine Codex Book 4 (Soothsayers); Codex Borgia tonalamatl; Codex Fejérváry-Mayer tonalpohualli

Pre-conquest, codified c. 1400-1521

In the 260-day sacred calendar, Centeōtl governs all days with coefficient 7 (chicome) — hence his frequent pairing with Chicomecōātl ('Seven Serpent') — and serves as the fourth of nine Lords of the Night, ruling the night hours in a nine-day cycle. Tonalpouhque (day-keepers) used his position to cast nativities: children born on 7 Flower or under his night lordship were said to be favored for agriculture but prone to excess if not disciplined. His image in the tonalamatl shows him with maize headdress and offering bowl. Integrates maize into time itself; every 260-day cycle reasserts Centeōtl's presence in human destiny. Explains why colonial sources list him among the most 'important' deities despite few narrative myths — his calendrical function was daily.

Centeōtl is simply the 'Aztec goddess of maize' (always female) — Primary sources most often describe Centeōtl as male (young man), while Chicomecōātl is female. The goddess label comes from conflating the two or from the variant name Centeōcihuātl. Both male and female forms are attested; calling Centeōtl exclusively a goddess erases the male youth iconography and the dyad structure.

Centeōtl is the Aztec god of agriculture in general — His domain is specifically cintli — mature, dried maize on the cob, the storable staple — not all agriculture. Other deities govern maguey (Mayahuel), amaranth, beans, or general earth fertility (Tlaltecuhtli, Tlaloc). He is central because maize was central, but not a generic farm god.

The black line on Centeōtl's face is war paint — The black stripe from eyebrow through cheek to jaw is a maize-specific marker, paralleled in Classic Maya foliated maize god images where it represents a leaf, husk shadow, or foliation. It is not generic warrior face paint (which uses different patterns for Tezcatlipoca, Mixcoatl, etc.).

Centeōtl is the same as the Maya maize god Yum Kaax — Yum Kaax is a Yucatec Maya name popularized by early 20th-century scholarship (Morley, Thompson) and now considered erroneous for the Classic Maya maize god. Centeōtl is Nahuatl, not Maya, and neither name correctly maps to the other culture's deity. The Classic Maya maize god is now called God E / Tonsured/Foliated Maize God in scholarship.

Aztec maize rituals were purely joyful thanksgiving with no sacrifice — Huey Tozoztli and related rites involved bloodletting, captive sacrifice, and theophagy (eating dough images of the god). Thanksgiving and sacrifice were inseparable in Nahua reciprocity (nextlahualli, 'debt payment'). Sanitized retellings omit this.
