Who It Is

In the heart of ancient Rome, on the darkest day of the year, a goddess stood with her lips sealed shut, guarding a secret so powerful that the fate of an empire depended on it. Her name was Angerona, and she held in her silence the true name of Rome itself — a name that, if spoken aloud, could summon the gods out of the city and leave it naked before its enemies. She was the goddess of the unspoken, the patron of secrets, the divine librarian of the word that could never be uttered. And yet, despite her power, she is one of the most forgotten deities of the Roman pantheon — a whisper of a goddess, fittingly, whose story has survived only in fragments. But what fragments they are.

As a goddess of silence, secrecy, and the protection of hidden names, Angerona's behavior is more about what she prevents than what she does. She guards the unspoken, the secret, and the sacred name. She is not a goddess of action but of restraint — of knowing when not to speak. In her role as a protector against throat diseases, she was invoked to heal or prevent angina and other afflictions of the throat and mouth. She was also associated with the endurance of suffering in silence, as Plutarch notes, teaching Romans to bear their grief without complaint.

Powers
Guardianship of the Secret Name of Rome

Angerona was believed to protect the true, secret name of the city of Rome. This name was never spoken aloud, for fear that enemies would use it in a ritual of evocatio (calling out the city's tutelary deity) to defeat Rome. Angerona's sealed mouth symbolized this protection.

Limit: The protection was contingent on the name remaining unspoken. If the name were ever uttered, the protection would fail.

Cure of Throat Diseases

Angerona was invoked to cure and prevent angina (quinsy, a throat infection) and other afflictions of the throat and mouth. Her name was derived by the Romans from 'angina' for this purpose.

Limit: This was a healing function, not a protective one. Her power was therapeutic, not preventive.

Relief from Anguish

Angerona could free people from 'angor' — the Latin word for anguish, distress, or anxiety. She was a goddess who eased the suffering of the human heart.

Limit: The relief was likely ritual in nature, sought through prayer and sacrifice at her festival.

Teaching Silence and Endurance

Plutarch suggests that Angerona's festival taught Romans to bear their suffering in silence, a Stoic-adjacent virtue of endurance without complaint.

Limit: This is an interpretive reading of the goddess's function, not a supernatural power.

Accounts I
The Guardian of Rome's Secret NameMacrobius, Saturnalia 1.10.7-9

Late Antiquity (5th century CE), but reflecting earlier tradition

In the most famous story associated with Angerona, she is the divine guardian of the secret name of Rome. The Romans believed that every city had a true name that was known only to its tutelary deity, and that if an enemy learned this name, they could perform a ritual called evocatio, calling the city's protective gods out of the city and leaving it defenseless. To prevent this, the true name of Rome was never spoken aloud, and Angerona, with her mouth bound and sealed, was the goddess who kept this secret safe. Her statue stood in the temple of Volupia (the goddess of pleasure and satisfaction), where it served as a constant reminder of the power of silence. The secret name of Rome was never revealed, and the city's divine protection was maintained. The story explains why Angerona was depicted with her mouth covered and why her festival was a time of ritual silence.

Accounts II
The Goddess of the Winter SolsticeVarro, De Lingua Latina 6.23; Festus, s.v. 'Angeronalia'

Republican era (1st century BCE)

Angerona's festival, the Angeronalia, was held on December 21, the winter solstice — the shortest day and longest night of the year. On this day, the pontiffs (the highest-ranking priests of Rome) performed sacrifices to Angerona in the temple of Volupia, or in the Regia, the ancient palace of the kings of Rome. The festival was a time to mark the turning of the year, when the sun began its return and the days began to lengthen. Angerona, as a goddess of silence and endurance, was honored at this dark time of year, when Romans were encouraged to bear the cold and darkness in silence, knowing that light would soon return. The festival marked the winter solstice and the beginning of the new year's cycle. It was a time of sacrifice and prayer for the health and well-being of the city.

The Healer of ThroatsFestus, De Verborum Significatu

Imperial era (2nd century CE)

In this tradition, Angerona was a healing goddess who protected against angina and other diseases of the throat. Her name was derived from the Latin 'angina' (quinsy, a severe throat infection), and she was invoked to cure those who suffered from this painful and potentially fatal condition. The connection between the goddess and the disease was so strong that some Romans believed she was named after the illness she cured. Angerona was added to the list of Roman healing deities, alongside Apollo, Aesculapius, and others.

Angerona was the Roman equivalent of the Greek goddess of silence, Harpocrates. — This is a late antique and Renaissance syncretism, not an original identification. The Romans did not have a direct equivalent to Harpocrates, and Angerona was a distinctly Roman goddess. The identification was made centuries after her cult had ceased to be active.

Angerona was a minor, unimportant goddess. — While Angerona is not as famous as Jupiter or Mars, she was an important state deity whose festival was officiated by the pontiffs, the highest-ranking priests of Rome. Her role as guardian of the secret name of Rome made her a crucial protector of the city.

The secret name of Rome was 'Amor' (Rome spelled backwards). — This is a modern myth, not an ancient one. No ancient source records this 'secret name,' and it is almost certainly a modern invention. The actual secret name of Rome, if it ever existed, is unknown.
