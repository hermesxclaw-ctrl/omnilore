Form

Angerona was typically depicted in Roman statuary and coins as a female figure with her mouth bound, sealed, or covered with a finger pressed to her lips. Macrobius describes her as 'with a bound and sealed mouth' (os obligatum et signatum). Some depictions show her with her finger raised to her lips in a gesture of silence (akin to the modern 'shh' gesture). She was also sometimes shown holding a cornucopia (horn of plenty), which has led to interpretations connecting her to abundance and the winter solstice, when the festival was held. The cornucopia may also be a symbol of the year's end and the promise of the new year's abundance.

Powers

Guardianship of the Secret Name of Rome Angerona was believed to protect the true, secret name of the city of Rome. This name was never spoken aloud, for fear that enemies would use it in a ritual of evocatio (calling out the city's tutelary deity) to defeat Rome. Angerona's sealed mouth symbolized this protection. Macrobius, Saturnalia; Pliny, Natural History The protection was contingent on the name remaining unspoken. If the name were ever uttered, the protection would fail. The actual secret name of Rome was never recorded, so we cannot know it. Some sources suggest it may have been related to the Etruscan name for Rome, but this is speculation.

Cure of Throat Diseases Angerona was invoked to cure and prevent angina (quinsy, a throat infection) and other afflictions of the throat and mouth. Her name was derived by the Romans from 'angina' for this purpose. Festus, De Verborum Significatu; Varro, De Lingua Latina This was a healing function, not a protective one. Her power was therapeutic, not preventive. The connection to angina may be a folk etymology rather than an original function of the goddess.

Relief from Anguish Angerona could free people from 'angor' — the Latin word for anguish, distress, or anxiety. She was a goddess who eased the suffering of the human heart. Varro, De Lingua Latina The relief was likely ritual in nature, sought through prayer and sacrifice at her festival. None noted.

Teaching Silence and Endurance Plutarch suggests that Angerona's festival taught Romans to bear their suffering in silence, a Stoic-adjacent virtue of endurance without complaint. Plutarch, Roman Questions This is an interpretive reading of the goddess's function, not a supernatural power. Plutarch himself offers multiple interpretations, and this is only one of them.

Behavior & Habitat

As a goddess of silence, secrecy, and the protection of hidden names, Angerona's behavior is more about what she prevents than what she does. She guards the unspoken, the secret, and the sacred name. She is not a goddess of action but of restraint — of knowing when not to speak. In her role as a protector against throat diseases, she was invoked to heal or prevent angina and other afflictions of the throat and mouth. She was also associated with the endurance of suffering in silence, as Plutarch notes, teaching Romans to bear their grief without complaint.

Accounts

In the most famous story associated with Angerona, she is the divine guardian of the secret name of Rome. The Romans believed that every city had a true name that was known only to its tutelary deity, and that if an enemy learned this name, they could perform a ritual called evocatio, calling the city's protective gods out of the city and leaving it defenseless. To prevent this, the true name of Rome was never spoken aloud, and Angerona, with her mouth bound and sealed, was the goddess who kept this secret safe. Her statue stood in the temple of Volupia (the goddess of pleasure and satisfaction), where it served as a constant reminder of the power of silence.

Angerona's festival, the Angeronalia, was held on December 21, the winter solstice — the shortest day and longest night of the year. On this day, the pontiffs (the highest-ranking priests of Rome) performed sacrifices to Angerona in the temple of Volupia, or in the Regia, the ancient palace of the kings of Rome. The festival was a time to mark the turning of the year, when the sun began its return and the days began to lengthen. Angerona, as a goddess of silence and endurance, was honored at this dark time of year, when Romans were encouraged to bear the cold and darkness in silence, knowing that light would soon return.

In this tradition, Angerona was a healing goddess who protected against angina and other diseases of the throat. Her name was derived from the Latin 'angina' (quinsy, a severe throat infection), and she was invoked to cure those who suffered from this painful and potentially fatal condition. The connection between the goddess and the disease was so strong that some Romans believed she was named after the illness she cured.

Content Notes

Angerona's role as guardian of Rome's secret name is directly connected to the Roman practice of evocatio, a ritual in which Roman commanders would call out the tutelary deity of an enemy city, promising them a better home in Rome if they would abandon their city. This practice was a form of spiritual siege warfare, designed to strip enemy cities of their divine protection before they were sacked. The fear that Rome itself might be subjected to this ritual was the reason the city's name was kept secret, and Angerona was the goddess who guarded this secret. The practice of evocatio is a reminder of the brutal realities of ancient warfare, in which even gods were not safe from conquest.

Modern scholars have debated the extent to which evocatio was actually practiced, but the ritual is well-attested in Roman military and religious history.

Macrobius, Saturnalia; Pliny, Natural History

Angerona's association with silence and endurance could be read as a divine sanction of the suffering of the lower classes, who were expected to bear their burdens without complaint. Plutarch's interpretation of the goddess as a teacher of silent endurance has been criticized by modern scholars as a form of ideological control, in which the powerless are encouraged to accept their suffering as a virtue.

This reading is a modern interpretation, not an ancient one, but it highlights the ways in which religious symbols can be used to maintain social hierarchies.

What We Don't Know

The primary gap in our knowledge of Angerona is the scarcity of primary sources. We have only a handful of literary references and a single fragmentary inscription. We do not know: (1) the origin of her cult, (2) the exact nature of her earliest worship, (3) the full scope of her powers and functions, (4) the details of her festival's rituals, (5) the appearance of her cult statue beyond the general description of a sealed mouth, (6) the relationship between her healing and protective functions, (7) the actual secret name of Rome, if it ever existed. Further research could focus on archaeological evidence from Rome, particularly the temple of Volupia and the Regia, and on comparative study of other Roman deities with similar functions.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Angerona as the divine equivalent of a government vault with a combination lock that no one knows, guarded by a security system that never sleeps. She is the patron saint of classified information, the goddess of NDAs, the celestial librarian of the file marked 'DO NOT OPEN.' If you've ever kept a secret so important that telling it would change everything, you understand Angerona. She is the reason we say 'loose lips sink ships' — except in her case, loose lips could sink an entire civilization.
