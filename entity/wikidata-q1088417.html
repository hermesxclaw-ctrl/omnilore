Who It Is

Before a stylus ever touched clay, the Sumerians imagined a goddess who already knew how to read it. Nisaba — lady of grain heaped in storehouses and of tablets lined in perfect wedges — was the patron every scribe thanked at the end of a lesson, the goddess who could count the stars, measure a field, and open the mouths of the great gods themselves.

In Sumerian literature Nisaba is consistently benevolent, wise, and maternal. She is 'lady of wisdom' (nin geštu2), 'professor of great wisdom' (geštu2 diri tuku-e), 'unsurpassed overseer' (ugulu-nu-diri) - titles emphasizing intellectual authority and administrative competence rather than martial power. She is approachable to scribes and kings: Lipit-Ishtar (Isin, c. 1934-1924 BCE) and Enlil-bani claim she bestowed wisdom upon them; scribes claim the right to teach is granted by her. Name & Tongue

Etymology — Unknown and generally considered unrecoverable. Written with the divine determinative DINGIR (𒀭) + NAGA sign (𒉀), a pictogram usually interpreted as a plant or sheaf of barley; the same NAGA sign with a different determinative writes her cult center Eresh. Syllabic spellings in Akkadian lexical text

Pronunciation — Sumerian /ni.sa.ba/ (conventional Assyriological reading Nisaba); Akkadian Nisaba; syllabic spellings ni-sa-ba in lexical lists confirm vocalization. Earlier literature renders Nidaba /nidaba/.

Aliases — Nanibgal (Sumerian DAN.NAGA / DAN.ŠE.NAGA), Nunbarshegunu, Nidaba (discredited reading), Nišpa (possible Mari variant, disputed), Aruru, Ezina-Kusu, Kusu (epithets used as names without implying identification)

Powers
Divine Literacy and Scribal Arts

Mastery of cuneiform writing, tablet composition, and scribal craft. She holds the lapis lazuli tablet and golden stylus, writes and reads all texts, and is invoked as the source of scribal skill. Scribes' colophon 'praise to Nisaba' acknowledges her as the giver of literacy.

Limit: Power is intellectual and bestowed, not autonomous creation; she grants the right to teach but does not replace human training. In later periods this domain is transferred to Nabu.

Accounting, Mathematics and Surveying

Numeracy, accounting of grain rations, field measurement, and land surveying. Epithet 'unsurpassed overseer' (ugulu-nu-diri) and association with measuring rod and line. She measures fields, checks accounts, and orients temple foundations by the stars.

Limit: Requires tools (rod, line, tablet); not omniscient - she consults tablets. Tied to administrative order, not wild nature.

Grain Provision and Agricultural Abundance

Deification of barley and grain heaps; ensures harvest, storage, and distribution of grain rations (še-ba). Called 'Nisaba of prosperity' in connection with Haya. Controls abundance and, by withdrawal, famine.

Limit: In late texts reduced to mere grain deification, losing scribal aspect; vulnerable to replacement by Ashnan/Ezina in some local traditions.

Bestowal of Wisdom and Kingship Legitimation

Grants wisdom (geštu2, nēmequ) to rulers and scribes. Lipit-Ishtar and Enlil-bani hymns claim she 'opened their ears' and gave wisdom. Wisdom includes counsel, justice, and ability to rule.

Limit: Wisdom is granted conditionally; kings must maintain cult and justice. Not a warrior's strength.

Opener of the Mouth of the Great Gods

Epithet ka-du8 gal diĝir-re-ne - she enables speech, counsel, and decision in the divine assembly. Interpreted as giving voice/wisdom to the gods, possibly linked to incantation and exorcism in late period.

Limit: Interpretation debated - may be metaphorical for wisdom rather than ritual mouth-opening (pit pî).

Astral Knowledge and Temple Orientation

Knowledge of stars and heavens for divination and temple foundation. In Gudea Cylinder A she 'consults the tablet of the heavens' and determines the temple's plan according to the stars.

Limit: Acts as consultant to Enki and Gudea, not independent creator; her tablet reflects heavenly writing.

Exorcistic and Incantatory Knowledge (Late)

First-millennium association with exorcism (ašipūtu) and protective incantations. Appears in Kalhu compilation alongside exorcistic goddesses.

Limit: Late and secondary; not prominent in earlier Sumerian sources. May reflect scholarly archaism rather than active cult.

Accounts I
Enlil and SudSumerian myth Enlil and Sud (ETCSL 1.2.2), Old Babylonian tablets from Nippur (CBS 8718 etc.)

Old Babylonian copies, composition likely Ur III or earlier (c. 2100-1800 BCE)

Nisaba is the mother of Sud, the city goddess of Shuruppak. Enlil, lord of Nippur, sees Sud and desires to marry her. He sends his sukkal Nuska as intermediary to Nisaba to ask for Sud's hand. Nisaba, described as deeply close to her daughter, at first protects Sud and sets conditions. Nuska negotiates, praising both mother and daughter. With Nisaba's permission, Enlil marries Sud, who is then elevated and renamed Ninlil, becoming Enlil's wife and queen of the gods. The myth explains the origin of Ninlil and legitimates Shuruppak's goddess within the Enlil circle. Aetiology for Ninlil's elevation; establishes Nisaba as mother-in-law of Enlil, explaining contradictory genealogies where she is both mother of Ninlil and later mother-in-law/daughter of Enlil. Reinforces Nisaba's status in Enlil's court and her maternal authority.

Hymn to Nisaba (Nisaba A)ETCSL 4.16.1, Old Babylonian scribal school text (multiple Nippur duplicates)

Old Babylonian, c. 1800 BCE

A 48-line hymn of self-praise put in Nisaba's mouth, used to teach scribes. Nisaba describes herself: born of Enlil? endowed with great wisdom, holding lapis tablet and golden stylus, measuring the heavens and earth, overseeing scribes, opening the mouth of the great gods. She claims to have been praised by Enki, to be the 'great scribe of heaven,' and to make the scribal art flourish. The hymn ends with a blessing for the scribe who copies it. Primary source for her epithets and functions; most quoted evidence for scribal ideology. Demonstrates that scribal training was theologically framed as devotion to Nisaba. Widely copied, indicating central pedagogical role.

The Curse of AgadeETCSL 2.1.5, Old Babylonian copies; historical-literary composition

Composition c. 2100 BCE, copies c. 1800 BCE

After King Naram-Sin of Akkad desecrates Enlil's temple Ekur at Nippur, the great gods decree the destruction of Agade. The text lists the assembly of gods who consent: Sin, Enki, Inanna, Ninurta, Ishkur, Nuska, and Nisaba. Nisaba's inclusion among the seven most prominent deities is noted by modern scholars as unusual, elevating her above many city gods. The gods send the Gutians to destroy Agade and inflict famine - a perversion of Nisaba's grain domain. Shows Nisaba's high rank in the pantheon at the end of the third millennium. Her association with the famine that follows links grain abundance to divine favor. Used by scholars to argue her importance beyond scribal circles.

Accounts II
Gudea's Dream and the Building of Eninnu (Gudea Cylinders)Gudea Cylinders A and B, Lagash II, c. 2120 BCE

Ur III precursor, c. 22nd century BCE

Ruler Gudea of Lagash dreams of a temple and seeks interpretation. The god Ningirsu appears, but Gudea first prays to Nisaba to interpret the dream and to determine the temple's plan by the stars. Nisaba, 'who knows the secrets of the heavenly writing,' consults her lapis tablet and reveals the astral pattern for the Eninnu temple. She measures the foundation and ensures its correct orientation. Gudea then builds the temple with her guidance. Earliest narrative linking Nisaba to astronomy, architecture, and royal legitimation. Establishes her as mediator of divine knowledge for kings. Frequently cited as evidence for her surveying function.

Hymn to Nisaba for Shulgi (Nisaba B) / Royal Hymns of Lipit-Ishtar and Enlil-baniETCSL 4.16.2 (Nisaba B) and ETCSL 2.5.5, 2.5.8 (royal hymns)

Ur III to Old Babylonian, c. 2094-1763 BCE

In Nisaba B, King Shulgi of Ur (c. 2094-2047 BCE) praises Nisaba as his personal patron of wisdom, claiming she gave him understanding of scribal art and made his reign wise. Similarly, hymns to Lipit-Ishtar of Isin and Enlil-bani of Isin credit Nisaba with bestowing wisdom (geštu2) and the ability to pronounce just judgments. The king's legitimacy is tied to Nisaba's gift of literacy and justice, not just martial prowess. Demonstrates political use of Nisaba: kingship requires scribal wisdom. Shows her role beyond schools into royal ideology. Provides evidence for female deity as source of male ruler's legitimacy, noted by Lambert as unusual.

The Kalhu Prayer and the Twin TraditionNeo-Assyrian compilation from Kalhu (Nimrud), CTN 4 201, SAA 3 13

Neo-Assyrian, 9th-7th century BCE (copying older material)

A prayer to Nisaba preserved in a collection of goddess prayers at Kalhu addresses her as 'queen of wisdom' and gives a unique genealogy: she is the twin sister of Enlil, daughter of Ea (equated with the cosmic river Irḫan, 'father of the gods of the universe'). This contradicts the more common tradition where she is Enlil's daughter (via Uraš) or mother-in-law. The text also associates her with exorcism, suggesting she protects against evil. Lambert notes the text 'seems to imply a desire not to have Anu as Nisaba's father,' revealing theological manipulation. Evidence for fluid genealogy and late theological speculation. Shows Nisaba's survival in first-millennium scholarship and her expanded role into magic. Illustrates how scribes rewrote parentage to fit cosmological schemes.

Nisaba's name is Nidaba and means 'lady of Saba' or a known place. — Reading Nidaba is obsolete; syllabic spellings confirm Nisaba. No place Saba is attested; etymology is unknown. The NAGA sign is a plant/sheaf pictogram, not a toponym.

Nisaba was simply the Sumerian version of the Babylonian god Nabu, or they are the same deity. — They are distinct deities of different gender, origin, and period. Nisaba is Sumerian grain/scribe goddess (3rd millennium); Nabu is Babylonian son of Marduk who gradually took over scribal functions in the 2nd-1st millennium. They coexisted for centuries.

Her name written logographically in Syria/Anatolia proves she was worshipped by Hurrians and Hittites. — Outside Mesopotamia DINGIR.NAGA was a logogram used by scribes to write local gods Dagan (Syria), Kumarbi (Hurrian), Halki (Hittite) - a writing convention, not evidence of cult or character similarity.

Nisaba was only a grain goddess who later 'became' a writing goddess after writing was invented. — In all surviving Sumerian literary texts the scribal association is primary and coexists with grain; administrative texts show both roles early. The evolutionary sequence is a modern assumption, not an ancient statement.

There are many statues and images securely identified as Nisaba. — No securely inscribed image exists. The Girsu vase 'vegetation goddess' is a hypothesis; Maxwell-Hyslop noted the inscription does not name her and Bau is an alternative. Most visual identifications are tentative.

Nisaba was a minor or obscure goddess. — She was among the most prominent deities in the Curse of Agade (listed with Enki, Inanna, etc.), central to the entire scribal curriculum, and invoked by kings for legitimation. Her prominence is underestimated due to modern focus on Inanna/Marduk.
