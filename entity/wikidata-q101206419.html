Who It Is

The Devil is perhaps the most recognizable figure of evil in Western culture, yet behind the cloven hooves and pitchfork lies a complex history of shifting identities, theological debates, and cultural reinterpretations that span millennia.

Adversary, tempter, accuser, deceiver, murderer, liar (John 8:44). In folklore: trickster, bargainer, sometimes foolish or easily outwitted. In literature: ambitious, proud, rebellious, charismatic.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Old English 'deofol', from Latin 'diabolus', from Greek 'diabolos' (slanderer, accuser), from 'diaballein' (to slander, literally 'to throw across').

Pronunciation — /ˈdɛvəl/

Aliases — Satan, Lucifer, Beelzebub, the Adversary, the Tempter, the Serpent

Ownership — Primarily Judeo-Christian, but also present in Islamic, Zoroastrian, and various folk traditions; adapted globally.

Names are cult layers, not one fixed label.

Powers
Supernatural influence and temptation

Can tempt humans to sin, possess individuals, and perform deceptive miracles.

Limit: Cannot act without God's permission (Job 1:12); limited by divine sovereignty.

Shape-shifting

Can appear as an angel of light, a serpent, or other forms.

Limit: Unclear if he can take any form or only specific ones.

Possession

Can enter and control humans or animals.

Limit: Exorcism in Jesus' name can expel him.

Knowledge and deception

Extensive knowledge of scripture and human weakness; can deceive even the elect (Matthew 24:24).

Limit: Not omniscient; can be resisted by faith.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Varies widely: from a serpent (Genesis), a roaring lion (1 Peter 5:8), an angel of light (2 Corinthians 11:14), to a monstrous dragon (Revelation). In medieval art: horned, cloven-hoofed, often with bat wings, tail, and pitchfork. Milton gives him a fallen angel's grandeur.

Symbols — Pitchfork, serpent, dragon, goat (Baphomet), inverted pentagram, owl, black dog, fire and brimstone, red or black colors, horns, wings (bat-like), tail.

Domains — Hell, the abyss, the wilderness (temptation of Christ), the air (Ephesians 2:2), the earth (roams about, Job 1:7). In folklore: crossroads, desolate places, sometimes the underworld.

Accounts I
The Fall of SatanIsaiah 14:12-15; Ezekiel 28:12-19; Revelation 12:7-9

Prophetic writings (8th-6th century BCE); Revelation (1st century CE)

Originally a high angel (Lucifer) who rebelled against God out of pride, was cast out of heaven along with his followers. Satan becomes the enemy of humanity, seeks to ruin God's creation.

The Temptation of Adam and EveGenesis 3

Unknown, written down c. 10th-6th century BCE

The serpent tempts Eve to eat the forbidden fruit, leading to the expulsion from Eden and the introduction of sin. Original sin, death, and suffering enter the world.

Accounts II
The Temptation of ChristMatthew 4:1-11; Luke 4:1-13

1st century CE

After Jesus' baptism, the Devil tempts him in the wilderness, offering power and glory, but Jesus resists with scripture. Jesus begins his ministry; the Devil departs 'until an opportune time'.

The Book of JobJob 1-2

c. 6th-4th century BCE

Satan, as an accuser in God's court, tests Job's faithfulness by taking his wealth, family, and health, but Job remains faithful. Job is restored; Satan is shown as subordinate to God.

The War in HeavenRevelation 12:7-9

1st century CE

Michael and his angels fight the dragon (Satan), who is defeated and thrown to earth. Satan's time is short; he persecutes the woman (Israel or the Church).

Nature of the DevilA personal, supernatural being.A symbol of evil or human inclination.

The Devil has horns and a pitchfork. — This image developed in medieval art, not in the Bible.

The Devil rules Hell and tortures sinners. — In Christian theology, Hell is a place of punishment prepared for the Devil and his angels (Matthew 25:41); the Devil himself is tormented, not the ruler.

The Devil is equal to God. — In orthodox Christianity, the Devil is a created being, far less powerful than God.

The Devil can be summoned and controlled. — Most religious traditions warn against such practices; the Devil is seen as untrustworthy and dangerous.
