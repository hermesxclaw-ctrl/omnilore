The Approach

The Devil is perhaps the most recognizable figure of evil in Western culture, yet behind the cloven hooves and pitchfork lies a complex history of shifting identities, theological debates, and cultural reinterpretations that span millennia.

Content advisory. Contains religious themes, depictions of evil, violence, and references to demonology and witchcraft.
Identity

Devil

Pronunciation /ˈdɛvəl/

Known As

SatanLuciferBeelzebubthe Adversarythe Tempterthe Serpentthe Dragonthe Prince of Darknessthe Evil OneIblisShaitan

Etymology

From Old English 'deofol', from Latin 'diabolus', from Greek 'diabolos' (slanderer, accuser), from 'diaballein' (to slander, literally 'to throw across').

Cultural Ownership

Primarily Judeo-Christian, but also present in Islamic, Zoroastrian, and various folk traditions; adapted globally.

Disputed Identifications

Whether Satan, Lucifer, and the Devil are distinct beings or the same entity is debated; identification with the serpent in Genesis is disputed; some traditions separate Beelzebub as a distinct demon.

Description

Appearance

Varies widely: from a serpent (Genesis), a roaring lion (1 Peter 5:8), an angel of light (2 Corinthians 11:14), to a monstrous dragon (Revelation). In medieval art: horned, cloven-hoofed, often with bat wings, tail, and pitchfork. Milton gives him a fallen angel's grandeur.

Symbols & Iconography

Pitchfork, serpent, dragon, goat (Baphomet), inverted pentagram, owl, black dog, fire and brimstone, red or black colors, horns, wings (bat-like), tail.

Behaviour & Personality

Adversary, tempter, accuser, deceiver, murderer, liar (John 8:44). In folklore: trickster, bargainer, sometimes foolish or easily outwitted. In literature: ambitious, proud, rebellious, charismatic.

Domains & Habitats

Hell, the abyss, the wilderness (temptation of Christ), the air (Ephesians 2:2), the earth (roams about, Job 1:7). In folklore: crossroads, desolate places, sometimes the underworld.

Version Differences

Ancient Jewish: a member of God's court, an accuser. New Testament: the enemy of God, ruler of demons. Islamic: Iblis, a jinn who refused to bow, not a fallen angel. Medieval Christian: a repulsive, bestial figure. Romantic era: a heroic rebel. Modern popular culture: often a comedic or sympathetic figure.

Powers & Abilities

Supernatural influence and temptation

Source Version New Testament; Christian theology

Description

Can tempt humans to sin, possess individuals, and perform deceptive miracles.

Limitations

Cannot act without God's permission (Job 1:12); limited by divine sovereignty.

Contradictions

Some traditions see him as all-powerful in the world, others as restricted.

Shape-shifting

Source Version 2 Corinthians 11:14; Genesis 3

Description

Can appear as an angel of light, a serpent, or other forms.

Limitations

Unclear if he can take any form or only specific ones.

Contradictions

No explicit limitation given.

Possession

Source Version Gospels (e.g., Mark 5:1-20)

Description

Can enter and control humans or animals.

Limitations

Exorcism in Jesus' name can expel him.

Contradictions

Some denominations downplay literal possession.

Knowledge and deception

Source Version Temptation of Christ (Matthew 4); Christian theology

Description

Extensive knowledge of scripture and human weakness; can deceive even the elect (Matthew 24:24).

Limitations

Not omniscient; can be resisted by faith.

Contradictions

Some traditions attribute near-omniscience, others not.

Source Isaiah 14:12-15; Ezekiel 28:12-19; Revelation 12:7-9Culture Judeo-ChristianPeriod Prophetic writings (8th-6th century BCE); Revelation (1st century CE)

Summary

Originally a high angel (Lucifer) who rebelled against God out of pride, was cast out of heaven along with his followers.

Participants

Lucifer/Satan

God

Michael the Archangel

fallen angels

Consequences

Satan becomes the enemy of humanity, seeks to ruin God's creation.

Variants

Some traditions see the fall as due to envy of humanity; others as a cosmic war.

The Temptation of Adam and Eve

Source Genesis 3Culture Judeo-ChristianPeriod Unknown, written down c. 10th-6th century BCE

Summary

The serpent tempts Eve to eat the forbidden fruit, leading to the expulsion from Eden and the introduction of sin.

Participants

Serpent (identified with Satan in later tradition)

Eve

Adam

Consequences

Original sin, death, and suffering enter the world.

Variants

In Islamic tradition, Iblis tempts both Adam and Eve; in some Jewish interpretations, the serpent is not Satan.

The Book of Job

Relationships

God

Type adversarySource Job 1; Revelation 20:10

Description

Satan opposes God's will, but is ultimately subordinate and will be defeated.

Version Note

In Job, Satan is an obedient servant; in later tradition, a rebel.

Conflicting Accounts

Some dualistic traditions (e.g., Manichaeism) see them as co-eternal powers.

Jesus Christ

Type adversarySource Matthew 4; Colossians 2:15

Description

Satan tempts Jesus, but is defeated by his death and resurrection.

Version Note

In Christianity, Christ's victory is central.

Conflicting Accounts

Some early Christian sects saw Jesus as a mere human tempted by Satan, not divine.

Adam and Eve

Type victimSource Genesis 3; Wisdom of Solomon 2:24

Description

Through the serpent, Satan deceives humanity into sin.

Version Note

The identification of the serpent with Satan is explicit in Wisdom and later tradition.

Conflicting Accounts

In Judaism, the serpent is not always Satan; some see it as a mere animal.

Iblis

Type equivalentSource Quran 2:34, 7:11

Description

In Islam, Iblis is the equivalent figure, a jinn who refused to bow to Adam.

Version Note

Iblis is not a fallen angel but a jinn; his disobedience is due to pride and racism.

Conflicting Accounts

Some Islamic scholars equate Iblis with the Devil; others distinguish.

Witches and warlocks

Type worshipperSource Malleus Maleficarum (1486); various witch trial records

Description

In Christian folklore and witch trials, the Devil was believed to make pacts with witches, granting powers in exchange for their souls.

Version Note

This belief was prominent in early modern Europe.

Conflicting Accounts

Modern historians often view witch trials as social persecution rather than actual devil worship.

Witch hunts and persecution

Source Historical records of witch trials (e.g., Salem witch trials, European inquisitions)

Description

Belief in the Devil fueled witch hunts in Europe and America, leading to thousands of executions, mostly women.

Scholarly Context

Modern scholarship sees these as complex social phenomena, not actual devil worship.

Anti-Semitic and misogynistic imagery

Source Medieval Christian art and polemics

Description

The Devil has been used to demonize marginalized groups, including Jews (e.g., blood libel) and women (e.g., as temptresses).

Scholarly Context

This is a historical misuse of religious imagery to justify persecution.

Satanic panic

Source News reports and legal cases (e.g., McMartin preschool trial)

Description

In the 1980s, moral panics about Satanic ritual abuse led to false accusations and wrongful convictions.

Scholarly Context

Subsequent investigations found no evidence of such abuse; the panic was a social phenomenon.

Contradictions & Disputes

Identity of Lucifer

Position A

Lucifer is Satan before his fall.

Position A — evidence

Christian tradition, e.g., Jerome's Vulgate, associating Lucifer with Satan.

Position B

Lucifer is a separate figure or a misinterpretation of Isaiah 14 referring to a Babylonian king.

Position B — evidence

Modern scholarship notes the Hebrew 'helel' refers to a king of Babylon, not a fallen angel.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars agree the Isaiah passage is about a human king, but the traditional identification persists in theology and culture.

The serpent in Genesis

Position A

The serpent is Satan.

Position A — evidence

Revelation 12:9, later Jewish and Christian tradition.

Position B

The serpent is not identified as Satan in the text itself.

Position B — evidence

Genesis simply says 'the serpent'; the identification is later.

Scholarly Consensus

The identification is a later interpretation, not explicit in the original text.

Nature of the Devil

Position A

A personal, supernatural being.

Position A — evidence

Biblical texts, church teaching.

Position B

A symbol of evil or human inclination.

Position B — evidence

Modern theological liberalism and secular interpretations.

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus; depends on theological stance.
