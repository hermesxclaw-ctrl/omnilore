Who It Is

No one bargains with Jarā. In Buddhist texts she is not a monster to be slain but the slow, brittle hardening itself — grey hair, wrinkled skin, loosening teeth, the dimming of the senses — rolling over every living being like mountains grinding inward from all sides, sparing neither warrior nor outcaste. To name her is to name the twelfth and final link of the chain that binds birth to suffering.

Doctrinal Jarā is impersonal, inexorable, and universal (sādhāraṇa). Texts stress three characteristics: (1) inevitability (avitarana) - no being who is born escapes it, including devas; (2) gradualness (anupubba) - aging proceeds moment by moment, not suddenly; (3) dukkha-nature - it frustrates desire for permanence and youth. It is not malevolent but lawful, conditioned by birth (jāti-paccayā jarāmaraṇaṃ). The Buddha enjoins not fear but frequent recollection (abhiṇhaṃ paccavekkhitabbaṃ) to arouse urgency (saṃvega). Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Vedic Sanskrit jarā, jaras, jarati, gerā 'to become brittle, to decay, to be consumed, to wear out'. From Proto-Indo-European root *ger- 'to grow old, mature, harden'. Cognate with Avestan zar-, Greek gēras (γῆρας) 'old age' and gerōn (γέρων) 'old man' (hence English geriatric, gerontology), La

Pronunciation — Sanskrit: /dʒɐˈraː/ (ja-RAA), Pāli: /dʒəˈraː/; anglicized as juh-RAH. Diacritic ā indicates long vowel. Maraṇa pronounced ma-RA-na with retroflex ṇ.

Aliases — Jara, Jarā, Jaras, Gerā, Jarāmaraṇa (when paired with Maraṇa), Jīrṇatā (synonym in later Sanskrit)

Ownership — Pan-Indic term originating in Vedic Sanskrit, systematized in early Buddhism (5th century BCE onwards) and retained in all Buddhist schools (Theravada, Mahayana, Vajrayana) as well

Powers
Inexorable Decay (Jarā as Dukkha)

Causes progressive deterioration of all conditioned phenomena: graying, wrinkling, loss of teeth, dimming of senses, loss of strength and memory, bending of posture, dependence on support. Not a power wielded but a law-like process.

Limit: Only operates where there has been birth (jāti); does not affect nibbana or an arahant's liberated mind (though their body still ages). Cannot be reversed by wealth, status, or divine power - Pabbatopama Sutta explicitly lists khattiya, brahmana, vessa, sudda, candala as equally trampled.

Conditioning Death (Jarā-paccayā Maraṇa continuum)

In the nidana chain, aging directly conditions death and the associated grief complex: sokaparidevadukkhadomanassupāyāsa (sorrow, lamentation, pain, distress, despair). Jarā is the visible prelude that makes death imminent.

Limit: Not deterministic in timing; lifespan varies by karma and realm. Some beings die without prolonged aging (akāla-maraṇa).

Flesh-Joining (Sandhāna) - Rākṣasī Jarā

Supernatural ability to physically join two living halves of flesh into a viable whole. She picks up two half-bodies expelled by Brihadratha's queens (after consuming a mango divided by sage Chandakaushika) and presses them together, whereupon they fuse into a living infant.

Limit: Requires two complementary halves from same conception; not creation ex nihilo. She acts out of curiosity, not assured success. Power is accidental/innate to rākṣasī nature, not learned.

Karmic Instrument (Hunter Jarā as Nimitta)

Unwittingly fulfills cosmic justice: his arrow becomes the instrument for Krishna's voluntary departure (svadhamatyaga) and the exhaustion of Gandhari's curse and Vali's vengeance. Krishna's foot bears a lotus mark mistaken for a deer's mouth.

Limit: No divine power himself; ordinary hunter. His act is error (mistaking foot for prey) and immediately regretted. Cannot harm Krishna against Krishna's will - Krishna permits it.

Contemplative Catalyst (Anussati)

When recollected intentionally (jarānussati, part of five daily recollections), Jarā generates saṃvega (spiritual urgency), dispels intoxication with youth (yobbana-mada), and motivates practice of Dhamma. Functions as a protective meditation.

Limit: Requires wise attention (yoniso manasikara); unwise reflection leads to depression or aversion. Only effective if paired with refuge in Buddha/Dhamma/Sangha (SN 3.25).

Form & Signs

Appearance — As a Buddhist doctrinal principle, Jarā has no fixed anthropomorphic form; texts describe its marks on the body rather than a body itself: khaṇḍicca (broken teeth), pālicca (grey hair), valittacatā (wrinkling of skin), āyuno saṃhāni (decline of life-force), indriyānaṃ paripāko (weakening/decay of the faculties - sight, hearing, strength, memory). Domains — Doctrinal domain: all realms of saṃsāra where birth occurs — kāma-loka (sense world including human, animal, hell, preta, asura, lower deva realms) and rūpa/arūpa realms where subtle aging still occurs as decline of lifespan. Only parinibbāna (final nirvana without remainder) is free from Jarā. In cosmology, even long-lived devas age and fall (cuti). Jarā operates wherever the five aggregates (khandhas) persist. Accounts I
The First Noble Truth - Birth, Aging, Illness, Death as SufferingDhammacakkappavattana Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 56.11

5th-3rd century BCE (first sermon at Sarnath)

In his first discourse after awakening at Deer Park, the Buddha defines dukkha for the five ascetics. He lists jarā and maraṇa alongside birth, illness, sorrow, lamentation, and the five aggregates of clinging as intrinsic suffering. The teaching establishes that aging and death are not accidental misfortunes but structural features of conditioned existence that must be understood to achieve liberation. The sutta becomes the foundational statement repeated in all schools. Establishes jarāmaraṇa as central to Buddhist soteriology; recited liturgically across Theravada, Mahayana, and Vajrayana; forms basis for all later Abhidhamma analysis of dukkha.

What is Aging? What is Death? - The Analytical DefinitionMaha-satipatthana Sutta DN 22 and Vibhanga Sutta SN 12.2 (Nidana Samyutta)

3rd century BCE - 1st century CE

The Buddha provides precise Abhidhammic definitions when teaching the Four Noble Truths within the Satipatthana framework and when analyzing Dependent Origination. Aging is defined phenomenologically: brokenness, graying, wrinkling, decline of life-force, weakening of faculties. Death is defined as deceasing, passing away, breaking up of aggregates, casting off of body, interruption of life faculty. This moves jarā from poetic lament to technical, observable process to be contemplated in mindfulness of dhammas. Becomes standard definition quoted in Visuddhimagga, Abhidharmakosha, and all commentaries; used in vipassana meditation instructions to note aging as it manifests moment-to-moment.

I Am Subject to Aging - The Five Daily RecollectionsUpajjhatthana Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 5.57

3rd century BCE

The Buddha enjoins all followers - monks, nuns, laymen, laywomen - to reflect often on five facts: I am subject to aging, illness, death, separation from the loved, and I am owner of my karma. For jarā, the reflection is 'jarādhammo'mhi jaraṃ anatīto' - I have not gone beyond aging. The purpose is to abandon intoxication with youth (yobbana-mada) and heedlessness, and to live diligently. The sutta explicitly says this contemplation is for everyone, not just monastics. Daily chanting practice in Theravada monasteries worldwide; foundation for maranasati and asubha practices; adapted in modern secular 'five remembrances' by Thich Nhat Hanh.

Accounts II
Like Mountains Crushing the World - The Simile of the MountainsPabbatopama Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 3.25 (Kosala Samyutta)

5th-3rd century BCE

King Pasenadi of Kosala visits the Buddha and asks what should be done. The Buddha asks him to imagine massive boulders, mountains pressing against the sky, moving in from all four directions crushing everything. So too, aging and death roll over all living beings - noble warriors, brahmins, merchants, workers, outcastes, scavengers - sparing nothing, trampling everything. The only refuge is to secure confidence in Buddha, Dhamma, Sangha and practice Dhamma in thought, word, and deed, which brings praise here and joy after death in heaven. Classic literary simile quoted in sermons and commentaries; illustrates universality and inescapability of jarāmaraṇa; reinforces that social hierarchy offers no protection.

The Painted Puppet Decays - The Chapter on Old AgeDhammapada, Jaravagga (Chapter 11, verses 146-156)

3rd century BCE - 2nd century CE (anthology)

Eleven verses meditate on the aged body to dispel attachment. Verse 146: 'What laughter, what joy when the world is ever burning? Shrouded in darkness, why not seek a light?' Verse 148-149 describes the body as 'a painted puppet, a heap of sores, diseased, much thought about, with no stability.' Verse 150: 'This body is worn out, a nest of diseases, dissolving.' The chapter was taught when the Buddha showed monks the decrepit body of the courtesan Sirima or the aged nun Uttara (commentarial stories). It links jarā directly to insight into anicca and anatta. Memorized widely; chanted at funerals; verses 146-148 common in meditation on foulness (patikulamanasikara); influences Buddhist poetry across Pali, Sanskrit, Chinese, and Japanese traditions.

Joined by Jarā - The Birth of JarasandhaMahabharata, Sabha Parva Chapters 17-18 (Pune Critical Edition 2.17-18); Harivamsa 31

c. 400 BCE - 400 CE (epic redaction)

King Brihadratha of Magadha has two queens but no heir. Sage Chandakaushika (or Gautama in some versions) gives him a mango imbued with power, to be divided between queens. Each queen bears half a child - one eye, one arm, one leg. Horrified, the halves are discarded on a rubbish heap. The rākṣasī Jarā, who lives on discarded flesh, finds them, and out of curiosity places the two halves together. They fuse instantly into a complete, vigorous infant who cries loudly. She presents the child to the king, who names him Jarasandha ('joined by Jara') and rewards Jarā, who is thereafter worshipped as Goddess Jara in Magadha. Jarasandha grows into the great adversary of Krishna. Explains name and invulnerability of Jarasandha (can only be killed by being split again, as Bhima does); establishes folk cult of Jarai/Jara Devi in Bihar; repeated in Puranas (Bhagavata 9.22, Brahma Purana).

The Hunter's Arrow - The Death of KrishnaMahabharata, Mausala Parva 4.12-20; Bhagavata Purana 11.30.33-38; Vishnu Purana 5.37

Mausala Parva c. 200-400 CE; Bhagavata Purana 8th-10th century CE

After the Yadava fratricide at Prabhasa, Krishna sits in yogic meditation under a tree in the forest, his left foot resting on his right thigh, its lotus-marked sole exposed. The hunter Jarā (also called Jara, Jara Shabara) prowling for deer mistakes the reddish sole for a deer's mouth and shoots a barbed arrow. Realizing he has shot a divine man, he rushes forward in terror. Krishna, though wounded mortally, comforts him, says this fulfills destiny - in Treta Yuga as Rama he had killed Vali from hiding, and Vali is now reborn as Jarā to return the act - and grants him heaven before ascending. Krishna's death marks the onset of Kali Yuga. Theologically explains how the immortal can die; links Ramayana and Mahabharata karma; site at Bhalka Tirtha (Somnath) becomes pilgrimage place; hunter becomes revered figure despite act.

Is jarāmaraṇa one link or two?Jarā and maraṇa constitute a single combined twelfth nidana (jarāmaraṇa as compound). This is how the 12-link formula is traditionally counted and chanted.Jarā and maraṇa are two distinct phenomena with separate definitions and should be counted separately, making 13 factors. Abhidhamma defines each separately with different lakshanas.
Are the rākṣasī Jarā and hunter Jarā the same entity?They are the same figure - a single mythological being named Jarā/Jara who appears in two epic episodes, reflecting a folk goddess of Magadha with both creative and destructive aspects.They are two distinct figures who happen to share a common name (Jara = old age) with no narrative connection; conflation is folk etymology.
Does jarā affect only the body or also mind/momentary dhammas?Jarā is primarily biological aging over a lifetime - gray hair, wrinkles, etc. - as described in suttas.Jarā is a momentary characteristic of all conditioned dhammas, occurring in each mind-moment as the 'aging' phase between arising and dissolution.

Jarāmaraṇa means only 'death' or is a god of death like Yama/Mara. — Jarā is specifically 'aging/old age/decay' and maraṇa is 'death'; the compound covers the entire process from decrepitude to dying and the grief that follows. It is not a deity but a conditioned process. Yama is the lord of death, Mara is temptation/death personified - distinct figures.

Buddhism is pessimistic because it says aging is suffering, implying old people are worthless. — The First Noble Truth identifies the experience of aging as dukkha (unsatisfactory, stressful) because it involves loss and impermanence, not because aged persons lack value. The same canon venerates elders (thera/theri) and enjoins caring for aged parents as a supreme duty (Sigalovada Sutta). The teaching is diagnostic, pointing to cessation.

Jara the hunter intentionally murdered Krishna and was punished. — All epic sources stress it was accidental - he mistook Krishna's foot for a deer - and that Krishna forgave him and granted him heaven, explaining it as karmic fulfillment (Vali's revenge) and Krishna's own will to depart. He was not punished.

Jarasandha was literally stitched together by a demon and was invulnerable. — The Mahabharata presents the joining as a miraculous folk etymology for the name Jarasandha ('joined by Jara'). His 'invulnerability' is narrative: he can only be killed by being split again (Bhima tears him apart), mirroring his birth. The text treats Jarā as a rākṣasī who is then worshipped as a goddess, not purely demonic.

Contemplating aging and death (jarānussati) is morbid and causes depression. — In canonical instruction (AN 5.57, SN 3.25), recollection of jarā is paired with refuge and diligent practice, intended to produce urgency (saṃvega) and dispassion, not despair. When done with wise attention and guidance, studies show it can reduce death anxiety; unwise attention without support can increase anxiety, which texts acknowledge.
