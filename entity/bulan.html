Who It Is

In the moonlit archipelago that would become the Philippines, ancient peoples looked up and saw not a cold, distant rock but a living deity—Bulan, the moon god of the Tagalog and Visayan peoples. Unlike the solitary moon gods of other cultures, Bulan was part of a celestial family, a being of light and shadow whose story intertwines with the great serpent Bakunawa, the sun goddess Arao, and the lunar deity Mayari. To understand Bulan is to glimpse the rich, complex cosmology of pre-colonial Philippines, where the heavens were a stage for drama, love, and cosmic struggle.

Bulan is portrayed as a brave and valiant deity, often engaged in cosmic battles against the serpent Bakunawa. He is also seen as a protector of the night, guiding travelers and lovers. In some stories, he is a playful and mischievous figure, sometimes causing eclipses by hiding his face. He is generally benevolent, but can be wrathful if offended. He is associated with the tides, and his moods are reflected in the phases of the moon.

It is cognate with other Austronesian terms for the moon, such as 'bulan' in Malay and Indonesian, 'bulan' in Javanese, and 'vulan' in some Philippine languages. Powers
Lunar Control

Bulan has the ability to control the phases of the moon, waxing and waning it at will. He can also cause eclipses by hiding his face or by being swallowed by Bakunawa.

Limit: He is not always able to prevent Bakunawa from swallowing the moon, and during an eclipse, his power wanes.

Tide Manipulation

As a moon deity, Bulan controls the tides of the ocean, pulling the waters with his light.

Limit: His influence on tides is strongest during full and new moons.

Superhuman Combat Skills

Bulan is a skilled warrior, often depicted wielding a bow and arrow or a sword. He uses these weapons to battle Bakunawa and other celestial threats.

Limit: He can be temporarily defeated or swallowed by Bakunawa, though he always manages to escape.

Illumination

Bulan can cast a silvery light that illuminates the night, guiding travelers and lovers. He can also create shadows and darkness when he chooses.

Limit: His light is weaker than the sun's, and he is absent during the day.

Shape-shifting (limited)

In some stories, Bulan can take human form to interact with mortals, often appearing as a handsome young man.

Limit: He may lose his powers while in human form, and he must return to the sky before dawn.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Bulan is typically described as a handsome young man, radiant and glowing with a pale light. In some accounts, he is depicted as a muscular warrior, while in others he is more effeminate. His skin is said to be fair, and he often wears a headdress or crown resembling a crescent moon. Symbols — The crescent moon is the primary symbol of Bulan. He is associated with the color silver, white, and pale gold. Common iconographic elements include the moon disc, a bow and arrow (used to shoot stars or to fight Bakunawa), and a shield. In some depictions, he holds a torch or a lantern, symbolizing his role as a light-bringer in the night. His symbols are often used in traditional tattoos and amulets.

Domains — Bulan resides in the sky, specifically in the moon itself. He is believed to dwell in a palace made of silver and light, from where he watches over the earth at night. He is also associated with the sea, as he controls the tides. His domain includes the night sky, the moon, and the oceans. In some myths, he descends to earth to interact with humans, particularly during festivals or when he takes a human lover.

Accounts I
The Battle with BakunawaVisayan mythology, recorded in various ethnographic sources

Pre-colonial

Bakunawa, a gigantic serpent, attempts to swallow the moon. Bulan, the moon god, battles the serpent to prevent the eclipse. In some versions, Bulan is aided by the sun god Arao and other deities. The battle is cyclical, recurring each time Bakunawa tries to devour the moon. Eventually, Bulan and the other gods manage to drive Bakunawa away, but the serpent returns periodically, causing eclipses. The story explains the occurrence of lunar eclipses. It also establishes Bulan as a protector of the celestial order and a heroic figure.

The Love Story of Bulan and AraoVisayan mythology, as recorded by Miguel de Loarca

Pre-colonial

Bulan, the moon god, is married to Arao, the sun goddess. They take turns crossing the sky, with Arao ruling the day and Bulan the night. Their love is tested by their separation, and they communicate through the twilight. Their union is believed to bring balance to the world. The story explains the alternation of day and night and the existence of dawn and dusk as moments of their meeting.

Accounts II
Bulan and the Mortal WomanVisayan folklore

Pre-colonial

Bulan descends to earth in human form and falls in love with a mortal woman. He courts her under the cover of night, and she bears him a child. However, their love is doomed because Bulan must return to the sky. The woman pines away, and their child becomes a hero. The story explains the origin of certain heroic lineages and the connection between the divine and mortal realms.

The Creation of the StarsTagalog and Visayan mythology

Pre-colonial

Bulan, while wandering the sky, drops pieces of his light, which become the stars. In some versions, he scatters silver dust to create the Milky Way. This story explains the origin of the stars and the Milky Way. The stars are seen as remnants of Bulan's light, and they guide travelers at night.

Bulan is the same as Mayari — While both are lunar deities, they are distinct in many sources. Mayari is primarily a Tagalog goddess, while Bulan is more prominent in Visayan mythology. They may have been merged in modern popular culture.

Bulan is a goddess — In most traditional accounts, Bulan is male. The female association comes from the Tagalog Mayari and from modern interpretations.

Bulan is the only moon deity in Philippine mythology — There are multiple moon deities across different ethnic groups, including Mayari, Bulan, and others. Each has distinct attributes and stories.

Bulan was worshipped with human sacrifice — There is no evidence of human sacrifice in association with Bulan. Pre-colonial Philippine religions did practice animal sacrifice, but not human sacrifice for lunar deities.
