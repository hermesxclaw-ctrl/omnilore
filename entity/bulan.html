Etymology

The name 'Bulan' is derived from the Proto-Malayo-Polynesian word *bulan, meaning 'moon' or 'month'. It is cognate with other Austronesian terms for the moon, such as 'bulan' in Malay and Indonesian, 'bulan' in Javanese, and 'vulan' in some Philippine languages. The name directly refers to the celestial body and the deity personifying it.

Cultural Ownership

Bulan is a deity from the pre-colonial mythologies of the Philippines, specifically associated with the Tagalog and Visayan peoples. It is part of the broader Austronesian mythological tradition. The deity is not exclusive to one ethnic group; variations exist across the archipelago. The name and concept are indigenous, though some colonial-era accounts were recorded by Spanish missionaries and chroniclers.

Disputed Identifications

There is some scholarly debate regarding the gender and attributes of Bulan. Some sources describe Bulan as a male deity, while others, particularly in Tagalog mythology, associate the moon with the goddess Mayari. The relationship between Bulan and Mayari is not entirely clear; they may be separate deities or aspects of the same lunar divinity. Additionally, some accounts conflate Bulan with the sun god Arao, but they are generally distinct. The exact nature of Bulan's role in the pantheon is disputed due to the scarcity and conflicting nature of primary sources.

Archive Dossier — Approach

In the moonlit archipelago that would become the Philippines, ancient peoples looked up and saw not a cold, distant rock but a living deity—Bulan, the moon god of the Tagalog and Visayan peoples. Unlike the solitary moon gods of other cultures, Bulan was part of a celestial family, a being of light and shadow whose story intertwines with the great serpent Bakunawa, the sun goddess Arao, and the lunar deity Mayari. To understand Bulan is to glimpse the rich, complex cosmology of pre-colonial Philippines, where the heavens were a stage for drama, love, and cosmic struggle.

Who It Is

Bulan is the moon deity in Bicolano mythology, distinct from the Tagalog Mayari. Bulan is described as a beautiful, gentle deity who controls the tides, the menstrual cycles of women, and the rhythm of sea-based agriculture. In the Bicolano epic Ibalon (the oldest surviving full narrative from pre-colonial Philippines), Bulan is the son of the supreme god Gugurang. He is associated with Mount Mayon — the volcano's perfect cone is said to be Bulan's eternal tribute to his lost love (in some versions) or his place of rest. The Bicolano people believed eclipses occurred when a giant sea serpent (Bakunawa) tried to swallow Bulan. During lunar eclipses, they would beat drums and gongs to scare the serpent away. Bulan was also the patron of fishermen, who prayed to him for calm seas and good catches. Offerings to Bulan included white chickens, coconut oil, and silver coins. The name Bulan simply means 'moon' in many Philippine languages, and the deity's gentle, protective nature contrasts with the more martial Mayari of the Tagalogs. In some versions, Bulan is female; in others, male — the moon's gender varies by region.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

Bulan is typically described as a handsome young man, radiant and glowing with a pale light. In some accounts, he is depicted as a muscular warrior, while in others he is more effeminate. His skin is said to be fair, and he often wears a headdress or crown resembling a crescent moon. He carries a bow and arrow or a sword, symbolizing his role as a hunter and warrior. In art, he is sometimes shown riding a carriage pulled by horses or in a boat, representing the moon's journey across the sky.

Symbols & Iconography

The crescent moon is the primary symbol of Bulan. He is associated with the color silver, white, and pale gold. Common iconographic elements include the moon disc, a bow and arrow (used to shoot stars or to fight Bakunawa), and a shield. In some depictions, he holds a torch or a lantern, symbolizing his role as a light-bringer in the night. His symbols are often used in traditional tattoos and amulets.

Behaviour & Personality

Bulan is portrayed as a brave and valiant deity, often engaged in cosmic battles against the serpent Bakunawa. He is also seen as a protector of the night, guiding travelers and lovers. In some stories, he is a playful and mischievous figure, sometimes causing eclipses by hiding his face. He is generally benevolent, but can be wrathful if offended. He is associated with the tides, and his moods are reflected in the phases of the moon.

Domains & Habitats

Bulan resides in the sky, specifically in the moon itself. He is believed to dwell in a palace made of silver and light, from where he watches over the earth at night. He is also associated with the sea, as he controls the tides. His domain includes the night sky, the moon, and the oceans. In some myths, he descends to earth to interact with humans, particularly during festivals or when he takes a human lover.

Version Differences

There are notable differences between Tagalog and Visayan versions. In Visayan mythology, Bulan is a male deity and the husband of the sun goddess Arao. In some Tagalog accounts, the moon is female and identified with Mayari, who is sometimes considered the daughter of Bathala. Other versions, particularly from the Bicol region, involve Bulan as a deity who battles the serpent Bakunawa. The relationship between Bulan and Mayari is ambiguous: they may be separate deities, with Mayari being a lunar goddess and Bulan a lunar god, or they may be the same deity with different names. Some modern retellings blend these versions, creating a more unified lunar deity.

Archive Dossier — Powers

Lunar Control

Source Version Visayan and Tagalog mythology

Description

Bulan has the ability to control the phases of the moon, waxing and waning it at will. He can also cause eclipses by hiding his face or by being swallowed by Bakunawa.

Limitations

He is not always able to prevent Bakunawa from swallowing the moon, and during an eclipse, his power wanes.

Contradictions

Some accounts attribute eclipses to Bakunawa's actions, while others suggest Bulan himself causes them as a sign of anger or mourning.

Tide Manipulation

Source Version Visayan mythology

Description

As a moon deity, Bulan controls the tides of the ocean, pulling the waters with his light.

Limitations

His influence on tides is strongest during full and new moons.

Contradictions

No major contradictions documented.

Superhuman Combat Skills

Source Version Visayan and Bicolano mythology

Description

Bulan is a skilled warrior, often depicted wielding a bow and arrow or a sword. He uses these weapons to battle Bakunawa and other celestial threats.

Limitations

He can be temporarily defeated or swallowed by Bakunawa, though he always manages to escape.

Contradictions

Some stories depict him as a passive victim, while others show him as an active fighter.

Illumination

Source Version General Philippine mythology

Description

Bulan can cast a silvery light that illuminates the night, guiding travelers and lovers. He can also create shadows and darkness when he chooses.

Limitations

His light is weaker than the sun's, and he is absent during the day.

Contradictions

No major contradictions documented.

Shape-shifting (limited)

Source Version Visayan folklore

Description

In some stories, Bulan can take human form to interact with mortals, often appearing as a handsome young man.

Limitations

He may lose his powers while in human form, and he must return to the sky before dawn.

Contradictions

Not all accounts mention this ability.

Archive Dossier — Relationships

Bakunawa

Type enemySource Visayan mythology

Description

Bakunawa is a massive serpent that attempts to swallow the moon. Bulan is his primary adversary, and they engage in eternal battle.

Version Note

In some versions, Bakunawa is not an enemy but a separate cosmic force that the gods must appease.

Conflicting Accounts

Some accounts suggest that Bakunawa is a servant of a higher deity, while others depict him as an independent malevolent being.

Arao

Type spouseSource Visayan mythology (Loarca)

Description

Arao is the sun goddess and the wife of Bulan in Visayan mythology. They rule the day and night respectively.

Version Note

In Tagalog mythology, the sun god is male and not married to a lunar deity.

Conflicting Accounts

Some sources describe Arao as male, and others describe Bulan and Arao as siblings rather than spouses.

Mayari

Type possibly same deity or siblingSource Tagalog mythology

Description

Mayari is a lunar deity in Tagalog mythology, often depicted as a beautiful goddess. The relationship between Mayari and Bulan is unclear; they may be the same deity under different names, or they may be siblings.

Version Note

In some accounts, Mayari is the daughter of Bathala, while Bulan is not mentioned. In others, they are both children of Bathala.

Conflicting Accounts

There is no consensus on whether they are the same or separate entities. Some scholars suggest that Mayari is a later syncretic deity influenced by Hispanic culture.

Gugurang

Type ally or superiorSource Bicolano mythology

Description

Gugurang is a supreme deity in Bicolano mythology. In some accounts, Bulan is a subordinate deity under Gugurang's authority. They may work together to maintain cosmic order.

Version Note

Gugurang is not commonly associated with Bulan in other regions.

Conflicting Accounts

The exact relationship is not well-documented; some sources do not mention any connection between the two.

Bathala

Type creator deity (possible father)Source Tagalog mythology

Description

Bathala is the supreme god in Tagalog mythology. In some accounts, he is the father of Mayari and possibly Bulan. He is the ruler of the heavens.

Version Note

Bathala is not mentioned in Visayan myths about Bulan.

Conflicting Accounts

Some sources do not list Bulan as a child of Bathala, instead presenting him as an independent deity.
