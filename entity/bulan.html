I am the light that pulls the tides and swells the womb. The sea knows my face, and the farmer plants by my phases.

Who It Is

Bulan is the moon deity in Bicolano mythology, distinct from the Tagalog Mayari. Bulan is described as a beautiful, gentle deity who controls the tides, the menstrual cycles of women, and the rhythm of sea-based agriculture. In the Bicolano epic Ibalon (the oldest surviving full narrative from pre-colonial Philippines), Bulan is the son of the supreme god Gugurang. He is associated with Mount Mayon — the volcano's perfect cone is said to be Bulan's eternal tribute to his lost love (in some versions) or his place of rest. The Bicolano people believed eclipses occurred when a giant sea serpent (Bakunawa) tried to swallow Bulan. During lunar eclipses, they would beat drums and gongs to scare the serpent away. Bulan was also the patron of fishermen, who prayed to him for calm seas and good catches. Offerings to Bulan included white chickens, coconut oil, and silver coins. The name Bulan simply means 'moon' in many Philippine languages, and the deity's gentle, protective nature contrasts with the more martial Mayari of the Tagalogs. In some versions, Bulan is female; in others, male — the moon's gender varies by region.

The Approach

In the moonlit archipelago that would become the Philippines, ancient peoples looked up and saw not a cold, distant rock but a living deity—Bulan, the moon god of the Tagalog and Visayan peoples. Unlike the solitary moon gods of other cultures, Bulan was part of a celestial family, a being of light and shadow whose story intertwines with the great serpent Bakunawa, the sun goddess Arao, and the lunar deity Mayari. To understand Bulan is to glimpse the rich, complex cosmology of pre-colonial Philippines, where the heavens were a stage for drama, love, and cosmic struggle.

Content advisory. None: No graphic or sensitive content is documented in the available sources. However, readers should note that the mythological narratives involve cosmic battles and the swallowing of the moon, which may be intense for very young children.

Etymology

The name 'Bulan' is derived from the Proto-Malayo-Polynesian word *bulan, meaning 'moon' or 'month'. It is cognate with other Austronesian terms for the moon, such as 'bulan' in Malay and Indonesian, 'bulan' in Javanese, and 'vulan' in some Philippine languages. The name directly refers to the celestial body and the deity personifying it.

Cultural Ownership

Bulan is a deity from the pre-colonial mythologies of the Philippines, specifically associated with the Tagalog and Visayan peoples. It is part of the broader Austronesian mythological tradition. The deity is not exclusive to one ethnic group; variations exist across the archipelago. The name and concept are indigenous, though some colonial-era accounts were recorded by Spanish missionaries and chroniclers.

Disputed Identifications

There is some scholarly debate regarding the gender and attributes of Bulan. Some sources describe Bulan as a male deity, while others, particularly in Tagalog mythology, associate the moon with the goddess Mayari. The relationship between Bulan and Mayari is not entirely clear; they may be separate deities or aspects of the same lunar divinity. Additionally, some accounts conflate Bulan with the sun god Arao, but they are generally distinct. The exact nature of Bulan's role in the pantheon is disputed due to the scarcity and conflicting nature of primary sources.

Description

Appearance

Bulan is typically described as a handsome young man, radiant and glowing with a pale light. In some accounts, he is depicted as a muscular warrior, while in others he is more effeminate. His skin is said to be fair, and he often wears a headdress or crown resembling a crescent moon. He carries a bow and arrow or a sword, symbolizing his role as a hunter and warrior. In art, he is sometimes shown riding a carriage pulled by horses or in a boat, representing the moon's journey across the sky.

Symbols & Iconography

The crescent moon is the primary symbol of Bulan. He is associated with the color silver, white, and pale gold. Common iconographic elements include the moon disc, a bow and arrow (used to shoot stars or to fight Bakunawa), and a shield. In some depictions, he holds a torch or a lantern, symbolizing his role as a light-bringer in the night. His symbols are often used in traditional tattoos and amulets.

Behaviour & Personality

Bulan is portrayed as a brave and valiant deity, often engaged in cosmic battles against the serpent Bakunawa. He is also seen as a protector of the night, guiding travelers and lovers. In some stories, he is a playful and mischievous figure, sometimes causing eclipses by hiding his face. He is generally benevolent, but can be wrathful if offended. He is associated with the tides, and his moods are reflected in the phases of the moon.

Domains & Habitats

Bulan resides in the sky, specifically in the moon itself. He is believed to dwell in a palace made of silver and light, from where he watches over the earth at night. He is also associated with the sea, as he controls the tides. His domain includes the night sky, the moon, and the oceans. In some myths, he descends to earth to interact with humans, particularly during festivals or when he takes a human lover.

Version Differences

There are notable differences between Tagalog and Visayan versions. In Visayan mythology, Bulan is a male deity and the husband of the sun goddess Arao. In some Tagalog accounts, the moon is female and identified with Mayari, who is sometimes considered the daughter of Bathala. Other versions, particularly from the Bicol region, involve Bulan as a deity who battles the serpent Bakunawa. The relationship between Bulan and Mayari is ambiguous: they may be separate deities, with Mayari being a lunar goddess and Bulan a lunar god, or they may be the same deity with different names. Some modern retellings blend these versions, creating a more unified lunar deity.

Stories & Myths

The Battle with Bakunawa

Source Visayan mythology, recorded in various ethnographic sourcesCulture VisayanPeriod Pre-colonial

Summary

Bakunawa, a gigantic serpent, attempts to swallow the moon. Bulan, the moon god, battles the serpent to prevent the eclipse. In some versions, Bulan is aided by the sun god Arao and other deities. The battle is cyclical, recurring each time Bakunawa tries to devour the moon. Eventually, Bulan and the other gods manage to drive Bakunawa away, but the serpent returns periodically, causing eclipses.

Participants

Bulan

Bakunawa

Arao (in some versions)

Consequences

The story explains the occurrence of lunar eclipses. It also establishes Bulan as a protector of the celestial order and a heroic figure.

Variants

In some versions, the battle is not physical but rather a chase, with Bulan fleeing from Bakunawa. In others, the gods use loud noises or fire to scare the serpent away.

The Love Story of Bulan and Arao

Source Visayan mythology, as recorded by Miguel de LoarcaCulture VisayanPeriod Pre-colonial

Summary

Bulan, the moon god, is married to Arao, the sun goddess. They take turns crossing the sky, with Arao ruling the day and Bulan the night. Their love is tested by their separation, and they communicate through the twilight. Their union is believed to bring balance to the world.

Participants

Bulan

Arao

Consequences

The story explains the alternation of day and night and the existence of dawn and dusk as moments of their meeting.

Variants

Some versions do not mention a romantic relationship, instead describing them as siblings or rivals.

Bulan and the Mortal Woman

Source Visayan folkloreCulture VisayanPeriod Pre-colonial

Summary

Bulan descends to earth in human form and falls in love with a mortal woman. He courts her under the cover of night, and she bears him a child. However, their love is doomed because Bulan must return to the sky. The woman pines away, and their child becomes a hero.

Participants

The story explains the origin of certain heroic lineages and the connection between the divine and mortal realms.

Variants

The identity of the woman and the child varies by region. In some versions, the child is a legendary warrior or a founder of a village.

The Creation of the Stars

Source Tagalog and Visayan mythologyCulture Tagalog and VisayanPeriod Pre-colonial

Summary

Bulan, while wandering the sky, drops pieces of his light, which become the stars. In some versions, he scatters silver dust to create the Milky Way. This story explains the origin of the stars and the Milky Way.

Participants

Bulan

Consequences

The stars are seen as remnants of Bulan's light, and they guide travelers at night.

Variants

Some versions attribute the creation of stars to other deities, such as Mayari or Bathala.

Dark History
Content advisory. The mythology includes themes of cosmic violence (battles with a serpent), and the swallowing of the moon during eclipses, which may be frightening to children. There are no documented accounts of human sacrifice or sexual violence associated with Bulan.

Cosmic Violence

Source Visayan mythology

Description

The myth of Bulan and Bakunawa involves violent battles in the sky, with the serpent attempting to devour the moon. This is a central theme and may reflect ancient fears of eclipses.

Scholarly Context

Eclipses were often interpreted as dangerous omens, and rituals were performed to drive away the serpent. The violence is mythological rather than historical.

Contradictions & Disputes

Gender of Bulan

Position A

Bulan is male (Visayan and Bicolano traditions)

Position A — evidence

Spanish colonial accounts such as Loarca's Relacion describe Bulan as a male deity; modern ethnographic studies of Visayan mythology also depict him as male.

Position B

Bulan is female (some Tagalog traditions, often identified with Mayari)

Position B — evidence

In some Tagalog myths, the moon is personified as a goddess, and the name 'Bulan' is used interchangeably with 'Mayari' in certain sources.

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus. The majority of sources identify Bulan as male, but the ambiguity remains due to regional variations and the syncretism with Mayari.

Relationship between Bulan and Mayari

Position A

Bulan and Mayari are the same deity

Position A — evidence

Both are lunar deities, and some sources use the names interchangeably. The root word 'bulan' means moon, so 'Bulan' may simply be a generic term for a moon deity, with Mayari being a specific name.

Position B

Bulan and Mayari are separate deities

Position B — evidence

In some myths, Mayari is described as a daughter of Bathala, while Bulan is not. They also have different attributes: Mayari is often a goddess of beauty and strength, while Bulan is a warrior god.

Scholarly Consensus

Unresolved. Scholars note that the pre-colonial pantheon was not standardized, and the relationship likely varied by region and time.

Bulan's role in eclipses

Position A

Eclipses are caused by Bakunawa swallowing the moon

Position A — evidence

The most widespread myth in the Visayas and Bicol region attributes eclipses to Bakunawa's attacks.

Position B

Eclipses are caused by Bulan hiding his face

Position B — evidence

Some Tagalog myths suggest that the moon deity voluntarily hides her face during eclipses, as a sign of mourning or anger.

Scholarly Consensus

The Bakunawa myth is more prevalent and better documented. The alternative explanation is a minority view.

Bulan's parentage

Position A

Bulan is a child of Bathala

Position A — evidence

In some Tagalog genealogies, Bathala is the supreme god and father of several deities, including Mayari. Some modern retellings extend this to include Bulan.

Position B

Bulan is an independent deity with no known parentage

Position B — evidence

In Visayan myths, Bulan is not associated with Bathala, who is a Tagalog deity. Visayan cosmology has different supreme deities.

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus. The pantheons of different ethnic groups in the Philippines were distinct, and syncretism occurred only later.

Primary Evidence

Boxer Codex (c. 1590), a Spanish manuscript containing early accounts of Philippine cultures

Type textDate c. 1590Locator Section on the Tagalogs and VisayansLanguage Spanish and Tagalog

Translation

The codex mentions the worship of the moon by the natives, referring to it as 'Bulan' or 'Buan' in some transcriptions. It describes the natives' belief that the moon was a deity who controlled the tides and the night.

Relacion de las Islas Filipinas (1582) by Miguel de Loarca

Type textDate 1582Locator Chapter on the beliefs of the VisayansLanguage Spanish

Translation

Loarca records that the Visayans worshipped the moon as a male deity named Bulan, who was considered the husband of the sun goddess Arao. They believed that the eclipse was caused by the serpent Bakunawa swallowing the moon.

Sucesos de las Islas Filipinas (1609) by Antonio de Morga

Type textDate 1609Locator Book 1, Chapter 8Language Spanish

Translation

Morga mentions the natives' reverence for the moon and the sun, and notes that they had idols representing these celestial bodies. He does not provide extensive detail about Bulan specifically.

Ethnographic studies by F. Landa Jocano, particularly 'Outline of Philippine Mythology' (1969)

Type textDate 1969Locator Section on Visayan mythologyLanguage English

Translation

Jocano synthesizes earlier accounts and presents Bulan as a moon deity in the Visayan pantheon, often depicted as a handsome young man who battles Bakunawa.
