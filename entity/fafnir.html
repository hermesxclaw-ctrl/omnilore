Who It Is

Fafnir is one of the most famous dragons in Germanic heroic legend, originating in the Norse Völsunga saga but central to the broader Germanic tradition that includes the Nibelungenlied. Originally Fafnir was a dwarf prince, the son of Hreidmar, gifted with a powerful arm and a fearless spirit. When the god Loki killed Hreidmar and paid a blood-price of gold that included the cursed ring Andvaranaut, Fafnir was overcome by greed. He murdered his own father to claim the treasure, transformed into a monstrous dragon (often described as a great wingless serpent or Lindwurm), and fled to Gnitaheid where he brooded over the gold for centuries. Fafnir's transformation was both physical and spiritual: he became cold-hearted, venomous, and utterly consumed by avarice. His blood was venomous, and his mere presence blighted the land around his lair. The hero Sigurd (Germanic Siegfried), tutored by the dwarf Regin (Fafnir's surviving brother), dug a pit in Fafnir's path and stabbed the dragon from below as it crawled over him to drink. As Fafnir died, he spoke with the dying dragon's wisdom, warning Sigurd of Regin's treachery and revealing the nature of the gold's curse. Sigurd then tasted Fafnir's blood, gaining the ability to understand the language of birds, who warned him that Regin intended to kill him. Fafnir's treasure — the Rhinemaidens' gold — becomes the source of the enduring Curse of the Nibelungs, driving the tragedy of the Nibelungenlied and Wagner's Ring Cycle.

Form

Fáfnir is described in the Völsunga saga as a great serpent or dragon, with a venomous breath that poisons the land around him. The Norse sources do not specify whether he had wings, legs, or scales — the description is minimal. Medieval Scandinavian iconography (such as the Ramsund carving) depicts him as a long, serpentine creature, often shown coiled around his hoard. Some later German versions describe him as having a serpent's body. He is large enough that Sigurd digs a pit in his path and stabs him from below as he crawls over.

Powers

Dragon form Fáfnir is transformed into a serpent/dragon as a result of his greed and the curse of the gold. His dragon form is physically powerful and terrifying, capable of causing great destruction. Norse (Völsunga saga, Poetic Edda) The transformation is a curse, not a blessing — it isolates him and makes him vulnerable to a determined hero. The exact nature of his dragon form (wings, legs, etc.) is not specified in Norse sources; later artistic depictions vary widely.

Venomous breath Fáfnir's breath is venomous, poisoning the land around him. This is a common attribute of dragons in Germanic mythology. Norse (Völsunga saga) His venom is dangerous but he cannot use it to prevent his own death. None recorded.

Shape-shifting (implied) His transformation from human to dragon implies a form of shape-shifting, though it is a one-way transformation. He does not appear to be able to revert to human form. Norse (Völsunga saga) Irreversible; he remains a dragon until his death. None recorded.

Cursed knowledge As a dying dragon, Fáfnir speaks with prophetic and esoteric knowledge, revealing information about the gods, fate, and the curse on the gold. This suggests that dragons in Norse myth possess deep, hidden knowledge. Norse (Fáfnismál) This knowledge is only revealed in his death throes. None recorded.

Invulnerability (Germanic variant) In the Nibelungenlied, the dragon's blood grants Siegfried invulnerability (except for a spot on his back), implying the dragon itself may have had similar properties, though this is not stated directly. Middle High German (Nibelungenlied) This is an attribute of the dragon's blood, not explicitly of the dragon itself. This is a variant; the Norse sources do not mention invulnerability from Fáfnir's blood.

Behavior & Habitat

Fáfnir is characterized by extreme greed, paranoia, and isolation. He guards his hoard obsessively, refusing to share it even with his brother Regin. He is described as 'watching' the gold and as being unwilling to let anyone near it. When dying, he speaks with surprising wisdom and cynicism — warning Sigurd that the gold will bring doom, and revealing knowledge about the gods and fate. He is not simply a mindless beast; he retains his human intelligence and memory, which makes his transformation more tragic.

Accounts

Fáfnir and his brother Regin kill their father Hreiðmarr to claim the gold that the gods Odin, Loki, and Hœnir had paid as wergild for the murder of Hreiðmarr's son Ótr. Loki had cursed the gold (specifically the ring Andvaranaut) so that it would bring doom to its possessor. Fáfnir, driven by greed, refuses to share the gold with Regin and transforms into a dragon to guard it alone.

Regin forges a mighty sword (Gram) for his foster-son Sigurd. Sigurd digs a pit on Fáfnir's path and, as the dragon crawls over, stabs him from below. As Fáfnir dies, he and Sigurd converse. Fáfnir warns Sigurd of the curse on the gold and the treachery of Regin, and shares his wisdom about fate and the gods. Sigurd then cuts out Fáfnir's heart and roasts it. When he tastes the blood, he gains the ability to understand the speech of birds, who warn him that Regin plans to kill him. Sigurd kills Regin and takes the gold.

In his dying dialogue, Fáfnir reveals that he was once a human and that his transformation was a result of his greed and the curse of the gold. He says he became a 'serpent' (ormr) and that he was driven to guard the hoard.

Field Notes

☘ The Ramsund carving in Sweden is one of the earliest visual depictions of the Fáfnir story, showing the sequence of events in a single image.

☘ The name 'Fáfnir' is thought to mean 'the embracing one', fitting for a dragon that coils around its treasure.

☘ In Wagner's opera, Fafner is a giant, not a human, and his transformation into a dragon is a deal made with the gods, not a curse from greed.

☘ The story of Fáfnir heavily influenced J.R.R. Tolkien's portrayal of Smaug in 'The Hobbit' — both dragons are intelligent, greedy, and speak with their would-be slayers.

☘ The concept of a dragon's hoard, popularized by Fáfnir, has become a standard trope in fantasy games like Dungeons & Dragons.

Scholarly Debates
Fáfnir's nature (human vs. giant)
In the Norse sources (Völsunga saga, Poetic Edda), Fáfnir is a human (son of Hreiðmarr) who transforms into a dragon. ⇌ In Wagner's Der Ring des Nibelungen, Fafner is a giant (one of the giants who built Valhalla) who transforms into a dragon.
Evidence for A: Völsunga saga, Reginsmál, Fáfnismál.
Evidence for B: Wagner's libretto for Das Rheingold and Siegfried.
The dragon's form (wings, legs, etc.)
Some artistic depictions show Fáfnir as a wingless, legless serpent (lindworm-like). ⇌ Others depict him as a more traditional dragon with legs and wings.
Evidence for A: The Ramsund carving and other medieval Scandinavian depictions show a serpentine form.
Evidence for B: Later European art and Wagnerian productions often depict a more conventional dragon.
The treasure's origin
In the Norse sources, the gold is the wergild paid by the gods for the killing of Ótr, cursed by Loki. ⇌ In the Nibelungenlied, the treasure belongs to the Nibelung brothers, and its origin is not connected to the gods.
Evidence for A: Völsunga saga, Reginsmál.
Evidence for B: Nibelungenlied.
Sigurd's invulnerability
In the Norse sources, Sigurd does not become invulnerable from Fáfnir's blood. ⇌ In the Nibelungenlied, Siegfried bathes in the dragon's blood and becomes invulnerable, except for a spot on his back where a leaf fell.
Evidence for A: Völsunga saga, Fáfnismál.
Evidence for B: Nibelungenlied.
What We Don't Know

The exact location of Gnitaheiðr is debated. The precise nature of Fáfnir's dragon form (wings, legs, scales) is not described in the sources. The relationship between the Norse Fáfnir and the unnamed dragon in the Nibelungenlied is a matter of scholarly discussion. The reception of Fáfnir in non-Western cultures (e.g., Japanese anime, Korean manhwa) has not been exhaustively catalogued. The role of Fáfnir in modern neopagan and Norse revival movements has not been thoroughly investigated.

A way to hold it in your mind

Fáfnir is the original 'dragon with a hoard' — the template for every greedy, intelligent dragon in fantasy. But he is also a cautionary tale: the dragon is not a separate species, but a human who let greed consume him. He is what happens when you value gold more than your own humanity.
