Who It Is

Before he was a dragon, Fáfnir was a man who killed his own father for a ring. The curse that drove him to transform into a serpent coiled around his gold is the same curse that would eventually doom Sigurd, the hero who slew him. Fáfnir is not just a monster — he is a tragedy, a warning, and a mirror of the greed that lurks in every hero's heart.

Fáfnir is characterized by extreme greed, paranoia, and isolation. He guards his hoard obsessively, refusing to share it even with his brother Regin. He is described as 'watching' the gold and as being unwilling to let anyone near it. When dying, he speaks with surprising wisdom and cynicism — warning Sigurd that the gold will bring doom, and revealing knowledge about the gods and fate. He is not simply a mindless beast; he retains his human intelligence and memory, which makes his transformation more tragic.

Powers
Dragon form

Fáfnir is transformed into a serpent/dragon as a result of his greed and the curse of the gold. His dragon form is physically powerful and terrifying, capable of causing great destruction.

Limit: The transformation is a curse, not a blessing — it isolates him and makes him vulnerable to a determined hero.

Venomous breath

Fáfnir's breath is venomous, poisoning the land around him. This is a common attribute of dragons in Germanic mythology.

Limit: His venom is dangerous but he cannot use it to prevent his own death.

Shape-shifting (implied)

His transformation from human to dragon implies a form of shape-shifting, though it is a one-way transformation. He does not appear to be able to revert to human form.

Limit: Irreversible; he remains a dragon until his death.

Cursed knowledge

As a dying dragon, Fáfnir speaks with prophetic and esoteric knowledge, revealing information about the gods, fate, and the curse on the gold. This suggests that dragons in Norse myth possess deep, hidden knowledge.

Limit: This knowledge is only revealed in his death throes.

Invulnerability (Germanic variant)

In the Nibelungenlied, the dragon's blood grants Siegfried invulnerability (except for a spot on his back), implying the dragon itself may have had similar properties, though this is not stated directly.

Limit: This is an attribute of the dragon's blood, not explicitly of the dragon itself.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Fáfnir is described in the Völsunga saga as a great serpent or dragon, with a venomous breath that poisons the land around him. The Norse sources do not specify whether he had wings, legs, or scales — the description is minimal. Medieval Scandinavian iconography (such as the Ramsund carving) depicts him as a long, serpentine creature, often shown coiled around his hoard. Symbols — Fáfnir symbolizes the destructive power of greed and the moral corruption that comes with hoarding wealth. He is also a symbol of transformation — from human to beast — representing the idea that excessive avarice dehumanizes. His heart, which Sigurd roasts and eats, symbolizes the acquisition of wisdom and the transfer of power. In art, he is often depicted coiled around his hoard, a visual shorthand for the entwinement of greed and death.

Domains — Fáfnir dwells on the Gnitaheiðr (Gnitaheid), a heath or moor, where he guards his treasure hoard. The location is likely in modern-day Germany or Scandinavia, though its exact identification is debated. The heath is described as desolate and dangerous, with a path leading to his lair. He also has a lair or earth-cave where the gold is stored.

Accounts I
The Killing of Hreiðmarr and the Curse of the GoldVölsunga saga, Reginsmál

Heroic age (legendary)

Fáfnir and his brother Regin kill their father Hreiðmarr to claim the gold that the gods Odin, Loki, and Hœnir had paid as wergild for the murder of Hreiðmarr's son Ótr. Loki had cursed the gold (specifically the ring Andvaranaut) so that it would bring doom to its possessor. Fáfnir, driven by greed, refuses to share the gold with Regin and transforms into a dragon to guard it alone. Fáfnir becomes a dragon; Regin seeks revenge and eventually plots to have his foster-son Sigurd kill Fáfnir.

Accounts II
The Slaying of FáfnirVölsunga saga, Fáfnismál, Ramsund carving

Heroic age (legendary)

Regin forges a mighty sword (Gram) for his foster-son Sigurd. Sigurd digs a pit on Fáfnir's path and, as the dragon crawls over, stabs him from below. As Fáfnir dies, he and Sigurd converse. Fáfnir warns Sigurd of the curse on the gold and the treachery of Regin, and shares his wisdom about fate and the gods. Sigurd then cuts out Fáfnir's heart and roasts it. When he tastes the blood, he gains the ability to understand the speech of birds, who warn him that Regin plans to kill him. Sigurd kills Regin and takes the gold. Sigurd gains the gold, the wisdom of the heart, and the ability to understand birds. The curse on the gold continues to bring doom to its new possessor, leading to Sigurd's eventual downfall.

Fáfnir's Transformation (as told by himself)Fáfnismál

Heroic age (legendary)

In his dying dialogue, Fáfnir reveals that he was once a human and that his transformation was a result of his greed and the curse of the gold. He says he became a 'serpent' (ormr) and that he was driven to guard the hoard. This provides the in-world explanation for his monstrous state and reinforces the moral lesson.

Sigurd's invulnerabilityIn the Norse sources, Sigurd does not become invulnerable from Fáfnir's blood.In the Nibelungenlied, Siegfried bathes in the dragon's blood and becomes invulnerable, except for a spot on his back where a leaf fell.

Fafnir is a giant in the original Norse myth. — In the original Norse sources, Fáfnir is a human, the son of Hreiðmarr, who transforms into a dragon due to his greed. The giant version is Wagner's invention.

Fafnir has wings. — The Norse sources do not specify whether Fáfnir has wings. The medieval depictions show him as a serpent. The winged dragon is a later artistic interpretation.

Fafnir's blood makes Sigurd invulnerable. — This is a detail from the Nibelungenlied, not the Norse sources. In the Norse version, Sigurd does not become invulnerable.

Fafnir is just a mindless beast. — Fáfnir is intelligent and articulate. He engages in a philosophical dialogue with Sigurd while dying, revealing wisdom about fate and the gods.

Fafnir is the same as the lindworm. — Fáfnir is a specific named dragon in the Völsung cycle. A 'lindworm' is a general type of serpent-dragon in Scandinavian folklore. They are related but not identical.
