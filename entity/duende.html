Who It Is

The Duende is one of the richest and most ambiguous figures in Spanish folklore — simultaneously a household goblin, a nature spirit, and the ineffable force of artistic inspiration. In domestic folklore, duendes are small, troublesome imps who hide household objects, make noises at night, and torment unkind residents. But the deeper concept of 'tener duende' (to have duende) has been elevated to a philosophy of art: the dark, chthonic, irrational force that possesses an artist in moments of transcendent performance. Federico García Lorca, in his famous 1933 lecture 'Theory and Play of the Duende,' distinguished duende from the angel (grace, light, inspiration from above) and the muse (intellect, classical form) — duende comes from below, from the blood, from the knowledge of death. It is what makes a flamenco singer's voice break with impossible emotion, a dancer's body tremble with truth, a matador's cape work become poetry. Duende is pain transmuted into art, the wound that sings. Lorca traced duende through Spanish cultural history — from the caves of Cádiz to the canvases of Goya's black paintings to the poetry of Quevedo. The domestic duende and the artistic duende are connected: both represent an untamable, irrational, and fundamentally Spanish confrontation with the dark forces beneath civilized surfaces. The concept has influenced artists worldwide and remains central to flamenco philosophy.
