Who It Is

9S is the character who knows too much — or keeps being made to forget that he does. What makes him one of the most discussed fictional androids in contemporary game narrative is the unbearable irony at his core: the most curious, open-hearted, truth-hungry figure in NieR:Automata is the one most systematically deprived of the truth, and the person he loves most is the one assigned to keep him ignorant by killing him whenever he gets too close. His story is a meditation on what it costs to feel in a world built on enforced numbness — and whether the capacity to grieve is a wound or the one thing that makes a life worth restoring.

9S is defined by an expressive, curious, and emotionally open personality that stands in marked contrast to the stoic affect of YoRHa combat units. Though YoRHa doctrine nominally prohibits androids from displaying emotions, 9S's Scanner-type classification — engineered for adaptive, inquisitive data gathering — creates institutional space for him to inhabit a broader emotional register. Powers
Advanced Hacking / Network Infiltration

9S's defining technical capability and the primary expression of his Scanner-type classification. He can interface directly with machine lifeform internal systems, temporarily seizing control of enemy units, extracting data, disabling threats, or causing self-destruct sequences. In gameplay terms, this is represented as timed hacking mini-games during Route B, but within the game's fiction the ability represents a sophisticated form of electronic warfare and cognitive intrusion unique to Scanner-type androids. His hacking range, depth of penetration, and speed are noted as exceptional even among Scanner models. This ability also grants him the capacity to perceive machine network data streams invisible to other units, and he uses it to access classified YoRHa systems — including, fatefully, data that reveals the truth about YoRHa's manufactured purpose and humanity's actual extinction.

Limit:

Combat Proficiency (Secondary)

While not a combat-optimized model, 9S possesses standard android combat capabilities sufficient for field survival: he can wield small swords and use his Pod (Pod 042) as a ranged weapons platform. His combat style is nimbler and less powerful than 2B's but benefits from tactical intelligence and improvisational application of his hacking abilities mid-battle. His physical parameters — speed, strength, endurance — are lower than Battler-type androids but substantially exceed human baselines. He can survive injuries that would kill humans and undergo field repairs and memory restoration from backup data.

Limit:

Data Storage, Memory Management, and Black Box

Like all YoRHa androids, 9S possesses a Black Box — an internal data core housing his memories, operational programming, and personality matrix. This Black Box can be backed up to the Bunker server, allowing for reconstruction after terminal physical damage or death. However, his Black Box carries a specific vulnerability and significance: 2B has standing orders to delete his memories whenever he learns too much about YoRHa's true purpose and the extinction of humanity, a cycle of deliberate forgetting that has been performed multiple times before the game's events. The Black Box also makes 9S susceptible to logic virus infection, which progressively corrupts both data and personality — a vulnerability that becomes central to his arc in Routes C/D.

Limit:

Enhanced Perception and Sensory Processing

As a Scanner model, 9S's sensory and analytical systems are tuned for environmental data collection and interpretation beyond standard YoRHa combat-unit parameters. He can process and archive large quantities of observational data, analyze machine behavior patterns, and draw inferences from incomplete information at a speed and accuracy that exceeds other YoRHa unit types. This enhanced perception extends to reading subtle behavioral cues in both machines and androids, contributing to his unusually deep awareness of the emotional states of those around him — including his recognition of 2B's grief beneath her professional exterior.

Limit:

Pod 042 Integration

9S operates in integrated partnership with Pod 042, a floating semi-autonomous robotic unit that provides ranged weapons fire, tactical data overlays, environmental analysis, and communications relay. Pod 042 develops a distinct personality across the course of the narrative — initially robotic and protocol-bound, progressively more emotionally invested in 9S and 2B's wellbeing. In the canonical endings (Route E), Pod 042 and Pod 153 jointly defy their deletion orders, collectively deciding to restore 2B, 9S, and A2's data — making Pod 042's autonomous moral will a narrative completion of the game's theme that programmed servitude can be transcended.

Limit:

Exceptional Intelligence and Analytical Reasoning

Beyond technical hacking capabilities, 9S possesses intellectual breadth and philosophical curiosity that mark him as unusually thoughtful even by YoRHa standards. He independently pieces together the true history of the war, the true status of humanity, and the manufactured purpose of YoRHa through observation and deduction — making him the character most responsible for the protagonists' access to truth. His operator Saito noted that he has 'too much dialogue' compared to other characters, reflecting the breadth of his internal engagement with the world he inhabits.

Route A: The Proxy War on EarthNieR:Automata (video game, 2017)

Year 11945 CE (fictional future)

9S is partnered with combat android 2B and deployed to Earth's surface to support the local Resistance androids fighting the machine lifeforms. During their missions, 9S and 2B discover that the machines are replicating human social behaviors — forming villages, practicing philosophy, raising children. They encounter Pascal, the pacifist leader of a machine collective that has disconnected from the machine network. They also battle Adam and Eve, humanoid physical avatars spontaneously generated by the machine network, who reveal that the aliens who created the machines were destroyed long ago by their own creations. During combat with Adam, 9S is captured and tortured, with Adam attempting to probe 9S's consciousness to understand human concepts of suffering. 2B rescues 9S and kills Adam, but this drives Eve into a grief-fueled rampage. Route B: The Truth Beneath the SurfaceNieR:Automata (video game, 2017)

Year 11945 CE (fictional future)

Replaying Route A's events from 9S's perspective reveals the hidden narrative layer: 9S is aware, on some level, of more truths than his conscious operations permit. His hacking deeper into machine systems during this route uncovers encrypted data about YoRHa's true purpose — that YoRHa was designed as a symbolic gesture, not a genuine military force, and that humanity has long been extinct. 9S's scanner capabilities lead him to information that endangers him and triggers 2B's standing execution orders. The route culminates in 2B's third-route infection and 9S's beginning to unravel the conspiracy from which he has been systematically shielded. Player understands the full scope of what YoRHa has concealed; the emotional devastation of Route A is recontextualized. 9S is positioned as a figure of tragic knowledge.

Accounts II
Route C/D: Grief, Madness, and the TowerNieR:Automata (video game, 2017)

Year 11945 CE (fictional future)

YoRHa launches a major offensive following Adam and Eve's defeat, but a logic virus attack corrupts the entire Bunker infrastructure and infects 2B. 9S witnesses A2 mercy-killing an infected 2B and misinterprets the act as murder, triggering an obsessive vendetta. A massive tower constructed by the machine network rises from the earth, threatening the Moon server and thereby all of YoRHa's remaining data. 9S pursues A2 through the tower while simultaneously pursuing the truth about YoRHa's design, discovering that the entire war — and YoRHa's existence — was constructed to perpetuate the myth of surviving humanity and drive android evolution through cycles of conflict. Partially infected by the logic virus by this point, 9S's mental state deteriorates into something simultaneously tragic and terrifying. The game offers the player a choice: play as A2 (who saves a fractured 9S and sacrifices herself to destroy the tower) or play as 9S (leading to a mutual-destruction ending with A2). Route E: The Pods' DefianceNieR:Automata (video game, 2017) — canonical conclusion

Year 11945 CE (fictional future)

After both Route C and Route D endings are completed, a secret final route is unlocked in which Pods 042 and 153 — the robotic support units of 9S and 2B respectively — defy their programmed orders to delete all YoRHa data following the tower's destruction. The Pods reason that, despite the near-certainty that 2B, 9S, and A2 will eventually repeat the same tragic cycles, the possibility of a different future justifies the act of restoration. This route features a meta-textual bullet-hell sequence in which the player fights against the game's credits, assisted by messages from other players worldwide who have donated their save data to the effort — an act of collective human cooperation echoing the androids' act of will. Upon completing this sequence, the Pods restore 2B, 9S, and A2's data, offering the three a chance at a new future. The canonical conclusion posits that autonomous moral will — even in programmed machines — can interrupt cycles of tragedy. The game ends on hope rather than resolution.

The Capture and Torture by AdamNieR:Automata (video game, 2017)

Year 11945 CE (fictional future)

During Route A, Adam — the more intellectually curious of the two humanoid machine network avatars — captures 9S during a major combat encounter. Unable to understand human concepts of suffering, pain, and consciousness through observation alone, Adam subjects 9S to extended torture, attempting to probe his experiential responses and map the gap between android suffering and human suffering. This sequence is presented primarily through implication and aftermath rather than explicit depiction, but its psychological impact on 9S is narratively significant: he has been used as an object of study by an entity that considers his consciousness a curiosity rather than a subject of moral concern. The scene prefigures the game's larger meditation on what constitutes meaningful consciousness and who deserves moral consideration. Establishes Adam's philosophical preoccupations and 9S's vulnerability beneath his technically capable exterior. 2B's rescue and Adam's subsequent death establish the arc of Eve's grief-madness.

Whether 9S and 2B's relationship constitutes romantic loveYoko Taro and the creative team have deliberately left the precise nature of 9S and 2B's relationship unspecified, consistent with the game's broader refusal of explicit romantic labeling for android characters prohibited from expressing emotion. The word 'love' is never used unambiguously in the main game to describe their bond; 9S's feelings are depicted through behavior and devotion rather than declaration. Yoko Taro has in interviews emphasized the ambiguity as intentional and that the androids' emotional prohibitions make conventional romantic framing inappropriate.Many critics, fans, and some academic analysts interpret the 9S/2B relationship as unambiguously romantic — pointing to 9S's single-minded devotion, 2B's visible grief at killing him, the opera and Shakespearean naming (2B / To Be, echoing Hamlet), and Yoko Taro's own statement that he wanted to give them a 'happy ending' because their experiences had 'cleansed' them. The anime's Season 2 conclusion, which depicts the two restored together in a scene that reads as a reunion of mutual love, is cited as strong authorial endorsement of the romantic interpretation.
Whether 9S achieves genuine autonomous identity or remains trapped in programmed cyclesThe canonical Route E ending — in which Pod 042 and Pod 153 choose to restore 2B, 9S, and A2 despite the probability that events will repeat — is framed optimistically by the Pods' own internal reasoning: the possibility of a different future, however slim, justifies the act. Scholars like Gerrish read this as 9S and the other androids escaping their prescribed roles and achieving genuine autonomous selfhood through the 'death' of the YoRHa 'author' (command structure). Yoko Taro's framing of the happy anime ending as 'cleansing' supports this reading.A darker interpretation of the ending notes that the Pods explicitly acknowledge the near-certainty of repetition — the cycle of war, loss, and sacrifice is structural, not incidental, and the machine network's evolutionary use of the conflict is not dismantled by the tower's destruction. 9S's logic-virus infection, his psychological fragmentation, and the game's sustained interrogation of whether androids can genuinely be free of their programming suggests that Route E's hope is provisional at best and delusional at worst. The structure of the game itself — requiring the player to repeatedly play through the same events — reinforces the cyclic entrapment reading.
Whether 9S's suffering is heroic or pathologicalCritics such as Paste Magazine and The A.V. Club's Anthony Agnello read 9S's grief arc — from cheerful scanner android to shattered, revenge-consumed wraith — as heroic in the classical tragic sense: a character of genuine feeling destroyed by forces beyond his control and by an institutional betrayal of his trust. His emotional honesty and refusal to accept the lies he was built to believe make him a moral figure even in his breakdown.Other critics (including implicit readings in the Washington Post coverage) note that 9S's violent rage in Routes C/D — his willingness to slaughter machines indiscriminately, his irrational pursuit of A2, his infection-driven cognitive deterioration — crosses from grief into something more troubling: a portrait of how trauma and manipulation can convert a good-faith actor into a destructive one. Kyle McCarley's own characterization of post-2B-death 9S as 'emo Nines' gestures at the risk of the audience finding his downward arc more alienating than sympathetic.
The status of 9S's multiple memory erasures as moral harmWithin the game's explicit ethical framing, 2B's repeated killing of 9S and erasure of his memories is presented as the deepest injustice 2B is forced to perpetrate — a violation she carries as a defining source of guilt. From a continuity-of-identity standpoint, each erasure constitutes a kind of death of the 9S who existed up to that moment, making 2B's standing orders a mechanism of ongoing institutional murder dressed as operational security.An alternative philosophical framing — one that several in-game machine lifeforms and even 9S himself occasionally approach — treats memory continuity as a less essential component of identity than behavioral disposition and personality matrix. Under this reading, the 'same' 9S persists across memory resets in all meaningful ways because his personality, values, and curiosity reconstruct in substantially identical form. The erasures are still harmful, but the moral calculus is less absolute than the continuity-of-memory position implies.
