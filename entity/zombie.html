Who It Is

The zombie is a creature of two births: one born of Haitian soil, where the dead are said to rise and toil in the cane fields, and another born of American cinema, where the dead rise to consume the living. Both are revenants, but they share little else—one is a slave, the other a predator; one is made by magic, the other by plague. To understand the zombie is to trace a single word across centuries and continents, from the plantations of Saint-Domingue to the screens of the modern world, where it has become a mirror for our deepest fears of death, contagion, and the collapse of civilization.

Haitian zombies are typically portrayed as mindless slaves, without free will, forced to work in the fields or perform other tasks for their master. They are not aggressive unless commanded. In modern media, zombies are driven by an insatiable hunger for human flesh or brains. They are usually depicted as lacking intelligence, but some variations retain basic motor skills or even cunning. They are often shown as contagious, spreading their condition through bites or scratches. Name & Tongue

Etymology — The English word 'zombie' is first recorded in the 18th century. It is traced to Bantu languages, particularly Kimbundu 'nzumbi' meaning 'ghost' or 'spirit'. The Haitian Creole 'zonbi' and Haitian French 'zombi' are direct predecessors. Some scholars also note possible connections to Kikongo 'zumbi'

Pronunciation — /ˈzɒmbi/ (zom-bee) in English; Haitian Creole: [zɔ̃bi]

Aliases — Zombi (Haitian French), Zonbi (Haitian Creole), Nzumbi (Kimbundu), The Walking Dead, Revenant, Undead

Powers
Reanimation

The ability to be brought back from the dead, typically through magic (Haitian) or viral infection (modern).

Limit: Requires an external agent (bokor, virus, radiation, etc.). Not autonomous.

Contagion

The ability to turn living humans into zombies through bites, scratches, or airborne transmission.

Limit: Not present in Haitian folklore. Transmission method varies.

Superhuman strength and endurance

Zombies are often depicted as stronger and more resilient than living humans, feeling no pain and requiring destruction of the brain to be stopped.

Limit: Not present in Haitian folklore. Even in modern media, some zombies are weak and easily dispatched.

Mind control (Haitian)

The bokor controls the zombie's actions, making it a mindless slave.

Limit: The zombie has no free will. The control is maintained through magic.

Immortality (relative)

Zombies do not age or die from natural causes, but can be destroyed by physical trauma, especially to the brain.

Limit: Not absolute; they can be killed.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Haitian folkloric zombies are typically described as having a blank, vacant stare, a shuffling gait, and a lack of will or emotion. They are often said to be pale, with a deathly complexion, and may show signs of physical decay. In modern media, zombies are usually depicted as decaying corpses with wounds, missing limbs, and a hunger for human flesh. They often have gray or green skin, sunken eyes, and tattered clothing. Their movement is typical

Symbols — In Haitian Vodou, the zombie is associated with the symbol of the cross (used in the creation ritual), the vèvè (ritual drawings), and the bokor's paraphernalia. In modern culture, the zombie is often depicted with a raised arm, a staggering walk, and a groaning sound. Iconography often includes blood, gore, and apocalyptic settings. The zombie has become a symbol of mindless consumerism, social conformity, and the fear of pandemics.

Domains — Haitian zombies are found in rural Haiti, often in sugar plantations or other agricultural settings. They are tied to the Vodou religion and the societal fears of enslavement and death. Modern zombies are ubiquitous in urban, suburban, and rural environments, often in post-apocalyptic settings. They are found in horror films, video games, and literature, and have become a staple of popular culture.

Accounts I
The Haitian Zombie: Dead Men Working in the Cane FieldsW.B. Seabrook, The Magic Island (1929)

20th century (reported)

Seabrook recounts a story of a man who encounters zombies working on a Haitian sugar plantation. They are described as dead bodies reanimated by a bokor, forced to labor without rest or will. The account is presented as a true paranormal experience. Introduced the concept of the voodoo zombie to Western culture, shaping popular perception.

Night of the Living DeadGeorge A. Romero (1968)

1968

A group of people barricade themselves in a farmhouse as the dead rise and attack the living, driven by an unexplained cause. The film is notable for its social commentary and its depiction of zombies as flesh-eating monsters. Established the modern archetype of the zombie as a flesh-eating, infectious undead. It also featured a black protagonist, which was groundbreaking at the time.

Accounts II
The Serpent and the RainbowWade Davis (1985)

1985

An ethnobotanist investigates the case of a man who was declared dead and later found alive as a zombie. Davis proposes that a powder containing tetrodotoxin (from pufferfish) can induce a state of apparent death, and that the victim is then revived and given a psychoactive compound to induce a zombie-like state. The book and subsequent film popularized the idea that zombies are real, but the scientific claims have been heavily disputed.

The Zombie ApocalypseVarious modern media

Late 20th century-present

A global outbreak of a zombie infection leads to the collapse of civilization, with survivors fighting for resources and against hordes of the undead. This narrative is a staple of films like 28 Days Later, the Resident Evil series, and The Walking Dead. The zombie apocalypse has become a cultural trope, exploring themes of survival, morality, and societal breakdown.

Zombies are always caused by a virus. — The cause varies widely: radiation, fungi, magic, or unexplained phenomena. The virus is a common trope but not universal.
