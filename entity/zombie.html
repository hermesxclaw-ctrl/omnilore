Who It Is

The most common enemy in the Resident Evil series. Humans infected with the T-Virus reanimate as shambling, flesh-hungry zombies. They have decayed flesh, vacant stares, and a slow, shambling gait. Despite their slowness, they are dangerous in numbers and can survive devastating injuries. Some retain basic tool use and environmental awareness.

Powers

Infection via bite, shambling horde, damage resilience

The Approach

The zombie is a creature of two births: one born of Haitian soil, where the dead are said to rise and toil in the cane fields, and another born of American cinema, where the dead rise to consume the living. Both are revenants, but they share little else—one is a slave, the other a predator; one is made by magic, the other by plague. To understand the zombie is to trace a single word across centuries and continents, from the plantations of Saint-Domingue to the screens of the modern world, where it has become a mirror for our deepest fears of death, contagion, and the collapse of civilization.

Content advisory. The Haitian zombie tradition involves themes of slavery, exploitation, and psychological trauma. Modern zombie media frequently depicts graphic violence, gore, cannibalism, and apocalyptic scenarios. Some portrayals may be interpreted as metaphors for racism, discrimination, and social marginalization. Reader discretion is advised.

Etymology

The English word 'zombie' is first recorded in the 18th century. It is traced to Bantu languages, particularly Kimbundu 'nzumbi' meaning 'ghost' or 'spirit'. The Haitian Creole 'zonbi' and Haitian French 'zombi' are direct predecessors. Some scholars also note possible connections to Kikongo 'zumbi' (fetish) or other Bantu roots. The exact linguistic path remains debated.

Cultural Ownership

The folkloric zombie originates in Haiti, rooted in West and Central African religious traditions brought by enslaved people, particularly from the Kongo and Angola regions. It is deeply tied to Haitian Vodou. The modern popular culture zombie is a global phenomenon, but its direct lineage is largely American, via George A. Romero's films.

Disputed Identifications

The relationship between the Haitian zombie and the African 'zumbi' is debated. Some scholars argue for a direct etymological and cultural link, while others see the Haitian zombie as a unique synthesis. Additionally, the zombie is often conflated with other undead or possessed figures in various cultures, but these are distinct. In modern media, 'zombie' is sometimes used loosely to describe any reanimated corpse, but the term is specifically defined by its Haitian origin and its Romero-inspired characteristics.

Description

Appearance

Haitian folkloric zombies are typically described as having a blank, vacant stare, a shuffling gait, and a lack of will or emotion. They are often said to be pale, with a deathly complexion, and may show signs of physical decay. In modern media, zombies are usually depicted as decaying corpses with wounds, missing limbs, and a hunger for human flesh. They often have gray or green skin, sunken eyes, and tattered clothing. Their movement is typically slow and shambling, though some variations show fast-moving zombies.

Symbols & Iconography

In Haitian Vodou, the zombie is associated with the symbol of the cross (used in the creation ritual), the vèvè (ritual drawings), and the bokor's paraphernalia. In modern culture, the zombie is often depicted with a raised arm, a staggering walk, and a groaning sound. Iconography often includes blood, gore, and apocalyptic settings. The zombie has become a symbol of mindless consumerism, social conformity, and the fear of pandemics.

Behaviour & Personality

Haitian zombies are typically portrayed as mindless slaves, without free will, forced to work in the fields or perform other tasks for their master. They are not aggressive unless commanded. In modern media, zombies are driven by an insatiable hunger for human flesh or brains. They are usually depicted as lacking intelligence, but some variations retain basic motor skills or even cunning. They are often shown as contagious, spreading their condition through bites or scratches. In humanized portrayals, zombies may retain memories, emotions, and the capacity for love, as seen in films like Warm Bodies.

Domains & Habitats

Haitian zombies are found in rural Haiti, often in sugar plantations or other agricultural settings. They are tied to the Vodou religion and the societal fears of enslavement and death. Modern zombies are ubiquitous in urban, suburban, and rural environments, often in post-apocalyptic settings. They are found in horror films, video games, and literature, and have become a staple of popular culture.

Version Differences

The Haitian zombie is a victim, created by a bokor to be an enslaved laborer. The modern zombie is a predator, often created by a virus or other scientific cause, and is a threat to society. The Haitian zombie is not necessarily infectious, while the modern zombie is almost always contagious. The Haitian zombie is a single individual under a master's control, while the modern zombie is part of a horde. The Haitian zombie is a metaphor for slavery and exploitation, while the modern zombie is a metaphor for consumerism, contagion, and societal collapse. Additionally, the modern zombie has evolved into more sympathetic figures in recent years, as seen in iZombie and Warm Bodies.

Source W.B. Seabrook, The Magic Island (1929)Culture HaitianPeriod 20th century (reported)

Summary

Seabrook recounts a story of a man who encounters zombies working on a Haitian sugar plantation. They are described as dead bodies reanimated by a bokor, forced to labor without rest or will. The account is presented as a true paranormal experience.

Haitian plantation workers

Bokor

The Serpent and the Rainbow

Source Wade Davis (1985)Culture HaitianPeriod 1985

Summary

An ethnobotanist investigates the case of a man who was declared dead and later found alive as a zombie. Davis proposes that a powder containing tetrodotoxin (from pufferfish) can induce a state of apparent death, and that the victim is then revived and given a psychoactive compound to induce a zombie-like state.

Participants

Wade Davis

Clairvius Narcisse

Bokors

Consequences

The book and subsequent film popularized the idea that zombies are real, but the scientific claims have been heavily disputed.

Variants

Some scientists have questioned Davis's methodology and conclusions, suggesting that the cases may have been misidentified or fabricated.

Slavery and exploitation

Source Scholarly analyses, e.g., 'The Zombie as a Metaphor for Slavery'

Description

The Haitian zombie is widely interpreted as a metaphor for the enslaved people who were forced to work on plantations. The idea of being turned into a zombie was a fear of being buried alive and losing one's soul, reflecting the real horrors of slavery.

Scholarly Context

Many scholars argue that the zombie belief arose from the trauma of the Middle Passage and plantation slavery, where the living were treated as dead, and the dead were believed to be enslaved in the afterlife.

Colonial distortion

Source Cultural studies

Description

Western portrayals of Haitian Vodou and zombies have often been sensationalized and racist, depicting Haitians as primitive and superstitious. This has contributed to a distorted view of Haitian culture.

Scholarly Context

The 'voodoo' zombie was popularized by Seabrook's book, which was written from a Western perspective and perpetuated stereotypes. Later films and media continued this trend.

Violence and gore

Source Film and media

Description

Modern zombie media often features extreme violence, gore, and cannibalism, which can be disturbing. The zombie apocalypse genre explores the breakdown of morality and the human capacity for violence.

Scholarly Context

The graphic content is often used for shock value and to explore dark themes, but it can also desensitize audiences.

Use of real drugs

Source Scientific and anthropological debates

Description

Wade Davis's claims about the use of tetrodotoxin in zombification have been controversial, as they involve the administration of dangerous substances. Some have criticized the sensationalism and potential harm to Haitian communities.

Scholarly Context

Davis's research has been both praised and criticized. Some argue that it provided evidence for the reality of zombies, while others question the ethics and accuracy of his findings.

Contradictions & Disputes

Etymology

Position A

The word 'zombie' derives from Kimbundu 'nzumbi'.

Position A — evidence

Dictionary citations trace to Kimbundu.

Position B

The word derives from Kikongo 'zumbi' or another Bantu language.

Position B — evidence

Some linguists propose Kikongo origins due to similar words and cultural connections.

Scholarly Consensus

The exact origin is uncertain; most agree on a Bantu root, but the specific language is debated.

Reality of Haitian zombies

Position A

Zombies are real, created by a bokor using a poison that induces a death-like state.

Position A — evidence

Wade Davis's research and testimonies of alleged zombies like Clairvius Narcisse.

Position B

Zombies are purely legendary or metaphorical, with no physical reality.

Position B — evidence

Scientific skepticism, lack of verifiable cases, and alternative explanations such as mental illness or fraud.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars consider the literal existence of zombies unproven and likely false, but the belief is culturally real.

Cause of reanimation in modern media

Position A

Zombies are caused by a virus.

Position A — evidence

Films like 28 Days Later and Resident Evil use viruses.

Position B

Zombies are caused by radiation, fungi, or other supernatural means.

Position B — evidence

Romero's original film never explains the cause, and later works use various explanations.

Scholarly Consensus

There is no consensus; the cause varies by work, and the ambiguity is often intentional.

Zombie intelligence

Position A

Zombies are mindless, with no intelligence or emotions.

Position A — evidence

Romero's zombies and most horror media depict them as mindless.

Position B

Zombies retain some intelligence and emotions, as shown in humanized portrayals.

Position B — evidence

Films like Warm Bodies and iZombie show zombies with consciousness and feelings.

Scholarly Consensus

The portrayal depends on the work; the archetype has evolved to include more sympathetic zombies.
