Who It Is

No mill in the old Turkic world ever ran alone. After the miller locked the door and went home, the wheel kept turning for someone else — a hunched, flour-white master who counted every grain, demanded his share, and would break the stones if he was cheated. Villagers called him Değirmen İyesi, the Owner of the Mill, and they left him bread, not out of kindness, but to keep the grinding from turning deadly.

Behavior is consistent across regions: territorial, contractual, and nocturnal — attested. By day the mill belongs to humans; by night it belongs to him. He expects a contract (pay, share, respect). If respected, he is protective and productive: keeps the wheel turning smoothly, prevents accidents, increases flour yield, warns of floods, and chases thieves. Powers
Control of Mill Machinery

Can start, stop, accelerate, reverse or crack the millstones and water-wheel without physical contact. Most frequently attested power. Informants report wheel turning with no water, or refusing to turn despite full sluice.

Limit: Power limited to his own mill; cannot affect distant mills. Daylight and presence of many humans weaken it. Iron nails and Quran recitation (late layer) can block it.

Invisibility and Auditory Manifestation

Makes himself invisible but produces grinding, sighing, footsteps, laughter, or imitation of miller's voice. Flour footprints appear with no visible walker.

Limit: Cannot remain invisible if flour is thrown or if ash is scattered (folk detection method). Revealed by dogs and cats.

Shapeshifting (Theriomorphism)

Assumes forms of domestic or wild animals inside the mill: white cat, black dog, hare, owl, frog, or rarely a horse. Tatar tradition most explicit.

Limit: Forms are small and remain flour-dusted; cannot become human other than old man. Transformation fails at dawn call to prayer (Islamic layer).

Flour Multiplication and Theft

Can increase flour yield for favored millers (blessing) or steal flour, scatter grain, and make sacks appear empty. Controls abundance.

Limit: Requires offering; without regular share, blessing reverses to theft. Cannot create grain from nothing — only redistributes.

Nocturnal Oppression (Karabasan Pressing)

Sits on chest of anyone who sleeps in the mill, causing night-mare, suffocation, paralysis — the karabasan motif transferred to mill context.

Limit: Only affects those who sleep on flour sacks or loft without permission. Protection: iron knife under pillow, not sleeping alone.

Hydraulic Control (Water Affinity)

As water-spirit subtype, can dam, muddy, or divert the mill-race, cause floods or drought in the channel, and drown intruders.

Limit: Only within mill-pond and sluice; cannot control rain or river beyond. Vulnerable to drought.

Illusion and Voice Mimicry

Calls miller by name in familiar voice, imitates grinding sounds to lure back at night, creates phantom lights in windows.

Limit: Illusions fail when approached with light or iron. Cannot mimic Quran recitation.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No single canonical iconography; appearance is highly variable and always described as liminal, dusty, and nocturnal — attested. Most common Anatolian and Tatar descriptions (Kalafat 1990; Urmancheev 1984): an old man (aksakal) of short stature, skin grey-white as flour, beard and eyebrows caked with flour-dust, wearing a flour-sack or old cap, sometimes with a millstone fragment on his back. Symbols — Symbols are functional, not decorative — attested. 1) Millstone (değirmen taşı): his seat and throne; cracking a stone is his ultimate punishment. 2) Flour dust (un): his veil and food; offerings of flour, bread or grain left on the stone. 3) Water-wheel and sluice (çark, oluk): his power source; blocking the water angers him. 4) Broom and sack: informants say he sweeps and sacks flour at night. 5) Threshold and doorway of the mill: ritual bounda

Domains — Domain is strictly the mill complex and its immediate hydraulic system — attested. Habitat includes: the mill building itself (especially loft, stone housing, and dark corners), the mill-pond / dam (bend), the sluice and water-channel, the wheel-pit, and the pile of chaff. Accounts I
The Miller Who Withheld the İye's Share (Un Payı)Yaşar Kalafat field recording, Erzurum village (informant: 68-year-old miller), published Doğu Anadolu'da Eski Türk İnançlarının İzleri 1990 p.115

Oral tradition recorded 1980s, story set in late Ottoman / early Republican era

A stingy miller decides to stop leaving the customary handful of flour (hak payı) on the stone for the Değirmen İyesi. For three nights the wheel stops at midnight despite full water. On the fourth night the miller hides and sees a flour-white old man sweeping flour back into the cracks and muttering 'Hakkımı ver' ('Give my due'). The miller apologizes and leaves double share with bread and salt; the wheel runs smoother than ever and his flour yield increases. Moral: the iye enforces fair measure. Establishes ritual norm: first scoop of flour or a small loaf left on millstone; taboo against cheating measure. Still practiced in some eastern Anatolian mills until 1970s.

The Apprentice Who Slept in the MillTatar folklore archive, Kayum Nasyri collection, retold in F. Urmancheev - Tatar Mifları (1984) pp.79-80, tale type 'Tegirmän İyäse malayny basar'

Recorded 1870s, narrative setting 19th century

A miller's apprentice (malay) is told never to sleep in the mill. Boasting, he stays overnight on sacks. At midnight the Tegirmän İyäse appears as a white cat, becomes a heavy old man, sits on his chest and presses until he cannot breathe, demanding 'Why do you occupy my place?' The boy is found at dawn speechless, flour-covered, with bruises. A mullah advises leaving bread and not sleeping there again; the boy recovers after three days. Explains taboo: 'Tegirmändä yatma' (Do not sleep in the mill). Links Değirmen İyesi to karabasan pressing motif. Used as cautionary tale for children.

The Quarrel of the Water Master and the Mill MasterBashkir oral narrative recorded by S. I. Rudenko 1912, summarized in Bahaeddin Ögel Türk Mitolojisi I p.478 and Fuzuli Bayat Türk Mitolojik Sistemi II p.214

Recorded early 20th century, archaic layer

Su İyesi (Water Master) and Tegirmän İyäse quarrel over the mill-pond. Su İyesi wants to flood the dam, Tegirmän İyäse wants steady flow. They gamble at night with pebbles; when Su İyesi loses, the water runs clear and steady; when Tegirmän İyäse loses, the wheel jams and fish die. Villagers hear them shouting and splashing. A respected elder mediates by sacrificing a black ram's head into the pond and pouring milk into the sluice, reconciling both iyeler. Illustrates hierarchy: Değirmen İyesi is subordinate to or allied with Su İyesi. Ritual of ram-head + milk offering at mill opening in spring attested in Bashkir and Tatar villages until 1930s collectivization.

Accounts II
The Abandoned Soviet Mill That Still GrindsContemporary fieldwork: Çulpan Zaripova Çetin - 'Tatar Türklerinde Varlıklar ve Demonolojik Varlıklar' (Turkish Studies, 2012) and Ekşi Sözlük / Tatar forum oral histories 2010s

Contemporary legend, set 1970s-1990s

After collectivization, a kolkhoz water-mill in Tatarstan is abandoned and electric mills replace it. At night locals still hear grinding from the ruin and see light in its windows. A tractor driver who tries to dismantle stones for building material finds his tractor stalled, stones too heavy, and hears a voice: 'Tegirmän mineke, timä!' ('The mill is mine, don't touch!'). He leaves an offering of bread and the tractor starts. The ruin is left untouched as 'iyäle' (possessed). Shows persistence of iye belief under Soviet atheism; explains taboo against reusing sacred/possessed structures. Similar stories recorded for abandoned Anatolian mills after migration to cities.

The Honest Miller and the Extra SackPertev Naili Boratav - 100 Soruda Türk Folkloru (1969) p.112 tale type 303, and Deniz Karakurt Türk Söylence Sözlüğü entry cross-ref; also Yaşar Kalafat 2006 p.205

Folktale, undated oral tradition, collected mid-20th century

A poor but honest miller always leaves the iye's share and sweeps clean. One famine winter, Değirmen İyesi repays him: each morning the miller finds an extra sack of fine white flour he did not grind, enough to feed his family and village. A greedy neighbor miller tries to imitate by leaving extra grain to trick the iye, but finds his sacks filled with chaff and mouse droppings. The iye rewards sincerity, not calculation. Moral economy tale: iye as enforcer of communal ethics and fair measure. Reinforces offering as reciprocal, not bribe. Tale type ATU 503? Borrowed motif of helpful house-spirit.

The Bride Who Entered the Mill at NightYaşar Kalafat - Türk Halk İnançları (1999) p. 147, Erzincan recording; also Fuzuli Bayat Türk Mitolojik Sistemi II p. 216 Azerbaijani variant

Recorded 1990s, story set within living memory

A new bride, curious or fleeing a quarrel, enters the mill at night to fetch flour. She sees a tall white figure grinding alone, flour dust swirling without wind. She screams, the figure vanishes, but she is found at dawn with her hair tangled with chaff and unable to speak for days. Elders say Değirmen İyesi was angered by a woman entering his male domain at night, or that he mistook her for an offering. Ritual remedy: bread baked from that flour is distributed to seven houses. Explains gendered taboo: women should not enter mills at night or during menstruation (impurity belief). Reflects patriarchal spatial rules projected onto spirit world. Remedy ritual attested.

Değirmen İyesi is an ancient Turkic god of mills worshipped with temples. — He is a low-level folk spirit (iye), not a high god (like Tengri or Ülgen). No temples, priests, or epic mythology; only household-level offerings and taboos. The 'god' framing is modern romantic inflation.

There is one single Değirmen İyesi for all mills, like a character. — Each mill has its own Değirmen İyesi — it is a type/office, not an individual. Like Ev İyesi, the term denotes the owner of that specific place.

Değirmen İyesi is the same as the Slavic Vodianoy or Domovoy and can be described with Slavic lore. — Functional analogy exists (water-mill spirit), but Turkic İye system has distinct terminology, rituals (flour/bread offering, threshold asking), and cosmology (Yer-Su). Direct substitution erases Turkic specificity and is contested by Turkic folklorists.

Değirmen İyesi is always evil and kills millers. — Ambivalent, not evil. If respected he is beneficial — increases yield, protects from theft, warns of danger. Malevolence is conditional punishment for broken reciprocity, not inherent nature. Many tales emphasize his helpfulness to honest millers.

Offerings to Değirmen İyesi were blood sacrifices. — Attested offerings are bloodless: handful of flour, first bread, salt, butter, milk poured into sluice. Ram-head sacrifice is for Su İyesi at larger water ceremonies, occasionally extended to mills but not typical for Değirmen İyesi alone. No evidence of human sacrifice.
