Who It Is

Zahhak (Avestan: Aži Dahāka) is the most fearsome dragon-tyrant of Persian mythology — a three-headed, six-eyed serpent-monster with thick iron fangs, who according to later tradition was also a human king corrupted by the devil (Angra Mainyu). In the Avesta, he is an ancient serpent created by Angra Mainyu to destroy the good creation. In the Shahnameh, Zahhak is a human prince whom Iblis (the devil) kisses on the shoulders; from the kiss wounds sprout two snakes that must be fed human brains daily. Zahhak overthrows the good king Jamshid and rules for a thousand years of tyranny, until the blacksmith Kaveh leads a rebellion and the hero Fereydun (Thraetaona in the Avesta) chains Zahhak in a cave on Mount Damavand, where he remains imprisoned until the end of time. Zahhak's myth is the central origin story of Iranian kingship: Kaveh's banner (Derafsh Kaviani) became the royal standard of successive Iranian dynasties. Zahhak represents absolute evil in the form of tyranny, corruption, and the abuse of power. His Serpent King archetype influenced later Islamic legends of Dajjal (the Antichrist) and Jewish/Christian apocalyptic traditions. He is the dragon that embodies the darkness of unjust rule, and his defeat by Fereydun is one of the foundational myths of Iranian identity — the triumph of justice over tyranny.

The Approach

In the beginning, there was a serpent with three heads and six eyes, born of the primal lie, who would one day wear the crown of the world and feed on the brains of the innocent. His name is Zahhak, the tyrant king of Iranian myth, whose shadow stretches from the sacred fires of Zoroastrianism to the epic verses of Ferdowsi, and whose legacy still slithers through the languages and legends of an entire region.

Content advisory. Contains depictions of patricide, tyranny, human sacrifice, cannibalism (implied), and violence. The story involves the killing of two young men daily to feed serpents, and the sawing of a king in half. These are mythological narratives and should be understood in their cultural and historical context.

Zahhak

Etymology

The name Zahhak derives from the Avestan 'Aži Dahāka'. 'Aži' means 'serpent' or 'dragon'. The meaning of 'Dahāka' is uncertain; suggestions include 'stinging', 'burning', 'manlike', 'huge', or 'foreign'. The Persian form 'Zahhāk' was influenced by the Arabic 'ḍaḥḥāk' meaning 'one who laughs'. In Zoroastrian tradition, 'Dahāg' is punningly interpreted as 'having ten sins' (dah = ten).

Cultural Ownership

Iranian (Persian) mythology, Zoroastrianism, and later Persian epic literature. The figure is also present in Armenian, Kurdish, and other regional traditions.

Disputed Identifications

The localization of Aži Dahāka's fortress 'Kuuiirinta' in the land 'Baβri' is debated; some identify Baβri with Babylon, but this is uncertain. The historical Dahae people have been suggested as an origin for the name, but this is speculative. In the Shāhnāmeh, Zahhāk is explicitly Arab, but this reflects medieval Persian political views rather than historical fact.

Description

Appearance

In the Avesta, Aži Dahāka is described as a monstrous serpent with three mouths, six eyes, and three heads, cunning and strong. In the Shāhnāmeh, Zahhāk is human but has two black serpents growing from his shoulders, which cannot be removed without regrowth.

Symbols & Iconography

Serpents/dragons, three heads, shoulders with snakes. In art, often depicted as a tyrant with snakes on his shoulders, or as a dragon.

Behaviour & Personality

In the Avesta, he is demonic and evil, the son of Ahriman, seeking to depopulate the world. In the Shāhnāmeh, he is handsome and clever but weak-willed, easily influenced by Ahriman, and becomes a cruel tyrant who feeds on human brains.

Domains & Habitats

The inaccessible fortress of Kuuiirinta in the land of Baβri (possibly Babylon). Later, his seat of power is in Iran, and he is imprisoned on Mount Damāvand.

Version Differences

The Avestan Aži Dahāka is a supernatural demon-dragon, while the Shāhnāmeh Zahhāk is a human (Arab) king. The story of the snakes on his shoulders is not in the Avesta but appears in later Zoroastrian texts and the Shāhnāmeh. The Shāhnāmeh also introduces the cooks Armayel and Garmayel and the Kurdish origin myth.

Stories & Myths

The Avestan Myth of Aži Dahāka

Source Avesta, DēnkardCulture ZoroastrianPeriod Ancient (c. 1000 BCE - 1000 CE)

Summary

Aži Dahāka, the three-headed dragon, is the son of Ahriman. He attacks Yima (Jamshid) and cuts him in half with a saw, but is beaten back by the fire god Ātar. After Yima's death, Dahāg becomes king and rules for a thousand years, oppressing the world. The hero Thraetaona (Frēdōn) eventually defeats him, but cannot kill him because vermin emerge from his wounds. Instead, he chains him and imprisons him on Mount Damāvand. At the end of time, Dahāg will break free and ravage the world until the hero Kirsāsp kills him.

Consequences

Aži Dahāka is imprisoned until the end of the world, but his eventual release and destruction are prophesied.

Variants

The Mēnog ī xrad offers an alternative view that Dahāg's rule was ultimately good, preventing the worse demon Xešm from ruling.

Zahhāk in the Shāhnāmeh

Source Shāhnāmeh by FerdowsiCulture PersianPeriod c. 1000 CE

Summary

Zahhāk, son of Merdās, is seduced by Ahriman, who convinces him to kill his father and take his throne. Ahriman then appears as a cook and, after feeding Zahhāk lavish meals, kisses his shoulders, causing two snakes to grow there. A physician (Ahriman in disguise) advises feeding the snakes with human brains. Zahhāk becomes emperor after defeating Jamshid, whom he saws in half. His daily tyranny involves killing two men to feed the snakes, until two cooks, Armayel and Garmayel, save one man each day by substituting sheep brains. These saved men become the ancestors of the Kurds. Zahhāk's reign ends when Fereydun, the son of Abtin, defeats him and chains him on Mount Damāvand.

Patricide

Source Shāhnāmeh

Description

Zahhāk kills his father Merdās by luring him into a pit.

Scholarly Context

This act is seen as the ultimate betrayal of family and order, leading to his corruption.

Human sacrifice and cannibalism

Source Shāhnāmeh

Description

Zahhāk's snakes are fed with the brains of two young men daily, implying murder and consumption of human brains.

Scholarly Context

This motif emphasizes the absolute evil of the tyrant and the dehumanization of his subjects.

Violence and murder

Source Shāhnāmeh

Description

Zahhāk saws Jamshid in half, a brutal execution.

Scholarly Context

This act symbolizes the transfer of divine kingship and the violent nature of usurpation.

Sexual exploitation

Source Shāhnāmeh

Description

Zahhāk takes Jamshid's daughters as slaves, implying sexual coercion.

Scholarly Context

This reflects the patriarchal norms of ancient societies and the fate of women in conquered kingdoms.

Contradictions & Disputes

Nature of Zahhāk

Position A

Aži Dahāka is a supernatural demon-dragon, son of Ahriman.

Position A — evidence

Avestan texts describe him as a monster with three heads.

Position B

Zahhāk is a human (Arab) king, corrupted by Ahriman.

Position B — evidence

Ferdowsi's Shāhnāmeh explicitly makes him human.

Scholarly Consensus

The Shāhnāmeh is a later, literary adaptation that humanizes the myth for epic purposes.

Origin of the name

Position A

Dahāka means 'stinging' or 'burning'.

Position A — evidence

Suggested etymologies from Sanskrit and Avestan roots.

Position B

Dahāka derives from the Dahae people or means 'foreign'.

Position B — evidence

Historical references to the Dahae tribe.

Scholarly Consensus

Uncertain; no consensus.

Location of Baβri

Position A

Baβri is Babylon.

Position A — evidence

Linguistic similarity with Bābiru.

Position B

Baβri is elsewhere.

Position B — evidence

Lack of definitive proof.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars accept the identification with Babylon, but it is not certain.
