Form

NOT ATTESTED. No Puranic verse describing Āḍi's physical form was located in the consulted material. Given his father Andhaka's stock Puranic iconography (a thousand heads, a thousand arms, two thousand eyes in some tellings; or two thousand arms and legs in others), a reader may imagine Āḍi as a war-form asura, but the Puranas as summarised assign him no independent bodily description. His one attested shape-change is at the level of disguise — he is explicitly said to take the form of a snake and thereafter the form of Pārvatī — so his 'appearance' in the surviving myth is characteristically borrowed, not his own. Any concrete visual description beyond this would be invention.

Powers

Conditional immortality (death-only-on-form-change) boon The central power of Āḍi's myth. Through tapasya (austerities) he won from Brahmā a boon of near-invulnerability: he would die only when he changed his form. Brahmā, taking advantage of the literal wording, granted exactly this — meaning Āḍi could endure any weapon but was fatally exposed the moment he shapeshifted into a new body. The power is therefore double-edged: it protects him from direct killing, but it makes his own strategy of disguise (which requires changing form) inherently lethal. The boon does not confer invincibility in battle — only conditional immunity to straightforward death. Brahmā made clear that all living beings must eventually die; he merely granted that Āḍi's death would be triggered by transformation. Changing form — the very manoeuvre Āḍi relies on for his infiltration — is the mechanism that kills him.

Shapeshifting / transformation boon-derived Granted as the means of fulfilling the immortality boon, Āḍi's power of transformation lets him assume other bodies. In the attested account he takes at least two forms: a serpent (to slip past Shiva's attendant Vīrabhadra into Shiva's private quarters) and the form of Pārvatī herself (to approach Shiva unguarded). The shape-changing is the load-bearing ability of the whole episode, and ironically it is what destroys him.

Tapasya (accumulated ascetic power) practice Like nearly all asuras in Puranic narrative, Āḍi's supernatural standing is purchased through tapasya — disciplined austerity that compels the gods to grant boons. He performs penance to please Brahmā specifically to overcome his father's fate. This is a generic asura ability (the standing condition of the purāṇic demon repertoire) rather than a unique trait.

Accounts

In the Matsya Purāṇa, after his father Andhaka was killed by Shiva, Āḍi learned that Pārvatī was practising tapasya in the forest. Recalling his father's death, he resolved to avenge it by killing Shiva — even though he knew he was outmatched. He undertook his own tapasya to please Brahmā. When Brahmā appeared and asked his wish, Āḍi asked for immortality. Brahmā replied that no living being can be truly immortal, for all that is born must die. Āḍi then clarified: he wanted to die only when he had changed form. Brahmā granted the condition literally, giving Āḍi the power of transformation and the conditional immortality that came with it. Armed with this loophole, Āḍi set out for Shiva.

Prepared to face Shiva, Āḍi entered Shiva's private quarters in the guise of a snake, evading the vigilance of Shiva's attendant Vīrabhadra. Inside, he took the form of Pārvatī and approached Shiva as though he were the goddess herself. Shiva offered an affectionate greeting to the apparent Pārvatī. But he quickly grew suspicious and, through his yogic sight (Yog), discerned the asura's true form beneath the disguise. Discovering the deception, Shiva struck Āḍi on the genitals with the Vajra (thunderbolt), killing him instantly.

A variant tradition — which the Wikipedia synthesis explicitly states is 'not mentioned in the texts' — reports a different boon: Brahmā granted Āḍi the chance to learn the art of warfare directly under Shiva himself. For some years Āḍi trained under the deity as his disciple. In time, however, he began to trouble the residents of Mount Kailāsa. On a later occasion he once more assumed the form of Pārvatī and presented himself before Shiva, asking for another chance. Shiva recognised the deception, killed him with his trishula (trident), and in the moment of his death Āḍi begged for forgiveness; the compassionate Shiva granted him moksha (liberation).

Scholarly Debates
Canonical vs alternate death
Āḍi is killed when Shiva strikes his genitals with the Vajra after Āḍi, disguised as Pārvatī, is unmasked by yoga (Matsya Purāṇa). ⇌ Āḍi is a redeemed disciple: he trains under Shiva, is ultimately killed by the trishula, begs forgiveness, and receives moksha (alternate telling).
Which Purana introduces Āḍi
The Wikipedia lead says Āḍi 'appears in the Matsya Purāṇa'. ⇌ The same article's Liṅga Purāṇa section says 'It is here that the reader is informed that Andhaka's son is Āḍi.'
The unsourced 'genitals' detail
Shiva killed Āḍi by striking him on the genitals with the Vajra. ⇌ No such precise manner-of-death is verifiable in primary Sanskrit source summaries the dossier could confirm.
Son-of-Andhaka vs son-of-Shiva classification
Āḍi is the son of Andhaka (canonical Wikidata description; Liṅga Purāṇa genealogy). ⇌ In the Shiva Purāṇa birth-legend, Andhaka is himself born from Parvati's sweat and Shiva's power, so to some theological generations Āḍi is a 'grandson-figure' of Shiva; careless retellings sometimes fold sons-of-Andhaka into 'sons of Shiva'.

A way to hold it in your mind

Every pantheon needs its walk-on villains, and the Sanskrit Puranas are crowded with them — asuras invented for a single chapter so a god can strike them down. Āḍi of the Andhaka line is one of the smallest of these: a son who inherited a father's war against Shiva and tried to finish it the only way he could, not with an army but with a costume. He won two concessions from Brahmā — a body that would not die until it changed, and a craft that let him change it at will — and turned that loophole into a serpent that slipped past Shiva's sentinel, then into the very shape of the goddess Pārvatī, so that the Lord of the universe would greet his destroyer as his own wife. It is a plan of perfect cunning and perfect self-defeat, because the disguise that puts him within striking distance is the same disguise that unmakes him. Shiva does not need an army to answer a false goddess; he needs only to look. And so the smallest assassin of the Andhaka lineage dies by the thing he trusted most — his borrowed skin.
