Who It Is

When the winter mists lift off Loktak Lake, the sky darkens not with clouds but with wings — thousands of ducks and wild geese answering a call no human ear made. The Meitei say that is Nganu Leima passing, the Duck Lady, mistress of every web-footed bird that touches water, calling her flock home.

Attested character traits from Moirang Kanglei narratives: obedient daughter of Salailen who accepts her father's command to descend to earth; co-wife who shares a household with her two sisters in an unusual sororal polygyny; nurturing provider who uses her power to summon ducks to feed or enrich her mortal husband's household; gentle but autonomous, ultimately choosing to return to her divine realm or sky abode when the mortal marriage fails or her divine nature reasserts. Powers
Summoning and Command of Ducks and Waterfowl

Ability to summon, command, and gather all ducks, geese, and waterfowl at any place she wishes, regardless of distance. Flocks appear instantly at her call. She is described as 'mistress of all ducks and waterfowl of the world.' Used in myths to provision her husband's household or to demonstrate divinity.

Limit: Limited to waterfowl (Anseriformes and similar aquatic birds); not attested to control other birds (pigeons belong to Khunu Leima, etc.) or fish (Ngaleima). Requires her will or ritual invocation; not passive. In some variants she must be present or invoked at a water site.

Avian Communication and Affinity

Understands and speaks the language of ducks/waterfowl; birds obey her as their queen and protector. Implies maternal/queenly authority over the species.

Limit: Not attested as shape-shifting into a duck in early sources; modern art sometimes shows winged cloak but textual shape-shifting is not clearly documented. Distinguish from Khunu Leima's dove affinity.

Sky-Earth Liminality and Descent

As daughter of sky god Salailen, she can descend from sky realm (Soraren) to earth and, in some variants, return. This reflects divine mobility between realms, common to Salailen's children.

Limit: Not a general flight power; tied to divine origin and paternal permission. Her return to sky often marks the end of the mortal marriage, suggesting conditional residence on earth.

Provision and Prosperity Blessing

Through her flocks, she brings food abundance, wealth (ducks as livestock and food source), and wetland fertility to her household or devotees. Ducks provide meat, eggs, and feathers.

Limit: Blessing is contingent on proper respect; myths where husband mistreats or reveals sisters' divine nature lead to withdrawal of blessing. Not an unlimited creation power.

Ritual Invocation and Protective Presence

In living Sanamahism, she can be invoked via Lai Haraoba ritual for protection of ducks, success in duck rearing, and health of wetlands. Maibi embodiment is considered her temporary presence.

Limit: Efficacy is faith-based and ritual-specific; not described as coercive miracle in ethnographic accounts.

Accounts I
The Descent of the Three Sky DaughtersMoirang Kanglei cycle; Sanamahi Laihui genealogy

Oral tradition of ancient origin; written puya recensions 18th-19th century; compiled in English by L. Kalachand (1995)

Salailen (Soraren), the sky god and father of many Lairembis, has three daughters renowned for their beauty: Khunu Leima (eldest, of doves/pigeons), Nganu Leima (middle, of ducks), and Shapi Leima (youngest, of rodents). At Salailen's command - or, in some variants, by their own curiosity about the earth - the three sisters descend from the sky realm to Kangleipak. They arrive in the Moirang region, near Loktak Lake, where they assume human form. Their divine origin is initially hidden; they appear as extraordinarily beautiful maidens. The descent establishes their liminal status: sky-born yet earth-dwelling, destined to mediate between realms. Establishes the triad's divine pedigree and their collective fate. Explains why three bird/rodent-associated goddesses share the same origin and why they act as a unit. Sets up the subsequent marriage to a single mortal, a test of divine-human coexistence.

The Marriage to the Same Mortal ManMoirang Kanglei - 'The Three Sisters' Marriage' episode

Traditional folklore; summarized in Hodson 1908 and Singh 1988

On earth, the three sisters encounter a mortal man - described in most scholarly summaries as a poor orphan, farmer, or commoner of Moirang (unnamed in early sources). Struck by their beauty and perhaps unaware of their divinity, he seeks to marry. The sisters, acting together, agree to marry him jointly - an unusual sororal polygyny where all three become wives to one husband, sharing a single household. This arrangement is presented as harmonious initially, with each sister contributing her divine gift to the household. Nganu Leima's contribution is waterfowl: she summons ducks to provide food and wealth. The marriage tests whether divine beings can sustain human domestic life. Creates the central tension of the cycle: can sky goddesses live as human wives? The shared husband motif explains the sisters' solidarity and later collective departure. Provides etiology for duck domestication and wetland abundance as gifts from Nganu Leima.

Nganu Leima Summons the Ducks to Feed the HouseholdMoirang Kanglei - domestic episodes of the three sisters

Folkloric; attested in 20th-century compilations

Living as human wives, the sisters must maintain a household with limited means. When the husband faces poverty or food scarcity, or when they wish to display their divine nature, Nganu Leima steps to the edge of the lake or courtyard and calls. Instantly, the sky fills with ducks and waterfowl - domestic ducks, wild teal, geese - who gather at her feet, allowing themselves to be caught, or laying eggs abundantly, or simply filling the yard as a sign of prosperity. Khunu Leima similarly commands pigeons/doves, Shapi Leima commands rodents (who bring grain or cause mischief). The miracle provides meat, eggs, and status, but also risks revealing their non-human origin to neighbors and to the husband. Demonstrates Nganu Leima's defining power and her role as provider. Neighbors' wonder and gossip increase, leading to suspicion. Husband's pride and later betrayal or indiscretion are foreshadowed. Establishes cultural association between the goddess and duck herding as a livelihood around Loktak.

Accounts II
The Revelation and the Return to the SkyMoirang Kanglei - concluding episode of the sisters' cycle

Traditional; recorded in Kalachand translation and e-pao.net summaries

The mortal marriage deteriorates. In the most common variant, the husband, pressured by curiosity or by villagers, breaks a taboo: he spies on the sisters' divine actions, questions their origin, mistreats them, or reveals their secret (that they are sky goddesses who command animals). Alternatively, the sisters themselves grow homesick for the sky realm or realize humans cannot respect their divine nature. Nganu Leima, with her sisters, decides to leave. She gives a final summoning - perhaps releasing her ducks to the wild as a parting gift or taking them with her - and the three sisters ascend back to Salailen's realm, or disappear into the wetlands, resuming their goddess forms. The husband is left bereft, his brief prosperity lost, serving as a moral about respecting the divine and about the impermanence of divine-human unions. Etiology for why waterfowl are wild and not fully domesticated, and why Loktak's birds are considered under divine protection. Reinforces boundary between human and divine; explains why the goddesses are venerated but not present as wives. Provides ritual

Nganu Leima and the Loktak Lake Wetland GuardianshipLiving Lai Haraoba oral tradition and contemporary Sanamahi revival narratives; ecological folklore

20th-21st century living tradition, rooted in older lake lore

Beyond the Moirang marriage cycle, Nganu Leima is invoked as the guardian spirit of Loktak Lake, the largest freshwater lake in Northeast India and a Ramsar wetland famous for its phumdis and migratory waterfowl. Fishermen and duck herders of the lake offer prayers to Nganu Leima before releasing ducks or during the winter arrival of migratory birds. She is said to protect the birds from overhunting and to punish those who take more than needed. During Lai Haraoba performances at Moirang and Thanga, her story is reenacted at the water's edge, and maibis chant to ensure the lake's ecological balance. This narrative is less a single plot than a persistent cultural geography: every flock on Loktak is her flock. Sustains living veneration and links mythology to contemporary wetland conservation discourse. Provides cultural justification for recent community-based conservation efforts that frame protection of migratory birds as respect for Nganu Leima. Reinforces her identity distinct from Ngaleima (fish) and Ereima (water itself).

The Sisters' Trial - Who Provides Best?Variant episode within Moirang Kanglei, reported in popular compilations and e-pao.net

Folkloric variant, 20th-century recorded

In some tellings, the three sisters engage in a friendly rivalry or a test set by their husband or by Salailen to see whose gift is most valuable. Khunu Leima fills the granary with pigeons/doves, Nganu Leima covers the pond with ducks, Shapi Leima fills the fields with rodents (or, paradoxically, controls them). Nganu Leima's ducks are judged most useful for food and feathers, but Shapi's rodents cause crop damage, leading to tension. The episode highlights the complementary yet competing nature of their domains and explains why ducks are valued while rodents are pests, even though both are under goddess protection. Differentiates the sisters' domains and moral valuations. Explains folk attitudes toward the animals. In some versions, Shapi Leima's association with rodents leads to her being less venerated, while Nganu Leima retains affection as provider of food.

Nganu Leima is the same as Ngaleima (fish goddess) — They are distinct goddesses with similar names but different domains: Nganu Leima = ducks/waterfowl (birds on water), Ngaleima = fish (animals in water). Both are daughters of Salailen in some lists and are often worshipped together, but have separate identities, stories, and ritual invocations.

Nganu Leima is a Hindu goddess or an avatar of Lakshmi/Saraswati — She is a Sanamahi (indigenous Meitei) goddess, part of a distinct religion that predates Vaishnavism in Manipur. While Hindu syncretism exists in Manipur, Nganu Leima is not part of the Hindu pantheon and is not described as an avatar in primary Sanamahi texts.

She is the wife of Sanamahi or a consort of a major male god — In the core Moirang Kanglei narrative she is the wife of a mortal man (shared with her sisters), not of Sanamahi. Sanamahi is her brother or senior kinsman in the pantheon genealogy. She is not primarily defined as a consort.

Nganu Leima can control all birds — Her power is specifically over ducks and waterfowl. Pigeons/doves belong to Khunu Leima, rodents to Shapi Leima, fish to Ngaleima. The triad's division of animal domains is intentional and complementary.

The story of the three sisters marrying one man is a modern invention or a fairy tale with no ritual basis — The narrative is a well-attested part of the Moirang Kanglei folklore cycle and is reenacted in Lai Haraoba ritual performance, indicating deep cultural embedding beyond a simple folktale. It is considered a sacred narrative (lai) within Sanamahism.
