Who It Is

In a universe where time itself spirals downward through ages of increasing sorrow, one man's name promised what the cosmos seemed to lack: infinity. Anantanātha — the Lord of the Endless — was not a god who created worlds, but a prince who conquered the most stubborn enemy of all: his own desires. In a tradition that counts 24 such spiritual emperors, he is the quiet 14th, a figure whose golden image, marked by a falcon, still watches over meditating monks from the carved caves of Ellora to the marble halls of Mount Abu. His story is not one of thunderbolts or seas parting, but of something arguably more radical: a man who looked at pleasure, power, and family, and walked away — not from despair, but from clarity.

As a Tīrthaṅkara, Anantanātha is described as having conquered all passions (kaṣāyas), possessing infinite knowledge (kevala jñāna), and being completely detached from worldly concerns. He is depicted as a teacher of the eternal Jain principles, embodying perfect equanimity and compassion.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Sanskrit: Ananta (अनन्त) meaning 'endless' or 'infinite' + nātha (नाथ) meaning 'lord' or 'protector'. Thus, 'Lord of the Infinite' or 'Infinite Lord'.

Pronunciation — uh-nun-tuh-NAH-thuh

Aliases — Anantnath, Ananta, Anant

Ownership — Jainism (primarily Śvetāmbara and Digambara traditions), with significant cultural presence in India, particularly Rajasthan, Gujarat, and Karnataka

Powers
Kevala Jñāna (Omniscience)

Like all Tīrthaṅkaras, Anantanātha attained infinite and perfect knowledge of all substances and modes across all time and space.

Limit: Attained only after complete destruction of all karmic matter

Divine Speech (Divyadhvani)

His sermons (samavasarana) were delivered in a divine, non-human language that all beings could understand in their own tongue.

Limit: Only during his teaching period after enlightenment

Miraculous powers (Riddhis)

Possessed various supernatural powers including the ability to appear in multiple places simultaneously, control elemental forces, and influence the minds of beings.

Limit: These powers are considered secondary to spiritual attainment and are not the focus of Jain practice

Absence of bodily functions

Like all Tīrthaṅkaras, he is described as having no need for food, sleep, or other bodily functions after attaining omniscience.

Limit: Only after enlightenment

Form & Signs

Appearance — Anantanātha is typically depicted in Jain iconography as a seated figure in the padmāsana (lotus position) or kāyotsarga (standing) posture, with a golden complexion. His distinguishing emblem is the falcon (or sometimes a hawk or eagle). He is shown with the śrīvatsa (a sacred mark) on his chest and a serene, meditative expression. In Digambara tradition, he is depicted without clothing; in Śvetāmbara tradition, he is shown wearing a simple whit

Symbols — His primary symbol is the falcon (śyena). His color is golden. He is associated with the śrīvatsa mark, the lotus, and the ashtamangala (eight auspicious symbols) in some representations. His yakṣa (attendant deity) is Pātāla (or Pātāla Yakṣa) and his yakṣī (female attendant) is Anantamati (or Anantā).

Domains — Anantanātha is associated with: the cosmic principle of infinite consciousness; the third age (duṣamā) of the descending cycle (avasarpinī); the spiritual liberation (mokṣa) of souls; the ideal of ahimsa (non-violence) and ascetic practice

Accounts I
The Life of AnantanāthaTriṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra by Hemachandra

12th century CE (compilation of earlier oral tradition)

Anantanātha was born to King Siṃhasena and Queen Suyaśā (or Suyaśas) in the city of Ayodhyā (or in some versions, Hastināpura). His birth was preceded by auspicious dreams (14 in the Śvetāmbara tradition, 16 in the Digambara tradition) seen by his mother. He was named Ananta (Infinite) because of the infinite merit he had accumulated. As a prince, he married and had a daughter (in some accounts) or a son (in others). He became disenchanted with worldly life after observing the suffering inherent in existence, renounced his kingdom, and became an ascetic. After about a year of intense meditation, he attained kevala jñāna under a Priyangu (or Priyaka) tree. He then spent the remainder of his life preaching the Jain dharma, establishing the four-fold order (chaturvidha sangha) of monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen. He attained mokṣa (liberation) at the age of 100 (some sources say 84), on the same day as his predecessor and successor Tīrthaṅkaras, in the region of Mount Sammeda (Parasnath Hill). Accounts II
The Auspicious Dreams of Queen SuyaśāJain hagiographic tradition

Ancient oral tradition, codified in early Jain texts

Before Anantanātha's birth, his mother Suyaśā dreamt of 14 (or 16) auspicious objects: an elephant, a bull, a lion, the goddess Lakshmi, a garland of flowers, the moon, the sun, a flag, a vase, a lotus pond, a white ocean, a celestial vehicle, a heap of jewels, and a blazing fire. These dreams were interpreted by the king's astrologers as signs that the child would become either a great emperor (chakravartin) or a Tīrthaṅkara. The dreams established the prophecy of his future greatness

The Renunciation of Prince AnantaTriṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra and other hagiographies

Ancient oral tradition, codified by 12th century CE

Despite his royal comforts, Prince Ananta felt the emptiness of worldly pleasures. He witnessed (in some versions) the suffering of animals, the fragility of the human body, or the inevitability of death, and decided to renounce. He distributed his wealth, bid farewell to his family, and became a wandering ascetic, tearing out his hair (a symbol of detachment) and adopting the life of a monk. Anantanātha is the same as the Hindu deity Ananta (the serpent Shesha) — In Jainism, Anantanātha is the 14th Tīrthaṅkara, a human who achieved liberation through ascetic practice. He is not a serpent deity. The Hindu Ananta is a divine serpent associated with Vishnu.

Jain Tīrthaṅkaras are gods to be worshipped — Tīrthaṅkaras are not creators or gods. They are perfected human beings who have achieved liberation (mokṣa) and serve as teachers. They are venerated as role models, not worshipped as deities.

Anantanātha is a minor figure with little significance — As one of the 24 Tīrthaṅkaras, he holds a central place in Jain cosmology and is the subject of significant temple art and hagiographic literature.
