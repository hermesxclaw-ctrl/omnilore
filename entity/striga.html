Who It Is

The Striga is the monster that launched the entire Witcher franchise — the creature Geralt must lift a curse from in the very first Witcher short story by Andrzej Sapkowski ('The Witcher,' 1986), later adapted as the opening cinematic and a major quest in the first Witcher game. Princess Adda, daughter of King Foltest of Temeria, was born as a striga — a monstrous, undead creature — due to a curse placed on her mother. By day, she lies in a tomb; by night, she rises as a feral, flesh-eating monster. Geralt is hired to lift the curse — but the ritual requires him to survive a night in her crypt, preventing the striga from returning to her sarcophagus before dawn. The Striga is the Witcher's thesis statement: monsters are not always evil, and 'killing the monster' sometimes means saving the human within.

Origin Post

Princess Adda was born a striga because her mother (also named Adda) engaged in an incestuous affair with her brother, King Foltest. When the older Adda became pregnant, she died giving birth to the cursed child. The infant was buried with her mother in the royal crypt, but the curse — born of incest and death — transformed her into a striga. For years, she emerged at night to terrorize Vyzima, killing dozens. King Foltest, despite the monster's nature, refused to allow anyone to kill his daughter — instead offering a fortune to any witcher who could lift the curse. Geralt succeeded, and Adda grew up to become a normal (if emotionally scarred) princess, later marrying King Radovid V of Redania.

Form

The striga appears as a gaunt, corpse-like woman — pale grey-green skin stretched over an emaciated frame. Long, matted black hair. Her face is skeletal: hollow cheeks, a lipless mouth filled with sharp teeth, sunken eyes that glow with feral yellow light. Her fingers end in long, iron-hard claws. She wears the tattered remnants of a burial shroud. She moves on all fours like an animal, with terrifying speed and unnatural angles. In the Witcher 1 cinematic, her design is particularly horrifying — a creature that was once a baby, twisted by curse into a nightmare.

Powers

Superhuman speed, strength, and agility. Razor-sharp claws that can penetrate plate armor. Paralytic scream — a wail that stuns and disorients. Undead regeneration — damage heals rapidly. Nocturnal existence: the striga is active only at night; by day, she lies inert in her sarcophagus. Her power is tied to the curse — if the curse is lifted, she becomes a normal human with no supernatural abilities.

Behavior & Habitat

Feral and animalistic — the striga has no language, no reason, no memory of her human identity. She hunts by instinct, driven by an insatiable hunger for flesh. There is no malice in her violence — she is a creature of pure, cursed instinct. This is what makes her tragic: the monster is not the person. The person is trapped inside, sleeping while the curse pilots her body.

Weaknesses & Wards

The curse can be lifted by preventing the striga from returning to her sarcophagus before dawn's third crowing — a specific ritual condition tied to the curse's origin. Silver weapons harm her but do not break the curse. The Axii sign can calm her temporarily. Her feral nature means she fights without strategy — she can be outmaneuvered by a patient opponent. She is bound to her crypt — she cannot leave the castle grounds.

Incident Log

Geralt enters the Vyzima royal crypt, seals the door, and waits. At dusk, the striga claws her way from the sarcophagus. A brutal, desperate fight through the night follows — Geralt sustaining grievous wounds, using signs and sword to delay rather than kill. As dawn approaches, the striga makes a final dash for the sarcophagus, and Geralt holds her back until the third cockcrow — the curse breaks, and a terrified, naked young woman collapses where the monster had been.

The CGI trailer depicts Geralt tracking the striga through the crypt, engaging in a savage fight that ends with him crouched over the creature at dawn, poised to strike — before realizing the curse has broken. The face of the young princess Adda, revealed in the sunrise, haunted a generation of gamers.

Field Notes

█ The Striga story ('The Witcher') was Andrzej Sapkowski's first published short story (1986) — the character's origin both in-universe and in reality.

█ The strzyga of Slavic folklore is a female vampire-demon born from babies who died unbaptized or from women who died in childbirth — the Witcher creatively adapted this into an incest-curse narrative.

█ The Witcher 1's opening cinematic was rendered by Platige Image, the same Polish studio that created the acclaimed 'Killing Monsters' trailer for The Witcher 3.

█ In the books, Geralt's fight with the striga leaves him so injured he requires weeks of healing — the games rarely reflect this level of consequence.

█ The striga fight in the Netflix series heavily used practical effects and a contortionist actor to create the creature's unnatural movements.

Cultural Significance

The Striga established the Witcher franchise's core moral principle: 'there is no such thing as a purely evil monster.' The short story and its game adaptation are the franchise's foundational text. The Witcher 1 cinematic, showing Geralt fighting the striga in the moonlit crypt, won multiple awards and remains one of the most celebrated game trailers.

Modern Interpretations

The Witcher Netflix series adapts the Striga story in Episode 3 ('Betrayer Moon'), with a visually stunning fight in the royal crypt. The striga appears as a contract in The Witcher 3. Copyright: CD Projekt S.A. / Andrzej Sapkowski.

A way to hold it in your mind

You were a baby. Your parents committed a sin, and you were punished for it — born dead, cursed, buried alive. You don't remember any of this. All you know is hunger and rage and the cold of the crypt. Then one night, a white-haired man with cat eyes locks himself in with you. He doesn't kill you. He fights you until the sun comes up, and when it does, you're human again — naked, terrified, and alive. That's the Striga: proof that monsters are made, not born, and that the hardest fights are the ones where you can't just kill the thing trying to kill you.
