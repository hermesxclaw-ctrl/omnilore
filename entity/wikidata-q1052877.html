The Approach

In the clanging bronze of a hero's shield, two immortal sisters race across the adamant, their serpent-haired heads gnashing and their wings beating against the air. They are Stheno and Euryale, the Gorgons who never die, forever chasing the man who slew their sister—and forever failing to catch him.

Content advisory. Contains mythological depictions of violence (beheading, pursuit), monstrous imagery, and themes of fear and vengeance. No explicit sexual content.
Identity

Stheno

Pronunciation STHEE-noh (UK: /ˈsθiːnoʊ/, US: /ˈsθɛnoʊ/)

Known As

SthennoStheino

Etymology

From Greek σθένος (sthénos), meaning 'strength, force, might'.

Cultural Ownership

Ancient Greek mythology; specifically from the archaic and classical periods of Greece.

Disputed Identifications

In some traditions, the Gorgons are three sisters, but occasionally Stheno and Euryale are conflated with other monstrous figures or with the Graeae (who are also daughters of Phorcys and Ceto).

Description

Appearance

In archaic art (c. 8th-5th century BCE), Stheno and Euryale are depicted with heads facing the viewer, seemingly without necks, atop running bodies in profile, with wings on their backs and curl-topped boots. Later depictions show smaller heads, necks, and less wild features. Literary sources mention snake hair (Pindar) and serpents hanging from their girdles (Shield of Heracles).

Symbols & Iconography

Wings, snakes, Gorgon mask (gorgoneion), often shown in running pose.

Behaviour & Personality

Fierce, monstrous, relentless in pursuit. They are driven by rage and grief over Medusa's death.

Domains & Habitats

Distant western realm near the Hesperides or in the underworld (location not specified in detail). They are chthonic and monstrous.

Version Differences

In Hesiod and Apollodorus, they are immortal. In Hyginus, they are daughters of the Gorgon (offspring of Typhon and Echidna) and Ceto, not directly of Phorcys and Ceto. Some later sources may conflate them with other creatures.

Stories & Myths

The Pursuit of Perseus

Source Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.4.3; Hesiod, Shield of Heracles 216-237Culture GreekPeriod Classical

Summary

After Perseus beheads Medusa, Stheno and Euryale pursue him. Perseus escapes by wearing Hades' cap of invisibility. The Shield of Heracles vividly describes the chase, with the Gorgons rushing after him on the shield.

Participants

Perseus

Stheno

Euryale

Consequences

Perseus escapes; the Gorgons fail to avenge their sister.

Variants

Nonnus adds that Perseus hears Euryale's bellowing. Pindar attributes the origin of the flute to Athena imitating Euryale's cry.

Violence and vengeance

Source Hesiod, Shield of Heracles 216-237

Description

The Gorgons' pursuit of Perseus is an act of vengeance for the murder of their sister. The scene is depicted with terrifying imagery, including gnashing teeth and serpents.

Scholarly Context

The Gorgons represent the monstrous 'other' in Greek mythology, embodying fear and the consequences of hubris.

Contradictions & Disputes

Parentage

Position A

Stheno and Euryale are daughters of Phorcys and Ceto (Hesiod, Apollodorus).

Position A — evidence

Hesiod's Theogony lists them as children of Phorcys and Ceto.

Position B

They are daughters of 'the Gorgon' and Ceto (Hyginus).

Position B — evidence

Hyginus's Fabulae gives an alternative genealogy.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars follow Hesiod's version as the standard.

Medusa's mortality

Position A

Only Medusa is mortal; Stheno and Euryale are immortal.

Position A — evidence

Hesiod and Apollodorus explicitly state Medusa is mortal and her sisters are not.

Position B

All three Gorgons are mortal in some early traditions (implied by Homer).

Position B — evidence

Homer's Odyssey mentions only one Gorgon, and later sources vary.

Scholarly Consensus

The consensus is that Medusa is mortal, her sisters immortal.

Known Gaps

No dedicated scholarly monograph on Stheno and Euryale; most information is embedded in works on Medusa and Gorgons. Iconographic evidence is vast but not systematically catalogued for these figures. Modern reception is under-researched beyond major adaptations. Exact origins of the Gorgon myth are debated.
