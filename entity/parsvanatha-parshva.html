Who It Is

In the ancient city of Varanasi, long before the Buddha walked the earth, a prince named Pārśva renounced his kingdom to seek liberation. His teachings of four great vows would echo through centuries, shaping a tradition that still thrives today. But who was this shadowy figure, half-legend, half-history? His story is a bridge between myth and reality, between the depths of asceticism and the heights of enlightenment.

According to hagiographies, Pārśvanātha was a compassionate and ascetic teacher who emphasized non-violence (ahimsa) and truthfulness. He renounced his princely life at a young age, practiced severe austerities, and preached the fourfold restraint (caturyāma dharma). He is described as calm, wise, and devoted to the welfare of all beings.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Pārśvanātha' is Sanskrit, meaning 'Lord of the Serpent' or 'He whose lord is Pārśva'. It is composed of 'pārśva' (side, flank, or serpent) and 'nātha' (lord, protector). The name likely refers to the serpent hood that shelters his head in iconography.

Pronunciation — PAARSH-va-NAA-tha

Aliases — Parshva, Pārasanātha, Pārśva, Parasnath

Fourfold Restraint (Caturyāma Dharma)

Pārśvanātha taught four great vows: non-violence (ahimsa), truthfulness (satya), non-stealing (asteya), and non-possession (aparigraha). These were the foundational ethical precepts for his ascetic community.

Limit: According to Śvētāmbara tradition, these vows did not include celibacy (brahmacharya), which was added by Mahāvīra. Digambara tradition, however, asserts that Pārśvanātha also practiced and taught celibacy.

Attainment of Kevala Jnana (Omniscience)

As a Tīrthaṅkara, Pārśvanātha is believed to have achieved omniscience (kevala jnana), the ultimate spiritual knowledge that transcends all limitations.

Limit: None

Miraculous Powers (Riddhis)

Hagiographic accounts attribute various supernatural powers to Pārśvanātha, such as the ability to perform miracles, heal, and protect his followers. These are considered secondary to his spiritual teachings.

Limit: These powers are not emphasized in the earliest sources and are considered by many scholars as legendary embellishments.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Pārśvanātha is typically depicted in Jain iconography with a dark blue or black complexion, seated in a meditation posture (padmasana) or standing in the kayotsarga pose. His distinguishing feature is a multi-headed serpent (usually seven) hood spread over his head, symbolizing his protection by the serpent king Dharaṇendra. Symbols — His primary iconographic symbol is the serpent hood, representing the cobra Dharaṇendra who shielded him during meditation. The serpent also symbolizes the Nāga cult that may have influenced his worship. In iconography, he is often flanked by Dharaṇendra and Padmāvatī, the serpent deity and goddess, who are his attendant spirits. His color is often green or black.

Domains — Pārśvanātha is associated with the city of Varanasi, where he was born and spent his early life. He later wandered as an ascetic through the Gangetic plains, finally attaining moksha (liberation) on Mount Sammeda (Parasnath Hill) in Jharkhand, which became a major pilgrimage site.

Accounts I
Life of PārśvanāthaKalpa Sūtra and other Jaina hagiographies

Traditional dates: 9th-8th century BCE; historical estimates: 8th-6th century BCE

Pārśvanātha was born to King Aśvasena and Queen Vāmādevī in Varanasi. He lived a princely life until he renounced the world at age 30. He practiced severe austerities for 83 days, then attained omniscience. He preached for 70 years, establishing a large following of monks, nuns, and laypeople. He died on Mount Sammeda, attaining moksha. He established a proto-Jaina ascetic community that later became the basis for Mahāvīra's reformed order. His teachings on non-violence had a lasting impact on Indian thought.

Accounts II
Encounter with the Serpent King DharaṇendraJaina legends

Later hagiographies

While meditating, Pārśvanātha was attacked by the demon Meghamalin. The serpent king Dharaṇendra and his consort Padmāvatī shielded him, creating a canopy of serpent hoods. This event is commemorated in his iconography. This legend explains the serpent hood in his images and the association with the Nāga deities.

Past Life as King Aśvasena's SonJaina universal history

Later texts

Jaina texts describe Pārśvanātha's previous births, detailing his spiritual progress over many lifetimes, including a birth as a prince named Aśvasena in a previous age. These stories reinforce the concept of karma and rebirth, and the gradual purification of the soul.
