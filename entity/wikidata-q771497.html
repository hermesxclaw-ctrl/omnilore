Who It Is

Before the sky had a name, before the earth had a shape, there was only water—and from that water, the first gods were born. Among them was Anshar, the 'sky pivot,' the point around which the entire cosmos would turn. He was not a warrior or a king; he was the boundary itself, the line between chaos and order. And when the forces of chaos rose to destroy his descendants, it was Anshar who called the gods to assembly and set in motion the events that would create the world as we know it. He is the grandfather of the gods, the one who knew when to step aside and let a younger power take the throne. In a pantheon of thunderous heroes and vengeful goddesses, Anshar is the quiet force that holds the story together—the cosmic ancestor who made it all possible.

Anshar's behavior in the Enuma Elish is that of a concerned patriarch. When Tiamat's chaos forces threaten the gods, Anshar is the one who convenes the divine assembly, seeks a champion, and ultimately sends Marduk to confront Tiamat. He is depicted as wise, cautious, and deferential to the younger gods' power—a figure who recognizes that the old order must yield to the new. In earlier traditions, his role is more passive; he is a primordial ancestor who fades into the background after begetting Anu.

Primordial Ancestry

Anshar is one of the first generation of gods born from the primordial waters (Apsu and Tiamat). He represents the first act of cosmic differentiation—the separation of the sky pivot from the earth pivot. His existence marks the transition from chaos to order.

Limit: As a primordial ancestor, Anshar is largely passive in the cosmic drama. He does not act directly but delegates to younger gods.

Father of Anu

Anshar begets Anu, the sky god, who becomes the head of the Mesopotamian pantheon. This establishes Anshar as a 'grandfather' figure in the divine hierarchy.

Limit: Anshar's authority is superseded by Anu's in later traditions, though Anshar remains an honored ancestor.

Convener of the Divine Assembly

In the Enuma Elish, Anshar convenes the gods to address the threat of Tiamat. He is the one who initiates the search for a champion, first consulting Ea and then commissioning Marduk.

Limit: Anshar cannot defeat Tiamat himself; he must rely on younger gods. His power is organizational, not martial.

Cosmic Boundary

Anshar personifies the upper sky pivot, the point around which the heavens rotate. He is the boundary between the ordered cosmos and the primordial chaos beyond.

Limit: This is a conceptual power, not a physical one. Anshar does not control the weather or celestial bodies; he IS the boundary.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No direct visual depictions of Anshar survive from ancient Mesopotamia. In iconography, he is likely represented by the horned crown (the symbol of divine authority) or as a bearded figure in divine robes, but these are generic divine attributes, not specific to Anshar. Symbols — The primary symbol associated with Anshar is the 'shar' (totality), often depicted as a circle or horizon line in cuneiform. The horned crown (divine headdress) is his generic symbol as a god. In the Enuma Elish, his name is written with the DINGIR determinative, marking him as a deity, but no unique cultic symbol or emblem is attested for him. The circle, representing totality and the horizon, is the most fitting conceptual symbol.

Domains — Anshar is the personification of the sky pivot or the totality of the heavens. He represents the uppermost celestial horizon, the boundary between the known cosmos and the primordial chaos. His domain is the 'upper heaven', the realm of the fixed stars and the celestial equator. Accounts I
The Birth of the Gods (Enuma Elish, Tablet I)Enuma Elish, Tablet I, lines 1-20

c. 12th century BCE

In the beginning, there was only Apsu (fresh water) and Tiamat (salt water). Their waters mingled, and from them were born Lahmu and Lahamu, then Anshar and Kishar. Anshar and Kishar 'prolonged the days, added to the years'—they aged and matured—and then Anshar begot Anu, his first-born, making him his equal. This genealogy establishes the divine order that will eventually lead to Marduk, the champion of Babylon. The birth of Anu establishes the sky god as the head of the pantheon, but also sets the stage for the conflict with Tiamat, as the younger gods' noise disturbs the primordial parents.

Accounts II
The Commissioning of Marduk (Enuma Elish, Tablets I-II)Enuma Elish, Tablets I-II

c. 12th century BCE

When Tiamat, enraged by the younger gods' noise, creates an army of monsters to destroy them, the gods panic. Anshar, the senior god, is consulted. He first sends Ea to confront Tiamat, but Ea fails. Anshar then turns to Marduk, his great-grandson, who agrees to fight Tiamat in exchange for supreme power over the gods. Marduk's conditions are accepted, and he is granted the kingship of the gods, setting the stage for his climactic battle with Tiamat. Marduk's victory over Tiamat leads to the creation of the cosmos from her body and the elevation of Marduk to supreme ruler of the gods. Anshar's role diminishes, but he remains an honored ancestor in the divine assembly.

Anshar in the Divine Assembly (various texts)An = Anum list; Assyrian royal inscriptions

c. 1200-600 BCE

In the An = Anum list, Anshar is listed as the father of Anu and the grandfather of Ea. He holds a place of honor in the divine genealogy but is not the focus of cultic worship. In Assyrian inscriptions, Anshar is invoked as an ancestral deity, and in some contexts, he is elevated to 'king of all the gods' (as Ashur), reflecting Assyrian imperial theology. Anshar's role shifts from a primordial ancestor to a politically significant figure in Assyrian theology, where his name is used to legitimize Assyrian kingship.

Anshar is the same as Ashur, the Assyrian god. — Anshar and Ashur are distinct deities. The conflation is a late, politically motivated development in Assyrian theology. In Babylonian and Sumerian traditions, Anshar is a primordial ancestor, not a national god.

Anshar is the 'king of the gods'. — Anshar is an honored ancestor, but he does not rule the pantheon. Anu is the head of the gods in most traditions, and Marduk (or Ashur) is elevated in later texts. Anshar's role is that of an elder statesman, not a king.

Anshar is a creator god who shaped the universe. — Anshar is a primordial ancestor, but he does not create the universe. The cosmos is formed from Tiamat's body by Marduk. Anshar's role is genealogical, not creative.

Anshar has a cult and temples. — No temples or cultic centers dedicated to Anshar are attested. He is a theological figure, not a focus of worship. This contrasts with gods like Marduk, Ishtar, or Enlil, who had major temples.
