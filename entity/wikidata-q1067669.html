Who It Is

In the shadowed archives of Greek myth and history, the name Charops flickers like a candle in a storm—sometimes a hero, sometimes a statesman, sometimes a wasp. Each bearer of this name carries a fragment of a story, yet none is complete. To meet Charops is to confront the limits of our knowledge and the seductive pull of the unknown.

In mythology, Charops is a minor character with no developed personality. In history, Charops of Epirus (the elder) is known for his political cunning and collaboration with Rome, while the younger is described as ruthless and oppressive.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Charops (Χάρωψ) is derived from the Greek words 'charis' (grace, beauty) and 'ops' (eye, face), meaning 'bright-eyed' or 'graceful-eyed'. It may also be related to 'charopoios', meaning 'gladdening the eye'.

Pronunciation — KA-rops (with a hard 'ch' as in 'k' in English, 'ops' as in 'ops' in 'eyeballs')

Aliases — Charopus, Χάρωψ

Ownership — Ancient Greek

Powers
Warrior prowess

In the Iliad, Charops is a Trojan warrior who fights in the Trojan War. He is killed by Odysseus, suggesting he was a capable fighter but not a major hero.

Limit: He was killed in battle, so his prowess was insufficient to overcome a major Greek hero.

Political influence

Charops of Epirus (the elder) was a statesman who wielded significant political influence in Epirus and maintained close ties with Rome. His grandson also held power, though more tyrannically.

Limit: Their influence was dependent on Roman support and the volatile politics of the region.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No reliable description exists for any mythological or historical Charops. The name suggests 'bright-eyed', but no physical attributes are recorded. In biological taxa, Charops wasps are typical ichneumon wasps, and Charopus beetles are soft-winged flower beetles, but these are not relevant to the mythological figure.

Symbols — No symbols or iconographic representations of any mythological or historical Charops are known.

Domains — Mythological Charops appears in the context of the Trojan War and as a son of Heracles. Historical Charops lived in Epirus, a region in northwestern Greece, during the 2nd century BC.

Accounts I
Death of Charops in the IliadHomer, Iliad 11.426

8th century BC (traditional date)

During the Trojan War, Charops, a Trojan warrior, is killed by Odysseus. His brother Socus then attacks Odysseus to avenge him, but is also killed. Socus's death leads to Odysseus being wounded, but he continues fighting.

Charops as a son of HeraclesPausanias, Description of Greece 10.36.4; other mythographers

Various, compiled in later sources

Charops is listed among the many sons of Heracles, but no specific deeds or stories are attached to him. He is a minor figure in the Heracleid genealogy. His existence contributes to the extensive lineage of Heracles, but he plays no significant role in mythology.

Accounts II
Charops, the Decennial ArchonChronicon (Eusebius)

753 BC (traditional date)

Charops was an Eupatridae (noble) who served as the first Decennial Archon of Athens, a position held for ten years. This was part of the transition from monarchy to democracy. His archonship marked a step towards democratic governance.

Charops of EpirusPolybius, Histories; Livy, Ab Urbe Condita

2nd century BC

Charops the Elder was a prominent statesman in Epirus who initially opposed Rome but later became a Roman ally. His grandson, Charops the Younger, succeeded him and was known for his oppressive rule, often using Roman power to eliminate rivals. Their collaboration with Rome contributed to the decline of Epirote independence and the eventual incorporation of Epirus into the Roman sphere.
