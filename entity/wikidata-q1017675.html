The Approach

In the age of sail, the North Atlantic was a sea of secrets, where a ship's crew could swear they saw an island that existed only on the edges of imagination. Buss Island was such a place—a phantom that haunted charts for over two centuries, luring explorers and mapmakers alike, until one man's search for the Northwest Passage finally erased it from the map.

Identity

Buss Island

Pronunciation /bʌs/

Known As

Bus IslandBusse IslandSunken Land of Buss

Etymology

Named after the type of vessel, a 'busse' (a Dutch two-masted fishing boat), used by the sailors who reportedly discovered it in 1578.

Cultural Ownership

European maritime tradition, particularly English and Dutch cartography and exploration narratives.

Disputed Identifications

Sometimes confused with Frisland, a phantom island also in the North Atlantic, and with Greenland due to navigational errors.

Description

Appearance

No reliable description exists. Early reports suggest a substantial landmass, but later charts reduced its size. Shepard claimed to have mapped it with harbors and coasts. The 'Sunken Land' was described as a shallow area, possibly a bank or submerged land.

Symbols & Iconography

On maps, it was often depicted as an island of varying size and shape, sometimes with a coastline and sometimes as a submerged shoal. No specific iconography beyond standard cartographic symbols.

Behaviour & Personality

Not applicable (a place, not a being).

Domains & Habitats

North Atlantic Ocean, between Ireland and Greenland, around 57°N (later adjusted to ~62°N). Possibly a real underwater bank or seamount.

Version Differences

Early maps showed a large island; later maps reduced its size; after 1745 it was labeled 'Sunken Land of Buss'. The exact location varied between 57°N and 62°N.

Stories & Myths

Discovery by the Emanuel (1578)

Source Frobisher's third expedition accountsCulture EnglishPeriod 16th century

Summary

During the return voyage, the ship Emanuel of Bridgwater sighted what appeared to be a large island. The crew named it Buss Island after their vessel type. It was placed on maps at about 57°N.

Participants

Crew of the Emanuel

Martin Frobisher (indirectly)

Consequences

The island appeared on maps for centuries, influencing navigation and exploration.

Variants

Some accounts suggest the sighting was a mirage or misidentification of Greenland or Baffin Island.

Thomas Shepard's exploration (1671)

Source Thomas Shepard's accountCulture EnglishPeriod 17th century

Summary

Shepard claimed to have sailed to the island from Dunkirk in the Golden Lion, explored its coast, and mapped it. He reported it as a real, habitable land.

Participants

Thomas Shepard

Crew of the Golden Lion

Consequences

His account temporarily revived belief in the island, but later searches failed to find it.

Variants

Some scholars doubt Shepard's story, suggesting it was fabricated or a misidentification.

John Ross's disproval (1818)

Source John Ross's expedition journalCulture BritishPeriod 19th century

Summary

During his first Arctic expedition, Ross sailed to the reported location and found no island, only deep water (no bottom at 180 fathoms). This definitively disproved the island's existence at that spot.

Participants

John Ross

Crew of the Isabella

Consequences

The island was removed from official charts, though it lingered on some maps for years.

Variants

None.

Contradictions & Disputes

Existence and location

Position A

Buss Island existed as a real landmass in the North Atlantic.

Position A — evidence

Early maps and Shepard's account.

Position B

Buss Island never existed; sightings were misidentifications or fabrications.

Position B — evidence

Ross's failure to find it and later oceanographic surveys.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars agree it was a phantom island, likely due to navigational errors or optical illusions.

Nature of the 'Sunken Land'

Position A

It was a sunken island, possibly a remnant of a lost land.

Position A — evidence

The name 'Sunken Land of Buss' and reports of shallow water.

Position B

It was a shallow bank or underwater feature, not a sunken island.

Position B — evidence

Later depth measurements showed no evidence of a sunken island.

Scholarly Consensus

The 'sunken land' likely referred to a shallow area, possibly a bank, but not a sunken island.

Primary Evidence

Various 16th-19th century maps and charts (e.g., those by Mercator, Ortelius, and later cartographers)
