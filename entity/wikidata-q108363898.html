Who It Is

Aeopolus never drives a chariot, steals a bride, or curses a house — his grandson does all of that. Aeopolus exists only as a name in the margin of a difficult Greek poem, the father of a woman whose son would betray a king and doom the family of Atreus. One scholion, one line of Byzantine commentary, is the entire mountain on which he stands.

No behavior, speech, motive, or personality is recorded. He functions solely as a genealogical linker: Cleobule daughter of Aeopolus. No myth shows him acting, speaking, ruling, or worshipping. Status: unknown.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Ancient Greek Αἰπόλος (Aipolos) literally means 'goatherd' from αἴξ, αἰγός (aix, aigos) 'goat' + πόλος (polos) 'herdsman, keeper' (cf. LSJ s.v. αἰπόλος). The form Αἰοπόλου in the scholion is genitive of Aipolos. The spelling Aeopolus reflects Latinization and confusion with Αἴολος (Aiolos, Aeolus) '

Pronunciation — Ancient Greek: [ai.pó.los] (ai-PO-los), genitive [ai.po.lúː] (ai-po-LOU). Anglicized: ee-OP-oh-lus / iːˈɒpələs / ay-oh-POH-lus. The initial diphthong ai- as in 'aisle'.

Aliases — Aipolus, Aipolos, Αἰοπόλου (Aiopolou, genitive), Αἰπόλος (Aipolos, nominative reconstruction), Aeolus (variant identification in some summaries)

Ownership — Greek mythology as transmitted through Hellenistic poetry (Lycophron) and Byzantine scholarship (John Tzetzes, 12th century Constantinople). No living-tradition, cult, or community

No cult site or sanctuary. Status: unattested; Elis/Pisa is hypothetical contextualization only.

Accounts I
Scholion to Lycophron Alexandra 157: Cleobule Daughter of Aeopolus, Mother of Myrtilus by HermesJohn Tzetzes, Scholia in Lycophronis Alexandram 157 (ed. Scheer 1881 Vol II p. 68-69)

12th century CE (commenting on 3rd century BCE poem)

In explicating Lycophron's riddling allusion to Myrtilus (the charioteer murdered by Pelops), Tzetzes supplies a genealogy absent from the poem: Myrtilus is the son of Hermes and Cleobule, and Cleobule is the daughter of Aeopolus (Αἰοπόλου), who is also called Aeolus (Αἰόλου). This is the sole surviving narrative anchor for Aeopolus: he is the maternal grandfather of Myrtilus. No story about Aeopolus's own deeds is told; he exists to locate Cleobule in mythic kinship. Establishes one of several competing maternal genealogies for Myrtilus; makes Aeopolus the ultimate human ancestor of the charioteer whose death curses the House of Pelops/Atreus.

The Competing Mothers of Myrtilus (Cleobule vs. Clymene vs. Clytie vs. Myrto)Comparative mythography: Tzetzes ad Lyc. 157; Scholia to Apollonius Rhodius 1.752; Hyginus Fabulae 224; Etymologicum Magnum; Smith Dictionary entry 'Myrtilus'

Hellenistic to Roman Imperial to Byzantine (3rd century BCE - 12th century CE)

Myrtilus's maternity is unstable. Tzetzes gives Cleobule daughter of Aeopolus. Other traditions give: Clymene (or Clytie) daughter of ???; Myrto; Phaethusa; or simply 'an Amazon' or 'Theobule'. Apollodorus (Epitome 2.6) avoids naming Myrtilus's mother at all. The multiplicity shows Aeopolus's lineage is one variant among at least five, not canonical. Modern handbooks (Grimal, Tripp) list Cleobule as 'one possible mother' precisely because of this scholion. Demonstrates that Aeopolus's place in myth depends entirely on acceptance of the Cleobule variant; if another mother is preferred, Aeopolus disappears from the family tree.

Accounts II
Hermes Fathers MyrtilusTzetzes ad Lyc. 157; Hesychius; Hyginus Fabulae 224; Pausanias 8.14.10 (Hermes as father implied)

Archaic to Byzantine (Hermes paternity is stable from at least 5th century BCE)

Across all variants, Myrtilus's father is consistently Hermes, messenger god, described as fathering Myrtilus on a mortal woman (Cleobule in the Aeopolus version). Hermes' paternity explains Myrtilus's skill with horses and chariots and his later role as divine-son trickster. In the Aeopolus version, Hermes is thus son-in-law to Aeopolus, though no interaction between Hermes and Aeopolus is narrated. Divine paternity elevates Myrtilus from mere servant to semi-divine figure, making his murder by Pelops a sacrilege that requires expiation and generates a curse.

Myrtilus the Charioteer Betrays Oenomaus for Pelops and HippodamiaApollodorus Epitome 2.6-8; Pausanias 5.1.6-7, 6.21.9-11, 8.14.10-12; Sophocles Oenomaus (fragmentary); Hyginus Fabulae 84; Diodorus Siculus 4.73

Classical to Imperial (5th century BCE - 2nd century CE for extant prose summaries; story older)

Myrtilus, charioteer of King Oenomaus of Pisa, is bribed (or seduced) by Pelops (or by Hippodamia, Oenomaus's daughter) to sabotage Oenomaus's chariot in the fatal race for Hippodamia's hand. He replaces the bronze linchpins with wax, causing Oenomaus to crash and die. Pelops wins Hippodamia and the kingdom. In this central Pelopid foundation myth, Aeopolus is not present, but his grandson is the pivot. The scholion's genealogy (Aeopolus -> Cleobule -> Myrtilus) is the backstory to this betrayal. Oenomaus dies, fulfilling an oracle that he would be killed by his son-in-law. Pelops becomes king of Pisa and gives his name to the Peloponnese. Myrtilus expects reward.

The Murder of Myrtilus and the Curse on the House of PelopsApollodorus Epitome 2.8; Pausanias 8.14.10-11; Hyginus Fabulae 84; Tzetzes ad Lyc. 157 continuation; Pindar Olympian 1 (allusion)

Archaic to Byzantine

After the race, Pelops murders Myrtilus to avoid paying the bribe or because Myrtilus attempted to rape Hippodamia (variant: Myrtilus tried to assault Hippodamia during the flight). Pelops throws Myrtilus from a cliff into the sea (the Myrtoan Sea, named after him) near Geraistos. As he dies, Myrtilus curses Pelops and his descendants. This curse is later cited as the origin of the misfortunes of Atreus, Thyestes, Agamemnon, and Orestes. Aeopolus, as Myrtilus's maternal grandfather, is the most distant human ancestor of the cursed line's victim, but he plays no active role in the murder or curse. Aetiology for the Myrtoan Sea; theological explanation for the curse on the Pelopids that drives the tragedies of the House of Atreus. Hermes places Myrtilus in the sky as Auriga (charioteer constellation) in some versions.

Aeopolus was the Greek god of winds or a king who controlled the winds. — That is Aeolus (Aiolos), keeper of winds in Odyssey 10 and/or son of Hellen. Aeopolus/Aipolos (goatherd) is a separate obscure genealogical figure attested only as father of Cleobule. The scholion's phrase 'also called Aeolus' has caused conflation, but no source gives Aeopolus wind powers.

Aeopolus appears in Homer, Apollodorus, or Ovid. — He does not. His sole appearance is in Tzetzes's 12th-century scholion to Lycophron. Homer, Apollodorus, and Ovid tell the Myrtilus story without naming Aeopolus.

Aeopolus has a known appearance or cult site. — No description, image, or sanctuary is linked to him. Any image labeled Aeopolus is mislabeled Aeolus or AI-generated.

Cleobule daughter of Aeopolus is the definitive mother of Myrtilus. — She is one of at least five competing variants (Clymene, Clytie, Myrto, etc.). No consensus exists.
