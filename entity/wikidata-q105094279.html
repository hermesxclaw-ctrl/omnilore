Who It Is

He was the strongest man who ever lived, yet his own life was a battleground of madness and redemption. From strangling serpents in his cradle to cleansing the world of monsters, Hercules stands as the ancient world's most enduring symbol of raw power and tragic humanity. But the Hercules we know today is not one story—it is a palimpsest of Roman, Etruscan, and Greek traditions, each layer adding new contradictions and new depths. Step beyond the Disney film and the comic-book hero to meet the real demigod who was both a protector of the weak and a pawn of the gods' vengeance.

Multifaceted: champion of the weak, protector, but also prone to madness and violence. He is both a civilizing hero and a figure of excess. His personal problems start at birth due to Juno's enmity.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The Latin 'Hercules' is borrowed from Etruscan 'Hercle' or 'Heracle', which in turn derives from the Greek 'Heracles' (Ἡρακλῆς), possibly meaning 'Glory of Hera' or 'Glory of the goddess Hera', though the exact etymology is disputed.

Pronunciation — Classical Latin: /ˈher.ku.leːs/; English: /ˈhɜːrkjʊliːz/ (HUR-kyuh-leez)

Aliases — Heracles, Hercle, Heracle, Herceler, Alcides, Hercules Romanus

Ownership — Roman and broader Italic cultures; also adopted by Germanic peoples via interpretatio romana. The Greek Heracles is a distinct but closely related figure.

Names are cult layers, not one fixed label.

Powers
Superhuman strength

Hercules possesses immense physical strength, demonstrated from infancy when he strangled serpents sent by Juno.

Limit: None known in myth, though he is not invincible.

Combat prowess

Skilled with club, bow, and in wrestling; he defeats numerous monsters and warriors.

Limit: None known.

Divine protection

As son of Jupiter, he receives some divine aid, but also suffers Juno's wrath.

Limit: Not always protected; he suffers madness.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Typically depicted as a muscular, bearded or beardless adult male, often holding a club and wearing the skin of the Nemean lion. In Italic votive bronzes, he is often shown in a warrior stance, sometimes with a lion skin draped over his arm or head.

Symbols — Club, lion skin, bow and arrows, apples of the Hesperides. The 'knot of Hercules' was used in bridal belts. The 'Rorschach Hercules' type features an abstract, geometric lion skin.

Domains — Worshipped throughout Italy, with sanctuaries in Samnium, the Apennines, and Rome (especially the Aventine Hill). Also associated with agricultural routes and salt trade, though this is debated.

Accounts I
Birth and the SerpentsPlautus, Amphitryon; various Roman sources

Mythic

Juno sends witches to prevent Hercules's birth, but they are tricked. She then sends serpents to kill him in his cradle, but the infant strangles them. In another version, Alcmene abandons him in the woods, and Minerva brings him to Juno, who suckles him until he bites her, spilling milk across the sky to form the Milky Way. Hercules gains further strength from Juno's milk; Juno's enmity persists.

Defeat of CacusLivy, Virgil, and others

Mythic

Hercules defeats the fire-breathing giant Cacus, who terrorized the countryside and stole cattle. This myth explains the origin of the Ara Maxima in Rome. Hercules becomes a protector of Rome; the Ara Maxima is established.

Accounts II
Madness and the Death of FamilySeneca, Hercules Furens

1st century CE

In a fit of madness sent by Juno, Hercules kills his wife and children. After recovering, he contemplates suicide but is persuaded to live by Theseus. Hercules's suffering is a central theme; he is purified through his labors.

Hercules in the UnderworldVarious, including Servius's commentary on Virgil

Late antiquity

Hercules descends to the underworld to fetch Cerberus as one of his labors. Later allegorists see this as overcoming earthly desires. Symbolic interpretation in late antiquity.
