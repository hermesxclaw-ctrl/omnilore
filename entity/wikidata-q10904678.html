Who It Is

Before the sun-goddess sent her grandson to rule the reed-plain of Japan, the heavenly smiths forged ten objects that could turn back death itself — two mirrors that watched the shore and the sea, a sword with eight hands, five jewels that gave, restored and multiplied life, and three scarves that could brush away serpents, wasps and all calamity. Together they were the Tokusa no Kandakara, the Ten Sacred Treasures, not to be confused with the Three Imperial Regalia. They were meant to pacify a restless soul — and to prove that a new dynasty held heaven's mandate.

Not anthropomorphic beings but numinous objects (shintai / yorishiro). In narrative they are passive instruments wielded by kami or emperors. In ritual texts they are 'shaken' (furu) and their sound/light is said to make the soul tremble and return. The Kujiki attributes agency to the jewels (giving life, reversing death) and to the scarves (repelling snakes and insects), but always through the holder's correct ritual performance. Powers
Okitsu-kagami and Hetsu-kagami (Off-shore and On-shore Mirrors)

Mirrors that illuminate, reveal hidden evil, and reflect the soul. In Chinkon ritual, their shaking and flashing is said to summon the wandering mitama back to the body. Kujiki describes them as mirrors that 'stand' (tatsu) to watch over sea and land.

Limit: Mirrors do not themselves revive; they prepare/attract the soul. Require correct ritualist (Nakatomi/Fujiwara lineage) and conjunction with jewels/hire. No offensive power attested.

Yatsuka no Tsurugi (Eight-Hand Sword)

Sword of martial authority and exorcism; cuts evil spirits and gives the holder legitimacy as heavenly descendant. At Isonokami, identified with Futsunomitama, the spirit of the sword itself as kami.

Limit: Not described as invincible; historical Mononobe defeat (587 CE) shows sword did not prevent military loss. Ritual use is apotropaic, not battlefield super-weapon in texts.

Iku-tama (Life Jewel / Living Jewel)

Jewel that bestows life, vitality and longevity; placed upon the body to infuse iku (life-force). In Chinkon-sai, shaken together with other tama to produce a jingling sound that enlivens the soul.

Limit: Does not reverse death alone; must be combined with Makaru-kaeshi no tama. Effect is ritual, not instantaneous medical cure in narrative.

Makaru-kaeshi no Tama (Jewel that Returns from Death / Resurrecting Jewel)

Jewel that calls back the dead or returns the soul from the underworld (Yomi). Described as the central necromantic jewel, used in mitama-furi to recall a soul that has departed.

Limit: Kujiki does not provide a narrative example of actual resurrection; power is ritual-claimed, not demonstrated in a story. Requires the full set and correct incantation (furu). Later folklore warns that incomplete performance fails.

Taru-tama (Full / Sufficient Jewel) and Chikaheshi no Tama (Road-Returning Jewel)

Taru-tama provides abundance, fulfillment and satiety (taru = to be sufficient); Chikaheshi no tama protects travelers, turning back misfortune on the road (chi = road, kahesu = to return). Together they ensure prosperity and safe passage.

Limit: Specific effects are vaguely defined; taru may refer to spiritual fulfillment rather than material wealth. Chikaheshi's road protection is apotropaic, not teleportation.

Orochi no Hire, Hachi no Hire, Kusagusa no Mono no Hire (Three Scarves)

Woven scarves/stoles that ward off specific calamities when waved: Orochi no hire repels serpents/dragons and water disasters; Hachi no hire repels stinging insects (bees/wasps) and by extension disease-bearing pests; Kusagusa no mono no hire repels all other evils and impurities (kusagusa = various things). In ritual they are shaken to create wind that purifies.

Limit: Protective, not offensive; must be waved by authorized ritualist. No narrative shows them used as weapons. Their material (asa, silk) is perishable, suggesting symbolic rather than durable objects.

Collective Power: Mitama-furi / Chinkon (Soul-Shaking / Soul-Pacification)

When the ten are shaken together (furu) with the incantation 'Iku-tama, taru-tama, makaru-kaeshi no tama...' the emperor's or individual's mitama is revitalized, extended in life, and protected from death pollution. This is the attested ritual function, not a combat power.

Limit: Ritual efficacy is faith-based; texts do not claim universal immortality. Historically performed only for the emperor (and later for crown prince). Requires purity, fasting, and correct lineage of officiant.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No contemporary illustration survives; descriptions are textual and functional. The set comprises ten distinct types: 1) Okitsu-kagami (mirror that stands off-shore / distant mirror) and 2) Hetsu-kagami (mirror that stands on-shore / near mirror) — described as bronze mirrors (kagami) with spiritual power to reveal and reflect; 3) Yatsuka no Tsurugi (eight-handspan / eight-grip sword) — a long iron sword (tsurugi) with multiple hilts or eight han

Accounts I
The Forging in Takamagahara and Bestowal on NinigiSendai Kuji Hongi (Kujiki) Book 3, Kogo Shui Book 1

Heian compilation of earlier oral/court tradition (9th century, describing mythic age of the gods)

In the High Plain of Heaven, the heavenly kami assemble after Amaterasu's withdrawal and return. To equip her grandson Ninigi no Mikoto for his descent to rule the Central Land of Reed Plains, the heavenly deities forge ten treasures. Amaterasu, Takamimusubi and other kami entrust them to Ame-no-Koyane and Futodama (ancestors of Nakatomi/Fujiwara and Imbe clans) or directly to Ninigi as proof of heavenly mandate and as tools to pacify the unruly earthly kami (kunitsukami) and to protect his soul on the dangerous descent. The treasures accompany the Three Regalia in some variants, but Kujiki emphasizes the Ten as the operative ritual set. Establishes the Ten as heavenly legitimization parallel to the Three Regalia; provides ritual charter for the later Imperial Chinkon-sai; links Mononobe/Nakatomi custodianship to descent myth.

Enshrinement at Isonokami by Emperor SujinNihon Shoki Book 5 (Emperor Sujin), Kujiki Book 5, Isonokami Jingu tradition, Engishiki

Mytho-historical (trad. 4th century BCE-4th century CE chronology; text compiled 720-927 CE)

During the reign of Emperor Sujin (trad. 97-30 BCE, historically early Kofun period), pestilence and disorder afflict Yamato. The emperor, after divination, separates the heavenly treasures from the palace to reduce their potent pollution and entrusts the sword and treasures to the Mononobe clan at the shrine of Isonokami (Isonokami Furu Jingu) in the sacred grove of Furu. There Futsunomitama, the spirit of the sword, is enshrined. The Mononobe become hereditary guardians (muraji) of the armory-treasury. Explains why Isonokami holds ancient swords/mirrors and why the Mononobe control military-religious power; sets up later Mononobe-Soga conflict over custodianship of sacred objects.

The Mononobe-Soga War and the Loss of Custodianship (587 CE)Nihon Shoki Book 20 (Emperor Yōmei, Sushun), Kujiki Book 5

Asuka period, 6th century (historical event recorded 720 CE)

Mononobe no Moriya, champion of the old kami worship and guardian of Isonokami treasures, opposes Soga no Umako's promotion of Buddhism. After Emperor Yōmei's death, the clans clash at the Battle of Shigisan (587). Moriya is defeated and killed; the Mononobe are slaughtered and their treasure-house at Isonokami is seized by the Soga and later by the Yamato court. The Ten Treasures remain enshrined but pass from Mononobe to imperial control, diminishing the clan's ritual monopoly. Marks the decline of the Mononobe and the rise of Soga/Buddhist influence; the treasures become fully imperial ritual objects. Later chronicles use the defeat to explain why the Ten are now handled by Nakatomi/Fujiwara ritualists in the Chinkon-sai rather than Mononobe warriors.

Accounts II
The Imperial Chinkon-sai: Shaking the Treasures for the Emperor's SoulEngishiki Book 8, Kyuchu Sanden tradition, Imperial Household Agency records

Heian (927 CE codified) to present (annual rite)

On the night of the last day of the 11th lunar month (now mid-December), in the Chinkon Hall of the Imperial Palace, Nakatomi/Fujiwara ritualists perform Mitama-furi. The Ten Sacred Treasures (or symbolic replicas) are placed on a stand before the emperor (or his mitama proxy). The officiant shakes them while reciting the norito: 'Iku-tama, taru-tama, makaru-kaeshi no tama, chikaheshi no tama...' The jingling and fluttering is said to call back the emperor's wandering soul, extend his life, and protect him from death pollution for the coming year. The rite is one of the most secret and sacred of the palace rituals. Continuity of the Ten as living ritual objects, not merely myth; the rite was revived in Meiji and continues under the Imperial Household Agency. Provides the only continuous attested use of the Ten from ancient to modern times.

Nigihayahi's Descent with the Ten Treasures (Alternative Descent Myth)Kujiki Book 3-4, Shinsen Shojiroku

Heian compilation of Mononobe clan tradition

In the Mononobe-centered variant, it is not Ninigi but Nigihayahi no Mikoto (heavenly grandson, ancestor of the Mononobe and Owari clans) who descends to Yamato ahead of Jimmu, bearing the Ten Sacred Treasures and heavenly weaponry. He lands at Isonokami/Furu and establishes the clan's claim to Yamato before the Jimmu eastern expedition. This makes the Ten the charter of the Mononobe, not just the imperial line. Creates a competing descent charter that legitimizes the Mononobe's independent possession of heavenly treasures; explains why Isonokami, not Ise, holds them. Historians view this as clan propaganda inserted into Kujiki.

Modern Folklore: The Lost Treasures and the Furu WindEdo-period kokugaku commentary (Motoori Norinaga, Hirata Atsutane), local Furu/Isonokami legend

Edo (18th-19th century) to modern local tradition

Local legend around Mt. Furu says that when the treasures are improperly handled, a divine wind (furu no kaze) rises and the scarves flutter by themselves, causing illness. Hirata Atsutane records a story of a villager who stole a jewel and was pursued by bees and serpents until he returned it to the shrine. The tale reinforces taboo against viewing or touching the treasures and explains why the shrine's treasure house is rarely opened. Reinforces the treasures' status as dangerous sacred objects (monoimi) and explains their invisibility; contributes to modern 'no verified visual record' policy at the shrine.

The Ten Sacred Treasures are the same as the Three Sacred Treasures (Imperial Regalia). — They are distinct sets. The Three (mirror, jewel, sword) are enthronement regalia enshrined at Ise, Atsuta and the Palace. The Ten are a separate ritual set (2 mirrors, 1 sword, 4 jewels, 3 scarves) used specifically for the Chinkon soul-pacification rite and housed traditionally at Isonokami. Kojiki/Nihon Shoki describe only the Three; the Ten appear only in Kogo Shui/Kujiki/Engishiki.

Each treasure grants a superpower like in anime (e.g., resurrecting anyone instantly, shooting beams). — Early texts describe ritual, apotropaic functions: the jewels and mirrors help recall and settle the soul when correctly shaken with incantation; the scarves ward off snakes/insects. No narrative shows flashy combat magic. Anime/game depictions (Touhou, Fate, Nioh) invent elemental powers for gameplay.

The Ten Treasures are on public display at Isonokami Jingu and can be photographed. — Isonokami's treasure house is not open with labeled Ten objects; the Chinkon-sai implements are secret palace ritual objects. What visitors see are Kofun-period swords and mirrors in museums, not identified as the canonical Ten. The shrine observes a 'no verified visual record' policy for the sacred set.

The Sendai Kuji Hongi is a reliable ancient source contemporary with Kojiki. — Kujiki is a Heian compilation (9th-10th century) with later interpolations, though it preserves older clan traditions. It is not contemporary with Kojiki (712) and its account of the Ten is not corroborated by earlier chronicles. Scholars use it critically, not as primary proof of antiquity.
