Who It Is

A name spoken across centuries, from the streets of medieval Florence to the pages of a Victorian adventure novel, Beatrice is less a single figure than a constellation of identities—a muse, a saint, a heroine, a corporation, a ship, a storm. To encounter 'Beatrice' is to ask: which one? And why does this name endure?

In Dante's Vita Nuova, Beatrice is portrayed as a paragon of virtue, courteous, and reserved. In the Divine Comedy, she is a guide, authoritative and loving, leading Dante through Paradise. In Haggard's novel, Beatrice is a strong-willed and independent woman, typical of Victorian heroines.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Italian 'Beatrice', derived from Latin 'Viatrix', meaning 'voyager' or 'traveler', later influenced by 'beata' (blessed).

Pronunciation — Italian: [be.aˈtriːtʃe]; English: /ˈbiːətrɪs/ or /biˈætrɪs/

Aliases — Bice, Beatrice Portinari, Beatrice 'Bice' di Folco Portinari

Ownership — Primarily Italian, specifically Florentine, due to Dante Alighieri's works. The name has been adopted in various cultures, including English, French, and Hungarian.

Powers
Divine guidance

In Dante's Divine Comedy, Beatrice possesses the ability to guide souls through Paradise, intercede with God, and illuminate divine truth.

Limit: Limited to the afterlife; she cannot intervene in the mortal world directly.

Inspirational muse

Beatrice Portinari inspired Dante's poetry, serving as a catalyst for his artistic and spiritual development.

Limit: Passive ability; her influence is through remembrance and idealization.

Heroic agency

In H. Rider Haggard's novel, Beatrice is a resourceful and determined protagonist who overcomes adversity.

Limit: Fictional setting; her abilities are those of a mortal woman.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Not specified in the source material. For Beatrice Portinari, historical descriptions are absent; Dante's portrayal is idealized and symbolic, often depicted in art as a young woman in medieval Florentine attire, with a serene and dignified bearing.

Symbols — In Dante's works, Beatrice is associated with light, divine grace, and the number three (symbolizing the Trinity). In art, she is often depicted with a red or white rose, representing love and purity.

Domains — Florence, Italy (for the historical figure). In the Divine Comedy, she resides in Paradise. In other works, settings vary: Victorian England in Haggard's novel, a fictional kingdom in Beatrice the Sixteenth.

Accounts I
The Vita NuovaDante Alighieri

13th century

Dante recounts his first meeting with Beatrice at age nine, his love for her, her greeting, his suffering, her death, and his subsequent spiritual awakening. Dante's poetic and philosophical development, leading to the Divine Comedy.

The Divine ComedyDante Alighieri

14th century

Beatrice appears as Dante's guide through Paradise, replacing Virgil. She leads him through the celestial spheres and intercedes for him with the Virgin Mary. Dante's salvation and the completion of his journey.

Beatrice (novel)H. Rider Haggard

1890

A novel about a woman named Beatrice who faces societal constraints and personal tragedy, ultimately finding strength. Exploration of themes like love, duty, and independence.

Beatrice the SixteenthThomas Baty (as Irene Clyde)

1909

A utopian novel about a woman named Beatrice who finds herself in a matriarchal society. Commentary on gender roles and society.
