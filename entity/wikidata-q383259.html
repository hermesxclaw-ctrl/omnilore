Form

His statues depict him as a mature man, often with a shaved head or short wig, a long kilt, and a scribe's roll or a papyrus in his hands, emphasizing his role as a learned official. He is shown with a somewhat heavy, realistic face, reflecting his age and wisdom. He is never depicted as a god in his own lifetime, but posthumously he is shown wearing the divine beard and holding the ankh.

Powers

Master Architect and Builder He oversaw the construction of many of Amenhotep III's most monumental projects, including parts of the Temple of Luxor, the mortuary temple of Amenhotep III, and the Colossi of Memnon. His ability to organize massive workforces and manage complex logistics was legendary. Historical (18th Dynasty) He was a mortal man, but his power was delegated by the king. None.

Royal Scribe and Vizier He held the title of Vizier, making him the highest-ranking official in the land after the king. He was the chief administrator, judge, and advisor. He was the 'ears and eyes' of the king. Historical (18th Dynasty) His power was bounded by the king's will. None.

Divine Intercessor (posthumous) After his death, he was deified and became a popular god of healing and justice. People would pray to him for oracles, cures, and help in legal disputes. His mortuary temple became a site of pilgrimage, where the sick would sleep to receive healing dreams. Deified (posthumous, from late 18th Dynasty onward) His powers were granted by the gods Amun and Thoth. None.

Wisdom and Counsel His sayings, collected in wisdom texts, were believed to offer practical and moral guidance. He was seen as a sage whose advice could solve problems and prevent disasters. Historical and posthumous His wisdom was mortal, but his posthumous reputation made it divine. None.

Behavior & Habitat

He was known for his intelligence, administrative competence, and diplomatic skill. He was a 'doer' — a man who got things done. He was also known as a sage, whose sayings were collected as wisdom literature. He was a loyal servant to the king, and his behavior set a standard for the ideal Egyptian official.

Accounts

Amenhotep, son of Hapu, tells his own story on his statues. He describes his humble birth in the town of Athribis, his rise through the ranks due to his intelligence and competence, and his appointment as chief architect and vizier. He boasts that he was 'one who was brought in because of his excellence, one who was introduced because of his deeds, one who was promoted because of his worth'.

He was responsible for the construction of the Colossi of Memnon, two massive stone statues of Amenhotep III that stood at the entrance to the king's mortuary temple. The sheer scale of the project, moving and carving stone blocks weighing over 700 tons each, was a testament to his organizational genius.

Centuries after his death, Amenhotep, son of Hapu, was worshipped as a god. He was seen as a healer and an oracle. His temple in Thebes became a famous healing sanctuary, where people would come from all over the Mediterranean to seek cures. He was often identified with the Greek god Asclepius.

Scholarly Debates
His exact role in building the Colossi of Memnon
He was the chief architect who designed and oversaw their construction. ⇌ He was the project manager who organized the logistics, but the design was done by others.
Evidence for A: His inscriptions boast of his role as 'chief of all the king's works'.
Evidence for B: No specific design documents survive, and the exact division of labor is unknown.
The nature of his deification
He was a 'living god' during his lifetime, worshipped in his own mortuary temple while still alive. ⇌ His deification was entirely posthumous, developing after his death.
Evidence for A: His mortuary temple was built during his lifetime.
Evidence for B: No evidence of a living cult is found from his lifetime; the healing cult dates to later periods.
What We Don't Know

The full text of his 'Instruction' is lost, with only fragments surviving. The exact layout of his mortuary temple is not fully known due to later destruction. The precise nature of his cult practices in the Greco-Roman period requires more research.
