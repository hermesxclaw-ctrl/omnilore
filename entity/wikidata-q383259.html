Who It Is

Before Imhotep, there was another man who became a god. He was not a king, but a commoner from a small town who, through sheer intelligence and competence, became the most powerful man in Egypt, built monuments that still stand today, and was so revered that he was worshipped as a deity of healing for over a thousand years after his death. This is the story of Amenhotep, son of Hapu, the architect who built his own immortality.

He was known for his intelligence, administrative competence, and diplomatic skill. He was a 'doer' — a man who got things done. He was also known as a sage, whose sayings were collected as wisdom literature. He was a loyal servant to the king, and his behavior set a standard for the ideal Egyptian official.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — His name means 'Amun is satisfied' or 'Amun is content'. The epithet 'son of Hapu' distinguishes him from the pharaohs of the same name, linking him to his father, a minor official named Hapu. He was also given the honorific 'He who is in the presence of the king' and later 'The Chief of the Seers'.

Pronunciation — Ah-men-HO-tep, son of HA-poo

Aliases — Amenophis, son of Hapu, Amenhotep sa Hapu, Huy, The Vizier Amenhotep

Ownership — Ancient Egyptian (18th Dynasty, New Kingdom period)

Powers
Master Architect and Builder

He oversaw the construction of many of Amenhotep III's most monumental projects, including parts of the Temple of Luxor, the mortuary temple of Amenhotep III, and the Colossi of Memnon. His ability to organize massive workforces and manage complex logistics was legendary.

Limit: He was a mortal man, but his power was delegated by the king.

Royal Scribe and Vizier

He held the title of Vizier, making him the highest-ranking official in the land after the king. He was the chief administrator, judge, and advisor. He was the 'ears and eyes' of the king.

Limit: His power was bounded by the king's will.

Divine Intercessor (posthumous)

After his death, he was deified and became a popular god of healing and justice. People would pray to him for oracles, cures, and help in legal disputes. His mortuary temple became a site of pilgrimage, where the sick would sleep to receive healing dreams.

Limit: His powers were granted by the gods Amun and Thoth.

Wisdom and Counsel

His sayings, collected in wisdom texts, were believed to offer practical and moral guidance. He was seen as a sage whose advice could solve problems and prevent disasters.

Limit: His wisdom was mortal, but his posthumous reputation made it divine.

Form & Signs

Appearance — His statues depict him as a mature man, often with a shaved head or short wig, a long kilt, and a scribe's roll or a papyrus in his hands, emphasizing his role as a learned official. He is shown with a somewhat heavy, realistic face, reflecting his age and wisdom. He is never depicted as a god in his own lifetime, but posthumously he is shown wearing the divine beard and holding the ankh.

Symbols — The scribe's roll (papyrus), the writing palette, the ankh (as a deity), the divine beard (as a deity), and the shen ring (as a symbol of protection).

Domains — As a mortal: architecture, public works, administration, and the king's service. As a deified figure: justice, healing, wisdom, and intercession with the gods. He was a patron of scribes and architects.

Accounts I
The King's Right HandBiographical inscriptions on his statues

c. 1390-1353 BCE

Amenhotep, son of Hapu, tells his own story on his statues. He describes his humble birth in the town of Athribis, his rise through the ranks due to his intelligence and competence, and his appointment as chief architect and vizier. He boasts that he was 'one who was brought in because of his excellence, one who was introduced because of his deeds, one who was promoted because of his worth'. He became the most powerful non-royal person in Egypt, and his name was honored for centuries.

Accounts II
The Master of the ColossiArchaeological and historical record

c. 1350 BCE

He was responsible for the construction of the Colossi of Memnon, two massive stone statues of Amenhotep III that stood at the entrance to the king's mortuary temple. The sheer scale of the project, moving and carving stone blocks weighing over 700 tons each, was a testament to his organizational genius. The Colossi have stood for over 3,000 years, a lasting monument to both the king and his architect.

The Deified SageGreco-Roman period texts and temple inscriptions

c. 300 BCE - 300 CE

Centuries after his death, Amenhotep, son of Hapu, was worshipped as a god. He was seen as a healer and an oracle. His temple in Thebes became a famous healing sanctuary, where people would come from all over the Mediterranean to seek cures. He was often identified with the Greek god Asclepius. His cult persisted for over 1,000 years after his death, making him one of the few non-royal Egyptians to achieve full deification.
