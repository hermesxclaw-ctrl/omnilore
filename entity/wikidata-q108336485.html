Who It Is

He never lifts a weapon, never speaks a line, and never appears again — yet without Arneus, the most crowded bedroom in Greek myth would not exist. He is a name in the genitive case, Ἀρνέου, father of Megamede, grandfather of fifty sisters who were all sent, one after another, to the bed of Heracles.

No behavior, speech, personality, motive, or moral character is attributed to Arneus in surviving texts. He does not act, speak, or make decisions in any narrative. His sole function is genealogical — to provide a maternal lineage for the fifty Thespiades, distinguishing them as legitimate daughters of a single queen (Megamede) rather than daughters of concubines. Any personality ascribed to him would be invention. Status: unknown.

Accounts I
The Genealogy of Megamede, Daughter of Arneus (Apollodoran Stemma)Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.4.10

Hellenistic source material compiled 1st-2nd century CE; reflects Classical/earlier Boeotian local history

In the most explicit attestation, Apollodorus records that King Thespius of Thespiae fathered fifty daughters 'by Megamede, daughter of Arneus' (ek Megamedes tes Arneou). This single clause establishes Arneus as maternal grandfather of the entire cohort of Thespiades. The passage immediately follows with the famous episode in which Heracles, while lodged with Thespius during his hunt for the Cithaeronian lion, sleeps with all fifty daughters, who each bear him a son. Arneus himself takes no further part; his function is to anchor the daughters as legitimate offspring of a single queen rather than of concubines. Provides legitimacy and unity to the Thespiades as a sisterhood; their sons by Heracles become the Heraclidae of Thespiae, later colonists. Establishes a rare complete maternal genealogy for a large mythic brood, contrasting with the more common 'daughters by many women' motif.

Heracles and the Fifty Daughters – The Night(s) in ThespiaeApollodorus 2.4.10; Diodorus Siculus 4.29.2-3; Pausanias 9.27.6

Archaic to Hellenistic tradition (earliest possible reference in lost Hesiodic or early lyric; fully attested in Classical mythographers)

While hunting the lion that ravaged the flocks of Thespius and Amphitryon on Mount Cithaeron, Heracles is entertained by Thespius for an extended period. Thespius, eager to have grandchildren by the hero, sends his daughters to Heracles' bed — in Apollodorus in a single night (with one daughter refusing, or all in one night according to variant), in Diodorus over fifty nights (one per night for fifty days), and in Pausanias as a deliberate act of hospitality. Each daughter (all granddaughters of Arneus in the Apollodoran version) conceives a son. The episode is the narrative reason Arneus's genealogy matters at all. Birth of fifty (or fifty-one) sons, the Thespiadai/Heraclidae; Thespius retains one son at home, sends others to colonize Sardinia under Iolaus; establishes Heracles' prodigious virility and Thespius's dynastic strategy. In some versions one daughter (refusing Heracles) becomes a virgin priestess.

The Hunt of the Cithaeronian Lion – The Hospitality that Preceded the SeductionApollodorus 2.4.9-10; Diodorus 4.29.2

Classical/Hellenistic

Before the episode with the daughters, Heracles at age eighteen is sent to hunt the lion of Cithaeron (or Thespiae), which preyed on the cattle of Amphitryon and Thespius. Thespius hosts the young hero for fifty days. During this hunt Heracles kills the lion and wears its skin (a precursor to the Nemean lion). The hospitality context explains why Thespius had access to Heracles for so long and why the daughters-as-gifts motif is framed as xenia. Arneus is not mentioned in the hunt itself, but his granddaughters are the reward Thespius offers at its conclusion. Establishes Heracles' first heroic hunt, his early lion-skin, and the debt of hospitality that Thespius exploits. Provides chronological anchor: Heracles is still beardless and unmarried, before his labors for Eurystheus.

Accounts II
The Anonymous Mothers Tradition – The Counter-Stemma Without ArneusDiodorus Siculus 4.29.3; Pausanias 9.27.6; Athenaeus Deipnosophistae 13.4 (556F) citing Herodorus; Hyginus Fabulae 162

Late Classical to Imperial (4th century BCE - 2nd century CE)

In the dominant alternative tradition, Thespius's fifty daughters are explicitly NOT the children of one wife. Diodorus says they were born 'from his concubines' (ek pallakidon), Athenaeus citing Herodorus says Thespius 'kept his daughters unwed to give them to Heracles,' and Pausanias simply omits any wife. In this version Arneus and Megamede do not exist; the daughters are a collective harem-offspring. The contrast illuminates ancient mythographic choices: Apollodorus's Megamede/Arneus lineage may reflect a Boeotian attempt to legitimize the daughters as children of a single astē queen, while the pallakides version emphasizes Thespius's quasi-oriental fertility and Heracles' hyper-potency. If Arneus is omitted, the daughters have no maternal grandfather and no single maternal lineage; their status shifts from legitimate princesses to daughters of a household of concubines, affecting how later sources treat their sons' legitimacy. Modern scholars (Gantz, Hard) cite this as evidence of competing local histories.

The Aftermath – Sons of the Thespiades and the Sardinian ColonizationApollodorus 2.4.11-2.7.6; Diodorus 4.29.3-4.30.1; Pausanias 10.17.5

Archaic colonization myth retrojected into heroic age; attested from 5th century BCE

After Heracles fathers sons on the fifty daughters — all grandsons of Arneus in the Apollodoran stemma — Heracles instructs Thespius to keep seven sons in Thespiae, send three to Thebes, and send the remaining forty with his nephew Iolaus to colonize Sardinia. This aetiology explains Sardinian Greek presence and Thespian Heraclid clans. In Apollodorus, the sons are explicitly 'sons of Heracles by the daughters of Thespius, daughters of Megamede daughter of Arneus,' making Arneus ancestor of a colonial lineage. One daughter who refused Heracles is made a priestess and remains virgin. Foundation myth for Sardinia (Heracleian colonization), for Thespian aristocratic families claiming descent from Heracles, and for a priesthood of Heracles at Thespiae. Gives Arneus, though never acting, a genealogical footprint across the Mediterranean.

Thespiae and the Cult of Eros – The Civic Context of Arneus's FamilyPausanias 9.27.1-9.31.3; Diodorus 4.29; Strabo 9.2.27

Classical to Imperial; cult attested from 6th century BCE

Thespiae, the city of Thespius and Megamede (and thus of Arneus's descendants), was famous not for Heracles but for Eros — housing Praxiteles' Eros statue and the Erotidia festival. Pausanias describes Thespiae as a city that celebrated love and fertility, which contextualizes the myth of fifty daughters offered to a hero as a civic fertility/ hospitality myth. While Arneus is never linked to Eros cult, his family's city provides the religious backdrop that may have made the mass-marriage myth plausible and even celebratory in local memory. Strabo notes Thespiae's territory at the foot of Helicon, fertile and populous. Explains why Thespiae preserved and promoted the 50-daughters myth as civic pride despite its strangeness; links genealogical myth to historical cult and festival. No direct consequence for Arneus, but situates his phantom genealogy in a real polis landscape.

Arneus was a king or god. — No source calls Arneus a king, god, or hero with powers. He is simply named as father of Megamede; his status, rank, and polis are unattested. The title 'king' belongs to his son-in-law Thespius, not to him.

Arneus is the same as Arnaeus/Irus, the beggar in the Odyssey. — Arnaeus (Ἀρναῖος) in Odyssey 18.1-7 is a distinct Ithacan beggar nicknamed Irus; his name is similar but not identical (Arnaeus vs. Arneus) and his story, date, and region are wholly separate. Ancient and modern lexica (Smith, Grimal) explicitly separate them.

The fifty daughters were all daughters of Megamede in every version of the myth. — Only Apollodorus gives Megamede daughter of Arneus as sole mother. The more widely attested version (Diodorus, Pausanias, Hyginus) makes the daughters children of many unnamed concubines, with no mention of Arneus at all. Arneus's granddaughters are thus 50 in one variant, zero in another.

Arneus appears in art or has a known depiction. — No vase, sculpture, coin, or painting has ever been identified as Arneus. He is a purely textual genealogical name with no iconography. Any image labeled 'Arneus' online is misattributed or AI-generated.
