Who It Is

Clay does not lie, and the oldest great story we possess was pressed into it while the walls of Nineveh still stood. Before Homer, before the Bible's scribes set down Genesis, before Achilles sulked in his tent, there was Gilgamesh of Uruk — two-thirds divine, one-third mortal, and a hundred percent a problem. He built the first great city, then tyrannized its people so brutally that the gods had to grow a wild man out of the steppe just to fight him. They brawled, became the closest of friends, chopped down a sacred forest, fought a goddess and killed her cosmic bull — and then Gilgamesh learned the single hardest lesson there is: that even he must die. His flight from that fate carried him across the Waters of Death to the one man the gods had made immortal, and it changed nothing. Attested: opens the epic as an arrogant, oppressive ruler who exercises the 'right of the first night' over new brides and exhausts his people; the gods create Enkidu as a counterweight. He is driven by a hunger for fame and renown (turning from kingship to monster-slaying to build a name). After Enkidu's death he becomes a grieving, desperate wanderer obsessed with escaping death; he fails every trial (sleep, the plant) and returns to Uruk with wisdom, mourning 'will never find the life you seek: when the gods created man they set death as his share'. Powers
Divine/Superhuman Strength

Attested: as a demigod ('two-thirds divine, one-third mortal') Gilgamesh possesses superhuman physical strength. He wrestles the wild man Enkidu to a draw (winning on stamina), kills the Bull of Heaven, and hews down cedars in the sacred forest on his own aspiration.

Limit: His strength is not absolute. He cannot defeat the Bull of Heaven alone (it swallows vast amounts of life and opens pits before Enkidu seizes it by the tail/horns); he panics and needs Enkidu's encouragement before the fight with Humbaba; he is finally undone by mortality itself.

Kingship, Ritual Authority and Building

Attested: king of Uruk with divine favour (Shamash is protector of the Uruk dynasty; Ninsun his divine mother intercedes). He commands warriors, administers justice, and builds the great walls of Uruk, credited by the Tummal Inscription. In the Sumerian poem 'Gilgamesh and Aga' he leads Uruk's successful revolt against Kish and mercifully forgives his defeated foe.

Limit: His authority is initially illegitimate in practice: the citizens cry out to the gods, who send Enkidu to curb him. The gods themselves punish the overreach (Humbaba's death, the Bull) with Enkidu's fatal illness.

Heroic Endurance and Journeying

Attested: he crosses seven mountains to the Cedar Forest; passes through the scorpion-men's tunnel of the sun at Mount Mashu in twelve 'double hours' just ahead of being burned alive; crosses the Waters of Death by cutting 120 punting poles; descends to the sea-bed with stones bound to his feet to seize the plant of rejuvenation.

Limit: He fails the fundamental trials set by Utnapishtim: he cannot stay awake six days and seven nights, and loses the plant to a snake. His endurance is impressive but cosmically futile. Attested in the Sippar (Old Babylonian) tablet rather than the later standard version is Shamash's separate warning of the quest's futility.

Dream Interpretation and Precognition (reconstructed)

Attested indirectly: Gilgamesh has five terrifying dreams on the road to the Cedar Forest, which Enkidu (and his mother Ninsun at the start) interpret. In the Pennsylvania (Old Babylonian) tablet Ninsun is credited with dream interpretation for him.

Limit: The dreams are omens of Humbaba, and Enkidu deliberately glosses them as good fortune; interpretation is human, not a supernatural power Gilgamesh controls.

Posthumous Deification / Judge of the Dead

Attested: by the later Early Dynastic period Gilgamesh was worshipped as a god at various sites in Sumer; king Utu-hengal (21st century BCE) adopted him as patron deity; the Third-Dynasty kings of Ur called him their 'divine brother'. 'The Death of Gilgamesh' has him arrayed after death as a judge in the Underworld beside Ningishzida and Dumuzi.

Limit: This is posthumous worship, not a power in life; his immortality is civic and religious, not bodily.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Attested: described as two-thirds divine and one-third mortal, with a body 'perfect in all its parts'. Depicted in genuine Mesopotamian art (clay plaques, cylinder seals) in scenes of fighting Humbaba or the Bull of Heaven; on the Golden bowl of Hasanlu he strides in a lion-skin kilt carrying a bow. The Creation and Taming of EnkiduStandard Babylonian Epic, Tablets I-II; Sumerian 'Gilgamesh, Enkidu, and the Netherworld' (variant)

Standard edition c. 1300–1000 BCE; Sumerian traditions c. 2100 BCE

Gilgamesh, two-thirds god and one-third man, oppresses Uruk: for the young women this takes the form of his claiming brides on their wedding night; for the young men (tablet damaged) presumably exhausting games, tests of strength, or forced labour. The people cry to the gods, who direct the mother-goddess Aruru to make an equal: Enkidu, an abundantly hairy 'wild man' living with the herds in the steppe. A trapper, whose livelihood Enkidu ruins by destroying his traps, reports him; Gilgamesh sends the sacred prostitute Shamhat to seduce him. After days of intimacy Enkidu is 'weakened' (civilized): his herd flees, and Shamhat consoles him, 'Do not grieve, you now have knowledge, like the gods.' She takes him to the shepherds' camp where he learns to eat bread, drink beer, and guard flocks. Learning of Gilgamesh's treatment of new brides, Enkidu travels to Uruk, blocks Gilgamesh at the wedding chamber, and they fight. After a fierce bout Gilgamesh wins, and they become close friends. The Slaying of Humbaba (Huwawa) in the Cedar ForestStandard Babylonian Epic, Tablets III-V; Sumerian 'Gilgamesh and Huwawa'

Standard edition c. 1300–1000 BCE; Sumerian c. 2100 BCE

To build a name for himself, Gilgamesh proposes an expedition to the Cedar Forest to cut precious timber and kill its guardian Humbaba, a monstrous demi-god appointed by Enlil. Warnings from Enkidu (who lived there as a wild man), the elders, and the city council do not deter him; Ninsun adopts Enkidu and petitions Shamash, protector of the Uruk dynasty, for their safety. Crossing seven mountains with fifty volunteers, they camp and read dreaming omens, then confront Humbaba, who threatens to disembowel Gilgamesh and feed his flesh to the birds. Gilgamesh is terrified, but with Enkidu's encouragement he fights; Shamash sends thirteen winds to bind the monster. Humbaba pleads for mercy and offers to serve Gilgamesh, but Enkidu argues that killing him will establish his fame forever. The heroes decapitate him (and his seven 'auras' or sons in some versions), cut down the great cedars, build a raft, and return down the Euphrates with the giant tree and Humbaba's head. The Rejection of Ishtar and the Bull of HeavenStandard Babylonian Epic, Tablet VI; Sumerian 'Gilgamesh and the Bull of Heaven'

Standard edition c. 1300–1000 BCE; Sumerian c. 2100 BCE

Returning clean and glorious to Uruk, Gilgamesh bathes and puts on royal robes; the goddess Ishtar (Inanna), 'fascinated by the beauty of Gilgamesh', proposes marriage, offering wealth and fame. Gilgamesh refuses, cataloguing the disastrous fates of her previous lovers (including Tammuz/Dumuzi). Enraged and weeping, Ishtar goes to her father Anu and demands the Bull of Heaven, threatening to raise the dead to devour the living and to scream so loudly she is heard by heaven and earth. Anu relents but warns Uruk will face seven years of famine; Ishtar provides provisions for seven years. The Bull wreaks devastation — drinking the rivers dry, opening pits that swallow three hundred men. Without divine aid Enkidu seizes it and Gilgamesh kills it; they offer its heart to Shamash. When Ishtar cries out, Enkidu hurls one of its hindquarters at her face, saying that if he could he would treat her the same way and 'lash her entrails to her side'. Accounts II
The Death of Enkidu and the Quest for ImmortalityStandard Babylonian Epic, Tablets VII-XI; Sippar/Meissner Old Babylonian fragments

Standard edition c. 1300–1000 BCE; Old Babylonian c. 1800 BCE

Enkidu dreams that the gods (Anu, Ea, Shamash) declare one hero must die for the two crimes; they choose Enkidu. His condition worsens over twelve days; he curses Shamhat and the door he made, then repents and blesses her, and laments he could not die heroically in battle. He dies; Gilgamesh clings to the corpse, denying the death until a maggot drops from its nose. Gilgamesh laments, tears his hair and clothes, commissions a funerary statue, and heaps grave-gifts so Enkidu is received well among the dead. Fearful now of his own death, Gilgamesh abandons kingship, wraps himself in skins, and wanders to seek Utnapishtim, 'the Faraway', the only human the gods made immortal. He slays lions for clothing, passes the scorpion-men and the tunnel of the sun at Mount Mashu, crosses the Garden of the Gods, meets the veiled alewife Siduri (who urges him to accept death and enjoy life — the famous 'carpe diem' counsel, and one of the epic's most-quoted passages), destoys Urshanabi's stone charms and so must cut 120 punting poles to cross the Waters of Death, and finally reaches Utnapishtim. Gilgamesh, Enkidu and the Netherworld (and Tablet XII)Sumerian poem 'Gilgamesh, Enkidu, and the Netherworld'; adapted as Tablet XII of the Standard Babylonian Epic

Sumerian c. 2100 BCE; adapted c. 1300–1000 BCE

In the Sumerian poem, the goddess Inanna moves a huluppu tree to her garden, but a serpent 'who knows no charm', the Anzû-bird, and the demon Lilitu invade it. Gilgamesh (here her brother) slays the serpent and drives the others off; she rewards him with a drum (pikku/ellag) and drumsticks (mikku/ekidma), which he loses, dropping into the Netherworld. Enkidu volunteers to retrieve them but breaks the underworld's strict rules by behaving as if alive, and is held forever. Gilgamesh begs the gods; Shamash (or Enki) opens a crack in the earth and Enkidu's shade rises, describing the grim conditions of the dead to his friend. Tablet XII is largely a word-for-word Akkadian translation of this Sumerian story, appended to the epic. Its Enkidu is alive again and Gilgamesh is kind to Ishtar — both inconsistencies with Tablets I-XI, marking it as an 'inorganic appendage' that rounds the twelve-tablet series on the theme of 'seeing' (understanding). The Revolt against Aga of KishSumerian poem 'Gilgamesh and Aga'

c. 2100 BCE

Uruk refuses to dig wells for the benefit of Kish, whose king Aga (Agga) holds hegemony over Sumer and besieges the city. Gilgamesh first asks the city elders to fight, and when they refuse, appeals to the assembly of the young men, who accept. Enkidu is sent to prepare the weapons and await Gilgamesh's order. Urged to yield nearly at the point of defeat, Gilgamesh holds out; in the climax he defeats the Kish army, captures Aga, but spares him, sending him back humiliated to Kish. The poem is a court-centred story about counsel (elders vs assembly), mercy in victory, and Uruk's standing — with no corresponding episode in the later Akkadian epic. Uruk wins its independence from Kish; Gilgamesh demonstrates the virtue of magnanimity toward a defeated rival (a theme also shadowed in the Humbaba episode).

The Death of Gilgamesh and His GlorificationSumerian poem 'The Death of Gilgamesh'; fragment from Mê-Turan (Tell Haddad)

c. 2100 BCE

Gilgamesh, grown old, dreams of how the gods will judge his fate after death. He takes counsel, prepares an elaborate funeral, offers gifts and sacrifices to the gods, and is promised a rank in the Underworld equal to that of the faithful servant-god Ningishzida. Comforted, he dies, and the Mê-Turan fragment relates that he was buried beneath the river bed, the men of Uruk temporarily diverting the flow of the Euphrates for the burial. He is then consecrated as a semi-god reigning and giving judgement over the dead, reunited with his family, priests, warriors, and his best friend Enkidu. Gilgamesh is fully deified as a judge of the underworld, an outcome that frames the mortal, despairing hero of the Akkadian epic against his enduring cult as a numinous judge.

Gilgamesh is a purely fictional character. — He appears in the Sumerian King List as the 5th king of Uruk with a (mythic) 126-year reign, and a contemporary name-fragment and later inscriptions reference him. Most historians accept a historical king likely existed, though his life is almost entirely obscured by legend.

The Epic of Gilgamesh is the oldest 'story' and simply a monster-hunt adventure. — It is the oldest surviving great work of narrative literature, but it is a composite of older Sumerian poems; and its central themes are friendship, grief, mortality and kingship rather than mere monster-slaying. The creature-fights are a means to a reflective end.

Utnapishtim's flood is a copy of Noah's Ark. — The opposite: the Gilgamesh/Atra-Hasis flood (Tablet XI, predating Genesis by centuries) is the older account from which the biblical narrative likely derives, or with which it shares a common Mesopotamian ancestor. Any direct 'copying' language must carry the Delitzsch/Panbabylonian baggage.

Gilgamesh is 'the biblical Nimrod' (or a solar god whose 12 tablets are the zodiac). — Both are discredited 19th/early-20th-century identifications with no modern scholarly support.

Gilgamesh losing the plant to a snake is a late, throwaway detail. — It is a central aetiological moment — explaining why snakes shed their skin and appear immortal — and mirrors the 'missed immortality' motif in the Odyssey (Odysseus leaving Calypso) and Alexander legends.

Gilgamesh's 'right of the first night' was a real ancient practice. — It is almost certainly a literary device in the epic to dramatise tyranny, not a documented historical custom of Uruk.
