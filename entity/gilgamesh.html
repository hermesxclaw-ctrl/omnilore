Who It Is

Gilgamesh is the protagonist of the oldest surviving great work of literature — a Sumerian king whose story, preserved on clay tablets in cuneiform, explores friendship, mortality, the fear of death, and the limits of human ambition with a profundity that still resonates 4,000 years later. Historical records confirm that Gilgamesh was a real king of Uruk in southern Mesopotamia during the Early Dynastic III period (c. 2600 BCE), where he was celebrated for building the great walls of the city. Within a few centuries, his deeds had passed into legend, and by c. 2100 BCE, Sumerian poets composed a cycle of poems about his exploits. The Standard Babylonian version of the Epic of Gilgamesh — 'He Who Saw the Deep' — was compiled by the scribe Sin-leqi-unninni around 1200 BCE and was discovered in the library of the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal at Nineveh in the 19th century. The epic opens with Gilgamesh as a tyrannical king, two-thirds divine and one-third mortal, who oppresses his people. The gods create a wild man, Enkidu, to challenge him. After a wrestling match that ends in mutual respect, Gilgamesh and Enkidu become inseparable companions. Together, they journey to the Cedar Forest to slay the monster Humbaba (its guardian, appointed by the god Enlil) — an act of heroic hubris that has lasting consequences. Gilgamesh then spurns the advances of the goddess Ishtar, who sends the Bull of Heaven to destroy Uruk; Gilgamesh and Enkidu slay it, but the gods decree that Enkidu must die. The second half of the epic is Gilgamesh's profound response to Enkidu's death: a terrified, grief-stricken quest for immortality. He travels to the ends of the earth to find Utnapishtim, the survivor of the Great Flood (a Mesopotamian Noah figure) who was granted eternal life by the gods. Utnapishtim tests Gilgamesh with the challenge to stay awake for seven days (Gilgamesh immediately falls asleep, proving his mortality) but ultimately reveals the secret of a plant that restores youth, growing at the bottom of the sea. Gilgamesh retrieves it but loses it to a serpent while bathing — the serpent sheds its skin and departs, explaining why snakes can rejuvenate and humans cannot. Gilgamesh returns to Uruk empty-handed but transformed: having accepted his mortality, he finds meaning not in eternal life but in the lasting works of civilization — the walls of Uruk, the legacy of his kingship. The epic's exploration of friendship, the terror of death, and the search for meaning in the face of mortality makes it one of the most enduring works in human history, a direct ancestor to Homer, the Bible (the Flood narrative is strikingly parallel to Genesis 6-9), and every subsequent hero's journey.
