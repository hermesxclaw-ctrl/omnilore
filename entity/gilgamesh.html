You who have fought with lions and traveled through mountains, why are you so afraid of death? I will go before you and shout, 'Go forward! Do not be afraid.' If I fall, I will have established my fame.

Who It Is

Gilgamesh is the protagonist of the oldest surviving great work of literature — a Sumerian king whose story, preserved on clay tablets in cuneiform, explores friendship, mortality, the fear of death, and the limits of human ambition with a profundity that still resonates 4,000 years later. Historical records confirm that Gilgamesh was a real king of Uruk in southern Mesopotamia during the Early Dynastic III period (c. 2600 BCE), where he was celebrated for building the great walls of the city. Within a few centuries, his deeds had passed into legend, and by c. 2100 BCE, Sumerian poets composed a cycle of poems about his exploits. The Standard Babylonian version of the Epic of Gilgamesh — 'He Who Saw the Deep' — was compiled by the scribe Sin-leqi-unninni around 1200 BCE and was discovered in the library of the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal at Nineveh in the 19th century. The epic opens with Gilgamesh as a tyrannical king, two-thirds divine and one-third mortal, who oppresses his people. The gods create a wild man, Enkidu, to challenge him. After a wrestling match that ends in mutual respect, Gilgamesh and Enkidu become inseparable companions. Together, they journey to the Cedar Forest to slay the monster Humbaba (its guardian, appointed by the god Enlil) — an act of heroic hubris that has lasting consequences. Gilgamesh then spurns the advances of the goddess Ishtar, who sends the Bull of Heaven to destroy Uruk; Gilgamesh and Enkidu slay it, but the gods decree that Enkidu must die. The second half of the epic is Gilgamesh's profound response to Enkidu's death: a terrified, grief-stricken quest for immortality. He travels to the ends of the earth to find Utnapishtim, the survivor of the Great Flood (a Mesopotamian Noah figure) who was granted eternal life by the gods. Utnapishtim tests Gilgamesh with the challenge to stay awake for seven days (Gilgamesh immediately falls asleep, proving his mortality) but ultimately reveals the secret of a plant that restores youth, growing at the bottom of the sea. Gilgamesh retrieves it but loses it to a serpent while bathing — the serpent sheds its skin and departs, explaining why snakes can rejuvenate and humans cannot. Gilgamesh returns to Uruk empty-handed but transformed: having accepted his mortality, he finds meaning not in eternal life but in the lasting works of civilization — the walls of Uruk, the legacy of his kingship. The epic's exploration of friendship, the terror of death, and the search for meaning in the face of mortality makes it one of the most enduring works in human history, a direct ancestor to Homer, the Bible (the Flood narrative is strikingly parallel to Genesis 6-9), and every subsequent hero's journey.

Form

Attested: described as two-thirds divine and one-third mortal, with a body 'perfect in all its parts'. Depicted in genuine Mesopotamian art (clay plaques, cylinder seals) in scenes of fighting Humbaba or the Bull of Heaven; on the Golden bowl of Hasanlu he strides in a lion-skin kilt carrying a bow. Reconstructed: popular identification of any long-haired heroic figure with four or six hair-curls as Gilgamesh is known to be incorrect. Emphasis on his physical beauty and lion-like strength recurs across versions.

Powers

Divine/Superhuman Strength Attested: as a demigod ('two-thirds divine, one-third mortal') Gilgamesh possesses superhuman physical strength. He wrestles the wild man Enkidu to a draw (winning on stamina), kills the Bull of Heaven, and hews down cedars in the sacred forest on his own aspiration. Standard Babylonian Epic; Sumerian poems His strength is not absolute. He cannot defeat the Bull of Heaven alone (it swallows vast amounts of life and opens pits before Enkidu seizes it by the tail/horns); he panics and needs Enkidu's encouragement before the fight with Humbaba; he is finally undone by mortality itself. The epic foregrounds his strength but continually shows him needing his companion, reinforcing that his heroism is collective (even Shamash's winds bind Humbaba).

Kingship, Ritual Authority and Building Attested: king of Uruk with divine favour (Shamash is protector of the Uruk dynasty; Ninsun his divine mother intercedes). He commands warriors, administers justice, and builds the great walls of Uruk, credited by the Tummal Inscription. In the Sumerian poem 'Gilgamesh and Aga' he leads Uruk's successful revolt against Kish and mercifully forgives his defeated foe. All versions His authority is initially illegitimate in practice: the citizens cry out to the gods, who send Enkidu to curb him. The gods themselves punish the overreach (Humbaba's death, the Bull) with Enkidu's fatal illness. The King List credits him a mythic 126-year reign. As a deified king he is also a judge of the dead in the Underworld (prayers on clay address him as such), but this underworld role is distinct from the mortal king of the epic's opening.

Heroic Endurance and Journeying Attested: he crosses seven mountains to the Cedar Forest; passes through the scorpion-men's tunnel of the sun at Mount Mashu in twelve 'double hours' just ahead of being burned alive; crosses the Waters of Death by cutting 120 punting poles; descends to the sea-bed with stones bound to his feet to seize the plant of rejuvenation. Standard Babylonian Epic, Tablets IV-V and IX-XI He fails the fundamental trials set by Utnapishtim: he cannot stay awake six days and seven nights, and loses the plant to a snake. His endurance is impressive but cosmically futile. Attested in the Sippar (Old Babylonian) tablet rather than the later standard version is Shamash's separate warning of the quest's futility. None major across versions, though the routing differs (Old Babylonian Sippar version has Siduri send him back to Uruk, with Utnapishtim 'not originally part of the tale' as reconstructed from the Meissner fragment).

Dream Interpretation and Precognition (reconstructed) Attested indirectly: Gilgamesh has five terrifying dreams on the road to the Cedar Forest, which Enkidu (and his mother Ninsun at the start) interpret. In the Pennsylvania (Old Babylonian) tablet Ninsun is credited with dream interpretation for him. Standard Babylonian Epic, Tablets I and IV; Pennsylvania tablet The dreams are omens of Humbaba, and Enkidu deliberately glosses them as good fortune; interpretation is human, not a supernatural power Gilgamesh controls. None.

Posthumous Deification / Judge of the Dead Attested: by the later Early Dynastic period Gilgamesh was worshipped as a god at various sites in Sumer; king Utu-hengal (21st century BCE) adopted him as patron deity; the Third-Dynasty kings of Ur called him their 'divine brother'. 'The Death of Gilgamesh' has him arrayed after death as a judge in the Underworld beside Ningishzida and Dumuzi. Sumerian poems; Third Dynasty of Ur royal inscriptions This is posthumous worship, not a power in life; his immortality is civic and religious, not bodily. The epic's central tension is exactly the reverse: Gilgamesh the living king cannot achieve bodily immortality, even though the dead Gilgamesh the deified king is immortalised in cult.

Accounts

Gilgamesh, two-thirds god and one-third man, oppresses Uruk: for the young women this takes the form of his claiming brides on their wedding night; for the young men (tablet damaged) presumably exhausting games, tests of strength, or forced labour. The people cry to the gods, who direct the mother-goddess Aruru to make an equal: Enkidu, an abundantly hairy 'wild man' living with the herds in the steppe. A trapper, whose livelihood Enkidu ruins by destroying his traps, reports him; Gilgamesh sends the sacred prostitute Shamhat to seduce him. After days of intimacy Enkidu is 'weakened' (civilized): his herd flees, and Shamhat consoles him, 'Do not grieve, you now have knowledge, like the gods.' She takes him to the shepherds' camp where he learns to eat bread, drink beer, and guard flocks. Learning of Gilgamesh's treatment of new brides, Enkidu travels to Uruk, blocks Gilgamesh at the wedding chamber, and they fight. After a fierce bout Gilgamesh wins, and they become close friends.

To build a name for himself, Gilgamesh proposes an expedition to the Cedar Forest to cut precious timber and kill its guardian Humbaba, a monstrous demi-god appointed by Enlil. Warnings from Enkidu (who lived there as a wild man), the elders, and the city council do not deter him; Ninsun adopts Enkidu and petitions Shamash, protector of the Uruk dynasty, for their safety. Crossing seven mountains with fifty volunteers, they camp and read dreaming omens, then confront Humbaba, who threatens to disembowel Gilgamesh and feed his flesh to the birds. Gilgamesh is terrified, but with Enkidu's encouragement he fights; Shamash sends thirteen winds to bind the monster. Humbaba pleads for mercy and offers to serve Gilgamesh, but Enkidu argues that killing him will establish his fame forever. The heroes decapitate him (and his seven 'auras' or sons in some versions), cut down the great cedars, build a raft, and return down the Euphrates with the giant tree and Humbaba's head.

Returning clean and glorious to Uruk, Gilgamesh bathes and puts on royal robes; the goddess Ishtar (Inanna), 'fascinated by the beauty of Gilgamesh', proposes marriage, offering wealth and fame. Gilgamesh refuses, cataloguing the disastrous fates of her previous lovers (including Tammuz/Dumuzi). Enraged and weeping, Ishtar goes to her father Anu and demands the Bull of Heaven, threatening to raise the dead to devour the living and to scream so loudly she is heard by heaven and earth. Anu relents but warns Uruk will face seven years of famine; Ishtar provides provisions for seven years. The Bull wreaks devastation — drinking the rivers dry, opening pits that swallow three hundred men. Without divine aid Enkidu seizes it and Gilgamesh kills it; they offer its heart to Shamash. When Ishtar cries out, Enkidu hurls one of its hindquarters at her face, saying that if he could he would treat her the same way and 'lash her entrails to her side'. Uruk celebrates; Enkidu has an ominous dream of his coming punishment.

Enkidu dreams that the gods (Anu, Ea, Shamash) declare one hero must die for the two crimes; they choose Enkidu. His condition worsens over twelve days; he curses Shamhat and the door he made, then repents and blesses her, and laments he could not die heroically in battle. He dies; Gilgamesh clings to the corpse, denying the death until a maggot drops from its nose. Gilgamesh laments, tears his hair and clothes, commissions a funerary statue, and heaps grave-gifts so Enkidu is received well among the dead. Fearful now of his own death, Gilgamesh abandons kingship, wraps himself in skins, and wanders to seek Utnapishtim, 'the Faraway', the only human the gods made immortal. He slays lions for clothing, passes the scorpion-men and the tunnel of the sun at Mount Mashu, crosses the Garden of the Gods, meets the veiled alewife Siduri (who urges him to accept death and enjoy life — the famous 'carpe diem' counsel, and one of the epic's most-quoted passages), destoys Urshanabi's stone charms and so must cut 120 punting poles to cross the Waters of Death, and finally reaches Utnapishtim.

In the Sumerian poem, the goddess Inanna moves a huluppu tree to her garden, but a serpent 'who knows no charm', the Anzû-bird, and the demon Lilitu invade it. Gilgamesh (here her brother) slays the serpent and drives the others off; she rewards him with a drum (pikku/ellag) and drumsticks (mikku/ekidma), which he loses, dropping into the Netherworld. Enkidu volunteers to retrieve them but breaks the underworld's strict rules by behaving as if alive, and is held forever. Gilgamesh begs the gods; Shamash (or Enki) opens a crack in the earth and Enkidu's shade rises, describing the grim conditions of the dead to his friend. Tablet XII is largely a word-for-word Akkadian translation of this Sumerian story, appended to the epic. Its Enkidu is alive again and Gilgamesh is kind to Ishtar — both inconsistencies with Tablets I-XI, marking it as an 'inorganic appendage' that rounds the twelve-tablet series on the theme of 'seeing' (understanding).

Uruk refuses to dig wells for the benefit of Kish, whose king Aga (Agga) holds hegemony over Sumer and besieges the city. Gilgamesh first asks the city elders to fight, and when they refuse, appeals to the assembly of the young men, who accept. Enkidu is sent to prepare the weapons and await Gilgamesh's order. Urged to yield nearly at the point of defeat, Gilgamesh holds out; in the climax he defeats the Kish army, captures Aga, but spares him, sending him back humiliated to Kish. The poem is a court-centred story about counsel (elders vs assembly), mercy in victory, and Uruk's standing — with no corresponding episode in the later Akkadian epic.

Gilgamesh, grown old, dreams of how the gods will judge his fate after death. He takes counsel, prepares an elaborate funeral, offers gifts and sacrifices to the gods, and is promised a rank in the Underworld equal to that of the faithful servant-god Ningishzida. Comforted, he dies, and the Mê-Turan fragment relates that he was buried beneath the river bed, the men of Uruk temporarily diverting the flow of the Euphrates for the burial. He is then consecrated as a semi-god reigning and giving judgement over the dead, reunited with his family, priests, warriors, and his best friend Enkidu.

Content Notes

The epic's opening is a portrait of abuse of power: Gilgamesh claims the right to deflower brides on their wedding night and exhausts his people through forced games, tests of strength, or labour. This is not celebrated as heroism — the citizens cry to the gods, and the whole epic is set in motion as correction. The 'right of the first night' (ius primae noctis) is not attested as a real historical practice in ancient Uruk; it is generally read as a literary device to dramatise his oppression and justify Enkidu's creation.

Modern scholarship treats the 'right of the first night' as almost certainly a literary convention rather than historical practice. The tablet's damage also makes the young men's oppression a matter of conjecture (games, strength-tests, or forced labour).

Standard Babylonian Epic, Tablet I; scholarly discussion

Gilgamesh and Enkidu kill the divine guardian Humbaba even as he pleads for mercy, then cut the sacred cedars — an act read as both ecological overreach and hubristic transgression against Enlil. In some versions his seven 'auras' or sons are slain too. The epic does not frame this as unambiguously heroic; it is the first crossing of the line that brings divine punishment.

The felling of the cedars and slaying of Humbaba has been interpreted through environmentalist, colonialist and 'wilderness-violation' lenses. The 1990s 'Green Movement' reading (Enkidu's death symbolising man's separation from nature) is a modern reception rather than an ancient claim.

Standard Babylonian Epic, Tablets III-V; Sumerian 'Gilgamesh and Huwawa'; Ishchali tablet

For killing Humbaba and the Bull of Heaven, the gods decree that one hero must die; Enkidu is chosen and dies of a wasting sickness over twelve days. Gilgamesh's grief is overwhelming. This is a story about an innocent-bearing punishment for the pair's shared deeds, and the arbitrariness of divine justice.

The punishment foregrounds Mesopotamian anxieties about mortal hubris versus divine order, and the theme that heroic fame cannot outrun divine sanction.

Standard Babylonian Epic, Tablets VII-VIII

Shamhat tames the wild Enkidu through extended intimacy. This is non-coercive (Enkidu is an adult wildman who consents), but it is a sexual civilising process described explicitly, and it transfers 'knowledge' at the cost of his wild strength. Enkidu later curses Shamhat on his deathbed before repenting.

The Adam-and-Eve/Sex-and-the-City-ish civilising metaphor is a standard reading; its explicit sexuality is often softened in bowdlerised translations.

Standard Babylonian Epic, Tablets I-II, VII

After rediscovery in 1849, the epic became a weapon in the Babel und Bibel ('Babylon and Bible') controversy. Friedrich Delitzsch's 1902 lecture argued Genesis' flood was copied from Gilgamesh; his views hardened by 1920-21 (Die große Täuschung) into an antisemitic claim that the Hebrew Bible was 'contaminated' by Babylonian influence and that the Old Testament should be eliminated for a 'true, Aryan' Christianity. Peter Jensen's Panbabylonianism declared nearly all biblical figures (even Jesus) 'nothing but' copies of Gilgamesh — a theory rejected by mainstream scholars.

The flood's Mesopotamian antecedents are real, but Delitzsch and Jensen's supersessionist, antisemitic and ahistorical extremes are discredited; the Religionsgeschichtliche Schule (Hermann Gunkel) explicitly refuted them.

Intellectual-history account in 'Gilgamesh in the arts and popular culture'; Delitzsch lectures; history of Assyriology

Saddam Hussein, who saw himself in Gilgamesh's shadow, wrote Zabibah and the King (2000), an allegory for the Gulf War casting a brutal king who grows just through a commoner woman; in 2003 he compared himself to the epic hero when pressed to step down. Archaeologists had earlier rebuilt the walls of Babylon under his regime in a propagandistic revival of Mesopotamian heritage.

A documented example of a modern autocrat appropriating an ancient epic for legitimacy; its self-serving framing requires critical distance.

What We Don't Know

The most significant gap is the unrecoverable historical core: no contemporary royal inscription from Gilgamesh's own hypothetical reign names him, so his historicity rests on the King List, the Ur name-fragment, and later commemorative inscriptions. The Standard Babylonian Epic is incomplete — about two-thirds recovered — with damaged tablets (especially the young-men oppression on Tablet I and parts of VII, VIII, X) forcing reconstruction from fragments and older versions. The Sapaudian 'Seven Auras' detail, the Tell Haddad burial account, and Aelian's Greek birth legend are each single-source. No primary-language philological engagement was possible in this tick beyond the English translation corpus and the ETCSL English renderings — original Akkadian/Sumerian text was not directly consulted. The precise shade of the Gilgamesh-Enkidu relationship, the transmission channel for any Homeric influence, and the exact Genesis-flood mechanism all remain open (see contradictions). Modern-reception coverage is drawn from the 'popular culture' register and is representative, not exhaustive — a work-by-work bibliography (all novels, operas, games) was not compiled. Language limitations: Armenian/Kurdish and Turkish-language secondary works (e.g. Melih Cevdet Anday's poetry) were not read.

A way to hold it in your mind

Clay does not lie, and the oldest great story we possess was pressed into it while the walls of Nineveh still stood. Before Homer, before the Bible's scribes set down Genesis, before Achilles sulked in his tent, there was Gilgamesh of Uruk — two-thirds divine, one-third mortal, and a hundred percent a problem. He built the first great city, then tyrannized its people so brutally that the gods had to grow a wild man out of the steppe just to fight him. They brawled, became the closest of friends, chopped down a sacred forest, fought a goddess and killed her cosmic bull — and then Gilgamesh learned the single hardest lesson there is: that even he must die. His flight from that fate carried him across the Waters of Death to the one man the gods had made immortal, and it changed nothing. He came home with empty hands and a full mind, and pointed at the brick walls of Uruk: 'Climb and walk, look at the foundations.' The epic that bears his name is the world's oldest surviving work of narrative literature, a buddy story, a monster hunt, a meditation on friendship and mortality — and, rediscovered only in 1849, it is still remaking whatever it touches.
