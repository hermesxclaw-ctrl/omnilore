Who It Is

In the mist of the Virunga volcanoes a silverback rises to his full height — nearly six feet and twice the weight of a man — beats his chest so the sound rolls like drums through the forest, and then gently cradles an infant in hands that could bend bamboo. The gorilla is at once the most human-like and most misunderstood of the great apes: not the chest-thumping monster of colonial legend, but a highly social, largely peaceful forest giant whose survival now hinges entirely on human choices.

Attested: Primarily terrestrial knuckle-walkers, but 2018 study of 77 Bwindi mountain gorillas found knuckle-walking only ~60% of locomotion; also fist-walking, palm-walking, and occasional bipedalism when carrying food or displaying. Highly social, living in stable troops (2-30+ individuals) led by dominant silverback, with multiple females, offspring, and subordinate blackback males. Troops are cohesive but not territorial in strict sense; home ranges overlap. Diet primarily herbivorous: leaves, stems, pith, fruit (western lowland more frugivorous), occasional invertebrates. Powers
Extreme physical strength and arm span

Can lift, drag, and bend vegetation requiring forces estimated at 4-9 times human strength; arm span to 2.7 m allows brachiation-like reach and striking. Silverback can snap bamboo, bend steel zoo bars when motivated. Strength is functional for foraging, nest building, and display combat.

Limit: Not superhuman in fictional sense; endurance limited, tires quickly in charges; strength does not confer invulnerability to bullets, snares, or disease. Captive overweight individuals less agile.

Knuckle-walking and versatile locomotion

Primary quadrupedal knuckle-walking with specialized hand anatomy; can also fist-walk, palm-walk, climb, and walk bipedally for short distances while carrying objects.

Limit: Not cursorial; cannot sustain fast bipedal running; arboreal ability declines with body mass, especially in large silverbacks.

Complex social intelligence and communication

Troop coordination, long-term memory, individual recognition, ~25 vocalizations, gestures, and learned local traditions (food processing techniques). Some individuals taught modified sign language (e.g., Koko) demonstrated several hundred signs and comprehension of spoken English.

Limit: No evidence of syntactic language in wild; sign-language results disputed for Clever Hans effects and lack of independent replication; wild communication is not language in human sense.

Ecological engineering and foraging expertise

Selective herbivory shapes forest understory; nest building nightly; use of sticks to test water depth (first documented tool use in wild gorillas, Breuer et al. 2005, Mbeli Bai, Congo).

Limit: Tool use rare and simple compared to chimpanzees; not habitual or cumulative culture.

Display and intimidation

Chest-beating (rapid cupped-hand strikes creating resonant thump), bipedal strutting, vegetation slapping, roaring, and bluff charges to deter rivals or threats without contact.

Limit: Usually bluff; actual fights rare and risky, can cause severe injury or death when they occur.

Accounts I
The Periplus of Hanno - The Hairy WomenPeriplus of Hanno the Navigator

c. 500 BC, surviving epitome circa 9th century AD

Hanno's expedition along the West African coast reaches the 'Horn of the South' and captures wild hairy people, mostly women, who bite and scratch and cannot be forced to follow. Interpreters call them gorillai. Three females are killed and their skins flayed and taken to Carthage, where they were displayed in the temple of Juno until Rome destroyed the city in 146 BC. The text is terse and ambiguous, mixing exploration, ethnography, and wonder-tale. Coined the word gorilla; skins reportedly displayed for 350 years as marvel; later source for Western name of the ape, though identity of the creatures remains unresolved.

Andrew Battel's Pongo - The Giant of AngolaSamuel Purchas, Purchas His Pilgrimes (1625), from Battel's manuscripts

1590-1605 travels, published 1625

English adventurer Andrew Battel, captive in Angola, describes two great apes: the lesser 'Jocko' (chimpanzee) and the greater 'Pongo' — a giant like a man but taller, hollow-eyed, hairy-bodied with bare face, ears and hands, dunnish color, walking always on legs with hands clasped on nape, living in troops that kill travelers and cannot be taken alive because ten men cannot hold one. Battel claims local people tell of Pongos abducting humans. Introduced the great ape to English readers; Buffon and later naturalists debated whether Pongo was real or chimera; Radermacher (1780s) dismissed it as fiction. Linnaeus later used Pongo as genus for orangutan, adding confusion.

Savage and Wyman Name the GorillaBoston Journal of Natural History Vol. 5 (1847)

1847

Baptist missionary-physician Thomas Staughton Savage, stationed in Liberia, obtains skulls and other remains of a large ape known locally as 'Engé-ena' (Mpongwe). With Harvard anatomist Jeffries Wyman, he describes Troglodytes gorilla as a new species distinct from the chimpanzee (then Troglodytes niger), detailing massive sagittal crest, large molars, and size. They explicitly derive the name from Hanno's gorillai. First scientific recognition of gorilla in Western taxonomy; triggers 19th-century gorilla craze, museum hunts, and sensational travelogues; later reclassified to genus Gorilla (Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire 1852).

Accounts II
Dian Fossey and Digit - Among the Virunga Mountain GorillasDian Fossey, Gorillas in the Mist (1983); field notes 1967-1985

1967-1985

Primatologist Dian Fossey establishes Karisoke Research Center between Karisimbi and Visoke volcanoes, habituates wild mountain gorillas to human observation, documents troop structure, vocalizations, infanticide, and individual personalities. Her favorite silverback Digit is killed by poachers in 1977 while defending his group; Fossey founds Digit Fund, intensifies anti-poaching patrols, and becomes controversial for aggressive conservation tactics. Fossey herself is murdered at Karisoke in 1985. Transformed scientific understanding and public sympathy; mountain gorilla population began slow recovery from ~250 in 1981 to >1,000 by 2018; established model for habituation-based eco-tourism; raised ethical debates over activist conservation methods.

Koko and the Question of Ape LanguageFrancine Patterson, The Education of Koko (1981); National Geographic and documentary records 1971-2018

1971-2018

Western lowland gorilla Hanabi-ko (Koko), born 1971 at San Francisco Zoo, was raised by psychologist Francine Patterson and taught a modified form of American Sign Language (GSL). Patterson reported Koko acquired ~1,000 signs, understood ~2,000 English words, invented compounds, expressed grief for her kitten All Ball, and scored 70-90 on IQ-adapted tests. Koko's interactions were filmed extensively and she became a celebrity advocate for conservation. Popularized idea of gorilla personhood and emotional depth; spurred ethical debate about ape captivity and language; scientific criticism questioned methodology, lack of blind testing, and over-interpretation. Koko died in 2018 at age 46.

The Bwindi Census - Finding a Hidden PopulationIUCN / Uganda Wildlife Authority censuses 1997-2018; Robbins et al.

1990s-2018

The Bwindi Impenetrable Forest population, long thought to be eastern lowland gorillas, was recognized through censuses and genetics as mountain gorillas isolated from Virunga by ~30 km of farmland. Intensive surveys using nest counts, genetic sampling from feces, and camera traps estimated ~340 individuals in Bwindi in 1997, rising to ~459 in 2018. Debate over whether Bwindi gorillas merit third subspecies status (sometimes called Gorilla beringei bwindi) reflects Ice Age forest fragmentation 261,000 years ago. Raised total mountain gorilla count above 1,000, allowing IUCN to downlist mountain gorilla from Critically Endangered to Endangered in 2018; intensified community-based conservation and Batwa resettlement controversies.

Gorillas are ferocious, chest-beating monsters that attack humans unprovoked. — Wild gorillas are predominantly shy and peaceful; chest-beating and bluff charges are ritualized displays to avoid fighting. Unprovoked attacks on humans are extremely rare and usually involve habituated or cornered silverbacks defending group. Dian Fossey and subsequent fieldworkers approached to within meters without aggression.

Gorillas are just big monkeys or will evolve into humans. — Gorillas are great apes, not monkeys (no tail, larger brain, different taxonomy). They are not ancestors of humans but cousins sharing a common ancestor ~7 million years ago; they are not 'trying' to become human. DNA similarity is 96-99% depending on whether non-coding and copy-number variation is counted, not 99% identical in all respects.

All gorillas live in mountains and beat their chests constantly. — Only ~1,000 mountain gorillas live in high montane forest; the vast majority (~316,000) are lowland gorillas in dense lowland forests and swamps down to sea level. Chest-beating is infrequent, context-specific signaling (often by silverbacks during display or play), not constant drumming.

Koko the gorilla spoke fluent sign language and had human-like conversations. — Koko learned several hundred modified ASL signs and showed impressive comprehension and emotional expression, but independent linguists dispute claims of fluent syntax, grammar, or human-like conversation; results were not double-blind and may involve cueing. Wild gorillas do not use sign language.
