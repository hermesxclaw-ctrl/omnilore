Who It Is

The Beholder is arguably the single most iconic original monster in the Dungeons & Dragons bestiary — a creature so distinctive and so wholly invented for the game that it has become synonymous with D&D itself. The Beholder is a floating sphere, typically 6-8 feet in diameter, dominated by a single large central eye and crowned with ten eye stalks, each ending in a smaller eye. No two parts of this design were accidental: the central eye projects a continuous anti-magic cone, suppressing all magical effects within its gaze, while each of the ten eye stalks can independently target and fire a different magical ray — including charm, paralysis, fear, petrification, death, disintegration, telekinesis, and sleep. This makes the Beholder one of the most tactically complex monsters in any TTRPG: a single creature that simultaneously serves as crowd control, artillery, and zone denial. The creature is fundamentally defined by its psychology. Beholders are xenophobically paranoid, narcissistic, and megalomaniacal. Each Beholder believes itself to be the perfect physical specimen of its species and considers all others (including other Beholders) to be hideous aberrations that must be destroyed. This prevents Beholders from forming societies or cooperating, though some variants (Hive Mothers) can compel cooperation. Beholders reproduce asexually: a Beholder's dreams can warp reality, and if a Beholder dreams of another Beholder, that creature may will itself into existence. This dream-based reproduction means that every Beholder is shaped by its progenitor's paranoid fantasies about what other Beholders might look like. Beholders construct elaborate, trap-filled lairs designed around exploiting their abilities: vertical shafts that they can float through, and chambers designed to funnel intruders into overlapping fields of fire from the different eye rays. They surround themselves with charmed minions and enslaved creatures, trusting no one but needing an audience for their perceived magnificence. The Beholder has been part of D&D since its earliest days — first appearing in the 1975 Greyhawk supplement — and has been a tentpole monster in every edition since. Wizards of the Coast has claimed the Beholder as one of its few legally protected 'Product Identity' monsters (alongside the Mind Flayer), meaning it cannot be used in other games' Open Game License content, cementing its status as uniquely D&D. The 2024 D&D rules update (One D&D) continues the Beholder's prominence. In wider culture, the Beholder has appeared in Stranger Things, various D&D-adjacent media, and the creature has become visual shorthand for D&D itself — a floating orb of eyes that instantly communicates 'this is Dungeons & Dragons.'

Form

The Beholder is a floating, roughly spherical creature, about the size of a large beach ball or small boulder (typically 4-8 feet in diameter). Its body is covered in tough, leathery skin, often with a mottled or chitinous texture, in shades of purple, magenta, green, or blue. Its most prominent feature is a single, massive, central eye that sits in the front of its body, surrounded by a large, bony socket. Around the top of its body, it has a ring of ten, flexible, snake-like eyestalks, each tipped with a smaller eye of a different color. It has a large, toothy maw filled with sharp, needle-like teeth, located on the underside of its body. It has no other limbs, no legs, and no wings; it floats and propels itself through the air via a combination of its central eye's power and an innate levitation ability.

Powers

Anti-Magic Cone The Beholder's central eye projects a 150-foot cone of anti-magic. Within this cone, all magic, magical items, and magical effects are suppressed. This is its primary defensive and offensive tool, neutralizing enemy spellcasters and magical defenses. All editions (1st-5th) The cone is only active when the Beholder's central eye is open (it can close it as a free action). The Beholder itself is not immune to magic, but its own eye rays are not affected by its own anti-magic cone. The exact length and shape of the cone have varied slightly between editions, but the core function remains consistent.

Eye Rays Each of the Beholder's ten eyestalks can fire a different magical ray. The specific effects have varied across editions, but classic examples include: Charm Person, Charm Monster, Sleep, Petrification, Fear, Slow, Disintegration, Death Ray, Telekinesis, and Flesh to Stone. Each ray is a targeted beam with a specific range (usually 150 feet). All editions (1st-5th) The Beholder can only fire a limited number of rays per round (typically 1-3 in early editions, up to 3 in 5th edition). It cannot fire a ray from an eyestalk that is closed or damaged. The rays are subject to the target's saving throws. The specific selection of rays has changed between editions, with some (like the Death Ray) being removed for balance reasons in later editions.

Levitation / Flight The Beholder is a natural levitator and can fly with perfect maneuverability at a speed of about 20 feet per round. It can hover, move in any direction, and rotate in place. All editions It cannot be knocked prone or grappled easily due to its flight, but it is still susceptible to being restrained by webs or other physical means. None.

Legendary Actions (5th Edition) In 5th Edition, the Beholder can take special actions outside of its turn, allowing it to use one of its eye rays at the end of another creature's turn. This makes it a highly dynamic and threatening opponent. 5th Edition (2014-present) It can only use a limited number of Legendary Actions per round (3) and cannot use the same ray twice in a row. This is a mechanical addition to make the creature more exciting in combat, not a lore-based change.

Paranoid Genius Beholders are exceptionally intelligent and possess an almost supernatural level of paranoia. This grants them a high Wisdom score and proficiency in Perception and Insight. They are masters of strategy, trap-making, and psychological manipulation. All editions Their paranoia can be a weakness, making them predictable to clever heroes who can exploit their obsessive fears. None.

Dream Lair (Ecology) Beholders are unique in that they do not reproduce sexually. Instead, a Beholder's dreams are so vivid and powerful that they can manifest into reality. A Beholder that has a particularly intense dream of another Beholder will, upon waking, find that its dream has spawned a new, younger Beholder. This new Beholder is often hostile to its 'parent' and will attempt to kill it to establish its own domain. Introduced in 2nd Edition, expanded in later editions (e.g., 'I, Tyrant'). This is a purely lore-based concept, not a mechanical ability. This has been a point of fascination and some debate among fans, as it redefines the classic 'monster' as a being of pure psychic will. It is canon in most editions, but some older modules presented Beholders as more traditional creatures.

Behavior & Habitat

Beholders are fiercely intelligent, paranoid, and supremely arrogant. They are solitary creatures that believe themselves to be the pinnacle of creation, viewing all other beings as inferior, threatening, or useful only as tools. They are obsessive hoarders of knowledge and treasure, but their primary motivation is survival and the elimination of any perceived threat. A Beholder's paranoia is so extreme that it often sees threats in its own dreams and reflections. They are meticulous planners and strategists, creating complex lairs filled with traps, minions, and enslaved servants. They are capable of speech, speaking in a deep, resonant voice, and are known for their long, rambling, and often self-aggrandizing monologues.

Accounts

The Beholder is the ultimate paranoid tyrant. It is a solitary mastermind that builds a fortress of traps and enslaved minions to protect its hoard of treasure and knowledge. It is a classic 'boss monster' that represents the dangers of absolute power and unchecked paranoia.

Xanathar is the most famous named Beholder in D&D lore. He is a crime lord who rules the underworld of the city of Waterdeep from his lair beneath Skullport. He is paranoid, capricious, and obsessed with his pet goldfish, Sylgar. His story is one of a powerful but unstable ruler whose paranoia makes him a constant threat to his own organization and the city above.

This is the foundational myth of Beholder reproduction. A Beholder's dreams are so powerful that they can spawn a new Beholder. This new creature is a 'clone' of the original, but with its own twisted personality. It immediately sees its parent as a rival and will attempt to usurp its territory. This cycle of dream-born violence ensures that Beholders are forever isolated and paranoid.

Field Notes

⛧ The Beholder was created by Terry Kuntz, a player in Gary Gygax's original Greyhawk campaign, and was later refined and named by Gygax.

⛧ The Beholder is one of the few D&D monsters to have its own dedicated sourcebook, 'I, Tyrant', which explores its ecology and psychology in depth.

⛧ In 5th Edition D&D, the Beholder is classified as 'Lawful Evil', a rare alignment for a monster, reflecting its tyrannical and ordered nature.

⛧ The Beholder's lair is always a complex, multi-level structure filled with traps, secret passages, and enslaved guardians, reflecting its paranoid genius.

⛧ The most famous Beholder, Xanathar, has a pet goldfish named Sylgar, which he dotes on and obsesses over, a bizarre and memorable detail that humanizes (in a twisted way) this otherwise alien creature.

Cultural Significance

The most recognizable original D&D monster. Product Identity protection makes it legally unique to D&D. The paranoid, narcissistic psychology is as distinctive as the physical design. Anti-magic eye plus ten different magical eye rays creates one of the most tactically interesting combat encounters in TTRPG history.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of the Beholder as a paranoid, narcissistic genius who is also a living fortress. It is a one-creature arms race, a floating artillery platform that is also a master strategist. It is the ultimate 'boss monster' — a single, self-contained threat that requires a party of heroes to work together to overcome. It is less a monster and more a force of nature, a test of a party's ability to think, plan, and act under extreme pressure.
