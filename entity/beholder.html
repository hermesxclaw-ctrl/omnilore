Who It Is

The Beholder is arguably the single most iconic original monster in the Dungeons & Dragons bestiary — a creature so distinctive and so wholly invented for the game that it has become synonymous with D&D itself. The Beholder is a floating sphere, typically 6-8 feet in diameter, dominated by a single large central eye and crowned with ten eye stalks, each ending in a smaller eye. No two parts of this design were accidental: the central eye projects a continuous anti-magic cone, suppressing all magical effects within its gaze, while each of the ten eye stalks can independently target and fire a different magical ray — including charm, paralysis, fear, petrification, death, disintegration, telekinesis, and sleep. This makes the Beholder one of the most tactically complex monsters in any TTRPG: a single creature that simultaneously serves as crowd control, artillery, and zone denial. The creature is fundamentally defined by its psychology. Beholders are xenophobically paranoid, narcissistic, and megalomaniacal. Each Beholder believes itself to be the perfect physical specimen of its species and considers all others (including other Beholders) to be hideous aberrations that must be destroyed. This prevents Beholders from forming societies or cooperating, though some variants (Hive Mothers) can compel cooperation. Beholders reproduce asexually: a Beholder's dreams can warp reality, and if a Beholder dreams of another Beholder, that creature may will itself into existence. This dream-based reproduction means that every Beholder is shaped by its progenitor's paranoid fantasies about what other Beholders might look like. Beholders construct elaborate, trap-filled lairs designed around exploiting their abilities: vertical shafts that they can float through, and chambers designed to funnel intruders into overlapping fields of fire from the different eye rays. They surround themselves with charmed minions and enslaved creatures, trusting no one but needing an audience for their perceived magnificence. The Beholder has been part of D&D since its earliest days — first appearing in the 1975 Greyhawk supplement — and has been a tentpole monster in every edition since. Wizards of the Coast has claimed the Beholder as one of its few legally protected 'Product Identity' monsters (alongside the Mind Flayer), meaning it cannot be used in other games' Open Game License content, cementing its status as uniquely D&D. The 2024 D&D rules update (One D&D) continues the Beholder's prominence. In wider culture, the Beholder has appeared in Stranger Things, various D&D-adjacent media, and the creature has become visual shorthand for D&D itself — a floating orb of eyes that instantly communicates 'this is Dungeons & Dragons.'

The Approach

Imagine a creature that is the embodiment of paranoia, a floating sphere of flesh and teeth, its single, massive eye glaring at you while a ring of ten smaller eyes, each a different color, swivel to track your every move. It is the Beholder, the Eye Tyrant, a monster so iconic that it has become the face of Dungeons & Dragons itself. It is not just a creature; it is a nightmare given form, a mastermind that believes itself to be the center of the universe, and it will do anything to protect its twisted vision of reality.

Etymology

The name 'Beholder' is a direct reference to its defining trait: the creature's many eyes and its ability to perceive everything around it, often before others are even aware of its presence. It is a modern coinage from the 1970s, a play on the verb 'to behold' — to see or observe. The term 'Tyrant' in its epithet reflects its absolute, solitary rule over its territory, a common theme in its lore.

Cultural Ownership

Dungeons & Dragons (D&D), a tabletop role-playing game. Created by Terry Kuntz and refined by Gary Gygax, first appearing in the 1975 supplement 'Greyhawk' for the original D&D game. It is now a proprietary creature of Wizards of the Coast (a subsidiary of Hasbro) and is considered one of the game's most iconic and recognizable monsters.

Disputed Identifications

There is no serious dispute about the Beholder's identity as a D&D creature. However, its visual and conceptual design has been influential, leading to 'inspired-by' creatures in other media. Some fans and critics have pointed to the 'Eye of the Deep' from earlier D&D supplements as a precursor, but the Beholder is a distinct and original creation. Its iconic status is undisputed within the hobby.

Description

Appearance

The Beholder is a floating, roughly spherical creature, about the size of a large beach ball or small boulder (typically 4-8 feet in diameter). Its body is covered in tough, leathery skin, often with a mottled or chitinous texture, in shades of purple, magenta, green, or blue. Its most prominent feature is a single, massive, central eye that sits in the front of its body, surrounded by a large, bony socket. Around the top of its body, it has a ring of ten, flexible, snake-like eyestalks, each tipped with a smaller eye of a different color. It has a large, toothy maw filled with sharp, needle-like teeth, located on the underside of its body. It has no other limbs, no legs, and no wings; it floats and propels itself through the air via a combination of its central eye's power and an innate levitation ability.

Behavior

Beholders are fiercely intelligent, paranoid, and supremely arrogant. They are solitary creatures that believe themselves to be the pinnacle of creation, viewing all other beings as inferior, threatening, or useful only as tools. They are obsessive hoarders of knowledge and treasure, but their primary motivation is survival and the elimination of any perceived threat. A Beholder's paranoia is so extreme that it often sees threats in its own dreams and reflections. They are meticulous planners and strategists, creating complex lairs filled with traps, minions, and enslaved servants. They are capable of speech, speaking in a deep, resonant voice, and are known for their long, rambling, and often self-aggrandizing monologues.

Domains

Beholders are typically found in underground environments: deep caverns, ruined cities, and the Underdark. They prefer to establish lairs in places that are defensible and offer a clear view of all approaches. They are not native to the surface world, but can be found there if their lair is breached or if they seek to conquer a new domain. They are also known to create 'dream lairs' in the Astral Plane, where they exist as psychic projections.

Symbols

The Beholder's primary symbol is its own image: a central eye with a ring of smaller eyes. This symbol is often used in its own heraldry, on its minions' gear, and in the traps it sets. It is a symbol of absolute surveillance, tyranny, and arcane power. In the broader D&D mythos, the Beholder is a symbol of a classic, high-level 'boss monster' encounter.

Powers & Abilities

Anti-Magic Cone

Source Version All editions (1st-5th)

Description

The Beholder's central eye projects a 150-foot cone of anti-magic. Within this cone, all magic, magical items, and magical effects are suppressed. This is its primary defensive and offensive tool, neutralizing enemy spellcasters and magical defenses.

Limitations

The cone is only active when the Beholder's central eye is open (it can close it as a free action). The Beholder itself is not immune to magic, but its own eye rays are not affected by its own anti-magic cone.

Contradictions

The exact length and shape of the cone have varied slightly between editions, but the core function remains consistent.

Eye Rays

Source Version All editions (1st-5th)

Description

Each of the Beholder's ten eyestalks can fire a different magical ray. The specific effects have varied across editions, but classic examples include: Charm Person, Charm Monster, Sleep, Petrification, Fear, Slow, Disintegration, Death Ray, Telekinesis, and Flesh to Stone. Each ray is a targeted beam with a specific range (usually 150 feet).

Limitations

The Beholder can only fire a limited number of rays per round (typically 1-3 in early editions, up to 3 in 5th edition). It cannot fire a ray from an eyestalk that is closed or damaged. The rays are subject to the target's saving throws.

Contradictions

The specific selection of rays has changed between editions, with some (like the Death Ray) being removed for balance reasons in later editions.

Levitation / Flight

Source Version All editions

Description

The Beholder is a natural levitator and can fly with perfect maneuverability at a speed of about 20 feet per round. It can hover, move in any direction, and rotate in place.

Limitations

It cannot be knocked prone or grappled easily due to its flight, but it is still susceptible to being restrained by webs or other physical means.

Contradictions

None.

Legendary Actions (5th Edition)

Source Version 5th Edition (2014-present)

Description

In 5th Edition, the Beholder can take special actions outside of its turn, allowing it to use one of its eye rays at the end of another creature's turn. This makes it a highly dynamic and threatening opponent.

Limitations

It can only use a limited number of Legendary Actions per round (3) and cannot use the same ray twice in a row.

Contradictions

This is a mechanical addition to make the creature more exciting in combat, not a lore-based change.

Paranoid Genius

Source Version All editions

Description

Beholders are exceptionally intelligent and possess an almost supernatural level of paranoia. This grants them a high Wisdom score and proficiency in Perception and Insight. They are masters of strategy, trap-making, and psychological manipulation.

Limitations

Their paranoia can be a weakness, making them predictable to clever heroes who can exploit their obsessive fears.

Contradictions

None.

Dream Lair (Ecology)

Source Version Introduced in 2nd Edition, expanded in later editions (e.g., 'I, Tyrant').

Description

Beholders are unique in that they do not reproduce sexually. Instead, a Beholder's dreams are so vivid and powerful that they can manifest into reality. A Beholder that has a particularly intense dream of another Beholder will, upon waking, find that its dream has spawned a new, younger Beholder. This new Beholder is often hostile to its 'parent' and will attempt to kill it to establish its own domain.

Limitations

This is a purely lore-based concept, not a mechanical ability.

Contradictions

This has been a point of fascination and some debate among fans, as it redefines the classic 'monster' as a being of pure psychic will. It is canon in most editions, but some older modules presented Beholders as more traditional creatures.

The Beholder as a Symbol of Tyranny

Source Various D&D modules and campaign settings (e.g., 'The Lost Caverns of Tsojcanth', 'I, Tyrant').Culture Dungeons & DragonsPeriod 1975 - present

Summary

The Beholder is the ultimate paranoid tyrant. It is a solitary mastermind that builds a fortress of traps and enslaved minions to protect its hoard of treasure and knowledge. It is a classic 'boss monster' that represents the dangers of absolute power and unchecked paranoia.

The Xanathar of Waterdeep

Source Waterdeep: Dragon Heist (2018) and other Waterdeep-based adventures.Culture Dungeons & DragonsPeriod 2018 - present

Summary

Xanathar is the most famous named Beholder in D&D lore. He is a crime lord who rules the underworld of the city of Waterdeep from his lair beneath Skullport. He is paranoid, capricious, and obsessed with his pet goldfish, Sylgar. His story is one of a powerful but unstable ruler whose paranoia makes him a constant threat to his own organization and the city above.

The Dream Birth of a Beholder

Source The Ecology of the Beholder (Dragon Magazine, various issues) and 'I, Tyrant' (1996).Culture Dungeons & DragonsPeriod 1980s-1990s (lore expanded)

Summary

This is the foundational myth of Beholder reproduction. A Beholder's dreams are so powerful that they can spawn a new Beholder. This new creature is a 'clone' of the original, but with its own twisted personality. It immediately sees its parent as a rival and will attempt to usurp its territory. This cycle of dream-born violence ensures that Beholders are forever isolated and paranoid.

Variants

Contradictions & Disputes

Reproduction Method

Position A

Beholders reproduce asexually through their own dreams, spawning new Beholders as psychic manifestations.

Position A — evidence

The 'Ecology of the Beholder' articles and later sourcebooks like 'I, Tyrant' explicitly describe the dream-birth method.

Position B

Beholders are traditional monsters that may reproduce in a more conventional, if unspecified, manner.

Position B — evidence

Older, more simplistic modules and early Monster Manual entries did not address reproduction, leading some to assume a more conventional method.

Scholarly Consensus

The dream-birth method is now the official, canonical explanation, but it remains a point of fascination and debate among fans.

The Nature of the Central Eye

Position A

The central eye projects a cone of anti-magic.

Position A — evidence

The Monster Manual in every edition states that the central eye projects an anti-magic cone.

Position B

The central eye is just a large, powerful eye that can see in all directions and is not inherently magical.

Position B — evidence

Some early descriptions and fan interpretations focus on the eye as a symbol of the Beholder's omniscient perception, without emphasizing the anti-magic function.

Scholarly Consensus

The anti-magic cone is the standard and official ability.

Intelligence and Motivation

Position A

Beholders are super-geniuses whose paranoia is a rational response to a universe that is out to get them.

Position A — evidence

Their high Intelligence scores and complex lair designs suggest a calculating, rational mind.

Position B

Beholders are arrogant and insane, and their paranoia is a self-fulfilling prophecy that makes them their own worst enemies.

Position B — evidence

Their tendency to see threats in their own dreams and reflections suggests a deep-seated madness.

Scholarly Consensus

Both are true. The Beholder's genius is twisted by its insanity, making it a terrifyingly unpredictable and effective villain.
