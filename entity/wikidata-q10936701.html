Who It Is

He has no hall. His body is a mountain. At the foot of Mount Miwa in Yamato, Ōmononushi — the Great Master of Spirits — accepts worship without an image, because the mountain itself is his shintai. He appears as light on the sea, as a red arrow that finds a woman in a ditch, as a handsome night visitor who leaves three loops of hemp thread trailing to a shrine, and as a small snake coiled in a comb box whose shame becomes a tomb of piled stone. In Japan's earliest chronicles he is both a stranger who saves the god of the land and, impossibly, that same god's own divided soul.

Behavior is ambivalent: benevolent helper and dangerous afflictor. He offers conditional aid to Ōkuninushi ('worship me and I will help you complete the land'), demands proper ritual enshrinement, and when neglected under Emperor Sujin sends devastating pestilence that only ceases when correctly propitiated. He is sexually active and liminal, crossing human-divine boundaries at night, impregnating women without initial consent disclosure (arrow, night visitation), then revealing identity via traceable thread. Powers
Land Creation Assistance

Promises and provides aid to Ōkuninushi in pacifying and developing Ashihara-no-Nakatsukuni after Sukunabikona's departure, enabling completion of kuni-zukuri.

Limit: Conditional on being enshrined on Mount Mimoro/Miwa in Yamato; does not act without worship. In Nihon Shoki version, power is framed as Ōnamuchi's own divided spirit rather than independent agency.

Pestilence and Healing

Can inflict and withdraw epidemic disease affecting the entire country. Under Emperor Sujin, causes plague that kills many until proper rites performed; cessation is immediate upon correct appointment of priest.

Limit: Requires specific human intermediary (Ōtataneko) of his own lineage and proper sake offerings by Ikuhi; divination/oracle needed to identify cause.

Shape-shifting and Nocturnal Visitation

Assumes forms of red arrow, handsome youth, small snake, and light. Enters women's chambers at night, impregnates without initial revelation.

Limit: Daytime form is taboo; when seen as snake he flees and severs relationship. Arrow form requires contact with genitals to conceive.

Fertility and Lineage Founding

Fathers or ancestors divine-human lines: Himetatara-Isukeyorihime (Jimmu's empress, ancestress of imperial line), Ōtataneko (ancestor of Kamo and Miwa priestly clans), and via Ikutamayorihime the Miwa lineage.

Limit: Offspring legitimacy is contested in sources (Kotoshironushi variant for Isuzuhime). Requires human female partner.

Oracular Communication

Communicates will via dreams to emperor and via possession/oracle of Yamato-Totohimomosohime. Delivers precise ritual instructions.

Limit: Message requires interpretation and verification by divination; not continuously available.

Guardianship of Imperial House

After cession of the land, installed (or installs his nigitama) in Yamato to protect Amaterasu's descendants alongside eighty myriads of deities / his children enshrined in Yamato shrines.

Limit: Role is protective/guardian, not sovereign; subordinate to Takamimusubi/Amaterasu heavenly order.

Sake Blessing

Sanctifies sake (miki) brewed by Ikuhi; sake becomes efficacious offering whose presentation is accompanied by sacred songs. Miwa remains center of sake brewing cult.

Limit: Requires ritually pure brewer and correct brewing; power resides in ritual context, not autonomous miracle.

Accounts I
The Illuminating Sea God and the Completion of the LandKojiki Book 1; Nihon Shoki Book 1 (Age of the Gods)

Mythic age (kuni-zukuri); texts 712/720 CE

Ōkuninushi (Ōnamuchi), descendant/son of Susanoo, labors to develop Ashihara-no-Nakatsukuni with the dwarf Sukunabikona who arrived across the sea. When Sukunabikona departs for Tokoyo no Kuni, Ōkuninushi laments. A deity illuminating the sea appears and says: 'If you enshrine me on Mount Mimoro in Yamato, I will help you.' Ōkuninushi complies. The Kojiki leaves the god unnamed at this point but context identifies him as Ōmononushi; the Nihon Shoki has the god declare himself to be Ōnamuchi's kushimitama and sakimitama and lists Ōmononushi among Ōnamuchi's names. Establishes Mount Miwa as Ōmononushi's sacred seat; completes creation of the terrestrial world; establishes mitama theology where a kami's spirit can be enshrined separately. Provides etiology for Ōmiwa Shrine.

The Cession of the Land and Ascent to Takamagahara as ŌmononushiNihon Shoki Book 1 variant (kuni-yuzuri)

Mythic age; text 720 CE

In a variant of the kuni-yuzuri (transfer of Ashihara-no-Nakatsukuni to heavenly deities), Ōnamuchi, now called Ōmononushi after withdrawing from physical rule to govern the unseen spirit world, ascends with his son Kotoshironushi to Takamagahara to pledge allegiance to the heavenly kami. Takamimusubi rewards him by giving his daughter Mihotsuhime as wife and sends him back to earth with eighty myriads of deities to guard the descendants of Amaterasu. Reframes Ōmononushi as post-cession, spirit-world form of Ōkuninushi and as imperial guardian; explains his continued earthly presence after surrender of the land; legitimizes Yamato court via heavenly marriage alliance.

The Red Arrow and the Birth of Himetatara-IsukeyorihimeKojiki Book 1 (Jimmu); Nihon Shoki Book 3 (Jimmu)

Mythic age, Jimmu's reign; texts 712/720

Kamuyamato-Iwarebiko (Emperor Jimmu) seeks a wife. He is told of Seyadatarahime, who was impregnated when Ōmononushi took the form of a red arrow and struck her genitals while she defecated in a ditch. The child, Hototatara-Isusukihime / Himetatara-Isukeyorihime (later Isuzuhime), is raised as Ōmononushi's daughter. Jimmu woos her, overcoming her taboo name (hoto = genitals) by renaming, and makes her his empress, mother of the next emperor. Provides divine ancestry for imperial empress and thus imperial line; explains taboo avoidance in name (hoto); links Miwa deity directly to Jimmu's legitimacy. Nihon Shoki immediately appends variant where Isuzuhime is daughter of Kotoshironushi (as giant wani) and Mizokuhihime/Tamakushihime.

Accounts II
Plague under Emperor Sujin and the Appointment of Ōtataneko and IkuhiKojiki Book 2 (Sujin); Nihon Shoki Book 5 (Sujin 5-7)

Legendary reign of Emperor Sujin (trad. 97-30 BCE, historically 3rd-4th century CE); texts 712/720

During Sujin's reign a severe pestilence ravages the country, with many deaths. Divination reveals kami displeasure. Ōmononushi appears in a dream to Sujin (Kojiki) or through oracle of Yamato-Totohimomosohime (Shoki), claiming responsibility and demanding worship by Ōtataneko, his descendant through Ikutamayorihime, and sake offerings brewed by Ikuhi. Sujin searches out Ōtataneko (found in Kawachi/ Yamato), appoints him priest at Miwa, and appoints Ikuhi as miki brewer. Ikuhi offers sacred sake with a song; Sujin responds with a song; a feast at the shrine follows and the plague ceases. Ōtataneko becomes ancestor of Kamo no Kimi and Miwa no Kimi clans. Etiology for Ōmiwa priesthood and for Miwa sake cult; demonstrates disease etiology as ritual neglect; establishes template for ekibyō (plague deity) pacification; historical correlate debated as reflection of 3rd-century epidemics or Yamato state formation.

Ikutamayorihime and the Three Loops of Hemp — Etiology of MiwaKojiki Book 2 (Sujin interlude)

Legendary Sujin era; text 712

The beautiful Ikutamayorihime is visited nightly by a handsome stranger who impregnates her. Her parents, wanting to know his identity, tell her to sew a hemp thread to his garment. Next morning the thread runs through the keyhole of her door and trails all the way to Mount Miwa, with only three loops (mi-wa) remaining in the chamber. They realize the lover is Ōmononushi, god of the mountain. The child is ancestor of Ōtataneko. The story explains the name Miwa as 'three loops.' Toponymic etiology for Miwa; links divine-human marriage to priestly lineage; exemplifies night-visiting (yobai) folklore motif and taboo on seeing divine lover.

Yamato-Totohimomosohime, the Snake in the Comb Box, and Hashihaka KofunNihon Shoki Book 5 (Sujin)

Legendary Sujin era; text 720

Yamato-Totohimomosohime, daughter of Emperor Kōrei and grandaunt of Sujin, becomes wife of Ōmononushi. He visits only at night. She asks to see his true form; he says she will be shocked, hides in her comb box, where she finds a small snake (kohebi). Startled, she cries out; the snake, ashamed and angered, flees to Mount Miwa. In remorse she stabs her genitals with a chopstick (hashi) and dies. She is buried in a tomb at the western foot of Miwa called Hashihaka (Chopstick Tomb), said to be built by carrying stones from Mount Ōsaka (Mount Nijō, 15.3 km away) in relays. The story serves as origin myth for Hashihaka Kofun, one of Japan's earliest keyhole tumuli. Etiology for Hashihaka Kofun (280m zenpō-kōen-fun, early Kofun period); illustrates taboo on seeing kami's true form and shame/honor dynamics; provides archaeological-mythic link between text and tomb; death by genital stabbing mirrors fertility/sexual themes.

Ōmononushi is the same as Ōkuninushi, just another name with no distinction. — Nihon Shoki does list Ōmononushi as a name/mitama of Ōnamuchi, but Kojiki treats them as distinct cooperating deities, and modern scholarship sees an originally independent Miwa mountain deity later theologized as Ōkuninushi's divided spirit. Collapsing them erases the Miwa cult's distinct plague/sake/mountain domains.

Ōmononushi is a snake god and always appears as a giant serpent or dragon. — Only one episode (Totohimomosohime) reveals a small snake in a comb box as his shameful true form; other forms are light, red arrow, and handsome youth. He is protean, not exclusively ophidian, and his primary shintai is the mountain itself, not a serpent statue.

Mount Miwa is just the location of his shrine. — Mount Miwa IS the shintai (sacred body) — Ōmiwa Shrine has no main hall (honden) because the mountain itself is the object of worship. The shrine worships the mountain directly, one of Japan's oldest aniconic traditions.

Konpira (Kotohira-gū) has always been Ōmononushi, a purely Shinto sea god. — Konpira was medieval Shinbutsu-shūgō deity combining Indian naga-king Kumbhīra with local maritime guardian and Miwa elements; Meiji government forcibly re-identified him as Ōmononushi in 1868 to de-Buddhify the shrine. Maritime functions are syncretic, not in early Ōmononushi texts.

Himetatara-Isuzuhime was definitely Ōmononushi's daughter, proving Miwa ancestry of imperial line. — Sources directly contradict: Kojiki/Shoki main text say Ōmononushi, but Shoki variant and later volumes say Kotoshironushi via wani transformation. Both traditions were preserved, showing contested genealogy, not settled fact.
