Who It Is

Born a slave, he stormed heaven. Offered immortality, he refused to taste it. Made the mightiest god's personal mount, he chose service over sovereignty. Garuda is everything a mythological figure should be: a bird of prey with the soul of a philosopher and the wingspan of a hurricane.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Sanskrit Garuḍa (गरुड). The root may derive from the Vedic adjective garutmat ('winged'), or possibly from gṛ ('to swallow'), referencing his immense size and role as devourer of serpents. Pali form: Garuḷa.

Aliases — Garuḍa (Sanskrit), Vainateya (son of Vinata), Tarkshya, Suparna ('beautiful wings'), Khageshvara ('Lord of Birds'), Nagantaka ('Destroyer of Serpents')

Powers
Cosmic flight and immense speed

Garuda's wingspan was said to darken the sky, and his speed was such that he could circle the universe in moments. When he beat his wings, mountains trembled and oceans parted. He could traverse the three worlds (heaven, earth, underworld) at will.

Limit:

Innate serpent-enmity and immunity

Garuda was born with an inherent hostility toward all nagas (serpents) and the power to devour them with impunity. His very presence drove serpents to flee or become paralyzed with fear. This enmity arose from his mother's subjugation by Kadru.

Limit:

Immense physical size and strength

At birth, Garuda's radiance rivaled the cosmic fire at the end of time. He could carry Vishnu, Lakshmi, and the entire earth on his wings. He once uprooted a massive banyan tree to perch upon, and carried an elephant and tortoise — two ancient sages in animal form — in his talons.

Limit:

Shape-shifting and size manipulation

Garuda could shrink to the size of a mosquito or expand to blot out the sun. This ability was crucial when he penetrated the defenses surrounding the amrita — he became small enough to pass through the razor-wheel barrier.

Limit:

Vedic wisdom and healing knowledge

The Garuda Purana attributes extensive knowledge of medicine, astrology, gemology, and the afterlife to Garuda. He is the narrator's interlocutor, asking questions that elicit Vishnu's teachings on death, karma, and rebirth.

Limit:

Immunity to divine weapons and poison

When Indra struck Garuda with his thunderbolt (vajra) during the theft of amrita, Garuda was unharmed. He merely shed a single feather in respect for the weapon's divine origin. He was immune to serpent venom.

Accounts I
The Birth of Garuda and the Enslavement of Vinata

The sage Kashyapa had two wives: Vinata and Kadru. Kadru asked for a thousand powerful naga sons; Vinata asked for two sons mightier than Kadru's. Kadru's eggs hatched quickly into serpents. Vinata's eggs did not hatch. Impatient, she cracked one egg open — revealing Aruna, only half-formed, who cursed his mother to five hundred years of slavery for her impatience and then rose to become the Sun's charioteer. She waited the full five hundred years for the second egg, from which Garuda burst forth in blazing splendor. Meanwhile, Kadru had tricked Vinata into a wager about the color of the divine horse Uchchaihshravas's tail. Kadru had her black serpent children cling to the white horse's tail, making it appear black. Vinata lost the wager and became Kadru's slave. Garuda was born into this servitude and swore to free his mother.

The Theft of Amrita — Garuda's Cosmic Heist

The serpents agreed to free Vinata if Garuda would bring them the amrita (nectar of immortality) from the gods. Garuda flew to Indra's heaven, passing through three obstacles: a ring of fire extinguished with water from rivers he carried in his beak; a spinning razor-wheel through which he shrank to pass; and two venomous serpents he killed with a blast of dust from his wings. He seized the amrita vessel and flew away. Indra struck him with the vajra (thunderbolt), but Garuda was unharmed and only dropped a single feather in respect. Impressed, Indra made a pact: Garuda would let Indra recover the amrita after the serpents were freed. Garuda delivered the amrita to the serpents, placed it on kusha grass, told them to bathe first, and while they bathed, Indra retrieved the nectar. The serpents licked the grass where the amrita had rested, splitting their tongues — which is why snakes have forked tongues.

Garuda Becomes Vishnu's Mount

During the amrita heist, Vishnu appeared to Garuda and, impressed by his self-restraint — he had not tasted even a drop of the amrita despite carrying it — offered him a boon. Garuda chose to be immortal and to serve as Vishnu's mount, positioned above him. Vishnu granted both, making Garuda his eternal vahana. Subsequently, Garuda's image was placed atop Vishnu's battle standard, and every Vaishnava temple features him kneeling at the entrance. The relationship between Vishnu and Garuda is unique in Hindu iconography: the god rides a being who is himself divine.

Accounts II
Garuda, the Elephant, and the Tortoise

Flying with a massive banyan tree branch as a perch, Garuda saw two sages — Vibhavasu and Supratika — who had been transformed into an elephant and a tortoise by a curse. The two were locked in eternal combat. Garuda seized both in his talons and continued flying, looking for a place to land. He found a branch of a celestial tree, but it broke under his weight. Still holding the branch — on which the diminutive sages known as the Valakhilyas were meditating — Garuda flew until Vishnu advised him to release the branch gently. He did so, and the Valakhilyas, grateful for his care, blessed him. This story emphasizes Garuda's combination of immense power and conscientious restraint.

Garuda Frees His Mother Through the Serpent Ransom

After the amrita heist, Garuda returned to the serpents' realm. The serpents released Vinata from slavery, honoring the deal. Garuda, having fulfilled his duty as a son, then took his vengeance: from that day forward, he and his descendants became the eternal devourers of serpents. The Garuda Purana adds that the mantra 'Garuda' chanted near a snakebite victim drives away the venom — the eternal opposition between eagle and serpent became encoded in Hindu ritual practice.

Garuda as the Narrator's Companion in the Garuda Purana

The Garuda Purana — one of the eighteen Mahapuranas — is structured as a dialogue between Vishnu and Garuda. Garuda poses questions about death, the afterlife, karma, ethics, medicine, and astrology, which Vishnu answers in detail. Unlike the martial epics, this Purana presents Garuda as a wise and inquiring disciple, showing his intellectual dimension. The text is ritually recited at Hindu funerals, especially in North India, making Garuda's voice the medium through which the living learn how to guide the dead.

Garuda in Buddhist Jataka: The Supanna Tales

In the Buddhist Pandara Jataka and other tales, Garuda (called Supanna or Garula) appears as a king of divine birds who waged war against the nagas. The Buddha, in a past life, mediated peace between them. In some versions, the garudas abduct naga princesses, sparking cosmic wars. Buddhist garudas are morally ambiguous — powerful but not always enlightened. In Chinese and Japanese Buddhism, the Karura (Garuda) is one of the eight guardian deities (Hachibushu), often depicted as a fire-breathing bird protecting the Dharma.
