Who They Are

Ah Puch (also called Cizin or Kisin) is the Maya god of death, decay, and the underworld. He is a horrifying figure depicted as a skeletal being covered in black spots (representing decomposition), with a bloated belly (from the gases of decay) and an owl on his head. He presides over Xibalba, the Maya underworld, and his name means 'The Flatuator' — a reference to the gases released by decomposing bodies.

The Approach

In the dark heart of the Maya underworld, a god of rot and ruin awaits the souls of the dead. He is Cizin, the Stinking One, whose breath is foul with decay and whose touch brings earthquakes. To the ancient Maya he was a fearsome lord of death, but his story echoes through time, from colonial chronicles to modern video games and novels.

Etymology

The name Cizin likely derives from the Yucatec Maya root 'ciz', meaning flatulence or foul stench, thus 'Stinking One'. 'Kisin' may be a variant. 'Ah Puch' may mean 'to melt' or 'to decompose'. 'Yum Cimil' translates as 'Lord of Death', 'Hun Ahau' as 'One Ruler'.

Cultural Ownership

Maya civilization, primarily Yucatán Peninsula and Lacandon region. The deity is shared across Maya cultural groups with regional variations.

Disputed Identifications

Scholars identify Cizin as God A in the Maya codices. Some equate him with Hun-Came and Vucub-Came from the Popol Vuh, though these are also names of the lords of Xibalba. Similarities to Aztec Mictlāntēcutli have been noted but are not direct identifications.

Description

Appearance

Cizin is typically depicted as a skeletal figure, often with a bloated belly, decaying flesh, and a foul odor. In codices, he may appear with a black body, owl-like features, or a death collar. Some depictions show him with a backward-facing head or as a dancing skeleton.

Symbols & Iconography

Associated with death, decay, earthquakes, and the underworld. Symbols include skeletal remains, the owl (a death omen), the dog (psychopomp), and the 'death eyes' (closed or crossed eyes). He is often shown with a collar of eyes or a necklace of death.

Behaviour & Personality

Cizin is a malevolent deity who delights in destruction and the torment of souls. He is associated with violent death, disease, and natural disasters. In some myths, he actively hunts souls and burns them.

Domains & Habitats

Ruler of the underworld (Metnal or Xibalba), though the specific name varies. He is also a god of earthquakes, causing the earth to tremble. He dwells in a place of decay and stench.

Version Differences

Yucatec Maya traditions emphasize his role as a death god and earthquake bringer. Lacandon Maya myths describe him as a soul-burner who tortures the dead. In the Popol Vuh, the lords of Xibalba (Hun-Came and Vucub-Came) are not explicitly called Cizin, but scholars often equate them. Modern interpretations, such as in video games, may depict him differently.

Stories & Myths

Lacandon Soul Burning

Source Lacandon oral traditionCulture Lacandon MayaPeriod 20th century (recorded)

Summary

When a person dies, Cizin burns the soul on his mouth and anus. If the soul complains, he douses it in cold water, causing more pain, and burns it again until it disintegrates into nothing. The soul then goes to the god Sucunyum, who cleanses it by spitting on his hand, allowing it to go where it pleases.

Cultural Significance

Ah Puch's cult was widespread but feared — the Maya did not pray to him willingly. He was the deity most associated with the dangerous, unpredictable aspects of existence. The Dresden Codex includes extensive rituals for propitiating Ah Puch and warding off his influence. In modern Maya communities, especially in the highlands of Guatemala, Kisin (as he is known in Tzotzil Maya) remains a feared figure, and offerings are made to him to keep him at a distance.
