Who They Are

Ah Puch (also called Cizin or Kisin) is the Maya god of death, decay, and the underworld. He is a horrifying figure depicted as a skeletal being covered in black spots (representing decomposition), with a bloated belly (from the gases of decay) and an owl on his head. He presides over Xibalba, the Maya underworld, and his name means 'The Flatuator' — a reference to the gases released by decomposing bodies.

The Approach

In the humid dark of the Yucatán, where the earth itself can shudder and crack, there was a god who did not rule the dead from a distant throne, but who came to you smelling of rot, his name a whisper of stench. This is Cizin, the Stinking One, the Lord of Decay, the Shaker of the Earth — a deity not of distant judgment, but of the immediate, visceral horror of death itself. His power was not in a book of judgment, but in the burning of your soul, the crumbling of your bones, and the very ground beneath your feet. He is a god to be feared, not loved, and his story is a descent into the terrifying heart of Maya belief.

Content advisory. Descriptions of death, decay, soul torture (burning), and references to human sacrifice. The Lacandon myth of soul burning is particularly graphic.

Etymology

The name Cizin likely derives from the Yucatec Maya root 'ciz,' meaning flatulence or foul stench, giving the name the sense of 'Stinking One,' a reference to the smell of decay. 'Ah Puch' is often translated as 'to melt' or 'to dissolve,' possibly referring to the decomposition of the body. 'Yum Cimil' translates to 'Lord of Death.' 'Hun Ahau' means 'One Ruler.'

Cultural Ownership

Yucatec Maya, Lacandon Maya, and broader Maya civilization (pre-Columbian Mesoamerica, specifically the Yucatán Peninsula region)

Disputed Identifications

Scholars generally refer to this deity as 'God A' in the post-Classic codices due to the multiple names and forms. The identification of Cizin/Kisin with Hunhau (a lord of the underworld in the Popol Vuh) is common but not universally agreed upon. Some scholars argue that Kisin (the 'flatulent one') is a distinct earthquake demon separate from the death god Ah Puch. The names Hun-Came and Vucub-Came are specifically from the K'iche' Maya Popol Vuh and their direct equivalence to Yucatec Cizin is a scholarly interpretation rather than an attested fact. Tzontemoc is a Nahuatl name (associated with Mictlāntēcutli), suggesting Aztec influence or scholarly conflation.

Cultural Ownership Note

The deity is primarily Yucatec and Lacandon Maya, but the scholarly category 'God A' spans multiple Maya regions and time periods. The Nahuatl name Tzontemoc indicates a syncretic or comparative layer.

Description

Appearance

In the codices, Cizin (God A) is typically depicted as a bloated, corpse-like figure with a skeletal jaw, often shown with black spots on his body representing decay. He may wear a collar of eyes (death eyes) and has been depicted with a large, sometimes twisted or phallic nose. His head is often shown with a distinctive 'death collar' and a skeletal or fleshless lower face. He is frequently shown in association with sacrificial victims, owls, and other death omens. In some representations, he is depicted as a dancing figure holding a staff or a sacrificial knife. The Lacandon Maya describe him as a being who smells of decay, aligning with the etymology of his name.

Symbols & Iconography

Key symbols include: the skeletal jaw, black body spots (decay), a 'death collar' (often with eyes), association with owls (specifically the screech owl, a death omen), the cimi (death) glyph, and sacrificial implements. He is often painted in black and white or with a black body. His association with the east and the rising sun in some contexts is noted, but his primary domain is the underworld (Metnal/Xibalba).

Behaviour & Personality

Cizin is a malevolent and destructive deity. He is the lord of death, disease, and earthquakes. He is not a trickster or a benevolent psychopomp; rather, he is an active agent of destruction who delights in the demise of humans. He is associated with war, sacrifice, and the brutal aspects of the afterlife. In Lacandon myth, he is an active tormentor of souls, burning them until they disintegrate. He is a figure to be feared and placated, not worshipped in a positive sense.

Domains & Habitats

His primary domain is Metnal, the Yucatec Maya underworld, a place of torment. He is also associated with the earth itself, as he is the god of earthquakes, which he causes by shaking the earth. He is connected to the east, where the sun rises, in some Yucatec traditions, though this connection is complex. He is also associated with battlefields and places of sacrifice.

Version Differences

There is significant variation between the Yucatec and Lacandon versions. The Yucatec Cizin/Ah Puch is a more generalized lord of death and the underworld. The Lacandon Kisin is more specifically an agent of punishment who burns souls. The K'iche' Popol Vuh presents Hun-Came and Vucub-Came as powerful lords of Xibalba who are ultimately tricked and defeated by the Hero Twins, a narrative that does not feature in the Yucatec sources. The Aztec name Tzontemoc suggests a conflation with Mictlāntēcutli, the Aztec lord of the dead, but this is a comparative association rather than an indigenous Maya identification.

Powers & Abilities

Lordship over Death

Source Version Yucatec, Lacandon, and general Maya (God A)

Description

As the primary Maya death god, Cizin rules over the dead and the process of dying. He is the ultimate authority in Metnal, the underworld.

Limitations

His power is generally confined to the underworld and the moment of death, though he can be invoked or appear on earth.

Contradictions

The extent of his power over the soul's final fate varies. In Lacandon myth, he destroys the soul, while in other traditions, the soul may go on to a different afterlife (e.g., the paradise of the Itzamna).

Causing Earthquakes

Source Version Yucatec Maya

Description

Cizin is the god of earthquakes. He is believed to shake the earth, causing destruction and death.

Limitations

Specific myths about how he causes earthquakes are not well-documented in surviving sources.

Contradictions

Some sources associate earthquakes with the Bacabs (who hold up the sky), suggesting a potential overlap or conflation of roles.

Soul Burning

Source Version Lacandon Maya

Description

In Lacandon tradition, Cizin burns the soul of the deceased on his mouth and anus. He douses the soul in cold water to make it complain more, then burns it again until it disintegrates into nothing.

Limitations

This power is post-mortem and specific to the Lacandon version of the afterlife.

Contradictions

The Lacandon tradition also includes the god Sucunyum, who cleanses the soul after Cizin's torment, allowing it to go free. This suggests Cizin's destruction is not absolute or that there is a counterbalancing force.

Causing Disease and Pestilence

Source Version General Maya (inferred from his role and iconography)

Description

As a death god, Cizin is associated with disease, pestilence, and illness. He is seen as a source of sickness that leads to death.

Limitations

Specific rituals to ward off his diseases are not as well-documented as those for other deities.

Contradictions

None noted.

Association with Sacrifice

Source Version Post-Classic Maya codices and iconography

Description

He is closely associated with human sacrifice, particularly heart extraction, and with the souls of warriors and sacrificial victims.

Limitations

His role in the sacrificial process is more as a receiver of the dead than as an active participant in the ritual.

Contradictions

None noted.

Stories & Myths

The Torment of Souls (Lacandon)

Source Lacandon oral tradition (documented by Robert Bruce and others)Culture Lacandon MayaPeriod 20th century (documented)

Summary

When a person dies, their soul (or 'spirit') goes to Cizin (Kisin). Cizin burns the soul on his mouth and his anus. When the soul complains, he douses it in cold water, causing it to complain even more. He then burns it again, repeating this process until the soul disintegrates into nothing. After this, the soul goes to the god Sucunyum, who spits in his hand to clean the soul, allowing it to go wherever it pleases.

Participants

Cizin (Kisin)

The deceased soul

Sucunyum

Consequences

The soul is destroyed by Cizin, but then 'reassembled' or cleansed by Sucunyum, suggesting a complex cycle of destruction and renewal. The soul is then free.

Variants

This is a distinctly Lacandon version. Other Maya groups likely had different beliefs about the soul's journey, though the concept of a dangerous underworld lord is common.

The Lords of Xibalba (Popol Vuh)

Source Popol VuhCulture K'iche' MayaPeriod Pre-Columbian, transcribed 16th-18th century

Summary

Hun-Came (One Death) and Vucub-Came (Seven Death) are the principal lords of Xibalba, the underworld. They invite the Hero Twins, Hunahpu and Xbalanque, to Xibalba for a ball game, but their true intent is to kill them. The Twins survive a series of deadly trials (the House of Darkness, the House of Knives, etc.) through cunning and magic. They eventually trick the lords into being sacrificed themselves, but then resurrect and defeat them, limiting their power.

Participants

Hun-Came

Vucub-Came

Hunahpu

Xbalanque

Other Lords of Xibalba

Consequences

The Hero Twins defeat death, establishing the precedence of the Maize God (their father) and the cycle of life over death. The power of the Xibalba lords is diminished, though they still rule the underworld.

Variants

This is the most famous Maya underworld narrative. It is K'iche', not Yucatec, and the direct identification of Hun-Came with Ah Puch is a scholarly link, not a direct textual equivalence.

Death as a Punishment

Source Diego de Landa's Relación de las cosas de YucatánCulture Yucatec Maya (post-colonial record)Period 16th century

Summary

Landa records that the Yucatec Maya believed that those who died by certain means (suicide, sacrifice, war, childbirth) went to a paradise, while those who died of ordinary illness went to a 'place of torment' ruled by a demon. This demon is often identified with Ah Puch/Hunhau, who would torment the souls.

Participants

The demon (Ah Puch/Hunhau)

The deceased souls

Consequences

This establishes a moral or circumstantial dimension to the afterlife, where the manner of death determines the soul's fate.

Variants

This is a colonial-era record, filtered through Landa's Christian perspective, and may not fully capture the pre-Columbian belief.

Dark History

Soul Torment

Source Lacandon oral tradition (as cited in provided material)

Description

The Lacandon myth describes Cizin burning the soul of the deceased on his mouth and anus, dousing them in cold water to prolong their suffering, and burning them until they disintegrate. This is a graphic depiction of post-mortem torture.

Scholarly Context

This myth reflects a belief in a punitive afterlife, which may have been used to enforce social norms. The burning and cleansing cycle can also be interpreted as a form of purification.

Human Sacrifice

Source Post-Classic Maya iconography and codices

Description

Cizin, as the lord of death, is associated with human sacrifice, particularly heart extraction. The souls of sacrificial victims were seen as a form of sustenance for the gods, including the death god.

Scholarly Context

Human sacrifice was a central part of Maya religion, believed to be necessary to maintain cosmic order and appease the gods. Cizin's association with it highlights his role as a consumer of death.

Earthquakes as Destruction

Source Yucatec Maya belief

Description

Cizin is the god of earthquakes, a natural force that causes death and destruction. This aligns him with a terrifying and uncontrollable aspect of the natural world.

Scholarly Context

Earthquakes are a real and frequent threat in the Yucatán, making Cizin a god of real, tangible terror, not just a remote underworld figure.

Colonial Distortion

Source Relación de las cosas de Yucatán

Description

Diego de Landa, a Spanish friar, described the Maya underworld as a 'place of torment' ruled by a demon. This description may have been colored by Christian concepts of Hell and used to justify the forced conversion of the Maya.

Scholarly Context

Colonial records must be read critically, as they often reflect the biases of the European authors and their agenda of religious conquest.

Contradictions & Disputes

Identity of the 'True' Death God

Position A

Cizin/Kisin is the primary Yucatec Maya death god, often identified with Ah Puch.

Position A — evidence

Yucatec sources and the provided material list Cizin/Kisin as the primary death god.

Position B

Hunhau is a separate, distinct death god, sometimes identified with Ah Puch, but not with Cizin/Kisin.

Position B — evidence

Some scholars and colonial sources distinguish between Hunhau and Kisin, with Hunhau being a more specific underworld lord.

Scholarly Consensus

There is no consensus. The names are often used interchangeably in popular and semi-scholarly sources, but specialists note regional and temporal differences. The umbrella term 'God A' is used to avoid this issue.

Cizin/Kisin as Earthquake God

Position A

Cizin/Kisin is the god of earthquakes.

Position A — evidence

The provided material explicitly states Cizin is the god of earthquakes.

Position B

The Bacabs, or other earth deities, are responsible for earthquakes.

Position B — evidence

In some Maya traditions, the Bacabs hold up the sky and their movements cause earthquakes. Cizin is a brother of the Bacabs in Lacandon myth, suggesting a possible conflation or shared role.

Scholarly Consensus

This is a point of variation. Cizin's role as an earthquake god is specific to certain Yucatec traditions, while other myths attribute earthquakes to other beings.

The Soul's Final Fate

Position A

Cizin destroys the soul completely (Lacandon).

Position A — evidence

The Lacandon myth of soul burning.

Position B

The soul is tormented but not destroyed, or goes to a different paradise (Yucatec, K'iche').

Position B — evidence

The K'iche' Popol Vuh and various Yucatec traditions describe different afterlives, including paradises, suggesting the soul is not universally destroyed.

Scholarly Consensus

There is no single Maya belief about the afterlife. The concept varied significantly between regions and time periods.

Hun-Came/Vucub-Came as Cizin

Position A

Hun-Came (One Death) and Vucub-Came (Seven Death) are the Yucatec Cizin under different names.

Position A — evidence

The provided material states 'To the Yucatán Mayas he was Hun-Came and Vucub-Came.'

Position B

Hun-Came and Vucub-Came are K'iche' gods, distinct from the Yucatec Cizin.

Position B — evidence

The Popol Vuh is a K'iche' text, and the lords of Xibalba are specific to that tradition. There is no direct evidence that the Yucatec Maya used these names for their death god.

Scholarly Consensus

This is a scholarly conflation. The names are not attested in Yucatec sources. The identification is based on functional similarity (lords of the underworld) rather than direct textual equivalence.

Cultural Significance

Ah Puch's cult was widespread but feared — the Maya did not pray to him willingly. He was the deity most associated with the dangerous, unpredictable aspects of existence. The Dresden Codex includes extensive rituals for propitiating Ah Puch and warding off his influence. In modern Maya communities, especially in the highlands of Guatemala, Kisin (as he is known in Tzotzil Maya) remains a feared figure, and offerings are made to him to keep him at a distance.
