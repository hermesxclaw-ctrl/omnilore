Who It Is

Uriel is the archangel who does not fight. While Michael wields a sword and Raphael carries a healing salve, Uriel carries a book, a cup, or a key — instruments of interpretation, custody, and revelation. He is the figure sent not to strike but to warn, not to heal but to explain, not to conquer but to stand at the gate and keep the boundary. This makes him the most intellectually intense of the archangels and the most alien to a culture that prizes warrior-saviors and miracle-workers. His portfolio — wisdom, truth, repentance, Tartarus, the sun, the North, the arts — is not a single coherent job description but a map of the places other archangels refuse to go.

The textual evidence depicts Uriel as an obedient, stern, and pedagogically rigorous figure rather than a compassionate intercessor. In 2 Esdras he answers Ezra's anguished questions with allegory and theological exposition, refusing to soften the message of judgment. In the Book of Enoch he executes the Most High's command to warn Noah without elaboration or comfort. In the Apocalypse of Peter he appears as 'the angel of repentance, who is graphically represented as being as pitiless as any demon' — a figure whose severity is not softened by pastoral warmth. Powers
Interpretation of apocalyptic visions

Uriel's primary attested power is the interpretation of prophetic and apocalyptic revelation. In 2 Esdras he explains to Ezra the meaning of the end-times vision, using allegorical method (a city built on a field, a forest planted in a city) to reveal that the messianic age arrives through supernatural intervention rather than historical process. This is not merely 'wisdom' but a specific hermeneutical authority — the capacity to decode hidden structures of time and fate.

Limit: Uriel's interpretive power is bounded by God's will; he answers Ezra's questions but refuses to reveal the full timeline of judgment, stating that the 'Most High' has not yet disclosed the 'end of the times.' He operates as an authorized revealer, not an autonomous seer.

Oversight of Tartarus and the abyss

In 1 Enoch 20:2 Uriel is described as the angel 'who is over the world and over Tartarus,' giving him jurisdiction over the prison of the fallen Watchers and the spirits of the dead. This is not a punitive power like Michael's combat role but an administrative and custodial authority — he stands at the gate of the deep and keeps the chaotic forces confined.

Limit: Uriel's custodianship of Tartarus is passive rather than active; he does not judge or punish but maintains the boundary. The fallen spirits 'stand till the day of the great judgment,' suggesting his authority is temporal and delegable.

Warning and prophetic announcement

Uriel is the angel sent by the Most High to warn Noah of the coming flood (1 Enoch 10:1–3). The Most High commands Uriel to 'go to Noah and tell him in my name Hide thyself and reveal to him the end that is approaching.' This is a one-way prophetic delivery: Uriel conveys the warning but does not negotiate, delay, or modify the judgment.

Limit: Uriel's warning is effective only for the one chosen (Noah); the text does not describe him as a mass-messenger or a figure who intervenes on behalf of groups.

Gatekeeping of Eden

Uriel is 'often identified as a cherub and the angel of repentance. He stands at the Gate of Eden with a fiery sword,' continuing the cherubic guard function from Genesis 3 after the fall. In the Life of Adam and Eve he is regarded as the spirit (one of the cherubs) of the third chapter of Genesis, and as one of the angels who helped bury Adam and Abel in Eden.

Limit: The Eden-gatekeeper role is shared with or transferred to other cherubic figures in different sources; Uriel's claim is strongest in the Adam and Eve tradition, weaker in canonical Genesis.

Patron of arts and sciences

In Eastern Orthodox and Anglican tradition Uriel is patron saint of the arts and sciences. The Ethiopian Orthodox iconography links him to the founding of churches (and thus sacred architecture and music). Thomas Heywood's 1635 Hierarchy of Blessed Angels describes him as an angel of the earth, and the University of Oxford's motto 'Dominus illuminatio mea' ('The Lord is my light') is explicitly interpreted as a translation of Uriel's name into Latin. In modern angelology he is identified as regent of the sun and constellations, presider over knowledge, and inspiration for creative work.

Limit: This role is post-medieval and confessional, not attested in ancient sources. It reflects the expansion of Uriel's portfolio in late medieval and early modern Christianity rather than original Jewish or early Christian tradition.

Accounts I
Uriel warns Noah of the FloodBook of Enoch (1 Enoch), Book of the Watchers

3rd–2nd century BCE

After the Watchers (Grigori) have taken human wives and produced the Nephilim, corrupting the earth, the Most High commissions Uriel to warn Noah of the coming deluge. Uriel is sent specifically because he 'is over the world and over Tartarus' and therefore has jurisdiction over the boundaries of human and cosmic existence. His message to Noah is direct and urgent: 'Hide thyself and reveal to him the end that is approaching: that the whole earth will be destroyed, and a deluge is about to come upon the whole earth, and will destroy all that is on it.' This makes Uriel the direct conduit through which divine preservation operates — not by negotiating with the chaos, but by forewarning the one vessel of continuity. Noah builds the ark and survives the flood, preserving the human line. Uriel's warning is the mechanism by which the divine decision to destroy the earth is partially circumvented for one righteous household. The narrative also positions Uriel as the eschatological archangel par excellence: he appears not as a warrior but as an interpreter and herald of cosmic transition.

Uriel interprets Ezra's apocalyptic visionSecond Book of Esdras (2 Esdras / 4 Esdras)

late 1st century CE

The prophet Ezra, devastated by the destruction of Jerusalem and the seeming abandonment of the covenant, demands that God explain the course of history and the fate of Israel. God answers not directly but through the angel Uriel, who engages Ezra in a sustained dialectical exchange over seven days. Uriel uses a series of allegorical images — a city built on a field, a forest planted in a city, a dragon guarding a palace, a sea with two paths accessible only by fire and water — to demonstrate that human reason cannot penetrate divine counsel. Ezra's anguished question 'Why does Israel not possess the world?' is met with the answer that the current age is under the sway of a hostile power, but that the messianic age will come 'not by earthquake, not by fire, not by pestilence, but by a sign' — a supernatural unveiling that bypasses historical process entirely. Ezra is comforted but not fully answered; the apocalyptic framework of 2 Esdras — that judgment is delayed but inevitable, and that the messianic intervention is supranatural — became foundational for Christian eschatology. Accounts II
Uriel and the Massacre of the InnocentsGospel of Pseudo-Matthew (apocryphal gospel)

8th–9th century CE (composition); earlier oral tradition

After the Magi depart, Herod orders the Massacre of the Innocents in Bethlehem. According to the apocryphal Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew, the archangel Uriel appears to Joseph in a dream and warns him to flee with Mary and Jesus to Egypt. In some recensions Uriel also rescues John the Baptist from the slaughter by carrying him and his mother Elizabeth to join the Holy Family during the Flight into Egypt. Their reunion is depicted in Leonardo da Vinci's Virgin of the Rocks, where the infant John may represent Uriel's rescue. This narrative established Uriel as a protector of the holy family and gave him an active Christological role in medieval piety. It also explains why Uriel became associated with Egypt and, through the Homily tradition, with Ethiopia — the flight routes became pilgrimage routes for his cult.

Uriel at the Crucifixion and the Ethiopian blood-sprinklingHomily on the Archangel Uriel (Ethiopian Orthodox tradition)

medieval (Ge'ez homiletic corpus)

At the moment of Christ's crucifixion, Uriel dips his wing in the blood and water flowing from Christ's pierced side and fills a cup with it. He and Michael then rush through the world sprinkling the blood over Ethiopia; in every place where a drop falls, a church is built. This narrative explains the dense network of Ethiopian Orthodox churches and establishes Uriel's distinctive chalice iconography. The story also functions as an origin myth for Ethiopian Christian identity, claiming that the land was evangelized not by human missionaries but directly by archangelic agency. This is the foundational Uriel narrative in Ethiopian tradition. It creates a direct physical link between the crucifixion and Ethiopian sacred geography. The chalice became Uriel's primary attribute in Ethiopian Orthodox art, distinguishing him from other archangels who carry swords, scrolls, or trumpets.

Uriel and the Council of Rome: from cult to suppressionCouncil of Rome (745 CE); Patrologia Latina LXXXIX

8th century CE

In 745 Pope Zachary convened the Council of Rome to address widespread angelolatry — the excessive veneration of angels that bordered on treating them as independent deities rather than ministers of the one God. The council condemned 'obsession with angelic intervention' and struck Uriel's name, among others, from the list of angels eligible for public veneration in the Roman Church. Only Michael, Gabriel, and Raphael — the three named in canonical scripture — remained licit objects of cultic reverence. Uriel's public cult was driven underground or redirected into folk Catholicism and Orthodox practice, where his veneration continued unabated. This is the single event that created the modern Catholic anomaly: Uriel is one of the best-known archangels in world culture yet has no official Catholic feast day, no public Mass, and no canonical recognition. The suppression paradoxically amplified his cultural presence — folk Catholicism kept his cult alive in Sicily, Spain, and Latin America, while Eastern Orthodoxy, Anglicanism, and Lutheranism preserved his formal veneration. Uriel is one of the three canonical Catholic archangels alongside Michael and Gabriel. — Uriel is not one of the three canonical Catholic archangels. The Catholic Church recognizes only Michael, Gabriel, and Raphael as archangels because they are the only ones named in canonical scripture. Uriel was explicitly struck from the veneration list at the Council of Rome in 745. Catholics may privately venerate Uriel, but there is no official feast day, no public Mass, and no canonical recognition.

Uriel is named in the Quran as one of the primary archangels. — The Quran names only Gabriel and Michael explicitly (Quran 2:98). Uriel (Mīkāʾīl in Arabic) is not mentioned. Islamic angelology does include a group of four or more archangels (Jibril, Mikail, Israfil, Azrael), but Uriel/Mikail is not among them; the Arabic Mīkāʾīl is a distinct figure associated with mercy and natural sustenance, not wisdom or repentance.

Uriel is the archangel of death. — Uriel is not the archangel of death in any primary source. The archangel of death in Jewish and Islamic tradition is Azrael, a figure sometimes conflated with Uriel in medieval Kabbalistic and occult texts. In the Book of Enoch Uriel oversees Tartarus and the abyss, but this is custodial rather than lethal. In the Apocalypse of Peter he is the angel of repentance, not death. The death-archangel association is a later occult conflation.

Uriel appears in the Bible as one of the named archangels. — Uriel does not appear in the Hebrew Bible, the New Testament, or the Quran by name. His appearances are confined to apocryphal and pseudepigraphical texts (1 Enoch, 2 Esdras, Testament of Solomon, Apocalypse of Peter, Life of Adam and Eve) and extracanonical traditions (Kabbalah, Merkabah, Homily on the Archangel Uriel). He is 'biblical' only in the loosest sense — known from texts that orbit the biblical canon but are not part of it in most traditions.

Uriel is the archangel who wrestled with Jacob at Peniel. — The figure who wrestles with Jacob at Peniel (Genesis 32:24–32) is never named in the text. Rabbinic tradition variously identifies him as Esau's guardian angel, Michael, or a theophany of God himself. Uriel is never associated with the Peniel wrestling in primary sources; the identification appears only in modern pop-culture mashups.
