Who It Is

Few myths are more haunted than the murder of Osiris. Slain by his brother, cut to pieces, and scattered across the length of the Nile valley, the god of the afterlife still found a way to return — and to leave his footprint on every corner of the ancient world and far beyond. Osiris is the god who had to be destroyed in order to be reborn, the king who lost his kingdom so that his son could win it back, and the mummy who gives every death a promise of resurrection. His is the oldest passion play on record, re-enacted for three thousand years, and his is the door through which the ancient Egyptians — and everyone who followed them — learned to hope that death is not the end.

Osiris was the great god of fertility, agriculture, the afterlife, the dead, resurrection, life and vegetation in ancient Egyptian religion — in the mature tradition, the pre-eminent lord of the underworld and judge of the dead. He was counted the eldest son of the earth-god Geb and the sky-goddess Nut; brother and husband of Isis; brother of Set, Nephthys and Horus the Elder; and posthumous father of Horus the Younger. Resurrection and rebirth of the dead

Osiris was the dying-and-rising god par excellence, the first to be associated with actual mummification. By being made whole again after dismemberment and living on in the Duat, he became the archetype of posthumous restoration: 'as Osiris rose from the dead, they [the dead pharaoh] would unite with him and inherit eternal life through imitative magic.' His revival was the mythological basis of all Egyptian embalming practice.

Limit:

Fertility, vegetation and the Nile inundation

Osiris's death-and-return was mapped onto the death and re-growth of crops and the flooding and retreating of the Nile. His regenerative 'Osiris beds' — mummy-shaped beds of soil sown with grain that germinated after watering — connected his resurrection with the seasonal growth of plants. Recent scholars emphasise 'the androgynous character of [Osiris's] fertility': he must be both castrated/dismembered and reassembled by the female Isis, whose embrace produces the perfect king Horus.

Limit:

Judgement of the dead

As king and regent of the underworld, Osiris presided over the posthumous tribunal. A person faced judgement by a tribunal of forty-two divine judges (the 42 Assessors of Maat); if found true to Maat, truth and right living, they were welcomed into his kingdom; if found guilty they were thrown to the soul-eating demon Ammit and failed to share in eternal life.

Limit:

Granting eternal life and the heliacal order

Osiris became 'the sovereign that granted all life, He Who is Permanently Benign and Youthful.' Through the hope of new life after death he became associated with the cycles of nature — the sprouting of vegetation, the annual Nile flood, and the heliacal rising of Orion and Sirius that opened the new year — effectively renewing the sun and the world through his union with Ra in the Amduat and Book of Gates.

Limit:

Ram/ba power as Banebdjedet

Osiris's ba (reputation, force of character, divine power — not 'soul' in the Western sense) was worshipped semi-independently at Mendes as the ram-god Banebdjedet, 'the ba of the lord of the djed,' holder of epithets such as 'Lord of the Sky' and 'Life of Ra.' A living sacred ram was kept, then mummified and buried in a ram necropolis. As Banebdjed, Osiris was even said to be Horus's father.

Limit:

Sovereign judge of the throne succession

As primeval king whose throne is rightfully Horus's, Osiris underpinned the pharaonic ideology of father-to-son succession. His myth set the pattern of the Osirian triad — Osiris as dead king, Horus as living king on earth, Isis as the king's mother — which was the model for every pharaonic accession and for the sacred kingship itself.
