Who It Is

Osiris is one of the most theologically profound deities of ancient Egypt — the murdered king who became lord of the dead, the god whose death and resurrection promised eternal life to every Egyptian who underwent proper funerary rites, and the divine judge before whom every soul must stand. His cult, centered at Abydos, was among the most widespread and enduring in Egyptian history, surviving for over three millennia and influencing Mediterranean mystery religions. The myth of Osiris — most completely preserved in Plutarch's De Iside et Osiride (c. 100 CE), though attested in Egyptian sources from the Pyramid Texts onward — is a drama of murder, love, resurrection, and eternal justice. Osiris was the wise and benevolent king of Egypt who taught humanity agriculture, law, and religion. His jealous brother Set conspired to murder him: at a banquet, Set displayed a beautiful chest and promised it to whomever it perfectly fit. When Osiris lay inside, Set and his conspirators slammed the lid, sealed it with molten lead, and threw it into the Nile. The chest drifted to Byblos (in Phoenicia, modern Lebanon), where it became embedded in a tamarisk tree that was cut for a palace column. Isis, Osiris's devoted wife and sister, searched the world for his body, recovered the chest, and returned it to Egypt — only for Set to find the body and tear it into fourteen pieces, scattering them across the land. Isis, with her sister Nephthys and the god Anubis, searched and recovered all the pieces; with her magic, she reassembled the body and, taking the form of a kite (bird), fanned life into Osiris long enough to conceive their son, Horus. Osiris then descended to the underworld, where he became its lord and judge. This myth established the paradigm for Egyptian salvation: as Osiris was murdered, reassembled, and resurrected, so every deceased person — identified with Osiris in the funerary texts — could be 'justified' (ma'at kheru) and granted eternal life through proper ritual. The 'Osirification' of the dead was the central goal of Egyptian funerary practice. The annual 'Mysteries of Osiris' at Abydos reenacted his death and resurrection in elaborate public and secret rituals, attracting pilgrims from across Egypt who sought to erect stelae near Osiris's 'tomb' and thereby participate in his resurrection. Osiris is depicted as a mummified king, his green or black skin symbolizing vegetation and the fertile Nile soil, his body wrapped in white linen, holding the crook and flail of kingship, wearing the atef crown (the white crown of Upper Egypt flanked by ostrich feathers). His face is serene, reflecting the peace of one who has conquered death. In the Greco-Roman period, Osiris was syncretized with the Apis bull as Serapis, and his mystery cult spread throughout the Mediterranean, influencing the development of Christian resurrection theology. Osiris is the ultimate dying-and-rising god — the archetypal promise that death is not the end.

Form

Osiris was the great god of fertility, agriculture, the afterlife, the dead, resurrection, life and vegetation in ancient Egyptian religion — in the mature tradition, the pre-eminent lord of the underworld and judge of the dead. He was counted the eldest son of the earth-god Geb and the sky-goddess Nut; brother and husband of Isis; brother of Set, Nephthys and Horus the Elder; and posthumous father of Horus the Younger. Through syncretism with the moon-god Iah he was also a lunar deity. Mythically a primeval king of Egypt descended from the creator Ra/Atum, he was dismembered by his jealous brother Set, reassembled and briefly revived by Isis and Nephthys, and became the first mummy — thereafter ruling the Duat (underworld) while his son Horus ruled the land of the living. His death-and-rebirth mapped onto the annual Nile inundation, the sprouting of vegetation and the heliacal rising of Orion and Sirius at the new year, making him the sovereign who 'grants all life'. In royal ideology the dead pharaoh was assimilated to Osiris (as Horus personified the living king), and from the Middle Kingdom onward any virtuous Egyptian could hope to emulate Osiris's resurrection and reach the afterlife by passing his posthumous judgement (the weighing of the heart against Maat before the 42 Assessors). Osiris was among the most widely worshipped Egyptian deities; his cult persisted until the very end of Egyptian temple religion, and his mystery, the tale of the dismembered and restored god, outlasted the fall of the pharaohs into Greco-Roman literature and modern culture.

Powers

Resurrection and rebirth of the dead Osiris was the dying-and-rising god par excellence, the first to be associated with actual mummification. By being made whole again after dismemberment and living on in the Duat, he became the archetype of posthumous restoration: 'as Osiris rose from the dead, they [the dead pharaoh] would unite with him and inherit eternal life through imitative magic.' His revival was the mythological basis of all Egyptian embalming practice. afterlife / underworld

Fertility, vegetation and the Nile inundation Osiris's death-and-return was mapped onto the death and re-growth of crops and the flooding and retreating of the Nile. His regenerative 'Osiris beds' — mummy-shaped beds of soil sown with grain that germinated after watering — connected his resurrection with the seasonal growth of plants. Recent scholars emphasise 'the androgynous character of [Osiris's] fertility': he must be both castrated/dismembered and reassembled by the female Isis, whose embrace produces the perfect king Horus. nature / kingship

Judgement of the dead As king and regent of the underworld, Osiris presided over the posthumous tribunal. A person faced judgement by a tribunal of forty-two divine judges (the 42 Assessors of Maat); if found true to Maat, truth and right living, they were welcomed into his kingdom; if found guilty they were thrown to the soul-eating demon Ammit and failed to share in eternal life. afterlife / justice

Granting eternal life and the heliacal order Osiris became 'the sovereign that granted all life, He Who is Permanently Benign and Youthful.' Through the hope of new life after death he became associated with the cycles of nature — the sprouting of vegetation, the annual Nile flood, and the heliacal rising of Orion and Sirius that opened the new year — effectively renewing the sun and the world through his union with Ra in the Amduat and Book of Gates. cosmos / kingship

Ram/ba power as Banebdjedet Osiris's ba (reputation, force of character, divine power — not 'soul' in the Western sense) was worshipped semi-independently at Mendes as the ram-god Banebdjedet, 'the ba of the lord of the djed,' holder of epithets such as 'Lord of the Sky' and 'Life of Ra.' A living sacred ram was kept, then mummified and buried in a ram necropolis. As Banebdjed, Osiris was even said to be Horus's father. power / animal-cult

Sovereign judge of the throne succession As primeval king whose throne is rightfully Horus's, Osiris underpinned the pharaonic ideology of father-to-son succession. His myth set the pattern of the Osirian triad — Osiris as dead king, Horus as living king on earth, Isis as the king's mother — which was the model for every pharaonic accession and for the sacred kingship itself. kingship / order (maat)

A way to hold it in your mind

Few myths are more haunted than the murder of Osiris. Slain by his brother, cut to pieces, and scattered across the length of the Nile valley, the god of the afterlife still found a way to return — and to leave his footprint on every corner of the ancient world and far beyond. Osiris is the god who had to be destroyed in order to be reborn, the king who lost his kingdom so that his son could win it back, and the mummy who gives every death a promise of resurrection. His is the oldest passion play on record, re-enacted for three thousand years, and his is the door through which the ancient Egyptians — and everyone who followed them — learned to hope that death is not the end.
