The Approach

In the twilight of Indian Buddhism, a fierce deity emerged from the crucible of charnel grounds and transgressive yoga—Cakrasaṃvara, the 'Binding of the Wheels,' whose mandala wheels entwine the cosmos in a dance of subjugation and liberation. Born from the meeting of Buddhist and Śaiva worlds, this tantric lord still spins his wheel in the rituals of Nepal and Tibet, a living paradox of violence and bliss.

Content advisory. This dossier includes descriptions of ritualized sexual yoga, consumption of polluting substances, and violent subjugation myths. Some readers may find these elements disturbing. They are presented in their historical and religious context.

Etymology

Cakrasaṃvara is a Sanskrit compound: cakra (wheel, circle) + saṃvara (binding, vow, union). The full name can be interpreted as 'Binding of the Wheels,' referring to the mandala's wheels and the binding of the ḍākinī net. Saṃvara also means 'vow' or 'sanctuary.' In Tibetan, 'khor lo bde mchog' means 'Wheel of Great Bliss.'

Cultural Ownership

The Cakrasaṃvara tradition is primarily associated with Indo-Tibetan Buddhism, particularly the Newar Buddhist community of the Kathmandu Valley and Tibetan Buddhists across Tibet, Mongolia, Russia, China, and the diaspora. It originated in early medieval India (c. 8th-9th century CE) within the siddha movement.

Disputed Identifications

There is scholarly debate about the origins of the deity Saṃvara, with some suggesting a connection to the Vedic asura Śamvara, an enemy of Indra. Others, like Asko Parpola, argue for pre-Aryan cultic roots. The relationship between the Buddhist Cakrasaṃvara and Śaiva deities like Bhairava is also contested, with some seeing appropriation and subordination, others a more complex syncretism.

Description

Appearance

Cakrasaṃvara is typically depicted as a fierce deity with a blue or dark blue body, four faces (each with three eyes), and twelve arms. He stands in a dancing posture (ālīḍha) on a corpse or on the Hindu deities Bhairava and Kālarātri, symbolizing the subjugation of Śaiva forces. He wears a tiger skin, a garland of skulls, and ornaments of human bone. His consort, Vajravārāhī, is often shown embracing him. His faces may be colored differently: blue, yellow, red, and green.

Symbols & Iconography

Key symbols include: the vajra and bell (method and wisdom), the khaṭvāṅga (skull staff), a damaru (drum), a skull cup, a flaying knife, and various weapons. The mandala of Cakrasaṃvara is a complex arrangement of wheels (cakras) of ḍākinīs and their consorts, with the central deity at the hub. The number of deities in the mandala varies, but 62 is common.

Behaviour & Personality

As a meditational deity (yiḍam), Cakrasaṃvara embodies the union of compassion and wisdom, and the attainment of supreme bliss. He is wrathful in appearance but compassionate in nature, using fierce means to overcome obstacles and ignorance. In tantric practice, he is visualized as a manifestation of the Buddha, and his fierce form is a skillful means to subdue negative forces.

Domains & Habitats

Cakrasaṃvara is associated with charnel grounds, which are liminal spaces for tantric practice. His mandala is visualized as encompassing the entire universe. He is also connected to the ḍākinīs, female deities who inhabit these liminal spaces. In the body, he is located at the heart center in body mandala practices.

Version Differences

There are variations in the iconography and mandala configurations across different traditions and texts. For example, some forms have 12 arms, others 24. The consort may be Vajravārāhī or another ḍākinī. The number of deities in the mandala can range from 62 to 70 or more. These differences reflect different lineages and textual traditions.

Stories & Myths

The Subjugation of Rudra

Source Cakrasaṃvara commentarial literatureCulture Buddhist (Indo-Tibetan)Period c. 9th-12th centuries

Summary

The myth explains how the Buddha Vajradhara manifested as Heruka to subdue the Śaiva deity Bhairava (Rudra) and his consort Kālarātri, who had become corrupted by their hatred and lust. Heruka took on Bhairava's form and, with the help of other deities, defeated and trampled him, placing him beneath his feet. This act symbolized the superiority of Buddhism over Śaivism and justified the appropriation of Śaiva elements.

Participants

Vajradhara Buddha

Heruka

Bhairava

Kālarātri

Various Buddhist deities

Consequences

The Śaiva deities were subordinated to the Buddhist hierarchy, and their forms were adopted by Buddhist deities as skillful means. This established the Cakrasaṃvara tradition as a dominant Buddhist tantra.

Variants

An earlier version in the Samayoga Tantra describes Heruka as born from Vajradhara's power to destroy evil beings who threatened the universe, burning them up in a purifying fire. Another myth in the Tattvasaṃgraha tells of Vajrapāṇi subjugating Śiva, who later becomes a Buddha named Bhasmeśvara.

The Origin of Heruka in the Samayoga Tantra

Source Samayoga TantraCulture BuddhistPeriod Possibly earlier than 8th century

Summary

When evil beings, stirred by Māra, threatened to destroy the universe, all the Hindu deities sought refuge in the Supreme Buddha Vajradhara. Vajradhara assembled all the Buddhas, and since peaceful means failed, they manifested ferocious forms. Heruka was born from Vajradhara's transcendent power and burned up the entire universe, purifying it.

Participants

Vajradhara

Māra

Evil beings

All Buddhas

Heruka

Consequences

The universe was purified, and Heruka became a powerful deity.

Variants

This myth was later adapted to emphasize the subjugation of Śaiva deities.

Transgressive practices

Source Root tantra and scholarly analyses

Description

The tantra includes practices involving sexual union, consumption of polluting substances, and charnel ground rituals.

Scholarly Context

These practices were likely symbolic and ritualized, not necessarily literal, and were part of the siddha movement's deliberate transgression of social norms.

Appropriation of Śaiva elements

Source Alexis Sanderson's research

Description

The tradition appropriated elements from the Śaiva Vidyāpīṭha tantras, including textual passages and practices.

Scholarly Context

This was a form of religious competition and synthesis, where Buddhist authors subordinated Śaiva deities in their mythology.

Violent mythology

Source Commentaries

Description

The myth of Heruka subjugating Bhairava involves destruction and violence.

Scholarly Context

In Buddhist tantra, wrathful deities are seen as compassionate manifestations that use fierce means to overcome ignorance and evil.
