Who It Is

Before the sun became supreme, the night sky over Mesopotamia belonged to an old bearded god who sailed the darkness in a barge and thought the moon outshone the sun. His worshippers called him by two names in two languages — Nanna and Sin — until the names blurred into one theonym so old that a king's inscription already confessed 'nobody can explain it'. He could swell a childless womb, drive a difficult birth, judge the dead on the moon's vanished night, or, in a fury felt as leprosy and fits, blight an oath-breaker for life. And for one feverish generation near the very end of Babylon, the least-known moon god rose to claim the throne of the universe itself — 'lord of the gods', holder of the kingship, the judgment and the wisdom of heaven — before being toppled alongside the king who exalted him, his temples looted, his city Harran left for a Persian conqueror to profess. Sin was perceived as a benign, luminous deity who could be petitioned for help. He provided light during the night (epithets 'the luminary of the heavens and earth', 'the luminary of all creation'). He guaranteed abundance and growth, especially in Ur and Harran — a role typically assumed by local tutelary deities. He could provide offspring to childless men and women, aided pregnant women and women in labour, and his common epithet 'father' (a-a) underlined both his generative power and his senior rank in the pantheon. Powers
Luminescence of the Night (Light-Giving)

Sin's defining power is the light he casts across the night sky as the moon. He is the 'luminary of the heavens and earth' (nannār šamê u erṣeti) and the 'luminary of all creation' (nannār kullati binīti), responsible for making the night passable and visible. Poetic comparison of the moon's monthly growth to the swelling of fruit (Akkadian inbu) occurs in Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian hemerologies.

Limit: The moon wanes and disappears each month; lunar eclipses were believed to be caused by Sin being surrounded by seven evil utukku demons sent by Anu, temporarily overwhelming his light.

Lunar Timekeeping and the d30 Logogram

Sin's very name could be the number 30 (the cuneiform logogram d30), because the lunar month has 30 days. He is thus a cosmic timekeeper whose phases define the calendar that structured Mesopotamian cult, festivals and hemerologies.

Limit: Not a power in the combat sense, but a calendrical/ritual authority.

Judge of the Underworld / the Dead

Sin could act as a divine judge in the underworld. In the First Elegy of the Pushkin Museum, Ludingira prays that the moon god will proclaim a good verdict for his deceased father. This role likely developed to explain the moon's monthly disappearance — Sin's judgment occurred on the day of the moon's disappearance (Sumerian u4-ná, Akkadian ūm bubbuli).

Limit: Dina Katz argues Sin was usually NOT associated with judgment of either the living or the dead, in contrast with Shamash; references to both as judges are nonetheless known from Old Babylonian texts. His underworld-judge role is occasional, not core.

Pastoral Abundance (Cattle, Dairy, Wild Steppe)

Sin was closely associated with cattle herding and dairy products. His secondary names Abkar ('shining cow') and Ablulu ('the one who makes the cows abundant') encode this. As a herdsman he could tend the stars as his flock, and he was also linked to sheep and to steppe wildlife (ibexes, gazelles). This pastoral face connected him to prosperity, abundance and growth, particularly in Ur and Harran.

Limit: None major.

Fertility and Midwifery (Giver of Offspring)

Sin was petitioned by childless men and women for offspring and aided pregnant women at the beginning of pregnancy and in labour. The incantation 'Cow of Sîn' relates that he sends a pair of lamassu guardian goddesses to assist with difficult births.

Limit: None stated.

Divination, Omen Authority, and Disease (Wrath Register)

In Mesopotamian medicine, skin diseases (especially leprosy, saḫaršubbû) and epileptic symptoms could be interpreted as manifestations of Sin's wrath. These afflictions appear in curse formulas as punishments he inflicts on oath-breakers. Nebuchadnezzar II may have renovated Ekišnugal partly because he credited the moon god with determining destiny through lunar omens.

Limit: Sin's wrath was punitive but his disease-association is a 'hand of' deity pattern shared with Ningal, Shamash, Adad and Geshtinanna.

Head-of-the-Pantheon Authority ('Theology of the Moon')

A distinct and theologically ambitious power: in a tradition documented from Old Babylonian theophoric names (Sîn-bēl-ili 'Sin is the lord of the gods', Sîn-šar-ili 'Sin is the king of the gods') through to the Harran inscriptions of Nabonidus, Sin is described as 'lord of the gods' possessing 'Enlilship, Anuship and Eaship'. An Old Babylonian Sumerian composition even depicts Sin presiding over the divine assembly (Ubšu'ukkin) with Anu, Enlil, Inanna, Utu, Enki and Ninhursag as his advisers. In first-millennium explanatory texts this became the 'Theology of the Moon', in which Sin holds the parṣū (divine powers) of Anu, Enlil and Ea during the first half of the lunar month.

Limit: This elevation was 'enthusiastically supported' by Nabonidus but largely limited in recognition; mainstream Mesopotamian religion usually made him a major but subordinate member of the pantheon under Enlil and Anu.

Heavenly Travel (the 'Great Boat of Heaven')

Sin traversed the night sky in a barge, the 'great boat of heaven' (Sumerian dmá-gul-la-an-na). His son the sun god was correspondingly the 'small boat of heaven' (dmá-bàn-da-an-na) — a hierarchical image that also reflects the Mesopotamian belief that the moon is larger than the sun (an inversion of our modern astronomical understanding).

Limit: None.

Accounts I
Enlil and Ninlil — the Conception of the Moon God'Enlil and Ninlil' (ETCSL 1.2.1), nearly complete 152-line Sumerian poem

Old Babylonian composition; narrative set in primeval time

Ninlil's mother Nunbarshegunu instructs her to bathe in the river (the canal Nunbirdu). There Enlil seduces her and impregnates her with their son, the moon-god Nanna. Because of this act, Enlil is banished to Kur, the underworld. Ninlil follows Enlil to the underworld, where he impersonates the 'man of the gate' (and then the man of the river of the nether world and the man of the ferryboat). Ninlil, not recognizing each concealed identity, repeatedly sleeps with the disguised Enlil, each time conceiving another child — the underworld gods Nergal, Ninazu and Enbilulu, who become substitutes or 'balancing' offspring so that Nanna does not have to remain in the underworld. The poem is both a genealogical myth explaining the origins of the moon god and of underworld gods, and a coming-of-age story of Enlil and Ninlil. Ninlil declares, 'As Enlil is your master, so am I also your mistress!' Nanna-Suen's Journey to Nibru (Nippur)Nanna-Suen's Journey to Nibru (ETCSL, Narratives featuring Nanna-Suen)

Old Babylonian composition; reflects a festival in which the moon god's statue was transported from Ur to Nippur

After a hymnic prologue praising Nippur, the narrative tells how the moon god dispatches his servants to obtain wood from various regions (including Ebla and Tummal) so that he can build a ship for a journey to visit his father Enlil in Nippur. He loads the vessel with gifts — cattle, sheep, birds, fish and other animals. He makes five stops along the way (Enegi, Larsa, Uruk, Shuruppak and Tummal), each time welcomed by a local goddess (Ningirida, Šerida, Inanna, Nin-unug, Ninlil), but he refuses to share the cargo meant for Enlil with any of them. Reaching Nippur, he is welcomed by the divine doorkeeper Kalkal and finally meets Enlil, from whom he requests a blessing for his city Ur, which he receives in the closing lines. Ceremonially links Ur and Nippur and the cults of Sin and Enlil; affirms Ur's blessed status under the moon god's patronage.

The Labbu Myth — Sin and the Slaying of the Sea-MonsterThe Labbu myth, single poorly-preserved copy from the library of Ashurbanipal

composed between c. 1800 BCE and 800 BCE (Lambert); Wiggermann favours before 1755 BCE

The gods conflict with the monster Labbu. At the sight of Labbu, Sin obscures his face with a cloak — an image widely read as a lunar eclipse, the moon god 'hiding' as the dragon advances. Sin subsequently advises Tishpak, the god apparently selected to battle the monster, coordinating the slaying of Labbu. Sin thus functions here as counsellor-oracle to the monster-slayer, a rare narrative role for him. Establishes Sin in an advisory/wise-older-god role and ties him to the dragon-slayer cycle.

Accounts II
Inanna's Descent — the Refusal of the FatherInanna's Descent into the Underworld (ETCSL 1.4.1)

Old Babylonian Sumerian version

When the goddess Inanna descends to the underworld, she instructs her sukkal (attendant) Ninshubur to petition the great gods if she does not return. Ninshubur pleads first with Enlil, then with Nanna/Sin, then with Enki. Both Enlil and Nanna refuse to help, mourning only for their daughter/servant 'in their own quarters'; it is Enki who sends flight-demon rescuers. Sin's presence reflects his well-attested role as Inanna's father, yet he declines to act — a telling contrast to his usual beneficence, and one that seems to have parallels in Gilgamesh, Enkidu, and the Netherworld and tablet XII of the Epic of Gilgamesh, where he also refuses to recover the dead. Positions Sin among the senior but passive gods who cannot or will not challenge the underworld's claim on the dead.

The Fall of Ur and the Lament for Sumer and UrLament over the Destruction of Sumer and Ur; also the Lament for Ur

Ur III / Isin-Larsa period, commemorating the fall of the Third Dynasty of Ur (c. 1940 BCE)

When a cataclysm — the Elamite destruction of his cult city Ur — befalls him, the moon god is said to leave his city alongside his wife Ningal. He asks Enlil to reverse the judgment of the divine assembly that brought about Ur's destruction; his request is initially denied. He eventually approaches Enlil a second time and receives a guarantee that Ur will be rebuilt, whereupon he and Ningal return to the city. The historical backdrop is the capture of the last king of the Third Dynasty of Ur, Ibbi-Sin, by the Elamites. Gives the moon god a poignant 'suffering god' narrative tied to real historical catastrophe, and confirms his role as intercessor for his city and his people.

Sin in the Epic of Gilgamesh — Patron of the Dead and the UnmovedEpic of Gilgamesh, Standard Babylonian edition

Standard Babylonian edition, first millennium BCE

In the Standard Babylonian Gilgamesh, some grave goods for the deceased Enkidu are dedicated to Sin, here called Namraṣit ('whose rise is luminous'); Andrew R. George suggests this reflects the belief that Sin accompanies the dead when not visible in the sky. A poorly preserved passage may describe Gilgamesh killing two lions and dedicating them to Sin after being reassured by the moon god in a dream. On tablet XII, when Enkidu is imprisoned in the underworld, Gilgamesh begs Sin, Enlil and Ea to help recover his companion; the first two refuse, and only Ea finds a way to raise Enkidu's spirit. An unusual variant of the epic replaces the names of Gilgamesh and Enkidu with the logograms d30 (Sin) and d40 (Ea), with Ur replacing Uruk — a strange, largely-unexplained borrowing. Reinforces Sin's underworld/judge-of-the-dead associations and his motif of refusing resurrection pleas.
