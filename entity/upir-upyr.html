Who It Is

Before Dracula ever stalked a castle, a dead man in a Slavic village refused to stay in his grave. His name—Upir, the ‘wicked vampire’—was whispered in an 11th‑century manuscript and condemned in a medieval sermon, long before Western Europe learned the word ‘vampire’. Bloated with stolen blood, worshipped and feared, the upir sits at the crossroads of pagan ritual, Christian anxiety, and the raw terror of a body that will not accept its own death.

In Slavic folk culture, the upir is primarily a harmful revenant rather than a psychologically nuanced character. It leaves its grave, often at night (though some traditions allow daytime activity), to visit its former home and community. Its behavior includes drinking the blood of sleeping relatives, suffocating victims, or draining their life force, leading to illness, wasting, or death. It may also engage in poltergeist‑like disturbances: knocking, pressing on the chest, or appearing in dreams. Powers
Blood‑drinking and life‑draining

The defining ability of the upir is to drink the blood of the living, usually while they sleep, thereby weakening or killing them. This may manifest as visible bite marks, unexplained anemia, wasting illness, or sudden death. In some accounts, the upir drains not only blood but also vital energy, leaving victims lethargic or haunted.

Limit: Requires proximity to the victim, typically within the same household or village. Often constrained to nocturnal visits or to the forty‑day post‑mortem period in some traditions.

Leaving the grave and corporeal mobility

The upir can rise from its grave, walk or move through the village, and return to its burial place before dawn or before discovery. It may enter houses, barns, or stables, and is capable of physical interaction (pressing on the chest, opening doors, disturbing objects).

Limit: In many traditions, the upir must return to its grave by a certain time (often sunrise) and is bound to the physical corpse. Destruction of the body (staking, decapitation, burning) ends its mobility.

Causing disease and misfortune

Beyond direct killing, the upir is blamed for outbreaks of illness, wasting diseases, and misfortune in the household or village. Its presence may be inferred from a series of unexplained deaths or sicknesses, leading communities to suspect a particular corpse and exhume it.

Limit: This ability is largely interpretive: villagers attribute disease to the upir when other explanations fail. It is not described as a conscious magical power but as a consequence of its visits.

Persistence after improper death or burial

Individuals who die ‘bad deaths’ (suicide, unbaptized, excommunicated, witches, criminals) or who are buried improperly (outside consecrated ground, without rituals) are believed to become upirs. This is less a power than a condition: the upir’s existence is enabled by social and ritual failure.

Limit: Dependent on cultural norms: not all traditions specify the same categories of ‘bad death’. The mechanism is moral and ritual rather than physical.

Resistance to ordinary decay and partial regeneration

Reports of suspected upirs describe corpses that appear unusually preserved, with fresh‑looking skin, flexible limbs, and apparent growth of hair and nails. This is interpreted as evidence of ongoing undead activity.

Limit: Modern science explains many of these features as natural post‑mortem processes (gas bloating, skin shrinkage), but within folklore they function as signs of upir activity.

Accounts I
Upir Likhyi (‘Wicked Vampire’)Book of Prophecy (Novgorod manuscript, 1047 CE)

11th century

In a manuscript known as the Book of Prophecy, a figure named Upir Likhyi (‘Wicked Upir/Vampire’) appears as a sobriquet for a Novgorodian prince or notable. The text itself is not a vampire tale; rather, the name functions as a marker of fearsome or uncanny reputation. Later scholars interpret this as the earliest written occurrence of the upir term, showing that by the mid‑11th century it was already associated with wickedness and perhaps with beliefs about undead or demonic beings. The story’s main consequence is lexical and cultural: it anchors the word upir in a specific historical context, demonstrating its use as a personal epithet and providing a terminus ante quem for the spread of upir beliefs.

Accounts II
Sacrifices to upyri in the Word of Saint GrigoriyWord of Saint Grigoriy (Слово святого Григория)

11th–13th centuries

The anti‑pagan treatise known as the Word of Saint Grigoriy condemns villagers for offering sacrifices to various pagan entities, including upyri. The text portrays upyri as beings worthy of cultic attention in the eyes of the villagers but as demonic or false gods from the Christian perspective. It does not narrate specific attacks or miracles, but it implies that upyri are powerful enough to receive offerings and to be feared or propitiated. The treatise contributes to the Christianization of Slavic lands by delegitimizing upir worship and framing it as idolatry. It also preserves evidence that upirs were part of a broader pantheon of feared beings, not merely marginal ghosts.

Village exhumations and destruction of suspected upirs18th‑century vampire epidemic reports and later ethnographic accounts (as synthesized in modern scholarship)

18th century and later, reflecting older beliefs

Numerous reports describe villages where a series of deaths or illnesses leads inhabitants to suspect that a recently deceased person has become an upir. The community exhumes the corpse, finds it bloated, with fresh‑looking blood and extended nails, and interprets these signs as proof of vampirism. They then drive a stake through the chest, decapitate the body, burn it, or scatter the ashes to prevent further attacks. These narratives are often recorded by outside observers (clergy, officials, travelers) and later analyzed by scholars as evidence of upir‑like beliefs. The destruction of the corpse is believed to end the epidemic of deaths or illnesses. Socially, these events reinforce communal norms about proper burial, moral behavior, and the dangers of deviating from religious or social expectations.
