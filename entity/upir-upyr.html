Who It Is

The Slavic vampire, one of the earliest recorded undead blood-drinkers in European folklore. The word 'upir' (Ukrainian: упир; Russian: упырь) appears in Old East Slavic texts as early as the 11th century. Unlike the aristocratic Romanian strigoi, the upir was typically a peasant — a person who died in sin, by suicide, or as a sorcerer, and returned as a bloated, ruddy-complexioned corpse that fed on the blood of the living. The upir was particularly active during epidemics, as plagues were blamed on its feeding. The modern word 'vampire' entered Western European languages via the Serbian 'vampir' during the 18th-century vampire hysteria in the Habsburg Empire — the cases of Arnold Paole and Peter Plogojowitz being medically documented by Austrian officials.

Form

Attested descriptions of upir / upyr in Slavic folklore emphasize its corporeal, grave‑bound nature rather than the aristocratic elegance of later literary vampires. Ethnographic and historical accounts describe a corpse that appears bloated or swollen, with dark‑reddish or purplish skin, often attributed to the accumulation of blood. Blood is said to seep from the mouth and nose, staining the burial shroud. Gums may recede, making the teeth appear unnaturally long, and fingernails seem extended as the surrounding skin shrinks. In some traditions, the upir retains a generally human form but with disturbing signs of decay and overfullness, sometimes accompanied by a foul odor. These features are attested in broader Eastern European vampire reports and are widely applied to the upir as a prototype of the vampire. Fine details of clothing vary by region, but the creature is typically imagined in its burial garments, occasionally with a cap or shroud partially covering the face. Where upir is used as a personal epithet (e.g., Upir Likhyi), no physical description survives, and any imagined appearance is reconstructed from later folklore.

Blood‑drinking and life‑draining The defining ability of the upir is to drink the blood of the living, usually while they sleep, thereby weakening or killing them. This may manifest as visible bite marks, unexplained anemia, wasting illness, or sudden death. In some accounts, the upir drains not only blood but also vital energy, leaving victims lethargic or haunted. General Slavic vampire folklore; applied to upir/upyr as prototype in linguistic and ethnographic studies. Requires proximity to the victim, typically within the same household or village. Often constrained to nocturnal visits or to the forty‑day post‑mortem period in some traditions. Some sources emphasize literal blood‑drinking; others describe more diffuse ‘sucking’ of life or breath. A minority of accounts treat the upir as more spirit‑like, blurring the line between corporeal feeding and spiritual affliction.

Leaving the grave and corporeal mobility The upir can rise from its grave, walk or move through the village, and return to its burial place before dawn or before discovery. It may enter houses, barns, or stables, and is capable of physical interaction (pressing on the chest, opening doors, disturbing objects). Eastern European vampire case reports and Slavic folk narratives; inferred for upir as the central Slavic term in these contexts. In many traditions, the upir must return to its grave by a certain time (often sunrise) and is bound to the physical corpse. Destruction of the body (staking, decapitation, burning) ends its mobility. Some accounts allow daytime activity, especially in later Balkan and East Slavic traditions, while others restrict movement to night. A few narratives treat the upir as more ghost‑like, suggesting less dependence on the grave.

Causing disease and misfortune Beyond direct killing, the upir is blamed for outbreaks of illness, wasting diseases, and misfortune in the household or village. Its presence may be inferred from a series of unexplained deaths or sicknesses, leading communities to suspect a particular corpse and exhume it. 18th‑century ‘vampire epidemic’ reports and later ethnographic analyses of Slavic communities. This ability is largely interpretive: villagers attribute disease to the upir when other explanations fail. It is not described as a conscious magical power but as a consequence of its visits. Some scholars argue that such attributions reflect attempts to explain contagious disease (rabies, pellagra, porphyria) rather than a distinct folkloric power. Others maintain that the belief in upir as a disease‑causer is integral to its role.

Persistence after improper death or burial Individuals who die ‘bad deaths’ (suicide, unbaptized, excommunicated, witches, criminals) or who are buried improperly (outside consecrated ground, without rituals) are believed to become upirs. This is less a power than a condition: the upir’s existence is enabled by social and ritual failure. Slavic folk beliefs about revenants and vampirism, as synthesized in modern scholarship. Dependent on cultural norms: not all traditions specify the same categories of ‘bad death’. The mechanism is moral and ritual rather than physical. Some accounts emphasize purely demonic origin (upir as a demon or evil spirit), downplaying the role of human corpses; others insist that upir is always a dead human whose soul returns to the body.

Resistance to ordinary decay and partial regeneration Reports of suspected upirs describe corpses that appear unusually preserved, with fresh‑looking skin, flexible limbs, and apparent growth of hair and nails. This is interpreted as evidence of ongoing undead activity. Historical vampire exhumation reports and ethnographic descriptions of Slavic burial practices. Modern science explains many of these features as natural post‑mortem processes (gas bloating, skin shrinkage), but within folklore they function as signs of upir activity. Some narratives treat these features as definitive proof of vampirism; others acknowledge that not all suspected upirs show such signs, leading to debates within communities about which corpses are truly dangerous.

Accounts

In a manuscript known as the Book of Prophecy, a figure named Upir Likhyi (‘Wicked Upir/Vampire’) appears as a sobriquet for a Novgorodian prince or notable. The text itself is not a vampire tale; rather, the name functions as a marker of fearsome or uncanny reputation. Later scholars interpret this as the earliest written occurrence of the upir term, showing that by the mid‑11th century it was already associated with wickedness and perhaps with beliefs about undead or demonic beings.

The anti‑pagan treatise known as the Word of Saint Grigoriy condemns villagers for offering sacrifices to various pagan entities, including upyri. The text portrays upyri as beings worthy of cultic attention in the eyes of the villagers but as demonic or false gods from the Christian perspective. It does not narrate specific attacks or miracles, but it implies that upyri are powerful enough to receive offerings and to be feared or propitiated.

Numerous reports describe villages where a series of deaths or illnesses leads inhabitants to suspect that a recently deceased person has become an upir. The community exhumes the corpse, finds it bloated, with fresh‑looking blood and extended nails, and interprets these signs as proof of vampirism. They then drive a stake through the chest, decapitate the body, burn it, or scatter the ashes to prevent further attacks. These narratives are often recorded by outside observers (clergy, officials, travelers) and later analyzed by scholars as evidence of upir‑like beliefs.

Content Notes

Villages fearing an upir often exhume the suspected corpse, drive a stake through its chest, decapitate it, burn the remains, and scatter the ashes. These acts are intended to prevent further attacks but involve extreme violence against the dead, sometimes without clear evidence beyond rumor and fear.

Scholars interpret these practices as attempts to manage epidemics and unexplained deaths, reflecting both genuine fear and the social power of communal decision‑making. They also highlight the tension between official religious norms (respect for the dead) and local survival strategies.

18th‑century vampire epidemic reports; ethnographic syntheses in Perkowski and later scholarship.

People who die by suicide, remain unbaptized, are accused of witchcraft, or otherwise occupy marginal social positions are disproportionately suspected of becoming upirs. Their bodies may be buried outside consecrated ground and later targeted for destructive rituals, reinforcing stigma and post‑mortem punishment.

Researchers note that vampirism accusations often fall on those already stigmatized, suggesting that upir beliefs can serve as tools of social control and moral policing. This dynamic raises ethical questions about how communities handle deviance and tragedy.

Slavic folk belief surveys; Perkowski’s The Darkling; broader studies of European revenant traditions.

Christian texts such as the Word of Saint Grigoriy condemn sacrifices to upyri as pagan idolatry, urging villagers to abandon these practices. This involves delegitimizing traditional beliefs and framing them as demonic, contributing to cultural and religious conflict.

Historians view such polemics as part of a broader campaign to reshape religious landscapes, often erasing or demonizing older traditions. Upir worship becomes a symbol of resistance or backwardness in these narratives, complicating modern efforts to reconstruct pre‑Christian belief systems.

Word of Saint Grigoriy; studies of Christianization in Slavic lands.

During periods of epidemic or unexplained illness, communities may attribute deaths to an upir and respond with violent rituals against a corpse. This can escalate tensions, provoke accusations, and justify extreme measures, sometimes leading to further trauma.

Medical historians and folklorists argue that upir beliefs intersect with attempts to understand and control disease. While such beliefs may provide psychological comfort, they can also divert attention from effective public health measures and reinforce harmful practices.

Historical reports of vampire epidemics; modern analyses linking vampirism to diseases such as rabies and porphyria.

What We Don't Know

Several dimensions remain incomplete or uncertain: (1) Primary narrative sources specifically describing upir attacks, appearance, and rituals in medieval Slavic texts are sparse; much detail is reconstructed from later vampire reports and comparative folklore. (2) Precise manuscript locators and critical editions for the Book of Prophecy (Upir Likhyi) and Word of Saint Grigoriy were not consulted directly here; reliance on secondary summaries introduces potential inaccuracies. (3) Regional variations (e.g., detailed Polish upiór tales, Ukrainian martvyets narratives, Balkan vampir traditions) are only sketched, not exhaustively catalogued. (4) The reception history of the specific term upir/upiór in modern media (films, games, comics) is underdeveloped; many works reference generic ‘vampires’ without naming upir explicitly. (5) Linguistic debates about etymology are summarized from a limited set of accessible articles and may omit important contributions in less widely available journals or languages. (6) Dark‑history aspects such as specific documented cases of scapegoating, legal responses, and church decrees against vampire practices require deeper archival research. Overall, this dossier offers a deep but not fully exhaustive synthesis, bounded by available online and widely cited scholarly materials as of July 30, 2026.

A way to hold it in your mind

Before Dracula ever stalked a castle, a dead man in a Slavic village refused to stay in his grave. His name—Upir, the ‘wicked vampire’—was whispered in an 11th‑century manuscript and condemned in a medieval sermon, long before Western Europe learned the word ‘vampire’. Bloated with stolen blood, worshipped and feared, the upir sits at the crossroads of pagan ritual, Christian anxiety, and the raw terror of a body that will not accept its own death.
