Who It Is

In the late nineteenth century, a former science teacher from Boston wielded an extraordinary power: the ability to veto any school textbook that mentioned alcohol in a way she deemed unsafe. Mary Hunt, superintendent of the Woman's Christian Temperance Union's Department of Scientific Temperance Instruction, transformed American classrooms into battlegrounds for prohibition, shaping the minds of millions of children and sparking a national debate about education, science, and democracy.

Mary Hunt was a determined and politically astute activist. She was methodical, providing step-by-step instructions to local superintendents, and uncompromising in her ideological stance, rejecting any textbook that did not fully align with her views on total abstinence. She was willing to engage in public controversy, as evidenced by her reply to the Committee of Fifty, which she had entered into the Congressional Record and distributed widely.

Powers
Veto power over textbooks

Mary Hunt achieved de facto power to veto any textbook that did not meet her criteria for acceptable temperance instruction. Publishers submitted drafts to her for approval, and she could reject books that mentioned medicinal use of alcohol or implied moderate drinking was safe.

Limit: Her power was based on her influence within the WCTU and the threat of WCTU chapters lobbying against non-approved textbooks. It was not an official legal authority.

Curriculum development

She developed instructional materials for teaching children about the dangers of alcohol, which were used in public schools across the United States.

Limit: The curriculum was based on the ideology of total abstinence, which was later criticized for being scientifically inaccurate.

Accounts I
Rise to Power in the WCTUZimmerman, 1992; source material

1879-1880s

Mary Hunt, a former science teacher, became interested in how textbooks addressed alcohol while helping her son study chemistry. She developed instructional materials and was invited by Frances Willard to speak at the WCTU national convention in 1879. By 1880, she convinced the WCTU to establish the Department of Scientific Temperance Instruction, with herself as Superintendent. Hunt gained significant influence over school textbooks across the United States, leading to mandatory temperance instruction for millions of children.

Accounts II
The Committee of Fifty ControversyBillings et al., 1905; Hunt, 1904; source material

1893-1904

The Committee of Fifty, a group of scholars studying the 'liquor problem,' criticized the WCTU's temperance instruction for its ideological purity and inaccuracies. Mary Hunt responded by charging the committee with prejudice and misrepresentation, and she had her reply entered into the Congressional Record and distributed over 100,000 copies. The controversy highlighted the tension between democratic education and ideological control, but Hunt's program continued.

Legacy and Posthumous ControversySource material; Timberlake, 1963

After 1906

After her death, questions arose about her finances. It was revealed that she had signed over royalties from textbooks to a charity, the Scientific Temperance Association, which was composed of herself, her pastor, and a few friends. This charity supported the maintenance of the WCTU's headquarters, which was also her residence. The controversy somewhat tarnished her legacy, but her impact on education was acknowledged.

Mary Hunt was a minor figure in the temperance movement. — She was a highly influential leader who shaped educational policy across the United States, with millions of children required to take her temperance instruction.

The WCTU's temperance instruction was based on sound science. — Many of the claims in the textbooks, such as the inevitability of alcohol abuse from moderate drinking, were not scientifically supported and were criticized by contemporary scholars.

Mary Hunt profited personally from her textbook endorsements. — She signed over her royalties to a charity, though the charity was closely tied to her personal interests.
