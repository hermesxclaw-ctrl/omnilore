Who It Is

Imagine the Roman Mediterranean: a sky of lead, the sea churning, and sailors crossing themselves as the wind shifts south. That wind had a face — a bearded, winged deity with a water vessel, ready to unleash chaos. His name was Auster, and he was the storm that could sink empires or bring the rains that saved them. He's not a god you'd pray to for gentle breezes; he's the one you'd pray to spare you. From Virgil's epic storms to the Tower of the Winds in Athens, Auster is the forgotten force of nature that shaped the ancient world's fears and fortunes.

Auster is described as a violent, storm-bringing wind, often associated with sudden squalls, heavy rain, and maritime danger. Unlike the more gentle Zephyrus (west wind), Auster is seen as a destructive force, capable of sinking ships and devastating crops. He is also associated with the intense heat of late summer, which could bring both life-giving rain and destructive storms.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name is likely derived from the Latin 'auster' meaning 'south' or 'south wind'. It is cognate with the Greek 'Notos' (Νότος), the god of the south wind. The root is shared with the Latin 'aestas' (summer) and 'aestus' (heat), reflecting the hot, dry nature of the wind.

Powers
Control over the South Wind

Auster has dominion over the south wind, able to summon or calm it at will. He can bring gentle breezes or violent storms depending on his mood.

Limit: His power is limited to the south wind; he cannot control winds from other directions. His influence is strongest in the Mediterranean region.

Storm Generation

Auster can summon powerful storms, bringing heavy rain, thunder, and lightning. He is particularly feared by sailors for his ability to create sudden, violent squalls.

Limit: His storms are typically short-lived but intense. He may not be able to sustain them for long periods.

Heat and Drought Manipulation

As a wind of the south, Auster is associated with intense heat. He can bring oppressive, humid heat that can wither crops or, conversely, bring life-giving rain to parched lands.

Limit: His heat is typically accompanied by humidity, making it distinct from the dry heat of the east wind.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Auster is typically depicted as a winged male deity, often with a beard, carrying a water vessel or conch shell. His hair and beard are frequently shown as wild and wind-blown, sometimes with water droplets or rain depicted around him. In some Roman mosaics, he is shown as a dark or dusky figure, reflecting the stormy nature of the south wind.

Symbols — Primary symbols include a water vessel or urn, a conch shell, and wings. He is often associated with the color black or dark blue, representing storm clouds. In the Tower of the Winds in Athens, his counterpart Notos is depicted holding a water vessel, a motif that carried over to Roman representations of Auster.

Domains — Auster's domain is the south wind, particularly the hot, moist winds that blow from the Mediterranean toward Italy. He was thought to dwell in the southern regions, possibly in Africa or the distant southern seas. In Roman thought, he was particularly associated with the stormy weather of the late summer and autumn, a dangerous time for Mediterranean navigation.

Accounts I
The Storm in the AeneidVirgil, Aeneid, Book 1

c. 29-19 BCE

In Virgil's epic, Juno persuades Aeolus, the king of the winds, to release the winds to destroy Aeneas's fleet. Auster is among the winds released, and he is described as rushing against the ships, bringing rain and storm. The storm wrecks several ships and forces Aeneas to land in Carthage. The storm sets the stage for Aeneas's arrival in Carthage and his tragic love affair with Dido.

Accounts II
The Flood in Ovid's MetamorphosesOvid, Metamorphoses, Book 1

c. 8 CE

In Ovid's account of the great flood sent by Jupiter to destroy humanity, Auster is depicted as a winged deity flying with his feathers dripping with water, his face shrouded in mist. He is part of the divine mechanism that brings the floodwaters, helping to drown the world. The flood destroys humanity, with only Deucalion and Pyrrha surviving.

The Tower of the WindsAndronicus of Cyrrhus, Tower of the Winds

c. 50 BCE

In the Tower of the Winds in Athens, the south wind (Notos/Auster) is depicted on the south side of the octagonal tower, holding a water vessel. The tower served as a public clock and weather station, and the depiction of the winds showed their directional and meteorological significance. The Tower of the Winds became a model for later depictions of the winds in Roman and Renaissance art.

Auster is a major god with a large cult following — Auster was a minor deity, primarily a personification of a natural force. He had no major temples or festivals dedicated to him, and his worship was largely limited to sailors and farmers seeking favorable winds.

Auster is the Roman equivalent of the Greek god Zephyrus — Zephyrus is the west wind, not the south wind. Auster's Greek counterpart is Notos.

Auster is always depicted as an old man — While some depictions show Auster as an older, bearded figure, others show him as a younger man. His iconography was not fixed, and representations varied across time and region.
