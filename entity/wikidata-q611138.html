Form

Auster is typically depicted as a winged male deity, often with a beard, carrying a water vessel or conch shell. His hair and beard are frequently shown as wild and wind-blown, sometimes with water droplets or rain depicted around him. In some Roman mosaics, he is shown as a dark or dusky figure, reflecting the stormy nature of the south wind.

Powers

Control over the South Wind Auster has dominion over the south wind, able to summon or calm it at will. He can bring gentle breezes or violent storms depending on his mood. Roman mythology, attested in Virgil and Seneca His power is limited to the south wind; he cannot control winds from other directions. His influence is strongest in the Mediterranean region. Some sources suggest he has power over all storm winds, while others limit him strictly to the south.

Storm Generation Auster can summon powerful storms, bringing heavy rain, thunder, and lightning. He is particularly feared by sailors for his ability to create sudden, violent squalls. Roman mythology, attested in Virgil's Aeneid His storms are typically short-lived but intense. He may not be able to sustain them for long periods. None found in primary sources.

Heat and Drought Manipulation As a wind of the south, Auster is associated with intense heat. He can bring oppressive, humid heat that can wither crops or, conversely, bring life-giving rain to parched lands. Roman agricultural and meteorological texts His heat is typically accompanied by humidity, making it distinct from the dry heat of the east wind. Some sources emphasize his rain-bringing aspect, others his drought-bringing aspect.

Accounts

In Virgil's epic, Juno persuades Aeolus, the king of the winds, to release the winds to destroy Aeneas's fleet. Auster is among the winds released, and he is described as rushing against the ships, bringing rain and storm. The storm wrecks several ships and forces Aeneas to land in Carthage.

In Ovid's account of the great flood sent by Jupiter to destroy humanity, Auster is depicted as a winged deity flying with his feathers dripping with water, his face shrouded in mist. He is part of the divine mechanism that brings the floodwaters, helping to drown the world.

In the Tower of the Winds in Athens, the south wind (Notos/Auster) is depicted on the south side of the octagonal tower, holding a water vessel. The tower served as a public clock and weather station, and the depiction of the winds showed their directional and meteorological significance.

Scholarly Debates
Auster vs. Vulturnus
Auster is the south wind, Vulturnus is the southeast wind ⇌ Auster and Vulturnus are the same deity
Evidence for A: Vitruvius and Pliny the Elder distinguish between the two winds
Evidence for B: Some later Roman sources use the names interchangeably
Auster's nature: gentle vs. violent
Auster is a violent, storm-bringing wind ⇌ Auster can be a gentle, rain-bringing wind
Evidence for A: Virgil and Ovid depict him as destructive
Evidence for B: Some agricultural writers describe him as beneficial for crops
Auster's relationship to Notos
Auster and Notos are the same deity ⇌ Auster and Notos are distinct deities
Evidence for A: Both are south winds with similar attributes
Evidence for B: Roman and Greek representations differ in character
What We Don't Know

Further research is needed into the archaeological evidence for the worship of Auster, particularly in North Africa and other Roman provinces. The specific rituals and prayers associated with his cult are not well documented. More research is needed into the representation of Auster in Roman art, particularly in mosaics and frescoes.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Auster as the ancient world's version of a Category 5 hurricane warning system — except the storm itself was a god. He's the personification of that moment when the weather turns, the sky darkens, and you know you're at the mercy of something far bigger than yourself. He's not a villain or a hero; he's the force of nature that reminds you how small you are.
