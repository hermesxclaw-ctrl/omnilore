Who It Is

In the heart of ancient Greece, a nymph's desperate plea for freedom echoed through the groves, and in that instant, she became the laurel tree—eternally beautiful, eternally untouched. This is the story of Daphne, the chase, and the transformation that gave Apollo his sacred tree.

In Ovid, Daphne is portrayed as a chaste nymph who flees from Apollo's advances. In Parthenius, she is a mortal girl who loves hunting and is determined to remain a virgin, avoiding all males. She is strong-willed and resourceful, but ultimately powerless against the gods.

Powers
Transformation into a laurel tree

Daphne is transformed into a laurel tree, either by her father Peneus (Ovid), Zeus (Parthenius), or Gaia (Hyginus). This is not a power she possesses but a divine intervention in response to her prayer.

Limit: She cannot reverse the transformation; it is permanent.

Hunting skills

In Parthenius and Pausanias, Daphne is a skilled huntress, leading a band of girls who hunt.

Limit: None specified.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In Ovid's Metamorphoses, Daphne is described as beautiful, with her hair flowing, but the text focuses on her transformation: her limbs become numb, bark encloses her breast, her hair turns into leaves, her arms into branches, her feet root in the ground. No other ancient source gives a detailed physical description. Modern artistic depictions often show her as a young woman with flowing hair, sometimes partially transformed into a tree.

Symbols — The laurel tree (Laurus nobilis) is the central symbol. Also associated with Artemis (as Artemis Daphnaia) and Apollo (as Apollo Daphnephoros, 'laurel-bearer'). The laurel wreath is a symbol of victory and poetic achievement.

Domains — In Thessaly, near the river Peneus; in Arcadia, near the river Ladon; in later traditions, near the Orontes River in Antioch. She is associated with groves and wilderness.

Accounts I
Apollo and Daphne (Ovid's version)Ovid, Metamorphoses

1st century AD

Cupid shoots Apollo with a golden arrow to make him fall in love with Daphne, and shoots Daphne with a leaden arrow to make her flee from love. Apollo pursues her relentlessly, but Daphne prays to her father Peneus to be saved. She is transformed into a laurel tree. Apollo embraces the tree, and declares that the laurel will be his sacred tree. The laurel becomes Apollo's sacred tree, and laurel wreaths are used to crown victors and poets.

Accounts II
Leucippus and Daphne (Parthenius's version)Parthenius, Erotica Pathemata

1st century BC (but based on Phylarchus, 3rd century BC)

Daphne, a mortal girl, is pursued by Leucippus, who disguises himself as a girl to join her hunting band. Apollo, also in love with Daphne, becomes jealous and causes the girls to bathe, revealing Leucippus's true sex. The girls kill Leucippus. Apollo then pursues Daphne, who prays to Zeus and is transformed into a laurel tree. Leucippus dies; Daphne becomes a laurel tree.

Daphne and Gaia (Hyginus's version)Hyginus, Fabulae

1st century AD

Apollo pursues Daphne, daughter of Peneus. She begs Gaia for help, and Gaia transforms her into a laurel tree. Apollo makes a wreath from the tree. Laurel becomes Apollo's sacred tree.

Daphne was a nymph in all versions. — In Parthenius, she is a mortal girl. Ovid calls her a nymph, but other sources differ.

Apollo's love was purely romantic. — The pursuit is often interpreted as attempted rape; Daphne's transformation is a desperate escape.

The laurel wreath was always a symbol of victory. — The association with victory developed after the myth; originally, the laurel was sacred to Apollo and used in his worship.
