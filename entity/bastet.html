Bastet, the cat-eyed lady of joy, who dances with the sistrum and protects the hearth from all harm.

Who They Are

Bastet is the cat-headed goddess of home, fertility, joy, music, and protection. Originally depicted as a lioness-headed warrior goddess (like Sekhmet), she evolved over time into a more benevolent cat-headed deity. Her cult center was at Bubastis (Per-Bast), where Herodotus describes a magnificent temple and annual festival that was one of the largest religious gatherings in Egypt. Bastet protects the home from evil spirits, disease, and misfortune, especially guarding pregnant women and children. She is associated with the sistrum (a musical rattle), the aegis (protective shield), and the Eye of Ra. Kittens were sacred to her, and killing a cat in Egypt was punishable by death. Cats were mummified and offered at her temples. Bastet's dual nature — both nurturing mother and fierce protector — reflects the paradox of the cat: loving lap-cuddler and merciless hunter. She is the daughter of Ra and wife of Ptah (in Memphis theology).

Attested in Pyramid Texts / Herodotus / Diodorus SiculusEra Old Kingdom to Roman Egypt (c. 2600 BCE-400 CE)

Form

Bastet has two major iconographic phases. Earliest depictions (Old Kingdom through Middle Kingdom) show her as a lioness or a woman with the head of a lioness, often holding a papyrus scepter or ankh, wearing a tight sheath dress and a sun disk or vulture headdress. From the Third Intermediate Period onward (c. 1070 BCE), she is increasingly depicted as a domestic cat or a slender woman with the head of a cat (Felis catus). In her fully developed form, she carries a ceremonial sistrum (rattle) in her right hand and an aegis (ornamental collar or gorget embossed with a lioness head) or a basket in her left hand. A lioness-head aegis is distinctive to her iconography. She is frequently shown with kittens at her feet, emphasizing her maternal aspect. Statuettes of Bastet were mass-produced in bronze, alabaster, and faience, many featuring complex details such as earrings, collars, and a wedjat-eye (Eye of Horus) pendant on a necklace.

Powers

Solar Protection (Eye of Ra) As one of the 'Eyes of Ra', Bastet serves as a fierce protector of the sun god, defending him against his eternal enemy, the chaos serpent Apep. She is also depicted as the defender of the king of Egypt, who was the earthly embodiment of Horus and the sun god. This solar connection means she wields the burning, destructive power of the sun — the same power that Sekhmet embodies in its unleashed form. Attested from Old Kingdom onward Her solar power is reactive and defensive — she protects the established order (ma'at) rather than initiating destruction. When fully unleashed (as Sekhmet), the power becomes uncontrollable and threatens creation itself. Later cat-form depictions downplay the solar/warrior aspect in favor of domesticity, creating tension between the two representations.

Cat Form and Transformation Bastet can manifest as a full domestic cat, a woman with a cat head, or a lioness. This shapeshifting ability reflects her dual nature — the domestic cat aspect is gentle, maternal, and protective of home and children; the lioness aspect is the fierce warrior. In Egyptian mythology, the cat form specifically embodies the concept of transformation from wild to tame, from dangerous to beneficent. Cat form: Third Intermediate Period onward. Lioness form: earliest attestations The cat form was a later theological development; the earliest records show only the lioness form. The transformation is not a voluntary shapeshift in the modern sense but rather a historical evolution of how the goddess was understood and depicted. Some later syncretic texts treat the cat and lioness forms as distinct goddesses (Bastet and Sekhmet) rather than aspects of a single deity.

Protection against Contagious Disease and Evil Spirits Bastet was invoked as a protectress against miasma, plague, and malevolent spirits. Her priests created protective amulets and ointments (the 'ointment jar' connection in her name). She was specifically called upon to defend pregnant women and young children — the most vulnerable members of the household — from supernatural harm. Attested throughout Egyptian history, especially in amuletic and funerary contexts This power is conditional on proper ritual appeasement — offerings, festivals, and correct behavior. Neglecting her cult could leave one vulnerable. None attested

Fertility and Childbirth Because of the domestic cat's reputation for prolific breeding and protective mothering, Bastet became a goddess of fertility, pregnancy, and childbirth. She was believed to protect both mother and child during the dangerous process of birth. She was also associated with sexual pleasure and feminine mystique. Attested from Middle Kingdom onward, growing in prominence with the cat-form iconography Shared with other fertility goddesses (Hathor, Isis, Taweret); her specific domain was the feline aspect of fertility. Artemis, with whom the Greeks identified Bastet, was a virgin goddess — a syncretism that created tension with Bastet's fertility associations.

Control over Music, Dance, and Intoxication Bastet is intimately associated with the sistrum (a sacred rattle), music, dance, and ritual intoxication. Her annual festival at Bubastis was the largest and most joyous in Egypt, featuring ecstatic dance, music, and prodigious wine consumption. The 'feasts of drunkenness' prescribed for her were believed to appease the goddess, cooling her lioness ferocity into feline contentment. Herodotus II.59-60, Egyptian temple texts from Bubastis The drunkenness ritual has a specific mythic purpose: it recapitulates the story where dyed beer pacified the raging Sekhmet/Bastet after the destruction of humanity. None attested for the festival itself. However, the Christianizing writers of Late Antiquity condemned these festivals as pagan debauchery.

Aegis and Sistrum (Ritual Weapons) Bastet's iconic ritual implements — the sistrum and the aegis — are not merely symbols but functional ritual objects. The sistrum's rattling sound was believed to drive away evil spirits and placate dangerous deities. The aegis (a lioness-headed collar or gorget) provided magical protection. Together they represent her dual role: the sistrum as an instrument of joyful worship, the aegis as a badge of protective power. Depicted consistently from New Kingdom onward These are ritual objects used in temple context, not weapons of war in the conventional sense. None attested

Accounts

When humanity conspired against the aging sun god Ra, he sent his Eye — the goddess Hathor in the form of Sekhmet — to punish them. Sekhmet descended upon humanity with such bloodlust that the Nile ran red. She slaughtered without mercy, and as the bodies piled high, she waded through blood with savage joy. Ra, initially furious with humanity, grew horrified at the scale of destruction. He ordered that 7,000 jars of beer be dyed red with ochre and poured across the fields. Sekhmet, mistaking the red beer for blood, drank greedily until she fell into a drunken stupor. When she awoke, her fury had abated, and humanity was spared. Bastet — in her gentler aspect as the cat goddess — emerged from this myth as the pacified, domesticated form of the same divine power: the lioness who became a cat after drinking from the lake of transformation. The 'isheru' (sacred lake) at Bubastis may have represented this mythic transformation.

Each night, the sun god Ra travels through the underworld in his solar barque (mandjet — the day barque; mesektet — the night barque). The great serpent Apep (Apophis), embodiment of chaos, lies in wait each night to devour the barque and plunge the world into eternal darkness. Bastet, as one of Ra's divine defenders, joins the crew of the solar barque. In some depictions, she takes the form of a great cat who beheads the serpent with her claws at the persea tree of Heliopolis — a key scene from the Book of the Dead (Chapter 17). This cat, described as 'the great cat who is at the persea tree in Heliopolis,' is an aspect of Bastet or Ra himself manifesting through the goddess. By defeating Apep, Bastet ensures the sun's rebirth each dawn.

Bastet was the consort of Ptah, the creator god and patron of craftsmen at Memphis, and together they had a son: Maahes, a lion-headed war god whose name means 'he who is true beside her'. Maahes (also known as Mihos, Mysis) inherited his mother's lioness ferocity. He was a god of war and protection, depicted as a lion or a lion-headed man holding a knife, devouring captives. The family triad of Ptah-Bastet-Maahes created a theological bridge between Memphis (the ancient capital and center of Ptah worship) and Bubastis (Bastet's cult center), suggesting deliberate theological integration during the New Kingdom or Third Intermediate Period.

The temple of Bastet at Bubastis housed a famous oracle, which gained considerable influence especially after Greeks began settling in the Delta during the Saite and Ptolemaic periods. The oracle was consulted for divine guidance on matters ranging from personal health to state policy. Because the Greeks identified Bastet with Artemis, the oracle attracted both Egyptian and Greek pilgrims, creating a unique syncretic cult center. The oracle functioned through a system of priestly interpretation — the goddess spoke through the movement of cult statues, through dream incubation, or through the behavior of the sacred temple cats. Though no detailed oracular procedure text survives from Bubastis specifically, the general pattern of Egyptian oracles (processions where the barque of the god would give 'yes' or 'no' answers by moving forward or backward) likely applied.

When the Persian king Cambyses II invaded Egypt in 525 BCE, he faced the Egyptian army at Pelusium, the fortress at the eastern edge of the Delta. Knowing the Egyptians' reverence for cats — especially those sacred to Bastet — Cambyses ordered his soldiers to paint or carry cats on their shields and to place cats, dogs, ibises, and other sacred animals in the front line of his army. The Egyptians, unwilling to harm the sacred animals, did not shoot their arrows or throw their spears, and the Persians won the battle decisively. Cambyses subsequently captured Bubastis itself, dismantled its walls, and began the period of Persian domination of Egypt (the 27th Dynasty). This story, preserved by the Greek military writer Polyaenus four centuries later, is likely apocryphal or exaggerated but reflects the perceived power of Bastet's cult even in the eyes of foreign conquerors.

Content Notes

Hundreds of thousands of cats were deliberately killed — often by strangulation or blunt-force trauma (skull fractures, dislocated spines) — before the age of two to be mummified as votive offerings to Bastet. Radiographic analysis of specimens from Saqqara confirms these methods. The scale of this practice (over 300,000 at Bubastis alone, additional thousands at Beni Hasan, Saqqara, and Speos Artemidos) indicates systematic cat breeding for sacrifice. Many 'mummies' sold to pilgrims contained only partial cat bones, mud, clay, or pebbles — fraudulent offerings that demonstrate the commercial exploitation of piety. The 1890 shipment of thousands of cat mummies to Liverpool being sold as fertilizer is a grim postscript to this practice.

This practice was not unique to cats — millions of animal mummies (ibises, dogs, crocodiles, baboons, falcons, fish) were produced across Egypt for votive purposes. The cat mummy industry was part of a broader ancient economy of religious sacrifice. Modern scholarship views this as evidence of the scale of popular religion rather than as cruelty anachronistically judged.

Radiographic studies of Saqqara cat mummies (1980s); Conway (1890), archaeological reports from Bubastis and Beni Hasan

After Egypt became a Roman province (30 BCE), a series of imperial edicts in the 4th-5th centuries CE gradually suppressed pagan worship. Temples including the great temple of Bastet at Bubastis were closed, impounded, or destroyed. The Theodosian decrees (391-392 CE) prohibited pagan rituals and burial at all cult sites. By 399 CE, destruction of pagan temples was mandated. By 415 CE, all property formerly dedicated to pagan cults was transferred to the Christian church. The cult of Bastet — which had endured for nearly three millennia — was forcibly extinguished. Many statues were smashed, temples quarried for stone, and the site of Bubastis fell into ruin.

This was part of a broader late-antique pattern of Christianization across the Roman Empire. The destruction was uneven — some temples were repurposed as churches, others systematically demolished. The loss of knowledge about Bastet's cult persisted until the decipherment of hieroglyphs in the 19th century.

Theodosian Code (CTh 16.10); archaeological evidence of temple destruction levels at Bubastis

According to Polyaenus, the Persian king Cambyses II exploited Egyptian reverence for Bastet's sacred cats by placing cats and other animals before his front lines at the Battle of Pelusium (525 BCE). The Egyptians allegedly ceased fighting rather than harm the animals, leading to their defeat and the Persian conquest of Egypt. If the story is true, Bastet's cult was weaponized as a psychological warfare tactic. If apocryphal, it still demonstrates how foreign powers perceived and exploited Egyptian religious taboos.

Herodotus — who also describes the battle and visited Egypt within a century of it — does NOT mention the cat stratagem, casting doubt on Polyaenus's account. Most modern historians treat the cat-shield story as legendary rather than factual, though it reflects authentic Egyptian attitudes toward cats.

Polyaenus VII.9 (2nd century CE, written four centuries after the event)

In the late 1880s, Édouard Naville excavated Bubastis for the Egypt Exploration Society, uncovering hundreds of thousands of cat mummies. The 'spoils' were dispersed: many cat mummies were sold as fertilizer in Liverpool (1890), others became museum specimens or private collections. William Martin Conway described the Beni Hasan excavations of 1890: 'The plundering of the cemetery was a sight to see... the path became strewn with mummy cloth and bits of cats' skulls and bones and fur in horrid positions, and the wind blew the fragments about and carried the stink afar.' This colonial-era excavation treated the mummified remains of what were once sacred animals as commercial goods and curiosities rather than as human-manufactured religious artifacts deserving of dignity.

This was standard 19th-century Egyptological practice. The ethical standards of modern archaeology — which would treat such remains with greater care — did not exist. The commercial sale of mummies as fertilizer was common for human as well as animal remains, reflecting Victorian-era attitudes toward non-European cultures.

Conway (1890); Naville (1887-1889) excavation reports; EES archives

Bastet and Sekhmet are two aspects of the same goddess, with Sekhmet representing the unleashed destructive power and Bastet the tamed, protective form. What We Don't Know

This dossier was researched primarily via Wikipedia Direct API (full article extraction for Bastet, Sekhmet, Cats in Ancient Egypt, Bubastis, and Ancient Egyptian Religion extracts) and Firebase search (limited due to billing exhaustion). The following gaps remain: (1) Detailed Egyptian temple inscriptions from Bubastis beyond Herodotus's description — the original excavation reports (Naville 1887-1889, in French) were not consulted. Published academic works like Lexicon der Ägyptologie entries and Quaegebeur (1991) on the Greco-Roman cult were not accessed. (2) The exact relationship between Bastet and the earlier goddess Bat — Bat (cow goddess of the 7th nome of Upper Egypt) shares the sistrum and the face depicted on Hathor-head columns; some scholars connect her name and iconography to Bastet's, but this was not explored. (3) Phoenician and Carthaginian reception of Bastet — the Phoenician rendering 'bst in Punic inscriptions suggests the cult spread beyond Egypt, but no Carthaginian material was consulted. (4) Nubian origins — the possibility of Bastet's Nubian origin mentioned in the literature was not verified against Nubian archaeological evidence. (5) Liturgical specifics — the precise rituals, hymns, and daily temple procedure at Bubastis remain obscure in the available English sources. The Greek geographer Strabo's description of Bubastis (17.1.27) was not consulted. (6) Modern archaeological work at Tell-Basta (Zagazig) since Naville — recent salvage excavations and their findings were not reviewed. (7) The 'sacred cat' Felis bubastis described by Ehrenberg (1830) — this taxonomic claim was not verified against modern zoological classification.

A way to hold it in your mind

She began as a lioness — a sun-scorched warrior wading through blood with her sister, drunk on slaughter, so terrible that the gods themselves had to trick her into peace with a lake of dyed beer. But three thousand years of worship sanded away the rage. By the time the Greeks arrived, the lioness had become a housecat, curled and purring at the hearth, a goddess of music and dance and the safety of mothers in childbirth. Bastet is the only deity in history known to have fundamentally changed species — from apex predator to domestic companion — without ceasing to be a god. She is Sekhmet who learned to love.
