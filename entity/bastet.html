Who It Is

She began as a lioness — a sun-scorched warrior wading through blood with her sister, drunk on slaughter, so terrible that the gods themselves had to trick her into peace with a lake of dyed beer. But three thousand years of worship sanded away the rage. By the time the Greeks arrived, the lioness had become a housecat, curled and purring at the hearth, a goddess of music and dance and the safety of mothers in childbirth. Bastet is the only deity in history known to have fundamentally changed species — from apex predator to domestic companion — without ceasing to be a god. She is Sekhmet who learned to love.

Bastet embodies a profound duality: the fierce protectiveness of a lioness combined with the nurturing grace of a domestic cat mother. She is simultaneously a solar warrior goddess who defends Ra against the serpent Apep, and a gentle goddess of home, fertility, and music. Her personality as described in Egyptian texts is that of a watchful guardian — quick to anger in defense of her charges but equally quick to purr when appeased. Powers
Solar Protection (Eye of Ra)

As one of the 'Eyes of Ra', Bastet serves as a fierce protector of the sun god, defending him against his eternal enemy, the chaos serpent Apep. She is also depicted as the defender of the king of Egypt, who was the earthly embodiment of Horus and the sun god. This solar connection means she wields the burning, destructive power of the sun — the same power that Sekhmet embodies in its unleashed form.

Limit: Her solar power is reactive and defensive — she protects the established order (ma'at) rather than initiating destruction. When fully unleashed (as Sekhmet), the power becomes uncontrollable and threatens creation itself.

Cat Form and Transformation

Bastet can manifest as a full domestic cat, a woman with a cat head, or a lioness. This shapeshifting ability reflects her dual nature — the domestic cat aspect is gentle, maternal, and protective of home and children; the lioness aspect is the fierce warrior. In Egyptian mythology, the cat form specifically embodies the concept of transformation from wild to tame, from dangerous to beneficent.

Limit: The cat form was a later theological development; the earliest records show only the lioness form. The transformation is not a voluntary shapeshift in the modern sense but rather a historical evolution of how the goddess was understood and depicted.

Protection against Contagious Disease and Evil Spirits

Bastet was invoked as a protectress against miasma, plague, and malevolent spirits. Her priests created protective amulets and ointments (the 'ointment jar' connection in her name). She was specifically called upon to defend pregnant women and young children — the most vulnerable members of the household — from supernatural harm.

Limit: This power is conditional on proper ritual appeasement — offerings, festivals, and correct behavior. Neglecting her cult could leave one vulnerable.

Fertility and Childbirth

Because of the domestic cat's reputation for prolific breeding and protective mothering, Bastet became a goddess of fertility, pregnancy, and childbirth. She was believed to protect both mother and child during the dangerous process of birth. She was also associated with sexual pleasure and feminine mystique.

Limit: Shared with other fertility goddesses (Hathor, Isis, Taweret); her specific domain was the feline aspect of fertility.

Control over Music, Dance, and Intoxication

Bastet is intimately associated with the sistrum (a sacred rattle), music, dance, and ritual intoxication. Her annual festival at Bubastis was the largest and most joyous in Egypt, featuring ecstatic dance, music, and prodigious wine consumption. The 'feasts of drunkenness' prescribed for her were believed to appease the goddess, cooling her lioness ferocity into feline contentment.

Limit: The drunkenness ritual has a specific mythic purpose: it recapitulates the story where dyed beer pacified the raging Sekhmet/Bastet after the destruction of humanity.

Aegis and Sistrum (Ritual Weapons)

Bastet's iconic ritual implements — the sistrum and the aegis — are not merely symbols but functional ritual objects. The sistrum's rattling sound was believed to drive away evil spirits and placate dangerous deities. The aegis (a lioness-headed collar or gorget) provided magical protection. Together they represent her dual role: the sistrum as an instrument of joyful worship, the aegis as a badge of protective power.

Limit: These are ritual objects used in temple context, not weapons of war in the conventional sense.

Accounts I
The Destruction of Humanity and the Drunkenness of the GoddessPapyrus Cairo 86637 (Calendar of Lucky and Unlucky Days); also referenced in the Book of the Heavenly Cow (New Kingdom)

New Kingdom (textual compilation); myth likely older

When humanity conspired against the aging sun god Ra, he sent his Eye — the goddess Hathor in the form of Sekhmet — to punish them. Sekhmet descended upon humanity with such bloodlust that the Nile ran red. She slaughtered without mercy, and as the bodies piled high, she waded through blood with savage joy. Ra, initially furious with humanity, grew horrified at the scale of destruction. He ordered that 7,000 jars of beer be dyed red with ochre and poured across the fields. Sekhmet, mistaking the red beer for blood, drank greedily until she fell into a drunken stupor. When she awoke, her fury had abated, and humanity was spared. Bastet — in her gentler aspect as the cat goddess — emerged from this myth as the pacified, domesticated form of the same divine power: the lioness who became a cat after drinking from the lake of transformation. The 'isheru' (sacred lake) at Bubastis may have represented this mythic transformation. Established the annual 'Feast of Drunkenness' celebrated at Bubastis and other temple sites. The myth explains how the fierce lioness goddess became the gentle cat

Bastet in the Solar Barque: Defeating ApepBook of the Dead (various chapters); funerary texts

New Kingdom through Ptolemaic period

Each night, the sun god Ra travels through the underworld in his solar barque (mandjet — the day barque; mesektet — the night barque). The great serpent Apep (Apophis), embodiment of chaos, lies in wait each night to devour the barque and plunge the world into eternal darkness. Bastet, as one of Ra's divine defenders, joins the crew of the solar barque. In some depictions, she takes the form of a great cat who beheads the serpent with her claws at the persea tree of Heliopolis — a key scene from the Book of the Dead (Chapter 17). This cat, described as 'the great cat who is at the persea tree in Heliopolis,' is an aspect of Bastet or Ra himself manifesting through the goddess. By defeating Apep, Bastet ensures the sun's rebirth each dawn. Reinforced Bastet's role as a solar protector deity. The image of the cat beheading the serpent under the persea tree became a powerful apotropaic symbol used on amulets and tomb paintings.

Accounts II
Bastet as Mother of MaahesEgyptian temple inscriptions (Luxor, Edfu); Plutarch, De Iside et Osiride

New Kingdom onward

Bastet was the consort of Ptah, the creator god and patron of craftsmen at Memphis, and together they had a son: Maahes, a lion-headed war god whose name means 'he who is true beside her'. Maahes (also known as Mihos, Mysis) inherited his mother's lioness ferocity. He was a god of war and protection, depicted as a lion or a lion-headed man holding a knife, devouring captives. The family triad of Ptah-Bastet-Maahes created a theological bridge between Memphis (the ancient capital and center of Ptah worship) and Bubastis (Bastet's cult center), suggesting deliberate theological integration during the New Kingdom or Third Intermediate Period. Created a family triad linking the Memphite and Bubastite theological systems. Maahes became a significant deity in his own right with temples at Leontopolis and elsewhere in the Delta.

The Oracle of Bastet at BubastisHerodotus II.83; various dedicatory inscriptions

Late Period through Ptolemaic (c. 664-30 BCE)

The temple of Bastet at Bubastis housed a famous oracle, which gained considerable influence especially after Greeks began settling in the Delta during the Saite and Ptolemaic periods. The oracle was consulted for divine guidance on matters ranging from personal health to state policy. Because the Greeks identified Bastet with Artemis, the oracle attracted both Egyptian and Greek pilgrims, creating a unique syncretic cult center. The oracle functioned through a system of priestly interpretation — the goddess spoke through the movement of cult statues, through dream incubation, or through the behavior of the sacred temple cats. Though no detailed oracular procedure text survives from Bubastis specifically, the general pattern of Egyptian oracles (processions where the barque of the god would give 'yes' or 'no' answers by moving forward or backward) likely applied. The oracle contributed to Bubastis's prosperity and fame throughout the Mediterranean world. It was one of several Egyptian oracles (along with Siwa, Buto, and Saïs) that influenced Greek religious practice.

Cambyses II and the Battle of Pelusium (525 BCE)Polyaenus, Stratagems, Book VII.9

Late Period, 27th Dynasty (Persian)

When the Persian king Cambyses II invaded Egypt in 525 BCE, he faced the Egyptian army at Pelusium, the fortress at the eastern edge of the Delta. Knowing the Egyptians' reverence for cats — especially those sacred to Bastet — Cambyses ordered his soldiers to paint or carry cats on their shields and to place cats, dogs, ibises, and other sacred animals in the front line of his army. The Egyptians, unwilling to harm the sacred animals, did not shoot their arrows or throw their spears, and the Persians won the battle decisively. Cambyses subsequently captured Bubastis itself, dismantled its walls, and began the period of Persian domination of Egypt (the 27th Dynasty). This story, preserved by the Greek military writer Polyaenus four centuries later, is likely apocryphal or exaggerated but reflects the perceived power of Bastet's cult even in the eyes of foreign conquerors. Egypt fell under Persian control. Bubastis declined in political importance. The story became a symbol of Egyptian piety and the strategic use of religious taboos in warfare.

Bastet vs. Sekhmet: One goddess or two?Bastet and Sekhmet are two aspects of the same goddess, with Sekhmet representing the unleashed destructive power and Bastet the tamed, protective form. The duality is expressed through the myth of the pacification through beer.Bastet and Sekhmet are historically and theologically distinct deities who originated in different cult centers (Bubastis vs. Memphis). Their syncretization was a late political/theological development of the Third Intermediate Period and should not be read backward into earlier periods.
Bastet's original form: Lioness or something else?Bastet was originally a fierce lioness goddess, worshipped as such from the Second Dynasty onward, with the cat form being a late (Third Intermediate Period) development.Bastet's original animal form is uncertain. The earliest evidence (Second Dynasty seal impressions) does not clearly depict her form. She may always have been associated with smaller felines rather than the Barbary lion, with the 'lioness' identification being an assumption based on later Sekhmet syncretism.
The Battle of Pelusium cat-shield story: historical or legendary?The story told by Polyaenus — that Cambyses II placed cats on Persian shields to exploit Egyptian taboos and win the Battle of Pelusium — is historically accurate and represents a documented instance of psychological warfare.The cat-shield story is a legendary anecdote that developed centuries after the battle. Herodotus, who wrote about Egypt within decades of the Persian conquest and describes the battle, does not mention it. Polyaenus wrote 600 years later and collected stratagems of dubious historicity.
The 'alabaster' etymology from Bastet's nameThe English word 'alabaster' derives — via Greek ἀλάβαστρος (alabastros) — from the name of the goddess Bastet, because the stone was used for ointment vessels associated with her cult.The etymology is speculative and unsupported by linguistic evidence. The Greek alabastros predates significant Greek contact with Bubastis, and the phonetic pathway from 'Bastet' to 'alabastros' requires implausible sound changes.
