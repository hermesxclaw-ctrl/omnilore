Who It Is

The Nixe is constitutionally ambivalent, and the ambivalence is patterned rather than random. The female Nixe of German village narrative can warn of coming storms, give gifts to children, and act as a benevolent house-spirit; the same figure drowns fishermen and abducts children, and a widely diffused legend has a band of nixe-daughters slipping into the evening dance among humans, returning home to the water late, and being killed by their father — the story ends with red blood rising to the surface. The male Nix is far more consistently negative: he is said to enjoy drowning children, a reputation exploited deliberately as a Kinderschreckfigur to keep the young away from millponds and riverbanks, and he abducts girls and women out of sexual interest, functioning as a house-spirit only rarely. Name & Tongue

Etymology — The word family descends from a Common Germanic etymon reconstructed as *nikwus / *nikwis(i), conventionally traced to the Proto-Indo-European root *neigʷ- 'to wash, to cleanse'. Cognates adduced for this root include Sanskrit nḗnēkti, Greek νίζω (nízō) and νίπτω (níptō), Old Irish nigid, and Latin

Pronunciation — German: [ˈnɪksə] ('NIK-suh'); masculine Nix [nɪks]. English 'nixie': /ˈnɪksi/ (NIK-see). Swedish näcken [ˈnɛkːɛn]; Norwegian nøkk [nœk]; Icelandic/Faroese nykur [ˈnɪːkʏr]; Finnish näkki [ˈnækːi].

Aliases — Nix (masculine singular, German), Nixen / Nixe (plural forms; German grammar distinguishes die Nixe [f., pl. Nixen] from der Nix [m., pl. Nixe]), Nickse (Hessian dialect, attested in Pfister-Schwaighusen 1885), Wassernixe (Middle High German wazzernixe, first attested in Konrad von Würzburg, late 13th c.), Wasserfrau ('water woman', the commonest German synonym for the female figure), Wassermann ('water man', the commonest German synonym for the male figure, statistically more frequent than Nix)

Ownership — The Nixe belongs, in the narrowest and most defensible sense, to the German-speaking Rhineland and the wider German Sprachraum, where 'Nixe' has functioned since the tenth century

Form & Signs

Appearance — There is no single Nixe body, and the incoherence is itself the datum: German folkloristics stresses that what 'Nix(e)' denoted varied sharply by period, region and register, and that the beautiful fish-tailed maiden of modern imagination cannot be projected back onto the medieval glosses. In the dominant later German pattern the female Nixe is a beautiful woman, human from the waist up and bearing a fish's tail below — the merwîp of the Nibelung

Symbols — The Nixe's iconographic signature is water made legible: the wet skirt-hem (nasser Kleidersaum) that unmasks her among humans; the comb and mirror of the hair-combing water-woman, inherited by Heine's Lorelei combing golden hair on her cliff; the fiddle or, regionally, the horn, flute or harmonica of the Nordic näck; and blood rising red through water as the sign that a nix has killed.
