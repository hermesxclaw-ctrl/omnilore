Who It Is

On a mist-bound ridge of Mount Katsuragi, a sovereign hunting party meets its own double — same banners, same bearing, same number of men — and when the emperor demands a name, the other commander answers in a single, unbreakable word: Hitokotonushi, the Master of One Word, who will grant one wish or speak one judgment and then fall silent forever.

Nihon Shoki: laconic, authoritative, morally decisive — 'decides good and evil with one word' — and awe-inspiring enough that Emperor Yūryaku, known for ferocity, immediately worships and withdraws rather than confronts. Not tricksterish or loquacious; taboo on many words. Medieval engi: stern, literal, punishes those who speak too many words or break a one-word vow; generous if petition is sincere and singular. Shugendo setsuwa: resentful servant who retaliates by slander when coerced, showing capacity for grudge (urami) and political agency. Folk practice (Ichigon-san): benevole

Powers
One-Word Judgment / Oracle (Hitokoto no Shinpan)

Decides good and evil, right and wrong, or true and false with a single word. In Nihon Shoki, this is his defining epithet. Functionally an oracular power: one utterance is final and morally binding.

Limit: Cannot or will not speak at length; one word only. Judgment is absolute but context-bound; does not explain reasoning. Requires direct encounter or formal petition.

One-Wish Granting (Ichigon Jōju)

Grants a single wish if petitioned with one word or one short, sincere phrase. Modern shrine practice: visitors whisper one wish into ichigon-ishi or pray with single-minded focus.

Limit: One wish per person or per visit; second words void the petition. Traditionally requires brevity, sincerity, and moral worthiness; greedy or contradictory wishes fail. Not omnipotent — cannot override fate or other kami's domains in some tellings.

Mimetic Manifestation / Doubling

Appears with a retinue that exactly mirrors the viewer's party in number, dress, and bearing, creating an uncanny double. Implies illusion, duplication, or status-mirroring power.

Limit: Only attested in imperial encounter; not described as general shapeshifting. Appears to be tied to Mount Katsuragi liminal space.

Mountain Lordship and Boundary Control

As nushi of Katsuragi, controls passage, weather on the mountain, and access to its interior. Hunters and ascetics must propitiate him.

Limit: Territorial — power wanes outside Katsuragi range. Subordinate to larger heavenly kami in court mythology.

Curse and Retribution (Tatari)

When offended, slandered, or when vow is broken, can inflict tatari — illness, misfortune, or political downfall. In Shugendo legend, his slander causes En no Gyoja's exile, implying supernatural influence at court.

Limit: Retribution is indirect (slander, not lightning); requires provocation. Can be bound/contained by stronger ascetic magic (En no Gyoja's spell).

Supernatural Labor (Bound Servant Form)

When bound by En no Gyoja's spell, performs superhuman construction — carrying stones to build a bridge between Mount Katsuragi and Mount Ōmine.

Limit: Only while bound; power is coerced, not voluntary. Ends when he rebels via slander. Some versions say he was bound with a vine or spell-cord and could not break free alone.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No canonical anthropomorphic iconography in Nara-period texts. Nihon Shoki describes Hitokotonushi as appearing as a human commander whose retinue exactly mirrors the emperor's — same clothing, same numbers, same bearing — implying a mimetic, double-like manifestation rather than a monstrous form. Symbols — Primary symbols: mountain (Mount Katsuragi itself as shintai), word/speech (koto), and the number one (ichi/hito). Ritual objects: ichigon-ishi (one-word stones) on shrine grounds where petitioners whisper one wish; ema and votive plaques often inscribed with single kanji or phrase. No fixed weapon or animal emblem; association with Katsuragi's evergreen and stone. In Shugendo context, bound by spell-cord (juhō) and associated with bridge-bu

Domains — Habitat: Mount Katsuragi (Katsuragi-san), the ridge between Yamato and Kawachi (modern Nara/Osaka border), specifically its peaks and passes. Domain: koto (word, utterance), kotowari (judgment), oracles, vows, and mountain boundaries. Functions as yama no kami (mountain deity) and sae no kami-like boundary guardian where the imperial hunting ground meets the wild. Also oracle (takusen) deity: gives one-word divination. Accounts I
Emperor Yūryaku Hunts on Mount Katsuragi and Meets His DoubleNihon Shoki, Vol.14, Emperor Yūryaku 7th year (trad. 462 CE)

Nara period compilation (720) describing late 5th century

Emperor Yūryaku (Ohatsuse no Wakatakeru), a vigorous and headstrong sovereign, goes hunting on Mount Katsuragi with his retinue. On the ridge he encounters another hunting party coming the opposite way, identical to his own in numbers, dress, and bearing, led by a figure who looks like the emperor himself. Yūryaku angrily demands, 'Who are you in this land where I alone am sovereign?' The other leader answers, 'I am Hitokotonushi, who decides good and evil with one word.' Recognizing a kami, Yūryaku immediately dismounts, worships, presents his sword and bow, and withdraws from the mountain, ordering his men not to hunt there. The kami accepts worship and vanishes. Establishes Hitokotonushi's status as a mountain sovereign equal in dignity to the earthly sovereign, and establishes taboo on hunting/violating Katsuragi. Becomes locus classicus for 'one-word' epithet and for imperial-kami etiquette. Later shrine foundation legend traces to this awe-encounter.

The Absence in the Kojiki — A Story of OmissionKojiki (712) — negative evidence; commentary by Kurano, Philippi, and Heldt

Nara period (712)

The Kojiki, which otherwise parallels Nihon Shoki for many Yūryaku legends, contains no Hitokotonushi. Its Yūryaku sections focus on the emperor's poems and romantic exploits, not mountain theophanies. The omission is not a narrative but a textual-historical story: why one court chronicle enshrines a Katsuragi word-god and the other erases him. Scholars propose Kojiki's compilers, tied to different clan sources (Ō no Yasumaro's lineage), omitted Katsuragi traditions that Nihon Shoki's more encyclopedic, Chinese-style compilation included. Demonstrates that kami canonicity was editorial. Hitokotonushi's survival depends on Nihon Shoki transmission; without it he might have remained a purely local oral deity. Modern scholarship uses the divergence to map clan politics of memory.

En no Gyoja Binds Hitokotonushi to Build a Stone BridgeShoku Nihongi (797) En no Ozunu entry; Nihon Ryoiki III.28; Shugendo engi (Minoodera engi, Katsuragi engi)

Early Heian (late 8th-early 9th century legends, set in late 7th century)

En no Ozunu (En no Gyoja), ascetic of Mount Katsuragi and founder of Shugendo, seeks to prove his supernatural power. He summons the local kami — identified as Hitokotonushi of Katsuragi — and binds him with a spell (or vine-cord), commanding him to carry stones and build a bridge between Mount Katsuragi and Mount Ōmine so ascetics can cross. Hitokotonushi, humiliated at being enslaved, nurses a grudge. He appears before the court (or sends a spirit) and slanders En, accusing him of plotting rebellion with his demonic powers. The court believes the kami and exiles En no Gyoja to Izu Ōshima (707 CE). Later, En is vindicated, but Hitokotonushi remains bound to the mountain as a chastened servant in some versions, or is released after atonement. Inverts Nara image: sovereign kami becomes bound demon/familiar, reflecting Buddhist ascetic ideology where buddhas and hijiri subjugate local kami. Explains En's exile and elevates Shugendo's power over native deities. At Katsuragi, stones said to be half-piled are shown as proof of the aborted bridge.

Accounts II
Ichigon-san — The One-Word Wish at Katsuragi Hitokotonushi JinjaKatsuragi Hitokotonushi Jinja engi; Edo-period pilgrimage diaries and contemporary shrine practice

Medieval to present (engi medieval, folk practice Edo 1603-1868 to contemporary)

At the foot of Mount Katsuragi, the shrine teaches: speak one wish, one word, with single-minded sincerity, and Hitokotonushi will grant it — but speak a second word and the wish is void. Pilgrims from Yamato and Kawachi climb to whisper into the ichigon-ishi, or write one kanji on ema: 'healing,' 'pass,' 'marriage.' Anecdotes abound: a mother who prayed 'live' for her sick child and the child recovered; a samurai who asked 'victory' and won but lost his voice for asking a second favor; a merchant who tried to list two wishes and received neither. The taboo on verbosity becomes moral teaching: human greed needs the discipline of one word. Sustains living cult; shrine remains known as Gose's Ichigon-san, a regional 'wish shrine' distinct from grand imperial shrines. Practice reinforces linguistic taboo (kotodama belief) that words have spirit power and must be used sparingly.

The Slander and the Exile — Hitokotonushi as TatarigamiSetsuwa collections (Nihon Ryoiki, Konjaku Monogatarishū variants) and Shugendo oral tradition

Heian to Kamakura (9th-13th century)

After being bound, Hitokotonushi does not forgive. He appears in the capital as a mysterious informant, telling the court that En no Gyoja is gathering demons to overthrow the emperor. The court, fearing sorcery, exiles En. In this telling Hitokotonushi acts as tatarigami — a wrathful deity who curses when disrespected. Later, when En's innocence is proven (or when En's disciple Karakuni no Muraji Hirotari pleads), Hitokotonushi is himself cursed to remain ugly or invisible, explaining why he is rarely seen. The story warns against both enslaving kami and slandering holy men. Establishes dual nature: benevolent oracle and dangerous wrathful deity. Provides etiology for En no Gyoja's exile and for Hitokotonushi's invisibility. Reinforces Heian court anxiety about mountain ascetics and local kami.

The Katsuragi Stones — The Unfinished Bridge as Landscape LegendLocal folklore, Gose City tourism and shrine signage; Shugendo engi

Medieval to present (landscape etiology)

On the slopes between Katsuragi and Ōmine, scattered large stones are pointed out as Hitokotonushi's abandoned work. When En no Gyoja bound him, Hitokotonushi was forced to carry boulders on his back to build a bridge for ascetics. Each night he piled stones; each dawn his slander interrupted the work. When En was exiled, the bridge was left half-built, and the stones remain where the kami dropped them. Villagers say on foggy nights you can hear the kami's single sigh — 'ah' — as he remembers his servitude. The legend turns geology into memory of coerced labor. Landscape becomes proof of myth; stones serve as tangible relic and pilgrimage stop. Reinforces Shugendo sacred geography linking Katsuragi and Ōmine as a single ritual range.

Hitokotonushi is a yōkai or ghost that haunts Mount Katsuragi. — In primary sources he is kami (deity), not yōkai. Yōkai classification is early modern (Toriyama Sekien onward). Shugendo setsuwa demonizes him as a bound demon, but Shinto orthodoxy classifies him as kami. Calling him yōkai collapses Shinto and yōkai taxonomies.

Hitokotonushi will grant any wish if you say it in one word, like a genie. — Nara source says he judges good and evil with one word — his word, not the petitioner's. Wish-granting is medieval folk elaboration with strict conditions (sincerity, brevity, moral worthiness, one wish only). Greedy or verbose petitions are said to fail or offend.

Hitokotonushi appears in the Kojiki as Emperor Yūryaku's companion. — He does not appear in Kojiki at all. The Yūryaku-Katsuragi encounter is unique to Nihon Shoki. Kojiki's Yūryaku sections contain poems and romances but no Hitokotonushi.

Hitokotonushi is the same as Kotoshironushi, just a different pronunciation. — Names share nushi and word-power, but genealogies, habitats (Izumo vs Katsuragi), and textual attestations are distinct. No early text equates them; Edo folk conflation is not scholarly consensus.
