Who It Is

Imagine a world where the sun is not just a star in the sky but a king whose daily journey decides whether anything lives at all. In ancient Egypt, that king was Ra: a falcon-headed blaze of light who creates gods from his own body, sails a perilous river of darkness every night, and once unleashed a lioness to drown humanity in blood. To follow Ra across three thousand years of texts and temples is to watch a civilization think through power, vulnerability, and the terrifying question of what happens if the sun does not rise.

Ra’s personality is complex and shifts with mythic context. In creation and kingship myths he appears as a sovereign, authoritative, and beneficent ruler who establishes maat (cosmic order) and sustains life. In the Book of the Heavenly Cow he is an aging, somewhat distant god whose anger at human rebellion leads to severe punishment via his Eye, yet he ultimately moderates the destruction and withdraws from direct rule on earth. In the Isis myth he is vulnerable, suffering from age and the serpent’s bite, and subject to the cunning of another deity. Ra is not portrayed as morally perfect;

Powers
Solar illumination and life-giving power

Ra embodies the physical sun and its light, which Egyptians understood as the fundamental source of warmth, growth, and life. His daily rising marks the beginning of time and activity; his light dispels darkness and chaos, enabling agriculture, social order, and the visibility of the world.

Limit: Ra’s light is cyclical: it disappears each night when he enters the Duat. The sun’s dependence on regeneration underscores that Ra is not an immutable static force but a recurring process.

Creation of gods, world, and humans

In Heliopolitan myths, Ra (often as Atum-Ra) self-creates from Nun and then generates the first divine pair (Shu and Tefnut), from whom the rest of the Ennead descends. He is credited with creating heaven, earth, underworld, and the beings within them. Humans are sometimes said to arise from his tears or sweat, leading to the phrase “Cattle of Ra” for humanity.

Limit: Other creation myths (Hermopolis, Memphis, Thebes) attribute creation to different gods (Ogdoad, Ptah, Amun), so Ra’s creative role is not exclusive across Egypt.

Maintenance of maat (cosmic order)

Ra is father of Maat, the principle of truth, justice, and cosmic order. His regular solar cycle and his rulership over gods and humans embody the maintenance of this order. As king of gods, he judges rebellions and establishes boundaries between divine and human realms.

Limit: Ra’s ability to maintain maat is challenged by human rebellion and by the forces of chaos (Apophis). His withdrawal from earth marks a shift in how maat is upheld, with human kings taking on part of the responsibility.

Underworld journey and regeneration

Each night Ra travels through the Duat in a solar barque, accompanied by gods and spirits. He reanimates the dead for a limited time, combats Apophis, and undergoes regeneration, emerging at dawn as Khepri. This cycle ensures the continuity of life and the cosmos.

Limit: Ra’s nightly vulnerability is real: if Apophis were to succeed, the sun would fail to rise and cosmic order would collapse. Rituals and spells are required to support Ra’s victory.

Royal legitimation and kingship

From the Fourth Dynasty onward, Egyptian kings adopt the title “Son of Ra,” presenting themselves as earthly manifestations or offspring of the sun god. Ra’s favor legitimizes their rule, and their pyramids, obelisks, and sun temples materialize the solar ideology.

Limit: Kingship ideology shifts over time, with Amun and other gods gaining prominence. The solar component remains important but is not always exclusive.

Punitive power via the Eye of Ra

Ra can send his Eye—personified as a goddess such as Sekhmet, Hathor, or other fierce forms—to punish rebels and enemies. In the Heavenly Cow myth, the Eye becomes Sekhmet, who slaughters humans in response to their rebellion.

Limit: Ra’s punitive power can exceed his intentions, requiring him to intervene (e.g., intoxicating Sekhmet with beer dyed red to halt the slaughter).

Magical and secret-name power

Ra possesses a hidden true name that encapsulates his essence and power. Knowledge of this name grants control over him, as shown when Isis extracts it through magical cunning.

Limit: Ra’s secret name is vulnerable to magical manipulation, indicating that even the supreme god is subject to the broader Egyptian logic of heka (magic) and divine names.

Accounts I
Heliopolitan Creation Myth (Atum-Ra and the Ennead)Pyramid Texts, Coffin Texts, temple inscriptions at Heliopolis; reconstructed from scattered allusions.

Old Kingdom through New Kingdom, with later elaborations

From the primeval waters Nun, the first god Atum (often identified with Ra or Atum-Ra) emerges on the benben mound. Alone, he generates Shu and Tefnut, who in turn produce Geb and Nut, and then Osiris, Isis, Set, and Nephthys, forming the Ennead. Ra/Atum-Ra creates heaven, earth, and underworld, establishes maat, and rules as king of gods and humans. This myth grounds the divine genealogy, cosmic structure, and royal ideology of Egypt. It legitimizes the pharaoh as successor to Ra’s primordial kingship and situates all later events within a created, ordered cosmos.

Ra’s Nightly Journey through the DuatAmduat, Book of Gates, Coffin Texts, royal tomb inscriptions.

New Kingdom, with antecedents in Middle Kingdom funerary texts

Each night, Ra enters the western horizon and travels through the twelve hours of the Duat in a solar barque. He encounters various regions, gods, and spirits, combats Apophis, and bestows temporary life on the dead. In the deepest hour, Ra unites with Osiris and undergoes regeneration, emerging at dawn as Khepri, the rising sun. The journey ensures the daily rebirth of the sun and the ongoing possibility of afterlife for the dead. It structures underworld geography and ritual, and it provides a model for the king’s posthumous existence.

Accounts II
The Destruction of Mankind (Myth of the Heavenly Cow)Book of the Heavenly Cow inscriptions in New Kingdom royal tombs.

18th–19th Dynasty

Humans rebel against Ra, speaking against him and plotting. Ra consults the other gods and sends his Eye, manifest as the goddess Sekhmet, to punish them. Sekhmet slaughters humans so fiercely that Ra fears total annihilation. To stop her, he orders beer dyed red to resemble blood; Sekhmet drinks it, becomes intoxicated, and ceases the slaughter. Ra then withdraws from earth, ascending to the sky on the back of the Heavenly Cow, and establishes a new order in which human kings rule and death becomes part of the world. The myth explains the origin of death, the separation of gods and humans, and the institution of human kingship. It underscores Ra’s capacity for wrath and mercy and the precariousness of human existence.

Isis and the Secret Name of RaNew Kingdom magical and narrative papyri (e.g., Papyrus Turin 1993).

New Kingdom, likely Ramesside period

The aging sun god Ra drools as he walks, leaving spittle on the ground. Isis gathers this spittle and mixes it with earth to create a magical serpent, which bites Ra. Stricken and unable to heal himself, Ra calls upon the gods. Isis offers to cure him if he reveals his hidden true name. After resistance, Ra finally discloses the name, and Isis heals him, gaining power over him through her knowledge. The story illustrates the centrality of heka (magic) and divine names in Egyptian thought, and it elevates Isis as a powerful, cunning goddess. It also portrays Ra as vulnerable, complicating his image as invincible solar ruler.

Ra as First King and the Departure of the GodsBook of the Heavenly Cow and related theological texts.

New Kingdom, with roots in earlier kingship ideology

Ra is said to have ruled on earth as the first king, during a time when gods and humans lived together without night or death. As Ra ages and humans rebel, the gods withdraw from earth, and Ra ascends to the sky. Human kings then rule in his stead, bearing the title “Son of Ra” and maintaining maat in a world now subject to death and evil. This narrative underpins the ideology of kingship, presenting the pharaoh as successor to Ra’s primordial rule and explaining why divine presence is mediated rather than direct.
