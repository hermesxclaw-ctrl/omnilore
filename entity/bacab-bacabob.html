Who They Are

The Bacab are four brothers gods in Maya mythology who hold up the sky at its four corners, preventing it from collapsing onto the earth. Each Bacab is associated with a cardinal direction, a color, and a specific year within the 52-year Calendar Round. When one Bacab tires and shifts his burden, the world experiences an earthquake.

The Approach

In the Yucatec Maya cosmos, four ancient, aged deities called Bacabs strain eternally at the four corners of the world, holding up the sky so that it never falls. These 'Pawahtuns' are not distant, passive giants; they are intimately woven into the daily life of the Maya—invoked in rituals for rain, crops, health, and even the well-being of bees. They are the thunderous grandfathers of the Gulf Coast, the guardians of directions, and the ever-present support of the universe itself.

Content advisory. Contains references to alcohol and drunkenness in later traditions, and to the destruction of the world by deluge. Depictions of violence in iconography (young warrior threatening deity) are mentioned. No graphic content.

Etymology

The name 'Bacab' is the generic Yucatec Maya term for these four deities. The plural form is 'Bacabob'.

Cultural Ownership

Yucatec Maya culture, primarily from the Yucatán Peninsula, with related traditions in the Gulf Coast region.

Disputed Identifications

The Bacabs are often conflated with the Pawahtuns, who are wind deities, and with the Chaacs, rain deities. Iconographically, they are identified as God N in the Schellhas-Zimmermann-Taube classification, but there are also conflations with Itzamna (God D) in hieroglyphic texts.

Description

Appearance

The Bacabs are depicted as aged, elderly deities. In iconography, God N is shown as an old man with a conch, turtle, snail, or spider web as attributes. He may wear a bee 'armour' or a netted headdress. Often represented fourfold, they are shown carrying the sky or the roof of a building.

Symbols & Iconography

Conch shell, turtle, snail, spider web, bee armour, netted headdress. They are associated with the four directions and the Year Bearer days.

Behaviour & Personality

In Yucatec traditions, they are the four brothers who hold up the sky. They are associated with rain and agriculture, and are invoked in divination and curing rituals. In later Gulf Coast traditions, they are lecherous, drunken old thunder deities, and are conceived as drowned ancestors serving for a year.

Domains & Habitats

The interior of the earth and its water deposits. They reside at the four cardinal points, holding up the sky. They are also associated with mountains and thunder.

Version Differences

In Yucatec tradition, they are four brothers created by God to hold up the sky. In Gulf Coast traditions, they are 'Grandfathers' who control rain, and are replaced annually. In iconography, they appear as God N, an aged deity of thunder, mountains, and the interior of the earth.

Stories & Myths

Creation and Sky-bearing

Source Book of Chilam Balam of Chumayel, also quoted in secondary sourcesCulture Yucatec MayaPeriod Prehispanic and Colonial

Summary

The four Bacabs were four brothers whom God placed at the four points of the world to hold up the sky. They escaped when the world was destroyed by the deluge.

Participants

God

Four Bacabs: Hobnil, Cantzicnal, Saccimi, Hosanek

Consequences

The sky is held up; the world is structured.

Variants

Francisco Hernández mentions a single Bacab, son of Itzamna and Ixchebelyax, who was humbled, killed, and revived.

Katun 11 Ahau and the Fall of the Sky

Bacab in a Shell

Source Chama vessels (iconography)Culture Maya (Late Classic)Period Late Classic (c. 600-900 CE)

Summary

A Bacab is hidden in a shell or snail, and a young warrior threatens him with a spear, knife, or razor to get him out. The shell symbolizes a cave.

Participants

Bacab

Young warrior (possibly Xb'alamQ'e)

Consequences

The Bacab is dragged out; this scene may allude to the first act of Rabinal Achí.

Variants

Oral tradition collected by Dieseldorff: Xb'alamQ'e tried to drag Xucaneb, the highest hill in Verapaz, who is the supreme Tzuul Taq'a. The old man resisted, forming the mountain range from Xucaneb to Senahú.

Cultural Significance

The Bacab personify the Maya understanding of the cosmos as a structure that requires constant maintenance. The sky is literally a burden held up by divine beings, and earthquakes are understood as their movement. This cosmology directly influenced Maya architecture, where 'atlantean' figures (human-like pillars with raised arms) were used as structural supports in buildings — physically representing the Bacab's cosmic role. The Bacab's directional system also structured Maya city planning, with the four directions and their associated colors reflected in the placement of temples, plazas, and causeways.
