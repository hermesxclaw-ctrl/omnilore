Who It Is

In the Yucatec Maya cosmos, four ancient, aged deities called Bacabs strain eternally at the four corners of the world, holding up the sky so that it never falls. These 'Pawahtuns' are not distant, passive giants; they are intimately woven into the daily life of the Maya—invoked in rituals for rain, crops, health, and even the well-being of bees. They are the thunderous grandfathers of the Gulf Coast, the guardians of directions, and the ever-present support of the universe itself.

In Yucatec traditions, they are the four brothers who hold up the sky. They are associated with rain and agriculture, and are invoked in divination and curing rituals. In later Gulf Coast traditions, they are lecherous, drunken old thunder deities, and are conceived as drowned ancestors serving for a year.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Bacab' is the generic Yucatec Maya term for these four deities. The plural form is 'Bacabob'.

Pronunciation — [ɓaˈkaɓ]

Aliases — Bacabob, Pawahtun, God N

Ownership — Yucatec Maya culture, primarily from the Yucatán Peninsula, with related traditions in the Gulf Coast region.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The Bacabs are depicted as aged, elderly deities. In iconography, God N is shown as an old man with a conch, turtle, snail, or spider web as attributes. He may wear a bee 'armour' or a netted headdress. Often represented fourfold, they are shown carrying the sky or the roof of a building.

Symbols — Conch shell, turtle, snail, spider web, bee armour, netted headdress. They are associated with the four directions and the Year Bearer days.

Domains — The interior of the earth and its water deposits. They reside at the four cardinal points, holding up the sky. They are also associated with mountains and thunder.

Accounts I
Creation and Sky-bearingBook of Chilam Balam of Chumayel, also quoted in secondary sources

Prehispanic and Colonial

The four Bacabs were four brothers whom God placed at the four points of the world to hold up the sky. They escaped when the world was destroyed by the deluge. The sky is held up; the world is structured.

Katun 11 Ahau and the Fall of the SkyBook of Chilam Balam of Chumayel

Colonial

In the cosmological upheaval of Katun 11 Ahau, Oxlahuntiku ('Thirteen-god') was humbled by Bolontiku ('Nine-god'). The sky would fall, and the four gods, the four Bacabs, were set up to prevent it. The Bacabs are established as sky-bearers.

Accounts II
Resurrection of the Maize GodIconography (e.g., on vessels)

Classic (c. 250-900 CE)

A Bacab inhabiting a turtle (representing the earth) is part of scenes of the resurrection of the maize god. The maize god is resurrected.

Bacab in a ShellChama vessels (iconography)

Late Classic (c. 600-900 CE)

A Bacab is hidden in a shell or snail, and a young warrior threatens him with a spear, knife, or razor to get him out. The shell symbolizes a cave. The Bacab is dragged out; this scene may allude to the first act of Rabinal Achí.
