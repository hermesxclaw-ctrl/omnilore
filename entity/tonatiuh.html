Who They Are

Tonatiuh is the Aztec sun god, the ruler of the Fifth Sun — the current and final age of the world. He is a warrior god who demands human blood to sustain his daily journey across the sky and to prevent the final destruction of the world. His image in the center of the famous Aztec Calendar Stone shows his consuming nature and his role as the axis of the cosmos.

Attested in Codices (Florentine, Borgia) / Aztec Calendar Stone / Oral TraditionEra Aztec Empire (c. 1325-1521 CE)

Form

Tonatiuh is typically depicted as a fierce, anthropomorphic solar deity with a round face, wide eyes, and a protruding tongue shaped like a sacrificial flint knife (tecpatl), symbolizing his hunger for blood. His face is often surrounded by solar rays or flames. He wears a headdress adorned with turquoise, feathers, and sometimes a solar disc. The Aztec Sun Stone central figure embodies his visage, incorporating stylized elements such as clawed hands holding human hearts.

Powers

Solar Movement Tonatiuh governs the movement of the sun across the sky, representing the passage of time and the maintenance of cosmic order. Aztec Calendar Stone, Florentine Codex His movement is contingent on receiving human blood and sacrifice; failure to sustain him results in cosmic catastrophe. While some sources depict him as autonomously powerful, others stress his dependency on sacrifice.

Demand for Human Sacrifice Tonatiuh requires human hearts and blood to gain strength for his daily journey and to prevent the destruction of the world. Florentine Codex, Codex Borgia This need is a cosmic necessity; without it, the sun would cease to move, causing the end of the current era. Some interpretations suggest symbolic rather than literal sacrifice; however, primary Aztec ritual practice confirms literal sacrifice.

Warrior Aspect Tonatiuh embodies martial strength and courage, often depicted as a solar warrior who battles darkness and chaos. Codex Borgia, Aztec Sun Stone His warrior role is symbolic of cosmic struggle rather than physical combat. Overlap with Huitzilopochtli causes some conflation of warrior attributes.

Accounts

After the destruction of the previous four suns (world ages), the gods gathered at Teotihuacan to create a new sun to restore balance. Two gods, Tecuciztecatl and Nanahuatzin, sacrificed themselves by leaping into a sacred fire. Nanahuatzin's humble sacrifice ignited the sun—Tonatiuh—who required the nourishment of human blood to continue his journey across the sky.

Tonatiuh is depicted as the central figure around whom the cosmos revolves. His face at the center of the Calendar Stone symbolizes his role as the axis mundi, the pivot of time and space, sustaining the universe through sacrifice and light.

Cultural Significance

Tonatiuh is the central figure of the Aztec Calendar Stone, the most famous work of Aztec sculpture, discovered in 1790 in the Zócalo of Mexico City. The stone depicts the four previous suns around the central image of Tonatiuh, representing the Aztec belief that each age ends in cataclysm and that the Fifth Sun is destined to be destroyed by earthquakes. The imperative of human sacrifice in Aztec religion — the moral and cosmic necessity of feeding the sun — is most directly embodied in Tonatiuh's cult. The Aztec flower wars (xochiyaoyotl) were fought specifically to capture prisoners for sacrifice to Tonatiuh, ensuring the sun's continued movement.

Scholarly Debates
Tonatiuh’s identity as a distinct sun god versus an aspect of Huitzilopochtli
Tonatiuh is a distinct solar deity responsible for the Fifth Sun, separate from Huitzilopochtli. ⇌ Tonatiuh is an aspect or manifestation of Huitzilopochtli, the Mexica war god who also embodies the sun.
Literal versus symbolic interpretation of human sacrifice to Tonatiuh
Human sacrifice was literal and essential for Tonatiuh’s cosmic function. ⇌ Sacrifices may be primarily symbolic or exaggerated by colonial sources.
Number and nature of previous suns (world ages)
There were four previous suns, each destroyed by different cataclysms, followed by Tonatiuh as the Fifth Sun. ⇌ Variations exist in the number and nature of previous suns and their destruction methods.

What We Don't Know

Limited direct pre-conquest textual sources on Tonatiuh’s detailed mythology; most knowledge derives from post-conquest codices and Spanish chroniclers, which may contain biases. Oral traditions are fragmentary and filtered through colonial interpretations. Some iconographic details remain open to interpretation due to damaged or incomplete codices and stones. The exact nature of Tonatiuh's relationship with Huitzilopochtli remains debated without definitive archaeological evidence to clarify conflation. The scale and voluntariness of human sacrifice require further archaeological and bioanthropological study.
