Who It Is

In the vast pantheon of Greek gods — with their thunderbolts, love affairs, and epic grudges — there exists a deity so obscure that he appears exactly once in all of ancient literature. His name, Alcidemos, means 'Strength of the People.' He had a temple in the Arcadian city of Tegea. And that is virtually everything we know. No myths. No statues. No worshippers' prayers. Just a name, a temple, and a footnote in a Roman-era travel guide. He is the deity equivalent of a background extra in a blockbuster film — technically present, completely unknown, and yet somehow fascinating precisely because of his silence.

No myths or narrative accounts describe Alcidemos' behavior, personality, or actions. He is a name attached to a temple in Pausanias' periegesis and little else.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Greek Ἀλκίδημος (Alkidemos), a compound of ἀλκή (alkē, 'strength, prowess, valour') and δῆμος (dēmos, 'people, community'). The name thus signifies 'he who has the strength of the people' or 'mighty for the people' — an epithet that emphasizes collective power rather than individual might.

Pronunciation — al-KID-eh-mos (English); al-KEE-day-mos (reconstructed Classical Greek)

Aliases — Alcidamas, Alexidemos, Alcidemus

Ownership — Greek (Arcadia specifically), with possible syncretic overlap with other regional deities

Form & Signs

Appearance — No description of Alcidemos' appearance survives. No cult statue, votive relief, or coin image has been securely identified. The god's visual form is entirely unknown — a frustrating blank in the record.

Symbols — No symbols, attributes, or iconographic markers are attested for Alcidemos. He is iconographically blank.

Domains — If the name's etymology is any guide, Alcidemos was a deity of communal strength — possibly a war god, a protector of the people, or a divine embodiment of civic unity. The domain is inferred from the name, not attested in any source.

No myth, legend, aition, or narrative involving Alcidemos survives in any ancient source. He is mentioned only in Pausanias' architectural survey of Tegea. The god remains a cipher — a name and a temple, but no story.

Accounts II
Possible association with EchemusPausanias 8.9.6 (contextual)

c. 150-175 CE

Pausanias mentions the temple of Alcidemos in the same passage where he discusses the tomb of Echemus, the legendary Arcadian king who slew Hyllus, son of Heracles, in single combat. The proximity is suggestive but not conclusive — the temple may have been near the tomb, or the association may be coincidental. If Alcidemos was connected to Echemus, he may have been a hero-god associated with the defense of Arcadia against the Heracleidae. This remains speculation.

Alcidemos was a major Greek deity with a rich mythology. — He is attested in exactly one ancient source (Pausanias) with no mythology whatsoever. His prominence is a modern artifact of database completeness, not ancient importance.

Alcidemos is the same as Alcidas or Alcimedon. — These are distinct names. Alcidas is a Spartan king; Alcimedon is a Trojan warrior in Homer. The similarity is coincidental.
