Who It Is

Beelzebub occupies a uniquely prominent position in demonic hierarchies as the 'Prince of Demons' — second only to Satan in many Christian traditions and sometimes identified as Satan himself. His name derives from the Philistine god Baal-Zebub ('Lord of Flies') worshipped at Ekron, whom King Ahaziah of Israel consulted in 2 Kings 1, provoking Elijah's condemnation. The Hebrew pun Baal-Zebul ('Lord of the Dwelling' or 'Lord of Dung') was a derogatory corruption, mocking the pagan deity. In the New Testament, Beelzebub appears multiple times: the Pharisees accuse Jesus of casting out demons 'by Beelzebub, the prince of demons' (Matthew 12:24), to which Jesus famously responds with the 'house divided' argument. This exchange cemented Beelzebub's role in Christian demonology as a figure of ultimate demonic authority. Medieval and Renaissance demonologists — including the Malleus Maleficarum, Johann Weyer's Pseudomonarchia Daemonum, and the Lesser Key of Solomon — elaborated a complex hierarchy in which Beelzebub rules as one of the supreme infernal trinity alongside Lucifer and either Satan or Leviathan. His specific domains include gluttony, idolatry, and flies — the latter connecting to his original name and the association of flies with decay, corruption, and spiritual pestilence. In John Milton's Paradise Lost, Beelzebub is Lucifer's chief lieutenant and confidant, the first to speak after the fall and the strategist who proposes corrupting God's new creation (humanity) as revenge. Milton portrays him as mournful yet resolved, a fallen majesty who counsels dark policy. In later demonological grimoires, Beelzebub is described as appearing as a gigantic fly or a figure surrounded by swarming insects, capable of spreading disease and corruption. His transformation from a Canaanite deity to the second-most-feared name in Christian demonology illustrates the long process by which competing gods are systematically demonized in monotheistic traditions. In some texts, he is the specific demon of gluttony — the lord of those consumed by appetite — extending the fly symbolism to the swarming, insatiable hunger of excess.
