Etymology

Swiss German diminutive of 'Wildmann' (wild man), with the suffix '-li' indicating smallness or endearment. Literally 'little wild man'.

Cultural Ownership

Swiss (Alpine), specifically the German-speaking regions of Switzerland, including the cantons of Bern, Uri, Schwyz, and Graubünden.

Disputed Identifications

Sometimes conflated with other Alpine wild men such as the 'Bergmönch' (mountain monk) or 'Barbegazi' (a gnome-like creature), but the Wildmannli is generally considered a distinct dwarf-like wild man. Some scholars see it as a regional variant of the broader European 'wild man' motif.

Archive Dossier — Approach

Deep in the Swiss Alps, where the peaks pierce the clouds and the forests are thick with shadow, there is said to live a creature no bigger than a child, yet strong enough to move boulders. They call it the Wildmannli—the little wild man—and those who have met it speak of a being that is both shy and strangely kind, a guardian of the mountains who asks only for a bit of bread in return for its help.

When the Alpine herder wakes to find his fence mended, his butter churned, and a wedge of the finest Emmental left on his doorstep, he knows the Wildmannli have visited in the night.

Who It Is

Die Wildmannli (also Wilde Männli — 'Little Wild Men') are small, shaggy, dwarf-like creatures from Swiss Alpine folklore, particularly in the Bernese Oberland, Valais, and Graubünden regions. They are described as tiny humanoids covered in thick, shaggy fur or mossy hair, with long beards, small horns, and wearing simple clothing made of leaves and animal skins. Wildmannli live in high mountain caves and rock crevices above the treeline, emerging in summer when the snow melts. They are fundamentally benevolent toward humans: they help alpine herders by guiding lost cows back to the barn, mending broken fences, churning butter, and protecting livestock from bears and wolves. In return, they ask only for a small portion of milk, cheese, or bread left at the cave entrance. Wildmannli are particularly known for their love of music — if a herder plays an alpenhorn or a zither, the Wildmannli will come out to dance and reward the musician with cheese or gold. However, they are intensely shy and will never show themselves fully to humans, working only at night or when no one is looking. If a human tries to capture a Wildmannli or steal its tools (especially its tiny ax or milk pail), the creature will disappear forever, and the cows will stop producing milk. In some traditions, the Wildmannli are believed to be the spirits of pagan Alpine peoples who retreated into the mountains when Christianity came, or the remnants of an ancient pre-human race. They share features with the Scandinavian Tomte, the German Kobold, and the British brownie. The Wildmannli are distinct from the Wild Man (Wilder Mann) of German tradition, who is a solitary, giant, hairy figure — the Wildmannli are specifically small, communal, and helpful.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

The Wildmannli is typically described as a small, dwarf-like being covered in hair or fur, sometimes with a beard. It may wear simple clothing made of moss or leaves, or go naked. It is often depicted with a staff or club.

Symbols & Iconography

In Swiss heraldry and woodcarvings, wild men are often shown with a club and a shield, but the Wildmannli as a dwarf variant is less commonly depicted. It may be associated with mountain caves and forests.

Behaviour & Personality

The Wildmannli is generally shy and elusive, avoiding human contact. It is known to be helpful on occasion, such as guiding lost travelers or assisting with chores, but can also be mischievous or vengeful if provoked. It is said to live in remote mountain areas, in caves or under rocks.

Domains & Habitats

Alpine regions of Switzerland, including high mountain passes, forests, and caves.

Version Differences

Some accounts describe it as a single creature, others as a small group. It is sometimes confused with the 'Bergmönch' (mountain monk) who is larger and more solitary. The Wildmannli may also be linked to the 'Barbegazi' of the French Alps, but that creature is more often described as a gnome with large feet.
