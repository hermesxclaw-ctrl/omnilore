Who They Are

The storm is getting closer. You can feel it in your teeth now. Thunder doesn't roll — it cracks, like the sky is being torn apart by something enormous. Then you see him: a red-bearded giant in a chariot pulled by goats, spinning a hammer that trails lightning. He's grinning. The giants are dead. You're not a giant. You hope he knows that.

Thor is the Norse god of thunder, lightning, storms, strength, and the protection of mankind. He is the son of Odin and the earth goddess Jörd (or Fjörgyn), and he is — contrary to the Marvel-influenced image — a red-bearded, hot-tempered giant of a god who loves fighting, drinking, and smashing things with his hammer Mjölnir. He is not the brightest of the gods, but he is the most beloved. While Odin is the god of kings and poets, Thor is the god of the common people — the farmer praying for rain, the fisherman begging for calm seas, the family blessing their home against spirits.

Thor's primary function is protection. He guards Midgard (the human world) and Asgard (the gods' realm) from the jotnar (giants), who represent the chaotic, destructive forces of nature. He spends most of his mythological existence traveling to Jotunheim to kill giants, often accompanied by Loki before the trickster's final betrayal. His hammer Mjölnir is not just a weapon — it blesses marriages, consecrates births, and hallows the dead. It is the symbol of cosmic order pushing back against chaos, the thunder that drives away the storm that threatens to swallow the world.

“The storm is getting closer.”
Origins

Thor is the son of Odin and Jörd (Earth), making him literally the child of the Sky and the Earth. He was raised in part by his foster parents Vingnir and Hlora, who gave him strength. Mjölnir was forged by the dwarves Brokkr and Sindri as part of a contest with Loki; its only flaw is a short handle caused by Loki (in fly form) biting Brokkr during the forging. Thor earned the hammer by successfully lifting it — no small feat, since Mjölnir's weight is partly magical — and has wielded it as his primary weapon ever since.

Form

A massively built man with wild red hair and a red beard that crackles with static electricity when he's angry. He has fierce, bright eyes the color of lightning. He wears a belt of strength (Megingjörð) that doubles his already-immense power and iron gloves (Járngreipr) required to grip Mjölnir. He is often depicted in a chariot pulled by two goats, Tanngrisnir and Tanngnjóstr (Teeth-Grinder and Teeth-Barer), whose bones he can magically resurrect after eating them. His face is weathered, scarred, and perpetually ready for a fight.

Powers

Mjölnir (Hammer) A war-hammer that is Thor's primary weapon. It is described as being able to level mountains, and it always returns to Thor's hand after being thrown. It also has a sanctifying function, used in blessings (e.g., at weddings and funerals). Prose Edda, Poetic Edda, saga literature The handle is famously short, due to Loki's mischief during its forging. It is also heavy and requires Thor's strength (and belt) to wield effectively. Some sources describe Mjölnir as a hammer, others as an axe or club; the short handle is attested in Skáldskaparmál.

Megingjörð (Belt of Strength) A belt that when worn doubles Thor's already-godly strength. Prose Edda, Gylfaginning It is a physical item, so it can be removed or lost (as in Þrymskviða, where Thor needs it to lift the hammer). None significant.

Járngreipr (Iron Gloves) Iron gloves required to grip Mjölnir's hot handle. Prose Edda, Gylfaginning Only useful with Mjölnir. None significant.

Gríðarvölr (Staff) A staff borrowed from the giantess Gríðr, used in the encounter with Geirröðr. Skáldskaparmál Not a permanent possession; used only in that specific adventure. None.

Chariot and Goats Thor rides a chariot pulled by two goats, Tanngrisnir and Tanngnjóstr. He can eat the goats and resurrect them the next day with Mjölnir, as long as their bones are unbroken. Gylfaginning, Þrymskviða The goats cannot be resurrected if their bones are broken (as in the story of Þjálfi and Röskva). Some sources describe Thor walking rather than riding the chariot (e.g., Hárbarðsljóð).

Behavior & Habitat

Thor is straightforward, honest, quick to anger, and quicker to forgive. He loves a good fight, a good drink, and a good story. He has little patience for trickery, poetry, or complex schemes — he solves problems by hitting them. He is fiercely protective of humanity and will fight anything that threatens Midgard, regardless of the odds. He has a temper that can get him into trouble and a surprising sense of humor about himself. He is deeply loyal to his family (including his wife Sif and his children) and to his oaths. He dislikes Loki but tolerates him as a traveling companion until the final betrayal at Ragnarök.

Weaknesses & Wards

Thor is not clever — he is repeatedly outwitted by giants and tricksters and relies on others (often Loki or humans) for strategy. His strength has limits, as proven when he failed to lift the Midgard Serpent disguised as a cat (he managed to lift one paw, which was impressive enough). He is susceptible to magic and illusion. His temper can be exploited to goad him into traps. He is fated to die at Ragnarök, killed by the venom of Jormungandr after slaying the serpent.

Accounts

The giant Þrymr steals Mjölnir and demands Freyja as his bride in exchange. Thor, with Loki's help, dresses as Freyja (with Loki as his handmaiden) and goes to the wedding. After eating and drinking prodigiously (and glaring at the giants), he retrieves Mjölnir and slaughters the giants.

Thor and the giant Hymir go fishing. Thor uses a giant ox head as bait and catches Jörmungandr, the World Serpent. The serpent thrashes, and Hymir cuts the line, but not before Thor's strength is demonstrated. The story ends with Thor and Hymir's contentious feast, after which Thor steals Hymir's cauldron.

Thor, Loki, Þjálfi, and Röskva travel to Jötunheimr. They face a series of challenges (eating, running, wrestling) that they fail, only to learn that Útgarða-Loki had tricked them with illusions (e.g., wrestling with old age itself). Thor's strength is shown to be immense but not absolute.

The giant Hrungnir challenges Thor to a duel. Hrungnir has a heart of stone and a shield of stone, but Thor throws Mjölnir, which shatters the giant's club and kills him. A whetstone from Hrungnir's club lodges in Thor's head, and the seeress Gróa fails to remove it (because she is distracted by news of her husband).

At Ragnarök, Thor fights Jörmungandr for the final time. He slays the serpent, but after walking nine steps, he succumbs to its venom and dies.

Cultural Significance

Thor was the most widely worshipped Norse god among the common people, and his hammer Mjölnir was the most popular amulet in the Viking world — pendants have been found in graves across Scandinavia, Britain, and Russia. 'Thor's Hammer' pendants were worn as a defiant response to Christianization. Thursday is named after him (Thor's Day). He represented the Norse ideal of physical courage, straightforward honor, and the willingness to face overwhelming odds without flinching.

Modern Interpretations

Thor's most prominent modern incarnation is the Marvel Comics version, which sanitizes and glamorizes him into a blond, clean-shaven superhero — a significant departure from the mythological red-bearded brawler. The Marvel films (Chris Hemsworth) have made him a global icon. The 'God of War: Ragnarök' game takes a darker approach, presenting Thor as a broken, alcoholic weapon of Odin who struggles with his role as a killer. Neil Gaiman's 'Norse Mythology' and 'American Gods' restore some of his complexity. He remains the archetype of the thunder-god hero, influencing everything from D&D paladins to modern Pagan revivalism.

Content Notes

Adam of Bremen describes the Temple at Uppsala where Thor is the most powerful god, and mentions that nine males of every species, including humans, are sacrificed every nine years. This is a Christian source, so its accuracy is debated, but it suggests a cultic practice that may have involved human sacrifice.

Historians debate the extent and nature of human sacrifice in Norse religion; some see it as rare, others as more common. The account is likely exaggerated but not entirely fabricated.

Adam of Bremen, Gesta Hammaburgensis (c. 1075)

Thor and other Norse gods were co-opted by Nazi ideology to promote Aryan supremacy and Germanic nationalism. This is a modern misuse of the mythology, not a reflection of ancient beliefs.

This appropriation is widely condemned by contemporary scholars and Heathen groups, who emphasize the original context of the myths.

Scholarly Debates
Thor's appearance
Thor has red hair and a red beard (Prose Edda). ⇌ Some early depictions (e.g., on runestones) show him with a beard but no color, and some modern interpretations depict him with blonde hair.
Evidence for A: Prose Edda, Gylfaginning ch. 21.
Evidence for B: Lack of color in archaeological evidence; later artistic license.
Thor's parentage
Thor is the son of Odin and Jörð. ⇌ In some sources, Thor is said to be the son of Odin and a giantess named Fjörgyn (which is another name for Jörð, but some argue it is a separate entity).
Evidence for A: Prose Edda.
Evidence for B: Vafþrúðnismál refers to Fjörgyn as Thor's mother.
Thor's role in the pantheon
Thor is the most powerful and popular god, especially among common people. ⇌ Odin is the highest god, and Thor is subordinate to him.
Evidence for A: Adam of Bremen (Thor as the most powerful in the temple); numerous Mjölnir finds.
Evidence for B: Snorri's framing in the Prose Edda, where Odin is the 'All-Father'.
Thor's death at Ragnarök
Thor kills Jörmungandr but dies from its venom after walking nine steps. ⇌ Some sources suggest Thor survives Ragnarök (e.g., the mention of Magni and Móði inheriting Mjölnir, but no explicit statement that Thor is dead).
Evidence for A: Vafþrúðnismál, Gylfaginning.
Evidence for B: Silence on Thor's survival; the inheritance of Mjölnir implies his death.
What We Don't Know

Further research needed on: (1) the exact nature of Thor's cult in Anglo-Saxon England (Thunor) — evidence is scarce; (2) the archaeological evidence for Mjölnir pendants and their use in rituals; (3) the relationship between Thor and the Celtic Taranis — comparative mythology is speculative; (4) the role of Thor in modern Heathenry, which is a living tradition and requires care; (5) the full list of place names derived from Thor (e.g., Thorshavn, Thorsberg) — a comprehensive survey is needed.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Thor as the ultimate bodyguard and bouncer of the Norse gods. He's not the brains of the operation (that's Odin), but he's the one you call when a giant crashes the party. He's the god of the common people, the farmer, the fisherman — the one who brings the thunder that waters the crops. He's also a god of boundaries: he protects the human world (Midgard) from the chaotic giants (Jötunheimr). He's the thunderstorm that breaks a drought, the strong arm that defends the village, and the hammer that blesses a marriage or a funeral. He's the god you want on your side in a bar fight — and in the end, he's the one who fights the ultimate monster, even knowing he'll die.
