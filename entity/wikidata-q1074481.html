Who It Is

An island shaped like a crescent moon, where the air carries the scent of mastic and the stones whisper tales of Homer, empires, and tragedy—this is Chios, a land of myth and history that has stood at the crossroads of civilizations for millennia.

The island's culture is a blend of Greek, Byzantine, Genoese, and Ottoman influences. The inhabitants are known for their maritime traditions, agricultural practices (especially mastic cultivation), and strong local identity. The medieval villages, particularly in the Mastichochoria, reflect a history of communal defense and self-governance.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Chios' is traditionally derived from Chios, son of Poseidon and a nymph, born amidst snowfall (Greek: χιών, chiōn, 'snow'). Alternatively, it may come from a nymph named Chione. Ancient names include Ophioussa ('snake island') and Pityoussa ('pine-tree island').

Pronunciation — Greek: [ˈçi.os]

Aliases — Scio, Chio, Sakız, Ophioussa, Pityoussa

Ownership — Greek; historically part of Byzantine, Genoese, and Ottoman spheres

Powers
Mastic Production

The island is renowned for its cultivation of mastic gum, a resin obtained from the lentisk tree (Pistacia lentiscus). This unique product has been a significant economic resource since antiquity, used in food, medicine, and cosmetics.

Limit: Mastic cultivation is limited to the southern part of the island, particularly the Mastichochoria villages.

Naval Power

In the Classical period, Chios possessed a powerful fleet, contributing 100 ships to the Ionian fleet at the Battle of Lade in 494 BC, the largest contingent of any Ionian state.

Limit: Naval power declined after the Persian Wars and subsequent conquests.

Chian Earth

A white clay found near Pyrgi, known in antiquity as an astringent and cosmetic. It was reputed to whiten and smooth the skin and prevent wrinkles, though considered less valuable than Lemnian earth for medicinal purposes.

Limit: Not widely used today; the exact composition and properties are not fully documented.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The island is crescent or kidney-shaped, 50 km long and 29 km wide, with mountainous terrain. The northern part is dominated by Mount Pelineon (1,297 m) and Mount Epos (1,188 m). The center is divided by the Provatas range. The landscape includes fertile valleys, terraced slopes, and rocky coastlines.

Symbols — The sphinx was the ancient symbol of Chios, appearing on its coins from the 7th century BC for nearly 900 years. The island is also associated with mastic, the resin from the lentisk tree, which is a key cultural and economic symbol.

Domains — Chios is located in the northern Aegean Sea, separated from the Anatolian mainland by the Chios Strait. It has a Mediterranean climate with warm, dry summers and mild, wet winters. The island is home to diverse habitats, including pine forests, maquis shrubland, and agricultural terraces.

Accounts I
Homeric ConnectionLocal tradition and ancient claims

Antiquity to present

Chios is one of several places claiming to be the birthplace of Homer. The suburb of Vrontados is traditionally associated with the poet, and an archaeological site known as 'Teacher's Rock' is said to be where Homer taught. The claim contributes to the island's cultural identity and attracts tourism.

Accounts II
Chios MassacreHistorical accounts

1822

During the Greek War of Independence, Ottoman troops massacred, expelled, or enslaved tens of thousands of Greeks on Chios. The event was a major atrocity that drew international attention and humanitarian response. The massacre devastated the island's population and economy. It also inspired artistic works, such as Delacroix's painting 'The Massacre of Chios'.

Nea Moni MonasteryHistorical records

11th century

The monastery of Nea Moni was founded with funds from Byzantine Emperor Constantine IX, after three monks petitioned him during his exile on Lesbos. It became a wealthy and influential religious center. The monastery became a UNESCO World Heritage Site and is a major tourist attraction. It was damaged in the 1822 massacre and the 1881 earthquake.
