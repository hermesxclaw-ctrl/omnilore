Who It Is

In the moonlit wastes of the Arabian desert, a lonely traveler hears a voice calling his name—a voice that sounds like his long-dead mother, or a sister he never had. But if he follows it, he will find not a warm embrace, but a feast of his own flesh. This is the ghul, a shape-shifting demon of pre-Islamic Arabia, a creature that has haunted the human imagination for millennia, evolving from a desert demon to the cemetery-dwelling ghoul of modern horror.

The ghul is a predator that lures humans, especially men, to their deaths. It may appear as a female relative or an old woman to gain trust. It eats human flesh, drinks blood, steals coins, and preys on children. It can take the form of the person it last ate. It is said to be afraid of iron and fire, and can be warded off by reciting the adhan or the Throne Verse.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Arabic غُول (ghūl), from غَالَ (ghāla) 'to seize'. The term entered English in 1786 via William Beckford's Vathek.

Pronunciation — /ɡuːl/ (English), /ɣuːl/ (Arabic)

Aliases — Ghoul, Ghulah (female), Umm Ghulah (Mother Ghoul), Ghul-e Biyaban

Ownership — Arabian / Islamic; pre-Islamic Arabia, later adopted into Islamic folklore and literature.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In Arabic folklore, the ghul is often described as a monstrous humanoid, sometimes with the ability to change shape. It may appear as a beautiful woman, an old hag, or a long-lost relative. Some sources describe it as a hyena-like creature. Al-Dimashqi describes it as a cave-dwelling animal. In modern Western depictions, ghouls are often undead, corpse-eating creatures with no fixed form.

Symbols — Cemeteries, deserts, abandoned places, the night. In art, often depicted as a demonic figure or a decaying humanoid.

Domains — Cemeteries, deserts, ruins, and other uninhabited places. In Islamic tradition, ghuls were banished to deserts and mountains.

Accounts I
Ta'abbata Sharra and the GhulPre-Islamic Arabic poetry

6th century CE

The poet Ta'abbata Sharra encounters a ghul in the desert and fights it. He defeats it and boasts of his victory. The poet's fame; the story is used to demonstrate the ghul's existence and the hero's prowess.

Umar Slays a GhulAl-Masudi, Muruj adh-dhahab

10th century CE (recorded)

On a journey to Syria, Caliph Umar encounters a ghul and kills it with his sword. Demonstrates the caliph's bravery and the ghul's vulnerability to weapons.

Accounts II
The Woodcutter's Wealthy SisterSyrian folktale, adapted in Britannica's Tales Around the World

Unknown (recorded in modern era)

A poor woodcutter meets a beautiful princess who claims to be his long-lost sister. His wife sees through the illusion, but the woodcutter ignores her. The ghul plans to eat them; the wife and children escape, leaving the woodcutter to be devoured. The woodcutter is eaten; the wife and children survive.
