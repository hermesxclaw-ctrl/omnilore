Who It Is

The Ghul (anglicized as Ghoul) is a shape-shifting desert demon from pre-Islamic Arabic folklore, later incorporated into Islamic demonology and popularized globally through the 1001 Nights. In its original form, the Ghul is a malevolent, cannibalistic monster that haunts the desert wilderness, graveyards, and remote ruins of the Arabian Peninsula. Unlike the modern zombie-like ghoul of Western fiction, the Arabic Ghul is a highly intelligent, shape-shifting trickster. It typically appears as a beautiful woman or a familiar traveler to lure lone wanderers from the caravan path; once the victim is isolated, the Ghul reveals its true form: a hideous creature covered in scales or fur, with glowing yellow eyes, sharp fangs, and long claws. Its diet consists primarily of human flesh, and it is said to dig up fresh graves to consume the dead (hence the modern association with graveyard-feeding ghouls). The Ghul is also known to feed on children and unwary travelers. In pre-Islamic Arabia, the Ghul (along with the Si'la, a related female demon) was considered a type of jinn — specifically, a devilish class of jinn that could shapeshift and possessed supernatural speed. The poet Ta'abbata Sharra famously boasted that he had killed a Ghul and that she was his only song. In Islamic tradition, the Ghul is a devil (shaytan) that can appear in different forms but is considered a creation of Iblis (Satan). The Ghul is also associated with the Si'la, a specifically female Ghul that seduces men and then devours them. Al-Mas'udi (10th century) wrote that Ghuls were real creatures inhabiting the Rub' al Khali (Empty Quarter). The Ghul entered English literature in 1786 through William Beckford's Gothic novel 'Vathek' and was further popularized by Bram Stoker's 'Dracula' and H.P. Lovecraft's Cthulhu Mythos. In modern fantasy, 'ghoul' has come to mean a zombie-like, grave-feeding undead creature, a significant departure from its intelligent, shape-shifting Arabic origins.
