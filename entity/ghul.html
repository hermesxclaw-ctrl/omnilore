Who It Is

The Ghul (anglicized as Ghoul) is a shape-shifting desert demon from pre-Islamic Arabic folklore, later incorporated into Islamic demonology and popularized globally through the 1001 Nights. In its original form, the Ghul is a malevolent, cannibalistic monster that haunts the desert wilderness, graveyards, and remote ruins of the Arabian Peninsula. Unlike the modern zombie-like ghoul of Western fiction, the Arabic Ghul is a highly intelligent, shape-shifting trickster. It typically appears as a beautiful woman or a familiar traveler to lure lone wanderers from the caravan path; once the victim is isolated, the Ghul reveals its true form: a hideous creature covered in scales or fur, with glowing yellow eyes, sharp fangs, and long claws. Its diet consists primarily of human flesh, and it is said to dig up fresh graves to consume the dead (hence the modern association with graveyard-feeding ghouls). The Ghul is also known to feed on children and unwary travelers. In pre-Islamic Arabia, the Ghul (along with the Si'la, a related female demon) was considered a type of jinn — specifically, a devilish class of jinn that could shapeshift and possessed supernatural speed. The poet Ta'abbata Sharra famously boasted that he had killed a Ghul and that she was his only song. In Islamic tradition, the Ghul is a devil (shaytan) that can appear in different forms but is considered a creation of Iblis (Satan). The Ghul is also associated with the Si'la, a specifically female Ghul that seduces men and then devours them. Al-Mas'udi (10th century) wrote that Ghuls were real creatures inhabiting the Rub' al Khali (Empty Quarter). The Ghul entered English literature in 1786 through William Beckford's Gothic novel 'Vathek' and was further popularized by Bram Stoker's 'Dracula' and H.P. Lovecraft's Cthulhu Mythos. In modern fantasy, 'ghoul' has come to mean a zombie-like, grave-feeding undead creature, a significant departure from its intelligent, shape-shifting Arabic origins.

The Approach

In the moonlit wastes of the Arabian desert, a lonely traveler hears a voice calling his name—a voice that sounds like his long-dead mother, or a sister he never had. But if he follows it, he will find not a warm embrace, but a feast of his own flesh. This is the ghul, a shape-shifting demon of pre-Islamic Arabia, a creature that has haunted the human imagination for millennia, evolving from a desert demon to the cemetery-dwelling ghoul of modern horror.

Content advisory. Contains descriptions of cannibalism, murder, child predation, and graphic violence. Some sources depict the ghul as a deceptive female figure; discussions of gender and monstrous femininity may be unsettling.
Identity

Ghul

Pronunciation /ɡuːl/ (English), /ɣuːl/ (Arabic)

Known As

GhoulGhulah (female)Umm Ghulah (Mother Ghoul)Ghul-e Biyaban

Etymology

From Arabic غُول (ghūl), from غَالَ (ghāla) 'to seize'. The term entered English in 1786 via William Beckford's Vathek.

Cultural Ownership

Arabian / Islamic; pre-Islamic Arabia, later adopted into Islamic folklore and literature.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars connect the ghul to the Mesopotamian Gallu, though this is debated. In modern usage, 'ghoul' may be conflated with other undead creatures; the ghul is distinct from the jinn, though often categorized among them.

Description

Appearance

In Arabic folklore, the ghul is often described as a monstrous humanoid, sometimes with the ability to change shape. It may appear as a beautiful woman, an old hag, or a long-lost relative. Some sources describe it as a hyena-like creature. Al-Dimashqi describes it as a cave-dwelling animal. In modern Western depictions, ghouls are often undead, corpse-eating creatures with no fixed form.

Symbols & Iconography

Cemeteries, deserts, abandoned places, the night. In art, often depicted as a demonic figure or a decaying humanoid.

Behaviour & Personality

The ghul is a predator that lures humans, especially men, to their deaths. It may appear as a female relative or an old woman to gain trust. It eats human flesh, drinks blood, steals coins, and preys on children. It can take the form of the person it last ate. It is said to be afraid of iron and fire, and can be warded off by reciting the adhan or the Throne Verse.

Domains & Habitats

Cemeteries, deserts, ruins, and other uninhabited places. In Islamic tradition, ghuls were banished to deserts and mountains.

Version Differences

In early Arabic, the ghul is feminine; later, masculine. The female ghulah is a distinct figure. In Western fiction, ghouls are often undead, while in Arabic folklore they are demonic beings. Modern versions may be vampires or zombies.

Contradictions & Disputes

Gender of the ghul

Position A

Early Arabic sources treat the ghul as feminine.

Position A — evidence

Pre-Islamic poetry and early folklore.

Position B

Later sources treat it as masculine, with Sila as the female counterpart.

Position B — evidence

Later medieval texts and hadith.

Scholarly Consensus

The ghul's gender shifted over time; both forms exist in different periods.

Existence of ghuls

Position A

Ghuls exist as real creatures.

Position A — evidence

Hadith and popular belief.

Position B

The Mu'tazilites denied their existence.

Position B — evidence

Rationalist theology.

Scholarly Consensus

Belief in ghuls was not universal in Islam; some rationalist schools rejected them.

Origin of the ghul

Position A

The ghul originated in pre-Islamic Arabia.

Position A — evidence

Linguistic and cultural evidence.

Position B — evidence

Scholarly Consensus

The pre-Islamic origin is well-established; the Gallu influence is speculative but plausible.
