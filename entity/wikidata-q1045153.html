The Approach

In the turbulent aftermath of Troy's fall, a son of the prophet Helenus and the Trojan princess Andromache carved out his own kingdom in the wilds of Epirus—yet his name survives only in scattered ancient references, a shadow of a legacy half-remembered.

Content advisory. None
Identity

Cestrinus

Pronunciation /ˈsɛstrɪnəs/ (English), /kesˈtrîːnos/ (Ancient Greek)

Known As

KestrinosKestrinusGenger (equated in one tradition)

Etymology

Ancient Greek Κεστρῖνος (Kestrînos), literally 'pieces of the fish' (from κέστρα, a fish, likely the sturgeon or a similar species). The name may be a toponymic derived from the region Cestrine.

Cultural Ownership

Greek mythology, specifically the Trojan cycle and the foundation legends of Epirus (northwestern Greece) and the Cimmerian Bosporus (around the Kerch Strait).

Disputed Identifications

One later tradition equates Cestrinus with King Genger of the Cimmerian Bosporus, but this is not widely attested and may be a local genealogical invention.

Description

Appearance

No ancient source describes Cestrinus's physical appearance. He is a legendary figure, likely imagined as a Trojan prince and warrior, but no iconography or detailed description survives.

Symbols & Iconography

None recorded. No coins, inscriptions, or artistic representations of Cestrinus are known.

Behaviour & Personality

Pausanias portrays Cestrinus as ambitious and dissatisfied with his position, willing to lead volunteers to carve out his own domain rather than accept a subordinate role under his half-brother Molossus. This suggests a strong-willed, independent character.

Domains & Habitats

Cestrinus was born and raised in Epirus, a region in northwestern Greece. After Helenus's death, he claimed the region north of the River Thyamis (modern Kalamas), which was thereafter called Cestrine. In the alternative tradition, he is associated with the Cimmerian Bosporus (around the Kerch Strait), ruling as King Genger.

Version Differences

The primary version (Pausanias) places Cestrinus in Epirus, founding the region of Cestrine. A secondary tradition equates him with King Genger of the Cimmerian Bosporus, but this is obscure and possibly a later invention.

Stories & Myths

The Division of Epirus

Source Pausanias, Description of Greece 1.11.1Culture Greek (Roman-era literary tradition)Period Heroic age (after the Trojan War), as transmitted in the 2nd century CE

Summary

After the fall of Troy, Helenus and Andromache settled in Epirus, where Helenus became king. They had a son, Cestrinus. Upon Helenus's death, Andromache's son by Neoptolemus (or by Helenus, according to some accounts) named Molossus succeeded to the throne. Cestrinus, displeased, took a band of volunteers and moved north of the River Thyamis, claiming that territory for himself. The region was named Cestrine after him.

Consequences

Establishment of the region of Cestrine in Epirus; Cestrinus became the eponymous founder of that territory.

Variants

Another tradition, recorded in later sources, makes Cestrinus king of the Cimmerian Bosporus, equated with King Genger, possibly a local ruler whose name was Hellenized.

Contradictions & Disputes

Parentage of Molossus

Position A

Molossus is the son of Neoptolemus and Andromache (common tradition).

Position A — evidence

Other mythographers, e.g., Hyginus, and the general tradition of Andromache as Neoptolemus's concubine.

Position B

Molossus is the son of Helenus and Andromache (implied by some readings of Pausanias).

Position B — evidence

Pausanias says only that Andromache had a son Molossus, without specifying father; if he were Neoptolemus's, he might have been born before Helenus's reign, but the chronology is unclear.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars accept that Molossus was the son of Neoptolemus, as stated in other sources, and that Pausanias's account is abbreviated.

Cestrinus's kingdom

Position A

Cestrinus founded a kingdom in Epirus north of the Thyamis river.

Position A — evidence

Pausanias 1.11.1.

Position B

Cestrinus was king of the Cimmerian Bosporus (as Genger).

Position B — evidence

A later tradition recorded in some sources (not specified in available material).

Scholarly Consensus

The Epirus tradition is considered the standard mythological account; the Bosporus identification is a fringe variant, possibly a local claim to Greek ancestry.
