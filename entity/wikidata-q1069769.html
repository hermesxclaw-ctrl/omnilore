The Approach

In the shadow of Troy, a king's city fell, and with it, the family of the woman who would become the Iliad's most poignant voice of loss. Eetion, ruler of Cilician Thebe, enters myth not as a hero of great deeds, but as a father whose death at Achilles' hands becomes a testament to the human cost of war.

Content advisory. Violence, death of family members, war themes.
Identity

Eetion

Pronunciation Ancient Greek: [ɛː.e.tí.ɔːn]; English approximation: ee-EE-tee-on

Known As

Eëtion

Etymology

The name Ἠετίων (Ēetíōn) is of uncertain etymology, possibly derived from a pre-Greek or Anatolian root. Some ancient sources connect it to the word αἰετός (aetos, 'eagle'), but this is speculative.

Cultural Ownership

Greek mythology, specifically the Homeric epic tradition; located in the Troad region of Anatolia (modern Turkey).

Disputed Identifications

Homer mentions another Eetion, father of Podes, who is distinct from Andromache's father. Some later sources conflate the two, but the Iliad treats them separately.

Description

Appearance

No physical description of Eetion survives in ancient sources. He is a king and warrior, likely depicted as mature and regal in later artistic representations, but no canonical image exists.

Symbols & Iconography

No specific symbols are associated with Eetion in ancient texts. In art, he may appear in scenes of the sack of Thebe, but no identifiable iconography is known.

Behaviour & Personality

Eetion is portrayed as a king and father, whose death at Achilles' hands is lamented by Andromache. He is not characterized in depth; he is a victim of war, treated with respect by Achilles, who refrains from despoiling his armor and gives him a proper funeral.

Domains & Habitats

King of Cilician Thebe, a city in the Troad region of Anatolia, near the river Eetion (also called Eetion's river). The city is sometimes identified with the later city of Thebe in Mysia.

Version Differences

In Homer, Eetion is the father of Andromache and seven sons. In later sources (e.g., Hyginus), he is also said to have a wife, Astynome (also known as Chryseis), who is captured by Achilles. Some traditions name his wife as Zeuxippe or otherwise leave her unnamed.

Stories & Myths

The Sack of Thebe

Source Homer, Iliad Book 6Culture GreekPeriod Heroic Age (Bronze Age), as depicted in epic poetry

Summary

During the Trojan War, Achilles leads a raid on the city of Cilician Thebe. He kills King Eetion and his seven sons. Andromache, Eetion's daughter and wife of Hector, later recounts the event to her husband, describing how Achilles, out of respect, did not strip Eetion's armor but burned him in his full panoply. The raid also results in the capture of Eetion's wife (unnamed in Homer) and the theft of valuable items including the horse Pedasus, a phorminx (lyre), and a discus later used in Patroclus' funeral games.

Consequences

Andromache loses her father and brothers, and her mother dies later, killed by Artemis. The captured goods become part of the Greek spoils. The raid reinforces Achilles' reputation as a fierce warrior.

Variants

Later sources (e.g., Dictys) provide additional details, such as the capture of Astynome (Chryseis) as Eetion's wife, but Homer does not name her.

Mass killing of Eetion and his sons

Source Iliad 6.421-424

Description

Achilles kills Eetion and all seven of his sons in a raid, a brutal act of war. Andromache's account emphasizes the loss and grief.

Scholarly Context

The raid is part of the heroic ethos of the epic, where sacking cities and killing warriors is common. The pathos in Andromache's speech highlights the human cost of war.

Capture and death of Eetion's wife

Source Iliad 6.425-428

Description

Eetion's wife is taken captive, and later dies by Artemis' arrow, likely as a result of the trauma or as a symbolic death.

Scholarly Context

The death of the mother by Artemis is a motif in Greek myth, often associated with sudden death of women. It underscores the devastation of the raid.
