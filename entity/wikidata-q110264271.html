Who It Is

In Mandaeism, death does not arrive unannounced or cruel. He comes as Ṣaureil — called "Death" by those who fear him, but "Truth" (Kushta) by those who know where he comes from — a radiant messenger from the World of Light who descends to tell Adam and his son Shitil that it is time to leave the earth and come home.

Ṣaureil is benevolent within Mandaean theology, despite his title 'angel of death.' He does not cause death violently or punish; he announces, escorts, and reveals. Behavior pattern: descends from the North (World of Light) at the command of the Great Life (Mana Rabba), delivers the message that the soul's time on earth is complete, and guides or summons the soul upward through the mataratta (watch-houses/toll stations) toward the Lightworld. Powers
Angel of Death — Announcement of Death

Ṣaureil's primary function is to announce the appointed time of death to righteous figures, specifically Adam (Adam Pagria/Adakas) and Shitil (Seth). He does not arbitrarily kill; he delivers the decree from the Great Life that the soul's earthly span is complete. The announcement takes the form of a call, voice, or letter from the Lightworld.

Limit: Cannot act before the appointed time; acts only at the command of the Great Life/Mana. Does not decide who dies; is a messenger, not a sovereign. In Mandaean theology, lifespan is determined by higher uthras.

Psychopomp — Escort of Souls through Mataratta

After announcement, Ṣaureil guides the soul upward through the mataratta (watch-houses or toll stations) guarded by Ruha and her sons, where the soul is interrogated and must present kushta and ritual purity (radiance, baptism). He protects the soul from detention by the Ruha's powers.

Limit: Soul must have received baptism (masbuta), alms, and masiqta rites; without these, even Ṣaureil cannot force passage — the soul may be detained. His protection is conditional on the soul's righteousness and ritual preparation.

Revealer of Kushta (Truth)

Ṣaureil embodies and reveals Kushta — cosmic Truth. To the ignorant he is 'Death,' but to the knowers (Mandaeans, gnostics) he is Kushta itself. This is an epistemological power: he reveals the true nature of death as return to the Lightworld, dispelling Ruha's illusion that death is annihilation.

Limit: Revelation is only understood by those with gnosis (manda). Worldly people perceive only 'Death.'

Lunar Epithet — Association with Sen (Moon)

Ṣaureil is listed as an epithet for Sen, the Moon, in Mandaean astronomical/astrological texts. In Mandaean cosmology, the Moon is a luminary created by Ptahil/Ruha but can serve as a vessel of light. The epithet suggests Ṣaureil's nocturnal or cyclical aspect, or that the Moon as a light in darkness parallels the angel who brings light at death.

Limit: Epithet does not mean Ṣaureil controls lunar phases or tides; Mandaeans view planets/moon as ambivalent, often negative, entities. The identification is honorific/esoteric, not a separate lunar deity power.

Qmamir Ziwa — Radiance/Treasury Aspect

As Ṣaureil Qmamir Ziwa, he is associated with Ziwa (Radiance), the substance of the Lightworld. The epithet implies he carries or guards radiant treasure/light that he bestows on ascending souls, or that he dwells in the treasury of radiance.

Limit: No detailed myth explains what Qmamir means or what treasury is guarded; epithet is not elaborated in narrative. Functionally identical to base Ṣaureil.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No canonical physical description of Ṣaureil survives in the published Ginza. Mandaean uthras (angels/beings of Light) are typically described as beings of radiance (ziwa), clothed in light, not flesh. Ṣaureil is not iconographically distinguished in Mandaean art, which avoids anthropomorphic depictions of uthras; Diwan Abatur and other scrolls depict uthras as abstract light-beings or priestly figures. Symbols — Primary symbols are textual, not visual: 1) The Letter (engirta) or Call from the World of Light that Ṣaureil delivers; 2) Kushta (Truth) as his true name — a central Mandaean theological concept personified; 3) Light/Radiance (Ziwa) in the epithet Qmamir Ziwa; 4) Lunar association — as epithet for Sen (Moon), he shares lunar symbolism (crescent, silver, nocturnal light) but Mandaean sources do not describe lunar iconography for Ṣaureil himself.

Domains — Domain: the threshold between the earthly world (Tibil) and the World of Light (Alma d-Nhura); the moment of death and the soul's ascent. He operates in both realms: originates in the Lightworld, manifests in the earthly world to call souls, and returns with them. Habitat in cosmology: the Lightworld, specifically associated with the northern heavens. As epithet for Sen (Moon), he is also linked to the lunar sphere — one of the seven planeta

Accounts I
Ṣaureil's Call to Adam — The First Announcement of DeathLeft Ginza Book 1, Chapter 1 (Ginza Smala 1.1) — Ginza Rabba

Late antiquity compilation (3rd-7th century CE); manuscripts 16th-19th century; earliest printed edition Petermann 1867

In the beginning of the Left Ginza, Adam Pagria (the earthly Adam, first man) has lived his allotted span in Tibil (the earth). From the World of Light, the Great Life sends Ṣaureil Qmamir Ziwa to announce his departure. Ṣaureil descends and calls to Adam: his time is complete, he must leave his body, his family, and the world made by Ptahil and Ruha, and ascend to his true home in the Lightworld. Adam is at first sorrowful, lamenting his children and the world, but Ṣaureil reveals himself not as 'Death' to be feared but as Kushta — Truth — and shows Adam the radiance awaiting him. Adam's soul then begins its ascent, aided by ritual words. The story establishes the Mandaean view that death for the righteous is not punishment but rescue. Establishes theological template for all Mandaean death: death is a call from Light, not an evil. Provides liturgical model for masiqta prayers where priest speaks as Ṣaureil to the deceased. Explains why Mandaeans call death 'Kushta' esoterically. Adam becomes prototype of the ascending soul.

Ṣaureil's Call to Shitil (Seth) — The Second PatriarchLeft Ginza Book 1, Chapter 2 (Ginza Smala 1.2) — Ginza Rabba

Late antiquity; same manuscript tradition as above

After Adam, his son Shitil (Classical Mandaic Šitil, corresponding to Seth) has become the pure lineage-bearer — in Mandaeism, Shitil is a major prophet and ancestor of true Mandaeans, not merely Adam's third son. Ṣaureil again descends with the same message: Shitil's time is fulfilled, he must leave Tibil. Shitil, being more gnostic than Adam, recognizes Ṣaureil immediately as Kushta and rejoices, asking only that his offspring (the Mandaeans, the 'sons of Light') be protected after him. Ṣaureil assures him that his seed will be guarded by Hibil Ziwa and that baptism will remain for them. Shitil's soul ascends more swiftly than Adam's, without lamentation, demonstrating the ideal Mandaean death — joyful recognition of Truth. Elevates Shitil as model Mandaean — death without fear. Reinforces lineage theology: Mandaeans are 'sons of Shitil.' Liturgically paired with Adam chapter in funeral recitations; priests recite both to comfort bereaved that patriarchs also died and ascended.

Ṣaureil Qmamir Ziwa and the Soul's Question — 'Who is Death?'Left Ginza Book 1, Chapter 3-4 (Ginza Smala 1.3-1.4) — exegetical dialogue sections

Late antiquity

A didactic dialogue follows the patriarchal narratives: an unnamed soul (or the collective 'souls' / nishmata) asks, 'Who is this who is called Death? Is he from Light or from Ruha?' The text answers through a revealer voice: Ṣaureil Qmamir Ziwa is from the Lightworld, from the treasury of Radiance (Qmamir Ziwa), sent by the Great Life. He is called Death (Mauta) by the worlds and by Ruha's creatures who do not know, but his hidden (kasia) name is Kushta. The passage explains that Ruha and her Seven tried to claim death as their power, but true death — the separation of soul from body to return to Light — belongs to the Lightworld. This story is not a narrative with plot but a revelation discourse, typical of Ginza's question-and-answer form. Provides theological clarification that death is not a demonic power but a Lightworld function. Used in priestly instruction to explain why Mandaeans should not fear death. Source for the famous aphorism 'Death is called Kushta.'

Accounts II
Ṣaureil as Sen (Moon) — The Lunar Epithet TraditionGinza Rabba colophons and lexical traditions; Drower & Macuch, A Mandaic Dictionary (1963) p. 391; Rudolph, Mandaeism (1978)

Late antiquity to medieval scribal tradition; dictionary attestation 1963 from manuscript evidence

In Mandaean cosmological lists, the Moon (Sen, Classical Mandaic ࡎࡉࡍ, cognate with Sin) is given secret names, one of which is Ṣaureil. The tradition states: 'The Moon is called Ṣaureil.' This is not a story with plot but a naming tradition preserved in Ginza colophons, astrological fragments, and priestly esoteric lists. The association may reflect the Moon's role as nocturnal light that guides, paralleling Ṣaureil who guides souls in the darkness of death, or a syncretism where the light within the Moon (its uthric aspect) is identified with the angel of death who brings light at the darkest moment. Mandaean cosmology views the Moon as one of the Seven, created by Ptahil and Ruha, yet the Moon is also the least malevolent of the Seven and associated with weekly rituals. Creates lasting scholarly debate about whether Ṣaureil is a Lightworld uthra or a planetary archon. Influences Mandaean calendar and baptism timing (baptisms ideally not on certain lunar days). Shows Mandaean tendency to give multiple secret names to celestial beings.

The Masiqta — Ṣaureil in the Death Mass LiturgyQolasta (Canonical Prayerbook) — Masiqta prayers, especially CP 49-70; Ginza Rabba Left Ginza Book 2-3 (soul ascent)

Liturgical core late antiquity; manuscripts 16th-20th century; Drower edition 1959

While Ṣaureil is named explicitly only in Left Ginza Book 1, his function is enacted in every masiqta (death mass) performed by Mandaean priests for a deceased person. In the ritual, the priest recites the ascent of the soul through the mataratta, where the soul is questioned by Ruha's watchers. The soul answers with kushta, baptism, and alms, and a Lightworld messenger — understood by priests as Ṣaureil in his Kushta aspect — calls the soul upward: 'Come, come, soul, from darkness to light.' The liturgy reenacts Ṣaureil's call to Adam/Shitil for every deceased Mandaean. The ritual lasts multiple hours/days and includes 70+ prayers, bread (pihta), water, and olive wreath. The story here is ritual, not mythic: each death becomes a repetition of Adam's call. Makes Ṣaureil a living liturgical presence, not just a mythic figure. Explains why Mandaeans do not fear death: every funeral reenacts the patriarchal promise. Provides continuity between scripture and practice.

Ṣaureil and the Watch-Houses (Mataratta) — The Soul's InterrogationGinza Rabba Right Ginza Book 5.4, Left Ginza Book 3; Qolasta prayers 9, 15, 49

Late antiquity

A composite narrative across Ginza and Qolasta describes the soul's journey after Ṣaureil's call: the soul rises but is stopped at successive mataratta (watch-houses), each guarded by a son of Ruha or a planetary archon who demands tolls and interrogates the soul: 'Who are you? From where do you come? What have you brought?' The soul, instructed by Ṣaureil/Kushta and by its baptismal knowledge, answers: 'I am a son of the Great Life, from the Lightworld, I bring Truth and baptism.' Ṣaureil's prior announcement has prepared the soul with the correct responses (kushta, pihta, mambuha). Without his call and the knowledge it conveys, the soul would be trapped. The story emphasizes that Ṣaureil does not fight the guardians but provides the gnosis to pass them. Explains Mandaean emphasis on knowledge (manda) over moral works alone; salvation is via knowing the passwords. Reinforces need for priests to teach the soul's responses before death. Parallels Gnostic toll-collector motifs in Sethian and Valentinian texts.

Is Ṣaureil the sole angel of death or one of several psychopomps?Ṣaureil is the angel of death — the specific being who announces death to Adam and Shitil and by extension to all souls. Left Ginza Book 1 presents him as the death angel.Mandaeism has multiple psychopomps/messengers (Hibil Ziwa, Anush Uthra, Shitil, and the generic Shalia/Messenger) who also escort souls; Ṣaureil is one among them, not exclusive.

Ṣaureil is the same as the Jewish angel Sariel/Suriel or the Islamic Azrael. — Ṣaureil is a distinct Mandaean figure with separate etymology, texts, and theology. Sariel (Hebrew שריאל) is a Jewish archangel in Enochic literature; Azrael (ʿAzrāʾīl) is Islamic angel of death. Phonetic similarity is coincidental; Mandaean texts never equate them. Ṣaureil's defining trait — being called 'Death' in the world but 'Truth' (Kushta) in Light — has no parallel in Sariel/Azrael traditions.

Ṣaureil is a grim, terrifying Grim Reaper figure who kills people. — In Mandaean sources Ṣaureil does not kill, reap, or terrify. He announces and escorts; his true name is Kushta (Truth), and his message is liberation. Adam's initial sorrow is comforted, and Shitil rejoices. Mandaean art does not depict him as skeletal or dark; uthras are beings of radiance. The 'angel of death' title is exoteric (how the world sees him); esoterically he is a savior figure.

Mandaeans worship Ṣaureil or pray to him for death. — Mandaeans do not worship angels; worship is directed to the Great Life (Mana Rabba). Ṣaureil is a messenger/uthra, not a deity. Mandaeans do not pray for death; they pray for protection, baptism, and successful ascent when death comes naturally. Masiqta rituals invoke Lightworld beings to aid the deceased, but not as worship of death.

Ṣaureil as Moon epithet means Mandaeans worship the Moon. — Mandaeans do not worship the Moon; the Moon (Sen) is one of the Seven Planets created by Ptahil/Ruha and is generally viewed as ambivalent, not divine. Calling the Moon 'Ṣaureil' is an esoteric epithet for its inner light, not worship. Mandaean ritual is oriented to the North (Lightworld) and to living water (yardna), not to lunar worship. Baptisms are timed with astrological considerations but not lunar worship.

Ṣaureil appears in the Bible, Quran, or mainstream Western occultism. — Ṣaureil appears only in Mandaean scriptures (Ginza Rabba, related lists). He is absent from the Hebrew Bible, New Testament, Quran, and from standard Jewish, Christian, and Islamic angelologies. He does not appear in Western grimoires or New Age angel lists except via modern internet copying from Mandaean sources.
