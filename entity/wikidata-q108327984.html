Who It Is

He could not move, could not stand, could not even swat a fly from his burned face — only stare, and want. In a cave full of starving demons, the bandit Onigumo made an offer no human should make: take my body, take my soul, just give me legs to walk to her. The demons accepted. What crawled out was no longer Onigumo, and not yet Naraku.

Folklore Onigumo: Predatory ambush yōkai. Lurks in mountains/forests, spins vast webs across paths to trap humans, sometimes mimics human voice to lure victims. Not a trickster but a territorial predator; some tales give it intelligence enough to bargain.

Inuyasha's Onigumo: Attested as cruel, selfish, and covetous in life - a bandit leader who killed without remorse. As an invalid, his personality narrows to obsessive lust and envy: he desires Kikyo not from love but from possessive craving, and envies Inuyasha's strength and freedom. Name & Tongue

Etymology — Japanese compound oni (鬼, ogre/demon, from Chinese gui) + kumo (蜘蛛, spider), with rendaku voicing to gumo. Literally 'demon spider' or 'ogre spider'. In folklore the term denotes a monstrous, often mountain-dwelling spider yōkai; in Takahashi's usage it is a personal name that literalizes the charac

Pronunciation — Japanese: o-ni-gu-mo [oɲiɡɯmo] (four morae, pitch accent on -gu- in standard Tokyo dialect: o-ní-gu-mò); English dub: oh-nee-GOO-moh / oh-nee-goo-MOH

Aliases — 鬼蜘蛛, Oni-Gumo, Demon Spider, Onigumo the Bandit, Naraku's Human Heart, Hitokumo (folk variant name in some regional retellings)

Ownership — 1) Japanese folklore tradition (anonymous, communal, with regional variants documented from the medieval to Edo period); 2) As a named fictional character: created by Rumiko Takaha

Powers
No supernatural powers as human (attested)

As a human bandit and later as a burned invalid, Onigumo possesses no spiritual power, yōki, or combat ability. His only 'power' is extraordinary malice/willpower that acts as a beacon for low-level yōkai. Kaede explicitly states Kikyo's spiritual power kept demons at bay while she tended him, but his impure heart still attracted them.

Limit: Completely paralyzed from severe burns and spinal injury; cannot move, feed himself, or fight. Dependent on Kikyo for survival. Cannot use the Shikon Jewel himself.

Demonic Pact and Fusion (adaptation-specific transformation)

By offering his body and soul to a horde of hundreds of yōkai/demons that had gathered in his cave, Onigumo allows them to consume and fuse with him. The demons devour his flesh and merge, creating a new composite being: Naraku. This is not a power Onigumo wields but a ritual sacrifice that erases his human form. The process requires his willing consent and his intense desire for Kikyo/Inuyasha's strength.

Limit: Irreversible; Onigumo ceases to exist as independent human. His consciousness persists only as a suppressed 'human heart' within Naraku, which Naraku can expel or suppress. The fusion requires proximity of many weak demons and Onigumo's strong craving as catalyst.

Persistence of the Human Heart (within Naraku)

Even after becoming Naraku, Onigumo's human heart and memories survive as a distinct, beating heart hidden inside Naraku's body. This heart retains Onigumo's love/obsession for Kikyo, causing Naraku to act against his own demonic logic (e.g., hesitation to kill Kikyo, creation of detachments like Muso). When expelled at Mount Hakurei, the heart takes form as Muso (無双), a faceless bandit-like incarnation that regains Onigumo's memories.

Limit: Heart is vulnerable - if destroyed, Naraku would lose his humanity and become purely demonic but also lose the will that sustains him; Naraku therefore hides it. Muso is mindless and short-lived without Naraku's support. Onigumo's will can only surface when Naraku's demonic power wanes.

Folklore Onigumo: Ensnaring Silk and Illusion (yōkai version)

Traditional Onigumo spins steel-strong silk across mountain paths, invisible to travelers until entangled. Some variants attribute to it the ability to create illusory inns or beautiful women to lure victims (more typical of Jorōgumo, but conflated in some compendia), and to control smaller spiders. Its silk is said to be impervious to blades and to drain vitality.

Limit: Bound to its web/territory; vulnerable to fire and to purified tools (in folk belief, burning the web kills the spider). Cannot shapeshift into full human form in most attested Onigumo tales (unlike Jorōgumo).

Naraku's Inherited Spider Abilities (derived from Onigumo's demons)

After fusion, the composite Naraku exhibits spider-like powers that narratively stem from the horde that consumed Onigumo: generation of miasma (shōki), creation of detachments (bunshin), barrier webs, and insect familiars (Saimyosho). These are not Onigumo's own powers but are attributed to his name and nature in-universe - Naraku is called 'the spider hanyō'.

Limit: Naraku's powers evolve by absorbing other yōkai; spider traits diminish as he incorporates stronger demons (e.g., after absorbing Moryomaru). Purification by Kikyo/Kagome's arrows and Tessaiga's Kongōsōha can pierce barriers.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Folklore Onigumo: Described in Edo and modern compendia as an enormous spider, often the size of a tatami mat or larger, black or reddish-black with a demonic (oni-like) face on its abdomen or cephalothorax, long bristled legs, and eyes that glow in mountain darkness. Unlike the beautiful woman-form of Jorōgumo, Onigumo is rarely anthropomorphic; its horror is in scale and facial mimicry. Some regional variants describe it as a normal-sized spide

Symbols — Spider web / cave as womb-tomb: In Inuyasha, Onigumo's cave is both sickbed and cocoon, filled with hundreds of small yōkai/demons drawn to his dark miasma. The spider web motif recurs in Naraku's later castle (silk-like barriers, spider-like demon Saimyosho). Prayer beads, bandages, and the Shikon Jewel shard (later) are associated objects. In folklore, Onigumo is associated with mountain passes, abandoned huts, and jizō statues wrappe

Domains — Folklore: Deep mountains (shinzan), old forests, mountain passes in Tōhoku and Chūbu regions, especially near Tōno, Nagano, and Gifu. Liminal spaces where human travel is vulnerable. Fictional: Sengoku-period Musashi/Sagami borderlands (fictionalized Warring States Japan). Specifically: a small cave on a hillside near the village where Kikyo and Kaede live (the cave where Kikyo sheltered him), later the same cave becomes Naraku's birthplace

Accounts I
The Bandit Onigumo and the Attempt on KikyoTakahashi, Rumiko. Inuyasha manga Ch. 47-48, 113; Anime Ep. 15, 20

Fictional Sengoku period; published 1997-1999

Onigumo is introduced via Kaede's flashback as a ruthless bandit leader who, hearing of the Shikon Jewel's power, leads his gang to attack Kikyo's village to steal it and abduct Kikyo. He is severely burned and crippled when he falls from a cliff during the raid (manga: after being repelled by Kikyo's spiritual power; anime: after Inuyasha's intervention is implied). Found near death by Kikyo, who, despite knowing his evil nature, takes him to a cave and nurses him out of pity. For months he lies bandaged and immobile, tended daily by Kikyo, while his obsession with her grows into possessive love and envy of Inuyasha, Kikyo's half-demon lover. Establishes Onigumo's cruelty and Kikyo's compassion, and the love triangle that motivates his later sacrifice. His paralysis sets the condition for the demonic pact - he would give anything to walk again and possess Kikyo.

The Cave Pact: Hundreds of Demons Devour OnigumoTakahashi, Rumiko. Inuyasha manga Ch. 114-115; Anime Ep. 20

Fictional Sengoku period; published 1999

Alone in the cave while Kikyo is away, Onigumo is surrounded by yōkai attracted to his dark soul and to the lingering scent of Kikyo's spiritual power. He hears/ senses them and makes a conscious bargain: 'Give me a body that can move freely. Give me Kikyo.' The demons swarm and consume him - a graphic sequence of spiders, centipedes, and amorphous yōkai crawling into his bandages and mouth. His body dissolves. Days later, when Kikyo returns, the cave is empty except for shed bandages and miasma. A new man stands at the cave mouth: Naraku, wearing Onigumo's tattered bandages as a baboon skin (hihō), with long black hair and spider-like markings on his back. Birth of the series' main antagonist. Naraku inherits Onigumo's memories and desire for Kikyo but is dominated by demonic nature. This act explains Naraku's spider motif, his baboon pelt, and his later ability to expel Onigumo's heart. The Shikon Jewel is not yet involved - Naraku will later manipulate Inuyasha and Kikyo to obtain it.

Naraku's Trick: The Deaths of Kikyo and Inuyasha's SealingTakahashi, Rumiko. Inuyasha manga Ch. 48-50, 115-118; Anime Ep. 1, 20

Fictional Sengoku period; published 1997, 1999

Newly born Naraku, using his shapeshifting, impersonates Inuyasha to mortally wound Kikyo and impersonates Kikyo to trick Inuyasha into stealing the Shikon Jewel. Believing each other betrayed, Kikyo seals Inuyasha to the Sacred Tree with a sacred arrow and dies from her wounds, ordering the Jewel burned with her body. Naraku obtains the Jewel from Kikyo's corpse (later revealed). Onigumo's will is complicit - Naraku's cruelty toward Kikyo is amplified by Onigumo's possessive desire to see her belong to no one else if not him. Creates the central tragedy that drives the entire series: Inuyasha's 50-year seal, Kikyo's death and later resurrection, and Kagome's arrival. Reveals Naraku's cunning and establishes Onigumo's human jealousy as the emotional core of Naraku's evil.

Accounts II
Muso - The Heart That Walked AgainTakahashi, Rumiko. Inuyasha manga Vol. 18 Ch. 173-175; Anime Ep. 61-62 'Muso's True Identity'

Fictional Sengoku period; published 2001

Naraku, troubled by Onigumo's lingering human emotions (especially hesitation to kill Kikyo), expels his human heart from his body at the border of a temple. The heart, containing Onigumo's memories, regenerates into a faceless, bandaged man who calls himself Muso (Muso = 'unparalleled', but also pun on musō 'dream'). Muso wanders, instinctively seeking Kikyo, killing monks who get in his way. When he regains his face - Onigumo's face - he remembers: 'I am Onigumo... I loved Kikyo...' He confronts Kikyo, who pities but rejects him, and is ultimately reabsorbed by Naraku, who declares human feeling useless. Proves Onigumo's consciousness survived inside Naraku for decades. Demonstrates that Naraku's weakness is Onigumo's love, a plot point that recurs at Mount Hakurei. Muso's reabsorption shows Naraku's rejection of humanity, but also that he cannot fully erase Onigumo.

Mount Hakurei: The Expulsion and Purification of Onigumo's HeartTakahashi, Rumiko. Inuyasha manga Vol. 36 Ch. 360-365; Anime Ep. 124-128

Fictional Sengoku period; published 2004

At the sacred Mount Hakurei, Naraku attempts to purge his human heart completely to become a full yōkai and remove his vulnerability to Kikyo. He expels Onigumo's heart (still beating, spider-marked) and leaves it in the purified barrier, where Kikyo finds it. Kikyo, understanding that Onigumo's love is Naraku's only tether to humanity, chooses not to destroy it immediately, telling Naraku: 'You cannot become a complete demon while Onigumo loves me.' The heart is later retrieved by Naraku's detachment Hakudoshi and reabsorbed when Naraku realizes he needs Onigumo's cunning. Kikyo's purification of the mountain ultimately fails to kill Naraku because she spares the heart. Clarifies the metaphysics: Naraku is not a single entity but a collective whose human core is both weakness and anchor. Kikyo's mercy toward Onigumo foreshadows her own tragic death - she cannot hate the human part of her killer. This arc is cited by scholars as Takahashi's Buddhist theme of attachment (shūjaku) preventing enlightenment/transformation.

The Final Battle Inside Naraku - Onigumo's Last GraspTakahashi, Rumiko. Inuyasha manga Vol. 56 Ch. 544-549; Anime The Final Act Ep. 25-26

Fictional Sengoku period; published 2008; aired 2010

During the final battle, Kagome is trapped inside Naraku's body. Inside, she finds a cavern where Onigumo's skeletal, spider-like will manifests as a vast web with Onigumo's burned face at its center, still repeating 'Kikyo...'. Naraku's demonic mass tries to crush Kagome, but Onigumo's lingering desire for Kikyo creates a fissure - a spider's thread of light that Kagome's purifying arrow follows. Kikyo's final act (her light transferred to Kagome) purifies Onigumo's web, allowing Kagome to destroy the Shikon Jewel's will and Naraku from within. Onigumo's last words are not of conquest but of Kikyo's name, as both he and Naraku dissolve into miasma. Onigumo's human attachment, long treated as Naraku's weakness, becomes the literal crack that allows his defeat. Thematically closes the loop: the bandit who wanted to possess Kikyo is destroyed by the purified love he could never understand. After Naraku's death, no trace of Onigumo remains.

Folklore: The Onigumo of the Mountain Pass (Tōno / Shinano variants)Yanagita Kunio / Sasaki Kizen. Tōno Monogatari Shūi; Mizuki Shigeru. Nihon Yōkai Daizen; Foster 2015 retelling

Oral tradition documented Meiji to Shōwa; Edo antecedents in yōkai emaki

Travelers on a mountain pass at dusk find a vast web stretched between cedars, with a spider the size of a straw hat at its center, its back bearing a pattern like an oni's face. In one Tōno variant, a woodcutter cuts the web and is pursued by the spider, which can run faster than a horse; he escapes by hiding behind a Jizō statue, and the spider, unable to pass the sacred stone, dissolves into mist. In a Shinano variant, the Onigumo lures a young woman by mimicking a baby's cry; when she approaches, it wraps her in silk and drains her. Villagers burn the web and the spider's lair, finding human bones. The tale functions as a caution against traveling at dusk and against greed (the spider punishes those who take more wood than needed). Etiological explanation for abandoned mountain huts wrapped in silk and for place names like Kumodani (Spider Valley). Reinforces Buddhist moral that mountain boundaries are sacred and that oni-spiders punish transgression. No connection to Inuyasha's plot beyond name and spider motif, but Takahashi likely drew on this archetype intentionally.

Was Onigumo's injury caused by Kikyo, Inuyasha, or a fall?Manga (Ch. 47) implies Onigumo was burned and crippled when his bandit gang's attack on Kikyo's village was repelled by Kikyo's spiritual power and he fell from a cliff while fleeing.Anime (Ep. 20) adds a shot of Inuyasha's silhouette during the raid, leading some viewers to believe Inuyasha injured him; fan translations sometimes state 'Inuyasha burned him.'
Is Onigumo a yōkai species or a human name?Folklorists (Mizuki, Foster) treat Onigumo as a distinct yōkai species - a giant mountain spider with an oni face.Inuyasha canon treats Onigumo as a human personal name that coincidentally matches the yōkai, with spider symbolism applied retroactively to Naraku.
Did Onigumo love Kikyo or merely covet her?Popular fan reading: Onigumo's love was genuine and tragic, and his transformation was driven by heartbreak.Textual/scholarly reading: Onigumo's feeling is yokubō (craving/possession), not ai (love); Takahashi frames it as selfish attachment that attracts impurity.

Onigumo is Naraku's father. — Onigumo is not Naraku's father but Naraku's human component. Naraku is Onigumo fused with a horde of yōkai; Onigumo's consciousness persists as Naraku's human heart. The relationship is metamorphosis/fusion, not parentage. Takahashi never uses the term 'father' (chichi).

Onigumo was a spider demon who fell in love with Kikyo. — Human Onigumo had no demonic powers or spider form before his pact. He was a human bandit who became a spider-associated hanyō only after being consumed. The folklore Onigumo is a spider yōkai, but the Inuyasha character is human until the cave scene. His spider marking appears only after fusion as Naraku.

Onigumo was turned into Naraku against his will by demons. — The manga and anime both stress consent: Onigumo willingly offered his body and soul, saying 'Take my flesh, give me Kikyo.' The demons did not possess him involuntarily; his desire was the catalyst. Kaede states: 'He called the demons to him with the darkness in his heart.'

Onigumo and Jorōgumo are the same yōkai. — They are distinct spider yōkai in Japanese folklore taxonomy. Jorōgumo is a beautiful woman who transforms into a spider to ensnare men; Onigumo is typically a giant, non-anthropomorphic spider with an oni-like face that traps travelers with webs. Takahashi's Naraku borrows from both (Jorōgumo's seduction via Kagura-like detachments, Onigumo's brute web), but they are not interchangeable in folklore scholarship.
