Who It Is

On the Bresle river where Normandy meets Picardy, a small castle town gave its name to one of Europe's most contested titles — a countship that became a duchy, then split into two parallel peerages on opposite sides of the Channel, claimed simultaneously by kings of France and England for three centuries. To be Count or Duke of Aumale was to hold a borderland, and borderlands are rarely quiet.

As a feudal dignity, 'behavior' is institutional: the count/duke owed counsel, justice and military service. The Anglo-Norman counts were Marcher-like frontier lords — Stephen conspired against William Rufus, William le Gros acted as quasi-prince in Yorkshire during the Anarchy, the Forz were notoriously litigious and rebellious under Henry III. The French counts after 1196 were royal vassals, often close to the crown (Renaud de Dammartin, then royal princes of Castile and Harcourt, then the ultra-Catholic Guise). The dukes of the 16th century were war leaders in the Italian Wars

Powers
Comital high justice and domainal revenue

Right to hold court, levy tolls, forest and market dues within Aumale, and to collect rents from Holderness and Domesday manors. The count controlled castle-guard, mills and fisheries on the Bresle.

Limit: Subject to Duke of Normandy/King of England before 1204, then to King of France; English revenues required separate administration and were vulnerable to confiscation during Anglo-French wars.

Military levy and castle right (droit de forteresse)

Obligation and right to maintain the castle of Aumale, summon knights (Holderness provided c. 30+ knights' fees), and serve in the ducal/royal host. William le Gros led Yorkshire levies at the Battle of the Standard (1138).

Limit: Fortress could be and was seized by the superior lord (Philip II in 1196); English holders after 1196 held no fortress.

Peerage privilege (pairie)

After 1547 as duché-pairie: hereditary peer of France with seat in Parlement of Paris, precedence above counts and marquises, right to be tried by peers, and ceremonial honors (coronet, mantle, entry).

Limit: Peerage could be extinguished for treason or lack of heirs and revert to Crown (as happened 1595-1618, 1652-1701); not transferable without royal letters.

English peerage creation and precedence

As Earl/Duke of Albemarle/Aumale in England: writ of summons to Parliament, precedence according to creation date, and (for dukes) highest rank below the royal family. Thomas of Woodstock and Edward of Norwich held dukedoms that placed them immediately after the royal dukes.

Limit: Titular only after 1196 — no land or jurisdiction in Aumale; could be forfeited/deprived (Woodstock murdered 1397, Edward deprived 1399).

Patronage, collection and legitimist capital (modern)

Henri d'Orléans used the title's prestige to build the Musée Condé at Chantilly — one of France's greatest art collections and libraries — and to assert Orléanist status during exile. Modern courtesy use confers social precedence within the House of Orléans.

Limit: No legal privilege since abolition of peerage in 1848/1870; courtesy title only, recognized by house law not French Republic.

Accounts I
The Dower That Made a Countess: Adelaide of Normandy and the Creation of the CountshipOrderic Vitalis, Historia Ecclesiastica; Domesday Book; Thompson on Adelaide; Chisholm 1911

1053-1087

Adelaide (Adélaïde de Normandie), half-sister of William the Conqueror and daughter of Duke Robert I, brings Aumale as dower. Thompson argues the fief was given to Adelaide by her first husband Enguerrand II of Ponthieu and retained by her through two subsequent marriages to Lambert of Boulogne (d.1054) and Odo of Troyes/Champagne (brother-in-law of William). Chisholm's older account says the archbishop of Rouen granted it to Odo directly. In either version, by 1069 Odo is styled Count jure uxoris, and Domesday (1086) records Adelaide herself as Countess of Aumale with English manors. Before 1090 their son Stephen succeeds. The episode establishes the pattern: Aumale transmitted through heiresses and claimed jure uxoris by husbands. Creation of a cross-Channel honour linking Norman Aumale to Yorkshire (Holderness granted to Odo in 1087). Establishes the comital title and the jure uxoris practice that will dominate its history (Hawise, Joan, Blanche, Marie).

The Rebel Count: Stephen of Aumale and the Conspiracy of 1095Orderic Vitalis; Anglo-Saxon Chronicle; Frank Barlow, William Rufus

1087-1127 (crisis 1095)

Stephen (Étienne d'Aumale, before 1070-1127), son of Odo and Adelaide, inherits both Aumale and Holderness in 1102 after his father's death. In 1095 he joins the conspiracy of Robert de Mowbray and others to depose William Rufus and place Stephen on the English throne as the Conqueror's nephew. The plot fails; William Rufus besieges but pardons Stephen, who retains his lands. Stephen later supports Henry I, founding Vota Abbey (Voto) and remaining a major northern magnate. His son William le Gros will make the family infamous during the Anarchy. Demonstrates the political weight of the Aumale honour — a plausible royal candidate. Stephen's survival preserves the Anglo-Norman line for another generation.

William le Gros, the Battle of the Standard and the AnarchyRichard of Hexham; Aelred of Rievaulx, Relatio de Standardo; Dalton on Yorkshire barons

1127-1179 (climax 1138)

William le Gros (Guillaume le Gros, c.1110-1179), Count of Aumale and Lord of Holderness and Earl of York (created by Stephen of England), is the most powerful man in Yorkshire during King Stephen's reign. On 22 August 1138 he is one of the English commanders at the Battle of the Standard (Northallerton) against David I of Scotland, fighting under the consecrated standards of the northern saints. Later he builds Scarborough Castle in defiance of Henry II, who forces its surrender in 1154. He founds Meaux Abbey (Holderness) and Thornton Abbey. Dying in 1179, he leaves his heiress Hawise. Peak of territorial power for the Anglo-Norman counts; Scarborough and Meaux remain lasting monuments. His heiress's marriages will fragment control and invite royal intervention.

The Heiress and Her Husbands: Hawise and the Forz InheritanceTurner 1971; Patent Rolls; Complete Peerage vol.1

1179-1274

Hawise (Havoise, d.1214), daughter of William le Gros, inherits Aumale and Holderness in 1179. She is married successively to William de Mandeville, 3rd Earl of Essex (made Count jure uxoris 1180-1189), and to William de Forz (de Fors, d.1195) of Oleron, who becomes Count jure uxoris 1189-1194. After Philip II captures Aumale in 1196, Hawise's English line continues as Earls of Albemarle without the Norman land: her son William de Forz II (d.1242), grandson William de Forz III (d.1260), great-grandson Thomas (d.1269) and great-granddaughter Aveline (d.1274). Aveline marries Edmund Crouchback, Earl of Lancaster, but dies childless. A claim by John de Eston (de Ashton, descendant of an illegitimate line) is bought off, and in 1278 the earldom and Holderness surrender to the Crown under Edward I. End of the original Anglo-Norman comital line; Holderness becomes Crown land, later granted to others. The French and English titles are now permanently separate.

Accounts II
The Confiscation: Philip Augustus Takes the Castle (1196) and the Split TitleRigord, Gesta Philippi Augusti; William the Breton; Powicke, Loss of Normandy

1196-1204

During the war between Philip II Augustus and Richard I (then John), Philip storms the castle of Aumale in 1196. He grants the comté to Renaud de Dammartin, Count of Dammartin, who had defected to the Capetians. Renaud's daughter Mathilde (Maud) carries it to Philip Hurepel (son of Philip II) and then to Afonso of Portugal (later Afonso III), then through Simon de Dammartin to Joan of Dammartin, Queen of Castile (married to Ferdinand III), and thus to the House of Castile (Ferdinand II, John I, John II, Blanche). After 1204 and the French conquest of Normandy, the kings of England no longer control Aumale but continue to style Hawise and her Forz descendants as Earl of Albemarle. From this moment there are two Aumales: a territorial French county and a titular English earldom. Creates the enduring Franco-English double title. The French line passes through Dammartin → Castile → Harcourt → Lorraine; the English line continues as Albemarle until 1274, then as new creations.

From Frontier County to Duché-Pairie: The Guise Creation (1547)Père Anselme, Histoire généalogique; Letters patent of Henri II; Stuart Carroll, Martyrs and Murderers (Guise)

1508-1595

By the early 16th century Aumale is held by Claude I of Lorraine (Claude de Lorraine, Duke of Guise). His son François (Francis, 1519-1563), styled Count of Aumale by courtesy, is one of Henri II's great captains. In 1547 Henri II erects Aumale into a duché-pairie for François. When François succeeds his father as Duke of Guise in 1550, he cedes Aumale to his younger brother Claude II (1526-1573), who becomes Duke of Aumale in his own right — founder of the House of Guise-Aumale. The line continues through Charles (1573-1595), who joins the Catholic League against Henri IV, is condemned for lèse-majesté, and flees to Brussels. The duchy is sequestered, then restored to his daughter Anne (1618-1638) and her Savoy-Nemours husbands, before reverting to the Crown. Aumale becomes one of the premier peerages of France, intimately tied to the ultra-Catholic Guise faction in the Wars of Religion. The 1595 forfeiture shows the Crown's power over even peerages.

English Shadows: Dukes of Aumale without Aumale (1385-1422)Patent Rolls; Complete Peerage; Shakespeare, Richard II; Given-Wilson, Henry IV

1385-1422

Long after losing the land, English kings recycle the Norman name as high peerage. Thomas of Woodstock, youngest son of Edward III, is created Duke of Aumale (also Duke of Gloucester) on 3 September 1385 — a title he never uses and which is forfeit when he is murdered at Calais in 1397 awaiting trial for treason. Edward of Norwich (Edward of York, 1373-1415), eldest son of Edmund of Langley, is created Duke of Aumale (also Earl of Rutland, later Duke of York) shortly after Woodstock's death; Shakespeare immortalizes him as the 'Duke of Aumerle' who wavers between Richard II and Henry IV, is deprived of the dukedom by Henry IV in 1399, and dies at Agincourt. Later, Thomas of Lancaster, Duke of Clarence (1387-1421), bears Earl of Aumale (1412) and Richard de Beauchamp, Earl of Warwick (1382-1439), is created Count of Aumale for life (1422) for service in France. After the Hundred Years' War, the spelling Albemarle prevails for new creations (see Duke/Earl of Albemarle). The Collector Prince: Henri d'Orléans, Duke of Aumale (1822-1897)Henri d'Orléans, Histoire des princes de Condé; Musée Condé archives; Gabriel de Broglie, L'Orléanisme

1822-1897 (title granted 1822)

Henri d'Orléans (1822-1897), fifth son of Louis-Philippe I, King of the French, is granted the revived title Duke of Aumale at birth. Soldier in the conquest of Algeria (where he famously captures the smala of Abd-el-Kader in 1843), he goes into exile in England after 1848, becoming a historian, bibliophile and art collector. In 1884 he bequeaths his restored Château de Chantilly and its collections to the Institut de France, creating the Musée Condé — one of France's great museums, housing the Très Riches Heures du Duc de Berry. He is elected to the Académie française. He dies childless in 1897; the title lapses until revived as courtesy in 2019 for Prince Foulques, Duke of Aumale and Count of Eu, grandson of Henri, Count of Paris. Transforms a feudal title into a cultural legacy: Chantilly as public museum. Final holder before modern courtesy revival; closes the long arc from frontier countship to princely patronage.

Aumale and Albemarle are two different places. — They are the same place. Albemarle is the Anglo-Latin scribal form (Albemarla) of French Aumale (Alba Marla). The spelling divergence after 1196 reflects two chanceries, not two geographies. The English peerage title Earl/Duke of Albemarle is the English rendering of Count/Duke of Aumale.

The Duke of Aumale always ruled the town of Aumale in Normandy. — After 1196 no English Earl/Duke of Aumale/Albemarle held the town; after 1595-1618 and 1652-1701 even French dukes were sometimes dispossessed and the duchy reverted to the Crown. Many English creations were purely titular peerages with no French land.

Shakespeare's Duke of Aumerle is fictional. — He is historical: Edward of Norwich, 2nd Duke of York, created Duke of Aumale in 1397, deprived in 1399, killed at Agincourt in 1415. Shakespeare dramatizes his real vacillation in 1399 but compresses events.

The County of Aumale was always a duchy. — It was a lordship before c.1069, a county from c.1069 to 1547 (nearly 500 years), and only a duché-pairie from 1547 onward. The ducal title is early modern, not medieval.
