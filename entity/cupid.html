Who It Is

Forget the cherubic baby on your Valentine's Day card. The real Cupid was a divine terrorist, a spoiled, winged brat armed with arrows that could turn a wise man into a fool, a virgin into a tree, and a peaceful world into a battlefield. He was the original agent of chaos, and his weapon of choice was love itself.

Cupid is a trickster, a divine agent provocateur. He is impulsive, willful, and utterly indifferent to the consequences of his actions. He delights in making the mighty fall in love with the unworthy, and the wise act foolishly. He is a mama's boy, often acting on Venus's orders but just as often acting on his own whim. His behavior is that of a spoiled, immortal child with a god's power and no sense of responsibility. He is not malicious in a cruel sense, but his actions are reckless and can cause immense suffering.

Powers
Arrow of Desire (Golden)

When struck by a golden arrow, the victim is filled with an overwhelming, irresistible passion for the next person they see. This power is absolute and can affect gods and mortals alike.

Limit: The desire is often one-sided and can lead to tragedy. It does not guarantee reciprocation.

Arrow of Aversion (Lead)

When struck by a lead arrow, the victim feels an intense repulsion and hatred for the next person they see.

Limit: This power is used to create conflict and imbalance in love.

Flight

Cupid can fly using his wings, allowing him to appear anywhere and strike any target with his arrows.

Limit: His flight is often depicted as erratic and playful, not a serious mode of travel.

Invisibility/Disguise

In the tale of Cupid and Psyche, Cupid is able to make himself invisible to Psyche, and he appears to her only as a disembodied voice in the dark. He can also adopt a disguise, as he does when he visits Psyche's sisters.

Limit: His invisibility is broken if Psyche looks upon him with a lamp, showing he is bound by the conditions of his own secret.

Cosmic Primordial Force (as Eros)

In his earlier Greek incarnation as Eros, he is one of the primordial forces of creation, born from Chaos, who brings order and harmony to the cosmos by uniting opposites (e.g., Earth and Sky).

Limit: This cosmic role is largely absent from his Roman persona as Cupid.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The most iconic and enduring image of Cupid is that of a chubby, winged infant or young boy, often depicted with a bow and a quiver of arrows. This is the cherubic 'putto' of Renaissance and Baroque art. However, this is a later development. Earlier Roman depictions, and the Greek Eros before him, often showed a beautiful, athletic adolescent or young man, also winged. Symbols — The bow and arrow are his primary symbols. His arrows come in two types: golden-tipped ones that cause uncontrollable desire, and lead-tipped ones that cause aversion or repulsion. Other symbols include wings (for the fleeting and unpredictable nature of love), a torch or flame (for passion), a blindfold (for love's irrationality), and roses (a flower sacred to Venus).

Domains — Love, desire, attraction, passion, and the physical and emotional forces that draw beings together. In his earlier, more cosmic form (as Eros), he is a primordial force of creation and cosmic harmony. As Cupid, his domain shrinks to the personal, romantic, and often chaotic realm of human (and divine) hearts.

Accounts I
The Revenge on Apollo (Apollo and Daphne)Ovid, 'Metamorphoses'

1st century CE

After Apollo mocked Cupid's archery skills, the boy god, enraged, shot Apollo with a golden arrow of desire and the nymph Daphne with a lead arrow of aversion. Apollo immediately fell in love with Daphne, who was filled with horror and fled from him. Apollo chased her, and just as he was about to catch her, Daphne's father, the river god Peneus, transformed her into a laurel tree. Apollo, still in love, made the laurel his sacred tree. This story is a prime example of Cupid's petty, vengeful nature and his power to cause immense suffering with a flick of his wrist. Daphne is lost to Apollo forever, becoming a tree. Apollo is left with eternal, unrequited love. The laurel becomes a symbol of poetic and artistic triumph.

Accounts II
The Tale of Cupid and PsycheApuleius, 'The Golden Ass'

2nd century CE

Psyche, a mortal princess of unsurpassed beauty, incurs the wrath of Venus, who sends Cupid to make her fall in love with a monster. Instead, Cupid is so struck by her beauty that he accidentally pricks himself with his own arrow and falls deeply in love with her. He arranges for her to be taken to a magical palace where he visits her every night in darkness, forbidding her to look upon his face. Psyche's jealous sisters convince her to light a lamp and see her mysterious husband. When she does, she sees the beautiful god, but he, betrayed, flees. Psyche must then complete a series of impossible tasks set by a vengeful Venus to win him back. After many trials, she succeeds, and is made immortal, marrying Cupid. Their daughter is named Voluptas (Pleasure). Psyche becomes a goddess. The story is a profound allegory of the soul's journey through trials to achieve divine love and immortality.

The Love Affairs of the GodsVarious (Ovid, Virgil)

1st century BCE - 1st century CE

Cupid is frequently invoked as the agent who causes the often embarrassing and tragic love affairs of the gods and heroes. In Virgil's 'Eclogues', he is called upon to make a woman fall in love. In Ovid's 'Metamorphoses', he is the force behind the incestuous love of Myrrha for her father, and the tragic love of Pyramus and Thisbe. He is the divine catalyst for chaos, making the world a more complicated and dangerous place. The consequences are always dramatic, often leading to transformation, death, or heroic quests.

Cupid is a cute, harmless baby who brings people together. — The ancient Cupid was a dangerous, unpredictable god whose arrows caused uncontrollable passion, leading to rape, suicide, and incest. He was a force of chaos, not a sweet matchmaker. The 'cute baby' image is a Renaissance and modern invention.

Cupid is the same as the Greek Eros. — While the Romans equated him with Eros, the two figures have different origins. Eros was a primordial force of cosmic creation, while Cupid was a more personal, mischievous god. The Roman Cupid is a simplification and an anthropomorphization of the older, more abstract Greek concept.

Cupid is always successful in his matchmaking. — Cupid's arrows cause desire, not happy relationships. Many of his matches are one-sided, tragic, or lead to disaster. The story of Apollo and Daphne is a clear example of a failed 'match' that ends in transformation and loss.

Cupid is a minor, unimportant deity. — In his role as the agent of Venus, Cupid has immense power. He can make gods and mortals alike act against their will, causing wars (the Trojan War was started over love), dynastic upheavals, and personal tragedies. His influence is felt in every love story in Roman mythology.
