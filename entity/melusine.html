Mélusine

The Serpent Fairy, Lady of Lusignan

also known asMelusineMelusinaMère LusineLa Fée MélusineSerpent FairyLady of Lusignan
TypeFairy / Shapeshifter
CultureFrench (Poitevin / Medieval)
Who It Is
Form

Mélusine is typically depicted as a beautiful woman from the waist up, with fair skin, long flowing hair, and often a serene or enigmatic expression. From the waist down, she possesses the tail of a serpent or fish, which she keeps hidden from her mortal husband. In some illustrations, she is shown with two tails, reminiscent of the mermaid heraldic figure, and occasionally with large wings, suggesting a hybrid of fairy and dragon. Her appearance is not monstrous but alluring, emphasizing her dual nature as both human and supernatural. In the medieval manuscripts, she is often portrayed in elegant courtly attire, with her tail discreetly curled beneath her gown, only revealed in scenes where her secret is discovered.

Powers

Shapeshifting Mélusine can transform her lower body from human legs to a serpent's tail, typically on Saturdays. This transformation is a central element of her legend and is tied to her curse. She can also assume a fully human form at other times, allowing her to interact with mortals without revealing her supernatural nature.

Supernatural Architecture Mélusine is said to be able to construct castles, fortresses, and other structures overnight, using her magical powers. This ability is demonstrated in the building of the Castle of Lusignan, which she erects in a single night, impressing her husband and the local populace.

Prophecy and Omens Mélusine possesses the ability to foresee future events, particularly those concerning her descendants. She prophesies the rise and fall of the Lusignan dynasty, and her appearances as a specter are considered omens of death or misfortune for the family.

Control over Water As a water spirit, Mélusine has dominion over fresh water sources, including springs, rivers, and wells. She can calm or stir waters, and her presence is often associated with the health and fertility of the surrounding land.

Cursing and Blessing Mélusine can bestow blessings of prosperity and fertility upon her husband and children, but she can also curse those who betray her. Her departure from Lusignan is accompanied by a curse that the castle will fall, and she is said to return as a wailing spirit to herald the death of her descendants.

Accounts

Raymondin, a young nobleman, accidentally kills his uncle in a hunting accident and flees in despair. He encounters Mélusine at the Fountain of Thirst in the forest of Colombiers. She offers him comfort and proposes marriage, but with a condition: he must never see her on Saturdays, when she will be in a transformed state. Raymondin agrees, and they marry. Mélusine uses her powers to build the Castle of Lusignan and to expand Raymondin's lands, bringing him wealth and power. They have many sons, each with a distinctive birthmark or deformity, such as a lion's claw or a single eye, but they grow to be strong and valiant knights.

After years of marriage, Raymondin's brother, the Count of Poitou, taunts him about Mélusine's mysterious absence on Saturdays, suggesting she is unfaithful. Raymondin, driven by jealousy and curiosity, peeks through a keyhole in the door of her chamber. He sees Mélusine bathing in a tub, her lower body transformed into a serpent's tail. He is horrified but does not reveal his discovery. Later, during an argument, he calls her a 'serpent' in front of their court, breaking his vow. Mélusine, heartbroken, transforms into a winged serpent and flies out the window, crying a lament. She circles the castle three times before disappearing, leaving behind a prophecy that the Lusignan line will fall and that she will return as a specter to warn of their deaths.

Modern Interpretations

Literature Le Roman de Mélusine Jean d'Arras c. 1392 Primary source This prose romance is the earliest and most detailed account of the Mélusine legend, commissioned by the Duke of Berry. It established the canonical narrative and has been the basis for most later adaptations. Literature La Chronique de Melusine Coudrette c. 1401 Alternate version A verse chronicle that retells the story with a more courtly and genealogical focus, emphasizing the Lusignan family's prestige. It is often compared to Jean d'Arras's version for its differences in tone and detail. Heraldry Coat of arms of the House of Lusignan Unknown 13th century Symbolic representation The Lusignan family adopted Mélusine as their heraldic emblem, depicting her as a two-tailed mermaid. This symbol was later used by the city of Luxembourg, cementing her association with the region. Opera Mélusine Aribert Reimann 1971 Adaptation A German-language opera based on the legend, which focuses on the tragic aspects of the story. It has been performed in major opera houses and is considered a significant modern interpretation. Film Mélusine Various Various Adaptation Several short films and animations have been made about Mélusine, often emphasizing her fairy nature and the romance with Raymondin. These are typically low-budget productions but contribute to her ongoing cultural presence. Scholarly Debates
Origin of Mélusine
Mélusine is a fairy of Celtic origin, possibly derived from the Irish goddess of sovereignty, who grants kingship to mortal rulers. ⇌ Mélusine is a literary creation of the 14th century, invented by Jean d'Arras to legitimize the Lusignan dynasty, with no direct pagan precursor.
Nature of Mélusine's curse
Mélusine's Saturday transformation is a punishment for a crime she committed against her mother, who cursed her to become a serpent every week. ⇌ The transformation is a voluntary act, part of her fairy nature, and not a curse, as she can control it and only hides it to protect her husband from the shock.
Mélusine's fate after departure
Mélusine becomes a wandering spirit, doomed to roam the earth until the Day of Judgment, appearing as a wailing ghost. ⇌ Mélusine returns to the fairy realm and is free to live among her kind, only occasionally visiting the mortal world to watch over her descendants.
What We Don't Know

There is limited scholarly consensus on the pre-medieval origins of Mélusine, with some scholars suggesting a possible connection to ancient Near Eastern water deities, but evidence is scarce. The exact relationship between the two main medieval texts (Jean d'Arras and Coudrette) and their sources is not fully understood, as both claim to be based on earlier chronicles that are now lost. Additionally, the spread of the Mélusine legend to Luxembourg and the Low Countries is not well documented, and the local variants have not been systematically compared. Further research is needed on the iconographic evolution of Mélusine in medieval art, particularly the transition from serpent to fish tail.
