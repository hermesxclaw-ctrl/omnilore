The Approach

Before the kilogram, before the SI system, there was the gram—a unit born from the French Revolution's dream of a rational world, defined by the weight of a cubic centimeter of melting ice. Its story spans from ancient Greek letters to the quantum-defined standards of today, a quiet revolution in how humanity measures matter.

Identity

Gram

Pronunciation /ɡræm/

Known As

grammeg

Etymology

From French 'gramme', from Late Latin 'gramma', from Greek 'γράμμα' (grámma), meaning 'letter' or 'a small weight'. In Late Antiquity, it denoted one twenty-fourth of an ounce (two oboli).

Cultural Ownership

International System of Units (SI), globally adopted; originally defined in France.

Disputed Identifications

None known; the unit is distinct from the mythical sword Gram.

Appearance

Official SI symbol: 'g'. No other symbols recognized. Abbreviations like 'gr', 'gm', 'Gm' are disallowed.

Used worldwide in commerce, science, and cooking.

Version Differences

Original 1795 definition used melting ice temperature; later changed to maximum density of water (~4 °C); final SI definition as 1/1000 of kilogram.

From ancient Greek to modern SI

Source Carmen de ponderibus et mensuris; historical linguisticsCulture Greek, Roman, EuropeanPeriod 4th century AD to present

Summary

The term 'gramma' evolved from meaning 'letter' to a small weight (1/24 ounce) in Late Antiquity, surviving in Greek and revived in Renaissance scholarship, eventually becoming the gram.

French National Convention decree of 1795

Type decreeDate 1795Locator Original decree revising the metric systemLanguage French

Translation

Defined gramme as the absolute weight of a volume of pure water equal to the cube of the hundredth part of a metre, at the temperature of melting ice.
