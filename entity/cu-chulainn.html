Who It Is

What kind of hero kills his own son without recognition, dies lashed upright to a stone, and transforms into a literal monster in battle? Cú Chulainn is the answer — and his story has been weaponized by revolutionaries, sanitized by Victorians, and claimed by both sides of a sectarian war. He's Ireland's Achilles, but far stranger and more tragic.

Powers
Ríastrad (warp spasm / battle frenzy)

Cú Chulainn's most terrifying ability: a supernatural battle transformation in which his body contorted hideously — one eye receded into his skull while the other bulged out, his hair bristled with points of fire, his calves reversed, and a 'hero's light' blazed from his forehead. In this state he attacked indiscriminately, friend and foe alike.

Limit:

Gáe Bulg (barbed spear)

A uniquely lethal weapon given by Scáthach that, once it entered a body, released thirty barbs filling every joint. It could only be removed by cutting away the flesh. Thrown with the foot in water, it produced a single entry wound but internal devastation.

Limit:

Superhuman combat prowess

Cú Chulainn possessed strength, speed, and skill far beyond mortal warriors. He could perform the 'salmon leap' (léim dar sliss) — vaulting enormous distances — and the 'apple feat,' catching multiple thrown projectiles without dropping any.

Limit:

Divine heritage and semi-divine nature

As the son of Lugh, the many-skilled god of the Tuatha Dé Danann, Cú Chulainn possessed innate supernatural qualities. He is often described explicitly as an incarnation (not merely a descendant) of Lugh, who appeared to assist him during the Táin.

Limit:

Geasa (sacred taboos/obligations)

Cú Chulainn was bound by multiple geasa — sacred obligations and prohibitions whose violation guaranteed death. His most famous geasa were never to eat dog meat and never to refuse hospitality. When these contradicted, his death became inevitable.

Limit:

Champion's light (lúan láith)

The 'hero's light' or 'warrior's moon' that blazed from Cú Chulainn's forehead during the ríastrad, described as a radiant halo visible at great distance. It marked him as a true champion in the Indo-European heroic tradition.

Accounts I
The Boyhood Deeds — How Sétanta Became Cú Chulainn

As a child of five, Sétanta left his home to join the boy-troop at Emain Macha, King Conchobar's court. Arriving without introduction, he was attacked by the 150 boys but defeated them all in a fury. Conchobar recognized his nephew. Later, invited to a feast at Culann the smith's fortress, Sétanta arrived late and was attacked by Culann's massive guard dog. He killed it by smashing it against a standing stone. To make amends, he offered to serve as Culann's guard dog until a replacement could be raised, earning the name Cú Chulainn — 'the Hound of Culann.' The druid Cathbad then prophesied: whoever took up arms that day would earn eternal fame but die young. Cú Chulainn chose glory.

Training with Scáthach and the Gáe Bulg

Cú Chulainn traveled to Alba (Scotland) to train under the warrior-woman Scáthach on the Isle of Skye. He overcame her fortress's defenses — leaping the 'Pupil's Bridge' that others feared — and became her finest student. Scáthach taught him the use of the Gáe Bulg, the deadly barbed spear. During his training, he fought and defeated Scáthach's rival Aífe in single combat. He spared Aífe's life on condition she bear him a son, Connla, leaving a gold thumb-ring as a token of recognition. He also received the sword Cruaidín Catutchenn. This training completed his transformation from boy warrior to hero.

The Táin Bó Cúailnge — Single Combat at the Ford

Queen Medb of Connacht invaded Ulster to steal the great brown bull of Cooley. Ulster's warriors were afflicted by a curse rendering them helpless. Only seventeen-year-old Cú Chulainn — not an Ulsterman by birth — was unaffected. He held off Medb's entire army at ford-crossings, invoking the right of single combat. Day after day, he fought and killed champion after champion, including his foster-brother Fer Diad in a three-day battle at a river ford. His father Lugh appeared to heal his wounds between combats. Finally, the Ulstermen recovered and drove Medb's forces back, but the bulls of Ulster and Connacht destroyed each other in a final, cosmic battle across Ireland.

Accounts II
The Tragic Death of Connla

Years after his training, Cú Chulainn's son Connla — born to Aífe — sailed to Ireland seeking his father. Cú Chulainn had forbidden anyone to let the boy land without identifying himself. When Connla refused to give his name (bound by a geis from Aífe), Cú Chulainn fought and killed him in single combat. Only after the mortal wound did he see the gold thumb-ring he had left as a token. In some versions, Cú Chulainn carries the dying boy to the assembled Ulstermen, crying out 'Here is my son!' The story is an Irish parallel to the Persian Rustam and Sohrab tragedy.

The Wasting Sickness and the Love of Fand

Cú Chulainn fell gravely ill — the 'wasting sickness' (serglige) — after a vision of two Otherworld women beating him with horsewhips. The god Manannán mac Lir's wife Fand had fallen in love with him and summoned him to the Otherworld. He went, fought battles for Fand's people, and became her lover. His mortal wife Emer discovered the affair and confronted them with a knife, offering to release him. Fand, moved by Emer's dignity, returned to her husband Manannán, who shook his magical cloak between Fand and Cú Chulainn to ensure they would never meet again. Cú Chulainn and Emer drank a draught of forgetfulness from the druids.

The Death of Cú Chulainn

Cú Chulainn's enemies — the children of warriors he had killed — conspired to trap him through his geasa. They offered him dog meat (forbidden to eat) at a feast (forbidden to refuse). He accepted, breaking his geis, and knew he was doomed. On the way to battle, three crones (the Morrígan in triple form) offered him roasted dog. He ate. His enemies then attacked. The Morrígan, in raven form, perched on his shoulder. Mortally wounded, Cú Chulainn lashed himself to a standing stone so he could die on his feet, facing his enemies. No one dared approach his body until the Morrígan landed on his shoulder as a raven — and only then did they know the great hero was dead. His loyal horse Liath Macha wept blood. The hero-light faded from his brow.

The Wooing of Emer

Cú Chulainn sought the hand of Emer, daughter of Forgall Monach, who opposed the match and set impossible conditions: Cú Chulainn must train with Scáthach. While he was abroad, Forgall tried to marry Emer to a Munster king. Cú Chulainn returned in the nick of time, stormed Forgall's fortress, killed Forgall and his men, and carried Emer away. Their marriage was marked by mutual respect — Emer was one of the few who could match Cú Chulainn's verbal dexterity, and she accepted his Otherworld affair with Fand with remarkable dignity.
