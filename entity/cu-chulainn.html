Who It Is

Cú Chulainn (born Sétanta) is the greatest hero of Irish mythology, the central figure of the Ulster Cycle and the 'Hound of Ulster' — a semi-divine warrior whose battle frenzy (ríastrad or 'warp spasm') transforms him into a monster of terrifying strength. As a child, he earned his name by killing Culann's ferocious guard-dog and offering to serve as its replacement. He was trained in arms by the legendary warrior-woman Scáthach in Scotland, mastering the fearsome Gáe Bolg (a barbed spear shot with the foot). Cú Chulainn is best known for his single-handed defense of the province of Ulster against the armies of Queen Medb of Connacht during the Táin Bó Cúailnge, fighting for days on end despite his wounds. He is also the hero of a great tragedy: he killed his own son, Conla, in combat; killed his friend Ferdiad in a three-day ford fight; and ultimately was betrayed by his own geasa (sacred taboos) and died bound to a standing stone, his enemies afraid to approach until a raven perched on his shoulder (the Morrígan claiming him). Cú Chulainn embodies the Celtic heroic ideal: courage beyond reason, loyalty to the point of death, and a tragic flaw (pride, rashness) that ensures his doom. He remains a symbol of Irish national identity.

Form

He is normally described as small, youthful and beardless, and usually dark. The Wooing of Emer and Bricriu's Feast call him 'a dark, sad man, comeliest of the men of Erin'; the Intoxication of the Ulstermen a 'little, black-browed man'. Yet the prophetess Fedelm in the Táin describes him as blond. The fullest description in the Táin gives him three-coloured hair (brown at the base, blood-red in the middle, golden at the crown), four dimples of yellow/green/crimson/blue in each cheek, seven pupils in each eye, and seven toes and fingers on each extremity. These hyper-mythic numbers are clearly literary convention, not literal anatomy.

Powers

Ríastrad (battle warp-spasm / battle frenzy) A transformative frenzy in which Cú Chulainn becomes 'a monstrous thing, hideous and shapeless'. The Táin describes his shanks and joints trembling, his body twisting inside his skin so his feet and shins switch to the rear, temple-sinews bulging to the nape of his neck, one eye sucked deep into his skull while the other falls out along his cheek, his mouth distorting until lungs and liver flap in his throat, hostile flames rising from his mouth, and hair spiked with rage. In this state he knows neither friend nor foe. After a battle he is so hot he must be cooled in a barrel of water (the first erupts, the second boils, the third warms pleasantly). Táin Bó Cúailnge Uncontrollable — makes him a danger to his own side; cools only by immersion. Depicted as hero-light ('hero-light' burns around him at his death) in some traditions versus the monstrous warp-spasm in the Táin.

Gáe Bulg (the barbed spear) A terrible barbed spear given by his teacher Scáthach, the technique of which was taught to him alone (not to Ferdiad). In the Book of Leinster it must be 'made ready for use on a stream and cast from the fork of the toes'; it enters the body with a single wound then opens into thirty barbs (some versions: seven heads each with seven barbs), and can only be removed by cutting the flesh away. It was made from the bone of the sea-monster Curruid, slain fighting another sea-monster Coinchenn. It proves invariably fatal when thrown — Cú Chulainn uses it only in extremis (against Ferdiad and against his own son Connla). Gáe Bulg article; Táin Bó Cúailnge; Book of Leinster Must be prepared on a stream and cast with the toes/fork of the foot; fatal follows from its barbs. The name's etymology is disputed ('belly spear' from bolg vs 'notched spear' from bolc vs Hamp's *balu-gaisos 'death-spear'); some sources describe it as a simple deadly spear rather than ritualized.

Supernatural martial feats from Scáthach Scáthach taught him all the arts of war, including extraordinary feats such as balancing on the butt of a spear (commemorated in a 2010 Armagh sculpture). He can distract and overcome the near-equal warrior-woman Aífe in single combat. Tochmarc Emire; Scáthach article

Incredible physical strength and precocity As a seven-year-old no weapon Conchobar gives him withstands his strength until the king's own weapons hold; he sets off on a foray and kills the three sons of Nechtan Scéne. He beats the Emain boy-troop single-handed. Slept only an hour off Scáthach's powerful sleeping-potion meant for 24 hours. Táin Bó Cúailnge flashback; boyhood deeds

Chariot warfare with Liath Macha and Dub Sainglend He fights from a chariot driven by his loyal charioteer Láeg, drawn by the two famous horses Liath Macha ('Grey of Macha') and Dub Sainglend ('Dark of Sainglend'). The horses reappear in his death tale, where Liath Macha is struck by a spear meant to kill the champion — 'king of horses'. Táin Bó Cúailnge; Aided Con Culainn

Geasa (taboos binding him) He is bound by a set of personal taboos (geasa), the breaking of which spiritually weakens him. Notably a ban against eating dog meat conflicts with the great hospitality-taboo; when a crone serves him dog meat he must break his name-geis ('Hound of Culann'), sealing his doom before his final battle. Aided Con Culainn Violating geasa weakens him fatally.

Accounts

Deichtine, sister/daughter+charioteer of King Conchobar, shelters in a magic house with the Ulstermen during a snowstorm hunting magical birds; a boy is born while a mare births twin colts, and next morning only the child and colts remain at the Brug na Bóinde (Newgrange). The god Lugh reveals he was the host and has put his child in her womb to be named Sétanta. In the earlier recension Deichtine aborts the divine child over a betrothal scandal and reconceives; in the later she is Lugh's wife and births Sétanta. The nobles of Ulster foster the child to several: Conchobar, Sencha, Blaí Briugu, Fergus mac Róich, the poet Amergin and his wife Findchóem.

As a child he joins the boy-troop at Emain Macha without asking protection, triggers his first ríastrad, beats the boys single-handed. Culann the smith invites Conchobar to a feast; Sétanta promises to follow after finishing a hurling game, but Conchobar forgets him and Culann looses his ferocious hound. Sétanta kills the monstrous hound in self-defence (smashing it against a standing stone, or driving a sliotar down its throat). He promises to rear Culann a replacement and guard his house meanwhile. The druid Cathbad renounces him: henceforth 'Cú Chulainn', 'Culann's Hound'.

The Ulstermen fear the beautiful young hero will steal wives and ruin daughters, and seek a wife for him. He will have none but Emer, daughter of Forgall Monach, who opposes the match and sends Cú Chulainn to train with the warrior-woman Scáthach at Dún Scáith on the Isle of Skye in hopes he dies. Cú Chulainn learns all arts of war from Scáthach (including use of the Gáe Bulg, taught only to him), becomes foster-brother to Ferdiad, overcomes Scáthach's rival (and sister) Aífe, leaves Aífe pregnant with Connla, and returns fully trained. He storms Forgall's fortress, killing 24 men, abducts Emer and steals the treasure; Forgall falls from the ramparts to his death. The druid Cathbad proposes that on the wedding night Conchobar (who holds the 'right of the first night') sleeps with Emer but Cathbad sleeps between them.

Eight years after leaving Aífe pregnant, their son Connla — bound by his mother's geasa never to reveal his name, never refuse a challenge, never turn back — comes to Ireland seeking his father. The Ulaid, alarmed by his precocious skill, send warriors; Connla defeats Conall Cernach. Emer warns Cú Chulainn the boy is his son, but he rebukes her ('heroic deeds are not performed with a woman's assistance') and fights him for Ulster's sake. Connla nearly matches him, deliberately missing a spear-shot once he realises his opponent is his father, but Cú Chulainn fatally wounds him with the Gáe Bulg. His dying words to his father: they would have 'carried the flag of Ulster to the gates of Rome and beyond'.

At seventeen he single-handedly defends Ulster against Medb of Connacht's vast army come to steal the stud bull Donn Cúailnge. The Ulster men are disabled by a curse causing labour-pains, so Cú Chulainn — who let the invasion start unobserved while with a woman — invokes the right of single combat at fords, defeating champion after champion for months. He refuses the Morrígan's offer of love, and she attacks him in animal forms (eel, wolf, heifer) during his combat with Lóch mac Mofemis; he wounds each form, then blesses and heals her when she appears as a milking crone. Wounded, he is healed by Lugh, then wreaks his most spectacular ríastrad, building walls of corpses. He yields to his foster-father Fergus (on condition of a favour returned), and fights a gruelling three-day duel with his best friend Ferdiad, whom he kills with the Gáe Bulg. He spares Medb when he has her at his mercy because he will not kill women, and guards her retreat.

The troublemaker Bricriu incites Cú Chulainn, Conall Cernach and Lóegaire Búadach to compete for the champion's portion. Cú Chulainn wins every test but the others refuse to accept. Cú Roí mac Dáire of Munster settles it by visiting each as a hideous churl and challenging them to behead him and let him return the blow. Conall and Lóegaire behead him then flee when he returns; only Cú Chulainn submits honourably, and Cú Roí spares him, declaring him champion.

Cú Roí, in disguise, joins a raid on Inis Fer Falga in return for his choice of spoils and chooses Blathnát, whom Cú Chulainn herself loves; when Cú Chulainn tries to stop him, Cú Roí cuts his hair and drives him into the ground. Cú Roí (like Samson) can only be killed under contrived circumstances; Blathnát betrays them to Cú Chulainn, who kills him, while Cú Roí's poet Ferchertne, enraged at the betrayal, leaps off a cliff with Blathnát. Separately, Cú Chulainn falls in love with Fand, wife of Manannán mac Lir; Emer tries to kill her rival but Fand's love moves her to give him up; Fand returns to Manannán, who shakes his cloak between them so they never meet again, and both couples drink a potion erasing the affair.

Medb conspires with Lugaid (son of Cú Roí), Erc, and the sons of those he killed to lure him to his death. His fate is sealed by breaking his geasa: a crone offers him dog meat, and the hospitality-taboo forces him to eat it (breaking the ban on dog-meat, resonant with his name 'Hound of Culann'), spiritually weakening him. Lugaid has three magical spears; by each a king falls: first his charioteer Láeg (king of chariot-drivers), then his horse Liath Macha (king of horses), then Cú Chulainn himself, mortally. He ties himself to a standing stone (traditionally Clochafarmore near Dundalk) to die on his feet facing his enemies. Only when a raven lands on his shoulder do they believe him dead. Lugaid takes his head, and as he does the hero-light burns and Cú Chulainn's sword falls, cutting off Lugaid's hand. Conall Cernach avenges him, killing Lugaid and Erc.

Content Notes

Cú Chulainn's signature tragedy is killing his own son Connla, whom he does not recognise. The tale stresses the geasa-bound boy's doomed refusal to give his name and Cú Chulainn's rebuke of Emer's warning ('heroic deeds are not performed with a woman's assistance'), framing the killing as honour-bound duty. His final grieving and the detail that for three days no calf of the Ulaid cattle survived show the dreadful cost.

Recognised by scholarship as part of a widespread Indo-European father-slays-son motif (Rostam/Sohrab, Hildebrandslied, Theseus).

Aided Óenfhir Aífe

During his training he overpowers the warrior-woman Aífe and, with his sword at her throat, spares her only on condition she cease hostilities with Scáthach and 'bear him a son'. He leaves her pregnant — an explicit use of sexual coercion as a condition of her life.

Read in scholarship as a heroic-economy motif (the hero must seed a son) rather than romantic love; the later tragedy of Connla in part inherits its dark causality.

Tochmarc Emire; Aífe article

Upon his wedding to Emer, King Conchobar holds the customary 'right of the first night' over his subjects' marriages. Fearing Cú Chulainn's reaction but not wishing to lose authority, a compromise is found: Conchobar sleeps with Emer but the druid Cathbad sleeps between them — a ritualised proxy without consummation.

The 'ius primae noctis'-type motif is debated as a historical institution vs literary topos in early Irish law; scholars are sceptical it was a real custom.

Tochmarc Emire

In his ríastrad frenzy he attacks the Connacht army and kills hundreds, 'building walls of corpses'. He defeats and kills champion after champion in single combat, and kills his own best friend Ferdiad with the Gáe Bulg in a three-day duel, then mourns him. The violence is unsparing and central to his identity.

The ríastrad's monstrous corporeality has been read as an exaggerated heroic-warrior ideology and, by some, as a commentary on the dehumanising cost of warfare.

Táin Bó Cúailnge

After his rescued lover Derbforgaill is mutilated by jealous women of Ulster and dies (and her husband Lugaid dies of grief), Cú Chulainn avenges them by demolishing the house the women are inside, killing 150 of them.

Exemplifies the catastrophic scale of heroic vengeance in the Ulster Cycle.

Lugaid Riab nDerg / Derbforgaill episode

His doom is sealed when he breaks his name-taboo by eating dog meat. The hospitality-taboo against refusing a guest's offering forces him, despite the resonance with his name 'Hound of Culann'. His enemies (Medb, Lugaid) have three magic spears engineered to bring him down.

The geis (taboo) system is a central mechanism of Irish heroic fate; violating it is archetypically fatal.

A way to hold it in your mind

Before the hero who would die standing, lashed to a stone on the plain of Muirthemne, Irish tradition gives us a child who ran onto a hurling field without asking protection and beat the whole boy-troop of Emain Macha with his bare hands — then chased after them to demand they acknowledge him. Cú Chulainn of the Ulster Cycle is the furious, tender, doomed engine of Irish epic: the demigod son of Lugh who acquired his name by killing a smith's hound in self-defence and offering to guard the house until a replacement grew, who stormed a goddess's advances and then healed her wounds, who slew his own son at a ford, and who, at the age of seventeen, held Queen Medb's entire Connacht army at bay by invoking the ancient right of single combat. His monster's frenzy — the ríastrad that turned his limbs backwards and his eye into his skull — is matched only by his fierce code of honour, and both together make him, in the words of one scholar brooding on the tradition, both Ireland's greatest hero and its most unbearable warning about the cost of glory.
