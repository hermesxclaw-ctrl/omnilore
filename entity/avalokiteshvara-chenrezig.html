Who It Is

In the vast pantheon of Mahayana Buddhism, no figure embodies compassion more completely than Avalokiteshvara, the bodhisattva who vowed to liberate all beings from suffering—and, upon realizing the impossibility of that task, shattered into pieces, only to be reassembled by his spiritual father into a being with eleven heads and a thousand arms, each hand holding an eye to perceive every cry for help.

Avalokiteshvara is the embodiment of compassion (karuna). He is depicted as ever-vigilant, constantly watching over beings, and responds to their cries with immediate aid. He is patient, gentle, and skillful in means (upaya), adapting his teachings to the needs of each being. He is also depicted as a bodhisattva on the path to Buddhahood, having postponed his own liberation until all beings are saved.

Pronunciation — Sanskrit: ah-vah-loh-keh-SHVE-rah; Tibetan: chen-ray-ZEEK

Aliases — Chenrezig, Guanyin, Kannon, Lokesvara, Padmapani, Natha

Powers
Compassionate liberation

Avalokiteshvara has the power to liberate beings from suffering and the cycle of rebirth. He can guide beings to rebirth in his pure land or in higher realms.

Limit: Beings must have some karmic affinity or faith; he cannot liberate those who have not accumulated merit or who do not call upon him.

Transformation into various forms

He can appear in any form—human, animal, or divine—to teach beings according to their needs. The Lotus Sutra lists 33 forms, including both male and female.

Limit: He only takes forms that are appropriate for the beings to be taught.

Protection from dangers

He protects beings from fire, drowning, murder, demons, imprisonment, robbers, and other perils. He also protects from the three poisons (greed, hatred, ignorance).

Limit: Protection is often conditional on sincere devotion and recitation of his name.

Thousand arms and eleven heads

In his thousand-armed form, he has a thousand arms to help beings and an eye in each palm to see their suffering. The eleven heads represent his ability to see all directions and his compassion for all beings.

Limit: This form is a symbolic representation of his unlimited compassion, not a physical limitation.

Mantra 'Om mani padme hum'

Recitation of this mantra invokes Avalokiteshvara's blessings and purifies negative karma. It is widely used in Tibetan Buddhism.

Limit: The mantra's efficacy is believed to depend on faith and correct recitation.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In Indian and Tibetan iconography, Avalokiteshvara is typically depicted as a princely figure, white in color, with four arms (or more), holding a lotus, a mala (rosary), and a wish-fulfilling jewel. In the thousand-armed form, he has eleven heads and a thousand arms, each with an eye in the palm. Symbols — Lotus (purity), mala (mindfulness), wish-fulfilling jewel (compassionate giving), willow branch (healing), vase of pure water (compassion), the mantra 'Om mani padme hum' (the essence of compassion), the syllable 'Hrih' (seed syllable), the deer skin over the shoulder (compassion for animals), the thousand arms and eyes (omnipresent compassion).

Domains — He resides in his pure land, Potalaka, which is variously located in southern India (Mount Potalaka) or in the celestial realm. In Tibetan cosmology, Potalaka is also identified with the Potala Palace in Lhasa, the residence of the Dalai Lamas. He is accessible to all beings in times of need, and his power extends throughout the six realms of samsara.

Accounts I
The Vow of CompassionKarandavyuha Sutra

circa 4th-5th century CE

Avalokiteshvara vows to liberate all beings from samsara. After striving for eons, he sees that countless beings still suffer. In despair, his head and body shatter into pieces. His spiritual father, Amitabha, reassembles him into a being with eleven heads and a thousand arms, blessing him with enhanced powers. Avalokiteshvara becomes a powerful bodhisattva with limitless means to help beings.

The Creation of the Tibetan PeopleMani Kabum

12th-13th century CE (text)

Avalokiteshvara, as Chenrezig, receives a blessing from Amitabha and is tasked with civilizing Tibet. He takes the form of a monkey and mates with a rock demoness, and their descendants become the Tibetan people. He then teaches them compassion and the Dharma. The Tibetan people are considered the spiritual children of Chenrezig, and Tibet becomes a Buddhist land.

Accounts II
The Miracle of the Lotus SutraLotus Sutra

1st century BCE-2nd century CE

The Buddha praises Avalokiteshvara's powers and teaches the assembly how to call upon him. He describes how Avalokiteshvara saves beings from dangers and appears in various forms to teach. The assembly praises Avalokiteshvara, and the chapter becomes a popular devotional text.

The Princess MiaoshanChinese legend, recorded in various texts

Song dynasty (10th-13th century CE)

A princess named Miaoshan defies her father's wish to marry, wanting to become a nun. Her father persecutes her, but she endures with compassion. She eventually becomes Guanyin, and her father is saved through her sacrifice. This story explains the female form of Guanyin in China.

Avalokiteshvara is a god who grants wishes. — He is a bodhisattva who helps beings achieve enlightenment, not a creator god. His powers are to aid in spiritual progress, not to grant worldly wishes without karmic context.

Guanyin is a separate goddess. — Guanyin is a manifestation of Avalokiteshvara in Chinese Buddhism. The gender transformation is a cultural adaptation.

The Dalai Lama is a god. — The Dalai Lama is considered an emanation of Avalokiteshvara, but he is a human being and a monk, not a deity.
