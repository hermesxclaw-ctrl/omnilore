Archive Dossier — Identity

Avalokiteshvara

Pronunciation Sanskrit: ah-vah-loh-keh-SHVE-rah; Tibetan: chen-ray-ZEEK

Known As

ChenrezigGuanyinKannonLokesvaraPadmapaniNathaAvalokitaSpyan-ras-gzigs

Etymology

From Sanskrit 'avalokita' (looking down) and 'ishvara' (lord), thus 'Lord who looks down [with compassion]'. Tibetan 'Chenrezig' (spyan-ras-gzigs) translates as 'one who gazes with steadfast eyes'.

Cultural Ownership

Pan-Buddhist; shared across India, Tibet, Nepal, China, Japan, Korea, Southeast Asia, and the Himalayan regions. In Tibet, Chenrezig is considered the patron deity of the Tibetan people, and the Dalai Lamas are regarded as his emanations.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars debate whether Avalokiteshvara originated as a solar deity or as a form of Shiva, but the consensus is that he is a uniquely Buddhist creation. In East Asia, the gender transformation of Avalokiteshvara into Guanyin has led to debates about whether Guanyin is a distinct deity or a manifestation. Some also conflate him with the Hindu deity Vishnu, but this is not supported by textual evidence.

Archive Dossier — Approach

In the vast pantheon of Mahayana Buddhism, no figure embodies compassion more completely than Avalokiteshvara, the bodhisattva who vowed to liberate all beings from suffering—and, upon realizing the impossibility of that task, shattered into pieces, only to be reassembled by his spiritual father into a being with eleven heads and a thousand arms, each hand holding an eye to perceive every cry for help.

Om Mani Padme Hum — the jewel in the lotus. The sound of compassion echoing through the ten directions. I have a thousand arms that reach where one cannot, and eleven eyes that see through every darkness.

Who It Is

Avalokiteshvara (Tibetan: Chenrezig, 'The One with the Compassionate Eye') is the bodhisattva of infinite compassion and the most important bodhisattva in Tibetan Buddhism. He is the embodiment of the compassion of all Buddhas. According to the Karandavyuha Sutra, when Avalokiteshvara saw the suffering of beings in samsara, his head shattered into eleven pieces from grief. The Buddha Amitabha reassembled the pieces into eleven heads (giving him the eleven-headed form). Then, when he reached out to help beings, his arms shattered; Amitabha gave him a thousand arms, each with an eye in the palm, so he could see and reach all suffering. The six-syllable mantra of Chenrezig — Om Mani Padme Hum — is the most famous and most recited mantra in Tibetan Buddhism, carved into rocks and prayer wheels across the Himalayas, Tibet, Mongolia, and beyond. The current Dalai Lamas are believed to be emanations of Chenrezig, making the deity both spiritually and politically central to Tibetan identity. The Potala Palace in Lhasa is named after Mount Potalaka, the celestial abode of Avalokiteshvara. Chenrezig is depicted in many forms: the four-armed form is most common, but the eleven-headed thousand-armed form (Chuchik Zhel Chuchak) is the most dramatic and recognizable. His worship embodies the core Mahayana ideal: the vow to postpone one's own liberation until every last being is freed from suffering.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

In Indian and Tibetan iconography, Avalokiteshvara is typically depicted as a princely figure, white in color, with four arms (or more), holding a lotus, a mala (rosary), and a wish-fulfilling jewel. In the thousand-armed form, he has eleven heads and a thousand arms, each with an eye in the palm. In East Asia, Guanyin is often depicted as a female figure in flowing robes, sometimes with a willow branch and a vase of pure water. In Tibet, Chenrezig is often depicted in the four-armed form, white, with the first pair of hands in prayer position, holding a jewel and a lotus.

Symbols & Iconography

Lotus (purity), mala (mindfulness), wish-fulfilling jewel (compassionate giving), willow branch (healing), vase of pure water (compassion), the mantra 'Om mani padme hum' (the essence of compassion), the syllable 'Hrih' (seed syllable), the deer skin over the shoulder (compassion for animals), the thousand arms and eyes (omnipresent compassion).

Behaviour & Personality

Avalokiteshvara is the embodiment of compassion (karuna). He is depicted as ever-vigilant, constantly watching over beings, and responds to their cries with immediate aid. He is patient, gentle, and skillful in means (upaya), adapting his teachings to the needs of each being. He is also depicted as a bodhisattva on the path to Buddhahood, having postponed his own liberation until all beings are saved.

Domains & Habitats

He resides in his pure land, Potalaka, which is variously located in southern India (Mount Potalaka) or in the celestial realm. In Tibetan cosmology, Potalaka is also identified with the Potala Palace in Lhasa, the residence of the Dalai Lamas. He is accessible to all beings in times of need, and his power extends throughout the six realms of samsara.

Version Differences

In India, Avalokiteshvara is male. In China, from the Song dynasty onward, he gradually became depicted as female (Guanyin), possibly influenced by the feminine qualities of compassion and by the story of the princess Miaoshan. In Japan, Kannon is often androgynous or male. In Tibet, Chenrezig is male and is considered the patron deity of Tibet. In Southeast Asia, Lokesvara is often depicted with multiple arms and is associated with the Khmer empire.

Attested in Indian Mahayana sutras (Lotus Sutra, Karandavyuha Sutra); transmitted to Tibet from the 7th centuryEra Indian origins c. 1st-5th century CE; central in Tibetan Buddhism from the 7th-8th century

Archive Dossier — Powers

Compassionate liberation

Source Version All traditions

Description

Avalokiteshvara has the power to liberate beings from suffering and the cycle of rebirth. He can guide beings to rebirth in his pure land or in higher realms.

Limitations

Beings must have some karmic affinity or faith; he cannot liberate those who have not accumulated merit or who do not call upon him.

Contradictions

Some texts suggest his power is universal, while others imply it is conditional.

Transformation into various forms

Source Version Lotus Sutra, Karandavyuha Sutra

Description

He can appear in any form—human, animal, or divine—to teach beings according to their needs. The Lotus Sutra lists 33 forms, including both male and female.

Limitations

He only takes forms that are appropriate for the beings to be taught.

Contradictions

None.

Protection from dangers

Source Version Lotus Sutra

Description

He protects beings from fire, drowning, murder, demons, imprisonment, robbers, and other perils. He also protects from the three poisons (greed, hatred, ignorance).

Limitations

Protection is often conditional on sincere devotion and recitation of his name.

Contradictions

Some interpretations suggest protection is automatic, others require faith.

Thousand arms and eleven heads

Source Version Karandavyuha Sutra and subsequent iconography

Description

In his thousand-armed form, he has a thousand arms to help beings and an eye in each palm to see their suffering. The eleven heads represent his ability to see all directions and his compassion for all beings.

Limitations

This form is a symbolic representation of his unlimited compassion, not a physical limitation.

Contradictions

The number of heads varies (11, 9, or 5) across traditions.

Mantra 'Om mani padme hum'

Source Version Karandavyuha Sutra

Description

Recitation of this mantra invokes Avalokiteshvara's blessings and purifies negative karma. It is widely used in Tibetan Buddhism.

Limitations

The mantra's efficacy is believed to depend on faith and correct recitation.

Contradictions

The exact meaning of the mantra is debated among scholars.

Archive Dossier — Relationships

Amitabha Buddha

Type spiritual fatherSource Karandavyuha Sutra, iconography

Description

Amitabha is Avalokiteshvara's teacher and spiritual father. He appears in Avalokiteshvara's crown and blessed him after his shattering.

Version Note

In some traditions, Avalokiteshvara is a manifestation of Amitabha.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Tara

Type consort/emanationSource Tibetan Buddhist tradition

Description

Tara is said to have been born from Avalokiteshvara's tears of compassion. She helps him in his work and is sometimes considered his consort or daughter.

Version Note

In some accounts, Tara is a separate bodhisattva; in others, she is a manifestation of Avalokiteshvara's compassion.

Conflicting Accounts

Some texts say she was born from his tears, others say she was born from a lotus.

Mahakala

Type protector/emanationSource Tibetan Buddhist iconography

Description

Mahakala is a wrathful manifestation of Avalokiteshvara in Tibetan Buddhism, serving as a protector of the Dharma.

Version Note

Mahakala is often considered a separate deity but is sometimes identified as a wrathful form of Chenrezig.

Conflicting Accounts

Some lineages identify Mahakala as a form of Shiva, not Avalokiteshvara.

Padmasambhava

Type associateSource Tibetan Buddhist tradition

Description

Padmasambhava, the great guru who brought Buddhism to Tibet, is closely associated with Chenrezig and is considered an emanation of Avalokiteshvara in some traditions.

Version Note

Padmasambhava is often depicted with Chenrezig in thangkas.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Manjushri

Type fellow bodhisattvaSource Mahayana iconography

Description

Manjushri, the bodhisattva of wisdom, is often depicted alongside Avalokiteshvara and Vajrapani in the 'Three Protectors'.

Version Note

They represent compassion, wisdom, and power respectively.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Dalai Lamas

Type emanationSource Tibetan Buddhist tradition

Description

The Dalai Lamas are considered incarnations of Chenrezig in Tibetan Buddhism.

Version Note

This belief is central to Tibetan political and spiritual leadership.

Conflicting Accounts

Some non-Tibetan Buddhists do not accept this claim.
