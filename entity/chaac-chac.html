Who It Is

When the Yucatán sky cracks open and rain pours down, it is said that Chaac has struck the clouds with his lightning axe. For the ancient Maya and their descendants, this god of rain, thunder, and lightning is not a distant myth but a living presence, honored in rituals, feared in his wrath, and intimately bound to the cycles of maize and life.

Chaac is a rain-bringer and agriculture patron, but also a war-like fury. He strikes clouds with his axe to produce thunder and rain. He opens mountains to release maize. In myths, he can be vengeful, as seen in his pursuit of the Sun and Moon.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Chaac (also spelled Chac) is derived from Yucatec Maya; in Classic Mayan it is Chaahk [t͡ʃaːhk], meaning 'rain' or 'storm'.

Pronunciation — Classic Mayan: [t͡ʃaːhk]; Yucatec: /tʃaak/

Aliases — Chac, Chaahk, Chac Xib Chaac, Bolon Dzacab

Ownership — Maya civilization, particularly Yucatec, Chorti, Tzotzil, Qʼeqchiʼ, and other Maya groups; also present in Classic Maya iconography and codices.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Chaac is depicted with a human body but reptilian or amphibian scales. His head is non-human, with fangs and a long, pendulous nose. In Classic art, he wears a shell ear ornament and carries a shield and a lightning axe. The axe may be personified as K'awiil (Bolon Dzacab).

Symbols — Lightning axe, shield, shell ear ornament, reptilian features. Four directional Chaacs with colors: red (east), white (north), black (west), yellow (south), green (center).

Domains — Sky, clouds, rain, thunder, lightning, mountains, caves, cenotes, and agricultural fields. He is associated with the cardinal directions and the center.

Chaac (Choc) is brother of Sun; they defeat their adoptive mother and her lover. Later, Chaac commits adultery with Sun's wife and is punished; his tears become rain. Origin of rain; war between Chaac and Sun.
