The Approach

Before there were kings, before there were cities, before humans knew how to cook food or sew skins, there was a single shining figure on a mountain — Keyumars, the Living Mortal, who held the light of kingship itself and watched over a world that had not yet learned to die.

Content advisory. Contains Zoroastrian cosmogonic violence and death, demonic assault, primordial death and mourning, parent-child death, and a variant tradition describing incestuous divine parentage (Ahura Mazda and his daughter Spenta Armaiti). No graphic sexual detail; cosmogonic semen and plant-birth motif described in non-graphic scholarly terms.
Identity

Keyumars

Pronunciation New Persian: /kæjuːˈmærs/ key-oo-MARS; Classical /kayōmart/; Avestan Gayō Maretan /ɡajō marətan/; Anglicized Keyumars, Kiomars

Known As

Gayō Maretan (Avestan)Gayōmart (Middle Persian, Pahlavi)Kayōmart (Middle Persian transcription)GayomardKiomarsKaiumarsKayumarsGayōmard Gar-shāh (King of the Mountains)Pēšdād (the First Lawgiver)Gilshah (variant in some Islamic sources)

Etymology

Avestan Gayō Maretan < gaya- 'life, living' + marətan 'mortal, human being'. Literally 'the living mortal'. Middle Persian Kayōmart < same compound. New Persian Keyumars / Kiomars continues the Pahlavi form. Cognate with Persian mard 'man, human' (مرد). Avestan Mashya and Mashyana (first mortal pair born from his seed) are from the same root marətan. Epithet pēšdād (پيشداد) means 'first to give law/justice', from pēš 'before, first' + dād 'law, justice'. Gar-shāh means 'King of the Mountains'.

Cultural Ownership

Ancient Iranian, specifically Zoroastrian scriptural and later Persian literary tradition. Attested first in the Avesta (Old and Young Avestan layers) and systematized in Sasanian-period Pahlavi literature (Bundahishn, Denkard). Re-elaborated as national mythic history in Ferdowsi's Shahnameh (c. 1010, Ghaznavid Iran) and preserved in living Zoroastrian (Parsi and Iranian Zoroastrian) tradition. Subsequently historicized in early Islamic universal histories. Not to be conflated with the biblical Adam despite later Islamic syncretism.

Disputed Identifications

Early Islamic historians attempted to harmonize Keyumars with biblical genealogy. Al-Tabari (History, vol.1) lists without endorsing: (1) Adam himself, (2) a son of Adam, (3) Gomer son of Japheth. Al-Masudi reports a rationalizing view that Keyumars was merely primus inter pares elected by proto-humans between Adam and Noah. Modern scholarship rejects these euhemeristic equations; Encyclopaedia Iranica (Shaki, Cereti) treats Gayōmart as an Indo-Iranian cosmogonic figure distinct from Semitic Adam, with parallels to Vedic Manu and Norse Ymir as 'first man / cosmic man' but not identical. Also disputed whether Mashya/Mashyana are his children (Bundahishn mainstream) or his siblings via Spenta Armaiti (variant reported in some Pahlavi traditions).

Description

Appearance

In Avestan and Pahlavi cosmology Gayōmart is not initially described with detailed physiognomy; he is the archetypal human created from mud (gil) on the left bank of the Veh-Dāit (Veh Rod) in Erān-vēj, the mythic center of the earth, opposite the primordial ox Gavevagdat. The Bundahishn tradition summarized by Safa specifies he was created in the form of a 15-year-old youth (javān-e 15-sāleh), radiant, neither male nor female in some accounts, or androgynous/sexless prototype. In Shahnameh iconography he is depicted as a venerable mountain king: tall, white-bearded, wearing leopard skins (palangineh) like his people who dwell in caves, seated on a throne on Mount Damavand/Alborz with sunlight-like farr (xvarenah) emanating from him. Persian miniatures (notably Shah Tahmasp Shahnameh, c.1524-1526, attributed to Sultan Muhammad) show him enthroned outdoors among animals and courtiers, wearing a crown and robe, with a halo of glory, surrounded by bowing leopards, lions and humans.

Symbols & Iconography

Primary symbol is the farr / xvarenah (Avestan xvarənah, Pahlavi xwarrah) - divine glory or charisma, depicted as a luminous nimbus or flame. Associated with mountains (Gar-shāh), leopard-skin clothing, cave dwelling, throne on high ground, and the primordial pairing with the white ox Gavevagdat. In Persepolis reliefs, the dying bull motif has been interpreted by some (Safa) as echoing Gavevagdat's death at the new year. Rhubarb plant (rīvās) is his vegetative memorial: Mashya and Mashyana grow as a single rhubarb stalk whose leaves form intertwined humans. Light vs darkness, spring (Nowruz, 1 Farvardin) vs winter/dragon. In Islamic miniatures his court represents the ideal of just kingship (dad).

Behaviour & Personality

Attested behavior differs sharply by layer. In Pahlavi cosmogony he is passive and contemplative: for 3000 years he lives without eating, speaking or praying, inwardly contemplating (menōg thought). He is Uhrmazd's helper, innocent and vulnerable, guarded by Astovidat. He does not fight until his time comes. In the Shahnameh he is active, wise, just and beloved: the first to establish royal custom (āyin-e shāhi), to prepare food and clothing properly, to practice law and justice (pēšdād), and to receive homage from all humans and animals. He is a mourning father (year-long lament for Siāmak) and a restrained avenger who waits for divine counsel (Sorush) before retaliating. In Masudi's rationalized version he is the most noble and senior descendant of the common father, chosen by consensus for his moral stature and accepts kingship only after the people swear obedience and renunciation of revolt. No attested negative traits; his perfection is part of his archetypal function.

Domains & Habitats

Erān-vēj (Airyana Vaejah), the mythic Iranian homeland at the center of the earth, on the Veh-Dāit riverbank, and specifically the mountains - hence Gar-shāh. In Pahlavi geography this is the Alborz range, later localized to Mount Damavand (Dunbawand) in Tabaristan by Tabari. His habitat is mountain caves before civilization; his throne is on the mountain summit. Cosmologically he belongs to the mēnōg (spiritual) then gētīg (material) creation; his death fertilizes the earth. In Shahnameh his domain is the whole inhabited world insofar as all humans and animals obey him, but his actual settlement is the mountain wilderness before cities exist. Associated with the first Gāhs (periods) of creation and with Nowruz (spring equinox) as the date of Ahriman's assault.

Version Differences

Avestan: sexless or androgynous first mortal, first sacrificer to Ahura Mazda, prototype without reign narrative. Pahlavi: elaborated cosmogonic biography - 6th Gāh creation from mud, 3000-year stillness, 30-year survival after Ahriman's attack, semen fertilized by sun producing rhubarb twins. Two Pahlavi variants on parentage: (a) direct creation by Ohrmazd from earth (standard Bundahishn), (b) birth from Ohrmazd's union with his daughter Spenta Armaiti (earth), making him brother to Mashya/Mashyana rather than their progenitor. Shahnameh: fully human first king (not sexless), father of Siāmak, grandfather of Hushang, 30-year reign, leopard-skin culture, farr-bearing monarch, no rhubarb birth in this section (that is displaced to later). Islamic histories: historicized as long-lived human lord at Dunbawand with 30 wives and children Mari/Mariyanah, or as elected constitutional monarch; Tabari explicitly distances himself from genealogical speculation. Modern scholarship distinguishes these layers and warns against harmonizing them.

Powers & Abilities

Xvarenah / Farr (Divine Glory)

Source VersionAvestan xvarənah (Yasht 19); Pahlavi xwarrah; Shahnameh (Ferdowsi, Khaleghi-Motlagh 1:38)

Supernatural radiance granted by Ahura Mazda/Ohrmazd, reserved for legitimate kings. In Shahnameh, God grants Keyumars the farr, making all humans and animals willingly pay homage and legitimizing his kingship. It is visible as light and is withdrawn upon moral failure (not in his case).

Limitations

Not a personal magic but a divine endowment; can be lost by sin. Keyumars retains it until death and passes kingship to Hushang. No attested active use as weapon.

Contradictions

Avestan texts treat xvarenah as a distinct deity/force that can flee; Shahnameh treats it as inherent royal charisma.

Primordial Vitality and Contemplative Endurance

Source VersionBundahishn 1-4; Safa summary of Pahlavi cosmogony; Denkard summary of Varshtmansar Nask

Created perfect, he lives 3000 years without need for food, speech or prayer, sustained inwardly, and after Ahriman's assault survives 30 years under demonic guard without being killed before his fated time.

Limitations

Vulnerability is temporal (zamān): Astovidat cannot kill him before his appointed time, but he is not immortal. He dies after 30 years and his death is necessary for future humanity.

Contradictions

Shahnameh gives him a 30-year reign as king, not a 30-year dying period; the 3000-year stillness is absent in Ferdowsi.

Generative Power - Semen as Seed of Humanity

Source VersionBundahishn 14.1-7; Greater Bundahishn TD2; Shaki Encyclopaedia Iranica 'Gayomard'

Upon death he falls on his left side and his semen (tukhm) spills to earth, is purified and fertilized by the sun (Khorshed), and after 40 years grows as a single rhubarb plant from which Mashya and Mashyana emerge as intertwined stalks, later separated into man and woman.

Limitations

Requires solar purification and 40-year gestation; not voluntary reproduction. In variant tradition he is not the progenitor but the brother of Mashya/Mashyana via Spenta Armaiti.

Contradictions

Shahnameh replaces this vegetative origin with normal patrilineal descent: Keyumars > Siāmak > Hushang. Islamic sources give him 30 wives and children Mari/Mariyanah.

Pēšdād - Institution of Law, Kingship and Culture

Source VersionShahnameh opening (Davis 2006, pp.5-7); Pahlavi epithet pēšdād; Aghaee 1993

As pēšdād he is the first to practice justice, establish royal practices (crown, throne, court), teach preparation of food, clothing from skins, and shelter. In Shahnameh he introduces civilization to cave-dwelling humans.

Limitations

Cultural hero role, not supernatural; his people still live primitively in mountains. No attested law code survives, only the title.

Contradictions

Pahlavi texts attribute lawgiving more broadly to later Pishdadian kings; Ferdowsi concentrates it in Keyumars.

Command over Humans and Animals

Source VersionShahnameh (Khaleghi-Motlagh 1:39-40)

So powerful and just that all humans, tame (ram) and wild (dad) animals pay homage (namāz bordand) voluntarily. Represents the ideal of universal kingship where nature acknowledges legitimacy.

Limitations

Moral authority, not animal control magic; animals do not fight for him. His son Siāmak must still raise an army against demons.

Contradictions

Absent in Avestan cosmogony where he is solitary with only Gavevagdat as companion.

Stories & Myths

Creation from Mud in Erān-vēj on the Veh-Dāit

Source Bundahishn (Greater Bundahishn) Ch.1-3; Zabihollah Safa summary of Middle Persian textsCulture Zoroastrian (Sasanian Pahlavi)Period Sasanian compilation (6th-9th c CE) of older Avestan lore

Summary

In the sixth Gāh (period) of creation, after Ohrmazd creates the primordial ox Gavevagdat from mud on the right bank of the Veh-Dāit in Erān-vēj (center of earth), he creates Gayōmart from mud on the left bank to be his helper. Gayōmart is formed as a 15-year-old youth, luminous, neither eating nor speaking, inwardly contemplating. He and Gavevagdat live 3000 years in peace while Ahriman lies stunned by the Ahunawar prayer.

Participants

Ohrmazd (Ahura Mazda)

Gayōmart

Gavevagdat (primordial ox)

Ahriman (Angra Mainyu) - stunned

Consequences

Establishes the dyad of first human and first animal as Ohrmazd's material creation; sets the 3000-year delay before evil's assault; explains the pairing of humanity and cattle in Zoroastrian cosmology.

Variants

Some texts say Gavevagdat created in fifth Gāh, Gayōmart in sixth; location sometimes generalized to Alborz. Avestan layer lacks the mud detail and Gāh chronology.

Ahriman's Assault at Nowruz and the Death of the Primordial Ox

Source Bundahishn Ch.4; Denkard 8 (Chihrdad Nask summary); Safa Hamaseh-sarayi dar IranCulture ZoroastrianPeriod Sasanian Pahlavi

Summary

At the end of the 3000-year stillness, the demoness Jēh (Jahi) cries out and awakens Ahriman from his stupor. Ahriman and his Dīvs attack the light. On the first day of Farvardin (spring equinox, Nowruz) Ahriman leaps onto earth as a dragon, creating death, illness, lust, thirst, hunger, and releasing Xrafstra (evil creeping creatures - reptiles, insects, rodents). In the catastrophe Gavevagdat dies - symbolically the old year giving way to the new, as depicted in Persepolis reliefs - and Ahriman assigns the demon Astovidat to guard Gayōmart, but cannot kill him before his fated time.

Participants

Ahriman

Jēh

Dīvs

Gayōmart

Gavevagdat

Astovidat

Consequences

Introduces death and evil into creation; explains origin of noxious creatures; establishes Nowruz as anniversary of both assault and renewal; Gayōmart's delayed death becomes necessary for future humanity.

Variants

Vendidad and other Avestan fragments give shorter assault narratives without Jēh detail. Some versions emphasize Ahriman's dragon form vs. more abstract darkness.

The Thirty-Year Dying and the Rhubarb Birth of Mashya and Mashyana

Source Bundahishn Ch.14-15; Shaki (2000) Encyclopaedia Iranica 'Gayomard'; Cereti (2015)Culture ZoroastrianPeriod Sasanian Pahlavi

Summary

Guarded by Astovidat, Gayōmart lives 30 years after the assault, then dies, falling on his left side. His semen spills to earth, is purified by the sun, and after 40 years grows as a rhubarb plant (rīvās) with a single stalk and 15 leaves. From this stalk emerge Mashya and Mashyana, intertwined, later separated by Ohrmazd into man and woman, the parents of all humanity. Their names are forms of marətan 'mortal'.

Participants

Gayōmart

Astovidat

Khorshed (Sun)

Spenta Armaiti (Earth, as recipient)

Mashya

Mashyana

Consequences

Explains origin of humanity from Gayōmart's seed; links human origin to plant life; establishes 40-year vegetative gestation; Mashya/Mashyana become first couple who then populate the world and first commit sin (eating, lying).

Variants

Major variant: Ohrmazd marries his daughter Spenta Armaiti, who bears Gayōmart and later Mashya/Mashyana as siblings, not descendants. In this version Gayōmart is not the progenitor. Shahnameh omits this entirely in favor of patrilineal descent.

Variant Parentage - Son of Ohrmazd and Spenta Armaiti

Source Pahlavi traditions cited in Shaki and Cereti; Bundahishn variant manuscriptsCulture Zoroastrian (minority tradition)Period Late Sasanian / early Islamic Pahlavi

Summary

In this tradition, Ahura Mazda (Ohrmazd) marries his daughter Spenta Armaiti (the Earth, Amesha Spenta) and she gives birth to Keyumars. Later she gives birth to Mashya and Mashyana. Thus Keyumars, Mashya and Mashyana share the same mother and are siblings, not father-children. This is noted as a considered tradition but not the mainstream Bundahishn account.

Participants

Ohrmazd

Spenta Armaiti

Gayōmart

Mashya

Mashyana

Consequences

Creates theological problem of divine incest, discussed by scholars as cosmogonic incest motif common in Indo-European myth (earth as daughter-wife). Highlights fluidity of Pahlavi cosmogony and need to separate attested vs. disputed layers.

Variants

Mainstream Bundahishn rejects this and keeps direct creation from mud. Islamic sources do not preserve this variant.

Keyumars as First Shah - The Pēšdād and the Leopard-Skin People

Source Ferdowsi, Shahnameh, Pādshāhi-ye Kayūmars (Khaleghi-Motlagh vol.1); Davis translationCulture Persian (Islamic-era national epic)Period c. 1010 CE (Ghaznavid period)

Summary

Ferdowsi opens with a bardic question: who first wore the crown? Keyumars arose first among humans who lived in mountain caves and wore leopard skins. He was the first to establish kingship, royal custom, food preparation, and justice (pēšdād). God granted him farr. All humans and animals paid homage. He ruled from the mountains in glory. His beloved son Siāmak was hated by Ahriman, who raised a demonic army under his own demonic son (the Black Div). The angel Sorush warned Keyumars. Siāmak led an army, accepted single combat, and was killed by the demon. Keyumars mourned a year, then on Sorush's counsel sent Siāmak's son Hushang, now grown, who defeated, bound and beheaded Ahriman's son. Keyumars died after a 30-year reign, leaving throne to Hushang, founder of the Pishdadian dynasty.

Participants

Keyumars

Siāmak (son)

Hushang (grandson)

Ahriman

Ahriman's demonic son (Div-e Siyah)

Sorush (Sraosha)

Humans and animals of the mountain

Consequences

Establishes the Pishdadian (Pishdad) dynasty and the model of just kingship; introduces the farr concept into epic history; sets up the eternal Iran vs. demonic chaos conflict; Hushang's succession continues civilization (discovery of fire, etc.).

Variants

Pahlavi Gayōmart has no son Siāmak or grandson Hushang; these are epic genealogies. Some Shahnameh manuscripts vary the demon's name and the mourning period.

The Election of Keyumars - Rationalized Constitutional Origin

Source Al-Masudi, Muruj al-Dhahab vol.1; also cited in SafaCulture Early Islamic historiography (Arabic)Period c. 947 CE (Abbasid period)

Summary

Al-Masudi reports that between Adam and Noah people spoke Syriac and obeyed no king. Keyumars was simply the most powerful and noble among his contemporaries. The people recognized that revolt, envy, tyranny and hatred are innate in man and only fear of authority can restrain them. After deliberation they came to Keyumars and said: 'You are the greatest and noblest among us, the last descendant of our common father, with no equal in this age. Take charge of our affairs and become our leader; we will offer respect, obedience and absolute submission.' Keyumars accepted after making them swear to obey and renounce revolt. Thus kingship is presented as social contract, not divine creation.

Participants

Keyumars

Proto-human community between Adam and Noah

Consequences

Demystifies mythic first king into political theory; reflects Mu'tazilite and Iranian-Islamic ideas of kingship by consent; contrasts with Shahnameh's divine farr model.

Variants

Tabari gives a different historicized version (see next). Ferdowsi ignores election theory.

Tabari's Historicized Keyumars at Dunbawand with Thirty Wives

Source Al-Tabari, Tarikh al-Rusul wa al-Muluk vol.1; Rosenthal translationCulture Early Islamic historiographyPeriod c. 915 CE

Summary

Tabari, after noting disputed identifications (Adam, son of Adam, Gomer) and refusing to adjudicate genealogy, describes Kayumarth as a long-lived lord (mu'ammar) who settled in the mountains of Dunbawand (Damavand) in Tabaristan. He had 30 wives, and his two favorite children were Mari and Mariyanah, whom he promoted; their descendants became the later kings of Persia. This euhemerizes the rhubarb twins Mashya/Mashyana into human children Mari/Mariyanah and multiplies wives to explain tribal origins.

Participants

Kayumarth

30 wives

Mari (son)

Mariyanah (son/daughter variant)

Descendant Persian kings

Consequences

Attempts to fit Keyumars into biblical-Islamic chronology and geography; localizes mythic Erān-vēj to real Damavand; transforms cosmogonic plant birth into polygamous human genealogy; Tabari's agnosticism about pedigree models scholarly caution.

Variants

Masudi's election vs. Tabari's mountain lord; Shahnameh's Siāmak/Hushang vs. Tabari's Mari/Mariyanah. Modern scholars see Mari/Mariyanah as Arabic rendering of Mashya/Mashyana.

Relationships
Keyumars→Ahura Mazda / Ohrmazd (Hormozd)creator

Creator and father-figure who creates Gayōmart from mud in the sixth Gāh to be his helper against Ahriman, and grants him farr in the Shahnameh. In variant tradition he is also his father via Spenta Armaiti. He also repels the Dīvs after 30 centuries and gives Gayōmart information via Zoroaster in Varshtmānsar Nask.

Keyumars→Spenta Armaiti (Spendarmad, Earth)family

Amesha Spenta of earth, daughter of Ohrmazd. In standard Bundahishn she receives Gayōmart's seed as earth. In variant tradition she is his mother (via Ohrmazd) and later mother of Mashya/Mashyana, making Gayōmart their brother.

Keyumars→Gavevagdat (Gav-e Evagdād, Primordial Ox)ally

The sole living companion of Gayōmart, created from mud on the opposite bank of Veh-Dāit. They live together 3000 years in peace. Its death in Ahriman's assault is the first death in creation and is linked to Nowruz symbolism and Persepolis bull iconography.

Keyumars→Mashya and Mashyana (Mashyag and Mashyanag)family

First human pair who grow as rhubarb from Gayōmart's sun-purified semen after 40 years. In mainstream they are his children/descendants and ancestors of all humans. In variant they are his siblings via Spenta Armaiti. In Tabari they appear historicized as Mari and Mariyanah, favorite children of Kayumarth with 30 wives.

Keyumars→Siāmak (Siyāmak)family

Beloved son of Keyumars in Shahnameh, beloved of all except Ahriman. Leads army against Ahriman's demonic son, accepts single combat and is killed, causing Keyumars's year-long mourning. No Pahlavi equivalent; his name means 'dark-eyed' or 'black-haired'.

Keyumars→Hushang (Haošyaŋha, Hōshang)family

Son of Siāmak, grandson of Keyumars. Grown by the time of vengeance, he leads the second army on Sorush's counsel, defeats and beheads Ahriman's son, and succeeds Keyumars to the throne as second Pishdadian king, later discoverer of fire and civilization.

Keyumars→Ahriman (Angra Mainyu, Gannag Menog)hostile

Evil spirit who after 3000-year stupor is awakened by Jēh, assaults creation as dragon on Nowruz, creates death and noxious creatures, kills Gavevagdat, and assigns Astovidat to guard Gayōmart. In Shahnameh he raises a demonic army under his son to destroy Keyumars's line out of envy.

Keyumars→Astovidat (Astwihad, Demon of Death)hostile

Dīv left by Ahriman to guard Gayōmart and prevent his premature death or rescue; embodies the inevitability of death at fated time. Guards him for 30 years until his appointed death.

Keyumars→Sorush (Sraoša, Surush)ally

Avestan angel of obedience and communication (Sraoša), who warns Keyumars of Ahriman's plot, advises mourning period, and counsels the second war via Hushang. Acts as divine messenger between Yazdan and the first king.

Keyumars→Jēh (Jahi, the Demonic Whore)hostile

Female demon whose cry awakens Ahriman from his 3000-year stupor induced by Ohrmazd's Ahunawar prayer, triggering the assault on Gayōmart and Gavevagdat. Represents lust and pollution in Zoroastrian demonology.

Primordial Death and Demonic Assault

Source Bundahishn 4; Shahnameh (Siāmak episode)

Description

Ahriman's attack introduces death into a previously deathless creation. Gavevagdat is killed, and Gayōmart is mortally wounded but lingers 30 years under demonic guard before dying. The assault includes creation of death, illness, thirst, hunger and Xrafstra (noxious creatures). Ferdowsi's version adds Siāmak's death in single combat and Hushang's beheading of the demon prince.

Scholarly Context

Scholars read this as Zoroastrian theodicy: evil is external assault, not inherent in creation. The Nowruz timing links death to renewal. No sanitization; violence is cosmologically necessary.

Variant Divine Incest - Ohrmazd and Spenta Armaiti

Source Shaki 2000; Cereti 2015 citing Pahlavi traditions

Description

A Pahlavi variant states Ohrmazd married his daughter Spenta Armaiti (Earth) who bore Gayōmart and later Mashya/Mashyana, making Gayōmart both son and brother to humanity's ancestors. This is cosmogonic incest, paralleling other Indo-European earth-daughter-wife motifs.

Scholarly Context

Encyclopaedia Iranica treats this as a minority tradition, not mainstream Bundahishn. It reflects mythic logic where earth is both mother and wife of the sky-father, not a moral prescription. Modern retellings often omit it.

Warning: Semen, Corpse and Rhubarb Birth — Show unsanitized · Archive policy & legal

Semen, Corpse and Rhubarb Birth

Source Bundahishn 14.1-7

Description

Gayōmart falls on his left side at death and his semen spills to earth, fertilized by sun, growing after 40 years as rhubarb from which Mashya/Mashyana emerge. The image combines corpse pollution (nasu), seminal impurity and vegetative birth, requiring solar purification.

Scholarly Context

Zoroastrian purity laws view corpse and semen as polluting; solar purification resolves this. The plant-human origin links humanity to the vegetable kingdom and explains rhubarb's ritual associations. Described plainly in sources without eroticization.

Warning: Historicized Polygamy and Child Favoritism — Show unsanitized · Archive policy & legal

Historicized Polygamy and Child Favoritism

Source Tabari vol.1, pp.171-175

Description

Tabari's historicized Kayumarth has 30 wives and elevates two favorite children Mari/Mariyanah over others, whose descendants become kings. This implies polygamous household and dynastic preference that marginalizes other offspring.

Scholarly Context

Tabari is euhemerizing myth into tribal genealogy; the 30 wives may reflect etiology for Iranian tribes. No further detail on wives' identities survives.

Contradictions & Disputes

Is Gayōmart the progenitor of Mashya/Mashyana or their brother?

Position A

Mainstream Bundahishn: Gayōmart's semen after death, purified by sun, grows as rhubarb after 40 years into Mashya and Mashyana, who are his children/descendants and first human couple.

Position A — evidence

Greater Bundahishn 14.1-7, TD2 manuscript; Shaki 2000 summarizes as standard; supported by Vendidad's Mashya/Mashyana as first mortals after Gayōmart.

Position B

Variant Pahlavi tradition: Ohrmazd marries his daughter Spenta Armaiti; she bears Gayōmart and later bears Mashya and Mashyana, making all three siblings, not parent-child.

Position B — evidence

Traditions cited by Cereti 2015 and Shaki 2000 as 'in other Zoroastrian traditions'; no extant full text, only references in later Pahlavi compilations.

Scholarly Consensus

Shaki and Cereti treat A as mainstream and B as minority variant; both are attested Pahlavi traditions. Modern scholarship flags the contradiction without harmonizing; B may reflect older cosmogonic incest pattern.

Is Keyumars a sexless archetype or a male king with family?

Position A

Avestan and Pahlavi cosmogony: Gayōmart is neither male nor female, or androgynous prototype, sexless first human, without wife or human children.

Position A — evidence

Avesta Yasna 26.4; Bundahishn creation from mud as 15-year-old youth without gendered reproduction; Denkard summary.

Position B

Shahnameh and Islamic histories: Keyumars is male, first king, father of Siāmak (Ferdowsi) or of 30 wives' children Mari/Mariyanah (Tabari), grandfather of Hushang.

Position B — evidence

Ferdowsi Shahnameh opening; Tabari vol.1; Masudi election narrative - all Islamic-era historicizations requiring male monarch with lineage.

Scholarly Consensus

Layers are diachronic, not contradictory in original contexts. Encyclopaedia Iranica distinguishes mythic archetype (early) from epic king (later). Harmonization is modern error.

Identification with biblical figures

Position A

Tabari reports some identify Kayumarth with Adam, or a son of Adam, or Gomer son of Japheth, attempting to fit Iranian myth into Genesis genealogy.

Position A — evidence

Tabari vol.1 pp.171-172; later Islamic universal histories repeat Gomer equation.

Position B

Tabari himself declines to decide, stating pedigree disputes are not his book's purpose; modern scholarship rejects equation, seeing Gayōmart as Indo-Iranian first man distinct from Semitic Adam.

Position B — evidence

Shaki, Cereti, and comparative Indo-European studies (Manu, Ymir parallels) treat Gayōmart as independent cosmogonic figure; Avestan chronology incompatible with Genesis.

Scholarly Consensus

Equation is medieval syncretism, not supported by primary Avestan/Pahlavi evidence. Tabari's agnosticism is praised as scholarly caution.

Duration of life/reign - 30 years vs 3000 years

Position A

Pahlavi: 3000-year stillness without needs, then 30 years of dying under Astovidat's guard after Ahriman's assault.

Position A — evidence

Bundahishn 1-4; Safa summary; Denkard Varshtmānsar fragment: 'For 30 centuries I kept world... Dīvs assaulted Gayōmart'.

Position B

Shahnameh: 30-year reign as king (pādshāhi-ye si sāl), with no 3000-year period mentioned; death after Hushang's vengeance.

Position B — evidence

Ferdowsi Khaleghi-Motlagh 1:48-49; Davis translation: 'He ruled thirty years'.

Scholarly Consensus

Different chronologies belong to different genres (cosmogony vs epic history). Pahlavi 30 years is dying period; Ferdowsi 30 years is reign. Not to be conflated.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

No extant Avestan manuscript gives a continuous Gayō Maretan narrative; reconstruction depends on fragmentary Yasna/Yasht/Vendidad references and later Pahlavi systematization. The Chihrdad and Varshtmānsar Nasks are lost; only Denkard summaries survive, so details of Ohrmazd's 30-century protection and Varshtmānsar dialogue are second-hand. Pahlavi manuscripts (TD1, TD2, DH) vary and have not been fully collated for Gayōmart passages in this dossier. Variant parentage via Spenta Armaiti is cited by Shaki/Cereti without full primary text citation - original Pahlavi passage needs verification. No archaeological image securely identified as Gayōmart before Safavid miniatures; Persepolis bull = Gavevagdat link is interpretive (Safa) not proven. Islamic sources (Tabari, Masudi) use Arabic renderings (Kayumarth, Mari/Mariyanah) with uncertain vocalization. Living Zoroastrian oral tradition and Parsi Rivayats not consulted. No verified openly licensed image of Gayōmart exists beyond public-domain Shah Tahmasp miniatures (rights need museum verification). Language limitation: Avestan and Pahlavi consulted via translations, not direct philological analysis.
