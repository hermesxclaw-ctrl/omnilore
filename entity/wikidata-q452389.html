Form

No physical description of Amaethon survives in any source. He is a shadowy figure, known more for his actions and lineage than any personal attributes. He is likely envisioned as a divine farmer or a powerful warrior, but this is pure speculation.

Powers

Divine Agriculture As the 'Divine Ploughman', Amaethon's primary power is over cultivation, harvest, and the land's bounty. He is a personification of the agricultural cycle. Inferred from his name and lineage. No specific limitations are recorded. None.

Liminal Theft / Control over Otherworldly Creatures Amaethon successfully stole a dog, a roebuck, and a lapwing from the King of the Otherworld. This demonstrates an ability to operate between worlds and take what is not rightfully his, challenging the boundaries of Annwn. The Four Branches of the Mabinogi and 'Cad Goddeu'. His thefts directly led to a catastrophic war, suggesting his actions have significant and dangerous consequences. None.

Instigator of the Battle of the Trees In 'Cad Goddeu', Amaethon is the primary catalyst for the legendary battle, where Gwydion animates trees to fight as an army. Amaethon's role is to have started the conflict, making him a powerful agent of change and chaos. Book of Taliesin, 'Cad Goddeu'. The nature of his power in this context is unclear; it is more an act of provocation than a display of direct force. The poem's obscurity means some scholars interpret the battle as an allegory for other conflicts, making Amaethon's role less literal.

Behavior & Habitat

His actions in the myths portray him as a clever, disruptive, and daring figure. He and his brother Gwydion are the instigators of the war with Annwn, suggesting a bold and perhaps mischievous nature. He is a catalyst for conflict, though his personal motives are never explained.

Accounts

Amaethon and his brother Gwydion, the great magician, journeyed to Annwn and stole three magical animals from King Arawn: a dog, a roebuck, and a lapwing. Arawn, enraged by this transgression, marched his forces into the mortal world to reclaim them. This led to a great war between the Children of Dôn (led by Gwydion and Amaethon) and the Children of Llyr (led by Arawn). The war was only ended when a man named Lleu Llaw Gyffes was created by the magic of Math and Gwydion to fight on their behalf.

This enigmatic poem recounts a battle instigated by Amaethon. Gwydion, through his magic, animates the trees of the forest to fight as an army. The poem lists the various trees and their roles in the battle (e.g., the alder fought in the front, the willow was late to arrive). The battle is against the forces of Annwn, led by Arawn. The poem is highly allegorical and its precise meaning is a subject of intense scholarly debate, with interpretations ranging from a mythical conflict to a metaphor for a historical battle or a cosmological event.

Scholarly Debates
The Nature of the Battle of the Trees

Potential Identification with Mars
Amaethon may have been a god of war, identified with the Roman Mars. ⇌ Amaethon is a god of agriculture, and any martial role is secondary or derived from his actions in myth.
Evidence for A: A single, damaged inscription from Roman Britain has been speculatively read as 'Mars Amaethon'.
Evidence for B: His name's etymology is clearly agricultural. His role in the war is as an instigator, not a warrior.
What We Don't Know

The primary gap is the lack of any independent mythology for Amaethon. He is a figure defined by his name and his actions in the stories of others. There is no evidence of a cult, no iconography, and no folklore associated with him. The full meaning of his role in 'Cad Goddeu' is likely to remain obscure. The speculative Roman inscription linking him to Mars is unverified. Research is limited by the small corpus of surviving Welsh mythological texts.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Amaethon as the original 'catalyst' character in a fantasy epic. He's not the hero, the villain, or the wizard—he's the guy who steals the MacGuffin in the first act, setting the entire plot in motion before disappearing into the background. He's the divine version of 'the inciting incident'. His story is not about him, but everything that happens because of him.
