Who It Is

In the pantheon of Welsh myth, Amaethon is the quiet hand on the plough, the divine farmer whose name whispers of tilled soil and golden harvests. But this god of the field also holds a darker, more disruptive power: he is the thief who stole from the King of the Otherworld, a single act of trespass that ignited a war between the divine families of Britain—a war so vast that the very trees of the forest rose up to fight.

His actions in the myths portray him as a clever, disruptive, and daring figure. He and his brother Gwydion are the instigators of the war with Annwn, suggesting a bold and perhaps mischievous nature. He is a catalyst for conflict, though his personal motives are never explained.

Powers
Divine Agriculture

As the 'Divine Ploughman', Amaethon's primary power is over cultivation, harvest, and the land's bounty. He is a personification of the agricultural cycle.

Limit: No specific limitations are recorded.

Liminal Theft / Control over Otherworldly Creatures

Amaethon successfully stole a dog, a roebuck, and a lapwing from the King of the Otherworld. This demonstrates an ability to operate between worlds and take what is not rightfully his, challenging the boundaries of Annwn.

Limit: His thefts directly led to a catastrophic war, suggesting his actions have significant and dangerous consequences.

Instigator of the Battle of the Trees

In 'Cad Goddeu', Amaethon is the primary catalyst for the legendary battle, where Gwydion animates trees to fight as an army. Amaethon's role is to have started the conflict, making him a powerful agent of change and chaos.

Limit: The nature of his power in this context is unclear; it is more an act of provocation than a display of direct force.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No physical description of Amaethon survives in any source. He is a shadowy figure, known more for his actions and lineage than any personal attributes. He is likely envisioned as a divine farmer or a powerful warrior, but this is pure speculation.

Symbols — No specific symbols are attested. However, by association, the plough, the roebuck, and the lapwing (the animals he stole from Annwn) could be considered his symbols, representing his agricultural and liminal powers.

Domains — Agriculture, cultivation, and ploughing. His name is his primary domain. He is a god associated with the land's fertility and the human (or divine) act of making it productive. He is also a figure of the 'Otherworld' boundary, as his thefts from Annwn blur the lines between worlds.

Accounts I
The Theft from Annwn and the War with the Children of LlyrThe Four Branches of the Mabinogi (Math fab Mathonwy)

The tale was written down in the medieval period, but reflects earlier oral traditions.

Amaethon and his brother Gwydion, the great magician, journeyed to Annwn and stole three magical animals from King Arawn: a dog, a roebuck, and a lapwing. Arawn, enraged by this transgression, marched his forces into the mortal world to reclaim them. This led to a great war between the Children of Dôn (led by Gwydion and Amaethon) and the Children of Llyr (led by Arawn). The war was only ended when a man named Lleu Llaw Gyffes was created by the magic of Math and Gwydion to fight on their behalf. The war resulted in the creation of Lleu Llaw Gyffes, a major hero of Welsh mythology. It also established a permanent enmity between the two divine families and solidified the boundary between the mortal world and Annwn.

Accounts II
The Battle of the Trees (Cad Goddeu)Book of Taliesin

The poem is preserved in a 14th-century manuscript but is likely much older.

This enigmatic poem recounts a battle instigated by Amaethon. Gwydion, through his magic, animates the trees of the forest to fight as an army. The poem lists the various trees and their roles in the battle (e.g., the alder fought in the front, the willow was late to arrive). The battle is against the forces of Annwn, led by Arawn. The poem is highly allegorical and its precise meaning is a subject of intense scholarly debate, with interpretations ranging from a mythical conflict to a metaphor for a historical battle or a cosmological event. The battle's outcome is not clearly stated in the poem, but it is implied to be a victory for the Children of Dôn. The poem is a cornerstone of Welsh mythological poetry.

Amaethon is a major, well-attested god with a rich mythology. — Amaethon is a minor, shadowy figure. He appears in only a few key episodes and has no independent myths or cult. His importance is largely derived from his association with Gwydion and the Children of Dôn.

Amaethon's theft was a heroic act of rebellion against the Otherworld. — The texts do not frame his theft as heroic. It is presented as a transgression that leads to a devastating war. His motives are never explained, and the act is simply a catalyst for conflict.

The 'Battle of the Trees' is a straightforward story about an army of sentient trees. — The only source for this battle is the extremely obscure and allegorical poem 'Cad Goddeu'. The literal interpretation is just one of many, and the poem's meaning is heavily debated.
