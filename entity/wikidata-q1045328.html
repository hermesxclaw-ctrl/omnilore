The Approach

In the grand assembly halls of Mahāyāna sūtras, the Buddha is often surrounded by more than human monks and nuns. Among the vast audience are eight classes of celestial and chthonic beings—gods, dragons, celestial musicians, and hungry spirits—who have gathered not as adversaries but as protectors of the Dharma. Known as the Eight Legions (Aṣṭasenā), this motley host bridges the divine and the demonic, the heavenly and the earthly, and their presence underscores the universality of the Buddhist path.

Content advisory. This entry discusses mythological beings that include figures traditionally considered warlike or demonic (e.g., Asuras, Yakṣas). Some sources may contain violent imagery, but this article focuses on their role in Buddhist cosmology and does not include graphic content.

Eight Legions

Etymology

The Sanskrit term Aṣṭasenā (अष्टसेना) is a compound of aṣṭa (अष्ट, 'eight') and senā (सेना, 'army' or 'legion'). The term 'senā' is often translated as 'army' in a general sense, but the source notes that the Eight Legions are each composed of several thousand foot soldiers, a unit size more precisely termed 'dhvajinī' (a body of troops bearing a standard). The Chinese translation '八部衆' (bābù zhòng) literally means 'eight divisions of beings'.

Cultural Ownership

The Eight Legions are a pan-Buddhist concept originating in ancient India, transmitted to East Asia (China, Japan, Korea, Vietnam) and Tibet, and now present globally through Buddhist diaspora and popular culture. The concept is primarily Mahāyāna, but similar groupings exist in other traditions.

Disputed Identifications

The exact composition of the Eight Legions varies across texts and traditions. The most common list includes Deva, Nāga, Yakṣa, Gandharva, Asura, Garuḍa, Kiṁnara, and Mahoraga. However, the Kōfuku-ji statue group substitutes Śuddhāvāsa (a deva) and Kumbhāṇḍa for the standard Deva and Yakṣa, indicating regional and doctrinal variations. Some lists also include other beings like Kinnaras or Garudas in different orders.

Description

Appearance

The Eight Legions are a diverse group of beings. Deva (gods) are often depicted as radiant, celestial beings. Nāgas are serpentine, often half-human, half-snake. Yakṣas are nature spirits, sometimes fierce. Gandharvas are celestial musicians, often depicted as handsome. Asuras are titanic beings, often warlike. Garuḍas are eagle-like birds. Kiṁnaras are part-human, part-bird or part-horse. Mahoragas are great serpents or beings with serpentine lower halves.

Symbols & Iconography

In art, the Eight Legions are often depicted as a group surrounding the Buddha. Each class has its own iconographic attributes: Devas may hold lotus or fly whisk; Nāgas often have a serpent canopy; Garuḍas have wings and beak; Asuras may have multiple arms and weapons; Gandharvas play musical instruments; Kiṁnaras are often shown with musical instruments or as celestial musicians.

Behaviour & Personality

As protectors of the Dharma, they are generally benevolent towards Buddhism, though some (like Asuras) are known for their pride and warlike nature. They are present at Buddha's teachings, listen to the Dharma, and vow to protect it and its followers.

Domains & Habitats

They inhabit various realms: Devas in the heavens (especially the Cāturmahārājakāyika heaven), Nāgas in oceans and underworlds, Yakṣas on earth and in forests, Gandharvas in the celestial realm of music, Asuras in their own realm (Asura-loka), Garuḍas in the sky, Kiṁnaras in the celestial realms, and Mahoragas in the earth or underworld.

Version Differences

The standard list includes Deva, Nāga, Yakṣa, Gandharva, Asura, Garuḍa, Kiṁnara, and Mahoraga. The Kōfuku-ji variant replaces Deva with Śuddhāvāsa (a specific deva) and Yakṣa with Kumbhāṇḍa. Other variations may include different orders or substitutions.

Powers & Abilities

Dharma protection

Source Version Mahāyāna sūtras, including Lotus Sutra and Golden Light Sutra

Description

The primary function of the Eight Legions is to protect the Buddhist Dharma and its practitioners. They are often depicted as guardians who ward off evil and obstacles.

Limitations

They are not omnipotent; their power is subordinate to the Buddha and the Dharma.

Contradictions

None noted.

Supernatural abilities

Source Version Buddhist cosmology and folklore

Description

Each class possesses its own supernatural abilities: Devas have divine powers and longevity; Nāgas can control weather and shape-shift; Yakṣas have magical powers and can appear or disappear; Gandharvas can produce enchanting music; Asuras have great strength and combat skills; Garuḍas can fly at incredible speeds and are enemies of Nāgas; Kiṁnaras can sing beautifully; Mahoragas have serpentine powers.

Limitations

These abilities are often limited by their karma and realm.

Contradictions

Some abilities are shared or overlap, but each class has distinct traits.

Stories & Myths

The Assembly at Vulture Peak

Source Lotus SutraCulture Indian, Mahāyāna BuddhistPeriod circa 1st century BCE – 2nd century CE

Summary

In the Lotus Sutra, the Buddha delivers a sermon at Vulture Peak, and the Eight Legions are among the vast assembly of beings who listen to the teaching. Their presence signifies the universality of the Dharma, reaching all realms of existence.

Participants

Buddha Śākyamuni

Eight Legions

Bodhisattvas

Arhats

Other beings

Consequences

The Eight Legions become witnesses to the Buddha's teachings and are inspired to protect the Dharma.

Variants

Similar assemblies are described in other Mahāyāna sūtras, with slight variations in the list of beings.

The Golden Light Sutra's Protection

Source Golden Light SutraCulture Indian, Mahāyāna BuddhistPeriod circa 2nd to 5th century CE

Summary

The Golden Light Sutra recounts the Buddha's teachings on the importance of protecting the state and the Dharma. The Eight Legions are among the deities who vow to protect the kings and countries that uphold this sutra.

Participants

Consequences

The Eight Legions become associated with protective rituals and state protection.

Variants

In East Asian Buddhism, this sutra was used in state-protection ceremonies.

Four Heavenly Kings (Lokapālas)

Type allySource Buddhist cosmology

Description

The Eight Legions often serve under the Four Heavenly Kings, who are themselves protectors of the Dharma.

Version Note

The Eight Legions are sometimes considered subordinates of the Lokapālas.

Conflicting Accounts

Some lists include the Lokapālas as part of the Eight Legions, but they are usually separate.

Dharma protectors (Dharmapālas)

Type allySource Buddhist iconography

Description

The Eight Legions are a subset of Dharmapālas, but the term Dharmapāla is broader and includes other deities like Mahākāla.

Version Note

In Vajrayāna, Dharmapālas may be wrathful deities; the Eight Legions are generally not wrathful.

Conflicting Accounts

None noted.

Asuras and Devas

Type hostileSource Buddhist mythology

Description

Asuras are traditionally enemies of the Devas, but in the Eight Legions they are united as protectors of the Dharma, transcending their usual conflict.

Version Note

The inclusion of Asuras in the Eight Legions symbolizes the reconciliation of opposing forces.

Conflicting Accounts

Some traditions still depict Asuras as hostile to Devas.

Contradictions & Disputes

Composition of the Eight Legions

Position A

The standard list includes Deva, Nāga, Yakṣa, Gandharva, Asura, Garuḍa, Kiṁnara, and Mahoraga.

Position A — evidence

Common in Mahāyāna sūtras and Chinese/Japanese Buddhism.

Position B

The Kōfuku-ji list replaces Deva with Śuddhāvāsa and Yakṣa with Kumbhāṇḍa.

Position B — evidence

Actual statue group at Kōfuku-ji.

Scholarly Consensus

There is no single canonical list; variations exist across texts and regions.

Nature of Asuras

Position A

Asuras are demonic beings, enemies of the gods.

Position A — evidence

Hindu mythology and Buddhist cosmology often depict Asuras as hostile.

Position B

Asuras can be protectors of the Dharma when they are converted.

Position B — evidence

In the Eight Legions, Asuras are included as protectors, and some sources describe them as having both positive and negative aspects.

Scholarly Consensus

Asuras have a dual nature; they are capable of both good and evil.
