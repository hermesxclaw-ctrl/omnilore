Who It Is

Inari is among the most popular and widely worshipped kami in Shinto, the kami of rice, prosperity, fertility, tea, sake, and agriculture. Inari is also associated with foxes (kitsune), who serve as Inari's messengers. Unlike most major kami, Inari has no fixed gender and is depicted as male, female, or androgynous depending on the tradition. Inari's principal shrine is Fushimi Inari Taisha in Kyoto, famous for its thousands of vermilion torii gates that form tunnels up Mount Inari, created by donations from worshippers. Inari's worship spread far beyond agriculture: the kami became associated with commerce, industry, and general prosperity. Blacksmiths, merchants, entertainers, and sake brewers all prayed to Inari. The kitsune (fox) statues at Inari shrines are often seen wearing red bibs, and offerings of inari sushi (fried tofu pockets) and sake are common. Inari's cult is one of the few that successfully transitioned from ancient Shinto to modern Japan — Inari shrines can be found in urban business districts and even atop skyscrapers. The kami embodies the Japanese archetype of abundance through hard work and respect for the land. Inari is often syncretized with the Buddhist goddess Dakini.

Origins

Inari's origins are ancient and multiple. The kami appears early in Shinto tradition as Ukanomitama (kami of food). The cult of Inari at Fushimi dates to 711 CE, when the kami was said to have appeared on Mount Inari. The fox connection came from folk beliefs that foxes were divine beings who protected rice fields.
