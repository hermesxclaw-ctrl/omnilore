Who It Is

Inari Ōkami, the enigmatic kami of rice, foxes, and prosperity, is one of the most beloved and widely worshipped deities in Japan. With over 30,000 shrines dedicated to this shape-shifting spirit, Inari's presence permeates Japanese culture, from ancient agricultural rites to modern business success. Yet, despite this ubiquity, Inari remains a figure of profound mystery, their origins shrouded in the mists of time and their identity fluid across centuries.

Inari is considered benevolent, granting prosperity, fertility, and success. They are also a protector of warriors and merchants. The kami is thought to be responsive to sincere prayers.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Inari' is often interpreted as 'rice carrier' (ine + nari), but alternative theories suggest 'grain grower' or derivation from the place name Inari, where the first shrine was established.

Pronunciation — ee-NAH-ree

Aliases — Inari, Oinari, Inari Daimyojin, Uka no Mitama no Kami, Toyouke Ōkami

Ownership — Japan, Shinto

Form & Signs

Appearance — Inari is depicted in various forms: as an old man, a young woman, or an androgynous figure. Often shown riding a white fox or accompanied by foxes. In art, they may hold a sheaf of rice, a jewel, or a key to the granary.

Symbols — Foxes (kitsune), rice, Mount Inari, keys, jewels (tama), and the color red (vermilion) associated with shrines.

Domains — Rice cultivation, agriculture, fertility, prosperity, business success, blacksmithing, swords, and protection. Inari's shrines are often located on mountains or near rice paddies.

Accounts I
The Origin of Inari ShrineYamashiro no Kuni Fudoki (fragment)

8th century (recorded)

A wealthy man named Mago-no-ushi made rice cakes, which turned into a white fox. The fox then led him to the mountain, where Inari appeared, declaring the spot sacred. Establishment of the Inari shrine on Mount Inari.

Accounts II
Inari and the FoxFolklore

Various

Inari is often accompanied by white foxes, considered messengers. They are believed to serve Inari and can possess people or cause mischief. Foxes are revered and feared; they are seen as Inari's agents.

Inari is only a fox deity. — Inari is a kami of agriculture and prosperity; foxes are messengers, not the deity itself.

Inari is exclusively a Shinto deity. — Inari has been syncretized with Buddhism (Dakiniten) and has been worshipped in both traditions.
