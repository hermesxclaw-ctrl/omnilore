Who It Is

The word hits like a raised eyebrow — bizarre — a label for the crooked, the costumed, the beautifully unaccountable. From a 17th-century French insult to a fetish magazine hidden in plain brown wrappers, a late-night Canadian sketch show, and a British newsstand bible of tattoos and UFOs, 'Bizarre' has never described one thing for long. It is a moving target for whatever a culture decides is too strange to be normal, and too fascinating to ignore.

Lexically, 'bizarre' behaves as a subjective evaluative adjective; what counts as bizarre is culturally contingent and historically mobile. What was bizarre in 1650 (a slashed doublet) differs from 1946 (a latex catsuit) or 1997 (a split tongue). As a title, projects named Bizarre perform deliberate outsider identity: they collect, display, and monetize the fringe. Editorial voice across both magazines is arch, amused, and encyclopedic - cataloguing the strange rather than explaining it away. Semantic Marking of Otherness

As an adjective, 'bizarre' performs the social power of marking a person, object, or behavior as outside normative categories. It does not describe objective properties but signals the speaker's cultural frame. Attested from 1648 in English (OED citation: Evelyn).

Limit: Highly subjective; overuse dilutes force. Considered informal in academic writing; scholars prefer specific descriptors. Cannot be falsified.

Subcultural Archiving and Legitimation

Both Bizarre magazines (Willie and British) exercised the ability to collect, photograph, and publish fringe practices (fetish fashion, body modification, occult, UFOlogy) and thereby move them from private rumor to documented subculture. British Bizarre in particular gave early mainstream coverage to tattoo, piercing, and suspension communities.

Limit: Legitimation was double-edged: increased visibility but also sensationalized and commodified. British Bizarre criticized for 'freak show' framing. Distribution limited by obscenity laws (Willie) and newsstand placement (British).

Broadcast Transgression within Censorship Limits

Bizarre (TV series) used sketch comedy to present sexual innuendo, cross-dressing, and stunt-based humor on advertiser-supported television, pushing boundaries of what could be shown on CTV/Showtime in early 1980s while remaining within broadcast standards via double entendre and the 'Super Dave' daredevil parody.

Limit: Content now dated; jokes reliant on 1980s gender stereotypes. American syndication edited for content. No supernatural or literal 'powers' - comedic performance only.

Accounts I
The Bearded Etymology: From Bizza to BizarreOxford English Dictionary; Bloch & von Wartburg Dictionnaire étymologique de la langue française; Etymonline

16th-17th century (first French attestations c. 1570s; English borrowing 1648)

Italian bizzarro appears in the 1500s meaning 'angry, choleric, capricious' from bizza 'fit of temper.' French borrows it as bizarre 'odd, fantastic.' English writers like John Evelyn (1648) import it to describe continental architecture and dress that seemed grotesquely irregular to English eyes. A rival 19th-century theory proposes Basque bizar 'beard' as source, via Spanish bizarro 'brave, bearded man' - the bearded soldier looked strange or fierce to others. Modern etymologists treat the Basque link as possible but unproven, leaving the origin as 'uncertain.' The word's own history is thus bizarre: a term for strangeness with a strange, unsettled parentage. Establishes 'bizarre' as a loanword marking foreignness itself; sets up centuries of use as a label for the exoticized other.

John Willie's Bizarre: The Fetish Magazine That Invented an AestheticBizarre No. 1-26 (1946-1959); Belier Press reprints; Taschen complete reprint (2009); Valerie Steele scholarship on fetish history

1946-1959

Cartoonist and fetish artist John Alexander Scott Coutts (as 'John Willie') launches Bizarre in Montreal and later New York as a mail-order magazine for bondage, high-heel, and latex enthusiasts. Mixing his own precise line drawings (notably the serial 'Sweet Gwendoline'), reader letters, and photographs, Willie creates a coherent visual language for what would later be called BDSM aesthetics. Published irregularly and sold under the counter due to obscenity laws, it runs to 26 issues before Willie's health fails (he dies 1962). Its mailing list and art become foundational for later fetish publications (AtomAge, Skin Two) and for the iconography later borrowed by mainstream fashion. Codifies fetish iconography (corsetry, bondage, stiletto) that persists in popular culture; creates first sustained English-language forum for fetish community self-recognition.

Bizarre on Late-Night TV: John Byner's Sketch CircusCTV broadcast logs; Showtime press kits; Library and Archives Canada; contemporary reviews (Toronto Star, Variety 1980-1986)

1979-1986

After a 1979 pilot 'Super Bizarre,' CTV launches Bizarre as a weekly sketch comedy hosted by impressionist John Byner, produced by Bob Einstein (later Super Dave) and Allan Blye. Filmed in Toronto with a live audience, it features rapid blackout sketches, celebrity impressions, and the breakout recurring character Super Dave Osborne (Einstein), a delusional stuntman whose elaborate stunts inevitably fail. Syndicated to Showtime in the US, it runs 141 episodes and becomes a staple of early pay-cable late-night, noted for risqué humor that pushed broadcast standards without explicit nudity, relying on innuendo and Byner's range of voices. Launches Super Dave franchise (later Super Dave TV series); establishes Toronto as viable sketch production center pre-SCTV dominance; remains in syndication and YouTube clips as cult artifact.

British Bizarre: The Alternative Bible of the NewsstandBizarre magazine (Dennis Publishing) Issue 1 (Feb 1997) - Issue 172 (Jan 2015); editorials by David McComb, etc.; British Library catalogue

1997-2015

Dennis Publishing launches Bizarre as a monthly alternative magazine subtitled for its early years as 'the magazine of the unusual.' Covering body modification, fetish, UFOs, true crime, cult film, and internet weirdness, it positions itself as a printed predecessor to the internet's 'weird' verticals. At its peak (early 2000s) it sells over 50,000 copies monthly, featuring interviews with tattoo artists, porn performers, and Fortean researchers alongside photo essays on suspension and scarification. Declining print sales and the migration of its audience to online platforms lead Dennis to close the print edition in January 2015, moving briefly to digital before archiving. Documents UK body-mod and fetish scene at moment of mainstreaming; archive now used as primary source for scholars of 2000s alternative culture; demonstrates print-to-digital transition for niche magazines.

Ships Named Bizarre: The Odd Vessels of the French NavyRochefort Arsenal construction records; Service historique de la Défense; Winfield & Roberts 'French Warships in the Age of Sail 1626-1786'

1692-1811

The French Navy names several warships Bizarre, following the fashion of naming ships after qualities (Audacieux, Fougueux). The first Bizarre (1692) is a 68-gun ship of the line built at Toulon, active in the Nine Years' War. A second Bizarre (1751) is a 64-gun ship built at Brest, serving in the Seven Years' War and later as a hulk. A third projected Bizarre (1811, 110-gun) at Rochefort is never completed due to Napoleon's fall. The name reflects the older French sense of bizarre as 'brave, dashing' as much as 'odd' - a compliment to a warship's striking appearance. Illustrates semantic shift of 'bizarre' in French; provides case study in naval naming conventions; wrecks and logs remain in French archives for maritime historians.

Bizarre the Rapper: From Detroit Underground to D12Discogs; AllMusic; D12 biography; Shady Records press

1995-present

Rufus Arthur Johnson, born July 5, 1976 in Detroit, adopts the stage name Bizarre as a member of the hip-hop collective D12 (Dirty Dozen), formed in 1996 with friends including Eminem, Proof, and Mr. Porter. Known for shock-value lyrics, obese persona, and shower-cap imagery, he releases solo albums including Hannicap Circus (2005) and Friday Night at St. Andrews (2010) on Shady Records. His persona literalizes 'bizarre' as artistic strategy: deliberately grotesque, comedic horrorcore that courts controversy while referencing Detroit's underground rap battles. Extends the word into hip-hop lexicon as stage identity; D12's multi-platinum success (Devil's Night 2001) brings the name to global charts; solo work maintains cult following.

"Bizarre comes from bazaar - it means like a weird marketplace." — False cognate. Bazaar is from Persian bāzār 'market' via Turkish and Italian; bizarre is from Italian bizzarro/French bizarre of disputed (possibly Basque) origin. They converged in English pronunciation but have no shared root.

The British Bizarre magazine and John Willie's Bizarre are the same publication, just different eras. — Two unrelated magazines separated by 40 years, different publishers, countries, and purposes. John Willie's was a small mail-order fetish art magazine (1946-1959); British Bizarre was a mass-market alternative lifestyle monthly (1997-2015). Reprints and library records sometimes merge them.

"Bizarre" as a ship name meant the French thought the ship was weird or faulty. — In 17th-18th century French, bizarre could mean 'dashing, strikingly handsome, brave' - a positive martial quality. Naming a ship of the line Bizarre was a compliment to its appearance, not an insult.

Bizarre Records and Some Bizzare Records are the same label (just a spelling error). — Distinct labels: Bizarre Records (Zappa/Cohen, 1968, US) vs. Some Bizzare Records (Stevo Pearce, 1981, UK). The single-vs-double 'r' spelling is intentional and legally distinct.
