Who It Is

Imagine a goddess so fierce that a queen of the Iceni, on the eve of a war that would shake the Roman Empire, released a hare from her robes and read the future in its frantic path. Her name, echoed across the Channel from Britain to the Alps, may have been Andarta — the 'Great Bear' of the Vocontii, a war-goddess who was more than a footnote to history, but a force of nature that Rome itself had to reckon with.

As a war goddess, her behavior is inferred: she was likely invoked for victory in battle, protection of the tribe, and possibly for the destruction of enemies. The Boudiccan ritual described by Dio, if it pertains to her, involved divination (releasing a hare and interpreting its path) and a sacred grove, suggesting a prophetic or oracular aspect to her worship.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Andarta is generally interpreted as a Gaulish compound. The most widely accepted etymology derives it from *ande- (an intensive prefix meaning 'very' or 'greatly') and *arto- (meaning 'bear'). Thus, Andarta would mean 'Great Bear' or 'Mighty Bear'. Powers
Bestowal of Victory

As a war goddess, Andarta was likely invoked to grant victory in battle to her worshippers. This is the primary function implied by her name's connection to Andraste, whom Cassius Dio explicitly calls a goddess of victory.

Limit: Unknown; likely dependent on proper ritual and sacrifice.

Divination

The ritual described by Dio, involving the release of a hare and the interpretation of its movements, suggests a power of prophecy or divination associated with the goddess.

Limit: The interpretation of the hare's path was likely the domain of a priestess or the queen herself.

Tutelary Protection

As a tribal goddess, she was likely seen as the protector of the Vocontii people and their lands. This is a common function for Celtic tribal deities.

Limit: Unknown.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No direct visual representation of Andarta has been definitively identified. Unlike some other Gaulish deities, there is no known statue or image that can be securely attributed to her. If she is related to Andraste, Cassius Dio's account describes a battlefield ritual involving a hare, but this does not describe her appearance. Symbols — The bear (based on the etymology). The hare (if the Boudiccan ritual is associated with her). A spear or sword (as a war goddess, though not attested). The sacred grove (again, from the Dio account).

Domains — Warfare, victory, tribal protection. The bear connection might also suggest domains of strength, ferocity, and sovereignty. As a goddess of the Vocontii, she was likely a tutelary deity of the tribe and its territory, a protector of the people and their land.

Accounts I
The Boudiccan Rebellion (if Andarta = Andraste)Cassius Dio, Roman History, Book 62

61 CE

Before her revolt against Roman rule, the Iceni queen Boudicca is said to have released a hare from the folds of her dress as a form of divination, interpreting its path as favorable. She then invoked the goddess Andraste (or Andate), calling her 'mistress of victory.' The ritual took place in a sacred grove. After a successful initial engagement, Boudicca's forces are said to have sacrificed captured Roman women to the goddess, hanging them and mutilating their bodies. This story, if it refers to Andarta, is the only narrative associated with her. The rebellion was ultimately crushed, and Boudicca died (either from illness or poison). The story became a foundational myth of British resistance to Roman rule.

Andarta is a well-known, major goddess in the Celtic pantheon. — Andarta is a highly obscure figure, known almost exclusively from a handful of inscriptions in a small region of Gaul. She is not a major or well-documented deity.

The Boudiccan story proves Andarta was a goddess of human sacrifice. — The story of the sacrifices comes from a single, hostile Roman source (Cassius Dio), writing over a century after the event. It is likely exaggerated or invented to portray the rebels as barbaric. There is no direct evidence for human sacrifice in Andarta's worship.

Andarta was a 'Great Bear' goddess in the sense of a mother goddess or earth goddess. — While the etymology suggests a bear connection, her attested function (from the inscriptions and the Boudiccan parallel) is that of a war and victory goddess, not a mother or earth goddess.
