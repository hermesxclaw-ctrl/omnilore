Chandra, the cool-rayed lord of the night, whose waxing and waning measure the days and whose light nourishes the growing world.

Who They Are

Chandra is the moon god, the lord of the stars and the night, the source of coolness and the ruler of plants, herbs, and the tides. He is one of the Navagraha (nine planetary deities) in Hindu astrology. Chandra is depicted as a young, handsome man with two arms, riding a chariot drawn by ten white horses (or an antelope). He is white in color, dressed in white, and adorned with pearls. He holds a lotus and a club. According to the Puranas, Chandra was married to the 27 daughters of Daksha (the Nakshatras, the lunar mansions), but he favored only Rohini, causing the other 26 wives to complain to their father. Daksha cursed Chandra to wane, but the other gods intervened — the curse was modified so that Chandra would wax and wane cyclically (explaining the lunar phases). Chandra is also involved in the myth of the 'Churning of the Ocean' (Samudra Manthana), when the moon emerged and was placed by Shiva on his head. The mind is said to be controlled by Chandra, and a well-placed moon in one's horoscope grants mental peace, beauty, and popularity. The festival of Karwa Chauth involves fasting from sunrise until moonrise in devotion to Chandra.

Form

Chandra is conventionally depicted as a youthful, resplendently white/pale god, often likened to milk or camphor (Karpura). In the most common iconography he holds a mace (gada) and rides a chariot with three wheels drawn by three or more white horses (up to ten). In Navagraha temple imagery he is shown riding a chariot pulled by an antelope or hare, or seated on a hare, carrying a white lotus; the hare is a direct visual pun on the epithet Shashank / 'hare-marked', i.e. the lunar surface's dark 'rabbit in the moon' markings. In the LACMA Book of Dreams folio he appears as a pale deity in royal, princely finery.

Powers

Lunar light and the waning/waxing cycle Chandra governs the phases of the Moon, which wax and wane in a fixed monthly rhythm. According to the Puranic myth this is a direct result of Daksha's curse and its mitigation — he wastes (wanes) half the month and recovers (waxes) the other half, especially after bathing at the Sarasvati Tirtha. His light is reflected, lit and nourished by the Sun. Puranic / Mahabharata Shalya Parva His brightness is not constant; he is subject to eclipse (swallowing by Rahu) and to the lingering effects of curses. In the Ganesha myth his wholeness is permanently broken — he can never be 'whole again'. The eclipse in the Rahu myth (Rahu swallows the moon) coexists with the Daksha-curse wane account; these are two separate etiologies for the moon's changes and are never harmonized.

Lord of vegetation, herbs, and medicinal plants As Oshadhipati ('lord of herbs') and the deity who nourishes the soma/amrita-bearing plants, Chandra is the astral patron of all vegetation and medicinal lore. The myth says when he was cursed and wasted, 'the medicinal plants and other living beings dependent on lunar influence' suffered too; conversely his return to full form restores plant potency. He was crowned 'king of stars and medicines' after serving as the calf while sages milked Bhumidevi (Earth as a cow) during Emperor Prithu's reign. Puranic; Bhumidevi/Prithu episode His healing/nourishing power is tied to his phase; a waning moon was mythically associated with diminished plant potency. None identified in sources; some traditions separate lunar influence from the independent soma creeper (Sarcostemma acidum) used in modern somayajna.

Holder of the amrita / chariot of the night Chandra is conceived as the reservoir of the divine nectar of immortality (amrita/soma), residing in the moon, which is how he nourishes plants and the gods. He rides across the night sky in a three-wheeled chariot drawn by white horses (up to ten) or, in Navagraha iconography, by antelopes or upon a hare. Vedic-Puranic; Navagraha iconography

Ruler of the mind, emotions, and fertility (Jyotisha) In Hindu astrology, Chandra is the graha of the mind (manas), emotions, memory, and fertility as well as the nourishing mother-principle. His position in the horoscope governs temperament, emotional stability, and domestic life; a favourable (waxing) Moon is a natural benefic, while a weak or afflicted Moon is malefic. He rules the nakshatras broadly and is specifically the ruler of Rohini (and per many systems is the karaka for the moon-sign). Jyotisha / Navagraha system

King of stars, waters, and lotuses As Taradhipa ('lord of stars'/'lord of the asterisms'), Udupati/Uduraj ('lord of waters'), and Kumudanatha ('lord of lotuses'), Chandra presides over the stellar sphere, the moisture-cycle, and night-blooming lotuses. His epithets encode his kingship over the whole nocturnal heavens and the waters nourishing plant life. Epithets in main article

Accounts

Tara, the beautiful wife of Brihaspati (preceptor of the Devas), felt neglected while Brihaspati attended to the gods' affairs. Chandra, visiting Brihaspati, was captivated by her and they fell in love and ran off together — or, in some Puranic versions, Chandra abducted and raped her. Brihaspati demanded her return; Chandra refused, arguing Tara had come willingly and an old husband did not suit a young wife. The Deva leadership split: Indra threatened war, the Asuras (led by their preceptor Shukra) sided with Chandra. A fierce Deva-Asura conflict threatened cosmic chaos until Brahma intervened, admonished Shukra and Chandra, and forced the couple's separation. Tara returned to Brihaspati already pregnant. A paternity dispute erupted; Tara long refused to name the father until the child within her asked directly, whereupon she confessed Chandra was the father. Brahma ruled the child legally Brihaspati's, but Brihaspati (wanting his wife back but not her affair-child) cursed the child — later named Budha — to be born genderless. Budha preferred his biological father's company; his son Pururavas founded the legendary lunar dynasty (Chandravamsha) named for his grandfather Chandra.

Chandra married the 27 daughters of the Prajapati Daksha, who personify the 27 nakshatras (lunar mansions). Despite 27 wives, he singularly favoured Rohini and spent most of his time with her. The other 26 wives, jealous, complained to their father. Daksha first tried persuasion; failing, he cursed Chandra to fall ill and waste away (kashayaroga / consumption, or leprosy in the Rohini tradition), losing his brightness. Because Chandra was also the god of vegetation, the plants began to die. The Devas begged Daksha to relent; he mitigated the curse so Chandra suffers his illness each fortnight and recovers the other, yielding the monthly wane and wax. In one version Shiva (husband of Daksha's daughter Sati) partially cured Chandra, in gratitude for which Chandra built the Somnath temple.

During the Churning of the Ocean, the Devas and Asuras sought the Amrita (nectar of immortality). The demon Rahu disguised himself as a Brahmin and tried to drink the nectar. Surya (Sun) and Chandra recognized the deception and alerted Vishnu, who severed Rahu's head with his Sudarshana Chakra. But because Rahu had swallowed nectar, both his head and body became immortal — forming the shadow-entities Rahu and Ketu. Nursing enmity toward Surya and Chandra for exposing him, Rahu periodically 'swallows' the Sun and Moon, producing solar and lunar eclipses.

After a mighty feast hosted by Kubera, an overstuffed Ganesha rode home on his mount Krauncha (a shrew/shrum) on a full-moon night. A snake crossed their path; the terrified mount ran off, knocking Ganesha to the ground, and he vomited out all the modaks he had eaten. Chandra laughed at him. Enraged, Ganesha broke off one of his tusks and flung it at the Moon, hurting it, and cursed Chandra to never be whole again. Hence it is forbidden to behold the Moon on Ganesh Chaturthi.

Chandra is counted among the Ashta-vasus, a group of eight elemental deities. With his wife Manohara he fathered four sons — Varchas, Shishira, Prana, and Ramana. Chandra's son Varchas was later reborn as Abhimanyu, the heroic son of Arjuna in the Mahabharata, famed for his penetration-and-death in the chakravyuha at Kurukshetra.

During the reign of Emperor Prithu, Bhumidevi (Earth) was transformed into a cow to provide nourishment to all beings. When the sages milked her, Chandra served as the calf. This pleased Brahma, who crowned Chandra as king of stars and medicines, consolidating his role as the astral patron of healing plants and the medicinal arts.

A way to hold it in your mind

The Moon does not shine of its own accord — the Hindus say it lives and dies each month, wasting to a sliver and back, because a furious father-in-law once cursed a moon-god for loving one wife too much. Chandra, the white god of the night, married all twenty-seven daughters of the Prajapati Daksha — the twenty-seven lunar mansions themselves — and still could not keep from favouring Rohini, the red star Aldebaran, over every other bride. Daksha's curse that he pine away every fortnight, lifted only to heal in the other half, is India's oldest and loveliest explanation for the waxing and waning of the Moon. Yet behind that romance lies a deity who ran off with the guru Jupiter's wife and touched off a war between gods and demons; who was himself accused of rape in a darker telling; who cannot keep whole; and whose name, in modern times, has gone to an Indian space programme, an American X-ray telescope, a fire-mage in a card game, and the pacifying drug of a dystopian future.
