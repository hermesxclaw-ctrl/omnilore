Who It Is

The Moon does not shine of its own accord — the Hindus say it lives and dies each month, wasting to a sliver and back, because a furious father-in-law once cursed a moon-god for loving one wife too much. Chandra, the white god of the night, married all twenty-seven daughters of the Prajapati Daksha — the twenty-seven lunar mansions themselves — and still could not keep from favouring Rohini, the red star Aldebaran, over every other bride. Daksha's curse that he pine away every fortnight, lifted only to heal in the other half, is India's oldest and loveliest explanation for the waxing and waning of the Moon. Yet behind that romance lies a deity who ran off with the guru Jupiter's wife and touched off a war between gods and demons; who was himself accused of rape in a darker telling; who cannot keep whole; and whose name, in modern times, has gone to an Indian space programme, an American

Chandra embodies the calm, reflective, nurturing, moisture-laden aspect of the cosmos, the night luminary whose light is reflected from Surya (the Sun). In myth he is portrayed as passionately amorous and willfully attached — he runs away with the married Tara, singularly favours Rohini among his 27 wives, and is moved to bitter quarrels. He is also fertile and generative (father of Budha and of four sons with Manohara), and his phases are said in Jyotisha to govern the human mind (manas), emotions, agriculture, fertility, and ritual timing. Powers
Lunar light and the waning/waxing cycle

Chandra governs the phases of the Moon, which wax and wane in a fixed monthly rhythm. According to the Puranic myth this is a direct result of Daksha's curse and its mitigation — he wastes (wanes) half the month and recovers (waxes) the other half, especially after bathing at the Sarasvati Tirtha. His light is reflected, lit and nourished by the Sun.

Limit: His brightness is not constant; he is subject to eclipse (swallowing by Rahu) and to the lingering effects of curses. In the Ganesha myth his wholeness is permanently broken — he can never be 'whole again'.

Lord of vegetation, herbs, and medicinal plants

As Oshadhipati ('lord of herbs') and the deity who nourishes the soma/amrita-bearing plants, Chandra is the astral patron of all vegetation and medicinal lore. The myth says when he was cursed and wasted, 'the medicinal plants and other living beings dependent on lunar influence' suffered too; conversely his return to full form restores plant potency. He was crowned 'king of stars and medicines' after serving as the calf while sages milked Bhumidevi (Earth as a cow) during Emperor Prithu's reign.

Limit: His healing/nourishing power is tied to his phase; a waning moon was mythically associated with diminished plant potency.

Holder of the amrita / chariot of the night

Chandra is conceived as the reservoir of the divine nectar of immortality (amrita/soma), residing in the moon, which is how he nourishes plants and the gods. He rides across the night sky in a three-wheeled chariot drawn by white horses (up to ten) or, in Navagraha iconography, by antelopes or upon a hare.

Ruler of the mind, emotions, and fertility (Jyotisha)

In Hindu astrology, Chandra is the graha of the mind (manas), emotions, memory, and fertility as well as the nourishing mother-principle. His position in the horoscope governs temperament, emotional stability, and domestic life; a favourable (waxing) Moon is a natural benefic, while a weak or afflicted Moon is malefic. He rules the nakshatras broadly and is specifically the ruler of Rohini (and per many systems is the karaka for the moon-sign).

Limit:

King of stars, waters, and lotuses

As Taradhipa ('lord of stars'/'lord of the asterisms'), Udupati/Uduraj ('lord of waters'), and Kumudanatha ('lord of lotuses'), Chandra presides over the stellar sphere, the moisture-cycle, and night-blooming lotuses. His epithets encode his kingship over the whole nocturnal heavens and the waters nourishing plant life.

Accounts I
The Tara affair and the birth of BudhaDevi Bhagavata Purana, Padma Purana, Vishnu Purana

c. 3rd-10th century CE

Tara, the beautiful wife of Brihaspati (preceptor of the Devas), felt neglected while Brihaspati attended to the gods' affairs. Chandra, visiting Brihaspati, was captivated by her and they fell in love and ran off together — or, in some Puranic versions, Chandra abducted and raped her. Brihaspati demanded her return; Chandra refused, arguing Tara had come willingly and an old husband did not suit a young wife. The Deva leadership split: Indra threatened war, the Asuras (led by their preceptor Shukra) sided with Chandra. A fierce Deva-Asura conflict threatened cosmic chaos until Brahma intervened, admonished Shukra and Chandra, and forced the couple's separation. Tara returned to Brihaspati already pregnant. A paternity dispute erupted; Tara long refused to name the father until the child within her asked directly, whereupon she confessed Chandra was the father. Brahma ruled the child legally Brihaspati's, but Brihaspati (wanting his wife back but not her affair-child) cursed the child — later named Budha — to be born genderless. The curse of Daksha and the waxing-waning MoonMahabharata Shalya Parva; Puranas (Vishnu Purana, Bhagavata Purana)

c. 400 BCE-1000 CE

Chandra married the 27 daughters of the Prajapati Daksha, who personify the 27 nakshatras (lunar mansions). Despite 27 wives, he singularly favoured Rohini and spent most of his time with her. The other 26 wives, jealous, complained to their father. Daksha first tried persuasion; failing, he cursed Chandra to fall ill and waste away (kashayaroga / consumption, or leprosy in the Rohini tradition), losing his brightness. Because Chandra was also the god of vegetation, the plants began to die. The Devas begged Daksha to relent; he mitigated the curse so Chandra suffers his illness each fortnight and recovers the other, yielding the monthly wane and wax. In one version Shiva (husband of Daksha's daughter Sati) partially cured Chandra, in gratitude for which Chandra built the Somnath temple. Etiology of the lunar cycle; Chandra's permanent union with the nakshatras; foundation-legend of the Somnath temple (one variant); the frame of lunar astrological influence on vegetation.

Rahu, Ketu, and the eclipseSamudra Manthan (Churning of the Ocean) — Bhagavata Purana, Vishnu Purana, Mahabharata

epic/Puranic

During the Churning of the Ocean, the Devas and Asuras sought the Amrita (nectar of immortality). The demon Rahu disguised himself as a Brahmin and tried to drink the nectar. Surya (Sun) and Chandra recognized the deception and alerted Vishnu, who severed Rahu's head with his Sudarshana Chakra. But because Rahu had swallowed nectar, both his head and body became immortal — forming the shadow-entities Rahu and Ketu. Nursing enmity toward Surya and Chandra for exposing him, Rahu periodically 'swallows' the Sun and Moon, producing solar and lunar eclipses. Origin of eclipses; Rahu and Ketu canonized as chaya-grahas (shadow planets) in the Navagraha; permanent enmity between the light-luminaries and Rahu.

Accounts II
Ganesha breaks the Moon's wholenessPopular Puranic/temple legend; cited in the main Chandra article

late Puranic / medieval and modern folklore

After a mighty feast hosted by Kubera, an overstuffed Ganesha rode home on his mount Krauncha (a shrew/shrum) on a full-moon night. A snake crossed their path; the terrified mount ran off, knocking Ganesha to the ground, and he vomited out all the modaks he had eaten. Chandra laughed at him. Enraged, Ganesha broke off one of his tusks and flung it at the Moon, hurting it, and cursed Chandra to never be whole again. Hence it is forbidden to behold the Moon on Ganesh Chaturthi. Secondary etiology for the Moon's waxing-waning and for the visible dark spots ('a big crater… a dark spot') on the lunar surface; the ritual prohibition against seeing the Moon on Ganesh Chaturthi.

Chandra as Varchas, reborn as AbhimanyuMahabharata (epic)

epic (c. 400 BCE-400 CE)

Chandra is counted among the Ashta-vasus, a group of eight elemental deities. With his wife Manohara he fathered four sons — Varchas, Shishira, Prana, and Ramana. Chandra's son Varchas was later reborn as Abhimanyu, the heroic son of Arjuna in the Mahabharata, famed for his penetration-and-death in the chakravyuha at Kurukshetra. Connects the lunar deity to the central Kuru-Pandava genealogy; explains Abhimanyu's great martial destiny as the partial incarnation of a lunar son.

Chandra as the calf of BhumideviPuranic; cited in the main Chandra article

Puranic

During the reign of Emperor Prithu, Bhumidevi (Earth) was transformed into a cow to provide nourishment to all beings. When the sages milked her, Chandra served as the calf. This pleased Brahma, who crowned Chandra as king of stars and medicines, consolidating his role as the astral patron of healing plants and the medicinal arts. Institutionalizes Chandra's kingship over the stellar sphere and the medicinal/healing domain and his link to earth's fertility.
