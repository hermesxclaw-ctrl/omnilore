Who It Is

In the mid-twentieth century, a ghost slipped into the pages of one of the most authoritative dictionaries of the English language. It had no etymology, no pronunciation, no meaning—yet for years it haunted the reference shelves of libraries and the minds of lexicographers. Its name was Dord, a word that never was, a testament to the human love of order and the occasional triumph of error.

As a non-existent word, 'dord' has no behavior or personality. Its 'behavior' is confined to its existence in reference works and its role in lexicographical history.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The word 'dord' was created by an error in the dictionary editing process. It was intended to be 'D or d', a lowercase abbreviation for 'density' used in physics and chemistry. A typist or editor omitted the space and the capital D, resulting in 'dord'. The etymology is thus a typographical accident

Pronunciation — The dictionary provided the pronunciation 'dôrd' (rhyming with 'ford'), but since the word was never spoken, this was an invented pronunciation based on the spelling.

Existence as a ghost word

The ability to appear in a major dictionary without being a real word, thereby misleading readers and editors for several years.

Form & Signs

Appearance — As a word, 'dord' has no physical appearance other than its spelling and the typographic representation in dictionaries. In the dictionary, it appeared as a lowercase boldface entry followed by the pronunciation and definition.

Symbols — No symbols or iconography are associated with 'dord'.

Domains — The word 'dord' inhabited dictionaries and reference works, particularly the 1934 Webster's Second International. It was not used in any living language community.

Accounts I
The Birth and Death of a Ghost WordGove (1954), Merriam-Webster (accessed 2026)

1934-1947

During the preparation of the 1934 Webster's Second International, an editor submitted a slip for the abbreviation 'D or d' (meaning 'density') to be included in the list of abbreviations. The slip was misread, and the entry was typeset as 'dord' with the definition 'density'. The error went unnoticed through proofreading and printing. In 1939, an editor noticed the entry while checking another word and flagged it as suspicious. The entry was eventually removed from the plates, and the correction was applied in the 1947 printing. The discovery was later documented by Philip Gove in American Speech. The incident highlighted the need for rigorous cross-checking in dictionary production and became a famous example of a 'ghost word' in lexicography. It also led to a procedural change: the use of a special symbol to indicate that an entry was an abbreviation, to prevent similar errors.
