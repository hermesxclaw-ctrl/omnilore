The Approach

In the mid-twentieth century, a ghost slipped into the pages of one of the most authoritative dictionaries of the English language. It had no etymology, no pronunciation, no meaning—yet for years it haunted the reference shelves of libraries and the minds of lexicographers. Its name was Dord, a word that never was, a testament to the human love of order and the occasional triumph of error.

Content advisory. None. This subject is a lexicographical curiosity with no disturbing material.
Identity

dord

Pronunciation The dictionary provided the pronunciation 'dôrd' (rhyming with 'ford'), but since the word was never spoken, this was an invented pronunciation based on the spelling.

Etymology

The word 'dord' was created by an error in the dictionary editing process. It was intended to be 'D or d', a lowercase abbreviation for 'density' used in physics and chemistry. A typist or editor omitted the space and the capital D, resulting in 'dord'. The etymology is thus a typographical accident, not a genuine linguistic root.

Cultural Ownership

The word is a product of American lexicography, specifically the G. and C. Merriam Company (now Merriam-Webster) during the mid-20th century. It is not associated with any particular culture or language community.

Disputed Identifications

There is no dispute about the origin of the word; it is universally acknowledged as a ghost word. However, some sources mistakenly claim that 'dord' was a misspelling of 'density' rather than an abbreviation error, which is a minor point of confusion.

As a word, 'dord' has no physical appearance other than its spelling and the typographic representation in dictionaries. In the dictionary, it appeared as a lowercase boldface entry followed by the pronunciation and definition.

The only version of 'dord' is the erroneous entry in the 1934 edition. It was removed from subsequent printings and editions, and it never appeared in any other dictionary. Later references to 'dord' are historical accounts, not redefinitions.

Stories & Myths

The Birth and Death of a Ghost Word

Source Gove (1954), Merriam-Webster (accessed 2026)Culture American lexicographyPeriod 1934-1947

Summary

During the preparation of the 1934 Webster's Second International, an editor submitted a slip for the abbreviation 'D or d' (meaning 'density') to be included in the list of abbreviations. The slip was misread, and the entry was typeset as 'dord' with the definition 'density'. The error went unnoticed through proofreading and printing. In 1939, an editor noticed the entry while checking another word and flagged it as suspicious. The entry was eventually removed from the plates, and the correction was applied in the 1947 printing. The discovery was later documented by Philip Gove in American Speech.

Participants

G. and C. Merriam Company editors

Philip Babcock Gove

Anonymous typist/editor who made the error

Consequences

The incident highlighted the need for rigorous cross-checking in dictionary production and became a famous example of a 'ghost word' in lexicography. It also led to a procedural change: the use of a special symbol to indicate that an entry was an abbreviation, to prevent similar errors.

Variants

The story is consistently told across sources, with minor differences in the exact year of discovery (some say 1939, others 1937) and the exact role of the editor who noticed it.
