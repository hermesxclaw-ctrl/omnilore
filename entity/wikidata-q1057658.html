The Approach

In the fierce world of the Ulster Cycle, where warriors boasted and bled for honor, Cet mac Mágach of Connacht was a name that made even the bravest Ulster heroes pause. His deeds were written in blood and severed heads, and his rivalry with Conall Cernach was the stuff of legend—a rivalry that would end in a ford, with both men near death, but only one walking away.

Content advisory. Contains descriptions of violence, decapitation, and gore typical of medieval Irish heroic literature. Some cultural sensitivities may arise regarding the glorification of violence.
Identity

Cet mac Mágach

Pronunciation Approximately 'Ket mak MAH-gakh' (with a guttural 'ch' sound at the end) in reconstructed Old Irish.

Known As

CetCét

Etymology

The name 'Cet' may derive from Old Irish 'cét', meaning 'hundred' or 'first', but this is uncertain. 'Mac Mágach' means 'son of Mágach', a patronymic.

Cultural Ownership

Connacht warrior in the Ulster Cycle of Irish mythology. Part of the medieval Irish literary tradition, likely composed by Christian monks but drawing on pre-Christian oral tradition.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars have speculated that Cet might be a euhemerized deity or a purely literary creation, but there is no consensus. His relationship to Conall Cernach is disputed: some versions imply he is Conall's maternal uncle, but this is not consistent across sources.

Description

Appearance

No explicit physical description of Cet survives in the texts. He is depicted as a formidable warrior, likely of noble bearing, but specific details are unknown.

Symbols & Iconography

No specific symbols or iconography are associated with Cet in the sources. His identity is tied to his deeds and his rivalry with Conall.

Behaviour & Personality

Cet is portrayed as proud, boastful, and ruthless, typical of Ulster Cycle heroes. He is cunning (stealing the brain of Mesgegra) and physically powerful. He shows respect for worthy opponents, as seen in his interaction with Conall at the feast.

Domains & Habitats

Cet is associated with Connacht, but no specific dwelling or territory is mentioned. He travels for raids and feasts.

Version Differences

In 'The Death of Conchobar', Cet is a killer using a sling; in the other tales, he is a hand-to-hand combatant. The relationship with Conall (as uncle or not) varies. The manner of his death is consistent (killed by Conall at a ford), but the details of the combat differ slightly between versions.

Stories & Myths

The Story of Mac Dá Thó's Pig

Source Manuscripts at Trinity College Dublin and Bodleian LibraryCulture Irish (Ulster Cycle)Period Medieval (12th century manuscript, but story likely older)

Summary

At a feast, warriors of Connacht and Ulster compete for the champion's portion. Cet shames all Ulster warriors by recounting how he defeated them. When Conall Cernach arrives, he boasts of defeating Cet's brother Anlúan, and Cet concedes, but says if Anlúan were present, he would outdo Conall. Conall then throws Anlúan's severed head at Cet.

Participants

Cet mac Mágach

Conall Cernach

Mac Dá Thó

Warriors of Connacht and Ulster

Consequences

Cet is humiliated and the feast descends into a general battle (not detailed in this summary).

Variants

Different manuscript versions may vary in the order of boasts or the number of warriors shamed.

The Death of Conchobar

Source Multiple manuscripts (at least five)Culture Irish (Ulster Cycle)Period Medieval

Summary

Cet steals the calcified brain of Mesgegra, king of Leinster, which Conall had taken as a trophy. He shoots it from his sling, embedding it in Conchobar's head. The doctors cannot remove it without killing him, so they sew it up, warning him not to get excited. Years later, Conchobar hears of the crucifixion of Christ, flies into a rage, and the brain bursts from his head, killing him.

Participants

Cet mac Mágach

Conchobar mac Nessa

Conall Cernach (indirectly)

Mesgegra (deceased)

Consequences

Conchobar dies, fulfilling a prophecy. Cet's act is seen as a successful assassination.

Variants

The story is synchronized with Christian history, making Conchobar's death occur at the time of Christ's crucifixion. Variations may include the exact circumstances of the brain's theft.

The Death of Cet mac Mágach

Source Book of Leinster (Edinburgh manuscript)Culture Irish (Ulster Cycle)Period Medieval

Summary

Cet raids Ulster, killing twenty-seven men and taking their heads. Conall Cernach pursues him through snow, catches up at a ford, and after a ferocious combat, kills Cet. Conall is left near death himself.

Participants

Cet mac Mágach

Conall Cernach

Charioteer of Conall

Consequences

Cet is dead, and Conall is gravely wounded but survives. This is the definitive end of their rivalry.

Variants

The Edinburgh manuscript is the only source for this tale; details of the combat may vary if other versions exist, but none are known.

Dark History
Content advisory. Graphic violence, decapitation, use of human remains as a weapon.
Warning: Graphic violence, decapitation, use of human remains as a weapon. —

Violence and decapitation

Source All tales

Description

Cet kills twenty-seven men and takes their heads as trophies. Conall decapitates Anlúan and throws his head at Cet. The use of a human brain as a sling bullet is a notable instance of using remains as a weapon.

Scholarly Context

These acts are typical of the heroic ethos of the Ulster Cycle, where taking heads and displaying prowess through violence were marks of honor. Scholars note that such descriptions may reflect actual Iron Age practices or literary exaggeration.
