The Approach

In the mists of Arthurian legend, where swords are forged in myth and kings are crowned by fate, there exists a spear whose name echoes through the halls of Camelot—Rhongomyniad, the lance of King Arthur. Unlike the famed Excalibur, this weapon is a shadow in the chronicles, mentioned in passing by medieval poets yet never given a full saga. Its very obscurity invites wonder: What power did it hold? Why did bards sing of it? And what became of it after the fall of the Round Table?

Identity

Rhongomyniad

Pronunciation Approximately 'hron-go-MIN-yad' (Welsh: [r̥ɔŋɡɔˈmɨnjad])

Known As

RhongomynRonRhongomyniad (spear)Rhongomyniad (lance)

Etymology

The name is Welsh, derived from 'rhon' (spear, lance, pike) and 'gomynt' or 'gwmyn' (related to 'gwm' meaning 'battle' or 'conflict'). Thus, Rhongomyniad broadly translates as 'the battle-spear' or 'the lance of conflict.' Some scholars suggest a connection to 'rhong' (piercing) and 'myniad' (going, rushing), giving 'the rushing piercer.'

Cultural Ownership

Welsh and broader Arthurian tradition. The spear is named in Welsh texts and later appears in English and French Arthurian romances, though its primary identity is Celtic.

Disputed Identifications

Some modern interpreters conflate Rhongomyniad with the Irish spear Gáe Bulg or the Norse spear Gungnir, but there is no textual evidence for such identifications. Others mistakenly equate it with the Holy Lance (Spear of Destiny), a Christian relic unrelated to Arthurian tradition.

Description

Appearance

The only direct description comes from 'The Dream of Rhonabwy,' where the spear is described as having a 'red tip' and being 'long and sharp.' The shaft is not described, but it is implied to be sturdy and fit for a king. No other physical details are given in any source.

Symbols & Iconography

In the Dream of Rhonabwy, the spear is one of the few named items of Arthur, symbolizing his martial power and sovereignty. Its red tip may evoke bloodshed and the violence of kingship.

Behaviour & Personality

As an object, it has no personality, but it is consistently associated with Arthur's prowess in battle. It is a weapon of war, not a magical artifact with a will of its own.

Domains & Habitats

It exists solely within the Arthurian world, appearing in battle scenes and as part of Arthur's armory. No specific realm or habitat is attached to it.

Version Differences

The name appears in Welsh and later English sources, but the spelling varies (Rhongomyniad, Rhongomyn, Ron, Rone). The spear is always Arthur's, but its role is minimal in all accounts. There is no version where it is given a backstory or special powers beyond being a formidable weapon.

Stories & Myths

The Dream of Rhonabwy

Source The MabinogionCulture WelshPeriod 13th century (story set in Arthur's time)

Summary

In this dream-vision, Rhonabwy, a warrior of a later era, falls asleep and enters Arthur's world. He sees Arthur and his knights preparing for battle against the Saxons at the Battle of Badon. Arthur is depicted playing a game of gwyddbwyll (a board game) with his knight Owain, while his army waits. Rhongomyniad is mentioned as Arthur's spear, but it plays no active role in the story.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

No dedicated academic study of Rhongomyniad exists; most references are passing. The spear's role in any specific battle is not detailed. The etymology is tentative. The exact spelling in early Welsh manuscripts is uncertain due to scribal variations. The spear's fate after Arthur's death is unknown. Modern adaptations, especially in Japanese media, have expanded its lore, but these are not historically accurate. The research could not verify the spear's appearance in any visual art before the modern era.
