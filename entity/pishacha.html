Who It Is

Imagine the lowliest thing the gods bothered to keep — a demon so starved, so close to the bone of what a spirit can be, that it has no name of its own except a word for the act of tearing raw flesh. That is the Pishacha: the flesh-eater of the cremation ground, the thing that slips in through your mouth while you sleep and rents your thoughts, the why behind every rattled night-ritual and every offering left out after a funeral. It is not the grand villain of the epics — it is the skulking hunger at the edge of the pyre, at the court's back stair, yoked as a chariot-hearse for stronger demons. And in a world that once named a real people after it, and codified a rape as 'the demon-marriage', the Pishacha is never quite the monster it seems to be: it is also a label thrown at whoever a society did not want to look like kin.

The Pishacha is cunning, malicious, and craven by nature. It operates by stealth and possession rather than open assault: it lurks in desolate places, feeds on human energy, and possesses the living — entering through the mouth — to alter their thoughts and drive them mad. Its hunger is total and unsubduable; the flesh of the living and the dead alike draws it. Yet it is not invincible nor even proud: it is repelled by mantras, frightened away by the proper funeral rites, and cowed by the gods (it worships Shiva and Parvati, and serves Kubera and Brahma). Powers
Corpse- and flesh-eating

The defining pishacha power and appetite: gorging on human flesh and on corpses at the cremation ground. They are flesh-eaters above all else — the class's Sanskrit etymology encodes the tearing and mangling of raw meat.

Limit: Flesh-eating is a need, not an unbounded power; a pishacha created by improper burial is described as cut off from eating and drinking (it can no longer consume), turning blind and suffering while still craving the living.

Shapeshifting and invisibility

Pishachas can assume any form at will and can become invisible. This enables their stealthy predations — appearing as a familiar face, a beast, or nothing at all before seizing a victim.

Limit: The scope of shapeshifting is not elaborated the way it is for rakshasas (who change size and take any creature's form); the pishacha's repertoire reads more as disguise and concealment than monumental transformation.

Possession through the mouth

Pishachas possess the living by entering through the mouth, hijacking thoughts and behaviour, and afflicting victims with a range of maladies and abnormalities up to outright insanity. The possessed person is said to be 'ridden' by the spirit.

Limit: Possession can be driven out. Certain mantras cure the afflicted and drive the pishacha away; the shraddha/funeral rite and offered portions keep pishachas at bay and can calm them.

Feeding on human energy

Beyond flesh, pishachas drain life-energy from the living, weakening or sickening their victims even without open possession. The two channels — gross flesh and subtle vitality — make them doubly dangerous as silent tormentors.

Limit: Energy-draining is countered by the same mantle and offering-apotropaia that repel possession.

Own demonic language — Paishachi

Pishachas are said to speak their own tongue, Paishachi, a 'goblin' speech so proverbial that an entire lost literature (the Brihatkatha) was reputed to be written in it. Daṇḍin's Kavyadarsha names it Bhutabhasha — 'the language of the dead/ghouls'.

Limit: Paishachi was a largely *literary* language per the grammarians, and is now almost wholly unattested; its use here is as much scholarly legend as demonic trait.

Accounts I
The Pishachas at the courts of the godsMahabharata (epic treatment)

Epic (c. 4th c. BCE–4th c. CE)

Far from being purely autonomous raiders, the Pishachas are depicted as denizens of divine space: they reside in the courts of Kubera (the god of wealth) or of Brahma (the creator), and they worship Shiva and Parvati. Their placement ties them into the hierarchy of celestial attendants and low-ranking divine servants rather than casting them as out-and-out cosmic rebels. This establishes the pishacha as an 'official' but despised demon-order — permitted, fed, and kept in their place by the gods they serve. Frames the Pishachas as a subordinate, bounded demon class within the divine order, in tension with their popular image as lawless flesh-eaters.

Pishachas in the Mahabharata warMahabharata

Epic

The epic conscripts the Pishachas onto both sides of the Kurukshetra war. They are said to have fought alongside the rakshasa-hero Ghatotkacha (son of Bhima and the rakshasi Hidimbi) against the Kaurava champion Karna, lending their mythic numbers to the Pandava cause. Yet in the opposite register they are also said to have served the Kauravas, specifically acting as the horses of the chariot of the demon Alambusha. The image of pishachas yoked as chariot-horses is striking: even in war they are beasts of burden to greater demons, their ferocity deployed as raw horsepower. Shows the pishachas as mercenary/levy troops with no strong factional allegiance, used by whichever side can dominate them; reinforces their low status as demonic rank-and-file and even draught-animals.

Accounts II
Kashyapa's curse and the six-month sand-sea (Nilamata Purana)Nilamata Purana (Kasmira Mahatmya)

c. 4th–8th century CE

In the Kashmir origin-myth, the valley (born of the drained lake Satisaras) was allotted to the Nagas. When the Nagas refused to share the land with the humans (Manavas), the sage Kashyapa cursed them to live with the Pisacas. On the Nagas' plea the curse was softened: the Pishachas would dwell in the valley for 'four ages' and would retire each year for six months to the sea of sand, during which the Manavas could return and live jointly with the Nagas. The cycle was broken when the Brahmin Candradeva, who had not emigrated with the others, was initiated by the Naga king Nila into the sixty-five rites and ceremonies that let the valley be permanently inhabited by humans. Gives the Pishachas a concrete, localised homeland-role in Kashmir's sacred geography, and ties the class to a folk ethnic division (the Dardic-speaking peoples of the north-west were themselves called 'Pishachas').

The improperly-buried dead become pishachasMain mythological treatment (funerary register)

Vedic–Puranic

A distinct origin-thread holds that when burials are not performed properly, the deceased is unable to escape the dead body for an indefinite time and turns into a pishacha. In this state it loses its ability to eat and drink, turns blind, and suffers immensely — yet it instinctively craves the living, senses their smell and presence, and may assault them. The tale moralises funeral obligation: neglected death-rites condemn the dead to a blind, starving ghosthood and create a danger to the living. Roots the modern pishacha/bhoot complex firmly in the ethics of the last rites; explains why shraddha-offerings are left out — to feed and placate the unquiet dead and keep them from attacking.

The Pishacha is essentially the same as a Rakshasa. — Classical texts keep them as distinct orders: rakshasas are the powerful, sometimes heroic man-eaters who drive the Ramayana and Mahabharata plots, while pishachas are smaller corpse-haunting spirits that serve as low-rank troops, chariot animals and court attendants.

Paishachi is a completely invented language with no relation to real people. — Paishachi is a largely-unattested literary language named 'spoken by pishachas', but it was (per the grammarians and colonial linguists) a genuine, if now near-lost, linguistic register; some scholars link it to the Dardic languages. The demon-name is entangled with a real human linguistic community.

All 'ghosts' in Indian folklore are pishachas. — Indian ghost taxonomy distinguishes bhoota, preta, vetala and pishacha as separate-spirit classes. Pishachas are specifically the flesh-eating/demon register; bhoota is the generic restless ghost, vetala the knowledgeable corpse-haunter.

The Pishachini of TV/film is an ancient, canonical goddess-figure. — The pishachini (feminine demoness) is attested, but the seductive, beautiful-woman vampire of shows like Pishachini (2022) is a modern pulp construction that grafts yakshi (seductive celestial spirit) lore and vampire tropes onto the older, unbolded demoness — not a faithful ancient portrait.
