Who It Is

On the night of 3 May, a young English solicitor stands in a lamplit castle doorway while a tall old man in black, clean-shaven save a long white moustache, takes his hand in a grip 'as cold as ice—more like the hand of a dead than a living man', and says: 'Welcome to my house! Enter freely and of your own will!' Within a week Jonathan Harker will watch that same hand open a razor cut on his own throat — no, the Count will watch Harker's blood and, at the shaving mirror, Harker will discover that his host casts no reflection at all. The Count who speaks those lines is the most imitated monster in Western culture, and almost everything the world thinks it knows about him — the fangs, the cape, the coffin, the death by sunlight — is a later film's invention. Name & Tongue

Etymology — Stoker took the name from the historical House of Drăculești: Vlad II of Wallachia was called Dracul after his admission to the Order of the Dragon (founded 1408, revived 1431 by Emperor Sigismund), and his son Vlad III was styled Drăculea, 'son of Dracul'. Pronunciation — Anglicised /ˈdrækjʊlə/ (DRACK-yoo-lə); Romanian Drăculea approximately [ˈdrəkule̯a], with the final syllable pronounced; the modern Romanian folk form Dracul(a) stresses the 'drac' syllable.

Aliases — Count Dracula (the novel's usual style), the Count (used by the vampire hunters throughout the novel), Dracula, Drakulya (older spelling of the name as it appears in the research Van Helsing reports from his friend Arminius), Count de Ville (pseudonym under which the Piccadilly house is purchased, reported in the novel's Chapter XX), Voivode Dracula (the historical counterpart Van Helsing tentatively identifies him with in Chapter XVIII)

Ownership — Anglo-Irish literature. Count Dracula was created by the Dublin-born novelist and theatre manager Bram Stoker (1847–1912) and published in London by Archibald Constable and Company

Powers
Undying life sustained by blood

Van Helsing's lecture (Ch. XVIII) is explicit: 'The vampire live on, and cannot die by mere passing of the time; he can flourish when that he can fatten on the blood of the living... he can even grow younger; that his vital faculties grow strenuous'. The Count does not eat or drink as men do — Harker never sees him eat. His victims become anemic and die as he feeds; the novel's doctors first read Lucy's decline as pernicious anemia (the 'chlorotic' diagnosis) before Van Helsing renames it.

Limit: He cannot flourish without blood ('he eat not as others'); he is bound to his earth-box for rest, and his strength waxes with feeding and wanes without it.

Superhuman strength

His handshake makes Harker wince (Ch. II); Van Helsing cites 'the strength of many of his hand' — the wolf attack at the castle door and his lifting Harker's luggage from the diligence (Ch. XVIII); at the end Jonathan hurls the great box from the cart with 'a strength which seemed incredible' (Ch. XXVII).

Limit: Not quantified in the text; presented as prodigious but not limitless (he is manhandled by the hunters in the Piccadilly scene).

Shapeshifting: wolf, bat, mist, dust, and smallness

Attested transformations: to a wolf (inferred from the Demeter landing, 'when he tear open the dog', Ch. XVIII); to a bat (seen at Whitby by Mina, at Carfax by Seward, at Lucy's window by Quincey); into a mist 'which he create', though 'the distance he can make this mist is limited, and it can only be round himself'; to 'elemental dust' on moonlight rays (as Harker saw the sisters); and he can 'become so small' that he slips through a hairbreadth space — Lucy, un-dead, passes through a crack in her tomb door (Ch. XVIII). He can also crawl head-down down a castle wall 'over that dreadful abyss, face down, with his cloak spreading out around him like great wings' (Ch. III).

Limit: When away from his earth-home he can change form only 'at noon or at exact sunrise or sunset' (Ch. XVIII).

Command over animals and the elements

Van Helsing: he 'can command all the meaner things: the rat, and the owl, and the bat—the moth, and the fox, and the wolf', and 'within his range, direct the elements; the storm, the fog, the thunder' (Ch. XVIII). The wolves of the Borgo Pass answer his will (Ch. I–II); in London a wolf from the Zoological Gardens (Bersicker, per the Ch. XII news report) breaks loose and comes to Lucy's window on his bidding; the rats of Carfax are his creatures; the storm and fog that finish the Demeter's voyage are laid at his door by the captain's log (Ch. VII).

Limit: 'Within his range' — the novel stresses his power over nature is bounded, unlike film versions that give him free weather control.

Mesmerism, hypnotic will, and the blood-communion link

He bends Lucy and Mina to his will while they sleepwalk or drowse; his eyes hold and compel (Harker's trances in the castle; Mina's dreamlike submission at the asylum). After he forces Mina to drink from a wound in his chest (Ch. XXI), a two-way psychic link is established: Van Helsing hypnotizes Mina and, through her, sees through the Count's eyes to track his whereabouts (Ch. XXIII–XXV), while the Count can read Mina's thoughts — a channel the hunters deliberately feed with false plans. The link is visible: the wafer-scar on Mina's forehead mirrors itself on the Count's face at the Piccadilly confrontation (Ch. XXIII).

Limit: The link is a vulnerability as much as a power; the hunters weaponize it. Hypnosis of Mina requires Van Helsing's skill and her cooperation.

Making vampires: the un-dead chain

Those he kills and who are not dispatched properly rise again as un-dead — the three sisters in his castle, Lucy after her death ('the dead travel fast' is not the novel's phrase, but the un-dead Lucy's return is). Van Helsing: 'if we fail in this our fight he must surely win... It is that we become as him; that we henceforward become foul things of the night like him' (Ch. XVIII). Mina is left with the threat of the same fate, which drives the campaign's urgency.

Limit: The transformation is not automatic: the body must be destroyed (stake, beheading, or the consecrated rites) to prevent it; Lucy is saved from it by Arthur's stake.

No reflection, no shadow

Harker's shaving mirror: 'There was no reflection of him in the mirror!' (Ch. II); the sisters 'threw no shadow on the floor' (Ch. III); Van Helsing: 'He throws no shadow; he make in the mirror no reflect' (Ch. XVIII).

Limit: A tell, not a weapon; the novel uses it as detection.

Restricted mobility: invitation, running water, and the day

Attested limits (all Ch. XVIII): 'He may not enter anywhere at the first, unless there be some one of the household who bid him to come; though afterwards he can come as he please' (the hunters exploit the loophole when they plan his reception); 'His power ceases, as does that of all evil things, at the coming of the day. Only at certain times can he have limited freedom'; 'he can only pass running water at the slack or the flood of the tide'. He must sleep in his native earth: 'when he have his earth-home, his coffin-home, his hell-home, the place unhallowed'.

Limit: Daylight weakens him but does not kill him — he is seen about London by day (Ch. XIV); sunlight destruction is a 1922 film invention.

Aversions and instruments of destruction

Attested (Ch. XVIII and dramatized scenes): garlic ('things which so afflict him that he has no power, as the garlic that we know of'); sacred objects — the crucifix and the Host, before which 'he take his place far off and silent with respect', and which sear Mina when Van Helsing touches the wafer to her forehead (Ch. XXII); a branch of wild rose laid on his coffin 'keep him that he move not from it'; 'a sacred bullet fired into the coffin kill him so that he be true dead'; 'the stake through him, we know already of its peace; or the cut-off head that giveth rest'. The actual killing of the Count (Ch. XXVII) uses Jonathan's Kukri to shear his throat and Quincey's Bowie knife in the heart, whereupon 'the whole body crumbled into dust'.

Limit: The stake is not the only weapon; the novel's own climax uses knives. Consecrated ground defeats his earth (the 'sterilisation' of the boxes with the Host, Ch. XXII).

Accounts I
The Making of the Count (Stoker's composition, 1890–1897)Rosenbach notes (facsimile ed. Eighteen-Bisang & Miller, 2008); Barbara Belford, Bram Stoker (1996); Harry Ludlam, A Biography of Dracula (1962)

1890–1897

Stoker, business manager of Henry Irving's Lyceum Theatre, began assembling notes for a vampire novel in the late 1880s. A holiday at Whitby in the summer of 1890 — where the name 'Dracula' reached him, almost certainly through William Wilkinson's 1820 account of Wallachia — decided the title; the notes show the Count originally called 'Count Wampyr' and the book working-titled 'The Un-Dead'. He transcribed Emily Gerard's Transylvanian superstitions into his notes, and the novel was published by Constable on 26 May 1897, priced six shillings, in yellow cloth. A literary vampire that fused 18th-century Balkan panic, Gerard's Carpathian lore, and a Victorian stage manager's sense of theatre; the novel has never been out of print.

Before the Novel: the Vampire of Medveđa and the Eighteenth-Century PanicsVisum et Repertum (1732); the Plogojowitz report (1725); Calmet's Dissertations (1746); Paul Barber, Vampires, Burial and Death (1988)

1725–1755

A decade of well-documented exhumations followed the death of the soldier Arnold Paole near Medveđa: bodies found fresh, plump, and bloody were staked, beheaded, and burned, and the official report by the army surgeon Johann Flückinger became a European bestseller of horror. The panic reached the Empress Maria Theresa, who in 1755 sent her personal physician to investigate and then banned exhumation and staking. This is the folkloric battery behind Stoker's novel: vampires who return to prey on kin, are bound to graves, and are destroyed by stake and fire. Established the 'rules' of the vampire as Europeans understood them before Stoker: nocturnal return, blood-feeding, kinship targeting, stake/beheading/burning remedies.

The Road to the Borgo Pass (novel Ch. I–IV)Stoker, Dracula (1897)

Internal dates 3–8 May (year unstated)

Jonathan Harker, a London solicitor's clerk, travels to Transylvania to settle a London property purchase for a client, Count Dracula. At Bistritz an innkeeper's wife presses a crucifix on him 'For your mother's sake'; on St. George's Eve the peasants speak of the eve's evil and the blue flames that mark buried treasure. The carriage he boards is driven by a silent figure with red eyes — Harker will later wonder if the driver and the Count are one. The castle door opens to a tall old man in black with a courtly welcome: 'Welcome to my house! Come freely. Go safely; and leave something of the happiness you bring!' Within days Harker is a prisoner. The shaving mirror shows no reflection; three voluptuous vampire women nearly take him in the moonlight until the Count claims him ('How dare you touch him, any of you?'); the Count delivers a long genealogical oration claiming descent from Attila and the Szekely wars; and Harker, searching the castle, finds the Count asleep in a great box of earth, then watches him crawl head-down down the castle wall like a lizard. The Demeter and the Landing at Whitby (novel Ch. VI–VIII)Stoker, Dracula (1897)

Internal dates July–August

While Mina Murray visits her friend Lucy Westenra in Whitby, the Russian schooner Demeter runs aground in a storm with only a lashed corpse at the wheel; the captain's log records the crew's deaths one by one at the hands of something aboard, until he alone remained, binding himself to the wheel. A huge dog — a wolf, the town decides — leaps ashore and vanishes into the churchyard. Lucy, who sleepwalks, is found in the graveyard at night, and thereafter declines with a mysterious wasting illness and two tiny wounds on her throat. Mina glimpses a tall dark man with red eyes watching from the cliff — 'His red eyes again! They are just the same.' Brings the Count to England with his fifty boxes of earth and makes Lucy his first victim; the 'large dog' and the storm establish his command of beasts and weather.

Accounts II
The Bloofer Lady, Lucy's Death, and the Un-Dead (novel Ch. VIII–XVI)Stoker, Dracula (1897)

Internal dates August–September

Lucy's suitors — Arthur Holmwood, Quincey Morris, and Dr. Seward — gather with Seward's teacher, Professor Abraham Van Helsing of Amsterdam, who alone reads the symptoms for what they are. Three blood transfusions from the three men keep Lucy alive until the night a wolf smashes her window and she dies. She is buried, and then the children of Hampstead begin turning up pale and frightened, telling of a beautiful 'bloofer lady'. Van Helsing, Arthur, and the others open the tomb to find the coffin empty and the sleeping Lucy un-dead, 'voluptuous' and transformed; Arthur, with 'the stake' sharpened to a fine point and a heavy hammer (Ch. XVI), does the terrible work, and the men seal the tomb with the Host. The scene is the novel's most explicit horror of ritual violence, rendered as mercy. Defines the un-dead state and the destruction ritual; Lucy's fate is the template for what threatens Mina.

Mina's Communion and the Scar (novel Ch. XIX–XXIII)Stoker, Dracula (1897)

Internal dates September–October

The hunters — now including the Harkers — identify Carfax as the Count's lair, sterilize his earth-boxes with the Host, and corner him at the Piccadilly house, where his face shows the same red scar Mina will bear. But the Count has already struck at the heart of the band: one night he enters the asylum while Seward and Van Helsing are away, kills Renfield for trying to warn the others, and forces Mina to drink from a wound he opens on his own chest — a communion that links their minds. When Van Helsing touches the Sacred Wafer to her forehead, it sears 'into the flesh as though it had been a piece of white-hot metal', and Mina wails, 'Unclean! Unclean! Even the Almighty shuns my polluted flesh!' She is saved from despair by the hunters' oath of loyalty and by Van Helsing's counter-use of her link: hypnotized, she can see through the Count's eyes. The novel's moral and supernatural pivot: the victim becomes the weapon. Mina's scar fades only with the Count's death.

The Chase and the Sunset at the Castle (novel Ch. XXIV–XXVII)Stoker, Dracula (1897)

Internal dates October–November

The hunters trace the Count by ship and rail toward Varna — and are deceived; the box has gone to Galatz, and by the time they turn north the Count has a head start up the rivers and across Transylvania. Mina, hypnotized, guides them; Van Helsing, separated with Mina, uses the wafer and a Holy circle to protect her and destroys the three sisters in the castle. On the last day, the Szgany wagon carrying the earth-box is intercepted at sunset above the Borgo Pass, where the Count must lie helpless in his box through the daylight hours. Jonathan's Kukri shears the throat, Quincey's Bowie knife takes the heart, and 'before our very eyes, and almost in the drawing of a breath, the whole body crumbled into dust and passed from our sight', leaving 'a look of peace' on the dissolving face. Quincey Morris dies of his wounds, and the closing note — 'Seven years ago we all went through the flames' — records a son named Quincey and a return visit to a castle that stands empty, 'every trace of all that had been... blotted out'. The Count's Own History (as he and Van Helsing tell it)Stoker, Dracula (1897), Ch. III and XVIII

Presented as centuries of Szekely and Transylvanian history

In his great speech Harker records, the Count claims the Szekelys' mixed blood of Huns, Magyars, and warrior races, and boasts of ancestors who fought the Turk, 'when the Hungarian king sent letters to us' — and asks whether 'Attila, whose blood is in these veins' was not as great as any devil. Van Helsing's later research (via 'my friend Arminius, of Buda-Pesth University') adds the historical layer: he 'must, indeed, have been that Voivode Dracula who won his name against the Turk', a scion of a 'great and noble race' with a reputation for cunning, who learned his secrets 'in the Scholomance, amongst the mountains over Lake Hermanstadt, where the devil claims the tenth scholar as his due'; the records speak of 'stregoica', 'ordog', and 'pokol' — witch, Satan, hell — and one manuscript names him 'wampyr'. The novel's own, hedged identification of the Count with the historical Dracula — hedged because it is Van Helsing's inference, reported second-hand, and contradicted by the Count's own Szekely-attila genealogy.

Count Dracula IS Vlad the Impaler. — The novel never asserts it; Van Helsing offers the identification as second-hand conjecture, and the Count's own genealogy contradicts Vlad's biography. The fusion is the work of McNally & Florescu (1972) and the tourist industry. See the contradictions register.

Sunlight kills vampires; Dracula dies at dawn. — In the novel, daylight only drains his power ('His power ceases... at the coming of the day'), and he is destroyed at sunset by a Kukri and a Bowie knife. Sunlight-death is a Nosferatu (1922) invention.

Dracula has fangs and drinks with them bared. — The novel describes 'peculiarly sharp white teeth' that protrude over the lips — not the retractable fangs of the cinema. Lugosi's 1931 Count had no fangs; Hammer's Christopher Lee (1958) made fangs canonical.

Dracula wears a tuxedo, cape, and widow's peak. — The novel's Count is an old man 'clad in black from head to foot' with a white moustache, clean-shaven cheeks, and hair growing 'scantily round the temples'. The evening-dress image comes from the 1924/1927 stage productions and Lugosi's film.

Lugosi's Dracula says 'I vant to suck your blood'. — The line does not occur in the 1931 film (whose famous line is 'I never drink—wine'). The catchphrase was grafted onto Lugosi's accent by later parodists and memory.

Stoker visited Transylvania and researched the region on site. — He never traveled east of Munich. His Transylvania is assembled from guidebooks and travel memoirs — Emily Gerard's essays above all — and the Borgo Pass setting matches no real castle.

Bran Castle is Dracula's castle. — The novel places the castle at the Borgo Pass (near today's Bistrița/Năsăud), hundreds of kilometers from Bran. Bran's 'Dracula's Castle' branding is 20th-century tourism; the historical Vlad's real stronghold was Poenari.

A wooden stake through the heart is the only way to kill him. — The novel's climax uses knives; the Ch. XVIII register lists the stake, beheading, a sacred bullet, and the wild rose as separate instruments; the hunters also 'sterilise' his earth with the Host.

Dracula sleeps in a coffin. — He rests in a box of his native earth — 'his earth-home, his coffin-home' — the coffin imagery being the cinematic translation of the earth-box.

The novel is set in 1897. — The internal dates run 3 May to 6 November of an unstated year; the calendar best fits 1893, while the closing note's 'seven years ago' suggests 1890. The novel was published 26 May 1897.

Dracula is 'the original vampire' and the novel is a record of ancient folklore. — The Count is a late-Victorian literary synthesis of 18th-century Balkan panic, Romanian and Greek revenant belief, and Gothic fiction (Carmilla above all). Eastern European vampire traditions — strigoi, moroi, vrykolakas — are older, different, and regionally varied; the novel's rulebook (invitation, running water at tide, noon transformations) is largely Stoker's own system.

The 1931 Browning film follows the novel faithfully. — It adapts the Deane/Balderston play, which cut the castle act, merged characters, and reduced Mina's role; the epistolary structure is gone entirely. The novel's full plot reached the screen only partially until later adaptations.

'Dracula' was always a vampire name in folklore. — The name belonged to a Wallachian princely house (the dragon-order honorific); its vampire association begins with Stoker's novel. Folk tradition called the undead strigoi or moroi, not Dracula.
