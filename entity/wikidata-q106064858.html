Who It Is

When you say 'Roman,' you might be summoning the ghost of an empire that stretched from Britain to the Euphrates, or the name of a Bulgarian town, a NASA telescope, or a character in a 2006 horror film. This is the story of a word that carries the weight of centuries, yet remains stubbornly ambiguous.

As a historical people, Romans were known for their discipline, pragmatism, legalism, and military prowess. They valued virtus (manliness/courage), pietas (duty to gods and family), and gravitas (seriousness). In modern contexts, 'Roman' as a name or brand may evoke strength, classicism, or reliability.

Powers
Military conquest and engineering

The Romans were able to conquer and administer a vast empire through superior military organization, road-building, aqueducts, and fortifications.

Limit: The empire eventually fell due to internal strife, economic pressures, and barbarian invasions.

Legal and governmental innovation

Roman law, including concepts like 'innocent until proven guilty,' has influenced Western legal systems. The republic's system of checks and balances inspired modern democracies.

Limit: Roman democracy was limited to citizens and excluded women, slaves, and non-citizens.

Cultural and linguistic influence

Latin, the language of the Romans, evolved into the Romance languages (Italian, French, Spanish, Portuguese, Romanian) and influenced English. Roman literature, art, and architecture have shaped Western culture.

Limit: Cultural influence is diffuse and not a 'power' in the sense of a directed ability.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The appearance of 'Roman' varies by context. Historically, Roman citizens wore the toga, and soldiers wore armor and helmets. In art, Romans are often depicted with realistic portraiture. Modern uses range from the Roman typeface (serif) to the Roman Space Telescope, which has no human form.

Symbols — The she-wolf (Lupa) suckling Romulus and Remus, the eagle (aquila) of the legions, the SPQR acronym, the fasces, the laurel wreath, and the Chi-Rho (for Christian Romans).

Domains — Originally a city-state in central Italy, the Roman civilization expanded to encompass the entire Mediterranean basin, parts of Europe, the Near East, and North Africa. The city of Rome itself was the center. In modern times, 'Roman' appears as a place name in Bulgaria, France, Romania, Russia, Canada, and elsewhere.

Accounts I
The Founding of RomeLivy, Plutarch, and other Roman historians

Mythical, traditionally 753 BC

Romulus and Remus, twin sons of Mars and Rhea Silvia, were abandoned and suckled by a she-wolf. They later founded a city, but Romulus killed Remus and became its first king. Rome was established, and the legend provided a divine origin for the Roman people.

The Roman RepublicLivy, Polybius, and others

509 BC - 27 BC

After overthrowing the monarchy, Rome became a republic governed by elected officials and a senate. It expanded through Italy and the Mediterranean, but internal conflicts led to civil wars and eventually the rise of emperors. The Republic's collapse led to the Roman Empire.

Accounts II
The Roman EmpireTacitus, Suetonius, Cassius Dio

27 BC - 476 AD (West)

Augustus became the first emperor, ushering in the Pax Romana. The empire reached its greatest extent under Trajan. It eventually split into Eastern and Western halves, with the West falling to Germanic invasions. The Western Empire's fall marked the beginning of the Middle Ages in Europe. The Eastern Empire continued as the Byzantine Empire.

Byzantine ContinuityConstantine Porphyrogenitus, Anna Komnene

330 AD - 1453 AD

The Eastern Roman Empire, with its capital at Constantinople, continued to call itself Roman (Rhomaioi). It preserved Greek and Roman learning and served as a bulwark against various invasions. The Byzantine Empire preserved classical knowledge and influenced the Renaissance. Its fall to the Ottomans in 1453 was a major historical turning point.
