The Approach

When you say 'Roman,' you might be summoning the ghost of an empire that stretched from Britain to the Euphrates, or the name of a Bulgarian town, a NASA telescope, or a character in a 2006 horror film. This is the story of a word that carries the weight of centuries, yet remains stubbornly ambiguous.

Content advisory. This entry deals with historical warfare, imperialism, and cultural appropriation. Some uses of the term 'Roman' have been politically charged, and the term 'Romani' is distinct and should not be conflated.
Identity

Roman

Pronunciation /ˈroʊmən/ (English), /roˈman/ (French, Romanian), /ˈrɔman/ (German)

Known As

RomansRomanusRhomaioiRomioiRumRomani

Etymology

From Latin 'Romanus,' derived from 'Roma' (Rome). The ultimate origin of 'Roma' is uncertain, possibly from Etruscan or Greek.

Cultural Ownership

The term 'Roman' is deeply associated with the city of Rome, the Roman Republic and Empire, and their cultural heirs, including the Byzantine Empire and the Catholic Church. It is also used in various modern contexts, from place names to personal names.

Disputed Identifications

The term 'Roman' has been claimed by various groups throughout history, including the Byzantine Greeks (Rhomaioi), the Catholic Church (Roman Catholic), and the Romani people (though this is a distinct ethnonym with a different etymology). The relationship between 'Roman' and 'Romani' is often confused.

Description

Appearance

The appearance of 'Roman' varies by context. Historically, Roman citizens wore the toga, and soldiers wore armor and helmets. In art, Romans are often depicted with realistic portraiture. Modern uses range from the Roman typeface (serif) to the Roman Space Telescope, which has no human form.

Symbols & Iconography

The she-wolf (Lupa) suckling Romulus and Remus, the eagle (aquila) of the legions, the SPQR acronym, the fasces, the laurel wreath, and the Chi-Rho (for Christian Romans).

Behaviour & Personality

As a historical people, Romans were known for their discipline, pragmatism, legalism, and military prowess. They valued virtus (manliness/courage), pietas (duty to gods and family), and gravitas (seriousness). In modern contexts, 'Roman' as a name or brand may evoke strength, classicism, or reliability.

Domains & Habitats

Originally a city-state in central Italy, the Roman civilization expanded to encompass the entire Mediterranean basin, parts of Europe, the Near East, and North Africa. The city of Rome itself was the center. In modern times, 'Roman' appears as a place name in Bulgaria, France, Romania, Russia, Canada, and elsewhere.

Version Differences

The term 'Roman' has evolved over millennia. In antiquity, it denoted a citizen of Rome. In the Byzantine period, it came to mean a subject of the Eastern Roman Empire, often Greek-speaking. In the medieval West, 'Roman' was associated with the papacy and the Holy Roman Empire. Today, it can refer to a modern Italian, a member of the Catholic Church, or a typeface.

Powers & Abilities

Military conquest and engineering

Source Version Historical Roman Republic and Empire

Description

The Romans were able to conquer and administer a vast empire through superior military organization, road-building, aqueducts, and fortifications.

Limitations

The empire eventually fell due to internal strife, economic pressures, and barbarian invasions.

Contradictions

Some romanticize Roman military might, while others emphasize its brutality and cost.

Legal and governmental innovation

Source Version Roman Republic and Empire

Description

Roman law, including concepts like 'innocent until proven guilty,' has influenced Western legal systems. The republic's system of checks and balances inspired modern democracies.

Limitations

Roman democracy was limited to citizens and excluded women, slaves, and non-citizens.

Contradictions

The Roman Republic was not a modern democracy, and its political institutions were often manipulated by powerful individuals.

Cultural and linguistic influence

Source Version All periods

Description

Latin, the language of the Romans, evolved into the Romance languages (Italian, French, Spanish, Portuguese, Romanian) and influenced English. Roman literature, art, and architecture have shaped Western culture.

Limitations

Cultural influence is diffuse and not a 'power' in the sense of a directed ability.

Contradictions

The Roman Empire was not solely Latin-speaking; Greek was equally important in the East.

Summary

Romulus and Remus, twin sons of Mars and Rhea Silvia, were abandoned and suckled by a she-wolf. They later founded a city, but Romulus killed Remus and became its first king.

The Roman Republic

Source Livy, Polybius, and othersCulture RomanPeriod 509 BC - 27 BC

Summary

After overthrowing the monarchy, Rome became a republic governed by elected officials and a senate. It expanded through Italy and the Mediterranean, but internal conflicts led to civil wars and eventually the rise of emperors.

The Republic's collapse led to the Roman Empire.

The Roman Empire

Source Tacitus, Suetonius, Cassius DioCulture RomanPeriod 27 BC - 476 AD (West)

Summary

Augustus became the first emperor, ushering in the Pax Romana. The empire reached its greatest extent under Trajan. It eventually split into Eastern and Western halves, with the West falling to Germanic invasions.

Participants

Augustus

Trajan

Constantine

Romulus Augustulus

Consequences

The Western Empire's fall marked the beginning of the Middle Ages in Europe. The Eastern Empire continued as the Byzantine Empire.

Variants

The 'fall' of Rome is debated; some see it as a gradual transformation, others as a catastrophic event.

Byzantine Continuity

Source Constantine Porphyrogenitus, Anna KomneneCulture ByzantinePeriod 330 AD - 1453 AD

Summary

The Eastern Roman Empire, with its capital at Constantinople, continued to call itself Roman (Rhomaioi). It preserved Greek and Roman learning and served as a bulwark against various invasions.

Participants

Justinian

Belisarius

Basil II

Constantine XI

Consequences

The Byzantine Empire preserved classical knowledge and influenced the Renaissance. Its fall to the Ottomans in 1453 was a major historical turning point.

Variants

Some Western Europeans refused to call the Byzantines 'Romans,' preferring 'Greeks.'

Relationships

Greeks

Type cultural rival and influenceSource Horace, Virgil, Plutarch

Description

Romans admired and adopted much of Greek culture, but also conquered Greece and often looked down on contemporary Greeks.

Version Note

In the Byzantine period, the distinction blurred as the empire became Greek-speaking.

Conflicting Accounts

Roman attitudes toward Greeks ranged from admiration to contempt.

Catholic Church

Type religious heirSource Various church documents

Description

The Catholic Church, centered in Rome, claims continuity with the Roman Empire and uses 'Roman' in its title.

Version Note

The term 'Roman Catholic' is used to distinguish from Eastern Orthodox and Protestant churches.

Conflicting Accounts

The Orthodox Church also claims Roman heritage but disputes the primacy of Rome.

Romani people

Type false cognateSource Linguistic and historical studies

Description

The Romani people are an ethnic group with origins in India, not related to Rome. The name 'Romani' comes from the Romani word 'rom' meaning 'man.'

Version Note

The similarity in spelling leads to frequent confusion.

Conflicting Accounts

Some people mistakenly believe Romani people are descendants of Romans, but this is historically inaccurate.

Dark History

Slavery

Source Various Roman legal texts, Seneca, Pliny

Description

Slavery was integral to Roman society, with slaves subjected to brutal conditions and lacking legal rights.

Scholarly Context

Historians estimate that slaves constituted a significant portion of the population, and slave revolts were brutally suppressed.

Conquest and Imperialism

Source Polybius, Julius Caesar, Tacitus

Description

Rome expanded through military conquest, often involving massacre, enslavement, and cultural suppression. Examples include the destruction of Carthage and the subjugation of Gaul.

Scholarly Context

Modern scholarship emphasizes the violent nature of Roman imperialism, contrasting with earlier romanticized views.

Persecution of Christians

Source Tacitus, Eusebius

Description

Early Christians were persecuted under various emperors, including Nero and Diocletian. They were sometimes executed in public spectacles.

Scholarly Context

The extent and intensity of persecution varied by time and region, and it was not a constant policy.

Contradictions & Disputes

Who were the true Romans?

Position A

The ancient Romans of the Republic and Empire, centered on Rome.

Position A — evidence

Western historiography traditionally focuses on Rome and the Latin West.

Position B

The Byzantine Greeks, who continued the Roman state and called themselves Romans.

Position B — evidence

Byzantine sources consistently use 'Roman' for their empire.

Scholarly Consensus

Modern scholars recognize both as Roman, with the Byzantines being the continuation of the Roman Empire.

Did the Roman Empire fall?

Position A

The Western Empire fell in 476 AD, and the Eastern Empire eventually fell in 1453.

Position A — evidence

Traditional periodization places the fall of Rome in 476 AD.

Position B

The Roman Empire transformed and continued in various forms, such as the Holy Roman Empire and the Ottoman claim to Roman succession.

Position B — evidence

Some argue that the empire's institutions and culture persisted for centuries.

Scholarly Consensus

The concept of 'fall' is debated; many prefer to speak of transformation.
