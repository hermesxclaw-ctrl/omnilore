We are thin as shadows, light as leaf-skeletons. The wind can break us, so we wait for still days. We taught you to paint, to hunt, to dance. The songs you sing were our songs first.

Who It Is

The Mimi (or Mimih) are ancestral spirit beings of Arnhem Land in Australia's Northern Territory — ethereal, stick-like figures who are among the most ancient subjects of human artistic representation. The Mimi are credited with teaching the first Aboriginal people how to hunt kangaroos, butcher game, paint on rock, and perform ceremonies. They are also among the oldest ancestors in the Aboriginal conception of the Dreamtime, predating the arrival of the Rainbow Serpent in some regional cosmologies.

Physically, the Mimi are depicted as extraordinarily thin, elongated humanoid figures — so slender and fragile that a strong wind could break them, which is why they live in rock crevices and emerge only on still days. In Arnhem Land rock art, Mimi figures are some of the earliest artistic styles — dynamic, energetic stick figures shown running, hunting, dancing, and fighting. Unlike the later 'X-ray' style art that shows internal organs and anatomical detail, Mimi art captures movement and action with startling vitality. The figures are often shown carrying spears, boomerangs, and dilly bags, and they are frequently depicted in complex narrative scenes that have persisted in oral tradition for thousands of years.

The Mimi's central mythological functions are as teachers and as the original artists. According to tradition, it was the Mimi who first painted on the rock walls of Arnhem Land, and they taught Aboriginal people the techniques and spiritual protocols of rock painting. Aboriginal custodians continued the Mimi's tradition, repainting and renewing the images over generations — though the oldest surviving Mimi-style paintings may be thousands of years old. The Mimi also taught the first humans how to hunt: how to track kangaroos, how to throw a spear, how to butcher game properly, and how to distribute the meat according to law. They taught the first ceremonies — the songs, dances, and body painting designs that remain central to Aboriginal religious life.

The Mimi's fragility is a crucial part of their character. Because they are so thin and light, they can be carried away by the wind, which is why they emerge only on still, windless days. This fragility makes them elusive; encountering a Mimi requires patience, stillness, and spiritual sensitivity. In some traditions, the Mimi can make themselves invisible, and only those with the appropriate spiritual 'eyes' can see them.

Mimi rock art has achieved international recognition for its antiquity and artistic sophistication. The dynamic Mimi figures — running, leaping, fighting, dancing — capture motion in a way that European art would not develop until much later. The Mimi also represent one of the deepest continuities of human artistic expression: the same spirit beings depicted in rock shelters tens of thousands of years ago continue to be described in living oral tradition today. This continuity makes Arnhem Land one of the most significant sites in the world for understanding the deep history of human spiritual and artistic expression.

The Mimi are not a single being but a race or type of spirit — there are many Mimi, some with individual names and personalities. In contemporary Aboriginal culture, the Mimi continue to be acknowledged in ceremony and art, and many Aboriginal artists from Arnhem Land credit the Mimi as the original inspiration and teachers of their art forms.

Form

Extremely thin and elongated human-like figures. They are often depicted with exaggerated long limbs, thin bodies, and sometimes with heads that appear slightly too large for their frames. This is not a stylistic error but a representation of their nature: they are so light and ethereal that they can slip through cracks in rocks and are blown by the wind. They are often shown in profile, in active poses.

They are not solid in the same way as humans. Behavior & Habitat

The Mimi are generally considered to be benevolent and helpful to humans, but they can be mischievous or dangerous if not treated with respect. They are known to be skilled hunters and are credited with teaching humans how to hunt, prepare food, and perform ceremonies. They live in the rocky escarpments and are rarely seen by humans, preferring to remain in their own realm.

Accounts

In the early days, the first humans were clumsy and lacked skills. The Mimi, who already lived in the rocky country, took pity on them or were persuaded to help. They emerged from their rock shelters and taught the humans how to make and use spears, how to track and hunt kangaroos and other animals, how to butcher the meat, and how to prepare it for eating. They also taught them about the plants that were good to eat and how to find water.

The Mimi are credited with creating the first rock paintings. They painted the images of the animals they hunted and the ceremonies they performed on the rock walls of the escarpment. These paintings were not just pictures; they were imbued with power and meaning. When humans came to the same places, they saw these paintings and learned from them, and they began to paint themselves, continuing the tradition.

While generally benevolent, the Mimi are not to be trifled with. They can be dangerous to people who are careless, who enter their sacred sites without permission, or who break the law. They can cause illness, misfortune, or even death. They are also known to be able to steal children or lead people astray in the bush.

Scholarly Debates
Benevolent vs. Malevolent Nature
The Mimi are fundamentally benevolent teachers who helped humans. ⇌ The Mimi are dangerous spirits who can cause harm and misfortune.
Evidence for A: Many stories of them teaching humans hunting and ceremony.
Evidence for B: Stories of them punishing transgressors, stealing children, and leading people astray.

The Mimi themselves painted the first rock art. ⇌ The rock art was painted by human ancestors, possibly with spiritual guidance from the Mimi.

What We Don't Know

The primary gap is the lack of a single, comprehensive, and culturally authorized public source that consolidates all Mimi stories and knowledge. Much of the detailed knowledge is held by specific Aboriginal custodians and is not meant to be public. My research is based on publicly available academic and tourist-oriented sources, which are excellent but cannot capture the full depth of the living tradition. The specific etymology of the word 'Mimi' is also not fully resolved in the sources I consulted.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of the Mimi as the original 'influencers' of the Arnhem Land world. They didn't have social media, but they had the rock walls of the escarpment. They 'posted' their knowledge—how to hunt, how to dance, how to live—for the first humans to see and learn from. Their 'content' was so good that people have been copying and re-posting it for thousands of years. They are the ultimate ancestors of every artist who has ever picked up a brush, and their gallery is the entire landscape.
