The Approach

In the darkness of the Maya underworld, a god with a flatulent name and a scorching breath waits to burn the souls of the dead. He is Cizin, the Stinking One, lord of decay and earthquakes, whose very title reeks of death. But this god of putrefaction is no mere demon; he is a complex figure whose myths and names echo across centuries and cultures, from ancient Yucatán to modern video games.

Content advisory. Contains descriptions of death, decay, and soul-burning in Maya mythology. Some content may be disturbing to sensitive readers.

Etymology

The name Cizin likely derives from the Yucatec Maya root 'ciz', meaning flatulence or foul stench, thus 'Stinking One'. Other names: Yum Cimil (Lord of Death), Hun Ahau (One Ruler), Ah Puch (to melt).

Cultural Ownership

Maya civilization, particularly Yucatán and Lacandon regions

Disputed Identifications

Scholars identify Cizin as God A in the codices. Some equate him with Hun-Came and Vucub-Came of the Popol Vuh, but this is contested. Similarities to Mictlāntēcutli (Aztec) are noted but not identity.

Description

Appearance

In Maya codices, God A is often depicted as a skeletal figure or a body with exposed ribs and skull-like head. Sometimes shown with a bloated, decaying body. May have black spots representing decay. Often adorned with death eyes (closed or rolled).

Symbols & Iconography

Skulls, bones, decay, black spots, owl (associated with death), dog (companion to the dead), the underworld (Xibalba/Metnal). Sometimes depicted with a collar of eyes or a mirror on the forehead.

Behaviour & Personality

Cizin is a malevolent deity who delights in death, decay, and destruction. He is associated with war, sacrifice, and earthquakes. He is feared and placated with offerings. In Lacandon myth, he burns souls of the dead, causing them pain and suffering.

Domains & Habitats

God of death, earthquakes, and the underworld (Metnal/Xibalba). He resides in the underworld, but can cause destruction on earth.

Version Differences

Different Maya groups had varying names and attributes. Yucatán Mayas associated him with Hun-Came and Vucub-Came; Lacandon Mayas have a specific myth of soul-burning; scholars identify him as God A in codices. Some names may be titles rather than distinct entities.

Stories & Myths

Lacandon myth of soul burning

Source Lacandon oral tradition (as recorded by ethnographers)Culture Lacandon MayaPeriod 20th century (recorded)

Summary

When a person dies, Cizin burns the soul on his mouth and anus. The soul complains, and Cizin douses it with cold water, causing more complaints. He then burns it again until the soul disintegrates into nothing. The soul then goes to the god Sucunyum, who spits in his hand to clean the soul, allowing it to go where it pleases.

Contradictions & Disputes

Identity as Hun-Came and Vucub-Came

Position A

Cizin is the same as Hun-Came and Vucub-Came, the death gods of the Popol Vuh.

Position A — evidence

The source says 'To the Yucatán Mayas he was Hun-Came and Vucub-Came.'

Position B

Cizin is a distinct deity, and Hun-Came and Vucub-Came are separate entities.

Position B — evidence

In the Popol Vuh, Hun-Came and Vucub-Came are specific rulers of Xibalba, not necessarily the same as Cizin.

Scholarly Consensus

Debated; some scholars conflate them, others see them as distinct manifestations of death.

Name usage

Position A

Cizin is the primary name.

Position A — evidence

The source uses Cizin as the main name.

Position B

Ah Puch is the primary name in some regions.

Position B — evidence

Many popular culture references use Ah Puch.

Scholarly Consensus

Cizin is common in Yucatec sources; Ah Puch is also attested.
