Etymology

The name Izanami is derived from the verb izanau (historical orthography izanafu) meaning 'to invite', with the suffix -mi being a feminine suffix. Thus, it translates as 'Female-who-invites'. An alternative theory by Shiratori Kurakichi suggests the root iza- (isa-) comes from isao (historical orthography isawo) meaning 'achievement' or 'merit'.

Cultural Ownership

Izanami is a central deity in Shinto, the indigenous religion of Japan. She is also significant in Japanese mythology and folklore, and is considered an ancestor of the Japanese imperial family.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars have drawn parallels between Izanami and other underworld goddesses such as Persephone or Baba Yaga, but these are comparative interpretations, not identifications within the tradition. In Tenrikyo, she is identified as one of the Ten Aspects of God's Providence, but this is a sectarian interpretation.

Archive Dossier — Approach

In the beginning, when the world was only a brine-soaked void beneath a floating bridge of heaven, two deities stood upon that bridge and stirred the sea with a jeweled spear. From the dripping salt, an island was born, and from that island, all of Japan. Yet the same goddess who gave birth to the archipelago would also give birth to death itself, and her descent into the shadowy land of Yomi would split the world of the living from the world of the dead forever. This is the story of Izanami, the Female-Who-Invites, whose love and wrath shaped the cosmos.

Who It Is

Izanami is the primal female creator kami of Japanese myth, the sister-wife of Izanagi who with him created the Japanese archipelago and gave birth to the first generation of kami. She died giving birth to the fire kami Kagutsuchi — her body was so badly burned that she descended to Yomi, the land of the dead. When Izanagi followed to retrieve her, he found her putrefied body covered in maggots, pursued by the hags of the underworld and the thunder kami that had grown from her rotting flesh. In her shame and rage at being seen in this state, she pursued Izanagi but failed to catch him before he sealed the entrance to Yomi with a massive boulder. From behind the boulder, Izanami declared she would kill 1,000 people a day; Izanagi responded he would ensure 1,500 births a day to counter her. Izanami is thus both the mother goddess who created life and the queen of the dead who calls souls to the underworld. She represents the cycle of birth and death — the inevitable end that comes from the same source as life. She is worshipped as a dread goddess of death at some shrines near the supposed entrance to Yomi.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

In the Kojiki, Izanami is described as beautiful and graceful before her death. After her descent into Yomi, she becomes a rotting form of flesh with maggots and foul creatures crawling over her body. In art, she is often depicted as a beautiful woman in traditional Japanese attire, sometimes with a comb in her hair.

Symbols & Iconography

Izanami is associated with the jeweled spear (Amenonuhoko), the pillar of heaven (Ame-no-mihashira), and the palace Yahiro-dono. In the underworld, she is linked to the peach that Izanagi threw to drive away demons, and the boulder (Chibiki no iwa) that separates the living and dead.

Behaviour & Personality

Izanami is portrayed as a creator goddess who is proactive in creation, but also as a figure who becomes wrathful and vengeful after being abandoned by Izanagi. She is determined and assertive, as seen in her decision to eat the food of the underworld and her curse to kill 1,000 people a day.

Domains & Habitats

Izanami is the goddess of both creation and death. She resides in Yomi, the shadowy land of the dead, after her death. Before that, she lived on Onogoroshima and later on the island of Awaji, according to the Nihon Shoki.

Version Differences

In the Kojiki, Izanami dies giving birth to Kagu-tsuchi, and Izanagi descends to Yomi to retrieve her. In the Nihon Shoki, Izanagi does not descend to Yomi; instead, he resides permanently on Awaji. Additionally, in the Nihon Shoki, the three deities Amaterasu, Tsukuyomi, and Susanoo are created by both Izanagi and Izanami, whereas in the Kojiki they are born from Izanagi's purification alone.

Origins

Izanami was born as the seventh generation of heavenly kami alongside Izanagi. She gave birth to the islands of Japan and numerous kami. When she bore Kagutsuchi (fire), she was fatally burned. She descended to Yomi, where she was transformed into a rotting corpse-goddess, and was sealed in the underworld by Izanagi.

Death and Underworld Dominion

Source Version Kojiki

Description

After her death, Izanami became the ruler of Yomi, the land of the dead. She has the power to cause death to the living, as seen in her curse to kill 1,000 people per day.

Limitations

She cannot leave Yomi because she has eaten the food of the underworld.

Contradictions

In the Nihon Shoki, Izanami does not become a ruler of Yomi; she remains on Awaji.

Cursing

Source Version Kojiki

Description

Izanami cursed the living to die at a rate of 1,000 per day.

Limitations

Izanagi countered by promising to give life to 1,500 per day.

Archive Dossier — Relationships

Izanagi

Type brother-husbandSource Kojiki, Nihon Shoki

Description

Izanami is both sister and wife to Izanagi. They are the creators of the Japanese islands and many deities. Their relationship is strained after Izanami's death and Izanagi's abandonment of her in Yomi.

Version Note

In both texts, they are a divine couple, but the Nihon Shoki omits the descent to Yomi.

Conflicting Accounts

None

Hiruko (Ebisu)

Type childSource Kojiki

Description

Hiruko was the first child of Izanagi and Izanami, born deformed and cast out to sea. He later became known as the god Ebisu.

Version Note

In the Nihon Shoki, there are variations in the story of Hiruko.

Conflicting Accounts

Some versions say Hiruko was placed in a boat and drifted away; others say he was abandoned.

Amaterasu, Tsukuyomi, Susanoo

Type children (in Nihon Shoki)Source Nihon Shoki

Description

In the Nihon Shoki, these three deities are created by both Izanagi and Izanami, making them their children. In the Kojiki, they are born from Izanagi alone.

Version Note

The Kojiki attributes their birth solely to Izanagi's purification.

Conflicting Accounts

The parentage of these deities is disputed between the two primary texts.
