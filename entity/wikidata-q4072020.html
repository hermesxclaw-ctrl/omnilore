Form

There are no textual descriptions of Atanua's physical form, as the Taíno did not write descriptive texts. The archaeological record provides the best evidence: she is commonly depicted as a stylized, squatting female figure in a birthing posture, with exaggerated genitalia and sometimes a frog-like face. This iconography emphasizes her role as the earth-mother and source of all life. She is often carved into stone or wood as a zemí, a vessel for spiritual power.

Powers

Cosmic Motherhood Atanua is the mother of Yúcahu, the supreme god of the Taíno. This makes her the ultimate source of the divine lineage and all creation. All primary sources (Pané, Oviedo, Las Casas) She is a creator but not necessarily a ruler. Her power is generative, not authoritarian. None recorded in the primary sources, but modern interpretations vary on whether she is an equal to Yúcahu or a subordinate figure.

Control over Water She is the mistress of the sea and the source of all freshwater. She is associated with the tides, the rain, and the rivers that nourish the land. Pané, Oviedo Her control is not described as arbitrary but as a natural function of her being. The storm goddess Guabancex is sometimes seen as a separate, more violent entity, while other scholars see her as a destructive aspect of Atanua's power.

Fertility and Creation As a mother goddess, she grants fertility to the land, to animals, and to women. She is invoked for successful childbirth and bountiful harvests. Inferred from iconography and Pané's account Her power is tied to the natural cycle; it is not a form of magic that can be harnessed by individuals. None. This is the most consistent aspect of her character.

Lunar Association She is explicitly linked to the moon, which in many cultures is tied to the menstrual cycle, fertility, and the rhythms of the sea. Pané The specific myths about her lunar role are not recorded. None.

Behavior & Habitat

As a primordial deity, Atanua is not described as having a 'personality' in the same way as later mythological figures. She is a force of nature: the source of water, the moon, and the fertility of the earth. She is the passive, generative principle from which the active male god Yúcahu (associated with cassava and the sun) is born.

Accounts

The Taíno believed that Atabey (Atanua) was the mother of Yúcahu, the supreme god who was associated with cassava (manioc), the staple crop, and the sun. The myth establishes Atanua as the ultimate female principle and the source of the primary male deity.

In one creation narrative, the primordial world was a dry, barren land. A hero or a giant (sometimes identified with the culture hero Deminán Caracaracol) broke open a sacred gourd (or a tree) that contained the sea, releasing all the waters and creating the oceans, lakes, and rivers. Atanua is often placed in this narrative as the guardian or the source of this water, tying her to the creation of the watery world.

A common Taíno myth tells of humanity emerging from a sacred cave (Cacibajagua). The sun and the moon also came out of this cave. Atanua, as a lunar deity and mother figure, is intimately connected to this myth of origin, representing the womb of the earth from which all things emerged.

Content Notes

The Taíno population of Hispaniola, estimated at hundreds of thousands or more, was decimated within a few decades of Columbus's arrival. This was a result of enslavement, forced labor in gold mines, massacres, and the introduction of Old World diseases. This genocide led to the loss of the full context of Atanua's mythology.

This is a well-documented tragedy. The loss of Taíno culture is the primary reason why Atanua's mythology is so fragmentary and subject to interpretation.

Historical consensus; accounts by Las Casas and others

The primary sources on Atanua were written by Spanish missionaries and colonists. They interpreted Taíno religion through a Christian lens, potentially misrepresenting or simplifying the goddess's role. For example, the emphasis on her as a 'virgin mother' might be a Christian framing of a more complex native concept.

Scholars must read the Spanish accounts critically, recognizing the biases and motives of the authors. This makes the 'true' pre-Columbian Atanua difficult to recover with certainty.

What We Don't Know

1. **Full Mythological Cycle:** The complete set of myths surrounding Atanua is lost. We only have fragments recorded by Spanish chroniclers. 2. **Ritual Practice:** The specific rituals, ceremonies, and festivals dedicated to her worship are not described in detail. 3. **Regional Variations:** We do not know how her worship and mythology varied between the different islands of the Greater Antilles. 4. **Iconographic Certainty:** While the frog-like female figure is strongly linked to her, we cannot be 100% certain of the identity of many zemís. 5. **Pre-Columbian Context:** The primary sources are filtered through a Spanish Christian lens. We lack any purely indigenous perspective. 6. **Language:** The exact meaning of her name and the original Taíno language is a matter of reconstruction and debate.

A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine a library containing the entire history, religion, and culture of a people. Now imagine that a fire was set, and only a few half-charred pages were saved. Atanua is one of those pages. We can read her name, we can see her image, and we know she was a mother and a creator. But the full book of her story is ash. She is a symbol of what was lost, and a testament to the resilience of a culture that refuses to be completely forgotten. She is less like a fully-formed character from Greek myth and more like a powerful, fragmented relic from a lost world — a world we can only glimpse through the eyes of her destroyers.
