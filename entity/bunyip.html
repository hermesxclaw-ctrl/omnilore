Who It Is

According to traditional accounts, if you stand at the edge of a deep, still billabong in south-eastern Australia and feel an unease that has nothing to do with crocodiles or currents, you are brushing against the territory of the bunyip—a being that is at once water spirit, colonial monster, and cryptid puzzle. Its body refuses to settle: enormous starfish in one story, seal-like predator in another, chimeric swamp creature in paintings and children’s books. Yet beneath the shifting shapes lies a constant: the bunyip marks water as a place of power, danger, and law. To follow its trail is to move from Aboriginal cosmologies to newspaper hoaxes, from lost carvings on creek banks to modern monster ecology, and to confront how colonization turned a local spirit into a national "monster" while Indigenous communities kept telling stories about the deep.

In Aboriginal traditions, the bunyip is typically characterized as a dangerous, sometimes punitive being associated with water. It may attack or devour humans who approach forbidden or unsafe places, especially at night or in disregard of cultural protocols. Stories often function as cautionary tales, warning children and travelers against swimming in deep holes, wandering near swamps, or ignoring elders’ guidance. The bunyip’s behavior is thus morally charged: it enforces respect for water and country. Personality traits in Indigenous accounts are less individualized than in later folklore

Powers
Aquatic predation and drowning

In many Aboriginal narratives, the bunyip has the ability to seize, drag, or drown humans and animals who enter or approach its water domain. This power is often implicit rather than anatomically detailed: the being is simply known to kill those who disrespect the water or ignore warnings. The Challicum bunyip story, in which a bunyip kills an Aboriginal man before being speared, exemplifies this lethal capacity.

Limit: This power is typically bound to specific places and conditions: deep waterholes, swamps, and river stretches, often at night or under taboo-breaking circumstances. Those who follow cultural protocols, avoid dangerous waters, or receive protection from elders and spirits may be safe.

Territorial guardianship of water places

Beyond predation, the bunyip functions as a guardian or enforcer of the sanctity of certain water sites. Its presence marks places that are spiritually charged, ecologically sensitive, or historically dangerous. By instilling fear, the bunyip helps regulate human movement and behavior in wetlands, contributing to what Weinstein calls "monster ecology"—a mutual shaping of monsters, people, and landscapes.

Limit: This guardianship is not universal; it applies to specific sites and communities. The bunyip does not protect humans in a benevolent sense but enforces boundaries; its role can be ambivalent, both protective of country and dangerous to individuals.

Terrifying voice or roar

Early colonial reports describe the bunyip as possessing a "terrible voice" that creates terror among Aboriginal people, sometimes likened to roaring or bellowing. This auditory power contributes to its reputation as a fearsome being and may reflect the sounds of real animals (e.g., bitterns, seals) reinterpreted within spirit lore.

Limit: The voice is heard near water at night or in remote wetlands; it is rarely associated with direct visual sightings. Some Indigenous accounts focus more on presence than sound, so the roaring motif may be partly colonial.

Shape-shifting or chimeric form

The bunyip’s highly variable descriptions—starfish-like, seal-like, dog-headed, horse-headed, feathered, furred—suggest a capacity for shape-shifting or, more cautiously, a chimeric conceptualization rather than a fixed body. In Indigenous contexts, spirits are not constrained by zoological categories and may appear differently to different witnesses or in different stories.

Limit: Shape-shifting is more an interpretive reconstruction than an explicitly attested power; few early sources state outright that the bunyip changes form. Instead, scholars infer this from the diversity of descriptions.

Moral and ecological warning function

The bunyip’s most significant "power" in Indigenous and colonial folklore is its capacity to shape human behavior through fear and story. By embodying the dangers of water, it warns against drowning, environmental disrespect, and colonial intrusion into wetlands. Weinstein’s concept of "monster-human mutualism" highlights how such beings help communities articulate anxieties about changing environments.

Limit: This function depends on active storytelling and cultural transmission; where bunyip lore is forgotten or trivialized, its warning power diminishes.

Accounts I
The Challicum BunyipReport in The Australasian (1851) and later antiquarian accounts (Reynell Johns)

Mid-19th century (story likely older in oral tradition)

According to nineteenth-century reports, Aboriginal people near Fiery Creek carved an outline figure into the creek bank representing a bunyip that had been speared after killing an Aboriginal man. The carving, approximately eleven paces long and four paces wide, was retraced annually by the community, preserving the memory of the event. The story combines lethal predation (the bunyip killing a man) with communal resistance (spearing the bunyip) and ritual remembrance (maintaining the carving). The bunyip’s killing of the man reinforces its reputation as a dangerous being associated with water. The community’s spearing of the bunyip and annual retracing of its image create a ritualized memory site, embedding the story in the landscape and marking the creek as spiritually charged. The carving’s later disappearance illustrates the vulnerability of Indigenous material heritage under colonial environmental and land-use change.

Moorundi Water Spirit of the Murray RiverGeorge French Angas, "Savage Life and Scenes in Australia and New Zealand" (1847)

Pre-contact oral tradition recorded mid-19th century

Angas reports that the Moorundi people fear a water spirit inhabiting the Murray River. They have difficulty describing it, but its most usual form is said to be that of an enormous starfish. The being is much dreaded and associated with the river’s depths. While Angas does not explicitly use the term "bunyip" in the quoted passage, later commentators connect this water spirit to bunyip traditions, highlighting a starfish-like variant of the being. The story underscores the bunyip’s role as a feared inhabitant of major rivers, shaping how people approach and conceptualize the water. The unusual starfish form challenges later mammalian-centric bunyip imagery and reveals the diversity of Indigenous water-spirit representations.

Accounts II
Ngarrindjeri Mulyawonk StoriesPhilip Weinstein, "Monster ecology: Australian Bunyips and thylacines interacting with people in changing environments" (MPhil thesis, ANU, 2025)

Contemporary oral tradition with deep historical roots

Weinstein’s fieldwork documents Ngarrindjeri stories of Mulyawonk, a water-associated being linked to bunyip traditions. Community members describe Mulyawonk as inhabiting certain river stretches and wetlands, punishing those who disrespect the water or ignore cultural protocols. The being is integral to the cultural landscape, reflecting anxieties about environmental change, colonial disruption, and the safety of children and travelers. Mulyawonk stories reinforce Ngarrindjeri law and ecological awareness, guiding behavior around water and articulating concerns about pollution, climate change, and loss of traditional knowledge. They also demonstrate the continuity and adaptation of bunyip-like beings in contemporary Indigenous life.

Colonial Bunyip Mania: The Murrumbidgee SkullPort Phillip Patriot (1847) and related newspaper reports; later analysis in Quirk (2023)

1840s

In the mid-1840s, colonial newspapers reported the discovery of a strange skull near the Murrumbidgee River, exhibited as possible evidence of the bunyip. The skull, said to have a single large eye socket and unusual features, attracted public attention and scientific debate. Naturalists later argued that the skull belonged to a deformed foal or another known animal, but the episode fueled bunyip mania, with newspapers publishing speculative descriptions and illustrations of the creature. The Murrumbidgee skull episode transformed the bunyip from an Indigenous water spirit into a colonial cryptid, embedding it in debates about science, hoax, and national identity. It also exemplifies how Indigenous knowledge was appropriated and distorted in settler media.
