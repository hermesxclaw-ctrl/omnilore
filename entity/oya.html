Who It Is

Oya (Oyá, Iansã) is the Yoruba orisha of wind, storms, lightning, death, and radical transformation. She is a warrior queen of immense power — the whirlwind that clears away the old to make way for the new, the sudden storm that reshapes the landscape, and the guardian of the cemetery gates who escorts souls to the realm of the ancestors. Her name is synonymous with sudden, irreversible change — the kind that tears down what no longer serves and leaves fertile ground behind.

Physically, Oya is depicted as a fierce, commanding woman, often dressed in burgundy, purple, or the multicolored fabrics of nine different patterns — her sacred number. She carries a sword or machete and an iruke (fly whisk made of a horse's tail) and is frequently shown in the midst of a cyclone, her skirts whirling like the tornadoes she commands. In Afro-Caribbean traditions, Oya is associated with the colors of the rainbow (nine distinct colors), copper, and the lightning bolt. Her presence is announced by the rustling of leaves and the sudden gust of wind that precedes a storm.

Oya's mythology intertwines closely with Shango, her husband and the god of thunder. While Shango hurls the lightning bolt, Oya is the wind that carries it. Together they form a divine partnership of storm energy — thunder and wind united in cosmic rhythm. According to myth, Oya gained her power over storms when Shango, fleeing his enemies, shared his magical lightning charm with her. She swallowed it and became the only female orisha who can command lightning. Her relationship with Ogun is also significant — in some traditions, she was originally Ogun's wife, but Shango's passion (and superior charm) won her away.

Oya's domain over death and cemeteries makes her one of the most formidable orishas. She is not death itself but the guardian of the transition between life and death, standing at the graveyard gates alongside the ancestors (Egungun). She is the orisha of the marketplace — traditionally located near cemeteries in Yoruba towns, symbolizing the commerce between living and dead. In Santería, Oyá is syncretized with Saint Teresa of Avila and Our Lady of Candlemas. Her offerings include eggplant, grapes, dark chocolate, and red wine. Her children are said to be intense, passionate, and unafraid of radical change. She is invoked for protection in crisis, for courage in transformation, and for the strength to let go of what must be released.
