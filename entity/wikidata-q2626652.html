Who It Is

At the rim of the Aztec underworld, where the roads of the ordinary dead slant down into Mictlan, there crouches a thing that is all black puma and rot — a guardian so 'horrid and gigantic' that its very shape reads like a warning, and whose roar is said to be so terrible that no living soul would ever set foot toward the gate it guards. Its name is Acolmiztli, 'Armed Puma' or, if you prefer the spelling that wanders the Spanish and Russian astrologers' heavens, Acolnahuácatl — a 'strong cat' of the dead, the smallest and strangest of the chamberlains of the Lord of Mictlan. And yet this guardian of an unmourned threshold is no anonymous footnote: the most celebrated poet-king of ancient Mexico, Nezahualcóyotl the Fasting Coyote of Texcoco, carried this deathkeeper's name in his own — Nezahualcoyotl Acolmiztli — binding the roaring gatekeeper of the dead to the most radiant, bookish, and

Attested: this is a guardian first and a personality second. Its defining behavior is guarding the entrance to the underworld and deterring the living from entering the realm of the dead; its supernatural roar is the mechanism of deterrence ('so terrible that the living dared not go underground'). It is presented as a monstrous, terrifying threshold-watcher rather than a tutelary or communicative god. No myths narrate it speaking, acting, or interacting with named heroes; its role is custodial and menacing.

Powers
Threshold Guardianship of Mictlan

The deity's PRIMARY attested function: as warder of the underworld entrance, it stands between the world of the living and the realm of the dead, ensuring that the living cannot pass into Mictlan. This is expressed in the Spanish article as guarding 'que los vivos no entraran al reino de los muertos' — that the living not enter the kingdom of the dead.

Limit: The god guards the threshold but does not govern the underworld overall — that is Mictlantecuhtli's dominion. Its authority is custodial/boundary, not sovereign. No source describes it judging, punishing, or escorting specific souls.

Supernatural Roar

The deity's roar is singled out in the sources as so terrible that it terrified the living and prevented them from going underground into Mictlan. The roar is the visible (audible) instrument of its deterrent guardianship — an apotropaic, awe-inflicting sound.

Limit: The roar functions against the living who approach the underworld; no source describes its effect on the dead, gods, or denizens of Mictlan. Its range or intensity beyond 'terrifying' is unspecified.

Monstrous Puma Form

Manifestation as a massive black puma with a corpse-like, cadaverous aspect. The animal form is integral to its identity; 'miztli' (puma/large feline) is itself embedded in its name Acolmiztli. It is classified as a 'monstruosa criatura menor' (monstrous minor creature) of Mictlan.

Limit: Limited to the monster-guardian register; there is no attested humanoid or anthropomorphized cult form, no divine regalia, and no independent shrine iconography associated with the puma body.

Connection to Royal Acolhua Naming (honorific association)

The deity's name was borne by royalty: Nezahualcóyotl, the famed poet-king (tlatoani) of Texcoco, carried 'Acolmiztli' in his full name ('Nezahualcoyotl Acolmiztli') and was, per one formulation, 'born Acolmiztli'. The Spanish article states Nezahualcóyotl 'carried his name in his honor', implying a patronal or honorific bond between the netherworld guardian and the Acolhua crown.

Limit: The precise nature of the bond is not agreed: honorific naming, patronal devotion, or onomastic coincidence are all offered. The deity itself plays no active role in the king's documented biography.

Domains — The underworld, Mictlan — specifically its threshold/entrance, where the deity stands as warder against the living. Accounts I
The Warder of the Underworld's DoorWikipedia en español 'Acolnahuácatl'; Russian Wikipedia 'Аколмистли' (via Wikipedia Direct API, 2026-08-01)

Pre-Columbian tradition, preserved in modern encyclopedias

No extended narrative survives in which Acolmiztli acts as a character; the tradition is a single, stark image rather than a plotted myth. The god is a colossal, black, cadaverous puma that crouches at the entrance to Mictlan, the Aztec underworld, and lets out a supernatural roar so terrible that the living are unable to pass down into the kingdom of the dead. In the wider cosmology, ordinary human dead descended into Mictlan through a long journey of tests and obstacles over four years, with the dog-headed god Xolotl serving as a guide of souls; the puma-god occupies the boundary — the front gate — of that realm. The 'story' of Acolmiztli, such as it is, is the story of a threshold nobody living may cross. The deity establishes the underworld as an impermeable province of the dead — the central fact of the Aztec afterlife cosmology that ordinary death is final for the living and guarded by fearsome boundary-keepers. The living fear a realm to which only the dying have passage.

Accounts II
The God Whose Name the Poet-King BoreWikipedia en español 'Acolnahuácatl'; Russian Wikipedia 'Аколмистли'; Nezahualcoyotl (tlatoani) on English Wikipedia (via Wikipedia Direct API, 2026-08-01)

15th century (reign of Nezahualcóyotl, 1402-1472), documented in early colonial histories

The most consequential recorded 'use' of Acolmiztli takes place not in the underworld but in the courts of Acolhuacan. Nezahualcóyotl, the celebrated philosopher-poet and tlatoani of Texcoco — the greatest figure of Acolhua royal culture and a co-founder of the Aztec Triple Alliance — carried the underworld puma-god's name throughout his life. His full recorded name is Nezahualcoyotl Acolmiztli; colonial historian traditions frame him as 'born Acolmiztli', and the Spanish article explicitly states that he bore the deity's name 'in its honor'. The name thus crossed from the grim, roaring gatekeeper of Mictlan to the humane, philosophizing 'Fasting Coyote' of Texcoco — an emblematic bridge between deathly guardian spirit and the living Acolhua dynasty. This naming is the single strongest claim to the deity's cultural weight: it places Acolmiztli within the intimate onomastic world of the Acolhua elite and links the 'minor' underworld creature to one of pre-Columbian Mexico's most famous kings.
