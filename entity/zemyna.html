Who It Is

In the deep forests and fertile fields of the Baltic lands, before the thunder of Perkūnas split the sky, the earth herself was a living, breathing goddess. Žemyna, the Mother Earth of Lithuanian tradition, was not a distant deity but the very ground beneath one's feet—the source of all life and the silent receiver of all death. Her cult, woven into daily rituals and whispered prayers, reveals a profound intimacy between a people and their land, a bond that survived centuries of change and still echoes in modern hymns.

Nurturing mother who nourishes all life; also associated with death as she receives the dead. She is benevolent but requires respect and offerings. She is addressed with affectionate diminutive names, indicating a close, personal relationship with worshippers.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Lithuanian 'žemė' meaning 'earth'. The name stems from the Proto-Indo-European Earth-goddess *Dʰéǵʰōm, related to Thracian Zemele ('mother earth') and Greek Semelē (Σεμέλη).

Pronunciation — Že-mī-na (approximate: ZHEH-mee-nah)

Aliases — Žemynėlė, Žemelė, Zemes māte (Latvian counterpart)

Ownership — Lithuanian; also recognized in broader Baltic context as similar to Latvian Zemes māte.

Powers
Fertility of the earth

Žemyna personifies the fertile earth and nourishes all life—human, plant, and animal. She ensures bountiful harvests and the continuation of life.

Limit: The earth requires fertilization by the heavens (rain and thunder); it was prohibited to plow or sow before the first thunder, or the earth would be barren.

Receiving the dead

Žemyna acts as ruler and guardian of the dead, receiving them into the earth upon death. All that is born of earth will return to earth.

Limit: None specified.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No specific physical description is recorded. Žemyna is likely envisioned as a maternal figure, but no iconography is attested. She is personified as the earth itself.

Symbols — Earth, fields, bread, beer, rooster/hen, black piglet. The act of kissing the earth is a symbolic gesture. No known visual depictions from ancient times.

Domains — The earth itself—fields, forests, and the underworld. She is present wherever the ground is, and her realm includes both the living surface and the abode of the dead beneath.

Accounts I
The Ritual of ŽemyneliautiMatthäus Prätorius, 'Deliciae Prussicae'

17th century (recorded)

At major celebrations (e.g., weddings) or agricultural works (e.g., harvest), the head of the household would drink a cup of beer, first spilling some on the ground and saying a short prayer. Then he would kill a rooster or hen, which would be cooked and eaten by the family. Each family member received a loaf of bread and said prayers, blessings, and greetings. The bones and scraps were sacrificed to the goddess (burned or buried). Ensured Žemyna's favor for fertility and prosperity.

Accounts II
Kissing the EarthFolklore

Traditional

People would kiss the earth saying a short prayer thanking Žemyna for all her gifts and acknowledging that one day they will return to her. Showed gratitude and humility before the goddess.
