Who It Is

Žemyna is the Baltic earth goddess, the fertile mother who receives seeds, nourishes crops, and ultimately takes back all bodies in death. Her name is directly derived from the word for 'earth' (Latvian zeme, Lithuanian žemė). She is one of the oldest and most fundamental Baltic deities, worshipped long before the Indo-European sky gods arrived. Žemyna was never fully demonized — she remained a figure of reverence even into the 20th century in rural Latvia and Lithuania. Rituals to Žemyna involved pouring beer or milk on the ground after plowing, leaving the last sheaf of grain in the field as her portion, and whispering prayers to the soil before planting. She is the mother who gives life and receives death: the first handful of earth thrown on a coffin was an offering to Žemyna. She is also the guardian of boundaries — a furrow plowed in her name creates a sacred line that evil spirits cannot cross. In some traditions, she is paired with Dievs as the primordial parents of the world: Dievs above and Žemyna below, their union producing all life. Her color is dark brown and green, and her symbols are the plow, the furrow, and the open hand. She has no temples — every field, every garden, every patch of forest floor is her shrine.

The Approach

In the deep forests and fertile fields of the Baltic lands, before the thunder of Perkūnas split the sky, the earth herself was a living, breathing goddess. Žemyna, the Mother Earth of Lithuanian tradition, was not a distant deity but the very ground beneath one's feet—the source of all life and the silent receiver of all death. Her cult, woven into daily rituals and whispered prayers, reveals a profound intimacy between a people and their land, a bond that survived centuries of change and still echoes in modern hymns.

Identity

Žemyna

Pronunciation Že-mī-na (approximate: ZHEH-mee-nah)

Known As

ŽemynėlėŽemelėZemes māte (Latvian counterpart)

Etymology

From Lithuanian 'žemė' meaning 'earth'. The name stems from the Proto-Indo-European Earth-goddess *Dʰéǵʰōm, related to Thracian Zemele ('mother earth') and Greek Semelē (Σεμέλη).

Cultural Ownership

Lithuanian; also recognized in broader Baltic context as similar to Latvian Zemes māte.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars associate Žemyna with the Greek Semele due to etymological links, but this is debated. There is also discussion about her relationship with male earth deities like Žemėpatis and Žemininkas, and whether they are separate entities or aspects of the same cult.

Description

Appearance

No specific physical description is recorded. Žemyna is likely envisioned as a maternal figure, but no iconography is attested. She is personified as the earth itself.

Symbols & Iconography

Earth, fields, bread, beer, rooster/hen, black piglet. The act of kissing the earth is a symbolic gesture. No known visual depictions from ancient times.

Behaviour & Personality

Nurturing mother who nourishes all life; also associated with death as she receives the dead. She is benevolent but requires respect and offerings. She is addressed with affectionate diminutive names, indicating a close, personal relationship with worshippers.

Domains & Habitats

The earth itself—fields, forests, and the underworld. She is present wherever the ground is, and her realm includes both the living surface and the abode of the dead beneath.

Version Differences

After the baptism of Lithuania, Žemyna's image and functions became influenced by the cult of the Virgin Mary. This syncretism may have altered some perceptions, but pre-Christian traditions persisted in folklore.

Powers & Abilities

Fertility of the earth

Source Version General Lithuanian belief

Description

Žemyna personifies the fertile earth and nourishes all life—human, plant, and animal. She ensures bountiful harvests and the continuation of life.

Limitations

The earth requires fertilization by the heavens (rain and thunder); it was prohibited to plow or sow before the first thunder, or the earth would be barren.

None recorded.

Stories & Myths

The Ritual of Žemyneliauti

Source Matthäus Prätorius, 'Deliciae Prussicae'Culture Lithuanian/PrussianPeriod 17th century (recorded)

Summary

At major celebrations (e.g., weddings) or agricultural works (e.g., harvest), the head of the household would drink a cup of beer, first spilling some on the ground and saying a short prayer. Then he would kill a rooster or hen, which would be cooked and eaten by the family. Each family member received a loaf of bread and said prayers, blessings, and greetings. The bones and scraps were sacrificed to the goddess (burned or buried).

Participants

Head of household

Family members

Consequences

Ensured Žemyna's favor for fertility and prosperity.

Variants

Other rites included burying bread baked from last crops of prior harvest in a field before new sowing and sacrifice of a black piglet.

Kissing the Earth

Source FolkloreCulture LithuanianPeriod Traditional

Summary

Animal sacrifice

Source Matthäus Prätorius

Description

Rituals involved killing a rooster or hen, and in some cases a black piglet, as offerings to Žemyna. The bones and scraps were burned or buried.

Scholarly Context

Animal sacrifice was common in pre-Christian Baltic religion and should be understood in its historical and cultural context.

Contradictions & Disputes

Husband of Žemyna

Position A

Žemyna is married to Perkūnas, the thunder god.

Position A — evidence

Historical sources on Baltic mythology often pair earth goddess with sky god.

Position B

Žemyna is married to Praamžius, a manifestation of the chief god Dievas.

Position B — evidence

Other sources mention Praamžius as her consort.

Scholarly Consensus

There is no consensus; both traditions exist, reflecting regional or temporal variations.

Connection to Semele

Position A

Žemyna is etymologically related to Greek Semele, indicating a common Indo-European origin.

Position A — evidence

Both names derive from *Dʰéǵʰōm.

Position B

The resemblance is coincidental or due to later borrowing.

Position B — evidence

The Greek Semele is a mortal in myth, not a goddess of earth.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars accept the etymological link but note divergences in mythology.

Identity of male earth deities

Position A

Žemėpatis and Žemininkas are separate deities.

Position A — evidence

They have distinct names and are described as male.

Position B

They are aspects or hypostases of Žemyna.

Position B — evidence

They share similar functions related to earth fertility.

Scholarly Consensus

The relationship is unclear due to sparse sources.
