Who It Is

Before Lady Justice ever donned her blindfold, before the scales of justice became a courtroom cliché, there was Dike—a goddess who didn't just hold the scales, she was the scales. She was the cosmic referee, the celestial auditor, the one who looked at humanity's greed and said, 'I'm out,' and literally flew to the stars to avoid watching the mess we were making. She's the reason we look up at the night sky and see a virgin and a set of scales, a permanent reminder that justice is not just a human invention, but a cosmic law.

Dike is the embodiment of moral order and fair judgment. She is not a capricious or vengeful goddess; rather, she is the principle that ensures the world operates according to a just order. She is the attendant (paredros) of Zeus, sitting beside him and advising him. She reports the injustices of mortals to him, as described by Hesiod. She is sickened by human greed and wickedness, as in Aratus's account, and chooses to abandon the world rather than participate in its corruption. Powers
Personification of Justice

As the living embodiment of 'dike' (justice, moral order), she is not a being who 'uses' a power, but rather the very principle itself. Her presence or absence defines whether a society or an action is just.

Limit: She does not enforce justice directly; she reports to Zeus, who acts. She is a principle, not a warrior.

Divine Retribution

In the myth of Meletus and Timagoras, Dike acts as an avenger, driving Timagoras to suicide after he caused Meletus's death. This suggests a power to inflict punishment or compel fate.

Limit: This is a rare and specific instance; it is not her standard mode of operation.

Celestial Abode

After leaving Earth, Dike took her place in the heavens as the constellation Virgo. Her scales became the constellation Libra. This grants her a cosmic perspective, watching over humanity from above.

Limit: This is a mythological explanation of the constellations, not a power in the traditional sense.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Dike is consistently depicted as a young, slender woman. Her most distinctive attribute is a balance scale (the symbol of Libra), which she holds in her hand. She is also frequently shown wearing a laurel wreath on her head. In art, she may carry a staff or a sword, symbolizing authority and punishment. In the depiction on the Chest of Cypselus, she is explicitly contrasted with the ugly Adikia (Injustice), suggesting Dike is attractive and fair.

Symbols — The balance scale (the constellation Libra), the laurel wreath, a staff or rod, a sword, wings (when identified with Astraea), the constellation Virgo.

Domains — Justice, moral order, fair judgment, human customs and social norms, legal process, courtrooms, the concept of 'right'. She rules over human justice, while her mother Themis rules over divine justice. She is the goddess of the 'dike' that governs human society, as opposed to the 'themis' that governs the cosmic and divine order. Accounts I
The Birth of DikeHesiod, Theogony

c. 700 BCE

Dike is born to Zeus and Themis, his second consort. She is one of the three Horae (Seasons), alongside her sisters Eunomia (Order) and Eirene (Peace). They are the second set of Horae born to Zeus; the first set (Thallo, Auxo, and Carpo) are also his daughters. Dike's birth establishes her as a fundamental principle of the cosmic order, a daughter of the supreme god and a sister to Order and Peace.

The Departure of Dike (Astraea)Aratus, Phaenomena

c. 3rd century BCE

During the Golden Age, Dike lived among mortals on Earth. In the Silver Age, she retreated to the mountains, but was still present. As humanity grew greedy and warlike in the Bronze Age, she was sickened by their behavior. She proclaimed that they would breed a viler progeny, and that wars and cruel bloodshed would come upon them. She then left the Earth entirely, ascending to the heavens, where she became the constellation Virgo. After her departure, the world declined further, and diseases arose. This myth explains the origin of the constellation Virgo and the presence of evil and disease in the world. It also serves as an etiology for the decline of the ages of man. Dike's departure marks a definitive break between the divine and human realms.

Accounts II
The Avenger of MeletusPtolemy Hephaestion (via Photius, Bibliotheca)

c. 1st-2nd century CE

A youth named Meletus fell in love with a boy named Timagoras. Timagoras rejected him cruelly, and Meletus, in despair, threw himself off a rock and died. Timagoras, showing no remorse, went to the rock and mocked Meletus's fate. Dike, enraged by this cruelty, compelled Timagoras to throw himself from the same rock, killing him. This story demonstrates Dike's role as an avenger of injustice, particularly against the cruel and arrogant. It is a moral lesson about the consequences of hybris (arrogance) and cruelty.

Dike at the Temple of Zeus at OlympiaPausanias, Description of Greece

c. 2nd century CE

The sculptures of the Temple of Zeus at Olympia, one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, were conceived with the 'dike' of Zeus as their unifying theme. The pediments and metopes depict scenes of divine justice, such as the labors of Heracles and the contest between Pelops and Oenomaus. Dike is thus the underlying principle of the temple's iconography, even if not directly depicted. This demonstrates the central importance of 'dike' in Greek religious thought. The temple was not just a monument to Zeus's power, but to his justice.

Paul on MaltaActs of the Apostles, 28:1-6

c. 1st-2nd century CE

After being shipwrecked on the island of Malta, the Apostle Paul is bitten by a viper while gathering firewood. The local people see this and conclude, 'No doubt this man is a murderer! Although he has escaped from the sea, Justice herself has not allowed him to live!' (NET). When Paul does not die, they change their minds and think he is a god. This passage shows that the concept of Dike (Justice) as a divine agent of punishment was still common in the 1st century CE, even among non-Greeks. It also demonstrates the early Christian engagement with and reinterpretation of pagan concepts.
