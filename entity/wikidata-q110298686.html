Who It Is

Tear open a bag of surume and you are holding the ocean made chewable — pale, fibrous shreds that smell of tide-flat and smoke, sweet and salty at once, that fight your teeth just enough to make you keep chewing. Called sakiika in Tokyo izakayas, yóu yú sī in Hong Kong department stores, ojingeochae in Seoul, and once sold as the 'Chewing Gum of the Orientals' in Singapore mama shops, this is not fresh squid but squid transformed by heat, sugar, time and memory into a pan-Asian bar snack, side dish, and nostalgia object.

As food, behavior is textural and social: requires sustained chewing, releasing sweet-salty-umami gradually; functions as otsumami/anju (food that prolongs drinking without filling) and as banchan that encourages rice consumption. Socially it is shareable, bag-passed, often eaten with hands. Preparation 'behavior' is transformation: raw squid is skinned, cooked 65-80°C for 3-5 min, cooled, grated/seasoned below 20°C for >4 hours with sugar, salt, sorbitol, sweeteners, organic acids and MSG, dried 40-45°C for 12-20 hours to 40% moisture, cold-aged two weeks+, flash-dried 110-120

Preservation and Shelf Stability

Drying to 25-27% moisture plus sugar/salt and vacuum/nitrogen packing inhibits microbial growth, allowing months of ambient storage without refrigeration. Cold aging and two-stage drying create intermediate-moisture food.

Limit: Not sterile; will mold if package breached or stored humid. Browning and amino-acid changes continue in storage; nitrogen packing slows but does not stop browning.

Umami Concentration and Flavor Delivery

Heat and drying concentrate free amino acids (glycine, alanine, proline), nucleotides and added MSG, producing intense sweet-savory-funky umami with sugar-chili-salt balance. Chewing slowly releases flavor, making it effective as drinking snack.

Limit: Flavor depends on squid species and seasoning ratio; over-drying or excessive browning produces bitter notes. MSG content varies by brand and is undisclosed in exact ratios.

Textural Work and Satiety Control

Fibrous, chewy-tough shreds require prolonged mastication ('jaw workout'), slowing consumption, promoting satiety with small volume, and pairing with alcohol by delaying intoxication without heavy fullness — the otsumami/anju function.

Limit: Can be tough for children, elderly, or those with dental issues. Texture hardens further if not airtight.

Culinary Versatility (Transformation)

Eaten straight from bag, or as ingredient: stir-fried as ojingeochae bokkeum, added to almond biscuits in Macau, tossed into salads, or grilled after rehydration. Acts as flavor base and protein.

Limit: Pre-seasoned product limits use in delicate dishes; high sugar/salt restricts dietary uses. Cannot be reconstituted to fresh squid texture.

Nutritional Density (Protein) with Sodium/Sugar Cost

High protein (~40-60% dry weight), low fat, contains taurine and minerals; but also high sodium and added sugars/sweeteners. Provides portable protein.

Limit: High sodium unsuitable for hypertension diets; sugar content significant. Cholesterol relatively high. Contamination risk (arsenic, cadmium) in poorly sourced product.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Pale to yellow-brown fibrous shreds, 1-3mm wide and 3-10cm long, with a slightly glossy, dry but pliable surface. Bulk form resembles coarse, off-white to amber shredded jerky or wood shavings. Vacuum or nitrogen-packed bags keep it chewy-tough; exposed product hardens. Prolonged storage deepens color to brown via Maillard and amino-acid browning, which consumers generally dislike. Thai/Loligo rolled variants are instead thin, flat sheets rolled

Accounts I
Pacific Coast Preservation — From Subsistence to SurumeJapanese fisheries history and FAO processing literature; Edo-period references to surume as preserved seafood

Pre-modern to early 20th century (attested Edo period 1603-1868 onward)

In Pacific coastal villages from Hokkaido to Kyushu, squid caught in autumn (especially Todarodes pacificus, surume-ika) was split, gutted and sun-dried on beach racks to survive winter. This whole dried squid (surume) became a staple protein, tribute good, and sake accompaniment. Industrialization in the 20th century mechanized the process — cooking, seasoning, controlled drying and shredding — turning whole dried squid into uniform sakiika shreds for mass retail and export, with the Japanese word surume imprinted on packages for English markets as 'dried shredded squid'. Created shelf-stable commodity that spread beyond fishing villages; established Japan as technical leader in dried squid processing that China and Korea later scaled.

Hong Kong in the 1970s — The Department Store Bag SnackHong Kong popular food histories and contemporary packaging history

1970s

After packaged surume/yóu yú sī began shipping to English-speaking regions, the snack was popularized and consumed regularly in Hong Kong during the 1970s, sold in bags in department stores and later supermarkets as a light snack (ling sik). Sweet-savory shredded squid fit Hong Kong's emerging convenience-snack market alongside shrimp chips and preserved plums. Established yóu yú sī as everyday Hong Kong snack idiom; created demand that later led to food-safety scrutiny and media reports of contamination in Hong Kong-packaged product.

Singapore's Chewing Gum of the Orientals — Pon Pon to Ken KenNational Heritage Board Roots.sg, Images of Singapore museum exhibit, Ken Ken company history

1960s-1992 and beyond

In early 1960s Singapore, seasoned prepared dried shredded squid was sold as Pon Pon, later as Ken Ken, in mama (mamak) shops — small provision stores that supplied daily amenities. Manufacturer Ken Ken marketed it with the tagline 'Chewing gum of the Orientals' in the 1970s, leaning into its prolonged chew. When Singapore banned chewing gum in 1992, the slogan stuck as nostalgic irony. The green-and-white Ken Ken packet became a childhood icon for older Singaporeans, alongside White Rabbit candy and haw flakes, before large chain supermarkets displaced mama shops and western snacks (chocolate, chips) displaced cuttlefish for younger consumers. The packaging is now museum-displayed at Sentosa's Images of Singapore. Product became heritage symbol; Ken Ken Cuttlefish remains available in major supermarkets as nostalgic line, but declining share among youth.

Accounts II
Japanese Otsumami — Surume with Sake and BeerJapanese izakaya culture guides and food ethnographies

Post-war Showa era (1950s-present), with roots earlier

In Japan, dried shredded squid (sakiika/surume) is classic otsumami — snacks consumed while drinking alcohol, not as meal. Served in small dishes in izakayas and at home, often with mayonnaise and shichimi togarashi, or grilled briefly (aburi surume) to release aroma. Its chew slows drinking and its umami complements beer and sake. It sits alongside other otsumami like cheese, edamame and jerky. Codified surume as drinking-culture food rather than confection; influenced product seasoning toward savory-smoky rather than candy-sweet.

Korean Anju and Banchan — Ojingeochae BokkeumKorean cookbooks and Korea Agro-Fisheries & Food Trade Corporation; recipe literature for ojingeochae bokkeum

1970s-present (modern banchan codification)

In Korean cuisine, dried shredded squid (ojingeochae) is eaten as anju (food to eat while drinking, especially soju and beer) and as banchan (small side dishes). The signature preparation ojingeochae bokkeum stir-fries the dry shreds in a glossy sauce of gochujang (chili pepper paste), garlic, soy sauce, mullyeot (corn syrup-like condiment, also called mulyeot), sesame oil and sometimes peanuts, turning pale shreds into sweet-spicy, chewy, red-coated strands served with rice or as dosirak side. Made dried squid a cooked dish, not just bag snack; spread Korean-style sweet-spicy profile internationally via Korean diaspora and K-food wave.

Macau Almond Biscuit and Singapore Mama Shop — CrossoversMacau food histories and Singapore oral histories of mama shops

1970s-1990s

In Macau, shredded squid began being sold as an addition to the local almond biscuit (almond cookie) shops, offering savory-sweet pairing for tourists. In Singapore, the same product lived in mama shops — street-corner provision stores that predated supermarkets — where children bought single-serve Ken Ken packets after school. Both cases show shredded squid as impulse, low-price snack embedded in small-shop economies before supermarket consolidation. Linked shredded squid to place-based food tourism (Macau) and to disappearing retail heritage (Singapore mama shops).

Dried shredded squid and dried shredded cuttlefish are different products. — In most retail contexts they are the same product with interchangeable English labeling; both usually contain squid (often Todarodes pacificus or Dosidicus gigas), not true cuttlefish, due to translation conflation.

The 'Chewing gum of the Orientals' was created because Singapore banned chewing gum. — The tagline was used by Ken Ken in the 1970s (and Pon Pon in early 1960s) before the 1992 chewing gum ban; the ban made the slogan more memorable in retrospect, but did not create it.

Dried shredded squid is a healthy, light diet snack because it is seafood. — It is high protein but also high in sodium and added sugar/sweeteners and MSG; some samples have shown heavy-metal contamination. Portion is light, nutrition is dense and salty.

All dried squid snacks are shredded. — Not all are shredded; some are rolled or flattened (e.g., Thai-style Loligo rolled squid) using smaller, more tender species, with different texture.

MSG in shredded squid makes it dangerous or causes 'Chinese Restaurant Syndrome' headaches. — At typical seasoning levels MSG is recognized as safe by JECFA, FDA and EFSA; double-blind studies do not support headache claims at culinary doses. Some 'no MSG' products use yeast extract with similar glutamates.
