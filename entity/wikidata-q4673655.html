Who It Is

In the high mountain valleys of central Tibet, when the Drikung monks prepared to face invaders, drought, or the drift of teachings into neglect, they did not summon an abstract cosmic Buddha. They called on their Grandmother. Achi Chokyi Drolma — whose very name fuses the most domestic of Tibetan kinship terms with the exalted salvific title of the goddess Tārā — is the odd religious founding figure who crosses the boundary between a remembered human matriarch and an eternal protector. A child conceived to the double omen of a sun and a white-light rosary, she refused every suitor, chose her own husband against the grain, produced the four sons from whom an entire school of Buddhism was born, and then died the way accomplished yogins do: not by expiring, but by riding a blue horse up into a Buddha Field, her body unbroken. Powers
Dharma protection / ward of obstacles

As the principal dharmapāla of the Drikung Kagyu, she is invoked to shield the lineage's teachings, monasteries and practitioners from both external threats (armed conflicts, enemies, hostile spirits, illness, disasters) and internal spiritual obstacles (meditation hindrances, obscured faith, the arising of negativity).

Limit: Conditioned traditionally on the practitioner's faith, refuge and alignment of ends with the Dharma; protection is not framed as a blank personal insurance.

Vajrayogini emanation and prophecy-fulfilment

The Cakrasaṃvara Tantra prophecy identifies her as a nirmāṇakāya (manifestation) of Vajrayogini who 'will come to Tidro cave in Drikung' as 'the head of the Karma Dakinis'. Her birth in the Drikung valley is read as the fulfillment of that prophecy, grounding her enlightened (not merely human) status.

Limit: This is doctrine, not historical biography; it does not predict any mundane boundary on her power.

Spiritual precocity and teaching

Miraculous early signs: her infant body was 'of purest white and radiating rays of light'; she spontaneously recited the mantra of Tārā as a small child and at age three taught it to others — a motif of innate enlightened competence in the hagiography.

Limit: Hagiographic; the miracle register is not presented as replicable.

Miraculous endowment / matter manipulation

At her own wedding, when the groom had no resources, she 'miraculously pulled a damaru from her right pocket and a kapala from her left', performed a mystic skyward dance, and instantly filled the house with the finest food, drink and clothing for the guests — demonstrated siddhi (accomplishment) over material scarcity.

Limit: Hagiographic miracle; told as a one-time proof of her yogic attainment, not a repeatable service.

Siddhi conferral via tsog offering

At Tingring cave she 'transformed a human corpse into a great tsog offering'; those able to partake of the feast were granted 'the ordinary and supreme siddhis'. She also composed a sadhana (self-practice liturgy) of herself, promising to protect the future essence of the Buddha's teaching.

Limit: Esoteric; partaking is framed as requiring readiness and pure samaya.

Deathless departure / bodily flight

Her death is described as an ascension without the ordinary dissolution of the body: 'My activities through this body have come to an end,' she said, and 'flew up to the Buddha Field on her blue horse without leaving her body.'

Limit: Purely hagiographic; marks her as an enlightened (nirvāṇa-capable) being rather than an ordinary deceased ancestor.

Lineage-ancestor blessing / prophetic protection

She prophesied her great-grandson Jigten Sumgön's birth and 'vowed to protect his lineage' — the core theological engine of her cult: a human matriarch who post-mortem took on eternal protective agency for the lineage she founded by blood.

Limit: The prophecy is a hagiographic motif; its 'efficacy' is a matter of devotional faith.

Form & Signs

Appearance — She is portrayed as a beautiful, youthful-but-maternal white-skinned woman. The white colour is iconographically the pacifying register (śānti activity) — purity, serene transcendence of ordinary grasping. She wears the silk scarves and bone ornaments of a dakini, marking her as an enlightened female figure beyond the bounds of householder status. Accounts I
The Prophecy and the Dream-ConceptionDrikung hagiography as conserved in the Achi Chokyi Drolma article and lineage sources

11th century (traditional setting)

A prophecy in the Cakrasaṃvara Tantra had foretold that 'the head of the Karma Dakinis' would appear at Tidro cave in Drikung as a manifestation of Vajrayogini. Near Shoto in the Drikung region of Ü-Tsang, a childless couple — Driza Dharzam and Nanam Chowopal — made a pilgrimage to Swayambhu in Nepal and prayed fervently for a child. One night the wife dreamed a shining sun rose in the east, dissolved into her womb and flooded the universe with light; that same night the husband dreamed a rosary of clear white light entered his wife's womb. They returned home, performed a ganachakra offering, and soon an extraordinary white-bodied, radiant daughter was born at Kyetrag Thang. The infant recited Tārā's mantra and by age three was teaching it to others. Establishes her as a divinely-foretold, enlightened child whose life is the practical fulfilment of the Cakrasaṃvara prophecy; sets up the lineage-protector role for the rest of the narrative.

The Refusal of Suitors and the Journey to KhamDrikung hagiography (young adulthood narrative)

11th century (traditional setting)

After her parents' early death she lived with an uncle and grew 'incredibly beautiful'. Many sought her hand, but she refused all with a revealing declaration: 'I will go to Kham and there lives a great yogi who is descended from the noble clan of the Kyura race. This yogi I will marry and our sons and daughters and future generations will be extraordinary persons who will benefit all sentient beings by spreading the essence of the Buddha's teachings.' Traveling with a merchant to Kham, she stopped at Dentod Tsonrur, declared 'This is the place I have to stay,' and went to meet the great saint Ame Tsultrim Gyatso, proposing their union as a deliberate instrument to produce enlightened descendants. Converts an arranged-marriage plot into a self-determined yogic vocation: the choice of mate is framed as choosing a vessel for enlightened lineage, not personal romance.

The Miraculous Wedding FeastDrikung hagiography (marriage narrative)

11th century (traditional setting)

On the marriage day Ame Tsultrim Gyatso had no possessions to arrange the ceremony. Achi reassured him, and in a show of yogic mastery pulled a damaru (hand-drum) from her right pocket and a kapala (skull cup) from her left, beat the drum, and made a mystic dance while gazing into the sky. Instantly the house filled with the finest food, drink and garments, satisfying the guests. She then bore four sons — Namkhe Wangchuk, Pekar Wangyal, Sonam Pal and Kathung Trushi — all said to be 'exceptionally intelligent' scholars in both temporal and spiritual matters. Demonstrates her siddhi and establishes the four-branch familial lineage; Pekar Wangyal's line (via grandson Naljor Dorje) produces Jigten Sumgön, the founder of Drikung Kagyu.

Accounts II
The Death-Flight from Tingring CaveDrikung hagiography (death narrative)

12th century (traditional setting)

In her last act she declared she had 'knowingly taken birth into samsara' to protect the Buddha's teachings and would grant ordinary and supreme siddhis to her followers. She led them to the sacred cave Tingring, said to hold termas and self-created statues of Buddhas, Bodhisattvas, Yidams, Dakinis and protectors. She transformed a human corpse into a great tsog offering — those who could partake gained siddhis — then composed a sadhana of herself and promised to guard the Buddha's teaching, specifically the essence of future teachings. Concluding 'My activities through this body have come to an end,' she flew to the Buddha Field on a blue horse without leaving her body. Converts a clan matriarch into a permanent enlightened protector; her sadhana enters lineage liturgy and her post-mortem protection becomes the doctrinal basis of the cult.

The Grandmother's Prophecy of Jigten SumgönJigten Sumgön (Wikipedia); Drikung Kagyu lineage histories

12th century (traditional setting)

Achi Chokyi Drolma, as the great-grandmother of the clan, prophesied the birth of Jigten Sumgön (the future founder of Drikung Kagyu) and vowed to protect his lineage. The prophecy binds the matriarch's pre-mortem words to the founding of the school her descendants would create. Jigten Sumgön (1143-1217), a prodigy — at eight he understood that phenomena are like reflections in a mirror — grew up to study under Phagmo Drupa and founded Drikung Thil Monastery in 1179, making him the single most important religious figure of the Drikung lineage. Cemented her as the lineage ancestor whose prophecy 'founded' the school, elevating a domestic maternal memory into a divine protective covenant.

Protector of the Nyingma Tertön Tsasum LingpaAchi Chokyi Drolma article; Nyingma lineage sources

17th century (traditional setting)

Beyond the Drikung Kagyu, Achi appears as a dharmapāla and dakini in the life story of the Nyingma treasure-revealer (tertön) Tsasum Lingpa (Wylie: rtsa gsum gling pa), a 17th-century crazy-wisdom terma master in the Nyingma tradition. She functions there as a protective presence accompanying his terma-revelation work, showing her reach across the Kagyu/Nyingma divide into the wider terma tradition. Indicates her religious receptivity extended beyond one school; the Nyingma later deployed her as a supporting protector in a terma context, analogously to how Kagyu lineages deployed her.

Achi Chokyi Drolma was the consort of Phagmo Drupa and mother of Jigten Sumgön. — The standard hagiography names her husband as Ame Tsultrim Gyatso and her relation to Jigten Sumgön as great-grandmother (through grandson Naljor Dorje). Phagmo Drupa was Jigten Sumgön's teacher, not Achi's husband.

Achi is just 'a nice grandmother deity' or an exotic 'enlightened grandma' figure. — She is a fully-fledged wrathful dharmapāla wielding a kartika and kapala, charged with subduing hostile forces and guarding the Dharma — her fierce protective register coexists with maternal warmth.

Her 'turning a corpse into a tsog offering' describes literal cannibalism. — Within Vajrayāna ritual symbolism it is a tantric transmutation of the corrupt/dead into the sacred feast, an archetype of mastery over impermanence drawn from the charnel-ground lineages.

She is exclusively a Drikung Kagyu deity. — She also appears as a dharmapāla/dakini in the Nyingma tertön Tsasum Lingpa's life story and in the Karma Kagyu refuge tree as Achi Chodron — a pan-Kagyu figure with a Nyingma incidence.
