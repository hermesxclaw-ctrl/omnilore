Etymology

'Neang' is a Khmer honorific for a woman, often meaning 'lady' or 'princess.' 'Kakey' is derived from the Pali/Sanskrit name 'Kākī,' which appears in the Jataka tales.

Cultural Ownership

Cambodian (Khmer) and Thai (Siamese); the tale is a shared cultural heritage with distinct national versions.

Disputed Identifications

The character is sometimes conflated with the Thai version 'Kaki' from 'Kaki Klon Suphap.' While similar, the two are distinct literary works with differing plots and outcomes.

Archive Dossier — Approach

In the royal court of 19th-century Siam, a young Cambodian prince—soon to be King Ang Duong—penned a melodramatic verse tale of a queen whose beauty and desire lead her down a path of betrayal and tragedy. Neang Kakey, a story of a faithless wife, has become a cornerstone of Khmer literature, a mirror of societal values, and a subject of enduring debate about gender and justice.

I am the daughter of the serpent king, the bride of the Brahmin, the mother of the Khmer. From my bloodline rose Angkor.

Who It Is

Neang Kakey is the legendary naga (serpent) princess who, according to Khmer foundation mythology, married the Indian Brahmin Preah Thong (Kaundinya) to establish the first Kingdom of Cambodia. The story appears in the earliest Khmer inscriptions and historical chronicles. According to the myth, Preah Thong arrived by sea and encountered the daughter of the Naga King, who ruled the land that would become Cambodia. They fell in love and married. The Naga King swallowed the waters of the land, creating the fertile plains of Cambodia, and built a magnificent city for the couple. The king's serpentine form provided the architectural inspiration for the naga balustrades that adorn Angkor Wat and other Khmer temples. As a wedding gift, the Naga King also taught Preah Thong the arts of irrigation, rice cultivation, and temple building — knowledge that made the Angkorian civilization possible. The descendants of Preah Thong and Neang Kakey were the Khmer kings. The naga association is so deep that the Khmer people are often referred to as 'children of the naga.' Neang Kakey represents the union of Indian and indigenous traditions that formed the bedrock of Khmer civilization. She is depicted in Angkorian sculpture as a beautiful woman crowned with serpent hoods.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

Neang Kakey is described as a woman of exceptional beauty, being the first among the royal consorts. The text emphasizes her physical attractiveness as a central element of the plot.

Symbols & Iconography

The story uses symbols of fidelity, betrayal, and divine punishment. The sea represents both abandonment and final judgment.

Behaviour & Personality

In the Khmer version, Neang Kakey is portrayed as inherently promiscuous and weak-willed, falling victim to the advances of the Garuda and later willingly engaging with Kânthan. She is seen as a cautionary example of female lust.

Domains & Habitats

The story is set in the mythical kingdom of Peareansey, under King Prohmtoat. It also includes the celestial realm of the Garuda.

Version Differences

The Khmer version has only three men who have sexual relations with Kakey (King Brahma, Guru, and Kunthean), while the Thai version has six (including Cham thieves and the king of Nokor Srey Tep). The Khmer version ends with Kakey drowning, while the Thai version has her rescued and taken as a concubine. The Khmer version blames Kakey entirely, while the Thai version shows more leniency.
