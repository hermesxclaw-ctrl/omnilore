Who It Is

In the royal court of 19th-century Siam, a young Cambodian prince—soon to be King Ang Duong—penned a melodramatic verse tale of a queen whose beauty and desire lead her down a path of betrayal and tragedy. Neang Kakey, a story of a faithless wife, has become a cornerstone of Khmer literature, a mirror of societal values, and a subject of enduring debate about gender and justice.

In the Khmer version, Neang Kakey is portrayed as inherently promiscuous and weak-willed, falling victim to the advances of the Garuda and later willingly engaging with Kânthan. She is seen as a cautionary example of female lust.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — 'Neang' is a Khmer honorific for a woman, often meaning 'lady' or 'princess.' 'Kakey' is derived from the Pali/Sanskrit name 'Kākī,' which appears in the Jataka tales.

Pronunciation — /niːəŋ kɑːkeɪ/

Aliases — Neag Kakey, Kakey, Nang Kakey

Ownership — Cambodian (Khmer) and Thai (Siamese); the tale is a shared cultural heritage with distinct national versions.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Neang Kakey is described as a woman of exceptional beauty, being the first among the royal consorts. The text emphasizes her physical attractiveness as a central element of the plot.

Symbols — The story uses symbols of fidelity, betrayal, and divine punishment. The sea represents both abandonment and final judgment.

Domains — The story is set in the mythical kingdom of Peareansey, under King Prohmtoat. It also includes the celestial realm of the Garuda.

Accounts I
The Tale of Neang KakeyKing Ang Duong's 'Neang Kakey' (1815)

19th century (set in mythical past)

In the kingdom of Peareansey, King Prohmtoat's chief consort, Neang Kakey, is deceived by the Garuda, a royal counselor, who sleeps with her at night. Later, another counselor, Kânthan, is sent to rescue her but instead becomes her daytime lover. When the Garuda discovers the affair, he repudiates her, and King Prohmtoat also rejects her. She is set adrift on the sea and drowns after seven days. Neang Kakey's death is seen as divine punishment for her sins, reinforcing the moral that unfaithfulness leads to tragedy.

Accounts II
The Thai Version: Kaki Klon SuphapHon Bunlong (Phra Khlang)

18th-19th century

Similar to the Khmer tale but with more sexual partners for Kakey (six men) and a more forgiving ending. The Thai author seems to pardon her mistakes. Kakey survives and is taken as a concubine, suggesting a less harsh moral judgment.
