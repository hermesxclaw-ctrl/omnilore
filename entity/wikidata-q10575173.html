The Approach

In the quiet valleys and rolling hills of what is now Northern California, the Pomo people speak of a creator whose name echoes through the ages—Madumda, the one who shaped the world from a mere thought. Unlike the distant, wrathful gods of some traditions, Madumda walked among the first humans, teaching them the sacred ways of life, and his legacy persists in the songs and stories of a people who still honor his gifts.

Content advisory. None.
Identity

Madumda

Pronunciation Uncertain; likely /məˈdʊmdə/ based on English phonetic approximations in ethnographic records.

Known As

MadumdaMadumdaKulanaKulanaKulana

Etymology

The name 'Madumda' is of Pomo origin, but its precise linguistic derivation is not documented in available sources. It may relate to concepts of creation or sky, but this is speculative.

Cultural Ownership

Pomo people, indigenous to Northern California, specifically the Clear Lake and Russian River areas.

Disputed Identifications

Some early ethnographers equated Madumda with the 'Great Spirit' of other Native American traditions, but this is a simplification. Within Pomo cosmology, Madumda is distinct from other supernatural beings like the Coyote trickster.

Description

Appearance

Madumda is not described in human form in most accounts; he is a formless, omnipresent creator. Some narratives imply he has human-like attributes, but no specific physical description is attested.

Symbols & Iconography

No known symbols or iconography. Madumda is not represented in material culture; his presence is felt in natural phenomena and through ritual.

Behaviour & Personality

Madumda is portrayed as benevolent and wise, but also distant. He creates the world and then withdraws, leaving humanity to manage with the help of culture heroes. He is not capricious or vengeful, but his absence after creation is notable.

Domains & Habitats

Madumda is associated with the sky and the cosmos, but he is not confined to a specific realm. He is present in the natural world, especially in sacred places like Mount Konocti.

Version Differences

Different Pomo groups (Eastern, Central, Northern, Southern) have varying accounts. Some attribute creation to a different being, or Madumda's role may be shared with other figures. Barrett notes that the Eastern Pomo revere Madumda as the chief deity, while other groups have more complex pantheons.

Stories & Myths

The Creation of the World

Source Barrett, S.A. 'Pomo Myths.' 1933.Culture Eastern PomoPeriod Pre-contact, recorded early 20th century

Summary

In the beginning, there was only water. Madumda, who existed before all things, created the earth by spreading mud from the depths. He then shaped the land, placed mountains, and created plants, animals, and finally humans. He taught the first people how to live, including how to make fire and tools.

Participants

Madumda

First humans

Consequences

Humanity was established with the knowledge needed to survive.

Variants

Some versions say Madumda created the world with the help of Coyote, who often messes things up.

The Departure of Madumda

Source Powers, Stephen. 'Tribes of California.' 1877.Culture PomoPeriod Pre-contact, recorded late 19th century

Summary

After creating the world and teaching humans, Madumda withdrew to the sky, leaving humanity to govern itself. He is still present, watching over them, but he no longer communicates directly except through dreams and visions.

Participants

Madumda

Consequences

Humans must rely on their own wisdom and the guidance of shamans.

Variants

Some accounts say he left because humans became disobedient.

Relationships

Coyote

Type associate/rivalSource Barrett, S.A. 'Pomo Myths.' 1933.

Description

In some Pomo myths, Coyote is a trickster who assists or interferes with Madumda's creation. He may be seen as a subordinate or a foil.

Version Note

In Eastern Pomo, Coyote is not prominent; in other groups, he is a major figure.

Conflicting Accounts

Some stories have Coyote as an independent creator, others as a subordinate.

Humans

Type creator/creationSource Powers, Stephen. 'Tribes of California.' 1877.

Description

Madumda created humans and gave them laws and customs. He is seen as a benefactor, but not a personal god.

Version Note

All Pomo groups acknowledge his role as creator.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Other deities

Type superiorSource Merriam, C. Hart. 'The Dawn of the World.' 1910.

Description

In some Pomo groups, there are other supernatural beings, such as the 'Old Woman' or 'Sun', but Madumda is usually the supreme being.

Version Note

The pantheon varies by group.

Conflicting Accounts

Some groups may not have a supreme being, or Madumda may be one of several.

Contradictions & Disputes

Identity as supreme being

Position A

Madumda is the sole creator and supreme deity of the Pomo.

Position A — evidence

Barrett's Eastern Pomo accounts.

Position B

Some Pomo groups have no supreme being or share creation among multiple deities.

Position B — evidence

Variations in other Pomo groups, as noted by Powers and Merriam.

Scholarly Consensus

There is no consensus; it is likely that the concept of a supreme being evolved over time and varied by community.

Unresolved; reflects the complex nature of Coyote in Native American myth.
