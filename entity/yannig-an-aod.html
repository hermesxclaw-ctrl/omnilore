Who It Is

Imagine walking along a moonlit shore in Brittany, when you hear a child's cry from the waves—but it's not a child. It's Yannig an Aod, the 'Little John of the Shore,' a spirit of the drowned who calls out for pity. Would you answer?

Yannig an Aod is characterized by a haunting, plaintive behavior, often calling out to passersby with a sound resembling an owl's hoot or a child's cry. The spirit is known to ask for pity or help, but heeding its call is considered dangerous, as it is believed that at the third call, the spirit will appear behind the person's back, potentially leading to misfortune or death. Despite its pitiful demeanor, Yannig is not malicious in a traditional sense; rather, it is a lost soul seeking release, but its presence is an omen of danger. Powers
Owl-like Call

Yannig an Aod can produce a sound resembling an owl's hoot, which it uses to attract attention. This call is said to be irresistible to hear, often drawing curious or compassionate individuals toward the shore. However, the call is a lure, and responding to it can lead to dangerous consequences, as the spirit may appear behind the person's back at the third call.

Limit:

Spectral Manifestation

The spirit can appear and disappear at will, often emerging from the sea mist or the waves themselves. It can manifest as a translucent figure or a glowing light, and it is said to be able to move silently across the water and land. This ability allows it to surprise and startle those who encounter it.

Limit:

Omen of Death

Encountering Yannig an Aod is considered an omen of impending death or disaster, particularly for those who heed its call. The spirit is believed to foreshadow drownings or shipwrecks, serving as a warning to sailors and coastal dwellers to avoid the sea during certain times.

Accounts I
The Fisherman's FateTraditional Breton oral tale, recorded in various folklore collections

19th century

A fisherman from the village of Plougrescant, returning home late at night, hears a plaintive hoot from the shore. Believing it to be a lost child, he calls out, but the sound repeats twice more. On the third call, he turns to find Yannig an Aod standing behind him, a small, dripping figure with hollow eyes. The fisherman crosses himself and flees, but the next day, he learns that his own son had drowned in the harbor, and the spirit had been a premonition of the tragedy. The fisherman's son dies, and the fisherman is haunted by guilt, believing he could have prevented the death if he had not ignored the spirit's warning.

Accounts II
The Widow's VigilCollected by Anatole Le Braz in 'La Légende de la mort chez les Bretons armoricains' (1893)

19th century

A widow whose husband was lost at sea spends every night on the cliffs, hoping to see his ghost. One night, she encounters Yannig an Aod, who mimics her husband's voice, calling her name. She follows the spirit to the water's edge, where it points to a spot in the waves. The next day, a body washes ashore, and she is finally able to give her husband a Christian burial. The spirit is seen no more after that, suggesting it was a benevolent guide. The widow finds closure and buries her husband, and the spirit disappears, possibly having found peace.
