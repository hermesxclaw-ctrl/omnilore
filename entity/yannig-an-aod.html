Who It Is

Yannig an Aod (Little John of the Shore) is a ghostly figure of Breton coastal folklore — the spirit of a drowned child who haunts the shoreline, beaches, and cliffs of Brittany. He appears as a small, pale boy dressed in rags or seaweed, often seen at twilight or during storms, his voice carried on the wind as a thin, plaintive cry (similar to the calls of seabirds). Yannig is the ghost of children claimed by the sea — a common tragedy in coastal Brittany, where children played on the dangerous shoreline and were swept away by rogue waves or fell from cliffs. He is not malevolent but profoundly mournful: he weeps on the shore, gathers shells and pebbles in patterns, and follows fishermen who work at night — sometimes warning them of danger, sometimes simply watching in silence. In some legends, Yannig can be heard singing a Breton lullaby (a luskellerez) to himself, the song his mother sang to him before he drowned, repeated endlessly into the wind. Fishermen who hear Yannig's cry know to be especially careful that night — the sea is hungry and remembers. Yannig an Aod is related to the broader Breton tradition of the 'Anaons' — the souls of the dead, particularly those who died without the last rites or whose bodies were never recovered (a constant reality in a maritime culture). Unlike the Ankou (death incarnate), Yannig is death's victim, not its agent — a child caught between land and sea, unable to find rest on either. He represents the grief of coastal communities, the children the sea took, and the belief that the shoreline is a liminal zone where the dead and the living overlap with every tide.
