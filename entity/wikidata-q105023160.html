The Approach

In the vast and often misunderstood pantheon of Brazilian spiritual traditions, few figures embody the fusion of indigenous, African, and European influences as vividly as Caboclo Irará Imoré da Selvagem. This entity, whose very name evokes the wildness of the forest and the resilience of the caboclo archetype, stands as a testament to the syncretic creativity of Afro-Brazilian religions. Yet, despite its evocative title, Irará Imoré da Selvagem remains a shadowy presence, known primarily through oral tradition and scattered references, its story a mosaic of fragments waiting to be assembled.

Etymology

The name is a composite: 'Caboclo' (a term for a person of mixed indigenous and European descent, or a spirit archetype in Umbanda), 'Irará' (possibly derived from Tupi or Yoruba, meaning uncertain; could relate to 'irara', a type of animal, or a deity name), 'Imoré' (possibly a corruption or variant of 'Imorê' or 'Moré', possibly referencing a place or entity), 'da Selvagem' (Portuguese for 'of the wild' or 'of the savage'). The full name suggests a spirit associated with the wilderness and indigenous heritage.

Colonial oppression and cultural erasure

Source Historical scholarship on Brazilian colonial history

Description

The development of caboclo spirits reflects the survival and adaptation of indigenous and African spiritual practices under European colonial domination. The term 'caboclo' was often used derisively, and indigenous peoples were subjected to forced assimilation and violence. This history is a backdrop to the veneration of caboclo spirits, who embody resistance and resilience.
