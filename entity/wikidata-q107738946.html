The Approach

Before tulips conquered gardens, before saffron became worth more than gold by weight, a leafless flower pushed through frozen ground or parched autumn soil with no visible stem — its goblet blooming directly from an underground corm. The Greeks called it krokos, the Romans carried it to Britain as dye and drug, and for 3,000 years humans have bent to pick its three red stigmas by hand, because Crocus refuses to be mechanized.

Content advisory. No graphic content. Dark-history section discusses historical use as abortifacient, toxic confusion with Colchicum leading to poisoning, and documented labor exploitation including child labor in modern saffron harvests. No explicit detail.
Identity

Crocus

Pronunciation UK /ˈkrəʊkəs/ KROH-kəs, US /ˈkroʊkəs/ KROH-kəs; plural crocuses /ˈkrəʊkəsɪz/ or croci /ˈkrəʊkaɪ/; Greek krokos /kró.kos/

Known As

crocusescrocikrokos (κρόκος)za'farān crocus (saffron crocus for C. sativus)Wild Saffron (early English herbal name)Crocus vernus agg. (spring crocus complex)

Etymology

Linnaean genus Crocus from Latin crocus, from Greek krokos (κρόκος) 'saffron, saffron crocus, thread', of probable Semitic origin (cf. Hebrew karkōm, Akkadian kurkanu, Sanskrit kunkumam). Attested in Mycenaean Linear B as ko-ro-ko? disputed. The Greek word already distinguished the plant and its product in Homer and Theophrastus. English 'crocus' 16th c. via Latin; 'saffron' via Old French safran from Arabic za'farān.

Cultural Ownership

No single ownership. Wild species native across Mediterranean Basin, North Africa, southern and central Europe, Aegean islands, Middle East, Central Asia to Xinjiang, China. Longest continuous cultural association with Eastern Mediterranean and Iranian Plateau as source of saffron. Modern center of cultivation and cultural heritage is Iran (producing ~90% of world saffron), with historical significance also in Minoan Crete, Kashmir, Spain (La Mancha), and Turkey. Genus is a wild biological taxon, not a culturally restricted folklore entity.

Disputed Identifications

1) Crocus vs Colchicum (meadow saffron, Colchicaceae): Confused in English herbals 16th-17th c. (Gerard, Parkinson) and still in vernacular 'autumn crocus' for Colchicum autumnale, which is highly toxic (colchicine). Morphologically distinct: Crocus has 3 stamens, inferior ovary, corm; Colchicum has 6 stamens, superior ovary, corm-like corm but different family. 2) Crocus vs Romulea: Linnaeus's C. bulbocodium is now Romulea bulbocodium (Iridaceae). 3) Crocus sativus vs wild autumn species (C. cartwrightianus, C. thomasii, C. pallasii): sterile triploid cultigen origin disputed. 4) Wikidata Q107738946 appears to conflate botanical genus with fictional-character archetype tagging; this dossier treats the attested biological genus. Mythological youth Krokos (Ovid, Met. 4.283) is eponym, not the plant itself, and requires separate page.

Description

Appearance

Diminutive, acaulescent (stemless above ground), cormous geophytic perennial herb. Corm globose to oblate, 1-5 cm diameter, covered by tunics derived from cataphyll bases — fibrous, membranous or coriaceous, reticulate, annulate or parallel-fibred, critical for identification. Contractile and fibrous roots pull corm to 5-20 cm depth. Leaves 3-10+, basal, linear, 5-30 mm wide, 10-118 cm long at maturity, bifacial, adaxially flat to channelled with distinctive pale median stripe (achlorophyllous parenchyma with air spaces), abaxially strongly keeled with two grooves, margins recurved, cross-section boat-shaped with keel square or rectangular (absent in C. carpetanus). Cataphylls 3-6 membranous sheaths enclosing leaves, bract and peduncle. Flower solitary or 2-5 per corm, actinomorphic, salver- to cup-shaped, large relative to plant (3-8 cm), peduncle subterranean, pedicel short, bract membranous, bracteole present or absent. Perianth gamophyllous, tube long, narrow, cylindrical, expanding apically, 6 tepals in two whorls 3+3, similar, white, yellow, orange, lilac to deep purple, often striped or blotched, outer whorl sometimes larger. Androecium 3 stamens, erect, linear, inserted at throat, anthers shorter than filaments, pollen inaperturate to spiraperturate. Gynoecium single exserted style with 3 to many branches, highly variable: simple, bifurcate or multifid, flattened or fringed, orange-red in saffron crocus; ovary inferior, 3 carpels, axile placentation, 3 locules, remaining underground until fruiting when pedicel elongates to raise capsule above soil. Fruit small membranous loculicidal capsule, ellipsoid to oblong-ellipsoid; seeds numerous, globose to ellipsoid, surface papillate to digitiform, sometimes arillate with elaiosome for ant dispersal; embryo sac Polygonum-type; outer integument sometimes with long papillae.

Symbols & Iconography

Primary symbol: saffron — color, fragrance, and value. In antiquity krokos signified luxury, dye (yellow-orange), perfume, and ritual: Minoan frescoes show saffron-gathering women and offering of stigmas; Classical Greeks used krokos as perfume (krokōtos), dye for royal robes (krokotos chiton), and bridal veil color. In medieval herbals, Crocus verus sativus depicted with three red stigmas exserted. In Christian and Islamic traditions saffron color denotes sanctity and hospitality. In modern horticultural iconography, spring crocus (C. vernus, C. chrysanthus) is emblem of late winter/early spring, renewal, appearing in Dutch still lifes and botanical plates (Besler, Redouté). Corm tunic fibre patterns are diagnostic icons in taxonomic illustration. No anthropomorphic iconography for genus itself; mythological Krokos is depicted as youth with flower in Ovidian illustrations.

Behaviour & Personality

Not applicable as plant genus. Phenology: hysteranthous, synanthous or proteranthous depending on species — flowers with, before, or after leaves. Autumn-flowering (e.g., C. sativus, C. speciosus) bloom Sept-Nov, vernal species (C. vernus, C. tommasinianus) Feb-Apr. Flowers nyctinastic: close at night and in overcast weather, open in sun, protecting reproductive parts. Geophytic dormancy after flowering: leaves photosynthesize then senesce, corm enters summer dormancy. Pollination primarily by bees (Apidae), especially honeybees and bumblebees, and flies; some self-compatible. Seed dispersal myrmecochorous (ant-mediated) where arillate. Vegetative propagation via daughter cormels on parent corm and via contractile roots adjusting depth. Habitat response: tolerates woodland, scrub, meadow, rocky slopes, alpine tundra to >3000 m.

Domains & Habitats

Native range: Mediterranean Basin through North Africa, central and southern Europe, Aegean islands, Levant, Anatolia, Caucasus, Iran, Central Asia to Xinjiang (western China). Habitats: open woodland, maquis scrub, montane meadows, rocky limestone slopes, alpine turf, semi-desert steppe, from sea level to alpine tundra. Soils well-drained, often calcareous. Climate: Mediterranean with winter-wet/summer-dry cycle for most species; continental for Central Asian taxa. Naturalized widely in temperate zones via cultivation (British Isles, North America). C. sativus is cultigen, unknown in wild, grown in irrigated fields in Iran (Khorasan), Kashmir, Spain, Greece (Kozani), Morocco, India.

Version Differences

Extreme morphological uniformity in leaves vs. high diversity in flowers, corm tunics, and phenology. Corm tunics: reticulate-fibrous (C. vernus group), membranous splitting into parallel fibres (C. biflorus group), coriaceous annulate (C. speciosus). Flower color: yellow/orange in sect. Crocus ser. Flavi (C. flavus, C. chrysanthus) vs. white-lilac-purple in most others; C. sativus unique with large lilac tepals and very long deep-red tripartite stigma. Style branching: simple entire (C. etruscus) vs. deeply divided multifid (C. sativus). Leaf keel square vs. rectangular vs. absent. Karyology highly variable: 2n=6 to 2n=70, extensive aneuploidy and polyploidy; C. sativus triploid 2n=24, sterile. Phytochemistry: crocins (water-soluble apocarotenoids) present in stigmas/styles of many species but commercially only in C. sativus; 6-hydroxyflavones unique to Crocus within Iridaceae; mangiferin in few species. Phenology splits genus into autumn- and spring-bloomers, historically treated as separate groups by Parkinson (27 spring vs 4 autumn in 1656).

Powers & Abilities

Saffron production (crocin/crocetin/picrocrocin/safranal synthesis)

Source VersionAttested in C. sativus; crocins also detected in floral tissues of other Crocus spp. at lower concentrations (e.g., C. cartwrightianus, C. alatavicus). Phytochemical surveys: Pfander & Schurtenberger (1982), Caballero-Ortega et al. (2007).

Stigmas and style branches accumulate apocarotenoids: crocins (diesters of crocetin, water-soluble yellow carotenoids) for color, picrocrocin (bitter glycoside) and its aglycone safranal (aroma). Harvested by hand, dried stigmas of C. sativus constitute saffron, one of world's most expensive spices by weight (~150,000 flowers per kg).

Limitations

Only C. sativus produces commercially viable yield; wild species stigmas too small/sparse. Sterile triploid, propagated only by corm division. Yield highly labor-intensive, vulnerable to weather, corm rot (Fusarium). Adulteration common.

Contradictions

Ancient sources attribute saffron to multiple krokos types; modern chemistry shows only C. sativus lineage has selected high crocin content.

Dye and pigment

Source VersionAttested from Minoan frescoes (c.1600 BC), Egyptian papyri, Theophrastus, Pliny NH 21.17; medieval dyestuff in Britain after Roman introduction.

Crocin provides water-soluble yellow-orange dye used since Bronze Age for textiles, manuscripts, cosmetics, and food coloring. Unlike carotenes, crocin is hydrophilic.

Limitations

Fugitive (light-sensitive), requires mordant, expensive; largely replaced by synthetic dyes post-19th c. but retained for food (paella, risotto).

Contradictions

None; but often confused with safflower (Carthamus) or turmeric as cheaper yellow dyes historically sold as 'saffron'.

Culinary and perfumery use

Source VersionClassical (Theophrastus, Dioscorides Mat. Med. 1.25), medieval Arab pharmacopoeias, modern Iranian culinary canon.

Flavor, color, aroma in cuisines (Persian, Kashmiri, Spanish, Italian). In antiquity krokos perfume (krokōton myron) and flavoring for wine.

Limitations

High dose (>5g) potentially toxic; recommended culinary dose 0.1-0.3g. Volatile safranal degrades with storage.

Contradictions

None.

Traditional medicinal and ritual properties

Source VersionDioscorides De Materia Medica 1.25 (krokos), Ibn Sina Canon; modern reviews: Schmidt et al. (2007), Broadhead et al. (2016).

In Dioscorides, Pliny, and Islamic medicine: emmenagogue, abortifacient, sedative, aphrodisiac, treatment for melancholy. Modern research investigates antioxidant, antidepressant, anti-inflammatory effects of crocetin/crocin (preclinical, small clinical trials).

Limitations

Historical abortifacient doses were toxic; modern efficacy not established as drug — supplements not regulated; high doses risk nausea, vomiting, uterine stimulation. Not a substitute for clinical treatment.

Contradictions

Traditional claims of broad panacea vs. limited modern evidence; studies often small, funded by saffron industry.

Ornamental early bloom and pollinator resource

Source VersionHorticultural literature from Clusius (1583) to RHS Plant Finder; ecological studies on bee visitation.

Spring species provide first nectar/pollen after winter; autumn species extend flowering season. Septal nectaries produce nectar at perianth throat. Widely cultivated as ornamental geophytes naturalizing in lawns.

Limitations

Short flowering period (1-2 weeks per clone); corms vulnerable to rodents (squirrels), corm rot, and lifting by frost.

Contradictions

None.

Geophytic survival (dormancy, contractile roots, corm tunics)

Source VersionBotanical morphology: Mathew (1982), Rudall (2002) Iridaceae anatomy.

Corm allows survival of summer drought/winter cold via dormancy; contractile roots adjust depth up to 20 cm; tunics protect from desiccation and predation; vegetative multiplication via cormels.

Limitations

Cannot survive waterlogged soils; requires summer baking for many Mediterranean species; over-division leads to small flowering size.

Contradictions

None.

Stories & Myths

Theophrastus names krokos

Source Theophrastus, Enquiry into Plants (Historia Plantarum) 6.6.11, 7.7.3Culture Ancient GreekPeriod c. 310 BC, Classical Greece

Summary

Theophrastus, Aristotle's pupil, gives the earliest surviving botanical description of krokos, distinguishing cultivated saffron from wild types and noting its cormous habit, narrow leaves, and fragrant stigmas. He places it among coronariae (garland plants) and notes propagation by corm and seed, and that the flower appears before leaves in some kinds. This establishes krokos as a recognized taxon in Western science.

Participants

Theophrastus of Eresus

krokos (Crocus spp.)

Consequences

Foundation for all later European herbal tradition; Dioscorides and Pliny build on his description; provides terminus ante quem for saffron cultivation in Aegean.

Variants

Hort translation vs. Amigues edition differ on whether Theophrastus describes two or three kinds; some scholars interpret his 'wild krokos' as C. cartwrightianus.

Romans bring saffron to Britain

Source Archaeobotanical and textual: Pliny Natural History 21.17; medieval tradition summarized in Mathew (1982) and Hawkes (1984)Culture Roman BritainPeriod 1st-4th century AD, with reintroduction narrative in medieval herbals

Summary

Roman occupation introduces C. sativus to Britain as dyestuff and condiment. Place-name Saffron Walden (Essex) later associated with medieval saffron fields, but tradition holds Romans first cultivated it. After Roman withdrawal, cultivation lapsed until Crusader reintroduction.

Participants

Roman colonists

Crocus sativus

Consequences

Establishes saffron as British crop; later medieval economy of Saffron Walden and Saffron Hill, London; linguistic persistence of 'saffron' for crocus in Turner (1548).

Variants

Some historians argue continuous cultivation vs. complete loss and 14th-c. reintroduction from the Levant via Crusaders; archaeobotanical evidence for Roman period is sparse and relies on pollen not corms.

Crusaders and the return of saffron to Western Europe

Source Tractatus de Herbis (c. 12th-14th c.); Parkinson (1629) and medieval trade recordsCulture Medieval Latin Christendom / Crusader StatesPeriod 12th-14th century

Summary

Western European herbals after the Crusades again depict true saffron crocus, suggesting reintroduction of corms from the Eastern Mediterranean. The 14th-century Tractatus de Herbis illustrates C. sativus with its three stigmas. Trade in saffron becomes major luxury commerce via Venice and Spain.

Participants

Crusaders

Venetian merchants

Crocus sativus corms

Consequences

Revival of saffron cultivation in Spain, France, England, and Italy; saffron becomes currency and tax commodity; adulteration statutes (e.g., Nuremberg Safranschau 1441) enacted.

Variants

Alternative route via Arab agricultural transfer in Spain (8th-13th c.) may have been more important than Crusader transport; both likely contributed.

Clusius and the Turkish crocuses arrive in Europe

Source Carolus Clusius (Charles de l'Écluse), Rariorum plantarum historia (1601); letters 1570s-1590sCulture Ottoman Empire to Habsburg Netherlands / Holy Roman EmpirePeriod 1570s-1600, late Renaissance

Summary

Clusius, prefect of the Imperial garden in Vienna, receives corms of spring crocuses from the Ottoman Empire via diplomatic gifts from Turkey. Gerard (1597) obtains specimens from Clusius and describes 'sundry sorts' — first clear European distinction between spring and autumn crocuses beyond saffron. By 1613 Besler's Hortus Eystettensis plates show diverse cultivars. This marks the birth of ornamental crocus horticulture.

Participants

Carolus Clusius

John Gerard

Ogier de Busbecq (imperial ambassador)

Crocus vernus, C. flavus, C. biflorus

Consequences

Ornamental crocus spreads as garden plant; foundation of Dutch bulb trade; taxonomic confusion increases as more species imported without locality data.

Variants

Gerard claims 11 forms; Clusius describes more; Parkinson later enumerates 27 spring + 4 autumn kinds, including C. verus sativus autumnalis as true saffron.

Linnaeus lumps and splits the genus

Source Carl Linnaeus, Species Plantarum (1753) p.36; later re-examination by Mathew and BrightonCulture Swedish / European Enlightenment taxonomyPeriod 1753

Summary

Linnaeus formally describes genus Crocus with three taxa: C. sativus var. officinalis and var. vernus (now C. vernus) and C. bulbocodium (now Romulea). He incorrectly treats spring and autumn saffron crocuses as varieties of one species, and includes a non-Crocus. Later botanists correct this: C. vernus and C. sativus recognized as distinct species, and Romulea segregated (1772). Lectotypification fixes C. sativus as type species.

Participants

Carl Linnaeus

Crocus sativus

Crocus vernus

Romulea bulbocodium

Consequences

Stabilizes nomenclature but creates century of confusion over spring vs autumn taxa; modern molecular phylogenetics (Harpke et al. 2013-2015) re-circumscribes sections and series.

Variants

Re-examination of Linnaean herbarium sheets shows multiple species mounted as 'C. vernus', indicating Linnaeus himself had mixed material.

The myth of Krokos and Smilax — the flower's eponym

Source Ovid, Metamorphoses 4.283; Galen and Nonnus Dionysiaca 12.86; also Servius on VirgilCulture Greco-Roman mythologyPeriod Augustan Rome (Ovid c. 8 AD) drawing on Hellenistic sources

Summary

Youth Krokos (Latin Crocus) loves nymph Smilax (Smilax, bindweed). Unrequited or tragically interrupted, the gods transform him into the saffron flower, whose red stigmas are his spilled blood, while Smilax becomes the yew or bindweed. Alternative version: Krokos accidentally kills his friend or is killed playing discus with Hermes, like Hyacinthus, and blood becomes flower. Ovid lists Crocus and Smilax among flowers born from metamorphosis. This aetiological myth explains the flower's name and its association with youthful death and spring.

Participants

Krokos/Crocus (youth)

Smilax (nymph)

Hermes (in variant)

Gods

Consequences

Provides folk etymology for krokos; Renaissance and later art depicts Crocus as floral youth; confusion persists between botanical and mythological entries in modern databases (hence 'heroic-legend' tagging). Botanically irrelevant but culturally attached to genus.

Variants

Ovid gives brief mention; Nonnus expands; Servius rationalizes as lovers turned into flowers; some sources make Smilax male. No ancient source connects myth to specific Crocus species.

Domestication of the sterile triploid saffron crocus

Source Botanical genetics: Mathew (1977), Negbi (1999), Harpke et al. (2013) Molecular Phylogenetics and EvolutionCulture Bronze Age Eastern Mediterranean / modern geneticsPeriod Putative domestication c. 1600-1000 BC; scientific resolution 20th-21st c.

Summary

Crocus sativus is a sterile triploid (2n=3x=24) that never sets viable seed and exists only as cultivated clone propagated by corm division for millennia. Cytogenetic and molecular studies show it is an autotriploid of C. cartwrightianus (wild Cretan/Aegean species) or allotriploid hybrid involving C. cartwrightianus and C. thomasii/C. pallasii. Selection for long stigmas and high crocin content occurred under human cultivation since Minoan times, as suggested by Akrotiri frescoes.

Participants

Crocus sativus

Crocus cartwrightianus

Crocus thomasii

Minoan cultivators

Consequences

Explains why saffron crocus has no wild population; informs conservation of wild relatives; underpins Geographical Indication protections (Krokos Kozanis, Azafrán de La Mancha).

Variants

Autopolyploid vs. hybrid origin still debated; recent genomic work (2020) supports multiple origins or introgression; some authors propose C. cartwrightianus alone sufficient.

Relationships
Crocus→Iridaceae (iris family)family

Crocus is placed in subfamily Crocoideae, tribe Croceae, sister to Romulea. Shares family traits: basal cauline leaves sheathing stem, petaloid perianth 3+3, 3 stamens, inferior ovary with 3 carpels, septal nectaries, loculicidal capsule. Molecular phylogeny confirms monophyly but with extensive reticulation.

Crocus→Crocus sativus L. (saffron crocus)creator

Type species of genus, autumn-flowering sterile triploid cultigen, source of saffron. All commercial saffron derives from its dried stigmas. Cultivated since at least Late Bronze Age; no known wild population.

Crocus→Colchicum autumnale L. and Colchicum spp. (meadow saffron)hostile

Toxic autumn-flowering cormous plant in Colchicaceae, vernacularly called 'autumn crocus' or 'meadow saffron'. Historical confusion in herbals (Gerard, Parkinson) and modern gardens leads to fatal colchicine poisoning when mistaken for Crocus. Distinguished by 6 stamens, superior ovary, and colchicine alkaloid.

Crocus→Romulea bulbocodium (L.) Sebast. & Maurifamily

Formerly Crocus bulbocodium L. (1753), transferred to Romulea in 1818. Closely related genus in same tribe, distinguished by narrower leaves and different corm tunic. Illustrates early generic circumscription errors.

Crocus→Homo sapiens — cultivators, traders, consumers (Iran, Kashmir, Spain, Greece)creator

Humans propagate Crocus vegetatively, select cultivars, and maintain global saffron economy. Iran (Khorasan) produces ~90% of world saffron; traditional hand-harvest by families. Saffron is labor-intensive: ~150,000 flowers/kg, harvest at dawn.

Crocus→Pollinators — Apis mellifera, Bombus spp., hoverfliesally

Primary pollinators attracted by septal nectar and pollen. Spring crocuses critical early-season forage when little else blooms. Bees buzz-pollinate; pollen inaperturate adapted to bee transport.

Crocus→Mythological Krokos/Crocus (youth, lover of Smilax)creator

Eponymous mythological figure whose metamorphosis explains flower's origin. Not botanical entity but culturally linked; databases often conflate. Requires separate page to avoid category error.

Crocus→Crocus cartwrightianus Herb. (wild progenitor)family

Wild autumn-flowering species from Crete and southern Greece, considered closest wild relative and probable diploid parent of C. sativus. Shares long red stigmas and crocin chemistry but smaller.

Abortifacient and emmenagogue use

Source Dioscorides De Mat. Med. 1.25; Riddle (1992) Contraception and Abortion from the Ancient World to the Renaissance

Description

Dioscorides, Pliny, and later Islamic and medieval herbals list krokos/saffron as emmenagogue and abortifacient at high doses. Folk medicine in Europe and Iran used saffron tea to 'bring menses'. Effective dose approaches toxic dose (5-10g can cause uterine bleeding, vomiting, dizziness). Historical use reflects limited reproductive control and risk to pregnant persons.

Scholarly Context

Riddle documents saffron among many plant abortifacients; efficacy and safety not established, use now discouraged medically; historical context important to avoid romanticizing 'natural' remedy.

Warning: Toxic confusion with Colchicum — poisoning and death — Show unsanitized · Archive policy & legal

Toxic confusion with Colchicum — poisoning and death

Source Brvar et al. (2004) Clinical Toxicology; UK NPIS reports

Description

Because Colchicum autumnale is called 'autumn crocus' or 'meadow saffron', foragers and gardeners have mistaken its corms and leaves for Crocus or wild garlic, ingesting colchicine. Colchicine poisoning causes gastrointestinal hemorrhage, multi-organ failure, and death; no antidote. Cases reported annually in Europe. Historical herbals' conflation contributed to risk.

Scholarly Context

Botanists stress morphological distinction (6 vs 3 stamens, superior vs inferior ovary) and vernacular name reform; public health advisories warn against foraging without expertise.

Warning: Labor exploitation and child labor in saffron production — Show unsanitized · Archive policy & legal

Labor exploitation and child labor in saffron production

Source FAO (2018) Saffron value chain; Human Rights Watch and ILO reports on agricultural child labor; Gresta et al. (2008)

Description

Saffron harvest requires hand-picking stigmas at dawn during short flowering window, often by women and children. Reports from Iran, Kashmir, and Afghanistan document low wages, debt bondage, and child involvement to meet labor demand. Adulteration with dyed stamens, safflower, or synthetic colorants exploits consumers and undercuts honest producers. Historic saffron fraud was capital offense (Nuremberg 1441, Augsburg).

Scholarly Context

Scholars distinguish traditional family farming from exploitative commercial practices; Geographical Indications and fair-trade certifications attempt to address, but enforcement uneven. Discussion avoids sensationalism while acknowledging documented exploitation.

Warning: Colonial and commercial adulteration and economic coercion — Show unsanitized · Archive policy & legal

Colonial and commercial adulteration and economic coercion

Source Willard (2002) Saffron history; modern market analyses (Ghorbani 2008)

Description

Saffron's high value incentivized systemic fraud: mixing with Carthamus (safflower), Curcuma, or dyed corn silk. Colonial trade (e.g., British in Kashmir) extracted saffron as revenue crop, displacing food crops. Modern sanctions and market volatility disproportionately affect Iranian smallholders despite Iran's dominance.

Scholarly Context

Economic botany frames saffron as luxury commodity with classic resource-curse dynamics; not to be confused with plant's biology.

Contradictions & Disputes

How many species in Crocus?

Position A

~90 species (Mathew 1982; traditional narrow circumscription)

Position A — evidence

Morphological species concept based on corm tunic, style, leaf anatomy; Mathew's keys; conservative lumping of variable complexes.

Position B

~100-110 species (Harpke et al. 2013-2015 molecular) up to 235 taxa including subspecies (Petersen et al. 2008; recent splits in C. biflorus, C. vernus complexes)

Position B — evidence

DNA barcoding and phylogenomics reveal cryptic species; e.g., C. biflorus s.l. split into >10 species; C. reticulatus complex split.

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus; Plants of the World Online (Kew) accepts ~100 species as of 2024, but notes ongoing revision; trend toward splitting with molecular data.

Origin of sterile triploid Crocus sativus

Position A

Autotriploid of C. cartwrightianus alone (autopolyploidy)

Position A — evidence

Karyotype similarity, GISH, and recent whole-genome data show C. sativus genome highly similar to C. cartwrightianus; triploid can arise via unreduced gamete within one species; Nemati et al. (2019) support single-parent.

Position B

Allotriploid hybrid of C. cartwrightianus × C. thomasii (or C. pallasii) (hybrid polyploidy)

Position B — evidence

Earlier cytogenetics (Karasawa 1933, Brighton 1977) and AFLP/RAPD suggested two parental genomes; Harpke et al. (2013) found C. thomasii alleles; chloroplast haplotypes differ.

Scholarly Consensus

Majority now favors C. cartwrightianus as sole or major parent with possible introgression; allotriploid hypothesis not fully rejected; 2020+ genomics leans autotriploid with historic gene flow.

Base chromosome number (x) of Crocus

Position A

x=3 or x=4 (early counts)

Position A — evidence

Early counts 2n=6 in C. minimus suggested x=3; some authors proposed x=4.

Position B

x=5 or x=6 with extensive dysploidy/aneuploidy; no single base number

Position B — evidence

Broad survey shows 2n=6 to 70 with many odd numbers; aneuploid series indicates dysploid reduction/increase; Brighton et al. (1973) and Mathew argue genus is paleopolyploid with multiple base numbers.

Scholarly Consensus

Consensus: genus exhibits extreme aneuploidy and no stable base number; x is not meaningful, dysploidy is derived.

Was saffron crocus wild or always cultivated?

Position A

C. sativus existed wild in Crete/Greece and was domesticated

Position A — evidence

Herbarium specimens labeled 'wild sativus' from Greece; Theophrastus mentions wild krokos; Minoan fresco suggests wild gathering.

Position B

C. sativus never existed wild; it is a human-made clone that arose under cultivation

Position B — evidence

No verified wild population despite extensive surveys; all 'wild' finds are escapes or C. cartwrightianus misidentified; sterility precludes wild persistence; genetic uniformity indicates single clonal origin.

Scholarly Consensus

Modern consensus strongly supports position B: C. sativus is cultigen with no wild population; wild progenitor is C. cartwrightianus.
