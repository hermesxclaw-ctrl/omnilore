This page is intentionally blank and ready for source-backed lore, versions, images, and connections. Añjanā's primary domain is motherhood, specifically the mother of a divine hero. She is also associated with devotion, penance, and the power of a mother's blessing. Her story is a foundational myth for the Hanuman tradition, and she is venerated as a goddess in her own right, particularly in parts of India. She is a symbol of the potential for the divine to be born into the world through a woman's devotion and sacrifice.

Form

Añjanā is consistently described as a woman of extraordinary beauty, often with a radiant, celestial glow. In the Ramcharitmanas, she is described as 'Añjanā, the beautiful one, whose form was like a flash of lightning' (translation). Her complexion is often described as golden or dark (shyama), and she is adorned with celestial ornaments. In her vanara form, she may have a simian appearance, but this is often idealized and depicted as regal and beautiful, not bestial. In art, she is typically depicted as a beautiful woman, sometimes with a monkey's tail or other subtle vanara features, standing beside or holding the infant Hanuman.

Powers

Divine Motherhood Her primary 'power' is her role as the mother of Hanuman, who is an incarnation of Shiva (in many traditions). This makes her a vessel for a major divine being, granting her a unique and elevated status in the Hindu pantheon. Hindu, Shiva Purana, Ramcharitmanas This power is not a personal ability but a conferred status. In Jain tradition, Hanuman is not an incarnation of Shiva, so this power is not applicable.

Apsara Beauty and Grace As the apsara Punjikastala, she possessed celestial beauty and the ability to enchant. This is a defining trait that led to her curse and remains a part of her identity. Hindu, Shiva Purana, Ramcharitmanas Her beauty was a source of vulnerability, leading to her curse. In some traditions, she is a vanara princess and not a cursed apsara, so this power is not present.

Power of Penance (Tapas) Añjanā performed intense penance for years to appease Shiva and obtain a son. This demonstrates immense spiritual power and the ability to influence the gods through austerity. Hindu, Shiva Purana, Ramcharitmanas Penance is a long, arduous process, not an instant power. None significant.

Mother's Blessing In some traditions, Añjanā's blessing is considered to have granted Hanuman his incredible strength and powers. Her blessing, given at his birth or during his childhood, is a potent force. Hindu, various folk traditions This is a narrative power, not a repeatable one. In other versions, Hanuman's powers are innate or granted by other gods.

Accounts

Añjanā was originally Punjikastala, a beautiful celestial nymph (apsara). While in a state of pride over her beauty, she mocked a sage's penance, or in some versions, she was merely seen and desired by a sage who cursed her for not reciprocating. The sage cursed her to be born as a vanara (monkey-woman) on Earth. Repentant, she was granted a boon that she would be freed from the curse after giving birth to an incarnation of Shiva. She was then born as Añjanā, the wife of Kesari.

Añjanā, desiring a son, performs intense penance. Pleased, Shiva grants her a boon. In the most popular version, the king Dasharatha is performing a fire sacrifice (Putrakameshti Yagna) to obtain sons. The divine pudding (payasa) from the sacrifice is distributed among his wives. A kite (or eagle) snatches a piece of the pudding and drops it into the hands of Añjanā, who is also meditating. She consumes it, and with the blessing of Vayu (the wind god), she conceives Hanuman, who is an incarnation of Shiva. Hanuman is born with immense strength and divine powers.

Añjanā is depicted as a loving and concerned mother. A popular story tells of a young Hanuman, mistaking the rising sun for a ripe fruit, leaping into the sky to grab it. Indra, the king of gods, strikes him with his thunderbolt, and Hanuman falls to the ground, injured. Añjanā is distraught and appeals to Vayu, who withdraws the air from the world in protest. The gods are forced to appease Vayu and bless Hanuman with numerous powers to restore the balance.

Scholarly Debates
Apsara vs. Vanara Birth
Añjanā was originally the apsara Punjikastala, cursed to be born as a vanara. ⇌ Añjanā was a vanara princess by birth.
Evidence for A: Shiva Purana, Ramcharitmanas, and numerous later texts explicitly detail this curse narrative.
Evidence for B: The core Valmiki Ramayana does not mention the curse or her apsara identity, presenting her simply as a vanara woman.
Role in Hanuman's Conception
Hanuman was conceived through the consumption of the divine pudding (payasa) from Dasharatha's sacrifice, with Vayu's divine influence. ⇌ Shiva directly transferred his essence to Añjanā, often through Vayu, in response to her penance.
Evidence for A: Valmiki Ramayana, Balakanda.
Evidence for B: Shiva Purana, Ramcharitmanas.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Añjanā as the ultimate 'origin story' character in a cosmic superhero saga. She is the mortal (or celestial) being who, through a twist of fate and immense personal sacrifice, becomes the vessel for a divine power. Her story is the prequel to the main epic, explaining why the hero is who he is. She is the 'Martha Kent' or 'Uncle Ben' of the Hindu mythological universe — the quiet, powerful force whose love and sacrifice make the hero's journey possible. Her tale is a reminder that behind every great legend is often an even more compelling story of a parent's devotion.
