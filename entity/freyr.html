Who It Is

The most famous god of the Norse world may well be the one who gave his sword away for love and so bought his own death. Freyr — whose very name is just the Old Norse word for 'Lord' — tumbled joyfully and catastrophically into the only extended myth that survives about him: he spies a giantess from Odin's all-seeing throne, wastes away with desire, and trades the one weapon that fights by itself for the promise of her body at a clearing called Barri. Nine nights' wait becomes an age of longing; the forfeit, meanwhile, hardens into the doom that kills him. Because when the world ends and the fire-giant Surtr comes on, the peace-loving, harvest-blessing ancestor of an entire royal line will face him empty-handed — and die. It is a fertility god's story written by fate itself: to make the fields bloom, the god must first disarm himself.

Fundamentally a fertility, peace, prosperity, fair-weather, and good-harvest god — gendered 'most renowned of the æsir' by Snorri. In his only extended myth he is emotionally passive and lovesick: he becomes taciturn, refuses to sleep or drink, and lets his servant Skírnir act on his behalf — even at the cost of giving away his own sword. He is not a warlike deity; he is the one who surrenders his weapon for love, which seals his doom. He is a lord/king figure, ancestor of royal lines, generous and peaceable.

Powers
The self-fighting sword (given away)

Freyr originally possessed a magical dwarf-made sword that 'fights jötnar by itself if wise be he who wields it'. He gave it to Skírnir as payment for wooing Gerðr, and never regained it. Its loss is fatal: at Ragnarök, lacking the sword, Freyr is killed by the fire-giant Surtr.

Limit: Freyr surrendered it; without it he has no weapon of this calibre, which is his signal fatal weakness.

Gullinbursti ('Gold Mane' / 'Golden Bristles')

A dwarf-made boar whose bristles/mane glow to illuminate the way. Freyr rode it to Baldr's funeral; Snorri's Gylfaginning says he rode in a wagon pulled by it (Húsdrápa has him riding it directly). Also known as Slíðrugtanni ('Sharp/Fearsome Tooth').

Limit: None recorded; boar is a cult symbol of his fertility.

Skíðblaðnir ('Assembled from thin pieces of wood')

The finest and most ingeniously created of ships, made by the dwarf Sons of Ívaldi. It always finds a fair wind, can sail both wind and water, is large enough to carry all the gods with war-gear, and can be folded like cloth and carried in a pocket when not needed.

Limit: In the euhemerized Ynglinga saga the ship and its magic are transferred to Odin, not Freyr.

Blóðughófi ('Bloody Hoof')

Freyr's horse, named only in the Nafnaþulur (poetic name-lists).

Limit: No stories use it; name-only attestation.

Blessing / effecting peace, fertility, prosperity, fair weather, good harvest

Freyr's core domain: good harvests, peace, prosperity, fair weather, and (via the phallic cult) fertility and marriage. Snorri's Gylfaginning says he is venerated for good harvest and peace and rules over weather and the produce of the fields; Egill Skallagrímsson's Arinbjarnarkviða says 'For Freyr and Niord have endowed Griotbiorn with a power of wealth'.

Limit: Adam of Bremen instead assigns control of weather/fields to Thor — a direct discrepancy with Snorri.

Kingship / royal ancestor-magic (euhemerized)

In the euhemerized accounts Freyr is an ancestor-god of the Swedish Yngling dynasty; his reign guarantees peace and good seasons, and the concealment of his death (his body in a three-window mound still receiving gold/silver/copper tribute) preserves good fortune for the land.

Limit: Euhemerized; treats Freyr as a mortal king, not a supernatural power.

Accounts I
Freyr and Gerðr (the giving-away of the sword) — SkírnismálPoetic Edda, Skírnismál; retold in Gylfaginning

Poem arguably early 10th c.; recorded 13th c.

Freyr sits in Odin's high seat Hliðskjálf and looks into all worlds; he sees the jötunn woman Gerðr in Jötunheimr and falls instantly, desperately lovesick, refusing to eat, sleep, or speak. His father Njörðr and Skaði send the page Skírnir to find the cause; Freyr confesses and, in exchange for the mission, gives Skírnir his own self-fighting sword and a steed. Skírnir rides to Gymir's hall, first offers Gerðr eleven golden apples (or apples of eternal life) and a ring like Draupnir; she refuses. He then turns to naked coercion: he threatens her father's death by the sword, and finally works a terrible curse on her with a gambanteinn wand and carved runes — she will be a public spectacle, suffer intolerable sexual frustration, be owned by the thurs Hrímgrímnir below Nágrind's gates, drink goat-urine, and waste away. Terrified, Gerðr yields and promises to meet Freyr at Barri in nine nights. Freyr, waiting, laments that a single night is an age. Freyr slays Beli with an antlerProse Edda (Gylfaginning); poem Vafþrúðnismál references

13th c. record

Because Freyr had given away his sword, when it came time to fight the jötunn Beli he had no blade and had to kill the giant with an antler (a stag's horn). The feat is a measure of his strength but also the irony of his self-disarmament. Demonstrates Freyr's martial capability even unarmed, while reinforcing that his great weapon is lost.

Accounts II
Baldr's funeralHúsdrápa; Gylfaginning; Skáldskaparmál (dwarf-gifts frame)

Skaldic c. 980; Prose Edda 13th c.

Freyr attends the funeral of the slain god Baldr. Húsdrápa depicts him riding Gullinbursti to the pyre; Snorri's Gylfaginning instead has him drawn in a chariot by the boar. The scene assembles the gods and their treasures around the burning ship, a set-piece of the Eddic world. Iconic; anchors Freyr's place in the divine assembly and his boar as a cult vehicle.

The Loki dwarf-wager and Freyr's treasuresProse Edda, Skáldskaparmál ch. 35; Grímnismál

13th c. record; Grímnismál earlier

After Loki cuts off the goddess Sif's hair, he must ransom himself by having dwarfs forge golden hair for Sif as well as Skíðblaðnir and the spear Gungnir. A further wager with Brokkr/Eitri (who bet Loki's head) produces Gullinbursti (for Freyr), Draupnir (for Odin), and Mjöllnir (for Thor). The dwarf-made items are doled out, and Freyr receives both the ship and the boar. Equips Freyr with his two signature treasures.

Euhemerized Freyr the Swedish kingYnglinga saga (Heimskringla); Gesta Danorum; Íslendingabók; Historia Norwegiæ; Ynglingatal

c. 1125-1230

Odin, a mortal 'Asian' warlord, conquers the North, settles in Sweden, and dies; Njörðr succeeds him; then Freyr (also called Yngvi) reigns over a golden age of peace and good harvests at Uppsala, so beloved that the Swedes believe he controls the seasons. The royal dynasty is named Ynglings from his honorific Yngvi. When Freyr dies (of illness, per Ynglinga saga), his men conceal the death, place his body in a great mound with three windows, and continue to bring tribute — gold, silver, copper — into the windows; not burning him, they keep sacrificing to him, since the peaceful times depend on his protective presence. Founds the legendary Yngling royal line, ancestor-gods of Norwegian kings (Harald Fairhair claimed descent). The three-window mound is a rich, distinctively euhemerized image of the sealed god.
