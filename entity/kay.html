Who It Is

Sir Kay (Cei in Welsh) is Arthur's foster-brother and the seneschal (steward) of Camelot — a figure who undergoes a remarkable transformation across the Arthurian tradition. In the earliest Welsh sources, Cei fab Cynyr is one of Arthur's mightiest warriors, possessing supernatural abilities: he can go nine days and nights without sleep or breath, he grows as tall as a tree when roused to battle, and his wounds heal with astonishing speed. He is fierce, loyal, and indispensable. In the French romances (Chrétien de Troyes) and Malory, he becomes the acid-tongued, boastful, often incompetent foil to the greater knights — the one whose arrogance is always punctured, whose challenges are always defeated, and whose rudeness provides comic relief.

Kay's defining moment in Malory is the Sword in the Stone. When the young Arthur, Kay's squire, forgets Kay's sword before a tournament, Arthur pulls the sword from the stone to give Kay a replacement. Kay immediately tries to claim credit, telling his father Sir Ector that he pulled the sword himself. When Ector demands the truth, Kay confesses — but his instinctive dishonesty establishes his character for the rest of the legend. He is not evil; he is merely weak in the face of temptation, and his weakness is magnified by his proximity to Arthur's greatness.

Kay's role as seneschal gives him a specific place in the chivalric economy. He manages the household of Camelot, organizes feasts, receives guests, and generally keeps the machinery of the court running. This administrative function, while less glamorous than the questing knight's, is essential — and Kay's caustic tongue may be the defense mechanism of a man whose role is necessary but unheroic. Some scholars read Kay as a satirist figure: the one who punctures chivalric pretension with sarcastic remarks that often contain a grain of truth.

Kay's relationship with Arthur is complex. As his foster-brother, he was raised alongside the future king, sharing a childhood that no other knight can claim. This intimacy allows Kay certain privileges — including the right to speak with a sharpness that no other knight would dare use toward Arthur. But it also feeds his resentment: he was the older brother, the legitimate son, the one who should have been the hero, and instead he is the bureaucrat serving a brother who eclipsed him.

In the Welsh tradition, Cei's death is recorded: he is killed by Gwyddawg, whom Arthur then kills in vengeance. His grave is at the island of Afallach (Avalon). The Welsh Cei is a figure of mythic power, and his degradation into the French/English Kay is one of the clearest examples of the transformation of Celtic mythological figures into medieval romance characters.
