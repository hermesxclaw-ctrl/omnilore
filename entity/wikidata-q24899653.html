Who It Is

In the vast celestial bureaucracy of the Shinto pantheon, where sun goddesses command the heavens and storm gods rage across the sea, there exists a name so obscure that even the gods themselves seem to have forgotten it. Ame no Tomi — 'Heavenly Wealth' — appears exactly once in Japan's oldest chronicle, a flicker of divine presence in the Kojiki's account of the second emperor's reign, and then vanishes into the mists of history. No shrines honor this kami. No myths celebrate its deeds. No art depicts its form. Yet this ghost of a deity, this footnote in the divine register, carries a name that whispers of cosmic abundance — a promise of prosperity so fundamental that it was written into the very foundations of the imperial state. Due to the fragmentary nature of the surviving evidence, Ame no Tomi's personality and behavior cannot be reliably characterized. The Kojiki reference suggests a connection to the early imperial court's ritual practices, implying a role as a protective or blessing-deity associated with the legitimacy and prosperity of the Yamato state. However, no myths describing specific actions, adventures, or divine interventions by Ame no Tomi are recorded.

The Kojiki reference suggests Ame no Tomi was invoked in early imperial court rituals. This implies a role in legitimizing the Yamato dynasty and maintaining cosmic order through proper ritual observance.

Accounts I
The Kojiki ReferenceKojiki, compiled by Ō no Yasumaro, based on oral traditions recited by Hieda no Are

Compiled 712 CE; events described date to the legendary reign of Emperor Suizei (traditionally 581-549 BCE, though this dating is not historically verified)

The Kojiki mentions Ame no Tomi in the context of Emperor Suizei's reign, the second emperor of Japan according to traditional chronology. The reference appears to connect Ame no Tomi to the establishment of court rituals or the divine lineage of the imperial family. The text provides no narrative detail, adventure, or mythic episode involving the deity. The reference establishes Ame no Tomi as a figure within the early Shinto cosmology, though its significance appears to have been limited even in the 8th century.

Accounts II
The Servitor Kami and the Ritual of the Imperial HouseKojiki (Record of Ancient Matters); modern Shinto reference works, including Bocking's A Popular Dictionary of Shinto.

Recorded in the Kojiki (712 CE) and reflected in later court ritual tradition.

Because Ame no Tomi appears within the Kojiki narrative of the early imperial reigns, he is understood as a servitor kami of the heavenly court whose name is preserved to account for the ancestral guardianship of the new ruling house. In later shrine tradition, figures of this class were honored through the conferral of priestly roles and through ritual oblations at the shrine of the imperial ancestors. Ame no Tomi's presence in the genealogical record functions less as a storied hero than as a marker of the continuity between the heavenly deities and the earthly imperial succession, reminding readers that the early emperors reigned with divine support rather than alone. Ame no Tomi's mention anchors his service to the second emperor's reign, reinforcing the claim that even the earliest, relatively sparely-documented rulers were accompanied by kami of heavenly origin.
