The Approach

In the shadow of Mount Helicon, where the Muses sang, a king's scheme unfolded—a plan to fill his halls with the seed of a hero. Among the fifty daughters sent to Heracles's bed, Heliconis's name survives only as a footnote to a legend, yet her son would carry the blood of the greatest Greek hero to the shores of Sardinia.

Content advisory. This myth contains themes of sexual exploitation, intoxication, and deception. Reader discretion is advised.
Identity

Heliconis

Pronunciation /hɛlɪˈkoʊnɪs/ (hel-i-KOH-nis)

Etymology

The name Heliconis (Ἑλικωνίς) means 'dweller on Helicon' in Ancient Greek, likely referencing Mount Helicon in Boeotia, associated with the Muses. It is a patronymic or toponymic epithet indicating her connection to the region.

Cultural Ownership

Ancient Greek mythology, specifically the myths of Boeotia and the Thespian princesses.

Disputed Identifications

There is no major dispute over Heliconis's identity, but she is one of the 50 daughters of Thespius, and in some versions the list of daughters varies. Some sources may not mention her by name.

Description

Appearance

No physical description of Heliconis survives in ancient sources. She is typically assumed to be a beautiful young princess, as are the other daughters of Thespius, but this is not attested.

Symbols & Iconography

No specific symbols or iconography are associated with Heliconis. In ancient art, the daughters of Thespius are rarely depicted individually; they may appear as a group in some scenes of Heracles's labors.

Behaviour & Personality

No direct characterization is provided in ancient sources. She is one of many daughters who bore children to Heracles. In one version, she is noted among those who slept with Heracles, but her personality is not described.

Domains & Habitats

Heliconis was a princess of Thespiae, a city in Boeotia, Greece. She likely resided in the royal palace of Thespius.

Version Differences

The main versions of the myth differ in the number of daughters and the duration of Heracles's stay. Apollodorus and Diodorus provide lists of the daughters' names, but they do not always agree. In Apollodorus, Heliconis is said to have borne Phalias. In other sources, the names may vary, and some daughters may be omitted.

Heliconis is a mortal woman and does not possess any supernatural abilities. Her significance lies solely in her role as a mother of one of Heracles's many sons.

Stories & Myths

The Thespian Daughters

Source Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.4.10; Diodorus Siculus, Library of History 4.29; Pausanias, Description of Greece 9.27; Athenaeus, Deipnosophistae 13.556Culture Ancient GreekPeriod Classical to Roman era (5th century BCE - 2nd century CE)

Summary

King Thespius of Thespiae, wishing to have his fifty daughters bear children by the great hero Heracles, entertained Heracles as a guest while he hunted the Cithaeronian lion. Each night, Thespius sent a different daughter to Heracles's bed, often making him drunk so he did not realize he was with a different woman. Heliconis was one of these daughters, and she conceived a son named Phalias. In some versions, the intercourse took place over seven nights, with seven daughters each night. In another variant, all daughters slept with Heracles in a single night, except one who refused and was punished.

Consequences

Heliconis gave birth to Phalias, who became one of the many sons of Heracles. These sons were sent by Heracles to colonize Sardinia, according to some accounts.

Variants

The number of daughters and the duration of the affair vary. In Apollodorus, Heracles slept with all fifty daughters in one night or over seven nights. In Diodorus, he slept with all but one. The names of the daughters also differ slightly between sources.

Sexual exploitation and deception

Source Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.4.10; Diodorus Siculus, Library of History 4.29

Description

Thespius deliberately made Heracles drunk and arranged for his daughters to sleep with him, without Heracles's knowledge or the daughters' explicit consent. This is a form of sexual exploitation and coercion, as the daughters were likely following their father's orders.

Scholarly Context

Classical scholars note that such myths reflect the patriarchal norms of ancient Greece, where women were often treated as property and used to secure alliances or produce heroic offspring. The story also highlights Heracles's excessive appetites and his role as a progenitor of many heroic lineages.
