Archive Dossier — Approach

In the green heart of Ireland, where the River Boyne curves past the great mound of Newgrange, there once lived a god who was never truly young and never truly old. Aengus Óg, the 'young son' of the Dagda, was a master of time itself, able to stretch a single day into an eternity and turn a dream into a lifelong quest. His story is one of love, trickery, and the eternal return of spring.

Aengus, the young god of love, who dreamed a woman into being and followed her across Ireland as a swan beneath the silver moon.

Who They Are

Aengus (or Óengus) is the god of love, youth, poetic inspiration, and dreams — the 'Mac Óg' ('Young Son') of the Tuatha Dé Danann. He is the son of the Dagda and Boann (the goddess of the River Boyne). Aengus lived at Bru na Bóinne (Newgrange), a Neolithic passage tomb the Dagda granted him by a trick: the Dagda let Aengus stay for 'a day and a night,' but Aengus claimed the site forever because days and nights are infinite. Aengus's most famous myth is 'The Dream of Aengus,' in which he falls in love with a woman he saw in a dream. He searched all of Ireland for her, eventually finding her as Caer Ibormeith, who alternated between human and swan form on Samhain. Aengus transformed himself into a swan to be with her — they flew away together, making the trees of the world resonate with their song (a myth explaining why swans sing beautifully at Samhain). Aengus wields a magical sword and has four birds of inspiration who whisper poetry to lovers. He also has the power to heal with kisses — anyone kissed by him would be granted eternal youth. He is the patron of young lovers and poets, representing the beauty, intensity, and fleeting nature of youthful love.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

In Scottish folklore, described as wearing shining gold raiment and a crimson royal robe, riding a white steed. In Irish myths, he is 'red-armed', 'mighty and stern', and an expert in arms.

Symbols & Iconography

Sword Moralltach (Great Fury), sword Beagalltach (Little Fury), spears Gáe Buide and Gáe Derg, golden harp with silver strings, white horse, swans, birds made from kisses.

Behaviour & Personality

Youthful, clever, deceptive but also loving and protective. He uses trickery to gain land, kills those who wrong his family, and rescues lovers. In Scottish folklore, he is romantic and brings spring.

Domains & Habitats

Brú na Bóinne (Newgrange), Tír na nÓg, associated with youth, love, summer, poetic inspiration.

Version Differences

In Irish mythology, he is son of Dagda and Boann; in Scottish folklore, son of Beira (Cailleach) and husband of Brigid. His foster-father varies: Elcmar or Midir in Irish, and his role differs.

Attested in Tochmarc Étaíne / Aislinge Oenguso / Lebor GabálaEra Medieval Irish (recorded 8th-12th c.), reflecting earlier oral tradition
