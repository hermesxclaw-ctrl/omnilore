Who It Is

Fourteen hundred and fifty new lives a day, promised against a thousand deaths a day — that is the wager Izanagi hurls at the rotting woman he abandoned in the underworld, and it is the whole of the Shinto cosmology in a single line: creation as a frantic, stubborn rearguard against mortality. Before there were gods of sun and storm, before there was an imperial house that claimed the sun's blood, there was a man who stirred the formless sea with a jewelled spear until a continent formed, who watched his sister-wife burn to death giving birth to fire, who hacked that fire into eight volcanoes, who then broke his word to the dead, glimpsed their corruption, and fled — all the grief and guilt of creation concentrated in one divine body. Izanagi is a decisive, sometimes rash, and emotionally volatile creator figure. He is shown as dutifully executing the mandate of the older gods (stirring the sea), then as an imperfect spouse who blames his wife for breaching ritual protocol, a grief-stricken widower who makes a doomed descent to Yomi, a betrayer of his own promise (looking at Izanami's rotting corpse), a coward who flees in terror, and afterwards a purifying ancestor who single-handedly begets the sun, moon, and storm through his own body. Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Izanagi is generally interpreted as deriving from the Western Old Japanese verb root iⁿzanap- / izanau, 'to invite' (historical orthography izanafu), combined with the masculine suffix -ki/-gi (the -mi/-gi pair marking male vs female). The literal translation is therefore 'Male-who-invites'

Pronunciation — [ì.(d͡)zä́.nä́.ɡ̟ʲí] in modern Japanese; with the honorific, Izanagi-no-Mikoto [ì.(d͡)zä́.nä́.ɡ̟ʲí nó̞ mʲí.kó̞.tó̞]. Older renderings include 'Izanaki' and, in the Nihon Shoki orthography, a version with -ŋ̟í.

Aliases — Izanagi-no-Mikoto, Izanagi-no-Kami, Izanaki, Izanagi no Oho-kami, 伊弉諾尊, 伊邪那岐命

Powers
Land-creation (Kuniumi) via the heavenly spear

Standing on the Floating Bridge of Heaven (Ame-no-ukihashi), Izanagi churns the primordial, jellyfish-like ocean with the jewelled spear Ame-no-Nuboko; the brine that drips from the spear's tip congeals into the first land, Onogoro Island. He and Izanami then descend, plant the heavenly pillar, and beget the rest of the Japanese archipelago (the Ōyashima and six further islands).

Limit: Requires the tool (the spear) given by the older gods; the first attempt at the marriage rite fails and produces deformed offspring (Hiruko), showing that ritual protocol governs the power's success.

Deity-begetting (Kamiumi) through his own body

After escaping Yomi, Izanagi cleanses himself (misogi) at the river-mouth in Himuka; deities spring from his discarded clothing, the water he splashes, and each immersion. Washing his left eye births the sun goddess Amaterasu-Ōmikami; washing his right eye births the moon deity Tsukuyomi-no-Mikoto; washing his nose births the storm god Susanoo-no-Mikoto — the 'Three Precious Children' (mihashira no uzu no miko).

Limit: This is a reactive, purgative power: he only generates deities after being polluted by contact with the dead, and only while ritually washing away that kegare (pollution).

Slaying of the fire god (Ame-no-ohabari sword)

In grief and rage over the death of Izanami (burned by the newborn fire god Kagutsuchi's birth), Izanagi decapitates Kagutsuchi with his ten-grasp sword (Ame-no-ohabari; the 'heavenly' blade also called Ame-no-Habakiri), cutting the corpse into eight pieces that become eight volcanoes; more deities issue from the blood and remains.

Limit: The slaying, while a 'power', is an act of retributive violence with vast collateral consequence (spawning volcanoes and a host of lesser kami) rather than a clean victory.

Descent into Yomi and the power to walk between life and death

Izanagi descends to the land of the dead (Yomi) in a doomed attempt to retrieve Izanami, demonstrating a unique, if limited, ability of a living deity to cross into the underworld — and to return, by fleeing, deceiving pursuers with thrown vegetation, and finally sealing the entrance with a great boulder (Chigaeshi no Ōkami).

Limit: Death can be seen but not undone: because Izanami has eaten food cooked at the furnace of Yomi she cannot return; Izanagi's own return pollutes him and forces the misogi that births the high pantheon.

Divine expulsion and world-division among the Three Precious Children

Izanagi formally apportions the cosmos to his children (Amaterasu: Takamagahara/high heaven; Tsukuyomi: the night or the sea-tides depending on version; Susanoo: the seas), and — when the weeping Susanoo refuses his appointed task and begs to go to his mother's land Ne-no-Katasu-Kuni — expels him 'with a divine expulsion', the ritual banishing that becomes a template for punishment in the Age of the Gods.

Limit: His authority over the children is real but not absolute: Susanoo later returns, wrecks havoc in Takamagahara, and drives Amaterasu into the heavenly rock-cave — episodes in which Izanagi plays no further saving role.

Apotropaic command over the peach

Fleeing Yomi, Izanagi throws three peaches at the hags of Yomi (Yomotsu-shikome) to drive them back, declares the peach divine, and bids it grow in the land of the living to aid people in need — instituting the peach as a sacred charm-fruit (a motif of Chinese origin, where peaches are apotropaic).

Limit: Close-range obstruction, not a ranged weapon of destruction; its permanent value is talismanic (warding death-taint), set for the living rather than used in combat.

Accounts I
The Five and Seven Generations: Izanagi as the last of the KamiyonanayoKojiki (Tenchi-kaibyaku chapter), Nihon Shoki

Recorded 712/720 CE; the narrative itself is primeval (Age of the Gods)

From a silent cosmic egg, order condenses into the High Plain of Heaven (Takamagahara). Five lone, sexless Kotoamatsukami (Two-in-One lords of the center of heaven and generative forces) appear first, then the Kamiyonanayo — seven generations: first two lone deities (Kuni-no-Tokotachi, Toyokumono), then five brother-sister married pairs (Uhijini/Suhijini, Tsunugui/Ikugui, Ōtonoji/Ōtonobe, Omodaru/Ayakashikone, and finally Izanagi and Izanami). The older gods charge the youngest couple to solidify the drifting, oil-like earth. Establishes Izanagi and Izanami as the terminal, operative pair of the primordial genealogy, the couple who carry the creating mandate into action.

The Stirring of Onogoro and the Great-Eight-Island Land-BirthKojiki (Kuniumi chapter), Nihon Shoki

primeval Age of the Gods

With the jewel-tipped Ame-no-Nuboko, Izanagi churns the sea from the Floating Bridge of Heaven; the salt drips form Onogoro Island, their first home. They erect the heavenly pillar and palace (Yahiro-dono, the eight-hiro hall), then devise a marriage rite: circling the pillar in opposite directions and greeting one another. When Izanami speaks first, Izanagi objects (the wife must not speak first); their first union produces the deformed 'leech-child' Hiruko and the island of foam Awashima, set adrift unacknowledged. They consult the older gods, learn the protocol error, repeat the rite with Izanagi speaking first, and the union becomes blessed: the eight great islands (Ōyashima) and six further islands, then the deities, are born in order. Creates the geography of the Japanese archipelago as mythologically understood (Hokkaidō and the Ryūkyūs absent), and establishes a foundational — heavily criticized, fertility-coded — origin for both the land and the marriage rite itself.

The Birth, Grief-Murder of Kagutsuchi, and Izanami's DeathKojiki; Nihon Shoki; Engishiki

primeval Age of the Gods

Izanami, giving birth to the fire deity Kagutsuchi (Hi-no-Kagutsuchi / Homusubi), is fatally burned. In grief and rage Izanagi beheads the newborn fire-god with his ten-grasp sword Ame-no-ohabari; Kagutsuchi's dismembered body becomes eight volcanoes and spawns dozens of deities, while further kami issue from Izanami's excreta, Kagutsuchi's blood, and Izanagi's tears. Roughly a dozen more gods appear as their firstborn/'death-throes' children. Izanami is buried on Mount Hiba at the border of Izumo and Hōki (in the Kojiki). The Engishiki records that Izanami, in dying, also births the water-goddess Mizuhanome to pacify the fire-god. Marks the entrance of death into the world ('the end of the creation of the world and the beginning of death'); Izanagi's filicide is the first divine act of violence and launches the genealogical burst of lesser kami.

Accounts II
The Descent into Yomi and the Sealing of the UnderworldKojiki; reflected in Izanami and Yomi articles

primeval Age of the Gods

Refusing to accept Izanami's death, Izanagi descends to Yomi, the land of the dead, to retrieve her. She warns that she has already eaten food cooked at Yomi's furnace and cannot return. Breaking his promise not to look at her, Izanagi lights a torch from his comb and sees Izanami's body rotting, swarming with maggots. He flees in terror. She, in shame and fury, sends the thunder-gods (Yakusanoikazuchi), the hags of Yomi (Yomotsu-shikome), and a host to catch him. Izanagi throws his vine and right comb (which become grapes and bamboo shoots the hags pause to eat), then hurls three peaches to drive them off, and finally seals the mouth of Yomi's slope (Yomotsu Hirasaka) with a great boulder. Izanami vows to kill a thousand a day; Izanagi vows to beget fifteen hundred — mortality begins to be countered by fecundity. Establishes the permanent separation of the living and the dead, the Shinto association of death with pollution (kegare), the practice of purification, and the daily counterbalance of death by birth. The entrance to Yomi is placed in Izumo province.

The Purification (Misogi) and the Birth of the Sun, Moon, and StormKojiki; Nihon Shoki (sixth/eighth variant)

primeval Age of the Gods

Shaken by his pollution from Yomi, Izanagi purifies himself by bathing at 'the plain of Awagihara at the river-mouth of Tachibana in Himuka on Tsukushi'. As he removes his clothing and washes, countless kami are born. Washing his left eye births the sun-goddess Amaterasu-Ōmikami; his right eye births the moon-deity Tsukuyomi-no-Mikoto; his nose births the storm-god Susanoo-no-Mikoto — the Three Precious Children. This misogi is the mythological charter for the purification rituals (harae/temizuya) at Shinto shrines. Izanagi then divides the realms among the three. From the one male parent spring the sun, moon, and storm who rule the cosmos; the rite anchors Shinto's central practice of purification by water; and the genesis of the sun-goddess as ancestress of the imperial house is secured (in the misogi version, from Izanagi ALONE).

The Division of Realms and the Divine Expulsion of SusanooKojiki; Nihon Shoki

primeval Age of the Gods

Izanagi apportions Takamagahara to Amaterasu, the night to Tsukuyomi, and the seas to Susanoo. Susanoo, however, 'kept crying and howling until his beard eight hands long hung over his chest,' withering the mountains and drying the rivers. He begs to be allowed to go to his mother's land, Ne-no-Katasu-Kuni. Furious, Izanagi expels him 'with a divine expulsion,' and from this point Izanagi largely disappears from the myth — the narrative torch passes to Susanoo's later havoc in Takamagahara and his slaying of Yamata no Orochi. Sets the cosmic order (sun/moon/storm rule distinct realms); demotes the troublesome Susanoo; and closes Izanagi's active career in the story cycle, since his later role is minimal.

Izanagi and Izanami were 'husband and wife' as an unremarkable couple. — They are explicit divine SIBLINGS (brother and sister) who wed — the last of a series of Kamiyonanayo sibling-pairs. The incest is foundational, not incidental, and is partly an archaic creator-myth trope.

The Three Precious Children were 'created by Izanagi' uniformly, so Amaterasu has no divine mother. — True in the Kojiki (single-parent misogi birth) but FALSE in the Nihon Shoki main narrative, where the couple beget them together; a Shoki variant even births them from mirrors and a glance. The 'no mother' reading is Kojiki-specific.

Izanagi's spear-stirring is a purely physical churn; the land just 'formed'. — The Ame-no-Nuboko sea-stirring is read by scholarship as explicit male-female fertility symbolism (the phallic spear churning the watery mother-sea to 'conceive' the islands). The story's sexual-reproductive charge is central, not accidental.

Izanagi represents an 'evil' or 'punishing' god, or conversely a wholly benevolent creator. — He is neither; he is a flawed, emotionally volatile creator — a grieving widower who breaks a vow, flees his wife's corpse, kills his fire-god son, and then, purified, single-handedly births the high pantheon. Blanket moral labels misread the text.

The Izanagi tectonic plate, the Persona 4 protagonist's Persona, and the deity are the same 'Izanagi.' — The plate (an extinct 130–100 Ma tectonic plate, 1982) and the game Persona are NAMESAKE homonyms — the deity and the cosmographic/game objects are unrelated beyond naming.
