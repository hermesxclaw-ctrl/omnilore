Who It Is

She is the wife who never eats — beautiful, hardworking, frugal, the perfect Edo-period helpmeet. But beneath her hair, hidden at the nape of her neck, her skull has split open to form a second mouth: lips, teeth, tongue, all fully functional, all ravenously hungry. Her hair moves like serpents, snatching rice from the storehouse and feeding the hidden maw while her demure face smiles at her husband across the table. She is futakuchi-onna, the two-mouthed woman — a yōkai who embodies the terrifying truth that a person can harbor an entire hidden self, ravenous and uncontrollable, beneath the most placid exterior.

The futakuchi-onna is fundamentally a tragic figure — a woman afflicted by a curse she did not choose. The second mouth operates independently of her will, often mumbling spiteful and threatening things. It demands constant feeding and can screech obscenely when denied food, causing the woman tremendous pain. The woman herself is often portrayed as otherwise normal and even kind, making her condition a source of profound shame and suffering. Powers
Second Mouth (Hidden Maw)

A fully functional second mouth on the back of the skull, complete with lips, teeth, and tongue. It can eat, drink, speak, and screech independently of the woman. It demands constant feeding and causes pain when denied. The mouth consumes food aggressively — in the prototypical story, the household's rice supply vanishes even though the wife appears to eat nothing.

Limit: The mouth requires actual food — it cannot subsist on spiritual sustenance. If starved, it causes the woman agony but does not appear to have independent lethal capabilities.

Prehensile Hair (Serpentine Tresses)

The futakuchi-onna's hair can move of its own accord, reaching out like tentacles or serpents to grab food and bring it to the second mouth. This ability is described as automatic and independent of the woman's control.

Limit: The hair appears to act only in service of the second mouth's hunger. No accounts describe it being used aggressively against others or for purposes beyond feeding.

Concealment (Hidden Monstrosity)

The futakuchi-onna can pass as a normal human woman indefinitely. The second mouth is hidden beneath her hair, which lies flat when not animated. Only when her hair is parted or when the mouth feeds itself is the truth revealed. This ability to conceal her supernatural nature is the central dramatic mechanism of the stories.

Limit: The concealment is anatomical, not magical — it can be discovered by anyone who parts her hair or observes her from behind while she eats.

Supernatural Endurance (Foodless Existence)

In the prototypical story, the futakuchi-onna appears to eat nothing through her normal mouth while remaining healthy, energetic, and a hard worker. This paradoxical vitality despite apparent starvation is noted by the husband.

Limit: The second mouth compensates by consuming food on her behalf. It is unclear whether she would die of starvation if both mouths were denied.

The supernatural element is concealed entirely: beneath her hair, on the back of her skull, the bone splits open to form a fully functional second mouth with lips, teeth, and a tongue. Domains — The futakuchi-onna is a domestic yōkai, typically found within the home — specifically in the role of a wife. She inhabits the same domestic spaces as ordinary women of Edo-period Japan: farmhouses, village homes. This domestic setting is key to the horror: the monster is not in the wilderness but at the hearth, hidden in plain sight.

Accounts I
The Miser and His Beautiful Wife (Prototypical Tale)Ehon Hyaku Monogatari (c. 1841) and oral tradition; this is the version recorded by Takehara Shunsensai and widely disseminated

Edo period (1603–1868), with likely earlier oral antecedents

In a small village lived a stingy miser who, because he could not bear the expense of feeding a wife, lived entirely alone. One day he met a woman of singular beauty, admired by all the villagers. Though she never ate anything, the miser immediately took her as his wife. She proved a hard worker and never asked for food — the miser was thrilled. But he began to wonder why his rice storage was steadily decreasing. One day the man pretended to leave for work but stayed to spy. To his horror, he saw his wife's hair part on the back of her head, her skull split open to reveal a gaping mouth. Her hair reached out like tentacles to grab rice and shove it into the hungry mouth. The miser fled in terror. The miser discovers his wife's supernatural nature and flees. The story does not record what becomes of either afterward — the revelation itself is the climax.

Accounts II
The Axe WoundJapanese oral folklore, regional variant

Pre-modern, oral tradition

A stingy woman who hoarded food and refused to share with her family was chopping wood when her husband's axe accidentally struck her in the back of the head. The wound was deep but never properly healed. Over time, the gash took on the character of a mouth — lips formed, teeth emerged, and it began to demand food. The wound-mouth spoke in a voice that gradually became more articulate, eventually revealing the woman's secret greed and cruelty to the entire village. The woman's secret nature is exposed by the talking wound. She becomes ostracized from the community.

The Starved Stepchild's RevengeJapanese oral folklore

Pre-modern, likely Muromachi or Edo period

A woman married a widower with a young daughter. Jealous of her stepchild, she fed her own children generously while starving the stepdaughter. The neglected child wasted away and died. Soon after, the woman began to experience terrible headaches, and a second mouth appeared on the back of her head. This mouth would whisper the story of the stepchild's suffering to anyone near enough to hear, and it demanded food constantly. Some versions say the stepchild's spirit had lodged itself in the stepmother's body to exact revenge. In other variants, it is the woman's own surviving daughter who is afflicted — the spirit of the dead half-sister takes possession of her as punishment for the mother's crime. The stepmother is exposed as a child-killer. The second mouth becomes an inescapable confession mechanism, forcing her to relive her crime every time it speaks.
