Who It Is

A woman with a second, hungry mouth on the back of her head, hidden beneath her hair. The second mouth demands constant feeding; its hair acts like tentacles to grab food. Often the result of a curse or punishment for a miserly woman who starved her stepchild.

Form

Normal-looking woman from the front; second mouth with full lips and teeth on the back of her skull, prehensile hair acting as tentacles

Powers

Prehensile hair

Insatiable hunger of second mouth

Hiding the second mouth under hair

Second Mouth (Hidden Maw) A fully functional second mouth on the back of the skull, complete with lips, teeth, and tongue. It can eat, drink, speak, and screech independently of the woman. It demands constant feeding and causes pain when denied. The mouth consumes food aggressively — in the prototypical story, the household's rice supply vanishes even though the wife appears to eat nothing. All versions The mouth requires actual food — it cannot subsist on spiritual sustenance. If starved, it causes the woman agony but does not appear to have independent lethal capabilities. Some accounts describe the mouth as bestial and mindless (an eating organ), while others credit it with independent speech, spiteful personality, and even cunning — suggesting a distinct consciousness rather than a mere anatomical anomaly.

Prehensile Hair (Serpentine Tresses) The futakuchi-onna's hair can move of its own accord, reaching out like tentacles or serpents to grab food and bring it to the second mouth. This ability is described as automatic and independent of the woman's control. Ehon Hyaku Monogatari and oral tradition The hair appears to act only in service of the second mouth's hunger. No accounts describe it being used aggressively against others or for purposes beyond feeding. None documented across versions — the prehensile hair is a consistent feature.

Concealment (Hidden Monstrosity) The futakuchi-onna can pass as a normal human woman indefinitely. The second mouth is hidden beneath her hair, which lies flat when not animated. Only when her hair is parted or when the mouth feeds itself is the truth revealed. This ability to conceal her supernatural nature is the central dramatic mechanism of the stories. All versions The concealment is anatomical, not magical — it can be discovered by anyone who parts her hair or observes her from behind while she eats. None.

Supernatural Endurance (Foodless Existence) In the prototypical story, the futakuchi-onna appears to eat nothing through her normal mouth while remaining healthy, energetic, and a hard worker. This paradoxical vitality despite apparent starvation is noted by the husband. Prototypical miser-wife story The second mouth compensates by consuming food on her behalf. It is unclear whether she would die of starvation if both mouths were denied. It is not explicitly stated in any source whether the nutrition from the second mouth sustains the woman's body or whether the second mouth's consumption is purely extraneous.

Behavior & Habitat

Tragic figure; the second mouth speaks independently, often cruelly, and drives the woman to madness and endless eating

Accounts

In a small village lived a stingy miser who, because he could not bear the expense of feeding a wife, lived entirely alone. One day he met a woman of singular beauty, admired by all the villagers. Though she never ate anything, the miser immediately took her as his wife. She proved a hard worker and never asked for food — the miser was thrilled. But he began to wonder why his rice storage was steadily decreasing. One day the man pretended to leave for work but stayed to spy. To his horror, he saw his wife's hair part on the back of her head, her skull split open to reveal a gaping mouth. Her hair reached out like tentacles to grab rice and shove it into the hungry mouth. The miser fled in terror.

A stingy woman who hoarded food and refused to share with her family was chopping wood when her husband's axe accidentally struck her in the back of the head. The wound was deep but never properly healed. Over time, the gash took on the character of a mouth — lips formed, teeth emerged, and it began to demand food. The wound-mouth spoke in a voice that gradually became more articulate, eventually revealing the woman's secret greed and cruelty to the entire village.

A woman married a widower with a young daughter. Jealous of her stepchild, she fed her own children generously while starving the stepdaughter. The neglected child wasted away and died. Soon after, the woman began to experience terrible headaches, and a second mouth appeared on the back of her head. This mouth would whisper the story of the stepchild's suffering to anyone near enough to hear, and it demanded food constantly. Some versions say the stepchild's spirit had lodged itself in the stepmother's body to exact revenge. In other variants, it is the woman's own surviving daughter who is afflicted — the spirit of the dead half-sister takes possession of her as punishment for the mother's crime.

Content Notes

In one origin variant, the futakuchi-onna was a stepmother who deliberately starved her stepchild to death while feeding her own children. The second mouth is presented as either the vengeful spirit of the dead child possessing the stepmother, or as a karmic punishment. This variant directly implicates the futakuchi-onna as a perpetrator of fatal child neglect — a far darker backstory than the 'miser's wife' variant where the woman herself is starving.

This variant reflects deep anxieties about remarriage and blended families in pre-modern Japan, where stepmother cruelty was a recognized social problem. The story functions as a cautionary tale about the karmic consequences of child abuse. It also intersects with the Japanese folk motif of the onryō (vengeful spirit) of a wronged child.

Japanese oral folklore

In one origin variant, the second mouth forms after a woman is accidentally struck in the back of the head by her husband's axe while chopping wood. The 'accidental' framing strains credibility in some tellings, with the husband's repressed violence suggested as a factor. The wound never heals and gradually transforms into a speaking mouth.

This variant can be read as a folkloric representation of domestic violence — a marital 'accident' that permanently marks and transforms the wife. The speaking wound that reveals the woman's secrets can be interpreted as the body speaking truth that society suppresses.

Japanese oral folklore

The futakuchi-onna's core horror is the revelation that a beautiful, demure, frugal woman harbors within her body a ravenous, uncontrolled appetite. The second mouth is a monstrous id that consumes without restraint. This reflects deep Edo-period anxieties about female appetite — both literal (food consumption) and metaphorical (sexual appetite, greed, desire). The beautiful woman who 'eats nothing' but secretly consumes everything is a metaphor for hidden female power that threatens patriarchal domestic economy.

Scholars such as Michael Dylan Foster and Zilia Papp have analyzed female yōkai as expressions of male anxiety about women who do not conform to expectations of self-denial and domestic service. The futakuchi-onna's second mouth is a literalization of the 'hidden appetite' that threatens to consume the household resources.

Foster (2009), feminist folklore analysis

A way to hold it in your mind

She is the wife who never eats — beautiful, hardworking, frugal, the perfect Edo-period helpmeet. But beneath her hair, hidden at the nape of her neck, her skull has split open to form a second mouth: lips, teeth, tongue, all fully functional, all ravenously hungry. Her hair moves like serpents, snatching rice from the storehouse and feeding the hidden maw while her demure face smiles at her husband across the table. She is futakuchi-onna, the two-mouthed woman — a yōkai who embodies the terrifying truth that a person can harbor an entire hidden self, ravenous and uncontrollable, beneath the most placid exterior.
