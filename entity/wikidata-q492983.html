Who It Is

In the year 60 CE, as the Roman Empire tightened its grip on a rebellious province, a queen stood in a sacred grove and released a hare from the folds of her dress. The animal's frantic dash was read as a divine omen, a sign of favor from a goddess whose name history nearly lost: Andraste. She was the war-goddess of the Iceni, a figure of terrifying power and blood-soaked rites, known to us only through the words of her enemies. But was she a real deity, or a Roman ghost conjured to justify an empire's cruelty?

Andraste's nature is inferred solely from Cassius Dio's account. She is depicted as a war-goddess who receives human sacrifices, particularly of prisoners, in sacred groves. She is also invoked for divination and victory in battle. Her behavior, as described, is consistent with a fierce, martial, and demanding tribal deity. It is crucial to note this is a Roman description, which may exaggerate the 'barbaric' nature of the rites to justify Roman conquest.

Powers
Bestowal of Victory

As a war-goddess, she was likely invoked to grant victory in battle. Boudica's prayer to her before the final battle against the Romans is a direct plea for this power.

Limit: In the narrative, her power is ultimately shown to be ineffective, as the Iceni are decisively defeated. This could be seen as a Roman literary device to demonstrate the superiority of Roman gods and destiny.

Divination (Omen of the Hare)

The ritual of releasing a hare and interpreting its path as an omen is explicitly tied to Andraste. This suggests a power of foresight or the ability to communicate divine will through natural signs.

Limit: The interpretation is left to the human observer (Boudica). The omens are not unambiguous; they require a seer or leader to interpret them.

Acceptance of Human Sacrifice

Dio states that prisoners were sacrificed to her in sacred groves. This is presented as a core part of her worship, a way to honor her and secure her favor.

Limit: This is an extremely controversial and heavily debated point. Roman sources often exaggerated claims of human sacrifice to dehumanize conquered peoples. While archaeological evidence for Iron Age human sacrifice in Britain exists, it is not definitively linked to this specific deity.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No physical description of Andraste survives. No statues, carvings, or coin imagery have been definitively linked to her. Her appearance is entirely unknown and must be considered unattested. Any modern depiction is purely speculative.

Symbols — The only recorded symbol explicitly associated with her is the hare, used in a divinatory ritual. Scholars also speculate she may be associated with the bear (based on the etymological link to Andarta), but this is unproven. The sacred grove itself, as a place of worship and sacrifice, is also a key symbolic attribute.

Domains — Inferred domains include: war, victory, divination, and possibly sovereignty or tribal protection. The context of her worship, tied to the Iceni's war of independence, strongly suggests a tutelary role for the tribe in times of conflict. The association with hares also suggests a connection to prophecy or lunar cycles, as hares were often seen as magical creatures in Celtic and broader European folklore.

Accounts I
The Invocation of Andraste before the Final BattleCassius Dio, 'Roman History', Book LXII

60-61 CE

As the Iceni and their allies prepared for a decisive battle against the Roman governor Suetonius Paulinus, Queen Boudica, in a chariot with her daughters, led a ritual. She released a hare from her robes, and when it ran in a favorable direction, the army cheered. She then raised her hands to the sky and invoked Andraste, thanking her and calling upon her 'as woman against woman' (referring to the Roman governor, whom she called a 'woman' as an insult). She declared they were fighting for liberty, honor, and their very lives, and swore to win or die. She vowed to make Andraste the heir of the Roman women's wealth and to offer sacrifices. Despite the powerful invocation, the Iceni were utterly defeated in the ensuing battle. Boudica is said to have died shortly after (by poison, according to Tacitus). The rebellion was crushed, and Roman control over Britain was solidified. The failure of Andraste's favor, as recorded by Dio, served as a moral lesson on the futility of rebelling against Rome.

Accounts II
Andraste and the Ritual of the HareCassius Dio, Roman History 62; modern reconstructions of Icenian rite

c. 60-61 CE

On the eve of the final confrontation between Boudica's forces and the Roman army under Suetonius Paulinus, Dio reports a public rite centered on Andraste. Boudica, standing in a chariot with her daughters, drew a hare from the folds of her robe and released it. The animal's running direction was read as an omen of victory, and the gathered tribesmen raised a shout in response. Dio records that Boudica invoked Andraste by name as the goddess who presides over the Britons' cause, contrasting her with the Roman deities, and that she thanked her for the part she would play in exacting vengeance for past wrongs. The hare was a sacred and portent-giving animal in several parts of the Celtic world, and Dio's account, while written from a Roman and hostile perspective and at a distance of more than a century, is the only explicit literary witness to the cult of Andraste. It links her to battle, victory, and the divine sanction of rebellion against Rome. The ritual frames the British uprising of 60-61 CE as a sacred war under Andraste's protection.
