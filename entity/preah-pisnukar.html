Who It Is

Every morning in Cambodia, before the first stone of a new house is laid, someone offers incense to the ghost of a builder who never finished his greatest job — because in the story that has outlived the empire, the architect of Angkor Wat did not die at his workbench but broke his own perfect sword, threw it into a river, and sailed away to China rather than be called a thief. That architect, Preah Pisnukar, is the Khmer face of a god who had already built the fortresses of demons, the weapons of gods, and the stairways between heaven and earth before Cambodia existed — and whose name is written into the full ceremonial name of Bangkok.

Across all traditions he is the consummate professional: precise, tireless, loyal to his royal or divine employers, and jealous of his craft. In the Lpoek Angkor Wat he acts as a project manager — summoning servants from all directions, ordering the clearing of the forest around Phnom Bakheng, and demanding high-quality stone. In the modern Khmer folktale he is proud and quick to withdraw: falsely accused of stealing royal iron, he proves his work, then destroys his own sword and sails away, never to return — a departure motif shared with master-builder legends elsewhere. Powers
Divine architecture and city-building

Preah Pisnukar/Vishvakarman builds palaces, temples, cities and entire celestial courts. The epic register credits him with Lanka (built originally for Shiva, later the stronghold of Kubera and then Ravana), Dvaraka for Krishna, Indraprastha for the Pandavas, and the dikpalas' courts in Svarga. The Khmer register gives him Angkor Wat: in Lpoek Angkor Wat he mobilizes servants from every direction, clears the forest around Phnom Bakheng and assembles high-quality stone into the palace Intapras. Thai tradition credits him with the three stairways (silver, gold, crystal) from Tavatimsa to Sankassa.

Limit: In the Khmer poem the work still requires gathered labor and materials — he is a supreme organizer rather than a word-made-stone creator. The one-night-construction claim circulating in tourism copy is not in the poem.

Weapon-smithing for the gods

He forges the weapons of the devas: Indra's vajra (from the sage Dadhichi's bones in the Puranic version), Vishnu's Sudarshana chakra and Shiva's trishula — the latter two created from the shavings of Surya's energy after he pared down one-eighth of the sun's radiance. He also made the chariots of all the gods. The Khmer royal register attaches the Preah Khan Reach, the sacred sword of Khmer sovereignty, to his forge: in the 19th century King Norodom believed the sword had been made by Vishvakarma, while an alternate court belief credited Shiva and Vishnu at Indra's request. In the modern Khmer folktale, Pisnokar smelts 200 kilograms of royal iron into a single blade thinner than a rice-blade.

Limit: The folktale's sword is so fine it seems useless: it splits a water jar and fells a great tree only after a delay, and the king mistakes the work for theft.

Craftsmanship, teaching and the shilpa-sastras

He is the originator of the artisan trades and their sacred manuals. The Brahmavaivarta Purana makes him and the nymph Ghritachi the progenitors of the florist, conch-worker, weaver, goldsmith, coppersmith, ironsmith, carpenter, stonemason, potter and painter communities; in South India five groups — carpenters, blacksmiths, goldsmiths, coppersmiths and stonemasons — are collectively called Vishwakarma. He is credited with the shilpa-sastra texts (Vishvakarmaprakasha, Aparajitapriccha and others) and with the Sthapatya Veda. Thai tradition makes him the teacher who brought building knowledge to humanity; Khmer tradition makes him the model every craftsman and dancer honors before work.

Limit: Attribution of the manuals is traditional, not historically documented; the Sthapatya Veda as a 'Veda' is a class of texts, not a fourth Veda in the canonical sense.

Engineering and celestial machines

The Thai Buddhist register turns him into a mechanical engineer: he built the vala-sanghatayanta, a revolving wheel-machine set around the stupa housing the Buddha's relics for King Ajatasattu, to keep intruders from the relics. The Khmer Wikipedia article likewise says he built flying chariots (ratha) in the sky for the gods and taught the monkey Nala to build the floating-stone bridge across the sea from southern India to Lanka (Rama Setu).

Limit: These are mythological machines; no independent attestation exists.

Creation of living beings and objects

At Brahma's request he created the apsara Tilottama, assembling her from the best qualities of all beings; in the Skanda Purana he sculpts the wooden image of Jagannath at Puri. The creation-of-Tilottama episode is known in Cambodia through the Ramayana tradition, where apsaras appear throughout the Angkor Wat reliefs.

Limit: Tilottama's creation is sometimes credited to Brahma's own craftsmanship with Vishvakarman as artisan-executant.

Accounts I
The palace built for the exiled prince (Lpoek Angkor Wat)Lpoek Angkor Wat (ល្បើកអង្គរវត្ត), early 17th century; transcribed 1878 by Étienne Aymonier; modern edition 2009

Composed c. 1598–1622, in the aftermath of the 1594 Siamese sack of Longvek

Prince Ketumala, son of Indra in a previous existence, cannot remain in the gods' realm: his human scent is unbearable to the devatas, so he is exiled to earth. Out of compassion, Indra sends his personal architect, Preah Pisnukar (Braḥ Bisṇukār, Vishvakarman), to build the prince a palace in the human realm. Preah Pisnukar summons servants from all over the world, orders the forest cleared around Phnom Bakheng, and brings high-quality stone. When the work is complete, Ketumala enters his new palace, named Intapras (Indraprastha), and rules as Maharaja. The poem, which also describes the Reamker bas-reliefs in the temple galleries, is read by scholars (Grégory Mikaelian) as a cosmogonic text commissioned to restore royal legitimacy after the fall of Longvek, and its hero Ketumala is identified with Suryavarman II, the historical builder of Angkor Wat, already half-forgotten by the 17th century. The poem becomes the founding charter of the belief that Angkor Wat was built by the divine architect; the palace Intapras is understood as Angkor Wat itself.

The half-celestial architect and the rice-blade sword (modern folktale)Folktale 'A Tale of Angkor Wat', retold in English by Suksiri Bounchan (PDF, khmerstorylovers3.yolasite.com); parallel retelling in seasia.co (2025) from Nhim's commentary

Oral tradition; retold 2000s–2020s

In this variant Preah Pisnokar is not a god but the half-celestial son of the heavenly maiden Tipsoda Chan and a Chinese merchant, Ta Seng; named Pisnokar as a child because of his gift for building sand castles, he is taken to heaven, trained by celestial craftsmen, and sent down by Indra to accompany Prince Ketumealea back to Cambodia. Crowned king, Ketumealea orders him to build Angkor Wat as a copy of the heavenly palace; the temple is built superb beyond any on earth. Years later the king gives the architect 200 kilograms of iron and demands the most powerful weapons. After three months Pisnokar returns with a single tiny sword, its blade thinner than a rice-blade. Accused of stealing the iron, the architect demonstrates the blade: it splits a water jar that spills, and drawn once across a great tree trunk, leaves the tree standing — until someone touches it and it crashes down. Word of the sword spreads, but when the king sends for him, Pisnokar is gone: witnesses see him at the port, breaking the sword in two, tossing it into the river, and boarding a ship for China. Accounts II
Sanjna, the daughter who fled the sunRigveda (Saranyu); Harivamsa; Puranas; Sanjna article (English Wikipedia)

Vedic to Puranic, c. 1200 BCE–500 CE

Vishvakarman's daughter Sanjna (earlier Saranyu) marries Vivasvant, the sun god Surya, but cannot endure his full radiance. She installs her shadow-double Chhaya in her place and flees, taking the form of a mare. Discovering the substitution, Surya seeks out Vishvakarman, who sets his daughter's father to work: the divine craftsman places the sun on his lathe and pares away one-eighth of its energy. From the shavings he fashions the weapons of the gods — Vishnu's Sudarshana chakra and Shiva's trishula — and the diminished Surya is reunited with Sanjna. Their children are Yama, the god of death; Yamuna, the river goddess; Vaivasvata Manu, the progenitor of humans; the Ashvin twins; and Revanta. The episode explains both why the sun is bearable to the world and where the gods' signature weapons come from; it grounds Vishvakarman's role as the gods' machinist.

The city of Lanka and the bridge to itRamayana (Uttara Kanda); Lanka article (English Wikipedia); Khmer Wikipedia on ព្រះពិស្ណុការ

Epic; reliefs 12th century

Vishvakarman builds the island fortress of Lanka, originally for Shiva; the rakshasa brothers Malyavan, Sumali and Mali seize it, rule for ages, and are eventually displaced by Kubera, who establishes his yaksha kingdom there; Ravana in turn expels his half-brother Kubera and rules Lanka until Rama kills him. To reach the island, the vanara Nala — son of Vishvakarman in the Ramayana, sometimes called his incarnation — builds the causeway Rama Setu with stones that float. The Khmer Wikipedia article retells the same episode in Khmer, saying the deity built Lanka for the yakshas and pisacas and taught the monkey Nala to lay the floating-stone bridge, named Ramasetu, from southern India to Lanka; the battle of Lanka is depicted in the famous reliefs of Angkor Wat, keeping the story continuously visible in Cambodia. Lanka becomes the archetypal demon-capital built by the gods' own architect; the Rama Setu bridge legend makes Vishvakarman's craft the precondition of Rama's victory.

The stairways from heaven (Thai Buddhist register)Jataka commentary tradition, summarized on Thai Wikipedia (พระวิศวกรรม)

Commentarial tradition, 5th century CE and later; still taught

In the Vessantara Jataka's aftermath the Buddha descends from Tavatimsa heaven at the end of the rains retreat, having taught the Abhidhamma to his mother. The divine engineer Phra Wisawakam builds the three stairways for the descent at Sankassa: one of silver, one of gold, one of crystal, running from the heavenly realm to the human world. The same craftsman built hermitages for bodhisattvas in earlier lives and, in another episode, the revolving wheel-machine that guarded the Buddha's relics enshrined by King Ajatasattu. The stories integrate the Indic architect into Theravada sacred geography and make him the builder of the physical link between heaven and earth — the same cosmic-building role he plays in the Khmer poem.

Angkor Wat was built by Preah Pisnukar in a single night. — The Lpoek Angkor Wat does not say one night: the architect summons labor from all directions, clears the forest around Phnom Bakheng and assembles high-quality stone — a project narrative, not an overnight miracle. Historical evidence indicates decades of construction (c. 1113–1150). The one-night version is tour-guide embellishment.

Preah Pisnukar is a purely Cambodian invention. — He is the Khmer form of the Indic Vishvakarman, present in Cambodia via Sanskritic religion and the Ramayana tradition; the same god is Thai Phra Wisawakam, Balinese Bhatara Wiswakarma, Japanese 毘首羯磨 and Chinese 毘首羯磨.

Vishnu is the Thai god of engineering. — The engineering god is Phra Wisanukam (Vishvakarman); the name eroded to Phra Wisanu (Vishnu) in popular speech, producing the mix-up. Thai Wikipedia carries a dedicated correction of this confusion.

The poem of Angkor Wat is an Angkor-period text. — It dates to c. 1598–1622, roughly five hundred years after the temple's construction, and reflects the post-Longvek court's need to re-sanctify kingship.

Aliens (Anunnaki) built Angkor Wat; Pisnokar was a half-breed extraterrestrial. — No evidence supports any extraterrestrial involvement; Khmer quarrying, hydraulics and epigraphy fully account for the monument. Khmer Wikipedia itself notes that ancient-astronaut theorists invoke the deity, and the dossier treats that invocation as reception data, not fact.

The Preah Khan Reach sacred sword was historically forged by Vishvakarma. — This is a 19th-century royal belief (King Norodom), part of the sword's sacral mythology — alongside the Shiva-and-Vishnu-at-Indra's-request variant. No inscription records a divine forger.
