Who It Is

In the cosmic court of Zoroastrianism, where every angel has a demonic shadow, Aeshma is the personification of rage itself — the one who turns a peaceful neighbor into a murderer with a single whisper. He is the ancient world's answer to the question: why do we lose our temper? And his answer is: because a demon made us do it.

Aeshma is the embodiment of destructive wrath, violence, and bloodlust. He is said to incite humans to anger, causing them to commit murder, pillage, and other acts of cruelty. He is particularly active during times of chaos, such as war, and is known to attack the righteous and the followers of the good religion. He is the antithesis of the archangel Sraosha, who represents obedience, discipline, and the divine order (asha). Aeshma is also associated with the demon of the 'wounding spear', suggesting a connection to the violence of battle.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Aeshma derives from Avestan *aēšma-, meaning 'wrath', 'fury', or 'violent rage'. It is cognate with Vedic Sanskrit *eṣma-* (also meaning 'rage' or 'fury') and with Old Persian *aišma-*. The Avestan root is *aēš-*, related to the concept of violent agitation or destructive anger. Powers
Wrath Incitement

Aeshma's primary power is to incite violent rage in mortals, causing them to act irrationally and destructively. He can inflame anger, jealousy, and hatred, leading to murder, war, and other atrocities.

Limit: His influence can be countered by the recitation of holy prayers (particularly the Ahuna Vairya) and by the presence of the archangel Sraosha, who embodies obedience and discipline.

Wounding Spear

Aeshma is associated with the 'wounding spear', a weapon that inflicts severe and painful wounds. This is likely a metaphor for his ability to cause sudden, violent harm, both physical and spiritual.

Limit: The spear is ineffective against those who are protected by the divine order (asha) and by the archangels, particularly Mithra and Verethragna.

Assault on the Soul

Aeshma is said to assault the souls of the righteous as they cross the Chinvat Bridge after death, attempting to drag them into hell. He is particularly active in the period after death, when the soul is judged.

Limit: He is ultimately repelled by the righteous conduct of the soul and by the protection of Sraosha and other divine beings.

Instigation of the Apocalypse

In the Zoroastrian eschatology, Aeshma is prophesied to play a key role in the final battle between good and evil. He will fight alongside the other demons against the forces of good, but will ultimately be defeated and destroyed.

Limit: He is fated to be overcome by Sraosha, who will strike him down at the end of time.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Aeshma is not described in detail in the Avesta, but later texts and artistic traditions often depict him as a fearsome, monstrous figure. He is typically associated with the spear or the mace as his weapons, and is sometimes shown with wild hair, a contorted face, and a body wreathed in flames or shadow. In Middle Persian art, he may appear as a horned demon or as a dark, armored warrior. However, these visual depictions are later interpretation

Symbols — Aeshma is symbolized by the spear, the mace, and the sword—weapons of violence and war. He is also associated with blood, fire (as a destructive force), and the darkness of the underworld. In some texts, he is linked to the demon of the 'wounding spear', which may symbolize the sudden, violent strike that causes chaos and suffering.

Domains — Aeshma's domain is the realm of wrath, violence, and destruction. He is said to dwell in the vicinity of the 'Bridge of the Separator' (Chinvat Bridge), where he lies in wait to assault the souls of the righteous as they cross. He is also active in the material world, particularly on battlefields and in places of conflict. Accounts I
Aeshma's Attack on Mithra's FollowersYasht 10 (the Hymn to Mithra)

Ancient (Avestan period, possibly 2nd–1st millennium BCE)

In this hymn, Aeshma is described as the 'demon of the wounding spear' who attacks the followers of Mithra. Mithra, the god of covenants and the sun, protects his devotees from Aeshma's assaults, and the demon is said to flee in terror from Mithra's presence. The story reinforces the power of Mithra as a protector and the idea that Aeshma, despite his violence, is ultimately powerless against the divine order.

The Defeat of Aeshma by VerethragnaYasht 14 (the Hymn to Verethragna)

Ancient (Avestan period)

Verethragna, the god of victory and strength, is said to defeat Aeshma in battle, driving him away with his divine power. This story emphasizes the triumph of righteous force over destructive wrath. The story serves as a mythological model for the ultimate victory of good over evil.

Accounts II
Aeshma and the Chinvat BridgeBundahishn, Arda Wiraz Namag

Late antique to medieval (3rd–10th centuries CE)

Aeshma is said to lie in wait at the Chinvat Bridge, the bridge that separates the world of the living from the world of the dead. He attempts to seize the souls of the righteous as they cross, but is repelled by their good deeds and by the protection of the archangels. The story reinforces the importance of living a righteous life and the belief in divine protection against demonic forces.

The Final Battle and the Destruction of AeshmaBundahishn (chapter 34)

Medieval (compiled 9th–12th centuries CE)

In the apocalyptic literature, Aeshma is prophesied to play a major role in the final battle between the forces of good and evil. He will fight alongside the other demons, but will be ultimately defeated and destroyed by Sraosha, the archangel of obedience and discipline. The story provides hope for the eventual triumph of good over evil and the restoration of the world to a state of perfection.

Aeshma is the same as the Christian demon Asmodeus. — Asmodeus is a demon from Jewish and Christian demonology, appearing in the Book of Tobit and later in grimoires like the Ars Goetia. While some scholars have suggested a possible etymological connection between Aeshma and Asmodeus, the two figures are distinct in their origins and characteristics. Aeshma is a Zoroastrian demon of wrath, while Asmodeus is a demon of lust and revenge in Jewish tradition. The connection is speculative and not universally accepted.

Aeshma is a fallen angel. — Zoroastrianism does not have a concept of 'fallen angels'. Aeshma is a daeva, a demon created by Ahriman (Angra Mainyu) to oppose the good creation of Ahura Mazda. He is not a being who fell from grace, but rather an inherently evil entity.

Aeshma is a god of war. — Aeshma is a demon of wrath and violence, but he is not a 'god' in the sense of a deity who receives worship. In Zoroastrianism, demons are not worshipped; they are opposed and ultimately destroyed. Aeshma is more akin to a personification of a vice than a god of a domain.

Aeshma is a minor demon. — Aeshma is one of the seven arch-demons in Zoroastrianism, making him a major figure in the demonic hierarchy. He is the direct adversary of the archangel Sraosha and plays a key role in the apocalyptic final battle.
