He is said to dwell in the vicinity of the 'Bridge of the Separator' (Chinvat Bridge), where he lies in wait to assault the souls of the righteous as they cross. He is also active in the material world, particularly on battlefields and in places of conflict. In the Zoroastrian cosmology, he is one of the seven arch-demons, each of whom opposes a specific archangel and represents a particular vice.

Form

Aeshma is not described in detail in the Avesta, but later texts and artistic traditions often depict him as a fearsome, monstrous figure. He is typically associated with the spear or the mace as his weapons, and is sometimes shown with wild hair, a contorted face, and a body wreathed in flames or shadow. In Middle Persian art, he may appear as a horned demon or as a dark, armored warrior. However, these visual depictions are later interpretations, and the original texts focus on his nature as a force of violent rage rather than on his physical form.

Powers

Wrath Incitement Aeshma's primary power is to incite violent rage in mortals, causing them to act irrationally and destructively. He can inflame anger, jealousy, and hatred, leading to murder, war, and other atrocities. Wounding Spear Aeshma is associated with the 'wounding spear', a weapon that inflicts severe and painful wounds. This is likely a metaphor for his ability to cause sudden, violent harm, both physical and spiritual. Avestan (Yasht 10, Yasht 14) The spear is ineffective against those who are protected by the divine order (asha) and by the archangels, particularly Mithra and Verethragna. Some scholars interpret the 'wounding spear' as a literal weapon, while others see it as a symbol of his destructive influence.

Assault on the Soul Aeshma is said to assault the souls of the righteous as they cross the Chinvat Bridge after death, attempting to drag them into hell. He is particularly active in the period after death, when the soul is judged. He will fight alongside the other demons against the forces of good, but will ultimately be defeated and destroyed. Behavior & Habitat

Aeshma is the embodiment of destructive wrath, violence, and bloodlust. He is said to incite humans to anger, causing them to commit murder, pillage, and other acts of cruelty. He is particularly active during times of chaos, such as war, and is known to attack the righteous and the followers of the good religion. He is the antithesis of the archangel Sraosha, who represents obedience, discipline, and the divine order (asha). Aeshma is also associated with the demon of the 'wounding spear', suggesting a connection to the violence of battle.

Accounts

In this hymn, Aeshma is described as the 'demon of the wounding spear' who attacks the followers of Mithra. Mithra, the god of covenants and the sun, protects his devotees from Aeshma's assaults, and the demon is said to flee in terror from Mithra's presence.

Verethragna, the god of victory and strength, is said to defeat Aeshma in battle, driving him away with his divine power. This story emphasizes the triumph of righteous force over destructive wrath.

Aeshma is said to lie in wait at the Chinvat Bridge, the bridge that separates the world of the living from the world of the dead. He attempts to seize the souls of the righteous as they cross, but is repelled by their good deeds and by the protection of the archangels.

In the apocalyptic literature, Aeshma is prophesied to play a major role in the final battle between the forces of good and evil. He will fight alongside the other demons, but will be ultimately defeated and destroyed by Sraosha, the archangel of obedience and discipline.

What We Don't Know

The primary sources for Aeshma are the Avestan texts, which are fragmentary and difficult to date precisely. The oral tradition that preceded the written texts is largely lost. The visual depictions of Aeshma in ancient Iranian art are not well-documented, and the specific iconography of the demon is a matter of interpretation. The connection between Aeshma and the later Jewish demon Asmodeus is speculative and requires further research. The reception of Aeshma in modern Iranian culture, beyond the classical texts, is not well-documented in the available sources. The research cutoff was 2026-08-01.

A way to hold it in your mind

If Zoroastrianism were a courtroom drama, Aeshma would be the prosecutor's worst nightmare — a rogue agent who incites the jury to violence instead of justice. He is the embodiment of 'losing your cool' on a cosmic scale, the ultimate personification of the destructive power of anger. In a world of order and light, he is the shadow of rage that lurks in every human heart, waiting to be unleashed.
