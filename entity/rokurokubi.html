Who It Is

Imagine falling asleep in your own bed, only to watch from a dream as your head floats across town, passes through walls, and peers into windows — and waking up with a bruise around your neck that proves it really happened. This is the rokurokubi, Japan's long-necked yōkai who has haunted inns, temples, and villages for over 350 years, blurring the line between nightmare and reality.

Rokurokubi exhibit a wide range of behaviors across different accounts, from wholly unaware to actively malicious. Many are portrayed as ordinary women who are unaware of their condition — they sleep normally while their heads wander independently, and they may wake from dreams that correspond to the head's activities (the 'dream chase' motif found in Sorori Monogatari). Others appear to transform consciously or suffer from a curse. Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'rokurokubi' (ろくろ首) derives from 'rokuro' (轆轤), which can refer to a potter's wheel, a water well's pulley, or an umbrella handle — all objects that elongate or rotate. The 'kubi' (首) means 'neck' or 'head'. The image is of a neck that extends like a pulley rope. The nukekubi (抜け首) subtype

Pronunciation — roh-koo-roh-koo-bee (four syllables, with a short 'o' and 'u' in each mora)

Aliases — Long-Necked Woman, Rokuro-kubi, Rokurokkubi (ろくろっ首), 轆轤首 (kanji), Nukekubi (抜け首 — subtype whose head detaches completely), Hitōban (飛頭蛮 — Chinese cognate, meaning 'flying head barbarian')

Ownership — Japanese folklore (yōkai tradition), with attested influences from Chinese folklore (hitōban/rakutō) and parallels across Southeast Asia, including the Indonesian kuyang and palasi

Powers
Neck Elongation

The rokurokubi's neck can stretch to extraordinary lengths, allowing the head to travel far from the body while remaining tethered. In woodblock depictions, the neck appears as a thin, sinuous cord that can extend 20 feet or more, reaching through doors, windows, and across rooms.

Limit: The neck extension appears to require sleep or a trance-like state. The condition is involuntary in most accounts — the subject does not control when it happens. In Ban Kōkei's account of the Yoshiwara geisha, the elongation was linked to her 'heart becoming loose' — possibly a metaphor for loss of self-control or moral relaxation.

Head Detachment (Nukekubi)

The head of a nukekubi detaches completely from the body and floats freely through the air. The body remains seated or lying down, inert and vulnerable. The head can fly at speed, pass through doors, and spy on or attack people. Some accounts describe the head using its ears as wings for flight.

Limit: The head must reunite with the body before dawn (in most accounts). If the body is moved or disturbed while the head is away, the head cannot reattach. This is the nukekubi's critical weakness and is exploited in multiple stories. In Hearn's account, the priest Kwairyō uses this weakness by moving the woodcutter's body to a different position. The body must remain undisturbed for reattachment.

Somnambulant Soul Projection

In several Edo-period sources, the rokurokubi phenomenon is explicitly interpreted as the soul (or 'consciousness') leaving the body during sleep, rather than a physical transformation. The floating head is an apparition of the sleeper's spirit. The sleeper may experience the head's adventures as a dream that corresponds exactly to events the head witnesses.

Limit: The sleeper has no awareness or control over the soul's wanderings. The experience is entirely passive — the sleeper 'wakes up' only when the head returns.

Blood Drinking

Some nukekubi attack sleeping victims and drink their blood. This vampiric quality aligns the Japanese nukekubi with its Southeast Asian counterparts (kuyang, penanggalan, krasue) and distinguishes it from the more benign neck-extension variant.

Limit: Blood drinking appears only in the nukekubi (head-detaching) subtype, not the neck-extension type. The victim must be asleep. The nukekubi is vulnerable during feeding — if the body is discovered and moved, the head cannot return.

Karmic Curse Inheritance

The rokurokubi condition may be inherited due to a parent's karmic sins. In Jippensha Ikku's Rekkoku Kaidan Kikigaki Zōshi, a father who murdered a woman and stole her money fathered a daughter who became a rokurokubi as a direct consequence of his actions. The daughter herself is not guilty — she suffers for her father's crime.

Limit: Only one account explicitly links rokurokubi to karmic inheritance. Most other stories present the condition as congenital but unexplained, or as an illness.

Oil Licking

In some Bunka-period tales, rokurokubi are said to lick the oil from paper lanterns or andon lamps. This peculiar behavior appears frequently in depictions of rokurokubi in yōkai art — the head approaches a lamp and extends a long tongue to consume the lamp oil.

Limit: Oil licking is a relatively minor, almost mischievous behavior — more nuisance than threat. It appears in stories where the rokurokubi is more pitiable than malevolent.

At night, particularly while asleep, their necks stretch to extraordinary lengths, sometimes extending 20 feet or more. In the neck-extension subtype, the head remains attached but travels far from the body on a thin, sinuous neck. Accounts I
The Priest and the Woodcutter's Family (The Hearn Version)Lafcadio Hearn, Kwaidan (1904); originally from Japanese oral/folkloric sources

Set in the Muromachi period (~1438, Eikyō War era); published 1904

Kwairyō (formerly Isogai Heidazaemon Taketsura, a vassal turned itinerant priest) is traveling through Kai Province. A woodcutter offers him lodging and warns him of 'Hairy Things.' Kwairyō recites sutras for the woodcutter, then retires. Unable to sleep, he discovers five headless bodies on the floor — the woodcutter and his family. Arokurokubi/nukekubi, their heads are outside bobbing and discussing how to eat him. Kwairyō moves the woodcutter's body to prevent reattachment, then fights the returning heads. Four flee; the woodcutter's head bites his sleeve and remains clamped even after death. Kwairyō travels to Suwa village, where an old man tells him the woodcutter's family were 'not real human beings' — rokurokubi who masqueraded as humans to lure and eat travelers. The head-bitten sleeve becomes a temple relic. The woodcutter's head remains clamped on Kwairyō's sleeve until he reaches Suwa. The head-bitten sleeve is preserved as a temple relic. Kwairyō continues his priestly journey, having demonstrated the virtue of a composed mind in the face of supernatural horror.

The Woman Who Dreamed of Being ChasedSorori Monogatari (1663)

1663, early Edo period

A man witnesses a nukekubi that transforms before his eyes: first into a chick, then into a woman's head. He draws his sword and chases the head, which flees back to a home. Inside, a woman's voice cries out: 'I had a scary dream. I was chased by a man with a sword. I ran away all the way back home and then I woke up.' The man realizes the floating head was the woman's soul, and her dream perfectly mirrored the events he experienced. No direct consequences are recorded in the tale, but it establishes the 'dream sync' motif that would become a hallmark of rokurokubi stories — the sleeper experiences the head's actions as a dream.

The Karmic Mound of RokurokubiJippensha Ikku, Rekkoku Kaidan Kikigaki Zōshi (late Edo period)

Late Edo period (early 1800s)

A monk named Kaishin from Enshū Province elopes with a woman named Oyotsu. When Oyotsu falls ill and their money runs out, Kaishin kills her. Years later, having returned to secular life, Kaishin sleeps with a young woman at an inn. The woman's neck stretches, and her face transforms into Oyotsu's visage, speaking of her resentment. Kaishin confesses everything to the girl's father, who reveals his own past crime: he murdered a woman, stole her money, and opened the inn. His daughter was born a rokurokubi as karmic punishment. Both men repent. Kaishin re-enters the priesthood and builds a grave mound for Oyotsu called the 'Rokurokubi no Tsuka' (Rokurokubi Mound), which serves as a warning for future generations. Two men confess their murders. The innkeeper's daughter is revealed as an innocent victim of her father's karma, not a monster. Kaishin builds the Rokurokubi Mound as a monument. The story strongly links the rokurokubi condition with Buddhist concepts of karmic consequence.

Accounts II
The White Dog's RevengeMatsura Seizan, Kasshi Yawa (c. 1821)

c. 1821

In Hitachi Province, a woman lies terminally ill. A peddler tells her husband that the liver of a white dog will cure her. The desperate husband kills the family's pet white dog and feeds its liver to his wife. She recovers. However, the next child born to them is a daughter who is a rokurokubi. One night, when the rokurokubi's head detaches and flies through the air, the ghost of the white dog appears, bites the head, and kills the rokurokubi — a supernatural revenge for the dog's murder. The dog's spirit avenges its own death. The rokurokubi daughter is killed. The tale echoes the 'faithful animal avenged' motif found in many Japanese ghost stories and the Buddhist principle that killing a sentient being creates karmic debt.

The Innkeeper's Wife of HigoYamaoka Genrin, Kokon Hyaku Monogatari Hyōban (1686)

1686

Priest Zetsugan visits an inn in Higo Province (modern Kumamoto Prefecture). During the night, he witnesses the innkeeper's wife's head leave her body and float in the air. By morning, it has returned, but she bears a red line around her neck. Yamaoka Genrin, in his commentary, references similar accounts from Chinese literature and notes that such things are 'often seen in South-East Asia.' He remarks that 'just as octopuses do not have eyes' — meaning that what seems impossible in one place may be ordinary in another — 'everything strange is in faraway lands.' The woman returns to normal but with the telltale neck mark. Genrin's scholarly commentary contextualizes the event within a broader East Asian and Southeast Asian folklore tradition.

The Maidservant and the SteamMatsura Seizan, Kasshi Yawa (c. 1821)

c. 1821

A female servant with a notably pale face is suspected of being a rokurokubi. Her master checks on her while she sleeps and witnesses a strange phenomenon: steam begins to rise gradually from her chest. The steam thickens and obscures her head, and suddenly it appears that her neck has stretched upward. Startled perhaps by her master's presence, the girl stirs and turns over, and her neck returns to normal. Despite looking completely ordinary when awake, she is fired and in fact has had difficulty maintaining any employment — always being dismissed shortly after being hired. The servant is fired despite looking completely normal. The narrative implies she has been fired from multiple positions — her condition (or reputation for having it) prevents stable employment, highlighting the social cost of being labeled a yōkai.

Physical detachment vs. soul projection — Is the head really detaching?The head literally detaches from the body. In multiple accounts, witnesses see a physical separation, the body is left with a clean stump, and the head can be physically interacted with (grabbed, bitten, thrown).The phenomenon is soul projection or somnambulism. In Sorori Monogatari and Hokusō Sadan, the 'head' is explicitly identified as the soul manifesting visually, and the sleeper experiences the event as a dream. The physical-seeming separation is a form of ectoplasm or visual illusion. The line around the neck may be a psychosomatic mark, not evidence of actual detachment.
Gender — Why are rokurokubi overwhelmingly female?Rokurokubi are specifically a female yōkai, and the condition is linked to female biology, psychology, or morality. Male rokurokubi are so rare (Shousai Hikki) they prove the rule. The connection to menstruation, childbirth, or female 'hysteria' (a common Edo-period trope) may explain the gendering.The female predominance is a literary convention rather than a folkloric fact. The earlier Chinese sources (hitōban) are not gendered. The gendering developed during the Edo period as part of a broader pattern that associated yōkai with female transgression and supernatural danger. The Shousai Hikki male nukekubi demonstrates the condition is not inherently female.
Single yōkai or multiple distinct phenomena?Rokurokubi is a single, unified yōkai with two subtypes (neck-extension and head-detachment). This is the position taken by most English-language yōkai references and by the Wikipedia article, which treats rokurokubi as the umbrella term with nukekubi as a subtype.Rokurokubi and nukekubi are either independent yōkai or the classification is reversing the relationship: nukekubi may be the original and rokurokubi a later visual misinterpretation. Some Japanese reference works list them separately. Tada Katsumi argues the neck-extension type emerged from artistic errors, not folk belief.
