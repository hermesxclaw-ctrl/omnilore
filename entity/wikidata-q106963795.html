The Approach

A name that means 'dog's tail' in ancient Greek, Cynosura wags through geography, mythology, and the stars—from a nymph who nursed Zeus to the constellation that guided sailors home.

Content advisory. None
Identity

Cynosura

Pronunciation sigh-NOSS-yoo-ruh (anglicized); ky-NO-soo-ra (reconstructed Ancient Greek)

Known As

KynosouraCynosura (nymph)Cynosura (constellation)Cynosura (Laconia)Cynosura (Salamis)

Etymology

From Ancient Greek Κυνόσουρα, literally 'dog's tail' (κύων 'dog' + οὐρά 'tail').

Cultural Ownership

Ancient Greek; later adopted into Roman and astronomical traditions.

Disputed Identifications

Whether the nymph Cynosura is identical with the constellation's namesake is disputed; some sources identify the constellation with the nymph, others with a different figure or simply the shape of a dog's tail.

Description

Appearance

As a nymph, Cynosura is not described in surviving sources; she is likely depicted as a young woman in later art, but no ancient description survives. As a constellation, Cynosura is a small bear-shaped asterism, the tail of which points to the celestial pole.

Symbols & Iconography

In astronomical iconography, Cynosura is depicted as a bear or a dog's tail; in mythology, she is associated with the nurturing of Zeus and the pole star.

Behaviour & Personality

As a nymph, Cynosura is known only as a nurse of Zeus; no personality traits are recorded. As a constellation, she is a fixed guide for navigation.

Domains & Habitats

Cynosura the nymph dwelled on Mount Ida in Crete, where she nursed Zeus; Cynosura the constellation circles the celestial pole; the promontory is on Salamis; the settlement is in Laconia.

Version Differences

The nymph and the constellation are often identified, but the promontory and settlement are distinct places. The constellation is sometimes called Helice (Ursa Major) in contrast, but the two are separate.

Powers & Abilities

Nurture of Zeus

Source Version Hesiodic tradition, scholia on Aratus

Description

As one of the nymphs who nursed the infant Zeus, Cynosura provided sustenance and protection, enabling his survival and eventual rise to kingship of the gods.

Limitations

No specific powers are attributed to her; her role is passive and supportive.

Contradictions

Some sources say Zeus was nursed by the goat Amalthea, others by nymphs; Cynosura is one of several named nurses.

Catasterism (Constellation Placement)

Source Version Hesiod, Aratus, later mythographers

Description

Cynosura was placed among the stars as the constellation Ursa Minor, becoming a permanent celestial marker.

Limitations

The catasterism is a reward, not an active power; it does not grant her agency.

Contradictions

Some versions say the constellation represents a dog, not a bear, and the name 'dog's tail' supports that.

Navigational Aid

Source Version Aratus, Phaenomena

Description

As the constellation Cynosura, she served as a guide for sailors, particularly the Phoenicians, who steered by the pole star.

Limitations

This is a functional attribute of the constellation, not a personal power.

Contradictions

None.

Stories & Myths

The Nursing of Zeus

Source Hesiodic tradition, scholia on AratusCulture Ancient GreekPeriod Archaic

Summary

In fear of his father Cronus, the infant Zeus was hidden in a cave on Mount Ida. Nymphs, including Cynosura, cared for him, feeding him with honey and the milk of the goat Amalthea. To honor her, Zeus later placed Cynosura among the stars.

Participants

Cynosura

Zeus

Amalthea

other nymphs

Consequences

Cynosura was catasterized as Ursa Minor, and the constellation became a guide for navigators.

Variants

Some versions omit the nymphs entirely, saying Zeus was nursed by the goat alone; others name different nymphs such as Adrasteia and Ida.

The Constellation Cynosura

Source Aratus, Phaenomena; later astronomyCulture Ancient Greek and PhoenicianPeriod Hellenistic and later

Summary

The constellation Ursa Minor, called Cynosura by the Greeks, was used by the Phoenicians for navigation because it contains the pole star. The Greeks themselves more often used Ursa Major, but the name Cynosura persisted.

Participants

Cynosura (constellation)

sailors

Consequences

The name Cynosura became synonymous with the pole star and the constellation, influencing later astronomical terminology.

Variants

Some ancient writers identified the constellation as a dog, not a bear, and the name 'dog's tail' suggests that interpretation.

The Promontory of Salamis

Source Strabo, GeographyCulture Ancient GreekPeriod Classical/Hellenistic

Summary

A promontory on Salamis Island, called Cynosura, overlooked the strait where the Greek fleet defeated the Persians in 480 BCE. It served as a vantage point and landmark.

Participants

Greek fleet

Persian fleet

Themistocles

Consequences

The promontory is associated with the Battle of Salamis, a pivotal event in Greek history.

Variants

No mythological variant; purely geographical.

The Settlement in Laconia

Source Pausanias, Description of GreeceCulture Ancient GreekPeriod Classical/Roman

Summary

Cynosura was a settlement in Laconia that later became part of Sparta. It was one of the villages that united to form the Spartan state.

Participants

Dorian settlers

Spartans

Consequences

Contributed to the foundation of Sparta, one of the most powerful city-states in ancient Greece.

Variants

No mythological variant; purely historical.

Cynosura (Ursa Minor) and Helice (Ursa Major) are two neighboring bear constellations, often mentioned together in astronomical texts.

Version Note

They are distinct constellations but share the bear motif.

Some sources call Ursa Minor the 'dog's tail' rather than a bear, creating a semantic tension.

Contradictions & Disputes

Identity of the constellation

Position A

Cynosura is a bear, representing the nymph who nursed Zeus.

Position A — evidence

Hesiodic scholia and Aratus call it a bear (ἄρκτος).

Position B

Cynosura is a dog, as the name 'dog's tail' suggests.

Position B — evidence

The etymology and some scholia interpret it as a dog's tail.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars accept the bear identification, but the dog interpretation persists in some sources.

Nymph or not

Position A

Cynosura is a nymph who nursed Zeus.

Position A — evidence

Hesiodic fragments and scholia on Aratus.

Position B

Cynosura is purely a celestial body with no mythological personification.

Position B — evidence

Aratus himself does not mention a nymph, only the constellation.

Scholarly Consensus

The mythological connection is plausible but not universally supported; Aratus may have omitted it for brevity.

Place names

Position A

The promontory and settlement are named after the nymph.

Position A — evidence

None directly linking them to the myth.

Position B

They are independently named for their shape (dog's tail).

Position B — evidence

The name is descriptive and could apply to any curved landform.

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus; likely independent usage.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

Primary sources for the nymph are fragmentary and indirect; no dedicated ancient text survives. The exact relationship between the nymph and the constellation is not fully documented. The promontory and settlement lack detailed archaeological or historical records beyond their mention by ancient geographers. Modern scholarly analyses specifically focused on Cynosura are scarce; most information comes from broader studies of Greek mythology and astronomy.

Research Scope

This dossier covers all attested meanings of Cynosura: the nymph, the constellation Ursa Minor, the promontory of Salamis, and the settlement in Laconia. It synthesizes available ancient sources and modern scholarship, flagging areas where evidence is thin or contradictory.
