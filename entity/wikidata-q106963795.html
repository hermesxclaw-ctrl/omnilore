Who It Is

A name that means 'dog's tail' in ancient Greek, Cynosura wags through geography, mythology, and the stars—from a nymph who nursed Zeus to the constellation that guided sailors home.

As a nymph, Cynosura is known only as a nurse of Zeus; no personality traits are recorded. As a constellation, she is a fixed guide for navigation.

Aliases — Kynosoura, Cynosura (nymph), Cynosura (constellation), Cynosura (Laconia), Cynosura (Salamis)

Ownership — Ancient Greek; later adopted into Roman and astronomical traditions.

Powers
Nurture of Zeus

As one of the nymphs who nursed the infant Zeus, Cynosura provided sustenance and protection, enabling his survival and eventual rise to kingship of the gods.

Limit: No specific powers are attributed to her; her role is passive and supportive.

Catasterism (Constellation Placement)

Cynosura was placed among the stars as the constellation Ursa Minor, becoming a permanent celestial marker.

Limit: The catasterism is a reward, not an active power; it does not grant her agency.

Navigational Aid

As the constellation Cynosura, she served as a guide for sailors, particularly the Phoenicians, who steered by the pole star.

Limit: This is a functional attribute of the constellation, not a personal power.

Form & Signs

Appearance — As a nymph, Cynosura is not described in surviving sources; she is likely depicted as a young woman in later art, but no ancient description survives. As a constellation, Cynosura is a small bear-shaped asterism, the tail of which points to the celestial pole.

Symbols — In astronomical iconography, Cynosura is depicted as a bear or a dog's tail; in mythology, she is associated with the nurturing of Zeus and the pole star.

Domains — Cynosura the nymph dwelled on Mount Ida in Crete, where she nursed Zeus; Cynosura the constellation circles the celestial pole; the promontory is on Salamis; the settlement is in Laconia.

Accounts I
The Nursing of ZeusHesiodic tradition, scholia on Aratus

Archaic

In fear of his father Cronus, the infant Zeus was hidden in a cave on Mount Ida. Nymphs, including Cynosura, cared for him, feeding him with honey and the milk of the goat Amalthea. To honor her, Zeus later placed Cynosura among the stars. Cynosura was catasterized as Ursa Minor, and the constellation became a guide for navigators.

The Constellation CynosuraAratus, Phaenomena; later astronomy

Hellenistic and later

The constellation Ursa Minor, called Cynosura by the Greeks, was used by the Phoenicians for navigation because it contains the pole star. The Greeks themselves more often used Ursa Major, but the name Cynosura persisted. The name Cynosura became synonymous with the pole star and the constellation, influencing later astronomical terminology.

Accounts II
The Promontory of SalamisStrabo, Geography

Classical/Hellenistic

A promontory on Salamis Island, called Cynosura, overlooked the strait where the Greek fleet defeated the Persians in 480 BCE. It served as a vantage point and landmark. The promontory is associated with the Battle of Salamis, a pivotal event in Greek history.

The Settlement in LaconiaPausanias, Description of Greece

Classical/Roman

Cynosura was a settlement in Laconia that later became part of Sparta. It was one of the villages that united to form the Spartan state. Contributed to the foundation of Sparta, one of the most powerful city-states in ancient Greece.
