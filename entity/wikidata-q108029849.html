Who It Is

Before a stone is sacred, it is just stone. After the rite — washing, anointing, a spoken formula, a touch of an altar — it cannot be stepped on, sold, or used for ordinary work. The sacred is not a glow you can see; it is a boundary humans draw around people, places and things and then treat as if the boundary were built by the gods themselves. From Roman tribunes declared untouchable to South Indian temple towers bathed in sanctified water every twelve years, holiness is how societies make the invisible feel inviolable.

Sacred things are treated as having agency and contagion: they bless, protect, demand avoidance, punish transgression, and transmit status by contact. Holiness in Priestly thought is described as 'effervescence of the Presence' that infuses and claims what it touches as divine property. Behavior expected of humans toward the sacred: reverence, awe (mysterium tremendum in Otto), avoidance of profane use, ritual purity, pilgrimage. Consecration and Sanctification (Making Sacred/Holy)

Ritual transformation of person, object, building or time from profane to sacred status through washing, vesting, anointing, sacrifice, mantra, or prayer. Examples: Aaronite priestly consecration, Christian church consecration, kumbhabhishekam, buddhabhiseka eye-opening, Jain pratishta.

Limit: Requires authorized officiant (priest, Acharya/Bhattaraka, agama-trained priest), correct materials and formulae; invalid if performed improperly. In Christianity many denominations require bishop. Reversibility varies.

Contagious Transmission and Claiming

Holiness spreads by contact and claims what it touches as divine property. Exodus 29:37: anything touching the sanctified altar becomes holy and thus forbidden for ordinary use. Priestly thought: holiness as effervescence that infuses surroundings.

Limit: Contagion is not automatic in all traditions; rabbinic and Christian moral holiness is not physically contagious. Requires purity; impurity can repel holiness.

Inviolability and Protective Taboo (Sacrosanctitas)

Sacred persons/objects become untouchable; harming them is sacrilege punishable by divine and communal sanction. Roman tribunes of the plebs were sacrosanctus — anyone harming them was sacer (accursed) and could be killed with impunity. Sacred ground cannot be violated.

Limit: Protection depends on collective recognition; when belief lapses, inviolability collapses. Later emperors appropriated tribunician power politically.

Purification, Wholeness and Moral Perfection

Holiness as wholeness/health (hailagaz < hailaz) and moral purity. Aquinas: holiness is virtue by which all acts are made subservient to God, outcome of sanctification/justification. Islamic tazkiah/taharat/ikhlas/qalb-is-salim: reform and purification of heart. Yields worthiness, blessing, and eligibility to approach deity.

Limit: Not achieved by ritual alone in these theologies; requires divine grace (Aquinas: infused moral virtue) and sustained ethical practice. Human holiness is always participated, not inherent.

Awe, Reverence and Social Cohesion

Sacred generates collective awe, reverence and group unity. Durkheim: sacred symbols (totems) embody group interests and are sustained through ritual. Eliade: hierophany provokes existential orientation and repetition of archetypal time.

Limit: Effect requires shared belief and performance; desacralization through repetition without belief or commercialization weakens power. Durkheim explicitly says sacred/profane is not good/evil.

Desecration and Deconsecration (Reversibility)

Sacred status can be removed or violated. Desecration is profanation/sacrilege; deconsecration is formal liturgical removal of sacred character to allow demolition or secular sale (Christian practice). In Judaism, hillul ha-Shem (desecration of Name) through sin.

Limit: Not all traditions allow deconsecration; some sacredness is considered indelible (e.g., character indelebilis of priestly ordination in Catholicism). Desecration often requires expiation.

Accounts I
The Consecration of Aaron and His SonsHebrew Bible, Exodus 29 and Leviticus 8; Priestly source

Literary setting Late Bronze Age; composition 6th-5th century BCE

At Sinai, YHWH commands Moses to consecrate Aaron and his sons as hereditary priests. Over seven days they are washed, vested in linen and gold, anointed with oil, and offered a bull and two rams. Blood is placed on right earlobe, thumb and big toe, and thrown against the altar. The priests and their garments become holy, set apart to serve in the Tabernacle. The rite concludes that their sanctification claims them as divine property. Establishes hereditary Aaronite priesthood and paradigm for all later Jewish and Christian priestly consecration; defines holiness as transmissible by anointing and contact; garments themselves become holy and must be guarded.

The Sanctification of the AltarExodus 29:36-37; Exodus 30:26-29

Priestly source, 6th-5th century BCE

Moses is to sanctify the altar of burnt offering for seven days with sin offerings. After sanctification, the altar becomes 'most holy' (qodesh qodashim) so that 'whatever touches the altar shall become holy' — and therefore forbidden for common use. Anointing oil similarly sanctifies Tabernacle, ark and vessels. Creates legal category of contagious holiness and prohibition on profane use; influences later Jewish law of me'ilah (misuse of sacred property) and Christian altar theology.

Sacrosanctitas and the Tribunes of the PlebsLivy, Ab Urbe Condita 2.33, 3.55; Dionysius of Halicarnassus; Lex Sacrata

Traditionally 494 BCE (first secession of plebs); codified 5th-3rd century BCE

To protect plebeian representatives from patrician violence, the plebs swore an oath (lex sacrata) declaring their tribunes sacrosanctus. Anyone who harmed a tribune became sacer — accursed, forfeit to the gods, and could be killed without penalty. The tribunician power (tribunicia potestas) later became the emblem of inviolability arrogated by emperors. Model for inviolability as sacred law; shows sacred can mean both holy and accursed; basis for later Western ideas of sanctity of office and diplomatic immunity.

Accounts II
Kumbhabhishekam: Renewing the Temple's PurityAgama Shastras; contemporary South Indian temple practice

Agamic codification c. 5th-12th century CE; living tradition to present, performed every 12 years

After construction or renovation, priests perform kumbhabhishekam: sanctified water from kalasha pots, infused with mantra and herbs, is poured over the vimana and deities. The rite purifies the temple, renews its sacred power, and re-establishes the deity's presence. Witnessing is said to grant a thousand punya (merit). Re-consecrates space as sacred; marks 12-year ritual cycle; demonstrates Hindu view of sacred as renewable purity requiring periodic renewal, not permanent once-for-all.

Buddhabhiseka: Consecrating the ImagePali and Sanskrit ritual manuals; Buddhābhiseka tradition

Attested from early medieval period (c. 5th century CE onward); continuous living ritual

A newly made Buddha or bodhisattva image is ceremonially consecrated, often through eye-opening (netra pinkama) and chanting, inviting the enlightened presence to animate the form. Rituals vary: sprinkling with scented water, recitation of sutras, insertion of relics or mantras inside hollow images. Afterward the image is treated as living embodiment, not mere art. Transforms crafted object into sacred support for veneration and merit; distinguishes consecrated image from unconsecrated copy; central to temple economy and devotion.

Panch Kalyanaka Pratishtha MahotsavaJain Agamas and contemporary Jain consecration manuals

Classical Jainism c. 5th century BCE onward; modern mass ceremonies from 20th century

When a new Jain temple is built or new Tirthankara icons installed, a multi-day festival enacts the five auspicious events (pancha kalyanaka) of a Tirthankara's life — conception, birth, renunciation, omniscience, liberation — culminating in pratishta (installation) and anjana (eye-opening). Supervision by an Acharya or Bhattaraka is required to validate consecration. Icons become sacred embodiments worthy of puja; without proper pratishta they remain mere stone; reinforces lineage authority and communal merit.

Is holiness contagious by contact or interior moral state?Priestly view: holiness is physical contagion - altar, oil and garments transmit holiness to whatever they touch, claiming it as divine property (Exodus 29:37).Prophetic/rabbinic/Aquinas/Sufi view: holiness is moral/spiritual interiority - sanctification through mercy, martyrdom, virtue, purification of heart (tazkiah), not touch.

Sacred means good and holy, profane means bad and evil. — Durkheim explicitly states sacred/profane is not good/evil. Sacred can be impure, dangerous or evil (e.g., sacer as accursed, demonic sacred), and profane can be good and necessary. Otto's holy includes dread (tremendum), not just attraction.

Holy and sacred are exactly the same word with the same history. — Different etymologies and theological loads: sacred < Latin sacer/PIE *seh2k- 'consecrate'; holy < Germanic hailagaz < hailaz 'whole, healthy'. Christianity often reserves holy for persons/relationships and sacred for objects/places/times, though overlap exists.

A sacred place or object has always been sacred and can never lose its status. — Sacred status is conferred by consecration and can be removed by deconsecration (Christian liturgical deconsecration before demolition/sale) or violated by desecration. Hindu kumbhabhishekam must be renewed every 12 years precisely because purity fades.

Sufi 'sanctification' (qaddasa Allahu sirrahu) means Sufis worship saints as holy beings like Christian saints. — In Islam, tazkiah/taharat is purification of heart; formula 'may God sanctify his secret' is a prayer for a saint (alive or dead), not deification. Tasawwuf is often misinterpreted as saint-worship; mainstream Sufi theology maintains tawhid.
