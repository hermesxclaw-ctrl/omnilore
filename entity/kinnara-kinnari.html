Who It Is

The Kinnaras and Kinnaris (male and female respectively) are a class of semi-divine celestial beings that appear prominently in the mythology, art, and literature of both Hindu and Buddhist traditions across South and Southeast Asia. They are depicted as creatures with the upper body of a beautiful human (male or female) and the lower body of a bird or swan—graceful, elegant beings known for their extraordinary musical ability, their skill in dance, and their role as celestial entertainers. In Hindu mythology, the Kinnaras inhabit the heavenly realm alongside the Gandharvas (celestial musicians), Apsaras (celestial dancers), and are often associated with Kubera, the god of wealth. They are guardians of music and art, and their songs are said to be so beautiful that even the gods pause to listen. In the Buddhist tradition, Kinnaras appear in Jataka tales (stories of the Buddha's past lives), where they are portrayed as gentle, loyal beings whose love and devotion are exemplary. One of the most famous Jataka stories, the Kinnari Jataka, tells of a Kinnari wife who saves her Kinnara husband from a hunter's arrow through the power of her love and her beautiful voice. In Thai culture, the Kinnari (known as 'Kinnaree,' literally 'What is this?') is an especially beloved figure. She appears extensively in Thai temple art, classical dance-drama (particularly the Manohra dance), literature, and even as the symbol of Thai Airways. Her image—half-woman, half-bird in graceful flight—epitomizes the ideal of transcendent feminine beauty. In Burmese and Cambodian art, Kinnaras and Kinnaris frequently appear as decorative motifs on temples and palaces, often depicted in loving pairs. In Indonesia, particularly in the Borobudur temple reliefs, Kinnaras flank the Buddha, playing instruments. They represent the bridge between the earthly and celestial, the human and the divine—beings whose art transcends the boundaries of ordinary existence.
