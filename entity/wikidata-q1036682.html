Who It Is

In the misty borderlands of the ancient world, where Scythian horsemen ranged beyond the reach of Greek maps, there was said to live a people who milked their mares and drank the milk of horses. The Greeks called them Hippemolges—the 'mare-milkers'—and placed them at the edge of the known earth, a people whose very name evoked a life bound to the horse, a life both strange and admirable.

Ancient sources characterize them as a simple, just, and warlike people who live on mare's milk and are skilled horsemen. Homer's epithet 'glorious' (ἀγαυοί) suggests a noble, heroic quality. Strabo emphasizes their frugality and justice, aligning them with the idealized nomadic life.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Greek ἱππημολγοί (hippēmolgoi), literally 'mare-milkers' — from ἵππος (hippos, 'horse') and ἀμέλγω (amelgō, 'to milk'). The name is a descriptive epithet, not a self-designation.

Pronunciation — hip-EM-ol-jeez (anglicized); Greek: hip-pay-mol-GOY

Aliases — Hippemolgi, Hippemolgoi, Mare-milkers

Ownership — Ancient Greek literature; the term is applied to a mythical or semi-mythical people of the Eurasian steppe, often associated with Scythians.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No detailed physical description survives. Ancient sources do not provide specific visual attributes beyond their nomadic, horse-riding lifestyle. They are likely depicted in Greek art as generic Scythians or nomads, but no direct iconography is attested.

Symbols — No specific symbols or iconography are known. The horse is implicit in their name, but no distinct emblem is recorded.

Domains — They were said to inhabit the Eurasian steppe, north of the Black Sea, in the region of Scythia. Homer places them in the far north, near the Thracians and Mysians, but later authors locate them more specifically in Scythia.

No specific narrative survivesHomer, Iliad 13.5-6

c. 8th century BCE (traditional date)

The Hippemolges appear only in a passing mention in the Iliad, where Zeus turns his gaze from the Trojan War to the land of the Thracians, Mysians, and the 'glorious Hippemolgi, who drink the milk of mares, and the Abii, the most just of men.' No story is told about them. The mention establishes them as a distant, legendary people at the edge of the world, notable for their diet and justice.
