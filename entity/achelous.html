Who It Is

Before the gods of Olympus had fully claimed their thrones, there was a river so vast and so ancient that its name became a word for all water. The Greeks called it Achelous, the greatest river in their land, and they knew that its god could shift from serpent to bull to a man with a torrent for a beard. When Heracles sought to marry Deianeira, it was Achelous who rose from the flood to challenge him—a battle that would tear a horn from the river-god's head and give the world its first cornucopia. But Achelous was more than a defeated suitor; he was the father of springs and Sirens, the purifier of a cursed hero, and a force that could drown the careless and create islands from the sea. This is the story of the river that shaped Greece itself.

Achelous is depicted as a powerful and prideful deity, quick to anger (as in the story of the Echinades), but also capable of pity (saving Perimele). He is a suitor for Deianeira, showing a desire for mortal women, and he is a purifier and helper to Alcmaeon. He is often portrayed as a tragic or unlucky lover in later Roman literature.

Powers
Shapeshifting

Achelous can change his form, appearing as a bull, a serpent, or a man with bull features. This is attested in Sophocles and Ovid.

Limit: In the contest with Heracles, he loses when in bull form; Heracles tears off his horn. The shapeshifting does not guarantee victory.

Control over water

As a river god, he can flood his banks, as he did to punish the nymphs who forgot him. He can also create islands and alter landscapes.

Limit: His power is localized to his river, but he can affect the surrounding land and sea.

Purification

Achelous can purify a person of blood-guilt, as he did for Alcmaeon.

Limit: This may require the person to come to his river; the purification is part of a larger ritual.

Father of springs and nymphs

Achelous is the father of several nymphs associated with springs (Pirene, Castalia, Dirce, Callirrhoe), and possibly all springs.

Limit: Not a power in the usual sense, but a generative ability.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Achelous is primarily a river god, often depicted as a man with bull horns or a bull's head, or as a bull. Sophocles describes him as appearing as a bull, a serpent, and a half-man/half-bull with a beard from which fountain water sprayed. In art, he is typically shown with a bull's horns and a beard, sometimes with a fish tail. Ovid implies he can take human form, as he narrates his own story in the Metamorphoses.

Symbols — Bull horns, cornucopia (from his broken horn), river reeds, water, and sometimes a serpent. In ancient art, he is often depicted in scenes with Heracles, especially on vases and coins.

Domains — The Achelous River in Acarnania, Greece, the largest river in Greece. As a river god, he is associated with fresh water in general, and his name was used metaphorically for water. He is the father of springs and nymphs, suggesting a broader domain over water sources.

Accounts I
The Contest with Heracles for DeianeiraSophocles, Women of Trachis; Ovid, Metamorphoses; Pindar (fragment)

Archaic to Classical (7th–5th century BC), later Roman

Achelous, as a suitor for Deianeira, fights Heracles for her hand. He transforms into a bull and is defeated. Heracles tears off one of his horns. In Ovid's version, the Naiads fill the horn with fruit and flowers, creating the cornucopia. In Pindar's version, Achelous trades the horn for Amalthea's horn. Achelous loses the marriage; the cornucopia is created; the horn becomes a symbol of abundance. The story also rationalizes Heracles' diversion of the river.

Alcmaeon's PurificationApollodorus, Bibliotheca; Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War

Classical (5th century BC) and later

Alcmaeon, guilty of matricide, wanders until he comes to the Achelous River. The river-god purifies him and gives him his daughter Callirrhoe in marriage. Achelous also commands him to dedicate the cursed necklace and robe at Delphi. Alcmaeon is freed from the curse and founds a new home on land formed by the river's silt.

Accounts II
The Creation of the Echinades IslandsOvid, Metamorphoses

1st century AD

Five nymphs forget to include Achelous in their sacrifices. In anger, he floods his banks and sweeps them into the sea, where they become the Echinades Islands. The islands are formed; the nymphs are transformed.

Perimele and the IslandOvid, Metamorphoses

1st century AD

Achelous rapes Perimele, daughter of Hippodamas. Her father throws her off a cliff, but Achelous prays to Poseidon, who turns her into an island. Perimele becomes an island; Achelous's act is partly mitigated by saving her.

Father of the SirensLycophron, Alexandra; Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica; Ovid, Metamorphoses

Hellenistic (3rd century BC) and later

Achelous is the father of the Sirens, with various mothers: a Muse (unnamed), Terpsichore, Melpomene, or Sterope. In some versions, the Sirens are born from his blood after Heracles breaks his horn. The Sirens become dangerous seductresses in later myth.

Achelous is a minor god with little importance. — He was a major river god, the largest in Greece, and his name was used for water in general.

The cornucopia was created by Zeus. — In the most common myth, it is created from Achelous's broken horn.

Achelous is purely a villain in the Heracles story. — He is a suitor, and in some versions, he is treated sympathetically as an unlucky lover.

Achelous is the father of all rivers. — He is a river god, but not the father of all rivers; Oceanus is the father of all river gods.
