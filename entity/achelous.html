Who It Is

Achelous is the foremost river god in Greek mythology, the personification of the largest river in Greece (the Achelous River, forming the boundary between Acarnania and Aetolia). He is the son of Oceanus and Tethys, making him the eldest of the 3,000 Potamoi (river gods). In myth, Achelous is best known for wrestling Heracles for the hand of Deianira — during the fight, Achelous transformed into a serpent and then a bull, but Heracles broke off one of his horns, which became the Cornucopia (Horn of Plenty). He is also the father of the Sirens, the nymph Callirhoe, and Castalia. Achelous was widely worshipped throughout Greece, and oracles existed at his shrines. In art, he is depicted as a bearded man with the horns of a bull, representing the roaring and power of floodwaters. His name was used by the Greeks as a generic term for water itself.

Origins

Achelous is the eldest of the 3,000 Potamoi, sons of Oceanus and Tethys. He was already ancient in Greek consciousness, and some traditions describe him as the primeval water god from whom all rivers descend. His contest with Heracles over Deianira is the central myth.

Attested in Homer's Iliad / Hesiod's Theogony / Ovid's MetamorphosesEra Archaic to Classical (c. 750 BCE onward)

Form

Achelous was characteristically depicted as a man-bull: a bull's (or bison's) head with a human face, bull-horned and bull-eared, with a heavy beaked, bison-like beard, either full-figure or as a torso, head, or votive mask. More rarely, specially by the fifth century BC, he appeared as a bull with a human head (Athens 1859), or with a bearded human upper torso attached to a long serpentine body ending in a fish's tail (an Attic stamnos from Cerveteri attributed to Oltos, British Museum E437, resembles Triton iconography). Early Attic versions (c. 570 BC) depict him as a centaur-like form or a bull with a man's head and beard.

Powers

Shapeshifting (protean transformation) Achelous could change his shape. Sophocles (Women of Trachis) has Deianeira describe him wooing her in three forms: a bull 'in visible form', a 'sheeny and coiled' serpent, and an ox-faced man with a human trunk, from whose thick-shaded beard streams of fountain-water sprayed. Ovid's Metamorphoses has him fight Heracles in his normal (human-ish) shape, then as a snake, then as a bull. This tripartite shape-shifting is the foundational power of the river-god, mapping onto the river's own bull-roar, serpentine course, and human-like banks. Attested (Sophocles; Ovid; Pindar summary)

The bull-form and the 'horns' of the river By ancient etymology the Achelous was called 'like a bull' from the roaring of its waters and 'from the bendings of its streams, which were called Horns' (Strabo). Achelous's bull-form and his single great horn were central to his myth and cult. The horn, when broken off by Heracles and filled with fruit by the Naiads/the Hesperides, became the Cornucopia (Horn of Plenty), the enduring ancient symbol of agricultural abundance. Attested (Homer-linked tradition; Strabo; Ovid; Hyginus)

Raging flood & control of river-waters Achelous could overflow his banks in a devastating flood. In the Echinades myth he 'tore forests from forests, fields from fields' and swept away to the sea the local nymphs who had forgotten to honour him (Ovid's Metamorphoses, narrated by Achelous himself). He was invoked against fire: Euripides has characters 'bring Achelous' (water) to douse a fire, and Aristophanes' Lysistrata chorus-leader, pouring water, cries 'Achelous, you're on!' Attested (Ovid; Euripides; Aristophanes)

Procreation of rivers, springs, and waters As the father of the Sirens and of spring-nymphs (Pirene, Castalia, Dirce), and — in one tradition — as the ultimate source of water itself, Achelous generated the waters of the world. The Derveni Papyrus calls the rivers the 'sinews of Achelous', and the Oxyrhynchus papyrus reports verses equating Achelous with Oceanus as the source of 'the whole sea'. Ephorus records the notion that by 'Achelous' the oracle at Dodona meant 'water' in general. Attested with variation (Hesiod frames Achelous as a river-god son of Oceanus; but papyrological traditions grant him primacy as water's source)

Creation of islands (Perimele; Echinades) Beside the destructive flood, Achelous could create new land. He asked Poseidon to save his lover Perimele (thrown into the sea by her father Hippodamas), and Poseidon turned her into an island. The Echinades Islands were explained as the land he split apart by his flood. Rationalising geographers (Strabo, Diodorus) identified this creative power with the actual silting up of the river delta, which created new arable land — the literal 'horn of plenty'. Attested (Ovid; rationalising accounts in Strabo and Diodorus)

Purification (katharsis) of a murderer Achelous purified the Argive hero Alcmaeon (who had killed his own mother Eriphyle) and gave him his daughter Callirrhoe as wife, after which Alcmaeon settled on newly-silted land at the river's mouth and was freed of his curse. This is a chthonic, purificatory function typical of river gods. Attested (Apollodorus; Thucydides)

Formerly displayed at a Kansas City department store, it is now in the Smithsonian American Art Museum; it was Benton's first mural on a river-related theme. Surface features on Ganymede are named after Ganymede/Troy-associated Greek or Ancient Near Eastern mythic figures. A way to hold it in your mind

Before there was a Horn of Plenty, there was a horn — a bull's horn torn off a bearded, bison-faced river-god in a wrestling match with Greece's greatest hero, the prize a princess who prayed she might die rather than be wed to the shape-shifter who wanted her. Achelous is the most Greek of deities in the most Greek of ways: immensely old, protean as the water he embodied, mighty enough that Homer used him to measure the strength of Zeus himself — and yet, in the end, a defeated suitor whose one severed horn became the world's enduring symbol of abundance. He is the only river-god the whole Greek world ever agreed to worship.
