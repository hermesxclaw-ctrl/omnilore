The Approach

In the folklore of Europe, the hag is a figure of profound ambiguity: a withered crone who may be a malevolent witch, a wise woman, or a shape-shifting goddess. She appears at the threshold between the human and the supernatural, often as a test or a threat. Her image has haunted fairy tales, myths, and modern horror, yet she is never simply evil—she is a mirror of cultural fears and reverence for the power of old age and the wild.

Content advisory. Depictions of hags may include themes of violence, cannibalism, and supernatural malevolence. Some portrayals reflect historical misogyny and ageism. Readers should approach with cultural sensitivity.

Etymology

The word 'hag' derives from Middle English 'hagge,' from Old English 'hægtesse,' meaning 'witch' or 'fury.' Cognates include Old High German 'hagazissa' and Dutch 'hagedis' (lizard). The root may relate to 'hedge,' suggesting a woman who dwells on the boundaries of civilization.

Cultural Ownership

Primarily European, with significant presence in Celtic (Irish, Scottish, Welsh), Germanic, Slavic, and Scandinavian traditions. Also found in Baltic and Finno-Ugric folklore.

Disputed Identifications

The hag is often conflated with witches, fairies, and goddesses. Some scholars argue that figures like the Cailleach are pre-Celtic deities, while others see them as literary inventions. The relationship between hags and specific goddesses (e.g., the Morrígan) is debated.

Description

Appearance

Typically depicted as an old, wizened woman with wrinkled skin, grey or wild hair, and a hunched posture. She may have missing teeth, a hooked nose, and wearing tattered clothing. In some traditions, she is a giantess or has supernatural features like blue skin (Cailleach Bheur) or iron teeth (Grýla).

Symbols & Iconography

Common symbols include the crone, the moon, the cauldron, the broom, and the threshold or crossroads. In Celtic art, hags may appear as birds (e.g., crows or ravens) associated with battle and prophecy. Sheela-na-gigs are architectural symbols possibly linked to hag worship.

Behaviour & Personality

Hags are often malevolent, preying on children or travelers, but they can also be wise, helpful, or guardians of nature. They may be shapeshifters, appearing as beautiful women to deceive. In some tales, they are tricksters who test heroes.

Domains & Habitats

Hags inhabit wild or liminal places: forests, mountains, caves, bogs, and the borders of human settlements. They are associated with winter, storms, and the elements. Baba Yaga lives in a hut on chicken legs in the deep forest; the Cailleach controls the weather.

Version Differences

Celtic hags (Cailleach, Badb) are often divine or semi-divine, tied to sovereignty and war. Germanic hags (e.g., Frau Holle) can be both benevolent and punishing. Slavic Baba Yaga is more clearly a witch, but with ambiguous morality. Scandinavian Grýla is a child-eating giantess. Modern depictions often simplify hags into evil witches.

Powers & Abilities

Shapeshifting

Source Version Celtic mythology (Morrígan, Badb)

Description

Hags can change form, often from old crone to young woman or animal. The Morrígan transforms into a crow, eel, and wolf.

Limitations

May be constrained by magical rules, e.g., must return to a certain form at certain times.

Contradictions

Some traditions emphasize fixed forms, while others allow free shapeshifting.

Weather Control

Source Version Scottish and Irish folklore

Description

The Cailleach is said to create storms, control snow, and shape the landscape by dropping rocks.

Limitations

Often seasonal, associated with winter.

Contradictions

Some stories attribute weather control to other deities, not specifically hags.

Cursing and Blessing

Source Version Various

Description

Hags can bestow curses or blessings, often related to sovereignty or fate. They may prophesy or grant gifts.

Limitations

Curses may be broken by specific actions or items.

Contradictions

The moral alignment of these powers varies widely.

Cannibalism and Child Theft

Source Version Germanic and Scandinavian folklore

Description

Many hags are child-eaters or steal children (e.g., Hansel and Gretel, Grýla).

Limitations

Often foiled by clever children.

Contradictions

Some hags are protectors of children instead.

Wisdom and Prophecy

Source Version Various, e.g., Celtic sovereignty figures

Description

Hags may possess deep knowledge, including healing and prophecy. They may act as seers.

Limitations

May require payment or a test.

Contradictions

Some traditions deny hags any positive traits.

Stories & Myths

The Morrígan and Cú Chulainn

Source Táin Bó CúailngeCulture IrishPeriod Early medieval (oral tradition earlier)

Summary

The Morrígan, a goddess of war, appears to Cú Chulainn as a hag milking a cow. She offers him a drink; he refuses, and she threatens to attack him. Later, she appears as an eel, a wolf, and a heifer in his battle, and he wounds her. She vows to curse him, and eventually he meets his doom.

Participants

Morrígan

Cú Chulainn

Consequences

Cú Chulainn is cursed and eventually dies, fulfilling the prophecy.

Variants

Different versions of the Táin describe the encounter differently; some have the Morrígan as a beautiful woman instead.

The Cailleach Bheur and the Creation of the Landscape

Source Scottish oral tradition, collected in Carmina GadelicaCulture Scottish GaelicPeriod Recorded 19th century, ancient origins

Summary

The Cailleach Bheur, a giantess, carries stones in her apron and drops them to form mountains and islands. She controls winter and is reborn each year at Samhain.

Participants

Cailleach Bheur

Consequences

The landscape is shaped; the seasons are established.

Variants

In some versions, she is a goddess of creation; in others, a hostile force.

Baba Yaga and Vasilisa

Source Russian fairy tale, collected by Alexander AfanasyevCulture RussianPeriod 19th century (oral tradition earlier)

Summary

Vasilisa is sent to Baba Yaga's hut to get fire. Baba Yaga sets her tasks, which she completes with the help of a magical doll. Baba Yaga gives her a skull with fire, which burns her cruel stepmother and stepsisters.

Participants

Vasilisa

Baba Yaga

Consequences

Vasilisa escapes and gains a better life; Baba Yaga remains a fearsome figure.

Variants

Baba Yaga appears in many tales with different roles, sometimes helper, sometimes villain.

Hansel and Gretel

Source Brothers Grimm, 'Kinder- und Hausmärchen'Culture GermanPeriod 19th century (oral tradition earlier)

Summary

Two children are abandoned in the forest and find a house made of candy. A hag lives there and plans to eat them. They outwit her and push her into the oven.

Participants

Variants

Cannibalism

Source Grimm, Icelandic sagas

Description

Hags in many tales eat children (e.g., Hansel and Gretel, Grýla).

Scholarly Context

This may reflect fears of famine and abandonment, or serve as a warning to children.

Misogyny and Ageism

Source Various

Description

Hags are often depicted as repulsive old women, reinforcing negative stereotypes of aging women.

Scholarly Context

Scholars like Marina Warner have analyzed the hag as a symbol of male fear of female power and aging.

Violence

Source Various

Description

Hags may be tortured or killed in tales, sometimes with graphic detail.

Scholarly Context

The violence is often justified as self-defense by protagonists.

Contradictions & Disputes

Divine vs. mortal

Position A

Hags are divine beings, goddesses in disguise.

Position A — evidence

Celtic figures like the Morrígan and Cailleach are clearly deities.

Position B

Hags are mortal witches or fairies.

Position B — evidence

In many fairy tales, hags are just old women with magic.

Scholarly Consensus

The hag is a liminal figure, often divine in older traditions, but degraded to a witch in later folklore.

Malevolent vs. benevolent

Position A

Hags are evil, child-eating monsters.

Position A — evidence

Grimm tales, Grýla.

Position B

Hags can be helpful, wise, or protective.

Position B — evidence

Baba Yaga sometimes helps, Cailleach is a creator.

Scholarly Consensus

Hags are ambiguous, reflecting the unpredictability of nature and fate.

Origin of the hag figure

Position A

Paleolithic goddess worship.

Position A — evidence

Sheela-na-gigs and prehistoric figurines.

Position B

Medieval misogynistic invention.

Position B — evidence

Lack of direct textual evidence for ancient hag worship.

Scholarly Consensus

The hag likely has ancient roots but was shaped by later cultural attitudes.
