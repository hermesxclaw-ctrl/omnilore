Who It Is

In the folklore of Europe, the hag is a figure of profound ambiguity: a withered crone who may be a malevolent witch, a wise woman, or a shape-shifting goddess. She appears at the threshold between the human and the supernatural, often as a test or a threat. Her image has haunted fairy tales, myths, and modern horror, yet she is never simply evil—she is a mirror of cultural fears and reverence for the power of old age and the wild.

Hags are often malevolent, preying on children or travelers, but they can also be wise, helpful, or guardians of nature. They may be shapeshifters, appearing as beautiful women to deceive. In some tales, they are tricksters who test heroes.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The word 'hag' derives from Middle English 'hagge,' from Old English 'hægtesse,' meaning 'witch' or 'fury.' Cognates include Old High German 'hagazissa' and Dutch 'hagedis' (lizard). The root may relate to 'hedge,' suggesting a woman who dwells on the boundaries of civilization.

Pronunciation — /hæɡ/

Aliases — Cailleach, Cailleach Bheur, Badb, Morrígan, Baba Yaga, Gyhldeptis

Ownership — Primarily European, with significant presence in Celtic (Irish, Scottish, Welsh), Germanic, Slavic, and Scandinavian traditions. Also found in Baltic and Finno-Ugric folklore.

Powers
Shapeshifting

Hags can change form, often from old crone to young woman or animal. The Morrígan transforms into a crow, eel, and wolf.

Limit: May be constrained by magical rules, e.g., must return to a certain form at certain times.

Weather Control

The Cailleach is said to create storms, control snow, and shape the landscape by dropping rocks.

Limit: Often seasonal, associated with winter.

Cursing and Blessing

Hags can bestow curses or blessings, often related to sovereignty or fate. They may prophesy or grant gifts.

Limit: Curses may be broken by specific actions or items.

Cannibalism and Child Theft

Many hags are child-eaters or steal children (e.g., Hansel and Gretel, Grýla).

Limit: Often foiled by clever children.

Wisdom and Prophecy

Hags may possess deep knowledge, including healing and prophecy. They may act as seers.

Limit: May require payment or a test.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Typically depicted as an old, wizened woman with wrinkled skin, grey or wild hair, and a hunched posture. She may have missing teeth, a hooked nose, and wearing tattered clothing. In some traditions, she is a giantess or has supernatural features like blue skin (Cailleach Bheur) or iron teeth (Grýla).

Symbols — Common symbols include the crone, the moon, the cauldron, the broom, and the threshold or crossroads. In Celtic art, hags may appear as birds (e.g., crows or ravens) associated with battle and prophecy. Sheela-na-gigs are architectural symbols possibly linked to hag worship.

Domains — Hags inhabit wild or liminal places: forests, mountains, caves, bogs, and the borders of human settlements. They are associated with winter, storms, and the elements. Baba Yaga lives in a hut on chicken legs in the deep forest; the Cailleach controls the weather.

Accounts I
The Morrígan and Cú ChulainnTáin Bó Cúailnge

Early medieval (oral tradition earlier)

The Morrígan, a goddess of war, appears to Cú Chulainn as a hag milking a cow. She offers him a drink; he refuses, and she threatens to attack him. Later, she appears as an eel, a wolf, and a heifer in his battle, and he wounds her. She vows to curse him, and eventually he meets his doom. Cú Chulainn is cursed and eventually dies, fulfilling the prophecy.

The Cailleach Bheur and the Creation of the LandscapeScottish oral tradition, collected in Carmina Gadelica

Recorded 19th century, ancient origins

The Cailleach Bheur, a giantess, carries stones in her apron and drops them to form mountains and islands. She controls winter and is reborn each year at Samhain. The landscape is shaped; the seasons are established.

Accounts II
Baba Yaga and VasilisaRussian fairy tale, collected by Alexander Afanasyev

19th century (oral tradition earlier)

Vasilisa is sent to Baba Yaga's hut to get fire. Baba Yaga sets her tasks, which she completes with the help of a magical doll. Baba Yaga gives her a skull with fire, which burns her cruel stepmother and stepsisters. Vasilisa escapes and gains a better life; Baba Yaga remains a fearsome figure.

Hansel and GretelBrothers Grimm, 'Kinder- und Hausmärchen'

19th century (oral tradition earlier)

Two children are abandoned in the forest and find a house made of candy. A hag lives there and plans to eat them. They outwit her and push her into the oven. The children escape with treasure and return home.
