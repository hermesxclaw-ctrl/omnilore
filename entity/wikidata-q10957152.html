Who It Is

In the marshes of Sanglin, something the size of a hill moved through the reeds, bristling and tusking, rooting up fields and men alike — the Chinese texts call it Fengxi, the Mound Boar, one of the half-dozen blights that only the divine archer Yi could end.

Behavior is attested as predatory and ravaging, not cunning. Texts say it 'was captured/killed at Sanglin' (禽封豨於桑林) after ravaging the area. Bowu Zhi and later glosses add that it 'ate humans, destroyed villages, and ruined fields.' No speech, no motive beyond bestial hunger, no moral agency — it is a hazard, not a villain with personality. Unlike later boar demons (e.g., Zhu Bajie), Fengxi shows no shapeshifting, no lust, no humor, no repentance. Its behavior is to inhabit marsh/wetland and emerge to devastate. Powers
Mound-like Bulk and Strength

Described as 'big as a hill' in later glosses; capable of trampling fields, breaking dikes, and uprooting trees. Its mass is its primary threat.

Limit: No flight, no shapeshifting, no projectile attack attested. Vulnerable to archery; Yi is able to 'capture/kill' (禽) it, indicating it can be felled by a superior archer. No regeneration attested.

Ravaging / Crop and Village Destruction

Habitat-scale devastation: destroys mulberry groves, grain fields, and settlements around Sanglin. Functions as an agricultural plague.

Limit: Range limited to Sanglin marsh and surrounds; does not threaten entire realm, only local area until Yao intervenes.

Man-Eating Predation

Explicitly a man-eater (食人) in encyclopedic glosses; preys on humans and livestock.

Limit: Ambush/pursuit predator only; no luring or deception. Cannot pursue beyond its marsh domain once Yi confronts it.

Tusks and Goring Charge

As a giant boar, its tusks are its weapon; later illustrations show a charging gore attack.

Limit: Melee only; no venom, no elemental breath. Countered by distance archery, which is how Yi defeats it.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Attested description is minimal and formulaic. Huainanzi and parallels give no detailed physiognomy beyond the name: a boar of monstrous, mound-like size (feng). Later encyclopedias (Bowu Zhi, Taiping Yulan) elaborate: a wild boar (xi/shi) 'as large as a hill' (大如丘陵), with coarse bristles, long tusks, and a snout capable of uprooting fields. No attested color, no anthropomorphic features, no clothing or regalia — it is a beast, not a shapeshifter

Symbols — Symbolically, Fengxi belongs to the 'six harms' (liu hai) or 'ten evils' eliminated by Yi under Emperor Yao — emblems of untamed wilderness and disorder. The boar embodies agricultural blight: it tramples dikes, devours crops, and eats people and livestock, threatening the agrarian order Yao represents. In iconography, it is rarely depicted alone; when shown, it appears in the Hou Yi cycle alongside Zaochi (Chisel-Tooth), Jiuy

Domains — Attested habitat: Sanglin (桑林, Mulberry Grove/Forest), a marshy or forested wetland. Huainanzi locates the capture specifically 'at Sanglin.' Later commentators place Sanglin in the Central Plains, sometimes identified with a site in modern Henan (variously near present-day Shangqiu or in the ancient Song region), but the location is literary rather than geographic and should be treated as mythic topography. Accounts I
Yao Commands Yi to Eliminate the Six Harms — Fengxi at SanglinHuainanzi, Benjing Xun 8

c. 139 BCE, purporting to describe the era of Emperor Yao (mythic antiquity)

In high antiquity, the world is plagued by prodigies. Emperor Yao, the sage king, orders the archer Yi (Hou Yi) to rid the realm of harms. Yi shoots Zaochi (Chisel-Tooth) in the wilds of Chouhua, kills Jiuying (Nine Infant) on the Xiong Waters, nets Dafeng (Great Wind) at the marsh of Qingqiu, shoots the ten suns in the sky and kills Yayu below, severs Xiushe (Long Serpent) at Lake Dongting, and 'captures Fengxi at Sanglin' (禽封豨於桑林). The sequence presents Fengxi as the final terrestrial beast in a systematic pacification of the world, allowing agriculture and settlement to resume. Removal of Fengxi restores order to Sanglin; Yao's virtue is demonstrated; Yi's status as culture hero is established. The episode becomes the canonical list of Yi's labors, analogous to Heracles in comparative discussions.

The Bowu Zhi Elaboration — Giant Boar Ravaging the MarshBowu Zhi (博物志) by Zhang Hua, preserved via Taiping Yulan 882

c. 290 CE, citing earlier Han lore

Zhang Hua expands the Huainanzi notice: In the marshes of Sanglin there was a great boar, its body like a hill, its bristles like reeds. It emerged to devour people and trample grain, and none dared approach. Yao heard of it and sent Yi, who shot it with bow and arrow. The text adds natural-history gloss, treating Fengxi as a prodigious but natural beast magnified to monstrous scale, consistent with Bowu Zhi's interest in anomalies (zhi guai). Provides the first descriptive expansion beyond the name; establishes the 'hill-sized boar' image that dominates later illustration. Cements Fengxi in the zhi guai (records of anomalies) genre.

Guo Pu's Exegetical Note — Defining the XiGuo Pu commentary to Shanhaijing and Erya

c. 310-324 CE

Commenting on the graph 豨, Guo Pu glosses: 'Xi is a wild boar; feng means big.' He links the term to the Huainanzi passage, explaining that Fengxi is not a separate species but a large wild boar that became a harm. He does not narrate a new story but anchors the myth in lexicography, ensuring later readers understand Fengxi as a zoological exaggeration rather than a composite monster. Standardizes the reading 'Great Boar' for all later leishu; prevents conflation with composite beasts like Jiuying. Influences all Song encyclopedias.

Accounts II
Song Encyclopedic Preservation — Fengxi in the Imperial CanonTaiping Yulan (太平御覽) 882 and Taiping Guangji (太平廣記) 427

977-983 CE, compiling Tang and earlier lost sources

The Song editors, under Li Fang, copy the Huainanzi-Bowu Zhi passage verbatim under the category of beasts (shou bu) and anomalies. Fengxi is listed among other Yi victims as proof of ancient prodigies. No new narrative is invented; the act is one of preservation, showing that by the Song, Fengxi was already an antiquarian reference rather than a living folk belief. Ensures survival of the Fengxi notice after loss of many Jin texts; becomes the source for Ming-Qing collectanea and modern dictionaries like Cihai.

Modern Rationalization and Children's Retelling — Hou Yi Shoots the BoarYuan Ke (袁珂) Zhongguo Gudai Shenhua (1950; revised Zhongguo Shenhua Chuanshuo 1984) and contemporary picture books e.g., Shanghai People's Fine Arts Publishing House 'Hou Yi She Ri' series (1979-present)

1950-present

Yuan Ke retells the Huainanzi cycle in modern vernacular, describing Fengxi as a 'hill-like wild boar that lived in the mulberry marsh, eating farmers and livestock, until Hou Yi tracked it and shot it dead with a great bow.' Picture books illustrate the scene: Yi kneeling with bow, giant black boar bursting from reeds, villagers hiding. The retelling adds visual drama and dialogue absent in Han sources ('The boar is here! Run!') but retains the core plot. Scholarly editions note that such giant boars may reflect Neolithic encounters with large Sus scrofa in the Yellow River wetlands. Introduces Fengxi to mass audiences after centuries of obscurity; fixes the popular image of a giant marsh boar. Scholarly rationalization separates mythic exaggeration from ecological memory.

Folk Etymology of Fengxi Place Names (Negative Case)Local gazetteers (difangzhi) for Chao'an, Zhuxi, Mingxi, Pucheng, Lusong, Guangfeng; modern toponymic studies

Ming-Qing gazetteers to present

None of the seven modern Fengxi places (枫溪/丰溪) claim descent from the boar myth; their names derive from 'maple ravine' (枫溪) or 'abundant ravine' (丰溪), describing local hydrology and flora. No gazetteer links the towns to the Huainanzi beast; the homophony is coincidental. This 'non-story' is included to prevent false etiology — a common internet claim that the towns are named after the monster is unattested. Clarifies that toponym and myth are homonyms with different characters and histories; prevents conflation in databases like Wikidata.

Fengxi is the same as Zhu Bajie (Pigsy) from Journey to the West, or its ancestor. — No pre-Ming text links them. Fengxi is a mute, hill-sized man-eating boar slain by Yi in antiquity; Zhu Bajie is a Ming literary creation — a former Marshal Tianpeng, anthropomorphic, comic, lustful, and ultimately redeemed as a Buddhist pilgrim. They share only the species (pig/boar) and Chinese cultural background.

The seven modern places named Fengxi (枫溪/丰溪) are named after the monster. — The places are named 枫溪 'Maple Ravine' or 丰溪 'Abundant Ravine,' describing local geography and flora. Their characters differ from the beast's 封豨/封豕. No gazetteer or toponymic study links them to the myth; the homophony in Mandarin is coincidental.

Fengxi could breathe fire, cause earthquakes, or had magical invulnerability. — No Han or Jin source attributes elemental powers or invulnerability. Its only attested traits are enormous size, strength, and man-eating. Fire/earthquake/poison abilities are inventions of modern games, fantasy bestiaries, and AI-generated summaries that merge it with other harms like Jiuying (which does have fire/water associations).

Fengxi appears in the Classic of Mountains and Seas (Shanhaijing) as a main entry. — The received Shanhaijing text does not contain a Fengxi entry. The name appears only in Guo Pu's commentary and later encyclopedias citing Huainanzi. Searchable Shanhaijing concordances show no 封豨 in the main text.
