Enlil, the great mountain, the king of the gods, whose wind brings life and whose wrath brings the flood.

Who They Are

Enlil is the god of wind, storms, and breath — the supreme king of the gods in the Sumerian pantheon (before Marduk's rise). He is the son of An (Heaven) and Ki (Earth), and he separates them, creating the space for human existence. Enlil dwells in the Ekur temple at Nippur, the religious center of Sumer. He holds the tablet of destinies and the royal scepter, granting kingship to earthly rulers. Enlil is a complex figure: he is the beneficent giver of agricultural prosperity (through the fertility brought by winds and rains), but also the destroyer who sends the Great Flood to wipe out humanity (in the Atrahasis Epic) because their noise disturbs his sleep. He is responsible for the confusion of languages (the myth of Enmerkar). His temper is legendary — he is quick to anger and slow to relent. Enlil's sacred number is 50, and his name means 'Lord Wind' or 'Lord Air.' He represents the raw, untamed power of nature that can nurture or annihilate. The Greeks identified him with Cronus.

Attested in Sumerian Temple Hymns / Atrahasis Epic / Gilgamesh EpicEra Sumerian to Neo-Babylonian (c. 3000-500 BCE)

Form

Enlil is never described in anthropomorphic detail in surviving texts. He is generally understood as a bearded, robed figure wearing a horned crown (the standard Mesopotamian divine headdress). His epithet 'Great Mountain' suggests a connection to the cosmic mountain, and he is sometimes symbolized by a horned cap or a sacred tree. No definitive cult statue of Enlil survives; the most famous was destroyed or lost. His presence is often indicated by the number 50 (his sacred number) and by the ram (his sacred animal).

Powers

Supreme Authority (Kingship) Enlil holds the 'enlilship' (lil-lu), the supreme divine authority that grants kingship to mortal rulers. He decrees the fates (nam-tar) of nations and individuals. No king could rule without his blessing. Sumerian and Akkadian His decrees are final and cannot be revoked, even by himself (as in the Flood myth, where he is bound by his own oath). In later Babylonian theology, Marduk is said to have received this authority from Enlil, effectively superseding him.

Control of Wind and Storm As 'Lord Wind,' Enlil commands the storm winds, the flood, and the tempest. He uses these powers to punish humanity and to enforce his will. Sumerian, Akkadian His powers are destructive but not creative; he is not a creator god in the sense of Enki/Ea. In some texts, Enlil's storm powers are shared with or delegated to his son Ninurta and his servant Ishkur/Adad.

Creator of Humanity (in some traditions) In some Sumerian texts, Enlil is credited with creating humanity (the 'black-headed people') by planting a 'seed' in the earth. However, the more common tradition credits Enki with creating humans from clay. Sumerian (some texts) Not a universal tradition; Enki is more commonly the creator. The Atrahasis Epic credits Enki with creation, while Enlil is the one who attempts to destroy humanity.

Holder of the Tablet of Destinies Enlil holds the Tablet of Destinies (ṭuppi šīmāti), which grants him the power to decree the fates of all things. This tablet is later taken by the monster Anzu (in the Anzu myth) and recovered by Ninurta. Akkadian (Anzu myth) The tablet can be stolen, as in the Anzu myth, demonstrating a vulnerability. In later Babylonian texts, Marduk holds the Tablet of Destinies.

Divine Word as Creative Power Enlil's word (inim) is a creative force. When he speaks, his decree is immediately realized. This is distinct from Enki's more intellectual cunning; Enlil's power is direct and absolute. Sumerian His word is binding, even on himself. None known.

Behavior & Habitat

Enlil is depicted as the ultimate authority figure: decisive, sometimes harsh, capable of great wrath but also of mercy. He is called 'the father of the gods' and 'the creator of the black-headed people' (humanity). He is portrayed as a king who delegates power but retains final authority. In myth, he is often aloof, communicating through dreams or intermediaries. His anger is terrifying (he sends floods, plagues, and destruction), but he can also be appeased and bless individuals (as with Utnapishtim). He is not capricious like Zeus; his decrees are final and often inscrutable.

Accounts

Ninlil, a young goddess, is bathing in the river. Enlil, seeing her, rapes her. His fellow gods, the Annunaki, are furious and arrest him, banishing him to the underworld. Ninlil follows him, but Enlil, in disguise, impregnates her three more times, fathering the gods Nergal (or Meslamtaea), Ninazu, and Enbilulu. The myth explains the birth of the moon god Nanna (the first child) and other deities, and establishes a pattern of divine transgression and punishment.

Humanity has become too noisy, disturbing Enlil's sleep. He decides to destroy them with plague, famine, and finally a great flood. Enki, his rival, secretly warns the sage Atrahasis (or Utnapishtim in Gilgamesh), who builds a boat and survives. After the flood, Enlil is furious but is persuaded by Enki to bless the survivor and establish a new order, creating a permanent separation between gods and humans.

The lion-headed bird Anzu steals the Tablet of Destinies from Enlil, gaining supreme power. The gods are paralyzed with fear. Enlil's son Ninurta is dispatched to fight Anzu, eventually defeating him and recovering the tablet, restoring order.

Enlil, in his wrath, decrees the destruction of the city of Ur. The gods mourn, but his decree is final. The lament describes the devastation and the loss of the city's patron goddess Ningal.

Enlil, separating heaven and earth, creates the pickaxe (al) to break the hard earth, allowing agriculture and civilization to flourish. He gives the tool to humanity, establishing the foundation of Mesopotamian society.

Scholarly Debates
Enlil's role as creator vs. Enki's role
Enlil is the creator of humanity (in some Sumerian texts). ⇌ Enki is the creator of humanity (in the Atrahasis Epic and other Akkadian texts).
Evidence for A: Sumerian texts like 'Enlil and Ninlil' and 'The Creation of the Pickaxe' attribute creation to Enlil.
Evidence for B: Akkadian texts like Atrahasis and the Epic of Gilgamesh attribute creation to Enki.
Enlil's authority vs. Marduk's authority
Enlil is the supreme god, holding the Tablet of Destinies. ⇌ Marduk is the supreme god, having taken the Tablet of Destinies and the 'enlilship' from Enlil.
Evidence for A: Sumerian and early Akkadian texts consistently place Enlil at the head of the pantheon.
Evidence for B: The Enuma Elish (c. 1100 BCE) describes Marduk's rise to supremacy, with Enlil's authority transferred to him.
Enlil's character: benevolent father vs. wrathful tyrant
Enlil is a benevolent creator and father, who blesses humanity and grants kingship. ⇌ Enlil is a wrathful, destructive god who sends floods and plagues to punish humanity.
Evidence for A: Hymns and prayers to Enlil emphasize his mercy and his role as a protector.
Evidence for B: The Flood myth and the Lamentations emphasize his destructive wrath.
What We Don't Know

There is no surviving visual depiction of Enlil that is definitively identified. The exact nature of his cult at Nippur is only partially understood. The relationship between the Sumerian Enlil and the Akkadian Ellil is not fully clear, nor is the extent to which his worship was 'personal' vs. 'state' religion. The influence of Enlil on later Jewish and Christian angelology and demonology is a subject of ongoing research.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Enlil as the CEO of a cosmic corporation. He doesn't do the hands-on work (that's Enki, the engineer), but he holds the authority, the budget, and the power to fire everyone. He's the one who signs the decree that ends the project (humanity, in the Flood myth). He's not evil — he's just the one who has to make the hard calls, and he doesn't like being kept awake at night by the noise of his employees. In the pantheon, he's the executive who delegates to specialists (Ninurta for security, Nanna for logistics) but keeps the final say. And when he makes a mistake (like the Flood), he has to live with the consequences, even if it means granting immortality to the one survivor who outsmarted him.
