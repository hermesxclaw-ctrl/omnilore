Form

No distinct physical description survives for any of the figures named Argeia. As a nymph, she would be imagined as a beautiful, youthful maiden. As a mortal princess, she would be depicted in the style of a noble Argive woman, likely in a peplos. In art, she is only identifiable through inscription or context, as she has no unique attributes.

Powers

Immortality (Oceanid version) As an Oceanid, Argeia is an immortal nymph, a daughter of the primordial gods Oceanus and Tethys. She does not age or die. Hesiod's Theogony, Pseudo-Apollodorus Her power is not active; it is an attribute of her nature as a minor goddess. None. This is the standard nature of Oceanids.

Mortality (Princess version) As the daughter of Adrastus, she is a mortal woman, subject to the dangers and tragedies of human life. Pseudo-Apollodorus, Hyginus She has no supernatural powers. The conflation of these two figures creates a contradiction, but they are distinct in the primary sources.

No Active Powers In no version does Argeia possess any active magical or martial powers. Her significance is genealogical, symbolic, and ethical, not supernatural. All versions N/A N/A

Behavior & Habitat

The behavior of the mortal Argeia is defined by her loyalty and piety. Her most famous act is her defiance of King Creon's edict forbidding the burial of her husband, Polyneices. She is portrayed as a devoted wife who risks her life to perform the sacred rites for her fallen husband.

Accounts

Argeia, the Oceanid, marries Polybus, the son of Agenor, and king of Sicyon. Their son is Argus Panoptes, the hundred-eyed giant who serves Hera as the guardian of Io.

Argeia, the daughter of King Adrastus of Argos, marries Polyneices, the exiled prince of Thebes. They have a son, Thersander. When Polyneices marches against Thebes as one of the Seven, he is killed in single combat with his brother, Eteocles. King Creon of Thebes decrees that Polyneices' body must remain unburied. Argeia, along with her sister-in-law Antigone, defies the edict and performs funeral rites for her husband's corpse.

In a less common tradition, Argeia is the daughter of Zeus and an Oceanid (possibly Harmonia). She marries Inachus, the river god, and becomes the mother of Phoroneus or Io. She gives her name to the city of Argos, serving as an aetiological figure.

Content Notes

The central story involving the mortal Argeia is set against the backdrop of a brutal civil war. Her husband's body is left to rot and be devoured by animals, a profound dishonor in ancient Greek culture. Her act of burying him is a direct defiance of the king's law, carrying the risk of death. This story deals with themes of state power vs. religious and familial duty.

The Theban cycle is a foundational exploration of the conflict between human law and divine or familial obligation. The unburied corpse is a central symbol of this conflict.

Sophocles, Antigone; Statius, Thebaid

Scholarly Debates
Identity of Argeia
Argeia is a primordial Oceanid nymph, daughter of Oceanus and Tethys. ⇌ Argeia is a mortal princess of Argos, daughter of King Adrastus.
Evidence for A: Hesiod's Theogony (c. 700 BCE) lists her among the Oceanids. Pseudo-Apollodorus names her as the mother of Argus Panopte
Evidence for B: Pseudo-Apollodorus and Hyginus name her as the wife of Polyneices and mother of Thersander.
Mother of Argus Panoptes
Argeia, daughter of Oceanus and Tethys, is his mother. ⇌ Ismene, daughter of the river god Asopus, is his mother.
Evidence for A: Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.1.2.
Evidence for B: Various scholia and local traditions.
Role in the Burial of Polyneices
Argeia is the primary agent of her husband's burial in some Roman sources (Statius). ⇌ Antigone is the primary agent in the Athenian tradition (Sophocles).
Evidence for A: Statius, Thebaid, Book 12.
Evidence for B: Sophocles, Antigone.
What We Don't Know

The primary gap is the lack of any archaeological or epigraphic evidence for a cult dedicated to a figure named Argeia. The evidence is entirely literary. The existence of a third, 'eponymous' Argeia is poorly attested and requires further investigation of scholia. The visual representation of Argeia in ancient art is unconfirmed, as she is rarely, if ever, labeled. The distinction between the Oceanid and the Princess is clear in the major sources but requires a nuanced reading of the genealogical traditions.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Argeia as a name that gets reused across different stories, like 'Alex' or 'Sam' in a modern setting. One is a background character in a cosmic family saga (the Oceanid). The other is a supporting character in a tragic war drama (the princess). They share a name and a hometown, but they are not the same person. Her story is a testament to the power of a name — and the power of a vow, as she chooses to honor her husband even when the state forbids it.
