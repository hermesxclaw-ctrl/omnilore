Who It Is

In the sprawling family tree of Greek myth, where gods beget monsters and kings beget tragedies, Argeia is a name that echoes in two distinct chambers. One is the quiet, primordial hall of the Oceanids, where she is a footnote in the birth of a hundred-eyed giant. The other is the blood-soaked battlefield of Thebes, where she is a princess who risks her life to defy a king's decree and give her fallen husband his final honor. She is not a goddess of power, but a figure of duty — a woman whose name simply means 'from Argos,' and who embodies the loyalty and grief that define the mortal experience.

The behavior of the mortal Argeia is defined by her loyalty and piety. Her most famous act is her defiance of King Creon's edict forbidding the burial of her husband, Polyneices. She is portrayed as a devoted wife who risks her life to perform the sacred rites for her fallen husband.

Powers
Immortality (Oceanid version)

As an Oceanid, Argeia is an immortal nymph, a daughter of the primordial gods Oceanus and Tethys. She does not age or die.

Limit: Her power is not active; it is an attribute of her nature as a minor goddess.

Mortality (Princess version)

As the daughter of Adrastus, she is a mortal woman, subject to the dangers and tragedies of human life.

Limit: She has no supernatural powers.

No Active Powers

In no version does Argeia possess any active magical or martial powers. Her significance is genealogical, symbolic, and ethical, not supernatural.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No distinct physical description survives for any of the figures named Argeia. As a nymph, she would be imagined as a beautiful, youthful maiden. As a mortal princess, she would be depicted in the style of a noble Argive woman, likely in a peplos. In art, she is only identifiable through inscription or context, as she has no unique attributes.

Symbols — There are no unique symbols for Argeia. As an Oceanid, she might be associated with water and a water vessel. As a mortal, she would be associated with the funerary rites of her husband. The name itself is her primary symbol, representing her Argive identity.

Domains — As an Oceanid, her domain is the freshwater of the earth, a specific spring or stream. As a mortal princess, her domain is the royal household of Argos and the tragic sphere of the Theban war. The name 'Argeia' as an epithet for Hera connects her to the city of Argos, a major center of her worship.

Accounts I
The Genealogy of Argus PanoptesPseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.1.2

Mythic (recorded 1st-2nd century CE)

Argeia, the Oceanid, marries Polybus, the son of Agenor, and king of Sicyon. Their son is Argus Panoptes, the hundred-eyed giant who serves Hera as the guardian of Io. This union produces one of the most famous monsters of Greek myth, Argus Panoptes, whose death at the hands of Hermes leads to Io's escape and the founding of the Argive cult of Hera.

Accounts II
The Wife of PolyneicesPseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.6.1; Hyginus, Fabulae 69; Statius, Thebaid

Mythic (recorded 1st century BCE - 2nd century CE)

Argeia, the daughter of King Adrastus of Argos, marries Polyneices, the exiled prince of Thebes. They have a son, Thersander. When Polyneices marches against Thebes as one of the Seven, he is killed in single combat with his brother, Eteocles. King Creon of Thebes decrees that Polyneices' body must remain unburied. Argeia, along with her sister-in-law Antigone, defies the edict and performs funeral rites for her husband's corpse. Her act of defiance solidifies her status as a paragon of wifely piety. It also contributes to the cycle of tragedy and vengeance that plagues the houses of Thebes and Argos. Her son, Thersander, later becomes one of the Epigoni, who successfully sack Thebes.

Late Antiquity

In a less common tradition, Argeia is the daughter of Zeus and an Oceanid (possibly Harmonia). She marries Inachus, the river god, and becomes the mother of Phoroneus or Io. She gives her name to the city of Argos, serving as an aetiological figure. This version provides a divine origin for the city of Argos and its royal line.

Argeia is a single, unified goddess. — The name is an epithet meaning 'Argive woman' and is applied to at least three distinct figures: an Oceanid nymph, a mortal princess, and a daughter of Zeus in an obscure tradition.

Argeia is the same person as Antigone. — They are distinct figures. Antigone is the daughter of Oedipus and the sister of Polyneices. Argeia is his wife. They are sisters-in-law and act together in some versions of the burial myth.

Argeia is a major, powerful goddess. — She is a minor figure. As a nymph, she is a genealogical footnote. As a mortal, she is a tragic character. She has no cult, no temples, and no independent mythology.
