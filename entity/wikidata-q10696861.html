Who It Is

In the heart of ancient Babylon, two young lovers, separated by a wall and a family feud, found a crack through which they whispered their devotion. Their story, a timeless tragedy of miscommunication and devotion, would stain the mulberry fruit forever and echo through millennia, inspiring tales of star-crossed lovers from Ovid to Shakespeare.

Pyramus is impetuous and quick to despair; Thisbe is brave (she flees but returns) and loyal. Both are deeply in love and willing to die for each other.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Pyramus (Πύραμος) is the Greek name of the Ceyhan River in Cilicia (modern Turkey). Thisbe (Θίσβη) may derive from a local spring or nymph. The names are likely pre-Greek in origin.

Pronunciation — PYR-uh-mus and THIZ-bee (English); PEE-rah-mos and THEES-bee (Classical Greek)

Aliases — Pyramus, Thisbe, Píramo y Tisbe (Spanish), Pyrame et Thisbée (French), Piramo e Tisbe (Italian)

Ownership — Primarily Greek and Roman mythology; the story is set in Babylon. It has been adopted into Western literature and art.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In Ovid's account, no detailed physical description is given. They are young, beautiful, and of marriageable age. In later art, they are depicted as classical youths.

Symbols — The mulberry tree (white fruit turning dark), the wall with a crack, the tomb of Ninus, a lioness, a sword, and a cloak are key symbols.

Domains — Babylon, in connected houses with a shared wall; the meeting place is near the tomb of Ninus under a mulberry tree.

Accounts I
Ovid's Pyramus and ThisbeOvid, Metamorphoses 4.55-166

1st century AD

Two Babylonian lovers, forbidden to marry, communicate through a crack in the wall. They plan to meet under a mulberry tree near the tomb of Ninus. Thisbe arrives first but flees when she sees a lioness with a bloody mouth, dropping her cloak. Pyramus finds the cloak, assumes Thisbe is dead, and kills himself. Thisbe returns, finds him dead, and kills herself with the same sword. The gods turn the mulberry fruit dark in honor of their love, and their ashes are placed in one urn. The mulberry fruit becomes dark; the lovers are buried together; the tale becomes a cautionary story about haste and miscommunication.

Accounts II
Cilician aetiological mythMosaic from Nea Paphos, Cyprus; Nicolaus Sophista

Possibly earlier than Ovid; mosaic dated 2nd century AD

Pyramus and Thisbe are transformed into the river Pyramus and a spring, explaining the local geography. Explains the origin of the river and spring.
