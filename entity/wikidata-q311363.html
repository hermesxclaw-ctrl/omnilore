Who It Is

Before the Kaaba was draped in black, before the call to prayer echoed across the desert, there was another power in Mecca. She was called Al-‘Uzzá, 'The Mightiest One,' and the Quraysh, the city's most powerful tribe, were her devoted servants. They invoked her name before battle, sacrificed game at her desert shrine, and believed their very survival depended on her favor. Her story is a tale of a goddess who was worshipped, feared, and ultimately destroyed — a powerful reminder that the gods we create are as mortal as we are.

As a goddess of might and victory, Al-‘Uzzá was likely perceived as a fierce, protective, and martial deity. She was invoked for success in battle, for the protection of caravans, and for general prosperity. The ritual of animal sacrifice (particularly of game) suggests a deity who required appeasement and bestowal of favor.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name is derived from the Arabic root ‘-z-z (عز), meaning 'to be mighty' or 'to be powerful.' Al-‘Uzzá thus translates to 'The Mightiest One' or 'The Most Powerful.' This etymology is widely accepted in academic literature, linking her name to concepts of strength and victory.

Pronunciation — al-‘Uz-zá (with the ‘ain as a pharyngeal fricative, and a glottal stop before the final long 'a').

Aliases — Uzza, Al-Uzza, Ozza, al-'Uzzā

Powers
Granting Victory in Battle

Al-‘Uzzá was invoked by warriors and tribes for success in conflict. Her name, 'The Mightiest One,' directly reflects this martial aspect. The Quraysh, the dominant tribe of Mecca, considered her their patroness and sought her blessing before military engagements.

Limit: Her power was likely considered contingent on proper ritual observance and sacrifice. Failure to honor her could result in defeat or misfortune.

Protection of Travelers and Caravans

As a major deity of the Quraysh, a tribe heavily involved in trade, Al-‘Uzzá was likely invoked for the safety of caravans traversing the dangerous Arabian desert. Her shrine at Nakhlah, located on a key trade route, would have served as a waystation for pilgrims and merchants.

Limit: This is an inferred function, not explicitly stated in primary texts.

Bestowing Prosperity and Abundance

Like many agricultural and tribal deities, Al-‘Uzzá was likely associated with general prosperity. The ritual of sacrifice, particularly of animals, was an act of propitiation to secure her favor for the tribe's well-being.

Limit: Not explicitly detailed in surviving sources.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No definitive visual representation of Al-‘Uzzá survives from the pre-Islamic Hijaz. The description by Ibn al-Kalbi of the 'idol' at Nakhlah is ambiguous, referring to a structure or object, possibly a sacred stone or a figure. Sozomen's identification with Aphrodite suggests she may have been conceptualized in a form similar to the Greek goddess, but this is a later, external interpretation. Symbols — Likely symbols include the sacred stone or idol at her shrine, the act of sacrifice, and possibly the planet Venus itself. In the Nabataean context, she may have been associated with a lion, as seen in some iconographic interpretations. The tree or grove at her shrine was also a key symbolic element. The 'ghabghab' (a place of blood sacrifice) was a central ritual symbol.

Domains — Her primary domain was the valley of Nakhlah, east of Mecca, where her principal shrine was located. She also had a temple in Ta'if. Her cult was centered in the Hijaz but extended into Nabataean territories. Her association with the planet Venus links her to the sky and celestial phenomena.

Accounts I
The Shrine at Nakhlah and the Sacrifice of GameKitab al-Asnam by Hisham ibn al-Kalbi

Late 6th - early 7th century CE (as described in the 8th-century source)

The Quraysh held Al-‘Uzzá in the highest esteem. Her shrine was located in the valley of Nakhlah, where a structure called a 'buss' was built over her sacred object. The ritual involved a 'ghabghab,' a place where blood was poured out. Notably, the Quraysh would not hunt game in the area without first offering a portion of it to Al-‘Uzzá, demonstrating her role as a provider and the strict rules of her cult. This practice solidified the tribe's bond with the goddess and ensured her favor, which was believed to bring victory and prosperity.

Accounts II
The Destruction of the Idol by Khalid ibn al-WalidKitab al-Asnam by Hisham ibn al-Kalbi; Sirah (biographies of Muhammad)

630 CE (8th year after Hijra)

Following the conquest of Mecca, the Prophet Muhammad dispatched Khalid ibn al-Walid to destroy the shrine of Al-‘Uzzá at Nakhlah. A famous anecdote describes how Khalid was met by a black, naked woman with disheveled hair who cursed him. Khalid struck her down, killing her, and then proceeded to demolish the shrine. This event symbolically marked the final triumph of Islam over the old pagan order in the region. The destruction of the shrine marked the definitive end of Al-‘Uzzá's formal cult in the Hijaz. The event is celebrated in Islamic tradition as a victory for monotheism.

The 'Satanic Verses' IncidentVarious early Islamic historical and biographical sources (e.g., al-Tabari, Ibn Sa'd)

c. 615 CE

This highly controversial episode, recorded in several early sources, recounts an alleged moment when the Prophet Muhammad, in a moment of weakness, is said to have uttered verses acknowledging Al-‘Uzzá, Al-Lat, and Manat as 'exalted swans' whose intercession could be hoped for. The Prophet is said to have been deceived by Satan, who put these words in his mouth. The verses were later retracted as a satanic suggestion, with the Qur'anic verses in Surah An-Najm (which condemn the goddesses) being revealed in their place. This story is rejected by most orthodox Muslim scholars as a fabrication, but it remains a major historical and literary point of discussion. The incident, if true, temporarily reconciled the Quraysh to Muhammad's message, but was immediately revoked. The story is a cornerstone of academic debate about the reliability of early Islamic sources and the nature of revelation.
