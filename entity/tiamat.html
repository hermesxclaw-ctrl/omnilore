Who It Is

Before there was sky or earth, before any god had a name, there was a single, restless sea—Tiamat. From her mingled waters the gods themselves were born, only to turn against their own mother in a battle so vast that its aftermath became the world you live in: rivers from her eyes, mountains from her breasts, clouds from her breath. Tiamat is not just a monster slain by a hero; she is the raw material of reality, the chaos that had to be broken to make order. To follow her story is to watch creation emerge from violence and to see how a forgotten goddess still haunts every river, every storm, every myth of dragons and deep seas.

Attested: Tiamat begins as a relatively passive, even conciliatory figure. When Apsû (Abzu), the fresh-water god, plans to destroy the noisy younger gods, she initially tries to calm him and prevent violence. After Apsû is killed by Ea, however, Tiamat is pressured by other deities to avenge him. She shifts into an actively hostile, warlike role, creating an army of eleven monsters and appointing Qingu as her champion. Powers
Primordial generative power

Tiamat embodies the salt sea whose mingling with Apsû’s fresh waters produces the first generation of gods. Her very existence as undifferentiated water is the medium of divine and cosmic emergence.

Limit: Her generative role is bound to the pre-creation state; once the cosmos is ordered and her body is divided, this primordial generative function ceases in its original form.

Creation of monstrous army (eleven creatures)

In preparation for war against the younger gods, Tiamat creates eleven terrifying creatures—often described as dragons, serpents, and hybrid monsters—to serve as her army. These embody chaos and terror, amplifying her threat.

Limit: The monsters are ultimately defeated along with Tiamat; their existence is tied to her rebellion and does not persist as independent cultic beings in later Mesopotamian religion.

Cosmic-scale combat power

Tiamat engages in a Chaoskampf—a cosmic battle against the younger gods, led by Marduk. She wields storms, magical forces, and her monstrous army, confronting Marduk in single combat.

Limit: She is vulnerable to Marduk’s strategic use of the ‘Evil Wind,’ net, and arrow. Once defeated and dismembered, she no longer acts as an independent agent.

Embodiment of chaos and resistance to order

Tiamat personifies primordial chaos, resisting the emerging order represented by the younger gods and especially Marduk. Her rebellion threatens to reverse creation and plunge the cosmos back into undifferentiated waters.

Limit: Her chaotic resistance is ultimately overcome and transformed into the very structure of ordered reality; chaos is not annihilated but reconfigured.

Transformative body (cosmic material)

After her defeat, Marduk uses Tiamat’s body as raw material to create heaven, earth, rivers, mountains, clouds, and other features. Her limbs and organs become the architecture of the cosmos.

Limit: This is a passive ‘power’ in narrative terms: Tiamat does not choose this transformation; it is imposed by Marduk. Nonetheless, it underscores her foundational role in cosmic structure.

Accounts I
Enūma Eliš (When on High)Babylonian creation epic on seven tablets

Old Babylonian to Kassite origins, standard first-millennium BCE form

The epic begins before heaven and earth are named, when Apsû (fresh water) and Tiamat (salt sea) mingle their waters. From this union the first gods are born. The younger gods become noisy and disruptive, prompting Apsû to plan their destruction. Ea learns of the plan, casts a spell, and kills Apsû, establishing his own dwelling above the slain god. Tiamat initially tries to calm Apsû and prevent violence, but after his death she is urged by other deities to seek vengeance. She creates eleven monstrous creatures and appoints Qingu as her champion, giving him the Tablet of Destinies. The gods, terrified, eventually choose Marduk as their leader on condition that he be granted supreme authority. Marduk confronts Tiamat in battle, using the ‘Evil Wind’ to distend her body, trapping her in a net, and shooting an arrow that pierces her heart. He then splits her corpse in two, forming heaven and earth, and fashions cosmic features from her body. Humanity is later created from the blood of Qingu to serve the gods. Accounts II
Old Akkadian incantation referencing TiamatFragmentary magical text (cited by Westenholz 1974)

Old Akkadian period (c. 23rd–22nd century BCE)

The incantation, preserved only in fragments, invokes Tiamat as a primordial sea figure in a ritual context. Details are sparse, but the text demonstrates that the name and concept of Tiamat existed prior to the fully developed Enūma Eliš narrative. This early attestation anchors Tiamat in a longer tradition of sea personification and suggests that her later role in Enūma Eliš builds on pre-existing mythic motifs.

Political vs. cosmological reading of Tiamat’s defeatTiamat’s defeat primarily encodes political realities—the rise of Babylon and Marduk, the centralization of power, and the suppression of older divine orders.The narrative is primarily cosmological, explaining how order arises from chaos, with political elements as secondary.
