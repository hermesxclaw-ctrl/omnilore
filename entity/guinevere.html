Who It Is

Queen Guinevere (Gwenhwyfar in Welsh, 'White Phantom' or 'White Fay') is the wife of King Arthur and the lover of Sir Lancelot — the woman whose adultery is simultaneously the greatest love story of the Middle Ages and the sin that tears the Round Table apart. In the earliest Welsh sources, she is a figure of worldly power: Gwenhwyfar appears in the Triads as one of the 'Three Great Queens of Arthur' (there were apparently three successive Gwenhwyfars, or perhaps three aspects of one queen). In the romances, she becomes the supreme object of courtly desire: the queen whose favor knights seek above all others, whose judgment of worth defines chivalric identity.

Guinevere's marriage to Arthur is, in the romances, a political union that becomes a loving but childless partnership. Her affair with Lancelot is the great counter-narrative to that marriage — a love so absolute that it transcends feudal loyalty, religious morality, and common sense. In Chrétien de Troyes's Lancelot, the Knight of the Cart, Guinevere is a figure of imperious emotional power: when Lancelot hesitates for a moment before climbing into a humiliating cart to rescue her, she punishes him with cold silence for days, and he nearly kills himself in despair. Her love is a force that demands absolute commitment, and Lancelot's devotion to her is his defining characteristic — greater than his loyalty to Arthur, greater than his duty as a knight, greater than his hope of achieving the Grail (which he cannot achieve because of his adultery with her).

The exposure of the affair is the hinge of Arthurian tragedy. In Malory, Sir Agravain and Sir Mordred trap Lancelot in Guinevere's chamber. Lancelot escapes, killing several knights in the process, but Guinevere is condemned to be burned at the stake for treason. Lancelot rescues her — accidentally killing Gawain's brothers Gareth and Gaheris, unarmed, in the chaos. Arthur pursues Lancelot to France, leaving Mordred as regent, and the war between the two greatest knights prevents either from being present to stop Mordred's usurpation.

Guinevere's ending varies across versions. In Malory, after Arthur's death, she rejects Lancelot and enters a convent, becoming a nun and dying in sanctity. Lancelot, seeing her example, becomes a priest and dies six weeks after her. In other versions, she is seized by Mordred and forced into marriage (or nearly so). In still others (the Vulgate Cycle), she is the False Guinevere — her identical half-sister who temporarily usurps her place as queen. Across all these variations, Guinevere remains the locus of desire, the cause of the Fall, and the figure through whom medieval poets explored the impossible conflicts between love and law, passion and order, the individual heart and the social world.
