Who It Is

Imagine a being so vast that it exists simultaneously as the formless ground of all reality, a radiant celestial presence, and a flesh-and-blood human walking among us, growing old and dying—yet never truly touched by change. This is the Trikāya, the Mahayana Buddhist doctrine of the three bodies of a Buddha, a vision of awakening that transcends any single form.

The Trikāya is not a personality but a set of functions. Dharmakāya is quiescent, unconditioned, and spontaneously active; Sambhogakāya is blissful, compassionate, and teaches advanced practitioners; Nirmāṇakāya is compassionate, appears in the world, and teaches ordinary beings. The doctrine emphasizes non-duality and the unity of these aspects.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Sanskrit: त्रिकाय (Trikāya), literally 'three bodies' or 'three collections'. 'Tri' means 'three', 'kāya' means 'body', 'collection', or 'basis'.

Pronunciation — Sanskrit: [t̪ɾikaːjɐ]; Chinese: sānshēn; Japanese: sanjin/sanshin; Korean: samsin; Vietnamese: tam thân; Tibetan: sku gsum.

Aliases — Three Bodies, Trikaya, 三身 (sānshēn), sanjin, sanshin, samsin

Ownership — The Trikāya is a central doctrine of Mahayana and Vajrayana Buddhism, with roots in Indian Yogācāra philosophy. It has been adopted and adapted across East Asian and Tibetan Buddhi

Powers
Dharmakāya

The ultimate body, identical with emptiness, suchness, and the true nature of reality. It is the source of all Buddha qualities and activities, yet it is without thought or intention. It is unconditioned, unarisen, and unceasing.

Limit: It is not a personal entity; it cannot be perceived by ordinary beings or even bodhisattvas. It is beyond conceptualization.

Sambhogakāya

The body of self-enjoyment, a divine, blissful form with infinite powers and forms. It appears to bodhisattvas in pure lands, teaching them Mahayana teachings. It is characterized by discriminating wisdom and is associated with the pure state of mental cognition.

Limit: It is only visible to advanced bodhisattvas, not to ordinary beings. It is not the ultimate reality but a compassionate manifestation.

Nirmāṇakāya

The manifestation body, which appears in the world in various forms, including human, animal, or inanimate, to help sentient beings. It is the historical Buddha Śākyamuni and other emanations. It is associated with all-accomplishing wisdom and the pure state of the five sense consciousnesses.

Limit: It is subject to birth, aging, sickness, and death, though these are merely appearances. It is not the ultimate reality but a compassionate means.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The Trikāya itself is not a visual entity but a conceptual framework. However, the three bodies are often iconographically represented: Dharmakāya as a formless or abstract symbol like a stupa or the letter 'A'; Sambhogakāya as celestial Buddhas with ornate robes and crowns, e.g., Amitābha; Nirmāṇakāya as the historical Buddha Śākyamuni in monk's robes. In Tibetan art, the three bodies are depicted as a hierarchy of forms.

Symbols — Dharmakāya is associated with the sky, space, and the color blue; Sambhogakāya with clouds, light, and jeweled ornaments; Nirmāṇakāya with earth and human forms. In some traditions, the three bodies are represented by the trikaya symbol of three circles or a triple wheel.

Domains — Dharmakāya is the ultimate reality, the dharmadhātu, beyond all realms; Sambhogakāya resides in pure lands (e.g., Sukhāvatī); Nirmāṇakāya appears in saṃsāra, in human and other forms. The three bodies are not spatially separate but are different ways of perceiving the same reality.

Accounts I
The Simile of the Sky, Cloud, and RainTrikāyasūtra

Unknown, likely early centuries CE

The Buddha explains that the Dharmakāya is like the sky, without nature; the Sambhogakāya is like a cloud, arising; and the Nirmāṇakāya is like rain, soaking everything. The three bodies are thus one continuum of reality and activity. This simile illustrates the non-dual nature of the three bodies and their function in the world.

Accounts II
The Golden Light Sutra's Teaching on the Triple BodySuvarṇaprabhāsa-sūtra

c. 5th century CE

The sutra contains a chapter explaining the triple body, emphasizing that the Dharma body is the true and ultimate body, while the other two are designations. It also associates each body with specific wisdoms and consciousnesses. This text became influential in East Asia and Tibet, shaping later interpretations.

The Buddhabhūmi-sūtra's Analogy of SpaceBuddhabhūmi-sūtra

c. 7th century CE

The sutra states that just as space neither arises nor ceases, yet forms arise and cease within it, so the purified dharma realm of the Tathagatas neither arises nor ceases, yet all activities for sentient beings appear within it. This analogy is cited in Yogācāra commentaries to explain the non-duality of the three bodies.

The Trikāya means that a Buddha has three separate bodies or personalities. — The three bodies are non-dual and are different aspects or functions of a single Buddhahood.

The historical Buddha was not a real person but a magical manifestation. — According to the Trikāya, the historical Buddha is a Nirmāṇakāya, but this does not negate his historical existence; it explains his nature as a compassionate manifestation.

The Trikāya is a polytheistic concept with multiple gods. — The Trikāya is a way to understand the nature of a Buddha, not a pantheon. The Sambhogakāya and Nirmāṇakāya are not separate deities but aspects of one Buddha.
