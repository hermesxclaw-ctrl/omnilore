The Approach

On a windswept mound in northwestern Turkey, the legendary city of Troy rises from the earth in layers—each one a silent testament to a civilization that endured for four millennia. Homer's Iliad immortalized its walls and heroes, but the real Troy was a thriving Bronze Age metropolis, a vassal of the Hittite Empire, and a place of repeated destruction and rebirth. Today, its ruins draw visitors who come to walk the same ground where myth and history intertwine, where a city's story is told not just in epic verse but in the very stones beneath their feet.

Content advisory. The mythological tradition of Troy includes themes of war, violence, death, and the brutal sack of a city. The archaeological site itself contains human remains and evidence of destruction layers. No explicit sexual content is present, but the broader Trojan War narrative includes rape and enslavement of captives, which may be mentioned in scholarly sources.
Identity

Troy

Pronunciation In English: /trɔɪ/ (Troy). In Greek: Troia (TROY-ah), Ilion (EE-lee-on). In Turkish: Truva (TROO-vah) or Troya (TROY-ah).

Known As

Etymology

In Greek, the city was called Troia (Τροία) and Ilion (Ἴλιον) or Ilios (Ἴλιος). The name 'Ilion' is derived from the mythological founder Ilus, and 'Troia' from Tros, another legendary founder. The Hittite name 'Wilusa' is related to 'Wilios', an earlier form of 'Ilios'. The Turkish name 'Truva' is derived from the Greek.

Cultural Ownership

Troy is a shared cultural heritage site, primarily associated with ancient Greek and Roman civilizations, but located in modern Turkey. It is recognized by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site, and its archaeological layers reflect Anatolian, Greek, and Roman influences.

Disputed Identifications

The exact location of Homeric Troy has been debated for centuries. While Hisarlık is now the most widely accepted site, some alternative locations have been proposed, such as Alexandria Troas and a site near Pınarbaşı. The identification of specific archaeological layers with the Homeric city remains a matter of scholarly debate.

Description

Appearance

The archaeological site of Troy consists of a mound (Hisarlık) with visible ruins of fortification walls, towers, gates, houses, and public buildings from various periods. The site is located on a hill overlooking the plain of the Scamander river. In its heyday, the city would have been a fortified citadel with lower town, surrounded by walls.

Symbols & Iconography

Troy is associated with the Trojan Horse, a symbol of deception and the city's fall. In ancient art, Troy is often depicted as a walled city with towers. The city's patron deity was Athena, and her temple was a prominent feature. The iconography of the site includes various artifacts such as pottery, jewelry, and sculptures found during excavations.

Behaviour & Personality

As a city, Troy's 'personality' is derived from its mythological and historical roles. In myth, Troy was a wealthy and powerful kingdom, led by King Priam, and was a center of culture and trade. Historically, it was a significant Bronze Age city that served as a vassal to the Hittite Empire and a hub of trade between the Aegean and Anatolia.

Domains & Habitats

Troy is located in the Troad region of northwestern Anatolia, near the Dardanelles. The city was situated on a hill, with a lower town extending to the plain. The surrounding area included fertile plains, rivers (Scamander and Simois), and access to the sea.

Version Differences

The city underwent multiple phases of construction and destruction, resulting in nine main layers. Troy I was an early Bronze Age settlement, Troy II was a wealthy citadel, Troy VI was a large and impressive city likely corresponding to the Hittite period, Troy VIIa shows signs of destruction that some associate with the Trojan War, and Troy VIII-IX were Greek and Roman cities.

Powers & Abilities

Fortification

Source Version Attested in archaeological remains and described in the Iliad.

Description

Troy was heavily fortified with massive stone walls, towers, and gates, making it a formidable defensive stronghold. The walls were often built with sloping faces and were strengthened over time.

Limitations

Despite its defenses, the city was sacked multiple times, suggesting that the walls were not impenetrable.

Contradictions

The exact height and construction of the walls vary between archaeological layers and literary descriptions.

Strategic Location

Source Version Historical analysis of the site's geography.

Description

Troy's position near the Dardanelles controlled access to the Black Sea and trade routes between the Aegean and Anatolia, giving it economic and military significance.

Limitations

The city's strategic importance may have made it a target for invasions.

Contradictions

Some scholars argue that the city's location was not as strategically significant as often claimed.

Divine Protection

Source Version Greek mythology, as told in the Iliad and other sources.

Description

In mythology, Troy was under the protection of gods, particularly Apollo and Poseidon, who helped build its walls. However, divine favor was withdrawn during the Trojan War.

Limitations

Divine protection did not prevent the city's eventual fall.

Contradictions

The gods were often portrayed as capricious, and their protection was conditional.

Stories & Myths

The Trojan War

Source Homer's Iliad, Epic Cycle, Virgil's AeneidCulture GreekPeriod Legendary, set in the Late Bronze Age (c. 12th century BC)

Summary

The Trojan War was a ten-year siege of Troy by a coalition of Greek (Achaean) forces, led by King Agamemnon, to retrieve Helen, the wife of Menelaus, who had been abducted by Paris, a prince of Troy. The war included many famous heroes such as Achilles, Hector, and Odysseus. The war ended with the cunning use of the Trojan Horse, a wooden horse that hid Greek soldiers, leading to the city's fall and destruction.

Consequences

The fall of Troy led to the city's destruction, the enslavement of its people, and the scattering of survivors, including Aeneas, who eventually founded Rome according to legend. The war also resulted in the deaths of many heroes on both sides.

Variants

There are multiple versions of the war's causes and events. Some accounts attribute the war to Zeus's plan to reduce the population, while others focus on the judgment of Paris. The destruction of Troy is sometimes described as complete, while other versions say parts of the city survived.

The Judgment of Paris

Source Greek mythology, CypriaCulture GreekPeriod Legendary, pre-Trojan War

Summary

Paris, a Trojan prince, was asked to judge a beauty contest among the goddesses Hera, Athena, and Aphrodite. He chose Aphrodite, who promised him the love of the most beautiful mortal woman, Helen of Sparta. This act led to the abduction of Helen and the subsequent Trojan War.

Participants

Aphrodite

Consequences

The judgment caused enmity among the goddesses, leading to their support for the Greek or Trojan sides during the war. It also set the stage for the war itself.

Variants

The story is consistent across sources, though the details of the bribe vary slightly.

The Building of Troy's Walls

Source Greek mythology, IliadCulture GreekPeriod Legendary, before the Trojan War

Summary

The walls of Troy were built by the gods Apollo and Poseidon, who were forced to serve the mortal king Laomedon. They built the walls, but Laomedon refused to pay them, leading to divine punishment and the eventual downfall of the city.

Participants

Apollo

Poseidon

Laomedon

Consequences

The refusal to pay the gods led to Apollo sending a plague and Poseidon a sea monster, which were later dealt with by Heracles. This also set a precedent for divine hostility toward Troy.

Variants

Some versions say only Poseidon built the walls, while Apollo herded cattle.

Sack of Troy

Source Virgil's Aeneid, Euripides' Trojan Women

Description

The city was destroyed and its inhabitants killed or enslaved. Men were slaughtered, women were taken as concubines, and children were killed. The mythological account describes the brutal treatment of the Trojans by the Greeks.

Scholarly Context

These accounts are literary, but they reflect ancient practices of warfare, where sacking cities and enslaving populations were common.

Human Sacrifice

Source Euripides' Hecuba, Quintus Smyrnaeus

Description

In some versions of the myth, Polyxena, Priam's daughter, is sacrificed at the tomb of Achilles to honor him. This is described in later epics.

Scholarly Context

Human sacrifice was not a regular practice in ancient Greek religion, but it appears in mythological narratives as a dramatic element.

Colonial and Nationalist Exploitation

Source Historical analyses of Schliemann's work

Description

The identification of Troy with Hisarlık was driven partly by European nationalist and colonialist interests in the 19th century, particularly by Schliemann, who sought to prove the historicity of Homeric epic and to establish a European heritage rooted in ancient Greece.

Scholarly Context

Schliemann's methods were destructive and his claims were often exaggerated. His work has been criticized for prioritizing myth over careful archaeology.

Contradictions & Disputes

Historical existence of the Trojan War

Position A

The Trojan War was a real historical event, as argued by Schliemann and some later scholars who see archaeological evidence of destruction at Troy VIIa.

Position A — evidence

Archaeological evidence of destruction layers at Troy VIIa, Hittite records mentioning a conflict in the area, and the existence of a powerful city at the time.

Position B

The Trojan War is purely mythological, with no historical basis, as argued by scholars who point to the lack of definitive evidence for a Greek attack.

Position B — evidence

No contemporary written records of the war, the mythological nature of the sources, and the lack of clear archaeological evidence for a siege or battle.

Scholarly Consensus

There is no consensus. Most scholars accept that there was a real city at Hisarlık, but the Trojan War's historicity is still debated.

Which archaeological layer corresponds to Homeric Troy

Position A

Troy VI was the city of Homer, as proposed by Dörpfeld, due to its impressive fortifications and evidence of destruction.

Position A — evidence

Troy VI had massive walls and was destroyed around 1250 BC, which aligns with some dating of the war.

Position B

Troy VIIa was the city of Homer, as argued by Blegen, due to evidence of a siege and destruction around the time traditionally given for the war.

Position B — evidence

Troy VIIa shows signs of a long siege, including storage pithoi and a destruction layer around 1180 BC, which some associate with the war.

Scholarly Consensus

There is no consensus. Troy VI was more impressive, but Troy VIIa shows more evidence of a conflict. Some scholars suggest the war may have been a composite of multiple conflicts.

The location of Troy

Position A

Hisarlık is the site of ancient Troy, as identified by Calvert and Schliemann and accepted by most scholars.

Position A — evidence

The archaeological remains at Hisarlık match the description of Troy in ancient sources, including its location on a hill and its proximity to the sea.

Position B

Alternative locations, such as Alexandria Troas or a site near Pınarbaşı, have been proposed, but they have not gained wide acceptance.

Position B — evidence

Some early travelers and scholars argued for different sites based on geographical features mentioned in the Iliad.

Scholarly Consensus

Hisarlık is overwhelmingly accepted as the site of Troy by modern scholars.
