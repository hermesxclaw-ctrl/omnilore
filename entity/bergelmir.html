Bergelmir

The Survivor, Father of the Frost Giants

Bergelmir, the lone survivor, who rode the flood of Ymir's blood and became the father of the frost giant race.

Who It Is

Bergelmir is the frost giant who survived Ymir's destruction and became the ancestor of all subsequent frost giants. He is the son of Þrúðgelmir (Thrudgelmir) and grandson of Ymir, the primordial giant. When Odin, Vili, and Vá killed Ymir and drowned all other frost giants in Ymir's gushing blood-wounds, Bergelmir and his wife alone survived by climbing onto a 'lúðr' (possibly a millstone or a wooden chest/ark) and floating upon the flood of blood. They then founded a new race of frost giants in Jötunheimr. The parallel to the biblical Noah is striking and may reflect either a common Indo-European flood myth or Christian influence on the Eddic tradition. Bergelmir represents the persistence of chaos — the principle that no matter how thoroughly the gods suppress the forces of disorder, some remnant always survives to regrow and eventually threaten the cosmos again. He is a transitional figure between the primordial generation of giants and the later frost giants who interact (and conflict) with the gods throughout the myths.

Attested in Poetic Edda (Vafþrúðnismál) / Prose Edda (Gylfaginning)Era Viking Age (c. 800-1100 CE), recorded 13th century

Form

No surviving source describes Bergelmir’s physical appearance in detail. As a jötunn and grandson of the primordial giant Aurgelmir/Ymir, he is implicitly of giant stature and nature, but this is reconstructed from his classification rather than directly attested. The poetic texts focus on his birth and placement on a lúðr, not on bodily traits, clothing, or distinguishing features. Any depiction of Bergelmir as a towering, humanoid frost giant in modern art or games is therefore an adaptation-specific visualization, not a textual description.

Powers

Survival of the blood-flood Bergelmir’s defining ‘ability’ is surviving the cosmic flood of Ymir’s blood that drowns all other frost giants. In Gylfaginning, he and his wife escape by boarding a lúðr, which functions as a vessel. This survival is not framed as a supernatural power in the sources but as a narrative role: he is the exception to total annihilation and the seed of future giants. Prose Edda, Gylfaginning 7 (Snorri’s expanded cosmogony) The text does not attribute magical powers or divine favor to Bergelmir; his survival is a single event tied to the lúðr. There is no evidence that he can control floods, blood, or cosmic forces. The Poetic Edda only states that Bergelmir was ‘laid on a lúðr’, which may imply infancy rather than active escape. Snorri’s version turns this into a Noah-like survival story, creating tension between passive placement and active flood navigation.

Progenitor of the frost giants Bergelmir is explicitly said to be the ancestor of the later frost giants: ‘from them are descended the Frost-giants’ (Gylfaginning). His ‘power’ is genealogical—he carries the giant race forward after the gods’ slaughter of Ymir. Poetic Edda, Vafþrúðnismál 29; Prose Edda, Gylfaginning 7 The sources do not describe his children or specific descendants by name, nor do they grant him authority over all giants in a political or religious sense. His role is foundational rather than ruling. Some scholars question whether Snorri’s explicit statement about all frost giants descending from Bergelmir is a systematizing move rather than a reflection of older tradition, since the poetic corpus does not spell this out.

Behavior & Habitat

Attested behavior is minimal but significant. Bergelmir is portrayed as a survivor: in Snorri’s Gylfaginning he and his unnamed wife actively escape the flood of Ymir’s blood by boarding a lúðr, preserving the frost-giant lineage. In Vafþrúðnismál, the emphasis is on his birth and placement on the lúðr, which may suggest a ritual or mythic framing of infancy rather than agency. Beyond this, no speech, deeds, or personality traits are recorded. His ‘behavior’ is therefore largely reconstructed as the archetypal role of the lone survivor and progenitor of a race.

Accounts

In a wisdom contest between Odin and the giant Vafþrúðnir, Odin asks who is oldest among the gods and giants and what the wise giant remembers first. Vafþrúðnir replies that many winters before the earth was shaped, Bergelmir was born, son of Þrúðgelmir and grandson of Aurgelmir (Ymir). He adds that his earliest memory is of the wise giant being laid on a lúðr. The poem thus situates Bergelmir in a primordial genealogy and hints at a ritual or mythic scene involving the lúðr.

After the gods Odin, Vili, and Vé slay the primordial giant Ymir, so much blood pours from his wounds that all frost giants drown. Only Bergelmir and his wife survive by climbing onto a lúðr, which carries them through the deluge. From this couple descend the later frost giants who inhabit the outer shores of the world-ocean. The narrative echoes flood-survivor motifs known from other traditions while remaining embedded in Norse cosmogony.

Field Notes

☘ Bergelmir’s entire mythic footprint fits on less than a page of text, yet he anchors the genealogy of all later frost giants.

☘ A small moon of Saturn is named Bergelmir, meaning that this obscure Norse giant now quietly orbits a gas giant in modern astronomy.

☘ The mysterious lúðr that saves Bergelmir may have been a flour-bin, making the cosmic flood-survivor’s ‘ark’ closer to a kitchen appliance than a grand ship.

A way to hold it in your mind

If Ymir is the raw cosmic ingredient and Odin the architect who carves a world from that body, then Bergelmir is the stubborn remainder—the bit of chaos that slips through the gods’ fingers. He is the glitch in the divine reset, the ancestor of every giant who still stands on the far shore of the world-ocean and reminds the gods that their victory was never total.
