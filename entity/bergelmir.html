Who It Is

Imagine the entire world drowning not in water, but in the blood of a slain cosmic giant—and in that crimson deluge, every frost giant dies except one couple clinging to a wooden trough. That survivor is Bergelmir, the almost-forgotten giant whose few surviving lines of verse carry the weight of an entire race’s second beginning. He is Noah without a covenant, a primordial refugee whose ‘ark’ may really be a mill-box, grinding grain at the edge of the universe while the gods build a world from his grandfather’s corpse.

Attested behavior is minimal but significant. Bergelmir is portrayed as a survivor: in Snorri’s Gylfaginning he and his unnamed wife actively escape the flood of Ymir’s blood by boarding a lúðr, preserving the frost-giant lineage. In Vafþrúðnismál, the emphasis is on his birth and placement on the lúðr, which may suggest a ritual or mythic framing of infancy rather than agency. Beyond this, no speech, deeds, or personality traits are recorded. His ‘behavior’ is therefore largely reconstructed as the archetypal role of the lone survivor and progenitor of a race.

Powers
Survival of the blood-flood

Bergelmir’s defining ‘ability’ is surviving the cosmic flood of Ymir’s blood that drowns all other frost giants. In Gylfaginning, he and his wife escape by boarding a lúðr, which functions as a vessel. This survival is not framed as a supernatural power in the sources but as a narrative role: he is the exception to total annihilation and the seed of future giants.

Limit: The text does not attribute magical powers or divine favor to Bergelmir; his survival is a single event tied to the lúðr. There is no evidence that he can control floods, blood, or cosmic forces.

Progenitor of the frost giants

Bergelmir is explicitly said to be the ancestor of the later frost giants: ‘from them are descended the Frost-giants’ (Gylfaginning). His ‘power’ is genealogical—he carries the giant race forward after the gods’ slaughter of Ymir.

Limit: The sources do not describe his children or specific descendants by name, nor do they grant him authority over all giants in a political or religious sense. His role is foundational rather than ruling.

Accounts I
The birth of Bergelmir before the making of the earthPoetic Edda, Vafþrúðnismál 29 and 35

Medieval (13th century manuscript, earlier oral tradition)

In a wisdom contest between Odin and the giant Vafþrúðnir, Odin asks who is oldest among the gods and giants and what the wise giant remembers first. Vafþrúðnir replies that many winters before the earth was shaped, Bergelmir was born, son of Þrúðgelmir and grandson of Aurgelmir (Ymir). He adds that his earliest memory is of the wise giant being laid on a lúðr. The poem thus situates Bergelmir in a primordial genealogy and hints at a ritual or mythic scene involving the lúðr. The story establishes Bergelmir as a key link in the giant lineage and as a figure whose birth predates the creation of the world. It also introduces the enigmatic lúðr, which later becomes central to Snorri’s flood narrative.

Accounts II
Bergelmir and the flood of Ymir’s bloodProse Edda, Gylfaginning 7

Medieval (early 13th century)

After the gods Odin, Vili, and Vé slay the primordial giant Ymir, so much blood pours from his wounds that all frost giants drown. Only Bergelmir and his wife survive by climbing onto a lúðr, which carries them through the deluge. From this couple descend the later frost giants who inhabit the outer shores of the world-ocean. The narrative echoes flood-survivor motifs known from other traditions while remaining embedded in Norse cosmogony. The flood marks a violent break between the primordial chaos and the ordered world made from Ymir’s body. Bergelmir’s survival ensures that the giants continue to exist as cosmic opponents and neighbors of the gods. The story also introduces a minimal flood pattern without moral judgment or covenant, differing from Near Eastern and biblical flood myths.
