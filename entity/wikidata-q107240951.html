Form

Horus is most often depicted as a falcon — most likely a lanner falcon or peregrine falcon — flying across the sky, or as a man with the head of a falcon, frequently crowned with the double crown (pschent) of Upper and Lower Egypt. As a sky god he was said to contain the sun and moon, his right eye being the sun and his left eye the moon. In his child form (Harpocrates/Heru-pa-khered) he appears as a naked youth with a sidelock of youth on the right of his head and a finger to his mouth, often wearing the united crowns, and sometimes seated on a lotus with his mother Isis. The elder Horus (Heru-ur) could be shown fully as a falcon, as a falcon-headed sphinx (hieracosphinx), or given the title Kemwer, 'the great black one'.

Powers

Embodiment of kingship (divine pharaoh) Horus is the god of legitimate kingship: the pharaoh is regarded as the living Horus on earth and becomes Osiris at death. The Horus-name was one of the traditional royal titles. His victory over Set is the mythic charter for the rightful succession of the throne of Egypt. Pyramid Texts; royal ideology across Egyptian history The kingship must be won/ratified; the throne was contested by Set for eighty years in the myth, and divine judgment (before the Ennead or Geb/Ra/Atum) is required to confirm it. The right to rule is variously awarded to Horus outright, or divided between Horus and Set, depending on the source; in the Memphite Theology Geb first divides the land then reverses himself and gives sole rule to Horus.

Sky dominion (solar and lunar eyes) As a sky god Horus contains the sun and the moon, his right eye being the sun and his left eye the moon; they traverse the sky as he, a falcon, flies across it. Restoration of his eye is equated with the waxing of the moon toward full brightness. Ancient Egyptian sky theology; New Kingdom and later texts The eye must be restored after Set's damage; its wholeness is the precondition for cosmic order (maat). Some Egyptologists (e.g., Rolf Krauss) argue the Eye of Horus originally represented Venus as morning/evening star and only later became the moon; others (Wilkinson) see a gradual distinction between lunar Eye of Horus and solar Eye of Ra.

Healing and protective power (the wedjat Eye) The Eye of Horus is a symbol of well-being, healing and protection, one of the most common amulets in Egyptian history. Healing spells equate the patient with Horus so he or she may be healed as Horus was; the wedjat guarded the dead in the afterlife and was painted on coffin sides and boat bows. Egyptian medical papyri; stelae; amulet tradition from Old Kingdom to Roman period The healing power derives from the mythological restoration of the eye performed by Thoth, Hathor, Isis, or fifteen deities; it is not Horus's alone. None major; but the 'pieces of the eye as fractions' reading (Möller, Gardiner) has been challenged by Jim Ritter.

Avenging protector and warrior Horus avenges his father Osiris by warring against Set, his uncle, and ultimately defeats him. He is a protector of the pharaoh (and, through the king, Egypt). In the Cult of the Festival of Victory at Edfu, the king reenacted Horus's slaying of Set as a hippopotamus with a harpoon. The Contendings of Horus and Set; Osiris myth; Edfu festival texts His victory is hard-won (eighty years of contests) and sometimes depends on Isis's cunning and the gods' judgment rather than brute force. Set is presented both as Horus's uncle (Osiris's murderer) and, in another tradition, as Horus's brother; some traditions reconcile Horus and Set by dividing the realm.

Divine mummification-linked role (First Mummy's heir) Through the Osiris myth, Horus inherits a kingship bound to the afterlife: only by making funerary offerings (including his own Eye) to Osiris can his father be fully enlivened as king of the dead. The offering of the Eye of Horus became the prototype of all funerary and temple offerings. Osiris myth; offering texts The restoration of Osiris depends on Horus's ritual action after he becomes king. None major.

Multiform deity (syncretism and roles) Horus manifests across many aspects: Horakhty (sun on the horizon), Har-em-akhet/Harmachis (associated with the sphinx), Heru-Behdeti (winged solar disk of Edfu), Harpara (young solar form in the Montu triad of Armant/North Karnak), and local forms. This allowed him to absorb or be absorbed by other gods through syncretism (e.g., Ra-Horakhty, Montu's son Harpara, a form of Thoth at Armant). Various regional cults and theological texts The distinctions among forms are not consistent across texts; Egyptologists disagree on whether they are separate gods or facets. Some forms (e.g., Hor Merti, Har-imy-shenut with a crocodile body) are highly localized and poorly attested.

Accounts

Isis gathers the dismembered parts of her murdered husband Osiris and, by her magic and the aid of Nephthys and others, restores his body just long enough to conceive a posthumous son. (In older Egyptian accounts Osiris's penis survives whole; in Plutarch's later Greek telling Isis has to fashion a phallic replacement because a fish ate the original, explaining the Egyptian taboo on eating fish. Some tellings say a catfish/Medjed or a crab ate it.) Pregnant, Isis flees into the papyrus thickets of the Nile Delta to hide from her brother Set, who murdered her husband and would kill her son. In this hidden 'nest of Horus' (called Akh-bity, 'papyrus thicket of the king of Lower Egypt'; Greek writers name it Khemmis near Buto) she gives birth and raises the vulnerable child. In the Metternich Stela tradition, Isis travels unrecognized among humans, guarded by seven scorpion deities who take revenge on a wealthy woman who refused her help.

After Osiris's murder, his adult son Horus challenges Set for the throne. Their rivalry is presented both as a violent war and as a legal judgment before the assembled gods (the Ennead), with Ra/Atum or Geb as judge and Thoth as conciliator. Across 'eighty years' of disputed contests, Set tries to dominate Horus by sexually assaulting him; Horus catches Set's semen in his hands and throws it in the river so he cannot be said to have been inseminated, then (with or without Isis) spreads his own semen on lettuce, Set's favourite food. When the gods summon each god's semen forth to test the claims, Set's semen answers from the river (invalidating his claim) while Horus's answers from inside Set — and appears on Set's forehead as a golden disk, which Thoth takes and places on his own head. The two feud over a boat race (Horus's wooden boat painted to look like stone wins against Set's real stone boat, which sinks). In one episode Isis, harpooning Set in combat, strikes her own son, and Horus cuts off her head in rage; Thoth restores her with a cow's head, explaining her horned headdress. Eventually Horus wins the judgment, becomes king, and offers food and his own Eye to Osiris, reviving him in the afterlife.

In the struggle between Horus and Set, Set damages or tears out one (or both) of Horus's eyes; sometimes he steals or tramples and eats it; in the Book of the Dead he strikes it in the form of a black boar. The eye is restored — 'filled' — most commonly by Thoth, who reassembles it, or by Hathor anointing Horus's sockets with the milk of a gazelle, or by Isis watering the buried eyes so they grow into lotuses (and, in Papyrus Jumilhac, into the first grapevines). In Greco-Roman temple texts, Thoth and fourteen other deities fill the eye with plants and minerals. The restoration represents the waxing of the moon to full brightness and the return of wholeness and order; the healed eye, the wedjat, becomes the supreme symbol of protection and the basis of funerary offering rites, since Horus offers his restored Eye to Osiris, who consumes it and is sustained in the afterlife.

The pharaoh was identified directly with Horus, who embodied kingship and protected the ruler. He was also the son of Ra, and through Horus the ruler held theological dominion over the whole world: the gods produced by Atum represent cosmic forces, Horus is their offspring, and by identifying Horus with Atum and then the pharaoh with Horus, the king's power is divinely grounded. 'The pharaoh as Horus in life became the pharaoh as Osiris in death,' united with the gods; each new pharaoh was a new incarnation of Horus. The earliest Horus was the tutelary deity of Nekhen in Upper Egypt, the first known national god, whose pre- and Early-Dynastic worshippers — the 'Followers of Horus' — conquered and unified Egypt.

An annual festival at the Temple of Horus at Edfu commemorated Horus's victory over Set with a sacred drama in which the king played Horus and a hippopotamus (or a model of one) played Set; the king harpooned the hippopotamus, commemorating and re-legitimizing the defeat of Set and the rule of Horus. The king was often represented by a priest, and the hippopotamus was commonly a model.

In a positive, harmonized aspect, the paired gods Horus and Set (or the solar forms Horakhty/Ra) jointly maintain order. While Set repels the serpent Apep from Ra's barque, Horus represents the sun and sky-order; in some hierarchies the two personify the union of Upper and Lower Egypt and the reconciliation of order and necessary disorder, a complementarity resolved 'into a united whole' per the Memphite Theology resolution.

Content Notes

In 'The Contendings of Horus and Set', Set tries to establish dominance by sexually assaulting Horus. The encounter is framed in Egyptian terms where semen is a potent, dangerous substance 'akin to poison'. Horus thwarts the attempt by catching Set's semen in his hands and throwing it in the river; Isis retaliates by making Set ingest Horus's semen via lettuce, which appears on Set's forehead as a golden disk — Set is thereby 'impregnated' by his rival's seed and 'gives birth' to the disk.

Egyptologist Herman te Velde interprets the sexual-abuse and eye-loss episodes as a single story: Set assaults Horus, Horus retaliates and impregnates Set, and Set comes to possess Horus's Eye as it appears on Set's head. The episode reflects Set's attested characterization as forceful, indiscriminate, sexually potent, and the danger of semen in Egyptian ritual thought.

Papyrus Chester Beatty I (Contendings); Osiris myth; Egyptian medical/healing texts about semen-as-poison

In the Contendings, Isis, harpooning Set during his combat with Horus, strikes her own son by mistake; Horus, enraged, cuts off Isis's head. Thoth restores her with the head of a cow, providing the mythic origin of the cow-horn headdress Isis wears.

Illustrates the myth's unglamorized violence and the theme of rash anger even in the hero; Thoth's healing power mends the harm.

The Contendings of Horus and Set

The Festival of Victory at the Temple of Edfu reenacted Horus's defeat of Set as a king harpooning a hippopotamus that played the role of Set. The ritual was a violent dramatization of divine victory and royal legitimacy.

The hippopotamus was commonly a model; the rite legitimized the pharaoh's rule by annually re-performing the mythic destruction of the enemy.

Edfu temple inscriptions

Horus's image as the divine champion of Egypt and symbol of unification was pressed into service of the pharaonic state's conquest and centralized divine-royal ideology; the 'Followers of Horus' mythology framed the unification conquest of Lower Egypt by an Upper Egyptian kingdom. In modern times, Horus symbolism has been reused by nationalist, esoteric, and commercial interests detached from its original cult.

The political origins of the Horus-Set conflict may reflect a real predynastic/Early-Dynastic contest, though Egyptologists have reached no definitive conclusions about the historical events behind the myth.

Pyramid Texts; royal ideology; modern reception

Beginning with William R. Cooper's 1877 book and popularized by the self-published 2008 work of 'Acharya S' (and others), a fringe tradition contends that the story of Horus shares many features with the later narrative of Jesus Christ. This outlook is controversial and rejected by mainstream scholarship.

Egyptologists and biblical scholars regard such claims as methodologically flawed surface comparisons that conflate separate traditions; they are flagged as misconceptions rather than academic conclusions.

Horus article (main); modern fringe publications

Horus's existence depends on the incestuous and posthumous union of his parents: Osiris and Isis are brother and sister (children of Geb and Nut), and Isis conceives Horus with the reassembled/brought-back-to-life Osiris. His elder form is also the child of the incestuous sibling pair Geb and Nut. The Egyptian royal gods' family is riddled with sibling unions that modern readers find disturbing.

Egyptian myth regularly depicts divine sibling marriage as part of royal-creation cosmology; it is a standard feature of the theology, presented without the moral framing modern audiences expect.

Scholarly Debates
Is Horus the son of Osiris and Isis, or the brother of Osiris?
Horus is the posthumous son of Osiris and Isis, the rightful heir avenging his murdered father. ⇌ Horus the Elder (Heru-ur) is the brother of Osiris, Isis, Set, and Nephthys (children of Geb and Nut).
Evidence for A: Standard Osirian tradition; Pyramid Texts and the bulk of Egyptian myth.
Evidence for B: Heru-ur tradition; Plutarch's placement of Horus the Elder among the five intercalary-day children.
Sun and moon, or Venus, as the Eye of Horus?
Horus's right eye is the sun and left eye is the moon; the moon's phases reflect the eye's injury and restoration. ⇌ The Eye of Horus originally represented Venus as morning and evening star.
Evidence for A: New Kingdom and later texts equating the two eyes with sun and moon.
Evidence for B: Rolf Krauss's argument that the eye was Venus before becoming the moon.
Were the wedjat-eye hieroglyph pieces really fractions of a hekat?
Yes — the pieces of the Eye of Horus stood for fractions 1/2 through 1/64. ⇌ No — the hieratic fraction signs had a separate origin and only later attached themselves to the eye.
Evidence for A: Möller (1911) and Gardiner (Egyptian Grammar) popularized the reading; retained in J. P. Allen's 2014 Middle Egyptian.

How does the Horus–Set rivalry end: sole triumph or division of the realm?
Horus wins the judgment outright and becomes sole rightful king; Set is defeated, exiled, or demonized. ⇌ The two gods divide the kingdom (Black Land vs Red Land; earth vs sky; Upper vs Lower Egypt), reconciled by Geb.
Evidence for A: Many new and later-period sources emphasizing Set's total defeat and humiliation.
Evidence for B: The Memphite Theology and numerous versions of the division.
Is the 'Horus–Jesus' parallel valid?
The story of Horus parallels the later story of Jesus (birth, death, resurrection, etc.), suggesting derivation or structural identity. ⇌ The parallels are superficial, constructed by cherry-picking and conflation, and are rejected by mainstream scholarship.
Evidence for A: Popular fringe claims (Cooper 1877; Acharya S 2008).
Evidence for B: Mainstream Egyptology and biblical studies; the claims conflate separate traditions and misattribute details.

A way to hold it in your mind

Uneasily perched at the top of a stone more than five thousand years old, a peregrine falcon stares down at the first pharaohs. Before the god of the resurrected Osiris, the avenging son, the patient rival of Set, Horus begins not as a deity of the afterlife at all but as the living heart of kingship itself — the falcon-god of a prehistoric Upper Egyptian town at Nekhen, whose earliest worshippers called themselves the Followers of Horus. Every king who mounted the throne after them replayed him: alive, he was Horus on earth; dead, he became Osiris in the Duat. But the god who embodied the throne was also the god whose eye Set tore out and threw into the marshes, where, in one telling, it grew into the first grapevines — and whose own semen, planted like a trick on his enemy's favourite lettuce, made the desert god 'give birth' to a golden disk. Sky, sun, moon, crown, wound and healing: Horus is the Egyptian image of order restored, a falcon with the whole cosmos for a body and a single, endlessly restored eye for a throne.
