Who It Is

Imagine a god who is every pharaoh who ever ruled — and the falcon who watches from the sky. Horus began as a local deity of a small Upper Egyptian town and rose to become the very definition of kingship itself, his Eye the most powerful protective symbol the ancient world ever produced. His battle with Set — a cosmic legal drama spanning 80 years, complete with sexual deception, divine tribunal, and celestial combat — is one of mythology's strangest and most compelling stories. What does it mean when your god is the king, and the king is a god?

Horus embodies legitimate kingship, filial piety, and cosmic order against chaos. In the Contendings of Horus and Seth, he displays both righteous determination and youthful vulnerability — he is temporarily blinded by Seth, has his hands cut off, and endures a protracted legal battle lasting 80 years before the divine tribunal. Yet he ultimately prevails through a combination of divine support, cleverness (aided by his mother Isis), and his inherent right to the throne. Divine Kingship

Every pharaoh was a living Horus on earth. The ruling king was a manifestation of Horus in life, becoming Osiris in death. This legitimized the entire Egyptian political system.

Limit:

Healing and Protection

The Eye of Horus (wedjat) was the most powerful protective amulet in Egyptian magic. After his eye was damaged by Seth, it was restored by Thoth, making it a symbol of healing and wholeness.

Limit:

Sky Dominion

As sky god, Horus's right eye was the sun and left eye the moon. His falcon wings spanned the visible heavens, and he could see all things from above.

Limit:

Solar Authority

In his form as Ra-Horakhty, he commanded the sun's daily journey across the sky, merging the Horus and Ra traditions into the ultimate solar deity.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Horus is most commonly depicted as a falcon — likely a lanner or peregrine falcon — or as a man with a falcon's head wearing the pschent, the double crown of Upper and Lower Egypt. This crown represents his dominion over the unified kingdom. In his form as Harpocrates (Horus the Child), he appears as a naked boy with a sidelock of youth, sucking his finger. As Ra-Horakhty, he is shown as a falcon or falcon-headed man with the sun disk on his head

Symbols — The Eye of Horus (wedjat) is his most famous symbol — a stylized falcon's eye representing protection, healing, and royal power. The pschent crown symbolizes unified Egypt. The falcon itself is his primary animal form. The winged sun disk (Behdeti) is a common temple decoration. The ankh he offers to pharaohs represents life.

Domains — Kingship, the sky, the sun, healing, protection, and war. His cult centers included Nekhen (Hierakonpolis) in Upper Egypt — his earliest center and one of the first urban settlements of predynastic Egypt, the 'City of the Falcon' where the earliest kings were crowned — and Edfu (Behdet), site of the magnificent Ptolemaic temple that is the best-preserved ancient Egyptian temple in existence. Accounts I
The Osiris Myth (Murder and Vengeance)Pyramid Texts, Plutarch's 'De Iside et Osiride'

Old Kingdom to Greco-Roman

Osiris, the righteous king, is murdered by his jealous brother Set, who dismembers the body and scatters it across Egypt. Isis, Osiris's wife and sister, magically reassembles his body and conceives Horus. She raises Horus in secret in the marshes of the Nile Delta, hiding him from Set. When Horus comes of age, he challenges Set for the throne of Egypt. After an epic conflict — encompassing physical combat, legal proceedings, and sexual contests — Horus is vindicated and crowned rightful king, while Osiris becomes ruler of the afterlife. Established the divine right of pharaohs. Every pharaoh was Horus on the throne. Osiris became judge of the dead. Set became the embodiment of chaos and foreign lands.

The Contendings of Horus and SethPapyrus Chester Beatty I (c. 1200 BCE)

New Kingdom

A dramatic and often comic narrative of the legal battle between Horus and Set for Osiris's throne. The trial lasts 80 years before the Ennead of gods. Set blinds Horus in one eye; Horus emasculates Set. Isis tricks Set into condemning himself. The gods debate, bribe, and argue. Set attempts to sexually dominate Horus to prove superiority, but Horus catches Set's semen and Isis spreads it on Set's lettuce — when Set eats it, he is magically 'pregnant,' proving Horus's dominance. Ra-Horakhty finally declares Horus the victor. Horus gains the throne of Egypt. Set is given a role commanding the storms and accompanying Ra through the underworld. Establishes the triumph of legitimate order over chaos.

The Eye of HorusVarious Pyramid and Coffin Texts

Old Kingdom onward

During the battle with Set, Horus's left eye is torn out and shattered into pieces. Thoth, the god of wisdom and magic, reassembles the fragments, miraculously restoring the eye. The restored eye — the wedjat — becomes the most powerful symbol of healing, protection, and wholeness in Egyptian magic. Each of the six pieces comes to represent a fraction (1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, 1/32, 1/64), creating the Eye of Horus fractions used in Egyptian mathematics and medicine. Created the wedjat amulet, the most ubiquitous protective symbol in Egyptian culture. The fractions were used in practical measurement and healing prescriptions.

Horus and the Winged DiskEdfu Temple inscriptions

Ptolemaic

Horus Behdety (Horus of Edfu) battles the enemies of Ra in the form of a great winged sun disk. He flies above the enemy forces, and whenever he looks down, enemies are struck with blindness and turn their weapons upon each other. This myth is inscribed on the walls of the Edfu temple and depicts Horus's role as divine warrior and protector of the cosmic order against the forces of chaos. The winged sun disk became a standard protective symbol placed above temple doorways and on stelae. Military standards adopted the winged-disk motif.

Horus the Child on the CrocodilesCippus stelae, Late Period

Late Period (664-332 BCE)

Horus as a child (Harpocrates) is depicted standing on two crocodiles while holding dangerous animals — snakes, scorpions, lions — in his hands. According to the myth, the infant Horus was bitten by a scorpion in the marshes and nearly died, but was healed by Thoth. The cippus stelae depicting this scene were used for magical healing: water poured over the stela absorbed its power and was drunk by the afflicted as medicine. Established Horus as a healer accessible to common people. Cippus stelae became important magical-medical tools throughout Egypt.

Horus and the Four SonsCoffin Texts and Book of the Dead

Middle Kingdom onward

Horus fathers or adopts four protective deities — Imsety, Hapy, Duamutef, and Qebehsenuef — who guard the canopic jars of the deceased. Each son protects a specific organ and is associated with a cardinal direction and a protective goddess. This extends Horus's protective function into the afterlife, ensuring the deceased's bodily integrity for resurrection. The canopic jar system of mummification and funerary protection. Ties Horus's protective power to the afterlife journey of every Egyptian.
