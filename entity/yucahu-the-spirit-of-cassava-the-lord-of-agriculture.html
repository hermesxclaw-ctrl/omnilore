Who It Is

Yúcahu (often given the extended name Yúcahu Bagua Maórocoti, meaning 'Lord of the Cassava, Water, and the Valleys') is the primary male deity of the Taíno pantheon — the spirit of the cassava (yucca), the staple root crop that formed the foundation of Taíno subsistence. Yúcahu was not represented anthropomorphically in the way of later Mediterranean gods; rather, his presence was recognized in three-pointed stone objects (trigonolites or 'zemí stones') that were buried in the conucos (raised agricultural mounds). These trigonolites — carved from limestone, serpentine, or jadeite — are among the most distinctive expressions of Taíno art: they often feature a stylized face on one point, a small animal on another, and a flat base. When buried in the conuco, they transmitted Yúcahu's fertility to the cassava. Yúcahu was also associated with the sea — the Caribbean that surrounded the islands — making him a deity of both agricultural and maritime bounty. He was the male counterpart to Atabey (the mother-goddess), and together they sustained the Taíno world.
