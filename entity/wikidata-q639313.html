It is the source of all creation, preservation, and destruction. It is also the domain of yoga and spiritual realization, as it embodies the perfect balance of opposing energies within the individual. In art and theology, it is a central icon in Shaiva temples, particularly in South India, and is a subject of philosophical commentary on the nature of reality and gender.

Form

Ardhanārīśvara is depicted as a single, anthropomorphic figure split down the middle. The right half is the male aspect, Shiva: it has a muscular build, matted hair piled in a topknot (jatamukuta), a third eye on the forehead, a tiger skin wrapped around the waist, a serpent as a sacred thread (yajnopavita), and holds attributes like a trishula (trident) and damaru (drum). The left half is the female aspect, Parvati: it has a fuller breast, a more curvaceous hip, long flowing hair, a lotus in hand, a mirror, and is adorned with jewelry, a sari-like garment, and a tilak (vermilion mark) on the forehead. The split is not just physical but extends to ornaments, garments, and even the third eye, which is only on the male side. The right side is often darker (representing Shiva's ascetic, fiery nature) and the left side lighter (representing Parvati's gentle, creative nature).

Behavior & Habitat

Ardhanārīśvara is a symbol of the synthesis of opposites. It is not a deity that 'acts' in a narrative sense, but rather a state of being that embodies the absolute, non-dual reality. Its 'behavior' is the cosmic dance of creation and destruction, the union of purusha (spirit, consciousness, male principle) and prakriti (matter, nature, female principle). As a subject of devotion, it represents the ideal of bhakti (devotion) that transcends gender, and the goal of moksha (liberation) where the soul merges with the divine.

Accounts

The sage Bhringi was a staunch devotee of Shiva alone, refusing to worship Parvati. To teach him that Shiva and Shakti are inseparable, Parvati cursed him to lose all his flesh and blood, reducing him to a skeleton. Shiva, pleased with his devotion, gave him a third leg to stand on. Parvati then, out of compassion, gave him flesh. To further demonstrate their unity, Shiva took the form of Ardhanārīśvara, hoping Bhringi would worship both. However, Bhringi, in his stubbornness, transformed into a beetle and circumambulated only the Shiva half of the deity.

The Ardhanārīśvara form is a visual representation of the primordial cosmic couple. In the beginning, there was only the absolute reality (Brahman), which is both male and female. This reality split into two: Purusha (the cosmic male, consciousness) and Prakriti (the cosmic female, nature). Their union is the source of all creation. Ardhanārīśvara is the state of this union before the split, or the state of ultimate realization after the cycle of creation and dissolution.

While the Nataraja form of Shiva is usually depicted as male, some texts and sculptures show a connection to Ardhanārīśvara. The cosmic dance of Shiva (Tandava) is the dance of creation, preservation, and destruction. When this dance is seen as the dance of the ultimate reality, it is the dance of Ardhanārīśvara, where both the male and female energies are in perfect harmony.

Modern Interpretations

Art Chola Bronze Sculptures Unknown Chola artists 10th-13th century CE direct The Chola period produced some of the most exquisite bronze representations of Ardhanārīśvara, characterized by graceful curves and intricate detail. Art Kushan Sculptures Unknown Kushan artists 1st-3rd century CE direct These are among the earliest surviving depictions, often simpler and more robust than later South Indian works. A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Ardhanārīśvara not as a deity that exists 'out there', but as a mirror held up to the universe. It reflects the idea that everything you see — light and dark, joy and sorrow, creation and destruction — is not a battle between opposites, but a dance. The deity is the dance itself, a single, dynamic, and inseparable whole. It's the ultimate 'yes, and' — not 'either/or'.
