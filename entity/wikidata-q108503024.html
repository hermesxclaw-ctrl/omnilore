Who It Is

Before there was an empire that called itself the House of Sasan, there was a provincial lord who never lived to wear its crown. Pappag — Middle Persian Pābag, New Persian Bābak — is the hinge between Parthian collapse and Sasanian dawn: a priest-prince of Pars whose dream-interpreters told him his grandson would rule the world, and who gambled a small court at Istakhr against the King of Kings.

As constructed in literary sources: Pābag is prudent, pious, and politically astute. In Kārnāmag, he is a dreamer and interpreter of dreams who acts on astrological counsel, arranging his daughter's marriage to Sasan to capture the promised farr for his lineage. In Tabari, he is a loyal temple custodian who becomes a calculating rebel, petitioning Artabanus for his son's succession and then defying him. Powers
Priestly custodianship of the Anahid fire-temple

Tabari and later sources describe Pābag as custodian (or steward) of the fire-temple of Anahid at Istakhr, giving him religious authority and control of temple estates and revenues. This is presented as the economic and ideological base for his revolt.

Limit: Not a supernatural power; administrative-religious office under Parthian overlordship. No evidence he performed miracles or rituals beyond normal priestly duties. Some scholars (Boyce) doubt the priestly title is historical and see it as Sasanian propaganda to link dynasty to priesthood.

Kingship and military command in Pars

After deposing Gochihr, Pābag assumes kingship of Istakhr and Pars, commands local levies, and secures recognition (or toleration) from Artabanus IV for his son Ardashir's succession. In Ferdowsi, he sends Ardashir to the court of Ardavan for training, indicating authority to dispose of forces.

Limit: Authority limited to Pars; remains nominally subordinate to Parthian King of Kings until Ardashir's later rebellion (224 CE). Dies before empire-wide conquest; his revolt is provincial, not imperial. Historical window ca. 208-211 CE only.

Dream interpretation and astrological legitimation

In Kārnāmag, Pābag's power is mantic: he dreams that Sasan's offspring will rule, summons dream-interpreters and astrologers who confirm the farr will pass through his line if he gives his daughter to Sasan. His decision to act on prophecy is presented as wisdom (xrad).

Limit: Literary motif (prophetic dream common in Iranian epic, cf. Cyrus, Ardashir). Not presented as personal supernatural ability but as access to specialists. No other source repeats this dream verbatim.

Dynastic founding and legitimation (farr transfer)

Pābag's chief 'ability' in Sasanian ideology is to transfer legitimate kingship to Ardashir. By being named as grandfather of Shapur I in royal inscriptions, he anchors the Sasanian house in both priestly and Kayanid antiquity, providing charisma that Ardashir exploits.

Limit: Posthumous ideological function; Pābag himself does not wield farr as active power. Historical agency ends at death ca. 211 CE; empire founded by son.

Accounts I
The Prophetic Dream and the Marriage to SasanKārnāmag ī Ardaxšīr ī Pābagān, Chapter 1

Middle Persian text ca. 6th-7th century, reflecting 3rd-century events

Pābag, lord of Pars, dreams that Sasan, a descendant of the Kayanids living in obscurity as a shepherd, will father a world-ruler. He summons interpreters who tell him the glory (xwarrah) will come through his own daughter if she marries Sasan. Pābag therefore gives his daughter in marriage to Sasan, and from this union Ardashir is born. The story establishes Sasanian legitimacy through both Kayanid blood (via Sasan) and Pābag's noble house. Birth of Ardashir Papakan, founder of Sasanian Empire; ideological fusion of priestly Pars and Kayanid antiquity. Sets up Pābag as conscious architect of dynasty.

The Deposition of Gochihr, King of IstakhrAl-Tabari, Ta'rīkh I, pp. 813-814; also Ferdowsi, Shahnameh

9th-10th century compilation of earlier Sasanian chronicle

Pābag, as custodian of the Anahid temple and commander under Gochihr (Gōčihr), king of the Bazrangi of Istakhr, rebels against his overlord. Tabari says Pābag killed or deposed Gochihr and seized Istakhr, then wrote to Parthian King Artabanus IV (Ardavan) asking that his son Ardashir be confirmed as successor. Artabanus, underestimating the threat, grants the request. Pābag becomes de facto king of Pars (ca. 208-211 CE), establishing territorial base for Ardashir's later empire-wide revolt in 224 CE. Marks transition from Bazrangi to Sasanian rule in Fars.

The Elevation of Ardashir over his BrothersKārnāmag ch. 2; Tabari I, p. 814; Shahnameh

6th-11th century literary tradition

Pābag had several sons, notably Shapur (Shapur of Pābag) who was older than Ardashir. Despite primogeniture, Pābag designates Ardashir — son of Sasan's line — as heir, recognizing his superior farr and character. He sends Ardashir to the court of Artabanus at Ctesiphon for education and military training, while Shapur remains in Pars. On Pābag's death, Shapur briefly succeeds but dies in an accident (a wall collapses), allowing Ardashir to claim Pars. Ensures Ardashir's succession despite not being eldest; explains why Sasanian dynasty is named after Sasan/Ardashir not Pābag's elder line. Shapur's accidental death is seen by some scholars as possible assassination narrative sanitized.

Accounts II
Pābag's Death and the Succession Crisis in ParsTabari I, p. 815; Kārnāmag ch. 3

Early Islamic compilation

Pābag dies ca. 211 CE after a short reign (approx. 208-211). His eldest son Shapur succeeds as king of Istakhr but reigns only briefly before dying when a wall or building collapses on him at Darabgerd (or Istakhr, variants differ). Ardashir, then at Darabgerd or at Artabanus's court, marches on Istakhr, defeats his brothers, and assumes rule of Pars, beginning his expansion. Ardashir consolidates Pars, then rebels against Parthia, culminating in Battle of Hormozdgan (224 CE) and Sasanian Empire. Pābag's death is the narrative hinge from local to imperial history.

The Kayanid Genealogy ClaimKārnāmag ch. 1; Ferdowsi Shahnameh introduction to Ardashir; Al-Tabari genealogy

Sasanian and early Islamic period

To legitimize new dynasty against Parthian Arsacids who claimed Achaemenid descent, Sasanian sources trace Pābag and Sasan to the mythical Kayanids — to King Bahman, son of Esfandiyar. Pābag is thus not a parvenu but restorer of ancient Iranian kingship. The story recounts how Sasan, descendant of Bahman, lived in poverty until Pābag recognized his lineage. Provides Sasanian house with antiquity surpassing Arsacids; used on inscriptions and coins. Becomes standard in Shahnameh and Islamic histories.

Pābag in the Byzantine Account of AgathiasAgathias, Histories 2.27

Late Antiquity, ca. 580 CE

Agathias, claiming access to royal Sasanian annals via interpreter Sergius, calls Pābag 'Pabecus' and describes him as father of Ardashir who, being of low birth but ambitious, revolted against Artabanus. Agathias adds a hostile detail that Pābag was a cobbler/astrologer, reflecting either Greek misunderstanding or Parthian hostile propaganda preserved in archives. Provides external, near-contemporary confirmation of Pābag's existence outside Persian tradition; shows how Sasanian origins were presented to foreign courts. The 'cobbler' slur illustrates contested memory.

Pappag/Pābag is the same as Bābak Khorramdin, the 9th-century rebel against the Abbasids. — Two distinct figures separated by 600 years share the New Persian name Bābak (from Middle Persian Pābag). Pābag (d. ca. 211 CE) is Sasanian founder-figure in Fars; Bābak Khorramdin (d. 838 CE) led Khurramite revolt in Azerbaijan. Conflation is common in popular internet lists due to identical transliteration.

Pābag founded the Sasanian Empire. — Pābag seized only Pars (Istakhr) and died ca. 211 CE before empire existed. His son Ardashir I founded the empire by defeating Artabanus IV in 224 CE and conquering Iran. Pābag is dynastic ancestor, not empire founder; Sasanian Empire is correctly called after Sasan/Ardashir, not Pābag.

Pābag was a poor cobbler or shepherd who rose from nothing (as in Agathias's hostile account). — Agathias's 'cobbler/astrologer' detail reflects either Greek misunderstanding or preserved Parthian hostile propaganda. Persian sources and modern scholarship show Pābag was already a wealthy temple lord and Bazrangi noble with army and estates. Low-status origin is not supported by contemporary epigraphy which calls him 'King'.

The Kārnāmag dream story is historical fact. — The prophetic dream is a literary topos (common in Iranian epic for Cyrus, Darius, Ardashir) to demonstrate divine farr. It is ideological fiction to legitimize dynasty via Kayanid descent, not a record of an actual dream. Historians treat it as reconstructed myth, not attested event.
