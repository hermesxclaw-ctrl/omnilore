Who It Is

Fifty daughters, one lion-skin hero, and a king who wanted grandsons more than guests — Polylaus was born into the most audacious breeding project in Greek myth, one of fifty half-brothers sired by Heracles in Thespiae and then scattered across the Mediterranean to become kings, colonists, and footnotes.

No personality, speech, or individual deeds are preserved for Polylaus. The narrative treats the 50 sons as a cohort whose behavior is decided for them: seven retained by Thespius, three sent to Thebes, forty dispatched to Sardinia under Iolaus (Apollodorus 2.7.8). Diodorus 4.29.3-4.30 presents the Sardinian group as obedient colonists who civilize Sardinia. Any reconstruction of Polylaus's character (brave, pious, etc.) would be hypothetical and is not attested. Distinguish: collective obedience vs individual agency — sources give the latter no role.

Heraclid Lineage and Heroic Status

As a son of Heracles, Polylaus possesses the prestige of Heraclid blood, implying exceptional strength, stature, and favor of Zeus/Heracles in colonial contexts. No individual feat of strength is narrated for Polylaus. The Sardinian colonists as a group are credited with civilizing acts (building, agriculture) under Iolaus's leadership (Diodorus 4.30).

Limit: No superhuman power, invulnerability, or divine gift is individually attested. He is mortal; not deified. Any heroic ability is inferred from paternity, not stated.

Oikist (Founder) Potential

If among the forty, Polylaus would share in the oikist role of the Thespiades in Sardinia, where the Iolaans were remembered as founders and received hero honors (Pausanias 10.17). This is a social-religious function rather than a power.

Limit: No city or tribe is named after Polylaus specifically; eponymy is collective (Thespiades / Iolaans). No oracle or foundation ritual is linked to him by name.

Accounts I
The Cithaeronian Lion HuntApollodorus Bibliotheca 2.4.9-10; Diodorus Siculus 4.29.1-2; Pausanias 9.26.6

Archaic myth, recorded Classical to Imperial period

At age eighteen, Heracles, living with King Amphitryon or at Thebes, is tasked with killing the lion ravaging Mount Cithaeron, which preyed on the herds of Amphitryon and Thespius. Thespius, king of Thespiae, hosts Heracles for 50 days during the hunt. Each day Heracles goes out to hunt the lion and each night returns to Thespiae, where Thespius lodges him. This extended hospitality sets the stage for the union with his daughters. Heracles eventually kills the lion (by strangling or weapons, variant) and thereafter wears its skin, prefiguring the Nemean lion motif. The hunt is the narrative justification for Heracles's prolonged stay and access to the daughters. Establishes Heracles's early heroic prowess before the Labours, binds Thespiae to Heracles through guest-friendship (xenia), and provides the etiology for the 50 sons. Thespiae gains Heraclid descendants.

The Night (or Nights) with the Fifty Daughters of ThespiusApollodorus 2.4.10; Athenaeus 13.4 citing Herodorus of Heraclea fr. 17 Fowler; Diodorus 4.29.2; Tzetzes Chiliades 2.221-227

Classical (Herodorus ca. 400 BCE) through Imperial

Thespius, desiring that all his daughters bear children by Heracles, arranges for Heracles to lie with them. In the most common version, Heracles sleeps with all fifty in a single night, believing each time he is with the same woman, or willingly with each. In extended versions, the liaison is spread over 50 nights, one daughter per night during the 50-day hunt. All fifty conceive. One daughter in Herodorus's version refuses and is punished by Heracles to remain a virgin priestess. Eurybia is one of those who does conceive, bearing Polylaus. The episode is framed as Thespius's pious eagerness for Heraclid offspring, not as Heracles's seduction. Birth of 50 sons, the Thespiades/Heraclids, including Polylaus. Creates a pool of Heraclid manpower for later colonization. Establishes Thespian claim to Heracles kinship.

The Birth and Naming of the Fifty ThespiadesApollodorus 2.7.8 (catalogue); Tzetzes Chiliades 2.229-232

Hellenistic/Imperial catalogue tradition

Each daughter bears a son. Apollodorus preserves the most complete catalogue, pairing Polylaus explicitly with Eurybia: 'Polylaus, son of Eurybia.' The sons are raised in Thespiae under Thespius. Their names, many with -laus/-leus/-cles elements, reflect heroic naming fashions. No individual birth omens or childhood deeds are recorded for Polylaus; his birth is part of a collective genealogy. The catalogue functions as a charter for later divisions. Creates a named Heraclid cohort. Polylaus's identity is fixed genealogically, enabling his later assignment to the Sardinian expedition.

Accounts II
The Division of the Sons: Seven, Three, and FortyApollodorus 2.7.8; Diodorus 4.29.3; Tzetzes 2.227-232; Pausanias 9.27.6

Hellenistic/Imperial

When the sons reach maturity, Heracles sends instructions from Mycenae or Thebes (after his Labours begin). He orders Thespius to retain seven sons in Thespiae, send three to Thebes, and send the remaining forty, joined by his nephew-companion Iolaus, to colonize Sardinia. Apollodorus gives the precise division and explicitly includes Polylaus among the forty sent to Sardinia. This tripartition explains Thespian, Theban, and Sardinian Heraclid populations and justifies ritual ties between the cities. Polylaus's mythic destiny is assigned to Sardinia. Establishes the Iolaans as a Heraclid people in the west, with Iolaus as oikist and the Thespiades as his followers.

The Sardinian Colonization with IolausDiodorus Siculus 4.29.3-4.30.3; 5.15.1-6; Pausanias 10.17.5; Strabo 5.2.7

Classical to Imperial, with Hellenistic colonization ideology

Led by Iolaus, nephew and charioteer of Heracles, the forty Thespiades including Polylaus sail to Sardinia. There they subdue or befriend the native inhabitants, build cities and public works, introduce Greek customs, and divide the island's best land. Diodorus describes them building 'fine works' and living free from Carthaginian domination for a time. Pausanias, writing centuries later, says the Iolaans still inhabited the interior and maintained Greek customs and shrines of Iolaus, and that the island's name Ichnusa was linked to them. Polylaus as one of the forty shares in this collective foundation; no individual foundation is ascribed to him. Etiology for Greek cultural claims on Sardinia and for hero cults of Iolaus and the Thespiades there. Explains Thespian-Sardinian kinship. Polylaus transitions from Boeotian prince to western colonist hero.

The Thespian Cult Context and the Refusing Daughter VariantAthenaeus 13.4 citing Herodorus; Pausanias 9.27.3-5 on Thespian Eros; Apollodorus 2.4.10

Classical (Herodorus) to Imperial

Thespiae was famous for the cult of Eros (a primordial stone, later Praxiteles's statue) and for Heracles worship. Herodorus's variant that one daughter refused Heracles and was condemned to remain a virgin priestess serving Heracles for life illuminates Thespian cult practice: a priestess of Heracles is attested epigraphically. While Eurybia is not the refuser, the variant shows the religious frame: the unions are not purely erotic but produce priestly and royal offspring. Pausanias notes Thespians honored Heracles as a god, making his sons semi-divine founders. Provides religious aetiology for a Thespian priesthood of Heracles and explains why 49 rather than 50 sons are sometimes counted in local tradition. Contextualizes Polylaus's birth within cult, not just genealogy.

Polylaus was a famous hero with his own labours or cult like his father Heracles — Polylaus has no individual deeds, powers, or cult attested. He is known only as a name in a genealogical catalogue and as part of a collective colonization group. All fame attaches to the group (Thespiades/Iolaans) or to Heracles and Iolaus, not to him personally.

Heracles seduced or raped the fifty daughters against Thespius's will — Ancient sources present Thespius as the instigator who actively desired Heraclid grandchildren and offered his daughters. Herodorus/Athenaeus even notes Thespius's eagerness. The daughters' consent is not discussed, but the father's will is presented as the driver, not Heracles's predation alone. The one refuser is punished by Heracles, showing coercion, but the overall frame is hospitality politics, not abduction.

The Sardinian colonization by Polylaus and his brothers is historical fact proving Greek settlement of Sardinia in the Bronze Age — No archaeological evidence supports a Mycenaean Greek colonization of Sardinia of this scale. Nuragic Sardinia was an indigenous Bronze Age civilization. The story is a Hellenistic charter myth, possibly created to claim kinship during Carthaginian/Roman contests over the island, as discussed by Diodorus's euhemerism and modern archaeology.
