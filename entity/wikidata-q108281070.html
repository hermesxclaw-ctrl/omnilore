Who It Is

Polylaus never had an epic of his own. He was one of fifty — a son born in a Boeotian king's calculated gamble, when Heracles hunted a lion on Mount Cithaeron and left behind a generation. His name means 'much-people,' yet ancient authors spare him barely a line before dispatching him, with thirty-nine half-brothers and his uncle Iolaus, across the sea to become a colonist on Sardinia.

No attested personality, speech, or moral character for Polylaus. The sources treat the 50 sons as a collective demographic unit for colonization, not as characters. Diodorus and Apollodorus assign agency to Heracles, Thespius, and Iolaus; the sons are objects of instruction ('keep seven, send three to Thebes, forty to Sardinia'). No dialogue, choice, or deed is attributed to Polylaus alone. Reconstructed: as one of the 40 sent to Sardinia, his narrative role is that of oikist (colonist). This is collective, not individual.

Powers
Heraclid lineage (no individual superhuman power attested)

As a son of Heracles, Polylaus belongs to the Heraclidae, who in Greek genealogical thought possessed prestige and potential arete, but no source credits Polylaus with superhuman strength, divine parentage powers, or heroic feats. Unlike his father, he is not described as performing labors, wielding weapons, or possessing divine gifts. His significance is genealogical and colonial, not martial.

Limit: Mortal; no immortality, apotheosis, or cult hero status attested. No invulnerability or special resistance. Not included among the Heraclidae who later claim the Peloponnese (those are descendants of Hyllus).

Oikist / colonist status (collective)

In the Sardinian tradition, the 40 Thespiades including Polylaus become founders of a colony under Iolaus. Diodorus 4.29.5-6 presents this as Heracles' explicit command to Thespius and Iolaus, giving the sons a political function as settlers who Hellenize Sardinia. This is a social role, not a supernatural ability.

Limit: Subordinate to Iolaus as leader; not an independent founder or eponymous hero. No city or tribe named after Polylaus individually.

Accounts I
The Cithaeronian Lion HuntApollodorus 2.4.9-10; Diodorus 4.29.1-2; Pausanias 9.26.6

Mythic time, set one generation before the Trojan War; textual sources Hellenistic to Imperial (1st c. BCE - 2nd c. CE)

A lion ravages the flocks of King Thespius of Thespiae and of Amphitryon on Mount Cithaeron. The young Heracles, aged about 18 and living at Thebes, volunteers to hunt it. Thespius hosts Heracles for an extended stay at Thespiae while the hunt lasts. This hospitality frames the conception of the 50 sons. Polylaus is not yet born but his conception is made possible by this hunt. Heracles kills the lion and wears its skin, a precursor to the Nemean lion motif. Establishes Heracles' early arete and his bond with Thespiae; provides narrative pretext for the mass liaison; explains why Thespius owed Heracles hospitality and why Heracles later entrusts his sons to Thespius and Iolaus.

The Night(s) with the Daughters of Thespius - Conception of PolylausApollodorus 2.4.10; Diodorus 4.29.2-3; Athenaeus 13.556F citing Herodorus; Tzetzes Chiliades 2.221-227

Mythic time; sources Classical to Byzantine

Wishing for grandchildren by Heracles, King Thespius offers his 50 daughters to the hero during his stay. Heracles lies with all 50, begetting a son on each. Apollodorus specifies that Eurybia bore Polylaus. The liaison is presented as consensual hospitality and royal strategy, though modern scholarship notes the daughters' lack of agency. Heracles is said to have believed he lay with the same woman each night, or to have been made drunk, depending on version. Birth of 50 Heraclid sons including Polylaus; creation of a Thespian Heraclid lineage; later justification for Sardinian colonization and Theban-Thespian kinship claims.

Birth and Naming at ThespiaeApollodorus 2.4.10 (roster)

Mythic time; Hellenistic compilation

Eurybia, daughter of Thespius and Megamede (daughter of Arneus) according to Apollodorus 2.4.10, bears Polylaus. The name Polylaus ('much-people') is listed without etymological story or omen. No birth narrative, infancy deed, or childhood episode is recorded for Polylaus individually, unlike for Heracles himself. The 50 sons are raised at Thespiae under Thespius' care. Establishes Polylaus' dual lineage: divine-adjacent paternal (Zeus via Heracles) and royal Boeotian maternal; qualifies him for later colonial dispatch. The collective upbringing explains why the sons are treated as a Thespian cohort rather than Thebans.

Accounts II
The Division of the Sons - Heracles' Instructions to ThespiusApollodorus 2.4.10; Diodorus 4.29.5

Mythic time; Hellenistic-Imperial

Before departing for his Labors or after, Heracles sends a message to Thespius (or instructs him directly) concerning the 50 sons: keep seven at Thespiae, send three to Thebes, and send forty, joined by Iolaus, to Sardinia to found a colony. Apollodorus gives the precise 7-3-40 split. This is the only passage that determines Polylaus' fate: as one of the forty, he is assigned to the Sardinian expedition. No source specifies which seven or three were chosen, so Polylaus' inclusion in the 40 is inferred from his not being otherwise distinguished. Diaspora of the Thespiades; aetiology for Thespian, Theban, and Sardinian Heraclid populations; establishes Iolaus as oikist leader. For Polylaus, it means departure from Boeotia and loss of individual narrative.

The Sardinian Colonization under IolausDiodorus 4.29.6, 4.30.1-3; Apollodorus 2.4.10; Pausanias 9.27.7, 10.17.5; Tzetzes 2.349-351

Mythic time; sources Hellenistic-Imperial, with later elaboration in Strabo 5.2.7 and pseudo-Aristotle Mirabilia

Iolaus, Heracles' nephew and charioteer, leads the forty Thespiades including Polylaus to Sardinia. There they settle, subdue the island, and are called Iolaeis or Iolaens. Diodorus describes them building works, introducing Greek customs, and later being associated with the Nuragic culture. Pausanias adds that Iolaus and the Thespiades' descendants still inhabited Sardinia in his day, and that the Iolaeis retained Greek rites. Polylaus is never singled out as founder of a specific city; his role is collective. Mythic Hellenization of Sardinia; explanation for place names (Iolaia) and for the 'Iolaes' tribe mentioned by later geographers; integration of Thespian Heraclids into western Mediterranean colonial ideology. No return or further deed is recorded for Polylaus.

Thespiae's Memory - Pausanias' VisitPausanias 9.27.1-9.27.7

2nd century CE (contemporary observation of mythic landscape)

Visiting Thespiae centuries later, Pausanias records local traditions: the sanctuary of Heracles, the statue of Eros by Praxiteles, and the story that Thespius had 50 daughters by Megamede and that Heracles fathered sons on them. He notes that the Thespians still honored Heracles and that some Thespiades were sent to Sardinia with Iolaus. This is not a mythic story per se but the reception of Polylaus' myth in situ, showing how Thespiae curated the memory of the 50 sons as civic pride. Preservation of the Thespiad tradition into the Roman period; evidence that the myth remained part of Thespian identity; source for later mythographers.

Polylaus was a famous hero who performed labors or had adventures like his father — No ancient source attributes any deed, labor, power, or adventure to Polylaus individually. He is solely a name in a genealogical list and a member of a collective colonial group. All heroics belong to Heracles or Iolaus.

Polylaus stayed in Thespiae or became king of Thespiae — According to Apollodorus' 7-3-40 scheme, only seven unnamed sons remained in Thespiae; the other 43 were sent away. Polylaus is counted among the 40 sent to Sardinia, not the seven who stayed. No source makes him king.

The 50 daughters of Thespius were willing participants and the episode was a romantic reward — Ancient sources frame the episode as Thespius' political strategy and Heracles' virility marvel, with no attention to daughters' consent. Modern readings highlight the transactional and coercive structure; daughters are not given voice or choice in any source.

Polylaus and his brothers were the Heraclidae who invaded the Peloponnese — The Heraclidae who attempt the Return to the Peloponnese are descendants of Hyllus (son of Heracles and Deianira), a different branch. The Thespiades/Sardinian Heraclids are a separate, Boeotian-Sardinian line with no role in the Dorian invasion myth.

There are statues, vases, or temples dedicated specifically to Polylaus — No archaeological or art-historical evidence links any image or cult site to Polylaus by name. Thespiae had a sanctuary of Heracles and famous Eros statues, but no individual Thespiad cult is attested.
