Who It Is

Somewhere on a heather island in a black lake, a wolf is waiting with a sword jammed between his jaws, and the foam that runs from his mouth is a river named Hope. That is the picture Norse myth leaves us: the gods raised this wolf themselves, fed him with their own hands, watched him grow a day at a time into the prophecy that one of them — the highest of them — would die in his mouth, and then spent the entire age of the world chaining him, cheating him, and gagging him rather than shed his blood on holy ground. One god paid the price of the binding in his own body: Týr, who put his right hand between those jaws as a pledge of good faith, and lost it when the gods broke their word. The wolf understood oaths well enough to demand one, and the gods understood theirs well enough to break it. Powers
Monstrous growth and strength

Raised by the gods themselves, he grows so fast that 'all prophecies declared that he was fated to be their destruction' (Gylfaginning 34). He shatters Lædingr with a single lash and snaps Drómi by straining and kicking, sending fragments flying — feats that became proverbs ('to loose from Lædingr', 'to strike out of Drómi') for anything achieved with enormous effort.

Limit: Brute force fails against Gleipnir, which tightens the harder he fights — the myth's central statement that cunning craft outmatches raw strength. His strength is also bound in time: he cannot act until the fetters break at Ragnarök.

The Ragnarök release and the world-devouring gape

When the bonds burst, he runs free with his lower jaw against the earth and his upper jaw against heaven, 'he would gape yet more if there were room', with fire from eyes and nostrils (Gylfaginning 51). He swallows Odin whole — 'that shall be his ending' — and in the poems the sun itself is his prey (Vafþrúðnismál 46: 'when Fenrir has assailed this one').

Limit: The act is single and fatal: immediately after swallowing Odin he is killed by Víðarr. The swallowing of Odin is the only kill the medieval texts reliably assign to him; the burning of the world belongs to Surtr and the fire-giants, and the article lead's phrase about Fenrir 'assisting in setting the world aflame' reflects the general collapse he participates in, not a fire-power of his own.

Progenitive power: the Ironwood broods

Völuspá 40 (Dronke) has the old giantess of Járnviðr breeding 'the broods of Fenrir', from which comes the moon-snatcher; Grímnismál 39 names Hati as Hróðvitnir's son; Snorri (Gylfaginning 51) makes Sköll and Hati his sons outright. The celestial wolves who eat the sun and moon are his lineage.

Limit: His progeny are cosmic functions (sun-chaser, moon-chaser) rather than a pack; the mother is an unnamed giantess of the Ironwood (sometimes identified with Angrboða by scholars — disputed).

Oath-awareness and cunning

He refuses to be bound without a pledge and insists on a hand in his mouth as a guarantee of good faith — the demand that costs Týr his hand and gives the story its hinge. He also correctly predicts that once bound he will get no help from the gods. This is the one power the gods cannot chain: his suspicion, which they defeat only by finding a volunteer brave enough to be maimed.

Limit: His cunning is real but insufficient: he cannot detect Gleipnir's nature until it is too late, and the text notes the gods laugh while only Týr loses his hand. He is out-thought once, and it costs him the age of the world.

Prophetic weight: the slayer of Odin

Every major source converges on the same fact: the wolf is fated to kill Odin — 'he shall be the slayer of Odin' (Gylfaginning 34), 'the wolf shall fell the father of men' (Vafþrúðnismál 53), 'Odin fares to fight with the wolf... there must fall the joy of Frigg' (Völuspá 53). This is less a power than a doom: the gods know it before the binding and bind him anyway, because they will not stain their sanctuary with his blood (Gylfaginning 34).

Limit: He is himself slain immediately after by Odin's son — the doom is reciprocal. The gods' refusal to kill him on holy ground (a legal-religious scruple, not mercy) is the only thing that lets him survive to Ragnarök.

Oral tradition predating c. 961 (see Hákonarmál); written c. 1220 and c. 1270s

Loki, already married to Sigyn, takes the giantess Angrboða in Jötunheimr and gets three children: the wolf, the serpent Jörmungandr, and the half-living Hel. Prophecy tells the gods this kindred will ruin them — worse for the mother's blood, worse still for the father's — so Odin sends gods to fetch the children. The serpent goes into the deep sea, Hel into Niflheim with dominion over nine worlds, and the wolf the Æsir bring up at home in their own stronghold, where only Týr dares approach to feed him. The wolf grows a day at a time until the gods, reading their own doom in his size, propose a game: test this fetter. Lædingr snaps. Drómi snaps. Fenrir at Ragnarök: the Wolf against the AllfatherPoetic Edda, Völuspá 44–58, Vafþrúðnismál 53; Prose Edda, Gylfaginning 51

Viking Age oral tradition; written 13th century

At the world's end the refrain begins: Garmr howls before Gnipahellir, 'the fetters will burst, and the wolf run free'. The sun darkens — Sköll swallows it, Hati the moon, the stars fall. The earth shakes, trees tear loose, and every bond in the world snaps with the same sound; Fenrir is loose. He comes on with his lower jaw against the ground and his upper jaw against the sky, fire burning from his eyes and nostrils, and beside him the Midgard Serpent vomits venom over air and sea. On the field Vígríðr the hosts meet: Odin rides out in gold helm with Gungnir in his fist — Thor at his other side, unable to help, locked with the serpent — and the wolf swallows the Allfather. That is his ending. The Wolves of the Ironwood: Sköll, Hati, and the Devouring of the LightsPoetic Edda, Völuspá 40, Grímnismál 39, Vafþrúðnismál 46–47; Prose Edda, Gylfaginning 12, 51

13th-century witnesses of older tradition

The völva sees an old woman sitting in the Ironwood east of Midgard who 'bred there the broods of Fenrir', and from all of them comes one that will be a moon-snatcher in troll's skin (Völuspá 40). Grímnismál 39 names the pair: Sköll, who follows the shining god (the sun) to the Ironwood, and Hati Hróðvitnisson, son of the fame-wolf, who waits for the moon. Snorri explains why the sun runs: two wolves are on her track, Sköll behind and Hati leaping before, and from the Ironwood witch's wolf-race will come the Moon-Hound, Mánagarmr, who will swallow the moon and spatter heaven and air with blood (Gylfaginning 12). At Ragnarök the chase ends: Sköll swallows the sun, Hati the moon, and the stars go out — but the sun has already borne a daughter, who will ride her mother's road in the new world, 'ere Fenris drags her forth' (Vafþrúðnismál 47; Gylfaginning 53). The eclipse of the lights is the cosmological overture of Ragnarök, and the renewed world gets a new sun through the very lineage that ate the old one — destruction and renewal share a family. The Wolf in Skaldic Memory: Hákonarmál and the KenningsHeimskringla, Hákonar saga góða (Hákonarmál, Eyvindr skáldaspillir); Prose Edda, Skáldskaparmál 2, 9, 11, 50, 58, 75; Háttatal 56

c. 961 CE and later

Before any manuscript of the Eddas existed, the bound wolf was already a figure of speech. Eyvindr skáldaspillir, mourning King Hákon the Good around 961, swears the Fenris Wolf will run free and ravage the realm of men before Norway sees so good a king again — using the breaking of the bonds as the yardstick of the impossible, which means the binding story was common knowledge in the tenth century. Two centuries of skalds keep the habit: Odin is 'the wolf's enemy' (Egill Skallagrímsson), Týr is 'the feeder of the wolf', Víðarr is 'the slayer of Fenrisúlfr', Hel is 'the monstrous wolf's sister' (Bragi Boddason, 9th century), and 'fenrir' simply becomes a poetic word for wolf or warg. In Snorri's euhemerized epilogue the wolf even becomes history: Fenrisúlfr is Pyrrhus, who killed a king at a temple altar and 'could be said to be a wolf according to their religion' for violating sanctuary — the old myth re-read as a Christian moral about temple-slayers. Is Garmr the same being as Fenrir?Garmr is a separate monster: the hound of Gnipahellir who duels Týr at Ragnarök while the wolf fights Odin (Snorri, Gylfaginning 51 — the only source that stages both).Garmr is Fenrir under another name: Völuspá's refrain ties the howl of Gnipahellir to the bursting of 'the fetters' and 'the wolf run free', and the bound-hound motif maps onto the bound wolf; Orchard (1997:43) argues Sköll, Hati and Garmr 'were originally simply all Fenrir' and that Snorri's tidy distinctions are his own systematizing.
Who devours the sun and moon?Fenrir himself assails the sun: 'from where will a sun come into the smooth heaven when Fenrir has assailed this one?' (Vafþrúðnismál 46).His sons do: Sköll swallows the sun and Hati the moon (Grímnismál 39; Gylfaginning 12, 51); Völuspá 40 gives only a 'moon-snatcher' from his broods.

Why did the gods raise the wolf at all?No reason is given; the text simply says the Æsir brought him up at home while Hel and the serpent were exiled (Gylfaginning 34).Lindow (2001:111–114) floats motives the text leaves open: 'because Odin had a connection with wolves? The Iranian parallel (Puhvel)Fenrir's binding and the swallowing of Odin echo the Zoroastrian tale of Taxma Urupi binding Angra Mainyu (Ahriman), who later swallows him; and Jamshid's hand in the demon's body, withered, parallels Týr's lost hand (Puhvel 1998:118–119).The parallels are typological coincidences of the bound-monster and maimed-hand motifs, which occur across many traditions without implying contact.

Fenrir swallows the sun and the moon at Ragnarök. — In the standard (Snorri's) account his sons Sköll and Hati devour the sun and moon; Fenrir swallows Odin. The poems, however, do name Fenrir as the sun's assailant in Vafþrúðnismál 46, so the popular version is a simplification of a genuine textual wobble, not pure invention.

Garmr and Fenrir are the same wolf, full stop. — That is a scholarly hypothesis (Orchard's, among others), not the text: Snorri stages Garmr and Fenrir as separate combatants in the same battle. The identification is reasonable and ancient-feeling but unproven.

Fenrir was a gentle, wronged creature whom the cruel gods betrayed for no reason. — The medieval texts give him no sweetness and no victimhood: he is a prophesied destroyer whom the gods raised, fed and then deceived. He is also no mere pawn — his own speeches show pride and suspicion. The 'innocent wolf' reading is a modern sympathetic interpretation (strong in Gaiman's retelling and internet discourse), not an attested characterization.

The gods chained Fenrir because he was evil. — The texts say they acted on prophecy ('all prophecies declared that he was fated to be their destruction') and on his alarming growth. He is never described as morally evil in the Eddas; he is dangerous, fated, and hungry. 'Evil' is imported by later moralizing and by alignment-based game design.

Marvel's Fenris (Thor: Ragnarok) is faithful to the myth. — The MCU's Fenris is female, serves Hela (a Marvel invention as his master), and fights the Hulk; the comics' Fenris Wolf is closer (Loki's son, chained with Gleipnir) but still a superhero-franchise character. Neither is evidence about Norse tradition.

Fenrir is a giant (jötunn) in the sources. — He is a wolf — the son of a god and a giantess, and Skáldskaparmál 75 does list his name among jötnar, but every narrative source treats him as a wolf. Calling him a giant is a category blur the medieval texts themselves half-share.

The chain was made of 'six impossible things' as a literal dwarf-forged artifact. — That is exactly what Gylfaginning 34 says — the six ingredients are attested, and Snorri even argues for their impossibility ('thou must have seen that a woman has no beard'). The misconception runs the other way: some modern retellings quietly swap or add ingredients (e.g. 'the sound of a cat's footsteps' is standard, but 'mountain roots' sometimes becomes 'the roots of a mountain' — a translation detail), or treat the list as symbolic invention. It is attested.

Ván means 'hope' and is just a nice river name. — The river Ván is the slaver of a gagged, howling wolf, and its name — 'hope' or 'expectation' — is the myth's grimmest joke: what the gods hope for is that the binding holds. The river is a monument to captivity, not scenery.

Fenrir 'assists in setting the world aflame'. — The burning of the world is Surtr's work. Fenrir's attested role is killing Odin and ravaging in the general collapse; the 'aflame' phrasing in some summaries over-reads his participation in Ragnarök's fire.
