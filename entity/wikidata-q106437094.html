Who It Is

The name Oenoe ripples across the ancient Greek world like a stone skipping over time, touching a dozen places and a handful of myths. But beneath the surface of this simple word lies a tangle of identities: a nymph, a queen, a fortress, a city, a battle—each with its own story, each demanding its own place in the archive.

In the myth of Oenoe of Tegea, she is portrayed as a devoted worshipper of Artemis, but her personality is otherwise not developed in surviving sources.

Transformation

In one myth, Oenoe, daughter of Cepheus, was transformed into a spring by Artemis, either as a reward for her devotion or to save her from rape.

Limit: This is a one-time event, not an ongoing ability.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No detailed physical description of the mythological Oenoe survives. As a nymph or mortal woman, she would have been imagined as beautiful, but specific features are unattested.

Symbols — No known symbols or iconography associated with Oenoe. The name's link to wine might suggest grape or vine imagery, but this is speculative.

Domains — As a nymph, Oenoe is associated with springs and water, particularly the spring at Tegea. As a place, Oenoe is a toponym for various towns and forts across Greece, often in agricultural regions.

Accounts I
The Transformation of OenoePseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.12; Pausanias, Description of Greece 8.47

Mythic (date unknown)

Oenoe was a daughter of Cepheus, king of Tegea, and a priestess of Artemis. When a giant or mortal pursued her, she fled to the temple of Artemis. The goddess either turned her into a spring to save her or, in another version, because Oenoe had neglected her duties. The spring became a local landmark. The spring at Tegea was named after Oenoe, and it became a site of local cult.

Accounts II
The Battle of OenoeThucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War 2.18

460 BC

Athenian forces besieged the Corinthian fort of Oenoe in the Megarid, leading to a battle. The Athenians were victorious, but the fort remained a point of contention. The battle was a minor engagement in the First Peloponnesian War, demonstrating Athenian aggression.
