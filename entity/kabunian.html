Who It Is

High in the Cordillera mountains of northern Luzon, where rice terraces step down the slopes like frozen waterfalls, the supreme deity of the Ifugao and other Igorot peoples is known by many names. To some, Kabunian is the creator who set the world in order; to others, a remote sky god who withdrew after the first ages. Yet in the living traditions of the highlands, Kabunian remains a presence—invoked in rituals, feared in storms, and honored in the rhythms of agricultural life. This is the story of a god who is both one and many, whose identity shifts with the river valleys and the generations that remember him.

Kabunian is generally seen as benevolent but remote. He is a creator and lawgiver who established the social order and the rules of conduct. He is just, punishing wrongdoing with illness, misfortune, or natural disasters, but he is also approachable through prayer and sacrifice. In some myths, he is a culture hero who taught people essential skills, while in others he withdraws from direct involvement after creation.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Kabunian' is believed to derive from a term meaning 'the one above' or 'sky dweller' in various Cordillera languages. The root 'bun-' or 'buni-' relates to the sky or upper region. In some Ifugao dialects, 'kabunian' also refers to the skyworld itself, the realm of the gods.

Pronunciation — kah-BOO-nyan (stress on the second syllable; 'ny' as in 'canyon')

Aliases — Kabulanan, Kabunyan, Kabunian, Lumawig (in some regions), Aponibalagen (in some accounts), Apo Kabunian

Powers
Creation

Kabunian created the world, including the earth, sky, and all living things. He is credited with forming the landscape and establishing the natural order.

Limit: None recorded; creation is a past act.

Lawgiving

Kabunian established the moral and social codes that govern human behavior. He is the source of customary law and the enforcer of taboos.

Limit: Law is transmitted through tradition and ritual.

Control of Weather

Kabunian controls thunder, lightning, and rain. He sends storms as punishment or withholding rain as a sign of displeasure.

Limit: His actions are often mediated by ancestral spirits or other deities.

Bestowal of Blessings

Kabunian grants fertility, good harvests, health, and prosperity to those who honor him properly. He answers prayers and accepts sacrifices.

Limit: Blessings are conditional on proper ritual observance and moral conduct.

Punishment

Kabunian punishes those who break taboos or commit serious offenses, sending illness, death, or natural disasters.

Limit: Punishment is not always immediate; it may be enacted through other spirits.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Descriptions of Kabunian's physical appearance are rare and vary by group. In some myths, he is an old man with white hair, living in the sky. In others, he is invisible or appears in dreams or omens. No standardized iconography exists; he is not typically represented in physical form, but is symbolically associated with the sky, thunder, and lightning.

Symbols — The sky, thunder, lightning, and rain are primary symbols. In agricultural rituals, the rice crop and the cycle of planting and harvest are intimately connected to Kabunian's favor. Some rituals involve offerings placed on elevated platforms or in sacred groves to reach the sky god. The 'bulul' (carved rice god figures) of the Ifugao are sometimes associated with the spirit world that includes Kabunian, but they are distinct entiti

Domains — Kabunian dwells in the sky, often in a specific part of the skyworld called 'Kabunian' itself. He is associated with the upper regions, clouds, and celestial phenomena. His influence extends over the entire earth, but especially over the agricultural cycle and the well-being of the community.

Accounts I
The Creation of the WorldIfugao oral tradition, recorded in Barton's 'The Religion of the Ifugaos'

Pre-colonial to present

In the beginning, there was only the sky and the sea. Kabunian, the sky god, descended and created the earth by throwing down stones, which formed the land. He then created the first people, often from clay or from a bamboo stalk, and taught them how to live. He established the rituals and the social order. The world was ordered, and humans received the knowledge of agriculture and ritual.

The Flood MythBontoc and Ifugao traditions

Pre-colonial to present

Kabunian, angered by human wickedness, sent a great flood to destroy the world. Only a few people survived, often by climbing a mountain or taking refuge in a boat. After the flood, Kabunian repopulated the earth with the survivors. The world was cleansed, and a new generation of humans repopulated the earth.

Accounts II
Lumawig the Culture HeroBontoc oral tradition, recorded by Cole and others

Pre-colonial to present

Lumawig, who is often identified with Kabunian, descended to earth and taught the people how to farm, build houses, and perform rituals. He also established the social classes and the rules of marriage. He eventually returned to the sky, but he continues to watch over the people. The Bontoc received civilization and the proper way to live.

The Origin of RiceIfugao tradition

Pre-colonial to present

In some myths, Kabunian gave rice to the people, or taught them how to cultivate it. The rice terraces are considered a gift from the gods, and rituals are performed to ensure a good harvest. Rice became the staple food and the center of Ifugao life.

Kabunian is the same as the Christian God. — Kabunian is a pre-colonial deity unique to the Cordillera peoples. Christian missionaries sometimes equated the two for conversion purposes, but the original understanding of Kabunian is distinct.

Kabunian is a minor nature spirit. — Kabunian is the supreme deity and creator god in many Cordillera traditions, not a minor spirit.

Kabunian is a Filipino national deity worshipped by all Filipinos. — Kabunian is specific to the Cordillera peoples; other Filipino groups have their own supreme deities, such as Bathala for the Tagalog.

Kabunian is no longer worshipped. — While many Cordillera peoples have converted to Christianity, some communities still maintain traditional rituals and beliefs, and there is a cultural revival movement.
