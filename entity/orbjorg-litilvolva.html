Who It Is

Step into the world of a Viking seeress, where a blue cloak and a staff could unlock the secrets of fate. Þorbjörg lítilvölva, the 'little völva' of Greenland, stands as one of the most vivid figures in Norse literature—her story is a window into the spiritual life of the Viking Age, where magic and prophecy were as real as the cold sea. Discover how a Christian woman's song could please ancient spirits, and how a prophecy shaped the destiny of a family that would later produce bishops.

Powers
Seiðr (prophetic divination)

Þorbjörg was a practitioner of seiðr, a form of Norse magic that involved prophecy, shaping the future, and communicating with spirits. In the saga, she performs a divination ritual to determine when the famine in Greenland will end. She requires a high seat, a special staff, and the singing of varðlokkur (spirit-lock songs) to enter a trance state. She then receives visions from the spirits and predicts that the famine will soon end, and also foretells Guðríðr's future, including her marriage and a great lineage. This ability is central to her role as a völva.

Limit:

Spirit communication

During her ritual, Þorbjörg is able to communicate with supernatural beings, described as 'spirits' or 'disir' (female ancestral spirits). She states that the spirits are reluctant to help because the proper songs are not sung, but once Guðríðr sings the varðlokkur, the spirits are pleased and reveal the information she seeks. This ability to mediate between the human and spirit worlds is a hallmark of völur.

Accounts I
The Prophecy at HerjólfsnesSaga of Erik the Red

c. 1000 CE (setting), 13th century (composition)

During a severe famine in the Norse settlement of Greenland, the people invite Þorbjörg to a farm at Herjólfsnes to perform a divination. She arrives in her ceremonial attire and is given a feast. She asks for women who know the varðlokkur, and only Guðríðr, a Christian woman, knows them. Guðríðr sings the songs, and Þorbjörg enters a trance. She then predicts that the famine will end in the spring, and she also foretells Guðríðr's future: she will marry a man named Þorsteinn, and from their lineage will come a great and glorious family. The prophecy is fulfilled, and Guðríðr becomes an ancestor of later bishops. The famine ends as predicted, and Guðríðr's future unfolds as foretold, including her marriage and her son Snorri, who is born in Vinland. The story reinforces the power of völur and the importance of proper ritual. It also highlights the interaction between pagan and Christian elements, as Guðríðr, a Christian, participates in a pagan ritual.

Accounts II
The Arrival of the SeeressSaga of Erik the Red

c. 1000 CE (setting), 13th century (composition)

When Þorbjörg arrives at the farm, she is received with great honor. The farmer Þorkell greets her and leads her to a high seat prepared for her. She is served a special meal of porridge and goat's milk, and she is given a place of prominence. The saga describes her clothing in detail, and she asks about the household's needs. This story establishes her authority and the community's reliance on her. It also shows the social role of völur as itinerant specialists who were both feared and respected. The arrival sets the stage for the divination ritual. It demonstrates the hospitality and reverence shown to völur, and it provides a vivid picture of Norse pagan practices. The story also introduces the tension between Christianity and paganism, as Guðríðr is a Christian who participates in the ritual.
