Þorbjörg lítilvölva

Thorbjorg the Little Völva, The Seeress, Wearer of the Blue Cloak

also known asThorbjorg the Little VölvaThe SeeressWearer of the Blue CloakThorbjorg the SeeressLítilvölva
TypeVölva / Seeress / Prophetess
CultureIcelandic
Who It Is
Form

The Saga of Erik the Red provides an exceptionally detailed description of Þorbjörg's attire and appearance, which is unique among saga characters. She wore a blue cloak, fastened with straps and adorned with precious stones, covering her from head to toe. Around her neck she wore a necklace of glass beads, and on her head a black lambskin hood lined with white cat fur. She carried a staff, known as a völva's staff, which was adorned with brass and set with stones at the top. Her shoes were made of calfskin with long laces, and on her hands she wore gloves of cat fur, white and furry inside. This ensemble is rich in symbolic meaning, with the blue cloak possibly representing the otherworldly or the sea, and the cat fur linking to the goddess Freyja, who was associated with cats and seiðr. Her appearance is often cited as the most complete depiction of a völva's ceremonial dress in Norse literature.

Powers

Seiðr (prophetic divination) Þorbjörg was a practitioner of seiðr, a form of Norse magic that involved prophecy, shaping the future, and communicating with spirits. In the saga, she performs a divination ritual to determine when the famine in Greenland will end. She requires a high seat, a special staff, and the singing of varðlokkur (spirit-lock songs) to enter a trance state. She then receives visions from the spirits and predicts that the famine will soon end, and also foretells Guðríðr's future, including her marriage and a great lineage. This ability is central to her role as a völva.

Spirit communication During her ritual, Þorbjörg is able to communicate with supernatural beings, described as 'spirits' or 'disir' (female ancestral spirits). She states that the spirits are reluctant to help because the proper songs are not sung, but once Guðríðr sings the varðlokkur, the spirits are pleased and reveal the information she seeks. This ability to mediate between the human and spirit worlds is a hallmark of völur.

Accounts

During a severe famine in the Norse settlement of Greenland, the people invite Þorbjörg to a farm at Herjólfsnes to perform a divination. She arrives in her ceremonial attire and is given a feast. She asks for women who know the varðlokkur, and only Guðríðr, a Christian woman, knows them. Guðríðr sings the songs, and Þorbjörg enters a trance. She then predicts that the famine will end in the spring, and she also foretells Guðríðr's future: she will marry a man named Þorsteinn, and from their lineage will come a great and glorious family. The prophecy is fulfilled, and Guðríðr becomes an ancestor of later bishops.

When Þorbjörg arrives at the farm, she is received with great honor. The farmer Þorkell greets her and leads her to a high seat prepared for her. She is served a special meal of porridge and goat's milk, and she is given a place of prominence. The saga describes her clothing in detail, and she asks about the household's needs. This story establishes her authority and the community's reliance on her. It also shows the social role of völur as itinerant specialists who were both feared and respected.

Scholarly Debates
Historical existence vs. literary fiction
Þorbjörg was a historical seeress who lived in Greenland around 1000 CE, and the saga preserves a genuine account of her. ⇌ Þorbjörg is a literary creation, invented by the saga author to add authenticity and drama to the narrative, and she never existed.
Meaning of 'lítilvölva'
The epithet means 'little völva' referring to her physical stature, as she may have been a small woman. ⇌ It means 'lesser völva' indicating a lower status compared to more powerful seers, or it could be a term of endearment.
What We Don't Know

The exact historical accuracy of Þorbjörg's depiction is unknown, as the saga was written centuries after the events. The lack of archaeological evidence for völva graves in Greenland makes it difficult to confirm her existence. Additionally, the full ritual of seiðr is not completely understood, and the varðlokkur songs are not preserved. The relationship between völur and Christianity is complex, and Þorbjörg's interaction with Guðríðr raises questions about religious syncretism. Further research could explore comparative seeress traditions in other Germanic cultures.
