Who It Is

Pandora is the first woman in Greek mythology, created by the gods as a punishment for humanity after Prometheus stole fire. Zeus ordered Hephaestus to fashion her from clay, and each Olympian bestowed a gift: beauty (Aphrodite), fine clothing and weaving skill (Athena), a deceitful mind and persuasive speech (Hermes). Her name means 'All-Gifted.' She was given a jar (pithos) — not a 'box,' a mistranslation by Erasmus — which she opened, releasing all the evils of the world: disease, toil, sorrow, and death. Only Hope (Elpis) remained inside.

Origins

After Prometheus stole fire, Zeus devised a punishment for humanity that would be beautiful, irresistible, and catastrophic. He ordered Hephaestus to mix earth and water and fashion a creature in the likeness of an immortal goddess — the first woman. Athena gave her fine clothing, a silver dress, a veil, and a golden crown worked with figures of every creature. Aphrodite gave her grace, beauty, and the power to inspire desire. Hermes gave her 'a shameless mind and a deceitful nature' — the capacity for lies and persuasive speech. The Graces and Persuasion adorned her with golden necklaces. She was called Pandora ('All-Gifted') because all the gods had given her gifts. Pandora was sent to Epimetheus ('Afterthought'), Prometheus's foolish brother. Prometheus had warned Epimetheus never to accept gifts from Zeus, but Epimetheus, dazzled by Pandora's beauty, took her in. Pandora came with a jar (pithos) — a large storage vessel, not a 'box' (which entered tradition through Erasmus's 16th-century Latin translation, 'pyxis'). Whether she was forbidden to open it or whether it was simply there is unclear from Hesiod. But she lifted the lid, and out flew all the evils that plague humanity: disease, hard labor, sorrow, envy, old age, and death. Only Hope (Elpis) remained — caught beneath the lid when she closed it. Whether Hope stayed to comfort humanity or was prevented from escaping to join the other evils has been debated since antiquity. In some readings, Hope is the one comfort mortals retain; in others, it is the final, most insidious evil — the delusion that things will improve. Hesiod's version is profoundly misogynistic: 'From her comes the race of women... the deadly race and tribe of women who live among mortal men.' But later interpretations have reclaimed Pandora as a figure of curiosity — the first scientist, the first questioner, whose 'sin' was wanting to know.

Attested in Oral Tradition / Hesiod's Works and Days / TheogonyEra Archaic Greece (c. 700 BCE)

Form

Pandora is depicted as a beautiful young woman, often emerging from the earth (called anodos — 'rising up') — a reference to her creation from clay. In classical art, she appears on the famous 'Pindar Cup' (5th century BCE) being fashioned by Hephaestus and Athena. In later art, the Pre-Raphaelites — Rossetti, Waterhouse — painted her as a luminous, melancholy figure, often holding the opened box, her expression hovering between curiosity and tragic awareness. Sensory evocation: the scent of wet clay, the gleam of golden gifts, and the terrible silence that follows when all the evils have been released.

Creation by the Gods Pandora was not born but made. Hephaestus molded her from earth and water, Athena breathed life into her and clothed her, Aphrodite gave her grace and desire, and Hermes gave her a 'shameless mind' and 'deceitful nature'. She is a synthetic being, a divine artifact, but also a mortal woman. Hesiod, Works and Days; Theogony She is mortal and has no special powers beyond those given to her. Her power lies in her appearance and her deception, which are gifts from the gods. None. All sources agree she was created by the gods.

The Jar of Evils Pandora was given a jar (pithos) by the gods, with instructions not to open it. When she did, she released all the evils that now plague humanity — sickness, toil, sorrow, death. She closed the lid just in time to keep Elpis (Hope) inside. Hesiod, Works and Days (the only classical source for the jar narrative) This is a one-time event, not a power she wields. The jar is a divine artifact, not a personal possession. The jar is a pithos (a large storage jar) in Hesiod, but is often depicted as a box (pyxis) in later art and literature. This is a mistranslation or reinterpretation that has become dominant in popular culture.

Behavior & Habitat

Pandora's behavior is defined by a single act: opening the jar. Was it curiosity, disobedience, fate, or divine manipulation? Hesiod does not dwell on her motivation — she is a tool of Zeus's punishment, not a fully developed character. Later interpretations have made her more sympathetic: a woman given gifts she didn't ask for, sent into a world she didn't understand, and blamed for consequences she couldn't foresee.

Weaknesses & Wards

Pandora's curiosity is both her defining trait and her fatal flaw. She could not resist looking — and in looking, she doomed humanity. She is, in Hesiod's telling, a trap: beautiful on the outside, containing evil within. But her vulnerability is also her humanity: she was created to fail, given every gift except wisdom, and then blamed for the inevitable result.

Accounts

After Prometheus steals fire from the gods and gives it to humanity, Zeus plots his revenge. He commands Hephaestus to mold a beautiful woman from earth and water, and the gods each bestow a gift upon her: Athena gives her skill and clothing, Aphrodite gives her grace, and Hermes gives her a deceitful mind. She is named Pandora, 'All-Gifted'. Hermes brings her to Epimetheus, who accepts her despite his brother Prometheus's warning never to accept gifts from Zeus. This act seals humanity's fate.

Pandora, driven by curiosity, opens the great jar (pithos) she was given. Out fly all the evils and miseries that now afflict humanity — sickness, sorrow, toil, envy, and death. She quickly claps the lid back on, but it is too late; only Elpis (Hope) remains inside. This is why, Hesiod says, there is no escape from evil for mortals, and why hope is both a comfort and a delusion.

Field Notes

☘ Pandora did NOT open a 'box' — the original Greek was pithos (a large storage jar). The error was introduced by Erasmus in the 16th century and became permanent.

☘ Pandora's name means 'All-Gifted' — a reference to the divine gifts she received, but also an ambivalent title: she is 'all-giving' as the source of all human troubles.

☘ Some scholars argue that Hope (Elpis) remaining in the jar is not a comfort but the final cruelty — the delusion that things will improve, preventing humanity from accepting its fate.

☘ Pandora was created as a punishment for humanity after Prometheus stole fire — making her the mythic counterpart to Eve in Genesis.

☘ The Pre-Raphaelite painter Dante Gabriel Rossetti created multiple studies and paintings of Pandora, depicting her as a luminous, melancholy figure.

Cultural Significance

Pandora's myth is the Greek version of the 'origin of evil' story, comparable to Eve in Genesis. Both stories feature a first woman whose actions bring suffering into the world, and both have been used to justify misogyny for millennia. The 'Pandora's box' metaphor has become universal, describing any action that unleashes uncontrollable consequences. In feminist scholarship, Pandora has been reinterpreted as a victim of divine patriarchy — created by gods, blamed by men, and defined by her relationship to male figures (Epimetheus, Prometheus, Zeus). The mistranslation of pithos (jar) to pyxis (box) is itself a fascinating case study in how textual errors shape cultural memory. In philosophy and literature, Pandora's myth has inspired reflections on curiosity, knowledge, hope, and the human condition — from Goethe to Nietzsche to modern fiction.

Modern Interpretations

Pandora appears in Rick Riordan's Percy Jackson series as 'Pandora's pithos' — a recurring artifact. In the God of War series, Pandora's Box is central to the plot of the first game and God of War III. In the 2009 film Avatar, the planet Pandora is named after her — a world of both beauty and danger. In the TV series Supernatural, Pandora's Box appears as a powerful artifact. In the Fate/Grand Order mobile game, Pandora appears as a character. In countless works of fiction, 'opening Pandora's box' serves as the inciting incident. The myth has been adapted in children's literature, graphic novels, and modern feminist retellings that present Pandora not as the villain but as a curious, complex figure whose 'sin' was wanting to understand the world.

Scholarly Debates
The Jar vs. The Box
Hesiod says Pandora opened a pithos, a large storage jar. ⇌ Popular culture depicts her opening a small box (pyxis).
Evidence for A: Hesiod, Works and Days, line 94: 'the great jar' (pithos).
Evidence for B: Erasmus, Adagia (1508), mistranslated pithos as 'pyxis' (box), and this error became standard in European art and litera
What was in the Jar?
Hesiod says the jar contained all evils, which were released to plague humanity. ⇌ Some later sources (e.g., Babrius) say the jar contained blessings, which flew away, leaving only hope.
Evidence for A: Hesiod, Works and Days, line 100-105.
Evidence for B: Babrius, Fable 58.
Who opened the Jar?
Pandora opened the jar out of curiosity. ⇌ Epimetheus opened it, or a satyr did (in Sophocles' version).
Evidence for A: Hesiod, Works and Days, line 94.
Evidence for B: Fragments of Sophocles' Pandora; later scholia.
Was Pandora a Goddess?
Pandora was the first mortal woman, created by the gods. ⇌ Pandora was originally a chthonic earth goddess, a form of Gaia, whose myth was later demoted to that of a mortal woman by Hesiod.
Evidence for A: Hesiod, Works and Days.
Evidence for B: The 'Anesidora' inscription on a vase, which links Pandora to earth-goddess imagery; scholarly theories from the 19th an
What We Don't Know

The lost Sophocles satyr play 'Pandora' would provide crucial evidence for a different, possibly comic, tradition. The 'Anesidora' vase inscription needs further study to determine its relation to the Hesiodic myth. The full history of the 'box' vs. 'jar' error in European art and literature has not been comprehensively documented. The reception of Pandora in non-Western cultures (e.g., Japanese anime, which often uses her as a trope) is a significant gap in the research.

A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine you were created yesterday. You're the first of your kind — the first woman. You were made by gods as a punishment for someone else's crime. You were given every gift: beauty, skill, speech — everything except wisdom and warning. You were handed a jar and told nothing about it. You opened it — because you were curious, because you wanted to understand, because nobody told you not to. And suddenly, every evil in the world — disease, death, suffering — flew out of that jar, and you realized you'd been set up. You are Pandora: the woman who has been blamed for three thousand years for doing what any curious person would do. And the thing that stayed in the jar? Hope. Make of that what you will.
