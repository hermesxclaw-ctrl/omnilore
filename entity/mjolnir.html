Who It Is

It is the humblest object in Asgard's armoury and the most consequential: a roughly forged iron hammer whose handle came out too short — blinked into existence when a dwarf's vision was blurred by a biting fly, and yet powerful enough that the giants' own sagas knew it by the skulls it had smashed. Mjölnir is the coiled paradox at the centre of the Norse cosmos: a crude workman's tool that sanctifies weddings, resurrects eaten goats, consecrates the funeral pyre of a slain god, and carries the thunder-god to his prophesied mutual doom at Ragnarök. It smashes Hrungnir's skull into fragments and, moments after a Ragnarök that destroys the world, is handed to Thor's sons so the reborn earth may still be guarded. Few mythic objects do so much: kill a god-killer serpent, found a priesthood of storm, and — in the twentieth century — become one of the most recognisable emblems on the planet.

As an object, Mjölnir has a personality defined by function and bondage to Thor: it is a reliable, never-failing weapon that always returns; it is also a sacred consecrating instrument spanning birth, marriage, and death. Its two registers — devastating weapon and hallowing implement — coexist without contradiction in the sources.

Powers
Unfailing strike

Brokkr tells the assembled gods that Mjölnir 'would be able to strike as heavily as he liked, whatever the target, and the hammer would not fail' — it never misses and no armour or jötunn can withstand it. Thor smashes the skulls of many jötnar with it, and in the Hrungnir duel it shatters the giant's whetstone and crushes Hrungnir's skull into fragments.

Limit: Described as near-unstoppable in combat; its one narrative defect is the short handle, a consequence of Loki's sabotage during its forging.

Return to hand

Brokkr specifies that if Thor throws the hammer, 'it would never miss, and never fly so far that it would not find its way back to his hand.'

Limit: None stated in the Eddas; the return is reliable.

Size change to pocket-size

Mjölnir 'was so small that it could be kept inside his shirt' when desired, per Brokkr's description of its properties.

Limit: Not elaborated in sources beyond the claim.

Sanctification and hallowing

Mjölnir is used as a sacred instrument to bless or hallow: to revive Thor's eaten-and-disassembled goats Tanngrisnir and Tanngnjóstr (Gylfaginning); to consecrate Baldr's ship-funeral pyre; and in Þrymskviða the captured hammer is to be laid in 'the bride's' lap by the goddess Vár to sanctify the marriage. Davidson summarises that the hammer's power 'covered birth, marriage, and death, burial, and cremation ceremonies, weapons and feasting, travelling, land-taking, and the making of oath between men' — a protection against evil.

Limit: Its consecrating power serves the community's well-being but is inseparable from Thor's wielding.

Lightning / storm instrument

The hammer is associated with thunder and lightning (the 'lightning-maker' etymology). Per Adam of Bremen, Thor 'governs thunder and lightning, winds and storms, fine weather and fertility.' Magnus the Strong's removal of the 'hammers of Jupiter' indicates temple instruments were used to imitate the crashing of the sky, showing the hammer's role as a storm-symbol.

Limit: The Kvinneby amulet invokes 'the lightning' holding evil away from Bofi, linking hammer to lightning-protection.

Worthiness enchantment (Marvel, adaptation-specific)

In the Marvel Comics/MCU continuity, Mjolnir is forged from the fictional metal uru and enchanted so that only 'those who are worthy' may lift it; it also grants flight, enhanced weather control, dimensional travel, and always returns when thrown. Odin's enchantment 'Whosoever holds this hammer, if he be worthy, shall possess the power of Thor.'

Limit: The worthiness test is the defining constraint — unworthy beings (including Thor himself in storylines like The Unworthy Thor) cannot lift it; Jane Foster temporarily wields it when Thor is deemed unworthy.

Accounts I
The Forging of Mjölnir (Loki's Wager)Prose Edda, Skáldskaparmál

Pagan-era tradition, written c. 1220

Loki cuts the goddess Sif's hair as a prank. Thor seizes Loki and threatens to break every bone in his body unless he finds a remedy. Loki goes to the svartálfar (dwarfs), and for him the Sons of Ivaldi make three treasures: Sif's gold hair, Freyr's ship Skíðblaðnir, and Odin's spear Gungnir. Loki then wagers his head with the dwarf Brokkr that his brother Eitri cannot make three objects of equal quality. As Eitri works, a fly ('Loki in disguise', by implication) bites him three times — on the arm, the neck, and finally the eyelid, drawing blood that blinds his vision mid-strike. Even so Eitri produces Gullinbursti (the golden boar), Draupnir (the multiplying ring), and finally forges an iron hammer: Mjölnir. The gods Odin, Thor, and Freyr judge Mjölnir the best of all the objects, 'but there was this defect in it that the end of the hammer was rather short.' The Theft of the Hammer (Þrymskviða)Poetic Edda, Þrymskviða

Eddic poem, pagan-era roots

Thor wakes and finds Mjölnir missing. Loki flies (using Freyja's feather cloak) to Jötunheimr and learns the jötunn Þrymr has buried it eight leagues deep and will only return it if Freyja is given to him as wife. Freyja is enraged and refuses. The gods hold a thing (assembly); Heimdallr proposes dressing Thor as a bride, which Thor indignantly rejects before Loki persuades him that without the hammer the jötnar will soon settle Asgard. Thor, cross-dressed in bridal gear with Brísingamen, rides with a disguised Loki as his 'maid' to Jötunheimr. Þrymr is startled by 'Freyja's' ravenous eating (one ox and eight salmon, three casks of mead) and by his bride-to-be's fiery, sleepless eyes; Loki covers each with excuses. Þrymr calls for Mjölnir to be brought to 'sanctify the bride' in the name of the goddess Vár. Thor, seeing his hammer, laughs internally, seizes it, strikes Þrymr dead, beats the gathered jötnar, and reclaims his weapon. Thor and Hymir Fishing for the World SerpentPoetic Edda, Hymiskviða; Gylfaginning retelling

Eddic, pagan-era roots

Thor and the god Týr travel to the jötunn Hymir's hall to win a great cauldron for Ægir's ale-feast. Thor rips the head off Hymir's best ox for bait, and the two row out to sea. Thor baits a line with the ox-head and hauls up the monstrous world-encircling serpent Jörmungandr, striking it violently on the top of its head with Mjölnir. In the Gylfaginning retelling, Thor throws the hammer and strikes off the serpent's head — though the enthroned figure 'Third' (and Snorri's recension) casts doubt on whether the serpent truly died, noting it may still live coiled around the world (a concession to the Ragnarök prophecy where serpent and Thor slay each other). Conflicting registers: an early triumph of the hammer over the world-serpent vs. the Ragnarök account in which the serpent survives, only for Thor to kill it at Ragnarök while perishing from its venom nine steps later.

Accounts II
The Duel with HrungnirProse Edda, Skáldskaparmál; Haustlöng

Skaldic/Prose Edda

The boastful jötunn Hrungnir, drunk in Asgard, is confronted by Thor, who appears with his hammer raised. They agree to meet in single combat at the border at Grjótúnagarðar. Hrungnir brings a whetstone as his only weapon. Thor hurls Mjölnir; Hrungnir hurls the whetstone. Mjölnir splits the whetstone in two — one half becoming the ancestor of all whetstones, the other lodging in Thor's head — while Mjölnir smashes Hrungnir's skull into fragments. Thor's victory at the cost of a whetstone splinter embedded in his own head — a detail of persistent discomfort for the god; the duel is preserved in skaldic verse (Haustlöng of Þjóðólfr of Hvinir).

Mjölnir at Ragnarök and Its InheritanceVöluspá; Vafþrúðnismál; Gylfaginning

Eddic

At Ragnarök, Thor battles the world serpent Jörmungandr a final time and slays it with Mjölnir, but is only able to take nine steps before collapsing dead, poisoned by the serpent's venom. In a reborn world that rises green and fertile, Thor's sons Móði and Magni appear holding their father's hammer — inheriting Mjölnir. Vafþrúðnismál (Vafþrúðnir's riddle) likewise records that Móði and Magni will wield Mjölnir after Ragnarök. The hammer outlives its first wielder, passing to Thor's sons to protect the renewed world — cementing Mjölnir as the enduring symbol of divine guardianship.

Consecrating Baldr's Funeral PyreGylfaginning; Baldr material

Prose Edda

At the ship-funeral of the slain god Baldr, Thor consecrates the burning ship (the ship Hringhorni) with his hammer. In a disturbing aside, a dwarf named Litr runs before Thor's feet, and Thor kicks him into the flames, where he burns to death. Establishes Mjölnir's sanctifying role over death and funerary rites — and illustrates the hammer's wielder's casual lethal cruelty toward a dwarf.

Mjölnir can only be lifted by the 'worthy' (the Marvel rule). — The worthiness enchantment is a Marvel Comics invention (from the 1962 Lee/Kirby comic). In the Norse sources the hammer is simply Thor's all-powerful weapon that returns to hand and can never miss; there is no worthiness test. Norse Thor is a blunt, mighty, often crude figure, not a morally pure one.

Mjölnir is forged from uru, a magical Asgardian metal. — In the Norse sources Mjölnir is forged from iron by the dwarfs Eitri and Brokkr; no special metal 'uru' is mentioned. 'Uru' and the near-indestructibility properties are Marvel inventions.

Thor's hammer is called a 'lucky charm' or purely a peace symbol. — Its modern use as a pendant and blessing symbol is real (and Heathenry's most common sacred artefact), but its primary Norse function is lethal combat — smashing the skulls of giants. The blessing/hallowing function coexists with, and does not replace, the weapon's violence.

The swastika was always a symbol of Thor's hammer in ancient times. — The identification of the swastika with 'Thor's hammer' is largely a late Icelandic folk-belief transfer ('By early modern times...'), though some scholars (Davidson) argue for an ancient connection. It is not a straightforward historical fact, and the modern swastika's Nazi association is entirely unrelated to Norse tradition.
