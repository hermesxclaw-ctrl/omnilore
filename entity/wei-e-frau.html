Who It Is

Imagine walking through the dimly lit corridors of a medieval castle, when suddenly a figure in a flowing white gown appears before you, her eyes hollow, her keys jingling softly. You know that within days, someone in the royal family will die. This is the Weiße Frau, the White Lady of the Hohenzollerns, a ghost that has haunted the German nobility for centuries. But is she a benevolent ancestress or a curse? And what does her legend tell us about the fears and beliefs of the past?

The Weiße Frau is typically a silent, solemn figure who appears only to members of the royal family or their close attendants. She does not speak, but communicates through gestures, such as pointing or beckoning. Her appearance is always a harbinger of death, usually of a family member, and she may appear several days or weeks before the event. In some accounts, she is seen weeping or wringing her hands, and her presence is accompanied by a chill in the air or the sound of rustling fabric. Powers
Death Premonition

The Weiße Frau has the ability to foresee and announce the death of a member of the royal family. Her appearance is an infallible omen, and the death typically occurs within a short period, often within a week or a month. She does not cause the death but merely predicts it, and her presence is taken as a solemn warning. This power is not active but rather a passive manifestation of her spectral nature, as she is bound to the family's fate.

Limit:

Spectral Intangibility

As a ghost, the Weiße Frau can pass through walls and doors, and she is not bound by physical constraints. She can appear and disappear at will, often vanishing when approached or when the observer tries to touch her. This ability allows her to traverse the corridors of castles and appear in locked rooms, making her presence all the more eerie.

Limit:

Emotional Influence

Her presence induces a profound sense of dread and melancholy in those who see her. Even if she does not speak, her appearance can cause witnesses to feel a chill, a sense of impending doom, or a deep sorrow. This emotional influence is part of her omen, as it prepares the family for the coming loss.

The White Lady of PlassenburgGerman folklore, chronicled in various regional histories

Medieval (circa 13th-15th century)

The most famous legend of the Weiße Frau is associated with the Plassenburg castle in Kulmbach, Bavaria. The story goes that Agnes of Orlamünde, a noblewoman, fell in love with a Hohenzollern prince, Albrecht the Fair. When she asked him to marry her, he jokingly said that he would only marry her if she had four eyes (i.e., if she were wealthy enough to have four eyes, a reference to a coat of arms). Misinterpreting this as a demand for her children's eyes, Agnes killed her two children with a needle. When the prince learned of this, he rejected her, and she was later executed for her crime. After her death, she is said to have appeared as the Weiße Frau, wandering the halls of Plassenburg and other Hohenzollern castles, foretelling deaths. This legend is often cited as the origin of the Hohenzollern White Lady, though historical records suggest Agnes was actually executed for a different crime, possibly infanticide, but the details are murky. Accounts II
The White Lady at the Berlin PalaceCourt chronicles and memoirs of the Hohenzollern court

17th-19th century

Numerous accounts from the Hohenzollern court describe sightings of the Weiße Frau in the Berlin Palace. One notable account is from the reign of Frederick William I (1713-1740), who reportedly saw the ghost in his chambers shortly before his death. The ghost was described as a tall woman in a white dress, holding a bunch of keys, who stood at the foot of his bed and pointed towards the door. Frederick William I, known for his piety, took this as a sign of his impending death and began to prepare his soul. Another account from the 19th century tells of a lady-in-waiting who saw the Weiße Frau in the corridor leading to the royal apartments, and within a week, the King of Prussia, Frederick William IV, fell ill and died. These sightings were taken seriously by the court, and the ghost was often mentioned in letters and diaries as a harbinger of death. The sightings reinforced the belief in the Weiße Frau as a death omen, and the ghost became a fixture in Prussian court culture. The White Lady of the HabsburgsAustrian folklore and court records

16th-20th century

The Habsburg family also had a tradition of a White Lady, sometimes identified with the ghost of a woman named 'Weiße Frau' who was said to have been a mistress of a Habsburg emperor. The ghost was reported at the Hofburg Palace in Vienna and at the Schönbrunn Palace. One famous sighting was in 1889, shortly before the death of Crown Prince Rudolf at Mayerling. A guard at the Hofburg claimed to have seen a white figure gliding through the corridors, and this was later interpreted as a premonition of the tragic event. The Habsburg White Lady was also said to appear before the death of Empress Elisabeth (Sisi) in 1898, though this is less well-documented. The Habsburg version is similar to the Hohenzollern, but with a more tragic and romantic overtone, reflecting the family's history of suicides and assassinations. The sightings added to the mystique of the Habsburg dynasty and were often used as evidence of the family's cursed fate.
