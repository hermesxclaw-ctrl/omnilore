Atum

Who It Is
Form

Atum is most often shown anthropomorphic — a striding or enthroned man wearing the divine tripartite wig or the pschent, the dual red-and-white Double Crown of Upper and Lower Egypt, reinforcing his kingship connection. In the Netherworld Books (Amduat and related) he appears as an old man leaning on a staff, an emblem of the aging evening sun. He is also rendered as a serpent (the form he resumes at the end of the cosmic cycle), and more occasionally as a mongoose, lion, bull, lizard, or ape; solar contexts show him as a scarab (Khepri-Atum), and cosmic/primeval contexts identify him with the benben mound itself. In the Graeco-Roman period he could be shown as a standing ape holding a bow and arrow.

Powers

Self-origination / auto-creation Atum created himself from the inert darkness of the primeval waters (Nun), the first being to emerge where nothing had existed. He is the source of all the kas (vital force) of every other being — in a key theological claim, everything in existence is said to be one of the kas of Atum, who originally contained all of creation within himself. Heliopolitan cosmogony; Pyramid Texts Self-creation is presented as an emergence from prior raw cosmic matter (Nu) rather than a creation of matter itself; he is a first mover of the manifest world, rivalised by Ptah (Memphis) and by Amun (Thebes) in other theologies. Memphite theology makes Atum a product of Ptah; Theban theology subsumes him into Amun.

Progenitive generation (sneeze, spit, hand, shadow) Atum produced the first divine couple, Shu (air) and Tefnut (moisture), through bodily acts: sneezing them out (a pun on the verb for 'sneeze' and Shu's name), spitting them out (a pun linking 'spit', tef, to Tefnut), through masturbation — the hand of the act personified as a goddess, the Hand of Atum, equated with Hathor, Iusaaset and Nebethetepet — or by a union with his own shadow. His hand could also be treated allegorically as his wife from her Heliopolitan beginning. Heliopolitan cosmogony; Pyramid Text 527; Coffin Texts; Bremner-Rind Papyrus The different mechanisms (sneeze vs spit vs masturbation vs shadow) are variant myths, not a single coherent account. Some accounts insist the twins came from his lips rather than by saliva/semen; others through the hand.

Sovereignty over the solar cycle (evening/aged sun) Atum governs the sinking, evening, and nocturnal register of the sun, entering the underworld each night as the aged old man leaning on a staff. In the binary solar theology Khepri-Atum spans dawn and dusk — the scarab-headed young god and the aging serpent-god together enclosing the complete daily circuit of morning and evening. Netherworld Books (Amduat); New Kingdom solar theology In the solar frame Atum is specifically the evening/primordial aspect; Ra governs the day and Khepri the dawn, so his solar power is bounded to the twilight/chthonic phase. As Ra-Atum he absorbs the daytime role, blurring the earlier division.

Lifting of the dead king to the stars In the Old Kingdom, Atum was believed to lift the dead king's soul from his pyramid up to the starry heavens — a funerary/astral power that made him a guardian and escort of the royal deceased through the afterlife journey. Old Kingdom Pyramid Texts / royal funerary theology Later Book of the Dead material reorients this toward individual (non-royal) afterlife aid. The double-headed serpent / celestial ferryman imagery overlaps with Shu and with the general solar barque theology.

End-of-time dissolution (the finisher) As 'the finisher of the world', Atum holds the prerogative to unmake creation. In the Coffin Texts he tells Osiris that after millions of years all will return to nothingness except the primeval waters; only Atum and Osiris will remain, as serpents, and Atum will dissolve everything he made back into Nun. He is accordingly the god of post-existence as much as of pre-existence. Coffin Texts (Atum–Osiris dialogue) States a cyclical dissolution and renewal rather than an absolute extinction; Osiris shares the survival register with him. Some modern paraphrases read this as a 'cosmic apocalypse', but the cycle implies a potential re-beginning.

Chthonic defence against Apophis (as Ra-Atum) In the nocturnal register, Ra-Atum confronts and defends against Apophis, the chaos-serpent of darkness, who lies below the horizon each night and must be subdued for the sun to be reborn. Images from the tomb of Ramesses I show Atum facing Apophis; the ritual 'Book of Overthrowing Apophis' encodes spells and wax-model rituals to aid the solar god's victory. New Kingdom Netherworld funerary art; Book of Overthrowing Apophis Apophis is never permanently destroyed, only repelled each night — the battle is cyclical, not once-for-all. Set (not Atum) is the more commonly named active defender of the solar barque; Atum's role is the aged evenin-sun frame from which the night-journey begins.

Accounts

In the beginning there was only Nun, the formless, lifeless primeval waters. From this darkness Atum emerged, self-created, settling upon the benben mound that rose first from the flood — a mound he could himself embody. Alone in the void, he generated the first divine couple out of his own body: Shu (air, 'emptiness') and Tefnut (moisture), produced through sneezing, spitting, or the masturbatory union of his hand with his own substance, with the hand personified as a goddess. The brother-sister pair gave rise to Geb (earth) and Nut (sky), who in turn produced Osiris, Isis, Set and Nephthys — completing the nine-god Ennead, whose eight lesser gods and all other things are finally extensions of Atum.

After Atum produced Shu and Tefnut, the curious siblings wandered out into the surrounding darkness of the primeval waters and disappeared. Grieved beyond bearing, Atum despatched his fiery Eye (the Eye of Ra) as a messenger to find and bring them back. When the eye returned them safely, Atum wept tears of joy — and those first tears became the first human beings. The story knots together his loneliness, his generative power, and his role as ancestor not only of the gods but of humanity.

Against the Heliopolitan version, the priests of Memphis taught that Atum and the whole Ennead were themselves brought into being by Ptah through an act of creative intelligence rather than physical generation. Ptah conceived the world in his heart (the seat of thought) and spoke its names with his tongue, producing the gods and all things. In this theology Atum is not the prime mover but the first product of Ptah's intellectual creation — a deliberately subordinated placement of the Heliopolitan creator within Memphite order.

Osiris asks Atum what will become of the world when it finally ends, and Atum answers that he will destroy everything he has made and return it to the waters of Nun, where he first came to be. After millions of years, only Atum himself and Osiris would remain, in the form of serpents, and the rest of creation would cease to exist outside the primeval waters. The passage ranks among the earliest sustained apocalyptic speculations in Egyptian literature, cast in the voice of the creator who is also the finisher.

As the aged evening sun, Atum(-Ra) descends into the underworld each dusk, where the chaos-serpent Apophis — the enemy of light and Maat — lurks below the horizon and must be subdued before the sun can rise again. Tomb art (notably that of Ramesses I) depicts the grey-bearded Atum confronting Apophis. Priests supported this victory through the annual Ritual of Overthrowing Apophis, crafting wax effigies of the serpent to be spat upon, mutilated and burned while spells were recited, and keeping the diagnostic Book of Overthrowing Apophis for the dismantling, fettering, and fire-immolating of the enemy.

Content Notes

Heliopolitan cosmogony explicitly describes Atum producing Shu and Tefnut through masturbation, the hand of the act personified as a female goddess (the Hand of Atum, equated with Hathor, Iusaaset or Nebethetepet). This bodily, taboo-violating framing of divine creation is one of the most striking — and, to later audiences, embarrassing — features of Egyptian theology, frequently sanitised or bowdlerised in popular retellings in favour of the 'cleaner' sneeze/spit versions.

Egyptologists read the various production-mechanisms (sneeze, spit, hand, shadow) as evolved pun- and theology-driven variants, not as degradation. The personified Hand illustrates the feminine principle within a self-sufficient male creator — a significant and non-pejorative theological idea.

Heliopolitan cosmogony; Iusaaset article; Pyramid Texts

In the Coffin Texts, Atum tells Osiris he will unmake everything, returning creation to the waters of Nun; only he and Osiris survive, as serpents, after millions of years. The affirmation of total and explicit cosmic dissolution is a dark counterweight to his role as benevolent creator — the same god who makes the world is the one who will end it.

Interpreted as a cyclical eschatology (return to the primeval sea as a phase, potentially re-beginning), not a nihilistic annihilation. Still, the stark 'everything ceases to exist except the waters' register is genuinely unsettling.

Coffin Texts (Atum–Osiris dialogue); main Atum article

To ensure the solar victory each night, priests performed the Ritual of Overthrowing Apophis, crafting wax effigies of the chaos-serpent that were spat upon, named for defilement by the left foot, speared, fettered, knifed, and burned while spells were recited. The liturgy (Book of Overthrowing Apophis) stages a detailed dismemberment and disposal of the enemy — graphic, quasi-violent ritual enacted on an image, feared lest the image itself confer power.

A standard Egyptian apotropaic ritual practice against chaos, comparable across ancient Near Eastern execration conventions; psychologically and religiously intense but not actual physical violence against people.

Apophis article; Book of Overthrowing Apophis

As the creator of the Heliopolitan Ennead, Atum was routinely absorbed into a modern orientalist template of Egyptian 'sun worship' that flatten the polytheistic, internally-debated theology into a single reductive picture, and that reframed bodily creation myths as 'primitive' fantasy. This historiographical flattening erased the doctrinal rivalry (Ptah, Amun, Aten) that the Egyptians themselves maintained.

Modern Egyptology (e.g. Allen, Wilkinson, te Velde tradition) reads the plural cosmogonies as genuine contemporaneous theology, countering the old single-narrative treatment.

Historiographical critique; creation-myths multiplicity recorded in primary texts

Heliopolis' great Temple of Ra-Atum was systematically stripped over the centuries — obelisks removed to Rome (Vatican obelisk, Montecitorio), London and New York (Cleopatra's Needles), and the remaining structures quarried for medieval Cairo's construction. Almost nothing of the primary cult site survives above ground except the Senusret I obelisk. The site, now buried 15–20 m beneath modern Cairo suburbs, is a loss not of Atum's doing but a quiet monument to how fully an once-supreme god's cult was dismantled.

The dispersal of Heliopolitan obelisks to Rome and the West is continuous with broader patterns of colonial-era appropriation of Egyptian monuments; the Vatican obelisk and Cleopatra's Needles remain prominent Western landmarks whose Heliopolitan provenance is a colonial story in the background of Atum's history.

A way to hold it in your mind

Before there was a sky, before there was even a world to stand on, there was only dark water — and rising alone out of it, the first being to exist anywhere: an old, weary god who made himself, made the universe out of his own sneeze and spit and stillness, and who one day — by his own quiet admission — intends to take it all back. Atum, 'the Complete One' of Heliopolis, is the least famous of Egypt's great gods and arguably the most consequential: every other god, every king, and every human being in the Egyptian cosmos descends from him, and he holds the key to the end of the world he began.
