Who It Is

The Almas (plural form; the name means 'wild man' in Mongolian) is a cryptid hominid reported from the remote mountainous and desert regions of Mongolia and the Altai. It is described as a bipedal, ape-like creature standing 5 to 6 feet tall, covered in reddish-brown or dark hair, with a human-like face, prominent brow ridges, and a flat nose. Unlike the Yeti or Bigfoot, Almases are reportedly more docile and curious than aggressive. Mongolian herders and nomadic peoples have reported sightings for centuries, describing them as shy creatures that live in caves, steal food from camps, and occasionally attempt to mimic human behavior. The creature entered Western scientific literature through the Russian naturalist Ivan Poliakov in 1881, who collected secondhand accounts. In the 1950s and 1960s, Soviet-era expeditions led by researchers like Boris Porshnev and Marie-Jeanne Koffmann investigated Almases, collecting dozens of eyewitness accounts from across Mongolia. Some researchers, including the French anthropologist Bernard Heuvelmans, suggested the Almas might represent a surviving population of Neanderthals or Homo erectus. In Mongolian shamanic tradition, the Almas is sometimes considered a nature spirit or a guardian of remote places rather than a flesh-and-blood animal. The legend persists in modern Mongolia, and occasional 'sightings' are still reported from the Altai Mountains and the Gobi Desert.
