Who It Is

A returning spirit or animated corpse in European medieval folklore — a dead person who rises from the grave to haunt the living. The word derives from Old French 'revenant' ('returning'). Revenants were widely documented across medieval Europe: in 12th-century England, William of Newburgh chronicled cases of corpses climbing out of graves to terrorize villages, spreading plague and beating people to death. Unlike modern zombies, medieval revenants were often reported as having a ruddy complexion (suggesting blood-drinking or continued life), were fully sentient, and specifically targeted their family and neighbors. The Church's response was formal exorcism and reburial, often with the body facing downward or decapitated. The revenant bridges the zombie tradition (reanimated corpse) with the vampire (blood-drinking, nocturnal activity).

Form

Attested: In Icelandic sagas, draugar are emphatically physical. They inhabit their burial mounds or houses, described as swollen, heavy, and often larger than in life, with dark or blue‑black skin suggestive of decay and cold. Glámr in Grettis saga is terrifyingly big, corpse‑pale, and glaring; other draugar are so weighty that living men struggle to move them. Some accounts note a stench of rot and the creaking of bones, reinforcing their status as animated corpses rather than transparent ghosts. Reconstructed: Later folklore and modern fantasy exaggerate these traits into “Norse zombies”—armoured skeletons or hulking cadavers with glowing eyes—drawing on but stylizing the saga imagery.

Corporeal undeath The draugr is an animated corpse retaining physical solidity, weight, and the ability to interact violently with the living—wrestling, striking, crushing, and blocking passage. Unlike a ghost, it can be grappled, dismembered, and burned. Icelandic sagas (e.g., Grettis saga, Eyrbyggja saga); summarized in Guerrero’s thesis and modern reference articles. Requires a preserved body and a grave or locus of attachment; once the corpse is destroyed (decapitated, burned, or otherwise ritually neutralized), the draugr ceases to function. Some later Christianized tales blur draugar with demonic apparitions or purely spiritual hauntings, creating tension between corporeal and incorporeal models of revenants.

Superhuman strength and size Draugar often exhibit strength beyond that of living humans, able to overpower seasoned warriors and move heavy objects. They may grow larger during confrontations, becoming giant‑like opponents. Saga narratives (Grettis saga’s Glámr episode; Eyrbyggja saga hauntings) and modern syntheses. Strength is localized to the draugr’s body and immediate environment; heroes can still defeat them through skill, courage, and ritual knowledge. Not all draugar are described as gigantic; some are simply heavy and stubborn, suggesting a spectrum rather than a fixed rule.

Treasure‑guarding and territorial haunting Many draugar guard treasure or property in their mounds, attacking anyone who attempts to rob or disturb them. They may haunt farms they once owned, enforcing their claim from beyond the grave. Icelandic sagas and later folklore; codified in modern reference discussions. Attachment appears bound to specific sites or possessions; removing or ritually redistributing the treasure can weaken the draugr’s motive. Some stories emphasize vengeance or spite more than treasure, suggesting multiple motives (property, honour, unfinished business).

Cursing and spreading misfortune Draugar can curse their opponents, as Glámr does to Grettir, causing long‑term bad luck, fear, or social isolation. They may also bring disease, accidents, or general misfortune to communities they haunt. Grettis saga (Glámr’s curse) and broader saga motifs; interpreted in Guerrero’s analysis as linked to hamingja (personal luck). Curses are narratively potent but ultimately finite; heroes can outlive or counteract them through other deeds or divine favour. Not all draugar curse; some simply kill or frighten, raising questions about whether cursing is a core trait or a narrative embellishment.

Shapeshifting and magical influence (disputed extent) Some sources and later folklore attribute shapeshifting (into animals or mist) and broader magical powers to draugar, including weather manipulation or psychic influence. Norwegian sea‑draugr traditions and modern secondary syntheses; less explicit in core Icelandic sagas. Often adaptation‑specific and not consistently attested in early texts; may reflect conflation with trolls, ghosts, or other beings. Scholars debate whether shapeshifting is an original draugr trait or a later folkloric expansion influenced by Christian demonology and broader European ghost lore.

Behavior & Habitat

Attested: Draugar are restless, aggressive, and intensely territorial. They guard treasure in their mounds, attack intruders, harry local farms, kill livestock, and sometimes ride roofs or prowl at night. Encounters often culminate in wrestling matches, where the draugr’s superhuman strength tests the hero’s courage and physical prowess. They may curse opponents, spread disease, or cause accidents, and they rarely negotiate; they embody unresolved grudges, possessiveness, and the refusal to accept death. Reconstructed/interpretive: Scholars read draugar as embodiments of social anxiety—about improper burial, broken obligations, or dishonourable deaths—where the dead enforce norms by punishing the living who fail them.

Accounts

A farm is plagued by a terrifying revenant, Glámr, a former worker who died under suspicious circumstances and rose as a draugr. Grettir the Strong confronts him in a brutal night‑time struggle. Glámr’s corpse‑like body is huge and heavy; the fight spills outdoors, where Grettir finally forces the draugr onto a rock and cuts off his head. Before dying, Glámr curses Grettir with fear of the dark and ill‑fortune, shaping the hero’s fate for the rest of the saga.

After a series of deaths and accidents at Fróðá farm, the dead begin to return. Corpses sit in the high‑seat, drip seawater, and silently join gatherings, terrifying the living. The hauntings escalate until a priest and local leaders exhume the bodies, burn them, and perform Christian rites, finally ending the plague of revenants.

A drowned sailor or fisherman becomes a sea‑draugr, haunting the waters where he died. He appears as a half‑rotted figure or a ghostly boat, luring ships onto rocks or capsizing them in storms. Those who see him are warned of impending disaster—or become victims themselves.

Content Notes

Heroes wrestle with heavy, decaying bodies, break bones, and decapitate draugar, then burn or otherwise destroy the remains to prevent further haunting. These acts are both heroic and gruesome, reflecting a willingness to desecrate graves in the name of communal safety.

Guerrero and other scholars interpret such violence as evidence of genuine fear of the returning dead and as ritualized attempts to enforce final death.

Grettis saga (Glámr episode), Eyrbyggja saga, and related saga material.

Glámr’s curse on Grettir inflicts lifelong fear of the dark and misfortune, effectively weaponizing trauma. Draugar can thus harm not only bodies but minds and reputations.

Read as an exploration of hamingja (luck) and the social consequences of being marked by the undead.

Grettis saga.

Stories and archaeological interpretations suggest that some grave‑mounds were opened not just for robbery but to “kill” their undead inhabitants, involving decapitation, pinning bodies, or removing heads.

These practices are linked to broader European fears of revenants and to legal attempts to regulate necromancy and grave disturbance.

Guerrero’s thesis (discussion of irregular burials and law codes against raising the dead).

What We Don't Know

This dossier focuses on Icelandic sagas, Norwegian folklore, and major modern references available in English. It does not exhaustively survey all Old Norse poetic uses of ‘draugr’ or every regional sea‑draugr tradition, and it relies on secondary summaries for some Norwegian material rather than direct access to all ethnographic collections. Precise dating and manuscript variants for individual saga episodes are not fully detailed here, and some etymological reconstructions remain debated among linguists. Sexual dimensions of draugr lore, hinted at in some scholarship, are under‑represented in widely accessible sources and would require closer reading of Old Norse texts beyond the scope of this summary.

A way to hold it in your mind

If a classic Hollywood zombie, a territorial ghost, and a grumpy mountain troll were fused into one stubborn personality, you’d get something close to a Norse draugr: a physically present, emotionally charged revenant who refuses to leave his mound, his treasure, or his grudges behind—and whose story says as much about the living community’s fears as it does about the dead themselves.
