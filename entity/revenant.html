Who It Is

Imagine a grave‑mound on a windswept Icelandic hillside. The villagers swear they hear something moving inside—heavy, deliberate, like a man who refuses to admit he is dead. When the hero finally breaks in, he doesn’t find a ghost but a swollen, blue‑black corpse that stands up, grabs him, and tries to drag him back into the dark. That stubborn, wrestling dead man—the draugr—is the Norse answer to the question: what happens when death doesn’t quite stick?

Attested: Draugar are restless, aggressive, and intensely territorial. They guard treasure in their mounds, attack intruders, harry local farms, kill livestock, and sometimes ride roofs or prowl at night. Encounters often culminate in wrestling matches, where the draugr’s superhuman strength tests the hero’s courage and physical prowess. They may curse opponents, spread disease, or cause accidents, and they rarely negotiate; they embody unresolved grudges, possessiveness, and the refusal to accept death. Powers
Corporeal undeath

The draugr is an animated corpse retaining physical solidity, weight, and the ability to interact violently with the living—wrestling, striking, crushing, and blocking passage. Unlike a ghost, it can be grappled, dismembered, and burned.

Limit: Requires a preserved body and a grave or locus of attachment; once the corpse is destroyed (decapitated, burned, or otherwise ritually neutralized), the draugr ceases to function.

Superhuman strength and size

Draugar often exhibit strength beyond that of living humans, able to overpower seasoned warriors and move heavy objects. They may grow larger during confrontations, becoming giant‑like opponents.

Limit: Strength is localized to the draugr’s body and immediate environment; heroes can still defeat them through skill, courage, and ritual knowledge.

Treasure‑guarding and territorial haunting

Many draugar guard treasure or property in their mounds, attacking anyone who attempts to rob or disturb them. They may haunt farms they once owned, enforcing their claim from beyond the grave.

Limit: Attachment appears bound to specific sites or possessions; removing or ritually redistributing the treasure can weaken the draugr’s motive.

Cursing and spreading misfortune

Draugar can curse their opponents, as Glámr does to Grettir, causing long‑term bad luck, fear, or social isolation. They may also bring disease, accidents, or general misfortune to communities they haunt.

Limit: Curses are narratively potent but ultimately finite; heroes can outlive or counteract them through other deeds or divine favour.

Shapeshifting and magical influence (disputed extent)

Some sources and later folklore attribute shapeshifting (into animals or mist) and broader magical powers to draugar, including weather manipulation or psychic influence.

Limit: Often adaptation‑specific and not consistently attested in early texts; may reflect conflation with trolls, ghosts, or other beings.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Attested: In Icelandic sagas, draugar are emphatically physical. They inhabit their burial mounds or houses, described as swollen, heavy, and often larger than in life, with dark or blue‑black skin suggestive of decay and cold. Glámr in Grettis saga is terrifyingly big, corpse‑pale, and glaring; other draugar are so weighty that living men struggle to move them. Some accounts note a stench of rot and the creaking of bones, reinforcing their statu

Symbols — Attested: Key iconographic motifs include the burial mound, grave goods (weapons, jewellery, treasure), and the wrestling hero confronting a corpse‑like figure. Decapitation, burning, and reburial function as ritual symbols of final death. In some texts, Odin’s epithet Draugadróttinn (“ruler of the dead”) links draugar conceptually to his governance of slain warriors. Modern iconography: Horned or winged helmets, runic inscriptions, and frost‑rim

Domains — Attested: Draugar are strongly associated with burial mounds (haugar), grave sites, and sometimes royal halls or farms they once owned. Sea‑draugar in Norwegian tradition haunt coastlines and wreck sites, luring sailors to doom. Their “domain” is the liminal boundary between the living community and the grave, especially places where wealth, land rights, or honour are contested. Accounts I
Grettir and the revenant GlámrGrettis saga Ásmundarsonar

13th–14th century saga literature

A farm is plagued by a terrifying revenant, Glámr, a former worker who died under suspicious circumstances and rose as a draugr. Grettir the Strong confronts him in a brutal night‑time struggle. Glámr’s corpse‑like body is huge and heavy; the fight spills outdoors, where Grettir finally forces the draugr onto a rock and cuts off his head. Before dying, Glámr curses Grettir with fear of the dark and ill‑fortune, shaping the hero’s fate for the rest of the saga. Grettir gains fame for defeating a powerful revenant but becomes haunted by fear and bad luck, illustrating the double‑edged nature of victory over the undead. The episode establishes a template for draugr encounters: physical combat, decapitation, and the lingering spiritual cost of confronting the restless dead.

Accounts II
The hauntings at FróðáEyrbyggja saga

13th century saga literature

After a series of deaths and accidents at Fróðá farm, the dead begin to return. Corpses sit in the high‑seat, drip seawater, and silently join gatherings, terrifying the living. The hauntings escalate until a priest and local leaders exhume the bodies, burn them, and perform Christian rites, finally ending the plague of revenants. The story dramatizes the clash between older grave‑cult beliefs and Christian burial practices. It shows draugar as a collective threat requiring communal action and ritual expertise, not just heroic wrestling.

Sea‑draugr and the doomed sailorNorwegian coastal folklore (various oral traditions)

Early modern to modern oral tradition, recorded in ethnographic collections

A drowned sailor or fisherman becomes a sea‑draugr, haunting the waters where he died. He appears as a half‑rotted figure or a ghostly boat, luring ships onto rocks or capsizing them in storms. Those who see him are warned of impending disaster—or become victims themselves. Sea‑draugar embody maritime fear and the dangers of the North Atlantic. They serve as cautionary figures about respect for the sea and proper burial of the drowned.
