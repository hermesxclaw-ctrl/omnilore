Who It Is

Before the storm god, before the sky god, before even the gods of the underworld, there was Alalu — a king who sat on the throne of heaven for nine short years, until his own cup-bearer rose up and cast him into the dark. He is the first domino in a cosmic chain of coups, a primordial ruler whose only legacy is the violence he set in motion. In the broken cuneiform tablets of the Hittites, he is the ghost at the beginning of the world, a king whose defeat sparks a cycle of castration, monstrous births, and world-ending giants. He is the reason the gods fear their own servants, and the reason the storm god Teshub looks over his shoulder. Alalu is the forgotten first act of a drama that nearly destroyed the universe.

Depicted as a primordial ruler who is defeated and overthrown. His behavior is largely passive in the surviving fragments; he is a narrative catalyst rather than an active hero. He flees after his defeat and later seeks revenge through his daughter's offspring.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Likely derived from a Hurrian root, possibly connected to the exclamation 'alalu' used in Hittite ritual songs, or to a word for a wild ram or mountain sheep. The exact etymology is disputed among Hittitologists.

Pronunciation — ah-LAH-loo

Aliases — Alala, Alālu, Allu

Ownership — Hurrian and Hittite mythology (Anatolia, Bronze Age, ~2nd millennium BCE)

Primordial Kingship

As the first king of the gods, Alalu held sovereign authority over the divine realm for a period of 9 years.

Limit: His reign was not eternal; he was overthrown by his cup-bearer Anu (the sky god).

Progenitor of Dynasties

His bloodline, through his daughter and her son Kumarbi, becomes the vehicle for challenging the supremacy of the storm god Teshub. His lineage is thus a source of cosmic conflict.

Limit: His power is indirect and exercised through his descendants.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No physical description survives in the cuneiform texts. He is only described as a primordial king of the gods. Later iconographic speculation is not supported by evidence.

Symbols — No symbols or iconography are attested. He is a figure of mythic narrative, not cult.

Domains — Primordial heavens (as king of the gods). After his overthrow, he descends to the 'Dark Earth' (the underworld).

Accounts I
The Song of Emergence (Kingship in Heaven)CTH 344

Late Bronze Age (c. 1400-1200 BCE)

In the beginning, Alalu was king of the gods in heaven, and Anu was his cup-bearer. After 9 years, Anu rebelled, defeated Alalu, and forced him to flee 'to the Dark Earth.' Anu then became king, with Kumarbi (Alalu's son, or grandson through his daughter) as his cup-bearer. After another 9 years, Kumarbi rebelled against Anu. In the ensuing struggle, Kumarbi bit off Anu's genitals and swallowed them. This act made Kumarbi pregnant with the storm god Teshub (and his siblings). Kumarbi spat out the semen, but Teshub was born from the 'good part' of Anu's body and eventually overthrew Kumarbi to become the supreme god. The narrative thus establishes a succession of divine kings: Alalu → Anu → Kumarbi → Teshub. Alalu's role is to be the first, the origin point of the cycle. Establishes the Hurrian/Hittite divine succession myth and the supremacy of Teshub. Alalu's flight to the underworld sets the stage for his later indirect revenge.

Accounts II
The Song of Ḫedammu (Alalu's Revenge)CTH 348

Late Bronze Age

Alalu, nursing his grievance in the underworld, plots revenge against the storm god Teshub. He persuades his (unnamed) daughter to seduce the Sea (the god of the sea). From this union is born Ḫedammu, a monstrous sea dragon. Ḫedammu grows to terrifying size and threatens to destroy the gods and the world. The gods are powerless against him until the goddess Šauška (Ištar) uses her beauty and magical arts to seduce and weaken him, allowing Teshub to defeat him. Alalu's plan for vengeance nearly succeeds, demonstrating his continued malevolent influence even in defeat. The story explains the origin of a sea-dragon adversary for the storm god.

The Song of Ullikummi (The Stone Giant)CTH 345

Late Bronze Age

After the failure of Ḫedammu, Kumarbi (Alalu's descendant) plots again. He impregnates a great rock with his seed, and she gives birth to Ullikummi, a giant made of diorite stone. The gods are unable to see or hear him at first. He grows on the shoulder of the giant Upelluri, threatening to crush the heavens. The gods are initially defeated, and Teshub is nearly in despair. Finally, the god Ea (Enki) discovers the weakness: Ullikummi is anchored to Upelluri's shoulder. Ea cuts him loose from his foundation, and the giant is destroyed. Alalu's line thus continues to generate existential threats to the divine order. The myth explains the final and most dangerous challenge to Teshub's rule, which is only overcome through the wisdom of Ea.

Alalu is a Hittite god. — He is a Hurrian god, whose myths were adopted and translated by the Hittites.

Alalu is a demon or an evil god. — He is a primordial king of the gods. His actions are motivated by a desire to regain his lost throne, which is a political, not a moral, struggle. He is not depicted as 'evil' in the texts.

Alalu is the Hurrian equivalent of the Greek Cronus. — While his story has parallels with the Greek succession myth, the more direct parallel is between Kumarbi and Cronus. Alalu's role is closer to that of Uranus (the primordial sky god who is overthrown).
