Form

No physical description survives in the cuneiform texts. He is only described as a primordial king of the gods. Later iconographic speculation is not supported by evidence.

Powers

Primordial Kingship As the first king of the gods, Alalu held sovereign authority over the divine realm for a period of 9 years. Song of Emergence (Hittite) His reign was not eternal; he was overthrown by his cup-bearer Anu (the sky god). None, but the nature of his kingship (absolute vs. first-among-equals) is not detailed.

Progenitor of Dynasties His bloodline, through his daughter and her son Kumarbi, becomes the vehicle for challenging the supremacy of the storm god Teshub. His lineage is thus a source of cosmic conflict. Song of Emergence, Song of Ullikummi His power is indirect and exercised through his descendants. None.

Accounts

In the beginning, Alalu was king of the gods in heaven, and Anu was his cup-bearer. After 9 years, Anu rebelled, defeated Alalu, and forced him to flee 'to the Dark Earth.' Anu then became king, with Kumarbi (Alalu's son, or grandson through his daughter) as his cup-bearer. After another 9 years, Kumarbi rebelled against Anu. In the ensuing struggle, Kumarbi bit off Anu's genitals and swallowed them. This act made Kumarbi pregnant with the storm god Teshub (and his siblings). Kumarbi spat out the semen, but Teshub was born from the 'good part' of Anu's body and eventually overthrew Kumarbi to become the supreme god. The narrative thus establishes a succession of divine kings: Alalu → Anu → Kumarbi → Teshub. Alalu's role is to be the first, the origin point of the cycle.

Alalu, nursing his grievance in the underworld, plots revenge against the storm god Teshub. He persuades his (unnamed) daughter to seduce the Sea (the god of the sea). From this union is born Ḫedammu, a monstrous sea dragon. Ḫedammu grows to terrifying size and threatens to destroy the gods and the world. The gods are powerless against him until the goddess Šauška (Ištar) uses her beauty and magical arts to seduce and weaken him, allowing Teshub to defeat him.

After the failure of Ḫedammu, Kumarbi (Alalu's descendant) plots again. He impregnates a great rock with his seed, and she gives birth to Ullikummi, a giant made of diorite stone. The gods are unable to see or hear him at first. He grows on the shoulder of the giant Upelluri, threatening to crush the heavens. The gods are initially defeated, and Teshub is nearly in despair. Finally, the god Ea (Enki) discovers the weakness: Ullikummi is anchored to Upelluri's shoulder. Ea cuts him loose from his foundation, and the giant is destroyed. Alalu's line thus continues to generate existential threats to the divine order.

Content Notes

Alalu is overthrown by his cup-bearer, Anu. This sets a precedent for all subsequent divine successions in the cycle, which are all violent.

This motif of violent succession is common in ancient Near Eastern myth and reflects a political reality of coups and usurpation.

Song of Emergence

Kumarbi bites off Anu's genitals and swallows them, becoming pregnant with the storm god. This is a graphic act of sexual violence that is central to the myth.

The act of castration is a powerful symbol of the transfer of power and the creation of a new generation of gods. It is a direct parallel to the Greek myth of Cronus castrating Uranus.

Scholarly Debates
Kumarbi's Genealogy
Kumarbi is Alalu's son. ⇌ Kumarbi is Alalu's grandson (son of Alalu's daughter).
Evidence for A: Some translations of the text imply a direct father-son relationship.
Evidence for B: Other readings suggest Kumarbi is the son of Alalu's daughter, which is a common motif in the cycle.
Alalu's Role
Alalu is a passive figure who simply flees after his defeat. ⇌ Alalu is an active antagonist who orchestrates revenge from the underworld.
Evidence for A: In the Song of Emergence, he is only mentioned as fleeing.
Evidence for B: In the Song of Ḫedammu, he is the instigator of the plot, proving he is not merely passive.
What We Don't Know

A significant gap is the lack of any surviving Hurrian-language version of the Song of Emergence. The Hittite text is a translation, and the nuances of the original Hurrian may be lost. Additionally, no cultic evidence for Alalu has been found, suggesting he was purely a mythic figure. The exact genealogy of Kumarbi is also uncertain due to the fragmentary nature of the text. Further research could explore the possible connections between Alalu and other primordial deities in neighboring cultures.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Alalu as the 'first draft' of a cosmic power structure. He's the CEO who gets fired by his own assistant (Anu), who is then fired by his own assistant (Kumarbi), who is then fired by his own son (Teshub). Alalu doesn't go quietly; he becomes the 'consultant' in the underworld, plotting a hostile takeover by hiring a sea monster and a giant rock to do his dirty work. He's the original 'retired but not defeated' villain, a reminder that in the ancient world, even a fallen god is never truly out of the game.
