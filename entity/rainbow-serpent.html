Who It Is

Across a continent of deserts, floodplains and hidden rock pools, one immense serpent coils through story and stone—its body carving rivers, its breath calling rain, its anger turning floods into graveyards of bone. The Rainbow Serpent is not a single tame myth but a living web of ancestral beings, each rooted in specific country, each binding water, law and memory together in the longest continuous oral tradition on Earth.

The Rainbow Serpent is consistently ambivalent: a giver of life and a potential destroyer. Attested accounts describe it as a creator of landforms, rivers and waterholes, a source of fertility for plants, animals and humans, and a sender of conception spirits to waterholes. At the same time, it punishes those who break sacred law or disturb its dwelling places, bringing storms, floods and sometimes swallowing people. Powers
Landscape creation and transformation

The Rainbow Serpent is credited with shaping rivers, valleys, hills, mountains and waterholes as it travelled across the unformed world in the Dreaming. Its massive body carved channels, raised ridges and left resting places that became permanent water sources.

Limit: Creation is tied to the ancestral epoch and specific journeys; contemporary changes to the landscape are not usually described as new acts of creation but as ongoing presence at established sites.

Control of water and rain

The Rainbow Serpent inhabits waterholes and springs, brings or withholds rain, and is responsible for regenerating rains and storms. It can cause floods when angered and is closely associated with fertility of plants and animals.

Limit: Control of rain is often shared with other beings such as Wandjina in the Kimberley; ceremonial protocols and law observance are required to ensure favourable rain.

Fertility and conception spirits

The Rainbow Serpent is a personification of fertility and increase, sending conception spirits from waterholes to women, ensuring propagation of plants, animals and humans.

Limit: Knowledge of conception spirits and associated rituals is often restricted; details are not publicly disclosed and may vary between clans.

Punishment and swallowing transgressors

When sacred law is broken or its dwelling places are disturbed, the Rainbow Serpent can act as an agent of punishment, bringing storms and floods, swallowing people and regurgitating their bones, which turn to stone and mark the event.

Limit: Punitive actions are usually framed within specific mythic episodes and ceremonial warnings rather than everyday occurrences.

Initiatory transformation

In certain initiation rituals, the Rainbow Serpent symbolically or mythically swallows novices and later regurgitates them, signifying their transformation into adults with new knowledge and responsibilities.

Limit: Details are highly restricted and often not shared outside ceremonial contexts; many descriptions in public sources are schematic.

Bestowal of magical powers and illness

Rainbow snakes can enter a person and endow them with magical powers, or leave "little rainbows" within the body that cause illness and death.

Limit: This ability is described specifically for certain Arnhem Land contexts and may not be present in all regional variants.

Accounts I
Yingarna and Ngalyod: the Rainbow Serpents of western Arnhem LandArnhem Land oral traditions; Aboriginal Art Online summary

Deep-time oral tradition, continuing into the present; documented in late 20th century

Yingarna, the female Rainbow Serpent, is the original creator being, mother of many ancestral beings and source of fertility. Ngalyod, the male Rainbow Serpent, transforms the land, moving through country and shaping waterholes, hills and valleys. They dwell in deep pools below waterfalls and are central to major fertility and increase ceremonies. Their stories explain the origin of particular features of the landscape and the responsibilities of people to care for those places and observe law. Establishment of sacred sites, fertility of country, and ongoing ceremonial obligations. Disturbing their waterholes or breaking law can result in sickness or disaster.

Wagyl and the creation of the Swan RiverNoongar oral tradition; mythological summaries

Deep-time oral tradition, continuing; recorded in 20th–21st century

In the Dreaming, Wagyl, a great water serpent, travelled across the coastal plains, carving the Swan River and associated waterways with its body. Where it rested or coiled, wetlands and springs formed. Wagyl remains present in these waterholes, protecting them and punishing those who pollute or disrespect them. Creation of the river system that sustains Noongar people; establishment of taboos and responsibilities regarding water sources.

Accounts II
Wollunqua’s journey from ThapauerluWarumungu oral tradition; mythological summaries

Deep-time oral tradition; recorded in early and mid-20th century ethnography

Wollunqua, a Rainbow Serpent of immense size, emerged from a waterhole called Thapauerlu. It travelled vast distances across the desert, its body so long that its tail remained coiled in the waterhole even as its head moved far away. Ceremonies recount its journey and mark the sites it created, reinforcing connections between people and country. Creation of multiple sacred sites and ceremonial tracks; reinforcement of kinship and land tenure through ritual.

Yurlunggur and the Djankawu sistersYolŋu oral tradition; ethnographic accounts

Deep-time oral tradition; documented in 20th century

Yurlunggur, the great father Rainbow Serpent, is associated with the journeys of the Djankawu sisters, ancestral women who travelled across country creating clans and law. In some narratives, Yurlunggur’s presence at waterholes and initiation sites frames the sisters’ actions and the transformation of novices. Establishment of clan identities, law and ceremonial structures; linking of serpent power with female ancestral creativity.

Dhakkan moving between waterholesKabi Kabi lore; Rainbow Serpent summary

Deep-time oral tradition; recorded in 20th–21st century

When a rainbow appears in the sky, it is said to be Dhakkan, the Rainbow Serpent, moving from one waterhole to another. Reliable rock holes are understood as its permanent dwellings, explaining why some waterholes remain even in drought. Association of rainbows with water security and spiritual presence; reinforcement of respect for waterholes.
