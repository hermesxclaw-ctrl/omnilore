I carved the rivers with my body. I dug the waterholes where I rested. When you see the rainbow after rain, you see my skin stretched across the sky. Respect the water — it is my blood, and it is your life.

Who It Is

The Rainbow Serpent is one of the most important, widespread, and ancient spiritual beings in Aboriginal Australian religion — a creator ancestor of the Dreamtime (the foundational era when ancestral beings shaped the landscape and established laws) who is intimately associated with water, rain, fertility, and the creative and destructive power of nature. Known by many names across different Aboriginal language groups (Wagyl in Noongar country of Western Australia, Yurlungur in Arnhem Land, Kanmare in Queensland, and many others), the Rainbow Serpent is depicted in rock art dating back thousands of years, making it one of the oldest continuously represented spiritual concepts in human history.

Physically, the Rainbow Serpent is depicted as a great snake, often with features of other animals — kangaroo-like head, emu-like wattle, crocodile-like tail — reflecting its role as a composite creator being that transcends any single natural form. In rock art across Australia, the Rainbow Serpent appears as a sinuous, elongated being, sometimes with the arc of a rainbow emanating from its body. Its skin is often shown with intricate patterns (cross-hatching, dots, stripes) representing the diversity of creation. During the wet season, the Rainbow Serpent's body is the rainbow that arcs across the sky after rain — the visible bridge between water (on earth) and the celestial realm.

The Rainbow Serpent's primary mythological role is as a creator and shaper of the landscape. During the Dreamtime, the Rainbow Serpent traveled across the formless, flat earth, its massive body carving out riverbeds, gorges, and waterholes. Where it rested, lakes and billabongs formed. Its movements pushed up mountains and gouged valleys. When it traveled underground, it created the subterranean water sources that sustain life in the desert. Thus, the entire Australian landscape — every waterhole, every ridge, every river winding through the outback — is the living body of the Rainbow Serpent, and the land is literally sacred ground.

The Rainbow Serpent is fundamentally a water-being, and in the arid Australian continent, water is the ultimate source of life. The Rainbow Serpent guards the waterholes and billabongs; it controls the rains and the monsoons; it punishes those who violate water-related laws or take more than their share. During the wet season, the Rainbow Serpent is said to travel between waterholes through underground channels and across the sky as lightning and rainbows. The booming thunder is the Serpent's voice; the lightning flash is its tongue. It is simultaneously the source of life-giving water and the potential destroyer who can unleash floods, drown those who disrespect sacred sites, or swallow the unwary.

The Rainbow Serpent is also deeply connected to human fertility and initiation. In many Aboriginal traditions, the Rainbow Serpent swallows young initiates and then regurgitates them, transformed into adults — a symbolic death and rebirth that enacts the Serpent's own creative power. Quartz crystals, which are associated with the Rainbow Serpent in many traditions, are used in initiation and healing ceremonies by Aboriginal 'clever men' (traditional healers).

The Rainbow Serpent continues to be a living spiritual reality for Aboriginal communities. Access to certain waterholes remains restricted and governed by traditional law; disturbing a Rainbow Serpent site can bring believed consequences. The Rainbow Serpent has also become a symbol of Aboriginal identity and cultural continuity in modern Australia, appearing in Indigenous art, literature, and political discourse as a powerful emblem of the deep spiritual connection between Aboriginal people and the land.

Form

Attested descriptions portray the Rainbow Serpent as a vast snake-like being, often of extraordinary length and thickness, capable of spanning large stretches of landscape. In western Arnhem Land, the Rainbow Snake is depicted as a composite creature: a huge python body adorned with water lilies, yams and tendrils, sometimes with a kangaroo or flying fox head and a crocodile tail. In Noongar and other regions, Wagyl and related beings are imagined as enormous carpet snakes or water serpents. Some traditions emphasize shimmering, multicoloured scales or an association with the rainbow arc in the sky; others focus on its subterranean or aquatic presence, coiled in deep waterholes. Sensory qualities are mostly reconstructed from iconography and oral description: the being is heavy, sinuous, powerful, and often invisible beneath the water, emerging in storms or floods.

Powers

Landscape creation and transformation The Rainbow Serpent is credited with shaping rivers, valleys, hills, mountains and waterholes as it travelled across the unformed world in the Dreaming. Its massive body carved channels, raised ridges and left resting places that became permanent water sources. Pan-Australian comparative corpus; specific narratives of Wagyl (Noongar), Ungud (Kimberley), Wollunqua (Warumungu), Ngalyod (Kunwinjku) and others Creation is tied to the ancestral epoch and specific journeys; contemporary changes to the landscape are not usually described as new acts of creation but as ongoing presence at established sites. Some scholars argue that not all serpent beings in Australian traditions are primary creators; in certain regions other ancestral beings share or supersede this role, leading to debate over how central the Rainbow Serpent is to creation narratives.

Control of water and rain The Rainbow Serpent inhabits waterholes and springs, brings or withholds rain, and is responsible for regenerating rains and storms. It can cause floods when angered and is closely associated with fertility of plants and animals. Chaloupka’s description of the Rainbow Snake; Aboriginal Art Online; regional oral traditions Control of rain is often shared with other beings such as Wandjina in the Kimberley; ceremonial protocols and law observance are required to ensure favourable rain. In some traditions, the serpent’s role is more localized, tied to particular waterholes rather than continental weather patterns, leading to differing emphases on its meteorological power.

Fertility and conception spirits The Rainbow Serpent is a personification of fertility and increase, sending conception spirits from waterholes to women, ensuring propagation of plants, animals and humans. Chaloupka, Journey in Time; Arnhem Land fertility ceremonies Knowledge of conception spirits and associated rituals is often restricted; details are not publicly disclosed and may vary between clans. Some scholars caution against overgeneralizing conception-spirit narratives, noting that not all Rainbow Serpent traditions explicitly mention this function.

Punishment and swallowing transgressors When sacred law is broken or its dwelling places are disturbed, the Rainbow Serpent can act as an agent of punishment, bringing storms and floods, swallowing people and regurgitating their bones, which turn to stone and mark the event. Chaloupka’s description; Arnhem Land oral traditions; central desert narratives Punitive actions are usually framed within specific mythic episodes and ceremonial warnings rather than everyday occurrences. Some modern retellings soften or omit the punitive aspect, emphasizing only benevolent creation, which conflicts with older accounts that stress its dangerous side.

Initiatory transformation In certain initiation rituals, the Rainbow Serpent symbolically or mythically swallows novices and later regurgitates them, signifying their transformation into adults with new knowledge and responsibilities. Yolŋu narratives of Yurlunggur and Julunggul; ethnographic accounts of initiation ceremonies Details are highly restricted and often not shared outside ceremonial contexts; many descriptions in public sources are schematic. Some scholars question whether swallowing should be interpreted literally, symbolically, or as a narrative device, leading to differing readings of the serpent’s role.

Bestowal of magical powers and illness Rainbow snakes can enter a person and endow them with magical powers, or leave "little rainbows" within the body that cause illness and death. Chaloupka, Journey in Time, p. 47 This ability is described specifically for certain Arnhem Land contexts and may not be present in all regional variants. Later popular accounts often omit this darker aspect, focusing instead on generalized spirituality and harmony.

Accounts

Yingarna, the female Rainbow Serpent, is the original creator being, mother of many ancestral beings and source of fertility. Ngalyod, the male Rainbow Serpent, transforms the land, moving through country and shaping waterholes, hills and valleys. They dwell in deep pools below waterfalls and are central to major fertility and increase ceremonies. Their stories explain the origin of particular features of the landscape and the responsibilities of people to care for those places and observe law.

In the Dreaming, Wagyl, a great water serpent, travelled across the coastal plains, carving the Swan River and associated waterways with its body. Where it rested or coiled, wetlands and springs formed. Wagyl remains present in these waterholes, protecting them and punishing those who pollute or disrespect them.

Wollunqua, a Rainbow Serpent of immense size, emerged from a waterhole called Thapauerlu. It travelled vast distances across the desert, its body so long that its tail remained coiled in the waterhole even as its head moved far away. Ceremonies recount its journey and mark the sites it created, reinforcing connections between people and country.

Yurlunggur, the great father Rainbow Serpent, is associated with the journeys of the Djankawu sisters, ancestral women who travelled across country creating clans and law. In some narratives, Yurlunggur’s presence at waterholes and initiation sites frames the sisters’ actions and the transformation of novices.

When a rainbow appears in the sky, it is said to be Dhakkan, the Rainbow Serpent, moving from one waterhole to another. Reliable rock holes are understood as its permanent dwellings, explaining why some waterholes remain even in drought.

Content Notes

In Arnhem Land and other regions, the Rainbow Serpent is said to bring storms and floods as punishment for those who transgress sacred law or disturb its waterholes. It can swallow people in great floods and regurgitate their bones, which turn into stone and remain as markers of the event.

Chaloupka interprets these narratives as part of a broader system of law enforcement and ecological caution, not mere terror stories. Modern readers may find the imagery disturbing, but it functions within a moral and cosmological framework.

George Chaloupka, Journey in Time; Aboriginal Art Online summary

Rainbow snakes can enter a person and leave "little rainbows" within the body, causing illness and eventual death. This is described as a consequence of improper behaviour or contact with forbidden places.

Anthropologists note that such narratives encode understandings of spiritual causation of illness and reinforce taboos around sacred sites.

Chaloupka, Journey in Time, p. 47

Colonial administrators and missionaries often dismissed Rainbow Serpent traditions as "pagan" or "superstitious" and attempted to suppress ceremonies, rock art repainting and transmission of stories. Some sites were damaged or destroyed by pastoral development, mining and tourism.

Historians and Indigenous scholars highlight how suppression disrupted ceremonial life and contributed to cultural loss, while also noting ongoing resilience and revival efforts.

General scholarship on Aboriginal religion and colonial history; discussions in Rainbow Serpent corpus articles

The name "Rainbow Serpent" has been used for commercial and entertainment purposes, such as the Rainbow Serpent Festival in Victoria, which is not directly controlled by traditional custodians of Rainbow Serpent sites. Some Aboriginal people have criticized such uses as appropriation or trivialization of sacred beings.

Debates around cultural appropriation and intellectual property emphasize the need for consent, benefit-sharing and respect when using sacred names and imagery.

What We Don't Know

Significant gaps remain due to the restricted nature of many Rainbow Serpent traditions: detailed ceremonial knowledge, specific site locations, and clan-based interpretations are not publicly available and must be learned from custodians. Regional coverage in this dossier is uneven, with more information from Arnhem Land, the Kimberley and southwest Western Australia than from many other language groups. Chronological precision for rock art and oral narratives is limited, relying on archaeological estimates and comparative dating. Some citations lack full bibliographic detail because only snippets or secondary references were accessible. Debates over unity versus plurality, symbolic versus literal readings of narratives, and the impact of colonial disruption are ongoing and cannot be fully resolved here.

A way to hold it in your mind

Across a continent of deserts, floodplains and hidden rock pools, one immense serpent coils through story and stone—its body carving rivers, its breath calling rain, its anger turning floods into graveyards of bone. The Rainbow Serpent is not a single tame myth but a living web of ancestral beings, each rooted in specific country, each binding water, law and memory together in the longest continuous oral tradition on Earth.
