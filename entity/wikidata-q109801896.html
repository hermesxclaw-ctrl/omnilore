Who It Is

Seven notes contain a whole world. In Solresol you can speak, whistle, play it on a violin, flash it in colors or tap it with your fingers — do, re, mi, fa, sol, la, si — and from those seven syllables Jean-François Sudre tried to build a universal language where 'good' becomes 'bad' simply by saying it backwards.

As a constructed philosophical language, Solresol behaves as an a priori taxonomic system with a posteriori phonology (solfège). It is isolating and analytic: words do not inflect for person or case except via external particles and diacritics. Word order is described as SVO with strict adjacency rules: adjectives follow nouns, indirect objects follow verbs, tense particles precede verbs. The language is designed for redundancy to survive musical transmission loss. Personality in contemporary description is utopian, earnest, and didactic — a product of early 19th-century French universalism

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From the solfège syllables sol - re - sol. Chosen by Jean-François Sudre as an euphonious and musically self-referential name; literally the sequence sol-re-sol. Originally introduced as 'Langue universelle' (Universal Language) in the 1820s-1830s, renamed 'Langue musicale universelle' by the 1840s,

Pronunciation — French /sɔl.ʁe.sɔl/ (sol-re-sol); English commonly /ˈsɒl.rɛ.sɒl/ or /soʊlˈrɛsoʊl/. Syllables follow standard solfège: do [do], re [ʁe], mi [mi], fa [fa], sol [sɔl], la [la], si [si]. No IPA prescribed by Sudre (predates IPA, 1888); modern users apply French or local phonology. Each syllable distinct and may be lengthened or stressed for grammatical function.

Aliases — Langue universelle, Langue musicale universelle, Sol-Re-Sol, Solrésol, Universal Musical Language

Seven-Syllable Combinatorial Lexicon

All content words built from permutations of do, re, mi, fa, sol, la, si, max five syllables per root. System yields 11,732 possible roots (7 one-syllable, 49 two-syllable, 336 three-syllable, 2,268 four-syllable, 9,072 five-syllable). Semantic fields assigned by initial syllable and length: e.g., do- words = humanity, sol- four-syllable without repetition = arts/sciences.

Limit: Paucity of phonological contrast creates high homophony risk and memory load; Gajewski mandates a perceptible pause between words to avoid segmentation ambiguity. Five-syllable limit caps lexicon, requiring compounding for modern concepts.

Multimodal Transduction

Any sequence can be transmitted via speech, singing, whistling, musical instrument (seven pitches), seven colors, seven numbers, seven hand signs (tonic sol-fa), flags, or taps. This was Sudre's central claim for accessibility to blind, deaf, and illiterate users and for telegraphy.

Limit: Pitch-based transmission is vulnerable to mistuning and octave ambiguity; color transmission requires shared lighting and color vision; no error-correction beyond redundancy and pause. Demonstrations were controlled, not field-tested at scale.

Mirror Antonymy (Reversal)

A subset of words form antonyms by reversing syllable order: fala 'good, tasty' ↔ lafa 'bad'; midominisol 'to be useful' ↔ solminidom 'to be useless'. Pedagogical feature for memorability.

Limit: Not productive for all words; many reversals are semantically unrelated (dorefare 'neck' vs refaredo 'wardrobe') or undefined. Gajewski warns it is a mnemonic aid, not a generative rule.

Diacritic Morphology: Number, Gender, Derivation

Plural marked by acute accent on final syllable, realized as lengthening (resimire 'brother' → resimiré 'brothers'). Feminine marked by macron/bar/hyphen on article or noun, realized as vowel repetition with glottal stop (la resimire 'the brother' → lā resimire / la-a resimire 'the sister'). Part of speech derived from verb via circumflex and rinforzando stress: mîdofa 'preference' (noun), midôfa 'preferable' (agent noun in 4-syllable), midofâ 'preferably' (adverb).

Limit: Gender marking defaults to masculine (all animate nouns/pronouns masculine unless marked), creating marked feminine. Plural marking only on first element of noun phrase (article carries it if present). Requires diacritics absent on many keyboards; speech realization subtle.

Categorical Semantics and Tense System

Lexical categories encoded by length and repetition: 1-2 syllable = pronouns/particles; repeated syllables = numbers, months, days, weather (redodo 'one', remimi 'two'); 4-syllable without repetition = thematic fields (do, re, mi, fa, sol, la, si classes), with repetition = religion, construction, adverbs, sickness, justice etc.; 5-syllable = natural history (animals, plants, minerals). Tense marked by particles with repeated syllables preceding verb (e.g., dore 'I' + tense + verb).

Limit: Highly taxonomic and Eurocentric (19th-century French worldview); new domains (technology, modern science) require ad hoc compounding. Tense particles increase utterance length.

Phonetic Transcription Mode for Proper Nouns

Sudre provided a method to transcribe French (and by extension other languages) phonetically into Solresol syllables for proper names and untranslatable terms, using solresol syllables as phonetic approximations mapped to IPA via contemporary French pronunciation.

Limit: Lossy and ambiguous due to only seven syllable types; requires shared phonetic table. Not a general loanword mechanism; modern community rarely uses it.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Solresol has no native script in the conventional sense; its 'appearance' is multimodal. In written form it is rendered as hyphenated or concatenated solfège syllables in Latin letters (e.g., dore, midominisol, solresol). Gajewski added a set of 7 arbitrary logographic symbols, a color code (seven rainbow colors mapped to do through si), and a numeric code (1-7). Accounts I
The Music Teacher's Invention (1817-1827)Sudre 1866 preface (autobiographical account); Large 1985, pp. 45-48

1817-1827

Jean-François Sudre, born in Albi in 1787 and trained as a violinist and music teacher, begins in 1817 to devise a language based solely on the seven solfège syllables, motivated by the idea that music is the only truly universal art. He develops a combinatorial dictionary and demonstrates that any word can be played, sung, or whistled. By 1827 he presents the system to the Académie des Beaux-Arts in Paris, seeking institutional validation. Académie issues a favorable but cautious report, encouraging further development. Sudre gains a public profile as 'inventeur de la langue musicale' and begins decades of demonstrations across France.

Patronage and the Prix de 10,000 Francs (1830s-1850s)Sudre 1866 dedications and testimonials; contemporary press in La France musicale; Eco 1993, pp. 317-318

1833-1862

Sudre tours and publishes pamphlets, securing endorsements from leading intellectuals. Victor Hugo, Alphonse de Lamartine, and Alexander von Humboldt write letters of support praising the ingenuity of a musical universal language. Under Napoleon III, Sudre receives a government grant/prize of 10,000 francs (often cited as 1860) to continue his work, and is made a Chevalier of the Legion of Honour. The language enjoys salon popularity as a curiosity and pedagogical tool. Financial support allows Sudre to refine his dictionary to ~3,000 words, but does not translate into institutional adoption. Endorsements become the chief marketing for later promoters, reproduced in every subsequent Solresol publication.

Posthumous Publication by Joséphine Sudre (1866)Sudre 1866 title page and widow's preface; Bibliothèque nationale de France catalogue

1862-1866

Sudre dies in Paris in 1862 without having published a definitive book. His widow, Joséphine Sudre (née Hugon), organizes his manuscripts and publishes Langue musicale universelle in 1866 with publisher G. Flaxland. The volume contains the grammar, French-Solresol dictionary, and methods for telegraphic and musical transmission. It is presented as the authoritative statement of Sudre's system. Creates a stable textual base for the language for the first time, enabling others to learn and teach it without direct contact with Sudre. Also freezes Sudre's lexicon, which later reformers will revise.

Accounts II
The Central Committee and the Gajewski Codification (1869-1902)Boleslas Gajewski 1902 introduction; Vincent Gajewski committee records; Large 1985

1869-1902

In 1869 Joséphine Sudre founds the Comité central d'étude et de propagation du Solresol, appointing Vincent Gajewski, a Parisian language professor, as president. Vincent and his son Boleslas Gajewski promote Solresol through classes, congresses, and publications. Boleslas publishes the Grammaire du Solrésol in 1902, formalizing grammar (plural, feminine, derivation via stress), adding color/symbol/stenographic alphabets, and revising much vocabulary for systemicity. This becomes the 'classic' Solresol. Peak of organized Solresol activity: small but international correspondence network, mentions at early international language congresses. However, the reforms create a split between Sudre purists and Gajewski users, and the movement is soon eclipsed by Volapük and Esperanto.

Eclipse by Volapük and Esperanto (1880-1914)Andrew Large 1985, ch. 3-4; Detlev Blanke 1985; Esperanto congress proceedings

1879-1914

The rise of Johann Martin Schleyer's Volapük (1879) and L. L. Zamenhof's Esperanto (1887) draws away the audience for universal language projects. Solresol, with its limited lexicon, musical transmission difficulties, and lack of a living community, fails to compete on learnability and expressivity. By the 1910s it is largely a historical footnote, maintained only by a handful of enthusiasts and bibliophiles. Organized teaching ceases; publications stop after ca. 1905. Solresol survives primarily as a case study in histories of artificial languages rather than as a spoken medium.

Digital Revival and Modern Solresol (1997-present)Stephen L. Rice translation 1997; sidosi.org archive; Okrent 2009, pp. 38-48; community Discord/Reddit logs

1997-2026

Stephen L. Rice's 1997 English translation of Gajewski's grammar, hosted online, reintroduces Solresol to anglophone conlangers. The site Sidosi.org (later solresol.org) compiles Sudre, Gajewski, and Rice materials, adds searchable dictionaries, and hosts the 'Modern Solresol' proposals (gender-neutral pronouns, revised honorifics). YouTube channels demonstrate whistled and played Solresol, and small learning groups form on Facebook, Reddit, and Discord. The language is now studied as both a historical artifact and a playable linguistic experiment. Stabilizes a digital corpus and introduces the language to a new generation, but also codifies the Sudre/Gajewski/Modern split. No revival of daily use; activity remains archival, artistic, and pedagogical. Scholarly interest persists via Eco, Okrent, and Yaguello.

Solresol is a language you can play on any instrument and any listener will understand it without training. — Understanding requires prior memorization of the 2,600+ word codebook and shared conventions for segmentation (pauses), octave, and tempo. Gajewski explicitly warns that without a pause between words, listeners become confused, and that instrumental performance requires agreed pitch mapping. It is a code, not an innately intelligible music.

Solresol has 11,732 words and is therefore a complete language. — 11,732 is the theoretical maximum of distinct 1-5 syllable combinations, not the attested lexicon. Sudre defined ~3,000 words, Gajewski ~2,600 plus compounds. Many combinations remain undefined, and the language lacks systematic means for modern technical vocabulary without compounding or borrowing via phonetic transcription.

Solresol is an ancient or natural musical language, or related to solfège's origins. — Solresol is a consciously invented a priori language from 1817-1866, using solfège syllables as convenient labels. Solfège itself dates to Guido of Arezzo (11th century) and has no linguistic content. Solresol postdates modern solfège by centuries.

Reversing any Solresol word gives its opposite. — Reversal antonymy is lexically specified for a subset of words (e.g., fala/lafa) as a mnemonic, not a productive morphological rule. Most reversals are unrelated (dorefare 'neck' vs refaredo 'wardrobe') or meaningless. Gajewski states the feature is limited and pedagogical.
