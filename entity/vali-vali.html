Who It Is

Vali is the Norse god of vengeance, born specifically to avenge the death of Baldr. He is the son of Odin and the goddess Rindr (a mortal princess in some versions), conceived through Odin's coercion after Baldr's death. Remarkably, Vali grew from infancy to full adulthood within a single day after his birth and immediately killed Höðr, the unwitting slayer of Baldr — thus fulfilling the prophecy that Baldr would be avenged by a child of Odin and Rindr. In the Gesta Danorum, Vali is described as the one who castrates the sorceress who had killed Baldr (a very different version). Vali is one of the survivors of Ragnarök, along with Vidarr, Thor's sons, and Baldr and Höðr returned. He represents the principle of divine justice carried out swiftly — a child becomes an avenger in a single day, showing that cosmic debts cannot be escaped. His name shares etymology with 'Valhalla' and 'valkyrie,' suggesting connection to the slain.

Origins

Vali is the son of Odin and Rindr. When Baldr was killed, the Æsir needed a son of Odin to avenge him because blood-debt could only be settled by kin. Odin used a magical spell (or seduction via coercion) to conceive Vali with Rindr. Vali was born, aged to adulthood in one day, and immediately killed Höðr.

Attested in Poetic Edda (Völuspá, Vafþrúðnismál, Baldrs Draumar) / Prose Edda / Gesta DanorumEra Viking Age (c. 800-1100 CE), recorded 13th century

Form

No surviving source gives a physical portrait of Váli. Statements about his appearance are entirely absent from the poetic and prose corpus — he is characterized wholly by deed and function, not by image. The consistent functional portrait, however, implies a figure of preternaturally rapid maturation (a full-grown warrior 'one night old') who is a skilled archer and a 'daring' fighter. Latin sources treat him as a mortal heroic prince (Bous/Boe) capable of slaying the king of Denmark/Sweden in single combat, so Saxo imagines a princely martial youth; but this is Saxo's euhemerization, not an attested description of the god. There are no surviving depictions of Váli in the Viking-age visual repertoire (unlike Víðarr, whose struggle with Fenrir has been proposed on the Gosforth Cross); his iconography is effectively nil, and reconstructing any appearance is pure inference.

Powers

One-day maturation / preternatural growth Váli grows from newborn to full adulthood within a single day (or night) of his birth. Völuspá stanza 33 and Baldrs draumar both stress that he was 'one night old' when he slew Höðr; the prophecy in Baldrs draumar says he 'will kill when one night old.' This is his defining and most remarked-upon power, making him a god who arrives fully-formed in the world to do a specific deed. Poetic Edda — Völuspá st. 33; Baldrs draumar st. 11; Prose Edda (Gylfaginning god-list, which implies adult form from birth). The maturation is bound to his purpose: he does not wash nor comb his head until the killing is done. The rapid growth is presented as a prophecy being fulfilled rather than a freely deployable power; no text suggests he could mature other individuals or repeat this. The 'one night old' figure is consistent across the sources, but the number of days between the killing of Höðr and the binding of Loki is not specified; the account implies both happen in rapid succession.

Oathbound vengeance-state (the unwashed avenger) Váli is marked by a ritualized refusal of normal hygiene and grooming — 'he will not wash hand, nor comb head' — until he has carried out the vengeance. This is more an ethical/ritual obligation than a conventional power, but in Old Norse blood-feud culture it functions as an active supernatural/legal charge: he is quiescent until the owed deed is settled, then the taboo lifts. Baldrs draumar st. 11; general Norse saga ethics of the unreserved avenger. None beyond its own binding: he cannot rest or be released from the obligation until the deed is complete. No direct contradictions; the withholding-of-washing is widely glossed as a parallel to other figures who postpone personal care until vengeance, but no source explicitly links the taboo to the rest of his powers.

Superb archery / 'most fortunate marksmanship' Gylfaginning explicitly names Váli a 'most fortunate marksman' and 'daring in fights.' This positions him as the archetypal divine archer of the Æsir (alongside the broader Norse archery tradition on their side of the divine), a skill consistent with his role using the bow in the vengeance narrative. Prose Edda — Gylfaginning god-list entry for Váli. Only his skill is attested; no specific magic bow or embodiment is recorded. None; archery is the single clearest combat skill stated.

Prowess at binding / overpowering a god (binding of Loki) In the Prose Edda, Váli (Óðinn's son) is the agent who binds Loki, the arch-trickster god, using the entrails of Loki's own son Narfi as the fetters. This implies sufficient strength and/or divine authority to restrain a deity who is otherwise unbound, and makes Váli the direct enactor of Loki's imprisonment that lasts until Ragnarök. Prose Edda — Gylfaginning ch. 50 (the binding); Völuspá st. 34 (Hauksbók reading 'vigbond snúa' = Váli twists slaughter-bonds). The binding is divinely sanctioned (the Æsir act collectively); Váli is the instrument, and the fetter is his victim's own son's entrails, not an independent magic item. The wolf-entrail fetter binds Loki into a state from which only Ragnarök releases him. The account muddles who undergoes the wolf-transformation: Gylfaginning says one son is turned into a wolf who kills the other, and the surviving son's entrails bind Loki. Whether that wolf-son is 'Váli son of Loki' or an unnamed son, and whether the binder is Óðinn's son Váli, is the crux of the whole Váli-son-of-Loki confusion documented in the main article.

Survival of Ragnarök Vafþrúðnismál stanza 51 prophesies that Váli, together with Víðarr, will dwell in the gods' home after Surtr's fires have subsided — i.e., he is one of the select handful of Æsir who survive the destruction and rebirth of the world. Poetic Edda — Vafþrúðnismál st. 51. His survival is stated but not explained; no combat role in Ragnarök is given him. The list of survivors varies across sources (Víðarr, Váli, Móði, Magni, and Höðr+Baldr in Völuspá st. 62 as reborn); Váli's presence on the survivor list is consistent in Vafþrúðnismál.

(Possible) berserker / ulfheðinn wolf-strength — speculative The main Váli article notes a scholarly suggestion that the wolf-transformation and 'wolf's strength and rage' that some readings attach to the binding scene may echo Odin's well-attested granting of animal fury to his chosen warriors, the ulfheðnar (wolf-warriors). On this reading, Váli could be a mythic origin-figure for the wolf-warrior legend. Prose Edda binding scene (son-of-Loki wolf) as read through the berserker/ulfheðinn scholarship; cross-referenced with the Berserker article (16,275 chars fetched). This is explicitly present in the Wikipedia main article as a hypothesis and is NOT asserted as fact. The wolf in the straightforward text is Loki's son, not Óðinn's Váli, so the berserker linkage is interpretive. Depends entirely on whether one accepts the 'Váli son of Loki' as a real tradition or (as mainstream scholarship holds) a scribal confusion; the hypothesis only coheres on the euhemerized reading that conflates the two.

Accounts

After the beloved god Baldr is killed — in the standard Eddic account, killed when the blind god Höðr, unwittingly guided by Loki, hurls a mistletoe shaft — Óðinn moves immediately to sire a son who can exact vengeance without the normal constraints of kinship-law stopping him (a brother killing a brother for slaying a brother would otherwise create an unpayable wergild). The völva Baldr's Dreams tells Óðinn: 'Rindr will bear Váli / in western halls; / that son of Óðinn / will kill when one night old.' Völuspá records the actual birth: 'A brother of Baldr / was born quickly: / he started — Óðinn's son — / slaying, at one night old.' Váli is thus a purpose-built avenger, grown to manhood in a day.

Váli, at one night old, slays Höðr — the blind god who, treacherously guided by Loki's hand on the spear, killed Baldr with the mistletoe that no oath protected. The killing is framed in Völuspá as the completion of the vengeance: the 'shaft of anguish' that Höðr loosed is answered by the brother-of-Baldr killing 'Hǫðr's adversary'-to-the-pyre. Because Höðr was an unwitting instrument (Loki aimed his hand), Váli's killing of him is usually understood as the price of restoring the balance rather than a punishment of malice — Höðr is the sacrificial deus-ex-machina of the blood-feud.

After Baldr's death and the gods' determination of Loki's guilt, the Æsir pursue, capture, and bind the trickster. In Gylfaginning ch. 50, the gods 'take Loki's sons' and — in the standard telling — change one into a wolf who tears the other apart; the surviving son's entrails are then used to bind Loki fast to a rock. The Óðinn-son Váli is named (via the Völuspá reading) as the one who 'twists the slaughter-bonds.' Beneath Loki, a serpent is placed to drip venom; Loki's wife Sigyn catches the venom in a bowl, and when she must empty it the dripping venom makes Loki writhe so violently that he causes earthquakes — a state that endures until Ragnarök.

In the wisdom contest between Óðinn (disguised as Gangráð) and the giant Vafþrúðnir, the giant states that after the world-burning fires of Surtr subside, 'in the gods' home Víðarr and Váli shall dwell,' alongside Móði and Magni inheriting Thor's hammer Mjǫllnir. Váli is therefore, with his half-brother Víðarr (the other great avenger), one of the handful of Æsir who survive the destruction and rebirth of the world.

Saxo Grammaticus recasts the god-myth as pseudo-history. Óðinn (Othinus) is a human sorcerer credited with divinity; after Balderus is slain by Hotherus (a mortal king of Denmark/Sweden, an alternate casting of Höðr as warrior-rival), Othinus consults seers and, to sire an avenger, travels to Ruthenia where he enchants the princess Rinda by touching her with rune-marked bark, driving her mad and (in Saxo's account) sleeping with her in various disguises. She bears Boe (or Bous). When grown, Boe is urged by his father to avenge Balderus; Hotherus, foreseeing his death, passes the kingdom to his son Rorik; Boe meets and slays Hotherus in battle, but dies of his wounds a day later, and the Rus' army buries him in a barrow.

Content Notes

Váli is conceived through Óðinn's sexual coercion of Rindr. The earliest independent hint is the 10th-century skaldic line 'seið Yggr til Rindar' ('Óðinn enchanted Rindr') — a seiðr (sorcery) that implies magical compulsion. Saxo Grammaticus makes the coercion explicit and brutal: Óðinn-as-wizard (posing as the warrior 'Roster'), repeatedly rejected by the princess Rinda, touches her with rune-inscribed bark that drives her mad, and in Saxo's telling then takes her by force in disguised form to father Boe/Bous. This is a genuine dark register: the 'hero' god's ergon to produce an avenger is achieved through the rape-like subjugation of a woman, framed in the sources without condemnation.

Scholars read Rindr's rape/enchantment as part of the larger pattern of Óðinn's morally ambiguous dealings (he is elsewhere called svikull, 'treacherous'). Some suggest the seiðr element feminizes Óðinn (seiðr was associated with women and was considered ergi, 'unmanly'), a notable tension — the all-father uses 'unmanly' magic to do a 'manly' work of vengeance. This non-consensual engendering is the direct precondition for Váli's existence.

Kormákr Ögmundarson, Sigurðardrápa st. 3; Saxo Grammaticus, Gesta Danorum Book III; Rindr article

Váli's first act upon being born is to slay Höðr, his own blind half-brother, who had killed Baldr only because Loki guided his hand. Höðr is a victim himself — the tool of Loki — yet the blood-order demands his death to balance Baldr's. The sources record no grief or hesitation; Váli is created precisely so that the killing can happen without unpayable kin-debt, because a brother killing a brother for a brother is a closed loop that no wergild can adjudicate. The 'unwashed avenger' vow dehumanizes Höðr into a purely thermodynamic payment.

This is the classic model of the Norse wer-geld / blood-feud logic being applied to the gods. Scholarship highlights that Baldr's death results in a chain-violence the gods cannot morally exit: they must kill the innocent instrument because the debt is owed, and only a purpose-built avatar avoids an impossible choice among the guilty.

Völuspá st. 33; Baldrs draumar st. 11; Höðr article

Váli completes the punishment of Loki by binding the trickster with the entrails of one of Loki's own sons (after, in the Prose Edda version, turning that son or his brother into a wolf that tears him apart). Loki is then left bound beneath a venom-dripping serpent; his wife Sigyn catches the venom in a bowl, and when she must empty the bowl the dripping venom makes Loki writhe in agony, which is said to cause earthquakes. This is a torture-imprisonment that endures until Ragnarök.

The punishment uses an ironic symmetry — the very source of Loki's progeny becomes his cage — and the venom-drip register is a punishment-typology with parallel in Norse myth. The earthquake-cause is an etiological detail. The filicidal wolf-son element (a son killing a brother before the entrails are used) adds a further layer of familial horror to the scene.

Gylfaginning ch. 50; Loki article; Völuspá st. 35 (Sigyn beside the bound Loki)

Váli is conceived and born with no other stated purpose than to kill. He is a created instrument of vengeance, grown to adulthood in one night, emotionally unburdened by life beyond the task. The poetic sources celebrate this as efficiency; a critical reading notes he is a being denied any identity beyond his function — a divine weapon who exists, acts, and (in Saxo) dies without ever having lived for himself.

Modern readings of Norse 'avenger-gods' (also applying to Víðarr) sometimes frame these as figures embodying the moral cost of the warrior ethic — beings who are only the sum of their vengeance function.

Baldrs draumar st. 11; Völuspá st. 33; Gesta Danorum III

In contemporary popular culture, Váli's dark mythic core (the rape-engendering, the filicide-bound torture of a god, the killing of an innocent) is routinely stripped out, and the name is often reused for unrelated heroic characters — e.g. 'Vali Lucifer' in High School DxD (a divine/human hybrid hero) and the team-name 'Vali Team' — with little or no connection to the actual god's brutal story. This sanitization-obfuscation is itself a form of distortion: the popular Váli is a clean fantasy hero while the attested Váli is an instrument of cosmically-sanctioned violence. (See Reception; the reuse is name-homonym, not adaptation of the myth.)

Cross-cultural study of myth-reception documents how names outlive their narratives; the Váli case is a clean example where a bare theonym is transplanted into a wholly new heroic register.

The wolf-son: whose son is torn apart, and whose entrails bind Loki?
In the standard Gylfaginning reading, Loki's sons Váli and Nari/Narfi are taken; one (Váli-son-of-Loki) is turned into a wolf that kills the other (Narfi), and the survivor's (Narfi's) guts bind Loki. ⇌ In the mainstream 'single Váli' reconstruction, the binder is Óðinn's Váli and the entrails are those of Loki's son Narfi/Nari (after his own brother, another Loki-son, killed him as a wolf).
Evidence for A: The plain Prose Edda text; the Lokasenna end-prose variant.
Evidence for B: The Völuspá/Hauksbók reading and the scholarly emendation producing Óðinn's Váli as the binder.

Etymology of 'Váli' remains unresolved in the sources consulted (the connection to valr 'the slain' is plausible but not confirmed), and a dedicated comparative-linguistics source would be needed to settle it.

A way to hold it in your mind

Váli is the god the Norse invented to make a murder clean. When Baldr — the most beloved, wept-for god — is killed by mistletoe hurled by a blind man's guiding hand, the Æsir find themselves trapped: a brother (Höðr) has slain a brother, and under their own blood-debt law no wergild, no payment, can settle a kill within the kindred. So Óðinn does the one thing the logic of the feud demands and every instinct of fatherhood rejects: he engineers an avatar of vengeance, a son born in a single night, grown to manhood before his first dawn, who will not wash his hand or comb his head until he has carried the body of the innocent instrument — his own brother — to the pyre. Váli is that purpose-made being: a god who exists only long enough to settle the account, bind the real culprit Loki with his own son's guts, and then stand — uncombed, unwashed, unsettling — at the edge of the world's end, prophesied to outlive it. He is the cold, necessary violence at the heart of the Norse cosmos: less a character than the machinery of justice itself, functioning exactly once, immaculately.
