Who It Is

Váli is the god the Norse invented to make a murder clean. When Baldr — the most beloved, wept-for god — is killed by mistletoe hurled by a blind man's guiding hand, the Æsir find themselves trapped: a brother (Höðr) has slain a brother, and under their own blood-debt law no wergild, no payment, can settle a kill within the kindred. So Óðinn does the one thing the logic of the feud demands and every instinct of fatherhood rejects: he engineers an avatar of vengeance, a son born in a single night, grown to manhood before his first dawn, who will not wash his hand or comb his head until he has carried the body of the innocent instrument — his own brother — to the pyre. Váli is that purpose-made being: a god who exists only long enough to settle the account, bind the real culprit Loki with his own son's guts, and then stand — uncombed, unwashed, unsettling — at the edge of the world's end,

Váli is defined by a single-minded, near-mechanical purpose: he is conceived explicitly to avenge Baldr's death and executes that purpose immediately upon being born. His key trait is the refusal of all normal life-rituals until the deed is done — he will not wash his hand or comb his head before bearing 'Baldr's adversary' to the pyre. This marks him as a driven, relentless, and emotionally focused avenger who subordinates everything to the blood-debt. Gylfaginning also calls him 'daring in fights.' Powers
One-day maturation / preternatural growth

Váli grows from newborn to full adulthood within a single day (or night) of his birth. Völuspá stanza 33 and Baldrs draumar both stress that he was 'one night old' when he slew Höðr; the prophecy in Baldrs draumar says he 'will kill when one night old.' This is his defining and most remarked-upon power, making him a god who arrives fully-formed in the world to do a specific deed.

Limit: The maturation is bound to his purpose: he does not wash nor comb his head until the killing is done. The rapid growth is presented as a prophecy being fulfilled rather than a freely deployable power; no text suggests he could mature other individuals or repeat this.

Oathbound vengeance-state (the unwashed avenger)

Váli is marked by a ritualized refusal of normal hygiene and grooming — 'he will not wash hand, nor comb head' — until he has carried out the vengeance. This is more an ethical/ritual obligation than a conventional power, but in Old Norse blood-feud culture it functions as an active supernatural/legal charge: he is quiescent until the owed deed is settled, then the taboo lifts.

Limit: None beyond its own binding: he cannot rest or be released from the obligation until the deed is complete.

Superb archery / 'most fortunate marksmanship'

Gylfaginning explicitly names Váli a 'most fortunate marksman' and 'daring in fights.' This positions him as the archetypal divine archer of the Æsir (alongside the broader Norse archery tradition on their side of the divine), a skill consistent with his role using the bow in the vengeance narrative.

Limit: Only his skill is attested; no specific magic bow or embodiment is recorded.

Prowess at binding / overpowering a god (binding of Loki)

In the Prose Edda, Váli (Óðinn's son) is the agent who binds Loki, the arch-trickster god, using the entrails of Loki's own son Narfi as the fetters. This implies sufficient strength and/or divine authority to restrain a deity who is otherwise unbound, and makes Váli the direct enactor of Loki's imprisonment that lasts until Ragnarök.

Limit: The binding is divinely sanctioned (the Æsir act collectively); Váli is the instrument, and the fetter is his victim's own son's entrails, not an independent magic item. The wolf-entrail fetter binds Loki into a state from which only Ragnarök releases him.

Survival of Ragnarök

Vafþrúðnismál stanza 51 prophesies that Váli, together with Víðarr, will dwell in the gods' home after Surtr's fires have subsided — i.e., he is one of the select handful of Æsir who survive the destruction and rebirth of the world.

Limit: His survival is stated but not explained; no combat role in Ragnarök is given him.

(Possible) berserker / ulfheðinn wolf-strength — speculative

The main Váli article notes a scholarly suggestion that the wolf-transformation and 'wolf's strength and rage' that some readings attach to the binding scene may echo Odin's well-attested granting of animal fury to his chosen warriors, the ulfheðnar (wolf-warriors). On this reading, Váli could be a mythic origin-figure for the wolf-warrior legend.

Limit: This is explicitly present in the Wikipedia main article as a hypothesis and is NOT asserted as fact. The wolf in the straightforward text is Loki's son, not Óðinn's Váli, so the berserker linkage is interpretive.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No surviving source gives a physical portrait of Váli. Statements about his appearance are entirely absent from the poetic and prose corpus — he is characterized wholly by deed and function, not by image. The consistent functional portrait, however, implies a figure of preternaturally rapid maturation (a full-grown warrior 'one night old') who is a skilled archer and a 'daring' fighter. Latin sources treat him as a mortal heroic prince (Bous/Boe)

Symbols — Váli's defining symbolic attributes are (1) the bow/archery, since Gylfaginning names him a 'most fortunate marksman,' and (2) the state of the oath-bound avenger who 'would not wash hand nor comb head' until vengeance was done — a ritualized, almost legalistic uncleanness that marks him as the instrument of an owed blood-deed (this trope of withholding washing/combing until vengeance is the same symbolic logic found else

Domains — Váli has no cult, no named hall (contrast Baldr's Breidablik), no attested temple, no archaeological trace, and no independent worship — he is a mythic figure who exists almost entirely within the Baldr-vengeance cycle and the Ragnarök survival register. Accounts I
The begetting of Váli to avenge Baldr (Baldrs draumar / Völuspá)Poetic Edda — Baldrs draumar st. 11; Völuspá st. 33-34

Oral tradition, recorded in the 13th century

After the beloved god Baldr is killed — in the standard Eddic account, killed when the blind god Höðr, unwittingly guided by Loki, hurls a mistletoe shaft — Óðinn moves immediately to sire a son who can exact vengeance without the normal constraints of kinship-law stopping him (a brother killing a brother for slaying a brother would otherwise create an unpayable wergild). The völva Baldr's Dreams tells Óðinn: 'Rindr will bear Váli / in western halls; / that son of Óðinn / will kill when one night old.' Völuspá records the actual birth: 'A brother of Baldr / was born quickly: / he started — Óðinn's son — / slaying, at one night old.' Váli is thus a purpose-built avenger, grown to manhood in a day. Váli exists solely to restore the violated blood-order; his killing of his own half-brother Höðr resolves the blood-debt that would otherwise demand restitution, and his refusal to wash/comb until the deed is done frames the vengeance as a legally-clean act.

The slaying of HöðrVöluspá st. 33-34; Gylfaginning god-list; Höðr article

Oral/poetic, recorded 13th century

Váli, at one night old, slays Höðr — the blind god who, treacherously guided by Loki's hand on the spear, killed Baldr with the mistletoe that no oath protected. The killing is framed in Völuspá as the completion of the vengeance: the 'shaft of anguish' that Höðr loosed is answered by the brother-of-Baldr killing 'Hǫðr's adversary'-to-the-pyre. Because Höðr was an unwitting instrument (Loki aimed his hand), Váli's killing of him is usually understood as the price of restoring the balance rather than a punishment of malice — Höðr is the sacrificial deus-ex-machina of the blood-feud. Höðr dies, the blood-debt for Baldr is paid, and the narrative moves to the punishment of the true instigator, Loki.

Accounts II
The binding of Loki with his son's entrailsProse Edda — Gylfaginning ch. 50; Völuspá st. 34 (Hauksbók variant); Loki article

c. 1220 compiled; older oral/poetic basis

After Baldr's death and the gods' determination of Loki's guilt, the Æsir pursue, capture, and bind the trickster. In Gylfaginning ch. 50, the gods 'take Loki's sons' and — in the standard telling — change one into a wolf who tears the other apart; the surviving son's entrails are then used to bind Loki fast to a rock. The Óðinn-son Váli is named (via the Völuspá reading) as the one who 'twists the slaughter-bonds.' Beneath Loki, a serpent is placed to drip venom; Loki's wife Sigyn catches the venom in a bowl, and when she must empty it the dripping venom makes Loki writhe so violently that he causes earthquakes — a state that endures until Ragnarök. Loki is imprisoned until Ragnarök, where he breaks free to fight the gods; the binding is the direct prelude to the final conflict, and Váli is the agent who sets the divine order's closing of accounts in motion.

Váli's survival of RagnarökVafþrúðnismál st. 51

Poetic, recorded 13th century

In the wisdom contest between Óðinn (disguised as Gangráð) and the giant Vafþrúðnir, the giant states that after the world-burning fires of Surtr subside, 'in the gods' home Víðarr and Váli shall dwell,' alongside Móði and Magni inheriting Thor's hammer Mjǫllnir. Váli is therefore, with his half-brother Víðarr (the other great avenger), one of the handful of Æsir who survive the destruction and rebirth of the world. Establishes Váli as part of the reborn pantheon; he and Víðarr together embody the theme that vengeance-figures outlive the order that demanded vengeance.

The euhemerized Boe/Bous (Saxo Grammaticus, Gesta Danorum)Gesta Danorum, Book III

c. 1200-1216

Saxo Grammaticus recasts the god-myth as pseudo-history. Óðinn (Othinus) is a human sorcerer credited with divinity; after Balderus is slain by Hotherus (a mortal king of Denmark/Sweden, an alternate casting of Höðr as warrior-rival), Othinus consults seers and, to sire an avenger, travels to Ruthenia where he enchants the princess Rinda by touching her with rune-marked bark, driving her mad and (in Saxo's account) sleeping with her in various disguises. She bears Boe (or Bous). When grown, Boe is urged by his father to avenge Balderus; Hotherus, foreseeing his death, passes the kingdom to his son Rorik; Boe meets and slays Hotherus in battle, but dies of his wounds a day later, and the Rus' army buries him in a barrow. This is the only source in which the avenger himself dies after the deed, and it historicizes the myth into a dynastic-war narrative. It is likewise notable for its explicitly coercive framing of Rinda's enchantment.
