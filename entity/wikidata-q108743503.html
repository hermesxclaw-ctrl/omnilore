Who It Is

Among the fifty-odd names shouted as the Argo pushed from Pagasae, one barely survives the voyage in memory: Thersanon of Andros, a son of the Sun himself, whose father Helios watches his children scatter across myth as kings, witches, and doomed youths — and whose own mother may have been a living woman, a dead princess turned to incense, or a sea-goddess misspelled by a single letter.

No narrative of deeds, speeches, or character traits survives. Catalogue entry implies heroic status and willingness to join Jason's quest. No attested motive beyond heroic adventure. Behavior, personality, values: unknown. Cannot be reconstructed from parentage alone without speculation.

Heroic Argonaut status

Membership in the Argonauts implies eligibility as a hero of Panhellenic stature, capable of enduring the voyage to Colchis. No specific feat, strength, or skill is attributed.

Limit: No deed narrated; cannot infer combat, sailing, or other expertise. Absence from other catalogues suggests limited traditional importance.

Solar lineage (potential, unattested)

As son of Helios, he is paternal half-brother to Aeetes, Circe, Pasiphae, Perses, and Phaethon in broader Helios genealogy. No text claims he inherited solar powers (light, chariot-driving, sight, oaths). Any such power would be hypothetical reconstruction, not attested.

Limit: No source credits him with heliotic powers, immortality, or magic. Must not be merged with Phaethon or Aeetes' abilities.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No physical description survives for Thersanon. No ancient text, vase painting, sculpture, or mosaic has been identified as depicting him. As son of Helios, later mythographic imagination might infer solar attributes (radiance, golden hair), but this is hypothetical and unattested. Hyginus gives only name, patronymic, and island origin. Attested: male, adult hero eligible for Argo. Unknown: age, stature, dress, attributes, iconography.

Symbols — No attested symbol or iconographic attribute. No verified visual record exists. Status: unknown / no verified visual record.

Domains — Attested habitat: Island of Andros (Cyclades) as birthplace/origin - 'ex Andro insula' (Hyginus). Accounts I
The Birth of Thersanon on AndrosHyginus, Fabulae 14

1st c. BCE - 2nd c. CE (mythographic handbook)

Hyginus records that Thersanon was born on the island of Andros in the Cyclades, son of Helios (Sol), the Titan sun-god who drives the chariot of the sun, and a woman named Leucothoe. No birth narrative, prophecy, or childhood deed is given. The entry is laconic, typical of Hyginus' catalogue style, which lists name, patronymic, and origin for each Argonaut. Andros itself has no other surviving myth linking it to Helios except this entry, making the birthplace distinctive but unexplained. Establishes Thersanon's divine paternity and island identity, distinguishing him from mainland Argonauts. Creates genealogical link to Aeetes and other Heliadae, which would later matter in Colchis.

The Catalogue of the Argonauts - Enrollment for the Quest of the Golden FleeceHyginus, Fabulae 14 (Argonautae enumerati)

Imperial Roman

In Hyginus' list of approximately 50-60 Argonauts, Thersanon appears among heroes summoned by Jason (Iason) to sail on the Argo to retrieve the Golden Fleece from King Aeetes of Colchis. Hyginus' list is one of the longest surviving catalogues, including many names absent from Apollonius. Thersanon's inclusion signals that at least one tradition counted him among the crew, though his position in the list carries no rank or function. No individual deed during mustering is described. Grants Thersanon Panhellenic heroic legitimacy. His presence makes him technically a participant in all subsequent Argonautic episodes, even though no episode names him individually.

The Voyage of the Argo (Collective Narrative Applied to Thersanon)General Argonautic tradition (Apollonius Rhodius Argonautica, Apollodorus Bibliotheca 1.9, Hyginus Fabulae 14-27) applied via Hyginus' membership claim

Archaic to Hellenistic core (8th-3rd c. BCE), transmitted via Roman handbooks

Although no source narrates Thersanon's actions at sea, his status as Argonaut would, by implication, place him aboard the Argo during its canonical perils: departure from Pagasae, the Lemnian sojourn, the Clashing Rocks (Symplegades), the rescue of Phineus from Harpies, and arrival in Colchis. No Hyginus fabula beyond the catalogue assigns him a role in rowing, fighting, or diplomacy. This story is therefore reconstructed as the narrative frame his membership entails, not an attested deed. Must be labeled as inferred frame, not documented feat. If taken literally, Thersanon would be witness to the acquisition of the Golden Fleece and the flight from Colchis with Medea. No source confirms his survival or return.

Accounts II
Kinship with Aeetes in Colchis - Encounter with a Half-BrotherHyginus Fabulae 14 (parentage) + Hesiod Theogony 956-962 / Apollodorus 1.9.1 / Ovid Met. 4 for Helios genealogy

Archaic genealogy + Roman synthesis

Helios is father of Aeetes, king of Colchis and keeper of the Golden Fleece, by Perseis (or Perse). If Thersanon is also son of Helios, Aeetes is his paternal half-brother. The Argonautic quest thus brings Thersanon to his own brother's kingdom as an adversary-ally of Jason. No ancient text describes their meeting, recognition, or interaction. The irony - a son of Helios helping to rob another son of Helios - is a modern scholarly observation (Hard 2004), not an ancient plot point. Hyginus never comments on the relationship. Creates a potential intra-Heliad conflict never explored in surviving literature. Highlights how Hyginus' catalogue generates genealogical implications without narrative follow-through.

The Fate of Leucothoe - The Mother's Death and MetamorphosisOvid, Metamorphoses 4.208-270; Hyginus Fabulae 14 (name only)

Augustan (8 CE) for Ovid; myth set in Babylonian pseudo-mythic past

Ovid tells that Helios (Sol) fell in love with Leucothoe, daughter of King Orchamus of Babylon. When Helios' former lover Clytie (daughter of Oceanus) betrayed the affair out of jealousy, Orchamus, enraged, buried Leucothoe alive despite her innocence. Helios could not revive her but sprinkled nectar and transformed her buried body into a frankincense tree, whose fragrance still recalls the sun-god's love. Ovid mentions no son. Hyginus' claim that Helios and Leucothoe had a son (Thersanon) therefore contradicts Ovid's timeline where Leucothoe dies a virgin princess. Scholars propose either Hyginus knew a lost variant where Leucothoe survived to bear a child, or he meant a different Leucothoe/Leucothea altogether. Explains origin of frankincense and etiology of Clytie's transformation into heliotrope. For Thersanon studies, it creates the central contradiction: can a dead, transformed woman be mother of an Argonaut? Forces textual criticism of Hyginus' orthography.

Andros - Island of the Sun's SonHyginus Fabulae 14; geographic tradition on Andros (Stephanos of Byzantium s.v. Andros, Strabo 10.5.3)

Hellenistic to Roman

Hyginus specifies 'ex Andro insula' - from the island of Andros. Andros, northernmost Cyclade, was mythologically associated with Andreus, with Dionysus (wine spring), and with various eponyms, but rarely with Helios. No other Helios child is localized to Andros, making Thersanon's origin anomalous. No surviving Andrian local history (e.g., Andrian chronicle) mentions him, and no inscription from Andros names him. The island attribution may preserve a lost local tradition or may be Hyginus' invention to distribute Argonaut origins geographically. Gives Thersanon a geographic anchor, useful for modern databases and maps, but without cult or archaeological corroboration. Highlights how mythographic catalogues assign origins to fill out Panhellenic representation.

Thersanon appears in Apollonius Rhodius' Argonautica or is a well-known Argonaut like Orpheus or Heracles. — He appears only in Hyginus Fabulae 14. He is absent from Apollonius (3rd c. BCE), Apollodorus, Valerius Flaccus, Pindar Pythian 4, and all vase paintings of the Argo. His fame is entirely modern handbook-derived.

Thersanon inherited sun powers from Helios, such as driving the sun chariot or wielding solar fire, or was a solar deity. — No source attributes any power, domain, or solar ability to him. Unlike Phaethon (who attempts to drive chariot) or Aeetes (who has Helios' gifts), Thersanon has no attested ability, weapon, or divine function. Any solar power is modern inference from paternity alone.

Leucothoe, his mother, was definitely the Babylonian princess turned into a frankincense tree, making Thersanon the son of a tree-nymph. — Ovid's Leucothoe dies and becomes a tree before bearing a child, and Hyginus never says his Leucothoe became a tree. If Hyginus meant the princess, he knew a different version where she survived; if he meant Leucothea the sea goddess, no tree is involved. The tree-mother idea merges two incompatible variants.
