Carnosaur

also known asCarnosaursAllosauroidea (in some usages)
TypeWarhammer Fantasy Entity
Who It Is
Form

Carnosaurs were large bipedal theropods with a long, narrow skull, large eye sockets, and serrated teeth. They had three fingers per hand, with the second and third digits approximately equal in length. The femur was longer than the tibia, and the pelvis had a triangular pubic boot. Tail chevrons had anterior and posterior bone growth. Skin impressions from Allosaurus and Concavenator show small scales, broad scutate scales on the neck and tail underside, and possibly quill knobs on the ulna of Concavenator (debated).

Powers

Powerful bite with slicing dentition Flat, serrated teeth with equally-sized denticles on both edges, designed for slicing flesh. The narrow skull allowed for efficient cutting. All carnosaurs, especially allosauroids Teeth were not designed for crushing bone; prey had to be sliced into manageable pieces. None significant.

Large body size Largest carnosaurs reached up to 10 meters in length (e.g., Giganotosaurus, Carcharodontosaurus). During the Cretaceous, some grew to sizes comparable to the largest tyrannosaurids. Carcharodontosauridae, Neovenatoridae Larger size may have reduced agility, as indicated by lower CFL muscle proportion. None.

Efficient locomotion Femur longer than tibia, and pelvis designed to reduce stress, with femoral inclination reducing bending and torsion stress. All carnosaurs Not as cursorial as later coelurosaurs. None.

Possible quill-like integument Concavenator preserves knobs on the ulna interpreted by some as quill knobs, suggesting primitive quills or feathers. Concavenator corcovatus Interpretation disputed; may be ligament attachment scars. Some authors support quill knobs, others ligament traces.

Accounts

Marsh coined Carnosauria based on large theropods like Allosaurus and Creosaurus. The group was initially a 'wastebasket' for all large theropods, leading to later revision.

Carcharodontosaurus and related forms were recognized as a distinct family of giant carnosaurs, with Giganotosaurus later found in South America, indicating a global distribution.

Discovery of a Spanish carnosaur with ulnar knobs sparked debate over the presence of primitive feathers in carnosaurs, challenging assumptions about integument evolution.

Proposed a clade uniting Neovenator with megaraptorans, suggesting carnosaurs survived into the Late Cretaceous with coelurosaur-like adaptations.

Scholarly Debates
Composition of Carnosauria
Carnosauria includes only Allosauroidea. ⇌ Carnosauria includes both Allosauroidea and Megalosauroidea.
Evidence for A: Holtz et al. (2004); many phylogenetic analyses.
Evidence for B: Rauhut & Pol (2019); some studies recover Megalosauroidea as paraphyletic with respect to Allosauroidea.
Affinities of Megaraptora
Megaraptorans are carnosaurs (Neovenatoridae). ⇌ Megaraptorans are basal coelurosaurs.
Evidence for A: Benson, Carrano & Brusatte (2010); Carrano et al. (2012).
Evidence for B: Novas et al. (2012); many later studies.
Concavenator quill knobs
Ulnar knobs are quill knobs, indicating feathers. ⇌ They are ligament attachment scars.
Evidence for A: Ortega et al. (2010) based on morphology and comparison with birds.
Evidence for B: Foth (2011) and others note similarities to non-feathered theropods.
What We Don't Know

Detailed anatomical data for many individual species is incomplete due to fragmentary fossils. The phylogenetic position of several taxa (e.g., Chilantaisaurus, Deltadromeus) remains uncertain. Skin impressions are known only from a few specimens. The debate over Concavenator quill knobs requires more fossil evidence. Comprehensive coverage of all named carnosaurs is beyond this dossier's scope.

A way to hold it in your mind

Before the great lizards of the Cretaceous reached their monstrous apogee, a lineage of predators stalked the Jurassic world with a killing anatomy built for slicing flesh from bone. These were the carnosaurs — not merely 'flesh lizards' in name, but a dynasty of apex hunters whose legacy echoes in every subsequent theropod, including the tyrannosaurs and birds. Their story is one of evolutionary innovation, scientific debate, and a name that has been both a dumping ground and a precise cladistic label.
