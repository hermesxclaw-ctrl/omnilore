It lies in the shallow water with just its back showing. And on its back are planks -- flat, upright planks, growing out of its skin, covered in green slime. The old people say this is Mbielu-Mbielu-Mbielu.

Who It Is

Mbielu-Mbielu-Mbielu ('the animal with planks growing out of its back') is one of the strangest cryptids reported from the Congo Basin. Its name, in the Lingala language, directly references its most distinctive feature: large, plank-like protrusions running along its back, described as being covered in algae or moss. Eyewitnesses describe a semi-aquatic creature resembling a lizard or crocodile but much larger -- about the size of a hippopotamus -- with these distinctive vertical plates or 'planks' on its back. The description is strikingly reminiscent of a stegosaur, the Jurassic dinosaur known for the vertical plates running along its spine. Unlike Mokele-mbembe (sauropod-like) or Emela-Ntouka (ceratopsian-like), Mbielu-Mbielu-Mbielu represents a third distinct 'living dinosaur' tradition in the Congo. Locals report that the creature is seen only rarely, typically basking on riverbanks on hot days, partially submerged. Its plates are said to be covered in algae from its long periods underwater. Roy Mackal, the University of Chicago biologist who popularized the Mokele-mbembe expeditions, also collected Mbielu-Mbielu-Mbielu accounts from the same region. Skeptics suggest the creature may be a deformed crocodile with algae-covered scutes, a hippopotamus glimpsed through vegetation, or a large python. However, the specificity and consistency of the 'planks' description across different informants is notable. Like other Congo cryptids, the extreme remoteness and inaccessibility of the Likouala swamps makes verification nearly impossible.

Form

Attested (cryptozoological reports): Mbielu-mbielu-mbielu is described almost exclusively by reference to its back. Witnesses and secondary writers speak of large ‘planks’ or ‘plates’ protruding from the dorsal surface, often covered in green algal growths when the animal surfaces. The rest of the body is rarely seen; villagers reportedly could not describe the head, limbs, or tail in detail, and most sightings occur when the creature is largely submerged. These reconstructions are explicitly speculative and based on analogy rather than indigenous description. Powers

Herbivorous feeding Mbielu-mbielu-mbielu is repeatedly described by local informants (as reported by Mackal and later writers) as herbivorous, feeding on plants rather than animals. This distinguishes it from crocodiles and many large reptiles in the region and is one reason cryptozoologists consider it anomalous. Attested in Mackal’s expedition reports and summarized in French Wikipedia and cryptid reference works. [[2]](https://cryptidz.fandom.com/wiki/Mbielu-Mbielu-Mbielu) [[0]](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mbielu-mbielu-mbielu) [[1]](https://en-academic.com/dic.nsf/enwiki/2547378) No direct observation of feeding has been documented; herbivory is inferred from villagers’ statements and the absence of predatory behavior in stories. Some zoological skeptics argue that herbivory may be a projection or misunderstanding, noting that crocodiles and other reptiles can occasionally be seen among vegetation and might be mischaracterized.

Semi-aquatic lifestyle The creature is said to be amphibious or semi-aquatic, spending most of its time submerged in rivers and marshes, with only its plated back visible above the surface. It is reportedly most active in the late afternoon. Attested in cryptozoological summaries (Cryptozoology.net, Encyclopaedia of Cryptozoology, Cryptid Wiki) and derived from Mackal’s field interviews. [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mbielu-mbielu-mbielu) [[4]](https://cryptidarchives.fandom.com/wiki/Mbielu-mbielu-mbielu) [[2]](https://cryptidz.fandom.com/wiki/Mbielu-Mbielu-Mbielu) No systematic observation exists; activity patterns and degree of aquatic adaptation are based on brief sightings and second-hand reports. Stegosaurid identification conflicts with this semi-aquatic behavior, as paleontological evidence does not support amphibious stegosaur ecology.

Dorsal plates (‘planks’) The defining feature of Mbielu-mbielu-mbielu is a series of large, plank-like or plate-like structures protruding from its back, often covered in green algae. These plates are the basis for its name and for comparisons with stegosaurid dinosaurs. Attested in Mackal’s reports and repeated across cryptozoological literature and reference works. [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mbielu-mbielu-mbielu) [[4]](https://cryptidarchives.fandom.com/wiki/Mbielu-mbielu-mbielu) [[2]](https://cryptidz.fandom.com/wiki/Mbielu-Mbielu-Mbielu) [[1]](https://en-academic.com/dic.nsf/enwiki/2547378) Only the dorsal region is consistently described; the exact shape, number, and arrangement of plates are not standardized across accounts. Different identifications (stegosaur, crocodilian with serrated back, giant monitor lizard, bichir fish) interpret the same ‘planks’ differently, leading to competing reconstructions.

Territorial or elusive behavior (inferred) The rarity of reported sightings and the lack of close encounters have led some writers to infer that Mbielu-mbielu-mbielu is either highly elusive or occupies remote, difficult-to-access territories within the swamp system. Inferred from overall cryptid catalogues and Mackal’s difficulty in obtaining detailed descriptions. [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mbielu-mbielu-mbielu) [[4]](https://cryptidarchives.fandom.com/wiki/Mbielu-mbielu-mbielu) [[2]](https://cryptidz.fandom.com/wiki/Mbielu-Mbielu-Mbielu) This is an interpretive inference; no explicit indigenous statement about territoriality or shyness is recorded. Skeptics argue that rarity of sightings is better explained by the non-existence of the creature or misidentification of known animals rather than by elusive behavior.

Accounts

During expeditions to the Congo Basin in 1979 and 1981, biologist Roy P. Mackal visited the villages of Bounila and Ebolo while searching for the better-known cryptid mokele-mbembe. There he heard of another animal, Mbielu-mbielu-mbielu, described by villagers as an amphibious, herbivorous creature with plates on its back. Witnesses reported seeing the animal in nearby rivers and marshes, usually with most of its body submerged and only the plated back visible, sometimes covered in green algae. Mackal noted that the pygmy informants were unable to describe the entire body, and he regarded the animal as an ‘enigma.’ [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mbielu-mbielu-mbielu) [[2]](https://cryptidz.fandom.com/wiki/Mbielu-Mbielu-Mbielu) [[0]](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mbielu-mbielu-mbielu) [[1]](https://en-academic.com/dic.nsf/enwiki/2547378)

One reported sighting of Mbielu-mbielu-mbielu allegedly occurred near Epena, at a place called Ikekesse, on an unspecified date. Witnesses claimed to see the animal emerging from the water, revealing its plated back. Details of the head, limbs, and tail were not recorded, and the report survives only as a brief note in cryptozoological compilations.

In 1980, a woman from Bouanila named Odette Gesonget was shown books containing images of various animals by Roy P. Mackal. Asked which image resembled the animal her parents had told her about, she selected a picture of a Stegosaurus as matching Mbielu-mbielu-mbielu. However, she clarified that she had never personally seen the creature; her knowledge was second-hand. Mackal and later writers used this selection to support the hypothesis that Mbielu-mbielu-mbielu might be a living stegosaurid, though Mackal himself acknowledged the tenuous nature of the evidence.

Mackal cites anecdotal evidence of African cave paintings depicting elephant-like animals with stegosaur-like backs—large bodies with dorsal plates or ridges. He suggests that such imagery might reflect cultural memory of animals resembling Mbielu-mbielu-mbielu or support the idea of a living stegosaurid. However, the specific location, dating, and scholarly interpretation of these paintings are not detailed in cryptozoological sources.

Content Notes

Cryptozoological treatments of Mbielu-mbielu-mbielu often frame the Likouala region as a mysterious, ‘lost world’ where dinosaurs might survive, echoing older colonial tropes of Africa as a timeless, primitive space. Indigenous witnesses are frequently presented as colorful informants rather than as agents with complex cosmologies, and their testimony is filtered through Euro-American expectations of ‘living dinosaurs.’

While specific academic critiques of Mbielu-mbielu-mbielu are scarce, broader studies of cryptozoology and colonial discourse note how African cryptids are often framed through exoticizing lenses. This entry extrapolates those critiques to the Mbielu-mbielu-mbielu case.

Interpretive synthesis based on Mackal’s expedition narrative and later cryptozoological literature; explicit colonial critique is not present in the primary sources but is widely discussed in scholarship on cryptozoology and colonial imagination (inferred). [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mbielu-mbielu-mbielu) [[1]](https://en-academic.com/dic.nsf/enwiki/2547378)

In the case of Mbielu-mbielu-mbielu, local pygmy communities’ brief and fragmentary descriptions are used to support elaborate speculative reconstructions (e.g., living stegosaurids) without robust consent or co-authorship. Odette Gesonget’s second-hand selection of a Stegosaurus image, despite her never having seen the creature, is repeatedly cited as evidence, illustrating how indigenous voices can be instrumentalized to validate pre-existing cryptozoological desires.

This pattern aligns with critiques of how Western researchers sometimes treat indigenous testimony as raw data to be molded into sensational narratives, rather than as situated knowledge requiring careful contextualization.

Cryptozoological accounts focus almost exclusively on Mbielu-mbielu-mbielu’s potential zoological identity and its resemblance to dinosaurs, with little attention to any ritual, spiritual, or mythic roles the creature might hold in local cosmologies. This omission may reflect a sanitization or erasure of darker, taboo, or sacred aspects of indigenous narratives, if such exist, in favor of a ‘scientific mystery’ framing.

The lack of cultural context is itself a dark-history issue, revealing priorities of cryptozoological research rather than the full spectrum of local knowledge.

What We Don't Know

Major gaps include: (1) absence of physical evidence—no photographs, specimens, or tracks reliably attributed to Mbielu-mbielu-mbielu; (2) lack of detailed, first-hand indigenous narratives beyond brief descriptive statements; (3) missing precise locators (pages, sections) for some key references in Mackal’s book and other works; (4) no independent archaeological or art-historical documentation of the alleged cave paintings with plated-backed animals; (5) limited linguistic analysis of the name mbielu-mbielu-mbielu in Baka and Lingala, beyond glosses reported by cryptozoologists; (6) absence of peer-reviewed zoological or anthropological studies focused specifically on Mbielu-mbielu-mbielu; (7) unclear whether local communities today still use the term or recognize the creature in the same way, as most data come from late 20th-century expeditions. These gaps constrain any attempt to move beyond cryptozoological speculation toward robust ethnographic or zoological understanding.

A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine standing on the bank of a Congo swamp in the late afternoon, watching the water lie flat and opaque—and then, without warning, a row of green, algae-coated ‘planks’ rises from the surface like the spine of some hidden machine. For the villagers of Bounila and Ebolo, that brief glimpse is all they claim to know of Mbielu-mbielu-mbielu, the ‘plated’ animal whose body remains stubbornly unseen. Whether it is misremembered reptile, folkloric river spirit, or the last echo of a dinosaur dream, the creature sits at the uneasy intersection of indigenous testimony and global hunger for living fossils.
