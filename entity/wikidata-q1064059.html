Who It Is

On a mythical mountain in the far west, a goddess as old as Chinese writing itself holds court in a palace of jade, dispensing immortality from peaches that ripen once every three thousand years. She is the Queen Mother of the West, a figure who has evolved from a fierce tiger-toothed deity of punishment into the serene matriarch of the Taoist pantheon—yet her story is far more complex than the serene image suggests.

Early depictions portray her as a fearsome deity of death and punishment, sending down pestilences. Later, she evolved into a benevolent mother goddess, dispenser of prosperity, longevity, and eternal bliss, and guardian of female Daoists. She is seen as a powerful, independent deity representing the ultimate yin.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Queen Mother of the West' is a calque of the Chinese 'Xiwangmu' (西王母), literally 'Western Royal Mother'. 'Yaochi Jinmu' (瑤池金母) means 'Golden Mother of the Jade (or Turquoise) Pond'. 'Wusheng Laomu' (無生老母) means 'Unborn Old Mother', and 'Wujimu' (無極母) means 'Infinite Mother'.

Pronunciation — Xiwangmu: shee-wahng-moo

Aliases — Xiwangmu (Chinese), Seiōbo (Japanese), Seowangmo (Korean), Tây Vương Mẫu (Vietnamese), Yaochi Jinmu (Golden Mother of the Jade Pond), Lady Queen Mother

Ownership — Chinese religion and mythology, with worship extending to Japan, Korea, Vietnam, and other neighboring countries. Also central to Chinese salvationist religions.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In early depictions (Classic of Mountains and Seas), she is described as a ferocious goddess with the teeth of a tiger and a tail like a leopard's (though the source material only mentions tiger's teeth). She wears a distinctive headdress from which Peaches of Immortality are suspended. In later Taoist iconography, she is depicted as a serene, regal mother goddess, often holding court in her palace, surrounded by female attendants.

Symbols — The Big Dipper is one of her symbols. Her palace on Mount Kunlun is a symbol of paradise and cosmic pillar. The Peaches of Immortality are closely associated with her, and she is often shown with them. The Jade Pond (Yaochi) is another symbol.

Domains — Her primary domain is the mythical Mount Kunlun in western China, where she has a palace. She is also associated with the Jade Pond (Yaochi). She is connected to the heavens and the west.

Accounts I
Oracle bone sacrificeShang dynasty oracle bone inscriptions

c. 1766-1122 BCE

The earliest records show sacrifices made to a 'Western Mother' alongside an 'Eastern Mother', indicating she was a powerful force deserving of ritual. Establishes her ancient origins.

Zhuangzi's accountZhuangzi

c. 4th century BCE

Zhuangzi lists the Queen Mother of the West as one of the highest deities who obtained the Dao and sits at Shao kuang, a spiritual western mountain range, with no known beginning or end. Establishes her as an immortal and celestial being.

Accounts II
Encounter with LaoziTu Kuang-ting, 'Records of the Assembled Transcendents'

Tang dynasty (compiled c. 910-920 CE)

In this account, the Queen Mother explicates the Scripture of Constant Purity and Quiet to Laozi and Yin Hsi, positioning her as Laozi's superior and ultimate author of the Dao De Jing. Highlights her role as a teacher and superior in Shangqing Taoism.

Tang dynasty poetryComplete Tang Poems

618-907 CE

The Queen Mother became a popular figure in poetry, with many poems referencing her, reflecting her mythology and her special relationship with women. Solidified her image as a benevolent goddess and guardian of women.

Wusheng Laomu in sectarian scripturesPrecious Scripture of the Dragon Flower

c. 1654

The scripture describes Wusheng Laomu, identified with the Queen Mother, as the Eternal Venerable Mother who conceives from herself and begets yin and yang, establishing Former Heaven. Establishes her as the creator goddess in salvationist religions.
