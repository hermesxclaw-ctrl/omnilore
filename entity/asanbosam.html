Who It Is

Imagine a forest where the trees themselves are the enemy. The Asanbosam doesn't hunt on the ground; it waits in the canopy, a silent predator with iron hooks for hands and a mouth full of metal teeth. It is the nightmare of the Ashanti people, a monster that drops from the branches to snatch the unwary, and its legend has been twisted into the West's 'vampire' canon. But the Asanbosam is not a creature of the grave — it is a creature of the wild, a reminder that the forest is a place of danger, and that the boundary between village and wilderness is a line you cross at your own risk.

The Asanbosam is an ambush predator. It hangs from branches and drops onto its victims, or reaches down with its hooked feet to snatch them up. It feeds on human blood and flesh, and is particularly fond of the blood of children and pregnant women. It is nocturnal and is rarely seen during the day. It is said to be terrified of fire and loud noises, and can be driven away with drums and torches.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name is of Akan (specifically Ashanti/Twi) origin. The exact root is debated, but it is often linked to the Ashanti word for 'vampire' or 'blood-sucker'. 'Sasabonsam' is a closely related or possibly alternate form, with 'bonsam' meaning 'evil spirit' or 'devil' in Twi.

Pronunciation — Ah-sahn-BOH-sahm

Aliases — Asanbosa, Sasabonsam, Asanbosam vampire, Ashanti vampire

Ownership — West African (specifically Ashanti/Akan people of Ghana)

Powers
Iron Hooks and Teeth

The Asanbosam's defining feature. It uses its iron hooks (on hands or feet) to grip tree branches and to tear into its prey. Its iron teeth are used to bite and drink blood.

Limit: The iron is heavy, and the creature is not fast on the ground. It relies on ambush from above.

Arboreal Ambush

It is an expert climber and lurker, able to remain motionless in the branches until prey passes below.

Limit: It is vulnerable when forced to the ground.

Superhuman Strength

It is strong enough to drag a full-grown adult up into the trees.

Limit: Not unlimited; it can be overpowered by a group of people.

Blood Consumption

It drinks the blood of its victims, a trait that has led to its classification as a 'vampire' by Western scholars.

Limit: It must feed regularly to survive.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The Asanbosam is described as a humanoid creature with iron teeth (sometimes described as iron hooks for teeth) and iron hooks for feet or hands. It lives in the branches of trees, often overhanging paths or rivers. It has a red or black complexion, and its hair may be matted or wild. Some descriptions give it a long tail, while others do not. It is often depicted as having a hunched posture, suited to its arboreal life.

Symbols — Iron hooks and teeth are the Asanbosam's primary symbols, representing its predatory nature and its connection to the dangerous, untamed forest. It is a symbol of the dangers that lurk outside the safety of the community, and of the importance of respecting the boundaries of the wild.

Domains — The Asanbosam's domain is the forest canopy, specifically trees that overhang paths, rivers, and villages. It is a liminal creature, dwelling on the boundary between the human village and the wild, dangerous forest. It is most active at night and during times of political or social upheaval.

Accounts I
The Asanbosam and the HunterAshanti oral tradition, documented by R.S. Rattray

Pre-colonial to early 20th century

A hunter, returning from a successful hunt, passes under a large tree. The Asanbosam drops from the branches, its iron hooks tearing into his shoulders. The hunter, though wounded, draws his machete and fights back. He manages to sever the creature's arm, and the Asanbosam, shrieking, flees into the forest. The hunter returns to his village as a hero, but he is forever marked by the scars of the encounter. The story serves as a warning about the dangers of the forest and the bravery of those who face them. The hunter is scarred but victorious. The Asanbosam is wounded and retreats. The story reinforces the idea that the forest is a place of danger that can be overcome with courage and skill.

Accounts II
The Child in the TreeAshanti oral tradition, documented by early anthropologists

Pre-colonial to early 20th century

A child is sent to fetch water from a stream. A tree overhangs the path, and the Asanbosam, hidden in the branches, reaches down and snatches the child. The villagers, hearing the child's screams, rush to the scene, but the creature has already escaped into the forest. The child is never seen again. This story is used to warn children against traveling alone and to instill a fear of the forest. The child is taken. The villagers are left grieving and terrified. The story reinforces the danger of the forest, especially for the vulnerable.
