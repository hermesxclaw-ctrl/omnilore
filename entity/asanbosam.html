Who It Is

A vampiric creature from the folklore of the Ashanti people of Ghana and neighboring regions. The asanbosam is a humanoid with iron teeth and hook-like iron feet, living in trees deep in the forest. It hangs upside-down from branches, waiting to drop onto unsuspecting travelers passing beneath. Once it captures a victim, it uses its iron teeth to tear open the throat and drink the blood. Its hook-feet allow it to swing from branch to branch and seize prey — a distinctly arboreal predator. The asanbosam represents the forest as a space of predatory danger and is part of a broader West African mythology of dangerous forest-dwelling beings, including the sasabonsam (a larger, more monstrous variant).

Form

The Asanbosam is described as a humanoid creature with iron teeth (sometimes described as iron hooks for teeth) and iron hooks for feet or hands. It lives in the branches of trees, often overhanging paths or rivers. It has a red or black complexion, and its hair may be matted or wild. Some descriptions give it a long tail, while others do not. It is often depicted as having a hunched posture, suited to its arboreal life.

The 'vampire' label is an etic (outsider) classification. The Asanbosam is not an undead creature, but a living monster.

Behavior & Habitat

The Asanbosam is an ambush predator. It hangs from branches and drops onto its victims, or reaches down with its hooked feet to snatch them up. It feeds on human blood and flesh, and is particularly fond of the blood of children and pregnant women. It is nocturnal and is rarely seen during the day. It is said to be terrified of fire and loud noises, and can be driven away with drums and torches.

Accounts

A hunter, returning from a successful hunt, passes under a large tree. The Asanbosam drops from the branches, its iron hooks tearing into his shoulders. The hunter, though wounded, draws his machete and fights back. He manages to sever the creature's arm, and the Asanbosam, shrieking, flees into the forest. The hunter returns to his village as a hero, but he is forever marked by the scars of the encounter. The story serves as a warning about the dangers of the forest and the bravery of those who face them.

A child is sent to fetch water from a stream. A tree overhangs the path, and the Asanbosam, hidden in the branches, reaches down and snatches the child. The villagers, hearing the child's screams, rush to the scene, but the creature has already escaped into the forest. The child is never seen again. This story is used to warn children against traveling alone and to instill a fear of the forest.

Field Notes

† The Asanbosam is one of the few 'vampire' creatures in world folklore that is not undead, but a living, breathing predator.

† Its iron hooks and teeth make it a favorite subject for monster designers in tabletop RPGs like Dungeons & Dragons.

† In some versions, the Asanbosam has a red complexion, linking it to the red earth of the forest floor.

† The Asanbosam's name is sometimes used interchangeably with 'Sasabonsam', but the Sasabonsam is often depicted as a winged, more demonic creature.

What We Don't Know

The primary sources are limited to early 20th-century European ethnographies and their derivatives. There is a lack of contemporary, indigenous-authored scholarship on the Asanbosam. The relationship between the Asanbosam and Sasabonsam remains unresolved. The full range of regional variants in Ghana is not documented. The creature's role in contemporary Ashanti culture and media (e.g., Nollywood films, Ghanaian literature) is not well-documented in accessible sources.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of the Asanbosam as the 'predator' of the forest canopy. It's not a ghost or a demon; it's more like a terrifying, blood-drinking ape with iron weapons grafted to its body. It's the 'drop bear' of West African folklore, but with a taste for blood instead of just a nasty bite. It is the ultimate ambush predator, a reminder that in the wild, the danger isn't always on the ground — sometimes it's looking down at you from the leaves above.
