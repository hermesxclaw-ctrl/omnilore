Who It Is

Perched high on medieval cathedrals, stone creatures with gaping mouths have stared down at the faithful for centuries. But these grotesque figures are more than mere decoration—they are functional water spouts, designed to throw rainwater clear of the walls. Their name, echoing the gurgle of water, hides a legend of dragons and saints, and their purpose has been debated from cloister to lecture hall.

Inanimate architectural features, but often designed with expressive features conveying menace, humor, or grotesqueness.

Powers
Water diversion

Functional waterspouts that channel rainwater from the roof and away from the walls, protecting masonry from erosion.

Limit: Their length determines how far water is thrown; heavy ones could fall and cause damage.

Apotropaic protection

Believed to ward off evil spirits and harm from the building and its inhabitants.

Limit: Not a physical ability but a supernatural one attributed to the sculptures.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Typically a carved stone figure, often elongated, with a trough in the back and a spout at the mouth to direct rainwater away from the building. Forms include fantastical animals, dragons, monsters, human-animal hybrids, and humorous figures. Ancient examples were often lion-headed.

Symbols — Apotropaic symbols meant to ward off evil spirits. Dragons and monsters symbolized chaos and evil, while human-animal hybrids represented sin and its consequences. Also served as reminders of the church's protection.

Domains — Found on roofs, parapets, and gutters of buildings, especially churches and cathedrals. Also on secular buildings like palaces and town halls.

Accounts I
Legend of La GargouilleGolden Legend and local Rouen tradition

Medieval (legend set in 7th century)

St. Romanus, bishop of Rouen, is said to have subdued a dragon called La Gargouille that terrorized the countryside. He either used a crucifix or captured it with the help of a condemned man. The dragon was led to Rouen and burned, but its head and neck survived, and were mounted on the cathedral to ward off evil. The head was mounted on the cathedral; the Archbishops of Rouen were granted the right to free a prisoner on the saint's feast day.
