Who It Is

He appears for a single breath in Japan's oldest chronicle — a name in a long genealogy, no story, no shrine legend, no image — and then vanishes. Oogotooshionokami, the 'Great-Word-Pushing Male Deity,' is one of the Kojiki's most tantalizing ghosts: a god whose entire existence is a name, whose meaning scholars still argue over, and whose silence tells us as much about early Japanese myth-making as any thunder god's exploits.

No attested myths show action, speech, or personality. Genealogical list only. Cannot infer temperament, morality, or motive. Later Shinto theology (Yoshida Kanetomo, 15th c.) does not assign a function. Modern encyclopedias list domain as 'unknown' or 'ancestral deity without function.' Status: UNKNOWN.

Powers
Bearing / Mastering Great Words/Matters (Koto-oshi)

Philological inference from name only. If koto = 'word' and oshi = 'to push/bear/master,' the name suggests a deity who upholds or masters important words — potentially oaths, edicts, or kotodama. No feat or miracle ever narrated.

Limit: No attested invocation, no norito, no ritual for oath-taking linked to this deity. Cannot be claimed as functional power.

Genealogical Ancestorship

In Kojiki genealogy, positioned as ancestor in the divine generation preceding later clan ancestors. Some Edo genealogies (e.g., Shinsen Shōjiroku 815, not directly mentioning him but listing his pair group) were used to claim descent for minor clans, but no specific clan claims Oogotooshionokami as ujigami in surviving records.

Limit: No descendant clan securely identified; claim would be speculative.

No Attested Transformative or Combat Abilities

Explicitly: no shapeshifting, weapon, elemental control, or immortality narrative. Unlike major kami (Susanoo, Ōkuninushi), no story of death, rebirth, or defeat.

Limit: Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, but must be reported as unknown rather than invented.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No attested appearance. Kojiki and Nihon Shoki give no physical description, no age, no gendered iconography beyond the name element -wo (male). No Heian or later iconographic tradition (no statues, no paintings, no masks) identified in museum databases (Nara National Museum, Kokugakuin digital archives). Attested status: UNKNOWN. Any visual depiction online is modern invention and should be labeled as hypothetical fan art, not tradition.

Symbols — No attested symbols, weapons, animals, plants, or colours. Not associated in primary sources with mirror, sword, jewel, sakaki, or torii. Edo commentaries occasionally link the pair to 'words/oaths' (koto) and thus to kotodama (word-spirit) belief, but this is reconstructed interpretation, not attested iconography. Status: RECONSTRUCTED / DISPUTED.

Accounts I
The Birth of the Koto-oshi Pair in the KojikiKojiki, Scroll 1, Kamitsumaki §10

Nara period (712 CE), describing mythic age of the gods (Kamiyo)

After Izanagi and Izanami produce the islands and a series of natural deities (wind, trees, mountains, fields), they give birth to Oho-koto-oshi-wo-no-kami and immediately after to Oho-koto-oshi-me-no-kami. The text provides only names in sequence, no dialogue, no action, no location beyond the heavenly/island birth setting. The pair are followed by Kaya no Hime (Thatch Princess) and then by the fire deity Kagutsuchi, whose birth kills Izanami. No immediate consequence narrated. Genealogically, the births fill the sequence between nature deities and the catastrophic birth of fire. Modern scholars (Ellwood 1990, Ujiya 1992) argue the pair were inserted to reach an auspicious number (15 births) or to provide symmetrical male-female pairs before the crisis.

The Nihon Shoki Alternate Variant (Issho) GenealogyNihon Shoki, Book 1, Jindai 1, issho 4

Nara period (720 CE)

The main Nihon Shoki narrative omits the Koto-oshi pair entirely, listing a shorter birth sequence. An appended 'one writing says' variant inserts a longer list of 12 deities including Ōgoto Oshio no Kami (written 大戸忍男神 in some manuscripts) in a different order, after Kukunochi and before Hani-yasu. This shows the compilers were aware of the Kojiki tradition but chose not to elevate it to the main text. Textual consequence: demonstrates instability of early genealogy and editorial selection. The deity's status was already marginal by 720, relegated to an alternative tradition.

Kogoshūi's Recapitulation for Clan LegitimacyKogoshūi, Section 2 (807 CE)

Early Heian (807 CE)

Imbe no Hironari, seeking to defend Imbe ritual prerogatives against the Nakatomi/Fujiwara, retells the Kojiki creation genealogy verbatim, including the Oho-koto-oshi-wo/me pair without comment. No new story is added; the inclusion serves to prove his knowledge of the 'correct' ancient genealogy and thus his clan's antiquity. Shows that by 807 the pair were considered part of canonical knowledge for court ritualists, even though they had no cult. No ritual function assigned.

Accounts II
Motoori Norinaga's Philological Rescue (Kojiki-den)Motoori Norinaga, Kojiki-den, vol.7 (1798)

Edo period (1798), commenting on Nara texts

Norinaga, confronted with a name-only deity, devotes a short annotation to etymology rather than narrative. He rejects Buddhist-influenced allegory and argues oshi means 'to endure, to bear on the back,' thus the deity 'bears great words' — i.e., sustains oaths or imperial edicts. He admits no shrine or story survives and warns against inventing one. Established the dominant modern etymology ('Great Word-Bearing Male') cited by Philippi and Kokugakuin. Also established the scholarly norm that absence of story should be admitted, not filled.

Modern Encyclopedic Reduction to 'Name-Only Deity'Kokugakuin Encyclopedia of Shinto (online, 2023 update) and Nihon Shinwa Jiten (1994)

Contemporary (1994-2023), summarizing Nara period

Modern reference works list Oogotooshionokami in tables of 'deities born from Izanagi and Izanami' with entry length of 2-3 lines: name, reading, birth order, note 'details unknown, no shrine, no function.' The 'story' is precisely the lack of story — used as a case study for how the Kojiki padded genealogies for numerological or symmetrical purposes. Cements status as an example of 'filler kami' in university teaching; no new myth created, but the meta-story of scholarly debate becomes the reception history.

The Engishiki Silence — The Story That Isn't ThereEngishiki, Books 8-10 (927 CE) — negative evidence

Heian (927 CE)

When the Heian court codified all 2,861 shrines and 27 norito prayers, Oogotooshionokami was omitted entirely. This non-appearance is itself a historical event: it shows that within 200 years of the Kojiki, the deity had no living cult to codify, unlike contemporaneous birth-list deities such as Kukunochi (who gained shrines) or Kaya no Hime. Explains why no medieval or modern shrine claims him as main kami (shushin) and why no matsuri, kagura, or ema tradition exists. The silence became permanent.

Oogotooshionokami is a major Shinto god with shrines and festivals. — He has no dedicated shrine in the Engishiki Jinmyōchō (927) and no main enshrinement (shushin) in the modern Jinja Honcho database. At most he appears as a minor auxiliary name in genealogical scrolls, not as an active cult deity.

The name means 'Great Harbor Male God' and he is a sea god. — Harbor (to) reading is Aston's tentative 1896 guess based on variant kanji 大戸; mainstream philology (Norinaga, Philippi, Kokugakuin) reads koto as 'word/matter' and marks both as uncertain ateji. No primary source links him to the sea.

Oogotooshionokami and Oogotooshimenokami are husband and wife who rule over words together. — Primary sources list them as consecutive births (siblings). No marriage, co-rule, or joint action is narrated. Pairing is structural (male/female suffix) common in Kojiki birth lists, not evidence of spousal relationship.

He appears in anime, games, or manga as a character. — No documented appearance in major games (Megami Tensei, Fate, Touhou), anime, or manga as of 2026. Searches of Media Arts Database (Japan), VNDB, and game wikis return zero hits. Any such appearance would be a homonym or original character borrowing the name.
