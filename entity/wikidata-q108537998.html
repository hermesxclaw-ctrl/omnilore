Who It Is

He has red hair in a world that distrusts it, cold blue eyes that have watched empires rot, and a curse that will not let him die. Karl Edward Wagner's Kane is not a barbarian who stumbles into sorcery — he is a sorcerer who prefers the sword, an immortal murderer who calls himself a scholar, and a man cursed by a mad god to wander forever, unable to find rest even in death. Where Conan breaks chains, Kane forges them for others.

Attested: Kane is amoral, hyper-intelligent, erudite, and utterly ruthless. He is a scholar, sorcerer, strategist, and swordsman who views morality as a useful fiction for controlling others. He is charismatic and can inspire loyalty in soldiers, but treats people as tools. He is driven by curiosity, boredom, and a hunger for knowledge and power rather than wealth or glory; Wagner calls him 'a man out of time, cursed with immortality and cursed with intellect.' Powers
Cursed Immortality / Inability to Die

Kane was cursed by a mad god (the creator deity of his people) after Kane killed him — or, in variant phrasing, after Kane's rebellion. The curse: Kane can be wounded and feels pain but cannot be killed by any means, including age, disease, or violence. He heals rapidly from otherwise fatal wounds. He has lived for thousands of years.

Limit: Not invulnerability — he can be injured, incapacitated, and suffers. He can be trapped or buried. He cannot die even if he wishes to; attempted suicide fails. The curse does not protect allies.

Master Swordsman (Left-Handed)

One of the deadliest swordsmen of his age, fighting left-handed with exceptional speed and strength. Prefers a heavy broadsword or bastard sword wielded one-handed.

Limit: Still mortal in skill — can be outnumbered, surprised, or matched by superior sorcery. No supernatural speed beyond human peak.

Sorcery and Occult Knowledge

Kane is a trained sorcerer and scholar of forbidden lore. Feats include summoning demons (Bloodstone), necromancy, illusion, mind-control, and creation of magical artifacts. He is not an innate magician but a learned one, requiring study, components, and ritual.

Limit: Sorcery is slow, costly, and dangerous; requires preparation, rare components, and exact ritual. It drains him and can fail catastrophically. He often prefers steel because sorcery is unreliable. He is vulnerable to stronger sorcerers and to elder entities.

Tactics, Strategy, and Statecraft

Brilliant military commander and political manipulator. Kane has led armies, sacked cities, and ruled as tyrant. He understands logistics, siege warfare, and court intrigue.

Limit: His amorality alienates allies; victories are often pyrrhic and he abandons causes when bored.

Erudition and Longevity of Knowledge

Due to millennia of life, Kane has accumulated vast knowledge of languages, history, medicine, alchemy, and forgotten sciences. He can read dead languages and recall fallen empires firsthand.

Limit: Knowledge is not omniscience; much has been forgotten or is irrelevant. His memory is human, not perfect.

Enhanced Resilience and Recovery

Rapid healing from wounds that would kill a normal man; resistance to disease and aging. Can survive blood loss, poison, and exposure longer than human norm.

Limit: Not instant regeneration; requires days to weeks for severe wounds. Pain is fully felt.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Attested consistently across all texts: Kane is tall (over six feet), powerfully muscled but lean and wolfish rather than bulky, with flaming red hair worn long, a close-trimmed red beard, and cold, pale blue eyes described as icy or glacial. His skin is pale. His most distinctive physical trait is left-handedness, emphasized as unnatural and sinister in-world. He is usually depicted in dark, functional clothing — black or grey — often with armor

Symbols — No personal sigil. In-world symbols associated with Kane: the sword (pragmatic violence over chivalry), the left hand (inversion, sinister), and eyes (cold observation). The Bloodstone itself — a dark, veined gem containing a demon — becomes his emblem in Bloodstone. Wagner deliberately avoids elaborate iconography; Kane's mark is absence of ornament. On meta-level, Wagner's introductions use the phrase 'the Mystic Swordsman'

Domains — Kane wanders a prehistoric Earth-like world that is not named as Earth but is implied to be a forgotten age before recorded history — Wagner described it as 'a world that might have been.' Settings are pseudo-medieval to early Renaissance in technology: city-states (Carsultyal, Ingoldi), steppes, frozen wastes, jungles, and ruined elder civilizations. Accounts I
Reflections for the Winter of My SoulDeath Angel's Shadow (1973), novella

1973 (written 1971-72)

In the frozen northern city of Carsultyal, Kane takes service under the decadent aristocrat Lages, manipulating a love triangle involving the noblewoman Efrel and a doomed poet. Kane orchestrates murder and betrayal to seize power, while haunted by his immortality in the endless winter. The story reveals Kane's origin curse in flashback: he slew his mad creator-god and was condemned to wander. It ends with Kane abandoning the ruined city to the ice, alone. Establishes Kane's core curse and amoral philosophy; considered Wagner's definitive Kane statement. Sets tone for all later stories as psychological horror rather than adventure.

Cold LightDeath Angel's Shadow (1973), novella

1973

Kane, acting as a sorcerer-mercenary, is hired to resurrect the dead wife of a nobleman using forbidden cold light — a form of necromantic stasis. The ritual succeeds partially, returning a thing that is not human. Kane's cold intellectual curiosity leads him to complete the experiment even knowing it will destroy his employer. The tale is a study in Kane's scientific amorality. Demonstrates Kane's sorcerous method and his willingness to sacrifice others for knowledge. Often cited as Wagner's Lovecraftian Kane story.

MirageDeath Angel's Shadow (1973), novella

1973

Kane wanders a desert and encounters a city that may be illusion, memory, or demon trap. He becomes entangled with a sorceress who claims to know the secret of ending his curse. The story blurs reality and hallucination, ending ambiguously with Kane killing the sorceress and the city vanishing, suggesting his curse is inescapable even by magic. Reinforces theme that Kane's curse cannot be lifted by mortal sorcery; deepens existential horror.

Darkness Weaves / Darkness Weaves with Many ShadesNovel, Powell Books 1970; revised Warner Books 1978

1970/1978

Kane arrives in the city of Carsultyal (continuity separate from 'Reflections') as the pawn and then master of the sorceress Efrel (called Felara in 1970 text). Efrel seeks to use Kane to steal the secret of immortality from the deathless wizard Thovnos. Kane betrays both sides, using sorcery and swordplay to claim the secret for himself, only to find it is a lie — true immortality is his curse alone. The novel introduces Kane's world of warring city-states and elder ruins. First Kane novel; establishes worldbuilding and Kane's role as mercenary-sorcerer. 1978 revision is considered definitive and expands Kane's backstory.

Accounts II
BloodstoneNovel, Warner Paperback Library 1975

1975

Kane discovers the Bloodstone, a sentient gem containing a demon that grants power at terrible cost. He manipulates the thief Teres and the warlord Elric? No — warlord Thov? Actually: Kane manipulates the outlaw Joal and the merchant house of Carsultyal to obtain the stone, then uses it to raise an army of the dead. The demon demands souls; Kane feeds it his enemies and finally his allies, learning that the stone's power is ultimately parasitic and cannot sate his boredom. He discards it, leaving a trail of corpses. Most famous Kane novel; defines Kane's relationship to demonic artifacts and his willingness to use necromancy on a mass scale. Adapted into plot summaries for many encyclopedias.

Dark CrusadeNovel, Warner Paperback Library 1976

1976

Kane joins a religious crusade as a mercenary captain, ostensibly to lead the army of the fanatical Prophet against the sorcerer-king of Thovnos. In truth Kane seeks the ancient technological/sorcerous weapons buried beneath the holy city. He orchestrates the crusade's slaughter from within, betraying both crusaders and defenders to seize the vault. The novel ends with Kane walking away from the burning city, his prize already proving hollow. Shows Kane as military strategist and critic of organized religion; Wagner's most overtly anti-religious Kane story, reflecting his nihilistic worldview.

Two Suns SettingNight Winds (1978), novelette

1975 (first published in Fantastic, 1975)

Kane defends the doomed city of Ingoldi against a barbarian horde, not from loyalty but because the city's library contains a book he wants. He organizes a brilliant defense, then abandons the city at the last moment when he secures the book, leaving defenders to die. The story is a cold study in utilitarian betrayal. Frequently anthologized as exemplary grimdark warfare fiction; demonstrates Kane's tactical genius and moral vacuum.

Sing a Last Song of ValdeseNight Winds (1978), novelette

1976 (first published in Chacal #2, 1976)

Kane, posing as a wandering bard and loremaster, becomes patron to the poet Valdese in a decaying city. He nurtures Valdese's genius while slowly destroying him through jealousy and manipulation, culminating in Valdese's suicide. Kane's attempt to preserve art through cruelty reveals his own inability to create, only to destroy and observe. Most introspective Kane story; explores Kane's envy of mortal creativity and his existential emptiness. Cited by critics as Wagner's most literary Kane tale.

Kane is just a Conan clone or pastiche. — Wagner created Kane explicitly as an anti-Conan: where Conan is a barbarian who distrusts magic and acts on instinct and honor, Kane is a cultured, cynical intellectual who uses sorcery and despises honor. Wagner was a Howard scholar (he edited Conan pastiches) and deliberately inverted Howard's archetype. Critics from Fritz Leiber to Moorcock recognized Kane as a deconstruction, not imitation.

Kane is a hero or antihero who does good despite himself. — Wagner insisted Kane is a villain protagonist. He rarely does good; when he helps others it is accidental or manipulative. Unlike Elric who seeks redemption, Kane embraces amorality. Wagner called him 'a cold-blooded killer' in his 1973 introduction.

Kane's immortality means he is invincible and cannot be hurt. — Kane can be wounded, tortured, and incapacitated; he feels pain fully and heals at a slightly accelerated but still human rate. His curse is that he cannot die, not that he cannot suffer. Multiple stories show him bedridden for weeks after battles.

Karl Edward Wagner's Kane is the same as Solomon Kane (Robert E. Howard) or Kane from Command & Conquer. — Three entirely separate characters: Solomon Kane (1928, Puritan avenger), Wagner's Kane (1970, immortal sorcerer-swordsman), and Command & Conquer's Kane (1995, charismatic cult leader). They share only the name, derived independently from Cain. No narrative or rights connection.

There is a Kane movie, TV series, or official game. — No authorized screen or game adaptation of Wagner's Kane has ever been produced. Wagner optioned film rights briefly in the 1980s but no film was made. All 'Kane' games and films refer to other Kanes (e.g., Kane & Lynch, Citizen Kane). Fan-made stats for D&D exist but are unofficial.
