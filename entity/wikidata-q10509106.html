Who It Is

In the annals of Buddhist hagiography, few figures embody the paradox of emptiness as vividly as Subhūti, the disciple who understood nothingness so profoundly that he could sit beneath a tree and have flowers rain from the heavens—yet who, in his own words, had spoken not a single word. His life spans the arc from a wealthy merchant's son to the foremost exponent of the Buddha's most radical teaching, and his legacy weaves through the Diamond Sutra, the Zen koans, and even the wild adventures of the Monkey King.

In Theravada accounts, Subhūti is known for his gentle, peaceful nature and his dedication to loving-kindness meditation. He is described as teaching without distinction and living in remote solitude. In Mahayana texts, he is portrayed as a deep thinker, often questioning the Buddha to elicit profound teachings on emptiness. In the northern tradition, he is said to have been hot-tempered in his youth but later became calm. In Chinese commentaries, he is depicted as compassionate but somewhat misguided in his alms-round practice, which the Buddha corrected.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Subhūti is derived from Sanskrit/Pali: 'Su' meaning 'good' or 'excellent' and 'bhūti' meaning 'being' or 'prosperity'. Thus, it can be interpreted as 'excellent being' or 'one who has good existence'. In Chinese, 須菩提 (Xūpútí) is a phonetic transliteration of the Sanskrit.

Pronunciation — Sanskrit: [s̪uˈbʱuːt̪i]; Pali: [s̪uˈbʱuːt̪i]; Mandarin: [ɕypʰutʰi]

Aliases — Subhuti (simplified), Xūpútí (Mandarin Chinese), Puti Zushi (Taoist fictional counterpart in Journey to the West), Nanda (previous life in Pali tradition)

Ownership — Buddhism (shared across Theravada, Mahayana, and Vajrayana traditions); also appears in Chinese folk religion and literature (as Puti Zushi).

Powers
Mastery of loving-kindness (mettā-jhāna)

Subhūti could enter deep meditative states of loving-kindness, which made his alms-rounds highly meritorious for donors. It also prevented rain from falling while he meditated in the open.

Limit: This ability is not described as a superpower but as a result of meditative attainment.

Understanding of emptiness (śūnyatā)

In Mahayana, Subhūti is the foremost in understanding the emptiness of all phenomena. He is able to articulate this profound teaching and is the Buddha's primary interlocutor in the Diamond Sutra.

Limit: Not described as a supernatural power but as an intellectual and spiritual attainment.

Ability to see the Buddha's spiritual body

When the Buddha descended from Tavatimsa heaven, Subhūti, by meditating, perceived the Buddha's dharmakaya before others saw his physical form.

Limit: This is a legendary event, not a general ability.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No specific physical description is given in the sources. Depictions in Buddhist art typically show him as a monk, often with a shaved head and wearing monastic robes, sometimes with a serene expression.

Symbols — In Mahayana art, Subhūti is often depicted in scenes of the Buddha teaching the Diamond Sutra. He may be shown holding a sutra or in a gesture of discussion. In Zen, the story of the flower shower is sometimes depicted. In Chinese literature, Puti Zushi is portrayed as a Taoist immortal with a long beard, holding a whisk, and often riding a crane.

Domains — As a monk, he lived in forests and monasteries, particularly Jetavana. He is associated with the city of Śrāvastī. In the story of the leaf hut, he meditated in the open air near Rājagaha.

Accounts I
The Previous Life of NandaPali Canon (commentarial tradition)

Time of Padumuttara Buddha (legendary past)

In a past life, Subhūti was a wealthy man named Nanda who became a hermit. When Padumuttara Buddha visited his hermitage, a monk skilled in metta gave a teaching. Other hermits attained arahantship, but Nanda instead resolved to become the foremost disciple in being worthy of gifts of a future Buddha. Nanda's aspiration was fulfilled in his last life as Subhūti.

Ordination at JetavanaPali tradition and northern Buddhist texts

Lifetime of Gautama Buddha (circa 5th century BCE)

Subhūti was present at the dedication of Jetavana Monastery, built by his brother (or uncle) Anāthapiṇḍika. Hearing the Buddha teach, he ordained as a monk. In the northern version, he had a bad temper but later calmed his mind through practice. Subhūti became a monk and eventually an arahant.

The Leaf Hut and the RainPali commentary (Dhammapada commentary)

Lifetime of Gautama Buddha

King Bimbisara promised to build a hut for Subhūti but forgot. Subhūti meditated in the open air, and due to his virtue, rain did not fall. When the king learned of this, he built a leaf hut, and rain immediately fell. Demonstrates Subhūti's spiritual power and the king's respect.

The Flower ShowerZen koan (also appears in the Diamond Sutra commentary)

Unknown (likely medieval China)

Subhūti sat under a tree in a state of sublime emptiness. Flowers fell around him. Gods praised his discourse on emptiness, but Subhūti said he had not spoken. The gods replied that since neither spoke nor heard, that was true emptiness. Blossoms showered like rain. Illustrates the ineffable nature of emptiness.

Accounts II
The Buddha's Descent from TavatimsaNorthern Buddhist texts (e.g., Dhammapada commentary, but also Mahayana sources)

Lifetime of Gautama Buddha

When the Buddha returned from heaven, disciples rushed to greet him. Subhūti, recalling the Buddha's teaching that one sees him through meditation, remained in meditation. The Buddha declared that Subhūti was the first to greet him, having seen his spiritual body. Highlights the superiority of seeing the Dharma over the physical form.

The Diamond Sutra DiscourseVajracchedikā Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra

Circa 1st century BCE - 1st century CE

The Buddha asks Subhūti how a bodhisattva should stand, progress, and control his thoughts. The dialogue explores emptiness, non-attachment, and the nature of reality. Subhūti's questions and the Buddha's answers form the core of the sutra. The Diamond Sutra becomes one of the most important Mahayana texts.

Prophecy in the Lotus SutraSaddharma Puṇḍarīka Sūtra

Circa 1st century BCE - 2nd century CE

In Chapter 6, the Buddha predicts that Subhūti, along with Mahākāśyapa, Mahākātyāyana, and Mahāmaudgalyāyana, will become Buddhas in the future. Affirms the eventual Buddhahood of all beings.

Puti Zushi and Sun WukongJourney to the West (Xiyouji)

16th century CE

The Monkey King Sun Wukong travels to the Cave of the Slanting Moon and Three Stars, where the Taoist sage Puti Zushi (based on Subhūti) teaches him supernatural powers, including the 72 transformations and the cloud somersault. The master later banishes Sun Wukong for showing off. Sun Wukong gains his powers and becomes a central figure in the novel.
