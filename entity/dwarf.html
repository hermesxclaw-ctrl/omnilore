Who It Is

The dwarves of Norse mythology are the master smiths and treasure-hoarders of the earth, born not of gods or men but from the rotting flesh of the primordial giant Ymir or, in Snorri Sturluson's account, from the blood and bones of the slain giant Brimir — maggot-like at first, until the gods gifted them reason and human shape. They are short, swarthy, and dwell in the stone beneath mountains, where they forge the most powerful artifacts of the Norse cosmos: Odin's spear Gungnir (which never misses), Thor's hammer Mjölnir (which returns when thrown), Freyr's ship Skíðblaðnir (which folds into a pocket), the golden-bristled boar Gullinbursti, and the necklace Brísingamen. The Dvergatal ('Catalogue of Dwarves') in the Völuspá stanza of the Poetic Edda lists over 100 named dwarves, including the great smiths Brokkr and Eitri, the wise Alvíss (turned to stone by Thor when he sought to marry Thor's daughter), and the treasure-guarding Andvari whose cursed ring and gold would later destroy Sigurd and the Volsungs. Unlike the elves who are luminous and ethereal, the dwarves are chthonic — literally of the earth — and their gifts to the gods are always double-edged: splendid but with a hidden tax. They appear across Germanic folklore as mine-gnomes, changelings, and hoard-guardians, and their modern descendants populate every fantasy world from Tolkien's Erebor to Dungeons & Dragons to Terry Pratchett's Discworld.

Origins

The Poetic Edda's Völuspá — the seeress's recitation — describes the dwarves being formed from Ymir's corpse by the gods, then catalogued across sixty stanzas of strange compound names (each a compressed story: Mjöðvitnir 'Mead-Wolf', Fíli 'File', Fundinn 'Found'). The Prose Edda adds that dwarves are 'of the earth' and will not tolerate sunlight — those caught above ground at dawn turn to stone, a fate that meets the dwarf Alvíss in the Alvíssmál poem when Thor tricks him with a riddle contest that lasts past sunrise.

Form

Short in stature, broad-shouldered, dark-haired or red-haired, with pale or swarthy skin — described in the Eddas as having a 'dvergr's stature' (stunted height). By the 19th-century Romantics and Wagner's operas, they acquired beards, mining caps, and a permanent stoop from centuries underground.

Powers

Peerless smithcraft — the forging of weapons, jewelry, and artifacts that exceed the work of gods. Deep-earth knowledge: they know the names and veins of every ore, the making of mead from mountain springs, and the riddle-magic of runes. Gift of foresight: many dwarves in the Eddas utter prophecies about the doom of heroes who take their treasures.Master craftsmanship and forging Dvergar are renowned for their ability to forge unparalleled artifacts, including weapons, rings, and other treasures. In Skáldskaparmál, dwarfs create Mjölnir, Gungnir, Draupnir, and other items that become central to the gods’ power. Their craft is often performed under time pressure or as part of a wager, yet the results are extraordinary. Prose Edda (Skáldskaparmál); Poetic Edda (Reginsmál, Völuspá dwarf catalog); Völsunga saga Their work typically requires payment, oaths, or specific conditions; they may be bound by contracts or coerced by gods. Crafting is not portrayed as limitless; it is tied to specific narrative tasks. Some sources treat dwarfs as the primary smiths of the gods, while others attribute certain artifacts to giants or gods themselves. Scholarly debate exists over whether dwarfs were originally a distinct class of craftsmen or a broader category of chthonic beings with multiple roles.Magical knowledge and wisdom Dvergar possess extensive cosmological and linguistic knowledge. Alvíss demonstrates mastery of names for natural phenomena across different beings’ vocabularies, suggesting a deep, cross-world understanding of reality. Poetic Edda – Alvíssmál Wisdom can be outmaneuvered by divine cunning; Thor defeats Alvíss by delaying him until sunrise, implying that dwarfs are vulnerable despite their knowledge. Not all dwarfs are portrayed as wise; some are greedy or short-sighted. The extent to which dwarfs as a class embody wisdom versus specialized individuals like Alvíss is debated.Cursing and fate-binding Dvergar can lay powerful curses on objects and people. Andvari curses his ring and gold, ensuring tragedy for its possessors, a curse that drives the Völsung narrative. Poetic Edda – Reginsmál; Völsunga saga Curses are tied to specific injustices (theft, coercion) and may require verbal declaration. They are not depicted as casual or omnipresent. Some scholars interpret dwarf curses as narrative devices rather than reflections of a broader belief in dwarfs as fate-weavers; others see them as part of a wider Germanic tradition of object-bound fate.Shape-shifting and concealment Certain dwarfs appear capable of changing form or moving between visibility and invisibility, often associated with stones or water. Andvari is described as living in a waterfall and may take the form of a fish, suggesting shape-shifting or dual nature. Poetic Edda – Reginsmál; Völsunga saga Shape-shifting is not universally attested for all dwarfs and may be specific to particular figures like Andvari. Interpretations differ on whether Andvari is truly a dwarf who shape-shifts, a spirit associated with the waterfall, or a narrative fusion of motifs.Association with disease and misfortune Runic evidence suggests that ‘dwarf’ could denote a harmful spirit or disease-causing entity, with inscriptions possibly intended to ward off such beings. Ribe runic inscription; discussed in Ugnius Mikučionis, 2017 The exact meaning of ‘dwarf’ in these inscriptions is uncertain; it may be metaphorical or refer to a specific type of affliction. This harmful-spirit role contrasts with the more neutral or even beneficial craftsman role in mythological texts, indicating multiple conceptual layers of ‘dwarf’ in Nordic belief.Divine smithcraft The dwarfs forge the load-bearing artifacts of the Norse cosmos: Thor's hammer Mjöllnir (which returns to the hand and shatters whatever it strikes), Odin's spear Gungnir (which never misses), the ring Draupnir (which drips eight new rings every ninth night), the boar Gullinbursti with its shining bristles, the foldable ship Skíðblaðnir, Sif's golden hair, the cursed sword Tyrfing, the necklace Brísingamen. The gods' power structure literally rests on dwarf-made hardware. Attested: Gylfaginning ch. 45 and Skáldskaparmál (Prose Edda); Hervarar saga; Sörla þáttr Their best work can be sabotaged: Loki, in fly-shape, bites the bellows-man's hand and the hammer comes out with a handle a hand's breadth too short. The craft is presented as a given — no source explains how it works. The same smiths who arm the gods also forge the instruments of mass death (Tyrfing); the tradition never reconciles the two.Curse and binding magic Andvari lays a curse on his ring that it shall be the death of its owners — two brothers and eight princes in the classic formulation — and the entire Völsung-Nibelung cycle runs on that curse. Dvalinn and Dulinn curse Tyrfing so that every draw must kill. Brokkr sews Loki's lips together with wire as a forfeit for the wager of the head. Attested: Reginsmál prose, Völsunga saga, Hervarar saga, Gylfaginning/Skáldskaparmál Curses have triggers and terms: the ring's power binds only its wearers; Tyrfing's curse fires when drawn; the head-wager fails on a legalistic loophole (the neck was not included in the stake). Dwarf magic is presented as nearly absolute in effect but remarkably pedantic in scope — a pattern the sagas use for comedy as often as horror.Shape-shifting Andvari lives in his waterfall in the form of a pike and is caught only when Loki borrows Rán's net; later folk tradition has the underground folk taking animal shapes freely. Attested: Reginsmál prose (pike); folklore layer for the rest The attested cases tie the shape to a place or a trap; there is no evidence of unrestricted transformation. None recorded between sources on this point; the pike episode is stable across Reginsmál and Völsunga saga.Mastery of names and lore Alvíss (All-Wise) can name every thing in every world — the poem is a catalogue of poetic synonyms for heaven, earth, moon, sun, fire and the rest, and the dwarf commands all of them. The dvergatal itself is a name-hoard: roughly a hundred dwarf names, many occurring nowhere else, functioning as compressed lore. Attested: Alvíssmál; Völuspá stanzas 10–16 Knowledge does not save Alvíss: he answers every question and is destroyed anyway, because the questions were a delaying tactic. Wisdom without cunning is the dwarf's recurring flaw. Alvíss is simultaneously the wisest being in the poem and its most easily tricked one; the text never resolves the tension.Invisibility and wonder-gear (later Germanic) The tarnkappe, the cloak or cap of invisibility and strength, belongs to the dwarf Alberich in the Nibelungenlied and appears again in the dwarf-king tale of Laurin, whose girdle grants the strength of twelve men. Attested: Middle High German epic, 13th century — NOT in the Norse sources; flagged as a distinct continental tradition These are properties of specific objects, not dwarf biology; the gear changes hands by combat or theft. Later fantasy has merged this German gear into the general Norse dwarf picture; the sources keep them separate.Vulnerability to daylight Alvíss is caught above ground at sunrise and, per the Codex Regius prose, turns to stone; Scandinavian folk tradition likewise has the underground folk flee or die in sunlight. Attested: Alvíssmál (poem plus possibly late prose frame); folklore layer The petrification rule is weak: the poem itself only announces the sunrise, and the turning-to-stone sentence may be a scribal gloss. No other Eddic text gives dwarfs a general sunlight weakness — that rule is chiefly trolls' property in later folklore. Some scholars read the episode as a one-off trick, others as a fossil of a general rule; the evidence cannot settle it.Affliction (Old English register) In Old English, dweorg names not only the being but a disease — a cramping, feverish torment — and the Lacnunga charm Wið dweorh is a remedy against it. The being and the sickness may be the same thing: a dwarf as an inside-attacking demon. Attested: Lacnunga (BL MS Harley 585); Old English glosses Whether the Anglo-Saxon disease-demon is the same population as the Norse smith-dwarfs is an open question; the two registers never meet in one text. This is the sharpest regional split in the material: craftsmen in the North, affliction in the West.Longevity Dwarfs outlast human generations in the saga material — Tyrfing's curse runs through a dynasty while its makers simply persist — and folk tradition treats the hill-folk as ageless. Reconstructed from Hervarar saga's timeline and folklore; no source states dwarf immortality outright No text says they cannot die; they are simply never shown dying of age. None attested; the longevity is inferred, not stated.

Weaknesses & Wards

Sunlight turns them to stone. Their greed is legendary — a curse follows the treasures they forge for others. They are bound to their subterranean dwellings and grow weak or sick when kept long above ground.

Accounts

After the world's first murder, the gods sit in judgment and decide who shall shape the dwarf-host out of Brimir's blood and Bláinn's limbs — the flesh of the slain giant Ymir. Snorri renders this literally: maggots bred in Ymir's body, and the gods gave them reason and human shape, though they remained beings of earth and stone. The first is Mótsognir, the second Durinn, and then the seeress recites the dvergatal, a roll of roughly a hundred names — Gandálfr, Bífurr, Bafurr, Bömburr, Nóri, Óri, Óinn, Fíli, Kíli, Þorinn, Eikinskjaldi and many hapax names no one can translate. The gods did not create their craftsmen from nothing: they recruited them out of a giant's corpse.

When the sons of Borr make the sky from Ymir's skull, they set four dwarfs — Austri, Vestri, Norðri, Suðri, East, West, North, South — beneath its four corners to bear it. The dwarfs are not decorations but infrastructure: the vault of heaven stands on their heads.

After Loki cuts Sif's hair, Thor forces him to make good. Loki commissions the sons of Ívaldi, who produce Gungnir, Skíðblaðnir and Sif's golden hair, then wagers his own head with the dwarf Brokkr that Brokkr's brother Eitri cannot match them. Brokkr works the bellows while Eitri forges: Gullinbursti, then Draupnir, then the hammer. Loki, in fly-shape, bites the bellows-blower to spoil the work; the hammer's handle comes out short — but it is still the greatest treasure of all. Loki loses his head and argues that the wager covered the head only, not the neck; the dwarf, denied his prize, sews Loki's lips shut with wire.

The dwarf Alvíss, all-wise, has arranged to marry Thor's daughter Þrúðr. Thor says the dwarf must first prove his wisdom and questions him through the night about the names of heaven, earth, moon, sun, clouds, wind and fire in the speech of gods, men, elves and giants. Alvíss answers everything — and Thor has only been buying time. At dawn he declares that the sun is up, and the dwarf, caught in daylight, turns to stone in the hall.

Loki kills Ótr, the otter-shape son of Hreiðmarr, and the gods must pay wergild. Loki borrows Rán's net and drags the dwarf Andvari from his waterfall in pike-form, taking his hoard and the ring Andvaranaut. Andvari's parting curse: the ring and gold shall be the death of their owners. The ransom buys Ótr's life and ends Hreiðmarr's: his sons Fáfnir and Reginn murder him for the gold, Fáfnir turns dragon on it, Reginn the dwarf-smith fosters Sigurðr and forges him the sword Gram — then plots his death. Sigurðr kills the dragon and the smith both, and the gold rolls on, killing its way through the Völsung and Nibelung lines to the end of the world's story.

King Sigrlami forces the dwarfs Dvalinn and Dulinn to forge him a sword that never fails and never rusts — and the smiths lay a curse on it: every time it is drawn it must kill a man, and it will bring down Sigrlami's own bloodline. The saga then spends generations watching Tyrfing keep its word, through Angantýr, Hervör and Heiðrekr, a sword that outlives every hand that holds it.

At Baldr's funeral, as the ship is launched and the pyre lit, a dwarf named Litr runs under Thor's feet. Thor, without a word, kicks him into the fire. The text gives no motive, no explanation, no aftermath: one dwarf, dead in a sentence, collateral damage of a god's grief.

Freyja covets a necklace the four dwarfs Alfrigg, Dvalinn, Berling and Grer have forged. Their price is a night with her apiece; she pays it and wears the Brísingamen. Loki, at Odin's urging, steals the necklace from her and tells Odin how she bought it — and Freyja, to win it back, is bound to stir up an endless battle between two kings, the Hjaðningavíg, so that the slain rise and fight again forever.

Cultural Significance

Dwarves represent the chthonic craftsman archetype across Indo-European myth. They appear in Germanic folklore as mine-spirits (Bergmännlein), in Scottish folklore as knockers, and in Slavic lore as skarbnik (treasure guardians). Tolkien's dwarves — Khazâd of Durin's Folk — resurrected the entire race for modern fantasy. Dungeons & Dragons codified them into game mechanics. In the real world, 'dwarf' in mining tradition was a practical superstition: he was the spirit the miner blamed for cave-ins and thanked for rich seams — a way of making geology into conversation.

Scholarly Debates
How dwarfs were created
From maggots in Ymir's flesh, given reason and human shape by the gods (Snorri, Gylfaginning chs. 14–15). ⇌ Shaped by the gods' decree out of Brimir's blood and Bláinn's limbs — Ymir's corpse under other names (Völuspá st. 9).
Were dwarfs small?
Short stature is a defining trait (the modern and folkloric image). ⇌ The earliest Norse sources do not stress size; height is rarely stated and not load-bearing (Mikučionis 2017; Ármann Jakobsson, The Hole: Problems in Medieval Dwarfology, Arv 61, 2005).
Dwarfs and black elves (svartálfar)
Dwarfs and svartálfar/dökkálfar are the same population (Snorri's equation, Svartálfaheimr). ⇌ They are separate categories that Snorri himself conflated; the poems keep elves and dwarfs distinct.
Female dwarfs
The earliest sources know only male dwarfs; no dwarf woman appears in the Eddas. ⇌ The texts mention dwarfs' sisters and daughters, and female dwarfs do appear in later saga literature and folklore.
Sunlight turns dwarfs to stone
Dwarfs cannot survive daylight — a general rule (popular and folkloric). ⇌ Only Alvíss is destroyed by sunrise, and the petrification sentence sits in a possibly late prose frame; elsewhere dwarfs show no such weakness.

A way to hold it in your mind

In the dim space between stone and story, the Norse dvergar stand as the hands that built the gods’ world—yet the texts that name them rarely pause to describe their faces. We know their hammer, their ring, their curse, and their wisdom long before we know their height. Following these elusive craftsmen from runic spells to heroic tragedy reveals a race of beings whose legacy has been buried twice: first under the mountains of myth, and then beneath the weight of modern fantasy that insists they must all be short, bearded miners. This dossier digs back down to the uneven bedrock of the medieval sources, where dwarfs are not a single stereotype but a shifting constellation of spirits, smiths, and fate-binders.
