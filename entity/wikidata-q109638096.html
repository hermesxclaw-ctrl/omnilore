Who It Is

He has no superpowers, no tragic curse, and no true name — only a smile cut too wide and a joke that never lands the same way twice. Since 1940, the Joker has been Batman's photographic negative: where Batman is order, trauma turned into discipline, the Joker is chaos, trauma turned into nihilism — and DC has never let you decide which one is pretending.

Attested: highly intelligent, obsessive, theatrical, sadistic, with compulsive need for attention and for Batman as audience/antithesis. Speech: puns, riddles, vaudeville patter, sudden shifts from jovial to murderous. Motivation varies by era: Golden Age — gangster who enjoys murder as punchline; Silver Age — prankster thief (Comics Code era sanitized); Bronze/Modern Age — nihilistic terrorist/philosopher who believes life is a bad joke and seeks to prove anyone can become like him (Killing Joke thesis). No consistent empathy; relationships are possessive or exploitative. Diagnoses in-univ

Powers
Chemical and Biochemical Expertise

Master chemist specializing in toxins, particularly 'Joker Venom' (Joker toxin) — causes uncontrollable laughter, tetanic rictus grin, death. Also creates hallucinogens, acids, laughing gas, and modified Smilex (Batman 1989). In comics, creates venom that reanimates corpses as laughing puppets.

Limit: Requires preparation, ingredients, delivery mechanism (spray, injection, gas). Antidotes exist; Batman has developed immunity/resistance in several stories but not consistently. Not instantaneous against sealed environments.

Genius Intellect and Criminal Planning

High IQ, strategic planning, engineering of elaborate death traps, manipulation of psychology, escape artistry. Outwits Arkham security repeatedly, engineers city-wide schemes (e.g., Endgame virus infecting Justice League).

Limit: No superhuman intellect compared to Lex Luthor or Brainiac in-universe; plans fail due to obsession with Batman and need for theatricality. Impulsive when denied attention.

Pain Tolerance and Durability (Non-superhuman)

Extraordinary tolerance for pain, survives falls, beatings, explosions that would kill ordinary human, often attributed to insanity/adrenaline rather than meta-human durability. Recovers quickly between issues for narrative reasons.

Limit: Still human; can be injured, shot, hospitalized. Deaths are often ambiguous and retconned. No healing factor.

Hand-to-Hand Combat and Weaponry

Proficient brawler, knife fighter, marksman; favors unconventional weapons: razor cards, acid flowers, joy buzzers, crowbars (used to murder Jason Todd), firearms, dynamite. Unpredictable fighting style compensates for lack of formal training.

Limit: No match for Batman in straight fight; relies on surprise, hostages, toxins. Physical strength is athletic, not superhuman.

Psychological Manipulation and Corruption

Ability to groom, gaslight and break victims (Harley Quinn's origin as Dr. Harleen Quinzel manipulated, Barbara Gordon's torture in Killing Joke, turning Gotham citizens into Jokerized minions via blood-borne pathogen in Endgame and New 52).

Limit: Not mind control; requires leverage, charisma, prolonged abuse. Many resist (Batman, Jim Gordon). Effect is psychological, not telepathic.

Immunity/Resistance to Own Toxins (Partial)

Often depicted as immune to Joker Venom and other toxins due to prolonged exposure and altered physiology from chemical bath.

Limit: Not universal; some stories show him affected by his own gas or by Scarecrow's fear toxin. Immunity is inconsistent.

Accounts I
The Joker (First Appearance)Batman #1

1940

A mysterious grinning killer announces via radio that he will murder Henry Claridge and steal the Claridge diamond. Despite police protection, Claridge dies with a grotesque grin, victim of Joker Venom. Batman and Robin pursue the Joker through jewel heists, each announced with a playing card. Joker is seemingly killed in a fight aboard a train, stabbed in the chest, but retconned to have survived. Establishes core modus operandi: theatrical announcements, poison, and obsession with Batman. Introduced Joker as recurring arch-nemesis; established Joker Venom and card motif. Editorial note originally intended Joker to die after two issues but saved by editor Whitney Ellsworth.

The Man Behind the Red Hood!Detective Comics #168

1951

Batman teaches a criminology class and recounts the case of the Red Hood, a tuxedoed criminal who used a domed red helmet to commit robberies. During a heist at the Ace Chemical plant, Batman confronts Red Hood, who leaps into a vat of chemicals to escape and is swept through a drain. Later, the Joker appears and Batman deduces Joker is the transformed Red Hood, bleached by chemicals. Joker is captured after attempting to steal from his old gang. First origin, establishing chemical-bath transformation. Became template for all later origins, though DC later declared no single origin definitive.

The Joker's Five-Way RevengeBatman #251

1973

After being paroled, Joker seeks revenge on five former henchmen who betrayed him, killing them one by one with elaborate, ironic murders while Batman races to stop him. Written by Dennis O'Neil and drawn by Neal Adams, story restores Joker as homicidal psychopath after Silver Age buffoonery, featuring the iconic cover of Joker looming over Batman. Re-established lethal Joker for modern era; influence on later horror-tinged portrayals. Often cited by creators (including Heath Ledger's preparation) as tonal blueprint.

Accounts II
A Death in the FamilyBatman #426-429

1988

Joker, having escaped Arkham, is hired by Middle Eastern terrorists and gains diplomatic immunity as Iranian ambassador. Jason Todd, the second Robin, seeks his birth mother in the Middle East and is lured into a warehouse in Ethiopia where Joker beats him brutally with a crowbar and leaves him with a bomb. Batman arrives too late; Jason and his mother Sheila Haywood die in the explosion. Joker later escapes justice due to immunity. Death of Robin, decided by fan phone poll (5343 votes to kill vs 5271 to save). Profound impact on Batman mythos; Jason later resurrected as Red Hood. Cemented Joker as child murderer, raising content advisories.

The Killing JokeBatman: The Killing Joke (one-shot)

1988

Intercutting Joker's possible origin as a failed comedian who agrees to be Red Hood to support his pregnant wife (who dies in accident) with present-day atrocity: Joker shoots Barbara Gordon (Batgirl) through the spine, paralyzing her, then kidnaps Commissioner Jim Gordon, strips him naked, and subjects him to a freak-show ride trying to prove 'one bad day' can drive anyone insane. Batman eventually confronts Joker, offers rehabilitation, and the two share a final, ambiguous laugh as police arrive. Paralysis of Barbara Gordon (later Oracle) sparked decades of debate about fridging and disability representation. Origin's 'multiple choice past' line ('If I'm going to have a past, I prefer it to be multiple choice!') became editorial policy that Joker has no definitive origin.

EndgameBatman #35-40 (Scott Snyder/Greg Capullo)

2014-2015

Joker returns after apparent death in Death of the Family, having spent years underground regenerating via 'dionesium' pit. He unleashes a blood-borne Joker virus across Gotham, turning citizens into cackling Jokerized hordes, and infects the Justice League. Batman, infected himself, fights Joker in a cavern beneath Gotham containing a paleolithic Joker-like painting, implying Joker is immortal or an archetype. Both die in combat as cave collapses, only to be resurrected via dionesium. Introduced immortality/ancient Joker hypothesis; led to 'three Jokers' mystery. Pushed Joker from gangster to quasi-supernatural plague.

Three JokersBatman: Three Jokers #1-3 (Geoff Johns/Jason Fabok)

2020

Batman, Batgirl (Barbara Gordon) and Red Hood (Jason Todd) investigate that there have been three distinct Jokers operating simultaneously: The Criminal (Golden Age mastermind), The Clown (Silver Age prankster), and The Comedian (Modern psychopath). They discover Joker created the other two via chemicals and that Joe Chill's role and Joker's abuse of his own family are explored. Ends with revelation that Joker knows Batman is Bruce Wayne and has always known, and that Bruce prevents Joker from having a family to stop cycle. Attempted to reconcile contradictory Joker portrayals in-universe; reception mixed, canon status debated (Black Label = out-of-continuity but referenced).

Joker's real name is definitively Jack Napier or Arthur Fleck. — Neither is main-comics canon. Napier is Tim Burton's 1989 film invention, later used as one alias among many in comics. Fleck is Todd Phillips's 2019 Elseworlds film. DC editorial maintains Joker has no confirmed birth name in Prime Earth continuity.

Joker is insane and therefore not responsible — he's just a crazy clown who can't help it. — Comics repeatedly show Joker as legally sane enough to plan, manipulate courts, and feign insanity to avoid death penalty. Many stories frame him as super-sane or rational psychopath, not psychotic.

Heath Ledger's Joker was based on The Killing Joke. — Ledger and Nolan cited Batman #251 and The Man Who Laughs as primary influences, not Killing Joke. Ledger's Joker has no origin, opposite of Killing Joke's sympathetic failed comedian.

Joker and Harley Quinn are a romantic, goals-worthy couple. — Canonically abusive; Joker manipulates, beats and attempts to kill Harley. DC and Harley's solo comics frame relationship as survival and escape, not romance.

Joker is based on the playing-card joker or a single historical jester. — Character was inspired by both a playing card and Conrad Veidt's face in The Man Who Laughs (1928); no single historical jester source.
