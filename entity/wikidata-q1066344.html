Who It Is

A name whispered across Greek myth, Cycnus (or Cygnus) is a figure of many guises—a son of Ares whose arrogance led to a fatal duel with Heracles, a grief-stricken lover of Phaethon transformed into a swan, and a king whose people became the swan-people of Liguria. Each Cycnus is a study in pride, loss, and metamorphosis, yet they share a single, haunting thread: the swan, that bird of beauty and sorrow.

The Cycnus who fights Heracles is arrogant and hubristic, challenging travelers to combat and killing them to build a temple to Apollo. He is a violent and impious figure. The Cycnus who mourns Phaethon is deeply grief-stricken, demonstrating loyalty and love. The Cycnus killed by Achilles is a skilled warrior but ultimately defeated.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The Greek name Κύκνος (Kýknos) means 'swan' (the bird). It derives from the Proto-Indo-European *kuknos, possibly onomatopoeic for the bird's call. The name is both a personal name and a common noun, which contributes to the confusion among multiple mythological figures.

Pronunciation — English: /ˈsɪknəs/ SIK-nəs; Classical Greek: /kýk.nos/; Latin: /ˈkʏk.nos/

Aliases — Cygnus, Kyknos, Cigno (Italian), Cygne (French), Swan (translation)

Ownership — Greek and Roman mythology; the name and myths are part of the broader Indo-European cultural sphere, but specifically Hellenic and later Roman literary tradition.

Powers
Superhuman strength and combat skill

As a son of Ares, Cycnus was a formidable warrior, capable of killing many travelers. He was strong enough to fight Heracles, though ultimately defeated.

Limit: Despite his strength, he was no match for Heracles, who was aided by Athena.

Invulnerability (in some versions)

In some later accounts, Cycnus (son of Apollo) was invulnerable to weapons, which is why Achilles had to strangle him. This is not mentioned in earlier sources.

Limit: Vulnerable to strangulation.

Transformation into a swan

In Ovid's Metamorphoses, Cycnus (son of Sthenelus) is transformed into a swan by Apollo (or Zeus) as a reward for his grief over Phaethon's death. This is a permanent metamorphosis.

Limit: Transformation is permanent; he loses his human form.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In most sources, Cycnus is described as a handsome, strong warrior, often clad in gleaming armor. In the Hesiodic Shield, he is described as a giant-like figure, but no specific physical traits beyond his warrior appearance are given. In Ovid's Metamorphoses, the transformed Cycnus becomes a swan—a large white bird with a long neck and graceful movements. Some artistic depictions show him as a swan or as a man with swan-like features.

Symbols — The swan is the central symbol, representing both beauty and sorrow. In art, Cycnus is often depicted in battle scenes (especially with Heracles) or as a swan. The armor of Cycnus in the Shield of Heracles is elaborately described, featuring images of various mythological scenes, which adds to his iconographic presence.

Domains — Cycnus is associated with various locations: the region around the river Peneus in Thessaly (for the son of Ares), the land of Liguria in northern Italy (for the son of Sthenelus), and the area around Troy (for the son of Apollo). He is also connected to the river Eridanus (often identified with the Po) in Phaethon's myth.

Accounts I
Cycnus and HeraclesHesiod, Shield of Heracles; Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.5.11

Archaic period (c. 7th-6th century BCE)

Cycnus, son of Ares and Pelopia, was a bandit who challenged travelers to single combat and killed them, using their skulls to build a temple to Apollo. He blocked the road to Delphi, and Heracles, on his way to the oracle, encountered him. They fought, and with the help of Athena, Heracles killed Cycnus. Ares intervened, but Athena drove him off. Cycnus was killed, and his father Ares was wounded. The temple to Apollo was never completed. Heracles continued his journey.

Accounts II
Cycnus and PhaethonOvid, Metamorphoses 2.367-380; Hyginus, Fabulae 154?

Classical to Roman period

Cycnus, son of Sthenelus, was a king of Liguria and a relative or friend of Phaethon. After Phaethon was struck by Zeus's lightning and fell into the river Eridanus, Cycnus mourned him deeply. The gods took pity on him and transformed him into a swan, so that he could dwell on the waters and sing mournful songs. Cycnus became a swan, and his people, the Ligurians, were also transformed into swans in some versions.

Cycnus and AchillesApollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.12.5; Hyginus, Fabulae 97?

Classical period (c. 5th century BCE)

Cycnus, son of Apollo and Hyrie (or other), was a Trojan ally during the Trojan War. He was invulnerable to weapons, but Achilles killed him by strangling him with his own helmet strap. In some versions, he was killed before the war began, during a raid on Achilles' homeland. Cycnus was killed, and his death was avenged by Apollo in some accounts.

All figures named Cycnus are the same person. — There are at least three distinct figures in ancient sources, with different parentage and stories.

Cycnus was a swan from birth. — In most myths, Cycnus was a human who was transformed into a swan, usually as a punishment or reward.

Cycnus was invulnerable like Achilles. — Only the Cycnus son of Apollo is sometimes said to be invulnerable, and even then, it is a late addition.
