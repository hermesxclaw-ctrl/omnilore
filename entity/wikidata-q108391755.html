Who It Is

The name Stratius means 'of the army' — and in Greek myth, four different men who bore it all died violently. One was a prince of wealthy Orchomenus whose family was shattered by Heracles; one was a pious son of old Nestor who poured wine for a goddess in disguise; one was torn apart and eaten by the Cyclops Polyphemus; and one was cut down in Odysseus's hall for courting another man's wife. Same name, four separate fates, none of them peaceful.

Orchomenian Stratius: attested only as a son in a genealogy; no personality described. His brother Erginus is described as arrogant and tribute-exacting, but Stratius is not characterized. Pylian Stratichus: attested as dutiful son participating in Nestor's sacrifice to Poseidon in Odyssey 3, implying piety and filial obedience; no speech or individual action recorded. Cyclopean Stratius: attested only as a victim; no behavior described beyond being a companion of Odysseus. Suitor Stratius: attested as one of 44 Zacynthian suitors occupying Odysseus's house, consuming his livestoc

Princely Status and Inheritance (Orchomenian and Pylian)

Both royal Stratii hold status as sons of kings (Clymenus of Orchomenus, Nestor of Pylos) with attendant rights to hospitality, sacrifice, and command. No supernatural power attested.

Limit: Mortal; subordinate to father and elder brothers; Orchomenian house is destroyed by Heracles and Thebans; Pylian house is subject to Telemachus's generation.

Ritual Competence (Pylian Stratichus)

Participates in Nestor's elaborate sacrifice of black bulls to Poseidon on the beach at Pylos, handling libations and ritual implements alongside his brothers when Telemachus and Athena (as Mentor) arrive.

Limit: No priestly office; acts under Nestor's direction; no independent ritual power.

Warrior Training (All figures, inferred)

As princes and companions/suitors in heroic age, presumed trained in spear, sword, and ship-handling, but no specific feat, weapon, or technique is attested for any Stratius by name.

Limit: No attested aristeia, no named weapon, no victory; two Stratii are explicitly victims (Cyclops and Mnesterophonia) showing martial inferiority to monster and to Odysseus.

No Supernatural Abilities

Explicitly: none of the four Stratii is credited with shapeshifting, magic, divine parentage, metamorphosis, or superhuman strength in any primary source. They are fully mortal.

Limit: Mortality is absolute; all traditions where death is recorded show them killed by superior force (Heracles's war, Polyphemus, Odysseus).

Form & Signs

Appearance — No ancient source describes the physical appearance of any Stratius. All four are adult or adolescent mortal men of princely or warrior status. Vase painting and sculpture do not identify any figure as Stratius by inscription. Attested: mortal male, Greek. Unknown: height, hair, clothing, armor, distinguishing marks. Any modern illustration is conjectural.

Symbols — No attested symbols, blazons, or iconographic attributes specific to any Stratius. The Orchomenian Stratius belongs to the Minyan royal house whose symbol in later tradition is the wealth of Orchomenus (charites, grain). The Pylian Stratius appears in sacrificial context with no personal emblem. The Cyclopean victim and the suitor have no symbols. No verified ancient visual record exists for any of them.

Domains — Orchomenian Stratius: palace at Orchomenus in Boeotia, center of Minyan power near Lake Copais. Pylian Stratichus: palace at Pylos (likely Ano Englianos) in Messenia. Cyclopean Stratius: ship-borne companion, killed in the cave of Polyphemus on the island of the Cyclopes (traditionally identified with Sicily). Suitor Stratius: native of Zacynthus (Zakynthos, Ionian Islands), resident as interloper in Odysseus's palace on Ithaca.

Accounts I
The Sons of Clymenus and the Fall of OrchomenusPausanias 9.37.1-3; Apollodorus Bibliotheca 2.4.11; scholia to Apollonius Rhodius 1.185

Archaic myth as recorded in Classical-Hellenistic sources (mythic time: generation before Heracles)

King Clymenus of the Minyans at Orchomenus fathers several sons: Erginus, Stratius, Arrhon, Pyleus, and Azeus (daughters Eurydice and Axia also named). Mother is given in scholia as Boudeia or Bouzyge, daughter of Lycus. When Clymenus is mortally wounded by Perieres the charioteer at a festival at Onchestus, dying Erginus exacts a heavy tribute on Thebes — 100 cattle yearly for 20 years. The young Heracles, encountering Erginus's heralds, mutilates them and then leads the Thebans to sack Orchomenus, killing Erginus. The fate of Stratius and his brothers after the sack is not narrated; Pausanias notes Erginus later made peace and lived to old age, but Stratius disappears from the record, implying death, exile, or loss of status with the city's fall. End of Minyan hegemony over Boeotia; rise of Thebes under Heracles; Orchomenus reduced to tributary status; genealogy becomes etiology for Boeotian politics.

The Sacrifice at Pylos — Stratichus Son of Nestor Receives TelemachusHomer Odyssey Book 3, lines 386-472

Archaic epic, 8th century BCE

Telemachus, seeking news of Odysseus, arrives at Pylos with Athena disguised as Mentor to find Nestor and his sons performing a grand sacrifice of 81 black bulls to Poseidon on the shore. Nestor's sons are listed: Perseus, Stratichus (Stratichos), Aretus, Echephron, Pisistratus, and Thrasymedes. Stratichus and his brothers assist: they flay victims, cut meat, and pour wine. Nestor prays, Athena/Mentor is honored, and the party feasts. Stratichus has no individual speech; his presence demonstrates the piety and order of Nestor's house contrasted with the disorder on Ithaca. He remains at Pylos when Telemachus departs for Sparta. Establishes Nestor as paradigm of lawful kingship; provides Telemachus with model of proper succession; no further narrative for Stratichus — he does not join Telemachus or appear in later books.

Accounts II
Stratius the Companion, Eaten by PolyphemusJohn Tzetzes, Allegories of the Odyssey 9.122-145; scholia to Odyssey 9.289

12th century CE, drawing on lost Hellenistic mythographic lists

When Odysseus and twelve picked men enter the cave of Polyphemus on the island of the Cyclopes (later identified with Sicily), the Cyclops traps them, blocks the entrance with a boulder, and begins eating them two by two. Homer names no victims, but Tzetzes, preserving a late catalogue, names six of the twelve who were killed and eaten before Odysseus blinds the monster: Antiphon, Euryleon, Apheidas, Kepheus, Stratius, and Menetos. Stratius is thus one of the six who did not survive to be rescued. The remaining six escape clinging to the bellies of rams. Reduces Odysseus's crew; demonstrates Polyphemus's savagery and impiety; motivates Odysseus's cunning revenge (blinding with heated olive stake).

Stratius the Suitor from Zacynthus, Killed in the MnesterophoniaApollodorus Epitome 7.26-33; Homer Odyssey 16.247-253 (context), 22.1-88 (slaughter)

Archaic epic with Hellenistic catalogue elaboration

Among the 108 suitors who infest Odysseus's palace during his 20-year absence, 44 come from Zacynthus. Apollodorus, elaborating on Homer's bare numbers, provides a catalogue that includes Stratius among the Zacynthians, alongside others like Antinous and Eurymachus (though those are from Ithaca/Dulichium). For three years they feast on Odysseus's cattle, drink his wine, and press Penelope to choose a husband, while plotting to kill Telemachus. Upon Odysseus's return disguised as a beggar, with the help of his son Telemachus, the swineherd Eumaeus, and the cowherd Philoetius, he strings his great bow, locks the hall, and slaughters all suitors. Stratius, like the other Zacynthians, is shot or speared in the hall; no individual death scene is given for him, but the collective fate is total annihilation, with bodies later removed and house purified with sulfur. Restoration of Odysseus's household and kingship; punishment of hubris (hybris) and violation of xenia (guest-host law); Zacynthus loses a generation of young nobles.

The Name Stratios as Epithet, Toponym and Cult TitleStephanus of Byzantium Ethnica s.v. Stratiai; Pausanias 4.31.5 (Zeus Stratios); inscriptions from Pontus and Caria

Classical to Byzantine (5th century BCE - 6th century CE)

Beyond personal name, Stratios ('Of the Army') is an epithet of Zeus (Zeus Stratios) worshipped by kings and armies, notably in Pontus where Mithridates VI maintained a sanctuary, and in Caria at Labraunda (Zeus Stratios/ Labrandeus). Stephanus notes a place called Stratia or Stratiai. The personal name Stratius thus overlaps with divine epithet and toponym, causing confusion in lexicography: some entries for 'Stratius' may originally refer to the god or place, not a hero. This explains why searches for 'Stratius' return Zeus Stratios cult and geographic names alongside the four mortals. Lexicographic conflation; modern databases mix divine epithet with mortal figures; need to separate theonym from anthroponym.

Stratius was a single person who was both son of Nestor and companion of Odysseus and suitor of Penelope. — Four distinct mortals share the name across different regions and generations: Orchomenian prince (generation before Trojan War), Pylian prince (contemporary of Telemachus), Ithacan crewman (Trojan War generation), and Zacynthian suitor (next generation). Ancient sources never identify them with each other; chronology makes single identity impossible (Orchomenian Stratius belongs to Heracles's era, Pylian to Nestor's old age).

Stratius had superpowers or was a god/divine figure. — All four Stratii are explicitly mortal princes/warriors with no divine parentage or supernatural abilities. The confusion arises from Zeus Stratios ('Zeus of the Army'), a cult epithet of Zeus, and from the 'Archetype: divine' tag in some databases. No primary source makes any Stratius a god.

Homer names Stratius as one of the men eaten by the Cyclops. — Homer never names any of Polyphemus's victims; the six names including Stratius appear only in the 12th-century Byzantine Allegories of John Tzetzes, who was compiling scholia, not quoting Homer. The Homeric text (Odyssey 9.289-293) is anonymous.

Stratius the suitor was a major villain like Antinous or Eurymachus. — Stratius is an anonymous name in Apollodorus's expanded catalogue; he has no speeches, deeds, or individual death scene in Homer or Apollodorus. The narrative focus is on Antinous, Eurymachus, and Amphinomus; Stratius is collective filler to reach the canonical 108 suitors.
