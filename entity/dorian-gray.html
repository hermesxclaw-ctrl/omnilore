Who It Is

The story of Dorian Gray is a timeless and thought-provoking tale that continues to fascinate readers and scholars to this day. Its exploration of the human condition, beauty, morality, and the supernatural raises important questions about the nature of reality and the human experience. As a cultural icon, Dorian Gray represents the tension between the pursuit of beauty and the pursuit of virtue, highlighting the dangers of unchecked vanity and the corrupting influence of desire.

Dorian Gray is a complex and multifaceted character, with a personality that is both charming and terrifying. He is driven by a desire for beauty and pleasure, and is willing to do whatever it takes to achieve his goals, including sacrificing his own morality and the well-being of those around him. However, he is also capable of great kindness and generosity, particularly in his early interactions with Sibyl Vane and Basil Hallward. Youth and Beauty

Dorian Gray's most significant power is his ability to remain youthful and beautiful, despite the corrupting influence of his desires and the passage of time. This power is reflected in his portrait, which becomes increasingly disfigured and twisted as the story progresses, while his physical appearance remains unchanged.

Accounts I
The Picture of Dorian GrayOscar Wilde

Late 19th century

The story of Dorian Gray is a philosophical and aesthetic exploration of the human condition, beauty, morality, and the supernatural. It follows the life of a young and beautiful man, Dorian Gray, who becomes infatuated with his own image and makes a Faustian bargain to sell his soul in exchange for eternal youth and beauty. As the story progresses, Dorian's portrait, which reflects the true state of his soul, becomes increasingly disfigured and twisted, revealing the corruption and decay that lies beneath his beautiful exterior. The story of Dorian Gray has significant consequences for the characters involved, including the destruction of Sibyl Vane, the corruption of Dorian Gray, and the moral and philosophical crisis of Basil Hallward. The story also has broader implications for the society in which it is set, highlighting the dangers of unchecked vanity and the corrupting influence of desire.

Late 19th century

The preface to The Picture of Dorian Gray is a philosophical and aesthetic manifesto that outlines the author's views on art, beauty, and morality. It argues that art should be appreciated for its own sake, rather than for any moral or didactic purpose, and that the pursuit of beauty is a fundamental aspect of the human experience. The preface to The Picture of Dorian Gray has had significant consequences for the way that the story is interpreted and understood. It provides a framework for understanding the author's intentions and the philosophical and aesthetic themes that underlie the narrative.
