Who It Is

In the crowded family tree of Iolcus, where every genealogist in antiquity wanted to name the mother of Greece's most famous voyager, one name flickers for only a moment and then disappears: Theognete, daughter of Laodicus. She is not the Alcimede most readers meet in the Argonautica, nor the Polymede who tricks Pelias in later handbooks. She is a third, almost ghostly candidate — a reminder that even Jason, whose ship the Argo is drawn on a thousand vases, had a mother whose identity the Greeks themselves could not agree on.

No personality, speech, or behavior is described for Theognete. In the Andron fragment she is a name in a genealogy only. By contrast, the Alcimede/Polymede variants acquire narrative behavior in later sources (e.g., Diodorus 4.50: Jason's mother curses Pelias and commits suicide with Aeson and Promachus when Pelias seizes power). Those actions must not be retrojected onto Theognete; they belong to a different variant tradition. For Theognete, character is unknown.

Powers
Mortal status (no supernatural powers attested)

Theognete is presented as a mortal woman of the Deucalionid stemma (through Iolcan royalty). No power, transformation, prophecy, witchcraft, or divine gift is attributed to her in any source. She does not perform magic, receive divine intervention, or survive death. This contrasts with Medea (Jason's later wife) and with Rhoeo's Dionysiac lineage, but Theognete herself has no abilities beyond the social role of basilissa (queen) and mother.

Limit: Mortality, political vulnerability to Pelias' coup. No resistance to usurpation described.

Genealogical function - mother of the Argonaut

Her sole narrative function is to provide Jason with legitimate Minyan/Thessalian royal blood through both parents. In Greek genealogical thought this legitimizes Jason's claim to Iolcus against Pelias. No feat or failure is recorded for her directly.

Limit: Function is passive; she does not act in the Argonautic expedition. Later death/suicide motifs belong to Alcimede/Polymede variants, not securely to Theognete.

Accounts I
The Andron Variant: Theognete Daughter of Laodicus Bears Jason to AesonAndron of Halicarnassus FGrH 10 F 16-17, preserved in Scholia on Apollonius Rhodius 1.45 and Apollodorus Bibliotheca 1.9.16

5th-4th century BCE original; transmitted 1st-2nd century CE and medieval scholia

Andron, a Halicarnassian logographer, records that Aeson son of Cretheus and Tyro married Theognete daughter of Laodicus, and that from this union were born Jason, the leader of the Argonauts, and in some accounts his younger brother Promachus. The notice is purely genealogical, embedded in a catalogue of variant mothers. No courtship, wedding, or birth narrative is elaborated; the point is to anchor Jason in a respectable but otherwise obscure Thessalian lineage distinct from the more famous Autolycus or Phylacus stemmata. Provides a minority stemma for Jason; demonstrates that even in the 5th century BCE the identity of Jason's mother was already disputed among logographers. Later mythographers cite Andron to show erudition, but the variant never becomes dominant.

The Dominant Variant: Alcimede Daughter of PhylacusHesiod Catalogue of Women fr. 38-40 MW, Pherecydes FGrH 3 F 104, Apollonius Rhodius Argonautica 1.47-48, Valerius Flaccus Argonautica 1.297

c. 580 BCE to 1st century CE

In the most widely transmitted tradition, Aeson's wife is Alcimede (Alkimede) daughter of Phylacus son of Deion and Clymene, and granddaughter of Aeolus. She bears Jason and, in some accounts, Promachus. Apollonius makes her the mother who weeps when Jason departs for the Argo, and Diodorus makes her die with Aeson and Promachus after Pelias' coup, cursing Pelias. This version ties Jason to the Phylacean royal house and to the Aeolid clan. Becomes the vulgate in modern handbooks (Graves, Morford). Establishes Jason's Aeolid legitimacy and provides a tragic death scene for his mother, often mistakenly transferred to Theognete.

The Hermetic Variant: Polymede / Polymele / Polypheme Daughter of AutolycusHesiod Catalogue of Women fr. 38 (alternate reading), Apollodorus Bibliotheca 1.9.16, Scholia on Apollonius Rhodius 1.45

Archaic to Imperial

Hesiod in one fragment and Apollodorus following him call Jason's mother Polymede (also transmitted as Polymele or Polypheme) daughter of Autolycus son of Hermes and Chione. This makes Jason the great-grandson of Hermes, god of trickery and travel — a fitting ancestry for the Argonaut. Autolycus himself is the notorious thief who stole cattle and taught Heracles to wrestle. The name confusion (Polymede vs Polymele vs Polypheme) reflects manuscript corruption, not three separate women. Gives Jason Hermetic cunning as inherited trait; explains his ability to deal with Medea and Pelias. Frequently cited as the 'Hesiodic' version, in direct competition with Andron's Theognete.

Accounts II
The Diodoran Variant: AmphinomeDiodorus Siculus Bibliotheca Historica 4.50.1-3; Apollodorus Bibliotheca 1.9.16 (some MSS)

1st century BCE; manuscript variants Imperial

Diodorus, rationalizing myth as history, names Jason's mother Amphinome, without patronymic in the best MSS. When Pelias usurps Iolcus and imprisons Aeson, Amphinome, Aeson, and young Promachus are forced to commit suicide (Aeson drinks bull's blood, Amphinome stabs herself, Promachus is murdered by Pelias). This tragic tableau becomes the standard Hellenistic pathos scene and is later transferred to Alcimede in other manuscripts, showing scribal interchangeability. Provides the dramatic suicide narrative that defines Jason's motivation for revenge; illustrates how a mother's name could be swapped without changing plot function.

The Dionysiac Variant: Rhoeo Daughter of StaphylusTzetzes Chiliades 6.55, Scholia on Apollonius Rhodius, Eudocia Violarium

Byzantine 12th century, drawing on Hellenistic mythography

A late variant makes Jason's mother Rhoeo (or Rhoio) daughter of Staphylus son of Dionysus and Ariadne, and sister of Parthenos and Molpadia. Staphylus is the mythical king of Peparethus who received Dionysus. This would make Jason a great-grandson of Dionysus and Ariadne, linking the Argonautic saga to the Cretan-Dionysiac cycle. No early source attests this; it appears to be a Hellenistic or later invention to connect Iolcus with the wine-god's family. Demonstrates the late mythographic tendency to aggrandize Jason's lineage by attaching him to more famous divine families; never widely adopted.

The Obscurest Variants: Arne and ScarpheTzetzes Chiliades 6.55, Eustathius on Homer, Scholia on Apollonius Rhodius 1.45 (late MSS)

12th century CE compilation, possibly reflecting lost Hellenistic catalogues

Two final names appear in the latest compilations: Arne (perhaps connected to the Boeotian nymph Arne, daughter of Aeolus) and Scarphe (or Scarpheia, otherwise unknown, possibly an eponym of the Locrian town Scarpheia). Both are listed without patronymic or narrative, simply as 'others say Arne' or 'others say Scarphe was mother of Jason.' They have no stories beyond the genealogical assertion and may reflect scribal errors or extremely local traditions that did not survive in narrative form. Show the full extent of the confusion: by late antiquity at least seven names circulated for one role. Theognete belongs in this list as the earliest-logographer variant, not as an independent heroine.

Theognete was a goddess, nymph, or had magical powers. — No source gives Theognete divinity, powers, or cult. She is a mortal queen-consort whose only role is genealogical. Confusion arises from her theophoric name (theos-born) and from conflation with Medea or with Rhoeo's Dionysiac lineage.

Theognete, Alcimede, Polymede, and Amphinome were all wives of Aeson or mothers of different children by him. — Ancient mythography treats them as mutually exclusive variants for the same single slot: the mother of Jason. Aeson had one wife in each telling; the sources say 'some say X, others say Y,' not that he had seven wives.

Jason's mother is consistently Alcimede in all ancient sources. — Alcimede is the most common but not the only ancient version. Hesiod gives Polymede, Andron gives Theognete, Diodorus gives Amphinome, Tzetzes preserves Rhoeo, Arne, Scarphe. The tradition was disputed from the 6th century BCE onward.

There is a famous painting or statue of Theognete. — No verified ancient or modern artwork depicts Theognete. No vase painting is inscribed with her name; LIMC (Lexicon Iconographicum Mythologiae Classicae) has no entry for Theognete. Any image labeled Theognete online is mislabeled or AI-generated.
