I find the crack you forgot to seal, the window you left ajar. I sit on your chest as you sleep, and I drink your breath. Iron frightens me. Garlic sickens me. Without these, you are mine.

Who It Is

The Prikuľič is a nightmare spirit of Eastern European Romani folklore — a disembodied entity that attacks sleeping victims, causing paralysis, breathlessness, and a crushing sensation on the chest (the classic 'night hag' experience). Unlike the Zmora (Slavic nightmare spirit), the Prikuľič is not necessarily the spirit of a living person but an independent malevolent entity that enters homes through any unsealed opening. It is particularly dangerous to pregnant women, newborn infants, and those who sleep alone. The Prikuľič feeds on the breath or life-force of victims, leaving them weakened and susceptible to illness. Protective measures include placing iron objects (knives, scissors, needles) under the pillow or at the threshold, hanging garlic or hawthorn over the bed, and reciting specific charms in Romani. The Prikuľič can also be driven away by placing a broom upside-down behind the door — a widespread European folk protection against nightmare spirits. In some Romani traditions, the Prikuľič is associated with Mule (the dead) — it may be a particularly aggressive form of Muló that attacks non-family members. The Prikuľič reflects the vulnerability of sleeping in temporary or improvised shelters (historic Roma living conditions) where the boundary between indoors and the dangerous night was more permeable. It also encodes the Romani understanding of illness as spiritual attack — disease is not a random biological event but a supernatural assault that must be diagnosed and countered through ritual and protective magic by a drabarni (healer) or chovihani (wise woman).

Form

The prikuľič is rarely described as having a fixed, physical form. It is most often perceived as a 'heavy shadow' or a 'dark weight' that settles over the dreamer. When it does manifest a shape, it is described as a formless, hunched silhouette, sometimes vaguely humanoid but more often resembling a dark mass or a large, heavy animal. It has no discernible face or features. Some accounts describe a cold, clammy sensation where the weight presses down. It does not speak.

Powers

Sleep Paralysis Inducement The prikuľič's primary and defining ability is to induce a state of sleep paralysis in its victim. The victim is conscious and aware of their surroundings but unable to move or speak, while feeling a crushing weight on their chest. Attested across all documented accounts. This is the core, non-negotiable characteristic of the entity. It can only act on a sleeping person. It cannot affect a person who is fully awake. The paralysis is temporary, breaking when the victim manages to struggle free or when an external force (a sound, a touch) intervenes. None. The experience is universally described as the same phenomenon.

Suffocation The prikuľič presses down on the chest, making it difficult or impossible to breathe. The victim feels as if they are being smothered. Attested in all accounts. This is the 'pressing' that gives the entity its name. It does not kill its victim. It causes extreme distress but not death. The sensation ceases when the paralysis breaks. Some modern interpreters suggest the entity could cause death in the weak or elderly, but traditional accounts do not describe lethal outcomes.

Fear Projection The prikuľič generates an overwhelming feeling of dread and terror in its victim, far beyond the physical sensation of the weight. Attested. The psychological terror is as important as the physical oppression. The fear is limited to the duration of the attack. None.

Invisibility/Intangibility The prikuľič is not visible to the waking eye and can pass through physical barriers. It is not a physical being. Attested. It is described as a 'shadow' or 'darkness'. It cannot be physically fought or restrained. It can only be warded off through ritual, objects, or prayer. None.

Accounts

A common narrative tells of a person who falls asleep and feels a great weight settle on their chest. They cannot open their eyes or call out. They feel a cold presence and the sensation of being smothered. They struggle, and just as they feel they will suffocate, the weight lifts and they can move again, gasping for air. The story is told as a warning about the dangers of the night and the importance of protective measures.

A man is repeatedly attacked by the prikuľič. An elder advises him to place a knife with an iron blade under his pillow. That night, when the weight settles on him, he is able to break free, and the entity is 'cut' or 'burned' by the iron. It does not return.

Field Notes

۩ The prikuľič's name comes from the Romani verb for 'to press' or 'to roll upon', perfectly describing the sensation of sleep paralysis.

۩ Unlike many European night hags, the prikuľič has no sexual or seductive element — it is pure, uncomplicated oppression.

۩ Iron is a universal protective charm against the prikuľič, echoing a common folk belief across many cultures that iron repels evil spirits.

۩ The prikuľič is a prime example of a culture explaining a real, universal neurological phenomenon (sleep paralysis) through its own unique spiritual lens.

۩ While often confused with the mulo (a Romani ghost), the prikuľič is a distinct entity — an impersonal predator, not a deceased relative.

Content Notes

Early 20th-century ethnographers sometimes framed the prikuľič belief as evidence of 'Gypsy superstition' and primitiveness, reinforcing harmful stereotypes. The belief was used to paint the Roma as a people trapped in irrational fear and backwardness.

Modern Romani studies scholars like Milena Hübschmannová have worked to re-contextualize these beliefs within a rich and valid spiritual worldview, free from the taint of colonial-era bias.

Josef Ješina's field notes, which, while valuable, are framed within a condescending colonial-era ethnographic perspective.

The prikuľič can be interpreted as a manifestation of the real, lived experience of oppression and helplessness. For a people historically subjected to slavery, persecution, and genocide, the feeling of a crushing, impersonal weight that pins you down and steals your breath is a potent metaphor for systemic oppression.

This is a modern interpretive lens, not a claim made by traditional informants. It highlights the psychological and cultural resonance of the myth.

Scholarly Debates
Origin of the Belief
The prikuľič is an indigenous Romani belief, developed independently from surrounding cultures. ⇌ The prikuľič is a borrowed or syncretic belief, adapted from the Slavic 'mora' tradition.
Evidence for A: The specific Romani name and the lack of the sexualized element present in Slavic folklore suggest a distinct tradition.
Evidence for B: The geographical overlap and the functional similarity are striking, suggesting cultural exchange.
Relationship to the Mulo
The prikuľič is a strictly separate entity from the mulo. ⇌ The prikuľič is a type of mulo, a malevolent manifestation of a dead person's spirit.
Evidence for A: Ethnographic accounts from scholars like Hübschmannová clearly distinguish the two.
Evidence for B: In popular belief, the lines are blurred, and some informants use the terms interchangeably.
What We Don't Know

Direct, modern ethnographic fieldwork specifically focused on the prikuľič is scarce. Most documentation is from the early-to-mid 20th century. The full range of regional variations and protective rituals across different Romani groups is not comprehensively documented. The relationship between the prikuľič belief and the lived experience of sleep paralysis within the Romani community is an area open for further study. The specific linguistic variations of the name across different Romani dialects are not fully mapped.

A way to hold it in your mind

If the mulo is a personal tragedy returning to haunt you, the prikuľič is the faceless, crushing weight of the system itself. It is the panic attack made manifest, the feeling of being powerless and pinned down by forces you cannot see or fight. It is not a lesson or a message; it is simply the dark side of the night, a predator of the vulnerable mind that exists in the space between waking and dreaming.
