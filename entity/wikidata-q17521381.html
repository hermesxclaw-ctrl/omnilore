Form

No canonical physical description survives. Based on Pausanias's description of the Chest of Cypselus, she would have appeared as a youthful female figure, one of two handmaidens flanking Zeus. She is presumed to be depicted holding or attending to Zeus's thunderbolt. Later artistic and literary interpretations often imagine her as a radiant, swift figure, perhaps with lightning-like attributes, but this is speculative.

Powers

Manifestation of Lightning Astrape is the lightning flash itself. Her power is the power of the phenomenon she embodies – the sudden, blinding, and destructive strike of a lightning bolt. Pausanias, Suda She is not an independent wielder of the thunderbolt; she serves Zeus. Her power is an extension of his. None directly, but the question is whether she 'controls' lightning or simply is a personification of it.

Servant of Zeus Her primary mythological function is to serve Zeus, attending to him and his thunderbolt. This places her in a position of immense, but delegated, power. Pausanias, Suda Her role is subservient. She acts on Zeus's will, not her own. None.

Behavior & Habitat

As a personified natural force, her 'behavior' is the behavior of lightning itself: sudden, brilliant, and destructive. In mythology, she is depicted as a dutiful servant of Zeus, attending to his weapon and perhaps delivering it when he commands. She is not a major actor in myths but a supporting figure, an embodiment of the raw power Zeus wields.

Accounts

The only 'story' is a static image. On the legendary Chest of Cypselus, an ornate artifact from the 7th century BCE, Zeus is depicted standing beside Astrape and Brontē. They are not shown as independent actors but as his ever-present attendants, holding or standing near his thunder and lightning. This tableau cements their role as the personified instruments of his power.

While not naming Astrape as a goddess, Greek poets constantly use the word astrapē to describe the flash of Zeus's thunderbolt. This poetic usage creates a conceptual bridge between the natural phenomenon and the divine will, which later mythographers and artists could personify.

Scholarly Debates
Status as a Goddess vs. Personification
Astrape is a distinct, minor goddess (daimon), a servant of Zeus with her own identity. ⇌ Astrape is simply a poetic personification of lightning, a literary device that was occasionally visualized but not a true object of cult or worship.
Evidence for A: Pausanias describes her as a named figure on the Chest of Cypselus. The Suda lists her as a deity.
Evidence for B: There is no evidence of a cult dedicated to Astrape, no temples, altars, or festivals. She does not appear as a characte
What We Don't Know

The primary gap is the loss of the Chest of Cypselus itself. All knowledge is filtered through Pausanias's description. There is no other known literary, artistic, or archaeological evidence from antiquity. Her appearance, personality, and any other myths she may have appeared in are completely unknown. The distinction between her as a 'daimon' and a 'theos' is a matter of scholarly interpretation.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Astrape as the personification of a camera flash, and Brontē as the 'click' of the shutter. They are the inseparable duo that makes the picture of Zeus's power complete. Or, if you're a modern movie fan, she's the special effects department of the Greek pantheon — essential, powerful, but never the star of the show.
