Who It Is

The dragon is one of the most universal mythological creatures, appearing in the folklore of nearly every major civilization. In Western tradition, dragons are typically depicted as massive, winged, fire-breathing reptiles embodying chaos, greed, and destruction — guardians of hoarded treasure who must be slain by heroes like Sigurd, Beowulf, or Saint George. These dragons represent primal forces to be conquered, often associated with Satanic symbolism in Christian iconography. In striking contrast, East Asian dragons (Chinese lóng, Japanese ryū, Korean yong) are benevolent water-deities associated with rainfall, rivers, and imperial power. They are long, serpentine creatures with four legs, often depicted holding pearls of wisdom and dwelling in underwater palaces. Chinese emperors claimed descent from dragons, and the creature remains a symbol of power, strength, and good fortune. Mesoamerican cultures featured the feathered serpent Quetzalcoatl, a creator deity bridging earth and sky. Across all traditions, dragons share common traits: serpentine or reptilian bodies, supernatural abilities, immense age and wisdom, and a fundamental connection to elemental forces — fire in the West, water in the East. Modern fantasy literature, from Tolkien's Smaug to the dragons of George R.R. Martin and D&D, has synthesized these traditions, creating a rich taxonomy of chromatic and metallic dragon species with complex personalities and societies.
