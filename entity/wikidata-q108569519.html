Who It Is

A scholar who has mastered every university faculty stares at his books at midnight and finds them ash. Outside his Wittenberg study the world is still medieval; inside, a man decides that if Heaven will not answer, Hell might. He will lay Scripture behind the door, sign his name in blood, and trade eternity for twenty-four years of knowing everything and having everything — the bargain that made the name Faust a warning in every Western language.

Archetype: the erudite malcontent. Early Faust is explicitly impious: 'He laid the Holy Scriptures behind the door and under the bench' (Historia, Ch.1), preferring medicine and astrology to theology, driven by curiositas and superbia. Marlowe intensifies intellectual pride and despair - Faustus oscillates between grandiose conjuring (to be as gods) and abject fear, yet refuses repentance. Etymology — Latin faustus = 'favourable, fortunate, auspicious' from favere 'to favour'. The legendary name is almost certainly a humanist Latinization of the German family name Faust/Feust, not a birth name. Contemporary humanists Latinized vernacular surnames; Johann Georg Faust is recorded as 'Faustus' in He

Powers
Diabolical Servitude and Conjuration

Through Mephistopheles, Faust commands illusion, teleportation, invisibility, and conjuration of spirits, treasure, and phantasms. In Historia he summons Alexander the Great, produces wine from a table, and rides a devil-horse. Marlowe's Faustus conjures grapes in winter, Helen, and humiliates the Pope.

Limit: Powers are delegated, not innate; devil obeys letter not spirit, often mocks. Cannot create true life or true love; Helen is a succubus/phantasm in early versions. In Goethe, Mephistopheles admits 'I cannot serve what you truly need.'

Unlimited Knowledge (Promised, Never Fully Delivered)

The core desire: knowledge of all faculties - theology, law, medicine, philosophy - and hidden natural philosophy (astronomy, alchemy, necromancy). The devil provides books, disputations, and astral travel.

Limit: Knowledge is encyclopedic but sterile; Faust remains existentially unsatisfied. Mephistopheles withholds salvific knowledge and gives evasive answers about Hell. Goethe's Faust learns that knowledge without deed is empty.

Youth Restoration and Seduction Glamour

In Goethe, the Witch's Kitchen potion rejuvenates Faust by 30 years, enabling seduction of Gretchen/Margarete; Mephistopheles provides jewels, social access, and erotic persuasion.

Limit: Youth is alchemical illusion; does not confer wisdom. Seduction destroys Gretchen and Faust's moral standing. In early chapbooks Faust's affairs are with succubi and Helen, not innocent maidens.

Wealth, Political Power and World-Shaping

Later Faust, especially Goethe Part Two, wields imperial finance (paper money creation), military engineering, and large-scale land reclamation (draining the sea). Early Faust conjures gold and feasts for patrons.

Limit: Wealth is devil's counterfeit or inflationary illusion; political power depends on Mephistopheles' counsel and leads to war and displacement (Baucis and Philemon episode). Final project remains unfinished at death.

Invulnerability and Flight (Conditional)

Devil-borne flight on a cloak or barrel, travel to Rome, Constantinople, and Hell; immunity from ordinary weapons while pact holds.

Limit: Protection ends at pact expiry; cannot escape the final hour. Flight is demonic transport, not personal levitation. In Marlowe, Faustus cannot leave Wittenberg to flee repentance.

Immortality of the Soul (Contested)

Faust bargains away his immortal soul, implying he possesses one to forfeit. Goethe ultimately has angels bear Faust's 'immortal part' (das Unsterbliche) to heaven, suggesting soul is not alienable.

Limit: Early versions treat soul as legal property transferable by blood signature; Goethe's wager voids strict sale, allowing grace. Theological contradiction drives tragedy.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No single canonical appearance; described through status markers. In the 1587 Historia and woodcut title page: a bearded doctor in scholar's gown and cap, middle-aged, holding a book or conjuring circle. Marlowe's Faustus is a Wittenberg (Wertenberge) doctor of divinity in his study, later in showy devil-given robes. Goethe's Faust is deliberately ageless: Part One opens as an elderly, world-weary professor in a Gothic study piled with skulls and

Symbols — Core symbols: the book (forbidden knowledge, necromancy), the blood-signed pact (chirographum), the magic circle, the crossroads, Mephistopheles as black dog/poodle, the hourglass and the clock striking eleven, Helen of Troy as embodiment of beauty, the poodle transforming into Mephistopheles, the Homunculus flask (Part Two). Early chapbook woodcuts show devils tearing Faust limb from limb. Goethe adds the pentagram (Drudenfuss) trapping Mephisto

Domains — Habitat is the study (Studierzimmer) as liminal space between university and cosmos; Wittenberg/Wertenberge university, later imperial court (Goethe Part Two, Act 1), Thessaly/Arcadia (Classical Walpurgis Night), and finally reclaimed littoral land by the sea where he would build a free people. Cosmology: Lutheran two-tier universe (Heaven/Hell) in early versions; Goethe overlays Hermetic, Paracelsian, and Enlightenment cosmology - Earth Spirit,

Accounts I
The 1587 Historia von D. Johann Fausten - The Damnable Life and Deserved DeathHistoria von D. Johann Fausten (Johann Spies, Frankfurt 1587)

1587, reflecting c.1480-1540 oral legend

Johann Faust, son of a peasant near Weimar, raised in Wittenberg, studies theology but rejects it for necromancy. He conjures Mephostophiles in a forest crossroads, signs a 24-year pact in blood, gains a spirit-servant, and embarks on prodigious conjurings: making wine flow, conjuring Alexander and Helen, flying to Rome to mock the Pope, and begetting a child with a succubus-Helen. Despite warnings from a pious neighbor and an old man, he despairs of forgiveness. At midnight on expiry, devils tear his body limb from limb, leaving his mangled corpse on a dunghill, his soul carried to Hell as warning. Establishes the archetypal Faustian bargain, Lutheran moral that curiositas over pietas leads to irrevocable damnation, and the chapbook marketplace for devil literature. Direct source for all later Fausts.

Marlowe's Tragical History of Doctor FaustusChristopher Marlowe, The Tragicall History of the Life and Death of Doctor Faustus (A-text 1604, B-text 1616, based on P.F. Gent translation 1592)

c.1592-1593, published 1604

Faustus, renowned doctor of divinity at Wittenberg, exhausts logic, medicine, law and divinity ('Divinity, adieu!'). He summons Mephistopheles, dismisses Hell as fable, and signs in blood for 24 years of service. He wastes his powers on pranks (papal humiliation, horse-courser trick), conjures Helen for Emperor Charles V, and is torn between repentance (Good Angel, Old Man) and despair. In the final hour he pleads for time to stand still, but devils enter and drag him offstage to Hell, his limbs later found torn asunder. Popularized Faust in England, fixed Wittenberg setting, introduced psychomachia (angels) and the iconic final-hour soliloquy. Established Faustus as Renaissance overreacher alongside Tamburlaine. Textual instability (A vs B) fuels centuries of editorial debate.

Goethe's Faust Part One - The Gretchen TragedyJohann Wolfgang von Goethe, Faust. Eine Tragödie (1808)

Conceived 1772-1775 (Urfaust), published 1808

Prologue in Heaven: Mephistopheles wagers with the Lord that he can corrupt Faust. Faust, suicidal in his study after failing to grasp the Earth Spirit, is saved by Easter bells. He and Mephistopheles make a wager, not a timed pact: if Faust ever says to a moment 'Linger, thou art so fair,' Mephistopheles wins his soul. Rejuvenated, Faust seduces the innocent Margarete (Gretchen) with Mephistopheles' aid (jewels, Marthe's complicity). Gretchen's mother dies from a sleeping potion Faust provides, her brother Valentin is slain by Faust, she bears an illegitimate child, drowns it, is condemned as infanticide, and awaits execution. Faust tries to rescue her with Mephistopheles' help but she refuses escape, submits to divine judgment, and is saved ('Ist gerettet!'), while Faust flees in despair. Shifts moral center from damnation to redemption and the 'Eternal Feminine.' Makes Gretchen, not Helen, the defining love. Becomes Germany's national drama; Gretchenfrage ('How standst thou with religion?') enters idiom.

Accounts II
Goethe's Faust Part Two - Empire, Antiquity and SalvationJohann Wolfgang von Goethe, Faust. Der Tragödie zweiter Teil (1832, posthumous)

Written 1825-1831, published 1832

Spirits forgive Faust and erase Gretchen's memory. Faust and Mephistopheles serve the Emperor: they invent paper money, conjure Paris and Helen for court entertainment, and Faust becomes obsessed with Helen, fathering Euphorion (Lord Byron allegory) who dies in Icarian fall. After Helena's departure, Faust turns to practical empire: draining a coastal marsh to create free land for a free people. He orders the removal of the old couple Baucis and Philemon, whom Mephistopheles murders. Blind and near death, Faust envisions his utopia and utters the fateful 'Verweile doch' to the anticipatory moment, dying. Mephistopheles tries to claim his soul, but angels, scattering roses and invoking Gretchen/Mater Gloriosa, bear Faust's immortal part to heaven, because 'Whoever strives in ceaseless effort, him we can save.' Completes Faust's arc from magician to modern technocrat-colonizer; controversial salvation divides critics. Provides source for 'Faustian bargain' as metaphor for technological hubris.

Pan Twardowski - The Polish FaustPolish oral legend, first literary attestation in 16th-17th century chronicles; codified by Józef Ignacy Kraszewski, Mistrz Twardowski (1840) and Adam Mickiewicz, Pani Twardowska (1832)

Parallel to German Faust, 16th century onward

Nobleman-magician Jan Twardowski, having studied in Kraków (where historical Faust allegedly studied), makes a pact with the devil for knowledge and magic, with clause that devil can take him only in Rome. He performs miracles, conjures King Sigismund Augustus's dead wife Barbara Radziwiłł, and evades the devil for years by never visiting Rome. Tricked into entering an inn called 'Rome' (Rzym), he is seized, but outwits the devil by reciting a prayer to the Virgin or leaping to the Moon, where he remains as observer. Unlike German Faust, he is often saved or suspended, not damned. Demonstrates independent or diffused Faustian motif in Slavic tradition; Kraków's Faust association (Faust studied there per Widman) may be etiological link. National Polish trickster-magician figure, distinct moral: cleverness and Marian devotion can cheat devil.

The Theophilus Legend - The Proto-FaustEutychianus (?) Greek account of Theophilus of Adana (6th century), Latin translation by Paul the Deacon (8th century); depicted on Notre-Dame de Paris north tympanum

6th century legend, recorded 8th-9th century

Theophilus, archdeacon of Adana, passed over for bishop, makes a written pact with the devil (via a Jewish intermediary) to regain office, sealing it with his seal. Repentant, he prays 40 days to the Virgin Mary, who retrieves the chirograph from the devil and places it on his chest, absolving him. He confesses publicly and dies in sanctity. Unlike Faust, intercession, not striving, saves him. Earliest Christian pact-with-devil narrative; establishes chirograph motif, Marian rescue, and moral that even apostasy is forgivable. Direct literary ancestor cited by scholars for Faust chapbooks.

Faust sold his soul for youth and love (or to seduce Gretchen). — In the original 1587 legend and Marlowe, Faust's motives are knowledge, power, and pleasure generally; Gretchen does not exist. The love-tragedy and rejuvenation are Goethe's 1808 invention, later popularized by Gounod's opera. Early Faust's lover is the demon-Helen, not an innocent maiden.

Faust is a medieval legend. — Faust is early modern (mid-16th century), not medieval. The historical Faust lived c.1480-1540, and the first printed Historia is 1587, contemporary with Elizabethan England. The medieval setting is a later romantic back-projection.

'Faustian bargain' means any deal with the devil for success. — Strictly, Faustian implies sacrificing spiritual/moral values specifically for knowledge, mastery, or unlimited striving, not just wealth or fame. The adjective derives from Goethe's philosophical Faust, not just any diabolical pact (e.g., Robert Johnson crossroads is Faustian only by analogy).

Mephistopheles is the Devil himself (Satan/Lucifer). — In all major versions Mephistopheles is a subordinate devil, servant of Lucifer/Satan, not the supreme Devil. Historia has Mephostophiles as one of many spirits; Marlowe distinguishes Lucifer and Mephistophilis; Goethe makes Mephistopheles a fallen, witty functionary who complains about his boss.

Goethe's Faust is saved because he was ultimately a good person. — Goethe's text is explicit that Faust commits grave sins (seduction, murder, manslaughter of Baucis and Philemon) and is saved not by moral goodness but by ceaseless striving plus grace and Gretchen's intercession. The final chorus says 'Whoever strives in ceaseless effort, him we can save' - a controversial, not moralistic, salvation.
