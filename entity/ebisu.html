Ebisu

God of Fishermen, Patron of Honest Work, The Smiling One

Ebisu, the smiling god of the sea, who brings the bountiful catch and blesses the honest work of humble hands.

Who It Is

Ebisu is the kami of fishermen, honest labor, commercial prosperity, and one of the Seven Lucky Gods (Shichifukujin). He is the only one of the seven who is purely Japanese in origin (the others were imported from India, China, or Buddhist traditions). Ebisu is depicted as a smiling, bearded figure wearing a tall cap (kazaori eboshi) and holding a fishing rod in one hand and a sea bream (tai) — a symbol of good fortune — under his arm or in the other hand. According to myth, Ebisu was born as Hira-Kogo ('leech child'), a boneless, limbless child of Izanagi and Izanami. They set him adrift in a boat, and after a long exile, he eventually washed ashore and grew into Ebisu. He is deaf (hence the origin story of the 'Fukuzōshi' picture scrolls showing him with a peculiar ear shape), but this does not diminish his powers. Ebisu is the patron of all those who work honestly — fishermen, tradesmen, and shopkeepers. His festival is celebrated on January 9-10 (Ebisu-kō), and the first fish of the season is dedicated to him. He is often paired with Daikokuten; the two statues are commonly found in small shops and homes.

Form

Ebisu is typically depicted as a cheerful, round-faced, bearded man wearing a tall black hat (kazaori eboshi) and flowing robes. He holds a fishing rod in his right hand and a large red sea bream (tai) tucked under his left arm. His smile is broad and benevolent, often shown laughing. He may appear slightly plump, suggesting prosperity and contentment. In some depictions, he is shown with a slightly hunched posture, perhaps echoing his origin as a physically disabled child. He is never shown with weapons, emphasizing his peaceful, benevolent nature.

Powers

Fortune-bringing Ebisu grants prosperity and good fortune, particularly in business and trade. His blessings are believed to manifest as increased customers, successful harvests, and bountiful catches. All traditions His blessings are associated with honest work, not gambling or windfalls. He rewards effort, not luck. Some folk tales depict Ebisu granting sudden wealth, while others emphasize his role as a patron of steady, honest labor.

Maritime protection Ebisu protects fishermen and sailors from storms, shipwrecks, and dangerous sea creatures. He is invoked for safe voyages and abundant catches. Fisherman traditions, especially in coastal regions Protection is conditional on proper ritual observance and respect for the sea. None documented — this is a consistent aspect of his worship.

Healing In some folk traditions, Ebisu is invoked for healing, particularly of children's ailments. This may stem from his own origin as a sickly child who survived abandonment. Regional folk traditions, especially in western Japan Not a primary domain; healing is a secondary, localized function. This aspect is not emphasized in the Seven Gods of Fortune tradition.

Deafness (in some traditions) In certain folk tales, Ebisu is described as deaf. This is sometimes interpreted as a metaphor for his impartiality — he cannot hear prayers, so he treats all equally. In other versions, his deafness is a disability he overcame, making him a patron of those with hearing impairments. Folk tales, especially from the Kanto region This is a folk attribute, not a canonical power. Some traditions emphasize his deafness as a positive trait (impartiality), while others see it as a weakness to be compensated for by his other senses.

Accounts

Izanagi and Izanami, the creator deities, give birth to Hiruko ('leech child') after a ritual error. The child is born without bones or limbs — a malformed, weak being. Disappointed, the parents place him in a reed boat and set him adrift on the sea. The child does not die; instead, he is carried by the currents to the eastern shores of Japan, where he is cared for by the Ainu people (in some versions) or by a fisherman's family (in others). After enduring hardship and growing stronger, he becomes Ebisu, the god of fortune and honest work.

Ebisu is gathered with six other deities — Daikokuten, Benzaiten, Bishamonten, Hotei, Fukurokuju, and Jurojin — aboard the treasure ship (takarabune). Together, they travel the world bringing fortune and happiness. Each god represents a different aspect of good fortune: Ebisu represents honest work and abundance from the sea. The seven gods are often depicted in a single image, and their combined blessings are sought during the New Year.

A poor fisherman catches a strange fish that speaks to him. The fish reveals itself to be Ebisu in disguise, testing the fisherman's compassion. The fisherman releases the fish, and as a reward, Ebisu grants him a net that never comes up empty. The fisherman becomes wealthy, but he does not forget his humble origins — he shares his fortune with his village and builds a shrine to Ebisu.

Ebisu is depicted as deaf, unable to hear the prayers of his worshippers. When asked why he cannot hear, he replies that if he could hear, he would favor those who pray loudly over those who pray quietly. His deafness, therefore, is a form of impartiality — he cannot be swayed by eloquence or wealth. He rewards all equally, based on their deeds, not their words.

Field Notes

☘ Ebisu is the only one of the Seven Gods of Fortune (Shichifukujin) with purely Japanese origins — the other six are derived from Indian, Chinese, or mixed sources.

☘ The Yebisu beer brand, named after the god, was first brewed in 1890 and is now one of Japan's most prestigious beer labels. The Tokyo neighborhood of Ebisu is named after the beer, not directly after the god.

☘ During the Toka Ebisu Festival in Osaka, worshippers buy decorated bamboo branches (fuku-zasa) and attach lucky charms to them. The festival attracts over a million visitors each January.

☘ Ebisu is almost always depicted holding a fishing rod and a sea bream (tai). The sea bream is a symbol of celebration in Japan, and its red color is considered auspicious.

☘ In some folk tales, Ebisu is deaf. Rather than a weakness, this is interpreted as a form of impartiality — he cannot hear prayers, so he cannot be swayed by eloquence or wealth.

☘ The character 夷 (used in some renderings of Ebisu's name) means 'barbarian' — a nod to his possible origins as a foreign or outsider deity.

☘ In the video game SMITE, Ebisu is a playable character who uses his fishing rod as a weapon. He is one of the few Japanese deities in the game.

☘ Ebisu's origin story — the abandoned child who survives — has been interpreted as a mythologized explanation for the resilience of marginalized groups in Japanese society.

Content Notes

The core origin story of Ebisu involves the rejection and abandonment of a child born with a severe physical disability. Hiruko is described as a 'leech child' — a being without bones or limbs — and is cast adrift in a reed boat by his own parents. This story reflects ancient Japanese attitudes toward disability, which were often harsh and exclusionary. However, the story also subverts this cruelty: the abandoned child survives and becomes a beloved god. Ebisu's origin thus carries a redemptive message, but the initial act of abandonment is a painful, uncomfortable element.

Some scholars interpret the Hiruko story as a mythologized explanation for the existence of outsider groups or as a reflection of ancient infanticide practices. Others see it as a purely symbolic narrative about cosmic order and the rejection of imperfection.

Kojiki, Nihon Shoki

The character 夷 (used in some renderings of Ebisu's name) means 'barbarian' or 'foreigner.' This connects Ebisu to the Emishi people, a group historically marginalized by the Yamato state. The association of a god of fortune with a 'barbarian' identity may reflect the absorption of foreign or peripheral cultures into the Japanese mainstream, but it also carries colonial undertones.

The relationship between Ebisu and the Emishi is debated. Some scholars see the god as a deified version of an Emishi leader or deity, while others argue the phonetic similarity is coincidental.

A way to hold it in your mind

Ebisu is the patron saint of the underdog and the working person. If the Seven Gods of Fortune were a company, Ebisu would be the employee who started at the bottom, was literally thrown out by management, and then came back to become the most beloved figure in the whole organization. He's the god of the fish market, the corner shop, the small business — not the casino. He rewards effort, not luck. If you've ever worked a hard day for an honest wage and felt like the universe owed you a break, Ebisu is on your side. He's the proof that even being abandoned at birth doesn't mean you can't become someone great — or in his case, a god.
