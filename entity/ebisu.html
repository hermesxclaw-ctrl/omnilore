Who It Is

Imagine a god who was thrown away by his own parents because he was born broken — a child without bones, cast adrift in a reed boat to die on the open sea. Now imagine that same child surviving, washing ashore, growing strong, and becoming one of the most beloved deities in Japan — a round-faced, laughing god of fortune who watches over fishermen, shopkeepers, and anyone who works with their hands. That is Ebisu: the god who turned rejection into redemption, the patron of honest work, and the only one of the Seven Gods of Fortune with a purely Japanese pedigree. He is the underdog who became a champion — and every year, millions of people still celebrate his unlikely victory.

Ebisu is a cheerful, generous, and approachable deity — the 'everyman' of the Seven Gods of Fortune. He is associated with honest work and its rewards, rather than luck or windfalls. He is patient (having endured abandonment and hardship in his origin story) and protective of those who work hard. He is not a trickster, though his origins as a 'barbarian' or outsider god give him an unpredictable, liminal quality. He is often paired with Daikokuten, with whom he shares a jovial rapport.

Powers
Fortune-bringing

Ebisu grants prosperity and good fortune, particularly in business and trade. His blessings are believed to manifest as increased customers, successful harvests, and bountiful catches.

Limit: His blessings are associated with honest work, not gambling or windfalls. He rewards effort, not luck.

Maritime protection

Ebisu protects fishermen and sailors from storms, shipwrecks, and dangerous sea creatures. He is invoked for safe voyages and abundant catches.

Limit: Protection is conditional on proper ritual observance and respect for the sea.

Healing

In some folk traditions, Ebisu is invoked for healing, particularly of children's ailments. This may stem from his own origin as a sickly child who survived abandonment.

Limit: Not a primary domain; healing is a secondary, localized function.

Deafness (in some traditions)

In certain folk tales, Ebisu is described as deaf. This is sometimes interpreted as a metaphor for his impartiality — he cannot hear prayers, so he treats all equally. In other versions, his deafness is a disability he overcame, making him a patron of those with hearing impairments.

Limit: This is a folk attribute, not a canonical power.

Accounts I
The Abandonment of HirukoKojiki, Nihon Shoki

Mythic age (recorded 8th century CE)

Izanagi and Izanami, the creator deities, give birth to Hiruko ('leech child') after a ritual error. The child is born without bones or limbs — a malformed, weak being. Disappointed, the parents place him in a reed boat and set him adrift on the sea. The child does not die; instead, he is carried by the currents to the eastern shores of Japan, where he is cared for by the Ainu people (in some versions) or by a fisherman's family (in others). After enduring hardship and growing stronger, he becomes Ebisu, the god of fortune and honest work. Hiruko's abandonment explains his status as an 'outsider' god — one who was rejected by the divine family but found strength through adversity. His story serves as a foundation myth for the value of perseverance and the dignity of labor. It also establishes his connection to the sea and to marginalized peoples.

Ebisu and the Seven Gods of FortuneVarious medieval and early modern texts, including the 'Shichifukujin' tradition

Muromachi to Edo period (14th–19th century CE)

Ebisu is gathered with six other deities — Daikokuten, Benzaiten, Bishamonten, Hotei, Fukurokuju, and Jurojin — aboard the treasure ship (takarabune). Together, they travel the world bringing fortune and happiness. Each god represents a different aspect of good fortune: Ebisu represents honest work and abundance from the sea. The seven gods are often depicted in a single image, and their combined blessings are sought during the New Year. The Seven Gods of Fortune tradition cemented Ebisu's status as a national deity of prosperity, transcending his regional origins. It also gave him a fixed role in the iconography of Japanese folk religion.

Accounts II
Ebisu and the Fisherman's DreamFolk tale, various regional collections

Edo period (documented)

A poor fisherman catches a strange fish that speaks to him. The fish reveals itself to be Ebisu in disguise, testing the fisherman's compassion. The fisherman releases the fish, and as a reward, Ebisu grants him a net that never comes up empty. The fisherman becomes wealthy, but he does not forget his humble origins — he shares his fortune with his village and builds a shrine to Ebisu. The tale reinforces the moral that kindness and honesty are rewarded. It also establishes the practice of returning a caught sea bream to the water as an offering to Ebisu.

Ebisu and the Deaf GodFolk tale, Kanto region

Edo period (documented)

Ebisu is depicted as deaf, unable to hear the prayers of his worshippers. When asked why he cannot hear, he replies that if he could hear, he would favor those who pray loudly over those who pray quietly. His deafness, therefore, is a form of impartiality — he cannot be swayed by eloquence or wealth. He rewards all equally, based on their deeds, not their words. This tale offers a theological justification for Ebisu's perceived silence in response to prayer. It reframes a potential weakness as a virtue.

Ebisu is a 'lucky charm' deity who grants wealth without effort. — Ebisu is specifically associated with honest work and its rewards. He is the patron of fishermen, merchants, and laborers — not of gamblers or those seeking windfalls. His blessings are earned through effort.

Ebisu is the same as Daikokuten. — Ebisu and Daikokuten are distinct deities. They are often paired, and some traditions describe Daikokuten as Ebisu's father, but they are not the same being. Daikokuten derives from the Indian deity Mahakala and is associated with wealth and the household, while Ebisu is purely Japanese and associated with the sea and honest labor.

The Ebisu neighborhood in Tokyo is named after the god. — The neighborhood is named after the Yebisu Beer brand, which was named after the god. The brewery (originally the Japan Beer Brewery Company) was located in the area, and the neighborhood took its name from the beer. So the connection to the god is indirect, through the beer brand.

Ebisu is a 'minor' or 'obscure' deity. — Ebisu is one of the most widely venerated deities in Japan. His shrines are numerous, and the Toka Ebisu Festival in Osaka attracts over a million visitors annually. He is a major figure in Japanese folk religion, not a footnote.
