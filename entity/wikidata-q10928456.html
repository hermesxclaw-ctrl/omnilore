Who It Is

In Old Japanese the earth did not simply quake — it *nai-ed*. The verb *nau* meant to shake, to tremble, to slip sideways, and the noun *nai* (*nawi*) was the earthquake itself. The god whose name is that word — Nai no Kami — is not a bright hero with a sword but the tremor personified: the unseen force that makes the pivot-stone shudder and the catfish thrash beneath the islands.

Attested behavior: causes ground to shake, rivers to change course, buildings to collapse; not morally framed as evil but as natural potency that must be pacified. In folk narrative, Nai no Kami is capricious and responds to neglect of worship or to the weakening of restraining deities (Takemikazuchi/Futsunushi). In some regional legends, Nai no Kami is appeased by offerings and festivals to prevent tremors. Powers
Seismic Generation (Nai Furu)

Causes the ground to quake, fissure, and heave. Attested folk etiology: when Nai no Kami moves/stretches, the earth shakes. Scale ranges from minor tremor to catastrophic city-destroying quake (as in 1855 Ansei Edo quake attributed to Namazu/Nai).

Limit: Not omnipotent — can be restrained by superior martial kami (Takemikazuchi, Futsunushi) and by the Kaname-ishi. In legend, Nai no Kami is dormant while pinned; quakes occur when restraint lapses (e.g., when Kashima deity leaves to attend divine assembly at Izumo in Kannazuki).

Ground Liquefaction and Landform Alteration

Associated with secondary effects: mud eruption, river course change, landslides, and creation of fissures. Hitachi Fudoki records earthquakes altering landscape, later linked to Nai agency in folk commentary.

Limit: Effects are collateral of shaking, not separate creation power; no attested power to create islands or mountains (unlike Ōkuninushi).

Pacification-Responsive Dormancy

Can be calmed or bound by ritual: offerings, festivals, sutra recitation (syncretic period), and physical restraint by Kaname-ishi. Villagers historically held Nai no Kami matsuri to prevent quakes.

Limit: Pacification is temporary and requires continual worship; neglect or ritual impurity allows reactivation. No attested permanent destruction of Nai no Kami.

No Attested Anthropomorphic Combat or Shapeshifting

Unlike many kami, Nai no Kami has no attested myths of sword combat, transformation, or sexual liaisons. Its agency is purely geophysical.

Limit: Absence may reflect source loss rather than definitive lack; flagged as unknown.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No attested anthropomorphic iconography specific to Nai no Kami in classical sources. Attested: aniconic or conceptual. In folk conception, Nai no Kami is invisible force or tremor itself. Where visualized in Edo and modern folk art, it is represented metonymically by (a) the giant catfish Namazu thrashing beneath the earth, (b) the Kaname-ishi pivot stone that pins it, or (c) a shaking ground with anthropomorphized earth. No Heian-period statue

Symbols — Primary symbols: 1) Kaname-ishi (要石, pivot stone) — the capstone that restrains the earthquake; 2) Namazu (鯰, catfish) — the animal agent whose movement is the quake; 3) Cracks/fissures in earth, overturned buildings in namazu-e prints. No attested regalia (sword, mirror, jewel) specific to Nai no Kami. In shrine context, shimenawa (sacred rope) around a stone may mark Nai no Kami pacification site. Disputed: some modern commercial illustrations

Domains — Domain: lithosphere and seismic activity — earthquakes, ground liquefaction, landslides, and by extension, earth movements. Habitat: conceptualized as dwelling beneath the earth (under the Kaname-ishi), at the roots of the islands, or in the muddy depths where Namazu lives. Accounts I
The Pivot Stone of Kashima — Takemikazuchi Pins the EarthquakeHitachi Fudoki (713-721), Kashima Jingu engi (medieval shrine legends), and Edo namazu-e commentary; summarized in Smits (2014) and Ashkenazi (2003)

Nara period text, elaborated Heian through Edo

The martial deity Takemikazuchi no Mikoto, enshrined at Kashima Jingu, drives his sword or the Kaname-ishi (pivot stone) through the earth to pin the giant catfish Namazu — the zoomorphic form/agent of Nai no Kami — preventing it from thrashing. When Takemikazuchi is absent (notably during the tenth lunar month when kami gather at Izumo for Kamiarizuki), the Namazu escapes and the earth *nai* (quakes). At Kashima the stone's tip is visible above ground; its root is said to extend to the earth's core. Pilgrims tested the stone's power by digging. Etiology for why eastern Japan (Kantō) experiences quakes when Kashima deity leaves; establishes Kashima-Katori as spiritual protectors of the capital against earthquakes; creates pilgrimage and apotropaic stone worship that persists today.

The Ansei Edo Earthquake and the Namazu Uprising (Ansei Jishin Namazu-e)Woodblock prints (namazu-e) produced after the Ansei Edo earthquake of 1855 (Ansei 2, 10th month); collection at National Museum of Japanese History and in Ouwehand (1964) Namazu-e and Their Themes

Edo period, 1855-1856

After the devastating M~7.0 Ansei Edo quake that killed ~7,000, hundreds of cheap woodblock prints depicted Namazu/Nai no Kami as cause and as social satirist. In one popular print, the Kashima deity scolds Namazu for causing death; Namazu pleads that it was provoked by human greed and that its thrashing redistributed wealth (destroying rich merchants' storehouses, creating work for carpenters). Other prints show Nai no Kami being placated with offerings or being punished. The prints functioned as both disaster explanation and social critique. Created the dominant popular image of earthquake deity as catfish; government censored prints for lèse-majesté/social satire; established template for modern Japanese earthquake folklore that persists in disaster education.

Kannazuki — The Month Without Gods When the Earth ShakesMedieval folk calendar belief recorded in Muromachi-period setsuwa and Edo encyclopedias (e.g., Wakan Sansai Zue, 1712); analyzed in Teeuwen & Rambelli (2003)

Medieval to early modern (attested 14th-18th c.)

In the tenth month (Kannazuki, 'Month Without Gods'), all kami leave their shrines to convene at Izumo Taisha, leaving their territories unguarded. Folk belief held that earthquakes were more likely in Kannazuki because Takemikazuchi had left Kashima, releasing Nai no Kami/Namazu. Villagers therefore held extra rites at Kashima's empty shrine or placed stones as temporary substitutes. Explains seasonal clustering of folk earthquake anxiety; reinforces Izumo-Kashima ritual network; still cited in modern folklore studies as example of calendrical risk perception.

Accounts II
Yanagita Kunio's Field Report — The Village Hokora for Nai no KamiYanagita Kunio, folklore field notes and 'Minkan Denshō' (1930s) and Ishizuka Takatoshi's folk religion surveys; summarized in Yanagita, The Legends of Tōno (1910) tradition and later compendia

Early 20th century ethnographic collection (reflecting Meiji-Taishō oral tradition)

Yanagita and his students recorded small roadside hokora (mini-shrines) dedicated to 'Nai no Kami' or 'Jishin no Kami' on the outskirts of villages in Ibaraki and Fukushima. Informants said the shrine was built after a remembered quake that split a rice field, and that annual offerings of sake and mochi were made to 'keep the ground still.' When the shrine was neglected during wartime, elders blamed a subsequent tremor on the deity's anger. The deity had no statue, only a natural stone with shimenawa. Demonstrates living folk religion outside state Shinto; shows Nai no Kami as ujigami (local tutelary) rather than national kami; provides rare direct attestation of theonym in ritual context.

The Hitachi Fudoki Earthquake — Land Torn by ShakingHitachi Fudoki (713-721), entry for Kashima District; translation in Aoki Michiko, Records of Wind and Earth (1997)

Nara period (713-721)

The gazetteer describes Kashima's terrain as shaped by divine action: 'The earth shook and hills collapsed, rivers were blocked.' While the text does not name Nai no Kami, later commentaries (Edo kokugaku) attribute such primordial shaking to Nai no Kami before Takemikazuchi subdued it. The passage provides the earliest topographic description linked to seismic myth in the Kashima cultic area. Anchors earthquake myth in imperial gazetteer geography; later Shinto historiography uses it to claim antiquity for Nai no Kami belief; modern geologists cite it as possible record of Jōgan or earlier Kantō quake.

Syncretic Pacification — Kashima Myōjin as Buddhist Avatar Subduing JishinKashima Daimyōjin engi emaki (medieval picture scrolls) and Honji suijaku literature; analyzed in Breen & Teeuwen, Shinto in History (2000)

Kamakura-Muromachi (13th-15th c.)

In medieval honji suijaku thought, Kashima Daimyōjin (Takemikazuchi) was considered an avatar (gongen) of a Buddhist divinity (variously Kannon or Śakra). The earthquake (Nai) was reinterpreted as a manifestation of karmic disturbance or of unruly earth spirits (jishin-majin). Ritual pacification involved Buddhist sutra chanting (Lotus Sutra) alongside Shinto offerings to keep Nai no Kami quiescent. The syncretic text claims Buddhist law ultimately restrains the quake. Shows medieval reinterpretation of folk earthquake deity within Buddhist cosmology; explains why some Edo prints show Buddhist deities scolding Namazu; Meiji shinbutsu bunri (separation) later stripped Buddhist elements, returning Nai to Shinto folk category.

Nai no Kami is the Japanese god of earthquakes like Poseidon is god of the sea — a major Olympian-style deity with myths and statues. — Nai no Kami is a minor, largely aniconic folk theonym for the earthquake phenomenon itself, not a major ranked kami in Kojiki/Nihon Shoki. No classical statue or grand myth cycle; its 'myths' are regional engi and Edo popular prints, not state mythology.

Nai no Kami and Namazu are completely separate — one is a god, one is a giant catfish monster. — In folk practice they are functionally identical or overlapping; Namazu is the Edo-period zoomorphic visualization of Nai no Kami. Separating them strictly is scholarly taxonomy, not folk reality.

The Kaname-ishi at Kashima is a magical stone that scientifically prevents earthquakes in Kantō. — The stone is a natural concretion with folk legend; it has no seismological effect. Its cultural role is apotropaic/psychological, not geophysical. Modern shrine literature explicitly says it is symbolic.

Nai no Kami appears in the Kojiki by name. — No passage in Kojiki or Nihon Shoki securely names 'Nai no Kami'. The word *nai* appears as common noun for quake, but the theonym is post-Nara folk development, attested in medieval engi and early modern folklore.
