Who It Is

Umay is not a distant queen on a mountaintop; she is the goddess who anoints a newborn's lips with a drop of celestial milk, who withdraws from a crying child and must be called back by a shaman's song, whose sixty golden tresses are the sun's own rays. Her name is the old Turkic word for placenta—she is literally the afterbirth made divine.

Umay is protective, maternal, and intimately involved in the daily welfare of infants. She is not aloof; when she leaves a child, that child falls ill, and shamans must intercede to bring her back. A sleeping infant's smile signals her presence; crying signals her absence. She also extends her care to communities, aiding in the acquisition of food, goods, and luck.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The Turkic word umāy originally denoted the placenta or afterbirth. By semantic extension it became the name of the goddess who safeguards women and children. Powers
Soul-Midwifery

Umay receives the souls of unborn children and keeps them in her celestial 'temple' on Mount Ymay-tas or Amay until the moment of birth. At birth she anoints the infant's lips with a drop from the Lake of Milk in the sky, granting the soul entry into the body.

Limit: Her power is limited to the pre-birth and early-childhood stage. Once a child has grown beyond infancy, her direct guardianship wanes.

Childhood Protection

She shields infants from harm and illness. When she withdraws from a child, the child becomes sick; shamans perform rites to summon her back. A smiling sleeping baby is interpreted as a sign of her proximity.

Limit: She does not prevent all childhood mortality; 'Kara Umay' is the name used when a child dies or when she turns away.

Fertility and Abundance

Beyond individual infants, Umay blesses communities with agricultural fertility, livestock, and general prosperity. Kyrgyz tradition holds that she helps peoples around the world obtain food, goods, and luck.

Limit: This communal aspect is less prominent in older Orkhon sources, suggesting it developed in later folk practice.

Solar Radiance

Associated with the sun, Umay is called Sarı Kız ('Yellow Maiden'). Her sixty golden tresses are likened to sun rays, and yellow is her sacred color. She emits light around her.

Limit: The solar association may reflect later syncretism with Central Asian sun cults rather than the earliest Orkhon layer.

Shapeshifting

She can take avian form—specifically a white bird, swan, or goose—and is sometimes linked to the Huma bird by popular etymology. She may also appear as a pink cloud in Yakut memory.

Limit: Avian forms are mentioned in Turkish and Siberian folklore but not in the Orkhon inscriptions, so they are likely later elaborations.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Umay is depicted as a middle-aged woman of gentle aspect, with long white or golden hair—sometimes specified as sixty tresses resembling sun rays. She wears white or silver garments and is frequently shown with a child at her side. In some Siberian and Altai traditions she can assume the form of a white bird, a swan, or a goose, and she is occasionally described with wings. Among the Khakas she is remembered as a delicate, white-haired lady watch

Symbols — The sun (she is called Sarı Kız, 'Yellow Maiden'), long golden hair, cradles, strings tied with miniature cradles, the olive tree, and sometimes the Huma bird by folk association. In Turkic iconography she is consistently paired with children.

Domains — Her primary domain is childbirth, infancy, and the souls of the unborn. She dwells in the sky, invisible to ordinary mortals, and maintains a 'temple' on Mount Ymay-tas or Amay where the souls of future babies are stored until birth. She descends to earth at the moment of birth, anointing the newborn with a drop from the celestial Lake of Milk.

Accounts I
The Mother Like Umay: Kul Tigin's InscriptionOrkhon inscriptions, Kul Tigin monument (c. 732 CE)

8th century CE

The Kul Tigin inscription invokes the khagan's mother under the formula 'Umay teg ögüm katun kutıŋa', declaring her to be like the goddess Umay herself. This royal consecration links Umay's protective power to the legitimacy and fertility of the khaganate: the mother of the ruler embodies the goddess, and through her the empire secures its future progeny. The inscription establishes a paradigm in which khagan wives (katuns or hatuns) are themselves considered manifestations of Umay, responsible for producing heirs who guarantee the empire's continuity.

Accounts II
The Cradle-String Ritual and the Crying ChildDivanü Lügat'it-Türk (11th c.); Karataev & Umaraliyev 2018

Medieval to modern

Turkic women desiring a child tie strings bearing small cradles as votive offerings to Umay. If an infant cries persistently, the community believes Umay has withdrawn from the child. A shaman is then dispatched to call her back. The sleeping infant's smile confirms her return. This ritual places Umay at the center of reproductive medicine and pediatric care in traditional society; her absence is a diagnostic explanation for illness, and her return is the cure.

The Umay Nine Tree at TeosTurkish Museums (2024); local Anatolian folklore

Contemporary memorial practice rooted in ancient tradition

An olive tree estimated at nearly 1,800 years old at the Teos Archaeological Site is known as 'The Umay Nine Tree' (Nana Umay). It is presented as a living symbol of Umay's ancient Anatolian presence, embodying the goddess's connection to childbirth, growth, and the enduring circle of life. The tree serves as a modern pilgrimage and ecological heritage object, anchoring Umay in both archaeological memory and living landscape.

Umay is the same as the Huma bird (Huma bird). — The Huma bird is a separate legendary avian entity. Umay is sometimes associated with birds or identified with the Huma by popular etymology, but the sources do not equate them.

Umay is a Tibetan deity because of the similar-sounding Umê script. — The Umê script is a Tibetan writing system; Umay is a Turkic goddess. The resemblance is coincidental.

Umay is exclusively a Turkish goddess. — While strongly attested in Turkish tradition, Umay is also worshipped (under variant names) among Kyrgyz, Kazakhs, Yakuts, Khakas, Altay, and other Siberian Turkic peoples.
