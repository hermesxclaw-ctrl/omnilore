Who It Is

Shisa are the lion-dog guardian figures ubiquitous in Ryukyuan (Okinawan) culture, placed on rooftops, at gates, and at the entrances of homes, businesses, and temples to ward off evil spirits. They are the Okinawan version of the Chinese guardian lion (shishi) and the Japanese komainu. A pair of shisa typically guards an entrance: the left one has an open mouth (to scare away evil spirits) and the right one has a closed mouth (to keep good spirits in), a variation on the traditional 'a-un' (om/ah) pattern of Buddhist guardian figures. Shisa are made from ceramic, stone, or concrete, ranging from small souvenirs to monumental statues several meters tall. According to Ryukyuan legend, a Chinese emissary gave a shisa figurine to the king of the Ryukyu Kingdom to protect against a destructive sea dragon that was terrorizing the coast. When the shisa was placed on the roof of the royal palace, the dragon was frightened away. The practice soon spread to common households. Today, shisa are the single most recognizable symbol of Okinawan culture — found everywhere in the islands and sold as souvenirs worldwide. They are not considered deities in the full sense but powerful talismans (and, in folk belief, beings with their own spirits). The left-hand shisa with the open mouth is traditionally called 'A' (agyo) and the right-hand one with the closed mouth 'Un' (un'gyo). A shisa is traditionally placed on the roof facing the direction from which evil is expected — most commonly the northwest.
