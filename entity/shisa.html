Who It Is

On Okinawan rooftops, a silent sentinel crouches—part lion, part dog, wholly guardian. The shisa's open mouth barks at unseen evils, while its closed mouth keeps good fortune close. But these stone creatures are no mere ornaments; they are the echoes of a dragon's defeat and a king's talisman, rooted in a legend that still shapes the islands' landscape.

As inanimate objects, shisa do not have behavior, but they are believed to possess protective powers. They are seen as vigilant sentinels, ever watchful over the homes and villages they guard.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The term 'shisa' is derived from the Okinawan dialect, likely a shortening of 'sisi' (lion), which itself comes from the Chinese 'shi' (lion). In the Yaenaman dialect, it is called 'Sisi,' closer to the original Japanese word.

Pronunciation — SHEE-sah

Aliases — Shīsā, Sisi, Shi-sa, Lion-dog, Okinawan guardian lion

Ownership — Ryukyuan (Okinawan) culture, with origins in Chinese guardian lion traditions.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Shisa are typically stone or ceramic figures resembling a cross between a lion and a dog. They are often placed in pairs on rooftops or flanking gates. The left shisa traditionally has a closed mouth, the right an open mouth, though this can vary. They may be painted in vibrant colors or left in natural stone.

Symbols — The open mouth is believed to ward off evil spirits, while the closed mouth keeps good spirits in. The shisa is a protective ward, symbolizing guardianship and the warding off of misfortune.

Domains — Shisa are found on Okinawa and in Ryukyuan communities, typically on rooftops, at gates, or inside homes. The 'Gana-mui Woods' near Naha is a legendary site associated with a shisa legend.

Accounts I
The Dragon of Naha PortChizue, Sesoko. Legends of Okinawa. 1969.

Legendary, likely pre-modern

A Chinese emissary gave the king of Shuri a necklace with a shisa figurine. The king wore it under his clothes. A sea dragon terrorized the village of Madanbashi, eating villagers and destroying property. The king visited, and a noro (priestess) instructed him via a boy named Chiga to face the dragon with the figurine. The king did so, a great roar sounded, and a boulder fell from heaven, crushing the dragon's tail. The dragon died, and the boulder and dragon's body became the Gana-mui Woods. The villagers built a large stone shisa to protect against the dragon's spirit. The dragon was defeated, the village saved, and the Gana-mui Woods was created. The tradition of placing shisa for protection was reinforced.

Accounts II
The Great Stone Shisa of TomimoriChizue, Sesoko. Legends of Okinawa. 1969.

Legendary, likely pre-modern

Tomimori Village near Kochinda Town suffered many fires. The villagers consulted Saiouzui, a Feng Shui master, who said the fires were caused by the power of Mt. Yaese. He advised building a stone shisa facing the mountain. They did, and the village was protected from fire ever since. The village was protected from fires, and a large stone shisa was erected.
