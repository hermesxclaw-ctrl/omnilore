Etymology

The term 'shisa' is derived from the Okinawan dialect, likely a shortening of 'sisi' (lion), which itself comes from the Chinese 'shi' (lion). In the Yaenaman dialect, it is called 'Sisi,' closer to the original Japanese word.

Cultural Ownership

Ryukyuan (Okinawan) culture, with origins in Chinese guardian lion traditions.

Disputed Identifications

Gender assignment of the open/closed mouth shisa varies among Okinawans; some believe the male is open-mouthed and female closed, while others hold the opposite. This is a matter of regional and individual interpretation, not a single canonical rule.

Archive Dossier — Approach

On Okinawan rooftops, a silent sentinel crouches—part lion, part dog, wholly guardian. The shisa's open mouth barks at unseen evils, while its closed mouth keeps good fortune close. But these stone creatures are no mere ornaments; they are the echoes of a dragon's defeat and a king's talisman, rooted in a legend that still shapes the islands' landscape.

I sit on the rooftops of the Ryukyus, my mouth open to swallow evil, my eyes fixed on the horizon. As long as the shisa watches, the home is safe.

Who It Is

Shisa are the lion-dog guardian figures ubiquitous in Ryukyuan (Okinawan) culture, placed on rooftops, at gates, and at the entrances of homes, businesses, and temples to ward off evil spirits. They are the Okinawan version of the Chinese guardian lion (shishi) and the Japanese komainu. A pair of shisa typically guards an entrance: the left one has an open mouth (to scare away evil spirits) and the right one has a closed mouth (to keep good spirits in), a variation on the traditional 'a-un' (om/ah) pattern of Buddhist guardian figures. Shisa are made from ceramic, stone, or concrete, ranging from small souvenirs to monumental statues several meters tall. According to Ryukyuan legend, a Chinese emissary gave a shisa figurine to the king of the Ryukyu Kingdom to protect against a destructive sea dragon that was terrorizing the coast. When the shisa was placed on the roof of the royal palace, the dragon was frightened away. The practice soon spread to common households. Today, shisa are the single most recognizable symbol of Okinawan culture — found everywhere in the islands and sold as souvenirs worldwide. They are not considered deities in the full sense but powerful talismans (and, in folk belief, beings with their own spirits). The left-hand shisa with the open mouth is traditionally called 'A' (agyo) and the right-hand one with the closed mouth 'Un' (un'gyo). A shisa is traditionally placed on the roof facing the direction from which evil is expected — most commonly the northwest.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

Shisa are typically stone or ceramic figures resembling a cross between a lion and a dog. They are often placed in pairs on rooftops or flanking gates. The left shisa traditionally has a closed mouth, the right an open mouth, though this can vary. They may be painted in vibrant colors or left in natural stone.

Symbols & Iconography

The open mouth is believed to ward off evil spirits, while the closed mouth keeps good spirits in. The shisa is a protective ward, symbolizing guardianship and the warding off of misfortune.

Behaviour & Personality

As inanimate objects, shisa do not have behavior, but they are believed to possess protective powers. They are seen as vigilant sentinels, ever watchful over the homes and villages they guard.

Domains & Habitats

Shisa are found on Okinawa and in Ryukyuan communities, typically on rooftops, at gates, or inside homes. The 'Gana-mui Woods' near Naha is a legendary site associated with a shisa legend.

Version Differences

Variations exist in the gender assignment of open/closed mouths. Some say male open, female closed; others say the reverse. The Yaenaman dialect calls them 'Sisi.' Modern depictions may vary in style, but the traditional form remains consistent.

The open-mouthed shisa is believed to scare away evil spirits and malevolent forces.

Limitations

Requires proper placement and orientation (typically on rooftops or gates).

Contradictions

Gender assignment varies, but the function of warding off evil is consistent.

Keeping good spirits in

Source Version Traditional Ryukyuan belief

Description

The closed-mouthed shisa is believed to keep good spirits and blessings from leaving the home.

Limitations

Must be paired with an open-mouthed shisa to be effective.

Contradictions

Some believe the closed mouth is the male, others the female, but the function remains the same.

Protection from fire

Source Version Legend from Tomimori Village

Description

In the Tomimori legend, a stone shisa was built to face Mt. Yaese and protect the village from fires.

Limitations

Specific to the Tomimori village legend; not a general power.

Contradictions

None known.

Defeating sea dragons

Source Version Legend from Chizue's 'Legends of Okinawa'

Description

In the legend of the Naha dragon, a shisa figurine held by the king caused a boulder to fall and crush the dragon's tail, killing it.

Limitations

Legendary, not a general power.

Contradictions

None known.
