Who It Is

Imagine commissioning a bespoke designer gown, refusing to pay the bill, and then being cursed by the goddess of fashion herself to run off with a handsome stranger who robs you blind and leaves you dead on a foreign island. That's the story of Alcinoe, the Corinthian princess who learned the hard way that in ancient Greece, you always tip your weaver—especially when the weaver has a direct line to Athena.

Alcinoe's behavior, as attested in the mythographic tradition, is characterized by a single, decisive act of impiety: she refuses to pay the wages of a skilled weaver, Nicandra. This act suggests a combination of arrogance, stinginess, and disregard for the dignity of craft labor. Her subsequent behavior—abandoning her husband and children to flee with a foreigner—is presented as the direct consequence of Athena's punishment rather than a pre-existing character flaw. Powers
None (mortal)

Alcinoe possesses no supernatural powers. She is a mortal woman whose only 'ability' of note is her capacity to commission fine textile work, which implies wealth and social standing. Her story is a cautionary tale about the misuse of mortal power—specifically, the power of a patron over an artisan—and the divine retribution that follows.

Limit: As a mortal, Alcinoe is subject to the whims of the gods and the consequences of her own actions. Her lack of power is the central point of her story: she has enough power to wrong a weaver but not enough to escape divine justice.

Accounts I
The Story of AlcinoeParthenius of Nicaea, Erotica Pathemata, Chapter 27

1st century BCE (composition); the underlying myth may be older

Alcinoe, daughter of Polybus, king of Corinth, commissions a fine robe from a weaver named Nicandra. When the work is complete, Alcinoe refuses to pay the agreed wages. Nicandra, in her distress, prays to Athena, the patron goddess of weaving, for justice. Athena hears the prayer and, in her wrath at the slight against her craft, afflicts Alcinoe with a mad passion for a man from Samos named Xanthus. Alcinoe, overcome by this divinely inflicted love, abandons her husband and children and sails to Samos with Xanthus. Once there, Xanthus reveals his true nature: he steals all of Alcinoe's possessions and abandons her. Destitute and alone, Alcinoe is killed by her own servants (who have accompanied her) or, in a variant, takes her own life. The story ends with the death of the impious princess, her punishment complete. Alcinoe dies in a foreign land, having lost her family, her wealth, and her life. Alcinoe is a goddess or a Nereid — Alcinoe is a mortal Corinthian princess. The confusion arises from the existence of a separate Nereid named Alcinoe in Hyginus' Fabulae, but the two figures are distinct.

Alcinoe's story is about romantic love — The story is primarily about divine punishment for impiety, specifically the mistreatment of a skilled artisan. The love affair with Xanthus is a punishment inflicted by Athena, not a romantic pursuit by Alcinoe.

Alcinoe is a villain — Alcinoe is a tragic figure whose initial transgression is disproportionately punished. She is not presented as a villain but as a woman who makes a single bad decision and suffers catastrophic consequences.

Gaps: The primary gap is the lack of any visual representation of Alcinoe in ancient art. No vase paintings, frescoes, or sculptures depicting her story are known to survive.
