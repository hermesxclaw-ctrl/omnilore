Who It Is

One name haunted Athens for three hundred years. Megakles meant great glory, but every man who bore it inherited blood-guilt, exile, or a violent death — from the archon who slaughtered suppliants clinging to Athena's altar, to the tycoon whose wealth got him banished by broken pottery, to the soldier who put on a king's cloak and died for it.

Characterizations are hostile and partisan, filtered through enemies and historians. Megakles II (632 BC): decisive and impious according to Thucydides — willing to violate suppliant asylum to kill Cylonians. Megakles III (c. 560 BC): portrayed by Herodotus as cunning, ambitious, and transactional — drives out Pisistratus, then allies with him via marriage, then breaks alliance when Pisistratus practices contraception (Herodotus 1.61: 'not in the customary way') to avoid polluted offspring; also status-seeking in competing for Agariste. Name & Tongue

Etymology — Ancient Greek Μεγακλῆς, from megas (μέγας) 'great, large' + kleos (κλέος) 'glory, fame, rumor' = 'great glory' or 'great fame'. Common aristocratic name in Archaic and Classical Greece, especially in the Alcmaeonidae genos.

Pronunciation — Ancient Greek /me.ɡa.klɛ̂ːs/ (meh-gah-KLAES); Latinized Megacles /ˈmɛɡ.ə.kliːz/; Modern Greek Megaklis /me.ɣaˈklis/

Aliases — Megacles, Μεγακλῆς, Megakles son of Alcmaeon, Megakles son of Hippocrates, Megakles of Athens, Megacles of Epirus

Ownership — Ancient Greek, specifically Athenian (Alcmaeonidae clan of Attica) for five successive Athenian figures, and Molossian/Epirote for one officer of Pyrrhus. Historical persons, not m

Powers
Eponymous Archonship and Genos Leadership

Held the chief magistracy of Athens (archon eponymos) giving name to the year; authority over religious and judicial matters and command of genos Alcmaeonidae with vast kinship network and wealth. Megakles II exercised power to order killing of suppliants; Megakles III could mobilize faction to expel Pisistratus.

Limit: Archon power limited by Areopagus and later by tyrant; after Cylon affair, Megakles II was tried, convicted, and exiled with entire genos, showing accountability to polis and religious law. Alcmaeonid power depended on Delphi and foreign alliances, not absolute.

Wealth, Equestrian and Delphic Influence

Alcmaeonid wealth from rebuilding Delphi temple and alleged gifts of Croesus (Herodotus 6.125), enabling chariot teams, bribery of Pythia (Herodotus 5.62-63), and lavish lifestyle. Megakles son of Hippocrates maintained a Pythian-winning tethrippon team, an ability requiring enormous capital.

Limit: Wealth made them targets for ostracism and accusations of corruption; Herodotus notes Alcmaeonids denied bribing Pythia for self-interest, but rumor persisted. Ostracism could strip political power despite wealth.

Marriage Alliance and Tyrant-Making

Ability to forge and break alliances via dynastic marriage: Megakles III married his daughter (unnamed, called Coesyra in later sources) to Pisistratus to restore him to tyranny, and earlier married Agariste daughter of Cleisthenes of Sicyon to link Alcmaeonids to Sicyonian tyranny.

Limit: Alliance collapsed when Pisistratus refused to beget children 'in the customary way' with Megakles' daughter, fearing the Alcmaeonid curse; Megakles could not compel tyrant to produce heirs, leading to second expulsion.

Military Deception and Self-Sacrifice (Epirote)

Megacles of Epirus performed battlefield substitution: exchanged armor, cloak, and helmet with King Pyrrhus to draw Roman attack onto himself, saving the king at Battle of Heraclea.

Limit: Ruse succeeded briefly but Megacles was killed by Publius Decius Mus (or according to Zonaras, by a Roman soldier); Pyrrhus had to reveal himself alive to prevent army collapse. Ability was single-use and fatal.

Hereditary Miasma (Curse)

Not a power but a negative supernatural status: after killing suppliants, Megakles II and his genos incurred miasma (pollution) that was believed to cause divine hostility, plague, and political disability for generations, requiring purification by Epimenides of Crete and later used as political weapon.

Limit: Miasma could be ritually purified but never fully erased in political rhetoric; Pericles still attacked as Alcmaeonid polluted centuries later.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No contemporary physical description survives for any Megakles. As Athenian aristocrats (eupatridai), the Alcmaeonid Megakles figures are assumed to have presented as elite adult males: bearded in Archaic vase conventions, wearing himation and chiton. Wealth is the only quasi-physical attribute attested: Herodotus (6.125) notes Alcmaeonid opulence from Delphi and Croesus, and ostraca call Megakles son of Hippocrates ostentatious and luxury-loving

Symbols — No verified portrait statue or coin portrait identified for any Megakles. Iconography is indirect: (1) Ostraca - pottery sherds incised with his name, sometimes with caricature or slur; one ostracon bears a sketch interpreted as a horse or luxury symbol. (2) Chariot-racing victory: Pythian 7 implies ownership of a four-horse chariot team (tethrippon), a supreme elite status symbol; Pindar links the Alcmaeonid house to wealth and equestrian victor

Domains — Athenian Megakles figures belong to the Alcmaeonidae genos, centered in Attica with estates in Athens and connections to Delphi (treasury), Sicyon (marriage alliance), and later exile sites. Political domain: archonship (eponymous archon), genos leadership, tyrant-making/breaking, and later democratic politics under ostracism. Accounts I
The Cylon Conspiracy and the Alcmaeonid CurseThucydides 1.126; Herodotus 5.71; Plutarch Solon 12; Aristotle Ath. Pol. 1

Archaic Greece, c. 636 or 632 BC (archonship of Megakles)

Cylon, Olympic victor and son-in-law of Theagenes of Megara, attempted to seize the Acropolis as tyrant during an Olympic festival, relying on Delphic oracle. Megakles as eponymous archon led the Athenian response, besieging Cylon's supporters on the Acropolis. The conspirators took refuge as suppliants at Athena Polias's altar. Megakles and fellow archons persuaded them to leave the altar with promise of safety (or tied a thread to the statue that broke), then killed them, some even at the altars of the Eumenides. For violating suppliant asylum, Megakles and the entire Alcmaeonidae were tried by a court of 300 nobles, convicted of sacrilege, and exiled; even the bones of dead Alcmaeonids were dug up and cast beyond borders. The miasma persisted for generations. Creation of hereditary religious pollution used for 200 years to attack Alcmaeonids; later purification by Epimenides; Spartans demanded expulsion of Pericles as polluted in 432 BC; foundational myth for Athenian concepts of asylum and pollution.

The Contest for Agariste of SicyonHerodotus 6.126-131

c. 575-571 BC

Cleisthenes, tyrant of Sicyon, advertised his daughter Agariste as bride for the best Greek. Suitors included Megakles son of Alcmaeon of Athens and Hippocleides son of Tisandros. After year-long tests of lineage, athletic and sympotic behavior, Cleisthenes favored Hippocleides, but Hippocleides danced drunkenly on a table, exposing himself, prompting Cleisthenes' famous 'Hippocleides doesn't care' (ou phrontis Hippocleidei). Cleisthenes then chose Megakles, marrying Agariste to him. Herodotus presents this as origin of Athenian Alcmaeonid prestige and democratic reform lineage. Birth of Hippocrates and Cleisthenes the reformer (founder of Athenian democracy); lineage to Pericles and Alcibiades; Alcmaeonid claim to panhellenic excellence.

The Phye Stratagem and the Alliance with PisistratusHerodotus 1.59-64; Aristotle Ath. Pol. 14

c. 560-556 BC (Pisistratus's first and second tyrannies)

Megakles son of Alcmaeon first led opposition that expelled Pisistratus in 560 BC. Soon after, Megakles quarreled with his own allies and offered to restore Pisistratus if Pisistratus married his daughter. To make the return impressive, they dressed a tall, beautiful woman named Phye from Paeania in full armor as Athena and drove her into Athens on a chariot with Pisistratus, heralds proclaiming Athena herself was bringing Pisistratus home. Athenians, Herodotus says, believed it and welcomed him. Herodotus adds his own skepticism: he finds it astonishing that Athenians, reputed wisest of Greeks, could be so deceived. The marriage failed when Pisistratus, unwilling to beget children with a cursed family, had intercourse 'not in the customary way' to avoid pregnancy. Megakles, enraged at the insult to his daughter, allied with Pisistratus's enemies and expelled him again. Second expulsion of Pisistratus (c. 556 BC); Pisistratus later returned with mercenaries and Battle of Pallene; demonstrated Alcmaeonid ability to make and break tyrants; story became classic example of political thea

Accounts II
The Shield Signal after Marathon and Accusation of MedismHerodotus 6.115-121, 6.131

490 BC, immediately after Battle of Marathon

After the Athenian victory at Marathon, a shield was flashed as a signal from Mount Pentelicon toward the Persian fleet, allegedly to signal them to sail round Cape Sounion and capture undefended Athens. Popular rumor blamed the Alcmaeonidae, whose leader at that time was Megakles son of Hippocrates. Herodotus reports the accusation but vigorously defends the Alcmaeonids, arguing they hated tyranny more than anyone and had bribed Delphi to free Athens, and that no Alcmaeonid shield signal occurred. He notes the Alcmaeonids were in disfavor and that later ostracism of Megakles shows lingering suspicion. Lingering suspicion of treason (medism) attached to Megakles; contributed to his ostracism four years later; Herodotus's defense shaped later historiography; modern debate on whether signal was real or panic.

Ostracism and the Pythian Victory in ExileOstraca evidence; Pindar Pythian 7; Aristotle Ath. Pol. 22.5-6; Androtion FGrH 324 F 6

486 BC (ostracism), Pythian Games 486 BC

In 486 BC, Megakles son of Hippocrates, nephew of Cleisthenes, was ostracized — the second or third known ostracism after Hipparchos son of Charmos. Over 4,000 ostraca bearing his name survive, many with abusive additions: 'adulterer' (moichos), accusations of luxury, and 'Megakles, don't ...' (Berti's reading 'me eretriaze'). Despite exile, he was proclaimed victor in the four-horse chariot race at the Pythian Games at Delphi in the same year. Pindar composed Pythian 7 to honor him, praising the Alcmaeonid house and its rebuilt temple of Apollo, while lamenting envy (phthonos) that struck him. The ode was performed in Athens despite his absence. Demonstrated ostracism as weapon against elite; exile did not erase elite prestige via panhellenic games; Pindar's ode is sole surviving epinician for an ostracized man; family retained influence, later producing Pericles.

The Armor Exchange at the Battle of HeracleaPlutarch Life of Pyrrhus 16.4-17.4; Zonaras 8.3 (Cassius Dio); Justin 18.1

280 BC, Battle of Heraclea (Lucania, Italy)

Megacles of Epirus, officer and companion of King Pyrrhus, accompanied Pyrrhus on reconnaissance of the Roman camp before Heraclea. In battle against consul Publius Valerius Laevinus, Pyrrhus's conspicuous armor made him a target. Megacles persuaded the king to exchange armor, cloak, and helmet with him. Romans concentrated on Megacles, thinking him Pyrrhus, and eventually killed him. A Roman named Publius Decius Mus (according to Plutarch) slew him and paraded the armor, shouting Pyrrhus was dead, causing Epirote disarray until Pyrrhus rode bareheaded along the lines to prove he lived. Megacles' sacrifice allowed Pyrrhus to win the battle, though at heavy cost. Pyrrhus won Heraclea, first Hellenistic vs. Roman battle; Megacles became exemplum of loyalty and devotio; story paralleled Roman Decius Mus's own family tradition of self-sacrifice; contributed to 'Pyrrhic victory' narrative.

The Legendary Archon 922-892 BCEusebius Chronicon (via Jerome), Parian Marble, List of Athenian Archons (IG I3 1031)

Early Iron Age, purported 10th-9th century BC

In the reconstructed list of life-archons and decennial archons, a Megakles appears as eponymous archon for a 30-year period 922-892 BC, between Polystratos and Diognetos. No deeds are recorded; his existence serves only to fill the chronology from Codrus to Creon. Modern scholarship regards this figure as likely invented by Hellenistic chronographers to provide continuous annual eponyms where none existed. Illustrates how Athenian chronology was artificially extended; not used in political narrative but appears in Smith's Dictionary as first Megacles.

Megacles was a single man who lived from 922 to 280 BC and did everything from killing Cylonians to dying for Pyrrhus. — At least six distinct historical individuals shared the name over 600 years: a legendary early archon, two Archaic Alcmaeonids (632 and 560 BC), a Classical ostracized aristocrat (486 BC), and an unrelated Epirote officer (280 BC). Ancient sources and modern summaries often collapse them, but prosopography separates them by patronymic (son of Alcmaeon vs. son of Hippocrates) and generation.

The Alcmaeonid curse was a magical curse that made Megakles and his descendants physically ill or monstrous. — Miasma was ritual pollution (religious impurity), not a fairy-tale curse. It meant exclusion from sacrifices and political vulnerability to prosecution, requiring purification. It was a social-religious status weaponized by opponents, not a supernatural disease. Pericles was still called 'cursed' 200 years later as political smear.

Megakles son of Hippocrates was ostracized for treason (medism) proven after Marathon. — He was ostracized in 486 BC by popular vote without trial; ostraca cite wealth, luxury, and adultery, not formal treason charge. Herodotus explicitly denies Alcmaeonid medism and no trial for treason is recorded. Ostracism was preemptive exile due to suspicion and envy, not conviction.

Megacles of Epirus was an Athenian Alcmaeonid who followed Pyrrhus. — Megacles of Epirus was a Molossian/Epirote companion of Pyrrhus, unrelated to Athenian Alcmaeonidae. Homonymy only; no genealogical link attested. Athenian Megakles line ends in 5th century BC; Epirote appears 200 years later in Italy.
