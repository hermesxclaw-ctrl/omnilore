Form

Attested iconography is rare and ambiguous. Unlike Mesopotamian gods, South Arabian deities were not typically depicted in anthropomorphic form. Attar is more commonly represented symbolically: by a spear or lance, which was a common cult object, and possibly by the oryx or antelope, which appear in associated art. Some stelae may show a stylized figure, but identification is uncertain. In Ugaritic myth, Athtar is described as a 'hero' but also as physically too small for Baal's throne, a narrative detail rather than a physical description.

Powers

Granting Victory in Battle Inscriptions frequently invoke Attar to grant victory to the king and his army. He is a divine warrior who fights on behalf of his worshippers. South Arabian royal inscriptions (Sabaean, Qatabanian) His favor is conditional on proper worship and oath-keeping. He punishes those who break treaties or neglect his cult. None attested; his martial role is consistent across inscriptions.

Protection of Property and Caravans Attar is invoked as a protector of trade caravans, wells, and property. He is a guardian deity for the mercantile and agricultural interests of the South Arabian kingdoms. South Arabian dedicatory inscriptions His protection is not automatic; it requires dedicatory offerings and adherence to the law. None attested.

Punishment of Oath-Breakers and Enemies Attar is frequently called upon to curse and destroy those who violate oaths, treaties, or the sanctity of the temple. He is a divine enforcer of justice. South Arabian legal and dedicatory texts His punishment is typically invoked through curses, suggesting he acts in response to specific violations. None attested.

Healing A few inscriptions request Attar's aid in healing the sick, indicating a broader protective role beyond warfare. South Arabian votive inscriptions This is a less prominent role compared to his martial and protective functions. None attested.

Kingship (Ugaritic Athtar) In the Ugaritic Baal Cycle, Athtar is appointed as a temporary substitute king of the gods after Baal's death. However, he is too small to sit on Baal's throne, so he descends to rule the earth instead. Ugaritic Baal Cycle (KTU 1.6 I) This is a narrative about his inadequacy for the highest divine office, not a power he successfully wields. He is a 'failed' king in this specific myth. This Ugaritic episode is a significant contrast to the powerful, successful Attar of South Arabian inscriptions. It may represent a different regional tradition or a mythic explanation for his role as a terrestrial, not celestial, king.

Behavior & Habitat

Attar's role in inscriptions is primarily as a protector and a punisher. He is invoked to grant victory, to protect caravans and property, to heal the sick, and to curse oath-breakers and enemies. He is a god of action and justice, often associated with warfare and the desert. He is not a creator god in the primary evidence, but rather a powerful, active force within the divine and human worlds.

Accounts

After the god Baal is killed by Mot (Death), the supreme god El searches for a replacement. He offers the throne to Athtar, the son of Athirat. Athtar ascends to the throne of Baal, but his feet do not reach the footstool and his head does not reach the top of the throne — he is physically too small for the job. Athtar admits his inadequacy, saying 'I cannot rule on the height of Zaphon,' and descends to rule the earth instead.

There is no single narrative myth, but a continuous, repetitive ritual and political relationship. Kings and commoners alike dedicate offerings to Attar. He is invoked to protect the kingdom, to grant victory in war, to curse enemies, and to sanctify treaties. His temples are major economic and political centers.

Attar's Status in the Pantheon
Attar is the supreme and most active deity in South Arabia. ⇌ Attar is a secondary deity, a failed king, as depicted in the Ugaritic Baal Cycle.
Evidence for A: Hundreds of South Arabian inscriptions invoke Attar as the primary national god; his temples are the most prominent.
Evidence for B: The Ugaritic myth of Athtar's failed attempt to sit on Baal's throne clearly subordinates him to Baal and El.
What We Don't Know

1) The full corpus of South Arabian inscriptions is vast and not fully translated or published. 2) The specific iconography of Attar is poorly understood; there is no definitive anthropomorphic depiction. 3) The relationship between the South Arabian Attar and the Ugaritic Athtar is a subject of ongoing scholarly debate, with no final consensus on the degree of continuity. 4) The exact rituals of his cult are not fully known. 5) The role of Attar in the daily lives of common people, beyond dedicatory inscriptions, is unclear.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Attar as the patron god of the ancient Yemeni kingdoms: a fierce, no-nonsense protector, judge, and warrior, like a divine combination of Mars and a guardian angel. He wasn't a creator god; he was the god you called on when you needed victory in battle, protection for your caravan, or vengeance on an oath-breaker. His temples were like combination courthouses, banks, and fortresses. The Ugaritic version of him is like a regional variant of the same mythic figure, but in a different pantheon, where he's the ambitious younger brother who just isn't quite big enough for the top job.
