Archive Dossier — Identity

Buckriders

Pronunciation BOK-ken-RAY-ders (Dutch)

Known As

Bokkenrijders (Dutch)Chevaliers du bouc (French)Bockreiter (German)

Etymology

From Dutch 'bok' (buck/goat) and 'rijder' (rider). The name literally means 'buck riders' or 'goat riders', referring to the alleged practice of riding flying goats or bucks.

Cultural Ownership

Primarily Belgian (Flemish) and Dutch (Limburg) folklore, with ties to the historical region of Overmaas and the Prince-Bishopric of Liège.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars argue the buckriders were purely a myth created by judicial hysteria, while others maintain they were real criminal gangs who exploited the superstition. The exact nature and extent of their supernatural activities are disputed.

Archive Dossier — Approach

In the moonlit fields of Limburg, farmers whispered of riders who soared over rooftops on horned bucks, their oaths renouncing God and swearing fealty to the Devil. These were the Bokkenrijders—feared as witches, hunted as criminals, and remembered as both victims and villains of a dark chapter in European history.

When the moon is full and the wind howls across the Kempen, the Buckriders mount their flying goats and ride through the sky, their lanterns flickering like fallen stars, robbing and pillaging until dawn.

Who It Is

De Bokkenrijders (Buckriders, also known in French as Les Chevaliers du Bouc — 'Goat Knights') are legendary figures from the folklore of the Belgian and Dutch Limburg region, as well as the Kempen area. According to tradition, the Buckriders were witches or demonic bandits who made a pact with the devil. They would anoint themselves with a magical flying ointment, mount enormous flying billy goats (bokken), and ride through the night sky to attend secret sabbaths, commit crimes, and terrorize the countryside. The Buckriders were said to be organized into bands that communicated via secret signals, broke into fortified houses, robbed churches of their gold and silver vessels, and disappeared into the night on their flying goats before anyone could catch them. Their legend exploded in the 18th century when a series of sensational criminal trials (1740s–1790s) swept the region: hundreds of people — mostly poor farmers, craftsmen, and vagabonds — were accused of being Buckriders, tortured into confessions, and executed. Up to 300 people were executed in the largest witch-hunt-like persecution in the Low Countries' history, despite the official end of witch trials. Modern historians believe the Buckriders trials were a moral panic, possibly involving a genuine but greatly exaggerated criminal network, but the folkloric image of the night-riding goat-mounting witch-band has persisted as a defining legend of the region. In modern Belgian and Dutch culture, the Buckriders are celebrated in folk festivals, museums, and literature as a symbol of regional identity and a cautionary tale about mass hysteria.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

According to folklore, buckriders were ordinary humans who, through a pact with the Devil, could ride flying goats or bucks through the air. They were often depicted as menacing figures, sometimes in dark clothing, but no consistent physical description exists.

Symbols & Iconography

The buck/goat is the central symbol, associated with the Devil and witchcraft. The 'ungodly oath' and the act of flying on a buck are key iconographic elements. In modern times, the buckrider has become a symbol of regional identity in Limburg.

Behaviour & Personality

Buckriders were believed to be ruthless criminals who robbed and murdered, using their supernatural ability to travel vast distances quickly. They were also thought to be witches who had renounced God and sworn allegiance to the Devil, participating in sabbaths and devil worship.

Domains & Habitats

The buckriders were said to operate in the southern Netherlands (Limburg) and northeastern Belgium, with a yearly meeting on the Mook Heath. They raided small communities, parsonages, churches, and remote farms.

Version Differences

There are two main versions: the folkloric supernatural version (witches riding flying bucks) and the historical version (criminal gangs using the myth for intimidation). The historical version is now widely accepted, but the folkloric elements persist in cultural representations.

Archive Dossier — Powers

Flight on flying bucks

Source Version Folkloric and trial accounts

Description

The ability to ride through the air on the back of a buck provided by the Devil, enabling rapid travel over vast distances.

Limitations

Not specified in sources; the flight was believed to be a gift from the Devil, possibly limited to nighttime.

Contradictions

Some accounts describe the buck as a real animal, others as a demonic illusion. The physical feasibility was doubted by contemporaries like Gaspard de Limpens.

Pact with the Devil

Source Version Trial accounts and folklore

Description

The buckriders were believed to have renounced God and sworn allegiance to the Devil, granting them supernatural powers.

Limitations

The pact required an 'ungodly oath', which was a central element in trials. The powers were conditional on loyalty to the Devil.

Contradictions

Historians debate whether such pacts were actually made or were fabrications of torture-induced confessions.

Intimidation and terror

Source Version Historical criminal gangs

Description

The mere name 'Buckriders' was used to frighten inhabitants, allowing criminal gangs to operate with impunity.

Limitations

This was a psychological tactic, not a supernatural power.

Contradictions

None.

Judicial authorities

Type persecutorsSource Trial records

Description

Authorities accused, tortured, and executed hundreds of suspected buckriders, often on flimsy evidence.

The trials were a form of witch-hunt, with over 90% of convictions resulting in capital punishment.
