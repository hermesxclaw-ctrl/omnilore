Who It Is

The Korrigan (plural: Korriganed) is the most famous fairy/dwarf of Breton folklore — a small, supernatural being that dwells near fountains, dolmens (megalithic tombs), and in forest clearings. Despite the name's diminutive implication, Korrigans are not necessarily small: some appear as beautiful women (Korrigan in female form) with flowing golden hair who dance in circles at night, while others are gnome-like male dwarves. The female Korrigan is a dangerous seductress: she lures human men to dance with her in fairy rings (cercle des korrigans). Those who join the dance find that a single night in the ring equals years in the human world — they emerge aged and broken. The Korrigan can also grant wishes or reveal hidden treasure (often beneath dolmens), but she demands a price and is merciless to those who break their word or fail her tests. The Korrigan's power peaks at night, particularly on full moons, and they are linked to ancient megaliths (menhirs and dolmens), where they perform their rites. In some traditions, the Korrigans are the spirits of the pre-Christian druidesses, cursed to fairy form and bound to the stones they once consecrated, waiting for the 'day of deliverance' that will never come. The Korrigan represents the Breton fascination with the megalithic past — Brittany has one of the highest concentrations of prehistoric standing stones in Europe (Carnac alone has over 3,000), and the Korrigan myth provides an explanation for these ancient monuments: they are the dance floors and treasure vaults of the fairy-folk. The Korrigan also connects to wider European fairy-lore (the 'fairy ring' is pan-European), but the Breton version is distinct in its emphasis on stone monuments, fountains, and the testing of human character.
