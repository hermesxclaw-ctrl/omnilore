Who It Is

Fifty daughters, fifty beds, one lion-slayer. Eurycapys of Thespiae was not born from romance but from a king's calculated bargain — one of fifty half-brothers fathered by Heracles in the shadow of Mount Cithaeron, then sorted like lots into three destinies: to stay, to serve Thebes, or to sail with Iolaus and carve a new Heraclid world on Sardinia.

No personality, speech, or moral characterization survives for Eurycapys. The narrative role is passive: conceived, born, and dispatched by father's order. No dialogue, choice, or deed is attributed to him individually. Collective behavior of the 40 sent to Sardinia: obedience to Heracles/Iolaus, foundation of colony, eponymous or ancestral role. Disputed/hypothetical: whether Eurycapys was among the 7 retained at Thespiae, the 3 sent to Thebes, or the 40 sent to Sardinia — sources do not assign individual sons to each group, only numbers.

Heraclid Lineage Status

As son of Heracles, Eurycapys holds the social-religious status of Heraclid — claimed descent from Zeus via Heracles. This confers heroic ancestry, eligibility for Thespian aristocratic genealogy, and in Sardinian tradition, foundation-hero status. No supernatural power is individually attested.

Limit: Mortal, not deified. No apotheosis, no cult attested individually. Status is genealogical, not active power.

Colonial Founder (Potential)

If among the 40, Eurycapys participates in the Iolaid colonization of Sardinia — a mythic charter for Greek presence on Sardinia, associated with subduing barbarians, building works, and introducing Greek rites. Diodorus credits the Iolaids with building, agriculture, and hero cult on Sardinia.

Limit: No individual feat is named for Eurycapys; the foundation is collective under Iolaus. Archaeologically, no Greek colony of this date exists on Sardinia; the myth is etiological.

No Attested Supernatural Powers

No source attributes superhuman strength, shapeshifting, prophecy, or divine gifts to Eurycapys. Unlike his father, he is not described as performing labors or wielding artifacts.

Limit: Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, but no tradition fills this gap.

Accounts I
The Cithaeronian Lion HuntApollodorus Bibliotheca 2.4.9-10; Diodorus Siculus 4.29.1-2; Pausanias 9.27.6

Heroic age narrative, recorded Classical-Hellenistic (5th c. BCE - 2nd c. CE)

At age 18, Heracles is hosted by King Thespius of Thespiae while hunting the lion that ravaged Cithaeron (distinct from the Nemean lion). Thespius, eager for grandsons by the hero, entertains Heracles for an extended stay. Each night (or each night for 50 nights), Thespius sends a different daughter to Heracles' bed, with Heracles believing he lies with the same woman. Heracles slays the lion and, during the same period, impregnates all fifty daughters. Conception of 50 sons including Eurycapys; establishment of Heraclid line at Thespiae; lion's skin as early trophy prefiguring Nemean lion; narrative explains Thespian claim to Heraclid kinship.

Birth of the Fifty ThespiadsApollodorus Bibliotheca 2.4.10 (list); Tzetzes Chiliades 2.221-227; Athenaeus 13.557 citing Herodorus

Classical mythography

The 50 daughters each bear a son. Apollodorus provides the only complete surviving mother-son list: Eurycapys is born to Clytippe (Κλυτίππη). The births occur at Thespiae, presumably shortly after Heracles' departure for his Labors. The sons are raised at Thespiae under their grandfather Thespius. Creation of a cohort of Heraclid youth who will later be divided by Heracles' order; Thespian aristocracy claims descent; no individual infancy story for Eurycapys.

Heracles' Tripartite Dispersal OrderApollodorus Bibliotheca 2.7.8; Diodorus Siculus 4.29.3

Hellenistic mythography, reflecting 5th c. BCE traditions

Years later, after his Labors and before his death, Heracles sends a message to Thespius (via herald or Iolaus) to dispose of the sons: keep seven at Thespiae, send three to Thebes, and send forty with his nephew/charioteer Iolaus to found a colony on Sardinia. The order is presented as Heracles' paternal provision and as a heroic colonization charter. Institutionalizes the division; explains Thespian, Theban, and Sardinian Heraclid populations; Iolaus becomes oikist (founder) of Sardinia; Eurycapys' ultimate location depends on which lot he fell into — unattested individually.

Accounts II
The Sardinian Expedition with IolausDiodorus Siculus 4.29.3-4.30.3; Pausanias 10.17.5; Apollodorus 2.7.8; Strabo 5.2.7

Archaic foundation myth retrojected to heroic age, recorded 1st c. BCE - 2nd c. CE

Iolaus leads the forty Thespiads (presumably including Eurycapys in collective accounts) to Sardinia. There they subdue the native barbarians, settle the fertile plains, build works (Iolaia), introduce Greek customs and hero cult of Iolaus, and are later called Iolaeis or Thespiadae. Diodorus presents this as the first Greek civilizing of Sardinia; Pausanias notes Iolaus' tomb and cult on the island. Etiology for Sardinian place names (Iolaia), Greek claims to Sardinia, hero cult of Iolaus; later Sardinian tribes claimed descent; no individual deeds for Eurycapys recorded within the expedition.

The Thespian Retention and Theban ContingentDiodorus 4.29.3; Pausanias 9.27.7; IG VII 1735 (Thespian inscriptions referencing Heraclid gene)

Classical to Hellenistic

Seven sons remain at Thespiae to perpetuate the royal house and priesthoods; three are sent to Thebes where they are integrated into Theban aristocracy (Thebes being Heracles' birthplace). This explains why Thespiae and Thebes both claimed Heraclid ancestry despite political rivalry. Eurycapys could belong to either group, but no source specifies. Thespiae maintains Heraclid legitimacy; Thebes absorbs Heraclids into its own elite; provides genealogical bridge between Boeotian poleis.

Variant Chronology: One Night, Seven Nights, or Fifty DaysAthenaeus 13.556F citing Herodorus; Apollodorus 2.4.10 scholia; Tzetzes 2.224

5th c. BCE (Herodorus) to 12th c. CE (Tzetzes)

Mythographers debated how Heracles could father 50 children so quickly. Herodorus rationalizes as one night (emphasizing Heracles' virility, with Thespius intoxicating him). Others extend to seven nights or fifty nights/days to make it plausible and to align with the 50-day lion hunt. The variant affects Eurycapys' conception narrative: was Clytippe visited on night 1 or night 50, sober or drunk? Illustrates ancient rationalization and moral framing; influences modern retellings' tone (marvel vs. sordid). No change to Eurycapys' genealogy, only to narrative plausibility.

Eurycapys was a famous hero who performed labors like his father. — No labor, deed, or individual myth is attested for Eurycapys. He is exclusively a genealogical figure in the collective list of 50 Thespiads. All 'feats' belong to Heracles or collectively to Iolaus' expedition.

All 50 sons went to Sardinia and founded the island's civilization. — Sources explicitly divide the sons: only 40 went to Sardinia; 7 stayed at Thespiae and 3 went to Thebes. Eurycapys' destination is not individually specified, and the Sardinian colony is a mythic charter, not a historical mass migration.

The Thespian episode involved consensual romance between Heracles and 50 willing princesses. — Ancient sources describe Thespius orchestrating the unions, sending daughters to Heracles' bed without his clear knowledge (and in Herodorus, while intoxicated). The daughters are voiceless parthenoi under paternal authority; one tradition says one daughter refused and was punished with perpetual virginity as a priestess. Consent is not discussed in sources, and power imbalance is central.

Eurycapys' mother was Megara, Heracles' wife. — Megara, daughter of Creon of Thebes, was Heracles' first wife and mother of different children (the Heracleidae killed by Heracles in madness). Eurycapys' mother is Clytippe, daughter of Thespius, per Apollodorus 2.4.10. The two genealogies are distinct.

The Cithaeronian lion is the same as the Nemean lion. — The Cithaeronian lion was an earlier, separate lion ravaging Mount Cithaeron near Thespiae, slain by young Heracles before the Twelve Labors. The Nemean lion, whose skin becomes Heracles' attribute, was slain later as Labor 1 near Nemea and was invulnerable. Sources distinguish them.
