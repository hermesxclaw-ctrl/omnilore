Who It Is

You're walking home along the river at twilight. Your grandmother always told you to be home before dark. You laughed at her. You're not laughing now. From the riverbank comes a sound that makes your heart stop: a woman weeping. Wailing. '¡Ay, mis hijos! ¡Ay, mis hijos!' — Oh, my children! Oh, my children! The sound is getting closer. You can see her now: a figure in white, impossibly thin, face hidden by a veil of wet black hair. She stops. She turns toward you. And then she asks the question your grandmother warned you about: '¿Dónde están mis hijos?' Where are my children? The river is right behind you. You can't remember if it was always that close.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — 'La Llorona' is Spanish for 'The Weeping Woman' or 'The Crier.' The name derives from the verb 'llorar' (to cry/weep), with the affectionate/diminutive feminine suffix '-ona' adding menace rather than affection. It is one of the most widely recognized ghost names in the Spanish-speaking world.

Aliases — The Weeping Woman, La Mujer del Río, The Wailing Woman, La Llorona del Río, The River Woman, The Crying Woman

Form & Signs

Appearance — La Llorona is almost universally described as appearing as a young woman in a white, flowing gown (or white veil), often drenched in water, with long, dark, wet hair and a pale, gaunt face. In some versions she is seen gliding over water, in others her feet never touch the ground. Some traditions describe her as impossibly thin or skeletal, and her signature is a piercing, high-pitched wail that sounds like a woman sobbing and calling out for her

Accounts I
The Colonial Drowned Woman (16th-century origin)Mexican colonial folklore, recorded in the folklore of Mexico City

Reported apparition circa 1550, colonial era

The earliest recorded account of La Llorona is set in Mexico City during the colonial period. A beautiful indigenous or mestiza woman was seduced and abandoned by a wealthy Spanish nobleman, and as a result of her shame and grief she drowned her children in the canal or river. Stricken with guilt, she then drowned herself. After death she could not find rest, and her ghost walks the waterways of the city weeping and calling for her sons. Her apparition was reported near the canals of the old city, and some accounts link the sightings to a named woman who actually drowned her children — a tragic figure who became legend.

Accounts II
The Unfaithful Wife and the RiverPan-Latin-American oral tradition; folkloric variants across Mexico and Central America

Oral tradition, documented from the 19th century onward

This is the most common folk version. A young woman from a poor family falls in love with and marries a wealthy man, and they have two or three children. The husband grows distant and takes another lover (or a higher social position), and in a fit of jealousy and rage the woman grabs her children and throws them into the river. She immediately regrets it and tries to save them but cannot. Drowned in grief, she spends eternity weeping along the riverbank, calling out to her children, and in some versions dragging careless wanderers into the water in her madness.

The Modern Bordertown HauntingContemporary oral legend, American Southwest and Mexico

20th-21st century

In the modern American Southwest, La Llorona's legend persists as an urban legend told to keep children safer. Parents warn their kids that if they stay out late or go near the irrigation ditches at night, La Llorona will come for them. Numerous reported sightings along rivers and ditches in places like the Rio Grande valley describe a woman in white wailing at the water's edge. The story has also absorbed contemporary anxieties — some versions tie her to the immigrant experience, a mother whose children are lost, making her a figure of both terror and tragic sympathy.
