The Approach

Before the age of scientific taxonomy, the oceans were crowded with beasts that blurred the line between the real and the imagined. Among them lurked the Sea Swine—a creature that may have been a porpoise, a fish that grunted like a pig, or a four-footed, one-eyed monster that swam the icy waters south of Iceland, depending on whom you asked and when.

Identity

Sea Swine

Pronunciation See-Swine (English); Por-cus Ma-ri-nus (Latin)

Known As

Porcus MarinusSea Hog

Etymology

Latin 'porcus' (pig/swine) + 'marinus' (of the sea). The name likely derives from perceived similarities to pigs, either in body shape or sound.

Cultural Ownership

Western European (Greek, Scandinavian, British) maritime folklore and early natural history

Disputed Identifications

Scholars dispute whether the name referred to a porpoise (due to round body shape) or to a fish that grunted like a pig. The Carta marina depiction is clearly fantastical, distinct from any real animal.

Description

Appearance

Descriptions vary: some accounts describe a creature 'headed like a Hog, toothed, and tusked like a Boar'; the Carta marina shows a four-footed beast with dragon's feet and a single eye at the navel.

Symbols & Iconography

In the Carta marina, the sea swine is depicted as a fearsome sea monster, symbolizing the unknown dangers of northern seas.

Behaviour & Personality

Reported to travel in packs of hundreds, suggesting a social or migratory behavior.

Domains & Habitats

Waters south of Iceland, and generally the North Atlantic.

Version Differences

Ancient Greek sources likely refer to a real animal (porpoise), while 16th-century cartographic depictions are fantastical. 16th-17th century textual accounts describe a boar-like creature, possibly conflating multiple animals.

Sea Hog reports

Source Various 16th-17th century accountsCulture Northern EuropeanPeriod 16th-17th century

Summary

Travelers reported sea hogs 'headed like a Hog, toothed, and tusked like a Boar', traveling in packs of hundreds.

Sea hogs

Consequences

Contributed to the belief in a distinct creature separate from ordinary fish.

Contradictions & Disputes

Identity of the sea swine

Position A

The sea swine is a porpoise, due to similar body shape.

Position A — evidence

Aristotle's description of porpoise body shape; John Ray's explicit identification.

Position B

The sea swine is a fish that grunts like a pig.

Position B — evidence

Classical scholars' interpretation of 'porcus' as referring to sound.

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus; the term likely referred to different animals at different times.

Fantastical depiction in Carta marina

Position A

The Carta marina depicts a mythical creature, not a real animal.

Position A — evidence

The creature has four dragon's feet and a single eye, impossible for a real animal.

Position B

The depiction may be a stylized representation of a real animal (e.g., porpoise).

Position B — evidence

Maps of the era often embellished real animals.

Scholarly Consensus

The Carta marina depiction is considered fantastical.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

The exact original Greek term and its precise context are not verified. The Carta marina woodcut image itself was not directly examined; only descriptions. Further research is needed into primary sources for 16th-17th century accounts. The dispute among classical scholars is not fully documented. Modern scholarship on this specific term is limited.

Research Scope

This research covers the historical and mythological concept of the 'Sea Swine' (Porcus Marinus) from ancient Greece through the 17th century, focusing on textual and cartographic sources. It does not cover modern media adaptations beyond historical references.
