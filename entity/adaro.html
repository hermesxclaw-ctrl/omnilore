Etymology

The term 'adaro' is from the languages of Makira (San Cristobal) in the Solomon Islands. Its precise linguistic roots are not well-documented; it may relate to words for 'spirit' or 'ghost' in local languages.

Cultural Ownership

Indigenous peoples of Makira (San Cristobal), Solomon Islands, specifically the Arosi and other language groups.

Disputed Identifications

Some early European accounts conflated Adaro with other sea spirits or ghosts. There is no scholarly consensus on whether Adaro are a distinct class or part of a broader category of nature spirits.

Who It Is

The adaro is the malignant half of a Solomon Islands theory of the soul made into a sea monster. In the belief of Makira (San Cristobal), a person has two spiritual parts: the aunga, the good and proper soul, which departs rightly at death — and the adaro, the wild residue, the appetite and anger, which does not. Freed of its person, the adaro takes to the sea as a man-fish: human-shaped but with gills behind its ears, tail-fins for feet, a horn like a shark's dorsal fin, a pike growing from its head like a swordfish's bill — a body assembled entirely from the dangerous parts of the ocean's fauna. Adaro travel in style: they ride waterspouts and slide along rainbows, and they cross the sea on the sun's path, the glare-road of light on the water where no fisherman can look directly. Their weapon is the flying fish: an adaro hurls them like javelins, and a man struck by a flying fish — a real hazard of Pacific night-fishing, where fish drawn to torches genuinely leap into canoes and faces — is knocked senseless or killed unless the proper offerings restore him. Adaro appear in dreams demanding tribute, teach songs and dances to those they favor, and are placated with offerings thrown into the sea at the places they own. The adaro is what remains of a person when you subtract everything worth keeping — and the Solomon Islanders, sensibly, gave it the open ocean and right of way on the light.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

Adaro are described as human-like but with fish-like features: they have fins instead of feet, gills at their ears, and a tail. They are said to be able to breathe underwater and on land. Some accounts describe them as having a shark-like appearance or being able to transform into fish.

Symbols & Iconography

No known traditional iconography. In modern contexts, they may be depicted in art or media as mermaid-like figures.

Behaviour & Personality

Adaro are malevolent spirits that seek to harm humans, particularly by causing drowning or by stealing their souls. They are said to be active during the day, especially when the sun is high, and they ride on the sun's rays, which is why they are called 'Sun-Path Riders'. They are cunning and dangerous, but can be driven away by certain charms or rituals.

Domains & Habitats

They inhabit the sea and coastal areas around Makira, especially reefs, lagoons, and deep water. They are associated with the sun's path on the water, and may travel along sunbeams.

Version Differences

Early accounts by Codrington and Ivens are similar, but later retellings may vary in details. Some versions may describe them as more fish-like, others more human. There is no single canonical version.

Origins

Makiran dual-soul doctrine recorded by the ethnographers Codrington and Fox; the adaro is the after-death career of the soul's unruly half, promoted from psychology to sea-hazard.

Attested in Solomon Islands oral tradition (Codrington, Fox ethnographies)Era Precolonial to present

Archive Dossier — Powers

Soul Theft

Source Version Codrington (1891), Ivens (1927)

Description

Adaro can steal the soul (or life-force) of a person, causing illness or death. This is often done by touching the person or by capturing their reflection in the water.

Limitations

The soul may be recovered by a shaman or through rituals.

Contradictions

None recorded.

Causing Drowning

Source Version Codrington (1891)

Description

Adaro are said to pull swimmers or canoeists underwater, causing them to drown.

Limitations

They may be warded off by charms or by avoiding the sea at certain times.

Contradictions

None recorded.

Shape-shifting

Source Version Ivens (1927)

Description

Some accounts suggest Adaro can transform into fish or other sea creatures, or appear as humans.

Limitations

Not consistently attested.

Contradictions

Codrington does not mention this; it may be a later embellishment.

Riding Sunbeams

Source Version Codrington (1891)

Description

Adaro are said to travel along sunbeams, which is why they are active during the day.

Limitations

None recorded.

Contradictions

None recorded.

Form

A man of the sea with gills behind the ears, fish-tail feet, a dorsal horn and a swordfish pike on the head — glimpsed in the glare of the sun's road or the spiral of a waterspout.

Powers

Waterspout-riding, rainbow-travel, flying-fish artillery, dream-extortion, song-teaching — and the ownership of specific sea-marks where tribute is owed.

Weaknesses & Wards

Offerings satisfy and bind it; its aggression follows its person's old grudges, so a life without enemies leaves a weaker adaro; and it is a creature of glare and spray, powerless in the calm dark.

Soul theft and death

Source Codrington, Ivens

Description

Adaro are believed to steal souls, causing illness and death. This reflects real fears of drowning and the dangers of the sea in island communities.

Scholarly Context

Soul theft is a common motif in Melanesian belief systems, often used to explain disease and death.

Archive Dossier — Stories

The Adaro and the Fisherman

Source Recorded by Walter Ivens, likely from Arosi informantsCulture Arosi people, MakiraPeriod Early 20th century

Summary

A fisherman is out on his canoe when he sees an Adaro swimming alongside. The Adaro tries to capsize the canoe, but the fisherman uses a charm (a piece of ginger or a special leaf) to drive it away. The Adaro disappears, and the fisherman returns safely.

Participants

Fisherman

Adaro

Consequences

The fisherman survives, and the story serves as a warning about the dangers of the sea and the efficacy of charms.

Variants

Similar stories exist across Melanesia about sea spirits trying to harm fishermen.

The Adaro and the Woman

Source Recorded by R.H. Codrington, possibly from San Cristobal informantsCulture Makira peoplePeriod Late 19th century

Summary

A woman goes to the shore to wash clothes. An Adaro rises from the water and touches her shadow, stealing her soul. She falls ill and dies. The community performs a ritual to recover her soul, but it is too late.

Participants

Woman

Adaro

Shaman

Consequences

The woman dies, illustrating the deadly power of Adaro and the importance of protective rituals.

Variants

In some versions, the soul is recovered if the ritual is performed quickly.

Archive Dossier — Archivist

Known Gaps

Limited primary sources; no access to original oral texts in Arosi. No archaeological or iconographic evidence. Modern reception poorly documented. Further fieldwork needed to capture contemporary beliefs.

Research Scope

This dossier synthesizes available ethnographic and mythological sources on the Adaro of the Solomon Islands, primarily from the late 19th and early 20th century accounts. It does not claim exhaustive coverage of all oral variants.

Cultural Significance

The adaro renders a whole psychology of the divided self into marine hazard-lore, and its flying-fish attacks encode a genuine occupational danger of torch-fishing the Pacific night.
