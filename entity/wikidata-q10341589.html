Who It Is

In the shadowed valleys of ancient Izumo, a serpent of impossible size—eight heads and eight tails—slithers through myth and memory. Its eyes glow like ripe cherries, its belly oozes blood, and from its severed tail emerges a blade that would become the symbol of Japan's imperial power. This is Yamata no Orochi, the eight-forked dragon whose defeat by the storm god Susanoo echoes through millennia, yet whose story is far more complex than a simple monster-slaying tale.

In the myth, it is a destructive entity that annually devours maidens. It is susceptible to sake, becoming drunk and sleeping. It is not portrayed with a distinct personality beyond its monstrous appetite.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name combines 'yamata' (八岐/八俣) meaning 'eight forks/branches' and 'orochi' (大蛇) meaning 'large snake' or 'dragon'. 'Orochi' derives from Old Japanese 'woröti', with debated origins possibly related to 'oro' (tail), 'ō' (big), or dialectal 'oro' (peak), plus 'chi' (spirit), cognate with 'mizuchi'

Pronunciation — Yah-mah-tah noh oh-roh-chee

Aliases — Yamata-no-Orochi, Yamato no Orochi, Koshi no Yamata no Orochi, Eight-Forked Serpent, Eight-Headed Dragon, Eight-Headed Serpent

Ownership — Japanese

Form & Signs

Appearance — In the Kojiki, the serpent is described with red eyes like ground cherries (akakagachi), eight heads and eight tails, with moss, cypress, and cedar growing on its body. It spans eight valleys and eight peaks, and its belly is bloody and inflamed. The Nihon Shoki similarly describes eight-forked head and tail, red eyes, and firs and cypresses on its back.

Symbols — Often depicted in art as a massive serpent with multiple heads and tails, sometimes with vegetation on its body. In modern popular culture, it is a common motif in Japanese media, symbolizing chaos and natural disaster.

Domains — Originates from the Koshi region (northern Japan), appears at the River Hi in Izumo. Associated with water and mountains.

Accounts I
The Slaying of Yamata no OrochiKojiki

Mythological (recorded 712 CE)

Susanoo, exiled from heaven, descends to Izumo and meets an old couple and their daughter Kushinada-hime. They tell him that the eight-headed serpent has devoured seven of their eight daughters and will soon take the last. Susanoo agrees to slay the serpent in exchange for Kushinada-hime's hand. He transforms her into a comb, instructs the couple to brew eight-fold sake and place it in eight vats behind eight gates. The serpent drinks, falls asleep, and Susanoo chops it to pieces. In the tail, he finds a magnificent sword, which he presents to Amaterasu. The sword becomes Kusanagi-no-Tsurugi, one of the Imperial Regalia. Susanoo marries Kushinada-hime and establishes his lineage.

Accounts II
Variants in the Nihon ShokiNihon Shoki

Recorded 720 CE

Version One: Similar to Kojiki but with different names for the couple (Ashinazuchi and Tenazuchi) and the daughter (Kushiinada-hime). Version Three: Susanoo meets a god Ashinazu-Tenazu and his pregnant wife Inada-no-Miyanushi-Susano-Yatsumimi. The serpent comes to devour the child, but Susanoo offers it sake, it sleeps, and he kills it. The sword is found in the tail. The sword is venerated at Atsuta Shrine.

Yamata no Orochi is a dragon in the Western sense. — It is a serpent, not a dragon with wings or fire-breathing.

The serpent had exactly eight heads and eight tails, no more. — The number eight may mean 'many' in ancient Japanese, so it could imply numerous heads and tails.

The sword Kusanagi was always known by that name. — It was originally called Ama-no-Murakumo-no-Tsurugi, later renamed Kusanagi.
