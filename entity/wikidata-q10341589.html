The Approach

In the shadowed valleys of ancient Izumo, a serpent of impossible size—eight heads and eight tails—slithers through myth and memory. Its eyes glow like ripe cherries, its belly oozes blood, and from its severed tail emerges a blade that would become the symbol of Japan's imperial power. This is Yamata no Orochi, the eight-forked dragon whose defeat by the storm god Susanoo echoes through millennia, yet whose story is far more complex than a simple monster-slaying tale.

Content advisory. Contains descriptions of violence, including the devouring of maidens and the dismemberment of the serpent. No graphic sexual content.
Identity

Yamata no Orochi

Pronunciation Yah-mah-tah noh oh-roh-chee

Etymology

The name combines 'yamata' (八岐/八俣) meaning 'eight forks/branches' and 'orochi' (大蛇) meaning 'large snake' or 'dragon'. 'Orochi' derives from Old Japanese 'woröti', with debated origins possibly related to 'oro' (tail), 'ō' (big), or dialectal 'oro' (peak), plus 'chi' (spirit), cognate with 'mizuchi' and 'ikazuchi'. 'Yamata' may relate to 'yatsu' (eight) used to mean 'many'.

Cultural Ownership

Japanese

Disputed Identifications

Some local traditions identify Yamata no Orochi with the deity Ibuki Daimyōjin at Mount Ibuki, and certain figures like Emperor Antoku and Longnü have been claimed as incarnations. These are non-canonical and regionally specific.

Description

Appearance

In the Kojiki, the serpent is described with red eyes like ground cherries (akakagachi), eight heads and eight tails, with moss, cypress, and cedar growing on its body. It spans eight valleys and eight peaks, and its belly is bloody and inflamed. The Nihon Shoki similarly describes eight-forked head and tail, red eyes, and firs and cypresses on its back.

Symbols & Iconography

Often depicted in art as a massive serpent with multiple heads and tails, sometimes with vegetation on its body. In modern popular culture, it is a common motif in Japanese media, symbolizing chaos and natural disaster.

Behaviour & Personality

In the myth, it is a destructive entity that annually devours maidens. It is susceptible to sake, becoming drunk and sleeping. It is not portrayed with a distinct personality beyond its monstrous appetite.

Domains & Habitats

Originates from the Koshi region (northern Japan), appears at the River Hi in Izumo. Associated with water and mountains.

Version Differences

The Kojiki provides the most detailed account, while the Nihon Shoki offers multiple variants with differences in location (River Hi vs. River E), the couple's names, and the method of killing (directly slaying vs. using sake to intoxicate). The sword's name also varies: Kusanagi-no-Tachi vs. Kusanagi-no-Tsurugi, with an alternative name Ama-no-Murakumo-no-Tsurugi.

Stories & Myths

The Slaying of Yamata no Orochi

Source KojikiCulture JapanesePeriod Mythological (recorded 712 CE)

Summary

Susanoo, exiled from heaven, descends to Izumo and meets an old couple and their daughter Kushinada-hime. They tell him that the eight-headed serpent has devoured seven of their eight daughters and will soon take the last. Susanoo agrees to slay the serpent in exchange for Kushinada-hime's hand. He transforms her into a comb, instructs the couple to brew eight-fold sake and place it in eight vats behind eight gates. The serpent drinks, falls asleep, and Susanoo chops it to pieces. In the tail, he finds a magnificent sword, which he presents to Amaterasu.

Consequences

The sword becomes Kusanagi-no-Tsurugi, one of the Imperial Regalia. Susanoo marries Kushinada-hime and establishes his lineage.

Variants

Nihon Shoki has multiple versions with different locations and details.

Variants in the Nihon Shoki

Source Nihon ShokiCulture JapanesePeriod Recorded 720 CE

Summary

Version One: Similar to Kojiki but with different names for the couple (Ashinazuchi and Tenazuchi) and the daughter (Kushiinada-hime). Version Three: Susanoo meets a god Ashinazu-Tenazu and his pregnant wife Inada-no-Miyanushi-Susano-Yatsumimi. The serpent comes to devour the child, but Susanoo offers it sake, it sleeps, and he kills it. The sword is found in the tail.
