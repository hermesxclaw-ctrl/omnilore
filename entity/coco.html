Coco

El Coco, The Bogeyman of Iberia, Sack Man

Who It Is
Form

The Coco is typically described as a shapeless, shadowy figure that lurks in the dark, often taking the form of a large sack or a dark mass with glowing eyes. In traditional depictions, it is a monstrous, hairy creature with a wide mouth and long claws, sometimes shown with a sack to carry away disobedient children. The coconut's three dark spots are often used as a visual metaphor for the Coco's face, with two eyes and a gaping mouth. In modern illustrations, it may appear as a more humanoid figure wearing a dark cloak, but always retains a menacing, indistinct quality that makes it adaptable to various fears. The Coco is rarely described in detail, which adds to its terror; it is the unseen threat that parents invoke to keep children in line.

Powers

Shapeshifting The Coco is said to be able to change its form, appearing as a shadow, an animal, or a humanoid figure, allowing it to adapt to the fears of its victims. This ability makes it difficult to identify and adds to its terrifying nature.

Invisibility The Coco can render itself invisible, lurking unseen in the dark until it chooses to reveal itself, often only as a pair of glowing eyes or a shadowy silhouette.

Supernatural Strength The Coco possesses immense physical strength, enabling it to carry away children in its sack with ease, breaking through doors or windows if necessary.

Night Omniscience The Coco is believed to know when children are misbehaving or refusing to sleep, even in the dark, suggesting a supernatural awareness of its potential victims' actions.

Accounts

A mother sings to her crying child, warning that if the child does not sleep, the Coco will come and take him away. The lullaby describes the Coco as a dark figure that lurks in the night, ready to snatch disobedient children. The mother's song is both a comfort and a threat, using the Coco as a tool to ensure the child's compliance.

In a small village, children are warned not to play near the woods at dusk, for the Sack Man (a local name for the Coco) roams there, carrying a large burlap sack. One evening, a boy named Pedro ignores the warning and ventures into the woods. He sees a tall, shadowy figure with a sack, and in fear, he runs back home, but the figure chases him. Pedro barely escapes, and from that day on, he never disobeys his parents again. The story serves as a cautionary tale about the dangers of disobedience and the unknown.

In Lobato's children's book series, the Cuca is depicted as an old, ugly witch with claws and a crocodile-like appearance, who sleeps upside down. She is a recurring antagonist who tries to capture the children of the Sítio, but is often outwitted by them and their magical friends. The Cuca represents the traditional Portuguese bogeyman adapted to Brazilian folklore, blending indigenous and African elements.

Modern Interpretations

Film Coco (2017) Pixar Animation Studios 2017 Inspiration for the character of 'Mamá Coco' and the Land of the Dead While the film 'Coco' is not directly about the bogeyman, it draws on Mexican folklore and the Day of the Dead, and its title is a nod to the cultural significance of the name. The film's portrayal of death and memory has been praised for its cultural sensitivity, though it does not feature the traditional Coco monster. Literature Sítio do Picapau Amarelo Monteiro Lobato 1920 Featured the Cuca as a recurring antagonist Lobato's work introduced the Cuca to a wide audience in Brazil, transforming her from a feared bogeyman into a more comical character. This adaptation has shaped modern Brazilian perceptions of the Cuca. Television El Chavo del Ocho Roberto Gómez Bolaños 1972 Mentioned in episodes as a threat to children The popular Mexican sitcom referenced the Coco (Cucuy) in several episodes, reinforcing its presence in contemporary Latin American culture as a humorous but recognizable figure.

What We Don't Know

There is limited scholarly research on the Coco's origins, with most information coming from oral traditions and popular culture. The figure's evolution in non-Iberian contexts, such as the Philippines or Equatorial Guinea, is under-documented. Additionally, the psychological impact of using the Coco as a disciplinary tool has not been extensively studied. Future research could explore regional variations in more depth and examine the Coco's role in contemporary digital media.
