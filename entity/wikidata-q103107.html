Who It Is

In the glittering court of Olympus, where Zeus's many loves were often mere footnotes, Leto stood apart—a Titan's daughter whom the queen of heaven herself hunted across the world. While Hera ignored the other goddesses who shared her husband's bed, she pursued Leto with relentless fury, driving the pregnant mother from land to land until even the waves refused her. Yet from this persecution emerged the radiant twins Apollo and Artemis, whose birth would transform a floating island into a sacred heart of the Greek world. Leto's story is one of endurance, maternal vengeance, and the quiet power of a goddess who, though often overshadowed by her children, shaped the very landscape of myth.

Hesiod describes her as 'always mild, kind to men and to the deathless gods, mild from the beginning,' the gentlest goddess in Olympus. Plato associates her name with 'willing' (to help) and 'mild'. However, she is also capable of fierce vengeance when insulted, as seen in the myths of Niobe and the Lycian peasants.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Leto' (Attic Greek: Λητώ) is of uncertain origin. Ancient etymologies linked it to λήθη (lēthē, 'forgetfulness') and λωτός (lotus), suggesting 'the hidden one'. Pronunciation — Ancient Greek: [lεːˈtɔː] (Attic), [laːtɔ̌ː] (Doric). English: /ˈliːtoʊ/.

Aliases — Latona (Roman), Lato (Doric), Letun (Etruscan), Leto (Attic)

Ownership — Primarily Greek, with strong Anatolian (Lycian) connections. Her cult was centered in Lycia, Delos, and Kos. The Romans adopted her as Latona.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Hesiod describes Leto as 'dark-gowned' (κυανόπεπλος). The Orphic Hymn 35 calls her 'dark-veiled'. The Homeric Hymn to Apollo mentions her golden hair. She is typically depicted as a matronly, veiled goddess, often holding a veil or accompanied by her children.

Symbols — In art, Leto is often shown with a veil, sometimes holding a torch or a branch. She is frequently depicted with Apollo and Artemis, especially on Delos. In some representations, she is shown with a spindle or a distaff, symbolizing her domestic role. Her sacred animals include the quail (associated with Ortygia) and the wolf (in Lycia).

Domains — Leto is a kourotrophic goddess of motherhood and childbirth. She resides on Olympus among the gods, but her cult centers are Delos, Lycia (especially the Letoon), and Kos. She is also associated with Hyperborea in some sources.

Accounts I
The Birth of Apollo and ArtemisHomeric Hymn 3; Pindar; later mythographers

Archaic-Classical

Zeus impregnates Leto. Hera, jealous, forbids any land to accept her. Leto wanders until she reaches Delos, a floating island not rooted to the earth, thus not 'land'. There she labors for nine days because Eileithyia, goddess of childbirth, is detained by Hera. Finally, with the help of the other goddesses and the arrival of Eileithyia, she gives birth to Apollo and Artemis (in later versions, twins). Apollo and Artemis slay Python and Tityos who harassed Leto. Delos becomes sacred. Leto is honored as kourotrophos.

Accounts II
The Lycian PeasantsOvid, Metamorphoses 5; Antoninus Liberalis

Hellenistic/Roman

While wandering with her infant twins, Leto tries to drink from a fountain in Lycia. Peasants refuse her and stir up mud. In punishment, she transforms them into frogs, forever bound to the water. The peasants become frogs; the story explains the origin of frogs in the region.

NiobeHomer, Iliad 24; Ovid, Metamorphoses 6; Aeschylus (lost)

Archaic-Classical

Niobe, queen of Thebes, boasts that she is a better mother than Leto because she has seven sons and seven daughters, while Leto has only two. Leto, insulted, calls upon her children. Apollo kills the sons, Artemis kills the daughters. Niobe, turned to stone, weeps eternally. Niobe's children die; she becomes a rock on Mount Sipylus. The story serves as a warning against hubris.

Leto was a minor goddess. — She was widely worshipped, especially in Lycia and Delos, and held a prominent place on Olympus.
