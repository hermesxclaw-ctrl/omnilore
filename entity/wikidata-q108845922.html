Who It Is

He is small, black, and brutally honest — the cat who rides a broom but never learns to fly, who can talk only to one witch and chooses to spend most of that gift complaining. Jiji, the familiar from Kiki's Delivery Service, is not a magical powerhouse or a cute sidekick who exists to sell plush toys, though he does that too. He is a barometer for growing up: chatty when childhood is loud, silent when it begins to recede.

Attested personality differs by version but core traits are consistent: loquacious, sardonic, cautious, and loyal. In both novel and film he is a worrier and a realist who questions Kiki's impulsive decisions. Film-Jiji (Rei Sakuma / Phil Hartman) is wry, deadpan, and openly sarcastic — his humor is the film's primary comic relief (e.g., 'I make a great padding for your broom'). Powers
Human Speech (Witch-Familiar Communication)

Ability to speak in human language (Japanese) and be understood exclusively by Kiki (and later, in novels, by his own mate and kittens). To all other humans he sounds like ordinary meowing. This is presented not as universal cat speech but as a witch-familiar bond.

Limit: Limited to Kiki as interlocutor in film; cannot communicate with other humans. In film, ability is lost after Kiki regains her flying powers, implying it was contingent on Kiki's magical/psychological state. No evidence of literacy, spellcasting, or understanding other languages beyond Japanese. Cannot speak while Kiki is magically depleted.

Enhanced Sapience and Sarcasm

Human-level intelligence, memory, and verbal wit. Provides navigational advice, moral commentary, and risk assessment. Functions as Kiki's conscience and comic foil.

Limit: Intelligence does not extend to magic, flying, or human social navigation; often wrong about human customs. Physically still a cat — cannot open doors, carry parcels, or defend Kiki beyond hissing.

Broom Passenger / Aerial Endurance

Can ride on Kiki's broom for extended flights without falling, clinging to the bristles or Kiki's shoulder. No independent flight.

Limit: Entirely dependent on Kiki's flying ability. When Kiki loses power (mid-film), Jiji cannot fly and must walk. Vulnerable to wind, rain, and dogs during flight. No magical propulsion of his own.

Feline Agility and Sensory Acuity

Standard cat abilities: night vision, acute hearing, balance, stealth, and landing on feet. Used for comic beats (sneaking bread, startling at dogs).

Limit: Realistic cat limits — cannot fight dogs, cannot survive long falls unaided, frightened by traffic and crowds in Koriko. No supernatural strength or nine lives depicted.

Silence as Narrative Power (Film-Only Loss)

In Miyazaki's ending, Jiji's shift from speech to meow signals Kiki's maturation and reintegration. The loss itself functions as a thematic ability — marking the transition from childhood dependency to adult self-reliance.

Limit: Irreversible within film continuity; Jiji does not regain speech in any canonical film sequel (none exists). Interpretive — not a diegetic power Jiji controls.

Accounts I
Departure at ThirteenKadono Eiko, Majo no Takkyūbin Vol.1 Chapter 1 (1985); Miyazaki film opening sequence (1989) 0:00-12:00

1985 (novel), 1989 (film)

On a clear full-moon night, 13-year-old witch Kiki must leave home for a year of independent training, as is witch custom. Jiji, her black cat since kittenhood, insists on accompanying her despite his preference for staying home. They launch from Kiki's parents' house on Kiki's mother's broom, with Jiji clinging to the front. In the film, the departure is exuberant and scored by Joe Hisaishi; Kiki's mother Kokiri and father Okino see them off while friends cheer. Jiji complains about the cold and Kiki's wobbly flying, establishing their dynamic. Establishes the witch-familiar bond and the coming-of-age premise. Jiji's decision to leave foreshadows his later independence. In film, this sequence introduces Koriko's world and Jiji's role as audience surrogate.

Crash Landing at Gütiokipänja BakeryKadono Vol.1 Chapter 2; Miyazaki film 12:00-28:00

1985/1989

After a night of rough flying and a near-collision with a blimp, Kiki and Jiji descend into the coastal city of Koriko. They are initially rejected and have nowhere to stay. Caught in rain, they take shelter at Gütiokipänja Bakery. Baker Osono, pregnant and kind, offers Kiki the attic room in exchange for help. Jiji is initially suspicious of the bakery's warmth and of Osono's husband Fukuo, but quickly claims the attic's sunny spot. He pretends to be a stuffed toy when customers coo over him, a running gag. Secures Kiki's base for her delivery business. Jiji's 'stuffed animal' performance becomes iconic visual and merchandise motif. Establishes domestic habitat for remainder of story.

The Stuffed-Cat Delivery and the Dog EncounterMiyazaki film 32:00-45:00; Kadono Vol.1 Chapter 4 'Kiki's First Job'

1989

Kiki's first delivery is a black cat plush that looks exactly like Jiji, for a boy named Jeff. Jiji is forced to play the toy while the real plush is lost, enduring being hugged and nearly dog-attacked. A large dog sniffs and barks at Jiji-toy, terrifying him. Later, Jiji is rescued and reunited, complaining bitterly about being mistaken for merchandise. In the novel, the episode is less perilous and Jiji volunteers more willingly. First test of Kiki's professionalism; Jiji's willingness to be humiliated for Kiki cements loyalty. Popularizes the 'two Jijis' visual (real cat vs plush) used in all subsequent art books.

Accounts II
Loss of Flight and Loss of VoiceMiyazaki film 58:00-78:00; Kadono Vol.1 Chapter 7-8 (different resolution)

1989

After a series of social humiliations — a snide customer, Tombo's friends mocking her, and exhaustion — Kiki loses her ability to fly and, simultaneously, her ability to understand Jiji. Jiji now meows normally. Kiki panics and visits Ursula the artist for counsel. Jiji, now silent, watches Kiki with concerned feline eyes but cannot advise her. Kiki's broom no longer responds. This is the film's central crisis, linking magical ability to self-confidence. Forces Kiki to confront burnout and identity beyond magic. Jiji's silence is the emotional barometer. In film canon, this silence persists after recovery, marking maturation. In novels, Kiki's slump is temporary and Jiji never loses speech, so this film-specific arc is the most discussed divergence.

The Dirigible Accident and Quiet ReunionMiyazaki film climax 85:00-102:00

1989

A dirigible airship moored for a festival breaks loose in high winds, leaving Tombo dangling from a rope. Kiki, having partially recovered her powers by helping Osono, borrows a street sweeper's push-broom and flies — wobbling but determined — to rescue him while crowds watch. Jiji watches from Osono's arms, meowing. Kiki succeeds, catching Tombo as he falls. Afterwards, Kiki returns to the bakery. She strokes Jiji and says she is glad to be home. Jiji meows — he does not answer in words — and Kiki smiles, accepting the new distance. The final montage shows Kiki's delivery business thriving, with Jiji sitting proudly on the shop sign, still silent but present. Resolves Kiki's arc from dependent child to capable young woman. Jiji's continued silence signals that Kiki no longer needs to externalize her inner voice. The ending is bittersweet and widely analyzed as Miyazaki's statement on growing up. No film sequel alters this status.

Jiji's Family — Marriage to Lily and Kittens (Novel Continuity)Kadono Vol.2 Majo no Takkyūbin 2: Kiki and Her New Magic (1993) Chapters 3-5; Vol.3-4 (2000-2004)

1993-2009

In the novel sequels, which Miyazaki did not adapt, Jiji meets Lily (Rirī), a beautiful white cat owned by a neighbor. Initially aloof, Jiji is smitten and, after comedic courtship involving stolen fish and territorial disputes, marries her. They have kittens — four in Vol.3 — named in some translations as Mimi and others. Jiji now divides his time between Kiki's attic and Lily's household, sometimes arriving late to deliveries because he was 'on kitten duty.' He teaches his kittens to speak to witches, extending the familiar lineage. Kiki, now 14-15, is amused and slightly jealous of Jiji's new independence, mirroring her own adolescence. Gives Jiji an independent adult life absent from the film, reinforcing Kadono's theme that everyone — even a cat — grows and forms attachments. Explains why novel-Jiji never loses speech: his language becomes part of a family, not just Kiki's projection. Often unknown to film-only fans, leading to misconception that Jiji remains forever solitary.

Jiji is a magical cat who can fly or cast spells. — Jiji has no flight or spellcasting. He rides Kiki's broom as passenger and his only supernatural trait is speaking with Kiki. He is otherwise a normal cat.

Jiji is female because he is voiced by a woman in Japanese. — Voiced by Rei Sakuma (female actor) in Japanese as is common for male child/animal roles, but character is male, uses 'boku,' and becomes a father in novels. English dubs use male actors.

Jiji remains able to talk at the end of the film; the meow is just a joke. — Miyazaki intentionally ends with Jiji meowing, not speaking, to signify Kiki's maturation. Director's interviews confirm this was deliberate and not a gag.

Jiji is from folklore or traditional Japanese yōkai lore. — Jiji is a wholly modern literary creation (1985), not folklore. He draws on European familiar tradition via children's literature, not Japanese folk belief about bakeneko or nekomata.

Jiji and the Jiji cat in Baba Is You are the same character. — Homonym only. Baba Is You's Jiji (2019) is a separate puzzle-game sprite by Finnish developer Hempuli Oy, with no narrative or design connection to Kadono/Miyazaki's cat.
