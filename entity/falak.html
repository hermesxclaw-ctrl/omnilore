Who It Is

Imagine a serpent so vast that it encircles the entire universe, its coils holding the foundations of existence, and its hunger for stars shaping the night sky. This is Falak, the cosmic dragon of Arabian legend, a being of paradox—both a pillar of order and a harbinger of chaos. As you explore this dossier, consider how ancient peoples used such myths to explain the mysteries of the cosmos, and how Falak's story echoes in modern culture, from music to film.

Falak is not an active agent in most narratives; rather, it is a passive cosmic support, holding the universe in place. However, it is said to be eternally hungry, and its constant swallowing of stars is believed to cause eclipses and the movement of celestial bodies. The serpent is also described as being in a state of perpetual torment, as the hellfire it channels burns its own flesh, yet it endures as part of the divine order. Cosmic Support

Falak holds the entire universe in place, serving as the lowest foundation of the cosmic structure. Its coils support the earth, heavens, and hells, preventing them from collapsing into the abyss.

Star Swallowing

Falak is said to swallow stars, causing them to vanish from the night sky. This ability is linked to eclipses and the perceived movement of celestial bodies, as the serpent's hunger is insatiable.

Hellfire Channeling

From its position in the seventh hell, Falak channels hellfire upward through the cosmic layers, influencing the temperature of the underworlds and the atmosphere. This power is both destructive and purifying, as it burns away impurities.

Immense Size and Strength

Falak's size is beyond human comprehension, with its body encircling the entire cosmos. Its strength is such that it can bear the weight of all creation without faltering, though it is bound by divine command.

Accounts I
The Cosmic Support of FalakOne Thousand and One Nights

c. 9th–14th century

In the frame tale of the Nights, a traveler seeks the meaning of the universe and is told of the hierarchy of beings: an angel holds the earth, which rests on a bull, which stands on a fish (Bahamut), which swims in a sea, and below that sea lies Falak, the great serpent. Falak's coils encircle the world, and its mouth is said to be filled with fire. The traveler learns that Falak's purpose is to contain the hellfire that would otherwise consume the world, and that its hunger for stars is a sign of the end times. The traveler gains wisdom about the fragility of existence and the divine order that sustains it. The story reinforces the idea that all creation is interconnected and dependent on God's will.

Accounts II
Falak and the Seven HellsStorytellingDB (based on Arabian folklore)

Medieval (recounted in modern source)

A pious man is granted a vision of the seven hells. He descends through layers of fire and torment until he reaches the lowest level, where he encounters Falak. The serpent is depicted as a massive creature with eyes like burning coals, and it is said to be the source of all hellfire. The man is told that Falak's suffering is eternal, as it must endure the fire it channels, and that its groans cause earthquakes on the surface. The vision ends with a warning to humanity to repent before the Day of Judgment. The man returns to his community and preaches repentance, leading many to reform their ways. The story serves as a moral cautionary tale about the consequences of sin.

The Star EaterOral tradition (collected in modern anthologies)

Unknown (oral tradition)

In a lesser-known tale, Falak is said to have once been a beautiful angel who rebelled against God and was transformed into a serpent as punishment. In its pride, it attempted to swallow the stars to assert its power, but God bound it in the depths of hell. Now, it is allowed to swallow stars only as a reminder of its fall, and each swallowed star becomes a tear in its eye, which falls as meteors. This story explains the origin of shooting stars and the Milky Way. The story provides an etiological explanation for celestial phenomena and serves as a warning against pride and rebellion.
