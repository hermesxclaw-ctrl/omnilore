Falak

The World Serpent, The Cosmic Dragon Below All, The One Who Swallows the Stars

TypeCosmic Serpent / Dragon

Who It Is
Form

Falak is described as a colossal serpent or dragon of incomprehensible size, dwelling in the seventh and lowest hell. Its body is so vast that it encircles the entire cosmos, and its coils are said to hold the foundations of the earth. The creature's scales are often depicted as dark, metallic, and glowing with embers, reflecting the hellfire that surrounds it. Its eyes are like burning suns, and its mouth is wide enough to swallow stars, hence the epithet 'The One Who Swallows the Stars.' In some illustrations, Falak is shown with multiple heads or horns, though the textual tradition primarily emphasizes its immense length and serpentine form. The serpent is often portrayed with a forked tongue and fangs dripping with venom, symbolizing its destructive potential.

Powers

Cosmic Support Falak holds the entire universe in place, serving as the lowest foundation of the cosmic structure. Its coils support the earth, heavens, and hells, preventing them from collapsing into the abyss.

Star Swallowing Falak is said to swallow stars, causing them to vanish from the night sky. This ability is linked to eclipses and the perceived movement of celestial bodies, as the serpent's hunger is insatiable.

Hellfire Channeling From its position in the seventh hell, Falak channels hellfire upward through the cosmic layers, influencing the temperature of the underworlds and the atmosphere. This power is both destructive and purifying, as it burns away impurities.

Immense Size and Strength Falak's size is beyond human comprehension, with its body encircling the entire cosmos. Its strength is such that it can bear the weight of all creation without faltering, though it is bound by divine command.

Accounts

In the frame tale of the Nights, a traveler seeks the meaning of the universe and is told of the hierarchy of beings: an angel holds the earth, which rests on a bull, which stands on a fish (Bahamut), which swims in a sea, and below that sea lies Falak, the great serpent. Falak's coils encircle the world, and its mouth is said to be filled with fire. The traveler learns that Falak's purpose is to contain the hellfire that would otherwise consume the world, and that its hunger for stars is a sign of the end times.

A pious man is granted a vision of the seven hells. He descends through layers of fire and torment until he reaches the lowest level, where he encounters Falak. The serpent is depicted as a massive creature with eyes like burning coals, and it is said to be the source of all hellfire. The man is told that Falak's suffering is eternal, as it must endure the fire it channels, and that its groans cause earthquakes on the surface. The vision ends with a warning to humanity to repent before the Day of Judgment.

In a lesser-known tale, Falak is said to have once been a beautiful angel who rebelled against God and was transformed into a serpent as punishment. In its pride, it attempted to swallow the stars to assert its power, but God bound it in the depths of hell. Now, it is allowed to swallow stars only as a reminder of its fall, and each swallowed star becomes a tear in its eye, which falls as meteors. This story explains the origin of shooting stars and the Milky Way.

Scholarly Debates
Position in the cosmic hierarchy
Falak resides below Bahamut (the fish) and above the bull and angel (Wikipedia source). ⇌ Some versions place Falak above Bahamut or omit the bull entirely, as noted in variant tellings.
Nature of Falak
Falak is a passive cosmic support, not an active agent (as in the Nights). ⇌ Some modern retellings portray Falak as a malevolent, world-devouring monster, emphasizing its destructive potential.
Etymology and origin
The name 'Falak' is derived from Arabic for 'celestial sphere' or 'orbit,' linking it to cosmic order. ⇌ Some scholars propose a foreign origin, such as Greek 'pelor' or Zoroastrian influences, suggesting a syncretic development.
What We Don't Know

The primary sources for Falak are limited to the One Thousand and One Nights and later retellings; there is no dedicated medieval manuscript solely about Falak. The exact origins of the myth are unclear, and the relationship between Falak and other cosmic serpents (e.g., Leviathan) has not been thoroughly studied. Additionally, the role of Falak in folk Islam, particularly in rural communities, is underdocumented. The connection between the cosmic serpent and the Central Asian music genre 'Falak' is underexplored in academic literature.
