Who It Is

Deep in the darkness of the primordial north, where the sun never rises, there dwells a dragon of fire and shadow—a being whose closed eyes plunge the world into night and whose open gaze brings the dawn. This is Zhulong, the Torch Dragon, a creature of ancient Chinese myth who holds the cosmos in a delicate balance between light and darkness.

Zhulong is a cosmic being that controls the cycle of day and night and the seasons. It does not eat, drink, or breathe, yet it brings wind and rain. Its actions are mechanical and impersonal, reflecting the natural order rather than personal will.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Zhulong (燭龍) combines 'zhu' (燭) meaning 'torch' or 'candle' and 'long' (龍) meaning 'dragon'. Thus, it is often translated as 'Torch Dragon' or 'Candle Dragon'. The alternative name Zhu Yin (燭陰) uses 'yin' (陰) meaning 'shade' or 'darkness', possibly referring to its role in the dark north.

Pronunciation — Zhu-long (approximately 'joo-long')

Aliases — Torch Dragon, Luminous Dragon, Candle Dragon, Zhu Yin

Ownership — Chinese mythology, specifically from the ancient text Shanhaijing (Classic of Mountains and Seas).

Powers
Control of Day and Night

When Zhulong opens its eyes, the world becomes day; when it closes them, night falls.

Limit: None stated; it is a passive function of its physical state.

Control of Seasons

Its breath creates winter and summer: blowing brings winter, inhaling brings summer.

Limit: Not mentioned in the Da Huang Bei Jing version.

Illumination of the North

Holding a candle in its mouth, Zhulong illuminates the otherwise dark northern regions.

Limit: Only in this version; the candle may be symbolic of its own light.

Control of Wind and Rain

It brings wind and rain without needing to eat, drink, or breathe.

Limit: It does not actively control weather; it simply exists and wind and rain occur.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Zhulong is described as having a human face and a serpent's body, entirely red in color. In some versions, it holds a candle in its mouth. It is an enormous creature, stretching across the northern sky.

Symbols — In art, Zhulong is often depicted as a red dragon with a human head, sometimes holding a torch or candle. It symbolizes the boundary between light and darkness, and the cyclical nature of time.

Domains — It resides in the far north, in the region of eternal darkness, specifically on Mount Zhong or Mount Zhangwei. This area is beyond the known world, where the sun never reaches.

Accounts I
The Torch Dragon in the Northern DarknessShanhaijing, Haiwai Bei Jing

Warring States to Han dynasty (c. 4th century BCE - 2nd century CE)

In the far north, beyond the Northwest Sea, there is a god with a human face and a serpent's body, red in color. It lives on Mount Zhong. When it opens its eyes, it is day; when it closes them, it is night. Its breath creates winter and summer. It does not eat, drink, or breathe, yet it brings wind and rain. This account establishes Zhulong as a primordial force that regulates time and weather, explaining the existence of day and night and the seasons.

Accounts II
The Candle Dragon of the Great WildernessShanhaijing, Da Huang Bei Jing

Warring States to Han dynasty (c. 4th century BCE - 2nd century CE)

In the northwest, beyond the Great Wilderness, there is a mountain called Zhangwei. There lives a god with a human face, a snake's body, and red color. It is called Zhu Yin. It holds a candle in its mouth to illuminate the ninefold darkness of the north. This version emphasizes Zhulong's role as a light-bringer in the eternal darkness, possibly symbolizing the sun's absence.
