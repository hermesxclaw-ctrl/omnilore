The Approach

Deep in the darkness of the primordial north, where the sun never rises, there dwells a dragon of fire and shadow—a being whose closed eyes plunge the world into night and whose open gaze brings the dawn. This is Zhulong, the Torch Dragon, a creature of ancient Chinese myth who holds the cosmos in a delicate balance between light and darkness.

Content advisory. Contains descriptions of a mythical creature and ancient cosmological concepts; no explicit dark material.
Identity

Zhulong

Pronunciation Zhu-long (approximately 'joo-long')

Known As

Torch DragonLuminous DragonCandle DragonZhu Yin

Etymology

The name Zhulong (燭龍) combines 'zhu' (燭) meaning 'torch' or 'candle' and 'long' (龍) meaning 'dragon'. Thus, it is often translated as 'Torch Dragon' or 'Candle Dragon'. The alternative name Zhu Yin (燭陰) uses 'yin' (陰) meaning 'shade' or 'darkness', possibly referring to its role in the dark north.

Cultural Ownership

Chinese mythology, specifically from the ancient text Shanhaijing (Classic of Mountains and Seas).

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars identify Zhulong with the god of the Northern Sea or with the star deity of the northern sky. Others see it as a personification of the aurora borealis, but this is speculative and not widely accepted.

Description

Appearance

Zhulong is described as having a human face and a serpent's body, entirely red in color. In some versions, it holds a candle in its mouth. It is an enormous creature, stretching across the northern sky.

Symbols & Iconography

In art, Zhulong is often depicted as a red dragon with a human head, sometimes holding a torch or candle. It symbolizes the boundary between light and darkness, and the cyclical nature of time.

Behaviour & Personality

Zhulong is a cosmic being that controls the cycle of day and night and the seasons. It does not eat, drink, or breathe, yet it brings wind and rain. Its actions are mechanical and impersonal, reflecting the natural order rather than personal will.

Domains & Habitats

It resides in the far north, in the region of eternal darkness, specifically on Mount Zhong or Mount Zhangwei. This area is beyond the known world, where the sun never reaches.

Version Differences

The two accounts in the Shanhaijing differ slightly: one emphasizes the opening and closing of its eyes as controlling day and night, while the other focuses on the candle it holds to illuminate the darkness. Later texts may add attributes like controlling the seasons or being a deity of the Northern Sea.

Powers & Abilities

Control of Day and Night

Source Version Shanhaijing, Haiwai Bei Jing

Description

When Zhulong opens its eyes, the world becomes day; when it closes them, night falls.

Limitations

None stated; it is a passive function of its physical state.

Contradictions

Some later interpretations may attribute this power to a separate deity, but the Shanhaijing clearly assigns it to Zhulong.

Control of Seasons

Source Version Shanhaijing, Haiwai Bei Jing

Description

Its breath creates winter and summer: blowing brings winter, inhaling brings summer.

Limitations

Not mentioned in the Da Huang Bei Jing version.

Contradictions

The Da Huang Bei Jing does not mention seasonal control, possibly a later addition or a different aspect.

Illumination of the North

Source Version Shanhaijing, Da Huang Bei Jing

Description

Holding a candle in its mouth, Zhulong illuminates the otherwise dark northern regions.

Limitations

Only in this version; the candle may be symbolic of its own light.

Contradictions

The Haiwai Bei Jing does not mention a candle, but the red color of its body could imply a glowing nature.

Control of Wind and Rain

Source Version Shanhaijing, Haiwai Bei Jing

Description

It brings wind and rain without needing to eat, drink, or breathe.

Limitations

It does not actively control weather; it simply exists and wind and rain occur.

Contradictions

None noted.

Stories & Myths

The Torch Dragon in the Northern Darkness

Source Shanhaijing, Haiwai Bei JingCulture ChinesePeriod Warring States to Han dynasty (c. 4th century BCE - 2nd century CE)

Summary

In the far north, beyond the Northwest Sea, there is a god with a human face and a serpent's body, red in color. It lives on Mount Zhong. When it opens its eyes, it is day; when it closes them, it is night. Its breath creates winter and summer. It does not eat, drink, or breathe, yet it brings wind and rain.

Participants

Zhulong

Consequences

This account establishes Zhulong as a primordial force that regulates time and weather, explaining the existence of day and night and the seasons.

Variants

The Da Huang Bei Jing version describes a similar being called Zhu Yin, holding a candle, but does not mention control of seasons.

The Candle Dragon of the Great Wilderness

Source Shanhaijing, Da Huang Bei JingCulture ChinesePeriod Warring States to Han dynasty (c. 4th century BCE - 2nd century CE)

Summary

In the northwest, beyond the Great Wilderness, there is a mountain called Zhangwei. There lives a god with a human face, a snake's body, and red color. It is called Zhu Yin. It holds a candle in its mouth to illuminate the ninefold darkness of the north.

Participants

Zhu Yin

Consequences

This version emphasizes Zhulong's role as a light-bringer in the eternal darkness, possibly symbolizing the sun's absence.

Variants

Some scholars consider Zhu Yin and Zhulong to be the same entity, with 'Yin' referring to the dark north.

Contradictions & Disputes

Zhulong vs Zhu Yin

Position A

Zhulong and Zhu Yin are the same entity, with different names in different chapters.

Position A — evidence

Both descriptions share similar physical traits (human face, snake body, red color) and location (north).

Position B

They are distinct beings, with Zhulong controlling day/night and Zhu Yin illuminating the north.

Position B — evidence

The functions differ: one controls time, the other provides light. Some scholars argue they are separate deities.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars treat them as the same being, with the name 'Zhu Yin' emphasizing its role in the dark north.

Control of seasons

Position A

Zhulong controls the seasons through its breath.

Position A — evidence

Explicitly stated in Haiwai Bei Jing.

Position B

This is a later addition or metaphorical interpretation.

Position B — evidence

Not mentioned in Da Huang Bei Jing; some interpret the breath as symbolic of natural cycles.

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus; the seasonal control is generally accepted as part of its myth.
