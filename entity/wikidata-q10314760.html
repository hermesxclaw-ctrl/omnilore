The Approach

In the 17th century, sailors on Mauritius told of a strange, flightless bird with reddish, hair-like plumage that would eagerly approach a piece of red cloth, only to be clubbed to death. This was the red rail, a bird that vanished from the island within a century of European discovery, leaving behind only bones, a few sketches, and tantalizing accounts.

Content advisory. Contains descriptions of animal extinction due to human activity, including hunting and predation by introduced species.

Etymology

The genus name Aphanapteryx means 'invisible-wing', from Greek aphanēs (unseen) and pteryx (wing). The specific name bonasia is unclear; it may derive from the hazel grouse (Tetrastes bonasia), or from Latin bonasus ('bull') or bonum assum ('good roast'). The alternative name Kuina mundyi uses Kuina, Japanese for 'water-rail', and mundyi after the traveller Peter Mundy.

Cultural Ownership

Mauritian endemic species; historically documented by European sailors and naturalists.

Disputed Identifications

Early accounts were misidentified as various species, including a small dodo; the yellowish variant described by Peter Mundy was once considered a distinct species (Kuina mundyi) but is likely a juvenile red rail.

Description

Appearance

The red rail was a little larger than a chicken, with reddish, hairlike plumage, dark legs, and a long, curved beak. Its wings were small, and its legs were slender for its size. It was similar to the Rodrigues rail but larger, with proportionally shorter wings. It has been compared to a kiwi or a limpkin in appearance.

Symbols & Iconography

No formal symbols; iconography limited to contemporary sketches and paintings.

Behaviour & Personality

The red rail was flightless and likely inquisitive, attracted to red objects, which hunters exploited. It fed on invertebrates, and snail shells with beak damage suggest its diet.

Domains & Habitats

Endemic to Mauritius, likely inhabiting forests and wetlands.

Version Differences

The yellowish colouration reported by Peter Mundy may represent a juvenile, not a distinct species. Early accounts varied in details, leading to multiple scientific names.

Extinction by humans

Source Historical accounts and extinction timeline

Description

The red rail was hunted to extinction by European sailors and colonists, who found it easy to catch due to its flightlessness and attraction to red cloth. Introduced species also contributed.

Scholarly Context

The last sighting was in 1693, and it is thought to have gone extinct around 1700.

Skeletal similarities (Newton and Günther, 1879).

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

Detailed genetic analysis is lacking due to the age of specimens. The exact phylogenetic position within Rallidae remains unresolved. The function of the red rail's attraction to red objects is not fully understood. The impact of specific introduced species on its decline is inferred but not directly documented. Some historical accounts may be lost or untranslated.
