Who It Is

In the 17th century, sailors on Mauritius told of a strange, flightless bird with reddish, hair-like plumage that would eagerly approach a piece of red cloth, only to be clubbed to death. This was the red rail, a bird that vanished from the island within a century of European discovery, leaving behind only bones, a few sketches, and tantalizing accounts.

The red rail was flightless and likely inquisitive, attracted to red objects, which hunters exploited. It fed on invertebrates, and snail shells with beak damage suggest its diet.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The genus name Aphanapteryx means 'invisible-wing', from Greek aphanēs (unseen) and pteryx (wing). The specific name bonasia is unclear; it may derive from the hazel grouse (Tetrastes bonasia), or from Latin bonasus ('bull') or bonum assum ('good roast'). Form & Signs

Appearance — The red rail was a little larger than a chicken, with reddish, hairlike plumage, dark legs, and a long, curved beak. Its wings were small, and its legs were slender for its size. It was similar to the Rodrigues rail but larger, with proportionally shorter wings. It has been compared to a kiwi or a limpkin in appearance.

Symbols — No formal symbols; iconography limited to contemporary sketches and paintings.

Domains — Endemic to Mauritius, likely inhabiting forests and wetlands.

Accounts I
Hunting with red cloth17th-century accounts

1600s

Hunters used red cloth to lure red rails within reach, then beat them with sticks. Contributed to the bird's extinction.

Accounts II
Scientific misidentification19th-century ornithological literature

1840s-1930s

Early descriptions and sketches were misattributed to multiple species, leading to a proliferation of invalid names. Confusion in taxonomy until subfossil remains clarified identity.

Rediscovery of fossilsMare aux Songes swamp and other sites

1860s-1890s

Subfossil bones were discovered, allowing correlation with historical accounts and illustrations. Established the red rail as a distinct rail species.
