Who It Is

In the tangled genealogies of Athenian myth, the name Praxithea appears like a recurring echo—now a water nymph who beds a king, now a queen who sacrifices her daughter, now a maiden who gives her life for the city. Five women share this name, each bound to the fate of Athens, each a thread in the city's legendary fabric.

The Naiad Praxithea is a consort and mother, appearing only in genealogies. The Athenian queen Praxithea is depicted as a devoted wife and mother, who willingly sacrifices her daughter to save Athens. The daughter of Leos is a self-sacrificing maiden. The Thespian princess is one of many daughters who consort with Heracles.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No specific physical description of any Praxithea survives in ancient sources. They are known only through genealogical and narrative contexts.

Symbols — No known symbols or iconography are associated with any Praxithea. The name is not linked to any specific attributes in art or cult.

Domains — The Naiad dwells in a spring or body of water, likely in or near Athens. The Athenian queen resides in the royal palace of Athens. The daughter of Leos is associated with Athens. The Thespian princess lives in Thespiae, Boeotia.

Accounts I
Praxithea the Naiad and the birth of PandionPseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.14.7

Mythical (pre-Trojan War)

Praxithea, a Naiad nymph, marries Erichthonius, the early king of Athens, and bears him a son, Pandion I. Her sister Zeuxippe marries Pandion, and their children include Erechtheus and Butes. She becomes an ancestor of later Athenian kings, establishing the royal line.

Praxithea, wife of Erechtheus, and the sacrifice of ChthoniaPseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.15.1; Euripides, Erechtheus (fragments)

Mythical (pre-Trojan War)

Praxithea, daughter of Phrasimus and Diogeneia, marries King Erechtheus of Athens. They have several children. During a war with Eleusis, an oracle demands the sacrifice of one of their daughters. Praxithea willingly offers her daughter Chthonia to save Athens. Athens is saved, but the sacrifice is tragic. The story is dramatized by Euripides.

Accounts II
The self-sacrifice of the daughters of LeosPausanias, Description of Greece 1.5.2; Suda, 'Leocorium'

Mythical (pre-Trojan War)

Praxithea (or Phrasithea) and her sisters Theope and Eubule, daughters of Leos, sacrifice themselves (or are sacrificed by their father) to save Athens from a plague or famine. A shrine called the Leocorium is dedicated to them. They are honored with a precinct in Athens.

Praxithea among the daughters of ThespiusDiodorus Siculus, Library of History 4.29

Mythical (generation of Heracles)

Praxithea is one of the fifty daughters of King Thespius. Heracles sleeps with all of them (except one) to produce many sons. Praxithea bears Heracles a son named Nephus. Her son Nephus becomes one of the many sons of Heracles who are sent to Sardinia.

All Praxitheas are the same person. — There are at least five distinct figures with this name in Greek mythology.

Praxithea is a goddess. — Most are mortal women or nymphs; the name means 'goddess' but they are not deities.
