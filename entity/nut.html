Nut, the star-spangled sky, who arches over the earth and swallows the sun each night to give birth to him each dawn.

Who They Are

Nut is the sky goddess, the star-dappled vault of heaven arched over the earth. Daughter of Shu (Air) and Tefnut (Moisture), sister and wife of Geb (Earth). She is depicted as a woman whose arched body forms the sky, her hands and feet touching the four cardinal points, covered with stars. Some depictions show her as a cow (the Celestial Cow) who gave birth to the sun each morning and swallowed it each night. Nut swallows Ra each evening and gives birth to him each dawn. She is the mother of Osiris, Isis, Set, Nephthys, and Horus (the Elder) — the great ennead of Heliopolis. The god Shu holds her apart from Geb, separating sky from earth. Nut protects the dead — the lids of coffins were painted with her image, and the deceased prayed: 'O Nut, spread yourself over me, place me among the imperishable stars.' Her role in the afterlife was to offer the deceased her womb for rebirth as a star.

Attested in Pyramid Texts / Book of the Dead / PlutarchEra Old Kingdom to Roman Egypt (c. 2600 BCE-400 CE)

Form

Nut is canonically depicted as a nude woman, covered with stars, arched over the earth with her fingers and toes touching the ground. In this pose she forms the vault of heaven. Her toes and fingertips were believed to touch the four cardinal points of north, south, east, and west. She is sometimes shown as a cow whose vast body forms the sky and heavens, and in 'cow' form she carries the sun god Ra on her back. Rarer forms include a sycamore tree and a giant sow suckling many piglets that represent the stars. Her headdress is the hieroglyph for a water-pot (nw), part of her name, which may also symbolize the uterus.

Powers

Encompassing the visible sky Nut is the sky itself, arched over the earth, and by her body 'covers' the semi-sphere of the visible sky as observed from the ground. Her fingers and toes touch the four cardinal points, demarcating the ordered world from the chaotic outside. Heliopolitan cosmology; attested across Pyramid/Coffin Texts and iconography She is the visible vault, not absolute space; the 'cover' is her own arched body. Some texts associate the boundaries with the Heh gods supporting her rather than her own limbs only.

Solar cycle: swallowing and rebirth of the sun During the day the sun and moon travel across her body; at dusk they are swallowed, pass through her belly through the night, and are reborn each dawn. This makes Nut the mechanism of the daily solar rebirth and of Ra's rejuvenation. Standard Egyptian solar theology None attested; the cycle is continuous and reliable. Spells in the Book of the Heavenly Cow at times assign the night sky to Thoth as deputy and the Heh gods as supporters, redistributing this function across deities.

Barrier against chaos Nut is the boundary separating the primordial watery chaos from the ordered cosmos. With Geb, her consort, she forms the permanent boundary between the primeval waters and the newly created world. Heliopolitan and Book of the Heavenly Cow cosmologies The barrier is breached in the 'fall' narrative when the world becomes imperfect. The role of boundary-holder is at times shared with or given to Shu and the Heh gods.

She Who Protects Ra Nut envelops and protects the sun god Ra, cradling him through the night so that he may be born anew at dawn. This is among her title-epithets. Title 'She Who Protects'; Book of the Heavenly Cow None attested. None documented.

She Who Holds a Thousand Souls Because of her role in Osiris' resurrection and the daily re-birth of Ra, Nut became a key goddess of the afterlife, drawing the dead into her starry body and sustaining them with food and wine. Funerary texts; Book of the Dead Access required the deceased to be judged worthy; the unworthy were confined by Geb. Some funerary traditions assign the judgment of souls to Osiris and his tribunal rather than to Nut.

Source of the celestial ladder (maqet) Nut provided the sacred ladder (maqet) by which Osiris ascended to her heavenly skies. The ladder symbol was placed in tombs to protect the deceased and invoke the aid of the sky goddess. Pyramid Texts tradition The ladder is a protective ritual object, not a portable 'power'. None documented.

Maternal protection of the dead Nut periodically uses her body, nourishment, and presence to protect the deceased from evil and the forces of death: 'I am Nut, and I have come so that I may enfold and protect you from all things evil.' Funerary liturgy and sarcophagus inscriptions Protection is invoked ritually; effectiveness tied to correct ritual and worthiness. None documented.

Accounts

In the creation story of Heliopolis, the creator Atum (or Ra-Atum) produced Shu (the air/emptiness) and Tefnut (moisture). Shu and Tefnut mated and gave birth to Nut, the sky, who in turn mated with her brother Geb, the earth. From the union of Geb and Nut came the four great deities of the next generation — Osiris, Isis, Set, and Nephthys — whose central story (the Osiris myth) threads through Egyptian religion. Nut is unusual among world mythologies because she is the sky deity while her male counterpart Geb is the earth — the inverse of the more common sky-father/earth-mother arrangement.

Nut was originally engaged to and continually procreating with Geb, lying atop the earth in a state of constant sexual union. The air god Shu, their father, forcibly separated the couple, holding Nut up as the arched vault of heaven and pinning Geb beneath. In mythological depictions Geb is shown reclining with his phallus still pointed toward Nut, a visible reminder of the interrupted union. The separation created the space of the cosmos in which ordered life could exist. Later interpretations read the separation in more human terms, exposing hostility and sexual jealousy between Shu and his son Geb, who in some accounts sought to usurp Shu and eventually took Tefnut as his chief queen in retaliation.

In Plutarch's account, Rhea (whom he equates with Nut) secretly consorts with Saturn (Geb), incurring a curse from the sun-god Helios (Ra or Atum): Rhea is forbidden to give birth on any day of the 360-day year. The god Mercury (Thoth) intervenes, gambling with the moon-goddess Selene and winning a seventieth portion of her moonlight. From this he constructs five additional days, which are added to the calendar as the Egyptian Epagomenal (intercalary) days. On these five days Rhea/Nut gives birth to her children: Osiris, Horus the Elder, Set (whom Plutarch equates with Typhon), Isis, and Nephthys. Plutarch adds that Osiris and Isis married even before birth, and that Set's birthday — the third intercalary day — was considered an omen of bad luck. The early Egyptologist E. A. Wallis Budge argued that Plutarch's version embellished or misinformed its way to a number of details the native Egyptian record does not support.

Humankind rebelled against the supreme sun god Ra. As divine punishment, Ra dispatched his eye as the goddess (Hathor/Sekhmet) to slaughter the rebels. In the aftermath, the survivors were separated from Ra, who now departed the earth to reside in the sky riding on the back of Nut, the heavenly cow. According to Erik Hornung's interpretation, suffering and death entered the world with this 'fall,' and the original unity of creation fractured. The creator god transforms into many heavenly bodies, creates the Fields of Paradise for the blessed dead, appoints Geb as heir, and hands the rule of humankind to Osiris, with Thoth ruling the night sky as deputy and Shu and the Heh gods supporting the sky goddess. Nut thus becomes the celestial vehicle that bears the sun aloft and the boundary separating the gods from humanity after the cosmic rupture.

A way to hold it in your mind

Somewhere between the chaotic waters and the dust of a newborn earth, a naked woman the size of the world arched her spine until her fingertips and toes touched the four horizons, her belly glittering with the night's stars — and she swallowed the sun whole each evening only to push it, gasping and reborn, into the next morning's sky. Nut is that woman: the oldest Egyptian mother-sky, who held Geb the earth as her lover, was torn from him by the very air, bore the murderous and the merciful gods alike into being, and whose body — painted blue on a hundred thousand coffin lids — became the final, caressing ceiling over the dead in their long sleep. To understand Nut is to understand why the Egyptians buried their kings beneath a star-clad arch that was also a mother, and why every dawn is, in their eyes, an act of resurrection that happens inside a goddess's belly.
