Who It Is

Somewhere between the chaotic waters and the dust of a newborn earth, a naked woman the size of the world arched her spine until her fingertips and toes touched the four horizons, her belly glittering with the night's stars — and she swallowed the sun whole each evening only to push it, gasping and reborn, into the next morning's sky. Nut is that woman: the oldest Egyptian mother-sky, who held Geb the earth as her lover, was torn from him by the very air, bore the murderous and the merciful gods alike into being, and whose body — painted blue on a hundred thousand coffin lids — became the final, caressing ceiling over the dead in their long sleep. To understand Nut is to understand why the Egyptians buried their kings beneath a star-clad arch that was also a mother, and why every dawn is, in their eyes, an act of resurrection that happens inside a goddess's belly.

Nut is a protective and maternal figure: she shelters the sun god Ra within her body each night, shields the ordered cosmos from the surrounding forces of chaos, and draws the dead into her star-filled sky to refresh them with food and wine. She is addressed as a mother: 'O my Mother Nut, stretch Yourself over me, that I may be placed among the imperishable stars which are in You, and that I may not die.' Name & Tongue

Etymology — The ancient Egyptian name Nwt itself literally means 'Sky', and is usually transcribed 'Nut'. Her name often includes the unpronounced determinative hieroglyph for 'sky' in the hieroglyphic record. Because vowels were long omitted from the writing of ancient Egyptian, the exact pronunciation is unce

Pronunciation — Reconstructed as /nuːt/, open 'U' as in English 'nut'. In Egyptological convention 'Nut' is pronounced to rhyme with 'but'. Coptic Ⲛⲉ (Ne) suggests a Late Egyptian monosyllable. By convention the name is transcribed with a glottal-less final t that was likely not pronounced in later phases of the language.

Aliases — Nwt, Nunet, Nenet, Nuit, Not, Nout

Ownership — Ancient Egypt. Nut belongs to the Heliopolitan Ennead, the nine-god creation group centered at Heliopolis (Iunu). She is one of the oldest attested deities of the Egyptian pantheon

Powers
Encompassing the visible sky

Nut is the sky itself, arched over the earth, and by her body 'covers' the semi-sphere of the visible sky as observed from the ground. Her fingers and toes touch the four cardinal points, demarcating the ordered world from the chaotic outside.

Limit: She is the visible vault, not absolute space; the 'cover' is her own arched body.

Solar cycle: swallowing and rebirth of the sun

During the day the sun and moon travel across her body; at dusk they are swallowed, pass through her belly through the night, and are reborn each dawn. This makes Nut the mechanism of the daily solar rebirth and of Ra's rejuvenation.

Limit: None attested; the cycle is continuous and reliable.

Barrier against chaos

Nut is the boundary separating the primordial watery chaos from the ordered cosmos. With Geb, her consort, she forms the permanent boundary between the primeval waters and the newly created world.

Limit: The barrier is breached in the 'fall' narrative when the world becomes imperfect.

She Who Protects Ra

Nut envelops and protects the sun god Ra, cradling him through the night so that he may be born anew at dawn. This is among her title-epithets.

Limit: None attested.

She Who Holds a Thousand Souls

Because of her role in Osiris' resurrection and the daily re-birth of Ra, Nut became a key goddess of the afterlife, drawing the dead into her starry body and sustaining them with food and wine.

Limit: Access required the deceased to be judged worthy; the unworthy were confined by Geb.

Source of the celestial ladder (maqet)

Nut provided the sacred ladder (maqet) by which Osiris ascended to her heavenly skies. The ladder symbol was placed in tombs to protect the deceased and invoke the aid of the sky goddess.

Limit: The ladder is a protective ritual object, not a portable 'power'.

Maternal protection of the dead

Nut periodically uses her body, nourishment, and presence to protect the deceased from evil and the forces of death: 'I am Nut, and I have come so that I may enfold and protect you from all things evil.'

Limit: Protection is invoked ritually; effectiveness tied to correct ritual and worthiness.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Nut is canonically depicted as a nude woman, covered with stars, arched over the earth with her fingers and toes touching the ground. In this pose she forms the vault of heaven. Her toes and fingertips were believed to touch the four cardinal points of north, south, east, and west. She is sometimes shown as a cow whose vast body forms the sky and heavens, and in 'cow' form she carries the sun god Ra on her back. Rarer forms include a sycamore tre

Symbols — The pot hieroglyph (nw); the sky hieroglyph (determinative); stars covering her body; the celestial ladder (maqet), a sacred symbol she granted to Osiris to enter heaven, placed in tombs to protect the dead; the sycamore tree as a nourishing form; the cow as the heavenly form bearing Ra. She is frequently painted on the inside lid of sarcophagi, and vaulted tomb ceilings were decorated in dark blue with stars as a representation of her outstretch

Domains — The sky in its totality: the visible day and night firmament, the stars, the sun and moon, the cosmos, astronomy and time-keeping, mothers and motherhood. Her domain is the vault of heaven arched over the earth of her brother-husband Geb. As a funerary deity she dwells in the celestial realm to which the blessed dead ascend, and her body — the starry sky — is the decorated ceiling of tombs and burials.

Accounts I
The Heliopolitan Creation: the birth of the SkyHeliopolis creation theology; attested in Pyramid/Coffin Texts

Old Kingdom (first attested) onward

In the creation story of Heliopolis, the creator Atum (or Ra-Atum) produced Shu (the air/emptiness) and Tefnut (moisture). Shu and Tefnut mated and gave birth to Nut, the sky, who in turn mated with her brother Geb, the earth. From the union of Geb and Nut came the four great deities of the next generation — Osiris, Isis, Set, and Nephthys — whose central story (the Osiris myth) threads through Egyptian religion. Nut is unusual among world mythologies because she is the sky deity while her male counterpart Geb is the earth — the inverse of the more common sky-father/earth-mother arrangement. Establishes the third generation of the Ennead and the lineage of the divine kings of Egypt.

The Separation of Nut and GebHeliopolitan mythology; Geb article synthesis

Old Kingdom onward

Nut was originally engaged to and continually procreating with Geb, lying atop the earth in a state of constant sexual union. The air god Shu, their father, forcibly separated the couple, holding Nut up as the arched vault of heaven and pinning Geb beneath. In mythological depictions Geb is shown reclining with his phallus still pointed toward Nut, a visible reminder of the interrupted union. The separation created the space of the cosmos in which ordered life could exist. Later interpretations read the separation in more human terms, exposing hostility and sexual jealousy between Shu and his son Geb, who in some accounts sought to usurp Shu and eventually took Tefnut as his chief queen in retaliation. Established the permanent division between sky and earth and created the habitable cosmic space.

Accounts II
Plutarch's five intercalary days (the birth of the Ennead's four/five gods)Plutarch, On Isis and Osiris

c. 100–120 CE

In Plutarch's account, Rhea (whom he equates with Nut) secretly consorts with Saturn (Geb), incurring a curse from the sun-god Helios (Ra or Atum): Rhea is forbidden to give birth on any day of the 360-day year. The god Mercury (Thoth) intervenes, gambling with the moon-goddess Selene and winning a seventieth portion of her moonlight. From this he constructs five additional days, which are added to the calendar as the Egyptian Epagomenal (intercalary) days. On these five days Rhea/Nut gives birth to her children: Osiris, Horus the Elder, Set (whom Plutarch equates with Typhon), Isis, and Nephthys. Plutarch adds that Osiris and Isis married even before birth, and that Set's birthday — the third intercalary day — was considered an omen of bad luck. The early Egyptologist E. A. Wallis Budge argued that Plutarch's version embellished or misinformed its way to a number of details the native Egyptian record does not support. Provides the etiological explanation for the five epagomenal days of the Egyptian calendar and the birthdays of the great gods.

The Book of the Heavenly Cow: the fall and Nut as Ra's mountBook of the Heavenly Cow

New Kingdom (text possibly Middle Kingdom); copies in tombs of Seti I, Ramesses II, Ramesses III, and Tutankhamun's shrine

Humankind rebelled against the supreme sun god Ra. As divine punishment, Ra dispatched his eye as the goddess (Hathor/Sekhmet) to slaughter the rebels. In the aftermath, the survivors were separated from Ra, who now departed the earth to reside in the sky riding on the back of Nut, the heavenly cow. According to Erik Hornung's interpretation, suffering and death entered the world with this 'fall,' and the original unity of creation fractured. The creator god transforms into many heavenly bodies, creates the Fields of Paradise for the blessed dead, appoints Geb as heir, and hands the rule of humankind to Osiris, with Thoth ruling the night sky as deputy and Shu and the Heh gods supporting the sky goddess. Nut thus becomes the celestial vehicle that bears the sun aloft and the boundary separating the gods from humanity after the cosmic rupture. Accounts for the imperfect state of the world, the origin of suffering and death, the distance of the gods, and the celestial order with Nut bearing the sun.
