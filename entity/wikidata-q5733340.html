Who It Is

Imagine a wind so violent that the Roman poet Horace used it as a metaphor for the uncontrollable fury of nature itself — a wind that could tear a ship apart and scatter an empire's fleet. That was Africus, the Roman god of the southwest wind, a force of elemental chaos that blew in from the burning coast of North Africa, carrying both the heat of the Sahara and the fury of the sea.

In Roman literature, Africus is consistently portrayed as a violent, stormy, and dangerous wind. He is described as bringing sudden squalls, heavy rain, and turbulent seas. He is a wind of disruption, capable of sinking ships and destroying crops. Unlike gentler winds like Favonius (the west wind), Africus is a force of chaos and destruction.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Derived directly from the Latin term for the African continent (Africa), with the adjectival suffix -icus meaning 'of or belonging to'. The name literally translates to 'the African one'.

Pronunciation — AF-rih-kus (Classical Latin: /ˈafrɪkʊs/)

Aliases — Afer, Libycus, Vulturnus

Ownership — Roman mythology, with roots in the broader Mediterranean wind-lore tradition. Adopted and adapted from Greek sources but given a distinctly Roman geographic and political identity.

Powers
Control of the Southwest Wind

As the divine personification of the southwest wind, Africus had dominion over this specific meteorological phenomenon. He could summon or calm the winds from that direction.

Limit: His power was limited to the southwest wind and its associated weather patterns. He did not control all winds, only his own.

Storm Generation

Africus was associated with violent storms, heavy rain, and turbulent seas. He could whip up waves and create dangerous conditions for sailors.

Limit: His storms were primarily maritime and coastal. There is no evidence he could cause earthquakes or other geological phenomena, though Lucretius uses him metaphorically for such power.

Bringer of Heat

Blowing from the southwest, Africus could carry the heat of the Sahara and the North African interior. This made him a wind of both heat and moisture, a potentially oppressive combination.

Limit: This is an inferred aspect based on his origin, not explicitly detailed in the primary texts.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Classical texts do not provide a detailed physical description of Africus as a personified deity. In artistic representations, winds were often depicted as bearded, winged male figures, sometimes with conch shells or holding drapery that billowed to indicate their direction. Africus would likely have been shown with a face or headdress suggesting African or North African features, and his drapery would billow from the southwest.

Symbols — The southwest direction; the storm cloud; the turbulent sea; the African coastline; the burning heat of the Sahara combined with the moisture of the sea. His name itself is a symbol of the Roman perception of Africa as a land of both great heat and violent storms.

Domains — Southwest wind; storms; rain; the sea; the coast of North Africa (the Roman province of Africa); the boundary between the known world and the vast, dangerous Atlantic beyond the Pillars of Hercules.

Accounts I
The Winds in the Tower of AeolusVirgil, Aeneid, Book 1 (though this is the Greek Aeolus, the Roman context is identical)

c. 29-19 BCE

In the Aeneid, Juno, seeking to destroy Aeneas's fleet, offers Aeolus, the king of the winds, a bride in exchange for unleashing his captive winds. Aeolus releases the winds, including the storm-bringing winds from the south and southwest, creating a massive storm that scatters the Trojan fleet. While Africus is not named specifically in this passage, the storm winds he releases are the kind of violent, destructive forces Africus personifies. This is the archetypal story of the destructive power of the storm winds in Roman epic. The Trojan fleet is scattered, Aeneas is separated from his men, and the stage is set for his arrival in Carthage, a city on the coast of Africa, the very region Africus comes from.

Accounts II
The Storm in Horace's OdesHorace, Odes 1.1

c. 23 BCE

Horace uses Africus as a symbol of the uncontrollable and terrifying power of nature. He describes the Africus as a wind that 'fights' against the sea, stirring up waves that reach the heavens. It is a vivid, poetic image of chaos and danger, used to contrast with the peaceful life of the farmer or the poet. The poem's imagery reinforces the Roman view of Africus as a force to be feared, a natural power that could disrupt the order of human life.

Africus is a purely Greek god. — Africus is a Roman wind deity. While the Roman wind system was influenced by Greek ideas, Africus's name and identity are uniquely Roman, tied to the Roman province of Africa.

Africus is the same as the modern 'africa' wind or the Sirocco. — The Sirocco is a modern wind that blows from the Sahara into the Mediterranean. While it shares a similar origin (North Africa), it is not the same as the ancient Roman concept of Africus. The Sirocco is a specific meteorological phenomenon, not a personified deity.

Africus was a major, widely-worshipped god. — Africus was a minor deity, a personification of a natural force. He was more of a concept in literature and natural philosophy than a god with temples, priests, and a dedicated cult.
