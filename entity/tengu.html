Who It Is

Powerful, semi-divine mountain goblins with both bird-like and human features. Originally depicted as crow-like demons (karasu tengu), later evolved into the long-nosed, red-faced humanoid form (daitengu). They are proud warriors, martial arts masters, and punishers of the vain and arrogant.

Form

Two forms: Karasu tengu (crow-headed, winged) and Daitengu (tall humanoid with red face, extremely long nose, wings, wearing mountain ascetic robes, carrying a feathered fan)

Flight Both bird-like kotengu and winged daitengu can fly; tengu taught human women (e.g. Benten in The Eccentric Family) and warriors to fly. Sōjōbō is described with both bird-like claws and feathered wings. Attested across medieval literature and later folklore; retained as a core modern ability Daitengu with primarily human form still possess wings; Sōjōbō himself 'walks' with difficulty in some modern portrayals (a back injury rendering him flightless in The Eccentric Family) - an adaptation-specific limitation, not an attested one. Rate of flight vs gust-generation with the fan is not consistently specified.

The magic feather fan (hauchiwa) The tengu's signature object, a fan of feathers (the daitengu carries a seven-feather fan as a sign of rank). It can stir up great winds and gales, and in folk tales ('The Tengu's Fan') can grow or shrink a person's nose. Folk tales + medieval/later art; the nose-magic appears in the popular tale of the tengu's fan In 'The Tengu's Fan', misuse backfires: a scoundrel accidentally fans himself while dozing and his nose grows grotesquely, reaching to heaven. Whether the fan is wind-power or nose-magic depends on the tale; collective tradition allows both.

Shapeshifting / invisibility Tengu can take the form of priests, nuns, hermits, or disguise themselves as yamabushi; they are also credited with invisibility. They can capture human form and appear as normal mountain priests (as Sōjōbō first appears to Ushiwakamaru). Widely attested medieval + Shugendō-influenced folklore True form (wings, huge nose, or beak) 'reappears' when the tengu is discovered or lets its disguise drop. None major.

Spirit possession Like foxes, tengu can spiritually possess human beings - especially women and girls - and speak through their mouths (a phenomenon, kitsunetsuki/possession-linked). A girl with poor dexterity was once possessed by a tengu who then instructed a samurai via her as an expert swordsman. 12th-13th century accounts extend the possession trope; the Inoue Enryō-related Inaba story is 19th-century Possession can be lifted or engage a host against their will. Whether possession is voluntary magic or an attack is left ambiguous.

Superhuman martial arts mastery and teaching Tengu possess great knowledge of swordsmanship, tactics, and magic, and are credited with transmitting it - above all Sōjōbō training the young Minamoto no Yoshitsune. After training 'he could run and jump beyond the limits of human power'. Legends also ascribe them a magic gun that never misses (given to a boy who spent three years with a tengu). From the 14th century; crystallised in the Yoshitsune cycle, Gikeiki, Heiji monogatari Knowledge is typically taught, not innate to humans who merely witness it. No major contradiction; the benevolence of the teaching varies (earlier versions portray it as a demon's scheme, later ones as sympathy for a wronged boy).

Weather and environmental phenomena Tengu cause wind, thunder-like noise, mysterious tree-cutting and falling sounds ('tengu daoshi'), disorienting laughter ('tengu warai'), pebble-storms and mountain rumbling ('tengu tsubute'), drumming ('tengu taiko'), hayashi music ('tengubayashi'), and kagura ('yamakagura') from the mountains at night. Regional folklore (Yamagata, Kanagawa, Gunma, Shizuoka, Sado, Gifu) 18th-19th c. These are localised, chiefly audible/atmospheric effects rather than targeted attacks; shooting a gun three times supposedly stops the tree-felling sound. Regional and inconsistent by nature.

Abduction and relocation Tengu perform kamikakushi - the taking of human beings, especially boys - returning them altered, elsewhere, or near death or madness, sometimes after being tricked into eating animal dung. Abductees may return after years with magic gifts. 13th century onward; the Yoshitsune cycle is itself read as a kamikakushi pattern (Foster) Victims are typically eventually returned, though often traumatised. Whether abduction is benevolent (Yoshitsune) or malicious (torture in Sōjōgatani) differs by narrative; Foster notes the 'fits the pattern' parallel.

Supernatural strength and power Sōjōbō is said to have the strength of 1,000 normal tengu ('a match for a thousand'). Tengu of note are ranked in a hierarchy with the daitengu on top. Sōjōbō lore; Genpei Jōsuiki rank system Even Sōjōbō can be nearly fatally wounded (by the Handcart Priest in the otogi-zōshi), showing tengu are not invulnerable. Feats of absolute power coexist with vulnerability in different tales.

Divination / prophecy Tengu can tell the future, and Sōjōbō predicts Yoshitsune's vengeance and future battles in the Noh play. Shugendō-influenced + Noh (Kurama-tengu) None stated. None.

Behavior & Habitat

Arrogant and proud; punishes corrupt Buddhist monks and vain people; can also train worthy warriors; fiercely territorial over mountains

Accounts

The boy Minamoto no Yoshitsune (born Ushiwaka-maru/Shanao) is exiled to the temple on Mount Kurama after the Taira clan kills his father, Yoshitomo. In Sōjōgatani (Bishop's Valley) he meets Sōjōbō, king of the tengu, who - seeing the boy's respectfulness and resolve - teaches him swordsmanship, tactics, and magic so he can avenge his father. After the training Yoshitsune can 'run and jump beyond the limits of human power', and in the sympathetic Noh version the Great Tengu promises to protect and support him in future battles before disappearing into the trees.

Emperor Sutoku was forced to abdicate, and when he raised the Hōgen Rebellion of 1156 to retake the throne from Emperor Go-Shirakawa he was defeated and exiled to Sanuki Province in Shikoku. In despair and torment he swore to haunt Japan as a great demon, and on death became a fearsome tengu with long nails and eyes like a kite's. His vengeful spirit (onryō) was blamed for the court's fall, the rise of the samurai, droughts, and unrest.

Throughout the 12th and 13th centuries tengu were established as the ghosts of vain, angry, or heretical priests who 'fell on the tengu-realm' (tengudō). The Genpei Jōsuiki has a god explain to Go-Shirakawa that these beings, being Buddhists, cannot go to Hell, yet by their bad principles cannot reach Heaven either; the knowledgeable become daitengu and the ignorant kotengu. They possess people (especially women), speak through them, rob temples, and assault the royal house - the Ōkagami reports Emperor Sanjō was blinded by the ghost of a priest who resented the throne. The Shasekishū (Buddhist parables) later distinguishes 'good' from 'bad' tengu, with the good ones as protectors who fell only through pride.

A boy looks through a piece of bamboo pretending he can see distant places. A curious tengu offers to trade his magic straw cloak (which renders the wearer invisible) for the 'magic' bamboo. Having duped the tengu, the boy misbehaves while invisible. In another version an ugly old man tricks a tengu into giving the cloak and causes mayhem; the tengu regains it through a riddle-exchange and punishes the man by turning him into a wolf.

An old man with a lump on his face encounters a band of tengu making merry in the mountains and joins their dancing, pleasing them so much they ask him back and promise a gift - then remove his lump, thinking he'll return for it. A greedy neighbour with his own lump tries to repeat the trick but dances badly and tries to steal the gift; disgusted, the tengu give him the first old man's lump in addition to his own.

The young Yoshitsune climbs from the temple slope up Mount Kurama to a jewelled palace containing hundreds of tengu, ruled by the Great Tengu (Sōjōbō). There he learns his father 'has been reborn as Dainichi Buddha in the Pure Land of Amida', and the Great Tengu and he pass together through the six planes of karmic transmigration to visit him. The palace is described as huge, elaborate, decorated with jewels, 'the seat of the court of their goblin-king' (de Benneville).

Japanese folklore (yōkai)
A way to hold it in your mind

The crow and the monk met in a haunted valley above Kyoto, and out of their meeting Japan's most durable symbol of pride, swordsmanship, and the mountain's dangerous magic was born. The tengu began as a bird - a black kite whose cry rode ahead of war, given a name (heavenly dog) borrowed from a Chinese meteor-hound that gnaws the moon - then sprouted a beak, a monk's robe, and finally the great red nose for which the world knows it. It has played, over a thousand years, every role on the spiritual scale: the arrogant priest's ghost punished on a 'tengu road' that is neither Heaven nor Hell, the vengeful ghost of a deposed emperor cursing Japan to its ruin, a gullible buffoon tricked out of its magic cloak by a peasant, and the sky-father mentor who taught the boy-warrior Yoshitsune to run beyond human limits. Today a tengu's face sits in your keyboard's emoji set and a tengu society wheels through the bullet-hell skies of Touhou, but the figure never shed the two things that made it: flight, and the fall that pride brings. To understand the tengu is to understand how Japan has, for ten centuries, told itself the story of the mountain as both a place of mastery and a place of judgment - and how a Chinese word for a dog became the long shadow that still haunts Japan's tallest pines.
