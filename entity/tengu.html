Who It Is

The crow and the monk met in a haunted valley above Kyoto, and out of their meeting Japan's most durable symbol of pride, swordsmanship, and the mountain's dangerous magic was born. The tengu began as a bird - a black kite whose cry rode ahead of war, given a name (heavenly dog) borrowed from a Chinese meteor-hound that gnaws the moon - then sprouted a beak, a monk's robe, and finally the great red nose for which the world knows it. It has played, over a thousand years, every role on the spiritual scale: the arrogant priest's ghost punished on a 'tengu road' that is neither Heaven nor Hell, the vengeful ghost of a deposed emperor cursing Japan to its ruin, a gullible buffoon tricked out of its magic cloak by a peasant, and the sky-father mentor who taught the boy-warrior Yoshitsune to run beyond human limits. Bipolar across history. The earliest tengu (Heian era) are troublesome, adversarial demons: enemies of Buddhism who mislead the pious, carry off monks and tie them to treetops, possess women (a form of kitsunetsuki/spirit possession), rob temples, and endow dangerous power. They are the 'ghosts of the arrogant' - fallen priests, nuns, and laypeople whose pride barred them from Heaven but who could not reach Hell. Powers
Flight

Both bird-like kotengu and winged daitengu can fly; tengu taught human women (e.g. Benten in The Eccentric Family) and warriors to fly. Sōjōbō is described with both bird-like claws and feathered wings.

Limit: Daitengu with primarily human form still possess wings; Sōjōbō himself 'walks' with difficulty in some modern portrayals (a back injury rendering him flightless in The Eccentric Family) - an adaptation-specific limitation, not an attested one.

The magic feather fan (hauchiwa)

The tengu's signature object, a fan of feathers (the daitengu carries a seven-feather fan as a sign of rank). It can stir up great winds and gales, and in folk tales ('The Tengu's Fan') can grow or shrink a person's nose.

Limit: In 'The Tengu's Fan', misuse backfires: a scoundrel accidentally fans himself while dozing and his nose grows grotesquely, reaching to heaven.

Shapeshifting / invisibility

Tengu can take the form of priests, nuns, hermits, or disguise themselves as yamabushi; they are also credited with invisibility. They can capture human form and appear as normal mountain priests (as Sōjōbō first appears to Ushiwakamaru).

Limit: True form (wings, huge nose, or beak) 'reappears' when the tengu is discovered or lets its disguise drop.

Spirit possession

Like foxes, tengu can spiritually possess human beings - especially women and girls - and speak through their mouths (a phenomenon, kitsunetsuki/possession-linked). A girl with poor dexterity was once possessed by a tengu who then instructed a samurai via her as an expert swordsman.

Limit: Possession can be lifted or engage a host against their will.

Superhuman martial arts mastery and teaching

Tengu possess great knowledge of swordsmanship, tactics, and magic, and are credited with transmitting it - above all Sōjōbō training the young Minamoto no Yoshitsune. After training 'he could run and jump beyond the limits of human power'. Legends also ascribe them a magic gun that never misses (given to a boy who spent three years with a tengu).

Limit: Knowledge is typically taught, not innate to humans who merely witness it.

Weather and environmental phenomena

Tengu cause wind, thunder-like noise, mysterious tree-cutting and falling sounds ('tengu daoshi'), disorienting laughter ('tengu warai'), pebble-storms and mountain rumbling ('tengu tsubute'), drumming ('tengu taiko'), hayashi music ('tengubayashi'), and kagura ('yamakagura') from the mountains at night.

Limit: These are localised, chiefly audible/atmospheric effects rather than targeted attacks; shooting a gun three times supposedly stops the tree-felling sound.

Abduction and relocation

Tengu perform kamikakushi - the taking of human beings, especially boys - returning them altered, elsewhere, or near death or madness, sometimes after being tricked into eating animal dung. Abductees may return after years with magic gifts.

Limit: Victims are typically eventually returned, though often traumatised.

Supernatural strength and power

Sōjōbō is said to have the strength of 1,000 normal tengu ('a match for a thousand'). Tengu of note are ranked in a hierarchy with the daitengu on top.

Limit: Even Sōjōbō can be nearly fatally wounded (by the Handcart Priest in the otogi-zōshi), showing tengu are not invulnerable.

Divination / prophecy

Tengu can tell the future, and Sōjōbō predicts Yoshitsune's vengeance and future battles in the Noh play.

Limit: None stated.

Accounts I
Ushiwakamaru and Sōjōbō (the Kurama tengu's training of Yoshitsune)Gikeiki, Heiji Monogatari, the Noh play Kurama-tengu, kōwakamai Miraiki ('Chronicle of the Future'), the otogi-zōshi Tengu no dairi ('The Palace of the Tengu')

Medieval (Muromachi recensions of a 12th-13th c. tradition)

The boy Minamoto no Yoshitsune (born Ushiwaka-maru/Shanao) is exiled to the temple on Mount Kurama after the Taira clan kills his father, Yoshitomo. In Sōjōgatani (Bishop's Valley) he meets Sōjōbō, king of the tengu, who - seeing the boy's respectfulness and resolve - teaches him swordsmanship, tactics, and magic so he can avenge his father. After the training Yoshitsune can 'run and jump beyond the limits of human power', and in the sympathetic Noh version the Great Tengu promises to protect and support him in future battles before disappearing into the trees. Establishes Yoshitsune as Japan's archetypal legendary warrior and cements the tengu as martial masters; the legend's fame is credited with softening the tengu's image from demon to sympathetic teacher. Themes of it appear across Noh, kōwakamai, ukiyo-e (Yoshitoshi, Hiroshige, Kyōsai, Kuniyoshi, Kunisada), and a famous festival dashi at the Sanno festival portraying Ushiwakamaru with Sōjōbō.

Emperor Sutoku becomes a tenguHōgen Monogatari (13th c. recension); Emperor Sutoku is one of the 'Three Great Onryō of Japan' (with Sugawara no Michizane and Taira no Masakado)

Events 1156; legend crystallised in the medieval war tale

Emperor Sutoku was forced to abdicate, and when he raised the Hōgen Rebellion of 1156 to retake the throne from Emperor Go-Shirakawa he was defeated and exiled to Sanuki Province in Shikoku. In despair and torment he swore to haunt Japan as a great demon, and on death became a fearsome tengu with long nails and eyes like a kite's. His vengeful spirit (onryō) was blamed for the court's fall, the rise of the samurai, droughts, and unrest. One of the most famous examples of the 'tengu as vengeful fallen being' register. Sutoku was enshrined at Shiramine Shrine in Kyoto and Kotohira-gū in Kagawa. Edo-period works depict him as an onryō, and the 2023 heavy-metal band Onmyo-Za produced a song about him.

The arrogant monk's fall and possession (tengu as ghosts of the proud)Kojidan, Ōkagami; the Genpei Jōsuiki account; Kitano-dera / Gagoze-related temple tales

12th-13th century

Throughout the 12th and 13th centuries tengu were established as the ghosts of vain, angry, or heretical priests who 'fell on the tengu-realm' (tengudō). The Genpei Jōsuiki has a god explain to Go-Shirakawa that these beings, being Buddhists, cannot go to Hell, yet by their bad principles cannot reach Heaven either; the knowledgeable become daitengu and the ignorant kotengu. They possess people (especially women), speak through them, rob temples, and assault the royal house - the Ōkagami reports Emperor Sanjō was blinded by the ghost of a priest who resented the throne. The Shasekishū (Buddhist parables) later distinguishes 'good' from 'bad' tengu, with the good ones as protectors who fell only through pride. Cemented the tengu's association with vanity - the origin of the idiom tengu ni naru ('becoming a tengu' = being conceited) - and their identity as the 'ghosts of the arrogant' rather than devils per se.

Accounts II
The Tengu's Magic CloakOral folk tale collected by Japanese folklorists (e.g. Fanny Hagin Mayer, Keigo Seki, Yanagita Kunio-school collections)

recorded 19th-20th c.; tale-type older

A boy looks through a piece of bamboo pretending he can see distant places. A curious tengu offers to trade his magic straw cloak (which renders the wearer invisible) for the 'magic' bamboo. Having duped the tengu, the boy misbehaves while invisible. In another version an ugly old man tricks a tengu into giving the cloak and causes mayhem; the tengu regains it through a riddle-exchange and punishes the man by turning him into a wolf. Exemplifies the human-tricks-tengu comic register in folk tales.

The Old Man's Lump Removed (Kobu-tori Jiisan)Japanese folk tale (widely recorded; namesake of the kobutori-jiisan tale type)

recorded 19th-20th c.

An old man with a lump on his face encounters a band of tengu making merry in the mountains and joins their dancing, pleasing them so much they ask him back and promise a gift - then remove his lump, thinking he'll return for it. A greedy neighbour with his own lump tries to repeat the trick but dances badly and tries to steal the gift; disgusted, the tengu give him the first old man's lump in addition to his own. A beloved comic moral tale: kindness is rewarded, greed punished - the tengu as fickle but morally-responsive merrymakers.

The Palace of the Tengu (Tengu no dairi)otogi-zōshi story (Muromachi-Edo fiction)

14th-17th century

The young Yoshitsune climbs from the temple slope up Mount Kurama to a jewelled palace containing hundreds of tengu, ruled by the Great Tengu (Sōjōbō). There he learns his father 'has been reborn as Dainichi Buddha in the Pure Land of Amida', and the Great Tengu and he pass together through the six planes of karmic transmigration to visit him. The palace is described as huge, elaborate, decorated with jewels, 'the seat of the court of their goblin-king' (de Benneville). Shows the fully-developed benevolent-tengu cosmology and the tengu palace-mytheme; reworked as the kōwakamai Miraiki.

Origin: Chinese tiāngǒu vs independent Japanese creatureThe name and characters tengu (天狗) are borrowed from the Chinese tiāngǒu 'Heavenly Dog', and early Japanese usage (per de Visser) may blend tiāngǒu with the huli jing fox-spirit before absorbing local kami.The Tiangou article asserts the two are 'independent mythological creatures with no common ancestor or origin' - tengu being bird/long-nose beings while tiāngǒu are dogs or meteors.
Tengu vs Garuda/Karura: descent or resemblanceThe tengu descends from the Hindu-Buddhist Garuḍa (Karura), whose human-winged form matches; the name tengu is written for garuda in the Emmyō Jizō-kyō.The identification is retrospective (Edo-era sutra) and the tengu is hostile to Buddhism while the garuda is its protector; de Visser found little evidence for a clean lineage.
Sarutahiko Ōkami as the Konoha-tengu modelSarutahiko (long nose, red face, monkey deity) is the original model of the Konoha-tengu; festival masks of the two are near-identical.This is an art-historical alignment of a *later* long-nose trait; the earlier bird/kite form predates any Sarutahiko link.
