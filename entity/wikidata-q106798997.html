The Approach

In the tangled lore of Greek myth, few relics are stranger than the shoulder blade of Pelops: a bone said to have been carved from ivory by the gods themselves, stolen by a hero, and eventually displayed as a wonder in a temple. Its story weaves together divine punishment, heroic theft, and a relic that blurred the line between myth and history.

Content advisory. Contains references to divine dismemberment, cannibalism (implied), and bodily relics; no explicit violence beyond mythological narrative.
Identity

Shoulder blade of Pelops

Pronunciation PEE-lops / SHOHL-der blayd

Known As

Shoulder of PelopsPelops' shoulder bladeIvory shoulder blade

Etymology

The name derives from the Greek 'Pelops' (Πέλοψ), meaning 'dark-faced' or 'gray-eyed', and 'omoplata' or 'os' for bone; the relic is simply the shoulder blade (scapula) of the hero Pelops.

Cultural Ownership

Ancient Greek, specifically associated with the sanctuary of Olympia and the Peloponnese; the relic was housed in a temple at Olympia.

Disputed Identifications

Some later sources identified the relic as a whalebone or fossil, but the mythic identity as Pelops' shoulder blade was widely accepted in antiquity. Modern scholars debate whether the relic was a genuine ancient artifact or a later invention.

Description

Appearance

According to Pausanias and myth, the shoulder blade was made of ivory, gleaming white, and unusually large. It was kept in a chest or displayed in the temple of Artemis at Olympia. Some accounts suggest it was a full scapula, possibly with traces of flesh or divine craftsmanship.

Symbols & Iconography

The shoulder blade symbolized divine favor, heroic identity, and the miraculous restoration of Pelops. It was a tangible link to the heroic age and was venerated as a relic with protective powers.

Behaviour & Personality

As an inanimate object, it had no behavior, but it was believed to possess apotropaic and healing properties. It was also a political and cultic symbol, used to assert the sanctity of Olympia.

Domains & Habitats

The relic was housed in the sanctuary of Olympia, specifically in the temple of Artemis, according to Pausanias. It was an object of pilgrimage and wonder.

Version Differences

Some versions of the myth say the shoulder was eaten by Demeter (who was distracted by grief for Persephone), while others say it was eaten by a mortal or by the gods collectively. The material is consistently ivory, but the exact size and appearance vary. Later rationalizing accounts suggested it was a fossil or a whale bone, not a human bone.

Powers & Abilities

Divine restoration

Source Version Pindar, Olympian 1; Pseudo-Apollodorus, Epitome

Description

The shoulder blade was part of Pelops' body after the gods restored him from a dismembered state; it was a permanent reminder of his divine favor and miraculous resurrection.

Limitations

It did not grant active powers to its possessor; its power was symbolic and cultic.

Contradictions

Some later rationalists denied its divine origin, suggesting it was a natural bone.

Apotropaic protection

Stories & Myths

The Dismemberment of Pelops

Source Pindar, Olympian 1; Pseudo-Apollodorus, EpitomeCulture Ancient GreekPeriod Mythic (pre-Trojan War)

Summary

Pelops' father Tantalus, wanting to test the gods, cut Pelops into pieces and served him as a stew at a banquet. The gods recognized the trick and did not eat, except Demeter, who, distracted by grief for her daughter Persephone, ate the shoulder. The gods then reassembled Pelops and replaced the missing shoulder with one made of ivory. Pelops was restored to life and later became a great hero, winning Hippodamia as his wife through a chariot race against her father Oenomaus.

Consequences

Pelops became the eponymous hero of the Peloponnese, and his line included Atreus and Agamemnon. The ivory shoulder became a sacred relic.

Variants

Some versions say the gods themselves ate the flesh, and only Demeter was tricked. Others say the shoulder was eaten by a mortal. Later rationalizations deny the miracle.

The Theft and Recovery of the Shoulder Blade

Source Pausanias, Description of Greece 5.13.4-6Culture Ancient GreekPeriod Historical (perhaps 5th century BCE)

Summary

Pausanias records that a man stole the shoulder blade from the temple at Olympia, but he was later caught and the relic was returned. The thief's name is not given, and the details are vague.

Participants

Consequences

The relic was restored, and its importance was reaffirmed.

Variants

The Relic at Olympia

Source Pausanias, Description of Greece 5.13.4-6Culture Ancient GreekPeriod 2nd century CE

Summary

Pausanias visited Olympia and saw the shoulder blade. He describes it as a large ivory bone, kept in the temple of Artemis. He notes that its size suggested it belonged to a giant, but he accepts the mythic explanation.

Participants

Consequences

The relic attested to the continuity of heroic myth in Roman times.

Variants

Pausanias also mentions that some people claimed it was a whale bone.

Dismemberment and cannibalism

Source Pseudo-Apollodorus, Epitome; Pindar, Olympian 1

Description

Tantalus dismembered his son Pelops and served him as a meal to the gods, a cannibalistic act. The gods, except Demeter, did not eat.

Scholarly Context

This myth is often interpreted as a reflection of ancient sacrificial practices or as a warning against impiety. The cannibalism is a central element of the narrative.

Contradictions & Disputes

Material of the shoulder blade

Position A

It was made of ivory by the gods.

Position A — evidence

Pindar and Pseudo-Apollodorus explicitly say ivory.

Position B

It was a natural bone (possibly a fossil or whale bone) later identified as Pelops'.

Position B — evidence

Pausanias reports that some believed it was a whale bone; modern rationalists suggest a fossil.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars accept the mythic ivory origin as literary, but the actual relic may have been a natural object.

Who ate the shoulder

Position A

Demeter ate it.

Position A — evidence

Pseudo-Apollodorus and Pindar (though Pindar does not specify Demeter).

Position B

A mortal or another god ate it.

Position B — evidence

Some scholia and later sources vary.

Scholarly Consensus

Demeter is the most common version, but variants exist.

Existence of the relic

Position A

The relic existed at Olympia and was a genuine ancient artifact.

Position A — evidence

Pausanias saw it and described it.

Position B

The relic was a later invention to attract pilgrims.

Position B — evidence

No earlier source mentions it as a relic; only the myth.

Scholarly Consensus

The relic likely existed in Pausanias's time, but its authenticity is debated.

Primary Evidence

Pausanias, Description of Greece 5.13.4-6

Type textDate 2nd century CELocator Book 5, chapter 13, sections 4-6Language Ancient Greek

Translation

Pausanias describes the shoulder blade of Pelops kept at Olympia, stating that it was made of ivory and was said to have been given by the gods when Pelops was restored to life. He also records that it was stolen by a certain man and later recovered.

Pindar, Olympian Odes 1.25-27

Type textDate 5th century BCELocator Olympian 1, lines 25-27Language Ancient Greek

Translation

Pindar alludes to the story of Pelops' ivory shoulder, mentioning that the gods gave him an ivory shoulder when he was reassembled after being served as a feast to the gods.

Bibliotheca (Pseudo-Apollodorus), Epitome 2.3-10
