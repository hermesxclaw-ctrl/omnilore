Who It Is

Before the world ends it begins with a sound. On the last day, when the world-tree groans and the sea boils and the dead ships row from the east, the whitesoft god on the rainbow bridge will at last raise his horn — the Gjallarhorn, heard 'in all worlds' — and blow it once, not as warning but as opening. Every god the medieval Norse built their cosmos on will hear that blast and arm for the war they cannot win. Heimdall, the all-seeing, all-hearing watchman who needs less sleep than a bird and can hear wool growing on a sheep's back, will blow the note that starts the burning of the world; and then, the texts say, the god who guarded everything will walk down to meet his old enemy Loki and kill him, and die himself, in the same breath. No other god in the Norse pantheon is so purely defined by a single future obligation — and none is so riddle-wrapped in its present: born of nine mothers

The unmoving, all-seeing, all-hearing watchman — the divine sentry who stands at the edge of the world on Bifröst. He requires almost no sleep, his senses pierce to the boundaries of the world, and he is defined by vigilance. He is called 'great and holy' and is a learned figure (in Rígsþula he teaches runes and doles out the social classes). In the flytings he speaks plainly and is mocked by Loki. He is both the father-of-humanity figure (through the Rígr role) and the warder of the gods — a duality even the medieval redactor struggled to hold together.

Etymology — The etymology of the name Heimdallr is obscure and contested. Powers
Near-total sensory perception (sight and hearing)

Heimdall needs less sleep than a bird, can see at night as well as by day and for 'a hundred leagues or more', and can hear grass growing on the earth and wool growing on sheep (Gylfaginning). He sees and hears to the boundaries of the world.

Limit: Some scholars connect the lost 'Heimdall's hljóð (hearing)' with Odin's forfeited eye — i.e. that Heimdall, like Odin, left a body part (an ear) in exchange for wisdom. This is an interpretive reading, not a stated mechanism.

Guardianship of the Bifröst bridge

He sits at the edge of heaven guarding the burning rainbow bridge against the mountain giants (berg-jötnar). He is the watcher who will first perceive the approach of Ragnarök's enemies.

Limit: The office binds him to his post; he is defined by the watching rather than by great martial feats.

The Gjallarhorn and its blast

Heimdall bears the resounding horn Gjallarhorn ('Yelling Horn'; possibly 'horn of the river Gjöll'), which 'can be heard in all worlds'. He will blow it mightily at the onset of Ragnarök to wake the gods.

Limit: Its sounding is unwelcome — it is the alarm of final war, not a festive call.

Golden teeth / gold-toothed appearance (Gullintanni)

Heimdall has golden teeth, hence the name Gullintanni. This is given as a plain bodily trait in Gylfaginning and Skáldskaparmál.

Limit: None attested.

Foresight 'like the Vanir'

Þrymskviða says Heimdall 'foresees, like the Vanir', lending him seer-like cognition beyond the Æsir.

Limit: The precise content of this foresight is not elaborated; scholars dispute whether it implies Vanir membership (Lindow: no other source supports that).

Originator of the social classes (as Rígr)

In Rígsþula, wandering the earth as Rígr, he sleeps between three pairs of couples and thereby begets the ancestors of the thralls, the free farmers, and the warrior-nobility — instituting the social hierarchy of humanity.

Limit: The Húsdrápa/Skáldskaparmál frame adds that he 'walks in the middle of roads' and gives advice and runes.

Shapeshifting / shape of a seal

In the Brísingamen contest he and Loki fight 'in the shape of seals' at Vágasker and Singasteinn.

Limit: The Húsdrápa is fragmentary; the full mechanics of the shape-change are lost.

The head as his sword

The kenning system equates 'Heimdall's head' with a sword ('the head is called his sword'; a sword is 'Vindhlér's helmet-filler'). This cryptic trait supports the ram-association interpretation (a ram's horns are on its head).

Limit: Heavily debated; no literal weapon named Hǫfuð is given a full heroic backstory in the primary sources (the Old Norse noun hǫfuð means 'head').

Accounts I
The birth from the Nine MothersGylfaginning; Skáldskaparmál (quoting Heimdalargaldr and Húsdrápa); likely Völuspá hin skamma

Medieval (13th-c. texts of earlier oral material)

Heimdallr is the son of Odin by nine mothers who are sisters — 'Offspring of nine mothers am I and of nine sisters am I the son' (Heimdalargaldr, via Gylfaginning). The Húsdrápa calls him 'son of eight mothers plus one'. Völuspá hin skamma (in Hyndluljóð) is read as telling that a mighty god was born at the edge of the world by nine jötun-maidens, nourished by earth-strength, the ice-cold sea, and swine's blood. The leading scholarly theory makes the Nine Mothers the wave-daughters of Ægir and Rán, so that Heimdallr is 'born from the waves'; Dumézil famously read him as 'the ram of the ninth wave' — the white-crested ninth wave to which the eight preceding ewes give birth. Establishes his liminal, wave-and-border birth, the foundation for his boundary-guard identity.

The contest for Brísingamen with Loki (the seal-fight)Skáldskaparmál ch. 8 and ch. 16, quoting Úlfr Uggason's Húsdrápa; Skáldskaparmál's list of Loki-kennings ('wrangler with Heimdall and Skadi')

10th-c. verse preserved in 13th-c. prose

Heimdallr and Loki contend at Vágasker and Singasteinn for the beautiful jewel Brísingamen (Freyja's treasured necklace). The two fight in the shape of seals, and Heimdallr is first to seize the 'sea-kidney' (jewel). This is why Skáldskaparmál lists Heimdall as 'the recoverer of Freyja's necklace' and calls Loki the 'wrangler with Heimdall'. Establishes the core Loki–Heimdall antagonism that climaxes in mutual death at Ragnarök.

Rígr institutes the social classes (Rígsþula)Rígsþula (Poetic Edda) with prose introduction

Medieval

The prose intro says Heimdall, 'a god among the Æsir', once wandered the seashore calling himself Rígr. In the poem he walks the high roads, visits three successive households, dines with each couple, and sleeps three nights between each pair in their bed. Each wife becomes pregnant, producing the ancestors of the thralls (slaves), the karlar (free farmers), and the jarlar (warrior-nobility). At the warrior hall, Rígr teaches runes, names a promising boy, and proclaims him his son; that line becomes great war-leaders. One descendant grows so skilled he shares runic knowledge with Heimdall and earns the title Rígr himself. Makes Heimdall the divine originator of human social hierarchy — a 'father of humanity' within the poem.

Accounts II
The counsel to disguise Thor as Freyja (Þrymskviða)Poetic Edda — Þrymskviða

Medieval

When the jötnar steal Thor's hammer Mjöllnir and demand Freyja as bride-price for its return, the gods debate at the thing. Heimdall proposes the stratagem of dressing Thor in bridal clothes as Freyja. The episode showcases Heimdall's practical cunning and his 'foresight like the Vanir'. Recovers Mjöllnir; cements Thor's mythic prominence.

The watch at Ragnarök and the mutual death with LokiVöluspá; Gylfaginning ch. 51

Medieval

At the onset of Ragnarök, after the three roosters crow and the world shudders, Heimdallr 'stands and blows Gjallarhorn with all his might', and the gods awaken and assemble at the thing. When the final battle comes on the field Vígríðr, Heimdall faces Loki; 'Loki fights Heimdallr and the two kill each other.' The world then burns (Surtr's fire) and is reborn. Heimdall's horn is the trigger that opens the final war; his death is one of the catalogue of god-deaths that ends the old world and admits the new.

The euhemerised settlement (Ynglinga saga)Heimskringla — Ynglinga saga ch. 5 (Snorri Sturluson)

c. 1230 CE

In Snorri's euhemerised account, when Odin and the Æsir settle in Sweden, estates are given out: Njörðr at Nóatún, Freyr at Uppsala, Heimdall at Himinbjörg, Thor at Þrúðvangr, Baldr at Breiðablik. The cosmological dwellings are thus historicised as landed estates of a mortal (or mortal-king) ruling clan. Models how Norse 'gods' were rationalised as deified ancestors in Christian-era historiography.
