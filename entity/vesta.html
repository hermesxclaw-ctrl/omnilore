Vesta

Ancient Roman religion (Italic / Proto-Indo-European hearth-cult stratum). Vesta is a Roman deity with deep Italic roots, not a Greek import. While later Roman literature systematically paired her with the Greek Hestia, the cult of Vesta predates significant Hellenistic influence and is attested in Lavinium and Alba Longa before Rome's historical period.

Etymology: Ancient scholars proposed several origins. Ovid derived the name from Latin vi stando ('standing by power'). Cicero and the grammarian Servius traced it to Greek Hestia, which Cornutus connected to hestanai dia pantos ('standing for ever'). A third hypothesis links Vesta to Latin vestio ('to clothe') or to Greek hestia ('hearth'). None of these derivations is universally accepted. The French comparative philologist Georges Dumézil (1898–1986) reconstructed a Proto-Indo-European root *h₁eu- ('burn'), yielding *h₁eu-s- / *h₁w-es-; the latter form appears in Vesta, while the former surfaces in Greek heuein ('to burn'), Latin urit ('burns'), and Vedic osathi ('burns'). Beekes, however, regarded Greek Hestia as probably unrelated to this root. James Wilson Poultney also noted Umbrian parallels: Uestisier (genitive) / Vestiçe (dative), related to libation formulae uestisiar and uesticatu, and Oscan Veskeí on the Agnone Dedication.

Primary evidence: Ovid, Fasti (Book 6, lines 249–298; Book 1, 391–456) (AD 8); Plutarch, Parallel Lives — Life of Romulus (chapter 2) (c. AD 100); Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Roman Antiquities (Book 4) (c. 20 BC); Cicero, De natura deorum (Book 2, sections 66–68) (45 BC)
