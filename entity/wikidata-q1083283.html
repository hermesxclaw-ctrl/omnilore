Who It Is

She was not born. She was built — hammered, stitched, and gifted into being by gods who intended her as a trap. Pandora, whose name means 'all-gifted' or 'all-giving,' arrives in Greek myth as the first woman, beautiful and terrible, carrying a jar that will unmake the golden ease of mankind. She never asked to be made, and she never asked to open what she was given.

Hesiodic characterization is hostile and external: she is given by Hermes a 'shameless mind and deceitful nature' (Theogony 572-578: kynon noon, epiklopon ethos), a 'bitch-like mind' and lies. Works and Days 77-80: Hermes gives her lies, wheedling words, and a thievish character. She is not given interiority or motive beyond divine programming; she acts as Zeus's dolos (trap) against men. Powers
Divine Gifts (Pan-dora)

Each Olympian endows her with a skill: Athena teaches weaving and adornment, Aphrodite grants grace and desire, Hermes grants speech and cunning, the Graces/Horai grant beauty and adornment. Collectively makes her irresistibly attractive and socially adept.

Limit: Gifts are not powers she controls; they are tools of Zeus's deception. She does not retain divine agency; she is mortal. No source shows her using weaving or persuasion independently.

Pithos-Bearer / Unleasher of Evils

Possession and opening of the pithos containing evils (kaka), diseases, toils, and death, which scatter among humanity. In Hesiod, the jar is in the house of Epimetheus; Pandora removes the lid (Works and Days 94-99).

Limit: She does not create the evils; Zeus sealed them. She cannot recall them. Elpis (Hope/Expectation) remains inside when she closes the lid. She has no immunity.

Thievish Speech and Deceit (Hermetic gift)

Hermes implants pseudEA (lies), artful words, and a thievish character, enabling manipulation and seduction. This is framed as a divine weapon, not learned skill.

Limit: No magical compulsion; functions as social deception. Later authors do not show her exercising this after the jar episode.

Earth-Rising / Anesidora Aspect (iconographic)

On 5th-century vases, Anesidora/Pandora rises from the earth, suggesting chthonic fertility power — sending up gifts from the ground (grain, life). This is not a narrative power in Hesiod but a visual theology.

Limit: No text explicitly says Pandora controls fertility; association is iconographic and etymological. May represent conflation with Ge/Gaia or Demeter.

Accounts I
The Fashioning of the First Woman (Theogony)Hesiod, Theogony 570-612

c. 700 BCE

After Prometheus steals fire for mankind, Zeus orders Hephaestus to mix earth and water and fashion a beautiful maiden, Athena to clothe her, Aphrodite to grant grace, and Hermes to give a dog-like mind and thievish character. She is led before gods and men as a 'beautiful evil' (kalon kakon), a wonder to behold. She is given to Epimetheus, who despite Prometheus's warning accepts her. From her comes the race of women, a 'great trouble' for men who must support them. Aetiology for the existence of women and marriage; establishes women as separate genos (race) from men; divine punishment for fire-theft shifts burden onto humanity.

Pandora and the Pithos (Works and Days)Hesiod, Works and Days 60-105

c. 700 BCE

Zeus commands the creation of Pandora as above, with detailed distribution of gifts and naming: 'all the gods gave a gift, wherefore men call her Pandora' (81-82). She is sent to Epimetheus with a pithos (jar). Until then men lived without evils, toil, or disease. Pandora removes the great lid (mega poma) and scatters countless miseries; only Elpis (Hope/Expectation) remains inside under the rim, by Zeus's will. The world is thereafter full of evils that wander silently. Aetiology for human suffering, disease, and labor; explains why evils are unseen and silent; introduces Elpis problem — whether hope is consolation or retained evil.

Pandora, Daughter of Deucalion (Bibliotheca)Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 1.7.2; also Hesiodic Catalogue of Women fr. 5 M-W

1st-2nd century CE (compiling earlier 6th century BCE genealogies)

In the post-flood genealogy, Deucalion and Pyrrha (the survivors of Zeus's flood) have children including Hellen, Amphictyon, Protogeneia, and Pandora. This Pandora is a mortal princess, not the first woman. She marries Zeus and bears Graecus (eponym of Greeks) or, in some variants, is mother of Latinus. The Bibliotheca thus splits Pandora into two figures: the primordial first woman and a later homonymous heroine. Creates genealogical bridge from flood to Hellenic tribes; causes confusion between two Pandoras that persists in lexica.

Accounts II
Pandora on the Base of the Athena ParthenosPausanias 1.24.7, describing Phidias' chryselephantine statue (438 BCE)

5th century BCE monument, described 2nd century CE

Pausanias records that the base of Phidias' Athena Parthenos in the Parthenon depicted the 'birth of Pandora' (Pandoras genesis) with twenty gods present. The scene showed her creation/adornment, confirming her importance in Periclean Athens as a Panhellenic origin figure paired with Athena's own birth. Demonstrates Pandora's visual prominence in Classical Athens; links her to Athena (craft) and to civic ideology of autochthony.

Anesidora Rising from the EarthAttic white-ground kylix, Tarquinia Painter (BM 1881,0528.1) and related vases; discussed in Harrison, Mythology and Monuments (1890) and Shapiro (1994)

c. 470-460 BCE

A woman labeled ANESIDORA rises half-bodied from the ground, with Hermes and satyrs nearby, sometimes with Epimetheus or Zeus. She is adorned and emerges as if from the earth itself, suggesting Pandora as earth-goddess who sends up gifts (grain, vegetation). This iconography predates or parallels Hesiodic texts and may preserve an older, more positive earth-mother Pandora. Fuels scholarly debate that Hesiod inverted a benign fertility goddess into a punitive figure; supports etymology 'all-giving' vs 'all-gifted'.

Pandora in Later Moralizing and Christian AllegoryHyginus Fabulae 142 (1st century CE); Fulgentius Mythologiae 2.7 (5th-6th century CE); Erasmus Adagia (1508)

1st century CE to 16th century

Hyginus condenses Hesiod: Pandora made from earth, given box with all evils. Fulgentius allegorizes her as 'all gifts of nature' corrupted by curiosity. Erasmus, translating Hesiod, renders pithos as pyxis (box), cementing 'Pandora's Box' in European languages. Renaissance art (Rosso Fiorentino, Cousin) and later literature recast her as Eve-like archetype of female curiosity. Shifts emphasis from Zeus's trap to female curiosity and disobedience; aligns Pandora with Eve; creates enduring idiom 'Pandora's box' and box iconography.

Pandora opened a box. — Hesiod's Greek is pithos, a large storage jar for grain/oil/wine, not a small box. The 'box' comes from Erasmus's 1508 mistranslation of pithos as pyxis. All archaic and classical sources describe a jar.

Pandora was punished for her curiosity, like Eve for disobedience. — In Hesiod, Pandora is not punished; she is the punishment — a trap made by Zeus to punish men for Prometheus's theft. She is not disobedient; she executes Zeus's design. The curiosity motive is absent in Hesiod and added later to align her with Eve.

Hope escaped to help humanity; only evils remained inside. — Hesiod says the opposite: evils scattered, only Elpis (Hope/Expectation) remained inside the jar, under the rim, not escaping. Whether this is good or bad is debated, but textually hope did NOT escape.

Pandora was the first human. — She was the first woman (gyne) in Hesiodic myth; men (andres) already existed, living without women, toil, or disease. She is the first female, not first human.

Pandora is a goddess with worship and powers. — Pandora has no cult, sanctuary, or worship in antiquity; she is a mortal archetype, an artificial woman. She has no divine powers beyond the gifts given to make her alluring. The Anesidora earth-goddess aspect is iconographic, not cultic.
