Who It Is

Imagine the world as a great, flat disc, and around its edge, coiled like a serpent, lies a creature so vast that it holds the entire realm in its grasp, its own tail in its mouth. This is Jörmungandr, the World Serpent, a being of such immense scale that it is less a monster and more a fundamental part of the cosmos itself. He is the boundary, the threat, and the promise of an ending, a silent, slumbering giant in the deep, waiting for the day he can let go.

Jörmungandr is primarily a passive, cosmic force in the myths, dwelling in the world sea and holding the world together. His only notable behaviors are his interactions with Thor, where he is provoked into a rage, and his prophesied role at Ragnarök, where he will release his tail and emerge from the sea to battle the gods. He is not depicted as actively malicious in his daily existence, but as a primordial entity whose very presence is a fundamental part of the world's structure. Thus, the name can be translated as 'The Huge Mons

Pronunciation — YOR-mun-GAHN-dur (approximate Old Norse pronunciation: 'JÖR-mun-gahn-dr')

Aliases — Jǫrmungandr, Miðgarðsormr, Midgard Serpent, World Serpent, Midgard Serpent, The Great Serpent

Ownership — Norse (Old Norse) mythology, specifically the medieval Icelandic literary tradition as recorded in the Poetic Edda and Prose Edda.

Powers
Immense Size and Strength

Jörmungandr is of planetary scale, able to encircle the entire world and bite his own tail. This gives him unimaginable physical power, capable of causing world-ending destruction simply by moving.

Limit: His size is also his limitation; he is confined to the world sea, unable to easily move beyond it.

Venomous Breath and Bite

The serpent is venomous, and his poison is potent enough to kill even the mighty Thor. At Ragnarök, Thor is said to take nine steps after their battle before succumbing to the serpent's poison.

Limit: The poison is a one-time use in the final battle, and the serpent himself is killed by Thor's hammer.

World-Encircling Constriction

By holding his tail, Jörmungandr forms a ring around Midgard, serving as a physical boundary for the world. When he releases his tail, it is a sign that the world is ending.

Limit: This is a passive ability; he is essentially a cosmic structure, not an active power he can wield.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Jörmungandr is described as an unfathomably large sea serpent, so immense that it encircles the entire world of Midgard and grasps its own tail in its mouth. The Prose Edda describes him as a 'worm' (ormr), which in Old Norse could refer to any serpentine creature, from a small snake to a dragon. His size is the defining feature; he is a being of planetary scale, not a mere monster that could be encountered in a fjord. The physical description is

Symbols — The primary symbol associated with Jörmungandr is the ouroboros, the serpent eating its own tail, which represents cyclicality, eternity, and the self-contained nature of the cosmos. He is also a symbol of the boundary, the encircling edge of the world, and the threat of chaos that lies just beyond the civilized order of Midgard.

Domains — Jörmungandr's domain is the world sea, the vast ocean that surrounds Midgard (the human world). He is the embodiment of the chaotic, untamed power of the sea and the boundary between the known world of humans and the unknown. His dwelling in the ocean makes him a chthonic and aquatic force, tied to the deep and the primal elements.

Accounts I
The Binding of Loki's ChildrenProse Edda (Gylfaginning, ch. 34)

Mythic past

Odin, learning of the monstrous children of Loki and Angrboða, has them brought to Asgard. He throws Jörmungandr into the great ocean that encircles Midgard. The serpent grows so large that he can surround the Earth and grasp his own tail, thus becoming the World Serpent. This establishes Jörmungandr's role as a boundary of the world and sets the stage for his future conflict with Thor.

Accounts II
Thor's Fishing TripProse Edda (Gylfaginning, ch. 48) and Poetic Edda (Hymiskviða)

Mythic past

Thor, accompanied by the giant Hymir, goes fishing. Thor uses a giant's head as bait and rows far out to sea. He casts his line and hooks Jörmungandr. As the serpent thrashes, Thor pulls mightily, and for a moment, the two come face to face. Thor raises his hammer to strike, but Hymir, terrified, cuts the line, and the serpent sinks back into the depths. Thor is enraged and throws Hymir overboard. This is the most famous encounter between Thor and Jörmungandr, demonstrating their mutual enmity and the serpent's immense power. It also shows the cowardice of Hymir.

The Battle of RagnarökProse Edda (Gylfaginning, ch. 51) and Poetic Edda (Völuspá)

The End of the World (Future)

At Ragnarök, Jörmungandr releases his tail and emerges from the sea, flooding the world with his movements. He joins the forces of the giants and the dead against the gods. He meets Thor on the battlefield. Thor kills the serpent with his hammer, Mjölnir, but as the serpent dies, it spews its venom onto Thor. Thor takes nine steps before collapsing, dead from the poison. Both Thor and Jörmungandr die in this mutual destruction. This is the ultimate fulfillment of their feud and a key event in the downfall of the old world order.

The nature of the 'worm'Jörmungandr is a 'worm' (ormr) in the Old Norse sources, which can be interpreted as a serpent or a dragon.Modern depictions often portray him as a more traditional dragon or a massive sea serpent with limbs or a more draconic head.

Jörmungandr is a dragon in the traditional European sense, with wings and legs. — The Old Norse sources consistently describe him as a 'worm' (ormr) or serpent, without any mention of wings or legs. He is a giant snake, not a dragon.

Jörmungandr is evil and malicious. — Jörmungandr is a primordial force of nature. He is not described as actively evil, but rather as a dangerous, chaotic being that is part of the world's structure. His actions at Ragnarök are part of a prophesied cosmic event, not a personal vendetta.

The story of Thor's fishing trip is a simple tale of a hero fighting a monster. — The story is rich with symbolic meaning, often interpreted as a struggle between the ordered world (Thor) and the chaotic forces of the deep (Jörmungandr). It also highlights themes of pride, fear, and the limits of even the strongest gods.
