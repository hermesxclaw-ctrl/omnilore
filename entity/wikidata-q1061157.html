The Approach

From the banks of the Euphrates to the candlelit altars of Renaissance Europe, love magic has been a persistent and potent force in human culture—a way to bend desire, secure affection, or break a heart, using spells, amulets, and rituals that blur the line between sacred and profane.

Content advisory. Contains discussions of sexual themes, coercion, and historical practices that may be considered unethical or harmful. Some spells and rituals described involve attempts to manipulate others' emotions or actions.
Identity

Love magic

Pronunciation N/A

Known As

Etymology

The term 'love magic' is a modern scholarly construct; historical terms vary by language and period, such as Greek 'philtra' (love charms) and Latin 'veneficium' (poisoning/sorcery).

Cultural Ownership

Pan-cultural; attested in many cultures worldwide, including Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Greco-Roman, Jewish, Christian, and Islamic traditions.

Disputed Identifications

Scholars debate the precise definition and boundaries of love magic, including whether it includes purely sexual attraction, affection, or both, and whether it is distinct from other forms of magic.

Description

Appearance

Love magic itself has no appearance; its material components include dolls, talismans, amulets, scrolls, potions, and other objects, which vary by culture and period.

Symbols & Iconography

Common symbols include hearts, arrows, cords, knots, and images of the desired person. In Renaissance Europe, Christian elements like holy candles and the Host were incorporated.

Behaviour & Personality

Love magic is performed with intent to start, preserve, or break up a relationship. Practitioners may be men or women, clergy or laity. The practice is often secretive and may involve rituals, incantations, and the use of physical objects.

Domains & Habitats

Love magic is practiced in domestic settings, temples, churches, and other sacred spaces. It is found in both urban and rural contexts across cultures.

Version Differences

Practices vary significantly by culture and period: Mesopotamian rituals on cuneiform tablets, Egyptian love spells on ostraca, Greco-Roman spells in the Greek Magical Papyri, medieval Christian adaptations, and Renaissance blends of Christian and pagan elements.

Powers & Abilities

Erotic attraction (eros)

Source Version Hellenistic Greece (Faraone's classification)

Description

Spells to induce lust or sexual desire in a target, often used by men or prostitutes.

Limitations

Often considered aggressive and may cause harm to the caster.

Contradictions

Faraone's gender-based classification is questioned by scholars like Irene Salvo.

Affection and fidelity (philia)

Source Version Hellenistic Greece (Faraone's classification)

Description

Spells to create or maintain affection, love, or friendship, often used by women to keep husbands faithful.

Limitations

Considered non-aggressive, but may still involve compulsion.

Contradictions

Some men used philia spells and some women used eros spells, challenging the strict dichotomy.

Relationship preservation or dissolution

Source Version Cross-cultural

Description

Magic to start, preserve, or break up a relationship, as defined by scholars.

Limitations

Believed to cause severe damage to the caster; often expensive.

Contradictions

Effectiveness may be enhanced if the victim believes in the spell and submits.

Coercive spells

Source Greek Magical Papyri, cuneiform tablets

Description

Many love spells aim to compel a target to love or lust after the caster, which raises ethical concerns about consent.

Scholarly Context

Scholars debate the ethical implications and the extent to which targets were believed to have free will.

Association with prostitutes and courtesans

Source Ruggiero, Faraone

Description

Love magic was often associated with prostitutes and courtesans, particularly in the early modern period.

Scholarly Context

This association may have been a stereotype that influenced witch trials.

Misogyny in witch trials

Source Malleus Maleficarum (Kramer, 1487)

Description

The Malleus Maleficarum linked witchcraft to female sexuality, influencing persecution of women.

Scholarly Context

Modern scholars note this text reflects one author's views and may not represent widespread beliefs.

Contradictions & Disputes

Gender and practice

Position A

Love magic was primarily practiced by women (Faraone, Ruggiero).

Position A — evidence

Faraone's classification of eros vs. philia spells; trial records showing women accused.

Position B

Men were the main casters (Dickie, Rider).

Position B — evidence

Dickie's demographic analysis; pastoral manuals often leave practitioners ungendered or male.

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus; the practice likely varied by period and context.

Definition of love magic

Position A

Love magic includes spells for sexual attraction, affection, and relationship preservation.

Position A — evidence

Common theme of starting, preserving, or breaking up relationships.

Position B

Love magic is narrowly defined as spells specifically for romantic love, excluding purely sexual magic.

Position B — evidence

Some scholars distinguish between erotic magic and love magic.

Scholarly Consensus

Definition varies; no universal agreement.

Effectiveness

Position A

Love magic was believed to be effective if performed correctly.

Position A — evidence

Widespread practice and belief in magic.

Position B

Love magic was ineffective unless the victim believed in it and submitted.

Position B — evidence

Renaissance sources suggest victims often submitted to the enchantment.

Scholarly Consensus

Effectiveness is a matter of belief; modern scholars view it as psychological.

Research Scope

This dossier covers love magic as a cross-cultural phenomenon, focusing on attested historical practices from ancient Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Greco-Roman world, late antiquity, the European Middle Ages, and the Renaissance. Modern and contemporary practices are noted but not exhaustively covered. The research relies on scholarly sources and primary texts where available.
