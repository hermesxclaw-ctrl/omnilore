Who It Is

Most vampires bite, feed, and kill. Not this one. The yara-ma-yha-who — a little red man, barely four feet tall, with a head too large for its body, a mouth that is a toothless frog's gape, and suckers on its fingers and toes — cannot bite you, does not drain you dry, and does not want you dead. It wants you smaller. And redder. And, eventually, one of them. It lives in the crown of the wild fig tree and waits — utterly still among the leaves — for a weary traveller to rest in the shade below. Then it drops. It takes only enough blood to weaken you, swallows you whole, drinks water, naps, and spits you back out alive — shorter than before, your skin faintly redder, marked as its next meal. Because the horror of the yara-ma-yha-who is not a single attack: it is the repeat business. Powers
Sucker-feeding (blood-drain)

The yara-ma-yha-who drops from the fig canopy and uses the octopus-like suckers on its fingers and toes to drain a victim's blood. Crucially this is non-fatal: it takes only enough to weaken the prey, leaving the victim alive to be processed.

Limit: Only targets living, lively-appearing prey; a motionless 'corpse' (playing dead) is ignored. Active only during the day.

Whole-body swallowing and regurgitation

After draining the victim, the creature swallows them whole (its toothless gape is large enough), drinks water, and sleeps. On waking it regurgitates the victim alive and unharmed save for two effects: shorter stature and a reddening of the skin. This is the monster's signature move, unique in the global vampire catalogue — the victim is returned, not killed.

Limit: The creature is slow, full, and sleepy after a meal — an escape window for anyone rescued mid-process. It must nap between swallow and regurgitate.

Incremental transformation of prey

Each complete swallow/nap/regurgitate cycle leaves the victim shorter and redder. Repeated exposure — and the tales say the creature prefers previous customers — gradually transforms the victim into a yara-ma-yha-who itself: red, sucker-fingered, climbing the fig tree to wait for new travellers. This is the slowest, most patient conversion mechanism of any monster in the OmniLore archive, functioning by degrees rather than by a single bite or a single night.

Limit: Transformation requires repetition; a single encounter merely shrinks and tints the victim. Escape, group attendance, or playing dead breaks the cycle.

Canopy stealth / stillness

The creature hangs utterly still among the figs when hungry, blending into the canopy so a resting traveller does not notice it until it drops.

Limit: It cannot pursue actively — it is slow and plodding once fed. It hunts only by stationary ambush.

The defining anatomical trademark is the cluster of octopus-like suckers on the undersides of its fingers and toes, which it uses to drain a victim's blood. Symbols — There is no canonical Aboriginal iconographic form; the creature is not a totemic ancestor and lacks the rock-art register of beings like the Quinkan. Its symbol in the folklore catalogue is the wild fig tree (Ficus, often the strangler fig) — a canopy of literal, ecological strangulation — and the suckered hand it drops from the leaves. Domains — The crowns of wild fig trees in the eastern woodlands and lower-Murray country of southeastern Australia. The fig is ecologically a strangler of other trees, so the creature in the canopy is a strangler-of-travellers in a deliberate mythic-ecological echo.

Accounts I
The Traveller Who Rest in the Fig ShadeDavid Unaipon via Ramsay Smith, 'Myths & Legends of the Australian Aboriginals' (1930); republished under Unaipon's name

Deep tradition; recorded 20th century

A lone traveller, wearied by the heat of fig-and-eucalyptus country, rests in the shade of a wild fig tree. The yara-ma-yha-who, hanging motionless in the canopy, drops onto him. It drains blood through its suckers — not enough to kill, just enough to weaken — then with its huge toothless mouth swallows the man whole. It drinks water and lies down to sleep. When it wakes it regurgitates the man, alive but shorter and with a red tint rising in his skin, and the man flees, smaller and redder than before. The story's lesson is the ambush: never rest under a lone fig tree in such country. The victim survives but is diminished — shorter, redder, now marked as a known target. The tale encodes the practical warning about sleeping in fig shade.

Accounts II
The Repeat Customer Who Became One of ThemDavid Unaipon via Ramsay Smith (1930); reinforced in later summaries and the OmniLore entity record

Deep tradition; recorded 20th century

The most chilling extension of the myth. The tales say the yara-ma-yha-who prefers those it has already caught. Each time a particular victim is taken, swallowed, and regurgitated, they emerge a little shorter and a little redder. The process is repeatable, patient, unhurried. The 'horror is the repeat business': caught once, you are marked; caught again and again, you stop coming back out as yourself. Eventually the day comes when the creature does not regurgitate a person at all — what emerges (or what simply climbs up into the tree) is a yara-ma-yha-who, red, sucker-fingered, waiting in the figs for the next traveller. The victim has been converted by increments into the thing that caught them. Gradual, repeatable metamorphosis — transformation as a loyalty program, the slowest conversion in the archive.

The Playing-Dead Escape and the Daytime RuleUnaipon/Smith tradition, as summarised in the Wikipedia article and folklore lists

Deep tradition; documented 20th century

The tradition provides precise, child-calibrated counters. The yara-ma-yha-who is active only during the day and takes only the living-seeming; a victim who plays dead until sunset is not swallowed. If you must run, run as soon as it turns away — the creature is slow, full, and sleepy after a meal. The deeper instruction is preventive: wait until dark to move, or better, never travel alone under fig country, and never let a child stray from the group into the lone-tree shade. The creature is defeated by stillness and patience — the same virtues by which it hunts. Its diurnal schedule (daylight-only) inverts the normal vampire night rule.

The yara-ma-yha-who is the same being as the Brazilian water-siren 'Yara' (Iara). — They are unrelated. 'Yara' (Iara) is a Tupi/Amazonian water spirit; the yara-ma-yha-who is a land-dwelling tree vampire of southeastern Australia. The name-similarity is coincidental.

It drinks blood fatally, like a conventional vampire. — The drain is explicitly non-fatal — it takes only enough to weaken. Fatal consumption is not attested; the signature is the alive-return.

The creature is active at night. — The tradition states the opposite: it is active only during the day and takes only lively prey; playing dead until sunset defeats it.

William Ramsay Smith authored the legend. — Smith published it under his name in 1930, but the manuscript was authored by David Unaipon. Modern scholarship attributes it to Unaipon, and it is republished under his name.
