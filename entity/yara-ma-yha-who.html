Who It Is

The yara-ma-yha-who is one of the strangest predators in world folklore: a little red man, barely four feet tall, with an oversized head, a toothless frog-like mouth, and suckers on the ends of his fingers and toes, who lives in the crown of wild fig trees and waits for travelers to rest in the shade below. Then he drops. The suckers drain blood — not fatally, just enough to weaken — and then comes the signature move, unique in the global monster catalogue: he swallows the victim whole, walks about, drinks water, naps, and then regurgitates them, alive, slightly smaller than before and slightly redder. And walks away. The horror is the repeat business: caught again — and the tales say he prefers previous customers — you emerge smaller and redder still, and again, and again, until the day you are no longer being regurgitated as a person at all: you have become a yara-ma-yha-who, red, sucker-fingered, climbing the fig tree to wait. The tradition's advice is precise and child-calibrated: never rest under a lone fig tree; if taken, play dead until sundown (he only swallows the living-seeming); and if you must run, run — he is slow, full, and sleepy after a meal. It is the perfect cautionary predator: a monster that punishes carelessness not with death, but with gradual, repeatable transformation into the thing that caught you.

Origins

Eastern Aboriginal tradition recorded in the twentieth century, most fully by the ethnographer-showman William Ramsay Smith; the fig tree — ecologically a strangler of other trees — hosts a strangler of travelers, a neat mythic ecology.

Attested in Aboriginal oral traditionEra Deep tradition; recorded 20th century

Form

A red-skinned little man with a huge head, enormous toothless gape, and octopus-like suckers on fingers and toes; sated, he waddles; hungry, he hangs utterly still among the figs.

Sucker-draining (blood-feed) The creature affixes the suckers on its fingers and toes to a victim and drains blood, but deliberately not to the point of death — the feed leaves the person weak but alive. Southeastern Australian Aboriginal tradition as recorded by David Unaipon / Ramsay Smith The drain is non-fatal by design; the creature wants a live, partially-weakened victim, not a corpse. None attested; the non-fatal drain is consistent across retellings.

Whole-body swallowing Having weakened its prey, the yara-ma-yha-who swallows the person whole, drinks water, and sleeps. When it awakens it regurgitates the victim alive. Same tradition Requires the victim be alive and (apparently) unresisting. Playing dead defeats the feeding cycle. The mechanics of being swallowed and regurgitated alive are not scrutinised in traditional sources; it is simply asserted.

Regurgitative transformation (diminution + ruddiness) Each regurgitation renders the victim shorter than before and tints their skin a reddish colour it did not previously have. Repeated captures shrink and redden the victim further, until the day they are no longer regurgitated as a person but climb the fig tree as — and become — a yara-ma-yha-who. Same tradition Transformation is gradual and requirement-driven: it only proceeds through repeat capture. A victim who escapes or follows the survival rules never transforms. The precise number of cycles before full conversion is not fixed in the source; different retellings imply 'repeatedly' without an exact count.

Camouflaged stillness Hungry, the creature hangs utterly still among the figs, indistinct to the resting traveller below until it drops. Descriptive colour in the tradition (and in the archive's own summary) Conjectural framing; only effective against someone who has actually stopped to rest in the tree's shade. None.

Weakness: day-active, living-prey only, sluggish after feeding The creature is active only during the day and only targets living prey. 'Playing dead' until sunset is the traditional counter. It is also slow, heavy, and sleepy after a meal, so flight once it is gorged succeeds. Same tradition These restrictions define how the survival rules work; they are advantages for the potential victim, not vulnerabilities in the usual sense. None attested.

Sucker-feeding (blood-drain) The yara-ma-yha-who drops from the fig canopy and uses the octopus-like suckers on its fingers and toes to drain a victim's blood. Crucially this is non-fatal: it takes only enough to weaken the prey, leaving the victim alive to be processed. Unaipon/Smith tradition Only targets living, lively-appearing prey; a motionless 'corpse' (playing dead) is ignored. Active only during the day. None in the surviving print on the blood-drain step itself; the non-fatality is a consistent, distinguishing feature.

Whole-body swallowing and regurgitation After draining the victim, the creature swallows them whole (its toothless gape is large enough), drinks water, and sleeps. On waking it regurgitates the victim alive and unharmed save for two effects: shorter stature and a reddening of the skin. This is the monster's signature move, unique in the global vampire catalogue — the victim is returned, not killed. Unaipon/Smith tradition The creature is slow, full, and sleepy after a meal — an escape window for anyone rescued mid-process. It must nap between swallow and regurgitate. The exact mechanism (how a sleeping creature's stomach preserves a living human) is never explained; the tradition treats it as grotesque folk-physics rather than anatomy.

Incremental transformation of prey Each complete swallow/nap/regurgitate cycle leaves the victim shorter and redder. Repeated exposure — and the tales say the creature prefers previous customers — gradually transforms the victim into a yara-ma-yha-who itself: red, sucker-fingered, climbing the fig tree to wait for new travellers. This is the slowest, most patient conversion mechanism of any monster in the OmniLore archive, functioning by degrees rather than by a single bite or a single night. Unaipon/Smith tradition Transformation requires repetition; a single encounter merely shrinks and tints the victim. Escape, group attendance, or playing dead breaks the cycle. The tradition does not specify how many cycles complete the change, nor whether the victim retains memory of their former identity.

Canopy stealth / stillness The creature hangs utterly still among the figs when hungry, blending into the canopy so a resting traveller does not notice it until it drops. Unaipon/Smith tradition It cannot pursue actively — it is slow and plodding once fed. It hunts only by stationary ambush. None attested.

Weaknesses & Wards

He takes only the upright and lively: playing dead defeats him; he is slow and sleepy after feeding; and children who stay with the group under open sky are outside his hunting method entirely.

Accounts

A lone traveller, wearied by the heat of fig-and-eucalyptus country, rests in the shade of a wild fig tree. The yara-ma-yha-who, hanging motionless in the canopy, drops onto him. It drains blood through its suckers — not enough to kill, just enough to weaken — then with its huge toothless mouth swallows the man whole. It drinks water and lies down to sleep. When it wakes it regurgitates the man, alive but shorter and with a red tint rising in his skin, and the man flees, smaller and redder than before. The story's lesson is the ambush: never rest under a lone fig tree in such country.

The most chilling extension of the myth. The tales say the yara-ma-yha-who prefers those it has already caught. Each time a particular victim is taken, swallowed, and regurgitated, they emerge a little shorter and a little redder. The process is repeatable, patient, unhurried. The 'horror is the repeat business': caught once, you are marked; caught again and again, you stop coming back out as yourself. Eventually the day comes when the creature does not regurgitate a person at all — what emerges (or what simply climbs up into the tree) is a yara-ma-yha-who, red, sucker-fingered, waiting in the figs for the next traveller. The victim has been converted by increments into the thing that caught them.

The tradition provides precise, child-calibrated counters. The yara-ma-yha-who is active only during the day and takes only the living-seeming; a victim who plays dead until sunset is not swallowed. If you must run, run as soon as it turns away — the creature is slow, full, and sleepy after a meal. The deeper instruction is preventive: wait until dark to move, or better, never travel alone under fig country, and never let a child stray from the group into the lone-tree shade.

Cultural Significance

A masterpiece of pedagogical monster-design: the rules (avoid lone fig trees, stay with the group, keep still if caught) encode real bushcraft for children in fig-and-heat country.

A way to hold it in your mind

Of all the predators in global folklore, the yara-ma-yha-who is the only one that does not want to kill you — it wants to keep you. It is the little red frog-man of the southeastern Australian fig trees who drops from the crown of a wild fig onto a resting traveller, drains their blood through suckers on its fingers and toes, swallows them whole, takes a nap, and then — lower your expectations for mercy — throws them back up slightly smaller and slightly redder than they were before. And then walks away, patient as the strangler-fig it lives in, counting on a repeat customer. Because the real horror of this creature is not the single meal: it is the mathematics of the return visit. Caught once, you shrink. Caught twice, you redden. Caught enough times, you stop being regurgitated as a person at all, and climb down into the tree to wait with the rest of them. It is, by any measure, a masterpiece of monster-design — and it belongs, first and most importantly, to a real man whose name the printed record forgot for half a century.
