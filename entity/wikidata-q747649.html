In the Shahnameh, his kingdom is the whole world, as he is the supreme tyrant. He is also associated with Mount Damavand, where he is ultimately imprisoned, and with the concept of foreign or 'evil' rule. In Zoroastrian cosmology, he is a counter-creation of Angra Mainyu, belonging to the sphere of darkness and the Lie.

Form

In the oldest Avestan sources, Azhi Dahaka is a monstrous, three-headed, three-mouthed, six-eyed serpent-dragon with a thousand senses (or a thousand 'perceptions'). He is described as having 'poisonous fangs' and being 'the great serpent'. In Middle Persian texts, he is also described as a three-headed dragon, sometimes with the heads being those of a man, a lion, and a dragon. In Ferdowsi's Shahnameh, he is a human king, handsome and powerful, but with two black serpents growing from his shoulders where Ahriman kissed him. These serpents are his defining feature and the source of his monstrous appetite.

Powers

Monstrous Form (Avestan/Azhdaha) As a dragon, he is a colossal, three-headed serpent with immense physical strength, poisonous fangs, and the ability to breathe fire (in some interpretations). He is a match for heroes and armies. Avesta, Middle Persian texts He is vulnerable to the hero Thraetaona/Fereydun, who is blessed by the divine glory (xvarənah). He is ultimately bound, not killed, suggesting he is nearly indestructible. The extent of his fire-breathing is not explicit in the earliest texts, but is a common later interpretation.

Shape-shifting (later tradition) In some later Middle Persian and Islamic-era texts, Azhdaha/Zahhak is said to be able to change his shape, often to deceive or escape. Middle Persian texts, post-Shahnameh folklore Not a prominent feature in Ferdowsi's version. None significant.

Demonic Pact (Shahnameh/Zahhak) His power comes from his pact with Ahriman (Iblis). Ahriman grants him the serpents on his shoulders, which require a daily sacrifice of two young men's brains. This pact is the source of his tyrannical power. Shahnameh He is dependent on Ahriman's favor and the daily sacrifice. This dependence is also his weakness, as it makes him hated by his people. This is a literary elaboration by Ferdowsi, not present in the Avestan sources.

Longevity He rules for a thousand years, an unnaturally long reign that marks him as a demonic or semi-divine figure. Middle Persian texts, Shahnameh His reign is eventually ended by Fereydun. None.

Behavior & Habitat

Azhdaha is the embodiment of the Lie (Druj) and tyranny. He is a deceiver, a usurper, and a destroyer. In his dragon form, he is a force of chaos that threatens the cosmic order. In his human form (Zahhak), he is a cunning and ambitious tyrant who murders his father, is seduced by evil, and rules through fear and cruelty. He is characterized by an insatiable hunger, both for power and, literally, for the brains of the young men of his kingdom. He is also a coward at heart, as shown when he tries to avoid his final confrontation with Fereydun.

Accounts

The hero Thraetaona (later Fereydun) sacrifices to the goddess Ardvi Sura Anahita and to the wind god Vayu to gain the strength to defeat Azhi Dahaka. The divine glory (xvarənah) that had been with the dragon flees from him. Thraetaona defeats the three-headed serpent and binds him in chains, imprisoning him on a mountain (later identified as Damavand).

Zahhak is the son of a virtuous king, but is seduced by Ahriman, who appears as a cook and feeds him delicious meats. In gratitude, Zahhak allows Ahriman to kiss his shoulders. From the kisses, two black serpents grow. Cutting them off is futile; they grow back. Ahriman, disguised as a physician, tells Zahhak that the serpents must be fed with the brains of two young men each day. Zahhak then murders his father and seizes the throne, beginning a thousand-year reign of terror. His kingdom is marked by injustice and fear. Eventually, the blacksmith Kaveh, whose sons have been taken for the serpents, leads a public rebellion with his leather apron as a standard (the Derafsh Kaviani). He rallies the people and the hidden prince Fereydun, who marches against Zahhak. Fereydun defeats him but does not kill him; he binds him and imprisons him in a cave on Mount Damavand, where he hangs, his heart gnawed by the serpents, for eternity.

After his defeat, Zahhak/Azhdaha is not killed but bound and hung in a cave on Mount Damavand. The chains are said to be driven into the rock. He is destined to remain there until the end of time, when he will break free and be finally slain by the hero Garshasp (or in some versions, by the Saoshyant, the savior).

Scholarly Debates
Form: Dragon vs. Human King
Azhdaha is a monstrous three-headed dragon (Avestan and Middle Persian texts). ⇌ Zahhak is a human king with serpent-shoulders (Shahnameh and later tradition).
Evidence for A: The Avesta and Pahlavi texts consistently describe him as a dragon.
Evidence for B: Ferdowsi's Shahnameh, the most influential text, describes him as a human king.
Final Fate: Killed vs. Bound
Azhdaha is bound and imprisoned in Mount Damavand, not killed. ⇌ He is slain by the hero.

What We Don't Know

A full review of the Armenian sources (Movses Khorenatsi) in the original language is needed. A more detailed survey of the reception of the myth in Kurdish and Central Asian folklore is also a gap. The specific iconographic history of the dragon form in Sasanian art requires further study. The exact relationship between the Avestan 'Aži Dahāka' and the Vedic 'Ahi' (serpent) Vritra should be explored in more depth.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Azhdaha/Zahhak as the original 'fallen hero' archetype, a thousand years before Darth Vader. He is the cautionary tale of how a good person can be corrupted by evil, and how tyranny feeds on the innocent. He is also the ultimate 'imprisoned monster' — imagine a dragon so powerful that the gods couldn't kill it, only chain it under a volcano, where it waits, gnawing at its bonds, for the end of the world.
