Who It Is

Avalokiteshvara (Avalokiteśvara) is the bodhisattva who embodies the compassion of all Buddhas—arguably the most widely venerated figure in Mahayana Buddhism, known across Asia by many names: Guanyin (China), Kannon (Japan), Chenrezig (Tibet), Quan Am (Vietnam), and Lokeshvara (Southeast Asia). His Sanskrit name means 'The Lord Who Looks Down [with compassion] Upon the World.' According to Mahayana scripture, Avalokiteshvara made a great vow before the Buddha Amitabha that he would not enter final nirvana until all sentient beings without exception had been liberated from suffering. When he realized the infinite number of beings and the seeming impossibility of his task, his head shattered into eleven pieces from the anguish. Amitabha reassembled the pieces into eleven heads (allowing him to see suffering in all directions) and gave him a thousand arms (so he could reach out to all beings simultaneously), each palm bearing an eye of wisdom. This thousand-armed, eleven-headed form (Sahasrabhuja Avalokiteshvara) is one of his most iconic representations. The Heart Sutra, one of the most important texts in all of Mahayana Buddhism, is a discourse between Avalokiteshvara and Shariputra on the nature of emptiness (shunyata). The mantra 'Om Mani Padme Hum' is his six-syllable mantra, believed to contain the essence of all Buddhist teachings. In East Asia, Avalokiteshvara underwent a remarkable gender transformation, becoming the female Guanyin, the 'Goddess of Mercy'—a development that began in China during the Tang dynasty and was fully established by the Song. Guanyin is often depicted in a flowing white robe, holding a vase of pure water and a willow branch, and is especially invoked by women seeking children, sailors facing storms, and all who suffer. In Tibetan Buddhism, Avalokiteshvara is considered the patron deity of Tibet, with the Dalai Lamas regarded as his human emanations.
