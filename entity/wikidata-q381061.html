Form

Aequitas is almost exclusively known from her depiction on Roman coins. She is typically shown as a mature, dignified female figure, standing or occasionally seated. Her standard attributes are the balance scale (libra) held in her right hand and a cornucopia (horn of plenty) in her left. She is often depicted with a long robe (stola) and sometimes a diadem or a veil. On some issues, she is shown holding a scepter instead of a cornucopia. Her appearance is consistent with other personifications of virtues (such as Liberalitas or Moneta), presenting an idealized, serene, and matronly figure.

Powers

Embodiment of Justice and Fairness As a personification, Aequitas's 'power' is conceptual. She represents the principle that makes legal and social interactions fair and balanced. In Roman thought, she was a quality of the state, not an agent who intervened in mortal affairs. Roman legal and philosophical texts She has no agency in any surviving myth. She is a symbolic figure, not an active deity.

Imperial Endorsement (Aequitas Augusti) In her role as Aequitas Augusti, she is a divine endorsement of the emperor's just rule. She signifies that the emperor's decisions and the economic policies of the empire are fair and divinely sanctioned. Imperial coinage and propaganda This is a political and symbolic function, not a supernatural one.

Guarantor of Honest Measure Her association with the libra (scale) makes her the patroness of fair weights and measures. This was a crucial function in the Roman economy, which relied on standardized weights for trade and taxation. There is no evidence of a dedicated cult or priesthood for Aequitas.

Behavior & Habitat

As a personification, Aequitas has no myths or narratives of her own. Her 'behavior' is encapsulated in her attributes: she weighs (the balance scale) and she provides (the cornucopia). She represents the ideal of fair exchange and honest measure. In the imperial context, she is not a dynamic or active deity but a static symbol of a quality the emperor possesses or bestows upon his people.

Accounts

Cicero, in his work on rhetorical argumentation, offers a philosophical framework for aequitas. He divides it into three parts: the first concerns our duty to the gods (pietas), the second our duty to the dead (sanctitas), and the third our duty to the living (iustitia). This is not a myth but a foundational philosophical definition that shaped how Romans understood the concept.

Aequitas Augusti is a recurring legend on imperial coins. This is a story of propaganda: the emperor, by associating himself with Aequitas, claims that his rule is just, his taxes are fair, and his reign brings prosperity (hence the cornucopia). The story is told not in text but in the ubiquitous imagery of the coin, which was seen by millions across the empire.

What We Don't Know

While her numismatic and legal significance is well-documented, there is a notable gap in our knowledge of any specific cult practices, temples, or priesthoods dedicated to Aequitas. The lack of a surviving myth or narrative makes it difficult to assess her 'personality' beyond her symbolic attributes. A deeper study of the specific coin types and their distribution across the empire could reveal more about how her image was used in different regions and periods. The relationship between the Roman concept of aequitas and the Greek concept of epieikeia deserves a more detailed comparative analysis.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Aequitas as the 'corporate logo' of the Roman Empire. She wasn't a goddess with stories and powers; she was a symbol of a core brand promise: 'Our rule is fair, and our empire is prosperous because of it.' She was the image on the official currency, the seal of approval on imperial policy, and the visual shorthand for everything the emperor wanted his subjects to believe about his reign.
