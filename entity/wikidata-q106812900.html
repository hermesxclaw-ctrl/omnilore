Who It Is

Before the sun rose, before the first hill of sand emerged from the Nun, there were eight — four frogs and four serpents, the Ogdoad, whose names whispered of chaos, darkness, and the hidden forces that would shape the world.

As primordial forces, the Ogdoad were not active personalities but rather personifications of abstract concepts: water, infinity, darkness, and hiddenness. They were believed to have created the world by bringing forth the sun god (often Atum or Ra) from the primeval waters, who then created the rest of the cosmos. They were also associated with the cycle of day and night, and with the annual Nile flood. In later periods, they were invoked in rituals for protection and resurrection.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The English term 'Ogdoad' derives from Greek ὀγδοάς (ogdoas), meaning 'eightfold' or 'group of eight'. The ancient Egyptian name for the group was ḫmnw, possibly meaning 'the eight', which also gave the name to the city of Hermopolis (Khemenu).

Pronunciation — og-DOH-ad (English); reconstructed Egyptian: kh-MEN-oo (for the group name).

Powers
Primordial Creation

The Ogdoad collectively represented the fundamental elements of the pre-creation universe: water, infinity, darkness, and hiddenness. They were believed to have brought forth the sun god through their interaction, leading to the creation of the world.

Limit: They are not usually depicted as actively creating the world themselves; rather, they are the raw material or the catalysts for creation.

Control over Chaos

As embodiments of chaos, the Ogdoad could influence the primordial waters, darkness, and infinite force. They were sometimes invoked to protect against chaos and disorder.

Limit: Their power was largely passive; they were not typically portrayed as intervening in the world.

Resurrection and Rebirth

The Ogdoad were associated with the cycle of rebirth, particularly in the afterlife. The deceased could be identified with the Ogdoad to gain resurrection.

Limit: This power was available only to the deceased who knew the appropriate spells.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The Ogdoad were typically depicted as four male deities with frog heads and four female deities with serpent heads. The males were often shown as frogs, and the females as serpents or cobras. In some depictions, they appear as entirely human or as anthropomorphic figures with animal heads. Symbols — The frog and serpent were their primary symbols, representing fertility, rebirth, and primordial chaos. The Ogdoad were also associated with the primordial hill (benben) and the lotus flower, from which the sun god emerged. In art, they are often shown as four pairs of frogs and serpents, or as a group of eight deities standing in a row. The number eight itself was sacred, representing completeness and cosmic order.

Domains — The Ogdoad resided in the primordial chaos (Nun), the watery abyss that existed before creation. They were also associated with the underworld (Duat) and with the region of Hermopolis, where they were worshipped. In some texts, they are said to dwell in the 'Island of Flames' or the 'Lake of Two Knives', mythological locations in the afterlife.

Accounts I
The Hermopolitan Creation MythCoffin Texts, Book of the Dead, and temple inscriptions

Old Kingdom to Greco-Roman period (c. 2400 BCE – 300 CE)

Before creation, there was only Nun, the primeval watery abyss. Within Nun, the Ogdoad existed as four pairs of frogs and serpents, representing the fundamental forces of chaos. They interacted in the darkness, and through their union, they generated a primeval mound (benben) or a lotus flower, from which the sun god emerged. The sun god then created the world and all living things. The creation of the world, the establishment of cosmic order (maat), and the cycle of day and night.

Accounts II
The Ogdoad in the AfterlifeBook of the Dead, Chapter 17

New Kingdom onwards (c. 1550 BCE)

In this funerary text, the deceased declares, 'I am the Ogdoad, the eight who are in the Abyss.' The Ogdoad are invoked to grant the deceased power over the elements and to ensure a successful journey through the underworld. The deceased gains resurrection and eternal life.

Plutarch's InterpretationPlutarch, 'De Iside et Osiride'

c. 100 CE

Plutarch, a Greek philosopher, described the Ogdoad as the first gods, children of Chaos, and equated them with the Greek deities of the underworld. He saw them as representing the chaotic forces that preceded order. This interpretation influenced later Greco-Roman understanding of Egyptian religion.
