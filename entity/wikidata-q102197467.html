The Approach

In the shadowed halls of Greek myth, where the boundaries between waking and dream blur, there exists a figure whose very name conjures the specter of nightmares. Ikelos, the daimon of terrifying visions, stands as a testament to the ancient Greeks' profound understanding of the human psyche's darker corners.

Content advisory. Contains themes of nightmares, psychological terror, and mythological depictions of fear.
Identity

Ikelos

Pronunciation IK-eh-los (Classical Greek: /í.ke.los/), also pronounced as FOH-beh-tor for the alternative name.

Known As

PhobetorIcelos

Etymology

The name Ikelos (Ἴκελος) is derived from the Greek word 'eikōn' or 'eikelos', meaning 'like' or 'resembling', reflecting his ability to assume forms that resemble reality. The alternative name Phobetor (Φοβήτωρ) means 'the frightener' or 'the terrifying one'.

Cultural Ownership

Ancient Greek, later adopted into Roman mythology as Phobetor.

Disputed Identifications

Some sources conflate Ikelos with other dream spirits such as Morpheus or Phantasos; the precise distinctions among the Oneiroi are not consistently maintained in ancient texts.

Description

Appearance

Ikelos is typically depicted as a winged daimon, often shown in the company of other Oneiroi. He is said to be able to take the forms of animals, birds, and serpents, and is sometimes portrayed as a dark, shadowy figure.

Symbols & Iconography

His symbols include the poppy, associated with sleep, and the horn through which false dreams pass. He is often depicted with wings, representing the fleeting nature of dreams.

Behaviour & Personality

Ikelos is a personification of nightmares, appearing in dreams to terrify mortals. He is one of the thousand sons of Hypnos, the god of sleep, and serves his father by bringing troubling visions to sleepers.

Domains & Habitats

He resides in the underworld, in the cave of Hypnos, where the river Lethe flows and poppies grow. From there, he and his brothers travel to the mortal world to deliver dreams.

Version Differences

Ovid names him Icelos among the gods but Phobetor among mortals, and assigns him the power to transform into beasts. In later traditions, the names and functions of the Oneiroi vary, with some sources attributing different powers to different figures.

Powers & Abilities

Shapeshifting

Source Version Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.640-643

Description

Ikelos can take the form of any animal, bird, or serpent, and appears in dreams in these forms to frighten sleepers.

Limitations

He only appears in dreams and cannot manifest in the waking world.

Contradictions

Some sources attribute this power to Phantasos instead, or conflate the two.

Dream Manipulation

Source Version General mythological tradition

Description

As a daimon of nightmares, Ikelos can influence the content of dreams, causing terrifying visions.

Limitations

His influence is limited to the dream state and does not directly affect waking life.

Contradictions

The extent of his power over dreams is not consistently described in ancient sources.

Stories & Myths

The Birth of the Oneiroi

Source Homer, Iliad (implied), Ovid, MetamorphosesCulture GreekPeriod Archaic to Classical

Summary

The Oneiroi, the dream spirits, are born from Hypnos, the god of sleep, and are responsible for delivering dreams to mortals. They are numerous, with Ovid mentioning a thousand sons.

Participants

Hypnos

Ikelos

Morpheus

Phantasos

Consequences

The Oneiroi serve as intermediaries between the gods and mortals, often conveying messages through dreams.

Variants

In Homer, dreams are personified as a single dream figure or as messengers from Zeus, not as a group of spirits.

The House of Sleep

Source Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.592-643Culture RomanPeriod 1st century AD

Summary

Ovid describes the cave of Sleep, where Hypnos resides surrounded by poppies and the river Lethe. Among his sons, Ikelos is noted for his ability to transform into beasts, and is called Phobetor by mortals.

Participants

Hypnos

Ikelos

Morpheus

Phantasos

Consequences

This passage establishes the distinct roles of the dream gods, with Ikelos specializing in animal forms.

Variants

Later authors, such as Statius, offer different descriptions of the dream gods' dwelling.

Relationships

Hypnos

Version Note

Ovid is the primary source for this genealogy.

Conflicting Accounts

Homer does not mention the Oneiroi as a group, so the father-son relationship is a later development.

Morpheus is his brother, who specializes in imitating human forms.

Version Note

Ovid distinguishes their functions.

Conflicting Accounts

Some later sources confuse the two, attributing human form imitation to Ikelos.

Phantasos

Type siblingSource Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.641-643

Description

Phantasos is his brother, who appears as inanimate objects in dreams.

Version Note

Ovid assigns different domains to each brother.

Conflicting Accounts

Later mythographers sometimes swap the powers of Phantasos and Ikelos.

Nightmares and Psychological Terror

Source Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.640-643

Description

Ikelos personifies nightmares, which in ancient belief could be sent by gods to torment mortals, sometimes as punishment or as omens of doom.

Scholarly Context

Modern psychology interprets nightmares as manifestations of anxiety, but in antiquity they were often seen as supernatural interventions.

Contradictions & Disputes

Name and Function

Position A

Ikelos is the name used by the gods, while Phobetor is the name used by mortals, and he transforms into beasts.

Position A — evidence

Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.640-643

Position B

Some later sources attribute the transformation into beasts to Phantasos, and human forms to Ikelos.

Position B — evidence

Later mythographers, such as Fulgentius, who may have misread Ovid.

Scholarly Consensus

Scholars generally follow Ovid's account as the most authoritative, but note the fluidity of the Oneiroi's identities in later traditions.

Number of Oneiroi

Position A

There are a thousand sons of Hypnos, as stated by Ovid.

Position A — evidence

Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.633-634

Position B

Homer refers to dreams as a singular entity or as messengers from Zeus, not as a group.

Position B — evidence

Homer, Iliad 2.6-34

Scholarly Consensus

The concept of the Oneiroi as a group is a later development, with Ovid being the first to enumerate them.
