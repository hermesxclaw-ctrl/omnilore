Who It Is

Before Pegasus ever spread his wings over Greece, the Eurasian steppe had already invented its own sky-steed. Tulpar is the Turkic world's winged horse — a creature born not from a gorgon's spilled blood but from the practical marriage of the two animals that made the steppe livable: the horse that carried the hunter and the falcon that hunted from above. Across the Altai Mountains, the Kazakh steppe, the Bashkir Urals, and the Turkish Anatolian plateau, this same winged form appears: sometimes as a mount for a shaman's spirit-journey, sometimes as the steed of a nation being born. Tulpar is not just a mythic curiosity — it is the living emblem of Kazakhstan's independence, the namesake of a Turkish tank, and a train that crosses the steppe faster than any horse could gallop.

Tulpar functions primarily as a mount for culture heroes, shamans, and divine beings rather than as an independent agent. In Turkic oral epic it appears at moments of crisis or transition — carrying a hero across impassable terrain, ferrying between the earthly realm and the upper world (Uçmag) of Tengri, or serving as the steed of a shaman during spirit-journeys (kamlar ibadeti). Unlike Pegasus, no surviving Turkic source attributes Tulpar with an independent will or voice; it is an extension of the rider's spiritual power. Etymology — From Proto-Turkic *tul- (fast, swift) + *par (horse): literally 'swift horse'. The root tul- carries connotations of speed, flight, and celestial motion, cognate with Turkish uçmak (to fly). The form Толпар appears in Bashkir and Tatar transliteration. Powers
Celestial Flight

Tulpar's defining ability is flight across both physical and spiritual realms. It traverses the distance between the earthly steppe and the upper world (Uçmag) of Tengri, carrying shamans on kam (spirit-journey) flights and heroes across geographic barriers. The wings are described as eagle or falcon wings — connecting Tulpar to the same raptor symbolism that marks the sky god's messengers. Flight is not merely physical: in shamanic contexts, riding Tulpar enables passage between worlds of the dead, the living, and the divine.

Limit:

Speed Beyond Measure

Even without flight, Tulpar is the fastest of horses — a trait encoded in its very name (Proto-Turkic *tul-, swift). It can cross the steppe in a single day, outrun any mortal steed, and reach destinations that would take caravans weeks. This speed reflects the real importance of the horse in Central Asian nomadic mobility: the Göktürk messengers could reportedly cover 500+ km across the steppe, and the Tulpar myth encodes that cultural reality as supernatural capacity.

Limit:

Spiritual Conveyance

Tulpar serves as the mount for shamans (kam) during kamlar ibadeti — the shamanic ritual in which the practitioner journeys to the upper world to consult with Ülgen or other tngri, or descends to the lower world governed by Erlik. In this function it parallels the Norse Sleipnir (Odin's eight-legged steed that traverses the nine worlds) and the Greek Pegasus (who carries Bellerophon to Olympus). It is not a creature that acts on its own initiative but a spiritual vehicle whose availability signals the shaman's legitimacy.

Limit:

Divine Favor Indicator

Appearance of a Tulpar — or the gift of riding one — is interpreted as a sign of celestial approval or selection by Tengri. In foundation myths (Ergenekon, Asena cluster), the winged horse appears to the culture-hero at the moment of collective rebirth or emergence, confirming that the people are under divine protection. This makes Tulpar a theophanic marker rather than merely a mount.

Limit:

Accounts I
The Ergenekon Emergence

The Epic of Ergenekon tells that the ancient Turkic peoples were trapped for four centuries in a mountain valley (Ergenekon) after a military defeat. A blacksmith melted a mountain of iron to create a passage, and when the people emerged, they rode out on Tulpars to reconquer the steppe. The winged horse here functions as the mount of national rebirth — the Turkic peoples' emergence from confinement into empire. This is the closest thing to a canonical 'Tulpar story' in the Turkic tradition, and it directly ties the creature to the founding moment of Turkic political identity.

The Falcon and the Mare: Origin of the Winged Horse

According to the most widely cited origin narrative (Wikipedia, flagged as needing verification), Tulpar was born from the human imagination merging two animals central to Central Asian hunting culture: the horse and the falconry bird. In the open steppe, hunters depended on both — the horse for mobility and the hunting bird (typically a golden eagle, bürküt, or falcon) for taking down game. Over generations of storytelling, these two indispensable partners fused into a single creature: a horse with the wings of a raptor. This origin story explains why Tulpar is distinct from Pegasus: it does not spring from a theogonic violence (Medusa's blood) but from the practical symbiosis of steppe life.

Accounts II
The Shaman's Night Flight

In Turkic shamanic practice, the kam (shaman) undertakes kamlar ibadeti — a ritual trance-journey to the upper world. The shaman mounts Tulpar and rides through the layers of the sky to reach Ülgen, the good creator god, or to retrieve a lost soul. The journey requires crossing the World Tree (Ağaç Ana) and passing through gates guarded by spirits. Tulpar's wings are said to make a sound like wind through an eagle's feathers during this journey, audible to those outside the trance as a rushing noise. This narrative is not a single literary text but a ritual scenario attested across Siberian and Central Asian shamanic traditions.

Tulpar and the Oghuz Khagan

The Oghuz Khagan is the legendary progenitor of the Oghuz Turkic tribes (ancestors of modern Turks, Azerbaijanis, and Turkmens). In some Oghuz narrative cycles, the Khagan receives a Tulpar as a gift from Tengri at the moment of his coronation or military triumph. The winged horse becomes the symbol of his divine right to rule. This story links Tulpar directly to political legitimacy in Turkic tradition — a motif that continues into modern heraldry (Kazakhstan, Bashkortostan).
