Who They Are

Luna was the Roman goddess of the moon, the female counterpart to Sol, the sun god. Her temple on the Aventine Hill, according to Roman tradition, was built by King Servius Tullius. She was identified with the Greek Selene and, increasingly in the imperial period, with Diana and Juno Lucina as a triple moon goddess. Luna's significance in Roman religion was primarily calendrical: the Roman calendar was originally lunar, and the pontiffs announced the start of each month by observing the new moon (hence 'calendar' from kalendae, the day of the new moon's first visible crescent).

Luna was depicted driving a biga (two-horse chariot) across the night sky, her head crowned with a crescent moon. She is often shown veiled, holding a torch, the billowing arc of her veil forming a crescent around her head — a motif that became standard in Roman art. As Luna Noctiluca ('Luna Who Shines by Night'), she illuminated the darkness. Roman writers described her nightly journey: as Sol descended into the western ocean, Luna rose from the east to begin her course. Her speed was less than Sol's — the moon's visible motion across the sky — making her chariot a biga rather than a quadriga.

Luna's mythology was thoroughly merged with Diana's as the triple goddess Luna-Diana-Hecate (or Luna-Diana-Proserpina), representing the moon in heaven, the huntress on earth, and the sorceress in the underworld. In this guise, she was both the serene lamp of the night sky and the dark goddess of crossroads magic. The Roman poet Propertius and the satirist Juvenal both reference witches who claimed to be able to 'draw down the moon' (deducere lunam) — a magical feat attributed to Thessalian witches who could compel Luna from her celestial path.

The relationship between Luna and Endymion — a beautiful shepherd granted eternal sleep and youth by Jupiter, visited nightly by the moon goddess — was a popular subject in Roman art, particularly on sarcophagi, where it symbolized the sleep of death and the hope of eternal union with the divine. Luna's cycle — waxing, full, waning, dark — made her the natural patroness of women's menstrual cycles and the rhythms of conception and birth. Her temple on the Aventine was near the temple of Diana, and the two goddesses were often honored together. The Vestal Virgins included Luna in their nightly prayers, as she was linked to the hearth-fire's eternal vigilance through the night.

Form

In Roman art, Luna is typically depicted as a beautiful woman with a crescent moon on her head or a lunar diadem, often driving a two-horse chariot (biga) across the night sky. She is frequently shown with a torch or veil. Unlike Greek Selene's more consistently serene depictions, Roman Luna could appear in both gentle and more martial or majestic forms, sometimes with a billowing cloak representing the night sky. On coins, she is often shown with a crescent behind her shoulders, a motif that became standard in imperial iconography.

Powers

Lunar Control Luna drives the moon across the night sky, controlling its phases and its light. She can dim or brighten the moon's glow at will. Attested in Roman poetry and hymns (e.g., Horace, Ovid) She is bound to the cosmic order (fatum); she cannot change the fundamental cycle of the moon, only its presentation. None known; her role as moon-charioteer is consistent across sources.

Influence over Women's Cycles As a goddess of the moon, Luna governs the menstrual cycle and, by extension, fertility and childbirth. She was invoked by women for safe delivery and regular cycles. Attested in Roman religious practice and later in Macrobius' synthesis Her influence is tied to the physical moon; she is not a goddess of healing or midwifery in the same way as Diana or Juno Lucina, though she is often invoked alongside them. The exact relationship between Luna, Diana, and Juno in childbirth is debated; some sources conflate them, others keep them distinct.

Dream Weaving Luna is associated with dreams and the night's visions. She can send prophetic or meaningful dreams to mortals, particularly those who sleep under the open sky. Inferred from her association with the night and later syncretic texts; not a prominent attested power in early Roman sources Dream influence is indirect and often symbolic rather than literal; she does not appear in dreams as a speaking character in most myths. This power is more strongly associated with other deities (e.g., Somnus, Morpheus); Luna's role is more as a patron of the dream state than a direct sender of visions.

Night Illumination Luna provides light in the darkness, guiding travelers and revealing what is hidden. She is a light-bringer of the night, contrasting with her brother Sol's daytime light. Attested in hymns and poetic invocations Her light is reflected, not her own; she cannot illuminate the underworld or places the moon does not shine. None.

Timekeeping and Calendar Authority Luna governs the month (mensis, from 'mensis' meaning month, related to 'moon'). Her phases mark the passage of time, and her festival on the Ides of March anchors the Roman calendar. Attested in Varro, Ovid, and Macrobius She is a marker of time, not a goddess of fate or destiny; she does not control the length of human life. The Roman calendar was primarily solar in its civil form, but lunar elements persisted; Luna's role in timekeeping is more symbolic than administrative.

Behavior & Habitat

Luna is a celestial charioteer, driving her horses through the night sky to mark the passage of time. She is a witness to the night's events, a guardian of women's cycles, and a bringer of dreams. She is generally benevolent but can be associated with the mysterious, the occult, and the liminal. Her behavior in myth is largely passive — she is more a cosmic force than a character with complex motivations, unlike the more active deities of Olympus.

Accounts

Luna drives her two-horse chariot across the night sky, marking the passage of hours and seasons. She is the sister of Sol (the Sun), who drives his own chariot by day. The two are the celestial markers of time's passage, their daily and nightly journeys framing the lives of mortals.

Luna falls in love with the mortal shepherd Endymion, who sleeps eternally on Mount Latmus. She visits him each night, gazing upon his beauty. This story is more prominently associated with Greek Selene, but Roman poets adopted it, sometimes using the name Luna.

Ovid records a festival of Luna on the Ides of March (March 15), held at her temple on the Palatine Hill. This festival is one of the few Roman religious observances explicitly dedicated to Luna, and it connects her to the beginning of the agricultural year and the return of spring.

Scholarly Debates
Luna vs. Diana
Luna and Diana are the same goddess, with Diana being the Roman name for the moon goddess. ⇌ Luna and Diana are distinct deities with separate origins and cults; their conflation is a late literary and religious development.
Evidence for A: Catullus (Carmen 34) invokes Diana as Luna; later mystery cults and Macrobius explicitly identify them.
Evidence for B: Varro lists them separately in the di consentes; Luna has her own temple on the Palatine, distinct from Diana's on the A

What We Don't Know

1) The exact location and architectural history of the Temple of Luna on the Palatine remain uncertain; further archaeological evidence is needed. 2) The specific rituals of Luna's festival on the Ides of March are poorly attested; more epigraphic or literary evidence would clarify her cult practice. 3) The degree and timing of Luna's conflation with Diana and Hecate are debated; a more detailed study of imperial-era inscriptions and magical texts could refine the timeline. 4) Luna's role in women's religious practice, particularly in comparison to Juno Lucina and Diana, is understudied; more evidence from votive deposits and dedicatory inscriptions is needed. 5) The relationship between Luna and the Etruscan deity Losna is not fully understood; comparative Etruscan-Roman studies could illuminate this connection.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Luna as the Roman pantheon's equivalent of the quiet, reliable systems administrator — she's not in the boardroom making dramatic decisions like Jupiter, nor out in the field like Mars, but without her, the entire network crashes. She's the night shift worker who keeps the lights on (literally), the one who makes sure the calendar runs on time, and the one women call when they need the universe to align. In modern terms, she's the moon itself: a rock that's been silently governing our tides, our calendars, and our imagination for millennia, without ever asking for credit.
