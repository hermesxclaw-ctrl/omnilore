Who It Is

Before the Romans ever built a temple, before the Greek poets sang of Selene, there was a simpler, more primal name whispered in the Italic hills: Luna. She was not a story — she was the light that silvered the fields, the clock that ruled the blood of women, the silent charioteer who dragged the night behind her. While her siblings Jupiter and Mars thundered with drama, Luna simply shone — and in that steady, unblinking light, she held the rhythm of every month, every harvest, every birth. She is the quiet cosmic bureaucrat of the Roman pantheon: no scandals, no wars, just an eternal shift that never ends.

Luna is a celestial charioteer, driving her horses through the night sky to mark the passage of time. She is a witness to the night's events, a guardian of women's cycles, and a bringer of dreams. She is generally benevolent but can be associated with the mysterious, the occult, and the liminal. Her behavior in myth is largely passive — she is more a cosmic force than a character with complex motivations, unlike the more active deities of Olympus.

Powers
Lunar Control

Luna drives the moon across the night sky, controlling its phases and its light. She can dim or brighten the moon's glow at will.

Limit: She is bound to the cosmic order (fatum); she cannot change the fundamental cycle of the moon, only its presentation.

Influence over Women's Cycles

As a goddess of the moon, Luna governs the menstrual cycle and, by extension, fertility and childbirth. She was invoked by women for safe delivery and regular cycles.

Limit: Her influence is tied to the physical moon; she is not a goddess of healing or midwifery in the same way as Diana or Juno Lucina, though she is often invoked alongside them.

Dream Weaving

Luna is associated with dreams and the night's visions. She can send prophetic or meaningful dreams to mortals, particularly those who sleep under the open sky.

Limit: Dream influence is indirect and often symbolic rather than literal; she does not appear in dreams as a speaking character in most myths.

Night Illumination

Luna provides light in the darkness, guiding travelers and revealing what is hidden. She is a light-bringer of the night, contrasting with her brother Sol's daytime light.

Limit: Her light is reflected, not her own; she cannot illuminate the underworld or places the moon does not shine.

Timekeeping and Calendar Authority

Luna governs the month (mensis, from 'mensis' meaning month, related to 'moon'). Her phases mark the passage of time, and her festival on the Ides of March anchors the Roman calendar.

Limit: She is a marker of time, not a goddess of fate or destiny; she does not control the length of human life.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In Roman art, Luna is typically depicted as a beautiful woman with a crescent moon on her head or a lunar diadem, often driving a two-horse chariot (biga) across the night sky. She is frequently shown with a torch or veil. Unlike Greek Selene's more consistently serene depictions, Roman Luna could appear in both gentle and more martial or majestic forms, sometimes with a billowing cloak representing the night sky. Symbols — The crescent moon (her most common symbol), the full moon, a chariot (biga) drawn by two horses (or sometimes oxen or mules), a torch, a veil, a diadem, and the bull (in some imperial coinage, possibly reflecting Greek Selene's association with the bull). Her sacred animals include the horse and, in some syncretic contexts, the bull. The colors white and silver are associated with her.

Domains — The Moon, night, women's menstrual cycles, childbirth (in her aspect as Lucina), dreams, the passage of time, the calendar (particularly the Kalends and Ides), and the liminal space between day and night. She also has dominion over the tides and, by extension, bodies of water influenced by the moon.

Accounts I
The Chariot of NightVarious Roman poets (e.g., Ovid, Metamorphoses; Virgil, Aeneid)

1st century BCE - 1st century CE

Luna drives her two-horse chariot across the night sky, marking the passage of hours and seasons. She is the sister of Sol (the Sun), who drives his own chariot by day. The two are the celestial markers of time's passage, their daily and nightly journeys framing the lives of mortals. The daily and nightly cycle is established; Luna's journey brings night, sleep, and dreams to mortals.

Accounts II
Luna and EndymionAdapted from Greek sources by Roman poets (e.g., Cicero, Ovid)

1st century BCE - 1st century CE

Luna falls in love with the mortal shepherd Endymion, who sleeps eternally on Mount Latmus. She visits him each night, gazing upon his beauty. This story is more prominently associated with Greek Selene, but Roman poets adopted it, sometimes using the name Luna. Endymion remains in eternal sleep, blessed and cursed by Luna's love; their story becomes a symbol of eternal, unfulfilled longing.

The Festival of the Ides of MarchOvid, Fasti 3.883-884

Augustan era (8 CE)

Ovid records a festival of Luna on the Ides of March (March 15), held at her temple on the Palatine Hill. This festival is one of the few Roman religious observances explicitly dedicated to Luna, and it connects her to the beginning of the agricultural year and the return of spring. The festival ensures Luna's favor for the coming year, particularly for the growth of crops and the fertility of women.

Luna is just the Roman name for the Greek goddess Selene. — Luna is an older, distinct Italic deity. While she was syncretized with Selene, she has her own origins, cult, and iconography that predate Greek influence.

Luna and Diana are the same goddess. — They were originally distinct. Luna is the moon itself; Diana is a goddess of the hunt, wilderness, and childbirth. Their conflation is a later development, particularly in poetry and mystery cults.

The moon in Roman religion was only associated with women. — While Luna was important to women's cycles, she was also a state deity with a temple on the Palatine, a place in the di consentes, and festivals observed by the whole community. She was a cosmic and civic deity, not a purely feminine one.

Luna had no myths of her own. — Luna's myths are less narrative-driven than those of other deities, but she appears in the Endymion story (adapted from Greek), in the cosmic chariot myth, and in the festival of the Ides of March. Her mythology is more about presence and function than about dramatic events.
