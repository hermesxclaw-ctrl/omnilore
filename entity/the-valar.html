Who It Is

The Valar are the fourteen greatest of the Ainur who chose to enter Arda (the physical world) and become its guardians, shapers, and rulers under Eru Ilúvatar. Tolkien explicitly modeled them not as polytheistic gods but as archangelic beings — 'the Powers' — who govern the world on behalf of the one true God, similar to the saints and angels of Catholic theology. The Valar (singular Vala) consist of seven Lords and seven Queens, though Melkor was originally the eighth Lord before his fall. The Lords are: Manwë Súlimo (King of the Valar, Lord of the Winds, closest to Eru), Ulmo (Lord of Waters, the most directly involved in Middle-earth), Aulë the Smith (master of crafts, creator of the Dwarves), Oromë the Hunter (first to discover the Elves), Mandos/Námo (Keeper of the Houses of the Dead, pronounces dooms), Lórien/Irmo (master of visions and dreams), and Tulkas the Strong (the last to arrive, the wrestler who laughed while fighting Melkor). The Queens are: Varda Elentári (Queen of the Stars, maker of the constellations, most beloved by the Elves), Yavanna Kementári (Giver of Fruits, creator of the Two Trees and the Ents), Nienna (Lady of Mercy, whose tears watered the Trees), Estë the Gentle (healer of hurts), Vairë the Weaver (who weaves the story of the world), Vána the Ever-Young (maker of flowers and birds), and Nessa the Dancer. Each Vala possesses a specific domain of influence but all are subordinate to Eru's ultimate will. Their greatest works include: Varda's stars (the first light the Elves saw upon awakening), Aulë's forging of the vessels for the Sun and Moon, and Yavanna's creation of the Two Trees of Valinor (Telperion and Laurelin) which provided light for Valinor before the Sun and Moon. The Valar's relationship with Middle-earth is complex: after the destruction of their first dwelling (Almaren) by Melkor, they withdrew to Aman and raised the Pelóri mountains as a defensive barrier, effectively isolating themselves — a decision Tolkien suggests was partly a mistake, as it abandoned Middle-earth to Melkor's dominion. They intervened only thrice in Middle-earth's affairs: the War for the Sake of the Elves (capturing Melkor), the War of Wrath (defeating Melkor permanently), and the sending of the Istari (Wizards) to guide the free peoples against Sauron. The Valar embody Tolkien's vision of divine governance: indirect, respectful of free will, sorrowful over evil but constrained by wisdom from forcibly imposing good. They represent the tension between divine power and divine restraint — the Valar could destroy Sauron, but doing so would cause such cataclysm as to undo their purpose. Their wisdom is knowing when not to act.
