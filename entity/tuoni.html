God of Death, Lord of Tuonela, Dark Host

Who They Are

Tuoni is the god of death and the ruler of Tuonela, the Finnish underworld — a dark island realm divided from the land of the living by the black river of Tuoni (Tuonen joki). He is grim, unforgiving, and permanent. Once a soul crosses into Tuonela, there is no return — unless, like Lemminkäinen, the soul is retrieved piece by piece from the river by a mother's love and powerful magic. Tuoni is not malevolent; he simply is. He embodies the absolute finality of death in a worldview that, unlike Christian eschatology, offered no doctrine of universal resurrection.

Tuoni is described as a grim old man with fingers like iron hooks and a hat of darkness. His wife is Tuonetar, the mistress of the underworld, who brews beer from the blood of the dead and serves it to new arrivals, erasing their memories of life. His daughters — the Maidens of Tuoni — spin threads of black and red at the banks of the river, weaving the fates of mortals. Their son (or servant) is the ferryman of the black river, who transports the dead in a boat of shadows. The swan of Tuoni, a black swan that glides on the river, is sacred and protected; to kill it is a death sentence (as Lemminkäinen discovers).

Entrance to Tuonela is guarded by Surma, a monstrous hound or serpent whose name has become a synonym for violent death in Finnish. Even the gods and heroes cannot simply enter Tuonela — they must cross the black river, and the living who attempt this journey (like Väinämöinen) find themselves transformed or attacked. When Väinämöinen journeys to Tuonela seeking magical knowledge, Tuoni's people attempt to kill him; he escapes by transforming into a snake and squeezing through the nets they spread across the river.

Tuonela shares features with other Finno-Ugric underworlds — the Sami Jabmeaimo (realm of the dead) and the Estonian Toonela — suggesting an ancient common tradition. Unlike the Greek Hades or the Norse Hel, Tuonela is not a place of judgment or differentiated reward/punishment. It is simply the place of the dead, a dark continuation that mirrors life but lacks its vitality. The dead drink, feast, and hunt in Tuonela, but they are shades, not souls awaiting resurrection.

Tuoni's cultural significance lies in his representation of a pre-Christian Finnish relationship with mortality. Death is not a punishment or a transition to a better world — it is an inevitability, a river that must be crossed, a realm that must be respected and feared. The healing incantations of Finnish folk magic frequently invoke Tuoni and Tuonela as the source of illness and the place where illness must be sent back: 'Go back to Tuonela, disease-bringer; return to the black river, spirit of pain.'
