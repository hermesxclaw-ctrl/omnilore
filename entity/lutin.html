Who It Is

In the moonlit farmhouses of Normandy, a mischievous sprite braids horses' manes into tangled elf-locks, saddles a phantom steed named Bayard, and slips through keyholes to play pranks on sleeping families. This is the lutin, a shape-shifting hobgoblin whose name echoes the Roman sea god Neptune, and whose legend crossed the Atlantic to haunt the attics and streets of Quebec and Detroit.

Lutins are mischievous but not inherently evil. They may help with chores or play pranks. In Quebec, they can be good or evil; good ones may control weather or shave the master's beard before he wakes on Sundays. Evil ones may blunt scythes or fill shoes with pebbles. They abhor salt and avoid crossing it.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Derived from Latin Neptunus, the Roman god of the sea. The form evolved from netun (c. 1176–1181) to nuiton, influenced by Old French nuit ('night'), and then to luitun/luiton, influenced by Old French luitier ('to fight'). Originally designated a sea monster, later shifted to a mischievous demon in

Pronunciation — [lytɛ̃] (French); lutine pronounced [lytin]

Aliases — luiton, luitun, netun, nuiton, lutine (female), Nain Rouge

Ownership — French, particularly Norman and Breton; also French-Canadian (Quebec and Newfoundland) and Franco-American (Detroit)

Form & Signs

Appearance — Lutins are typically small, humanoid creatures, often described as ugly or grotesque. They may appear as animals, especially cats (white cats in Quebec), dogs, rabbits, or horses. The Nain Rouge is a childlike creature with red or black fur boots, blazing red eyes, and rotten teeth. In d'Aulnoy's tale, lutins can be invisible or appear in natural form.

Symbols — The red hat with two feathers is a symbol of invisibility in 'Le Prince Lutin'. Elf-locks (tangled hair) are associated with lutins. The horse form, Le Cheval Bayard, is a specific manifestation.

Domains — Household spirits, often found in homes, stables, and barns. In Normandy, they are akin to brownies. They also inhabit natural settings like forests and water. In Quebec, they live in or near homes, often as pets. In Newfoundland, they are associated with causing fairy-locks. The Nain Rouge is tied to Detroit.

Accounts I
Le Prince LutinMarie Catherine d'Aulnoy, fairy tale

1697

A prince is transformed into a lutin and gains the ability to be invisible, travel instantly, and pass through elements. He uses these powers to help others and win love. The tale popularized the lutin as a magical being with grand powers.

The Nain Rouge of DetroitDetroit urban legend

18th century onward

The Nain Rouge, a red dwarf, appears before disasters in Detroit, such as fires, wars, and economic downturns. It is said to be a harbinger of doom. The legend persists, with annual parades (Marche du Nain Rouge) to ward off bad luck.

Accounts II
Lutins in Quebec householdsQuebec folklore

17th-20th century

Lutins live in homes as pets or animal spirits. They may help or harass the family depending on treatment. Salt repels them. Belief influenced daily practices, such as spilling salt to keep lutins away.
