Who It Is

In the shadow of Sparta's austere temples, a figure of pale and emaciated form stood bound, her hands tied behind her—an unsettling reminder that hunger itself was a deity to be reckoned with. Limos, the personification of famine, was no distant myth but a presence that could enter a home and dwell there, an enemy of the gods and a companion to the idle.

Limos is portrayed as a hostile entity that accompanies the idle, dwelling in homes as an unwelcome housemate. It is an enemy of the gods, representing the harsh consequences of strife and laziness.

Personification of Famine

Limos embodies the abstract concept of famine and hunger, bringing starvation to those it afflicts.

Limit: As an abstraction, Limos has no independent agency; it is a consequence of strife and idleness.

Dwelling in Homes

Limos is described as a 'companion' and 'housemate', suggesting it can enter and reside in a household, bringing want.

Limit: This is metaphorical language describing the effect of idleness.

Form & Signs

Appearance — At Sparta, a painting depicted Limos 'in the form of a woman' described as 'a woman pale, and emaciated, with her hands tied behind her.' No other physical descriptions are attested.

Symbols — No specific symbols are attested. The Spartan painting emphasizes emaciation and bound hands, symbolizing the helplessness and suffering of famine.

Domains — Limos is a personification of famine and hunger; it is not associated with a specific realm but is said to enter and dwell in human homes.

Accounts I
Limos as Child of ErisHesiod, Theogony

Archaic

Limos is one of the many children of Eris (Strife), born without a father. These children are personified abstractions representing the harmful consequences of discord. Establishes Limos as a primordial force of harm arising from strife.

Limos and the Idle ManHesiod, Works and Days

Archaic

Hesiod contrasts Demeter, who loves the hard-working man and fills his granary, with Limos, who hates the idle man and is ever his companion. Moral lesson: work is necessary to avoid famine.

Accounts II
Limos as Hostile HousemateSemonides

Archaic

Semonides describes Limos as 'a hostile housemate, enemy of the gods', emphasizing its invasive and unwelcome nature. Reinforces the idea of famine as a domestic affliction.

Spartan Cult and DepictionsAthenaeus; Pausanias (implied)

Classical

Limos was one of seven abstractions respected at Sparta, including Phobos, Aidos, Hypnos, Thanatos, Gelos, and Eros. A painting at the temple of Apollo showed Limos as a woman, pale and emaciated, with hands tied behind her. Demonstrates the Spartan practice of deifying abstract concepts related to physical and psychological states.
