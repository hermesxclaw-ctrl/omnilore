Who It Is

She did not go into the woods to hunt. Leucone followed her husband because she thought he was meeting another woman there — and his own hounds, savage, lion-trained and left unsupervised, tore her apart before he could call them off. In a single Hellenistic paragraph, a Thessalian marriage becomes a study in jealousy, misreading, and a grief so absolute that the hunter kills the dogs that killed his wife and then kills himself on her pyre.

As characterized by Parthenius: Leucone is a young wife initially passive, then anxious and suspicious after repeated neglect. Cyanippus returns home too tired to speak or does not return at all, sleeping in the forest. After many occurrences (pollakis), Leucone infers erotic betrayal (hypopteuousa) and acts on jealousy, following secretly. The narrative does not present her as a huntress by inclination, but as a worried spouse driven to surveillance. Powers
Mortal status: no supernatural powers

Leucone is explicitly a mortal woman with no divine parentage, metamorphosis, prophetic, magical, or heroic abilities. Her only 'ability' in the narrative is to don hunting clothing and follow undetected for a time, which fails due to canine scent. Cyanippus likewise has no supernatural power; his skill is mundane hunting of large predators, and his dogs are described as exceptionally savage but natural animals, not hellhounds or divine agents.

Limit: Human vulnerability to animal attack; inability to control hounds at distance; lack of divine protection. The story's pathos depends on her complete lack of protection or power once mistaken for prey.

Accounts I
Leucone and Cyanippus — The Core Narrative (Parthenius, Erotica Pathemata 10)Parthenius of Nicaea, Erotica Pathemata 10

c. 50-20 BCE

Cyanippus, a Thessalian, begged Leucone's parents for her hand and married her. He loved hunting lions and bears more than his marriage, returning home too exhausted to speak or sleeping overnight in the woods. After this happened many times, Leucone suspected he was meeting another woman in the forest rather than hunting, dressed in hunting gear, and followed him secretly without his knowledge. While Cyanippus was elsewhere, his extremely savage hunting hounds scented Leucone, took her for a wild beast, and tore her to pieces in his absence. After some time Cyanippus found her mangled body, was seized by immense grief, and with the help of his hunting companions built and lit her funeral pyre. He placed her body on it, slaughtered all the hounds responsible, and after much weeping killed himself. Double death: Leucone's sparagmos by dogs and Cyanippus's suicide on her pyre after killing the dogs. The marriage line ends; no children are mentioned. The story becomes an exemplum of destructive jealousy, neglect, and misrecognition, grouped by Parthenius among 'sorrows of love' for poet

Transmission Witness: Paradoxographic Epitome (Westermann's Collection)Anton Westermann, Paradoxographoe (1839) reproducing Parthenius and related paradoxographers

1839 (collection); source material 1st century BCE - Byzantine epitomes

Westermann's edition does not add a new Leucone plot but demonstrates how the story survived: copied as a marvel/tragic love anecdote among collections of extraordinary tales (paradoxa) and erotica. The inclusion confirms Leucone's status as a literary curiosity rather than a widely retold myth. The text is identical to Parthenius 10, underscoring the single-source tradition. Preservation for modern scholarship; no new narrative consequences. Shows that by the 19th century Leucone was known only to specialists, not to broader mythographic imagination.

Comparative Paradigm: Pamphilus and Eurydice (The Earliest Hellenistic Suicide-after-Grief Model)Parthenius of Nicaea, Erotica Pathemata (chapter on Pamphilus); discussed by Avezzù 2016

Hellenistic, pre-Parthenius tradition (3rd-1st century BCE)

Pamphilus causes the death of Eurydice (or vice versa in some summaries) and kills himself in grief, establishing the narrative template that Leucone/Cyanippus replicates: a lover indirectly causes the beloved's death and then suicides from guilt/grief. Avezzù identifies this as probably the earliest example of the type, showing Leucone's story is not isolated but part of a Hellenistic fashion for civic tragic love stories where private eros leads to public death and often to civic consequences. Establishes literary genealogy for Leucone; scholars use it to date the taste for such stories to Hellenistic urban contexts where tragedy was repurposed for prose fiction.

Accounts II
Comparative Paradigm: Anthippe and Cichyrus (Parthenius 32)Parthenius of Nicaea, Erotica Pathemata 32; Avezzù 2016 analysis

Hellenistic (source: local histories of Epirus)

Anthippe, like Leucone, is a woman whose death (in some versions by accident or assault) leads her lover Cichyrus to suicide. The story shares the Leucone structure: female victim, male survivor who blames himself and self-kills, with hunting or pastoral background and aetiological link to a city (Cichyrus). It demonstrates the regional adaptability of the Leucone-type plot across Greek local histories. Shows the Leucone plot was a movable type, attached to different Thessalian/Epirote locales to explain place names and moralize about love and neglect.

Comparative Paradigm: Pyramus and Thisbe (Ovid, Metamorphoses 4.55-166) — The Canonical DescendantOvid, Metamorphoses Book 4; later retold by Hyginus, Chaucer, Shakespeare

8 CE (Ovid)

Pyramus and Thisbe, Babylonian lovers forbidden to marry, communicate through a wall, arrange to meet at Ninus's tomb, Thisbe arrives first, flees a lioness, drops her veil, Pyramus finds the bloodied veil, assumes she is dead, kills himself with his sword; Thisbe returns, finds him dying, and kills herself with the same sword. Like Leucone/Cyanippus, it is a double suicide driven by misinterpretation of evidence (bloodied clothing/veil) and absence at the critical moment. Ovid's version became the most famous reflex of the Hellenistic type to which Leucone belongs and directly inspired Shakespeare. Became the dominant Western tragic-lovers template, eclipsing Leucone. Provides the clearest evidence for the long reception of the Hellenistic love-death pattern that Leucone exemplifies. Shakespeare's Romeo and Juliet (1595-1597) is explicitly in this lineage, as Avezzù traces.

Modern Mythographic Fixing: Grimal and Brill's New Pauly SynthesesPierre Grimal, Dictionary of Classical Mythology (1987) entry 'Leucone' / 'Cyanippus'; Hubert Cancik/Helmuth Schneider (eds.), Brill's New Pauly, entry 'Cyanippus' by Alexander Frey (2006)

1987 and 2006 (modern reception, summarizing ancient source)

Both handbooks retell Parthenius 10 in compressed form for reference use: Leucone, wife of Thessalian Cyanippus, killed by his dogs when she follows him from jealousy; he suicides. Grimal adds cross-references to Actaeon and Procris; Frey adds bibliography and notes the story's classification among Hellenistic Erotica Pathemata. These entries do not create new plot but fix Leucone's canonical summary for contemporary readers and demonstrate how a one-paragraph Hellenistic anecdote becomes a stable dictionary entry. Stabilizes Leucone for modern reception; ensures she appears in classical handbooks despite having no cult or artistic tradition. Demonstrates the handbook tendency to state 'Thessaly' definitively and to normalize name spellings.

Leucone was a nymph, huntress of Artemis, or goddess like Leucothea — Parthenius presents Leucone as a mortal married woman with no divine parentage, virginity vow, or Artemisian affiliation. She wears hunting gear only as a one-time disguise to spy on her husband, not as a lifestyle. Leucothea (Ino) and Leucothoe are entirely different figures with different etymologies and myths.

Leucone was killed as punishment by Artemis for impiety, like Actaeon — No divinity causes or wills Leucone's death. Parthenius describes a naturalistic accident: savage dogs mistake a disguised human for game while the master is absent. Unlike Actaeon (who offends Artemis) or Procris (where Cephalus's magic javelin never misses), there is no curse, no offended goddess, and no magical weapon. The tragedy is domestic negligence and misrecognition.

Leucone's story is ancient Thessalian folklore with cult and ritual — There is no evidence for cult, ritual, sanctuary, festival, or oral folklore for Leucone. She appears only in a learned Hellenistic handbook for poets (Parthenius) and its epitomes. Thessaly provides a literary setting (forests with lions/bears), not a documented local cult. The story's function was literary pathos, not religious practice.

Cyanippus deliberately killed Leucone or set his dogs on her — Parthenius is explicit that Cyanippus was absent when the dogs attacked and that the dogs acted on their own, mistaking her for a beast. He is guilty of neglect and of owning uncontrolled savage dogs, but not of intentional uxoricide. His extreme grief, dog-slaughter, and suicide are presented as proof he loved her and did not intend harm.
