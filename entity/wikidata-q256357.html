Who It Is

Before the Romans built their grand city of Mérida, a powerful queen of the dead already ruled the dark, fertile river valleys of the Tagus. Her name was Ataegina, and although she never appears in any surviving book or epic poem, the Romans who conquered her land felt her presence so strongly that they gave her a new name—Proserpina—and built her altars in their new capital. She is a ghost made of stone: a goddess known to us only through the desperate, grateful vows of her worshippers, etched into granite and marble. To meet her is to see the shadowy religion of ancient Iberia through the only window we have: a handful of inscriptions, a sacrificed goat, and a goddess who refused to disappear.

As a chthonic deity associated with the underworld and the cycle of seasons, she would have been seen as a powerful, potentially capricious force of nature. She was a goddess of the underworld, death, and rebirth, likely overseeing the transition between worlds. Her personality is not described in any source; we can only infer a stern, vital, and necessary power from her domain. She was a goddess to be propitiated with offerings, not necessarily loved.

Powers
Chthonic Sovereignty

As a goddess of the underworld, she held power over the realm of the dead. This is inferred from her syncretism with Proserpina and the chthonic nature of her cult.

Limit: Her power was likely limited to the underworld and its inhabitants, though as a nature goddess she could influence the cycle of life and death on earth.

Rebirth and Renewal

If the etymology from *ati-gen- is correct, she was a goddess of reincarnation or the cyclical return of life. This would make her a counterpart to the dying-and-rising vegetation deities of the Mediterranean.

Limit: This is a linguistic hypothesis, not a documented belief. The exact nature of the 'rebirth' she oversaw is unknown.

Acceptance of Sacrifice

She received animal sacrifices, specifically goats or rams, as part of votive offerings. This is the only directly attested ritual act associated with her.

Limit: The sacrifice was likely a means of seeking her favor, healing, or protection, but the specific purposes are not detailed in the inscriptions.

Domains — Underworld, death, rebirth, the cycle of seasons, nature, and possibly healing. Her sanctuary was likely in a cave or a grove, typical of chthonic deities. Accounts I
The Syncretism with ProserpinaEpigraphic evidence from Roman Spain

1st-3rd centuries CE

When the Romans conquered Lusitania, they encountered a powerful local goddess of the underworld. Rather than suppress her, they identified her with their own Proserpina, the queen of the underworld. This was a common Roman strategy of cultural assimilation. The local goddess retained her native name, Ataecina, but was given the Roman epithet 'Proserpina' to make her comprehensible to Roman administrators and settlers. The cult of Ataecina Proserpina flourished, especially in and around the new Roman city of Emerita Augusta (Mérida). The syncretism preserved Ataegina's worship, but it also overwrote her original, indigenous identity. We can only see her through a Roman lens. The local population likely continued to see her as their own goddess, while the Romans saw her as a local manifestation of their own deity.

Accounts II
The Vow of the GoatInscription CIL II 605

2nd century CE (approx.)

A now-anonymous individual, facing an illness or danger, made a vow (votum) to Ataecina Proserpina. The vow was likely to offer a sacrifice if the goddess intervened. The inscription records the fulfillment of this vow: a goat (or ram) was sacrificed in her honor. The altar was then dedicated to her. This is a tiny, personal story of faith and desperation captured in stone. It tells us that Ataegina was a goddess people turned to in times of crisis, a powerful intercessor with the world beyond. The inscription is our most direct evidence of her cult's practice. It shows a transactional but sincere form of worship, common in the Roman world. It also confirms her chthonic nature, as the sacrifice was likely offered at night or in a subterranean context.

Ataegina was a 'lost' or 'secret' goddess. — She was not 'lost'. She was a major, publicly worshipped deity in her region, with numerous inscriptions and a state-sanctioned cult. She is 'lost' only in the sense that her mythology was not written down and her temple has not been definitively found.

She was a mother goddess. — There is no evidence she was a mother goddess. Her association is with the underworld and cyclical rebirth, not with maternity or childbirth.

Her worship was identical to the Greek/Roman cult of Persephone. — While she was officially syncretized with Proserpina, the local, indigenous cult likely had its own distinct rituals, beliefs, and mythology that were not simply copies of the Roman version. The Roman layer is the only one we can see clearly.
