Form

There is no definitive, surviving anthropomorphic representation of Ataegina that is universally agreed upon. Iconographic evidence is scarce and ambiguous. A bronze statuette from Mérida, sometimes identified as Ataecina, is a matter of scholarly debate. She may have been depicted in a manner similar to Proserpina, but this is an assumption. The most common visual representation associated with her is the goat, based on the epigraphic evidence of goat sacrifice and the possible etymological link to the word for 'she-goat' in some Celtic languages (though this is disputed).

Powers

Chthonic Sovereignty As a goddess of the underworld, she held power over the realm of the dead. This is inferred from her syncretism with Proserpina and the chthonic nature of her cult. Romanized (Ataecina Proserpina) Her power was likely limited to the underworld and its inhabitants, though as a nature goddess she could influence the cycle of life and death on earth. None, as this is an inference from her identification with Proserpina.

Rebirth and Renewal If the etymology from *ati-gen- is correct, she was a goddess of reincarnation or the cyclical return of life. This would make her a counterpart to the dying-and-rising vegetation deities of the Mediterranean. Reconstructed (Indigenous) This is a linguistic hypothesis, not a documented belief. The exact nature of the 'rebirth' she oversaw is unknown. The etymology is disputed; a non-Celtic origin for the name is possible.

Acceptance of Sacrifice She received animal sacrifices, specifically goats or rams, as part of votive offerings. This is the only directly attested ritual act associated with her. Romanized (Epigraphic evidence) The sacrifice was likely a means of seeking her favor, healing, or protection, but the specific purposes are not detailed in the inscriptions. The exact animal (goat vs. ram) is debated in the translation of the inscription.

Accounts

When the Romans conquered Lusitania, they encountered a powerful local goddess of the underworld. Rather than suppress her, they identified her with their own Proserpina, the queen of the underworld. This was a common Roman strategy of cultural assimilation. The local goddess retained her native name, Ataecina, but was given the Roman epithet 'Proserpina' to make her comprehensible to Roman administrators and settlers. The cult of Ataecina Proserpina flourished, especially in and around the new Roman city of Emerita Augusta (Mérida).

A now-anonymous individual, facing an illness or danger, made a vow (votum) to Ataecina Proserpina. The vow was likely to offer a sacrifice if the goddess intervened. The inscription records the fulfillment of this vow: a goat (or ram) was sacrificed in her honor. The altar was then dedicated to her. This is a tiny, personal story of faith and desperation captured in stone. It tells us that Ataegina was a goddess people turned to in times of crisis, a powerful intercessor with the world beyond.

Content Notes

The only directly attested ritual practice of her cult is the sacrifice of goats or rams. This was a standard practice in both indigenous Iberian and Roman religions, but it involved the killing of an animal. The blood of the animal was likely offered to the goddess, and the meat may have been consumed in a ritual feast.

Animal sacrifice was a normative, not a deviant, practice in the ancient world. It was a means of communicating with the divine and was not seen as cruel or unusual by the practitioners.

Inscription CIL II 605

Ataegina was a goddess of the underworld, a realm associated with death, decay, and the unknown. Her worship would have involved rituals and beliefs that confronted the reality of death. This is not 'dark' in a moral sense, but it is a domain that modern audiences often find unsettling.

Chthonic deities were not 'evil' in the ancient world. They were powerful, necessary forces that governed the cycle of life and death. They required different rituals (e.g., night offerings, sacrifices into pits) than the 'ouranic' (heavenly) gods, but they were equally deserving of respect and worship.

What We Don't Know

1) No indigenous mythology or narratives survive; her original character is a reconstruction. 2) The exact location of her primary sanctuary at Turobriga is unknown. 3) No definitive, universally accepted anthropomorphic image of her has been identified. 4) The full extent of her pre-Roman cult is unknown, as we only have evidence from the Roman period. 5) The nature of her 'rebirth' function, if any, is speculative and based on a disputed etymology. 6) No direct mention of her in classical literature has been found.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Ataegina as the ultimate 'local hero' of the divine world. She was the queen of the underworld for her people, but she wasn't a global celebrity like Zeus or Odin. She was a powerful, local force of nature, tied to the rivers and hills of her homeland. When the Roman Empire moved in, she didn't get a movie deal or a global franchise; she got a name change—'Ataecina Proserpina'—like a local band signing with a big label and being told to sound more like the current top 40. We only have the 'major label' version of her, but the local, original version is still there, hidden beneath the Roman gloss, waiting for more archaeological finds to bring her back into the spotlight.
