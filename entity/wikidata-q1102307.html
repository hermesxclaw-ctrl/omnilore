Who It Is

She stands above courthouses with a sword in one hand and scales in the other, eyes sometimes bound, sometimes wide open — not a goddess who ever demanded sacrifice, but an idea the Romans dressed as a goddess so that emperors could claim they ruled justly. Lady Justice is less ancient than she looks, and more argued-over than she seems impartial.

As allegory, not a mythic actor with personality in ancient narrative. Roman Justitia embodies the virtue of iustitia: steadiness, incorruptibility, favour to no party, association with imperial legitimacy. Later allegorical literature (e.g., Cesare Ripa's Iconologia 1593, 1603) codifies her as grave, chaste, maidenly, incorruptible virgin whose 'maidenly form guarantees impartiality.' Powers
Weighing and Balancing (Psychostasia / Libra)

Holds beam scales to weigh evidence, testimony, and arguments of opposing parties; metaphor that justice 'tips in favour' of the weightier/meritorious side. Derived from Egyptian weighing of heart against feather of Maat, transmitted via Greek Dike's scales.

Limit: Scales require external input of evidence; allegory does not judge without human legal process. Satirical readings show scales can be manipulated or blind justice cannot see what is weighed.

Enforcement and Authority (Sword)

Wields a double-edged sword representing coercive power of law, authority to protect and to punish, swiftness and finality of judgment.

Limit: Sword is symbolic; allegory does not enact violence herself. Modern reinterpretations (India 2024) deliberately remove sword to signal shift from retributive to constitutional justice.

Impartiality / Blind Impartiality

Blindfold signifies justice applied without regard to wealth, power, identity or status; or in satirical origin, blindness to injustice occurring before her.

Limit: Impartiality is aspirational; critique notes blindfold can also signify willful blindness, opacity, or denial of context (e.g., India's 2024 'law is not blind, it sees everyone equally'). Old Bailey and other sites argue maidenly virtue makes blindfold redundant.

Legitimation of Rule

As Iustitia Augusta, confers legitimacy on emperor/regime that claims to protect justice; presence on coins, clipeus virtutis and temple signals just rule.

Limit: Power is propagandistic, not theological; Justitia had temple/shrine but appears to have been viewed more as artistic personification than deeply worshipped deity with priesthood and myth cycle.

Paired Deliberation with Prudentia

Often appears as pair with Prudentia (Prudence), indicating justice requires wisdom, foresight and right reason, not mere force.

Limit: Pairing is iconographic convention, not mythic partnership with narrative.

Accounts I
The Clipeus Virtutis: Justice as Augustan VirtueRes Gestae Divi Augusti 34.2; Cassius Dio 53.16.4; Augustus' clipeus virtutis in Curia Julia

27 BCE - 14 CE (Augustan)

After receiving the title Augustus in 27 BCE, the Senate honors Augustus with a golden shield (clipeus virtutis) placed in the Curia Julia, inscribed with four virtues: virtus, clementia, iustitia, pietas. Justice is thus elevated as a defining quality of the new regime, not yet a fully independent goddess but a personifiable virtue. This act inaugurates the political use of Iustitia that will later be embodied as a female figure on coins and in dedications. Creates ideological template where every emperor must associate himself with justice; paves way for personified Justitia/Iustitia Augusta on coinage and for a temple under Tiberius.

Temple of Justitia and Tiberian CultLiterary references to aedes Justitiae; Ovid?; Tacitus Annals; numismatic evidence; discussed in Clark (2007) and Stafford (2000)

c. 22-37 CE (Tiberian)

Emperor Tiberius establishes a temple or shrine (aedes/sacrarium) of Justitia in Rome, formalizing the Augustan virtue as a goddess with cult space. Though called a goddess with temple and cult shrine, contemporary and modern analysis suggests she remained primarily an artistic symbolic personification rather than a deity with deep religious significance, priesthood or mythic narrative comparable to Jupiter or Juno. Gives Justitia architectural presence in Rome but does not generate extensive myth; reinforces imperial propaganda that the emperor is protector of justice.

Vespasian's Iustitia Augusta Coins: Justice EnthronedRIC II Vespasian 65, 271, 465; BMCRE II; British Museum Collection

71-79 CE (Flavian)

After civil war of 69 CE, Vespasian mints coins showing Justitia seated on throne, legend IVSTITIA AVGVSTA. The goddess is diademed, draped, holding sceptre or patera (later scales/sword). The image proclaims the new Flavian regime as restorer of justice after Nero and civil war. Many emperors after Vespasian reuse the type to proclaim themselves protectors of justice. Creates durable visual corpus for Justitia; establishes enthroned, dignified female type that evolves into standing Lady Justice with scales and sword.

Accounts II
The Weighing of the Heart: Scales from Maat to DikeBook of the Dead Spell 125 (Papyrus of Ani, BM EA 10470); Hesiod Works and Days 220-224; later Greek and Roman adoption

c. 1550 BCE (Egyptian New Kingdom) to c. 700 BCE (Hesiod) to Roman period

In Spell 125, jackal-headed Anubis weighs the deceased's heart against the feather of truth of goddess Maat, who embodies truth, fairness and justice; good hearts enter afterlife, heavy hearts are fed to Ammit. Greeks adopt balance scales as attribute of Dike ('If some god had been holding level the balance of Dike'), and Romans transfer it to Justitia. Lady Justice is often depicted with scales suspended from one hand to balance evidence and arguments, tipping in favour of one side. Scales become globally recognized symbol of justice, described as rare example of a symbol that completely lacks ambiguity; provides deep iconographic genealogy but not direct cult continuity.

The Blindfold Inverted: From Satire to Impartiality (Hans Gieng 1543)Hans Gieng, Gerechtigkeitsbrunnen, Bern (1543); Robert Jacob, 'The Eye of the Law' (1994); Desmond Manderson scholarship; Ripa Iconologia (1593/1603)

1543 CE onward; reinterpretation 17th-18th centuries

Earliest Roman coins show Justitia with eyes uncovered. In 1543 Hans Gieng's Fountain of Justice in Bern shows Justice blindfolded, sword and scales in hand, trampling figures of Pope, Emperor, Sultan and Mayor at her feet. The blindfold was originally a satirical addition showing Justice as blind to injustice carried on before her, possibly influenced by fool/blindfold motifs and by Sebastian Brant's Ship of Fools (1494). Over time, especially during Enlightenment, the meaning is reinterpreted positively as impartiality — justice applied without regard to wealth, power or status. Creates dominant modern image of blindfolded Justice, but also persistent debate; many sculptures deliberately omit blindfold (Old Bailey, London) or reinterpret it.

The Unblindfolded Justice of the Old BaileyCentral Criminal Court (Old Bailey) building records, City of London; F.W. Pomeroy statue (1907); courthouse brochures

1902-1907 CE (Edwardian) to present

Atop the dome of the Old Bailey, London's Central Criminal Court, stands F.W. Pomeroy's 3.5m bronze Lady Justice without blindfold, holding sword and scales. Courthouse literature explains this is because Lady Justice was originally not blindfolded and because her 'maidenly form' is supposed to guarantee her impartiality, rendering the blindfold redundant. The statue thus preserves pre-1543 iconography as a conscious archaism. Provides canonical example of deliberate omission of blindfold; cited in legal-heritage tours and scholarship on iconographic variation.

Nyay Devi: India Reimagines Lady Justice (2024)Supreme Court of India announcement, October 2024; statements by Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud; press coverage (The Hindu, Indian Express, BBC, 2024)

October 2024 CE

The Supreme Court of India inaugurates a new template statue, Nyay Devi, for use in Indian courts. The blindfold is removed and the sword replaced by a book representing the Constitution of India. She wears a sari and blouse, crown (mukut), bindi on forehead, bangles and Indian jewellery. Chief Justice Chandrachud declares 'the law is not blind, it sees everyone equally.' The removal of the sword symbolizes India's shift to new criminal laws (Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita) focusing on nyay (justice) rather than punishment/retribution (danda). Signals decolonial and constitutional reinterpretation; sparks national and global debate about whether blindfold and sword should remain universal symbols; new statues commissioned for courts across India.

Lady Justice has been blindfolded since ancient Greece and Rome. — Roman Justitia was never blindfolded; earliest coins and statues show eyes uncovered. Blindfold first appears in 1543 (Hans Gieng, Bern) and only becomes common after mid-16th century.

Lady Justice is the ancient Greek goddess Themis (or Dike). — Roman Iustitia is an Augustan-era (late 1st century BCE) personification of an imperial virtue, not the Greek Titaness Themis or her daughter Dike. They are distinct in genealogy, cult and myth, though later syncretized.

The scales of justice were invented by the Greeks or Romans. — Scales as justice symbol date to Egyptian Book of the Dead (Anubis weighing heart against feather of Maat, c. 1550 BCE), later adopted by Greeks for Dike and then Romans for Justitia. Greek/Roman use is adaptation, not invention.

The blindfold always meant impartiality. — Earliest blindfold was satirical — showing Justice as blind to the injustice and corruption before her, related to fool/blindness motifs. The positive 'impartiality' meaning is an Enlightenment reinterpretation that inverted the original insult.

Lady Justice always carries a sword and scales together. — Attributes varied: early Vespasianic coins show sceptre/patera, not scales/sword; medieval images sometimes omit sword; Shelby County variant uses human-scale balance; 2024 Indian Nyay Devi omits sword entirely in favor of Constitution book.

Justitia was a major, anciently worshipped goddess with extensive myths like Venus or Minerva. — Justitia had a temple/shrine in Rome (Tiberian) but appears to have been viewed from the onset more as artistic symbolic personification for imperial propaganda than as deity with rich myth cycle, priesthood or festivals. No surviving myths of her adventures exist comparable to Olympians.
