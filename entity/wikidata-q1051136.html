The Approach

In a remote corner of the French Alps, a single weathered stone whispers a name that has echoed through millennia: Cathubodua, the battle crow. Though only one inscription survives, this enigmatic Gaulish goddess has captured modern imagination, her name conjuring images of war-birds and fierce sovereignty. But who was she really? The evidence is tantalizingly thin, and the story is one of scholarly detective work and bold reconstruction.

Content advisory. None. The subject is a fragmentary ancient deity with no surviving myths or dark narratives.
Identity

Cathubodua

Pronunciation Reconstructed as /kaθuˈbodwaː/ in Gaulish, with modern English approximation /kæθuːˈbɒdjuːə/.

Etymology

Derived from Proto-Celtic *Katu-bodwā, meaning 'battle crow' or 'battle-fighting'. The Gaulish elements *katu- (battle) and *bodwā (crow, fight) combine. The name is reconstructed from the inscription ATHVBODVAE, assuming an initial C was lost.

Cultural Ownership

Gaulish (ancient Celtic), specifically the region of Haute Savoie, eastern France. The inscription is from the Roman period, likely 1st-3rd century CE.

Disputed Identifications

The name is sometimes linked to the Irish Badb (a war goddess) and the Welsh *Bodw- forms, but these are speculative. The inscription's reading is uncertain; some scholars read ATEBODVAE, a personal name, rather than Cathubodua.

Description

Appearance

No ancient depiction survives. Based on the name 'battle crow', she may have been envisioned as a crow or raven associated with battlefields, but this is speculative.

Symbols & Iconography

The crow/raven is the key symbol inferred from the name. In Celtic iconography, crows and ravens often accompany war deities, such as the Irish Morrígan and Badb.

Behaviour & Personality

Unknown. The name suggests a martial character, possibly a goddess of battle or victory, but no myths or cult practices are attested.

Domains & Habitats

Likely associated with war and battlefields, but no specific sanctuary or cult site is known beyond the inscription location.

Contradictions & Disputes

Reading of the inscription

Position A

The inscription reads ATHVBODVAE, with initial C lost, giving Cathubodua.

Position A — evidence

The stone has a damaged beginning; the name fits Gaulish theonym patterns.

Position B

The inscription reads ATEBODVAE, a personal name, not a goddess.

Position B — evidence

Other inscriptions with ATEBODVAE are personal names; no other evidence for the goddess.

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus; the majority of scholars accept Cathubodua as a goddess, but some remain skeptical.

Connection to Irish Badb

Position A

Cathubodua is the Gaulish equivalent of the Irish Badb, both meaning 'battle crow'.

Position A — evidence

Linguistic reconstruction and shared martial iconography.

Position B

The similarity is coincidental or due to common Indo-European heritage, not direct equivalence.

Position B — evidence

No direct evidence links the two; the Irish Badb is well-attested, Cathubodua is not.

Scholarly Consensus

The connection is plausible but unproven.

Primary Evidence

CIL 12: 2525; found at Mieussy, Haute Savoie, France

Type inscriptionDate Roman period, likely 1st-3rd century CELocator The inscription reads: ATHVBODVAE AVG SERVILIA TERENTIA S L MLanguage Latin (with Gaulish theonym)

Translation

To Athubodua Augusta, Servilia Terentia (set this up) willingly and deservedly (or: in fulfillment of a vow). The name is often emended to Cathubodua.

Three other inscriptions from Austria and Slovenia (CIL 3: 4753, 11550, 14359) mention ATEBODVAE, ATEBODVVS, ATEBODVI.

Type inscriptionDate Roman periodLocator These are personal names, possibly related or unrelated.Language Latin

Translation

The names are likely personal names, not theonyms.
