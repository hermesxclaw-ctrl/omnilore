Who It Is

Arabic had no letter for /g/ — until Persian scribes took Kāf (ک), drew a stroke through its back, and invented Gāf (گ), a single diagonal line that unlocked an entire sound world and then traveled from Shiraz to Senegal to Sumatra.

As a grapheme, Gāf behaves as a fully productive consonant letter: it joins cursively on both sides (dual-joining), participates in ligatures, takes obligatory dots/strokes to distinguish minimal pairs (e.g., Persian کار kār 'work' vs. گار gār suffix; گل gol 'flower' vs. کل kol 'all'). It is stable in Persian, Urdu, Kurdish, and Pashto orthographies where /g/ is phonemic. Name & Tongue

Etymology — Persian گاف (gāf), from the name of the Arabic letter Kāf (ک / ك, from Phoenician kaph 'palm of hand') with a distinguishing diacritic. The Persian term gāf is itself a borrowing of the Arabic letter name kāf with Persian phonology. The descriptive Arabic grammatical term is al-kāf al-muʿajjama or k

Pronunciation — Persian: /ɡɒːf/ (gāf), IPA [ɡɒːf]; Arabic linguistic discussion: /ɡaːf/ or /dʒaːf/ depending on dialect; Jawi Malay: /ɡa/; Pashto: /ɡaf/; English scholarly: /ɡɑːf/ or /ɡæf/. Letter represents voiced velar stop /ɡ/ in all standard Perso-Arabic orthographies; in some Arabic vernacular transcriptions it may represent /ɡ/ vs. /dʒ/ vs. /q/ distinctions.

Aliases — Gaf, Gāf, Guef, Gaf-e Farsi, Kāf-e Gāf, Persian Gaf

Ownership — Originated within New Persian scribal culture (9th-10th century CE, Samanid and early Ghaznavid period) as one of the four letters added to the Arabic abjad to write Persian phonem

Phonemic Representation /g/

Represents the voiced velar stop /ɡ/ in all Perso-Arabic orthographies, contrasting with /k/ (ک), /q/ (ق), and /dʒ/ (ج). Allows writing of native Persian words (e.g., بزرگ bozorg 'large', گفتن goftan 'to say') and loanwords that would otherwise be ambiguous.

Limit: In Arabic MSA, /g/ is not phonemic; use of گ is considered non-standard and may be normalized to ج or ق by spellcheckers. In some fonts, گ may be misrendered as ک if the additional stroke is not supported.

Abjad Numerical Value 5000 (Jawi 6000)

In the extended abjad system (ḥisāb al-jummal), where Arabic letters have numerical values, the five Persian additions are assigned values beyond 1000: پ=2? Actually Persian additions: پ, چ, ژ, گ assigned 2000-5000? Gāf specifically = 5000 (Jawi variant ݢ = 6000 in some Southeast Asian abjad lists). Used for chronograms, numerology, and foliation.

Limit: Not used in standard Arabic abjad (which stops at غ=1000); values beyond 1000 are post-classical and vary by tradition. Modern usage is largely historical/talismanic, not computational.

Morphological Productivity

Enables productive Persian morphology: suffixes -gār (گار), -gī (گی), -gān (گان), and verb stems with /g/ (e.g., گرفتن gereftan). Without Gāf, these morphemes would collapse with /k/ forms, obscuring etymology.

Limit: Some Middle Persian /g/ words were historically written with ق or ک before standardization; early texts show variation.

Dialectal Transcription Tool

In vernacular Arabic orthography, Gāf provides a precise tool to transcribe /g/ in dialect words and foreign loans: Iraqi gahwa (گهوة) for qahwa, Egyptian gamil (گميل) in informal writing, Moroccan agadir (أݣادير). Also used for transliteration of English /g/ (e.g., گوگل Google).

Limit: Not recognized by Arabic Language Academies for MSA; formal publications avoid it. Competing conventions (ڨ, ݣ, ج, غ, ق) fragment usage.

Digital Distinction and Searchability

As distinct Unicode codepoints, Gāf variants enable accurate digital search, sorting, and text-to-speech for Perso-Arabic languages. Proper encoding prevents conflation of Persian گل (flower) with Arabic كل (all).

Limit: Legacy encodings (e.g., Windows-1256) lacked گ, causing fallback to ك or ?. Many fonts still lack proper positional forms for ݢ, ګ, ڮ, ࢴ.

Form & Signs

Appearance — All Gāf forms are derived from Kāf. Base shapes: Arabic Kāf (ك) with two strokes forming a diagonal stem and a bowl, and Persian/Urdu Keheh (ک) with a diagonal stem and hamza-like head. Gāf adds a diacritic: 1) Kāf with line (گ U+06AF) — Persian Keheh plus an additional short diagonal stroke through the ascender (often described as a line or hamza-like bar); 2) Kāf with ring (ګ U+06AB) — Pashto form with a small circle/ring inside the bowl; 3) Kā

Symbols — No religious iconography; purely phonographic. Symbolic value in abjad numerology: گ = 5000, used historically for chronograms (tarikh), pagination, and talismanic squares. In Persian literary culture, گ as the marker of native Persian /g/ versus Arabic /q/ and /k/ became emblematic of Persian linguistic identity (zaban-e farsi vs. arabi). In Jawi and Pegon, ݢ/ڮ became markers of Islamicate literacy in Southeast Asia, appearing on mosque inscript

Domains — Primary habitat: the 32-letter Persian alphabet (الفبای فارسی) and its derivatives: Urdu (38 letters), Sindhi (52), Kurdish Sorani (33), Balochi, Punjabi Shahmukhi, Pashto (44), and historically Ottoman Turkish (33-35). Secondary habitats: Jawi script for Malay (37 letters including ݢ), Pegon script for Javanese/Sundanese/Madurese (including ࢴ/ڮ), Arwi script for Tamil Muslims, and Tausug Arabic script. Tertiary habitat: ad-hoc Arabic dialect ort

Accounts I
The Four Additions: Inventing Gāf to Write PersianHistory of the Persian alphabet; Lazard (1963), Perry (2005), Daniels & Bright (1996) synthesis of early New Persian orthography

9th-10th century CE (Abbasid period, rise of New Persian)

When Arabic script was adopted for New Persian after the Islamic conquest, scribes faced four Persian phonemes with no Arabic equivalent: /p/, /tʃ/, /ʒ/, and /g/. Arabic had /f/ (ف), /dʒ/ (ج), /z/ (ز), and /k/ (ك) but not their voiced/affricated counterparts. Scribes solved this by adding diacritics to existing letters: Pe (پ) from Be (ب) with three dots, Che (چ) from Jim (ج) with three dots, Zhe (ژ) from Ze (ز) with three dots, and Gāf (گ) from Kāf (ک) with a line/stroke. The earliest systematic use appears in the 10th century in eastern Iran, allowing Persian to be written without ambiguity for the first time in the Arabic script. This innovation is attributed to anonymous scribal practice rather than a single inventor, and became standardized by the 11th century. Enabled a full Persian literary renaissance in Arabic script, including the Shahnameh and scientific translations; established the 32-letter Persian alphabet still used today; provided the model for all later Perso-Arabic adaptations (Urdu, Ottoman, Jawi).

Gāf in the Shahnameh and Classical PoetryFerdowsi, Shahnameh (c. 977-1010 CE), critical editions (Moscow, Khaleghi-Motlagh); Hafez, Divan; Rumi, Masnavi

10th-14th century CE (Classical Persian literature)

Ferdowsi's Shahnameh, the Persian national epic of ~50,000 couplets, is among the earliest extensive texts to use Gāf consistently. Words central to the epic's lexicon require it: گرشاسپ (Garshasp), گرگان (Gorgan), بزرگ (bozorg), جنگ (jang 'war'), گرز (gorz 'mace'). The consistent use of گ allowed Ferdowsi to preserve the Middle Persian /g/ that Arabic script would have erased, maintaining etymological links to Pahlavi. Later poets (Hafez, Saadi, Rumi) rely on Gāf for rhyme and meter distinctions (e.g., گل gol vs. کل kol). Manuscript variants show scribes sometimes omitted the stroke, creating textual-critical problems for modern editors distinguishing گ/ک. Standardized Gāf in the literary canon; created a continuous scribal tradition where گ became non-optional for correct Persian; modern critical editions must collate Gāf/Kāf variants.

Westward and Eastward: Ottoman and Indian AdoptionsOttoman imlâ manuals; Urdu orthography history (Shackle 1970, King 1994)

14th-19th century CE

As Persian served as the administrative and literary lingua franca from Anatolia to Bengal, Gāf traveled with it. Ottoman Turkish adopted the Persian alphabet wholesale, using گ for /g/ (e.g., گوزل güzel 'beautiful') while also creating a separate letter for /ŋ/ (ڭ, kef-i saghir or sağır kef) — a Kāf/Qāf with three dots or a special form — to write the Turkic nasal. In India, the Delhi Sultanate and Mughals adapted Persian script for Hindavi/Urdu, retaining گ for /g/ (e.g., Urdu گھر ghar 'house', گانا gānā 'song') within the 38-letter Urdu alphabet. The stroke-based Gāf thus became the pan-Islamicate marker for /g/ from the Balkans to Bengal, with local typographic styles (nasta'liq in Iran/India, naskh in Ottoman). Established Gāf as a core letter across three major literary languages; Ottoman distinction between گ (/g/) and ڭ (/ŋ/) clarified Turkic phonology until the 1928 Latin alphabet reform abolished Arabic script in Turkey; Urdu retains گ as essential.

Accounts II
The Jawi Voyage: Gāf Becomes ݢ in the Malay WorldTerengganu Inscription Stone (1303 CE); Sejarah Melayu (Malay Annals); Gallop & Arps studies of Jawi manuscripts

14th-20th century CE

Islamization of the Malay Archipelago brought Perso-Arabic script via Persian and Indian traders. Malay had phonemic /g/ and /ŋ/ and /p/ etc., requiring the Persian additions plus new letters. Malay scribes adopted گ but modified it to ݢ (Kāf with a dot above, U+0762) to represent /g/ (e.g., ݢاڠ gang 'gang', اݢام agama 'religion'), while also using ڠ (ng) for /ŋ/. The Terengganu Stone, the earliest dated Jawi inscription, already shows the adapted orthography. Jawi became the script of Malay sultanates, religious scholarship, and early printing. In the 20th century, Malaysia and Indonesia largely shifted to Latin (Rumi), but Jawi remains co-official in Brunei, Malaysia (religious contexts), and for Pegon in Java. Created a distinct Southeast Asian Perso-Arabic tradition with its own Gāf form (ݢ); Unicode later encoded it separately from Persian گ to preserve Jawi textual heritage; Pegon variants (ڮ/ࢴ) extended it to Javanese, Sundanese, and Tamil Arwi.

Gāf in the Hindu Kush: Pashto ګ and the RingPashto orthography standards (Academy of Sciences of Afghanistan); Penzl (1955) A Grammar of Pashto; Henderson (1983)

16th century CE to present (standardized 20th century)

Pashto, an Eastern Iranian language with phonemic /g/ and retroflexes, adapted Perso-Arabic script with additional letters beyond Persian. For /g/, Pashto uses ګ (Kāf with ring, U+06AB) rather than Persian گ. The ring (a small circle inside the bowl) distinguishes it from ک (/k/) and from other Pashto Kāf derivatives like ښ (/ʂ/) and ږ (/ʐ/). The choice of ring over line reflects local calligraphic preference and the need to avoid collision with other dotted letters in the dense Pashto alphabet (44 letters). ګ appears in core Pashto vocabulary: ګل (gul 'flower'), ګران (grān 'expensive/heavy'), and is taught as a separate letter in Afghan primary schools. Established a distinct Pashto Gāf that is not interchangeable with Persian گ in Unicode or pedagogy; font designers must support both; cross-border text exchange between Persian and Pashto requires codepoint awareness.

The Vernacular Underground: Gāf in Arabic DialectsDialect orthography studies (Holes 1995 on Iraqi Arabic; Caubet on Moroccan Darija); social media corpus studies

19th century to present (especially post-2000 digital informal writing)

Modern Standard Arabic lacks /g/, but many dialects have it: Iraqi /g/ from historical /q/ (e.g., qalb > galb 'heart'), Moroccan /g/ from Berber substrate, Egyptian /g/ from /dʒ/. Speakers writing dialect informally needed a letter. Three competing solutions emerged: Iraq/Levant adopted Persian گ (گهوة gahwa for قهوة), Morocco adopted ݣ/ڭ (based on Kāf/Qāf with three dots, e.g., أݣادير Agadir), Tunisia/Algeria adopted ڨ (Qāf with three dots above). None is standard in MSA; all are considered non-standard but are ubiquitous in texting, advertising, and dialect literature. The 2020s saw increased Unicode support and keyboard inclusion for these vernacular letters, legitimizing them digitally despite academy resistance. Created a fragmented, region-specific Gāf landscape where the same sound is written four different ways across the Arab world; caused encoding and font challenges; highlighted tension between prescriptive MSA and descriptive dialect writing.

Gāf is an original Arabic letter. — Arabic's 28-letter abjad has no /g/ phoneme and no Gāf. Gāf was invented by Persian scribes (9th-10th century) by adding a stroke to Kāf, then borrowed back into Arabic only for writing Persian loanwords and later for dialectal /g/. Classical Arabic grammarians (Sibawayh) do not list it.

All Arabic-script /g/ letters are the same Gāf. — There are at least four distinct lineages: Kāf+line (گ Persian), Kāf+dot (ݢ Jawi), Kāf+ring (ګ Pashto), and Qāf+dots (ڨ Tunisian, ݣ Moroccan). They have different Unicode codepoints, histories, and regional correctness. Using Persian گ in a Moroccan Berber text is orthographically incorrect.

Gāf and Qāf are interchangeable for /g/. — In Persian, Urdu, and Pashto they are phonemically distinct: گ /g/ vs. ق /q/ (uvular) vs. ک /k/. Minimal pairs exist (e.g., Persian گر ghar 'cave' vs. قرن qarn 'century'). In Arabic dialects, historic /q/ > /g/ shift (e.g., Iraqi galb for qalb) creates overlap, but orthographically they remain distinct in standard orthography.

The Jawi letter ݢ is just a font variant of Persian گ. — Unicode encodes them separately (U+06AF vs. U+0762) because Malay manuscript heritage and sorting rules require distinction. Jawi ݢ is based on Arabic Kāf (ك) with a dot, Persian گ on Keheh (ک) with a line; they have different joining behavior in some fonts and different abjad values.

Gāf has always looked like گ. — Early manuscripts show experimentation: Kāf with two dots, with hamza, or undifferentiated from Kāf. The single diagonal line became standard only after the 11th century, and regional forms (ring, dot, three dots) diverged later for Pashto, Jawi, and Pegon.
