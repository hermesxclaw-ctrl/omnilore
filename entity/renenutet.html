Who It Is

Picture the granary at the height of the harvest, and the moment every pair of hands in the work-team freezes: over the piled grain, a cobra has spread its hood. In ancient Egypt, that cobra was not a threat to flee but a goddess to thank — Renenutet, 'She Who Nourishes,' the rearing serpent who guarded Egypt's food and, with the same body, guarded the king from birth to grave. She is the rare deity who is both wet-nurse and venom: the milk that feeds the pharaoh and the fire-spitting eye that burns his enemies off the battlefield are one and the same creature. She fed Egypt, named its children's fortune, and — bent under the later weight of Isis and of a Greek princess legend that tried to set a serpent against the infant Moses — never quite shed her cobra skin.

Dual-natured. In her nurture aspect Renenutet is a gentle nurse, mother, and wet-nurse who rears the pharaoh and feeds the people; she 'maintains everybody' and is a bringer of happiness associated with milk and breastfeeding. In her protective/solar aspect she is a fierce guardian whose cobra-stare (like Wadjet's) defeats enemies, and as a goddess of the scorching sun she could be linked to destructive burning. She is fundamentally a threshold-guardian: she protects the food-store, the newborn, the pharaoh, and the boundary of the field.

Powers
Nourishment and 'rearing' of the pharaoh

Renenutet nursed, fondled, and reared the pharaoh from birth to death. She is repeatedly called 'mistress of provisions,' 'mistress of the offerings,' 'mistress of the food,' and 'the venerable of the double granary.' She 'maintains everybody.' Her name itself encodes the nursing/rearing function.

Limit: A nurture/ecological function rather than a war-vector; in her nursing aspect she does not harm.

Protection of food stores against pests

Her images, set in granaries, kitchens, ovens, cellars, and on wine-stoppers, guard stored food against insects, mice, and snakes. She combines provider with protector-of-the-provided — a functional, almost apotropaic household role.

Limit: Tied to household/field rather than cosmic danger.

The uraeus-stare / destructive solar eye

Syncretized with Wadjet, Renenutet could exercise the cobra-gaze 'which slaughters enemies.' As a solar-protective cobra she joins the eye-of-Ra complex, where the uraeus spits fire from the solar eye at the enemies of the sun-god and king.

Limit: Her destructive aspect is activated by threat to the king or the sun; she is not an untethered destroyer.

Determination of individual fortune / the 'renen'

Renenutet is the agent of the individual's renen, the fortune/outcome assigned at birth. As counterpart and consort-form of Shai ('destiny'), she assigns the 'ren' (name/fortune) that determines a person's life-course, complementing Shai's fixing of life's length.

Limit: Her role is paired and subordinate in some late theologies: Isis ultimately rules over both Renenutet and Shai.

Fertility of the fields (vegetation and soil)

Renenutet embodied the fertility of both the vegetation and the soil itself. Combined with Sobek (the inundation that deposited silt), she turned the Faiyum's annual flood into abundant yield; she blesses the first fruits of the harvest and wine production.

Limit: Her agrarian power is seasonally tied to the flood cycle (cooperates with Sobek/Hapi).

Appearance — Renenutet is most characteristically a standing cobra (uraeus) — the hooded Egyptian cobra reared up — or a woman with the head of a cobra. When shown as a full female figure she often wears an open crown of horned disk and double plumes, and she may nurse or hold the young Horus/prince. Accounts I
The Birth of the Grain and the Festival of RenenutetFestival tradition (New Kingdom origin) + temple liturgy

New Kingdom onward; the festival fell at the close of Pharmouthi and the opening of Pashons

The annual Festival of Renenutet marked the first day of the harvest and was understood as the day on which Renenutet gave birth to her son Nepri, the god of grain. The rites symbolically linked the birth of child-gods to the gathering of the new crops — the divine infants were providers of fertility whose cyclical rebirth, as young solar gods, ensured the sun's regeneration. The reigning monarch was woven into the festival as the divine child of the local triad, reinforcing royal-succession legitimacy. In Thebes the festival came especially to honor the child-god Khonsu-pa-khered, after whom the month Pashons was named. Festive anointing of the harvest, royal legitimacy, and the astro-agrarian calendar link between the harvest and the newborn sun.

Accounts II
The Nurse of the PharaohRoyal and funerary doctrine; 'she who maintains everybody'

Old Kingdom–New Kingdom

Renenutet was the 'nurse' who tended the pharaoh from birth to death, rearing him like a wet-nurse rears an infant. As the royal wet-nurse in cobra form she stands guard at the crown, her hood spread as protection; she is 'the venerable of the double granary,' feeding both the king and the two lands. The nurturing serpent who suckles the king thereby fuses maternal care with the venomous protection of sovereignty. Establishes the milk/venom duality of the royal uraeus and the theological claim that the king is fed and guarded by the harvest-goddess-nurse herself.

Mother of NehebkauCoffin Texts spell tradition; Geb's paternal link to snakes

Middle Kingdom (Coffin Texts) onward

When considered the mother of Nehebkau — the primeval snake god who guards the souls of the grain and is sometimes counted among the children of Geb and the earth — Renenutet is cast as Geb's consort and the mother of the serpent. Nehebkau is occasionally himself shown as a snake, so Renenutet, the parent snake of the snake god, crowns a serpentine genealogy of the underworld's fertility. The pairing with Geb (the earth god and father of snakes) gives Renenutet a consort through the earth-divinity whose body grows barley on its ribs. Anchors Renenutet within the earth-fertility cosmology and explains her snake-genealogy link to the underworld.
