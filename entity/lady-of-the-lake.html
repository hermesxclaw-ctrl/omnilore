Who It Is

The Lady of the Lake is one of the most enigmatic and powerful figures in Arthurian legend — a faerie enchantress who dwells in an otherworldly domain beneath (or beyond) a lake, who gives Arthur his sword Excalibur, who steals the infant Lancelot and raises him to be the perfect knight, and who ultimately imprisons Merlin in a cave or tree, removing the greatest magical protector from Arthur's court. Her identity is fluid across different texts: she is sometimes named Nimue, Viviane, Ninienne, or Eviene; sometimes she is a single figure, sometimes one of several Ladies of the Lake. She is a gatekeeper between the human world and Avalon, between chivalry and magic, between the male order of the Round Table and the feminine power of the otherworld.

Her most iconic moment is the presentation of Excalibur. In Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur, as Arthur rides by a lake after breaking the Sword from the Stone, an arm 'clothed in white samite' rises from the water holding a sword. The Lady of the Lake appears and tells Arthur that the sword — Excalibur — is hers to give, and she will give it in exchange for a future gift. Arthur accepts, and the exchange establishes a covenant that will define his reign: masculine rule is enabled by feminine magic, but the debt must eventually be paid.

The Lady of the Lake's relationship with Merlin is the most disturbing and compelling aspect of her story. In the Vulgate Merlin and Malory, Nimue (as the Lady is named) is a young woman who becomes Merlin's student and lover. Merlin teaches her magic, but she uses his own teachings to imprison him — in a cave, under a stone, or within a hawthorn tree — where he remains forever. Her motivations vary: in some versions, she fears his sexual advances; in others, she is simply ambitious and coldly eliminates the only power greater than her own. Merlin, who foresees his own doom but is powerless to prevent it, walks into the trap willingly, undone by love.

Despite the apparent villainy of imprisoning Merlin, the Lady of the Lake is not consistently villainous. She raises Lancelot with love and care, making him the greatest knight in the world. She saves Arthur from the enchanted sword of Sir Accolon. She appears at the end of Arthur's life as one of the three queens who bear him to Avalon. Her moral ambiguity is essential to her character: she is of the otherworld, and otherworldly ethics do not map cleanly onto human categories of good and evil. She is the giver of Excalibur and the taker of Merlin; the foster-mother of Lancelot and the architect of his tragedy. She is the most visible manifestation of the feminine supernatural in Arthurian literature — a power that enables, constrains, and ultimately survives the masculine world of the Round Table.
