Who It Is

Astylochus never speaks, never fights alone, and never earns an epithet — he is one of the fifty-odd young lords from Dulichium who sailed to Ithaca to win a queen who did not want them, ate another man's livestock for three years, and died in his hall without ever being singled out by name in the act of dying.

No individual behavior or speech is attributed to Astylochus. Collectively, the suitors from Dulichium are characterized in Odyssey as: occupying Odysseus's oikos uninvited for ca. three years; consuming his livestock and wine; pressuring Penelope to choose a husband; plotting to murder Telemachus (Od. 16.364-392, 20.241-242); insulting the disguised Odysseus and the beggar Irus; and failing the bow contest through lack of strength and divine disfavor. Mortal aristocratic status and martial capacity

As a mnestēr from Dulichium, Astylochus is implicitly a young elite male of sufficient birth and wealth to contend for Penelope and to bear arms. The suitors collectively are described as capable of hunting, feasting, and fighting, but inferior to Odysseus, Telemachus, and the loyal herdsmen in combat. No supernatural power, divine parentage, or heroic feat is attributed to him.

Limit: Mortality; no divine protection. In Odyssey 22, Athena actively aids Odysseus and hinders the suitors (22.205-223). The suitors' spears miss or are turned aside; their numbers do not overcome the four defenders. Astylochus shares this collective vulnerability.

No attested individual skill, weapon, or artifact

No source assigns Astylochus a personal weapon, armor, horse, ship, or heirloom. In the Mnesterophonia, suitors grab swords, spears, and tables as shields (Od. 22.74-86), but no item is linked to Astylochus by name. He does not wield a named bow, nor does he possess magical or ritual ability.

Limit: Complete lack of attestation means no powers can be reconstructed. Not to be filled by analogy to leaders like Antinous or Eurymachus.

Accounts I
The Catalogue of Suitors: Fifty-two (or Fifty-seven) from DulichiumHomer, Odyssey 16.247-253; Apollodorus, Epitome 7.26

Archaic epic (8th-7th c. BCE) with Hellenistic-Roman mythographic elaboration (1st-2nd c. CE)

With Odysseus presumed dead after twenty years' absence, chieftains and princes from the surrounding islands gather to press Penelope to remarry and thus claim Odysseus's kingship and household. Homer has Telemachus, guided by Athena-Mentor, learn the scale of the threat: 52 men from Dulichium, 24 from Same, 20 from Zacynthus, and 12 from Ithaca itself, led in Ithaca by Antinous and Eurymachus. Apollodorus, drawing on a fuller or later list, gives 57 from Dulichium — among whom the manuscripts include Astylochus — plus 23, 44, and 12 respectively. The Dulichians are the largest contingent, reflecting Dulichium's size and proximity. Astylochus is not singled out; his name is one of those supplied to fill the anonymous mass, indicating he sailed from Dulichium with the others and took up residence in or around Odysseus's palace. Establishes the numerical and political pressure on Penelope and Telemachus; explains why the palace economy is strained and why Telemachus must seek allies in Pylos and Sparta. The Occupation of the Palace and Penelope's StratagemHomer, Odyssey 1.106-155, 2.85-128, 19.137-156; Apollodorus, Epitome 7.27-28

Archaic epic / Classical reception

For three years the suitors lodge in Odysseus's megaron, feasting daily on his cattle, sheep, goats and wine, and demanding Penelope choose one of them. Penelope delays by the famous weaving trick: she promises to choose after finishing a shroud for Laertes, weaving by day and unraveling by night for three years until a maid betrays her (Od. 2.93-110, 19.149-152). During this occupation the suitors collectively harass the household, insult guests, abuse the beggar Irus, and exploit enslaved women (notably Melantho). Astylochus, as one of the Dulichians, is understood to be present throughout this period, sharing in the feasting and pressure, though no anecdote isolates him. Apollodorus summarizes the same occupation more briefly. The delay preserves Penelope's fidelity and Odysseus's kingship, but intensifies the suitors' impatience and their plot against Telemachus. The moral framing of the Odyssey condemns the suitors' violation of xenia (guest-host law) and oikos, justifying their eventual killing.

Accounts II
The Plot Against TelemachusHomer, Odyssey 4.625-672, 16.364-392, 20.241-242

Archaic epic

When Telemachus sails to Pylos and Sparta to seek news of Odysseus, Antinous proposes ambushing and murdering him on his return, setting a ship at Asteris strait. Amphinomus dissents mildly, but the majority consent. The plot is overheard by Medon the herald and revealed to Penelope, who is distressed. Athena thwarts the ambush, and Telemachus slips past. The episode shows the suitors crossing from parasitic guests to active murderers. As a member of the body that 'all approved' Antinous's plan (Od. 16.394-398 notes dissent only from Amphinomus), Astylochus is implicitly complicit, though not named as a speaker or actor. Marks the suitors as legally and morally killable in Greek terms; their intent to kill the heir justifies Odysseus's revenge. Telemachus's survival allows him to return and join his father.

The Return of Odysseus as Beggar and the Bow ContestHomer, Odyssey 13-21 (especially 16.1-320, 17.1-606, 18.1-428, 19.1-604, 21.1-434); Apollodorus, Epitome 7.31-32

Archaic epic

Odysseus, returned to Ithaca via Phaeacia, is disguised as a beggar by Athena and lodged by Eumaeus. He observes the suitors' behavior in the hall: they mock him, throw a stool (Ctesippus), and are rebuked by Telemachus and Penelope. Penelope, prompted by Athena and Odysseus, proposes the contest of the bow: whoever strings Odysseus's great bow and shoots through twelve axe heads will win her hand (Od. 21.1-79). One by one the suitors try and fail - Leodes first, then others; Eurymachus nearly succeeds but fails. The Dulichians, including Astylochus, are among those who attempt and fail, demonstrating they lack Odysseus's strength and are not favored by the gods. Telemachus almost strings the bow himself but is stopped by Odysseus. Odysseus then reveals himself to Eumaeus and Philoetius and prepares the slaughter. The failed contest proves the suitors unworthy to succeed Odysseus; it also gets the bow into Odysseus's hands and clears the hall of weapons (Telemachus removes arms at Odysseus's instruction, Od. 19.1-33). Astylochus's failure is collective and implicit.

The Mnesterophonia: Slaughter of the Suitors in the HallHomer, Odyssey 22.1-329; Apollodorus, Epitome 7.33; cf. Odyssey 24.1-14 (psychopompeia)

Archaic epic and mythographic summary

With the bow in hand, Odysseus casts off his rags, strings the bow without effort, and shoots Antinous through the throat (Od. 22.8-21). He reveals himself: 'Dogs, you thought I would never return...' (22.35-41). The suitors scramble for weapons, but Telemachus has removed them; they find only the bow and spears Odysseus left. Eurymachus appeals, is shot; Amphinomus charges and is killed by Telemachus. Melanthius tries to bring armor from the storeroom but is bound by Eumaeus and Philoetius. Athena, appearing as Mentor, deflects suitors' spears and terrifies them as a swallow. Odysseus, Telemachus, Eumaeus and Philoetius kill the entire mass - Homer says not one escaped (22.380-381). Apollodorus explicitly: 'with the help of Eumaeus and Philoetius and Telemachus, Odysseus slew all the suitors' (Epit. 7.33). Astylochus dies here, struck down in the hall, presumably by arrow or spear, among the heap of bodies. Astylochus was a major character with his own story, speeches, or heroic deeds. — No speech, deed, or physical description is attributed to him in any ancient source. He is a catalogue name only, one of the anonymous Dulichian mass. All narratives about him are collective suitor narratives; his individuality is modern encyclopedic convenience.

Homer names Astylochus among the suitors. — Homer never names Astylochus. Homer gives only numbers for Dulichium (52) and names a handful of suitors individually, none of whom is Astylochus. The name survives only in Pseudo-Apollodorus's Epitome and works derived from it.

The suitors were all from Ithaca and were Penelope's local neighbors. — Only 12 suitors were Ithacans (led by Antinous and Eurymachus). The majority were from Dulichium (52/57), Same (24/23), and Zacynthus (20/44), i.e., from surrounding islands. Astylochus was explicitly Dulichian, not Ithacan.

Astylochus survived or escaped the slaughter, or was spared as a 'good suitor' like Amphinomus or Leodes. — No source spares Astylochus. Only two figures are spared in Odyssey 22: the herald Medon and the bard Phemius (and arguably Halitherses is not a suitor). Even the relatively decent Amphinomus and the innocent Leodes are killed. Astylochus dies with the rest; Apollodorus says 'all' were slain.
