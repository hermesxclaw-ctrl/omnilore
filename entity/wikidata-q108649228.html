Who It Is

He has no speeches, no backstory, no single deed to his name — only a homeland, a hungry claim on another man's house, and a death in a hall that suddenly filled with arrows. Astylochus of Dulichium is one of the 50-odd suitors who bet that Penelope's husband would never come home, and whose wager is settled in Book 22 of the Odyssey when the beggar on the threshold strings his bow.

No individualized speech, motive, or personality is attested. Collectively, the suitors (including Astylochus) are characterized in the Odyssey as: violating xenia by indefinitely occupying the oikos, consuming its livestock and wine, pressuring Penelope to remarry, plotting to murder Telemachus (Od. 4.660-672, 16.364-392), and insulting the disguised Odysseus and loyal servants. Whether Astylochus participated in the ambush plot is not individually stated but he is present as part of the collective. Aristocratic status and martial equipment (implied)

As a mnéstér (suitor) from Dulichium, Astylochus is implicitly of elite birth, entitled to bear arms, attend feasts, and compete for Penelope. Like other suitors he would be armed when the slaughter begins (swords, spears hung on hall walls, Od. 22.24-25), but Homer notes Telemachus had removed most arms beforehand.

Limit: Mortal human; no supernatural power, divine parentage, or heroic feat is attested. Outmatched by Odysseus with bow and by divine support (Athena). Collective failure to string Odysseus' bow (Od. 21) applies to him as part of group.

As one of the Dulichian suitors he is implicitly a young adult aristocrat (kouros / mnéstér) of marriageable and military age, but Homer gives individualized appearance only to leaders Antinous and Eurymachus. In visual tradition the suitors as a group are sometimes shown as banqueters or as fallen bodies beneath Odysseus' arrows (e.g., Campanian red-figure, 4th c. Domains — Habitat: Dulichium (Δουλίχιον) as point of origin; during narrative, the palace (megaron) of Odysseus on Ithaca. Dulichium's location is itself disputed (see Contradictions) but is an island in the Echinades group off the west coast of Greece, part of Odysseus' former kingdom or allied territory. Domain: secular aristocratic competition for Penelope's hand and control of the Ithacan oikos and its prestige (geras). No divine domain.

Accounts I
The Catalogue of the Suitors: 52 (or 57) from DulichiumHomer, Odyssey 16.245-253; Apollodorus, Epitome 7.26

Archaic epic (8th-7th c. BCE) fixed; mythographic codification 1st-2nd c. CE

Telemachus, returned from Pylos and Sparta, is told by Odysseus (still disguised) how many enemies occupy his hall. Homer enumerates: 52 from Dulichium, 24 from Same, 20 from Zacynthus, 12 from Ithaca — 108 total. Apollodorus, drawing on a fuller catalogue tradition, lists 57 from Dulichium (including Astylochus by name), 23 from Same, 44 from Zacynthus, 12 from Ithaca. Astylochus appears only as a name in this list, with no patronymic or deed. The catalogue establishes the scale of the violation of xenia and the odds Odysseus will face. Narratively justifies Odysseus' need for divine aid and stratagem; establishes Astylochus' membership in the doomed group. Mythographically preserves his name for later lexica.

The Occupation of the Palace and Violation of XeniaHomer, Odyssey Books 1, 2, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20

Archaic

For nearly four years (three years of Penelope's weaving ruse plus one), the suitors, including Astylochus, have lodged themselves in Odysseus' megaron, feasting daily on his cattle, sheep, goats and wine, compelling his servants, insulting guests, and pressing Penelope to choose a new husband. They claim to be courting honorably but Homer frames their stay as hubris and a violation of Zeus-protected xenia (guest-host law). Astylochus is never singled out but is collectively responsible for this occupation; no source exonerates any Dulichian. Creates the moral and legal justification for their eventual killing; in Greek ethics, violation of xenia is punishable by the gods. Sets stage for Athena's support of Odysseus.

The Plot to Murder TelemachusHomer, Odyssey 4.660-672, 16.364-392, 16.406-408

Archaic

Antinous proposes and the suitors agree to ambush and kill Telemachus on his return from Pylos, stationing a ship at Asteris. The plot is overheard by Penelope and later revealed to Telemachus by Athena/Mentor. Homer does not name which suitors volunteer for the ambush crew; the plan is presented as collective. As a Dulichian present in the hall, Astylochus is by implication complicit in the conspiracy, though no line names him as an ambusher. The plot fails when Telemachus evades the strait via Athena's guidance. Escalates suitors' guilt from parasitism to attempted kin-murder, sealing their fate. Telemachus' survival allows him to fight alongside his father.

Accounts II
The Contest of the Bow and the UnmaskingHomer, Odyssey 21.1-434

Archaic

Penelope, prompted by Athena and Odysseus, brings out Odysseus' great bow and axes, declaring she will marry whoever strings the bow and shoots through twelve axe heads. One by one the suitors try and fail, unable to string it; Eurymachus and Antinous fail last. Telemachus nearly succeeds but is signaled to stop. The disguised Odysseus requests a turn, over suitors' objections, and strings it effortlessly. Astylochus is not named among those who attempt, but as a suitor he is present, fails implicitly, and witnesses the unmasking. This is his last living scene before the slaughter. Divine sign that legitimate kingship returns; suitors' failure proves their unworthiness. Triggers immediate transition to Mnesterophonia.

The Mnesterophonia — Slaughter in the Hall (Death of Astylochus)Homer, Odyssey 22.1-329; Apollodorus, Epitome 7.32-33

Archaic (Homer); 1st-2nd c. CE (Apollodorus)

At Odysseus' signal, he shoots Antinous through the throat, then reveals himself. Eurymachus is killed next. The suitors rush for weapons but find the hall stripped (Telemachus had removed arms). With Athena's aid and with Telemachus, Eumaeus and Philoetius fighting beside him, Odysseus kills the remaining suitors one by one. Homer describes the killing as collective — 'they fell as thrushes or doves' — without naming most victims individually. Apollodorus confirms: 'with the help of Eumaeus and Philoetius and Telemachus, Odysseus slew all the suitors.' Astylochus dies here, struck by arrow, spear or sword; no source gives him a distinctive death or last words. His body is later removed and burial is denied initially, then suitors' kin retrieve them (Od. 24). Restoration of Odysseus' oikos and kingship; triggers vengeance of suitors' families and Athena's final peace in Odyssey 24. Astylochus' death is the only narrative closure his figure receives.

The Suitors in the UnderworldHomer, Odyssey 24.1-204 (Second Nekyia)

Archaic

Hermes leads the psuchai (souls) of the slain suitors, gibbering like bats, to Hades. There they meet Achilles and Agamemnon, who hear Amphimedon's account of their death. The suitors are presented as a chattering crowd, not as individuals. Astylochus, as one of the slain, is implicitly among this procession, though not named. His shade joins the collective suitor dead, whose story is retold by Amphimedon as a cautionary tale of hubris. Epic closure: the suitors' story is memorialized in the underworld, contrasting Odysseus' nostos with Agamemnon's murder. No individual afterlife for Astylochus is described.

Astylochus was a prince or had a known father and lineage. — No patronymic, father, or lineage is given in any source. Unlike major suitors (Antinous son of Eupeithes, Eurymachus son of Polybus), Astylochus appears as a bare name in a list. Any family tree assigning him parents is modern invention.

Astylochus had a distinctive death scene or was killed by Telemachus specifically. — Homer describes the slaughter collectively and names only a few victims individually (Antinous, Eurymachus, Amphinomus, Leodes). No source singles out who killed Astylochus — whether Odysseus, Telemachus, Eumaeus or Philoetius. Apollodorus says all four killed suitors collectively.

The suitors, including Astylochus, were all from Ithaca and were Penelope's neighbors. — Only 12 suitors were Ithacan; the majority were from Dulichium (52/57), Same (24), and Zacynthus (20). Astylochus was explicitly Dulichian, i.e., an off-island aristocrat who traveled to Ithaca to compete.

Astylochus appears as a character in games like Hades or Assassin's Creed Odyssey. — No video game, film, or novel to date gives Astylochus an individualized appearance or dialogue. He appears only as an unnamed member of the suitor collective when suitors appear at all. Search of credits, scripts, and game databases shows no Astylochus NPC.
