Who It Is

Before it was a sparkly sticker on a laptop, the unicorn was a monster: a fierce, untamable beast that could only be caught by a virgin, whose horn could purge poison and purify water. It was a real animal to ancient Greeks, a symbol of Christ to medieval Christians, and a national emblem of Scotland. And it may have been a rhinoceros all along.

Greek sources describe the unicorn as fierce and untamable, impossible to capture alive. Medieval bestiaries (following the Physiologus) describe it as gentle and shy, but only approachable by a virgin maiden; it will lay its head in her lap and fall asleep, allowing hunters to capture it. In heraldry, the unicorn is a symbol of strength and purity, often depicted chained to show its power is controlled.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Latin 'unicornis' (one-horned), itself from Latin 'unus' (one) and 'cornu' (horn); the Greek term is 'monokeros' (single horn), used by Ctesias and later naturalists. The English word derives via Old French 'unicorne'.

Pronunciation — YOO-ni-korn (English); mə-NOK-ər-əs (Greek-derived 'monoceros')

Aliases — Monocerus, Monoceros, Karkadann (Persian/Arabic), Qilin (Chinese counterpart, distinct)

Ownership — Ancient Greek and Indian accounts (as a real animal); later developed in medieval European bestiaries, heraldry, and Christian allegory; the qilin is a separate Chinese mythologica

Powers
Poison purification

The horn of the unicorn (alicorn) was believed to neutralize poison, making cups carved from it able to detect or nullify poisoned drinks. This belief is attested in Greek (Ctesias), Roman (Pliny, Isidore), and medieval European sources, and persisted into the early modern period.

Limit: No evidence that a real horn (e.g., narwhal tusk sold as alicorn) ever worked; the belief was disproven by modern science.

Untamability

Greek and Roman sources (Pliny, Solinus) describe the unicorn as impossible to capture alive, due to its ferocity and speed.

Limit: Only a virgin can tame it (medieval version); otherwise it is deadly.

Healing properties

The horn was believed to cure epilepsy, plague, and other diseases, and to purify water. This belief was widespread in medieval and Renaissance Europe, leading to a trade in narwhal tusks sold as unicorn horns.

Limit: No medicinal efficacy; the belief was based on the horn's rarity and association with purity.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In Greek sources (Ctesias): a wild ass-sized animal with a white body, purple/blue head, and a single horn (about 1.5 cubits long) that is white at the base, black in the middle, and red at the tip. In Pliny: a horse-like body with a stag's head, elephant's feet, boar's tail, and a single black horn. Symbols — The horn (alicorn) as a symbol of purity, healing, and protection against poison; the unicorn as a symbol of chastity, virginity, and Christ (in Christian bestiaries); in heraldry, a symbol of strength, loyalty, and Scotland (as the national animal). The color white symbolizes purity; the spiral horn represents divine power.

Domains — Terrestrial realm; found in the wilds of India (Greek accounts), in forests and mountains (medieval European bestiaries), and in heraldic iconography as a symbol of royalty and purity. In Christian allegory, the unicorn represents Christ or the Virgin Mary, and its horn symbolizes spiritual healing.

Accounts I
The Unicorn and the Virgin (Physiologus)Physiologus (Greek, c. 2nd–4th century CE)

c. 2nd–4th century CE

The unicorn is a small, gentle animal that cannot be captured by hunters. Only a pure virgin can lure it; it will approach her, lay its head in her lap, and fall asleep, allowing hunters to capture it. The story is allegorized as Christ being captured by the Virgin Mary. The allegory became a standard motif in medieval art and literature, symbolizing the Incarnation and the purity of the Virgin Mary.

The Unicorn as a Real Indian Beast (Ctesias)Ctesias, 'Indica' (fragments in Photius)

c. 400 BCE

Ctesias describes a wild ass-sized animal in India with a white body, purple head, and a single horn; the horn is used to make cups that protect against poison. This is the earliest written account and set the pattern for later naturalists. Established the unicorn as a real, if exotic, animal in Greek natural history; later authors (Aristotle, Pliny) repeated and embellished it.

Accounts II
The Unicorn in the Bible (KJV translation of 're'em')Bible (King James Version, 1611)

1611

The Hebrew word 're'em' is translated as 'unicorn' in the KJV, appearing in several passages (Numbers 23:22, Job 39:9–10, Psalm 22:21, etc.). The 'unicorn' is depicted as a powerful, untamable beast, symbolizing God's strength. The KJV translation cemented the unicorn in English-speaking Christian tradition, despite modern scholarship identifying the animal as a wild ox (aurochs) or oryx.

The Unicorn Tapestries (The Hunt of the Unicorn)Seven tapestries, possibly woven in the Southern Netherlands, now at the Metropolitan Museum of Art (The Cloisters)

c. 1495–1505 CE

A series of seven tapestries depicting a unicorn hunt, from the initial sighting to the capture and resurrection of the unicorn. The tapestries are rich in symbolism, often interpreted as an allegory of the Passion of Christ or of courtly love. The tapestries are a masterpiece of medieval art and a key visual source for the unicorn's iconography in the West.

Unicorns are a purely European invention. — The earliest written account is from Greek natural history (Ctesias), based on Indian accounts of the rhinoceros; independent unicorn-like creatures appear in Chinese (qilin) and Persian (karkadann) traditions.

Unicorn horns are real and have magical healing properties. — No real unicorn horn exists; medieval 'unicorn horns' were often narwhal tusks, which have no magical properties.

The unicorn in the Bible is a mythical creature. — The Hebrew 're'em' likely referred to a real animal (wild ox/aurochs), not a mythical one; the KJV translation is a later interpretation.
