Who It Is

The unicorn is a legendary creature typically depicted as a white horse with a single spiral horn projecting from its forehead. Its mythology evolved across millennia and cultures. The earliest written accounts come from the Greek physician Ctesias, who described the 'wild ass of India' — a fleet-footed animal with a white body, red head, blue eyes, and a single multicolored horn whose shavings could neutralize poison. These medicinal properties became central to the unicorn legend throughout the medieval period, leading to a thriving trade in 'unicorn horn' (usually narwhal tusk) believed to detect and cure poisons. In medieval Christianity, the unicorn became an allegory for Christ: the fierce, untamable creature that could only be captured by a virgin maiden, symbolizing the Incarnation and Christ's sacrifice. The famous 'Unicorn Tapestries' at The Cloisters depict this hunt allegory in exquisite detail. The unicorn also appears in the King James Bible (though as a mistranslation of the Hebrew re'em, a wild ox) and in medieval bestiaries as a symbol of purity and strength. In heraldry, particularly Scottish, the unicorn represents power tempered by gentleness — it is the national animal of Scotland and appears on the Royal Coat of Arms of the United Kingdom. Modern fantasy has reimagined unicorns as ethereal forest-dwelling beings of pure magic, a trope popularized by works like The Last Unicorn by Peter S. Beagle.

Attested in Folk TaleEra Ctesias (c. 400 BCE) describes a wild ass with a single horn from India; Indus Valley seals (c. 2600 BCE) depict unicorn-like animals

Form

In Greek sources (Ctesias): a wild ass-sized animal with a white body, purple/blue head, and a single horn (about 1.5 cubits long) that is white at the base, black in the middle, and red at the tip. In Pliny: a horse-like body with a stag's head, elephant's feet, boar's tail, and a single black horn. In medieval bestiaries: a small, goat-like or horse-like creature with a single spiral horn, often white or silver, with cloven hooves (associated with purity). In heraldry: a horse-like creature with a goat's beard, lion's tail, and a long spiral horn, often shown with a collar and chain (in Scottish arms).

Powers

Poison purification The horn of the unicorn (alicorn) was believed to neutralize poison, making cups carved from it able to detect or nullify poisoned drinks. This belief is attested in Greek (Ctesias), Roman (Pliny, Isidore), and medieval European sources, and persisted into the early modern period. Attested in Greek, Roman, and medieval bestiaries; also in apothecary lore. No evidence that a real horn (e.g., narwhal tusk sold as alicorn) ever worked; the belief was disproven by modern science. Some sources say the horn only purifies water, others say it heals all poisons; no consensus in ancient texts.

Untamability Greek and Roman sources (Pliny, Solinus) describe the unicorn as impossible to capture alive, due to its ferocity and speed. Attested in Pliny and Solinus; contradicted by medieval bestiary accounts where it is captured by a virgin. Only a virgin can tame it (medieval version); otherwise it is deadly. The medieval virgin-capture legend contradicts the ancient untamability; some later texts harmonize by saying the unicorn is only gentle with virgins, but fierce otherwise.

Healing properties The horn was believed to cure epilepsy, plague, and other diseases, and to purify water. This belief was widespread in medieval and Renaissance Europe, leading to a trade in narwhal tusks sold as unicorn horns. Attested in medieval bestiaries and apothecary manuals; the trade in narwhal tusks is documented in European market records. No medicinal efficacy; the belief was based on the horn's rarity and association with purity. Some sources claim the horn heals all diseases; others only mention poison resistance.

Behavior & Habitat

Greek sources describe the unicorn as fierce and untamable, impossible to capture alive. Medieval bestiaries (following the Physiologus) describe it as gentle and shy, but only approachable by a virgin maiden; it will lay its head in her lap and fall asleep, allowing hunters to capture it. In heraldry, the unicorn is a symbol of strength and purity, often depicted chained to show its power is controlled.

Accounts

The unicorn is a small, gentle animal that cannot be captured by hunters. Only a pure virgin can lure it; it will approach her, lay its head in her lap, and fall asleep, allowing hunters to capture it. The story is allegorized as Christ being captured by the Virgin Mary.

Ctesias describes a wild ass-sized animal in India with a white body, purple head, and a single horn; the horn is used to make cups that protect against poison. This is the earliest written account and set the pattern for later naturalists.

The Hebrew word 're'em' is translated as 'unicorn' in the KJV, appearing in several passages (Numbers 23:22, Job 39:9–10, Psalm 22:21, etc.). The 'unicorn' is depicted as a powerful, untamable beast, symbolizing God's strength.

A series of seven tapestries depicting a unicorn hunt, from the initial sighting to the capture and resurrection of the unicorn. The tapestries are rich in symbolism, often interpreted as an allegory of the Passion of Christ or of courtly love.

Field Notes

❀ The unicorn is the national animal of Scotland, appearing on the UK royal arms.

❀ Medieval 'unicorn horns' sold in apothecaries were often narwhal tusks, which were worth a fortune.

❀ Marco Polo explicitly debunked the unicorn myth, saying the 'unicorn' he saw in Sumatra was 'ugly' and 'not at all like we describe them'.

❀ The unicorn is one of the few mythical creatures that appears in the Bible (in the KJV translation), though it was likely a wild ox.

❀ The Unicorn Tapestries at The Cloisters are one of the most famous surviving medieval artworks.

Scholarly Debates
The unicorn's appearance
Ctesias: wild ass-sized, white body, purple head, horn with three colors (white, black, red). ⇌ Pliny: horse-like body, stag's head, elephant's feet, boar's tail, black horn.
The unicorn's temperament
Ancient sources: fierce, untamable, impossible to capture. ⇌ Medieval bestiaries: gentle, shy, captured by a virgin.
The identity of the Biblical 're'em'
KJV translates as 'unicorn'. ⇌ Modern scholarship: aurochs (wild ox) or oryx.

What We Don't Know

The exact origin of the unicorn's association with virgins is unclear; the Physiologus is the earliest source, but its own sources are unknown. The relationship between the Greek 'monokeros' and the Hebrew 're'em' is still debated. The unicorn's iconography in Eastern traditions (qilin, karkadann) is distinct but rarely compared in Western scholarship; a full comparative study is lacking.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of the unicorn as a cultural shapeshifter: it started as a traveler's tall tale about a one-horned Indian beast, became a symbol of Christ and purity in medieval Europe, and now is a universal symbol of fantasy, uniqueness, and internet whimsy. Its history is a mirror of how humans project their hopes and fears onto the unknown.
