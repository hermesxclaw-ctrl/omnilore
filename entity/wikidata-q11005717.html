Who It Is

You will not see him arrive. The milk you left out is gone, the horse's mane is braided into impossible knots overnight, a cold laugh flicks behind the pantry door — and a blue-white light bobs over the marsh to lead you astray. In French and Catalan hearths they call him le follet, the little mad one: not quite fairy, not quite demon, a household sprite who can be the best servant you never hired or the worst poltergeist you cannot evict.

Ambivalent trickster-household spirit. Attested behaviors: nocturnal domestic work (sweeping, threshing, caring for horses) if propitiated with food (milk, bread, chestnuts) and respect; poltergeist harassment if offended (rapping, throwing stones, hiding objects, souring milk, pinching sleepers, tangling hair). Loves horses — braids manes into 'lutin braids' (nattes de lutin) to ride at night. Name & Tongue

Etymology — French follet is diminutive of Old French fol (modern fou) 'mad, foolish, possessed', from Latin follis 'bellows, windbag, empty head, fool'. Diminutive suffix -et gives 'little mad one / little fool'. First attested in Middle French esprit follet (16th century). Catalan follet shares same Latin roo

Pronunciation — French: /fɔ.lɛ/ (fo-LEH); plural follets /fɔ.lɛ/; Catalan (Central): /fuˈʎet/ (foo-YET), plural follets /fuˈʎets/; Occitan: /fuˈlet/

Aliases — Esprit follet, Lutin follet, Folet, Follet Catalan, Follets (plural), Follaton (diminutive, regional)

Ownership — Living folk tradition of France (Normandy, Brittany, Picardy, Île-de-France, Languedoc, Provence, Poitou) and Catalonia (including Valencian Country, Balearic Islands, Northern Cat

Powers
Invisibility and rapid disappearance

Core ability to remain unseen while acting; heard by raps, laughter, or objects moving. Can become visible at will, often only glimpsed. In some tales becomes visible when trapped or named.

Limit: Cannot remain invisible if given new clothing (folk motif) or if master reveals his name; in Catalan tales, sprig of certain herb or holy water forces visibility. Not unlimited — must remain near domicile.

Poltergeist-like object manipulation

Moves furniture, throws stones, hides tools, stacks hay, braids horse manes, tangles flax, spills or curdles milk, raps on walls. Can do heavy domestic work overnight (threshing, sweeping) with superhuman speed.

Limit: Work undone if watched; stops if thanked or paid directly; cannot cross threshold protected by iron, salt, or blessed palm (regional variant). Strength limited to household scale.

Shapeshifting and animal form

Occasionally takes form of cat, dog, hare, or small child; more commonly appears as flickering flame (feu follet) to lure travelers. Catalan follet rarely shapeshifts beyond humanoid; French follet more protean.

Limit: Transformation is temporary and often to deceive; cannot maintain large form long. Flame form is vulnerable to wind and prayer.

Treasure knowledge and finding

Knows location of hidden treasure, can reveal it to master if bound via bottle/ring ritual (Petit Albert, Catalan tales). Guards buried treasure in its own right.

Limit: Requires pact or capture; treasure often proves illusory (coal, leaves) if follet is tricked or offended; Christian sources warn treasure is demonic bait.

Horse mastery and mane-braiding

Special affinity for horses and stables; rides horses at night to exhaustion, braids manes and tails into elaborate 'nattes de lutin' that cannot be undone without angering the spirit. Also cares for favored horses.

Limit: Only affects stabled horses; can be prevented by hanging stone with hole (pierre trouée), sickle, or goat in stable. Exhaustion can harm horse, leading farmers to expel follet.

Luring as will-o'-the-wisp (feu follet)

When identified with feu follet, appears as blue-white or reddish flame over marshes, cemeteries, or crossroads, leading night travelers into bogs, water, or circles. Associated with souls of unbaptized children or miserly spirits.

Limit: Bound to wet or liminal ground; disappears when approached, reappears behind traveler; can be dispelled by turning coat inside out, driving iron nail, or prayer. Cannot enter consecrated ground in some variants.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Attested French follet: rarely seen directly; when described, small (child-sized to knee-high), agile, often invisible or glimpsed as shadow, small man in old-fashioned peasant clothes, sometimes with red cap. No wings. Can appear as animal (cat, dog) or as flickering light when conflated with feu follet. Catalan follet: more consistently anthropomorphic — tiny man 30-40 cm tall, pale or reddish face, large head, pointed ears, wearing tight jacke

Symbols — Symbols: braided horse mane (natte de lutin / cua de follet), tangled flax, moved or stacked objects, spilled milk, small footprints in ash, flickering blue-white flame over water (when feu follet aspect invoked), bottle or vial (Catalan follet imprisoned), broom or hearth. Iconography in 19th-century prints (Collin de Plancy/Le Breton 1863) shows follet as small impish demon with bat wings — a demonological visualization, not folk self-image. Pi

Domains — French: farmhouses, stables, barns, attics, hearths, mills, and marsh edges where feu follet appears. Attached to a specific household or farm, not wilderness. Moves with family if pleased, or remains with house. Catalan: similar domestic sites plus caves, abandoned mills (molí), under bridges, and inside enclosed objects (bottles, rings, canes) after capture. Treasure guardianship in caves or under hearthstones. Feu follet aspect: wetlands, ceme

Accounts I
The Follet Who Did the Threshing (Normandy)Paul Sébillot, Le Folk-lore de la France, Vol. II (1905), collected at Saint-Cast, Côtes-du-Nord

Collected 1880s-1900s, tale set in undated peasant past

A farmer notices his grain is threshed overnight and his horses groomed. He hides and sees a small follet no higher than a boot, working furiously. He leaves a bowl of milk and a piece of bread each night and prospers. His wife, wanting to thank it, sews it a tiny new coat and leaves it by the hearth. The follet tries it on, cries 'I am paid! I must go!' and vanishes, never to return, and the farm's luck leaves with him. Etiology for taboo on rewarding household spirits with clothing; explains why prosperity leaves when spirit is offended by payment. Parallels brownie/kobold departure motif across Europe.

The Braided Manes of PontorsonPaul Sébillot, Littérature orale de la Haute-Bretagne (1881) p. 112; also Sébillot 1905

Oral tradition recorded 1880s

Stable boys find every morning the horses' manes plaited into tight, intricate braids full of straw and burrs, and horses sweating as if ridden all night. An old herdsman says it is the follet who rides them to the sabbat. They hang a pierced stone (pierre percée) and a goat in the stable; the braids cease but rapping is heard on the roof for three nights as the expelled follet protests. Explains apotropaic use of pierced stones and goats in stables; links follet to night-riding and sabbat folklore.

The Follet in the Bottle (Catalan) — El follet de l'ampollaJoan Amades, Folklore de Catalunya. Rondallística (1950) no. 412; also Costumari Català V

Collected 1920s-1940s from oral informants in Empordà and Selva

A miller finds a small green bottle sealed with wax in his mill. Opening it releases a follet no taller than a hand, wearing a red barretina, who declares 'Now you are my master, but you must keep me busy or I will torment you.' The miller sets it to count grains of sand, to bring water in a sieve, and finally to twist a rope of sand. While the follet is occupied, the miller recorks the bottle and throws it into the sea. Fishermen later find it and the cycle repeats. Bottle-imprisonment motif explains follet as controllable familiar; moral about endless tasks to bind trickster (like Rumpelstiltskin tasks). Basis for modern Catalan children's image of follet in bottle.

Accounts II
The Esprit Follet Exorcised at LyonJean-Baptiste Thiers, Traité des superstitions (1679) Livre IV, and contemporary pamphlet Relation de ce qui s'est passé chez M. Morel à Lyon (c. 1680) cited by Thiers

1679-1680, reported as contemporary event

In a house in Lyon, an esprit follet throws stones, moves dishes, beats servants, and answers questions by raps. Priests are called; the spirit claims to be the soul of a deceased lodger. After exorcisms, holy water, and discovery of a maid accused of ventriloquism/fraud, the disturbances cease. Thiers uses the case to argue such spirits are either demons or human imposture, never neutral sprites, and warns against seeking their help for treasure. Illustrates clerical demonization and skepticism toward folk follet belief; source for later demonologists (Collin de Plancy). Shows early modern overlap of poltergeist, possession, and fraud accusations.

The Feu Follet of the Marais de DolPaul Sébillot, Le Folk-lore de la France Vol. III (1906) pp. 47-49; George Sand, Légendes rustiques (1858) pp. 62-65

Tradition recorded mid-19th to early 20th century

On the marshes between Dol and Mont Saint-Michel, a pale flame (feu follet) appears on All Souls' Night, said to be the soul of a drowned miser or unbaptized infant. It beckons travelers, mimics a lantern of an inn, and leads them into mire. One traveler escapes by turning his coat inside out and walking backward, or by planting his knife in the ground. The flame shrieks and vanishes. Old people say to pray and not follow. Encodes danger of marshes and moral about avarice; provides apotropaic instructions (inside-out coat, iron, prayer). Demonstrates conflation of follet sprite with will-o'-the-wisp light.

The Follet and the Treasure Under the HearthClaude Seignolle, Le Folklore du Languedoc (1960) p. 234; also Petit Albert treasure ritual (1706)

Collected 1950s; ritual text 1706-1752

A peasant is told by a follet (or obtains a follet familier via Petit Albert ritual) that treasure lies under his hearthstone, guarded by the follet itself. The ritual requires a black hen, an egg laid on Friday, and not looking back. The peasant digs, finds a pot of gold, but breaks the taboo by speaking or looking back, and finds only charcoal or the pot vanishes with laughter. The follet declares 'You were too greedy.' Moral tale about greed and ritual exactitude; links follet to European treasure-guardian dwarf tradition. Shows learned grimoire influence on folk treasure hunting.

Follet is just the French word for will-o'-the-wisp and nothing else. — Feu follet is the will-o'-the-wisp; follet alone in folklore primarily denotes an anthropomorphic household sprite (lutin). The light is one of its possible manifestations, not its definition. Many English sources mistranslate follet as solely 'will-o'-the-wisp'.

Follets are fairies with wings who grant wishes like Tinker Bell. — Folk follets have no wings, are not 'fairies' in the Victorian sense, and rarely grant wishes except when bound in bottle tales where wishes are contractual and often tricksterish. Winged imp image comes from 1863 Le Breton illustrations for Dictionnaire infernal, not folk art.

Follet is a single, well-defined creature with a fixed appearance (red hat, green clothes). — Appearance is highly variable and often invisible; red hat is specifically Catalan, not pan-French. Many French accounts give no visual description at all beyond 'small' or 'like a child'.

The fashion magazine Le Follet and submarine Follet are about the mythical creature. — Both are name-only borrowings evoking lightness/elusiveness; neither contains folklore content. Magazine is about fashion plates, submarine is a Naïade-class vessel named for sprite's elusiveness.

Follets were always considered evil demons in the past. — Peasant tradition was ambivalent and often affectionate; demonization was clerical/learned overlay from 17th-19th century demonology. Field collections show many households welcomed follet as helpful.
