Who It Is

The survival of Otodus megalodon -- the largest shark ever to exist, reaching lengths of 50 to 60 feet or more -- is one of the most popular and hotly debated topics in cryptozoology. Megalodon dominated Earth's oceans from approximately 23 to 3.6 million years ago during the Miocene and Pliocene epochs, preying on whales and other large marine mammals. Its extinction is attributed to cooling oceans, declining food sources, and competition from emerging predators like the great white shark and killer whales. However, reports of unusually large sharks -- far exceeding the largest confirmed great whites (20+ feet) -- have persisted in maritime folklore for centuries. Cryptozoologists point to several lines of evidence: (1) megalodon teeth found in the Pacific that were initially dated to only 11,000 years ago (later revised to millions of years, but the original dating caused excitement); (2) the 1918 account of Australian fishermen who claimed their crayfish pots were destroyed by a shark estimated at 100+ feet long; (3) the continued discovery of 'living fossils' like the coelacanth, proving that large prehistoric creatures can survive undetected in the deep ocean; (4) the vastness and inaccessibility of the deep ocean (over 80% unexplored), which could theoretically hide a surviving population. Most marine biologists firmly reject the survival theory, noting megalodon was a coastal/shallow-water predator, not a deep-sea species, and that its fossilized teeth would continue to appear in younger sediments if it had persisted. Despite scientific consensus, the Megalodon survival hypothesis remains one of the most popular cryptozoological ideas, fueled by Discovery Channel specials and the 'Meg' novel and film franchise.
