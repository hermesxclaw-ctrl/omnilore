Who It Is

Iseult the Fair (Isolde, Yseult, Iseult la Belle) is the Irish princess whose love for Tristan constitutes the greatest tragic romance of the Middle Ages. She is a healer, a queen, and a lover — a woman of extraordinary skill and passion trapped between political duty and an all-consuming love she did not choose. (The 'Iseult of the White Hands,' Tristan's wife, is a distinct character — a Breton princess whom Tristan marries but cannot love, creating the tragic triangle.)

Iseult first encounters Tristan when he arrives in Ireland as a wounded, anonymous knight. She heals him, using her knowledge of herbs and medicine — she is famous as a healer, her skills rivaling those of her mother, the Queen of Ireland, who brewed the love potion. During the healing, Iseult notices a splinter in Tristan's sword matches a fragment found in the head of her uncle Morholt, whom Tristan killed — she realizes she is healing her uncle's murderer but spares him. When Tristan returns a second time — now as Mark's ambassador seeking Iseult's hand — the political alliance is sealed, and Iseult prepares to marry a man she has never met.

The love potion transforms everything. Iseult's mother brewed it to ensure the political marriage would produce love, entrusting it to Iseult's maid Brangaine. On the ship to Cornwall, Tristan and Iseult, thirsty, mistake the potion for wine. The moment they drink, the world rearranges itself: Mark is forgotten, duty is forgotten, only this love — absolute, consuming, preternatural — exists. Iseult's first reaction, in Gottfried's version, is anguish: 'This is my death!' She knows immediately that this love will destroy her.

Iseult's life at Mark's court is a continuous performance of deceit that strains her integrity to the breaking point. She must pretend to love Mark while her heart belongs to Tristan. She must endure public accusations and trials — including an ordeal by hot iron in which she swears, with carefully parsed words, that no man has held her in his arms except Mark and the pilgrim who carried her from the boat (who was Tristan in disguise). The iron does not burn her. Her intelligence and verbal cunning are her survival tools in a world where a woman's adultery is a capital crime.

Iseult's death is the final act of the tragedy. Summoned to heal the dying Tristan, she sails for Brittany with white sails raised as the signal of hope. But Tristan dies before she arrives, deceived by his wife's lie about black sails. Iseult reaches the shore, finds his body, and dies beside him — of grief, of love, of the impossibility of living in a world without him. King Mark, learning the truth, has them buried on opposite sides of the chapel, but the plants that grow from their graves intertwine. Iseult is buried with the love potion's recipe next to her heart — the potion that was both her ecstasy and her doom.
