Who It Is

Sir Gareth of Orkney is the youngest son of King Lot and Morgause, the brother of Gawain, Gaheris, and Agravain — and the most purely good-hearted of Arthur's knights. His story, 'The Tale of Sir Gareth of Orkney' in Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur, is a romance of disguised identity and proven worth: a prince who arrives at Camelot anonymously and accepts the most menial position — kitchen boy — to prove himself on his own merits rather than his family name.

Gareth arrives at Arthur's court on Pentecost, leaning on the shoulders of two men (a detail that may suggest he is crippled or simply too humble to ride a horse). He asks three boons: food and drink for a year, and a second boon to be named later. Arthur grants both, and Sir Kay — with characteristic cruelty — dubs him 'Beaumains' ('Fair Hands') and sets him to work in the kitchen, mocking him as a lazy vagabond. Gareth endures the humiliation in silence, learning humility alongside craft. A year later, a lady named Linet (or Lynette) arrives begging for a champion to rescue her sister Lyonesse from the Red Knight of the Red Lands. Gareth claims the quest as his second boon, revealing his identity only after enduring Linet's relentless scorn — she calls him a 'kitchen knave' and a 'stinking dishwasher' throughout their journey.

Gareth's adventures on this quest form an ascending scale of knightly prowess. He defeats six thieves, two knights at a ford, the Black Knight, the Green Knight, the Red Knight (not the same as Linet's enemy), and Sir Persaunt of Indigo, gaining their loyalty and sending each to Camelot as his prisoner. Each victory is harder than the last, and Linet's attitude shifts from contempt to grudging respect to admiration. The final battle — against the Red Knight of the Red Lands, who holds Lyonesse captive — is a forty-knight melee that tests Gareth's endurance as much as his skill.

Gareth's love for Lyonesse is pure and sanctioned — neither adulterous like Lancelot's nor transactional like an arranged marriage. Their union represents the chivalric ideal functioning as it should: love ennobles the knight, the knight wins the lady through valor, and the marriage seals the social order. This makes Gareth the counterpoint to the adulterous tragedy of Lancelot and Guinevere. Gareth's love for Lancelot — whom he idolizes — adds a further tragic dimension. When Lancelot accidentally kills Gareth during the rescue of Guinevere from the stake (Gareth is unarmed and caught in the chaos), Gawain's grief transforms into implacable vengeance. Gareth's death — the death of the most innocent knight — signals that the Round Table's tragedy has gone beyond right and wrong into the realm of irreparable loss.
