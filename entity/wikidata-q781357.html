Who It Is

Before Rome ruled Italy, the Etruscans filled their world with gods who were wilder and stranger than the tidy Greek pantheon. Among them was Artume, a huntress who didn't just chase deer through the forests of Tuscany — she also carried a torch into the land of the dead. Imagine Artemis, but with less divine drama and more of a quiet, deadly focus on the forest's edge, and you're getting close.

Artume embodies the untamed aspects of nature—the hunt, the forest, and wild animals. She is a protector of young creatures and a mistress of the chase. Her personality is one of independence and fierce autonomy, reflecting the Etruscan appreciation for the wild and the boundaries between civilization and the natural world. She is not a mother goddess or a domestic deity; her domain is the liminal space of the wilderness.

Powers
Mastery of the Hunt

Artume is a skilled huntress, able to track and kill any prey with her bow. She is the patron of hunters and the one who decides the fate of animals.

Limit: None documented; her skill is presented as absolute within her domain.

Protection of the Wild

As a goddess of the wilderness, she protects young animals and the sanctity of the forest. She can be wrathful against those who overhunt or desecrate her domain.

Limit: Her protection is limited to her domain; she does not extend it to civilized spaces.

Chthonic Guidance

In funerary art, Artume appears as a guide or protector of the deceased, helping them navigate the afterlife. This aspect links her to the underworld and the cycle of death and rebirth.

Limit: This power is only evidenced in funerary settings; her role as a psychopomp is not explicitly stated in texts.

Lunar Association

Like Artemis, Artume is associated with the moon, particularly the crescent. This links her to the night, the passage of time, and the cyclical nature of life.

Limit: The extent of her lunar worship is unclear; no lunar-specific rituals are documented.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Artume is typically depicted as a young, athletic woman, often in a short tunic or hunting garb, mirroring Greek Artemis. She is shown with a bow and arrows, sometimes accompanied by a deer or a dog. On Etruscan mirrors, she appears in narrative scenes, often interacting with other deities or heroes. Her iconography is less martial and more focused on the wild and the hunt compared to her Greek counterpart.

Symbols — Her primary symbols are the bow and arrow, the deer, and the hunting dog. The crescent moon is also associated with her, linking her to the night and the celestial realm. In Etruscan art, she is sometimes shown with a branch or a torch, symbolizing her connection to the wilderness and the underworld. The deer, in particular, is a recurring motif, representing the animals she protects and hunts.

Domains — Her domain is the forest and the hunt. She is associated with the moon, like Artemis, and with the cycle of life and death in the animal kingdom. Sanctuaries dedicated to her were likely located in wooded areas or on the edges of settlements, marking the boundary between the cultivated and the wild. She also holds a chthonic aspect, as evidenced by her presence in funerary art, where she guides or protects the deceased in the afterlife.

Accounts I
Artume and the Deer (Reconstructed)Etruscan mirror engravings

4th–3rd century BCE

A common scene on Etruscan mirrors shows Artume with a deer, sometimes holding it or standing beside it. This scene is often interpreted as a representation of her power over the wild, or as a specific myth where she tames or protects a sacred deer. The exact narrative is lost, but the imagery emphasizes her close bond with the animal. The scene reinforces her identity as a hunt and nature goddess, and may have served as a protective amulet for the mirror's owner.

Accounts II
Artume in the Underworld (Reconstructed)Etruscan funerary urns

3rd–1st century BCE

On funerary reliefs, Artume is depicted in scenes of the afterlife, often alongside the deceased or other chthonic deities. She appears as a guide or a guardian, leading the soul to its final resting place. This role is more prominent than in Greek art, suggesting a unique Etruscan emphasis on her protective powers beyond death. Her presence in funerary art provides comfort and assurance of safe passage to the afterlife.

Artume is just the Etruscan name for Diana. — Artume is the Etruscan adaptation of the Greek Artemis, not the Roman Diana. While Diana was also derived from Artemis, she was a distinct Roman goddess. Artume predates Roman dominance and has her own Etruscan characteristics.

Artume has the same extensive mythology as Greek Artemis. — Artume's mythology is extremely sparse. She lacks the complex birth story, the many myths of punishment and transformation, and the extensive family drama of Greek Artemis. She is primarily a functional deity of the hunt and the wild, with a significant funerary role.

Artume was a minor, unimportant goddess. — While her mythology is sparse, her presence in votive offerings, temple dedications, and funerary art indicates she was a significant and widely worshipped deity, particularly in rural and hunting communities.
