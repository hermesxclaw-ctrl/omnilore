The Approach

Before the first thunderclap of a battle, before the senator's vote, before the king's decision, the ancients looked to the sky, the entrails of a sacrificed sheep, or the flight of a bird for a sign. An omen was more than a superstition; it was a message from the divine, a warning or a promise that could alter the course of empires. From the liver of a lamb to the cry of a raven, the world was alive with portents, and those who could read them held the keys to fate itself.

Content advisory. This article discusses ancient religious practices involving animal sacrifice and the execution of substitute kings, which some readers may find disturbing. It also touches on historical instances of violence and assassination associated with omens.

Etymology

The English word 'omen' derives from the Latin 'omen', of uncertain origin. Some ancient sources connected it to 'os' (mouth) or 'orendum' (something spoken), but modern linguists have not reached a consensus.

Cultural Ownership

The concept of omens is universal, but the specific term and systematic interpretation are rooted in ancient Mediterranean and Near Eastern cultures, particularly Roman, Greek, and Mesopotamian civilizations.

Disputed Identifications

The exact nature of omens as divine messages versus natural coincidences has been debated since antiquity. Cicero, for instance, questioned the validity of personal omens, while official augury was a state institution. There is also scholarly debate over the extent to which omens were believed versus used as political tools.

Description

Appearance

Omens themselves are phenomena, not physical entities. They appear as natural events (eclipses, lightning, abnormal births), animal behaviors (flight of birds, entrails of sacrifices), or even chance words and incidents.

Symbols & Iconography

In art, omens are often depicted through symbols: the eagle, the raven, the lightning bolt of Zeus/Jupiter, the liver, and the tripod of the Pythia. Roman coins sometimes showed augural symbols like the lituus (curved staff).

Behaviour & Personality

Omens are believed to be messages from the gods, but their nature is ambiguous: they can be good or bad, and their interpretation depends on context and the skill of the interpreter. They are not sentient but are considered deliberate signs.

Domains & Habitats

Omens occur in the natural world: the sky (birds, lightning, eclipses), the earth (abnormal births, springs), and the animal kingdom. They are also associated with specific places like temples, augural sites, and battlefields.

Version Differences

Different cultures had distinct systems: Mesopotamian hepatoscopy, Greek bird augury, Roman auspices and prodigies, and astrological omens. The meaning of signs could vary: in Rome, left was often favorable, in Greece, right.

Powers & Abilities

Foretelling the Future

Source Version General ancient belief

Description

Omens are believed to reveal future events, often of significant consequence (death, war, political change).

Limitations

Interpretation is required; omens are often ambiguous and require specialized knowledge.

Contradictions

Some philosophers like Cicero argued that omens were coincidental, not predictive.

Divine Communication

Source Version Greek and Roman religion

Description

Omens are seen as messages from the gods, conveying their will or warnings.

Limitations

The gods may not always send clear signs; misinterpretation can lead to disaster.

Contradictions

In some philosophical traditions, the gods were seen as uninvolved in human affairs.

Averting Disaster

Source Version Roman practice of expiating prodigies

Description

By heeding omens, individuals or states could take action to avoid negative outcomes, such as performing rituals of expiation or postponing events.

Limitations

Not all omens could be averted; some were considered fixed.

Contradictions

The effectiveness of such measures was debated.

Stories & Myths

The Substitute King of Assyria

Source Mesopotamian royal inscriptions and lettersCulture AssyrianPeriod 7th century BC

Summary

When an ominous sign threatened the king, a substitute king was placed on the throne to absorb the evil. After the danger passed, the substitute was executed and the real king resumed power.

Participants

King Esarhaddon

Substitute king

Consequences

The practice was believed to protect the king from fate, but it involved human sacrifice.

Variants

Similar practices are attested in other Mesopotamian periods.

Gaius Flaminius at Lake Trasimene

Source Livy, Ab Urbe ConditaCulture RomanPeriod 217 BC

Summary

Consul Flaminius disregarded multiple bad omens—his horse collapsed, the sacred chickens would not eat—and led his army into an ambush, resulting in a catastrophic defeat.

Participants

Gaius Flaminius

Hannibal

Consequences

The Roman army was destroyed, and Flaminius was killed.

Variants

Other sources, like Polybius, may have different details.

The Omen of Cauneas

Source Cicero, De DivinationeCulture RomanPeriod 53 BC

Summary

The orator Licinius Crassus heard a fig-seller cry 'Cauneas!' (Caunean figs), which sounded like 'Cave ne eas' (Beware, don't go). He ignored it and was killed on campaign against the Parthians.

Participants

Licinius Crassus

Consequences

Crassus's death was interpreted as the fulfillment of the omen.

Variants

Cicero cites this as an example of coincidental omens.

Caligula's Dream

Source Suetonius, Life of CaligulaCulture RomanPeriod 41 AD

Summary

Caligula dreamed that he stood before Jupiter's throne and was kicked down to earth. He ignored the premonition and was assassinated the next day.

Participants

Caligula

Consequences

Caligula's death fulfilled the omen.

Variants

Similar dreams are recorded for other emperors.

Description

Contradictions & Disputes

Validity of Omens

Position A

Omens are divine messages and can be reliably interpreted.

Position A — evidence

State-sponsored augury and many historical accounts of fulfilled omens.

Position B

Omens are coincidences and have no predictive power.

Position B — evidence

Cicero's De Divinatione, which argues against personal omens.

Scholarly Consensus

Modern scholarship sees omens as a cultural belief, not a supernatural phenomenon.

Left vs. Right

Position A

In Rome, left was favorable, right unfavorable.

Position A — evidence

Roman augury texts.

Position B

In Greece, right was favorable, left unfavorable.

Position B — evidence

Greek sources on bird omens.

Scholarly Consensus

The difference reflects cultural variation in interpreting signs.

Nature of Prodigies

Position A

Prodigies are unnatural and indicate divine anger.

Position A — evidence

Roman Senate's official recognition and expiation.

Position B

Prodigies are natural events misinterpreted as signs.

Position B — evidence

Modern scientific understanding of phenomena like 'blood rain'.

Scholarly Consensus

Prodigies were often natural occurrences given religious significance.
