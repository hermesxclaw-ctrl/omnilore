Who It Is

Before the first thunderclap of a battle, before the senator's vote, before the king's decision, the ancients looked to the sky, the entrails of a sacrificed sheep, or the flight of a bird for a sign. An omen was more than a superstition; it was a message from the divine, a warning or a promise that could alter the course of empires. From the liver of a lamb to the cry of a raven, the world was alive with portents, and those who could read them held the keys to fate itself.

Omens are believed to be messages from the gods, but their nature is ambiguous: they can be good or bad, and their interpretation depends on context and the skill of the interpreter. They are not sentient but are considered deliberate signs.

Powers
Foretelling the Future

Omens are believed to reveal future events, often of significant consequence (death, war, political change).

Limit: Interpretation is required; omens are often ambiguous and require specialized knowledge.

Divine Communication

Omens are seen as messages from the gods, conveying their will or warnings.

Limit: The gods may not always send clear signs; misinterpretation can lead to disaster.

Averting Disaster

By heeding omens, individuals or states could take action to avoid negative outcomes, such as performing rituals of expiation or postponing events.

Limit: Not all omens could be averted; some were considered fixed.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Omens themselves are phenomena, not physical entities. They appear as natural events (eclipses, lightning, abnormal births), animal behaviors (flight of birds, entrails of sacrifices), or even chance words and incidents.

Symbols — In art, omens are often depicted through symbols: the eagle, the raven, the lightning bolt of Zeus/Jupiter, the liver, and the tripod of the Pythia. Roman coins sometimes showed augural symbols like the lituus (curved staff).

Domains — Omens occur in the natural world: the sky (birds, lightning, eclipses), the earth (abnormal births, springs), and the animal kingdom. They are also associated with specific places like temples, augural sites, and battlefields.

Accounts I
The Substitute King of AssyriaMesopotamian royal inscriptions and letters

7th century BC

When an ominous sign threatened the king, a substitute king was placed on the throne to absorb the evil. After the danger passed, the substitute was executed and the real king resumed power. The practice was believed to protect the king from fate, but it involved human sacrifice.

Gaius Flaminius at Lake TrasimeneLivy, Ab Urbe Condita

217 BC

Consul Flaminius disregarded multiple bad omens—his horse collapsed, the sacred chickens would not eat—and led his army into an ambush, resulting in a catastrophic defeat. The Roman army was destroyed, and Flaminius was killed.

Accounts II
The Omen of CauneasCicero, De Divinatione

53 BC

The orator Licinius Crassus heard a fig-seller cry 'Cauneas!' (Caunean figs), which sounded like 'Cave ne eas' (Beware, don't go). He ignored it and was killed on campaign against the Parthians. Crassus's death was interpreted as the fulfillment of the omen.

Caligula's DreamSuetonius, Life of Caligula

41 AD

Caligula dreamed that he stood before Jupiter's throne and was kicked down to earth. He ignored the premonition and was assassinated the next day. Caligula's death fulfilled the omen.

Omens are always bad. — Omens can be good or bad; the term is often associated with negative events, but positive omens exist.

Omens are solely a Roman or Greek concept. — Belief in omens is universal and predates Greco-Roman culture, with evidence from Mesopotamia and elsewhere.

All omens are sent by gods. — Some omens might be natural signs without divine agency, though many cultures attributed them to gods.
