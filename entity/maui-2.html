Archive Dossier — Identity

Māui

Pronunciation MAU-ee (Māori: [ˈmaːʉi])

Known As

Māui-tikitikiMāui-tikitiki-a-TarangaMāui-pōtikiMāui te whare kinoMaui (Hawaiian)Maui-tikitiki-a-Taranga

Etymology

The name 'Māui' is common across Polynesian languages. Its exact etymology is uncertain, but it is often associated with trickster and culture hero figures. The Māori epithet 'tikitiki' means 'top-knot', referring to the topknot of hair he wore, and 'a-Taranga' indicates his descent from Taranga. 'Pōtiki' means 'last born', and 'te whare kino' means 'the house of trouble'.

Cultural Ownership

Māori (New Zealand), Hawaiian, and other Polynesian cultures. Each culture has its own distinct traditions and genealogies.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars debate whether Māui was a historical chief or purely mythological. His parentage varies: in Māori tradition he is the son of Taranga and Makeatutara, while in Hawaiian tradition he is the son of ʻAkalana and Hina. Some traditions consider him a demigod, others a human hero with supernatural powers.

Archive Dossier — Approach

Before the sun learned to pace itself, before the islands of Aotearoa rose from the deep, there was Māui—the trickster who stole fire from a goddess's fingernails, fished up continents with a jawbone hook, and died laughing inside the jaws of death itself. He is the hero who made the world habitable, and the fool whose ambition finally undid him.

Māui is the great culture hero and trickster of Polynesian mythology, celebrated throughout the Pacific from Hawaii to New Zealand to Easter Island. He is a demigod of extraordinary ambition, cleverness, and strength, famous for numerous heroic feats: fishing up islands from the sea (including the North Island of New Zealand, Te Ika-a-Māui), slowing the sun with a rope to make days longer, stealing fire from the underworld for humanity, lifting the sky to give people more room, and attempting to conquer death by entering the sleeping goddess Hine-nui-te-pō to reverse death's power.

Who It Is

Māui is the great culture hero and trickster of Polynesian mythology, celebrated throughout the Pacific from Hawaii to New Zealand to Easter Island. He is a demigod of extraordinary ambition, cleverness, and strength, famous for numerous heroic feats: fishing up islands from the sea (including the North Island of New Zealand, Te Ika-a-Māui), slowing the sun with a rope to make days longer, stealing fire from the underworld for humanity, lifting the sky to give people more room, and attempting to conquer death by entering the sleeping goddess Hine-nui-te-pō to reverse death's power. Each of these feats made life better for humanity. Māui is the youngest of five brothers, often described as having been born prematurely and raised by the gods. He wields a magical jawbone (or hook) from his grandmother Murirangawhenua. His death came during his attempt to defeat death — crushed by Hine-nui-te-pō's vaginal teeth (the 'decapods'). He is an archetype of the clever underdog who uses wit to overcome impossible odds.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

Māui is described variously as a handsome young man or a wise old priest, depending on the tradition. In Māori art, he is often depicted with a topknot. He is a shape-shifter, able to transform into birds and worms.

Symbols & Iconography

Māui's symbols include the fishhook (particularly the magical jawbone hook), the topknot, the sun (which he snared), and the islands he fished up. In Hawaiian iconography, he is associated with the bird (Alae) and the fire sticks.

Behaviour & Personality

Māui is a trickster and culture hero: clever, mischievous, bold, and sometimes reckless. He uses his wits to overcome obstacles and often acts for the benefit of humanity, though his tricks can also cause trouble. He is known for his perseverance and courage, but also his pride and curiosity, which ultimately lead to his death.

Domains & Habitats

Māui operates across the earth, the heavens, and the underworld. He travels by canoe, climbs mountains, and descends to the underworld. His home is often associated with his mother's village, Hawaiki, and he journeys to the east to catch the sun.

Version Differences

Māui's parentage, status, and specific exploits vary across Polynesian cultures. In Māori tradition, he is the son of Taranga and Makeatutara, and his fishing up of the North Island is a central story. In Hawaiian tradition, he is the son of ʻAkalana and Hina, and his exploits include making birds visible and taming the sun. In other traditions, such as those from the New Hebrides (Vanuatu), his stories have distinctive local structures.

Origins

Māui was born to Māui-taha, Māui-roto, Māui-pae, Māui-waho, and Māui-tikitiki-a-Taranga — his name indicates he was the youngest. His mother Taranga wrapped him in her topknot and threw him into the sea, but the gods rescued and raised him. He found his way back to his family and began his extraordinary exploits.

Archive Dossier — Powers

Shape-shifting

Source Version Māori, Hawaiian, and other Polynesian traditions

Description

Māui can transform into various forms, such as a worm, a bird, or other creatures. This ability is used in his attempt to conquer death.

Limitations

The transformation may be limited in duration or require concentration; in the death myth, his transformation is discovered by birds.

Contradictions

Some versions do not emphasize shape-shifting, focusing instead on his cunning and physical strength.

Superhuman strength and endurance

Source Version Māori and Hawaiian traditions

Description

Māui is strong enough to lift the sky, pull up islands, and battle the sun. He also has great stamina for long journeys.

Limitations

His strength is not absolute; he is vulnerable to the goddess of death.

Contradictions

In some Hawaiian stories, he is a poor fisherman, suggesting his strength is not always effective in ordinary tasks.

Magic fishhook

Source Version Māori and Hawaiian traditions

Description

Māui possesses a magic fishhook made from the jawbone of his grandmother (in Māori tradition) or from the bones of divine ancestors (in Hawaiian tradition). This hook can pull up islands from the sea.

Limitations

The hook's power may require specific rituals or conditions, such as baiting with blood or sacred birds.

Contradictions

The hook's origin and material differ across traditions.

Ability to slow the sun

Source Version Māori and Hawaiian traditions

Description

Māui captures the sun with ropes and strikes it with his jawbone, forcing it to travel more slowly across the sky, thus lengthening the day.

Limitations

The sun's agreement is conditional; it may not be a permanent binding.

Contradictions

The method varies: in Māori tradition, he uses a noose and a jawbone; in Hawaiian, he uses ropes and a club.

Fire-making knowledge

Source Version Māori and Hawaiian traditions

Description

Māui obtains the secret of fire from the goddess Mahuika (in Māori) or from the leader of a tribe of birds (in Hawaiian) and teaches it to humanity.

Limitations

The fire is obtained through trickery and may have been dangerous to acquire.

Contradictions

The source of fire and the method of obtaining it differ.

Making birds visible

Source Version Hawaiian tradition

Description

In Hawaiian tradition, Māui makes birds visible to human eyes, as they were previously invisible.

Limitations

This power is specific to the Hawaiian cycle.

Contradictions

No known contradictions.

Archive Dossier — Relationships

Taranga

Type motherSource Māori tradition

Description

Taranga is Māui's mother, a rangatira (chief) in Māori tradition. She gives him the task of obtaining fire.

Version Note

In Hawaiian tradition, his mother is Hina-a-ke-ahi.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Makeatutara

Type fatherSource Māori tradition

Description

Makeatutara is Māui's father in Māori tradition. He is a supernatural being who performs the baptism ritual for Māui.

Version Note

In Hawaiian tradition, his father is ʻAkalana.

Conflicting Accounts

Some traditions say Māui was found and raised by his mother alone.

Murirangawhenua

Type grandmotherSource Māori tradition

Description

Murirangawhenua gives Māui her jawbone to use as a fishhook and a weapon.

Version Note

In Hawaiian tradition, the hook is made from the bones of divine ancestors.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Mahuika

Type grandmother (in some versions)Source Māori tradition

Description

Mahuika is the fire goddess, sometimes identified as Māui's grandmother. She provides fire to humanity.

Version Note

In Hawaiian tradition, the fire secret comes from a bird.

Conflicting Accounts

Some versions do not specify a familial relationship.

Hine-nui-te-pō

Type adversarySource Māori tradition

Description

Hine-nui-te-pō is the goddess of death. Māui attempts to conquer death by entering her body, but she kills him.

Version Note

This story is central to Māori mythology.

Conflicting Accounts

Some versions say he was killed by her obsidian teeth, others by her crushing him.

Tama-nui-te-rā

Type adversarySource Māori tradition

Description

The sun god, whom Māui captures and forces to move slowly.

Version Note

In Hawaiian tradition, the sun is called Lā.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

His brothers

Type familySource Māori and Hawaiian traditions

Description

Māui has several brothers who often exclude him from activities. In some stories, they are jealous or mocking.

Version Note

In Hawaiian tradition, he has four brothers: Māui-mua, Māui-waena, Māui-kiʻikiʻi, and Māui-a-kalana.

Conflicting Accounts

In some versions, they help him; in others, they hinder him.

Hina

Type mother (Hawaiian)Source Hawaiian tradition

Description

In Hawaiian tradition, Hina is Māui's mother, also known as Hina-a-ke-ahi.

Version Note

She is associated with the moon and fire.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Pīwakawaka (fantails)

Type unwitting adversariesSource Māori tradition

Description

The fantails laugh at Māui's attempt to enter Hine-nui-te-pō, waking her and causing his death.

Version Note

In some versions, they are seen as messengers of death.

Conflicting Accounts

None.
