Who It Is

Before the sun learned to pace itself, before the islands of Aotearoa rose from the deep, there was Māui—the trickster who stole fire from a goddess's fingernails, fished up continents with a jawbone hook, and died laughing inside the jaws of death itself. He is the hero who made the world habitable, and the fool whose ambition finally undid him.

Māui is a trickster and culture hero: clever, mischievous, bold, and sometimes reckless. He uses his wits to overcome obstacles and often acts for the benefit of humanity, though his tricks can also cause trouble. He is known for his perseverance and courage, but also his pride and curiosity, which ultimately lead to his death.

Powers
Shape-shifting

Māui can transform into various forms, such as a worm, a bird, or other creatures. This ability is used in his attempt to conquer death.

Limit: The transformation may be limited in duration or require concentration; in the death myth, his transformation is discovered by birds.

Superhuman strength and endurance

Māui is strong enough to lift the sky, pull up islands, and battle the sun. He also has great stamina for long journeys.

Limit: His strength is not absolute; he is vulnerable to the goddess of death.

Magic fishhook

Māui possesses a magic fishhook made from the jawbone of his grandmother (in Māori tradition) or from the bones of divine ancestors (in Hawaiian tradition). This hook can pull up islands from the sea.

Limit: The hook's power may require specific rituals or conditions, such as baiting with blood or sacred birds.

Ability to slow the sun

Māui captures the sun with ropes and strikes it with his jawbone, forcing it to travel more slowly across the sky, thus lengthening the day.

Limit: The sun's agreement is conditional; it may not be a permanent binding.

Fire-making knowledge

Māui obtains the secret of fire from the goddess Mahuika (in Māori) or from the leader of a tribe of birds (in Hawaiian) and teaches it to humanity.

Limit: The fire is obtained through trickery and may have been dangerous to acquire.

Making birds visible

In Hawaiian tradition, Māui makes birds visible to human eyes, as they were previously invisible.

Limit: This power is specific to the Hawaiian cycle.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Māui is described variously as a handsome young man or a wise old priest, depending on the tradition. In Māori art, he is often depicted with a topknot. He is a shape-shifter, able to transform into birds and worms.

Symbols — Māui's symbols include the fishhook (particularly the magical jawbone hook), the topknot, the sun (which he snared), and the islands he fished up. In Hawaiian iconography, he is associated with the bird (Alae) and the fire sticks.

Domains — Māui operates across the earth, the heavens, and the underworld. He travels by canoe, climbs mountains, and descends to the underworld. His home is often associated with his mother's village, Hawaiki, and he journeys to the east to catch the sun.

Accounts I
Fishing up the North Island (Te Ika-a-Māui)Māori oral tradition; recorded by Grey, Best, and others

Mythological (pre-European)

Māui's brothers refuse to take him fishing. He hides in their canoe, and when they are far out at sea, he reveals himself. Using his grandmother's jawbone as a hook and baiting it with his own blood, he catches a giant fish. The fish becomes the North Island of New Zealand, and his brothers chop it up, creating the mountains and valleys. The North Island is formed; the South Island is sometimes identified as Māui's canoe.

Māui brings fire to the worldMāori oral tradition

Mythological

Māui extinguishes all the fires in his village, then travels to the underworld to ask the fire goddess Mahuika for more. She gives him one of her burning fingernails, but he extinguishes them one by one, angering her. She throws her last nail at him, but it misses and lands in certain trees, giving them the power to produce fire when rubbed together. Fire is discovered; humans learn to make fire from specific woods.

Māui slows the sunMāori oral tradition

Mythological

The sun moves too quickly, leaving little daylight. Māui and his brothers travel to the sun's resting place, set a noose, and catch it. Māui strikes the sun with his jawbone until it agrees to move slowly. The days become longer, allowing more time for work and food.

Accounts II
The death of MāuiMāori oral tradition

Mythological

Māui attempts to make humans immortal by entering the goddess of death, Hine-nui-te-pō, through her vagina and exiting through her mouth while she sleeps. He transforms into a worm, but the fantail birds laugh and wake her. She crushes him with the obsidian teeth in her vagina, killing him. Māui dies, and humanity remains mortal.

Making birds visible (Hawaiian)Hawaiian oral tradition

Mythological

Birds were invisible to humans, but Māui could see them. Out of compassion, he made them visible to everyone. Humans can now see birds.

Creation of the Hawaiian IslandsHawaiian oral tradition

Mythological

Māui goes fishing with his brothers, who mock him for being a poor fisherman. He uses his magic hook baited with a sacred bird to pull up islands. He tells his brothers not to look back, but one does, and the line snaps, leaving fewer islands than there could have been. The Hawaiian Islands are formed.
