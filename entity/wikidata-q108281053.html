Who It Is

He has no statues, no epithets, no labors of his own — only a patronymic and a ship to Sardinia. Astybies was one of fifty sons Heracles fathered on the fifty daughters of King Thespius in the shadow of a lion hunt, a child conceived as hospitality, politics, and mythic population engineering all at once, then sorted like lot — seven to stay, three to Thebes, forty to sail west with Iolaus and become the ancestors the Greeks would later claim for a distant island.

No individual personality, speech, or moral character is preserved. The narrative role is purely genealogical and colonial: to be born, to be counted, and to be dispatched. Diodorus and Apollodorus present the sons as obedient to Heracles' final instructions, implying piety and deference to paternal authority. No myths show Astybies speaking, choosing, or acting independently. Status: unattested; do not invent traits.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Ancient Greek Ἀστυβίης (Astubiēs). First element ἄστυ (asty) = 'town, city, citadel'; second element -βίης likely from βία (bia) = 'force, violence, bodily strength' (as in -biēs names like Eurusbiēs, Astybiē). The compound would mean 'Force of the City' or 'He Who Has City-Strength.' No ancient ety

Aliases — Astybies son of Heracles, Astybies Thespiades

Ownership — Ancient Greek, specifically Boeotian (Thespian) mythic genealogy. Part of the Heracles-Thespius cycle attached to Thespiae in Boeotia and to the Heraclid colonization myth for Sard

Powers
Heraclid Lineage (No Individual Feats Attested)

As a son of Heracles, Astybies would have been considered of semi-divine descent with implied strength and arete, but no source attributes to him any superhuman strength, weapon skill, transformation, or divine power. The Thespiads collectively are described as founders and warriors in Sardinia, but no individual aristeia is recorded for Astybies.

Limit: No attested feats; cannot be assumed to share Heracles' invulnerability, lion-skin, or apotheosis. Mortality is assumed.

Colonial Founder Status (Collective)

If among the forty sent to Sardinia, Astybies would have participated in the Iolaus-led colonization, described by Diodorus and Pausanias as subduing natives, draining marshes, building works, and establishing Greek cults. The ability is political/military leadership as part of a group, not supernatural.

Limit: Not individually named as leader; Iolaus is the leader. No source confirms Astybies was in the Sardinian forty.

Symbols — No personal symbol, animal, weapon, or cult object. Collective symbols of the Thespiads are Heraclean: lion skin, club, and by extension the Cithaeronian lion of their conception story. In Sardinia, the Iolaans (descendants of the forty) were associated with Iolaus hero-cult, Nuragic structures reinterpreted as Greek foundations, and later Roman sources associate them with the 'Iolaos' shrine. Accounts I
The Cithaeronian Lion HuntApollodorus Bibliotheca 2.4.9-10; Diodorus Siculus 4.29.2

Archaic mythic past, recorded Classical-Hellenistic

At age eighteen, Heracles, living with Amphitryon or at Thebes, is tasked or volunteers to kill the lion ravaging Cithaeron, which preyed on the herds of Amphitryon and Thespius. King Thespius of Thespiae hosts Heracles for an extended hunt. The lion is slain (later its skin becomes Heracles' attribute, though the Nemean lion later supersedes it). The hunt provides the narrative pretext for Heracles' prolonged stay in the Thespian palace and access to the fifty daughters. Establishes Heracles' early arete before the Labours, creates debt of hospitality that Thespius exploits to obtain Heraclid heirs, and explains the lion-skin iconography in Thespian cult.

The Night(s) with the Fifty DaughtersApollodorus 2.4.10; Athenaeus Deipnosophistae 13.4 citing Herodorus; Tzetzes Chiliades 2.221-227

Mythic past, recorded 4th c. BCE - 2nd c. CE

Thespius, desiring grandchildren by Heracles, offers his fifty daughters to the hero. While drunk or deceived (Apollodorus says Heracles thought it was the same woman each night), Heracles sleeps with all fifty — in one night according to epitome tradition, over fifty nights or seven nights according to other variants. Calametis conceives Astybies. One youngest daughter in some versions refuses and is condemned to remain a virgin priestess of Heracles. Conception of fifty (or fifty-one) Heraclid sons including Astybies; establishes the Thespiad genos; creates later need to dispose of a large cohort of semi-divine sons.

Birth of the ThespiadsApollodorus 2.7.8 (catalogue); Diodorus 4.29.3

Mythic genealogical tradition

Each daughter bears a son. Apollodorus preserves a catalogue correlating each mother with son: Calametis — Astybies. The births are not narrated individually; they are listed as a genealogical register. The sons are raised in Thespiae under Thespius' care while Heracles continues his Labours and later life. Creates a large Heraclid population in Boeotia, too many to inherit locally, motivating the later colonial dispatch. No individual birth omens or childhood deeds are recorded for Astybies.

Accounts II
Heracles' Dispensation: The Tripartition of the SonsApollodorus 2.7.8; Diodorus 4.29.3-4.30.1

Mythic past, recorded Hellenistic

Years later, Heracles (before his death or during his time in Lydia/before apotheosis, depending on chronology) sends instructions to Thespius: keep seven sons in Thespiae, send three to Thebes, and send the remaining forty, joined by his nephew Iolaus, to colonize Sardinia. The message is sometimes delivered directly, sometimes via Iolaus. The division resolves the succession crisis and fulfills a Panhellenic colonization aition. Institutionalizes Thespian Heracles cult (seven remain as aristocracy/priests), strengthens Theban Heraclid presence (three), and provides mythic charter for Greek presence in Sardinia (forty). Astybies' fate depends on lot, but no source assigns him to a specific group.

The Sardinian Colonization under IolausDiodorus 4.29.3-4.30.2; Pausanias 10.17.5; Apollodorus 2.7.8

Mythic colonization era, recorded Classical-Hellenistic

Forty Thespiads, led by Iolaus (Heracles' nephew and charioteer), sail to Sardinia. There they subdue the island's inhabitants, found cities, introduce Greek customs, drain marshes, and are later called Iolaans or Ioleans. They are heroized; Iolaus receives cult. The colony explains Nuragic towers as Greek foundations in Greek eyes and legitimizes later Athenian/Carthaginian interests. If Astybies was among the forty, this is his only possible adult deed. Aition for Sardinian toponyms and cults; Thespian claim to western Mediterranean; Heraclid diaspora. Pausanias claims the Iolaans still inhabited the interior in his day and preserved Greek customs.

The Virgin Priestess VariantAthenaeus 13.4 (Herodorus FGrH 31 F14); Tzetzes ad Lycophron 1327

Classical variant (Herodorus c. 400 BCE)

In Herodorus' version preserved by Athenaeus, one daughter (the youngest or eldest depending on telling) refuses to sleep with Heracles and is kept by him as a virgin priestess. This reduces the number of sons to 49 or creates a 51st daughter. The story introduces a moral/ritual element: Heracles as founder of a virgin priesthood, and explains why Thespiae had a priestess of Heracles who remained celibate. Explains Thespian cult practice (virgin priestess of Heracles) and accounts for discrepancy between 50 daughters and 51 sons in some lists. Astybies' mother Calametis is not the refuser, so his birth is unaffected, but the variant shows the tradition was not monolithic.
