Who It Is

The Bruxa is the Portuguese witch — a feared figure in rural communities who flies through the night sky in the form of a moth, bat, or bird, entering homes through keyholes and chimney openings to suck the blood of sleeping victims, especially infants. The term 'bruxa' shares etymology with the broader Iberian 'bruja' (witch), but the Portuguese Bruxa has distinct characteristics. She is often an older woman, marginal to the community (widow, spinster, healer), who removes her skin before flying — leaving it hidden under a slab or in a tree hollow. If the skin is found and salted, the Bruxa cannot re-enter it and dies at dawn. The Bruxa's power to enter locked homes is neutralized by placing scissors under the pillow, a broom behind the door, or a bowl of mustard seeds that she is compelled to count one by one. She is also associated with the evil eye (mau-olhado), particularly harming children and livestock. The Portuguese Inquisition tried hundreds of accused bruxas, particularly in rural areas, with records providing rich documentation of folk beliefs. Unlike the more complex Strega of Italy, the Portuguese Bruxa in popular conception was almost uniformly malevolent — a parasite on the community who had made a pact with the Devil. However, she also represents anxieties about female autonomy and the marginal old woman who lives outside patriarchal control — feared because she is free.
