The Approach

In Vietnam, the zodiac is not merely a calendar but a living tapestry of folklore, where the celestial rabbit yields to the cat and the sheep gives way to the goat, reflecting a culture that has reimagined the heavens in its own image. This is the story of a system that has shaped festivals, marriages, and daily life for millennia, and yet remains little known beyond its borders.

Content advisory. No dark material. Some historical claims regarding the origin of the Vietnamese zodiac are speculative and debated; readers should consult scholarly sources for nuanced perspectives.
Identity

Vietnamese zodiac

Pronunciation mɨəj haːi kɔn zaːp (approximate)

Known As

Tử viLịch âmMười hai con giáp

Etymology

The term 'Vietnamese zodiac' derives from the French 'zodiaque vietnamien', but in Vietnamese it is 'mười hai con giáp' (twelve earthly branches) or simply 'con giáp' (earthly branches). The word 'giáp' refers to the stems and branches system of the calendar.

Cultural Ownership

Vietnam, with roots in the broader East Asian cultural sphere, particularly Chinese calendrical systems.

Disputed Identifications

The most famous dispute is the replacement of the rabbit with the cat in the Vietnamese zodiac. Another is the use of 'goat' instead of 'sheep' for the eighth sign. These differences are often attributed to linguistic or cultural factors, but no single explanation is universally accepted.

Description

Appearance

The Vietnamese zodiac is typically represented as a cycle of twelve animals, each associated with a year. In art, these animals are depicted in various styles, from traditional woodblock prints to modern illustrations. The animals are: Rat, Water Buffalo, Tiger, Cat, Dragon, Snake, Horse, Goat, Monkey, Rooster, Dog, and Pig.

Symbols & Iconography

Each animal has symbolic meanings: Rat (cleverness), Buffalo (diligence), Tiger (courage), Cat (gentleness), Dragon (power), Snake (wisdom), Horse (vitality), Goat (creativity), Monkey (intelligence), Rooster (confidence), Dog (loyalty), Pig (prosperity). These symbols appear in statues, paintings, and festive decorations.

Behaviour & Personality

The zodiac is not a sentient entity but a cultural construct. It influences personality traits attributed to individuals born in each year, and it guides auspicious activities such as marriages, business openings, and travel.

Domains & Habitats

The zodiac permeates Vietnamese life: it is used in the lunar calendar, Tết (New Year) celebrations, and astrology. It is also present in literature, art, and everyday language.

Version Differences

The Vietnamese zodiac differs from the Chinese zodiac in two key animals: the cat replaces the rabbit, and the goat replaces the sheep. Additionally, the Vietnamese zodiac is sometimes ordered differently in local traditions, though the standard order follows the Chinese sequence.

Powers & Abilities

Astrological influence

Description

Each zodiac sign is believed to influence the personality, fortune, and compatibility of individuals born under it.

Limitations

Belief in astrological influence is not universal; it is considered cultural rather than scientifically proven.

Contradictions

Some sources claim that the cat was added because the Vietnamese word for rabbit (mão) sounds like 'cat' (mèo), but this is etymologically dubious.

Calendrical timekeeping

Source Version Standard Vietnamese calendar.

Description

The zodiac is used to mark years in the lunar calendar, which determines the dates of Tết and other festivals.

Limitations

The lunar calendar is no longer the official calendar, but it remains culturally significant.

Contradictions

Stories & Myths

The Legend of the Jade Emperor's Race

Source Vietnamese folk traditionCulture VietnamesePeriod Unknown (mythological)

Summary

The Jade Emperor invited all animals to a race to determine the order of the zodiac. The rat won by riding on the buffalo's back and jumping off at the finish line. The cat, who had been tricked by the rat into missing the race, was replaced by the rabbit in the Chinese version, but in Vietnam, the cat was included, and the rabbit was omitted.

Contradictions & Disputes

Cat vs. Rabbit

Position A

The cat replaced the rabbit because the Vietnamese word for rabbit (mão) sounds like 'cat' (mèo).

Position A — evidence

Linguistic similarity; some sources claim that the Chinese character for rabbit (卯) is pronounced 'mão' in Vietnamese, which is close to 'mèo'.

Position B

The cat was already part of the native Vietnamese zodiac before Chinese influence.

Position B — evidence

Historical records suggest that the Vietnamese zodiac may have been influenced by the Cham or other local cultures that included a cat.

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus. Most scholars lean toward the linguistic explanation, but some argue for independent origin.

Goat vs. Sheep

Position A

The Vietnamese zodiac uses the goat because the Chinese character for 'sheep' (羊) is ambiguous and can mean both sheep and goat.

Position A — evidence

In Chinese, 'yang' can refer to both animals, and Vietnamese translators may have chosen the goat for clarity.

Position B

The Vietnamese deliberately chose the goat because it is more common in Vietnam than sheep.

Position B — evidence

Goats are more familiar in Vietnamese agriculture, so the choice may reflect local fauna.

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus; both explanations are plausible.

Known Gaps

Access to primary Vietnamese sources was limited due to language barriers; most information was gathered from English-language secondary sources. The exact historical origin of the cat and goat replacements remains unresolved. Further research could involve consulting Vietnamese-language academic texts and local folklore archives.

Research Scope

This research focuses on the Vietnamese zodiac as a cultural and calendrical system, with emphasis on its distinctive differences from the Chinese zodiac, its role in Vietnamese society, and its modern adaptations. Primary sources include Vietnamese historical texts and scholarly works; however, access to Vietnamese-language sources was limited, and some details may be incomplete.
