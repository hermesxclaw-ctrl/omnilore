Who It Is

In the pantheon of Chinese folklore, Shunfeng'er stands as the celestial listener par excellence—a deity whose very name whispers of wind-borne hearing and whose ears are said to catch the faintest cries across the mortal realm. Though less renowned than his companion Qianliyan, the thousand-mile eyes, Shunfeng'er embodies the ancient human yearning to transcend distance, to hear the voices of the faraway, and to know what lies beyond the horizon.

As a deity, Shunfeng'er is typically portrayed as a loyal and vigilant guardian, using his hearing to protect the faithful and report on distant events. In the novel, he is initially an antagonist, but after his defeat and deification, he becomes a protector.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Shunfeng'er' (顺风耳) literally translates as 'ear that follows the wind' or 'obedient-to-the-wind ear', combining 'shun' (顺, meaning 'along with' or 'obedient to'), 'feng' (风, 'wind'), and 'er' (耳, 'ear'). The name signifies the ability to hear sounds carried by the wind over great distances

Powers
Supernatural Hearing

Shunfeng'er can hear sounds from incredibly long distances, reportedly up to a thousand miles. He can discern conversations, detect approaching enemies, and perceive hidden events.

Limit: The exact limitations are not specified in available sources. In the novel, his ability is countered by magical means or by the superior powers of the Zhou heroes.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Traditional depictions in temple art and opera often show Shunfeng'er as a fierce warrior with exaggerated, prominent ears, sometimes with wind motifs. He may be depicted with a green or blue face, wearing armor, and holding a weapon or a magical artifact. In the Fengshen Yanyi, he is described as a demon-like general with extraordinary hearing.

Symbols — His primary symbol is the ear, often depicted as oversized or with wind swirls. He is frequently paired with Qianliyan (Thousand-Mile Eye) as a duo representing hearing and sight. In temple iconography, they are often placed at entrances as guardian figures.

Domains — He is associated with the celestial realm and acts as a messenger or lookout. In folk practice, he is invoked for protection and for gaining knowledge of distant events.

Accounts I
The Two Demon Generals of the ShangFengshen Yanyi

Ming dynasty (novel set in the 11th century BCE)

Shunfeng'er and his companion Qianliyan serve as generals under King Zhou of the Shang dynasty. They use their extraordinary senses to aid the corrupt king, but are eventually defeated by the forces of King Wu and Jiang Ziya. After their deaths, they are posthumously deified as guardians. Their defeat contributes to the fall of the Shang dynasty. Their deification integrates them into the celestial bureaucracy as minor guardian deities.

Accounts II
Guardians at Temple GatesFolk religion and temple practices

Ming dynasty to present

In many Taoist and folk temples, statues of Shunfeng'er and Qianliyan are placed at the entrance to ward off evil and to serve as the eyes and ears of the temple, watching and listening for any threats or prayers. They are venerated as protectors who can hear the prayers of the faithful and detect malevolent spirits.
