Who It Is

The Greek Sphinx is a monstrous creature with the head and bust of a woman, the body of a lioness, the wings of a great bird, and sometimes a serpent-tipped tail. Sent by Hera (or Ares) to punish Thebes, she terrorized the city with a deadly riddle: 'What walks on four legs in the morning, two at noon, and three in the evening?' Those who failed to answer were strangled and devoured — hence her name (from sphingein, 'to strangle'). Oedipus solved the riddle ('Man'), and the Sphinx threw herself from a cliff in defeat. She represents the terrifying power of the unknown made articulate — the monster that kills with words.

Origins

The Greek Sphinx (distinct from the Egyptian sphinx) was sent by Hera (or Ares, or Dionysus) to punish Thebes for the crimes of King Laius, who had abducted and assaulted the young prince Chrysippus. The Sphinx took up position on Mount Phicium (hence her alternate name, Phix) overlooking the road to Thebes. She waylaid travelers and posed her riddle. The Thebans, unable to defeat her, offered the throne and the widow of Laius (Jocasta) to anyone who could solve it. Oedipus, fleeing Corinth to escape the prophecy that he would kill his father and marry his mother, encountered the Sphinx. His answer — 'Man, who crawls as an infant, walks upright in adulthood, and uses a cane in old age' — was correct. The Sphinx killed herself (or was killed by Oedipus). Oedipus was made king, married Jocasta (his mother), and the prophecy was fulfilled. The riddle has multiple ancient versions. One variant asks: 'There are two sisters: one gives birth to the other, and she, in turn, gives birth to the first' (Day and Night).

Attested in Oral Tradition / Sophocles / ApollodorusEra Archaic to Classical (c. 700-400 BCE)

Form

The Sphinx is a hybrid of terrifying beauty: a woman's face (often serene, sometimes fierce), a lioness's body (predatory power), eagle wings (swift, inescapable danger), and a serpent tail (venomous death). Her female face is crucial — she represents the terrifying 'other' that is simultaneously alluring and deadly. In art, she appears on vases, reliefs, and architectural sculpture, often as a guardian figure. The contrast with the Egyptian sphinx (male, benevolent guardian) is stark: the Greek Sphinx is explicitly female and malevolent. Sensory evocation: the rustle of wings, the feline grace of a predator, the cold intelligence of a face that asks you questions — and kills you when you fail.

Riddle Posing The Sphinx's primary power is her ability to pose an unanswerable riddle. This is not a simple question but a test of intellect and wisdom that her victims are unable to pass. The power lies in the riddle's inherent difficulty and the mortal penalty for failure. All versions Her power is contingent on the riddle being unanswered. Once the riddle is solved (by Oedipus), her power is broken and she is destroyed. None, though the riddle's wording varies (see stories).

Physical Devouring The Sphinx is a man-eating beast. She physically seizes and devours those who fail her riddle. This is her enforcement mechanism. All versions She is ultimately vulnerable to being defeated or destroyed, as shown by Oedipus's victory. None.

Flight As a winged creature, the Sphinx can fly. This allows her to move quickly, attack from above, and control the road she occupies. All Greek versions Not explicitly used as a tactical advantage in the surviving stories, but implied by her iconography. None.

Superhuman Strength and Ferocity As a lion-bodied monster, she possesses immense physical strength and a predatory nature, making her a formidable physical opponent in addition to her intellectual challenge. All versions Not directly tested in the myths, as she is defeated intellectually. None.

Behavior & Habitat

The Sphinx is unique among Greek monsters: she is intelligent, articulate, and kills not with claws but with questions. She represents the terror of the unknown that speaks — the monster that engages you cognitively before destroying you physically. She is a guardian of thresholds (the road to Thebes, the boundary between ignorance and knowledge), and her riddle forces a confrontation with the nature of human existence.

Weaknesses & Wards

The Sphinx's riddle, once solved, neutralizes her entire power. She is defined by her intellectual challenge — and when that challenge is met, she has no recourse but self-destruction. She is, in a sense, a monster created by a single question, and the right answer kills her.

Accounts

The Sphinx appears at the entrance to Thebes, posing a riddle to all who pass and devouring those who fail. The riddle, as recorded by Apollodorus, is: 'What has one voice, and yet becomes four-footed, two-footed, and three-footed?' Oedipus answers: 'Man—who crawls on all fours as a baby, walks on two legs as an adult, and uses a staff in old age.' Upon hearing the correct answer, the Sphinx, in despair, throws herself from the acropolis and dies. Oedipus is then hailed as the savior of Thebes and given the kingship and the hand of Queen Jocasta, unknowingly fulfilling the prophecy that he would kill his father and marry his mother.

The Sphinx is often understood as a divine punishment sent against Thebes. The specific reason varies: some say she was sent by Hera to punish the Thebans for the crime of Laius (Oedipus's father), who had abducted and raped the young Chrysippus. The Sphinx is thus an instrument of divine wrath, a plague upon the city that can only be lifted by solving her riddle.

Hesiod provides the earliest surviving genealogy for the Sphinx (called Phix). She is the offspring of Orthrus (the two-headed dog of Geryon) and the Chimera (or, in some readings, Echidna). This places her in a family of monstrous, composite creatures, siblings to the Nemean Lion. This genealogy establishes her as a creature of chaos and primordial danger, part of a monstrous lineage that heroes must overcome.

Cultural Significance

The Sphinx's riddle — the universal question about human existence — has resonated for millennia. The answer ('Man') is the first great humanistic statement in Western literature: the measure of all things is humanity itself. In Sophocles's Oedipus Rex, the Sphinx is a symbol of the truth that Oedipus can solve external riddles but cannot see the truth of his own life. The Sphinx has become a universal symbol of mystery, enigma, and the unknowable. The Egyptian Sphinx (male, benevolent, silent) and the Greek Sphinx (female, malevolent, questioning) represent two very different archetypes of the guardian-monster. In psychoanalysis, the Sphinx represents the riddle of existence that every individual must solve. The 'Riddle of the Sphinx' is referenced in countless works of literature, film, and popular culture. In art, the Sphinx has been a favorite subject from ancient Greece to the Symbolists (Moreau, von Stuck) to Surrealism.

Modern Interpretations

The Sphinx appears in Rick Riordan's Percy Jackson series as a monster that administers tests rather than killing directly. In the video game Hades II (early access), the Sphinx appears. In the 1997 anime Evangelion, the Sphinx is referenced in the psychological themes. In the Batman comics and films, the Riddler's love of riddles directly evokes the Sphinx archetype. In the Harry Potter series, the Sphinx appears in the Triwizard Maze. In the film Gods of Egypt (2016), the Sphinx appears. The 'Riddle of the Sphinx' is a recurring reference in mystery fiction, puzzle games, and escape rooms.

A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine a monster that doesn't chase you, doesn't roar, doesn't bite. Instead, it asks you a question. If you answer wrong, it kills you. If you answer right, it dies. The question is about yourself — about what it means to be human. The Sphinx is the most philosophical monster in mythology: she doesn't test your strength or speed or courage. She tests your understanding. She wants to know if you know what you are. And if you don't — if you can't answer the most basic question about the human condition — then you don't deserve to live.
