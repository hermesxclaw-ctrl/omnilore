Who It Is

Imagine a creature that doesn't just kill you—it asks you a question first. Not a simple question, but one that has haunted humanity for millennia: 'What walks on four legs in the morning, two at noon, and three in the evening?' Get it wrong, and you're dinner. Get it right, and you've just outsmarted a monster that has devoured the best minds of a city. This is the Sphinx: part lion, part woman, part bird, and entirely a problem.

The Sphinx is a predator and a tester. Her defining behavior is posing riddles to travelers and devouring those who cannot answer. She is territorial, occupying the road to Thebes and demanding passage. She is also characterized by a kind of monstrous pride or hubris, believing herself unbeatable in her intellectual challenge. Her self-destruction upon being defeated suggests a creature whose identity is intrinsically tied to her role as an unanswerable challenge.

Powers
Riddle Posing

The Sphinx's primary power is her ability to pose an unanswerable riddle. This is not a simple question but a test of intellect and wisdom that her victims are unable to pass. The power lies in the riddle's inherent difficulty and the mortal penalty for failure.

Limit: Her power is contingent on the riddle being unanswered. Once the riddle is solved (by Oedipus), her power is broken and she is destroyed.

Physical Devouring

The Sphinx is a man-eating beast. She physically seizes and devours those who fail her riddle. This is her enforcement mechanism.

Limit: She is ultimately vulnerable to being defeated or destroyed, as shown by Oedipus's victory.

Flight

As a winged creature, the Sphinx can fly. This allows her to move quickly, attack from above, and control the road she occupies.

Limit: Not explicitly used as a tactical advantage in the surviving stories, but implied by her iconography.

Superhuman Strength and Ferocity

As a lion-bodied monster, she possesses immense physical strength and a predatory nature, making her a formidable physical opponent in addition to her intellectual challenge.

Limit: Not directly tested in the myths, as she is defeated intellectually.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The Greek Sphinx is consistently depicted as a composite creature: the body of a lion, the wings of a bird (typically an eagle), and the head and breasts of a woman. She is female, unlike her Egyptian counterpart. In vase painting, she is often shown seated upright on her haunches, with her wings raised. She is sometimes shown wearing a headdress or crown. Her expression is typically stern or predatory.

Symbols — The Sphinx symbolizes enigma, the destructive power of knowledge, the danger of the liminal, and the threat of the feminine. She is a symbol of the unsolvable, the boundary between human and animal, and the consequences of hubris. In her role as a devourer, she also represents death and the underworld. She is a guardian figure, but a hostile one, inverting the protective role of similar composite creatures.

Domains — The Sphinx is primarily associated with the city of Thebes in Boeotia, specifically the road leading to the city and the nearby Mount Phikion (named after her). She is a liminal creature, occupying the threshold between the civilized world of the city and the wild, dangerous outside. She is also associated with death and the underworld, as her victims are devoured.

Accounts I
The Riddle of the Sphinx and OedipusSophocles (Oedipus Rex), Apollodorus (Bibliotheca), and other sources

Archaic/Classical (myth likely older); literary sources from 5th century BCE onwards

The Sphinx appears at the entrance to Thebes, posing a riddle to all who pass and devouring those who fail. The riddle, as recorded by Apollodorus, is: 'What has one voice, and yet becomes four-footed, two-footed, and three-footed?' Oedipus answers: 'Man—who crawls on all fours as a baby, walks on two legs as an adult, and uses a staff in old age.' Upon hearing the correct answer, the Sphinx, in despair, throws herself from the acropolis and dies. Oedipus is then hailed as the savior of Thebes and given the kingship and the hand of Queen Jocasta, unknowingly fulfilling the prophecy that he would kill his father and marry his mother. The immediate consequence is the liberation of Thebes from the Sphinx's terror and Oedipus's rise to power. The deeper, tragic consequence is that this very act of salvation sets the stage for his downfall, as it leads him to unknowingly marry his mother, Jocasta.

Accounts II
The Sphinx as a Scourge Sent by the GodsVarious (implied in Sophocles and other tragedians)

Classical

The Sphinx is often understood as a divine punishment sent against Thebes. The specific reason varies: some say she was sent by Hera to punish the Thebans for the crime of Laius (Oedipus's father), who had abducted and raped the young Chrysippus. The Sphinx is thus an instrument of divine wrath, a plague upon the city that can only be lifted by solving her riddle. The Sphinx's reign of terror is the immediate crisis that Oedipus resolves, and it is the catalyst for his tragic destiny. It also serves as a narrative explanation for the city's suffering and the need for a hero.

The Sphinx in the Theogony (Genealogy)Hesiod, Theogony

c. 700 BCE

Hesiod provides the earliest surviving genealogy for the Sphinx (called Phix). She is the offspring of Orthrus (the two-headed dog of Geryon) and the Chimera (or, in some readings, Echidna). This places her in a family of monstrous, composite creatures, siblings to the Nemean Lion. This genealogy establishes her as a creature of chaos and primordial danger, part of a monstrous lineage that heroes must overcome. This genealogy firmly roots the Sphinx in the Greek mythological cosmos as a monster of the old order, destined to be defeated by a hero (Oedipus).

Greek vs. Egyptian SphinxThe Greek Sphinx and the Egyptian Sphinx are the same or similar entitiesThey are distinct entities from different cultures with different roles and iconography

The Sphinx is a single, universal creature from ancient Egypt. — There are two distinct Sphinx traditions: the Greek (female, winged, riddle-posing) and the Egyptian (male, wingless, guardian). The Greek Sphinx is the one associated with the riddle and Oedipus.

The Sphinx's riddle is 'What walks on four legs in the morning, two legs at noon, and three legs in the evening?' — This is the standard modern version, but the earliest sources do not quote the riddle. The first recorded version is from Apollodorus, and it is slightly different: 'What has one voice, and yet becomes four-footed, two-footed, and three-footed?'

The Sphinx was a benevolent guardian who protected Thebes. — The Greek Sphinx was a hostile, man-eating monster who terrorized Thebes. She was not a protector but a scourge.

The Sphinx was a symbol of wisdom and knowledge. — In Greek myth, she was a symbol of the destructive power of knowledge. Her riddle was a deadly test, not a gift of wisdom. She represents the danger of the unknown and the consequences of failing to understand.

The Sphinx was a male creature. — The Greek Sphinx is consistently female. The Egyptian Sphinx is male. The conflation of the two has led to the idea that the Sphinx is male.
