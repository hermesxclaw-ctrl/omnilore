Who It Is

Imagine the world without a sun. Not a cloudy day, but a world where the sky is a permanent, lightless void, and all warmth and life are gone. This is the world the Japanese gods created when they accidentally drove the most powerful deity in the heavens, Amaterasu, to hide in a cave. This is the story of the goddess who holds the sun in her heart, and the party that was thrown to bring her—and the world—back to life.

Amaterasu is portrayed as a benevolent, wise, and nurturing deity, the embodiment of order, light, and life. However, she is also shown to be vulnerable and capable of deep sorrow and withdrawal, as seen in the rock-cave episode. She is a figure of supreme authority, whose will is law for the other kami and for the imperial court. She is decisive and protective, as seen in her dispatch of her grandson Ninigi to pacify the earthly realm.

Powers
Solar Radiance

Amaterasu's primary power is her control over the sun and the light of the world. When she is present, the world is fertile and alive. When she retreats, the world is plunged into darkness, and all order collapses.

Limit: Her power is not absolute; she can be driven to despair and withdraw her light, demonstrating a psychological vulnerability.

Divine Authority and Command

As the sovereign of Takamagahara, her commands are absolute. She can dispatch deities, delegate authority, and set cosmic order in motion. Her decision to send her grandson Ninigi to rule the earthly realm is the foundation of the imperial mandate.

Limit: Her authority is often challenged by her brother Susanoo, and she must rely on the counsel of other wise deities.

Weaving

In the cave myth, she is described as weaving in her sacred hall. This is not just a domestic activity; it is a cosmic act of creating order and the fabric of the world. Her weaving is a symbol of her creative and civilizing power.

Limit: It is this activity that is interrupted by Susanoo's violent actions, leading to her withdrawal.

Gift of the Three Regalia

She bestows the Mirror, Sword, and Jewel upon her grandson Ninigi. These objects are not just gifts; they are imbued with her divine authority and become the symbols of imperial rule. They are a tangible extension of her power on Earth.

Limit: The power is delegated, not directly wielded by her on Earth.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Amaterasu is famously not depicted in Shinto art, as her radiance is considered too great to be captured. In later folk art and popular culture, she is often depicted as a beautiful, radiant woman, sometimes holding a mirror or a spear. In her most famous myth, she is described as a powerful deity whose presence brings light and life to the world. Her absence from the world causes total darkness and cosmic distress.

Symbols — The Yata no Kagami (Eight-Span Mirror) is her primary symbol, representing her brilliance and truth. The rising sun is her ultimate symbol, often depicted on the Japanese flag and in various cultural contexts. The imperial chrysanthemum crest is also associated with her. In some artistic depictions, she is shown emerging from the cave, bringing with her the dawn.

Domains — Her primary domain is the sun, the heavens (Takamagahara, the High Plain of Heaven), and the source of all life and light. She is also the patron deity of the imperial family and, by extension, the Japanese nation. Her chief terrestrial sanctuary is the Ise Grand Shrine. Her 'habitat' is both the celestial realm and the inner sanctum of the shrine where the mirror is kept.

Accounts I
The Birth of the Sun GoddessKojiki, Nihon Shoki

Age of the Gods (Kamiyo)

After creating the islands of Japan, Izanagi and Izanami gave birth to many deities. When Izanami died giving birth to the fire god, a grief-stricken Izanagi journeyed to the underworld (Yomi) to retrieve her. Horrified by her putrefied form, he fled and performed a purification ritual (misogi) upon his return. As he washed his left eye, the radiant goddess Amaterasu was born. He then washed his right eye, and the moon god Tsukuyomi was born. Finally, from his nose, the tempestuous storm god Susanoo was born. Izanagi was overjoyed and divided the world among his three children: Amaterasu would rule the High Plain of Heaven, Tsukuyomi the night, and Susanoo the seas. This establishes Amaterasu's supreme position in the celestial hierarchy and her relationship with her siblings, setting the stage for the conflict with Susanoo.

The Rock-Cave of Heaven (Ama-no-Iwato)Kojiki, Nihon Shoki

Age of the Gods (Kamiyo)

Susanoo, envious and destructive, repeatedly terrorized Takamagahara. His final outrage was to flay a heavenly horse and throw it into Amaterasu's sacred weaving hall, killing one of her weaving maidens. In grief and rage, Amaterasu retreated into the Ama-no-Iwato, a heavenly rock cave, sealing the entrance with a boulder. The world was plunged into darkness and chaos. The eight million kami gathered to devise a plan to lure her out. The goddess Ame-no-Uzume, a master of revelry, performed a wild and obscene dance on an overturned tub, causing the assembled deities to laugh uproariously. Curious, Amaterasu opened the cave door a crack to see what was happening. The god Ame-no-Tajikarao (the Heavenly Strong-Arm Man) seized the moment and pulled the door open, while the other kami stretched a sacred rope (shimenawa) behind her to prevent her from re-entering. Light was restored to the world. This myth explains the origin of the sun's cycle, the importance of ritual and celebration (the kagura dance), and the use of the shimenawa to mark sacred space. Accounts II
The Pacification of the Central Land of the Reed PlainsKojiki, Nihon Shoki

Age of the Gods (Kamiyo)

After the exile of Susanoo, Amaterasu decided to bring the earthly realm (the Central Land of the Reed Plains) under her divine authority. She first sent her son Ame-no-Oshihomimi, then the god Ame-no-Hohi, but both failed. Finally, she sent Ame-no-Wakahiko, who was killed for his treachery. She then dispatched the fearsome god Takemikazuchi, who subdued the earthly deities, including the powerful Ōkuninushi, through a combination of force and negotiation. With the land pacified, Amaterasu sent her grandson, Ninigi-no-Mikoto, to rule, bestowing upon him the Three Imperial Regalia. This myth is the divine charter for the Japanese imperial line, establishing its legitimacy as the direct descendant of the sun goddess and its mandate to rule the land.

The Sun and the MoonNihon Shoki

Age of the Gods (Kamiyo)

Amaterasu, concerned about the state of the earthly realm, sent the moon god Tsukuyomi to investigate. During his visit, the food goddess Ukemochi-no-kami prepared a feast for him. However, Tsukuyomi was disgusted to see that the food was produced from her own body (by vomiting, defecating, and other bodily functions) and, in a fit of rage, killed her. When Amaterasu learned of this, she was furious and banished Tsukuyomi from her presence, declaring they could no longer share the same sky. This is why the sun and the moon are never seen together. This myth explains the separation of day and night and establishes Amaterasu's clear moral authority and her intolerance for cruelty and waste.

Amaterasu is the creator of the world. — She is the goddess of the sun and the ancestress of the imperial line, but the world was created by Izanagi and Izanami. Her role is to bring light and life to an already-created world.

Amaterasu is a gentle, passive goddess with no agency. — While benevolent, she is also a powerful and decisive ruler. She banishes her brother, dispatches gods to conquer the earthly realm, and withdraws her light in protest. She is a figure of immense power and will.

The Japanese flag's red circle is a direct depiction of Amaterasu. — The flag's red disc is a symbol of the sun, which is Amaterasu's domain. However, it is not a direct anthropomorphic depiction of the goddess herself.

Amaterasu is the same as the Chinese goddess Xihe or other sun deities. — While there are parallels, Amaterasu is a distinct Japanese deity with her own unique myths, relationships, and political function. Syncretism is a scholarly hypothesis, not a fact.
