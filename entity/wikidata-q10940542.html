Form

Amaterasu is famously not depicted in Shinto art, as her radiance is considered too great to be captured. In later folk art and popular culture, she is often depicted as a beautiful, radiant woman, sometimes holding a mirror or a spear. In her most famous myth, she is described as a powerful deity whose presence brings light and life to the world. Her absence from the world causes total darkness and cosmic distress.

Powers

Solar Radiance Amaterasu's primary power is her control over the sun and the light of the world. When she is present, the world is fertile and alive. When she retreats, the world is plunged into darkness, and all order collapses. All versions (Kojiki, Nihon Shoki) Her power is not absolute; she can be driven to despair and withdraw her light, demonstrating a psychological vulnerability. None.

Divine Authority and Command As the sovereign of Takamagahara, her commands are absolute. She can dispatch deities, delegate authority, and set cosmic order in motion. Her decision to send her grandson Ninigi to rule the earthly realm is the foundation of the imperial mandate. All versions Her authority is often challenged by her brother Susanoo, and she must rely on the counsel of other wise deities. None.

Weaving In the cave myth, she is described as weaving in her sacred hall. This is not just a domestic activity; it is a cosmic act of creating order and the fabric of the world. Her weaving is a symbol of her creative and civilizing power. Kojiki and Nihon Shoki It is this activity that is interrupted by Susanoo's violent actions, leading to her withdrawal. None.

Gift of the Three Regalia She bestows the Mirror, Sword, and Jewel upon her grandson Ninigi. These objects are not just gifts; they are imbued with her divine authority and become the symbols of imperial rule. They are a tangible extension of her power on Earth. All versions The power is delegated, not directly wielded by her on Earth. None.

Accounts

After creating the islands of Japan, Izanagi and Izanami gave birth to many deities. When Izanami died giving birth to the fire god, a grief-stricken Izanagi journeyed to the underworld (Yomi) to retrieve her. Horrified by her putrefied form, he fled and performed a purification ritual (misogi) upon his return. As he washed his left eye, the radiant goddess Amaterasu was born. He then washed his right eye, and the moon god Tsukuyomi was born. Finally, from his nose, the tempestuous storm god Susanoo was born. Izanagi was overjoyed and divided the world among his three children: Amaterasu would rule the High Plain of Heaven, Tsukuyomi the night, and Susanoo the seas.

Susanoo, envious and destructive, repeatedly terrorized Takamagahara. His final outrage was to flay a heavenly horse and throw it into Amaterasu's sacred weaving hall, killing one of her weaving maidens. In grief and rage, Amaterasu retreated into the Ama-no-Iwato, a heavenly rock cave, sealing the entrance with a boulder. The world was plunged into darkness and chaos. The eight million kami gathered to devise a plan to lure her out. The goddess Ame-no-Uzume, a master of revelry, performed a wild and obscene dance on an overturned tub, causing the assembled deities to laugh uproariously. Curious, Amaterasu opened the cave door a crack to see what was happening. The god Ame-no-Tajikarao (the Heavenly Strong-Arm Man) seized the moment and pulled the door open, while the other kami stretched a sacred rope (shimenawa) behind her to prevent her from re-entering. Light was restored to the world.

After the exile of Susanoo, Amaterasu decided to bring the earthly realm (the Central Land of the Reed Plains) under her divine authority. She first sent her son Ame-no-Oshihomimi, then the god Ame-no-Hohi, but both failed. Finally, she sent Ame-no-Wakahiko, who was killed for his treachery. She then dispatched the fearsome god Takemikazuchi, who subdued the earthly deities, including the powerful Ōkuninushi, through a combination of force and negotiation. With the land pacified, Amaterasu sent her grandson, Ninigi-no-Mikoto, to rule, bestowing upon him the Three Imperial Regalia.

Amaterasu, concerned about the state of the earthly realm, sent the moon god Tsukuyomi to investigate. During his visit, the food goddess Ukemochi-no-kami prepared a feast for him. However, Tsukuyomi was disgusted to see that the food was produced from her own body (by vomiting, defecating, and other bodily functions) and, in a fit of rage, killed her. When Amaterasu learned of this, she was furious and banished Tsukuyomi from her presence, declaring they could no longer share the same sky. This is why the sun and the moon are never seen together.

Modern Interpretations

Video Game Ōkami (2006) Clover Studio, Capcom 2006 direct The player character is Amaterasu, reimagined as a white wolf. The game's central plot is directly based on the Ama-no-Iwato myth, where the player must restore color and life to a world drained by darkness. The game is a love letter to Japanese mythology and art. Manga/Anime Naruto Masashi Kishimoto 1999-2014 (manga), 2002-2017 (anime) inspired The character Itachi Uchiha has a technique called 'Amaterasu,' which manifests as black flames that burn for seven days. This is a direct reference to the goddess and her association with fire and light, albeit in a destructive form. Anime Noragami Adachitoka 2010-present (manga), 2014 (anime) inspired The series features a character named Amaterasu who is the ruler of the heavens, a powerful and somewhat aloof figure. The depiction draws on the goddess's authority and celestial domain. Video Game Shin Megami Tensei series Atlus 1987-present direct Amaterasu is a recurring demon/Persona in the series, often depicted as a radiant woman. Her abilities are typically fire-based, reflecting her solar nature. Film The Legend of the Stardust Brothers (1985) Macoto Tezuka 1985 inspired The film features a rock musical sequence that references the Ama-no-Iwato myth, showing the power of music and celebration to bring light back to the world. Scholarly Debates
Birth of Amaterasu
Born from Izanagi's left eye (Kojiki) ⇌ Born from a white copper mirror held by Izanagi, or from his left eye (Nihon Shoki variants)
Evidence for A: Kojiki is the oldest surviving written record.
Evidence for B: Nihon Shoki offers multiple variants, showing a less fixed tradition.
The cause of Susanoo's exile
He was exiled for his violent behavior in Takamagahara, including flaying a horse and killing a weaving maiden (Kojiki). ⇌ He was exiled for his general disruptive behavior, but the cave episode is the primary catalyst (Nihon Shoki).
Evidence for A: Kojiki presents a more personal and dramatic conflict.
Evidence for B: Nihon Shoki presents a more cosmic and political conflict.
The nature of the imperial mandate
The imperial family's rule is a divine mandate from Amaterasu, making the emperor a living god (State Shinto). ⇌ The emperor is a symbol of the state and the unity of the people, not a divine figure (Post-WWII Constitution).
Evidence for A: The Kojiki and Nihon Shoki, and the pre-war imperial ideology.
Evidence for B: The Japanese Constitution (1947), which defines the emperor as a symbol.
What We Don't Know

The primary texts (Kojiki and Nihon Shoki) are the definitive sources, but a deeper analysis of the various modern academic interpretations of these texts would be beneficial. The specific rituals and practices of the Ise Grand Shrine, which are largely secret, are not fully documented in public sources. The impact of Amaterasu on modern Japanese national identity and the post-war reinterpretation of her role is a complex topic that requires more specialized research.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Amaterasu not just as a goddess, but as the ultimate symbol of the Japanese imperial state's divine right to rule. Her myth is a political and religious charter, explaining not only the existence of the sun but also the very structure of Japanese society. In modern times, she has been re-imagined as a cultural icon, a symbol of resilience, and a source of artistic inspiration, from ancient chronicles to a video game where you play as her in the form of a wolf.
