Who It Is

Imagine a goddess who is both the poison and the cure, a divine physician who wears a scorpion on her head like a crown. This is Serqet, the ancient Egyptian mistress of venom, who could kill with a thought and heal with a word. She was the patron of doctors who faced the deadliest creatures of the desert, and the guardian who stood watch over the bodies of pharaohs. She is a paradox made divine: a mother who nurses kings, and a force of nature that commands the sting of death.

Serqet is a paradoxical figure: she is a dangerous, venomous force, but her behavior is that of a fierce protector and a nurturing mother. She is not a violent or aggressive deity; rather, she is a healer who neutralizes the very poison she embodies. She is depicted as a guardian who stands vigil, a nurse who tends to the sick, and a powerful ally who uses her sting to defend the innocent and the deceased. She is a 'scorpion-mother,' fiercely protective of her charges, whether they are the king, the gods, or the blessed dead.

Powers
Healing Venomous Bites and Stings

Serqet's primary power is the ability to heal the effects of venom, particularly from scorpions and snakes. She was invoked in countless spells and rituals to cure the afflicted, and her priests were often physicians who specialized in toxicology.

Limit: Her power was typically accessed through ritual, incantation, and the application of medicines. It was not an instantaneous or automatic miracle, but a divine force that empowered human healers.

Protection and Guardianship

She acts as a divine bodyguard, protecting the king, the gods, and the deceased from harm. She is one of the four goddesses who guard the canopic chest, and she stands at the corners of the royal sarcophagus, warding off evil.

Limit: Her protection is typically focused on the physical body and the integrity of the tomb, rather than on abstract concepts like the soul's journey.

Mastery over Venom as a Weapon

While primarily a healer, Serqet also commands venom as a weapon. She can direct her scorpion-familiar to sting her enemies, or she can inflict poison as a form of divine punishment.

Limit: This power is used defensively or punitively, not as a tool of aggression. She is a protector, not a conqueror.

Nursing and Sustenance

In the Pyramid Texts, Serqet gives her breast to the deceased king, providing him with divine milk and sustaining him in the afterlife. This makes her a mother-goddess figure, a source of life and royal legitimacy.

Limit: This power is specifically directed at the divine king or the blessed dead, not the general population.

Accounts I
The Protection of Horus from Scorpions (The Metternich Stela)The Metternich Stela, a late-period magical stela (c. 360-343 BCE).

Late Period (c. 4th century BCE)

The goddess Isis, having fled the wrath of Set, is hiding in the marshes of the Delta with her infant son Horus. She is accompanied by seven scorpions, servants of Serqet, who act as her guardians. One of the scorpions, Tefen, hides under the rudder of a boat and stings a wealthy woman who had refused Isis shelter. The woman is poisoned and near death. Isis, upon learning of this, is initially angry but is moved by the woman's repentance. Serqet, or the scorpions themselves, are then invoked to draw the poison out, and the woman is healed. This story establishes the power of Serqet's scorpions to both inflict and cure venomous harm. The story reinforces the efficacy of magical healing and the power of the divine protectors. It also establishes a moral lesson about hospitality and repentance.

Accounts II
The Guardian of the Canopic JarsBook of the Dead, Chapter 17; various funerary inscriptions.

New Kingdom onward (c. 1550 BCE - Roman period)

In the funerary ritual, the deceased's internal organs were removed and placed in four canopic jars. Each jar was guarded by one of the Four Sons of Horus: Imsety (liver), Hapy (lungs), Duamutef (stomach), and Qebehsenuef (intestines). Each of these sons was in turn protected by a goddess. Serqet was the protectress of Qebehsenuef, the falcon-headed son who guarded the intestines. This role placed her as a direct guardian of the physical integrity of the deceased in the tomb. This role made Serqet an indispensable part of the Egyptian funerary cult, ensuring her presence in every elite burial.

The Nurse of the KingPyramid Texts, Utterance 234.

Old Kingdom (c. 2400-2300 BCE)

The Pyramid Texts, the oldest religious texts in the world, depict Serqet as a divine mother to the deceased king. She gives him her breast to suckle, providing him with life-giving milk and ensuring his rebirth in the afterlife. This act places her in the same category as Isis, Nephthys, and Neith, who all serve as divine nurses to the king. This text establishes her ancient pedigree as a royal and motherly deity, a role she maintains throughout Egyptian history.

Serqet is a 'goddess of scorpions' in the same way that Poseidon is a 'god of the sea.' — Her domain is not the scorpions themselves, but the power over their venom. She is a goddess of healing and protection from venomous creatures, not a matriarch of all scorpions. She embodies the cure as much as the poison.

Serqet is a minor or obscure goddess. — While she did not have a major cult center with a vast temple complex, she was a standard and essential figure in Egyptian religion, particularly in funerary practice and personal piety. Her presence on royal shrines and in the Book of the Dead shows her importance.

Serqet is purely a protector of the dead. — Her funerary role is important, but she was equally a goddess of the living. She was invoked to heal scorpion stings, a common and deadly threat in Egypt, and her priests were practical physicians. She was a deity of daily life, not just the afterlife.
