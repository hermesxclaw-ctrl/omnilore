The Approach

In the vast tapestry of Hindu mythology, few figures embody the paradox of immense strength and unwavering duty as vividly as Balarama, the elder brother of Krishna. Wielding a mighty plow as his weapon, he is the god of agriculture, intoxication, and the unyielding force that upholds dharma itself.

Content advisory. Contains references to violence, including killing of demons and a fatal encounter with a rival. Depictions of alcohol consumption may be present.
Identity

Balarama

Pronunciation Buh-luh-RAH-muh

Known As

BaladevaBalabhadraHalayudhaSankarshanaRamaRauhineyaMusaliNilambara

Etymology

The name Balarama means 'Rama of strength' (bala = strength, Rama = a name meaning 'pleasing' or 'dark'). It may also be derived from 'Bala' (strength) and 'Rama' (the deity).

Cultural Ownership

Hinduism, particularly Vaishnavism; also significant in Jainism and Buddhism as a figure in their narratives.

Disputed Identifications

Some traditions identify Balarama as an incarnation of the serpent Shesha, while others see him as a form of Vishnu himself. In Jainism, he is a hero (Baladeva) in the line of the 63 Shalakapurushas.

Description

Appearance

Balarama is typically depicted as fair-skinned (or white), in contrast to Krishna's dark complexion. He is tall and muscular, often wearing blue or yellow garments. His attributes are a plow (hala) and a pestle (musala), and he is often shown with a snake-hood canopy over his head, symbolizing his association with Shesha.

Symbols & Iconography

Plow (hala), pestle (musala), snake hood, earrings, and a garland of wild flowers. He is often depicted with a drinking cup or in a state of intoxication.

Behaviour & Personality

Balarama is known for his immense physical strength, straightforwardness, and a tendency to be impulsive. He is loyal to his family, especially Krishna, and is a staunch upholder of dharma. He enjoys agriculture, music, and drinking.

Domains & Habitats

Agriculture, strength, intoxication, and the protection of devotees. He is associated with the region of Braj (Mathura/Vrindavan) and the sacred river Yamuna.

Version Differences

In the Mahabharata, Balarama is a mighty warrior and teacher of both Bhima and Duryodhana in mace-fighting. In the Puranas, his divine nature is more emphasized, including his role as an avatar of Shesha. In Jainism, he is a non-violent hero who ultimately renounces the world.

Stories & Myths

Birth and Childhood

Source Vishnu Purana, Bhagavata PuranaCulture HinduPeriod Mythic

Summary

Balarama was conceived as the seventh child of Devaki, but was transferred to the womb of Rohini to escape Kamsa's infanticide. He was born as Sankarshana, the one who was drawn away. He grew up in Gokula with Krishna, performing many childhood feats, including killing the demon Dhenuka and the bull demon Arishtasura.

Variants

Some texts say he was born directly from Devaki and then transferred; others say he was an incarnation of Shesha from the start.

Source Bhagavata PuranaCulture HinduPeriod Mythic

The Duel with Duryodhana

Source MahabharataCulture HinduPeriod Epic

Summary

Balarama taught both Bhima and Duryodhana the art of mace-fighting. When the Kurukshetra war was about to begin, he went on a pilgrimage to avoid taking sides. Later, he witnessed the final mace duel between Bhima and Duryodhana and was furious when Bhima struck Duryodhana below the belt, a foul blow. He threatened to kill Bhima, but Krishna intervened.

Participants

Balarama

Bhima

Duryodhana

Krishna

Consequences

Balarama remained neutral in the war but showed his adherence to fair combat.

Variants

In some versions, Balarama does not teach Duryodhana.

The Curse on the Yadavas

Source Mahabharata, Mausala ParvaCulture HinduPeriod Epic

Summary

After the destruction of the Yadava dynasty, Balarama, in a state of grief and meditation, gave up his body. Some accounts say he cursed the Yadavas for mocking him, leading to their destruction.

Participants

Balarama

Yadavas

Consequences

His death marks the end of the Yadava era and the beginning of the Kali Yuga.

Variants

In some Puranas, he does not curse but simply departs.

The Taming of the Yamuna

Source Bhagavata PuranaCulture HinduPeriod Mythic

Summary

Balarama, in a fit of intoxication, called the Yamuna river to come to him. When she refused, he dragged her with his plow, forcing her to follow his will.

Participants

Yamuna

Consequences

Demonstrated his power over nature and his temper.

Variants

Dark History

Violence in killing demons

Source Bhagavata Purana

Description

Balarama killed many demons, often with great violence, such as splitting Dhenuka's body and hurling it into a tree.

Scholarly Context

These acts are seen as protective in nature, upholding dharma.

Intoxication and reckless behavior

Source Bhagavata Purana

Description

Balarama is frequently depicted as drunk, and his intoxication led to the taming of the Yamuna and other impulsive acts.

Scholarly Context

Some interpret this as a humanizing trait, while others see it as symbolic of divine play.

Curse on the Yadavas

Source Mahabharata

Description

In some accounts, Balarama cursed the Yadavas, leading to their mutual destruction, though this is more often attributed to a general curse by sages.

Scholarly Context

This event is part of the larger narrative of the decline of the Yadava dynasty.

Contradictions & Disputes

Incarnation of Shesha vs. Vishnu

Position A

Balarama is an avatar of Shesha, the serpent.

Position A — evidence

Puranas explicitly state this.

Position B

Balarama is an avatar of Vishnu himself.

Position B — evidence

Some Vaishnava traditions consider him a form of Vishnu.

Scholarly Consensus

The Shesha avatar is the most widely accepted.

Role in Mahabharata war

Position A

Balarama was neutral and went on a pilgrimage.

Position A — evidence

Mahabharata states he left for pilgrimage.

Position B

He was present and participated in some events.

Position B — evidence

Some regional versions place him at the war.

Scholarly Consensus

The pilgrimage is the standard account.

Death and departure

Position A

He gave up his body through yoga.

Position A — evidence

Mahabharata describes his ascension.

Position B

He was killed by a curse or accident.

Position B — evidence

Some later texts say he died due to a curse.

Scholarly Consensus

Yogic departure is the primary narrative.
