Who It Is

Phalias never slew a monster or stole a throne — his entire myth is a single, strange inheritance. He is one of fifty sons Heracles fathered on the fifty daughters of King Thespius while hunting a lion on Mount Cithaeron, and then sorted like a living legacy: seven kept at Thespiae, three sent to Thebes, forty shipped to Sardinia with Iolaus to found a colony. No hymn describes his face, no vase names his deed; he exists as a genealogical footnote that reveals how Greeks used Heracles' seed to claim cities.

No personality, speech, or moral character is attested for Phalias in any source. He is a name in a list, not a dramatized character. The narrative treats him and his brothers as a group to be distributed by Heracles' instructions. Behavior is therefore unattested. Do not infer bravery, piety, or leadership from his father.

Heraclid Lineage

As son of Heracles, Phalias is by descent a Heraclid, implying heroic ancestry and potential claim to Heracles' prestige. No specific superhuman strength, technique, or divine favor is attested for him individually.

Limit: No feats, labors, or powers are recorded for Phalias. Any Heraclid status is genealogical, not demonstrated. Cannot be assumed to share Heracles' strength or invulnerability.

Colonial Founder Status (Collective)

If among the forty, Phalias would be counted as an oikist (founder) of the Sardinian colony led by Iolaus, which became the Iolaans/ Ilians of Sardinia. This is a political/ancestral status, not a supernatural ability.

Limit: Individual assignment to Sardinia vs Thespiae vs Thebes is not preserved by name. No city or tribe is eponymously named for Phalias. No founder cult is attested for him.

No Attested Individual Power

Explicitly: no ancient source attributes to Phalias a weapon, transformation, prophecy, or special skill. This absence is itself the evidence — he is a genealogical marker, not a wonder-worker.

Limit: Do not invent powers from Heracles' repertoire. Modern adaptations sometimes give all Heraclids generic 'super-strength' — that is adaptation-specific invention, not attested.

Accounts I
Heracles and the Cithaeronian LionApollodorus 2.4.9-10; Diodorus Siculus 4.29.2; Pausanias 9.27.6

Mythic heroic age; sources 1st century BCE - 2nd century CE

At age eighteen, Heracles, living with King Thespius or hunting from Thebes, is tasked with killing the lion ravaging Mount Cithaeron, which preyed on Thespius' and Amphitryon's herds. He hunts the lion for fifty days. Thespius hosts Heracles lavishly during the hunt. The lion is slain — Heracles thereafter wears its skin in some accounts, prefiguring the Nemean lion. This hunt provides the narrative pretext for Heracles' extended stay and access to Thespius' daughters. Establishes Heracles' early heroic prowess before the Twelve Labors; creates hospitality bond with Thespius that explains the subsequent union with the daughters; etiology for Thespian claims of Heraclid descent.

The Union with the Fifty Daughters of ThespiusApollodorus 2.4.10; Diodorus 4.29.2-3; Athenaeus 13.556F citing Herodorus; Tzetzes Chiliades 2.221-227

Mythic; literary attestations Classical to Byzantine

Thespius, who had fifty daughters by his wife Megamede (daughter of Arneus) according to Apollodorus, offers them to Heracles. Over the course of the hunt, Heracles lies with all fifty — in one night according to some epitomes, in one per night for fifty nights according to Diodorus and others, or in one week in a variant. Heliconis is the daughter assigned to Phalias' conception. Thespius is said to have desired that each daughter bear a child by the hero, and Heracles, thinking each time it was the same woman or being made drunk, complies. The eldest and youngest daughters bear twins in some accounts, implying 51 children, but the canonical count remains 50 sons. Conception of the fifty Thespiadae, including Phalias. Establishes a large Heraclid kin group tied to Thespiae. Later authors debate morality and plausibility; Athenaeus preserves a more eroticized banquet-talk version.

The Birth and Naming of Phalias son of HeliconisApollodorus 2.7.8 (Frazer appendix list of Thespiades)

Mythic genealogy; list preserved in 1st-2nd century CE text

In Apollodorus' catalogue, each daughter is paired with a son: 'Heliconis bore Phalias.' This is the sole attestation that names Phalias' mother specifically. No birth narrative, omen, or childhood deed is given. The list functions as a genealogical charter, not a story. Phalias is one entry among fifty, with no further epithet. Fixes Phalias' place in the Thespian stemma. Provides the only individualizing detail for him (maternity). Allows later compilers to count him among Heraclidae.

Accounts II
The Division of the Sons: Seven, Three, and FortyApollodorus 2.7.8; Diodorus 4.29.3; Pausanias 9.27.7

Mythic colonization charter

When the sons are grown, Heracles sends instructions to Thespius: keep seven of the sons in Thespiae, send three to Thebes, and dispatch the remaining forty, together with his nephew Iolaus, to colonize Sardinia. This tripartite division explains Thespian, Theban, and Sardinian Heraclid populations. The message is Heracles' only recorded paternal act toward the Thespiadae after conception. Creates etiology for Sardinian Iolaans (Iolaos) and for Heraclid families at Thespiae and Thebes. Provides mythic justification for Greek interest in Sardinia. Phalias' ultimate fate depends on which group he was assigned to — unattested individually.

The Sardinian Colony of Iolaus and the ThespiadaeDiodorus Siculus 4.29.3-4.30.3, 5.15.1-5; Pausanias 10.17.5; Apollodorus 2.7.8

Mythic colonization; sources Hellenistic-Roman

Iolaus, Heracles' nephew and charioteer, leads the forty (or fewer) Thespiadae to Sardinia. They seize the island, found cities, and become the Iolaans, later called Ilians, living in the interior. They build works attributed to Daedalus (Daidaleia), live in freedom, and resist Carthaginians and Romans in later rationalizations. Diodorus presents this as the first Greek colonization of Sardinia, making the Thespiadae ancestors of a distinct Sardinian people who retained Greek customs and Heracles worship. Etiology for Sardinian toponyms, nuraghi reinterpreted as Greek works, and cults of Iolaus and Heracles in Sardinia. Thespiae gains prestige as mother-city. No individual deeds of Phalias in Sardinia are recorded.

Variant Chronology: One Night, One Week, or Fifty DaysTzetzes Chiliades 2.221-227; Apollodorus epitome; Diodorus 4.29.2; Athenaeus 13.556F

Hellenistic to Byzantine reception

Ancient mythographers explicitly disagreed on how long Heracles' union with the daughters lasted. The 'one night' version maximizes Heracles' virility; the 'fifty days/nights' version aligns with the fifty-day lion hunt and makes the liaison sequential and perhaps more plausible as hospitality. Tzetzes notes both traditions. This is not a separate adventure but a contested detail of the same episode that affects how Phalias' conception is dated relative to his brothers — simultaneously or sequentially. Illustrates how mythic time is manipulated for effect; influences modern retellings' tone (marvel vs extended hospitality). For Phalias, it determines whether he is a twin-contemporary or sequential sibling, but no source exploits this for his characterization.

Phalias was a famous hero who performed labors or had superpowers like his father Heracles. — No ancient source attributes any deed, power, or labor to Phalias individually. He is purely a genealogical name in a list of 50 sons. Any 'powers' are modern invention conflating him with Heracles.

All 50 sons of Heracles and the Thespiades went to Sardinia to found a colony. — Ancient sources are explicit that only forty (or a portion) went to Sardinia with Iolaus; seven remained in Thespiae and three were sent to Thebes per Apollodorus 2.7.8. Phalias' individual destination is unattested.

The daughters of Thespius willingly competed to sleep with Heracles in a romantic or consensual episode. — Ancient sources frame the episode as Thespius' arrangement — he 'offered' or 'sent' daughters, sometimes plying Heracles with wine or deceiving him. The daughters' consent or willingness is never described; the narrative is about hospitality exchange and lineage, not romance. Modern sanitized retellings often omit the power imbalance.

Phalias has a distinct appearance, symbol, or cult statue surviving from antiquity. — No iconography, cult site, or visual attribute is attested for Phalias. LIMC and Beazley Archive have no entry for him. Any image labeled 'Phalias' online is mislabeled or generic.
