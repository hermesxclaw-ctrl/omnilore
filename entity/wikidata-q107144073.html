The Approach

In the shadow of Mount Cithaeron, where a lion terrorized the cattle of Thespiae, a king's desperate plan would entwine the fates of fifty princesses with the greatest hero of Greece. Among them was Eurytele, a name whispered in genealogies, a mother of a hero, yet a woman whose own story remains a single line in the vast tapestry of myth.

Content advisory. This myth involves deceptive sexual encounters and non-consensual acts; reader discretion is advised.
Identity

Eurytele

Pronunciation yoo-RIT-uh-lee (English approximation); in Classical Greek: eu-ry-te-lē

Etymology

The name Eurytele (Εὐρυτέλη) is derived from Greek εὐρύς (eurys, 'wide, broad') and τέλος (telos, 'end, purpose, fulfillment'), possibly meaning 'wide-reaching purpose' or 'broad accomplishment'.

Cultural Ownership

Ancient Greek mythology, specifically the region of Thespiae in Boeotia

Disputed Identifications

There is no known dispute about her identity; she is one of the fifty daughters of Thespius, but the specific mother of each daughter is not individually recorded.

Description

Appearance

No ancient source describes Eurytele's physical appearance. As a daughter of Thespius, she was likely a princess of Thespiae, but no specific attributes are recorded. In artistic depictions of the daughters of Thespius, they are typically shown as young Greek women, but no distinct iconography for Eurytele exists.

Symbols & Iconography

No symbols or iconography are specifically associated with Eurytele. In general, the daughters of Thespius may be represented in group scenes with Heracles, but individual identification is rare.

Behaviour & Personality

Eurytele's personality is not detailed in any surviving source. She is one of the fifty sisters who bore children to Heracles, and in one version, one sister (not Eurytele) refused and was punished. Eurytele's role is passive; she is a recipient of Heracles' advances.

Domains & Habitats

Eurytele lived in Thespiae, a city in Boeotia, central Greece. She was a princess, daughter of King Thespius. No other domains or habitats are recorded.

Version Differences

The main differences in the myth concern the duration and circumstances of Heracles' union with the daughters. Apollodorus says he slept with all fifty in one night or over fifty nights (depending on interpretation). Diodorus says he slept with them all in one night except one. Athenaeus mentions a version where he slept with them over seven nights, with seven each night. Tzetzes records a similar version. Eurytele's specific role does not change; she is always one of the daughters who bore a son to Heracles.

Stories & Myths

The Daughters of Thespius and Heracles

Source Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.4.10; Diodorus Siculus, Bibliotheca Historica 4.29.3; Athenaeus, Deipnosophistae 13.556fCulture Ancient GreekPeriod Archaic/Classical mythology, recorded in later sources

Summary

King Thespius of Thespiae, wanting his daughters to bear children by the great hero Heracles, hosted him while he hunted the Cithaeronian lion. Over the course of the hunt, Heracles slept with all fifty daughters, believing each time he was with the same woman. Eurytele was one of these daughters, and she bore a son named Leucippus.

Consequences

Eurytele gave birth to Leucippus, who became one of the many sons of Heracles. These sons, the Heracleidae, later played a significant role in Greek mythology and history.

Variants

In one version (Diodorus), Heracles slept with all the daughters in a single night except one who refused; that sister was punished by being forced to remain a virgin priestess. Athenaeus and Tzetzes record a version where the unions took place over seven nights, with seven daughters each night. Apollodorus's text is ambiguous, possibly meaning fifty nights.

Deception and sexual exploitation

Source Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.4.10; Diodorus 4.29.3

Description

Thespius deliberately made Heracles drunk and arranged for him to sleep with his daughters, leading to fifty pregnancies. The daughters were likely not consulted, and Heracles was unaware he was with different women each time. This raises issues of consent and exploitation, though the ancient context viewed it as a heroic lineage.

Scholarly Context

Classicists note that the myth serves to provide Heracles with many sons and to honor the Thespian nobility. The sexual encounters are not romanticized in the sources but are a matter of genealogical necessity.

Contradictions & Disputes

Number of nights Heracles spent with the daughters

Position A

Apollodorus: He slept with all fifty in one night (or over fifty nights, text ambiguous).

Position A — evidence

Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.4.10: 'And he had intercourse with them all in one night, or in fifty nights.'

Position B

Athenaeus and Tzetzes: He slept with them over seven nights, seven each night.

Position B — evidence

Athenaeus, Deipnosophistae 13.556f; Tzetzes, Chiliades 2.221-222.

Scholarly Consensus

There is no consensus; scholars note the discrepancy but do not resolve it. The single-night version is more common in modern retellings.

The sister who refused

Position A

Diodorus: One daughter refused and was punished by being forced to remain a virgin priestess.

Position A — evidence

Diodorus Siculus, Bibliotheca Historica 4.29.3.

Position B

Apollodorus and others do not mention this refusal.

Position B — evidence

Absence in other sources.

Scholarly Consensus

This is a variant unique to Diodorus; the name of the refusing sister is not given. It is not universally accepted as part of the myth.
