Who It Is

Along the Niger River at dusk, Songhay elders warn children not to linger where the water turns dark and still. That is where the Zin live — invisible owners of the river, white as river foam, who can lend a fisherman fortune, steal a bride, or pull a child under in a single silent eddy. They are not mermaids, not quite jinn, and they have been bargained with on the banks of the Niger for centuries.

Ambivalent and contractual. Zin are described as owners/masters (koy) of water sources — they control fish, water availability, and fertility. They can be benevolent if properly respected with offerings and taboos, granting abundant fish, children, or protection from drowning; and malevolent if offended, causing illness, infertility, madness, drought, or drowning. They are capricious, easily offended by noise, pollution, or violation of taboos (washing with soap at sacred pools, sexual activity near water). They communicate through possession of human hosts (often women) and through dreams.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Songhay-Zarma (Songhai) term zin (plural ziney). Etymology disputed. Most scholars note formal and functional convergence with Arabic jinn (al-jinn), suggesting borrowing and re-semanticization after Islamization of the Niger bend from the 11th century onward, while retaining pre-Islamic Niger River

Pronunciation — Songhay-Zarma: /zin/ IPA [zĩː] or [zin]; French ethnographic literature often writes 'zin' or 'zine'; English approximation 'zeen'. Plural ziney pronounced [ziːnej].

Aliases — Zin, Zina, Ziney (plural), Tchirey Zin (variant orthography), Water Zin

Ownership — Primarily Songhay (Songhai), Zarma (Djerma/Zarma), and Dendi peoples of the Niger River valley in present-day Niger, Mali, Burkina Faso, Benin and northern Nigeria. Also documented

Powers
Invisibility and shape-shifting

Ability to remain unseen or to appear as human, white-clad figure, whirlwind, or aquatic animal (fish, serpent). Attested in all primary sources.

Limit: Cannot be seen without their consent; mediums claim to see them only when possessed. Some narratives say they cast no shadow or leave wet footprints.

Possession (spirit mount)

Enter and control human bodies, causing shaking, glossolalia, singing in spirit language, and conveying messages or demands. Central to yenendi and holey horey ceremonies.

Limit: Requires vulnerable or chosen host; can be expelled by zima (spirit priest) through drumming, incense, and negotiation. Not all zin possess; some only afflict from distance.

Control of water and aquatic life

As owners of water, they regulate river levels, well water, rainfall locally, and abundance of fish. Fishermen must ask permission and offer first catch.

Limit: Power is territorial — a zin of one pool cannot command another's water. Offended zin withhold fish or dry the well.

Infliction of illness and misfortune

Cause fever, infertility, paralysis, madness (hauka), skin lesions, and especially drowning. Illness attributed to zin is diagnosed by diviners.

Limit: Affliction is reversible through offerings, sacrifice (white sheep, milk), or ritual washing. Biomedical treatment alone considered insufficient for zin-caused illness.

Granting fertility, fortune, and knowledge

If propitiated, zin can grant children to barren women, success in fishing or trade, and esoteric knowledge to sohanci.

Limit: Gifts require ongoing reciprocal obligations and taboos (e.g., not eating certain fish, sexual abstinence on certain days). Breaking contract revokes gift and brings worse affliction.

Abduction and spirit marriage

Ability to lure or abduct humans, especially women and children near water, to live in underwater villages as spouses or servants. Returnees report time distortion.

Limit: Abductees can be retrieved by powerful sohanci if quickly sought; prolonged stay makes return difficult. Iron and Quranic verses said to repel abduction in Islamized versions.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Attested as primarily invisible. When visible, described consistently across Songhay-Zarma sources as tall, pale or white-skinned (harey - white), with long straight hair, sometimes beautiful human form, sometimes as whirlwind, white foam, or large fish/serpent. Rouch (1960) records informants describing zin as 'white people of the water' who live in palaces under the river. Stoller (1987) notes they are said to appear at night or at midday near

Symbols — Core symbols: white (color of water spirits and purity/danger), still deep water, river stones, white calabashes, milk and millet porridge offerings, white cloth tied at river shrines. Among sorko (fisher) lineages, the harakoy (river master) shrine often includes stones said to be zin dwellings. No ancient inscriptional iconography; archaeological evidence is indirect (river shrine deposits). Modern museum representation uses photographs of Nige

Domains — Strictly aquatic: Niger River main channel, deep pools (gundu), tributaries, permanent lakes (e.g., Lake Faguibine region), wells, springs, and marshes in the Sahel. Each significant water source is said to have its own zin family. Cosmology: Zin inhabit a parallel underwater world mirroring human villages — with houses, markets, livestock — invisible to humans. Accounts I
The Fisherman and the Zin of the Deep Pool (Sorko origin narrative)Jean Rouch, La religion et la magie Songhay (1960) and Olivier de Sardan (1982) sorko traditions

Pre-colonial oral tradition, recorded 1950s-1960s

A sorko ancestor fishes at a deep gundu pool without offering. His net comes up empty for days. A white figure rises at noon and demands the first fish and a bowl of milk. After the fisherman complies, his nets overflow, but he is warned never to fish on Fridays or to bring a menstruating woman to the boat. When his son violates the taboo, the pool dries and the son sickens until restitution is made. Establishes covenant between sorko lineage and territorial zin; explains taboos governing fishing and ritual calendar; legitimizes sorko authority as mediators with water spirits.

The Girl Taken as Zin BridePaul Stoller, In Sorcery's Shadow (1987) pp. 112-118; Rouch field notes

Oral narrative contemporary to 1980s fieldwork, with older roots

A beautiful girl goes to fetch water at dusk at a well known to house zin. She is possessed, speaks in a strange voice, and disappears for three days. She is found sitting by the well, claiming she has been living in a shining underwater town where a handsome zin man wants to marry her and has given her fine cloth. Her family calls a zima who negotiates: the zin will release her if they sacrifice a white ram and never send her to that well again. She recovers but is said to remain vulnerable to possession. Explains etiology of possession and taboo around dusk water collection; reinforces ritual authority of zima; narrative used to warn girls about liminal water times.

The Zin Who Withheld the WellOlivier de Sardan, Concepts et conceptions songhay-zarma (1982) and Stoller, The Taste of Ethnographic Things (1989)

Oral tradition recorded 1970s-1980s; reflects Sahelian drought context 1968-1974

During a dry season, a village well fails despite being deep. Divination reveals the well's zin is angry because villagers washed soiled clothes and poured blood near its mouth. The zin demands the well be cleaned, encircled with white cloth, and given millet and milk, and that a blacksmith not forge near it. After the rite, water returns but is said to taste different, marking zin's continued presence. Encodes ecological management and hygiene rules for scarce Sahelian water; demonstrates zin as enforcers of environmental taboos; cited by scholars as example of spiritual resource governance.

Accounts II
Dongo and the Zin — Thunder Chases the Water SpiritsRouch (1960) Dongo cycle; Stoller (1987) sohanci lore

Mythic past (cosmological narrative), recorded mid-20th century

Dongo, the thunder god and son of the sky, pursues the zin who have stolen cattle and hidden them under the river. Dongo hurls lightning into the water, boiling it and forcing the zin to release the cattle. The zin flee to deeper pools and henceforth fear thunder. This explains why zin are said to be quiet during storms and why thunder is invoked to expel water possession. Situates zin within broader holey pantheon hierarchy; provides ritual logic for using loud noise and iron (associated with Dongo) to counter zin affliction; links water and sky domains.

The Sohanci Who Wrestled a Zin for PowerStoller and Olkes (1987) apprenticeship narrative of Adamu Jenitongo; Rouch on sohance initiation

Initiatory biography, late 19th to 20th century lineage memory

A young sohanci candidate is sent by his master to spend a night alone at a feared pool. A zin appears as a whirlwind and wrestles him. If the candidate does not flee and can seize the zin's cap or staff, the zin is bound to serve him, teaching him medicines and how to command water. If he flees, he goes mad. The successful sohanci returns with a river stone that becomes his power object, but must thereafter observe strict taboos and feed his zin ally regularly. Explains source of sohanci magical power and river stones as power objects; establishes ambivalent master-servant reversal; illustrates cost of esoteric knowledge (lifelong obligations, social isolation).

The White Mother of the River (comparative Mami Wata/Zin overlap narrative)Drewal (2008) comparative commentary; Rouch short film 'Mammy Water' (1956) narration

Mid-20th century syncretic narrative

In a market town where river trade meets coastal traders, a story circulates of a pale woman with long hair who sits on a rock combing her hair with a golden comb, offering wealth to those who will not reveal her. Some call her Mami Wata, others call her a zin. An informant in Rouch's film insists 'our zin do not have fish tails — that is the white people's Mami Wata', asserting distinction while acknowledging convergence in trader stories. Documents contemporary syncretism and boundary maintenance between inland Zin and coastal Mami Wata complexes; shows how trade, migration, and media spread water spirit imagery; used by scholars to argue against collapsing distinct traditions.

Zin are the same as the biblical Zin Desert (Midbar Zin). — Unrelated homonym. Biblical Zin (Hebrew Ṣin, Numbers 13:21) is a wilderness in the Negev/Sinai region, a Semitic toponym with no linguistic or cultural link to Songhay-Zarma zin water spirits. Conflation arises solely from identical Roman-letter spelling.

Zin are just African mermaids (Mami Wata). — Zin are inland Niger River spirits, traditionally invisible or white human-like, without fish tails, tied to specific pools/wells and lineage covenants. Mami Wata is a distinct, largely coastal/urban complex with mermaid iconography, European-derived imagery, and wealth symbolism. Scholars (Drewal, Frank) document difference, though recent popular culture merges them.

Zin are the same as the Haitian Vodou zin (three-legged cauldron). — Haitian Vodou zin is a ritual iron cauldron (Creole zin) used in kanzo initiation to hold sacred liquids; it is an object, not a spirit, and its name derives from different etymology. No cosmological link to West African water spirits despite shared West African heritage in Vodou.

Zin belief is extinct folklore or mere superstition. — Zin observances remain a living tradition among Songhay-Zarma riverine communities, practiced alongside Islam, with ongoing possession ceremonies, shrine maintenance, and ecological taboos documented into the 2010s. Framing as 'extinct myth' erases living practitioners.
