The Approach

In the bustling harbors of Taiwan and Fujian, where the sea meets the sky, sailors once offered incense to five fearsome kings—former generals, poets, and even a legendary emperor—each with a story of water, death, and divine power. These are the Shuixian Zunwang, the Honorable Water Immortal Kings, guardians of ships and sailors, whose cult rose from the ashes of a dynasty and the waves of the Qiantang Bore.

Content advisory. Contains descriptions of suicide, assassination, and violent death in mythological contexts.

Etymology

The name Shuǐxiān Zūnwáng (水仙尊王) combines shuǐ (水, 'water'), xiān (仙, 'immortal' or 'transcendent'), zūn (尊, 'honorable' or 'venerable'), and wáng (王, 'king'). Thus, it translates as 'Honorable Water Immortal Kings'.

Cultural Ownership

Chinese folk religion, particularly in Fujian and Taiwan, with roots in the worship of individual water deities.

Disputed Identifications

The fifth member of the quintet is disputed: either 'King Ao' (Ao Wang) or Lu Ban. Also, some interpretations consider 'Zunwang' to refer to a single deity, usually Yu the Great.

Description

Appearance

As a group, they are typically depicted as five bearded officials or warriors in imperial robes, each holding symbols of their individual legends. Yu the Great is often shown with a plow or measuring tool. Wu Zixu may hold a sword or be associated with waves. Xiang Yu is depicted with a halberd and armor. Qu Yuan is shown as a poet with a scroll or in scholar's robes. The fifth, whether Ao or Lu Ban, is depicted either as a strong warrior or as a craftsman with tools.

Symbols & Iconography

Common symbols include water waves, ships, dragons, and the characters for 'water' (水) and 'king' (王). In temples, they are often placed near the entrance or in a dedicated hall.

Behaviour & Personality

As deities, they are believed to protect ships and sailors, ensuring safe passage. They are also seen as patrons of water-related activities and as arbiters of justice, reflecting their historical roles as heroes who fought against injustice.

Domains & Habitats

They reside in the watery realms, particularly the seas and rivers. Their temples are often located in coastal areas, ports, and along rivers. They are particularly venerated in Fujian and Taiwan.

Version Differences

The fifth member varies between Ao and Lu Ban. Additionally, some traditions may emphasize one deity over others, and the iconography may vary regionally.

The nature of their control varies: some are more about water management, others about water-related death.

Stories & Myths

Yu the Great and the Flood

Source Chinese mythology, 'Shujing' (Book of Documents)Culture ChinesePeriod Legendary, c. 2000 BCE (associated with Erlitou culture)

Summary

Yu the Great tamed the Great Flood by dredging rivers and channeling water, becoming the first emperor of the Xia dynasty. He is credited with flood control and water management.

Participants

Yu the Great

Gun (his father)

The people of China

Consequences

Yu became a legendary hero and a model ruler, and his efforts brought order to the land.

Variants

Some versions say Yu was assisted by divine creatures, others emphasize his tireless work.

Wu Zixu's Revenge and Death

Source Sima Qian's 'Records of the Grand Historian'Culture ChinesePeriod Spring and Autumn period (c. 6th-5th century BCE)

Summary

Wu Zixu, a Chu noble, fled to Wu after his father and brother were killed by the Chu king. He helped Prince Ji Guang assassinate the king and become King Helü. He then led Wu's invasion of Chu, desecrating the king's corpse. Later, under King Fuchai, he was forced to commit suicide, and his body was thrown into the Qiantang River. His spirit became the God of the Waves, controlling the tidal bore.

Participants

Wu Zixu

King Helü

King Fuchai

King Ping of Chu

Consequences

Wu Zixu's death led to his deification as a water god, and he is worshipped as a protector of fishermen and sailors.

Variants

Some accounts say he was beheaded, others say he was thrown into the river in a leather bag.

Xiang Yu's Last Stand

Source Sima Qian's 'Records of the Grand Historian'Culture ChinesePeriod Chu-Han Contention (c. 206-202 BCE)

Summary

Xiang Yu, the Hegemon-King of Western Chu, was defeated by Liu Bang's forces. He retreated to the Wu River, where he refused to cross to safety. He committed suicide, and his body was torn apart by soldiers. His body reportedly remained standing in the river, and he became a water deity.

Participants

Xiang Yu

Liu Bang

Han soldiers

Consequences

Xiang Yu's tragic death made him a symbol of heroism and a local water deity.

Variants

Some versions say he was killed in battle, others say he killed himself.

Qu Yuan's Suicide

Source Sima Qian's 'Records of the Grand Historian'Culture ChinesePeriod Warring States period (c. 3rd century BCE)

Summary

Qu Yuan, a poet and minister of Chu, was slandered and exiled. In despair, he drowned himself in the Miluo River, holding a rock. His death is commemorated during the Dragon Boat Festival.

Participants

Qu Yuan

King Huai of Chu

Corrupt officials

Consequences

Qu Yuan became a symbol of patriotism and is honored annually. Some connect the Dragon Boat Festival to his memory, though this is debated.

Variants

Some believe the festival predates Qu Yuan and was originally associated with Wu Zixu.

Ao's Conquest and Death

Source Chinese mythology, 'Bamboo Annals'Culture ChinesePeriod Xia dynasty (legendary)

Summary

Ao (or Jiao), the strong son of Han Zhuo, conquered the state of Ge for his father. He is credited with inventing ships, which he could sail over land and water. He was killed by King Shaokang, who restored the Xia dynasty.

Participants

Ao

Han Zhuo

King Shaokang

Consequences

Ao's death avenged the usurpation of the Xia throne, and his invention of ships contributed to his deification as a water deity.

Variants

Some sources say he was killed in battle, others say he was assassinated.

Lu Ban's Inventions

Source Various Chinese texts, e.g., 'Mozi'Culture ChinesePeriod Spring and Autumn period (c. 5th century BCE)

Summary

Lu Ban was a master craftsman who invented or improved many tools, including the saw, square, planer, drill, shovel, and ink marker. He is revered as the god of carpentry and masonry.

Participants

Lu Ban

Consequences

Lu Ban became a legendary figure and a patron deity of craftsmen. His association with water is less direct, but he built boats and other watercraft.

Variants

Some stories depict him as a magician or a divine being.

Suicide

Source Historical records (Shiji)

Description

Wu Zixu, Xiang Yu, and Qu Yuan all committed suicide. Wu Zixu was forced to do so, Xiang Yu chose death over capture, and Qu Yuan drowned himself in despair.

Scholarly Context

Suicide in ancient China was often seen as a noble act of protest or honor.

Political Violence

Source Historical records (Shiji)

Description

Wu Zixu's revenge involved exhuming and whipping the corpse of the Chu king. Xiang Yu's death involved his body being torn apart by soldiers.

Scholarly Context

Such acts were part of the brutal politics of the time.

Assassination

Source Historical records (Shiji)

Description

Wu Zixu helped Prince Ji Guang assassinate King Liao of Wu to put Helü on the throne.

Scholarly Context

Assassination was a common means of political change in ancient China.

Contradictions & Disputes

Fifth Member

Position A

The fifth member is Ao (or Jiao), a mythical strongman and inventor of ships.

Position A — evidence

Ao's story is ancient and connected to ships.

Position B

The fifth member is Lu Ban, the historical craftsman.

Position B — evidence

Lu Ban is more famous and widely worshipped.

Scholarly Consensus

No clear consensus; both are venerated in different regions.

Origin of the Quintet

Position A

The quintet arose from a misunderstanding of Wu Zixu's title 'King Wu' (伍王) as 'Five Kings'.

Position A — evidence

The similarity of the characters 伍 and 五.

Position B

The five deities were deliberately grouped together due to their water associations.

Position B — evidence

The deities were already worshipped individually before being grouped.

Scholarly Consensus

The misunderstanding theory is plausible but not proven.

Dragon Boat Festival

Position A

The festival commemorates Qu Yuan's suicide.

Position A — evidence

Popular tradition and local legends.

Position B

The festival originally commemorated Wu Zixu.

Position B — evidence

Some historical texts associate the festival with Wu Zixu.

Scholarly Consensus

The festival likely predates both, but the association with Qu Yuan is more widespread.
