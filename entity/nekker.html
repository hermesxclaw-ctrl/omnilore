Who It Is

In the misty waters of the Low Countries, a shape shifts beneath the surface—sometimes a handsome man, sometimes a horse, sometimes a shadow. The nekker, a water spirit of Belgian and Dutch folklore, is not a single creature but a shapeshifter of many faces, luring the unwary to a watery doom or teaching a chosen musician the secrets of enchanting music. To know the nekker is to understand the deep, ambivalent relationship between the people of the lowlands and the waters that both sustain and threaten them.

The nekker is a complex being. It is often malevolent, luring women and children to drown, but it can also be benevolent, teaching music to those who approach it correctly. It is known for its enchanting music, which can mesmerize listeners. It is also said to be an omen of drowning, screaming at spots where fatalities will occur. It can be capricious and dangerous, but also lonely and longing for salvation, as seen in later Romantic folklore.

Powers
Shapeshifting

The nekker can change its form to appear as a human, animal, or object. It can appear as a handsome man, a horse, a dog, a cat, a bull, or even floating treasure.

Limit: The shapeshifting may be limited by certain conditions, such as being called by name, which can cause its death. Some forms may be vulnerable to steel.

Enchanting Music

The nekker plays music so beautiful that it lures listeners to the water, leading to their drowning. It can also teach humans to play music so skillfully that trees dance and waterfalls stop.

Limit: The music is most dangerous to women, children, pregnant women, and unbaptised children. It may be resisted by calling the nekker's name or throwing steel into the water.

Drowning

The nekker can directly drag people into water, causing their death. It also acts as an omen, screaming at spots where drownings will occur.

Limit: Victims can protect themselves by throwing steel into the water.

Curses and Bargains

The nekker can grant wishes or provide fish in exchange for promises, often involving the sacrifice of a person or soul. It may also be bribed with offerings to teach music.

Limit: Bargains are dangerous and often lead to tragedy. The nekker can be defeated by calling its name.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The nekker is a shapeshifter with no fixed true form. It can appear as a handsome man, often playing violin or other instruments, sometimes elegantly dressed, sometimes naked. It may also manifest as a 'brook horse' (Bäckahästen), a horse that lures people to ride it and then plunges into the water. Other animal forms include dogs, cats, bulls, and even three-legged animals. It can also appear as floating objects or treasure. In some accounts, it

Symbols — The nekker is associated with water, particularly lakes, streams, and wells. It is often depicted with a musical instrument, especially the violin. The 'nixie rose' (water lily) is a floral symbol associated with the creature in Scandinavia. In art, it is often depicted as a fair, naked youth, though this is a Romantic-era idealization.

Domains — The nekker inhabits freshwater bodies: lakes, rivers, streams, wells, and waterfalls. It is particularly active on Midsummer's Night, Christmas Eve, and Thursdays. It requires regular contact with water and grows despondent without it.

Accounts I
The Nekker and the Fisherman's DaughterTale from the forest of Tiveden, Sweden (but analogous to Low Countries' stories)

Traditional folklore

A poor fisherman promises his beautiful daughter to the nixie in exchange for great catches of fish. On her eighteenth birthday, the nixie comes to claim her, but the girl stabs herself to death rather than go with him. Her blood stains the water lilies red. The water lilies in the lake are red from that time on, symbolizing the girl's sacrifice and the nixie's cruelty.

Accounts II
The Nixie's MusicVarious Scandinavian and German folktales

Traditional folklore

A musician approaches the nixie (or Fossegrim) and offers a sacrifice (three drops of blood, a black animal, brännvin, or snus) to learn enchanting music. The nixie teaches him to play so well that trees dance and waterfalls stop. The musician gains extraordinary skill, but the encounter is dangerous and may require a sacrifice.

The Boy and the NäckPoem by E. J. Stagnelius (19th century)

Romantic era

A little boy pities the nixie's fate, saying he will never be a 'child of God'. The nixie is moved to tears and never plays again in the silvery brook. The boy saves his own life by showing compassion. The nixie's loneliness is highlighted; the boy's empathy prevents his drowning.

The nixie is always a beautiful woman. — In Germanic folklore, the nixie is often male. The female Nixe is a German variant. In Scandinavian and Dutch folklore, the spirit is typically male.

The nixie is purely evil. — The nixie can be benevolent, teaching music or helping fishermen, and some stories show it as lonely and seeking salvation.

The nixie is the same as a mermaid. — While the German Nixe is a mermaid-like creature, the male nix is not. The nix is a shapeshifter, often appearing as a man or horse.

The nekker in The Witcher is based on the folklore. — The 'nekker' in The Witcher series is a small, goblin-like creature, not a water spirit. It is a name-only reference.
