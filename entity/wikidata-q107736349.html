Who It Is

Nothing called 'stainless' stays untouched. In a Sheffield laboratory in 1913 a discarded steel sample refused to rust; fifty years earlier on a battlefield in Virginia a white flag refused to stay clean, stained by blood and wind. Between the metallurgy and the mythology, 'stainless' has come to mean both a hard, chromium-bright alloy and an impossible moral claim — to be without stain.

Not applicable as personality for material/corporate/flag referents. Cultural behavior of the term: 'stainless' functions as an absolute claim that invites contest. Metallurgically, stainless steels do stain, pit, and corrode under chloride, acid, or oxygen-depleted conditions — behavior depends on grade, finish, and environment. Austenitic grades (304, 316) are non-magnetic (or weakly magnetic when cold-worked), ductile, and highly formable; martensitic grades (410, 420) are hardenable and magnetic, used for cutlery. Powers
Corrosion resistance via passive chromium oxide layer

Chromium ≥10.5% forms an adherent, transparent Cr2O3 layer that self-repairs in presence of oxygen, dramatically slowing rust and acid attack compared to carbon steel. Not absolute immunity.

Limit: Fails under chloride pitting (e.g., 304 in seawater), crevice corrosion, sensitization (chromium carbide precipitation at 450-850°C depleting grain boundaries), and reducing acids (hydrochloric). Requires oxygen; scratches repassivate only if oxygen available. 304 resists ~200 ppm chloride at room temperature; 316 (with 2-3% Mo) resists ~1000 ppm.

Mechanical strength, hardness, and formability (grade-dependent)

Austenitic grades: high ductility, work-hardening, cryogenic toughness, non-hardenable by heat treatment. Martensitic grades: hardenable to 50+ HRC via quench and temper for cutting edges. Duplex: high yield strength (~450 MPa) with good toughness.

Limit: Austenitic is relatively soft in annealed state (~150-200 HV) and galls; martensitic has lower corrosion resistance and brittleness if not tempered; ferritic has poor weldability in thick sections.

Hygienic and biocompatible surface

Non-porous, easily electropolished, resistant to sterilization (autoclave, gamma, chemical), and biocompatible (316L, 316LVM for implants). Basis for surgical instruments, food processing, and implant standards (ASTM F138, ISO 5832-1).

Limit: Nickel leaching can cause allergic reaction (nickel allergy ~10-15% population); not inherently antimicrobial — biofilms can form if not cleaned.

Aesthetic reflectivity and architectural durability

High specular reflectance, ability to take mirror polish, and retention of finish without painting or coating. Used for monumental sculpture and curtain walls with 50+ year maintenance intervals.

Limit: Fingerprints and surface contamination visible on mirror finishes; requires periodic cleaning in urban atmospheres to avoid tea staining (surface discoloration). High initial cost vs carbon steel.

Flag as signaling and political symbol (Stainless Banner)

As national flag, functioned to identify Confederate government, military units, and vessels, and to assert sovereignty and ideological claims (states' rights, slavery). White field intended to be distinctive at sea and on battlefield when limp.

Limit: White field caused confusion with flag of truce when windless; led to addition of red bar in 1865 Third National Flag. As symbol, now widely recognized as emblem of slavery and white supremacy, restricted in public display contexts.

Game engine physics and destruction simulation (Stainless Games)

Proprietary physics and vehicle deformation systems developed for Carmageddon series, noted for real-time car crushing, environmental destruction, and dark humor. Studio also developed proprietary card-game AI and network code for Magic: The Gathering titles.

Limit: Studio size (~30-50 staff) limits AAA scope; physics systems are adaptation-specific, not universal powers.

Accounts I
The Rustless Sample That Would Not Etch — Harry Brearley at Brown-Firth, SheffieldSheffield City Archives, Brearley notebooks; Harry Brearley, 'Knotted String' (1941); Harold M. Cobb, 'The History of Stainless Steel' (ASM International, 2010), Chapter 2

1913-1915

On 13 August 1913, metallurgist Harry Brearley (1871-1948), employed by Brown-Firth Research Laboratories to solve erosion in rifle barrels, cast experimental heat 1008 with 12.8% chromium. The sample resisted nitric acid etching and was set aside. Weeks later Brearley noticed it had not rusted in the scrap pile while other steels had. He recognized the corrosion resistance, had cutlery made by R.F. Mosley, and with Ernest Stuart coined 'stainless steel' after 'rustless steel' was deemed unappealing for cutlery marketing. Brearley left Brown-Firth in 1915 after patent disputes; Firth's patent (1915) and Brearley's US patent (1916) followed. Launched commercial stainless cutlery (1914-1915), then global alloy family. Sheffield became 'Steel City' for stainless. Brearley's martensitic 12% Cr steel became Type 410; Hatfield's later austenitic 18/8 became Type 304, the dominant grade today.

Nirosta and V2A — Krupp's Austenitic Stainless Steel in EssenBenno Strauss and Eduard Maurer, German Patent 304,126 (filed 18 Oct 1912); Krupp Archives, Essen; Cobb (2010) Chapter 3; ThyssenKrupp historical publications

1912-1927

At Krupp in Essen, engineers Benno Strauss and Eduard Maurer, seeking corrosion-resistant alloys for chemical and naval use, systematically tested chromium-nickel steels. On 18 October 1912 they patented austenitic steel with ~18% Cr, ~8% Ni (later called V2A - Versuchsschmelze 2 Austenit, and commercialized as Nirosta - Nicht Rostender Stahl). Unlike Brearley's hardenable martensitic steel, this was non-magnetic, highly ductile, and acid-resistant, ideal for chemical vessels. Krupp kept it as trade secret until post-WWI, then licensed globally. Created the 18/8 austenitic family (modern 304/316) which now represents ~70% of stainless production. Nirosta became the generic term in continental Europe. Enabled chemical, food, and architectural applications impossible with martensitic grades.

Adoption of the Stainless Banner — The Second Confederate National FlagJournal of the Confederate Congress, 1 May 1863; Savannah Morning News editorials by William T. Thompson, 23 April and 4 May 1863; John M. Coski, 'The Confederate Battle Flag' (Harvard UP, 2005), Chapter 2

1863-05-01 to 1865-03-04

After confusion between the first national flag ('Stars and Bars') and the US Stars and Stripes on the battlefield, the Confederate Congress adopted a new national flag on 1 May 1863: a white field with the Army of Northern Virginia battle flag as canton. Editor William T. Thompson, credited with the design, wrote that the white field signified 'the supremacy of the white man' and that the battle flag's blue cross represented the Southern Cross. The flag was first hoisted over Richmond on 2 May 1863 and draped over Stonewall Jackson's coffin. At sea and when limp, it resembled a flag of truce, causing accidental surrenders. Served as national flag until 4 March 1865, when a vertical red bar was added to create the Third National Flag ('Blood-Stained Banner') to avoid truce confusion. Surviving wartime examples are rare (estimated <30). Modern vexillology treats it as distinct from the battle flag, though popular culture conflates them.

Accounts II
A Crown of Nirosta — The Chrysler Building's Stainless Steel SpireNYC Landmarks Preservation Commission LP-0834 (1978); National Park Service, 'Chrysler Building' (n.d.); Krupp Nirosta product brochure 1930; Cobb (2010) Chapter 7

1929-1930

Architect William Van Alen, designing the Chrysler Building (405 Lexington Avenue, Manhattan) to be the world's tallest, specified Krupp's Nirosta austenitic stainless steel for the seven terraced arches of the crown, spire, gargoyles, and automobile-ornament friezes. Fabricated by the German company Krupp and installed September 1929 - May 1930, the 56-meter spire was secretly assembled inside the shaft and raised in 90 minutes on 23 October 1929. It remains the first large-scale architectural use of stainless steel in the United States and has required only periodic washing in nearly a century, demonstrating long-term atmospheric corrosion resistance. Proved stainless steel's architectural viability and aesthetic potential, launching its use in the Empire State Building spire, Gateway Arch (1965), and countless curtain walls. Established 18/8 steel as the standard for exterior cladding.

Carmageddon and the BBFC Ban — Stainless Games' Controversial DebutBritish Board of Film Classification decision 1997; SCi/Eidos press releases 1997; Edge Magazine review No. 47 (1997); Companies House filings for Stainless Games Ltd

1997-1998

Stainless Games, founded on the Isle of Wight in 1994 by Patrick Buckland and Neil Barnden (ex-Atari, ex-Syzygy), developed 'Carmageddon' (published by SCi/Eidos, June 1997) — a vehicular combat game where points were awarded for running over pedestrians (zombies in censored German/UK versions) and destroying opponents. The BBFC initially refused classification in the UK, requiring the replacement of humans with zombies and green blood to pass with an 18 certificate. Despite or because of controversy, it sold over 2 million copies, won PC Gamer Game of the Year, and spawned sequels. The studio's physics engine, notable for real-time deformation, became its signature. Established Stainless Games as a physics-driven studio; led to Carmageddon II (1998), Carmageddon: Reincarnation (2015, Kickstarter-funded), and Carmageddon: Max Damage (2016). Demonstrated UK indie viability and the regulatory limits of video game violence in the 1990s.

From Cutlery to Surgical Steel — Stainless in the Operating Theatre and the KitchenBSSA fact sheets; ASTM F138-19 Standard Specification for Wrought 18Cr-14Ni-2.5Mo Stainless Steel Bar and Wire for Surgical Implants; British Medical Journal archives 1930s-1940s

1924-1945 (interwar to WWII)

After Hatfield's 18/8 steel (1924, later Type 304) and the molybdenum-bearing 316 (1929, marine grade), stainless steel rapidly replaced carbon steel and silver in cutlery, cookware, and surgical instruments. By 1935 Sheffield cutlers produced stainless table knives en masse; by WWII, US Army field hospitals adopted stainless surgical instruments and sterilization trays because they withstood autoclaving and did not contaminate wounds with rust. Post-war, 316L (low carbon, 1940s) and 316LVM (vacuum-melted) became the standard for bone plates, screws, and later stents, governed by ASTM and ISO standards. Made modern sterile surgery and food hygiene possible at scale; stainless now accounts for >90% of surgical instruments and food-processing equipment. Created the distinction between 'cutlery grade' (410, 420) and 'implant grade' (316LVM).

Stainless steel is a single metal and never rusts or needs maintenance. — Stainless steel is a family of >150 grades with different compositions and corrosion resistance. All grades can rust, pit, or stain if wrong grade is used, if surface is contaminated with carbon steel particles, or if not cleaned (tea staining). 304 is unsuitable for marine environments; 316 or duplex is required. Regular washing to remove chlorides is necessary.

The Stainless Banner is the same as the Confederate battle flag (the blue cross on red). — The Stainless Banner is the second national flag of the Confederacy (May 1863-March 1865): a white field with the battle flag as a canton (upper hoist). The battle flag alone was never the national flag (except as canton). The Stainless Banner was replaced because its white field resembled a flag of truce when limp, leading to the third national flag with a red vertical bar.

Stainless Games is an American studio that made Empire Earth, or is the same as Stainless Steel Studios. — Stainless Games is a British studio (Isle of Wight, founded 1994) known for Carmageddon and Magic: The Gathering adaptations. Stainless Steel Studios was a separate American studio (Cambridge, MA, 1998-2005) that made Empire Earth and Empires: Dawn of the Modern World, then closed. The names are coincidentally similar.

'Stainless' as an adjective means morally perfect, and therefore a 'stainless hero' is historically accurate for medieval legends. — While 'stainless' has meant 'without moral stain' since the 14th century (e.g., 'stainless knight' in Spenser), its use as a heroic epithet is literary, not a historical title for a specific legendary figure.
