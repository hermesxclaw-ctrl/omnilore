The Approach

The word promises impossibility: stain-less, as if any surface, reputation, or flag could stay untouched forever. From a Sheffield steelworks that refused to rust, to a white Confederate banner that stained history itself, to a British game studio that made cars explode for fun, 'Stainless' has never been one thing — it is a boast, a metallurgical fact, and a brand, all pretending to be clean.

Content advisory. Discussion of slavery, white supremacy and Confederate symbolism in relation to the Stainless Banner (1863-1865). No graphic violence or sexual content. Corporate and technical history is otherwise non-sensitive.

Etymology

Modern English stain + -less 'without'. Stain < Old French desteindre / destain 'to discolor', from Latin dis- + tingere 'to dye, tinge'. Adjective 'stainless' attested from 16th c. in moral sense 'without blemish, pure' (e.g., Shakespeare, Hamlet c.1599-1601 'stainless king'), later literal sense 'not susceptible to staining' applied to steel after 1913. OED first citation for stainless steel 1915.

Cultural Ownership

No single cultural owner. The lexeme is common English. Specific referents are owned by distinct histories: stainless steel is a generic metallurgical family in the public domain; the Stainless Banner is a historical state symbol of the Confederate States of America (1861-1865); Stainless Games Ltd is a private UK company; Stainless Broadcasting Company is a private US broadcaster (Michigan).

Description

Appearance

Depends on referent. (1) As adjective: no visual form; moral/technical descriptor. (2) Stainless steel: silvery-grey, highly reflective when polished, matte when brushed; chromium oxide passive layer invisible to eye. Typical sheet/ingot/cutlery/architectural cladding. (3) Stainless Banner: white field (pure white bunting) with canton (upper hoist) containing the Confederate battle flag: red field, blue saltire edged white bearing 13 white five-pointed stars. Proportions 1:2, canton square occupying upper hoist two-thirds of hoist height. Fringe and construction varied by maker. (4) Corporate identities: Stainless Games logo (1990s-2010s) wordmark with metallic/industrial type; Stainless Broadcasting corporate identity limited to FCC filings and station branding (WEYI, WBSF).

Symbols & Iconography

Stainless steel symbolizes modernity, hygiene, permanence, and affordability — used in Art Deco (Chrysler Building spire, 1930), medical instruments, and kitchenware. The Stainless Banner's white field was explicitly described by its designer William Porcher Miles and contemporaries as representing 'the supremacy of the white man' (Savannah Morning News, 1863) and purity of the Confederate cause; post-war Lost Cause iconography re-used it. The corporate names leverage 'stainless' as connotation of clean, durable, untarnished technology/entertainment.

Behaviour & Personality

Not applicable as single character. As material, stainless steel is passive, corrosion-resistant, non-reactive under normal conditions, but not inert: it can pit, crevice-corrode, and stain under chloride, acid, or high-temperature exposure. As flag, the Stainless Banner was criticized as impractical (easily mistaken for flag of truce at sea/distance, easily soiled). As companies, Stainless Games is known for anarchic vehicular combat humor; Stainless Broadcasting for regional commercial television operation.

Domains & Habitats

Linguistic domain: English moral and technical discourse worldwide. Metallurgical domain: kitchens, hospitals, chemical plants, architecture, transport, cutlery, firearms, aerospace — anywhere corrosion resistance is valued. Historical-political domain: Confederate South 1863-1865, now museums, reenactment, and white-supremacist appropriation contexts. Industrial-geographic: Stainless Games — Newport, Isle of Wight, UK; Stainless Broadcasting — Saginaw / Flint / Bay City, Michigan, US broadcast market.

Version Differences

(1) Steel: >150 grades (austenitic 304/316, ferritic 430, martensitic 410/420, duplex) with differing chromium (10.5-30%), nickel, molybdenum content; early Brearley martensitic vs later Strauss-Maurer austenitic (1912 Germany). (2) Flag: De jure 1863 design vs de facto variants with differing star counts (11-13), canton sizes, and later 1865 Third National flag which added red vertical bar to solve soiling/truce confusion. (3) Companies: Stainless Software (1994-2006) vs Stainless Games Ltd (rebrand) — same legal entity, evolving portfolio; Stainless Broadcasting ownership has shifted through Barrington Broadcasting and later groups, call signs changed.

Powers & Abilities

Corrosion resistance (passive chromium oxide layer)

Source VersionStainless steel metallurgy, all grades, attested 1913-present

Chromium ≥10.5% forms self-healing Cr2O3 layer that prevents oxidation in air and water. Basis for 'stainless' claim.

Limitations

Not stain-proof. Vulnerable to chloride pitting (e.g., seawater), crevice corrosion, intergranular corrosion if improperly heat-treated, and strong acids/bases. Requires maintenance. Early martensitic grades stained more easily than modern 316.

Contradictions

Popular belief that stainless steel never rusts vs technical reality that it can and does under conditions; marketing vs metallurgy.

Mechanical strength and formability (grade-dependent)

Source VersionMaterials science, attested

High tensile strength, hardness (martensitic), ductility (austenitic), and heat resistance. Enables cutlery, surgical tools, pressure vessels, architectural cladding.

Limitations

Trade-offs: hardest grades are less corrosion-resistant and more brittle; most formable grades are non-hardenable and lower strength. Welding can sensitize and reduce resistance.

Contradictions

None; well-characterized by ASTM/AISI standards.

Hygienic and aesthetic persistence

Source VersionCultural and industrial reception, 1920s-present

Non-porous, easy to sterilize, retains polish; therefore adopted for medical, food-processing, and design contexts. Symbolic 'clean' power.

Limitations

Surface can harbor biofilm if scratched; polished finish requires upkeep; fingerprint visibility on bright annealed finishes.

Contradictions

Marketing as permanently hygienic vs need for regular cleaning protocols in hospitals (EN 1672, NSF standards).

Symbolic charge (flag)

Source VersionConfederate States 1863-1865; modern reception

As national flag, the Stainless Banner asserted sovereignty and racial ideology; as historical artifact it now carries strong associative power in memory politics.

Limitations

Design failed functionally (confused with truce flag, soiled quickly); politically toxic, restricted display in many contexts.

Contradictions

Contemporary Confederate claim of 'purity' vs modern scholarly consensus that white field explicitly encoded white supremacy (Miles, Coski).

Stories & Myths

The Rust That Would Not Come — Brearley in Sheffield

Source Harold M. Cobb, The History of Stainless Steel (ASM International, 2010); Kelham Island Museum archives; The New York Times 1915Culture British industrial / metallurgicalPeriod 1912-1915

Summary

At Firth Brown's Brown-Firth Research Laboratory in Sheffield, metallurgist Harry Brearley (1871-1948) was tasked with solving erosion in rifle barrels. On 13 August 1913 he cast steel with ~12.8% chromium and found the etched sample resisted acid attack and did not rust in the yard. Initially dismissed, the alloy was developed for cutlery by Ernest Stuart, marketed as 'rustless steel' then 'stainless steel' by cutler R.F. Mosley. Brearley left Firth to join rival Brown Bayley, leading to patent disputes. Parallel work by Elwood Haynes (US, 1911-1912 stellite), Christian Dantsizen (US, 1911), and Eduard Maurer & Benno Strauss (Germany, 1912 austenitic 18/8) shows independent convergence.

Consequences

Creation of a global alloy family (>50 million tonnes/year by 2020s); Sheffield's cutlery revival; decades of patent litigation (Haynes vs Brearley vs Crucible Steel); establishment of AISI/SAE grading system.

Variants

Who 'invented' stainless steel is contested by nation and grade: Brearley (martensitic, UK), Haynes (martensitic, US), Strauss-Maurer (austenitic, Germany). Cobb argues co-invention.

A Flag Too White — The Stainless Banner is Adopted

Source Journal of the Confederate Congress, 1 May 1863; William Porcher Miles correspondence; John M. Coski, The Confederate Battle Flag (Harvard UP, 2005)Culture Confederate States of AmericaPeriod 1863-1865

Summary

After a year of debate to replace the Stars and Bars (too similar to US Stars and Stripes on battlefield), the Confederate Congress adopted a new national flag: white field with the Army of Northern Virginia battle flag as canton. Editor William T. Thompson (Savannah Morning News, 28 April 1863) celebrated the white field as emblematic of white supremacy. The flag was first hoisted over Richmond 14 May 1863. In practice, the large white field was impractical — at sea or distance it looked like a flag of truce, and it soiled quickly. General P.G.T. Beauregard and others complained. In 1865 a vertical red bar was added at the fly (Third National flag), but the war ended weeks later.

Participants

Confederate Congress

William Porcher Miles (designer of battle flag canton)

William T. Thompson

Jefferson Davis

P.G.T. Beauregard

Consequences

Official second national flag for two years; limited surviving examples; became central to Lost Cause memory and later white-supremacist display; now held primarily in museums with contextual interpretation.

Variants

De jure design vs field-made variants with 7-13 stars, differing canton proportions, and cotton vs bunting construction; some units never received it and kept battle flags.

Carmageddon and the Isle of Wight — Stainless Games is Founded

Source Companies House filing 02955021; Retro Gamer #86 (2011) interview with Patrick Buckland and Neil Barnden; MobyGames databaseCulture British video game industryPeriod 1994-present

Summary

Programmers Patrick Buckland and Neil Barnden, formerly of SCi, founded Stainless Software on the Isle of Wight in September 1994 to develop 3D engine technology. Their debut Carmageddon (1997, published by SCi/Interplay) — a dystopian vehicular combat racer where pedestrians could be run over — was banned or censored in several countries and became a cult hit, spawning expansions and sequels. After a period as work-for-hire (notably Magic: The Gathering — Duels of the Planeswalkers series for Wizards of the Coast, 2009-2014), the studio rebranded Stainless Games and returned to Carmageddon with Kickstarter-funded Carmageddon: Reincarnation (2015) and Carmageddon: Max Damage (2016).

Channel 25 — Stainless Broadcasting in Mid-Michigan

Source FCC History Cards and Licensing and Management System; Broadcasting & Cable Yearbook 1975-2010; WEYI-TV station historyCulture American local televisionPeriod 1972-present (Stainless era from 1970s)

Summary

Stainless Broadcasting Company, based in Michigan, acquired and operated television stations serving the Flint-Saginaw-Bay City market, notably WEYI-TV Channel 25 (NBC affiliate, licensed to Saginaw) and later WBSF (CW). As a small-group owner, it navigated FCC ownership rules, network affiliation switches, and the 2009 digital transition. Like many small broadcasters, it eventually sold to larger groups (Barrington Broadcasting in 2004, later Sinclair via Barrington), but FCC records retain Stainless Broadcasting as licensee of record for a period. Its history illustrates consolidation of US local TV.

From Moral Purity to Kitchen Sink — The Word 'Stainless' Travels

Source Oxford English Dictionary; Shakespeare concordances; Corpus of Historical American English (COHA)Culture English language, literary and commercialPeriod 1590s-present

Summary

Earliest 'stainless' is moral: 'stainless honour,' 'stainless knight' in Elizabethan and Jacobean literature (Spenser, Shakespeare, Milton). The 19th-century hygiene movement literalized it (stainless white, stainless linen). After 1915, 'stainless steel' dominated usage, spawning compounds: stainless-steel sink (1920s), stainless-steel rat (1961), stainless-steel revolution (architecture). Advertising in the 1920s-30s sold 'stainless' as modern, scientific, and classless. By late 20th c., the moral sense receded except in fixed phrases ('stainless reputation'), while the technical sense became generic.

Participants

English authors and advertisers

Sheffield cutlers

American architects and industrial designers

Consequences

Semantic shift from ethical to material; 'stainless' now primarily evokes metal to most speakers; moral sense survives as literary archaism.

Variants

US vs UK spelling and compounds identical; translation equivalents differ: French acier inoxydable, German Edelstahl, Japanese sutenresu kō.

White-supremacist symbolism of the Stainless Banner

Source Thompson editorial, Savannah Morning News 1863, reprinted in Coski (2005) p. 26; Miles letter to Beauregard 1861

Description

Contemporary proponents explicitly linked the white field to white supremacy. Savannah Morning News editor William T. Thompson wrote 28 April 1863 that the white field represented 'the supremacy of the white man' and that the flag should be called the 'White Man's Flag.' William Porcher Miles approved. This was not a later imposition but documented intent.

Scholarly Context

Modern historians (Coski, Bonner, Levin) emphasize that the Confederacy was founded to preserve slavery (see Alexander Stephens' Cornerstone Speech 1861) and its flags must be interpreted in that context. Lost Cause reinterpretations after 1870s attempted to recast the flag as purely about heritage or purity, obscuring racial intent.

Warning: Practical failure and truce-flag confusion — Show unsanitized · Archive policy & legal

Practical failure and truce-flag confusion

Source Coski (2005) pp. 32-34; Confederate Navy correspondence 1863-64

Description

The Stainless Banner's large white field caused dangerous confusion with flags of truce/surrender on battlefields and at sea, potentially endangering bearers. It also soiled quickly in field conditions, undermining the 'stainless' boast. The 1865 addition of a red bar was an explicit admission of design failure.

Scholarly Context

Flag historians cite this as example of ideological symbolism overriding vexillological function.

Warning: Carmageddon censorship and moral panic — Show unsanitized · Archive policy & legal

Carmageddon censorship and moral panic

Source BBFC decision 1997; German BPjM indexing; Retro Gamer #86

Description

Stainless Games' Carmageddon (1997) rewarded running over pedestrians for points, prompting bans in Germany and Brazil, and mandatory censorship in the UK (zombies with green blood) and Australia. The British Board of Film Classification initially refused classification. Debates concerned desensitization to vehicular violence.

Scholarly Context

Game studies scholars (e.g., Newman, 2004) use Carmageddon as case study in 1990s video-game regulation and the distinction between cartoon violence and realistic harm.

Contradictions & Disputes

Who invented stainless steel?

Position A

Harry Brearley (Sheffield, 1913) invented stainless steel — UK standard narrative, martensitic 12.8% Cr cutlery steel.

Position A — evidence

Brearley's 1913 cast, 1915 commercialization, and British patent priority; Cobb pp. 33-52; Kelham Island artifacts.

Position B

Simultaneous independent invention: Elwood Haynes (US, 1911-12) and Benno Strauss / Eduard Maurer (Germany, 1912 austenitic 18/8) also invented stainless steel; no single inventor.

Position B — evidence

Haynes US patent application 12 June 1912 (granted 1919), Dantsizen GE lab notes 1911, Krupp DRP 304,126 filed 18 Oct 1912 for austenitic grade; all meet modern ≥10.5% Cr definition. Cobb pp. 53-78 argues co-invention by grade family.

Scholarly Consensus

Co-invention consensus in materials history: different grades invented in parallel; Brearley credited for martensitic cutlery steel, Strauss-Maurer for austenitic, Haynes for American martensitic. National popular histories still privilege local figure.

Meaning of the white field on the Stainless Banner

Position A

White field symbolized purity of the Confederate cause and the supremacy of the white race (contemporary 1863 statements).

Position A — evidence

Thompson editorial 28 April 1863 ('White Man's Flag'), Miles correspondence, Stephens' Cornerstone Speech context; Coski pp. 25-27.

Position B

White field symbolized purity, peace, or surrender-to-God, with no racial intent (Lost Cause post-war reinterpretation, 1880s-1900s).

Position B — evidence

Later memoirs by Confederate veterans (e.g., 1890s UCV publications) recasting flag as emblem of purity without mentioning race; adopted by heritage groups.

Scholarly Consensus

Modern vexillologists and historians (Coski, Bonner, Foster) affirm Position A as documented contemporary intent; Position B is revisionist.

Is 'stainless' steel actually stainless?

Position A

Marketing and popular usage: stainless steel does not stain, rust, or corrode.

Position A — evidence

Consumer advertising 1920s-present, product labeling ('stainless'), anecdotal experience with kitchen sinks.

Position B

Metallurgical reality: stainless steel is stain-resistant, not stain-proof; it can and does corrode under chloride, acid, crevice, or sensitization conditions.

Position B — evidence

ASTM standards, corrosion testing (ASTM A262, G48), and field failures; Cobb Ch. 10; numerous YouTube/consumer tests showing rust on 430 and 304 in saltwater.

Scholarly Consensus

Position B is correct; industry now prefers 'corrosion-resistant steel' in technical contexts, but 'stainless' remains commercial term.
