Who It Is

Before the sun rose over the ancient steppes, a name was spoken that would shape the spiritual landscape of Iran and beyond: Ahura Mazda, the Wise Lord. In the silence of dawn, Zoroaster saw a being of pure light, and from that vision came a faith that redefined the cosmos as a battleground between truth and deceit. Ahura Mazda is not merely a god; he is the embodiment of wisdom and the architect of a moral universe where every soul chooses its allegiance.

Ahura Mazda is depicted as all-knowing, all-good, the creator and sustainer of the universe, the source of Asha (truth/order). He is benevolent, wise, and just, but also a warrior against evil. He is not anthropomorphically capricious but is a moral being who aids humans in the cosmic struggle.

Powers
Creator of the Universe

Ahura Mazda created the sky, earth, water, plants, animals, and humans, as well as the Amesha Spentas (Holy Immortals) and yazatas.

Limit: He created the world in a state of goodness, but it is subject to attack by Angra Mainyu.

Omniscience and Wisdom

Mazda means wisdom; he is all-knowing and the source of all truth (Asha).

Limit: None stated.

Supreme Judge and Moral Guide

He judges souls after death and guides humanity through the Amesha Spentas and the revelation of Zoroaster.

Limit: He requires human cooperation in the cosmic struggle.

Master of Fire and Light

He is associated with fire (Atar) and light, which are his symbols and agents.

Limit: Fire is a creation, not Ahura Mazda himself.

Immortality and Eternity

Ahura Mazda is eternal and uncreated, existing before and after the material world.

Limit: In Zurvanism, he is created and will be annihilated at the end of time.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In ancient Achaemenid art, Ahura Mazda was represented symbolically as an empty chariot drawn by white horses, or as a winged figure in a disk (often identified as a symbol of royal glory). In Sassanid art, he appears as a male figure with a crown or nimbus, sometimes on horseback. After the iconoclastic movement, no physical representation was used.

Symbols — The winged disk (Faravahar), fire (Atar), light, the sky, the sun, the chariot, the dog-star (Sirius), and the number 6 (associated with the Amesha Spentas).

Domains — Ahura Mazda is the sky deity, residing in the highest heaven (Garothman). He is associated with light, the sun, and the celestial realm. He is also present in the sacred fire (Atar) as a symbol.

Accounts I
Zoroaster's RevelationZoroastrian tradition (later hagiographies, e.g., Denkard)

Traditionally c. 1200-600 BC, but likely earlier

At age 30, Zoroaster was fetching water at dawn for a ritual when he saw a shining being, Vohu Manah (Good Thought), who led him to the presence of Ahura Mazda. There he received the principles of the Good Religion and was chosen to preach it. Zoroaster began preaching, leading to the establishment of Zoroastrianism and the rejection of the daevas.

Accounts II
The Cosmic StruggleAvesta (Yasna 30, 45), later Pahlavi texts (Bundahishn)

Oral tradition, recorded from 4th century AD

Ahura Mazda created the world in a state of light and truth, but Angra Mainyu, the destructive spirit, attacked it, introducing evil, death, and suffering. The two are locked in a finite struggle that will end with the victory of Ahura Mazda. Humanity must choose sides; the world is a battleground between Asha and Druj.

The Creation of the Amesha SpentasAvesta, Pahlavi texts

Achaemenid to Sassanid periods

Ahura Mazda created six (or seven with himself) Amesha Spentas, each embodying an aspect of his nature, such as Vohu Manah (Good Thought), Asha Vahishta (Best Truth), and Spenta Armaiti (Holy Devotion). These beings serve as intermediaries and models for human conduct.

Ahura Mazda is the same as the Judeo-Christian God. — He is a distinct deity with a dualistic cosmology, not a monotheistic God in the same sense.

Zoroastrianism is the first monotheistic religion. — It is often considered henotheistic or dualistic, as it acknowledges Angra Mainyu as an evil force.

The Faravahar is a depiction of Ahura Mazda. — The Faravahar is a symbol of the human soul or divine guidance, not a direct image of Ahura Mazda.

Ahura Mazda is the same as the Hindu god Varuna. — They share a common Indo-Iranian origin but are distinct deities with different roles.
