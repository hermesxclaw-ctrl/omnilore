Who It Is

Ahura Mazda (meaning 'The Wise Lord' or 'Lord of Wisdom') is the supreme deity, the uncreated creator, and the source of all goodness in Zoroastrianism—one of the world's oldest continuously practiced religions. Revealed through the prophet Zoroaster (Zarathustra) in approximately the second millennium BCE on the Central Asian steppes, Ahura Mazda represents the most developed form of monotheism in the pre-Abrahamic world, with profound influence on Judaism, Christianity, and Islam during and after the Babylonian exile and through the Persian empires. Ahura Mazda is the personification of asha (truth, order, righteousness)—the cosmic principle of harmony and justice that opposes druj (falsehood, chaos, evil). He is not a nature deity but a transcendent, ethical, and spiritual being: the source of light, life, wisdom, and goodness. Zoroaster's revolutionary teaching was that Ahura Mazda is the sole uncreated being; all other divine entities, the Amesha Spentas (Holy Immortals), are his emanations or creations—aspects of his divine nature rather than independent gods. The Achaemenid kings, particularly Darius I and Xerxes, prominently invoked Ahura Mazda in their royal inscriptions at Persepolis and Behistun, depicting the deity as the source of their legitimacy and the guarantor of their rule. The famous Faravahar, the winged disc symbol often associated with Zoroastrianism, likely represents the khvarenah (divine glory) bestowed by Ahura Mazda or possibly Ahura Mazda himself in symbolic form. Ahura Mazda's cosmic struggle against Angra Mainyu (the Destructive Spirit) is not a dualism of equal powers—in orthodox Zoroastrian theology, Ahura Mazda is ultimately supreme, and evil will be definitively vanquished at the end of time in a great renovation (Frashokereti). This eschatological framework—a linear, progressive history ending in the triumph of good—was a radical departure from the cyclical cosmologies of other ancient religions and profoundly shaped Western religious thought.

Attested in The Gathas (hymns of Zoroaster, c. 1500–1000 BCE) / AvestaEra c. 1500–1000 BCE (Zoroaster's lifetime); state religion of Achaemenid, Parthian, and Sassanian empires

Form

In the Achaemenid period Ahura Mazda had no anthropomorphic image at the royal court: the sole representation was an empty ceremonial chariot drawn by white horses, kept ready to bear the god into battle. Human depiction began in the western satrapies in the late 5th century BCE, and a statue was erected by the Persian governor of Lydia in 365 BCE under Artaxerxes II. In Sasanian investiture reliefs he is a dignified standing or mounted male figure whose long beard and crown echo the ruler he empowers. Scholars now believe the famous winged disk-and-figure symbol long labelled 'Ahura Mazda' in western scholarship actually represents the royal khvarenah — the personification of royal-divine glory — rather than the god himself. Within worship he remains conceptually immaterial, approached through light: fire and the sun.

Powers

Uncreated Creator (Dātār) Ahura Mazda is the sole, uncreated creator of all that is good: the cosmos, the Amesha Spentas, the yazatas, the sixteen lands of the earth, and humanity. His stock Avestan epithet is dadvah, 'Creator', and creation is exclusively his domain — only he can bring good substance (getig, the material world) out of first principle. He conceives creation first through his mind (with Spenta Mainyu), a doctrine affirmed across the Gathas, Yasna, and Vendidad 1. Gathas; Yasna; Vendidad 1; reinforced in the Bundahishn He creates only good; he cannot (by his nature) create evil. In the earliest Gathic scheme evil has no genuine substance (a-hastīh) — it is negation, not creation. Zurvanism contradicts the uncreated-creator claim by making Ahura Mazda a created twin son of Zurvan. A few Yasna verses (e.g. Yasna 57.17) describe the two 'principles' both creating the world, which contradicts the sole-creator doctrine; scholars date this conflation to roughly the 4th century BCE (reflected in Aristotle, apud Diogenes Laertius).

Omniscience / the all-wise Lord The defining attribute encoded in his very name — Mazda means 'wisdom'. Ahura Mazda is all-knowing: in the Bundahishn he perceives Angra Mainyu's hostility in advance and knows of Ahriman's intent, while Ahriman lacks foresight. His omniscience grounds the 'trap' cosmology in which the created world is a sort of bait-and-front that Ahriman, blinded by greed, attacks and exhausts himself against. Bundahishn 1.16; the epistles of Zatspram 3.23; Shkand Gumanig Vichar 4.63–4.79 He is omniscient but in Mazdean orthodoxy does not pre-punish evil, because punishing before the act would violate his justice and goodness — this is the theological explanation for why the world must exist at all. Some scholars treat his foresight as a late rationalisation; the question of whether omniscience includes foreknowledge of every free choice remains a live theological debate.

Sovereignty over the cosmic war Ahura Mazda is the commander of the good creation in its struggle against Angra Mainyu and the daevas. He does not (in the Gathas) fight alone but enlists the aid of humans, the yazatas, and the archangels. His recital of the Ahuna Vairya (the most sacred prayer) puts Angra Mainyu into a stupor (Yasna 19.15), and his ultimate victory is guaranteed: at the end of time Angra Mainyu and his legions are destroyed, the world is renewed (Frashokereti), and the earth becomes 'a level plain' of one blessed people. Yasna 19.15; Yasht 19.96; Plutarch's cosmogony; the Denkard The victory is eschatological, not immediate; the world-in-between is a mixed state of good and invasive evil, and human ethical choices directly effect the outcome. In Zurvanism the twins are made all-but-equal (Ohrmuzd granted kingship only after 9,000 years), temporarily undermining Ahura Mazda's supremacy.

Bestower of royal legitimacy (the Good Religion's king-making) Across the Achaemenid and Sasanian empires Ahura Mazda is invoked as the source of royal power: Darius declares the kingship was given him by Ahuramazda, and later Sasanian investiture reliefs depict the god handing the ring of authority to the shah. Five Sasanian kings even took the throne-name Hormizd (from Ōhramazdē). Behistun Inscription; Sasanian investiture reliefs; royal name-usage This is a political-theological function; the Achaemenids' own Zoroastrianism is separately debated, though their worship of Ahura Mazda is not. Whether Achaemenid state religion was Zoroastrianism proper or an earlier Mazdaism (with Ahura Mazda co-equal to Mithra) is a long-running scholarly dispute (Boyce, Benveniste, Zaehner disagree).

Source of the Amesha Spentas and yazatas Ahura Mazda brought into being the seven Amesha Spentas ('Holy Immortals') — including Vohu Manah (Good Thought), Asha Vahishta (Best Truth/Order), Spenta Armaiti (Holy Devotion), and Kshathra Vairya (Desirable Dominion) — and the yazatas, the worship-worthy good spirits who aid him. In the Younger Avesta one of these, Spenta Mainyu, is so thoroughly identified with him that Ahura Mazda is effectively assimilated to the Bounteous Spirit as his instrument of creation. Gathas; Younger Avesta; Yasht 13 The yazatas are subordinate aids, not rivals; they no more split his supremacy than archangels split a monotheistic god's. Exactly where Spenta Mainyu ends and Ahura Mazda begins is the pivot of the Haug/Haug-opponents dispute about the twin-spirit doctrine.

Accounts

Around the age of thirty, the priest Zarathushtra Spitama was drawing water at dawn for a sacred ritual when he saw a shining figure — the Amesha Spenta Vohu Manah, Good Thought — who led him into the presence of Ahura Mazda. There he was taught the cardinal principles of the Good Religion that became Zoroastrianism. The revelation made Ahura Mazda the source of all goodness, worthy of the highest worship, a Supreme Lord who had created the yazatas to aid him, and who needed human aid in the cosmic struggle against Angra Mainyu. Zoroaster came to see some of the older Iranian gods as daevas — 'wrong gods' unworthy of worship, creations of the destructive spirit.

In the Vendidad's cosmogony, Ahura Mazda created sixteen good lands, each a homeland and stage for his creation. Against each he created, the destructive spirit Angra Mainyu countered with a scourge: winter, sickness, lust, the serpent of the river, poisonous flies, and so on. In the Bundahishn the battle is elaborated: after Ahura Mazda recites the Ahuna Vairya, Ahriman is temporarily incapacitated and cannot create; when he recovers he creates Jeh, the primal seductress, then flees to fashion his demons. Ahriman slays the primal bull, but the moon rescues its seed and from it springs all animal life. The universe is divided between Ohrmuzd and the yazads on one side and Ahriman with his devs on the other, with mankind caught in the middle, obliged to resist evil through good thoughts, words, and deeds.

Plutarch presents the dualist cosmology the Greeks took from the Iranians: Oromazes (Ahura Mazda), born from the purest light, and Areimanius (Angra Mainyu), born from darkness, are at war. Oromazes created six gods (Good Thought, Truth, Order, Wisdom, Wealth, and the Artificer of Pleasure), and Areimanius created an equal number of rivals. Oromazes then enlarged himself to thrice his size, set the heavens with stars, placed the Dog-star as guardian, and put twenty-four gods in an egg; Areimanius's creatures pierced the egg, mixing evil into good. Between the two stood Mithras the Mediator. The account ends with the promise of Areimanius's annihilation and a level plain of one blessed people.

In Zurvanism, the first principle was Zurvan, Infinite Time, who existed alone and desired a child that would create 'heaven and hell and everything in between'. He sacrificed for a thousand years but began to doubt the sacrifice's efficacy; at the moment of doubt he conceived twins — Ohrmuzd (Ahura Mazda) for the sacrifice and Ahriman for the doubt. Zurvan resolved to make the first-born sovereign. Ohrmuzd, learning this, told his brother; Ahriman ripped open the womb and emerged first, and Zurvan conceded him kinghip — but only for 9,000 years, after which Ohrmuzd would rule for eternity.

The most sacred Zoroastrian prayer, the Ahuna Vairya (Yatha Ahu Vairyo), is held to be Ahura Mazda's own recital — when he speaks it, Angra Mainyu is cast into a stupor and cannot act. The same Gathic worldview promises that at the destined time Angra Mainyu, 'engaged in bringing on pestilence and famine', will be annihilated through his own devices; then the dead are raised, the world is made new and level, a single blessed people speaks one tongue, and Ahura Mazda's sovereignty is absolute and unblemished forever.

Content Notes

From the Behistun Inscription onward, Ahura Mazda was invoked as the author of Achaemenid royal conquest and the punisher of rebels who 'followed the Lie'. The empty white-horse chariot accompanied the Persian army into battle — a holy instrument of war. Later Sasanian investiture reliefs show Ahura Mazda personally handing the ring of absolute monarchy to the shah. The god's name was thus woven into imperial ideology, legitimising campaigns, executions of rebels (Darius impaled the rebel leader Phraortes and others at Bisotun), and the maintenance of empire by force, in the name of divine truth.

This is a documented historical function, not a narrative invention; the 'divine truth versus the Lie' framing was an explicit royal ideology of legitimacy.

Behistun Inscription; Herodotus on the Persian practice; Sasanian reliefs

Zoroastrianism's stark dualism implicates Ahura Mazda in a cosmos afflicted by death, disease, winter, and vice that he did not create. The destructive spirit Angra Mainyu counters every one of the good god's sixteen lands with a scourge and is, in the Bundahishn, the killer of the primal bull and the inventor of Jeh, the primal seductress who afflicts women with menstruation. The burden of theodicy — how a perfect creator God coexists with un-created evil — is handled differently across Mazdaism (evil is alien negation to be finally destroyed), Zurvanism (twins of Time), and modern monotheising readings.

The theodicy debate is central; some feminist and historical critics focus on the misogynistic figure of Jeh and the demonisation of menstruation in the Pahlavi texts.

Vendidad 1; Bundahishn; Denkard; Haug's interpretation

Under the Sasanians, Zoroastrianism (in Mazdean and, on some readings, Zurvanite forms) functioned as an established imperial religion, with a high priest (mowbadān-mowbad) and royal patronage. The 'Good Religion' was bound to the state; forms others considered heretical (Zurvanism) rose and fell partly with royal favour. Sasanian iconoclasm removed earlier images of the god — an act of religious reform enforced by the state. This marriage of altar and throne was the context in which Ahura Mazda, as supreme god, is inseparable from political power and the suppression of dissenting Zoroastrian tendencies.

Historical scholarship notes the state-supported codification of the Avesta under the Sasanians and the political dimension of 'orthodox' vs. 'heretical' struggle.

Sasanian history; the Kartir inscription; Zurvanism's rise under Shapur I and decline

Following the Arab–Muslim conquest of Iran in the 7th century, Zoroastrianism was gradually persecuted and supplanted by Islam, and Zoroastrians came under the status of protected but subordinate dhimmi. Ahura Mazda's divine Name was sometimes pressed into apologetic reinterpretation: some early Muslim-era Persian translators rendered the Quranic Iblis/Shaitan as 'Ahriman', and Zoroastrian apologists tried monotheising revisions (e.g. 'Allah and Iblis are brothers' in a 15th-century Persian book) to blunt Muslim mockery of 'two gods'. The community shrank drastically, surviving in Iran and among the Parsis of India.

The dhimmi status created real pressure; Zoroastrian apologetics of the centuries after conquest negotiated survival, sometimes by flattening their own dualism.

History of post-conquest Iran; Dhalla on the Gayomarthians; early Persian Quran commentaries

In the 19th century Martin Haug, a Western philologist, re-read Yasna 30.3 to argue that Angra Mainyu and Spenta Mainyu were both emanations created by Ahura Mazda — Angra Mainyu choosing evil by free will — effectively turning the ancient faith into a monotheism. The Bombay Parsis, then under pressure from Christian missionaries who attacked Zoroastrianism's 'dualistic' belief in an uncreated God of evil, gratefully adopted this reading and defended it publicly; it circulated so widely that it 'is today almost universally accepted as doctrine'. Scholars increasingly argue this reading distorts the evidence, replacing the ancient, independent evil principle with a comfortable subordinate one.

This is a documented instance of a foreign reinterpretation being adopted for political/apologetic reasons and then naturalised; it concerns how Ahura Mazda's contrastive relation to evil is understood today, and carries a clear orientalist-scholarship dimension.

Haug (1884 'Essays on the Sacred Language, Writings, and Religion of the Parsis'); Parsi polemics of the era

Plutarch describes a (reportedly Persian) apotropic ritual to Arimanius in which the unnamed plant 'omomi' (Haoma/Soma) was pounded with the blood of a sacrificed wolf and cast where the sun never shines. It is a vivid, if second-hand and partly hostile, witness to (or caricature of) ancient Iranian rites around the dark principle that stands over against Ahura Mazda.

Mary Boyce judged Plutarch 'fairly accurate' on basics, but the wolf-blood detail is treated with caution; it is a Greek outsider account, not a Zoroastrian ritual text.

Plutarch, 'On Isis and Osiris'

A way to hold it in your mind

Before Muhammad, before the Kaaba, before the great empires of the West, the Persians of the Achaemenid court invoked a single all-wise Lord whose name they chiselled in three languages at the cliff of Behistun: by the grace of Ahuramazda, King Darius declared, he held the kingship, and the rebels who followed the Lie were broken at its foot. Yet this same god rode to war in an empty chariot drawn by white horses — for in his own court he had no image, only light and the promise that, at the end of all things, the darkness that had dared to counter his sixteen lands would be burned to nothing, and the world would become a level plain of one blessed people. This is the story of Ahura Mazda, the Wise Lord, first and most-frequently-named god of the Gathas, and of the titanic dualism he anchors — a god of such magnetic simplicity and such ferocious complexity that his name now adorns everything from a Tokyo car company to the highest prayer of a living religion.
