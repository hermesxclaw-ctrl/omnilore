Form

As Odin, Alfadur is typically depicted as an older man with a long beard, often wearing a wide-brimmed hat and a cloak, sometimes with a spear (Gungnir). He may be missing an eye, having sacrificed it for wisdom. A raven or two (Huginn and Muninn) might accompany him.

Powers

All-Father Status Alfadur is the highest of the gods, the patriarch of the Æsir. His authority is acknowledged by other gods, though not without challenge. Prose Edda (Gylfaginning) His authority is not absolute; he is subject to fate (Ragnarök) and must act strategically. The Prose Edda presents him as the creator, but other sources (e.g., Völuspá) describe a more complex creation involving the gods as a group.

Wisdom Odin's wisdom is legendary. He sacrificed his eye in Mimir's well to gain profound knowledge and hung on Yggdrasil for nine nights to learn the runes. Poetic Edda (Völuspá, Hávamál) His wisdom is hard-won and often incomplete; he must continually seek more knowledge. Despite his wisdom, he is sometimes fooled or outwitted by giants and others.

Seiðr (Magic) Odin is a master of seiðr, a form of Norse magic associated with fate, prophecy, and shamanic practices. This is often considered a feminine art, which adds a layer of complexity to his character. Poetic Edda (Lokasenna), Ynglinga saga The practice of seiðr was considered unmanly (ergi) in Viking society, and Odin is taunted for it by Loki. The exact nature and extent of his seiðr are debated, and it is sometimes conflated with other forms of magic.

War and Death Odin is the god of war and death. He chooses half of the warriors slain in battle (the einherjar) to dwell in Valhalla, preparing for Ragnarök. Poetic Edda (Grímnismál), Prose Edda He does not control all deaths in battle; Freyja claims the other half for her field, Fólkvangr. His role as a war god is not purely martial; he is also associated with strategy, poetry, and frenzy (berserker state).

Behavior & Habitat

Alfadur is the chief of the Æsir gods. He is a god of wisdom, war, death, poetry, and magic. He is cunning, ambitious, and often tests mortals and other gods with riddles and disguises. He is not a benevolent father figure in the modern sense, but a complex, sometimes ruthless, seeker of knowledge and power.

Accounts

Odin sacrificed one of his eyes at Mimir's well to gain wisdom and understanding of the cosmos. This act is a central myth explaining his profound knowledge.

Odin hung himself on the World Tree Yggdrasil for nine nights, pierced by his own spear, without food or drink, to learn the secrets of the runes. He 'took up the runes, screaming' and gained their power.

Odin, through a series of deceptions, stole the Mead of Poetry, a magical brew that grants the gift of poetry and wisdom. He escaped as an eagle, spilling some of the mead to the earth for all poets, but kept the main portion for the gods.

The gods, fearing the wolf Fenrir, tricked him into being bound with a magical fetter (Gleipnir). As a pledge of good faith, Tyr placed his hand in Fenrir's mouth; when the wolf realized he was tricked, he bit off Tyr's hand. Odin is present as the leader of the gods in this act of deception.

Odin is associated with hanged men and gallows. He is said to be able to speak with the dead and to take warriors from the battlefield. This dark aspect connects him to the dead and the mysteries of the afterlife.

Amid the many stories told under the name of the highest Norse god, the very title 'Allfather' (Old Norse Alfodr) carries its own small history. In the surviving corpus the word appears only once in the oldest verse, in the wisdom-contest of the Vafthrudnismal, where the giant asks the disguised god who the Alfodr is; the answer names the father of all the gods. Snorri Sturluson, writing in the thirteenth century, then adopts the title as his standard way of speaking of the highest god, folded into his account of the god who gained wisdom by sacrificing an eye and who presides over the slain. The title thus moves from a single archaic verse into the defining byname of the king of the Norse gods.

Modern Interpretations

Literature Der Ring des Nibelungen Richard Wagner 1876 inspired Wagner's Wotan is a complex, flawed god, clearly based on Odin, who struggles with his own laws and ambitions. Literature The Lord of the Rings J.R.R. Tolkien 1954-55 inspired Gandalf, the wandering wizard, is heavily inspired by Odin, particularly the 'Wanderer' aspect. Comics Marvel Comics (Thor) Stan Lee, Larry Lieber, Jack Kirby 1962 direct Odin is a central character in Marvel's Thor comics, portrayed as the All-Father, King of Asgard. This version is a direct adaptation but with Marvel's own spin. Film Marvel Cinematic Universe (Thor, Avengers) Marvel Studios 2011-2019 direct Anthony Hopkins and Jeff Bridges portray Odin in the MCU, a powerful but flawed king. This is the most widely recognized modern depiction. Video Games God of War (2018) Santa Monica Studio 2018 inspired Odin is a major off-screen antagonist, a manipulative and feared figure, setting up the sequel. Video Games God of War Ragnarök Santa Monica Studio 2022 direct Odin is a fully realized, cunning, and deceptive antagonist, a master manipulator who is both charming and terrifying. Television American Gods Neil Gaiman (novel), Bryan Fuller & Michael Green (TV) 2017-2021 direct Mr. Wednesday is a modern, con-man rendition of Odin, trying to survive in a world that has forgotten the old gods. Music Various Metal Bands Various (e.g., Amon Amarth, Tyr, Led Zeppelin) 1970s-present inspired Odin is a frequent subject in heavy metal and rock music

Scholarly Debates
Odin's Role as Creator
Snorri Sturluson (Prose Edda) presents Odin as the All-Father and creator who, with his brothers, made the world from the body of the giant Ymir. ⇌ The Poetic Edda (Völuspá) describes the gods as sons of Borr who created the world, but Odin is not singled out as the sole creator. The creation is a group effort.
Evidence for A: Gylfaginning, chapters 6-9.
Evidence for B: Völuspá, stanzas 3-9.
Odin's Wisdom vs. His Folly
Odin is the god of wisdom, having sacrificed his eye and hung on Yggdrasil for knowledge. ⇌ Odin is often outwitted by giants and other beings, and his actions lead to his own doom at Ragnarök.
Evidence for A: Völuspá, Hávamál.
Evidence for B: Various myths, including the theft of the Mead of Poetry and the binding of Fenrir.
Odin and Seiðr
Odin is a master of seiðr, a powerful form of magic. ⇌ Seiðr is considered a feminine practice (ergi), and Odin is mocked for it by Loki.
Evidence for A: Ynglinga saga, Lokasenna.
Evidence for B: Lokasenna, stanza 24.
What We Don't Know

The specific spelling 'Alfadur' is not well-attested in primary sources. The research is based on the identification of this name with the more common 'Alfǫðr' (All-Father), a name for Odin. Further research could explore the specific use of this spelling in any modern or historical texts, but the evidence strongly points to Odin. There is no evidence of a separate deity named Alfadur.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Alfadur as the ultimate 'player of the game.' He is not the benevolent king of a fantasy realm; he is a complex, strategic genius who is willing to make terrible sacrifices and use any means necessary to achieve his goals. He's the CEO who will sell his own company to save it, the general who will sacrifice a battalion to win the war, the scholar who will break every rule to get the knowledge he needs. He's a figure of immense power and immense tragedy, a god who is trying to cheat fate itself.
