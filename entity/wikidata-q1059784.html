The Approach

In the shadow of Medea's fire, another daughter of Colchis moved through Greek myth with quiet power—Chalciope, whose name means 'bronze-face,' appears in six different stories, a woman of many faces, each one a key to a hero's fate.

Identity

Chalciope

Pronunciation /kælˈsaɪ.ə.pi/ (kal-SY-ə-pee)

Known As

Chalcippe

Etymology

From Greek χαλκός (chalkos) 'bronze' + ὤψ (ōps) 'face, eye', meaning 'bronze-face' or 'bronze-eyed'.

Cultural Ownership

Greek mythology

Disputed Identifications

The name is shared by several distinct mythological figures; ancient sources do not always clarify which is meant, and later mythographers may conflate them.

Description

Appearance

No physical description survives in ancient sources. The name 'bronze-face' may imply a striking or metallic complexion, but this is speculative.

Symbols & Iconography

No known iconography specifically identified as Chalciope.

Behaviour & Personality

As wife of Phrixus, she is depicted as loyal and protective, aiding her sons and mediating between her father Aeëtes and the Argonauts. Other figures named Chalciope have little characterization.

Domains & Habitats

Colchis (for the daughter of Aeëtes); Athens (for the wife of Aegeus); Cos (for the mother of Thessalus).

Version Differences

The name is used for multiple distinct individuals: (1) daughter of Aeëtes and wife of Phrixus; (2) daughter of Rhexenor (or Chalcodon) and second wife of Aegeus; (3) daughter of Eurypylus of Cos, mother of Thessalus by Heracles; (4) wife of Thessalus, mother of Antiphus; (5) daughter of Phalerus; (6) mother of Linus by Apollo. Some sources spell the name 'Chalcippe'.

Stories & Myths

Chalciope, daughter of Aeëtes, wife of Phrixus

Source Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 1.9.1; Hyginus, Fabulae 14Culture GreekPeriod Mythic

Summary

Chalciope, daughter of King Aeëtes of Colchis, married Phrixus, who fled Greece on the golden ram. They had several sons, including Cytissorus, Phrontis, Melas, and Argus. After Phrixus's death, Chalciope remained in Colchis and later aided the Argonauts, helping Jason win the Golden Fleece by pleading with her father and assisting her sons, who were shipwrecked and rescued by the Argonauts.

Consequences

Her intervention facilitated Jason's acquisition of the Golden Fleece, leading to Medea's involvement and the subsequent tragedies.

Variants

Some accounts name her mother as Asterodeia or Hecate; others give her sons different names.

Chalciope, daughter of Rhexenor, wife of Aegeus

Source Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.15.6Culture GreekPeriod Mythic

Summary

Chalciope, daughter of Rhexenor (or Chalcodon), was the second wife of King Aegeus of Athens. She bore him no children, so he gave her to a friend.

Participants

Chalciope

Aegeus

Consequences

Aegeus then sought to father an heir, ultimately fathering Theseus with Aethra.

Variants

Her parentage is uncertain; some say her father was Chalcodon of Euboea.

Chalciope, daughter of Eurypylus of Cos, was seduced by Heracles and bore him a son, Thessalus, who later became king of Cos and gave his name to Thessaly.

Chalciope, mother of Linus

Source Hyginus, Fabulae 97Culture GreekPeriod Mythic

Summary

According to Hyginus, Chalciope was the mother of Linus, the famous musician, by Apollo. This Linus was a teacher of Heracles.

Known Gaps

No primary archaeological or epigraphic evidence directly naming Chalciope has been found. The scarcity of sources limits detailed description. The relationships among the various Chalciopes remain uncertain due to fragmentary evidence. Further research in scholia and less-known mythographers might clarify some points.
