Who It Is

Imagine being the only one who cannot see the game, and the one who guides your hand is a liar. Höðr, the blind god of Asgard, is the most tragic figure in Norse mythology — a weapon of fate, an innocent hand that felled the sun, and the unwitting spark that lit the pyre of Ragnarök. He is the god who killed his brother, and yet, in the end, he is forgiven.

In the Eddic tradition, Höðr is a tragic figure — innocent, blind, and manipulated by Loki, who guides his hand to throw the mistletoe dart. He is described by Snorri as strong but one whom the gods 'would rather not be called upon', due to the tragic consequences of his actions. In Saxo's version, he is a proactive, ambitious, and ultimately victorious warrior-king who defeats Balderus in battle, but who later dies after being betrayed by Othinus. Powers
Superhuman Strength

Snorri explicitly states that Höðr is 'strong enough' (or 'very strong'). This is a typical attribute of the Aesir, but it is highlighted in his case, perhaps to emphasize that his blindness is his only real weakness.

Limit: His strength is not tied to any specific weapon or feat; it is a general attribute, and his blindness makes it difficult to use effectively in combat.

Blindness (as a narrative/divine trait)

Höðr's blindness is not a 'power' in the traditional sense, but it is a defining trait. It makes him the perfect instrument for Loki's treachery — he cannot see what he is doing, and thus his guilt is mitigated. This has been interpreted as a symbol of fate, of the random and senseless nature of evil, or as a necessary condition for the tragedy to unfold.

Limit: Obviously, blindness is a severe limitation in most contexts.

Kingship and Martial Prowess (Saxo's version)

Saxo's Hotherus is a cunning and successful king and warrior. He wins Nanna's hand, defeats Balderus in battle, and rules Denmark. He is also skilled in magic, using illusions to escape from Othinus.

Limit: He is ultimately mortal and dies, betrayed by Othinus.

Appearance — The Eddic sources are frustratingly sparse on Höðr's physical description. We know he is blind (or at least described as such by Snorri), and that he is strong. The Völuspá stanza describing the killing of Baldr does not explicitly state he is blind, but Snorri's account makes this a central feature. Symbols — The mistletoe dart (or arrow/spear) is the primary symbol associated with Höðr, though it is more a symbol of the crime than of the god himself. Blindness is his defining characteristic. In Saxo, the magic sword 'Mistilteinn' (which means 'mistletoe') is the key weapon, linking the two traditions. Domains — Höðr is not associated with any specific domain, unlike other gods (e.g., Thor with thunder, Freyr with fertility). He is primarily defined by his role in the Baldr myth — the blind slayer, the instrument of fate. In Saxo, he is a king of Denmark, and his 'domain' is political and martial. In the Eddic tradition, he is a god of the Aesir, dwelling in Asgard, but without a clear sphere of influence.

Accounts I
The Death of Baldr (Eddic Version)Prose Edda (Gylfaginning), Völuspá, Baldrs draumar

13th century (written), earlier oral tradition

Baldr, the beloved son of Odin and Frigg, begins to have dreams of his own death. Frigg extracts an oath from all things in the world — fire, water, metals, stones, diseases, beasts, birds, poisons, and plants — that they will not harm him. All things swear the oath, except the mistletoe, which Frigg deems too young and insignificant to bother with. The gods, now invulnerable to Baldr, make a sport of throwing weapons and objects at him, which all bounce off harmlessly. Loki, the trickster, discovers Frigg's oversight and fashions a dart (or arrow) from the mistletoe. He finds Höðr, who is blind and standing apart from the game, and asks him why he is not participating. Höðr says he has nothing to throw and cannot see where Baldr is. Loki says he will help, places the mistletoe dart in Höðr's hand, and guides his aim. Höðr throws the dart, which pierces Baldr and kills him. The gods are struck dumb with grief and horror. Accounts II
The Battle of Brávellir and the Death of Hotherus (Saxo's Version)Gesta Danorum, Book 3

c. 1200

In Saxo's euhemerized account, Hotherus is a mortal king of Denmark, the son of Hothbrod. Balderus is a demigod, son of Othinus, who is invulnerable to all weapons except a magic sword called Mistilteinn. Both desire the hand of Nanna, the daughter of a Swedish king. Hotherus wins her, and Balderus, enraged, makes war on him. Hotherus, using his wits and the magic sword, defeats Balderus in battle. Balderus is wounded and later dies. Othinus, grieving for his son, seeks revenge. He rapes Rinda, a mortal princess, to beget a son, Bous, who is destined to kill Hotherus. Bous and Hotherus fight; Hotherus is mortally wounded but kills Bous. Hotherus dies and is buried in a royal mound. The story is interwoven with other Danish legends and is clearly a historicized adaptation of the myth. Hotherus becomes a legendary Danish king. The story provides a genealogical and heroic foundation for Danish kingship, but it loses the cosmic and tragic dimensions of the Eddic version.

Höðr was evil or malicious. — In the Eddic tradition, Höðr is a tragic, innocent victim of Loki's manipulation. He is blind and does not know what he is throwing. He is not a villain, but a pawn. This is a key part of the tragedy.

The mistletoe was a powerful, magical weapon. — In the Eddic tradition, the mistletoe is specifically noted as being 'young' and 'insignificant' — so much so that Frigg did not bother to extract an oath from it. Its power lies not in itself, but in the fact that it was the only thing that could harm Baldr. The irony is that the most humble of plants becomes the instrument of a god's death.

Höðr's blindness is a punishment from Odin. — There is no evidence for this in any source. Höðr is simply described as blind from the start, with no explanation given. It is a precondition for the story, not a consequence.

Höðr and Baldr were rivals for Nanna's love in the original myth. — This is unique to Saxo's euhemerized version. In the Eddic tradition, Nanna is Baldr's wife, and there is no romantic connection to Höðr. The love triangle is a later, historicized addition.

Höðr will be resurrected after Ragnarök. — The Völuspá mentions that after Ragnarök, Baldr and Höðr will return from Hel and live together in the reborn world, reconciled. This is a powerful image of forgiveness and healing, but it is often overlooked. Höðr is not 'resurrected' in the sense of being brought back to life, but is part of the new world that emerges from the ashes.
