Who It Is

Höðr (Hod, Hodr) is the blind Norse god, son of Odin and Frigg, who — deceived and guided by Loki — threw the mistletoe dart that killed his brother Baldr. His story is one of tragedy and innocence: because Baldr was invulnerable to all things except mistletoe, and Höðr was blind, Loki placed the mistletoe dart in his hand and guided his aim. Höðr thus became the unwitting instrument of the most pivotal death in Norse mythology — the event that sets Ragnarök in motion. After Baldr's death, Höðr was killed by Vali, another son of Odin (born specifically to avenge Baldr). In the Gesta Danorum (Saxo Grammaticus's version), Höðr is a heroic mortal king who defeats Baldr in battle for the love of Nanna. In the Eddas, however, Höðr is a tragic figure — a god who killed his brother without malice, through deception. Höðr is destined to return after Ragnarök, reconciled with Baldr, to rebuild the world.

Origins

Höðr is the son of Odin and Frigg. When Frigg extracted oaths from all things not to harm Baldr, she overlooked the mistletoe because it seemed too young and harmless. Loki learned this, took a mistletoe shoot, made a dart from it, and tricked the blind Höðr into throwing it at Baldr during a game.

Attested in Poetic Edda (Völuspá, Baldrs Draumar) / Prose Edda (Gylfaginning) / Gesta DanorumEra Viking Age (c. 800-1100 CE), recorded 13th century

Form

The Eddic sources are frustratingly sparse on Höðr's physical description. We know he is blind (or at least described as such by Snorri), and that he is strong. The Völuspá stanza describing the killing of Baldr does not explicitly state he is blind, but Snorri's account makes this a central feature. Saxo's version describes Hotherus as a handsome, skilled, and charismatic mortal king, likely reflecting the euhemerized nature of his account. No surviving artistic depiction from the Viking Age or medieval period is known.

Powers

Superhuman Strength Snorri explicitly states that Höðr is 'strong enough' (or 'very strong'). This is a typical attribute of the Aesir, but it is highlighted in his case, perhaps to emphasize that his blindness is his only real weakness. Prose Edda (Gylfaginning), Eddic tradition His strength is not tied to any specific weapon or feat; it is a general attribute, and his blindness makes it difficult to use effectively in combat. Saxo's Hotherus is not described as superhumanly strong, but as a skilled mortal warrior.

Blindness (as a narrative/divine trait) Höðr's blindness is not a 'power' in the traditional sense, but it is a defining trait. It makes him the perfect instrument for Loki's treachery — he cannot see what he is doing, and thus his guilt is mitigated. This has been interpreted as a symbol of fate, of the random and senseless nature of evil, or as a necessary condition for the tragedy to unfold. Prose Edda, Eddic tradition Obviously, blindness is a severe limitation in most contexts. In Saxo, Hotherus is not blind at all. This is the most significant difference between the two traditions.

Kingship and Martial Prowess (Saxo's version) Saxo's Hotherus is a cunning and successful king and warrior. He wins Nanna's hand, defeats Balderus in battle, and rules Denmark. He is also skilled in magic, using illusions to escape from Othinus. Gesta Danorum He is ultimately mortal and dies, betrayed by Othinus. This portrayal is almost entirely incompatible with the Eddic version, where Höðr is a passive, blind god.

Behavior & Habitat

In the Eddic tradition, Höðr is a tragic figure — innocent, blind, and manipulated by Loki, who guides his hand to throw the mistletoe dart. He is described by Snorri as strong but one whom the gods 'would rather not be called upon', due to the tragic consequences of his actions. In Saxo's version, he is a proactive, ambitious, and ultimately victorious warrior-king who defeats Balderus in battle, but who later dies after being betrayed by Othinus. The two traditions present almost opposite personalities: the passive, tragic victim of the Eddas versus the active, heroic (if flawed) king of Saxo.

Accounts

Baldr, the beloved son of Odin and Frigg, begins to have dreams of his own death. Frigg extracts an oath from all things in the world — fire, water, metals, stones, diseases, beasts, birds, poisons, and plants — that they will not harm him. All things swear the oath, except the mistletoe, which Frigg deems too young and insignificant to bother with. The gods, now invulnerable to Baldr, make a sport of throwing weapons and objects at him, which all bounce off harmlessly. Loki, the trickster, discovers Frigg's oversight and fashions a dart (or arrow) from the mistletoe. He finds Höðr, who is blind and standing apart from the game, and asks him why he is not participating. Höðr says he has nothing to throw and cannot see where Baldr is. Loki says he will help, places the mistletoe dart in Höðr's hand, and guides his aim. Höðr throws the dart, which pierces Baldr and kills him. The gods are struck dumb with grief and horror. Frigg asks who among them will ride to Hel and offer a ransom for Baldr's return. Hermóðr volunteers and rides Sleipnir to Hel. The giantess Þökk (almost certainly Loki in disguise) refuses to weep for Baldr, and he must remain in Hel. The death of Baldr is a primary omen of Ragnarök.

In Saxo's euhemerized account, Hotherus is a mortal king of Denmark, the son of Hothbrod. Balderus is a demigod, son of Othinus, who is invulnerable to all weapons except a magic sword called Mistilteinn. Both desire the hand of Nanna, the daughter of a Swedish king. Hotherus wins her, and Balderus, enraged, makes war on him. Hotherus, using his wits and the magic sword, defeats Balderus in battle. Balderus is wounded and later dies. Othinus, grieving for his son, seeks revenge. He rapes Rinda, a mortal princess, to beget a son, Bous, who is destined to kill Hotherus. Bous and Hotherus fight; Hotherus is mortally wounded but kills Bous. Hotherus dies and is buried in a royal mound. The story is interwoven with other Danish legends and is clearly a historicized adaptation of the myth.

Modern Interpretations

Opera Der Ring des Nibelungen (The Ring of the Nibelung) Richard Wagner 1876 inspired Wagner's Ring cycle is a major retelling of Norse mythology. While Höðr does not appear directly, the character of Hagen (in Götterdämmerung) is often seen as partially inspired by the tragic, manipulated figure. The theme of a doomed hero manipulated by a trickster figure is a key parallel. Literature American Gods Neil Gaiman 2001 direct Höðr appears as a minor character, an old, blind god living in a retirement community for forgotten deities. Gaiman's portrayal emphasizes his tragic backstory and his regret over killing Baldr. Literature Norse Mythology Neil Gaiman 2017 direct Gaiman retells the story of Baldr's death, including Höðr's role, in a vivid and accessible prose style, bringing the myth to a new generation of readers. Comic Book The Mighty Thor (Marvel Comics) Stan Lee, Jack Kirby, et al. 1962-present direct Höðr appears in the Marvel Universe as a supporting character. He is depicted as a blind Asgardian god who is often shown as a tragic figure. He has been involved in various storylines, including the 'War of the Realms' event. Video Game God of War Ragnarök Santa Monica Studio 2022 direct Höðr is mentioned and appears in a flashback sequence during the game, depicting the death of Baldr. Scholarly Debates
Höðr's Blindness
Höðr is blind (Eddic tradition). ⇌ Hotherus is not blind (Saxo's version).
Evidence for A: Prose Edda (Gylfaginning): 'Höðr is the name of one of the Aesir, who is blind.'
Evidence for B: Gesta Danorum describes Hotherus as a handsome, skilled warrior-king; no mention of blindness.
Loki's Role
Loki is the primary instigator, tricking Höðr into killing Baldr (Eddic tradition). ⇌ Loki plays no role; Hotherus acts out of jealousy and rivalry (Saxo's version).
Evidence for A: Prose Edda (Gylfaginning), chapter 49.
Evidence for B: Gesta Danorum, Book 3; no Loki figure appears.
The Weapon
The weapon is a dart or arrow made of mistletoe (Eddic tradition). ⇌ The weapon is a magic sword called Mistilteinn (Saxo's version).
Evidence for A: Prose Edda (Gylfaginning), chapter 49.
Evidence for B: Gesta Danorum, Book 3: 'Hotherus... was armed with a sword called Mistilteinn.'
The Nature of Höðr/Hotherus
Höðr is a god (Aesir), son of Odin (Eddic tradition). ⇌ Hotherus is a mortal king of Denmark (Saxo's version).
Evidence for A: Prose Edda (Gylfaginning), Völuspá.
Evidence for B: Gesta Danorum, Book 3.
What We Don't Know

1) No archaeological evidence or contemporary Viking-Age depictions of Höðr are known. 2) The relationship between the Icelandic Eddic tradition and the Danish Saxo tradition is still debated; no intermediate versions have survived. 3) The exact nature of the original, pre-13th-century myth (before Snorri's potential additions) is unknown. 4) The etymology of the name 'Höðr' is uncertain, with multiple possible derivations. 5) There is no detailed analysis of Höðr's role in the broader Indo-European 'blind slayer' motif, which could be a fruitful area of research.

A way to hold it in your mind

Höðr is the ultimate 'wrong place, wrong time' figure — the mythological equivalent of a person who is handed a weapon and told to shoot, only to discover they were tricked into killing their own brother. He is less like a villain and more like a tragic character in a Greek play, a pawn of the gods and fate. His story is a powerful reminder that even the most innocent actions can have devastating consequences, and that sometimes, the greatest tragedy is not evil, but ignorance.
