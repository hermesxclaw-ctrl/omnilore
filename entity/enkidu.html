Who It Is

Before the first city, before the first king, there was a man who ran with gazelles and drank at the watering holes with the wild beasts. He was Enkidu, born of clay and the breath of the gods, the mirror image of a tyrant king, and his story is the oldest tragedy of friendship ever told. This is the tale of the wild man who became human, the companion who died for his friend, and the ghost who taught a king the true cost of immortality.

Initially, Enkidu lives entirely in the wilderness, unaware of human civilization, and acts as a protector of the wild animals, thwarting the huntsman's traps. He is innocent, uncultured, and driven by instinct. After his sexual encounter with the harlot Shamhat, he is 'tamed': the animals flee him, and he gains understanding and reason. He then journeys to Uruk, where he challenges Gilgamesh to a contest of strength. After their fight, they become inseparable companions. Powers
Superhuman Strength

Enkidu is created as an equal to Gilgamesh, 'strong as a rock from the sky' (Andrew George translation). He wrestles Gilgamesh to a draw, and later helps him defeat the Bull of Heaven and Humbaba.

Limit: His strength is matched only by Gilgamesh, and he is mortal.

Animal Kinship

Before his taming, Enkidu lives with wild animals, and they trust him. He can run with gazelles and drink at their watering holes. This ability is lost after his sexual encounter with Shamhat, as the animals flee him.

Limit: Lost permanently after civilization.

Combat Prowess

Enkidu is a formidable warrior, skilled in hand-to-hand combat and with weapons (he learns to use a sword and bow after becoming civilized). He slays the Bull of Heaven and helps defeat Humbaba.

Limit: He is mortal and can be wounded or killed.

Dream Interpretation (in some versions)

In the Old Babylonian version, Enkidu interprets Gilgamesh's dreams as favorable omens. In the Standard version, Gilgamesh interprets his own dreams, but Enkidu offers his own interpretations, showing wisdom.

Limit: Not a formal power, but a sign of his acquired wisdom.

Ghostly Return

After his death, Enkidu's spirit is summoned by Gilgamesh from the Underworld, and he describes the bleak conditions of the dead.

Limit: He is a ghost, not a living being.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Enkidu is created from clay by the goddess Aruru, who shapes him in the image of the sky-god Anu. He is described as a 'wild man' with hair covering his body, long locks like a woman's, and a body covered in matted fur. He wears no clothes, grazes with the gazelles, and drinks from the watering holes with the wild beasts. Symbols — The wild man, the steppe, the gazelle, the harlot's embrace (as a symbol of civilization), the cedar forest, the lion (as a foe and later a symbol of his strength), and the netherworld (his ghost).

Domains — The wilderness (steppe), the Cedar Forest (where he fights Humbaba), Uruk (as a companion), and the Underworld (after his death, his ghost returns).

Accounts I
The Creation of EnkiduEpic of Gilgamesh, Tablet I

Old Babylonian (c. 1800 BCE) / Standard Babylonian (7th c. BCE)

The gods create Enkidu in response to the prayers of the people of Uruk, who are oppressed by Gilgamesh's arrogance. Aruru, the mother goddess, shapes him from clay in the image of Anu. He is a wild man, living with animals, and he destroys the traps of the huntsman, who then complains to Gilgamesh. Gilgamesh sends the harlot Shamhat to seduce Enkidu, which tames him and alienates him from the animals. He then goes to Uruk, where he challenges Gilgamesh. Enkidu becomes Gilgamesh's companion, and their friendship is the central relationship of the epic.

The Wrestling MatchEpic of Gilgamesh, Tablet II

Old Babylonian / Standard Babylonian

Enkidu arrives in Uruk and blocks the door of the temple of the goddess Ishtar, preventing Gilgamesh from exercising his 'right of the first night' (a custom that is actually not attested in the epic, but is a modern interpretation). The two wrestle, and Enkidu holds his own, but eventually acknowledges Gilgamesh's superiority. They embrace and become friends. The two become inseparable companions, and Gilgamesh's tyranny over Uruk ends.

The Slaying of HumbabaEpic of Gilgamesh, Tablets II–V

Old Babylonian / Standard Babylonian

Gilgamesh proposes they journey to the Cedar Forest to slay the monstrous guardian Humbaba, to gain fame and establish their names. Enkidu, who knows Humbaba's power, tries to dissuade him, but ultimately accompanies him. They receive the blessing of the sun-god Shamash, who provides them with winds and weapons. They defeat Humbaba, who begs for mercy, but Gilgamesh kills him (in some versions, Enkidu urges Gilgamesh to kill him). The gods decide that for slaying Humbaba, Enkidu must die (in the Standard version, it is for the Bull of Heaven as well).

Accounts II
The Bull of HeavenEpic of Gilgamesh, Tablet VI

Old Babylonian / Standard Babylonian

The goddess Ishtar, rejected by Gilgamesh, sends the Bull of Heaven to punish Uruk. Gilgamesh and Enkidu together slay the Bull, and Enkidu taunts Ishtar, throwing the bull's thigh at her. The gods decree that Enkidu must die for his role in the slaying of Humbaba and the Bull.

The Death of EnkiduEpic of Gilgamesh, Tablet VII

Old Babylonian / Standard Babylonian

Enkidu falls ill after the slaying of the Bull of Heaven, afflicted by a disease sent by the gods. He curses the harlot Shamhat and the huntsman, but Shamash rebukes him, reminding him of the good things he has gained: the love of Gilgamesh, fine food and clothing, and a proper burial. Enkidu retracts his curse and dies, lamenting his fate. Gilgamesh mourns Enkidu deeply, and this loss sends him on a quest for immortality.

The Ghost of EnkiduEpic of Gilgamesh, Tablet XII (adapted from Sumerian poem 'Gilgamesh and the Netherworld')

Old Babylonian / Standard Babylonian (later addition)

Gilgamesh, seeking to bring Enkidu back, breaks a ritual taboo, and Enkidu's ghost is allowed to rise from the Underworld. He describes the fate of the dead, showing that the afterlife is bleak and joyless. The tablet is a somber conclusion to the epic, emphasizing the finality of death.

Enkidu was a 'wild man' who was a primitive beast, lacking intelligence. — Enkidu is created as an equal to Gilgamesh, and after his taming, he gains wisdom and understanding. He is not a brute, but a complex character who mourns his loss of innocence.

Enkidu and Gilgamesh were lovers. — The text is ambiguous. They are described as companions, and their relationship is intensely emotional, but there is no explicit sexual relationship in the text.

Enkidu was a 'hairy beast' with horns and a tail, like a satyr. — The text describes him as having hair all over his body, but he is human in form. Later art may depict him with bull-like features, but this is not in the text.

Enkidu was killed by Gilgamesh. — Enkidu dies of a disease sent by the gods, not by Gilgamesh.
