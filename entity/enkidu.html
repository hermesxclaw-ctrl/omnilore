Who It Is

Enkidu is the wild man created by the gods to be Gilgamesh's equal and counterweight — and who becomes, through their profound friendship, the emotional and moral heart of the world's oldest epic. When the people of Uruk cry out against Gilgamesh's tyranny, the goddess Aruru creates Enkidu from clay and spittle, shaping him in the image of the god of war Ninurta and casting him into the wilderness. Enkidu lives among the animals as one of them — hairy, naked, eating grass with the gazelles, drinking at the waterholes, undoing hunters' traps and freeing their prey. He is innocent of civilization, language, and mortality. The transition from wild man to civilized being is initiated by the temple prostitute Shamhat, sent by Gilgamesh to tame him. Shamhat seduces Enkidu over six days and seven nights, after which the animals flee from him — he has become human. She teaches him to eat bread and drink beer, to anoint himself with oil, and to wear clothing, then brings him to Uruk to confront Gilgamesh. Their first meeting is a wrestling match of cosmic proportions: Enkidu blocks Gilgamesh's entrance to a bridal chamber (asserting the rights the king has been abusing), they fight through the streets, and emerge from the struggle not as enemies but as brothers. This friendship transforms both: Gilgamesh gains a companion who tempers his excess, and Enkidu gains purpose and identity within human society. Together, they undertake the epic's great adventures: the journey to the Cedar Forest to slay Humbaba, and the defense of Uruk against the Bull of Heaven sent by the spurned goddess Ishtar. Enkidu's role in these events is crucial but ultimately tragic. He dreams of the gods' council, where it is decreed that one of the two companions must die for the slaying of Humbaba and the Bull. He falls ill with a wasting sickness and dies over twelve excruciating days, cursing Shamhat for civilizing him (though Shamash, the sun god, reminds him of all the good that civilization gave him) and blessing her before the end. Enkidu's death is the pivot of the epic: Gilgamesh's grief is inconsolable, his lament over Enkidu's body one of the most moving passages in ancient literature. Enkidu's death confronts Gilgamesh — and the reader — with the inescapable reality of mortality, launching the king on his failed quest for eternal life. In the final tablet, Gilgamesh summons Enkidu's shade from the underworld to learn the fate of the dead; the grim description of the afterlife confirms that the true immortality Enkidu achieved is not through continued existence but through the love and memory of his friend — a conclusion that anticipates the humanist wisdom of later ages. Enkidu is literature's first great companion character, and his arc from wild innocence through human love to tragic death defines the epic's emotional and philosophical core.

Form

Enkidu is created from clay by the goddess Aruru, who shapes him in the image of the sky-god Anu. He is described as a 'wild man' with hair covering his body, long locks like a woman's, and a body covered in matted fur. He wears no clothes, grazes with the gazelles, and drinks from the watering holes with the wild beasts. He is immensely strong, capable of wrestling a lion and overpowering the wild animals of the steppe. In later iconography, he is depicted as a hairy, muscular man with bull-like features, sometimes with horns or a tail, but the texts are clear that he is human in form, not a bull-man.

Powers

Superhuman Strength Enkidu is created as an equal to Gilgamesh, 'strong as a rock from the sky' (Andrew George translation). He wrestles Gilgamesh to a draw, and later helps him defeat the Bull of Heaven and Humbaba. Standard Babylonian Epic, Tablets I–VI His strength is matched only by Gilgamesh, and he is mortal. None significant.

Animal Kinship Before his taming, Enkidu lives with wild animals, and they trust him. He can run with gazelles and drink at their watering holes. This ability is lost after his sexual encounter with Shamhat, as the animals flee him. Standard Babylonian Epic, Tablet I Lost permanently after civilization. None.

Combat Prowess Enkidu is a formidable warrior, skilled in hand-to-hand combat and with weapons (he learns to use a sword and bow after becoming civilized). He slays the Bull of Heaven and helps defeat Humbaba. Standard Babylonian Epic, Tablets II–VI He is mortal and can be wounded or killed. None.

Dream Interpretation (in some versions) In the Old Babylonian version, Enkidu interprets Gilgamesh's dreams as favorable omens. In the Standard version, Gilgamesh interprets his own dreams, but Enkidu offers his own interpretations, showing wisdom. Old Babylonian tablets; Standard Babylonian Epic, Tablet IV Not a formal power, but a sign of his acquired wisdom. Some scholars see this as a later addition.

Ghostly Return After his death, Enkidu's spirit is summoned by Gilgamesh from the Underworld, and he describes the bleak conditions of the dead. Standard Babylonian Epic, Tablet XII (adapted from Sumerian poem 'Gilgamesh and the Netherworld') He is a ghost, not a living being. The Tablet XII is a later addition, and some scholars argue it is not original to the epic.

Behavior & Habitat

Initially, Enkidu lives entirely in the wilderness, unaware of human civilization, and acts as a protector of the wild animals, thwarting the huntsman's traps. He is innocent, uncultured, and driven by instinct. After his sexual encounter with the harlot Shamhat, he is 'tamed': the animals flee him, and he gains understanding and reason. He then journeys to Uruk, where he challenges Gilgamesh to a contest of strength. After their fight, they become inseparable companions. Enkidu is loyal, courageous, and deeply emotional—he mourns the death of the animals he once protected, and his own death scene is one of the most poignant in ancient literature. He curses the harlot who civilized him, then retracts the curse after the sun-god Shamash reminds him of the benefits of civilization.

Accounts

The gods create Enkidu in response to the prayers of the people of Uruk, who are oppressed by Gilgamesh's arrogance. Aruru, the mother goddess, shapes him from clay in the image of Anu. He is a wild man, living with animals, and he destroys the traps of the huntsman, who then complains to Gilgamesh. Gilgamesh sends the harlot Shamhat to seduce Enkidu, which tames him and alienates him from the animals. He then goes to Uruk, where he challenges Gilgamesh.

Enkidu arrives in Uruk and blocks the door of the temple of the goddess Ishtar, preventing Gilgamesh from exercising his 'right of the first night' (a custom that is actually not attested in the epic, but is a modern interpretation). The two wrestle, and Enkidu holds his own, but eventually acknowledges Gilgamesh's superiority. They embrace and become friends.

Gilgamesh proposes they journey to the Cedar Forest to slay the monstrous guardian Humbaba, to gain fame and establish their names. Enkidu, who knows Humbaba's power, tries to dissuade him, but ultimately accompanies him. They receive the blessing of the sun-god Shamash, who provides them with winds and weapons. They defeat Humbaba, who begs for mercy, but Gilgamesh kills him (in some versions, Enkidu urges Gilgamesh to kill him).

The goddess Ishtar, rejected by Gilgamesh, sends the Bull of Heaven to punish Uruk. Gilgamesh and Enkidu together slay the Bull, and Enkidu taunts Ishtar, throwing the bull's thigh at her.

Enkidu falls ill after the slaying of the Bull of Heaven, afflicted by a disease sent by the gods. He curses the harlot Shamhat and the huntsman, but Shamash rebukes him, reminding him of the good things he has gained: the love of Gilgamesh, fine food and clothing, and a proper burial. Enkidu retracts his curse and dies, lamenting his fate.

Gilgamesh, seeking to bring Enkidu back, breaks a ritual taboo, and Enkidu's ghost is allowed to rise from the Underworld. He describes the fate of the dead, showing that the afterlife is bleak and joyless.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Enkidu as the original 'nature vs. nurture' experiment: a being created to be a mirror of a king, but who becomes more human than the king himself. His arc from wild animal to civilized companion to tragic ghost is a timeless story about the cost of civilization and the value of friendship.
