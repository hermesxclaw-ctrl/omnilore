Who It Is

Before the Pawnee people planted a single kernel of corn, they first had to make peace with their mother. Her name was Atira, and she was not a distant goddess in the sky, but the very soil beneath their feet, the green shoots in their fields, and the golden ears that sustained their lives. She was the wife of the sky god Tirawa, but her power was far more intimate—the power of the earth to bring forth life, to nourish, and to protect. To understand Atira is to understand a world where the sacred was not separate from the everyday act of eating, and where the most profound cosmic forces were as close as one's own hearth.

Atira is a benevolent, nurturing, and life-giving force. She is the mother who provides for her children, the Pawnee people. She is associated with the home, the hearth, and the family. Her personality is one of steadfast care and generosity, but she also demands respect and proper ritual observance in return for her gifts.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name is of Pawnee origin. In the Pawnee language, 'Atira' translates to 'Mother Corn' or 'Corn Mother.' The name is a compound of 'ati' (mother) and 'ra' (corn). It is a title of deep respect, personifying the life-giving power of the earth as embodied in the staple crop.

Pronunciation — ah-TEE-rah (most common scholarly rendering). Some oral traditions may stress the first syllable, 'AH-ti-rah.'

Aliases — Atira (Pawnee), A-ti-ra, Ati-ra, Mother Earth (as a title)

Ownership — Pawnee Nation (Chatiks si chatiks - 'Men of Men'). The Pawnee are a Caddoan-speaking people indigenous to the Great Plains, historically centered in what is now Nebraska and Kansas

Powers
Life-Giving Sustenance

Atira provides the primary food source for the Pawnee people. She is the power behind the growth of corn, ensuring the survival of the tribe through her bounty.

Limit: Her bounty is not unconditional. It requires proper ritual observance, hard work, and respect.

Fertility of the Earth

As Mother Earth, she is the source of all agricultural and, by extension, all terrestrial life. She makes the land fruitful.

Limit: Her fertility is tied to the celestial will of Tirawa, her husband.

Nurturing and Protection

She is the protector of the home, the family, and the community. She provides a spiritual and physical sanctuary for her children.

Limit: Her protection is for those who honor her.

Intercession with Tirawa

As the wife of Tirawa, the supreme creator, Atira can act as an intercessor for the people. The Hako ceremony is a means of seeking her aid and, through her, the blessings of Tirawa.

Limit: The ceremony must be performed correctly by a qualified priest.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Atira is not described in human or animal form in the primary sources. She is an anthropomorphic principle embodied in the corn plant itself. Her 'appearance' is the field of growing corn, the ripened ears, and the kernels that sustain life. She is the earth, the soil, and the nourishing power within it.

Symbols — Corn (maize) is her primary symbol. The ear of corn is a sacred object, often used in rituals. The earth itself is her symbol. The Hako ceremony's sacred bundle contains a corn stalk, representing her. The colors associated with her are those of the earth and corn: browns, greens, and yellows.

Domains — Her primary domain is the earth, specifically the agricultural land. She is the Mother Earth, but more specifically, she is the Corn Mother. Her 'habitat' is the cultivated field, the garden, and the home where corn is stored and prepared. She is also a central figure in the cosmos, dwelling in the west, the direction associated with the home and the evening.

Accounts I
The Creation of the Universe and the Role of AtiraDorsey, 'Traditions of the Skidi Pawnee'

Recorded 1904; ancient oral tradition

In the beginning, Tirawa, the Sky God, existed alone. He created the celestial beings and then the earth. To rule the earth and give it life, he created a wife for himself, Atira, the Earth Mother. He placed her in the west, the direction of the home, and gave her the power of fertility. Their union, the sky above and the earth below, made life possible. The creation of the world and the establishment of the cosmic order. Atira becomes the mother of all living things on earth.

Accounts II
The Hako CeremonyFletcher, 'The Hako: A Pawnee Ceremony'

Recorded 1904; ancient oral tradition

The Hako is the most sacred Pawnee ceremony, a complex ritual of peace, friendship, and cosmic renewal. It is a journey of a 'father' party to a 'son' party, seeking to bind them in a sacred relationship. Atira is central to this ceremony. The sacred bundle contains a corn stalk, which is her symbol, and the songs and prayers invoke her blessings. The ceremony is a prayer for the well-being of children, the fertility of the earth, and the continuation of life. The renewal of social bonds, the blessing of the people, and the maintenance of harmony between the human and celestial realms.

The Planting CeremonyMurie, 'Ceremonies of the Pawnee'

Recorded early 20th century; ancient oral tradition

Before planting, the Pawnee held ceremonies to honor Atira and ask for her blessing. The first ears of corn from the harvest were offered back to her. The corn was treated with great respect, as it was the body of the Mother. A successful growing season and the continued favor of the Earth Mother.

The Power of Atira vs. TirawaTirawa is the supreme, all-powerful creator, and Atira is a lesser, albeit important, deity subordinate to him.Atira is an equal partner to Tirawa, representing a necessary and complementary force. The universe cannot function without both.

Atira is just a generic 'Mother Earth' goddess, similar to those of other cultures. — While she is an Earth Mother, she is specifically the Corn Mother. Her identity is deeply tied to the agricultural cycle of the Pawnee and the specific plant of corn. She is not a generic fertility figure.

Atira is a passive figure, simply a vessel for Tirawa's creative power. — Atira is an active and powerful deity. She is the direct source of life and sustenance. The Hako ceremony is a complex ritual that seeks her active intervention for the well-being of the people. She is not passive; she is the nurturing, life-giving force of the world.

The Pawnee religion was centered on violent sacrifice. — While the Morning Star ceremony involved human sacrifice, it was not the central or most common practice. The religion was overwhelmingly focused on agricultural fertility, cosmic harmony, and social well-being, as exemplified by the peaceful and complex Hako ceremony, which honors Atira and Tirawa.
