This page is intentionally blank and ready for source-backed lore, versions, images, and connections. Likely a healing and/or beneficent helper-goddess of the Romano-Germanic Lower Rhineland. Depending on the preferred etymology her domain is (1) healing / making-well (Neumann), (2) universal divine giving / all-blessing (the Ala- prefix and Scardigli's nomen-agentis reading), or (3) a supreme-universal mother figure comparable to Frija/Frigg (de Vries). All domains are inferred from a single inscription and the name; none is documented independently.

Form

No depiction of Alateivia survives. The single votive altar bears only an inscription; there is no figural relief, image, or attribute that would allow a reconstruction of her appearance. If she was conceived in the manner of the Lower-Rhine Matronae (three seated mother-goddesses with fruit and bread), that is an inference from her possible association with that class and is not documented for her specifically.

Powers

Healing / making-well (inferred from the *tew- etymology and the stonemot medicus dedicator) If Günter Neumann's governing-compound reading is accepted, Alateivia is a healing goddess whose very name expresses the act of 'making whole / making ready' — the name bearing a verbal stem (Gothic taujan, gataujan; OHG zouwen) that denotes to do, to work, to make ready. The dedicator of her only altar was a physician (medicus) named Divos, and the stone was set up ex iussu ('on the goddess's command'), which strongly suggests a therapeutic context: a sick person was healed, the physician acted at the god's direction, and the recovery was commemorated on the votive altar. Etymology (Neumann 1987/2008); the single Xanten inscription and the medicus dedicant The therapeutic role is inferential — name reading plus the doctor dedicant — and is explicitly rejected a priori by Jan de Vries, who calls any link between the dedicant's profession and the goddess's function 'purely hypothetical'. No miracle, healing narrative, or cult practice is independently attested. The healing reading stands or falls with the *tew- etymology. The rival *deiwo- reading ('all-goddess') gives no specific healing function and would instead make her a universal beneficent power. De Vries rejected the healing link outright; Neumann defended it.

Universal divine giving / beneficence (from the Ala- prefix, 'all-giving') The initial element Ala- (archaicized Alla-, 'all, entire, complete') recurs in a family of Romano-Germanic goddess names — Alagabiae, Alaisiagae, Alaferhviae, Alaterviae — with a blessing- or bestowing-granting function. Piergiuseppe Scardigli read these Ala- names as nomina agentis and interpreted the Alateiviae, with reservation, as '(the goddess/matrons) bestowing all divine things / all-goddess'. This yields a beneficent, all-giving divine figure rather than a narrowly medical one. Theonym morphology (Scardigli 1989; comparisons in the Alaferhviae literature) The 'all-blessing' reading is morphological and comparative, not backed by any narrative. Whether she bestows 'all', or all of a specific good (healing, life, protection), is not determinable from the single text. Competes with and contradicts the narrowly medical reading; also unsettles whether she is a plural matronal group (Alateiviae, plural -ae) or a single goddess (Alateivia).

Supreme / universal motherhood (de Vries's cautious Frija-comparison) Jan de Vries tentatively interpreted Alateivia as a universal or main goddess and compared or equated her with the Germanic goddess Frija (Frigg). On this reading the 'Ala-' element and the -teivia ('divine') element combine into a supreme female deity whose cult, while attested only at Xanten, points to a grander pan-Germanic conception of a mother-goddess of all. Jan de Vries, Altgermanische Religionsgeschichte vol. 2 (1970), p. 318 This is the most speculative of the three readings: it extrapolates a supreme goddess from a single provincial votive stone. De Vries himself offered it with caution ('vorsichtig'), and Neumann's healing reading is generally preferred in more recent scholarship. The Frija equation is a comparison, not a demonstrated identity. Directly contradicts Neumann's localization of her as a non-Matronal helper/situational deity on the Lower Rhine, and contradicts the minimal-evidence reading of her as merely a local healing numen.

Behavior & Habitat

Alateivia's sphere of action is inferred from (a) the ex iussu revelation formula of her inscription and (b) the identity of her single attested dedicator, a physician (medicus) named Divos. If Günter Neumann's 'the completely healing one' (*tew- / 'to make ready, to work') etymology is correct, she is a healing helper-goddess invoked by a sick person's doctor in a moment of illness, the dedication recording the god's directive that the physician act. If the alternative 'all-goddess' reading is preferred, she functions as a universal beneficent diva. Either way the epigraphically grounded picture is of a local divine power who directed and supported a private act of healing or deliverance, receiving a small thank-offering altar in return.

Accounts

A physician (medicus) named Divos erected a small stone altar — about 33 by 22 cm, with a plain base, an inscribed plate and a crown, and an unfinished back — in obedience to a direct command of the goddess Alateivia (ex iussu). Because the text is cast as a revelation- or instruction-inscription (Offenbarungs-Inschrift), its structure dramatizes the classic Romano-Germanic votive transaction: a person in crisis or in need (here plausibly a patient, or the physician himself) prayed or consulted the goddess; the goddess answered, directing the medicus to act; the dedicator carried out the instruction and paid his vow with the stone. Whoever the beneficiary, the altar is the fossil of a healed transaction — a divine command honoured with a thank-offering.

In 1822 the small Alateivia altar was discovered in a garden before the Clever Tor (Cleves Gate) at Xanten, on the ground of the medieval town that had grown up over the abandoned Roman colonia. It first passed into the hands of a local parish priest, Spenrath; after his death it entered the collection of Philipp Houben; and on Houben's death the stone came to the Bonner Provinzialmuseum (the Rhineland's principal antiquities collection). Its journey from a Xanten garden, through two private hands, into a public provincial museum encapsulates how Romano-Germanic stone inscriptions from the Lower Rhine survived the centuries and entered modern scholarship.

The name Alateivia has been fought over by the leading scholars of early Germanic religion as a test case in how much may be recovered from a single theonym. Gutenbrunner set the terms in 1936. De Vries read the -teivia as 'divine' and cautiously elevated her to a universal goddess comparable to Frija. Scardigli preferred a nomen-agentis reading of the Ala- names and saw the Alateiviae as 'the all-divine-bestowing' goddesses. Neumann then shifted the ground: noting that Germanic ī had already absorbed IE ei by the altar's date, he dismissed the 'god' root and instead derived -teivia from a 'to do / to make ready' verbal stem, renaming her 'the completely healing one' and locating her — not among the Matronae nor as a supreme goddess — but among the Lower-Rhine helper/situational goddesses. The debate shows how a name with two vowels (the -ei- ligature) can decide a goddess's entire reconstructed role.

What We Don't Know

The evidence base for Alateivia is a single votive inscription, so the gaps are intrinsic and should be stated plainly. (1) I could not retrieve the verbatim text or a full scholarly edition of the inscription (the Clauss-Slaby photograph is linked from the German Wikipedia but its transcription was not re-extracted here), so I rely on the German article's description of a clean five-line ex iussu text with a TE ligature and a medicus named Divos. (2) The exact inventory/reference number of the stone in its current repository (Bonner Provinzialmuseum provenance; the museum is today the LVR-Landesmuseum Bonn) was not verified against an epigraphic catalogue number here, though the Clauss-Slaby and Heidelberg databases index it. (3) No German/scholarly primary text beyond the encyclopedic article was read directly; the interpretation rests on the secondary summaries (Gutenbrunner, de Vries, Neumann, Scardigli, Simek) as reported. (4) Whether Alateivia is plural (Alateiviae) or singular is not resolved by the edition I saw. (5) No reliable evidence was found for any modern artistic, literary, film, or game reception of Alateivia (she is too obscure); the modern reception is entirely scholarly/database. (6) The Frija and Týr/Mars Thingsus links are etymological inferences, not attested historical relationships, and are flagged as such. A German-language primary edition of the inscription and Neumann's 1987 essay are the narrowest targets for closing these gaps.

A way to hold it in your mind

Somewhere beneath the medieval streets of Xanten, in a garden that once stood before the Clever Tor, a small stone — thirty-three centimetres tall — was pulled from the ground in 1822. On it, in five lines of Latin, a Roman doctor named Divos recorded that he had acted on the direct command of a goddess called Alateivia. That stone, the whole of her life, is the quiet prayer of an entire small religion: no temples, no myths, no crowd of worshippers — just one physician who listened when a local god spoke, and carved the reply. Her name is a tiny fault-line in the history of words, because scholars cannot agree whether the -teivia in it means 'divine' — making her an All-Goddess, a kind of long-vanished Frigg — or 'to make whole', naming her instead the Healer of a single river bend. Either way she is the archive's reminder of how much of the ancient sacred world survives on a single inscribed sentence.
