Who It Is

In the bustling, polyglot streets of Roman Cologne, a nameless citizen once carved a name into stone: Alateivia. She was not Jupiter, not Mars, not a goddess of the imperial pantheon. She was something older, a whisper of a nourishing power from a world before Rome, a local 'Great Mother' who survived not in marble temples or epic poems, but only on a single, weather-worn altar. Her story is not one of mythic battles, but of quiet devotion — and of how an entire goddess, her myths and her magic, can vanish into the silence of history, leaving behind only her name and our questions.

Unknown. As a theonym only, her cultic behavior is not described. However, the nature of the votive inscriptions (ex voto) indicates she was a goddess to whom vows were made and from whom favors were sought, likely related to health, fertility, or protection, given the 'nourishing' etymology of her name.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The theonym Alateivia is of Celtic origin, though it appears in a Roman provincial context. The name is generally analyzed as a compound. The first element, *ala-, is widely attested in Celtic personal names and theonyms (e.g., Alator, Alaunus) and is typically linked to the Proto-Celtic root *al-o-

Powers
Nourishment and Fertility (Reconstructed)

The name 'Alateivia' strongly suggests a goddess of nourishment, abundance, and fertility. She may have been invoked to ensure good harvests, the health of livestock, and the well-being of children and mothers. This power is inferred from her name, not from any surviving myth.

Limit: Entirely unknown; no myths or legends define her limits.

Vow-Fulfillment (Attested)

The votive inscriptions demonstrate that she was believed to have the power to hear and answer prayers, and that dedicants believed they owed her a 'vow' (votum) in return for her aid. This is the only directly attested 'power'—the power to intervene in human affairs in response to prayer.

Limit: Unknown; the nature of the requests is not recorded.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No physical description of Alateivia survives in any text or artifact. She is known only from votive inscriptions. There are no surviving statues, reliefs, or coins depicting her. Any description of her appearance is purely speculative and must be labeled as such.

Symbols — No symbols are attested. The votive altar itself is the only symbol of her cult that survives.

Domains — Based on the etymology of her name ('Nourishing Goddess'), her domain is plausibly inferred to be fertility, nourishment, motherhood, and the protection of the household or tribe. This is a reconstruction, not an attested fact.

Accounts I
The Silent Cult of the RhinelandEpigraphic record (CIL XIII 8606, AE 1925, 0005)

2nd–3rd century CE

No narrative myths or legends about Alateivia survive. The only 'story' is the archaeological one: a local goddess, worshipped by a community in the Roman province of Germania Inferior, who received votive dedications from her followers. The dedicants, whose names are partially lost to time, set up stone altars to honor her and record their fulfilled vows. The cult appears to have been localized and did not spread beyond the Cologne region. There is no evidence of a temple, only of personal votive offerings. The cult left a small but permanent mark on the epigraphic record of the Roman Empire, providing modern scholars with a single, tantalizing glimpse of a local goddess whose mythology is forever lost.

Accounts II
Epigraphic Rediscovery in the Modern EraArchaeological record (CIL publication history; museum acquisition records)

19th–20th century CE

The second chapter in Alateivia's story is not ancient but modern: the discovery, publication, and study of her inscriptions by epigraphers. The CIL XIII volume, compiled under Theodor Mommsen and later editors, brought the Lower Rhine votive inscriptions to scholarly attention in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Later, the AE 1925, 0005 entry added a second confirmed inscription. Each discovery was a small event in the history of classical scholarship — a stone recognized, a name deciphered, a goddess briefly resurrected from oblivion. These modern 'discoveries' are the only reason Alateivia exists in any database today. Without these scholarly interventions, Alateivia would be completely unknown. The modern rediscovery of her inscriptions transformed a forgotten local goddess into a data point in the grand catalog of Roman religion — a transformation that is itself a kind of narrative, if not mythology.

Alateivia is a major, well-known goddess with a rich mythology. — She is an extremely obscure deity known only from two votive inscriptions. She has no surviving mythology, no temples, and no images. She is a name and a cultic practice, nothing more.

She is a Germanic goddess. — The linguistic evidence points more strongly to a Celtic origin for her name, despite the find-spot in a province with Germanic populations.

She is a 'mother goddess' like the Matronae. — This is a plausible but unproven conjecture. Her name suggests a nourishing function, but she is not attested as a Matrona, and her cult appears to have been distinct.
