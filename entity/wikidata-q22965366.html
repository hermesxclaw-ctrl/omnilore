Form

No physical description of Alateivia survives in any text or artifact. She is known only from votive inscriptions. There are no surviving statues, reliefs, or coins depicting her. Any description of her appearance is purely speculative and must be labeled as such.

Powers

Nourishment and Fertility (Reconstructed) The name 'Alateivia' strongly suggests a goddess of nourishment, abundance, and fertility. She may have been invoked to ensure good harvests, the health of livestock, and the well-being of children and mothers. This power is inferred from her name, not from any surviving myth. Reconstructed from theonymic analysis Entirely unknown; no myths or legends define her limits. None, as no other powers are attested.

Vow-Fulfillment (Attested) The votive inscriptions demonstrate that she was believed to have the power to hear and answer prayers, and that dedicants believed they owed her a 'vow' (votum) in return for her aid. This is the only directly attested 'power'—the power to intervene in human affairs in response to prayer. Attested in CIL XIII 8606 and AE 1925, 0005 Unknown; the nature of the requests is not recorded. None.

Behavior & Habitat

Unknown. As a theonym only, her cultic behavior is not described. However, the nature of the votive inscriptions (ex voto) indicates she was a goddess to whom vows were made and from whom favors were sought, likely related to health, fertility, or protection, given the 'nourishing' etymology of her name.

Accounts

No narrative myths or legends about Alateivia survive. The only 'story' is the archaeological one: a local goddess, worshipped by a community in the Roman province of Germania Inferior, who received votive dedications from her followers. The dedicants, whose names are partially lost to time, set up stone altars to honor her and record their fulfilled vows. The cult appears to have been localized and did not spread beyond the Cologne region. There is no evidence of a temple, only of personal votive offerings.

The second chapter in Alateivia's story is not ancient but modern: the discovery, publication, and study of her inscriptions by epigraphers. The CIL XIII volume, compiled under Theodor Mommsen and later editors, brought the Lower Rhine votive inscriptions to scholarly attention in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Later, the AE 1925, 0005 entry added a second confirmed inscription. Each discovery was a small event in the history of classical scholarship — a stone recognized, a name deciphered, a goddess briefly resurrected from oblivion. These modern 'discoveries' are the only reason Alateivia exists in any database today.

Content Notes

The very existence of Alateivia as a named deity is a result of Romanization. Her original, pre-Roman cult was likely oral and left no written record. The Roman practice of inscribing votive offerings is the only reason we know her name. The process of Romanization often involved the gradual erosion of indigenous languages and traditions. Alateivia's name, though Celtic, was preserved only because it was rendered in Latin letters on a Roman-style altar. The full scope of her original cult—her myths, her rituals, her priests—was lost, likely as a direct result of cultural assimilation and the passage of time.

This is a general phenomenon of the Roman Empire, not a specific act of violence against this goddess. The loss of her mythology is a form of cultural erasure, but it is not a story of active suppression or persecution.

Scholarly Debates
Ethnic origin: Celtic vs. Germanic
Celtic origin: The name is best explained by Celtic etymology (*ala- 'nourish' + *dēwā 'goddess'). ⇌ Germanic origin: The find-spot in Germania Inferior suggests a possible Germanic goddess, and some scholars argue for a Germanic etymology of the name.
Evidence for A: Linguistic analysis of the theonym's components points to well-attested Celtic roots.
Evidence for B: Geographic context of the find; the province was home to both Germanic and Celtic populations.

A way to hold it in your mind

Alateivia is the ancient equivalent of a small, local patron saint — not a globally famous figure, but a deeply important one to a single community. Think of her as a beloved, local folk healer or a patron of a small village's harvest festival. She was the goddess you prayed to for a healthy baby or a good crop, not for victory in war. Her entire 'fandom' was a handful of people in one corner of the Roman Empire, and all we have left of her cult is a couple of 'thank you' notes carved in stone.
