Who It Is

The barghest is the north of England's answer to Black Shuck, and the crueler sibling: a monstrous black dog with huge teeth and claws and burning eyes, haunting the moors, wooded glens, and above all the snickelways — the medieval alleys — of the city of York. Where Shuck will sometimes walk a traveler safely home, the barghest keeps a stricter ledger: it appears only as a death-omen, and it observes rank. When a notable person of the district is about to die, the barghest leads a procession through the streets — all the dogs of the neighborhood following it, howling in chorus — a funeral parade that precedes the funeral. Those who glimpse it clearly die soon; those who catch only a partial look survive with a wound that never fully heals. It cannot cross the running water of the Ure, and travelers planned their night crossings accordingly. The barghest is also a shapeshifter in the older tellings — appearing as a headless man, a white cat, a rabbit, a black dog the size of a bear — but the dog-form owned the terror, and lent its name to goblin-dogs in fantasy literature ever after.

Death & Rising

Northern English black-dog tradition, possibly from burh-ghest — town ghost — or German berg-geist, mountain spirit. Strongest around York, Whitby, and the Trollers Gill ravine, where the barghest was said to lair.

Attested in Yorkshire oral traditionEra Recorded 18th-19th century; tradition older

Form

A great black hound with fire-coal eyes, oversized fangs and claws, sometimes chained (the sound of dragging chains announces it), sometimes shifting to headless human or pale animal forms.

Powers

Death-omen appearance ranked by the victim's prominence; the canine funeral procession; shapeshifting; wounds that never heal from its claws.

Weaknesses & Wards

Running water — it cannot cross the River Ure; and like most of its family it is bound to its district, a horror with a home range.

Field Notes

† York ghost-walk guides still route tourists through the snickelways with barghest tales.

† The dogs-of-the-town funeral procession is unique to the barghest among British black dogs.

† A local ballad blames the death of a Trollers Gill nightwalker on meeting the barghest at the ravine mouth.

Cultural Significance

The barghest gave its name to the goblin-hounds of Dungeons and Dragons and countless fantasy bestiaries, and Trollers Gill in the Yorkshire Dales still trades on its legend.
