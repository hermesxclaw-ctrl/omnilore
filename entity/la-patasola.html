Archive Dossier — Identity

La Patasola

Pronunciation lah pah-tah-SOH-lah

Known As

La Pata SolaThe One-Foot

Etymology

From Spanish 'pata' (leg or foot of an animal) and 'sola' (alone), literally 'the one-footed one'.

Cultural Ownership

Colombian folklore, particularly Andean and Amazonian regions; also present in Venezuelan and Ecuadorian variants.

Disputed Identifications

Some sources conflate her with the Madremonte (Mother of the Forest) or the Tunda (a similar one-legged spirit in Pacific coastal folklore). She is sometimes seen as a type of 'Tunda' but has distinct traits.

Archive Dossier — Approach

Deep in the Colombian Andes, where the mist clings to the coffee slopes and the rivers run cold, there is a sound that makes even the boldest hunter freeze: the uneven thump of a single foot on the forest floor. She is La Patasola, the One-Foot, a spirit of the wild that punishes those who break the forest's laws — and lures the unfaithful to their doom.

La Patasola is the guardian-horror of the Colombian wilderness: a woman with a single leg ending in a cloven hoof, who appears to men who go too deep into the forest — loggers, hunters, miners, herders straying past the edge of what is theirs. She begins as whatever the man most wants to see: a beautiful woman, a lost lover, sometimes the exact face of his wife, calling from just beyond the firelight.

Who It Is

La Patasola is the guardian-horror of the Colombian wilderness: a woman with a single leg ending in a cloven hoof, who appears to men who go too deep into the forest — loggers, hunters, miners, herders straying past the edge of what is theirs. She begins as whatever the man most wants to see: a beautiful woman, a lost lover, sometimes the exact face of his wife, calling from just beyond the firelight. Follow her and the forest closes like a trap: paths vanish, sounds turn, and the beautiful woman turns too — into a one-legged hag with bulging eyes, jaguar fangs, and a hunger for blood and flesh. In the oldest tellings she was a woman punished for betrayal — her leg cut off with an axe, in some versions by a husband who found her with another man — and her rage against men has been feeding ever since. She hops with impossible speed on her single leg, cries like a lost child or a wailing woman to bait rescuers, and sucks the blood of those she catches. But she is also, unmistakably, the jungle's own immune system: she attacks those who cut, burn, hunt, and take too much. Woodsmen's protections are practical: axes and machetes left crossed, tobacco smoke, dogs — and the surest one, staying out of the deep forest at night, which was always the point.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

La Patasola is typically described as a beautiful woman from the front, but with a single leg ending in a hoof or a twisted foot. Her eyes are often said to be glowing or hollow. She may appear as a seductive woman in white or rags, sometimes with wild hair. From behind, she is hideous, with a gaping mouth or no face.

Symbols & Iconography

The single foot is her defining symbol, representing her liminal nature between human and animal. She is associated with the forest, rivers, and caves. Some depictions show her with a machete or a child in her arms, but these are less consistent.

Behaviour & Personality

La Patasola is a vengeful spirit who punishes those who harm the forest (hunters, loggers, miners) and those who betray romantic partners. She is a seductress who lures unfaithful men into the wild, then reveals her true form and kills them. She also protects wild animals and may attack those who mistreat them. She is capricious and can be appeased with offerings.

Domains & Habitats

Deep forests, mountains, rivers, and caves in Colombia, especially in the Andean coffee region and the Amazon. She is said to appear near water sources and in isolated trails.

Version Differences

In some regions, she is a forest guardian similar to the Madremonte; in others, she is purely a malevolent seductress. Some versions say she was once a woman cursed for killing her unfaithful lover, others that she is a demon. In the Pacific coast, she is sometimes identified with the Tunda, who has a similar one-legged appearance but is more associated with children.

Origins

Andean and inland Colombian oral tradition, one of the great female forest-avengers of Latin America alongside Venezuela's La Sayona and Brazil's Curupira — colonial-era guilt and frontier fear given one terrible leg.

Shapeshifting

Source Version General Colombian folklore

Can appear as a beautiful woman to lure victims, or as a familiar person (spouse, friend) to deceive.

Limitations

Cannot maintain the illusion for long; the single foot is always visible if observed closely.

Contradictions

Some versions say she can also transform into animals like a pig or a dog.

Superhuman strength and speed

Source Version General

Description

Able to outrun and overpower her victims, despite having one leg.

Limitations

None specified.

Contradictions

None.

Illusion and disorientation

Source Version General

Description

Can make her victims lose their way in the forest, causing them to wander in circles.

Limitations

May be countered by prayer or carrying certain amulets (e.g., a knife, tobacco).

Contradictions

Some say she only attacks those who are already lost or who have broken a taboo.

Form

First a beauty, then the truth: one leg, hoofed, wild hair, fanged mouth, eyes like a beast at torchlight; she hops but is never slow.

Powers

Illusion and mimicry — faces, voices, a child's cry; forest-warping so trails betray; blood-drinking; sovereign speed in terrain where two-legged pursuit fails.

Weaknesses & Wards

Crossed axes and machetes repel her; tobacco and dogs unsettle her; the boundary of the wild — she has no power in the cleared, tended field.

Archive Dossier — Relationships

Madremonte

Type kindredSource Folklore studies

Description

Both are female forest spirits in Colombian folklore, often confused. Madremonte is more protective of the environment, while Patasola is more vengeful and seductive.

Version Note

Some say they are the same entity in different aspects.

Conflicting Accounts

Some regions treat them as separate, others as one.

Curupira

Type kindredSource Comparative folklore

Description

A Brazilian forest spirit with backward feet, also a protector of animals and punisher of hunters. Shares the motif of reversed or single feet.

Version Note

Both are part of a wider South American tradition of one-legged or backward-footed spirits.

Conflicting Accounts

No direct conflict, but some scholars see them as regional variants of a common archetype.

Saci

Type kindredSource Comparative folklore

Description

A Brazilian one-legged trickster spirit. Shares the single-leg motif but is more mischievous than malevolent.

Version Note

Likely a cultural cousin, but with different functions.

Conflicting Accounts

No direct connection, but the one-legged trait is notable.

Archive Dossier — Stories

The Unfaithful Husband

Source Collected in Colombian folklore anthologiesCulture ColombianPeriod 20th century

Summary

A man who is unfaithful to his wife meets a beautiful woman in the forest. She leads him deep into the woods, then reveals her single hoof and attacks him. He dies, and his body is found torn apart. The story warns against infidelity and entering the forest with bad intentions.

Participants

La Patasola

Unfaithful man

Consequences

Death of the man; the community recognizes her work.

Variants

In some versions, the man escapes if he repents or if he is not truly evil.

The Hunter's Punishment

Source Oral traditionCulture ColombianPeriod 20th century

Summary

A hunter who kills more than he needs is confronted by La Patasola. She appears as a beautiful woman and asks him to dance. As they dance, she leads him to a cliff, and he falls to his death. The story teaches respect for nature.

Participants

La Patasola

Hunter

Consequences

Death of the hunter; the forest remains protected.

Variants

Sometimes she simply scares the hunter and takes his catch.
