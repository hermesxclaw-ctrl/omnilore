Who It Is

Champ, the legendary lake monster of Lake Champlain, is North America's most famous aquatic cryptid after the Loch Ness Monster. The 125-mile-long lake, straddling Vermont, New York, and Quebec, has generated hundreds of sightings of a large serpentine creature. The Abenaki people called the creature 'Tatoskok' and warned of a great horned serpent inhabiting the lake. The modern era of Champ sightings began in 1819 when settlers near Port Henry, New York reported a 'black serpentine monster' approximately 187 feet long. The most famous evidence is the 1977 'Mansi photograph' -- taken by Sandra Mansi showing what appears to be a long-necked creature with a humped back rising from the water. The photo has withstood basic scrutiny with no evidence of manipulation, though skeptics note it could be a floating log or bird. In 2005, two fishermen captured video footage of something large moving beneath the water's surface. Possible explanations include a surviving plesiosaur population, a giant sturgeon or gar, a long-necked seal, or misidentified waves, logs, and bird flocks. Lake Champlain's depth (up to 400 feet) and connection to the Atlantic via the Richelieu and St. Lawrence Rivers provide a plausible pathway for a marine creature to have become landlocked. Vermont and New York have both passed resolutions protecting Champ.
