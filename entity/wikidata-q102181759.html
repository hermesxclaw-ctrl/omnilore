Who It Is

She saw the future with perfect clarity—and no one believed a word she said. Cassandra of Troy, cursed by Apollo, has haunted the Western imagination for nearly three thousand years. But who was she before the curse? And why does her story still resonate today?

Cassandra is portrayed as intelligent, passionate, and tormented. She speaks truth but is doomed to be disbelieved. In Aeschylus, she is shown as a frantic, visionary figure who sees the past and future clearly but is powerless to avert tragedy.

Prophecy

The ability to see future events accurately. She foretold the fall of Troy, the death of Agamemnon, and her own fate.

Limit: Her prophecies are never believed by others, making them useless in preventing disaster.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In classical art, Cassandra is often depicted as a young woman with dark hair, sometimes wearing a prophet's headband or holding a laurel branch. She is frequently shown in scenes of supplication or seizure.

Symbols — Associated with the laurel (Apollo's sacred plant), the tripod, and sometimes the raven. In vase paintings, she is often seen clinging to the Palladium (a statue of Athena) for protection.

Domains — Troy, the royal palace, the temple of Apollo. After the fall of Troy, she is taken to Mycenae.

Accounts I
The Curse of ApolloAeschylus's Agamemnon, Apollodorus

Classical antiquity

Apollo desired Cassandra and granted her the gift of prophecy. When she refused his advances, he cursed her so that no one would believe her prophecies. Cassandra's warnings about the Trojan Horse and other dangers are ignored, leading to the fall of Troy.

Accounts II
The Fall of TroyHomer's Iliad, Virgil's Aeneid

8th century BCE - 1st century BCE

Cassandra warns the Trojans not to trust the Greek gift of the wooden horse, but she is not believed. During the sack of Troy, she seeks refuge in the temple of Athena, where she is raped by Ajax the Lesser. Ajax's sacrilege angers Athena, who punishes the Greeks on their journey home. Cassandra is taken as a war prize by Agamemnon.

Cassandra in MycenaeAeschylus's Agamemnon

5th century BCE

Cassandra arrives in Mycenae with Agamemnon. She prophesies their murder at the hands of Clytemnestra and Aegisthus, but again is not believed. She enters the palace knowing she will die. Both Agamemnon and Cassandra are murdered. Cassandra's death is seen as just punishment for the house of Atreus.

Cassandra was always a prophetess in Greek mythology. — Her prophetic ability and the curse are not mentioned in Homer; they appear in later sources, particularly the tragedians.

Cassandra was a priestess of Apollo. — She is often associated with Apollo, but her role as a priestess is not explicitly stated in early sources.

Cassandra's warnings were ignored because of the curse. — In some versions, her warnings were ignored simply because people did not believe her, but the curse is the primary explanation in later sources.
