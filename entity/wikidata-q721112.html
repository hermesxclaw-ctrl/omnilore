Form

Asarualimnunna is a divine name and warlike epithet of the Mesopotamian incantation-god Asalluhi (also Asarluhi, Asalluhe), attested in Sumerian hymnic and Babylonian epic sources. It belongs to a tightly clustered family of names built on the ancient stem Asar — Asaru, Asarualim, Asarualimnunna, Asaruludu — which the Sumerian Temple Hymns apply to Asalluhi as epithets, and which the Standard Babylonian creation myth Enūma Eliš later folds, with the other absorbed Sumerian gods, into the fifty names of Marduk. The specific meaning encoded by the -ualimnunna element is bison imagery: 'Asar, the princely bison' or 'Asar, the bison of the prince', a martial, animal-strength register that Mari Johandi reads as evoking the young-warrior-god archetype best known from Ninurta. It is precisely this combative dimension that the Wikidata record captures when it classifies Q721112 as a Sumerian deity of war. Asarualimnunna is therefore not an independently worshipped separate god so much as the specialized warlike face of a deity whose larger career ran from third-millennium exorcist of Eridu and patron of Kuara, through an Old Babylonian syncretism with the rising city-god Marduk, to a permanent place among the fifty names of the Babylonian national god. Tracing the name requires holding three sources in view simultaneously: the Sumerian Temple Hymn that minted the bison epithet, the incantation corpus in which Asar/Asalluhi acts as Enki's son and senior divine exorcist, and the Enūma Eliš list that canonized the name as one of Marduk's titles. Each layer carries a slightly different theological job for the same glyphs.

Powers

Divine primacy in exorcism (Asalluhi as 'exorcist of the gods') Asar/Asalluhi, the god whose warlike name Asarualimnunna is, functioned as the supreme divine exorcist of the Mesopotamian pantheon. In texts outside the standard incantation formula he is described outright as an exorcist, even 'the exorcist of the gods', the figure to whom priestly incantations were ascribed as their true source.

The Asar-ualimnunna 'princely bison' war-strength The -ualimnunna element loads the name with the bison (alim) plus princely (nunna) register. In the Sumerian Temple Hymns this translates into aggressive, martial epithets for the deity: 'the strong prince', 'the hero', and imagery likening him to a leopard and a storm. Mari Johandi reads the cluster as evoking the young-warrior-god archetype exemplified by Ninurta — the face Wikidata codes as 'deity of war'.

Execution of Enki's ritual instructions (he is the doer, not the decider) In the Asalluhi-Enki incantation type, Asalluhi notices a malady, reports it to his father Enki, and receives precise instructions which he then executes. Enki assures his son that 'everything he knows, Asar knows too', yet Asalluhi consistently plays the executor: he lacks independent initiative and needs Enki's encouragement, so his power is delegated martial-and-ritual competence rather than sovereign wisdom.

Authority over purification and consecration Asalluhi features in consecration incantations that prepare objects and materials for ritual use, particularly those connected with purification. He is the god who recites the 'incantation of Eridu' in the Old Babylonian period, a role earlier assumed by the human priest.

Power to grant wisdom to kings (an Enki-traits transfer) In a couple of texts Asalluhi grants wisdom to Mesopotamian kings, taking over a role usually reserved for his father Enki. The sharing of traits is attributed to the father-son relationship.

Ambiguous power to 'cancel incantations' Asalluhi's implied ability to cancel incantations could point to a less benevolent side to the god, and is one of the markers that connects him to disease and plague (see dark history).

Modern Interpretations

Anthropological and Assyriological scholarship (Cunningham, Johandi, Lambert) The Asar/Asalluhi/Asaralimnuna relationship is a live question in modern Assyriology. Graham Cunningham argues Asar and Asalluhi may have originally been separate gods; Mari Johandi treats the separation as a way to distinguish Marduk from Asalluhi; Wilfred G. Lambert reads the Marduk absorption as political. No dedicated modern monograph treats Asarualimnunna alone, but the name recurs in every edition of the Enūma Eliš. Encyclopedic and reference treatment The Asar-name family appears in the standard god lists (An = Anum), in Assyriological dictionaries, and in Sumerian-literature reference works. Wikipedia treats the cluster under the Asalluhi article, which explicitly names Asaralimnuna as a principal epithet. Occult and esoteric revival The fifty names of Marduk, including the Asar-cluster, circulate in contemporary esoteric and neopagan literature, Anglophone devotional texts, and Babyloniam-theology readings. These are secondary and derivative — none treats Asarualimnunna with critical Assyriological rigor, and the accuracy varies from careful to fanciful. Modern fiction and games: deliberately NOT asserted An extensive Wikipedia-API search returned no verified standalone appearance of Asarualimnunna in film, games, anime or literature. What We Don't Know

No primary Sumerian or Akkadian text was accessed in the original cuneiform in this session; all content derives from English-language secondary scholarship and inline quotations. The precise ordinal position of Asarualimnunna in the canonical fifty-name list of Enūma Eliš remains unresolved across the consulted transcriptions and so is deliberately not asserted. No dedicated modern art-historical or iconographic study of the bison imagery of Asar/Asalluhi in Temple Hymns was located; the animal register is discussed only in passing in the Asalluhi article. The female-identification texts (one Mesopotamian, two Ugaritic) that scholars call 'scribal mistakes' have not been individually traced to their editions; needs the original publications to weigh the 'mistake' verdict. Modern reception could not be verified beyond reference works; a dedicated multilingual survey (German and French Assyriological literature, e.g. KAV, RLA entries) would be needed to confirm or rule out overlooked appearances.

A way to hold it in your mind

Long before the fifty names of Marduk made him a footnote in the Babylonian national myth, there was a princely bison. Asarualimnunna — 'Asar, the bison of the prince' — began as the warlike face of a Sumerian exorcist who reported to his father Enki when the plague came, and who ended up as one more title worn by the god who tore Tiamat apart.
