Who It Is

In the shadow of the Roman Empire, at the crossroads of the Moselle River, the Treveri people did not pray to Jupiter or Mars. They prayed to a goddess whose name is now a whisper on a broken stone: Ancamna, the 'very crooked one,' a local spirit of healing and the land, whose silent altars outlasted the legions that once marched over them.

As a local goddess, Ancamna's 'behavior' is inferred from her epithets and associations. Her pairing with Lenus Mars suggests a role as a consort or divine partner, possibly associated with healing, fertility, and the protection of the local community. She was likely a tutelary goddess of the Treveri tribe, watching over the springs, rivers, and the land itself. She was a receiving figure for votive offerings, a listener to prayers.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No definitive depiction of Ancamna survives. Unlike major Roman goddesses, she is not shown in a standard iconographic form on the surviving monuments. On the few altars where she is named, she is not depicted with unique attributes. It is likely she was a local, chthonic or nature spirit, and her image may have been aniconic or represented by a local symbol that has not survived. The only certain iconographic evidence is the pairing with Lenus M

Symbols — No specific symbols are attested. By extension from her association with Lenus Mars, she might have been associated with the raven (a bird sacred to Mars), but this is speculation. Her name, if derived from 'crooked,' might link her to winding rivers or serpents, but this is unverified. The lack of iconography is itself a key fact: she was a deity of the spoken word, of the vow and the offering, rather than of the grand image.

Domains — Likely domains include: healing, local springs and water sources, fertility of the land and people, protection of the Treveri tribe, and the chthonic realm. The exact scope is unknown, but her association with a major healing sanctuary (Altbachtal) and a healing god (Lenus Mars) strongly points to a therapeutic and protective function.

Accounts I
The Silent Vows of the TreveriReconstructed from votive inscriptions at the Altbachtal sanctuary.

2nd-3rd century CE

The people of Trier, the Treveri, would come to the sanctuary at Altbachtal to seek healing. They would offer small altars and inscriptions to Ancamna, often alongside her consort Lenus Mars. These were not grand myths, but personal stories of illness, hope, and gratitude. A soldier might vow an altar for his recovery from a wound; a mother might offer one for her child's fever. Ancamna was the listener, the silent recipient of these desperate prayers, her name etched in stone as a permanent record of a plea made in the living world. The sanctuary became a major religious center, and the inscriptions provide the only direct evidence we have of Ancamna's worship.

Ancamna was a major pan-Celtic goddess. — She was a highly local deity, only attested in a small area around Trier. She was not known across the Celtic world.

She was a river goddess. — This is a common assumption for Celtic goddesses, but there is no direct evidence for it. Her association was with the sanctuary, not a specific river.

She was a Roman goddess. — Her name is Celtic. She is a Gallo-Roman deity, representing a fusion of cultures, not a purely Roman import.

The exact nature of her powers is also speculative, as is her pre-Roman origin. The scarcity of inscriptions limits our understanding of her cult's rituals and social role.
