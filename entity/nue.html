Who It Is

There is a moment in the Tale of the Heike when a black cloud settles over the emperor's own roof, and from inside it comes a cry that is no bird, no beast, no human — 'something that cries with the voice of a nue, its true nature unknown.' Japan's chimera is the nightmare given anatomy: a monkey-headed, tiger-limbed, tanuki-bodied, serpent-tailed thing that exists in the space where the familiar refuses to stay recognizable. It began, scholars believe, as nothing more sinister than a real thrush singing after dark, whose sorrowful voice the Heian court took for an omen of imperial catastrophe — and a culture's dread assembled a monster around the sound, the way a shape assembles itself around a noise heard in the dark. Predominantly nocturnal, the nue is a creature of ill omen and wasting dread rather than overt violence. Its eerie 'hyoo hyoo' cry (resembling the scaly thrush) curdles courage and was taken in the Heian court to herald catastrophe; courtiers performed apotropaic prayers whenever the bird's voice was heard. It can shape-shift, famously into a flying black cloud. In the Ehime legend its motivation is recast: the nue was an aggrieved mother flying to test/mourn her son — far from a mindless omen. Powers
Shape-shifting / black cloud flight

The nue is said to be able to shape-shift, often into a black cloud that can fly. This is how it travels the night skies and veils the Seiryō-den in the Heike episode.

Limit: The cloud-form is the vehicle; the creature is still vulnerable to a well-aimed arrow, as the Yorimasa tale demonstrates.

The eerie cry ('hyoo hyoo') and fear-induction

The nue's cry resembles that of the scaly thrush and is described as 'terribly eerie.' It curdles courage in veteran soldiers and, in the court, was read as an omen of catastrophe that sickened Emperor Konoe/Nijō. Courtiers performed apotropaic rituals when the real bird's voice was heard.

Limit: The cry afflicts through dread and malady of spirit rather than physical attack; the narrative's resolution is a single arrow shot.

Infliction of nightmare / wasting illness

In the Heike, the emperor fell into illness that neither medicine nor prayers could cure, consequent to the nue's visit — a wasting brought by the omen-creature rather than a physical wound.

Limit: The malady lifted immediately once the nue was shot down, implying it was tied to the creature's presence.

Composite 'chimeric' anatomy

As Japan's chimera, the nue assembles the features of monkey, tiger, tanuki/raccoon dog, and snake (or, per Genpei Jōsuiki: tiger's back, tanuki's limbs, fox's tail, cat's head, chicken's torso). One argued reading ties the composite to the Sexagenary-cycle directions of the zodiac (northeast Tiger, southeast Snake, southwest Monkey, northwest Dog/Boar).

Limit: It is the unclassifiability itself — a body never cleanly one animal — that both frightens and defines it.

Postmortem transmutation (remains and spirit)

The nue's corpse is credited with posthumous agency: in one legend its spirit became the horse Kinoshita (stolen by Taira no Munemori, leading to Yorimasa's ruin); in another its remains carried a vengeful curse that tormented those who tore down the Nuezuka mound, and digging up a purported burial at Kiyomizu-dera in the Edo period brought a curse.

Limit: These are 'remains' legends about residual curse/spirit agency rather than powers of the living creature.

True-form concealment / unknowability

At its core the nue is defined by being of 'unknown true form' — in the Heike it is never named as such, only 'something that cries with the voice of a nue.' This extends the word as a class: anything whose true nature cannot be identified is called a nue.

Limit: This is a definitional/aesthetic power rather than an active combat ability, but it is why the nue defeats identification as a fixed beast.

Accounts I
The Slaying of the Nue (Heike Monogatari)Heike Monogatari; also Settsu Meisho Zue

Late Heian (the episode is set in the reign of Emperor Konoe, c. 1142–1155, or Nijō; the Tale dates to the 13th century)

In the closing years of the Heian period, a cloud of black smoke gathered over the Seiryō-den, the emperor's residence, accompanied by an eerie resounding cry that frightened Emperor Nijō (in some tellings Konoe). The emperor fell ill; neither medicine nor prayers availed. A close associate recalled that Minamoto no Yoshiie had once quelled a similar bird-cry mystery with an arrow, and the master archer Minamoto no Yorimasa was summoned. One night Yorimasa went out with his servant Ino Hayata, armed with an arrow made from an arrowhead he had inherited from his ancestor Minamoto no Yorimitsu and the tailfeathers of a mountain bird. As black smoke covered the palace, Yorimasa shot into it; there was a shriek, and a nue fell near Nijō Castle, where Ino Hayata seized and finished it off. Two or three cries of the common cuckoo were heard in the sky, signalling that peace had returned; the emperor's health instantly recovered, and Yorimasa was rewarded with the sword Shishiō ('King of Lions'). The Disposal of the Nue's Corpse (Nuezuka legends)Heike Monogatari; Settsu Meisho Zue; Ashiwake bune; Genpei Seisuiki; Kandenjihitsu

Edo period codification of medieval legend

Fearful of a curse, the people of Kyoto put the nue's corpse in a boat and set it adrift down the Kamo River. It floated down the Yodo River, briefly grounded on the Higashinari shore in Osaka, floated into the sea, and washed up between the Ashiya and Sumiyoshi rivers. The people of Ashiya courteously gave it a burial and raised a commemorative mound, the Nuezuka. The Edo geography Ashiwake bune records that when a nue drifted ashore on the Yodo, the villagers — fearing a curse — notified the abbot of Boon-ji, who mourned, buried it, and raised a mound. When that mound was torn down at the start of the Meiji period, the nue's vengeful spirit tormented nearby residents, and the mound had to be hastily rebuilt. Other traditions place a burial at Kiyomizu-dera in Kyoto (a curse attending the Edo-period digging) and scattered remains across the Lake Hamana region of Shizuoka, where place-names Nueshiro, Dozaki, Hanehira, and Ona recall the head, torso, wings, and tail. Accounts II
The Horse Kinoshita (the nue's revenge)regional legend (recounted in the nue tradition)

pre-modern

The spirit of the dead nue is said to have turned into a fine horse named Kinoshita, which Yorimasa raised. Because the horse was so good, it was stolen by Taira no Munemori; Yorimasa raised an army against the Taira over the theft, and this embroilment led to his ruin. The tale thus casts the nue as taking revenge on its slayer after death — the omen-creature engineered its own vengeance through a stolen horse. Yorimasa's downfall, read as the nue's posthumous triumph.

The Ehime Mother-Nue (Kumakōgen legend)regional legend (Kumakōgen, Kamiukena District, Ehime Prefecture)

pre-modern regional legend

In Ehime, the legend holds that the true identity of the nue was Yorimasa's own mother. In the era when the Taira clan was at its peak, Yorimasa's mother lived in hiding in her home district. At a pond called Azoga-ike within a mountain region, she prayed to the pond's guardian dragon for her son's good fortune in battle and the revival of the Genji (Minamoto clan). Her body transformed into a nue as a result of that prayer and her hatred of the Taira, and she flew toward Kyoto. As the nue that represented the mother, she made the emperor ill so that her own son could accomplish a triumph by slaying her. Pierced by Yorimasa's arrow, she flew back to Azoga-ike and became the pond's guardian, but died of her arrow wound. A poignant rereading of the same slaying as a mother sacrificing herself for her son's glory.

The Nue in Noh (Zeami's Nue)Noh play Nue, attributed to Zeami Motokiyo

Kiri-noh repertory, codified in the Muromachi period

Zeami (attributed) adapted the Heike slaying into a noh play, one of the standard demonplays (kiri-noh) performed as the fifth piece of a day's program. The play dramatizes the nue as a tsukimono (moon-spirit / omen-being), using the masked figure of the chimera in the palette of noh's numinous other-world. Its inclusion in the fixed noh repertory fixed the nue's place in the Japanese dramatic canon. Canonized the nue as a dramatic figure in the classical theater tradition.
