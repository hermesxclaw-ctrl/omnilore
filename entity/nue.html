Who It Is

The nue is Japan's chimera: the head of a monkey, the body of a tanuki, the limbs of a tiger, and a living snake for a tail, gliding through midnight skies inside a black cloud and crying with the voice of a scaly thrush — a sound so mournful the Heian court considered it an omen of catastrophe. Its most famous appearance is in the Tale of the Heike: in 1153, Emperor Konoe began suffering nightly terrors as a dark cloud settled over the palace roof at the hour of the ox. The samurai Minamoto no Yorimasa stood watch with his bow, fired a single arrow into the cloud, and the nue fell screaming from the sky. Its body was considered so cursed that the court sealed it in a boat and set it adrift rather than bury it in the capital's soil. The nue endures as Japan's emblem of the formless dread that visits the powerful at night — the nightmare given anatomy.

Origins

The word nue originally named a real bird — White's thrush — whose eerie night-cry was considered ill-omened as early as the Kojiki. The composite monster crystallized around that dreadful sound in Heian court literature, the way a shape assembles itself around a noise heard in the dark.

Form

Monkey-headed, tanuki-bodied, tiger-limbed, serpent-tailed, wreathed in black cloud. Some accounts insist it was never clearly seen at all — only the cry is certain.

Powers

Flight within a smoke-black cloud, infliction of nightmares and wasting illness on those it circles, and a cry that curdles courage in veteran soldiers.Shape-shifting / black cloud flight The nue is said to be able to shape-shift, often into a black cloud that can fly. This is how it travels the night skies and veils the Seiryō-den in the Heike episode. Heike Monogatari tradition and general yōkai folklore The cloud-form is the vehicle; the creature is still vulnerable to a well-aimed arrow, as the Yorimasa tale demonstrates. Some tellings emphasize the cloud as a veil around the true, never-seen body; others treat the cloud itself as a shape it adopts.The eerie cry ('hyoo hyoo') and fear-induction The nue's cry resembles that of the scaly thrush and is described as 'terribly eerie.' It curdles courage in veteran soldiers and, in the court, was read as an omen of catastrophe that sickened Emperor Konoe/Nijō. Courtiers performed apotropaic rituals when the real bird's voice was heard. Heike Monogatari; Heian court practice The cry afflicts through dread and malady of spirit rather than physical attack; the narrative's resolution is a single arrow shot. Whether the sickness is caused by the creature's presence, its cry, or the general ill-omen is left ambiguous across tellings.Infliction of nightmare / wasting illness In the Heike, the emperor fell into illness that neither medicine nor prayers could cure, consequent to the nue's visit — a wasting brought by the omen-creature rather than a physical wound. Heike Monogatari (Emperor Konoe/Nijō episode) The malady lifted immediately once the nue was shot down, implying it was tied to the creature's presence. Not all versions name the illness as specific; several only describe terror and the omen.Composite 'chimeric' anatomy As Japan's chimera, the nue assembles the features of monkey, tiger, tanuki/raccoon dog, and snake (or, per Genpei Jōsuiki: tiger's back, tanuki's limbs, fox's tail, cat's head, chicken's torso). One argued reading ties the composite to the Sexagenary-cycle directions of the zodiac (northeast Tiger, southeast Snake, southwest Monkey, northwest Dog/Boar). Different regions claim the far-flung disposal (Osaka/Ashiya mound vs Kyoto/Kiyomizu-dera vs Lake Hamana vs Ehime), implying the 'true' resting place is irrecoverable.True-form concealment / unknowability At its core the nue is defined by being of 'unknown true form' — in the Heike it is never named as such, only 'something that cries with the voice of a nue.' This extends the word as a class: anything whose true nature cannot be identified is called a nue. Tension between the 'never seen' framing and the elaborate composite descriptions that later tradition fixed upon it.

Weaknesses & Wards

A true arrow. Yorimasa's shot — guided, he said, by prayer to the war god Hachiman — remains the canonical remedy.

Accounts

In the closing years of the Heian period, a cloud of black smoke gathered over the Seiryō-den, the emperor's residence, accompanied by an eerie resounding cry that frightened Emperor Nijō (in some tellings Konoe). The emperor fell ill; neither medicine nor prayers availed. A close associate recalled that Minamoto no Yoshiie had once quelled a similar bird-cry mystery with an arrow, and the master archer Minamoto no Yorimasa was summoned. One night Yorimasa went out with his servant Ino Hayata, armed with an arrow made from an arrowhead he had inherited from his ancestor Minamoto no Yorimitsu and the tailfeathers of a mountain bird. As black smoke covered the palace, Yorimasa shot into it; there was a shriek, and a nue fell near Nijō Castle, where Ino Hayata seized and finished it off. Two or three cries of the common cuckoo were heard in the sky, signalling that peace had returned; the emperor's health instantly recovered, and Yorimasa was rewarded with the sword Shishiō ('King of Lions').

Fearful of a curse, the people of Kyoto put the nue's corpse in a boat and set it adrift down the Kamo River. It floated down the Yodo River, briefly grounded on the Higashinari shore in Osaka, floated into the sea, and washed up between the Ashiya and Sumiyoshi rivers. The people of Ashiya courteously gave it a burial and raised a commemorative mound, the Nuezuka. The Edo geography Ashiwake bune records that when a nue drifted ashore on the Yodo, the villagers — fearing a curse — notified the abbot of Boon-ji, who mourned, buried it, and raised a mound. When that mound was torn down at the start of the Meiji period, the nue's vengeful spirit tormented nearby residents, and the mound had to be hastily rebuilt. Other traditions place a burial at Kiyomizu-dera in Kyoto (a curse attending the Edo-period digging) and scattered remains across the Lake Hamana region of Shizuoka, where place-names Nueshiro, Dozaki, Hanehira, and Ona recall the head, torso, wings, and tail.

The spirit of the dead nue is said to have turned into a fine horse named Kinoshita, which Yorimasa raised. Because the horse was so good, it was stolen by Taira no Munemori; Yorimasa raised an army against the Taira over the theft, and this embroilment led to his ruin. The tale thus casts the nue as taking revenge on its slayer after death — the omen-creature engineered its own vengeance through a stolen horse.

In Ehime, the legend holds that the true identity of the nue was Yorimasa's own mother. In the era when the Taira clan was at its peak, Yorimasa's mother lived in hiding in her home district. At a pond called Azoga-ike within a mountain region, she prayed to the pond's guardian dragon for her son's good fortune in battle and the revival of the Genji (Minamoto clan). Her body transformed into a nue as a result of that prayer and her hatred of the Taira, and she flew toward Kyoto. As the nue that represented the mother, she made the emperor ill so that her own son could accomplish a triumph by slaying her. Pierced by Yorimasa's arrow, she flew back to Azoga-ike and became the pond's guardian, but died of her arrow wound.

Zeami (attributed) adapted the Heike slaying into a noh play, one of the standard demonplays (kiri-noh) performed as the fifth piece of a day's program. The play dramatizes the nue as a tsukimono (moon-spirit / omen-being), using the masked figure of the chimera in the palette of noh's numinous other-world. Its inclusion in the fixed noh repertory fixed the nue's place in the Japanese dramatic canon.

Cultural Significance

Nue-zuka (nue mounds) said to hold pieces of the creature's body still exist near Osaka and Kyoto, and locals have historically blamed disturbances of them for misfortune. The nue gave Japanese the adjective nue-teki — chimeric, unclassifiable — still used for politicians of shifting allegiance.

A way to hold it in your mind

There is a moment in the Tale of the Heike when a black cloud settles over the emperor's own roof, and from inside it comes a cry that is no bird, no beast, no human — 'something that cries with the voice of a nue, its true nature unknown.' Japan's chimera is the nightmare given anatomy: a monkey-headed, tiger-limbed, tanuki-bodied, serpent-tailed thing that exists in the space where the familiar refuses to stay recognizable. It began, scholars believe, as nothing more sinister than a real thrush singing after dark, whose sorrowful voice the Heian court took for an omen of imperial catastrophe — and a culture's dread assembled a monster around the sound, the way a shape assembles itself around a noise heard in the dark. When the archer Minamoto no Yorimasa finally shot it from the sky, he was rewarded with a sword that survives to this day; but in the Ehime hills they tell a different, stranger version — that the thing he killed was his own mother, who had prayed to become it. The nue endures because it resists what it is: unknowability given a face, and a warning that some things refuse to be identified the moment they are seen.
