The Dybbuk Box

Etymology

The term 'dybbuk' (also 'dibbuk') comes from the Hebrew word 'dibbuk' (דיבוק), meaning 'attachment' or 'clinging'. In Jewish folklore, a dybbuk is a dislocated soul that possesses a living person, often to resolve unfinished business. The box is so named because it is allegedly inhabited by such a spirit.

Cultural Ownership

The Dybbuk Box is an American internet legend, but it draws heavily on Jewish folklore, specifically the concept of the dybbuk from Kabbalistic tradition. The box itself is a physical object, a mid-20th-century wine cabinet, that gained notoriety through an eBay listing and subsequent media coverage.

Disputed Identifications

The authenticity of the box is heavily disputed. Skeptics argue it is a hoax perpetrated by Kevin Mannis, the original seller. Believers, including Jason Haxton and Zak Bagans, claim it is genuinely haunted. The box's Jewish provenance (allegedly from a Holocaust survivor's estate) is also questioned, with some suggesting Mannis fabricated this backstory.

Archive Dossier — Approach

In 2003, a wine cabinet listed on eBay ignited an international obsession, its seller claiming it contained a malevolent spirit known as a dybbuk. The box, allegedly purchased from a Holocaust survivor's estate, would go on to inspire a Hollywood horror franchise and spark endless debate: Is it a genuine artifact of the supernatural, a masterful piece of performance art, or something in between? The story of the Dybbuk Box is a modern legend, a collision of Jewish folklore, internet culture, and the enduring human desire to believe in the unseen.

In 2003, an eBay listing for a small wine cabinet came with a story: bought at the estate sale of a 103-year-old Holocaust survivor, who had called it a 'dybbuk box' — in Jewish folklore, a dybbuk is a clinging spirit of the restless dead — and warned it must never be opened. The seller reported the classics in escalating order: a shop trashed overnight by something that broke every bulb; the smell of cat urine and jasmine; nightmares — the same nightmare, he claimed, reported independently by everyone who kept the box; his mother's stroke on the day he gave it to her; hair loss, hives, electronics dying.

Who It Is

In 2003, an eBay listing for a small wine cabinet came with a story: bought at the estate sale of a 103-year-old Holocaust survivor, who had called it a 'dybbuk box' — in Jewish folklore, a dybbuk is a clinging spirit of the restless dead — and warned it must never be opened. The seller reported the classics in escalating order: a shop trashed overnight by something that broke every bulb; the smell of cat urine and jasmine; nightmares — the same nightmare, he claimed, reported independently by everyone who kept the box; his mother's stroke on the day he gave it to her; hair loss, hives, electronics dying. The listing went viral before 'viral' was a word for it, sold for $280, and each subsequent owner added chapters until the box became the internet's first genuinely famous haunted object — the ancestor of every cursed-item listing since. Inside: two 1920s pennies, two locks of hair, a granite slab engraved SHALOM, a dried rosebud, a goblet, and a wine cup — an assemblage that reads, depending on your commitments, as the sealed apparatus of a spirit-binding or as the keepsakes of a long life, which is the entire genre in one sentence. The trail ends, fittingly for our century, in a museum of the paranormal in Las Vegas, where the box sits behind glass; its later keeper conceded the backstory was likely invented for the auction, and the hauntings kept getting reported anyway. The dybbuk is real folklore; the box is real furniture; the ghost, as always, lives in the listing.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

The Dybbuk Box is a wooden wine cabinet, approximately 2 feet tall, with two doors and a drawer. It is described as having a vintage, mid-century design, possibly from the 1940s or 1950s. The wood is dark, and the interior contains two shelves and a small drawer. It is not inherently sinister in appearance; its reputation comes from its alleged supernatural inhabitants.

Symbols & Iconography

The box itself bears no known symbols, but it is often depicted in media with Hebrew inscriptions or mystical imagery, though these are likely artistic embellishments. The concept of a 'dybbuk' has become iconic in horror, symbolizing unresolved trauma and the supernatural.

Behaviour & Personality

The box is not a sentient being, but it is said to exhibit paranormal activity: emitting foul odors, causing nightmares, attracting flies, and influencing negative emotions in those nearby. It is believed to house a malevolent dybbuk that seeks to possess or torment individuals.

Domains & Habitats

The box's physical domain is wherever it is kept. It has allegedly moved between owners: Kevin Mannis, Jason Haxton, and currently Zak Bagans, who displays it at his Haunted Museum in Las Vegas. The box's spiritual domain is the liminal space between the living and the dead, as per Jewish folklore.

Version Differences

There are two main versions of the box's story: the original eBay narrative (Mannis's account) and the expanded narrative popularized by Haxton's book and Bagans's documentary. The core elements remain consistent, but details such as the box's provenance, the number of owners, and the specific events vary across retellings.

Provenance

Kevin Mannis's 2003 eBay narrative (he later acknowledged authoring the tale) grafted authentic dybbuk folklore onto an estate-sale cabinet; passed through owners including Jason Haxton (who wrote a book) to Zak Bagans's Haunted Museum; inspired the 2012 film The Possession.

Holocaust connection

Source Kevin Mannis's account, Jason Haxton's book

Description

The box is alleged to have originated from a Holocaust survivor's estate, and the dybbuk is said to be the spirit of a victim. This connection adds a layer of historical trauma to the legend.

The Holocaust is a real historical event, and using it as a backdrop for a horror story can be seen as exploitative or insensitive, though it also reflects a common trope of haunted objects carrying the weight of tragedy.

Archive Dossier — Stories

The eBay Listing

Source Kevin Mannis's eBay auctionCulture American internet culturePeriod 2003

Summary

Kevin Mannis, a Portland, Oregon antique dealer, listed a wine cabinet for sale on eBay, claiming it was haunted by a dybbuk. He described a series of disturbing events: nightmares, strange smells, and a sense of dread. The listing went viral, and the box was eventually sold to Jason Haxton.

Participants

Kevin Mannis

Jason Haxton

Consequences

The listing launched the Dybbuk Box into internet fame, leading to media coverage, a book, and a film.

Variants

Some versions claim the box was purchased from a Holocaust survivor's estate, while others omit this detail.

Jason Haxton's Ordeal

Source The Dibbuk Box (book)Culture American paranormal subculturePeriod 2003-2012

Summary

Jason Haxton, a medical professional, purchased the box and experienced a series of health problems, nightmares, and paranormal activity. He researched the box's origins, allegedly tracing it to a Holocaust survivor and a dybbuk that had been trapped. He eventually contacted a rabbi to perform an exorcism, but later transferred the box to Zak Bagans.

Participants

Jason Haxton

Zak Bagans

Rabbi (unnamed)

Consequences

Variants

Haxton's account is more detailed and includes a backstory about the dybbuk's origin, which is not present in the original eBay listing.

Known Gaps

Verification of the box's origins and authenticity is impossible without access to primary documentation or forensic analysis. The original eBay listing is no longer available, and the box's provenance is based solely on personal claims. The Holocaust connection is unverified and may be fabricated. The current location and condition of the box are known but not independently verified. This research relies on secondary sources and media accounts; no direct access to the box or its owners was possible. The research cutoff date is August 1, 2026.

Research Scope

This dossier covers the Dybbuk Box as a modern internet legend and purported haunted object, from its 2003 eBay listing to its current status as a cultural phenomenon. It examines the object's claimed origins, the key figures involved (Kevin Mannis, Jason Haxton, Zak Bagans), the box's physical description, the lore surrounding its alleged powers, its reception in media and popular culture, and the various contradictions and misconceptions that have arisen. The research draws primarily on media reports, the book 'The Dibbuk Box' by Jason Haxton, the documentary 'The Haunting of... The Dybbuk Box', and the film 'The Possession'. Due to the nature of the subject, verifiable primary evidence is limited; much of the narrative relies on personal accounts and unverified claims.
