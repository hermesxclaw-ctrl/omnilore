Who It Is

He has no myth, no monster, no miracle - just a name on a lost statue at Delphi. Yet Erasus, son of Triphylus, grandson of bear-born Arcas, is a perfect ghost in the Greek family tree: a single line in Pausanias that holds together an entire Arcadian claim to a disputed borderland.

No behavior, speech, motive, or personality is recorded. Erasus performs no action in myth; he is a name in a genealogy. He is not described as king, warrior, founder, or priest in the surviving passage. His inclusion in the Delphic dedication implies heroic ancestor status for the Arcadians, but the text does not characterize him.

Powers
No attested powers or heroic feats

Erasus is not credited with any supernatural ability, transformation, weapon, or feat in any surviving source. He is a genealogical link, not an active mythological agent. Inclusion among heroic ancestors in a Delphic dedication suggests the Arcadians accorded him timē (honor) as an elite ancestor, but no specific power is described.

Limit: Cannot be assessed; no powers to limit. Modern attempts to assign him pastoral or bear-related powers via his grandfather Arcas would be conflation and are not supported.

Ancestral prestige (social, not supernatural)

His sole function is genealogical prestige: as grandson of Arcas (the culture-hero who taught Arcadians agriculture and weaving, Paus. 8.4.1) and son of the eponym Triphylus, his name legitimizes Arcadian and Triphylian identity. This is a social-religious capital, not a magical ability.

Limit: Prestige is not described as conferring oracular or protective power; no cult, oracle, or invocation of Erasus is attested.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No physical description, iconography, or attribute is attested for Erasus in any surviving ancient text, vase-painting, sculpture, or coin. As a purely genealogical figure mentioned only as 'son of Triphylus' in a list of statues, he has no described anatomy, clothing, age, or symbols. Any visual reconstruction would be invention. Symbols — None attested. No animal, plant, weapon, or cult symbol is associated with Erasus. His father Triphylus is the eponym of Triphylia (Paus. 5.5.3) and thus associated with that region's identity; his grandfather Arcas is associated with the bear (arktos), Arcadian pastoralism, and agriculture (Paus. 8.4.1-3), but these are not transferred to Erasus in sources.

Domains — Genealogically Arcadian. By descent he belongs to the Arcadian royal house founded by Arcas in the Peloponnese (central Arcadia). No specific city, sanctuary, or tomb is attributed to him. Triphylia, associated with his father, lies in western Peloponnese (Elis/Arcadia border), but Erasus himself has no territorial eponymy.

Accounts I
The Arcadian Dedication at Delphi - The Only Direct Attestation of ErasusPausanias, Description of Greece 10.9.5-6

2nd century CE description of a Classical/Hellenistic votive (likely 4th century BCE or after a military victory)

While touring Delphi, Pausanias describes a group dedication made by the Arcadians on the terrace near the sanctuary of Apollo. The monument comprised statues of their eponymous ancestors: Arcas himself, his sons Elatus, Apheidas, Azan, and Triphylus (son of Arcas by Laodameia daughter of Amyklas), and Erasus son of Triphylus, along with other Arcadian heroes. Pausanias lists them to explain who the statues represent and to clarify Triphylus' parentage, which he notes was disputed. No narrative action is ascribed to Erasus; his presence in the group signals his status as a recognized Arcadian ancestor worthy of Panhellenic display. Pausanias does not record the occasion for the dedication, but context suggests it commemorated an Arcadian collective achievement. Provides the sole surviving proof that Erasus was remembered in Arcadian official genealogy into the Roman period. Establishes the Triphylus-Arcas link and by extension Erasus' place in the stemma. No further mythic consequences are narrated.

Arcas, Son of Zeus and Callisto, Founder of ArcadiaPausanias 8.4.1-8.5.4; Apollodorus Bibliotheca 3.8.2, 3.9.1; Hyginus Fabulae 155, 176

Archaic to Hellenistic myth, codified in Classical and Roman mythography; Pausanias 2nd century CE

Arcas (Arkás), grandfather of Erasus, was the son of Zeus and the nymph Callisto, daughter of Lycaon. After Callisto was transformed into a bear (by Hera or Artemis) and nearly killed by her own son Arcas while hunting, Zeus catasterized them as Ursa Major and Arctophylax. Arcas became king of the Pelasgians, who were thereafter called Arcadians. He introduced agriculture (bread-making), weaving, and pastoral arts taught by Triptolemus, and married either Erato (a Dryad), Leanira, Laodameia, or Meganira depending on source. His sons divided Arcadia: Azan received Azania, Apheidas Tegea, Elatus Mount Cyllene, while Triphylus (in Pausanias' version) became eponym of Triphylia. This story provides the mythic charter for the family into which Erasus is born, but Erasus himself plays no role in it. Creates the Arcadian ethnos and kingship that Erasus inherits by blood. Explains why Arcadians claimed autochthony and cultural primacy. The division of Arcadia among Arcas' sons sets the political geography that later authors use to locate Triphylus and, by extension, Erasus.

Accounts II
Triphylus, Eponym of Triphylia, Son of ArcasPausanias 5.5.3, 10.9.5; Stephanus of Byzantium s.v. Triphylia; Strabo 8.3.12

Classical to Roman geographic tradition; Pausanias 2nd century CE

Triphylus, father of Erasus, is himself a secondary genealogical figure. Pausanias explains that Triphylia, the district on the west coast of the Peloponnese between Elis and Messenia, was named after Triphylus. At 5.5.3 he notes the Eleans claimed Triphylia belonged to Elis, while Arcadians claimed it via Triphylus. At 10.9.5 he clarifies Triphylus was a son of Arcas, not an autochthon, and that his mother was Laodameia daughter of Amyklas of Sparta, distinguishing him from Arcas' sons by Erato. No deeds, reign, or death story is given for Triphylus beyond his eponymy and his son Erasus. Strabo and Stephanus preserve the toponymic tradition without adding biographical detail. Establishes Triphylus as a personified place-name, a common Greek mythic device. Gives Erasus a regional anchor: as son of the eponym, he is implicitly Triphylian-Arcadian. The territorial dispute over Triphylia (Arcadian vs. Elean) shows how genealogy served political claims.

The Lycaonid Stemma and the Bear Ancestry of ArcadiaPausanias 8.2.1-8.3.7, 8.17.6; Apollodorus 3.8.1-2; Ovid Metamorphoses 1.209-243

Archaic myth, elaborated in Hellenistic and Roman sources

The deeper ancestry of Erasus runs through Lycaon, king of Arcadia before Arcas. Lycaon, son of Pelasgus, had many sons (including Nyctimus) and a daughter Callisto. In the most famous story, Lycaon tested Zeus by serving him human flesh (his son Nyctimus or a hostage), was turned into a wolf, and his sons were destroyed by Zeus' thunderbolt, save Nyctimus. Callisto, his daughter, bore Arcas to Zeus. This dark foundation myth explains the Arcadians' claim to be pre-Selenian (before the moon) and autochthonous. Erasus, as great-great-grandson of Lycaon via Callisto-Arcas-Triphylus, belongs to this Lycaonid line, though no source explicitly traces the line that far for him - the reconstruction is modern, combining Pausanian stemmata. Places Erasus within the most archaic Greek royal genealogy, linking him to themes of impiety, transformation, and Zeus' justice. No direct consequence for Erasus; he inherits the prestige and the shadow of Lycaon's impiety by descent.

Pausanias' Periegesis: How a Minor Ancestor Survived in a TraveloguePausanias, Description of Greece, Books 5, 8, 10 (overall work); modern scholarship: Frazer 1898, Habicht 1985, Pretzler 2007

c. 150-180 CE composition; 1898-2020s reception

Erasus survives not through epic or tragedy but through Pausanias' antiquarian travel guide. Pausanias' method was autopsy (seeing monuments) plus local exegesis (asking guides). At Delphi he catalogues dedications to preserve local histories that might otherwise be lost. His habit of clarifying genealogies ('Triphylus was son of Arcas by Laodameia, not by Erato') is why Erasus is named at all - to complete the list of statues. Without Pausanias' decision to record the Arcadian dedication in detail, Erasus would be entirely unknown; no other author mentions him. Modern scholarship treats this as an example of how periegetic literature preserves micro-genealogies that epic and tragedy ignored. Demonstrates the fragmentary nature of Greek myth: entire figures exist only as names in lists. For OmniLore, it exemplifies the 'genealogical ghost' - a figure with attested name and parentage but no story, whose significance is purely as a node in a kinship network used for political identity.

Erasus was a king of Arcadia or Triphylia who ruled and founded cities. — No ancient source calls Erasus a king, founder, or ruler. Kingship in Arcadia after Arcas went to his sons Azan, Apheidas, and Elatus, who divided the land (Paus. 8.4.2-3). Triphylus is eponym but not described as reigning; Erasus is only 'son of Triphylus' in a votive list. Modern genealogical websites that list him as 'King Erasus' are inventing a title.

Erasus had myths, adventures, or powers like other Greek heroes (e.g., fought monsters, had transformations). — Zero narrative myths, powers, or iconography are attested for Erasus. He appears only as a name in a statue group. Unlike Arcas (bear myth, agriculture) or Lycaon (wolf transformation), Erasus has no story. Attributing deeds to him is fabrication.

Erasus is the same as Erasinos, the river-god, or a variant spelling. — Distinct figures: Erasinos (Ἐρασῖνος) is a river in Argos and Arcadia with its own myths (Paus. 2.36.6, 8.54.3), while Erasus (Ἔρασος) is a mortal Arcadian prince. Different etymology, different genealogy, different attestations. No ancient source equates them.

The name Erasus appears in many ancient sources, showing he was well-known. — Erasus appears in exactly one surviving ancient passage (Paus. 10.9.5) and its manuscript copies/translations. All modern mentions derive from that single line. He is one of the most thinly attested figures in Greek mythography.
