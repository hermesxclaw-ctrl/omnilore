Who It Is

In the catalogue of Arcadian kings that Pausanias copied at Delphi, a single name flickers and vanishes: Erasus, son of Triphylus, grandson of Arcas himself. He has no adventures, no transformations, no cult — only a place in a genealogy that was meant to explain why a corner of the Peloponnese was called Triphylia. His obscurity is the point: Erasus shows how Greek myth preserved even the thinnest branches of a royal family tree to anchor landscape to lineage.

No anecdotes, speeches, moral judgments, or personality traits are preserved for Erasus. He appears solely as a genealogical link (son of Triphylus) in a dedicatory inscription list. No behavior, motive, or social role beyond 'son of Triphylus' is described. Any characterization would be invention. Status: unattested.

Accounts I
The Genealogy of Erasus at Delphi (Pausanias 10.9.5)Pausanias, Description of Greece 10.9.5

2nd century CE (describing a 4th century BCE dedication)

Pausanias, touring Delphi, describes a dedication set up by the Arcadians. In enumerating the Arcadian kings represented, he inserts a genealogy: Arcas had a son Triphylus, and Triphylus had a son Erasus. The passage is not a narrative myth but a catalogue note to explain who the statues represent. Erasus appears only as a name in this list, with no deed attached. The dedication itself commemorated an Arcadian victory or collective offering; the statues included Arcas and his descendants. Provides the sole attestation of Erasus; anchors Triphylus's lineage; used by modern scholars to reconstruct Arcadian king-lists and eponymous geography.

Arcas, Son of Zeus and Callisto, Founder of ArcadiaPausanias 8.3.6-8.4.1; Apollodorus Bibliotheca 3.8.2; Ovid Metamorphoses 2.401-530

Archaic to Hellenistic (myth), recorded 1st-2nd c. CE

Arcas, grandfather of Erasus, is the central Arcadian culture hero. Born to Zeus and the nymph Callisto (daughter of Lycaon), he was exposed as an infant, preserved by Hermes or raised by Maia, and became king. He taught the Pelasgians agriculture, weaving, and bread-making, giving his name to Arcadia. This is the deep background that gives Erasus his royal claim, though Erasus plays no role in the story itself. Establishes the divine-royal lineage from which Erasus descends; explains Arcadian identity and eponymy; Callisto's catasterism as Ursa Major becomes panhellenic.

Triphylus and the Eponymy of TriphyliaPausanias 10.9.5; Strabo Geography 8.3.12; Stephanus of Byzantium s.v. Triphylia; Polybius 4.77

Hellenistic to Roman Imperial (geographical tradition)

Triphylus, father of Erasus, is an eponymous hero invented or preserved to explain the name Triphylia ('land of three tribes') on the west coast of the Peloponnese. Pausanias calls him son of Arcas; Strabo discusses Triphylia as a district contested between Elis and Arcadia. No myths of deeds survive for Triphylus beyond his eponymous function; his son Erasus extends the lineage by one generation, perhaps to provide a further genealogical depth without narrative. Explains regional ethnonym; Triphylus's sons (including Erasus) represent Arcadian claims to Triphylian territory. Modern scholarship (Nielsen 2002, Roy 2007) treats Triphylus as a late genealogical construct.

Accounts II
The Division of Arcadia Among the Sons of ArcasPausanias 8.4.2-8.4.7

2nd century CE (recording earlier local histories by Ariaethus and others)

After Arcas, Arcadia was divided among his three principal sons: Azan received Azania, Apheidas received Tegea, and Elatus received Mount Cyllene and later Phocis. This tripartition is the canonical succession. Triphylus (and thus Erasus) does not appear in this division, indicating he belongs to a separate, perhaps Triphylian, branch or a later insertion to account for territory outside core Arcadia. Erasus is absent from the succession narrative, implying he did not inherit a kingdom. Highlights Erasus's marginal status; demonstrates how Greek genealogies could have parallel branches for different regions; used to argue Triphylus-Erasus line is aetiological rather than narrative.

The Arcadian Dedication at Delphi - Historical ContextPausanias 10.9.3-10.9.6; Jacquemin, Offrandes monumentales à Delphes (1999); archaeological record of Delphi

Early 4th century BCE (dedication), 2nd c. CE (Pausanias's autopsy)

The monument Pausanias saw was an Arcadian collective dedication at Delphi, likely after a 4th-century victory (possibly after the Battle of Mantinea 362 BCE or earlier Arcadian synoecism). It comprised statues of Arcadian heroes and kings, including Arcas, Elatus, Apheidas, Azan, and Triphylus with his son Erasus. The inclusion of obscure figures like Erasus shows the Arcadians' desire to display a deep, complete genealogy at the panhellenic sanctuary. The statues themselves are lost; only Pausanias's description survives. Only reason Erasus's name survived; illustrates practice of genealogical display as political propaganda; Delphi's archaeological inventory cannot confirm statues, but Pausanias is generally reliable for such dedications.

Callisto's Transformation and the Bear Lineage (Deep Ancestral Myth)Hesiod Catalogue of Women fr. 163 M-W; Apollodorus 3.8.2; Ovid Metamorphoses 2.401-530; Pausanias 8.3.6

Archaic (Hesiod) to Augustan (Ovid)

Erasus's great-grandmother Callisto, daughter of Lycaon, was a companion of Artemis, violated by Zeus, transformed into a bear (by Hera or Artemis), and later catasterized as the Great Bear. Her son Arcas nearly killed her while hunting before Zeus placed both in the sky. This dark ancestral story underlies the entire Arcadian royal house, including Erasus, though Erasus himself is never linked to the bear motif in surviving texts. Provides mythic charter for Arcadian identity ('bear people'); explains constellation myth; forms the darkest material in Erasus's extended family tree.

Erasus was a king of Arcadia or Triphylia. — No source says Erasus ruled. Pausanias only calls him son of Triphylus. The kings who divided Arcadia were Azan, Apheidas, and Elatus (Paus. 8.4.3). Erasus is never given a kingdom, city foundation, or reign.

Erasus is the same as Erasinus, the river-god. — Distinct figures. Erasinus (Ἐρασῖνος) is a river in Argolis and Arcadia personified as a god, son of Oceanus, with cult and iconography. Erasus (Ἔρασος) is a mortal prince, son of Triphylus, with no cult. Names are similar but not identical in Greek and never equated anciently.

Erasus has myths, powers, or adventures like other Arcadian heroes. — Zero myths, powers, or stories are attested for Erasus beyond his name in a genealogy. He has no metamorphosis, no heroic deed, no love affair, and no death story. Any such details found online are modern invention or misattribution from Arcas or other figures.
