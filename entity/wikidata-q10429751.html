The Approach

On a moonless night in the mountains of Nara, a faint jingling echoes through the trees. An old woman emerges, her face a mask of thick white powder, dragging a mirror behind her. To meet her gaze is to risk terror—or, if legend holds true, to glimpse the hidden face of a bodhisattva.

Identity

Oshiroibabā

Pronunciation OH-shee-roi-bah-bah

Known As

Oshiroibabā-san白粉ばばあ白粉ばあさん

Etymology

The name combines 'oshiroi' (白粉, white facial powder) and 'babā' (ばばあ, old woman), literally meaning 'white powder old woman'.

Cultural Ownership

Japanese folklore

Disputed Identifications

The identity of Oshiroibabā is ambiguous: it may refer to a specific yōkai from Nara folklore, an attendant of the goddess Shifunsenjo as depicted by Toriyama Sekien, or a separate legendary figure associated with Hase-dera temple. The relationship between these figures is unclear.

Description

Appearance

Oshiroibabā is typically depicted as an old woman with a severely bent back, wearing a large broken umbrella on her head, holding a walking stick in one hand and a sake flask (tokkuri) in the other. Her face is covered in thick, messy white powder, which is said to be terrifying to behold. In some accounts, she drags a mirror behind her, making a jingling sound.

Symbols & Iconography

White facial powder (oshiroi), mirror, walking stick, sake flask, broken umbrella.

Behaviour & Personality

She is said to appear at night, often in mountainous areas or near rivers, and may approach travelers. Some stories suggest she begs for white powder or sake. In the Hase-dera legend, she is a benevolent figure who aids monks through miraculous means.

Domains & Habitats

Totsukawa River in Yoshino District, Nara Prefecture; mountains and foothills; possibly snowy regions (per Fujisawa, though unverified).

Version Differences

The Nara folklore version is a yōkai that appears near the Totsukawa River, dragging a mirror and making a jingling sound. Sekien's illustration depicts her as an attendant of Shifunsenjo, with a different appearance (broken umbrella, flask). Fujisawa's account places her in Noto, Ishikawa, on snowy nights, linked to yuki-onna, but this is likely an invention. The Hase-dera legend describes a different 'oshiroibabaa'—an old woman with white-powdered face who is an incarnation of Kannon, not a yōkai.

Stories & Myths

The Painter Monks of Hase-dera

Source Temple legendCulture Japanese BuddhistPeriod Muromachi period (1538)

Summary

During the 6th year of Tenbun (1538), painter monks gathered at Hase-dera to paint a giant image of Kannon. When Ashikaga soldiers destroyed crops, the monks feared starvation. A young monk guided them to a well where an old woman (Oshiroibabaa) was grinding rice. The rice miraculously multiplied. One monk threw a pebble to see her face, and she revealed a dazzling light, her face covered in white powder and wrinkled from toil. All bowed in reverence, and the monks completed the grand image.

Participants

Painter monks

Kōshin Shōnin

Ashikaga shogunate soldiers

Oshiroibabaa

Consequences

The monks completed the large image of Kannon; a hall was dedicated to Oshiroibabaa at Hase-dera, and an annual ceremony applied white powder to her statue until the Meiji era.

Variants

The legend exists in Hase-dera's oral tradition; no other known variants.

Primary Evidence

Konjaku Hyakki Shūi by Toriyama Sekien

Type textDate c. 1781Locator Illustration and commentaryLanguage Japanese

Translation

Sekien's commentary states: 'The goddess of red and white oshiroi powder is called Shifunsenjo, and oshiroibabā must be an attendant of this goddess.'

Zusetsu Minzoku Zenshū by Morihiko Fujisawa

Type textDate 1930sLocator Noto region entryLanguage Japanese

Translation

Fujisawa claims Oshiroibabā appears on snowy nights in Noto, Ishikawa Prefecture, seeking sake, and is related to yuki-onna. However, no corroborating local tradition has been found.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

Detailed primary sources in Japanese are not fully accessed. The exact origin of the Totsukawa River legend is unclear. The relationship between the Nara yōkai and the Hase-dera legend is ambiguous. Fujisawa's claim is likely invented, but no definitive debunking source is cited. Further research into Japanese folklore archives and temple records is needed.

Research Scope

This dossier focuses on the yōkai Oshiroibabā as depicted in Japanese folklore and art, primarily from the Edo-period hyakki yagyō tradition and regional legends from Nara Prefecture. It includes the legend associated with Hase-dera temple. Due to the scarcity of detailed primary sources in English, much of the information is synthesized from Japanese folklore scholarship and the works of Toriyama Sekien. The research is limited to sources available online; further archival research in Japanese would be required for a more exhaustive account.
