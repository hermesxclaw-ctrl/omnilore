Who It Is

The Pombero (from Guarani 'Pombéro' — 'spirit of the night') is one of the most widely believed-in mythological figures in contemporary Paraguayan and northeastern Argentine folk culture — a small, hairy, dark-skinned forest spirit who acts as a guardian of the birds and all forest creatures. He is described as short, with thick body hair, large hands, and the ability to become invisible, mimic bird calls, and move through the forest without sound. The Pombero is not malevolent — he is capricious and transactional. Rural people leave offerings to him: tobacco, honey, rum, or food placed at the edge of the forest. In return, he may protect their crops, watch over their children, or help find lost objects. However, if offended or neglected, the Pombero becomes a menace: he steals food, frightens livestock, throws stones at houses, and is said to impregnate solitary women (a folk explanation for unexplained pregnancies). Belief in the Pombero is so widespread in rural Paraguay that it functions as a living folklore system: people leave nightly offerings, warn children not to whistle in the forest at night (the Pombero's call), and attribute mysterious events to his presence. Unlike many mythological figures that have faded to pure narrative, the Pombero remains a genuinely believed-in entity whose existence structures daily behaviors. Anthropologists have documented the Pombero's incorporation into millenarian and syncretic religious movements, and he appears regularly in Paraguayan literature, music, and film.

Attested in Oral tradition / widespread folk beliefEra Pre-colonial to present (one of the most active folk beliefs in modern Paraguay)
