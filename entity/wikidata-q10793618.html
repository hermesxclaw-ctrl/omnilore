Who It Is

She appears only once, in a single Latin sentence written by a German priest who wanted her to disappear. In the 1160s, as Saxon dukes and Danish kings pressed against the last pagan stronghold of the Polabian Slavs, the chronicler Helmold of Bosau noted that the Obodrites under Prince Niklot had revived their old rites and that among their gods the Goddess of Ratzeburg, whom they called Siwa, stood foremost. No statue of her has ever been found, no prayer to her survives, no myth tells what she did. Only her name — Živa, “the Living One” — remains, a word that is also a woman’s name, a wish for long life, and perhaps the title of a greater goddess whose true name was already being forgotten.

Unknown. The epithet 'Living' suggests a benevolent, life-affirming character — perhaps a protector of birth, vitality, or longevity — but no myth, speech, or action is preserved. Helmold's framing as a pagan goddess opposed to Christianity implies she was a central communal deity receiving cult, not a demon or minor spirit in his view. Whether she was chthonic, celestial, or domestic is unattested. All personality traits in modern literature are retrojections.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Unanimously derived by modern linguists from Proto-Slavic *živъ 'alive, living' (PIE *gʷeih₃- 'to live'), feminine adjective *živa 'alive, living'. The theonym *Živa is formally identical to the Slavic feminine personal name Živa, well attested: Old Polabian *Živa (Latin Zive, 1336), Old Polish Żywa

Pronunciation — Proto-Slavic *Živa [ʒiˈʋa]; Polish Żywa / Żywia [ˈʐɨ.va] / [ˈʐɨ.vja]; Czech Živa [ˈʒɪ.va]; German scholarly Siwa [ˈziːva]; Latin Siwa [ˈsiː.wa]. The grapheme <w> in medieval Latin manuscripts represents [v].

Aliases — Siwa, Siwe, Silue, Synna, Syuua, Sywa

Powers
Life-Giving / Vitality (reconstructed from etymology)

From *živa 'alive, living', scholars reconstruct a domain over life, birth, longevity, or spring revival. No spell, miracle, or feat is narrated. Function is inferred, not described.

Limit: Entirely hypothetical; no source says she healed, resurrected, granted fertility, or controlled seasons. Cannot be cited as attested ability.

Tribal Protection / Patronage of Ratzeburg

Helmold's title Raceburgensium Dea implies she was the tutelary deity of the Ratzeburg community, receiving cult on behalf of the tribe. Protection of the gord and its people would be expected.

Limit: No ritual, prayer, or intervention is described. Extent of protection (military, agricultural, reproductive) unknown. Cult disappears from record after Niklot's death ca. 1160 and German reconquest.

Onomastic Power (wishing name)

As a personal name, Živa functioned as a wishing name: naming a child Živa was intended to ensure she 'be alive and live long' (apotropaic/beneficent naming). If the goddess bore this name as epithet, the name itself was the power — invocation of life.

Limit: This is a function of the name in human anthroponymy, not necessarily a divine miracle. No source shows the goddess granting long life in narrative.

Accounts I
The Pagan Revival under Prince Niklot and the Goddess of RatzeburgHelmold of Bosau, Chronica Slavorum, Book I, ch. 52 (Schmeidler ed.)

Contemporary report of events ca. 1131-1160, written ca. 1167-1172

After the death of Prince Henry of the Obodrites (1127) and the collapse of German ecclesiastical organization in Wagria, Prince Niklot of the Obodrites (r. 1131-1160) reasserted paganism. Helmold writes that idolatry flourished, churches were neglected, and among the various gods the goddess Siwa of the Polabians / Goddess of Ratzeburg held prominence. This is the sole narrative in which Živa acts — as the recipient of revived cult during a period of political resistance to Saxon and Danish Christian expansion. Helmold frames it as apostasy to be corrected by mission and conquest. Provides the only attestation of Živa. Demonstrates that as late as the mid-12th century a goddess cult could be publicly restored as an act of political sovereignty. Prefigures the Wendish Crusade (1147) and Henry the Lion's campaigns that would end Obodrite independence after Niklot's death in 1160.

The Fall of Ratzeburg and the End of the CultHelmold, Chronica Slavorum I, 84-87; Arnold of Lübeck; Saxo Grammaticus, Gesta Danorum

Events 1159-1163, narrated late 12th-early 13th century

After Niklot was killed in 1160 during Henry the Lion's campaign, his son Pribislav initially resisted then submitted and converted to Christianity (1164). Henry the Lion granted Ratzeburg to the Duke and re-established the Bishopric of Ratzeburg (1154, restored 1162). Helmold describes destruction of pagan shrines and baptism of the population. Živa is never mentioned again, implying her shrine (if any) was destroyed or abandoned without comment. The silence marks the cult's extinction as a living institution. Explains why Živa left no continuity: unlike Svantevit at Arkona (destroyed 1168 with detailed description), her cult ended without a destruction narrative, contributing to her obscurity.

The Manuscript Journey: How Siwa Became ŽivaTextual criticism of Chronica Slavorum manuscripts (Schmeidler 1937; later Slavists)

13th century copies → 19th-20th century philological reconstruction

For centuries the goddess was known only as Latin Siwa. Comparative study of Copenhagen (Siwa), Lübeck (Siwe), Szczecin (Synna < *Syuua), and Vienna (Silue) copies showed the original contained <w> ([v]) and that Szczecin's Synna resulted from <u>→<n> confusion and <uu>=<w>. Linguists restored *Sywa and, via regular Slavic sound correspondences, *Živa. This philological detective story transformed a mysterious 'Siwa' into a transparent Slavic word for 'living', enabling the modern etymology and Mokosh hypothesis. Established the canonical reading Živa used in all modern scholarship and neopaganism; disproved readings *Siva or *Silue. Demonstrates how a single letter determines a goddess's identity.

Accounts II
The Romantic Invention of the Pan-Slavic Mother Živa19th-century Slavic Romantic scholarship and literature: Jan Kollár, Slávy dcera (1824); Pavel Jozef Šafárik; Karel Jaromír Erben; Hanuš Máchal, Nákres slovanského bájesloví (1891)

1820s-1890s

Seeking a Slavic pantheon to rival the Classical, Romantics elevated Helmold's local goddess into Živa/Żywia, the great pan-Slavic goddess of life, spring, and fertility, consort of Perun or wife of Svarog, often depicted as a beautiful woman with wheat sheaf and child. Máchal and others listed her alongside Perun, Veles, and Morana as a major deity of all Slavs, citing no new primary evidence but extrapolating from etymology and folklore. This Živa entered encyclopedias, schoolbooks, and art, creating a popular image far beyond Helmold's single notice. Created the enduring popular misconception of Živa as a major pan-Slavic goddess. Also provoked critical reaction: Brückner (1918) and later scholars dismantled the Romantic pantheon, returning Živa to her status as a single-source Polabian deity.

Živa as Epithet of Mokosh: The Ivanov-Toporov HypothesisVyacheslav V. Ivanov and Vladimir N. Toporov, Issledovanija v oblasti slavjanskich drevnostej (1974) and later articles; summarized in Szyjewski 2003

1970s-1980s, still debated

Noting that Mokosh, the East Slavic women's goddess, is absent from West Slavic sources, while Živa appears only among West Slavs and shares semantics of life/moisture/earth, Ivanov and Toporov proposed Živa was not an independent goddess but a West Slavic epithet or taboo replacement name for Mokosh, preserved after Christianization in the Polabian personal name Živa. In this reading, the 'Goddess of Ratzeburg' was Mokosh under a descriptive title 'the Living One'. The hypothesis links West and East Slavic religion and explains the distribution gap. If accepted, Živa ceases to be a separate deity and becomes evidence for a unified Slavic goddess. If rejected, she remains an isolated local figure. Most Western scholars treat it as intriguing but unproven due to lack of corroborating West Slavic Mokosh evidence.

The Return of Żywia in Contemporary Slavic Native FaithEthnography of Rodzimowierstwo / Rodnovery: groups such as Rodzima Wiara, Rodzimy Kościół Polski, Czech and Slovak pagan communities; fieldwork by Simpson & Filip 2013, etc.

1990s-present

Since the 1990s, neopagan communities have revived Żywia/Živa as a living goddess, performing spring rites, name-giving ceremonies, and women's mysteries in her honor. Rituals are newly composed, often invoking her as Lady of Life, protector of children, and spring counterpart to Marzanna (goddess of winter/death). Practitioners cite Helmold and etymology as legitimation but create new liturgy, iconography (flower-crowned woman), and calendar (e.g., Jare Święto). This is not a continuation but a reconstruction/revival, openly acknowledged by most groups. Gives Živa a contemporary religious life absent for 860 years, generating new folklore, art, and music. Creates feedback loop where neopagan usage is sometimes mistaken for ancient tradition.

Živa was a major pan-Slavic goddess worshipped by all Slavs from the Elbe to Russia, as important as Perun or Veles. — She is attested only once, for one tribe of the Obodritic confederation at Ratzeburg in the mid-12th century. No East Slavic, South Slavic, or other West Slavic source mentions her. Pan-Slavic status is a 19th-century Romantic expansion without primary evidence.

Sanskrit Shiva < Sanskrit śiva 'auspicious'. No etymological, cultural, or historical connection. Latin Siwa is a German scribe's rendering of Slavic ž.

We know what Živa looked like — a young fertile woman with long blonde hair, wheat sheaf, flowers, or holding a child — and have ancient statues of her. — No description, image, or statue survives. Živa's myths and powers are well known — she ruled spring, defeated Marzanna, granted life and healing. — No myth, story, or specific power is preserved.
