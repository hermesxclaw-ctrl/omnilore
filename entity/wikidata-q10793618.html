The Approach

She appears only once, in a single Latin sentence written by a German priest who wanted her to disappear. In the 1160s, as Saxon dukes and Danish kings pressed against the last pagan stronghold of the Polabian Slavs, the chronicler Helmold of Bosau noted that the Obodrites under Prince Niklot had revived their old rites and that among their gods the Goddess of Ratzeburg, whom they called Siwa, stood foremost. No statue of her has ever been found, no prayer to her survives, no myth tells what she did. Only her name — Živa, “the Living One” — remains, a word that is also a woman’s name, a wish for long life, and perhaps the title of a greater goddess whose true name was already being forgotten.

Content advisory. No explicit sexual or graphic ritual detail is attested for Živa. Context includes medieval religious warfare, forced Christianization, colonial violence against Slavic populations, and scholarly discussion of human sacrifice among Polabian Slavs (unattested specifically for Živa). No sanitized imagery; absence of evidence is stated plainly.
Identity

Živa

Pronunciation Proto-Slavic *Živa [ʒiˈʋa]; Polish Żywa / Żywia [ˈʐɨ.va] / [ˈʐɨ.vja]; Czech Živa [ˈʒɪ.va]; German scholarly Siwa [ˈziːva]; Latin Siwa [ˈsiː.wa]. The grapheme <w> in medieval Latin manuscripts represents [v].

Etymology

Unanimously derived by modern linguists from Proto-Slavic *živъ 'alive, living' (PIE *gʷeih₃- 'to live'), feminine adjective *živa 'alive, living'. The theonym *Živa is formally identical to the Slavic feminine personal name Živa, well attested: Old Polabian *Živa (Latin Zive, 1336), Old Polish Żywa (Latin Siwa, Sywa, Szywa, Szyva), Serbo-Croatian Živa, Bulgarian Zhiva, Czech Živa. Scholars interpret it either as a wishing name ('may she be alive, may she live long') or a characterizing epithet ('she who is alive, the Living One'). Vyacheslav Ivanov and Vladimir Toporov proposed it was not an independent theonym but an epithet of the East Slavic goddess Mokosh that survived among the Polabians after Christianization. No alternative etymology (e.g., from *siva 'grey') is accepted in current scholarship.

Cultural Ownership

Polabian Slavs (Elbe Slavs), specifically one tribe of the Obodritic (Abodrite) confederation whose principal gord (fortified settlement) was Ratzeburg (Ratibor, Latin Raceburg). Attestation is West Slavic, 12th century, in the contact zone between pagan Slavic polity and expanding German-Danish Christendom. Not a pan-Slavic deity in primary evidence; later pan-Slavic extension is a 19th-century scholarly and neopagan construction. Care is required not to project East Slavic or South Slavic material onto this strictly Polabian attestation.

Disputed Identifications

1) Identification with Mokosh (Ivanov/Toporov) — disputed, no direct evidence. 2) Confusion with Hindu Shiva due to Latin Siwa spelling — false homonym, no genetic link. 3) Conflation with a generic pan-Slavic 'goddess of life' Živa/Żywia invented by 19th-century Romantics (e.g., Kollár, Máchal) — modern scholarly consensus rejects as overgeneralization from single Helmold passage. 4) Misreading Siva vs Siwa — Vienna manuscript Silue is a scribal distortion; Copenhagen/Lübeck/Szczecin forms point to *Sywa/*Živa, not *Siva. 5) Asteroid 140 Siwa is named after this goddess, not a separate mythological figure.

Description

Appearance

No contemporary description of Živa's appearance, clothing, age, attributes, or iconography survives. Helmold provides no physical details. No statue, carving, coin, or image is linked to her in any primary source. Later images labeled 'Živa' (19th-21st century paintings, neopagan statues of a fertile woman with wheat, flowers, or child) are modern inventions without source basis and must be labeled adaptation-specific / hypothetical. If she was a mother goddess as Helmold's context suggests, a mature female figure might be inferred, but this is reconstruction, not attestation.

Symbols & Iconography

No symbols are attested. Modern associations with grain, spring greenery, cuckoo, or life-giving water are extrapolated from the etymology 'living' and from generic fertility-goddess typology, not from Polabian evidence. No animal, plant, color, or object is linked to Živa in Helmold or any other medieval source. Any iconographic program for a dossier must use a 'no verified visual record' state or abstract typographic treatment, not a fabricated idol.

Behaviour & Personality

Unknown. The epithet 'Living' suggests a benevolent, life-affirming character — perhaps a protector of birth, vitality, or longevity — but no myth, speech, or action is preserved. Helmold's framing as a pagan goddess opposed to Christianity implies she was a central communal deity receiving cult, not a demon or minor spirit in his view. Whether she was chthonic, celestial, or domestic is unattested. All personality traits in modern literature are retrojections.

Domains & Habitats

Attested domain: patron goddess of the tribe whose main gord was Ratzeburg, within the Obodritic confederation (Wagrians/Polabians). Helmold calls her Raceburgensium Dea. Her functional domain is inferred from name: life, vitality, possibly fertility and healing, but no source lists a portfolio (unlike, e.g., Perun = thunder). Habitat: presumably a shrine or sacred grove at or near Ratzeburg; no temple is described. Cosmology: Polabian pagan cosmology is poorly documented; placement of Živa within it is unknown.

Version Differences

Only one medieval version exists (Helmold's Siwa). All other 'versions' are historiographical layers: 1) Medieval Latin Siwa (12th c., local Polabian goddess). 2) Early modern antiquarian Siwa (16th-18th c. chronicle copies, no new data). 3) Romantic pan-Slavic Živa (19th c., expanded into great life-goddess of all Slavs). 4) Critical philological Živa (late 19th-20th c., reduced back to single-source Ratzeburg deity + personal name). 5) Neopagan Żywia (late 20th-21st c., living goddess reconstructed for ritual). These must not be merged into a single continuous biography.

Powers & Abilities

Life-Giving / Vitality (reconstructed from etymology)

Source VersionModern linguistic reconstruction (Brückner, Ivanov-Toporov, Szyjewski) based on etymology and mother-goddess framing in Helmold; not an attested power in primary text

From *živa 'alive, living', scholars reconstruct a domain over life, birth, longevity, or spring revival. No spell, miracle, or feat is narrated. Function is inferred, not described.

Limitations

Entirely hypothetical; no source says she healed, resurrected, granted fertility, or controlled seasons. Cannot be cited as attested ability.

Contradictions

If Ivanov-Toporov are correct and Živa is an epithet of Mokosh, her domain would be earth, moisture, women's work, and fate, not abstract 'life'. Other scholars keep Živa as independent vitality goddess, leaving Mokosh separate.

Tribal Protection / Patronage of Ratzeburg

Source VersionHelmold, Chronica Slavorum I,52; attested function as polity goddess

Helmold's title Raceburgensium Dea implies she was the tutelary deity of the Ratzeburg community, receiving cult on behalf of the tribe. Protection of the gord and its people would be expected.

Limitations

No ritual, prayer, or intervention is described. Extent of protection (military, agricultural, reproductive) unknown. Cult disappears from record after Niklot's death ca. 1160 and German reconquest.

Contradictions

Some scholars argue 'Goddess of Ratzeburg' is Helmold's gloss, not the Slavs' own term; the goddess may have had wider Obodrite worship, with Ratzeburg as only the chronicler's reference point.

Onomastic Power (wishing name)

Source VersionOnomastic evidence 14th c. onward; linguistic analysis of Slavic naming practices

As a personal name, Živa functioned as a wishing name: naming a child Živa was intended to ensure she 'be alive and live long' (apotropaic/beneficent naming). If the goddess bore this name as epithet, the name itself was the power — invocation of life.

Limitations

This is a function of the name in human anthroponymy, not necessarily a divine miracle. No source shows the goddess granting long life in narrative.

Contradictions

None; widely accepted that Slavic *živa names are wishing names. Dispute is only whether the theonym originated as such a name applied to an unnamed goddess.

Stories & Myths

The Pagan Revival under Prince Niklot and the Goddess of Ratzeburg

Source Helmold of Bosau, Chronica Slavorum, Book I, ch. 52 (Schmeidler ed.)Culture Polabian Slavic (Obodrites) reported by Saxon Christian chroniclerPeriod Contemporary report of events ca. 1131-1160, written ca. 1167-1172

Summary

After the death of Prince Henry of the Obodrites (1127) and the collapse of German ecclesiastical organization in Wagria, Prince Niklot of the Obodrites (r. 1131-1160) reasserted paganism. Helmold writes that idolatry flourished, churches were neglected, and among the various gods the goddess Siwa of the Polabians / Goddess of Ratzeburg held prominence. This is the sole narrative in which Živa acts — as the recipient of revived cult during a period of political resistance to Saxon and Danish Christian expansion. Helmold frames it as apostasy to be corrected by mission and conquest.

Participants

Niklot, Prince of the Obodrites

The Polabians / Obodrites of Ratzeburg

Helmold of Bosau (narrator, priest of Bosau)

Siwa/Živa (goddess, cult object)

Consequences

Provides the only attestation of Živa. Demonstrates that as late as the mid-12th century a goddess cult could be publicly restored as an act of political sovereignty. Prefigures the Wendish Crusade (1147) and Henry the Lion's campaigns that would end Obodrite independence after Niklot's death in 1160.

Variants

Four manuscript readings of the name (Siwa/Siwe/Silue/Synna) reflect scribal transmission, not variant myths. No alternative version of the revival story survives. Arnold of Lübeck does not retell it.

The Fall of Ratzeburg and the End of the Cult

Source Helmold, Chronica Slavorum I, 84-87; Arnold of Lübeck; Saxo Grammaticus, Gesta DanorumCulture Saxon/Danish Christian historiographyPeriod Events 1159-1163, narrated late 12th-early 13th century

Summary

After Niklot was killed in 1160 during Henry the Lion's campaign, his son Pribislav initially resisted then submitted and converted to Christianity (1164). Henry the Lion granted Ratzeburg to the Duke and re-established the Bishopric of Ratzeburg (1154, restored 1162). Helmold describes destruction of pagan shrines and baptism of the population. Živa is never mentioned again, implying her shrine (if any) was destroyed or abandoned without comment. The silence marks the cult's extinction as a living institution.

Participants

Niklot (killed 1160)

Pribislav, son of Niklot

Henry the Lion, Duke of Saxony

Bishop Gerold of Oldenburg/Ratzeburg

Population of Ratzeburg

Consequences

Explains why Živa left no continuity: unlike Svantevit at Arkona (destroyed 1168 with detailed description), her cult ended without a destruction narrative, contributing to her obscurity.

Variants

No variant attributes Živa's temple destruction to a specific miracle; the event is inferred from general Christianization notices.

The Manuscript Journey: How Siwa Became Živa

Source Textual criticism of Chronica Slavorum manuscripts (Schmeidler 1937; later Slavists)Culture German/Latin scribal culture and modern Slavic philologyPeriod 13th century copies → 19th-20th century philological reconstruction

Summary

For centuries the goddess was known only as Latin Siwa. Comparative study of Copenhagen (Siwa), Lübeck (Siwe), Szczecin (Synna < *Syuua), and Vienna (Silue) copies showed the original contained <w> ([v]) and that Szczecin's Synna resulted from <u>→<n> confusion and <uu>=<w>. Linguists restored *Sywa and, via regular Slavic sound correspondences, *Živa. This philological detective story transformed a mysterious 'Siwa' into a transparent Slavic word for 'living', enabling the modern etymology and Mokosh hypothesis.

Participants

Anonymous medieval scribes

Bernhard Schmeidler (editor, 1937)

Polish and Czech linguists (Brückner, Máchal, etc.)

The grapheme <w>

Consequences

Established the canonical reading Živa used in all modern scholarship and neopaganism; disproved readings *Siva or *Silue. Demonstrates how a single letter determines a goddess's identity.

Variants

Vienna Silue remains an outlier, sometimes cited as evidence for alternative reading *Žilva, now rejected.

The Romantic Invention of the Pan-Slavic Mother Živa

Source 19th-century Slavic Romantic scholarship and literature: Jan Kollár, Slávy dcera (1824); Pavel Jozef Šafárik; Karel Jaromír Erben; Hanuš Máchal, Nákres slovanského bájesloví (1891)Culture Czech, Slovak, Polish, Russian Romantic nationalismPeriod 1820s-1890s

Summary

Seeking a Slavic pantheon to rival the Classical, Romantics elevated Helmold's local goddess into Živa/Żywia, the great pan-Slavic goddess of life, spring, and fertility, consort of Perun or wife of Svarog, often depicted as a beautiful woman with wheat sheaf and child. Máchal and others listed her alongside Perun, Veles, and Morana as a major deity of all Slavs, citing no new primary evidence but extrapolating from etymology and folklore. This Živa entered encyclopedias, schoolbooks, and art, creating a popular image far beyond Helmold's single notice.

Participants

Jan Kollár

Hanuš Máchal

Aleksander Brückner (as critic of this expansion)

The reading public of the Slavic National Revival

Consequences

Created the enduring popular misconception of Živa as a major pan-Slavic goddess. Also provoked critical reaction: Brückner (1918) and later scholars dismantled the Romantic pantheon, returning Živa to her status as a single-source Polabian deity.

Variants

Different Romantics gave her different consorts, genealogies, and festivals (e.g., spring rites on May 1), none attested medievally.

Živa as Epithet of Mokosh: The Ivanov-Toporov Hypothesis

Source Vyacheslav V. Ivanov and Vladimir N. Toporov, Issledovanija v oblasti slavjanskich drevnostej (1974) and later articles; summarized in Szyjewski 2003Culture Soviet/Russian structuralist mythology (Moscow school)Period 1970s-1980s, still debated

Summary

Noting that Mokosh, the East Slavic women's goddess, is absent from West Slavic sources, while Živa appears only among West Slavs and shares semantics of life/moisture/earth, Ivanov and Toporov proposed Živa was not an independent goddess but a West Slavic epithet or taboo replacement name for Mokosh, preserved after Christianization in the Polabian personal name Živa. In this reading, the 'Goddess of Ratzeburg' was Mokosh under a descriptive title 'the Living One'. The hypothesis links West and East Slavic religion and explains the distribution gap.

Participants

Vyacheslav Ivanov

Vladimir Toporov

Mokosh (East Slavic goddess)

Živa (as epithet)

Polabian Slavs as carriers

Consequences

If accepted, Živa ceases to be a separate deity and becomes evidence for a unified Slavic goddess. If rejected, she remains an isolated local figure. Most Western scholars treat it as intriguing but unproven due to lack of corroborating West Slavic Mokosh evidence.

Variants

Alternative: Živa as independent vitality goddess, Mokosh as separate earth-moisture goddess; or Živa as later folk memory of Mokosh without direct continuity.

The Return of Żywia in Contemporary Slavic Native Faith

Source Ethnography of Rodzimowierstwo / Rodnovery: groups such as Rodzima Wiara, Rodzimy Kościół Polski, Czech and Slovak pagan communities; fieldwork by Simpson & Filip 2013, etc.Culture Modern Polish, Czech, and pan-Slavic neopaganismPeriod 1990s-present

Summary

Since the 1990s, neopagan communities have revived Żywia/Živa as a living goddess, performing spring rites, name-giving ceremonies, and women's mysteries in her honor. Rituals are newly composed, often invoking her as Lady of Life, protector of children, and spring counterpart to Marzanna (goddess of winter/death). Practitioners cite Helmold and etymology as legitimation but create new liturgy, iconography (flower-crowned woman), and calendar (e.g., Jare Święto). This is not a continuation but a reconstruction/revival, openly acknowledged by most groups.

Participants

Modern Rodnovers / Rodzimowiercy

Żywia as invoked goddess

Folklorists and ethnographers documenting revival

Consequences

Gives Živa a contemporary religious life absent for 860 years, generating new folklore, art, and music. Creates feedback loop where neopagan usage is sometimes mistaken for ancient tradition.

Variants

Different groups vary liturgy: some equate Żywia with Mokosh, others keep her distinct; some celebrate her on May 1, others at summer solstice.

Relationships
Živa→Helmold of Bosaucreator

Priest and chronicler (ca. 1120-1177) whose Chronica Slavorum is the sole source for Živa. He was not a worshipper but a hostile witness who recorded her cult to illustrate pagan relapse. Without Helmold, Živa would be entirely unknown.

Živa→Niklot (Naklot), Prince of the Obodritesworshipper

Obodrite ruler (r. 1131-1160) who restored pagan cult, including that of Živa, as part of political resistance to German ecclesiastical lordship. Helmold presents him as instigator of the revival. Killed in 1160 fighting Henry the Lion.

Živa→The Obodrites / Polabian Slavs of Ratzeburgworshipper

The tribal community whose Raceburg (Ratzeburg) gord was the cult center. They are the collective worshippers implied by Raceburgensium Dea. After 1160 they were forcibly Christianized and Germanized; Polabian language survived until the 18th century in Hanover.

Živa→Ratzeburg (Raceburg, Ratibor)ally

Island gord on Lake Ratzeburg, principal stronghold of the Polabian tribe in question. Helmold identifies Živa specifically as its goddess, suggesting a shrine or sacred site there. After conquest it became a German bishopric and town; no pagan temple has been identified archaeologically.

Živa→Mokosh (Mokoš, Mokosz)ally

East Slavic goddess of earth, moisture, women's work, fate, and spinning, attested in Primary Chronicle and folk tradition. Ivanov and Toporov proposed Živa was her West Slavic epithet ('the Living One'), explaining why Mokosh is absent in West Slavic sources and Živa absent in East Slavic sources.

Živa→Pribislav, son of Niklothostile

Obodrite prince (d. 1178) who succeeded Niklot, initially continued resistance, then converted to Christianity in 1164 and became founder of the Christian House of Mecklenburg. His conversion marks the definitive end of public cult of Živa.

Živa→Henry the Lion (Heinrich der Löwe)hostile

Duke of Saxony (1129-1195) whose campaigns (1147 Wendish Crusade, 1160 war) destroyed Obodrite independence and imposed Christianity. Helmold writes from his political sphere. The cult of Živa is presented as obstacle to Henry's missionary-colonial project.

Živa→Prove, Podaga, Radegast and other Polabian deitiesally

Other Polabian gods mentioned by Helmold and contemporaries: Prove (Proue) god of Oldenburg/Starigard, Podaga of Plön, Radegast/Svarozhits of Rethra. Živa belongs to this regional pantheon, but no myth links them as family. Helmold lists them as co-objects of pagan revival.

Wendish Crusade and Forced Christianization

Source Helmold, Chronica Slavorum; Bernard of Clairvaux, letter for Wendish Crusade 1147; modern analysis in Christiansen, The Northern Crusades (1997)

Description

The context of Živa's last attestation is violent conquest. The 1147 Wendish Crusade, called by Bernard of Clairvaux with the slogan 'baptism or death,' targeted the Obodrites and Wagrians. Helmold describes devastation, tribute, and church restoration alternating with pagan revolt. After 1160, Henry the Lion's war killed Niklot, burned villages, and reimposed tithes and baptism. The cult of Živa was extinguished not by theological debate but by military defeat and colonization. Helmold, as a missionary priest, celebrates this as divine victory, obscuring Slavic perspectives.

Scholarly Context

Modern scholarship (e.g., Christiansen, Bartlett) frames this as part of the Northern Crusades and German Ostsiedlung — a colonial process involving land seizure, German settlement, and cultural erasure. Živa's disappearance is an index of that erasure.

Alleged Human Sacrifice and Pagan Atrocity Topoi

Source Helmold I,52; Adam of Bremen, Gesta Hammaburgensis; Thietmar of Merseburg, Chronicon

Description

Helmold and other German chroniclers accuse Polabian Slavs of human sacrifice, idolatrous orgies, and hostility to Christians, including at Rethra and Arkona. No such accusation is made specifically about Živa or Ratzeburg, but the general topos colors any reading of her cult. Thietmar and Adam of Bremen describe sacrifices at other shrines. Whether these reports are eyewitness or literary demonization is debated.

Scholarly Context

Scholars (Słupecki, Zaroff) caution that German clerical sources used human-sacrifice accusations as propaganda to justify crusade. Archaeology has found limited evidence for ritual killing at some Slavic sites, but none at Ratzeburg. Do not attribute human sacrifice to Živa without evidence; flag as unattested and possibly polemical.

Erasure and Germanization of the Polabians

Source Słupecki 1994; National Museum records on Polabian language extinction

Description

After Christianization, the Polabian Slavs of the Ratzeburg region were gradually Germanized; their language (Polabian) survived only in the Hanoverian Wendland until the 18th century, and their religion was demonized as witchcraft. No Polabian voice about Živa survives — only the German priest's single sentence. This archival silence is itself a colonial wound: a goddess remembered only by her conquerors' label.

Scholarly Context

Postcolonial readings of Slavic paganism emphasize that 'source scarcity' is not neutral but product of conquest and source survival bias. Responsible retelling must note that Živa's story is told entirely through hostile sources.

Contradictions & Disputes

Is Živa an independent goddess or an epithet of Mokosh?

Position A

Živa is an epithet/title of Mokosh, the East Slavic women's goddess, preserved in the West as a taboo or descriptive name ('the Living One'). Explains complementary distribution: Mokosh absent in West Slavic sources, Živa absent in East Slavic sources.

Position A — evidence

Ivanov & Toporov 1974: structural comparison, semantics of life/earth, onomastic survival; Szyjewski notes plausibility. Absence of Mokosh in Helmold despite detailed Polabian notices is suggestive.

Position B

Živa is an independent Polabian goddess, a local patron of Ratzeburg whose name happens to be a common Slavic personal name/wishing name. No evidence links her functionally to Mokosh's domains (earth, moisture, spinning).

Position B — evidence

Brückner, Słupecki, and most Western Slavists: no medieval text equates the names; Mokosh's functions are not 'life' in abstract but specific women's work; Živa's etymology is transparent and does not require Mokosh. Single-source deity should not be merged without positive evidence.

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus; hypothesis remains intriguing but unproven. Most handbooks present both views and label the identification as hypothetical/reconstructed, not attested.

Original reading of the theonym: Živa vs Siva vs Silue

Position A

Original was *Živa (*Sywa with Slavic ž = [ʒ]), from *živъ 'alive'. Supported by Copenhagen Siwa, Lübeck Siwe, Szczecin Synna (= *Syuua).

Position A — evidence

Philological analysis (Schmeidler, Brückner, Popowska-Taborska): <w> = [v], double <uu> = <w>, <u>/<n> confusion explains Synna; Slavic *ž regularly rendered as Latin s in German sources; personal name parallels confirm Živa.

Position B

Vienna Silue suggests alternative *Žilva or *Sila, or that Siwa is a Latinization of *Siva 'grey' or unrelated name.

Position B — evidence

Vienna manuscript is earliest? No, but its Silue cannot be derived from Siwa without emendation; some early antiquarians read Siva and linked to Hindu Shiva.

Scholarly Consensus

Overwhelming consensus for *Živa; Silue dismissed as scribal corruption. Modern standard is Živa/Żywia.

Historicity of Helmold's report: accurate ethnography or clerical topos?

Position A

Helmold accurately records a genuine pagan revival under Niklot, including a real local goddess at Ratzeburg, based on eyewitness or near-contemporary informants in Wagria.

Position A — evidence

Helmold is generally reliable on Obodrite politics, names Niklot and other verifiable figures, and distinguishes different tribal gods (Prove, Podaga, Siwa) rather than using generic 'idols'; his chronology matches other sources.

Position B

Helmold exaggerates or invents pagan relapse as a literary topos to justify mission, crusade, and tithe restoration; Siwa may be a generic 'idol' name or misunderstanding of a personal name/place name.

Position B — evidence

Helmold writes as missionary propagandist; no other source confirms Siwa; absence of archaeology and silence of Arnold of Lübeck raise doubt; single attestation is weak evidential basis.

Scholarly Consensus

Majority accept core historicity — Niklot did restore pagan practices — but acknowledge Helmold's bias and that details about Siwa cannot be corroborated. Treated as attested but thinly evidenced, not dismissed as fabrication.

Copenhagen/Lübeck/Szczecin forms all point to grapheme <w> = [v], reconstructable as *Syuua = *Sywa = *Živa. Vienna Silue is considered most corrupted. Double <uu> = <w> convention confirms *Sywa.

Onomastic attestations of the personal name Živa / Żywa identical to the theonym

Type documentaryDate 1336 and later medieval recordsLocator Old Polabian personal name *Živa attested as Latin Zive (1336); Old Polish surname Żywa attested as Latin Siwa, Sywa, Szywa, Szyva in 14th-15th c. documents; Serbo-Croatian Živa, Bulgarian Zhiva as living given namesLanguage Latin documents rendering Slavic vernacular; Slavic languages

Translation

Demonstrates *Živa was a productive feminine anthroponym meaning 'living', supporting interpretation of the theonym as deified personal name/epithet rather than opaque divine name.

Translation

Absence of material correlates is itself primary evidence: unlike Svantevit at Arkona or Triglav at Szczecin, Živa has no archaeology, iconography, or epigraphy. All visual reconstructions are modern inventions.

Arnold of Lübeck, Chronica Slavorum (continuation of Helmold)

Type textDate ca. 1210Locator Continuation covering 1172-1209; does not mention Siwa/Živa independentlyLanguage Latin

Translation

Silence is evidential: Arnold, who continued Helmold's chronicle and described the final Christianization of the Obodrites under Pribislav and Henry the Lion, does not repeat or expand the Živa notice, indicating her cult did not survive as a literary topos beyond Helmold's single notice.

Known Gaps
