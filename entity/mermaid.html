Who It Is

Mermaids are legendary aquatic creatures with the upper body of a human and the tail of a fish, appearing in the folklore of virtually every seafaring culture. The earliest known mermaid-like deity is Atargatis, a Syrian goddess who transformed herself into a fish out of shame after accidentally killing her lover — becoming a being half-woman, half-fish. Similar beings appear across cultures: the Greek sirens (originally bird-women, later conflated with mermaids), the Irish merrow, the Slavic rusalka, the West African Mami Wata, the Brazilian Iara, and the Ningyo of Japan. In European folklore, mermaids were often omens of disaster: sighting one was said to foretell storms, shipwrecks, or drowning. They were associated with the sirens' deadly song, luring sailors to their deaths on rocky shores — though this conflation is post-classical. Some traditions, however, portray mermaids more sympathetically: the selkies of Scottish and Irish folklore are seal-women who can shed their skins to become human, forming loving relationships with mortal men until their seal skins are found and they return to the sea. Hans Christian Andersen's 'The Little Mermaid' (1837) fundamentally transformed the mermaid in popular culture, creating the now-familiar tragic romantic figure who sacrifices everything for love — though Andersen's original ending is far darker than Disney's adaptation. Christopher Columbus and other explorers reported mermaid sightings (likely manatees or dugongs), and the 'Fiji mermaid' hoax — a taxidermied monkey torso sewn to a fish tail — was famously exhibited by P.T. Barnum. Mermaids continue to capture the popular imagination, representing the allure and danger of the unknown deep, the boundary between worlds, and the fantasy of a life beyond the human.

Attested in Folk TaleEra Atargatis (Syrian fish-goddess) c. 1000 BCE; earliest mermaid tale in One Thousand and One Nights (9th century); first English account of mermaids c. 1200

Form

The canonical mermaid is a humanoid female from the waist up, with a fish's tail from the waist down. Scales may cover the entire lower body or be limited to the tail fin. Hair is typically long and flowing, often associated with water imagery. Skin tones vary from human-like to pale, greenish, or blue-tinted. Variations include webbed hands, gills, sharp teeth, or a fish-like mouth. The male counterpart, the merman, is rarer in folklore. Depictions range from the beautiful, human-like mermaids of European tradition to the monstrous, fish-faced 'sirens' of some medieval bestiaries.

Powers

Enchanting Song The ability to sing with supernatural beauty, captivating human listeners and luring them to the sea. In some traditions, the song is irresistible and leads to death by drowning. In others, it can soothe or heal. Greek sirens (Odyssey), European folklore Some traditions note that mermaids lose their voice on land, or that their song only works at sea. In Andersen's tale, the mermaid's voice is taken by the sea witch, implying it is a removable attribute. Other traditions do not mention this.

Shape-shifting The ability to transform between human and mermaid form, or to appear as a fully human woman on land. Some traditions require a magical object (like a sealskin) or a specific ritual. Celtic merrows, selkies, Japanese ningyo Often temporary or conditional. Some mermaids cannot survive on land for long, or must return to the sea at specific times. In some tales, the mermaid is permanently human on land and permanently fish-tailed in water. In others, the transformation is voluntary and reversible.

Immortality or Extended Lifespan Mermaids are often depicted as living for centuries or being immortal, though not all traditions agree. European folklore, Andersen's tale In Andersen's tale, mermaids live for 300 years but dissolve into sea foam upon death. Some traditions hold that they can gain a soul only by marrying a human. The length of a mermaid's life varies wildly between traditions, from a few decades (some Slavic rusalki) to true immortality (some Greek Nereids).

Prophecy and Omens The ability to foresee the future, particularly storms, shipwrecks, and death. Some mermaids are said to appear as harbingers of doom. European maritime folklore Not universally attested. Some traditions see mermaids as bringers of good luck instead. The same mermaid may be a good omen in one story and a bad omen in another.

Drowning and Dragging The physical ability to drag humans underwater, either out of malice, playfulness, or a desire for companionship. European folklore, Slavic rusalki Some traditions note that mermaids are weak on land and can be overpowered by a determined human. Not all mermaids are hostile; many are merely curious or lonely.

Behavior & Habitat

Behavior varies dramatically by culture. In European folklore, mermaids are often capricious and dangerous—luring sailors to their deaths with song, or dragging swimmers underwater. They may also fall in love with humans, sometimes marrying them (with a condition, such as hiding their tail or returning to the sea on a specific day). Some traditions depict them as benevolent or even tragic figures. In West African and African-diasporic traditions, Mami Wata is a powerful, seductive spirit who brings wealth and madness. In Japanese folklore, ningyo are fish-like humanoids whose flesh grants immortality.

Accounts

Atargatis, a goddess of fertility and the moon, accidentally kills her mortal lover, a shepherd. Overcome with grief and shame, she casts herself into a lake. The gods transform her into a fish, but her beauty is so great that only her lower half becomes piscine—her upper half remains human. She becomes the first attested mermaid figure.

Odysseus, warned by Circe, stops his crew's ears with wax and has himself bound to the mast so he can hear the sirens' song without succumbing. The sirens sing of the glory of Troy and promise him knowledge of all things. Their song is so irresistible that even bound, Odysseus begs to be released.

A young mermaid princess falls in love with a human prince after saving him from a shipwreck. She makes a deal with the sea witch: her voice in exchange for legs. But the transformation is agonizing, and she must win the prince's love or die. The prince marries another woman, and the mermaid is offered a chance to save herself by killing him—but she refuses. She dissolves into sea foam, but is given a chance at an eternal soul through good deeds.

The merrow (from Irish 'murúch', sea-maid) is a mermaid of Irish waters. She wears a magical cap, the 'cohuleen druith', which allows her to travel underwater. If a human steals her cap, she is trapped on land and may marry a mortal. But if she ever recovers her cap, she will return to the sea, often leaving her human family behind.

The rusalka is the spirit of a young woman who died by drowning, often a suicide or an unbaptized child. She appears as a beautiful, pale maiden with wet, green hair. She lives in rivers and lakes, and during the week of Rusalia (the summer solstice), she comes ashore to dance in the fields. She lures young men into the water and drowns them, seeking companionship in death.

Field Notes

❀ The word 'mermaid' comes from the Old English 'merewif' (sea-woman), which predates the Middle English 'mermayde' by several centuries.

❀ In the 16th century, the English explorer Henry Hudson reported sighting a mermaid in the Arctic Ocean. His crew described her as having 'a very white body' and 'a tail like a porpoise'.

❀ The Starbucks logo is a siren, not a mermaid—though the distinction is often lost. The original 1971 logo was based on a 16th-century Norse woodcut of a two-tailed siren.

❀ The Japanese ningyo (人魚) is not a beautiful woman. It is a fish with a human face, and its flesh is said to grant immortality. Eating it is a serious taboo.

❀ In some Celtic traditions, mermaids are not separate beings but the souls of drowned maidens, doomed to lure others to the same fate.

❀ The 'Mermaid of Warsaw' is a symbol of the city of Warsaw, Poland, and appears on its coat of arms. She is a protector, not a seductress.

❀ The first known depiction of a mermaid in a work of fiction is in the 'Arabian Nights' (One Thousand and One Nights), in the tale of 'The City of Brass'.

Cultural Significance

Represents liminality — the boundary between known and unknown, human and nature, land and sea. Symbol of feminine allure and danger. Andersen's tale made the mermaid a tragic romantic archetype.

A way to hold it in your mind

The mermaid is the sea's most persistent metaphor: the boundary between the human and the wild, the known and the unknown. She is the sailor's fever dream, the poet's muse, the child's fantasy, and the scholar's puzzle. She can be a goddess, a monster, a victim, a predator, a lover, or a warning. She is a mirror that reflects whatever we project onto her—and she always has been. To study the mermaid is to study the human imagination itself, and to see how we have tried to make sense of the vast, unknowable ocean that surrounds us.
