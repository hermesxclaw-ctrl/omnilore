Who It Is

Before she was a Disney princess with a seashell bra, before she was a Starbucks logo, before she was a meme about the ocean's top predators—the mermaid was a goddess who drowned herself for love, a fish-faced prophet who taught humanity the arts of civilization, and a bird-woman whose song promised sailors the knowledge of all things. The mermaid is the sea's oldest secret, a creature who has been worshipped, feared, desired, and sanitized for over four thousand years. She is a mirror of our own longing for the unknown, and a warning about what happens when we try to pull the wild into our world. From the blood-soaked temples of ancient Syria to the neon-lit depths of an anime ocean, the mermaid has never stopped changing—and she has never stopped luring us in.

Behavior varies dramatically by culture. In European folklore, mermaids are often capricious and dangerous—luring sailors to their deaths with song, or dragging swimmers underwater. They may also fall in love with humans, sometimes marrying them (with a condition, such as hiding their tail or returning to the sea on a specific day). Some traditions depict them as benevolent or even tragic figures. In West African and African-diasporic traditions, Mami Wata is a powerful, seductive spirit who brings wealth and madness. Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Middle English 'mermayde', a compound of 'mere' (sea, lake) and 'mayde' (maid). The male form 'merman' follows the same pattern. The word entered English around the 14th century, replacing the older Old English 'merewif' (sea-woman).

Powers
Enchanting Song

The ability to sing with supernatural beauty, captivating human listeners and luring them to the sea. In some traditions, the song is irresistible and leads to death by drowning. In others, it can soothe or heal.

Limit: Some traditions note that mermaids lose their voice on land, or that their song only works at sea.

Shape-shifting

The ability to transform between human and mermaid form, or to appear as a fully human woman on land. Some traditions require a magical object (like a sealskin) or a specific ritual.

Limit: Often temporary or conditional. Some mermaids cannot survive on land for long, or must return to the sea at specific times.

Immortality or Extended Lifespan

Mermaids are often depicted as living for centuries or being immortal, though not all traditions agree.

Limit: In Andersen's tale, mermaids live for 300 years but dissolve into sea foam upon death. Some traditions hold that they can gain a soul only by marrying a human.

Prophecy and Omens

The ability to foresee the future, particularly storms, shipwrecks, and death. Some mermaids are said to appear as harbingers of doom.

Limit: Not universally attested. Some traditions see mermaids as bringers of good luck instead.

Drowning and Dragging

The physical ability to drag humans underwater, either out of malice, playfulness, or a desire for companionship.

Limit: Some traditions note that mermaids are weak on land and can be overpowered by a determined human.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The canonical mermaid is a humanoid female from the waist up, with a fish's tail from the waist down. Scales may cover the entire lower body or be limited to the tail fin. Hair is typically long and flowing, often associated with water imagery. Skin tones vary from human-like to pale, greenish, or blue-tinted. Variations include webbed hands, gills, sharp teeth, or a fish-like mouth. The male counterpart, the merman, is rarer in folklore. Depicti

Symbols — The mermaid symbolizes the duality of human nature—the civilized and the wild, the rational and the instinctual. In Christian iconography, she represents lust and temptation, often depicted as a symbol of the sin of vanity. In maritime culture, mermaids are omens: sometimes of storms and death, sometimes of good fortune. The mermaid also symbolizes the dangerous allure of the unknown, and the boundary between the human world and the natural world

Domains — Primarily saltwater seas and oceans, but also freshwater rivers, lakes, and wells. In some traditions mermaids dwell in underwater palaces of coral and pearl. The boundaries between salt and fresh water are often significant: some mermaids lose their power or die in freshwater, while others (like the Slavic rusalka) are exclusively freshwater spirits.

Accounts I
The Legend of AtargatisLucian of Samosata, 'On the Syrian Goddess'

c. 1000 BCE (attested 2nd century CE)

Atargatis, a goddess of fertility and the moon, accidentally kills her mortal lover, a shepherd. Overcome with grief and shame, she casts herself into a lake. The gods transform her into a fish, but her beauty is so great that only her lower half becomes piscine—her upper half remains human. She becomes the first attested mermaid figure. Atargatis becomes a goddess of the sea, worshipped in temples with sacred fish pools. Her cult spreads across the Near East.

The Sirens of the OdysseyHomer, 'Odyssey', Book XII

c. 8th century BCE

Odysseus, warned by Circe, stops his crew's ears with wax and has himself bound to the mast so he can hear the sirens' song without succumbing. The sirens sing of the glory of Troy and promise him knowledge of all things. Their song is so irresistible that even bound, Odysseus begs to be released. Odysseus survives, but the sirens, having failed to lure him, are said to have thrown themselves into the sea and drowned.

Accounts II
The Little MermaidHans Christian Andersen

1837

A young mermaid princess falls in love with a human prince after saving him from a shipwreck. She makes a deal with the sea witch: her voice in exchange for legs. But the transformation is agonizing, and she must win the prince's love or die. The prince marries another woman, and the mermaid is offered a chance to save herself by killing him—but she refuses. She dissolves into sea foam, but is given a chance at an eternal soul through good deeds. The tale ends tragically, but with a spiritual redemption. It is a meditation on sacrifice, unrequited love, and the longing for an immortal soul.

The Merrow of Irish FolkloreIrish oral tradition, collected in 'Fairy and Folk Tales of the Irish Peasantry' (W.B. Yeats, 1888)

Medieval to modern

The merrow (from Irish 'murúch', sea-maid) is a mermaid of Irish waters. She wears a magical cap, the 'cohuleen druith', which allows her to travel underwater. If a human steals her cap, she is trapped on land and may marry a mortal. But if she ever recovers her cap, she will return to the sea, often leaving her human family behind. The merrow's children are often said to have webbed fingers or scales on their skin. The tale of the stolen cap is a warning about the impossibility of fully containing the wild.

The Rusalka of Slavic FolkloreSlavic oral tradition

Medieval to modern

The rusalka is the spirit of a young woman who died by drowning, often a suicide or an unbaptized child. She appears as a beautiful, pale maiden with wet, green hair. She lives in rivers and lakes, and during the week of Rusalia (the summer solstice), she comes ashore to dance in the fields. She lures young men into the water and drowns them, seeking companionship in death. The rusalka is a tragic figure, both victim and predator. She represents the danger of untimely death and the liminal space between the living and the dead.

Mermaids are always beautiful, romantic figures — Many traditional mermaids are monstrous, terrifying, or simply strange. The Japanese ningyo is a fish-faced creature whose flesh grants immortality. The Slavic rusalka is a vengeful ghost. The male merrow is described as having a pig-like snout.

The Little Mermaid is a story about a girl who marries a prince — Andersen's original tale ends with the mermaid dissolving into sea foam. The happy ending is a Disney invention.

Sirens are mermaids — In classical Greek mythology, sirens are bird-women. The fish-tail is a later medieval addition.

Mermaids are a purely European myth — Fish-tailed humanoids appear in the myths of Mesopotamia, West Africa (Mami Wata), Japan (ningyo), South America (Iara), and many other cultures.

Mermaids are always female — Male mermen exist in folklore, though they are less common and often depicted as uglier or more monstrous than their female counterparts.
