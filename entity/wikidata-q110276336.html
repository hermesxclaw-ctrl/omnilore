Who It Is

Before Athens swallowed Attica whole, there were twelve cities. Sphettus was one — a hill-country town on the road that winds around the northern shoulder of Mount Hymettus, where the fifty sons of Pallas once gathered an army to take Athens by surprise, and where, centuries later, Socrates found one of his most loyal listeners.

As a community, Sphettus functioned as a typical Attic deme: a local citizen assembly (agora/deme assembly) under a demarch (dēmarchos), managing local cults, land registers (lēxiarchikon grammateion), festivals, and deme finances. Its citizens (Sphēttioi) participated in Athenian democracy as members of Acamantis, serving as bouleutai, jurors, hoplites and trireme crews. Mythic character: in legend, Sphettus is associated with the Pallantid faction — hostile to central Athens — suggesting a tradition of inland aristocratic resistance to Thesean synoecism.

Powers
Polis autonomy (mythic pre-synoecism)

In Atthidographic tradition, Sphettus as one of the twelve cities of Cecrops exercised independent kingship/political control over its district before Theseus's synoecism. No details of institutions preserved.

Limit: Mythic-historiographic reconstruction, not contemporary evidence; other traditions give different numbers/lists of cities.

Deme self-government (Cleisthenean)

After 508/7 BCE, Sphettus as deme had an assembly of demotai, elected demarch, deme treasurers, and power to pass decrees (psēphismata), manage deme land, cults, and membership rolls; maintained lēxiarchikon grammateion (citizen register).

Limit: Subject to polis law and oversight; could not conduct foreign policy, coinage, or war independently.

Bouleutic representation

Sphettus contributed 3 members to the Athenian Boule of 500 (50 per tribe), indicating a mid-sized deme. Bouleutai served annual terms, proposed legislation, and oversaw administration.

Limit: Quota may have changed after 307/6 BCE with creation of Macedonian tribes; 3 is Classical quota.

Military levy

As deme, Sphettus furnished hoplites and later ephebes to the Athenian army via its tribe Acamantis; demesmen served in tribal regiments. Pallantid myth implies earlier capacity to field a force marching on Athens.

Limit: No specific Sphettian casualty list or general preserved; mythic army size (50 Pallantidae plus allies) is legendary.

Cult and festival organization

Deme managed local sacrifices, sanctuaries, and participation in polis festivals (Dionysia, Panathenaia, Eleusinia). Demarch presided over deme Dionysia and other rites.

Limit: No Sphettian sacred calendar or sanctuary inventory has been found, unlike Erchia or Thorikos.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No ancient description of the town's appearance survives. As a deme, Sphettus was a nucleated village with surrounding farmland (chora) on the western Mesogeia plain, at the interface of the plain and the northern foothills of Mount Hymettus (modern Imittos). The terrain is rolling limestone hills, scrub maquis, olive and vine cultivation, and seasonal streams draining toward the Saronic Gulf. Surface survey (Traill, Goette, Lohmann) reports Clas

Symbols — No deme-specific coinage or emblem is attested. As member of tribe Acamantis (eponym Acamas, son of Theseus), Sphettus shared tribal iconography of the hero Acamas. Deme decrees would have used the standard Athenian formulae and seals; funerary monuments of Sphēttioi follow Attic white-ground and marble stele conventions with no distinctive local style identified. No cult statue or deme emblem is recorded.

Domains — Geographic domain: western Mesogeia (or eastern Paralia per variant tradition), inland plain east of Hymettus, altitude c. 150-250m, c. 16 km southeast of Athens acropolis, c. 3-4 km northwest of modern Koropi. Political domain: trittys of Acamantis (Mesogaea or Paralia); deme quota 3 bouleutai. Accounts I
The Twelve Cities of Cecrops and the Synoecism of TheseusStrabo 9.1.20 citing Philochorus FGrH 328; Plutarch Theseus 24; Stephanus of Byzantium

Mythic Bronze Age, codified 4th century BCE - 1st century CE

Atthidographers record that Attica was originally divided into twelve independent poleis under Cecrops, one of which was Sphettus. Theseus, upon becoming king of Athens, persuaded or compelled the twelve to undergo synoecism (synoikismos) — dissolving their separate prytaneia and councils and centralizing government on the Athenian Acropolis, while allowing local residence. Sphettus thus lost polis independence but survived as a place-name and later deme. Strabo's list preserves the canonical dodecapolis; Philochorus gave the constitutional details. Aetiology for Athenian unification and for the persistence of deme identities after Cleisthenes; Sphettus's memory as former polis justified its later status and pride. Provided ideological charter for Athenian centralism.

Pallas and the Pallantidae March from SphettusPlutarch Theseus 13; Pausanias 1.22.2 (Pallas); Euripides fragment; Harpocration s.v. Pallantidae

Mythic generation before Theseus (Late Bronze Age in myth), recorded Classical-Hellenistic to Roman

When Aegeus ruled Athens without a recognized heir, his brother Pallas, who had received the inland districts (Mesogaea/Paralia) as his lot, expected to succeed. Pallas and his fifty sons (Pallantidae) plotted to seize Athens. According to Plutarch, they divided forces: Pallas with part marched openly from Gargettus, while his sons marched from Sphettus along the Sphettian Way to take Athens from the opposite side in a pincer. The herald Leos of Agnus betrayed the plan to Theseus, who had just arrived and been recognized by Aegeus. Theseus ambushed the Sphettus contingent at Gargettus and slaughtered the Pallantidae, securing his succession. Pallas's fate varies. Establishes Sphettus as Pallantid base and explains the Sphettian Way's strategic importance; aetiology for enmity between Pallene/Gargettus/Sphettus and Athens; Theseus's first political-military victory. Ritual consequence: Pallantidae's descendants allegedly excluded from certain priesthoods.

The Sphettian Way — Road Around HymettusPlutarch Theseus 13; Harpocration s.v. Sphēttia hodos; Photius Lexicon; Modern topographical studies (Pritchett, Traill, Goette)

Archaic to Roman road use, described Classical to Byzantine lexicography

The Sphettian Way was the upland route from the Mesogeia plain to Athens that passed via Sphettus and around the northern tip of Mount Hymettus, as opposed to the coastal road or the pass through Hymettus. Its existence is inferred from the Pallantid strategy: to march 'round the northern extremity of Hymettus' (Smith) and approach Athens from the north-east. Lexicographers gloss 'Sphēttia hodos' as a known road. Modern topographers trace it via modern Koropi-Spata-Athens corridor, controlling access to the plain and Hymettian marble/honey trade. Explains Sphettus's strategic value; road remained in use through Classical and Roman periods for deme communication with asty; modern identification of Sphettus depends on reconstructing this road.

Accounts II
Aeschines of Sphettus — Socratic Philosopher and Deme CelebrityDiogenes Laertius 2.60-64; Plato Apology 33e; Xenophon Memorabilia; Lysias fragment; Cicero De Inventione

c. 430 - c. 350 BCE (late 5th - early 4th century BCE)

Aeschines, son of Lysanias, demesman of Sphettus (Aischinēs Sphēttios), was a devoted follower of Socrates, present at Socrates's trial and death (Phaedo 59b). Too poor to pay Sophists, he worked as a clerk and perfume-seller to support his family while studying philosophy. After Socrates's execution, he wrote Socratic dialogues (Aspasia, Alcibiades, Telauges, etc.), of which fragments survive, and taught rhetoric, later living in Syracuse at the court of Dionysius. His deme epithet 'of Sphettus' is his standard identifier in all sources, making Sphettus famous through its most famous son. Plato and Xenophon mention him as part of Socratic circle. Sphettus gains lasting fame in philosophy; the demotic 'Sphēttios' becomes a philosophical brand; modern scholarship on Socratic literature centers on Aeschines's dialogues. Deme identity preserved through prosopography.

Cleisthenes Creates the Deme Sphettus (508/7 BCE)Herodotus 5.66-69; Aristotle Athenaion Politeia 21; IG I³ 102; Whitehead 1986; Traill 1975, 1986

Late Archaic, 508/7 BCE

Cleisthenes, to break aristocratic kinship power, reorganized Attica into 139 demes grouped into 30 trittyes and 10 tribes. Sphettus, former polis/town, was enrolled as a deme in the tribe Acamantis, with three bouleutai. Citizens henceforth registered by demotic (e.g., 'Aischinēs Sphēttios') rather than patronymic, and deme assembly took over local administration. Sphettus's boundaries were surveyed and its citizen roll (lēxiarchikon) established. The reform made Sphettus a foundational unit of Athenian democracy for the next 700 years. Sphettus transformed from village to formal democratic subunit; deme identity became hereditary even after moving to Athens; deme decrees and demarch records begin. Basis for all later epigraphic attestations.

Rediscovery: From Sphittos Toponym to Modern Koropi SurveyWilliam Smith 1854-57; Milchhoefer 1883; Pritchett 1965; Traill 1975; Goette 2001; Pleiades 579885

19th-21st century CE

Early travelers (Leake, Ross) noted the toponym Sphittos surviving near Koropi. Milchhoefer and later Pritchett argued Sphettus must lie on the Sphettian Way around north Hymettus, northwest of Koropi. Surface surveys in the 1960s-80s (Traill, Lauter, Lohmann) found Classical sherds and terrace walls below the chapel of Agios Christos northwest of Koropi, now the accepted location (Pleiades coordinates 37.925°N 23.857°E, precision 1000m). No systematic excavation has been conducted; identification remains based on toponymy, road logic, and sherd scatter, not inscriptions in situ. The site is on private agricultural land and not developed as an archaeological park. Sphettus placed on modern archaeological maps (Barrington Atlas Map 59, Pleiades, ToposText); Barrington and Pleiades list it as 'unlocated precise' with 1km uncertainty; heritage management remains minimal.

Sphettus was a major city like Athens or Eleusis — In Classical times Sphettus was a medium-sized deme (3 bouleutai, c. 300-400 adult male citizens) — a village and farmland, not a walled city. Its 'city' status belongs only to the mythic dodecapolis tradition before synoecism, which is a retrojected origin story, not a Bronze Age urban reality.

Aeschines of Sphettus was the famous orator Aeschines who opposed Demosthenes — Two different men: Aeschines Socraticus of Sphettus (c. 430-350 BCE, Socratic philosopher, tribe Acamantis) vs. Aeschines the orator of Cothocidae (389-314 BCE, tribe Pandionis). Ancient sources already confused them; Diogenes Laertius explicitly distinguishes them.

The Sphettian Way went over Mount Hymettus — Ancient and modern topographers agree it went around the northern end of Hymettus, not over the summit. Pallas's pincer strategy required a northern circuit to approach Athens from the northeast while Pallas came from Gargettus. Over-mountain path would be impassable for an army.

Sphettus has been excavated and is a visitable archaeological site — No systematic excavation has been published; identification rests on toponymy and surface sherds NW of Koropi below Christos chapel on private farmland. There is no archaeological park, museum, or visible monument labeled Sphettus. Pleiades and Barrington mark it as approximate.
