Who It Is

Say 'Lamas' and you summon three worlds at once: a circle of maroon-robed Tibetan teachers whose title means 'none above,' a sun-baked hill town in the Peruvian Amazon where Quechua is still sung in the plaza, and a scatter of stone-walled parishes in northern Portugal whose name means mud and marsh. One word, four etymologies, and centuries of travelers who confused them.

(1) Lamas as religious role: teachers of Dharma, givers of refuge, tantric initiation (wang), and pastoral care; expected to exhibit bodhicitta, vinaya discipline, and scholarly mastery; behaviors range from scholastic debate to retreat. Reincarnate lamas trained from childhood. (2) Lamista people: Quechua-speaking agriculturalists, known for dry-land farming, pottery, and syncretic Catholic-Andean festivals (Fiesta de Santa Rosa de Lima, Carnaval Lamista). (3) No personality for toponyms; LAMAS society behavior is scholarly publication, excavation, and lecture program.

Powers
Dharma Teaching and Transmission (Tibetan lama)

Authority to give teachings (lung), explanations (tri), and tantric empowerments (wang/kur) that authorize practice. Attested as lineage-based ritual competence, not supernatural power.

Limit: Requires lineage authorization; cannot invent empowerments; vinaya rules restrict certain teachings to prepared students; not all lamas hold tantric lineage.

Tulku Recognition and Continuity

Reincarnate lamas (tulku, Mong. khutukhtu) are identified as continuations of predecessors via divination, test of possessions, and oracular confirmation. Provides institutional continuity.

Limit: Recognition disputed between claimants; Chinese state claims authority over reincarnation approvals (2007 State Religious Affairs Bureau Order No. 5); many lamas are not tulkus.

Lamista Ecological Knowledge (Peruvian Lamas)

Collective agroecological expertise: drought-resistant maize, cotton, and pijuayo cultivation, forest management, and medicinal plant knowledge maintained by Lamas Quechua communities.

Limit: Knowledge is community-distributed and threatened by land pressure, Spanish-language schooling, and migration; not individually 'owned.'

Institutional Authority and Legal Standing

Lamas (abbots, incarnates) historically held estates, legal courts, and taxation rights in Tibet and Mongolia; modern Lamas Province municipality holds administrative powers (taxation, planning); LAMAS society holds publishing and archaeological authority.

Limit: Tibetan lama political power ended 1959 in Tibet; Peruvian province powers limited by national decentralization law; LAMAS has no statutory planning power.

Accounts I
The Etymology and Codification of bla-ma as 'None Above'Sakya Pandita Kunga Gyeltsen, sDom gsum rab dbye (Clear Differentiation of the Three Vows), c. 1232; Je Tsongkhapa, Lamrim Chenmo, 1402

13th-15th century

Tibetan scholasticism formalized bla-ma as the indispensable spiritual teacher. Sakya Pandita distinguished bla-ma from ordinary monk, requiring ten qualifications (knowledge, ethics, compassion). Tsongkhapa systematized guru devotion (bla-ma'i rnal 'byor) as foundation of the path. The title expanded from 'venerable' to institutional head of monastery and reincarnation line, spreading to Mongolia as 'lama' for any monk. Established guru devotion as central to Tibetan Buddhism; created lineage-based authority structure still extant; Mongolian usage broadened term.

The Founding of Lamas and the Wayku MitmaSpanish colonial chronicles (Relaciones Geográficas, 1580s), Scazzocchio 1979 'Curare y Mitmas,' and Peruvian Law of 10 October 1933 creating Provincia de Lamas

Pre-Inca to 1933

The Lamistas are descendants of Chanka and Pocra mitma (resettled colonists) moved by the Inca from the Ayacucho/Andahuaylas region to the eastern frontier to control the Chachapoyas and Moyobamba. After Inca collapse and Spanish conquest, they coalesced on the hill of Lamas. Spanish founded Santa Cruz de los Motilones de Lamas in 1656. Republican Peru made Lamas capital of its province in 1933, separating it from San Martín. The Wayku barrio preserves Quechua language and distinct identity. Created a Quechua-speaking enclave in Amazonian lowlands, culturally distinct from surrounding mestizo and Awajún populations; Wayku now recognized as comunidad nativa/kechwa.

Lamas Quechua: Survival of San Martín QuechuaPark, Carlson & Coombs, A Grammar of San Martín Quechua (2011); Ethnologue qvs; Ministry of Culture Peru, Base de Datos de Pueblos Indígenas

Documented 20th-21st century; language roots to 15th century mitma

Lamas Quechua (qvs) is the southernmost Northern Quechua variety, isolated from main Quechua continuum. Despite pressure from Spanish, it remains vigorous in Wayku and surrounding hamlets, used in agriculture, festivals, and increasingly in bilingual intercultural education. It retains archaic features lost elsewhere and shows Amazonian substrate influence. Recognized as living indigenous language; bilingual schools established; orthography standardized; UNESCO Atlas lists as vulnerable but not endangered.

Accounts II
Santa Maria de Lamas and the Cork EmpireArquivo Municipal de Santa Maria da Feira; Alves 1998 História da Indústria da Cortiça; Museu de Santa Maria de Lamas

12th century (parish attested) to 20th century industrial peak

Santa Maria de Lamas, in Aveiro District, grew from a medieval marshland parish (lama = bog) to Portugal's cork capital in the early 20th century under industrialist Henrique Amorim. The Museu de Santa Maria de Lamas (founded 1959) houses his eclectic collection. The parish exemplifies how a toponym describing marginal land became an industrial center. Created Portugal's cork museum and archive; parish became freguesia with 5,000+ inhabitants; name Lamas preserved despite 2013 merger proposals.

Lamas, Norfolk and the Domesday Clay VillageDomesday Book (1086) folio 136r; Ekwall, Concise Oxford Dictionary of English Place-Names (1960); Norfolk Heritage Explorer

1086 to present

Recorded as Lamers/Lammas in Domesday as a small manor in the Hundred of South Erpingham, held by William de Warenne. The name derives from Old English lām 'loam, clay' reflecting heavy boulder-clay soils by the River Bure. Now part of civil parish Buxton with Lamas, it retains St Andrew's Church (14th century) and Lammas Hall. Preserved as conservation village; example of Norfolk clay-land settlement; often confused with Lammas festival (loaf-mass) due to spelling.

LAMAS: The London and Middlesex Archaeological SocietyLAMAS Transactions, Vol.1 (1856) inaugural address; Society charter 1855; Royal Historical Society records

1855-present

Founded 14 December 1855 at Crosby Hall, Bishopsgate, to investigate the archaeology and history of London and Middlesex. Published Transactions of the London and Middlesex Archaeological Society continuously since 1856, excavated Roman London, and campaigned for preservation of historic buildings. Abbreviation LAMAS appears in archaeological citations across the UK. Major publisher of London archaeology; archive now at Museum of London; LAMAS acronym widely used in Historic Environment Records.

Lamas, Peru was founded by Tibetan lamas or named after them. — No historical connection; Peruvian Lamas derives from Quechuan ethnonym Lamista and Spanish colonial foundation Santa Cruz de los Motilones de Lamas (1656). Tibetan term never used in Andes before 20th-century tourism.

All Tibetan lamas are reincarnations and can perform miracles. — Most lamas are appointed teachers/scholars; only a minority are recognized tulkus (reincarnates). Canonical texts warn against displaying siddhis; miraculous powers are hagiographic, not institutional requirement.

Lamas and llamas are the same word; the animal is named after the priest. — Distinct etymologies: lama < Tibetan bla-ma via Mongolian; llama < Quechua llama (camelid). Linnaean genus Lama for animal is coincidental homograph from Quechua, not Tibetan.

Lamas Quechua is just Spanish-accented Quechua or a dialect of Inca Quechua. — It is a distinct Northern Quechua variety with unique phonology, lexicon with Amazonian loanwords, and history as a mitma transplant, not directly descended from Cusco Inca Quechua.

LAMAS is an organization of Tibetan lamas in London. — LAMAS is the London and Middlesex Archaeological Society (1855), a British archaeology society; acronym collision only.
