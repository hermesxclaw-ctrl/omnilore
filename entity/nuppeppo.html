Who It Is

A blob-like mass of sagging, rotting flesh with rudimentary hands and feet. It has a vague humanoid shape, no discernible facial features except dark indentations, and emits an overwhelming stench of rotting meat. Despite its horrifying appearance, it is passive and harmless.

Form

Amorphous blob of pale, sagging flesh, vaguely humanoid; about the size of a human; dark eye-like spots; folds of fat obscuring features

Powers

Flesh-like malleability

Overpowering odor

Slow movement

Flesh of Eternal Youth Those who consume nuppeppō's flesh are said to be granted eternal youth. In the Isshōwa account, the creature was retroactively identified as the 'Feng' (封) from Chinese pharmaceutical texts, whose meat is a panacea that 'grants great power' and can be made into a powerful curative medicine. Isshōwa (Bunka period); Bái Zé Tú (Chinese text reference) One must acquire and consume the flesh — the nuppeppō must be caught, killed, and butchered, which the 1609 Sunpu Castle account suggests is extremely difficult due to the creature's speed. The authenticity of the Chinese Feng identification is uncertain. The panacea claim appears only in the retrospective Chinese textual identification, not in the original Japanese folk tradition. It may represent a later scholarly synthesis rather than authentic folk belief.

Supernatural Speed and Elusiveness The nikujin (meat-person) sighted at Sunpu Castle in 1609 moved so fast it could not be captured despite the efforts of multiple servants. It was only after Tokugawa Ieyasu ordered it driven away rather than captured that the servants succeeded in forcing it into the mountains. Isshōwa (Bunka period, retelling of 1609 event) This speed appears specific to the Sunpu Castle account and may be a property of the nikujin form rather than nuppeppō generally. Other accounts do not mention speed. Other descriptions portray nuppeppō as slow and lumbering. The Shinpachiro Mayama (Isshōwa) version may describe a different creature entirely.

Deceptive Transformation (Impersonation) Nuppeppō can impersonate a human, appearing as an ordinary person before revealing its true form. The Shingo Zade Hōdai Mōgyū notes that 'in the past, they'd come disguised as a doctor, but now they just come as is.' Katsumi Tada interprets this as a performance of 'whitening' — the creature feigns ignorance, approaches with apparent friendliness, then reveals its terrifying form. Shingo Zade Hōdai Mōgyū (1781); Katsumi Tada's analysis The impersonation is described as ultimately transparent — the creature eventually reveals itself. The 'doctor' disguise mentioned may reflect a specific satirical target (criticism of incompetent doctors). This directly connects nuppeppō to the noppera-bō tradition of faceless impersonators. Some scholars argue they are the same entity; others maintain they are distinct.

Overpowering Odor Nuppeppō emits a repulsive body odor said to rival the smell of rotting flesh. The stench lingers in its wake and is the creature's most immediately noticeable feature. Affiliated lore suggests the creature is 'born as the changed form of dead meat,' which would explain its decomposing odor. Shōwa/Heisei period folklore literature; yokai.com database The original primary source for the rotting-meat birth origin is unknown. It may be a modern invention similar to Fujisawa's temple association. Not mentioned in any Edo-period source. The stench description appears exclusively in post-Shōwa literature.

Fat-Sucking and Needle-Eating The Shingo Zade Hōdai Mōgyū describes nuppeppō as sucking the fat from corpses and eating 'to the fullest with a needle.' This may reference a folk belief about the creature's feeding habits, or it may be a comic/absurdist element consistent with the sharebon genre. Shingo Zade Hōdai Mōgyū (1781, sharebon genre) Sharebon are comic and ribald publications, not ethnographic records. This detail may be satirical rather than factual folklore. No other source describes feeding behavior. The yokai.com database states 'Diet: unknown.'

Natural Elusiveness Beyond supernatural speed, nuppeppō is simply difficult to catch or pin down. In the old picture book by Inui Yūhei, it is described as 'the disguised form of an old toad' — a creature known for its sudden movements and ability to hide. Inui Yūhei manuscript (Shisui Library); Shingo Zade Hōdai Mōgyū The toad connection may be a late Edo literary device rather than folk belief. The toad identification is unique to the Inui Yūhei manuscript and contradicts the 'dead meat' origin theory.

Behavior & Habitat

Wanders aimlessly at night, particularly in graveyards and deserted streets; said that eating its flesh grants immortality

Accounts

In 1609 (Keichō 14), at the courtyard of Sunpu Castle in Suruga Province (present-day Shizuoka), a bizarre creature appeared: a blob of flesh shaped like a young child, with hands but no fingers. It could barely be called human — observers named it a nikujin ('meat-person'). Guards immediately recognized it as a yōkai, given that entering a high-security shōgunal castle was impossible for any ordinary being. When they tried to capture it, the creature moved with such impossible speed that it evaded every attempt. Tokugawa Ieyasu, the retired shōgun who resided at Sunpu Castle, ordered the servants to drive it out rather than capture it, and they succeeded in forcing the creature into the mountains. Later, a person knowledgeable in Chinese pharmacology identified the creature as the 'Feng' (封) described in ancient Chinese texts, specifically the Bái Zé Tú (White Marsh Diagram). According to this interpretation, eating the creature's flesh would grant extraordinary power and serve as a panacea. The scholar regretted the missed opportunity for what would have been a sovereign-specific medicine.

According to Katsumi Tada's interpretation of the word 'whitening' (白化), nuppeppō performs a three-act deception. First, it impersonates an ordinary human, approaching a lone traveler as if it were a normal person — deliberately pretending not to know or recognize its own monstrous nature. Second, it speaks in a friendly, familiar manner, putting the traveler at ease. Third, once the victim's guard is fully lowered, the nuppeppō 'becomes open and unconcealed': its features dissolve into the blank, sagging form of a white monster, as if it had wiped away its false face to reveal the featureless flesh beneath. In this view, the creature does not attack or consume — its power lies entirely in the revelation of its own formlessness. The Shingo Zade Hōdai Mōgyū adds that the nuppeppō formerly disguised itself as a doctor before adopting its current policy of appearing openly, suggesting an evolution in the yōkai's strategy over time.

In the kaidan collection Shinsetsu Hyakumonogatari (Meiwa 4/1767), a nupperibō (ぬっぺりほう) — closely related or identical to nuppeppō — made a spooky appearance at Nijōgawara in Kyoto, near the Nijō-ōhashi bridge. Victims who encountered the creature came away with thick hairs attached to their clothing, leading investigators to blame the events on 'the tricks of some furry beast' such as a mujina (badger) or tanuki (raccoon dog). This account explicitly treats the faceless being not as a genuine yōkai species but as the shapeshifting disguise of a mundane trickster animal.

Content Notes

The Bakemono no e scroll's entry on nuppeppō is preceded and followed by yōkai associated with infant death. The 13th entry (nuppeppō) appears in a sequence where the adjacent yōkai relate to a mother whose actions led to an infant's death. This context positions nuppeppō within a sub-theme of dead children and maternal grief, though the scroll provides no explanatory text to confirm this interpretation.

This is a visual sequencing observation by scholars who have analyzed the scroll's thematic groupings. It is not an explicit claim about nuppeppō's nature.

Bakemono no e (c. 1660, Brigham Young University)

The 1781 sharebon Shingo Zade Hōdai Mōgyū describes nuppeppō sucking the fat from dead bodies and eating with a needle. While the sharebon genre is comic and satirical, the detail establishes a clear association with dead flesh.

Sharebon are ribald comic fiction, not ethnographic records. The corpse consumption may be a darkly humorous invention rather than a genuine folk belief.

Shingo Zade Hōdai Mōgyū (1781)

Modern folklore literature states that nuppeppō is 'born as the changed form of dead meat' — effectively spontaneous generation from decaying flesh. However, the original primary source for this claim is unknown and cannot be verified.

This appears to be another post-hoc invention, similar to Fujisawa's temple association. It may derive from the nikujin ('meat-person') label in the Isshōwa account.

Modern yōkai databases and literature (Shōwa period onward)

The Shingo Zade Hōdai Mōgyū states nuppeppō 'used to come disguised as a doctor.' Given the sharebon's satirical nature, this likely targets incompetent or predatory medical practitioners — a common trope in Edo-period satire, where yōkai were used as vehicles for social criticism.

The doctor disguise frames nuppeppō as a parasite on the vulnerable — those seeking medical help — rather than a random monster.

Shingo Zade Hōdai Mōgyū (1781)

The retrospective identification of nuppeppō with the Chinese 'Feng' from the Bái Zé Tú represents a form of Chinese scholarly authority being imposed on a Japanese folk creature. The Sunpu Castle story's 'regret' framing — that a miracle cure was driven away — treats the creature as a resource to be consumed rather than a being to be respected.

This reflects the broader pattern of Chinese pharmacopeia being applied to Japanese folkloric creatures, sometimes erasing their indigenous character.

Isshōwa; Bái Zé Tú reference

Scholarly Debates
Distinct Species vs. Noppera-bō Variant
Nuppeppō is a distinct yōkai species with its own visual tradition (blob-like, wrinkled, with embedded face/body) and should not be conflated with noppera-bō. The Wikipedia article maintains separate entries with distinct illustrations. ⇌ Nuppeppō is simply an older name or variant form of noppera-bō. The folklorist Katsumi Tada argues that noppera-bō's modern faceless-human form is a later development, and the original concept was the nuppeppō-type merged face-body. The 1767 term 'nupperibō' (ぬっぺりほう) bridges the two names phonetically.
Evidence for A: Distinct visual traditions in Edo-period art; separate entries in major encyclopedias
Evidence for B: Shared etymological root ('nupperi'); Tada's scholarly analysis; the Shingo Zade Hōdai Mōgyū describes nuppeppō as havin
Chinese Feng Identification: Authentic or Retrospective?
The nikujin of Sunpu Castle was genuinely the Chinese Feng, and nuppeppō is simply the Japanese name for this creature. The Bái Zé Tú provides a credible textual source. ⇌ The Feng identification is a post-hoc scholarly synthesis by a Bunka-period pharmaceutical scholar. It represents Chinese textual authority being imposed on a Japanese folk creature that had no relationship to Chinese bestiary traditions.
Evidence for A: The Bái Zé Tú describes a creature whose flesh grants great power — consistent with the nikujin description
Evidence for B: No Edo-period emaki or yōkai text makes this connection; it appears only in the Isshōwa, where it is attributed to a sin
Temple Habitat: Genuine Tradition or Fujisawa's Invention?
Nuppeppō traditionally haunts abandoned temples, as stated in multiple Shōwa and Heisei period yōkai reference works. ⇌ The temple association is entirely fabricated by the folklorist Morihiko Fujisawa in his Yōkai Gadan Zenshū Nihonhen Jō, where he wrote 'on the eaves of old temples would appear the nuppepō, almost like a lump of trouble itself.' This was based on the background of Sekien's illustration, which Fujisawa misinterpreted as a literal setting.
Evidence for A: Widely repeated in popular yōkai literature from the 20th century onward
Evidence for B: No Edo-period source mentions temples; the emaki provide no habitat information; the Shingo Zade Hōdai Mōgyū mentions nu
Speed and Elusiveness: Fast or Slow?
Nuppeppō is slow and lumbering, as described in most modern yōkai databases and popular literature. ⇌ The Sunpu Castle nikujin moved with supernatural speed that prevented capture. The creature's association with toads (Inui Yūhei manuscript) also implies quick, darting movement.
Evidence for A: Modern descriptions emphasize lazy, aimless wandering
Evidence for B: Isshōwa account explicitly states it 'moved so fast, it could not be captured'
Birth from Dead Meat: Genuine Folklore or Modern Invention?
Nuppeppō is born as 'the changed form of dead meat' — effectively a spontaneous generation from decomposing human flesh. ⇌ This origin story has no basis in Edo-period sources. The original primary source is unknown and unverifiable. It is likely a modern invention, perhaps inspired by the nikujin label or Frankenstein comparisons.
Evidence for A: Repeated in several modern yōkai databases
Evidence for B: Not found in any Edo or Meiji source; Wikipedia explicitly notes 'the original primary source for this is unknown'

A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine a lump of pale, sagging human flesh the size of a child, its features blurred into a single wrinkled mass, shuffling through a moonless graveyard with the casual aimlessness of a lost pedestrian — this is the nuppeppō, a yōkai whose very name is a slur against women who wear too much makeup, a creature that may or may not be a panacea, a toad in disguise, a doctor impersonator, a faceless horror, or simply a very wrinkly sweet potato with four short limbs.
