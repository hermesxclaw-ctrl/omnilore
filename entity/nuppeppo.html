Who It Is

Imagine a lump of pale, sagging human flesh the size of a child, its features blurred into a single wrinkled mass, shuffling through a moonless graveyard with the casual aimlessness of a lost pedestrian — this is the nuppeppō, a yōkai whose very name is a slur against women who wear too much makeup, a creature that may or may not be a panacea, a toad in disguise, a doctor impersonator, a faceless horror, or simply a very wrinkly sweet potato with four short limbs.

Nuppeppō is almost entirely passive and harmless despite its horrifying appearance. It wanders aimlessly at night through deserted streets, graveyards, and abandoned temple grounds. It is described as solitary but has reportedly been seen in groups. Its primary activity seems to be lumbering about, and it may derive some satisfaction from the shock and disgust it provokes in passersby. Powers
Flesh of Eternal Youth

Those who consume nuppeppō's flesh are said to be granted eternal youth. In the Isshōwa account, the creature was retroactively identified as the 'Feng' (封) from Chinese pharmaceutical texts, whose meat is a panacea that 'grants great power' and can be made into a powerful curative medicine.

Limit: One must acquire and consume the flesh — the nuppeppō must be caught, killed, and butchered, which the 1609 Sunpu Castle account suggests is extremely difficult due to the creature's speed. The authenticity of the Chinese Feng identification is uncertain.

Supernatural Speed and Elusiveness

The nikujin (meat-person) sighted at Sunpu Castle in 1609 moved so fast it could not be captured despite the efforts of multiple servants. It was only after Tokugawa Ieyasu ordered it driven away rather than captured that the servants succeeded in forcing it into the mountains.

Limit: This speed appears specific to the Sunpu Castle account and may be a property of the nikujin form rather than nuppeppō generally. Other accounts do not mention speed.

Deceptive Transformation (Impersonation)

Nuppeppō can impersonate a human, appearing as an ordinary person before revealing its true form. The Shingo Zade Hōdai Mōgyū notes that 'in the past, they'd come disguised as a doctor, but now they just come as is.' Katsumi Tada interprets this as a performance of 'whitening' — the creature feigns ignorance, approaches with apparent friendliness, then reveals its terrifying form.

Limit: The impersonation is described as ultimately transparent — the creature eventually reveals itself. The 'doctor' disguise mentioned may reflect a specific satirical target (criticism of incompetent doctors).

Overpowering Odor

Nuppeppō emits a repulsive body odor said to rival the smell of rotting flesh. The stench lingers in its wake and is the creature's most immediately noticeable feature. Affiliated lore suggests the creature is 'born as the changed form of dead meat,' which would explain its decomposing odor.

Limit: The original primary source for the rotting-meat birth origin is unknown. It may be a modern invention similar to Fujisawa's temple association.

Fat-Sucking and Needle-Eating

The Shingo Zade Hōdai Mōgyū describes nuppeppō as sucking the fat from corpses and eating 'to the fullest with a needle.' This may reference a folk belief about the creature's feeding habits, or it may be a comic/absurdist element consistent with the sharebon genre.

Limit: Sharebon are comic and ribald publications, not ethnographic records. This detail may be satirical rather than factual folklore.

Natural Elusiveness

Beyond supernatural speed, nuppeppō is simply difficult to catch or pin down. In the old picture book by Inui Yūhei, it is described as 'the disguised form of an old toad' — a creature known for its sudden movements and ability to hide.

Limit: The toad connection may be a late Edo literary device rather than folk belief.

Accounts I
The Nikujin of Sunpu Castle (1609)Isshōwa (一宵話, 'One Evening Story') — Bunka period (1804–1818)

1609 (event) / Bunka period (written)

In 1609 (Keichō 14), at the courtyard of Sunpu Castle in Suruga Province (present-day Shizuoka), a bizarre creature appeared: a blob of flesh shaped like a young child, with hands but no fingers. It could barely be called human — observers named it a nikujin ('meat-person'). Guards immediately recognized it as a yōkai, given that entering a high-security shōgunal castle was impossible for any ordinary being. When they tried to capture it, the creature moved with such impossible speed that it evaded every attempt. Tokugawa Ieyasu, the retired shōgun who resided at Sunpu Castle, ordered the servants to drive it out rather than capture it, and they succeeded in forcing the creature into the mountains. Later, a person knowledgeable in Chinese pharmacology identified the creature as the 'Feng' (封) described in ancient Chinese texts, specifically the Bái Zé Tú (White Marsh Diagram). According to this interpretation, eating the creature's flesh would grant extraordinary power and serve as a panacea. Accounts II
The Nuppeppō and the Noppera-bō DeceptionKatsumi Tada's yōkai analysis; Shingo Zade Hōdai Mōgyū (1781)

1781 (source) / Late 20th century (analysis)

According to Katsumi Tada's interpretation of the word 'whitening' (白化), nuppeppō performs a three-act deception. First, it impersonates an ordinary human, approaching a lone traveler as if it were a normal person — deliberately pretending not to know or recognize its own monstrous nature. Second, it speaks in a friendly, familiar manner, putting the traveler at ease. Third, once the victim's guard is fully lowered, the nuppeppō 'becomes open and unconcealed': its features dissolve into the blank, sagging form of a white monster, as if it had wiped away its false face to reveal the featureless flesh beneath. In this view, the creature does not attack or consume — its power lies entirely in the revelation of its own formlessness. The Shingo Zade Hōdai Mōgyū adds that the nuppeppō formerly disguised itself as a doctor before adopting its current policy of appearing openly, suggesting an evolution in the yōkai's strategy over time. The Nuppeppō at Nijōgawara (1767)Shinsetsu Hyakumonogatari (1767); referenced in Noppera-bō folklore

1767

In the kaidan collection Shinsetsu Hyakumonogatari (Meiwa 4/1767), a nupperibō (ぬっぺりほう) — closely related or identical to nuppeppō — made a spooky appearance at Nijōgawara in Kyoto, near the Nijō-ōhashi bridge. Victims who encountered the creature came away with thick hairs attached to their clothing, leading investigators to blame the events on 'the tricks of some furry beast' such as a mujina (badger) or tanuki (raccoon dog). This account explicitly treats the faceless being not as a genuine yōkai species but as the shapeshifting disguise of a mundane trickster animal. The events were attributed to an animal shapeshifter rather than a supernatural entity, undermining the nuppeppō's independent existence.

The folklorist Katsumi Tada argues that noppera-bō's modern faceless-human form is a later development, and the original concept was the nuppeppō-type merged face-body. The 1767 term 'nupperibō' (ぬっぺりほう) bridges the two names phonetically.
