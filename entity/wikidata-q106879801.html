The Approach

In the tangled genealogies of Greek myth, where every hero and nymph seems to carry a dozen names and a hundred conflicting stories, Evandre stands as a shadowy figure—a name attached to at least two distinct traditions, each with its own claims and contradictions. One Evandre is a daughter of the river-god Strymon, linked to the founding of a city and the embrace of a god; another is a warrior in the Trojan War, whose brief appearance in Homer's Iliad has puzzled readers for millennia. Their stories, though separate, share a common thread: the difficulty of pinning down a figure who exists only in fragments, and the temptation to fill those gaps with invention rather than admit uncertainty.

Identity

Evandre (Εὔανδρος)

Pronunciation yoo-AN-dros (classical Greek); ih-VAN-der (Latinized)

Known As

EuandrosEvander (Latinized)Evander of Pallene (for the Arcadian figure)

Etymology

From Greek εὖ (eu, 'good') and ἀνήρ (aner, 'man'), meaning 'good man' or 'strong man'.

Cultural Ownership

Greek mythology, specifically within the broader Hellenic tradition; the name also appears in Roman mythology via the Arcadian Evander, but the dossier treats the Greek figures primarily.

Disputed Identifications

The name Evandre is applied to at least two distinct figures: (1) a daughter of the river-god Strymon, mother of the seer Melampous (or in some versions, of Euchenor), and (2) a Trojan warrior killed by Menelaus in the Iliad. Some later sources conflate the Arcadian Evander with the Greek figures, but ancient authors generally keep them separate. There is no evidence that the two Greek figures are the same.

Description

Appearance

No ancient source describes Evandre's appearance. For the daughter of Strymon, she is only identified as a nymph and mother; for the Trojan warrior, he is simply a warrior killed in battle. No visual representation survives.

Symbols & Iconography

None. No symbols or iconographic attributes are associated with either figure.

Behaviour & Personality

Not described in any source. The daughter of Strymon is passive, appearing only as a mother; the Trojan warrior is merely a casualty.

Domains & Habitats

The daughter of Strymon is associated with Thrace, as Strymon is a river in Thrace. The Trojan warrior is from Troy, but his specific origin is unknown.

Version Differences

The two figures are completely distinct: one is a female nymph, the other a male warrior. The name may also be confused with the Arcadian Evander, a culture hero who founded Pallantium in Italy, but that figure is separate and well-attested.

Stories & Myths

Evandre, mother of Melampous (or Euchenor)

Source Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 1.9.16; also mentioned in scholia and later mythographersCulture GreekPeriod Archaic to Roman

Summary

In one tradition, Evandre is a daughter of the river-god Strymon. She becomes the mother of the seer Melampous, either by Amythaon or by another father. Some versions instead name her as the mother of Euchenor, a warrior at Troy. The story is fragmentary and rarely told in full.

Participants

Evandre

Strymon (father)

Melampous (son, in some versions)

Amythaon (possible consort)

Euchenor (son, in other versions)

Consequences

Melampous becomes a famous seer and healer, but Evandre herself drops out of the narrative.

Variants

The identity of her son varies: Apollodorus mentions Melampous, but other sources suggest Euchenor. The father is uncertain.

Evandre, Trojan warrior killed by Menelaus

Source Homer, Iliad 16.694Culture Greek (epic poetry)Period c. 8th century BCE

Summary

During the battle over the body of Patroclus, Menelaus kills a Trojan named Evandre. The brief mention is part of a catalogue of kills, and no background is given.

Participants

Evandre

Menelaus

Consequences

Evandre dies; his death is one of many in the Iliad.

Variants

None.

The son of Evander in the Myrmidon catalogue

Source Homer, Iliad 16.194-197Culture Greek (epic poetry)Period c. 8th century BCE

Summary

Homer lists a leader of the Myrmidons as 'the son of Evander', but the name is not given. The passage is notoriously problematic and has been emended by scholars.

Participants

Consequences

The son of Evander leads troops, but his identity is unknown.

Variants

Scholiasts suggest various names, but none is authoritative.

Primary Evidence

Homer, Iliad 16.194-197 (ancient Greek epic, 8th century BCE)

Type textDate c. 8th century BCELocator Book 16, lines 194-197Language Ancient Greek

Translation

In the catalogue of the Myrmidons, Homer lists 'the son of Evander' as one of the five leaders, alongside Menesthius, Eudorus, Peisander, and Phoenix. The text does not give the son's name, and the passage is often emended.

Homer, Iliad 16.694 (ancient Greek epic, 8th century BCE)

Type textDate c. 8th century BCELocator Book 16, line 694Language Ancient Greek

Translation

During the fighting, Menelaus kills 'Euandros' (Evander), a Trojan warrior, with a spear thrust. No further details are given.

Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 1.9.16 (Greek mythographer, 1st-2nd century CE)

Type textDate 1st-2nd century CELocator Book 1, chapter 9, section 16Language Ancient Greek

Translation

Apollodorus recounts that Melampous, the famous seer, was the son of Amythaon and Idomene, but he also mentions a tradition that Melampous was the son of 'Euandros' and a nymph. The exact wording is uncertain, but the name appears in some manuscripts.

Scholia on Homer's Iliad (ancient and Byzantine commentaries)

Type textDate Various, from Hellenistic to Byzantine periodsLocator On Iliad 16.194 and 16.694Language Ancient Greek

Translation

The scholia offer conflicting explanations: some say the son of Evander in the Myrmidon catalogue is 'Peisander', others say 'Phoenix', and some even suggest that Evander is a corruption of 'Eudorus' or 'Eudoxus'. For the Trojan warrior, the scholia note that his name might be a patronymic, but no further details.
