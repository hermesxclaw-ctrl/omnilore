Who It Is

In the Gaelic-speaking world, the landscape itself is a living record of an ancient, formidable presence: the Cailleach, the divine hag who shaped mountains with her hammer and controlled the storms of winter. She is not a distant myth but a force immanent in the craggy peaks, the roaring whirlpools, and the harsh winds of the Celtic north—a figure both feared and respected, whose stories are etched into the very geography of Ireland and Scotland.

She is a personification of winter: fierce, powerful, and controlling. She herds deer, fights the spring, and is often seen as hostile to human endeavors. She is also a creator and shaper of the landscape, sometimes accidentally, sometimes intentionally. She is a liminal figure, associated with boundaries and transitions.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Old Irish Caillech ('veiled one'), adjectival form of caille ('veil'), an early loan from Latin pallium ('woollen cloak'). The name literally means 'old woman, hag' in modern Irish and Scottish Gaelic. The epithet 'Bhéarra'/'Bheurra' is debated; possibly from a word meaning 'sharp, shrill, inim

Pronunciation — Irish: [ˈkal̠ʲəx, kəˈl̠ʲax]; Scottish Gaelic: [ˈkʰaʎəx]

Aliases — Cailleach Bhéarra (Irish), Cailleach Bheurra (Scottish Gaelic), Beira, Queen of Winter (Scotland, per Mackenzie), Caillagh (Manx), Caillagh ny Groamagh (Manx, 'gloomy hag'), Digdi/Digde (in The Lament of the Old Woman of Beara)

Ownership — Gaelic cultures: Ireland, Scotland, Isle of Man. The figure is pan-Gaelic with regional variations.

Powers
Landscape Creation

She shapes mountains and hills by dropping rocks from her creel or using her hammer. She can create mountains intentionally as stepping stones or accidentally as she strides across the land.

Limit: Not specified; she is a giantess with immense strength.

Weather Control

She controls winter storms and the cold. Her staff freezes the ground. She can raise windstorms (as the Storm Hags).

Limit: Her power wanes at Bealltainn (May 1) when she turns to stone or is replaced by Brìghde.

Immortality and Regeneration

She is said to have had seven periods of youth, and she can grow younger by drinking from the Well of Youth (per Mackenzie).

Limit: In the Lament, she laments her lost youth and old age. Mackenzie says she grows younger after the longest night.

Transformation

She can turn to stone on Bealltainn and revert to humanoid form on Samhainn. In Manx legend, she appears as a giant bird.

Limit: The transformation is seasonal.

Cursing and Trickery

She can trick heroes, as with Fionn mac Cumhaill, and can throw boulders to pin enemies. She may also bestow blessings or curses on harvests.

Limit: Not specified

Form & Signs

Appearance — The Cailleach is typically depicted as a hag or old woman. In some accounts, she is a giantess with one eye, white hair, dark blue skin, and rust-colored teeth (per Mackenzie). She is often associated with a staff or hammer. In Manx tradition, she appears as a gigantic bird on St. Bride's Day.

Symbols — Associated with mountains, rocks, storms, winter, deer, and cattle. Her attributes include a hammer (for shaping hills), a staff (that freezes the ground), and a creel (wicker basket) from which she drops rocks to form mountains. She is also linked to the plaid (féileadh mòr) which she washes in the whirlpool to create snow.

Domains — She dwells in mountains, crags, and rocky places. Notable locations: Hag's Head (Cliffs of Moher), Slieve na Calliagh (Loughcrew), Slieve Gullion, Ben Cruachan, and the Gulf of Corryvreckan. She is also associated with the sea and storms.

Accounts I
The Lament of the Old Woman of BearaOld Irish poem, 8th-9th century

Early medieval

The poem is a monologue of an old woman, identified as the Cailleach, who laments her lost youth and beauty. She reflects on her past life, including having fifty foster-children and seven periods of youth. She contrasts her former glory with her current state of old age and poverty. The poem is a meditation on aging and mortality, and it has been interpreted as a Christian allegory or a pagan lament.

The Cailleach and Fionn mac CumhaillIrish folklore (Slieve Gullion)

Medieval to modern folklore

The Cailleach, known as the Calliagh Beara, tricks Fionn mac Cumhaill into diving into a lake to retrieve a ring, which she claims he lost. When he dives in, he emerges as an old man. The Cailleach's trickery highlights her cunning and power over heroes. Fionn is transformed into an old man, and the lake becomes known as the 'Lake of the Old Man'.

Accounts II
The Washing of the PlaidScottish folklore (west coast)

Modern folklore

The Cailleach ushers in winter by washing her great plaid in the Gulf of Corryvreckan. The process takes three days, and the roar of the tempest can be heard up to twenty miles inland. When finished, her plaid is white, and snow covers the land. This explains the onset of winter and the origin of snow.

The Corn DollyIrish and Scottish folklore

Pre-modern agricultural customs

The first farmer to finish the grain harvest made a corn dolly representing the Cailleach from the last sheaf. The dolly was tossed into the field of a neighbor who had not yet finished. The last farmer had to keep the dolly for the year, implying they had to feed and house the hag all winter. This custom created competition and social pressure to finish harvest quickly.

The Cailleach is a single, unified goddess across all Gaelic cultures. — The Cailleach is a complex figure with many regional variations and local manifestations. She is not a single, monolithic deity.

She is the same as the Sheela na Gig. — The connection is speculative. The word 'síle' is sometimes interpreted as 'hag', but there is no direct evidence linking the Cailleach to Sheela na Gig carvings.

The Cailleach is a malevolent witch. — She is a personification of winter and natural forces, which can be harsh but also creative. She is not inherently evil.

Beira, Queen of Winter, is an ancient Scottish goddess. — The name Beira appears only in Mackenzie's 20th-century retellings and is not attested in earlier sources.
