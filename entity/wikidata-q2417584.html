Who It Is

Imagine a god so woven into the identity of a nation that his temple is the royal mausoleum, his festival is a state holiday, and his name is a direct echo of the supreme deity of the Persian Empire. This was Aramazd, the creator god of pre-Christian Armenia, a figure who stood at the crossroads of the Zoroastrian East and the Hellenistic West. But his story doesn't end with worship—it ends with a hammer blow. In 301 CE, a Christian priest named Gregory walked into Aramazd's temple at Eriza and toppled his golden statue, shattering not just an idol, but the spiritual foundation of an entire era. The god who gave kings their crowns was himself dethroned, his memory preserved only by the very clergy who destroyed him.

As a supreme deity, Aramazd was the creator of heaven and earth, the source of all good things, and the giver of victory and kingship. He was a father god, the head of the pantheon, and a figure of authority and justice. He was not a god of chaotic or ecstatic worship but of state-sponsored, ordered ritual. He was seen as a protector of the king and the kingdom.

Aramazd was the source of all earthly blessings: harvest, fertility, health, and prosperity. His title 'Parartich' (giver of all good things) reflects this role.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No pre-Christian Armenian image or statue of Aramazd survives. Agathangelos describes a statue of Aramazd at Eriza that was made of a precious material (possibly gold, silver, or bronze overlaid with gold) and was toppled by Gregory the Illuminator. The description is not detailed enough to reconstruct its iconography. Based on his syncretism with Zeus and Ahura Mazda, he was likely depicted as a mature, bearded figure, possibly enthroned, holdin

Symbols — Likely included the eagle (a symbol of Zeus and a common Iranian royal symbol), the thunderbolt (Zeus), the scepter (kingship), and possibly solar symbols. The mountain-top temple at Ani-Kammakh suggests a connection to high places and the sky. These are inferred from his syncretism, not directly attested.

Domains — Creator of the universe, source of all fertility and abundance (the 'giver of all good things'), god of kingship and royal legitimacy, god of wisdom and counsel, supreme judge. His worship was closely tied to the Armenian monarchy and the state religion.

Unknown (pre-4th century CE)

Aramazd was believed to have created the heavens and the earth. The specific details of this creation myth are not preserved in Armenian sources, but his role as the supreme creator god is clear. Established his supremacy over the other gods.

The Birth of the GodsMovses Khorenatsi, 'History of Armenia', Book I, chapter 5

Unknown (pre-4th century CE)

Khorenatsi preserves a genealogical tradition in which Aramazd is the father of Anahit, the mother of the gods, and the grandfather of Astghik and Vahagn (through Anahit's union with Tigranes, a legendary king). This story establishes Aramazd as the patriarch of the Armenian pantheon. Created a divine family tree that mirrored the royal family structure.

Accounts II
The Royal Burial at Ani-KammakhMovses Khorenatsi, 'History of Armenia', Book II

Arsacid dynasty (1st-4th century CE)

The temple of Aramazd at Ani-Kammakh was the traditional burial place of the Arsacid kings of Armenia. This practice linked the dynasty's fate to the god's favor and made the temple a national shrine. Solidified the bond between Aramazd and the Armenian monarchy.

The Festival at BagavanMovses Khorenatsi, 'History of Armenia', Book II, chapter 62

Pre-4th century CE

Khorenatsi mentions a major festival in honor of Aramazd celebrated at Bagavan, where the king would offer sacrifices. This was a state-sponsored event that reinforced national unity. Reinforced the state religion and the king's role as its head.

The Destruction of the IdolsAgathangelos, 'History of the Armenians'

301 CE (traditional date)

After the conversion of King Tiridates III, Gregory the Illuminator led a campaign to destroy the pagan temples. He traveled to Eriza, the site of a major temple of Aramazd, and toppled the statue of the god, along with those of Anahit and Vahagn. This event marks the definitive end of Aramazd's official cult. The destruction of the temples and idols marked the triumph of Christianity over the old religion. The temple at Eriza was converted into a Christian church.
