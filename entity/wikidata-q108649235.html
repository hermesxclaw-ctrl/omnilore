Who It Is

He never speaks a line, never looses an arrow, never begs for mercy by name — and yet Agerochus of Dulichium is already marked for death the moment he steps onto Ithaca. One of more than fifty young lords who sail to court a woman who has not said yes, eat her household bare, and plot to murder her son, his entire myth is a collective crime and a collective punishment, ending on the bloody floor of Odysseus' hall.

No individual personality attested. Collective behavior attested for all suitors including Agerochus: (1) hubris and overweening (hyperēphania) - occupying another man's house uninvited; (2) parasitic consumption (Odyssey 1.106-112, 2.55-58) - slaughtering Odysseus' livestock and drinking his wine daily for ~3 years; (3) coercive courtship - pressing Penelope to choose among them while she delays with the shroud trick; (4) conspiracy to murder Telemachus (Odyssey 16.364-392, 20.241-242); (5) disrespect to guests and beggars (abuse of Odysseus disguised as beggar, Odyssey 17-18). Aristocratic Status and Martial Equipment

As a mnēstēr from Dulichium, Agerochus is by definition a man of elite birth, entitled to contend for Penelope and to bear arms. Collectively the suitors are armed with swords and spears kept in the hall (though Telemachus removes them in Odyssey 19). No supernatural powers are attested; he is a mortal human.

Limit: Mortal; outmatched by Odysseus, Telemachus, and the swineherd/cowherd with Athena's aid. Fails to string Odysseus' bow (Odyssey 21, collective failure). No individual feat is recorded.

Collective Hubris / Xenia Violation (Narrative Function)

Not a power but a narrative ability to violate hospitality norms with impunity until divine retribution. The suitors collectively consume resources and threaten the oikos. This is a moral/social role, not a supernatural ability.

Limit: Subject to dike (justice) and the wrath of Zeus Xenios and Athena; their hubris guarantees their destruction.

Accounts I
The Catalogue of the Suitors - Arrival from DulichiumHomer, Odyssey 16.247-253; Apollodorus, Epitome 7.26-27

Archaic (Homer) systematized in Roman-era mythography

With Odysseus presumed dead after 10 years at Troy and 10 years wandering, noble youths from the surrounding islands press Penelope to remarry to secure the kingship of Ithaca. From Dulichium, the largest contingent, 52 (Homer) / 57 (Apollodorus) young lords sail to Ithaca, among them Agerochus. They take up residence in Odysseus' palace, ostensibly as suitors, effectively as occupiers. Apollodorus is the only source to give Agerochus a name within this catalogue; Homer leaves him anonymous within the Dulichian fifty-two. Establishes the scale of the crisis Telemachus faces and the justification for the eventual mass killing. The Dulichian majority emphasizes that the pressure comes from outside Ithaca itself, from Odysseus' own island dependencies.

The Occupation and the Shroud TrickHomer, Odyssey 2.85-110, 19.137-156, 24.120-150

Archaic

For three years the suitors, including Agerochus, feast daily in the hall, slaughtering oxen, sheep and goats, drinking wine, and demanding Penelope choose a husband. Penelope delays them by claiming she must first weave a burial shroud for Laertes, Odysseus' father, which she weaves by day and unravels by night for three years until a maid betrays her. The suitors are depicted as idle, arrogant, and increasingly impatient, wasting the estate. Agerochus participates anonymously in this collective consumption; no individual speech or action is singled out. The trick buys time for Odysseus' return but when discovered, Penelope is forced to promise a decision, leading to the bow contest. The economic devastation of the oikos becomes the moral charge against the suitors at trial in the underworld (Odyssey 24).

The Plot to Murder TelemachusHomer, Odyssey 16.364-392, 16.411-412, 20.241-242

Archaic

When Telemachus returns from his voyage to Pylos and Sparta, Antinous proposes ambushing and killing him at sea before he reaches Ithaca, to prevent him from asserting his inheritance. The suitors collectively assent. Amphinomus, the Dulichian, dissents mildly, suggesting they first consult the gods. The ambush at the strait between Ithaca and Same fails because Athena warns Telemachus and he evades it. Agerochus, as one of the Dulichians, is implicitly complicit in this conspiracy, though not named as speaker or dissenter. The attempted murder transforms the suitors from mere parasites to capital criminals in Greek ethical terms, justifying their later killing as defensive justice rather than mere revenge. Penelope's lament (16.417-433) heightens pathos.

Accounts II
The Contest of the BowHomer, Odyssey 21.1-187, 21.245-268; Apollodorus Epitome 7.27-28

Archaic / Hellenistic summary

Penelope, prompted by Athena and Eurymachus' pressure, brings out Odysseus' great bow and announces she will marry whoever strings it and shoots through twelve axe heads. The suitors, including Agerochus, attempt in order, beginning with Leodes. All fail to string the bow; their lack of strength and arete is emphasized. Eurymachus nearly succeeds but fails. Telemachus almost strings it but is stopped by Odysseus' signal. The contest exposes the suitors' illegitimacy - they cannot wield the rightful king's weapon. Agerochus is among the unnamed majority who fail; no individual attempt is described. Failure proves the suitors are not worthy successors; Odysseus' subsequent success proves his identity and right to kill them. The axes (pelekes) become a symbol of failed usurpation.

The Mnesterophonia - Slaughter in the HallHomer, Odyssey 22.1-329; Apollodorus Epitome 7.33

Archaic / Hellenistic

After stringing the bow and shooting through the axes, Odysseus reveals himself, and with Telemachus, the swineherd Eumaeus and cowherd Philoetius, barricades the hall and slaughters the suitors. Athena assists. The suitors, trapped without their spears (removed by Telemachus), attempt to fight with tables and swords. Eurymachus and Antinous die first. Amphinomus is killed by Telemachus. The rest, including Agerochus, are shot or speared. Homer describes the scene as like fish gasping on a beach (22.384-389). Apollodorus summarizes: 'Odysseus with Telemachus and Eumaeus and Philoetius slew the suitors.' Agerochus dies here, without individual death scene, lament, or burial mention; his corpse is later removed by the maids and suitors' kin come to reclaim bodies (Odyssey 24). Restores Odysseus' household and kingship; triggers the suitors' kin's revenge attempt (Odyssey 24.413-548) and Athena's final peace. The Second Nekyia - Suitors in HadesHomer, Odyssey 24.1-204

Archaic

Hermes leads the souls (psychai) of the slain suitors, squeaking like bats, to Hades, where they encounter Achilles and Agamemnon. Amphimedon, a suitor from Ithaca, narrates their fate to Agamemnon, describing how they courted Penelope, were killed by Odysseus' stratagem, and now lie unburied in Odysseus' hall. Though Agerochus is not named, as one of the Dulichian dead he is part of this chattering crowd of ghosts. Agamemnon contrasts Penelope's fidelity with Clytemnestra's treachery. Provides post-mortem moral judgment on the suitors and closure to the epic. Their unburied state emphasizes their dishonorable death. No individual afterlife distinction is made for Agerochus.

Agerochus is named by Homer among Penelope's suitors. — Homer never names Agerochus. Odyssey 16 gives only numbers (52 from Dulichium) and names only Antinous, Eurymachus, Amphinomus, Leodes, etc. The name appears solely in Apollodorus' Epitome 7.26, a 1st-2nd century CE mythographic handbook that systematized and named the anonymous Homeric mass.

Agerochus had a distinct personality, appearance, or heroic deed. — No ancient source gives him any individual trait, speech, lineage, or deed. He is a name in a list, killed collectively in the mnesterophonia. Any description of his looks, weapons, or character is modern invention.

There were 56 other suitors from Dulichium with Agerochus, making 57 total from that island, and this matches Homer. — Homer says 52 from Dulichium (total 108). Apollodorus says 57 from Dulichium (total 112). The figure '56 other wooers' is a misreading of Apollodorus' 57 (i.e., 56 others + Agerochus) and does not match Homer's 52.

Agerochus was a king or had divine powers. — He is a mortal aristocratic youth (mnēstēr), not a king, god, or hero with powers. The suitors are defined by their lack of arete - they cannot string Odysseus' bow - and their mortality.
