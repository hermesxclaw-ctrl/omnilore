Who It Is

At the foot of the Velian Hill, where the Republic tore down a would-be king's house to prove no man should stand above Rome, the Romans built a shrine to a goddess whose name meant winning — and keeping what you won. Her name was Vica Pota, and almost everything about her has been forgotten except that.

No mythic personality, speeches, or character anecdotes survive except the single genealogical role as mother of Diespiter in Seneca's satire. The etymology implies a functional personality: a deity who enables and stabilizes victory, not just the moment of winning (vincere) but the subsequent holding (potiri). This contrasts with Greek Nike's more ephemeral victory. Dumézil placed her among deities of 'taking possession' and sovereignty. All personality reconstruction is inferential from the name and topography; no narrative character is attested.

Powers
Vincendi potiundique potestas (Power of Conquering and Gaining Mastery)

The core functional power implied by the name and Cicero's etymology: to grant or embody the ability to conquer (vincere) and thereafter to take possession and hold (potiri). This suggests a two-phase power: victory in conflict and consolidation of its fruits (political control, legal possession, territorial mastery).

Limit: Not described as a battlefield epiphany like Victoria/Nike; no attested miracles, interventions, or myths of aid. Scope appears civic-juridical rather than purely military. No evidence of oracular or healing powers.

Topographical Guardianship of the Velia

Cult presence on the politically charged site of Publicola's demolished house suggests a tutelary or commemorative function: marking the place where aristocratic overreach was checked and public liberty asserted. The goddess anchors collective memory of the Republic's founding act.

Limit: No source explicitly states she was a guardian of the site; inference from location. No evidence of ongoing civic rituals beyond January 5 festival.

Divine Maternity of Diespiter

In Seneca's satire, Vica Pota is mother of Diespiter, an archaic name for Jupiter (Diespiter < *Dyeus pater). This implies a theogonic seniority: a goddess who births the sky-father.

Limit: Attested only in a comic, parodic context where genealogies are deliberately absurd (the gods debate Claudius' deification). Cannot be taken as canonical Roman theology. Contradicted by all other genealogies where Jupiter is son of Saturn.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No ancient description of Vica Pota's appearance, statue type, dress, age, or attributes survives. No securely identified cult statue, relief, coin image, or votive has been attributed to her by archaeologists. Wissowa and Latte emphasize that unlike Victoria/Nike, she is not described as winged and no iconographic tradition is attested. Any modern illustration is therefore hypothetical and must be labeled as such. Status: unknown.

Symbols — No attested symbols. By antiquarian analogy with Victoria, later compilers assumed victory symbols (palm, wreath, wings), but Asconius' identification is interpretatio, not evidence for original iconography. The goddess's name itself — conquering and mastering — suggests her symbol was conceptual/legal (potestas, possession) rather than visual. No archaeological assemblage from the Velia site has produced an inscribed dedication to Vica

Domains — Domain: vincendi atque potiundi — conquering and gaining mastery/possession. Not a personification of abstract 'Victory' in the later Hellenized sense (Wissowa), but an archaic power over successful seizure and retention, possibly with political-juridical overtones (control of the Velia, the Regia, the Forum). Accounts I
The House on the Velia and the Shrine of Vica PotaLivy, Ab Urbe Condita 2.7.12; corroborated by Plutarch, Life of Publicola 10.1-4

Archaic Rome (traditional date 509 BCE), narrated in Augustan historiography

After the expulsion of Tarquin the Proud, Publius Valerius, colleague of Brutus, built a grand house on the summit of the Velian Hill overlooking the Forum. The people, seeing the imposing house on high ground, feared he aimed at kingship and murmured against him. To dispel suspicion, Valerius — thereafter called Publicola ('people-cherisher') — demolished his own house in a single night and moved to a lower site. The vacant, leveled area on the Velia later became the site of the aedes of Vica Pota. Livy presents this as an etiology: the goddess of 'mastery' occupies the ground where mastery over the people was renounced. Established the topographical memory of republican libertas; provided an aetiology for the shrine's location in the civic center near the Regia and Forum. The story links Vica Pota to the ideological foundation of the Republic — not military victory abroad but political self-restraint at home.

Cicero's Etymology: Vincendi atque PotiundiCicero (fragment) preserved in Asconius Pedianus, In Toga Candida

Late Republic (Cicero 64 BCE; Asconius mid-1st c. CE)

In a lost passage of his speech In Toga Candida, Cicero explained the obscure name Vica Pota by deriving it from vincendi atque potiundi — 'from conquering and gaining possession.' Asconius, commenting a century later, preserves the etymology and adds his own equation: this goddess is Victoria. The anecdote shows late Republican intellectuals struggling to explain an archaic name whose linguistic roots were already opaque, using Stoic-style etymological speculation. Fixed the canonical ancient interpretation of the name for all subsequent lexicographers (Festus, etc.). Created the long-lasting conflation with Victoria that dominates handbooks until Wissowa's critique. Modern linguists still debate whether Cicero's folk-etymology is correct, but it remains the only ancient analysis.

Vica Pota, Mother of DiespiterSeneca, Apocolocyntosis Divi Claudii 9.4

54 CE

In Seneca's Menippean satire on the deification of Claudius, the Olympian gods hold a council. The narrator lists divine attendees with mock-pedantic genealogies: 'Vica Pota, mother of Diespiter...' Diespiter, an archaic form of Jupiter (Diespiter = Jupiter), is here treated as a separate, younger god, son of an obscure archaic goddess. The joke depends on the audience recognizing both names as antiquarian curiosities dragged into a bureaucratic heavenly senate. Provides the only surviving divine genealogy for Vica Pota. Demonstrates that by the mid-1st c. CE she was considered an ancient, almost forgotten figure suitable for erudite humor. Sparked modern debate (Cook vs. Wissowa) over whether Diespiter means Jupiter or Dis Pater.

Accounts II
The Festival of January 5Fasti Antiates Maiores, Fasti Praenestini (Degrassi XIII.2)

Republican calendar, recorded in 1st c. BCE - 1st c. CE Fasti

The Roman calendar marks January 5 (ante diem Nonas Ianuarias) as the festival of Vica Pota. No ritual description, prayer, or sacrifice is preserved. The date falls in the immediate post-Janus period, at the start of the civic year, when magistrates entered office and vows for the state's welfare were made. The placement suggests a state-level observance, not a private rite, but its content is lost. Attests to the persistence of the cult into the late Republic/early Empire as a calendrical observance. The lack of detail exemplifies how many archaic cults survived as names on calendars after their mythology was lost.

Asconius' Syncretism: Vica Pota is VictoriaAsconius Pedianus, Commentarius in Ciceronem

c. 50-70 CE

Commenting on Cicero, Asconius flatly states 'Vica Pota est Victoria' — Vica Pota is Victoria. Writing at a time when Greek Nike had thoroughly shaped the iconography of Roman Victoria (winged, with wreath and palm), Asconius collapses the archaic Italic goddess into the familiar Hellenized victory goddess. This is an act of interpretatio Romana applied within Roman religion itself, updating an old name to a contemporary theological equivalent. Created a scholarly tradition that lasted until the 19th-20th century in which Vica Pota was treated as merely an epithet or old name for Victoria. Wissowa (1902) and Latte (1960) reversed this, arguing she predated and differed from Victoria, and that Asconius' equation obscures her original character as a goddess of possession/mastery, not personified victory.

Preller's Etruscan Hypothesis: Lasa VecuLudwig Preller, Römische Mythologie (1st ed. 1858, 2nd ed. 1865)

19th century

Preller, seeking Etruscan substrates for early Roman religion, proposed that Vica Pota should be identified with the Etruscan divine figure Lasa Vecu (also attested as Lasa Vecuvia, an attendant of Turan/Aphrodite on Etruscan mirrors). He argued phonetic similarity (Vica ~ Vecu) and a shared sphere of 'taking possession' or love-magic. The conjecture was presented as tentative and has not been widely accepted. Illustrates 19th-century tendency to Etruscanize early Roman cults. The hypothesis was criticized by Wissowa (who noted Lasa Vecu's very different function as a nymph-like attendant, not a civic victory goddess) and is now cited only as an example of unsupported etymological speculation. No Etruscan inscription links Lasa Vecu to the Velia or to January 5.

Vica Pota was the Roman goddess of victory, equivalent to Greek Nike, and depicted as winged with a wreath. — No ancient source describes her appearance or gives her wings, wreath, or palm. Those are attributes of Victoria/Nike, which influenced Victoria from the 3rd c. BCE onward. Vica Pota is an older Italic goddess of 'conquering and holding' whose iconography is completely unattested. Equating her visually with Nike is anachronistic.

Vica Pota had a large temple on the Palatine or Capitoline and was widely worshipped across the empire. — Her only attested shrine is the small aedes on the Velia, on the site of Publicola's house, near the Forum/Regia. No other temple, altar, or votive deposit is securely attributed to her. Her festival on January 5 is the only calendrical attestation; she does not appear in imperial dedications or provincial inscriptions. She was a highly localized, archaic cult.

Vica Pota is an Etruscan goddess (Lasa Vecu) adopted by the Romans. — No primary source calls her Etruscan or links her to Lasa Vecu. The Etruscan connection is a single 19th-century conjecture by Ludwig Preller based on perceived name similarity, rejected by Wissowa and all subsequent scholarship. Her cult site on the Velia and Roman calendar festival point to a Roman/Italic origin.

Vica Pota is the mother of Jupiter in standard Roman mythology. — In canonical Roman genealogy Jupiter is son of Saturn and Ops/Rhea. Vica Pota as mother of Diespiter appears only in Seneca's Apocolocyntosis, a Menippean satire that deliberately invents absurd genealogies for comic effect. It is not a genuine mythic tradition.

Vacuna and Vica Pota are the same goddess under different names. — Ancient sources treat them separately: Vica Pota on the Velia with January 5 festival; Vacuna as a Sabine goddess (Horace, Ovid) with sanctuary near the Sabine hills and associations with rest after labor. No ancient text equates them; the conflation appears only in modern handbooks that group obscure Italic goddesses together.
