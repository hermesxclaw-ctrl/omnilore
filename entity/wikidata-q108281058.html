Who It Is

In Athens in 302 BCE, when Macedonian garrisons decided who could speak and who would be exiled, a comic poet named Archedicus was handed a commission: destroy a man with jokes. His target was Demochares, nephew of Demosthenes and voice of democratic resistance. His patron was the historian Timaeus. Only two play-titles survive — Diamartanon and Thesauros — but behind them lies a rare window into how New Comedy became a weapon in the wars of Alexander's successors.

Ancient testimonia characterize him not by personality but by political alignment: partisan of Antipater and the Macedonian party, hostile to Demochares at Timaeus's urging. This suggests a poet willing to use comedy for political invective (psogos), unusual for New Comedy which is often described as apolitical compared to Old Comedy. Polybius's portrait of Timaeus as bitter and slanderous indirectly colors Archedicus as an instrument of that enmity. No independent anecdotes about his character survive. Powers
New Comedy Playwriting (Fabula Palliata precursor)

Composition of comedies in the style of New Comedy: five-act structure, prologue, everyday Athenian citizens (senex, adulescens, meretrix, servus callidus), recognition and marriage plots, iambic trimeter and trochaic tetrameter. Attested only by titles, not by surviving lines.

Limit: No lines survive to assess meter, diction, or dramaturgy. Cannot verify productivity beyond two titles; Suda may be incomplete. No didascalic inscriptions record victories.

Political Invective Comedy (Psogos)

Use of comedy for direct personal attack (onomasti komodein) against Demochares, nephew of Demosthenes, at Timaeus's instigation, and advocacy for Antipater/Macedonian party. This revives Old Comedy-style political satire within New Comedy frame, targeting a contemporary orator-politician.

Limit: No fragment preserves the actual abuse. Unknown whether attack was in a full play or in parabasis-like interludes. Unknown if it succeeded theatrically or politically.

Title-Based Plot Construction

Ability to construct plots around New Comedy topoi: Diamartanon ('The Man Who Misses/Fails/Goes Astray') suggests a hamartia plot - a character who misses a target, fails in love/business, or is morally astray; Thesauros ('The Treasure') suggests hidden gold, inheritance, or dowry confusion - a standard New Comedy engine (cf. Menander Thesauros fragment, Plautus Trinummus, Philemon Thesauros).

Limit: No hypotheses or fragments confirm plots. Titles are common in New Comedy (Philemon and Menander both have Thesauros), so reconstruction is hypothetical. Must be labeled reconstructed/hypothetical, not attested.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No physical description, portrait, mask, or iconography is attested for Archedicus. As an Athenian citizen comic poet of the New Comedy, he would have been male, likely over 30 at floruit, eligible to produce plays at the City Dionysia and Lenaea. No bust, inscription with image, or vase painting is associated. Status: unknown.

Symbols — No personal symbols. The two play titles suggest stock New Comedy iconography: Diamartanon implies a hamartia/mistake plot (perhaps a young lover missing his aim), Thesauros implies the ubiquitous treasure-pot (thesauros) motif seen in Menander, Plautus (Aulularia/Trinummus) and later comedy - a hidden cache driving mistaken identity. No evidence of divine or mythological symbols.

Domains — Domain: Athenian theatre - Theatre of Dionysus Eleuthereus on the south slope of the Acropolis, and possibly the Lenaean theatre. Habitat: Athens under Macedonian control (Lamian War 323-322 BCE, Antipater's oligarchy 321-319, Cassander's control 317-307, then Demetrius Poliorcetes 307-301). Accounts I
The Commission Against Demochares (c. 302 BCE)Suda alpha 4105; Polybius 12.6a, 12.23 on Timaeus's enmity to Demochares; [Plutarch] Vitae X Oratorum 847f-850b

Hellenistic, Diadochi period, c. 302 BCE (floruit given by Suda as 'about Antipater' - Antipater died 319, so 302 suggests Cassander/Antipatrid regime memory)

At the instigation of the historian Timaeus of Tauromenium, Archedicus wrote comedies attacking Demochares (c. 355-275 BCE), son of Laches and nephew of Demosthenes. Demochares was a prominent democratic orator who opposed Antipater and later Cassander, was exiled in 322 and 317, and returned under Demetrius Poliorcetes. Timaeus, himself hostile to Demochares and Agathocles, allegedly recruited Archedicus to lampoon Demochares on stage, aligning comedy with Macedonian oligarchic propaganda. The Suda's phrasing 'supported Antipater and the Macedonian party' suggests Archedicus was a known Antipatrid partisan, not a neutral poet. No immediate outcome is recorded, but the episode is cited as evidence that New Comedy was not wholly apolitical. Demochares survived to become a major historian and statesman (author of History of his times, used by Timaeus and Polybius). The attack illustrates how Diadochi rivalries instrumentalized theatre. Polybius later used Timaeus's bias as a case study in historiographic malpractice.

Diamartanon (The Man Who Misses / Goes Astray)Suda alpha 4105; Kassel-Austin PCG II Archedicus fr. 1 (title only); Meineke FCG I p. 477

New Comedy, c. 302 BCE

One of two plays attributed to Archedicus. The title Διαμαρτάνων is present participle of διαμαρτάνω 'to miss the mark, fail, go astray, be mistaken'. In comic titles it typically denotes a young man who fails in love, misses an appointment, or misjudges a situation, leading to complications resolved by anagnorisis. No fragment, hypothesis, or plot summary survives. The title is otherwise unattested in comedy, making it distinctive to Archedicus. Comparative titles: Menander's Dyskolos, Apistos, etc. Play is lost. Title preserved only in lexicographic tradition. No later adaptation attested. Its uniqueness suggests it was not confused with other poets' works, unlike Thesauros.

Accounts II
Thesauros (The Treasure)Suda alpha 4105; Kassel-Austin PCG II Archedicus fr. 2 (title only); cf. Menander Thesauros frr., Philemon Thesauros, Diphilus Thesauros

New Comedy, c. 302 BCE

Second attested play. Title Θησαυρός 'Treasure, hoard, treasure-chest' is one of the most common New Comedy titles (also used by Menander, Philemon, Diphilus, Philippides, Timocles). Typical plot: an old man hides gold, a slave or youth discovers it, confusion over ownership/dowry/marriage ensues, often with a recognition. No fragment of Archedicus's version survives to distinguish it. Athenaeus 7.292f lists many Thesauros plays in his catalogue of comedy titles, illustrating the topos. Lost. The common title creates risk of misattribution: fragments assigned to Menander's or Philemon's Thesauros could theoretically belong to Archedicus, but Kassel-Austin keep them separate due to lack of citation with author name. No ancient hypothesis survives.

Flourishing under the Macedonian Party (Antipatrid Athens 322-307 BCE)Suda 'γέγονε δὲ ἐπὶ Ἀντιπάτρου' (flourished under Antipater); Diodorus Siculus 18.18, 18.48, 18.74 on Antipater's settlement of Athens; Plutarch Phocion 26-35

c. 322-302 BCE

The Suda dates Archedicus's acme to 'the time of Antipater' (321-319 BCE) but modern scholarship emends to c. 302 BCE (Meineke, Kirchner PA 2426) to reconcile with Demochares's activity. This places him in Athens when Antipater imposed an oligarchy (322), disenfranchising 12,000 thetes, installing Phocion, exiling Demosthenes and Demochares. As a supporter of Antipater and the Macedonian party, Archedicus would have been among the propertied elite who benefited from the 2,000-drachma census, or at least a poet willing to flatter the regime for choregic support. His career thus spans the Lamian War aftermath, Cassander's control via Demetrius of Phalerum (317-307), and the brief democratic restoration by Demetrius Poliorcetes (307). Explains why Archedicus attacked Demochares: Demochares led democratic resistance and was exiled by the regime Archedicus supported. After 307, when Demetrius Poliorcetes restored democracy and Demochares returned, pro-Macedonian poets like Archedicus likely lost favor or left Athens. No record of his later life.

Survival as Lexicographic Ghost (Byzantine Transmission)Suda 10th c.; Eudocia?; Photius Lexicon; Kassel-Austin PCG; Smith Dictionary 1870

10th century CE to present

Archedicus survives only because Byzantine lexicographers excerpted Hellenistic pinakes (Callimachus, Aristophanes of Byzantium, Didymus). The Suda's entry is the sole biographical notice; no fragment is quoted by Athenaeus, Stobaeus, or Pollux, indicating his plays were already rare by the 2nd century CE. Modern philologists from Meineke (1839) to Kassel-Austin (1991) preserve him in fragment collections as a testimonia-only poet, a 'ghost' whose work is known by title alone. His inclusion in Smith's Dictionary (1870) made him accessible to Anglophone readers, copied verbatim into many later encyclopedias. Archedicus became a standard example in histories of New Comedy of a political poet, cited to qualify the view that New Comedy was apolitical. His ghostly preservation illustrates differential survival: Menander survives via papyri, Archedicus via one lexicon line.

Archedicus's plays survive or fragments are quoted — No fragment, line, or plot summary survives. Only two titles are preserved in the Suda. Any online 'fragment' attributed to him is misattributed from Menander's or Philemon's Thesauros. Kassel-Austin PCG lists zero fragments, only testimonia.

Archedicus was an Old Comedy poet like Aristophanes who mocked politicians on stage — He was New Comedy (late 4th century), a generation after Old Comedy. While he did attack Demochares, his plays otherwise followed New Comedy domestic conventions (treasure plots, mistaken identity), not Old Comedy fantasy or chorus-led abuse. The Suda explicitly calls him 'of the New Comedy'.

Archedicus supported Alexander the Great directly — Alexander died 323 BCE before Archedicus's floruit. He supported Antipater/Cassander, Alexander's successors who controlled Athens. The 'Macedonian party' refers to the Diadochi, not Alexander himself.

Diamartanon means 'The Braggart' or 'The Liar' — Edmonds (1959) mistranslated Diamartanon; the verb διαμαρτάνω means 'to miss, fail, go astray', not 'to brag' (alazoneia) or 'to lie'. Correct is 'The Man Who Misses/Fails' or 'The Man Who Goes Astray'.
