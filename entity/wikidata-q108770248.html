Who It Is

On a small oil-flask painted in Paestum over two thousand years ago, seven young women stand in a garden that never existed, their names written neatly above their heads like place-cards at a divine banquet. Six of them are familiar nymphs of evening. The seventh — Donakis, the Reed — may not belong there at all.

No individual myth assigns Donakis dialogue, motive, or personality. Collectively, the Hesperides are characterized as: (1) dutiful garden-tenders appointed by Hera; (2) sweet singers - Euripides calls them 'minstrel maids' (Hippolytus 742-751) who possess the power of sweet song; (3) shy or elusive guardians who flee or lament when Heracles approaches (Apollonius Rhodius); (4) sometimes helpful (creating a spring for the Argonauts). Powers
Guardianship of the Golden Apples (collective Hesperides function)

As a putative Hesperide, Donakis would share the duty of tending Hera's golden apple trees and guarding them alongside the dragon Ladon. The apples confer immortality or divine favor; the Hesperides water and tend the trees. No text describes Donakis performing this act individually.

Limit: Hesperides are not owners of the garden; they are servants of Hera. They cannot prevent Heracles (or Atlas) from taking the apples, and they are powerless against Ladon's death. Their guardianship fails in every surviving narrative.

Enchanting Song

Euripides and later poets attribute sweet, Siren-like singing to the Hesperides ('minstrel maids' - mousopoioi). Apollonius describes them singing softly even as they mourn. As one of the singing nymphs, Donakis would possess this musical power.

Limit: Song is not a weapon or compulsion; it is aesthetic and ritual. It does not deter Heracles or other intruders.

Nymphic Longevity and Ageless Beauty

As nymphs (nymphai), Hesperides are long-lived, ageless, divine-adjacent beings, neither fully immortal Olympians nor mortals. They dwell at the world's edge beyond normal time.

Limit: They are vulnerable to grief and transformation: in Apollonius, after Ladon's death they turn to dust and are restored only by divine pity. They are not invulnerable.

Botanical Personification (Reed) - hypothetical

If Donakis is a personification of donax, her 'power' is allegorical: embodying the reed's qualities - flexibility, hollow musicality (panpipes), association with marshes, use in crafts. In South Italian vase-painting, such personifications often appear as decorative attendants without mythic agency.

Limit: Personifications have no narrative agency or cult power; they are artistic devices.

Accounts I
The Wedding Gift of Gaia and the Planting of the Golden Apple TreesPherecydes of Athens (fragment via Athenaeus, Apollodorus 2.5.11, Diodorus 4.26), Hesiod Theogony 215-216, 333-335

Archaic (Hesiod c. 700 BCE) through Hellenistic compilation

When Zeus married Hera, Gaia brought branches bearing golden apples as a nuptial gift. Hera, admiring them, asked Gaia to plant them in her garden, which extended to Mount Atlas at the world's western edge. The Hesperides - however many and whoever their parents - were appointed to tend the grove, and Hera set the sleepless hundred-headed dragon Ladon to coil around the tree(s). This aetiology establishes the garden that Donakis, if a Hesperide, would inhabit. No version names Donakis individually; she would be among the anonymous 'daughters of Evening' performing this eternal horticultural service. Creation of the western paradise that becomes the object of Heracles' quest and the source of the Apple of Discord. Establishes Hesperides as liminal guardians between divine and mortal worlds.

Heracles' Eleventh Labour: The Theft of the Golden ApplesApollodorus Bibliotheca 2.5.11; Diodorus Siculus 4.26-27; Apollonius Rhodius 4.1396-1449 (aftermath); Hyginus Fabulae 30; Paestan and Attic vase-paintings including Meidias Hydria and Asteas lekythos

Classical to Roman Imperial (5th century BCE - 2nd century CE compilations)

After completing ten labours, Heracles is ordered by Eurystheus to fetch the golden apples, a task declared void of help. Heracles captures the shape-shifting Old Man of the Sea (Nereus/Proteus) to learn the location, travels via the Caucasus where he frees Prometheus, kills Antaeus in Libya and Busiris in Egypt en route, and finally reaches the Atlas garden. In one version he kills Ladon and plucks the apples himself; in the more famous version he asks Atlas - father or kinsman of the Hesperides - to fetch them while he holds up the sky. Atlas agrees but then refuses to resume his burden, offering to deliver the apples himself; Heracles tricks him into taking the heavens back by asking him to hold them briefly while he adjusts his cloak, then flees with the apples. Athena later returns the apples to the garden because they are sacred. On Attic pottery Heracles is shown at ease in the garden, attended by the maidens. The Argonauts' Encounter with the Mourning HesperidesApollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 4.1396-1484

3rd century BCE

The day after Heracles has killed Ladon and taken the apples, the Argonauts, parched and lost in the Libyan desert after carrying the Argo, stumble onto the Hesperian plain. They find the Hesperides - Aigle, Erytheis, and Hespere in Apollonius's count - turned to dust and bitterly lamenting, transformed into trees or earth in grief for the slain serpent and stolen fruit. Orpheus prays to them; they take pity, transform back, and cause a spring to gush from the sand (or reveal one) and point the heroes to water. The episode emphasizes the Hesperides' compassion and their connection to life-giving water in a barren land. Donakis is not named here, but the scene is the literary parallel to the vase's garden setting, and the Asteas painter may have known this type of lamentation iconography. Argonauts survive the desert; Hesperides demonstrate benevolence despite loss; spring becomes a local aetiology. Shows Hesperides as more than passive guardians.

Accounts II
Eris and the Apple of DiscordCypria (fragmentary Epic Cycle), later summarized by Proclus and Hyginus; Lucian, Dialogues of the Gods; no early source explicitly says the apple came from the Hesperides garden, but later tradition conflates

Archaic Epic Cycle (c. 7th-6th century BCE) through Imperial syncretism

At the wedding of Peleus and Thetis (to which Eris, Discord, was not invited), Eris obtains a golden apple - in later syncretic accounts said to be from the Garden of the Hesperides - and inscribes it 'to the fairest,' tossing it among Hera, Athena, and Aphrodite. The ensuing Judgement of Paris leads to the Trojan War. While the earliest sources call it simply a golden apple, medieval and Renaissance commentators explicitly identified it with the Hesperidean apples, thereby retroactively giving the Hesperides (and by extension any member like Donakis) a role in the war's causation. The claim is disputed and likely a later harmonization. Judgement of Paris, Trojan War cycle. Elevates the garden's apples from wonder to world-historical catalyst.

The Atlas Genealogy and the AtlantidesDiodorus Siculus 4.27.2; Hesiod Theogony 215, 517-520; Servius on Aeneid 4.484; Hyginus Fabulae Preface

Archaic through Roman antiquity

Multiple genealogies make the Hesperides 'Atlantides' - daughters of Atlas. In Diodorus, Atlas is a wise astronomer-king in the far west whose seven daughters tend the golden apples; in Hesiod, Atlas holds the sky while his daughters (in some readings) dwell nearby. Servius records a tradition where they are daughters of Hesperus, the Evening Star, making them sisters of the Hesperus star itself. Phorcys/Ceto and Nyx/Erebus variants make them primordial sea or night beings. For Donakis, if she is an Atlantid, she would be sister to the Pleiades and Hyades (also Atlantides), linking reed-nymph to star-nymphs - an odd but not impossible mythic kinship. The Asteas vase's inclusion of Calypso (also an Atlantid in some traditions) suggests the painter was playing with Atlantid associations. Explains why Hesperides are called Atlantides and locates them geographically in the west. Creates overlapping identities that cause ancient and modern confusion about who counts as a Hesperide.

The Western Garden in Roman Geography and Renaissance PoetryPliny the Elder Natural History 5.1-3, 6.36; Silius Italicus Punica 3.253; Pomponius Mela; Renaissance poets (Ariosto, Spenser, Milton)

1st century CE through 16th-17th century CE

By the Roman period the garden had become a poetic convention more than a cult site. Pliny places the garden at Lixus (modern Larache, Morocco) where Heracles fought Antaeus, and says the Hesperides live on two Atlantic islands. Silius Italicus has the Massylii come from the woods of the Hesperides. Renaissance poets revive the garden as an allegory of earthly paradise, botanical wonder, and imperial exoticism, identifying the golden apples with oranges or citrons (Greek hesperidoeidē, modern portokáli). In this long reception, individual Hesperides names - especially obscure ones like Donakis - are rarely revived; when they are, they appear in learned lists copied from Hyginus or vase catalogues, not as characters. Donakis thus survives only as a footnote in antiquarian botany and vase scholarship. Garden becomes literary topos and botanical eponym (Hesperidoideae, hesperidium fruit type). Preserves Hesperides in language even as their individual stories fade.

Donakis is a well-known Greek goddess with her own myths, powers, and cult, like Calypso or Aigle. — Donakis is attested only once, as a name painted above a figure on a single South Italian vase (c. 360-330 BCE). She has no surviving myth, cult, temple, or literary narrative. Any powers or stories assigned to her are inferred from the collective Hesperides or from her reed etymology.

The seven names on the Asteas lekythos (including Donakis) are the canonical seven Hesperides recognized by the Greeks. — The Asteas seven (Aiopis, Antheia, Donakis, Calypso, Mermesa, Nelisa, Tara) is an outlier. Canonical literary lists contain different names (Aigle, Erytheia, Hesperia, Arethusa, etc.) and never include Donakis. The vase represents a local Paestan invention, not pan-Hellenic canon.

The golden apples of the Hesperides were oranges, and therefore Donakis's garden was an orange grove near Portugal. — The orange identification is a Renaissance and early modern rationalization; oranges were unknown to Archaic Greeks. The Greek botanical term hesperidoeidē was applied retroactively to citrus. The garden's location near Portugal (portokáli) is folk etymology, not ancient evidence. The apples were mythical golden apples (mela chrysea), not citrus.

Donakis's name is related to the evening star Hesperus, like other Hesperides. — Donakis derives from donax 'reed', not from hesperos 'evening'. Unlike Hespere/Hesperia/Erytheis (which contain hesper- or erythros 'red' evening-sky roots), Donakis is botanical. This etymological mismatch is key to McPhee's argument she is not a true Hesperide.
