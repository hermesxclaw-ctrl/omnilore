Who It Is

In the bustling intellectual arena of ancient Athens, a slight, sharp-witted philosopher from Cilicia dared to tell his teacher, 'Give me the principles, and I will find the proofs myself.' That audacious student, Chrysippus, would go on to forge Stoicism into a philosophical powerhouse that would dominate the Greco-Roman world for centuries, earning him the posthumous title of the 'Second Founder of Stoicism.'

Chrysippus was known for his intellectual audacity, self-confidence, and prolific writing. He was said to write 500 lines a day and composed over 705 works. He was also noted for his dialectical skill and his tendency to argue both sides of a question. His style was criticized as diffuse and obscure, but his abilities were highly regarded. He was also known for his devotion to logic and his willingness to tackle paradoxes.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Chrysippus (Χρύσιππος) is Greek, derived from 'chrysos' (gold) and 'hippos' (horse), meaning 'golden horse.'

Pronunciation — /kraɪˈsɪpəs/ (kry-SIP-əs) in English; in Greek: Χρύσιππος (KHRY-sip-pos).

Aliases — Chrysippus, Chrusippos, Second Founder of Stoicism

Ownership — Greek (Stoic philosophy), though possibly of Phoenician descent; born in Soli, Cilicia (in modern-day Turkey), active in Athens.

Propositional Logic

Chrysippus developed a system of propositional logic that dealt with the interrelations of whole propositions (e.g., 'If it is day, it is light'), in contrast to Aristotle's term logic. He identified five types of molecular propositions (conjunction, disjunction, conditional, etc.) and established truth conditions for them.

Limit: The system was formal and abstract, but it was intended to be applicable to reasoning about the world. Some later critics found it overly complex.

Syllogistic

Chrysippus created a deductive system based on five 'indemonstrable' syllogisms (basic argument forms) and four 'themata' (inference rules) to reduce complex arguments to the basic forms. This was a significant advance in formal logic.

Limit: Only two of the four themata survive. The system was later superseded by more modern logic.

Logic of Conditionals

Chrysippus adopted a strict interpretation of the conditional: a conditional is true if the denial of the consequent is incompatible with the antecedent. This corresponds to the modern 'strict conditional' and avoided paradoxes like the one proposed by Philo.

Limit: This strict interpretation made many ordinary conditionals false, which some found counterintuitive.

Analysis of Paradoxes

Chrysippus wrote extensively on the liar paradox, amphiboly, and other conundrums, attempting to refute them or show their solutions. He devoted 28 works (66 books) to puzzles and paradoxes.

Limit: Many of his solutions are lost, and some were criticized by later philosophers.

Theory of Knowledge

Chrysippus elaborated the Stoic theory of knowledge, including the role of sense perception, presentation (phantasia), and assent (synkatathesis). He distinguished between cognitive and non-cognitive impressions.

Limit: The details are fragmentary and subject to scholarly interpretation.

Ethical Therapy

Chrysippus taught that ethics depends on understanding the universe, and he developed a 'therapy of the passions' aimed at extirpating unruly emotions that disturb the soul. He emphasized the role of reason and virtue.

Limit: The therapy required rigorous philosophical training and was sometimes criticized as impractical.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Chrysippus was reputed to be slight in stature (Diogenes Laërtius). No other physical descriptions survive.

Symbols — No specific symbols or iconography are associated with Chrysippus in ancient art. Later artistic depictions often show him as a typical Greek philosopher with a beard and himation.

Domains — He lived in Athens, where he was active in the Stoa Poikile (Painted Porch), the headquarters of the Stoic school. He likely also frequented the Academy and the Lyceum.

Accounts I
Life and EducationDiogenes Laërtius, 'Lives of the Philosophers', Book 7

3rd century BC

Chrysippus was born in Soli, Cilicia, possibly of Phoenician descent. He lost his inherited property to the king's treasury, moved to Athens, and became a pupil of Cleanthes. He also attended lectures at the Academy. He was known for his intellectual audacity and prolific writing. He succeeded Cleanthes as head of the Stoic school and became the most influential Stoic philosopher of his time.

Accounts II
Death by LaughterDiogenes Laërtius, 'Lives of the Philosophers', Book 7, section 185

3rd century BC

One account says Chrysippus died of laughter after seeing a donkey eating figs and exclaiming, 'Now give the donkey a drink of pure wine to wash down the figs.' Another account says he died from dizziness after drinking undiluted wine at a feast. His death at age 73 marked the end of his leadership of the Stoic school; he was succeeded by Zeno of Tarsus.

The Request to CleanthesDiogenes Laërtius, 'Lives of the Philosophers', Book 7, section 179

3rd century BC

Chrysippus reportedly said to Cleanthes, 'Give me the principles, and I will find the proofs myself,' demonstrating his confidence in his logical abilities. This anecdote illustrates his self-reliance and his contribution to Stoic logic.

Chrysippus believed in absolute determinism that denies free will. — He sought a role for personal agency within a deterministic universe, developing a compatibilist view.

Stoic logic is the same as Aristotelian logic. — Stoic logic is propositional, while Aristotelian logic is term-based.
