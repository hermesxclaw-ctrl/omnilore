The Approach

In the bustling intellectual arena of ancient Athens, a slight, sharp-witted philosopher from Cilicia dared to tell his teacher, 'Give me the principles, and I will find the proofs myself.' That audacious student, Chrysippus, would go on to forge Stoicism into a philosophical powerhouse that would dominate the Greco-Roman world for centuries, earning him the posthumous title of the 'Second Founder of Stoicism.'

Content advisory. Contains accounts of death by excessive drinking and laughter, and references to ancient philosophical debates that may involve logical paradoxes. No explicit violent or sexual content.
Identity

Chrysippus of Soli

Pronunciation /kraɪˈsɪpəs/ (kry-SIP-əs) in English; in Greek: Χρύσιππος (KHRY-sip-pos).

Known As

ChrysippusChrusipposSecond Founder of Stoicism

Etymology

The name Chrysippus (Χρύσιππος) is Greek, derived from 'chrysos' (gold) and 'hippos' (horse), meaning 'golden horse.'

Cultural Ownership

Greek (Stoic philosophy), though possibly of Phoenician descent; born in Soli, Cilicia (in modern-day Turkey), active in Athens.

Disputed Identifications

There is a mythological Chrysippus (son of Pelops) with whom the philosopher is sometimes confused in later sources. Also, some ancient sources attribute works to Chrysippus that may be by other Stoics, but the core identity is well-established.

Description

Appearance

Chrysippus was reputed to be slight in stature (Diogenes Laërtius). No other physical descriptions survive.

Symbols & Iconography

No specific symbols or iconography are associated with Chrysippus in ancient art. Later artistic depictions often show him as a typical Greek philosopher with a beard and himation.

Behaviour & Personality

Chrysippus was known for his intellectual audacity, self-confidence, and prolific writing. He was said to write 500 lines a day and composed over 705 works. He was also noted for his dialectical skill and his tendency to argue both sides of a question. His style was criticized as diffuse and obscure, but his abilities were highly regarded. He was also known for his devotion to logic and his willingness to tackle paradoxes.

Domains & Habitats

He lived in Athens, where he was active in the Stoa Poikile (Painted Porch), the headquarters of the Stoic school. He likely also frequented the Academy and the Lyceum.

Version Differences

There are no significant version differences in his biography, but ancient accounts of his death vary (see stories).

Powers & Abilities

Propositional Logic

Source Version Stoic logic as developed by Chrysippus; known from Sextus Empiricus, Diogenes Laërtius, and others.

Description

Chrysippus developed a system of propositional logic that dealt with the interrelations of whole propositions (e.g., 'If it is day, it is light'), in contrast to Aristotle's term logic. He identified five types of molecular propositions (conjunction, disjunction, conditional, etc.) and established truth conditions for them.

Limitations

The system was formal and abstract, but it was intended to be applicable to reasoning about the world. Some later critics found it overly complex.

Contradictions

Some ancient sources attribute the development of propositional logic to earlier Megarian logicians (Diodorus Cronus, Philo), but Chrysippus is credited with systematizing it.

Syllogistic

Source Version Stoic syllogistic; known from later summaries, especially by Sextus Empiricus and Diogenes Laërtius.

Description

Chrysippus created a deductive system based on five 'indemonstrable' syllogisms (basic argument forms) and four 'themata' (inference rules) to reduce complex arguments to the basic forms. This was a significant advance in formal logic.

Limitations

Only two of the four themata survive. The system was later superseded by more modern logic.

Contradictions

Some scholars debate whether the Stoic syllogistic was fully original or indebted to earlier Peripatetic work.

Logic of Conditionals

Source Version Stoic logic; discussed by Sextus Empiricus (Against the Logicians).

Description

Chrysippus adopted a strict interpretation of the conditional: a conditional is true if the denial of the consequent is incompatible with the antecedent. This corresponds to the modern 'strict conditional' and avoided paradoxes like the one proposed by Philo.

Limitations

This strict interpretation made many ordinary conditionals false, which some found counterintuitive.

Contradictions

Philo and Diodorus had different truth conditions for conditionals; Chrysippus's view was one among several.

Analysis of Paradoxes

Source Version Stoic logic; known from Diogenes Laërtius's list of works.

Description

Chrysippus wrote extensively on the liar paradox, amphiboly, and other conundrums, attempting to refute them or show their solutions. He devoted 28 works (66 books) to puzzles and paradoxes.

Limitations

Many of his solutions are lost, and some were criticized by later philosophers.

Contradictions

Later logicians often disagreed with his resolutions.

Theory of Knowledge

Source Version Stoic epistemology; known from Sextus Empiricus, Cicero, and others.

Description

Chrysippus elaborated the Stoic theory of knowledge, including the role of sense perception, presentation (phantasia), and assent (synkatathesis). He distinguished between cognitive and non-cognitive impressions.

Limitations

The details are fragmentary and subject to scholarly interpretation.

Contradictions

There were debates within Stoicism about the nature of cognitive impressions.

Ethical Therapy

Source Version Stoic ethics; known from Galen, Seneca, and others.

Description

Chrysippus taught that ethics depends on understanding the universe, and he developed a 'therapy of the passions' aimed at extirpating unruly emotions that disturb the soul. He emphasized the role of reason and virtue.

Limitations

The therapy required rigorous philosophical training and was sometimes criticized as impractical.

Contradictions

Some later Stoics (e.g., Posidonius) disagreed with Chrysippus's cognitive account of the passions.

Stories & Myths

Life and Education

Source Diogenes Laërtius, 'Lives of the Philosophers', Book 7Culture GreekPeriod 3rd century BC

Summary

Chrysippus was born in Soli, Cilicia, possibly of Phoenician descent. He lost his inherited property to the king's treasury, moved to Athens, and became a pupil of Cleanthes. He also attended lectures at the Academy. He was known for his intellectual audacity and prolific writing.

Participants

Lacydes

Consequences

He succeeded Cleanthes as head of the Stoic school and became the most influential Stoic philosopher of his time.

Variants

Some details of his life, such as his exact birth date, are uncertain.

Death by Laughter

Source Diogenes Laërtius, 'Lives of the Philosophers', Book 7, section 185Culture GreekPeriod 3rd century BC

Summary

One account says Chrysippus died of laughter after seeing a donkey eating figs and exclaiming, 'Now give the donkey a drink of pure wine to wash down the figs.' Another account says he died from dizziness after drinking undiluted wine at a feast.

Participants

Chrysippus

a donkey

Consequences

His death at age 73 marked the end of his leadership of the Stoic school; he was succeeded by Zeno of Tarsus.

Variants

Two versions exist; the laughter version is more famous.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

The vast majority of Chrysippus's original writings are lost; we rely on fragments and later doxography. The Herculaneum papyri may yield more texts, but they are fragmentary. His exact philosophical positions on many topics (e.g., the details of his ethics) are reconstructed and debated. The date of his birth is approximate (c. 279 BC). The full list of his 705 works is not extant.

Research Scope

This dossier covers the life, works, and philosophical contributions of Chrysippus of Soli, the third head of the Stoic school. It draws primarily from Diogenes Laërtius's 'Lives and Opinions of Eminent Philosophers,' fragments preserved by later authors (Cicero, Seneca, Galen, Plutarch, Sextus Empiricus), and modern scholarship on Stoic logic and philosophy. The research is limited by the loss of Chrysippus's original works; almost all knowledge comes from doxographical reports and fragments. The research cutoff date is August 2026.
