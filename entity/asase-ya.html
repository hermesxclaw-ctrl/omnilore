Who They Are

Asase Ya (also called Asase Yaa or Aberewa) is the Earth goddess of the Akan people of Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire — the divine womb from which all life springs and to which all life returns. She is the wife of Nyame, the sky god, and together they form the primordial cosmic pair: Nyame above, Asase Ya below, their union generating the fertility that sustains the world. In Akan religion, Asase Ya is one of the most important and frequently invoked deities, second only to Nyame himself, and her worship permeates daily life through agricultural rituals, oaths, and libations.

Physically, Asase Ya is conceived as the literal body of the Earth itself — the soil, the fields, the clay from which pots are molded. In Akan thought, Thursday is her sacred day (her name 'Yaa' means one born on Thursday), and on this day, no tilling, digging, or working of the soil is permitted — a sabbath dedicated to the Earth that rests. She is not typically represented in figurative sculpture but rather through the earth itself; her shrines are groves, fields, and the family plot where ancestors are buried. However, Akan terracotta figures and funerary art often allude to her as a maternal figure, full-breasted and seated, representing the nourishing earth.

Asase Ya's primary function is as the source and sustainer of agricultural fertility. Before planting, Akan farmers pour libations of palm wine or water onto the soil and pray to Asase Ya for a bountiful harvest. At harvest, the first fruits are offered to her in thanksgiving. But her domain extends beyond agriculture: she is also the goddess of truth and justice. In traditional Akan society, the most solemn oath one could swear was to touch the earth with one's lips — invoking Asase Ya as witness. Perjury before the Earth goddess was believed to bring certain death or catastrophe. She is also the keeper of the dead; the Akan proverb 'All men are children of Asase Ya and no one is a stranger to her' refers to the universal return of all bodies to the soil in burial.

A poignant myth tells of Asase Ya's relationship with humanity and hunger. Long ago, the sky (Nyame) was so close to the earth that people could reach up and break off pieces to eat. But an old woman, pounding fufu (a staple food), kept hitting Nyame with her long pestle. Annoyed, Nyame withdrew higher and higher into the sky, taking the easy food with him. Asase Ya, pitying humanity's hunger, promised that if they worked her soil with respect, she would provide food forever. This myth explains the origin of agriculture and the sacred bond between farmers and the earth.

Asase Ya's worship continues in modern Ghana, both in traditional religion and syncretically within Christianity and Islam. The annual yam festival (Homowo) in many Akan communities honors her fertility. Her moral authority — as the witness of oaths and the receiver of the dead — makes her one of the most resilient figures in Akan spirituality.
