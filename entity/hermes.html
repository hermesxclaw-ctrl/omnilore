Who It Is

Hermes is the god who never stays still—born at dawn, stealing cattle by night, and by midday inventing music that will charm his enemies. He slips between Olympus and the underworld, between market stalls and mountain caves, between honest trade and audacious theft. Wherever a boundary is drawn, Hermes is already there, testing it, crossing it, or turning it into a meeting place. To follow Hermes through myth, cult, and art is to trace the restless movement of messages, souls, and desires across the ancient Mediterranean.

Hermes is consistently portrayed as cunning, quick-witted, and playful, embodying the archetype of the trickster. In the Homeric Hymn to Hermes, he steals Apollo’s cattle on the day of his birth, invents the lyre, and uses clever rhetoric to negotiate reconciliation, demonstrating precocious intelligence and audacity. He is a mediator who can move freely between Olympus, earth, and the underworld, serving as messenger of the gods and psychopomp guiding souls. Powers
Divine messenger and herald

Hermes serves as the official messenger of Zeus and other Olympian gods, conveying commands, warnings, and negotiations between divine and mortal realms. In the Odyssey, he is sent to instruct Calypso to release Odysseus and later aids Odysseus against Circe. In the Iliad, he guides Priam safely through the Achaean camp to Achilles. As herald, he possesses the authority to speak on behalf of the gods and to traverse cosmic boundaries swiftly.

Limit: Hermes’ authority as messenger is derivative of Zeus and the Olympian hierarchy; he does not independently determine divine policy. His role can be superseded or complemented by other messengers (e.g., Iris) in some traditions.

Psychopomp (guide of souls)

Hermes escorts the souls of the dead from the world of the living to Hades, ensuring safe passage and proper transition. In Odyssey Book 24, he leads the souls of the slain suitors to the underworld. This function aligns with his broader role as mediator of boundaries and transitions, including those between life and death.

Limit: Hermes does not rule the underworld; he guides souls but does not judge them or determine their fate. His psychopomp role coexists with that of other deities associated with death and the underworld (e.g., Charon, Thanatos, Hades).

Trickster and patron of thieves

Hermes embodies cunning, deception, and playful transgression. The Homeric Hymn to Hermes narrates his theft of Apollo’s cattle, his inventive concealment of tracks, and his persuasive rhetoric in negotiating reconciliation. He is explicitly associated with thieves, liars, and those who profit from clever manipulation, and he can grant or withhold luck in such endeavors.

Limit: Hermes’ trickery is often framed within a broader cosmic order; his deceptions typically lead to new arrangements or inventions rather than pure chaos. He is not a god of indiscriminate destruction.

Inventor of the lyre and patron of music

According to the Homeric Hymn to Hermes, the newborn god fashions a lyre from a tortoise shell, reeds, and gut strings, then plays it skillfully. He later gifts the lyre to Apollo as part of their reconciliation, establishing a mythic origin for the instrument and linking Hermes to musical creativity and innovation.

Limit: Apollo becomes the primary god of music and the lyre in most traditions; Hermes’ role is foundational and inventive rather than central in ongoing musical cult.

God of commerce, trade, and profit

Hermes (and Roman Mercury) presides over markets, merchants, and economic exchange. He is associated with weights and measures, contracts, and the circulation of goods. Treasure found unexpectedly is sometimes attributed to his favor, and he can grant luck in business, whether honest or dishonest.

Limit: Hermes is not a god of agricultural production or large-scale state finance; his domain focuses on exchange, mobility, and individual or small-scale commerce.

Protector of travelers, shepherds, and athletes

Hermes safeguards those who move through space—travelers on roads, shepherds tending flocks, and athletes competing in games. His speed and agility make him an ideal patron of physical movement and endurance. Cults and festivals often invoke him in connection with athletic contests and safe journeys.

Limit: Protection is conditional and not absolute; Hermes can also be associated with misdirection and risk, reflecting the ambivalence of travel and competition.

Mediator and boundary-crosser

Hermes specializes in crossing and negotiating boundaries—geographical, social, and metaphysical. He moves between Olympus, earth, and the underworld; between gods and mortals; and between different social spheres such as market and countryside. Herms mark physical boundaries, while Hermes himself embodies the possibility of crossing them.

Limit: Hermes mediates but does not always resolve conflicts; his interventions can be partial or ambiguous.

Dream-sending and last libation before sleep

In some traditions, Hermes is associated with dreams and receives the last libation before sleep, suggesting a role in guiding or influencing nocturnal experiences. This aligns with his broader function as mediator of messages and transitions.

Limit: Hermes is not the only dream-associated deity; others such as Hypnos and Morpheus also play roles. Evidence for systematic dream cult to Hermes is limited.

Fertility and ithyphallic power

Arcadian and other rustic cults depict Hermes in ithyphallic form, emphasizing male fertility, sexual potency, and generative power. Herms, with their prominent phallic elements, function as apotropaic and fertility symbols at boundaries and in public spaces.

Limit: This fertility aspect is more pronounced in certain regions (e.g., Arcadia) and periods than in pan-Hellenic literary representations, which often foreground Hermes’ messenger and trickster roles.

Accounts I
The birth of Hermes and the theft of Apollo’s cattleHomeric Hymn to Hermes

Archaic (c. 6th century BCE, traditional dating)

Hermes is born at dawn to Maia and Zeus in a cave on Mount Cyllene. By midday, the infant god leaves his cradle, discovers a tortoise, and invents the lyre from its shell. That night, he steals the cattle of his half-brother Apollo, cleverly reversing their hooves and walking them backward to conceal the tracks. Apollo discovers the theft and confronts Hermes, who denies and deflects with witty speech. The dispute is brought before Zeus, who laughs at Hermes’ audacity but orders reconciliation. Hermes returns the cattle and gifts the lyre to Apollo, establishing a bond between them and securing his place among the Olympians as a clever, inventive, and mediating deity. Hermes gains recognition and honor among the gods, establishes his role as trickster and inventor, and forges a lasting relationship with Apollo through the exchange of cattle and lyre. The story explains the origin of the lyre and Hermes’ association with music, theft, and negotiation.

Hermes and Odysseus: Circe and the herb molyHomer, Odyssey Book 10

Archaic (c. 8th century BCE, traditional dating)

When Odysseus’ men are transformed into swine by the sorceress Circe, Hermes appears to Odysseus in the guise of a young man. He warns Odysseus about Circe’s magic and gives him the herb moly, which protects him from her potion. Odysseus follows Hermes’ instructions, confronts Circe, and compels her to restore his men and host him honorably. Hermes thus acts as a protective guide, enabling Odysseus to navigate a dangerous encounter through divine assistance and strategic knowledge. Odysseus survives Circe’s magic, his men are restored, and he gains a crucial ally for his journey. Hermes’ intervention underscores his role as helper of heroes, mediator of knowledge, and provider of protective remedies.

Hermes as psychopomp: leading the souls of the suitorsHomer, Odyssey Book 24

Archaic

After Odysseus kills the suitors who had overrun his household, Hermes gathers their souls and leads them to the underworld. He uses his golden wand to marshal them like birds, guiding them along the path to Hades. The narrative briefly follows the souls as they encounter the shades of Achilles and Agamemnon, highlighting Hermes’ role as orderly conductor of the dead. The suitors’ souls reach the underworld, and the story illustrates the proper transition from life to death under divine guidance. Hermes’ psychopomp function is clearly articulated, reinforcing his liminal and mediating character.

Accounts II
Hermes and Argus Panoptes: the liberation of IoLater Greek mythological tradition (e.g., Ovid’s Metamorphoses for Latin version; Greek sources alluded in scholia and mythographers)

Classical and Hellenistic/early Roman

Zeus transforms the mortal woman Io into a cow to hide her from Hera, but Hera sets the many-eyed giant Argus Panoptes to guard her. Zeus sends Hermes to rescue Io. Hermes lulls Argus to sleep with stories and music, then kills him, freeing Io. Hera later commemorates Argus by placing his eyes on the tail of the peacock. Hermes earns the epithet Argeiphontes (slayer of Argus) from this deed. Io is freed from Argus’ surveillance, though her wanderings continue. Hermes gains a significant epithet and reputation as a cunning killer who uses deception and artistry rather than brute force. The myth also reflects tensions among the Olympians and the vulnerability of mortals caught in divine conflicts.

Hermes guiding Priam to AchillesHomer, Iliad Book 24

Archaic

King Priam of Troy secretly travels to the Achaean camp to ransom the body of his son Hector from Achilles. Zeus sends Hermes to escort Priam safely. Hermes appears in the guise of a young warrior, drives Priam’s chariot, and guides him through the hostile camp, rendering him invisible to guards. After Priam meets Achilles and secures Hector’s body, Hermes leads him back. The episode highlights Hermes’ protective guidance and his ability to move unnoticed through dangerous spaces. Priam successfully ransoms Hector’s body, enabling proper funeral rites and a temporary suspension of hostilities. Hermes’ intervention underscores his role as mediator and protector of vulnerable mortals in extreme situations.

Hermes and Pan: rustic offspring and pastoral cultVarious mythographical and cultic sources; Pausanias; later compilations

Classical and Hellenistic

In several traditions, Hermes is named as the father of Pan, the goat-footed god of shepherds and wild nature, by a nymph or other rustic figure. Pan’s birth is greeted with laughter and delight among the gods, and he becomes closely associated with Arcadian landscapes and pastoral cult. Hermes’ paternity links him to rustic fertility and the wild, complementing his more urban and commercial roles. Hermes’ family ties extend into the rustic pantheon, reinforcing his connection to shepherds and countryside. Pan’s existence adds another dimension to Hermes’ fertility and pastoral associations.
