Hermes is the messenger of the gods, the god of boundaries, travel, commerce, thieves, athletes, and the guide of souls to the underworld. He is the most versatile and approachable of the Olympians — a trickster, a diplomat, an inventor, and a psychopomp.

Who They Are

Hermes is the messenger of the gods, the god of boundaries, travel, commerce, thieves, athletes, and the guide of souls to the underworld. He is the most versatile and approachable of the Olympians — a trickster, a diplomat, an inventor, and a psychopomp. Born in a cave on Mount Cyllene in Arcadia, the son of Zeus and the nymph Maia, Hermes demonstrated his precocious cunning within hours of his birth by stealing Apollo's cattle and inventing the lyre. His Roman equivalent, Mercury, gave his name to the planet, the element (quicksilver), and the quality of mercurial changeability.

Origins

The most complete account of Hermes's birth and early exploits is the Homeric Hymn to Hermes (c. 6th century BCE). Maia, a shy nymph who 'avoided the company of the blessed gods and lived in a shadowy cave' on Mount Cyllene in Arcadia, was visited by Zeus in the dead of night while Hera slept. She gave birth to Hermes — 'a child of many shifts, crafty of counsel, a robber, a driver of cattle, a guide of dreams, a watcher by night, a thief at the gates' — and wrapped him in swaddling clothes.

Within hours of his birth, Hermes crept from his cradle. At the threshold of the cave, he found a tortoise. 'A marvelous sign!' he declared, and immediately killed it, hollowed out its shell, stretched oxhide across it, and attached two arms and seven strings of sheep gut — inventing the lyre, the instrument that would become Apollo's signature. He played a song about his own birth, about Zeus and Maia, and about 'the famous house of the nymph' — a self-mythologizing performance from a newborn.

Still restless, Hermes traveled to Pieria (northern Greece), where the cattle of Apollo grazed. He stole fifty cattle, driving them backward (so their tracks would point the wrong way) and fashioning sandals from wicker and myrtle branches to obscure his own footprints. An old man working in his vineyard saw him and was warned by Hermes to remain silent. When Apollo eventually tracked the cattle to the cave, the infant Hermes played innocent, retreating into his swaddling clothes and denying any knowledge of the theft.

Zeus, called to judge the dispute, was so amused by his infant son's cunning that he laughed aloud and ordered the brothers to reconcile. Hermes gave Apollo the lyre, and Apollo was so enchanted that he granted Hermes dominion over cattle, the caduceus (herald's staff), and knowledge of divination through the Thriae (bee-women oracles). The two became the closest of the Olympian siblings — Apollo representing civilization and light, Hermes representing the clever, boundary-crossing intelligence that makes civilization possible.

Hermes's role as psychopomp (soul-guide) — the god who escorts the souls of the deceased to the underworld — is established throughout Homer. In the Odyssey (Book 24), he guides the souls of the suitors slain by Odysseus to Hades, holding his golden wand and leading them 'squeaking like bats in the depths of a cavern.' This function makes Hermes the only Olympian (besides Hades and Persephone) with unrestricted access to all three realms: Olympus, Earth, and the Underworld.

Hermes's slaying of Argus earned him the epithet Argeiphontes (Argus-Slayer). When Zeus transformed Io into a white heifer to hide her from Hera, Hera set the hundred-eyed giant Argus to guard her. Zeus sent Hermes to free Io. Hermes approached Argus disguised as a shepherd, lulled him to sleep with stories and music, and then killed him with a stone or sword — earning the epithet that would define his mythology.

Hermes's many children include Pan (by the nymph Dryope or Penelope), Hermaphroditus (with Aphrodite — a being of dual gender), and Autolycus (a master thief who could make himself and his stolen goods invisible — a gift from his father). In some traditions, he is the father of the god Priapus and the ancestor of Odysseus (through Autolycus, Odysseus's maternal grandfather).

The etymology of Hermes's name may connect to 'herma' (a stone heap, a boundary marker) — the herms that dotted Greek roads and marked boundaries. These square pillars topped with Hermes's head and featuring an erect phallus were apotropaic guardians of travelers and boundaries, and their defacement (the mutilation of the herms in 415 BCE, on the eve of the Athenian expedition to Sicily) was one of the great religious scandals of classical Athens.

Form

Hermes is depicted as a lithe, athletic young man — the ideal of the ephebe, a youth on the cusp of adulthood. He is typically beardless in classical art, with a slim but muscled physique built for speed rather than power. His body is that of a runner: lean legs, a narrow waist, and the efficient musculature of someone whose primary mode of movement is flight. His face is clever and alert, with a hint of mischief in the expression — the look of someone who knows more than he's telling.

Hermes's most distinctive attributes are his winged sandals (talaria), which allow him to fly with the speed of thought. In some depictions, the wings sprout from his ankles; in others, they emerge from his hat. The sandals are often golden, and their wings are described as white or iridescent. With them, Hermes can travel between Olympus, Earth, and the Underworld in moments.

The petasos — a broad-brimmed traveler's hat — is his second attribute, often shown with small wings. In earlier art, Hermes wears this hat pushed back on his head; in later art, it is sometimes replaced by a winged cap. The combination of winged sandals and winged hat emphasizes his role as the god of swift movement across all boundaries.

The caduceus (kerykeion) is Hermes's herald's staff — a golden wand entwined with two serpents, topped with wings. Originally a simple herald's staff (a symbol of diplomatic immunity), the caduceus acquired its serpents through syncretism and later confusion with Asclepius's single-serpent rod (the Rod of Asclepius, the symbol of medicine). The caduceus is the symbol of commerce and negotiation — a staff that opens doors, seals deals, and guarantees safe passage for messengers.

Hermes also carries a purse (symbolizing commerce), a sword (the harpe, with which he slew Argus), and sometimes a lyre or syrinx (pan pipes). His chlamys (traveler's cloak) is thrown over one shoulder, emphasizing his role as the eternal traveler.

In the famous 'Hermes and the Infant Dionysus' by Praxiteles (c. 340 BCE, Olympia), Hermes is shown as a beautiful youth leaning against a tree trunk, holding the baby Dionysus in his left arm, his right arm (now missing) probably holding a bunch of grapes to amuse the infant. The statue captures Hermes's grace, his role as protector of the young god, and the relaxed, approachable quality that distinguishes him from more remote Olympians.

Sensory evocation: Hermes's presence is the breeze that rustles through crossroads at twilight — quick, unexpected, there and gone. He smells of fresh earth from the road, ozone from the heights, and the faint, clean scent of the air before a storm. His voice is quick, clever, and musical — capable of persuasive eloquence or rapid-fire wit. His laughter is frequent and genuine; he is the only god who seems to genuinely enjoy his work. To encounter Hermes is to feel luck turn — a sudden windfall, an unexpected opportunity, a door opening where none was visible before.

Powers

Divine messenger and herald Hermes serves as the official messenger of Zeus and other Olympian gods, conveying commands, warnings, and negotiations between divine and mortal realms. In the Odyssey, he is sent to instruct Calypso to release Odysseus and later aids Odysseus against Circe. In the Iliad, he guides Priam safely through the Achaean camp to Achilles. As herald, he possesses the authority to speak on behalf of the gods and to traverse cosmic boundaries swiftly. Homeric epic (Iliad, Odyssey); broader Greek mythological tradition; Roman Mercury adaptations. Hermes’ authority as messenger is derivative of Zeus and the Olympian hierarchy; he does not independently determine divine policy. His role can be superseded or complemented by other messengers (e.g., Iris) in some traditions. Some texts emphasize Iris as primary messenger, especially in earlier epic, while others foreground Hermes. Scholarly debate concerns whether Hermes gradually supplanted Iris as the standard divine messenger or whether their roles remained complementary.

Psychopomp (guide of souls) Hermes escorts the souls of the dead from the world of the living to Hades, ensuring safe passage and proper transition. In Odyssey Book 24, he leads the souls of the slain suitors to the underworld. This function aligns with his broader role as mediator of boundaries and transitions, including those between life and death. Homeric epic; later Greek religious and philosophical texts; Kerényi’s interpretive synthesis. Hermes does not rule the underworld; he guides souls but does not judge them or determine their fate. His psychopomp role coexists with that of other deities associated with death and the underworld (e.g., Charon, Thanatos, Hades). Some cults and texts emphasize other psychopomp figures more strongly, leading to debate over how central Hermes’ psychopomp function was in everyday religious practice versus literary representation.

Trickster and patron of thieves Hermes embodies cunning, deception, and playful transgression. The Homeric Hymn to Hermes narrates his theft of Apollo’s cattle, his inventive concealment of tracks, and his persuasive rhetoric in negotiating reconciliation. He is explicitly associated with thieves, liars, and those who profit from clever manipulation, and he can grant or withhold luck in such endeavors. Homeric Hymn to Hermes; later mythological and cultic references; EBSCO Research Starter summary. Hermes’ trickery is often framed within a broader cosmic order; his deceptions typically lead to new arrangements or inventions rather than pure chaos. He is not a god of indiscriminate destruction. Some later moralizing traditions downplay his patronage of theft, emphasizing instead his role in commerce and honest gain. Modern receptions sometimes sanitize his trickster aspects, creating tension between ancient and contemporary portrayals.

Inventor of the lyre and patron of music According to the Homeric Hymn to Hermes, the newborn god fashions a lyre from a tortoise shell, reeds, and gut strings, then plays it skillfully. He later gifts the lyre to Apollo as part of their reconciliation, establishing a mythic origin for the instrument and linking Hermes to musical creativity and innovation. Homeric Hymn to Hermes; later literary references. Apollo becomes the primary god of music and the lyre in most traditions; Hermes’ role is foundational and inventive rather than central in ongoing musical cult. Some sources attribute the invention of the lyre or kithara to other figures or treat the story as symbolic rather than literal, leading to interpretive debates about the myth’s function.

God of commerce, trade, and profit Hermes (and Roman Mercury) presides over markets, merchants, and economic exchange. He is associated with weights and measures, contracts, and the circulation of goods. Treasure found unexpectedly is sometimes attributed to his favor, and he can grant luck in business, whether honest or dishonest. Greek cult practices; EBSCO Research Starter; Roman religious and material culture; Burkert’s synthesis. Hermes is not a god of agricultural production or large-scale state finance; his domain focuses on exchange, mobility, and individual or small-scale commerce. Ancient and modern interpretations differ on whether Hermes primarily blesses honest trade or also legitimizes profit through trickery and exploitation. Roman Mercury’s strong commercial emphasis can overshadow other Greek aspects, creating tension in cross-cultural readings.

Protector of travelers, shepherds, and athletes Hermes safeguards those who move through space—travelers on roads, shepherds tending flocks, and athletes competing in games. His speed and agility make him an ideal patron of physical movement and endurance. Cults and festivals often invoke him in connection with athletic contests and safe journeys. Literary references (Homer, hymns); cultic evidence; EBSCO Research Starter; Britannica summary. Protection is conditional and not absolute; Hermes can also be associated with misdirection and risk, reflecting the ambivalence of travel and competition. Some local cults emphasize other protective deities more strongly, and modern receptions may focus on Hermes’ messenger role rather than his athletic patronage.

Mediator and boundary-crosser Hermes specializes in crossing and negotiating boundaries—geographical, social, and metaphysical. He moves between Olympus, earth, and the underworld; between gods and mortals; and between different social spheres such as market and countryside. Herms mark physical boundaries, while Hermes himself embodies the possibility of crossing them. Tracking Hermes, Pursuing Mercury (multiple essays); Kerényi; Burkert; archaeological evidence of herms. Hermes mediates but does not always resolve conflicts; his interventions can be partial or ambiguous. Scholars debate whether boundary mediation is Hermes’ primary defining function or one aspect among many (trickster, fertility, commerce). Different theoretical approaches (structuralist, psychological, historical) emphasize different facets.

Dream-sending and last libation before sleep In some traditions, Hermes is associated with dreams and receives the last libation before sleep, suggesting a role in guiding or influencing nocturnal experiences. This aligns with his broader function as mediator of messages and transitions. Cultic and literary references summarized in Britannica and scholarly works. Hermes is not the only dream-associated deity; others such as Hypnos and Morpheus also play roles. Evidence for systematic dream cult to Hermes is limited. Some scholars treat Hermes’ dream role as secondary or derivative, while others see it as integral to his liminal character.

Fertility and ithyphallic power Arcadian and other rustic cults depict Hermes in ithyphallic form, emphasizing male fertility, sexual potency, and generative power. Herms, with their prominent phallic elements, function as apotropaic and fertility symbols at boundaries and in public spaces. Pausanias; archaeological evidence of herms; Burkert; regional cult studies. This fertility aspect is more pronounced in certain regions (e.g., Arcadia) and periods than in pan-Hellenic literary representations, which often foreground Hermes’ messenger and trickster roles. Later philosophical and Christian receptions tend to suppress or reinterpret Hermes’ sexual and fertility imagery, creating tension between ancient cultic realities and later moral frameworks.

Behavior & Habitat

Hermes is the most genial and least threatening of the Olympians. He treats mortals with a familiarity that other gods lack, appearing as a helpful stranger, a guide, or an advisor rather than a terrifying divine presence. His relationship with Odysseus in the Odyssey — appearing to warn him about Circe's magic and providing the herb moly as protection — exemplifies his role as the god who helps mortals navigate dangerous transitions.

Hermes's dual nature as trickster and culture-hero is fundamental. He steals Apollo's cattle and then, when caught, gives Apollo the lyre — transforming theft into gift, conflict into reconciliation, and establishing music as a divine art. This pattern — transgression that produces cultural innovation — marks Hermes as the god who operates at boundaries and, by crossing them, expands the realm of the possible.

Among the gods, Hermes maintains cordial relationships with almost everyone. He is Apollo's closest friend among the Olympians. He delivers Zeus's messages without complaint. He is the only god who regularly visits Hades (and seems to enjoy the trip). His irreverent humor and lack of pretension make him welcome in every divine court, and his diplomatic skills make him indispensable when divine conflicts escalate.

Hermes's relationship with mortals is characterized by practical helpfulness. He protects travelers, guides lost souls, grants luck to merchants, and defends athletes. His herm markers dotted every Greek road, offering protection and guidance to wayfarers. He is the god who helps you find your way — literally and metaphorically — and his aid is practical rather than salvific.

Hermes's erotic life is active but less fraught than Zeus's or Apollo's. His children include Pan (the result of his union with a nymph) and Hermaphroditus (the dual-gendered being born of his union with Aphrodite). His love affairs are characterized by the same playful, boundary-crossing energy that defines his character.

Weaknesses & Wards

Hermes's amorality regarding property makes him untrustworthy in certain contexts — he is the god of thieves as well as merchants, and his blessing can as easily facilitate crime as commerce. His trickster nature means he is rarely taken entirely seriously, and his authority — unlike Zeus's thunderbolt or Athena's wisdom — is situational and contingent. He cannot command; he can only negotiate, persuade, and assist. His speed and cleverness make him invulnerable in some ways but ill-suited for sustained conflict. He lacks the raw power of his Olympian siblings and would lose any direct confrontation with a more powerful god. His loyalty is ultimately to Zeus, not to the mortals he helps — he will abandon them if Zeus commands.

Accounts

Hermes is born at dawn to Maia and Zeus in a cave on Mount Cyllene. By midday, the infant god leaves his cradle, discovers a tortoise, and invents the lyre from its shell. That night, he steals the cattle of his half-brother Apollo, cleverly reversing their hooves and walking them backward to conceal the tracks. Apollo discovers the theft and confronts Hermes, who denies and deflects with witty speech. The dispute is brought before Zeus, who laughs at Hermes’ audacity but orders reconciliation. Hermes returns the cattle and gifts the lyre to Apollo, establishing a bond between them and securing his place among the Olympians as a clever, inventive, and mediating deity.

When Odysseus’ men are transformed into swine by the sorceress Circe, Hermes appears to Odysseus in the guise of a young man. He warns Odysseus about Circe’s magic and gives him the herb moly, which protects him from her potion. Odysseus follows Hermes’ instructions, confronts Circe, and compels her to restore his men and host him honorably. Hermes thus acts as a protective guide, enabling Odysseus to navigate a dangerous encounter through divine assistance and strategic knowledge.

After Odysseus kills the suitors who had overrun his household, Hermes gathers their souls and leads them to the underworld. He uses his golden wand to marshal them like birds, guiding them along the path to Hades. The narrative briefly follows the souls as they encounter the shades of Achilles and Agamemnon, highlighting Hermes’ role as orderly conductor of the dead.

Zeus transforms the mortal woman Io into a cow to hide her from Hera, but Hera sets the many-eyed giant Argus Panoptes to guard her. Zeus sends Hermes to rescue Io. Hermes lulls Argus to sleep with stories and music, then kills him, freeing Io. Hera later commemorates Argus by placing his eyes on the tail of the peacock. Hermes earns the epithet Argeiphontes (slayer of Argus) from this deed.

King Priam of Troy secretly travels to the Achaean camp to ransom the body of his son Hector from Achilles. Zeus sends Hermes to escort Priam safely. Hermes appears in the guise of a young warrior, drives Priam’s chariot, and guides him through the hostile camp, rendering him invisible to guards. After Priam meets Achilles and secures Hector’s body, Hermes leads him back. The episode highlights Hermes’ protective guidance and his ability to move unnoticed through dangerous spaces.

In several traditions, Hermes is named as the father of Pan, the goat-footed god of shepherds and wild nature, by a nymph or other rustic figure. Pan’s birth is greeted with laughter and delight among the gods, and he becomes closely associated with Arcadian landscapes and pastoral cult. Hermes’ paternity links him to rustic fertility and the wild, complementing his more urban and commercial roles.

Cultural Significance

Hermes was one of the most widely worshipped and culturally integrated Greek gods. His herms (square pillars topped with a bust of Hermes, featuring an erect phallus) stood at crossroads, boundaries, and doorways throughout Greece as apotropaic guardians. The mutilation of the herms in Athens in 415 BCE was a scandal that helped doom the Sicilian Expedition. The Hermaea was a festival of athletic and gymnastic competitions, reflecting Hermes's role as the patron of the gymnasium and palaestra. In Roman religion, Hermes became Mercury, the god of commerce, travelers, and thieves — 'mercurial' describes someone quick-witted and unpredictable. The planet Mercury, named for his swiftness, has the shortest orbital period. The caduceus (confused with the Rod of Asclepius) became a symbol of medicine in the modern era. In alchemy, 'mercury' (quicksilver) was the principle of fluidity and transformation. In Jungian psychology, Hermes/Mercury represents the archetype of the trickster and the guide of souls (psychopomp). In Christianity, Hermes's role as messenger was partially absorbed into the figure of angels (divine messengers), and his role as soul-guide influenced the figure of Saint Peter at the Pearly Gates. The hermetic tradition, which traces its origins to Hermes Trismegistus (a syncretic fusion of Hermes and the Egyptian god Thoth), produced a vast corpus of alchemical, astrological, and philosophical texts that influenced the Renaissance and the development of Western esotericism.

Modern Interpretations

Hermes/Mercury appears across modern media as the swift, clever, and unreliable god. In Disney's Hercules (1997), Hermes is a comedic background character. In Rick Riordan's Percy Jackson series, Hermes appears as a jogging, cell-phone-using god who runs a delivery service — a witty modernization of his messenger role. His son Luke Castellan is the series' primary antagonist in the first arc, adding tragic depth to Hermes's character as a father who failed his son. In the Hades video game (2020), Hermes offers speed-based boons (faster attack, faster movement, extra dashes) and his dialogue is rapid-fire and witty, delivered at double-speed — a perfect ludic translation of his mythological swiftness. In God of War III (2010), Hermes appears as a taunting, speed-based boss whom Kratos catches and kills — his death releases a plague of insects upon the world. In Age of Mythology (2002), Hermes is a minor god option for Zeus, granting cavalry speed bonuses. In Smite (2014), Hermes is a playable messenger god. In the DC Universe, the Flash's speed and Mercury's winged sandals are frequently compared; the helmet of the original Flash (Jay Garrick) explicitly references Mercury. In Marvel Comics, Hermes appears among the Olympians. In the 2018 film The House That Jack Built, the protagonist compares himself to Hermes as the guide of souls. In the TV series American Gods, the character of Wednesday (Odin) shares Hermes's trickster archetype, and the theme of forgotten gods adapting to modern life resonates with Hermes's mercurial adaptability.

Content Notes

Hermes’ theft of Apollo’s cattle, though framed playfully in the Homeric Hymn, involves deliberate deception, property violation, and manipulation of evidence (reversed hoofprints, concealment of tracks). As patron of thieves and liars, Hermes legitimizes or at least sacralizes forms of dishonesty and exploitation, reflecting ancient ambivalence about cunning and profit.

Scholars interpret Hermes’ theft as a paradigmatic trickster episode, exploring its structural role in myth, its reflection of social attitudes toward cunning, and its function in establishing Hermes’ identity. Some emphasize its playful tone; others highlight its darker implications for property and trust.

Homeric Hymn to Hermes; EBSCO Research Starter; scholarly analyses in Tracking Hermes, Pursuing Mercury.

Hermes kills Argus Panoptes, the many-eyed guardian set by Hera to watch Io. He uses storytelling and music to lull Argus to sleep, then strikes him down. This act involves premeditated killing and complicity in Zeus’ exploitation of Io, who is transformed and pursued because of Zeus’ desire.

Interpretations examine Hermes’ role as Argeiphontes, the ethics of his actions, and the broader dynamics of divine power and mortal vulnerability. Some readings emphasize Hermes’ loyalty to Zeus; others critique the violence and manipulation involved.

Mythological tradition; Ovid, Metamorphoses; later summaries.

Herms, pillar-like boundary markers associated with Hermes, often feature prominent phallic representations. These objects function as apotropaic and fertility symbols but also reflect explicit sexual imagery in public spaces. Hermes’ Arcadian cult includes ithyphallic statues, emphasizing male sexual potency and generative power.

Scholars analyze herms in relation to fertility, boundary protection, and social norms about sexuality. Modern sensibilities may find such imagery uncomfortable, and later religious traditions often censored or destroyed phallic cult objects. The 415 BCE mutilation of herms in Athens (though not directly about Hermes’ myth) illustrates the political and religious sensitivity surrounding these images.

Pausanias; archaeological evidence; Burkert’s Greek Religion; regional cult studies.

Hermes participates in or facilitates situations where mortals suffer because of divine desires and conflicts. In the Io myth, his killing of Argus serves Zeus’ interest in Io, whose transformation and wandering result from divine jealousy and lust. Hermes’ role as messenger and agent of Zeus can place him on the side of coercive divine power, even when he aids individual mortals like Priam or Odysseus.

Critical readings highlight the asymmetry of power between gods and mortals and the ways Hermes’ mediation can both alleviate and reinforce that asymmetry. Feminist and postcolonial approaches examine the impact of such myths on perceptions of gender, agency, and authority.

Io and Argus myth; Homeric epic; later mythographical sources.

A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine you are the world's most overqualified delivery driver. You have wings on your shoes, you can be anywhere in the universe in seconds, and your boss is the literal king of the gods. You handle all diplomatic communications, guide the souls of the dead to the underworld (a commute that never gets less weird), protect travelers and merchants, and invent musical instruments in your spare time. You're also a pathological prankster who once stole your brother's car before you were a day old and then talked your way out of it.
