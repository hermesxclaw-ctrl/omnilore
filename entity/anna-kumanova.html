Who It Is

Anna Kumanova is a Romani goddess of divination, prophecy, and fate — the patroness of fortune-tellers and the spiritual source of the Romani gift for seeing the future. Her name associates her with Kumane/Kumanovo in the Balkans, suggesting a localized origin later spread through Romani migrations. She is envisioned as a seated woman of middle age, her hands perpetually shuffling cards or casting bones, and her eyes half-closed in trance. Anna Kumanova does not control fate but reads it, revealing what the stars, the cards, or the lines of the palm have already written. She is impartial and unforthcoming — she shows what is, not what one wishes to see. Romani fortune-tellers historically invoked her name as part of their ritual preparations, and it was considered taboo to seek her aid on behalf of oneself (only for others). Today, she remains a symbol of Romani cultural identity in the face of persecution — the idea that the Romani possess a sacred, hidden knowledge protected by a maternal goddess-figure who cannot be colonized or stolen by the gadje (non-Roma).

Form

The archive's description envisions 'a seated woman of middle age, her hands perpetually shuffling cards or casting bones, and her eyes half-closed in trance.' This is an image constructed from the stock iconography of the Romani fortune-teller in Western popular culture rather than from any attested religious art or text: no statue, icon, manuscript miniature, or oral description of an 'Anna Kumanova' was located. The seated prophetess with closed eyes is a universal diviner type (cf. the sibyl, the oracle of Delphi), and the shuffling of cards is an anachronism retrojected onto an undefined 'pre-modern' era, since playing cards and tarot only reached Europe in the late 14th–15th centuries (Tarot article). The description should be read as a creative reconstruction, not evidence.

Powers

Augury / Divination of fate The archive's central claim: Anna Kumanova 'does not control fate but reads it,' revealing 'what the stars, the cards, or the lines of the palm have already written.' As an attested power-set this is entirely derivative of the real catalogue of Romani-associated divination methods documented in the Fortune-telling article — chiromancy (palmistry), cartomancy (playing/tarot cards), crystallomancy (crystal ball/scrying), tasseography (tea leaves), and cleromancy (casting lots, bones, or stones). Recorded only in the OmniLore archive record (unnamed/unsourced modern reconstruction); the underlying methods are genuinely attested Romani-associated praxis. Even within the reconstructed fiction she cannot alter the future she sees — only reveal it, and, per the record's ethic, only for others, never for herself. No independent source attributes any of these powers to a named deity 'Anna Kumanova.'

Second sight / trance vision The record describes 'her eyes half-closed in trance' and consultation of 'a polished stone.' This is the classic language of scrying/crystallomancy and of the seer-state, widely but non-specifically attached to fortune-tellers across cultures (including the Roma in popular perception). Modern reconstruction; scrying is a documented divination method (Fortune-telling article, 'Scrying: by looking at or into reflective objects'). Unverifiable as a named deity-power; cold-reading and the Barnum effect are the skeptical explanations offered for the perceived accuracy of such readings (Fortune-telling article, Critical analysis). None found among separate mythic sources because none exist for this figure.

Patronage of fortune-tellers Claimed patroness of Romani fortune-tellers, 'invoked as part of their ritual preparations.' The real, documented Romani religious geography does contain patron and protective figures — above all Saint Sarah (Sara e Kali) at Saintes-Maries-de-la-Mer, whose May pilgrimage is the central Romani devotional gathering — but no documented practice invokes 'Anna Kumanova.' Reconstruction; the genuine patron-saint parallel is Sara e Kali (Saint Sarah). No fieldwork, prayer text, or practitioner testimony naming 'Anna Kumanova' was found. Conflates Catholic saint-veneration (Sara e Kali) with pantheon-goddess theology, which Romani religious practice, overwhelmingly Christian or Muslim in its workings, does not support.

Impartiality and the prohibition on self-serving readings The rule that it is 'taboo to seek her aid on behalf of oneself (only for others)' aligns with attested Romani attitudes toward powerful knowledge and the dangers of mišto ai bijav (good fortune misused) and taboos of purity and obligation (mahrime/kxwa). Archive record; consistent with documented Romani moral framework (cf. beng dossier's treatment of mahrime). Attested as an ethic, not as a deity's commandment to a named goddess. The origin of the 'only-for-others' rule is unplaceable; comparable rules exist in many diviner traditions and cannot be isolated to this figure.

Accounts

The archive's own summary asserts that Anna Kumanova's 'name associates her with Kumane/Kumanovo in the Balkans, suggesting a localized origin later spread through Romani migrations.' Because no source corroborates this, the narrative cannot stand as a story with attestation: it is an inferred back-story built from the toponym. The only genuinely documented 'origin' connecting the name to Roma is geographic and pedestrian — the 1861 von Hahn census record of a Gypsy community on Kumanovo's outskirts.

The single most important 'story' that the 'Anna Kumanova' record is almost certainly groping toward. Each May the town of Saintes-Maries-de-la-Mer receives 25,000–40,000 Roma pilgrims. The high point is the lowering of the reliquary chest of the Three Marys and the opening of the crypt so that the statue of the 'dark-skinned Saint Sara of the Roma pilgrimage' — the Roma's own saint — can be honoured. Peter's-saint figure, Sara is told as either the Egyptian servant of the Three Marys who accompanied them to Gaul, or a local woman who welcomed the saints ashore. Statues of Sara and the Maries are carried in procession into the sea. Tellingly, the crypt also houses a 4th-century BC taurobolic altar of Mithras — an Indo-Iranian cult object — giving the site a genuinely ancient, if contested, Indo-Iranian echo relevant to debates about Romani origins in India/Iran.

Kumanovo (Albanian definite: Kumanova) is a real North Macedonian city named for the Cumans/Kipchaks who settled c. the early 12th century. In 1861 the Austrian diplomat Johann Georg von Hahn counted 650 dwellings, of which 300 Muslim and 350 Christian Bulgarian, 'in addition to 30 Gypsy in the outskirts,' of a total population of 3,500. Today the city, around 77,500 strong per the 2002 census, retains a significant minority population including ethnic Albanians, Serbs, and, in the broader region, Roma/Ashkali/Egyptian communities. The region also contains the Kokino megalithic observatory (c. 4,000 years old, NASA's fourth-ranked). None of this is myth — but it is the plausible geographic seed from which a modern 'Kumanova' goddess-name could be minted by grafting a deity onto a real Roma-inhabited place.

Content Notes

The figure 'Anna Kumanova' as a 'Romani goddess' has no attestation in any located source. Presenting it as an authentic deity of Romani 'oral tradition' would be a fabrication that mirrors an old, damaging European habit: outsiders inventing an exotic 'Gypsy pantheon.' The late-19th-century folklorist Heinrich von Wlislocki famously did exactly this, and modern Romani scholarship treats his systematized pantheon of Gypsy fairies and demons as heavily embroidered (documented in the beng dossier). The OmniLore record must therefore be flagable as at-risk of reproducing this pattern.

When documenting contested/marginal traditions, the ethical standard is to state attestation status explicitly rather than to award an invented figure the prestige of an ancient deity.

beng dossier's treatment of Wlislocki (1890s); modern Romani scholarship (Hancock, Muscianisi 2022).

The association of Roma with palm-reading, card-reading, and theft-by-divination ('gypsy curses', fortune-teller fraud) has long been used to marginalize, criminalize, and evict Romani communities. European anti-*Roma* laws and municipal bans on fortune-telling historically targeted Roma disproportionately. The fortune-telling stereotype, even when 'flattering,' is part of the apparatus of antiziganism. Any archive entry that instrumentalizes a 'fortune-teller goddess' must be careful not to re-circulate the stereotype as fact.

The legal status of fortune-telling is a contested area of religious liberty and consumer-protection law; its history is entangled with race-based discrimination against Roma.

Fortune-telling (Wikipedia, Legality: municipal/state bans historically and currently; NY S.165.35 class B misdemeanor; Saudi Arabia death penalty for sorcery); Romani-historical scholarship.

While not about this goddess, the Kumanovo region's Roma and other minorities have endured real historical trauma — Ottoman-era marginalization, wartime persecutions of Roma during the Holocaust (the Porajmos) across occupied Europe including Yugoslavia, and ongoing social exclusion. The 'goddess' record carries no such content, but placing a Romani figure in this Balkan context without acknowledging the community's real 'dark history' of persecution would be sanitizing.

Must be stated as general historical context, NOT attributed to the (fictional) goddess.

Kumanovo (Wikipedia, History); general scholarship on the Porajmos.

A way to hold it in your mind

There is, so far as any encyclopedia can tell, no goddess named Anna Kumanova — and naming that absence, rather than decorating it, is the honest way to keep a treasure-hunt of 'all things unreal' from quietly minting the unreal as real. The record in this archive dresses a figure in the full regalia of the classic Gypsy fortune-teller of the imagination: shuffling cards, casting bones, a polished stone, a tent at the crossroads, a maternal goddess who reads the fate of the gadje but is forbidden to read her own. Every one of those props is genuinely, documented-ly Romani-adjacent. But the name itself traces to a Macedonian city named for a nomad Turkic tribe, the Cumans, and the real matron of Romani fortune is not a pagan pantheon queen at all but a dark-skinned pilgrim saint, Sara e Kali, carried into the surf at Saintes-Maries-de-la-Mer each May by tens of thousands of worshippers. To study 'Anna Kumanova' is therefore to study a beautiful deception standing exactly where a real tradition stands — and to learn the difference between an artifact of racism and a people's actual, sacred luck.
