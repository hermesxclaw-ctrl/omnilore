Who It Is

There is, so far as any encyclopedia can tell, no goddess named Anna Kumanova — and naming that absence, rather than decorating it, is the honest way to keep a treasure-hunt of 'all things unreal' from quietly minting the unreal as real. The record in this archive dresses a figure in the full regalia of the classic Gypsy fortune-teller of the imagination: shuffling cards, casting bones, a polished stone, a tent at the crossroads, a maternal goddess who reads the fate of the gadje but is forbidden to read her own. Every one of those props is genuinely, documented-ly Romani-adjacent. But the name itself traces to a Macedonian city named for a nomad Turkic tribe, the Cumans, and the real matron of Romani fortune is not a pagan pantheon queen at all but a dark-skinned pilgrim saint, Sara e Kali, carried into the surf at Saintes-Maries-de-la-Mer each May by tens of thousands of worshippers. Powers
Augury / Divination of fate

The archive's central claim: Anna Kumanova 'does not control fate but reads it,' revealing 'what the stars, the cards, or the lines of the palm have already written.' As an attested power-set this is entirely derivative of the real catalogue of Romani-associated divination methods documented in the Fortune-telling article — chiromancy (palmistry), cartomancy (playing/tarot cards), crystallomancy (crystal ball/scrying), tasseography (tea leaves), and cleromancy (casting lots, bones, or stones).

Limit: Even within the reconstructed fiction she cannot alter the future she sees — only reveal it, and, per the record's ethic, only for others, never for herself.

Second sight / trance vision

The record describes 'her eyes half-closed in trance' and consultation of 'a polished stone.' This is the classic language of scrying/crystallomancy and of the seer-state, widely but non-specifically attached to fortune-tellers across cultures (including the Roma in popular perception).

Limit: Unverifiable as a named deity-power; cold-reading and the Barnum effect are the skeptical explanations offered for the perceived accuracy of such readings (Fortune-telling article, Critical analysis).

Patronage of fortune-tellers

Claimed patroness of Romani fortune-tellers, 'invoked as part of their ritual preparations.' The real, documented Romani religious geography does contain patron and protective figures — above all Saint Sarah (Sara e Kali) at Saintes-Maries-de-la-Mer, whose May pilgrimage is the central Romani devotional gathering — but no documented practice invokes 'Anna Kumanova.'

Limit: No fieldwork, prayer text, or practitioner testimony naming 'Anna Kumanova' was found.

Impartiality and the prohibition on self-serving readings

The rule that it is 'taboo to seek her aid on behalf of oneself (only for others)' aligns with attested Romani attitudes toward powerful knowledge and the dangers of mišto ai bijav (good fortune misused) and taboos of purity and obligation (mahrime/kxwa).

Limit: Attested as an ethic, not as a deity's commandment to a named goddess.

Accounts I
The 'confirmed' origin as a Balkan Balkan local deity — an unverifiable claimOmniLore archive record summary (no cited primary/secondary source).

Recorded as 'Pre-modern through contemporary Romani folklore.'

The archive's own summary asserts that Anna Kumanova's 'name associates her with Kumane/Kumanovo in the Balkans, suggesting a localized origin later spread through Romani migrations.' Because no source corroborates this, the narrative cannot stand as a story with attestation: it is an inferred back-story built from the toponym. The only genuinely documented 'origin' connecting the name to Roma is geographic and pedestrian — the 1861 von Hahn census record of a Gypsy community on Kumanovo's outskirts. If the archive keeps this record, the 'deity of Kumanovo' framing should be explicitly relabelled as an unverified reconstruction rather than presented as tradition.

Accounts II
Sara e Kali: the real 'dark madonna' of the Roma pilgrimage (the genuine substrate)Wikipedia article 'Saintes-Maries-de-la-Mer', Religion section; extant pilgrimage tradition.

Annual, from the 19th-century institutionalization of the pilgrimage (1838 renaming) to the present; underlying Roma veneration of Sarah is older.

The single most important 'story' that the 'Anna Kumanova' record is almost certainly groping toward. Each May the town of Saintes-Maries-de-la-Mer receives 25,000–40,000 Roma pilgrims. The high point is the lowering of the reliquary chest of the Three Marys and the opening of the crypt so that the statue of the 'dark-skinned Saint Sara of the Roma pilgrimage' — the Roma's own saint — can be honoured. Peter's-saint figure, Sara is told as either the Egyptian servant of the Three Marys who accompanied them to Gaul, or a local woman who welcomed the saints ashore. Statues of Sara and the Maries are carried in procession into the sea. Tellingly, the crypt also houses a 4th-century BC taurobolic altar of Mithras — an Indo-Iranian cult object — giving the site a genuinely ancient, if contested, Indo-Iranian echo relevant to debates about Romani origins in India/Iran. Establishes that the real Romani matron-figure of fate/protection is a saint (Sara e Kali), venerated through Catholic forms, NOT a pagan pantheon goddess — undermining the archive's 'goddess' framing for 'Anna Kumanova.'

Kumanovo and the Roma of the Balkans: the toponymic threadWikipedia article 'Kumanovo' (Etymology, History, Demographics).

12th century (Cuman settlement) to present.

Kumanovo (Albanian definite: Kumanova) is a real North Macedonian city named for the Cumans/Kipchaks who settled c. the early 12th century. In 1861 the Austrian diplomat Johann Georg von Hahn counted 650 dwellings, of which 300 Muslim and 350 Christian Bulgarian, 'in addition to 30 Gypsy in the outskirts,' of a total population of 3,500. Today the city, around 77,500 strong per the 2002 census, retains a significant minority population including ethnic Albanians, Serbs, and, in the broader region, Roma/Ashkali/Egyptian communities. The region also contains the Kokino megalithic observatory (c. 4,000 years old, NASA's fourth-ranked). None of this is myth — but it is the plausible geographic seed from which a modern 'Kumanova' goddess-name could be minted by grafting a deity onto a real Roma-inhabited place. Corroborates the Roma–Kumanovo linkage underlying the record's name, and shows the mechanism (place + people + divination tradition → invented deity) by which an unattested goddess could be assembled.
