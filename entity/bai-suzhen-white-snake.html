Who It Is

One of the most beloved love stories in the Chinese-speaking world begins with a man who dissolves into water, leaving only his head. The beautiful widow who wrecked him was, in truth, a great white snake coiled beneath a locust tree — and she had been eating men's hearts and livers for pleasure. That is the earliest documented ancestor of Bai Suzhen, the gentle white-snake bride whose name means 'Pure and Chaste', who today is worshipped in a live-snake temple in Taiwan and whose gravestone-like imprisonment tower in Hangzhou — collapsed in 1924, rebuilt in 2002 — draws pilgrims and tourists by the thousands. Powers
Shape-shifting between snake and human form

Bai Suzhen's defining power: after ~1000-1800 years of Taoist cultivation she can transform between a great white snake and a beautiful young woman. In some versions she is granted human form by the 'essence of the Dragon King of the East China Sea' or by consuming an immortality pill; in the widespread opera version she becomes human after a millennium of disciplined Taoist practice on Mount Emei, then is accepted as a disciple by the goddess Lishan Laomu (or guided by Guan Yin/Guanyin in the 1992 TV version).

Limit: Her transformation is undone by realgar wine (xionghuang), traditionally drunk on the Dragon Boat Festival (Duanwu); when she drinks it she reverts to her serpentine form. In several versions she is weakened ('her powers are limited') while pregnant with Xu Xian's child. The green companion Xiaoqing is less practiced and reverts more easily.

Weather/water magic — the flooding of Jinshan Temple

In her confrontation with Fahai at Jinshan Temple (Zhenjiang), Bai Suzhen summons a storm and floods the temple with water in an attempt to rescue the imprisoned Xu Xian. The flood causes heavy collateral damage and drowns innocent people.

Limit: While pregnant, her powers are diminished and she ultimately fails to free Xu Xian by force; she must flee to Hangzhou. The Buddhist guardian deities (Weituo, Qielan/Sangharama) summoned by Fahai overwhelm her, and she is subsequently imprisoned.

Medical expertise and healing

Bai Suzhen and Xu Xian operate a successful Chinese herbal-medicine shop (in Zhenjiang, after their Suzhou exile); her knowledge of natural remedies, acquired through centuries of Taoist mastery, makes the business thrive and earns the couple local respect. In the folk-worship layer, she and Xu Xian 'once practiced Chinese medicine' and believers come to them seeking healing and health advice.

Limit: None documented; her healing is presented as genuinely beneficial and is central to her positive image.

The stealing of the immortal herb (Lingzhi/ganoderma)

When Xu Xian dies of shock at seeing her true form, Bai Suzhen forces her way to Mount Kunlun (in some versions directly to Heaven) to steal the magical lingzhi/ganoderma 'immortal herb' that restores him to life. She braves 'danger' — the herb is guarded by disciples of the Old Man of the South Pole / the immortals — and succeeds.

Limit: The theft is immensely risky (she faces the guardians of the immortals), but her desperation and courage carry it through; the feat is the emotional climax of her devotion.

Flight and combat (with Xiaoqing)

Bai Suzhen engages in supernatural combat with drawn weapons against Fahai's guardian deities, alongside Xiaoqing; in several versions the two sisters wield magic swords or a hairpin-wand. In the 2019/2021 animated films, Bai Suzhen crafts an illusory 'raft' crossing the West Lake with a hairpin and fights with a transformable hairpin wand. In the fight at Jinshan she also summons 'fierce snakes' (in The Sorcerer and the White Snake, 2011) to repel her enemies.

Limit: She is defeated by Fahai and his guardian deities; in the 2021 Green Snake the hairpin is destroyed and her magic is fully neutralized when she is trapped.

Access to/forging of spiritual cultivation and apotheosis

Bai Suzhen's entire arc is framed by Taoist-Buddhist cultivation: after a millennium of austerity she seeks to earn her immortality through good deeds; she is accepted by goddesses (Lishan Laomu) and eventually, in the folk-religion layer, achieves deification (Madam White Snake goddess) or becomes the mount of Yaotai Laomu as she continues to assist all living beings.

Limit: Her apotheosis requires selfless suffering (the centuries of imprisonment under the pagoda) — it is earned, not automatic; in the opera the Buddha realm (represented by Fahai) opposes her as an unregenerate demon until the son's filial piety moves Heaven.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Bai Suzhen is most often depicted as a pure-white snake capable of transforming into an exceptionally beautiful young woman dressed in white (hence 'Lady White'). Her snake form is a large white serpent (in the early Tang 'Li Huang' version, a white snake coiled beneath a tree; in the huaben tradition, a great snake that dissolves a man's body to water). In human form she is conventionally portrayed as elegant, graceful, and boundlessly devoted.

Symbols — Her emblem is the white snake itself; she is frequently shown alongside the Leifeng Pagoda (the tower that imprisons her), the West Lake/Broken Bridge (where she first meets Xu Xian — the Broken Bridge of Hangzhou is now a literal tourist landmark tied to the legend), and the lingzhi/ganoderma magical herb (the immortality fungus she steals to revive Xu Xian). Accounts I
The Tang 'Li Huang' haunting (oldest stratum)Boyi Zhi (Gu Shenzi), mid-Tang; included in Taiping Guangji

c. 807 setting; 9th century text

Li Huang, nephew of the Salt and Iron Commissioner, meets a beautiful white-clad widow (aided by an old 'aunt' in blue-green), is seduced, spends days with her, and upon returning home dissolves into water, leaving only his head. Neighbors report nothing in the empty garden but a large white snake coiled beneath a Chinese honey locust tree. A variant (protagonist Li Guan, appended in the Taiping Guangji) ends with his head splitting open; his family finds a withered tree and small white snakes under its roots, which they kill. This is the oldest attested ancestor of the White Snake legend — the original malicious snake-daemon who eats (or dissolves) men. Establishes the template of the beautiful snake-woman who seduces and destroys a man; her nature is exposed post-mortem by a dissolving body and a snake-coiled tree.

The Song/Ming 'Three Pagodas of West Lake' huabenQingpingshantang Huaben (Hong Pian ed.), written record of a storyteller's script

c. 1541-1551 (Jiajing era, Ming) — though the setting is the 1174-1189 Chunxi era of the Southern Song

Xi Xuanzan, rescued by the Daoist Xi Zhenren after being thrice entrapped by three shape-shifting demons at West Lake — a lady in white (a white snake), an old woman in black (an otter), and 'Maonu' (a black chicken) who saves him. Xi Zhenren seals them under three stone pagodas (the prototype of Leifeng). Here 'the three pagodas of West Lake' are stone markers, and the white snake still eats human hearts and livers, seducing Xuanzan into losing years of his vitality. Seals the 'suppress the snake under pagodas' motif that later attaches to the real Leifeng Pagoda; the Daoist (later Buddhist-monk) exorcist role is established.

Accounts II
Feng Menglong's 1624 'Madam White Is Kept Forever Under the Thunder Peak Tower'Stories to Caution the World (Jingshi Tongyan, Vol. 28), Feng Menglong

1624

Xu Xuan, a young man in a herbal medicine shop in Lin'an, meets Bai Niangzi and her blue-green maid Qingqing (a fish spirit) on a boat in the rain at the Qingming festival; he lends them an umbrella. She claims widowhood, they become intimate, and she gives him 50 taels of silver (which turn out to be stolen treasury money — Xu is arrested and exiled to Suzhou). In Suzhou she reappears, they marry; he is later exiled again to Zhenjiang over jewelry she gave him. In Zhenjiang, a lecherous birthday host spies her in the bathroom and sees a giant white snake, frightening him to death. Xu visits Jinshan Temple and meets the Zen master Fahai, who recognizes the demon; when Bai arrives to reclaim Xu, she creates a storm/flood. Fahai drives them away. Eventually Fahai traps her with an alms bowl, reveals she is a white snake (Qingqing a blue fish), and imprisons them both under the Leifeng Pagoda. Xu becomes a monk and dies meditating. The modern opera version (the canonical 'popular' plot)Synthesized Peking-opera form; formalized by playwright Tian Han's revisions (1943/1952/1955) and elements from Fang Chengpei's 1771 Leifeng Pagoda

20th century crystallization of older elements

A white snake and a blue-green snake on Mount Emei transform into Bai Suzhen and Xiaoqing, become sworn sisters, and travel to Hangzhou/West Lake, meeting young Xu Xian on a ferry in the rain; he lends his umbrella; they fall in love and marry, opening a medicine shop. Fahai, the abbot of Jinshan Temple, tells Xu his wife is a snake; when Xu, following Fahai's advice, makes Bai drink realgar wine on the Dragon Boat Festival, she reveals her serpent form and Xu dies of shock. Bai steals the ganoderma herb and revives him, but Xu remains fearful. He goes to Jinshan Temple where Fahai imprisons him; Bai and Xiaoqing fight Fahai, and Bai floods the temple (weakened by her pregnancy), failing to free him; Xiaoqing helps her escape. Xu escapes, realizes her love is genuine, and reunites with her at the Broken Bridge (where Xiaoqing nearly kills him in fury). Bai gives birth to their son Xu Mengjiao (later Xu Shilin). Snake-daemon vs devoted snake-brideThe White Snake is originally a lethal, lust-driven she-daemon who hunts and destroys men (Tang 'Li Huang'; Song 'Three Pagodas').She is a wronged, selfless romantic heroine who endures millennia of imprisonment for love (Qing opera/tanci; modern canon).
The reason for her human transformationBai becomes human through self-cultivation (a millennium of Taoist discipline on Mount Emei).She becomes human through external agency — the 'essence of the Dragon King of the East China Sea' or by swallowing the immortality pill Lü Dongbin dropped (which itself grants +500 years), or guided by Guan Yin.
Fahai's character and motivationFahai is a righteous, heroic demon-slayer; Xu Xuan even becomes his disciple (Feng Menglong, 1624).Fahai is a heartless, fanatical, family-wrecking villain — the jealous reincarnation of the terrapin who missed the immortality pill (Baishe Quanzhuan), or simply a narrow zealot.
The ending — redemption vs permanent imprisonmentBai Suzhen is imprisoned under the Leifeng Pagoda forever (or until her son's filial piety / Xiaoqing's fire frees her).Varying release mechanisms: the son's filial-piety rescue, Xiaoqing's Samadhi-fire toppling of the pagoda, or no release at all (eternal imprisonment).
Xiaoqing's nature and ultimate fateXiaoqing is a blue-green fish spirit (Feng Menglong).Xiaoqing is a green snake demon aged ~700-800 years (later plays and the modern canon).
Cross-cultural identity: Lamia vs Melusine vs uniquely ChineseThe White Snake is a Chinese instantiation of the universal 'snake-wife' tale type (ATU 411 'The King and the Lamia').She is a sui generis Chinese figure whose parallels (Lamia, Melusine) are typological coincidences, not genetic descendants.
