Who It Is

Bai Suzhen is a white snake who cultivated Taoist magic for a thousand years until she could take the form of a beautiful woman — and then did the one thing spirits are never supposed to do: she fell honestly, faithfully, domestically in love with a mortal. She married the gentle pharmacist Xu Xian, opened a medicine shop with him in Hangzhou, healed the poor for free, and carried his child. It is one of the only great legends where the monster's deception conceals nothing monstrous. The antagonist is a monk: Fahai of Golden Mountain Temple, who considers a spirit-human marriage an abomination regardless of its tenderness. He tricks Xu Xian into serving his wife realgar wine at the Dragon Boat Festival, forcing her true serpent form and frightening her husband to death; she storms heaven itself to steal the herb of immortality and revive him; Fahai finally imprisons her beneath Leifeng Pagoda. When the real Leifeng Pagoda collapsed in 1924, crowds dug through the rubble — and the writer Lu Xun published a famous essay cheering that the old jailer had finally fallen.

Origins

Evolved from Tang-Song snake-demon cautionary tales into a Ming dynasty romance, then flipped its moral entirely: the demon became the heroine and the exorcist the villain — a rare recorded case of a legend switching sides.

Attested in Legend of the White Snake (Ming dynasty opera and tales)Era Song dynasty setting; Ming codification

Form

A woman of luminous pale beauty in white robes, accompanied by her sworn sister Xiaoqing, the green snake; in her true form, a great white serpent.

Powers

A millennium of cultivated magic: shapeshifting, healing, weather-command — she flooded Golden Mountain Temple with a summoned sea — and the theft of the immortality herb from the Kunlun mountains.Shape-shifting between snake and human form Bai Suzhen's defining power: after ~1000-1800 years of Taoist cultivation she can transform between a great white snake and a beautiful young woman. In some versions she is granted human form by the 'essence of the Dragon King of the East China Sea' or by consuming an immortality pill; in the widespread opera version she becomes human after a millennium of disciplined Taoist practice on Mount Emei, then is accepted as a disciple by the goddess Lishan Laomu (or guided by Guan Yin/Guanyin in the 1992 TV version). Attested across all strata from the Tang to modern film; the specific mechanism (self-cultivation vs pill vs Dragon King blessing vs Guan Yin guidance) varies by version. Her transformation is undone by realgar wine (xionghuang), traditionally drunk on the Dragon Boat Festival (Duanwu); when she drinks it she reverts to her serpentine form. In several versions she is weakened ('her powers are limited') while pregnant with Xu Xian's child. The green companion Xiaoqing is less practiced and reverts more easily. The reason for transformation differs: self-cultivation (opera), swallowing the immortality tangyuan (pill) Lü Dongbin dropped into the lake (prequel), or being touched by the Dragon King's essence (folk-religion version). These are harmonized in the popular telling but are formally separate traditions.Weather/water magic — the flooding of Jinshan Temple In her confrontation with Fahai at Jinshan Temple (Zhenjiang), Bai Suzhen summons a storm and floods the temple with water in an attempt to rescue the imprisoned Xu Xian. The flood causes heavy collateral damage and drowns innocent people. Attested from the Ming version onward; some texts gloss it as 'the explanation for the frequent flooding of the Yellow River', while others locate it as a local Yangtze delta flood. While pregnant, her powers are diminished and she ultimately fails to free Xu Xian by force; she must flee to Hangzhou. The Buddhist guardian deities (Weituo, Qielan/Sangharama) summoned by Fahai overwhelm her, and she is subsequently imprisoned. The moral weight of the flood differs across versions — in early/Ming tellings it is a straightforward demonic act; in sympathetic retellings it is the desperate act of a wronged wife; in the 1993 Green Snake film the flood is a natural disaster she helps quell, and the temple-flood is reframed.Medical expertise and healing Bai Suzhen and Xu Xian operate a successful Chinese herbal-medicine shop (in Zhenjiang, after their Suzhou exile); her knowledge of natural remedies, acquired through centuries of Taoist mastery, makes the business thrive and earns the couple local respect. In the folk-worship layer, she and Xu Xian 'once practiced Chinese medicine' and believers come to them seeking healing and health advice. Attested from the Feng Menglong version onward; heavily foregrounded in modern sympathetic versions and in the White Snake Temple's benevolent-goddess framing. None documented; her healing is presented as genuinely beneficial and is central to her positive image. The medicine is a devotional asset (healing goddess) in one register but, in the early daemon layer, a cover for his enchantment — the credibility of 'Lady White's pharmacy' is a modern sympathetic addition.The stealing of the immortal herb (Lingzhi/ganoderma) When Xu Xian dies of shock at seeing her true form, Bai Suzhen forces her way to Mount Kunlun (in some versions directly to Heaven) to steal the magical lingzhi/ganoderma 'immortal herb' that restores him to life. She braves 'danger' — the herb is guarded by disciples of the Old Man of the South Pole / the immortals — and succeeds. Introduced by Fang Chengpei in 1771; now a fixed episode of the opera and most adaptations. The theft is immensely risky (she faces the guardians of the immortals), but her desperation and courage carry it through; the feat is the emotional climax of her devotion. The location (Mount Kunlun vs Mount Emei vs 'Heaven') and the guardian (Old Man of the South Pole's disciples vs unspecified immortals) differ across texts; the episode is absent from the earlier daemon versions.Flight and combat (with Xiaoqing) Bai Suzhen engages in supernatural combat with drawn weapons against Fahai's guardian deities, alongside Xiaoqing; in several versions the two sisters wield magic swords or a hairpin-wand. In the 2019/2021 animated films, Bai Suzhen crafts an illusory 'raft' crossing the West Lake with a hairpin and fights with a transformable hairpin wand. In the fight at Jinshan she also summons 'fierce snakes' (in The Sorcerer and the White Snake, 2011) to repel her enemies. Attested in the opera up to modern films; weapon details are adaptation-specific. She is defeated by Fahai and his guardian deities; in the 2021 Green Snake the hairpin is destroyed and her magic is fully neutralized when she is trapped. The specifics of her weapons (swords, hairpin) are modern film inventions not present in the classical opera; the classical text emphasizes water-magic and the summoned flood more than sword-fighting.Access to/forging of spiritual cultivation and apotheosis Bai Suzhen's entire arc is framed by Taoist-Buddhist cultivation: after a millennium of austerity she seeks to earn her immortality through good deeds; she is accepted by goddesses (Lishan Laomu) and eventually, in the folk-religion layer, achieves deification (Madam White Snake goddess) or becomes the mount of Yaotai Laomu as she continues to assist all living beings. Folk-religion and devotional versions; also the modern revisionist theme of saṃsāra/redemption. Her apotheosis requires selfless suffering (the centuries of imprisonment under the pagoda) — it is earned, not automatic; in the opera the Buddha realm (represented by Fahai) opposes her as an unregenerate demon until the son's filial piety moves Heaven. Whether she 'becomes a deity' at all is contested: in the popular opera she is merely released, while the devotional and celestially-driven versions deify her. The Buddhist establishment (Fahai) sees her as irredeemable; Taoist-coded sympathetic versions see her as a rightful immortal.

Weaknesses & Wards

Realgar wine, which forces her true form; the binding alms-bowl of Fahai; the pagoda that held her until her son's filial devotion freed her.

Accounts

Li Huang, nephew of the Salt and Iron Commissioner, meets a beautiful white-clad widow (aided by an old 'aunt' in blue-green), is seduced, spends days with her, and upon returning home dissolves into water, leaving only his head. Neighbors report nothing in the empty garden but a large white snake coiled beneath a Chinese honey locust tree. A variant (protagonist Li Guan, appended in the Taiping Guangji) ends with his head splitting open; his family finds a withered tree and small white snakes under its roots, which they kill. This is the oldest attested ancestor of the White Snake legend — the original malicious snake-daemon who eats (or dissolves) men.

Xi Xuanzan, rescued by the Daoist Xi Zhenren after being thrice entrapped by three shape-shifting demons at West Lake — a lady in white (a white snake), an old woman in black (an otter), and 'Maonu' (a black chicken) who saves him. Xi Zhenren seals them under three stone pagodas (the prototype of Leifeng). Here 'the three pagodas of West Lake' are stone markers, and the white snake still eats human hearts and livers, seducing Xuanzan into losing years of his vitality.

Xu Xuan, a young man in a herbal medicine shop in Lin'an, meets Bai Niangzi and her blue-green maid Qingqing (a fish spirit) on a boat in the rain at the Qingming festival; he lends them an umbrella. She claims widowhood, they become intimate, and she gives him 50 taels of silver (which turn out to be stolen treasury money — Xu is arrested and exiled to Suzhou). In Suzhou she reappears, they marry; he is later exiled again to Zhenjiang over jewelry she gave him. In Zhenjiang, a lecherous birthday host spies her in the bathroom and sees a giant white snake, frightening him to death. Xu visits Jinshan Temple and meets the Zen master Fahai, who recognizes the demon; when Bai arrives to reclaim Xu, she creates a storm/flood. Fahai drives them away. Eventually Fahai traps her with an alms bowl, reveals she is a white snake (Qingqing a blue fish), and imprisons them both under the Leifeng Pagoda. Xu becomes a monk and dies meditating.

A white snake and a blue-green snake on Mount Emei transform into Bai Suzhen and Xiaoqing, become sworn sisters, and travel to Hangzhou/West Lake, meeting young Xu Xian on a ferry in the rain; he lends his umbrella; they fall in love and marry, opening a medicine shop. Fahai, the abbot of Jinshan Temple, tells Xu his wife is a snake; when Xu, following Fahai's advice, makes Bai drink realgar wine on the Dragon Boat Festival, she reveals her serpent form and Xu dies of shock. Bai steals the ganoderma herb and revives him, but Xu remains fearful. He goes to Jinshan Temple where Fahai imprisons him; Bai and Xiaoqing fight Fahai, and Bai floods the temple (weakened by her pregnancy), failing to free him; Xiaoqing helps her escape. Xu escapes, realizes her love is genuine, and reunites with her at the Broken Bridge (where Xiaoqing nearly kills him in fury). Bai gives birth to their son Xu Mengjiao (later Xu Shilin). Fahai tracks them down, imprisons Bai under the Leifeng Pagoda despite Xu's pleas; Xiaoqing flees vowing vengeance. In the redemptive endings, Bai's son earns the top-scholar (zhuangyuan) title and his filial piety moves Heaven (or Xiaoqing masters fire-magic and topples the pagoda) to free her.

Cultural Significance

One of China's Four Great Folktales, endlessly staged in opera, film, and television. Her flooding of the temple and imprisonment under Leifeng Pagoda are among the most famous scenes in Chinese theater.

Scholarly Debates
Snake-daemon vs devoted snake-bride
The White Snake is originally a lethal, lust-driven she-daemon who hunts and destroys men (Tang 'Li Huang'; Song 'Three Pagodas'). ⇌ She is a wronged, selfless romantic heroine who endures millennia of imprisonment for love (Qing opera/tanci; modern canon).
The reason for her human transformation
Bai becomes human through self-cultivation (a millennium of Taoist discipline on Mount Emei). ⇌ She becomes human through external agency — the 'essence of the Dragon King of the East China Sea' or by swallowing the immortality pill Lü Dongbin dropped (which itself grants +500 years), or guided by Guan Yin.
Fahai's character and motivation
Fahai is a righteous, heroic demon-slayer; Xu Xuan even becomes his disciple (Feng Menglong, 1624). ⇌ Fahai is a heartless, fanatical, family-wrecking villain — the jealous reincarnation of the terrapin who missed the immortality pill (Baishe Quanzhuan), or simply a narrow zealot.
The ending — redemption vs permanent imprisonment
Bai Suzhen is imprisoned under the Leifeng Pagoda forever (or until her son's filial piety / Xiaoqing's fire frees her). ⇌ Varying release mechanisms: the son's filial-piety rescue, Xiaoqing's Samadhi-fire toppling of the pagoda, or no release at all (eternal imprisonment).
Xiaoqing's nature and ultimate fate
Xiaoqing is a blue-green fish spirit (Feng Menglong). ⇌ Xiaoqing is a green snake demon aged ~700-800 years (later plays and the modern canon).

A way to hold it in your mind

One of the most beloved love stories in the Chinese-speaking world begins with a man who dissolves into water, leaving only his head. The beautiful widow who wrecked him was, in truth, a great white snake coiled beneath a locust tree — and she had been eating men's hearts and livers for pleasure. That is the earliest documented ancestor of Bai Suzhen, the gentle white-snake bride whose name means 'Pure and Chaste', who today is worshipped in a live-snake temple in Taiwan and whose gravestone-like imprisonment tower in Hangzhou — collapsed in 1924, rebuilt in 2002 — draws pilgrims and tourists by the thousands. Over a thousand years, Chinese storytellers took the murderous lamia of the Tang dynasty and, retelling by retelling, opera by opera, turned her into the transcendent figure of forbidden love who would flood a monastery, brave the immortals to steal a life-restoring herb, and endure centuries beneath a pagoda for the sake of one human husband. She is not one figure. She is a whole history of China arguing with itself about love, women, demons, and grace.
