Klaubauf

Pronunciation KLOU-bowf (with 'ou' as in 'house' and 'bowf' rhyming with 'cow' + 'f'). In German: [ˈklaʊ̯baʊ̯f].

Etymology

The name 'Klaubauf' is of Bavarian-Austrian origin, likely derived from the Middle High German 'klūben' (to split, cleave, or pick) and 'auf' (up), possibly referring to the figure's habit of picking up or snatching naughty children. It may also relate to 'Klaube' (a claw or pincer), suggesting a grasping, claw-like nature. The name is closely related to 'Krampus', which comes from the Old High German 'krampōn' (to claw or grip).

Cultural Ownership

Austrian (specifically Tyrol and Carinthia), also present in Bavaria (Germany). The figure is part of the broader Alpine-Bavarian tradition of Saint Nicholas companions, sharing characteristics with Krampus and other Perchten figures.

Disputed Identifications

The Klaubauf is often considered synonymous with Krampus, but some regional traditions distinguish the two. In some areas, Klaubauf may refer to a specific type of Krampus or a separate figure with similar attributes. The relationship is not clearly defined in scholarship, and the terms are sometimes used interchangeably in popular culture.

Archive Dossier — Approach

In the frost-bitten villages of the Austrian Alps, as the first snows of December seal the mountain passes, a figure emerges from the shadows of folklore—part beast, part man, wrapped in fur and clanging with bells. This is the Klaubauf, the fearsome companion of Saint Nicholas, a guardian of winter morality who has haunted the imaginations of Tyrolean and Carinthian children for centuries. More than a mere bogeyman, the Klaubauf embodies the raw, untamed forces of nature and the stern discipline of a bygone era, a living relic of pre-Christian Alpine tradition that still parades through the streets each Krampusnacht.

The Klaubauf's head is a monstrous hand, and his fingers close around the arm of any child who has lied or stolen, stuffing them into the sack he carries on his back.

Who It Is

Der Klaubauf is a fearsome companion figure from Austrian Alpine folklore, particularly in Tyrol and Carinthia, who accompanies Saint Nicholas during the Krampuslauf (Krampus run) and also appears during the Perchtenlauf. The Klaubauf is distinct from the more widely known Krampus in several key ways: whereas Krampus is a shaggy, goat-like demon, the Klaubauf is depicted as a tall, muscular humanoid with a hand-shaped head or, in some traditions, a single enormous claw or hand — the name 'Klaubauf' literally means 'grasp up' or 'pinch-open.' His most terrifying feature is that his entire head is a giant hand or his mouth is replaced by what looks like a grasping claw, used to snatch up naughty children. He wears heavy chains that he drags through the streets, creating a deafening clatter, and carries a large bag or basket for stuffing children into. In Tyrolean tradition, the Klaubauf mask (Larve) is carved from wood with exaggerated features: bulging eyes, a gaping mouth, and often a single horn. The Klaubauf tradition is part of the broader Perchten culture — pre-Christian Alpine winter spirits that were assimilated into the Christmas season. Unlike Krampus, who specifically appears on December 5th (Krampusnacht), Klaubaufs appear throughout the Advent season and into Epiphany. The Klaubauf represents the punitive aspect of winter, enforcing moral behavior through ritualized terror. Today, Klaubauf runs (Klaubaufläufe) are held in Tyrolean villages, where young men in elaborately carved masks and heavy fur costumes parade through the streets.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

The Klaubauf is typically depicted as a dark, shaggy, demonic figure, often covered in fur or straw, with a carved wooden mask bearing fangs, horns, and a long tongue. He may have cloven hooves or bare feet, and he often carries a bundle of birch switches (Ruten) and a sack. His face is often blackened with soot, and he may have unruly hair. In some portrayals, he resembles a rustic, darker version of Saint Nicholas, wearing a similar robe but in dark colors, or he may appear as a wild beast-like creature.

Symbols & Iconography

The Klaubauf's symbols include the birch rod or switch (Rute), the sack for carrying naughty children, chains or bells (cowbells) to announce his approach, and a mask with horns and a long tongue. His fur and soot-blackened appearance connect him to the wild, untamed natural world and to the soot of the chimney, linking him to domestic hearth and the underworld.

Behaviour & Personality

The Klaubauf is a threatening, punitive figure who accompanies Saint Nicholas on his rounds on December 5th (Klaubaufnacht) and December 6th. He is boisterous, intimidating, and aggressive, chasing and frightening children and adults alike. He may demand that children recite prayers or perform tricks, and he rewards good behavior with treats (often given by Nicholas) and punishes bad behavior with switches, coal, or abduction in his sack. In some traditions, he acts independently of Nicholas, roaming the streets and entering homes to enforce discipline.

Domains & Habitats

The Klaubauf inhabits the Alpine regions of Tyrol and Carinthia in Austria, as well as parts of Bavaria. He appears primarily during the Advent season, especially on the eve of Saint Nicholas Day (December 5th) and on the day itself (December 6th). He is also associated with the Perchtenlaufen traditions, where he appears during the Twelve Nights of Christmas (Rauhnächte).

Version Differences

Regional variations exist: In some areas, the Klaubauf is a single figure, while in others, there are groups of Klaubäufe (plural). In the Tyrol, the Klaubauf is often called 'Tuifl' (Devil). In Carinthia, he may be known as 'Klaubauf' or 'Krampus'. The appearance can vary from a shaggy, straw-covered 'wild man' to a more demonic, horned figure. Some versions carry chains, others only switches. The behavior also varies: some are silent, others roar and clang bells. The relationship with Saint Nicholas also varies; in some traditions, Nicholas and Klaubauf appear together, in others, Klaubauf appears alone.

Archive Dossier — Powers

Supernatural Strength

Source Version Common to all versions

Description

The Klaubauf is believed to possess great physical strength, allowing him to carry heavy sacks of naughty children and to physically discipline them.

Limitations

Not explicitly stated, but his power is typically directed at children who misbehave.

Contradictions

None noted.

Intimidation and Fear

Source Version Common to all versions

Description

His terrifying appearance, loud bells, and aggressive behavior are designed to instill fear and obedience in children.

Limitations

His power is psychological; it relies on the belief and fear of the community.

Contradictions

None noted.

Chimney Access

Source Version Attested in some traditions

Description

Like other Nicholas companions, the Klaubauf is said to enter homes through chimneys, which explains his soot-blackened appearance.

Limitations

Not universally mentioned; some versions have him entering through doors.

Contradictions

Some accounts do not mention chimney entry.

Abduction

Source Version Attested in some versions (similar to Knecht Ruprecht traditions)

Description

In some traditions, the Klaubauf carries naughty children away in his sack, either to his lair in the forest or to a river, where they are never seen again.

Limitations

This is a threat used to frighten children; actual abductions are not recorded.

Contradictions

Not all versions include abduction; some only use physical punishment.

Archive Dossier — Relationships

Saint Nicholas

Type Companion / ServantSource Companions of Saint Nicholas tradition

Description

The Klaubauf acts as a foil to Saint Nicholas, who is the benevolent gift-giver. While Nicholas rewards good children, the Klaubauf punishes the naughty ones. This pairing embodies the dual nature of moral judgment.

Version Note

In some regions, they appear together; in others, the Klaubauf appears independently.

Conflicting Accounts

Some accounts suggest the Klaubauf is a separate figure, not directly associated with Nicholas.

Krampus

Type Related figure / Possible synonymSource Folklore scholarship

Description

The Klaubauf is closely related to Krampus, and in many contexts, the terms are used interchangeably. Both are demonic companions of Saint Nicholas with similar appearances and roles.

Version Note

In Austria, Klaubauf is the regional name, while Krampus is more widely used in Bavaria and other areas.

Conflicting Accounts

Some scholars treat them as distinct, with Klaubauf being a more localized variant.

Perchta

Type Related figureSource Folklore studies

Description

The Klaubauf is associated with Perchta, a goddess or spirit of the Twelve Nights. Both are part of the Perchten traditions and share a wild, punitive nature.

Version Note

In some processions, Perchta leads the Perchten, and the Klaubauf is among her followers.

Conflicting Accounts

The exact relationship is not clearly defined; they may be seen as separate entities within the same tradition.
