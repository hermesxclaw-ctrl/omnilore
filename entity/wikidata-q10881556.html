Who It Is

Before Japan had forests, it had a god who carried them in his hands. Isotakeru no Mikoto — the son of the storm god Susanoo — did not slay a dragon or rule the heavens. His myth is quieter and more fundamental: he sailed from Korea with handfuls of tree seeds and sowed the entire Japanese archipelago, turning a bare land into a country of cedar, camphor, and pine.

Characterized as dutiful, industrious, and nurturing — a contrast to his father Susanoo's violent, impetuous nature. In the chronicles he obeys his father, carries and sows seeds without resistance, and cooperates with his sisters. He is not a trickster, warrior, or judge; his role is civilizing/cultivating: transforming wilderness into habitable, forested land. No speeches are recorded for him directly; he acts rather than speaks. Later folk interpretation emphasizes diligence, stewardship, and filial piety.

Powers
Afforestation and Tree Propagation

Core attested power: to carry, transport, and sow tree seeds that germinate into forests covering Japan. Nihon Shoki states he and his sisters 'took the seeds of trees and planted them' throughout the land, making Japan forested and habitable.

Limit: Requires physical seeds and suitable land; not creation ex nihilo but cultivation. Needs cooperation of sisters who carry different species. No attested ability to grow trees instantaneously by magic; process described as planting/sowing.

Patronage of Timber and Woodcraft

Derived authority over timber suitable for building and shipbuilding. Tradition holds that the trees he planted provided wood for palaces, shrines, and boats. Venerated by carpenters, shipwrights, and woodworkers.

Limit: Patronage, not direct control; invoked for success and safety, not commanding wood. Modern extension to forestry management is interpretive.

Land Cultivation and Territorial Claiming

By planting forests, he makes land productive and claims it for Japanese habitation. In some variants Susanoo grants him the land of Kii as reward, establishing territorial kami status.

Limit: Authority limited to forested/wild lands, not paddies or sea. Subordinate to Susanoo and later to Yamato court kami.

Divine Endurance and Exile Survival

Survives expulsion from heaven and sojourn in Silla (Korea) alongside Susanoo, enduring exile and sea crossing with seeds intact.

Limit: No combat feats; endurance is filial and migratory, not martial.

Accounts I
Birth as Son of Susanoo in TakamagaharaKojiki (712) Kamitsumaki; Nihon Shoki (720) Jindai

Asuka-Nara period codification of older oral tradition; mythic time: Age of the Gods

Isotakeru is born as the son of Susanoo no Mikoto, the storm kami. His mother is not named in the early chronicles; later commentary speculates but no attested mother. He has two younger sisters, Oyatsuhime (Great House Princess) and Tsumatsuhime (Consecrated Princess). He lives in Takamagahara until Susanoo's violent rampage against Amaterasu leads to Susanoo's expulsion by the heavenly council. Establishes Isotakeru's heavenly lineage but also his status as child of an exiled god, explaining his later association with earthly rather than heavenly realms. Sets up exile narrative.

Exile and Descent to Silla (Kara)Nihon Shoki Jindai, main text and variant 1

Nara period text; mythic time: Age of the Gods, immediately after Susanoo's expulsion

After being expelled from Takamagahara, Susanoo descends, taking his three children with him. According to the Nihon Shoki, they descend not directly to Japan but to the land of Silla (Shiragi) or Kara (Korea). There, Susanoo declares the land undesirable and decides to return. Isotakeru remains dutiful throughout the exile, carrying the tree seeds that will later be used to forest Japan. Explains the Korean episode that provides the seeds. This passage has become the most politically sensitive part of the myth, later used in imperial historiography to assert ancient Japanese presence in Korea. Scholars debate whether Silla reflects genuine memory of migration or a literary device.

The Carrying of Tree Seeds from KaraNihon Shoki Jindai, variant accounts

Nara period; mythic time: Age of the Gods

While in Kara/Silla, Susanoo and Isotakeru gather the seeds of every kind of tree. Nihon Shoki states they 'took down the seeds of trees' (or 'pulled up trees by the roots' in one variant) to bring to Japan. Isotakeru is specifically described as bearing or carrying these seeds, often with his sisters each carrying different species. The seeds are the mythic germplasm of Japan's forests. Provides etiology for Japan's diverse forests. Establishes Isotakeru as culture hero who imports botanical wealth. The act transforms him from mere exile's son to bringer of civilization.

Accounts II
Sowing the Forests of JapanNihon Shoki Jindai, variant 2 and 3; Kojiki allusion

Nara period; mythic time: Age of the Gods, after return from Kara

Returning from Kara, Susanoo and his children sail to Japan and systematically sow the seeds across the archipelago. They plant sugi and kusu in Tsukushi, various trees in Kii, and distribute species according to land suitability. Isotakeru and his sisters divide the labor: tradition at Itakiso holds that Isotakeru carried sugi, Oyatsuhime carried kusu, etc. After sowing, Japan becomes 'blue with mountains' and habitable, with timber for palaces and ships. Susanoo then declares the work complete. Etiological myth explaining Japan's forested landscape and the origin of usable timber. Provides divine charter for forestry and justifies human use of forests as continuation of kami's work. The sowing list is sometimes read as ancient botanical knowledge.

Enshrinement at Kii and Foundation of Itakiso JinjaItakiso Jinja engi (shrine origin legend); Engishiki Jinmyocho (927); Nihon Shoki variant where Susanoo grants Kii to Itakeru

Heian period codification (Engishiki) reflecting earlier local tradition; mythic time: after afforestation

After completing the planting, Isotakeru settles in Kii Province (modern Wakayama). One Nihon Shoki variant has Susanoo say, 'I will give the land of Kii to Itakeru.' He dwells at Itakiso, where he is enshrined together with his sisters. The site becomes Itakiso Jinja, the Ichinomiya of Kii, listed as a Myojin Taisha in the Engishiki. He remains there as the permanent guardian kami of wood, receiving offerings from the Yamato court and later from forestry guilds. Transforms wandering planter kami into localized ujigami with permanent cult site. Explains why Kii, a heavily forested peninsula, is his cult center. Establishes continuous worship from at least 10th century to present.

Association with Shipbuilding and the First BoatsMedieval shrine tradition and Norinaga commentary on Nihon Shoki; extended folklore

Medieval to Edo period elaboration on Nara myth

Because the trees Isotakeru planted provided timber for boats, later tradition credits him as patron of shipbuilding. Some medieval engi claim the first boats in Japan were built from trees he sowed, and that Susanoo's own ship from Kara was made possible by his forestry. Fishermen and shipwrights in Kii and along the Seto Inland Sea invoked Isotakeru for safe timber and safe voyages. Expands domain from forestry to maritime craft, linking forest and sea economies. Explains votive offerings of miniature boats at Itakiso Jinja.

Isotakeru is the same as Kukunochi, the Shinto tree god. — They are distinct kami in Kojiki genealogy. Kukunochi no Kami is the ancestor of trees themselves, born to Izanagi and Izanami early in creation. Isotakeru is a later generation, son of Susanoo, whose role is to plant and distribute already-existing tree seeds. Shrines and Engishiki keep them separate.

Isotakeru is a major, widely-known Japanese god like Amaterasu or Susanoo. — He is a secondary but functionally important kami. He appears only briefly in the chronicles and is primarily venerated in Kii and among forestry communities, not nationally as a major cult like Hachiman or Inari. His fame is regional and occupational.

Isotakeru invented trees or created them by magic. — He did not create trees; he transported and sowed seeds. The myth emphasizes cultivation and distribution, not creation ex nihilo. The trees' origin is either heaven or Korea, not his own body or magic.

The Silla/Kara episode proves ancient Japanese rule over Korea. — The passage is myth, not historiography. It describes a divine exile, not a military conquest, and exists in variant forms (some without Korea at all). Post-war scholarship rejects its use as historical evidence for Japanese dominion over Mimana/Imna.

Isotakeru appears as a major playable character in video games like Okami or Shin Megami Tensei. — As of 2026, no major commercial game features Isotakeru as a named, playable character. He appears in databases like Megami Tensei Wiki as a demon/persona entry in some titles (derived from encyclopedic lists), but not as a prominent figure. His sisters are even less represented. Confusion arises from Susanoo and Okuninushi who do appear prominently.
