Who It Is

On 25 December 1991, a man who had tried to save Soviet socialism by making it honest watched the red flag come down over the Kremlin and went home without an empire. Mikhail Gorbachev did not intend to dissolve the Soviet Union — he intended to make it work — and that paradox is why he is remembered simultaneously as a Nobel laureate who ended the Cold War and, in his own country, as the leader who lost it.

Attested by colleagues (Chernyaev, Yakovlev, Matlock, Thatcher) as exceptionally hardworking, loquacious, didactic, and intellectually curious — unusual for Brezhnev-era apparatchiks. Memoirists describe marathon Politburo debates, appetite for policy papers, and tendency to lecture interlocutors. Charismatic with foreign leaders and crowds, but often perceived as indecisive and overly legalistic by both hardliners and radicals. Powers
General Secretary of the CPSU - Party Control

As General Secretary (11 March 1985 - 24 August 1991), held supreme authority over Communist Party appointments (nomenklatura), ideology, and policy agenda. Could place and remove regional secretaries, Politburo members, and editors. Used this to replace Brezhnev-era cadres with reformers (Yakovlev, Shevardnadze, Ryzhkov).

Limit: Constrained by Politburo collective, Central Committee conservatism, and need for consensus. Could not rule by decree alone until creation of Presidency in 1990. Lost control as glasnost empowered criticism of Party itself.

Perestroika (Restructuring) - Economic Decentralization

Program to decentralize economic decision-making, introduce limited market mechanisms (Law on State Enterprise 1987, Law on Cooperatives 1988), and allow limited private initiative while retaining socialist ownership. Sought efficiency without capitalism.

Limit: Neither plan nor market; created shortages, fiscal crisis, and 'spontaneous privatization' by managers. No price liberalization until late, causing suppressed inflation. Failed to improve living standards; GDP declined 1990-1991.

Glasnost and Demokratizatsiya - Informational and Political Opening

Glasnost ('openness') permitted unprecedented freedom of speech, press, and historical inquiry; release of political prisoners (Sakharov 1986), open coverage of Chernobyl, Stalinist crimes, and contemporary problems. Demokratizatsiya led to contested elections for Congress of People's Deputies (1989) and republic presidencies.

Limit: Did not initially intend full liberal democracy; retained censorship limits until 1990. Empowered nationalist mobilization that escaped control. Media freedom remained dependent on Party tolerance, not law, until 1990 Press Law.

Novoe Myshlenie (New Thinking) - Foreign Policy and Nuclear Restraint

Doctrine that universal human values and mutual security supersede class struggle; enabled INF Treaty (1987) eliminating intermediate-range missiles, START negotiations, withdrawal from Afghanistan (1988-1989), and non-intervention in Eastern Europe (Sinatra Doctrine). Direct personal diplomacy with Reagan, Bush, Thatcher, Kohl.

Limit: Dependent on Western reciprocity; could not prevent NATO's continued existence or secure binding non-expansion pledges. Military-industrial complex and KGB resisted. Domestic critics saw as capitulation.

Presidency of the USSR - Executive State Power

Created March 1990 as first and only President of the Soviet Union, with veto, decree, and appointment powers, plus control of armed forces and foreign policy. Intended to provide executive authority independent of Party.

Limit: Elected by Congress, not popular vote, weakening legitimacy vs. Yeltsin's popularly elected Russian presidency (June 1991). Increasingly powerless as republics declared sovereignty and ignored Moscow decrees. No control over economy after 1990.

Accounts I
Combine Harvester and Order of the Red Banner (1946-1949)Gorbachev Memoirs (1996) pp. 23-34; Taubman, Gorbachev (2017) pp. 23-31; Soviet award records

1946-1949 (late Stalin era)

As a teenager, Gorbachev worked five consecutive summers with his father Sergey operating a combine harvester on the collective farm. In 1948 they harvested over 800 tons of grain, earning Sergey the Order of Lenin and 17-year-old Mikhail the Order of the Red Banner of Labour in 1949 — an exceptionally rare honor for a schoolboy. The award and worker-peasant origin helped secure his admission to Moscow State University law faculty without entrance exam in 1950 and his early candidate membership in the Party. Established proletarian credentials, enabled Moscow education, and instilled lifelong belief in agricultural reform and the dignity of labor, later reflected in his early focus on agricultural policy in Stavropol.

The Stavropol Years and the Great Stavropol Canal (1970-1978)Brown, The Gorbachev Factor (1996) ch.2; Gorbachev Memoirs ch.3; Stavropol Krai archives

1970-1978

Appointed First Secretary of Stavropol Regional Committee in 1970 at age 39, Gorbachev oversaw the construction of the Great Stavropol Canal, a 480-km irrigation system bringing Kuban water to arid steppe, and promoted agricultural modernization. He hosted visiting leaders including Yuri Andropov (who had a dacha in the region) and cultivated a reputation as an efficient, incorruptible, and relatively open provincial boss. His handling of the 1972 drought and willingness to discuss problems candidly impressed Andropov and Brezhnev's circle. Built patronage network and national visibility; Andropov's sponsorship led to his transfer to Moscow as Central Committee Secretary for Agriculture in 1978, placing him on succession path.

Election as General Secretary and the Anti-Alcohol Campaign (1985-1986)Politburo minutes 11 March 1985 (Chernyaev notes); Gorbachev, Perestroika (1987); Taubman pp. 147-168

March 1985 - 1986

After deaths of Brezhnev (1982), Andropov (1984), and Chernenko (1985), the Politburo elected Gorbachev General Secretary on 11 March 1985, with crucial support from Gromyko who nominated him. Initially committed to uskorenie (acceleration) and discipline, his first major domestic campaign was the anti-alcohol campaign (May 1985), sharply restricting vodka sales, raising prices, and destroying vineyards, to combat widespread alcoholism and low productivity. Anti-alcohol campaign reduced consumption initially but caused sugar shortages (moonshining), loss of state revenue, and popular resentment, and is widely judged a failure that worsened fiscal crisis. It demonstrated Gorbachev's willingness to use coercive social engineering before turning to glasnost.

Accounts II
Glasnost, Chernobyl, and the Congress of People's Deputies (1986-1989)Gorbachev Memoirs ch. 8-12; Plokhy, Chernobyl History; Congress transcripts 1989

1986-1989

After initial hesitation over Chernobyl (26 April 1986) — delayed public disclosure for 18 days — Gorbachev concluded secrecy was fatal and accelerated glasnost. He released Andrei Sakharov from exile (Dec 1986), allowed publication of previously banned works (e.g., Children of the Arbat), and permitted televised, contested elections for the new Congress of People's Deputies in March 1989. The Congress, broadcast live, featured fierce criticism of the Party, KGB, and Gorbachev himself (notably Sakharov and Yeltsin), shocking the population. Created genuine public sphere and rehabilitated history, but also unleashed nationalist and secessionist movements in Baltics, Caucasus, and Ukraine, and destroyed Party's ideological monopoly. Gorbachev's popularity peaked abroad while declining domestically as shortages worsened.

Reykjavik, the INF Treaty, and Letting Eastern Europe Go (1986-1989)Matlock, Reagan and Gorbachev (2004); FitzGerald, Way Out There in the Blue; INF Treaty text; Malta Summit records

1986-1989

At Reykjavik (Oct 1986) Gorbachev and Reagan came close to agreeing to eliminate all nuclear weapons, failing only over SDI. Building trust, they signed the INF Treaty in Washington (8 Dec 1987) eliminating all intermediate-range missiles — first treaty to reduce nuclear arsenals. Gorbachev then refused to use force as Poland (Solidarity), Hungary, East Germany, Czechoslovakia, Bulgaria and Romania abandoned communism in 1989, famously telling East German leader Honecker that 'life punishes those who come too late.' He accepted the fall of the Berlin Wall (9 Nov 1989) without intervention and negotiated German reunification within NATO (1990) in exchange for economic aid and verbal assurances. Ended Cold War division of Europe, enabled German reunification, earned Gorbachev Nobel Peace Prize (1990) and global acclaim. Domestically, hardliners viewed non-intervention as betrayal and strategic defeat, fueling coup plotting. NATO expansion controversy originates in these negotiations.

The August Coup and the Dissolution of the USSR (19-25 December 1991)Gorbachev Memoirs ch. 18-19; Yeltsin, Against the Grain; State Emergency Committee decrees; Belavezha Accords text

19 August 1991 - 25 December 1991

Hardliners (Vice President Yanayev, KGB chief Kryuchkov, Defense Minister Yazov) formed the State Committee on the State of Emergency (GKChP) and placed Gorbachev under house arrest at Foros, Crimea, on 19 August 1991, demanding reversal of reforms and preservation of Union. The coup collapsed in three days due to Yeltsin's resistance atop a tank outside the White House, military hesitation, and popular mobilization. Gorbachev returned to Moscow but was politically eclipsed by Yeltsin, who banned the CPSU. After Ukraine's independence referendum (1 Dec 1991, 92% for independence), Yeltsin, Kravchuk (Ukraine) and Shushkevich (Belarus) signed the Belavezha Accords (8 Dec) dissolving the USSR. Gorbachev resigned as President on 25 December 1991 in a televised address; the Soviet flag was lowered. End of the Soviet Union as a state; creation of 15 independent republics; Gorbachev left without a country to preside over, becoming a private citizen and head of the Gorbachev Foundation. Defined his historical legacy as reformer who destroyed what he sought to save.

NATO Expansion Promise (1990)Western leaders (Baker, Genscher, Kohl) verbally promised Gorbachev NATO would not expand 'one inch eastward' beyond Germany in exchange for German reunification, then betrayed promise.No legally binding promise was ever made; discussions concerned only East German territory in 1990, before Warsaw Pact collapse, and no treaty limits NATO enlargement.
Why did perestroika fail economically?Failure due to Gorbachev's half-measures and refusal to embrace market prices and private property; 'neither plan nor market' created worst of both.Failure due to structural impossibility: Soviet economy was unreformable without collapse; any reform would have caused crisis, and hardliner sabotage plus oil price collapse made success impossible.

Gorbachev 'tore down the Berlin Wall' or ordered it opened. — The Wall's opening on 9 Nov 1989 resulted from a botched East German press conference (Günter Schabowski) and mass pressure; Gorbachev's crucial decision was not to intervene militarily to save the East German regime, as his predecessors had in Hungary 1956 and Prague 1968. He did not order the Wall opened and was surprised by timing.

Gorbachev was universally loved in Russia as a democratic hero. — In Western polls he is admired; in Russian polls (Levada, VTsIOM) he has consistently ranked among least popular 20th-century leaders, blamed for economic collapse, loss of superpower status, and chaos of 1990s. Only after death did some reassessment occur, but still divisive.

Perestroika and glasnost were Western-style capitalism and free speech from the start. — Initially perestroika meant accelerating socialism via discipline and technology (uskorenie), and glasnost meant criticism to improve socialism, not liberal democracy. Full freedom of press and contested elections only emerged 1988-1990, and private property was not legalized until late 1990. Gorbachev remained Marxist-Leninist until at least 1989.

Gorbachev won the Cold War for the West / Reagan forced him to surrender. — Gorbachev's New Thinking held that Cold War was unwinnable and immoral; he sought mutual security, not surrender. Scholars stress his agency: he chose to end arms race and reduce ideology, not because of bankruptcy alone (USSR could have muddled on). Both superpowers de-escalated.

His birthmark was photoshopped or symbolized disease. — The nevus flammeus is a benign capillary malformation present from birth, well-documented in childhood photos. Early Soviet photo retouchers sometimes softened it, but it was never hidden in person. No health significance.
