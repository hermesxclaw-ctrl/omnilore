Who It Is

A thatched roof is not a hat pulled over a house but a living skin — tens of thousands of straws or reeds combed, bundled and pinned with hazel spars so tightly that rain never penetrates, air is trapped like a feather duvet, and the ridge wears a patterned cap that tells you, at a glance, which valley the thatcher learned his craft in.

As a material system, thatch behaves as a thatched mass: rain is shed by gravity and surface tension along overlapping stems, not by waterproof membrane; 90% of water runs off the outer 50 mm. Bulk stays dry and still-air trapped between densely packed stems provides insulation (U-value ~0.35 W/m2K for 300 mm). It breathes, buffering humidity. It is lightweight (approx. 35 kg/m2 for reed, less than many tiles) but vulnerable to wind uplift if fixings fail, to fire if ignited at ridge or eaves, and to biodegradation — surface composts at ~20-30 mm per decade, requiring periodic re-ridging. Powers
Water shedding by mass and overlap

Overlapping courses 300-350 mm thick shed water by gravity and surface tension along stems. No membrane needed; bulk remains dry. Effective to wind-driven rain when pitch >45 degrees and fixings correctly covered.

Limit: Fails if pitch <35 degrees, if fixings (sways/spars) become exposed, if moss dams water, or if thatch is compacted. Horizontal flashings and valleys are weak points.

Thermal insulation and breathability

Dense packed air between stems gives thermal resistance R ~2.5-3.0 for 300 mm, plus hygroscopic buffering. Keeps interiors cool in tropics and warm in winter in temperate zones. Acoustic damping also significant.

Limit: Insulation value drops if thatch becomes saturated or excessively thinned by decay. Does not meet modern U-value standards alone without additional insulation in some building codes.

Lightweight structural load

Thatch at ~35-45 kg/m2 is lighter than clay tiles (~45-60 kg/m2) or slate, allowing lighter rafter structures and steep pitches without heavy trusses.

Limit: Weight increases when wet; snow load on thick thatch can be significant. Requires strong fixing to resist wind uplift.

Biodegradability and spar-coat accumulation

Surface weathers to compost, allowing new coat to be sparred over old — creating archaeological stratigraphy up to 2.1 m thick preserving medieval straw types (wheat, rye, maslin).

Limit: Water reed tradition is one-coat, stripped not coated, so no accumulation. Excessive build-up can overload structure and trap moisture if not managed.

Fire vulnerability and fire resistance paradox

Dry thatch is combustible and can burn rapidly if ignited at eaves/ridge. However dense, damp thatch is difficult to ignite and burns slowly. Traditional lime parging, fire boards and modern fire-retardant barriers and spark arrestors mitigate risk.

Limit: Thatched roofs have higher insurance premiums; chimneys must be lined and heightened, barbecues and fireworks prohibited nearby. Thatched properties suffer disproportionate fire loss when fire occurs.

Form & Signs

Appearance — A finished thatched roof is a deep, textured mass 30-40 cm thick (often much thicker where spar-coating has accumulated to >2 m over centuries). Long straw shows a shaggy, soft outline with visible butts and a slightly uneven surface; combed wheat reed is neater, with straight combed straw and a crisp eave line; water reed is the most sharply cut, with a tight, brushed surface and a blocky, geometric ridge. Colour weathers from golden straw-yello

Symbols — In Britain, thatch iconography signals rustic continuity, heritage and, since the late 20th century, affluence and conservation. In art, thatched cottages are shorthand for the pastoral — e.g., John Constable, Thomas Hardy. In Bali, black ijuk (Arenga pinnata fibre) thatch on meru towers signals sacred status; only temples and palaces use it, graded by roof tiers. Accounts I
The deep time of thatch: from Palaeolithic shelters to Neolithic strawArchaeological inference + archaeobotany of later roofs; Paul Robinson & Letts syntheses

Late Palaeolithic (c. 12,000 BP) inferred to Early Neolithic (c. 4000 BCE) attested by cereal cultivation

No direct Palaeolithic thatch survives, but wild vegetation — water reed, bulrush, broom, heather, rushes — was almost certainly used to cover shelters, as it was the only available roofing. Definitive shift occurs with Neolithic cereal farming: straw becomes available in quantity. Although no Neolithic straw thatch survives in Europe, the logic of material culture and later medieval SBT evidence showing wheat/rye/maslin dominance indicates straw thatching began with farming and continued unbroken to the medieval period. Established thatch as the default roofing for millennia before tile or slate; created a continuous tradition whose medieval base coats still preserve Neolithic-descended crop varieties.

The medieval English roof that never died: smoke-blackened thatchLetts, J. Smoke Blackened Thatch (1999); Historic England surveys; dendrochronology of base coats

Medieval (c. 1300-1500) to present (roofs still carrying medieval base coats)

In open-hall houses without chimneys, smoke from the central hearth permeated the roof space, sterilizing and blackening the underside of the thatch. When chimneys were inserted (15th-16th century), these smoke-blackened base coats were left in place and new straw sparred over them. Over 250 roofs in southern England retain base coats over 500 years old, preserving medieval wheat varieties that grew to 1.8 m tall on poor soils, providing both grain and durable straw. These roofs are accidental archives of medieval agriculture. Provides direct evidence of medieval crop genetics, farming practice and thatching technique; underpins conservation policy to retain SBT rather than strip.

Hale — the Hawaiian house that breathesEllis, Polynesian Researches (1829); Malo, Hawaiian Antiquities (1898); Bishop Museum ethnography

Pre-contact to 19th century; revival from 1970s

The hale is a timber frame lashed with olona or coconut sinnet, roofed and walled with pili grass (Heteropogon contortus), ti leaves (Cordyline fruticosa) or lauhala (Pandanus tectorius). Thatch panels are sewn and tied, not sparred, allowing ventilation in tropical heat. Different hale types (hale mua, hale noa, hale wa'a) use different thatch densities. After near disappearance under colonial timber and tin, master builders have revived hale construction on cultural grounds. Hale remains a potent cultural symbol; modern hale at Pu'uhonua o Honaunau and Polynesian Cultural Center demonstrate continuity.

Accounts II
Na Bure and the Fijian fan-palm roofFiji Museum; Roth, Fiji's Native Architecture; ethnographic fieldwork in Na Bure village

Pre-colonial to present

In Na Bure and across Fiji, the bure (house) combines steep fan-palm leaf roofs with layered reed walls. Palm leaves are folded and sewn over reeds, creating a feathered thatch that sheds heavy tropical rain. Walls use layered reeds bound with magimagi (coconut fibre sinnet). The high pitch and deep eaves create cool, dark interiors. Colonial administrators often dismissed bure as impermanent, but the technique is engineered for cyclone resistance — the thatch can be rapidly repaired after storms from local materials. Bure remains national architectural icon (seen on Fijian currency and resorts), though many rural bure have been replaced by corrugated iron.

Attap, ijuk and alang-alang — Southeast Asian palm and grass thatchWaterson, The Living House (1990); Indonesian Ministry of Education and Culture documentation of Balinese temple architecture

Ancient to present

In lowland Southeast Asia, nipa palm (Nypa fruticans) leaves are sewn into panels called attap, used for both roofs and walls of dwellings on stilts. In Bali, the black, hair-like fibres of the sugar palm (Arenga pinnata), called ijuk or duk, thatch the tiered meru towers of temples — the only material considered pure enough for the gods, graded by number of tiers (3,5,7,9,11). In Bali and Hawaii, alang-alang grass thatch covers both houses and shrines. Ijuk is exceptionally durable (up to 50 years) and fire-resistant compared to straw. Attap dwellings defined vernacular settlement across archipelago; ijuk remains mandatory for sacred architecture, sustaining Arenga palm cultivation.

Maya, Aztec and Inca thatch and its rediscoveryWauchope, Modern Maya Houses (1938); Coe, The Maya; Inca Garcilaso de la Vega; recent lidar and experimental archaeology (2010s)

Pre-Columbian (Classic/Postclassic) to present in rural Yucatan

Maya, Aztec and Inca commoners lived predominantly in thatched buildings: wattle-and-daub walls with steep palm or grass thatch. Spanish chroniclers described them but focused on stone temples, so fiber architecture was under-recorded. In Yucatan today, traditional Maya houses (na) still use palm thatch (xa'an) with a distinctive steep, rounded ridge. Until lidar and re-examination of posthole patterns in the 2000s, archaeologists underestimated the complexity and scale of thatched urban housing around stone centres. Revised population estimates for Mesoamerican cities upward; living Maya thatch tradition recognized as continuity, not reconstruction.

The rise, fall and gentrification of English thatchCox & Thorp, Thatch Thatching in England 1790-1940; Moir & Letts surveys; 2013 Historic England thatch census

Late 18th century to present

Until the late 1800s thatch was the only roofing available to most rural English people. Welsh slate distribution from 1820 and railways brought alternatives. Thatched properties peaked mid-1800s with agricultural expansion, then declined sharply with late-Victorian agricultural depression and rural depopulation — thatch became a mark of poverty. Numbers fell and thatchers dwindled. Since the 1990s thatch has inverted to a symbol of wealth and ecological virtue: approx. 60,000 thatched properties remain in UK (2013 estimate), served by ~1,000 full-time thatchers, with premium prices, strict listing protection and renewed interest in sustainable materials. Thatch now protected heritage; skills shortage and material supply (80% water reed imported) are critical issues; plant variety legislation blocks heritage wheat seed trade.

Thatch is highly flammable and any spark will set it ablaze like straw kindling. — Dense, well-laid thatch is difficult to ignite; it requires sustained flame at the surface. Most thatched fires start at chimneys, eaves or ridge from faulty flues or discarded cigarettes/fireworks, not spontaneous ignition. Fire barriers and lined chimneys reduce risk dramatically.

Thatch is full of vermin and insects by nature. — Well-maintained thatch with wire netting, proper eaves closure and regular re-ridging largely excludes vermin. Historical vermin problems reflected poverty-related neglect, open eaves and grain storage in roof spaces, not inherent material.

Thatch is cheap, primitive roofing for the poor. — Historically true in England until c. 1880, but today thatch is among the most expensive roofing in the UK (re-thatch £15k-£40k+, re-ridge £3k-£8k) and a symbol of wealth, heritage premium and ecological choice. In tropics, thatch remains low-cost local material but is being displaced by corrugated iron as status symbol.

Thatch lasts only 10-15 years and needs constant replacement. — The ridge needs replacement every 12-15 years, but the main coat lasts 25-60 years depending on material and pitch. Thickness does not determine longevity — depth of thatch covering fixings does.

Any straw or reed can be used for thatching. — Only specific tall-stemmed heritage wheat varieties, properly harvested (binder, not combine) and stored, or well-managed water reed (Phragmites australis) produce durable thatch. Modern short-straw combine-harvested straw and nitrogen-weakened straw decays quickly. UK seed law further restricts heritage wheat seed trade.
