Who It Is

Imagine the most piercing ache you've ever felt — the sharp sting of a wound, the hollow weight of grief, the dull throb of a fever. Now imagine that pain as a living thing, a spirit born from Strife herself, unleashed upon the world to make sure no mortal ever forgets what suffering feels like. That's Algos — not a god you pray to, but a presence you can't escape. In Hesiod's Theogony, Algos is one of the grim children of Eris, the goddess of discord, who gave birth to a whole family of horrors without a father: Toil, Famine, Battles, Murders, Lies, and the Algea — the Pains themselves. They're the background radiation of Greek mythology, the reason why every hero's journey includes a wound, every tragedy ends in tears, and every mortal life carries its share of sorrow. Powers
Infliction of Pain

The Algea have the power to bring physical and emotional suffering upon mortals. They are agents of distress who manifest as the experience of pain itself.

Limit: They are personifications rather than active agents in most myths; they do not appear as characters with individual agency in surviving narratives.

Accounts I
Birth from ErisHesiod, Theogony 226-232

c. 700 BCE

Algos is born from Eris (Strife), the goddess of discord and contention, who conceived alone without a father. Eris gave birth to a brood of personified evils: Toil, Forgetfulness, Famine, the Pains (Algea), Battles, Wars, Murders, Slaughters, Quarrels, Lies, Tales, Disputes, Lawlessness, Ruin, and Oath. These beings represent the consequences that follow in the wake of strife and discord. The Algea are unleashed upon the world as constant companions of human suffering, inseparable from the human condition.

Accounts II
Fragmentary Preservation of Local MythologyVarious fragmentary texts, scholia, and lexicographical entries

Classical to Late Antique

Much of what we know about minor Greek and Roman deities comes not from complete mythological narratives but from fragments: brief mentions in lexicographical works, marginal notes (scholia) on major authors, and passing references in geographical or antiquarian texts. The 10th-century Byzantine encyclopedia known as the Suda, the 12th-century Etymologicum Magnum, and the scholia on Homer, Pindar, and the tragedians are particularly rich sources for these fragmentary survivals. The compilers of these works had access to manuscripts now lost that preserved mythological details absent from the surviving literary canon. For many obscure deities, these late, derivative sources are the only textual evidence available. The challenge for modern scholarship is to evaluate the reliability of these fragments: some may preserve genuine archaic traditions, while others may represent late antique or Byzantine scholarly inventions designed to fill gaps in the mythological record.
