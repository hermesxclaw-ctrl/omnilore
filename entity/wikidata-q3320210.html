Form

No canonical physical description survives. As a personification, Algos would have been depicted anthropomorphically, but no ancient vase paintings or sculptures are specifically identified as Algos. The plural Algea are described in poetry as the embodiments of physical and mental suffering. Without direct iconographic evidence, the physical form of this deity remains a matter of speculation. In the context of the broader regional artistic tradition, the deity may have been represented according to the standard conventions for divine beings of its cultural milieu—anthropomorphic, distinguished by specific attributes or symbols that identified its domain and function. The absence of surviving imagery is itself a significant data point, suggesting that the cult was either aniconic (using non-figural symbols rather than anthropomorphic statues) or that its material culture was too modest to have survived the centuries.

Powers

Infliction of Pain The Algea have the power to bring physical and emotional suffering upon mortals. They are agents of distress who manifest as the experience of pain itself. Hesiodic tradition They are personifications rather than active agents in most myths; they do not appear as characters with individual agency in surviving narratives. None recorded.

Behavior & Habitat

As a daimon (spirit) of pain, Algos exists to inflict suffering on mortals. The Algea are among the malevolent spirits released by Eris, dwelling in the world to cause grief, disease, and sorrow. They are not worshipped but feared, representing the inescapable presence of pain in human existence.

Accounts

Algos is born from Eris (Strife), the goddess of discord and contention, who conceived alone without a father. Eris gave birth to a brood of personified evils: Toil, Forgetfulness, Famine, the Pains (Algea), Battles, Wars, Murders, Slaughters, Quarrels, Lies, Tales, Disputes, Lawlessness, Ruin, and Oath. These beings represent the consequences that follow in the wake of strife and discord.

Much of what we know about minor Greek and Roman deities comes not from complete mythological narratives but from fragments: brief mentions in lexicographical works, marginal notes (scholia) on major authors, and passing references in geographical or antiquarian texts. The 10th-century Byzantine encyclopedia known as the Suda, the 12th-century Etymologicum Magnum, and the scholia on Homer, Pindar, and the tragedians are particularly rich sources for these fragmentary survivals. The compilers of these works had access to manuscripts now lost that preserved mythological details absent from the surviving literary canon. For many obscure deities, these late, derivative sources are the only textual evidence available. The challenge for modern scholarship is to evaluate the reliability of these fragments: some may preserve genuine archaic traditions, while others may represent late antique or Byzantine scholarly inventions designed to fill gaps in the mythological record.

Scholarly Debates
Algos vs. Oizys
Algos and Oizys are separate deities, both children of Eris, with distinct domains (pain vs. misery/anxiety). ⇌ They are the same entity or interchangeable personifications of suffering.
Evidence for A: Hesiod's Theogony lists them separately (Algea at line 227, Oizys at line 226).
Evidence for B: Later scholiasts and compilers sometimes conflate them.
What We Don't Know

No vase paintings or artistic depictions of Algos/Algea are identified. No primary sources beyond Hesiod's Theogony are known. The distinction between Algos and Oizys in later sources requires more research. No modern popular culture adaptations of Algos as a character are documented (the name is used in medical terminology but not in narrative media). The study of such fragmentary evidence continues to advance as new technologies, including digital imaging of worn inscriptions and computational analysis of textual corpora, enable scholars to extract information from sources previously considered illegible or uninformative.

A way to hold it in your mind

If the Greek pantheon were a corporate structure, Algos wouldn't be the CEO or even a department head — it would be the annual performance review, the mandatory overtime, the office cold that goes around every winter. It's not a character; it's a condition. Born from Strife (Eris) as one of her many dysfunctional children, Algos is the universe's way of saying 'no pain, no gain' — except without the gain. It's the tax every mortal pays for existing, the fine print in the contract of being alive. You don't worship it, you don't sacrifice to it, you just... endure it. And in a mythology full of dramatic gods and epic heroes, that quiet, inescapable presence makes Algos oddly relatable. It's the god of the Tuesday afternoon headache, the 3 AM existential dread, the papercut on your finger that stings more than it should. Algos is the reason why every culture has a word for 'ouch'.
