Who It Is

Onesippus never speaks, never fights a monster alone, and never gets a statue — yet his name preserves a strange Boeotian bargain: fifty daughters, one hero, one lion, and a colonial future parcelled out like an inheritance. He is one of the fifty Thespiades, son of Heracles and Chryseis, whose entire biography is a single collective sentence that later Greeks used to explain why Thespiae worshipped Heracles and why Sardinia had Greek heroes in its foundation myth.

No personality, speech, or individual deed is narrated for Onesippus. Sources treat the 50 sons as a corporate group: they are obedient to Heracles' later instructions, divided without recorded resistance. Diodorus and Apollodorus present them as instruments of Heracles' colonial plan rather than agents with motives. Any attribution of temperament (brave, pious, etc.) is unattested and should not be invented.

Powers
Heraclid Lineage (inherited heroic status)

As a son of Heracles, Onesippus is by descent a Heraclid, implying exceptional strength and heroic ancestry in Greek genealogical logic. No individual feat of strength is narrated for him.

Limit: No attested superhuman power, weapon, or technique specific to Onesippus. Cannot be credited with Heracles' labors or lion-slaying. Status: reconstructed from lineage, not attested as active power.

Oikist / Colonist Founder Role (collective)

As one of the forty sons sent to Sardinia with Iolaus, Onesippus participates in the charter myth for the Iolaids, the Greek-mythical colonists of Sardinia. In Greek colonization myths, oikists receive heroic honors and land division.

Limit: No city is named as founded by Onesippus alone; the colony is collective under Iolaus. No archaeological attestation. Later sources (Diodorus 4.30) say the Iolaids built works and maintained Greek customs, but do not attribute specific foundations to Onesippus.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No physical description of Onesippus is attested in any ancient source. No vase painting, sculpture, coin, or relief is identified as Onesippus individually. As a son of Heracles, later artistic tradition would by convention show Thespiades as youthful male figures, beardless, athletic, but this is reconstruction, not attestation. Status: unknown / unattested for individual appearance. Collective iconography of Heracles and the Cithaeronian lion

Symbols — No individual symbol, attribute, or animal is associated with Onesippus. The collective Thespiades are symbolically linked to the Cithaeronian lion skin (their conception occurs during that hunt) and to colonial foundation (Sardinia). Thespiae's civic symbols were Eros (cult statue by Praxiteles, Pausanias 9.27.5) and Heracles, not any individual Thespiad. No verified visual record exists for Onesippus; any modern illustration is hypothetica

Domains — Mythic habitat: Thespiae in Boeotia (birthplace, maternal home), then according to Apollodorus 2.4.11 and Diodorus 4.29.3, Onesippus was among the forty dispatched to Sardinia with Iolaus to found a colony. Alternative division (seven remain at Thespiae under Thespius' kingship) would place some brothers in Boeotia, but Apollodorus explicitly counts Onesippus among the Sardinian group in most reconstructions of the list. Accounts I
The Cithaeronian Lion and the Night(s) at ThespiaeApollodorus Bibliotheca 2.4.9-10; Diodorus 4.29.1-2; Athenaeus 13.569f citing Herodorus

Mythic time (generation before Trojan War); textual attestation Classical to Imperial (Herodorus c. 400 BCE to Apollodorus c. 100-200 CE)

King Thespius of Thespiae hosts Heracles while the hero hunts the lion that ravages Cithaeron and Thespian cattle. Thespius, desiring grandsons by the greatest hero, offers his fifty daughters to Heracles. Over the course of the hunt — one night, a week, or fifty days depending on variant — Heracles sleeps with each daughter. Chryseis conceives Onesippus. The lion is killed and flayed; Heracles wears its skin in some iconography. The episode explains Thespiae's special relationship to Heracles and provides an origin for fifty sons at once. Birth of fifty sons, the Thespiades, including Onesippus; establishment of Heracles as ancestor of Thespian aristocracy; aetiology for Heracles cult at Thespiae.

Birth of the Thespiades and NamingApollodorus 2.4.10 (name list); Tzetzes Chiliades 2.221-227

Imperial compilation preserving Classical material

Each daughter bears a son. Apollodorus lists each mother-son pair: 'Chryseis bore Onesippus.' The sons are raised at Thespiae under Thespius. No individual childhood story is given for Onesippus; the list itself is the story — a catalog that functions as a genealogical charter. The names, including Onesippus, are preserved as proof of Thespian-Heraclid kinship. Creation of a large Heraclid kin group in Boeotia; later division of the group determines Thespian, Theban, and Sardinian Heraclid lines.

Heracles' Tripartite Disposition of His SonsApollodorus 2.4.11; Diodorus 4.29.3

Imperial mythography

Years later, Heracles sends instructions to Thespius: retain seven sons to remain in Thespiae and succeed to the kingship; send three to Thebes; send the remaining forty, joined by his nephew/companion Iolaus, to colonize Sardinia. Apollodorus presents this as a direct message; Diodorus frames it as Heracles' plan for his progeny. Onesippus is counted among the forty Sardinia-bound sons in the standard reconstruction of Apollodorus' list. Political charter: explains three Heraclid branches — Thespian royalty, Theban citizens, and Sardinian Iolaids. For Onesippus, it determines his mythic destiny as colonist rather than Boeotian king.

Accounts II
The Sardinian Colony of the IolaidsDiodorus 4.29.3-4.30.3; Pausanias 9.27.7, 10.17.5; Apollodorus 2.4.11

Mythic colonization era; narrated in Hellenistic/Imperial sources

Led by Iolaus, the forty sons sail to Sardinia, subdue the island's inhabitants, found cities and sanctuaries, introduce Greek customs, and are thereafter heroized as the Iolaids. Diodorus elaborates that they built substantial works (the Iolaia), drained marshes, planted olive trees, and maintained independence for generations. Onesippus, as one of the forty, is a co-founder. The myth provides a Greek heroic origin for Sardinian peoples and explains Nuragic structures as Greek works in antiquity. Aetiology for Greek claims on Sardinia; hero cult of Iolaus and the Thespiades on Sardinia; Thespian prestige through overseas kin. Historically, no Greek colony of this type existed in Sardinia; archaeology shows Nuragic culture, Phoenician and later Carthaginian presence.

Thespian Cult of Heracles and the Thespiades as AncestorsPausanias 9.27.5-7; IG VII 1735-1750 (Thespian inscriptions mentioning Heracles); Diodorus 4.29

Archaic to Roman period (cult attested archaeologically from 6th century BCE onward)

Thespiae maintained a prominent cult of Heracles and of Eros. Pausanias notes Thespians honor Heracles especially, and that they claim descent through the Thespiades. The fifty sons function as eponymous ancestors for Thespian clans and as justification for Thespian participation in Heracles festivals. Onesippus, though not individually worshipped, is part of the collective ancestor group that legitimizes Thespian identity. Inscriptions from Thespiae record priesthoods and dedications to Heracles, contextualizing the myth in lived religion. Religious and political capital for Thespiae; integration of Heracles into Boeotian genealogy; survival of the myth in local memory into Roman times.

Variant Chronology: One Night, One Week, or Fifty DaysAthenaeus 13.569f-570a citing Herodorus, Apollodorus, Diodorus; Tzetzes 2.222

Classical (Herodorus) to Byzantine (Tzetzes)

Already in antiquity, authors debated how long Heracles' liaison lasted. Herodorus (cited by Athenaeus) gives 'one night' to magnify Heracles' potency; others give seven days or fifty days (one daughter per night during the lion hunt). The variant is not about Onesippus alone but determines whether his conception was part of a single prodigious night or a prolonged hospitality. The debate reveals ancient awareness of the story's implausibility and attempts to rationalize it. Shows the myth was actively interpreted, not fixed; influences modern retellings that choose the more sensational 'one night' version for drama.

Onesippus was a son of Heracles and Megara, Deianeira, or Omphale. — Onesippus is specifically a Thespiad, son of Heracles and Chryseis, daughter of Thespius. Heracles' sons by Megara (e.g., Therimachus, Creontiades) were killed by Heracles in his madness and are distinct; sons by Deianeira include Hyllus. Confusion arises because Heracles had many sons with similar names.

All fifty sons of Heracles stayed in Thespiae and became kings there. — According to Apollodorus and Diodorus, only seven remained in Thespiae; three were sent to Thebes and forty (including Onesippus) were sent to colonize Sardinia with Iolaus. The myth explicitly disperses them.

The Sardinian colony of the Thespiades is a historically verified Greek settlement, and Onesippus was a historical colonist. — No archaeological evidence supports a Greek Heraclid colony in Sardinia of this type. Sardinia in the relevant period was Nuragic, then Phoenician/Carthaginian. The Iolaid colony is a charter myth, not history, likely invented to explain Greek claims or to Hellenize Sardinian monuments (nuraghi) as Greek works.

Onesippus had famous individual adventures, powers, or a distinct appearance described in myth. — No individual deed, power, appearance, or speech is attested for Onesippus. He appears only as a name in a catalog. Any image labeled 'Onesippus' online is modern invention or mislabeled Heracles.
