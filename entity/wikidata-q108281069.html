Who It Is

Onesippus never slew a monster or stole fire — his entire existence fits in a single line of a genealogical list. He was one of fifty sons Heracles fathered on the fifty daughters of King Thespius in the shadow of Mount Cithaeron, a living footnote to a lion hunt that became a dynastic breeding program. If you blink, you miss him; if you look closely, you see how Greek myth built colonies, legitimized kings, and turned hospitality into history.

No personality, speech, moral character, or individual deeds are narrated for Onesippus. He is a genealogical entry, not a character with agency. Later mythographic tradition treats the fifty sons as a group: seven retained at Thespiae, three sent to Thebes, forty sent to colonize Sardinia under Iolaus. Which group Onesippus belonged to is not specified individually in surviving texts; the disposition is reported collectively. Status: unknown / collective only.

Powers
Heracleid Lineage (No Individual Power Attested)

Onesippus is ascribed no individual superhuman power, skill, transformation, weapon, or feat. As a son of Heracles he belongs genealogically to the Heracleidae, who in Greek tradition claim descent-derived prestige and in some accounts Heraclean strength, but no text attributes strength, endurance, or divine favor to Onesippus specifically.

Limit: No attested abilities to limit; any attribution of Heraclean strength would be reconstruction, not attested fact.

Colonial Founder Status (Collective, Conditional)

If Onesippus was among the forty sent to Sardinia with Iolaus, he would share in the mythic role of oikist (colonial founder) of the Sardinian Heracleid settlement. Diodorus and Pausanias present the Sardinian expedition as a civilizing/colonizing mission. No individual foundation act, city, or eponym is linked to Onesippus alone.

Limit: Conditional and collective; source does not place Onesippus specifically in the Sardinian forty. Alternative tradition keeps seven in Thespiae and sends three to Thebes — Onesippus could have been in either group with no colonial role.

Accounts I
The Cithaeronian Lion HuntApollodorus Bibliotheca 2.4.9-10; Diodorus Siculus 4.29.2-3; Pausanias 9.26.6

Mythic time of Heracles' youth (before Twelve Labors); literary form Hellenistic-Roman (1st c. BCE - 2nd c. CE)

At age eighteen, Heracles, living with Amphitryon, hunts the lion of Mount Cithaeron which preyed on the herds of Amphitryon and Thespius, king of Thespiae. Thespius hosts Heracles for an extended period during the hunt. The lion is eventually killed by Heracles, who thereafter wears its skin. The hunt provides the narrative pretext for Heracles' prolonged stay at Thespiae and his union with the king's daughters. Establishes Heracles' early heroic credentials and his tie to Thespiae; creates the hospitality context for the conception of the fifty sons including Onesippus; lion skin becomes Heraclean attribute.

The Union with the Fifty Daughters of ThespiusApollodorus 2.4.10; Diodorus 4.29.3; Athenaeus 13.4 citing Herodorus of Heraclea; Pausanias 9.27.6; Tzetzes Chiliades 2.221-227

Mythic; literary sources Classical to Byzantine

Wishing to have grandchildren by Heracles, King Thespius entertains Heracles as guest and sends his fifty daughters to his bed — in one version one per night for fifty nights, in another all in a single night, in another seven nights with multiple daughters per night. Heracles, sometimes described as unaware that partners changed, impregnates all fifty. Chryseis, one of the daughters, conceives Onesippus. The episode is presented as xenia (guest-host) arrangement and dynastic strategy, not romance. Conception and birth of fifty sons, the Thespiads/Heracleidae, including Onesippus. Provides Thespiae with Heraclean descendants and a mythic kinship claim to Heracles.

The Birth of Onesippus, Son of ChryseisApollodorus 2.4.10 (catalogue entry: 'Chryseis bore Onesippus')

Mythic; catalogued in Hellenistic mythography

Following the union at Thespiae, each daughter bears a son. Chryseis bears Onesippus. The catalogue lists each mother-son pair without further narrative. No birth omens, prophecies, or individual childhood deeds are recorded for Onesippus, unlike for Heracles himself. Onesippus enters the genealogical record as one of the fifty Thespian Heracleidae. His name preserves Thespian onomastic tradition. No individual inheritance or birthright is specified.

Accounts II
Heracles' Disposition of the Fifty Sons: Thespiae, Thebes, and SardiniaApollodorus 2.4.10-2.7.6; Diodorus 4.29.3-4.30.1; Pausanias 9.27.7; Tzetzes 2.34

Mythic (generation before Trojan War); literary Hellenistic-Roman

Years later, Heracles sends instructions to Thespius regarding the sons: keep seven at Thespiae, send three to Thebes, and send the remaining forty, joined by his nephew-companion Iolaus, to colonize the island of Sardinia. This tripartite division converts a reproductive feat into a political/colonial program, distributing Heraclean blood to Boeotia and the western Mediterranean. Creates etiology for Thespian aristocratic families claiming Heraclean descent, for Theban Heracleidae, and for the Sardinian colony (later associated with the Iolaans/Nuragic Sardinians). Provides mythic charter for Greek presence in Sardinia.

The Sardinian Colony of Iolaus and the ThespiadsDiodorus Siculus 4.29.3-4.30.3, 5.15.1-5.15.6; Pausanias 10.17.5-9; Apollodorus 2.7.6

Mythic colonial age; elaborated in Classical and Hellenistic historiography

Iolaus leads the forty (or a contingent of the) sons of Heracles and Thespius's daughters to Sardinia. There they subdue the island, found cities, introduce Greek customs, and become the Iolaans or Thespiads. Diodorus describes them civilizing the island, building works, and later being honored as heroes. Pausanias adds that Iolaus's expedition gave Sardinia its Greek name Ichnoussa and that the colonists' descendants persisted. If Onesippus was among the forty, he participates anonymously in this collective foundation; no source names his individual deeds there. Mythic explanation for Greek cultural claims in Sardinia and for the Nuragic towers (nuraghi) sometimes attributed to Iolaus in antiquity. Establishes Heracleid presence in the West, parallel to other Heracles western wanderings.

The Thespian and Theban Remnants: Those Who StayedApollodorus 2.4.10; Pausanias 9.27.6-7; Inscriptional evidence for Thespian Heracleia festival (IG VII 1735 etc., not naming Onesippus)

Mythic; reflected in Classical polis institutions

Seven sons remain in Thespiae, presumably to perpetuate the local royal line and priesthoods, while three are settled in Thebes, linking Thespiae to its powerful neighbor. The Thespian polis later celebrated the Heracleia and Erotidia festivals, honoring Heracles and Eros, and aristocratic families claimed descent from Thespius's daughters. Onesippus may have been among those who stayed, but no source assigns him to a specific city; his fate after birth is unrecorded individually. Provides Thespiae with enduring Heraclean legitimacy; explains Thespian-Theban kinship ties. The lack of individual assignment shows the catalogue's function was collective prestige, not biography.

Onesippus was a famous hero who performed labors like his father Heracles. — No individual deeds, labors, or adventures are attested for Onesippus. He is a name in a genealogical catalogue; his only narrative context is collective (conception and dispatch). Any heroic feats attributed to him are modern invention or confusion with Heracles himself.

The fifty daughters of Thespius willingly sought out Heracles as lovers. — Ancient sources present the unions as arranged by King Thespius, who sent his daughters to Heracles, in some versions by deception. The daughters' desire or consent is never affirmed; the episode is framed as paternal dynastic strategy and hospitality exchange, not romance.

The Cithaeronian lion is the same as the Nemean lion. — Distinct lions. The Cithaeronian lion was Heracles' youthful hunt near Thespiae before the Twelve Labors; the Nemean lion was the First Labor, whose skin became invulnerable armor. Ancient sources keep them separate.

Onesippus founded a city or gave his name to a place in Sardinia. — No city, tribe, or toponym is linked to Onesippus individually. The Sardinian foundation is attributed collectively to Iolaus and the forty, or to Iolaus alone. No eponym 'Onesippus' is attested in Sardinian geography or epigraphy.

All fifty sons went to Sardinia. — Only forty (in Apollodorus) went to Sardinia with Iolaus; seven remained in Thespiae and three were settled in Thebes. The tripartition is explicit in the most detailed source.
