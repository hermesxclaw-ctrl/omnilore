Who It Is

In the shadow of Mount Tmolus, a woman is named only to explain her son: Theogone, lover of Ares, mother of a Lydian king whose lust and violent death gave a mountain its name. She appears for a single sentence in a disputed ancient text — and then vanishes, leaving behind questions about rape, retribution, and how much of Greek myth is local invention.

No behavior, speech, personality, motives, or moral characterization is attributed to Theogone. She is a genealogical figure only: mother of Tmolus by Ares. Whether the union was consensual, forced, or marital is not stated. No agency is described beyond giving birth. All interpretation beyond 'mother of Tmolus' would be reconstruction and is avoided here. Status: unattested.

Powers
No attested supernatural powers

Theogone is not described as a goddess, nymph, sorceress, or heroine with powers. No transformation, prophecy, combat feat, or magical ability is attributed to her in any source. She functions solely as a mortal genealogical figure (mother of a king by a god). This absence is attested: the text does not grant her powers, rather than powers being lost in transmission.

Limit: N/A - mortal status implied by lack of divine epithets and by narrative role

Maternal link to divine paternity (via Ares)

Her only narrative function is to provide a divine father (Ares) for Tmolus, conferring heroic status on the Lydian king. This is a common mythic pattern (mortal woman + god = eponymous king) but is not a power she wields.

Limit: No further offspring or divine favor is recorded for her

Form & Signs

Appearance — No physical description of Theogone is attested in any surviving source. No epithets, age, clothing, or iconographic attributes are given. The text identifies her only by name and by her sexual union with Ares. No vase painting, sculpture, coin, or inscription has been identified as depicting her. Status: unknown.

Symbols — No symbols, animals, plants, or objects are associated with Theogone. Her partner Ares is associated with spear, shield, and war, but no source transfers these to her. No cult statue or votive is attested. Status: unknown / unattested.

Domains — No domain (e.g., goddess of...) is assigned to Theogone. Her narrative habitat is Lydia in western Anatolia, via her son's kingship. Lydia's capital Sardis and Mount Tmolus (Bozdağ range between Sardis and Hypaepa) form the geographic context, but Theogone herself is not localized to a city or sanctuary. Her consort Ares is not typically Lydian, suggesting a Greek importation of divine paternity for a local king. Status: attested only a

Accounts I
The Union of Ares and TheogonePseudo-Plutarch, De fluviis 7.1

Hellenistic/Roman imperial (text dated 1st-2nd c. CE or later, purporting to record older Lydian lore)

The text states laconically that Tmolus was the son of Ares and Theogone. No courtship, setting, or circumstance is given. The union explains Tmolus's warlike character and royal legitimacy: descent from the god of war. No other details about Theogone's origin, family, or status are provided. The brevity suggests the author assumed the genealogical formula was sufficient and that Theogone needed no introduction beyond her name. Birth of Tmolus, who becomes king of Lydia and eponym of Mount Tmolus. Theogone disappears from the narrative after this genealogical note.

Tmolus King of LydiaPseudo-Plutarch, De fluviis 7.1 (with geographic corroboration from Strabo 13.4.5, Herodotus 1.84 on Lydian kingship)

Mythic time (before the historical Lydian dynasty of Gyges, ca. 680 BCE in historiography)

Tmolus, son of Ares and Theogone, rules as king of Lydia. The text calls him 'king of Lydia' (βασιλεύς Λυδίας). His kingdom is centered near Mount Tmolus (ancient Carmanorion), the range that separates the Hermus and Cayster valleys. No deeds of kingship are described except his crime and death; no wars, laws, or foundations are attributed to him beyond the eponymy. Establishes Tmolus as eponymous ruler; his death leads to renaming of the mountain. Provides aetiology for the toponym Tmolus.

The Violation of ArrhippePseudo-Plutarch, De fluviis 7.1

Mythic time

Tmolus, while hunting on the mountain, sees Arrhippe (also spelled Arrhippe, Arriphe), a nymph and companion (or follower) of Artemis, and 'offers violence to her' (Greek: βιάζομαι, Latin: vim infert; Goodwin: 'offered violence'). The standard scholarly reading is rape. Arrhippe is described as one of the followers of Artemis, emphasizing the sacrilege: Artemis is the virgin goddess who protects her companions. The assault occurs in Artemis's sphere (mountain/wilderness). No dialogue or resistance is detailed beyond the act itself. Arrhippe's trauma leads to her suicide (see next story); Tmolus incurs divine retribution from Artemis. The story follows a common Greek pattern of punishment for sexual violence against Artemis's nymphs (compare Callisto, Coronis).

Accounts II
Arrhippe's Suicide and Artemis's WrathPseudo-Plutarch, De fluviis 7.1

Mythic time

After the assault, Arrhippe, unable to bear the violation and perhaps fearing Artemis's anger or shame, hangs herself (or in some translations, 'destroyed herself'). Artemis, enraged by the violation of her follower, arranges punishment for Tmolus. The goddess's role is explicit: the subsequent death by bull is presented as divine retribution (τιμωρία). This mirrors Artemis's vengeance in other myths where her nymphs are violated. Arrhippe's death intensifies the pollution (miasma) around Tmolus and justifies his gruesome death. Establishes Artemis as moral agent and explains why a bull (possibly sacred or sent by the goddess) becomes the instrument of justice.

The Death of Tmolus by the Mad BullPseudo-Plutarch, De fluviis 7.1-7.2

Mythic time

As punishment, Tmolus is gored to death by a mad bull on the mountain. The text: 'he was tossed by a furious bull and, being gored, died' or 'was gored by a bull driven mad.' The bull may be understood as sent by Artemis or as a manifestation of divine anger. He is buried on the mountain that previously was called Carmanorion. After his burial, the mountain is renamed Tmolus after him. His son (?) or successor (?) is not named; the kingship passes out of the narrative. Aetiology for Mount Tmolus (modern Bozdağ, 2157m, in Manisa Province, Turkey). The mountain retains the name through antiquity (Homer Iliad 2.866, Herodotus, Strabo) and is the setting for later myths (Midas, Pan vs Apollo). Tmolus's violent death serves as moral exemplum about hubris and violation of Artemis.

Aetiology of Mount Tmolus and the Lydian LandscapePseudo-Plutarch, De fluviis 7.2; Strabo Geography 13.4.5; Pliny Natural History 5.30

Archaic to Hellenistic (toponym already in Homer)

The story explains why the Lydian mountain is called Tmolus. Before Tmolus's burial it was called Carmanorion (or Carmanorium). After his death and burial there, it took his name. The mountain overlooks Sardis and was famed for wine, saffron, and gold-bearing rivers (Pactolus). The aetiology links Theogone's lineage to the physical landscape, making her the ancestress of a toponym. Strabo and others describe Mount Tmolus without mentioning Theogone, showing the aetiology was not universally known. Geographic permanence: the name Tmolus survives into Roman, Byzantine, and modern usage (Turkish Bozdağ). The story provides a mythic charter for Lydian identity. Theogone's only lasting legacy is toponymic, through her son.

Theogone was a Greek goddess, Titaness, or important deity. — Theogone is not called a goddess in any source; she is presented as a mortal woman who bore a son to Ares. Her name's etymology ('god-born') describes her relationship to a god, not divinity itself. No cult, epithet, or divine domain is attested.

Theogone appears in Hesiod's Theogony or Homer. — She does not appear in Hesiod, Homer, or any archaic source. Her sole attestation is in the much later Pseudo-Plutarch De fluviis (1st-2nd c. CE or later), a paradoxographical work of doubtful authenticity.

Tmolus was always the mountain-god who judged Pan vs. Apollo, and Theogone was his mother in that story. — Ovid's mountain-god Tmolus has no parentage given and is not connected to Theogone. Theogone belongs only to the Pseudo-Plutarch king-tradition. The two Tmolus traditions (king vs. god) are distinct and not combined in any ancient source.

Plutarch himself recorded Theogone as authentic history. — The work is Pseudo-Plutarch, not the famous philosopher Plutarch of Chaeronea. Modern scholarship unanimously regards De fluviis as pseudepigraphic and largely fictional. Citing it as 'Plutarch says...' overstates its authority.

The story of Theogone and Tmolus is an authentic ancient Lydian myth preserved from Lydian sources. — No Lydian-language source survives; all we have is a Greek paradoxographer's late retelling that cites sources now considered invented. While the toponym Tmolus is genuinely ancient (already in Homer), the specific genealogy and rape-punishment plot may be Hellenistic invention for entertainment and moralizing.
