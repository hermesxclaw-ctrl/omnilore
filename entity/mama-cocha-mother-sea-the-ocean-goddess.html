Mama Cocha (Mother Sea / the Ocean-Goddess)

also known asMother Sea / the Ocean-Goddess
Typegoddess (sea, fish, mariners)
Who It Is
Form

Mama Cocha is typically depicted as a mature woman with flowing dark hair, often adorned with a crown of seashells or coral. She may be shown emerging from the ocean waves, sometimes with a fish tail, reminiscent of a mermaid, or holding a fishing net and a vessel of water. In some Andean representations, she is portrayed with blue and green garments that mirror the sea, and her eyes are said to reflect the depth of the ocean. Unlike the earth goddess Pachamama, who is often shown as a fertile, grounded figure, Mama Cocha is ethereal and fluid, with a serene yet powerful demeanor.

Powers

Control over waters Mama Cocha can command the tides, waves, and currents of the ocean, as well as the flow of rivers and lakes. She can calm storms or raise them, and she determines the availability of fish and other marine life.

Protection of mariners She safeguards fishermen, sailors, and travelers on water, guiding them through treacherous conditions and ensuring safe passage. Her favor is sought before voyages, and offerings are made to appease her.

Fertility and abundance As a water goddess, she is believed to bring fertility to the land through rainfall and irrigation. She also grants abundance in fishing and agriculture, and is invoked for the health of children and livestock.

Accounts

In the beginning, the world was dry and barren. The god Viracocha, the creator, decided to bring forth water to sustain life. He called upon Mama Cocha, the mother of waters, who wept tears that filled the valleys, forming the oceans and lakes. Her tears created Lake Titicaca, which became a sacred place where the sun and moon were born. Mama Cocha then taught the people how to fish and navigate the waters, and she became their protector.

A fishing village on the coast of Peru had grown prosperous but forgot to offer thanks to Mama Cocha. They became arrogant, boasting that they no longer needed her blessings. In her anger, Mama Cocha raised a massive storm, sinking their boats and flooding their homes. The survivors begged for forgiveness, and she relented, but she demanded that they always remember her power. From that day, the villagers held an annual festival in her honor, offering fish and chicha (corn beer) to the sea.

A poor fisherman on Lake Titicaca was unable to catch any fish for days. One night, he dreamed of a beautiful woman rising from the lake, who told him to cast his net in a specific spot. He obeyed, and his net filled with an abundance of fish. He shared his catch with his village, and in gratitude, he built a small shrine to Mama Cocha on the shore. The shrine became a place of pilgrimage, and the fisherman's family prospered.

Scholarly Debates
Origin of Mama Cocha
She is a primordial goddess who existed before creation, as part of the Inca pantheon. ⇌ She is a later addition, influenced by coastal cultures such as the Moche and Chimú, and was incorporated into Inca mythology during imperial expansion.
Relationship to Viracocha
Mama Cocha is the wife of Viracocha, as stated in some colonial chronicles. ⇌ She is his daughter, or entirely unrelated, as other sources suggest she was a local deity later syncretized with the creator god.
Domain
Mama Cocha is exclusively a sea goddess, with no authority over freshwater bodies. ⇌ She is a goddess of all waters, including lakes, rivers, and rain, as evidenced by her connection to Lake Titicaca.
What We Don't Know

There is limited archaeological evidence specifically dedicated to Mama Cocha, as Inca worship often occurred at natural water features rather than constructed temples. The colonial chronicles provide conflicting accounts of her mythology, and many original Quechua texts were destroyed by the Spanish. Further research is needed to clarify her relationship with other water deities and her role in pre-Inca cultures.

A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine a goddess who commands the ocean's fury and calm, who weeps tears that become lakes, and who protects every fisherman who casts a net. Mama Cocha, the Mother Sea, is a figure of immense power and tenderness, and her story is as deep as the waters she rules.
