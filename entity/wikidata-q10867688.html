Who It Is

Before the pyramids had names, the Egyptians watched a bright figure stride across the southern winter sky — three stars in a perfect line for his belt, a boat beneath his feet, and beside him the blazing star we call Sirius. They called him Sah. For 70 days each summer he vanished below the horizon, as if mummified in the underworld, and when he rose again at dawn, the Nile began to swell and the New Year began. To become Sah was to cheat death itself.

Sah is not characterized by elaborate mythic personality like Horus or Seth; he is a celestial, calendrical, and funerary power. Behavior: he sails the southern sky (Book of Nut: 'Sah sails to the south of the sky'), sets for 70 days (period of mummification and invisibility), then rises heliacally with Sopdet to herald the New Year and the Nile inundation. He is benevolent, protective of the dead king, and a progenitor – father of Sopdu. In Pyramid Texts he is a companion and announcer of the king's ascent: the king 'becomes a star, becomes Sah.' Powers
Stellar embodiment and time regulation

Sah IS the constellation we call Orion. His heliacal rising and setting, together with Sopdet (Sirius), marked the Egyptian New Year (wepet-renpet) and the onset of the akhet inundation. The 70-day invisibility of Sah below the horizon was the mythic model for the 70-day embalming period.

Limit: Bound to celestial mechanics; disappears annually and must be reborn – not omnipresent. Power is cyclical, not constant.

Progenitive and genealogical power

Father of Sopdu (Sopd), the falcon god of the east and guardian of frontiers, through union with Sopdet. This establishes Sah as patriarch of a stellar family that protects kingship and borders.

Limit: Genealogy is stellar-theological, not narrative-mythic with adventures; no stories of Sah raising Sopdu.

Resurrection and transfiguration (as Sah-Osiris)

From Middle Kingdom onward, Sah is identified with Osiris as the resurrected king of the dead in the southern sky. The deceased, by becoming Sah/Orion, achieves akh status and joins the imperishable stars. Spell: 'I am Sah who treads his Two Lands' equates the dead with the god's renewal.

Limit: Requires correct rites, justification (maa-kheru), and preservation of the sah-body (mummy). Not automatic.

Navigation and protection of the dead king

Sah, with Sopdet, ferries and guides the king's ascent to the sky: 'The sky conceives you with Sah, the dawn gives you birth with Sopdet.' He opens the sky's doors and announces the king among the gods.

Limit: Acts as psychopomp/guide, not warrior; does not battle Apophis like Ra or Seth.

Association with the sah (mummified spiritual body) – homophonic power of ennoblement

The noun sah (sꜥḥ) denotes the ennobled, incorruptible mummy-body achieved after embalming and rites. While not a 'power' of the god Sah per se, the homophony was exploited theologically: to become a sah (blessed dead) is to become like Sah (imperishable star). The sah-body is durable, shining, and capable of dwelling among gods.

Limit: Requires 70-day mummification, opening-of-the-mouth, and moral justification; vulnerable to destruction of the corpse before rites complete.

Accounts I
The Conception of the King with Sah and SopdetPyramid Texts PT 442 (Pyr. 819–822), Pyramid of Unas, also Pepi I and Merenre

c. 2350–2300 BCE (5th Dynasty)

The king's rebirth is described cosmologically: 'The sky is pregnant with you with Sah, the dawn-light gives birth to you with Sopdet.' Sah (Orion) and Sopdet (Sirius) are the celestial parents who conceive and bear the king as a star. The king is washed by the gods, takes his place in the sky, and sails with Sah. The spell is recited to ensure the king joins the southern sky's brightest constellation and is not left among the dead on earth. Establishes the earliest attested stellar parentage for the king; template for later 'becoming Sah' formulae; links kingship to the Sothic calendar.

Sah Fathers Sopdu, Lord of the EastPyramid Texts PT 211 §133, PT 619 §1717; later temple inscriptions at Dendera and Edfu

Old Kingdom through Ptolemaic

Sah united with Sopdet begets Sopdu (Sopd), the falcon god who guards Egypt's eastern frontier and the Sinai turquoise routes. Sopdu is called 'Sopdu, son of Sah.' The genealogy makes the stellar couple progenitors of a protective frontier god, linking the southern sky to eastern horizons and royal expansion. Creates a stellar family triad (Sah-Sopdet-Sopdu) mirroring Osiris-Isis-Horus; Sopdu's cult at Saft el-Henna claims stellar legitimacy.

The 70-Day Disappearance and Return – Sah's Death and EmbalmingFundamentals of the Course of the Stars (Book of Nut), pCarlsberg I and Seti I cenotaph text; commentary in Neugebauer & Parker EAT I

Composition c. 1800 BCE, copies 1290 BCE–200 CE

The text explains that Sah and Sopdet spend 70 days in the Duat, invisible below the horizon, while the decans set and rise. This period is explicitly linked to the 70 days of mummification: as Sah is embalmed and purified in the Duat, so the corpse is embalmed on earth. When Sah rises heliacally in summer, he is reborn, purified, and sails south again. The king/deceased shares this fate: to disappear, be made a sah (mummy), and rise as Sah (star). Provides theological justification for 70-day embalming; anchors civil calendar to stellar cycle; later cited by classical authors (Herodotus II.86 gives 70 days for embalming).

Accounts II
The King Joins the Imperishable Stars as SahPyramid Texts PT 216 §151, PT 569 §1435, PT 466 §883; Coffin Texts 44, 227

Old Kingdom–Middle Kingdom

Spells declare the king/deceased has become Sah: 'I am Sah who treads his Two Lands' (CT 227). He is told to 'cross the sky with Sah, traverse the Duat with Osiris.' The deceased sails in Sah's barque, is announced by Sah to the gods, and takes his seat among the ikhemu-sek (imperishable circumpolar stars) and the unwearying stars led by Sah. The transformation is both astral and moral – the sah-body must be justified. Democratization of the afterlife: from royal pyramid to coffins of officials, anyone could become Sah-Osiris; central to Book of the Dead chapters 69–71.

Sah Sails the Southern Sky – The Astronomical Ceiling NarrativeTomb of Senenmut (TT 353) astronomical ceiling; Tomb of Seti I (KV 17) and Ramesses IV (KV 2) ceilings; Dendera pronaos

New Kingdom (c. 1473 BCE) to Ptolemaic (c. 50 BCE)

Iconographic narrative without spoken myth: Sah is shown in his barque sailing southward along the Milky Way, while Sopdet sails nearby. Inscriptions label decans and state 'Sah is in the south of the sky after Sopdet.' The ceilings map the night sky for the deceased to navigate eternally. At Dendera, Sah is accompanied by offering priests and labeled as 'Sah-Osiris, the great god in the south.' Creates a permanent star map for the afterlife; preserves Egyptian constellation shapes; becomes key source for modern reconstruction of Egyptian astronomy.

Sah-Osiris – The Syncretized Resurrected GodCoffin Texts Spell 644, Book of the Dead Spell 178, Temple of Isis at Philae and Dendera Osirian chapels, Plutarch De Iside et Osiride 21 (interpretatio graeca)

Middle Kingdom (c. 2050 BCE) through Roman Period (c. 200 CE); Plutarch c. 120 CE

Over centuries Sah and Osiris converge: Sah is called 'Osiris-Sah' and Osiris is called 'the Sah in the south.' The deceased, identified with Osiris, rises as Sah in the south to join Re in the east. Plutarch, reporting Egyptian theology, notes Egyptians call Orion the soul of Osiris and Sirius the soul of Isis. At Dendera, Osiris's tomb is shown under Sah's stars, linking stellar and chthonic resurrection. Theological merger that dominates Late Period funerary religion; explains why Orion's Belt was called 'the soul of Osiris' in classical sources; fuels modern Orion Correlation Theory.

Sah is the Egyptian god of the constellation Orion, exactly the same as the Greek Orion myth (hunter killed by scorpion). — Sah is the Egyptian personification of the stars we call Orion, but has no hunter myth, no scorpion story, and no Greek narrative. His myths are about sailing the sky, fathering Sopdu, and resurrecting with Osiris. The Greek Orion is a later interpretatio graeca overlay.

Sah and the sah-spiritual body are the same – Sah is the god of the soul's spiritual body. — They are homophones distinguished by hieroglyphic determinatives. Sah (god, star determinative) = Orion. sah (noun, mummy determinative) = the transfigured mummy/body. Egyptians linked them poetically, but they are not one god of the soul.

Ancient Egyptians built the pyramids to align perfectly with Sah/Orion's Belt as a star map on earth. — No Egyptian text states this. Pyramid Texts link the king to Sah theologically, not architecturally. Archaeology shows Giza pyramids were built sequentially with cardinal solar alignments, not a single Orion mirror. The Orion Correlation Theory is a modern proposal rejected by Egyptologists.

Sah was a major creator god like Ra or Amun, with temples and priesthoods dedicated solely to him. — Sah had no independent cult center or priesthood like Amun or Ptah. He was a stellar/calendrical god venerated in astronomical ceilings, funerary texts, and as part of Osirian temple rites (especially at Dendera and Abydos), not as a standalone temple deity.

Sah is pronounced 'Sah' and means 'pig' or 'son' in Egyptian. — sꜥḥ (Sah) is unrelated to sa 'son' (sꜣ) or sha 'pig' (šꜣj). The consonants include ꜥ and ḥ, distinct phonemes in Egyptian. Confusion arises from omitting diacritics.
