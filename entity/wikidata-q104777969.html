Who It Is

Ajunsú is a spirit of earth and epidemic — a healer who carries smallpox in one hand and medicine in the other. In Brazil's Candomblé tradition, this orixá walks through fever and dust, transforming suffering into power.

Ajunsú/Omolu is simultaneously feared and revered. He brings disease — particularly smallpox, leprosy, and epidemic illness — but also heals. He is close to Iku (the force of death) and is often petitioned during outbreaks. His followers offer grains, beans, and popcorn; red and white are his colors. He is associated with heat and fever, which are understood as his transformative presence working on the body.

Powers
Healing of skin diseases and epidemics

Babalú-Aye/Ajunsú is the principal orixá for skin diseases, inflammation, epilepsy, catalepsy, and airborne epidemics. His cult powers and spells are used against all kinds of diseases, but especially those that mark the body externally. He is also called upon during outbreaks of contagious illness.

Limit: Healing is ritual-dependent and may require specific offerings, songs, and trance states. Not all illnesses are within his domain.

Bringing and removing illness

Babalú-Aye is closely associated with both the origin and cure of disease. He can send smallpox or other afflictions as punishment or test, and he can withdraw them through ritual intervention. This dual role makes him one of the most feared and respected orixás.

Limit: His wrath is often tied to broken taboos or neglected rituals.

Heat and purification

Heat is a property of Babalú-Aye — fever, the body heating up to expel disease, and geothermal warmth from the earth are all seen as his action. Sacrifices to him must be made during the day when temperature is high. This association makes him a transformer of impurity into purifying power.

Limit: The heat association is symbolic and ritual, not meteorological.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In Candomblé ritual and art, Babalú-Aye (and by extension Ajunsú) is often depicted wearing a raffia skirt or palm-fibre covering, referencing the scabs and sores he carries and also heals. He is associated with earth and dust — his face may be painted with white and blue patterns, and he carries a staff or xaxará (a rattle made of palm fronds and beads). Accounts I
Babalú-Ayé and the great smallpox epidemicYoruba oral tradition; Candomblé ritual lore

Pre-colonial to present

According to Yoruba tradition, Babalú-Ayé once walked among humans carrying a calabash full of smallpox. Where he stepped, disease followed. When the people realized what was happening, they begged Olodumare to stop him. Olodumare ordered Babalú-Ayé to walk only on sand, so his footprints would no longer infect the earth directly. This myth explains both the origin of smallpox and the ritual necessity of propitiating Babalú-Ayé to avoid or cure disease. The story establishes Babalú-Ayé's dual nature as bringer and healer of disease, and it justifies ritual offerings to keep him benevolent.

The orphan raised by the earthYoruba/Candomblé oral tradition

Pre-colonial

A common narrative describes Babalú-Ayé as an abandoned or orphaned child raised by the earth itself. Because of his lowly origins and physical affliction, he develops immense spiritual power over illness and healing. His rise from outcast to powerful orixá becomes a paradigm for the transformative potential of suffering. The story teaches that spiritual authority can emerge from suffering and marginalization.

Accounts II
Ajunsú at the Candomblé terreiroCandomblé ritual practice; ethnographic accounts

19th century – present

In Candomblé terreiros (houses), Ajunsú/Omolu is invoked through specific rhythms, songs, and dances. The initiate possessed by Ajunsú may hobble, simulate illness, and then dramatically transform into health — enacting the orixá's journey from suffering to power. Offerings of popcorn, beans, and red wine are made, and the xaxará rattle is shaken to call his presence. The ritual reaffirms communal bonds and channels the orixá's healing power into the community.

The day of the heat — Omolu's festivalCandomblé festival calendar; Yoruba tradition

Annual festival, traditional date August

Omolu/Ajunsú's festival day falls in the hottest part of the year. Devotees wear red and white, dance through dust and heat, and make offerings during the hottest hours. The festival publicly enacts the orixá's connection to fever and purification. In some traditions, participants cover themselves with palm fiber or raffia, mimicking Omolu's characteristic covering. The festival strengthens communal identity and publicly channels the orixá's transformative power.
