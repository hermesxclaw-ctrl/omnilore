Form

In Candomblé ritual and art, Babalú-Aye (and by extension Ajunsú) is often depicted wearing a raffia skirt or palm-fibre covering, referencing the scabs and sores he carries and also heals. He is associated with earth and dust — his face may be painted with white and blue patterns, and he carries a staff or xaxará (a rattle made of palm fronds and beads). In some traditions he hobbles because of illness, and he may wear a metal crown. In popular Brazilian culture, his image appears in carnival groups and street festivals.

Powers

Healing of skin diseases and epidemics Babalú-Aye/Ajunsú is the principal orixá for skin diseases, inflammation, epilepsy, catalepsy, and airborne epidemics. His cult powers and spells are used against all kinds of diseases, but especially those that mark the body externally. He is also called upon during outbreaks of contagious illness. English Wikipedia 'Babalú-Ayé'; Yoruba/Candomblé ritual practice Healing is ritual-dependent and may require specific offerings, songs, and trance states. Not all illnesses are within his domain. Some fear him precisely because he is believed to bring disease; his dual nature as healer and bringer of sickness creates ambivalence.

Bringing and removing illness Babalú-Aye is closely associated with both the origin and cure of disease. He can send smallpox or other afflictions as punishment or test, and he can withdraw them through ritual intervention. This dual role makes him one of the most feared and respected orixás. Yoruba religion; Candomblé tradition; English Wikipedia 'Babalú-Ayé' His wrath is often tied to broken taboos or neglected rituals. His feared aspect is sometimes emphasized more than his healing aspect in popular culture.

Heat and purification Heat is a property of Babalú-Aye — fever, the body heating up to expel disease, and geothermal warmth from the earth are all seen as his action. Sacrifices to him must be made during the day when temperature is high. This association makes him a transformer of impurity into purifying power. English Wikipedia 'Babalú-Ayé' The heat association is symbolic and ritual, not meteorological. None recorded.

Behavior & Habitat

Ajunsú/Omolu is simultaneously feared and revered. He brings disease — particularly smallpox, leprosy, and epidemic illness — but also heals. He is close to Iku (the force of death) and is often petitioned during outbreaks. His followers offer grains, beans, and popcorn; red and white are his colors. He is associated with heat and fever, which are understood as his transformative presence working on the body.

Accounts

According to Yoruba tradition, Babalú-Ayé once walked among humans carrying a calabash full of smallpox. Where he stepped, disease followed. When the people realized what was happening, they begged Olodumare to stop him. Olodumare ordered Babalú-Ayé to walk only on sand, so his footprints would no longer infect the earth directly. This myth explains both the origin of smallpox and the ritual necessity of propitiating Babalú-Ayé to avoid or cure disease.

A common narrative describes Babalú-Ayé as an abandoned or orphaned child raised by the earth itself. Because of his lowly origins and physical affliction, he develops immense spiritual power over illness and healing. His rise from outcast to powerful orixá becomes a paradigm for the transformative potential of suffering.

In Candomblé terreiros (houses), Ajunsú/Omolu is invoked through specific rhythms, songs, and dances. The initiate possessed by Ajunsú may hobble, simulate illness, and then dramatically transform into health — enacting the orixá's journey from suffering to power. Offerings of popcorn, beans, and red wine are made, and the xaxará rattle is shaken to call his presence.

Omolu/Ajunsú's festival day falls in the hottest part of the year. Devotees wear red and white, dance through dust and heat, and make offerings during the hottest hours. The festival publicly enacts the orixá's connection to fever and purification. In some traditions, participants cover themselves with palm fiber or raffia, mimicking Omolu's characteristic covering.

Content Notes

Babalú-Ayé/Ajunsú is directly associated with smallpox, one of history's most devastating epidemic diseases. In the Americas, the African deity was syncretized with Catholic saints associated with plague and illness. The orixá's feared aspect reflects real historical suffering from epidemic disease.

African diaspora religions frequently encoded experiences of epidemic disease within their spiritual frameworks.

What We Don't Know

Specific ethnographic details about Ajunsú as a distinct name — as opposed to Omolu/Babalú-Ayé — are limited. More Portuguese-language sources from Brazil would help confirm ritual specifics unique to Ajunsú rather than the broader Omolu tradition.

A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine a healer who walks with a limp because he carries the world's sickness in his own body, yet he can cure you if you bring him popcorn at noon. That is Ajunsú.
