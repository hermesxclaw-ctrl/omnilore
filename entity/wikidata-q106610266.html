Who It Is

In the ancient city of Bethulia, as the Assyrian army encircles the walls and the people cry out in despair, one widow refuses to surrender. Judith, whose name means 'praised' or 'Jewess,' will not wait for a miracle—she will become one, using her beauty, her faith, and a single sword stroke to change the fate of her nation.

Judith is portrayed as deeply religious, wise, courageous, and independent. She is also cunning and manipulative, using her beauty and charm to deceive the enemy. She is a widow who lives piously, fasting and praying, before taking action. She is decisive, resourceful, and unafraid to challenge her own people's lack of faith.

Powers
Beauty and Charm

Judith uses her physical attractiveness and conversational skill to gain access to Holofernes and win his trust.

Limit: Requires personal risk and moral compromise; effective only in specific circumstances.

Strategic Deception

She deceives Holofernes with false promises of information about the Israelites, allowing her to enter his tent and kill him.

Limit: Requires careful planning and nerve.

Physical Courage

She decapitates Holofernes in a single stroke, demonstrating physical strength and resolve.

Limit: None specified; requires proximity to the victim.

Divine Favor

Her piety and trust in God are rewarded with success; the text attributes her victory to God's intervention.

Limit: Dependent on her faith and obedience.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Judith is described as exceptionally beautiful, a quality she uses to gain access to Holofernes. The text emphasizes her attractive appearance, her rich clothing, and her adornment, which she dons after a period of mourning. Later artistic depictions often show her with a sword or holding the head of Holofernes.

Symbols — Judith is often depicted with a sword, the severed head of Holofernes, and sometimes a maidservant carrying the head in a sack. In Christian art, she is a symbol of chastity, humility, and divine deliverance; in Jewish tradition, she is a heroine of faith and courage.

Domains — Bethulia, a city in Judea (possibly Shechem); the Assyrian military camp; later, her home in Bethulia.

Accounts I
The Siege of BethuliaBook of Judith

Assigned to the reign of Manasseh (7th century BCE) by traditional interpretation; modern scholars view as Hellenistic-era composition (2nd century BCE)

King Nebuchadnezzar of Assyria sends his general Holofernes to subdue the nations of the west. Holofernes besieges the city of Bethulia, cutting off water supply. The people, led by Uzziah, agree to surrender if God does not deliver them within five days. Judith, a pious widow, rebukes them for their lack of faith and devises a plan. She dresses in her finest clothes, goes to the Assyrian camp with her maid, and is brought to Holofernes, who is captivated by her beauty. Over several days, she gains his trust and is invited to a banquet. When Holofernes becomes drunk, Judith seizes his sword and decapitates him. She returns to Bethulia with his head, and the Israelites display it, causing the Assyrians to flee in panic. The Israelites pursue and defeat them. Israel is saved, Jerusalem is spared, and Judith is celebrated as a national heroine. She returns to her widow's life and remains unmarried, gaining great renown.

Judith is a historical figure. — Most scholars consider her fictional.

The story is set during the Babylonian exile. — The setting is anachronistic; probably Hellenistic period.

Judith is a symbol of female empowerment in all traditions. — Some feminist scholars critique her use of sexuality; her story has been used to reinforce negative stereotypes of women.

The Book of Judith is part of the Jewish canon. — It is not in the Hebrew Bible.

Judith's story is the origin of the Hanukkah dairy tradition. — The midrashic variant may have influenced the custom, but it is not the sole origin.
