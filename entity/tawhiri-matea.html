Archive Dossier — Identity

Tāwhiri-mātea

Pronunciation Tah-wee-ree MAH-teh-ah (approximate; Māori vowels: 'a' as in 'father', 'i' as in 'machine', 'u' as in 'rule', 'e' as in 'egg', 'o' as in 'or'). The macron over the 'ā' indicates a long vowel sound.

Known As

TāwhiriTāwhiri-mateaTāwhirimāteaTāwhiri-mātea (with macrons)Tawhiri-ma-teaTāwhiri-mātea (wind god)

Etymology

The name is often interpreted as 'the fair wind' or 'the wind that is gentle', but more commonly understood as 'the fierce wind' or 'the storm'. 'Tāwhiri' relates to wind or weather, and 'mātea' can mean 'clear' or 'white', but in this context it is often glossed as 'fierce' or 'stormy'. Some scholars suggest it means 'the wind that is distant' or 'the wind that is separate'.

Cultural Ownership

Māori (indigenous people of Aotearoa/New Zealand). Tāwhiri-mātea is a prominent figure in the mythology of all iwi (tribes), though specific stories and attributes may vary regionally.

Disputed Identifications

There is some variation among iwi regarding the exact genealogy and role of Tāwhiri-mātea. Some traditions place him as the eldest son of Ranginui and Papatūānuku, others as one of the younger. His role as god of winds is consistent, but some traditions also associate him with storms, clouds, and even the sun's heat. No major disputed identifications with other deities are documented, but his relationship with Tāne (god of forests) is sometimes portrayed as more reconciliatory in some versions.

Archive Dossier — Approach

Before the sky was lifted from the earth, before the forests grew and the seas were filled, there was Tāwhiri-mātea, the breath of the storm. He is the Māori god of winds and weather, the fierce child of Ranginui and Papatūānuku, whose rage at the separation of his parents unleashed the first tempests upon the world. In the whirling winds and crashing waves, his presence is still felt, a force of nature both feared and revered.

Who It Is

Tāwhiri-mātea is the Māori god of winds, storms, and weather — one of the children of Rangi-nui (Sky Father) and Papa-tū-ā-nuku (Earth Mother). He is unique among the separating children of Rangi and Papa: when Tāne forced his parents apart, Tāwhiri sided with his father Rangi. Enraged by the separation and by his brothers' betrayal, Tāwhiri unleashed the fury of the winds against them. He created the great storms, hurricanes, squalls, and the driving rain to attack his brothers. He pursued Tāne (god of forests) with winds that felled giant trees, chased Tangaroa (god of the sea) into the depths with towering waves, and pursued Rongo and Haumia until they hid in their mother Papa's body. Only Tūmatauenga (war god) withstood Tāwhiri's assaults. Tāwhiri is the father of winds, each with a specific direction and aspect: the north wind, south wind, east wind, west wind, and the whirlwind. After his defeat of the other brothers (except Tū), Tāwhiri retreated to be with his father in the sky, where he occasionally unleashes storms to remind his brothers of his power. Maori navigators and farmers watched Tāwhiri carefully to predict weather.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

Tāwhiri-mātea is not described in detail in most accounts. He is often personified as the wind itself, or as a powerful, invisible force. Some later artistic depictions show him as a fierce warrior with windswept hair, but these are modern interpretations. In traditional Māori culture, he is more likely to be felt than seen, manifesting in the howling of the wind, the roar of the storm, and the fury of the sea.

Symbols & Iconography

The wind, storm clouds, thunder, lightning, and the tempestuous sea. He is associated with the four winds, and sometimes with specific wind directions. His 'war parties' are the wind gusts and storms that he sends. In some carvings, he may be represented by spiral patterns symbolizing wind or whirlwinds.

Behaviour & Personality

Tāwhiri-mātea is depicted as a fierce, vengeful, and powerful deity. He is deeply loyal to his parents, particularly his father Ranginui, and his anger at the separation of his parents leads him to wage war against his brothers. He is relentless in his pursuit of vengeance, and his storms are a constant reminder of his power. He is also seen as a protector of his father, keeping the sky separate from the earth.

Domains & Habitats

He resides in the sky, particularly in the upper realms where winds originate. He commands the winds, clouds, storms, and weather phenomena. He is also associated with the sea, as his winds whip up waves and storms at sea.

Version Differences

In some regional traditions, the order of the war between Tāwhiri-mātea and his brothers differs, or the outcome is different. For example, in some versions, Tāwhiri-mātea defeats some brothers but is eventually overcome by Tāne or Tumatauenga. In others, he continues to battle them eternally. The specific list of brothers he attacks also varies; some accounts include Rongo (god of cultivated food), others omit him. Some traditions also give him a more peaceful aspect, as the bringer of gentle breezes, but the dominant theme is his stormy nature.

Origins

Tāwhiri is the son of Rangi and Papa. He alone among the brothers opposed the separation of his parents. He joined his father in the sky and created all the winds to attack his brothers. He will never be fully pacified.

Attested in Māori Oral Tradition / Various Tribal ChantsEra Pre-colonial Māori, recorded 19th century

Archive Dossier — Powers

Control over winds

Source Version General Māori tradition; specifically in the creation myth where he sends wind to attack his brothers.

Description

Tāwhiri-mātea commands all the winds of the world. He can summon gentle breezes or unleash violent gales, hurricanes, and tornadoes.

Limitations

His power is limited to the atmosphere and weather; he cannot directly affect the earth or sea except through wind-driven waves.

Contradictions

Some traditions attribute specific winds to other deities, but Tāwhiri-mātea is the overarching wind god.

Control over storms and weather

Source Version In the creation myth, he attacks his brothers with storms and tempests.

Description

He can create storms, thunder, lightning, rain, hail, and other weather phenomena. He uses these as weapons in his battles.

Limitations

His weather control is limited to the sky and its effects on the earth; he cannot directly control the earth's interior or the deep sea.

Contradictions

None known.

Summoning of wind hosts

Source Version In some accounts, he sends his children, the winds, to attack his brothers.

Description

He can summon his children, the various winds, to do his bidding. These winds are personified as his offspring or servants.

Limitations

The winds are independent entities but obey his command.

Contradictions

The names and number of his children vary.

Flight and speed

Source Version Implied in his nature as a wind god.

Description

As the wind, he can move at incredible speeds and travel anywhere in the sky.

Limitations

He is bound to the atmosphere; he cannot travel underground or underwater without creating storms.

Contradictions

None.

Ranginui (Sky Father)

Type FatherSource Creation myth, as recorded by Grey.

Description

Tāwhiri-mātea is the son of Ranginui. He is fiercely loyal to his father and opposes the separation. He follows Ranginui into the sky and fights to avenge the separation.

Version Note

Consistent across versions.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Papatūānuku (Earth Mother)

Type MotherSource Creation myth.

Description

Tāwhiri-mātea is the son of Papatūānuku. He loves his mother but is angry at the separation, and his storms often lash the earth, which is his mother's body.

Version Note

Consistent.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Tāne (god of forests)

Type Brother and enemySource Creation myth.

Description

Tāne is the brother who separated their parents. Tāwhiri-mātea attacks Tāne with violent winds, destroying his forests. They remain in opposition.

Version Note

In some versions, Tāwhiri-mātea is eventually defeated by Tāne, but the enmity persists.

Conflicting Accounts

Some traditions say Tāwhiri-mātea was never defeated, and the wind still battles the forest.

Tangaroa (god of the sea)

Type Brother and enemySource Creation myth.

Description

Tāwhiri-mātea attacks Tangaroa with storms, causing turbulent seas. Their conflict represents the battle between wind and sea.

Version Note

Consistent.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Tumatauenga (god of war)

Type Brother and rivalSource Creation myth.

Description

Tumatauenga is the only brother who withstands Tāwhiri-mātea's attacks. Their conflict is central to the myth, establishing Tumatauenga's supremacy as the god of war.

Version Note

In some versions, Tumatauenga defeats Tāwhiri-mātea, but the wind remains a force to be reckoned with.

Conflicting Accounts

The exact outcome of their battle varies.

Tāwhiri-mātea attacks Rongo with storms and floods, destroying his crops. Rongo is often hidden by Papatūānuku to protect him.
