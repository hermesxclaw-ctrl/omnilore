Who It Is

Before the sky was lifted from the earth, before the forests grew and the seas were filled, there was Tāwhiri-mātea, the breath of the storm. He is the Māori god of winds and weather, the fierce child of Ranginui and Papatūānuku, whose rage at the separation of his parents unleashed the first tempests upon the world. In the whirling winds and crashing waves, his presence is still felt, a force of nature both feared and revered.

Tāwhiri-mātea is depicted as a fierce, vengeful, and powerful deity. He is deeply loyal to his parents, particularly his father Ranginui, and his anger at the separation of his parents leads him to wage war against his brothers. He is relentless in his pursuit of vengeance, and his storms are a constant reminder of his power. He is also seen as a protector of his father, keeping the sky separate from the earth.

Powers
Control over winds

Tāwhiri-mātea commands all the winds of the world. He can summon gentle breezes or unleash violent gales, hurricanes, and tornadoes.

Limit: His power is limited to the atmosphere and weather; he cannot directly affect the earth or sea except through wind-driven waves.

Control over storms and weather

He can create storms, thunder, lightning, rain, hail, and other weather phenomena. He uses these as weapons in his battles.

Limit: His weather control is limited to the sky and its effects on the earth; he cannot directly control the earth's interior or the deep sea.

Summoning of wind hosts

He can summon his children, the various winds, to do his bidding. These winds are personified as his offspring or servants.

Limit: The winds are independent entities but obey his command.

Flight and speed

As the wind, he can move at incredible speeds and travel anywhere in the sky.

Limit: He is bound to the atmosphere; he cannot travel underground or underwater without creating storms.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Tāwhiri-mātea is not described in detail in most accounts. He is often personified as the wind itself, or as a powerful, invisible force. Some later artistic depictions show him as a fierce warrior with windswept hair, but these are modern interpretations. In traditional Māori culture, he is more likely to be felt than seen, manifesting in the howling of the wind, the roar of the storm, and the fury of the sea.

Symbols — The wind, storm clouds, thunder, lightning, and the tempestuous sea. He is associated with the four winds, and sometimes with specific wind directions. His 'war parties' are the wind gusts and storms that he sends. In some carvings, he may be represented by spiral patterns symbolizing wind or whirlwinds.

Domains — He resides in the sky, particularly in the upper realms where winds originate. He commands the winds, clouds, storms, and weather phenomena. He is also associated with the sea, as his winds whip up waves and storms at sea.

Accounts I
The Separation of Heaven and Earth and the War of the GodsRecorded by George Grey and other ethnographers from Māori oral tradition.

Mythological creation era

In the beginning, Ranginui (Sky Father) and Papatūānuku (Earth Mother) lay in a close embrace, their children trapped in the darkness between them. The children conspired to separate their parents to let in light. Tāne, god of forests, eventually succeeded in forcing them apart, pushing Ranginui high into the sky. Tāwhiri-mātea, who had opposed the separation, was enraged. He followed his father into the sky and, from there, launched a fierce attack on his brothers. He sent winds and storms against Tāne, tearing down his forests; he attacked Tangaroa, god of the sea, whipping up waves and causing chaos; he assailed Rongo, god of cultivated food, and Haumia, god of wild food, with storms and floods. Only Tumatauenga, god of war, stood firm and withstood the onslaught. The battle raged, and Tāwhiri-mātea's attacks continue to this day, as storms and winds batter the earth. The separation of heaven and earth allowed light to enter the world. Tāwhiri-mātea and the WhirlwindVarious regional traditions.

Unknown

Some stories describe Tāwhiri-mātea creating whirlwinds and tornados as a display of his power, often punishing those who displease him or as a reminder of his presence. These stories are less well-documented and vary by region. Whirlwinds are seen as his manifestations, and people may offer prayers to appease him.

Tāwhiri-mātea is the god of thunder and lightning. — He is primarily the god of winds and storms. Thunder and lightning may be associated with him, but they are not his exclusive domain. Some traditions attribute thunder to other deities, like Tāwhaki or Whaitiri.

Tāwhiri-mātea is an evil god. — He is not evil; he is a force of nature. His actions are driven by loyalty to his father and anger at the separation. He is a complex figure, not a villain.

Tāwhiri-mātea is the son of Tāne. — He is the son of Ranginui and Papatūānuku, and thus the brother of Tāne.
