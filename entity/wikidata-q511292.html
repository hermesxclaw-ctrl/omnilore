Form

There are no known surviving depictions of Anzar from antiquity. He is not represented in human or animal form in any confirmed artifact. His 'appearance' is conceptual: he is the sky itself, the clouds, and the life-giving rain. In ritual, he is often represented symbolically by a stone, a branch, or a bowl of water.

Powers

Rain-making His primary and defining power. He controls the rain, and by extension, the fertility of the land and the survival of the community. Attested in all known Berber traditions. He can be persuaded through ritual, but is not always predictable. He is not a universally benevolent god; he must be appeased and courted. None on his core power, but the methods to invoke it vary by region.

Control over Sky and Weather As a sky god, he commands thunder, lightning, clouds, and storms, which are seen as manifestations of his presence or actions. Attested in the general conception of him as a sky deity. His power is focused on the atmospheric realm, not on the underworld or the sea (though rain feeds the latter). Some scholars argue he is a purely local rain spirit, not a universal sky god.

Bringer of Fertility By sending rain, he ensures the growth of crops and the fertility of flocks, making him a central figure in agricultural life. Inferred from his role in agricultural rituals. His fertility is dependent on his rain-making. He is not a god of animal or human fecundity directly. None.

Behavior & Habitat

Anzar is a weather deity, responsible for sending rain. He is portrayed as a powerful, sometimes capricious force of nature. When pleased, he brings fertility and abundance; when angered or neglected, he withholds rain, causing drought, famine, and hardship. His behavior is often anthropomorphized in ritual as that of a bridegroom seeking a bride.

Accounts

In times of severe drought, a ritual was performed to force Anzar to send rain. A young, unmarried woman (or a man dressed as a bride) was chosen to be the 'Bride of Anzar'. She was dressed in bridal finery and led in a procession through the village to a local saint's shrine, a spring, or a riverbed. The procession was accompanied by a specific chant, 'Anzar, Anzar, give us water.' The bride was then either symbolically married to the sky god by pouring water over her, or in some versions, she was dunked in the water or had a bowl of water poured over her head, symbolizing the consummation of the marriage and the subsequent release of rain. The ritual was often joyful but also carried a sense of desperation and urgency.

Anzar, the rain-giver, is implicitly in a state of tension with the sun, which dries the land. The rain ritual can be seen as a plea for Anzar to overcome the sun's power. This is not an explicit narrative but is a logical and thematic undercurrent of the rituals.

What We Don't Know

All primary evidence is from ethnographic records of the late 19th and early 20th centuries. We have no pre-Islamic Berber texts or inscriptions that mention Anzar by name. The exact nature of his pre-Roman cult, his mythology, and his iconography (if any) remain unknown. The full geographic extent of his worship is also not fully mapped. Research on the living tradition is needed to see if any remnants of the ritual survive in remote areas today.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Anzar as the ultimate long-distance boyfriend. He lives far away (in the sky), he's moody and unpredictable, and he only shows up when he feels like it. The entire community has to dress up, throw a party, and offer him a symbolic bride just to get his attention and hope he finally commits to sending some rain. He's the original 'dry texter'.
