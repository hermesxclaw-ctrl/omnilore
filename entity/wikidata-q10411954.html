Who It Is

Before Alexander was 'the Great,' before he conquered Egypt, the Alexander Romance claims he was conceived in a most unusual way: not by a king of Macedon, but by a desperate, fleeing Egyptian pharaoh who used magic to impersonate a god and seduce his own trusting wife, Anippe. She is the forgotten queen at the heart of a world-conquering lie—a woman whose only crime was being beautiful, pious, and married to a man who saw her as a vessel for his ambition. Her story is a dark, fascinating prequel to one of history's most enduring legends.

Anippe is portrayed as a dutiful, loyal, and trusting wife. She is devoted to her husband, Nectanebo. Her primary action is to confide in him about her fear of being abandoned. This vulnerability makes her susceptible to his scheme. She is depicted as pious and believing in the gods, as she is willing to accept the divine origin of her dream visitor. Her behavior is that of a virtuous, unsuspecting queen, a perfect victim for the manipulative king.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Anippe appears to be of Greek origin. It is likely a Hellenized form of an ancient Egyptian name, though the exact Egyptian root is not definitively attested in available sources. The suffix '-ippe' is common in Greek renderings of foreign names, often associated with horses (from 'hippos')

Pronunciation — An-IP-ee (stress on the second syllable, with a long 'e' as in 'see')

Powers
Royal Status

As the queen of Egypt, she holds the highest rank in the land. This status is what makes her a symbol of Egypt itself and her child a potential heir to the throne.

Limit: Her power is entirely dependent on her husband. She has no independent authority or agency.

Beauty

Her beauty is the catalyst for the entire plot. It makes her desirable to Nectanebo and is the reason he chooses her as the vessel for his son.

Limit: It is a passive power that makes her a target, not an active force she wields.

Dreams and Prophecy (Indirect)

Anippe is the recipient of a divine dream prophecy. While she does not control this power, she is the conduit through which the prophecy of Alexander's divine birth is delivered. This makes her a passive oracle.

Limit: She has no control over the dream or its meaning. She is a vessel for divine communication.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Anippe is described as the beautiful wife of the pharaoh Nectanebo II. The text emphasizes her beauty as the catalyst for the plot. She is presented as a queen of Egypt, likely envisioned in regal attire appropriate to her station. The text does not provide a detailed physical description, but her beauty is a key plot point, making her desirable to both the king and the magician.

Symbols — In the context of the Alexander Romance, Anippe symbolizes the subjugated, passive, and fertile land of Egypt. She represents the nation that is conquered and impregnated by a foreign power (the Greek-speaking world) through deception. Her dream encounter with the god Ammon (Amun), a central Egyptian deity, symbolizes the divine sanction and legitimacy of the future conqueror, Alexander. Accounts I
The Seduction of AnippeThe Alexander Romance, Book I, Chapter 2-3

Composed c. 3rd century CE, set in the 4th century BCE

The last native Egyptian pharaoh, Nectanebo II, is a powerful magician. He learns from his magical arts that his kingdom is doomed to be conquered by the Persians. Rather than face this fate, he flees Egypt and travels to the court of Philip II of Macedon. There, he uses his magic to seduce Philip's wife, Olympias, by appearing to her as the god Ammon. Before this, however, the text recounts an earlier episode: Nectanebo, while still in Egypt, is captivated by the beauty of his own wife, Anippe. He desires to father a son who would be a great conqueror. He tells her that she will be visited by the god Ammon, and that she will bear his divine child. He then uses his magical arts to appear to her in the form of the god Ammon, complete with the god's ram's horns, and sleeps with her. Anippe, believing she has been visited by a god, is honored and keeps the encounter secret. She becomes pregnant with Alexander. This story establishes Alexander's true paternity (as the son of the magician-king) while providing a divine cover story (as the son of Ammon) that legitimizes his rule over Egypt

Accounts II
The Birth of the Pharaoh's Son and the Claim to EgyptThe Alexander Romance (Pseudo-Callisthenes), Book I; Ethiopic and Syriac recensions

Composed c. 3rd century CE; set in the 4th century BCE

After Nectanebo II, disguised as the god Ammon, sleeps with Anippe, she believes she has been honored by a divine visitation and keeps the encounter secret. When she later bears a son, the child is presented as the offspring of a god, a claim that becomes central to his legitimacy. In the Alexander Romance, this boy grows up to be Alexander the Great, and the story of his Egyptian divine paternity is used to explain how a Macedonian conqueror could also be accepted as the legitimate heir of the native pharaohs. The romance weaves Anippe's child into a double inheritance: by blood he is the son of the Egyptian magician-king Nectanebo, and by divine claim he is the son of Ammon (Amun), the chief god of Egypt. Anippe herself fades from the narrative once her role as the vessel of this prophetic birth is fulfilled, a pattern typical of such foundational-foundling tales where the mother's main function is to transmit royal and divine legitimacy. Anippe was a real Egyptian queen. — There is no historical evidence for her existence. She is a fictional character from the Alexander Romance, a Hellenistic novel.

Anippe is the same as Olympias, Alexander's mother. — They are distinct characters. Olympias is the wife of Philip II of Macedon, while Anippe is the wife of Nectanebo II. The Alexander Romance gives Alexander two different mothers in two different episodes.

Anippe was a powerful sorceress or a participant in the magical plot. — Anippe is a passive victim. She has no magical abilities and is unaware of the deception being practiced upon her. She is a pawn in Nectanebo's scheme.
