Who It Is

A scholar sits alone at midnight, brush poised above a half-finished essay, when the paper window slides open and a woman of impossible beauty steps through. Her skin glows like moonlight, her laughter ripples the lamplight, and with a single glance she seems to know every secret he has tried to bury beneath his books. By dawn, he will be transformed—rescued, ruined, or something in between—and the fox spirit who visited him will vanish into the long, tangled history of Chinese imagination. The huli jing, the fox spirit of nine transformations, is never just one thing: demon and deity, lover and predator, scapegoat and savior, it prowls the borderlands of Chinese myth, literature, and everyday speech, leaving pawprints wherever desire and danger meet.

The personality of the huli jing is profoundly ambivalent and varies by period and genre. In early zhiguai and some sections of Shan Hai Jing, foxes are strange but not necessarily evil; they can bring fortune or misfortune depending on how humans treat them. By the Tang and Song dynasties, fox spirits increasingly appear as seductresses who enter scholars’ studies at night, offering companionship, sexual pleasure, and sometimes literary inspiration, while secretly draining their yang essence. Powers
Shapeshifting into human form

The most fundamental power of the huli jing is shapeshifting, particularly into human form. Classical and vernacular tales describe foxes that, after decades or centuries of cultivation, can assume the appearance of a young woman or, less commonly, a man. This transformation is often perfect, fooling even close observers, though certain signs—such as a lingering tail, animal tracks, or a reflection revealing fur—may betray the fox’s true nature. Shapeshifting allows the fox to enter human society, form relationships, and pursue its own goals of cultivation, pleasure, or revenge. In some stories, foxes can also transform objects, create illusions of houses or banquets, or alter the perceived environment around their victims. This ability is attested in numerous Liaozhai zhiyi tales and earlier zhiguai records.

Limit: Transformation typically requires long-term cultivation and is not available to ordinary foxes. Some tales suggest that foxes must avoid certain ritual objects (such as talismans or mirrors) that can reveal or disrupt their disguise. Strong yang energy, religious power, or exorcistic rites can force a fox back into animal form.

Absorption of vital essence (yang qi)

Many narratives describe huli jing as feeding on human vital essence, particularly the yang energy of men. This is often linked to sexual encounters: the fox seduces a scholar, and over time he becomes pale, exhausted, and ill, his life force drained. In some stories, the fox explicitly states that it needs human essence to continue its cultivation toward immortality. The mechanism is sometimes metaphorical, sometimes quasi-medical, reflecting traditional Chinese theories of qi and sexual energy. This power positions the fox as both lover and predator, blurring the line between intimacy and consumption.

Limit: Foxes risk attracting attention if they drain too much essence, leading to the victim’s death and potential intervention by exorcists or officials. Some tales show foxes moderating their feeding or even refraining from it out of affection, suggesting that this power is not always exercised.

Illusion and glamour

Huli jing frequently employ illusion to create false environments, conjure phantom banquets, or appear and disappear at will. Scholars may find themselves in a splendid house that later proves to be a ruined temple, or enjoy rich food that turns out to be simple fare. The fox’s glamour extends to emotional manipulation: it can inspire intense love, obsession, or fear. These illusions are not always malicious; some foxes use them to comfort lonely scholars or to hide from hostile humans.

Limit: Illusions can be dispelled by ritual specialists, strong moral resolve, or the intervention of higher deities. Some tales suggest that foxes cannot maintain complex illusions indefinitely and must retreat before dawn.

Spiritual cultivation and longevity

Fox spirits are paradigmatic examples of non-human beings that achieve spiritual cultivation. By absorbing moonlight, practicing meditation, and sometimes feeding on human essence, a fox can live for centuries, gain multiple tails, and eventually ascend as a fox immortal (huxian) or fox god (hushen). Some tales describe foxes achieving a quasi-Buddhist or Daoist enlightenment, transcending their earlier predatory behavior. Others depict foxes seeking official recognition from celestial bureaucracies, becoming registered deities with specific domains. This cultivation path mirrors human religious practice but is pursued outside orthodox institutions.

Limit: Cultivation is precarious: foxes can be killed by humans, exorcised by priests, or punished by higher gods for misbehavior. Excessive indulgence in lust or cruelty may prevent a fox from ascending, trapping it in a demonic state.

Possession and influence (Daji case)

In Fengshen Yanyi, a nine-tailed fox spirit is sent by the goddess Nüwa to possess the body of Daji, a human woman, in order to punish King Zhou of Shang. Once inside Daji, the fox manipulates the king, encourages sadistic punishments, and contributes to the moral collapse of the dynasty. This narrative attributes to the fox the power of spiritual possession, political influence, and the ability to incite extreme cruelty. The fox’s power here is less about seduction of a single scholar and more about systemic corruption of a ruler and his court.

Limit: The fox operates under Nüwa’s command and is ultimately defeated when the Zhou forces overthrow King Zhou and the fox is exposed and killed. Its power is thus contingent on divine sanction and subject to cosmic retribution.

Minor magical abilities (healing, divination, protection)

In folk religion, fox spirits sometimes function as protective deities or oracular beings. Worshippers may seek their aid for healing, fertility, or fortune, and fox altars are associated with spirit-mediumship and divination. Stories recount foxes warning humans of impending danger, curing illnesses, or granting children to barren couples. These benevolent powers contrast with the predatory abilities emphasized in moralizing tales.

Limit: Fox aid is often conditional on proper offerings and respectful behavior. Failure to honor the fox can result in illness or misfortune, suggesting a transactional relationship.

Accounts I
Early multi-tailed fox in Shan Hai JingShan Hai Jing (Classic of Mountains and Seas), various pre-Han and Han layers with later commentaries.

Early references likely compiled between the 4th century BCE and 2nd century CE, with significant later editorial work.

One of the earliest textual attestations of a multi-tailed fox appears in Shan Hai Jing, where a nine-tailed fox (jiuweihu) is described as inhabiting a particular region. The creature is noted for its unusual appearance—multiple tails—and is sometimes associated with auspiciousness or fertility. The text does not yet present the fox as a seductive demon but as a strange, powerful animal whose presence signals a particular kind of landscape and its numinous qualities. Later commentators and readers retroactively connect this nine-tailed fox to the broader tradition of huli jing, treating it as an ancestral form of the fox spirit archetype. The Shan Hai Jing reference establishes a textual foundation for later associations between foxes, multiple tails, and spiritual power. It also anchors the fox in a cosmological geography, linking it to specific mountains and regions. Over time, this early depiction is reinterpreted through the lens of later demonological and romantic fox tales.

Zhiguai tales of fox seductressesTang and Song dynasty zhiguai collections (records of anomalies) and later miscellanies.

Approximately 7th–13th centuries CE.

In numerous zhiguai tales, foxes appear as seductive women who visit scholars at night. A typical story involves a lonely scholar studying for the imperial examinations in a remote house. A beautiful woman arrives, claiming to be a neighbor or traveler, and offers companionship. Over time, the scholar becomes increasingly weak and ill, until a friend, servant, or exorcist discovers that the woman is a fox spirit. Ritual intervention or physical confrontation drives the fox away, and the scholar either recovers or dies. These tales emphasize the dangers of lust, the vulnerability of scholars, and the porous boundary between human and spirit worlds. Such stories reinforce moral lessons about sexual restraint and vigilance against demonic temptation. They also contribute to the emerging stereotype of the huli jing as a life-draining seductress. At the same time, they reflect social anxieties about examination failure, isolation, and the precarious status of literati.

Pu Songling’s fox maidens in Liaozhai zhiyiLiaozhai zhiyi (Strange Stories from a Chinese Studio) by Pu Songling.

Late 17th century (Qing dynasty), with printed editions in the 18th century.

Pu Songling’s Liaozhai zhiyi contains numerous stories featuring fox maidens, including well-known tales such as "Xiaocui" and "Yingning." In these narratives, fox spirits form complex relationships with human scholars. Xiaocui, for example, is a fox who becomes the devoted partner of a failed scholar, supporting him emotionally and materially, and ultimately contributing to his success. Yingning is a fox maiden whose laughter and innocence challenge social norms. These stories often invert gender expectations: male scholars are physically fragile and emotionally dependent, while fox women are strong, resourceful, and capable of mutual salvation. The foxes are not merely demons but agents of transformation, revealing the limitations of human society and offering alternative models of love and partnership. Liaozhai’s fox tales reshape the huli jing archetype, emphasizing ambivalence and mutual salvation rather than simple predation. Accounts II
Daji and the fall of the Shang in Fengshen YanyiFengshen Yanyi (Investiture of the Gods), a Ming dynasty novel.

Likely composed in the 16th century, drawing on earlier legends.

Fengshen Yanyi narrates the downfall of the Shang dynasty and the rise of the Zhou, framing the story as a cosmic struggle involving gods, immortals, and demons. A central figure is Daji, the beautiful daughter of a general who becomes the consort of King Zhou of Shang. In the novel, the goddess Nüwa sends a nine-tailed fox spirit to possess Daji as punishment for the king’s disrespect. Under the fox’s influence, Daji encourages King Zhou to indulge in extreme cruelty and decadence, including the infamous "paolao" torture device and other sadistic entertainments. The kingdom descends into moral chaos, prompting righteous forces to rebel. Eventually, the fox is exposed and killed, and Daji’s role in the dynasty’s collapse becomes emblematic of the dangerous, seductive huli jing. The Daji narrative cements the association between fox spirits and political corruption, cruelty, and the downfall of dynasties. Fox immortal cults in North ChinaEthnographic and religious studies of fox worship in North China.

Late imperial to modern periods (19th–20th centuries and beyond).

In various regions of North China, fox spirits are worshipped as local deities or household gods. Temples and shrines dedicated to fox immortals (huxian) host rituals for healing, divination, and protection. Spirit-mediums may be possessed by fox entities, delivering oracles and advice. Worshippers offer incense, food, and paper money, seeking blessings or relief from illness. These practices treat fox spirits as ambivalent but powerful beings who can be propitiated rather than simply feared. The fox’s liminal status makes it an effective mediator between human communities and the unseen world. Fox cults complicate the purely demonic image of huli jing, embedding them in everyday religious life as pragmatic deities. They also provoke conflict with orthodox religious authorities, who sometimes condemn fox worship as heterodox or superstitious. The persistence of these cults into the modern era demonstrates the fox spirit’s adaptability and deep cultural resonance.

Modern romantic fox dramas and novelsContemporary Chinese television dramas, web novels, and fantasy literature.

Late 20th century to present.

Modern media frequently reimagine huli jing as romantic protagonists or antagonists in fantasy dramas and novels. Television series and web fiction depict fox spirits as beautiful immortals navigating love, betrayal, and cosmic politics. These stories often draw loosely on Liaozhai motifs and the Daji legend but reshape them for contemporary audiences, emphasizing emotional depth, personal choice, and spectacular magic. Fox heroines may struggle between their demonic nature and human love, seeking redemption or a peaceful life. Male fox spirits also appear as enigmatic lovers or rivals. The trickster aspect remains, but the moral framing is more flexible, allowing fox characters to be heroes, antiheroes, or tragic figures. These modern narratives expand the huli jing’s reach beyond traditional folklore, embedding it in global fantasy culture and online fandom. They also influence popular perceptions of fox spirits, often softening their darker aspects and foregrounding romance and personal growth.
