Hræsvelgr sits in eagle's skin at the world's end, and when he stirs his great wings, the winds arise.

Who It Is

Hræsvelgr ('Corpse-Swallower') is a colossal jötunn who sits at the northern edge of the sky in the form of an eagle. According to the Vafþrúðnismál, when he beats his wings, the wind blows across the world. He is thus the source of all wind in Norse cosmology — a giant eagle whose wingbeats drive the weather. His name ('corpse-swallower') connects him to the battlefield and the death-goddess Hel, suggesting that the wind he creates carries the souls of the dead or represents the exhalation of the underworld. Hræsvelgr's eagle form associates him with the great eagle that sits atop Yggdrasil and the unnamed eagle who battles Níðhöggr at the roots of the World Tree — though Hræsvelgr may be a separate figure from the Yggdrasil eagle. Some scholars identify Hræsvelgr with the unnamed giant mentioned in Völuspá who carries the slain to Hel. Unlike the anthropomorphic giants, Hræsvelgr exists in a purely elemental form — a giant who has become a natural force rather than a personality. He represents the Norse understanding that the most powerful beings in the cosmos are not the gods but the natural forces: the wind, the fire, the frost — impersonal, vast, and indifferent to human affairs.

Attested in Poetic Edda (Vafþrúðnismál); Prose Edda (Gylfaginning)Era Viking Age (c. 800–1100 CE); recorded 13th century

Form

Hræsvelgr is described as a jötunn (giant) who takes the form of an eagle. The text specifies that he sits 'at the end of heaven' (or 'at the edge of the sky'), suggesting a position at the northern or outermost boundary of the world. His eagle form is not merely a disguise but is integral to his function: the wind is generated by the beating of his wings. He is not described in further physical detail in the surviving sources.

Powers

Wind Generation The beating of Hræsvelgr's eagle wings is the direct cause of all wind that blows over the world of humans (Miðgarðr). The text says 'from his wings, they say, the wind comes over all men.' Vafþrúðnismál, stanza 37 (Poetic Edda) The wind is a passive byproduct of his existence, not a directed or willed act. He does not control storms, lightning, or thunder; he simply is, and wind results. This is an etiological explanation, not a combat ability. The text does not specify whether the wind comes from all his wings or only when he beats them, nor does it describe a cycle of wind intensity. Later sources do not expand on this.

Accounts

Óðinn, disguised as the traveler Gagnráðr, engages in a wisdom contest with the giant Vafþrúðnir. Among the questions asked is: 'Whence does the wind come, so that it passes over the sea? Men do not see it itself.' Vafþrúðnir answers that Hræsvelgr, a jötunn in eagle's form, sits at the end of heaven, and the wind comes from his wings. This is the entirety of the narrative involving Hræsvelgr.

Snorri Sturluson expands on the Eddic account, placing Hræsvelgr at the northernmost edge of the world. He sits in giant-eagle form at the boundary where the heavens meet the earth, and when he flaps his wings to take flight, the winds rush out from beneath them and sweep across the world. His position at the cosmic boundary connects him to the primordial realm of the giants (Jötunheimr) and the chaotic forces that exist at the margins of the ordered Norse cosmos. When the winds are especially fierce, it is said that Hræsvelgr is agitated, perhaps stirred by events in the world below.

Field Notes

۩ Hræsvelgr appears in exactly one stanza of one poem in the entire surviving corpus of Norse mythology.

۩ His name, 'Corpse-Swallower,' is one of the most metal names in all of mythology, yet he is not a villain or a monster — he's just a giant eagle doing his job.

۩ The Prose Edda, Snorri Sturluson's comprehensive handbook of Norse mythology, does not mention Hræsvelgr at all, suggesting he may have been a more obscure figure even in the medieval period.

۩ In the video game God of War Ragnarök, the 'Corpse Eater' is a giant eagle at the edge of the world whose wings generate wind — a direct, if loose, adaptation of Hræsvelgr.

۩ The name is so evocative that it has been borrowed for a dark magic spell in the Fire Emblem series and a dragon in Final Fantasy XIV.

Content Notes

The etymology of the name is the only connection to death or corpses. The text provides no narrative context for this name. It may imply a lost myth or simply a poetic, evocative epithet for the north wind, which was associated with cold and death.

Scholars note the name's ominous tone but have no evidence of a 'corpse-eating' myth. It may be a poetic kenning for the wind that sweeps over battlefields and burial mounds, or it may be a remnant of an older, now-lost tradition.

What We Don't Know

The primary gap is the lack of any narrative context for Hræsvelgr beyond his single mention. We do not know why he is called 'Corpse-Swallower,' whether he had any interactions with other beings, or if he played a role in Ragnarök. The Prose Edda's omission of him is notable. Research is limited by the survival of only one primary source. Further investigation could explore later Scandinavian folklore for possible reflexes of a wind-eagle belief.

A way to hold it in your mind

Hræsvelgr is not a character in a story; he is a force of nature personified. Think of him less like Zeus or Thor, and more like a mythological embodiment of gravity or the rotation of the Earth. He is the 'why' behind the wind, a poetic explanation for a natural phenomenon. He is the ultimate background character — the world would be different without him, but he never does anything. He just sits there, at the edge of everything, and the wind blows.
