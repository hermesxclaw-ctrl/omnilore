Who It Is

At the very edge of the world, where the sky meets the unknown, sits a giant in the form of an eagle. He does not hunt, he does not fight, he does not speak. He simply is, and the wind that howls across your face on a cold winter's day is the byproduct of his existence. His name is Hræsvelgr, the Corpse-Swallower, and he is one of the most enigmatic and evocative figures in all of Norse mythology — a cosmic functionary whose very being shapes the world, yet who appears in only a single stanza of a single poem, forever silent, forever sitting, forever generating the breath of the world.

The texts do not describe Hræsvelgr's personality or behavior beyond his static posture: he sits at the edge of heaven and generates wind. He is not portrayed as interacting with gods or other beings, nor does he engage in the typical giant-god conflicts. He is a cosmic functionary, an embodiment of a natural phenomenon, rather than an active character.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name is a compound of Old Norse hræ ('corpse') and svelgr ('swallower', 'devourer', 'gulper'). The most common scholarly translation is 'Corpse-Swallower' or 'Corpse-Gulper', though some render it as 'Corpse-Eater'. The name is attested in the Poetic Edda, specifically in the poem Vafþrúðnismál.

Pronunciation — Approximately /ˈhrɛːsˌwɛlɡər/ or /ˈhræsˌwɛlɡər/ in reconstructed Old Norse. A common anglicized approximation is 'HRESS-vel-gr'.

Aliases — Hræsvelg, Hraesvelgr, Hräswelgr, Corpse-Swallower, Corpse-Gulper, Corpse-Eater

Powers
Wind Generation

The beating of Hræsvelgr's eagle wings is the direct cause of all wind that blows over the world of humans (Miðgarðr). The text says 'from his wings, they say, the wind comes over all men.'

Limit: The wind is a passive byproduct of his existence, not a directed or willed act. He does not control storms, lightning, or thunder; he simply is, and wind results. This is an etiological explanation, not a combat ability.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Hræsvelgr is described as a jötunn (giant) who takes the form of an eagle. The text specifies that he sits 'at the end of heaven' (or 'at the edge of the sky'), suggesting a position at the northern or outermost boundary of the world. His eagle form is not merely a disguise but is integral to his function: the wind is generated by the beating of his wings. He is not described in further physical detail in the surviving sources.

Symbols — The primary symbol is the eagle itself, specifically a giant eagle at the world's edge. In Norse cosmology, eagles are associated with the upper realms, wisdom, and the world-tree (cf. the unnamed eagle in Grímnismál). However, Hræsvelgr's eagle is distinguished by its wind-generating function, which is unique to him in the surviving corpus. Domains — His domain is the northern edge of the sky, the boundary between the known world and the unknown beyond. This placement suggests a primal, elemental role in the cosmic geography. He is not associated with any particular hall, realm, or dwelling place other than this liminal sky-boundary.

Accounts I
The Riddle of the WindVafþrúðnismál, stanzas 36-38 (Poetic Edda)

Poem likely composed c. 10th-11th century CE; recorded in the Codex Regius c. 1270s CE

Óðinn, disguised as the traveler Gagnráðr, engages in a wisdom contest with the giant Vafþrúðnir. Among the questions asked is: 'Whence does the wind come, so that it passes over the sea? Men do not see it itself.' Vafþrúðnir answers that Hræsvelgr, a jötunn in eagle's form, sits at the end of heaven, and the wind comes from his wings. This is the entirety of the narrative involving Hræsvelgr. The answer is part of Vafþrúðnir's demonstration of his vast knowledge, which ultimately proves insufficient, as Óðinn asks a question only he can answer (what Óðinn whispered into the ear of the slain Baldr), leading to Vafþrúðnir's defeat and implied death.

Accounts II
Hræsvelgr at the Edge of the WorldSnorri Sturluson, Prose Edda (Gylfaginning); Vafþrúðnismál, Poetic Edda

10th-13th century CE

Snorri Sturluson expands on the Eddic account, placing Hræsvelgr at the northernmost edge of the world. He sits in giant-eagle form at the boundary where the heavens meet the earth, and when he flaps his wings to take flight, the winds rush out from beneath them and sweep across the world. His position at the cosmic boundary connects him to the primordial realm of the giants (Jötunheimr) and the chaotic forces that exist at the margins of the ordered Norse cosmos. When the winds are especially fierce, it is said that Hræsvelgr is agitated, perhaps stirred by events in the world below. The story reinforces the Norse understanding of natural phenomena as the actions of supernatural beings at the world's edges. The winds, essential for sailing and unpredictable in their force, are given a mythic origin that explains both their regularity and their occasional violence.

Hræsvelgr is the eagle perched on top of Yggdrasill. — This is a modern conflation. The Yggdrasill eagle is unnamed in Grímnismál and is associated with wisdom, not wind. Hræsvelgr's sole function is to generate wind.

Hræsvelgr causes the wind by flapping his wings. — The text says the wind comes 'from his wings' (af vængjum hans), which implies a passive generation or a byproduct of his posture, not a deliberate flapping action. The text does not describe him flapping.

Hræsvelgr is a god. — He is a jötunn (giant), a member of the race of beings who are often, but not always, antagonists to the gods. He is not worshipped or counted among the Æsir or Vanir.
