Who It Is

Before the thrones of Olympus, before Zeus wielded the lightning, there was a serpent who coiled around the world itself — Ophion, the primordial ruler of heaven, whose name was whispered only in the oldest of songs.

As a primordial ruler, Ophion is portrayed as powerful and proud, but ultimately overthrown by a younger generation of gods. His role is largely passive in surviving myths, serving as a symbol of the pre-Olympian order.

Primordial sovereignty

Ophion held dominion over the cosmos before the Titans, symbolizing the primordial order.

Limit: His power was usurped by Cronus and Rhea, indicating he was not invincible.

Shape-shifting

As a serpent deity, Ophion may have possessed the ability to change form, though this is not explicitly attested.

Limit: Not directly attested in sources.

Cosmic knowledge

Serpents in Greek myth often symbolize hidden wisdom; Ophion may have possessed esoteric knowledge as a primordial being.

Limit: No direct evidence.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Ophion is typically depicted as a colossal serpent or dragon-like being, sometimes with human-like features or the ability to take human form. In some accounts, he is described as a great serpent coiled around the world or the cosmic axis.

Symbols — The serpent itself is the primary symbol, often associated with primordial chaos, wisdom, and cosmic power. In art, he may be shown entwined with Eurynome, or as a celestial dragon.

Domains — He ruled over Olympus or the heavens before the Titans, and after his defeat, he was cast into Oceanus, the world-encircling river, or into Tartarus.

Accounts I
The Rule of Ophion and EurynomeOrphic fragments; Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 1.503-511

Archaic/Classical period (tradition), recorded in Hellenistic era

In the beginning, Ophion and his consort Eurynome, daughter of Oceanus, ruled over Olympus. They were the first sovereigns of the cosmos, before the Titans. However, Cronus and Rhea, the Titan siblings, overthrew them, casting Ophion into the waves of Oceanus and seizing power. The Titans assumed dominion over the cosmos, which later led to the Titanomachy and the rise of Zeus.

Accounts II
Ophion and the FloodLycophron, Alexandra 1191

3rd century BCE

Lycophron alludes to a myth in which Ophion, a serpent, is associated with a great flood, possibly the same flood that Deucalion survived. The serpent may have been a cause or a survivor of the deluge. The flood myth is connected to the renewal of humanity.
