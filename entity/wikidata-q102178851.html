Who It Is

In the vast constellation of Japanese heroic legend, few names burn as brightly or as ambiguously as Mikoto Yamato—a figure whose very identity blurs the line between myth, history, and modern imagination. Whether a mortal prince, a divine warrior, or a symbol of imperial destiny, Yamato's story is a tapestry woven from ancient chronicles, medieval romances, and contemporary retellings, each layer adding a new facet to an already multifaceted hero.

Yamato Takeru is portrayed as impetuous, brave, and sometimes cruel. He deceives his enemies, kills without hesitation, and shows great sorrow when separated from his wife. His character is complex: a hero and a flawed human.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Yamato Takeru' translates roughly as 'The Brave of Yamato' or 'The Hero of Yamato,' reflecting his role as a central figure in the Yamato state's heroic tradition. 'Takeru' implies valor and martial prowess.

Pronunciation — yah-mah-toh tah-keh-roo

Aliases — Prince Ōsu (小碓命), Yamato-takeru-no-mikoto, Yamato Takeru, Ousu no Mikoto, The Brave of Yamato

Ownership — Japan; specifically the Yamato court and its descendants. He is a central figure in Shinto mythology and imperial tradition.

Powers
Superhuman strength and martial prowess

He defeats powerful enemies, including the chieftains of Kumaso and the rebel Takeru, through sheer combat skill.

Limit: None specified, but he is mortal and eventually succumbs to illness.

Divine protection and magical items

He receives a magical sword from the goddess of the sea, and his aunt Yamato-hime gives him a bag that allows him to summon the wind. He also uses the 'grass-cutting sword' to survive a fire.

Limit: The sword is given for a specific purpose; the bag's contents are limited.

Disguise and deception

He disguises himself as a woman to assassinate the Kumaso leaders.

Limit: Requires cunning and opportunity.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No canonical description exists in the primary sources. He is typically depicted in later art as a warrior prince in armor, often with a sword and a fierce expression.

Symbols — The sword 'Kusanagi-no-Tsurugi' (Grass-Cutting Sword) is his most iconic attribute, later one of the Three Imperial Regalia. He is also associated with the white bird that appears after his death.

Domains — He is associated with the Yamato region, but his travels take him across Japan, from the eastern provinces to the west. His death occurs on Mount Ibuki.

Accounts I
The Subjugation of the KumasoKojiki, Nihon Shoki

Mythical age (traditionally 1st-2nd century CE)

Prince Ōsu, later Yamato Takeru, is sent by his father Emperor Keikō to subdue the Kumaso people in Kyushu. He dresses as a woman, infiltrates a banquet, and kills the two Kumaso leaders, one of whom acknowledges his valor and gives him the name Yamato Takeru. Yamato Takeru gains fame, but his father, fearing his power, sends him on further campaigns to the east.

Accounts II
The Eastern CampaignKojiki, Nihon Shoki

Mythical age

Yamato Takeru is sent to pacify the eastern provinces. He defeats various enemies, including the rebellious Takeru of Izumo, and survives a treacherous fire on a moor by using the grass-cutting sword. He also falls in love with and marries Princess Miyazu, but later abandons her. He gains control of the east, but his arrogance leads to his eventual downfall.

The Death of Yamato TakeruKojiki, Nihon Shoki

Mythical age

After returning from the east, Yamato Takeru is challenged by the deity of Mount Ibuki. He ignores the deity's warning and is struck by a sudden illness. He declines, and his soul turns into a white bird that flies away. His death is mourned, and his sword is enshrined at Atsuta Shrine.
