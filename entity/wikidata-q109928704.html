Who It Is

There is no ancient ballad that names her — and that is the clue. Robina Goodfellow appears in the record not as a medieval fairy but as a modern, heroic-legend recasting of England's most famous hobgoblin, Robin Goodfellow, the shapeshifting Puck who swept hearths for cream and led travelers astray for a laugh.

Robina is classified as heroic-legend, implying a benevolent, protective recasting. Archetype Robin is ambivalent: helpful to tidy households (sweeps, churns, grinds malt) and cruel to the idle, lazy, or rude (pinches, misleads, spoils cream). Shakespeare's Puck is obedient to Oberon, mischievous but not malevolent, remorseful when his mistake causes harm (lovers' quarrel). Folklore Robin is more anarchic, sexually suggestive (kissing maids, fathering changelings in some tales), and mercenary. Powers
Shapeshifting (Metamorphosis)

Robin can transform into animals (horse, hound, hog, bear, ass), objects (stool, crab-apple), and mimic voices. In Midsummer he becomes an ass-headed weaver's double.

Limit: Transformations are temporary and often for pranks; cannot permanently become human; in some tales is trapped if his true name is spoken or if given clothes (brownie motif, disputed for Robin).

Superhuman Speed and Invisibility

Can 'put a girdle round about the earth in forty minutes' (Shakespeare 2.1.175), becomes invisible at will, enters locked houses.

Limit: Bound to night in folklore ('merry wanderer of the night'); daylight weakens or banishes him; must be summoned or propitiated with cream/bread.

Domestic Fairy Work (Household Blessing/Curse)

Sweeps house, churns butter, grinds malt, threshes corn for tidy maids; pinches, curdles milk, spoils ale, tangles hair for slovenly servants.

Limit: Requires offering (bowl of cream, bread, or milk left on hearth); offended if thanked in Christian terms or given clothes; work undone if watched.

Illusion, Glamour and Love-Philtre Handling

As Puck, administers Oberon's love-juice (juice of 'love-in-idleness' pansy) causing infatuation; creates fog, false sounds, and bewilderment to lead travelers astray (Will-o'-the-wisp function).

Limit: Cannot create true love, only misdirected lust; effect is temporary and reversible with antidote (Dian's bud); glamour fails against iron, church bells, or daylight (folklore).

Accounts I
The Birth of Robin Goodfellow (Son of Oberon and a Country Lass)Robin Good-Fellow, His Mad Prankes and Merry Jests (1628 pamphlet), Chapter 1

Early Stuart, reflecting late Elizabethan oral tradition

A comely country maid walking in the woods is seduced (or in some readings, raped) by Oberon, King of Fairy. She bears a child, Robin, who is hairy and laughing at birth and soon shows fairy powers. He is raised by his mother, alternately helps and shames her, and is eventually claimed by Oberon as his page. The tale establishes Robin's hybrid human-fairy status and justifies his liminal behavior. Explains Robin's dual nature and allegiance to fairy court; establishes template for later 'changeling' and 'fairy bastard' stories. For Robina, no equivalent origin story is attested; a modern version would likely gender-swap the child but no text was found.

Puck's Errand: The Love Potion MistakeWilliam Shakespeare, A Midsummer Night's Dream, Acts 2-4

c. 1595-1596

Oberon quarrels with Titania over an Indian changeling boy. He orders Puck (called Robin Goodfellow) to fetch the flower 'love-in-idleness' and anoint the eyes of Titania and the Athenian lovers so they fall in love with the first creature they see. Puck mistakes Lysander for Demetrius, causing both men to love Helena, leading to near-fatal quarrels. He later corrects the error with an antidote and, for sport, transforms Bottom's head into an ass's head, which Titania (under enchantment) adores. Canonical literary codification of Robin as Oberon's lieutenant; source of all later 'Puck as love-potion agent' iconography. Moral: fairy power is fallible and must be supervised.

The Dairy Pranks: Cream, Churning, and Pinching MaidsRobin Good-Fellow pamphlet Chapters 4-7; Reginald Scot Discoverie of Witchcraft 4.10; corroborated in Briggs (1976)

16th-17th century (oral roots older)

Robin haunts farmhouses. If maids leave a bowl of cream or white bread on the hearth, he churns butter, sweeps, and finishes chores overnight. If they are sluttish or fail to leave payment, he pinches them black and blue, tangles their hair into 'elf-locks,' curdles milk, lets the cream turn, or leads them into ditches. He also shape-shifts into a stool to make gossiping women fall. The stories are moral exempla about domestic diligence and propitiation. Established Robin as brownie-like household spirit; explains archaeological 'fairy bowls' and folk practice of leaving offerings. Sanitized Victorian versions omit pinching and sexual harassment.

Accounts II
Leading Travelers Astray (Will-o'-the-Wisp / Jack-o'-Lantern)Robin Good-Fellow pamphlet Chapter 9; Shakespeare 2.1.32-38 ('those that 'Hobgoblin' call... and 'sweet Puck'... are you not he / That frights the maidens... and mislead night wanderers'); Milton, Paradise Lost Book 9 reference to 'friar's lantern'

16th-17th century

At night Robin with a lantern or candle misleads travelers on heaths and commons, mimicking voices, laughing, and leaving them 'in bog and mire.' He also frightens maidens and skims milk. The same function is later assigned to Will-o'-the-wisp and Jack-o'-Lantern, with which Robin is conflated. Explains dangerous marsh lights; reinforces taboo against night travel; provides etiology for 'Puck-led' idiom.

Nymphidia: Puck in the Fairy CourtMichael Drayton, Nymphidia, The Court of Fairy (1627)

Jacobean

Drayton describes the fairy court in miniature: Oberon and Queen Mab (not Titania) preside, and 'Hobgoblin' (Robin) is the court's fool and messenger, riding a grasshopper. He is sent to punish mortals who neglect fairy rites. The poem synthesizes folk and Shakespearean traditions and popularizes the tiny, insect-riding fairy aesthetic. Bridges folk Robin and literary Puck; influences Victorian fairy painting (e.g., Richard Dadd).

Robina Goodfellow is an ancient English fairy queen or goddess. — No ancient or medieval source attests a female Robina Goodfellow. The name is masculine Robin Goodfellow in all early sources; Robina appears to be a 21st-century coinage. Fairy queens in early sources are Titania (Shakespeare's invention) or Mab, not Robina.

Robin Goodfellow was invented by Shakespeare. — Robin Goodfellow was proverbial before Shakespeare. Reginald Scot lists him in 1584, a decade before Midsummer. Shakespeare codified and popularized the Puck=Robin equation but did not invent the figure.

Puck and Robin Goodfellow have always been the same and are always harmless and cute. — The identity was debated and the figure was often frightening, hairy, and sexually threatening in early folklore; the cute, tiny, child-friendly Puck is a Victorian and Disney-era sanitization.
