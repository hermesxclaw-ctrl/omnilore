The Approach

In the shadowed groves of ancient Greece, where the boar's bristle and the myrtle's bloom intertwine, there dwelt a goddess known by a name that whispers of triple power—Triclaria. Though her worship has faded into the dust of millennia, her epithets and sanctuaries still echo in the fragments of poets and the stones of forgotten temples, inviting us to piece together the enigma of a deity who may have been Artemis herself, or a power wholly her own.

Content advisory. None. This entry concerns a fragmentary ancient Greek deity; no graphic or sensitive material is involved.
Identity

Triclaria

Pronunciation trih-KLAR-ee-uh (English approximation); in Greek: Τρικλαρία (Triklaria).

Etymology

The name 'Triclaria' is likely derived from Greek elements: 'tri-' meaning 'three' and 'klar-' possibly related to 'klaros' (lot, inheritance) or 'kleos' (glory), but the exact derivation is uncertain. It may signify 'thrice-called' or 'of the three lots,' though these are speculative.

Cultural Ownership

Ancient Greek, specifically associated with the region of Achaea in the Peloponnese, particularly the city of Patras.

Disputed Identifications

Some ancient sources and modern scholars identify Triclaria as an epithet of Artemis, while others suggest she might be a local, independent goddess who was later syncretized with Artemis. The evidence is insufficient to resolve this definitively.

Description

Appearance

No direct description of Triclaria's appearance survives. If she is an epithet of Artemis, she would share the typical iconography of Artemis: a young huntress, often depicted with a bow and arrows, wearing a short chiton, and accompanied by a deer or hunting dog. However, local variations might exist, and no specific image of Triclaria is known.

Symbols & Iconography

Possible symbols include the bow, arrow, deer, and hunting implements, consistent with Artemis. The name 'Triclaria' itself might have been associated with a triple form or a triple shrine, but this is speculative.

Behaviour & Personality

As an aspect of Artemis, she would be a virgin goddess of the hunt, wilderness, and childbirth, protective of young animals and women. She might also have had a local role as a protector of the city of Patras.

Domains & Habitats

Triclaria's cult was centered in Patras, Achaea, Greece. Likely sanctuaries included a temple or sacred grove, possibly near a spring or a natural feature. She would have been a local deity with a regional following.

Version Differences

The main difference is whether Triclaria was an independent goddess or an epithet of Artemis. Pausanias states that the Patrans called Artemis 'Triclaria,' but this could be a later syncretism. No other distinct versions or traditions are known.

Stories & Myths

Foundation of the Cult of Triclaria in Patras

Source Pausanias, Description of Greece 7.19Culture Ancient Greek (Achaean)Period Mythical/historical, possibly Archaic or earlier

Summary

Pausanias records that the people of Patras worshipped Artemis under the name Triclaria. He does not provide a founding myth, but it is likely that the cult was established in the city's early history, possibly brought by settlers or founded by a legendary figure. The festival in her honor may have included processions and sacrifices.

Contradictions & Disputes

Independent goddess vs. epithet of Artemis

Triclaria is solely an epithet of Artemis, as stated by Pausanias.

Position A — evidence

Pausanias's explicit statement.

Position B

Triclaria may have been an originally independent local goddess later syncretized with Artemis.

Position B — evidence

Scholarly Consensus

The majority accept Pausanias's identification, but the possibility of a pre-Artemis local cult remains open due to lack of evidence.
