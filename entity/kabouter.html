Who It Is

De Kabouter (plural Kabouters) is a gnome-like household and nature spirit from Dutch folklore, closely related to the German Kobold, the Scandinavian Tomte, and the English brownie. Kabouters are described as tiny, bearded old men, rarely more than 30 centimeters tall, wearing pointed red caps, blue or brown tunics, wooden clogs (klompen), and carrying small lanterns or tools. They inhabit three distinct environments: domestic spaces (where they help with household chores in return for food offerings), forests and fields (where they protect wild animals and plants), and underground (where they guard hidden treasures). Unlike the more mischievous German Kobold, the Dutch Kabouter is fundamentally benevolent. Errors or debts in accounts were traditionally blamed on Kabouters (accountants called small unexplained discrepancies 'kabouters'), and farmers left offerings of milk and bread in their barns to ensure the Kabouters' continued goodwill. The Dutch poet and physician Dr. Willem van den Hout (pseudonym: Jan van den Berg) popularized the Kabouter in modern literature through the beloved children's character Paulus de Boskabouter (Paulus the Forest Gnome) in the 1940s. In Dutch public culture, the Kabouter has become an iconic national figure, often used in public art and garden decorations. The 'Kabouter movement' (Kabouterbeweging) was also a 1970s Dutch progressive political movement that used the gnome as its symbol, advocating for environmentalism and participatory democracy. The word 'kabouter' is also the root of the English 'kobold' and the general term for gnome in Germanic languages.
