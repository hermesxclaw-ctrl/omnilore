Form

Each of the eight Bhairavas has distinct iconography, typically depicted with: multiple arms (usually 4 or 6), holding various weapons and implements (trident, drum, skull-cup, sword, noose, axe), adorned with serpents and skull garlands, often standing on a dog (his vahana), with a fierce expression and protruding fangs. Colors vary: white, red, yellow, black, blue, etc. depending on the specific Bhairava.

Powers

Directional Guardianship Each Bhairava guards one of the eight cardinal and intercardinal directions, protecting the universe and sacred cities from evil forces. Kashi Khanda tradition Each is limited to his specific direction Some sources assign different directions to different Bhairavas

Destruction of Evil As forms of Bhairava, they have the power to annihilate demons, negative forces, and obstacles. General Shaiva tradition Often described as acting on behalf of Shiva or Devi None noted

Bestowal of Protection Devotees who worship the Ashta Bhairavar are believed to receive protection from various dangers, diseases, and planetary afflictions. Temple traditions Requires devotion and proper worship None noted

Granting of Siddhis Each Bhairava is associated with specific supernatural powers (siddhis) that can be attained through dedicated worship. Tantric texts Requires initiation and spiritual discipline Details vary between texts and traditions

Accounts

When Brahma arrogantly claimed superiority over Shiva, Shiva manifested Bhairava from his anger to punish Brahma. Bhairava cut off one of Brahma's five heads, an act that caused the skull to stick to his hand, leading him on a wandering penance until he reached Kashi (Varanasi), where the skull fell away and he was freed from the sin of Brahmahatya (killing a Brahmin).

The Kashi Khanda describes how the Ashta Bhairavar were established as the eight directional guardians of Varanasi, each stationed at a specific location on the city's perimeter to protect it from all evil.

In Tamil Nadu and Karnataka, the Ashta Bhairavar are worshipped in major Shiva temples, often with special shrines or panels depicting all eight forms. They are invoked for protection, healing, and removal of obstacles.

Content Notes

The central myth involves Bhairava cutting off one of Brahma's heads, an act of violence against a creator god. This story is not sanitized in traditional sources.

In Hindu mythology, this act is interpreted as a necessary correction of Brahma's arrogance, not as murder. It also serves as the origin of Bhairava's association with skulls and the sin of Brahmanicide.

Shiva Purana

Bhairava is associated with cremation grounds, skulls, and the destruction of the ego, which can be disturbing to modern sensibilities.

In Tantra, these associations symbolize the transcendence of death and attachment, not glorification of violence.

What We Don't Know

Detailed iconographic descriptions of each individual Bhairava from primary sources (Agamas) need further extraction. Regional variations in South India (Tamil Nadu, Karnataka) need more specific documentation. Contemporary worship practices and festivals dedicated to the Ashta Bhairavar need further research. The relationship between the Ashta Bhairavar and other groups of eight deities (e.g., Ashta Dikpalas) needs clarification.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of the Ashta Bhairavar as the eight specialized 'security protocols' of the divine — each one a different mode of protection, from the wrathful guardian who destroys demons to the fierce yogi who grants spiritual powers. They are not eight separate gods, but eight facets of a single, terrifyingly protective divine presence. Like the different alarm systems in a high-tech fortress, each has its own trigger, its own response, and its own area of coverage.
