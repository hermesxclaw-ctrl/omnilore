Who It Is

In the labyrinthine lanes of Varanasi, the oldest living city on Earth, eight terrifying guardians watch the horizon — each with a skull-cup, a serpent, and a dog at his feet. They are the Ashta Bhairavar, the eight fierce faces of Shiva's wrath, and they are the reason why the city of death is also the city of liberation. Imagine a divine security system where each guard is a different flavor of apocalyptic fury.

The Ashta Bhairavar are fierce guardian deities — protective, wrathful, and terrifying to evil forces, yet approachable as protectors of devotees. They are associated with the destruction of negative forces, the removal of obstacles, and the guardianship of sacred space and time. As forms of Shiva, they embody the paradox of destruction as a form of grace.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Sanskrit 'ashta' (eight) + 'bhairava' (the terrible, the fearsome one). Bhairava derives from the root 'bhr' (to be dreadful) or 'bhi' (fear) + 'rava' (roar), thus 'the one who roars with terror'.

Pronunciation — ASH-ta BY-rah-var

Aliases — Ashta Bhairava, Eight Bhairavas, Ashtabhairava

Ownership — Hinduism (primarily Shaivism, with significant presence in South Indian temple traditions, especially Tamil Nadu and Karnataka)

Powers
Directional Guardianship

Each Bhairava guards one of the eight cardinal and intercardinal directions, protecting the universe and sacred cities from evil forces.

Limit: Each is limited to his specific direction

Destruction of Evil

As forms of Bhairava, they have the power to annihilate demons, negative forces, and obstacles.

Limit: Often described as acting on behalf of Shiva or Devi

Bestowal of Protection

Devotees who worship the Ashta Bhairavar are believed to receive protection from various dangers, diseases, and planetary afflictions.

Limit: Requires devotion and proper worship

Granting of Siddhis

Each Bhairava is associated with specific supernatural powers (siddhis) that can be attained through dedicated worship.

Limit: Requires initiation and spiritual discipline

Form & Signs

Appearance — Each of the eight Bhairavas has distinct iconography, typically depicted with: multiple arms (usually 4 or 6), holding various weapons and implements (trident, drum, skull-cup, sword, noose, axe), adorned with serpents and skull garlands, often standing on a dog (his vahana), with a fierce expression and protruding fangs. Colors vary: white, red, yellow, black, blue, etc. depending on the specific Bhairava.

Symbols — Common symbols: trident (trishula), drum (damaru), skull-cup (kapala), sword, noose, dog (vahana), serpent ornaments, crescent moon, third eye, matted hair, rudraksha beads. Each Bhairava has specific colors, weapons, and attributes distinguishing him from the others.

Domains — Primarily associated with the city of Varanasi (Kashi), where they guard the eight directions. Also found in South Indian temples, particularly in Tamil Nadu, where they are associated with specific shrines and are believed to protect the eight directions of the universe. Each Bhairava is a guardian of a specific direction: Asitanga (north), Ruru (northeast), Chanda (east), Krodha (southeast), Unmatta (south), Kapala (southwest), Bhishana (west),

Accounts I
The Origin of BhairavaShiva Purana

Mythic

When Brahma arrogantly claimed superiority over Shiva, Shiva manifested Bhairava from his anger to punish Brahma. Bhairava cut off one of Brahma's five heads, an act that caused the skull to stick to his hand, leading him on a wandering penance until he reached Kashi (Varanasi), where the skull fell away and he was freed from the sin of Brahmahatya (killing a Brahmin). Bhairava became the guardian of Kashi, and his eight forms (Ashta Bhairavar) were established as protectors of the city's directions.

Accounts II
Guardians of KashiSkanda Purana (Kashi Khanda)

Mythic

The Kashi Khanda describes how the Ashta Bhairavar were established as the eight directional guardians of Varanasi, each stationed at a specific location on the city's perimeter to protect it from all evil. The Ashta Bhairavar became central to the sacred geography of Varanasi, with specific temples and shrines dedicated to each.

Bhairava in South Indian Temple TraditionsShaiva Agamas, temple inscriptions

Medieval to modern

In Tamil Nadu and Karnataka, the Ashta Bhairavar are worshipped in major Shiva temples, often with special shrines or panels depicting all eight forms. They are invoked for protection, healing, and removal of obstacles. The Ashta Bhairavar became an integral part of South Indian temple worship, with festivals and rituals dedicated to them.

The Ashta Bhairavar are demons or evil spirits — They are fierce forms of Shiva, a major Hindu deity, and are worshipped as protectors. Their terrifying appearance is symbolic of their power to destroy evil.

Worship of Bhairava involves blood sacrifice or black magic — Mainstream worship of Bhairava and the Ashta Bhairavar involves standard Hindu rituals: offerings of flowers, incense, lamps, and food. Tantric practices are a specialized and minority tradition.

The Ashta Bhairavar are the same as the Ashta Dikpalas — The Ashta Dikpalas are the eight guardians of the directions in Hindu mythology (Indra, Agni, Yama, etc.). The Ashta Bhairavar are a separate group, though they also guard directions, particularly in Kashi.
