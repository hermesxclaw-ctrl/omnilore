The Approach

In the quiet between worlds, a messenger descends—winged, radiant, and terrifying. Angels are the bridge between the mortal and the divine, appearing in the visions of prophets and the dreams of the faithful. But these beings of light are not merely gentle guardians; they are warriors, judges, and sometimes rebels. From the fiery seraphim to the fallen Lucifer, the angelic host embodies humanity's deepest hopes and fears about the unseen.

Content advisory. This entry discusses religious concepts, including depictions of divine judgment, warfare, and fallen angels. It includes references to violence and evil in religious texts, but does not contain graphic or explicit content.

Etymology

The English word 'angel' derives from Old English 'engel' and Old French 'angele', both from Late Latin 'angelus', borrowed from Late Greek 'angelos' (ἄγγελος), meaning 'messenger'. The earliest form is Mycenaean 'a-ke-ro' in Linear B. Dutch linguist R. S. P. Beekes suggests it may be an Oriental loan, akin to 'angaros' (Persian mounted courier). The Septuagint uses 'angelos' to translate the Hebrew 'mal'akh', which simply means 'messenger'.

Cultural Ownership

The concept of angels is most prominently developed in the Abrahamic religions (Judaism, Christianity, Islam), with significant influences from Zoroastrianism. It is also present in other traditions, but this research focuses on these primary contexts.

Disputed Identifications

There is debate over whether the 'Malak YHWH' (Angel of the Lord) is a distinct being, an aspect of God, or a theophany. In Zoroastrianism, the Amesha Spentas are sometimes considered angels but are more accurately emanations of Ahura Mazda. The identification of Satan as a fallen angel is a later Christian development not explicit in early Jewish texts.

Description

Appearance

Angels are typically depicted as humanoid beings of extraordinary beauty, often with bird-like wings and halos, radiating divine light. However, biblical descriptions vary: seraphim have six wings (Isaiah 6:2), cherubim have four faces and four wings (Ezekiel 1:6), and some angels appear as ordinary men (Genesis 18:2). In art, they are often shown as beautiful youths or androgynous figures, but some texts describe them as fearsome or inhuman, with wheels within wheels (Ezekiel 1:15-21).

Symbols & Iconography

Wings, halos, trumpets, swords, scales, and divine light. In Christian art, angels are often depicted with white robes, golden halos, and feathered wings. In Islamic tradition, angels are described as having wings in pairs, triplets, or quadruplets (Quran 35:1).

Behaviour & Personality

Angels are generally portrayed as obedient servants of God, carrying out divine commands. They can be messengers, warriors, protectors, and guides. They are often depicted as lacking free will in Jewish tradition, but in Christianity, some angels chose to rebel, indicating a degree of free will. They are usually benevolent, but can also execute judgment and punishment.

Domains & Habitats

Heaven, the divine realm, and sometimes the earthly realm as intermediaries. They are considered spiritual beings that inhabit the celestial sphere. In Jewish and Christian cosmology, they reside in the presence of God, while in Islam, they are created from light and inhabit the heavens.

Version Differences

In Judaism, angels are often seen as extensions of God's will, with no independent agency. In Christianity, they are created beings with free will, leading to the concept of fallen angels. In Islam, angels are created from light, have no free will, and are always obedient. Zoroastrianism has angelic-like beings (Amesha Spentas, Fravashis) that are emanations or warriors, not distinct messengers. The hierarchy of angels varies: Dionysius the Areopagite proposed nine choirs (Seraphim, Cherubim, Thrones, Dominions, Virtues, Powers, Principalities, Archangels, Angels), while Jewish tradition often mentions specific archangels like Michael, Gabriel, Raphael, and Uriel.

Flight

Description

Angels are often depicted with wings, enabling them to fly between heaven and earth.

Limitations

Not always present; some angels appear without wings.

Contradictions

Some texts describe angels as having no physical form, so flight is metaphorical.

Divine Messaging

Source Version Hebrew Bible, New Testament, Quran

Description

Angels serve as messengers conveying God's will to humans.

Limitations

They deliver only the message given by God.

Contradictions

None significant.

Warfare

Source Version War Scroll (Qumran), Revelation, Islamic tradition (Jibril's role in battles)

Description

Angels engage in cosmic battles against evil forces.

Limitations

They fight only under God's command.

Contradictions

In some traditions, angels are peaceful and do not engage in physical conflict.

Guardianship

Source Version Psalm 91:11, Daniel 10:13, concept of guardian angels

Description

Angels protect and guide individuals or nations.

The extent of their protection is debated.

Shape-shifting

Description

Angels can appear in human form or other forms.

They may only do so with divine permission.

Contradictions

Angels possess divine knowledge and can reveal it to humans.

Angelic warfare

Source Exodus, Revelation

Description

Angels are depicted as warriors who execute God's judgment, including destruction (e.g., the angel of death in the Passover story, Exodus 12:23).

Scholarly Context

In ancient Near Eastern context, divine warfare was common; angels as warriors reflect God's sovereignty over history.

Fallen angels

Source Enochic literature, Revelation

Description

The concept of fallen angels, particularly Satan, has been used to explain evil and demonic forces.

Scholarly Context

The Book of Enoch describes angels who rebel and corrupt humanity, a theme that influenced later Christian demonology.

Sanitization of angelic violence

Source 2 Kings 19:35, Revelation

Description

Popular culture often portrays angels as gentle, but biblical texts include terrifying and destructive angels.

Scholarly Context

Modern depictions often soften the terrifying aspects of angels, which may misrepresent the biblical tradition.

Contradictions & Disputes

Nature of angels

Position A

Angels are non-corporeal, purely spiritual beings.

Position A — evidence

Hebrews 1:14 describes them as 'spirits'.

Position B

Angels can take physical form and interact with the material world.

Position B — evidence

Genesis 18-19 shows angels eating and staying with Abraham.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars agree angels are spiritual but can manifest physically when God wills.

Free will

Position A

Angels have free will and can rebel (Christianity).

Position A — evidence

Revelation 12:7-9 describes a war in heaven.

Position B

Angels are incapable of disobedience (Islam, Judaism).

Position B — evidence

Quran 66:6 says angels 'do not disobey Allah in what He commands them'.

Scholarly Consensus

The difference stems from theological views on the nature of God and creation.

The Angel of the Lord

Position A

The Angel of the Lord is a distinct created being.

Position A — evidence

Some passages distinguish the Angel from God (e.g., 2 Samuel 24:16).

Position B

The Angel of the Lord is a manifestation of God himself (theophany).

Position B — evidence

Other passages imply the Angel is divine (e.g., Exodus 3:2-6).

Scholarly Consensus

There is no consensus; it may be a development over time.
