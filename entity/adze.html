Archive Dossier — Identity

Adze

Pronunciation /ɑːˈdzeɪ/ or /ˈɑːdzeɪ/

Known As

AdzetsiThe Firefly WitchAdze-vampire

Etymology

The name 'Adze' (pronounced ah-DZAY or AH-dzay) is a Ewe word. Its precise etymological roots are not extensively documented in Western scholarship. It may be related to concepts of transformation or the firefly itself. The variant 'Adzetsi' may incorporate a suffix indicating a person or entity.

Cultural Ownership

Ewe people of southeastern Ghana and southern Togo, particularly in the Volta Region. The Adze is a figure within the traditional spiritual and folkloric belief system of the Ewe, which includes a complex pantheon of gods (vodu), spirits, and ancestral forces. It is not a pan-African figure but specific to this cultural-linguistic group.

Disputed Identifications

Some early European colonial and missionary accounts conflated the Adze with the European vampire or witch, imposing a Christian demonological framework. Later comparative mythology often groups it with other 'vampiric' entities (e.g., the Impundulu of southern Africa, the Obayifo of the Akan), but these are distinct figures with different origins and behaviors. The Adze is fundamentally a type of malevolent witch or sorcerer who can transform, not an undead revenant.

Archive Dossier — Approach

Imagine a creature so small it can drift through a keyhole, a creature that looks like nothing more than a pretty spark of light dancing in the night. This is the Adze of the Ewe people—a vampire that does not lurk in shadows or fly on bat wings, but comes to you as a firefly, beautiful and utterly deceptive, to drink your blood while you sleep.

The adze is the vampire of the Ewe people of Ghana and Togo, and its genius is its size: it flies through the night not as a bat or a shadow but as a firefly — a drifting, pretty spark that slips through any crack, under any door, past any defense sized for something monstrous. Inside the house it feeds, drinking blood from sleepers — children above all — who then sicken and waste with what the modern clinic calls malaria and the old knowledge called the adze's kiss: the insect-borne night-death mapped onto folklore with terrible accuracy.

Who It Is

The adze is the vampire of the Ewe people of Ghana and Togo, and its genius is its size: it flies through the night not as a bat or a shadow but as a firefly — a drifting, pretty spark that slips through any crack, under any door, past any defense sized for something monstrous. Inside the house it feeds, drinking blood from sleepers — children above all — who then sicken and waste with what the modern clinic calls malaria and the old knowledge called the adze's kiss: the insect-borne night-death mapped onto folklore with terrible accuracy. Caught in the act — trapped in a jar, swatted from the air — the adze transforms into its true shape: a hunched, black-skinned humanoid horror. And here the adze becomes something darker than a monster: in its human-linked form it possesses, riding witches (adzetsi) within the community, so that the hunt for the firefly became, historically, a hunt for neighbors. The adze cannot be bargained with and has no taste for anything but blood, palm oil, and coconut water — the pantry of the region reimagined as a vampire's diet. Its lesson is the one the mosquito net finally answered: the deadliest thing in the dark is small, glowing, and already inside.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

The Adze's most distinctive and attested form is that of a firefly or a spark of light. It is described as a drifting, glowing point that moves through the night sky, often appearing to be a small, harmless insect. This is its primary form when traveling and seeking victims. In its human form, the Adze is a person (often, but not exclusively, a woman) who is a witch (dzokoto or adzeto). There are no widely attested descriptions of a monstrous or hybrid form. The transformation is complete, not a partial hybrid. Some accounts suggest that if the Adze is captured in its firefly form, it can be forced to resume its human shape, revealing the witch in the community.

Symbols & Iconography

The primary symbol is the firefly itself, representing deceptive innocence and the danger that can come from something seemingly beautiful and small. The color of the light is not consistently specified in sources, but fireflies in the Volta region typically emit a yellow-green glow. The Adze is also associated with darkness and the night, and with the ability to pass through barriers.

Behaviour & Personality

The Adze is driven by a malevolent will, specifically a thirst for human blood and coconut water (or palm wine, depending on the version). It is cunning, patient, and deceptive, using its tiny form to infiltrate homes undetected. It is not portrayed as a mindless predator but as a conscious, intelligent being with malicious intent. It is also associated with causing illness, misfortune, and death, not just through direct blood-drinking but through spiritual attack. The Adze is inherently secretive; it lives as a normal member of the community by day.

Domains & Habitats

The Adze is a creature of the night, operating in and around human villages. It is said to live in the forest or bush during the day, but its true 'home' is the human community it preys upon. It is particularly associated with the night sky of the Volta region.

Version Differences

There is relatively little variation in the core description of the Adze across the available ethnographic sources. The main differences are in the details: the preferred drink (coconut water vs. palm wine), the precise method of capture (trapping in a gourd vs. a net), and the exact nature of the 'witch' identity (is it an inherited spirit, a learned skill, or a possession?). These variations likely reflect different local traditions within the broader Ewe culture, as well as the limitations of early ethnographic work.

Death & Rising

Ewe witchcraft-and-vampire tradition of the Volta region, encoding insect-vector disease, child mortality, and communal witchcraft anxiety into a single luminous predator.

Archive Dossier — Powers

Transformation into a Firefly

Source Version Attested in all major ethnographic accounts (Ellis 1890, Westermann 1905, Field 1937).

Description

The Adze's primary and most famous power is the ability to transform its entire being into a firefly (or a spark of light). This allows it to fly, move silently, and pass through any opening, no matter how small.

Limitations

The transformation is apparently complete and voluntary, but the Adze can be trapped or forced back into human form if captured in its firefly state.

Contradictions

None significant.

Blood and Fluid Consumption

Source Version Attested in multiple sources.

Description

The Adze is said to drink the blood of its human victims, often while they sleep. It also has a noted taste for coconut water or palm wine.

Limitations

It must feed regularly. The act of feeding is often associated with causing illness or weakness in the victim.

Contradictions

Whether the blood-drinking is literal or a spiritual/metaphorical act is a point of interpretation. Some accounts treat it as a physical act, others as a form of spiritual predation.

Causing Illness and Misfortune

Source Version Attested in oral traditions.

Description

Beyond direct feeding, the Adze is believed to be able to cause sickness, bad luck, accidents, and even death to its victims or their families through supernatural means.

Limitations

The Adze's power is not absolute; it can be countered by protective rituals, charms, and the intervention of powerful priests or diviners.

Contradictions

None significant.

Passing Through Barriers

Source Version Attested in oral traditions and modern summaries.

Description

In its firefly form, the Adze can pass through keyholes, cracks in walls, under doors, and through any other small opening, making it nearly impossible to keep out of a home by conventional means.

Limitations

The ability is a direct consequence of its size and form, not a separate power to phase through solid matter.

Contradictions

None.

Possession?

Source Version Implied in some accounts of witchcraft.

Description

Some sources suggest the Adze can possess or influence people, particularly those who are already vulnerable or who have a 'weak soul.' This is a debated point.

Limitations

Not well-defined in the available literature.

Contradictions

Other sources present the Adze as a witch who transforms, not a spirit that possesses. The line between 'being an Adze' and 'being possessed by an Adze' is blurry in some accounts.

Form

A firefly's wandering spark in the dark house; cornered, a squat black manikin with hooked fingers — and in its social form, the ordinary face of a possessed neighbor.

Powers

Insect-form infiltration through any gap, blood-feeding that wastes children, possession of human hosts, and the seeding of witchcraft accusation — a monster that attacks the body and the community in the same night.

Weaknesses & Wards

It must be caught transformed to be killed; nets and sealed rooms defeat the firefly; and communal rites cleanse the possessed — remedies that map, one to one, onto screens, sanitation, and reconciliation.

Archive Dossier — Relationships

Mami Wata

Type Related entity (different tradition)Source Comparative mythology studies (e.g., Drewal 1988).

Description

Mami Wata is a water spirit venerated in many West and Central African cultures. While both are powerful spiritual beings, they are distinct. Mami Wata is not typically a vampiric entity, but she can be dangerous and demanding. Some syncretic traditions may blur the lines, but in orthodox Ewe belief, they are separate.

Version Note

Modern syncretism may connect them, but this is not part of the core Adze tradition.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Impundulu (Lightning Bird)

Type Related entity (different tradition)Source Comparative folklore studies.

Description

The Impundulu is a vampiric bird from the folklore of the Zulu and other southern African peoples. It shares the characteristic of blood-drinking and being associated with a witch. However, the Impundulu is a familiar or servant of a witch, not the witch themselves. The Adze is the witch.

Version Note

This is a typological comparison, not a claim of historical connection.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Dzokoto / Adzeto (Ewe Witch)

Type IdentitySource Ellis (1890), Westermann (1905).

Description

In Ewe belief, the Adze is not a separate species of monster; it is a witch (dzokoto or adzeto) who possesses the power of transformation. Therefore, the Adze is the witch, and the witch is the Adze. They are one and the same.

Version Note

This is the core understanding of the Adze within its original cultural context.

Conflicting Accounts

Some popular modern accounts treat the Adze as a separate 'vampire' creature, which is a distortion.

Bokonɔ (Diviner/Priest)

Type Antagonist/CountermeasureSource Field (1937), Herskovits (1938).

Description

The bokonɔ is a diviner and priest within the Ewe vodu tradition. They are the primary authority for identifying Adze activity, diagnosing spiritual attacks, and performing protective or cleansing rituals.

Version Note

The role of the bokonɔ is central to the social management of Adze beliefs.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Victims of the Adze

Type VictimSource Oral traditions.

Description

The victims of the Adze are typically members of the community, often relatives or neighbors of the witch. The Adze is believed to prey on those closest to it, making it a potent source of social paranoia.

Version Note

The close relationship between witch and victim is a common theme in witchcraft beliefs globally.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Witchcraft Accusations and Violence

Source Anthropological studies of Ewe and neighboring societies (e.g., Meyer 1999, Parish 2000).

Description

Belief in the Adze is not merely a folkloric curiosity; it has real-world consequences. Accusations of being an Adze (i.e., a witch) have historically led to social ostracism, forced confessions, ritual purification, banishment, and in some cases, murder. This is a pattern seen in many societies with strong witchcraft beliefs. The accused are often women, the elderly, the poor, or other marginalized individuals.

Scholarly Context

This is a well-documented aspect of the social history of the region. Modern Ghanaian and Togolese law prohibits such violence, but beliefs persist, and accusations can still lead to vigilante action.

Colonial and Missionary Distortion

Source Ellis (1890), and critical analyses of colonial ethnography.

Description

Early European accounts (like Ellis 1890) were written by colonial officers and missionaries who approached Ewe religion with a Christian and Eurocentric bias. They often framed the Adze as a 'devil' or 'demon,' imposing a Christian dualistic framework (God vs. Satan) onto a more complex indigenous spiritual landscape. This distorted the Ewe understanding of the Adze as one type of spiritual being among many, not an embodiment of absolute evil.

Scholarly Context

Postcolonial studies have extensively critiqued how colonial ethnography misrepresented and simplified African religions to justify missionary activity and colonial rule.

Blood-Drinking and 'Savage' Stereotypes

Source Sensationalist colonial literature and missionary reports.

Description

The Adze's blood-drinking nature was sensationalized in some early accounts, feeding into a Western stereotype of 'savage' African rituals and beliefs. This contributed to a dehumanizing portrayal of African cultures. The focus on the 'vampiric' aspect often overshadowed the more nuanced social and spiritual functions of the belief.

Scholarly Context

This is part of a broader pattern of 'othering' non-European cultures by focusing on their most alien or frightening beliefs.

Archive Dossier — Stories

The Firefly in the House

Source Ewe oral tradition, collected by various ethnographers.Culture EwePeriod Pre-colonial to present day (as a folk belief)

Summary

A common narrative involves a person waking up at night to see a single, unusually bright firefly hovering inside their home. The person, knowing the danger, tries to trap it in a gourd or calabash. If they succeed, the firefly will transform back into a human being, revealing the identity of the Adze-witch. The witch will then beg for release, often offering promises or bribes. The person must decide whether to kill the witch or expose them to the community.

Participants

The Adze (in firefly form)

The human who discovers it

Consequences

If the Adze is captured and killed, the witch dies in their human form as well. If released, the Adze may continue its predations or may seek revenge on the person who trapped it.

Variants

The method of capture varies: a gourd, a net, a piece of cloth. The fate of the captured Adze also varies: some stories say it must be killed immediately, others say it can be forced to serve its captor.

The Witch's Secret

Source Ewe oral tradition.Culture EwePeriod Pre-colonial to present day

Summary

A person is suspected of being an Adze. They are observed acting strangely at night, or their neighbors notice a firefly that seems to follow them. A diviner (bokonɔ) is consulted, who confirms the person's identity as a witch. The community may then confront the person, perform a cleansing ritual, or drive them out of the village. This story reflects the social function of the Adze belief as a way to explain misfortune and identify antisocial individuals.

Participants

The suspected Adze

The community

The diviner

Consequences

The accused may be ostracized, forced to undergo a ritual, or killed. The social fabric is reinforced by identifying and removing a perceived threat.

Variants

The outcome depends heavily on the specific community and the power of the accused. A wealthy or powerful person might be able to resist the accusation.

Archive Dossier — Archivist

Known Gaps

1. **Ewe-Language Sources:** No direct access to Ewe-language texts or oral recordings. All information is from English and German secondary sources. 2. **Contemporary Practice:** Limited information on how the Adze belief functions in contemporary Ewe society (e.g., in urban areas, among Christians, etc.). 3. **Specific Rituals:** No detailed descriptions of the specific rituals used to protect against or combat the Adze. 4. **Gender:** The common claim that the Adze is 'usually a woman' needs more rigorous sourcing. While many witchcraft accusations target women, the primary sources do not exclusively state this. 5. **Iconography:** No known traditional visual representations (carvings, drawings) of the Adze from the pre-colonial period. Its iconography is primarily described in words.

Research Scope

This research is based on a review of major English-language ethnographic sources from the late 19th and early 20th centuries, supplemented by modern scholarly analysis and popular culture references. It does not include primary research in the Ewe language or fieldwork in Ghana/Togo.

Archive Dossier — Contradictions

Is the Adze a separate entity or a witch?

Position A

The Adze is a distinct, supernatural 'vampire' creature that can possess or transform a person.

Position A — evidence

Some popular modern sources (e.g., monster encyclopedias, game bestiaries) treat it as a creature type.

Position B

The Adze is not a separate entity; it is the transformed form of a human witch. The witch IS the Adze.

Position B — evidence

Early and authoritative ethnographic sources (Ellis, Westermann, Field) consistently state that the Adze is a witch who can transform, not a separate monster.

Scholarly Consensus

The anthropological consensus supports position B: the Adze is a witch. The 'separate creature' view is a modern simplification or pop-culture distortion.

What does the Adze drink?

Position A

The Adze primarily drinks human blood.

Position A — evidence

Most general summaries emphasize blood-drinking, fitting the 'vampire' archetype.

Position B

The Adze primarily drinks coconut water or palm wine. Blood-drinking is a secondary or metaphorical act.

Position B — evidence

Some specific ethnographic accounts mention a preference for coconut water or palm wine as a key detail.

Scholarly Consensus

There is no single consensus; the available evidence suggests both blood and other fluids are part of its lore, possibly varying by local tradition.

How is the Adze captured?

Position A

It can be trapped in a gourd or calabash.

Position A — evidence

Multiple oral traditions and summaries mention the gourd method.

Position B

It can be caught in a net or with a piece of cloth.

Position B — evidence

Other versions mention a net or cloth as the capture tool.

Scholarly Consensus

This is a minor variation in oral tradition, not a major contradiction. The method likely varied by region and storyteller.
