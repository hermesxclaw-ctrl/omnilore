Who It Is

Saulė is the radiant sun goddess of Baltic mythology, a warm and generous mother figure who rides a golden chariot drawn by white horses across the sky each day. She wears a crown of amber and a silver belt, and her golden necklace — the morning dew — she distributes to the earth each dawn. Saulė is the bride of Mēness (the moon), but their marriage is tempestuous and cyclical: Mēness is unfaithful, so Saulė leaves him, and only her daughter Auseklis (the Morning Star) reconciles them briefly at each new month. Saulė is central to Baltic cosmology: the dainas speak of her as the source of all life, warmth, and growth. The Baltic love affair with amber is directly tied to Saulė — amber is her tears, or pieces of her broken sun-boat, washed ashore after she sinks into the sea each evening. She is the protector of orphans and the unfortunate, and her presence is invoked at weddings, births, and harvests. The Latvian midsummer festival Jāņi (St. John's Day) includes extensive sun-symbolism: bonfires, flower crowns, and all-night vigils to honor Saulė at her brightest moment. Unlike many Indo-European sun deities who are male, Saulė is emphatically female — a distinctive feature of Baltic mythology.

The Approach

Before the thunder god Perkūnas wielded his hammer, before the Moon's infidelity was written in the sky, there was Saulė—the Baltic Sun herself, a golden mother who rides a chariot of fire across the heavens by day and sails a silver boat through the world-sea by night. She is the warmth that wakes orphans, the bride betrayed by a wandering Moon, and the blazing wheel whose turning marks the longest day and the darkest night. To the Latvians and Lithuanians, the world itself is simply 'under the Sun.'

Identity

Saulė

Pronunciation Lithuanian: [ˈsau̯lʲeː]; Latvian: [ˈsaule]

Known As

Saule (Latvian)Saulytė (diminutive, Lithuanian)Saules māte (Latvian, 'Mother Sun')

Etymology

From Proto-Baltic *Sauliā > *Saulē, meaning 'sun'. The Lithuanian 'Saulė' and Latvian 'Saule' are also the standard words for the sun.

Cultural Ownership

Baltic peoples, primarily Lithuanians and Latvians; also recognized in Prussian mythology as a solar deity.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars have attempted to equate Saulė with other Indo-European solar deities (e.g., Greek Helios, Slavic Dazhbog) due to shared motifs, but these are speculative and not universally accepted. Within Baltic tradition, she is distinct from the earth goddess Žemyna, though they are related as mother-daughter in some myths.

Description

Appearance

Saulė is anthropomorphized as a beautiful woman. In Latvian dainas, she is dressed in garments woven with gold thread, wearing gold shoes, a sparkling crown, and a silk or silver costume. She is also described as wearing clothes of red, gold, silver, and white. In the morning, she is 'red' or 'in a red tree.' In Lithuanian tradition, the sun is a golden wheel or circle.

Symbols & Iconography

Gold is her primary symbol: golden shoes, crown, boat, chariot, horses, and various golden objects (rings, ribbons, tassels). The rose is associated with the setting and rising sun. The silver hill and silver gated castle are also symbolic. The 'golden wheel' is a common motif.

Behaviour & Personality

Saulė is a nurturing mother figure, especially to orphans; she delays her rise to comfort them. She is also a betrayed wife, who divorced Mėnuo after his infidelity. She is depicted as dancing on a silver hill, driving her chariot with fiery sparks, and sailing her boat at night. She is powerful and responsible for all life on Earth.

Domains & Habitats

She dwells in a silver gated castle at the end of the sea, in the east, or on an island in the sea. At night she sleeps in a silver cradle in the sea or at the bottom of a lake. She travels across the sky by day and through the sea by night.

Version Differences

In Lithuanian tradition, she is more often depicted as a mother and wife; in Latvian, the emphasis is on her golden attire and her daughters. The color of her horses varies: white, golden, silver, diamond, yellow, or fiery. Some accounts say she has three horses; others imply a hundred. The vehicle varies: chariot, carriage, sleigh, or boat. The punishment of Mėnuo differs: he is cut in two, divorced, or disfigured.

The Marriage of Saulė and Mėnuo

Source Lithuanian and Latvian folkloreCulture BalticPeriod Unknown (pre-Christian)

Summary

Saulė and Mėnuo (the Moon) were married. Mėnuo fell in love with Aušrinė (the morning star). Perkūnas punished him for infidelity.

Participants

Saulė

Mėnuo

Aušrinė

Perkūnas

Consequences

Mėnuo was punished in various ways: cut into two pieces (repeated monthly), divorced from Saulė, or disfigured. The Sun and Moon now alternate in the sky.

Variants

Three versions of the punishment: (1) Mėnuo is cut into two pieces each month; (2) they divorced, and both wanted to see their daughter Žemyna, so the Sun shines by day and the Moon at night; (3) Mėnuo's face was disfigured by Dievas or Saulė.

Contradictions & Disputes

Punishment of Mėnuo

Position A

Perkūnas cut Mėnuo into two pieces, and the punishment is repeated every month.

Position A — evidence

Lithuanian folklore accounts.

Position B

Mėnuo and Saulė divorced, and they both wanted to see their daughter Žemyna, so the Sun shines by day and the Moon at night.

Position B — evidence

Another Lithuanian folkloric version.

Scholarly Consensus

There is no single canonical version; the variations reflect regional and temporal differences.

Identity of the punisher

Position A

Perkūnas punished Mėnuo.

Position A — evidence

Common in Lithuanian mythology.

Position B

Dievas or Saulė disfigured Mėnuo's face.

Position B — evidence

Some accounts mention Dievas or Saulė.

Scholarly Consensus

The evidence is mixed; Perkūnas is most commonly cited, but other versions exist.

Number and color of horses

Position A

Saulė has a hundred horses (Lithuanian riddles).

Position A — evidence

Lithuanian riddles: 'šimtas arklių nepavež'.

Position B

She has three horses of gold, silver, and diamond colors (Latvian dainas).

Position B — evidence

Latvian dainas describe three steeds.

Scholarly Consensus

The number and color vary by region and song; no single canon.

Vehicle type

Position A

She rides a chariot or carriage.

Position A — evidence

Lithuanian and Latvian descriptions of a chariot with copper wheels or a golden chariot.

Position B

She sails a boat.

Position B — evidence

Latvian dainas describe a golden or silver boat.

Scholarly Consensus

Both are attested; the boat is for night travel, the chariot for day.
