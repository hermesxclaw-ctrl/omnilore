The Approach

In the dead of night, a demon lurks in the shadows of a warrior's home, plotting revenge against the legendary slayer of Shuten-dōji. This is Kidōmaru, a creature of vengeance and cunning, whose tale weaves through Japanese folklore, capturing the imagination across centuries.

Content advisory. Contains depictions of violence, killing of animals, and themes of revenge and abandonment.
Identity

Kidōmaru

Pronunciation Kee-doh-mah-roo

Known As

Kidō鬼童丸鬼同丸

Etymology

The name Kidōmaru (鬼童丸) combines 'ki' (oni/demon), 'dō' (child), and 'maru' (a common suffix for male names, often used for young boys). It can be interpreted as 'demon child' or 'oni child'.

Cultural Ownership

Japanese folklore, specifically from the Kamakura period setsuwa collection Kokon Chomonjū and later yōkai illustrations and warrior tales.

Disputed Identifications

Some sources identify Kidōmaru as the child of Shuten-dōji, while others suggest he was a bandit or an abandoned child from Mount Hiei. These are not necessarily contradictory but represent different traditions.

Description

Appearance

In the Kokon Chomonjū, Kidōmaru is described as an oni, but his physical appearance is not detailed. In Toriyama Sekien's illustration, he is depicted in the snow wearing the skin of a cattle, suggesting a monstrous or beastly form. In legends, as a child of Shuten-dōji, he is said to have had teeth at birth and grew to be a fearsome oni.

Symbols & Iconography

Kidōmaru is often depicted in relation to cattle skin, as in Sekien's illustration, and in ukiyo-e prints showing him in combat or in the contest with Hakamadare.

Behaviour & Personality

Kidōmaru is portrayed as vengeful and cunning, lying in wait to ambush Yorimitsu. He is also shown as a skilled fighter, capable of tearing chains and defeating deer and boars as a child.

Domains & Habitats

According to the Zentaiheiki, he lived in a cave in the mountains after being expelled from Mount Hiei. In the Kokon Chomonjū, he is encountered at the home of Minamoto no Yorinobu and later hides in a cattle at Ichihara field.

Version Differences

The Kokon Chomonjū presents him as an oni caught at a toilet, while the Zentaiheiki suggests he was originally a human child who became a bandit. The oral tradition from Fukuchiyama makes him the son of Shuten-dōji. These versions differ in origin and nature.

Stories & Myths

The Ambush at the Toilet

Source Kokon ChomonjūCulture JapanesePeriod Kamakura period

Summary

Minamoto no Yorimitsu, known for slaying Shuten-dōji, visited his brother Yorinobu's home. Kidōmaru was caught at the toilet. Yorimitsu advised restraining him with chains, but Kidōmaru tore them off and, bearing a grudge, watched Yorimitsu's bed. Yorimitsu announced he would visit Kurama temple, and Kidōmaru went ahead, killed a cow, hid inside it, and waited. Yorimitsu saw through the ruse and ordered Watanabe no Tsuna to shoot the cow. Kidōmaru emerged and tried to slash Yorimitsu, but Yorimitsu struck him down with one blow.

Consequences

Kidōmaru was killed.

Variants

The Zentaiheiki version gives a different origin for Kidōmaru and may have a different encounter.

The Child of Shuten-dōji

Source Oral tradition from Kumohara, FukuchiyamaCulture JapanesePeriod Unknown (recorded in modern times)

Summary

After Yorimitsu slew Shuten-dōji, a captured girl became mentally ill and gave birth to Shuten-dōji's child at Kumohara. The child had teeth at birth and by age 7 or 8 killed deer and boars with rocks. He grew up to become Kidōmaru and sought revenge on Yorimitsu.

Consequences

Kidōmaru dedicated his life to avenging his father.

Variants

This is a local legend, not widely recorded.

Dark History

Violence

Source Kokon Chomonjū, oral tradition

Description

Kidōmaru kills a cow to use as a disguise, and in the oral tradition, as a child he kills deer and boars. He also attempts to kill Yorimitsu.

Scholarly Context

These acts are typical of oni and bandit figures in folklore.

Abandonment

Source Zentaiheiki

Description

In the Zentaiheiki, Kidōmaru is described as an abandoned child who became a bandit after being expelled from Mount Hiei.

Scholarly Context

This reflects a common trope of outcast figures in Japanese warrior tales.

Mental illness

Source Oral tradition

Description

The mother of Kidōmaru in the oral tradition becomes mentally disordered after her ordeal.

Scholarly Context

This is a tragic element of the legend, showing the human cost of Shuten-dōji's raids.

Contradictions & Disputes

Origin of Kidōmaru

Position A

Kidōmaru is an oni, as stated in the Kokon Chomonjū.

Position A — evidence

The Kokon Chomonjū explicitly calls him an oni.

Position B

Kidōmaru was originally a human child who became a bandit, as per the Zentaiheiki.

Position B — evidence

The Zentaiheiki provides a detailed backstory of a human child from Mount Hiei.

Scholarly Consensus

There is no consensus; different sources present different origins, reflecting the fluid nature of folklore.

Parentage

Position A

Kidōmaru is the son of Shuten-dōji.

Position A — evidence

Oral tradition from Fukuchiyama claims this.

Position B

Kidōmaru is not related to Shuten-dōji.

Position B — evidence

The Kokon Chomonjū does not mention any connection to Shuten-dōji.

Scholarly Consensus

The oral tradition is a later local legend; the original setsuwa does not link them.

Research Scope

This dossier synthesizes information from the Kokon Chomonjū, the Konjaku Hyakki Shūi, the Zentaiheiki, and various ukiyo-e prints, along with regional legends. The focus is on the mythological and literary tradition of Kidōmaru as an oni and bandit, with attention to historical and cultural context. Modern adaptations are noted where documented.
