Form

In Ugaritic texts, Anat is described as a young, fierce warrior maiden, often depicted with her spear or sword, wading in blood. She is 'the maiden Anat' (btlt 'nt), yet paradoxically a goddess of both war and fertility. In Egyptian art, she is depicted as a woman wearing the White Crown of Upper Egypt with streamers, holding a spear and shield, sometimes riding a horse. She is often shown with the was-scepter and ankh. Her iconography is martial, emphasizing her role as a divine warrior.

Powers

Divine Warrior Anat is depicted as a supremely skilled and savage fighter, capable of defeating armies single-handedly. In the Baal Cycle, she 'heaps up heads' and 'ties up hands' in a frenzy of battle, wading through blood and gore. Ugaritic (Baal Cycle) Her violence is indiscriminate and can be directed at allies or innocents if she is enraged. None noted; her martial prowess is consistent across Ugaritic and Egyptian sources.

Resurrection/Revivification After Baal's death, Anat descends to the underworld, finds his body, and with the sun goddess Shapsh's help, buries him and performs mourning rites. She later assists in his resurrection, demonstrating power over death, or at least access to the underworld. Ugaritic (Baal Cycle) The mechanics of the resurrection are attributed to El's decree and Shapsh's assistance; Anat's role is more as a catalyst and mourner than a sole agent of resurrection. The text is fragmentary; some scholars argue her role is primarily funerary, not resurrectionary, in this specific episode.

Divine Ferocity and Curse Anat can curse or bless individuals. In the Aqhat epic, she curses the land with barrenness after Aqhat refuses her his bow, and she arranges his murder. Ugaritic (Epic of Aqhat) Her power seems subject to the broader divine council (El's authority), but her personal curses are effective. None.

Divine Patronage In Egypt, she grants the pharaoh victory in battle, protection, and martial prowess. She is a patron of the chariot corps. Egyptian (New Kingdom) Her patronage is tied to the pharaoh's role as the maintainer of Ma'at (cosmic order). None.

Accounts

The text describes Anat's insatiable bloodlust. She battles in the valley, heaping up heads and hands, wading through blood. She is described as 'satisfied' only after a massive slaughter, after which she prepares a peace offering and cleanses herself, highlighting the ritual cycle of violence and purification.

Aqhat, a mortal hero, is given a magnificent bow by the craftsman god Kothar-wa-Khasis. Anat covets the bow and offers Aqhat immortality and wealth in exchange. Aqhat refuses, insulting her. Enraged, Anat plots his murder. She enlists Yatpan, who kills Aqhat while disguised as a hawk. Anat then mourns the hero's death, and the land suffers a drought as a result of her curse. The hero's sister, Paghat, vows revenge.

After Baal is killed by Mot (Death), Anat searches for him across the earth. She finds his body, mourns him with a ritual feast of oxen, sheep, and goats, and with the help of Shapsh (the Sun), she buries him. She then confronts Mot, seizes him, and with a sickle, winnows him, burns him, grinds him, and sows his remains in the field, symbolically destroying Death. This act, combined with El's dream, leads to Baal's resurrection.

Content Notes

Anat's defining characteristic is her extreme, gleeful violence. The Baal Cycle describes her 'heaping up heads' and 'wading in blood' in a battle frenzy. This is not sanitized in the text; it is presented as a core aspect of her divine power.

Scholars interpret this as a reflection of the brutal realities of Late Bronze Age warfare and the Canaanite understanding of divine power as both creative and destructive. Her violence is often ritualized, tied to cycles of death and rebirth.

Ugaritic Baal Cycle (KTU 1.3)

Anat has a mortal hero murdered because he refused to give her his bow. This is a clear act of murder motivated by pride and covetousness, and it brings drought and suffering upon the land.

This story serves as an aition for drought and a cautionary tale about the dangers of offending the gods. It also highlights the disparity in power between mortals and deities, and the capricious nature of divine justice.

What We Don't Know

Direct archaeological evidence for the worship of 'Anatis' as a distinct, named deity is sparse. The Greco-Roman inscriptions need further epigraphic study to determine whether they refer to Anat or Anahita in specific instances. The full text of the Epic of Aqhat is fragmentary, leaving the ending unclear. The nuances of Anat's relationship with Baal (whether consort or solely sister) remain debated. Modern reception is largely based on Ugaritic texts, not the later 'Anatis' form.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Anat as the ultimate action-hero protagonist of the Bronze Age: a one-woman army, fiercely loyal to her family (Baal), but with a hair-trigger temper and a body count that would make a modern video game villain blush. She's less a 'goddess of love' and more the patron deity of 'overwhelming force as a solution to all problems.' If she were a modern character, she'd be a blend of Wonder Woman's combat prowess, Kratos's rage, and a hurricane's indifference to what it destroys. She is the raw, untamed power of the storm and the battlefield, a force of nature you'd rather have on your side than against it.
