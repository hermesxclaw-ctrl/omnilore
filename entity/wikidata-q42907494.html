Who It Is

Imagine a deity who doesn't just win battles — she wades through the gore of them, heaping heads like trophies, her laughter echoing over the carnage. That's Anat, the Canaanite warrior-goddess, a figure of terrifying, untamed power who was worshipped for millennia before her name was garbled into 'Anatis' by the Greeks. She is the primordial chaos that defends cosmic order, the bloodthirsty maiden who mourns her brother with a ferocity that shakes the underworld. Forget polite, sanitized mythology; this is a goddess who demands you take her seriously, or she might just arrange your untimely demise.

Anat is characterized by violent, impetuous, and passionate behavior. She is a 'bloodthirsty' goddess who revels in battle, yet she is also fiercely loyal to her brother Baal, mourning and avenging his death. She is depicted as demanding, imperious, and capricious, but also as a protector of her chosen heroes (or a destroyer of those who offend her). She is a figure of untamed, chaotic power.

Powers
Divine Warrior

Anat is depicted as a supremely skilled and savage fighter, capable of defeating armies single-handedly. In the Baal Cycle, she 'heaps up heads' and 'ties up hands' in a frenzy of battle, wading through blood and gore.

Limit: Her violence is indiscriminate and can be directed at allies or innocents if she is enraged.

Resurrection/Revivification

After Baal's death, Anat descends to the underworld, finds his body, and with the sun goddess Shapsh's help, buries him and performs mourning rites. She later assists in his resurrection, demonstrating power over death, or at least access to the underworld.

Limit: The mechanics of the resurrection are attributed to El's decree and Shapsh's assistance; Anat's role is more as a catalyst and mourner than a sole agent of resurrection.

Divine Ferocity and Curse

Anat can curse or bless individuals. In the Aqhat epic, she curses the land with barrenness after Aqhat refuses her his bow, and she arranges his murder.

Limit: Her power seems subject to the broader divine council (El's authority), but her personal curses are effective.

Divine Patronage

In Egypt, she grants the pharaoh victory in battle, protection, and martial prowess. She is a patron of the chariot corps.

Limit: Her patronage is tied to the pharaoh's role as the maintainer of Ma'at (cosmic order).

Form & Signs

Appearance — In Ugaritic texts, Anat is described as a young, fierce warrior maiden, often depicted with her spear or sword, wading in blood. She is 'the maiden Anat' (btlt 'nt), yet paradoxically a goddess of both war and fertility. In Egyptian art, she is depicted as a woman wearing the White Crown of Upper Egypt with streamers, holding a spear and shield, sometimes riding a horse. She is often shown with the was-scepter and ankh. Her iconography is martial

Symbols — Spear, shield, sword, battle-axe, the White Crown of Upper Egypt (in Egyptian iconography), the ankh, the was-scepter, the horse (in Egypt). She is associated with lions (a common Canaanite iconographic motif for warrior deities). In Ugaritic texts, she is associated with the liver (as a site of emotion) and with the 'dew of heaven' (a fertility symbol).

Domains — War, combat, hunting, fertility, and sovereignty. Her habitats include the divine mountain of Baal (Mount Saphon), the underworld (where she descends to retrieve Baal), and the battlefield. In Egypt, she is a martial deity and protector of the pharaoh, particularly in chariot warfare.

Accounts I
Anat's Rampage (Baal Cycle)Ugaritic Baal Cycle (KTU 1.3)

Late Bronze Age (c. 14th–12th c. BCE)

The text describes Anat's insatiable bloodlust. She battles in the valley, heaping up heads and hands, wading through blood. She is described as 'satisfied' only after a massive slaughter, after which she prepares a peace offering and cleanses herself, highlighting the ritual cycle of violence and purification. Establishes Anat as the preeminent divine warrior, a force of chaotic violence that paradoxically maintains cosmic order. It also demonstrates her emotional volatility.

Accounts II
Anat and the Death of AqhatUgaritic Epic of Aqhat (KTU 1.17–1.19)

Late Bronze Age (c. 14th–12th c. BCE)

Aqhat, a mortal hero, is given a magnificent bow by the craftsman god Kothar-wa-Khasis. Anat covets the bow and offers Aqhat immortality and wealth in exchange. Aqhat refuses, insulting her. Enraged, Anat plots his murder. She enlists Yatpan, who kills Aqhat while disguised as a hawk. Anat then mourns the hero's death, and the land suffers a drought as a result of her curse. The hero's sister, Paghat, vows revenge. The story explains the origin of drought and the fragility of human life. It shows Anat's capricious and vengeful nature, and her willingness to kill a mortal over a slight.

Anat's Search and Mourning for BaalUgaritic Baal Cycle (KTU 1.5–1.6)

Late Bronze Age (c. 14th–12th c. BCE)

After Baal is killed by Mot (Death), Anat searches for him across the earth. She finds his body, mourns him with a ritual feast of oxen, sheep, and goats, and with the help of Shapsh (the Sun), she buries him. She then confronts Mot, seizes him, and with a sickle, winnows him, burns him, grinds him, and sows his remains in the field, symbolically destroying Death. This act, combined with El's dream, leads to Baal's resurrection. Demonstrates Anat's loyalty and love for her brother, and her role in the cycle of life, death, and rebirth. It establishes her as a direct opponent of Mot (Death).

Anatis is the same as Anahita. — They are distinct deities from different cultures (Canaanite vs. Persian), whose names were conflated by Greco-Roman writers.

Anat is a 'love goddess' in the same vein as Aphrodite. — While she has associations with fertility, her primary and defining characteristic in Ugaritic texts is martial violence. She is not a goddess of romantic love.

Anat is a demon or a purely evil figure. — In her original context, she was a powerful and respected deity. Her violence was a form of divine power, both creative and destructive, essential to maintaining cosmic order. She was not 'evil' but was capricious and terrifying.
