Who It Is

Jasy Jatere is the Guarani moon-fragment who became the siesta's most dangerous secret. Among the seven cursed children of Tau and Kerana, he stands apart: not a monster, but a small, golden-staffed figure with the power to steal children from their own doorsteps during the hottest hours of the day. Whether he returns them with a kiss and no memory, or blinds them in a cave and feeds them to his cannibal brother, depends entirely on which version of the myth you heard—and on whether the teller wanted to protect you or terrify you.

His behavior shifts radically across versions. In the kinder telling he is playful, generous, and protective: he whisks disobedient children into hidden forest glades, feeds them honey and fruit, and returns them home with a memory-erasing kiss at siesta's end. In the darker tradition he is a kidnapper and enforcer of the siesta: invisible until he chooses to appear, he lures children away with a whistle, renders them insensible through his staff, and either imprisons them in caves or surrenders them to his brother Ao Ao for consumption. Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Jasy Jatere is composed of Guaraní elements: 'Jasy' (also spelled 'Yasy') denotes the moon, while 'Jatere' relates to the notion of a fragment or small piece. The compound therefore translates literally as 'a little piece of the moon.' The spelling varies across colonial and modern sources

Powers
Siesta trance induction

Jasy Jatere can render children insensible or entranced simply by allowing them to view his golden staff. The effect is instantaneous and dissolves only at the end of the siesta or upon his will. The power does not work on adults in most accounts, which limits its social scope to the child population during midday rest hours.

Limit: Ineffective against adults; negated if the staff is taken from him; duration tied to siesta window.

Invisibility

He is generally invisible to human eyes, able to move through villages undetected while searching for children who are awake during the siesta. This ability distinguishes him from more corporeal forest spirits like Kurupi.

Limit: He may choose to manifest, especially to children; his invisibility is not absolute.

Whistle lure

He possesses a distinct, recognizable whistle that he uses to draw children away from their homes and into the forest. The whistle is described as a specific auditory signature rather than a generic call.

Limit: Only effective on children not napping; efficacy depends on the child's curiosity and the setting.

Treasure guardianship

Jasy Jatere is sometimes described as a protector or keeper of hidden treasures, particularly those buried in forested or remote locations. This role appears most prominently in captivity narratives where a human who has taken his staff demands gold or riches in exchange for its return.

Limit: The treasure motif is secondary and inconsistent; not present in all versions.

Magical kiss (benevolent variant)

In the kinder telling, after playing with children in the forest, he bestows a magical kiss that causes them to forget the entire encounter and return home refreshed at the end of the siesta.

Limit: Only appears in the benevolent version; absent from the darker kidnapper tradition.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Jasy Jatere is unusual among the seven cursed children of Tau and Kerana because he does not inherit a monstrous form. He is consistently described as a diminutive adult or child, standing far below human average height, with light blonde hair and occasionally blue eyes—a striking anomaly in the indigenous Guaraní world, where such features mark him as belonging to a liminal, non-human order. Accounts I
The Seven Cursed Children of Tau and KeranaGuarani creation mythology, as recorded by Jesuit missionaries and twentieth-century ethnographers

pre-colonial oral tradition; first written records sixteenth–twentieth centuries CE

Tau, the spirit of evil, fell in love with the beautiful maiden Kerana. She rejected him, but he kidnapped her, and from their union were born seven monstrous children. The Guaraní word for 'cursed' in this context denotes both their hybrid nature and their destined role as agents of disorder in the world. Jasy Jatere, the fourth child in some enumerations, is unique among them: he is not monstrous. His siblings include Ao Ao (the cannibalistic sheep-monster), Kurupi (the phallic forest abductor), Mbói Tu'ĩ (the serpent-parrot), and others. The myth explains the origin of several types of misfortune, wild animals, and mysterious noises in the forest. Establishes Jasy Jatere as a liminal figure: cursed like his brothers but physically distinct, granting him a unique social role among Guarani spirits.

Accounts II
The Siesta VisitorParaguayan oral tradition; recorded by Heisecke and others

ongoing folk tradition

A child who refuses to nap during the siesta draws the attention of Jasy Jatere. He materializes, shows his golden staff, and the child falls into a trance. He then whistles and leads the child into the forest. In the benevolent version, the child is fed honey and fruit in a hidden glade, plays until weary, receives a memory-erasing kiss, and is deposited back home unharmed. In the darker version, the child is imprisoned in a cave, blinded, and sustained on raw forest produce until feral; in the most gruesome telling, the child is handed over to Ao Ao to be devoured. Reinforces the cultural norm of the siesta; serves as a disciplinary bogeyman for Paraguayan children.

The Captured Jasy JatereFolk narrative; comparative analysis with European leprechaun tales

unclear; possibly post-contact synthesis

In a narrative parallel to the captured leprechaun motif, a human manages to seize Jasy Jatere's golden staff. Deprived of his power source, the spirit breaks down and weeps like a small child. The captor may then demand the location of hidden forest treasures in exchange for returning the staff. The story encodes a transactional logic: the spirit's power is externalized in an object, and that object can be stolen to force compliance. Suggests an indigenous equivalent to the 'fairy gold' and 'leprechaun's pot' narrative patterns found across Eurasia and the Americas.

Jasy Jatere is the Guarani moon goddess. — He is a moon-fragment being, not the moon itself. The moon goddess in Tupi-Guarani cosmology is Jaci (Jasy), a distinct female deity. Jasy Jatere's etymology links him to the moon, but his role is siesta-lord and yerba mate protector, not lunar cycler.

Jasy Jatere is a Guarani demon or devil-figure. — He is a complex liminal spirit, neither wholly evil nor wholly good. His darker actions are contextualized within oral variants used for child discipline, and he retains protective functions (yerba mate, hidden treasures) that have no demonic valence.

All Guarani myths were written down by the Jesuits. — Jesuit missionaries recorded some myths, but the majority of surviving Guarani narratives were transcribed by nineteenth- and twentieth-century ethnographers, linguists, and folklorists working with native speakers. The Guaraní language had no indigenous writing system before modern standardization.
