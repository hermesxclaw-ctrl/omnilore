Who It Is

Skanda (Kartikeya/Murugan) is the god of war, born from Shiva's seed to defeat the demon Tarakasura. Nurtured by six Krittikas (Pleiades), he developed six faces. He rides a peacock and wields the vel — a divine spear. In Tamil Nadu, Murugan is one of the most beloved deities and associated with the Tamil language itself.

Attested in Puranas / Sangam Tamil literatureEra Puranic period to present

Form

Skanda/Kartikeya is typically depicted as an ever-youthful warrior — a handsome, radiant adolescent with one head or six heads (Shanmukha/Arumukha, 'six-faced'). He rides or stands near an Indian peafowl named Paravani, and his battle banner carries an emblem of a rooster. He wields the divine spear called the vel, which his mother Parvati gave him as an embodiment of her shakti. Ancient Yaudheya/Kushan coins show him with one or six heads; early Gandhara/Mathura sculptures show a single head, dressed in a dhoti and armour, spear in one hand, rooster on the other. Tamil sources describe him glowing 'like the sun rising from the emerald sea'. Some early iconography used an elephant mount, giving way to the six-faced-on-peacock form after the 6th century CE.

Powers

Martial supremacy / god of war Skanda-Kartikeya is the divine commander-in-chief of the army of the devas (Devasenapati). He is credited with destroying the asuras Tarakasura, Surapadma (in the Tamil Kanda Puranam), and Simhamukha, and with leading vast celestial armies. The Tarakasura episode in the Skanda Purana describes him hurling a 'terrible shakti' that loosed 'a million missiles' and felled 'a thousand million vehicles'. Skanda Purana; Kanda Puranam; Mahabharata His power is framed by destiny and timing — in the Taraka myth he could only slay the asura before sunset of the seventh day, and initially hesitated because Taraka was a devotee of Rudra (Shiva); Krishna had to manipulate Taraka into attacking Shiva before Kartikeya would strike. Some texts present him as unassailable, while others show the devas' combined onslaught failing against Taraka until Kartikeya's destiny was fulfilled; the older Vedic 'Kumara' layers identify the martial figure with Agni rather than a son of Shiva.

The vel (divine spear) and discrimination The vel is a divine spear granted by his mother Parvati, an embodiment of the power of Shakti. On obtaining it, Kartikeya was 'imparted with the knowledge of distinguishing between good and evil.' It also cleaves reality: in the Surapadma myth the vel splits a colossal mango tree in two, with each half becoming a peacock (his mount) and a rooster (his banner). Kanda Puranam; Skanda Purana; Thaipusam theology The vel is a weapon of justice wielded at his discretion; the myth stresses he hesitated to use it against a Rudra-devotee, showing moral constraint on its power. The vel is consistently his defining weapon across traditions, but the Surapadma mango-tree-split account is Tamil/Kanda-Puranam-specific, and the origin-stories of the weapon differ (given by Parvati vs. forged by a shakti).

Commander and army-builder Shiva granted Kartikeya an army of 30,000 warriors to fight the asura brothers, led by nine sub-commanders headed by Virabahu. These nine men were born from nine lesser clones of Shakti who appeared from her ankle-ornament (silambu). He is the leader of the celestial host and is given the epithet Mahasena ('great warrior'). Kanda Puranam; epigraphic/historical (Mahasena as patron deity of the Yaudheyas) His military role depends on the devas petitioning Shiva and on divine gifts; in some narratives the asuras initially gain the upper hand before his own power turns the war. The number and origin of his lieutenants differ between texts; the nine-Shakti-from-anklet account is specific to the Kanda Puranam.

Philosopher-teacher of Shaiva Siddhanta Far from a pure warrior, Kartikeya is revered as a philosopher-teacher. He is the key exponent of Shaiva Siddhanta theology and (as Subrahmanya) was regarded as a philosopher; as Murugan he is the patron and teacher of Tamil literature and poetry. He teaches the sage Narada (as Sanat-Kumara) in the Chandogya Upanishad, and in the Kanda Puranam he teaches even his father Shiva the meaning of the sacred Aum. Chandogya Upanishad; Shaiva Siddhanta; Tamil tradition This teacher register is most developed in Tamil texts and Shaiva Siddhanta; in the north he is primarily a martial/celibate youth. The historic shift from warrior to philosopher (noted by scholars of the Chola and Vijayanagara periods) means different eras emphasize different power sets; the pre-coalescence Murugan was an ancestor-warrior, while Skanda-as-Subrahmanya was the philosopher.

Cosmic play / childish powers In the Kanda Puranam, the child Kartikeya plays in the cosmos: he fiddles with the orbits of the planets, stacks the mountains of Kailasha on top of Mount Meru, stops the flow of the Ganges, and imprisons Brahma for failing to explain the meaning of Aum (thereby asserting the supremacy of the sacred syllable he then teaches his father). Kanda Puranam These are mythic child-feats emblematic of his divine status rather than codified, repeatable powers; they blend playfulness with cosmic authority. Not present in all texts — the cosmic-play register is characteristic of the Tamil Kanda Puranam, contrasting with the more sober martial registers of Sanskrit sources.

Swiftness / guardian protection (Buddhist manifestation) In the Mahayana Buddhist manifestation as Weituo (Chinese) / Idaten (Japanese), Skanda is famed for extraordinary speed and for warding off fire and theft, protecting monasteries and the Sangha. His vow is to guard the Dharma; in the Golden Light Sutra tradition he chases down a demon who stole the Buddha's relics to retrieve them at Mount Meru. Golden Light Sutra tradition; Chinese/Tibetan/Japanese monastic practice This is a transformed, localized power-set — a monastic guardian's swiftness and protection rather than a war-god's weapons. Whether Weituo is literally the Hindu Kartikeya, a distinct manifestation of Vajrapani, or an independent Chinese development is disputed in the sources; the name-sharing makes the two figures easy to conflate.

Accounts

The central origin narrative motivates the whole cult: Tarakasura won a boon from Brahma that only a son of Shiva could kill him, yet Shiva was a celibate yogi. To end his tyranny, Indra sent Kamadeva (with Rati and Vasanta) to disturb Shiva's meditation and inflame his love for Parvati; Shiva burned Kamadeva to ashes but then turned his attention to Parvati, who had performed severe tapas to win him, and took her as his consort. In the Mahabharata version, Shiva and Parvati were disturbed during sex, Shiva's seed spilled, was incubated in the Ganges and preserved by Agni's heat, and was born as the baby Kartikeya. In the Kanda Puranam (Tamil) version, five additional heads manifested on Shiva and a divine spark flew from each of six heads; the sparks passed from Vayu to Agni, were deposited in the Ganges (which began to evaporate), were carried by Ganga to Saravana Lake, developed into six baby boys, were raised by the six Krittikas, and were then fused by Parvati into the six-headed Kartikeya.

Indra sends the god of love, Kamadeva, with his friend Vasanta (Spring) to interrupt Shiva's meditation and make him fall for Parvati. As Kama looses his flowery arrow and the whole world blooms into a false spring, Shiva is momentarily stirred — then, realizing the interference, grows furious and incinerates Kama to ashes with the fire of his third eye. The episode both delays and enables the divine births needed to defeat Tarakasura. The 'vapourised love spirit roaming free' was later adopted by Vaishnavas (c. 16th century) as having been reincarnated in Vasudeva, and became the theme of Rabindranath Tagore's poem 'Madanbhasmer Par' (after the burning of Kama).

Kartikeya, commander of the devas, meets Tarakasura's vast daitya army commanded by Kalanemi. The asuras initially route the devas; Indra is struck down; Krishna (avatar of Vishnu) joins the battle and duels Kalanemi, who swallows Krishna and Garuda whole before Krishna slices his belly open with the Sudarshana Chakra. Krishna exhorts Kartikeya to slay Taraka before sunset — his seventh day — or the asura cannot be killed. Kartikeya hesitates because Taraka is a devotee of Rudra (Shiva), so Krishna manipulates Taraka into attacking Shiva. At the climax a woman emerges from Tarakasura's head: she is the Shakti who had resided in him since his great penance, and she deserts him because his adharma (unrighteousness) has outweighed his dharma. Kartikeya seizes that Shakti and pierces Taraka's heart with it, fulfilling his destiny.

The asura brother Surapadma, who with Tarakasura and Simhamukha had won near-immortality by worshipping Shiva, harried the devas, killed several of Indra's sons and coveted Indrani, Indra's wife. Kartikeya's messenger Viravakutevar urged Surapadma to desist; when he refused, Murugan declared war. All of Surapadma's sons save one were slain. Unwilling to concede, Surapadma retreated to the sea and took the form of a colossal mango tree spreading across the three worlds. Murugan split the tree with his vel: one half became a peacock (which he took as his vahana) and the other a rooster (his battle standard). The site of the final victory is identified as Tiruchendur, and the festival of Thaipusam commemorates it. The slaying is said to mark the onset of the Kali Yuga.

The sage Narada presented Shiva with a Gnana palam, a fruit of knowledge (usually regarded as a mango). Shiva proposed dividing it between his sons Ganesha and Kartikeya, but Narada said it could not be divided, so it would go to whichever son first circled the world three times. Kartikeya set off at once on his peacock; Ganesha, however, reasoned that the world was nothing more than his parents Shiva and Shakti combined, circumambulated them, and won the fruit. Returning to find himself outwitted, a furious Kartikeya discarded all his worldly belongings and left Kailasha, settling in the Palani Hills as a hermit. In a related Palani-lore episode, the sage Agastya sent the asura Idumban to carry two hillocks (Sivagiri and Shaktigiri) south; Idumban set them down at Palani and could not lift one, fought a youth (Kartikeya) who stood atop it, and was defeated. Agastya identified the youth, and the hill(s) stayed at Palani; Kartikeya later revived Idumban as his devotee. Idumban's carrying of the hills inspired the practice of kavadi-attam.

Content Notes

Kartikeya/Mahasena was the patron deity of the warlike Yaudheya confederacy and later the Kushans and (after Samudragupta's conquest of the Yaudheyas) the Gupta empire, whose emperors (Kumaragupta I, Skandagupta) patronized him. His martial identity elevated raiding and conquest; his heavenly 'army of the devas' mirrored and legitimized terrestrial kingly armies. This is a militarist cult function that contemporary devotional framing largely softens into cosmic metaphor.

Epigraphical-numismatic evidence documents the war-god's patronage; scholars read his rise as tied to the militarized polities of North/Central India in the 1st-4th c. CE.

Kartikeya article (Historical development); Bilsad pillar inscription

The core cycle is built on killing that the gods deliberately engineer: Tarakasura is slain by a god created for exactly that purpose (and only after Krishna plots to make him attack Shiva); Surapadma is sliced apart with a spear. The narratives carry triumphalist, even genocidal-register language (deva armies cutting 'masses of daitya warriors'). This glorification of divinely-ordained violence is the classic war-god register and resists modern sanitization.

Scholars analyze these as moral-cosmology war stories encoding dharma-vs-adharma; not literal history. The 'born specifically to kill X' structure is a recurring asura-boon trope.

Skanda Purana / Kanda Puranam; Tarakasura; Surapadma

Devotees pierce their skin, tongue, or cheeks with vel skewers, bear heavy (up to 30 kg) kavadis attached to the body through metal hooks, practice fire-walking and flagellation, and enter trance. The practice is framed as a 'burden' balancing a spiritual debt (sometimes ancestral/familial obligation). It was banned/suppressed in India proper (public self-mutilation prohibited by law) but flourishes, notably in Malaysia (Batu Caves) and Singapore.

Anthropologists note the trance-claim that devotees are 'able to enter a trance to overcome pain'; the debt-bondage framing ties the ritual to petition/thanks for a loved one's healing rather than self-harm for its own sake.

Kavadi Aattam; Thaipusam

Several canonical birth variants involve covert sexuality bordering on deception/coercion: Svaha impersonates six of the (married) Saptarshi wives in turn to have sex with Agni (Vana Parva); Shiva and Parvati are 'disturbed during sex' and Shiva inadvertently spills semen (the catalytic 'accident'). The Brahma-blessed and Indra-orchestrated attempt to make Shiva conceive strays into divine manipulation of a consort. These frames normalize non-consensual/concealed intimacy among divine beings.

The Svaha-impersonation account is frankly a tale of tricked sexuality; the 'disturbed during sex' frame is the seed myth for the whole cycle.

Mahabharata Vana Parva (Svaha/Agni); Mahabharata Shalya/Anushasana Parvas; Kumarasambhava

Kalidasa's Canto VIII, depicting the lovemaking of Shiva and Parvati (the divine parents whose son is the god), was suppressed from many editions and denounced as blasphemous by medieval moralists (Vāmana, Kṣemendra, Ānandavardhana debated it); a legend holds Parvati cursed Kalidasa for the 'obscene' descriptions so he could not finish the poem. Modern English renderings omit the explicit passages for 'moral tastes' — an example of sanctioned religion silencing erotic art.

Scholarship (Mallinātha commentary history; Vāmana; Kṣemendra's criticism of 'thematic impropriety'; modern defense by Arunagirinatha) shows a long battle over whether depicting divine marital intimacy is indecent — read as both a censorship case and a debate on representing high deities as ordinary couples.

Kumarasambhava (Canto VIII discussion)

Several female figures are cast in subordinate, contested, or sexualised roles: Surapadma covets Indrani; Valli is a tribal 'daughter given' in marriage; the nine sub-commanders are 'borne by nine shaktis from Parvati's broken anklet'; Rati is widowed by Kama's immolation; the Krittika mothers are reduced to surrogate nursemaids. Classical and modern feminist critique flags the male-centric divine power structure.

Reflects the broader patriarchal lens of the Puranic/epic corpus rather than being unique to Skanda; the 'shakti as weapon given by mother to son' dynamic partially re-centres feminine power.

Scholarly Debates
Parentage: Shiva/Parvati vs Agni/Svaha vs Korravai
Skanda is universally the son of Shiva and Parvati (Puranic/Shaiva mainstream). ⇌ The Vana Parva of the Mahabharata makes him the son of Agni and Svaha; early Tamil Sangam tradition names Korravai his mother; Rigvedic 'Kumara' is sometimes Agni, son of Ushas and Purusha.
Evidence for A: Skanda Purana, Mahabharata Shalya/Anushasana Parvas, Shiva Purana, Kumarasambhava.
Evidence for B: Mahabharata Vana Parva (Svaha impersonating the Saptarshi wives); Tolkappiyam/Sangam; Rigveda 5.2 and Shatapatha Brahman
One head vs six heads (Shanmukha)
Skanda is six-faced (Shanmukha/Arumukha), fused from six boys or bearing six Krittika-raised births. ⇌ One-headed depictions are equally (indeed more) ancient and common — Yaudheya/Kushan coins are mostly single-headed; six-head iconography is post-Gupta.
Evidence for A: Six-spark birth (Skanda Purana); Tirumurukarruppatai describes six faces and twelve arms.
Evidence for B: Numismatic and sculptural record; the Santorana-style single-head images.
Celibate bachelor vs married consort
Kartikeya is a celibate bachelor (Kumara), especially in North/East Indian traditions where he is a perpetual youth. ⇌ He is married — to Devasena/Devayanai (and, in Tamil tradition, also Valli).
Evidence for A: North/East worship as the unmarried Kumara (e.g., clay models for newlyweds at Kartik festival); the celibate hermit of
Evidence for B: Panguni Uthiram celebrates his marriage to Devasena; Mahabharata and Skanda Purana give him the consort Devasena; Tamil
Murugan 'adopted' vs 'composite' vs 'same deity all along'
Murugan was a Tamil deity later adopted and incorporated into the North Indian pantheon (Raman Varadara). ⇌ Murugan is a composite of a southern Murugan and a northern Skanda/Mahasena (G. S. Ghurye), or they coalesced over time while some Tamil traits remained unique (Norman Cutler).
Evidence for A: Sangam literature's independently-dated Tamil Murugan.
Evidence for B: Archaeological/epigraphical evidence; the syncretic blending visible in coins and later iconography.
God of war-hero vs god of wisdom/teacher
Skanda is pre-eminently the god of war and army commander (Mahasena). ⇌ Skanda is a philosopher-teacher and patron of Tamil knowledge (Subrahmanya; Shaiva Siddhanta exponent; Sanat-Kumara of the Chandogya Upanishad).
Evidence for A: Martial Puranic narratives; royal war-cult patronage.
Evidence for B: Chandogya Upanishad ch.7 (Sanat-Kumara); late-Chola elevation of the teaching role; the 'vel as wisdom' symbolism.

A way to hold it in your mind

Before he was a name on a spear, he was a plan hatched in heaven because a demon had rigged the cosmos: Tarakasura had bargained with Brahma to be invincible except against the son of a god who seemed certain never to have one. So the gods broke Shiva's meditation with love, paid for it by burning the god of desire to ash, and pointed Shiva's awakened eye toward Parvati—until six blazing sparks leapt from his six faces, fell through fire into the Ganges, grew into six boys, and were fused into one radiant, six-faced, ever-young god who rides a peacock, carries a spear that is his mother's own power, and who would become, by turns, the commander of heaven's army, the hermit of Palani, the teacher of a Tamil tongue, and — under another name — a monk's swift guard against thieves and fire. Skanda is the rare deity who is at once the most Indian god of war and a quiet teacher of the soul, and every one of those faces is authentically his.
