Who It Is

Before he was a name on a spear, he was a plan hatched in heaven because a demon had rigged the cosmos: Tarakasura had bargained with Brahma to be invincible except against the son of a god who seemed certain never to have one. So the gods broke Shiva's meditation with love, paid for it by burning the god of desire to ash, and pointed Shiva's awakened eye toward Parvati—until six blazing sparks leapt from his six faces, fell through fire into the Ganges, grew into six boys, and were fused into one radiant, six-faced, ever-young god who rides a peacock, carries a spear that is his mother's own power, and who would become, by turns, the commander of heaven's army, the hermit of Palani, the teacher of a Tamil tongue, and — under another name — a monk's swift guard against thieves and fire. Powers
Martial supremacy / god of war

Skanda-Kartikeya is the divine commander-in-chief of the army of the devas (Devasenapati). He is credited with destroying the asuras Tarakasura, Surapadma (in the Tamil Kanda Puranam), and Simhamukha, and with leading vast celestial armies. The Tarakasura episode in the Skanda Purana describes him hurling a 'terrible shakti' that loosed 'a million missiles' and felled 'a thousand million vehicles'.

Limit: His power is framed by destiny and timing — in the Taraka myth he could only slay the asura before sunset of the seventh day, and initially hesitated because Taraka was a devotee of Rudra (Shiva); Krishna had to manipulate Taraka into attacking Shiva before Kartikeya would strike.

The vel (divine spear) and discrimination

The vel is a divine spear granted by his mother Parvati, an embodiment of the power of Shakti. On obtaining it, Kartikeya was 'imparted with the knowledge of distinguishing between good and evil.' It also cleaves reality: in the Surapadma myth the vel splits a colossal mango tree in two, with each half becoming a peacock (his mount) and a rooster (his banner).

Limit: The vel is a weapon of justice wielded at his discretion; the myth stresses he hesitated to use it against a Rudra-devotee, showing moral constraint on its power.

Commander and army-builder

Shiva granted Kartikeya an army of 30,000 warriors to fight the asura brothers, led by nine sub-commanders headed by Virabahu. These nine men were born from nine lesser clones of Shakti who appeared from her ankle-ornament (silambu). He is the leader of the celestial host and is given the epithet Mahasena ('great warrior').

Limit: His military role depends on the devas petitioning Shiva and on divine gifts; in some narratives the asuras initially gain the upper hand before his own power turns the war.

Philosopher-teacher of Shaiva Siddhanta

Far from a pure warrior, Kartikeya is revered as a philosopher-teacher. He is the key exponent of Shaiva Siddhanta theology and (as Subrahmanya) was regarded as a philosopher; as Murugan he is the patron and teacher of Tamil literature and poetry. He teaches the sage Narada (as Sanat-Kumara) in the Chandogya Upanishad, and in the Kanda Puranam he teaches even his father Shiva the meaning of the sacred Aum.

Limit: This teacher register is most developed in Tamil texts and Shaiva Siddhanta; in the north he is primarily a martial/celibate youth.

Cosmic play / childish powers

In the Kanda Puranam, the child Kartikeya plays in the cosmos: he fiddles with the orbits of the planets, stacks the mountains of Kailasha on top of Mount Meru, stops the flow of the Ganges, and imprisons Brahma for failing to explain the meaning of Aum (thereby asserting the supremacy of the sacred syllable he then teaches his father).

Limit: These are mythic child-feats emblematic of his divine status rather than codified, repeatable powers; they blend playfulness with cosmic authority.

Swiftness / guardian protection (Buddhist manifestation)

In the Mahayana Buddhist manifestation as Weituo (Chinese) / Idaten (Japanese), Skanda is famed for extraordinary speed and for warding off fire and theft, protecting monasteries and the Sangha. His vow is to guard the Dharma; in the Golden Light Sutra tradition he chases down a demon who stole the Buddha's relics to retrieve them at Mount Meru.

Limit: This is a transformed, localized power-set — a monastic guardian's swiftness and protection rather than a war-god's weapons.

Accounts I
The birth of the six-headed war-godMahabharata (Shalya/Anushasana Parva); Skanda Purana; Kumarasambhava; Kanda Puranam

Epic (Shalya/Anushasana registers) through medieval (Kanda Puranam, 17th century)

The central origin narrative motivates the whole cult: Tarakasura won a boon from Brahma that only a son of Shiva could kill him, yet Shiva was a celibate yogi. To end his tyranny, Indra sent Kamadeva (with Rati and Vasanta) to disturb Shiva's meditation and inflame his love for Parvati; Shiva burned Kamadeva to ashes but then turned his attention to Parvati, who had performed severe tapas to win him, and took her as his consort. In the Mahabharata version, Shiva and Parvati were disturbed during sex, Shiva's seed spilled, was incubated in the Ganges and preserved by Agni's heat, and was born as the baby Kartikeya. In the Kanda Puranam (Tamil) version, five additional heads manifested on Shiva and a divine spark flew from each of six heads; the sparks passed from Vayu to Agni, were deposited in the Ganges (which began to evaporate), were carried by Ganga to Saravana Lake, developed into six baby boys, were raised by the six Krittikas, and were then fused by Parvati into the six-headed Kartikeya. The burning of Kamadeva and the seeds of the warKumarasambhava (Canto III); Tarakasura account

c. 5th century CE (Kumarasambhava)

Indra sends the god of love, Kamadeva, with his friend Vasanta (Spring) to interrupt Shiva's meditation and make him fall for Parvati. As Kama looses his flowery arrow and the whole world blooms into a false spring, Shiva is momentarily stirred — then, realizing the interference, grows furious and incinerates Kama to ashes with the fire of his third eye. The episode both delays and enables the divine births needed to defeat Tarakasura. The 'vapourised love spirit roaming free' was later adopted by Vaishnavas (c. 16th century) as having been reincarnated in Vasudeva, and became the theme of Rabindranath Tagore's poem 'Madanbhasmer Par' (after the burning of Kama). Kama is destroyed (his wife Rati laments in Canto IV), and Shiva's wrath gives way to his union with Parvati, ultimately producing Kartikeya. The episode is a foundational dark/eros story about ascetic power versus divine love.

Accounts II
The war with Tarakasura and the deserting ShaktiSkanda Purana (via the Tarakasura article)

Puranic

Kartikeya, commander of the devas, meets Tarakasura's vast daitya army commanded by Kalanemi. The asuras initially route the devas; Indra is struck down; Krishna (avatar of Vishnu) joins the battle and duels Kalanemi, who swallows Krishna and Garuda whole before Krishna slices his belly open with the Sudarshana Chakra. Krishna exhorts Kartikeya to slay Taraka before sunset — his seventh day — or the asura cannot be killed. Kartikeya hesitates because Taraka is a devotee of Rudra (Shiva), so Krishna manipulates Taraka into attacking Shiva. At the climax a woman emerges from Tarakasura's head: she is the Shakti who had resided in him since his great penance, and she deserts him because his adharma (unrighteousness) has outweighed his dharma. Kartikeya seizes that Shakti and pierces Taraka's heart with it, fulfilling his destiny. Tarakasura is slain, the devas are delivered, and Kartikeya is vindicated as the gods' commander. The Shakti-abandonment motif frames the demon's death as the desertion of divine grace from the wicked.

The slaying of Surapadma: peacock and rooster from the mango treeKanda Puranam; Skanda Purana; celebrated at Thaipusam

Kanda Puranam (c. 17th century); festival theology

The asura brother Surapadma, who with Tarakasura and Simhamukha had won near-immortality by worshipping Shiva, harried the devas, killed several of Indra's sons and coveted Indrani, Indra's wife. Kartikeya's messenger Viravakutevar urged Surapadma to desist; when he refused, Murugan declared war. All of Surapadma's sons save one were slain. Unwilling to concede, Surapadma retreated to the sea and took the form of a colossal mango tree spreading across the three worlds. Murugan split the tree with his vel: one half became a peacock (which he took as his vahana) and the other a rooster (his battle standard). The site of the final victory is identified as Tiruchendur, and the festival of Thaipusam commemorates it. The slaying is said to mark the onset of the Kali Yuga. The asura is destroyed, and his transformed halves become two of Kartikeya's defining symbols (peacock mount, rooster flag). Thaipusam (with its kavadi body-piercing rites) celebrates the victory.

The fruit of wisdom: Ganesha circumambulates the parentsKanda Puranam; Kavadi/Idumban tradition; Palani temple lore

Puranic/Tamil folklore

The sage Narada presented Shiva with a Gnana palam, a fruit of knowledge (usually regarded as a mango). Shiva proposed dividing it between his sons Ganesha and Kartikeya, but Narada said it could not be divided, so it would go to whichever son first circled the world three times. Kartikeya set off at once on his peacock; Ganesha, however, reasoned that the world was nothing more than his parents Shiva and Shakti combined, circumambulated them, and won the fruit. Returning to find himself outwitted, a furious Kartikeya discarded all his worldly belongings and left Kailasha, settling in the Palani Hills as a hermit. In a related Palani-lore episode, the sage Agastya sent the asura Idumban to carry two hillocks (Sivagiri and Shaktigiri) south; Idumban set them down at Palani and could not lift one, fought a youth (Kartikeya) who stood atop it, and was defeated. Agastya identified the youth, and the hill(s) stayed at Palani; Kartikeya later revived Idumban as his devotee. Idumban's carrying of the hills inspired the practice of kavadi-attam. Skanda and Murugan are two different gods who happen to look alike. — They are the same deity as it evolved over time: the Tamil Murugan and the Northern Vedic/Sanskrit Skanda-Kartikeya coalesced (per mainstream scholarship) into one figure, with some uniquely Tamil traits surviving. Modern devotional practice treats them as one. The distinction scholars draw is historical/stratal, not two separate living gods.

The 2023 film 'Skanda' is a mythological adaptation of the god. — It is a contemporary Telugu action-drama about a violent anti-hero named Skanda; it borrows only the name (god-of-war resonance) and is not an adaptation of any Skanda mythology.

Skanda's 'six heads' means he literally has six personalities or is a six-fold god. — The six faces symbolize his six Krittika nurses / six fused births and his multifaceted nature (and twelve arms in Tamil description), not six separate persons or a trinity-like multiplicity.

The Buddhist Skanda (Weituo/Idaten) is simply the Hindu war-god who converted. — The relationship is genuinely debated — Weituo may derive from the Hindu Kartikeya or be a Vajrapani manifestation or an independent figure. His Chinese-monastery guardian function (relic-recovery, dharmapala) is distinct from the Hindu war-god's cycle.

Kartikeya was historically the pre-eminent all-India god and Ganesha 'stole his thunder' recently. — Skanda's prominence did decline after the 7th century in the north/west as Ganesha's rose, while southern Murugan cults grew — but the relationship is a long process, not a simple usurpation, and Murugan remains hugely prominent in the south and Tamil diaspora.

The Skanda Purana is a book about the god's biography. — Despite being named after Skanda, he does not feature more prominently in it than in other Shaiva Puranas; it is chiefly an encyclopedic sacred-geography (tirtha-mahatmya) and theology text sanctifying pilgrimage sites across South Asia.
