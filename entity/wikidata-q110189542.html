Who It Is

For fifteen years a city of welded rooms tumbled silently above Earth at eight kilometres per second — hissing with oxygen generators, glittering with solar wings, scarred by fire and collision — and inside it, humans learned for the first time how to actually live in space. Its name was Mir, Russian for both 'peace' and 'world,' and it was both: a fragile peace between Cold War rivals and a whole world in orbit.

As a machine, Mir behaved as a semi-autonomous, continuously inhabited microgravity laboratory in low Earth orbit (296-421 km, 51.6° inclination, period ~89 min, 15.7 orbits/day). Attitude control via 6-12 gyrodynes (control moment gyros) plus thrusters; orbit maintenance via Progress reboosts. Life support: closed-loop oxygen via Elektron electrolysis (water → O2 + H2) and Vozdukh molecular sieve CO2 scrubbers, with backup solid-fuel oxygen generators (SFOG/Vika) and stored O2. Name & Tongue

Etymology — Russian мир (mir), a homonym meaning both 'peace' and 'world' (also 'commune/society' in pre-revolutionary usage). From Proto-Slavic *mirъ 'peace, world'. The dual meaning was intentionally invoked by Soviet publicity: a peaceful world in orbit. IPA: [ˈmʲir].

Pronunciation — [ˈmʲir] — English approximation 'meer' (one syllable, rhymes with 'near'). Russian м-и-р, stressed on the single vowel.

Powers
Modular Expansion and Reconfiguration

First station to use Lyappa arm to relocate 20-tonne modules from axial to lateral ports, enabling incremental growth from one to seven pressurized modules without shutdown.

Limit: Node had only two Konus drogues; required depressurization and EVA to manually relocate drogue before each new module. Lyappa arm single-point failure would strand module on axial port.

Long-Duration Life Support

Elektron water electrolysis (up to 1 kg O2/day per unit), Vozdukh CO2 removal, and water recovery from humidity condensate and urine (SRVK-2M) allowed continuous habitation for 3,644 days cumulative and 437-day single flight.

Limit: Elektron prone to failure and required frequent maintenance; Vozdukh degraded; SFOG candles caused 1997 fire; water loop never fully closed — required Progress resupply of ~2.5 tonnes consumables per 2 months.

Microgravity Research Suite

~23,000 experiments in biology (quail, wheat, protein crystals), human physiology (cardiovascular, bone loss, immune), materials (semiconductors, alloys in Gallar, Korund furnaces), astrophysics (Kvant-1 Roentgen X-ray, Glazar UV), Earth observation and meteorology.

Limit: Vibration from gyrodynes and crew movement degraded microgravity quality to 10^-4–10^-5 g during operations; power and thermal constraints limited furnace throughput.

Orbital Control and Resupply Interface

Automated docking via Kurs (Progress, Soyuz, TKS-derived modules), refueling via aft port propellant transfer, and reboost via Progress engines to counter decay (~1.5-2 km/month). Supported Soyuz, Progress, and Shuttle (via APAS-89 on Docking Module).

Limit: Kurs failures caused manual TORU dockings (notably Progress M-34 collision 1997); aft propellant lines leaked; Shuttle could only dock via Kristall/Docking Module, not Core aft.

Extravehicular Activity Platform

Two airlocks (Kvant-2 extended, Core backup) supporting 80 EVAs totaling ~400 hours: assembly, repair, science (exposed cassettes), and manual drogue moves. Strela cranes allowed translation along hull without tethered free flight.

Limit: Orlan-DM suits limited to ~6 hrs, required pre-breathe; Kvant-2 airlock hatch failed to seal in 1990, requiring contingency.

Power Generation and Distribution

GaAs and Si photovoltaic arrays on Core, Kvant-1, Kvant-2, Kristall, Spektr, Priroda feeding 28V DC bus with Ni-Cd batteries for eclipse. Peak ~42 kW design, ~25 kW usable late mission.

Limit: Array degradation from radiation and debris (~0.5%/month), shadowing by modules, and loss of Spektr arrays after collision permanently reduced power.

Accounts I
Decree and Rush to the Pad — Building Mir Under a Political DeadlineCPSU Decree 17 Feb 1976; KB Salyut drawings 1982-1983; Energia histories; Siddiqi (2000)

1976-1986

Mir was authorized as an improved Salyut with a five-port spherical node to host 20-tonne TKS-derived modules. Development stalled when resources were diverted to Buran. In early 1984 Valentin Glushko was ordered to orbit Mir by early 1986 for the 27th Party Congress. On 12 April 1985 the unfinished Base Block was shipped to Baikonur with 1,100 of 2,500 cables needing rework. Communications tests in October, a scrub on 16 Feb 1986, and successful Proton launch on 19 Feb 1986 at 21:28 UTC met the deadline by six days. The station entered orbit as the USSR's last great orbital project. Established third-generation modular architecture; DOS-7 became Mir Core, DOS-8 became ISS Zvezda. Demonstrated Soviet ability to launch a station on political schedule despite incomplete ground testing, at cost of early on-orbit anomalies.

First Inhabitants and the Only Station-to-Station Voyage — Soyuz T-15Soyuz T-15 flight logs; TASS reports; NASA Historical Data Book

1986-03-13 to 1986-07-16

Leonid Kizim and Vladimir Solovyov launched on Soyuz T-15 to activate Mir (EO-1). After 50 days they undocked, flew to the aging Salyut 7 4,000 km away, docked, retrieved 400 kg of experiments and equipment, conducted two EVAs, then returned to Mir — the only crew transfer between two stations in history. They completed Mir commissioning before returning to Earth. The feat proved inter-orbital navigation and station mothballing. Validated Mir as operational; salvaged Salyut 7 science; remains unique in spaceflight history. Soyuz T-15's 125-day flight set endurance template.

Growing the Crystal City — Kvant-1, Kvant-2 and KristallEnergia module documentation; Portree (1995) Mir Hardware Heritage; mission reports 1987-1990

1987-1990

Kvant-1 (astrophysics, 11 Apr 1987) arrived via TKS tug to the aft port after a Kurs failure forced manual approach — adding X-ray telescopes that discovered new sources. Kvant-2 (6 Dec 1989) brought the large airlock, water recycling, and gyrodynes; it docked fore then Lyappa-armed to the lateral port after cosmonauts EVA-relocated the drogue. Kristall (10 Jun 1990) added materials furnaces for semiconductor and protein crystal growth and a special androgynous docking port for Buran/Shuttle. Each arrival required depressurizing the node and hand-moving the docking cone — a dangerous, unprecedented procedure. Mir became a true multi-module laboratory; Kristall's APAS-89 port later enabled Shuttle dockings, foreshadowing ISS.

Accounts II
Polyakov's Year and the Endurance RecordsRKA medical reports; NASA Life Sciences Data Archive; Polyakov interviews

1994-01-08 to 1995-03-22

Physician-cosmonaut Valeri Polyakov launched on Soyuz TM-18 (EO-15/16/17) and remained aboard Mir for 437 days 17 hours — the longest single human spaceflight ever, still unbroken. He orbited Earth ~7,000 times, exercising 2 hrs daily, conducting biomedical self-experiments to prove humans could survive a Mars transit. He landed walking, albeit with assistance. Mir itself accumulated 3,644 days of continuous occupation (1989-1999), a record until ISS surpassed it in 2010. Provided core data for long-duration physiology (bone loss ~1.5%/month, muscle atrophy, immune changes) used for ISS and Mars planning. Demonstrated psychological endurance limits.

The International House — Euromir, Shuttle-Mir and a Crowded OrbitESA Euromir reports 1994-95; NASA SP-4225 Shuttle-Mir; STS-71, STS-74 logs

1992-1998

After the USSR's collapse, Russia invited partners to fund Mir. ESA's Euromir missions (Ulf Merbold 1994, Thomas Reiter 1995 — 179 days, first ESA long-duration) and NASA's Shuttle-Mir (1995-1998) kept Mir alive. STS-71 (June 1995) saw Atlantis dock to Kristall, exchange crews (Norman Thagard up, 115 days), and briefly host 10 people — Mir's peak occupancy. STS-74 delivered the 4.7-tonne Docking Module to allow Shuttle clearance. Seven NASA long-duration astronauts (Thagard, Shannon Lucid 188 days, John Blaha, Jerry Linenger, Michael Foale, David Wolf, Andrew Thomas) lived on Mir, learning hard lessons about language, safety culture and autonomy. Preserved Russian human spaceflight through economic collapse; taught NASA how to build and operate ISS with Russia; exposed cultural and safety frictions that later shaped ISS protocols.

Fire and Night — The 1997 CrisesRKA anomaly reports; NASA Shuttle-Mir oral histories; Foale/Linenger memoirs; CAIB background

1997-02-23 to 1997-06-25

On 23 Feb 1997 a solid-fuel oxygen generator (SFOG/Vika) ignited in Kvant-1, spewing flame and toxic smoke for 90 seconds; crew donned masks, fire blocked access to one Soyuz. The fire melted insulation and was extinguished with difficulty. Four months later, on 25 June 1997, Progress M-34, under manual TORU control by Vasily Tsibliyev, collided with Spektr at ~3 m/s, puncturing the module, decompressing it, and crippling 40% of power. Michael Foale and crewmates sealed Spektr's hatch after cutting cables, saving the station but leaving Spektr permanently vacuum. Attitude control was lost for days until power and gyrodynes were restored. Mir survived but was wounded; NASA debated abandoning Shuttle-Mir. Spektr remained sealed, power limited, and station entered terminal decline. Lessons directly fed ISS fire and debris procedures.

The Last Light — Deorbit Over the PacificTsUP deorbit plan; Progress M1-5 flight data; US Space Command tracking; Koptev statement 2001

2000-2001 (deorbit 23 Mar 2001)

Funding cut off — ISS commitments and MirCorp's failed commercial lease left no budget. RKA chose controlled deorbit over abandonment. Progress M1-5, launched 24 Jan 2001, docked to Mir and on 23 March fired three burns to lower perigee. At ~05:44 UTC Mir entered atmosphere over the South Pacific, breaking up at 90-80 km; debris fell near 40°S 160°W (Point Nemo area). Total cost cited by Yuri Koptev as $4.2bn over lifetime. Five of the ten longest flights ever remained Mir flights as of 2026. Zvezda (DOS-8) — Mir's backup core — already orbited as ISS's service module, carrying Mir's DNA forward. Ended 15-year orbital life (12.5 years inhabited, 86,331 orbits, 105 visitors from 12 nations, 80 EVAs). Cleared orbit for ISS; established precedent for controlled deorbit of large stations. Mir's legacy hardware and lessons live on in ISS Russian Segment.

Mir was abandoned and fell out of the sky uncontrolled. — Mir's deorbit was fully controlled via Progress M1-5 burns targeting the South Pacific Uninhabited Area near Point Nemo. An uncontrolled decay would have risked populated areas; Russia spent final funds to ensure controlled entry.

Mir means only 'peace' and was named to signal the Cold War was over. — Mir means both 'peace' and 'world' — the name predates détente (decreed 1976) and was chosen for modular 'world' connotation as much as peace. Shuttle-Mir cooperation came a decade later.

The 1997 fire destroyed Mir and forced immediate evacuation. — The SFOG fire burned ~90 seconds, filled the station with smoke, but was extinguished. Crew remained aboard for two more years. The station was never evacuated; both Soyuz lifeboats remained docked.

Mir was a single launch like Skylab. — Mir was assembled from seven separately launched pressurized modules over ten years (1986-1996) plus external trusses — the first LEGO-like station. Core alone was 20.9 tonnes; completed complex ~130 tonnes.

No one died on Mir, so it was safe. — No fatalities occurred, but Mir experienced multiple life-threatening events (fire, collision, coolant leaks, power loss, tumbling) with significant luck involved. Safety margins eroded as it operated beyond design life without full redundancy.
