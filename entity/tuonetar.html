Tuonetar

Mistress of the Dead, Queen of Tuonela, Brewer of Oblivion

Who They Are

Tuonetar is the queen of Tuonela, wife of Tuoni, and the dark mother of the Finnish underworld. She is sometimes called 'the good mistress of Tuonela' — a euphemism born of the same fear that led Greeks to call the Furies 'the Kindly Ones.' Her most distinctive function is brewing the beer of oblivion: a dark, bitter drink made from the blood of the dead, served to every soul that crosses the black river into her domain. This drink erases the memories of earthly life, completing the soul's transition from the living to the dead.

Tuonetar is described as an old crone with iron teeth and fingers like the claws of a bird. She stands on the far bank of the river, welcoming — or rather, receiving — the newly dead. Her daughters, the Maidens of Tuoni, assist her: they spin threads of fate, row the boat of the dead across the black river, and serve the beer of oblivion. Together, they form a feminine death-triad not unlike the Greek Moirai (Fates), the Norse Norns, or the Slavic Sudjenice — weavers and spinners who control the transition between life and death.

In the Kalevala, Tuonetar plays a significant role in the resurrection of Lemminkäinen. When Lemminkäinen's mother comes to the banks of the black river seeking her son's dismembered body, Tuonetar's son — the ferryman — initially refuses to help. But Lemminkäinen's mother, with her powerful magic and maternal persistence, eventually recovers every piece of her son's body from the waters. Tuonetar does not actively oppose this — death, in Finnish mythology, is not a moral force but a natural one. The dead are in Tuonela; if someone manages to retrieve them, that is the living's affair.

Tuonetar's brewing of the beer of oblivion connects her to the ancient Indo-European motif of the 'cup of forgetfulness' that souls must drink upon entering the afterlife. The Greek Lethe ('Forgetfulness') is the closest parallel, and the motif appears in Indian, Iranian, and Norse traditions as well. The Finnish version emphasizes the materiality of the drink — it is beer, the everyday drink of the living, but corrupted: made of blood, served in skulls, its effect the annihilation of identity. Tuonetar thus presides over the final transformation of the person: having lived, having died, having crossed the river, the soul now forgets who it was and becomes simply one of the dead — part of the anonymous multitude of Tuonela.

Tuonetar's figure also appears in Finnish folk incantations against death and disease. Healers would sing: 'Tuonetar, good mistress, take back your iron teeth; release this soul from your dark brewing; let this one forget the taste of your beer.' These incantations reveal the folk understanding of illness as a premature taste of death's oblivion — and of healing as the reversal of Tuonetar's hospitality.
