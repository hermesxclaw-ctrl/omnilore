Who It Is

In the Kalevala's most sinister hospitality scene, the Queen of Tuonela offers Väinämöinen a golden goblet of beer — and the drink is brewed from frog spawn, snakes, and worms. Drink it, and you forget you ever existed.

Tuonetar is simultaneously gracious and lethal. She greets arrivals with apparent warmth, offering drink and shelter, but the beer of oblivion (unohduksen olut) annihilates memory and prevents return to the living. She interrogates visitors, refuses to yield sacred knowledge (the three magic words), and mobilizes her entire household — son, nets, river — to detain intruders. She is protective of Tuonela's exclusivity: only her own children may leave freely. Her cruelty is familial and systemic; she births and nurtures plagues, diseases, and death-deities.

Powers
Beer of Oblivion

Tuonetar brews a black poison from frog spawn, venomous snakes, lizards, adders, and worms, presented in a golden goblet as beer. Consumption causes the drinker to forget they ever existed and bars their return to the land of the living. This is her signature weapon against intruders to Tuonela.

Limit:

Magic Wand / Sleep Induction

She wields a magic wand that can put visitors to sleep, used against Väinämöinen when he resists yielding the three magic words.

Limit:

Net-Weaving Household

Her three-fingered son weaves a thousand nets of iron and copper to catch escapees attempting to flee Tuonela via the river of Tuoni. The net infrastructure is automated and exhaustive — Väinämöinen only escapes by shapeshifting into a serpent.

Limit:

Parthenogenetic Fertility

Tuonetar births Kipu-Tyttö, Kivutar, Vammatar, Kalma, Loviatar, and Tuonenpoika, plus numerous plagues, diseases, demons, and monsters. She is the source-code of Finnish death and suffering personified.

Limit:

Gatekeeping Tuonela

She and Tuoni jointly govern Tuonela and enforce its exit-rule: only their children may leave. All other souls are trapped, erased, or enslaved. Her hospitality is a snare.

Accounts I
Väinämöinen's Descent and Escape (Runo XVI)The Kalevala, Runo XVI (Elias Lönnrot, 1849)

The shaman-hero Väinämöinen journeys to Tuonela seeking the three magic words that will close the gaping wound in the sky (a cosmological repair quest). Tuonetar receives him with apparent hospitality, offering the golden goblet of beer-of-oblivion. Väinämöinen perceives the ruse and refuses the drink. When he demands the three words, Tuonetar refuses and declares he will never leave alive. She strikes him with her magic wand, plunging him into sleep. Her three-fingered son weaves a thousand iron and copper nets across the river of Tuoni to prevent escape. Väinämöinen awakens, shapeshifts into a serpent, and slips through the net mesh. He returns to Kalevala and warns humanity to aim wisely in life lest they end up in Tuonela.

Accounts II
The Birth of the Nine Diseases (via Loviatar)The Kalevala, Runo XLV (Elias Lönnrot, 1849)

In Runo 45 of the Kalevala, Lönnrot splits the underworld goddess figure into two: Louhi (queen of Pohjola) and Loviatar (blind daughter of Tuoni). Loviatar is impregnated by a great wind and gives birth to nine sons who are the Nine Diseases — personifications of illness and decay. This episode positions Tuonetar as the matriarchal source of Finnish medical pathology, ruling over a household whose children include plagues and demons.

Kalman Impi and the Serpent's VenomThe Kalevala (various runes); Castrén folk-poem collections

In the Kalevala, Kalman Impi ('Maiden of Kalma') — identified as a daughter or associate of Kalma, herself a child of Tuonetar — gives the snake its venomous fangs. This mythic ascription connects Tuonetar's lineage to the origin of reptilian danger in the Finnish natural world, echoing her own poison-brewing and net-weaving household.
