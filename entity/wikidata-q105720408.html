Who It Is

Xenon, the 'stranger' among the noble gases, was once thought to be utterly inert, a recluse of the periodic table. Yet this heavy, colorless gas, present in Earth's atmosphere only in trace amounts, has been coaxed into forming compounds, has illuminated our world with brilliant flashes, and now serves as a window into the invisible realms of dark matter and the deep history of our solar system.

Xenon is generally unreactive due to its full outer electron shell (eight valence electrons), making it a noble gas. It is, however, more reactive than lighter noble gases and can form compounds with highly electronegative elements like fluorine and oxygen. It is dense, about 4.5 times denser than air, and can act as a solvent when liquid.

Etymology — From the Greek word ξένον (xénon), the neuter singular of ξένος (xénos), meaning 'foreign(er)', 'strange(r)', or 'guest'. The name was suggested by its discoverer, William Ramsay, to reflect its rarity and unfamiliar nature.

Powers
Inertness

Xenon is generally chemically inert due to its complete outer electron shell (8 electrons), making it stable and unreactive under most conditions.

Limit: It can react with strong oxidizing agents like platinum hexafluoride.

Compound formation

Xenon can form compounds, most notably xenon hexafluoroplatinate (the first noble gas compound), as well as fluorides, oxides, and other derivatives.

Limit: Requires highly reactive partners; most compounds involve fluorine or oxygen.

Emission of light

When excited by an electrical discharge, xenon emits a blue or lavender glow, making it useful in flash lamps and arc lamps.

Limit: Requires electrical excitation.

Anesthetic properties

Xenon can act as a general anesthetic, first demonstrated in animals and humans in the 1940s and 1950s.

Limit: It is expensive and requires specialized equipment.

Solvent capability (liquid)

Liquid xenon is an excellent solvent due to its high polarizability, capable of dissolving hydrocarbons, biological molecules, and even water.

Limit: Requires cryogenic conditions.

Metallic phase under pressure

Under gigapascals of pressure, xenon becomes metallic, and at about 155 GPa it is completely metallic, appearing sky blue.

Limit: Requires extreme pressure.

Isotopic tracing

Xenon isotope ratios are used to study the early history of the Solar System and nuclear processes.

Limit: Requires sensitive mass spectrometry.

Neutron absorption (xenon-135)

Xenon-135 is a significant neutron absorber in nuclear reactors, affecting reactor operation.

Limit: It is a fission product and can cause 'xenon poisoning'.

Form & Signs

Appearance — At standard temperature and pressure, xenon is a colorless, odorless, and tasteless gas. In a gas-discharge tube, it emits a blue or lavender glow. As a solid, it is a crystalline material; under high pressure, it becomes a metallic solid that appears sky blue because it absorbs red light.

Symbols — The chemical symbol Xe is used universally. In scientific contexts, xenon is often depicted in diagrams of the periodic table. Its emission spectrum, with intense blue lines, is a characteristic visual signature.

Domains — Xenon is found in trace amounts in Earth's atmosphere (about 87 parts per billion by volume) and in gases from some mineral springs. It is also present in the solar system, notably enriched in Jupiter's atmosphere. Commercially, it is extracted from liquid air.

Accounts I
Discovery of XenonRamsay and Travers, 1898

Late 19th century

After discovering krypton and neon, Ramsay and Travers continued to investigate the residue from evaporating liquid air. On July 12, 1898, they found a new, heavier gas, which they named xenon, from the Greek for 'stranger'. Ramsay estimated its atmospheric abundance as 1 part in 20 million. Established xenon as a new element and expanded knowledge of noble gases.

The First Noble Gas CompoundNeil Bartlett, 1962

1960s

Neil Bartlett, working at the University of British Columbia, discovered that platinum hexafluoride could oxidize oxygen. Noting the similar ionization potentials of O2 and xenon, he hypothesized that PtF6 could also oxidize xenon. On March 23, 1962, he mixed xenon with PtF6 and produced xenon hexafluoroplatinate, the first known noble gas compound, overturning the long-held belief that noble gases were completely inert. Opened up the field of noble gas chemistry; more than 80 xenon compounds were known by 1971.

Accounts II
IBM in AtomsIBM Research, 1989/1990

Late 20th century

IBM scientists used a scanning tunneling microscope to arrange 35 individual xenon atoms on a nickel crystal to spell out 'IBM'. This was the first time atoms had been precisely positioned on a flat surface, demonstrating the ability to manipulate matter at the atomic scale. Pioneered nanotechnology and atomic-scale manipulation.

Xenon AnesthesiaVarious, 1939-1951

Mid 20th century

Albert R. Behnke Jr. studied the effects of breathing mixtures on deep-sea divers and deduced that xenon could serve as an anesthetic. After preliminary work by Nikolay Lazarev (1941) and John H. Lawrence (1946, on mice), Stuart C. Cullen first used xenon as a surgical anesthetic on two patients in 1951. Established xenon as a viable anesthetic, though its use remains limited due to cost and availability.
