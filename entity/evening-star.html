Who It Is

Did you know that the Evening Star, the brilliant planet Venus, was not just a celestial body for the Pawnee people, but a living goddess who created humanity and gave them the gift of corn? Her story is a testament to the deep connection between the sky and the earth, and it challenges our modern understanding of astronomy and mythology.

The Evening Star is portrayed as a nurturing and protective mother figure, embodying the qualities of fertility, wisdom, and compassion. She is said to be gentle and kind, but also powerful and formidable when her children are threatened. In Pawnee myths, she is the mediator between the sky and the earth, and she often intercedes on behalf of humans with the higher deities. She is also a goddess of love and beauty, but her love is not romantic in the Greek sense; rather, it is the love of a mother for her children. Etymology — The term 'Evening Star' refers to the planet Venus when it appears in the western sky after sunset. In the Pawnee language, the star is known as 'Pahukatawa' or 'Pahuk' in some contexts, though the specific name for the deity is often translated as 'The Beautiful Woman' or 'Star Woman.' Powers
Fertility and Agricultural Blessing

The Evening Star has the power to bestow fertility on the earth, ensuring bountiful crops, particularly corn. She is believed to control the growth of plants and the reproduction of animals, and she is invoked in planting and harvesting ceremonies. Her blessing is essential for the survival of the Pawnee people, as she provides the staple food of corn.

Limit:

Celestial Guidance

As the Evening Star, she guides travelers and hunters during the twilight hours, providing light and direction. She is also a navigational aid for the Pawnee, who used the stars for seasonal and ceremonial calendars. Her position in the sky marks the time for planting and other important activities.

Limit:

Creation of Humanity

In Pawnee mythology, the Evening Star, along with the Morning Star, created the first humans. She shaped the first woman from the earth and breathed life into her, making her the mother of all humanity. This power establishes her as a creator deity and a central figure in Pawnee origin stories.

Limit:

Protection and Intercession

She acts as a protector of her people, warding off evil spirits and malevolent forces. She intercedes with the higher gods on behalf of humans, offering prayers and sacrifices to ensure their well-being. Her protective power is invoked in times of danger or illness.

Accounts I
The Creation of HumanityPawnee oral tradition, recorded by George A. Dorsey in 'The Pawnee Mythology' (1906)

Pre-contact to early 20th century

In the beginning, the Evening Star and the Morning Star were the first beings in the sky. They decided to create humans to populate the earth. The Morning Star, a powerful warrior, and the Evening Star, a beautiful woman, came together and created the first man and woman. The Evening Star shaped the woman from the earth and gave her life, while the Morning Star gave the man strength. They placed the humans on the earth and taught them how to live, including the cultivation of corn, which the Evening Star provided. This act established the Pawnee as the children of the stars, and the Evening Star became their mother. The creation of humanity established the Pawnee people as descendants of the celestial deities, giving them a sacred connection to the stars. The Evening Star became the mother of all, and her role as a fertility goddess was solidified. The story also explains the origin of corn, which became the staple crop of the Pawnee.

Accounts II
The Evening Star and the Morning Star SacrificePawnee ritual, recorded by James R. Murie in 'Ceremonies of the Pawnee' (1921)

Pre-contact to 19th century

The Pawnee performed an annual sacrifice to the Morning Star, which involved a captive, usually a young woman, who was offered to the Morning Star. The Evening Star was believed to be the wife of the Morning Star, and she was said to be jealous of the sacrifice, as it was a gift to her husband. In the ritual, the captive was tied to a scaffold and shot with arrows, symbolizing the Morning Star's power. The Evening Star was invoked to ensure the fertility of the earth and the success of the crops. The sacrifice was a complex ceremony that involved the entire community, and it was believed to renew the cosmic order. The sacrifice was believed to ensure the continuation of the world, as it pleased the Morning Star and the Evening Star. It also reinforced the social hierarchy and the power of the priests. The ritual was eventually suppressed by the U.S. government in the 19th century, but it remains a significant part of Pawnee history.

The Evening Star and the Corn MaidenPawnee legend, recorded by George Bird Grinnell in 'Pawnee Hero Stories and Folk-Tales' (1889)

19th century

A young Pawnee man, lost in the forest, encounters a beautiful woman who is the Evening Star in disguise. She gives him a gift of corn and teaches him how to plant and harvest it. She tells him that she is the mother of all, and that he must share this knowledge with his people. The man returns to his village and teaches the people, and they become prosperous. The Evening Star then disappears, but her spirit remains in the cornfields, watching over the people. The story explains the origin of corn and the agricultural practices of the Pawnee. It also reinforces the Evening Star's role as a benevolent provider and teacher. The man becomes a hero, and the story is used to teach the importance of gratitude and respect for the gifts of the gods.
