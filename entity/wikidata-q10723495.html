Who It Is

In the shadowed courts beneath the mortal world, where the rivers of the dead converge, there sits a judge whose name is whispered with both fear and reverence: Yen-Lo, the sovereign arbiter of the afterlife. To the living, he is a distant and terrible figure; to the dead, he is the final authority on the fate of the soul. His court is not a place of mercy or cruelty, but of unyielding cosmic law—where every deed is weighed, every life is accounted, and every soul is assigned its due path toward rebirth or punishment.

Yen-Lo is portrayed as an impartial and incorruptible judge who upholds the cosmic law of karma. He is not inherently evil but is strict and unyielding. He shows no favoritism, and his judgments are based solely on the deeds of the deceased. In some traditions, he is compassionate, offering souls the chance to repent and be reborn. He is also depicted as a powerful ruler who commands a vast bureaucracy of demons and officials in the underworld.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Yen-Lo' is a transliteration of the Chinese 'Yánluó' (閻羅), which itself derives from the Sanskrit 'Yama', the Vedic deity of death. The full title 'Yánluó Wáng' (閻羅王) means 'King Yanluo', often rendered as 'King Yama'. In Chinese, the character '閻' (yán) refers to the gate of the underworl

Pronunciation — Yen-Lo is typically pronounced /jɛn loʊ/ in English. In Mandarin, it is 'Yánluó' (IPA: [jɛ̌n lwǒ]).

Aliases — Yama, Yanluo, Yanluo Wang, King Yan, Yama Raja, Yen-lo Wang

Ownership — Yen-Lo is a figure shared across Chinese folk religion, Daoism, and Chinese Buddhism. He is also known in Japanese as Enma (or Emma), in Korean as Yeomna, and in Vietnamese as Diêm

Powers
Judgment of Souls

Yen-Lo has the authority to judge the souls of the deceased, determining their fate based on their actions in life. He consults the book of life and death, which records every person's deeds, and assigns punishments or rewards accordingly.

Limit: His judgment is bound by karmic law; he cannot arbitrarily alter a soul's fate. In some traditions, his judgments can be influenced by the prayers and rituals of the living.

Authority over the Underworld

Yen-Lo commands the demons and officials of the underworld, including Ox-Head and Horse-Face, and oversees the administration of Diyu. He has the power to summon souls to his court and to direct them to appropriate hells.

Limit: In the Ten Kings tradition, his authority is limited to his specific court and period.

Knowledge of Life and Death

Yen-Lo possesses the book of life and death, which contains the lifespan and fate of every living being. He can read this book to know when a person is to die and what their next rebirth will be.

Limit: The book is said to be maintained by a celestial bureaucracy, and Yen-Lo may not be able to change the entries without higher authority.

Punishment and Reward

Yen-Lo can inflict punishments on souls in hell, ranging from physical torments to prolonged suffering, and can also grant rewards, such as favorable rebirths or heavenly pleasures.

Limit: Punishments are meted out according to karmic desert, not arbitrary will.

Accounts I
The Judgment of the DeadBuddhist and folk traditions, including the Shiwang Jing and popular morality books

From the Tang dynasty (7th-10th century) onwards

After death, a soul is escorted to the underworld by demons. It appears before Yen-Lo, who consults the book of life and death and weighs the soul's deeds on a scale. The soul is then sentenced to appropriate punishments in hell or to a favorable rebirth. The soul may be given a period of time to repent or to receive the merits transferred by living relatives. The soul undergoes punishment or rebirth, and the living are encouraged to perform rituals to aid the deceased.

Accounts II
Sun Wukong's Rebellion in the UnderworldJourney to the West, by Wu Cheng'en (16th century)

Ming dynasty (16th century)

The Monkey King Sun Wukong is summoned to the underworld after his death. Instead of submitting to judgment, he erupts in fury, threatens Yen-Lo, and forcibly erases his name and the names of all monkeys from the book of life and death, thereby gaining immortality. Yen-Lo is powerless to stop him and later appeals to the Jade Emperor. Sun Wukong becomes immortal and defies the celestial order, leading to his eventual subjugation by the Buddha.

The Ten Kings of the UnderworldBuddhist sutras and liturgical texts, including the Shiwang Jing

Tang to Song dynasties (7th-13th century)

The underworld is governed by ten kings, each presiding over a court that judges specific aspects of a soul's life. Yen-Lo is typically the fifth king, presiding over the court that judges souls on the 35th day after death. Each king has a specific role in the process of judgment and rebirth. The Ten Kings tradition shapes funerary rituals, including the offering of prayers and sacrifices on specific days after death.

Yen-Lo is a demon or evil spirit. — Yen-Lo is a deity and judge, not a demon. He is a respected figure in Chinese religion, though fearsome.

Yen-Lo is the same as the Devil or Satan. — Yen-Lo is not a figure of evil; he is an impartial administrator of cosmic justice. The concept of a malevolent devil is absent in traditional Chinese religion.

Yen-Lo is a single, unchanging figure. — Yen-Lo's role and attributes have evolved over time and vary across regions and traditions.

The underworld is a place of eternal punishment. — In Buddhist and Daoist thought, punishments in Diyu are temporary and serve as purification before rebirth. The concept of eternal damnation is not typical.
