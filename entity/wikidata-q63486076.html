Who It Is

The 5th spirit is one of six nameless, voiceless (in solo terms) supernatural attendants in Jules Massenet's 1899 opera Cendrillon — she has no aria, no individual spoken line, and is distinguished only by the number 5 in the score. And yet Wikidata has assigned her a unique entity identifier (Q63486076) with labels in four languages, placing her alongside Hamlet, Sherlock Holmes, and Ariel in the catalogue of fictional characters whose existence has been deemed worth knowing. Why does she matter enough to catalogue? Because in the operatic tradition of the opéra féerie, the minor supernatural figure — the attendant, the escort, the weaver of bowers — is not incidental but structural: without six distinct soprano and mezzo voices sustaining the enchanted atmosphere of Act III, Massenet's carefully built magical world collapses into a single fairy godmother surrounded by extras. The 5th spirit, as a collectively defined minor role within the six-spirit ensemble, is given no independent spoken or sung solo text in the vocal score; her dramatic personality must be inferred from the ensemble's function and from the broader operatic type she inhabits. Magical Transformation Assistance

In Act I the 5th spirit, with her five companions, participates in the collective enchantment that transforms Cendrillon's plain household dress into magnificent ball attire and conjures the legendary glass slippers. The Heugel score's 'Scherzo des esprits' (Act I, Scene VI) is the musical setting for this sequence, a light, rapid piece that characterises the spirits' participation in the Fairy Godmother's ritual of metamorphosis. The transformation magic is cooperative: La Fée provides the directing intelligence and primary power, while the ensemble of spirits amplifies and executes the enchantment. No individual attribute distinguishes the 5th spirit's contribution from that of the others in this collective exercise.

Limit:

Enchanted Bower Creation

In the second tableau of Act III the spirits weave a magical arbour of flowers around the enchanted oak tree, concealing Cendrillon and Prince Charming from each other's view as they are drawn to the same spot by the Fairy Godmother's design. This floral bower is not merely decorative but constitutes a magical trial: the lovers must recognise each other by voice alone, without the distraction of sight, testing the depth and authenticity of their love. The spirits maintain the bower until the lovers' mutual recognition, at which point the flowers dissolve and the pair are revealed to each other. This ability — the conjuration of a living, architecturally purposive floral structure — is a collective supernatural capability unique to the six-spirit ensemble in this opera.

Limit:

Slumber Induction and Protective Vigil

After Cendrillon and Prince Charming exchange their vows of love beneath the enchanted oak, the spirits sustain the enchanted atmosphere that causes both lovers to fall into a mystical, healing sleep. This sleep serves a narrative function: it suspends the lovers in a timeless fairy-tale interval, after which Cendrillon wakes in her father's house with no memory of whether the enchanted encounter was real or a dream. The spirits' vigil during this enchanted sleep protects the lovers from interruption and marks the liminal space of the fairy forest as distinct from ordinary human time. The collective protective function of the six spirits during this slumber scene is underscored by the music of the Act III second tableau.

Limit:

Supernatural Atmosphere Maintenance

Beyond specific acts of transformation or botanical conjuration, the 5th spirit and her companions function throughout their appearances as maintainers of the enchanted atmosphere — the 'bluish light' specified by librettist Caïn in the Act III stage directions, the will-o'-the-wisp (feux follets) ballet environment, and the general separation of the fairy realm from ordinary social reality. Their collective presence signals to the audience that they are in a space where normal causality is suspended and where the logic of fairy tale obtains. In operatic terms this atmospheric function is realised primarily through the choral and ensemble singing of the spirits, whose voices blend into the orchestral texture to create the ethereal sound-world of the supernatural scenes.

Limit:

Inter-Realm Mediation

The spirits serve as intermediaries between the sovereign magical power of La Fée and the human protagonists, channelling the Fairy Godmother's enchantments into the material world. In this mediating role they are essential to the opera's magical mechanics: La Fée cannot simply decree changes in the world without the spirits who execute them. This function parallels the role of seraphic or angelic intermediaries in theological cosmologies and reflects the hierarchical structuring of the supernatural world that characterises the opéra féerie tradition. The 5th spirit, as one of six co-equal mediators, participates in this role without individual priority.

Accounts I
The Transformation of CendrillonMassenet, Jules; Caïn, Henri. Cendrillon. Act I, Scene VI. Éditions Heugel, Paris, 1898/1899.

1899 (opera premiere); narrative derives from Perrault, 1697

After the de la Haltière household departs for the royal ball, Cendrillon falls asleep by the warm hearth, lamenting her exclusion from the festivities. The Fairy Godmother (La Fée) arrives with her six attendant spirits. The spirits participate in the magical transformation of Cendrillon: her humble clothes become a magnificent gown, and the glass slippers that will protect her from recognition by her family are conjured. The spirits' contribution is captured in the 'Scherzo des esprits,' a lively ensemble number that conveys their delight in the magical work. La Fée warns Cendrillon that the enchantment will last only until midnight. The 5th spirit, as part of this ensemble, is present and active throughout the transformation ceremony. Cendrillon is magically equipped to attend the royal ball, initiating the central romantic plot of the opera. Without this transformation, she would have remained at home and never encountered Prince Charming.

The Enchanted Forest: Trial and Reunion at the Magic OakMassenet, Jules; Caïn, Henri. Cendrillon. Act III, second tableau. Éditions Heugel, Paris, 1898/1899.

1899 (opera premiere)

In the second tableau of Act III, set in an enchanted forest beneath a magical oak tree, the six spirits weave a bower of flowers that obscures Cendrillon and Prince Charming from each other's sight even as they are drawn to the same place by the Fairy Godmother's magic. Unable to see each other, the lovers call out, guided only by their voices; they confess their love and recognise each other purely through the power of feeling. The flower arbour then dissolves, revealing the pair to each other. Surrounded by the spirits, who maintain the enchanted atmosphere, the lovers exchange vows of love and fall into a mystical slumber in each other's arms. The 5th spirit participates throughout this scene as part of the collective supernatural ensemble. The magical union of Cendrillon and the Prince is confirmed on a spiritual level prior to their public reunion in Act IV. The slumber they enter preserves them in an enchanted state until the opera's resolution. Cendrillon awakens with only a dreamlike memory of the encounter, which her father initially dismisses as a fever-dream.

Accounts II
The Scherzo des espritsMassenet, Jules. Cendrillon. Act I, Scene VI. Full score, Éditions Heugel, 1898. Catalogued in IMSLP as 'Scherzo des esprits (Act I, Scene VI).'

1898–1899

Act I, Scene VI of the score contains a dedicated musical number catalogued in the International Music Score Library Project as the 'Scherzo des esprits.' This scherzo — a quick, playful, light piece — is the principal musical portrait of the six spirits as an active ensemble. It occurs during or immediately around the magical transformation sequence, and its character (scherzo implies lightness, speed, and wit) confirms the spirits' role at this point as joyful, energetic supernatural helpers rather than solemn ceremonial figures. The 5th spirit participates fully in this ensemble number. Piano arrangements of the Scherzo were published (arranged by Anschütz), indicating its standalone concert currency. The Scherzo establishes the spirits' musical identity in the opera and provides the audience with their most extended musical characterisation. Its playful character sets the emotional register of the Act I magical sequence.

The Grand Première: Opéra-Comique, 24 May 1899Casaglia, Gherardo (2005). 'Cendrillon, 24 May 1899.' L'Almanacco di Gherardo Casaglia. (Premiere cast documentation.)

1899

The world premiere of Cendrillon on 24 May 1899 at the newly refurbished Salle Favart (Opéra-Comique), Paris, marked the first performance of the 5th spirit role. The premiere was conducted by Alexandre Luigini. The role was created by a singer identified in the cast table as 'Craponne,' a soprano. Massenet, following his habitual practice, avoided attending the first night, remaining instead at Enghien-les-Bains with his wife; he received a telegram from bass Lucien Fugère the following day confirming the opera's triumphant reception. The premiere production featured special effects made possible by the theatre's new electric lighting installation, which the spirits' scenes would have exploited for luminous supernatural effects. The opera ran for fifty performances in its first season — an exceptional success by the standards of the period. Cendrillon was established as one of Massenet's most successful works. Modern Revival Cycle: Santa Fe (2006) to Metropolitan Opera (2018)Woolfe, Zachary (13 April 2018). 'Review: The Stepchild of Cinderella Operas Finally Makes It to the Met.' The New York Times.

2006–2018

The Laurent Pelly production of Cendrillon, first staged at the Santa Fe Opera in 2006 with Joyce DiDonato as Cendrillon and Eglise Gutiérrez as La Fée, defined the contemporary canonical staging of the opera and brought the six spirits (including the 5th) to international prominence in a new visual and dramaturgical framework. The production transferred to the Royal Opera House, London, in July 2011 (also with DiDonato and Alice Coote as the Prince, conducted by Bertrand de Billy), was released as a DVD on Virgin Classical, and finally received its Metropolitan Opera premiere in April 2018, 119 years after the world premiere. The New York Times noted that the Met production represented the opera's belated arrival at the world's largest operatic institution. Throughout this revival cycle, the six spirits maintained their ensemble role in the enchanted forest and transformation scenes. The Pelly production revived international interest in Cendrillon and secured its place in the repertoire of major opera houses worldwide. Voice type consistency across productions and editionsThe Heugel vocal score (1898/1899) and the Wikipedia role table derived from primary sources assign the 5th spirit unambiguously to soprano, contrasting with the mezzo-soprano designation of the 1st and 4th spirits. This voice-type differentiation implies that Massenet and Caïn intended at least some degree of timbral variety within the spirit ensemble, with four sopranos (2nd, 3rd, 5th, 6th) and two mezzo-sopranos (1st, 4th) creating a blended but varied sound.In many modern productions the six spirits are collectively described as 'soprano, mezzo-soprano, contralto' (StageAgent) without assignation to specific numbered roles, and casting directors sometimes treat the spirit voices as interchangeable within a flexible ensemble. The Scribd-hosted Castel edition of the libretto lists character voice types but does not always maintain strict one-to-one correspondence with Massenet's original designations. The result is a practical tradition in which the soprano identity of the 5th spirit as distinct from the mezzo-soprano identities of the 1st and 4th is not consistently enforced in production.
Act I versus Act III as the spirits' primary dramatic locusThe IMSLP catalogue entry for Cendrillon explicitly lists 'Scherzo des esprits (Act I, Scene VI)' as a musically significant and independently arranged number, suggesting that Massenet himself regarded Act I as an important showcase for the spirits ensemble. A piano arrangement by Anschütz was published, giving the Scherzo independent concert circulation and implying that Act I is the spirits' most musically characterised moment.Synopsis accounts — including those of the Metropolitan Opera, Glyndebourne, and the New Grove Dictionary — consistently emphasise Act III (the enchanted forest, the magic oak, the bower, the slumber) as the scene in which the spirits make their most theatrically significant contribution. Act III is where they are named in stage directions as surrounding the lovers, where they maintain the enchanted atmosphere, and where the visual spectacle of the spirits as a group is most dramatically developed. From a dramaturgical perspective, Act III is the spirits' defining scene.
Individual versus collective identity of the numbered spiritsMassenet and Caïn's decision to number the spirits from 1 to 6 and to assign each a distinct voice type constitutes an act of individuation: each spirit is a named role with a specific singer identified in the premiere cast list (1st: Marie Delorn; 2nd: Françoise Oswald; 3rd: Vilma; 4th: Stéphane; 5th: Craponne; 6th: Fouqué). The Wikidata database treats the 5th spirit as a discrete entity (Q63486076) with its own multilingual labels and descriptions, confirming that the scholarly and knowledge-organisation communities recognise a meaningful individual identity for this role.No solo aria, recitative, or individually attributed spoken line is assigned to any of the six spirits in the extant vocal score. The spirits function entirely as an undifferentiated chorus in the drama; their numbering is a logistical tool for casting (ensuring appropriate voice-type coverage) rather than a dramaturgical signal of individual character. The StageAgent entry groups all six under the single ensemble listing 'Six Spirits,' and most programme notes and scholarly treatments do not distinguish between the individual spirits beyond their ordinal numbers. In performance the 5th spirit is effectively anonymous.
Relationship to the Perrault source textThe six spirits of Cendrillon are a direct operatic elaboration of the supernatural assistance tradition in Perrault's tale, which features a single omnipotent fairy godmother. Caïn and Massenet multiplied and differentiated this source figure to meet the theatrical requirements of the opéra féerie — a genre that demanded spectacular choral and ballet effects — producing the six spirits as a natural operatic amplification of the source's supernatural apparatus.The six numbered spirits have no textual basis whatsoever in Perrault's 'Cendrillon ou la petite pantoufle de verre' (1697) and cannot meaningfully be described as an 'elaboration' of the source. They are an entirely new invention of the librettist, belonging to the genre conventions of the opéra féerie and the Parisian theatrical tradition rather than to any folk or literary Cinderella narrative. Describing them as derived from Perrault risks obscuring the extent to which Caïn transformed and supplemented his source material.
