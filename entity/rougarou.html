Who It Is

The Louisiana bayou at midnight: a chorus of bullfrogs, the metallic hum of cicadas, and somewhere beyond the cypress knees — a howl that doesn't belong to any animal you know. Your grandmother's voice echoes in your memory: 'Don't stay out past dark, cher. The Rougarou walks the swamp, and he's always looking for company.' You quicken your pace on the shell road, but the howl is closer now.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Cajun French evolution of the standard French 'loup-garou' (loup = wolf + garou = werewolf, from Frankish *werawulf). The 'l' sound eroded in Cajun pronunciation, producing 'rougarou.'

Aliases — Loup-Garou, Roux-Ga-Roux, Rugaroo, Rugaru, Cajun Werewolf

Accounts I
The Seven-Year Lent BreakerTerrebonne Parish oral tradition

A man who broke Lent seven years in a row was cursed to become the Rougarou. For 101 days, he roamed the swamps, howling his grief. His wife, warned by the local traiteur (folk healer), kept a knife blessed with holy water beside her bed. When the Rougarou appeared at her window one night, she cut its arm — drawing blood and breaking the curse. The next morning, her husband returned, fully human but bearing a scar on his arm. The Lent lesson was carved into parish memory: the Rougarou sees all who break their sacred obligations.

The Rougarou of Bayou LafourcheLafourche Parish, recorded by Barry Jean Ancelet

In the early 20th century, residents of Bayou Lafourche reported a creature walking upright along the bayou banks at night, its red eyes visible through the cypress trees. A fisherman who stayed out too late encountered it face-to-face. It spoke in Cajun French: 'Cher, you're out late.' The fisherman, remembering the old advice, did not run — running triggers the chase instinct. He walked backward, never breaking eye contact, until he reached his pirogue. The creature watched but did not attack. For years afterward, the fisherman refused to travel the bayou after sunset.

Accounts II
The Courir de Mardi Gras RougarouMamou and Eunice, Louisiana — Mardi Gras tradition

During the traditional Cajun Courir de Mardi Gras (Mardi Gras run), masked riders on horseback travel from house to house collecting ingredients for a communal gumbo. One of the traditional costumes is the Rougarou — a figure dressed in tattered clothes, wearing a wolf-like mask or a hood with animal features. This figure symbolizes the chaos and wildness of the season before Lent's discipline. The Rougarou chases children, who shriek with equal parts terror and delight. The tradition transforms the monster from genuine threat to celebratory symbol, marking Lent's approach.

The Healer and the Rougarou's BloodAcadia Parish traiteur tradition

A traiteur (Cajun folk healer) was called to treat a man who had been found wandering the swamp, feverish and speaking in growls. The traiteur recognized the signs of the Rougarou curse. He prepared a ritual using salt blessed at Easter Mass, powdered brick from the St. Martin de Tours church, and a silver coin from the year of the afflicted man's birth. After three nights of prayer and herbal poultices, the man's fever broke. He remembered nothing of his time in the swamp. The traiteur never revealed the cure's details, saying only: 'Some things, the swamp keeps.'
