Who It Is

In the corner of a medieval Japanese storeroom, a paper umbrella waits out a century. Give it one more year and countless pots, sandals, lutes, and lanterns around it will cross the same invisible line and turn — each sprouting an eye, a tongue, a pair of feet — into furious, animate things that have survived being thrown away. The Japanese believed a household tool quietly accumulates a spirit, and that the only mercy you can grant it is to smash it before it wakes; but the ones you cast off at ninety-nine years just become angrier. What began as a folk rule about not wasting old objects became a Buddhist sermon, a night parade of a hundred demons, and finally the most beloved mascot of haunted-house lore — a haunting that, scholars now suspect, may never have been believed at all.

The central behavioral trait is REVENGE AGAINST WASTE. Objects abandoned or thrown away before their 100th year, or disrespected after long service, become tsukumogami 'by some means other than the mere passage of time' and 'band together to take revenge upon those who were wasteful or threw them away thoughtlessly.' This is tied to the mottainai ethic — the sense that waste is a spiritual insult. In modern popular understanding they are 'at most [prankster-ish].' Powers
Acquisition of a spirit after ~100 years

The core transformation: a tool that faithfully serves (or is kept) roughly a century accumulates a kami/spirit and becomes self-aware, animate tsukumogami. The word tsukumo ('ninety-nine years') signals the threshold is felt as a near-century, with 九十九 written to imply 'of great age' rather than a literal number.

Limit: The numbers 100/99 'do not need to be taken literally' — they are a folk shorthand for 'significantly old.' Not all old objects transform; and per Komatsu the idea largely died out in recent generations, so the power is historical/conceptual rather than an observed folk practice today.

Shapeshifting ('take on the appearance of …')

Once awakened, tsukumogami 'deceive people's hearts' and take forms in three named categories: human (male/female, old/young), demonic (chimi akki — oni-like), and animal (korō yakan). Most depictions show hybrid tool-bodies with a face, eye, limb, or tongue rather than a fully human body.

Limit: The form-change is depicted visually; no classical text describes a precise transformation mechanic or limits.

Trickery and non-lethal haunting (pranks)

The majority of tsukumogami are pranksters, not killers — they startle, deceive, and 'cause a ruckus.' The Kasa-obake sneaks up and licks people with a huge tongue to 'scare … but is not harmful.' Modern readings stress they are 'harmless and at most [tend] to play occasional pranks.'

Limit: This benign register is the postwar 'manga-character' framing; it coexists with a darker capacity for collective vengeance.

Collective revenge against waste (mottainai enforcement)

Objects thrown away thoughtlessly or 'a year from one hundred' become angry tsukumogami and group together to take revenge on the wasteful/discarding owner. This is functionally a folk enforcement of the mottainai (don't-waste) ethic — discard is framed as spiritual harm to the object.

Limit: Attested as belief rather than narrative; most documented stories underplay the revenge and keep it a fright.

Ritual quiescence via house-cleaning (susus-barai/consolation)

Tsukumogami are prevented from forming by the susu-harai — the annual year-end 'sweeping soot' house-cleaning that ritually discards old objects before they reach 100. Today, jinja ceremonies are performed to 'console broken and unusable items' so they stay benign.

Limit: Apropo: objects spirited away as 'waste during susu-harai' are precisely the ones said to transform, so the ritual is at once the preventive and the trigger.

Buddhist-doctrine vehicle (propagative function)

Elison & Smith (1987) document that the tsukumogami myth, by the 10th century, was used to spread the doctrines of Shingon Esoteric Buddhism 'to a variety of audiences, ranging from the educated to the relatively unsophisticated,' capitalizing on pre-existing animist belief in object-spirits.

Limit: Analytical rather than a 'power'; it explains the concept's durability by its utility to the Buddhist establishment.

The Tsukumogami Emaki lists three categories of form-change: 'the appearance of people male and female, old and young'; 'the likeness of chimi akki' (chthonic demons); and 'the shape of korō yakan' (animals). Accounts I
The Night Parade of One Hundred Demons (Hyakki Yagyō)Hyakki Yagyō Emaki, Muromachi period

Muromachi (1336–1573)

The celebrated scroll depicts a nocturnal parade of tool-bodied yōkai — umbrellas, pots, basins, lutes, and other aged objects — moving in festival procession. Modern scholarship reads these figures as the 'youbutsu' (aged objects) of the Tsukumogami Emaki, giving the tsukumogami their most visually iconic mass-appearance. Codified the image of object-spirits marching as a collective; became the template for countless later yōkai parades in art and pop culture.

The Hundred-Year Transformation Told in the Tsukumogami EmakiTsukumogami Emaki (Tenpō recension)

concept Muromachi; Kanji recension of the emakimono

The emakimono opens by quoting a (later-unverifiable) treatise called the Onmyō Zakki to state that a tool which passes one hundred years changes and acquires a spirit, deceiving people's hearts, and is then called a tsukumogami. It adds the crucial iron-gate: people began ritually discarding objects before they turned 100 (susu-harai), but objects thrown away 'a year from one hundred' grow angry and become yōkai through a cause other than mere age, then raise a ruckus. Established the two-part folk rule — prevent transformation by pre-emptively discarding; suffer transformation of the discarded-and-resentful.

The Furisode/Surface-Tool Burial and the Consolation Ritualfolk religious practice; Reider; jinja tradition

continuing to present

To prevent old objects from turning into vengeful tsukumogami, households ritually discard unusable items during year-end cleaning, and shrine ceremonies are 'performed to console broken and unusable items.' The practice frames disposal as a respectful release rather than careless waste, neutralising the object's spiritual grievance. Institutionalized a non-violent covenant between humans and objects; underpins the mottainai ethic.

Accounts II
The Animated Tea-Caddy of Matsunaga Hisahide (attested anecdote)Elison & Smith (1987)

16th century

A famous Sengoku-period folk anecdote holds that Matsunaga Hisahide used an animated tea caddy — a tsukumogami, i.e. an aged 'living' tea utensil treasured as a warrior's prize — as a bargaining chip in peace negotiations with Oda Nobunaga. The story shows the tsukumogami concept crossing from household animism into samurai politics and the aesthetics of tea. Demonstrates that in the medieval period an object-spirit could carry real diplomatic and status weight.

The Kasa-obake That LicksEdō–period karuta and yōkai sugoroku; postwar media

Edo period onward

The umbrella spirit (kasa-obake) — one eye, one leg, lolling tongue — is the most famous and durable tsukumogami. It appears from Edo-period obake karuta and Ansei-era sugoroku (as 'One-footed from Sagizaka') and, after the war, becomes the archetypal haunted-house obake. Its signature prank is to sneak up and lick victims with its enormous tongue, startling but not harming them. Even though it appears in no eyewitness folklore and no classical text confirms it as a 'tsukumogami,' it became the representative object-yōkai and face of the whole class.

The Nuribotoke of the Neglected Buddhist AltarGazu Hyakki Yagyō (Sekien); Hyakkai Zukan; Hyakki Yagyō Emaki

Edo period

The Nuribotoke ('Lacquered Buddha') emerges from a neglected Buddhist altar (butsudan) left open overnight or ill-maintained: an animated black corpse, its flesh lacquered like a Buddha image, with a bloated belly and both eyeballs dangling from their sockets, often with a long black appendage (drawn as a catfish tail or long hair) from its back. It appears first in the Hyakki Yagyō Emaki as kurobō ('black bonze'). Yoked the tsukumogami idea to Buddhist ritual negligence — an object-spirit born of an altar dishonored by disuse.
