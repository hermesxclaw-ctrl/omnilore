Who It Is

One Greek letter rules every circuit you have ever touched. The capital omega — Ω — is not a mystical rune but a precisely defined choke point: the resistance that lets exactly one volt push exactly one ampere. Behind that elegant ratio lies two centuries of feuding telegraph engineers, mercury columns in Paris, and finally a definition that floats free of any physical object and anchors itself to the constants of the universe itself.

As a unit, the ohm quantifies opposition to electric current. In linear, ohmic materials (many metals at constant temperature), resistance is approximately constant over ranges of voltage and temperature, obeying Ohm's law V = I·R. In non-linear devices (thermistors, varistors, diodes, filament lamps), resistance varies strongly with temperature, voltage, current, or light. In AC circuits, the ohm also measures impedance (Z) and reactance, where phase matters. Name & Tongue

Etymology — Eponym from German physicist Georg Simon Ohm (1789–1854), who formulated the proportionality between voltage and current now known as Ohm's law (Die galvanische Kette, 1827). The unit name was proposed by the British Association for the Advancement of Science (BAAS) Committee on Electrical Standards

Pronunciation — English: /oʊm/ (OHM), rhymes with 'home' without the h; German original Ohm /oːm/. Plurals: ohms. Prefixed forms: kilohm (also spelled kiloohm), megohm (also megaohm), milliohm, microhm. US usage commonly elides the double vowel in kiloohm/megaohm.

Aliases — Ω, ohms, Ω (omega), SI ohm, absolute ohm, international ohm (historical)

Quantification of electrical resistance

Defines resistance R = V/I. One ohm is the resistance between two points of a conductor when one volt applied produces one ampere, conductor not being seat of electromotive force.

Limit: Definition assumes DC steady state, linear, isothermal conditions. Real conductors show temperature coefficient, frequency dependence, and non-ohmic behavior.

Coherent interconversion with all SI electrical units

Allows exact algebraic interconversion: 1 Ω = 1 V/A = 1 S⁻¹ = 1 W/A² = 1 s/F = 1 H/s. Enables power calculations P = V·I = V²/R = I²R without conversion factors.

Limit: Coherence holds only when using SI units (watt, volt, ampere). Using non-SI units (e.g., horsepower, statohm) requires conversion factors.

Measurement of impedance and reactance

In AC circuits, impedance Z, resistance R, inductive reactance X_L and capacitive reactance X_C are all expressed in ohms, with complex representation Z = R + jX.

Limit: Impedance magnitude in ohms does not fully describe phase; two circuits with same |Z| in ohms can behave differently.

Power dissipation relation (Joule heating)

Links resistance to thermal power: P = I²R. Determines heating, efficiency, and rating of resistors, wires, and fuses.

Limit: Assumes all electrical work converts to heat; in reactive or radiating systems power relations are more complex (real vs apparent power).

Quantum realization via von Klitzing constant

Primary realization uses quantum Hall effect: quantized Hall resistance R_H = h/(i·e²) = R_K / i, where R_K = h/e² ≈ 25812.807459... Ω exactly defined. Provides reproducibility to ~10^-9.

Limit: Requires cryogenic temperatures (~0.3-1.5 K), strong magnetic fields (~10 T), and specialized 2D electron gas devices (GaAs/AlGaAs or graphene). Not a portable artifact.

Reciprocal conductance (siemens)

Conductance G = 1/R measured in siemens (S). 1 S = 1 Ω⁻¹. Historically called mho (℧). Enables alternative formulation I = G·V.

Limit: Siemens also used for admittance and susceptance in AC; context required.

Accounts I
Georg Ohm and the Galvanic Circuit (1825-1827)Georg Simon Ohm, Die galvanische Kette mathematisch bearbeitet (1827); contemporary reviews in Poggendorff's Annalen

1825-1827 (publication 1827)

Schoolteacher Georg Ohm, working with bismuth-copper thermocouples and galvanometers in Cologne, systematically varied wire length and measured current, finding current proportional to electromotive force and inversely proportional to total circuit resistance. He expressed this as X = a/(b+x), where b is internal and x external resistance. Initially dismissed and criticized (notably by Georg Pohl), the law was later recognized after verification by Pouillet, Lenz and Jacobi. The work laid the quantitative foundation for circuit theory, though Ohm himself never defined a unit of resistance - that came decades later. Established linear relationship V ∝ I; enabled telegraph engineering; posthumously eponymized in unit name (1860s). Ohm's experimental apparatus is preserved in Deutsches Museum.

The BAAS Coherent Unit and the Birth of the Ohm (1861-1865)BAAS Reports 1861-1865; Latimer Clark and Charles Bright proposals; William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) and James Clerk Maxwell committee work

1861-1865

With submarine telegraph cables failing due to inconsistent resistance standards (miles of copper wire, Jacobi's Etalon, Siemens mercury units), the BAAS Committee on Electrical Standards sought a coherent system linking mechanical energy to electrical units. They defined resistance in absolute electromagnetic CGS units as velocity (cm/s) and chose a practical magnitude: 10^9 CGS units = 10^7 metres per second = one quadrant of Earth per second, approximated by a standard coil of wire. Werner Siemens had proposed a mercury column (1 m long, 1 mm² cross-section at 0°C) in 1860; the BAAS rejected artifact-based definitions in favor of absolute measure. The name 'ohm' (or initially 'ohma') was adopted by 1867 to honor Ohm. Created first internationally discussed coherent electrical units (ohm, volt, farad). Produced physical standard coils (BA ohm) distributed to laboratories. Established principle that electrical units should derive from mass-length-time without arbitrary artifacts.

Mercury, Paris and the International Ohm (1881-1908)1881 International Electrical Congress (Paris) resolutions; 1884 Paris Conference; 1908 London International Conference on Electrical Units and Standards

1881-1908

The 1881 Paris Congress formally adopted the ohm, volt, ampere, coulomb and farad as practical units. To make the ohm realizable, it defined the legal ohm via a mercury column: initially 1 mm² cross-section, later precisely 106.3 cm length and 14.4521 g mass of mercury at 0°C (1908). National laboratories maintained glass tubes filled with triple-distilled mercury. This artifact definition, though reproducible to ~0.02%, was sensitive to glass expansion, mercury purity, and temperature control, and differed from the absolute (mechanical) ohm by ~0.05%. Gave telegraph and power industries a stable, legally enforceable resistance standard for 40 years. Created the distinction 'international ohm' vs 'absolute ohm' that persisted until 1948, requiring conversion factor 1 int Ω = 1.00049 abs Ω.

Accounts II
The Absolute Ohm and the Quantum Hall Revolution (1948-1990)9th CGPM Resolution 2 (1948) adopting absolute MKS-A units; BIPM Comptes Rendus; Klaus von Klitzing, Physical Review Letters 45, 494 (1980)

1948-1990

On 1 January 1948, the CGPM abrogated the international units and redefined the ohm absolutely via the mechanical units: 1 Ω = 1 kg·m²·s⁻³·A⁻², realized via calculable inductors, capacitors and the Thompson-Lampard theorem. In 1980, Klaus von Klitzing discovered that the Hall resistance of a two-dimensional electron gas at low temperature and high magnetic field is quantized in steps of h/e², independent of material. By 1988, the CIPM recommended conventional values for the Josephson constant K_J-90 and von Klitzing constant R_K-90 for worldwide calibration, creating the practical system of 1990 where laboratories kept Ω_90, slightly offset from SI. Ohm became reproducible to parts in 10^9 without mercury. The 1990 conventional system unified worldwide electrical calibrations but introduced a known offset from true SI that lasted 29 years.

The 2019 SI Redefinition: Ohm from Constants of Nature (2019-present)26th CGPM Resolution 1 (2018); BIPM SI Brochure 9th ed. 2019; Mise en pratique for the ampere and ohm

2018-2019 (effective 2019-05-20, World Metrology Day)

The CGPM fixed the numerical values of Planck constant h = 6.62607015×10⁻³⁴ J·s and elementary charge e = 1.602176634×10⁻¹⁹ C exactly. Since the ampere is now defined by e and the kilogram by h, the ohm (Ω = kg·m²·s⁻³·A⁻²) is now exact in terms of h and e via R_K = h/e². The conventional 1990 values were abrogated; the quantum Hall and Josephson effects now realize the SI ohm and volt directly with no offset. The ohm is thus no longer tied to any artifact, mercury column, or even a specific experiment - it is a fixed fraction of the quantum of resistance. All electrical units became exact multiples of fundamental constants; the ohm's uncertainty moved from definition to realization. Practical calibrations shifted by ~+1.8×10⁻⁸ for resistance and ~-1.1×10⁻⁷ for voltage on 2019-05-20, requiring industry adjustment.

Ohm's Law in the Classroom and the Workbench - The Unit in UseTextbooks from Ganot (1850s) to modern curricula; IEC 60050; resistor manufacturing standards

1870-present

Once defined, the ohm became the everyday language of circuit design. Resistor color codes (1920s-), preferred values (E-series), and multimeter dials all read in Ω, kΩ, MΩ. Students worldwide learn V=IR as first circuit law. The unit scales over 30 orders of magnitude: from ~10⁻⁸ Ω for copper busbars to 10¹⁶ Ω for PTFE insulation. Non-ohmic devices are still characterized by differential resistance dV/dI in ohms, preserving the unit even where Ohm's law fails. Made quantitative electronics possible; enabled interchangeable parts and global supply chains; embedded Ω in Unicode, ASCII fallback 'ohm', and engineering slang ('a 4k7 resistor').

Georg Ohm invented or defined the ohm unit. — Ohm discovered the law V ∝ I (1827) but died in 1854 before any unit bore his name. The unit was proposed by Latimer Clark and the BAAS Committee in 1861 and named 'ohm'/'ohma' around 1867, posthumously honoring him.

Ω and ℧ are the same unit or interchangeable symbols. — Ω is ohm (resistance). ℧ (inverted Ω, U+2127) was mho, the old name for siemens (conductance = 1/Ω). Mho is deprecated since 1971; correct SI is S.

An ohm is a fundamental physical constant like the speed of light. — Ohm is a human-chosen magnitude: historically 10^9 CGS units, now defined as exact fraction of h/e². The von Klitzing constant R_K ≈25812.8 Ω is the physical constant; the ohm itself is the unit we use to measure it.

Resistance in ohms and impedance in ohms are different units. — Both are measured in ohms; impedance is complex resistance (R + jX) that includes phase. Saying 'impedance is 50 ohms' is correct but incomplete without frequency/phase context.

The ohm symbol is different from the Greek letter omega and must be typed separately. — Unicode has two code points (U+03A9 Ω and U+2126 Ω OHM SIGN) but they are canonically equivalent and render identically. BIPM and Unicode advise using U+03A9 (Greek omega) for the unit; U+2126 exists only for round-trip compatibility with legacy East Asian encodings.

Higher ohms always means more heat/power. — Depends on what is held constant: at fixed voltage, P = V²/R so higher R means less power; at fixed current, P = I²R so higher R means more power. Both follow from same law.
