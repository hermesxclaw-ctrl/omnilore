The Approach

One Greek letter rules every circuit you have ever touched. The capital omega — Ω — is not a mystical rune but a precisely defined choke point: the resistance that lets exactly one volt push exactly one ampere. Behind that elegant ratio lies two centuries of feuding telegraph engineers, mercury columns in Paris, and finally a definition that floats free of any physical object and anchors itself to the constants of the universe itself.

Content advisory. No graphic content. Technical historical material includes discussion of 19th-century laboratory working conditions and colonial telegraph expansion context.
Identity

ohm

Pronunciation English: /oʊm/ (OHM), rhymes with 'home' without the h; German original Ohm /oːm/. Plurals: ohms. Prefixed forms: kilohm (also spelled kiloohm), megohm (also megaohm), milliohm, microhm. US usage commonly elides the double vowel in kiloohm/megaohm.

Known As

ΩohmsΩ (omega)SI ohmabsolute ohminternational ohm (historical)

Etymology

Eponym from German physicist Georg Simon Ohm (1789–1854), who formulated the proportionality between voltage and current now known as Ohm's law (Die galvanische Kette, 1827). The unit name was proposed by the British Association for the Advancement of Science (BAAS) Committee on Electrical Standards in the 1860s and adopted internationally at the 1881 International Electrical Congress in Paris. The symbol Ω is the uppercase Greek letter omega, chosen as an initial for Ohm. The term was made an SI derived unit with special name in 1948 (9th CGPM) and retained through the 2019 SI revision.

Cultural Ownership

International scientific commons. Maintained by the International Bureau of Weights and Measures (BIPM) under authority of the General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM) and realized nationally by metrology institutes (NIST (USA), PTB (Germany), NPL (UK), LNE, NMIJ, etc.). No single cultural or Indigenous ownership; eponym is German but unit is international public domain. Symbol and definition are defined in ISO 80000 and IEC 60027.

Disputed Identifications

1) Not to be confused with the Greek letter omega as used in other sciences (angular frequency ω, cosmology density parameter Ω). 2) Not to be confused with the DC Comics character Ohm or the Warframe weapon Ohm - homonyms. 3) Historical distinction required between SI (absolute) ohm, international ohm (1908 London definition based on mercury column), BAAS ohm / B.A. unit (~0.9866 Ω), and legal ohm variants - numerically different units sharing the name. 4) Siemens (S) / mho (℧) is conductance, the reciprocal ohm, not a variant spelling. 5) Impedance (Z) is measured in ohms but is not resistance alone.

Description

Appearance

Symbol: Ω, uppercase Greek omega. Unicode U+03A9 GREEK CAPITAL LETTER OMEGA (canonical) and U+2126 OHM SIGN (compatibility equivalent, canonically decomposes to Ω). In print, upright roman Ω, not italic. Prefixed forms use SI prefixes: mΩ, kΩ (or kΩ with kilo-), MΩ, GΩ, etc. On schematics, resistance value often written adjacent to zigzag or rectangle symbol; the Ω may be replaced by the letter R as decimal separator (e.g., 4R7 = 4.7 Ω) to avoid losing decimal points on diagrams. No physical appearance beyond representation; the unit itself is abstract.

Symbols & Iconography

Omega has become visual shorthand for electrical resistance and, by extension, electronics culture. The ℧ (U+2127 INVERTED OHM SIGN) historically represented mho, now siemens. The ohm symbol appears on multimeters, resistor color-code charts, and component markings. In popular design, Ω is used as an icon for 'resistance' in protest art and tattoos, conflating electrical and political resistance. Metrologically, the ohm is part of the coherent SI system: Ω = V/A = 1/S = W/A² = V²/W = s/F = H/s = Wb/C = J·s/C² = kg·m²/(s³·A²).

Behaviour & Personality

As a unit, the ohm quantifies opposition to electric current. In linear, ohmic materials (many metals at constant temperature), resistance is approximately constant over ranges of voltage and temperature, obeying Ohm's law V = I·R. In non-linear devices (thermistors, varistors, diodes, filament lamps), resistance varies strongly with temperature, voltage, current, or light. In AC circuits, the ohm also measures impedance (Z) and reactance, where phase matters. Behaviorally, higher resistance at fixed voltage means lower current and lower power dissipation (P = V²/R); at fixed current, higher resistance means higher power dissipation (P = I²R).

Domains & Habitats

Domain: electromagnetism, electrical engineering, physics, metrology. Habitat: every electrical circuit, from nanoscale transistors (milliohms of interconnect) to insulation (teraohms), to quantum Hall devices used as primary standards. Realization habitat: national metrology laboratories at ~1 K and high magnetic fields for quantum Hall, and calculable cross-capacitors for absolute farad/ohm traceability. Everyday habitat: resistors, sensors, heaters, multimeters, datasheets, and educational laboratories worldwide.

Version Differences

Attested historical variants with different magnitudes: BAAS/B.A. unit (1865) = 0.9866 modern Ω; Siemens mercury unit (1860) ~0.953 Ω; British Association ohm (1865) ~0.988 Ω; legal ohm (1884) = 1.0112 B.A. units; international ohm (1908-1946) = 1.00049 absolute ohms; absolute ohm (1948) defined via mechanical units. Since 1948, the SI ohm has been coherent with metre-kilogram-second-ampere; since 1990, practical calibration used conventional values V_90 and Ω_90 based on Josephson and quantum Hall effects (Ω_90 = 1.000000088... Ω_SI). Since 2019-05-20, the SI ohm is exact via fixed h and e, and the conventional 1990 values were abrogated - the realization uncertainty is now in the experiment, not the definition.

Powers & Abilities

Quantification of electrical resistance

Source VersionSI Brochure 9th ed. 2019; IEC 60050

Defines resistance R = V/I. One ohm is the resistance between two points of a conductor when one volt applied produces one ampere, conductor not being seat of electromotive force.

Limitations

Definition assumes DC steady state, linear, isothermal conditions. Real conductors show temperature coefficient, frequency dependence, and non-ohmic behavior.

Contradictions

Historical mercury-column definitions gave slightly different magnitude; 2019 definition is exact but realization has experimental uncertainty ~10^-9.

Coherent interconversion with all SI electrical units

Source VersionSI coherent system since 1948 MKS-A

Allows exact algebraic interconversion: 1 Ω = 1 V/A = 1 S⁻¹ = 1 W/A² = 1 s/F = 1 H/s. Enables power calculations P = V·I = V²/R = I²R without conversion factors.

Limitations

Coherence holds only when using SI units (watt, volt, ampere). Using non-SI units (e.g., horsepower, statohm) requires conversion factors.

Contradictions

In CGS-ESU and CGS-EMU systems, resistance dimensions differ (s/cm vs cm/s) - not coherent with SI.

Measurement of impedance and reactance

Source VersionSteinmetz / Kennelly AC theory, IEC 60027

In AC circuits, impedance Z, resistance R, inductive reactance X_L and capacitive reactance X_C are all expressed in ohms, with complex representation Z = R + jX.

Limitations

Impedance magnitude in ohms does not fully describe phase; two circuits with same |Z| in ohms can behave differently.

Contradictions

Popular misconception that 'ohms = resistance only' - technically impedance also in ohms but includes phase.

Power dissipation relation (Joule heating)

Source VersionJoule 1841, combined with Ohm's law

Links resistance to thermal power: P = I²R. Determines heating, efficiency, and rating of resistors, wires, and fuses.

Limitations

Assumes all electrical work converts to heat; in reactive or radiating systems power relations are more complex (real vs apparent power).

Contradictions

None; but non-linear resistors violate constant-R assumption, so instantaneous R must be used.

Quantum realization via von Klitzing constant

Source Versionvon Klitzing 1980 discovery; CCEM mise en pratique 2019

Primary realization uses quantum Hall effect: quantized Hall resistance R_H = h/(i·e²) = R_K / i, where R_K = h/e² ≈ 25812.807459... Ω exactly defined. Provides reproducibility to ~10^-9.

Limitations

Requires cryogenic temperatures (~0.3-1.5 K), strong magnetic fields (~10 T), and specialized 2D electron gas devices (GaAs/AlGaAs or graphene). Not a portable artifact.

Contradictions

Pre-2019 conventional value R_K-90 = 25812.807 Ω exactly was offset from SI by ~1.8×10^-8; now exact SI value differs.

Reciprocal conductance (siemens)

Source VersionSI, 14th CGPM 1971 adoption of siemens

Conductance G = 1/R measured in siemens (S). 1 S = 1 Ω⁻¹. Historically called mho (℧). Enables alternative formulation I = G·V.

Limitations

Siemens also used for admittance and susceptance in AC; context required.

Contradictions

None, but older literature uses mho which is deprecated.

Stories & Myths

Georg Ohm and the Galvanic Circuit (1825-1827)

Source Georg Simon Ohm, Die galvanische Kette mathematisch bearbeitet (1827); contemporary reviews in Poggendorff's AnnalenCulture German physics, early 19th-century Naturphilosophie vs experimental physicsPeriod 1825-1827 (publication 1827)

Summary

Schoolteacher Georg Ohm, working with bismuth-copper thermocouples and galvanometers in Cologne, systematically varied wire length and measured current, finding current proportional to electromotive force and inversely proportional to total circuit resistance. He expressed this as X = a/(b+x), where b is internal and x external resistance. Initially dismissed and criticized (notably by Georg Pohl), the law was later recognized after verification by Pouillet, Lenz and Jacobi. The work laid the quantitative foundation for circuit theory, though Ohm himself never defined a unit of resistance - that came decades later.

Participants

Georg Simon Ohm

Johann Schweigger (galvanometer)

Claude Pouillet (independent verification)

Consequences

Established linear relationship V ∝ I; enabled telegraph engineering; posthumously eponymized in unit name (1860s). Ohm's experimental apparatus is preserved in Deutsches Museum.

Variants

Ohm's original formulation used logarithmic and geometric language; modern V=IR form was popularized later by Maxwell and others. Some histories overstate immediate acceptance - actually met with several years of neglect.

The BAAS Coherent Unit and the Birth of the Ohm (1861-1865)

Source BAAS Reports 1861-1865; Latimer Clark and Charles Bright proposals; William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) and James Clerk Maxwell committee workCulture British Victorian electrotechnology, telegraph industryPeriod 1861-1865

Summary

With submarine telegraph cables failing due to inconsistent resistance standards (miles of copper wire, Jacobi's Etalon, Siemens mercury units), the BAAS Committee on Electrical Standards sought a coherent system linking mechanical energy to electrical units. They defined resistance in absolute electromagnetic CGS units as velocity (cm/s) and chose a practical magnitude: 10^9 CGS units = 10^7 metres per second = one quadrant of Earth per second, approximated by a standard coil of wire. Werner Siemens had proposed a mercury column (1 m long, 1 mm² cross-section at 0°C) in 1860; the BAAS rejected artifact-based definitions in favor of absolute measure. The name 'ohm' (or initially 'ohma') was adopted by 1867 to honor Ohm.

Participants

William Thomson (Lord Kelvin)

James Clerk Maxwell

Josiah Latimer Clark

Charles Bright

Werner von Siemens (rival proposal)

Fleeming Jenkin

Consequences

Created first internationally discussed coherent electrical units (ohm, volt, farad). Produced physical standard coils (BA ohm) distributed to laboratories. Established principle that electrical units should derive from mass-length-time without arbitrary artifacts.

Variants

The BA unit was later found to be ~1.3% low due to error in determination of the ohm's absolute value; corrected coils issued in 1865. Siemens unit continued in continental Europe until 1880s, causing dual-system confusion.

Mercury, Paris and the International Ohm (1881-1908)

Source 1881 International Electrical Congress (Paris) resolutions; 1884 Paris Conference; 1908 London International Conference on Electrical Units and StandardsCulture International metrology, late 19th-century imperial sciencePeriod 1881-1908

Summary

The 1881 Paris Congress formally adopted the ohm, volt, ampere, coulomb and farad as practical units. To make the ohm realizable, it defined the legal ohm via a mercury column: initially 1 mm² cross-section, later precisely 106.3 cm length and 14.4521 g mass of mercury at 0°C (1908). National laboratories maintained glass tubes filled with triple-distilled mercury. This artifact definition, though reproducible to ~0.02%, was sensitive to glass expansion, mercury purity, and temperature control, and differed from the absolute (mechanical) ohm by ~0.05%.

Participants

International Electrical Congress delegates

BIPM

National Physical Laboratory (UK)

Physikalisch-Technische Reichsanstalt (Germany)

Consequences

Gave telegraph and power industries a stable, legally enforceable resistance standard for 40 years. Created the distinction 'international ohm' vs 'absolute ohm' that persisted until 1948, requiring conversion factor 1 int Ω = 1.00049 abs Ω.

Variants

French, German and British mercury apparatus gave slightly different values; the 106.3 cm length was a compromise. The US adopted the international ohm via NIST mercury standards.

The Absolute Ohm and the Quantum Hall Revolution (1948-1990)

Source 9th CGPM Resolution 2 (1948) adopting absolute MKS-A units; BIPM Comptes Rendus; Klaus von Klitzing, Physical Review Letters 45, 494 (1980)Culture Post-WWII SI unification, condensed matter physicsPeriod 1948-1990

Summary

On 1 January 1948, the CGPM abrogated the international units and redefined the ohm absolutely via the mechanical units: 1 Ω = 1 kg·m²·s⁻³·A⁻², realized via calculable inductors, capacitors and the Thompson-Lampard theorem. In 1980, Klaus von Klitzing discovered that the Hall resistance of a two-dimensional electron gas at low temperature and high magnetic field is quantized in steps of h/e², independent of material. By 1988, the CIPM recommended conventional values for the Josephson constant K_J-90 and von Klitzing constant R_K-90 for worldwide calibration, creating the practical system of 1990 where laboratories kept Ω_90, slightly offset from SI.

Participants

BIPM Consultative Committee for Electricity

Klaus von Klitzing

Thompson and Lampard (calculable capacitor)

CIPM

Consequences

Ohm became reproducible to parts in 10^9 without mercury. The 1990 conventional system unified worldwide electrical calibrations but introduced a known offset from true SI that lasted 29 years.

Variants

Some narratives credit only von Klitzing; in fact calculable capacitor work by Clothier, Thompson-Lampard and Cutkosky was equally crucial for linking farad to mechanical length.

The 2019 SI Redefinition: Ohm from Constants of Nature (2019-present)

Source 26th CGPM Resolution 1 (2018); BIPM SI Brochure 9th ed. 2019; Mise en pratique for the ampere and ohmCulture Modern quantum metrology, SI revisionPeriod 2018-2019 (effective 2019-05-20, World Metrology Day)

Summary

The CGPM fixed the numerical values of Planck constant h = 6.62607015×10⁻³⁴ J·s and elementary charge e = 1.602176634×10⁻¹⁹ C exactly. Since the ampere is now defined by e and the kilogram by h, the ohm (Ω = kg·m²·s⁻³·A⁻²) is now exact in terms of h and e via R_K = h/e². The conventional 1990 values were abrogated; the quantum Hall and Josephson effects now realize the SI ohm and volt directly with no offset. The ohm is thus no longer tied to any artifact, mercury column, or even a specific experiment - it is a fixed fraction of the quantum of resistance.

Participants

BIPM

26th CGPM delegates

National metrology institutes (NIST, PTB, NMIJ, etc.)

CODATA

Consequences

All electrical units became exact multiples of fundamental constants; the ohm's uncertainty moved from definition to realization. Practical calibrations shifted by ~+1.8×10⁻⁸ for resistance and ~-1.1×10⁻⁷ for voltage on 2019-05-20, requiring industry adjustment.

Variants

Popular accounts sometimes claim the kilogram redefinition alone changed the ohm; in fact both h and e fixes were required, plus the existing definitions of metre and second.

Ohm's Law in the Classroom and the Workbench - The Unit in Use

Source Textbooks from Ganot (1850s) to modern curricula; IEC 60050; resistor manufacturing standardsCulture Global electrical education and electronics industryPeriod 1870-present

Summary

Once defined, the ohm became the everyday language of circuit design. Resistor color codes (1920s-), preferred values (E-series), and multimeter dials all read in Ω, kΩ, MΩ. Students worldwide learn V=IR as first circuit law. The unit scales over 30 orders of magnitude: from ~10⁻⁸ Ω for copper busbars to 10¹⁶ Ω for PTFE insulation. Non-ohmic devices are still characterized by differential resistance dV/dI in ohms, preserving the unit even where Ohm's law fails.

Participants

Electrical engineering educators

IEC Technical Committee 1

Resistor manufacturers (Vishay, Yageo, etc.)

Consequences

Made quantitative electronics possible; enabled interchangeable parts and global supply chains; embedded Ω in Unicode, ASCII fallback 'ohm', and engineering slang ('a 4k7 resistor').

Variants

US textbooks often reduce 'kiloohm' to 'kilohm' and 'megaohm' to 'megohm' - accepted variant spellings per IEEE/ANSI.

Relationships

German physicist (1789-1854) whose 1827 law V = I·R provided the physical relationship that the unit quantifies. Unit named in his honor 1860s, not by his own proposal.

ohm→Volt (V)ally

SI derived unit of electric potential. Ohm is defined as volt per ampere (Ω = V/A). Together with ampere, forms the electrical triad.

SI base unit of electric current. Ohm = V/A = kg·m²·s⁻³·A⁻². Since 2019, ampere defined by fixing elementary charge e, which in turn fixes ohm via h/e².

SI unit of conductance, admittance and susceptance. 1 S = 1 Ω⁻¹ = 1 A/V. Historical name mho (ohm backwards) with symbol ℧, now deprecated in favor of siemens (1971).

Physical phenomenon providing primary realization of ohm since 1990. Quantized Hall resistance R_H = R_K / i where R_K = h/e² ≈ 25812.807459... Ω. Realizes ohm without artifact.

Colonial telegraph context

Source Hunt, Bruce J. The Maxwellians (1991); BAAS Reports context

Description

Demand for a universal ohm was driven in large part by British imperial submarine cable projects (e.g., transatlantic, Indian Ocean cables) that served colonial governance and commerce. Standardization benefited those projects.

Scholarly Context

Historians note metrology as tool of empire, but the ohm's technical definition itself is not ideologically charged; no sanitization needed, but context should not be erased.

Contradictions & Disputes

Magnitude of the ohm: artifact vs absolute

Position A

Ohm should be defined by a physical artifact (mercury column of specified dimensions) for practical reproducibility.

Position A — evidence

Siemens 1860 proposal and 1908 international ohm (106.3 cm, 14.4521 g Hg) gave reproducible standard to ~0.02% with available technology; favored by telegraph engineers.

Position B

Ohm should be defined absolutely via mechanical units (mass, length, time) to ensure coherence with energy and avoid artifact drift.

Position B — evidence

Maxwell, Thomson and BAAS dimensional analysis showed resistance = velocity, so 10^9 CGS units = 10^7 m/s; absolute system eliminates conversion factors. Mercury standards later shown to be 0.05% high vs absolute.

Scholarly Consensus

Absolute (coherent) view prevailed: international artifact abrogated 1948; SI now defines ohm via constants, realized via quantum effects. Historical artifact values are now conversion factors, not definitions.

Spelling of prefixed forms: kiloohm vs kilohm

Position A

Prescriptive SI: kiloohm, megaohm (retain both vowels per SI prefix + unit).

Position A — evidence

BIPM SI Brochure and ISO 80000 retain kiloohm/megaohm; OED lists kilo-ohm as standard.

Position B

Common US usage: kilohm, megohm (elide duplicate vowel for euphony).

Position B — evidence

IEEE Std 260.1, ANSI, and US dictionaries list kilohm/megohm as accepted variants; industry datasheets overwhelmingly use kilohm/megohm.

Scholarly Consensus

Both are accepted; BIPM tolerates elided forms in practice, but formal SI documents use kiloohm/megaohm.

Is the ohm a base or derived unit?

Position A

Ohm is a derived unit (V/A) with special name.

Position A — evidence

SI Brochure Table 4 classifies ohm as derived unit with special name and symbol, expressed as kg·m²·s⁻³·A⁻².

Position B

In practice ohm behaves as base for electrical calibration (quantum Hall provides ohm directly).

Position B — evidence

Metrology mise en pratique realizes ohm directly via R_K = h/e² without first realizing volt and ampere separately; some argue this makes it effectively base-like.

Scholarly Consensus

Formally derived per SI; realization method does not change classification. SI maintains 7 base units, ohm remains derived.
