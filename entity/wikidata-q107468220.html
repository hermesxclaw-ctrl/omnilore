The Approach

In the scorched ruins of Las Vegas, a man who has outlived the apocalypse by a century and a half sits in a sealed chamber, his withered body suspended in a life-support machine, his mind ruling a city of neon and robots. He is Robert Edwin House, the last true capitalist of the old world, and he believes he alone can save humanity—if only he can bend the Mojave to his will.

Content advisory. Contains themes of authoritarianism, manipulation, and morally ambiguous decision-making. Fictional depictions of violence and slavery are present in the game's world.
Identity

Robert Edwin House

Known As

Mr. House

Etymology

The surname 'House' may evoke a business dynasty or a ruling house, fitting his role as a corporate magnate. The title 'Mr.' is a formal address that underscores his status as a businessman.

Cultural Ownership

Created by Obsidian Entertainment for Bethesda Softworks' Fallout franchise. He is a fictional character in the post-apocalyptic role-playing game Fallout: New Vegas.

Disputed Identifications

None known. His identity as a pre-war businessman and current ruler of New Vegas is firmly established in the game.

Description

Appearance

In his chamber, Mr. House appears as a frail, elderly man with pale, thin skin, connected to an array of life-support tubes and monitors. He wears a simple robe, and his physical form is largely immobile. He interacts with the world through a network of screens and robotic proxies.

Symbols & Iconography

The Lucky 38 casino is his symbol of power, a towering monument in the New Vegas skyline. His Securitron robots bear his likeness on their screens, displaying his face and conveying his words.

Behaviour & Personality

Mr. House is highly intelligent, calculating, and pragmatic. He speaks in a measured, precise manner, often displaying a dry wit. He is deeply confident in his own abilities and dismissive of those he considers inferior. He values order and long-term planning over short-term sentiment.

Domains & Habitats

His domain is the city of New Vegas, particularly the Lucky 38 casino, where he resides in a secure suite. He has influence over the Mojave Wasteland through his Securitron network and his control of the Hoover Dam's power output.

Version Differences

No significant version differences exist within the game itself, but his portrayal in fan discussions and analysis varies, with some viewing him as a visionary and others as a tyrant.

Stories & Myths

The Rise of Mr. House

Source Fallout: New Vegas, in-game terminals and dialogueCulture Pre-war and post-war AmericanPeriod Pre-Great War to 2281

Summary

Robert House was a wealthy businessman and visionary who foresaw the nuclear apocalypse. He developed the Platinum Chip and the Securitron army to secure his future. After the bombs fell, he placed himself in cryogenic stasis, and upon awakening, he built New Vegas from the ruins of Las Vegas, establishing himself as its ruler.

Consequences

He became the de facto dictator of New Vegas, maintaining its independence and prosperity through his control of the Hoover Dam.

The Courier's Encounter

Source Fallout: New Vegas, main questlineCulture Post-apocalypticPeriod 2281

Summary

The player character, the Courier, is hired to deliver the Platinum Chip to Mr. House. After being ambushed by Benny, the Courier eventually meets Mr. House, who tasks them with retrieving the Chip and defeating his rivals. The player can choose to ally with him or betray him.

Authoritarian rule

Source Fallout: New Vegas

Description

Mr. House rules New Vegas as a technocratic dictator, maintaining order through an army of robots and strict control over the city's resources. He is willing to sacrifice individual freedoms for his vision of stability.

Scholarly Context

This reflects themes of technocracy and utilitarian governance in post-apocalyptic fiction.

Potential manipulation

Source Fallout: New Vegas

Description

He is revealed to have manipulated events and people to achieve his goals, including the creation of the Platinum Chip and his dealings with pre-war corporations.

Scholarly Context

This is a common trope in dystopian narratives, where the 'benevolent' ruler is often morally ambiguous.
