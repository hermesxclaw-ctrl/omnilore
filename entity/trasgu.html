Etymology

Derived from the Latin 'trans' (beyond) or 'transitus' (passage), possibly referring to a being that crosses thresholds or moves between worlds. Also linked to the Spanish 'trasgo' meaning goblin.

Cultural Ownership

Northern Spain (Asturias, Cantabria, Galicia) and northern Portugal (Trás-os-Montes). Celtic origin claimed.

Disputed Identifications

Sometimes conflated with orcs, goblins, or trolls in translations; distinct from the cuélebre (dragon) and other spirits.

Archive Dossier — Approach

In the quiet hours of an Asturian farmhouse, a small figure with a red cap and a hole in his left hand might be tidying the kitchen—or, if crossed, hurling pots and spooking cattle. This is the trasgu, a domestic goblin of northern Spain, a creature of mischief and hidden help, whose pranks have echoed through centuries of Iberian folklore.

The Trasgu entered through the keyhole, his red cap bobbing in the darkness. He swept the floor, mended the broken chair, and then — for no reason at all — poured salt into the sugar bowl and laughed.

Who It Is

The Trasgu (also Trasgo, Trasno) is a mischievous goblin-like creature from the folklore of northern Spain, particularly Asturias, Cantabria, Galicia, and León. He is described as a small, dark-skinned man with a hunched back, long arms, a crooked nose, and wearing a red cap (the same cap worn by Asturian farm laborers) and a leather apron. He often has a hole in his left hand through which he constantly loses things — a telltale sign of his identity. The Trasgu lives in houses, barns, and stables, typically in the attic, the stable loft, or behind the hearth. Like the German Heinzelmännchen or the Scottish brownie, the Trasgu can be helpful: he performs household chores at night, repairs broken tools, and tends to livestock. However, his help comes with a constant stream of irritating pranks — he moves objects around, hides keys, switches salt with sugar, trips sleeping family members, and makes loud noises at night. Getting rid of a Trasgu is notoriously difficult. The most common method is to trick him into performing a seemingly impossible task, such as carrying water in a sieve or counting the grains of rice in a sack. If he fails, he leaves in humiliation. Alternatively, offering him a tiny suit of clothing (as in the English brownie tradition) offends him and causes him to depart. The Trasgu is considered inoffensive but relentless, and Asturian families traditionally accept his presence as part of rural life. The name derives from the Latin 'transitus,' meaning 'passage' or 'crossing.'

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

Typically a tiny man with a limp in his right leg, dark skin, red clothing, and a pointed red hat. He has a hole in his left hand. Sometimes described with horns, tail, sheep ears, long legs, and a black/gray cloak. Alternatively, small with long thin legs and a tight dark brown dress.

Symbols & Iconography

Red cap, hole in left hand, limp, domestic settings, nocturnal activity.

Behaviour & Personality

Mischievous, nervous, helpful if treated well, destructive if offended. Moves objects, breaks pots, spooks cattle, spills water. Does house chores at night when pleased.

Domains & Habitats

Homes, especially kitchens and stables; in Cantabria, forests.

Version Differences

Asturian: domestic goblin; Cantabrian: forest goblin with green eyes and leaf clothing; Galician/Portuguese: similar domestic trickster; Brazilian: associated with Saci-like myths.
