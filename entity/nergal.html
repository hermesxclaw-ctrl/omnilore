Who It Is

Nergal is the cosmic fire extinguisher: the god you call when you want your enemies burned, your plagues spread, and your dead judged. He is not a god of gentle transitions, but of violent endings and the harsh, unyielding laws of the underworld. He is the Mesopotamian god of war, plague, and the Land of No Return, a terrifying figure whose power was both a curse and a necessary shield for the ancient world. He is the red star in the sky that promised blood, and the unseen hand that could strike you down with a fever. To understand Nergal is to understand the reality of the ancient Near East, where life was fragile, death was ever-present, and the gods were forces of nature to be appeased, not loved.

Nergal is a deity of terrifying power and unpredictable wrath. He is a bringer of war, plague, and pestilence, a destroyer of cities and armies. He is not a god of wisdom or creation, but of destruction and the harsh realities of death and the underworld. He is fierce, impulsive, and easily angered. However, he is also a god who can be appeased, and his destructive power was invoked to protect kings and armies. Powers
Bringer of Plague and Pestilence

Nergal's most feared power. He could unleash devastating epidemics upon humans and animals. The 'hand of Nergal' was a common idiom for a fatal disease. He was believed to be able to strike individuals or entire populations with illness.

Limit: His power could be appeased through ritual, prayer, and offerings. His wrath was often seen as a punishment for sin or neglect, implying he could be swayed.

God of War

Nergal was a powerful war god who marched before armies, broke enemy lines, and brought victory to his followers. He was invoked in royal inscriptions for military success and was seen as the divine executioner of the king's enemies.

Limit: His support was not guaranteed; he could also abandon a king and cause his defeat.

Lord of the Underworld

Nergal ruled the underworld, Kur, alongside Ereshkigal. He presided over the dead, judged souls, and could decide their fate. He was the master of the 'Land of No Return'.

Limit: In some traditions, he was subordinate to Ereshkigal as the primary ruler of the underworld, while in others he was her equal or even dominant. The boundary of his power was the underworld itself.

Bringer of Famine and Destruction

As Erra, he could cause famine by destroying crops and livestock. He could also bring about the destruction of cities and kingdoms through war, plague, and natural disasters.

Limit: His destructive power is often framed as a punishment for a city's sins, and he can be persuaded to relent, as in the Epic of Erra where he is eventually convinced to stop his rampage.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Nergal was not typically depicted in anthropomorphic form in iconography that survives. His primary symbol was the lion-headed mace or a double-headed mace, a weapon of war. He was also associated with the lion itself, and could be depicted as a lion or a lion-headed figure. In some depictions, he is shown as a warrior god with a bow and arrow, or holding a sickle-sword. Symbols — Lion-headed mace, double-headed mace, the lion, the bow and arrow, the sickle-sword, the planet Mars, the number 14 (sometimes associated with him), the dog (as an underworld creature). His temple was the E-Meslam ('House of the Warrior of the Underworld').

Domains — Underworld, War, Plague, Pestilence, Famine, Death, Destruction, Fire, the Scorching Sun (as Erra), the Planet Mars, Kutha (his city). His domains are the negative and destructive forces of the cosmos, but also the necessary forces that maintain cosmic order by eliminating the weak and the rebellious.

Accounts I
The Descent of Nergal to the UnderworldThe Myth of Nergal and Ereshkigal (Amarna and Assur versions)

c. 14th-7th century BCE

Nergal, a powerful god, is disrespectful to the messenger of Ereshkigal, the queen of the underworld. As punishment, he is ordered to descend to the underworld to apologize. In the Amarna version, he is warned not to eat, drink, or sit in the underworld, but he is seduced by Ereshkigal's beauty (or in some versions, he is tricked). He ends up sleeping with her for six days. On the seventh day, he tries to leave but is stopped by the gatekeeper. In the Assur version, he is persuaded to descend by Ereshkigal's messenger, and after a series of trials, he again sleeps with her. In both, he ultimately becomes her husband and the co-ruler of the underworld, thus explaining his position as Lord of the Dead. Nergal becomes the husband of Ereshkigal and the co-ruler of the underworld. This myth explains his dual role as a god of war and plague (a celestial force) and a god of the underworld (a chthonic force).

Accounts II
The Epic of ErraThe Epic of Erra (Tablets I-V)

c. 8th century BCE

Erra (Nergal) is restless and bored. He is persuaded by his advisor, Ishum, to unleash his destructive fury on the world, specifically on Babylon. He stirs up chaos, causing the gods to abandon their cities and the people to turn against each other. He plans to destroy Babylon and its people. However, Ishum eventually persuades him to stop, and the epic ends with Erra returning to his temple in Kutha, promising to be a source of order and protection for the land. The epic is a complex meditation on divine wrath, political upheaval, and the fragility of civilization. The epic explains a period of great turmoil and disaster, likely a time of political instability and plague. It establishes Erra as a powerful and terrifying force who can be appeased, and it emphasizes the importance of proper ritual and devotion to maintain cosmic order.

The Slaying of the DemonsVarious incantation texts and royal inscriptions

c. 2nd-1st millennium BCE

Nergal is often invoked as a protector who slays demons, evil spirits, and malevolent forces that threaten humanity. He is the divine warrior who uses his mace to crush the enemies of the gods and the king. Nergal is established as a powerful apotropaic force, capable of warding off evil and protecting the faithful.

Nergal is the Mesopotamian equivalent of the Devil or Satan. — Nergal is not a figure of pure evil or a cosmic antagonist. He is a powerful and dangerous god, but he is a part of the divine order. He brings destruction, but this destruction can be a form of divine justice or a necessary purge. He is a god to be feared and appeased, not a demonic being.

Nergal is the Mesopotamian Hades. — While Nergal is a god of the underworld, he is not a simple parallel to the Greek Hades. Hades is a passive, impartial ruler of the dead. Nergal is an active, violent god of war and plague who also happens to rule the underworld. His personality and domains are much more aggressive and destructive.

Nergal is a demon. — In Mesopotamian religion, Nergal is a god, not a demon. Demons were lesser, malevolent spirits, often chaotic and not worshipped. Nergal was a major deity with temples, priests, and a cult. His destructive powers were a part of his divine nature, not a sign of demonic status.

Nergal's power is solely destructive. — While Nergal is a god of destruction, he is also a protector. He is invoked to ward off evil spirits, protect armies, and punish enemies. His destructive power is a tool that can be used for divine justice and the maintenance of order. He is also a ruler of the underworld, a necessary part of the cosmic order.
