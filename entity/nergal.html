Nergal, the lord of the underworld, whose breath carries pestilence and whose sword is drawn against the wicked.

Who They Are

Nergal is the god of war, plague, pestilence, and the underworld — a figure of destruction and death. He is the son of Enlil and Ninlil (or of Enki in some traditions). His name means 'Lord of the Great City,' a euphemism for the underworld (the 'Great City' being Kutha, the city of the dead). Nergal is depicted with a lion-headed mace, a scimitar, and sometimes as a lion-man hybrid. In the myth of Nergal and Ereshkigal, Nergal offends the underworld queen Ereshkigal by refusing to stand when her messenger arrives at a feast of the gods. Ereshkigal demands he be sent to the underworld. Enki advises Nergal to refuse food, drink, and bathing there, and (in the longer version) Nergal storms the underworld, grabs Ereshkigal by the hair, and is about to kill her when she offers marriage — he becomes her husband and co-ruler. Nergal is identified with the planet Mars (the red planet of war) and the scorching summer sun that brings plague. His cult center was at Kutha. Plague and pestilence were seen as Nergal's breath upon the land — he could 'walk' through a city and leave it full of corpses.

Attested in Nergal and Ereshkigal / Amarna Letters / IncantationsEra Sumerian to Neo-Babylonian (c. 3000-500 BCE)

Form

Nergal was not typically depicted in anthropomorphic form in iconography that survives. His primary symbol was the lion-headed mace or a double-headed mace, a weapon of war. He was also associated with the lion itself, and could be depicted as a lion or a lion-headed figure. In some depictions, he is shown as a warrior god with a bow and arrow, or holding a sickle-sword. His association with the planet Mars led to him being identified with the red, baleful star. In later syncretistic art, he could be depicted in the guise of other warrior gods.

Powers

Bringer of Plague and Pestilence Nergal's most feared power. He could unleash devastating epidemics upon humans and animals. The 'hand of Nergal' was a common idiom for a fatal disease. He was believed to be able to strike individuals or entire populations with illness. Akkadian and Sumerian texts, including royal inscriptions and incantations. His power could be appeased through ritual, prayer, and offerings. His wrath was often seen as a punishment for sin or neglect, implying he could be swayed. The god Erra, who is often syncretized with Nergal, is specifically named as the bringer of plague in the Epic of Erra, while Nergal's own role is sometimes more broadly defined as a god of death and the underworld.

God of War Nergal was a powerful war god who marched before armies, broke enemy lines, and brought victory to his followers. He was invoked in royal inscriptions for military success and was seen as the divine executioner of the king's enemies. Assyrian royal inscriptions, hymns, and prayers. His support was not guaranteed; he could also abandon a king and cause his defeat. While he is a god of war, his primary role in the underworld is as a judge and ruler, not a warrior. The two aspects are sometimes presented as distinct.

Lord of the Underworld Nergal ruled the underworld, Kur, alongside Ereshkigal. He presided over the dead, judged souls, and could decide their fate. He was the master of the 'Land of No Return'. The Myth of Nergal and Ereshkigal, various underworld texts. In some traditions, he was subordinate to Ereshkigal as the primary ruler of the underworld, while in others he was her equal or even dominant. The boundary of his power was the underworld itself. The Sumerian goddess Ereshkigal is the original and primary ruler of the underworld. Nergal's role as co-ruler is a later development, and the dynamics of their power sharing vary between versions of the myth.

Bringer of Famine and Destruction As Erra, he could cause famine by destroying crops and livestock. He could also bring about the destruction of cities and kingdoms through war, plague, and natural disasters. The Epic of Erra. His destructive power is often framed as a punishment for a city's sins, and he can be persuaded to relent, as in the Epic of Erra where he is eventually convinced to stop his rampage. The Epic of Erra explicitly states that Erra (Nergal) is not a god of creation, but of destruction. However, his destruction is often framed as a necessary purge that ultimately allows for renewal.

Behavior & Habitat

Nergal is a deity of terrifying power and unpredictable wrath. He is a bringer of war, plague, and pestilence, a destroyer of cities and armies. He is not a god of wisdom or creation, but of destruction and the harsh realities of death and the underworld. He is fierce, impulsive, and easily angered. However, he is also a god who can be appeased, and his destructive power was invoked to protect kings and armies. He is a god who maintains order through fear and force, punishing the wicked and the rebellious. In the myth of Nergal and Ereshkigal, he is also shown as impulsive, arrogant, and disrespectful, yet ultimately capable of love and becoming a responsible ruler of the underworld.

Accounts

Nergal, a powerful god, is disrespectful to the messenger of Ereshkigal, the queen of the underworld. As punishment, he is ordered to descend to the underworld to apologize. In the Amarna version, he is warned not to eat, drink, or sit in the underworld, but he is seduced by Ereshkigal's beauty (or in some versions, he is tricked). He ends up sleeping with her for six days. On the seventh day, he tries to leave but is stopped by the gatekeeper. In the Assur version, he is persuaded to descend by Ereshkigal's messenger, and after a series of trials, he again sleeps with her. In both, he ultimately becomes her husband and the co-ruler of the underworld, thus explaining his position as Lord of the Dead.

Erra (Nergal) is restless and bored. He is persuaded by his advisor, Ishum, to unleash his destructive fury on the world, specifically on Babylon. He stirs up chaos, causing the gods to abandon their cities and the people to turn against each other. He plans to destroy Babylon and its people. However, Ishum eventually persuades him to stop, and the epic ends with Erra returning to his temple in Kutha, promising to be a source of order and protection for the land. The epic is a complex meditation on divine wrath, political upheaval, and the fragility of civilization.

Nergal is often invoked as a protector who slays demons, evil spirits, and malevolent forces that threaten humanity. He is the divine warrior who uses his mace to crush the enemies of the gods and the king.

A way to hold it in your mind

If Enlil is the CEO of the divine corporation and Shamash is the head of Legal and Justice, then Nergal is the Head of Security and Hazardous Materials. He handles the messy, dangerous, and necessary work of eliminating threats, enforcing punishment, and managing the ultimate waste disposal (the underworld). He is not a 'bad guy', but he is the guy you call when you need a problem permanently and violently removed.
