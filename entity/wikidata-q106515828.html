The Approach

At a festival honoring the sea god Neptune, a disguised god competes for the heart of a mortal nymph—but the path to love is paved with rivalry, prophecy, and a devastating wave of divine wrath.

Content advisory. Depictions of divine violence, attempted murder, and drowning.
Identity

Naïs

Pronunciation nah-EESS

Known As

Le triomphe de la paix (original title)

Etymology

The name 'Naïs' is of Greek origin, possibly derived from 'naiein' (to flow) or associated with water nymphs (Naiads).

Cultural Ownership

French Baroque opera; part of the Western classical music tradition.

Disputed Identifications

The character Naïs is likely a literary invention of the librettist Louis de Cahusac, not a figure from ancient Greek mythology, though her name evokes Naiads.

Description

Appearance

No specific physical description of Naïs is provided in the source material. As a nymph, she is likely portrayed as a beautiful young woman in pastoral attire.

Symbols & Iconography

Naïs is associated with the sea, nymphs, and the Isthmian Games. Her father is Tiresias, the blind prophet.

Behaviour & Personality

Naïs is portrayed as virtuous and initially resistant to Neptune's advances. She is modest, as shown by her discomfort when her name is linked with the god's. She falls in love with the disguised Neptune for his character, not his divine status.

Domains & Habitats

Naïs lives on the Isthmus of Corinth, near the sea. She is a nymph, likely associated with fresh water, but her story is tied to the sea god.

Version Differences

No major version differences are documented. The opera is the primary source for this character.

Stories & Myths

The Opera Naïs

Source Libretto by Louis de Cahusac, music by Jean-Philippe RameauCulture French BaroquePeriod 18th century

Summary

Neptune, in love with the nymph Naïs, disguises himself as a mortal to win her love on his own merits. He competes with Télénus and Astérion for her affection. Tiresias, Naïs's father, warns the rivals to beware the sea god, which they misinterpret as a command to sacrifice their rival. They attempt to kill Neptune, who drowns them. He reveals his identity and takes Naïs to his underwater palace, where he makes her a goddess.

Consequences

Naïs becomes a goddess; her mortal suitors are killed; peace is restored.

Variants

The prologue allegorically represents the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle, with Jupiter restoring order among the gods, symbolizing the peace.

Warning: Divine violence, attempted murder, drowning. —

Attempted murder and drowning

Source Opera libretto

Description

Télénus and Astérion, misinterpreting Tiresias's prophecy, attempt to kill the disguised Neptune. In retaliation, Neptune summons waves that drown them.

Scholarly Context

The violence is allegorical, representing the triumph of peace over discord, and is typical of Baroque opera's use of mythological themes.
