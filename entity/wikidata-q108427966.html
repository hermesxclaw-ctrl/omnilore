Who It Is

While the Amazon queen Penthesilea cut down Achaeans outside Troy, a different kind of fire caught inside the walls — not on the battlefield, but among the Trojan women watching from above. One noblewoman, Tisiphone daughter of Antimachus, threw down her distaff, picked up a spear, and tried to lead every grieving wife and mother in the city out to die fighting rather than live as slaves.

Attested behavior in Posthomerica 1.404-435: passionate, eloquent, and militant. Inspired by watching Penthesilea's aristeia, she experiences 'fiery passion for the fray' (Way) and delivers a sustained deliberative speech (approx. 30 lines) urging Trojan women to fight. Powers
Rhetorical Leadership and Exhortation

Ability to move a large group of Trojan women to immediate martial action through a single public speech. Her words 'thrilled all those women' and caused collective arming. This is a social/charismatic power, not supernatural.

Limit: Persuasion is temporary and reversible; Theano's counter-speech immediately undoes her influence. No authority beyond peers; she does not command soldiers or hold office.

Noble Status and Social Capital

As daughter of Antimachus (a leading Trojan counselor bribed with gold by Paris) and wife of Meneptolemus, she has high social standing enabling her to address other noblewomen as 'Friends' and be heard. Implied wealth and household authority.

Limit: Status does not translate to military training or legal right to fight; Theano explicitly denies women's capacity for war regardless of rank.

Martial Aspiration (Attempted, Not Realized)

Willingness and attempted assumption of hoplite-like combat: donning armor and intending to fight Danaans outside the walls. Described as 'mail-clad, afire to battle.' No actual combat feat is performed; intervention prevents it.

Limit: Explicitly stated to lack training, endurance, and physical conditioning for war ('Never your limbs have toiled in conflict yet... your strength can never be as that of Danaan men, men trained in daily battle' - Theano). Would have died if not stopped ('these had died in that wild battle, as their husbands died').

Accounts I
The Exhortation of the Trojan WomenQuintus Smyrnaeus, Posthomerica Book 1, lines 404-438 (Way 1913)

c. 3rd century CE, narrating mythical Trojan War c. 12th century BCE in epic time

After Penthesilea the Amazon arrives to aid Troy and performs prodigious feats against the Achaeans, the Trojan women watch from the walls and marvel. Tisiphone, daughter of Antimachus and wife of Meneptolemus, is seized by war-lust and delivers a long speech urging all Trojan women to arm and fight alongside their men. She argues women are not made differently from men - they have same eyes, limbs, breath and nourishment - and points to Penthesilea, a woman far excelling men, as proof. She reminds them each has lost sons, husbands, fathers or brothers, and that the alternative to fighting is imminent slavery for themselves and their children after the city burns. Her speech inflames the women, who throw aside wool and distaffs and rush to seize weapons, likened by the poet to bees swarming from a hive in spring. Immediate mobilization of Trojan women; they arm themselves and prepare to sally from the city. Creates narrative crisis that requires intervention. Demonstrates desperation of Troy and theme of women transgressing gender roles in extremis.

Theano's Dissuasion and the Averting of DisasterQuintus Smyrnaeus, Posthomerica Book 1, lines 439-476 (Way 1913)

c. 3rd century CE

As the armed women are about to exit the city to certain death, the aged priestess Theano - wife of Antenor and priestess of Athena, renowned for wisdom garnered from years - intervenes with a dissuasive speech. She calls them 'infatuate ones' and argues they have never toiled in battle, lack training, and cannot match Danaan men who train daily. She contrasts them with Amazons, who from birth endure men's toil, ride horses and delight in war, and whose queen is said to be daughter of Ares; no ordinary woman can compare. She argues division of labor is best: women should stay at the loom, men at war, and that the military situation does not require female sacrifice - the Achaeans are falling and Trojan men are waxing strong. The women heed her and remain within, watching from afar. The poet confirms they would otherwise have died like the Amazons and their husbands. Women are saved from annihilation; gender norms are reasserted; Theano's wisdom is validated. Tisiphone's initiative fails but is preserved as example of noble despair. Narrative returns to Penthesilea's combat and death

The Bribe of Antimachus - Family BackgroundHomer, Iliad Book 11, lines 122-135 (Murray translation)

c. 8th century BCE

During the embassy before the war, Paris (Alexandros) and Antimachus' political alliance is revealed when Agamemnon captures Antimachus' sons Pisander and Hippolochus. The narrator explains Antimachus was the Trojan who most strongly urged that Helen not be returned to Menelaus, because Paris had bribed him with gold and rich gifts. His sons therefore beg Agamemnon for ransom, offering much treasure from their father's house, but Agamemnon refuses, citing their father's guilt, and kills them. Establishes Antimachus family as wealthy, pro-war, and morally compromised by bribery; explains Trojan intransigence over Helen. For Tisiphone, it establishes her natal oikos as politically influential but tainted, and foreshadows loss of brothers. Her own stance in Quintus - urging war - ironically continues her father's hawkish line.

Accounts II
The Deaths of Pisander and HippolochusHomer, Iliad Book 11, lines 138-147

c. 8th century BCE

Pisander and Hippolochus, sons of Antimachus, are riding together in one chariot when Agamemnon confronts them. Their horses shy, they are thrown, and Agamemnon kills Pisander with a spear through the chest and Hippolochus by cutting off his arms and head. Agamemnon explicitly denies ransom, saying Antimachus' counsel must be paid for. Nestor later references the episode as example of Agamemnon's ferocity. Extinction of two of Tisiphone's brothers before the events of Posthomerica Book 1 (which occurs late in war, after Hector's death). By the time Tisiphone speaks, she is already a bereaved sister; her lament that 'some grieve for brethren and for kinsmen lost' includes her own loss. Reduces Antimachus' male heirs.

The Death of Hippomachus son of AntimachusHomer, Iliad Book 12, lines 188-194

c. 8th century BCE

During the battle at the Achaean wall, Leonteus, companion of Polypoetes, kills Hippomachus son of Antimachus with a spear thrust. The death is listed among the casualties as the Trojans assault the fortifications. This is a third son of Antimachus, distinct from Pisander and Hippolochus, indicating Antimachus had at least three (or four, counting Hippolochus variant spelling) sons. Further depletion of Tisiphone's brothers; by late war she may be the sole surviving child of Antimachus. Explains her prominence among Trojan women - as senior surviving daughter of a leading house.

Penthesilea's Arrival and Aristeia - Catalyst for TisiphoneQuintus Smyrnaeus, Posthomerica Book 1, lines 18-326; also Epic Cycle Aethiopis summary (Proclus)

Mythical time: after Hector's death, before Achilles' death

Penthesilea, daughter of Ares and queen of the Amazons, arrives at Troy seeking death in battle to atone for accidentally killing her sister Hippolyta. Priam welcomes her. She arms magnificently and sallies forth, killing many Achaeans including Machaon and others, and is likened to a goddess. Her prowess is watched by Trojans and Trojan women from the walls, providing the direct visual stimulus for Tisiphone's speech. She is eventually killed by Achilles, who then mourns her beauty (and Thersites is killed for mocking his grief). Provides narrative and thematic frame for Tisiphone episode: female martial excellence as both inspiration and warning. The Amazons' ultimate death foreshadows what would have happened to Trojan women had they fought. Penthesilea's story is the best-known female warrior paradigm in Troy literature.

Tisiphone daughter of Antimachus is the same as Tisiphone the Fury who punishes murderers in the Underworld. — They are homonyms only. The Fury Tisiphone is a chthonic goddess, daughter of Gaia and Uranus (or Nyx), one of the three Erinyes, with snakes in her hair, who torments Orestes and others. The Trojan Tisiphone is a mortal noblewoman, daughter of Antimachus, wife of Meneptolemus, with no divine powers, who appears only in Quintus Smyrnaeus Book 1. Ancient sources never confuse them; modern confusion arises from identical spelling.

Tisiphone actually fought the Greeks alongside Penthesilea and the Amazons. — She never fights. Quintus explicitly states the women armed and were about to sally, but Theano's intervention stopped them, and they watched from afar. The poet adds 'And now without the city these had died in that wild battle, as their husbands died. The strong Amazons died, had not one voice of wisdom cried...' Her martial action is averted.

Hippodameia and Tisiphone are two different Trojan women, both wives of Trojan men. — They are variant readings for the same character in the same lines of Quintus 1.404-405. Manuscripts diverge, and translators choose one. There is no evidence for two separate women in that scene; Vian's apparatus treats them as textual variants, not two persons.

Tisiphone was a daughter of Priam. — She is daughter of Antimachus, a counselor, not of Priam. The confusion arises from the variant where her husband is called 'Tisiphonus son of Priam,' leading readers to misattribute the patronymic to her. No source calls her Priamid.
