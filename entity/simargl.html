Who It Is

On a hill outside the walls of tenth-century Kiev, Vladimir the Great set up six idols in wood and metal — and one name in that list, 'Simargl', has driven over a century of scholarly argument precisely because nobody can agree on what it even is. The chronicle gives us the name once, and cannot even spell it the same way in its surviving copies; a later Christian sermon either repeats the same god or quietly splits it into two, 'Sim and Rgl'. Is Simargl a borrowed Iranian dog-bird, a shy pair of agricultural worker-gods, a Soviet archaeologist's plant-protector, or a fire god with the wrong name? The answer is that the historical record is so thin that Simargl is less a deity with a personality than a four-century-old palimpsest onto which every era has written its own fears and hopes — from twelfth-century monks denouncing demonic fire rites to a modern Welsh novelist chaining the crea

Reconstructed, not attested. As a plant-protector (Rybakov) Simargl is a benevolent guardian of growth, a lower-order deity mediating between the high sky gods and the earth. As an Iranian-descended intermediary (Vasilyev) he is a psychopomp-like mediator between worlds who, in Eastern Iranian mythology, protected human communities. As the two deities Sěm and Rgel they are agricultural worker-gods. No surviving narrative dramatizes Simargl's personality, motive or speech; all characterizations are scholarly reconstruction.

Powers
Guardianship of plants and growth

In Rybakov's reconstruction, Simargl was the god of seeds, sprouts and roots of plants, the protector of shoots and greens, acting as an intermediary between the highest gods of heaven and the earth. He was a deity of a lower order charged with nurturing vegetation.

Limit: Rybakov's 'plant-god' reading is explicitly questioned by Vasilyev, who notes that no Iranian or Indian source depicts the Simurgh as a protector of plants, so Simargl could not have inherited that competence.

Intermediary between heaven and earth / worlds

Vasilyev argues the common Indo-Iranian characteristic of the Simurgh was to mediate between worlds (a psychopomp-like role), and that at a later stage in Eastern Iranian mythology it became the protector of humans, especially human communities/clusters — which may explain its introduction into Vladimir's 980 pantheon.

Limit: Vasilyev stresses there is no evidence that among the Eastern Iranians the Simurgh was pictured with a dog's head; it was more likely a large predatory bird, so Simargl as dog-bird is not guaranteed.

Fire and hearth (Ognebog reading)

The Russian alias 'Огнебог' (Ognebog, 'Fire-god') and Czech 'Ogněbog' suggest Simargl was sometimes viewed as a fire deity. The Sermon's framing of offerings 'with fire under the fields of sheaves' connects the figure(s) to fire and the grain harvest, blending the fire and agricultural registers.

Limit: No primary East Slavic text explicitly calls Simargl a fire god; the Ognebog alias is a later scholarly/folk label, not an attested theonym in the two source texts.

Dark-cutting / harvest deity (Rgel)

In the two-deity reading, Rgel (from PS *rьglъ, from *regti 'to cut') is a deity of cutting the harvest and of making clearings (holes, fissures, cleared places), derived from the PIE root *h₁regʷ- 'to be dark'. This makes Rgel a god of agricultural labour — reaping and land-clearing.

Limit: Łuczyński rejects Brückner's and Witczak & Kaczor's readings on orthographic grounds (erroneous hard sign rъ vs the attested soft sign rь).

Sowing deity (Sěm)

In the two-deity reading, Sěm derives from PS *sêmъ 'sowing' (from *sěti 'to sow'), i.e. 'that which is sown' — a god of sowing and fertility of the fields.

Limit: Witczak & Kaczor's broader Italo-Celtic parallels (Sēmūnes, Sabine Sancus) are criticized by Łuczyński as reconstructing a different proto-form *seVmōn-.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Attested and reconstructed. No primary source describes Simargl's appearance — the winged-dog iconography is an inference. The dominant reconstruction (Trever/Rybakov) depicts Simargl as a winged dog with fangs and claws, an image Rybakov claimed to find on East Slavic bracelets and other metalwork. The Iranian source figure — the Simurgh — is described (in Persian and Iranian tradition) as a peacock-sized bird with the head of a dog and the claw

Accounts I
Vladimir the Great erects the pantheon (980)Primary Chronicle (PVL), entry under the year 980

980 CE

After Vladimir I (later the baptizer of Rus') consolidated his rule in Kiev, he set up idols on a hill outside his palace: Perun in wood with a silver head and a gold moustache, and Khors, Dazhbog, Stribog, Simargl and Mokosh. The chronicler says sacrifices were offered and the people 'took their sons and daughters to them and sacrificed them to the devils', profaning the earth and the hill with blood. This is Simargl's only appearance in the Primary Chronicle, listed among the six principal gods of the Kievan state cult. Establishes Simargl as one of the six state deities of Vladimir's pre-Christian pantheon; the listing is the basis of nearly all modern reconstructions.

Accounts II
The Sermon's list of idols and the fire under the sheavesSermon by One Who Loves Christ (second half of the 12th century)

c. 1150-1200

A later Christian sermon condemns 'demonic festivities — dancing, music and profane songs, and offerings to the idols', specifying that believers 'with fire under the fields of sheaves pray to the Vily, to Mokosh, and Sim and Rgl, to Perun, Rod, the Rozhanitsy'. Here the figure appears as two separate deities, 'Sim and Rgl' — the textual basis for the two-deity theory. The context links them to a harvest/fire ritual around sheaves. Supplies the only second attestation of the theonym and the only source that splits it into Sěm and Rgel.

The Iranian borrowing (Trever's thesis)Kamilla Trever, 1933; developed by Boris Rybakov and accepted by Gieysztor, Ivanov & Toporov, Vasilyev (with caveats)

Proposed 1933; dominant view since

Trever proposed that Simargl is the East Slavic reflex of the Iranian Simurgh (Middle Persian sēnmurw, Avestan mərəγō Saēnō), a winged creature with a dog's head that protected plants. The borrowing was sourced to Persian Simurg or Scythian-Sarmatian Sěnmary. Rybakov built this into the full plant-god reconstruction (Simargl-Pereplut), while Vasilyev qualified it: he accepts the linguistic plausibility but rejects the 'plant-god' competence on the grounds that no Iranian/Indian source shows the Simurgh as a plant protector, and questions whether the Eastern-Iranian form was a dog-head at all. Established the single-deity reading now dominant in scholarship; triggered the linguistic objections that keep the question open.

Simargl was a canonical, fully-described winged-dog god with a rich personality, powers, temples and rituals. — Simargl is mentioned in exactly two dated sources (the 980 Primary Chronicle entry and the 12th-century Sermon) and appears in neither with a description of appearance, powers, personality, cult or ritual. The winged-dog image, plant-protector role and fire identity are all scholarly reconstructions layered onto an extremely thin attestation.

The internet-thought 'Simargl is the Slavic fire god' (Ognebog) is the deity's fixed core identity. — The fire-god label is a later Russian/Czech alias, not an attested theonym in either source text. It risks conflating Simargl with the separately-attested fire gods Svarog and Svarozhich.

The American Gods doomsday hound chained to Polaris describes an actual historical Slavic belief. — Gaiman's Simargl-as-world-devouring-hound is original myth-building for the novel, not an attested East Slavic tradition. Neither the Chronicle nor the Sermon describes Simargl as a chained world-eating hound.
