Attested in Wikidata public knowledge graph — Q16737262

Form

No canonical physical description survives from antiquity. In later art influenced by her syncretism with Nemesis, she is depicted as a winged goddess holding scales or a wheel of fortune. As a Cretan nymph, she is imagined as a young woman of the wild, attending a divine infant. The absence of a fixed form suggests she was more a force than a personality.

Powers

Inevitability Her name itself is her power: she cannot be escaped. Those who flee justice find her waiting at the end of every road. Hellenistic syncretism with Nemesis She punishes hubris and excess, not ordinary human error; the just have nothing to fear from her. Whether she is a separate goddess or an aspect of Nemesis is unresolved in the sources.

Divine Nursing She nourished the infant Zeus with honey and the milk of Amalthea, keeping him alive and hidden from Cronus. Cretan nymph tradition (Apollodorus) This is a one-time mythological act, not a repeatable power. Some sources assign the nursing role to the nymph Ida or to the goat Amalthea herself.

Hearing and Punishing The Orphic Hymn describes her as one who hears the prayers of the wronged and punishes the wicked, including the proud and the cruel. Orphic tradition Her punishments, while inescapable, are not always immediate; the delay is part of the terror. None recorded.

Cosmic Ordering In Hellenistic and Neoplatonic thought, Adrasteia became a principle of cosmic balance — the law that every excess must be corrected. Philosophical reinterpretation (Plato, Laws 4.717d; later Neoplatonists) This is a philosophical abstraction, not a cultic power. Philosophers disagreed on whether she was a person, a daimon, or a natural law.

Behavior & Habitat

As a nurse of Zeus, she is protective, resourceful, and secretive — hiding the infant god from his father Cronus. As Nemesis-Adrasteia, she is implacable, just, and terrifying: she tracks hubris, punishes the arrogant, and restores cosmic balance without mercy or favor.

Accounts

When Rhea gave birth to Zeus, she hid him in a cave on Mount Ida in Crete to save him from being devoured by Cronus. The nymphs Adrasteia and Ida took charge of the infant, feeding him honey and the milk of the goat Amalthea. The Curetes (or Curtes) danced and clashed their shields to drown out the baby's cries. Zeus grew to adulthood in this hidden nursery, then returned to overthrow his father.

As an aspect of Nemesis, Adrasteia punishes those who overstep human limits. The Orphic Hymn describes her as the 'dread goddess' who hears the cries of the oppressed and brings down the proud. In Aeschylus, the chorus warns that her altar must be approached with reverence, for she punishes the arrogant who forget their mortality.

Near Cyzicus in Asia Minor, a cave sanctuary was dedicated to Adrasteia, likely a continuation of an older Phrygian mother-goddess cult. The site drew pilgrims who sought her protection and oracular guidance. The cave setting connects her to the earth and to hidden, chthonic powers.

What We Don't Know

No primary source describes Adrasteia's appearance in detail. The Phrygian cave sanctuary near Cyzicus has not been fully excavated; its exact rituals and iconography remain unknown. The transition from nymph to Nemesis-aspect is not documented in any surviving source, leaving the merger's chronology and cause to inference. Modern scholarship on her cult is limited; no dedicated monograph exists.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Adrasteia as the ancient Greek version of 'what goes around comes around' — but with teeth. She's the reason you knock on wood after saying something good about yourself. She's the cosmic speed bump for the arrogant, and the divine babysitter who kept the king of the gods alive. If karma had a name in the ancient world, it was hers.
