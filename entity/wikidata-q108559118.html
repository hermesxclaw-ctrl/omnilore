Who It Is

In the wind-walled hall on the floating island of Lipara, twelve children feast together every night — six sons beside six daughters, music never ceasing, the smell of roast meat drifting out over the sea. Among the daughters sits Eurygone, named only once in the surviving record, her entire existence bound up in that strange, close-kept family. She has no monsters to slay and no metamorphosis to suffer, yet her name preserves a question the Greeks themselves debated: what does it mean when a father marries his children to each other to keep his house from breaking apart?

No speeches, actions, decisions, or personality traits are attributed to Eurygone individually. Collectively, the Aeolids are described as living in concord (homophrosyne) and continuous feasting (Homer: 'they feast continually'), and Tzetzes repeats the claim that Aeolus married sons to daughters 'to preserve concord and affection among them.' Whether this reflects Eurygone's own agency, consent, or character is never discussed in sources. Status: unknown / collective only.

Powers
No attested supernatural power

No source attributes any divine power, magical ability, transformation, weapon, or skill to Eurygone. She is presented as a mortal princess, daughter of a mortal (or semi-divine) keeper of the winds. Her father controls winds via divine favour/knowledge, but this power is not described as inherited by daughters.

Limit: Not applicable; absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, but no tradition suggests powers.

Status as wind-keeper's daughter (social prestige)

As daughter of Aeolus, Eurygone shares in her father's hospitality (xenia) and high status; the family is visited by heroes (Odysseus) and described as beloved by gods. This is social, not supernatural, capital.

Limit: Hospitality is extended by Aeolus himself; daughters are not shown exercising independent authority.

Accounts I
Naming the Daughters: Tzetzes Lists Eurygone Among the AeolidsJohn Tzetzes, Allegories of the Odyssey 14.88-96 (Goldwyn & Kokkini 2015)

12th century CE

In his allegorical exposition of Odyssey 10, Tzetzes enumerates the children of Aeolus of Lipara to explain Homer's brief notice. He names the sons as Periphas, Agenor, Euchenor, Klymenos, Xouthos, Macareus and the daughters as Klymene, Kallithyia, Eurygone, Lysidike, Kanake and one unnamed daughter. He repeats Homer's statement that Aeolus 'yoked in marriage his sons and daughters, including Eurygone, in order to preserve concord and affection among them.' This is the only surviving text that preserves the name Eurygone, likely drawing on a lost Hellenistic mythographic handbook or scholion. Provides the sole onomastic attestation for Eurygone; without Tzetzes she would be an anonymous member of a collective. Demonstrates how Byzantine scholars preserved and systematized fragmentary genealogies. Modern databases (Lexicon Iconographicum Mythologiae Classicae, Wikidata, Parada) depend entirely on this passage for Eurygone's entry.

Yoked in Marriage: Sibling Unions to Preserve ConcordHomer Odyssey 10.7 + Tzetzes Allegories + Scholia to Odyssey 10.5-7

Archaic to Byzantine (750 BCE - 12th c. CE)

Both Homer and Tzetzes state that Aeolus gave his daughters to his sons as wives. Homer presents it without comment, as a mark of the family's closeness; Tzetzes explicitly moralizes it as a deliberate policy 'to preserve concord and affection (homonoia kai philotes).' The scholia debate whether this reflects ancient custom, poetic idealization, or an allegory for the harmony of winds. For Eurygone, this means she was married to one of her own brothers — which brother is never specified, and no children are attributed to the union in any source. Creates the central dark-history and interpretive problem of the Aeolids: endogamy as utopian concord versus incest taboo. Later tragedians (Euripides' Aeolus about Kanake and Macareus) dramatize the tragic potential of the same structure, though Eurygone is not part of that tragedy. Modern scholarship reads the motif as mythic exaggeration of aristocratic endogamy or as narrative isolation of the wind-keeper's family from normal exogamy.

Accounts II
The Guest in the Hall: Odysseus Entertained by Aeolus' FamilyHomer, Odyssey 10.13-76

c. 750-650 BCE

Odysseus and his crew arrive at Aeolia and are hospitably received for a full month. They feast in the hall where Aeolus' sons and daughters — including, by implication, Eurygone — are present. Aeolus eventually gives Odysseus a bag containing all winds except Zephyrus to aid his homecoming. The episode ends with the crew's folly in opening the bag near Ithaca. Eurygone is not singled out in action or dialogue; her presence is collective, part of the feasting household that witnesses and participates in xenia (guest-friendship). The extended stay implies daily interaction between the Aeolids and the Ithacans. Provides the only narrative action in which Eurygone can be placed, albeit anonymously. Establishes the Aeolids as participants in one of the Odyssey's key hospitality episodes, contrasting with later inhospitable hosts (Polyphemus, Laestrygonians). The bag-of-winds motif becomes Aeolus' defining attribute in all later reception.

Two Aeoluses: The Keeper of the Winds vs. the Ancestor of the AeoliansDiodorus Siculus 5.7-8; Pseudo-Apollodorus Bibliotheca 1.7.3; Pausanias 10.38.4; scholia

5th century BCE (Pindar, Euripides) through 2nd century CE

Greek tradition preserves two distinct figures named Aeolus: (1) Aeolus son of Hippotes, keeper of the winds on Lipara, father of Eurygone; (2) Aeolus son of Hellen and Orseis, eponym of the Aeolian Greeks, father of sons Cretheus, Sisyphus, Athamas, etc., and daughters like Canace (a different Canace/Kanake). Ancient and modern compilers frequently conflate them, leading to genealogical chaos. Eurygone belongs strictly to the first, Liparian Aeolus, whose children do not appear in the Hellenic stemma. Recognizing the split is essential to avoid assigning Eurygone siblings like Sisyphus or a husband outside her brother-group. Explains why Eurygone appears in no broad genealogical handbook that follows the Hellenic Aeolus. Clarifies that searches for 'Aeolus daughters' often return the wrong set. Modern reference works (Grimal, Tripp, Smith) now explicitly separate the two Aeoluses, placing Eurygone only in the Hippotade entry.

The Sister's Shadow: Kanake, Macareus, and the Tragic Counterpoint to Eurygone's Silent MarriageEuripides, Aeolus (fragmentary tragedy, TrGF 5.1 F 14-41); Ovid Heroides 11; Plutarch Parallela

5th century BCE (Euripides) - 1st century CE (Ovid)

While Eurygone's sibling marriage is presented as harmonious and sanctioned by her father, her sister Kanake's union with brother Macareus (in Euripides' lost Aeolus) is presented as illicit love leading to tragedy: Aeolus discovers the incest, sends Kanake a sword to kill herself, and exposes her infant. Ovid's Heroides 11 gives Kanake a despairing letter to Macareus. This parallel shows that the same family structure could be narrated as either utopian concord (Homer/Tzetzes on Eurygone) or catastrophe (Euripides/Ovid on Kanake). Eurygone is never implicated in the tragic version; her marriage, by contrast, is described as stable and without issue or incident, highlighting the selective silence of the sources. Demonstrates the narrative range of Aeolid incest: from normalized endogamy to tragic transgression. Explains why modern audiences often assume all Aeolid sibling marriages ended badly — they are generalizing Kanake's story to Eurygone, for whom no tragedy is attested. Eurygone was a wind goddess or had power over winds like her father. — No source attributes wind powers to Eurygone or any daughter. Aeolus' control of winds is his alone (or his learned skill per Diodorus). Daughters are mortal princesses in a feasting household, not deities. The inference that 'children of wind-keeper = wind powers' is modern invention, likely from games and Percy Jackson-style demigod logic.

Eurygone's story is the same as Kanake's — she fell in love with her brother Macareus and was forced to kill herself. — Kanake's tragic incest with Macareus (Euripides/Ovid) is a separate narrative about Eurygone's sister. Eurygone is never linked to Macareus specifically, never described as having an illicit affair, and no source gives her a tragic death. Her sibling marriage is described as father-arranged and harmonious, not as clandestine passion.

Aeolus is the Greek god of the winds and Eurygone is a divine princess. — Homer and Diodorus present Aeolus son of Hippotes as a mortal (or favored mortal) entrusted with winds, not a god; the god of winds is Aeolus only in later Roman/Virgilian reception, and even then as a minor deity. Eurygone is mortal, with no cult or divine status.

Eurygone appears in Homer's Odyssey by name. — Homer never names any daughter; he says only 'six daughters' married to six sons (Od. 10.5-7). The name Eurygone appears first in Tzetzes, 1,800 years later. Any claim of Homeric naming is false.
