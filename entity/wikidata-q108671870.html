Who It Is

Hold a domino and you hold a paradox: a tiny, blank-backed rectangle that can be a die, a card, a mask, and a falling city. Two squares of pips — or nothing at all — contain the mathematics of combinations, the memory of Song-dynasty peddlers, the echo of French parlors, and the physics of a chain that can topple for miles. It has no face, yet everyone recognizes it.

As artifact, domino has no agency; its 'behavior' is combinatorial and physical: tiles are shuffled face-down (boneyard), drawn, matched end-to-end by equal pip count, block, score, or topple. Social behavior is highly variable by game: cooperative partnership (Domino Whist, Texas 42), cutthroat blocking (Block), point-racing (Muggins/All Fives, Fives and Threes), network branching (Chicken Foot, Mexican Train with central spinner). In toppling, behavior is purely physics — standing on edge, sequential kinetic transfer.

Powers
Combinatorial Matching (Blocking)

Core mechanic: a tile may be placed only if one end matches the pip count of an open end of the layout. This creates blocking when no player can match.

Limit: Limited by hand composition and open ends; in Block, inability to play passes turn. No tile can match a blank unless blank is in set.

Scoring by Multiples (Fives and Threes / All Fives / Muggins)

Points scored when open-end pip totals are divisible by 5 (or 3 and 5). In Muggins/All Fives, each 5 pips = 5 points; in British Fives and Threes, 5s and 3s score separately. Cribbage board originally used to track to 31, later 150/200 points.

Limit: Only counts at specific moments (after placement); requires arithmetic; house rules vary on whether all open ends or just line ends count.

Drawing / Fishing

When unable to match, player draws from boneyard until able to play or boneyard empty. Called Domino à la Pêche (Fishing Domino) in early French rules.

Limit: Boneyard size limits draws; in Block Game drawing is prohibited.

Spinner / Pivot Branching

A double tile (often double-twelve or designated spinner) allows play off all four sides, creating branching layout. Metal pin (spinner/pivot) in center of some tiles facilitates rotation.

Limit: Only designated doubles act as spinners; rules vary on whether spinner must be covered. Not present in traditional European or Chinese sets.

Toppling Chain Reaction

Tiles stood on edge transfer momentum when toppled; domino effect used for entertainment installations and world-record attempts. Not a game rule but physical affordance of tile shape and weight.

Limit: Requires uniform spacing, flat surface, protection from breeze (especially card-stock sets); lightweight sets easily disrupted.

Divinatory Randomization

Use as lots/bones for divination: casting/throwing dominoes to read patterns, analogous to dice or bone throwing in Chinese culture and African diaspora traditions.

Limit: Interpretation systems are culture-specific and not standardized; not attested as primary function in European sources.

Form & Signs

Appearance — European-style domino: rectangular tile, typically ~1:2 ratio, divided by central line (often incised) into two square ends. Each end bears 0-6 pips (spots/dots) in double-six set, arranged as on six-sided dice except blank (0) is empty; larger sets extend to 9, 12, 15, 18 pips per end. Pips traditionally inlaid/painted black or white on bone/ivory/ebony ground; modern sets use color-coded pips per value for readability or Arabic numerals on larg

Symbols — Pip arrangements echo dice iconography; blank signifies zero/void. Double tiles (same value both ends, e.g., double-six) hold special status as highest or as spinner/pivot. In Chinese sets, color symbolism (red 1 and 4) and suit division carry cultural weight. European luxury materials themselves signal status. The double-one's two single pips visually echo the eyes of the Venetian domino mask, motivating the name transfer.

Accounts I
Pupai on the Streets of Hangzhou — The Song Peddler's TrayZhou Mi, Former Events in Wulin (Wulin jiu shi)

12th-13th century (reign of Emperor Xiaozong 1162-1189, text compiled c.1274-1298)

Zhou Mi lists wares sold by itinerant peddlers in the Southern Song capital Hangzhou: among them 'pupai' (gambling plaques) and dice. The passage does not describe rules, but places plaque-tiles in the commercial and gambling economy of the capital. Later Ming author Lu Rong uses the same term for unmistakable domino tiles, suggesting continuity. The Manual of the Xuanhe Period, purportedly by Qu You (1341-1427), later claims to codify domino play, though its authenticity is disputed and may be a retrospective forgery projecting the game back to the Xuanhe era (1119-1125). Establishes terminological anchor for Chinese domino antecedents; frames dominoes as originally functionally identical to playing cards and as representations of two-dice throws (21 results). Provides basis for 32-piece civil/military suit system.

The French Parlor Invention — From Mask to TileL'Avantcoureur (1 April 1762) and Dictionnaire de Trévoux (1771)

mid-18th century (c.1750-1771)

Modern European dominoes appear in France mid-18th century, structurally distinct from Chinese sets (28 pieces with blanks vs 32 without). The name 'domino' is first recorded in 1762, with dictionary entry by 1771. Two pre-existing meanings for domino — the Venetian carnival hooded costume/white mask and peasant woodcuts — provide the etymological source, with the double-one tile's resemblance to the mask cited as transfer point. Earliest European rules describe a simple Block Game for two or four players; later French rules add Domino à la Pêche (Fishing/Draw) and a three-hand pool game. From France the game spreads rapidly to Britain, Austria, southern Germany and Italy. Creates the canonical 28-piece double-six set as European standard; establishes Block and Draw as foundational rule families; fixes name and iconography in European languages.

Prisoners and Pubs — Dominoes Crosses the ChannelEnglish reprints of French Block and Draw rules; later British league tradition

late 18th century to 19th century

Tradition holds French prisoners-of-war introduced dominoes to England in the late 1700s. Early English forms replicate French Block Game and Draw Game with little change for over half a century. Over time dominoes becomes a staple pub and league game, culminating in the dominance of Fives and Threes as Britain's most popular league and pub game — a scoring game where open ends divisible by three or five score. Naturalizes dominoes in British social life; preserves Block/Draw rules while incubating scoring innovations that later feed back to America.

Accounts II
Cribbage on Tiles — The Scoring RevolutionEnglish and American sources 1863; The American Hoyle 1864; The Modern Pocket Hoyle 1868

1863-1871

In 1863 a new game called All Fives, Fives or Cribbage Dominoes appears simultaneously in English and American sources — the first scoring domino game, borrowing cribbage's counting and board to 31, but making 5 domino spots equal 1 game point. In 1864 The American Hoyle describes three new variants: Muggins, Bergen and Rounce alongside Block and Draw. Muggins drops the cribbage board, makes 5 spots = 5 points, game to 200 (2 players) or 150 (3-4), but initially lacks the 'muggins rule' (challenging opponent's missed score) despite name. The 1868 Modern Pocket Hoyle adds the muggins challenge rule; both rule sets are reprinted in parallel for ~20 years before the challenge version prevails. From ~1871, new scoring refinements continue. Transforms dominoes from pure blocking/drawing to point-racing; establishes All Fives/Muggins family as dominant American scoring tradition and ancestor to modern Fives and Threes and Texas 42 scoring logic.

Trains Across Texas — Extended Sets and Branching GamesModern game codifications (Texas 42, Chicken Foot, Mexican Train, Double Fives, Concentration)

20th century (Texas 42 early 20th c.; Chicken Foot and Mexican Train late 20th c.)

To accommodate more than four players, sets are extended by increasing maximum pips by three: double-nine (55 tiles), double-twelve (91), double-fifteen (136), double-eighteen (190) — the practical maximum. Larger sets introduce readability aids (color-coded pips, Arabic numerals) and spinner pivots. New games exploit larger sets and branching: Texas 42 (a trick-taking bidding game using dominoes as cards, Texas' official state domino game), Chicken Foot (doubles must be answered on three sides forming chicken-foot shape), Mexican Train (central spinner with personal and communal trains), plus Concentration (memory), Double Fives, Matador and Domino Whist. Domino toppling as separate entertainment also grows, using the tile's physicality rather than its numbers. Expands domino set from 28 to up to 190 pieces; shifts design toward colored/translucent plastics and numeral tiles; creates distinct American train-game family and competitive toppling subculture.

Bones That Speak — Divination and the Afterlife of TilesEthnographic reports of Chinese bone throwing and African diaspora divination; observation of domino toppling as performance

17th century to present

Beyond gaming, dominoes have been used for divination — casting tiles like dice or bones to read fate, documented in Chinese culture and in African diaspora traditions. In the West, dominoes also acquire a second life as kinetic sculpture: domino toppling, where thousands of tiles are stood on edge to create chain-reaction installations, filmed for television and online video. Both uses treat the tile not as a matching piece but as a randomizer or physical mass. Demonstrates domino set as generic randomizing/physical device akin to dice or cards, adaptable to ritual and spectacle beyond competitive play.

Domino is named for the number two (Latin duo) or for having two sides. — No connection to the number two in any language. Name comes from French domino (Venetian carnival hooded costume/white mask); double-one tile resembles mask. Polyomino was coined from domino in 1953, not the reverse.

Dominoes were invented in Europe. — Earliest domino-like tiles are Chinese (Song dynasty, 12th-13th century pupai, 32-piece civil/military set). European 28-piece set appears mid-18th century France with no confirmed link to Chinese set.

Chinese dominoes traditionally had blanks and 28 pieces. — Traditional Chinese set is 32 pieces with no blanks; blanks appear only in 17th-century Chinese variants. 28-piece double-six with blanks is European standard.
