Who It Is

Sir Tristan (Tristram, Drustanus in Celtic) is one of the greatest knights of the Round Table and the protagonist of what is arguably the most famous love story of the Middle Ages — the doomed romance of Tristan and Iseult (Isolde, Yseult). His tale predates the Lancelot-Guinevere story and profoundly influenced it: Tristan is the original adulterous lover in Arthurian romance. His name, from the French triste ('sad'), was given to him because his mother died in childbirth — he was born into sorrow and lived his entire life under its sign.

Tristan's story begins with his uncle, King Mark of Cornwall, who sends him to Ireland to bring back the Irish princess Iseult as Mark's bride. On the return voyage, Tristan and Iseult accidentally drink a love potion intended for Mark and Iseult's wedding night — a potion brewed by Iseult's mother to ensure the political marriage would be a loving one. Instantly, Tristan and Iseult fall into a love that is absolute, irresistible, and catastrophic. They cannot resist each other; they cannot stop loving; they cannot escape the political consequences of their adultery. The love potion, in the earliest versions, has a time limit (three or four years), but even after its effects wear off, Tristan and Iseult choose to continue their love — making the potion a catalyst rather than an excuse.

Tristan's life with Iseult at Mark's court is a continuous series of deceptions, narrow escapes, and exiles. The lovers communicate through elaborate codes (wood shavings floated down a stream, positioning of swords, carefully worded oaths). They are caught, exiled to the forest, discovered sleeping with a naked sword between them (which convinces Mark of their 'innocence' — the sword-as-barrier is a recurring motif), and ultimately separated. Tristan marries another woman — Iseult of the White Hands — but cannot consummate the marriage because his heart belongs to Iseult the Fair. This 'second Iseult' becomes a figure of bitter resentment in some versions, a patient wife in others.

Tristan's death is legend. Mortally wounded by a poisoned spear, he sends for Iseult the Fair, the only healer who can save him. He asks that the ship returning fly white sails if she is aboard, black sails if she is not. Iseult of the White Hands, his wife, consumed by jealousy, tells him the sails are black. Tristan dies of despair before Iseult reaches shore. She arrives, finds him dead, and dies beside him. Mark, learning the truth of the love potion, buries them side by side; a briar or honeysuckle grows from Tristan's grave and twines into Iseult's, and no matter how many times it is cut back, it grows again — the love that cannot be severed.

Tristan's integration into the Arthurian corpus was a later development. In Malory, he is a knight of the Round Table, second only to Lancelot in prowess, and his tragic love for Iseult parallels Lancelot's for Guinevere. But Tristan's story retains its independent, Celtic-inflected identity — the love potion, the sea voyages, the forest exile — distinguishing it from the more courtly French idiom of Lancelot's love.
