Who It Is

In the grand cosmic drama of Japan's creation, when the heavenly deities descended to claim the land of the reed plains, they did not come alone. Among the thunder gods and divine warriors marched a figure whose name whispers of a humbler, yet essential, duty: Ame no Mikemochi, the bearer of heavenly food. While Takemikazuchi brandished his sword and Futsunushi readied his bow, this quiet deity carried the sacred provisions—the rice, the sake, the offerings that would sustain the divine mission and sanctify the land. In a pantheon of storm and fire, this unassuming food-bearer reminds us that even gods must eat, and that the act of feeding is itself a sacred, world-shaping power.

The deity's primary attested action is participation in the divine mission to pacify the Central Land of the Reed Plains. The deity is part of the heavenly cohort sent to establish order, suggesting a role as a divine functionary who ensures the proper provisioning of the heavenly deities during their earthly mission. The deity's presence underscores the importance of food and offerings in the cosmic order.

Powers
Divine Food Provisioning

The deity's primary function is the bearing and provisioning of sacred food for the heavenly deities. This is not a martial or creative power but a sustaining and ritual function, ensuring the divine cohort is properly nourished during its mission.

Limit: The deity's role is supportive rather than central; no independent feats are attributed to Ame no Mikemochi. The deity appears only in the context of the land-subjugation narrative and does not act independently.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The Kojiki and Nihon Shoki provide no physical description of Ame no Mikemochi. The deity is not depicted in classical Japanese art, and there are no surviving iconographic traditions. In later Shinto practice, the deity is conceptualized as a divine figure bearing offerings, likely envisioned in the formal courtly attire of the Yamato period, though this is speculative reconstruction rather than attested description.

Symbols — No specific symbols are attested for Ame no Mikemochi. The deity's name itself serves as the primary symbol, encoding the function of food-bearing. In the broader context of Shinto, rice, sake, and other offerings are conceptually linked to the deity's domain, though no direct symbolic connection is documented.

Domains — Food, offerings, divine sustenance, provisioning of deities, the sacred meal, and the ritual of feeding the gods. The deity embodies the concept of mikemochi (food bearing) that underlies Shinto food offerings (shinsen) and the ritual meals shared between deities and humans.

Accounts I
The Pacification of the Central Land of the Reed PlainsKojiki and Nihon Shoki

Mythological age (Kamiyo, 'Age of the Gods')

After the creation of the Japanese islands and the establishment of the heavenly realm of Takamagahara, the land of Ashihara no Nakatsukuni (the Central Land of the Reed Plains) fell under the control of earthly deities (kunitsukami) who resisted heavenly authority. The sun goddess Amaterasu, seeking to establish her grandson Ninigi as ruler of the land, dispatched a series of divine messengers to subdue the earthly deities. The first messengers failed. In response, Amaterasu and the heavenly deities convened in the riverbed of the Heavenly Ame no Yasu River to deliberate. They chose the thunder deity Takemikazuchi and the hunting deity Futsunushi to lead the mission, with Ame no Mikemochi accompanying them as part of the divine retinue. The heavenly deities descended to the land, confronted the earthly deity Ōkuninushi and his forces, and demanded submission. Takemikazuchi's martial prowess and the negotiation with Ōkuninushi led to the peaceful transfer of authority, with Ōkuninushi retiring to the unseen world. Accounts II
The Succession of the Food DeitiesShinto tradition recorded in the Kojiki; modern shrine hagiography; Bocking, A Popular Dictionary of Shinto.

Mythic age of the kami; recorded in the eighth-century chronicles and later shrine traditions.

Ame no Mikemochi belongs to the class of heavenly food kami whose names carry the element 'miketsu' or 'mike' (august food), a designation shared with figures such as Miketsukami and at times related to the grain and fertility deity Ukemochi (Ōgetsu-hime). In the pacification of the Central Land, the heavenly deities ensure that the earth is provided with sustenance through these food kami, who become the mythic patrons of agriculture, cooking, and the imperial food rituals. Ame no Mikemochi thus embodies the heavenly guarantee that the newly descended ruling line will never want for food, tying divine authority to the material prosperity of the realm. The story positions Ame no Mikemochi within the food-kami constellation that sanctifies nourishment and agriculture, supporting the imperial rituals of first-fruits and the belief that the divine line sustains the fields of Japan.
