Who It Is

Hera sits on her golden throne at the heart of Olympus, white-armed and ox-eyed, the very image of divine queenship—and yet the stories that swirl around her are anything but serene. She is the goddess who guards marriage and family, but also the one who drives a hero mad until he kills his children, who turns a priestess into a wandering cow, who persuades a mortal woman into a fatal embrace with lightning. To follow Hera through epic, cult, and art is to watch the ideal of order constantly threatened by the very anger that defends it.

In Homeric epic, Hera is defined by her close tie to Zeus as sister and wife, and by a pronounced tendency toward cholos (anger) and eris (strife, rivalry). She is a sovereign goddess who fiercely defends her timai (honors) and reacts with relentless hostility to Zeus’s infidelities and to the children born from them. Her anger drives much of the divine politics in the Iliad: she supports the Achaeans against Troy, schemes with Athena, and even deceives Zeus by seduction to influence the course of battle. Powers
Queenship and sovereignty among the Olympian gods

Hera is repeatedly described as the wife of Zeus and queen of the gods, seated on a golden throne and sharing in the highest divine honors. Her status grants her authority in divine councils, influence over other gods, and a central role in maintaining or contesting the order of the pantheon.

Limit: Her sovereignty is constrained by Zeus’s ultimate power: she cannot overturn his decrees outright and must resort to persuasion, deception, or alliances with other gods. Her attempts to rebel against Zeus (as in later mythic traditions of a divine conspiracy) are punished and ultimately fail.

Patronage of marriage and family

Hera is the patron of marriage (gamos) and the protector of married women and families. Her epiclesis Teleia (“of fulfilled marriage”) and cult practices at Argos, Samos, and other sites emphasize her role in legitimizing unions, safeguarding the conjugal bond, and overseeing the social institution of marriage.

Limit: In myth, Hera’s protection is largely reserved for legitimate marriages and children; she often acts against illegitimate offspring and extramarital relationships. Her patronage does not guarantee harmony: she herself experiences a troubled marriage with Zeus, marked by jealousy and conflict.

Influence over childbirth and female life stages

Through her daughter Eileithyia and through cult practices, Hera is associated with childbirth and the transitions of female life (maidenhood, marriage, motherhood). She can delay or obstruct birth (as in the myths of Alcmene and Leto) or grant protection and successful delivery.

Limit: Hera’s influence can be ambivalent: she may hinder childbirth out of jealousy or anger, and her protection is not universal. Her power is often mediated through Eileithyia and other divine agents.

Divine anger, vengeance, and punitive power

Hera’s cholos (anger) and eris (strife) are central to her mythic identity. She punishes Zeus’s lovers (Io, Semele, Leto, Callisto) and their children (Heracles, Dionysus) through persecution, madness, monstrous adversaries, and prolonged suffering. In the Iliad, her anger against Troy and its royal house drives her support for the Achaeans and shapes the divine dimension of the war.

Limit: Hera’s vengeance is powerful but not absolute: Zeus can restrain her, other gods can intervene, and human heroes can ultimately achieve apotheosis or redemption despite her hostility (e.g., Heracles).

Deception, persuasion, and erotic manipulation

Hera is capable of sophisticated deception and erotic manipulation, most famously in Iliad 14, where she seduces Zeus with the help of Aphrodite’s girdle to distract him and influence the course of the battle. She also plots with Athena and other gods to achieve her aims.

Limit: Her success depends on Zeus’s susceptibility and the cooperation of other gods. Deception carries risks: if discovered, Hera faces Zeus’s anger and potential punishment.

Protection and patronage of specific cities and regions

Hera serves as a patron goddess of Argos, Samos, and Olympia, among other places. Her temples (Heraia) and festivals (Heraia games) mark her as a civic and regional protector, linked to political identity, communal cohesion, and ritual prestige.

Limit: Her patronage is localized and can vary in emphasis (marriage, kingship, maritime power) depending on the region. She does not uniformly protect all cities; in myth she actively opposes Troy.

Possible role in monstrous births and cosmic opposition

Some hymnic and mythographic traditions attribute to Hera a role in the birth of Typhon/Typhaon, a monstrous adversary of Zeus, as a kind of counter-creation or revenge for Zeus’s solo birth of Athena. This positions Hera as a participant in cosmic opposition and the testing of Zeus’s sovereignty.

Limit: These accounts are not uniform and often conflict with other genealogies of Typhon. Hera’s role is sometimes minimized or omitted.

Accounts I
Hera in the Iliad: divine politics and the Trojan WarHomer, Iliad

Archaic (c. 8th century BCE)

In the Iliad, Hera is a central divine actor. She supports the Achaeans against Troy, motivated by longstanding hatred of the city and its royal house. She argues with Zeus, attempts to sway his decisions, and collaborates with Athena to aid the Greek warriors. In Book 14, she seduces Zeus with Aphrodite’s girdle to distract him, allowing Poseidon to assist the Achaeans. Hera’s anger, strategic plotting, and marital conflict with Zeus shape the divine dimension of the war and underscore the fragility of cosmic order. Hera’s actions contribute to the ebb and flow of battle, the suffering of Troy, and the eventual destruction of the city. Her conflict with Zeus reveals tensions within the divine hierarchy and the limits of her power.

The Judgment of Paris and Hera’s lost prizeMythic tradition preserved in later sources (e.g., Apollodorus, Pseudo-Hyginus, scholia)

Archaic core, but primarily preserved in later mythographic texts

In the myth of the Judgment of Paris, Hera, Athena, and Aphrodite compete for the title of ‘most beautiful’ and the golden apple. Paris awards the prize to Aphrodite, who promises him Helen, thereby slighting Hera and Athena. Hera’s anger at this judgment becomes one of the mythic explanations for her hostility toward Troy and its royal house. Hera’s resentment contributes to divine support for the Achaeans and the eventual destruction of Troy. The story also illustrates competition among goddesses and the dangerous consequences of divine beauty contests.

Hera’s marriage to Zeus and the divine conspiracyHomeric and later mythic traditions; scholia; Apollodorus

Archaic core with later elaborations

Hera is both sister and wife of Zeus. Their marriage symbolizes the highest form of divine union and the foundation of the Olympian order. Some traditions recount a conspiracy by Hera and other gods (Poseidon, Athena) to bind Zeus and challenge his rule. Zeus is eventually freed, punishes the conspirators, and reasserts his sovereignty. Hera’s role in this conspiracy underscores her ambition and willingness to contest Zeus’s authority. The failed conspiracy reinforces Zeus’s supremacy and results in Hera’s humiliation or temporary suspension of her powers. It also reveals the potential instability of divine rule.

Hera and the birth of HephaestusHesiod, Theogony; Apollodorus; later mythographic traditions

Archaic and Hellenistic

Hephaestus’s birth is a locus of contradiction. In some accounts, Hera bears Hephaestus alone, without Zeus, in retaliation for Zeus’s solo birth of Athena. In other versions, Hephaestus is the son of both Zeus and Hera. Hera later rejects Hephaestus because of his lameness, casting him from Olympus. He survives, becomes a master craftsman, and eventually returns to Olympus, sometimes forcing Hera to accept him through his skill and cunning. The story highlights themes of divine reproduction, gendered rivalry, and the marginalization of disabled offspring. Hephaestus’s rejection and return underscore the complex dynamics of maternal power and divine craftsmanship.

Accounts II
Hera and Heracles: persecution and apotheosisApollodorus 2.4.6–12; Pindar, Nemean 1; Pausanias; various tragedies and later literature

Archaic to Classical and later

Heracles, the son of Zeus and the mortal woman Alcmene, becomes the target of Hera’s relentless hostility. Hera delays his birth by manipulating Eileithyia, sends serpents to kill him in his cradle, and later drives him mad, causing him to kill his own children. Many of Heracles’ labors involve monsters and challenges associated with Hera’s anger (e.g., the Hydra, the Nemean Lion). Despite her persecution, Heracles ultimately achieves apotheosis and is reconciled with Hera, sometimes depicted as marrying her daughter Hebe in Olympus. Hera’s persecution shapes Heracles’ heroic identity and suffering. The eventual reconciliation suggests a complex resolution of divine anger and heroic endurance.

Hera and Io: transformation and wanderingApollodorus 2.1.3; Aeschylus, Prometheus Bound; later mythographic sources

Archaic core with Classical elaborations

Io, a priestess of Hera and lover of Zeus, is transformed into a cow to conceal the affair. Hera, suspecting the truth, demands the cow as a gift and sets the many-eyed Argus to guard her. Zeus sends Hermes to kill Argus and free Io, but Hera then sends a gadfly to torment Io, driving her into long wanderings across the world. Io’s suffering and eventual restoration are linked to the genealogy of future heroes and peoples. Io’s transformation and wandering exemplify Hera’s punitive power toward Zeus’s lovers. The story also connects Greek myth to broader geographic and genealogical narratives.

Hera and Semele: destruction and birth of DionysusApollodorus 3.4.4; later mythographic and literary sources

Classical and Hellenistic

Semele, a mortal lover of Zeus, becomes pregnant with Dionysus. Hera, jealous, disguises herself and persuades Semele to demand that Zeus appear in his full divine glory. When Zeus does so, Semele is destroyed by his thunder and lightning, but the unborn Dionysus is rescued and sewn into Zeus’s thigh. Hera later continues to oppose Dionysus and his cult. Hera’s manipulation leads to Semele’s death and the unusual birth of Dionysus, a god associated with ecstasy and transgression. The story underscores Hera’s role in policing divine-mortal boundaries and punishing illicit relationships.

Hera and Leto: obstruction of childbirthHomeric Hymn to Apollo; Apollodorus 1.4.2; later mythographic sources

Archaic and later

Leto, pregnant by Zeus with Apollo and Artemis, is persecuted by Hera, who forbids any land to receive her for childbirth and delays Eileithyia’s assistance. Eventually, the floating island of Delos accepts Leto, and the twins are born. Hera’s obstruction dramatizes the vulnerability of divine pregnancy and the power of social and cosmic exclusion. The story explains the special status of Delos and the birth of Apollo and Artemis. Hera’s hostility reinforces her role as guardian of legitimate marriage and opponent of Zeus’s extramarital affairs.
