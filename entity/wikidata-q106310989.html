The Approach

In the celestial courts of Chinese myth, three sisters guard the very gate through which every soul enters the world. Qiongxiao, the middle of the Three Celestial Maidens, once wielded a golden gourd that could capture immortals and a pair of scissors that could sever a dragon's spine—yet her greatest legacy is the quiet, unyielding power of birth itself, a power so profound that even gods must bow before it.

Identity

Qiongxiao Niangniang

Pronunciation Chiung-hsiao Niang-niang (approximate Mandarin pronunciation: [tɕʰjʊ́ŋ ɕjáu njaŋ njaŋ])

Known As

Zhao Qiongxiao瓊宵娘娘QiongxiaoLady of the Jade Firmament

Etymology

The name Qiongxiao (瓊宵) combines 'qiong' (瓊), meaning 'jade' or 'precious', and 'xiao' (宵), meaning 'night' or 'firmament'. The title Niangniang (娘娘) is an honorific for goddesses, often translated as 'Lady' or 'Madam'. The full title can be rendered as 'Lady of the Jade Firmament'.

Cultural Ownership

Chinese folk religion, Daoism, and Chinese mythology. The character originates in the Ming dynasty novel Investiture of the Gods (Fengshen Yanyi) and is venerated primarily in mainland China, Taiwan, Singapore, and Malaysia.

Disputed Identifications

Some sources may conflate Qiongxiao with her sisters Yunxiao and Bixiao, or with other fertility goddesses such as Zhusheng Niangniang and Songzi Niangniang. In folk practice, the Three Celestial Maidens are often merged with these childbirth deities, leading to ambiguity in individual identities.

Description

Appearance

The provided source does not give a detailed physical description of Qiongxiao. In traditional iconography, she is often depicted as a beautiful woman in celestial robes, sometimes with a swan as her mount. In the novel, she is described as agile and martial. Specific visual details vary by temple and artist.

Symbols & Iconography

Key symbols include the Hunyuan Gold Gourd (混元金斗), a gourd that can capture and strip immortals of their cultivation; the Golden Dragon Scissors (金蛟剪), a pair of scissors that can be transformed into dragons to attack enemies; and the swan, her divine mount. In folk worship, the gourd also symbolizes childbirth, as it is believed all beings pass through it before birth.

Behaviour & Personality

In the novel, Qiongxiao is portrayed as emotionally expressive and loyal to her brother. She is the first to urge revenge, weeps openly at her brother's death, and nearly faints from grief. She is also depicted as a capable warrior, more agile than her sisters. In folk religion, she is a benevolent goddess who protects women and children.

Domains & Habitats

Qiongxiao resides on Sanxian Island (三仙岛) with her sisters in the celestial realm. In local folklore, she is associated with Qiongxiao Mountain on Mount Taibai in Shaanxi, where she is said to have been transformed after her defeat.

Version Differences

The novel presents Qiongxiao as a Jie Sect disciple who fights against the Zhou dynasty. Later folk religion emphasizes her role as a fertility goddess. The novel's violent death is not emphasized in worship contexts, where she is seen as a compassionate deity. Some later versions of the story alter the manner of her death, but the core narrative remains consistent.

Stories & Myths

The Vengeance of the Three Sisters

Source Investiture of the GodsCulture ChinesePeriod Ming dynasty (16th century)

Summary

After their brother Zhao Gongming is killed by Lu Ya Zhenren, Qiongxiao is the first to urge her sister Yunxiao to avenge him. When his body is returned, she weeps and nearly faints. The sisters then join the Shang forces and set up the Grand Nine-Curves Yellow River Formation to capture Chan Sect immortals.

Consequences

The formation captures several immortals and strips the Twelve Golden Immortals of much of their cultivation. Yuanshi Tianzun and Laozi intervene, dismantle the formation, and kill the sisters. After the Zhou victory, Jiang Ziya appoints them as the 'Ganying Suishi Xiangu' (感应随世仙姑), setting the stage for their later worship.

Variants

Some later versions state that Qiongxiao's skull was shattered by the blow that killed her. Local folklore in Shaanxi says the sisters were transformed into three peaks on Mount Taibai.

The Transformation into Mountains

Source Local folklore in ShaanxiCulture Chinese regionalPeriod Unknown

Summary

Local tradition holds that after the sisters were defeated in the Battle of the Nine-Curves Yellow River Formation, they were transformed into three peaks on Mount Taibai: Qiongxiao Mountain, Yunxiao Mountain, and Bixiao Mountain. The peaks face the main summit, Baxiantai, in a bowing posture, symbolizing their submission.
