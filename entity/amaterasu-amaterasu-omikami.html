Who It Is

Amaterasu is the supreme sun goddess of the Shinto pantheon, the most revered kami in Japanese religion, and the mythical ancestor of the Imperial House of Japan. She is the daughter of Izanagi, born from his left eye when he washed his face after returning from the underworld. She rules over Takamagahara (the High Plain of Heaven) and is the central figure in the Japanese creation myth. Her most famous story is her withdrawal into the Heavenly Rock Cave (Ama-no-Iwato) after a violent argument with her brother Susanoo-no-Mikoto. Her withdrawal plunged the world into darkness and chaos. The other kami lured her out with a mirror, sacred jewels, and the uproarious laughter of the goddess Ame-no-Uzume, who danced wildly. When Amaterasu peeked out to see what was happening, she saw her own reflection in the mirror (which she had never seen before) and was drawn forth, restoring light to the world. The sun goddess is enshrined at the Grand Shrine of Ise, the holiest site in Shinto, and her sacred mirror (Yata-no-Kagami) is one of the three Imperial Regalia of Japan. The Emperor of Japan is traditionally considered her direct descendant, establishing the divine legitimacy of the imperial line.

Origins

Amaterasu was born from Izanagi's left eye during his purification after visiting the underworld. She was given dominion over the heavens. Her brother Susanoo was given the sea but refused his domain, causing chaos on both earth and heaven, leading to the rock cave incident.

The Approach

From the cave of heaven to the imperial throne of Japan, Amaterasu-Ōmikami, the sun goddess, is not merely a deity of light but the radiant heart of Shinto, whose withdrawal plunged the world into darkness and whose return restored order—a myth that has shaped an entire nation's identity for over a millennium.

Content advisory. None of the traditional myths contain explicit violence or sexual content, but modern nationalist appropriations may be politically charged; handle with historical context.

Etymology

The name 'Amaterasu' is commonly interpreted as 'shining in heaven' (from ama 'heaven' + terasu 'to shine'), but alternative etymologies include 'shining from the sky' or 'heavenly shining one'. The suffix '-Ōmikami' means 'great august deity'.

Cultural Ownership

Japan; central deity of Shinto, ancestral goddess of the Imperial family.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars have linked Amaterasu with earlier solar deities or with the sun goddess of other cultures, but these are speculative. Within Japanese mythology, she is distinct from other kami; no major disputes about her identity.

Description

Appearance

Amaterasu is rarely described physically in the myths. She is associated with the sun and light; in art she is often depicted as a beautiful woman, sometimes emerging from the cave, radiating light. She may be shown with a mirror (Yata no Kagami) or jewel (Yasakani no Magatama). In imperial regalia, she is symbolized by the mirror.

Symbols & Iconography

Sun disk, mirror (Yata no Kagami), jewel (Yasakani no Magatama), and the sacred sword (Kusanagi no Tsurugi) are associated with her. The mirror is enshrined at Ise as her sacred symbol. She is often depicted with a radiant halo or as a sun.

Behaviour & Personality

In the myths, Amaterasu is portrayed as wise, benevolent, and sometimes reclusive. She is deeply hurt by her brother Susanoo's destructive behavior and retreats into a cave, causing cosmic darkness. She is also decisive, as when she uses a mirror to lure herself out. She takes a central role in the divine council and establishes the imperial lineage by sending her grandson Ninigi to earth.

Domains & Habitats

Amaterasu rules the Takamagahara (High Plain of Heaven). She is the goddess of the sun and light, and by extension, of the imperial family. Her primary earthly shrine is the Grand Shrine of Ise (Naikū), where she is enshrined as the supreme kami.

Version Differences

The Kojiki and Nihon Shoki differ in details: the Nihon Shoki contains multiple variant accounts of the cave hiding and the circumstances of Susanoo's banishment. In some variants, Amaterasu's role in the imperial descent is more pronounced. The relationship with Susanoo also varies: in one Nihon Shoki variant, Susanoo weeps and causes disasters; in another, he is less antagonistic. The exact number of deities involved and the sequence of events can differ.

Stories & Myths

The Cave Hiding (Ama-no-Iwato)

Source Kojiki, Nihon ShokiCulture JapanesePeriod Age of the Gods (mythical)

Summary

After Susanoo's violent rampage, including flaying a horse and throwing it into Amaterasu's weaving hall, she retreats into a cave (Ama-no-Iwato) in grief. The world falls into darkness and chaos. The other kami devise a plan to lure her out: they gather a cockerel, make a mirror, and have the goddess Ame-no-Uzume perform a lewd dance on an overturned tub. The kami laugh at the spectacle, and curious, Amaterasu peeks out. She sees her own reflection in the mirror and is dazzled; the kami pull her out and seal the cave, restoring light.

Consequences

Light returns to the world; Susanoo is punished (fined and banished). The mirror becomes a sacred object and is later enshrined at Ise.

Variants

In the Nihon Shoki, there are multiple versions: in one, the cave is closed with a rock; in another, the dance is more explicitly sexual; in a third, Amaterasu is tricked with a jewel rather than a mirror. The exact sequence of events and the number of kami involved vary.

The Susanoo Conflict

Source Kojiki, Nihon ShokiCulture JapanesePeriod Age of the Gods

Summary

Susanoo, the storm god, asks to visit his mother in the underworld. Before leaving, he goes to Takamagahara to see Amaterasu. She suspects his intentions and challenges him to a test of divine offspring. They chew and spit out objects, and from the resulting children, they judge who is truthful. Susanoo is proven to have a pure heart, but then he goes on a rampage, destroying rice fields, defecating in the palace, and flaying a horse. Amaterasu hides in the cave.

Participants

Amaterasu

Susanoo

other kami

Consequences

Susanoo is banished from heaven and descends to Izumo, where he later slays the serpent Yamata-no-Orochi.

Variants

The Nihon Shoki includes variants where Susanoo's actions are less severe, and where the conflict is resolved more peacefully. In one version, he is not banished but leaves voluntarily.

The Descent of Ninigi

Source Kojiki, Nihon ShokiCulture JapanesePeriod Age of the Gods

Summary

Amaterasu decides to send her grandson Ninigi to rule the earthly realm (Ashihara no Nakatsukuni). She gives him the three sacred treasures: the mirror, the jewel, and the sword. She tells him that the mirror is her spirit and to worship it as herself. Ninigi descends to Mount Takachiho in Kyushu and establishes the imperial line.

Consequences

The imperial family of Japan claims direct descent from Amaterasu, legitimizing their rule. The regalia become symbols of imperial authority.

Variants

The Nihon Shoki includes a variant where Ninigi is accompanied by a different set of deities, and where the earthly rulers are pacified differently.

Dark History
Content advisory. The myth itself is benign, but the use of Amaterasu in Japanese nationalism and militarism during the early 20th century may be sensitive.

Nationalist appropriation

Source Historical analyses of State Shinto

Description

During the Meiji era and through World War II, Amaterasu was used to justify imperial expansion and the divinity of the emperor, leading to state-sponsored Shinto and the coercion of subjects to worship the emperor as divine.

Scholarly Context

Scholars such as Helen Hardacre and John Breen have documented how the state manipulated Shinto and Amaterasu's symbolism for political ends.

Gender dynamics

Source Feminist analyses of Shinto

Description

Amaterasu is a female deity, but in later interpretations, the imperial line is male-dominated. Some feminist scholars note that the prominence of a sun goddess did not translate into women's equality in Japanese society.

Scholarly Context

Scholars like Noriko Kawahashi and others have discussed the paradoxical position of Amaterasu.
