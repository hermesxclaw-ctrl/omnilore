Who It Is

Before there was a universe, there was a word for its edges — and that word was a goddess. Her name was Aditi, and it meant 'no edge at all.' In the oldest hymns of the Rigveda, she is the infinite expanse that contains all things and is contained by nothing — the cosmic loophole in the fabric of reality. Imagine the universe as a great rope, knotted and bound by the laws of cause and effect. Aditi is the one who whispers: 'Untie it.' She is the mother of the gods who keep the cosmos orderly, and the force that reminds them — and us — that order is not the same as limitation.

As a cosmic principle, Aditi is not described as having human-like behavior. She is the supportive, nourishing ground of all existence — the infinite matrix from which gods and creation emerge. In later Puranic narratives, she is a devoted mother who intercedes on behalf of her sons, the Adityas, and who undergoes ascetic penance to gain boons.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Sanskrit अदिति (aditi), literally meaning 'unbound', 'boundless', 'free', or 'without limits'. The prefix 'a-' negates 'diti' (bondage, binding, or limitation).

Pronunciation — AH-dih-tee (Sanskrit: ɐd̪ɪt̪ɪ)

Aliases — The Boundless, The Infinite, Mother of the Gods, Aditi the Undivided

Ownership — Hinduism (Vedic tradition); appears in the Rigveda (c. 1500–1200 BCE), the oldest of the four Vedas, and later in the Puranas.

Infinitude and Boundlessness

Aditi embodies the infinite, unbounded expanse of existence. She is the cosmic matrix that contains all things and yet is not limited by them. She represents the principle that reality is not closed or finite but open and limitless.

Limit: As an abstract principle, she does not act directly in most narratives but rather provides the condition for all other divine action.

Motherhood of the Gods

Aditi is the mother of the Adityas, a group of eight (or twelve) gods who include Varuna (cosmic order), Mitra (friendship/contracts), Aryaman (hospitality), Bhaga (prosperity), Daksa (ritual skill), Amsa (share/portion), and the sun god Savitr or Surya. She is thus the mother of the principal deities who uphold cosmic and social order.

Limit: The number and identity of the Adityas varies across texts.

Grantor of Freedom

Aditi is invoked in hymns to release worshippers from bondage — both physical (ropes, fetters, imprisonment) and spiritual (sin, guilt, the cycle of rebirth). She is the goddess who 'looses the bonds'.

Limit: This role is primarily soteriological (concerned with liberation) rather than martial or political.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In Vedic texts, Aditi has no fixed physical form — she is described as formless, boundless space or the infinite expanse of the sky and heavens. Later Puranic texts personify her as a maternal goddess, sometimes depicted as a beautiful woman seated on a lotus or throne, often with multiple arms holding symbolic objects. Some artistic traditions depict her as a cow, symbolizing nourishing abundance.

Symbols — The boundless sky; the cow (nourishing abundance); the number eight (she is said to have eight sons — the eight Adityas — who represent the eight directions or forms of the sun); the concept of freedom from binding (moksha's precursor).

Domains — Infinitude, boundlessness, cosmic order (rita), motherhood of the gods, the sky/heavens, freedom from constraint, the past and the future, the primordial matrix of creation.

Accounts I
The Birth of the AdityasRigveda 10.72; later Puranic retellings

c. 1500–1200 BCE (Rigveda); Puranic versions c. 400–1000 CE

In the Rigveda's enigmatic cosmogonic hymn (10.72), Aditi is said to have prepared a sacrificial portion and given birth to the Adityas. The hymn is cryptic: 'Aditi was born as your daughter, O Daksha' — suggesting a circular, paradoxical genealogy. In later Puranic versions, Aditi marries the sage Kashyapa and bears the twelve Adityas, who become the guardians of the twelve months and the forms of the sun. One of her sons is Indra (in some lists), and Vamana, the dwarf avatar of Vishnu, is also counted among her sons. The Adityas become the principal upholders of cosmic order (rita), with Varuna as the sovereign judge of moral law and Mitra as the guardian of contracts and friendship. Aditi's motherhood establishes her as the 'Mother of the Gods'.

The Liberation of the BoundRigveda 1.89, 1.113, 8.25

c. 1500–1200 BCE

In several hymns, Aditi is invoked as the goddess who releases worshippers from fetters — both literal and metaphorical. The Rigveda 1.89 famously asks: 'May Aditi free us from all sin, from all guilt, from all bondage.' She is called upon alongside Varuna, who is the judge who binds sinners, suggesting that Aditi is the compassionate counterbalance to Varuna's strict justice. Aditi becomes established as a goddess of mercy and liberation, complementary to Varuna's role as enforcer of cosmic law.

Accounts II
The Birth of Vamana (Dwarf Avatar)Bhagavata Purana, Vishnu Purana

c. 400–1000 CE

When the demon king Bali conquered the heavens, the gods appealed to Vishnu for help. Vishnu was born as Vamana, a dwarf, to Aditi and Kashyapa. Vamana approached Bali and asked for three paces of land. When Bali granted it, Vamana grew to cosmic proportions and covered the earth, the heavens, and finally Bali himself in three strides, restoring cosmic order. Aditi becomes the mother of Vishnu in his Vamana avatar, cementing her status as a supremely important divine mother. The Vamana story is celebrated in the Onam festival in Kerala.

Aditi's PenanceVishnu Purana, Bhagavata Purana

c. 400–1000 CE

In a later Puranic narrative, Aditi undergoes severe ascetic penance (tapas) to please Vishnu and gain his help in restoring the gods' power, which had been diminished by the demon king Bali. Impressed by her devotion, Vishnu agrees to be born as her son Vamana. Aditi's penance establishes her as a model of devoted motherhood and ascetic power in later Hindu tradition.

Aditi is a 'Hindu goddess' in the same sense as Durga or Lakshmi — Aditi in the Rigveda is primarily an abstract cosmic principle of boundlessness, not a personified goddess with a mythology. Her personification as a mother goddess is a later development.

Aditi is the 'Earth goddess' — Aditi is more closely associated with the sky, heavens, and infinite space than with the earth. The earth goddess is Prithvi.

Aditi is the mother of all gods — Aditi is the mother of the Adityas specifically, a defined group of deities. She is not the mother of all gods in the Hindu pantheon (e.g., Shiva and Agni have different origins).
