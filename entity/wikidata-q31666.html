Form

In Vedic texts, Aditi has no fixed physical form — she is described as formless, boundless space or the infinite expanse of the sky and heavens. Later Puranic texts personify her as a maternal goddess, sometimes depicted as a beautiful woman seated on a lotus or throne, often with multiple arms holding symbolic objects. Some artistic traditions depict her as a cow, symbolizing nourishing abundance.

Powers

Infinitude and Boundlessness Aditi embodies the infinite, unbounded expanse of existence. She is the cosmic matrix that contains all things and yet is not limited by them. She represents the principle that reality is not closed or finite but open and limitless. Rigveda (all periods) As an abstract principle, she does not act directly in most narratives but rather provides the condition for all other divine action. Some hymns treat her as a specific goddess with personality, while others treat her as a philosophical concept.

Motherhood of the Gods Aditi is the mother of the Adityas, a group of eight (or twelve) gods who include Varuna (cosmic order), Mitra (friendship/contracts), Aryaman (hospitality), Bhaga (prosperity), Daksa (ritual skill), Amsa (share/portion), and the sun god Savitr or Surya. She is thus the mother of the principal deities who uphold cosmic and social order. Rigveda and later Puranic texts The number and identity of the Adityas varies across texts. Rigveda 10.72 describes a different origin for the Adityas — born from a surrogate, the goddess Aditi is said to have cooked a portion of the cosmic sacrifice to give birth to them, suggesting a more complex ritual birth story.

Grantor of Freedom Aditi is invoked in hymns to release worshippers from bondage — both physical (ropes, fetters, imprisonment) and spiritual (sin, guilt, the cycle of rebirth). She is the goddess who 'looses the bonds'. Rigveda (especially hymns to Varuna where Aditi is called upon as a liberator) This role is primarily soteriological (concerned with liberation) rather than martial or political. None noted.

Behavior & Habitat

As a cosmic principle, Aditi is not described as having human-like behavior. She is the supportive, nourishing ground of all existence — the infinite matrix from which gods and creation emerge. In later Puranic narratives, she is a devoted mother who intercedes on behalf of her sons, the Adityas, and who undergoes ascetic penance to gain boons.

Accounts

In the Rigveda's enigmatic cosmogonic hymn (10.72), Aditi is said to have prepared a sacrificial portion and given birth to the Adityas. The hymn is cryptic: 'Aditi was born as your daughter, O Daksha' — suggesting a circular, paradoxical genealogy. In later Puranic versions, Aditi marries the sage Kashyapa and bears the twelve Adityas, who become the guardians of the twelve months and the forms of the sun. One of her sons is Indra (in some lists), and Vamana, the dwarf avatar of Vishnu, is also counted among her sons.

In several hymns, Aditi is invoked as the goddess who releases worshippers from fetters — both literal and metaphorical. The Rigveda 1.89 famously asks: 'May Aditi free us from all sin, from all guilt, from all bondage.' She is called upon alongside Varuna, who is the judge who binds sinners, suggesting that Aditi is the compassionate counterbalance to Varuna's strict justice.

When the demon king Bali conquered the heavens, the gods appealed to Vishnu for help. Vishnu was born as Vamana, a dwarf, to Aditi and Kashyapa. Vamana approached Bali and asked for three paces of land. When Bali granted it, Vamana grew to cosmic proportions and covered the earth, the heavens, and finally Bali himself in three strides, restoring cosmic order.

In a later Puranic narrative, Aditi undergoes severe ascetic penance (tapas) to please Vishnu and gain his help in restoring the gods' power, which had been diminished by the demon king Bali. Impressed by her devotion, Vishnu agrees to be born as her son Vamana.
