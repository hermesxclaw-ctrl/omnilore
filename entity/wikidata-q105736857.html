The Approach

The name Jūbei echoes through Japanese history and pop culture, from a legendary swordsman shrouded in mystery to a cat-eared hero in a futuristic fighting game. Who is the real Jūbei, and how did his name become a symbol of the master swordsman?

Content advisory. None
Identity

Jūbei

Pronunciation JOO-bay

Known As

JuubeiJubeiYagyu Jubei

Etymology

Japanese given name; the kanji 十兵衛 (Jūbei) combines 'ten' (十) and 'soldier/guard' (兵衛). Historically a common name for samurai, especially in the Yagyū clan.

Cultural Ownership

Japanese; primarily associated with the samurai class and Japanese popular culture.

Disputed Identifications

The name is shared by several historical and fictional figures; the most famous is Yagyū Jūbei Mitsuyoshi, but the name is also used for various characters in anime, manga, and video games. Some fictional characters are loosely based on the historical figure, while others are entirely original.

Description

Appearance

Varies by version. The historical Yagyū Jūbei is often depicted as a samurai with an eyepatch, though this is disputed. Fictional versions range from a ninja (Jubei Kibagami) to a cat-like swordsman (BlazBlue).

Symbols & Iconography

Often associated with swordsmanship, the Yagyū clan, and sometimes an eyepatch.

Behaviour & Personality

Varies; historical figure is known as a skilled swordsman. Fictional versions often portray him as a master swordsman, sometimes with a laid-back or stoic personality.

Domains & Habitats

Feudal Japan in historical and many fictional settings; other settings may vary.

Version Differences

Significant differences between historical and fictional portrayals. The historical Jūbei's life is not well-documented, leading to fictional embellishments such as the eyepatch and superhuman abilities.

Stories & Myths

Historical Yagyū Jūbei Mitsuyoshi

Source Yagyū clan records, later biographiesCulture JapanesePeriod Edo period (1607–1650)

Summary

Yagyū Jūbei Mitsuyoshi was the son of Yagyū Munenori, the shogun's sword instructor. He served the Tokugawa shogunate. Little is known about his life; many stories about him are likely fictional.

Consequences

His legacy as a master swordsman inspired many fictional works.

Variants

The famous story of his losing an eye is disputed; some claim he lost it in a duel, others that it was a self-inflicted wound during training.
