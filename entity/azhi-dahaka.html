Who It Is

Azhi Dahaka (also known as Zahhak in the Shahnameh) is a monstrous, three-headed dragon-tyrant from Persian mythology, one of the most significant and terrifying figures in the Zoroastrian and Iranian mythological tradition. In the Avesta (the oldest Zoroastrian scripture), Azhi Dahaka is described as a three-headed, three-mouthed, six-eyed serpent-dragon with a thousand senses, created by the evil spirit Ahriman (Angra Mainyu) to destroy the world. He is the embodiment of tyranny, evil, and lies. In Ferdowsi's later Shahnameh, Azhi Dahaka is transformed into a human-tyrant named Zahhak (Zahhāk), who is seduced by Ahriman, becomes a serpent-shouldered king, and rules Iran for a thousand years with cruelty and terror. According to Shahnameh: Zahhak was a prince of Babylon who was tempted by Iblis (Ahriman in Islamic form). Iblis kissed Zahhak's shoulders, and from each shoulder grew a black serpent that could only be fed with the brains of young men. Daily, two young men were killed to feed the serpents. Zahhak's tyranny became unbearable until the blacksmith Kaveh (Kāveh) rebelled, raising his leather apron as a battle standard (the Derafsh Kaviani, the legendary banner of Iran). Kaveh led the people to support the rightful hero Fereydun (Faridun), who overthrew Zahhak and imprisoned him forever in a cave on Mount Damavand, chained with unbreakable bonds of lion skin. There, Zahhak's heart will continue to beat until the end of the world, when he will break free for a final battle before being destroyed by the hero Garshasp or Karshasp. Azhi Dahaka appears in the Younger Avesta as a creation of Ahriman that the fire-god Atar attempted to destroy. The Dragon King of Evil in Persian mythology, Azhi Dahaka has profoundly influenced later Iranian culture — the name 'Zahhak' remains synonymous with tyranny in Persian literature and political discourse.

The Approach

In the burning heart of Zoroastrian myth, there coils a dragon with three heads and a thousand senses, whose name alone—Azhi Dahaka—was enough to make warriors pause and priests pray. He was the storm that swallowed kings, the lie that wore a crown, and the shadow that even the gods could not ignore.

Content advisory. Depictions of violence, tyranny, and mythological conflict; no graphic or explicit content.
Identity

Azhi Dahaka

Pronunciation Avestan: /ˈaʒi daˈhaːka/; Modern Persian: /zæhˈhɒːk/

Known As

Etymology

Avestan 'Aži' (serpent/dragon) + 'Dahāka' (possibly 'man from Dahā' or 'stinging' or 'destructive'). In Middle Persian, 'Dahāg' or 'Bēvar Asp' (he of ten thousand horses). In New Persian, 'Zahhāk'.

Cultural Ownership

Persian (Zoroastrian) mythology; also present in Iranian folklore and later Islamic-era Persian epic (Shahnameh). Shared with broader Indo-Iranian dragon myths.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars connect Dahaka to the Vedic dragon Vritra (both are serpentine obstacles to cosmic order), but the equation is debated. In Manichaean texts, he appears as a demon. His historical prototype is sometimes identified with the Median king Astyages or a Babylonian tyrant, but this is speculative.

Description

Appearance

In the Avesta, Azhi Dahaka is a three-headed, three-mouthed, six-eyed dragon with a thousand senses. He is described as 'a serpent of the worst kind' and is often depicted as a monstrous reptile. In the Shahnameh, he appears as a human king with two serpent heads growing from his shoulders, which require human brains as food. Some later traditions describe him as having the form of a man with serpentine features, or as a dragon with three heads that can be human-like. Artistic depictions vary: sometimes a dragon, sometimes a man with serpents.

Symbols & Iconography

The three heads symbolize the threefold evil (bad thoughts, bad words, bad deeds) or the three classes of society corrupted. The serpents on his shoulders represent the destructive forces of Angra Mainyu. In art, he is often shown chained, representing the containment of evil. In Persian miniature painting, he appears as a crowned king with serpents, or as a dragon in combat with heroes.

Behaviour & Personality

Azhi Dahaka is the embodiment of the Lie (druj) and tyranny. He is cunning, ambitious, and ruthless, desiring to make the world full of evil. He is a usurper who kills his father and rules through terror. In the Shahnameh, he is depicted as a tyrant who devours the brains of his subjects, symbolizing the destruction of wisdom and life. He is also cowardly when confronted by true heroes, as seen when he attempts to bargain with Fereydun.

Domains & Habitats

He is associated with the land of Bawri (Babylon) in the Avesta. In the Shahnameh, he rules over Iran and Arabia. His prison is Mount Damavand, a volcanic peak in the Alborz mountains, where he is bound in chains. He is also linked to the underworld and the forces of darkness.

Version Differences

In the Avesta, he is a cosmic dragon created by Angra Mainyu, with no human origin. In the Shahnameh and later Persian tradition, he is a human prince who becomes demonic through a pact with Ahriman. The number of heads varies: Avesta says three heads, while some later texts describe him with three heads but also mention a thousand senses. The method of his binding also differs: in the Avesta, Thraetaona binds him; in the Shahnameh, Fereydun does, but after a struggle and a curse. Some traditions say he will escape at the end of time, while others say he is already dead.

Stories & Myths

Creation of Dahaka

Source Avesta (Yasht 19), BundahishnCulture ZoroastrianPeriod Ancient (pre-6th century BCE oral tradition)

Summary

Angra Mainyu, the evil spirit, created Azhi Dahaka as the greatest of his demons to destroy the world. He was designed to be the antithesis of creation, spreading chaos and evil.

Participants

Angra Mainyu

Azhi Dahaka

Consequences

Dahaka becomes the chief antagonist of the Zoroastrian cosmic order, threatening to corrupt all creation.

Variants

In the Bundahishn, he is said to have been born from the union of Angra Mainyu and a demoness, or to have been created from the sins of the world.

The Binding of Dahaka

Source Avesta (Yasht 19), ShahnamehCulture Zoroastrian / PersianPeriod Avestan period and later epic (c. 10th century CE)

Summary

The hero Thraetaona (Fereydun in Persian) fights Dahaka, who has been terrorizing the world. Thraetaona strikes him with his mace, but Dahaka's body bursts with vermin and scorpions, so Thraetaona binds him in chains and carries him to Mount Damavand, where he nails him to the rock.

Participants

Thraetaona/Fereydun

Azhi Dahaka

Ahriman

Consequences

Dahaka is imprisoned, but his presence continues to cause earthquakes and eruptions. He will remain until the end of time.

Variants

In the Shahnameh, Fereydun captures Zahhak after a long war, but spares his life because Zahhak's mother was a good woman. He binds him in a cave on Damavand. In some versions, Zahhak is also cursed by Fereydun to be devoured by his own serpents.

The Tyranny of Zahhak

Source ShahnamehCulture Persian (Islamic-era epic)Period c. 1010 CE

Summary

Zahhak, a prince, is seduced by Ahriman, who appears as a cook. After Zahhak kills his father, Ahriman kisses his shoulders, and two serpents grow there. They demand human brains, so Zahhak orders his subjects to be killed daily. His tyranny lasts a thousand years until the blacksmith Kaveh revolts, using his leather apron as a banner. Fereydun leads an army, captures Zahhak, and imprisons him.

Participants

Zahhak

Ahriman

Kaveh

Fereydun

the people of Iran

Consequences

The overthrow of Zahhak restores righteousness. The story explains the origin of the Iranian national flag (the Derafsh Kaviani).

Variants

In some versions, Zahhak is not human but a demon. In others, he is a descendant of the evil king Jamshid. The number of years of his rule varies (some say 1000, others 500).

The Final Battle

Source Bundahishn, Yasht 19Culture Zoroastrian eschatologyPeriod Sasanian era (3rd-7th century CE) texts

Summary

At the end of time, Dahaka will break free from his chains and devastate the world. The hero Keresaspa (Garshasp) will rise from the dead to slay him, aided by the divine being Sraosha. His death will mark the final triumph of good over evil.

Participants

Keresaspa

Azhi Dahaka

Sraosha

Consequences

The destruction of Dahaka allows the final renovation and the resurrection of the dead.

Variants

In some texts, it is not Keresaspa but another hero who kills him. The exact details of the battle are vague.

Dark History
Content advisory. Depictions of violence, tyranny, human sacrifice, and cannibalism (feeding brains to serpents).

Human sacrifice and cannibalism

Source Shahnameh

Description

In the Shahnameh, Zahhak requires two young men's brains to be fed to the serpents on his shoulders every day. This is a form of human sacrifice and cannibalism, as the brains are consumed by the serpents.

Scholarly Context

Scholars interpret this as a metaphor for the destructive nature of tyranny, which consumes the people's lives and intellect. It may also reflect ancient practices of sacrifice to appease demons.

Usurpation and patricide

Source Shahnameh

Description

Zahhak kills his father to gain the throne, and later kills Jamshid, the rightful king.

Scholarly Context

Patricide is a common motif in myths to symbolize the overthrow of order by chaos.

Tyranny and oppression

Source Shahnameh

Description

Zahhak's rule is marked by fear, murder, and oppression of the people.

Scholarly Context

He represents the archetypal tyrant in Iranian mythology, used to justify kingship and the fight against evil.

Contradictions & Disputes

Human vs. dragon form

Position A

Position A — evidence

Scholarly Consensus

Scholars view the Shahnameh as a later anthropomorphization of the myth, blending human and dragon traits. The two forms are not mutually exclusive in the mythological evolution.

Origin and creation

Position A

Dahaka was created directly by Angra Mainyu.

Zahhak was a human prince who became evil through Ahriman's temptation.

The Avestan version is older; the Shahnameh version reflects a later moralistic interpretation where evil is a choice, not a creation.

Primary Evidence

Avesta: Yasht 19 (Zamyad Yasht)

Type textDate c. 1000-600 BCE (oral tradition older)Locator Yt 19.46-50, 19.92-93Language Avestan

Translation

Describes Dahaka as a three-mouthed, three-headed, six-eyed dragon with a thousand senses, created by Angra Mainyu to destroy the world. He threatens to make the world full of evil. The hero Thraetaona (Fereydun) binds him in chains at Mount Damavand.

Avesta: Vendidad (Videvdat)

Type textDate c. 6th century BCELocator Vd 1.18, 19.5-6Language Avestan

Translation

Mentions Dahaka as a demonic being dwelling in the land of Bawri (Babylon). He is associated with the worst sins and is said to be the most powerful of the demons.

Bundahishn (Greater Iranian Book of Creation)

Type textDate 9th century CE (based on earlier tradition)Locator Ch. 29, 31Language Middle Persian (Pahlavi)

Translation

Provides a fuller biography: Dahaka was a prince who was seduced by Ahriman, killed his father, and ruled for a thousand years. He was defeated by Fereydun and imprisoned in Mount Damavand, where he will remain until the end of time, when he will break free and be slain by the hero Keresaspa (Garshasp).

Denkard (Acts of the Religion)

Type textDate 9th-10th century CELocator Book 7, 9Language Middle Persian (Pahlavi)

Translation

Discusses Dahaka as a symbol of evil and the Lie, and his eventual destruction at the final renovation.

Shahnameh (Book of Kings) by Ferdowsi

Type textDate c. 1010 CELocator The reign of Zahhak, lines 2000-5000 approx.Language New Persian

Translation

Epic retelling: Zahhak is a human prince who is tricked by Ahriman (Iblis) into killing his father. Ahriman kisses his shoulders, and two serpents grow there, which must be fed with human brains daily. Zahhak rules tyrannically for a thousand years until the blacksmith Kaveh leads a revolt, and Fereydun, with the help of the smith's apron as a banner, overthrows him. He is chained in a cave on Mount Damavand.
