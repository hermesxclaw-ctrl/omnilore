Who It Is

In the burning heart of Zoroastrian myth, there coils a dragon with three heads and a thousand senses, whose name alone—Azhi Dahaka—was enough to make warriors pause and priests pray. He was the storm that swallowed kings, the lie that wore a crown, and the shadow that even the gods could not ignore.

Azhi Dahaka is the embodiment of the Lie (druj) and tyranny. He is cunning, ambitious, and ruthless, desiring to make the world full of evil. He is a usurper who kills his father and rules through terror. In the Shahnameh, he is depicted as a tyrant who devours the brains of his subjects, symbolizing the destruction of wisdom and life. He is also cowardly when confronted by true heroes, as seen when he attempts to bargain with Fereydun.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Avestan 'Aži' (serpent/dragon) + 'Dahāka' (possibly 'man from Dahā' or 'stinging' or 'destructive'). In Middle Persian, 'Dahāg' or 'Bēvar Asp' (he of ten thousand horses). In New Persian, 'Zahhāk'.

Pronunciation — Avestan: /ˈaʒi daˈhaːka/; Modern Persian: /zæhˈhɒːk/

Aliases — Zahhak, Zohhak, Dahaka, Aži Dahāka, Azhdahā, Bēvar Asp

Ownership — Persian (Zoroastrian) mythology; also present in Iranian folklore and later Islamic-era Persian epic (Shahnameh). Shared with broader Indo-Iranian dragon myths.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In the Avesta, Azhi Dahaka is a three-headed, three-mouthed, six-eyed dragon with a thousand senses. He is described as 'a serpent of the worst kind' and is often depicted as a monstrous reptile. In the Shahnameh, he appears as a human king with two serpent heads growing from his shoulders, which require human brains as food. Some later traditions describe him as having the form of a man with serpentine features, or as a dragon with three heads t

Symbols — The three heads symbolize the threefold evil (bad thoughts, bad words, bad deeds) or the three classes of society corrupted. The serpents on his shoulders represent the destructive forces of Angra Mainyu. In art, he is often shown chained, representing the containment of evil. In Persian miniature painting, he appears as a crowned king with serpents, or as a dragon in combat with heroes.

Domains — He is associated with the land of Bawri (Babylon) in the Avesta. In the Shahnameh, he rules over Iran and Arabia. His prison is Mount Damavand, a volcanic peak in the Alborz mountains, where he is bound in chains. He is also linked to the underworld and the forces of darkness.

Accounts I
Creation of DahakaAvesta (Yasht 19), Bundahishn

Ancient (pre-6th century BCE oral tradition)

Angra Mainyu, the evil spirit, created Azhi Dahaka as the greatest of his demons to destroy the world. He was designed to be the antithesis of creation, spreading chaos and evil. Dahaka becomes the chief antagonist of the Zoroastrian cosmic order, threatening to corrupt all creation.

The Binding of DahakaAvesta (Yasht 19), Shahnameh

Avestan period and later epic (c. 10th century CE)

The hero Thraetaona (Fereydun in Persian) fights Dahaka, who has been terrorizing the world. Thraetaona strikes him with his mace, but Dahaka's body bursts with vermin and scorpions, so Thraetaona binds him in chains and carries him to Mount Damavand, where he nails him to the rock. Dahaka is imprisoned, but his presence continues to cause earthquakes and eruptions. He will remain until the end of time.

Accounts II
The Tyranny of ZahhakShahnameh

c. 1010 CE

Zahhak, a prince, is seduced by Ahriman, who appears as a cook. After Zahhak kills his father, Ahriman kisses his shoulders, and two serpents grow there. They demand human brains, so Zahhak orders his subjects to be killed daily. His tyranny lasts a thousand years until the blacksmith Kaveh revolts, using his leather apron as a banner. Fereydun leads an army, captures Zahhak, and imprisons him. The overthrow of Zahhak restores righteousness. The story explains the origin of the Iranian national flag (the Derafsh Kaviani).

The Final BattleBundahishn, Yasht 19

Sasanian era (3rd-7th century CE) texts

At the end of time, Dahaka will break free from his chains and devastate the world. The hero Keresaspa (Garshasp) will rise from the dead to slay him, aided by the divine being Sraosha. His death will mark the final triumph of good over evil. The destruction of Dahaka allows the final renovation and the resurrection of the dead.

Azhi Dahaka is a simple dragon. — He is a complex figure with a rich mythological history, ranging from a cosmic dragon to a human tyrant.

He is the same as the dragon in the story of Saint George. — There is no direct connection; they are distinct myths from different cultures.

The Shahnameh story is historically accurate. — It is a literary epic with mythological elements, not a historical account.

He is a Satan figure in Zoroastrianism. — He is a demonic being created by Angra Mainyu, but he is not the chief evil deity; he is a subordinate demon.
