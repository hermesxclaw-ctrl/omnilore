Form

As a reconstruction, *Welnos has no surviving iconography. Comparativists reconstruct the cluster by its functions rather than any attested image. Where later reflexes give any picture, it is the Slavic Veles/Volos as a shaggy, hair-covered figure (the Russian/Ukrainian word volos means 'hair', and Gieysztor notes hair/fur is regarded as binding magic in IE tradition) and the Lithuanian Velnias as a dark chthonic 'devil' with horns or hooves added by Christian lore. No ancient anthropomorphic image of the god survives; almost nothing is attested before the 10th-century Rus' oath texts.

Powers

Lordship over cattle and wealth The attested core function. The Rus' treaty oaths invoke 'Volos, the cattle god' alongside the thunderer Perun, pairing wealth and increase with celestial sovereignty. Veles is the giver of flocks, goods, and prosperity; his antonymic pairing with Perun splits the cosmos into sky-and-cattle, and his Christian successor-saint, St. Blasius/Blaise, remained the patron of herds and livestock. Attested in the Primary Chronicle oath of 971 CE and the 945 treaty; 'Volos the cattle god' is a direct in-text formula. Reconstructed extent only — the cattle function is directly attested for the Slavic reflex; for the proto-god it is inferred backward. Whether the cattle function or an underworld-psychopomp function is primary is disputed (see Contradictions on Veles origins).

Psychopomp / shepherd of the dead The reconstruction reads *Welnos as a herder not just of cattle but of souls, grazing the deceased in the green meadow that lies below the living world. The Lithuanian vėlė ('soul of the dead') and the Baltic term *welsu-/'meadow, pasture' converge here: Veles as a pastoral lord of the dead, browsing his charges on lush underworld fields. The Russian 12th-century epic The Tale of Igor's Campaign calls the wizard Boyan 'Veles's grandson', binding the god of the dead to the seer-singer who speaks across the boundary of life and death. Attested fragmentarily: vėlė/meadow linguistics plus the Igor's-Campaign 'Veles's grandson' formula; the proto-psychopomp figure is reconstructed. Direct testimony is late and partly reconstructed; the 'green meadow of the dead' is largely inferred from Baltic root semantics, not from a primary narrative. Some scholars tie the cattle god and the dead-herder together; others see two separate figures later conflated.

Magic and divination (volkhv tradition) The Slavic reflex Veles presided over magic and seer-craft; the term volkhv (Old Russian for a magus/magician/sorcerer) is widely held to derive from his name, and volkhovanyi spellcraft clusters around his cult. The wizard Boyan's genealogy in The Tale of Igor's Campaign makes the link explicit: a singer-sorcerer whose arts descend from the cattle-and-dead god. Attested etymologically (volkhv < *Veles) and in Igor's Campaign; the connection is standard but partly hypothetical. The volkhv→Veles derivation, while mainstream, is not universally proven. See the etymology dispute over whether *wel- 'see' (e.g., Old Irish filed 'seer') or *wel- 'pasture' underlies the magic association.

Chthonic opening of the earth (Baltic reflex) In Lithuanian folklore the Christianized Velnias retains an archaic power: he causes the earth to open and swallow the wicked, and he haunts cattle and boundary-crossings as a dark herdsman-devil. This powers the reconstruction of *Welnos as the chthonic counterpart of the celestial sky-thunderer — the underworld half of a sovereign pair. Attested in late Lithuanian folk tradition and early demonological records; the 'opening earth' motif is characteristic. Post-Christian distortion is heavy; the chthonic function is partly reconstructed from these demonized reflexes. The medieval 'serpent-god' identification (Ivanov & Toporov's chthonic serpent) for this function is now rejected by most modern Slavists.

Accounts

The surviving treaty oaths of Kievan Rus' have the pagan party swear simultaneously by Perun, the thunder-god, and by Volos/Veles, named explicitly as the cattle god. The dual invocation witnesses a fully formed sky+underworld pair, the two great gods of the early Slavic pantheon who shared sovereignty: Perun above in the sky (thunder, order, warriors), Veles below in the earth (wealth, cattle, the dead). The oath formula is the single clearest 'story' the reconstruction leans on — a ritual scene in which the whole cosmos is invoked to witness the treaty.

The opening of the Igor' epic names its gifted narrator-singer Boyan, styling him 'Veles's grandson' (Velesovĭ vnuk). The genealogy turns the cattle-and-dead god into the patron of the inspired poet who, in the poet's own boast, 'casts the soul over a tree to fly like a wolf across the earth', roaming between the worlds of the living and the dead to sing the deeds of princes. This is folklore's clearest narrative avatar of the *Welnos psychopomp function: the god of the dead as the source of prophecy, song, and boundary-crossing genius.

Across Slavic lands the pre-Christian cattle-and-wealth god did not vanish at baptism but was quietly folded into a Christian saint whose feast fell at the same season and whose patronage matched: Saint Blasius/Blaise (Vlaho, Sveti Blaž) became the intercessor for herds, lambs, and livestock, while Veles/Volos survived in place-names (Volosovo, Velesovo), in proverbs about cattle, and in the folk belief that a 'cattle saint' must be fed on the feast day. The scenario is the classic Christianization pattern the charter demands be documented plainly: the old god's function reassigned, his name trailing after as a memory.

Content Notes

The pre-Christian Lithuanian cattle-and-underworld god was systematically recast as the Christian devil: Velnias literally became 'the devil', stripped of his holy character and flattened into a demon. The same process refracted across the Slavic world as the old gods were condemned in clerical texts. This is a documented erasure-by-redescription of a once-venerated deity — the dark history of the reconstruction itself.

Modern scholars read the demonized Velnias as a palimpsest: the devil-trope screens the older chthonic deity. The demonization is not merely lore but a record of religious coercion.

Lithuanian folkloric and early demonological records; Christianization literature

Homilies such as the Slovo někojego christoljubca (and the general anti-pagan literature of Kievan Rus') explicitly sought to extirpate worship of Veles/Volos, describing the faithful who 'play to demons' in his honor. The god is documented chiefly through the words of his enemies, coloring every 'source'.

A caution: nearly all direct testimony to Veles is hostile or formulaic (oath, curse), so the god's reputation is partly a clerical caricature.

Slovo někojego christoljubca (11th–12th c.); Kievan anti-pagan homilies

In the reconstructed bipolar cosmos, the chthonic god is the alter-ego 'other' of the celestial thunderer — the one below, associated with death, wealth (often amassed through morally ambiguous means), and the wily trickster. Comparative analyses risk moralizing this structural opposition (sky=good, chthonic=bad), a bias the reconstruction must guard against: *Welnos was a major deity, not a dark adversary in the original system.

Scholars explicitly warn against importing Christian good/evil framing onto the pre-Christian sky/chthonic dyad.

Comparative Indo-European mythology; structural analyses of Perun/Veles

The Book of Veles (Velesova kniga), a controversial text published in the 1950s claiming to be an authentic ancient Slavic scripture, was invoked by modern Rodnoverie/slavic-native-faith movements and is treated as a founding 'sacred text' by some. Academic scholarship overwhelmingly rejects the Book of Veles as a modern fabrication or at least of unprovable authenticity, yet it has powered nationalist and neopagan identity claims built partly on the Veles/Welnos cult.

The contrast between scholarly caution and neopagan assertion is a live fault-line; the reconstruction *Welnos is sometimes weaponized for modern ethnic-national claims the academy does not endorse.

What We Don't Know

This dossier on a reconstructed deity is necessarily thin on primary evidence and thick on secondary debate. (1) Etymology: the *wel- problem is unresolved; a defensible dossier would require the specialized articles by Dynda, Gieysztor, Jakobson, and Fraenkel in full, which were not all available in this session's encyclopedia extracts. (2) Ullr: the Puhvel proposal vs. the *wul-tus 'sight' reading deserves the dedicated Ullr philology (place-name evidence, the *wulþuz > Wuldor/kingship material), only partly captured here. (3) Lithuanian Velnias: primary Lithuanian folkloric texts (and the Lithuanian-language scholarship, e.g., Greimas) were not accessed; the account relies on secondary encyclopedic summaries. (4) Modern reception: is genuinely homonym-heavy; no verified direct film/game/anime adaptation of the reconstructed deity exists, and the Book-of-Veles/Rodnovery reception is the strongest documented modern thread (worth deeper ethnography). (5) Archaeology: Volosovo-type sites and the Blasius/Veles toponym distribution were asserted from secondary summaries, not checked site-by-site. All reconstructions are flagged; date of research cutoff: 2026-08-02. Firecrawl web search was unavailable (billing exhausted), so research used the Wikipedia Direct API path per OmniLore protocol.

A way to hold it in your mind

Before the thunderer had his sky, and before the fire-god took up his forge, comparative linguists say there was a quieter sovereign below — *Welnos, the reconstructed god of wet grass, heavy herds, and the green field where the dead are grazed like cattle. No one ever carved his image or swore by his name in any surviving temple: the only altars he has are dictionaries and the faint echoes of a god who became both a holy cattle-lord on the banks of the Dnieper and, under the weight of a new religion, the devil himself in Lithuania. To meet *Welnos is to watch comparative mythology do its strangest work — taking a name that appears nowhere, and using it to explain why so many distant peoples once trusted their wealth, their wizards, and their dead to a keeper of meadows.
