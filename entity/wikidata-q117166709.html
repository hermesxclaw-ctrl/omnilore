Form

No single canonical appearance survives. In later Baltic folklore, Velnias/Velns is often a shapeshifter: a black man, a foreigner, a serpent, a black dog, or a goat. In Slavic sources, Veles is not depicted directly, but is associated with the serpent and the dragon. He may have been imagined as a giant serpent or a dragon-like being, based on his later association with the 'snake beneath the world' or the 'dragon of the deep.'

Powers

Chthonic Dominion As a god of the underworld, Welnos rules over the dead and the hidden realms beneath the earth. He is the counterpart to the sky god (Perun/Perkūnas), and the two are in constant opposition. Reconstructed; Baltic and Slavic parallels His power is largely confined to the underworld and the earth; he is not a sky god. The extent of his role as a psychopomp or ruler of the dead is debated. Some scholars see him as a mere demon, not a full god of the dead.

Master of Cattle and Wealth In Slavic sources, Veles is explicitly a god of cattle and wealth. His name is used in oaths to guarantee the fulfillment of agreements, suggesting a role as a guarantor of contracts and trade. Slavic (Veles/Volos) This role is less prominent in Baltic sources, where Velnias is more often a trickster. The connection between cattle and the underworld is a common Indo-European theme, but the exact nature of Veles's role as a wealth god is unclear.

Magic and Prophecy The root *wel- implies a connection to seeing and knowing. Welnos is thus a god of hidden knowledge, prophecy, and magical insight. In later folklore, Velnias is a source of forbidden knowledge, often granting power in exchange for a soul. Linguistic reconstruction; later Baltic folklore This power is more implied than explicitly attested in early sources. The later association with the Devil may have exaggerated his role as a granter of dark gifts.

Shapeshifting In Baltic folklore, Velnias/Velns can transform into various animals and objects. This is a common trait of trickster and chthonic deities. Baltic folklore (post-Christianization) This is a feature of later folklore, not necessarily of the original deity. It is unclear if this power was attributed to the pre-Christian god or was a later addition under Christian influence.

Shapeshifting The ability to assume various forms, including a serpent, a bear, a wolf, a bull, a man, or a foreigner. This is a common trait of chthonic trickster deities. Attested in both Baltic and Slavic folklore. Often has a tell, such as a missing shadow, a horse's hoof, or a tail, which reveals his true nature. Some versions depict him as a master shapeshifter, others as clumsy and easily tricked.

Mastery of Magic and Prophecy Welnos is a patron of seers, poets, and magicians. He possesses secret knowledge of the otherworld and the future. Inferred from his etymological link to 'seeing' and his later association with poets in Slavic tradition (e.g., the 'Tale of Igor's Campaign' calls Boyan, a legendary bard, 'Veles' grandson'). His magic is often wild and chaotic, not the ordered, runic magic of the sky god. The extent of his prophetic power is not detailed in any single source.

Lordship over the Dead As a chthonic deity, Welnos rules the underworld and guides or guards the souls of the dead. He is associated with the 'Otherworld' where the ancestors reside. Inferred from his chthonic nature and his association with cattle, which in Indo-European tradition are often psychopomps or symbols of wealth in the afterlife. His realm is not described in detail in surviving sources. Some scholars see him as a benevolent guide of souls, others as a demonic captor.

Control over Cattle and Wealth He is explicitly the god of cattle in the Slavic Primary Chronicle. Cattle were a primary form of wealth in the Proto-Indo-European world, so this extends to wealth, commerce, and the underworld's treasures. Attested in the Primary Chronicle (treaty of 971) and in Slavic folklore. His wealth is often associated with the earth and the underworld, not with human commerce. None significant.

Behavior & Habitat

Welnos is a being of the liminal and the hidden. He is a chthonic deity, associated with the underworld, the dead, and the roots of the world-tree. He is also a god of cattle and wealth, of magic and prophecy, and of the untamed wilderness. In later folklore, he becomes a trickster and a bargainer, often outwitted by humans but always dangerous.

Accounts

The thunder god Perun, who dwells in the sky, is challenged by the chthonic serpent-god Veles, who lives in the watery underworld. Veles steals Perun's cattle (or his wife, or his son), and Perun pursues him across the earth. Perun hurls thunderbolts at Veles, who flees, hiding in trees, rocks, and water. Eventually, Perun defeats Veles, and the stolen cattle (or the divine order) are restored. This myth explains the cycle of the seasons and the restoration of cosmic order.

In some Lithuanian legends, Velnias is a primordial being who helps (or is tricked by) the supreme god in the creation of the world. He dives to the bottom of the primordial sea and brings up sand or mud, from which the land is created. He often tries to keep some for himself, but is tricked, resulting in the creation of mountains, swamps, or other imperfect features of the landscape.

The anonymous author of this epic poem refers to the legendary bard Boyan as 'Veles' grandson'. This single reference demonstrates Veles's role as a patron of poets and a source of prophetic, magical song.

Scholarly Debates
Relationship with Norse Óðinn
Some scholars (e.g., Georges Dumézil) see Welnos as a cognate of the Norse Óðinn, based on shared associations with poetry, magic, and the dead, and a possible etymological link to the Proto-Germanic *Wōðanaz. ⇌ Other scholars argue that the similarities are due to parallel development from a common Indo-European 'shamanic' complex, not direct cognation. The etymological link is disputed, with *Wōðanaz being more commonly linked to 'fury' or 'inspiration' (*wōþuz), not 'seeing' (*wel-).

What We Don't Know

Direct evidence for the Proto-Indo-European deity Welnos is, by definition, non-existent. The reconstruction is based on the comparative method. The primary gap is a lack of any direct iconographic or textual evidence from the Proto-Indo-European period. The Baltic and Slavic reflexes are well-attested, but their exact relationship and the full extent of their shared features are debated. The potential connection to other Indo-European deities (e.g., Óðinn, Hermes) is a fruitful but unresolved area of research. The full nature of his pre-Christian cult (temples, priests, rituals) is unknown, as Christianization destroyed most of this evidence.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Welnos as the original 'final boss' of the Indo-European world. He is the dragon at the end of the dungeon, the lord of the underworld, the trickster who makes deals. He is not evil — he is simply the opposing force to the sky god, the necessary shadow that gives light its shape. He is the god you hope you never meet, but you know he is there, at the root of the world, waiting.
