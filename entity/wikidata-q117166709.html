Form

No single canonical appearance survives. In later Baltic folklore, Velnias/Velns is often a shapeshifter: a black man, a foreigner, a serpent, a black dog, or a goat. In Slavic sources, Veles is not depicted directly, but is associated with the serpent and the dragon. He may have been imagined as a giant serpent or a dragon-like being, based on his later association with the 'snake beneath the world' or the 'dragon of the deep.'

Powers

Chthonic Dominion As a god of the underworld, Welnos rules over the dead and the hidden realms beneath the earth. He is the counterpart to the sky god (Perun/Perkūnas), and the two are in constant opposition. Reconstructed; Baltic and Slavic parallels His power is largely confined to the underworld and the earth; he is not a sky god. The extent of his role as a psychopomp or ruler of the dead is debated. Some scholars see him as a mere demon, not a full god of the dead.

Master of Cattle and Wealth In Slavic sources, Veles is explicitly a god of cattle and wealth. His name is used in oaths to guarantee the fulfillment of agreements, suggesting a role as a guarantor of contracts and trade. Slavic (Veles/Volos) This role is less prominent in Baltic sources, where Velnias is more often a trickster. The connection between cattle and the underworld is a common Indo-European theme, but the exact nature of Veles's role as a wealth god is unclear.

Magic and Prophecy The root *wel- implies a connection to seeing and knowing. Welnos is thus a god of hidden knowledge, prophecy, and magical insight. In later folklore, Velnias is a source of forbidden knowledge, often granting power in exchange for a soul. Linguistic reconstruction; later Baltic folklore This power is more implied than explicitly attested in early sources. The later association with the Devil may have exaggerated his role as a granter of dark gifts.

Shapeshifting In Baltic folklore, Velnias/Velns can transform into various animals and objects. This is a common trait of trickster and chthonic deities. Baltic folklore (post-Christianization) This is a feature of later folklore, not necessarily of the original deity. It is unclear if this power was attributed to the pre-Christian god or was a later addition under Christian influence.

Behavior & Habitat

Welnos is a being of the liminal and the hidden. He is a chthonic deity, associated with the underworld, the dead, and the roots of the world-tree. He is also a god of cattle and wealth, of magic and prophecy, and of the untamed wilderness. In later folklore, he becomes a trickster and a bargainer, often outwitted by humans but always dangerous.

Accounts

The central myth of Welnos is his eternal conflict with Perun, the thunder god. Welnos, as a serpent or dragon, steals Perun's cattle, his wife, or his children, and hides in the underworld, in the roots of the world-tree, or under a rock. Perun, from his mountain top, hurls thunderbolts at him, chasing him across the sky and the earth. The battle ends with Perun striking the rock or tree, freeing his property and restoring cosmic order.

Welnos is associated with boundaries, crossroads, and liminal spaces. He is the one who stands at the edge of the known world, guarding the threshold between the living and the dead. In later folklore, he is often found at crossroads, offering bargains to travelers.

In countless folk tales, Velnias/Velns appears as a trickster who makes bargains with humans. He offers wealth, power, or knowledge in exchange for a soul, a service, or a seemingly impossible task. He is often outwitted by clever peasants, who use his own rules against him.

What We Don't Know

The primary gap is the lack of any direct, pre-Christian attestation of Welnos. All knowledge is reconstructed from later Baltic and Slavic sources, which are filtered through a Christian lens. The exact nature of his mythology, his relationship to other deities, and his role in Proto-Indo-European religion remain matters of scholarly debate. Further research into Baltic and Slavic folklore, archaeology, and linguistics may help to fill these gaps.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Welnos as the original 'final boss' of the Indo-European world. He is the dragon at the end of the dungeon, the lord of the underworld, the trickster who makes deals. He is not evil — he is simply the opposing force to the sky god, the necessary shadow that gives light its shape. He is the god you hope you never meet, but you know he is there, at the root of the world, waiting.
