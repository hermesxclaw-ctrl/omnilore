The Approach

The Big Mac is not merely a hamburger; it is a cultural artifact, a global economic indicator, and a symbol of American capitalism that has been dissected, parodied, and loved for over half a century. With its three-tiered sesame seed bun and its secret sauce, it has transcended its fast-food origins to become a subject of legal battles, advertising lore, and even economic theory.

Content advisory. No dark material in the traditional sense, but discussions of capitalism, cultural imperialism, and legal disputes may be relevant. Contains descriptions of food products.

Etymology

The name 'Big Mac' was coined by Esther Glickstein Rose, a McDonald's advertising secretary, as the third attempt after 'Aristocrat' and 'Blue Ribbon Burger' failed. The name emphasizes the burger's large size and uses 'Mac' as a nod to McDonald's.

Cultural Ownership

Created by McDonald's, an American multinational fast food chain. The Big Mac is globally recognized and has been used as a symbol of American culture and economic influence.

Disputed Identifications

The Big Mac is sometimes confused with other McDonald's burgers like the Quarter Pounder or the Big Boy from the Big Boy chain, but it is distinct in its three-bun, two-patty construction. The 'Big Mac' name is trademarked, but its generic use has been debated in legal contexts.

Description

Appearance

The Big Mac features a three-slice sesame seed bun with two beef patties, one slice of processed American cheese, shredded iceberg lettuce, two dill pickle slices, minced onions, and a special sauce. The middle bun slice separates the two patties, creating a distinctive stacked appearance.

Symbols & Iconography

The Big Mac is often depicted in advertising with a focus on its ingredients, and its packaging has evolved from a collapsible square cardboard container to a polystyrene clamshell, and later to more environmentally friendly materials. The 'M' logo and the jingle are iconic.

Behaviour & Personality

As a product, the Big Mac is known for its consistent taste and quality across locations. It is marketed as a satisfying, filling burger, and its special sauce is a closely guarded secret recipe (though a copycat recipe has been released by McDonald's chef).

Domains & Habitats

The Big Mac is available at McDonald's restaurants worldwide. It is also sold as a limited-time sauce in some regions and has been featured in promotional events.

Version Differences

There are numerous regional and seasonal variants, including the Mega Mac (four patties), Chicken Big Mac (chicken patties), Maharaja Mac (chicken in India due to beef restrictions), and various limited-edition versions like the Giga Big Mac in Japan. The standard Big Mac has remained largely unchanged since its introduction, but ingredient sourcing and preservative use have been adjusted over time.

Stories & Myths

Creation of the Big Mac

Source McDonald's history and interviews with Jim DelligattiCulture AmericanPeriod 1960s

Summary

Jim Delligatti, a McDonald's franchisee in the Pittsburgh area, created the Big Mac in 1967 to compete with the Big Boy hamburger. He copied the double-deck concept from the Big Boy chain. The burger was first sold at his Uniontown, Pennsylvania restaurant for 45 cents. After its success, it was added to the national menu in 1968.

Participants

Jim Delligatti

Esther Glickstein Rose (named the burger)

Consequences

The Big Mac became McDonald's flagship product and a global phenomenon.

Variants

The original name attempts were 'Aristocrat' and 'Blue Ribbon Burger', both of which failed.

Trademark Battle with Supermac's

Source EUIPO and European Court of Justice rulingsCulture EuropeanPeriod 2019-2024

Summary

McDonald's sued Irish fast-food chain Supermac's for trademark infringement, claiming the 'SuperMac' name would confuse consumers. In 2019, EUIPO ruled in Supermac's favor, partly because McDonald's submitted a Wikipedia article as evidence, which was deemed unreliable. McDonald's later provided 700 pages of evidence, leading to an annulment in 2023. Supermac's appealed to the European Court of Justice, which ruled in 2024 that McDonald's had not proven use of the trademark for poultry products or restaurant services, but the ruling did not affect the Big Mac burger trademark.

Consequences

The case highlighted the importance of robust evidence in trademark disputes and the challenges of proving genuine use.

Variants

The decision was partial, allowing McDonald's to retain the trademark for the burger itself.
