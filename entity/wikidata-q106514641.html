Who It Is

The Big Mac is not merely a hamburger; it is a cultural artifact, a global economic indicator, and a symbol of American capitalism that has been dissected, parodied, and loved for over half a century. With its three-tiered sesame seed bun and its secret sauce, it has transcended its fast-food origins to become a subject of legal battles, advertising lore, and even economic theory.

As a product, the Big Mac is known for its consistent taste and quality across locations. It is marketed as a satisfying, filling burger, and its special sauce is a closely guarded secret recipe (though a copycat recipe has been released by McDonald's chef).

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Big Mac' was coined by Esther Glickstein Rose, a McDonald's advertising secretary, as the third attempt after 'Aristocrat' and 'Blue Ribbon Burger' failed. The name emphasizes the burger's large size and uses 'Mac' as a nod to McDonald's.

Pronunciation — /bɪɡ mæk/

Aliases — Aristocrat (former name), Blue Ribbon Burger (former name), Mega Mac (variant), Double Big Mac (variant), Big Big Mac (variant), Denali Mac (variant)

Ownership — Created by McDonald's, an American multinational fast food chain. The Big Mac is globally recognized and has been used as a symbol of American culture and economic influence.

Cultural Icon Status

The Big Mac has become a symbol of American capitalism and globalization, referenced in media, economics, and politics.

Limit: Its cultural significance varies by region; in some countries, it may be seen as a symbol of cultural imperialism.

Economic Indicator

The Big Mac Index, published by The Economist, uses the price of the Big Mac to compare purchasing power parity between countries.

Limit: The index has limitations, such as not accounting for local labor costs or trade barriers.

Marketing Power

The Big Mac has been the subject of memorable advertising campaigns, including the 'Two all-beef patties' jingle and the McDonaldland character Officer Big Mac.

Limit: Advertising effectiveness can decline over time; some campaigns have been discontinued due to legal issues.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The Big Mac features a three-slice sesame seed bun with two beef patties, one slice of processed American cheese, shredded iceberg lettuce, two dill pickle slices, minced onions, and a special sauce. The middle bun slice separates the two patties, creating a distinctive stacked appearance.

Symbols — The Big Mac is often depicted in advertising with a focus on its ingredients, and its packaging has evolved from a collapsible square cardboard container to a polystyrene clamshell, and later to more environmentally friendly materials. The 'M' logo and the jingle are iconic.

Domains — The Big Mac is available at McDonald's restaurants worldwide. It is also sold as a limited-time sauce in some regions and has been featured in promotional events.

Accounts I
Creation of the Big MacMcDonald's history and interviews with Jim Delligatti

1960s

Jim Delligatti, a McDonald's franchisee in the Pittsburgh area, created the Big Mac in 1967 to compete with the Big Boy hamburger. He copied the double-deck concept from the Big Boy chain. The burger was first sold at his Uniontown, Pennsylvania restaurant for 45 cents. After its success, it was added to the national menu in 1968. The Big Mac became McDonald's flagship product and a global phenomenon.

Accounts II
Trademark Battle with Supermac'sEUIPO and European Court of Justice rulings

2019-2024

McDonald's sued Irish fast-food chain Supermac's for trademark infringement, claiming the 'SuperMac' name would confuse consumers. In 2019, EUIPO ruled in Supermac's favor, partly because McDonald's submitted a Wikipedia article as evidence, which was deemed unreliable. McDonald's later provided 700 pages of evidence, leading to an annulment in 2023. Supermac's appealed to the European Court of Justice, which ruled in 2024 that McDonald's had not proven use of the trademark for poultry products or restaurant services, but the ruling did not affect the Big Mac burger trademark. The case highlighted the importance of robust evidence in trademark disputes and the challenges of proving genuine use.
