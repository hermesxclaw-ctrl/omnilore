Who It Is

In the moonlit gardens of Persian imagination, there flits a creature of such beauty that poets have compared their beloveds to it for a thousand years. The parī, or peri, is no mere fairy of Western lore—it is a being of paradox: a winged spirit of exquisite grace, yet one that can abduct the unwary to its hidden realm; a creature of light that once consorted with demons, and a being whose name may or may not be kin to the English 'fairy.' To encounter a parī is to risk losing one's reason, for love and enchantment are its weapons, and its world, Parīstān, lies just beyond the veil of our own.

Parīs are morally ambivalent, neither purely good nor evil. They can be benevolent, aiding humans, or malevolent, abducting them or punishing social transgressions. They are often capricious and may fall in love with mortals, but their affection can turn to wrath if offended. They live in societies similar to human ones, with kings and social structures. They are also known to abduct humans to attend their weddings or to take them to Parīstān.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The Persian word parī (پری) derives from Middle Persian parīg, from Old Persian *parikā-. The word may be related to the Persian par meaning 'wing,' though other etymologies have been proposed. The connection to the English 'fairy' is disputed; some suggest a common origin in the sense of 'enchantme

Pronunciation — pæˈriː (Persian); 'peh-ree' in English

Aliases — peri, pari, parik, fairies, jinn (sometimes used interchangeably)

Ownership — Persian (Zoroastrian and Islamic), with spread to Turkish, South Asian, Central Asian, and Middle Eastern folklore

Powers
Flight

Parīs have wings and can fly through the air.

Limit: None specified

Shape-shifting

They can transform into doves or other forms.

Limit: Not specified

Enchantment and Love Magic

Parīs can cast love spells, causing mortals to fall in love with them or with others. They can also steal reason, leading to madness (dīvānah).

Limit: The effect may be temporary or conditional on the victim's actions.

Abduction

Parīs can abduct humans, either physically or psychologically, taking them to Parīstān or to their weddings.

Limit: Abductions may be temporary or permanent; victims may lose consciousness.

Healing and Protection

In some traditions, parīs aid shamans in healing, such as preventing miscarriage or protecting from evil jinn.

Limit: Requires the aid of a spiritual master.

Immortality

As supernatural beings, parīs are likely long-lived or immortal, though not explicitly stated in sources.

Limit: Not specified

Form & Signs

Appearance — Parīs are described as winged creatures of immense beauty, often female, with an erotic appeal to mortal men. In Turkish tales, they may appear with cloud-like garments of green color and can take the shape of doves. They are often depicted as luminous or ethereal, with a beauty that is almost otherworldly.

Symbols — Wings are a key symbol. They are associated with the realm of Parīstān, a hidden world. In art, they may be depicted as beautiful women with wings, sometimes with halos or in garden settings. The green color of garments in Turkish tradition may symbolize nature or the supernatural.

Domains — Parīs inhabit Parīstān, a hidden world or realm, often imagined as a beautiful, magical place. They also dwell in natural settings like gardens, forests, and near water, where they may bathe. In some traditions, they are associated with the air and can fly.

Accounts I
Keyumars and the Warning of SorushFerdowsi's Shahnameh

c. 1010 CE

At the beginning of the Shahnameh, the divinity Sorush appears in the form of a parī to warn Keyumars, the first man and shah, and his son Siamak of the threats posed by Ahriman, the destructive spirit. Later, parīs are part of the mythological army that Keyumars assembles to defeat Ahriman and his demonic son. Keyumars and his forces are able to resist Ahriman, establishing the cosmic struggle between good and evil.

The Story of Ahmad and Pari BanuOne Thousand and One Nights

Medieval (compiled over centuries)

Prince Ahmad meets and marries the beautiful princess Pari Banu, who is a parī. He must complete difficult tasks, which he accomplishes with her aid. The tale combines two originally separate stories, with the parī appearing in the latter part. Ahmad succeeds in his tasks and presumably lives happily with his fairy-wife.

Accounts II
Fath-Ali Shah Qajar and the ParīArthur de Gobineau's travel report

19th century

A dervish warns Fath-Ali Shah Qajar that he must take precautions to meet a parī, as her affection might turn to wrath. The Shah prepares an elaborate pavilion with golden and silver vessels, but falls asleep and wakes to find no parī and the dervish gone. The story is told with mockery, suggesting the Shah's gullibility. The story reflects the popularity of belief in parīs in Iran, but also Western skepticism.

Paradise and the PeriThomas Moore's Lalla Rookh

1817

A peri seeks entrance to heaven by offering an angel the gift most dear to God. She first offers a drop of blood from a soldier who died for liberty, then a sigh from a maiden who died with her lover rather than survive in exile, and finally a tear from an evil old man who repented upon seeing a child praying. The third gift gains her entry. The peri is admitted to heaven, symbolizing the power of repentance.

Peris are the same as fairies in Western folklore. — While there are similarities (winged, beautiful, supernatural), the parī has distinct origins and characteristics, such as being associated with jinn and having a specific realm (Parīstān). The etymology is disputed.

Peris are always female and benign. — They are often female, but not necessarily; and they can be dangerous, abducting humans or punishing them.

Peris are purely good spirits. — They are morally ambivalent; in early Persian belief they were evil spirits, and in Islamic thought they can be either angelic or demonic.
