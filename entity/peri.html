Who It Is

The Peri (also Pari, from Middle Persian 'parīk') is a beautiful, winged, fairy-like spirit in Persian mythology and later in Islamic and Indo-Persian folklore. In Zoroastrian cosmology, the original Parīk were female deities or spirits associated with nature, fertility, and the wilderness — but they were considered dangerous, seductive, and potentially demonic in the early Zoroastrian texts (the Avesta), where they are classified as followers of the druj (falsehood/deceit) who attempt to seduce men away from righteousness. However, by the time of the Shahnameh and later Persian poetry, the Peri had been thoroughly transformed into benevolent, ethereal beings — exquisitely beautiful winged spirits, often described as being caught between angels and demons, neither fully good nor evil, but generally kind and helpful to humans. In Islamic folklore, the Peri are spirits of fallen angels who are not condemned to hell but are barred from paradise until they atone. They live in the atmosphere between heaven and earth, in gardens and beautiful groves, and they are ruled by a king named Suleiman (Solomon). Peris are described as stunningly beautiful beings with luminous skin, brilliant wings (often in colors of white, gold, blue, or rainbow), and compassionate hearts. They are the Persian equivalent of the European fairy or the English elf, but with a distinctly Persian sensibility — associated with poetry, music, and sublime beauty. The Peri was introduced to Western literature through Thomas Moore's epic poem 'Lalla Rookh' (1817), where a Peri is the protagonist of 'Paradise and the Peri,' one of the most famous sections, which tells the story of a Peri trying to gain entry to paradise. In modern Persian, 'pari' simply means 'fairy' or 'angel,' and the word has entered many languages including Turkish ('peri'), Urdu ('pari'), and Bengali ('pori'). The word 'fairy' in English is not directly related etymologically (it derives from Latin 'fata' through Old French 'faerie'), but the Peri is conceptually the closest Persian equivalent.
