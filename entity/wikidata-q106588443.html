The Approach

Before it became a symbol of mathematical summation, a badge of quality control, or a meme for solitary self-reliance, Sigma was simply a letter—the eighteenth in the Greek alphabet—whose journey from Phoenician shores to the keyboards of the digital age traces the very evolution of Western writing itself. Its double life, as both a humble phonetic mark and a vessel for profound scientific and cultural meaning, makes it a singular artifact in the archive of human communication.

Etymology

The name 'sigma' is of uncertain origin. One hypothesis suggests it derives from the Phoenician letter samekh (𐤎), which continued through Greek xi (Ξ). Another proposes it is a Greek innovation meaning 'hissing', from the root of σίζω (sízō, 'I hiss'), from Proto-Greek *sig-jō. The original name may have been 'san', but due to the complicated early history of the Greek epichoric alphabets, san came to be identified as a separate letter (Ϻ). Herodotus reports that 'san' was the Dorian name for the same letter called 'sigma' by the Ionians.

Cultural Ownership

The Greek letter sigma is part of the Greek alphabet, originating in ancient Greece and continuing into modern Greek. It has been adopted into many other writing systems, including the Latin alphabet (as the ancestor of 'S'), Cyrillic, and Coptic. Its use in mathematics and science is global.

Disputed Identifications

The historical relationship between sigma and san (Ϻ) is debated. Some sources treat them as the same letter with different names, while others consider them separate letters. The etymology of the name 'sigma' is also disputed, with hypotheses linking it to Phoenician samekh or to a Greek root meaning 'hissing'.

Description

Appearance

Sigma has two lowercase forms: σ (used in the middle of words) and ς (used at the end). The uppercase form is Σ. In handwritten Greek during the Hellenistic period, a simplified C-like shape called lunate sigma (Ϲ, ϲ) became common. There are also dotted and reversed variants used in ancient editorial practice.

Symbols & Iconography

Sigma is used as a symbol in various fields: Σ for summation in mathematics, σ for standard deviation in statistics, sigma bonds in chemistry, and the Sigma receptor in biology. It also appears in religious contexts, such as the Christogram ΙϹ ΧϹ.

Behaviour & Personality

As a letter, sigma has no behavior or personality. Its usage reflects the sounds and meanings assigned by human languages and disciplines.

Domains & Habitats

Sigma is found in the Greek alphabet, both ancient and modern, and in derived alphabets such as Latin (as 'S'), Cyrillic (С), and Coptic (Ⲥ). It is used in mathematics, science, engineering, finance, and linguistics.

Version Differences

The main variants are the standard sigma (Σ, σ, ς) and lunate sigma (Ϲ, ϲ). There are also dotted lunate (Ͼ), reversed (Ͻ), and reversed dotted (Ͽ) forms used in ancient texts. The Cyrillic letter Es (С) derives from lunate sigma, while the Latin letter S derives from the standard form.

Stories & Myths

Origin from Phoenician shin

Source Historical linguistic evidenceCulture Phoenician, GreekPeriod 8th century BC and earlier

Summary

The shape and alphabetic position of sigma are derived from the Phoenician letter shin (𐤔). The Greeks adapted the Phoenician alphabet, and sigma became the eighteenth letter.

Participants

Phoenician scribes

Early Greek adaptors

Consequences

Sigma became a fundamental part of the Greek alphabet, later influencing many other scripts.

Variants

The original name may have been 'san', but it became 'sigma' among Ionians.

Development of lunate sigma

Source Epigraphic evidenceCulture Hellenistic GreekPeriod 4th–3rd century BC

Summary

In handwritten Greek, the epigraphic form of Σ was simplified into a C-like shape, known as lunate sigma. This became the standard in late antiquity and the Middle Ages.

Participants

Greek scribes

Consequences

Lunate sigma influenced the Cyrillic letter С and Coptic Ⲥ, and is still used in decorative typefaces in Greece.

Variants

Dotted and reversed lunate sigmas were used as editorial signs by Aristarchus of Samothrace.

Adoption into Old Italic alphabets

Source Epigraphic evidenceCulture Etruscan, Oscan, LatinPeriod 8th century BC onwards

Summary

Sigma was adopted into Old Italic alphabets, and a simplified three-stroke version was incorporated into Etruscan, Oscan, and early Latin, eventually becoming the Latin letter S.

Participants

Etruscan, Oscan, Latin speakers

Consequences

The Latin alphabet, the most widely used writing system today, derives its 'S' from sigma.

Variants

The three-stroke version also influenced the early runic alphabet.
