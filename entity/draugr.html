Who It Is

In Nordic folklore and the Icelandic sagas, the draugr (Old Norse: draugr; plural: draugar) is a malevolent undead revenant — the Norse counterpart to the modern zombie. Unlike the shambling corpse of popular culture, the draugr is immensely strong, often bloated to monstrous proportions, and possesses supernatural abilities: it can increase its size at will, shape-shift into animals (seal, horse, bull), enter the dreams of the living, and drive men mad with fear. Draugar guard their burial mounds jealously, killing anyone who disturbs their treasure. They must be defeated through physical combat — often decapitation and cremation — as they are impervious to conventional weapons. The most famous draugr is Glámr from Grettir's Saga.

Form

The draugr is described as a physical, corporeal corpse, not an ethereal ghost. Its appearance is typically swollen, blackened, or hideously bloated, often with an unnatural blue or dark coloration ('hel-blár', death-blue). It is often described as being immensely heavy and strong, with a stench of decay. Some are described as having their eyes open, and they are often said to be able to see in the dark. They are frequently clad in the grave-goods they were buried with, sometimes with a helmet and a sword. The mound-dweller (haugbúi) is the most common form, sitting or lying within its burial mound, but the draugr can also be a 'wandering' spirit that leaves its grave to terrorize the living.

Supernatural Strength and Weight The draugr is consistently described as being impossibly heavy and strong. In Grettis saga, the hero struggles to grapple with Glámr, and the creature's weight is a key factor. This physicality is a core feature, distinguishing it from a mere ghost. Attested across multiple sagas (Grettis saga, Eyrbyggja saga) The draugr can be physically overcome by a strong and courageous hero, though it often requires a desperate struggle. While its physicality is consistent, the exact degree of strength varies from saga to saga, often to serve the narrative.

Shape-shifting and Illusion Some draugar are described as being able to change their form. The most famous example is in Hrómundar saga Gripssonar, where the draugr Þráinn is said to be able to take the form of a seal. Others can create illusions or cause themselves to appear in different places. Attested in Hrómundar saga and other late legendary sagas This power is not universally attributed to all draugar; it is more common in the later, more fantastical sagas. The sagas are not consistent on this point; the earlier Icelandic family sagas tend to depict the draugr as more purely physical.

Cursing The draugr Glámr's curse on Grettir is a central plot device in Grettis saga. He curses Grettir to be a criminal, to be an outlaw, and to be unable to gain strength in darkness. This shows the draugr's ability to affect the fate of the living, not just their physical safety. Attested in Grettis saga The curse is a specific narrative event, not a general power of all draugar. The mechanism of the curse is not explained; it is portrayed as a supernatural act of will by the dying/undead creature.

Control over Weather and Darkness Draugar are often associated with storms, fog, and unnatural darkness. Glámr, for example, is said to have brought bad weather and a sense of dread to the valley. Attested in Grettis saga and other sagas This is often a passive or ambient effect of the draugr's presence rather than a conscious, targeted ability. The extent of this control is not clearly defined in the sources.

Invulnerability to Conventional Weapons A key feature of the draugr is that it cannot be killed by normal means. Stabbing or cutting it with a sword has little effect. It must be decapitated, and often, the head must be placed between its legs or burned to ashes to prevent its return. Attested in Grettis saga and Eyrbyggja saga The draugr is vulnerable to decapitation and fire. This is the standard method for permanently destroying it. Some sagas suggest that the draugr can be 'killed' by wrestling it to the ground and then decapitating it, while others imply a more ritualistic process.

Behavior & Habitat

The draugr is almost universally malevolent, though with notable exceptions (e.g., Gunnarr in Njáls saga is a more benign, prophetic figure). Its behaviors include: 1) Guarding its grave-mound and the treasure within it, attacking anyone who dares to enter. 2) 'Riding' the roofs of houses at night, causing them to groan and creak, a terrifying auditory phenomenon. 3) Killing livestock and people, often by crushing them or driving them mad. 4) Driving the living from their homes and farms, effectively annexing the land for itself. 5) Wrestling with the living, a common motif in the sagas, where a hero must physically overpower the creature. 6) Cursing the living, as seen with Glámr, who curses Grettir. The draugr is often depicted as being able to control the weather, creating fog and darkness, and to cause disease and misfortune.

Accounts

Glámr, a Swedish herdsman, is hired to tend sheep at a farm in a haunted valley. He is a fearless but surly and impious man. He is found dead, seemingly crushed to death, and his body is discovered to be black and swollen. He begins to haunt the area, riding the roofs of the farmhouse, killing livestock, and driving the inhabitants away. Grettir Ásmundarson, a hero of great strength, comes to the farm to deal with the draugr. He waits in the hall and engages Glámr in a colossal wrestling match. The fight is so violent that the hall is nearly destroyed. Grettir eventually throws Glámr down, but the draugr fixes him with a terrible gaze and utters a curse: Grettir will be an outlaw, a criminal, and will never grow stronger in darkness. As Glámr dies (or is destroyed), he spits in Grettir's face, sealing the curse. Grettir then cuts off Glámr's head and places it between his legs.

Þórólfr is a powerful and aggressive chieftain. After his death, he is buried in a mound, but his corpse does not rest. He begins to haunt the nearby farms, killing shepherds and cattle, and causing great terror. His body is found to be swollen and heavy. The saga describes how he 'rides' the roof of the farmhouse at night. The living attempt to deal with him by digging him up, but he is so heavy that they can barely move him. They eventually burn his body on a pyre, and only then does the haunting cease.

The hero Hrómundr is tasked with entering the burial mound of the king Þráinn to retrieve a sword. The mound is filled with treasure and guarded by the draugr Þráinn, who is described as able to shape-shift, taking the form of a seal. Hrómundr enters the mound, fights the draugr, and defeats it, taking the sword and treasure. This story is a classic example of the 'hero enters the mound to win treasure' motif.

Gunnarr of Hlíðarendi is a great hero who is slain and buried in his mound. His son and mother hear a song coming from within the mound, which is a poem composed by Gunnarr himself. This is a rare example of a mound-dweller who is not malevolent but is instead a poetic and prophetic figure. The song is a lament and a prophecy.

Field Notes

† The draugr's ability to 'ride' the roof of a house is a recurring motif, and it's often described as a sign of a truly powerful and malevolent spirit.

† The method for destroying a draugr is very specific: you must cut off its head, and often place it between its legs, or burn the body to ashes. Simply stabbing it with a sword is useless.

† The draugr Glámr's curse on Grettir is one of the most pivotal moments in all of Icelandic saga literature, setting the tragic course for the hero's life.

† The name 'Glámr' itself may be related to the word for 'gloom' or 'darkness', fitting for a creature that brings fear and shadows.

† In some sagas, the draugr is said to be able to see in the dark, making it an even more terrifying opponent in the pitch-black of a Scandinavian winter.

Scholarly Debates
Physical vs. Ethereal Nature
The draugr is a physical, corporeal corpse that can be wrestled and decapitated (as in Grettis saga). ⇌ The draugr is a more ethereal spirit that can pass through walls, shape-shift, and cause illusions (as in some later legendary sagas).
Evidence for A: Detailed accounts of physical fights and the need to burn the body.
Evidence for B: Accounts of draugar appearing in different places, taking animal forms, and being less bound by physical laws.
Motive for Haunting
The draugr is driven by greed, guarding the treasure in its mound. ⇌ The draugr is driven by a malevolent will to harm the living, often because of a wicked life or improper burial.
Evidence for A: Stories of heroes entering mounds to steal treasure (e.g., Hrómundar saga).
Evidence for B: Stories of draugar attacking farms and families without any treasure involved (e.g., Eyrbyggja saga, Laxdæla saga).
What We Don't Know

A full comparative analysis of the draugr with other Indo-European revenant figures (e.g., the Vrykolakas, the Nachzehrer) would be valuable. The archaeological evidence for burial practices that might have been intended to prevent revenants (e.g., decapitation, placing stones on graves) is not fully explored here. The evolution of the term 'draugr' in modern Icelandic and its use in contemporary folklore is also a gap.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of the draugr as the ultimate 'unresolved issue' of the Norse world. If a person died violently, was wicked in life, or was not given the proper burial rites, they could come back as a draugr. It's a physical manifestation of social and spiritual disorder. The hero who fights the draugr isn't just fighting a monster; they're restoring order to the community, putting the dead back in their place, and ensuring that the proper boundaries between life and death are respected. It's a terrifying, but deeply meaningful, monster.
