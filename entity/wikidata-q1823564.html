As 'daughter of the Mist' and 'carrier of the water jar,' she presides over the life-giving waters that are distinct from the sea (Yamm) and the rivers. Her domain is the arable land and its productivity, rather than the wild or the deep. She is a chthonic-earth figure, but distinct from the underworld goddesses.

Form

No direct description of Arsay's appearance survives in the Ugaritic texts. Based on her epithet, 'the one who carries the water jar,' she is typically reconstructed as a young maiden, possibly a water-bearer or a goddess of springs, wells, or dew. She is a divine maiden, not a mother goddess. Her iconography, if it existed, is unknown.

Powers

Control over Dew and Mist Arsay is the personification of the life-giving moisture of the earth. As the 'daughter of the Mist' and the one who carries the water jar, she is responsible for the gentle, fertilizing waters that sustain crops. Ugaritic (KTU 1.3 I 24) Her power is specific to this domain. There is no evidence she controls the sea, the storms, or the rivers. She is a passive, generative force, not a warrior. None

Divine Status As the daughter of El, she is a member of the divine council and possesses immortality and supernatural authority. Ugaritic (all texts) Her authority is limited to her domain and her status as a member of the pantheon. She has no known epic role or mythic agency. None

Behavior & Habitat

Arsay is a passive figure in the surviving myths. She does not speak or act as a primary agent. She is listed, invited, and receives offerings. She is part of the divine retinue and is a daughter of the high god, El. Her role is largely one of presence and association.

Accounts

After Ba'al's palace is built, he hosts a great feast for the gods. The messenger deity (likely Gupanu and Ugaru, or a single messenger) is sent to invite the deities, including 'Arsay, daughter of Yamm,' to the celebration. Her presence is part of the formal recognition of Ba'al's kingship.

In a parallel passage, the messenger is instructed to deliver a message to 'Arsay, daughter of Yamm, the maiden, daughter of the Mist, the one who carries the water jar, the one who holds the dew-bucket.' This is her most detailed description and establishes her role as a water-bearer.

Scholarly Debates
Parentage: Daughter of El vs. Daughter of Yamm
Arsay is the daughter of El, the supreme god. This is the most direct and consistent statement in the texts. ⇌ Arsay is the daughter of Yamm, the sea god. This is stated in a parallel epithet.
Evidence for A: The phrase 'Arsay, daughter of Yamm' is used in parallel with 'the maiden, daughter of the Mist.' This suggests a poetic
Evidence for B: The literal reading of the text. Some scholars argue for a separate tradition or a different Yamm.
Function: Earth Goddess vs. Water Goddess
Arsay is an earth goddess, as her name suggests ('the Earthy One'). ⇌ Arsay is a water/mist goddess, as her epithets suggest ('daughter of the Mist,' 'carrier of the water jar').
Evidence for A: The etymology of her name from 'earth'. The association with the fertile soil.
Evidence for B: Her epithets clearly link her to mist and dew. She is a water-bearer.
What We Don't Know

The primary gap is the lack of any mythological narrative featuring Arsay as an active participant. Her character is defined entirely by her epithets and her presence in lists. Her iconography is unknown. The specific rituals of her cult are lost. The exact nature of her relationship to Yamm and the meaning of that epithet remain unresolved. The connection (if any) between Arsay and later Phoenician or Punic deities is speculative.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Arsay as the divine personification of a morning garden: the damp soil, the dew on the leaves, the mist rising from the ground as the sun comes up. She is not the rainstorm (that's Ba'al) or the sea (that's Yamm). She is the quiet, invisible moisture that makes things grow. In a modern analogy, she is the background process that keeps the system running — essential, but never in the spotlight. She is the supporting character in a divine drama, the one who shows up to the feast, brings the water, and is never mentioned again.
