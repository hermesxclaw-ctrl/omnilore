Agneya presides over the southeast direction (Agneya-kona), the element of fire, the ritual sacrificial fire (yajna), the digestive fire, the fire of anger and passion, and the transformative power of tapas (austerity). She is also associated with the month of Bhadrapada in some regional calendars.

Form

Agneya is typically depicted as a radiant goddess with a golden or fiery complexion, often seated on a ram or goat, holding a fire pot (agnikunda) or a flaming torch. She may carry a spear, a trident, or a rosary, and her hair is depicted as flames or adorned with fire. In some iconography, she has three eyes and four arms, symbolizing her omniscience and all-encompassing power. Her clothing is often red or saffron, echoing the colors of fire and ritual sacrifice.

Powers

Fire Manipulation As the daughter of Agni, Agneya commands all forms of fire — from the sacrificial flame to the cosmic fire at the end of time (pralaya). She can create, extinguish, and shape fire at will. Puranic and Tantric traditions Her power is tied to the element of fire; she is less potent in water-dominated realms. Some texts describe her as a passive embodiment of the southeast direction, while others grant her independent agency.

Purification and Transformation Agneya's fire is said to burn away karmic impurities, negative energies, and spiritual obstacles. She is invoked in purification rituals (shanti) and in the transformation of the soul. Tantric and ritual traditions Her purification requires the devotee's sincere intention and ritual purity. None significant.

Guardianship of the Southeast As the guardian of the Agneya-kona, she protects the southeast direction and all who dwell within it. She is one of the Ashtadikpalas (guardians of the eight directions) or their feminine counterparts. Vastu Shastra and Puranic texts Her guardianship is directional; she does not govern other cardinal points. In some lists, the southeast is governed by Agni himself, with Agneya as his consort or aspect; in others, she is the independent ruler.

Boons of Vitality and Courage Worshippers seek Agneya's blessings for physical strength, courage, and the inner fire to overcome challenges. She is also invoked for healthy digestion and metabolic balance. Folk and Ayurvedic traditions Boons are granted based on the devotee's faith and deeds. None significant.

Behavior & Habitat

Agneya is described as fierce yet protective, embodying the transformative and purifying nature of fire. She is believed to consume impurities and negativity, burning away obstacles and illusions. She is also associated with the digestive fire (jatharagni), the vital energy that sustains life, and with the inner fire of spiritual aspiration. Her behavior is dual: she can be a fierce destroyer of evil or a gentle nurturer of the sacred flame.

Accounts

Agneya is born from the sacred fire of a yajna (sacrifice), emerging from the flames as a radiant goddess. She is either the daughter of Agni and Svaha, or a self-manifested form of Agni's feminine energy. Her birth symbolizes the power of ritual fire to generate divine consciousness.

In the Mahabharata, Agni, afflicted by indigestion, consumes the Khandava forest with the help of Arjuna and Krishna. In a regional variant, Agneya accompanies her father, guiding the flames and ensuring the forest's transformation into a fertile plain. She is depicted as both a destroyer of the old and a catalyst for renewal.

In the cosmic order, the eight directions are assigned guardian deities. The southeast (Agneya-kona) is given to Agni, and in some traditions, to his daughter Agneya. She is depicted as a fierce protector, warding off evil spirits, diseases, and negative energies from the southeast. Her presence ensures the prosperity and safety of homes and temples that align with this direction.

Scholarly Debates
Agneya as a deity vs. an epithet
Agneya is a distinct goddess, daughter of Agni, with her own iconography and worship. ⇌ Agneya is merely an epithet for Agni's feminine aspect or for the direction itself, not a separate deity.
Evidence for A: Puranic and Tantric texts that describe her as a goddess with specific attributes and rituals.
Evidence for B: The scarcity of Agneya in early Vedic texts, where Agni is exclusively male, and the absence of a widespread cult.
Parentage
Agneya is the daughter of Agni and Svaha. ⇌ Agneya is self-born (svayambhu) from the sacrificial fire, without a mother.
Evidence for A: Regional folklore and some Puranic verses that name Svaha as consort and mother.
Evidence for B: Tantric texts that emphasize her autonomous, self-generated nature as a shakti.
Role in the Ashtadikpalas
Agneya is the independent guardian of the southeast, replacing Agni in some lists. ⇌ Agneya is merely the shakti of Agni, who remains the primary guardian of the southeast.
Evidence for A: Vastu Shastra texts that invoke Agneya as the directional deity for ritual purposes.
Evidence for B: The more common lists of Ashtadikpalas that include Agni as the male guardian.
What We Don't Know

1. No primary Vedic text explicitly names Agneya; her origin is entirely Puranic and Tantric. 2. Regional folklore variations are not fully documented; field research in South India would be needed. 3. The exact iconographic details vary between texts; no comprehensive iconographic study of Agneya exists. 4. The relationship between Agneya and other fire goddesses (e.g., Svaha, Agneyi) is not clearly defined. 5. No modern academic monograph has been dedicated solely to Agneya.

A way to hold it in your mind

If Agni is the god of fire, think of Agneya as the goddess of the *place* where fire lives — the southeast corner, the kitchen hearth, the inner furnace of the body. She is not the fire itself, but the guardian of its domain, the one who ensures that the flame is used well. She is the sacred geometry of the home, the direction you face when you cook, the energy you invoke when you light a lamp. She is fire's home address, and she protects all who dwell within it.
