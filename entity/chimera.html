Who It Is

Imagine a creature so impossible that its very name became a synonym for the fantastical—a lion that breathes fire, with a goat's head sprouting from its back and a serpent for a tail. This is the Chimera, the original hybrid monster of Greek myth, a beast born from the union of the storm-giant Typhon and the serpent-woman Echidna. It terrorized the mountains of Lycia until a hero named Bellerophon, riding the winged horse Pegasus, descended from the sky to end its reign of fire. But the Chimera's story is more than a monster-slaying tale—it's a window into how ancient peoples explained the natural world, a symbol of chaos that civilization must overcome, and a creature whose legacy lives on in every impossible hybrid, every genetic mosaic, every 'chimeric' idea that has ever been called into being.

The Chimera is described as a destructive, ravenous beast that terrorized Lycia, killing livestock and burning villages with its fire breath. It is never depicted as intelligent or speaking—it is a pure force of bestial destruction, unlike the Sphinx (which poses riddles) or the Hydra (which has a healing regenerative power). The Chimera's behavior is straightforwardly predatory: it is a monster to be slain, not a test to be passed.

Powers
Fire breath

The Chimera breathes 'raging fire' (Hesiod) or 'blazing fire' (Homer). This is its most distinctive ability and the primary threat it poses to the Lycian countryside. The fire is described as 'unquenchable' in some sources, suggesting a supernatural, non-natural flame.

Limit: The fire breath does not protect the Chimera from a well-aimed spear or arrow. Bellerophon kills it by shooting it from above on Pegasus, suggesting the creature had no defense against aerial attacks.

Composite body

The Chimera combines the strength of a lion, the agility of a goat, and the venomous bite of a serpent. Hesiod gives it three heads, each capable of attacking independently. The creature's hybrid nature makes it a formidable opponent in close combat.

Limit: The composite body appears to be mortal—Apollodorus notes that Bellerophon 'shot down the parts of it that were mortal,' implying the creature could be killed by conventional weapons.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The Chimera is a composite creature: a lion's head and front body, a goat's head emerging from its back or middle, and a serpent or dragon for a tail. Hesiod gives it three heads—lion, goat, and snake. It breathes fire. Homer emphasizes the lion-front and snake-tail with a goat in the middle. Symbols — The Chimera has become the archetype of the composite monster—the word 'chimaera' in English now means any fanciful or impossible hybrid, or an organism with genetically different cells. In heraldry, the chimera appears as a common device. The creature symbolizes the untamed, destructive forces of nature, the challenge of the heroic quest, and the monstrous 'other' that must be overcome for civilization to flourish.

Domains — The Chimera is associated with Lycia in Anatolia. Its lair was traditionally placed near Mount Chimaera (modern Yanartaş in Turkey), a location where natural methane fires burn continuously from the ground—a phenomenon that may explain the creature's fire-breathing nature. The creature is also associated with the broader region of Caria and Lycia, and with the Underworld as the offspring of Echidna and Typhon.

The Slaying of the Chimera by BellerophonHomer, Iliad 6; Hesiod, Theogony; Apollodorus, Bibliotheca; Pindar, Olympian Odes

Archaic to Classical period (8th-4th century BCE)

The hero Bellerophon, son of Glaucus (or Poseidon), was exiled from Corinth after killing a man. He sought purification from King Proetus of Tiryns. Proetus's wife, Stheneboea (or Anteia), falsely accused Bellerophon of attempting to seduce her after he rejected her advances. Proetus, unwilling to kill a guest, sent Bellerophon to his father-in-law, King Iobates of Lycia, with sealed letters instructing Iobates to kill the bearer. Iobates, also reluctant to violate guest-friendship, instead assigned Bellerophon a series of deadly tasks, the first of which was to slay the Chimera that was ravaging Lycia. Bellerophon, aided by the winged horse Pegasus (which he had tamed with a golden bridle given by Athena), flew above the Chimera and killed it—either by shooting it with arrows, thrusting a spear into its mouth, or dropping a lead weight that melted in its fire breath and suffocated it. Accounts II
The Origin and Nature of the ChimeraHesiod, Theogony 319-325; Homer, Iliad 6.179-183; Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.3.1

Archaic period (8th-7th century BCE)

The Chimera was one of the monstrous children of Typhon and Echidna, alongside Cerberus, the Hydra, and Orthrus. Hesiod describes her as a fire-breathing creature with three heads: one of a lion, one of a goat rising from her back, and one of a serpent or dragon as her tail. Homer describes her as "a thing of immortal make, not human, lion-fronted and snake behind, a goat in the middle, and snorting out the breath of the terrible flame of bright fire." The Chimera was raised by Amisodarus, king of Caria, and terrorized the region of Lycia until Bellerophon was sent to slay her. The creature's composite nature symbolized the untamed, chaotic forces at the edges of the Greek world. The Chimera's birth cemented the line of monsters that would provide challenges for future Greek heroes. As the sibling of the Hydra (slain by Heracles) and Cerberus (captured by Heracles), the Chimera is part of a broader mythological cycle of heroic monster-slaying that defined Greek identity.

The Chimera is a single beast with a lion's head, a goat's head on its back, and a snake for a tail. — This is the standard modern depiction, but ancient sources vary. Hesiod gives the Chimera three heads (lion, goat, snake), while Homer describes a lion-fronted body with a goat in the middle and a snake tail—which may imply a single head. The visual culture of ancient Greece predominantly shows a lion-headed creature with a goat's head emerging from its back.

The Chimera is a fire-breathing dragon with wings. — The Chimera does not have wings in any ancient source. The wings often added in modern depictions are a conflation with Pegasus, Bellerophon's winged horse. The Chimera is consistently described as a land-bound creature.

The Chimera is a female creature. — The Greek word 'Chimaira' is grammatically feminine, and the creature is indeed referred to with feminine pronouns in Greek texts. However, the creature's gender is not a significant feature of the myth—it is a monster, not a character with a gendered identity. The feminine grammatical gender may simply reflect the word's ending.

The Chimera is the same as the Sphinx. — The Chimera and the Sphinx are distinct creatures. The Sphinx has a lion's body and a human head, while the Chimera is a lion-goat-serpent composite. They are sometimes confused because both are composite creatures from Greek myth, but they have different origins, genealogies, and stories.
