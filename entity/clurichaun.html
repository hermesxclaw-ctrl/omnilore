Archive Dossier — Identity

Clurichaun

Pronunciation KLOO-ri-khawn (approximate)

Known As

CluricauneCluracanClúrachánClobhair-ceannThe Cellar Fairy

Etymology

From Irish 'clobhair-ceann', possibly meaning 'glutton' or 'drinker' (uncertain). The term 'clurichaun' is an anglicization.

Cultural Ownership

Irish folklore; specifically Munster and other southern regions, but found throughout Ireland.

Disputed Identifications

Often conflated with leprechauns; some folklorists see the clurichaun as a leprechaun on a drinking spree, while others consider them distinct regional variations. Also linked to the Germanic biersal/kobold.

Archive Dossier — Approach

Deep in the damp, shadowed cellars of old Irish estates, a small figure perches astride a wine cask, tankard in hand, singing a slurred tune. This is the clurichaun—a solitary fairy of intoxicating mischief, who might guard your household with fierce loyalty one moment and terrorize your cook the next. More than a mere leprechaun's drunk cousin, the clurichaun embodies the Irish fascination with drink, hospitality, and the unpredictable spirits that dwell at the edges of domestic life.

The clurichaun is the leprechaun's disreputable cousin: a solitary little fairy in a red cap and silver-buckled shoes who has renounced shoemaking, savings, and daylight industry in favor of a single vocation — living in your wine cellar and drinking it. He is folklore's perfect barfly, and like every barfly he comes with a code: treat him well — leave him his share, keep the casks in order, never try to evict him — and the clurichaun becomes the best cellar-manager in Ireland, guarding the wine from thieves, tightening leaky taps, terrorizing dishonest servants who water the whiskey, and riding the sheep or the shepherd's dog around the yard by moonlight purely for sport.

Who It Is

The clurichaun is the leprechaun's disreputable cousin: a solitary little fairy in a red cap and silver-buckled shoes who has renounced shoemaking, savings, and daylight industry in favor of a single vocation — living in your wine cellar and drinking it. He is folklore's perfect barfly, and like every barfly he comes with a code: treat him well — leave him his share, keep the casks in order, never try to evict him — and the clurichaun becomes the best cellar-manager in Ireland, guarding the wine from thieves, tightening leaky taps, terrorizing dishonest servants who water the whiskey, and riding the sheep or the shepherd's dog around the yard by moonlight purely for sport. Cross him — bottle him up, stint him, or announce his eviction — and every cask sours, every bottle breaks, and the house learns the tradition's central clause: if you try to move away from a clurichaun, he simply climbs into a cask and moves with you. The great recorded tale (Croker collected it in Cork) is of the haunted cellar of a Mr. MacCarthy, whose little tenant Naggeneen drank his best claret, mimicked his voice, and when threatened with the house's sale, cheerfully promised to come along in the moving-van. The clurichaun is the fairy of the settled account and the standing tab — proof that in Irish folk economics, some tenants cannot be evicted, only befriended, and the rent is paid in port.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

Typically a small man, about 6 inches tall, with a face like a withered apple, twinkling eyes, and a red and purple nose from heavy drinking. Wears a red nightcap, a short leather apron, light blue stockings, and shoes with large silver buckles. Some accounts say he wears red like other solitary fairies. In Gaiman's Sandman, he appears as a tall, elegant blond fairy due to glamour.

Symbols & Iconography

Associated with wine casks, tankards, clay pipes ('fairy pipes'), and leather aprons. Often depicted with a jug of ale or a hammer.

Behaviour & Personality

Mischievous, drunken, and fond of practical jokes. He can be helpful to the household he attaches himself to, protecting property and lives, but is quick to anger if slighted. He loves drinking, singing, and pipe smoking. He is a solitary fairy, not found in groups.

Domains & Habitats

Haunts breweries, pubs, wine cellars, and domestic cellars. He is a household spirit of sorts, attached to a particular family or place.

Version Differences

Descriptions vary: some emphasize his small size, others (Gaiman) depict him as tall. Some accounts merge him with leprechaun traits (shoemaking, treasure guarding). His helpfulness vs. malevolence varies by tale.

Origins

Munster tradition (especially Cork), collected by Thomas Crofton Croker in the 1820s; scholars still argue whether he is a distinct species or simply a leprechaun on a bender — a taxonomic dispute both parties would enjoy.

Magical Purse

Source Version Traditional folklore (conflated with leprechaun)

Description

Carries a purse (or pewter cup) that either contains a 'lucky shilling' that always returns, or is always full of money. He may carry two purses, one with the magic coin, one with a normal coin to trick captors.

Limitations

If captured, he can vanish if the captor looks away.

Contradictions

Some accounts say he has only one purse; others say two.

Knowledge of Hidden Treasure

Source Version Traditional folklore (conflated with leprechaun)

Description

Like leprechauns, he may know the location of hidden treasure and can be forced to reveal it, but he may create illusions of multiple markers.

Limitations

Must be captured and forced.

Contradictions

This trait is often attributed to leprechauns, not clurichauns in older sources.

Servitude Enchantment

Source Version Traditional folklore ('Master and Man')

Description

Can compel a person to serve him for a set period if they accept a drink and refuse to pay.

Limitations

Can be broken by invoking the blessing of God.

Contradictions

None.

Form

A wizened little man in a red cap and old-fashioned coat with silver buckles, flushed of nose, found astride a cask with a jug — or heard singing, muffled and content, from inside the wine racks at midnight.

Powers

Cellar-guardianship, thief-detection, tap-mending, livestock-riding by night, voice-mimicry — and the unbeatable relocation trick: he moves with the household, in the barrel, every time.

Weaknesses & Wards

Thirst governs him — a fair share keeps him loyal, and an empty cellar (the one eviction that works) sends him wandering; but spite is always cheaper for him than for you, so the tradition's advice is simply: pour.

Field Notes

❀ Croker's Naggeneen — who promised to move house inside a cask — is the type-specimen of unbudgeable tenancy.

❀ Distinguishing clurichaun from leprechaun: the shoemaker saves gold; the cellar-cousin has converted his entirely to liquid assets.

❀ Some Cork households historically left the smallest keg unbunged for him — shrinkage, pre-negotiated.

Violence against servants

Source Keightley, Fairy Mythology

Description

In 'Little Wildbean', the clurichaun drags the cook down the cellar stairs, causing severe injuries.

Scholarly Context

This reflects the fairy's role as a punisher of disrespect, but also the harsh treatment of servants in the period.

Alcoholism

Source Various folklore

Description

The clurichaun is defined by excessive drinking, which may reflect Irish stereotypes of drunkenness.

Scholarly Context

Some scholars note that such portrayals may have been exaggerated by English collectors, reinforcing negative stereotypes.

The Haunted Cellar

Source Thomas Crofton Croker, Fairy Legends and Traditions of the South of IrelandCulture IrishPeriod 19th century (collected)

Summary

A clurichaun named Naggeneen haunts the wine cellar of an Irish lord, drinking and playing pranks. He is discovered by the master, who tries to move, but Naggeneen implies he will move with him.

Participants

Naggeneen

Irish lord

Consequences

The lord decides to stay, and the clurichaun continues to haunt the cellar.

Variants

Similar motifs appear in 'Master and Man' and the story of Little Wildbean.

Master and Man

Summary

Billy Mac Daniel accepts a drink from a clurichaun but refuses to pay, so he is compelled to serve the fairy for seven years and a day. He eventually breaks the servitude by invoking God's blessing.

Participants

Clurichaun

Consequences

Variants

Little Wildbean

Source Thomas Keightley, The Fairy MythologyCulture IrishPeriod 19th century (collected)

Summary

A clurichaun named Little Wildbean haunts the cellar of a Quaker gentleman, Harris. He is helpful, stopping beer leaks, but when the cook leaves him a poor dinner, he drags her down the cellar stairs, injuring her.

Participants

Little Wildbean

Mr. Harris

Cook

Consequences

The cook is bedridden for three weeks; Harris tries to move but Wildbean moves with him.

Variants

Shows the dual nature of helpfulness and vengeance.

The clurichaun is a distinct being, a cellar fairy focused on drinking.

Croker, Keightley, O'Kearney describe him as separate.

Position B

The clurichaun is merely a leprechaun on a drinking spree.

Most folklorists consider them distinct but related; the conflation is a later development.

Position A

Cultural Significance

The clurichaun carries the Irish theology of the household spirit into the pub age — the brownie's contract rewritten for the wine trade — and through Neil Gaiman's Sandman (as the fairy Cluracan) he staggered into modern fantasy canon.
