Who It Is

Teke Teke is a gruesome Japanese urban legend about the vengeful ghost of a young woman -- often said to be a schoolgirl -- who was cut in half at the waist by a train, either accidentally or by suicide. Her spirit now haunts urban areas, particularly train stations and railway crossings. She drags herself along using only her hands and elbows, her torso scraping against the ground, producing the distinctive 'teke teke' sound that gives her her name (an onomatopoeia for the scraping sound). She moves with surprising speed despite lacking her lower body, and if she catches a victim, she kills them with a scythe or large knife, cutting them in half at the waist to make them like her. Some versions say she asks victims where her legs are, and there is no correct answer. Another variant says she appears to children walking home from school and challenges them to a race. The legend has multiple purported origin stories: one involves a bullied schoolgirl who fell (or was pushed) onto train tracks; another involves a young woman who committed suicide by train after a romantic betrayal. Teke Teke gained widespread notoriety through Japanese internet horror forums in the 1990s and was adapted into a successful J-horror film series ('Teke Teke' 2009 and 'Teke Teke 2' 2009). The legend's power lies in its body horror -- the grotesque image of a human torso dragging itself along the ground -- and the fear of urban spaces like train stations where sudden, accidental death is a real possibility.

Form

Teke Teke is a female ghost whose body is cut in half at the waist; she possesses only a head, torso, and arms/elbows, with no legs or lower body. She drags her upper torso along the ground on her hands or elbows, leaving a scraping sound trailing behind her. Some versions depict a blade or scythe that she uses to cut victims; the cut end of her own body is sometimes described as dripping blood. The zhang/zh logographic label '爬行妖怪' ('crawling monster') reflects the dominant mental image — a human upper body crawling along urban asphalt. Artist's depictions (e.g. the en.wiki lead image Teketeke.png) render her as a pale, dark-haired schoolgirl/young-woman torso on pavement, often in a school uniform tunic or dark clothing.

Powers

The reproduce-the-wound kill Teke Teke's defining act: when she catches a victim, she slices them in half at the waist, killing them in a manner that mimics her own disfigurement. She does this out of spite — a displaced, compulsive re-enactment of the injury that (per legend) she suffered and died from. Core legend register (all major summaries); 'slice them in half at the waist with a scythe' in Japanese-urban-legend context. She appears to act mainly at night and near rail/overpass infrastructure. She is not described as omnipresent; her hunting is localised to transit spaces and, in the Kashima variant, bathrooms. Weapon varies (her own cut torso vs a scythe vs bare hands); whether she chases persistently or strikes only those who 'look back' differs by telling.

The 'teke teke' locomotion She has no lower body, so she drags her torso along the ground on her hands or elbows, producing the scraping/clicking 'teke teke' sound that names her. This is more than transportation — the sound functions as her signature, the early-warning sign by which potential victims know she is near. All registers; the onomatopoeic origin is the one stable element across tellings. The audible warning is arguably her one exploitable vulnerability for a careful victim — but no canonical defence is stated. Whether the sound comes from elbows, forearms, or the cut end of the body differs by version.

The month / knowledge contagion (Kashima variant) In the Kashima Reiko iteration, merely learning the story triggers a deadline: she will appear to you within one month. The only reliable defence is to spread the story to others — making the legend self-propagating. This is a classic viral 'chain' structure common to Japanese school urban legends. 'Kashima Reiko' register (Wikipedia legend section). Contagion is knowledge-based, not physical; it is the mechanism that keeps the legend alive across generations of schoolchildren. The fixed deadline ('one month') and whether spreading the story alone truly neutralises her are folk-consensus rules, not documented religious canon.

The interrogation and the correct answer (bathroom variant) In her bathroom haunting, Kashima Reiko asks occupants whether they know where her legs are. An unacceptable answer results in being torn or sliced in half. Two survival answers circulate: reply that her legs are 'on the Meishin Expressway', or say the phrase 'kamen shinin ma' ('mask death demon'), which some gloss as the phonetic root of the name Kashima. 'Kashima Reiko' / bathroom register; Wikipedia notes both escapes. The correct-answer mechanic gives the victim a defined escape hatch — a folklore riddle structure rather than an unstoppable killer. The two offered answers (Meishin Expressway vs 'kamen shinin ma') are not harmonised; different tellings privilege one.

Sensitivity to red (film register only) In the 2009 film Teketeke, the entity is framed around a colour phobia: the spirits are attracted to or alerted by the colour red, which the central victims strip from themselves (red car, red clothes) in an attempt to escape. This is explicitly adaptation-sourced craft, not part of the attested oral legend. Teketeke (film), 2009 — plot section (Wikipedia). Not attested in the core urban legend; should NOT be treated as canonical shared lore. None within the film; but introducing red-as-trigger contradicts the colour-insensitive core legend where the only relevant features are location, sound, and the killing.

Accounts

A young woman or schoolgirl falls onto a railway line in Northern Japan and is sliced in half by a train. She does not die instantly — she survives the accident, in agony, and dies only because of the station staff's neglect (they do not help or alert in time). Missing her lower body, she rises as a vengeful spirit. Now she drags her torso along the ground on her hands or elbows and waits in urban transit spaces at night. When she catches an individual, she slices them in half at the waist with a scythe, recreating her own disfigurement out of spite.

A young woman named Kashima Reiko died when her legs were severed by a train after she fell on the tracks. Her legless ghost haunts bathroom stalls, asking occupants 'do you know where my legs are?'. If a person gives an answer she deems unacceptable, she tears or cuts them in half. Learning her story makes her appear within one month; spreading the story is said to hold her off. Survivors report that answering her legs are on the Meishin Expressway, or saying 'kamen shinin ma' ('mask death demon' — possibly the phonetic root of the name Kashima), lets you walk away intact.

The wholesome children's song 'Satchan' — about a girl who loves bananas but can only eat a half before feeling full — gets retrofitted by rumour into a Teke Teke origin. The 'half' is read as foreshadowing a body cut in half. A popular online version claims that a schoolgirl named Sachiko Kiritani died in a train accident matching Teke Teke's story, and that a student who found it funny added a mock fourth verse about the death; that student was later found dead without his legs.

The film reframes the legend. Reiko Kashima was a nurse who, in 1948, was raped by an American soldier; traumatised, she associated the colour red with the bleeding of the assault and discarded every red object she saw. She committed suicide by jumping off an overpass in Mikasa-cho. Her spirit became the Teke Teke that haunts overpasses, attracted to the colour red. Characters who survive a first encounter are said to die three days later. Cutting red clothing and accessories is presented as a way to evade her.

Scholarly Debates
One ghost or two? Teke Teke vs Kashima Reiko
They are the same entity — en.wiki redirects 'Kashima Reiko' to 'Teke Teke'; Japanese urban-legend articles list 'Teke Teke (or Kashima Reiko)' as a single entry; the 2009 film fuses them. ⇌ They are distinct — some tellings present Kashima Reiko as a separate legless bathroom ghost with an interrogation mechanic and a one-month contagion rule, whereas core Teke Teke is a silent predatory station spirit.
Cause of death and guilt
The girl dies by dismemberment (her body cut in half) — her own death is the cut. ⇌ In the film she dies by suicide (jumping from an overpass) after a rape, only becoming legless posthumously / as narrated horror.
The kill weapon
She cuts victims with a scythe or bladed tool. ⇌ She 'slices them in half' using her own bisected body / hands with no stated weapon.
Targeting and the 'look back' rule
She chases anyone she encounters at night and cuts on catching them. ⇌ She strikes only those who look back at her (a common overpass-legend mechanic).
Audibility as warning vs inevitability
The 'teke teke' sound lets a wary person hear her coming. ⇌ The sound, once heard, marks you as targeted; in the film even survivors die three days later.

A way to hold it in your mind

Read this description slowly, on any platform after dark: first you would hear the sound before you ever saw her — a rhythm, not a voice; teke… teke… teke, sharp and dry, like a palm or a bone end rapping out a count against the concrete of a near-empty platform. In the version most children learn, the girl who makes that sound is already hollow of anything except one fixed idea: that whoever crosses her path should end the way she did. The train that took the lower half of her body in Northern Japan did not kill her at once; the station staff let her die in pain, and so the ageless, legless torso drags itself through the night repeating that first unearned cut on strangers. And the deepest discomfort of Teke Teke is not the monster — it is the truth that, in this legend, the horror has a name and a schedule: learn her story, and within a month she may come to find yours. That is why the children whisper her to one another in the dark. They are not just scaring each other. They are paying the story forward so that, maybe, it will leave its teller alone.
