Who It Is

Read this description slowly, on any platform after dark: first you would hear the sound before you ever saw her — a rhythm, not a voice; teke… teke… teke, sharp and dry, like a palm or a bone end rapping out a count against the concrete of a near-empty platform. In the version most children learn, the girl who makes that sound is already hollow of anything except one fixed idea: that whoever crosses her path should end the way she did. The train that took the lower half of her body in Northern Japan did not kill her at once; the station staff let her die in pain, and so the ageless, legless torso drags itself through the night repeating that first unearned cut on strangers. And the deepest discomfort of Teke Teke is not the monster — it is the truth that, in this legend, the horror has a name and a schedule: learn her story, and within a month she may come to find yours. She is a predatory, vengeful (onryō) spirit driven to reproduce her own disfigurement on victims: she chases those who encounter her at night and slices them in half at the waist, out of spite mirroring her own death. She lurks in urban areas and roams train stations after dark. She is patient, silent except for the 'teke teke' scraping. In the Kashima Reiko variant she turns interrogative: she haunts bathroom stalls and asks occupants whether they know where her legs are; an unacceptable answer leads to being torn or sliced in two. Etymology — The name is onomatopoeic, directly encoding the entity's signature sound. 'Teke teke' (テケテケ) renders the rhythmic scraping, clicking, or pattering noise produced as the upper half of the torso drags itself along hard ground using the hands or forearms/elbows. Powers
The reproduce-the-wound kill

Teke Teke's defining act: when she catches a victim, she slices them in half at the waist, killing them in a manner that mimics her own disfigurement. She does this out of spite — a displaced, compulsive re-enactment of the injury that (per legend) she suffered and died from.

Limit: She appears to act mainly at night and near rail/overpass infrastructure. She is not described as omnipresent; her hunting is localised to transit spaces and, in the Kashima variant, bathrooms.

The 'teke teke' locomotion

She has no lower body, so she drags her torso along the ground on her hands or elbows, producing the scraping/clicking 'teke teke' sound that names her. This is more than transportation — the sound functions as her signature, the early-warning sign by which potential victims know she is near.

Limit: The audible warning is arguably her one exploitable vulnerability for a careful victim — but no canonical defence is stated.

The month / knowledge contagion (Kashima variant)

In the Kashima Reiko iteration, merely learning the story triggers a deadline: she will appear to you within one month. The only reliable defence is to spread the story to others — making the legend self-propagating. This is a classic viral 'chain' structure common to Japanese school urban legends.

Limit: Contagion is knowledge-based, not physical; it is the mechanism that keeps the legend alive across generations of schoolchildren.

The interrogation and the correct answer (bathroom variant)

In her bathroom haunting, Kashima Reiko asks occupants whether they know where her legs are. An unacceptable answer results in being torn or sliced in half. Two survival answers circulate: reply that her legs are 'on the Meishin Expressway', or say the phrase 'kamen shinin ma' ('mask death demon'), which some gloss as the phonetic root of the name Kashima.

Limit: The correct-answer mechanic gives the victim a defined escape hatch — a folklore riddle structure rather than an unstoppable killer.

Sensitivity to red (film register only)

In the 2009 film Teketeke, the entity is framed around a colour phobia: the spirits are attracted to or alerted by the colour red, which the central victims strip from themselves (red car, red clothes) in an attempt to escape. This is explicitly adaptation-sourced craft, not part of the attested oral legend.

Limit: Not attested in the core urban legend; should NOT be treated as canonical shared lore.

She drags her upper torso along the ground on her hands or elbows, leaving a scraping sound trailing behind her. Accounts I
The rail death and the first cutCore Japanese urban legend as collated by Wikipedia / yokai.com.

Modern; circulating in earnest from the late 20th century across schoolyards and early internet.

A young woman or schoolgirl falls onto a railway line in Northern Japan and is sliced in half by a train. She does not die instantly — she survives the accident, in agony, and dies only because of the station staff's neglect (they do not help or alert in time). Missing her lower body, she rises as a vengeful spirit. Now she drags her torso along the ground on her hands or elbows and waits in urban transit spaces at night. When she catches an individual, she slices them in half at the waist with a scythe, recreating her own disfigurement out of spite. Perpetual re-enactment of a violent death on strangers; the onomatopoeic name fixed to the dragging sound.

Kashima Reiko and the missing legs'Kashima Reiko' variant; Wikipedia (both the legend section and the redirect chain).

Predates the Teke Teke name in many tellings; Yuki Yoshida argues mutual influence.

A young woman named Kashima Reiko died when her legs were severed by a train after she fell on the tracks. Her legless ghost haunts bathroom stalls, asking occupants 'do you know where my legs are?'. If a person gives an answer she deems unacceptable, she tears or cuts them in half. Learning her story makes her appear within one month; spreading the story is said to hold her off. Survivors report that answering her legs are on the Meishin Expressway, or saying 'kamen shinin ma' ('mask death demon' — possibly the phonetic root of the name Kashima), lets you walk away intact. Establishes the contagious-knowledge mechanic that keeps the legend in circulation among schoolchildren.

Accounts II
Satchan's half-banana and the fourth verseThe children's song 'Satchan' (1959, lyrics Hiroo Sakata, music Megumi Ōnaka) + associated urban legend.

Song 1959; the urban-legends adaptation circulating online in the late 20th / early 21st century.

The wholesome children's song 'Satchan' — about a girl who loves bananas but can only eat a half before feeling full — gets retrofitted by rumour into a Teke Teke origin. The 'half' is read as foreshadowing a body cut in half. A popular online version claims that a schoolgirl named Sachiko Kiritani died in a train accident matching Teke Teke's story, and that a student who found it funny added a mock fourth verse about the death; that student was later found dead without his legs. A beloved children's earworm acquires a lethal double meaning; demonstrates how urban legends graft onto innocent cultural texts.

The film's backstory: Reiko Kashima, 1948, Mikasa-cho (adaptation)Teketeke (2009), dir. Kōji Shiraishi, written Takeki Akimoto — plot as documented on Wikipedia.

Set in the present (2009) with a flashback to 1948.

The film reframes the legend. Reiko Kashima was a nurse who, in 1948, was raped by an American soldier; traumatised, she associated the colour red with the bleeding of the assault and discarded every red object she saw. She committed suicide by jumping off an overpass in Mikasa-cho. Her spirit became the Teke Teke that haunts overpasses, attracted to the colour red. Characters who survive a first encounter are said to die three days later. Cutting red clothing and accessories is presented as a way to evade her. Humanises and historicises the legend, giving it a named victim, a date, and a motive; the rape-red trauma is the film's own invention and must not be mistaken for attested folklore.

Teke Teke is an ancient classical yōkai from Japanese mythology/fairy-tale collections. — She is a modern urban legend (都市伝説), not a figure of classical yōkai texts, onmyōdō, or Shinto/Buddhist canon. She belongs to late-20th/early-21st-century school and internet folklore.

The 1948 'raped nurse Reiko Kashima' backstory and the red-colour sensitivity are part of the original legend. — They are inventions of the 2009 film Teketeke. The core legend is a rail-death onryō with no named victim, no rape, no date, and no colour trigger.

Teke Teke and Kashima Reiko are definitively the same ghost. — This is contested; they may be one story, two mirrored variants, or close cousins. Yuki Yoshida argues mutual influence, not identity.

The 'Satchan' song is authentically about Teke Teke. — It is a genuine 1959 children's song about a girl who can only eat half a banana; the Teke Teke connection is a purely folk/internet re-reading imposed on the innocent lyric later.
