Who It Is

The greatest archer in the history of any myth could put an arrow through nine suns — celestial birds of fire that were killing the world by day — and then, for the rest of his life, could not land a single shot on the moon. Hou Yi is the god of the bow the way Perun is the god of the storm: his power is absolute right up until the moment it is not. He is the rare mythic hero who breaks the cosmos, is rewarded for saving it, and is defeated by something smaller than an arrow — a wife's choice, a pupil's envy, a peach bough. Chinese myth gives him every triumph a hero could want and then shows that the same pride which felled nine suns could not keep one woman safe.

Attested personality is split hard between two registers. The classical hero is obedient, decisive, and protective: he tries to reason with the suns, then intimidates, then acts; he is dispatched by Yao to clear the world of monsters and does so without complaint. The other register — the self-corrupted king — has him grow arrogant and tyrannical after being crowned, killing Hebo and seizing Luoshen, and hunting ruthlessly. Powers
Divine Archery — the Bow That Downs Suns

Yi's defining power. With the bow and arrows given to him by Di Jun (Emperor Jun — Birrell 1993, 314), he shot nine suns out of the sky, each falling sun manifesting on earth as a dead three-legged golden crow. This is not ordinary marksmanship but a world-calibrating act: it repaired the cosmos by removing nine-tenths of its light.

Limit: He is bound by heaven's politics and by his own temperament. He spares the last sun at the plea of King Yao and the sun-mother Xihe (or because the final arrow is stolen). His arrows reach gods (Hebo) but cannot stop Chang'e's ascent — he fires at the fleeing goddess in the tyrant version and misses, and he is eventually killed by a lowly apprentice. The bow is a gift, not an innate right; its power does not immunise him from mortal, material fate.

Master Hunter and Giant-Slayer (the Six Menaces)

Directed by Emperor Yao to rid the land of monsters, Yi slew, maimed, or imprisoned the Yayu, Zaochi, Jiuying, Dafeng, Fengxi, and Xiushe (the last read as the giant serpent Bashe). This made him the empire's pest-control champion in the burning age.

Limit: Each quarry was a genuine threat requiring bespoke tactics; the list is discrete (six named beasts), not open-ended, and shows him as a specialist dispatched on missions rather than a universal dominator.

Xian / God-of-Archery Status (Disputed Divinity)

He is 'sometimes portrayed as a god of archery or a xian descended from heaven to aid mankind', and alternately 'half-divine' or 'fully mortal'. As a xian he partakes of immortality lore (the elixir, flight, the celestial court) yet is denied lasting immortality by the very tale that defines him.

Limit: The xian status is partial and reversible — he never secures the elixir, and the attempted permanent immortality passes to his wife. His divinity is a contested ranking, not a stable quality.

Elixir of Immortality (Possessed, Never Consumed)

He obtains the pill of immortality from the gods (or from Xiwangmu, the Queen Mother of the West). Rather than take it alone he keeps it for himself and his wife, but it is consumed by Chang'e, who ascends to the moon when she drinks it.

Limit: Its one use is stolen/denied to him. In the hero-king reading he is left permanently mortal and dies of grief; in the tyrant reading his immortality is forestalled because Chang'e acts to stop an immortal tyrant.

Autonomy to Spare/Condemn Heavenly Kin

By sparing the tenth sun (or being forced to), Yi effectively arbitrates the world's daylight — the first, and for the archive the starkest, illustration that even the children of the highest gods answer to an archer's discretion and to the needs of mortal prosperity.

Limit: His discretion is bounded: heaven's authorities (Yao, Xihe) intercede, and the cosmic order does not let him finish the job.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Classical and Han-art depictions show Hou Yi as the archetypal hunter-archer: a powerful man drawing the great red bow of heaven, a quiver of white-feathered arrows at his back, occasionally wreathed or levitating as befits his dual status as both xian and mortal. Domains — Mortal Yangtze-Huanghe culture-hero zone, tied to the Xia court, the Yellow River, and the realm beneath the ten suns. His hunt spans the charged 'burning age' landscape of a scorched earth. His most enduring habitat is the astronomical/Mid-Autumn axis: he belongs to the sky that he emptied and, post-Chang'e, to the moon he can never reach. Accounts I
The Ten Suns and the World-Girdling Shot (Houyi Shooting the Sun)Chu Ci; Huainanzi; Shanhaijing (Di Jun/Xihe frame); Mid-Autumn and folk cycles.

Legendary antiquity (classical sources; framed under Emperor Yao's reign)

There were originally ten suns — in the classical account the sons of Di Jun and Xihe, three-legged crows that roosted in the Fusang mulberry tree at the edge of the world, taking turns to cross the sky on a chariot Xihe drove. One day all ten rose at once to play together, scorching the earth, boiling rivers, charring crops, and driving the plants and creatures to the verge of death. The emperor Yao tasked Yi (Hou Yi) to rein them in. Yi first reasoned with the suns, then feigned shooting to intimidate them. When they refused to heed him, he shot them one by one, dropping nine as dead golden crows; only one remained. Spared at the entreaty of Yao and Xihe — or because a brave boy, or Yao himself, stole the final arrow — the tenth sun keeps its course to this day. The event is remembered in Chinese as 'Houyi Shooting the Sun' (后羿射日), one of the nation's core folk myths. The world is re-lit and re-walked by a single sun; nine celestial siblings die; Yi's reward (the elixir) sets up the entire Chang'e tragedy; and a strand of scholarship reads the ten-suns as relic of an ancient ten-da

Chang'e and the Elixir — the Moon Flight (Chang'e Flying to the Moon, 嫦娥奔月)Guicang fragment (Zhou); Chu Ci; Mid-Autumn Festival tradition; countless literary retellings.

Zhou onward (first attested in Guicang divination fragments, before 256 BCE)

Hou Yi is rewarded for saving the world with the elixir of immortality. Unwilling to be immortal without his beloved wife, he stores it with Chang'e. While Yi hunts, his apprentice Fengmeng / Peng Meng breaks in and demands the elixir; Chang'e swallows both portions herself rather than surrender them, and is carried up past the heavens to the moon, choosing it so she may live near her husband. When Yi returns he is heartbroken, lays out her favourite fruits and cakes, and sacrifices to her. In the alternate (older) telling Chang'e 'steals' the elixir to escape Yi; in the king-fiend reading she seizes it to deny a would-be immortal tyrant, and Yi shoots at her as she flies but misses. Chang'e becomes the Moon goddess, resides in the Vast-Cold Palace (Guanghangu), and with the Moon rabbit (Yu Tu) and Wu Gang the cassia-chopper populates the moon's mythography. Her departure bequeaths China the Mid-Autumn Festival and makes 'Chang'e' the name of the Chinese lunar program.

Accounts II
Yi the Slayer of the Six MenacesClassical monster-clearing cycle (Huainanzi lineage).

Legendary antiquity (under Emperor Yao's commission)

Beyond the suns, Yi is dispatched to end a catalogue of beasts tormenting humankind: the Yayu, Zaochi, Jiuying, Dafeng, Fengxi, and Xiushe (Bashe). He slays, maims, or imprisons each in turn, restoring order to a land that the burning age and its megafauna had made ungovernable. Establishes Yi's reputation as the universal hunter who answers heaven's commission on earth; the list becomes the standard litany of his labours.

The Shooting of Hebo and the Taking of LuoshenChu Ci 'Heavenly Questions'; Wang Yi's Han commentary; Huainanzi.

Attested Warring States-Han

The Heavenly Questions records: 'The Emperor sent Hou Yi to reform the people of Xia. Why did he shoot Hebo and take his wife Luoshen?' In the Han commentator Wang Yi's fuller account, the River Lord transformed himself into a white dragon to travel the river; Yi shot out his left eye. When Hebo complained to Di (the Supreme God) and demanded Yi's death, Di sided with Yi: having 'become a beast', Hebo had forfeited the usual protections, while Yi 'acted properly'. The Huainanzi gives a different, avenging motive — that Yi shot Hebo for drowning people. Whichever the reason, the Lo River goddess Luoshen (daughter or consort of Fuxi, drowned in the Luo) ends the tale taken as Yi's own, and later Chinese poetry (notably Cao Zhi's Luoshen Fu) memorialises her. A moral paradox at the heart of Yi's myth: the same archer who defends the world by divine commission is also the one who shoots a god and seizes his consort, seeding the 'hero-turned-tyrant' register and the great love-poem tradition of Luoshen.

The Death of Yi by Feng MengChinese mythic corpus (Feng Meng article).

Classical; told alongside the Chang'e cycle

His arc ends in betrayal by his own apprentice. After Chang'e's flight, Yi resolves that his skills must outlive him and takes on Feng Meng. The apprentice becomes expert but seethes with envy — especially after a competition in which Yi kills as many geese as Feng Meng while facing the harder target. Feng Meng tries twice to ambush Yi with bow and arrow, each volley being stopped by Yi's perfect return. Finally, falling back on a club cut from a peach tree (a wood some traditions read as inimical to the archer-sage), he beats the master to death. This is why the arc which opens with the archer saving the world closes with the archer being killed by a peach bough. Yi's death by a pupil completes the tragic arc and colours the Mid-Autumn reading of Feng Meng as the man whose greedy intrusion set Chang'e's flight in motion.
