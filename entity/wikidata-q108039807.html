Who It Is

In the karst caves and black lakes of Slovenia and northern Croatia, villagers once whispered not of zmaj - the familiar Slavic dragon - but of something older and more serpentine: the Poje, or Pozoj. Part lindworm, part storm-cloud, it lay coiled around hidden water and hidden gold, waiting for a maiden, a saint, or a careless drought to call it forth.

Behavior is bestial, not personable. Pozoj is solitary, territorial, and largely dormant until disturbed. Attested motives: guarding (water source, cave entrance, buried treasure, often said to be Turkish or Roman gold), devouring (livestock, humans, especially maidens offered as sacrifice), and weather-making (its anger or flight causes storms, hail, flooding, or drought if its lake is drained). Powers
Enormous size and constricting/devouring strength

Capable of swallowing an ox, horse, or human whole; crushing with coils; blocking a spring or lake outlet with its body. Narrators emphasize its gullet and 'požirati' (to swallow).

Limit: Physically bound to water/cave; slow on dry land in some accounts; cannot pass church-consecrated ground or bell-ringing radius.

Venomous breath, slime and poisonous trail

Its breath, saliva, or the slime left behind scorches vegetation, poisons water, and sickens humans and cattle. Grass where it crawled does not grow for years.

Limit: Effect is local and temporary; can be cleansed by holy water or fire. Not always lethal, often causes illness rather than instant death.

Weather and water control

Its presence or anger causes storms, hail, cloudbursts, flooding, or conversely drought if its lake is disturbed. Linked to 'pozoj's clouds' and hail-making. Some narratives say it drinks lakes dry or makes springs disappear.

Limit: Power is tied to its habitat; if killed or driven out, weather normalizes. Kresnik or St. George can counter it. Not a universal weather god, only local hydro-meteorological disturbance.

Treasure guarding and chthonic knowledge

Guards buried treasure (often 'Turkish gold' or Roman coins) in caves or under lakes. Its hoard is cursed; taking it brings illness or storm. Sometimes said to know hidden paths through karst underground.

Limit: Cannot use treasure itself; bound to guard. Treasure can be taken if beast is killed or tricked with Christian aid.

Limited shapeshifting and illusion (disputed)

A minority of late tales (conflated with zmaj) claim it can appear as a dark cloud, log, or handsome man to lure maidens. Kropej notes this is atypical for Pozoj and likely borrowed from zmaj/vedomec traditions.

Limit: Even in these tales, transformation is imperfect (eyes remain fiery, slime remains). Holy symbols reveal true form.

Vulnerabilities and countermeasures

Vulnerable to St. George/Jurij's lance, kresnik's shamanic battle, church bells, consecrated weapons, and sunlight (in some tales it must return to water before dawn). Killing it often requires striking the head or heart, or burning the corpse to prevent return.

Limit: Countermeasures require communal ritual (procession, bell-ringing) or a designated hero; ordinary weapons often fail.

Accounts I
The Pozoj of Lake Cerknica (Cerkniško jezero)Johann Weikhard von Valvasor, Die Ehre dess Hertzogthums Crain (1689) II; retold in Kelemina 1930 and Kropej 2012 p. 104

Early modern attestation (17th century) of older oral belief; intermittent lake folklore of medieval origin

The intermittent karst lake Cerknica, which appears and disappears through sinkholes, was said to be inhabited by a huge lindworm/Pozoj that lived in the lake's caves (Velika Karlovica). When the lake drained, the beast blocked the ponors (sinkholes) or drank the water; when angered, it caused sudden flooding and storms. Valvasor reports locals believed the creature's movements explained the lake's mysterious filling and emptying, and that hunters had seen its trail. Later folk tales add that the Pozoj demanded livestock and was finally driven out by bell-ringing and prayer. Etiological explanation for karst hydrology; establishment of apotropaic bell-ringing and processions at the lake; later tourism narrative for Cerknica.

The Maiden Tribute at the CaveKelemina 1930, tale no. 24 ('Deklica in pozoj'); Kropej 2012 catalogue 3.2.7; field record GNI M 12 (Littoral)

19th-century oral collection; motif likely older (maiden sacrifice to dragon is medieval European)

A village near a cave (identified variously as Postojna, Predjama, or Križna jama) must sacrifice a maiden each year to the Pozoj that guards the spring, otherwise it poisons the water and brings hail on crops. A girl (often a miller's daughter) is offered, but is saved by St. George/Jurij, a kresnik, or a clever shepherd who uses a consecrated sword, mirror, or bell. The beast is slain, its blood poisons the ground, and the spring flows clear. Foundation legend for a church dedicated to St. George near a spring; explanation for barren patch where Pozoj's blood fell; moral about Christian triumph over pagan chthonic force.

The Shepherd Who Killed the Pozoj with a Consecrated KnifeKelemina 1930, tale no. 92; Kropej 2012 p. 108

19th-century oral

A Pozoj living in a swamp below the pasture devours sheep and a shepherd boy. An old shepherd, advised by a priest, has his knife blessed at three churches, waits until the beast sleeps at noon (when it basks), and cuts off its head. The corpse must be burned, and its ashes buried under a cross, otherwise two new Pozoji grow from its blood. The shepherd is rewarded but must leave the pasture because the ground remains poisonous for seven years. Etiology for a barren swamp patch and a wayside cross; illustration of Christian folk magic (blessing, triple consecration) as countermeasure.

Accounts II
The Pozoj and the Kresnik's Storm BattleKropej 2012 pp. 105-107; Šmitek 2004 p. 112; field records of kresnik vs. pozoj battles (Istria, Vipava)

Oral tradition recorded 19th-20th century; cosmology considered pre-Christian substratum

On stormy nights, the kresnik (shamanic protector, born with caul) leaves his body as a white animal or light and battles the Pozoj, who appears as a black cloud, black animal, or serpent, for control of hail and rain. If the kresnik wins, crops are saved; if the Pozoj wins, hail destroys the fields. Villagers support the kresnik by banging tools and ringing bells. This is not a single narrative but a recurring mythological scenario narrated as personal experience by kresnik claimants. Explains hail and crop failure; reinforces social role of kresnik as community protector; demonstrates dualistic cosmology (light/dry vs. dark/wet).

The Treasure of the Pozoj's CaveKelemina 1930, tale no. 57 ('Zaklad in pozoj'); Kropej 2012 p. 106; SEM archive E 2890 notes

19th-century oral

A cave near Predjama or in the Kočevje forest is said to hold Turkish gold guarded by a Pozoj coiled around it. A greedy man tries to steal the gold while the beast sleeps, but the Pozoj awakens, pursues him as a rolling fire or slime trail, and he only escapes by throwing consecrated bread or crossing running water. Another variant: a poor man is shown the treasure in a dream by the Pozoj, but is told he may take only what he can carry without looking back; he looks back and the gold turns to leaves or the cave mouth closes. Warning against greed; etiology for inaccessible cave chambers and for 'fool's gold' (leaves) motifs; reinforces taboo on disturbing chthonic guardians.

St. George (Sv. Jurij) and the Pozoj of Ljubljana MarshSynthetic tale combining Valvasor 1689 (dragon of Ljubljana), Kelemina 1930 introduction, and modern civic legend; analyzed in Kropej 2012 pp. 111-112

Medieval hagiographic motif retrojected onto local marsh; 19th-century folklorization

The Ljubljana Marsh (Ljubljansko barje), a former lake, was said to be inhabited by a Pozoj that made the marsh impassable and poisoned cattle. St. George, patron of Ljubljana, battles the beast at the marsh edge, spears it, and the city is freed. The slain beast's body becomes the Ljubljana Dragon, later adopted as the city's heraldic emblem (13th century city seal, later 1901 Dragon Bridge). Folklorists note this is a learned conflation: the civic 'zmaj' is not called Pozoj in medieval charters, but folk etymology links them. Origin myth for Ljubljana's dragon emblem; Christianization narrative; modern tourism identity. Kropej flags this as an example of how Pozoj motifs were absorbed into civic heraldry and then re-folklorized.

Poje/Pozoj is the same as the Ljubljana Dragon (Zmaj) on the city coat of arms. — The civic Zmaj is a heraldic symbol (13th century seal, 1901 bridge) that absorbed some Pozoj/lindworm iconography but is not called Pozoj in medieval sources. Folk Pozoj is a feared, poisonous, water-bound lindworm; the civic dragon is a stylized, winged heraldic beast and modern mascot. Conflating them erases the folk distinction Kropej emphasizes.

Pozoj breathes fire like a Western dragon. — Earliest attested Pozoj traits are poison, slime, and weather, not fire-breathing. Fire-breathing belongs to the zmaj or to literary dragons and was retrojected onto Pozoj in late 20th-century children's media.

Poje is an ancient pan-Slavic god or demon known across all Slavic countries. — Pozoj/Poje is a regional South Slavic (Slovene/northern Croatian) folk belief, not a pan-Slavic deity. No evidence in East or West Slavic medieval sources; its distribution is limited to the eastern Alps and northern Dinaric karst.

There is a rich, distinct mythology of 'Poje' separate from 'Pozoj' with its own stories. — No corpus of stories indexed under the exact form 'Poje' was located.
