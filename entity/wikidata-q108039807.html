The Approach

In the karst caves and black lakes of Slovenia and northern Croatia, villagers once whispered not of zmaj - the familiar Slavic dragon - but of something older and more serpentine: the Poje, or Pozoj. Part lindworm, part storm-cloud, it lay coiled around hidden water and hidden gold, waiting for a maiden, a saint, or a careless drought to call it forth.

Content advisory. Folklore contains motifs of maiden abduction, human sacrifice/devouring, drowning, and storm-related destruction. No explicit sexual violence is core to the Poje tradition, but some variant tales involve coercive abduction for marriage. Dark history entries discuss these plainly.
Identity

Poje

Pronunciation Slovene/Croatian: PO-yeh / po-ZOY [ˈpɔːjɛ] ~ [pɔˈzɔj]; Slovene standard 'pozoj' [pɔˈzɔːj]; dialectal 'pojej' [pɔˈjeːj]. The Wikidata form 'Poje' would be pronounced /ˈpoːjɛ/ in Slovene orthography.

Etymology

Standard Slovene and Kajkavian Croatian form is 'pozoj' (pronounced po-ZOY). Etymology is disputed and not securely attested. Kropej (2012) and Bezlaj's Etimološki slovar slovenskega jezika note no consensus: hypotheses include derivation from Proto-Slavic *pozobati / *po- + *zoj (related to 'to devour, gorge'), or folk-etymological association with 'pojati' (to devour) / 'požirati' (to swallow), reflecting the creature's devouring nature. The form 'Poje' appears to be a shortened, dialectal or orthographic variant without separate etymology; no independent etymological study of 'Poje' as distinct from 'Pozoj' was located. All etymologies should be treated as reconstructed/disputed.

Cultural Ownership

Slovene and northern Croatian (Kajkavian, Istrian, Littoral, Carniolan) folk tradition, within broader South Slavic and Alpine Slavic dragon/lindworm complex. Attested primarily in Slovenian folk narrative collections (19th-20th century) and in Carniolan early modern chorography (Valvasor). Not a pan-Slavic deity but a regional demonological beast. Living tradition is memorialized rather than actively worshipped; care is needed not to conflate with the heraldic 'Ljubljana Dragon' (Zmaj) which is a later civic symbol partially derived from Pozoj motifs.

Disputed Identifications

1) Whether Poje/Pozoj is distinct from 'zmaj' (zmaj, dragon) and 'aždaja/aždaha' (aždaja, often a multi-headed dragon) or simply a local name for the same category. Kropej (2012) argues Pozoj is a specific subtype: more serpentine, chthonic, water-bound, often without the zmaj's anthropomorphic or heroic traits. Popular sources collapse them. 2) Whether Pozoj is a lindworm (two-legged, wingless) vs. full dragon - descriptions vary. 3) Wikidata Q108039807 isolates 'Poje' as a separate entity, but no scholarly source treats 'Poje' as separate from 'Pozoj'; this appears to be a database duplication/variant entry, not a distinct folklore figure. Flagged as disputed identification.

Description

Appearance

Attested descriptions (Kropej 2012; Kelemina 1930) consistently portray Pozoj/Poje as a huge serpentine beast, not the winged, fire-breathing zmaj of high folklore. Most accounts: body of an enormous snake or lindworm, 3-10 meters long, thick as a tree trunk or wine barrel, with a heavy, often horned or crested head, glowing or fiery eyes, and a wide, devouring maw. Some variants add two short forelegs or chicken-like feet (lindworm trait), a comb or crown, or a beard. Scales are described as dark, black-green, or metallic, sometimes shining like copper. Wings are generally absent in older folk accounts; when present (later, under influence of literary dragons), they are membranous and small. A distinctive trait is its association with water and slime: it is said to be slimy, to leave a burning or poisonous trail, and to dwell in wet darkness. No canonical anthropomorphic form; shapeshifting into human is attributed to zmaj, not typically to Pozoj, though a few late tales conflate them. All details are attested in variant oral records, not a single canon.

Symbols & Iconography

Symbols: water (lake, swamp, spring, karst cave), darkness and the chthonic underworld, treasure/gold, storm-cloud, the devouring mouth. Iconography is sparse before the 19th century; folk carvings show a coiled serpent with a crowned head. Modern civic iconography (Ljubljana Dragon Bridge, 1901, architect Jurij Zaninović, sculptor Zaninović/Vodnik workshop) visually quotes the Pozoj/lindworm but is labeled 'zmaj' for city heraldry. Apotropaic marks (crosses, St. George) are paired with Pozoj to signal Christian dominance over the pagan/chthonic beast. No medieval manuscript illumination securely labeled 'pozoj' has been identified; Valvasor includes engravings of lindwurms in Carniola that serve as early iconographic evidence.

Behaviour & Personality

Behavior is bestial, not personable. Pozoj is solitary, territorial, and largely dormant until disturbed. Attested motives: guarding (water source, cave entrance, buried treasure, often said to be Turkish or Roman gold), devouring (livestock, humans, especially maidens offered as sacrifice), and weather-making (its anger or flight causes storms, hail, flooding, or drought if its lake is drained). It is not a trickster or teacher; it does not bargain except in a few tales where it demands regular tribute. It is vulnerable to Christian symbols, church bells, and St. George/Jurij, and can be driven out or killed by a hero, often with help from a kresnik (shamanic opponent of chthonic forces). Personality descriptors from narrators: 'požrešen' (gluttonous), 'strašen' (terrible), 'zvit' (cunning) in the sense of hiding. No evidence of a benevolent Pozoj; helper-dragon roles belong to zmaj in other traditions (Kropej distinguishes this).

Domains & Habitats

Domains: chthonic water and karst. Habitats attested: intermittent lakes (Lake Cerknica/Cerkniško jezero is the locus classicus), swamps, springs, deep pools in the Soča, Sava and Drava basins, caves (Postojna, Predjama, Križna jama), and marshy forests. It is also a weather domain being: linked to clouds, hail, and the 'pozoj's wind' that precedes storms. Cosmology: in Slovene folk cosmology, Pozoj belongs to the lower, wet, dark world opposed to the bright, dry world of kresnik and the Christian sky. It is not a ruler of a realm but a guardian of thresholds (water/land, surface/underworld).

Version Differences

Regional variation is significant. Littoral/Istrian versions emphasize a marine or lake serpent that blocks water. Upper Carniolan versions emphasize a cave lindworm with legs. Kajkavian Croatian versions (Međimurje, Zagorje) use 'pozoj' for a dragon that lives in old willows or barns and brings hail. In some late 20th-century retellings, Pozoj is fully conflated with 'zmaj' and given wings and fire-breathing, which Kropej flags as adaptation-specific modernization. No evidence for a distinct 'Poje' version separate from these; the form 'Poje' likely reflects a dialectal phonetic reduction (pozoj > pojej > poje) in western Slovene dialects. Treated here as version difference, not separate entity.

Powers & Abilities

Enormous size and constricting/devouring strength

Source VersionKelemina 1930 tale nos. 24, 92; Kropej 2012 p. 101; Valvasor 1689 on Cerknica

Capable of swallowing an ox, horse, or human whole; crushing with coils; blocking a spring or lake outlet with its body. Narrators emphasize its gullet and 'požirati' (to swallow).

Limitations

Physically bound to water/cave; slow on dry land in some accounts; cannot pass church-consecrated ground or bell-ringing radius.

Contradictions

Some tales give it legs and agility on land; others make it helpless outside water.

Venomous breath, slime and poisonous trail

Source VersionKropej 2012 p. 103; field record GNI M 12; Kelemina 1930 p. 33

Its breath, saliva, or the slime left behind scorches vegetation, poisons water, and sickens humans and cattle. Grass where it crawled does not grow for years.

Limitations

Effect is local and temporary; can be cleansed by holy water or fire. Not always lethal, often causes illness rather than instant death.

Contradictions

Older accounts stress poison; later literary accounts replace poison with fire-breathing, which is not attested in earliest oral records.

Weather and water control

Source VersionValvasor 1689 on Cerknica intermittent lake; Kropej 2012 pp. 105-107; Šmitek 2004 on karst mythology

Its presence or anger causes storms, hail, cloudbursts, flooding, or conversely drought if its lake is disturbed. Linked to 'pozoj's clouds' and hail-making. Some narratives say it drinks lakes dry or makes springs disappear.

Limitations

Power is tied to its habitat; if killed or driven out, weather normalizes. Kresnik or St. George can counter it. Not a universal weather god, only local hydro-meteorological disturbance.

Contradictions

Whether Pozoj causes storms actively or is merely an omen of them varies by narrator.

Treasure guarding and chthonic knowledge

Source VersionKelemina 1930 tale no. 57; Kropej 2012 p. 106

Guards buried treasure (often 'Turkish gold' or Roman coins) in caves or under lakes. Its hoard is cursed; taking it brings illness or storm. Sometimes said to know hidden paths through karst underground.

Limitations

Cannot use treasure itself; bound to guard. Treasure can be taken if beast is killed or tricked with Christian aid.

Contradictions

Some tales treat treasure as its own, others as merely incidental to its lair.

Limited shapeshifting and illusion (disputed)

Source VersionKropej 2012 p. 110 (notes conflation); Kelemina 1930 variant in Littoral

A minority of late tales (conflated with zmaj) claim it can appear as a dark cloud, log, or handsome man to lure maidens. Kropej notes this is atypical for Pozoj and likely borrowed from zmaj/vedomec traditions.

Limitations

Even in these tales, transformation is imperfect (eyes remain fiery, slime remains). Holy symbols reveal true form.

Contradictions

Most core Pozoj accounts deny shapeshifting entirely, describing it as purely bestial.

Vulnerabilities and countermeasures

Source VersionKropej 2012 pp. 108-112; Valvasor 1689; folk prayers against pozoj recorded in SEM archives

Vulnerable to St. George/Jurij's lance, kresnik's shamanic battle, church bells, consecrated weapons, and sunlight (in some tales it must return to water before dawn). Killing it often requires striking the head or heart, or burning the corpse to prevent return.

Limitations

Countermeasures require communal ritual (procession, bell-ringing) or a designated hero; ordinary weapons often fail.

Contradictions

Whether it can be killed permanently or merely driven to another lake/cave varies.

Stories & Myths

The Pozoj of Lake Cerknica (Cerkniško jezero)

Source Johann Weikhard von Valvasor, Die Ehre dess Hertzogthums Crain (1689) II; retold in Kelemina 1930 and Kropej 2012 p. 104Culture Slovene (Inner Carniola, Notranjska)Period Early modern attestation (17th century) of older oral belief; intermittent lake folklore of medieval origin

Summary

The intermittent karst lake Cerknica, which appears and disappears through sinkholes, was said to be inhabited by a huge lindworm/Pozoj that lived in the lake's caves (Velika Karlovica). When the lake drained, the beast blocked the ponors (sinkholes) or drank the water; when angered, it caused sudden flooding and storms. Valvasor reports locals believed the creature's movements explained the lake's mysterious filling and emptying, and that hunters had seen its trail. Later folk tales add that the Pozoj demanded livestock and was finally driven out by bell-ringing and prayer.

Participants

Pozoj (lake lindworm)

Lake Cerknica villagers

Hunters/fishermen

Clergy (bell-ringers)

Consequences

Etiological explanation for karst hydrology; establishment of apotropaic bell-ringing and processions at the lake; later tourism narrative for Cerknica.

Variants

Some variants make the beast a giant eel or fish; others call it 'jezerni zmaj'. In one 19th-century variant recorded by Kelemina, the Pozoj is killed by a kresnik who battles it in cloud form.

The Maiden Tribute at the Cave

Source Kelemina 1930, tale no. 24 ('Deklica in pozoj'); Kropej 2012 catalogue 3.2.7; field record GNI M 12 (Littoral)Culture Slovene (Littoral and Karst)Period 19th-century oral collection; motif likely older (maiden sacrifice to dragon is medieval European)

Summary

A village near a cave (identified variously as Postojna, Predjama, or Križna jama) must sacrifice a maiden each year to the Pozoj that guards the spring, otherwise it poisons the water and brings hail on crops. A girl (often a miller's daughter) is offered, but is saved by St. George/Jurij, a kresnik, or a clever shepherd who uses a consecrated sword, mirror, or bell. The beast is slain, its blood poisons the ground, and the spring flows clear.

Participants

Pozoj

Maiden (victim)

Village elders

Rescuer (St. George / kresnik / shepherd)

Spring/water source

Consequences

Foundation legend for a church dedicated to St. George near a spring; explanation for barren patch where Pozoj's blood fell; moral about Christian triumph over pagan chthonic force.

Variants

In some variants the tribute is livestock, not a maiden; in others the maiden is not killed but abducted to the cave as the beast's bride, and must be rescued. Kropej notes the 'bride' variant shows zmaj conflation.

The Shepherd Who Killed the Pozoj with a Consecrated Knife

Source Kelemina 1930, tale no. 92; Kropej 2012 p. 108Culture Slovene (Upper Carniola)Period 19th-century oral

Summary

A Pozoj living in a swamp below the pasture devours sheep and a shepherd boy. An old shepherd, advised by a priest, has his knife blessed at three churches, waits until the beast sleeps at noon (when it basks), and cuts off its head. The corpse must be burned, and its ashes buried under a cross, otherwise two new Pozoji grow from its blood. The shepherd is rewarded but must leave the pasture because the ground remains poisonous for seven years.

Participants

Pozoj (swamp lindworm)

Shepherd hero

Priest

Flock/villagers

Consequences

Etiology for a barren swamp patch and a wayside cross; illustration of Christian folk magic (blessing, triple consecration) as countermeasure.

Variants

Variant in Croatian Zagorje makes the hero a charcoal burner; another variant has the Pozoj's head continue to snap after decapitation.

The Pozoj and the Kresnik's Storm Battle

Source Kropej 2012 pp. 105-107; Šmitek 2004 p. 112; field records of kresnik vs. pozoj battles (Istria, Vipava)Culture Slovene (Istria, Vipava Valley, Karst)Period Oral tradition recorded 19th-20th century; cosmology considered pre-Christian substratum

Summary

On stormy nights, the kresnik (shamanic protector, born with caul) leaves his body as a white animal or light and battles the Pozoj, who appears as a black cloud, black animal, or serpent, for control of hail and rain. If the kresnik wins, crops are saved; if the Pozoj wins, hail destroys the fields. Villagers support the kresnik by banging tools and ringing bells. This is not a single narrative but a recurring mythological scenario narrated as personal experience by kresnik claimants.

Participants

Pozoj (as black cloud/serpent)

Kresnik (white protector)

Villagers (ritual supporters)

Crops/hail

Consequences

Explains hail and crop failure; reinforces social role of kresnik as community protector; demonstrates dualistic cosmology (light/dry vs. dark/wet).

Variants

In some villages the opponent is called 'štrigon' or 'vedomec' rather than Pozoj; in others the battle is between two kresniki. Kropej stresses Pozoj is one of several chthonic opponents, not the only one.

The Treasure of the Pozoj's Cave

Source Kelemina 1930, tale no. 57 ('Zaklad in pozoj'); Kropej 2012 p. 106; SEM archive E 2890 notesCulture Slovene (Notranjska, Dolenjska)Period 19th-century oral

Summary

A cave near Predjama or in the Kočevje forest is said to hold Turkish gold guarded by a Pozoj coiled around it. A greedy man tries to steal the gold while the beast sleeps, but the Pozoj awakens, pursues him as a rolling fire or slime trail, and he only escapes by throwing consecrated bread or crossing running water. Another variant: a poor man is shown the treasure in a dream by the Pozoj, but is told he may take only what he can carry without looking back; he looks back and the gold turns to leaves or the cave mouth closes.

Participants

Pozoj (treasure guardian)

Treasure seeker (poor man / greedy man)

Dream sender / priest (adviser)

Consequences

Warning against greed; etiology for inaccessible cave chambers and for 'fool's gold' (leaves) motifs; reinforces taboo on disturbing chthonic guardians.

Variants

In one variant the treasure is not gold but the Pozoj's own egg or crown, which grants wealth but brings hail. In another, the Pozoj is already dead and its skeleton guards the hoard.

St. George (Sv. Jurij) and the Pozoj of Ljubljana Marsh

Source Synthetic tale combining Valvasor 1689 (dragon of Ljubljana), Kelemina 1930 introduction, and modern civic legend; analyzed in Kropej 2012 pp. 111-112Culture Slovene (Ljubljana Basin)Period Medieval hagiographic motif retrojected onto local marsh; 19th-century folklorization

Summary

The Ljubljana Marsh (Ljubljansko barje), a former lake, was said to be inhabited by a Pozoj that made the marsh impassable and poisoned cattle. St. George, patron of Ljubljana, battles the beast at the marsh edge, spears it, and the city is freed. The slain beast's body becomes the Ljubljana Dragon, later adopted as the city's heraldic emblem (13th century city seal, later 1901 Dragon Bridge). Folklorists note this is a learned conflation: the civic 'zmaj' is not called Pozoj in medieval charters, but folk etymology links them.

Participants

Pozoj (marsh dragon)

St. George / Sv. Jurij

Citizens of Ljubljana

Marsh/water

Consequences

Origin myth for Ljubljana's dragon emblem; Christianization narrative; modern tourism identity. Kropej flags this as an example of how Pozoj motifs were absorbed into civic heraldry and then re-folklorized.

Variants

Some tellings replace St. George with Jason and the Argonauts (humanist invention by Valvasor linking Ljubljana to Emona); others make the hero a kresnik.

Relationships
Poje→Zmaj (Slavic dragon)related

Zmaj is the broader, more anthropomorphic South Slavic dragon, often with wings, fire, and ability to shapeshift into a man. Pozoj is considered by scholars (Kropej 2012) a regional, more bestial, water-bound subtype or local name for the same semantic field. Folk narrators often use the terms interchangeably, but ethnologists distinguish them: zmaj can be helper or lover, Pozoj never.

Poje→Aždaja / Aždaharelated

Aždaja (from Persian aždahā) is the multi-headed, often nine-headed dragon of South Slavic epic and folk tales, more monstrous and often associated with holding back waters. Pozoj and Aždaja overlap as water-blocking serpents, but Aždaja is typically larger, multi-headed, and appears in epic songs, while Pozoj appears in local legends and belief narratives.

Poje→Kresnik (shamanic protector)hostile

Kresnik is the hereditary protector (born with caul, in shirt) who battles chthonic beings including Pozoj for weather and fertility. The relationship is cosmological enmity: white/dry/bright vs. black/wet/dark. Kresnik's soul leaves body to fight Pozoj in storm-cloud form.

Poje→St. George / Sv. Jurijhostile

Christian dragon-slayer saint who replaces or assists kresnik in later, Christianized variants. Churches dedicated to St. George are often built on former Pozoj sites (springs, cave mouths) to signify dominance. Bell-ringing and processions on St. George's Day (April 23) are apotropaic against Pozoj.

Poje→Vile (fairies) and other water spiritsrelated

Vile, povodni mož (water man), and rusalke share water habitats with Pozoj but are not allies. Folklore places them in same lakes/caves, sometimes as victims or as warners: vile may warn villagers of Pozoj's anger, or be driven away by its poison.

Poje→Human community (villagers, shepherds, maidens)victim

The primary human relationship is as victim/tribute-giver and later as dragon-slayer community. Villagers offer livestock or maidens, suffer poisoned water and hail, and collectively perform apotropaic rituals (bell-ringing, processions, consecrated weapons). Maidens are specifically targeted in sacrifice/abduction motifs.

Poje→Treasure / Gold (Turkish gold, Roman coins)ownership

Pozoj is guardian of chthonic treasure, often described as Turkish gold left after Ottoman raids or Roman hoard. The treasure is cursed and cannot be taken without Christian aid. This links Pozoj to broader European dragon-guardian motif.

Maiden sacrifice and abduction

Source Kelemina 1930 no. 24; Kropej 2012 pp. 108-110; comparative motif ATU 300 (Dragon Slayer)

Description

Several Pozoj legends require annual maiden tribute to keep water flowing or hail away, or describe the beast abducting a girl to its cave as a 'bride'. The motif mirrors broader European dragon-maiden sacrifice tales. No historical evidence of actual human sacrifice to Pozoj exists; scholars (Kropej 2012) treat it as narrative dramatization of anxieties about water, fertility, and patriarchal control of women's bodies.

Scholarly Context

Kropej and Šmitek read the motif as symbolic, not historical, reflecting Christian vs. chthonic dualism and social control. Modern retellings sometimes sanitize the abduction as romance; original tales emphasize terror and coercion.

Warning: Poisoning and environmental devastation — Show unsanitized · Archive policy & legal

Poisoning and environmental devastation

Source Kropej 2012 p. 103; field record GNI M 12; Valvasor 1689

Description

Pozoj's slime, breath, or blood poisons springs, kills livestock, and renders fields barren for years (seven years is a common formula). This reflects real karst anxieties: water contamination and crop failure after floods. Tales describe children sickening after drinking from a Pozoj spring.

Scholarly Context

Ethnologists interpret poisoning motif as folk explanation for waterborne illness and karst hydrology, not as supernatural sadism. No sanitization needed, but should not be romanticized as 'guardian of nature'.

Warning: Colonial and nationalist distortion — Show unsanitized · Archive policy & legal

Colonial and nationalist distortion

Source Kropej 2012 introduction; analysis of Kelemina's editorial practice; Valvasor's natural-historical framing

Description

19th-century nationalist collectors (including Kelemina's sources) sometimes edited Pozoj tales to emphasize Slovene antiquity or to Christianize the beast more thoroughly, removing ambiguous or pagan elements. Valvasor (1689), writing for a German-speaking Habsburg audience, framed the lindworm as a natural curiosity to be catalogued, downplaying Slovene folk cosmology. Later Yugoslav tourism repurposed the Pozoj/Zmaj as a cute civic mascot, erasing its terrifying aspects.

Scholarly Context

Scholars flag these layers as distorting: the 'cute dragon' of Ljubljana souvenirs is a sanitized adaptation, not the folk Pozoj. Responsible retelling should note the sanitization.

Contradictions & Disputes

Is Poje distinct from Pozoj?

Position A

Poje is a distinct entity, separate from Pozoj, as implied by Wikidata Q108039807 as a separate item.

Position A — evidence

Wikidata separation and the existence of the string 'Poje' in some wordlists; no other evidence located.

Position B

Poje is simply a dialectal/orthographic variant of Pozoj (pozoj > pojej > poje), not a separate folklore figure; no scholarly source treats it as distinct.

Position B — evidence

All scholarly corpora (Kelemina 1930, Kropej 2012, Šmitek 2004) use 'pozoj/pozoj/pojej' and list 'poje' only as a phonetic variant if at all; no tale is indexed under 'Poje' alone; field archives show dialectal reduction in western Slovene dialects.

Scholarly Consensus

Consensus supports Position B. This dossier treats Poje as variant of Pozoj and flags the Wikidata separation as a database artifact, not a folklore distinction.

Appearance: lindworm vs. winged dragon

Position A

Pozoj is a wingless lindworm/serpent with at most two small legs, poisonous, water-bound.

Position A — evidence

Oldest oral records (Valvasor 1689, Kelemina's 19th-century field records, Kropej's catalogue) describe wingless, slimy, poisonous serpent; wings and fire are absent.

Position B

Pozoj is a full winged, fire-breathing dragon identical to zmaj.

Position B — evidence

Later 20th-century retellings, children's books, and tourism art (Dragon Bridge) show winged dragons labeled pozoj/zmaj interchangeably; some late field records conflate terms.

Scholarly Consensus

Scholars (Kropej 2012) argue Position A is the traditional form; Position B is modern conflation/adaptation. Both are documented as variants, but should not be harmonized.

Moral alignment: purely evil vs. ambivalent guardian

Position A

Pozoj is purely malevolent: devouring, poisoning, hail-bringing, to be killed.

Position A — evidence

Tales of maiden tribute, poisoned water, and kresnik battles frame it as enemy to be destroyed with Christian aid.

Position B

Pozoj is an ambivalent chthonic guardian, part of natural order, not evil but dangerous if disturbed.

Position B — evidence

Some ethnological interpretation (Hrobat Virloget, Šmitek) reads Pozoj as embodiment of karst water power - dangerous but not morally evil, guarding thresholds; killing it can cause drought, suggesting ecological balance.

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus; both readings are attested. Folk narrators tend to Position A; modern ecocritical scholarship leans to Position B. Dossier presents both without harmonizing.

Primary Evidence

Johann Weikhard von Valvasor - Die Ehre dess Hertzogthums Crain (The Glory of the Duchy of Carniola)

Type textDate 1689Locator Book II, chapters on Karst lakes and dragons/lindwurms of Carniola; esp. discussion of the dragon of Lake Cerknica and lindworms in caves (pp. 697-704 in 1689 Nuremberg edition)Language German (Early New High German), with Latin and Slovene toponyms

Translation

No complete modern English translation; partial Slovene translation Slava vojvodine Kranjske (1950s). Valvasor reports local belief in huge serpents/dragons inhabiting Lake Cerknica and karst caves, capable of causing weather disturbances. He treats them as natural-historical reports, not pure myth, providing earliest written attestation of the Pozoj-type belief in Carniola.

Jakob Kelemina - Bajke in pripovedke slovenskega ljudstva z mitološkim uvodom (Fairy Tales and Folk Tales of the Slovene People with a Mythological Introduction)
