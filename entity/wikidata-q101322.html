The Approach

In the darkest depths beneath the world of the living, where even the gods fear to tread, lies Tartarus—a primeval abyss and a prison of unimaginable horror. It is both a cosmic entity and a place, a chasm older than the Olympians, where the most dangerous enemies of the divine order are cast to suffer for eternity. To understand Tartarus is to confront the ancient Greek imagination of the ultimate abyss, a realm that shaped their myths of punishment, cosmic justice, and the terrifying power of the primordial forces that predate creation itself.

Content advisory. Contains themes of imprisonment, eternal punishment, torture, and cosmic violence as depicted in ancient Greek mythology. Some descriptions of suffering may be disturbing to modern readers.
Identity

Tartarus

Pronunciation /ˈtɑːrtərəs/ (US), /ˈtɑːtərəs/ (UK)

Known As

TartarosTartara

Etymology

The etymology of Tartarus is uncertain. Some ancient sources derived it from the Greek verb 'tarassein' (to disturb, to throw into confusion), but this is likely a folk etymology. Modern scholars have proposed connections to the Hittite or Hurrian word 'tartaru' (underworld), but this is speculative. The name may also be related to the Greek word 'tartaros' meaning 'that which is below Hades'.

Cultural Ownership

Ancient Greek, later adopted by Roman culture as a synonym for the underworld's deepest prison.

Disputed Identifications

Tartarus is sometimes conflated with Hades (the underworld realm) or with the Christian concept of Hell. In Orphic and later traditions, Tartarus is personified as a primordial deity, but in mainstream Greek mythology, it is primarily a place. The distinction between Tartarus as a place and as a god is a point of scholarly debate.

Description

Appearance

Tartarus is described as a dark, misty, and gloomy abyss, located as far beneath Hades as the earth is beneath the sky. Hesiod says that a bronze anvil would take nine days and nights to fall from the earth to Tartarus (Theogony 720-725). It is surrounded by a bronze wall and gates of bronze, with a root of silver (Theogony 726-733). Plato describes it as a vast cavern with rivers of fire and mud (Phaedo 111c-114c). In later sources, it is often depicted as a fiery place of torment, though early descriptions emphasize cold and darkness.

Symbols & Iconography

Tartarus is symbolically associated with the abyss, imprisonment, and cosmic punishment. In ancient art, it is rarely depicted directly, but scenes of the punishment of Titans or Tityos often imply its setting. The bronze wall and gates symbolize its inescapable nature.

Behaviour & Personality

When personified, Tartarus is a primordial deity, one of the first beings to exist after Chaos. He has no distinct personality or myths of his own; he is primarily a passive cosmic entity. As a place, Tartarus is a prison and a realm of punishment, impersonal and unchanging.

Domains & Habitats

Tartarus is the deepest region of the underworld, beneath Hades itself. It is the prison of the Titans, the Hecatoncheires, and other enemies of the gods. It is also the home of the Giants (in some accounts) and the place where the most heinous mortal sinners are punished after death.

Version Differences

In early Greek sources (Hesiod, Homer), Tartarus is primarily a place, a primordial abyss. Later Orphic tradition personifies Tartarus as a deity, the offspring of Aether and Hemera (or Chaos). Roman authors like Virgil and Ovid emphasize Tartarus as a place of punishment for sinners, incorporating it into a more elaborate afterlife geography. In Christian reception, Tartarus is often equated with Hell, though this is a later reinterpretation.

Powers & Abilities

Imprisonment

Source Version Hesiod, Theogony; Homer, Iliad

Description

Tartarus has the ability to hold even the most powerful beings, including Titans and Giants. It is described as inescapable, with bronze gates and walls.

Limitations

The prison is only effective when the prisoners are bound by the gods; it is not inherently sentient.

Contradictions

Some myths, such as the release of the Titans in Orphic tradition, suggest that prisoners can be freed, contradicting the idea of inescapability.

Primordial Existence

Source Version Hesiod, Theogony; Apollodorus, Bibliotheca

Description

As a primordial entity, Tartarus is one of the first beings to exist, emerging from Chaos. He is a generative force, fathering Typhon with Gaia.

Limitations

Tartarus is not an active deity; he does not intervene in mortal affairs.

Contradictions

The Orphic tradition places Tartarus as a later being, born from Aether and Hemera, conflicting with Hesiod's account.

Stories & Myths

The Birth of Tartarus

Source Hesiod, Theogony 116-124Culture Ancient GreekPeriod c. 700 BCE

Summary

In the beginning, Chaos came into being, followed by Earth (Gaia), Tartarus, and Eros. Tartarus is thus one of the first entities to exist, a primordial abyss beneath the earth.

Participants

Chaos

Gaia

Tartarus

Eros

Consequences

Tartarus becomes the foundation of the cosmos, a deep pit below the earth where later cosmic conflicts will be resolved.

Variants

In Orphic cosmogony, Tartarus is born from Aether and Hemera, not Chaos.

The Imprisonment of the Titans

Source Hesiod, Theogony 720-819Culture Ancient GreekPeriod c. 700 BCE

Summary

After the Titanomachy, Zeus and the Olympians cast the Titans into Tartarus. The Hecatoncheires guard them. The Titans are bound in chains, and a bronze anvil would take nine days to fall from earth to Tartarus.

Participants

Zeus

Olympians

Titans

Hecatoncheires

Consequences

The Titans remain imprisoned for eternity, establishing Tartarus as the ultimate prison for divine enemies.

Variants

Some later sources, like Orphic tradition, describe the eventual release of the Titans, particularly Cronus, who becomes a king in the Isles of the Blessed.

Zeus's Threat to the Gods

Source Homer, Iliad 8.13-16Culture Ancient GreekPeriod c. 8th century BCE

Summary

In the Iliad, Zeus threatens to cast any god who disobeys him into Tartarus, 'far below Hades, where the Titans are imprisoned.' This shows Tartarus as a place of punishment for even the gods.

Participants

Zeus

Olympian gods

Consequences

Reinforces the authority of Zeus and the terror of Tartarus.

Variants

None.

The Punishment of Sinners

Source Virgil, Aeneid 6.548-627; Plato, Phaedo 111c-114cCulture Roman, GreekPeriod c. 19 BCE, c. 4th century BCE

Summary

In later sources, Tartarus becomes a place where mortal sinners are punished for their crimes: those who committed murder, treason, or other heinous acts. Plato describes it as a chasm with rivers of fire and mud, where souls are purified or punished. Virgil provides detailed descriptions of punishments for various crimes.

Participants

Damned souls

Rhadamanthus (judge in some accounts)

Consequences

Tartarus becomes a central part of the geography of the afterlife, influencing later Christian concepts of Hell.

Variants

Homer's Odyssey does not mention Tartarus as a place of punishment for mortals; it is reserved for divine enemies.

The Birth of Typhon

Source Hesiod, Theogony 820-869; Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 1.2.1Culture Ancient GreekPeriod c. 700 BCE, 1st-2nd century CE

Summary

After the fall of the Titans, Gaia, angry at Zeus, mates with Tartarus and gives birth to Typhon, a monstrous giant who challenges Zeus. Typhon is described as so huge that his head touches the stars.

Participants

Gaia

Tartarus

Typhon

Zeus

Consequences

Typhon is defeated by Zeus and thrown into Tartarus (or in some versions, buried under Mount Etna). This story establishes Tartarus as a place of containment for the most dangerous monsters.

Variants

In some versions, Typhon is born from Hera alone, or from Cronus and Gaia.

Relationships

Gaia (Earth)

Type consort/familySource Hesiod, Theogony 820-869; Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 1.2.1

Description

Tartarus is the consort of Gaia, and together they produce Typhon (in Hesiod) and the Giants (in some accounts).

Version Note

This is the primary genealogical relationship. In Orphic tradition, Tartarus is not consort to Gaia.

Conflicting Accounts

In Orphic cosmogony, Tartarus is born from Aether and Hemera, and his role as consort to Gaia is less emphasized.

Zeus

Type enemy/wardenSource Homer, Iliad 8.13-16; Hesiod, Theogony 720-819

Description

Zeus is the ruler who casts his enemies into Tartarus and uses it as a threat to maintain order. Tartarus is the prison, Zeus is the jailer.

Version Note

Tartarus is not a character in these stories; it is a location.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Titans

Type prisonerSource Hesiod, Theogony 720-819

Description

The Titans are imprisoned in Tartarus after their defeat in the Titanomachy.

Version Note

In later Orphic tradition, some Titans are released.

Conflicting Accounts

Orphic texts suggest Cronus is freed and rules over the Isles of the Blessed, contradicting Hesiod's eternal imprisonment.

Typhon

Type offspringSource Hesiod, Theogony 820-869

Description

Tartarus is the father of Typhon, the monstrous storm giant, through Gaia.

Version Note

In some versions, Typhon is the son of Hera or Cronus.

Conflicting Accounts

Various parentage accounts exist; Tartarus is one of several possible fathers.

Hades

Type location relationshipSource Homer, Iliad 8.13-16; Hesiod, Theogony 720-725

Description

Tartarus is sometimes described as being beneath Hades, but in some sources, it is used as a synonym for the underworld itself.

Version Note

In later sources, Tartarus is a distinct region within the underworld.

Conflicting Accounts

Some writers use 'Tartarus' interchangeably with 'Hades' to mean the entire underworld.

Eternal Punishment

Source Hesiod, Theogony; Virgil, Aeneid; Plato, Phaedo

Description

Tartarus is a place of eternal torment for the enemies of the gods and, in later sources, for mortal sinners. The punishments are often graphic, including being bound in chains, being burned, or being subjected to physical torture.

Scholarly Context

Scholars interpret these descriptions as reflecting ancient Greek concepts of justice, cosmic order, and the afterlife. The punishments are often seen as proportional to the crimes committed.

Imprisonment of the Titans

Source Hesiod, Theogony 720-819

Description

The Titans, including Cronus, are imprisoned in Tartarus after being defeated by Zeus. This is an act of divine violence and political suppression.

Scholarly Context

This myth is often read as a metaphor for the triumph of order over chaos and the establishment of Zeus's rule.

Contradictions & Disputes

Tartarus as a deity vs. a place

Position A

Tartarus is a primordial deity, a personified abyss that emerges from Chaos and is a generative force.

Position A — evidence

Hesiod, Theogony 116-124; Orphic fragments; Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 1.1.2.

Position B

Tartarus is primarily a place, a geographic region of the underworld used as a prison.

Position B — evidence

Homer, Iliad 8.13-16; most of Hesiod's Theogony; Plato, Phaedo.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars consider the personification of Tartarus as a deity to be a later, Orphic development. In mainstream Greek religion, Tartarus is a place.

Location of Tartarus relative to Hades

Position A

Tartarus is a distinct region beneath Hades, as far below Hades as Hades is below the earth.

Position A — evidence

Hesiod, Theogony 720-725; Plato, Phaedo 111c-114c.

Position B

Tartarus is synonymous with Hades, or the entire underworld is sometimes called Tartarus.

Position B — evidence

Homer, Iliad 8.13-16; various later writers.

Scholarly Consensus

In the earliest sources, Tartarus is distinct, but later usage becomes more fluid. The distinction is often context-dependent.

Who is punished in Tartarus?

Position A

Tartarus is reserved for divine enemies, such as Titans and Giants, not mortals.

Position A — evidence

Hesiod, Theogony; Homer, Iliad and Odyssey.

Position B

In later sources, Tartarus is a place of punishment for mortal sinners.

Position B — evidence

Plato, Phaedo; Virgil, Aeneid; later Christian-influenced texts.

Scholarly Consensus

The original function of Tartarus was as a prison for divine beings, but by the classical period, it was extended to include mortals who committed heinous crimes.
