Who It Is

There is a small patch of earth in the heart of Tokyo, between skyscrapers, that a construction crew will not clear — a head-mound raised over the severed skull of a rebel who 'died' nearly eleven centuries ago. Every few decades the plans come again, and every few decades the jackhammers stop, because someone dies on the site, or a collapse buries a crew, and the project quietly moves around the untouched marker. Japan's greatest supernatural engine is not a monster that you kill or outrun. It is a legal-system of grief and grievance so powerful that it outlasts its killers, outlives its cities, and — if the wrong was great enough — gets the emperor himself to kneel down, build you a shrine, and make you a god. That is the onryō: the dead who refuse to be dead, whose rage reshapes not just haunted wells but the course of Japanese history.

Onryō are depicted as driven by an all-consuming grudge born of injustice, abandonment, betrayal, or violent death. They are described in the folklore as vengeful, cruel, deranged, egotistical, bloodthirsty, and heartless. Their vengeance (tatari, 祟り) can strike the specific wrongdoer or, in the most powerful cases, escalate into untargeted wrath against humanity generally. Powers
Tatari (祟り) — curse/vengeance

The core onryō power: the ability to exact retribution (tatari) on the wrongdoer. This can manifest as direct death (as Fujiwara no Hirotsugu's spirit was believed to have killed the priest Genbō), or as targeted misfortune. The vengeance does not require physical contact with the enemy.

Limit: Traditionally thought to be answerable to ritual appeasement (memorial rites, deification, restoration of rank) and to exorcism.

Natural-disaster manifestation

The most powerful onryō are believed capable of causing earthquakes, fires, storms, drought, famine, and pestilence. Prince Sawara's spirit was blamed for plagues and, according to several scholars, drove Emperor Kanmu to move the capital (to Nagaoka-kyō, then Kyoto) to escape his wrath.

Limit: Historically the preserve of powerful, high-ranking victims (nobles, emperors, famed scholars) rather than common folk.

Posthumous power inversion (weak-to-mighty)

Onryō gain power in death proportional to their powerlessness in life. Women, servants, and low-ranking people — the genre's most common onryō — become 'far more powerful after death than they were in life.' Oiwa (Yotsuya Kaidan) proceeds from a delicate victim to a mighty avenger; Okiku, a mere dish-washing servant, becomes a fixture haunt of Himeji Castle.

Limit: This inversion is a genre rule rather than a strict theological one.

Possession and ikiryō attack

Onryō (and their living counterpart ikiryō) can possess living victims, causing illness, madness, or death. The Heian canon's clearest example is Lady Rokujō's ikiryō in The Tale of Genji, which possesses Genji's pregnant wife Aoi no Ue and brings about her death; mononoke was the Heian term for the possessing vengeful spirit blamed for courtly disease.

Limit: Possession traditionally required the complex ritual apparatus of onmyōdō and Buddhist exorcism; a 'yorimashi' (mouthpiece, usually a servant or apprentice) was used to draw the mononoke out.

Environmental/cursed-space haunt (modern register)

In modern J-horror the onryō's vengeance detaches from a single named enemy and becomes environmental: Sadako Yamamura kills through a cursed videotape, Kayako Saeki through a cursed house (ju-on, the 'grudge' that takes on a life of its own). Anyone who *encounters* the medium — viewer or visitor — becomes a victim.

Limit: This is a 20th-century reconfiguration rather than a traditional doctrine.

Apparitional/psychological torment

Onryō can inflict torment without killing: Oiwa's signature is psychological torture of her husband rather than physical retribution. Okiku's 'counting to nine then shrieking' is an obsessional, eternal gesture of accusation. The genre stresses that onryō motive can outlast the wrongdoer, becoming self-perpetuating.

Limit: Some versions require external help to break the cycle (a diviner, a priest, a well-trained actor's pilgrimage).

Accounts I
The Three Great Onryō — the appeasement of Sugawara no MichizaneKitano Tenman-gū tradition; chronicles (Nihon Sandai Jitsuroku); modern scholarly accounts (Ivan Morris, The Nobility of Failure)

c. 901–993 (death 903; deified over following decades)

Sugawara no Michizane (845–903), the greatest scholar-poet of the Heian court, was politically destroyed by his rival Fujiwara no Tokihira, exiled to Dazaifu in Kyūshū, and died in 903. Within a few years plague and drought spread, Emperor Daigo's sons died in succession, the Great Audience Hall of the palace was struck by lightning, and the city suffered weeks of rainstorms and floods. The court concluded that Michizane's onryō was responsible. They built the Kitano Tenman-gū shrine to appease him, posthumously restored his titles, burned the exile order, and erased the exile from the record. Even this proved insufficient: 70 years later he was elevated to Daijō-daijin (Chancellor of the Realm) and deified as Tenjin-sama, the sky-and-storms god who eventually became the benign patron of scholarship, calligraphy, and poetry. Established the canonical onryō-to-benign-kami transformation; founded the Tenjin cult and the nationwide Tenman-gū shrine network (Dazaifu, Kitano); made Michizane the archetype of the wronged-statesman-turned-patron-god.

The rebellion and head-mound of Taira no MasakadoShōmonki (c. 940s); Edo-era legend; living Masakado-zuka cult; Kanda Shrine

c. 931–940 (rebellion); 940 to present (cult)

Taira no Masakado, a provincial magnate of the Kanmu Taira clan, led the first recorded uprising against the central government (the Jōhei-Tengyō Rebellion). After a series of clan feuds over land and marriage, he attacked the provincial government in 939, conquered Shimotsuke and Kōzuke, and claimed the title of 'New Emperor.' The central government put a bounty on his head, and 59 days later his cousin Taira no Sadamori and Fujiwara no Hidesato killed him at the Battle of Kojima (940). His head was brought to the capital and buried at Shibasaki — the future site of Edo/Tokyo. The associated kubizuka (head-mound, Masakado-zuka) became the anchor of a living onryō cult: the fortunes of Edo and Tokyo were said to wax and wane with the respect paid to the shrine, and neglect brought disasters. To this day the mound, sitting in some of the most expensive land in the world near the Imperial Palace, is carefully maintained, and construction attempts repeatedly died when workers were killed in 'accidents.' Founded a Tokyo-specific tradition of onryō appeasement; enshrined Masakado at Kanda

Emperor Sutoku and the cursed scripturesGukanshō; Edo literary works (Ugetsu Monogatari, Chinsetsu Yumiharizuki); ukiyo-e of Utagawa Yoshitsuya; Onmyo-Za song 'Shiramine' (2023)

reign 1123–1142; exile 1156; death 1164; legend develops after

Emperor Sutoku (1119–1164), the 75th emperor, lost the Hōgen Rebellion in 1156 to forces loyal to his half-brother Emperor Go-Shirakawa and was exiled to Sanuki Province (Shikoku). He took monastic vows and copied numerous scriptures, offering them to the court — which, fearing they were cursed, refused to accept them. Snubbed, Sutoku was said to have died of resentment; upon his death he became an onryō. Everything from the subsequent fall in fortune of the imperial court, the rise of the samurai class, droughts, and internal unrest was blamed on his haunting. Made Sutoku one of the Three Great Onryō; enshrined at Shiramine Shrine (Kyoto) and Kotohira-gū (Kagawa); the subject of Edo-period art and a 2023 heavy-metal song.

Accounts II
Yotsuya Kaidan — the vengeance of OiwaTōkaidō Yotsuya Kaidan, kabuki by Tsuruya Nanboku IV, first staged July 1825

1825; adapted to film 30+ times through to the present

The most famous onryō narrative. Tamiya Iemon, a rōnin, murders his father-in-law Yotsuya Samon and soon abandons his wife Oiwa. To escape her, the Itō family — whose granddaughter Oume desires Iemon — send Oiwa a 'facial cream' that is in fact a poison that disfigures her face (drooping eye, lost hair). When Iemon sees her ruined face he recoils; Oiwa, realising she has been deceived, is shown her reflection, grows hysterical, and accidentally stabs herself through the throat with a sword while fleeing, dying cursing Iemon's name. She returns as an onryō so powerful that she tricks Iemon into killing his new bride Oume and her grandfather, and drives him to madness in an isolated mountain retreat. The play deliberately brings the spirits 'out of the temples and into the homes of common people,' shocking audiences with the exchange of power and powerlessness: Oiwa the delicate victim becomes the mighty avenger. Banchō Sarayashiki — Okiku and the nine platesBanchō Sarayashiki, bunraku 1741; kabuki 1824; best-known modern version by Okamoto Kido, 1916

1741–present

The 'Okiku' onryō story. Okiku, a beautiful serving maid (at Himeji Castle in the folk version; in the household of samurai Aoyama Tetsuzan in others), repeatedly refuses her master's advances. He hides one of ten precious plates she is responsible for, then either accuses her of theft or pressures her to become his mistress. She refuses; he has her beaten, suspended over a well, tortured, and finally slashed and thrown in. She returns as an onryō that counts the plates one through nine each night and then shrieks at the missing tenth. In some versions an exorcist or neighbor shouts 'ten' to free her; in others she is doomed to search forever for the unattainable plate. Made Okiku one of the 'three great yūrei' (with Oiwa and the lovesick Otsuyu); fixed the well-haunt in Japanese horror; Himeji's Okiku-ido ('Okiku's Well') is a tourist site; the 'Okiku beetle' (byasa larva) legend names a reincarnation of the maid.

The Lady Rokujō — the living spirit (ikiryō)The Tale of Genji (Murasaki Shikibu, c. 1000); the Noh play Aoi no Ue

c. 1000; Noh adaptation medieval

Though strictly an ikiryō (living spirit) rather than a dead onryō, Lady Rokujō is the canonical Heian exemplar of the possession register that onryō bodily continuities. A proud, ambitious noblewoman who is Genji's lover, she represses her jealousy when Genji's wife Aoi no Ue becomes pregnant; her separated spirit manifests as a mononoke/ikiryō that possesses and ultimately kills Aoi no Ue in childbirth. After Rokujō's own death her spirit continues to torment Genji's later consorts (Murasaki no Ue, Onna-sannomiya). The Noh Aoi no Ue dramatises her as a jealous-demon whose mask (hannya) captures the state of woman-turned-demon by obsession. Established the jealous-woman-possessing-rival as a core Japanese motif, connecting the ikiryō/mononoke system to the onryō continuum; foundational for the hannya mask's meaning.
