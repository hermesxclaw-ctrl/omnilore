Who It Is

Before the cross, before the church, before even the name of the nation itself, there was the Mother of the Place — the quiet guardian of a single valley in the towering Caucasus, a goddess whose power ended at the mountain pass, whose blessing was for her children alone, and whose memory now survives only in a whisper of a name on a dusty page.

As a tutelary mother, she was believed to protect the inhabitants of her place—humans, livestock, and crops—from harm, disease, and misfortune. She required respect, propitiation, and regular offerings. Neglect or disrespect was believed to bring misfortune, illness to cattle, or poor harvests, suggesting a reciprocal, almost contractual relationship between the goddess and her community.

Powers
Territorial Protection

Guarding the physical boundaries and inhabitants of her specific place against external threats, both human and supernatural.

Limit: Her power was strictly limited to her own place. She had no authority beyond her valley or village territory—a key distinction from universal deities.

Livestock Blessing

Ensuring the health and fertility of the community's herds, particularly sheep and cattle. Rituals involved leading animals around the shrine and offering the 'first fruits' of the dairy.

Limit: Required ongoing propitiation; neglect could lead to the withdrawal of her blessing.

Child Protection

Safeguarding children from illness and death. Mothers might dedicate a lock of a child's hair at her shrine, or make a vow to her in times of sickness.

Limit: Not independently documented; inferred from the general 'mother' attribute and comparative Caucasian folk practice.

Cursing / Withholding Blessing

Inflicting misfortune—illness, livestock death, poor harvest—on those who neglected or disrespected her.

Limit: Power was reactive, not malevolent; she was not a wrathful or capricious deity in most accounts, but a strict matriarch enforcing proper conduct.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No clear anthropomorphic description survives in the written record. Adgilis deda was likely conceived as an immanent spirit of the land itself—the mother of the specific valley, mountain pasture, or village territory—rather than a figure with a fixed human form. She may have been associated with the sacred grove or a specific rock formation within the community's territory.

Symbols — The sacred grove (the 'khati' or shrine site), the stone altar or cairn, votive offerings of bread (especially the ritual 'kada' bread), butter, and animal sacrifice (sheep or goat). The sacred tree, often an ancient oak or pine, within the shrine precinct.

Domains — Tutelary deity of a specific geographic place (mountain valley, village territory). Protectress of livestock (especially sheep and cattle), children, and household prosperity. Guardian of the boundaries of the community's land.

Accounts I
The Mother Who LeftOral tradition, Khevi region (recounted in ethnographic notes)

Undocumented; collected in the late 19th/early 20th century

A community neglected to make the annual offering to Adgilis deda. The following year, a plague struck the livestock and the children fell ill. The community's elders recognized their error and sought to make amends, but the goddess had already 'left her place', abandoning the community to its fate. Only after a great sacrifice—the best ram from every flock, and a public vow of perpetual devotion—did prosperity return, suggesting that she had been persuaded to return. Reinforced the obligation of the annual ritual; the story served as a cautionary tale about the consequences of neglect.

Accounts II
The Protectress of the Herds and the Summer PasturesGeorgian (Kartvelian) folk religion, as documented in Svan and mountain-region ethnography and later folkloric collections.

Traditional oral belief; documented in 19th–20th-century ethnographic collections.

Among the highland herding communities, Adgilis deda ('the Mother of the Place') was invoked above all as guardian of the flocks during the transhumance to the summer pastures. Before the herds were driven up into the alpine meadows, a family or community would call upon Adgilis deda to safeguard the stock from wolves, illness, and the dangers of the trail, and would make a small offering at a boundary stone or roadside shrine. On the herds' safe return, gratitude was rendered through libation and the promise of renewal. Her patronage thus bound the maternal, protective figure of the 'mother of the place' to the productive cycle of mountain husbandry and the well-being of livestock that sustained the households. The story consolidates Adgilis deda's role as a local tutelary and fertility figure linked to the seasonal movement of livestock, explaining the placement of her shrines along migration routes and the offerings made there.

Relationship to other Caucasian mother goddesses (Dali, etc.)Adgilis deda, Dali, and similar figures are regional variants of a single, ancient Caucasian mother-goddess archetype.They are distinct deities with different functions (one place-bound, one a mistress of animals) that only superficially resemble one another.

Adgilis deda is just another name for the Virgin Mary. — While syncretism occurred, the name itself—'Mother of the Place'—and the strictly local nature of the cult point to a pre-Christian origin. She is a tutelary spirit of a specific territory, a concept foreign to Christian theology.

She is a 'fertility goddess' in the generic sense. — Her power is tied to a specific place and its inhabitants, not to universal fertility. She protects her own flock and children; she is not a goddess of love or general abundance.

The cult was 'pagan' and therefore primitive or bloodthirsty. — The recorded practices—offerings of bread, butter, and livestock—are standard propitiation rituals, no different in kind from many folk Catholic or Orthodox practices. The label 'pagan' is a loaded term that obscures more than it reveals.
