Who It Is

The Lobisomem is the Portuguese werewolf — a human, almost always male, doomed to transform into a wolf or wolf-like creature at night. In Portuguese folklore, the curse of the Lobisomem is often hereditary: the seventh son of a seventh son is destined to become a Lobisomem, or a child born after six daughters, or a child born on a Friday under a waning moon. The transformation is involuntary and tragic — the Lobisomem strips naked at a crossroads, rolls in the mud or dust, and rises as a wolf. Unlike the predatory werewolf of Hollywood, the Portuguese Lobisomem is often depicted with pathos: a cursed man who must visit seven crossroads, seven churchyards, and seven villages before dawn, attacking livestock but rarely killing humans unless provoked. His power can be broken by drawing blood (making him bleed, after which he is free), by calling his baptismal name, or by striking him with a blessed object. The Lobisomem's human form is pale, thin, and melancholic — a man who dreads the full moon and the compulsion of his curse. Brazilian folklore expanded the Lobisomem legend in distinctive directions: in some regions, the Lobisomem is a man who has had sexual relations with a comadre (godmother of his child), a transgression that triggers the curse. The Lobisomem is deeply connected to Portuguese fatalism — the sense that destiny (fado) cannot be escaped, that the curse is carried in the blood, and that the monster is, in some sense, the victim of a fate written before birth.
