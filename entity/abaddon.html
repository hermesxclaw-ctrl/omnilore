Who It Is

Abaddon (Hebrew: 'destruction' or 'place of destruction') represents one of the most fearsome concepts in Abrahamic apocalyptic literature — the personified force of annihilation who rules the bottomless pit from which the demonic locust horde emerges at the end of days. In the Hebrew Bible, 'Abaddon' appears primarily as a poetic synonym for Sheol (the grave, the underworld), paired with death itself in parallel constructions: 'Sheol is naked before God, and Abaddon has no covering' (Job 26:6). Wisdom literature treats Abaddon as an insatiable personified force alongside Death: 'Sheol and Abaddon are never satisfied' (Proverbs 27:20). The Book of Job mentions it as a place of fire consuming the wicked. In the Dead Sea Scrolls (particularly the War Scroll and the Thanksgiving Hymns), Abaddon features as a realm of destruction associated with Belial and the forces of darkness that will be destroyed in the final eschatological battle. The most vivid and terrifying portrait of Abaddon emerges in the Book of Revelation (9:1-11). When the fifth angel sounds his trumpet, a star falls from heaven and is given the key to the shaft of the bottomless pit. From this abyss rises smoke darkening the sun, and from the smoke emerges a horde of demonic locusts with the power of scorpions, commanded to torment (but not kill) those without the seal of God. Revelation 9:11 identifies their king: 'They had a king over them, which is the angel of the bottomless pit, whose name in the Hebrew tongue is Abaddon, but in the Greek tongue hath his name Apollyon'—both names meaning 'Destroyer.' This identification has sparked centuries of theological debate: is Abaddon an angel of God fulfilling a divine mission of judgment, or a demonic entity? Some interpreters identify Abaddon with Satan, others as a distinct fallen angel, and still others as a neutral instrument of divine wrath. In later Christian demonology and occult traditions (including the Lesser Key of Solomon), Abaddon is classified among the demons of Hell and sometimes invoked in black magic rituals. In modern popular culture, Abaddon appears widely — from the Warhammer 40K universe (Abaddon the Despoiler) to the TV series Supernatural — always carrying connotations of apocalyptic destruction and bottomless evil.

Form

No physical description exists in any canonical text. The Book of Revelation describes the 'angel of the abyss' but gives no visual details. Later Christian demonology and artistic tradition sometimes depict Abaddon as a fearsome angelic figure, often dark-winged, carrying a spear or sword, or as a monstrous being rising from the abyss. In some medieval texts, he is described as a colossal figure with a crown, commanding armies of locusts. However, all post-biblical appearances are interpretive inventions, not attested descriptions.

Powers

Kingship over the Abyss In Revelation 9:11, Abaddon is described as 'king over the angel of the abyss' (or 'their king, the angel of the abyss' depending on translation). He rules the abyss and its inhabitants. New Testament (Revelation 9:11) The text does not state whether this authority is autonomous or delegated by God. The angel who opens the abyss in Revelation 9:1 is a different figure who receives a key from heaven, suggesting a hierarchy of authority. Some interpretations see Abaddon as Satan himself, others as a separate demonic being, others as a servant of God's judgment. The text is ambiguous.

Command of the Locust Swarm Abaddon commands the demonic locusts that emerge from the abyss. These are not ordinary insects but supernatural beings with scorpion-like tails that torment unsealed humans for five months. New Testament (Revelation 9:3-10) The locusts are explicitly forbidden from harming the grass, trees, or any green plant (Revelation 9:4), and cannot kill their victims — only torment them. None in the text itself, but later interpretations differ on whether the locusts are demons, fallen angels, or symbolic of human armies.

Angel of Destruction In later Christian demonology and some Jewish mystical traditions, Abaddon is personified as an angel or demon of destruction, sometimes equated with a destroying force of God's wrath. Later tradition (post-biblical) This is an interpretive development, not a canonical description. Whether Abaddon is a servant of God or a rebel against God is a matter of theological dispute.

Behavior & Habitat

In Revelation, Abaddon is the king of the demonic locusts that emerge from the abyss. He commands them and is the authority over the abyss itself. The locusts are described as having the appearance of war-horses, with human faces, lion's teeth, iron breastplates, and tails like scorpions. They are forbidden to harm the grass or trees, only people without the seal of God on their foreheads, and they torment them for five months. This is an apocalyptic judgment scenario — Abaddon is the agent of torment, though whether he is acting under God's permission or as a rebel is not stated in the text.

Accounts

When the fifth angel sounds his trumpet, a star falls from heaven to earth, and the star is given the key to the shaft of the abyss. When the shaft is opened, smoke rises like from a great furnace, darkening the sun and the air. From the smoke emerge locusts with the power of scorpions. They are commanded not to harm the grass or trees, but only people without God's seal. They torment them for five months, and death flees from those they attack. The locusts are described in grotesque detail: shaped like war-horses, with human faces, women's hair, lion's teeth, iron breastplates, and tails like scorpions. They have a king: the angel of the abyss, whose Hebrew name is Abaddon and Greek name Apollyon.

Abaddon appears as a poetic name for the realm of the dead, the place of destruction and ruin. It is paired with Sheol and described as being naked before God, who alone can see into it. In Proverbs, Abaddon and death are said to be 'never satisfied', and in Job, it is described as a place where wisdom is not found. It is consistently a place, never a person.

Rabbinic literature sometimes names Abaddon as one of the seven names of Gehenna, or as a division of the underworld where the wicked are punished. In some Kabbalistic texts, Abaddon is a realm of impurity and destruction. The figure is never personified as a demon in mainstream Jewish tradition.

Scholarly Debates
Place vs. Person
Abaddon is a place — the realm of destruction, synonymous with Sheol or Gehenna. ⇌ Abaddon is a person — the angel of the abyss, a demonic king.
Evidence for A: All Hebrew Bible uses are clearly place-based. Rabbinic tradition maintains this.
Evidence for B: Revelation 9:11 explicitly personifies Abaddon as an angel.
Identity: Satan vs. Separate Being
Abaddon is Satan himself, or a title for Satan. ⇌ Abaddon is a separate, distinct demonic or angelic being.
Evidence for A: Some early Christian commentators and later demonologists equate the two. The 'angel of the abyss' is seen as the devil.
Evidence for B: Revelation presents Satan as a separate figure (the dragon, the accuser). Abaddon is never called Satan in the text.
Agent of God vs. Rebel
Abaddon is a servant of God, executing divine judgment. ⇌ Abaddon is a demonic rebel, acting against God but under His sovereign control.
Evidence for A: The trumpet judgments are presented as God's plan. Abaddon is part of that sequence.
Evidence for B: The locusts torment people, which seems evil. Abaddon is called a 'destroyer', which is a negative title.

A way to hold it in your mind

Abaddon is the cosmic equivalent of a wildfire that is both a natural disaster and a controlled burn. In the Hebrew Bible, he is the 'place of destruction' — the void where ruin happens. In Revelation, he becomes the 'angel of that void' — the one who holds the keys to the furnace. He is not a villain in a cape; he is a function of the universe, the personified endpoint of everything that is destroyed. Think of him as the warden of the cosmic incinerator, and the locusts as his guards. Whether he is a good warden or a rogue one is a question the text leaves open — and that is exactly what makes him so compelling.
