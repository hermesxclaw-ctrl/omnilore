Who It Is

In the shadowed depths of biblical tradition, a name whispers of utter ruin: Abaddon, the Destroyer. From the Hebrew scriptures' abyss of death to the apocalyptic locust army of Revelation, this figure embodies the terrifying power of divine judgment. But is Abaddon a place of destruction, an angel of God, or the devil himself? The answer shifts across millennia, from Jewish mysticism to Christian apocalypse, from medieval epics to modern fantasy, revealing a being as multifaceted as the cultures that fear and revere it.

In Revelation, he is a king who leads a destructive army, but his actions are under divine authority. In the Enthronement of Abaddon, he is a terrifying but obedient angel who fulfills God's commands. In the Acts of Thomas, he is a demonic force opposing God.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Hebrew 'ăḇaddōn (אֲבַדּוֹן) is an intensive form of the root 'āḇaḏ (אָבַד), meaning 'perish' or 'destroy'. The Greek Apollyon (Ἀπολλύων) is the active participle of apóllymi (ἀπόλλυμι), 'to destroy'. The Septuagint uses apṓleia (ἀπώλεια) for 'destruction'.

Pronunciation — Hebrew: /ə.vadˈdon/; Greek: /a.polˈly.ɔːn/; English: /ˈæbəˌdɒn/

Aliases — Apollyon, The Destroyer, Angel of the Abyss, King of the Locusts, Exterminans (Latin), Muriel (in some traditions)

Ownership — Primarily Jewish and Christian traditions; also appears in Mandaean scriptures. The name has been adopted in modern popular culture globally.

Names are cult layers, not one fixed label.

Powers
Kingship over the locusts

Abaddon is king of the locusts that torment humanity for five months.

Limit: The locusts are not allowed to kill; only torment. They cannot harm those sealed by God.

Key to the abyss

Abaddon is described as having the key to the bottomless pit, allowing him to release the locusts.

Limit: The key is given by God or an angel; it is not a personal power.

Guardianship and authority over creation

In the Enthronement of Abaddon, Abaddon was given authority over all beings, including angels and demons, and was made a guardian after collecting earth for Adam.

Limit: He acts under God's command and is promised to save those who venerate him.

Presence at the resurrection

In the Book of the Resurrection of Jesus Christ, Abaddon is present at the tomb, witnessing the resurrection.

Limit: No specific powers are attributed in this account.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In Revelation, Abaddon is not described physically, but his army is: locusts like horses with human faces, women's hair, lions' teeth, iron breastplates, and tails with scorpion stingers. In later Christian art, Abaddon is sometimes depicted as a demonic angel or a fallen angel, but there is no canonical description. In the Acts of Thomas, he is a demon, but no physical form is given.

Symbols — The abyss (bottomless pit) is his domain. Locusts are his army. The key to the abyss is a symbol of his authority. In medieval literature, he is associated with pagan deities, but this is polemical.

Domains — The abyss (bottomless pit), Sheol, Gehenna. In Mandaeism, the Abaddons are realms in the World of Darkness.

Accounts I
The Abyss and the Locusts (Revelation 9)Book of Revelation

1st century CE

When the fifth angel sounds his trumpet, a star falls from heaven and is given the key to the bottomless pit. Opening it, smoke rises and locusts emerge, commanded by Abaddon/Apollyon. They torment those without God's seal for five months. The torment is a divine judgment, but it does not lead to repentance.

The Creation of Adam and the Enthronement of AbaddonPseudo-Timothy of Alexandria, The Enthronement of Abaddon

5th-6th century CE

The angel Muriel is tasked by God to gather earth for Adam's creation. After completing this, he is renamed Abaddon and appointed guardian. He becomes so feared that even angels and demons tremble. He is promised that those who venerate him will be saved, and he will have a role in the Last Judgment, taking souls to the Valley of Josaphat. Abaddon becomes a powerful but obedient angel, and a mediator for the faithful.

Accounts II
The Pilgrim's Progress (Apollyon's Encounter)John Bunyan, The Pilgrim's Progress

1678

Christian, the protagonist, fights Apollyon, a demonic figure, in the Valley of Humiliation. The battle lasts over half a day, and Christian is wounded, but he eventually overcomes Apollyon with his sword. Christian's victory strengthens his faith and continues his journey.

The Song of Roland's MisrepresentationSong of Roland

11th century CE

The epic poem depicts the Saracens worshipping a trinity of idols: Mahound (Muhammad), Apollyon, and Termagant. This is a polemical misrepresentation of Islam, which is strictly monotheistic. This portrayal influenced medieval Christian views of Islam and contributed to religious prejudice.

The House of the Abaddons (Mandaean Myth)Ginza Rabba (Left Ginza)

Late antique to medieval

The final poem of the Left Ginza describes the 'House of the Abaddons' as a realm in the World of Darkness, where the souls of the wicked are punished. The Abaddons are multiple realms of destruction. The Abaddons serve as a place of purification or punishment, depending on Mandaean soteriology.

Abaddon is always a demon or Satan. — In the Bible, Abaddon is primarily a place of destruction, and in Revelation, he is an angel under God's authority. The demonic interpretation is a later development.

Abaddon is a deity worshipped by Muslims. — This is a medieval Christian polemic; Islam is monotheistic and does not worship Abaddon.

Abaddon is the same as the Devil. — While some identify him with Satan, the biblical text does not explicitly say so.
