Who It Is
Form

Abaddon is often depicted as a powerful angel or demon, associated with destruction, chaos, and the underworld. In some traditions, he is described as a massive locust-like creature, while in others, he is depicted as a humanoid figure with wings and a crown of thorns.

Powers

Destruction Abaddon is said to have the power to bring about destruction and chaos, often through the use of locusts or other agents of destruction.

Accounts

In the Book of Revelation, Abaddon is described as the angel of the abyss, responsible for bringing about destruction and chaos.

In Jewish mythology, Abaddon is said to have been involved in the War in Heaven, a conflict between angels and demons.

The Approach

In the shadowed depths of biblical tradition, a name whispers of utter ruin: Abaddon, the Destroyer. From the Hebrew scriptures' abyss of death to the apocalyptic locust army of Revelation, this figure embodies the terrifying power of divine judgment. But is Abaddon a place of destruction, an angel of God, or the devil himself? The answer shifts across millennia, from Jewish mysticism to Christian apocalypse, from medieval epics to modern fantasy, revealing a being as multifaceted as the cultures that fear and revere it.

Content advisory. The subject involves apocalyptic imagery, divine judgment, demonic forces, and violence (locusts tormenting people). Some interpretations associate Abaddon with Satan or Antichrist, which may be disturbing to some readers. Medieval Christian literature contains polemical and inaccurate depictions of Islam and its prophet, which are presented here for historical context with critical commentary.
Identity

Abaddon

Pronunciation Hebrew: /ə.vadˈdon/; Greek: /a.polˈly.ɔːn/; English: /ˈæbəˌdɒn/

Known As

ApollyonThe DestroyerAngel of the AbyssKing of the LocustsExterminans (Latin)Muriel (in some traditions)Abaddon (Hebrew: אֲבַדּוֹן)

Etymology

Hebrew 'ăḇaddōn (אֲבַדּוֹן) is an intensive form of the root 'āḇaḏ (אָבַד), meaning 'perish' or 'destroy'. The Greek Apollyon (Ἀπολλύων) is the active participle of apóllymi (ἀπόλλυμι), 'to destroy'. The Septuagint uses apṓleia (ἀπώλεια) for 'destruction'.

Cultural Ownership

Primarily Jewish and Christian traditions; also appears in Mandaean scriptures. The name has been adopted in modern popular culture globally.

Disputed Identifications

Whether Abaddon is a place, an angel of God, a demon, or Satan himself is disputed. In Revelation, the angel is often identified with Satan by some commentators (e.g., Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, Halley), while others (e.g., The Interpreter's Bible, Jehovah's Witnesses) see him as a servant of God, possibly even Jesus Christ. In medieval Christian literature, Abaddon/Apollyon was erroneously associated with Islamic deities.

Description

Appearance

In Revelation, Abaddon is not described physically, but his army is: locusts like horses with human faces, women's hair, lions' teeth, iron breastplates, and tails with scorpion stingers. In later Christian art, Abaddon is sometimes depicted as a demonic angel or a fallen angel, but there is no canonical description. In the Acts of Thomas, he is a demon, but no physical form is given.

Symbols & Iconography

The abyss (bottomless pit) is his domain. Locusts are his army. The key to the abyss is a symbol of his authority. In medieval literature, he is associated with pagan deities, but this is polemical.

Behaviour & Personality

In Revelation, he is a king who leads a destructive army, but his actions are under divine authority. In the Enthronement of Abaddon, he is a terrifying but obedient angel who fulfills God's commands. In the Acts of Thomas, he is a demonic force opposing God.

Domains & Habitats

The abyss (bottomless pit), Sheol, Gehenna. In Mandaeism, the Abaddons are realms in the World of Darkness.

Version Differences

In the Hebrew Bible, Abaddon is a place of destruction. In Revelation, he is an angel. In rabbinic literature, he is a realm of punishment. In Gnostic texts, he is a demon. In medieval Christian literature, he is a demonic deity. In Mandaeism, he is a collective of underworld realms.

Powers & Abilities

Kingship over the locusts

Source VersionNew Testament (Revelation 9:11)

Abaddon is king of the locusts that torment humanity for five months.

Limitations

The locusts are not allowed to kill; only torment. They cannot harm those sealed by God.

Contradictions

None noted.

Key to the abyss

Source VersionNew Testament (Revelation 9:1-2)

Abaddon is described as having the key to the bottomless pit, allowing him to release the locusts.

Limitations

The key is given by God or an angel; it is not a personal power.

Contradictions

Some interpret the angel with the key as a different being, but Revelation 9:11 identifies the king as Abaddon.

Guardianship and authority over creation

Source VersionPseudo-Timothy of Alexandria

In the Enthronement of Abaddon, Abaddon was given authority over all beings, including angels and demons, and was made a guardian after collecting earth for Adam.

Limitations

He acts under God's command and is promised to save those who venerate him.

Contradictions

This is a later apocryphal tradition not in canonical scripture.

Presence at the resurrection

Source VersionBartholomew the Apostle

In the Book of the Resurrection of Jesus Christ, Abaddon is present at the tomb, witnessing the resurrection.

Limitations

No specific powers are attributed in this account.

Contradictions

This is apocryphal.

Stories & Myths

The Abyss and the Locusts (Revelation 9)

Source Book of RevelationCulture Early ChristianPeriod 1st century CE

Summary

When the fifth angel sounds his trumpet, a star falls from heaven and is given the key to the bottomless pit. Opening it, smoke rises and locusts emerge, commanded by Abaddon/Apollyon. They torment those without God's seal for five months.

Participants

Abaddon

Locusts

The fifth angel

Humanity

Consequences

The torment is a divine judgment, but it does not lead to repentance.

Variants

Some interpret the 'star' as a different angel, but the text identifies the king of the locusts as Abaddon.

The Creation of Adam and the Enthronement of Abaddon

Source Pseudo-Timothy of Alexandria, The Enthronement of AbaddonCulture Egyptian Christianity (Coptic)Period 5th-6th century CE

Summary

The angel Muriel is tasked by God to gather earth for Adam's creation. After completing this, he is renamed Abaddon and appointed guardian. He becomes so feared that even angels and demons tremble. He is promised that those who venerate him will be saved, and he will have a role in the Last Judgment, taking souls to the Valley of Josaphat.

Participants

Abaddon (Muriel)

God

Adam

Angels

Demons

Consequences

Abaddon becomes a powerful but obedient angel, and a mediator for the faithful.

Variants

This is a non-canonical tradition that elevates Abaddon to a positive role.

The Pilgrim's Progress (Apollyon's Encounter)

Source John Bunyan, The Pilgrim's ProgressCulture English ChristianPeriod 1678

Summary

Christian, the protagonist, fights Apollyon, a demonic figure, in the Valley of Humiliation. The battle lasts over half a day, and Christian is wounded, but he eventually overcomes Apollyon with his sword.

Participants

Christian

Apollyon

Consequences

Christian's victory strengthens his faith and continues his journey.

Variants

Bunyan uses Apollyon as a personification of evil, drawing on Revelation.

The Song of Roland's Misrepresentation

Source Song of RolandCulture Medieval FrankishPeriod 11th century CE

Summary

The epic poem depicts the Saracens worshipping a trinity of idols: Mahound (Muhammad), Apollyon, and Termagant. This is a polemical misrepresentation of Islam, which is strictly monotheistic.

Participants

Saracens

Mahound

Apollyon

Termagant

Consequences

This portrayal influenced medieval Christian views of Islam and contributed to religious prejudice.

Variants

This is not a story of Abaddon per se, but an example of how the name was used in medieval anti-Islamic polemic.

The House of the Abaddons (Mandaean Myth)

Source Ginza Rabba (Left Ginza)Culture MandaeanPeriod Late antique to medieval

Summary

The final poem of the Left Ginza describes the 'House of the Abaddons' as a realm in the World of Darkness, where the souls of the wicked are punished. The Abaddons are multiple realms of destruction.

Participants

Souls of the dead

Rulers of the underworld

Consequences

The Abaddons serve as a place of purification or punishment, depending on Mandaean soteriology.

Variants

Mandaeism has both upper and lower Abaddons, indicating a complex cosmology.

Relationships
Abaddon→Godservant

In Revelation, Abaddon acts as an agent of divine judgment. In the Enthronement of Abaddon, he is a loyal angel who follows God's commands.

Abaddon→Satanpotential identification

Many Christian commentators have identified Abaddon as Satan himself, based on the destructive nature of his actions.

Abaddon→Jesus Christpotential identification

Jehovah's Witnesses interpret the angel with the key to the abyss (Revelation 20:1-3) as Jesus, and thus Abaddon as another name for the resurrected Jesus.

Abaddon→The Locustsking/commander

Abaddon is the king of the locusts, who obey his command.

Abaddon→Muhammadfalse association

In the Song of Roland, Abaddon (as Apollyon) is falsely associated with Muhammad as a deity worshipped by Muslims.

Abaddon→Adamcreator/guardian

In the Enthronement of Abaddon, Abaddon (as Muriel) collects the earth for Adam's creation and is later appointed as a guardian over creation.

Contradictions & Disputes

Place vs. Angel

Position A

Abaddon is a place of destruction (Hebrew Bible).

Position A — evidence

Job 26:6; Proverbs 15:11 etc.

Position B

Abaddon is an angel (Revelation).

Position B — evidence

Revelation 9:11

Scholarly Consensus

The Hebrew Bible uses it as a place; the New Testament personifies it. Most scholars see a development.

Agent of God vs. Satan

Position A

Abaddon is an angel of God, performing judgment.

Position A — evidence

Revelation 9 context; The Interpreter's Bible

Position B

Abaddon is Satan or a demon.

Position B — evidence

Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus; it depends on interpretation of apocalyptic symbolism.

Identity with Jesus

Position A

Abaddon is another name for Jesus (Jehovah's Witnesses).

Position A — evidence

Revelation 20:1-3; Watch Tower publications

Position B

Abaddon is a distinct being.

Position B — evidence

Mainstream Christian theology

Scholarly Consensus

This is a minority view, not accepted by most Christian denominations.

Hebrew Bible (Masoretic Text) - Job 26:6; 28:22; 31:12; Psalm 88:11; Proverbs 15:11; 27:20

Type textDate circa 6th-4th century BCE (final form)Locator Job 26:6; 28:22; 31:12; Psalm 88:11; Proverbs 15:11; 27:20Language Hebrew

Translation

Abaddon appears as a place of destruction, often paired with Sheol. For example, Job 26:6: 'Sheol is naked before Him; Abaddon has no cover.'

Septuagint (Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible)

Type textDate 3rd-2nd century BCELocator Job 26:6; 28:22; 31:12; Psalm 88:11; Proverbs 15:11; 27:20; Proverbs 6:32Language Greek

Translation

Renders 'Abaddon' as 'apṓleia' (destruction). In Proverbs 6:32, the Greek uses 'apoleian' (accusative of apoleia) to translate the Hebrew participial form of shachath.
