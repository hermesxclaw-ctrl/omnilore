Who It Is

Daramulum (also Dharramalan, Thuremlin, and other regional variants) is the son, messenger, or earthly representative of Baiame in southeastern Australian Aboriginal belief systems. His name means 'one-legged' or 'lame,' reflecting his most distinctive physical characteristic: he has only one leg. This asymmetry is not a defect but a mark of his sacred, liminal nature — he exists between the sky (Baiame's realm) and the earth, between the ordinary world and the sacred. Daramulum is particularly associated with the bora (initiation) ceremonies. When Baiame ascended to the sky after creating the world, he left Daramulum to oversee the transmission of the Law to new generations of men. During the bora, the voice of Daramulum is heard in the whirring of the bullroarer (a sacred sound-making instrument), and its sound is interpreted as his direct presence. The bullroarer's whirring is considered one of the most sacred sounds in Aboriginal ritual, and its mysteries are revealed only to initiated men. Daramulum's image — a one-legged figure — appears in rock engravings across the Sydney Basin and New South Wales, often in sites associated with initiation grounds. The Daramulum figure was also recorded by early colonial observers around Sydney Cove and the Blue Mountains, providing some of the earliest European documentation of Aboriginal spiritual belief. Daramulum is not a distant, abstract deity but an immediately present spiritual force — his voice cuts through the air of the bora ground, and his one-legged form is carved into the living rock. He represents the principle that sacred knowledge must be transmitted, that the Law requires teachers, and that initiation into spiritual adulthood is one of the most consequential acts in human life.
