Who It Is

Entelides never slew a lion, never labored for a king, and never earned a cult — he was one of fifty sons conceived in the shadow of a lion hunt, named once in a catalogue and then dispatched to colonize an island at the edge of the Greek world. His story is not his own, but the story of how Heracles turned a Boeotian king's hospitality into a dynasty.

No personality, speech, moral choice, or deed is attributed to Entelides individually. The collective sons are described only as obedient to Heracles' disposition: seven remain in Thespiae, three go to Thebes, forty sail with Iolaus. Diodorus (4.29.3) characterizes the Sardinian colonists as civilizing founders. Apollodorus presents them as passive recipients of paternal command. No source gives Entelides agency, dialogue, or ethos. Distinguish: attested = collective obedience; reconstructed = none; disputed = none.

Powers
Heraclid Lineage (No Individual Power Attested)

No source attributes a superhuman power, skill, transformation, weapon mastery, or divine gift to Entelides specifically. As a son of Heracles, mythographic tradition implies descent from Zeus and heroic ancestry, but no feat, labor, or arete is narrated for him. He is not listed among Heracles' deified or hero-cult sons (like Telephus or Hyllus).

Limit: Mortal; no immortality, invulnerability, or divine patronage attested. Subject to Heracles' paternal command regarding settlement. No resistance or counter-ability recorded.

Colonial Founder Status (Collective)

In Diodorus and Apollodorus, the forty Thestiadae sent to Sardinia become oikists (founders) with Iolaus, credited with civilizing the island, building works, and establishing Greek presence. This is a collective political/colonial role, not a supernatural ability. Entelides, if among the forty, would share this founder status, but no individual foundation is named for him.

Limit: Founder status is dependent on Iolaus' leadership; no independent city or lineage traced to Entelides survives in sources.

Accounts I
The Cithaeronian Lion HuntApollodorus 2.4.9-10; Diodorus 4.29.2; Pausanias 9.27.6

Mythic heroic age (set before Heracles' Labours); recorded Classical to Imperial period

A lion ravages the flocks of Amphitryon and Thespius on Mount Cithaeron. The young Heracles, aged eighteen and living at Thespiae or passing through, undertakes to kill it. He lodges with King Thespius of Thespiae for an extended period while hunting. He eventually kills the lion (later wearing its skin, prefiguring the Nemean lion). The hunt provides the narrative frame for his liaison with the Thespian daughters. Establishes Heracles' early heroic prowess; creates hospitality bond with Thespius that motivates the king to offer his daughters; results in conception of 50 sons including Entelides.

The Night(s) with the Thestiades - Conception of EntelidesApollodorus 2.4.10; Athenaeus 13.556F citing Herodorus FGrHist 31 F20; Diodorus 4.29.2-3; Tzetzes Chiliades 2.221-225

Mythic; recorded 5th century BCE (Herodorus) to 12th century CE (Tzetzes)

Thespius, desiring that his daughters bear children by Heracles, entertains the hero. Heracles lies with all fifty daughters, begetting a son on each. Menippis bears Entelides. In Apollodorus' version, Heracles sleeps with one per night for 50 nights (with one daughter repeating, so one night he has two), thinking each time it is the same woman. In Herodorus/Athenaeus, it occurs in a single night (with Thespius substituting daughters). In Diodorus, it occurs over seven nights. Entelides is thus conceived as part of a collective dynastic begetting. Birth of 50 Heraclid sons; establishes Thespian-Heraclid kinship; later used to explain Heraclid claims in Boeotia and Sardinia. Entelides' birth has no separate consequence beyond membership in the cohort.

The Birth and Naming of the Fifty SonsApollodorus 2.4.10 (catalogue); Tzetzes Chiliades 2.493-497 (list)

Imperial / Byzantine compilation of earlier catalogues

Each daughter bears a son. Apollodorus preserves a catalogue correlating each mother with a son's name; Entelides is listed as the son of Menippis. The list functions as a genealogical register rather than narrative. No birth prodigies or oracles are attached to Entelides individually, unlike major heroes. Creates a complete Thespian Heraclid genealogy used by later authors to enumerate Heracles' progeny (often cited as his largest single set of offspring). Provides onomastic record; Entelides' name is preserved solely through this list.

Accounts II
Heracles' Disposition - The Division of the SonsApollodorus 2.7.8

Mythic; recorded Imperial period

Years later, when the sons have grown, Heracles sends a message to Thespius from exile or servitude (after his madness and before/after his labours, chronology varies). He instructs: retain seven sons in Thespiae, send three to Thebes, and send the remaining forty, joined by his nephew and companion Iolaus, to colonize Sardinia. The message treats the sons as a collective resource for dynastic and colonial policy. Explains three distinct Heraclid branches: Thespian, Theban, Sardinian. Provides aetiology for Greek presence in Sardinia and for Thespian aristocratic claims. Entelides' future is determined by this lot, but his assignment is not individually recorded.

The Sardinian Colony of Iolaus and the ThestiadaeDiodorus 4.29.3-4.30.3; Pausanias 10.17.5; Apollodorus 2.7.8; Strabo 5.2.7 (on Iolaus in Sardinia)

Archaic colonial memory retrojected to heroic age; recorded Classical to Imperial

Iolaus, Heracles' nephew and charioteer, leads the forty Thestiadae (and other Thespians) to Sardinia. There they subdue natives, found cities, introduce Greek customs, build the 'Iolaeia' and works later called Nuraghi by some authors, and establish a lasting Greek-Sardinian population called Iolaeans or Ilienses. Diodorus presents this as a civilizing mission; Pausanias notes they were the first Greeks to colonize Sardinia. If Entelides was among the forty, he participates as a founder, but no act is individualized. Aetiology for Sardinian toponyms, cults of Iolaus (hero cult in Sardinia and Thebes), and Greek claims to western Mediterranean. Later sources (Aristotle, Mirabilia 100) associate Iolaus' colonists with distinctive Sardinian institutions. No individual tomb or cult for Entelides is recorded.

The Theban and Thespian RemnantsApollodorus 2.7.8; Pausanias 9.27.6-7 (Thespian cults)

Mythic / historical cult practice

The seven sons retained in Thespiae remain as local aristocrats, presumably integrating into Thespian elite and maintaining Heracles and Eros cults for which Thespiae was famous. The three sent to Thebes enter Theban Heraclid lineage, distinct from the Dorian Heraclids via Hyllus. Entelides could belong to either group in theory, but no source places him. Pausanias' description of Thespiae mentions honors to Heracles and the Thespiades without naming individual sons. Explains Heraclid presence in Boeotia separate from Peloponnesian Heraclids; provides local genealogical prestige. For Entelides, consequence is silence: without assignment, his later life, marriage, progeny, and death are unattested.

Entelides was a famous hero with his own labours or cult. — No individual deed, labour, cult, sanctuary, or iconography is attested for Entelides. He appears only as a name in a genealogical catalogue. All narrative belongs to the collective Thestiadae and to Heracles/Iolaus.

Heracles seduced the 50 daughters romantically over time, with mutual consent. — Ancient sources frame the liaison as Thespius' deliberate breeding plan and Heracles' hospitality obligation, with daughters as passive gifts. Consent is never discussed; power asymmetry (king's guest, hero's dominance, daughters' youth and seclusion) is central. Herodorus' one-night version emphasizes deception (Thespius substituting daughters).

The Cithaeronian lion is the same as the Nemean lion. — They are distinct lions in mythography: Cithaeronian lion is Heracles' youthful hunt at Thespiae (before labours), whose skin he sometimes wears early; Nemean lion is the First Labour for Eurystheus, whose skin becomes his canonical attribute. Some early art conflates, but texts distinguish.

All 50 sons went to Sardinia and founded cities named after themselves. — Only forty (per Apollodorus) went to Sardinia with Iolaus; seven remained in Thespiae and three went to Thebes. No city in Sardinia is named for Entelides individually; founders are treated collectively as Iolaeans.

Entelides' name proves a Greek etymology meaning 'perfect' and indicates his character. — Etymology is opaque; while ἐντελής means 'complete,' no ancient source etymologizes Entelides, and no character trait is attached to him. Deriving personality from name is modern speculation.
