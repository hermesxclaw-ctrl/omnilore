Who It Is

The Moura Encantada (Enchanted Moorish Maiden) is one of the most pervasive figures in Portuguese folklore — a beautiful young woman, often of Moorish origin, magically imprisoned in caves, ancient dolmens, wells, ruined castles, or beneath Roman bridges. She is the guardian of vast hidden treasure, and she appears to mortals offering to share her riches if they can break her enchantment. The conditions for breaking the spell are always difficult or impossible: the hero must perform a specific task (kiss a serpent, carry her across a river at midnight without speaking, count grains of sand) and almost always fails. The failed attempt often ends with the Moura crying out 'Ai de mim! Faltou-me tanto como isto!' (Woe is me! I lacked only this much!) — the treasure vanishes, and the hero is left with a single gold coin or a piece of enchanted charcoal that turns to gold if he kept it. The Moura represents a complex layer of Portuguese history: the memory of the Moorish presence in Iberia (711-1492), overlaid with pre-Roman goddess worship (megalithic sites), fairy traditions, and the folk-Catholic concept of souls in purgatory. Many place-names across Portugal contain 'Moura' or 'Mourão,' marking legendary sites. She is often conflated with the 'Moura Fiandeira' (Spinning Moorish Maiden) who weaves with a golden spindle at the mouth of her cave, and the 'Moura-Pedra' (Stone Maiden) — petrified dolmens said to be enchanted women frozen in time. The enchantment motif encodes themes of religious and ethnic transformation: the Enchanted Moura is the beautiful other who must be integrated (through the breaking of the spell) into Christian Portuguese identity to release her hidden gifts.
