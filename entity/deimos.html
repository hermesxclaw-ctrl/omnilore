Deimos, twin of terror, whose dread presence turns the hearts of heroes to water before the clash of arms.

Who They Are

Deimos is the god of terror and dread, the twin brother of Phobos (Fear) and the son of Ares and Aphrodite. While Phobos inspires the sudden panic that breaks ranks, Deimos embodies the deeper, lingering dread that precedes and follows combat — the cold awareness of impending violence. In the Iliad, he and Phobos ride with Ares in his war chariot, their grim visages unsettling the bravest warriors. Deimos was also depicted on the shield of Heracles alongside Phobos, serving as a warning to all who faced the hero. In later mythology, Deimos was sometimes associated with the panic that accompanies natural disasters and divine punishment. The Romans called him Metus or Formido. The moons of Mars (discovered in 1877) were named Phobos and Deimos after these twin deities, in keeping with the planet's association with the god of war.

Attested in Hesiod's Theogony / Homer's IliadEra Archaic Greece (c. 700 BCE)

Form

In literary sources, Deimos is often paired with Phobos, and their appearances are not individually distinguished. They are typically described as 'terrible' (δεινώ) or 'fearful' (φοβερώ) gods. In art, they are depicted as youthful, fully armed warriors, often bearded or beardless, and almost always in the company of their father Ares. They are not depicted as monstrous or deformed, but rather as idealized, martial figures whose presence signifies the atmosphere of the battlefield.

Powers

Instill Dread The primary power of Deimos is to project an aura of overwhelming fear and dread onto mortals, particularly soldiers. This is not a physical attack but a psychological one, sapping morale and resolve before a conflict even begins. Homer, Hesiod, and all subsequent epic poets. His power is most effective on those who are already in a state of emotional vulnerability, such as soldiers before battle. He does not have the power to cause absolute, mindless panic (that is more the domain of Phobos), nor does he have any power outside the context of war and conflict. He cannot make someone brave; he can only make them afraid. None noted. His power is consistently described as an influence over the emotions of men in war.

Divine Charioteer Along with Phobos, Deimos serves as the charioteer for his father Ares, yoking his horses and driving him into and out of battle. This is a supporting role, facilitating Ares's presence on the battlefield. Homer, Iliad Book 15; Quintus Smyrnaeus. This is a task, not a power. It implies a degree of physicality and presence on the battlefield that is more than just an aura. They are able to physically interact with the divine world in a way that most personifications do not. None.

Embodiment of Fear (Personification) As a personification, Deimos is the concept of dread made manifest. His very presence is fear. This means he is not a separate being who causes fear, but the fear itself. This gives him a kind of omnipresence in any context where dread exists. Hesiod, Theogony; general Greek philosophical thought. As an abstract personification, he has no independent will or agency in most narratives. He is a force, not a character. He cannot be reasoned with, bribed, or defeated in combat because he is not a physical entity in the same way as a god like Ares. The line between a 'person' and a 'force' is blurred. In epic, he acts; in allegory, he simply is.

Personification of Terror Deimos is the embodiment of the feeling of terror. His very presence can inspire dread, panic, and a loss of morale in opposing armies. He is a psychological weapon, capable of breaking the spirit of soldiers before a single blow is struck. Homeric and Hesiodic tradition His power is not physical but psychological. He cannot win a battle through strength; he relies on fear to weaken his enemies. His influence is most potent on the battlefield, and his power over a calm, rational mind is diminished. None. He is consistently a personification rather than a major actor with independent feats.

Divine Retinue As a son of Ares, Deimos serves as an attendant and charioteer, part of the divine entourage that accompanies the god of war into battle. He and his brother Phobos yoke the horses of Ares' war-chariot. Homeric tradition (Iliad) This role is subordinate. He is a servant to his father's will, not an independent commander of divine forces. None.

Accounts

Deimos is consistently depicted as a member of Ares' entourage, a personified force of terror that follows the god of war into battle. He is not a central character in any major myth but is a constant presence in the backdrop of epic warfare.

Hesiod records the birth of Deimos and his twin Phobos as the offspring of Ares and Aphrodite. Their very existence is an allegory for the union of war and love, which paradoxically produces not harmony but the twin horrors of terror and panic.

The poet describes the shield of Heracles, which features a depiction of Deimos and Phobos alongside Ares and other figures of war. This shows how the personifications were used as decorative, symbolic imagery in art.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Deimos not as a character in a story, but as a weather system. He is the pressure drop before a hurricane, the oppressive stillness before an earthquake. He doesn't fight, he doesn't scheme, he simply settles over a place and changes the atmosphere. He is the psychological climate of war, and every soldier who has ever felt their stomach drop at the sound of a distant trumpet has met him.
