Who It Is

In the ancient North, a goddess of love and war drove a chariot pulled by cats, wept gold for her absent husband, and claimed half the battle-slain for her shimmering field. She was Freyja, the Lady, a Vanir whose power and desires shaped the fate of gods and mortals alike.

Freyja is passionate, assertive, and powerful. She is invoked for fertility and love, but also for war. She is described as the most approachable of the goddesses for prayers. She shows anger (shaking halls, snorting) when provoked.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Old Norse Freyja means 'Lady' or 'Mistress', from Proto-Germanic *frawjōn. Cognate with Old Saxon frūa and Old High German frouwa (modern German Frau). It is an epithet, likely replacing an unattested personal name.

Pronunciation — FRAY-yah

Aliases — Gefn, Hörn, Mardöll, Sýr, Vanadís, Valfreyja

Ownership — Old Norse / Germanic (Scandinavian) mythology

Powers
Seiðr

Freyja is a practitioner of seiðr, a form of Norse magic involving seeing and influencing the future.

Limit: Not detailed in sources; she taught it to the Æsir.

Falcon cloak

A cloak of falcon feathers that allows the wearer to shift into falcon form. Freya lends it to Loki and others.

Limit: Not a personal power; the cloak is an artifact.

Chariot with cats

Freyja drives a chariot pulled by two cats.

Limit: No known limitations.

Boar Hildisvíni

She is accompanied by the boar Hildisvíni, who is actually her human servant Óttar in disguise.

Limit: The boar is a transformed human, not a separate creature.

Brísingamen

A magnificent necklace, possibly of amber or gold, that she owns. It is mentioned in Þrymskviða and Sörla þáttr.

Limit: In Sörla þáttr, she obtains it from four dwarves in exchange for sleeping with each of them.

Tears of gold

She cries tears of red gold for her absent husband Óðr.

Limit: None.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Freyja is described as beautiful. She wears the necklace Brísingamen and a cloak of falcon feathers. She rides a chariot pulled by two cats and is accompanied by the boar Hildisvíni.

Symbols — Cats, boar, falcon cloak, Brísingamen, Fólkvangr, Sessrúmnir.

Domains — She rules over Fólkvangr, a heavenly field, where she receives half of those slain in battle. Within it is her hall Sessrúmnir. She is associated with fertility, love, war, gold, and seiðr (magic).

Accounts I
The Building of Asgard's WallGylfaginning (Prose Edda)

Mythological

An unnamed builder offers to build a wall around Asgard, demanding Freyja as payment. Loki, disguised as a mare, distracts the builder's horse, causing the builder to fail and be killed by Thor. The wall is unfinished, and the builder is killed.

Loki's Flyting (Lokasenna)Poetic Edda

Mythological

At a feast, Loki insults Freyja, accusing her of promiscuity and of being astride her brother Freyr. Njörðr defends her, saying a woman having a lover is harmless. The flyting continues; Loki is eventually chased away.

Accounts II
The Theft of Mjöllnir (Þrymskviða)Poetic Edda

Mythological

Thor's hammer is stolen by Þrymr, who demands Freyja as his bride. Freyja refuses, and Thor dresses as Freyja with Loki as his maid to retrieve the hammer. Thor gets his hammer back and kills Þrymr and the jötnar.

HyndluljóðPoetic Edda

Mythological

Freyja turns her servant Óttar into a boar (Hildisvíni) and visits the jötunn Hyndla to force her to recite Óttar's ancestry, so he can claim his inheritance. She threatens Hyndla with fire. Freyja obtains the ancestry information.

Sörla þáttrFlateyjarbók

14th century

A short story that tells of Freyja sleeping with four dwarves to obtain the Brísingamen necklace, and later being tricked by Loki into causing an eternal battle between two kings. The necklace is obtained; an eternal battle is set.

Freyja is the same as Frigg — They are separate goddesses in the primary sources, though they may share a common origin.

Freyja is a goddess of love and beauty only — She is also associated with war, death, magic, and fertility.

Freyja's cats are lions or large felines — The cats are not specified in the sources; they are often depicted as domestic or wild cats, not lions.
