Who It Is

In the freezing black waters beneath the Arctic ice, there is a god who decides who eats and who starves. He is Aipaloovik, the sea god of the Inuit — a power as vast, as cold, and as changeable as the ocean itself. He is not worshipped in temples or praised in hymns. He is propitiated with offerings of fresh water poured into holes in the ice, and he is feared with the deep, primal fear of a hunter who knows that the sea can take everything in a heartbeat. When the seals vanish and the ice groans, when the kayak tips and the water closes over, when the storm comes down from nowhere — that is Aipaloovik, reminding humanity who truly rules the Arctic.

Aipaloovik is a capricious and powerful deity who must be treated with profound respect. He controls the sea mammals — seals, walruses, whales — that are essential to Inuit survival. When properly honored, he provides abundant game and calm seas. When offended — through taboo violations, disrespect, or improper conduct — he withholds game, raises storms, and capsizes kayaks. He is described as a hunter's deity, more concerned with the conduct of men than women (in contrast to Sedna, who is primarily concerned with women's taboos). Powers
Control of Sea Mammals

Aipaloovik controls the behavior, location, and abundance of sea mammals — seals, walruses, whales, and fish. He can grant or withhold game from hunters, driving prey away from hunting grounds or delivering them into the hunter's path. He is described as keeping the sea mammals in a great underwater pen or house, releasing them when pleased.

Limit: His control is not absolute — Sedna, in traditions where she is supreme, can override his decisions. He is subject to the overarching rules of taboo and proper conduct.

Weather Control

Aipaloovik can raise storms, create calm seas, control currents, and influence ice conditions. He can capsize kayaks, break up ice floes, or provide safe passage. His weather control is directly tied to his mood and the conduct of humans.

Limit: His weather control appears limited to maritime conditions — he does not control land weather or the sky (which belongs to other spirits).

Shape-Shifting

Some accounts describe Aipaloovik as capable of taking the form of a sea mammal — particularly a large seal or walrus — to observe humans or to interact with them directly. In this form, he is said to be recognizable by his unusually intelligent eyes.

Limit: This power is rarely described and may be a later addition or a confusion with other shape-shifting spirits.

Punishment of Taboo Violations

Aipaloovik actively punishes those who violate hunting taboos, particularly those related to sea mammals. Punishments include withholding game, causing accidents at sea, drowning hunters, and sinking kayaks. He is particularly offended by the mixing of land and sea foods, improper handling of sea mammal bones, and disrespect toward sea mammals.

Limit: His punishments are generally limited to those who hunt or travel on the sea — land-based offenses are the concern of other spirits.

Accounts I
The Sea God and the HunterOral tradition collected by Franz Boas among Baffin Island Inuit, 1880s

Pre-contact to early contact period

A skilled hunter named Angutimmarik grew arrogant after many successful hunts. He boasted that he no longer needed to make offerings to Aipaloovik, saying 'the sea gives freely to those who are strong.' The sea god heard this boast and withdrew all sea mammals from the waters around the hunter's camp. For weeks, the hunter found nothing — no seals, no walruses, no fish. His family grew hungry. Finally, humbled, the hunter traveled to the floe edge and poured fresh water into the sea, begging forgiveness. Aipaloovik accepted the offering, and the sea mammals returned. The hunter never boasted again. The story reinforces the importance of humility and proper offerings. It demonstrates Aipaloovik's power to withhold game and his willingness to forgive genuine repentance.

Accounts II
The Offering of Fresh WaterOral tradition documented by Knud Rasmussen among the Netsilik Inuit, 1920s

Pre-contact to early contact period

During a winter of extreme scarcity, when the sea ice was too thick for seals to maintain their breathing holes, the community's angakkuq (shaman) was consulted. The shaman traveled in spirit form to the bottom of the sea, where he found Aipaloovik in his underwater house. The sea god was angry because hunters had failed to offer fresh water — a precious commodity in winter when all water is frozen. The shaman returned and instructed the community to melt snow and pour the fresh water into a hole in the sea ice. The offering was made, and within days, the seals returned to their breathing holes. The community survived the winter. The story establishes the importance of fresh water offerings to sea spirits and the role of the shaman as intermediary between humans and the sea god.

The Whale That Was a GodOral tradition from Alaska Yup'ik communities, documented by early 20th-century ethnographers

Pre-contact to early contact period

A whaling crew encountered a massive bowhead whale that seemed to be deliberately leading them away from good hunting grounds. The crew's captain, an experienced whaler, recognized the whale as Aipaloovik in disguise, testing the crew's respect. The captain ordered his men to lower their harpoons and instead pour fresh water on the whale's back. The whale dove deep and returned moments later, leading the crew to a pod of whales that filled their umiak. The captain explained to his crew that the sea god rewards those who show respect even to the animals they hunt. The story reinforces the importance of respect in hunting and the belief that sea mammals are not merely prey but beings with agency and connection to the divine.

Aipaloovik is just another name for Sedna — Aipaloovik is a distinct deity in most Inuit traditions, typically described as male and associated with sea hunting. While some early ethnographers conflated the two, Inuit tradition generally distinguishes between them.

Aipaloovik is a 'Poseidon' figure — While Aipaloovik is a sea deity, the comparison to Greek Poseidon is misleading. Inuit sea deities are not anthropomorphic gods in the Mediterranean sense — they are powerful spirits to be propitiated, not worshipped in temples. The relationship between humans and Aipaloovik is transactional and pragmatic, not devotional.

Inuit mythology is primitive or simple — Inuit cosmology is sophisticated and complex, with a rich pantheon of spirits, complex taboo systems, and deep philosophical engagement with the natural world. The apparent simplicity of some accounts reflects the difficulty of translating oral traditions into written form, not the simplicity of the beliefs themselves.

Aipaloovik is a demon or evil spirit — Aipaloovik is not evil in the Christian sense. He is a powerful natural force who punishes disrespect and rewards proper conduct. His actions are not motivated by malice but by the maintenance of cosmic order and respect for the natural world.
