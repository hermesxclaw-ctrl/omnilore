Who They Are

Aipaloovik's domain is the sea — specifically the sea ice, the open water, and the creatures that live within. He governs: (1) The behavior and availability of sea mammals — seals, walruses, whales, and fish; (2) The weather at sea — storms, calms, currents, and ice conditions; (3) The success or failure of sea hunting expeditions; (4) The safety of those who travel on the sea — kayakers, umiak crews, and ice fishers; (5) The boundary between the human world and the undersea world. He is sometimes described as dwelling in a great house at the bottom of the sea, similar to Sedna's dwelling, where he keeps the sea mammals in pens or cages. He is associated with the dangerous edges of the Inuit world — the floe edge where ice meets open water, the leads in the ice, the deep channels where whales travel. He is a liminal figure, dwelling between the known world of the ice and the unknown world of the deep.

Form

Aipaloovik is described in the sparse surviving accounts as a powerful male figure associated with the sea. Unlike Sedna, who is often depicted as a mermaid-like creature or a woman with fish-like lower body, Aipaloovik is typically described as fully anthropomorphic — a powerful man who dwells beneath the waves. Some accounts describe him as having a face that is 'like the sea' — changeable, sometimes calm, sometimes terrible. He may be depicted wearing sea-mammal skins or adorned with whalebone and ivory. In some traditions, he is described as having dark, wet-looking skin, like a seal or walrus, and hair that streams like kelp. His eyes are sometimes described as being like the surface of still water — reflecting but revealing nothing. Some accounts describe him as enormous, capable of rising from the depths like an iceberg. His voice, when heard, is described as like the sound of ice breaking or the roar of a storm. He is rarely depicted in Inuit art, unlike Sedna, suggesting his cult was less prominent or more localized.

Powers

Control of Sea Mammals Aipaloovik controls the behavior, location, and abundance of sea mammals — seals, walruses, whales, and fish. He can grant or withhold game from hunters, driving prey away from hunting grounds or delivering them into the hunter's path. He is described as keeping the sea mammals in a great underwater pen or house, releasing them when pleased. Attested across multiple Inuit traditions (Baffin Island, Netsilik, Alaska Yup'ik) His control is not absolute — Sedna, in traditions where she is supreme, can override his decisions. He is subject to the overarching rules of taboo and proper conduct. Some traditions describe him as subordinate to Sedna; others present him as independent or even superior.

Weather Control Aipaloovik can raise storms, create calm seas, control currents, and influence ice conditions. He can capsize kayaks, break up ice floes, or provide safe passage. His weather control is directly tied to his mood and the conduct of humans. Attested in Baffin Island and Greenlandic traditions His weather control appears limited to maritime conditions — he does not control land weather or the sky (which belongs to other spirits). Some accounts suggest he shares weather control with other deities, particularly Sedna and the wind spirits.

Shape-Shifting Some accounts describe Aipaloovik as capable of taking the form of a sea mammal — particularly a large seal or walrus — to observe humans or to interact with them directly. In this form, he is said to be recognizable by his unusually intelligent eyes. Occasional accounts from Labrador and Baffin Island traditions This power is rarely described and may be a later addition or a confusion with other shape-shifting spirits. Not attested in all traditions; may be a regional variant or a confusion with Sedna's transformations.

Punishment of Taboo Violations Aipaloovik actively punishes those who violate hunting taboos, particularly those related to sea mammals. Punishments include withholding game, causing accidents at sea, drowning hunters, and sinking kayaks. He is particularly offended by the mixing of land and sea foods, improper handling of sea mammal bones, and disrespect toward sea mammals. Attested across multiple Inuit traditions His punishments are generally limited to those who hunt or travel on the sea — land-based offenses are the concern of other spirits. The specific taboos he enforces vary between regions, suggesting local elaboration of his cult.

Behavior & Habitat

Aipaloovik is a capricious and powerful deity who must be treated with profound respect. He controls the sea mammals — seals, walruses, whales — that are essential to Inuit survival. When properly honored, he provides abundant game and calm seas. When offended — through taboo violations, disrespect, or improper conduct — he withholds game, raises storms, and capsizes kayaks. He is described as a hunter's deity, more concerned with the conduct of men than women (in contrast to Sedna, who is primarily concerned with women's taboos). He rewards skilled and respectful hunters with success and punishes the arrogant or careless. He is not evil — he is a force of nature, as impartial as the sea itself. He expects offerings, particularly of fresh water (which is scarce in the Arctic) and meat. He is particularly offended by the mixing of land and sea foods, or by cooking sea mammals over lamps fueled by land-animal fat. His temper is described as quick but not malicious — he punishes transgressions but is also quick to forgive when properly propitiated.

Accounts

A skilled hunter named Angutimmarik grew arrogant after many successful hunts. He boasted that he no longer needed to make offerings to Aipaloovik, saying 'the sea gives freely to those who are strong.' The sea god heard this boast and withdrew all sea mammals from the waters around the hunter's camp. For weeks, the hunter found nothing — no seals, no walruses, no fish. His family grew hungry. Finally, humbled, the hunter traveled to the floe edge and poured fresh water into the sea, begging forgiveness. Aipaloovik accepted the offering, and the sea mammals returned. The hunter never boasted again.

During a winter of extreme scarcity, when the sea ice was too thick for seals to maintain their breathing holes, the community's angakkuq (shaman) was consulted. The shaman traveled in spirit form to the bottom of the sea, where he found Aipaloovik in his underwater house. The sea god was angry because hunters had failed to offer fresh water — a precious commodity in winter when all water is frozen. The shaman returned and instructed the community to melt snow and pour the fresh water into a hole in the sea ice. The offering was made, and within days, the seals returned to their breathing holes. The community survived the winter.

A whaling crew encountered a massive bowhead whale that seemed to be deliberately leading them away from good hunting grounds. The crew's captain, an experienced whaler, recognized the whale as Aipaloovik in disguise, testing the crew's respect. The captain ordered his men to lower their harpoons and instead pour fresh water on the whale's back. The whale dove deep and returned moments later, leading the crew to a pod of whales that filled their umiak. The captain explained to his crew that the sea god rewards those who show respect even to the animals they hunt.

What We Don't Know

Significant gaps remain in the documentation of Aipaloovik. Most surviving accounts come from European ethnographers of the 19th and early 20th centuries, who may have imposed their own frameworks on Inuit beliefs. Contemporary Inuit elders may hold additional knowledge not yet documented. The relationship between Aipaloovik and other sea spirits in different Inuit regions remains incompletely understood. No comprehensive study of Aipaloovik as a distinct figure has been published. The deity's role in contemporary Inuit religious practice is undocumented. The exact etymology of the name remains uncertain. The relationship between Aipaloovik and the broader pan-Arctic sea deity tradition requires further research. A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Aipaloovik not as a god in the Greek or Norse sense — not a Zeus with a trident — but as the ocean itself given a face and a will. He is the reason the seals come and the reason they vanish. He is the storm that sinks the kayak and the calm that lets the hunter return home. He is the Arctic sea as a living being: beautiful, terrible, and utterly indifferent to human suffering. He is what you pray to when your family is hungry and the ice is too thick. He is what you fear when the water is too rough and the shore is too far. He is the sea — and in the Arctic, the sea is everything.
