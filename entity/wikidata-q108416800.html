Who It Is

Stand on the little bridge over the Pedele in northern Latvia and you can walk from one country to another before your coffee cools — Valka on the Latvian side, Valga on the Estonian side, one Livonian market town cut in half by a line drawn in 1920 and then erased, almost, by Schengen in 2007. Its slogan is not metaphor: "One Town, Two Countries."

As a municipality, Valka functions as an administrative, educational and service centre for northern Vidzeme. Historically characterized as a Livonian market and administrative town (Landtag seat), then as an educational centre (Vidzeme Teacher's Seminary — first higher education institution on territory of modern Latvia, per municipal history) and railway junction. Contemporary civic personality emphasizes cross-border cooperation, bilingual Latvian-Estonian everyday contact, and depopulation/economic challenges linked to higher wages in Estonia. Administrative Centre Function

Serves as centre of Valka Municipality, providing municipal government, primary school, gymnasium (secondary school), and Institute of Latvia-Estonia for further education. Historical administrative role as seat of the Landtag of the Livonian Confederation from 1419.

Limit: Small population and budget; many higher-order services concentrated in Valmiera, Riga, or Valga/Tartu. Post-2021 administrative reform reduced autonomy.

Educational Hub (Vidzeme Teacher's Seminary Legacy)

Late 19th-century importance derived from Vidzeme Teacher's Seminary (Vidzemes skolotāju seminārs), described as first higher education institution on Latvian territory, training Latvian and Estonian teachers (notably Jānis Cimze). Contemporary legacy via Valka Gymnasium and Institute of Latvia-Estonia.

Limit: Seminary closed long ago; current institutions are secondary/further education, not university-level.

Railway Junction and Transport Node

Developed in late 19th century as important railway junction in Livland. First narrow-gauge railway line in territory of modern Latvia stretched from Valka to Pärnu (Estonia). Current connection via Valga railway station (on Estonian side) links to Riga, Tartu and Tallinn networks; cross-border bus integration post-Schengen.

Limit: Narrow-gauge Valka-Pärnu line no longer operates as originally; current rail service depends on Estonian infrastructure at Valga station. Town itself has no independent long-distance station building in operation as of recent years.

Cross-Border Twin-Town Integration

Joint urban management with Valga under slogan 'One Town, Two Countries.' Since Schengen (2007) border crossing points removed, roads and fences opened, common public bus (line 3, ATG Bussiliinid OÜ, 3x daily as of 2025) and shared services. Enables free movement, joint cultural events, and labour market integration.

Limit: Legal, tax, health, education and residency systems remain separate national systems; 2016 reports of Valka residents registering in Valga for higher Estonian wages/benefits illustrate asymmetry. Bus frequency limited (3x daily).

Cultural and Theatrical Venue

Hosts Valka Town Theatre (Valkas pilsētas teātris) and local museum/heritage sites commemorating 1917 independence decision and flag raising. Serves as venue for Latvian-Estonian cultural exchange.

Limit: Small professional capacity; relies on regional and cross-border programming.

Accounts I
First Mention and Livonian Market Town (Walk)Livonian Confederation documents and Riga Archbishopric records (secondary summaries)

1286 (first mention) to 1419

Walk is first mentioned in 1286 as a settlement in Livonia. Its location on trade routes and the Pedele ford supported market functions. By 1419 it had risen to become the seat of the Landtag (diet) of the Livonian Confederation, the assembly of bishops, Order and vassals governing Terra Mariana. Established Walk as a political centre of Livonia despite modest size; Landtag tradition underpins later claims to historic importance.

City Rights under Stefan BatoryRoyal privilege of Stefan Batory, King of Poland and Grand Duke of Lithuania

1584

After the Livonian War and the incorporation of southern Livonia into the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, King Stefan Batory granted Walk city rights (Riga law model) in 1584, giving it municipal autonomy, market rights and legal status. Formal urban legal status; however, as municipal histories note, real growth came only centuries later.

Vidzeme Teacher's Seminary and Railway BoomLatvian educational histories; Estonian/Latvian railway histories; municipal history

Late 19th century (seminary 1853-1890s in Valka; railway junction development 1880s-1890s)

Walk/Valka gained national importance when the Vidzeme Teacher's Seminary (led by Jānis Cimze) operated here, training a generation of Latvian and Estonian teachers, choirmasters and cultural activists — described as the first higher education institution on Latvian territory. Simultaneously, the town became a railway junction, with the first narrow-gauge line in modern Latvian territory built from Valka to Pärnu, connecting Livland to the Baltic coast and stimulating commerce and population growth. Created an educated Latvian-Estonian elite; established Valka as a transport hub; population and urban development peaked before WWI.

Accounts II
The 1917 Decision for Latvian Independence and First Flag RaisingProtocols of the Latvian Provisional National Council; Latvian historiography; Valka Municipality commemoration

15 November 1917

In Valka, then near the front line and hosting many Latvian refugees and activists, the Latvian Provisional National Council (Latviešu Pagaidu Nacionālā padome) met and resolved to proclaim an independent Republic of Latvia. On this occasion the red-white-red Latvian flag was raised for the first time in Valka. The town served as a de facto centre for Latvian political organization while Riga was under German occupation. Symbolic birthplace of independent Latvia narrative; Valka's central place in Latvian national memory; led to 1918 proclamation in Riga and subsequent War of Independence.

Division of Walk into Valka and Valga (Tallents Line)Tallents Commission arbitration; Estonian and Latvian interwar diplomatic records; British Foreign Office papers

1918-1920 (dispute); 1 July 1920 (division implemented)

After WWI and the Estonian and Latvian Wars of Independence, both new states claimed Walk, which had an ethnically mixed population (Latvian, Estonian, German, Russian). Clashes and competing administrations led to British mediation. An international jury headed by Colonel Stephen George Tallents drew a border through the town, largely along ethnic settlement patterns and the Pedele, awarding the larger northeastern part (including the railway station) to Estonia as Valga and the southwestern part to Latvia as Valka. The compromise was implemented on 1 July 1920. Created two separate towns where one had existed; families and streets divided; railway station ended up on Estonian side, shaping economic asymmetry for a century. Border became international and later Soviet internal, then EU external, then Schengen internal.

Schengen Erasure and Contemporary Twin-Town LifeEU Schengen enlargement documents; Estonian/Latvian government press releases 2007-2008; Estonian Public Broadcasting (ERR) and Latvian media 2016; ATG Bussiliinid schedule 2025 per supplied material

2007-present

On 21 December 2007 Estonia and Latvia joined the Schengen Area, abolishing systematic border controls. At Valka-Valga all border crossing points were removed and roads and fences opened. The towns announced common public transport; as of 2025 Estonian company ATG Bussiliinid OÜ operates city bus No. 3 connecting Valga station and Valka three times daily. In 2016 media reported that due to higher welfare and salaries in Estonia, many Valka residents had registered as inhabitants of Valga while continuing to live/work across the border, highlighting economic disparity despite open borders. Joint branding 'One Town, Two Countries' is used for tourism and EU Interreg projects. Restored de facto urban unity for daily life while preserving two legal systems; increased commuting, joint events, and EU-funded cooperation; exposed fiscal and social policy arbitrage between the two states.

Valka and Valga are the same town with two names. — Since 1 July 1920 they are two legally distinct towns in two sovereign states (Latvia and Estonia) with separate administrations, budgets, and legal systems, though urban fabric is contiguous and Schengen allows free movement. Twin-town branding does not equal juridical merger.

Valka is a fictional character (heroic legend) from folklore or fantasy. — Valka is a real inhabited Latvian border town with documented history since 1286. Confusion arises from the fictional character Valka in DreamWorks' How to Train Your Dragon 2 (2014) and other fantasy uses of the name, plus erroneous metadata in supplied prompt labeling Type=Fictional character.

The Latvian flag was first created or first raised in Riga in 1918. — The red-white-red flag design has earlier origins, but its first raising as a national symbol is commemorated in Valka on 15 November 1917 during the Latvian Provisional National Council session; the 18 November 1918 proclamation in Riga is the independence declaration, not the first flag raising.

The border between Valka and Valga was always open and the towns were always twins. — The towns were a single town (Walk) until 1920, then a hard international border (1920-1940), then a Soviet internal SSR border with controls (1940-1991), then an EU external border with customs (1991-2007), and only since 21 December 2007 a Schengen open border. 'Twin towns' is a post-1920 concept and post-2007 cooperation model.
