Who It Is

Haumea is the Hawaiian goddess of fertility, childbirth, and the earth — a powerful, ancient deity who is both the mother of Pele and the other fire goddesses and a shapeshifting trickster in her own right. She is able to take on different forms, particularly appearing as an old woman to test or help humans. Haumea is associated with the hala (pandanus) tree and with the childbirth practices of ancient Hawaiians. She is the mother of many important deities, including Pele, Kāne-milohaʻi, Kā-moho-aliʻi, Hiʻiaka, Laka, and others. In some traditions, she is married to Wākea (Sky Father). A key myth involves Haumea's magical ability to give birth from different parts of her body, enabling her to have many children without aging. She also saved her daughter Pele from the sea goddess Namaka by throwing her across the islands. Haumea represents the generative, nurturing power of the earth, but also its cycles of death and rebirth. The dwarf planet Haumea (discovered 2004) is named after her, as are its moons Hiʻiaka and Namaka.

Origins

Haumea is one of the most ancient Hawaiian deities, sometimes described as arriving from Kahiki (Tahiti). She is the wife of Wākea (Sky Father) and mother of the goddesses who shaped the Hawaiian landscape. She taught Hawaiians the sacred birthing practices and blessed the hala tree.

The Approach

In the volcanic heart of Hawaiʻi, where the earth splits and gives birth to islands, there dwells a goddess of formidable power and paradoxical grace—Haumea, the mother of Pele and the keeper of the land's fecundity. Her story is one of transformation, of life wrested from stone, and of a divine family whose fiery passions shaped the archipelago itself.

Content advisory. Contains themes of incest, divine violence, and coercion in some traditional narratives. These elements are presented with scholarly context and do not reflect modern values.

Etymology

The name 'Haumea' is often interpreted as 'red' or 'sacred' (from 'haʻu' meaning 'red' or 'sacred', and 'mea' meaning 'thing' or 'being'), but the exact etymology is uncertain and debated among linguists.

Cultural Ownership

Native Hawaiian religion and culture

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars equate Haumea with the earth goddess Papa, wife of Wakea, though this is not universally accepted. Others see her as a distinct deity associated with childbirth and fertility.

Description

Appearance

Haumea is not consistently described in physical form; she is a shape-shifter, often taking the form of an old woman or a young maiden. In some stories, she appears as a beautiful woman, in others as a hag. No canonical physical description exists.

Symbols & Iconography

Associated with the earth, fertility, childbirth, and the island of Hawaiʻi itself. She is linked to the ʻōhiʻa tree and the lehua blossom, and to the volcanic forces that create land.

Behaviour & Personality

Haumea is depicted as a powerful, sometimes capricious deity, deeply connected to the land and its life-giving forces. She is a mother figure, though her actions can be morally ambiguous, especially in stories involving her relationships with her descendants.

Domains & Habitats

She resides in the earth and is associated with the island of Hawaiʻi, particularly the volcanic regions. She is a goddess of childbirth, fertility, and the earth itself.

Version Differences

In some traditions, Haumea is identified with Papa, the earth mother, while in others she is a separate deity. Her role in the birth of Pele varies: sometimes she is Pele's mother, sometimes her grandmother. The stories of her incestuous relationships with her descendants are present in some versions but suppressed or altered in others.

Stories & Myths

The Birth of Pele

Source Various Hawaiian oral traditions, documented by Beckwith and othersCulture HawaiianPeriod Pre-contact to 19th century

Summary

Haumea gives birth to Pele, the volcano goddess, often from her thigh or in a miraculous manner. In some versions, Pele is born from Haumea's side, and the birth is accompanied by volcanic eruptions and earthquakes.

Participants

Haumea

Pele

other siblings

Consequences

Pele becomes the dominant volcano deity, and her rivalry with her sister Nāmaka leads to the creation of the Hawaiian Islands.

Variants

In some accounts, Haumea is Pele's grandmother, and the birth is attributed to other deities.

Haumea and the Incestuous Unions

Source Documented in Fornander and other collectionsCulture HawaiianPeriod Pre-contact

Summary

Haumea engages in relationships with her descendants to renew the earth and maintain her divine power. These stories are often used to explain the sacred nature of certain genealogies and the concept of divine incest in Hawaiian nobility.

Participants

Haumea

various descendants

Consequences

These unions produce powerful offspring and reinforce the mana (spiritual power) of certain lines, but also present moral complexities.

Variants

Some versions omit these stories or reinterpret them symbolically.

Dark History

Incestuous relationships

Source Fornander, Beckwith

Description

Haumea is said to have had sexual relations with her descendants to maintain her youth and power. These stories are part of sacred genealogies but are often uncomfortable for modern readers.

Scholarly Context

Incest among Hawaiian deities is interpreted as symbolic of the cyclical nature of life and the renewal of the land, not as a sanction for human behavior. In Hawaiian culture, the aliʻi (nobility) sometimes practiced incest to preserve mana, but this was not common or universally accepted.

Violent childbirth

Source Various traditions

Description

The birth of Pele is described as violent, involving volcanic eruptions and earthquakes, which may reflect the destructive power of nature.

Scholarly Context

This is a typical motif in volcano goddess myths, representing the dual creative/destructive nature of volcanic forces.

Contradictions & Disputes

Haumea and Papa

Position A

Haumea is the same as Papa, the earth mother.

Position A — evidence

Some genealogies use the names interchangeably; both are associated with the earth and Wakea.

Position B

Haumea is a distinct deity from Papa.

Position B — evidence

Other traditions give Haumea a distinct role in the birth of Pele and her own set of myths.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars treat them as separate but related deities, with syncretism occurring in some traditions.

Pele's parentage

Position A

Haumea is Pele's mother.

Position A — evidence

Many chants explicitly name Haumea as Pele's mother.

Position B

Haumea is Pele's grandmother.

Position B — evidence

Some genealogical lists place Haumea one generation above Pele's mother.

Scholarly Consensus

The mother version is more common, but the grandmother version is accepted as a variant.
