Who It Is

He has no speeches, no epithets, no aristeia — only a name on a death list. Hagius of Dulichium is one of the fifty-seven young aristocrats who sailed to Ithaca to claim Penelope and never sailed home, his entire mythic existence compressed into a single catalogue line and a mass grave at the end of the Odyssey.

No individual personality, speeches, or deeds are preserved. Collectively, the suitors — and therefore Hagius by inclusion — are characterized in the Odyssey and Apollodorus as: violating xenia (guest-host law) by occupying Odysseus' oikos, consuming his livestock and wine, pressuring Penelope for marriage, plotting to murder Telemachus (Od. 16.383-385, 20.241), and insulting servants (Melanthius, maids). In Apollodorus, they are simply a list of names awaiting execution. Whether Hagius was among the more violent (like Antinous and Eurymachus) or more moderate (like Amphinomus) is unat

Powers
Mortal aristocratic status

As a suitor from Dulichium, Hagius is implicitly of high birth and wealth sufficient to compete for Penelope, whose marriage would confer control over Odysseus' estate and alliance with Ithaca. This is a social power, not supernatural.

Limit: Status confers no authority once Odysseus returns; collectively the suitors fail to secure marriage and are legally and morally condemned for violating xenia.

Conventional heroic-age martial ability (inferred, unattested individually)

All suitors are assumed capable of bearing arms (spears, swords, bows) and are killed in armed combat in Od. 22. No feat is individually attributed to Hagius; he is slain along with the group by Odysseus, Telemachus, Eumaeus and Philoetius, with Athena's aid.

Limit: Overwhelmed by Odysseus' archery and close combat; no resistance succeeds. No attested kill, wound, or defense by Hagius. Mortal vulnerability to weapons and death.

No supernatural powers

No divine parentage, transformation, prophecy, magic, or superhuman ability is ever attributed to Hagius in any source. He is a fully mortal human without cult or heroization.

Accounts I
The Catalogue of the Suitors from DulichiumPseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca, Epitome 7.26-27

1st-2nd century CE (compilation); purports to describe heroic age c. 13th century BCE mythic time

Apollodorus, after summarizing the Odyssey, provides the only complete prosopography of Penelope's suitors to fill Homer's numerical gap. He lists 57 names from Dulichium, including Hagius, alongside 23 from Same, 29 from Zacynthus, and 12 from Ithaca (total 121 in some manuscripts due to textual variation, vs. Homer's 108). Hagius appears mid-list among Dulichians with no patronymic, epithet, or deed. The catalogue functions as an antiquarian appendix, turning Homer's anonymous mass into named individuals for completeness. This is Hagius' sole attestation as a distinct figure. Establishes Hagius' canonical identity and origin; provides the only source for his name. Later lexica (e.g., Bell's New Pantheon, modern databases) derive entirely from this passage.

The Occupation of the House of OdysseusHomer, Odyssey Books 1-4, 13-20

c. 8th-7th century BCE

During Odysseus' twenty-year absence (ten at Troy, ten wandering), 108 aristocrats from Dulichium, Same, Zacynthus and Ithaca occupy his palace on Ithaca. They feast daily on his cattle, sheep, goats and wine, sleep with his maidservants, and press Penelope to choose a new husband, arguing that her husband is dead and the oikos needs a kyrios. Penelope delays by the stratagem of weaving and unweaving a shroud for Laertes. Hagius, as one of the Dulichians, is by definition part of this collective occupation, though no individual action is ascribed to him. The narrative emphasizes their collective violation of xenia (hospitality), hubris, and atē (recklessness). Sets moral and legal justification for their eventual slaughter; demonstrates the social crisis of an absent basileus. The suitors' presence drives Telemachus' journey to Pylos and Sparta.

Penelope's Stratagem of the ShroudHomer, Odyssey 2.93-110, 19.137-156, 24.129-148; Apollodorus Epit. 7.31

Archaic to Hellenistic

To avoid choosing among the suitors, Penelope announces she will choose a husband after she finishes weaving a burial shroud (sindon) for Laertes, Odysseus' aged father. For three years she weaves by day and unravels by night, deceiving the suitors. When a maid betrays her, the suitors demand she finish and choose. This story does not name Hagius but explains how he and the others were kept in suspense for years. It foregrounds Penelope's mētis (cunning) against the suitors' pressure. Delays the marriage decision, preserves the household, and heightens suitor frustration, leading to their plot against Telemachus. In reception, the shroud becomes a feminist symbol of resistance.

Accounts II
The Plot to Ambush TelemachusHomer, Odyssey 4.660-672, 16.344-408; Apollodorus Epit. 7.32

Archaic

When Telemachus sails to Pylos and Sparta to seek news of Odysseus, Antinous leads a faction of suitors to ambush and kill him in the strait between Ithaca and Same upon his return. Amphinomus dissents. The plot fails because Athena warns Telemachus and he evades them. Hagius is not named as participant or dissenter; as a Dulichian he is statistically likely to have been counted among the conspirators in Homer's collective phrasing ('the suitors'), but no source distinguishes his stance. The episode marks the suitors' transition from parasitic guests to attempted murderers. Seals the suitors' guilt beyond xenia-violation to blood-guilt, justifying lethal retribution. Telemachus' survival allows him to fight alongside his father.

The Mnesterophonia: Slaughter of the SuitorsHomer, Odyssey Book 22; Apollodorus Epitome 7.33; cf. Hyginus Fab. 126

Archaic (Homer) and 1st-2nd century CE (Apollodorus)

Upon revealing himself, Odysseus, with Telemachus, Eumaeus and Philoetius, and with Athena's support, locks the hall and kills all suitors. He begins with Antinous shot through the throat with an arrow, then Eurymachus, then systematically kills the rest as they scramble for weapons. The Dulichians, including Hagius, are killed in this mass execution; no individual death scene is given for Hagius. In Odyssey 22, Leodes and Phemius and Medon are singled out for supplication, and Amphinomus is killed by Telemachus, but the majority die anonymously. Apollodorus summarizes: 'Odysseus with Telemachus and Eumaeus and Philoetius slew them all.' Hagius' death is therefore collective and unattested in detail. Restores Odysseus' oikos and kingship; suitors' souls are led by Hermes to Hades in Odyssey 24 (second nekyia). Their families later seek vengeance (Od. 24.412-548) until Athena imposes peace. For Hagius, this is narrative terminus; no burial, hero cult, or afterlife individuality is described.

The Second Nekyia: The Shades of the Suitors in HadesHomer, Odyssey 24.1-204

Archaic

Hermes psychopompos leads the shades (psychai) of the slain suitors, gibbering like bats, to Hades where they encounter Achilles, Agamemnon and others. Amphimedon, a Dulichian suitor, narrates their death to Agamemnon. The passage provides the only post-mortem collective fate for Dulichians. Hagius, as one of the slain, is implicitly among the bat-like shades, though not named. Agamemnon contrasts Penelope's fidelity with Clytemnestra's treachery, framing the suitors' death as just. Provides eschatological closure; no individual afterlife for Hagius beyond collective shade. No heroization or cult follows, unlike some other slain heroes.

Hagius was a major suitor with a distinct personality, speeches, or duel with Odysseus. — No ancient source gives Hagius any individual words, deeds, or death scene. He is only a name in a list (Apollodorus Epit. 7.26) and dies anonymously in the collective slaughter (Homer Od. 22; Apollodorus Epit. 7.33). Any detailed biography online that assigns him traits (e.g., 'cruel', 'handsome', 'leader') is modern invention or conflation with Antinous/Eurymachus.

Hagius appears in Homer's Odyssey by name. — Homer never names Hagius. The Odyssey counts 52 Dulichians but names only a few suitors individually (Antinous, Eurymachus, Amphinomus, Agelaus, Leocritus, etc.). Hagius first appears ~800 years after Homer in the Bibliotheca attributed to Apollodorus.

The name Hagius means 'holy saint' and indicates he was a priest or had religious powers. — Ἅγιος means 'holy, devoted to the gods' as an adjective, but as a personal name in the heroic age it does not confer priesthood or supernatural power. No source depicts Hagius as a priest, seer, or holy man; the etymology is onomastic, not functional. The Christian sense of 'Saint Hagios' is anachronistic by ~1000 years.

All suitors came from Dulichium or all were from Ithaca. — In both Homer and Apollodorus, suitors came from four distinct places: Dulichium (largest contingent, including Hagius), Same (Cephallenia), Zacynthus, and Ithaca itself. Hagius is specifically Dulichian, not Ithacan, meaning he was an overseas interloper, not a local Ithacan noble.
