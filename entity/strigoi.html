Who It Is

In Romanian mythology, strigoi are troubled spirits of the dead that have risen from the grave. They are attributed with the ability to transform into animals, become invisible, and drain the vitality and blood of their victims. Strigoi are closely related to the Italian strega (witch) and the Latin strix (screech-owl, a blood-drinking night-bird), from which the Romanian term derives. There are two types: strigoi vii (living strigoi — witches born with a caul who can send their souls out at night) and strigoi morți (dead strigoi — reanimated corpses that return to feed on their families). Traditional apotropaic practices include exhuming suspected strigoi and driving a stake through the heart, removing the heart and burning it, or placing garlic in the mouth. Bram Stoker's Dracula drew heavily on strigoi folklore.

Form

Attested descriptions in Romanian oral narratives and ethnographic reports portray strigoi mort as a corpse that does not rest properly: the body may appear unnaturally preserved, swollen, ruddy, or with blood at the mouth, and is often suspected when the grave earth sinks or moves, or when noises are heard near the cemetery at night. Living strigoi are described as ordinary people with subtle signs—being born with a caul, a tail-like appendage, or other bodily anomalies; having unusual eyes; or bearing a reputation for malefic influence—who can leave their bodies at night in spirit or animal form. Reconstructed imagery, influenced by modern vampire fiction, often adds fangs, aristocratic clothing, and bat-like features, but these are adaptation-specific and not consistently present in traditional accounts.

Return from the grave Strigoi mort are believed to rise from their graves, physically or in spirit, to visit homes, attack sleepers, or roam the village, especially at night or on certain ritual dates. Traditional Romanian oral folklore as documented in Oltenia and other regions; analyzed by Chereches & Badea and Colta. Their activity is typically constrained to night-time and to periods of ritual vulnerability; exhumation, staking, burning the heart, or specific Christian rites are believed to end their power. Some accounts describe only spiritual visitation (dreams, apparitions), while others insist on physical exhumation signs; scholars debate whether these represent different belief layers or narrative exaggeration.

Draining vitality and causing illness Strigoi are said to ‘suck’ life, luck, or health from victims, leading to wasting diseases, insomnia, nightmares, or sudden weakness, sometimes described metaphorically as drinking blood. Romanian oral narratives and ethnographic reports; generalized in modern vampire scholarship. The effect can be reversed or halted through protective rituals, charms, and community intervention; not all misfortune is automatically attributed to strigoi, and accusations are socially negotiated. Older sources emphasize generalized ‘consumption’ or misfortune rather than explicit blood-drinking; modern horror adaptations often retroject the blood motif, blurring the line between strigoi and imported vampire imagery.

Shape-shifting and nocturnal wandering Living strigoi are believed to leave their bodies at night, taking the form of animals (cats, dogs, wolves, birds) or luminous shapes, and wandering to harm or spy on others. Folkloric accounts summarized in ethnological works and in the Wikipedia overview; discussed comparatively by Žiga. The wandering is tied to sleep or trance states; certain rituals at birth or death aim to prevent the emergence of such abilities. Some traditions treat this as metaphorical or as a dream phenomenon, while others insist on literal transformation; scholarly consensus sees a spectrum from psychological explanation to full supernatural belief.

Weather and harvest influence Strigoi are occasionally blamed for storms, crop failures, or blights, acting as agents of cosmic disorder when the dead are not properly appeased. Central European folk imagination as analyzed by Colta, with Romanian examples. This power is diffuse and not individually targeted; communal rituals and church services are used to restore balance. Not all regions attribute weather phenomena to strigoi; some reserve such powers for other beings (vârcolac, vâlve), leading to overlapping and contested attributions.

Ambivalent protection and consolation A minority of narratives depict strigoi (often recently deceased relatives) returning to comfort family members, warn them of dangers, or help resolve unfinished business. Oral testimony from Oltenia analyzed by Chereches & Badea, highlighting non-malevolent aspects. Such benevolent visits are usually brief and tied to mourning; they may still be considered dangerous if prolonged or if they prevent proper detachment. This ambivalence contrasts with the dominant image of strigoi as harmful; some scholars interpret these figures as closer to ancestral spirits than to vampires, questioning whether they should be labeled strigoi at all.

Behavior & Habitat

In oral folklore, strigoi are halfway beings who cross repeatedly between the worlds of the living and the dead. Some return to torment relatives, drain their vitality, cause wasting illnesses, or ruin harvests and livestock; others, more ambivalent, may appear to comfort family members or warn them of dangers. They are associated with nocturnal wandering, haunting houses and stables, sitting on sleepers’ chests, and ‘stealing’ milk or luck. Living strigoi are blamed for the evil eye, sudden misfortunes, and unexplained deaths, and may unconsciously or deliberately project harmful forces. Modern literary and cinematic versions emphasize predatory, blood-drinking behavior and a more individualized, villainous personality, often giving strigoi motives of revenge, hunger, or existential loneliness that are largely interpretive rather than directly attested.

Accounts

An elderly informant recounts episodes in which villagers suspect a deceased person of becoming a strigoi after a series of misfortunes—animals dying suddenly, children falling ill, and strange noises near the cemetery. The community decides to exhume the body, discovering signs interpreted as continued life (fresh-looking corpse, blood). They stake the heart and burn it, performing prayers to end the haunting. The narrative interweaves explanations of the evil eye, birth with a caul, and other protective rituals, showing how strigoi belief structures everyday responses to crisis.

Alecsandri’s poems draw on rural superstition about strigoi to create atmospheric narratives of nocturnal fear, graveyard encounters, and the tension between rational enlightenment and lingering belief. The strigoi figure appears as a spectral embodiment of guilt and unresolved love, blending folkloric motifs with romantic literary sensibility.

In Vampirul, the first Romanian novel to feature an explicit vampire antagonist, imported vampire imagery from French and German models intersects with local strigoi superstition. The narrative stages a conflict between modern, urban characters and a rural environment haunted by undead beliefs, using strigoi as a background superstition that both foreshadows and contrasts with the more spectacular, foreign-style vampire.

Field Notes

† Not all strigoi are purely evil in folklore; some return to comfort relatives or warn them of danger, blurring the line between monster and ancestor.

A way to hold it in your mind

If the modern vampire is a sleek export product—refined in novels, films, and streaming series—then the strigoi is the raw, local ingredient: a knot of fears about death, envy, and unfinished business that villagers tried to manage with shovels, prayers, and whispered stories. To understand strigoi is to listen to how a community talks about what happens when the dead refuse to let the living go, and how those conversations were later repackaged into the global vampire myth.
