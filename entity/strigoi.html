Who It Is

Imagine a village where the dead do not always stay politely in their graves—where a sudden illness, a dying cow, or a strange noise at the cemetery can trigger a midnight gathering, shovels in hand, to confront a corpse suspected of still walking among the living. That uneasy figure, halfway between superstition and nightmare, is the strigoi: Romania’s restless dead, whose story is less about caped aristocrats and more about the fragile line between community, memory, and fear.

In oral folklore, strigoi are halfway beings who cross repeatedly between the worlds of the living and the dead. Some return to torment relatives, drain their vitality, cause wasting illnesses, or ruin harvests and livestock; others, more ambivalent, may appear to comfort family members or warn them of dangers. They are associated with nocturnal wandering, haunting houses and stables, sitting on sleepers’ chests, and ‘stealing’ milk or luck. Powers
Return from the grave

Strigoi mort are believed to rise from their graves, physically or in spirit, to visit homes, attack sleepers, or roam the village, especially at night or on certain ritual dates.

Limit: Their activity is typically constrained to night-time and to periods of ritual vulnerability; exhumation, staking, burning the heart, or specific Christian rites are believed to end their power.

Draining vitality and causing illness

Strigoi are said to ‘suck’ life, luck, or health from victims, leading to wasting diseases, insomnia, nightmares, or sudden weakness, sometimes described metaphorically as drinking blood.

Limit: The effect can be reversed or halted through protective rituals, charms, and community intervention; not all misfortune is automatically attributed to strigoi, and accusations are socially negotiated.

Shape-shifting and nocturnal wandering

Living strigoi are believed to leave their bodies at night, taking the form of animals (cats, dogs, wolves, birds) or luminous shapes, and wandering to harm or spy on others.

Limit: The wandering is tied to sleep or trance states; certain rituals at birth or death aim to prevent the emergence of such abilities.

Weather and harvest influence

Strigoi are occasionally blamed for storms, crop failures, or blights, acting as agents of cosmic disorder when the dead are not properly appeased.

Limit: This power is diffuse and not individually targeted; communal rituals and church services are used to restore balance.

Ambivalent protection and consolation

A minority of narratives depict strigoi (often recently deceased relatives) returning to comfort family members, warn them of dangers, or help resolve unfinished business.

Limit: Such benevolent visits are usually brief and tied to mourning; they may still be considered dangerous if prolonged or if they prevent proper detachment.

Accounts I
The Oltenia strigoi testimonyOral narrative from M. Trăistaru (Comănești, Olt), recorded in Madrid, 2016; analyzed by Chereches & Badea.

20th–21st century oral tradition

An elderly informant recounts episodes in which villagers suspect a deceased person of becoming a strigoi after a series of misfortunes—animals dying suddenly, children falling ill, and strange noises near the cemetery. The community decides to exhume the body, discovering signs interpreted as continued life (fresh-looking corpse, blood). They stake the heart and burn it, performing prayers to end the haunting. The narrative interweaves explanations of the evil eye, birth with a caul, and other protective rituals, showing how strigoi belief structures everyday responses to crisis. The ritual restores a sense of safety and order; the story reinforces communal norms about proper burial, vigilance against envy, and the necessity of collective action against supernatural threats.

Accounts II
Vasile Alecsandri’s ‘Strigoiul’ and related poemsPoetic works by Vasile Alecsandri, discussed in Žiga’s thesis under “Două poezii ale lui Vasile Alecsandri” and “Strigoiul”.

19th century

Alecsandri’s poems draw on rural superstition about strigoi to create atmospheric narratives of nocturnal fear, graveyard encounters, and the tension between rational enlightenment and lingering belief. The strigoi figure appears as a spectral embodiment of guilt and unresolved love, blending folkloric motifs with romantic literary sensibility. These poems help canonize strigoi as a literary motif, bridging oral tradition and written culture and influencing later Romanian authors who treat strigoi as a symbol of backwardness, superstition, or national gothic.

Strigoi and the early Romanian vampire novel ‘Vampirul’G.M. Amza & Al. Bilciurescu, Vampirul (1938), as contextualized by Martin.

Interwar period (1930s)

In Vampirul, the first Romanian novel to feature an explicit vampire antagonist, imported vampire imagery from French and German models intersects with local strigoi superstition. The narrative stages a conflict between modern, urban characters and a rural environment haunted by undead beliefs, using strigoi as a background superstition that both foreshadows and contrasts with the more spectacular, foreign-style vampire. The novel marks a shift from strigoi as primarily a superstition label to a component of a broader, transnational vampire mythos, contributing to the later conflation of strigoi and Dracula in popular imagination.

Living vs dead strigoi (strigoi viu vs strigoi mort)Some traditions and scholars treat living and dead strigoi as distinct categories: living strigoi are humans with special marks or powers, while dead strigoi are revenant corpses.Other accounts present them as phases of the same condition, with a marked person becoming a strigoi in life and continuing as a strigoi after death.
Strigoi as vampires vs ghosts vs witchesPopular culture and some scholarship classify strigoi primarily as vampires, emphasizing blood-drinking and undead corporeality.Other researchers stress their overlap with ghosts, witches, and generalized evil spirits, arguing that ‘vampire’ is an imported label that only partially fits traditional strigoi.
Etymology and linguistic originStrigoi derives directly from Latin striga/strix via Romance developments (e.g., Italian strega), reflecting continuity from ancient witch-bird imagery.The term may have developed through multiple influences, including Balkan and Central European witch and vampire vocabularies, making a simple linear derivation uncertain.
Romani vs Romanian cultural ownershipSome popular sources and casual references describe strigoi as a ‘Romani’ or ‘Gypsy’ vampire, linking the figure to Roma communities and stereotypes.Ethnographic and linguistic evidence places strigoi firmly in Romanian-speaking rural culture, with no strong indication that it originates specifically in Romani tradition.
