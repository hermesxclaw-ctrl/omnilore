Who It Is

Imagine a goddess who commands the snows of a mountain so tall that it rises from the seafloor to the heavens, and who dares to challenge the fire goddess Pele herself. That is Poliʻahu, the Lady of the White Mantle, whose icy breath can quench the most furious lava. Her story is one of love, betrayal, and the eternal struggle between fire and ice, and it invites us to explore the sacred peaks of Hawaiʻi with new eyes.

Poliʻahu is portrayed as a calm, dignified, and somewhat aloof deity, embodying the cold and serene nature of the snow. She is known for her rivalry with Pele, the volcano goddess, and she often engages in contests of power, such as covering Pele's lava flows with snow. Despite her cool demeanor, she is also compassionate, as seen in her love for the chief Aiwohikupua, though she ultimately chooses her divine duty over mortal love. She is a protector of the mountain and its sacredness, and she is said to punish those who disrespect her domain.

Powers
Snow and Ice Manipulation

Poliʻahu can summon snowstorms, freeze the ground, and cover vast areas with ice and snow. She uses this power to protect her mountain home and to challenge Pele's volcanic forces, often blanketing Pele's lava flows with snow to cool and stop them.

Limit:

Weather Control

As a snow goddess, Poliʻahu has control over winter weather patterns, including cold fronts, blizzards, and freezing temperatures. She can create extreme cold that affects the entire island, and her presence is said to bring a drop in temperature.

Limit:

Immortality and Divine Form

Poliʻahu is an immortal deity who can appear in human form or as a spirit of the mountain. She is not subject to aging or death, and she can travel between the spiritual and mortal realms at will.

Limit:

Shapeshifting

In some legends, Poliʻahu can transform herself into a beautiful woman to interact with mortals, or into a cold mist or snowstorm to conceal her movements. This ability allows her to move unseen and to test the intentions of those who approach her domain.

Accounts I
Poliʻahu and AiwohikupuaHawaiian oral tradition, recorded in various collections

Pre-contact to 19th century

Poliʻahu met the chief Aiwohikupua on the eastern slope of Mauna Kea, and the two fell deeply in love. Aiwohikupua took her to his home on Kauaʻi, but upon arrival, Poliʻahu discovered that he was already betrothed to another princess. Feeling betrayed, she left him and returned to Mauna Kea, vowing to remain a solitary goddess. In some versions, she punishes Aiwohikupua by freezing his heart or turning him into a stone, while in others she simply disappears, leaving him to regret his actions. Poliʻahu's heart is hardened, and she becomes more distant from mortals, focusing on her role as a snow goddess. Aiwohikupua is left with a broken heart and a lesson about honesty and fidelity.

Accounts II
The Battle of Fire and Snow: Poliʻahu vs. PeleHawaiian mythology, as told in various sources

Pre-contact to present

Pele, the volcano goddess, sought to expand her domain by sending lava flows down the slopes of Mauna Kea. Poliʻahu, angered by this intrusion, descended from the summit and engaged Pele in a fierce battle. The two goddesses fought for days, with Pele hurling molten rock and Poliʻahu responding with blizzards and ice. Ultimately, Poliʻahu's cold proved stronger, and she froze Pele's lava, forcing the volcano goddess to retreat. The battle left the landscape scarred, but Poliʻahu emerged victorious, and the snows of Mauna Kea remained undisturbed. The rivalry between Poliʻahu and Pele is established, and the two become eternal enemies. The battle also explains the presence of both snow and volcanic rock on Mauna Kea, as the two forces continue to clash.

The Love of Poliʻahu (A Modern Retelling)Hawaiian folklore anthology, 2010

Contemporary

This modern retelling focuses on Poliʻahu's emotional journey after her failed love affair with Aiwohikupua. It explores her loneliness and her decision to embrace her role as a guardian of the mountain, finding solace in the cold and the silence. The story emphasizes her strength and independence, and it ends with her watching over Mauna Kea, a sentinel of snow. The story reinforces Poliʻahu's identity as a powerful, self-sufficient deity, and it offers a more sympathetic portrayal of her character.
