Poli'ahu

Snow Goddess of Mauna Kea; Lady of the White Mantle; Queen of the Summit

TypeSnow/Winter Deity / Nature Spirit
CultureHawaiian
Who It Is
Form

Poliʻahu is typically depicted as a beautiful, ethereal woman with fair skin and long, flowing black hair that cascades like the dark volcanic rock of Mauna Kea. She is often shown wearing a white, flowing robe or mantle that represents the snow covering the mountain's summit, and her presence is accompanied by a chill in the air and the scent of frost. In some artistic representations, she is surrounded by snowflakes or icicles, and her eyes are said to be as cold and clear as the winter sky. Her beauty is described as both alluring and intimidating, reflecting the harsh yet majestic nature of the high-altitude environment she inhabits.

Powers

Snow and Ice Manipulation Poliʻahu can summon snowstorms, freeze the ground, and cover vast areas with ice and snow. She uses this power to protect her mountain home and to challenge Pele's volcanic forces, often blanketing Pele's lava flows with snow to cool and stop them.

Weather Control As a snow goddess, Poliʻahu has control over winter weather patterns, including cold fronts, blizzards, and freezing temperatures. She can create extreme cold that affects the entire island, and her presence is said to bring a drop in temperature.

Immortality and Divine Form Poliʻahu is an immortal deity who can appear in human form or as a spirit of the mountain. She is not subject to aging or death, and she can travel between the spiritual and mortal realms at will.

Shapeshifting In some legends, Poliʻahu can transform herself into a beautiful woman to interact with mortals, or into a cold mist or snowstorm to conceal her movements. This ability allows her to move unseen and to test the intentions of those who approach her domain.

Accounts

Poliʻahu met the chief Aiwohikupua on the eastern slope of Mauna Kea, and the two fell deeply in love. Aiwohikupua took her to his home on Kauaʻi, but upon arrival, Poliʻahu discovered that he was already betrothed to another princess. Feeling betrayed, she left him and returned to Mauna Kea, vowing to remain a solitary goddess. In some versions, she punishes Aiwohikupua by freezing his heart or turning him into a stone, while in others she simply disappears, leaving him to regret his actions.

Pele, the volcano goddess, sought to expand her domain by sending lava flows down the slopes of Mauna Kea. Poliʻahu, angered by this intrusion, descended from the summit and engaged Pele in a fierce battle. The two goddesses fought for days, with Pele hurling molten rock and Poliʻahu responding with blizzards and ice. Ultimately, Poliʻahu's cold proved stronger, and she froze Pele's lava, forcing the volcano goddess to retreat. The battle left the landscape scarred, but Poliʻahu emerged victorious, and the snows of Mauna Kea remained undisturbed.

This modern retelling focuses on Poliʻahu's emotional journey after her failed love affair with Aiwohikupua. It explores her loneliness and her decision to embrace her role as a guardian of the mountain, finding solace in the cold and the silence. The story emphasizes her strength and independence, and it ends with her watching over Mauna Kea, a sentinel of snow.

Scholarly Debates
Number of Snow Goddesses
Some sources say there are four snow goddesses, including Poliʻahu. ⇌ Other sources mention only three, or list Poliʻahu as the sole snow deity.
Outcome of the Love Affair with Aiwohikupua
Poliʻahu leaves Aiwohikupua and returns to Mauna Kea, forgiving him. ⇌ Poliʻahu punishes Aiwohikupua by turning him into stone or freezing him.
Role in the Battle with Pele
Poliʻahu fights Pele alone and wins. ⇌ Poliʻahu and her sisters together defeat Pele, with Poliʻahu playing a minor role.
What We Don't Know

Further research is needed to clarify the exact number of snow goddesses in Hawaiian mythology and to trace the origins of the variations in Poliʻahu's stories. Additionally, the role of Poliʻahu in contemporary Hawaiian cultural practices, particularly in relation to the protection of Mauna Kea, is an area that requires more documentation. The influence of Christian missionaries on the preservation and alteration of these legends is also a topic that merits deeper investigation.

A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine a goddess who commands the snows of a mountain so tall that it rises from the seafloor to the heavens, and who dares to challenge the fire goddess Pele herself. That is Poliʻahu, the Lady of the White Mantle, whose icy breath can quench the most furious lava. Her story is one of love, betrayal, and the eternal struggle between fire and ice, and it invites us to explore the sacred peaks of Hawaiʻi with new eyes.
