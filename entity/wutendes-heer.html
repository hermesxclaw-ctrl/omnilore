Who It Is

The Wütendes Heer (Furious Army) — also known as the Wild Hunt or Wilde Jagd — is a terrifying spectral procession that races across the night sky and through Alpine valleys during the Twelve Nights (Raunächte), especially on New Year's Eve. It consists of a roaring horde of ghostly riders, howling hounds, and shrieking spirits, led by a fearsome figure — sometimes identified as Odin/Wotan in Germanic regions, Perchta in the Alpine south, or the Devil in Christianized versions. To witness the Wild Hunt is a death omen: those caught in its path are swept up and carried to the realm of the dead unless they throw themselves flat on the ground and look away. On the other hand, those who help a lost spirit from the Hunt find their way home may receive prophetic dreams or a magical token. The hunt represents the restless dead, unbaptized children, and souls who died violently, perpetually riding through the winter storm. Alpine farmers would leave doors and windows open on certain nights so the spirits would not break through, and would mark their doors with chalk crosses as protection. The Wütendes Heer is one of the most persistent and widespread supernatural beliefs in the Alpine region, with attested variants from Bavaria to the Grisons.

The Approach

On storm-lashed nights in the Alps, when the wind howls through the passes and the snow obscures the moon, villagers once barred their doors and hid their children. For they believed that the Wild Hunt—das Wütende Heer—was abroad, a spectral army of the dead led by a fearsome figure, riding the gale to claim the souls of the unwary. This is not a single tale but a living, shifting tradition, woven from centuries of fear, folklore, and the raw power of the mountains.

Content advisory. Contains themes of death, supernatural terror, and folk beliefs that may involve violence and abduction. Some regional variants include antisemitic or anti-Roma elements; these are noted for scholarly context and not endorsed.

Etymology

The German 'Wütendes Heer' translates to 'Furious Host' or 'Raging Army.' 'Wütend' derives from Middle High German 'wüeten,' meaning to rage or rave, connected to Old High German 'wuot' (fury, madness). The name reflects the chaotic, violent nature of the spectral procession. The term 'Wild Hunt' (Wilde Jagd) emphasizes the hunting aspect, while regional names like 'Perchtenjagd' link it to the Perchten figures.

Cultural Ownership

Pan-Alpine: German, Austrian, Swiss, and South Tyrolean. The tradition is also widespread across Germanic and Celtic Europe, but this dossier focuses on the Alpine expression. It is a shared folk belief with deep Christian and pre-Christian layers.

Disputed Identifications

The leader of the Wild Hunt is variously identified as Odin (Wotan), a legendary king (e.g., Theodoric the Great, Charlemagne), a biblical figure (Herod, Cain), or a local ghost (e.g., a cursed nobleman). In Alpine regions, female leaders like Perchta or Holda are sometimes associated. These identifications are not mutually exclusive in folklore, and scholars debate their origins and interrelations.

Description

Appearance

The Wütendes Heer is typically described as a ghostly procession of riders, hounds, and foot soldiers moving through the sky or along forest paths. The host is often led by a figure on a black horse, sometimes accompanied by a pack of baying hounds. The appearance varies: some accounts describe the host as a band of armed warriors, others as a rabble of the dead, including the unbaptized, suicides, and those who died violently. The leader may be headless, or have a fiery face, and is often accompanied by a horn. The procession is accompanied by howling winds, the barking of dogs, and the sound of hunting horns. In some Alpine regions, the host is associated with the Perchten, who wear masks and bells during the Twelve Nights of Christmas.

Symbols & Iconography

Key symbols include the wild hunt itself (a metaphor for chaos and death), the leader's horse (often black or white), hounds (often black with glowing eyes), horns (signaling the hunt), and the storm (as the manifestation of the host's passage). In Christianized art, the hunt may be depicted as a punishment for sinners. In Alpine folk art, the Perchten masks and costumes are linked to the hunt.

Behaviour & Personality

The Wütendes Heer is a restless, malevolent force. It is said to appear during the Twelve Nights of Christmas (between Christmas and Epiphany), as well as during storms and other liminal times. The host abducts people, especially those who are out after dark, and forces them to join the hunt. It can be warded off by staying indoors, hiding, or using protective charms. The leader is often seen as a punishing figure, carrying off sinners or the unbaptized. Some accounts describe the host as benevolent, rewarding those who help them, but this is less common.

Domains & Habitats

The Wütendes Heer is primarily associated with the sky and the wilderness: mountains, forests, and isolated passes. It is particularly active during the Twelve Nights, when the boundary between the living and the dead is thin. The host is also said to frequent old battlefields, graveyards, and crossroads.

Version Differences

Regional differences are significant. In northern Germany, the leader is often Odin or a legendary king; in the Alps, the leader may be Perchta or a local ghost. In some Swiss variants, the host is led by a woman named 'Strägga' or 'Selga.' In South Tyrol, the hunt is sometimes called 'Perchtenjagd' and is associated with the Perchten figures. The composition of the host also varies: sometimes it includes fairies or elves, other times only the dead. Some versions emphasize the hunt's role as a psychopomp, leading souls to the afterlife, while others see it as a demonic horde.

Powers & Abilities

Spectral Abduction

Source Version Pan-Alpine, attested in many legends.

Description

The host can forcibly take living people, especially those who venture out during the hunt, and carry them into the sky or the underworld. Abductees may be returned after a period, but often are changed or marked.

Limitations

The host is said to be powerless against those who stay inside, remain silent, or use protective charms (e.g., iron, sacred words).

Contradictions

Some accounts say abductees are never seen again; others say they return with knowledge or blessings.

Storm Summoning

Source Version Common across all Alpine regions.

Description

The passage of the Wütendes Heer is accompanied by violent storms, howling winds, and thunder. The host is believed to bring the storm, or to ride upon it.

Limitations

The storm is temporary and may be warded off by church bells or prayer.

Contradictions

Some traditions say the storm is sent as punishment, others that it is simply the noise of the hunt.

Psychopomp

Source Version Attested in older Germanic tradition; in Alpine regions, this role is less emphasized but present in some legends.

Description

The host is sometimes seen as a guide of souls, leading the recently dead to the afterlife. This is particularly associated with the leader, who may be a deity or a legendary hero.

Limitations

The host only appears at certain times, and its role as psychopomp is not always explicit.

Contradictions

In Christianized versions, the host is more often seen as a demonic force, not a benevolent guide.

Punishment of Sinners

Source Version Widespread in Alpine legends, especially those with Christian moralizing.

Description

The hunt is often described as a punitive force, chasing and capturing sinners, the unbaptized, or those who died violently. The leader may judge the souls of the dead.

Limitations

The host only targets those who are out after dark or who have committed specific sins.

Contradictions

Some traditions say the host itself is composed of the damned, not punishers.

Shape-shifting

Source Version Occasional in Alpine legends.

Description

The leader of the hunt is sometimes said to be able to change shape, appearing as a giant, a beast, or a ghostly rider.

Limitations

Not a consistent trait; the leader's form is often fixed.

Contradictions

The leader's identity and appearance vary widely, but shape-shifting is not a central motif.

Stories & Myths

The Hunter and the Host

Source Collected in Alpensagen by Theodor VernalekenCulture AustrianPeriod 19th century

Summary

A hunter who boasted that he feared nothing went out during the Twelve Nights. He encountered the Wütendes Heer and was swept up by the ghostly riders. He was found days later, pale and mute, and died shortly after. His soul was said to have joined the hunt.

Participants

Hunter

Wütendes Heer

Consequences

The hunter's death served as a warning against arrogance and venturing out during the hunt.

Variants

Similar tales exist in Bavaria and Switzerland, often with a moral lesson.

The Perchtenjagd in South Tyrol

Source Regional folklore collectionsCulture South TyroleanPeriod 19th-20th century

Summary

During the Perchten nights, the Perchten (masked figures) are said to join the Wild Hunt. They chase away evil spirits and bring fertility, but also punish the wicked. In some villages, people dress as Perchten to mimic the hunt, believing it protects their homes.

Participants

Perchten

Consequences

The tradition of Perchten runs continues today, blending ancient and Christian elements.

Some communities emphasize the benevolent aspect, others the terrifying.

Summary

A traveler lost at a crossroads encountered a procession of the dead led by a woman in white. She warned him to turn back, or he would be taken. He ignored her and was found dead the next morning, his face frozen in terror.

Relationships

Perchta

Type associatedSource Regional folklore, scholarly interpretations.

Description

Perchta, a goddess or spirit of the Twelve Nights, is sometimes identified as the leader of the Wild Hunt in Alpine regions. She is also associated with the Perchten figures who accompany the hunt.

Version Note

In some areas, Perchta leads the hunt; in others, she is a separate figure who punishes naughty children.

Conflicting Accounts

Some scholars see Perchta as a distinct deity from the hunt's leader, while others conflate them.

Krampus

Type associatedSource Modern folklore and popular culture.

Description

Krampus, a horned figure who punishes children during the Advent season, is sometimes said to be part of the Wild Hunt's entourage, especially in the Perchten tradition.

Version Note

The association is strongest in the Perchtenläufe (Perchten runs) of the Alps.

Conflicting Accounts

Krampus is historically a separate figure, but modern adaptations often merge him with the Wild Hunt.

Odin

Type leader (in some versions)Source Grimm, Deutsche Mythologie; medieval chronicles.

Description

Odin (Wotan) is the most common leader of the Wild Hunt in Germanic tradition. In the Alps, his role is less prominent but present in older legends.

Version Note

In Alpine folklore, Odin is often replaced by local figures, but the name 'Wütendes Heer' retains the root 'Wotan'.

Conflicting Accounts

Scholars debate whether the Wild Hunt originated with Odin or with a more ancient chthonic deity.

The Dead

Type membersSource Numerous legends.

Description

The Wütendes Heer is composed of the dead, particularly those who died prematurely, violently, or unbaptized. They are sometimes seen as the souls of the damned, forced to ride until their sins are purged.

Version Note

The composition varies: sometimes only warriors, sometimes a mixed crowd.

Conflicting Accounts

Some accounts say the dead are being punished; others say they are simply wandering.

The Living

Type victims/observersSource Many legends.

Description

Living people may be abducted by the hunt, or may witness it and survive to tell the tale. Those who are taken are often never seen again.

Version Note

Survivors are often marked by madness or prophetic abilities.

Conflicting Accounts

Some say the living can join the hunt willingly, but this is rare and dangerous.

Abduction and Violence

Source Various Alpine legends.

Description

The hunt is often described as abducting humans, especially children or those who venture out at night. The abductees are never returned or return changed.

Scholarly Context

This reflects the dangers of the wilderness and the need for community protection.

Demonization of Outsiders

Source Some medieval chronicles and later folklore collections.

Description

In some accounts, the hunt is said to target Jews, Roma, or other minorities, reflecting medieval antisemitism and xenophobia.

Scholarly Context

These elements are not universal and are often later additions. They must be acknowledged as harmful stereotypes.

Punishment of Sinners

Source Christian-influenced legends.

Description

The hunt is frequently portrayed as a divine punishment for sinners, including suicides, murderers, and the unbaptized. This reinforces moral codes through fear.

Scholarly Context

This reflects the Christianization of pre-Christian beliefs, transforming the hunts from a natural phenomenon to a moral lesson.

Contradictions & Disputes

Leader Identity

Position A

The leader is Odin (Wotan), a pagan god.

Position A — evidence

Medieval chronicles, place names, and Grimm's analysis.

Position B

The leader is a historical figure, such as Theodoric the Great or Charlemagne.

Position B — evidence

Local legends that name specific kings or heroes.

Scholarly Consensus

Scholars agree that the leader varies by region and era; the Odin connection is strongest in Scandinavia and northern Germany, while in the Alps, local figures dominate. There is no single original leader.

Benevolent vs. Malevolent

Position A

The Wild Hunt is a benevolent force that guides souls and brings fertility.

Position A — evidence

Some legends describe the hunt rewarding those who help or showing respect for the dead.

Position B

The Wild Hunt is a demonic host that punishes and abducts.

Position B — evidence

Most legends emphasize fear and danger.

Scholarly Consensus

The character of the hunt is ambiguous and context-dependent. In Christianized versions, it is more malevolent; pre-Christian versions may have been more neutral.

Origin of the Belief

Position A

The Wild Hunt originated as a folk explanation for storms and natural phenomena.

Position A — evidence

The association with storms and the sky.

Position B

The Wild Hunt originated as a myth of the dead and the afterlife.

Position B — evidence

The composition of the host as the dead, and its psychopomp role.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars see a combination: the hunt is a mythologization of natural events, but with deep roots in ancestor veneration and beliefs about the dead.

Relation to Perchta

Position A

Perchta is the leader of the Wild Hunt in Alpine regions.

Position A — evidence

Some legends explicitly name Perchta as the leader.

Position B

Perchta is a separate figure, associated with the Twelve Nights but not the hunt.

Position B — evidence

Other legends describe Perchta as a household spirit who punishes children, with no hunting role.

Scholarly Consensus

The relationship is complex; Perchta's role may have been assimilated to the hunt in some areas, but she has an independent origin.

Research Scope

This dossier focuses on the pan-Alpine tradition of the Wütendes Heer (Wild Hunt), spanning German, Austrian, Swiss, and South Tyrolean variants. It synthesizes available scholarly and folkloric sources, but due to the lack of a comprehensive encyclopedia article, many details are reconstructed from comparative folklore and regional collections.
