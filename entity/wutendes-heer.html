Who It Is

On storm-lashed nights in the Alps, when the wind howls through the passes and the snow obscures the moon, villagers once barred their doors and hid their children. For they believed that the Wild Hunt—das Wütende Heer—was abroad, a spectral army of the dead led by a fearsome figure, riding the gale to claim the souls of the unwary. This is not a single tale but a living, shifting tradition, woven from centuries of fear, folklore, and the raw power of the mountains.

The Wütendes Heer is a restless, malevolent force. It is said to appear during the Twelve Nights of Christmas (between Christmas and Epiphany), as well as during storms and other liminal times. The host abducts people, especially those who are out after dark, and forces them to join the hunt. It can be warded off by staying indoors, hiding, or using protective charms. The leader is often seen as a punishing figure, carrying off sinners or the unbaptized. Some accounts describe the host as benevolent, rewarding those who help them, but this is less common.

Powers
Spectral Abduction

The host can forcibly take living people, especially those who venture out during the hunt, and carry them into the sky or the underworld. Abductees may be returned after a period, but often are changed or marked.

Limit: The host is said to be powerless against those who stay inside, remain silent, or use protective charms (e.g., iron, sacred words).

Storm Summoning

The passage of the Wütendes Heer is accompanied by violent storms, howling winds, and thunder. The host is believed to bring the storm, or to ride upon it.

Limit: The storm is temporary and may be warded off by church bells or prayer.

Psychopomp

The host is sometimes seen as a guide of souls, leading the recently dead to the afterlife. This is particularly associated with the leader, who may be a deity or a legendary hero.

Limit: The host only appears at certain times, and its role as psychopomp is not always explicit.

Punishment of Sinners

The hunt is often described as a punitive force, chasing and capturing sinners, the unbaptized, or those who died violently. The leader may judge the souls of the dead.

Limit: The host only targets those who are out after dark or who have committed specific sins.

Shape-shifting

The leader of the hunt is sometimes said to be able to change shape, appearing as a giant, a beast, or a ghostly rider.

Limit: Not a consistent trait; the leader's form is often fixed.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The Wütendes Heer is typically described as a ghostly procession of riders, hounds, and foot soldiers moving through the sky or along forest paths. The host is often led by a figure on a black horse, sometimes accompanied by a pack of baying hounds. The appearance varies: some accounts describe the host as a band of armed warriors, others as a rabble of the dead, including the unbaptized, suicides, and those who died violently. Symbols — Key symbols include the wild hunt itself (a metaphor for chaos and death), the leader's horse (often black or white), hounds (often black with glowing eyes), horns (signaling the hunt), and the storm (as the manifestation of the host's passage). In Christianized art, the hunt may be depicted as a punishment for sinners. In Alpine folk art, the Perchten masks and costumes are linked to the hunt.

Domains — The Wütendes Heer is primarily associated with the sky and the wilderness: mountains, forests, and isolated passes. It is particularly active during the Twelve Nights, when the boundary between the living and the dead is thin. The host is also said to frequent old battlefields, graveyards, and crossroads.

Accounts I
The Hunter and the HostCollected in Alpensagen by Theodor Vernaleken

19th century

A hunter who boasted that he feared nothing went out during the Twelve Nights. He encountered the Wütendes Heer and was swept up by the ghostly riders. He was found days later, pale and mute, and died shortly after. His soul was said to have joined the hunt. The hunter's death served as a warning against arrogance and venturing out during the hunt.

The Perchtenjagd in South TyrolRegional folklore collections

19th-20th century

During the Perchten nights, the Perchten (masked figures) are said to join the Wild Hunt. They chase away evil spirits and bring fertility, but also punish the wicked. In some villages, people dress as Perchten to mimic the hunt, believing it protects their homes. The tradition of Perchten runs continues today, blending ancient and Christian elements.

Accounts II
The Ghostly Procession at the CrossroadsSwiss folklore, collected in Sagen aus der Schweiz

19th century

A traveler lost at a crossroads encountered a procession of the dead led by a woman in white. She warned him to turn back, or he would be taken. He ignored her and was found dead the next morning, his face frozen in terror. The story reinforces the danger of traveling alone at night.

Odin's Hunt in the AlpsGrimm's Deutsche Mythologie, citing earlier sources

Medieval to early modern

The Wild Hunt is led by Odin (Wotan), who rides across the sky with his army of fallen warriors. In the Alps, this belief merged with local legends, and Odin was sometimes replaced by local heroes or saints. The hunt was a portent of war or disaster.

The Wild Hunt is always led by Odin. — In the Alps, the leader is often a local ghost, a legendary king, or Perchta. Odin is only one of many possible leaders, and his association is strongest in Scandinavia and northern Germany.

The Wild Hunt is purely evil. — The hunt is ambiguous; it can be a punisher of sinners, a psychopomp, or even a bringer of fertility. Its moral character varies by story and region.

The Wild Hunt is a single, unified myth. — It is a collection of local legends with common motifs but significant variation. There is no canonical version.

The Wild Hunt is exclusively a Germanic or Alpine phenomenon. — Similar beliefs exist across Europe, including Celtic, Slavic, and Romance cultures. The Alpine version is one regional expression.
