Form

Arebati is not described in physical or anthropomorphic terms in the documented Efé oral traditions. He is a sky being, associated with the heavens, the moon, and celestial phenomena. He is not depicted in material art or iconography; his presence is felt in the sky and in the forest's relationship to it. The lack of physical description is itself significant — Arebati is a force, a presence, a source, not a figure to be imaged.

Powers

Creation of Humanity Arebati is credited with the creation of the first humans. The specific mechanism is not detailed in the available sources — he is simply the originator of human life. Efé oral tradition (Turnbull) No limitations recorded; this is an act of origin, not an ongoing power. None recorded within the Efé tradition.

Gift of Fire Arebati gave fire to humanity. This is a foundational cultural gift, marking the transition from a purely natural existence to a cultural one. Fire is essential for cooking, warmth, and protection in the forest. Efé oral tradition (Turnbull) The gift is given; its maintenance is a human responsibility. None recorded within the Efé tradition.

Sky Sovereignty Arebati rules the sky, the moon, and celestial phenomena. He is not a god of weather or thunder — that role belongs to other beings in related traditions (e.g., Tore for the Mbuti). His sovereignty is over the ordered heavens, not the chaotic storm. Efé oral tradition (Turnbull) His power is celestial; the forest is a separate realm with its own spirits and forces. Some early accounts conflated Arebati with thunder deities; this is a conflation error.

Accounts

Arebati, the sky being, created the first humans. The Efé people are his descendants, placed in the forest to live in harmony with it. The act of creation is not elaborated with dramatic detail; it is a simple, foundational truth of Efé existence.

Arebati gave fire to humanity. This gift is the foundation of Efé culture — cooking, warmth, protection. It is a mark of Arebati's benevolence and his care for his creations.

Scholarly Debates
Arebati vs. Khonvum vs. Tore
Early ethnographers sometimes conflated Arebati (Efé) with Khonvum or Tore (Mbuti), treating them as the same deity under different names. ⇌ Modern anthropology distinguishes these as separate deities of separate, though related, peoples.
Evidence for A: Early comparative surveys that grouped 'pygmy religions' together.
Evidence for B: Detailed ethnographic fieldwork (Turnbull) showing distinct cosmologies.
Arebati as a 'high god'
Some comparative religion scholars categorize Arebati as a 'high god' or 'sky god' in the classic Africanist sense. ⇌ Others argue that this categorization imposes a Western theological framework on a cosmology that is fundamentally different — Arebati is not a ruler of a pantheon, but a singular, distant creator.
Evidence for A: Comparative surveys of African high gods.
Evidence for B: Turnbull's emphasis on the Efé's non-hierarchical, forest-focused worldview.
What We Don't Know

1) No primary Efé-language sources were accessed; all knowledge is mediated through Turnbull's English translations. 2) No information on Arebati in contemporary Efé religious practice — does he remain an active figure, or has his role shifted? 3) No information on any Efé-specific rituals or prayers directed at Arebati beyond general invocation. 4) No material culture associated with Arebati has been documented. 5) No information on how Arebati is understood by younger generations of Efé in the 21st century.

A way to hold it in your mind

Arebati is like the sun on a clear day — you don't see a face, you don't hear a voice, but you feel the warmth, and you know it's the reason you're alive. He's the ultimate distant benefactor, the cosmic 'someone up there' who got things started and then let you get on with it. He's not a micromanager god, not a thunder-thrower, not a judge. He's the quiet, generous creator who gave you fire and a forest to live in, and trusts you to make it work.
