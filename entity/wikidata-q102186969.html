Who It Is

Four thousand years before the Greek gods captured the Western imagination, the Queen of Heaven ruled Mesopotamia with a ferocity no pantheon has matched since. She was lover and warrior, lawgiver and destroyer of mountains. She seized civilization itself from a drunken god, descended into death and clawed her way back, and when the greatest king in history rejected her, she nearly destroyed his world. Ishtar — or Inanna, as Sumer knew her — was not one goddess but many: a composite of contradictions that scholars still struggle to untangle. Would you dare to descend into her story?

Inanna/Ishtar is among the most complex and contradictory deities in any pantheon. She is fierce, ambitious, and sexually assertive — a goddess who seizes domains from other gods, destroys those who challenge her (including an entire mountain range), and demands absolute worship. Yet she also weeps for her lost lover Dumuzid and shows genuine vulnerability in the underworld. Enheduanna's hymns describe her as a 'dragon' who rains fire and flood upon enemies, a storm-bird who shrieks across the heavens. Name & Tongue

Etymology — Inanna may derive from Sumerian nin-an-ak, meaning 'Lady of Heaven'. Ishtar is of Semitic derivation, possibly related to the West Semitic god Attar. Originally separate deities, they were syncretized during Sargon of Akkad's reign.

Pronunciation — /ˈɪʃtɑːr/ (Ishtar), /ɪˈnɑːnə/ (Inanna)

Aliases — Inanna, Queen of Heaven, Lady of Uruk, Morning and Evening Star, Ishtar of Arbela, Ishtar of Nineveh

Ownership — Mesopotamian religion (Sumerian, Akkadian, Babylonian, Assyrian)

Divine Justice

Alongside her twin brother Utu/Shamash, Inanna enforces cosmic law, punishing transgressors with devastating force including destroying Mount Ebih.

Limit:

Control of the Mes

Inanna obtained the mes — the divine decrees governing all aspects of civilization (both positive and negative) — from Enki through trickery, giving her dominion over cultural and cosmic order.

Limit:

Planetary Dominion

As the goddess of Venus, she governs both the morning and evening star, embodying the dualities of love and war, creation and destruction.

Limit:

Gender Transformation

Akkadian hymns describe Ishtar transforming men into women and women into men, reflecting her dominion over gender boundaries.

Limit:

Underworld Authority

She is the only major deity to successfully descend into and return from the Mesopotamian underworld (Kur/Irkalla), though at the cost of her husband Dumuzid.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Ishtar is typically depicted as a goddess equipped with weapons on her back, wearing a horned helmet, and standing in a dominant posture upon a lion. She is frequently shown with the eight-pointed star (representing Venus) and the hook-shaped knot of reeds. In Akkadian seals (2350-2150 BCE), she appears armed and regal. Her iconography emphasizes both her martial and sensual aspects — she is simultaneously a war goddess in battle array and a beau

Symbols — The eight-pointed star of Venus, the lion (her mount), the hook-shaped reed ring-post (𒈹), the rosette, the dove, and weapons. The reed ring-post served as a boundary marker between profane and sacred space at temple entrances. The planet Venus as both morning and evening star reflects her dual nature.

Accounts I
Inanna's Descent to the UnderworldSumerian narrative poem (c. 1900-1600 BCE)

Old Babylonian

Inanna descends to the underworld ruled by her sister Ereshkigal, passing through seven gates and surrendering her divine garments and powers at each. Upon reaching the throne room naked, the seven judges strike her dead and hang her corpse on a hook. Three days later, Enki sends two sexless beings who revive her with the 'food of life' and 'water of life.' The galla demons demand a replacement, and Inanna offers her husband Dumuzid, who had not mourned her. Dumuzid's sister Geshtinanna volunteers to share his fate, resulting in the seasonal cycle. Established the cycle of seasons through Dumuzid's half-year sojourn in the underworld. Demonstrated that even the Queen of Heaven cannot escape death.

Inanna and the God of Wisdom (Enki)Sumerian myth 'Inanna and Enki'

Old Babylonian

Inanna visits Enki in Eridu, where he hosts a drunken feast. In his intoxicated state, Enki gifts Inanna the mes — over 100 divine decrees governing civilization including kingship, priestly offices, arts, crafts, and even negative concepts. Inanna loads them onto her Boat of Heaven and departs for Uruk. When Enki sobers and sends monsters to retrieve them, Inanna's servant Ninshubur defends the boat. Inanna successfully brings the mes to Uruk, transferring the center of civilization from Eridu. Uruk became the new center of civilization, replacing Eridu. Inanna gained authority over all aspects of human culture.

Inanna and Mt. EbihSumerian poem 'Inanna and Ebih'

Old Babylonian

The mountain range Ebih refuses to bow to Inanna's authority. Enraged, Inanna petitions An, the sky god, for permission to destroy it. An initially refuses, warning of Ebih's verdant bounty, but Inanna's fury prevails. She unleashes a devastating assault: storms, floods, and fire that shatter the mountain, killing its vegetation and inhabitants. She declares herself victorious over the defiant mountain. Demonstrates Inanna's capacity for catastrophic destruction when her divine authority is challenged. Establishes her as an unstoppable force of nature.

Accounts II
Ishtar and GilgameshEpic of Gilgamesh, Tablet VI (Standard Akkadian)

c. 1200 BCE

After Gilgamesh returns from slaying Humbaba, Ishtar proposes marriage, offering him wealth, power, and status. Gilgamesh scornfully refuses, cataloguing the grim fates of her former lovers: Dumuzid condemned to annual mourning, a shepherd turned into a wolf, a gardener turned into a crippled mole, and others. Enraged, Ishtar ascends to heaven and demands Anu give her the Bull of Heaven to destroy Gilgamesh. When Anu hesitates, she threatens to break open the gates of the underworld and release the dead. She unleashes the Bull, which devastates Uruk for seven years until Gilgamesh and Enkidu slay it. This leads to the gods decreeing Enkidu must die. Enkidu's death, Gilgamesh's confrontation with mortality, and his subsequent quest for immortality.

Inanna and ShukaletudaSumerian poem

Old Babylonian

The gardener Shukaletuda finds Inanna sleeping under a poplar tree and rapes her while she sleeps. Upon waking and discovering the violation, Inanna's fury is cataclysmic. She unleashes three plagues upon the land: first turning all water to blood, then unleashing devastating storms, and finally blocking all roads. Shukaletuda hides among the city's population, and Inanna must search for him, demonstrating that even divine justice requires investigation. When she finally locates him, she strikes him dead. Establishes Inanna as an avenger of sexual violence. Demonstrates the principle that divine retribution, while inevitable, may require discernment.

Inanna and BiluluSumerian poem

Old Babylonian

After Dumuzid is killed by bandits, Inanna discovers that the bandit woman Bilulu and her son Girgire were responsible. In a furious quest for vengeance, Inanna tracks them down and kills Bilulu, transforming her into a divine being who must pour out funerary libations for Dumuzid in perpetuity. This story connects to the larger Dumuzid cycle of lamentation and seasonal death-rebirth. Established the funerary cult of Dumuzid. Demonstrated Inanna's role as divine avenger for her beloved.
