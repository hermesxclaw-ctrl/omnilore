He is the father of the gods, the king of the divine assembly, the source of all authority and kingship. His role is not over a specific natural phenomenon (like Enlil with wind/storms or Enki with water/wisdom) but over the entire cosmos. He is the ultimate source of the divine order (me).

Form

Anu is rarely depicted in human form in surviving art. He is usually symbolized by a horned crown (the divine symbol of supreme authority), a star or rosette, or a mountain. When he is shown, he appears as a mature, bearded king seated on a throne, often wearing a multi-horned crown, signifying his supreme rank. The number '60' was sacred to him, representing the highest tier of the divine hierarchy.

Powers

Absolute Kingship of the Gods Anu is the supreme authority in the divine assembly. His word is law, and his decrees (terētu) are final. Other gods, even powerful ones like Enlil and Ea, defer to his judgment. He is the ultimate source of the king's mandate on earth. Sumerian and Akkadian texts His power is largely administrative and juridical. He rarely exercises direct physical power in myths, often delegating tasks to other gods. In many myths, Enlil appears to be the more active and powerful god, leading some scholars to argue that Enlil had de facto power, while Anu's was more ceremonial.

The Divine Me (mes) Anu holds the 'me' — the fundamental cosmic laws, powers, and institutions of civilization. He is the one who originally possessed them and distributed them to other gods. They are the blueprints of the universe. Sumerian texts, e.g., 'Inanna and Enki' In the myth 'Inanna and Enki', Enki holds the me in his domain, and Inanna tricks him into giving them to her, suggesting that the me can be transferred and are not exclusively Anu's. The myth of 'Inanna and Enki' implies Enki is the guardian of the me, which may contradict the idea of Anu as their sole source.

Creator of Deities and Beings As the father of the gods, Anu's primary power is creation. He created the Anunnaki (the great gods), and in the Epic of Gilgamesh, he is asked by the gods to create a match for Gilgamesh, resulting in Enkidu. Epic of Gilgamesh; Enuma Elish He does not create alone; he is often part of a divine council decision. His creations are often made to solve a specific problem. In the Enuma Elish, creation is performed by Marduk, not Anu, showing a shift in creation theology.

Granting Kingship Anu is the ultimate source of earthly kingship. The king is the 'appointee of Anu' or 'the one who is called by Anu'. He grants the crown, the throne, and the scepter to the chosen ruler. Royal inscriptions throughout Mesopotamian history (e.g., Sumerian King List) This power is often delegated to other gods like Enlil or Marduk, who are said to 'call' the king. The Sumerian King List states kingship 'descended from heaven' without specifying a single god, though Anu is often implied.

The Bull of Heaven Anu could send the Bull of Heaven, a monstrous celestial beast, to cause destruction on earth. This was a direct exercise of his power to punish or terrorize. Epic of Gilgamesh, Tablet VI This power is granted to his daughter Ishtar, who must ask him for it. He only gives it to her with reluctance, and it is ultimately defeated by Gilgamesh and Enkidu. The Bull of Heaven is a divine weapon, but its defeat by mortals suggests that even Anu's power can be challenged.

Behavior & Habitat

Anu is the archetypal 'king of the gods' — distant, authoritative, and somewhat passive. He does not often descend to the mortal realm or engage in the petty squabbles of other deities. He functions as a final arbiter, a source of authority, and a genealogical head. In myths, he is often asked for permission or aid, rather than acting directly. He is the source of the divine mandate (me), the cosmic laws that govern the universe.

Accounts

The gods hear the complaints of the people of Uruk about the arrogant king Gilgamesh. They appeal to Anu, the father of the gods. Anu instructs the goddess Aruru to create a wild man, Enkidu, who will be a match for Gilgamesh and humble him.

After Gilgamesh rejects the goddess Ishtar's advances, she goes to her father Anu and demands the Bull of Heaven to punish Gilgamesh. Anu hesitates, warning her of the consequences, but ultimately gives in to her threat to break open the underworld. The Bull wreaks havoc on Uruk until Gilgamesh and Enkidu slay it.

The epic describes the creation of the universe from the primordial waters of Apsu (fresh water) and Tiamat (salt water). These two beget the first generation of gods, including Lahmu and Lahamu, then Anshar and Kishar, and finally Anu. Anu is the son of Anshar and Kishar, and the father of Ea (Nudimmud).

Ishtar descends to the underworld to challenge her sister Ereshkigal. She is trapped and killed. The god Ea creates a male prostitute, Asushunamir, to rescue her. Anu is not a direct actor in this myth but is referenced as her father, and the gods appeal to him for aid.

Content Notes

Anu's role as the source of kingship was used to legitimize the absolute power of Mesopotamian kings. This divine mandate could be used to justify oppressive rule, warfare, and the extraction of tribute from subjugated peoples.

Historians view this as a classic example of religion being used to legitimize political power and social hierarchy.

Royal inscriptions, such as those of Sargon of Akkad and Hammurabi

In the Epic of Gilgamesh, Anu sends the Bull of Heaven to destroy Uruk and its people as a punishment for Gilgamesh's actions. This is an act of collective punishment, targeting the innocent along with the intended target.

This demonstrates that the gods in Mesopotamian myth could be capricious and destructive, and that mortals were often caught in the crossfire of divine conflicts.

Epic of Gilgamesh, Tablet VI

Anu is the ultimate patriarch of the divine realm. His authority is absolute, and his power is passed down through a male lineage (Anu -> Ea -> Marduk). This divine model reinforced a patriarchal social order on earth.

Feminist scholarship on ancient religions highlights how the divine hierarchy often mirrored and legitimized the patriarchal structure of human society.

Scholarly Debates
Genealogy of Enlil
Enlil is the son of Anu and Ki (Earth). ⇌ Enlil is a brother of Anu, not his son.
Evidence for A: Sumerian text 'Enlil and Ninlil' and other early Sumerian sources.
Evidence for B: Some Akkadian god lists and the Enuma Elish, which do not mention Enlil as Anu's son, instead placing Ea in that role.
Anu's Role vs. Enlil's Role
Anu is the supreme god, the ultimate authority. ⇌ Enlil is the de facto ruler, the most powerful and active god.
Evidence for A: Anu's title as 'King of the Gods' and his role as the source of authority in god lists.
Evidence for B: Enlil's active role in myths like the Flood story (Atrahasis), where he is the one who decides to destroy humanity, and
Anu's Consort
Anu's consort is Antu. ⇌ Anu's consort is Ki (Earth).
Evidence for A: Standard Akkadian god lists (An = Anum) and temple records from Uruk.
Evidence for B: Early Sumerian texts that describe An (Sky) and Ki (Earth) as the primordial couple.
The Nature of His Power
Anu is a creator god, actively involved in creation. ⇌ Anu is a passive, distant figurehead.
Evidence for A: His role in creating Enkidu and his presence in the theogony of the Enuma Elish.
Evidence for B: His lack of direct action in most myths. He is often a figure who is asked for permission, but does not act himself.
What We Don't Know

The primary gap is the lack of a dedicated, in-depth scholarly monograph solely on Anu. Most information is scattered across general works on Mesopotamian religion. The exact nature of his worship in the earliest Uruk period (before 3000 BCE) is poorly understood due to the limitations of the archaeological and textual record. The relationship between the Sumerian 'An' and the Akkadian 'Anu' is clear in name but the theological evolution is not fully documented. The visual depictions of Anu are rare and ambiguous, making it difficult to confirm his iconography beyond the horned crown.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Anu as the Chairman of the Board of the divine corporation 'Cosmos Inc.' He doesn't get his hands dirty with the day-to-day operations (that's for the Enlils and the Eas of the world), but no major decision, merger, or acquisition (like creating a hero or sending a divine bull) happens without his sign-off. He's the ultimate authority, the one who signs the checks and sets the bylaws, even if he's rarely seen on the factory floor. His power is not in what he does, but in what he can authorize.
