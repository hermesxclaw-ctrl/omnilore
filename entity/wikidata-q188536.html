Who It Is

Before Zeus hurled his first thunderbolt, before Odin hung from Yggdrasil, there was Anu — the silent, starry vault of the sky itself, given a name and a throne. He was the ultimate king of the Mesopotamian gods, a figure so high and so distant that he rarely bothered to descend to the mortal realm. He was the cosmic CEO, the absentee landlord of the universe, who delegated the messy business of creation, destruction, and divine squabbling to his children and grandchildren. Yet, his authority was absolute: kings ruled by his decree, the gods deferred to his judgment, and even the great hero Gilgamesh found himself humbled by a creature made at Anu's command. To understand Anu is to understand the very concept of divine order — the idea that the universe is a kingdom, and every kingdom needs a king.

Anu is the archetypal 'king of the gods' — distant, authoritative, and somewhat passive. He does not often descend to the mortal realm or engage in the petty squabbles of other deities. He functions as a final arbiter, a source of authority, and a genealogical head. In myths, he is often asked for permission or aid, rather than acting directly. He is the source of the divine mandate (me), the cosmic laws that govern the universe.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Anu derives from the Sumerian word 'An', which simply means 'sky' or 'heaven'. In Akkadian, this became 'Anu'. The name is a direct and literal identification of the god with the sky itself, suggesting a primeval, elemental force rather than a deity with a distinct personality.

Pronunciation — AH-noo (Akkadian), AHN (Sumerian)

Aliases — Anum, Anu-um, An (Sumerian), Ilum, A-nu, Annu

Powers
Absolute Kingship of the Gods

Anu is the supreme authority in the divine assembly. His word is law, and his decrees (terētu) are final. Other gods, even powerful ones like Enlil and Ea, defer to his judgment. He is the ultimate source of the king's mandate on earth.

Limit: His power is largely administrative and juridical. He rarely exercises direct physical power in myths, often delegating tasks to other gods.

The Divine Me (mes)

Anu holds the 'me' — the fundamental cosmic laws, powers, and institutions of civilization. He is the one who originally possessed them and distributed them to other gods. They are the blueprints of the universe.

Limit: In the myth 'Inanna and Enki', Enki holds the me in his domain, and Inanna tricks him into giving them to her, suggesting that the me can be transferred and are not exclusively Anu's.

Creator of Deities and Beings

As the father of the gods, Anu's primary power is creation. He created the Anunnaki (the great gods), and in the Epic of Gilgamesh, he is asked by the gods to create a match for Gilgamesh, resulting in Enkidu.

Limit: He does not create alone; he is often part of a divine council decision. His creations are often made to solve a specific problem.

Granting Kingship

Anu is the ultimate source of earthly kingship. The king is the 'appointee of Anu' or 'the one who is called by Anu'. He grants the crown, the throne, and the scepter to the chosen ruler.

Limit: This power is often delegated to other gods like Enlil or Marduk, who are said to 'call' the king.

The Bull of Heaven

Anu could send the Bull of Heaven, a monstrous celestial beast, to cause destruction on earth. This was a direct exercise of his power to punish or terrorize.

Limit: This power is granted to his daughter Ishtar, who must ask him for it. He only gives it to her with reluctance, and it is ultimately defeated by Gilgamesh and Enkidu.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Anu is rarely depicted in human form in surviving art. He is usually symbolized by a horned crown (the divine symbol of supreme authority), a star or rosette, or a mountain. When he is shown, he appears as a mature, bearded king seated on a throne, often wearing a multi-horned crown, signifying his supreme rank. The number '60' was sacred to him, representing the highest tier of the divine hierarchy.

Symbols — The horned crown (tiara), the star (often the star of Ishtar, his daughter, but also a general symbol for 'god'), the rosette, the mountain, the number 60, the sacred tree (in some contexts). The bull was a common sacrificial animal to him, but the bull is more specifically associated with his son, the storm god Adad/Ishkur.

Domains — The sky, the heavens, the cosmos itself. He is the father of the gods, the king of the divine assembly, the source of all authority and kingship. His role is not over a specific natural phenomenon (like Enlil with wind/storms or Enki with water/wisdom) but over the entire cosmos. He is the ultimate source of the divine order (me).

Accounts I
The Creation of EnkiduEpic of Gilgamesh, Tablet I

c. 1300-1000 BCE (Standard version)

The gods hear the complaints of the people of Uruk about the arrogant king Gilgamesh. They appeal to Anu, the father of the gods. Anu instructs the goddess Aruru to create a wild man, Enkidu, who will be a match for Gilgamesh and humble him. Enkidu is created, becomes Gilgamesh's friend, and together they undertake heroic quests, leading to Enkidu's death and Gilgamesh's search for immortality.

The Bull of HeavenEpic of Gilgamesh, Tablet VI

c. 1300-1000 BCE (Standard version)

After Gilgamesh rejects the goddess Ishtar's advances, she goes to her father Anu and demands the Bull of Heaven to punish Gilgamesh. Anu hesitates, warning her of the consequences, but ultimately gives in to her threat to break open the underworld. The Bull wreaks havoc on Uruk until Gilgamesh and Enkidu slay it. The slaying of the Bull leads to a divine council in which Anu and Enlil decree that one of the heroes must die. Enkidu is chosen, leading to his death and the second half of the epic.

Accounts II
The Theogony of the Enuma ElishEnuma Elish, Tablet I

c. 1100 BCE

The epic describes the creation of the universe from the primordial waters of Apsu (fresh water) and Tiamat (salt water). These two beget the first generation of gods, including Lahmu and Lahamu, then Anshar and Kishar, and finally Anu. Anu is the son of Anshar and Kishar, and the father of Ea (Nudimmud). Anu's generation is the one that conflicts with the older gods. Ea defeats Apsu, and Marduk (Ea's son) defeats Tiamat, establishing Babylon's supremacy. Anu's role is that of a transitional figure, part of the lineage that leads to Marduk.

The Descent of IshtarThe Descent of Ishtar to the Underworld

c. 1300-1000 BCE

Ishtar descends to the underworld to challenge her sister Ereshkigal. She is trapped and killed. The god Ea creates a male prostitute, Asushunamir, to rescue her. Anu is not a direct actor in this myth but is referenced as her father, and the gods appeal to him for aid. Ishtar's resurrection is a metaphor for the cycle of seasons. The myth explains the changing of the seasons and the power of the underworld.
