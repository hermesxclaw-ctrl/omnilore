Who It Is

The winter has lasted four months, and you've run out of food. Your family is too weak to move. That's when you start hearing it — a whisper in the wind that sounds like your own voice. It tells you that hunger isn't a weakness. It's a door. And if you're brave enough to walk through it, you'll never be hungry again. You think about the sound of your children's bones cracking. The whisper laughs. It knows you're thinking about it.

The Wendigo is a malevolent spirit from Algonquian folklore, born from the ultimate taboo: cannibalism in times of starvation. It is the embodiment of insatiable greed and hunger — a creature that grows in proportion to what it eats, so it can never be full. The more it consumes, the larger and hungrier it becomes. It is freezing cold, gaunt to the point of emaciation, and its heart is a solid block of ice. It smells of rotting meat and copper — the scent of death and old blood.

But the Wendigo is not merely a monster that eats people. It is a spirit of possession. When someone resorts to cannibalism to survive a desperate winter, the Wendigo enters them. They become Wendigo — consumed by an endless, maddening hunger that drives them to devour everyone they ever loved. The Wendigo is the horror of what starvation makes people do, and the even greater horror that they might come to enjoy it. It is not a creature that jumps out of the woods. It is a creature that was your neighbor, your friend, your father — until the hunger took them and left something else wearing their face.

Origins

The Wendigo originates in the spiritual traditions of Algonquian-speaking peoples — including the Cree, Ojibwe, Innu, and other indigenous nations of the northern United States and Canada. It is not a single creature but a spirit that can manifest in multiple hosts. The first Wendigo was said to have been a warrior who made a deal with dark spirits for power and was transformed into a hunger-mad monster. In other versions, the Wendigo is a nature spirit corrupted by human desperation and taboo-breaking. The condition 'Wendigo psychosis' was historically documented among Algonquian communities — individuals who developed an obsessive craving for human flesh during severe winters.

Form

In the most terrifying accounts, the Wendigo is gaunt to the point of skeletal transparency — skin stretched tight over bones, eyes sunken deep in their sockets, lips chewed away by its own hunger. It is tall, sometimes 15 feet or more, towering and emaciated, with grey or ash-colored skin. Its fingers are elongated into claws, and its teeth are jagged, broken needles. Some accounts give it antlers — a modern artistic addition that has become iconic, though not universally traditional. It may have a deer skull for a head in some versions. In all depictions, it is emaciated, cold, and starving no matter what it eats.

Powers

Insatiable hunger with proportional growth Every meal makes the wendigo larger, so it can never be full; it grows in proportion to what it eats and remains perpetually starving (Johnston 2001:222, 226; Johnston 1990:166; Schwarz 1969:11). Attested across Ojibwe, Cree, and related traditions; universal in the documentary record. The hunger is also the being's undoing: it can be baited, distracted, or exploited by communities (as in the Rama story, where villagers use the wendigo's appetite and credulity against it). None serious; the growth motif is consistent, though pop culture sometimes depicts it as mere size increase without the moral weight.

Possession and transformation of humans Humans overpowered by greed, or driven by famine to cannibalism, can become wendigos; transformation can also come from prolonged contact with a wendigo. Communities watch for signs — strange dreams, withdrawal, violent impulses — and respond with curing or, in the worst cases, killing the afflicted (Johnston 2001:222–225; Johnston 1990:167; Brightman 1988). Attested in traditional narratives and in the historical cases (Swift Runner; the 1661 Jesuit Relations account). Some traditions hold the transformation reversible if caught early — Cree folklore prescribes fatty meats and animal grease, with the afflicted sometimes vomiting ice as part of the cure (Rohrl 1970:97–101). Whether transformation is irreversible once cannibalism occurs is debated across communities; some emphasize healing, others inevitability.

Speech, cognition, tool use, and survival of dismemberment The Rama First Nation story (collected 1918) shows a wendigo that talks, threatens, carries a knife, tests a captive for fatness, survives having both legs cut off, and eats the marrow from its own severed bones before being killed and dismembered (Marsden 1918:104–105). Attested in documented oral narrative. Even this wendigo is finally killed by communal action — it is not invulnerable. The same story was flagged by editors as containing unusual elements (tool use, autocannibalism); treat as one documented specimen, not a universal template.

Voice mimicry, airborne speed, and the 'burning feet' In Blackwood's 1910 novella the wendigo cries the guide's name in a 'windy, crying voice' from the sky, carries him off with 'fiery speed,' and returns him as a changed being with 'great changed feet' (Blackwood 1910). Adaptation-specific (Blackwood and later horror); not an attested feature of the older Algonquian accounts. Literary invention; often misread as traditional. Some modern tellings borrow the mimicry wholesale, which is why it must be labeled.

Foul stench and unseasonable chill The wendigo's approach is said to be announced by an odor of decay and death and by a sudden, unnatural cold (Johnston 2001:221; Zarka 2019). Attested in Johnston's description and echoed in several regional accounts. Sensory detail varies; not all accounts include it. None.

Heart of ice and vulnerability to fire Some representations describe a heart of ice; fire and burning appear as means of destruction in some traditions, and the ice-heart motif resonates with the ice-vomiting cures recorded among Cree (Zarka 2019; Rohrl 1970). Variant-specific; strongest in modern retellings. Not a universal weakness system; do not present fire/silver as a codified rule set outside specific accounts. Different communities and storytellers give different vulnerabilities; the 'silver bullet' style rules of modern horror are not traditional.

Conceptual power: the wendigo as greed and empire As a concept, the wendigo applies to any person, movement, or institution infected by corrosive, self-aggrandizing consumption — DeSanti's 'marker' of imbalance, Schedler's 'consuming forms of exclusion and assimilation,' Lockhard's 'social cannibalism' that respects no borders (DeSanti 2015:197; Schedler 2011:32; Lockhard 2008:209–219). Documented contemporary Indigenous and scholarly usage; Ravenous (1999) dramatizes the reading of manifest destiny as wendigo appetite (DeSanti 2015:195). Metaphorical, not physical; nonetheless a central part of the figure's modern meaning. Some critics worry the metaphor flattens the spiritual reality of the being; both registers coexist in Indigenous usage.

Spirit Possession The wendigo is primarily a malevolent spirit that can possess human beings, particularly those weakened by famine, isolation, or greed. The possessed develop an insatiable craving for human flesh and may commit murder and cannibalism. Algonquian traditional belief; Jesuit Relations (1661) The concept of 'Wendigo psychosis' as a psychiatric condition is hotly debated. Some scholars consider it a fabrication by anthropologists. Some accounts describe wendigos as physical creatures rather than possessing spirits.

Proportional Growth Whenever a wendigo eats a person, it grows in proportion to the meal consumed. This means it can never be full or satisfied — the hunger perpetually exceeds the body. This creates the paradox of a creature simultaneously gluttonous and emaciated. Ojibwe, Eastern Cree, Naskapi, and Innu traditions This trait is specific to certain Algonquian subgroups and absent from others. None within the traditions that include it.

Near-Immortality / Regeneration In some tales, the wendigo can survive extreme dismemberment and engages in autocannibalism. In the Marsden folktale, a wendigo whose legs are cut off calmly eats the marrow from its own severed bones and survives until killed completely. Lottie Chicogquaw Marsden collection (Chippewas of Rama) Not all traditions include this ability; it may be specific to certain narrative traditions. Wendigos can be killed in traditional lore — usually by a medicine person like Jack Fiddler.

Environmental Influence The wendigo's approach is heralded by environmental signs: a sudden unseasonable chill, a foul stench of decay, and sometimes a whistling wind. It embodies winter itself. Various Algonquian traditions These signs are narrative and atmospheric; they are not presented as 'powers' in the same way as possession. None.

Behavior & Habitat

The Wendigo hunts constantly, driven by a hunger that can never be satisfied. It prefers isolated victims — travelers, hunters, families snowed in for the winter. It is intelligent and cruel, enjoying the psychological torment of its prey before killing them. It mimics voices to create confusion and false hope. It often targets those who are already desperate, drawn to the emotional state that creates new Wendigos. Once it has consumed someone, it may save a piece of them — a finger, an ear — to savor later, because the hunger is not just physical but spiritual.

Weaknesses & Wards

Fire is its primary weakness — Wendigos are frozen creatures and can be destroyed by intense heat. Silver, in some traditions, can harm them. Certain shamans and medicine people know rituals to exorcise or destroy Wendigo spirits. Decapitation and burning the heart (the block of ice) is a traditional method of permanently killing one. It is bound by winter — it weakens or retreats in warm weather. Its hunger is a weakness as much as a strength; it can be baited or distracted by food, and its compulsion to devour can override its intelligence.

Accounts

During the winter of 1878, Plains Cree trapper Swift Runner faced starvation with his family. His eldest son died. Despite being only 25 miles from a Hudson's Bay Company food supply post, Swift Runner butchered and ate his wife and all five remaining children. Given his proximity to food supplies and the complete consumption of his family, authorities determined this was not survival cannibalism but wendigo psychosis. Swift Runner confessed and was hanged at Fort Saskatchewan.

Jack Fiddler (Zhauwuno-geezhigo-gaubow), an Oji-Cree chief and renowned medicine man, was known for his power to defeat wendigos — spiritual beings that possessed people and made them cannibalistic. Over his lifetime, he claimed to have defeated fourteen wendigos. In 1907, he and his brother Joseph were arrested by Canadian authorities for the killing of a woman believed to be afflicted with wendigo psychosis. Jack committed suicide in custody. Joseph was tried, sentenced to life imprisonment, pardoned, but died in jail three days before receiving the pardon.

A giant wendigo steals a thin Indian boy but doesn't eat him immediately, waiting for him to fatten up. The wendigo travels with the boy, periodically cutting his hand to check if he's fat enough. They arrive at an Indian village and the wendigo sends the boy to get food, giving him a time limit. The boy warns the villagers, who find and attack the wendigo, cutting off its legs. When they return to check, the wendigo is eating marrow from its own severed leg bones and boasts: 'I have eaten lots of Indians, no wonder they are fat.' The villagers then kill and dismember it completely.

During times of famine, a satirical ceremonial dance was performed to reinforce the wendigo taboo against cannibalism. Participants wore masks and danced backward around a drum, symbolically enacting the chaos and wrongness of wendigo behavior. The ceremony served as both a spiritual prophylactic and a community-binding ritual during moments of extreme stress.

Field Notes

Cultural Significance

The Wendigo is far more than a campfire story — it is a deeply serious figure in Algonquian spiritual and ethical traditions. It embodies the taboo against cannibalism, the most fundamental violation of human community. The concept of 'Wendigo psychosis' reflects real cultural practices around starvation and survival. The Wendigo serves as a warning about greed in all its forms — not just hunger for flesh, but hunger for wealth, power, and consumption that destroys communities. It is a critique of selfishness made flesh, a monster born from the death of empathy.

Modern Interpretations

The Wendigo has been adopted into mainstream horror media, often stripped of its original cultural context. Stephen King's 'Pet Sematary' incorporates Wendigo elements. The video game 'Until Dawn' features Wendigos as the primary antagonists — former humans transformed by cannibalism. Marvel Comics features a Wendigo character. The TV show 'Supernatural' and 'Hannibal' have both featured Wendigo-themed episodes. Unfortunately, many modern depictions reduce the Wendigo to a simple monster or cryptid, losing the spiritual and ethical dimensions that make it truly horrifying. Many Algonquian communities discourage casual use of the name, believing speaking it attracts the spirit's attention.

What We Don't Know

The exact relationship between wendigo folklore and historical famine events among Algonquian peoples has not been comprehensively mapped. The transition of the wendigo from a culturally specific spiritual concept to a generic pop-culture monster is well-documented in literary studies but less studied in terms of its impact on Indigenous communities. The question of wendigo psychosis's historical reality remains unresolved and may benefit from collaborative research between Indigenous knowledge-keepers and medical anthropologists. The specific role of the wechuge in Athabaskan traditions is significantly understudied compared to the Algonquian wendigo.

A way to hold it in your mind

In the deep winter forests of the Subarctic, where hunger can hollow a body and silence can press like ice against the ribs, stories speak of a being that grows larger with every bite it takes. This is the Wendigo—a manitou of insatiable appetite, born at the edge of starvation and greed. For some, it is a monstrous cannibal stalking snowbound hunters; for others, it is the face of colonial extraction, devouring land and lives with the same cold hunger. To follow the Wendigo through Algonquian worlds and modern horror is to trace a path through fear, law, trauma, and resistance, where every footprint in the snow asks what happens when we consume more than we can ever repay.
