Who It Is

Picture a winter that will not end. The lake is frozen to the bottom of its name, the moose have gone south or died, and the only thing left in the cache is birch bark. In the long dark of such a season, the Cree and the Ojibwe and the Innu told a particular kind of story — not to frighten children at a fire, but to keep a starving family from becoming something worse than dead. They said there is a being that grows with every bite it takes, so it can never be full: the wendigo, the manitou of hunger and greed, the thing a person becomes when they eat the people they love. It walks the snow country gaunt as a skeleton in the shape of your neighbor, and its heart, in some tellings, is ice. It is winter itself, hunger itself, selfishness itself, given a face. Etymology — The English word is a loanword, partly from Cree wīhtikōw and partly from Ojibwe wiindigoo, both Algonquian languages. Comparable English forms are attested since 1714, when the spelling 'Whitego' appears in colonial writing; the modern spelling 'wendigo' was popularized by Algernon Blackwood's 1910

Pronunciation — English approximations: /ˈwɛndɪɡoʊ/ (wendigo) and /ˈwɪndɪɡoʊ/ (windigo). The Ojibwe form wiindigoo and Cree wīhtikōw carry vowel lengths and pitches that English spellings flatten; the doubled 'ii' in wiindigoo indicates a long vowel.

Aliases — Windigo, Wiindigoo (Ojibwe), Wīhtikōw (Cree), Witiko, Wétiko, Wheetigo

Powers
Insatiable hunger with proportional growth

Every meal makes the wendigo larger, so it can never be full; it grows in proportion to what it eats and remains perpetually starving (Johnston 2001:222, 226; Johnston 1990:166; Schwarz 1969:11).

Limit: The hunger is also the being's undoing: it can be baited, distracted, or exploited by communities (as in the Rama story, where villagers use the wendigo's appetite and credulity against it).

Possession and transformation of humans

Humans overpowered by greed, or driven by famine to cannibalism, can become wendigos; transformation can also come from prolonged contact with a wendigo. Communities watch for signs — strange dreams, withdrawal, violent impulses — and respond with curing or, in the worst cases, killing the afflicted (Johnston 2001:222–225; Johnston 1990:167; Brightman 1988).

Limit: Some traditions hold the transformation reversible if caught early — Cree folklore prescribes fatty meats and animal grease, with the afflicted sometimes vomiting ice as part of the cure (Rohrl 1970:97–101).

Speech, cognition, tool use, and survival of dismemberment

The Rama First Nation story (collected 1918) shows a wendigo that talks, threatens, carries a knife, tests a captive for fatness, survives having both legs cut off, and eats the marrow from its own severed bones before being killed and dismembered (Marsden 1918:104–105).

Limit: Even this wendigo is finally killed by communal action — it is not invulnerable.

Voice mimicry, airborne speed, and the 'burning feet'

In Blackwood's 1910 novella the wendigo cries the guide's name in a 'windy, crying voice' from the sky, carries him off with 'fiery speed,' and returns him as a changed being with 'great changed feet' (Blackwood 1910).

Limit: Literary invention; often misread as traditional.

Foul stench and unseasonable chill

The wendigo's approach is said to be announced by an odor of decay and death and by a sudden, unnatural cold (Johnston 2001:221; Zarka 2019).

Limit: Sensory detail varies; not all accounts include it.

Heart of ice and vulnerability to fire

Some representations describe a heart of ice; fire and burning appear as means of destruction in some traditions, and the ice-heart motif resonates with the ice-vomiting cures recorded among Cree (Zarka 2019; Rohrl 1970).

Limit: Not a universal weakness system; do not present fire/silver as a codified rule set outside specific accounts.

Conceptual power: the wendigo as greed and empire

As a concept, the wendigo applies to any person, movement, or institution infected by corrosive, self-aggrandizing consumption — DeSanti's 'marker' of imbalance, Schedler's 'consuming forms of exclusion and assimilation,' Lockhard's 'social cannibalism' that respects no borders (DeSanti 2015:197; Schedler 2011:32; Lockhard 2008:209–219).

Limit: Metaphorical, not physical; nonetheless a central part of the figure's modern meaning.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The most widely attested traditional description, given by Ojibwe scholar Basil Johnston, is not a beast with antlers but a human-shaped horror: a being gaunt to the point of emaciation, its desiccated skin stretched over protruding bones, its complexion the ash-gray of death, eyes sunken deep into their sockets, lips tattered and bloody, giving off an odor of decay, decomposition, death, and corruption (Johnston 2001:221). Symbols — The wendigo symbolizes gluttony, greed, and excess — never satisfied after killing and consuming one person, it searches constantly for new victims (Goldman 2005:89). It is bound to winter, cold, famine, and starvation (Brightman 1988:362), and in Ojibwe and Cree understanding it embodies the breaking of the deepest communal taboo, cannibalism, and the selfishness that lets a person devour kin. Domains — The wendigo belongs to the boreal forest and subarctic winter: the deep snow country of the northern Great Lakes, the Canadian Shield, and the northern plains. Its power is bound to winter scarcity — famine, failed hunts, isolated camps — and it is said to be felt in howling wind, creaking trees, and the stillness of snowbound woods (Brightman 1988:362). Accounts I
The Windigo and the Stolen Boy (Rama First Nation)Marsden 1918, Story No. 104, 'Ojibwa Myths and Tales'

Documented early 20th century; story itself of indeterminate age

A big Windigo steals an Indian boy but finds him too thin to eat, so it travels with him, waiting for him to fatten. The Windigo carries a knife and cuts the boy's hand to test whether he is fat enough. The boy warns a village, showing his scarred hand; the villagers cut off the Windigo's legs, return to find it alive and eating the marrow from its own severed leg bones, and then kill it and cut it to pieces — 'This was the end of this Giant Windigo.' The community's decisive, cooperative action destroys the monster; the story models collective defense against an appetite that preys on the isolated and the weak.

The Afflicted Deputies (Jesuit Relations, 1661)The Jesuit Relations, vol. XLVI (Thwaites ed.)

1661

The earliest written account of the condition later called windigo psychosis: men deputed to summon nations to the North Sea were seized by an ailment 'unknown to us, but not very unusual among the people we were seeking' — a more-than-canine hunger that made them pounce on women, children, and men 'like veritable werewolves,' unable to be satisfied. Since death was the only remedy their people knew, the afflicted were slain to stop the killing. The report documents both the belief and the community's lethal response to it, and it is the foundational European text on the subject — read with care, because the missionary lens shapes what it records.

Swift Runner (Alberta, 1878–1879)Brightman 1988:352–353, 373; press coverage (Hanon 2008)

1878–1879

Swift Runner, a Plains Cree trapper, spent a starving winter with his family near Fort Saskatchewan, Alberta; his eldest son died. Though emergency food waited only twenty-five miles away at a Hudson's Bay Company post, Swift Runner butchered and ate his wife and five remaining children. He confessed, was tried for murder, and was executed at Fort Saskatchewan in 1879. Commentators read the case as wendigo psychosis because the killing happened so near to food — not survival cannibalism but something else, a man in the grip of the hunger. One of the most famous documented wendigo cases; it enters the historical record through court and press accounts and is discussed by Brightman as evidence for the reality of the affliction in some form.

Jack Fiddler of Sandy Lake (1907)Fiddler & Stevens 1985 (Killing the Shamen)

Died 1907

Jack Fiddler was an Oji-Cree chief and medicine man renowned for destroying wendigos — in practice, for executing people believed to be afflicted. In 1907 he and his brother Joseph were arrested by Canadian authorities for homicide. Jack died by suicide while in custody; Joseph was tried, sentenced to life imprisonment, later pardoned, and died in jail three days before the pardon reached him. The case marks the collision of the traditional windigo response with Canadian criminal law, and it remains a contested memory: some see Fiddler as a protector, others as a killer; the colonial legal system's intervention produced its own tragedy.

Accounts II
The Famine Transformation (the underlying narrative pattern)Synthesis across Johnston 2001, Brightman 1988, Teicher 1960

Documented 17th–20th centuries; pattern presumably older

The core narrative recurs across the subarctic: a group faces famine; one person, driven by hunger and fear, dreams of eating people, withdraws, and begins to crave human flesh; the community recognizes the signs and responds — with grease and fatty food, with ceremony, with exile, or in the worst cases with execution. The story is a machine for enforcing the taboo that keeps a starving band from eating itself. The pattern gave rise to the ethnographic category 'windigo psychosis' and to the 1980s scholarly war over whether the affliction was real, an artifact of belief, or an artifact of the ethnographers (Marano 1982; Brightman 1988; Waldram 2004).

The Wendigo (Blackwood, 1910)Algernon Blackwood, 'The Wendigo' (Project Gutenberg #10897)

1910

Four men in the Canadian bush — two hunters, a doctor, and the guide Défago — camp near a region the Indians will not enter. In the night a voice cries 'Dé-fa-go!' from the sky; the guide answers and is gone, screaming 'Oh! oh! My feet of fire! My burning feet of fire! Oh! oh! This height and fiery speed!' Later the survivors glimpse Défago again, transformed, his feet 'changed.' The story never shows the wendigo directly; it works through voice, wind, and absence. The novella popularized the English spelling 'wendigo' (OED 2024) and set the template for the horror-wendigo; Joe Nazare (2000) argues its 'subtly-demonizing rhetoric transforms the Wendigo from a native myth into a descriptive template for the Indian savage.'

The Wendigo as Colonial Critique (contemporary Indigenous literature)Vizenor's Bearheart (via Schedler 2011; Lockhard 2008); Hogan's Solar Storms (Hans 2003); Erdrich's The Round House (2012); Adler's Wrist (2016); also Boyden's Three Day Road and Highway's Kiss of the Fur Queen as cited in prior enrichment

1978–present

Indigenous writers deploy the wendigo as a lens on war trauma, residential-school violence, sexual violence, and corporate extraction. In Hogan's Solar Storms the creature is bound to corporate voracity; in Erdrich's The Round House the novel's antagonist, a rapist who desecrates a sacred site, is read as a wendigo who must be stopped; in Vizenor's Bearheart the wiindigoo becomes a figure of 'Native transmotion' and sovereignty — a reclamation of the being as an analytical tool rather than a monster for hire. These works reframe the wendigo as a term of diagnosis for the modern world's appetites — pipelines, clear-cuts, conquest — and as a site of Indigenous creative authority.

Until Dawn (2015)Supermassive Games; IGN review (O'Brien 2015); Johnson 2022

2015

The interactive horror game Until Dawn is set on a mountain once used by a mining company whose trapped workers turned to cannibalism; the survivors became wendigos — fast, gaunt, white-skinned creatures that infest the mountain. The game's wendigos are central antagonists and visually iconic, and the game is the single most influential recent popularization of the figure. Francesca Amee Johnson (2022) cites Until Dawn as a leading example of the problematic trope: mostly non-Native writers using an Indigenous antagonist 'for white protagonists to defeat repeatedly,' misappropriating and demonizing the culture the myth comes from.

Was windigo psychosis a real historical phenomenon?No — largely an artifact. Marano (1982) argued the syndrome was 'an emic-etic confusion': ethnographers took stories at face value, and many 'cases' were accusations that justified ostracism or execution. Waldram (2004) went further: no actual cases have ever been studied, and the disorder is a scholarly construction.Yes — credible eyewitness accounts exist. Brightman (1988) assembled eyewitness and archival evidence (including the Swift Runner case) and defended the phenomenon's historicity.
Giant or human-sized?Many Algonquian traditions describe wendigos as giants many times larger than humans (Ojibwe, Eastern Cree, Westmain Swampy Cree, Naskapi, Innu).Other traditions describe beings of roughly human scale — the terror being that they pass as people.
Antlers and deer featuresTraditional descriptions — Johnston's above all — describe a gaunt, ash-gray, lipless humanoid with no antlers.Popular culture depicts a deer-skulled, antlered beast (Antlers 2021; Until Dawn; internet art).
Spirit, transformed human, or metaphor?The wendigo is a distinct spiritual being (a manitou) that possesses people (Johnston 2001).The wendigo is a human transformed by taboo-breaking, and the 'being' is secondary to the condition.
Relation to the wechugeThe wechuge is essentially the Athabaskan wendigo.The wechuge is a distinct being: cannibalistic, yes, but 'enlightened with ancestral insights,' not malevolent in the same way.
Age of the traditionThe wendigo is ancient, predating European contact (the master entity record files it under 'Medieval').Its antiquity is unknowable; the earliest written attestation is the 1661 Jesuit Relations, and the English loanword appears only in 1714 (OED).

The wendigo is an antlered deer-skull monster, as seen in films and games. — Attested traditional descriptions — Basil Johnston's above all — describe a gaunt, ash-gray, emaciated humanoid with tattered lips and an odor of decay, not an antlered beast (Johnston 2001:221). The antlered look is twentieth- and twenty-first-century pop art.

The wendigo and the Navajo skin-walker are the same thing. — They come from unrelated peoples (Algonquian vs. Diné), unrelated languages, and different ceremonial worlds. The equation is a widespread internet conflation that members of both communities generally reject.

Wendigo psychosis is an official psychiatric diagnosis. — It has never been in the DSM. ICD-10 mentions 'Windigo' among culture-specific disorders while explicitly noting that controversial studies question its legitimacy (WHO 1993:213–225); Marano (1982) and Waldram (2004) argue the category was constructed by scholars. It is best described as a documented culture-bound belief, not a confirmed disorder.

The wendigo is a cryptid — a flesh-and-blood animal like Bigfoot. — The wendigo is a spiritual and moral figure in Algonquian traditions, tied to winter, famine, and the cannibalism taboo. The 'flesh-and-blood cryptid' framing comes from paranormal-hunting circles and from Marvel Comics' bigfoot-like Wendigo (1973), which is a named adaptation, not folklore.

It's just a campfire monster for horror movies. — For many Algonquian communities the wendigo is a serious spiritual and ethical teaching about greed and the limits of consumption — and in some communities the word itself is not spoken casually (Horn 2013; Johnston 2001). Treating it as a generic spooky monster erases that.

Saying the name 'wendigo' summons it, so you must never say it. — Some communities do maintain taboos around the word and around winter storytelling, but this is tradition-specific and not a universal rule; the 'summoning' mechanic is mostly a horror-fiction device.

Native people no longer believe in the wendigo; it's extinct folklore. — The wendigo remains a living concept: community commentary (Horn 2013), Ojibwe scholarship (DeSanti 2015; Johnston 2001), and a substantial contemporary Indigenous literature (Vizenor, Hogan, Erdrich, Adler, and others) engage it as spiritual reality, moral diagnosis, and political metaphor.

The wendigo grows forever and can never be destroyed. — Growth with consumption is attested (Johnston 2001:222, 226; Schwarz 1969:11), but the stories also show wendigos being killed — by communal action (Marsden 1918), by curing (Rohrl 1970), and in some accounts by fire. It is powerful, not omnipotent.
