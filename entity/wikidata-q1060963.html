The Approach

Imagine a creature that feasts on the dead, lurking in graveyards and mountain shadows—a spirit so feared that even its name became a byword for the uncanny. In Japanese folklore, the mōryō is a shape-shifting ghoul that steals corpses and devours the innards of the deceased, a being that blurs the line between nature spirit and malevolent ghost.

Content advisory. Depictions of death, corpse theft, and consumption of human remains. Some sources describe harm to the living, including violence and supernatural attacks.
Identity

Mōryō

Pronunciation Mō-ryō (Japanese: もーりょー); Wangliang (Chinese: wǎng-liǎng)

Known As

Mizuha魍魎罔両美豆波Wangliang (Chinese)Mōryō (Japanese)

Etymology

The Japanese term 'mōryō' (魍魎) is derived from the Chinese 'wǎngliǎng' (魍魎), which appears in classical Chinese texts. The alternative reading 'mizuha' (美豆波) is a Japanese kun'yomi that may relate to water or spirits. The characters themselves combine radicals associated with ghosts and demons.

Cultural Ownership

The mōryō originates in Chinese mythology and was later adopted into Japanese folklore, where it became associated with various yōkai and nature spirits. It is thus shared between Chinese and Japanese cultural traditions, with distinct developments in each.

Disputed Identifications

In Japan, the mōryō is sometimes identified with the kasha, a corpse-stealing yōkai, due to similarities in behavior. However, this identification is not universally accepted, and some scholars treat them as distinct entities. Additionally, the mōryō is sometimes conflated with other nature spirits, such as kodama or yamabiko, but these are separate yōkai.

Description

Appearance

According to the Huainanzi, mōryō resemble a three-year-old child, with dark red skin, red eyes, long ears, and beautiful hair. Later Japanese depictions may vary, often showing them as shadowy or monstrous figures.

Symbols & Iconography

In Chinese tradition, mōryō are associated with graves, darkness, and the consumption of the dead. They are sometimes depicted with daggers or tools used to enter graves. In Japan, they are linked to yōkai lore and are sometimes depicted in ukiyo-e art as grotesque creatures.

Behaviour & Personality

Mōryō are nocturnal spirits that haunt graveyards, mountains, and rivers. They are known to steal corpses and consume the innards or brains of the dead. They are shy of tigers and oak trees, which can be used to repel or kill them. In the Mimibukuro tale, a mōryō can disguise itself as a human retainer, indicating shape-shifting abilities.

Domains & Habitats

Mountains, rivers, trees, rocks, graveyards, and other liminal spaces. They are nature spirits that can inhabit various environmental features.

Version Differences

Chinese sources describe mōryō as nature spirits with specific physical traits, while Japanese folklore adapts them as yōkai that steal corpses, often conflating them with kasha. The appearance and behavior may vary between regions and eras.

Stories & Myths

The Mōryō Retainer

Source Mimibukuro by Negishi ShizumoriCulture JapanesePeriod Edo period

Summary

A government official named Shibata had a loyal retainer who, one evening, revealed he was not human but a mōryō. He explained that it was his turn to steal a corpse from a certain village, and he had to resign. The next day, the retainer disappeared. At a funeral in that village, dark clouds suddenly covered the sky, and when they cleared, the corpse was gone from the coffin.

Participants

Shibata

The retainer (mōryō)

Villagers

Consequences

The retainer vanished, and the corpse was stolen, demonstrating the mōryō's role as a corpse thief.

Variants

Similar stories are told about kasha, indicating a conflation between the two yōkai.

Contradictions & Disputes

Identification with kasha

Position A

Mōryō are the same as kasha because both steal corpses.

Position A — evidence

Edo-period texts like Mimibukuro use the name mōryō for stories similar to kasha.

Position B

Mōryō are distinct from kasha, with different origins and characteristics.

Position B — evidence

Kasha are specifically associated with funeral processions and are often depicted as cats, while mōryō have a broader nature-spirit origin.

Scholarly Consensus

There is no consensus; some scholars treat them as synonymous, others as distinct.

Physical description

Position A

Mōryō resemble a three-year-old child with dark red skin and red eyes.

Position A — evidence

Huainanzi provides a specific description.

Position B

Mōryō are formless or shape-shifting spirits.

Position B — evidence

Later Japanese tales suggest they can take human form, implying no fixed appearance.

Scholarly Consensus

The Huainanzi description is early and specific, but later folklore allows for shape-shifting.

Primary Evidence

Huainanzi (Chinese philosophical text)

Type textDate 2nd century BCELocator Chapter on SpiritualityLanguage Chinese

Translation

Mōryō have a shape like that of a three-year-old little child, are dark red in color, have red eyes, long ears, and beautiful hair.

Compendium of Materia Medica (Bencao Gangmu) by Li Shizhen

Type textDate 1596Locator Section on anomalous creaturesLanguage Chinese

Translation

Mōryō like to eat the innards of the dead. It would then perform the 'Rites of Zhou', take a dagger-axe and go into the grave hole, and bring destruction. In its true nature, the mōryō is fearful of tigers and oak, and is given the name 弗述. They go underground and eat the brains of the dead, but it is said that when an oak is pressed against their necks, they die.

Mimibukuro by Negishi Shizumori

Type textDate Edo period (18th century)Locator Tale of the mōryō retainerLanguage Japanese

Translation

A retainer reveals himself to be a mōryō and resigns, stating he must steal a corpse. The next day, he disappears, and at a funeral, a dark cloud steals the corpse from the coffin.
