Who They Are

Ares is the god of war in its most brutal, chaotic, and bloodthirsty form — the personification of violence, carnage, and the terror of battle. Unlike Athena, who governs strategic warfare, Ares represents war's raw, mindless savagery. He is the son of Zeus and Hera, and in Homer's Iliad, Zeus tells him: 'To me you are the most hateful of all the gods who hold Olympus, for strife is always dear to you, and wars and battles.' Ares embodies the uncomfortable truth that the Greeks both needed and despised war. His Roman equivalent, Mars, was far more respected — the father of Romulus and Remus and the patron deity of the Roman state.

Origins

Ares is the legitimate son of Zeus and Hera, born within their marriage — a detail that sets him apart from many Olympians (Athena, Apollo, Artemis, Hermes, Dionysus, and others were children of Zeus by other partners). As Hera's son, Ares shared her fierce loyalty to family and her capacity for implacable anger. His birth, unlike many gods, is not marked by a dramatic narrative in the standard sources — he simply IS, the eternal principle of violence that exists wherever conflict arises.

In Hesiod's Theogony, Ares is listed among the children of Zeus and Hera, alongside Hebe (Youth) and Eileithyia (Childbirth) — a grouping that ironically juxtaposes the god of destructive war with the goddess of youthful renewal and the goddess of bringing new life into the world. In Homer's Iliad, Ares's divine lineage is clear, but his relationship with his parents is strained. Zeus explicitly states his hatred for his son. Hera, by contrast, shows maternal protectiveness — when Ares is wounded by Diomedes (guided by Athena), Hera complains to Zeus on her son's behalf.

Ares's companions in war are his children by Aphrodite: Deimos (Terror) and Phobos (Fear), who accompany him into battle and spread panic among combatants. Eris (Strife), his sister, is also his frequent companion. Together, they represent the psychological dimensions of warfare — the terror that breaks soldiers' ranks, the fear that turns victory into rout, and the strife that drives men to kill each other.

The most famous myth involving Ares's children is the story of the Dragon of Thebes, slain by Cadmus. Ares had created a dragon (or serpent) that guarded a spring near the future site of Thebes. When Cadmus killed the dragon, he was sentenced by Ares to serve the god for an 'eternal year' (eight human years). After completing his service, Cadmus was given Harmonia, Ares's daughter by Aphrodite, as his wife — a union that linked the god of war to the founding of one of Greece's greatest cities. The marriage of Cadmus and Harmonia, attended by all the gods, represents the taming of martial violence into civic order.

Ares's affair with Aphrodite is his most narratively prominent myth. The mismatch between the god of war and the goddess of love captivated Greek imagination. Hephaestus, Aphrodite's husband, forged an invisible net of unbreakable chains and trapped the lovers in bed together. He summoned all the gods to witness their humiliation. The male gods, according to Homer's comic account in the Odyssey, gathered around and laughed — Hermes commented that he would gladly endure three times as many chains to lie with Aphrodite. After Hephaestus released them (at Poseidon's intercession), Ares fled to Thrace, his traditional homeland, and Aphrodite to Cyprus.

Ares's association with Thrace — the region north of Greece, considered barbarous and warlike — reflects the Greek view that war-god worship belonged to 'barbarian' cultures. The Amazons, the warrior women who lived without men, were said to be daughters of Ares and the nymph Harmonia (not the same as Cadmus's wife). They worshipped Ares as their patron and built a temple to him on an island in the Black Sea. The Amazon queen Hippolyta's magical belt, which Heracles was sent to retrieve as his ninth labor, was a gift from Ares.

In the Roman tradition, Mars underwent a radical transformation. He was not merely a war god but the father of Romulus and Remus (by the Vestal Virgin Rhea Silvia), and thus the divine ancestor of the Roman people. Mars was second only to Jupiter in the Roman pantheon, and his cult was central to Roman identity. The Campus Martius (Field of Mars) was the training ground for Roman soldiers and the site of important political assemblies. The month of March (Martius) was named after him and marked the beginning of the military campaign season. This elevated status contrasts starkly with the Greek Ares, who was feared, despised, and marginalized within the Olympian order.

In some Orphic traditions, Ares takes on a cosmic, allegorical role. He is identified with the principle of division and conflict that drives the differentiation of the primordial unity — the necessary violence by which the One becomes the Many. This philosophical Ares is far removed from the blood-soaked warrior of Homer.

Form

Ares is depicted in Greek art as the archetypal warrior — a strong, muscular, bearded man (in earlier art) or a clean-shaven youth (in later Hellenistic and Roman art) in full battle armor. His physique is that of a lifelong soldier: massive shoulders, a thick neck, powerful arms and legs, and a torso hardened by combat. Where Zeus's body conveys settled authority and Apollo's conveys athletic grace, Ares's body conveys raw, explosive power — the build of a man who kills with his hands.

In earlier Greek vase painting, Ares appears fully armored with a crested helmet, a round shield (aspis), a spear, and a sword. He is often shown in the thick of battle, his face contorted with the fury of combat — a stark contrast to the serene composure of Athena even in her martial aspects. His helmet typically has a massive crest, making him appear even larger and more intimidating. In some depictions, his armor is splattered with blood, emphasizing his proximity to the visceral reality of killing.

The 'Ares Ludovisi,' a Roman copy of a Greek original (now in the Palazzo Altemps, Rome), depicts the god seated, his shield beside him, his sword sheathed. He appears contemplative rather than raging — a rare depiction that may represent Ares at rest after battle. An eros (cupid) plays at his feet, referencing his affair with Aphrodite and the intertwining of love and war.

His sacred animals are creatures associated with battlefields: the vulture (which feeds on corpses), the dog (which accompanied armies and scavenged the dead), and the wild boar (whose ferocity mirrors his own). The serpent, associated with the dragon he sired at Thebes, and the woodpecker (in the Roman Mars) are also his creatures.

Sensory evocation: Ares smells of sweat, blood, and bronze — the unmistakable odor of close combat. His voice is a battle cry that can be heard across entire armies, an inarticulate roar of pure aggression. The ground trembles when he strides onto a battlefield. His presence raises the heart rate, floods the system with adrenaline, and awakens the oldest, darkest part of the human brain — the part that kills and exults in killing. To be in Ares's presence is to feel your civilized restraints dissolve, replaced by the pure, terrible joy of violence.

Powers

Superhuman Strength and Combat Prowess As a god, Ares possesses immense strength and is a formidable fighter. He is described as 'man-slaughtering' and 'unconquerable' in his element. His prowess is often undermined by his lack of strategy and his tendency to be outwitted. He is beaten by Athena in battle and trapped by his own wife's husband.

Behavior & Habitat

Ares is consumed by the love of violence. Unlike Athena, who treats war as a regrettable necessity to be conducted with strategy and discipline, Ares exults in the chaos, terror, and bloodshed of battle for its own sake. He has no allegiance — he fights for whichever side offers the greater slaughter. In the Iliad, he initially supports the Trojans, but his loyalty is fickle; when Athena intervenes, he readily switches sides when defeated. His behavior on the battlefield is described with visceral horror by Homer: he 'raged like a black whirlwind,' and the ground was drenched with blood wherever he passed.

Among the Olympians, Ares is isolated and despised. Zeus hates him. Athena considers him an embarrassment to the concept of war. The other gods avoid him, and he avoids them. His only genuine connections are with Aphrodite (whose affair with him scandalized Olympus) and Hera (who protects him with maternal instinct). Eris (Strife) and Enyo (a war goddess sometimes identified as his sister or daughter) are his companions, along with Deimos and Phobos.

Ares's love for Aphrodite introduces complexity to his otherwise one-dimensional character. Their affair produced Eros (Cupid), Harmonia, Deimos, and Phobos — a family that spans the full range from love to terror. The myth suggests that war and love are intimately connected: both are forms of passion that overwhelm reason, both create and destroy, and both are irresistible forces that humans and gods cannot control.

Ares's relationship with his mortal children is notably protective. When his son Cycnus (a brutal bandit who killed travelers to build a temple from their bones) was killed by Heracles, Ares attacked Heracles in a rage and had to be restrained by Athena. When his daughter Alcippe was raped by Halirrhothius, son of Poseidon, Ares killed the rapist — and was put on trial for murder at the Areopagus, the first murder trial in Athenian legend. The court acquitted him, and the hill was named 'Areopagus' (Hill of Ares) after the event.

Weaknesses & Wards

Ares's fundamental weakness is the absence of discipline, strategy, and self-control. He is pure impulse — all rage, no plan — which makes him predictably unpredictable and ultimately defeatable by any opponent who possesses intelligence. His defeats at the hands of Athena, the Aloadae, and Heracles demonstrate that brute force, unchanneled by wisdom, is self-defeating. Ares cannot inspire loyalty or build institutions — he can only destroy. He represents war without purpose, violence without cause, strength without direction. The Greeks, who understood war as a necessary evil to be managed through discipline and strategy, saw Ares as the aspect of war that must be contained, not celebrated. His humiliation in the bronze jar — trapped, helpless, forgotten for thirteen months — is the ultimate symbol of his futility: the god of war, imprisoned by his own lack of wit, rendered irrelevant until someone else remembered he existed.

Accounts

During the Trojan War, Ares fights on the side of the Trojans. The Greek hero Diomedes, spurred on by Athena, throws a spear at Ares, and Athena herself drives it into his belly. Ares bellows in agony and flees to Olympus, where he complains to Zeus about Athena's constant interference.

Ares and Aphrodite, the wife of Hephaestus, begin a secret affair. The sun-god Helios sees them and tells Hephaestus. The clever smith-god forges an unbreakable, invisible net and traps the lovers in bed together. He then summons all the gods to witness their shame.

Ares is the father of the dragon that guarded the spring of Ares near Thebes. When Cadmus killed the dragon and sowed its teeth, armed men called the Spartoi ('sown men') sprang from the ground. These Spartoi were the ancestors of the Theban nobility.

The giant twins Otus and Ephialtes, the Aloadae, once captured Ares and imprisoned him in a bronze jar for thirteen months. He would have perished (or been permanently weakened) if not for the intervention of Hermes, who rescued him.

Cultural Significance

Ares was worshipped throughout the Greek world but with notably less enthusiasm than other Olympians. His cults were concentrated in regions considered warlike: Thrace, Sparta, and Crete. In Sparta, Ares was honored as a patron of the city's militaristic culture, and his statue at Geronthrae (in Laconia) was worshipped by women as well as men. At Therapne, near Sparta, Ares was paired with Aphrodite in a temple that acknowledged the union of war and love. In Athens, the Areopagus hill was named after Ares (according to legend, because of the trial for the murder of Halirrhothius), and the court that met there — the most prestigious judicial body in Athens — carried his name, transforming martial violence into civic justice. The temple of Ares in the Athenian Agora, built in the 5th century BCE and relocated in the Roman period, stood as a monument to the god's presence in the heart of Athenian civic life. In Thebes, Ares's dragon-son connected the god to the city's foundation myth through Cadmus and Harmonia. In Thrace and Scythia, Ares was worshipped with distinctive rituals: at his temple on an island in the Black Sea, the Tauri sacrificed shipwrecked sailors to the goddess identified with Artemis (or Iphigenia). In Rome, Mars underwent a complete rehabilitation. As the father of Romulus and Remus, he was the divine ancestor of the Roman people. The Campus Martius was the training ground for soldiers; the Temple of Mars Ultor (Mars the Avenger) in the Forum of Augustus commemorated the vengeance against Caesar's assassins. Mars was a god of agriculture as well as war, reflecting the Roman understanding of conquest as a means to secure farmland. The month of March, named after Mars, opened the military campaign season. In astrology, the planet Mars (Ares's Roman counterpart) is associated with aggression, energy, passion, and conflict — the 'red planet' aptly named for the blood-stained god of war. The adjective 'martial' (from Mars) describes all things military.

Modern Interpretations

Ares appears across modern media, often as a villain or anti-hero. In DC Comics' Wonder Woman, Ares is her primary antagonist — the god of war who seeks to perpetuate eternal conflict, opposed by Diana, who fights for peace. In the 2017 film Wonder Woman, Ares (David Thewlis) is the hidden villain, posing as a British politician while secretly fueling World War I. In Rick Riordan's Percy Jackson series, Ares appears as a bullying, motorcycle-riding thug who embodies toxic masculinity. In God of War (2005), Ares is the primary antagonist — the god who tricked Kratos into killing his family, setting the entire series in motion. Kratos kills Ares at the end of the first game and becomes the new God of War. In Hades (2020), Ares offers boons based on 'Doom' effects (delayed burst damage) and his dialogue reveals a surprisingly philosophical bent — he contemplates the nature of conflict itself. In Age of Mythology (2002), Ares is a minor god for Zeus, granting infantry attack bonuses. In Smite (2014), Ares is a guardian-class god with chain and fire abilities. In the TV series Xena: Warrior Princess, Ares is a recurring anti-hero, simultaneously Xena's patron and antagonist, played with seductive menace by Kevin Smith. In the Marvel Cinematic Universe, Ares has not yet appeared, but in Marvel Comics, he is a member of the Olympians who has served as both Avenger and antagonist.

Content Notes

Ares is the personification of the most brutal and horrific aspects of war. He is not a noble warrior; he is a bloodthirsty killer who delights in slaughter and the destruction of cities. His myths and cults are a reflection of the grim realities of ancient warfare, which involved rape, enslavement, and the wholesale slaughter of civilian populations.

Scholars see Ares as a 'safe' target for expressing the dark side of the Greek military ethos. By making him a hated and often foolish god, the Greeks could acknowledge the horrors of war while simultaneously distancing themselves from them, and from him.

Homer, Iliad; various tragedies

The story of Ares and Aphrodite is fundamentally about adultery and public humiliation. Ares is caught in the act and ridiculed by the entire pantheon. This story serves to shame Ares and to reinforce social norms about marriage and fidelity.

This myth is often interpreted as a comedic tale that reinforces social order. The laughter of the gods at Ares' expense is a form of social control, mocking the deviant figure.

What We Don't Know

Primary sources for a dedicated 'Ares' cult are sparse compared to other Olympians. The full text of the Epic Cycle, which might contain more Ares myths, is lost. The specific iconography of Ares in the Archaic period is difficult to distinguish from generic warrior figures. A deeper dive into the archaeological record of Spartan and Theban cults would be valuable.

A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine you are the toughest guy in a biker gang. You live for the fight — the adrenaline, the impact of fists on flesh, the roar of combat. You don't do strategy meetings or care about 'objectives.' You just want to hit things until they stop moving. Your dad (the big boss) hates you. Your sister (the CEO's favorite) embarrasses you in fights and lectures you about 'tactical discipline.' Your girlfriend is the most beautiful woman in the world, and everyone's jealous. One time, two giant brothers stuffed you into a metal barrel and left you there for over a year — and nobody even NOTICED you were missing. You are Ares: the god of war, perpetually losing, perpetually angry, and absolutely incapable of learning from your mistakes. And yet — when the battle starts and the blood flows — there's no one, no one, who enjoys it more than you.
