Who It Is

Imagine the god of war not as a noble general, but as the war itself: the screaming chaos, the blood-madness, the moment a soldier breaks and runs. That is Ares. He is the god the other gods mock, the father who despises him, and the warrior who gets beaten up by a mortal. He is the ugly, embarrassing, and utterly necessary truth of what it means to fight. He is not the glory of war—he is the bill that comes due.

Homeric Ares is a force of nature: a 'man-slaughtering,' 'shield-piercing,' 'city-sacking' god who delights in the clash of arms and the screams of the dying. He is not strategic; he is pure, unthinking violence. He is also a coward and a whiner. When wounded, he flees to Olympus and complains to Zeus, who tells him he is the most hated of all gods, loving only strife, war, and battles. Powers
Superhuman Strength and Combat Prowess

As a god, Ares possesses immense strength and is a formidable fighter. He is described as 'man-slaughtering' and 'unconquerable' in his element.

Limit: Despite his power, he is not invincible. In the Iliad, he is wounded by a mortal, Diomedes, who is empowered by Athena. This shows that even a god can be harmed by a hero with divine aid.

Immortality and Divine Resilience

Like all Olympians, he is immortal and does not age. He can be wounded, but he cannot die from his wounds.

Limit: His wounds are excruciating and can incapacitate him. His cry of pain when speared by Diomedes is described as being as loud as the war cry of nine or ten thousand men.

Battlefield Presence and Warping

His presence on a battlefield is said to cause panic and terror, and his war cry is a weapon in itself, capable of striking fear into the hearts of entire armies.

Limit: This presence is a double-edged sword. While it can terrify enemies, it can also cause chaos and disorder among his own allies. He is a god of chaos, not of order.

Shapeshifting (Limited)

In some myths, he is said to be able to change his appearance, often taking the form of a mortal warrior to fight alongside or against heroes.

Limit: This is not a common or well-defined power in the primary sources. It is more of a general divine ability.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In early Greek art, Ares is depicted as a mature, bearded warrior in full hoplite armor — helmet, shield, spear. He is indistinguishable from any other Greek warrior except for his divine context. In later, Classical and Hellenistic art (and Roman copies), he becomes a youthful, beardless, athletic figure, often nude or semi-nude, with a more contemplative or even melancholic expression. His sacred animals include the boar and the serpent. The torch is also a symbol, representing the burning of cities. Domains — His domain is the battlefield itself—the dust, the noise, the blood, the panic. He is not the god of strategy (that's Athena) or of the soldier's craft. He is the god of the actual, ugly, terrifying moment of combat. He dwells on Mount Olympus, but his true home is wherever men are killing each other. He has a special affinity for Thrace, a region the Greeks considered wild and barbaric, and is said to have a palace there.

Accounts I
The Wounding of Ares (Iliad, Book 5)Homer, Iliad

c. 8th century BCE

During the Trojan War, Ares fights on the side of the Trojans. The Greek hero Diomedes, spurred on by Athena, throws a spear at Ares, and Athena herself drives it into his belly. Ares bellows in agony and flees to Olympus, where he complains to Zeus about Athena's constant interference. Zeus berates Ares, calling him the most hateful of all gods. This scene establishes Ares' role as a foil to Athena and his lack of respect among the gods. It also shows that even a god can be humiliated on the battlefield.

The Love Affair of Ares and Aphrodite (Odyssey, Book 8)Homer, Odyssey

c. 8th century BCE

Ares and Aphrodite, the wife of Hephaestus, begin a secret affair. The sun-god Helios sees them and tells Hephaestus. The clever smith-god forges an unbreakable, invisible net and traps the lovers in bed together. He then summons all the gods to witness their shame. The gods laugh at the pair, and Ares is forced to pay a fine for his adultery before being released. This story is the most famous myth about Ares and highlights his role as a foolish, lustful figure who is easily outwitted. It also establishes the love triangle that is central to many later stories.

Accounts II
The Founding of Thebes and the SpartoiVarious, including Pausanias and the Epic Cycle (fragments)

Archaic/Classical

Ares is the father of the dragon that guarded the spring of Ares near Thebes. When Cadmus killed the dragon and sowed its teeth, armed men called the Spartoi ('sown men') sprang from the ground. These Spartoi were the ancestors of the Theban nobility. This myth establishes Ares as a progenitor of the Theban people. Cadmus later married Harmonia, the daughter of Ares and Aphrodite, cementing the connection. This gives Ares a role as a founder-figure, which is unusual for him.

The Imprisonment of the AloadaeHomer, Iliad, Book 5 (mentioned); Apollodorus, Library

c. 8th century BCE / 2nd century CE

The giant twins Otus and Ephialtes, the Aloadae, once captured Ares and imprisoned him in a bronze jar for thirteen months. He would have perished (or been permanently weakened) if not for the intervention of Hermes, who rescued him. This story is a profound humiliation for Ares. It shows that even a god of war can be overpowered by mortals with divine blood. It also underscores his lack of cunning; he was trapped by a simple trick.

Ares was a popular, well-loved god. — Ares was one of the least popular and most despised of the Olympians. His own father, Zeus, says he hates him more than any other god. He had few major temples and was rarely depicted in art compared to other gods.

Ares was a great warrior and a match for Athena. — In Greek myth, Ares is repeatedly defeated and humiliated. He is wounded by a mortal, trapped in a net, and imprisoned by giants. Athena, the goddess of strategic warfare, consistently outwits and defeats him.

Ares and Mars are exactly the same. — While the Romans identified Mars with Ares, the two gods were very different in character and importance. Mars was a central, revered god of the Roman state, a father-figure and protector of agriculture, while Ares was a marginal, often despised figure in Greece.
