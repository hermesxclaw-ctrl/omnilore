Who It Is

Before she was the weeping, brilliant Lin Daiyu of the Jia household, she was a wordless herb on the bank of a celestial river — the Crimson Pearl Flower, Jiangzhucao — who could do only one thing: receive. For years a celestial servant watered her with dew, and when she finally gained a spirit, she had no gold or power to repay him, only a vow: if he must go down to the dusty world of men, she would go too, and pay him back in a lifetime of tears.

As a plant, Jiangzhucao is sentient but passive - it receives, grows, and develops consciousness through gratitude. Its sole volitional act in celestial form is the vow to repay irrigation with a lifetime of tears (以泪为偿). This defines its personality as sensitive, indebted, and melancholic. When incarnated as Lin Daiyu, these traits manifest as: acute sensitivity, poetic talent, pride masking vulnerability, weeping as emotional and spiritual practice, and a fatalistic awareness of impermanence. Powers
Sentience and Spiritual Transformation

As an immortal herb by a numinous river, Jiangzhucao develops consciousness through prolonged exposure to celestial dew and proximity to the Three Lives Stone. It can shed its vegetal body (草胎木质) and assume human (female immortal) form. This is not self-powered cultivation but grace-dependent metamorphosis.

Limit: Transformation requires external nurture (Shenying's dew); cannot self-cultivate. After transformation, she remains bound by karmic debt and must reincarnate to repay.

Karmic Memory and Reincarnation

Retains memory of debt across lives. The vow to repay with tears creates a karmic contract (木石前盟) that determines rebirth as Lin Daiyu and binds her fate to the Shenying Servant's earthly form, Jia Baoyu. Associated with the Three Lives Stone's ability to reveal past and future lives.

Limit: Memory is veiled after mortal birth; Daiyu does not consciously remember being Jiangzhucao, only experiences déjà vu and inexplicable affinity/sorrow. Cannot control rebirth timing or avoid debt.

Tears as Repayment (Metaphysical Efficacy)

Her tears are not ordinary weeping but efficacious repayment that gradually extinguishes the debt of irrigation. The novel quantifies this as 'all the tears of a lifetime' (一生所有眼泪). Each tear is spiritually potent; when tears are exhausted, life ends.

Limit: Tears must be shed for the Shenying/Baoyu specifically - grief for him, not generic sorrow, counts. Cannot be repaid by other means (service, poetry, loyalty). Once tears run dry, death is inevitable, even if she wishes to live.

Poetic and Prophetic Sensitivity (as Lin Daiyu)

After incarnation, retains celestial sensitivity manifesting as extraordinary poetic talent, acute aesthetic perception, and presentiment of impermanence. Composes funeral flower rites (葬花吟) and laments that echo her plant origin. In celestial form, implied ability to perceive karmic registers.

Limit: Talent does not confer power to alter fate; sensitivity increases suffering. Physically frail, prone to consumption (痨病), which limits agency.

Accounts I
The Herb by the Western Spirit RiverCao Xueqin, Hong Lou Meng, Chapter 1 (Gengchen manuscript and Cheng-Gao 1791)

c. 1750s-1791 (Qing Qianlong era)

On the bank of the Western Spirit River, beside the Three Lives Stone which records past and future lives, there grows a solitary crimson pearl herb (绛珠草). The herb is nourished by the spiritual environment of the Land of Illusion but remains a wordless plant until the Divine Luminescent Attendant (神瑛侍者), a stone's attendant in the celestial court, takes pity and waters it daily with sweet dew (甘露). Over long years the herb absorbs this grace, gains essence, and sheds its vegetal womb to become a female immortal. This is the origin myth that frames the entire novel, told to Zhen Shiyin by the Buddhist monk and Daoist priest as he dreams. Establishes the karmic premise (Wood-Stone Alliance, 木石前盟) that will drive the earthly tragedy. The herb's transformation creates a debt that can only be repaid in the mortal world. Without this story, Lin Daiyu's weeping and early death have no metaphysical cause.

The Vow to Repay with TearsCao Xueqin, Hong Lou Meng, Chapter 1, continuation of prologue

1750s

After attaining human form, the Crimson Pearl Immortal Herb reflects that she has no sweet dew, no precious offering to repay the Shenying Servant's years of nurture. She resolves that if he descends to the mortal world to experience life, she will follow him down and repay him with all the tears she can shed in a lifetime. The Fairy Disenchantment and the Buddhist monk acknowledge this vow and arrange the descent: the Stone (which the Servant attends) will enter the world as Jia Baoyu, and the Herb will enter as Lin Daiyu. The vow is formalized as a karmic contract, recorded in the celestial registers. Creates the novel's central fatalism: Daiyu must weep for Baoyu, and her death when tears are exhausted is predestined. The vow explains why Daiyu cries at nearly every meeting with Baoyu and why their love cannot be fulfilled - the debt must be paid, not consummated in marriage.

Entry into the Mortal World as Lin DaiyuCao Xueqin, Hong Lou Meng, Chapters 2-3 (Lin Daiyu enters the Jia household)

1750s, earthly narrative set in fictionalized Qing capital

In fulfillment of the celestial vow, Jiangzhucao is born as Lin Daiyu, daughter of salt commissioner Lin Ruhai and Jia Min (daughter of the Jia family). Orphaned young - mother dies, father dies - she is taken into her maternal grandmother's house, the Rongguo Mansion. There she meets Jia Baoyu, the earthly form of the Shenying Servant, who carries the通灵宝玉 (Numinous Jade) that is the Stone's incarnation. At first sight both feel they have met before; Baoyu famously says 'This sister I have seen before.' Daiyu's frail health, weeping, and poetic brilliance immediately manifest the herb's nature in human form. She takes residence in Xiaoxiang Lodge, a bamboo grove that mirrors her riverside origin. Transposes celestial debt into domestic tragedy. Daiyu's position as an orphaned, dependent cousin makes her tears socially as well as karmically determined - she has no secure status except through Baoyu. Her bamboo lodge becomes the earthly Western Spirit River bank.

Accounts II
The Flower Burial and the Lament (Daiyu Buries Flowers)Cao Xueqin, Hong Lou Meng, Chapter 23 and Chapter 27-28 (Daiyu's 'Flower Burial' and 'Burial of Flowers Poem' 葬花吟)

1750s (within novel's internal calendar, spring)

Seeing fallen petals in the garden, Daiyu - identifying with the flowers as fellow ephemeral beings - gathers them in a silk pouch and buries them with ritual, weeping as she does so. She composes the long poem 'Burial of Flowers' (葬花吟), which laments that beauty must fade, that fallen flowers and the poet herself will be unmourned. Baoyu overhears and is moved to tears, recognizing in her lament their shared mortality. The scene is the most explicit earthly echo of Jiangzhucao's plant consciousness: Daiyu mourns flowers as kin, as if mourning herself. Establishes Daiyu's identification with vegetal ephemerality - she is both mourner and mourned flower. The poem becomes the novel's most quoted lyric and cements the Jiangzhucao imagery in cultural memory. Foreshadows her own unmourned death and the later 'tears exhausted' fulfillment.

Tears Exhausted - Death and ReturnCao Xueqin/Gao E, Hong Lou Meng, Chapters 96-98 (Daiyu's death); foreshadowed in Chapter 5's registers

1791 Cheng-Gao ending; Cao's intended ending debated but tear-exhaustion consistent

As the Jia family arranges Baoyu's marriage to Xue Baochai (the Gold-Jade alliance, 金玉良缘) to secure family fortunes, Daiyu, already consumptive, learns of the betrayal. She burns her poems, severs worldly ties, and weeps until, in the Cheng-Gao version, she dies as Baoyu is being married, her final tears shed for him. The celestial logic is explicit: her lifetime's tears are now exhausted, the debt of dew repaid, and the Crimson Pearl Herb's vow fulfilled. In some readings, her soul returns to the Land of Illusion, resuming herb/immortal form beside the river, free of debt. Baoyu later becomes a monk, remembering her. Closes the karmic loop opened in Chapter 1. The Wood-Stone Alliance ends not in union but in repayment and separation, explaining why the novel's central love must fail. Culturally, this death scene became the archetype of the tragic talented woman (才女薄命) in Chinese literature.

The Registers of the Land of Illusion - Foretelling Jiangzhu's FateCao Xueqin, Hong Lou Meng, Chapter 5: Baoyu's dream in the Land of Illusion (太虚幻境)

1750s

Baoyu, guided by the Fairy Disenchantment, enters her palace and is shown the registers that record the fates of the Jinling girls. He sees the register for Lin Daiyu, paired with Xue Baochai, with a poem and image indicating 'a crimson pearl's tears' and 'a mountain of gold and jade.' The Fairy shows him the song 'The Wood-Stone Alliance' and warns him of the illusory nature of passion. The register explicitly links Daiyu to Jiangzhucao's tear debt and predicts that Baoyu's affinity with Daiyu is karmic but doomed, while his marriage to Baochai is worldly and cold. Baoyu does not understand and forgets upon waking. Provides internal prophecy that Jiangzhucao's story must end in tears, not marriage. Anchors the celestial myth in the novel's dream structure and gives readers (but not Baoyu) foreknowledge. Source for countless later adaptations' use of 'Wood-Stone vs Gold-Jade' dichotomy.

Jiangzhucao is an ancient Chinese goddess or folk deity worshipped before Dream of the Red Chamber. — Jiangzhucao has no temple, cult, or pre-Cao textual existence. She is a literary creation of the 18th century for the novel's mythic frame. Unlike Chang'e or Nüwa, she has no folk religion or ritual tradition.

Jiangzhucao's tears are just romantic sadness; she cries because she is overly sensitive. — In the novel's logic tears are karmic currency - a formal repayment of a specific debt of dew. She must weep for Baoyu specifically, and death occurs when the quota is paid. Sensitivity is the human expression of a metaphysical contract, not just personality.

Jiangzhucao and Jia Baoyu's love is the 'Wood-Stone Alliance' and it is fulfilled in marriage in some happy-ending versions. — The Wood-Stone Alliance by definition cannot be fulfilled in marriage within the novel's cosmology; its fulfillment is repayment through tears and separation. The marriage that occurs is the Gold-Jade Alliance (Baoyu-Baochai). No canonical manuscript has a happy Wood-Stone marriage; such endings are modern fanfiction or bowdlerized opera endings.

Jiangzhucao appears as a separate flower fairy fighting alongside Lin Daiyu in the original novel. — They never appear together; Jiangzhucao is Daiyu's prior incarnation, not a companion. Scenes showing both together are entirely modern adaptation inventions for visual appeal.
