Who It Is

In the bustling Roman city of Perge, where the Taurus Mountains meet the Pamphylian plain, stood a goddess who looked nothing like the fleet-footed huntress of Greek myth. She was a veiled, column-like 'Queen', a silent, powerful block of stone draped in cloth, crowned with a basket, her arms outstretched in eternal blessing. This was Artemis Pergaia, a deity who was both the soul of her city and a stubborn survivor of a much older, wilder world. While her famous cousin at Ephesus was festooned with what looked like a necklace of eggs, this Lady of Perge was austere, mysterious, and utterly unlike any Olympian. She was the divine embodiment of the city itself—a tyrant-protector, a mistress of deer, and a living link to the Anatolian mother-goddesses who ruled these lands long before the Greeks ever arrived. As a goddess, her behavior is inferred from cult practice. She is a protective, civic deity, the 'Queen of Perge' (Vanassa Preiia). She is a bestower of sovereignty and prosperity, protecting the city and its citizens. She is also a mistress of the natural world, particularly of wild animals. Her festivals involved processions, sacrifices (likely including deer), games, and musical contests.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Artemis' is of uncertain, likely pre-Greek origin. The epithet 'Pergaia' comes from the city of Perge in Pamphylia (modern-day Antalya Province, Turkey), where she was the principal deity. Her local title 'Vanassa Preiia' is Luwian/Anatolian, meaning 'Queen of Perge'.

Pronunciation — Ar-TEH-mis of PER-gee (Pergaia: per-GAY-ah)

Aliases — Artemis Pergaia, Mistress of the Beasts, Potnia Theron, The Pergaean Lady, Vanassa Preiia

Ownership — Greco-Anatolian hybrid deity, primarily worshipped in Pamphylia (southern Anatolia, modern Turkey). She represents a syncretism of Greek Artemis with local Anatolian mother-goddess

Powers
Civic Protection

As the principal deity of Perge, she was believed to guard the city, its walls, and its citizens. She was the divine patroness of the polis, and her temple was the religious heart of the community.

Limit: Her protective power was generally considered to be geographically limited to Perge and its immediate territory, although her fame spread further.

Mistress of Animals

Like many Anatolian and Near Eastern goddesses (and Artemis in her wider Greek role), she was seen as the mistress of wild beasts, particularly deer. This is shown on coins where she is accompanied by a deer, and in the likely practice of deer sacrifice at her festival.

Limit: This role seems less emphasized than in the Greek Artemis's role as a hunter; here she is more a protector/controller of animals than a huntress.

Sovereignty and Prosperity

Her title 'Vanassa Preiia' (Queen of Perge) suggests a role in legitimizing local rulers and ensuring the prosperity of the city and its agricultural territory.

Limit: This is a reconstructed aspect, inferred from her title and the nature of local rulers.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The cult statue of Artemis of Perge, as depicted on coins and in some sculptural fragments, is strikingly different from the typical Greek Artemis (the young, short-chitoned hunter). She is shown as a hieratic, frontal, columnar figure, heavily veiled, with a kalathos (basket-shaped crown) on her head. She holds her arms out from her sides, often with the hands open. Accounts I
The Foundation of the Cult at PergeReconstructed from archaeological and epigraphic evidence

Possibly as early as the Late Bronze Age, but certainly by the Archaic period (8th-6th c. BCE)

The exact foundation myth is lost, but the archaeological record shows a continuity of cult practice at the site from a very early period. The sanctuary was built on a raised terrace, and the cult seems to have been a local, pre-Greek or early-Greek hybrid. The goddess was likely a local 'Mistress of Animals' and city-protector who was later identified with the Greek Artemis as Greek culture became dominant in Pamphylia. The creation of a powerful, wealthy, and famous sanctuary that was the center of Pamphylian religious life for over a millennium.

Accounts II
The Festival of the PergaiaInscriptions and general Greek festival practice

Hellenistic and Roman Imperial periods

An annual festival was held in honor of Artemis Pergaia. It likely involved a grand procession to the temple, the sacrifice of animals (possibly deer, as well as the usual cattle, sheep, and goats), athletic games, and musical contests. The festival would have been a major civic event, drawing attendees from across Pamphylia and beyond. It was an opportunity for the city to display its wealth and piety, and for citizens to reaffirm their identity as Pergaeans. Reinforced civic unity and the central role of the goddess in the city's identity. Provided economic and social opportunities.

The Christian TransformationArchaeological evidence of the temple's later phases

5th-6th century CE

With the Christianization of the Roman Empire, the temple of Artemis Pergaia was eventually abandoned and then repurposed. The cella was converted into a Christian basilica, with the cult statue removed and destroyed or defaced. This dramatic transformation marks the end of the ancient cult and the victory of Christianity in the city. The stones of the pagan temple were reused for the new Christian building, a powerful symbol of religious supersession. The end of the cult of Artemis Pergaia. The physical remains of her temple were incorporated into a new religious landscape.

Artemis of Perge is the same as Artemis of Ephesus. — They are distinct local cults of goddesses identified with Greek Artemis. They share some regional characteristics, but have different iconography, different cities, and different histories.

The cult statue of Artemis of Perge was 'many-breasted' like the one at Ephesus. — The Pergaean statue is not depicted with multiple breasts. It is veiled and columnar, with a kalathos crown, but the 'many-breasted' feature is specific to the Ephesian cult.

The goddess was a purely Greek import. — The cult has deep local Anatolian roots, evidenced by the non-Greek form of the cult statue, the Luwian title, and the archaeological continuity. The Greek name and identity were a later, albeit important, layer.
