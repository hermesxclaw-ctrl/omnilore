Form

The cult statue of Artemis of Perge, as depicted on coins and in some sculptural fragments, is strikingly different from the typical Greek Artemis (the young, short-chitoned hunter). She is shown as a hieratic, frontal, columnar figure, heavily veiled, with a kalathos (basket-shaped crown) on her head. She holds her arms out from her sides, often with the hands open. The body is often shown as a solid, unarticulated mass, suggesting a very ancient, aniconic or semi-aniconic origin. Unlike the Ephesians Artemis, she is not depicted with multiple breasts, but the veiled, columnar form is similar.

Powers

Civic Protection As the principal deity of Perge, she was believed to guard the city, its walls, and its citizens. She was the divine patroness of the polis, and her temple was the religious heart of the community. Attested through inscriptions, coinage, and the central location of her sanctuary within the city. Her protective power was generally considered to be geographically limited to Perge and its immediate territory, although her fame spread further. None recorded.

Mistress of Animals Like many Anatolian and Near Eastern goddesses (and Artemis in her wider Greek role), she was seen as the mistress of wild beasts, particularly deer. This is shown on coins where she is accompanied by a deer, and in the likely practice of deer sacrifice at her festival. Iconographic and archaeological evidence. This role seems less emphasized than in the Greek Artemis's role as a hunter; here she is more a protector/controller of animals than a huntress. Some scholars see this as a purely Greek influence, while others see it as a continuation of a pre-Greek 'Potnia Theron' tradition.

Sovereignty and Prosperity Her title 'Vanassa Preiia' (Queen of Perge) suggests a role in legitimizing local rulers and ensuring the prosperity of the city and its agricultural territory. Luwian title and general Anatolian religious concepts. This is a reconstructed aspect, inferred from her title and the nature of local rulers. The exact nature of her relationship with the city's magistrates and any royal dynasty is not fully clear.

Behavior & Habitat

As a goddess, her behavior is inferred from cult practice. She is a protective, civic deity, the 'Queen of Perge' (Vanassa Preiia). She is a bestower of sovereignty and prosperity, protecting the city and its citizens. She is also a mistress of the natural world, particularly of wild animals. Her festivals involved processions, sacrifices (likely including deer), games, and musical contests.

Accounts

The exact foundation myth is lost, but the archaeological record shows a continuity of cult practice at the site from a very early period. The sanctuary was built on a raised terrace, and the cult seems to have been a local, pre-Greek or early-Greek hybrid. The goddess was likely a local 'Mistress of Animals' and city-protector who was later identified with the Greek Artemis as Greek culture became dominant in Pamphylia.

An annual festival was held in honor of Artemis Pergaia. It likely involved a grand procession to the temple, the sacrifice of animals (possibly deer, as well as the usual cattle, sheep, and goats), athletic games, and musical contests. The festival would have been a major civic event, drawing attendees from across Pamphylia and beyond. It was an opportunity for the city to display its wealth and piety, and for citizens to reaffirm their identity as Pergaeans.

With the Christianization of the Roman Empire, the temple of Artemis Pergaia was eventually abandoned and then repurposed. The cella was converted into a Christian basilica, with the cult statue removed and destroyed or defaced. This dramatic transformation marks the end of the ancient cult and the victory of Christianity in the city. The stones of the pagan temple were reused for the new Christian building, a powerful symbol of religious supersession.

What We Don't Know

The primary gap is the lack of a surviving foundation myth or detailed literary description of the cult's rituals. The nature of the pre-Greek, Luwian goddess is almost entirely reconstructed from iconography and titles. The specific details of the festival of the Pergaia are unknown. The exact process of the temple's conversion to a church is not documented. The full corpus of inscriptions from Perge has not been comprehensively published in a modern, accessible edition.

A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine a small town's central square, dominated by a towering, abstract statue of a veiled woman that has been there since before anyone can remember. It's not a realistic portrait, but a powerful, mysterious symbol of the town itself. The locals know she's 'the Lady', the one who watches over them. When a new, sophisticated culture arrives and talks about their own goddess of the hunt, the townspeople nod and say, 'Oh yes, that's our Lady too—we just call her by your name now.' They keep their own strange, ancient statue, but they give it a new, familiar name. That's exactly what happened at Perge. The local 'Queen' was so powerful and so important that the Greeks simply added her to their own pantheon, calling her Artemis, even though she was something much older and more local. She's a perfect example of how ancient religion was less about belief and more about place, power, and identity.
