Who It Is

Beneath the packed earth of a Jin-dynasty courtyard, something barks where no dog should be. You hear it before you see the crack — a muffled, distant yelping rising through solid ground — and when the soil finally splits, there are puppies in the dark, eyes still sealed, too large for any ordinary litter. In early Chinese anomaly literature they have a name: Dilang, the Earth Wolf, also called Xiquan, the Hole Dog. They are not pets, not monsters to be slain, but omens that surface unbidden and then vanish, leaving death or disaster in their wake.

Attested behavior is minimal and consistent: (1) Auditory precursor — distant barking/yelping heard through earth before discovery (遠聞犬聲, 聞地中有犬聲). (2) Subterranean nesting in burrows described as 'like a serpent's retreat' (如蛇穴) or simply under floors/beds and residences. (3) Maternal care — at least one case (He Xu) observes a mother present who escapes when exposed. Powers
Subterranean existence

Ability to live, gestate, and nurse young entirely underground in sealed burrows without apparent surface access. Puppies are born and survive for at least days beneath packed earth/floorboards.

Limit: Not unlimited — burrows are shallow enough that sounds penetrate to surface and that humans can dig to them; mother must presumably surface to hunt (implied by thin condition). No evidence of tunneling at will through rock or magical earth-phasing; described as inhabiting existing holes.

Omen / portent function (yao)

Presence is interpreted as a prodigy foretelling human disaster — specifically violent death of the finder or destruction of his community. The creature itself does not cause the disaster but its appearance correlates with it in the historiographic pattern.

Limit: Correlation is post hoc historiographic; Huai Yao himself does not die in the account, and Xie's Changzhou case has no recorded disaster. The omen is not deterministic or controllable.

Auditory manifestation through earth

Barking, yelping, or puppy cries audible at a distance through solid ground, often for multiple nights before discovery. Described as 'distant sounds of dogs' (遠聞犬聲) and 'sounds of dogs beneath his bed' (聞犬聲在床下).

Limit: Sound is that of normal puppies, not supernatural howling; no claim of ventriloquism or human speech. In Changzhou case, sound was heard but no source ever found, suggesting possible misidentification.

Exceptional growth and predatory prowess (single case)

He Xu's surviving male puppy grew to be unusually effective at hunting or devouring beasts (善噬獸), implying above-average size, ferocity, or hunting ability compared to domestic dogs.

Limit: Only one of four puppies in that litter survived; female died. No details on what beasts it devoured or whether it remained tame. No supernatural strength or invulnerability claimed.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Attested: Described exclusively as canids (犬) found underground. Puppies (犬子) are the only life stage directly observed; all accounts describe them as newborns with eyes not yet opened (目未開). Size is consistently larger than normal domestic puppies (皆大於常犬). Symbols — No verified visual record exists. No Han or Jin tomb murals, stone reliefs, or Ming illustrations securely identified as Dilang. The creature functions symbolically rather than iconographically: as a wuxing (Five Phases) omen of earth-related anomaly — something that should live above ground appearing below ground, violating proper spatial order. Accounts I
Huai Yao and the Burrow in Lou CountyGan Bao, Soushen Ji, juan 18

Narrative set during Yuankang era (291-299 CE) of Emperor Hui of Jin; text compiled c. 350 CE

In Lou County, Wu Commandery, a man named Huai Yao (懷瑤) heard distant dog sounds and discovered a burrow hole 'the size of a serpent's retreat' (如蛇穴). Digging, he found two Xiquan puppies, one male and one female, eyes not yet opened, both larger than ordinary puppies. He returned them to the den and covered it with an old grindstone. The next day he could not find the spot again. No death or disaster is recorded for Huai Yao himself, unlike later cases. The vanishing of the den after being marked is presented as proof of the anomaly's supernatural elusiveness. The story establishes the template: auditory discovery, oversized newborns, failed relocation.

Zhang Mao and the Dogs Beneath the BedGan Bao, Soushen Ji, juan 18; also paraphrased in Jin Shu Wuxing Zhi

Set during Taixing era (318-321 CE) of Emperor Yuan of Jin; protagonist died 322 CE

Zhang Mao (張茂), Grand Protector (太守) of Wu Commandery, heard dogs barking beneath his bed but could find nothing. Eventually the earth cracked open and revealed two puppies. He attempted to raise them, but both died. In the following year (322), Zhang was killed during the rebellion of Wang Dun (王敦). Explicit omen fulfillment: the anomaly is retrospectively interpreted as foretelling Zhang's violent death in a major rebellion that nearly toppled the Eastern Jin. The failure to rear the puppies underscores that such prodigies cannot be domesticated.

Accounts II
Sun Wuzhong and the White Puppies of JiyangFang Xuanling et al., Jin Shu, juan 27, Wuxing Zhi

Set under Emperor Xiaowu of Jin (r. 373-396), event culminating in 402 CE

General Sun Wuzhong (孫無終), serving under Emperor Xiaowu, found two white puppies (白犬二), one male and one female, beneath his residence in Jiyang County (既陽縣). He tried but failed to rear them. Soon after, in 402, he was killed by the rebel Huan Xuan (桓玄) during Huan's usurpation. Again, the discovery is framed as a wuxing portent of the finder's death in civil war. The detail of white color is unique to this case and may carry symbolic weight (white associated with mourning, metal phase, or western direction in wuxing thought). The failure to rear mirrors Zhang Mao's case.

He Xu and the Blue Mother of Qian CountyFang Xuanling et al., Jin Shu, juan 27, Wuxing Zhi

434 CE (Liu Song period, but recorded in Jin Shu treatise as continuation of Jin omenology)

In Qian County (灊縣), a man named He Xu (何旭) found two puppies underground. Their mother was present — described as thin/emaciated (羸) and qing (青, blue/green/grey) — and escaped when the nest was exposed. He Xu tried to rear the puppies; the female died but the male survived and grew to be 'good at devouring beasts' (善噬獸). Later, He Xu's village was destroyed by 'barbarians' (蠻). Most detailed outcome: partial success in rearing, with the surviving male showing exceptional predatory ability, but still culminating in communal disaster rather than personal death. Provides the only description of an adult Dilang and the only case of survival to adulthood.

The Changzhou Sounds That Could Not Be FoundXie Zhaozhe, Wuzazu, juan 9

Ming dynasty, described as 'recent' (近事) relative to 1616 publication; likely late 16th century

Xie Zhaozhe records a recent case from Changzhou (長洲, in modern Suzhou area): for several nights, puppy sounds were heard coming from underground. Much digging was undertaken, but nothing was discovered. No disaster is recorded; the story ends in failure to verify. Xie includes it to show the persistence of the belief but also to demonstrate its elusiveness, contrasting with the Jin cases where puppies were actually found. It marks a shift from omen narrative to anecdotal curiosity.

Dilang is a Chinese werewolf or shapeshifting wolf spirit. — Primary texts never describe transformation, human form, or lycanthropy. Dilang is consistently a dog/wolf-like animal that lives underground and is found as puppies; it does not turn into or possess humans. Werewolf conflation comes from literal translation 'Earth Wolf' and modern fantasy tropes.

Dilang is the same as the Tibetan 'earth dog' or Himalayan marmot folklore. — No primary source links Dilang to Tibet or the Himalayas; all attested finds are in the lower Yangtze Wu region (Jiangsu/Zhejiang/Anhui). The Tibetan 'earth dog' is a different folklore complex, and marmots are not canids.

Dilang puppies have magical powers, can control earth, or are used as familiars. — No attested power beyond living underground and serving as omen. Only one puppy (He Xu's male) is described as 'good at devouring beasts,' which is a natural hunting trait, not magic. No text grants earth-control, speech, or familiar status.

The Dilang is extensively illustrated in ancient Chinese art and Shan Hai Jing. — Dilang does not appear in Shan Hai Jing (Classic of Mountains and Seas) at all; its earliest source is Shizi, and no verified pre-modern illustration survives. Any 'ancient' images online are modern reconstructions.
