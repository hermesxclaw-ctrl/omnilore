Who It Is

Sköll and Hati are two monstrous wolves who ceaselessly chase the sun and moon across the sky in Norse mythology. Sköll pursues the sun (Sól), while Hati ('Hate') runs after the moon (Máni). According to the Grímnismál, the wolves will one day catch their prey at Ragnarök: Sköll will swallow the sun, and Hati will devour the moon, plunging the world into darkness. They are the children of Fenrir (or, in some accounts, of a giantess named Ironwood, who raised an entire pack of wolves to pursue the celestial bodies). Sköll and Hati explain the daily and monthly movement of the sun and moon: the heavenly bodies are fleeing, and the wolves are always gaining — the approach of the wolf to its prey causes the seasons (the sun moving low in winter when the wolf is closer), and the occasional eclipse is the wolf nearly catching the sun or moon. This chase is not random but part of the inescapable machinery of fate: the wolves were created specifically to hunt the sun and moon, and their eventual success is foreordained. Unlike Fenrir (who is a singular, character-driven figure), Sköll and Hati are more purely elemental — they are the hunger of darkness for light, the force of entropy that drives toward the end of all things. They represent the Norse sense that the cosmos is a race against time, and that the wolves are always, always gaining.

Attested in Poetic Edda (Grímnismál, Vafþrúðnismál); Prose Edda (Gylfaginning)Era Viking Age (c. 800–1100 CE); recorded 13th century

Form

Sköll and Hati are described as monstrous wolves of enormous size, though no specific physical details (color, eye color, fur texture) are given in the sources. They are cosmic in scale, large enough to chase the sun and moon across the sky. In later artistic depictions, they are often shown as black or grey wolves with glowing eyes, their jaws open wide, ready to devour their prey.

Powers

Eternal Pursuit They can chase the sun and moon across the sky without tiring, maintaining their speed for all of time until Ragnarök. This is not a magical ability but a fundamental aspect of their nature. Poetic Edda, Prose Edda They cannot catch their prey until Ragnarök; their failure to do so is part of the cosmic order. None

Behavior & Habitat

They are relentless, eternal pursuers. Sköll chases the sun (Sol) during the day, and Hati chases the moon (Máni) during the night. They are not merely following—they are hunting, driven by an insatiable hunger. Their pursuit explains the apparent motion of the celestial bodies across the sky. At Ragnarök, they will finally catch their prey, causing the sun and moon to be swallowed, plunging the world into darkness.

Accounts

The gods placed the sun (Sol) and moon (Máni) in chariots to ride across the sky, marking the passage of time. But two wolves, Sköll and Hati, were set loose (or were born) to chase them. Sköll pursues the sun, Hati the moon. Their eternal chase explains why the sun and moon move across the sky—they are fleeing their predators. The wolves will never catch them until Ragnarök, when they will finally devour the celestial bodies, and the world will be plunged into darkness.

In Völuspá, a seeress describes a giantess (often interpreted as a witch) living in Járnviðr (Ironwood), who raises the kin of Fenrir. Among these are the wolves that will steal the sun and moon. The passage is brief but establishes the wolves' lineage and their role in the coming apocalypse.

What We Don't Know

There are no surviving depictions of Sköll and Hati from the Viking Age, so their physical appearance is a matter of conjecture. The exact nature of their mother is unclear. The relationship between Sköll and Hati and other wolves (like Geri and Freki, Odin's wolves) is not explored in the sources. The myth of the wolves chasing the sun and moon is not as well-documented as other Norse myths, and there may be lost oral traditions that provided more detail.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Sköll and Hati as the original 'ticking clock' of Norse mythology. They are the reason time exists—the sun and moon move because they are being chased. They are also the embodiment of 'doom'—no matter how fast the sun and moon run, the wolves will eventually win. It's a reminder that all things must end, and that even the brightest lights can be extinguished. But it's not a sad story; it's a story about the cycle of life, death, and rebirth, and the inevitability of change.
