Form

In Greco-Roman art, Aion is typically depicted as a handsome, youthful man (or occasionally a mature bearded figure) standing within a celestial circle or zodiac ring, symbolizing the eternal cycle of the heavens. He is often shown with a lion's head (especially in Mithraic contexts), holding a key (to the gates of heaven), a thunderbolt (power over the cosmos), and a staff or scepter. A serpent is frequently coiled around his body, eating its own tail (the ouroboros), symbolizing the self-renewing, eternal cycle of time. His head may be shown with the sun's rays or a globe, and he is sometimes depicted with wings.

Powers

Eternal Time Manipulation Aion embodies boundless, cyclical, and eternal time — the very framework of existence. He is not subject to time but is its source and master. He governs the great ages (aeons) of the cosmos, their creation, flourishing, and destruction. Orphic, Neoplatonic, and Mithraic traditions As a personification, his 'powers' are conceptual rather than narrative; he is the principle of time itself, not a deity who 'uses' it. Some sources (e.g., Nonnus) describe him as a distinct, almost anthropomorphic god, while others (e.g., Damascius) treat him as a purely abstract metaphysical principle.

Cosmic Sovereignty Aion is the ruler of the universe and the heavens. He holds the keys to the gates of heaven and the celestial spheres, controlling the passage of souls and the movement of the stars. Mithraic mysteries; Orphic hymns His sovereignty is often depicted as abstract and impersonal, without the narrative agency of a Zeus or a Jupiter. The extent of his power relative to other primordial deities (e.g., Chaos, Phanes) varies significantly across different cults and philosophical systems.

Cycle of Renewal Aion embodies the cyclical nature of time — the eternal return of the same ages. He is the power that allows the cosmos to be destroyed and reborn in an endless cycle. Stoic and Orphic cosmology; Mithraic iconography This is a cosmological principle rather than a discrete 'power' used in a narrative. The Stoic concept of 'ekpyrosis' (world-conflagration) and rebirth is sometimes associated with Aion's cycle, but the precise relationship is debated.

Behavior & Habitat

As a primordial, abstract deity, Aion does not engage in the anthropomorphic adventures of the Olympian gods. He is a personification of a cosmic principle — eternal, boundless, cyclical time. He is depicted as a serene, all-powerful, and inscrutable force that governs the turning of the ages, the cycles of birth and destruction, and the endless renewal of the cosmos.

Accounts

In the Orphic cosmogony, Aion (or Chronos in some versions) is the primordial, self-created deity who emerges from the cosmic egg. He is the first principle, the 'father of all things', who generates the aether, chaos, and the silver egg from which the god Phanes (the creator) is born. Aion is thus the ultimate ancestor of the gods and the cosmos itself.

Aion is widely identified with the enigmatic lion-headed god (Leontocephaline) found in Mithraic temples. This figure, wrapped in the coils of a serpent, holding keys and a thunderbolt, represents the infinite, unbounded time that governs the Mithraic cosmos. He is the lord of the ages, the power that binds the universe together and ensures its eternal cycle of creation and destruction.

Plutarch, in his interpretation of the Osiris myth, states that Horus was born from Isis after she had intercourse with the 'spirit' of the dead Osiris. He equates this spirit with Aion, the 'eternity' that is the father of all things. This syncretic move connects the Greek concept of Aion to the Egyptian theology of divine kingship and the eternal renewal of the pharaoh.

Modern Interpretations

Philosophy Neoplatonic philosophy (Plotinus, Porphyry, Damascius) Plotinus et al. 3rd-6th century CE direct Aion becomes a central concept in Neoplatonism as the eternal, unchanging reality that is opposed to time (chronos). This philosophical usage profoundly influenced later Christian theology (e.g., Augustine's concept of eternity). Theology Gnosticism Various Gnostic sects 2nd-4th century CE inspired The Gnostic concept of the 'aeons' — divine emanations from the ultimate God — is directly derived from Aion. The Gnostic aeons are not identical to the deity Aion, but they share the same name and the concept of eternal, divine principles. Literature Modern fantasy and science fiction Various (e.g., H.P. Lovecraft, Neil Gaiman, Philip Pullman) 20th-21st century inspired The concept of personified, cyclical time has been a recurring trope in speculative fiction. Neil Gaiman's 'The Sandman' features a character named 'The Endless' who personify abstract concepts, including Destiny, Death, and Dream, which are conceptually similar to Aion. Scholarly Debates
Aion vs. Chronos
Aion and Chronos are the same deity, or at least interchangeable names for the same concept. ⇌ Aion and Chronos are distinct deities: Aion is eternal, cyclical, and boundless; Chronos is linear, measured, and finite.
Evidence for A: Orphic fragments often use the names interchangeably; both are depicted as primordial gods in similar ways.
Evidence for B: Neoplatonic philosophers (e.g., Plotinus, Damascius) explicitly distinguish aion (eternity) from chronos (time).
Identity of the Mithraic Lion-headed God
The lion-headed god (Leontocephaline) is Aion. ⇌ The lion-headed god is Ahriman (the Zoroastrian evil spirit).
Evidence for A: The figure is wrapped in a serpent (ouroboros) and holds a key and thunderbolt, symbols associated with time and cosmic
Evidence for B: Some ancient texts (e.g., Porphyry) mention a lion-headed figure in Mithraic context that could be interpreted as a male
Aion's Role in the Orphic Theogony
Aion is the primordial, self-created deity from whom all else emerges. ⇌ Aion is a later emanation, not the absolute first principle.
Evidence for A: Some Orphic fragments describe Aion as the 'first-born' and 'father of all things'.
Evidence for B: Other fragments suggest that Chronos (or another figure) is the first principle, with Aion emerging later.
What We Don't Know

The primary evidence for Aion is fragmentary and heavily reliant on later philosophical interpretations (Neoplatonism) and syncretic cults (Mithraism). The exact nature of his worship in the Eleusinian Mysteries is not well-documented. The relationship between the Greek Aion and the Persian Zurvan requires further comparative study. The iconography of the Mithraic Leontocephaline is well-attested but its precise theological meaning remains debated. Direct evidence of devotional practice (prayers, rituals) dedicated specifically to Aion is scarce.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of time as a line — that's Chronos, the god of sequential, measured time, the one who ticks away your life. Now think of time as a circle — a vast, eternal loop where everything repeats, the stars turn, the ages rise and fall, and the cosmos breathes in and out. That circle is Aion. He's not the ticking of the clock; he's the clock itself, the entire mechanism of the universe, the eternal return of all things. If Chronos is the 'time since', Aion is the 'time that always is'.
