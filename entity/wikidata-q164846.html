Who It Is

Imagine a god who doesn't just rule time — he IS time, but not the ticking seconds of your watch. He is the time of the turning of the zodiac, the slow breathing of the universe, the endless loop of the serpent devouring its own tail. This is Aion, the Greco-Roman deity of eternal, cyclical time, a figure who emerged from the fusion of Greek philosophy, Persian mysticism, and Egyptian theology. He is the awe-inspiring, terrifying, and utterly fascinating personification of the cosmos's endless cycle of creation and destruction, a god whose image — a lion-headed figure wrapped in a serpent — still haunts the ruins of Roman temples and the pages of fantasy novels today.

As a primordial, abstract deity, Aion does not engage in the anthropomorphic adventures of the Olympian gods. He is a personification of a cosmic principle — eternal, boundless, cyclical time. He is depicted as a serene, all-powerful, and inscrutable force that governs the turning of the ages, the cycles of birth and destruction, and the endless renewal of the cosmos.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Greek αἰών (aiṓn), meaning 'lifetime', 'age', 'generation', 'long period of time', and ultimately 'eternity'. The word is related to the Latin 'aevum' and the English 'eon'. In philosophical usage, it came to denote eternal, undivided time as opposed to chronos (sequential, measured time).

Pronunciation — EYE-on (English); ah-ee-OWN (ancient Greek)

Aliases — Aeon, Aionos, Aeternitas, Saeculum, Deus Aeternus

Eternal Time Manipulation

Aion embodies boundless, cyclical, and eternal time — the very framework of existence. He is not subject to time but is its source and master. He governs the great ages (aeons) of the cosmos, their creation, flourishing, and destruction.

Limit: As a personification, his 'powers' are conceptual rather than narrative; he is the principle of time itself, not a deity who 'uses' it.

Cosmic Sovereignty

Aion is the ruler of the universe and the heavens. He holds the keys to the gates of heaven and the celestial spheres, controlling the passage of souls and the movement of the stars.

Limit: His sovereignty is often depicted as abstract and impersonal, without the narrative agency of a Zeus or a Jupiter.

Cycle of Renewal

Aion embodies the cyclical nature of time — the eternal return of the same ages. He is the power that allows the cosmos to be destroyed and reborn in an endless cycle.

Limit: This is a cosmological principle rather than a discrete 'power' used in a narrative.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In Greco-Roman art, Aion is typically depicted as a handsome, youthful man (or occasionally a mature bearded figure) standing within a celestial circle or zodiac ring, symbolizing the eternal cycle of the heavens. He is often shown with a lion's head (especially in Mithraic contexts), holding a key (to the gates of heaven), a thunderbolt (power over the cosmos), and a staff or scepter. Accounts I
Aion in the Orphic TheogonyOrphic fragments (collected in Kern's 'Orphicorum Fragmenta')

c. 6th century BCE - 3rd century CE

In the Orphic cosmogony, Aion (or Chronos in some versions) is the primordial, self-created deity who emerges from the cosmic egg. He is the first principle, the 'father of all things', who generates the aether, chaos, and the silver egg from which the god Phanes (the creator) is born. Aion is thus the ultimate ancestor of the gods and the cosmos itself. Aion establishes the framework of eternal time in which the entire Orphic cosmogony unfolds. He is the grandfather of the Olympian gods.

Accounts II
Aion in the Mithraic MysteriesArchaeological evidence from Mithraea (reliefs, sculptures); textual references in Porphyry and other Neoplatonists

1st - 4th century CE

Aion is widely identified with the enigmatic lion-headed god (Leontocephaline) found in Mithraic temples. This figure, wrapped in the coils of a serpent, holding keys and a thunderbolt, represents the infinite, unbounded time that governs the Mithraic cosmos. He is the lord of the ages, the power that binds the universe together and ensures its eternal cycle of creation and destruction. Aion's presence in the Mithraeum reinforces the cult's emphasis on cosmic order, fate, and the journey of the soul through the celestial spheres.

Aion as the Father of HorusPlutarch, 'De Iside et Osiride'

c. 100 CE

Plutarch, in his interpretation of the Osiris myth, states that Horus was born from Isis after she had intercourse with the 'spirit' of the dead Osiris. He equates this spirit with Aion, the 'eternity' that is the father of all things. This syncretic move connects the Greek concept of Aion to the Egyptian theology of divine kingship and the eternal renewal of the pharaoh. This text demonstrates how Aion was used as a theological bridge between Greek and Egyptian religious concepts in the Hellenistic period.

Aion is the same as Chronos, the god of time. — While often conflated, they are conceptually distinct: Aion is eternal, cyclical, and boundless time; Chronos is linear, measured, and finite time. The distinction is crucial in Neoplatonic philosophy.

Aion is a purely Greek deity. — Aion is a syncretic figure that emerged in the Hellenistic period, incorporating influences from Zoroastrianism (Zurvan, the god of infinite time), Egyptian religion, and Near Eastern traditions.

The Mithraic lion-headed god is a demon or evil figure. — The most widely accepted scholarly interpretation is that the lion-headed god is Aion, a positive, albeit awe-inspiring, personification of eternal time. The figure's fearsome appearance is meant to convey the power and mystery of time, not evil.
