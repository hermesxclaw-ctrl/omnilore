Who It Is

Imagine a being so powerful that it can command the seas and shake the heavens, yet so proud that it would rather be imprisoned in a bottle for centuries than bow to a mortal. This is the Marid, the most rebellious of the jinn, whose tales have inspired both terror and wonder for over a thousand years. But what if I told you that the Marid you think you know from 'Aladdin' is a pale shadow of the original? Dive into the depths of Islamic folklore and uncover the true nature of this enigmatic entity.

Marids are characterized by their immense pride and rebelliousness. They are described as the most powerful and arrogant of the jinn, often defying divine authority and the commands of prophets. They are known to be malicious and destructive, causing chaos and harm to humans out of spite or for amusement. However, they are also bound by magical oaths and can be compelled to serve a master, though they will seek to twist the letter of the command to cause misfortune. Powers
Wish Granting

Marids are famously capable of granting wishes to those who command them, often through a magical object like a ring or lamp. However, the wishes are frequently granted in a malevolent or ironic manner, as the Marid seeks to fulfill the letter of the wish while subverting its intent. This power is limited by the Marid's own pride and the constraints of the binding magic, but it is a defining trait in folklore.

Limit:

Shape-shifting

Marids can assume various forms, including human, animal, or hybrid shapes. They can appear as beautiful humans to deceive, or as terrifying beasts to intimidate. This ability is used to interact with humans and to evade capture or detection.

Limit:

Elemental Control

As beings of the sea, Marids have dominion over water, able to summon storms, create whirlpools, and control tides. They also have some influence over air and fire, allowing them to fly and generate flames. In some accounts, they can cause earthquakes and other natural disasters.

Limit:

Superhuman Strength and Size

Marids are described as having immense physical power, capable of lifting mountains, crushing buildings, and causing widespread destruction. Their size is often colossal, making them formidable opponents even for other supernatural beings.

Limit:

Invisibility and Teleportation

Like all jinn, Marids are invisible to the human eye in their natural state, though they can become visible at will. They can also teleport across vast distances, moving between the earthly realm and the unseen world in an instant.

Accounts I
The Fisherman and the JinniOne Thousand and One Nights

9th-14th century CE

A poor fisherman casts his net and catches a brass bottle sealed with the seal of Solomon. When he opens it, a massive Marid emerges, declaring that he will kill the fisherman as a reward for freeing him, because he had grown bitter after centuries of imprisonment. The fisherman, using his wits, tricks the Marid by asking how such a huge being could fit in a small bottle, and when the Marid demonstrates by shrinking back into the bottle, the fisherman seals it again. The Marid pleads for mercy, and the fisherman releases him after the Marid promises to reward him with a pond of rare fish, which leads to further adventures. The fisherman gains wealth and favor with the king, while the Marid is bound to serve him. The tale illustrates the power of human intelligence over supernatural might.

Accounts II
The Marid and the Prophet SolomonIslamic folklore and Qur'anic commentary

7th century CE onwards

According to tradition, King Solomon (Sulayman) had authority over the jinn, including the Marids. He commanded them to build the Temple in Jerusalem and to perform other tasks. One Marid, named Sakhr, rebelled and stole Solomon's ring, taking his throne and impersonating him for forty days. Solomon eventually regained his ring and punished the Marid by imprisoning him in a bottle and throwing it into the sea. This story is referenced in the Qur'an (Surah Sad, 38:34-35) and elaborated in hadith and folklore. The Marid is bound and cast into the sea, and Solomon's authority is restored. The tale underscores the theme of divine favor and the subjugation of rebellious jinn.

The Marid Who Spied on the HeavensQur'anic exegesis and hadith

7th century CE

The Qur'an mentions that the heavens are guarded by shooting stars, which are used to repel devils (shayatin) who attempt to eavesdrop on divine decrees. Marids, as a type of shaytan, are among those who try to climb to the lower heaven to steal secrets. They are struck by meteors and cast down. This event is described in Surah Al-Saffat (37:7-10) and Surah Al-Jinn (72:8-9). The Marids are portrayed as rebellious beings who defy divine boundaries. The Marids are repelled and punished, reinforcing the idea that they are enemies of divine order. This story is used to explain the origin of shooting stars.
