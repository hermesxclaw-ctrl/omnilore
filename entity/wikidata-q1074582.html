The Approach

In the highest heaven, beyond the nine layers of the sky, there dwelt a lady whose name promised abundance: Tōnacācihuātl, the Lady of Our Sustenance. She was the silent partner in creation, the feminine half of the primordial pair, and from her realm the souls of humans descended to earth. Her story is not one of dramatic battles, but of the quiet, essential acts of making life possible—of food, of fertility, of the very flesh that sustains us. Yet even this gentle figure has a shadow: a tale of a flint knife, cast out of heaven, from which a thousand gods were born.

Content advisory. Contains references to childbirth, creation myths, and a story involving a flint knife that is thrown from heaven, which may be interpreted as an act of violence or rejection. No explicit sexual or violent content.
Identity

Tōnacācihuātl

Etymology

The name is a compound of tōnacā and cihuātl. Cihuātl means 'woman' or 'lady'. Tōnacā is more complex: it may derive from tonacā (without the long 'o'), consisting of nacatl ('human flesh' or 'food') with the possessive prefix to- ('our'), yielding 'Lady of Our Food' or 'Lady of Our Flesh', commonly rendered 'Lady of Our Sustenance'. Alternatively, tōnac simply means 'abundance', giving 'Lady of Abundance'.

Cultural Ownership

Central Mexican (Aztec/Mexica) culture, part of the broader Mesoamerican religious tradition.

Disputed Identifications

Most colonial manuscripts equate her with Ōmecihuātl, the feminine aspect of the primordial duality. Some sources treat them as distinct, but the general scholarly consensus is that they represent the same goddess under different names or aspects.

Description

Appearance

Specific physical descriptions are not provided in the source material. She is depicted in the Codex Telleriano-Remensis, but the exact imagery is not described. As a creator goddess, she may be portrayed in a regal manner, but this is speculative.

Symbols & Iconography

Associated with fertility and abundance, possibly symbolized by corn or other sustenance. In pre-Columbian art, she appears near copulating humans, indicating her association with procreation.

Behaviour & Personality

As a creator and fertility goddess, she is associated with nurturing and sustaining life. She is a passive or cooperative figure in creation, working alongside her consort. No myths depict her with a distinct personality or active role beyond creation.

Domains & Habitats

Resides in Ōmeyōcān, the 13th and highest heaven, also called 'the place of duality'. This is the origin point for human souls, which descend to earth.

Version Differences

Colonial sources often equate her with Ōmecihuātl. Some sources may present them as distinct, but the general trend is syncretism. The name Tōnacācihuātl emphasizes her role in sustenance, while Ōmecihuātl emphasizes duality.

Stories & Myths

The Creation of Souls and the Origin of the Gods

Source Codex Ríos, History of the Mexicans as Told by Their Paintings, Histoyre du Mechique, Florentine Codex, Codex ChimalpopocaCulture Aztec (Mexica)Period Pre-Columbian, recorded in the 16th century

Summary

Tōnacācihuātl and her consort Tōnacātēcuhtli reside in Ōmeyōcān, the highest heaven. Human souls originate there and descend to earth. In some accounts, she gives birth to many gods. One specific account from the Codex Chimalpopoca tells that after giving birth to many children, she gave birth to a flint knife (tecpatl). The other gods, frightened and amazed, threw it down to earth. It fell at Chicomoztoc, the 'Seven Caves', and from it emerged 1600 gods and goddesses who spread across the earth, sea, underworld, and heavens.

Participants

Tōnacācihuātl

Tōnacātēcuhtli

The other gods (her children)

The 1600 gods (Nauhtzonteteo)

Consequences

The flint's fall leads to the creation of numerous deities who populate the cosmos. This myth explains the origin of many gods.

Variants

The story of the flint is not found in other major sources, which typically describe the creation of gods in different ways. Other sources emphasize the role of the pair in creating souls rather than giving birth to a flint.

Midwives' Blessing

Source Florentine CodexCulture Aztec (Mexica)Period 16th century

Summary

After bathing a newborn, midwives would address the child, saying they were created in the place of duality, above the nine heavens, by Ōmetēuctli and Ōmecihuātl, the heavenly lady. This establishes Tōnacācihuātl as the divine mother of all humans.

Planting Ritual

Source Treatise on the Heathen Superstitions by Hernando Ruiz de AlarcónCulture Aztec (Mexica)Period 1629

Summary

A shaman, when planting corn, entrusts the seed to the earth deity Tlaltecuhtli and calls the kernel 'nohueltiuh Tōnacācihuātl' ('my sister, the Lady of Abundance'). This shows her continued importance in agricultural rituals.

Relationships

Tōnacātēcuhtli

Type consortSource Codex Ríos, Florentine Codex, Codex Chimalpopoca

Description

Her male counterpart and husband. Together they reside in Ōmeyōcān and are the primordial creator pair.

Version Note

In some sources, this pair is identified with Ōmetēuctli and Ōmecihuātl.

Conflicting Accounts

Some sources treat Tōnacācihuātl and Tōnacātēcuhtli as distinct from Ōmetēuctli and Ōmecihuātl, but most colonial manuscripts equate them.

The Casting Out of the Flint

Source Codex Chimalpopoca

Description

After Tōnacācihuātl gives birth to a flint, the other gods are frightened and throw it down to earth. This act is not described as violent, but it is an expulsion. The flint then gives rise to 1600 gods, which could be seen as a positive outcome, but the initial rejection is notable.

Scholarly Context

This myth is unique to this source and may represent a local tradition. It highlights the sometimes ambivalent nature of divine birth and the emergence of powerful forces.

Contradictions & Disputes

Identity with Ōmecihuātl

Position A

Tōnacācihuātl is the same as Ōmecihuātl.

Position A — evidence

Most colonial manuscripts equate them, and the Florentine Codex uses the names interchangeably in the midwives' blessing.

Position B

They are distinct deities.

Position B — evidence

Some scholars argue that the name Tōnacācihuātl emphasizes her role in sustenance, while Ōmecihuātl emphasizes duality, and they may have been separate originally.

Scholarly Consensus

The general consensus is that they are the same goddess, with Tōnacācihuātl being a title or aspect.

The Birth of the Flint

Position A

Tōnacācihuātl gave birth to a flint from which 1600 gods emerged.

Position A — evidence

The Codex Chimalpopoca contains this account.

Position B

This event is not part of the standard creation myth.

Position B — evidence

Other major sources do not mention this episode.

Scholarly Consensus

This is a variant tradition, possibly local, and not universally accepted as canonical.

Research Scope

This dossier focuses on the Aztec (Mexica) goddess Tōnacācihuātl, primarily as documented in colonial-era Nahuatl and Spanish sources. It covers her identity, etymology, role in creation, associations with fertility and sustenance, her depiction in codices, and her relationship with other deities. Modern reception and popular culture references are limited and are noted as such. The research cutoff is August 2026.
