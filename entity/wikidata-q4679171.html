Who It Is

In the vast pantheon of Greek mythology, where gods thunder and heroes clash, Admete is a whisper — a name spoken twice, in two different worlds. As an Oceanid, she is one of three thousand daughters of the world-encircling river, a divine drop in an endless ocean of nymphs. As a princess, she is the daughter of a king who would become the greatest hero's greatest tormentor — and the woman whose desire for a magical belt would send Heracles to the edge of the known world, to fight an army of warrior women, and to kill a queen. She never lifts a spear, never casts a spell, never speaks a word in any surviving text. Yet her simple wish — 'I want that' — becomes the engine of one of the twelve most famous quests in human storytelling. Admete is the quiet force behind the thunder, proof that in mythology, even the forgotten can move the world.

The Oceanid Admete is primarily a genealogical figure, appearing in lists of nymphs without individual behavioral characterization. The Admete of the Heracles cycle is characterized by a notable desire for a prized object — the girdle of Hippolyta — which functions as the catalyst for one of Heracles' most famous labours. This desire, while seemingly simple, demonstrates the power of mortal (or semi-divine) desire to drive divine heroic action.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Admete (Ἀδμήτη) is the feminine form of Admetus (Ἄδμητος), meaning 'untamed', 'unconquered', or 'not to be tamed'. It derives from the Greek root 'dam-' (to tame or subdue) with the alpha-privative prefix, suggesting someone or something that resists domination.

Pronunciation — ad-MEE-tee (English), ahd-MAY-tay (Classical Greek approximation)

Aliases — Admeta, Admete (Oceanid), Admete (daughter of Eurystheus), Admete (priestess of Hera)

Ownership — Ancient Greek mythology and religion, specifically within the wider Hellenic pantheon. The name appears in multiple distinct mythological contexts, most notably as an Oceanid nymph

Powers
Nymphic immortality (Oceanid version)

As an Oceanid, Admete would possess the long-lived nature of the nymphs, though not necessarily full immortality. Nymphs could live extremely long lives but were not necessarily ageless.

Limit: Not explicitly attested; nymphic longevity is assumed rather than stated for individual Oceanids.

Royal authority and desire (princess version)

As the daughter of Eurystheus, Admete's desire for the Amazon girdle demonstrates the power of royal will to direct heroic action, even against the hero's wishes.

Limit: Purely mortal agency; her desire is fulfilled only through the actions of Heracles.

Form & Signs

Appearance — As an Oceanid nymph, Admete would have been envisioned in classical art as a beautiful young woman, often depicted in the company of her sisters, sometimes with water-related attributes such as a hydria (water jar) or aquatic plants. No distinct physical attributes are attested for her specifically. As the daughter of Eurystheus, she would have been depicted as a mortal princess in royal attire, though again, no specific visual tradition survives

Symbols — For the Oceanid: water, springs, rivers, moisture, nurturing streams. For the princess: the girdle (zone) of Hippolyta — a symbol of Amazonian power and virginity, and by extension, of female sovereignty and martial prowess.

Domains — As an Oceanid, Admete belongs to the vast pantheon of water nymphs who personify springs, rivers, seas, and other bodies of water. The Oceanids, as daughters of Oceanus and Tethys, were associated with the great world-encircling river Oceanus and were often seen as the ancestors of rivers and springs. As a mortal princess, Admete belongs to the royal house of Tiryns, one of the great Mycenaean citadels.

Accounts I
The Catalogue of the OceanidsHesiod's Theogony, lines 346-350

circa 700 BCE

Hesiod lists Admete among the daughters of Oceanus and Tethys, the great primordial couple whose offspring number in the thousands. The Oceanids are the personifications of springs, rivers, and the life-giving waters of the earth. Admete is named in a sequence of sisters, some of whom (like Electra, Doris, and Callirrhoe) have more prominent mythological roles. Admete is established as part of the vast divine genealogy that populates Greek mythology's watery realms.

Accounts II
The Ninth Labour: The Girdle of HippolytaPseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.5.9; Diodorus Siculus 4.16; Euripides, Heracles 414-418

Archaic through Classical periods

Eurystheus commands Heracles to fetch the girdle of Hippolyta, queen of the Amazons, because his daughter Admete desires it. The girdle was a gift from Ares to Hippolyta, symbolizing her martial authority. Heracles sails to the land of the Amazons, where he initially receives a peaceful welcome from Hippolyta, who is willing to give him the girdle. However, Hera, ever hostile to Heracles, spreads a rumor among the Amazons that Heracles intends to abduct their queen, provoking an attack. Heracles, believing he has been betrayed, kills Hippolyta and takes the girdle, defeating the Amazon army in battle. The labour results in the death of Hippolyta and the defeat of the Amazons. Heracles returns to Mycenae with the girdle, which is given to Admete. The story also establishes a connection between the Greek world and the mythical land of the Amazons, and provides a mythological justification for Greek views of Amazonian martial culture.

The Robe of Hera (Possible conflation)Scholia to Homer's Iliad; various late antique sources

Hellenistic through Roman periods

Some later traditions suggest that Admete, daughter of Eurystheus, was a priestess of Hera who was involved in the theft of Hera's sacred robe, which was then given to the Amazons. This conflation may explain why the girdle of Hippolyta was specifically desired — as a replacement for a stolen sacred object. However, this tradition is late and uncertain. If accepted, this tradition would provide a more complex motivation for the girdle labour, tying it to sacrilege and divine punishment.

Admete is a major goddess with her own mythology. — Admete is a minor figure in Greek mythology. The Oceanid appears only in genealogical lists, and the princess appears primarily as a motivation for one of Heracles' labours. She has no independent mythology, cult, or major narrative role.

Admete is the same as the wife of Admetus. — Admete (feminine) and Admetus (masculine) are related names but refer to different figures. The wife of Admetus, king of Pherae, is Alcestis, not Admete. The similarity of names can lead to confusion.

The girdle of Hippolyta was a simple belt or sash. — The girdle (zone) in ancient Greek culture was a complex garment that could symbolize virginity, marital status, and authority. In the context of Hippolyta, it was a gift from Ares and symbolized her military command. It was not merely a fashion accessory.
