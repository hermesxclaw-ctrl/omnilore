Who It Is

He entered the ring to laughter that was never just laughter. In Belle Époque Paris, when the white clown Footit chased him with a slapstick and the crowd roared, Chocolat — a tall, graceful Afro-Cuban man in a world that had no name for a Black star — turned humiliation into art, and for a moment made the most fashionable city in the world watch a Black man dance on his own terms.

Attested behavior: highly skilled physical comedian, dancer, acrobat and mime; noted for agility, musicality and improvisation. Press accounts (often racist) describe him as good-natured, patient under Footit's blows, and adept at turning the 'victim' role into audience sympathy. Colleagues' recollections (collected by Noiriel) suggest professionalism, quiet dignity, and later struggles with typecasting and financial insecurity. Powers
Auguste clowning and slapstick

Mastery of the auguste role: receiving and comically absorbing blows, falls, and chases from the white clown, with exaggerated reactions, facial malleability and timing to elicit laughter and sympathy.

Limit: Role required submission to physical comedy that reinforced racial hierarchy; limited ability to headline without Footit; typecast as perpetual victim.

Dance and acrobatic agility

Exceptional dancer (jig, cakewalk, waltz parody) and acrobat; noted for leaps, splits, and musical timing. Toulouse-Lautrec's 'Chocolat dansant' captures mid-motion elegance.

Limit: No formal ballet training documented; physical demands contributed to later health decline; not a trapeze or equestrian specialist.

Pantomime and musical clowning

Mime, mimicry, and playing of instruments and props (chair, saxophone parody, drum) as part of entrées such as 'William Tell' and 'La Noce de Chocolat'.

Limit: Acts were short entrées (10-20 min), not full-length narratives; dependent on Footit's white-clown authority structure.

Linguistic and improvisational wit

Reported ability to improvise in French with Spanish-accented delivery, using malapropisms and wordplay that became part of the act; later attempted solo sketches.

Limit: French was second language; press often mocked accent rather than credited wit; solo career without Footit was commercially unsuccessful.

Accounts I
From Cuba to Bilbao: Childhood and EnslavementGérard Noiriel, Chocolat la véritable histoire d'un homme sans nom (2016); Bordeaux death record; Tony Grice circus archives

c.1868 - c.1886

Born Rafael Padilla in Cuba around 1868, likely near Havana, to parents of African descent in the final decade of slavery in Cuba (abolished 1886). Oral tradition and later press claim he was sold or given as a child servant to a Spanish family and taken to Spain, possibly via merchant Patricio Castaño. In Bilbao he worked as a domestic servant and then as a groom and helper for the traveling clown Tony Grice, who employed him as an assistant and gradually introduced him to circus work. No birth certificate has been located; details rely on death record, Grice family recollections, and press interviews. Acquisition of circus skills, Spanish language, and entry into European entertainment circuits; severance from Cuban family and identity, with name 'Rafael Padilla' retained in civil records.

Arrival in Paris and the Nouveau CirqueNouveau Cirque archives; Le Courrier français 1886-1889; Noiriel 2016 ch.3

1886-1889

Brought to Paris by Tony Grice's circus in 1886, Padilla worked as an attendant and occasional performer. Spotted by the director of the Nouveau Cirque, he was hired as a clown under the stage name 'Chocolat', initially in minor roles and as a foil in equestrian and pantomime acts. The name, already used for Black performers in France, marked him as an exotic novelty. Established stage identity 'Chocolat' and entry into Paris's premier intimate circus, setting the stage for partnership with Footit.

Footit et Chocolat: The Celebrated Duo (1889-1910)Nouveau Cirque programmes 1889-1910; Lumière films c.1900; press reviews in Gil Blas, Le Rire

1889-1910

In 1889 English white clown George Footit (1864-1921) partnered with Chocolat, creating the classic white-clown/auguste duo that defined modern clowning. Footit, in whiteface and elegant costume, played the domineering, pedantic authority; Chocolat, in red and black, played the naive, graceful, perpetually punished auguste. Entrées such as 'William Tell', 'Le Policeman' and 'La Noce de Chocolat' combined slapstick, chase, and dance, drawing fashionable Parisian audiences and making them the most famous clowns in France for two decades. Codified the Footit-Chocolat structure still taught in clown schools; made Chocolat the first Black star of French popular stage, but within a racist comic hierarchy; extensive visual reproduction in posters and early cinema.

Accounts II
Chocolat at the Moulin Rouge and in Toulouse-Lautrec's ParisHenri de Toulouse-Lautrec lithographs 1895-1896; La Revue Blanche; Musée d'Orsay holdings

1895-1899

Beyond the circus, Chocolat performed in Montmartre venues including the Moulin Rouge and Achille's bar, where he danced the cakewalk and other African-American influenced dances. Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec, fascinated by marginal performers, sketched and lithographed him repeatedly, notably in 'Chocolat dansant dans le bar Achille' (1896) and studies of Footit et Chocolat. These images, unlike crude press caricatures, convey movement and individuality and remain the most reproduced visual record of Chocolat. Secured Chocolat's place in art history; provided primary visual evidence countering racist press caricature; helped disseminate his image to broader public.

Separation, Decline and Final Years in BordeauxPress 1910-1914; Bordeaux municipal archives; Noiriel 2016 ch.6-7

1910-1917

After Footit left the Nouveau Cirque in 1910, Chocolat attempted solo careers, touring with circuses and in pantomimes, but without Footit's structure and with changing tastes (cinema, music-hall), success waned. He suffered financial hardship, health problems, and reportedly alcohol-related illness. He toured with his family and performed in Bordeaux, where he died on 4 November 1917 at age ~49, in relative obscurity. He was buried in Bordeaux, grave later unmarked until recent commemoration. End of the most famous clown duo of the era; decades of oblivion until late 20th-century rediscovery by circus historians and anti-racist scholars.

Rediscovery and Reinterpretation: From Archive to BiopicGérard Noiriel & Pascal Blanchard scholarship 2012-2016; Roschdy Zem film Chocolat (2016) starring Omar Sy and James Thierrée

2012-2016 (depicting 1880s-1910s)

Historian Gérard Noiriel's archival research recovered Chocolat's civil identity and contextualized his career within French colonial racism, leading to the 2016 biopic Chocolat directed by Roschdy Zem. The film dramatizes Padilla's life from Cuba to Paris, his partnership and eventual break with Footit, and his struggle for dignity, using composite characters (e.g., circus artist Marie Grice) and invented dialogue to convey documented dynamics. Renewed public recognition, commemorative plaque at Nouveau Cirque site, reissue of Toulouse-Lautrec images with contextualization, and debate about dramatization vs. historical accuracy.

Chocolat was African, not Cuban, and was brought directly from Africa to France as an exotic import. — Best archival evidence (Bordeaux death record, Grice testimony, Noiriel research) indicates Afro-Cuban birth near Havana c.1868 and transit via Spain (Bilbao) before Paris. No evidence of direct African birth or import as part of a colonial exhibition.

Chocolat is a fictional character from the novel/film Chocolat (Joanne Harris). — Joanne Harris's Chocolat (1999) has no character named Chocolat; title refers to chocolate. The clown Chocolat is a historical person (Rafael Padilla, 1868-1917) unrelated to the novel.

Footit et Chocolat were equal partners and friends offstage. — They were a long-running professional duo with cordial relations, but billing, pay and creative control favored Footit. Offstage they moved in different social circles, and Chocolat struggled after the split while Footit retained status.

The name Chocolat was a cute nickname he chose himself. — Chocolat was a generic, racialized stage name imposed on Black performers in 19th-century France (referencing skin color as chocolate). It was assigned by circus management, not chosen as a personal artistic name; his civil name remained Rafael Padilla.

Photographs and Toulouse-Lautrec posters show Chocolat as a buffoonish caricature, proving he was not a skilled artist. — Posters include both racist caricatures (enlarged lips, subservient posture) and Toulouse-Lautrec's more observational, dynamic studies that emphasize grace and dance skill. Contemporary reviews, despite racist language, consistently praise his agility and comic timing.
