Who It Is

Imagine a hero whose arrows can knock suns out of the sky—and who might also shoot a river god in the eye. Hou Yi stands at that unsettling crossroads where cosmic rescue and human wrongdoing share the same bowstring. He is the archer who cools a burning world, the husband whose wife escapes to the moon with immortality, and, in some tellings, the usurper whose rule brings grief to an entire dynasty. To follow his story is to watch the sky itself become a stage for both salvation and failure.

Hou Yi’s behavior is deeply ambivalent in the sources. In some narratives, he is the paradigmatic savior: he responds to Emperor Yao’s call, shoots down nine of ten suns to end a catastrophic drought, and slays a roster of monsters threatening human communities. In other strands, especially those highlighted in Chu ci commentary and later retellings, he becomes a tyrant who usurps the Xia throne, indulges in excessive hunting, alters laws arbitrarily, and brings suffering to his subjects. Powers
Supreme archery

Hou Yi is renowned as an unparalleled archer capable of shooting down celestial bodies and slaying powerful monsters. His arrows can pierce suns, strike divine beings, and subdue creatures that threaten the world.

Limit: The texts do not specify intrinsic limits to his archery, but narrative structure implies that his power is constrained by cosmic order: he is permitted to shoot nine suns but must leave one to sustain life. His archery is also subject to moral evaluation—when turned against gods or used in oppressive ways, it becomes a sign of transgression rather than virtue.

Shooting down nine suns

In the age of Emperor Yao, ten suns appear simultaneously, scorching the earth. Hou Yi shoots down nine of them, leaving one sun to preserve life, thereby restoring cosmic balance and saving humanity from destruction.

Limit: He does not extinguish all suns; the narrative insists on the necessity of a single remaining sun. This suggests a limit grounded in cosmological necessity rather than personal inability.

Monster-slaying and disaster expulsion

Hou Yi is tasked with destroying a series of monsters—Yayu, Zaochi, Jiuying, Dafeng, Fengxi, Xiushe—who devastate lands and waters. His actions cleanse the environment and protect human communities.

Limit: His interventions are episodic and tied to imperial command (Yao’s dispatch). They do not permanently eliminate all chaos but represent decisive acts of restoration.

Access to the elixir of immortality

Hou Yi obtains a drug or elixir of immortality from the Queen Mother of the West, a powerful goddess associated with longevity and transcendence.

Limit: Hou Yi himself does not ultimately consume the elixir in the best-known version; instead, Chang’e steals it. His access is thus conditional and precarious, and immortality slips from his grasp.

Political power and usurpation

In certain traditions, Yi destroys the Xia dynasty, kills Xiahou Xiang, and sets himself up as ruler, exercising sovereign power over the realm.

Limit: His rule is portrayed as morally flawed—marked by reckless hunting and harmful legal changes—and ultimately ends when he is killed by Han Zhuo in some accounts.

Accounts I
Hou Yi Shoots the Nine SunsHuainanzi 淮南子, Benjing 本經; synthesized in modern scholarship (e.g., Liu Quanzhi, Masako Mori).

Pre-Qin mythic setting; textual attestation in Western Han (2nd century BCE).

In the reign of Emperor Yao, ten suns rise together, burning crops and grass so that people face starvation. Monsters ravage the land and waters. Yao commands the master archer Yi to act. Yi travels across plains, rivers, marshes, and forests, slaying monsters and finally drawing his bow against the sky. He shoots down nine of the ten suns, which fall from the heavens, leaving one sun to sustain life. The world cools, crops can grow, and order is restored. Cosmic balance is re-established; Hou Yi becomes celebrated as a heroic savior. The myth becomes a foundational narrative about environmental catastrophe and human intervention.

Hou Yi, Chang’e, and the Elixir of ImmortalityHuainanzi passage on the Queen Mother of the West; later literary and religious elaborations; modern interpretive essays.

Textual core in Western Han; extensive later development in medieval and modern tradition.

Hou Yi, after his heroic deeds, receives or acquires a drug of immortality from the Queen Mother of the West. His wife Chang’e learns of the elixir. In the most familiar version, she steals it and consumes it herself, ascending to the moon and becoming a luminous goddess. Hou Yi is left behind on earth, bereft of his wife and of immortality. Their separation becomes a central motif in Mid-Autumn Festival lore, where the moon is imagined as the site of Chang’e’s exile and beauty, and Hou Yi as the mortal archer who cannot follow. Chang’e becomes the moon goddess; Hou Yi remains mortal and sorrowful. The story links solar and lunar mythologies and becomes a key narrative for the emotional imagination of the moon.

Accounts II
Yi the Usurper of XiaChu ci "Tianwen" with Wang Yi’s commentary; later historical-mythological narratives as surveyed by Masako Mori.

Mythic setting in the Xia dynasty; textual attestation from Han dynasty commentaries onward.

In this strand of tradition, Yi is dispatched by the Heavenly Emperor to save the people of Xia from calamity. Instead of acting purely as a savior, he kills Xiahou Xiang, destroys the Xia dynasty, and seizes the throne. As ruler, he indulges in excessive hunting and alters the laws of the land, causing widespread suffering. Eventually, he is overthrown and killed by Han Zhuo, ending his tyrannical reign. Yi’s image shifts from hero to usurper and oppressor. The story becomes a cautionary tale about the corruption of power and the dangers of rulers who prioritize personal pleasure over just governance.

Yi’s Violence Against DeitiesClassical references collated in Masako Mori’s "Restoring the Epic of Hou Yi"; Chu ci and other texts.

Mythic setting; textual attestation from Han and later periods.

In scattered passages, Yi is accused of ravishing the goddess Luo Pin and shooting her husband, the river god He Bo, in the eye. These acts are presented as transgressive and sacrilegious, marking Yi as a figure whose power extends even against divine beings but whose moral compass is deeply flawed. Yi’s reputation becomes darker and more ambiguous. He is not only a hero who saves humans from cosmic threats but also a perpetrator of sexual violence and sacrilege, complicating any simple heroic reading.
