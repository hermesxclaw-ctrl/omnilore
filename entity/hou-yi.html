Who It Is

Hou Yi (Hòu Yì) is the legendary archer of Chinese mythology, celebrated as the greatest marksman who ever lived and the hero who saved the world from destruction. According to the myth, there was a time when the ten suns—originally the ten sons of the solar deity Di Jun, each taking the form of a golden three-legged crow—refused to take turns crossing the sky and instead rose all at once. The combined heat of ten suns scorched the earth, dried up rivers and oceans, melted rocks, and threatened to annihilate all life. Crops withered, forests blazed, and monstrous creatures emerged to terrorize the people. The divine emperor Yao pleaded to the heavens for aid. Hou Yi, armed with a magical red bow and white arrows gifted by the gods, descended to earth. One by one, he shot down nine of the ten suns, leaving only one to provide warmth and light. With the world saved from incineration, Hou Yi then turned his bow against the other scourges plaguing humanity: he slew the monstrous boar Yayu, the dragon-serpent Jiuying, the wind-destroying bird Dafeng, and several other mythical beasts. For these deeds, he received the elixir of immortality from Xiwangmu—setting in motion the tragedy of his wife Chang'e's ascent to the moon. Some versions of the myth say Hou Yi later became a tyrant, and Chang'e drank the elixir to prevent him from living forever as a despot. Either way, Hou Yi's legacy is that of the archetypal hero whose immense power saved the world but whose personal story ended in sorrow. He was deified as a tutelary spirit of archery and later incorporated into the Taoist celestial hierarchy.

Form

Attested descriptions of Hou Yi focus less on detailed physical traits and more on his function as an archer and hero. He is consistently portrayed as a master of the bow, often imagined with a powerful bow drawn against the blazing suns or monstrous beings. Later visual traditions—paintings, temple murals, and modern media—typically depict him as a robust, heroic male figure in archaic Chinese attire, sometimes with a quiver of arrows and a stance reminiscent of a warrior-god. These visual details are largely reconstructed from artistic convention rather than specified in early texts.

Powers

Supreme archery Hou Yi is renowned as an unparalleled archer capable of shooting down celestial bodies and slaying powerful monsters. His arrows can pierce suns, strike divine beings, and subdue creatures that threaten the world. Huainanzi Benjing; multiple early mythological and literary sources as collated in Masako Mori’s "Restoring the Epic of Hou Yi". The texts do not specify intrinsic limits to his archery, but narrative structure implies that his power is constrained by cosmic order: he is permitted to shoot nine suns but must leave one to sustain life. His archery is also subject to moral evaluation—when turned against gods or used in oppressive ways, it becomes a sign of transgression rather than virtue. Some scholars argue that references to Yi as an archer may derive from multiple figures or a generic title, suggesting that not all feats attributed to "Yi" belong to a single Hou Yi. Others, like Mori, propose that these scattered feats once formed a coherent epic centered on one tragic hero.

Shooting down nine suns In the age of Emperor Yao, ten suns appear simultaneously, scorching the earth. Hou Yi shoots down nine of them, leaving one sun to preserve life, thereby restoring cosmic balance and saving humanity from destruction. Huainanzi Benjing; widely repeated in later mythological compilations and popular retellings. He does not extinguish all suns; the narrative insists on the necessity of a single remaining sun. This suggests a limit grounded in cosmological necessity rather than personal inability. Some reconstructions treat the ten suns as mythic birds (often three-legged crows) in the Fusang tree, while others emphasize them as literal suns. The exact cosmological model varies across sources and artistic traditions.

Monster-slaying and disaster expulsion Hou Yi is tasked with destroying a series of monsters—Yayu, Zaochi, Jiuying, Dafeng, Fengxi, Xiushe—who devastate lands and waters. His actions cleanse the environment and protect human communities. Huainanzi Benjing; later mythological summaries; Liu Quanzhi’s analysis of Hou Yi as a disaster-expelling deity. His interventions are episodic and tied to imperial command (Yao’s dispatch). They do not permanently eliminate all chaos but represent decisive acts of restoration. Some scholars interpret these monsters as personifications of specific natural phenomena (storms, floods, droughts), while others treat them as purely mythic beings. The degree of historicization varies among Doubting Antiquity school scholars and later interpreters.

Access to the elixir of immortality Hou Yi obtains a drug or elixir of immortality from the Queen Mother of the West, a powerful goddess associated with longevity and transcendence. Huainanzi passage on Hou Yi and Chang’e; later Daoist and popular religious elaborations. Hou Yi himself does not ultimately consume the elixir in the best-known version; instead, Chang’e steals it. His access is thus conditional and precarious, and immortality slips from his grasp. Some later versions suggest that Hou Yi intended to share the elixir with Chang’e, while others portray him as hoarding it. The moral framing of his relationship to immortality shifts across retellings.

Political power and usurpation In certain traditions, Yi destroys the Xia dynasty, kills Xiahou Xiang, and sets himself up as ruler, exercising sovereign power over the realm. Chu ci "Tianwen" with Wang Yi’s commentary; later historical-mythological narratives. His rule is portrayed as morally flawed—marked by reckless hunting and harmful legal changes—and ultimately ends when he is killed by Han Zhuo in some accounts. Scholars debate whether this tyrant Yi is identical with the heroic Hou Yi of the suns myth. Some propose multiple figures named Yi or a title for archers; others argue for a single complex character whose story includes both heroic and tragic phases.

Behavior & Habitat

Hou Yi’s behavior is deeply ambivalent in the sources. In some narratives, he is the paradigmatic savior: he responds to Emperor Yao’s call, shoots down nine of ten suns to end a catastrophic drought, and slays a roster of monsters threatening human communities. In other strands, especially those highlighted in Chu ci commentary and later retellings, he becomes a tyrant who usurps the Xia throne, indulges in excessive hunting, alters laws arbitrarily, and brings suffering to his subjects. Additional episodes portray him committing sexual violence against the goddess Luo Pin and maiming the river god He Bo, suggesting a figure whose heroism can slide into hubris and abuse. This duality—cosmic rescuer versus earthly oppressor—is one of the central tensions in his mythic persona.

Accounts

In the reign of Emperor Yao, ten suns rise together, burning crops and grass so that people face starvation. Monsters ravage the land and waters. Yao commands the master archer Yi to act. Yi travels across plains, rivers, marshes, and forests, slaying monsters and finally drawing his bow against the sky. He shoots down nine of the ten suns, which fall from the heavens, leaving one sun to sustain life. The world cools, crops can grow, and order is restored.

Hou Yi, after his heroic deeds, receives or acquires a drug of immortality from the Queen Mother of the West. His wife Chang’e learns of the elixir. In the most familiar version, she steals it and consumes it herself, ascending to the moon and becoming a luminous goddess. Hou Yi is left behind on earth, bereft of his wife and of immortality. Their separation becomes a central motif in Mid-Autumn Festival lore, where the moon is imagined as the site of Chang’e’s exile and beauty, and Hou Yi as the mortal archer who cannot follow.

In this strand of tradition, Yi is dispatched by the Heavenly Emperor to save the people of Xia from calamity. Instead of acting purely as a savior, he kills Xiahou Xiang, destroys the Xia dynasty, and seizes the throne. As ruler, he indulges in excessive hunting and alters the laws of the land, causing widespread suffering. Eventually, he is overthrown and killed by Han Zhuo, ending his tyrannical reign.

In scattered passages, Yi is accused of ravishing the goddess Luo Pin and shooting her husband, the river god He Bo, in the eye. These acts are presented as transgressive and sacrilegious, marking Yi as a figure whose power extends even against divine beings but whose moral compass is deeply flawed.

What We Don't Know

Several gaps remain. The exact wording and line-by-line context of some classical passages (especially those concerning Luo Pin and He Bo) are known primarily through secondary scholarship rather than direct consultation of critical editions in this research pass. Visual and ritual evidence—such as specific temple murals, statues, and local cult practices dedicated to Hou Yi—were not systematically surveyed and would require targeted fieldwork or specialized art-historical databases. The reconstruction of a unified "Epic of Hou Yi" is hypothetical and cannot be verified by a surviving continuous text. Detailed page and line references for all modern translations of Huainanzi and Chu ci are not provided here due to edition variability. This dossier relies mainly on accessible English-language and some Chinese-language scholarship up to August 2026 and does not exhaust regional folk variants or contemporary popular retellings in all Chinese dialects.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Hou Yi as a mythic sniper whose target is not just monsters but the balance of the cosmos—and sometimes, disastrously, other people’s lives. His bow is the instrument that can cool a burning planet, but it is also the weapon that can topple dynasties and wound gods. When you picture him drawing the string, you’re watching a culture wrestle with the double-edged nature of power: the same skill that saves the world can, in the wrong moment, ruin it.
