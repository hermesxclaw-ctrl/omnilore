Who It Is

Before the Romans had gods of love and war, they had a god of 'sweeping bad stuff away'. His name was Averruncus, and he was the divine equivalent of a spiritual janitor, a cosmic 'delete' button for misfortune. He had no face, no myths, and no temple—just a job: to avert evil. In a world full of capricious deities, Averruncus was the one you called when you just wanted things to not go wrong.

Averruncus is best understood not as a character but as a divine function. His 'behavior' is his action: he averts evil, misfortune, and calamity. He is reactive, a protector who acts when called upon. He has no known myths, no loves, no wars, and no personality quirks.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name is derived from the Latin verb 'averruncare', meaning 'to avert' or 'to ward off'. This verb itself is a combination of 'ab' (away) and 'verrere' (to sweep or brush). Thus, Averruncus is literally 'the Averter' or 'He Who Sweeps Away' (misfortune).

Pronunciation — /ˌævəˈrʌŋkəs/ (av-ə-RUNK-əs). The 'u' is pronounced as in 'rung', and the stress falls on the second syllable.

Aliases — Averruncus Deus, Deus Averruncus, Averruncus (Roman deity)

Ownership — Roman religion, specifically the di indigetes, a class of highly specialized, functional deities who presided over specific aspects of daily life and agricultural cycles. Averruncu

Powers
Averruncatio (The Power to Avert)

The singular and total power of Averruncus is to avert evil, misfortune, and calamity. This is a broad-spectrum apotropaic function. He could be called upon to prevent anything bad from happening, from a poor harvest to a military defeat to a personal illness.

Limit: His power was entirely dependent on being invoked. He was not an omnipresent guardian but a specialized tool to be used in times of need. His power was also likely seen as temporary, requiring repeated invocation.

Protection from Agricultural Blight

Given the agrarian nature of early Roman society, a primary function of Averruncus would have been to protect crops from blight, rust, and other agricultural disasters. This is an inference from his general function and the context of Roman religious practice.

Limit: This is a specialized application of his general power, not a separate ability.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No physical description of Averruncus survives. He was almost certainly aniconic (not represented in human or animal form). Like many of the di indigetes, he was a numen, a divine force or will, rather than a personified god with a mythology. He was a function, not a personality.

Symbols — He has no known symbols or iconography. His 'symbol' is his name, which was itself a prayer. The act of naming him was the act of invoking his power.

Domains — His domain is the abstract realm of misfortune and evil. He was invoked to ward off everything from crop blight and disease to general bad luck and evil influences. He was a 'catch-all' apotropaic deity, residing not in a specific temple but in the ritual invocation itself.
