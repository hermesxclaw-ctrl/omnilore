Who It Is

In the shadow of Mount Olympus, where the gods played dice with mortal lives, there was a name whispered with reverence and fear: Alexandra. But she was not a goddess you would find in a temple of her own. She was Hera, the queen of heaven, wearing a mask of protection over a heart of fire. To the Spartans of Laconia, she was the shield that guarded their city. To the Trojans, she was the storm that would bring their walls crashing down. And to a cursed princess of Troy, she was a name that promised safety but delivered only doom. Alexandra is a name that has meant many things to many people—a protector, a prophetess, a queen, a curse. And in every telling, she reminds us that the line between guardian and destroyer is thinner than a thread of fate.

As Hera Alexandra, the goddess would embody the protective and queenly aspects of Hera—guarding cities, protecting women, and overseeing marriage and childbirth. Her behavior in myth reflects the jealous and vindictive nature of Hera, but the Alexandra epithet specifically emphasizes her role as a defender.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Alexandra is the feminine form of Alexander, derived from the Greek Ἀλέξανδρος (Aléxandros), meaning 'defender of men' or 'protector of mankind'. The name is composed of ἀλέξω (aléxō), meaning 'to defend' or 'to protect', and ἀνήρ (anḗr), meaning 'man' or 'person'.

Powers
Divine Protection

As 'protector of men', Alexandra would possess the power to shield cities and individuals from harm, a function attributed to Hera in her civic role.

Limit: The scope of this protection is not defined in surviving sources; it is inferred from the epithet and general Hera mythology.

Queenship and Authority

As Hera, Alexandra wields authority over the gods and mortals, with power to influence fate, punish transgressors, and oversee oaths.

Limit: Her power is subordinate to Zeus, her husband and king of the gods.

Prophecy (via Cassandra association)

If the name is associated with Cassandra/Alexandra, prophetic abilities are implied, though this is a separate figure from Hera.

Limit: This is a conflation of two distinct figures; the prophetic power belongs to Cassandra, not Hera.

Form & Signs

Appearance — As an epithet of Hera, Alexandra would share the iconography of the queen of the gods: a matronly woman of regal bearing, often depicted with a crown or diadem, holding a scepter or pomegranate. In her protective aspect, she might be shown with a spear or shield, though this is speculative. No independent visual representation of 'Alexandra' as a distinct deity exists.

Symbols — As Hera: the diadem, scepter, pomegranate, peacock, and cow. The epithet Alexandra might add martial symbols such as a spear or shield, though these are not explicitly attested.

Domains — Protection of cities and people, queenship, marriage, childbirth, and the domestic sphere. The epithet 'Alexandra' suggests a martial or protective dimension, aligning with Hera's role as a civic guardian.

Accounts I
The Trojan War and the Judgment of ParisHomer, Iliad; various Greek tragedies

c. 8th century BCE (Homer)

Paris, also called Alexander, judges a beauty contest between Hera, Athena, and Aphrodite. He chooses Aphrodite, earning Hera's undying enmity. Hera's wrath fuels the Trojan War, and she actively aids the Greeks against Troy. The name Alexandra, as an epithet of Hera, may reflect her role as a protector of the Greeks, though this is not explicit in the Iliad. The war devastates Troy and leads to the deaths of countless heroes, including Hector and Achilles. Hera's vindictiveness is a central theme of the epic.

Accounts II
Hera's Worship in LaconiaPausanias, Description of Greece

c. 2nd century CE

Pausanias records the worship of Hera Alexandra in Laconia, a region associated with the Spartans. The epithet suggests a local cult that emphasized Hera's protective role, possibly linked to military defense or civic stability. The cult reflects the local adaptation of a Panhellenic deity to regional needs.

Cassandra as AlexandraVarious Greek tragedies, including Aeschylus's Agamemnon

c. 5th century BCE

In some traditions, the Trojan princess Cassandra is given the name Alexandra. She is cursed by Apollo to speak true prophecies that no one believes. Her role as a prophetess who foresees the fall of Troy and her own death is a tragic counterpoint to the protective connotation of the name. Cassandra's prophecies go unheeded, leading to the destruction of Troy and her own murder by Clytemnestra.

Alexandra is a standalone goddess in Greek mythology. — Alexandra is an epithet of Hera, not an independent deity. The name is also used for Cassandra in some traditions, but there is no separate goddess named Alexandra.

Hera is simply a jealous, spiteful goddess. — While Hera's jealousy is a prominent theme in myth, she is also a protector of women, marriage, and cities. Her epithet Alexandra highlights this protective aspect, which is often overshadowed in popular culture.

The name Alexandra is exclusively associated with Alexander the Great. — The name predates Alexander the Great by centuries and has been used for numerous historical and mythological figures, including Hera, Cassandra, and various queens and saints.
