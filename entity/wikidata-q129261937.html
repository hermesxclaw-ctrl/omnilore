Form

As an epithet of Hera, Alexandra would share the iconography of the queen of the gods: a matronly woman of regal bearing, often depicted with a crown or diadem, holding a scepter or pomegranate. In her protective aspect, she might be shown with a spear or shield, though this is speculative. No independent visual representation of 'Alexandra' as a distinct deity exists.

Powers

Divine Protection As 'protector of men', Alexandra would possess the power to shield cities and individuals from harm, a function attributed to Hera in her civic role. Attested in inscriptions and Pausanias's account of Laconian worship The scope of this protection is not defined in surviving sources; it is inferred from the epithet and general Hera mythology. None directly attested; the protective aspect is inferred from the name's meaning.

Queenship and Authority As Hera, Alexandra wields authority over the gods and mortals, with power to influence fate, punish transgressors, and oversee oaths. Attested across Greek mythology Her power is subordinate to Zeus, her husband and king of the gods. Hera's authority is often challenged by Zeus and other gods, creating contradictions in her perceived power.

Prophecy (via Cassandra association) If the name is associated with Cassandra/Alexandra, prophetic abilities are implied, though this is a separate figure from Hera. Hellenistic and Roman sources distinguish Cassandra from Hera This is a conflation of two distinct figures; the prophetic power belongs to Cassandra, not Hera. The identification of Cassandra as Alexandra is a naming variant, not a divine aspect.

Behavior & Habitat

As Hera Alexandra, the goddess would embody the protective and queenly aspects of Hera—guarding cities, protecting women, and overseeing marriage and childbirth. Her behavior in myth reflects the jealous and vindictive nature of Hera, but the Alexandra epithet specifically emphasizes her role as a defender.

Accounts

Paris, also called Alexander, judges a beauty contest between Hera, Athena, and Aphrodite. He chooses Aphrodite, earning Hera's undying enmity. Hera's wrath fuels the Trojan War, and she actively aids the Greeks against Troy. The name Alexandra, as an epithet of Hera, may reflect her role as a protector of the Greeks, though this is not explicit in the Iliad.

Pausanias records the worship of Hera Alexandra in Laconia, a region associated with the Spartans. The epithet suggests a local cult that emphasized Hera's protective role, possibly linked to military defense or civic stability.

In some traditions, the Trojan princess Cassandra is given the name Alexandra. She is cursed by Apollo to speak true prophecies that no one believes. Her role as a prophetess who foresees the fall of Troy and her own death is a tragic counterpoint to the protective connotation of the name.

Content Notes

Hera's wrath against Paris and the Trojans leads to a decade-long war marked by slaughter, rape, and the destruction of a civilization. Her vengeance is disproportionate and cruel, reflecting the darker aspects of Greek divine morality.

Scholars note that Hera's vindictiveness is a literary device that drives the plot of the Iliad, but it also reflects ancient Greek anxieties about divine power and the capriciousness of the gods.

Homer, Iliad; various tragedies

Cassandra, as Alexandra, is cursed by Apollo to prophesy truth without being believed. Her knowledge of the future is a burden, and her warnings are ignored, leading to her death and the fall of Troy.

The curse is a punishment for rejecting Apollo's advances, a theme that raises questions about consent, divine power, and the silencing of women's voices in ancient Greek society.

Scholarly Debates
Identity of Alexandra
Alexandra is an epithet of Hera, emphasizing her protective role. ⇌ Alexandra is a name for Cassandra, the Trojan prophetess.
Evidence for A: Inscriptions and Pausanias reference Hera Alexandra in Laconia.
Evidence for B: Later Greek sources and scholia use Alexandra for Cassandra.
Hera's Role in the Trojan War
Hera is a vengeful, petty goddess who punishes Paris for his judgment. ⇌ Hera is a protector of the Greeks and a champion of civilized order against the barbaric Trojans.
Evidence for A: Homer's Iliad depicts Hera's wrath as personal and vindictive.
Evidence for B: Hera's epithet Alexandra suggests a protective role, and her support of the Greeks aligns with her civic function.
The Name 'Alexander' and 'Alexandra'
The name is of Greek origin, meaning 'defender of men'. ⇌ The name may have pre-Greek origins, possibly related to Hittite or Luwian words for 'protector'.
Evidence for A: The Greek etymology is widely accepted.
Evidence for B: Some scholars propose a Near Eastern origin, though evidence is speculative.
What We Don't Know

The primary gap is the lack of a dedicated cult or myth for Alexandra as a distinct deity. The evidence is limited to inscriptions and brief mentions in Pausanias. The association with Cassandra is a naming variant, not a divine identity. Further research into Laconian epigraphy and local cult practices could shed light on the specific rituals associated with Hera Alexandra. The modern reception of Alexandra as a deity is essentially nonexistent; the name is more commonly associated with historical figures.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Alexandra not as a single character, but as a title—like 'The Protector.' In one story, it's the name of a queen of the gods who will burn your city to the ground if you cross her. In another, it's the name of a princess who sees the future and can't stop it. It's a name that promises safety but often delivers tragedy. If you met Alexandra in a modern story, she'd be the one who says 'I'll keep you safe' right before everything goes wrong—because she's not just a protector, she's a force of nature, and nature doesn't make promises it can't break.
