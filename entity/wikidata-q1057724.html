The Approach

In the shadow of Balor of the Evil Eye, there stood a prophetess whose warnings were as sharp as her javelin—Cethlenn of the Crooked Teeth, a Fomorian queen who foresaw her people's doom and fought to the last.

Content advisory. Contains mythological violence and descriptions of battle wounds.
Identity

Cethlenn

Pronunciation Modern Irish Ceithlinn is pronounced approximately 'Kehlen'.

Etymology

The name Cethlenn is of uncertain etymology. It appears in Old Irish as Cethlenn, Cethleann, Ceithlenn, or Ceithlionn. The nickname 'Chaisfhiaclach' means 'crooked-toothed' from cas 'twisted' and fhiaclach 'toothed'. The variant 'Chraos-Fhiaclach' may mean 'ravening tooth' or 'gaping tooth'.

Cultural Ownership

Irish mythology, specifically the Fomorian tradition.

Disputed Identifications

Some local traditions associate Cethlenn with the naming of Enniskillen, but this is not universally accepted. The exact nature of her role as Balor's wife is stated only in later sources.

Description

Appearance

Cethlenn is described as having crooked or twisted teeth, earning her the nickname 'of the Crooked Teeth'. No other physical description is given in the sources.

Symbols & Iconography

No specific symbols or iconography are associated with Cethlenn in the sources.

Behaviour & Personality

She is portrayed as a prophetess and a warrior. She actively participates in battle, wounding the Dagda. Her prophecy to Balor shows her as a voice of warning.

Domains & Habitats

As a Fomorian, she is associated with the sea and the otherworldly realm of the Fomorians. She is also linked to the area around Enniskillen in local legend.

Version Differences

In the Lebor Gabála Érenn, she is not explicitly called Balor's wife, but later sources like Ogygia state this. The nickname varies: 'Chaisfhiaclach' in Oidheadh Chloinne Tuireann, 'Chraos-Fhiaclach' in Bruidhean Chaorthainn.

Stories & Myths

The Second Battle of Mag Tuired

Source Lebor Gabála ÉrennCulture IrishPeriod Mythological

Summary

Cethlenn fought against the Tuatha Dé Danann and wounded the Dagda with a javelin. The wound eventually caused his death after 120 years.

Participants

Cethlenn

Dagda

Tuatha Dé Danann

Fomorians

Consequences

The Dagda's death was delayed, but the Fomorians were defeated.

Variants

The story is repeated in later annals and histories with minor variations.

The Prophecy of Lug's Arrival

Source Oidheadh Chloinne TuireannCulture IrishPeriod Early modern

Summary

Cethlenn identifies Lug as her grandson and warns Balor that the Fomorians' days are numbered once Lug comes to Ireland.

Participants

Cethlenn

Balor

Lug

Consequences

The prophecy is fulfilled when Lug kills Balor.

Variants

Arthur C. L. Brown notes a folktale variant where Lug uses a spear made by Gavnin Gow, rather than a sling stone.

The Legend of Enniskillen

Source Local tradition, Annals of ClonmacnoiseCulture IrishPeriod Early modern to modern

Summary

According to local lore, Cethlenn was injured and swam to Enniskillen (Innis Cethlenn) where she died. The town is said to be named after her.

Participants

Cethlenn

Consequences

The naming of Enniskillen and a prophecy about the island flooding.

Variants

The Annals of Clonmacnoise mention the eponym but not the swimming. Later local historians elaborated.

Contradictions & Disputes

Role as Balor's wife

Position A

Cethlenn is Balor's wife.

Position A — evidence

Ogygia (1685) states she is his wife.

Position B

Cethlenn is not explicitly named as Balor's wife in the earliest sources.

Position B — evidence

Lebor Gabála Érenn does not call her his wife.

Scholarly Consensus

Scholars generally accept her as Balor's wife based on later sources.

Death of Cethlenn

Position A

Cethlenn fell in battle.

Position A — evidence

John O'Mahony's commentary suggests she fell in battle.

Position B

Cethlenn swam to Enniskillen and died there.

Position B — evidence

Local legend and the Annals of Clonmacnoise imply she died at Enniskillen.

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus; the local legend is not corroborated by early sources.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

The etymology of the name Cethlenn is uncertain. The exact circumstances of her death are not attested in early sources. The legend of her swimming to Enniskillen is not found in early medieval sources. Modern adaptations and appearances beyond the noted ones are not surveyed. The pronunciation is based on modern Irish and may not reflect Old Irish.

Research Scope

This dossier focuses on the attested sources for Cethlenn in Irish mythology, including early modern texts and local traditions. It does not cover modern adaptations beyond noted appearances.
