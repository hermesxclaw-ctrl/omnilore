Who It Is

In the long genealogical chants that open the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki, hundreds of kami flash by in a single line — a name, a parent, a place — and then vanish. Tahiriki-Shimarumi-no-Kami is one of those vanishing names: attested in Japanese source-lists as a discrete kami, but without a surviving myth, image, or shrine narrative in readily accessible English-language scholarship. To understand such a figure is to understand how Shinto remembers — not only through dramatic stories, but through lists, etymologies, and the quiet persistence of a name in a shrine register.

No narrative episode survives in accessible translation that would allow characterization of motives, personality, speech, or moral alignment. In the Kojiki/Nihon Shoki genealogical lists, many kami appear solely as names linking generations, without behavioral description. Any personality ascribed would be reconstruction or adaptation-specific invention. Status: unknown / unattested.

Izanagi and Izanami, the primordial pair, generate the Japanese archipelago (Kuni-umi) and then generate a host of kami (Kamiumi) representing natural phenomena, settlements, and crafts. Dozens of kami are named in rapid succession, often with only a parentage note. Tahiriki-Shimarumi-no-Kami, if attested, would belong to this enumerative layer: a name preserving a clan or place affiliation rather than a plotted episode. No specific action (speech, combat, transformation) is recorded for this kami in accessible translations of these chapters. The consequence of this enumerative technique is the creation of a divine genealogy that legitimates Yamato rule and links imperial and clan ancestors to the cosmogony. Establishment of the divine genealogy from which Amaterasu, Susanoo, and the imperial ancestor Ninigi descend. Minor names like Tahiriki-Shimarumi-no-Kami, if present, serve as connective tissue in this genealogy; their absence from narrative does not indicate insignificance in the original ritual-political context.

Beyond cosmogony, the texts preserve extended genealogies: e.g., the descendants of Susanoo, the lineage of Okuninushi, the ancestry of Ninigi. In these lists, a kami may appear only as 'X begat Y' or 'X is enshrined at Z.' For example, Kojiki 1:20-30 contains sequences like 'Next he begat...' followed by 5-10 names without further elaboration. The Munakata Hypothesis - Possible Affiliation with Sea and Island GoddessesKojiki 1:13 (birth of Munakata Sanjojin from Amaterasu and Susanoo's oath); Nihon Shoki 1:9 variant; Engishiki Jinmyocho Munakata shrine entries

Mythic oath episode (Kamiyo), compiled 8th century; enshrinement at Munakata Taisha (Fukuoka) from at least 8th century onward

During the oath (ukehi) between Amaterasu and Susanoo, three goddesses are born from Amaterasu's jewels: Tagirihime (also read Tahiriki-hime), Ichikishimahime, and Tagitsuhime - the Munakata goddesses, protectors of sea routes. The phonetic match Tahiriki ~ Tagiri is striking. If Tahiriki-Shimarumi-no-Kami is a variant reading, conflation, or compound of Tagirihime with a second element (Shimarumi, perhaps Ichikishima?), the 'story' would be the oath episode and subsequent enshrinement on Okinoshima, Oshima, and Hetsumiya. However, NO source located explicitly equates Tahiriki-Shimarumi-no-Kami with Tagirihime or Ichikishimahime. This hypothesis is presented as a research lead, not as fact. The consequence of conflation would be misattribution of a major sea-goddess narrative to an obscure name. Protection of maritime travel between Yamato and Korea; major shrine complex (UNESCO World Heritage Okinoshima). Misidentification would inflate this obscure entry's importance.
