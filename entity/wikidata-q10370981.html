Who It Is

In the shadowed corners of Japanese folklore, where the veil between the human and the supernatural grows thin, there exists a creature of haunting beauty and peril—the Kejōrō. Neither fully woman nor beast, she is said to lure the unwary with an enchanting song, only to reveal her true, monstrous nature. Yet, her story is not merely a cautionary tale; it is a mirror reflecting the fears and fascinations of a culture that has long danced with the spirits of the otherworld.

The Kejōrō is said to be a deceptive creature, using her appearance and voice to lure men, especially travelers and priests, to their doom. She may sing or call out for help, only to attack when the victim approaches. Some stories suggest she is a vengeful spirit (onryō) of a woman wronged in life, while others portray her as a demonic predator.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Kejōrō' (毛女郎) combines 'ke' (毛, hair) and 'jōrō' (女郎, courtesan or prostitute), literally meaning 'hairy woman' or 'hairy courtesan'.

Pronunciation — keh-JOH-roh

Aliases — Kejoro, Ke-Jōrō, Hairy Courtesan

Illusion and Deception

The Kejōrō can appear as a beautiful, alluring woman to attract victims, hiding her true hairy form.

Limit: The illusion may be broken if the victim sees her reflection or gets close enough to notice her hair.

Enchanting Voice

She may sing or call out in a seductive or pitiful manner to lure victims.

Limit: Not mentioned in all accounts.

Supernatural Strength

Some tales imply that the Kejōrō possesses great physical strength to overpower her victims.

Limit: Not explicitly stated.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The Kejōrō is typically described as a woman with long, wild hair covering her entire body, obscuring her features. She may appear as a beautiful woman from a distance, but upon closer inspection, her body is covered in thick, coarse hair, and her face may be distorted or animalistic. Some accounts describe her as having sharp claws or fangs.

Symbols — In ukiyo-e and yōkai art, the Kejōrō is often depicted as a woman with exaggerated, flowing hair, sometimes shown in a seductive pose. The hair symbolizes both her allure and her monstrous nature.

Domains — Kejōrō are often associated with remote, mountainous regions, forests, and abandoned roads, where they prey on lone travelers. They are also linked to waterways, such as rivers and bridges.

Accounts I
The Priest and the Hairy WomanOral tradition

Unknown, likely Edo period or earlier

A traveling priest encounters a beautiful woman weeping by the roadside. She claims to be lost and asks for his help. As he approaches, he notices her hands are covered in hair. Realizing she is a yōkai, he recites a sutra, causing her to flee into the forest. The priest escapes, and the Kejōrō is repelled by the power of Buddhist scripture.

The Fisherman's EncounterLocal legend

Unknown

A fisherman hears a woman singing from a riverbank at night. He rows closer, but sees that the singer is covered in hair and has glowing eyes. He quickly rows away, and the creature dives into the water. The fisherman escapes, and the Kejōrō disappears.
