The Approach

In the shadowed corners of Japanese folklore, where the veil between the human and the supernatural grows thin, there exists a creature of haunting beauty and peril—the Kejōrō. Neither fully woman nor beast, she is said to lure the unwary with an enchanting song, only to reveal her true, monstrous nature. Yet, her story is not merely a cautionary tale; it is a mirror reflecting the fears and fascinations of a culture that has long danced with the spirits of the otherworld.

Content advisory. This entry contains descriptions of supernatural predation, violence, and themes of deception and death. Reader discretion is advised.
Identity

Kejōrō

Pronunciation keh-JOH-roh

Known As

KejoroKe-JōrōHairy Courtesan

Etymology

The name 'Kejōrō' (毛女郎) combines 'ke' (毛, hair) and 'jōrō' (女郎, courtesan or prostitute), literally meaning 'hairy woman' or 'hairy courtesan'.

Cultural Ownership

Japanese

Disputed Identifications

Some sources conflate Kejōrō with other hairy yōkai such as the 'Kechō' or 'Kemushi-baba', but these are likely distinct entities. The exact lineage and classification of Kejōrō remain unclear, with some scholars suggesting it may be a regional variant of the 'Nō-peri' or a type of 'Kijo' (demon woman).

Description

Appearance

The Kejōrō is typically described as a woman with long, wild hair covering her entire body, obscuring her features. She may appear as a beautiful woman from a distance, but upon closer inspection, her body is covered in thick, coarse hair, and her face may be distorted or animalistic. Some accounts describe her as having sharp claws or fangs.

Symbols & Iconography

In ukiyo-e and yōkai art, the Kejōrō is often depicted as a woman with exaggerated, flowing hair, sometimes shown in a seductive pose. The hair symbolizes both her allure and her monstrous nature.

Behaviour & Personality

The Kejōrō is said to be a deceptive creature, using her appearance and voice to lure men, especially travelers and priests, to their doom. She may sing or call out for help, only to attack when the victim approaches. Some stories suggest she is a vengeful spirit (onryō) of a woman wronged in life, while others portray her as a demonic predator.

Domains & Habitats

Kejōrō are often associated with remote, mountainous regions, forests, and abandoned roads, where they prey on lone travelers. They are also linked to waterways, such as rivers and bridges.

Version Differences

There is little documented variation, but some regional tales describe the Kejōrō as a shape-shifter who can appear as a beautiful woman, while others emphasize her grotesque, hairy appearance as immediate. In some versions, she is a single entity; in others, she is a type of yōkai.

Stories & Myths

The Priest and the Hairy Woman

Source Oral traditionCulture JapanesePeriod Unknown, likely Edo period or earlier

Summary

A traveling priest encounters a beautiful woman weeping by the roadside. She claims to be lost and asks for his help. As he approaches, he notices her hands are covered in hair. Realizing she is a yōkai, he recites a sutra, causing her to flee into the forest.

Participants

A priest

A Kejōrō

Consequences

The priest escapes, and the Kejōrō is repelled by the power of Buddhist scripture.

Variants

In some versions, the priest is not so lucky and is devoured.

The Fisherman's Encounter

Source Local legendCulture JapanesePeriod Unknown

Summary

A fisherman hears a woman singing from a riverbank at night. He rows closer, but sees that the singer is covered in hair and has glowing eyes. He quickly rows away, and the creature dives into the water.

Participants

A fisherman

A Kejōrō

Consequences

The fisherman escapes, and the Kejōrō disappears.

Variants

Some versions say the creature is a river spirit, not a Kejōrō.

Possible origin as vengeful spirit

Source Folklore analysis

Description

Some interpretations suggest the Kejōrō may be the ghost of a woman who suffered betrayal or abuse, becoming a monster in death. This adds a tragic dimension to the creature.

Scholarly Context

Similar to other onryō figures, the Kejōrō may embody the consequences of societal mistreatment of women.
