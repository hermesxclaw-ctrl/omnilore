Who It Is

Before the Guarani people had a written language, they had a name for the force that lit the sky, shaped the first humans from clay, and set the cosmic balance between a good spirit and an evil one. That name was Tupã. He is not the Christian God wearing indigenous clothing, nor a generic thunder deity borrowed from better-documented pantheons. He is the architect of light in one of South America's most persistent living mythologies, and his decisions still echo in the monsters that stalk Paraguayan oral tradition and in the star map above the southern hemisphere.

As a creator-god, Tupã is characterized by intentional, ordered demiurgy: he fashions light, separates realms, and sets cosmic polarity in motion. He is not a capricious trickster; his behavior follows a generative logic. Some traditions portray him as distant after creation, while others keep him actively present in sunlight and sacred fire.

Powers
Cosmic creation

Tupã is credited with the formation of the universe, the earth, and the celestial bodies. In Guarani narratives he fashions light itself, establishing day and the solar order. This is not a subordinate demiurgical act but the foundational creative power of the supreme deity.

Limit: None attested in indigenous sources; he is the uncaused first cause.

Human formation

Tupã shapes the first humans from clay, following a widespread South American anthropogonic motif. This act establishes the relationship between humanity and the earth, with Tupã as both potter and life-giver.

Limit: The exact process varies by oral tradition; no single fixed ritual or text governs the narrative.

Generation of moral polarity

Tupã creates Angatupyry (the spirit of good) and Tau (the spirit of evil), thereby establishing the fundamental moral duality of Guarani cosmology. This is a deliberate creative act, not an accidental overflow.

Limit: The balance of power between the two spirits is disputed: some versions depict Tau as an equal rival, others as a subordinate corruption.

Solar/celestial lordship

Tupã's residence is the Sun, and he governs the diurnal sky. Light is both his creation and his medium, making sunlight an epiphany of the divine.

Limit: Limited evidence for specific solar cult practices; much was destroyed by missionization.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Tupã is rarely described with fixed anthropomorphic iconography in surviving sources. Where depicted, he is associated with solar radiance, fire, and celestial light rather than a stable human-like form. Colonial-era missionary texts sometimes framed him as a bearded white man, but these are European projections. Indigenous representations emphasize his role as a creative force rather than a portrait subject.

Symbols — Sun, light, fire, clay, and the cosmic egg are the dominant symbolic motifs tied to Tupã. The sun is explicitly named as his residence, and creation-from-clay motifs link him to the first humans. No standardized iconographic canon exists because Tupi-Guarani traditions favored oral and ritual transmission over monumental imagery.

Domains — Creation, light, the Sun, celestial realms, thunder (by later association with the Portuguese term for thunder god). He dwells in or as the Sun and governs the primordial ordering of the universe.

Accounts I
The creation of light and the ordered cosmosGuarani creation myth (oral tradition documented by missionaries and ethnographers)

Pre-Columbian, transmitted through colonial and modern oral tradition

In the beginning there was only darkness and chaos. Tupã spoke or acted to create light, separating day from night and ordering the sky. He placed the Sun as his dwelling and the lamp of the world. This act established the basic architecture of the cosmos: heavens above, earth below, and light as the governing principle. A structured cosmos in which humanity could later emerge; the Sun became the visible throne of the creator.

The fashioning of humanity from clayGuarani creation myth (ethnographic record)

Pre-Columbian

After ordering light and the sky, Tupã formed the first humans from clay, breathing life into them. This established humanity's clay-nature and its dependence on the creator. The act is not a one-time accident but the deliberate inauguration of the human race. Humanity enters the world with a material link to the earth (clay) and a spiritual link to the creator (breath/life).

Accounts II
The birth of Angatupyry and Tau and the moral orderGuarani creation myth; corroborated by Tau (mythology) article

Pre-Columbian

Tupã generated two complementary spirits: Angatupyry, the embodiment of goodness, and Tau, the embodiment of evil. He placed them among humanity, leaving Tau on Earth with humans while Angatupyry remained in the celestial realm or walked among the righteous. This established a dualistic moral geography that is central to Guarani ethics. Good and evil became cosmological forces with independent agency; humans were placed in the middle, subject to both. Tau's later rape of Kerana and the curse on his descendants are direct outgrowths of this moral polarity.

Tau's violation of Kerana and Arasy's curseGuarani myth cycle, documented through ethnographic compilation

Pre-Columbian

Tau, the evil spirit created by Tupã, raped Kerana, a mortal or divine woman. The high goddess Arasy punished Tau with a curse: all of his descendants with Kerana would be monstrous, deformed, and dangerous. The seven legendary monsters of Guarani myth—Teju Jagua, Ao Ao, Mbói Tu'ĩ, Moñái, Kurupi, Jasy Jatere, and Luison—are the cursed offspring of this union. The Guarani monster pantheon is born from a single act of cosmic violation, linking the origin of evil directly to the misdeeds of Tau and the justice of Arasy.
