Who It Is

Kū (or Kū-ka-ili-moku, 'Kū the Land-Snatcher') is the Hawaiian god of war, politics, and governance — the fierce, masculine force that protects and expands the kingdom. He is one of the four major akua and was the patron deity of King Kamehameha I, who unified the Hawaiian Islands under a single rule in the late 18th and early 19th centuries. Every major Hawaiian temple (heiau luakini) dedicated to Kū required human sacrifice, and the rituals were conducted by the powerful kahuna (priestly) class under the king's authority. Kamehameha I built the massive Pu'ukoholā Heiau on the Big Island, dedicated to Kū, as part of his campaign to unify the islands. Kū's sacred images (ki'i) are among the most iconic surviving examples of Hawaiian wood carving — large, fierce-faced figures with snarling mouths that are now preserved in museums worldwide (notably the British Museum and the Bishop Museum). However, Kū is not merely a god of violence; his name means 'to stand upright,' and he is also associated with governance, law, building, and the masculine responsibility to protect family and community. Different forms of Kū (kinolau) govern different aspects of male activity, from deep-sea fishing (Kū'ula-kai) to woodworking (Kū-mauna) to sorcery (Kū-waha-ilo). After the overthrow of the kapu system in 1819 under Liholiho (Kamehameha II), the worship of Kū was largely abandoned, though his carvings and legacy survive as powerful symbols of Hawaiian sovereignty and warrior tradition.
