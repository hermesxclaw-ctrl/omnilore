Who It Is

In the green mountains of the Basque Country, where ancient oaks stand sentinel and storms roll off the Bay of Biscay, there is a lady who commands the weather and the very rocks of the earth. She is Mari, the supreme goddess of Basque mythology, a figure of immense power and caprice, whose favor can bring life-giving rain or devastating hail. Her abode is the cave of Anboto, a jagged peak that pierces the clouds, and from there she presides over a world of spirits, shepherds, and the forces of nature. To meet her is to face the untamed heart of the Basque land itself.

Mari is a powerful and capricious being. She is often described as just but severe, rewarding those who respect her and punishing those who offend her. She can be generous, granting gifts of wealth or knowledge, but also vengeful, causing storms, hail, or other misfortunes. She is associated with fertility and abundance, but also with destruction. She is a guardian of nature and the supernatural order, and she interacts with humans, especially shepherds, who may encounter her on mountains.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Mari' is of uncertain etymology. It may derive from the Basque word 'mari' meaning 'lady' or 'mistress', or from the Christian name 'Maria' (Mary), reflecting syncretism with the Virgin Mary. Some scholars suggest a pre-Christian origin linked to a mother goddess figure.

Powers
Weather Control

Mari can cause storms, hail, rain, wind, and lightning. She is often seen as a ball of fire or light that travels between mountains, and this is associated with weather changes.

Limit: She is not omniscient; her powers are localized to her domains and she may be bound by certain rules, such as not entering churches.

Shapeshifting

She can transform into various animals, including a bull, a ram, a goat, a horse, a crow, a vulture, and a serpent. She can also appear as a ball of fire or a beautiful woman.

Limit: Her transformations may be limited by her location or the time of day; some forms are more common in certain regions.

Flight

She can fly through the air, often as a ball of fire or light, traveling between her mountain abodes.

Limit: None specified.

Bestowing Gifts and Curses

She can grant wealth, knowledge, or good fortune to those who please her, and bring misfortune, illness, or death to those who offend her.

Limit: Her gifts may come with conditions or be ambiguous.

Knowledge of the Future

She is sometimes said to know the future and can reveal it to chosen individuals.

Limit: She rarely shares this knowledge openly.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Mari is typically described as a beautiful woman, often dressed in a long green or red gown, sometimes with golden hair. She may appear as a lady of noble bearing, but she can also transform into various animal forms, including a bull, a ram, a goat, a horse, a crow, a vulture, or a ball of fire. Symbols — Her primary symbols are the cave, the mountain, the weather (especially storms and hail), the rainbow, the ram (as her animal form), the comb, and the sickle. She is associated with the element of fire, as she often appears as a ball of fire or a flame. Her dwelling places are caves in mountains, most notably Anboto, but also others like Aralar, Gaztelu, and Murumendi.

Domains — Her primary domain is the mountains and caves of the Basque Country. She is a goddess of nature, weather, and the earth. She is also associated with the home and domestic life, as she sometimes appears in human dwellings. She is connected to the underworld, as her caves are seen as entrances to a subterranean realm.

Mari and the ShepherdBarandiaran's folklore collections

Unknown; oral tradition collected in 20th century

A shepherd encounters Mari in a cave. She asks him to help her with a task, such as combing her hair or holding her child. If he does so respectfully, she rewards him with gold or a magical object. If he refuses or acts rudely, she punishes him with storms or by leading him astray. The shepherd's fortune changes based on his behavior, teaching a moral lesson about respect for the supernatural.

Mari and SugaarBasque folklore

Unknown

Mari is married to Sugaar, a serpent or dragon-like being. They often quarrel, and their disputes cause storms and tempests. When they meet, they may cause violent weather. In some accounts, they are separated, and Mari lives in Anboto while Sugaar lives elsewhere. Their union explains the origin of storms and their children are said to be the genii or spirits of the Basque country.

Mari and the PriestBasque folklore

Unknown

A priest attempts to exorcise or banish Mari from her cave, but she resists. In some versions, she throws a stone at the priest, which becomes a landmark. In others, she disappears and reappears elsewhere. The priest fails to subdue her, demonstrating her power over the Christian church.

Mari and the MillBasque folklore

Unknown

Mari is said to visit a mill at night and use the millstone to grind grain. If the miller leaves a gift for her, she grinds it; if not, she may cause damage. The miller's fortune depends on his generosity.

Mari is the same as the Virgin Mary. — While there is syncretism, Mari is a distinct pre-Christian figure. The name may share etymology, but the attributes and stories are different.

Mari is a witch or evil spirit. — Mari is a neutral or benevolent figure in most accounts, though she can punish those who disrespect her. The demonization is a result of Christian reinterpretation.

Mari only lives in Anboto. — Mari is associated with several mountains and caves, including Aralar, Gaztelu, and Murumendi. Anboto is the most famous, but not exclusive.
