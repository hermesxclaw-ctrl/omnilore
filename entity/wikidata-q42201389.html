Who It Is

Hold a flawless topaz to the light and it barely sparkles — until you learn to look. Colorless in the ground, often mistaken for its humbler cousin citrine, this aluminum-fluorine silicate is one of the hardest minerals on Earth, yet it will split cleanly with a single sharp tap. For three thousand years humans have argued over its name, mined it from a legendary island that never held it, irradiated it to an impossible blue, and worn it over the heart to cure madness, fever, and the evil eye.

As a mineral, 'behavior' is physicochemical: forms as an accessory mineral in felsic igneous rocks (especially fluorine-rich granites and pegmatites), sedimentary and hydrothermally altered rocks, and in vapor cavities in rhyolites. Crystal structure alternates sheets of (F,OH)2O and O along (010) with Al3+ in octahedral sites and Si4+ in tetrahedral sites; fluorine can be substituted by hydroxyl up to 30 mol.% in nature; OH-dominant topaz synthesized in labs but not found naturally. Powers
Exceptional Hardness (Mohs 8)

Scratches quartz and most gems; used as abrasive for lower-quality material and as refractory due to high-temperature stability. Only corundum (9) and diamond (10) are harder among common gems.

Limit: Hardness does not equal toughness; perfect cleavage makes topaz brittle and prone to splitting along {001} if struck. Must be treated with greater care than corundum despite similar hardness.

Low Refractive Index and High Polish

RI 1.606-1.644, relatively low for a gem, but takes an exceptionally fine polish producing a slippery feel distinct from quartz. In brilliant cut, light performance is less fiery than high-RI gems.

Limit: Large tables/facets can appear dull or 'well-like' compared to diamond or zircon; requires careful cutting to maximize life.

Color Modification by Treatment

Heat and/or radiation can convert colorless/gray/pale material to deep blue, reddish-orange, pale green, pink, or purple. Chromium substitution naturally creates pink/red.

Limit: Treated colors may fade (notably some imperial pinks in sunlight) and must be disclosed; irradiated blue topaz requires regulatory cooling period for residual radioactivity.

Industrial Refractory and Flux

Used to produce refractory materials for high-temperature environments and as a flux in steel production due to alumina-silica-fluorine composition.

Limit: Calcining topaz releases fluorine gas, posing health and environmental hazards requiring scrubbing and controls.

Alleged Protective and Medicinal Powers (Folklore)

Ancient Romans: protection from danger while traveling. Middle Ages Europe: attached to left arm to protect from curses and evil eye; believed to enhance mental powers. English superstition: cured lunacy. Medieval medical lore: increased body heat to relieve cold/fever. India: worn over heart to grant beauty, intelligence, longevity.

Limit: No scientific evidence; historical beliefs, not pharmacology. Should not be used as medical advice. Variants differ by region and period.

Diagnostic Physical Tests

Specific gravity (~3.53) allows distinction from citrine/quartz (~2.65) by weight-volume or heavy-liquid test (bromoform/methylene iodide: quartz floats, topaz sinks). Perfect cleavage {001} diagnostic.

Limit: Requires unset stone and sensitive scale or heavy liquids with safety precautions.

Trace element impurities and color centers produce pale blue, golden-brown to yellow-orange, wine-red, pale gray, reddish-orange, pale green, and rare pink to violet hues. Accounts I
The Fog-Shrouded Island of TopazosPliny the Elder, Naturalis Historia, Book 37

1st century CE (Flavian Rome), describing earlier Hellenistic tradition

Pliny reports that Topazos is a legendary island in the Red Sea, shrouded in fog and difficult to find, from which a yellow stone called topazios was first mined. Sailors searched for it at night, and the island was said to be guarded or hidden by the gods. The stone was prized for its golden color. Modern mineralogists identify this ancient 'topaz' as chrysolite — yellowish olivine (peridot) from St. John's Island (Zabargad), which indeed produces peridot, not modern topaz. The story explains the etymological transfer of the island's name to a different mineral in 1737. Established the dominant Western etymology for 'topaz' for two millennia; created persistent confusion between ancient and modern topaz in translations and lapidaries; gave St. John's Island its classical name.

Tamraparni: Sri Lanka's Topaz Export to the Ancient MediterraneanClassical geographers (Alexander Polyhistor, Strabo via secondary citation) and archaeological gem trade evidence; Pliny's reference to Topazius

Late Hellenistic to Early Roman, c. 2nd century BCE - 1st century CE

Ancient Sri Lanka, known as Tamraparni, exported yellow gemstones — called topazes in Greek sources — to Greece and Egypt. The island's wealth in gems led foreign geographers to name it Topazius/Topapwene, 'land of topaz.' These gems, shipped via Red Sea ports, were likely yellow sapphires, chrysoberyls, or peridots rather than modern topaz, but they cemented the association between Sri Lanka and the name topaz in Western imagination. Linked Sri Lankan identity to gem trade in classical literature; contributed to the broad medieval use of 'topaz' for any yellow gem; modern Sri Lanka remains a source of alluvial topaz alongside sapphire and chrysoberyl.

The Biblical Pitdah — From Septuagint Topazion to King James TopazHebrew Bible (Exodus 28:17, 28:20; Ezekiel 28:13), Septuagint, King James Version (1611)

Iron Age Hebrew text; LXX 3rd-2nd century BCE; KJV 1611

The High Priest's breastplate (hoshen) includes twelve stones, one named pitdah in Hebrew. The Septuagint translators rendered pitdah as topazion. English translations following the LXX, including the KJV, thus list 'topaz' among the breastplate gems and the foundations of the New Jerusalem (Revelation 21:20). Modern biblical archaeology and mineralogy argue pitdah/topazion was not modern topaz but probably chrysolite/peridot or chrysoberyl, which were available in the Levant and Red Sea region, whereas true topaz was not known there. Embedded 'topaz' in Western religious imagination as a sacred gem; caused centuries of misidentification in sermons, art, and jewelry claiming biblical topaz; modern translations often footnote the uncertainty or render as 'peridot' or 'chrysolite.'

Accounts II
Thomas Nicols and the English Lapidary — Topaz Enters Modern MineralogyThomas Nicols, A Lapidary or History of Gemstones (1652)

Mid-17th century, Commonwealth period

Thomas Nicols, drawing on Pliny, Anselmus de Boodt, and contemporary jewelers, dedicated two chapters to topaz in his systematic treatise. He rehearsed the medieval lore — that topaz cures lunacy, wards curses, and enhances intellect — while attempting to distinguish true topaz from other yellow stones by hardness and weight. His work marks the transition from medieval lapidary magic to early scientific gemology, though he still repeats humoral and astrological claims. The book helped fix the English word 'topaz' in print before the mineral was chemically defined in 1737. Provided a baseline for later mineralogists to refine the definition; preserved medieval folklore for modern study; cited by later gemologists as evidence of pre-modern 'topaz' as a color term, not a species.

Imperial Topaz and the Brazilian Gold RushPortuguese colonial mining records, modern gemological histories of Minas Gerais (Ouro Preto)

18th century (discovery c. 1720s-1760s) to present

Portuguese colonists seeking gold in Minas Gerais discovered alluvial deposits of vivid yellow to pink-orange topaz near Ouro Preto (then Vila Rica). The gems, later called imperial topaz after the Brazilian Empire (1822-1889), became a symbol of imperial luxury; the Braganza crown jewels include large imperial topazes. Mining relied heavily on enslaved African labor under harsh conditions. Today Brazil remains the world's premier source of imperial topaz, with production from Antônio Pereira and Rodrigo Silva deposits; stones are often heat-treated to enhance pink/violet hues, and some fade with prolonged sunlight. Established Brazil as the type locality for gem topaz in the popular mind; created a distinct trade category 'imperial topaz' commanding high prices; left a legacy of colonial extraction and environmental alteration in Minas Gerais.

Blue Topaz, Texas, and the American State Gem MovementTexas Legislature House Concurrent Resolution (1969), Utah Legislature (1969), GIA gem treatment literature

Mid-20th century to present (treatment technology 1950s-1970s; designations 1969)

Naturally occurring blue topaz is rare. In the 1950s-1970s, development of heat treatment and neutron/electron irradiation allowed transformation of abundant colorless topaz (from Brazil, Nigeria, Texas) into vivid Swiss blue and London blue. The gems became affordable birthstone jewelry for December (blue topaz often marketed alongside turquoise and zircon). Lobbying by jewelers and rockhound societies led Texas to designate blue topaz as its state gemstone in 1969 (Mason County topaz), and Utah to designate topaz (precious topaz) as its state gemstone the same year (Topaz Mountain). The 4th wedding anniversary is blue topaz, the 23rd is imperial topaz. Created mass market for treated blue topaz, now one of the most common colored gems in the US; institutionalized topaz in American civic symbolism; raised disclosure and consumer-education issues about natural vs. treated color.

Ancient Egyptians and Romans wore modern topaz as we know it. — Ancient 'topaz' was a different mineral — most likely peridot/chrysolite from St. John's Island. The aluminum fluorosilicate now called topaz was not distinguished until 1737. References to topaz in Pliny and the Bible describe the older yellow stone.

All blue topaz is natural and its color is permanent and untreated. — Natural vivid blue topaz is exceptionally rare. Virtually all Swiss blue and London blue topaz on the market is colorless topaz that has been heated and irradiated; color is generally stable but requires disclosure and, for neutron-treated stones, regulatory cooling.

Topaz is a soft or fragile gem because it can split, so it must be low quality. — Topaz is very hard (Mohs 8) and takes an exceptional polish, but it has perfect cleavage making it brittle on one plane. Hardness (scratch resistance) and toughness (breakage resistance) are different properties.

Mystic topaz is a natural rainbow variety of topaz. — Mystic topaz is colorless topaz artificially coated via vapor deposition to create a rainbow iridescence. It is a treated product, not a natural variety, and the coating can wear or scratch.

Topaz cures lunacy, fever, or wards curses, and wearing it over the heart grants beauty and longevity. — These are documented medieval and early modern folklore beliefs (Roman travel protection, English lunacy cure, medieval evil-eye ward, Indian heart lore) with no scientific medical evidence. They are valuable for cultural history but not medical advice.

Topaz and citrine are the same stone or can be identified by color alone. — Golden topaz and citrine (yellow quartz) can look similar but are distinct species: topaz is denser (~25% heavier per volume), harder, has perfect cleavage and lower RI, and feels more slippery when polished. Tests include specific gravity, hardness, and heavy liquids (quartz floats in bromoform, topaz sinks).
