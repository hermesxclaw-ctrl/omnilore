Who It Is

Taiowa is the supreme, infinite, and unknowable creator in Hopi cosmology — the ultimate source from which all existence emanates. In the Hopi creation narrative preserved by Chief White Bear and recorded in Frank Waters' 'Book of the Hopi' (1963), Taiowa is the first entity, existing before space, time, or substance. Taiowa then created Sotuknang, his nephew, to carry out the work of forming the worlds. Taiowa is so transcendent that Hopi tradition scarcely anthropomorphizes him; he has no physical form, no personality in the human sense, and no direct interaction with the material world. Instead, he is the silent, infinite presence behind all things — comparable to Brahman in Hindu philosophy or the Neoplatonic concept of the One. The Hopi themselves rarely invoke Taiowa directly in ritual, working instead through the kachinas, Sotuknang, and other intermediary spirits. This sophisticated metaphysics — an infinite, impersonal first cause delegating creation to intermediary agents — parallels some of the most abstract theological systems in world history and demonstrates that indigenous American cosmologies were fully capable of profound philosophical abstraction. Taiowa's nature is expressed not in mythic stories but in the Hopi way of life (Hopi means 'peaceful people'), which seeks harmony with the cosmic order he established.

Form

Attested sources emphasize that Taiowa/Tawa is a sun spirit or sun god rather than a human-shaped deity with detailed physical description. In Waters’ and Courlander’s accounts, Taiowa is described conceptually as the only being in endless space, a presence rather than a figure. Popular summaries call Tawa/Taiowa the “sun god” or “sun spirit,” implying radiant, solar imagery, but do not record specific Hopi iconography for Taiowa distinct from broader sun symbolism. This absence of detailed description is itself significant: Taiowa functions as an abstract, cosmic Creator more than a personified character. Any imagined anthropomorphic form is therefore hypothetical and not attested in primary Hopi narratives available to outsiders.

Powers

Primordial existence beyond space and time Taiowa is described as the only being existing in the midst of endless space (Tokpella) before any worlds or creatures. This primordial existence positions him as the ultimate first cause in Hopi cosmology, prior to all differentiation of substance, time, and beings. Waters’ “Book of the Hopi” (Oraibi elders); summarized by Misra and encyclopedic entries. No explicit limitations are stated; Taiowa’s existence is absolute within the narrative frame. However, Hopi cosmology also emphasizes mystery and the limits of human understanding, implying that the full nature of Taiowa is beyond human comprehension. Some accounts simply call Tawa the first being and creator without explicitly naming Taiowa as distinct. This raises the question of whether ‘Taiowa’ is a title, variant, or separate aspect of the same primordial reality.

Conception of the plan of creation Taiowa conceives the entire plan of creation in his mind: the universes, the succession of worlds, and the moral structure that will govern them. He then brings forth Sotuknang to execute this plan, indicating a power of cosmic design rather than direct craftsmanship. Waters; echoed in Misra’s summary. Taiowa delegates the actual formation of universes and life to Sotuknang and Spider Woman, suggesting that his role is more architect and source than artisan. This is a narrative limitation rather than a metaphysical one. Other versions attribute direct formation of the First World to Tawa/Taiowa, blurring the distinction between planning and making.

Creation (or commissioning) of Sotuknang Taiowa brings forth Sotuknang, described as his “nephew” or instrument, and charges him with creating nine universes and the worlds within them. This act establishes a hierarchy of beings and a chain of delegated creative power. Waters; Courlander; encyclopedic summaries. Sotuknang is the one who gathers water, air, and substance to form worlds; Taiowa’s creative act is limited to bringing forth and empowering Sotuknang in the narratives available. Some sources emphasize Tawa as father/uncle of Sotuknang without using the name Taiowa, again raising identification questions.

Oversight of the Four Worlds cycle Although Sotuknang and Spider Woman carry out the destruction and renewal of worlds, the entire cycle of First, Second, Third, and Fourth Worlds unfolds under Taiowa’s original plan. The moral lessons—respect for the Creator, harmony, avoidance of greed and war—are grounded in Taiowa’s will. Waters; Misra; general Hopi mythology summaries. Taiowa does not appear as an active character intervening in each world; his oversight is implicit. Human beings interact more directly with Sotuknang, Spider Woman, Masauwu, and kachinas. Some Hopi stories emphasize other beings (Hard Being Woman, Masauwu) as central to particular world transitions, which can make Taiowa’s role seem more distant or symbolic.

Cosmic authority and moral order Taiowa’s power is not only creative but normative: the First People are instructed (through Sotuknang) to respect the Creator at all times, live in wisdom and harmony, and remember their obligations. When they fail, worlds are destroyed. Taiowa thus embodies the ultimate moral authority in Hopi cosmology. Waters; Misra; Courlander. The narratives stress human responsibility and the mediating roles of other beings; Taiowa’s authority is not depicted as micromanaging individual lives but as setting conditions and consequences. Some scholars caution that Christian-influenced interpretations may overemphasize a monotheistic moral lawgiver model; Hopi cosmology is more relational and plural than such readings suggest.

Accounts

In the beginning, only Taiowa existed in endless space. He conceived the plan of creation and brought forth Sotuknang as his instrument. Taiowa instructed Sotuknang to gather what he needed from space and to transform it into solid, liquid, and gaseous substances, forming nine universes. Sotuknang obeyed, creating the universes and the First World. Taiowa remained the unseen Creator whose will guided the process, while Sotuknang became the architect of manifest reality.

After the universes are formed, Sotuknang, under Taiowa’s plan, creates Spider Woman to help shape life. Spider Woman sings the Creation Song and brings forth nature and the First People. The people are given speech, wisdom, and the ability to reproduce, and are instructed to remember their Creator. Over time, each world falls into imbalance—forgetting the Creator, succumbing to greed, or waging war. Sotuknang destroys the First World with fire, the Second with freezing, and the Third with flood, preserving only those who remember the Creator. These survivors emerge into the Fourth World, where they eventually become the Hopi, living in a harsh landscape designed to remind them of their dependence on Taiowa.

While Taiowa does not appear as a character in specific ritual myths in accessible sources, Hopi ceremonial life is framed as remembering the Creator through songs, dances, and rituals. The Hopi maintain a sacred covenant with Masauwu, the caretaker of the earth and Lord of the Dead, living as humble farmers and respecting the land. Through their ceremonies, they remember Taiowa and the kachina spirits, seeking to maintain harmony and balance in the world.

Content Notes

In the Four Worlds narrative, worlds are destroyed when their inhabitants fall into corruption—forgetting the Creator, hoarding goods, or waging war. The First World is destroyed by fire, the Second by freezing, and the Third by flood. These cataclysms kill most beings, sparing only a few who remember the Creator and take refuge (often with the Ant People). While Taiowa does not personally wield the destructive forces, the cycle unfolds under his original plan, making him indirectly responsible for cosmic judgment.

Scholars note parallels between these cataclysms and other world-renewal myths (e.g., flood narratives), but emphasize that Hopi stories are distinct and embedded in local cosmology. The moral dimension—destruction as consequence of imbalance—is central.

Waters; Misra; Hopi mythology summaries.

Hopi elders and scholars such as Courlander stress that Hopi are reticent about discussing ritual secrets and religion-oriented traditions with outsiders. Stories told to anthropologists and writers may omit sensitive material or alter details. European Christian ideas may have influenced some external interpretations of Taiowa as a monotheistic, omnipotent Creator, potentially distorting indigenous understandings.

Researchers caution that published accounts, including Waters’ influential book, may be incomplete or shaped by the expectations of non-Hopi audiences. Any reconstruction of Taiowa’s nature must therefore acknowledge the possibility of colonial framing and respect the limits of outsider knowledge.

Courlander; academic summaries of Hopi mythology; Rollinson’s course notes.

What We Don't Know

No dedicated, peer-reviewed monograph focused solely on Taiowa as a distinct figure was located in accessible web sources; most discussions treat him within broader Hopi mythology. The internal Hopi linguistic etymology of ‘Taiowa/Tawa’ is not documented in detail, and phonetic transcriptions from native speakers are scarce. Because Hopi sacred narratives are orally transmitted and often kept secret, external accounts (Waters, Courlander, etc.) may be incomplete or intentionally altered. Variations between mesas and villages are acknowledged but not fully mapped, making it impossible to present a single authoritative Taiowa narrative. Ritual iconography specifically tied to Taiowa, as distinct from general sun symbols or kachina imagery, is not clearly described in public sources. Finally, this research is limited to English-language materials accessible online as of 2026-07-30 and cannot incorporate restricted archives or Hopi-internal teachings.

A way to hold it in your mind

Before there were worlds, before sun or corn or kachina dances on the mesas, Hopi storytellers say there was only Taiowa—an unseen presence in endless space, thinking the universe into being. From that solitary mind came the architect Sotuknang, the singing Spider Woman, and the fragile succession of worlds that burn, freeze, and drown when people forget their Creator. To follow Taiowa through Hopi cosmology is to step into a universe where the sun is not just a star, but the memory of a plan older than time.
