Who It Is

Before there were worlds, before sun or corn or kachina dances on the mesas, Hopi storytellers say there was only Taiowa—an unseen presence in endless space, thinking the universe into being. From that solitary mind came the architect Sotuknang, the singing Spider Woman, and the fragile succession of worlds that burn, freeze, and drown when people forget their Creator. To follow Taiowa through Hopi cosmology is to step into a universe where the sun is not just a star, but the memory of a plan older than time.

In the creation narratives, Taiowa is portrayed as utterly transcendent, calm, and purposeful. He exists alone in endless space, conceives a plan of creation, and brings forth Sotuknang as an instrument of his will. Taiowa does not engage in the emotional, reactive behavior seen in many mythic gods; instead, he sets the pattern and delegates. His values, as reflected through Sotuknang’s instructions to the First People, emphasize respect for the Creator, harmony, wisdom, and balance. Primordial existence beyond space and time

Taiowa is described as the only being existing in the midst of endless space (Tokpella) before any worlds or creatures. This primordial existence positions him as the ultimate first cause in Hopi cosmology, prior to all differentiation of substance, time, and beings.

Limit: No explicit limitations are stated; Taiowa’s existence is absolute within the narrative frame. However, Hopi cosmology also emphasizes mystery and the limits of human understanding, implying that the full nature of Taiowa is beyond human comprehension.

Conception of the plan of creation

Taiowa conceives the entire plan of creation in his mind: the universes, the succession of worlds, and the moral structure that will govern them. He then brings forth Sotuknang to execute this plan, indicating a power of cosmic design rather than direct craftsmanship.

Limit: Taiowa delegates the actual formation of universes and life to Sotuknang and Spider Woman, suggesting that his role is more architect and source than artisan. This is a narrative limitation rather than a metaphysical one.

Creation (or commissioning) of Sotuknang

Taiowa brings forth Sotuknang, described as his “nephew” or instrument, and charges him with creating nine universes and the worlds within them. This act establishes a hierarchy of beings and a chain of delegated creative power.

Limit: Sotuknang is the one who gathers water, air, and substance to form worlds; Taiowa’s creative act is limited to bringing forth and empowering Sotuknang in the narratives available.

Oversight of the Four Worlds cycle

Although Sotuknang and Spider Woman carry out the destruction and renewal of worlds, the entire cycle of First, Second, Third, and Fourth Worlds unfolds under Taiowa’s original plan. The moral lessons—respect for the Creator, harmony, avoidance of greed and war—are grounded in Taiowa’s will.

Limit: Taiowa does not appear as an active character intervening in each world; his oversight is implicit. Human beings interact more directly with Sotuknang, Spider Woman, Masauwu, and kachinas.

Cosmic authority and moral order

Taiowa’s power is not only creative but normative: the First People are instructed (through Sotuknang) to respect the Creator at all times, live in wisdom and harmony, and remember their obligations. When they fail, worlds are destroyed. Taiowa thus embodies the ultimate moral authority in Hopi cosmology.

Limit: The narratives stress human responsibility and the mediating roles of other beings; Taiowa’s authority is not depicted as micromanaging individual lives but as setting conditions and consequences.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Attested sources emphasize that Taiowa/Tawa is a sun spirit or sun god rather than a human-shaped deity with detailed physical description. In Waters’ and Courlander’s accounts, Taiowa is described conceptually as the only being in endless space, a presence rather than a figure. Symbols — The primary symbolic association of Taiowa is with the sun and with creation itself. In Hopi cosmology, the sun is central to life, agriculture, and ceremonial cycles, and mothers traditionally seek blessings from the sun for newborn children. While kachina figures and sun symbols appear widely in Hopi art, publicly accessible sources do not clearly identify a specific, standardized visual icon for Taiowa distinct from other sun-related imagery.

Domains — Taiowa’s domain is the totality of existence: endless space (Tokpella), the universes, and the succession of worlds. He is associated with the sun and with the cosmic order that underlies Hopi cosmology. Unlike more localized deities such as Masauwu (earth, death, caretaker of the Fourth World) or kachina spirits tied to specific phenomena, Taiowa occupies a meta-domain beyond space and time, functioning as the ultimate first cause. Accounts I
Taiowa and the creation of Sotuknang and the universesFrank Waters, The Book of the Hopi (Oraibi elders’ accounts)

Modern recording of traditional oral narrative (20th century; content presented as ancient)

In the beginning, only Taiowa existed in endless space. He conceived the plan of creation and brought forth Sotuknang as his instrument. Taiowa instructed Sotuknang to gather what he needed from space and to transform it into solid, liquid, and gaseous substances, forming nine universes. Sotuknang obeyed, creating the universes and the First World. Taiowa remained the unseen Creator whose will guided the process, while Sotuknang became the architect of manifest reality. The universes and the First World come into being, establishing the basic structure of reality and the hierarchy between Creator and agent. This sets the stage for the creation of life and the moral drama of subsequent worlds.

Accounts II
Taiowa’s plan and the Four WorldsWaters; Misra’s synthesis; general Hopi mythology summaries

Traditional oral narrative, recorded in 20th–21st century sources

After the universes are formed, Sotuknang, under Taiowa’s plan, creates Spider Woman to help shape life. Spider Woman sings the Creation Song and brings forth nature and the First People. The people are given speech, wisdom, and the ability to reproduce, and are instructed to remember their Creator. Over time, each world falls into imbalance—forgetting the Creator, succumbing to greed, or waging war. Sotuknang destroys the First World with fire, the Second with freezing, and the Third with flood, preserving only those who remember the Creator. These survivors emerge into the Fourth World, where they eventually become the Hopi, living in a harsh landscape designed to remind them of their dependence on Taiowa. The cyclical destruction and renewal of worlds teaches the central lesson of Hopi cosmology: humanity must live in balance, respect the Creator, and honor the earth. The Hopi’s present situation in the Fourth World is framed as both a gift and a test.

Taiowa remembered in Hopi ceremonies and covenant with MasauwuWaters; Misra; general Hopi religion notes

Traditional practice, observed and described in modern ethnographic and interpretive works

While Taiowa does not appear as a character in specific ritual myths in accessible sources, Hopi ceremonial life is framed as remembering the Creator through songs, dances, and rituals. The Hopi maintain a sacred covenant with Masauwu, the caretaker of the earth and Lord of the Dead, living as humble farmers and respecting the land. Through their ceremonies, they remember Taiowa and the kachina spirits, seeking to maintain harmony and balance in the world. The ongoing remembrance of Taiowa through ritual sustains the moral and cosmological order, helping to prevent the Fourth World from falling into the same corruption that destroyed previous worlds.
