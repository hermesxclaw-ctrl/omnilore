Who It Is
Form

The 3rd spirit is a soprano member of a six-spirit ensemble that embodies La Fée's (the Fairy Godmother's) supernatural retinue in Massenet's Cendrillon. As an otherworldly being in the tradition of the French theatrical féerie, the spirit is canonically imagined as a luminous, ethereal figure associated with flowers, moonlight, and enchanted forest imagery. The Act III Tableau 2 stage directions describe 'an enchanted arbour of flowers' ('un berceau de fleurs') that materializes around the lovers, and the spirits are integral to this magical environment, implicitly manifesting as flower-spirits or wood-spirits of the enchanted oak ('Le chêne enchanté'). In the original 1899 Opéra-Comique production, the newly installed electrical system of the Salle Favart was deployed to create spectacular fairy-light effects, giving the spirit figures a supernatural luminosity impossible in earlier gas-lit productions — the spirits would have been dressed in pale, light-reflecting fabrics designed to catch the new electric stage lighting. Laurent Pelly's landmark 2006 production (Santa Fe Opera, later Royal Opera House 2011, Metropolitan Opera 2018) situated the entire opera within a storybook aesthetic, costuming the spirits in stylized fairy-tale garb that blended Perraultian imagery with theatrical whimsy; photographs from the Royal Opera House production show the spirits in flowing pale-gold costumes with wings or floral headpieces suggesting their role as nature-spirits. In the 1993 Welsh National Opera production directed by Robert Carsen, the spirits occupied a more austere, dreamlike aesthetic. Across nearly all productions, the spirits are distinguished from the realistic human characters by costume palette — whites, silvers, greens, and blues that code the supernatural against the warmer tones of the court and domestic scenes. The 3rd spirit shares the visual identity of the full ensemble; individual differentiation is a staging choice, not a score requirement.

Powers

Collective enchantment through choral song The six spirits function as a single sonic-magical unit in Massenet's score. Their combined voices create the sonic aura of magical transformation and enchanted sleep in Act III. The 3rd spirit's soprano line is one of six interlocking vocal parts that together produce the otherworldly shimmering texture Massenet uses to represent La Fée's magical authority. In the operatic dramaturgy, the act of singing together is itself the mechanism of enchantment: the spirits' collective music draws the lovers into the enchanted bower and sustains the sleep that seals their mystical union.

Physical manifestation and dissolution As supernatural spirits in the féerie tradition, the spirits appear and disappear at La Fée's command, materializing in the human world (Act I, the domestic hearth scene) and in the enchanted forest (Act III) and dissolving back into the magical realm when their work is done. Stage directions indicate their physical presence around the sleeping lovers — they 'surround' the couple — implying a capacity to occupy and control physical space in ways mortal characters cannot.

Creation and dissolution of the enchanted bower The libretto and stage directions for Act III, Tableau 2 describe 'an enchanted arbour of flowers' ('un berceau de fleurs') that materializes to screen the lovers from each other during their recognition scene and then dissolves once they recognize one another. This transformation of the forest space is attributed to the spirit ensemble collectively, reflecting their power over the natural world of the enchanted forest — they can cause flowers to bloom and withdraw at will.

Presiding over enchanted slumber At the climax of Act III, the lovers 'fall into a slumber in each other's arms' while 'surrounded by the spirits.' The spirits are thus the custodians of this enchanted sleep — a magical state that transports the lovers outside of ordinary time and confirms their love in a dimension beyond mortal interference. This custodial role over enchanted sleep recalls parallel operatic figures such as the Norns of Wagner's Götterdämmerung and the Willis of Giselle who preside over mortals in liminal states.

Embodiment of La Fée's magical authority The spirits are an extension of La Fée's power — individually they have no independent magical agency, but collectively they constitute the organizational structure through which La Fée's enchantments are enacted, maintained, and concluded. In this sense their 'power' is delegated divine-adjacent authority, analogous in the operatic tradition to the angelic choirs of French grand opera. The 3rd spirit's soprano voice, placed in the middle register of the ensemble, provides harmonic glue — she is neither the highest nor the lowest voice but a structural pillar of the six-part texture.

Accounts

La Fée arrives at the domestic hearth where Cendrillon sleeps, accompanied by her spirit retinue (including the 3rd spirit). Together they transform Cendrillon — giving her magnificent ball clothes and the protective glass slippers — and warn her that the enchantment will end at midnight. The spirit ensemble provides the sonic landscape of magical transformation and departure.

Under the enchanted oak in a magical forest, La Fée's spirits draw the Prince and Cendrillon together. An enchanted bower of flowers separates them so that they can hear but not see each other, forcing a recognition through voice alone before sight. The spirits surround the lovers as they fall into enchanted sleep in each other's arms — a magical confirmation of their love that will have force in the waking world of Act IV.

The spirits are implied to be present in Act IV's magical atmosphere even as the scene returns to the human world. A royal herald (voice off-stage) summons all eligible princesses to try on the glass slipper, and the march of princesses leads into the final scene of recognition and reunion. The spirits' influence has set in motion the events of Act IV, though their visible role ends with Act III.

Massenet originally composed a prologue in which the characters — presumably including La Fée's spirit retinue — were introduced to the audience before the opera began. The prologue invited the audience 'to escape from dark realities (pour échapper à des réalités sombres) and to believe in the fabulous.' Albert Carré, director of the Opéra-Comique, persuaded Massenet to drop it before the premiere. The final words of the suppressed prologue were recycled as the opera's epilogue.

Scholarly Debates
Voice type designation: soprano vs. mezzo-soprano for the spirit ensemble
The score and premiere cast list (1899) clearly designate the 3rd spirit as soprano, alongside the 2nd, 5th, and 6th spirits, while reserving mezzo-soprano for the 1st and 4th spirits. The Wikidata record (Q63486071) confirms voice type P412 = Q30903 (soprano). ⇌ Some revival production cast sheets and opera database entries have inconsistently grouped all six spirits under a generic 'soprano/mezzo' designation without distinguishing individual voices, creating confusion in secondary sources about which specific spirits are soprano versus mezzo. Opera-arias.com correctly distinguishes them, but some theater program notes have not preserved the individual distinctions.
Whether the spirits are 'ghosts' or 'fairies' — ontological status
Wikidata lists P31 = Q30061299 ('ghost in a work of fiction') as one of the 3rd spirit's instance-of values, alongside Q50386450 ('operatic character'). This classification reads the French 'esprit' (spirit) as ghost — a disembodied soul of the dead. ⇌ The dramaturgy of Cendrillon positions the spirits not as ghosts of the dead but as fairy attendants of La Fée — entities of the magical realm rather than spirits of deceased humans. The 'ghost' classification in Wikidata appears to be an automatic tag applied to any entity whose label contains 'spirit,' rather than a considered dramaturgical judgment. Most opera scholars and performers classify these figures as fairy spirits (esprits féeriques) rather than ghosts.
Number of distinct lines vs. choral unison for the spirit ensemble
The libretto lists six individually named spirits with six distinct voice parts, implying distinct musical lines and at minimum some moments of individual textural identity in the score. ⇌ In many practical production situations, particularly smaller opera companies and student productions, the six spirits are cast as a standard 2+2+2 choral ensemble (two mezzos, four sopranos) with the individual numerical designations serving purely as administrative role-labeling rather than marking musically distinct parts. In these productions, the '3rd spirit' has no sonic individuality distinguishable from the 2nd, 5th, or 6th. Whether Massenet's score truly differentiates the six spirit voices individually throughout, or treats them as a choral block with occasional individual assignments, requires score-level analysis not undertaken in available secondary literature.
The identity of premiere performer 'Vilma'
The premiere cast list records '3me esprit: Mlle Vilma' — suggesting a minor comprimario performer recorded only by a stage pseudonym or first name, typical of chorus-level assignments at the Opéra-Comique in 1899. ⇌ It is possible that 'Vilma' was a recognized performer who simply used a single stage name — a convention not uncommon in late 19th-century French opera. Without access to the Opéra-Comique's internal administrative records (held at the Archives nationales de France), the identity of Vilma cannot be definitively confirmed or denied as a pseudonym versus a documented stage name.
What We Don't Know

No score-level musicological analysis of the six spirit parts has been found in secondary literature to confirm or deny the degree of individual voice differentiation in Massenet's writing. The identity of premiere performer 'Vilma' is unresolved; Opéra-Comique administrative records at the Archives nationales de France would be needed to identify her fully. No recording specifically spotlighting the spirit ensemble as a vocal subject has been identified; the 3rd spirit's individual lines are not isolated in any available audio or video recording. A way to hold it in your mind

The 3rd spirit is one of the least visible characters in the entire French operatic canon — a soprano voice in a six-part ensemble serving a fairy godmother in Jules Massenet's beloved 1899 Cendrillon. Yet this tiny role connects extraordinary history: the premiere singer known only as 'Vilma,' the first opera staged under electric light at the Salle Favart, the Perraultian tradition of supernatural women orchestrating human love, and 119 years of productions culminating in Joyce DiDonato's celebrated Metropolitan Opera debut of the role. Ask: what does it mean that the most essential magical act in the Cinderella story — the confirmation of the lovers' destiny in an enchanted forest — is witnessed and sustained by six unnamed spirits no one ever knows by name?
