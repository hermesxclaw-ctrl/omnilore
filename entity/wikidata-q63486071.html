Who It Is

The 3rd spirit is one of the least visible characters in the entire French operatic canon — a soprano voice in a six-part ensemble serving a fairy godmother in Jules Massenet's beloved 1899 Cendrillon. Yet this tiny role connects extraordinary history: the premiere singer known only as 'Vilma,' the first opera staged under electric light at the Salle Favart, the Perraultian tradition of supernatural women orchestrating human love, and 119 years of productions culminating in Joyce DiDonato's celebrated Metropolitan Opera debut of the role. Ask: what does it mean that the most essential magical act in the Cinderella story — the confirmation of the lovers' destiny in an enchanted forest — is witnessed and sustained by six unnamed spirits no one ever knows by name?

As an ensemble comprimario character with no individuated lines in the libretto, the 3rd spirit's personality is entirely collective: she partakes in the shared nature of all six spirits, which is devoted service to love, enchantment, and magical order. The spirits are the agents of the fairy-tale's mechanics — they draw the lovers together in Act III, surround them in the enchanted bower, and preside over the enchanted slumber that represents the mystical confirmation of their love. Collective enchantment through choral song

The six spirits function as a single sonic-magical unit in Massenet's score. Their combined voices create the sonic aura of magical transformation and enchanted sleep in Act III. The 3rd spirit's soprano line is one of six interlocking vocal parts that together produce the otherworldly shimmering texture Massenet uses to represent La Fée's magical authority. In the operatic dramaturgy, the act of singing together is itself the mechanism of enchantment: the spirits' collective music draws the lovers into the enchanted bower and sustains the sleep that seals their mystical union.

Limit:

Physical manifestation and dissolution

As supernatural spirits in the féerie tradition, the spirits appear and disappear at La Fée's command, materializing in the human world (Act I, the domestic hearth scene) and in the enchanted forest (Act III) and dissolving back into the magical realm when their work is done. Stage directions indicate their physical presence around the sleeping lovers — they 'surround' the couple — implying a capacity to occupy and control physical space in ways mortal characters cannot.

Limit:

Creation and dissolution of the enchanted bower

The libretto and stage directions for Act III, Tableau 2 describe 'an enchanted arbour of flowers' ('un berceau de fleurs') that materializes to screen the lovers from each other during their recognition scene and then dissolves once they recognize one another. This transformation of the forest space is attributed to the spirit ensemble collectively, reflecting their power over the natural world of the enchanted forest — they can cause flowers to bloom and withdraw at will.

Limit:

Presiding over enchanted slumber

At the climax of Act III, the lovers 'fall into a slumber in each other's arms' while 'surrounded by the spirits.' The spirits are thus the custodians of this enchanted sleep — a magical state that transports the lovers outside of ordinary time and confirms their love in a dimension beyond mortal interference. This custodial role over enchanted sleep recalls parallel operatic figures such as the Norns of Wagner's Götterdämmerung and the Willis of Giselle who preside over mortals in liminal states.

Limit:

Embodiment of La Fée's magical authority

The spirits are an extension of La Fée's power — individually they have no independent magical agency, but collectively they constitute the organizational structure through which La Fée's enchantments are enacted, maintained, and concluded. In this sense their 'power' is delegated divine-adjacent authority, analogous in the operatic tradition to the angelic choirs of French grand opera. The 3rd spirit's soprano voice, placed in the middle register of the ensemble, provides harmonic glue — she is neither the highest nor the lowest voice but a structural pillar of the six-part texture.

Accounts I
The transformation of Cendrillon (Act I)Cendrillon, libretto by Henri Caïn, music by Jules Massenet. Heugel, Paris, 1899.

Premiere 1899; fictional setting vaguely 'period of Louis XIII' per some production notes, but essentially timeless fairy-tale France

La Fée arrives at the domestic hearth where Cendrillon sleeps, accompanied by her spirit retinue (including the 3rd spirit). Together they transform Cendrillon — giving her magnificent ball clothes and the protective glass slippers — and warn her that the enchantment will end at midnight. The spirit ensemble provides the sonic landscape of magical transformation and departure. Cendrillon attends the royal ball, meets the Prince, and falls in love. The midnight deadline creates the dramatic crisis of the opera.

The enchanted forest and the confirmation of love (Act III, Tableau 2)Cendrillon, Act III, Scene 2. Libretto: Caïn. Score: Massenet.

Premiere 1899

Under the enchanted oak in a magical forest, La Fée's spirits draw the Prince and Cendrillon together. An enchanted bower of flowers separates them so that they can hear but not see each other, forcing a recognition through voice alone before sight. The spirits surround the lovers as they fall into enchanted sleep in each other's arms — a magical confirmation of their love that will have force in the waking world of Act IV. The enchanted slumber becomes the lovers' shared dream-memory that Pandolfe tries to convince Cendrillon was 'only a dream' in Act IV — but the physical evidence of the glass slipper confirms its reality. The forest scene is the emotional and magical center of the opera.

Accounts II
The gathering of eligible princesses (Act IV, Tableau 1 transition)Cendrillon, Act IV. Libretto: Caïn. Score: Massenet.

1899

The spirits are implied to be present in Act IV's magical atmosphere even as the scene returns to the human world. A royal herald (voice off-stage) summons all eligible princesses to try on the glass slipper, and the march of princesses leads into the final scene of recognition and reunion. The spirits' influence has set in motion the events of Act IV, though their visible role ends with Act III. The lovers are reunited before the full court, ending the opera with celebration.

The suppressed prologueAlbert Carré / Jules Massenet correspondence; discussed in New Grove Dictionary of Opera entry on Cendrillon; see also Ivor Guest, 'The Romantic Ballet in Paris' for theatrical context.

Conceived 1893–1895; suppressed before 1899 premiere

Massenet originally composed a prologue in which the characters — presumably including La Fée's spirit retinue — were introduced to the audience before the opera began. The prologue invited the audience 'to escape from dark realities (pour échapper à des réalités sombres) and to believe in the fabulous.' Albert Carré, director of the Opéra-Comique, persuaded Massenet to drop it before the premiere. The final words of the suppressed prologue were recycled as the opera's epilogue. The spirits lost a formal introduction. Without the prologue, their entrance in Act I has the quality of a sudden magical apparition, which arguably serves the dramaturgy better than a formal introduction would have.

Voice type designation: soprano vs. mezzo-soprano for the spirit ensembleThe score and premiere cast list (1899) clearly designate the 3rd spirit as soprano, alongside the 2nd, 5th, and 6th spirits, while reserving mezzo-soprano for the 1st and 4th spirits. The Wikidata record (Q63486071) confirms voice type P412 = Q30903 (soprano).Some revival production cast sheets and opera database entries have inconsistently grouped all six spirits under a generic 'soprano/mezzo' designation without distinguishing individual voices, creating confusion in secondary sources about which specific spirits are soprano versus mezzo. Opera-arias.com correctly distinguishes them, but some theater program notes have not preserved the individual distinctions.
Whether the spirits are 'ghosts' or 'fairies' — ontological statusWikidata lists P31 = Q30061299 ('ghost in a work of fiction') as one of the 3rd spirit's instance-of values, alongside Q50386450 ('operatic character'). This classification reads the French 'esprit' (spirit) as ghost — a disembodied soul of the dead.The dramaturgy of Cendrillon positions the spirits not as ghosts of the dead but as fairy attendants of La Fée — entities of the magical realm rather than spirits of deceased humans. The 'ghost' classification in Wikidata appears to be an automatic tag applied to any entity whose label contains 'spirit,' rather than a considered dramaturgical judgment. Most opera scholars and performers classify these figures as fairy spirits (esprits féeriques) rather than ghosts.
Number of distinct lines vs. choral unison for the spirit ensembleThe libretto lists six individually named spirits with six distinct voice parts, implying distinct musical lines and at minimum some moments of individual textural identity in the score.In many practical production situations, particularly smaller opera companies and student productions, the six spirits are cast as a standard 2+2+2 choral ensemble (two mezzos, four sopranos) with the individual numerical designations serving purely as administrative role-labeling rather than marking musically distinct parts. In these productions, the '3rd spirit' has no sonic individuality distinguishable from the 2nd, 5th, or 6th. Whether Massenet's score truly differentiates the six spirit voices individually throughout, or treats them as a choral block with occasional individual assignments, requires score-level analysis not undertaken in available secondary literature.
The identity of premiere performer 'Vilma'The premiere cast list records '3me esprit: Mlle Vilma' — suggesting a minor comprimario performer recorded only by a stage pseudonym or first name, typical of chorus-level assignments at the Opéra-Comique in 1899.It is possible that 'Vilma' was a recognized performer who simply used a single stage name — a convention not uncommon in late 19th-century French opera. Without access to the Opéra-Comique's internal administrative records (held at the Archives nationales de France), the identity of Vilma cannot be definitively confirmed or denied as a pseudonym versus a documented stage name.
