I have built four worlds. Each one fell because its people forgot who they were. I will build again — but only for those who remember.

Who It Is

Sotuknang is the active creator-demiurge in Hopi cosmology — the nephew of Taiowa who was tasked with taking the infinite potential of Taiowa's being and shaping it into the manifest universe. According to the Hopi emergence narrative, Sotuknang created nine universes or worlds, including the first world (Tokpela, 'endless space'), and populated them with life. When human beings in successive worlds fell into corruption — first through sexual misconduct, then through greed and materialism, then through warfare — Sotuknang destroyed each world in turn (by fire, ice, and flood, respectively) and guided the faithful minority to the next world through an emergence sipapu (opening). The Hopi believe they are currently living in the Fourth World (Tuwaqachi), having emerged from the Third World at the Grand Canyon. At the end of each cycle, Sotuknang is assisted by Spider Grandmother (Kokyangwuti), who helps the righteous survive. Sotuknang is thus both creator and destroyer, a figure of cosmic justice who maintains the balance between Taiowa's infinite peace and the chaotic potential of human free will. Unlike the remote Taiowa, Sotuknang engages directly with the world's fate and will one day return to preside over the end of the Fourth World. The Hopi prophecies associated with him — including warnings about environmental destruction and social collapse — have attracted considerable attention from eco-spiritual movements.

Form

Attested descriptions of Sotuknang’s physical appearance are sparse. Primary ethnographic texts focus on his actions and cosmic role rather than bodily form. In most English retellings, he is presented as an abstract, personified organizing principle rather than a vividly anthropomorphic figure. Some modern summaries describe him as a ‘manifestation of the finite’ emerging from Taiowa’s infinity, but this is interpretive language rather than a traditional visual description. No consistent iconography of Sotuknang as a kachina or masked dancer is documented in the cited sources; his presence is primarily narrative and cosmological. (Attested: narrative role; Unknown: detailed anatomy, clothing, colors.)

Powers

Creation of nine universes/worlds Sotuknang is tasked by Taiowa/Tawa to create nine universes or worlds according to the Creator’s plan. He organizes the elements, gathers water to form the sea, and air to form the winds, and establishes the First World (Tokpela) and subsequent worlds as stages for human spiritual development. Oraibi elders’ account as summarized by Railsback and popular retellings drawing on Voth and Stephen/Mindeleff. He acts under Taiowa’s authority and does not originate the plan; his creative power is delegated rather than absolute. Some Hopi accounts attribute creation directly to Tawa/Taiowa without mentioning Sotuknang, while others make Sotuknang central. Scholarly consensus treats these as variant traditions rather than attempts to harmonize them.

Delegation of life-creation to Spider Woman Sotuknang creates Spider Woman (Spider Grandmother, Kokyangwuti) and grants her the power to create life. She sings the Creation Song and brings forth twins and other beings, populating the worlds according to Sotuknang’s instructions. Oraibi creation story (Voth 1905; summarized in Railsback and Grunge article). Spider Woman acts as intermediary; Sotuknang does not personally shape every being but oversees the process. Other traditions emphasize Hard-Being Woman of the East and West (Húruing Wuhti) as creators of life, or attribute life-creation to different female deities. The exact division of creative labor among Sotuknang, Spider Woman, and Hard-Being Woman varies by source.

Destruction of corrupted worlds by fire, ice, and flood When humans in each world forget their Creator, close the kopavi (soft spot at the crown of the head), and become materialistic, violent, or morally corrupt, Sotuknang destroys the world: the First World by fire, the Second by ice, the Third by flood. He preserves a faithful remnant by sending them to shelter with the ants or underground, then leads them into the next world. Four Worlds/Emergence myth as summarized by Lockett (1933), Railsback, Nicole Lau’s blog, and Grunge article. His destructive acts are conditional on moral failure; he does not destroy arbitrarily. Faithful beings are always given a path to survival and renewal. Some versions emphasize different causes of destruction (e.g., war, overconsumption) or different sequences of elements. The number of worlds and their names can differ among mesas.

Moral oversight and enforcement of cosmic law Sotuknang monitors human behavior across worlds, reminding them to remember their Creator and live in balance. He judges when corruption has become complete and initiates destruction and renewal. His power is thus both cosmological and ethical. Interpretive synthesis of emergence narratives in Lockett, Voth, and modern summaries. He operates within a framework set by Taiowa and does not appear as a personal judge in everyday human affairs; his interventions are world-scale rather than individual. Some Hopi teachings may attribute moral oversight to other beings (e.g., Masau’u, kachinas) in more localized contexts; Sotuknang’s role is primarily in the mythic past.

Granting languages and dispersal of peoples In some accounts, Sotuknang gives four different languages to the first people created by Spider Woman, sending them to spread across the globe. This dispersal sets up later migrations and the eventual emergence of the Hopi in the Fourth World. Oraibi creation story as summarized in Railsback and derivative articles. This is a mythic explanation of linguistic and cultural diversity, not a literal historical account. Other versions of the emergence myth do not mention language-giving explicitly or attribute it to other deities; the detail is not universal across Hopi tradition.

Accounts

Taiowa, the infinite Creator, brings forth Sotuknang, calling him his nephew and manifestation of the finite. Taiowa instructs Sotuknang to create nine universes according to his plan. Sotuknang gathers water to make the sea and air to make the winds, shaping the First World, Tokpela (Endless Space), in perfect harmony. He then creates Spider Woman, giving her the power to sing the Creation Song and bring life into being. Spider Woman fashions twins and other beings, and the first people live in balance, with kopavi (soft spots) on their heads through which they receive wisdom directly from their Creators.

Over time, people in the First World become obsessed with material things, close their kopavi, and forget their Creator. Division and conflict arise. Seeing that the world has become corrupted, Sotuknang destroys it with fire, burning away the wickedness. He saves the faithful by sending them to shelter underground with Spider Woman and the ants. After the cleansing, Sotuknang creates the Second World, Tokpa (Dark Midnight), and leads the faithful to emerge into it.

In the Second World, people develop villages, agriculture, and trade. Initially they remember the lessons of the First World, but gradually they become obsessed with possessions and hoarding. When corruption becomes complete, Sotuknang destroys the Second World with ice, freezing hearts and lands. Again, Spider Woman leads the faithful underground. In the Third World, people develop complex societies but descend into war and violence. Sotuknang destroys this world with a great flood. The survivors rise from the earth and are sent migrating across the land, eventually settling between the Colorado River and the Rio Grande, becoming the Hopi in the harsh landscape of the Fourth World, designed to remind them of their dependence on the Creator.

After Spider Woman creates the first people, Sotuknang gives them four different languages and sends them to spread across the world. Each group travels to different regions, carrying the memory of the Creator. Over time, many forget, but a remnant maintains the teachings and eventually becomes the Hopi after emergence into the Fourth World.

Content Notes

In the emergence myth, Sotuknang destroys entire worlds when humans become corrupt—burning the First World with fire, freezing the Second with ice, and flooding the Third. These acts imply the death of vast numbers of beings, with only a small faithful remnant surviving underground. The stories are didactic but also stark depictions of cosmic violence.

Scholars interpret these destructions as moral allegories about balance and spiritual neglect rather than literal historical events. However, they remain central to Hopi cosmology and are not sanitized in traditional tellings.

Lockett 1933; Voth 1905; modern summaries of the Four Worlds myth.

Much of what is written about Sotuknang comes from ethnographers and missionaries who recorded Hopi stories in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Hopi informants may have withheld ritual secrets or altered stories for outsiders. The recording process occurred under U.S. colonial pressure, boarding schools, and religious suppression, which could distort or fragment traditional knowledge.

Folklorists like Harold Courlander and others emphasize that Hopi oral tradition is diverse and that stories told to non-Hopi may not represent genuine beliefs. Modern Hopi voices call for careful, respectful use of these texts and recognition of colonial contexts.

Hopi mythology article noting Hopi reticence; Lockett 1933 preface; general scholarship on Hopi ethnography.

The narrative that worlds are destroyed when humans become corrupt can be misused in modern contexts to justify punitive or apocalyptic ideologies. Some New Age and comparative-mythology writers overlay Hopi stories with external eschatological frameworks, sometimes ignoring Hopi perspectives.

Responsible scholarship distinguishes Hopi teachings from imported apocalyptic narratives and warns against appropriating Hopi cosmology for sensationalist or commercial purposes.

What We Don't Know

No dedicated Wikipedia article or major monograph focuses solely on Sotuknang; information is scattered across broader Hopi mythology sources. Primary Hopi-language texts and ritual commentaries that might clarify Sotuknang’s name, pronunciation, and iconography are not readily accessible in open digital archives. Ethnographic accounts are shaped by colonial contexts and may omit or alter sacred details. There is limited documentation of how contemporary Hopi practitioners speak about Sotuknang today, and no detailed record of his role in specific ceremonies beyond general creation and emergence narratives. Cross-cultural comparisons (e.g., to Vishnu or Awonawilona) are speculative and not Hopi-attested. The ‘beast’ archetype label in the prompt does not match the available evidence, which consistently presents Sotuknang as a cosmic architect rather than an animal or monstrous being.

A way to hold it in your mind

In Hopi stories of the beginning, the universe does not simply appear—it is built. Out of Taiowa’s infinite mind steps Sotuknang, a named edge to the boundless, tasked with gathering water into seas, air into winds, and worlds into a ladder of moral tests. He is not a thunderous war god or a trickster coyote, but the quiet architect who burns, freezes, and floods entire worlds when their inhabitants forget why they were made. To follow Sotuknang through the Four Worlds is to watch creation itself learn, fail, and begin again.
