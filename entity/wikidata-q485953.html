Who They Are

This page is intentionally blank and ready for source-backed lore, versions, images, and connections. Ananse is the lord of stories and the keeper of folklore. He is associated with wisdom (though he often hoards or misuses it), with cunning, with the spider's web as a symbol of connection and creation, and with the sky (in some myths, he climbs to the sky god's realm). He is a liminal figure, moving between the human world, the animal world, and the spirit world. In the diaspora, he became a symbol of survival, resistance, and wit for enslaved people.

Form

Ananse is depicted as a spider, but not always a literal one. In Akan oral tradition, he often appears as a man with spider-like traits: he may have eight legs, or be a normal man who can transform into a spider. In modern visual culture (picture books, animation), he is almost always a spider with a human face, expressive eyes, and often wearing human clothes. In Akan goldweights, he is shown as a stylized spider, sometimes with a human head.

Powers

Transformation Ananse can shift between human and spider form, and can also transform into other objects or animals to deceive others. Akan and Jamaican traditions He cannot maintain a form if he is distracted or if his trick is discovered. Some versions present him as always a spider, others as always a man with spider aspects.

Superhuman Cunning His primary 'power' is his intellect. He can outwit gods, giants, kings, and animals through trickery, wordplay, and psychological manipulation. Universal across all traditions His greed and arrogance often lead him to overreach, causing his plans to backfire. None.

Web Spinning / Net Weaving He can spin webs to catch prey, trap enemies, or create bridges and ladders (e.g., to climb to the sky). Akan and diaspora traditions Webs can be broken by stronger forces. In some tales, he does not use webs at all, relying purely on verbal trickery.

Sky Journey In the famous 'Sky God' tale, Ananse climbs to the sky (sometimes on a spider's thread) to bargain with the god Nyame. Akan tradition Requires a specific method (e.g., a long thread, a ladder of objects). The method of ascent varies by version.

Behavior & Habitat

Ananse is the quintessential trickster: clever, greedy, lazy, boastful, and selfish, yet also a culture hero who brings wisdom, stories, and survival skills to humanity. He is a master of disguise and deception, often using his wits to outsmart creatures far larger and stronger than himself. He is not a heroic figure in the moral sense; he is a survivor who bends rules, breaks promises, and cheats, yet his actions often result in positive outcomes for the community (or at least for himself). He is a glutton, a liar, and a coward, but also charming and resourceful.

Accounts

The sky god Nyame owns all the stories. Ananse offers to buy them. Nyame sets impossible tasks: capture Onini the python, Osebo the leopard, Mmoboro the hornets, and Mmoatia the fairy. Ananse uses trickery: he tricks the python into stretching along a branch to be measured, then ties him down; he digs a pit for the leopard; he convinces the hornets to enter a gourd to shelter from rain; he uses a doll coated in gum to capture the fairy. He presents all four to Nyame, who gives him the stories. From then on, all stories are 'spider stories' (Anansesem).

Ananse collects all the world's wisdom into a pot to hoard it for himself. He tries to hang the pot on a tree, but cannot tie it while also holding it. His son (or wife) points out that he could tie the pot on his back first. Ananse realizes he did not have all the wisdom, and in anger throws the pot down, scattering wisdom across the world for everyone to share.

Ananse desires a magical drum that can summon food or wealth. He uses trickery to steal it from a spirit or another animal, but his greed leads to his downfall, often losing the drum and being left with nothing.

Anansi, angry at being excluded from a feast or wanting to steal food, is caught by a doll made of tar (or gum). He strikes the doll, gets stuck, and is captured. This tale is also famous as the 'Tar Baby' story in African-American folklore (often retold with Br'er Rabbit).

Anansi tricks a farmer or a spirit into giving him a magical yam that can feed the whole village, but his greed or boastfulness leads to the yam losing its power or exploding, causing chaos.

In some versions, Ananse seeks to marry the sky god's daughter, requiring him to perform impossible tasks. His cleverness wins him the bride, but his subsequent behavior often leads to conflict.

Scholarly Debates
Is Ananse a god?
Ananse is a divine being, a 'spider-god' who interacts with the sky god and holds power over stories. ⇌ Ananse is a mortal (or animal) character with supernatural cunning, not a deity in the Akan pantheon.
Evidence for A: His ability to bargain with Nyame and his role as a culture hero suggest divine status.
Evidence for B: Akan cosmology does not typically include Ananse in the pantheon of worshipped deities (abosom); he is a beloved charact
Ananse's physical form
Ananse is always a spider. ⇌ Ananse is a man who can transform into a spider.
Evidence for A: Depictions in goldweights and many tales emphasize his spider nature.
Evidence for B: Many tales describe him walking, talking, and acting like a human, with spider features only sometimes mentioned.
The origin of the 'Tar Baby' tale
The 'Tar Baby' story is an African-derived tale, brought to the Americas by enslaved people. ⇌ The 'Tar Baby' is a Native American or European tale that was adopted by African-American storytellers.
Evidence for A: The motif exists in West African folklore (e.g., Anansi and the gum doll).
Evidence for B: Similar motifs exist in other cultures; the specific 'tar baby' may have multiple origins.
What We Don't Know

Deep research into specific Akan regional variants (e.g., Fante, Akuapem) is needed. A comprehensive index of Anansi appearances in modern media (comics, games, music) is incomplete. The exact relationship between Anansi and other Akan spiritual beings (abosom) requires further theological study. The role of Anansi in contemporary Akan religious practice (e.g., in neo-traditional movements) is under-documented.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Anansi as the original Bugs Bunny with a PhD in survival. He is the trickster who proves that brains can beat brawn, that the little guy can outsmart the king, and that sometimes the only way to win is to cheat — but he also shows that greed and arrogance always have a price. He is a spider, a man, a god, and a rebel, all woven into one.
