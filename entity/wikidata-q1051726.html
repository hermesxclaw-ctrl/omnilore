Who It Is

In the candlelit chambers of Dendera, where the sistrum's rattle once echoed the joy of a divine birth, a young god named Ihy personified the very essence of festive music. He was the child of Hathor, the golden cow goddess, and Horus the elder, and his name itself was the sound of the hand-held rattle that accompanied his mother's rituals. Ihy was not a warrior or a creator, but a symbol of pure, unbridled delight—the embodiment of the ecstatic clatter that drove away sorrow and welcomed the divine.

Ihy is the personification of the jubilant sound of the sistrum. He is associated with music, dance, and joy, and his name is an onomatopoeic expression of the rattle's sound. He is a gentle, protective deity, particularly for children and mothers. In the Pyramid Texts, he is called 'the great one who is in the sistrum' and is said to 'pacify the heart of the gods' with his music.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Ihy is likely an onomatopoeic word imitating the sound of the sistrum, a rattle used in Hathor's cult. It may also be related to the word for 'calf', referencing his mother's bovine form. The Egyptians themselves associated the name with the noisemaker, as noted in the source material.

Pronunciation — The exact vocalization is uncertain; Egyptologists typically render it as 'Ihy' (ee-hee). The ancient pronunciation may have been closer to 'Yee-hu'.

Aliases — Ahi, Ihi, Ihu, Horus-Ihy

Ownership — Ancient Egyptian; specifically associated with the cult center at Dendera, but venerated throughout Egypt.

Powers
Musical power

Ihy's sistrum playing could drive away evil spirits and bring joy to the gods. He was believed to 'pacify' the hearts of deities with his music.

Limit: His power was tied to the ritual use of the sistrum; outside of that context, he had no martial or cosmic powers.

Fertility and rebirth

As a child of Hathor, he was associated with fertility, birth, and the renewal of life. He was present at the birth of the king and in the afterlife assisted the deceased in being reborn.

Limit: His role was supportive, not creative; he did not create life but facilitated it.

Protection of children

Ihy was invoked to protect children from harm, especially during the vulnerable period after birth. His image was often placed on amulets and birth houses.

Limit: This power is inferred from his iconography and amuletic use; no specific spells in his name are known.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Ihy is typically depicted as a naked child with a sidelock of youth (the 'lock of Horus'), curly hair, and a finger to his mouth. He wears a necklace (menat) and holds a sistrum (a rattle-like instrument). In some depictions, he is shown as a child with a uraeus (royal serpent) on his forehead, and occasionally with a lunar disk and crescent, linking him to the moon. His skin is often shown as green or black, symbolizing fertility and rebirth.

Symbols — His primary symbols are the sistrum and the menat necklace, both associated with his mother Hathor. He is also linked to the lotus flower, as a child of the cow goddess, and to the moon, as a celestial child. In the Mammisi at Dendera, he is shown in scenes of his divine birth and in ritual processions.

Domains — His main cult site was the temple complex at Dendera, where he was worshipped alongside Hathor and Horus. He also had shrines at Edfu, Philae, and other Hathor cult centers. In the afterlife, he was believed to help the deceased navigate the underworld, providing them with bread and beer.

Accounts I
The Divine Birth at DenderaTemple of Dendera, Mammisi inscriptions

Ptolemaic/Roman

The Mammisi at Dendera contains reliefs and texts depicting the birth of Ihy. The scenes show Hathor giving birth to Ihy in a papyrus thicket, attended by divine midwives. The child is presented to the gods, and his birth is celebrated with music and dance. This myth legitimized the king's divine birth, as the pharaoh was considered the living Horus, and Ihy was seen as the eternal child. Established Ihy as a key figure in the royal cult and in the celebration of the Khoiak festival, where his birth was reenacted.

Accounts II
The Sistrum Player in the UnderworldBook of the Dead, Chapter 178

New Kingdom onward

In the Book of the Dead, Ihy is mentioned as one of the deities who provides bread and beer to the deceased. He is depicted as a child with a sistrum, playing music to soothe the souls of the dead and to guard them on their journey through the Duat. His presence in the afterlife ensured the deceased's sustenance and safe passage.

The Khoiak FestivalDendera temple texts

Ptolemaic/Roman

During the Khoiak festival, the mysteries of Osiris were performed, and Ihy was involved in the rituals. He was believed to be present at the birth of the new Osiris, and his sistrum playing was part of the celebratory rites. The festival included 13 acts of mystery plays about fertility, which were held in the Mammisi. Reinforced the cycle of death and rebirth and the role of Ihy as a herald of joy.
