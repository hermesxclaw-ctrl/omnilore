I followed the cod and carved the river. Where I rested, the lakes formed. Where I stepped, the springs rose. The Murray is my journey made visible — every bend remembers my paddle.

Who It Is

Ngurunderi is the great ancestral hero and creator being of the Ngarrindjeri people of the Lower Murray River and Coorong region of South Australia. According to Ngarrindjeri Dreaming, Ngurunderi traveled down the Murray River in a bark canoe, chasing the giant Murray cod (Ponde), whose thrashing body created the river's winding course. When Ngurunderi caught and cut up the cod, the pieces became the fish species of the river. At every stopping point along his journey, Ngurunderi performed acts of creation: he raised the hills, carved the lakes, created the freshwater springs, and established the laws and ceremonies of the Ngarrindjeri people. At the end of his journey, Ngurunderi crossed to Kangaroo Island (Karta), where he ascended into the sky to become a star or spirit. The Murray River itself is the physical evidence of Ngurunderi's journey — every bend, cliff, and lake has a story from his travels. Ngurunderi also created the first man (by carving him from a tree) and the first woman (from a termite mound). He established the kinship system that still governs Ngarrindjeri society. The Ngurunderi Dreaming is recorded in Ngarrindjeri land claims and native title determinations, where it serves as evidence of continuous connection to the land. The Ngurunderi story is one of the most detailed and extensive Aboriginal Dreaming narratives, comparable in scope to the great epics of other cultures. He is a heroic figure — wise, powerful, and just — who gave his people the land, the law, and the means to live on the river that bears the marks of his passage.

Form

Attested descriptions of Ngurunderi focus more on his actions and relationships than on detailed physical features, reflecting the nature of many Aboriginal Australian ancestral narratives. In the recorded stories, Ngurunderi appears as a mature male ancestor, traveling in a bark canoe, wielding spears and a stone knife, and accompanied or opposed by other beings. He is sometimes implicitly human-like in form—able to smell cooking fish, build huts, sleep under trees, fight with weapons, and swim—but he also exhibits capacities beyond ordinary humans, such as commanding waters to rise and transforming objects and landscapes. In some accounts, his huts become hills and his canoe becomes the Milky Way, suggesting a body and material presence that can be translated into cosmic and geological forms. Visual representations in museum exhibitions and artworks often depict him as an Aboriginal man with traditional clothing and implements, but these are modern artistic interpretations rather than detailed traditional iconographic prescriptions. Overall, his appearance is best described as a powerful ancestral man whose form is flexible enough to be associated with landforms and celestial features.

Powers

Landscape creation and modification Ngurunderi possesses the ability to shape and modify the landscape on a large scale. In the Dreaming, he travels down the Murray River while it is still a small stream, and his pursuit of Ponde, combined with the fish’s thrashing tail, results in the widening and bending of the river. His missed spear throws are linked to the formation of Long Island and straight stretches of the river. He creates a giant she-oak tree on Kangaroo Island, and his cast spear becomes a rocky reef at the island’s western end. His command for the waters to rise at Backstairs Passage leads to the drowning of his wives and the formation of the Pages Islands. These acts collectively demonstrate a power to transform rivers, coasts, islands, and reefs through intentional actions. Attested in Berndt’s account via Dreaming Stories summary, South Australian Museum booklet, ArcGIS StoryMap, and Kangaroo Island heritage article. The narratives do not explicitly state limitations on Ngurunderi’s landscape-shaping powers, but they are contextually tied to his journey and specific moral or relational situations. He does not arbitrarily reshape all environments; his acts are embedded in pursuit, conflict, and law enforcement. There is no direct contradiction about his ability to shape the landscape, but scholarly interpretations differ on whether these acts should be read primarily as cosmological myth, as encoded environmental memory, or both. Nunn et al. argue that elements of the story may preserve Holocene sea-level changes, while other scholars emphasize its spiritual and legal dimensions.

Creation of fish species After Ponde is finally speared in Lake Alexandrina, Ngurunderi cuts up the giant cod with his stone knife. Each piece becomes a different species of fish in the river. This act establishes the diversity of fish available to the Ngarrindjeri people and links their food resources directly to Ngurunderi’s creative power. Dreaming Stories summary, ArcGIS StoryMap, South Australian Museum booklet. The creation of fish species is a one-time foundational act in the Dreaming; the narratives do not describe Ngurunderi continually creating new species. The act is tied to the specific event of Ponde’s death. No major contradictions are recorded regarding this ability. However, different versions may list or emphasize different fish species, and some educational summaries simplify the account without detailing all species.

Transformation of objects into celestial and terrestrial features Ngurunderi can transform his own possessions and structures into enduring features of the cosmos and landscape. In one account, his huts become two hills when he abandons his camp to pursue his wives. His bark canoe becomes the Milky Way, providing a celestial pathway associated with his journey and the afterlife. His spear, cast into the sea, becomes a rocky reef. These transformations illustrate a power to convert everyday objects into large-scale features that persist across time. Dreaming Stories summary (huts becoming hills, canoe becoming Milky Way), ArcGIS StoryMap (spear becoming reef). The narratives do not present this power as something Ngurunderi uses casually; each transformation occurs at a significant turning point in his story—abandoning his camp, transitioning to the spirit world, or marking the end of his earthly journey. There are minor variations in which objects are said to transform and which features they become. Some versions mention the huts becoming hills; others focus more on the canoe and spear. These differences reflect local emphases rather than direct contradictions.

Control over water and sea-level Ngurunderi demonstrates a dramatic ability to command water. At Backstairs Passage, when he sees his wives wading across the shallow channel between the mainland and Naroongowie (Kangaroo Island), he becomes angry and orders the water to rise and drown them. The waters respond with a terrific rush, carrying the women back toward the mainland and drowning them. This act is linked to the flooding of Backstairs Passage and the separation of Kangaroo Island from the mainland in some interpretations. ArcGIS StoryMap, Kangaroo Island heritage article, Nunn et al. (environmental interpretation). This control is depicted in a specific moral context—punishing his wives for breaking a taboo and running away. The narratives do not describe Ngurunderi as constantly manipulating sea-level; the act is singular and consequential. Environmental scholars debate whether this narrative element encodes a memory of actual Holocene sea-level rise or is purely mythic. Nunn et al. argue for a correlation with paleo-geographic data, while others caution against over-literalizing oral traditions.

Law-giving and social regulation Ngurunderi is consistently described as a law-giver who establishes the rules of living for the Ngarrindjeri people. His narratives encode food taboos (such as the prohibition on women cooking bony bream), kinship relations, and appropriate behavior. His punitive response to his wives’ transgression demonstrates his role in enforcing these laws. He is also associated with the broader moral order that governs who will join him in the Milky Way after death—those who follow his laws. Dreaming Stories summary, South Australian Museum booklet, ArcGIS StoryMap. The specific corpus of laws attributed to Ngurunderi is not fully detailed in the accessible sources; much of this knowledge is likely held within Ngarrindjeri community teachings and may be restricted. The narratives available to outsiders focus on a few illustrative taboos and moral lessons. Modern readers and scholars may interpret Ngurunderi’s enforcement of law, particularly against his wives, as harsh or patriarchal, while traditional frameworks understand it within a different moral and cosmological context. This can create tension between external ethical critiques and internal cultural logics.

Combat and sorcery Ngurunderi engages in a significant conflict with a great sorcerer named Parampari near Kingston. The two men fight using both weapons and magical powers. Ngurunderi ultimately defeats Parampari and burns his body in a huge fire. This episode demonstrates Ngurunderi’s prowess in combat and his ability to overcome dangerous spiritual adversaries. Dreaming Stories summary (based on Berndt’s account). The narrative does not elaborate on the specific techniques or spells used in the fight; it simply notes that both weapons and magic were involved. Details of Ngurunderi’s sorcery are not fully disclosed in accessible sources. No major contradictions are recorded about this episode, but some versions of the Ngurunderi story omit Parampari entirely, focusing instead on the river and the wives. This reflects selective emphasis rather than disagreement about the event.

Transition to and residence in the spirit world After his earthly journey, Ngurunderi travels to Waleruwar, the spirit world, and takes up residence in the Milky Way. He throws away his weapons and moves to his home in the sky, where the people who follow his laws will join him when they die. This indicates a power to cross between earthly and celestial realms and to serve as a guardian or host for the dead. ArcGIS StoryMap, Dreaming Stories summary. The narratives do not describe Ngurunderi returning to earth after this transition; his role becomes that of a celestial ancestor. Details of his activities in Waleruwar are not elaborated in the accessible sources. There is no recorded contradiction about his residence in the Milky Way, but different sources use slightly different terms for the spirit world and emphasize different aspects of his post-earthly role.

Accounts

In the Dreaming, Ngurunderi travels down the Murray River in a bark canoe, searching for his two wives who have run away from him. At that time, the river is only a small stream below the junction with the Darling River. A giant cod fish named Ponde swims ahead of Ngurunderi, widening the river with sweeps of its tail. Ngurunderi chases Ponde, trying to spear it from his canoe. Near Murray Bridge, he throws a spear but misses; this act is associated with the formation of Long Island. At Tailem Bend, he throws another spear, and Ponde surges ahead, creating a long straight stretch in the river. Ngurunderi continues his pursuit until Ponde leaves the Murray and swims into Lake Alexandrina. With the help of Nepele, the brother of Ngurunderi’s wives, Ponde is finally speared. Ngurunderi cuts up the giant fish with his stone knife, and each piece becomes a different species of fish in the river, providing food for the Ngarrindjeri people. This story explains the winding course of the Murray River, the existence of specific landforms, and the origin of fish diversity.

While Ngurunderi is pursuing Ponde, his two wives make camp and cook bony bream on their fire. Bony bream is a fish forbidden to Ngarrindjeri women, and their act constitutes a serious breach of food law. Ngurunderi smells the fish cooking and knows his wives are nearby. He abandons his camp, leaving his huts and canoe behind; in some accounts, his huts become two hills and his canoe becomes the Milky Way. Hearing Ngurunderi approaching, the women hastily build a raft of reeds and grass-trees and escape across Lake Albert. On the other side, their raft turns back into reeds and grass-trees. The women hurry south along the coast, while Ngurunderi follows them as far as Kingston. Eventually, he reaches Cape Jervis and sees his wives wading halfway across the shallow channel that separates Naroongowie (Kangaroo Island) from the mainland. Angered by their disobedience and flight, Ngurunderi orders the water to rise and drown them. The waters surge with a terrific rush, carrying the women back toward the mainland and drowning them. Their bodies become the rocky Pages Islands in Backstairs Passage. The story explains the origin of these islands and encodes a powerful lesson about law, gendered taboos, and the consequences of transgression.

After the drowning of his wives and the creation of the Pages Islands, Ngurunderi swims out to Naroongowie (Kangaroo Island). It is a hot day, and he desires shade. He creates a she-oak tree that is the largest in Australia and tries to sleep beneath it. However, the wailing of his drowned wives, now associated with the Pages Islands and the surrounding waters, keeps him awake. Realizing that his time on earth is ending, Ngurunderi decides to travel to Waleruwar, the spirit world. He walks to the western end of the island and casts his spear into the sea; the resulting rocky reef remains visible. He throws away all his weapons and moves to his home in the Milky Way, where those who follow his laws will join him after death. The story explains the presence of certain reefs and trees on Kangaroo Island and establishes Ngurunderi’s transition from an earthly ancestor to a celestial being.

During his southward journey, Ngurunderi encounters a great sorcerer named Parampari near Kingston. Parampari is a powerful and dangerous figure. The two men engage in a fierce battle, using both physical weapons and magical powers. The fight is intense and prolonged, but Ngurunderi ultimately prevails. He burns Parampari’s body in a huge fire, eliminating the threat he posed. This episode demonstrates Ngurunderi’s strength, courage, and mastery of both martial and spiritual techniques, and it reinforces his role as protector and law-giver.

Beyond specific episodes, Ngurunderi’s story functions as a comprehensive framework for Ngarrindjeri culture. Museum and educational materials use his Dreaming track to introduce topics such as bark canoe construction, fishing techniques, net-making, hunting and food gathering, plant medicine, families and clans, shelter and clothing, initiation, games and wood-carving, basketry, reeds and grass-trees, family conflict and law, ancient tools, and death and the afterlife. Ngurunderi is presented as the main ancestor who established laws and social practices before leaving the earth to live in the sky. His narrative thus serves as a backbone for understanding Ngarrindjeri relationships to land, water, animals, plants, and each other.

Content Notes

In the coastal segment of the Ngurunderi narrative, his two wives run away from him and break a food taboo by cooking bony bream, a fish forbidden to Ngarrindjeri women. When Ngurunderi discovers their transgression and sees them wading across the shallow channel between the mainland and Naroongowie (Kangaroo Island), he becomes angry and orders the water to rise and drown them. The waters surge, carrying the women back toward the mainland and drowning them. Their bodies become the rocky Pages Islands. This episode involves lethal violence against women as a form of law enforcement and encodes a gendered hierarchy in food taboos and punishment.

Traditional Ngarrindjeri frameworks understand this act within a system of ancestral law and cosmological order. Modern feminist and critical readings may highlight the gendered violence and question the moral implications. The narratives themselves do not offer alternative outcomes, and the story remains a central part of Ngarrindjeri Dreaming. Scholars must navigate respect for cultural authority while acknowledging the presence of harsh punitive elements.

ArcGIS StoryMap; Kangaroo Island heritage article; Dreaming Stories summary.

The first published version of the Ngurunderi Dreaming appeared in an Adelaide newspaper in 1842, only six years after the proclamation of the colony of South Australia. Later, anthropologists such as Ronald Berndt recorded detailed accounts from Ngarrindjeri elders like Albert Karloan. These publications made Ngurunderi’s stories accessible to non-Indigenous audiences but also involved the extraction of sacred narratives into colonial print culture, often without full control by Indigenous custodians.

Contemporary Indigenous and decolonial scholarship critiques the ways in which anthropologists and colonial institutions collected and disseminated Aboriginal stories, sometimes misrepresenting or oversimplifying them and contributing to cultural disruption. At the same time, these records have become important archives for communities seeking to reclaim and revitalize traditions. Ngurunderi’s narrative sits at the intersection of these dynamics.

Kangaroo Island heritage article (note on 1842 publication); Dreaming Stories summary (reference to Berndt 1940).

The South Australian Museum booklet notes that from the early 1830s, Ngarrindjeri people experienced massive dislocation due to European colonization. Christian missions such as Point McLeay (Raukkan) served as refuges but also weakened traditional culture and rights. Ngarrindjeri people suffered loss of land, rights, and cultural practices, and many lived in town fringe camps until the 1960s.

This history is part of a broader pattern of colonial dispossession, forced assimilation, and racism against Aboriginal Australians. Ngurunderi’s story, while originating in pre-colonial Dreaming, has been reframed and sometimes sanitized in mission and museum contexts. Scholars and community members work to reassert Indigenous control over the narrative and to situate it within a history of resistance and survival.

South Australian Museum booklet (historical introduction).

Educational summaries of Ngurunderi’s story, such as those on museum and heritage websites, present shortened versions that emphasize landscape creation and law-giving but may omit more sensitive ritual details or deeper cosmological teachings. This selective disclosure can be a protective measure by Indigenous custodians but also reflects the constraints of public-facing materials.

Scholars must recognize that publicly available narratives are not exhaustive and that some aspects of Ngurunderi’s story are restricted or held within community-only contexts. Attempts to reconstruct a complete cosmology from public sources risk overstepping cultural protocols. The gap between public and restricted knowledge is itself a product of colonial histories and ongoing struggles over representation.

What We Don't Know

Several important gaps remain in this dossier. First, detailed linguistic analysis of the name "Ngurunderi" and its morphemes is not available in the consulted sources; a full etymology would require access to specialized Ngarrindjeri language materials and community expertise. Second, precise publication dates and page numbers for key anthropological works (Berndt 1940, Tindale’s writings, and the South Australian Museum booklet) are not fully accessible in the summaries used here, limiting the ability to provide exact locators. Third, many aspects of Ngurunderi’s law-giving role, ritual associations, and cosmological teachings are likely held within Ngarrindjeri community contexts and may be restricted; public-facing materials offer only partial views. Fourth, the internal Ngarrindjeri debates and contemporary reinterpretations of Ngurunderi’s story are not extensively documented in open-access scholarship, making it difficult to capture the full range of community perspectives. Fifth, the environmental interpretation of the narrative by Nunn et al. is relatively recent and may be subject to further critique and refinement; this dossier reflects the state of discussion as of mid-2026. Finally, this research relies primarily on English-language sources and does not include direct Ngarrindjeri-language transcripts or community-authored monographs, which would be essential for a truly exhaustive account.

A way to hold it in your mind

If you stand on the shore where the Murray River spills into Lake Alexandrina and trace the bends of the water with your eyes, Ngurunderi is already there. In Ngarrindjeri memory, every curve of the river, every island and reef, every shimmer of the Milky Way overhead is a footprint of his journey—a pursuit of runaway wives, a chase after a giant cod, a battle with a sorcerer, and finally a walk into the spirit world. His story is not just a myth about a distant past; it is a living map of law, landscape, and loss, carried through centuries of colonial disruption and now read anew by archaeologists, elders, and curious visitors who realize that the river’s shape and the sky’s bright band are, in this tradition, the work of a single ancestral traveler.
