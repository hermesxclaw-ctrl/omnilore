Who It Is

If you stand on the shore where the Murray River spills into Lake Alexandrina and trace the bends of the water with your eyes, Ngurunderi is already there. In Ngarrindjeri memory, every curve of the river, every island and reef, every shimmer of the Milky Way overhead is a footprint of his journey—a pursuit of runaway wives, a chase after a giant cod, a battle with a sorcerer, and finally a walk into the spirit world. His story is not just a myth about a distant past; it is a living map of law, landscape, and loss, carried through centuries of colonial disruption and now read anew by archaeologists, elders, and curious visitors who realize that the river’s shape and the sky’s bright band are, in this tradition, the work of a single ancestral traveler.

Ngurunderi’s personality, as reconstructed from multiple versions, is complex and sometimes morally ambivalent. He is consistently portrayed as a law-giver and creator, establishing the rules of living, shaping the land, and providing fish and other resources for his people. He is determined and persistent, pursuing his runaway wives over long distances, chasing Ponde down the river, and confronting the sorcerer Parampari. Powers
Landscape creation and modification

Ngurunderi possesses the ability to shape and modify the landscape on a large scale. In the Dreaming, he travels down the Murray River while it is still a small stream, and his pursuit of Ponde, combined with the fish’s thrashing tail, results in the widening and bending of the river. His missed spear throws are linked to the formation of Long Island and straight stretches of the river. He creates a giant she-oak tree on Kangaroo Island, and his cast spear becomes a rocky reef at the island’s western end. His command for the waters to rise at Backstairs Passage leads to the drowning of his wives and the formation of the Pages Islands. These acts collectively demonstrate a power to transform rivers, coasts, islands, and reefs through intentional actions.

Limit: The narratives do not explicitly state limitations on Ngurunderi’s landscape-shaping powers, but they are contextually tied to his journey and specific moral or relational situations. He does not arbitrarily reshape all environments; his acts are embedded in pursuit, conflict, and law enforcement.

Creation of fish species

After Ponde is finally speared in Lake Alexandrina, Ngurunderi cuts up the giant cod with his stone knife. Each piece becomes a different species of fish in the river. This act establishes the diversity of fish available to the Ngarrindjeri people and links their food resources directly to Ngurunderi’s creative power.

Limit: The creation of fish species is a one-time foundational act in the Dreaming; the narratives do not describe Ngurunderi continually creating new species. The act is tied to the specific event of Ponde’s death.

Transformation of objects into celestial and terrestrial features

Ngurunderi can transform his own possessions and structures into enduring features of the cosmos and landscape. In one account, his huts become two hills when he abandons his camp to pursue his wives. His bark canoe becomes the Milky Way, providing a celestial pathway associated with his journey and the afterlife. His spear, cast into the sea, becomes a rocky reef. These transformations illustrate a power to convert everyday objects into large-scale features that persist across time.

Limit: The narratives do not present this power as something Ngurunderi uses casually; each transformation occurs at a significant turning point in his story—abandoning his camp, transitioning to the spirit world, or marking the end of his earthly journey.

Control over water and sea-level

Ngurunderi demonstrates a dramatic ability to command water. At Backstairs Passage, when he sees his wives wading across the shallow channel between the mainland and Naroongowie (Kangaroo Island), he becomes angry and orders the water to rise and drown them. The waters respond with a terrific rush, carrying the women back toward the mainland and drowning them. This act is linked to the flooding of Backstairs Passage and the separation of Kangaroo Island from the mainland in some interpretations.

Limit: This control is depicted in a specific moral context—punishing his wives for breaking a taboo and running away. The narratives do not describe Ngurunderi as constantly manipulating sea-level; the act is singular and consequential.

Law-giving and social regulation

Ngurunderi is consistently described as a law-giver who establishes the rules of living for the Ngarrindjeri people. His narratives encode food taboos (such as the prohibition on women cooking bony bream), kinship relations, and appropriate behavior. His punitive response to his wives’ transgression demonstrates his role in enforcing these laws. He is also associated with the broader moral order that governs who will join him in the Milky Way after death—those who follow his laws.

Limit: The specific corpus of laws attributed to Ngurunderi is not fully detailed in the accessible sources; much of this knowledge is likely held within Ngarrindjeri community teachings and may be restricted. The narratives available to outsiders focus on a few illustrative taboos and moral lessons.

Combat and sorcery

Ngurunderi engages in a significant conflict with a great sorcerer named Parampari near Kingston. The two men fight using both weapons and magical powers. Ngurunderi ultimately defeats Parampari and burns his body in a huge fire. This episode demonstrates Ngurunderi’s prowess in combat and his ability to overcome dangerous spiritual adversaries.

Limit: The narrative does not elaborate on the specific techniques or spells used in the fight; it simply notes that both weapons and magic were involved. Details of Ngurunderi’s sorcery are not fully disclosed in accessible sources.

Transition to and residence in the spirit world

After his earthly journey, Ngurunderi travels to Waleruwar, the spirit world, and takes up residence in the Milky Way. He throws away his weapons and moves to his home in the sky, where the people who follow his laws will join him when they die. This indicates a power to cross between earthly and celestial realms and to serve as a guardian or host for the dead.

Limit: The narratives do not describe Ngurunderi returning to earth after this transition; his role becomes that of a celestial ancestor. Details of his activities in Waleruwar are not elaborated in the accessible sources.

Accounts I
Ngurunderi and Ponde: Creation of the Murray River and its fishDreaming Stories summary (based on Berndt 1940 and Albert Karloan’s account); South Australian Museum booklet; ArcGIS StoryMap.

Traditional Dreaming narrative, recorded in colonial and twentieth-century texts (first published version in 1842; anthropological recording in 1939–1940).

In the Dreaming, Ngurunderi travels down the Murray River in a bark canoe, searching for his two wives who have run away from him. At that time, the river is only a small stream below the junction with the Darling River. A giant cod fish named Ponde swims ahead of Ngurunderi, widening the river with sweeps of its tail. Ngurunderi chases Ponde, trying to spear it from his canoe. Near Murray Bridge, he throws a spear but misses; this act is associated with the formation of Long Island. At Tailem Bend, he throws another spear, and Ponde surges ahead, creating a long straight stretch in the river. Ngurunderi continues his pursuit until Ponde leaves the Murray and swims into Lake Alexandrina. With the help of Nepele, the brother of Ngurunderi’s wives, Ponde is finally speared. Ngurunderi cuts up the giant fish with his stone knife, and each piece becomes a different species of fish in the river, providing food for the Ngarrindjeri people. Ngurunderi’s wives, the forbidden fish, and the drowning at Backstairs PassageArcGIS StoryMap; Kangaroo Island First Nations History article; Dreaming Stories summary (partial overlap).

Traditional Dreaming narrative, textualized in colonial and modern sources.

While Ngurunderi is pursuing Ponde, his two wives make camp and cook bony bream on their fire. Bony bream is a fish forbidden to Ngarrindjeri women, and their act constitutes a serious breach of food law. Ngurunderi smells the fish cooking and knows his wives are nearby. He abandons his camp, leaving his huts and canoe behind; in some accounts, his huts become two hills and his canoe becomes the Milky Way. Hearing Ngurunderi approaching, the women hastily build a raft of reeds and grass-trees and escape across Lake Albert. On the other side, their raft turns back into reeds and grass-trees. The women hurry south along the coast, while Ngurunderi follows them as far as Kingston. Eventually, he reaches Cape Jervis and sees his wives wading halfway across the shallow channel that separates Naroongowie (Kangaroo Island) from the mainland. Angered by their disobedience and flight, Ngurunderi orders the water to rise and drown them. The waters surge with a terrific rush, carrying the women back toward the mainland and drowning them. Accounts II
Ngurunderi on Kangaroo Island: She-oak, wailing, and departure to WaleruwarArcGIS StoryMap; Kangaroo Island heritage article.

Traditional Dreaming narrative, recorded in modern heritage and educational materials.

After the drowning of his wives and the creation of the Pages Islands, Ngurunderi swims out to Naroongowie (Kangaroo Island). It is a hot day, and he desires shade. He creates a she-oak tree that is the largest in Australia and tries to sleep beneath it. However, the wailing of his drowned wives, now associated with the Pages Islands and the surrounding waters, keeps him awake. Realizing that his time on earth is ending, Ngurunderi decides to travel to Waleruwar, the spirit world. He walks to the western end of the island and casts his spear into the sea; the resulting rocky reef remains visible. He throws away all his weapons and moves to his home in the Milky Way, where those who follow his laws will join him after death. The story explains the presence of certain reefs and trees on Kangaroo Island and establishes Ngurunderi’s transition from an earthly ancestor to a celestial being. Ngurunderi’s earthly journey concludes, and he becomes a resident of the spirit world and the Milky Way. The she-oak tree and the reef serve as physical reminders of his presence and decisions. Ngurunderi and Parampari: Battle with the sorcererDreaming Stories summary (based on Berndt’s account).

Traditional Dreaming narrative, recorded in anthropological literature.

During his southward journey, Ngurunderi encounters a great sorcerer named Parampari near Kingston. Parampari is a powerful and dangerous figure. The two men engage in a fierce battle, using both physical weapons and magical powers. The fight is intense and prolonged, but Ngurunderi ultimately prevails. He burns Parampari’s body in a huge fire, eliminating the threat he posed. This episode demonstrates Ngurunderi’s strength, courage, and mastery of both martial and spiritual techniques, and it reinforces his role as protector and law-giver. Parampari is defeated and destroyed, removing a source of danger from the landscape. Ngurunderi’s status as a powerful ancestor capable of overcoming sorcerers is affirmed. The story may also serve as a warning about the misuse of magic and the consequences of opposing the ancestral law-giver.

Ngurunderi as law-giver and shaper of Ngarrindjeri cultureSouth Australian Museum booklet; Dreaming Stories educational materials.

Traditional Dreaming framework, interpreted in modern museum and educational contexts.

Beyond specific episodes, Ngurunderi’s story functions as a comprehensive framework for Ngarrindjeri culture. Museum and educational materials use his Dreaming track to introduce topics such as bark canoe construction, fishing techniques, net-making, hunting and food gathering, plant medicine, families and clans, shelter and clothing, initiation, games and wood-carving, basketry, reeds and grass-trees, family conflict and law, ancient tools, and death and the afterlife. Ngurunderi is presented as the main ancestor who established laws and social practices before leaving the earth to live in the sky. His narrative thus serves as a backbone for understanding Ngarrindjeri relationships to land, water, animals, plants, and each other. Ngurunderi’s law-giving role shapes Ngarrindjeri social structure, religious life, and material culture. His story provides a coherent way to link specific practices and places to ancestral authority.
