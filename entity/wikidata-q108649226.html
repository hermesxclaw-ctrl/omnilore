Who It Is

Among the 108 young nobles who ate Odysseus out of house and home, Pseras of Dulichium is a name without a face — one of 57 men from a single island who crossed the sea to claim Penelope, and who never sailed home. He has no speeches, no lineage, and no deeds beyond his presence in the doomed crowd, yet his single attestation preserves how Greek myth remembered collective guilt: not as one villain, but as a generation of aristocrats who mistook hospitality for entitlement and paid for it together.

No individual personality, speech, or motive is attributed to Pseras. Collectively, the Dulichian suitors are characterized in Odyssey and Apollodorus as violators of xenia (guest-host reciprocity): they occupy Odysseus' oikos uninvited for c. 3-4 years, consume his livestock and wine, pressure Penelope to remarry, plot to murder Telemachus (Od. 16.364-392), and ignore omens and counsel (Halitherses, Mentor). Name & Tongue

Etymology — Ancient Greek Ψηρᾶς (Psēras). Etymology unattested in ancient sources and not explained by Apollodorus. No secure derivation in LSJ or Beekes' Etymological Dictionary of Greek. Formally resembles ψήρ (psēr) / ψαρός (psaros, 'starling, speckled') and ψήρα (psēra, 'itch, mange') but no ancient author

Powers
Mortal Aristocratic Status

Pseras is a mortal human, specifically a Dulichian nobleman eligible to compete for Penelope's hand and the kingship of Ithaca that marriage would confer. His 'power' is social: wealth sufficient to leave his homeland and sustain a long residence as a suitor, and presumably martial training expected of elite youths (use of spear, sword, bow). No supernatural ability is attributed.

Limit: Mortal; subject to death by weapons. No divine parentage, metamorphosis, or magical protection. Fails to string Odysseus' bow (Od. 21.118-185, collective failure of all suitors).

Violation of Xenia (Narrative Function, Not Power)

As a suitor, Pseras participates in the collective abuse of hospitality: consuming another man's oikos without reciprocity, courting his wife under duress, and plotting against his heir. In Greek moral cosmology this is not a power but a transgression that invites divine retribution (themn).

Limit: The transgression is punished by death; no source suggests Pseras repents, is spared, or is singled out for clemency (unlike the bard Phemius and herald Medon, spared in Od. 22.330-380).

Weapon Use (Implied, Not Attested Individually)

During the Mnesterophonia, the suitors attempt to arm themselves (Od. 22.101-112, Melanthius fetches armor). Pseras would have fought with spear/sword alongside the others before being killed by Odysseus, Telemachus, Eumaeus or Philoetius. No individual feat or duel is recorded for him.

Limit: Unsuccessful; all suitors die. No source credits Pseras with wounding any defender. Apollodorus states Odysseus killed them 'with the help of Eumaeus, Philoetius, and Telemachus' without assigning individual kills except for a few Homeric leaders.

Accounts I
The Catalogue of the Dulichian SuitorsApollodorus, Bibliotheca, Epitome 7.26

1st-2nd century CE (compilation); reflects Hellenistic scholarly systematization of epic material

Apollodorus, summarizing the lost epic and tragic traditions, enumerates the 108 suitors of Penelope by homeland. From Dulichium, the largest contingent, he lists 57 names in a single catalogue: '...Pseras...' among them, alongside Sostratus, Halius, Polyctor and others. The catalogue has no narrative beyond the names; it functions as a reference list to give substance to the anonymous mass described by Homer. Pseras appears here and nowhere else as a named individual. The list establishes his origin (Dulichium), his status (mnester, wooer), and his inclusion in the collective fate. Provides the sole onomastic attestation for Pseras; without this catalogue he would be entirely anonymous. Establishes the Apollodoran total of 57 Dulichians vs Homer's 52, a discrepancy noted by scholars.

The Occupation of Odysseus' PalaceHomer, Odyssey Books 1-16; summarized in Apollodorus Epitome 7.26-32

c. 750-650 BCE (Homer); 1st-2nd century CE (Apollodorus summary)

With Odysseus absent for ten years at Troy and ten years wandering, 108 young nobles from Dulichium, Same, Zacynthus and Ithaca itself settle in his palace on Ithaca. They feast daily on his cattle, sheep, goats and wine, compel his household to serve them, and press Penelope to choose a new husband, arguing that the king is dead. Pseras, as one of the Dulichian contingent, is implied to have participated in this multi-year occupation (c. 3-4 years of intense wooing). They reject Telemachus' demand to leave (Od. 1.368-380, 2.138-145) and ignore omens (the eagle portent in Od. 2.146-154, Halitherses' prophecy). Creates the moral and legal justification for the suitors' eventual killing; establishes the violation of xenia that the Odyssey presents as punishable by gods and men. Pseras shares collective culpability.

Penelope's Stratagem of the WebHomer, Odyssey 2.93-110, 19.137-156, 24.129-148; Apollodorus Epitome 7.31

c. 750-650 BCE; summarized 1st-2nd century CE

To delay remarriage, Penelope announces she will choose a suitor after she finishes weaving a shroud for Laertes, Odysseus' father. For three years she weaves by day and unravels by night, deceiving the suitors. When a maid betrays her, the suitors discover the trick and demand an immediate decision. Pseras is among those deceived and then angered by the revelation; the episode heightens tension and demonstrates Penelope's metis against the suitors' pressure. Apollodorus notes the trick briefly: 'Penelope tricked them by weaving.' Delays the crisis until Odysseus' return; demonstrates suitors' entitlement and lack of perception. No suitor, including Pseras, is said to have seen through the trick independently.

Accounts II
The Contest of the BowHomer, Odyssey Book 21; Apollodorus Epitome 7.32

c. 750-650 BCE; summarized 1st-2nd century CE

Penelope, prompted by Athena and Odysseus (disguised as a beggar), proposes a contest: whoever strings Odysseus' great bow and shoots through twelve axe heads will win her hand. Leodes fails first, then all suitors in turn attempt and fail to string the bow — Telemachus nearly succeeds but is stopped by Odysseus. The bow is then given to the disguised Odysseus. As a Dulichian suitor, Pseras is among those who attempt and fail; Homer describes the collective failure ('no one could string it') without naming each attempter. Apollodorus: 'they were unable to bend the bow.' Proves no suitor is worthy to replace Odysseus; legitimizes Odysseus' subsequent killing as both marital contest and divine test. Pseras' failure is collective, not individual.

The Mnesterophonia — The Slaughter of the SuitorsHomer, Odyssey Book 22; Apollodorus, Bibliotheca, Epitome 7.33

c. 750-650 BCE (Homer); 1st-2nd century CE (Apollodorus)

After stringing the bow, Odysseus shoots Antinous first, reveals himself, and with Telemachus, the swineherd Eumaeus and the cowherd Philoetius, systematically kills all 108 suitors in the megaron. The suitors, briefly armed via Melanthius' theft of weapons, are trapped and cut down. Apollodorus compresses Homer's extended battle into one sentence: 'Odysseus, with the help of Eumaeus and Philoetius and Telemachus, killed all the suitors.' Pseras dies in this massacre; his killer is not individually named, but he is included in the 'all' (πάντας). Homer notes the suitors' souls are led to Hades by Hermes (Od. 24.1-14). Ends the wooing; restores Odysseus' household; triggers the suitors' kin's failed revenge in Odyssey 24 and Apollodorus Epit. 7.33-34. Pseras' death is final; no burial or afterlife individual narrative is given beyond the collective psychopompia.

Aftermath: The Suitors' Shades and the Kin's RevengeHomer, Odyssey Book 24.1-471; Apollodorus Epitome 7.33-34

c. 750-650 BCE; summarized 1st-2nd century CE

Hermes leads the shades (psychai) of the slain suitors to the underworld, where they recount their deaths to Achilles and Agamemnon (Od. 24.125-190). On Ithaca, the families of the suitors, led by Antinous' father Eupeithes, attempt to avenge them and attack Odysseus, but are defeated with Athena's intervention, and a peace is imposed. Pseras' shade is implicitly among the chattering shades ('like bats' Od. 24.6), and his kin among the Dulichians would be among the avengers, though no Dulichian father is named individually for Pseras. Apollodorus notes Odysseus' subsequent exile to Thesprotia to appease Poseidon. Provides closure to the suitor narrative and explains why no cult or memorial for individual suitors like Pseras developed — they are remembered as a cautionary mass, not as heroes. The peace treaty ends blood feud.

Pseras appears in Homer's Odyssey and has his own speeches or deeds. — Pseras is never named in Homer. Homer names only a handful of suitors (Antinous, Eurymachus, Amphinomus, Leodes, etc.) and gives only totals for each homeland. Pseras appears solely in Apollodorus' later catalogue (Epit. 7.26). Any description of his personality or individual actions is modern invention.

Pseras was a prince or had a known father/genealogy. — No parentage, lineage, or royal title is given for Pseras in any source. Apollodorus lists him only as 'from Dulichium.' Unlike some suitors who are given patronymics in other traditions, Pseras has no attested father or family.

Dulichium is definitely modern Ithaca or a known island, so Pseras' homeland is certain. — The location of Homeric Dulichium is one of the most disputed problems in Homeric geography (see Contradictions). No identification is secure; Pseras' homeland cannot be mapped to a modern island with certainty.

Pseras survived or was spared like Phemius the bard. — Apollodorus states all suitors were killed (Epit. 7.33: 'πάντας ἀπέκτεινεν'). Homer explicitly spares only Phemius (bard) and Medon (herald) plus spares the maids' lives initially; no suitor is spared. Pseras shares the collective death.
