Form

No canonical visual descriptions survive. In art, she is occasionally depicted as a matronly figure, a nurse, or a prostitute, but her iconography is not stable. Roman coinage from the Republican period may show her as a veiled female figure, but this is highly speculative.

Powers

Divine Fertility As a goddess, she was believed to have power over fertility, both of the earth and of the Roman people. Her festival was linked to the agricultural cycle and the sowing of crops. Goddess of fertility Not well-attested in specific prayers or rituals; her power is inferred from her cult and associations. None directly, but her fertility role is often overshadowed by her other, more narrative, roles.

Foster-Motherhood In the Romulus and Remus myth, she is the human nurse who raises the twins. This role grants her a symbolic 'mother of Rome' status. Nurse of Romulus and Remus This is a narrative role, not a supernatural power. Conflicts with the courtesan tradition, where she has no children.

Chthonic Authority As 'Mater Larum' (Mother of the Lares) or a goddess of the dead, she holds sway over the spirits of the ancestors and the underworld. Goddess of the Lares and the dead This is an inferred role, based on the nature of the Larentalia and its association with the dead. The identification with the Lares is contested; some see her as their mother, others as just a festival honoree.

Wealth and Generosity In the courtesan tradition, she acquires immense wealth and bequeaths it to the Roman people, effectively founding a public trust. This grants her a legendary posthumous power over Roman civic life. Courtesan of Hercules This is a legendary power, not a supernatural one. The source of her wealth differs: from the god Hercules or from a rich husband named Tarrutius.

Behavior & Habitat

Acca Larentia's behavior varies wildly depending on the myth. As a nurse, she is nurturing, devoted, and protective. As a prostitute, she is shrewd, opportunistic, and ultimately generous. As a goddess, she is a chthonic fertility figure associated with the earth and the dead.

Accounts

The shepherd Faustulus finds the abandoned twins Romulus and Remus being suckled by a she-wolf (lupa) at the foot of the Palatine Hill. He takes them home to his wife, Acca Larentia, who raises them as her own sons. This story positions Acca as a simple, virtuous matron, the human counterpart to the miraculous she-wolf. Some sources (like Livy) wryly note that 'lupa' could also mean 'prostitute', hinting at the other tradition.

During the reign of King Ancus Marcius (or another king), the custodian of the temple of Hercules grew bored and challenged the god to a game of dice. The stakes: a meal and a woman. Hercules won, and the custodian, to fulfill his side, hired the most famous prostitute in Rome, Acca Larentia, and locked her in the temple for the night. Hercules slept with her and, as payment, told her to go to the forum the next morning and marry the first man she met. That man was a wealthy Etruscan named Tarrutius. When Tarrutius died, he left everything to Acca, who in turn left it all to the Roman people. The grateful Romans instituted the Larentalia in her honor.

A separate tradition identifies Acca Larentia with Larunda (or Mania), the mother of the Lares, the ancestral guardian spirits of the Roman household. In this role, she is a chthonic mother figure, presiding over the dead and the hearth.

Scholarly Debates
Identity: Nurse vs. Prostitute
She is the virtuous wife of Faustulus and nurse of Romulus and Remus. ⇌ She is a prostitute who sleeps with Hercules and marries a rich man.
Evidence for A: Livy, Plutarch (Romulus 4-5), Dionysius of Halicarnassus (1.84)
Evidence for B: Ovid (Fasti 2.413-434), Aulus Gellius (7.7), Macrobius (1.10)
Her Connection to the Lares
She is the mother of the Lares (Mater Larum). ⇌ She is just the namesake of the Larentalia, which is a festival for the dead, not the Lares.
Evidence for A: Macrobius (1.10), some scholia
Evidence for B: Cicero (ad Brutum 1.15), which only mentions the festival for the dead
The Nature of Hercules' 'Gift'
Hercules tells her to marry the first man she meets, which is a wise and generous gift. ⇌ Hercules essentially pimps her out, and the 'gift' is a way to get rid of her.
Evidence for A: Ovid (Fasti 2.413-434)
Evidence for B: Aulus Gellius (7.7), where the god 'tore her garment'
What We Don't Know

The primary gap is the lack of a single, coherent narrative. The two major traditions (nurse vs. prostitute) are fundamentally incompatible, and no ancient source attempts to reconcile them. The exact nature of the Larentalia ritual is also unclear; we know it involved sacrifices at her tomb, but the precise details are lost. The connection to the Lares is plausible but unproven. Most scholarship is in English, German, and French; Italian scholarship is also significant but less accessible. A comprehensive review of all epigraphic evidence for the Larentalia would be valuable.

A way to hold it in your mind

Acca Larentia is the Roman equivalent of a folk saint who started as a sex worker and became a patron of the city. She's like a combination of the Virgin Mary (as the mother of the founders) and a savvy businesswoman who outsmarted a god. If Rome were a movie, she'd be the clever courtesan who ends up running the city — and then becomes a ghost who protects it.
