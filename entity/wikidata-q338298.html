Who It Is

Imagine a goddess who was a prostitute, a nurse, a millionaire, and the mother of ghosts — all at once. That's Acca Larentia, the Roman deity whose festival on December 23rd was as confusing as her biography. One story says she raised the founders of Rome; another says she won her fortune by sleeping with a god in a dice game. The Romans didn't care if the stories contradicted each other. They just knew that on the darkest day of the year, you made an offering to Acca Larentia, or the dead might get restless.

Acca Larentia's behavior varies wildly depending on the myth. As a nurse, she is nurturing, devoted, and protective. As a prostitute, she is shrewd, opportunistic, and ultimately generous. As a goddess, she is a chthonic fertility figure associated with the earth and the dead.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name is likely connected to the Lares, the guardian deities of the household and crossroads in Roman religion. The epithet 'Larentia' or 'Larentina' may derive from 'Lares' or from an older Etruscan root. The name 'Acca' is often interpreted as a motherly or nurse figure, possibly from an archai

Pronunciation — AH-kah lah-REN-tee-ah (Classical Latin: /ˈak.ka laˈren.ti.a/)

Aliases — Acca Larentina, Larentia, Larentina, Mater Larum, Larunda, Mania

Ownership — Roman (ancient Italic), with Etruscan influences. The myth and cult are firmly Roman, though some scholars suggest an Etruscan origin for the name and the associated Lares.

Powers
Divine Fertility

As a goddess, she was believed to have power over fertility, both of the earth and of the Roman people. Her festival was linked to the agricultural cycle and the sowing of crops.

Limit: Not well-attested in specific prayers or rituals; her power is inferred from her cult and associations.

Foster-Motherhood

In the Romulus and Remus myth, she is the human nurse who raises the twins. This role grants her a symbolic 'mother of Rome' status.

Limit: This is a narrative role, not a supernatural power.

Chthonic Authority

As 'Mater Larum' (Mother of the Lares) or a goddess of the dead, she holds sway over the spirits of the ancestors and the underworld.

Limit: This is an inferred role, based on the nature of the Larentalia and its association with the dead.

Wealth and Generosity

In the courtesan tradition, she acquires immense wealth and bequeaths it to the Roman people, effectively founding a public trust. This grants her a legendary posthumous power over Roman civic life.

Limit: This is a legendary power, not a supernatural one.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No canonical visual descriptions survive. In art, she is occasionally depicted as a matronly figure, a nurse, or a prostitute, but her iconography is not stable. Roman coinage from the Republican period may show her as a veiled female figure, but this is highly speculative.

Symbols — The she-wolf (in her role as foster-mother), the Lares (household gods), the earth and its bounty, and the dice (in the Hercules myth).

Domains — Fertility, the earth, the household (in her connection to the Lares), and the underworld (as a chthonic deity). Her festival, the Larentalia, was a time for honoring the dead and the Lares.

The Nurse of Romulus and RemusLivy, Plutarch, Dionysius of Halicarnassus

Archaic (legendary)

The shepherd Faustulus finds the abandoned twins Romulus and Remus being suckled by a she-wolf (lupa) at the foot of the Palatine Hill. He takes them home to his wife, Acca Larentia, who raises them as her own sons. This story positions Acca as a simple, virtuous matron, the human counterpart to the miraculous she-wolf. Some sources (like Livy) wryly note that 'lupa' could also mean 'prostitute', hinting at the other tradition. The twins grow up to found Rome, making Acca Larentia the symbolic foster-mother of the Roman people.

Accounts II
The Courtesan of HerculesOvid, Aulus Gellius, Macrobius

Archaic (legendary)

During the reign of King Ancus Marcius (or another king), the custodian of the temple of Hercules grew bored and challenged the god to a game of dice. The stakes: a meal and a woman. Hercules won, and the custodian, to fulfill his side, hired the most famous prostitute in Rome, Acca Larentia, and locked her in the temple for the night. Hercules slept with her and, as payment, told her to go to the forum the next morning and marry the first man she met. That man was a wealthy Etruscan named Tarrutius. When Tarrutius died, he left everything to Acca, who in turn left it all to the Roman people. The grateful Romans instituted the Larentalia in her honor. The Roman people receive a massive inheritance, and Acca is deified as a goddess of the earth and fertility.

The Mother of the LaresMacrobius, various scholia

Archaic (religious)

A separate tradition identifies Acca Larentia with Larunda (or Mania), the mother of the Lares, the ancestral guardian spirits of the Roman household. In this role, she is a chthonic mother figure, presiding over the dead and the hearth. Her festival, the Larentalia, becomes a day for propitiating the dead and the household gods.

Acca Larentia was a historical person. — She is a legendary figure, likely a personification of a fertility cult or a mythological aetiology for a festival. There is no historical evidence for her existence.

She was the 'she-wolf' (lupa) that suckled Romulus and Remus. — In the most common version, the she-wolf is a literal animal. Acca Larentia is the human nurse who raises the twins after they are found. The conflation arises because 'lupa' can also mean 'prostitute', which is the other tradition about Acca.

She was a 'goddess of prostitution'. — She is a goddess of fertility and the earth, and she was a prostitute in one of her myths. This is a significant distinction. She is not a patroness of sex work; she is a chthonic fertility figure who happened to have been a prostitute in her mortal life.

The Larentalia was a wild, orgiastic festival. — The Larentalia was a solemn festival for the dead (parentatio), involving sacrifices at the tomb of Acca Larentia. It was not a celebration of licentiousness.
