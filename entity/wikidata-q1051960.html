The Approach

In the dawn of Japanese myth, a hunter's careless loss of a fish-hook would plunge him into the depths of the sea, win him the hand of a sea goddess, and set the stage for the birth of an emperor. This is the story of Hoori, the youngest son of the gods, whose journey from land to sea and back again forged the lineage of Japan's imperial line.

Content advisory. None
Identity

Hoori

Pronunciation HOH-oh-ree

Known As

Hoori no MikotoHikohohodemi no MikotoYamasachihiko

Etymology

The name Hoori is often written with the character 火 (fire), but etymologically it is linked to 穂 (ho, meaning 'ear of grain' or 'crop'), and 'ori' (折り) meaning 'to bend', suggesting a crop so rich it bends under its own weight. Another name, Hohodemi, means 'many harvests'.

Cultural Ownership

Japanese mythology, Shinto tradition

Disputed Identifications

Some sources identify Hoori with the mountain god Yamasachihiko, but this is an epithet rather than a separate identity. There is no significant scholarly dispute over his identification.

Description

Appearance

No specific physical description is given in the primary sources. He is typically depicted in art as a young hunter in ancient Japanese attire.

Symbols & Iconography

Associated with the fish-hook, the sea, and agricultural abundance. In art, he is often shown with a fish-hook or in the palace of the sea god.

Behaviour & Personality

Hoori is portrayed as impulsive and somewhat careless (lending and losing his brother's hook), but also brave and resourceful (descending to the sea bottom). He is homesick after three years, showing attachment to his homeland.

Domains & Habitats

Land (as a hunter) and the sea (underworld palace of Ōwatatsumi). He is later associated with agriculture, particularly rice cultivation.

Version Differences

The Kojiki and Nihon Shoki differ in minor details: the Nihon Shoki includes additional episodes and variations, such as alternative accounts of the fish-hook's recovery and the identity of the sea god's daughter. The Kojiki is more concise.

Stories & Myths

The Lost Fish-Hook

Source Kojiki, Nihon ShokiCulture JapanesePeriod Age of the Gods (mythological)

Summary

Hoori, a hunter, borrows his elder brother Hoderi's fish-hook and loses it. Hoderi demands the original hook, refusing replacements. Hoori descends to the sea bottom, where he meets Toyotamahime, daughter of the sea god. The sea god helps him find the hook and gives him magical jewels to control the tides. Hoori marries Toyotamahime and lives in the sea palace for three years.

Participants

Hoori

Hoderi

Toyotamahime

Ōwatatsumi

Consequences

Hoori returns the hook to Hoderi, and later uses the tide jewels to subjugate his brother. The birth of his son Ugayafukiaezu continues the imperial lineage.

Variants

The Nihon Shoki includes a version where Hoori returns the hook and Hoderi submits voluntarily; another where the sea god gives him a different hook.

The Birth of Ugayafukiaezu and the Wani

Source Kojiki, Nihon ShokiCulture JapanesePeriod Age of the Gods (mythological)

Summary

Toyotamahime gives birth to Hoori's son in a hut on the beach. She requests Hoori not to look at her during childbirth. He breaks the taboo and sees her true form as a Wani (sea dragon or shark). Ashamed, she returns to the sea, leaving the child, who is raised by her sister Tamayorihime.

Participants

Hoori

Toyotamahime

Ugayafukiaezu

Tamayorihime

Consequences

Toyotamahime abandons her family, and the child Ugayafukiaezu becomes the father of Emperor Jimmu.

Variants

In some versions, Toyotamahime stays long enough to nurse the child; in others, she never sees him.

Dark History

Sibling coercion

Source Kojiki, Nihon Shoki

Description

Hoori forces his elder brother to lend him the fish-hook, and later uses magical tide jewels to subjugate him, causing Hoderi to become a vassal.

Scholarly Context

This reflects ancient Japanese concepts of hierarchy and the divine right of the imperial line, but also shows the use of power to enforce submission.

Taboo violation

Source Kojiki, Nihon Shoki

Description

Hoori breaks his promise to not look at his wife during childbirth, leading to her shame and abandonment.

Scholarly Context

The taboo against viewing childbirth is a common motif in Japanese mythology, reflecting beliefs about pollution and the sanctity of birth.

Contradictions & Disputes

Reconciliation with Hoderi

Position A

Hoori subjugates Hoderi using tide jewels (Kojiki).

Position A — evidence

Kojiki text explicitly describes the use of tide jewels to humble Hoderi.

Position B

Hoderi submits voluntarily after Hoori returns the hook (Nihon Shoki variant).

Position B — evidence

Nihon Shoki includes a version where Hoderi yields without force.

Scholarly Consensus

Scholars note that the Kojiki version emphasizes the dominance of the imperial line, while the Nihon Shoki variant may reflect a more conciliatory political stance.
