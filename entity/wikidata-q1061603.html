Who It Is

In the damp corners of gardens and the margins of ponds, a creature sits motionless, its warty skin a map of ancient secrets. The toad, often dismissed as a humble amphibian, carries with it a rich tapestry of biology, folklore, and cultural significance that stretches across continents and centuries. From the parotoid glands that produce potent toxins to the mythical Money Toad of Chinese prosperity, the toad is far more than a warty frog—it is a symbol, a survivor, and a subject of enduring fascination.

Toads are primarily terrestrial, only returning to water to breed. They exhibit breeding site fidelity (philopatry), returning to their natal ponds. They communicate via advertisement calls, especially during breeding season. They are generally solitary, nocturnal, and feed on insects and other invertebrates. They have a defensive mechanism: the parotoid glands secrete a toxic substance that deters predators.

Powers
Parotoid gland toxin secretion

Toads have large parotoid glands behind their eyes that secrete a milky toxin called bufotoxin. This toxin can deter predators, causing irritation or more severe effects in some animals. It is not harmful to humans unless ingested or in contact with mucous membranes.

Limit: The toxin is not always lethal; effectiveness depends on the species and the predator. It is a passive defense, not an active attack.

Breeding site fidelity (philopatry)

Toads return to their natal ponds to breed, which allows them to recognize kin and avoid mating with close relatives. This behavior reduces inbreeding depression.

Limit: Not all species exhibit the same degree of philopatry.

Advertisement calls

Male toads produce advertisement calls to attract females and establish territory. These calls also serve as cues for kin recognition.

Limit: The calls are species-specific and vary in intensity and frequency.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Toads are characterized by short legs, a stout body, and dry, leathery skin with large bumps. The largest bumps cover the parotoid glands, which are located behind the eyes. The skin is often warty, but the warts are not pathological; they are fixed in size and present on healthy specimens. Coloration varies by species, often brown, grey, or olive, sometimes with patterns.

Symbols — In cultural iconography, toads are often depicted as warty, squat creatures, associated with damp places and sometimes with witchcraft or magic. In Chinese culture, the Money Toad (Jin Chan) is a feng shui charm for prosperity, often depicted with a coin in its mouth.

Domains — Toads inhabit a variety of terrestrial habitats, including forests, grasslands, and gardens. They prefer moist environments but can tolerate drier conditions than frogs. In the UK, common toads often climb trees to hide in hollows or nest boxes. They are found on every continent except Antarctica and Australia (except for introduced species).

The Money Toad (Jin Chan)Chinese folklore and feng shui practice

Ancient to modern

The Money Toad, also known as Jin Chan, is a feng shui charm that is believed to attract wealth and prosperity. It is often depicted with a coin in its mouth and is placed in homes or businesses. The Money Toad is widely used as a symbol of prosperity in Chinese culture.

Toads cause warts if handled. — The bumps on toads are parotoid glands, not warts, and they are not infectious. Warts are caused by viruses.
