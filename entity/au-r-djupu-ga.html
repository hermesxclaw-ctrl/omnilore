Who It Is

Imagine a woman who, in the Viking Age, commanded a ship across the North Atlantic, freed her slaves, and established a dynasty that would shape Iceland's history—Auðr djúpúðga was not just a settler, but a queen of her own making. Her story challenges the male-dominated narrative of the sagas and offers a glimpse into the power of women in medieval Norse society.

Auðr is portrayed as wise, decisive, and deeply religious, having converted to Christianity during a pilgrimage to Rome. She is also depicted as fiercely independent, having taken control of her family's fortunes after the death of her husband and son, and she led her kin to Iceland to escape political turmoil. Her generosity is highlighted in the sagas, as she distributed land to her followers and freed her slaves, granting them their own farms. Name & Tongue

Etymology — The Old Norse name 'Auðr' means 'prosperity' or 'wealth', and the epithet 'djúpúðga' translates as 'deep-minded' or 'deep-thinking', reflecting her reputed wisdom and foresight. In some sources, she is also called 'Unnr', which may be a variant or a separate name meaning 'wave' or 'beloved'.

Pronunciation — OW-thur DYOOP-ooth-ga (approximate Old Norse: /ˈauðr ˈdjuːpuːðɣa/)

Leadership and Navigation

Auðr is credited with leading her extended family and followers on a perilous sea voyage from the Hebrides to Iceland, demonstrating exceptional navigational skills and organizational ability. She is said to have commanded a large ship (knarr) and successfully landed in the Dales, where she established a settlement. This ability is not supernatural but reflects her real-world prowess as a matriarch and settler.

Christian Piety and Blessing

Auðr's Christian faith is depicted as a source of spiritual power. She erected a cross at Krosshólar and held prayers there, and her piety is said to have brought prosperity to her descendants. In some traditions, her blessing or curse was believed to influence the fortunes of her family, as seen in the Laxdæla saga where she bestows lands and blessings on her followers.

Accounts I
The Settlement of the DalesLaxdæla saga and Landnámabók

9th-10th century (Viking Age)

After the death of her husband Óleifr the White and her son Þorsteinn, Auðr decides to leave the Hebrides, where she had been living, to escape political instability. She commissions a knarr and sails to Iceland with her family and followers, including her grandsons and freed slaves. Upon arrival, she claims land in the Dales, establishing her farm at Hvammr. She distributes land to her followers, freeing her slaves and granting them farms, and erects a cross at Krosshólar where she prays. She dies peacefully, and her descendants become prominent in Icelandic history. Auðr's settlement established a powerful lineage in the Dales, and her descendants, such as the poet and warrior Grettir Ásmundarson, are celebrated in Icelandic sagas. Her distribution of land and freeing of slaves set a precedent for landnám practices and demonstrated her authority as a matriarch.

Accounts II
The Pilgrimage to RomeLaxdæla saga

9th-10th century

Before settling in Iceland, Auðr undertakes a pilgrimage to Rome, a significant journey for a Viking Age woman. This act demonstrates her deep Christian commitment and her desire to seek spiritual guidance. The pilgrimage is mentioned as a testament to her faith, and it is said that she never returned to the Hebrides after this journey, instead going directly to Iceland. Her pilgrimage reinforces her reputation as a devout Christian and sets her apart from many contemporary Norse settlers who were still pagan. It also establishes a tradition of Christian practice among her descendants.

The Legendary Auðr and Harald WarthoothSögubrot, Hversu Noregr byggdist, and Lay of Hyndla

Legendary (pre-Viking Age)

In this legendary tradition, Auðr is the daughter of Ivar Vidfamne, a powerful Swedish king, and the mother of Harald Warthooth, a legendary Danish king. She is married to Hrœrekr Ringslinger, and after his death, she returns to her father's court. This version portrays her as a political figure in the power struggles of the legendary kings, and her son Harald becomes a famous warrior. This tradition connects Auðr to the legendary history of Scandinavia, making her a key figure in the genealogies of royal houses. It also creates a separate identity from the Icelandic settler, leading to scholarly debate about whether they are the same person.
