Form

Aeternitas is typically depicted on Roman coins as a female figure, often veiled or with flowing hair. She holds various attributes: a phoenix (symbol of rebirth and long life), a globe (representing the eternal Roman world), a scepter, a cornucopia, or a torch. She may be shown raising her hand in a gesture of blessing or holding a veil over her head, a sign of eternity. On the Column of Antoninus Pius, she is depicted as a winged or semi-winged figure carrying the emperor and empress into the heavens, sometimes identified as Aion but with clear Aeternitas-like attributes.

Powers

Guarantee of Imperial Eternity The primary 'power' of Aeternitas is her ability to ensure the Roman Empire and its emperor would last forever. She is the divine personification of the state's immortality. Roman Imperial cult, 1st-3rd century CE This is a propaganda function, not a mythological power. Her 'power' is symbolic and political, not magical or narrative. The constant need to proclaim Aeternitas on coins and inscriptions actually highlights the empire's anxieties about succession crises, civil wars, and external threats—the very things that could end it.

Symbolic Renewal (Phoenix) When depicted with a phoenix, Aeternitas embodies the idea of cyclical renewal—the empire may face challenges, but it will rise again, like the mythical bird, from its own ashes. Coinage of Antoninus Pius and later emperors The phoenix is a symbol, not a power she wields. It represents the hope of renewal, not an actual ability to resurrect or regenerate. The phoenix is a Greek/Egyptian myth, and its use in Roman iconography is a syncretic borrowing. It does not have a native Roman mythological basis.

Accounts

On the base of the column erected to honor the deified emperor Antoninus Pius and his wife Faustina, Aeternitas (or Aion, but with Aeternitas-like attributes) is depicted as a winged figure carrying the imperial couple into the heavens. Two eagles fly at her side, and the personification of the Campus Martius (the Roman field of Mars) looks on. This scene is not a myth but a political-religious allegory: the emperor's soul is immortal, and the empire he ruled is eternal.

Aeternitas is a common reverse type on Roman coins. She appears with legends like 'AETERNITAS AUGUSTA' (Eternity of the Emperor) or 'AETERNITAS ORBIS' (Eternity of the World). These coins were not just currency; they were mass-media propaganda, spreading the message of imperial stability and divine mandate to every corner of the empire. The message was clear: as long as the emperor and the gods are aligned, Rome will never fall.

Modern Interpretations

Art Renaissance allegorical paintings Various, including Giorgio Vasari and others 16th century inspired Renaissance artists and humanists revived the figure of Aeternitas as an allegory of eternal fame, divine providence, or the immortality of the soul, often stripping her of her specifically Roman imperial context. Literature Dante's Divine Comedy (Inferno, Purgatorio, Paradiso) Dante Alighieri c. 1320 inspired While Dante does not name Aeternitas, the concept of 'etterno' (eternal) is central to his vision of God, heaven, and hell. The Roman concept of aeternitas informs his medieval Christian understanding of eternal time. Game Various strategy and empire-building games (e.g., Civilization series, Rome: Total War) Various (Firaxis, Creative Assembly, etc.) 1991-present reference The concept of 'Roma Aeterna' is often a thematic or mechanical element in these games, though Aeternitas herself rarely appears as a named character. The idea of an 'eternal empire' is a common gameplay goal. Film Gladiator (2000) Ridley Scott 2000 reference The film's opening line, 'There was once a dream that was Rome,' and its depiction of the Roman Empire's grandeur and corruption, taps into the myth of 'Roma Aeterna' without ever naming Aeternitas. What We Don't Know

Detailed archaeological survey of all known depictions of Aeternitas on coins and reliefs is incomplete. A comprehensive study of her appearance in provincial coinage (non-Roman mints) would be valuable. The relationship between Aeternitas and other personifications (e.g., Felicitas, Salus, Providentia) in imperial propaganda deserves further study. The reception of Aeternitas in early Christian art and literature is only partially explored.

A way to hold it in your mind

Aeternitas is less like a character in a story and more like a logo for a corporation that promised to be 'the forever company.' She's the Roman Empire's brand identity—'Eternity, Guaranteed'—stamped on every coin, carved on every monument, and repeated in every official speech. She's not a goddess you worship; she's a promise you're supposed to believe.
