Who It Is

The Mare (Old Norse: mara, related to Old English mære, German Mahr) is the original nightmare — a female supernatural being who 'rides' sleeping victims, sitting on their chest and causing suffocation, panic, and terrifying dreams. She is the etymological source of the English word 'nightmare' (night + mare, not night + female horse). In Old Norse and Scandinavian belief, the Mare is typically a woman whose soul leaves her body at night — often a woman with joined eyebrows (a sign of her nature) or one who has been cursed or born under certain signs. She 'rides' through keyholes or under doors to find her victim, then sits on their chest, compressing their lungs and feeding on their anxiety and fear. Victims wake gasping, paralyzed, with the sensation of immense weight on their chest and an inability to cry out — the classic symptoms of what modern science calls sleep paralysis. The Mare also 'rides' horses (hence 'mare' in its equine sense may be related), tangling their manes into 'mare knots' or 'elf-locks' that cannot be untangled by human hands. In the Ynglinga saga, King Vanlandi of Sweden is killed by a Mare conjured by his abandoned Finnish wife — the spirit crushes him to death in his sleep. The Mare can be warded off by placing a knife or scissors under the pillow, hanging a scythe over the bed, or — most commonly — by blocking all openings and reciting protective charms. The Mare represents the universal human experience of sleep paralysis, culturally encoded as supernatural assault — a night-time vulnerability that blurs the boundary between the sleeping body and an invading spirit.

Form

Attested (general folklore): The mare/mara is most often described not in detailed physical terms but through its effects: an invisible or shadowy presence that sits or walks on the sleeper’s chest, causing suffocation and terror. When given a form, it is typically imagined as a female being—sometimes a gaunt woman, sometimes a witch-like figure, occasionally a small, goblin-like creature. In some Scandinavian traditions, the mare can appear as an animal (e.g., a cat or other small creature) that slips through keyholes or cracks to reach the sleeper. The Wikipedia illustration by Kasia Walentynowicz shows a cat-shaped mare, reflecting one strand of visual imagination rather than a canonical medieval description. Reconstructed: Because many accounts focus on the experience rather than the entity’s body, any detailed anatomy (height, clothing, facial features) is largely speculative and varies by artist and storyteller.

Powers

Chest-pressing and suffocation Attested: The core ability of the mare is to sit, walk, or ride on a sleeper’s chest, producing a sensation of heavy weight, difficulty breathing, and paralysis. Victims report being unable to move or cry out, overwhelmed by terror. This matches modern descriptions of sleep paralysis, but in folklore it is attributed to the mare’s deliberate oppression. Germanic and Scandinavian folklore as summarized in encyclopedic sources (Wikipedia, HandWiki, Grokipedia; ScienceInsights article on historical accounts). Attested: The mare’s attacks occur at night during sleep or liminal waking; protective measures (iron objects, knives under pillows, prayers) are believed to prevent or interrupt the attack. The mare is constrained by physical barriers in some tales, needing cracks or keyholes to enter. Interpretive contradiction: Modern scholars interpret the experience as sleep paralysis, a neurological state, whereas traditional belief treats it as an external spirit attack. The folkloric description of suffocation is consistent, but the underlying cause is disputed.

Inducing nightmares and terrifying visions Attested: The mare is explicitly linked to nightmares—disturbing, vivid dreams of threat, pursuit, or oppression. The English word "nightmare" originally referred to the spirit itself before shifting to the experience. The mare’s presence is said to generate terrifying dreams and hallucinations. Germanic and Scandinavian linguistic and folklore traditions; etymological discussions in Wikipedia, HandWiki, Grokipedia, and modern linguistic articles. Attested: The nightmares are confined to the period of the mare’s visit; protective rituals aim to prevent the spirit from approaching, thereby averting the dreams. None significant within folklore; the main tension is between supernatural and psychological explanations.

Riding horses to exhaustion Attested: The mare is believed to ride horses during the night, leaving them exhausted, sweating, and sometimes with tangled manes by morning. This nocturnal riding explains unexplained fatigue or distress in animals. Scandinavian and German folklore as summarized in Wikipedia and HandWiki. Attested: The mare’s riding is limited to night; owners may attempt protective measures (e.g., charms, iron) in stables to prevent the mare from mounting the animals. None explicit; the belief coexists with more mundane explanations for animal fatigue.

Entangling hair and creating "marelocks" Attested: The mare can tangle the hair of sleeping humans and animals, producing tight knots called marelocks (marflätor/martovor in Swedish; marefletter/marefloker in Norwegian). These tangles are interpreted as physical traces of the mare’s nocturnal activity. Scandinavian folklore (Wikipedia, HandWiki). Attested: This effect is limited to hair and is a secondary sign rather than the primary harm. Scholarly context: Some researchers connect marelocks to the medical phenomenon of the "Polish plait" (a hair disease), suggesting that the folkloric explanation overlays a physical condition.

Shapeshifting and entering through small openings Attested: In several Scandinavian traditions, the mare is said to be able to change shape and size, slipping through keyholes, cracks, or other tiny openings to reach the sleeper. This ability underscores its uncanny, liminal nature. Scandinavian folklore as summarized in secondary sources (Wikipedia, Grokipedia). Attested: The mare is still constrained by the need for an opening; completely sealed spaces are believed to offer protection. None explicit; variation lies in how literally this ability is described across regions.

Association with wandering souls or witches Attested (regional): Some traditions describe the mare as the soul of a living person—often a woman—whose spirit leaves her body during sleep or trance to torment others. In others, witches can take on animal forms and act as mares while their bodies lie in trance. Folklore interpretations referenced by Paul Devereux and summarized in HandWiki and related sources. Attested: This version ties the mare’s activity to the witch’s physical state (trance, sleep) and implies that social or ritual actions against the witch can stop the attacks. Contradiction: Other traditions insist the mare is a distinct demon or spirit, not a human soul. Scholars note that these are parallel explanatory models rather than a single coherent doctrine.

Accounts

Attested motif: A sleeper experiences intense pressure on the chest, inability to move or speak, and overwhelming fear. Upon waking, they attribute the attack to the mara, a female spirit that entered the room through a small opening and sat on their chest. The next morning, their hair is tangled into tight knots, and horses in the stable are exhausted and sweating. Protective measures—placing iron objects near the bed, reciting prayers, or changing sleeping position—are adopted to prevent further visits.

Attested motif: A farmer discovers that his horses are repeatedly found in the morning drenched in sweat, trembling, and exhausted, despite being left in the stable overnight. The community explains this as the mare riding the horses during the night. The farmer places iron objects and charms in the stable, and the horses’ condition improves, confirming the belief in the mare’s nocturnal riding.

Attested motif: A person is repeatedly tormented at night by a crushing presence, interpreted as a zmora/mara. In some accounts, the zmora is the restless soul of an unbaptized child or a wronged person. Rituals to protect the sleeper include specific prayers, charms, and social actions (e.g., ensuring proper burial or baptism).
