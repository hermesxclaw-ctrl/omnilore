Who It Is

Imagine waking in the dark, lungs locked, body frozen, certain that something unseen is sitting on your chest. In Norse and Scandinavian tradition, that crushing presence has a name—the mare, or mara—a female nightmare spirit that rides the body and mind at night, tangling hair, exhausting horses, and turning sleep into a battlefield between breath and terror.

Attested: The mare is consistently portrayed as malicious or at least harmful: it seeks out sleepers, presses on their chests, causes terrifying dreams, and leaves them exhausted, sweating, and sometimes physically marked (tangled hair, exhausted horses). It is opportunistic, entering houses through small openings, and may repeatedly torment particular victims. In some traditions, the mare is linked to witches whose spirits travel in trance, suggesting intentional, hostile behavior by a human agent. Powers
Chest-pressing and suffocation

Attested: The core ability of the mare is to sit, walk, or ride on a sleeper’s chest, producing a sensation of heavy weight, difficulty breathing, and paralysis. Victims report being unable to move or cry out, overwhelmed by terror. This matches modern descriptions of sleep paralysis, but in folklore it is attributed to the mare’s deliberate oppression.

Limit: Attested: The mare’s attacks occur at night during sleep or liminal waking; protective measures (iron objects, knives under pillows, prayers) are believed to prevent or interrupt the attack. The mare is constrained by physical barriers in some tales, needing cracks or keyholes to enter.

Inducing nightmares and terrifying visions

Attested: The mare is explicitly linked to nightmares—disturbing, vivid dreams of threat, pursuit, or oppression. The English word "nightmare" originally referred to the spirit itself before shifting to the experience. The mare’s presence is said to generate terrifying dreams and hallucinations.

Limit: Attested: The nightmares are confined to the period of the mare’s visit; protective rituals aim to prevent the spirit from approaching, thereby averting the dreams.

Riding horses to exhaustion

Attested: The mare is believed to ride horses during the night, leaving them exhausted, sweating, and sometimes with tangled manes by morning. This nocturnal riding explains unexplained fatigue or distress in animals.

Limit: Attested: The mare’s riding is limited to night; owners may attempt protective measures (e.g., charms, iron) in stables to prevent the mare from mounting the animals.

Entangling hair and creating "marelocks"

Attested: The mare can tangle the hair of sleeping humans and animals, producing tight knots called marelocks (marflätor/martovor in Swedish; marefletter/marefloker in Norwegian). These tangles are interpreted as physical traces of the mare’s nocturnal activity.

Limit: Attested: This effect is limited to hair and is a secondary sign rather than the primary harm.

Shapeshifting and entering through small openings

Attested: In several Scandinavian traditions, the mare is said to be able to change shape and size, slipping through keyholes, cracks, or other tiny openings to reach the sleeper. This ability underscores its uncanny, liminal nature.

Limit: Attested: The mare is still constrained by the need for an opening; completely sealed spaces are believed to offer protection.

Association with wandering souls or witches

Attested (regional): Some traditions describe the mare as the soul of a living person—often a woman—whose spirit leaves her body during sleep or trance to torment others. In others, witches can take on animal forms and act as mares while their bodies lie in trance.

Limit: Attested: This version ties the mare’s activity to the witch’s physical state (trance, sleep) and implies that social or ritual actions against the witch can stop the attacks.

Pre-modern and early modern folklore (chronology reconstructed from linguistic and ethnographic evidence; precise dates for individual tales are largely unknown in the consulted sources).

Attested motif: A sleeper experiences intense pressure on the chest, inability to move or speak, and overwhelming fear. Upon waking, they attribute the attack to the mara, a female spirit that entered the room through a small opening and sat on their chest. The next morning, their hair is tangled into tight knots, and horses in the stable are exhausted and sweating. Protective measures—placing iron objects near the bed, reciting prayers, or changing sleeping position—are adopted to prevent further visits. Attested: The victim suffers physical exhaustion, fear, and sometimes long-term anxiety about sleep. Socially, repeated attacks may lead to suspicion of witches or ill-wishers in the community, prompting accusations or ritual countermeasures.

Accounts II
Germanic mare and exhausted horsesGerman and Scandinavian folklore motifs summarized in Wikipedia and HandWiki.

Early modern rural folklore (exact dating of individual stories not provided in the consulted sources).

Attested motif: A farmer discovers that his horses are repeatedly found in the morning drenched in sweat, trembling, and exhausted, despite being left in the stable overnight. The community explains this as the mare riding the horses during the night. The farmer places iron objects and charms in the stable, and the horses’ condition improves, confirming the belief in the mare’s nocturnal riding. Attested: The belief shapes practical behavior—protective charms, rearrangement of stables—and reinforces the idea of nocturnal spirits interacting with domestic animals.
