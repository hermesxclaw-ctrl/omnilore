Who It Is

Marpsius never speaks, never fights alone, and never gets a backstory — he is one of 57 young nobles from Dulichium who sailed to Ithaca to claim Penelope's hand, ate another man's livestock for three years, and died in a hail of arrows when that man came home.

No individualized personality is attested. Collectively, the suitors from Dulichium are characterized in Odyssey and Apollodorus as: occupying Odysseus's oikos, consuming his livestock and wine, pressuring Penelope to choose a new husband, plotting to murder Telemachus (Odyssey 4.660-672, 16.371-392), and violating xenia (guest-host law). Whether Marpsius participated in the plot against Telemachus or was a more passive follower like Amphinomus is unattested. Apollodorus adds no moral differentiation among the Dulichians. Status: collective behavior only; individual agency unknown.

Mortal aristocratic status

As a suitor, Marpsius holds the social power of a noble youth eligible for kingship through marriage to Penelope. This is political/social capital, not supernatural ability. No divine parentage, metamorphosis, or heroic feat is attributed.

Limit: Mortal; subject to hunger, wounds, death. No resistance to weapons. His status depends entirely on Penelope's choice and does not confer authority in Ithaca while Odysseus lives.

Arms and banquet presence (collective)

Implicit ability to bear arms (sword, spear) and participate in the contest of the bow. Odyssey 21 describes suitors individually failing to string Odysseus's bow. As part of the group, Marpsius would have attempted and failed, then fought with whatever weapons were at hand during the slaughter.

Limit: Fails to string the bow - a test of strength and divine favor that only Odysseus passes. During the slaughter, suitors are deprived of arms (Telemachus removed them in Odyssey 19.4-13) and are killed while largely defenseless.

Accounts I
The Catalogue of the Suitors from DulichiumPseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca Epitome 7.26-30

1st-2nd century CE, summarizing earlier epic tradition

After Odysseus fails to return from Troy, 112 nobles court Penelope. Apollodorus lists them by homeland to systematize the Homeric total. From Dulichium come 57, including Marpsius. The catalogue is the sole attestation of Marpsius's name. It functions as a mythographic inventory, not a narrative with individual deeds, preserving names otherwise lost from the Epic Cycle. The list includes better-known Dulichians like Amphinomus son of Nisus (the most sympathetic suitor) and others otherwise unknown. Establishes Marpsius as canonical suitor in the post-Homeric tradition; provides the onomastic evidence for all later references. Without this catalogue, Marpsius would be unattested.

The Occupation of Odysseus's PalaceHomer, Odyssey Books 1-20 (collective narrative); summarized in Apollodorus Epitome 7.31

c. 8th-7th century BCE

For three years, the suitors, including Marpsius as part of the Dulichian contingent, lodge in Odysseus's hall on Ithaca. They consume his cattle, sheep, goats, and wine, pressure Penelope to remarry, offer gifts, and abuse hospitality. Penelope delays by weaving and unweaving Laertes's shroud (Odyssey 2.93-110, 19.137-156). The suitors, while courting, effectively besiege the household economically and politically. Marpsius has no individual speech or action; his participation is collective. Creates the crisis that necessitates Odysseus's revenge. Establishes the suitors' guilt under Greek concepts of xenia and themis. Sets up Telemachus's maturation and Athena's intervention.

The Plot to Murder TelemachusHomer, Odyssey 4.660-672; 16.371-392

c. 8th-7th century BCE

Led by Antinous, a faction of suitors ambush Telemachus on his return from Pylos and Sparta, planning to kill him at sea near Asteris. The plot fails when Athena warns Telemachus and he evades them. Whether all suitors, including Marpsius, conspired is not specified; Homer says 'the suitors' plotted, with Antinous as instigator and Amphinomus dissenting (16.400-406). By inclusion in the collective, Marpsius is implicated in the tradition of attempted murder of a youth, but no source singles him out. Heightens the suitors' criminality and justifies their later killing. Demonstrates Telemachus's transition from passive youth to active hero who must be protected by the gods.

Accounts II
The Contest of the BowHomer, Odyssey Book 21; Apollodorus Epitome 7.32-33

c. 8th-7th century BCE

Penelope, prompted by Athena and Odysseus (disguised as a beggar), proposes to marry whoever can string Odysseus's great bow and shoot through twelve axe heads. One by one the suitors try and fail; Leodes fails, Eurymachus fails, Antinous fails. Telemachus nearly succeeds but is stopped by Odysseus. The bow is then given to the beggar, who strings it easily. Marpsius, as one of the Dulichians, is among the anonymous failers. No source describes his individual attempt. Proves Odysseus's unique kingship and divine favor; exposes suitors as unworthy. Directly triggers the slaughter, as Odysseus now has weapon in hand and doors secured.

The Mnesterophonia: Slaughter of the SuitorsHomer, Odyssey Book 22; Pseudo-Apollodorus Epitome 7.33

c. 8th-7th century BCE (Homer); 1st-2nd century CE (Apollodorus)

With the palace doors barred by Eumaeus and Philoetius, Odysseus, joined by Telemachus, Eumaeus (swineherd), and Philoetius (cowherd), shoots the suitors. Odysseus kills Antinous first with an arrow through the throat, then Eurymachus. Telemachus brings arms for the loyalists. The suitors, trapped and largely unarmed, are killed systematically. Apollodorus explicitly states: 'Odysseus with Telemachus and Eumaeus and Philoetius shot down the suitors' - including Marpsius. No individual death scene for Marpsius is preserved; he dies as part of the mass execution. Afterwards, the disloyal maids and Melanthius are also executed. Restores Odysseus's oikos and kingship; ends the courtship crisis. In later tradition, the families of the slain suitors attempt revenge (Odyssey 24.412-548) but are stopped by Athena. Marpsius's death is final; no tomb, cult, or ghost tradition is attested for him individually, though Odyssey 24.1-14 shows Hermes leading suitors' souls to Hades.

Aftermath: The Suitors in the Underworld and Ithacan ReconciliationHomer, Odyssey Book 24.1-204; Apollodorus Epitome 7.33-34

c. 8th-7th century BCE

Hermes psychopompos leads the souls of the suitors, including Marpsius collectively, to Hades, where they meet Achilles and Agamemnon. Agamemnon contrasts Penelope's fidelity with Clytemnestra's betrayal. On Ithaca, the kinsmen of the suitors, led by Eupeithes (father of Antinous), attempt to avenge them but are defeated by Odysseus, Telemachus, and Laertes, with Athena imposing peace. Marpsius has no speaking role among the dead; his soul is part of the undifferentiated crowd. Provides epic closure and moral commentary on fidelity vs. betrayal. Explains why no hero cult for suitors developed. Athena's peace ends the blood feud.

Marpsius appears in Homer's Odyssey by name. — Homer never names Marpsius. He gives only totals for Dulichians (52) and names only a few suitors individually (Antinous, Eurymachus, Amphinomus, etc.). Marpsius appears only in the much later catalogue of Pseudo-Apollodorus, Epitome 7.27.

Marpsius had a distinct appearance, weapon, or power. — No ancient source gives Marpsius any individual description, divine parentage, or superhuman ability. He is a mortal noble with no attested iconography. Any image labeled 'Marpsius' online is a generic suitor or AI invention.

Marpsius was a prince or king of Dulichium. — He is described only as one of 57 suitors from Dulichium, not as its king. The ruler of Dulichium in epic is Meges son of Phyleus (Iliad 2.625). Marpsius's rank is 'suitor' (mnéster), implying noble birth but not kingship.

All suitors were killed solely by Odysseus alone. — Both Homer (Odyssey 22) and Apollodorus (Epitome 7.33) explicitly state Odysseus was assisted by Telemachus, Eumaeus, and Philoetius (and Athena as Mentor in Homer). Marpsius's death is a collective execution by four men, not a solo duel.
