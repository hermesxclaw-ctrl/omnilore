Who It Is

The glaistig is the Highlands' great both-things-at-once spirit: a beautiful woman from the waist up, a grey goat from the waist down, wearing a long green dress to hide the truth — and depending on the glen, she is a household guardian or a drowner of men. In her dark aspect she waits at fords and lochsides, sings a traveler close, dances with him, and takes his blood like a courteous vampire, or leads him into deep water. In her bright aspect — and this is the older, stronger tradition — she is a tutelary spirit of house and herd: a green lady attached to a family's castle, folding into her care the cattle, the dairy, and above all the household's children and its servants, doing farm work by night for a bowl of milk poured into a hollow stone. The glaistig of legend was once a mortal woman of rank, put under fairy enchantment, which is why she keeps trying to run a household. Cattle-folds observed her milk-offering for generations; when a laird's house fell or modernized and the milk stopped, the green lady's weeping was heard the night the family left — grief from something that had outlived the arrangement.

Origins

Highland tradition collected by John Gregorson Campbell and others; her name derives from Gaelic glas — grey-green. She merges the water-horror of the fuath family with the brownie-like household spirit, one body holding both possibilities.

Attested in Highland oral tradition; Campbell's collectionsEra Recorded 19th century; tradition older

Form

A pale beauty in a sweeping green gown, golden-haired, with the hind parts of a goat concealed beneath — betrayed at the dance by the click of hooves.

Powers

Water-luring and blood-taking in her dark aspect; herd-guarding, night-labor, child-protection, and banshee-like death-wailing for her adopted family in her bright one.

Weaknesses & Wards

Iron and holy words, like all her kind; cold steel through the green gown breaks the enchantment. The milk-offering binds her — and its neglect breaks her heart before it breaks the bargain.

The Approach

In the mist-locked glens of the Scottish Highlands, where the boundary between the human world and the realm of the fae blurs with the twilight, there wanders a figure both beautiful and terrible: the glaistig. Sometimes a green-clad woman of haunting grace, sometimes a goat-legged guardian of herds, she embodies the wild duality of the land itself—nurturing and vengeful, protector and predator, a spirit who has haunted the Celtic imagination for centuries.

Content advisory. Contains themes of violence, supernatural predation, and cultural appropriation; some accounts involve the killing of children or adults. The subject is rooted in Scottish folklore and should be approached with respect for its cultural origins.
Identity

Glaistig

Pronunciation GLAS-tik (with a hard 'g' and 't' as in 'stick')

Known As

The Green LadyMaighdean UaineThe Green Glaistig

Etymology

The name 'glaistig' is derived from Scottish Gaelic, likely from 'glas' meaning 'green' or 'grey', possibly referencing her green attire or her association with water and cattle. The term may also be related to 'glaise' meaning 'stream' or 'burn'.

Cultural Ownership

Scottish Highland folklore, particularly Gaelic-speaking regions; also present in Irish tradition as a similar water spirit.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars conflate the glaistig with other female fae such as the banshee or the bean-nighe, but they are distinct figures. The glaistig's goat-like appearance has led to comparisons with the Greek satyr or the Christian devil, but these are later interpretations. There is debate over whether the glaistig is a single entity or a class of similar spirits.

Description

Appearance

The glaistig is typically described as a beautiful woman dressed in a green gown, often with long flowing hair. However, her lower body may be that of a goat, or she may have goat's hooves or horns. In some accounts, she appears as an old hag, but can transform into a young woman. Her eyes are sometimes described as wild or glowing.

Symbols & Iconography

Green clothing, goat attributes, association with streams and cattle, sometimes depicted with a comb or mirror (as with other fairy women).

Behaviour & Personality

The glaistig is dual-natured. She is a protector of cattle and herds, and may assist farmers who treat her with respect. She is also known to lure travelers, especially men, to their deaths, either by drowning them in streams or by leading them astray. She is capricious and can be vengeful if insulted or if her boundaries are crossed.

Domains & Habitats

Highland glens, streams, waterfalls, and areas near cattle pastures. She is particularly associated with the region of Argyll and the Hebrides.

Version Differences

Some versions emphasize her role as a cattle guardian, while others focus on her predatory nature. In some tales, she is a solitary spirit; in others, she is part of a larger fairy court. The goat-legged form is more common in later accounts, possibly influenced by Christian iconography of the devil.

Stories & Myths

The Glaistig of the Cattle

Source Oral tradition, recorded in various folklore collectionsCulture Scottish HighlandsPeriod Unknown (pre-19th century)

Summary

A farmer leaves his cattle in a glen, and a green-clad woman appears to watch over them. She protects them from wolves and thieves, and the farmer's herd thrives. In gratitude, the farmer leaves offerings of milk and bread. However, one day he spies on her and sees her goat legs; she curses him and his cattle die.

Participants

Farmer

Glaistig

Consequences

The farmer loses his livelihood and learns to respect the fae's privacy.

Variants

In some versions, the farmer tries to marry the glaistig, but she flees when he discovers her true nature.

The Glaistig and the Midwife

Source Oral tradition, recorded in various collectionsCulture Scottish HighlandsPeriod Unknown

Summary

A glaistig asks a human midwife to assist her in childbirth. The midwife agrees and is rewarded with the gift of second sight or a magical ointment. However, when the midwife tells others of her experience, the glaistig appears and blinds her in one eye.

Contradictions & Disputes

Physical form

Position A

The glaistig is a beautiful woman with goat's legs or hooves.

Position A — evidence

Later accounts and some carvings depict goat features.

Position B

The glaistig is a beautiful woman with no animal features, only green clothing.

Position B — evidence

Earlier accounts may not mention goat legs, focusing on her green dress.

Scholarly Consensus

The goat-legged form is likely a later addition, possibly influenced by Christian imagery of the devil.

Nature as protector vs predator

Position A

The glaistig is a benevolent guardian of cattle and herds.

Position A — evidence

Many tales from farming communities emphasize her protective role.

Position B

The glaistig is a malevolent spirit who lures and kills humans.

Position B — evidence

Numerous tales of her luring men to death are recorded.

Scholarly Consensus

The glaistig is dual-natured, embodying both aspects, similar to many fairy beings.

Relationship to banshee

Position A

The glaistig is a type of banshee.

Position A — evidence

Both are female spirits associated with death in some accounts.

Position B

The glaistig is distinct from the banshee.

Position B — evidence

The banshee is specifically a death omen, while the glaistig has a broader role.

Scholarly Consensus

They are distinct figures, though sometimes confused in popular culture.

Cultural Significance

Half the castles of the western Highlands kept a Green Lady story, and hollowed milk-stones survive at farm sites. She embodies the Gaelic instinct that the uncanny is not evil or good but contracted — feed it, respect it, and it herds your cattle.
