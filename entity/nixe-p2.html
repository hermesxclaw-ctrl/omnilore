Powers
Shapeshifting (Gestaltwandlung)

The most consistently attested power across the whole complex. Nordic tradition holds the näck/nøkk to be so protean that informants doubted he possessed any true shape at all: he appears as a naked or elegantly dressed young fiddler, as a floating log or a glint of treasure luring waders out of their depth, as a black or white dog, cat, bull or horse, and in some regions as a three-legged animal. The bäckahäst or nykur is the equine mode of the same being. The Faroese material specifies a hard limit that proves the rule — the nykur can take the shape of any four-legged animal but cannot form the horns of a ram or male lamb, and so is unmasked by anyone who looks for them. Jacob Grimm read the residual animal traits of the German figures, the nix's slit ear and the nixe's wet skirt, as vestiges marking them out as 'higher beings' who could take animal form at will. Britannica's synthesis adds a further mode: the nix may also make itself simply invisible.

Limit:

Enchanting music and the compulsion to dance

Grimm's Deutsche Mythologie states the principle for the German material: 'Like the sirens, the Nixie by her song draws listening youth to herself, and then into the deep.' In the Nordic tradition the power is instrumental rather than vocal, and formidably specific. The strömkarl's lay has eleven variations, of which only ten may be danced to; the eleventh belongs to the night spirit and his host, and if it is played, 'tables and benches, cups and cans, greybeards and grandmothers, blind and lame, even babes in the cradle begin to dance'. The Fossegrim can teach a human to play so movingly that trees dance and waterfalls halt. The compulsion is genuinely dangerous — the dancing does not stop of its own accord — and the modern Swedish game Bramble: The Mountain King (2023) reconstructs it as a boss encounter in which Näcken's violin inflicts an unstoppable dancing mania on villagers.

Limit:

Prophecy and knowledge of hidden futures

In Âventiure 25 of the Nibelungenlied, Hagen finds water-women bathing on the Danube and steals their clothing; ransoming their garments, Hadeburg tells him the flattering lie that the Burgundians will win great honour in Etzel's land, and Sigelinde then reveals the truth — that none will return alive except the king's chaplain. Hagen attempts to falsify the prophecy by throwing the chaplain overboard mid-crossing; the chaplain, who cannot swim, is carried safely to the far bank, and Hagen destroys the ferry, accepting doom. The prophetic gift is thus bound to a bargaining structure (the seized garments) and to a moral one: the water-women's truth cannot be evaded, and the attempt to evade it is what confirms it. Nordic tradition preserves a narrower divinatory version: the nixie screaming like a loon announces an imminent drowning.

Limit:

Drowning and abduction

The Nixe's defining lethal capacity, and the one most clearly rooted in social function. German tradition credits the male Nix with a taste for drowning children and states plainly that he was deployed as a Kinderschreckfigur — a bogey invoked to keep children off riverbanks and away from mill ponds — and with abducting girls and women out of sexual desire. The female Nixe drowns fishermen and steals children. In Scandinavia the nixie is held to be most dangerous to women who are pregnant and to children who are unbaptised, and most active on Midsummer's Night, on Christmas Eve, and on Thursdays. The mechanism is often seduction rather than force: Goethe's fisherman is drawn 'half by her, half sinking of himself' (Halb zog sie ihn, halb sank er hin), and the brook horse's method is to offer a magnificent ride and then plunge, with the rider adhering to its back.

Limit:

Adhesion (the brook-horse's back)

A power specific to the equine form. A person who mounts the bäckahäst, nykur or kelpie cannot dismount: the Faroese account states that anyone who sits on a nykur's back sticks fast and is carried into the deep, and that a child's mere touch of the tail is enough — one Takmýri tale has a group of children stuck to the tail while the youngest, who addressed the creature by a name approximating 'nykur', breaks the spell and is saved. The Icelandic and Faroese nykur is also credited with prodigious haulage, dragging enormous stones ashore at Húsavík in Sandoy and at Eiði in Eysturoy, geological features still pointed to as evidence. The counter-power is equally specific: carving a cross into the creature's back binds it, and the Lake Hjärtasjön tale in Nerike has a servant girl bridle the brook horse and plough with it all day, the furrow reportedly still visible.

Limit:

Teaching supernatural musicianship (for a price)

The Fossegrim and Strömkarl trade their art on strict terms. The payment schedules recorded are precise and vary regionally: a white he-goat thrown with averted head into a waterfall that flows northward; a fenalår (cured leg of mutton) offered three consecutive Thursday evenings, or on a Thursday evening with the head turned away; three drops of blood; a black animal, often a black cat; brännvin or snus. If the offering is judged too meagre the spirit teaches only tuning; if it satisfies him, he seizes the pupil's right hand and draws the fingers along the strings until they bleed, after which the pupil plays so well that trees dance and waterfalls stop. Norwegian and Swedish tradition attaches the legend to real virtuosos — Myllarguten (Torgeir Augundsson, 1801–1872) and Ole Bull (1810–1880) — and the annual Årets Näck competition still crowns a fiddler who plays naked in a waterfall.

Limit:

Granting wealth, fertility and restored fortune under contract

The Nixe of KHM 181 finds the ruined miller at his pond and offers to make him 'richer and happier than ever before' in exchange for whatever has just been born in his house — which proves to be his son. In the Tiveden legend of Lake Fagertärn the näck offers a poor fisherman abundant catches in return for his daughter when she turns eighteen. A gentler contract underlies the midwife legend, widespread in German-speaking lands, in which a Nixe requires a human woman to attend her birth and rewards her generously and honestly. The pattern is consistent: the Nixe does not steal wealth into being, she exchanges it, and the exchange is always weighted so that the human undervalues what he is selling.

Limit:

Weather-sense and protective warning

German folk narrative credits the female Nixe with foreknowledge of storms, which she communicates to the humans she favours, and with giving gifts to children — the benevolent face of the same ambivalence that makes her a drowner. Nordic tradition retains a related but inverted capacity: the nixie's cry, likened to the call of a loon, foretells a drowning rather than preventing one. The Nixen who visit village dances and markets, recognisable only by their wet hems, are participants in human sociality, not merely predators upon it.

Limit:

Soul-acquisition through human marriage (Paracelsian branch)

Not a folk power but a learned doctrine with immense downstream consequences. Paracelsus, in the Liber de Nymphis, sylphis, pygmaeis et salamandris (published posthumously 1566), classes the undine among four elemental races and holds that she lacks an immortal soul but may acquire one by marrying a human and bearing his child. The doctrine drives Fouqué's Undine (1811), where the acquisition of a soul brings Undine into the reach of grief and betrayal, and Andersen's Den lille Havfrue (1837), where the mermaid's three-hundred-year soulless lifespan is exchanged for a bargain that fails. Paracelsian lore adds that children of such unions bear a 'watermark' — used by Hansjörg Schneider in Das Wasserzeichen (1997), where the protagonist Moses Binswanger carries a cleft in his throat.

Limit:

Vulnerability structure (steel, the cross, and the true name)

The Nixe's powers are described as tightly as her defences, and the counter-measures are a body of practical knowledge in themselves. Steel or iron — a knife thrown into the water or struck into the ground before bathing — repels the nixie. The sign of the cross halts her; a cross cut into the brook-horse's back binds it. Above all, naming works: calling the nixie by her true name strips her power, and in the strongest Nordic formulation it kills her outright. The Finnish protective formula reverses the positions — 'näkki onto the land, me into the water' — and Finnish runic songs cast water-borne disease back into the black mud from which it came. The magic-flight of KHM 79 shows the pursuit itself can be broken by thrown objects: a brush becomes a hill of bristles, a comb a hill of teeth, a mirror a hill of glass.

Hagen and the Danube Water-WomenNibelungenlied, Âventiure 25; retold in the Þiðreks saga

c. 1180–1210 CE

As the Burgundians ride east to Etzel's court, Hagen goes ahead to find a ford across the flooded Danube and comes upon water-women (merwîp) bathing. He seizes their garments; to ransom them, Hadeburg prophesies that the Burgundians will win great honour in Hunland. When the clothes are returned, Sigelinde tells the truth: none of them will come home alive except the king's chaplain. Hagen, testing the prophecy, throws the chaplain into the river mid-crossing and pushes him under with a pole; the chaplain, who cannot swim, is nevertheless carried to the near bank and walks back to Worms. Hagen then smashes the ferry, sealing the army's fate, and rides on knowing they are doomed. The prophecy holds absolutely; Hagen's attempted falsification is what confirms it. His destruction of the ferry converts foreknowledge into fatalism and commits the Burgundians to annihilation in Etzel's hall. The episode supplies the direct model for Wagner's Rheintöchter and is the earliest narrative appearance of Germanic water-women in a major German literary work.

Die Wassernixe (The Water-Nix)Brothers Grimm, Kinder- und Hausmärchen no. 79

collected and published 1812–1857

A brother and sister playing by a well fall in. The nix who lives there catches them and puts them to work: the girl must spin tangled, filthy flax and carry water in a bucket full of holes; the boy must fell a tree with a blunt axe. Fed nothing but stone-hard dumplings, they wait until the nix has gone to church and flee. She pursues them with great leaps. The girl throws a brush behind them and it becomes a mountain of bristles with thousands of spines; the nix climbs it. The boy throws a comb, which becomes a mountain of teeth; the nix clings her way over. The girl throws a mirror, which becomes a glass mountain too slippery to climb. The nix goes home to fetch her axe to break the glass, and by the time she returns the children are far away — and she must go back to her well. The children escape; the nix is confined again to her water, unable to pursue beyond it. The tale encodes both the Nixe's power over those who enter her element and the hard boundary of that power at the water's edge, and it preserves the international magic-flight motif in a specifically German water-spirit

A miller falls into poverty. Walking by his mill-pond at dawn he meets the nixie, who rises from the water and offers to make him richer than ever before if he will give her 'what has just been born in his house'. Thinking of a puppy or a kitten, he agrees — and finds his wife has borne a son. Prosperity returns, but the parents keep the boy from the water. He grows up a huntsman; one day, having killed a deer, he washes the blood from his hands at the pond, and the nixie rises, embraces him and draws him under. Der Fischer (The Fisherman)Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, ballad, 1779

1779

A fisherman sits by rushing water, cool to the heart, watching his line. The water surges and parts, and a moist woman (ein feuchtes Weib) rises out of it. She sings to him and speaks to him: why does he lure her brood up with human wit and cunning into the death-heat of the air? If he knew how well the little fish fare in the deep, he would come down as he is and only then be whole. She asks whether the sun and moon do not bathe themselves in the sea and come back doubly fair, whether the deep-wet sky does not beckon him, whether his own face does not call him into the eternal dew. The water surges, wets his bare foot; his heart swells as at a greeting from his love. She speaks to him, she sings to him — and it is done: half she drew him, half he sank, and he was never seen again. The Marburg Lahn-Nixe of 1615Anonymous broadsheet 'Nympha Marpurgensis Lanicola, die Marpurger Wassernix' (Marburg: Rudolf Hutwelckers, 1615); reprinted with critiques in Johann Hornung, Cista medica (1626); transmitted by Ole Worm, Danicorum Monumentorum libri sex (1643)

13 October 1615; reception through the 18th century

On 13 October 1615 a being was reported in the river Lahn near the Elisabeth Mill at Marburg. It did not look like a woman: witnesses described a rope- or serpent-like form that continually re-formed itself out of the water, and reported that the hands of those who reached for it went numb with cold. The Marburg printer Rudolf Hutwelckers promptly issued an anonymous Latin didactic poem in epic metre in which the nixe herself speaks, insisting that she is harmless and portends no evil — a publication probably calculated to profit from the live controversy over Paracelsus's doctrine of elemental beings. In 1626 Johann Hornung reprinted the poem in his Cista medica together with three critical responses by the chemist Andreas Libavius, who explained the apparition naturalistically as gases and vapours and used the case to attack both the theologians' demonological reading and the Paracelsian and Rosicrucian natural-philosophical one. The LoreleiClemens Brentano, 'Zu Bacharach am Rheine' in the novel Godwi (1801); Heinrich Heine, 'Ich weiß nicht, was soll es bedeuten' (1824); Friedrich Silcher's setting (1837)

invented 1801; canonical from 1824; set to music 1837

On a 132-metre slate rock on the right bank of the Rhine near Sankt Goarshausen, at the river's narrowest and most treacherous point, a maiden sits combing her golden hair and singing. Boatmen looking up at her fail to watch the current and rocks below, and are wrecked. Brentano's original Lore Lay is a woman of Bacharach whose beauty destroys men and who, brought before a bishop who cannot condemn her, is sent to a nunnery; on the way she climbs the rock to look for her lover's boat, sees him, and falls to her death. Heine's much shorter ballad discards the biography and keeps only the image and its effect, ending with the boatman swallowed by the waves — 'und das hat mit ihrem Singen die Lore-Ley getan'. The most successful synthetic folk legend in German literature: universally believed to be an ancient Rhine tale, it is barely two centuries old. The Rhinemaidens and the Theft of the RhinegoldRichard Wagner, Das Rheingold and Götterdämmerung, in Der Ring des Nibelungen

libretto 1848–1852; music 1853–1874; first complete cycle Bayreuth 1876

Three sisters — Woglinde, Wellgunde and Flosshilde — swim in the depths of the Rhine guarding a hoard of gold. The dwarf Alberich climbs down and tries to court each in turn; all three mock him. In his humiliation he overhears them betray the gold's secret: whoever forswears love can forge from it a ring conferring mastery of the world. Alberich curses love and seizes the gold, and the whole tragedy of the cycle follows from that theft. At the very end of Götterdämmerung, after Brünnhilde has ridden into Siegfried's pyre, the Rhine overflows, the maidens drag Hagen into the depths, and the ring returns to the water. The Rhinemaidens frame the entire Ring — first and last characters heard — and are the only figures in it with no source in the Poetic or Prose Edda, derived instead from the Nibelungenlied's Danube water-women. Their guardianship of the gold and the renunciation-of-love condition are Wagner's own inventions and have no folkloric precedent. The Fossegrim's Lesson and the Näck of Lake FagertärnNorwegian and Swedish legendry; Samuel Ödman, Chorographia Bahusiensis; Tiveden local tradition

attested from the 17th century; collected in the 18th and 19th

Two linked bodies of Nordic narrative. In the first, a fiddler who wishes to play beyond human ability seeks out the Fossegrim in his waterfall and makes an offering: a white he-goat thrown head-averted into a north-flowing fall, or a cured leg of mutton offered on three consecutive Thursday evenings, or three drops of blood, or a black cat, or brännvin and snus. If the gift is thought too poor, the spirit teaches only tuning; if it satisfies him, he takes the pupil's right hand and draws the fingers along the strings until they bleed, and thereafter the pupil plays so that the trees dance and the waterfalls stand still. In the second, a poor fisherman at Lake Fagertärn in Tiveden is offered abundant catches by the näck in exchange for his daughter when she turns eighteen. On the appointed day she goes to the shore, and rather than go down to him she stabs herself; her blood spreads through the water, and ever since the water lilies of Fagertärn — näckrosor, 'nixie roses' — have bloomed red. The Brook Horse Bridled at Lake HjärtasjönSwedish folk legend, Nerike (Närke); parallel Faroese nykur narratives from Húsavík, Eiði and Takmýri

collected in the 19th century from older oral tradition

A pale, magnificent horse appears in the mists by Lake Hjärtasjön in Nerike, offering itself to be ridden — its method being to carry the rider into the water and drown him. A servant girl, forewarned, slips a bridle onto the bäckahäst and keeps a piece of steel in her pocket, and it becomes docile; she ploughs with it all day, and the furrow it cut is said to be visible still. In the Faroese parallels the nykur draws great stones ashore at Húsavík in Sandoy and at Eiði in Eysturoy; a group of children touch its tail at Takmýri and stick fast, and only the youngest, who stumbles over the creature's true name in trying to say it, is released — after which the nykur bolts and its tail breaks. Establishes the practical counter-lore around the water-horse: steel, a bridle, the sign of the cross, a cross carved into the back, and above all the true name. The plough furrow and the hauled stones convert the legend into landscape, making the story locally unfalsifiable — the evidence is still there to be pointed at.

Undine's Marriage and BetrayalFriedrich de la Motte Fouqué, Undine (novella, 1811), developing Paracelsus, Liber de Nymphis (1566)

1811

A fisherman and his wife raise a foundling girl, Undine, who is wild, mocking and strange. The knight Huldbrand, riding through the haunted forest, comes to their cottage, falls in love with her and marries her; only then does she reveal that she is a water spirit, sent among humans so that by marriage she might gain an immortal soul. With the soul comes suffering. Her uncle Kühleborn, a shapeshifting stream-spirit, harasses the household. Huldbrand's affections drift to Bertalda, the fisherman's true lost daughter. Undine warns him never to rebuke her on the water; on a voyage down the Danube, when Bertalda's coral necklace is snatched away and Undine produces a better one from the river, Huldbrand curses her, and she sinks into the Danube weeping. Huldbrand prepares to marry Bertalda; the sealed courtyard fountain is opened, Undine rises from it, and she kills him with her embrace and her tears. She becomes a spring encircling his grave.
