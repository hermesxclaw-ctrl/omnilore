Pricolici

also known asPricoliciприколичприколик
Typefolklore
CultureRomanian
Who It Is
Form

The Pricolici always appears as a wolf or a large, fierce dog — never in human form after death. It is distinctly bipedal in some Wallachian accounts, standing upright on two legs like a monstrous werewolf, which distinguishes it from the four-footed lupine form of the classical Western werewolf. Its fur is described as matted and gray-black, the color of a wolf that has not seen sunlight for months. The face retains a vestigial human cast: the eyes are often said to glow with a faint intelligence, and some survivors describe seeing a human expression frozen in the lupine visage — the face of the violent man the creature was in life. Its limbs are elongated and powerful, ending in claws capable of tearing through doors and wooden coffins. In Moldavian tradition, it can grow to an unnatural size, reported as 'larger than any wolf in the Carpathians.' When it adopts animal forms other than wolf (bull, horse, owl, bat), the transitions are accompanied by a nauseating reek of grave-earth and decaying meat. The creature leaves no footprints in snow or mud in some accounts — a classic revenant trait shared with the strigoi — though other witnesses report paw-prints that merge into human footprints halfway along the trail.

Powers

Shapeshifting Transforms between wolf, dog, bull, horse, owl, and bat forms. The wolf or bipedal wolf form is the default. Transformation is voluntary and permanent after death — the creature does not revert to human shape.

Animal Command Summons and directs wolves, dogs, owls, and bats. The commanded animals act with coordinated intelligence, surrounding prey and channeling it toward the Pricolici itself.

Hypnotic Gaze and Voice Paralyzes victims with its howl and eye-contact. Survivors report an inability to move or scream when the Pricolici fixes its gaze on them.

Superhuman Strength and Speed Rends doors, wooden coffins, and stone grave markers. Outruns horses on mountain roads. Immune to conventional weapons — bullets and blades pass through it or fail to wound it fatally.

Invisibility and Phantom Movement Some accounts describe it as leaving no footprints or becoming momentarily invisible. This connects it to the broader Romanian revenant tradition (strigoi, moroi) of phasic materiality.

Voice Mimicry Speaks in the voice of the deceased person whose body it inhabits, using familial knowledge and remembered speech patterns to deceive and lure victims.

Conditional Immortality Cannot be killed by ordinary means. Requires ritual destruction: hawthorn stake through the heart, decapitation, and/or consecration of the grave. Failure to complete all rites risks resurrection.

Behavior & Habitat

The Pricolici hunts alone or with wolf-packs it commands through vocalizations. Its howl is described as chilling, 'sounding like no wolf this side of Hell,' and serves both to summon wolves and to paralyze human listeners with primal terror. It attacks silently and without warning, targeting solitary travelers, shepherds, and children — never groups. It enters homes through chimneys, gaps under doors, or simply materializes inside locked rooms, a trait shared with Slavic supernatural intruders. It feeds on blood and raw flesh, and accounts describe it smearing blood on its muzzle like a ritual anointment after feeding. It is malicious and deliberate, not merely animal: it stalks specific victims over multiple nights, revisiting the scene of a previous attack to finish what it started. It speaks in the voice of the dead person it was, using intimate knowledge of the victim's fears and family secrets to torment them before killing. In some Wallachian variants, it returns nightly to the house of its surviving relatives, standing outside and calling their names until they open the door.

Accounts

A traditional Romanian folktale recorded in Moldavian oral tradition tells of a man so brutal that neighbors refused to attend his funeral. Within three weeks of his burial, shepherds reported that a wolf the size of a calf was prowling the high pastures at night, killing sheep silently and without feeding in the ordinary wolf manner — the carcasses were found drained of blood. The village elder recognized the signature of a Pricolici and organized a night watch. On the third night, the creature appeared at the edge of the torchlight, standing upright, its face unmistakably that of the dead man. The elder drove a hawthorn stake through its heart; the body convulsed, let out a shriek heard across three villages, and collapsed into ash. The wolf was never seen again.

A midwife in rural Moldavia delivered a baby boy born with a caul over his face. The midwife warned the parents to burn the membrane immediately, but the mother secretly preserved it as a talisman. The boy grew into a brooding, violent man, and after his death during a lunar eclipse, the village was plagued by a wolf-like creature that walked on two legs and spoke with the dead man's voice. It was only destroyed when a traveling priest located the hidden caul and burned it, then consecrated the grave with holy water and a hawthorn stake.

A Wallachian account from the southern Carpathians describes a mountain pass haunted by a Pricolici that commanded a wolf-pack. Travelers who crossed after dark heard a chilling howl, and moments later wolves would surround them with coordinated precision. One survivor, a merchant from Pitești, reported that the Pricolici stood upright and spoke to him in the voice of his deceased father, demanding his eldest son as tribute. The merchant refused and survived by climbing an oak tree until dawn, when the wolves vanished.

Modern Interpretations

Wikipedia / Wikidata The Pricolici has a stub-class Wikipedia article (Q4378475) flagged for additional references as of January 2025. It is categorized under Romanian legendary creatures, shapeshifters, therianthropes, vampires, and werewolves, reflecting its hybrid classification problem in comparative folklore taxonomy. The Witcher (Andrzej Sapkowski, 1993) A Pricolici appears as an antagonist in Sapkowski's 'The Last Wish,' bringing the entity into the Anglophone fantasy mainstream. Sapkowski drew on the broader Balkan werewolf-vampire tradition; the Pricolici in the story is recognizable as the Romanian subtype despite the Slavic narrative frame. Romanian ethnographic literature The Pricolici is a staple of 19th and early 20th-century Romanian ethnographic collections, appearing in the works of folklorists such as Simion Florea Marian and Lazăr Șăineanu. These sources are primarily in Romanian with limited English translation, contributing to the entity's relative obscurity outside Romania. Mythical Encyclopedia (online) The modern web reference Mythical Encyclopedia has a dedicated Pricolici article, indicating sustained popular-culture interest among English-speaking folklore enthusiasts.

What We Don't Know

The Pricolici article on English Wikipedia is stub-class and explicitly flagged for additional references (January 2025). The etymology remains unresolved — no definitive Latin or Slavic root reconstruction has been published in accessible sources. The relationship between the Romanian Pricolici and the Slavic prikolik/priklyuchich requires comparative etymological fieldwork across Romanian, Serbian, and Russian dialect corpora. The regional variants (bipedal Wallachian vs. quadrupedal Moldavian) need systematic ethnographic mapping. Primary Romanian-language sources (Simion Florea Marian, Lazăr Șăineanu) should be consulted directly for attestation counts and narrative variants. Modern sightings attributed to Pricolici (20th-century wolf attacks) require anthropological investigation of Carpathian rural belief continuity. Cross-reference with Albanian Vurvolaka, Greek Vrykolakas, and Slavic Varkolak would clarify the entity's position in the Balkan shapeshifter taxonomy.

A way to hold it in your mind

In the haunted Carpathian borderlands where Romania's Wallachian and Moldavian highlands meet, the Pricolici is not a metaphor — it is an explanation. When shepherds found their flocks slaughtered and drained in the 19th century, when a wolf killed three children in a single night outside Pitești, when villagers exhumed a corpse and found the mouth smeared with fresh blood three weeks after burial, the diagnosis was unambiguous: a Pricolici had come back to harm the living. Unlike Dracula, who belongs to the literature of aristocrats, the Pricolici belongs to the folklore of survival — of peasants who buried a violent man and then had to dig him up again.
