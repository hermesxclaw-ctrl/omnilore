Form

An/Anu is rarely described in physical terms in surviving texts. He is a primordial, cosmic force. His primary symbol is the horned crown (the 'tiara' of divinity), which is the standard iconographic marker for all major gods in Mesopotamian art. On kudurrus (boundary stones), he is represented by the symbol of a star or a horned crown on a shrine. He is often described as residing in the 'highest heaven,' a place of pure, unadulterated divine light, beyond the reach of mortals and most other gods. His appearance is one of absolute, distant authority.

Powers

Source of Divine Authority (The Me) An holds the 'me' — the fundamental decrees or powers that order the universe, civilization, and all divine institutions. He can bestow these on other gods, cities, or kings. This is his most fundamental power: the power to legitimize all other power. Sumerian and Akkadian theology He is not the creator of the universe in most myths. In the Enuma Elish, creation starts with Apsu and Tiamat, and Anu is a later generation. He is the organizer and legitimizer, not the creator. Some later texts (e.g., the Enuma Elish) grant Marduk the power of the 'me' of Anu, showing that the concept was transferable.

Kingship Bestowal An is the ultimate source of kingship. The Sumerian King List states that kingship was 'lowered from heaven' (i.e., from An). He gives kings the right to rule, and his authority is the divine mandate behind the earthly king. Sumerian King List, royal inscriptions The power to rule is delegated. An does not rule on earth; he grants the authority to do so to a chosen king. The power of kingship is also said to be granted by Enlil in later periods, showing a shift in active authority.

Granting Divine Instruments An can grant powerful divine weapons or artifacts to other gods or heroes. The most famous example is the Bull of Heaven (Gugalanna), which he grants to his daughter Ishtar in the Epic of Gilgamesh to punish Gilgamesh. Epic of Gilgamesh (Standard Babylonian Version) This power is exercised through delegation; he does not wield the weapon himself. The Bull of Heaven is a powerful entity, but it is ultimately defeated by Gilgamesh and Enkidu. None known.

Supreme Arbitration An is the final court of appeal for disputes among the gods. In the Epic of Gilgamesh, when Gilgamesh and Enkidu kill the Bull of Heaven, the gods convene, and it is Anu (or Enlil, in some versions) who decides that one of the heroes must die as punishment. Epic of Gilgamesh His judgments are final and not subject to appeal. He is not always the one who enforces the judgment; that is left to others. In the same story, it is Enlil who is the primary voice of judgment, showing the active role of Enlil in the later tradition.

Accounts

The goddess Ishtar, spurned by the hero Gilgamesh, ascends to heaven to her father Anu and demands the Bull of Heaven. She threatens to break the gates of the underworld and raise the dead to devour the living if he refuses. Anu, wary of the consequences, grants her the Bull, which wreaks havoc on the city of Uruk. Gilgamesh and Enkidu slay the Bull, an act that seals Enkidu's fate.

In the Babylonian creation epic, the genealogy of the gods begins with Apsu (fresh water) and Tiamat (salt water). They beget Lahmu and Lahamu, then Anshar and Kishar, who in turn beget Anu. Anu then begets Ea (Enki). Anu is a passive figure in this myth, a link in the chain of generations. He is present but does not play a major role in the climactic battle against Tiamat, which is won by his great-grandson, Marduk.

In this myth, Alalu is the first king of heaven, but is overthrown by his cupbearer Anu. Anu rules for nine years, but is then overthrown by his own cupbearer, Kumarbi. During the struggle, Anu attempts to flee, but Kumarbi bites off his genitals and swallows them. This causes Kumarbi to become pregnant with the storm-god Teshub (and other deities), who eventually overthrows Kumarbi. This myth is a clear parallel to the Greek myth of Uranus, Cronus, and Zeus.

Content Notes

The Hurrian/Hittite myth of 'Kingship in Heaven' depicts the overthrow of Anu by his cupbearer Kumarbi. Kumarbi bites off Anu's genitals, an act of sexual violence that is both a power grab and a way to absorb Anu's procreative power. This myth is a stark depiction of the brutal nature of divine succession in ancient Near Eastern thought.

This myth is often compared to the Greek myth of Cronus castrating Uranus, suggesting a shared narrative tradition or a common Indo-European or Near Eastern heritage.

CTH 344

In the Epic of Gilgamesh, Anu is the one who grants Ishtar the Bull of Heaven, knowing it will cause massive destruction in Uruk. He does so to appease his daughter, showing a divine disregard for human suffering. The gods' council, which includes Anu, then decides that Enkidu must die for the act of killing the Bull, a decision that is a form of divine scapegoating. Anu is complicit in this injustice.

This is a common theme in Mesopotamian literature: the gods are not just, they are powerful. Human suffering is often a side effect of divine politics.

Scholarly Debates
Anu as Creator vs. Anu as Organizer
Anu is the supreme god and source of all power, sometimes seen as the 'creator' of the divine order. ⇌ Anu is not the creator of the universe. In the Enuma Elish, creation begins with Apsu and Tiamat, and Anu is a later generation. He is an organizer and legitimizer, not a creator.
Evidence for A: Sumerian hymns and prayers that elevate Anu to a position of supreme authority.
Evidence for B: The Enuma Elish and other creation myths that detail a genealogy of gods, with Anu as a later figure.
The Identity of the Bull of Heaven
The Bull of Heaven (Gugalanna) is a distinct divine being created by Anu for the purpose of punishing Gilgamesh. ⇌ Gugalanna is the husband of Ereshkigal, the queen of the underworld, and his death in the Epic of Gilgamesh is a major plot point that motivates Ereshkigal's actions in the 'Descent of Inanna'.
Evidence for A: The Epic of Gilgamesh, where the Bull is simply a divine weapon.
Evidence for B: The Sumerian poem 'Descent of Inanna', where Ereshkigal is described as grieving for her husband Gugalanna.
Anu's Role in the Hurrian/Hittite Myth
The Hurrian/Hittite 'Kingship in Heaven' myth is a direct adaptation of a Mesopotamian myth about Anu. ⇌ The Hurrian/Hittite myth is a distinct cultural creation, borrowing only the name 'Anu' and the concept of a sky god, but creating a new narrative.
Evidence for A: The use of the name 'Anu' and the general theme of divine succession.
Evidence for B: No Sumerian or Akkadian myth of Anu being castrated and deposed exists. The narrative of Kumarbi is unique to the Hurria
What We Don't Know

Further research is needed on: (1) the specific iconography of Anu in different periods (e.g., the 'star of Anu' identification); (2) the cult of Anu in Uruk during the Seleucid period, where his temple was revived; (3) the full text of the 'Kingship in Heaven' myth and its scholarly interpretations; (4) the specific theological relationship between Anu, Enlil, and Marduk in Babylonian theology; (5) the reception of Anu in modern popular culture, particularly in video games and anime, which is a rapidly evolving field.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Anu as the CEO of a cosmic corporation. He doesn't get his hands dirty with the day-to-day operations (that's Enlil's job as COO). He doesn't solve problems with cleverness (that's Enki, the CTO). He doesn't go on rampages (that's Ishtar, the unstable head of marketing). He sits in the corner office, makes the final calls, and signs the checks. His power is so absolute that he doesn't need to use it. He is the ultimate authority figure, the one whose approval everyone seeks, and the one who can afford to be completely detached from the consequences of his decisions.
