Who It Is

Before there was Zeus, before there was Odin, there was Anu — the sky itself, the silent, distant father of the gods of ancient Iraq. He was the ultimate source of all authority, the one who handed down kingship from heaven, yet he rarely lifted a finger. He was the ultimate absentee ruler: all-powerful, all-knowing, and utterly detached. While his children — the storm-god Enlil, the cunning Enki, the tempestuous Ishtar — fought, schemed, and raged across the earth and heavens, Anu sat in the highest heaven, the final judge, the ultimate authority, the one who granted the Bull of Heaven to his furious daughter and then let the consequences fall where they may. He is the god of 'because I said so' — the archetype of the distant, unassailable father figure, whose power is so absolute he has nothing left to do.

An is the archetype of the 'senior god' — aloof, regal, and delegating. He rarely acts directly in myths. He is the final arbiter of disputes among the gods, the one who grants kingship, and the one who bestows the 'me' (the divine decrees that order civilization). He is not capricious or wrathful like Ishtar, nor a schemer like Ea. He is the ultimate source of order, and his authority is so absolute that he can afford to be passive. He is a father figure, but a distant one.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name An (𒀭) is the Sumerian word for 'sky' or 'heaven'. It is written with the cuneiform sign DIĜIR, which is also the determinative for 'deity' itself, showing how deeply intertwined the concept of the sky and the divine were for the Sumerians. In Akkadian, the name becomes Anu, a direct loan.

Pronunciation — An (Sumerian: /an/), Anu (Akkadian: /ˈaː.nu/). The 'u' is a grammatical suffix in Akkadian, so the root is 'An'.

Aliases — Anu, Anum, Anu-um, Ilum, The Sky Father, King of the Gods

Powers
Source of Divine Authority (The Me)

An holds the 'me' — the fundamental decrees or powers that order the universe, civilization, and all divine institutions. He can bestow these on other gods, cities, or kings. This is his most fundamental power: the power to legitimize all other power.

Limit: He is not the creator of the universe in most myths. In the Enuma Elish, creation starts with Apsu and Tiamat, and Anu is a later generation. He is the organizer and legitimizer, not the creator.

Kingship Bestowal

An is the ultimate source of kingship. The Sumerian King List states that kingship was 'lowered from heaven' (i.e., from An). He gives kings the right to rule, and his authority is the divine mandate behind the earthly king.

Limit: The power to rule is delegated. An does not rule on earth; he grants the authority to do so to a chosen king.

Granting Divine Instruments

An can grant powerful divine weapons or artifacts to other gods or heroes. The most famous example is the Bull of Heaven (Gugalanna), which he grants to his daughter Ishtar in the Epic of Gilgamesh to punish Gilgamesh.

Limit: This power is exercised through delegation; he does not wield the weapon himself. The Bull of Heaven is a powerful entity, but it is ultimately defeated by Gilgamesh and Enkidu.

Supreme Arbitration

An is the final court of appeal for disputes among the gods. In the Epic of Gilgamesh, when Gilgamesh and Enkidu kill the Bull of Heaven, the gods convene, and it is Anu (or Enlil, in some versions) who decides that one of the heroes must die as punishment.

Limit: His judgments are final and not subject to appeal. He is not always the one who enforces the judgment; that is left to others.

Form & Signs

Appearance — An/Anu is rarely described in physical terms in surviving texts. He is a primordial, cosmic force. His primary symbol is the horned crown (the 'tiara' of divinity), which is the standard iconographic marker for all major gods in Mesopotamian art. On kudurrus (boundary stones), he is represented by the symbol of a star or a horned crown on a shrine. Symbols — The horned crown (specifically the multi-horned crown, often with four or seven pairs of horns). The star (specifically the 'star of Anu', possibly a specific star or constellation, though not definitively identified). The number 60 (in the sexagesimal system, 60 was his sacred number, representing completeness and the highest rank in the divine hierarchy). A shrine or altar with a horned crown on top is his standard symbol on kudurrus.

Domains — Sky, Heaven, the highest heaven (the 'heaven of Anu'). He is the lord of the sky itself. His domain is the source of all divine authority and cosmic order. He is also associated with the concept of kingship, as kingship 'descended from heaven' (from An). The city of Uruk was his primary cult center, and his temple there was the Eanna ('House of Heaven'). He also had a prominent temple in Assur (the Ehursagkurkurra).

Accounts I
The Epic of Gilgamesh: The Bull of HeavenStandard Babylonian Version, Tablet VI

c. 1200 BCE (codified)

The goddess Ishtar, spurned by the hero Gilgamesh, ascends to heaven to her father Anu and demands the Bull of Heaven. She threatens to break the gates of the underworld and raise the dead to devour the living if he refuses. Anu, wary of the consequences, grants her the Bull, which wreaks havoc on the city of Uruk. Gilgamesh and Enkidu slay the Bull, an act that seals Enkidu's fate. The death of the Bull leads to the gods' council, where it is decided that Enkidu must die. This event is the catalyst for the second half of the epic, where Gilgamesh confronts mortality.

Accounts II
The Creation of the World (Enuma Elish)Enuma Elish, Tablet I

c. 1100 BCE

In the Babylonian creation epic, the genealogy of the gods begins with Apsu (fresh water) and Tiamat (salt water). They beget Lahmu and Lahamu, then Anshar and Kishar, who in turn beget Anu. Anu then begets Ea (Enki). Anu is a passive figure in this myth, a link in the chain of generations. He is present but does not play a major role in the climactic battle against Tiamat, which is won by his great-grandson, Marduk. Establishes Anu's place as a senior god but not the ultimate creator or hero. Marduk, his descendant, becomes the supreme god, absorbing Anu's authority.

The Kingship in Heaven (Hurrian/Hittite Myth)CTH 344

c. 1400-1200 BCE

In this myth, Alalu is the first king of heaven, but is overthrown by his cupbearer Anu. Anu rules for nine years, but is then overthrown by his own cupbearer, Kumarbi. During the struggle, Anu attempts to flee, but Kumarbi bites off his genitals and swallows them. This causes Kumarbi to become pregnant with the storm-god Teshub (and other deities), who eventually overthrows Kumarbi. This myth is a clear parallel to the Greek myth of Uranus, Cronus, and Zeus. Establishes a cycle of divine succession through violence. Anu is a deposed god, a figure of the past. His castration leads to the birth of the next generation of gods.

Anu was the creator of the universe. — Anu was the head of the pantheon and the source of divine authority, but he was not the creator. In the Enuma Elish, creation begins with Apsu and Tiamat. In Sumerian myths, Enki is often the creator of humanity.

Anu was a active, involved god who directly ruled the world. — Anu is notably aloof and passive. He delegates power to Enlil, Ea, and Marduk. He is the final arbiter, not the daily ruler.

Anu is the same as the Greek Uranus. — They are structurally similar (both are sky gods who are overthrown), but they are distinct figures from different cultures. The Hurrian/Hittite Anu is a closer parallel to Uranus than the Mesopotamian Anu, who is never overthrown.
