Who It Is

A shadow passes over you — but when you look up, you don't see a bird. The shadow on the ground is unmistakably human, yet the winged shape above has antlers. It banks in the Mediterranean sun, a creature that should not exist: the forequarters of a stag, the hindquarters of an eagle, and a hunger for something only a human heart can satisfy. Jorge Luis Borges imagined it into being in 1957, citing a manuscript no one can find. But here's the question Borges himself would have loved: if enough people believe in a monster, does it become real?

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Coined by Jorge Luis Borges in 1957, etymological origin unknown. Possibly derived from Latin 'pernix' (swift, nimble) or Greek 'pteron' (wing). Not attested in any medieval source.

Aliases — Peritio (French), Winged Stag, Stag-Bird

Accounts I
Borges's Found Fragment — The Siege of the Roman ShipsJorge Luis Borges, Book of Imaginary Beings (1957)

Borges claims to quote a 16th-century scholar who wrote: 'The Perytons had their original dwelling in Atlantis... When they sallied forth to attack the Roman ships, they bewildered the sailors by their human shadows. The Romans believed they were being attacked by men who could fly, and their formations broke.' According to the fragment, the Sibyl of Erythraea had prophesied that Perytons would one day destroy Rome. The Perytons, however, were unable to kill Aeneas — the Roman ancestor — and were thus foiled in their apocalyptic ambition.

The Shadow That Was Not Its OwnBorges, Book of Imaginary Beings

The Peryton's anomalous shadow — that of a human, not a beast — is its most disquieting feature. Borges reports that this shadow was 'the projection of a past life, for Perytons were once men who, through some terrible sin, were transformed into these hybrid beasts.' This element introduces a moral dimension: the Peryton is not a natural creature but a transformed human, its animal form a punishment, and its consumption of human hearts a grim reminder of what it has lost.

Accounts II
The Atlantean ExodusBorges, Book of Imaginary Beings

When Atlantis sank beneath the waves, the Perytons — alone among its inhabitants — escaped destruction by taking to the air. They flew eastward across the Mediterranean, settling in the mountains of Hispania, Gaul, and Italia. This origin myth frames the Peryton as a survivor of the lost golden age, a fragment of a destroyed world that haunts its successor civilizations. The Peryton thus becomes a living fossil of prehistory, embodying the Atlantis myth in animal form.

The Heraldic Winged Deer — A Separate TraditionMedieval European heraldry, 14th-16th centuries

Independent of Borges, the winged deer or pterelaphus appears in medieval heraldry as a charge on coats of arms, symbolizing swiftness and nobility. These heraldic creatures have deer bodies with bird or bat wings on their backs — not the composite forequarters/hindquarters hybrid of the Peryton. While superficially similar, the heraldic tradition has no connection to Borges's invention and no associated shadow lore. The Peryton and the pterelaphus are separate strands of the winged-deer motif that have converged in modern perception.
