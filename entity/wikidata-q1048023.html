Who It Is

In the brutal world of Greek myth, few creatures embody raw, untamed ferocity like the Mares of Diomedes—four flesh-eating horses whose monstrous appetite was so infamous that capturing them became one of the Twelve Labors of Heracles, a task that would push the hero to the edge of savagery itself.

The mares are described as wild, uncontrollable, and ferocious, with an insatiable appetite for human flesh. They are said to be so fierce that they had to be restrained with chains or iron mangers. After being fed Diomedes' body, they reportedly became calm and manageable, suggesting that their savagery was somehow linked to their diet or the influence of their cruel master.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name derives from their owner, Diomedes (Greek: Διομήδης), a Thracian king. The word 'mares' (Greek: ἵπποι, hippoi) specifically denotes female horses, though some sources refer to them generically as horses.

Pronunciation — dye-OM-ih-deez

Aliases — Horses of Diomedes, Mares of the Thracian Diomedes, Cannibal Horses

Powers
Cannibalism

The mares devour human flesh, a trait that makes them uniquely monstrous among horses. This is their defining characteristic.

Limit: They can be sated or calmed by feeding them a specific human (Diomedes).

Superhuman Strength and Ferocity

The mares are incredibly strong and fierce, capable of overpowering and killing armed men. They are said to be unmanageable except by their master.

Limit: They are eventually subdued by Heracles, who uses his immense strength and wit.

Speed and Stamina

As horses, they are presumably swift and tireless, though this is not emphasized in surviving texts.

Limit: None.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The mares are typically depicted as large, powerful horses, often with fiery or wild eyes. In ancient art, they are shown with bristling manes and aggressive postures. Some sources describe them as having bronze or iron hooves, and they are often portrayed as foaming at the mouth. Their exact color is not specified, but they are usually shown as dark or reddish.

Symbols — The mares symbolize untamed nature, savagery, and the dangers of excessive violence. In vase paintings, they are often shown with a human victim, emphasizing their cannibalistic nature. Their feeding on human flesh marks them as monstrous and unnatural, a perversion of the natural order.

Domains — The mares were kept in the stables of Diomedes, located in the city of Tirida (or possibly other locations in Thrace, such as the region near the River Strymon). Thrace was considered a wild, barbaric land in Greek imagination, fitting for such monstrous creatures.

Classical antiquity (mythic time)

As the eighth of his Twelve Labors, Heracles was ordered by King Eurystheus to fetch the man-eating mares of Diomedes, king of the Bistones in Thrace. Heracles sailed to Thrace with a band of volunteers. Upon arrival, he overpowered the grooms and drove the mares to the sea. Diomedes and his men pursued, but Heracles turned, killed Diomedes, and fed his body to the mares. The mares, having consumed their master, became calm and were then easily led to Eurystheus. Heracles presented them to Eurystheus, who dedicated them to Hera. In some versions, they were set free and later destroyed by wild beasts on Mount Olympus. Heracles successfully completed the labor, but the mares' cannibalistic nature was temporarily sated. The city of Abdera was founded in memory of Abderos, who was devoured by the mares in some accounts.
