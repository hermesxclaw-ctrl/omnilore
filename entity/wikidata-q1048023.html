The Approach

In the brutal world of Greek myth, few creatures embody raw, untamed ferocity like the Mares of Diomedes—four flesh-eating horses whose monstrous appetite was so infamous that capturing them became one of the Twelve Labors of Heracles, a task that would push the hero to the edge of savagery itself.

Content advisory. Depictions of animal cruelty, violence, and cannibalism (the mares' consumption of human flesh). The myth involves the killing of Diomedes and the feeding of his body to the mares, which may be disturbing to some readers.
Identity

Mares of Diomedes

Pronunciation dye-OM-ih-deez

Known As

Horses of DiomedesMares of the Thracian DiomedesCannibal Horses

Etymology

The name derives from their owner, Diomedes (Greek: Διομήδης), a Thracian king. The word 'mares' (Greek: ἵπποι, hippoi) specifically denotes female horses, though some sources refer to them generically as horses.

Cultural Ownership

Greek mythology, specifically the tradition of the Twelve Labors of Heracles. The myth is part of the Panhellenic cycle and was known across the Greek world, with later Roman adaptations.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars question whether the mares were actually flesh-eating or merely untamed, and whether Diomedes himself was a historical figure or purely mythical. There is also debate over the location of the stables and the exact number of mares (usually four, but sometimes two or more).

Description

Appearance

The mares are typically depicted as large, powerful horses, often with fiery or wild eyes. In ancient art, they are shown with bristling manes and aggressive postures. Some sources describe them as having bronze or iron hooves, and they are often portrayed as foaming at the mouth. Their exact color is not specified, but they are usually shown as dark or reddish.

Symbols & Iconography

The mares symbolize untamed nature, savagery, and the dangers of excessive violence. In vase paintings, they are often shown with a human victim, emphasizing their cannibalistic nature. Their feeding on human flesh marks them as monstrous and unnatural, a perversion of the natural order.

Behaviour & Personality

The mares are described as wild, uncontrollable, and ferocious, with an insatiable appetite for human flesh. They are said to be so fierce that they had to be restrained with chains or iron mangers. After being fed Diomedes' body, they reportedly became calm and manageable, suggesting that their savagery was somehow linked to their diet or the influence of their cruel master.

Domains & Habitats

The mares were kept in the stables of Diomedes, located in the city of Tirida (or possibly other locations in Thrace, such as the region near the River Strymon). Thrace was considered a wild, barbaric land in Greek imagination, fitting for such monstrous creatures.

Version Differences

The number of mares varies: Apollodorus says four, while some sources mention two (Podagros, Lampon, Xanthos, Deinos are named in some accounts). The method of their capture also varies: some say Heracles killed Diomedes and fed him to the mares; others say he captured Diomedes and forced him to be eaten. The fate of the mares after being brought to Eurystheus is inconsistent: some say they were set free and roamed, others that they were sacrificed or destroyed.

Stories & Myths

The Eighth Labor of Heracles: The Mares of Diomedes

Source Apollodorus, Bibliotheca; Diodorus Siculus; Hyginus; various vase paintingsCulture GreekPeriod Classical antiquity (mythic time)

Summary

As the eighth of his Twelve Labors, Heracles was ordered by King Eurystheus to fetch the man-eating mares of Diomedes, king of the Bistones in Thrace. Heracles sailed to Thrace with a band of volunteers. Upon arrival, he overpowered the grooms and drove the mares to the sea. Diomedes and his men pursued, but Heracles turned, killed Diomedes, and fed his body to the mares. The mares, having consumed their master, became calm and were then easily led to Eurystheus. Heracles presented them to Eurystheus, who dedicated them to Hera. In some versions, they were set free and later destroyed by wild beasts on Mount Olympus.

Participants

Heracles

Diomedes

The Mares (Podagros, Lampon, Xanthos, Deinos)

Abderos (Heracles' companion, killed by the mares in some versions)

Consequences

Heracles successfully completed the labor, but the mares' cannibalistic nature was temporarily sated. The city of Abdera was founded in memory of Abderos, who was devoured by the mares in some accounts.

Variants

In one variant, Heracles first captures the mares, but Diomedes attacks; during the battle, Abderos is killed by the mares, prompting Heracles to kill Diomedes and feed him to the mares. In another, Heracles simply kills Diomedes and then captures the mares. Some versions say the mares were destroyed after the labor.

Dark History
Content advisory. Animal cruelty, violence, cannibalism, death.
Warning: Animal cruelty, violence, cannibalism, death. —

Cannibalism

Source Apollodorus, Diodorus

Description

The mares are fed human flesh, and Diomedes himself is fed to them. This is the central horror of the myth.

Scholarly Context

Cannibalism in myth often symbolizes extreme otherness and the breakdown of civil order. The mares' consumption of human flesh marks them as monstrous and outside the natural order.

Cruelty to animals

Source All sources

Description

The mares are force-fed human flesh, which is a form of animal cruelty. Also, Heracles' use of violence against the mares (though they are monsters) may be seen as harsh.

Scholarly Context

The myth reflects ancient Greek attitudes towards animals and the justification of violence against 'monstrous' creatures.

Violence and murder

Source All sources

Description

Heracles kills Diomedes, which is a brutal act, though it is framed as just retribution for Diomedes' cruelty.

Scholarly Context

The labor is a demonstration of Heracles' strength and his role as a civilizing force, but it also shows the extreme violence inherent in Greek heroism.

Contradictions & Disputes

Number of mares

Position A

Four mares (Apollodorus)

Position A — evidence

Apollodorus explicitly says 'four'.

Position B

Two mares (some later sources)

Position B — evidence

Some vase paintings show only two horses.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars accept four as the canonical number, but the variance may be due to artistic license or regional variation.

Method of capture

Position A

Heracles killed Diomedes and fed him to the mares, then captured them.

Position A — evidence

Diodorus and Hyginus imply this order.

Position B

Heracles captured the mares first, then killed Diomedes when he attacked.

Position B — evidence

Apollodorus implies the mares were captured after Diomedes was killed.

Scholarly Consensus

The order is ambiguous; both sequences appear in different sources, and the myth likely had multiple oral variants.

Fate of the mares after the labor

Position A

They were set free and wandered until they were destroyed by wild beasts.

Position A — evidence

Some scholia and later writers.

Position B

They were sacrificed to Hera or otherwise killed.

Position B — evidence

Other sources say they were dedicated to Hera and left to roam.

Scholarly Consensus

The myth does not provide a clear ending; the mares' fate is largely unknown.
