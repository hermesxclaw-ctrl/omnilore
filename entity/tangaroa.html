My children fill the ocean: the fish, the whale, the octopus with its knowing eye. Your canoes float on my back, your nets steal from my house. I give — and I take. The sea has no debts.

Who They Are

Tangaroa is the Polynesian god of the sea — one of the most ancient, widespread, and powerful deities in Pacific religion. Known by many names across the Polynesian Triangle (Tangaroa in Māori and Cook Islands, Tagaloa in Samoan, Ta'aroa in Tahitian, Kanaloa in Hawaiian, Tangaloa in Tongan), he is consistently associated with the ocean and its creatures. In some traditions, Tangaroa is the supreme creator god; in others, he is one of the children of Rangi and Papa (Sky and Earth) who fled to the sea after the separation of his parents. His worship was essential across a civilization whose very existence depended on mastery of the ocean — for navigation, fishing, and cultural identity.

Physically, Tangaroa is often associated with marine creatures as his manifestations. He is particularly linked to the octopus (te wheke a Tangaroa — 'of Tangaroa'), the whale, and the shark. In Māori carving, Tangaroa is sometimes depicted with fish-scale patterns and ocean motifs. In Tahitian tradition, Ta'aroa was envisioned as a primordial being who dwelled inside a cosmic shell (Rumia) before creation, containing the entire universe within himself. When he broke free, he created the world from pieces of his shell and his own body — the sky from the upper half and the earth from the lower half, with his spine becoming the mountains and his blood the oceans.

Tangaroa's relationship with other gods is defined by conflict — specifically, his war with Tāne. After Tāne separated Rangi and Papa, the children of the primal parents took different domains: Tāne claimed the forests, Rongo the cultivated foods, Tū the realm of war, and Tangaroa the sea. But a conflict arose: Tāne's children (the trees) provided the wood for canoes, nets, and fishhooks — all tools used by Tū's children (humanity) to catch Tangaroa's children (the fish). Tangaroa viewed this as theft and aggression. In retaliation, Tangaroa's children (the sea) would sometimes destroy Tāne's children through floods, tidal waves, and the erosion of the coastline — or capsize canoes and drown humans. This cosmic conflict between forest and sea establishes the dynamic tension that structures Māori environmental philosophy.

Tangaroa's worship was especially important for fishing communities throughout Polynesia. Before launching canoes, fishermen would offer karakia (prayers) to Tangaroa, asking for safety and a good catch. The first fish caught was often returned to the sea as an offering. When building canoes — a process that required cutting down Tāne's trees — careful rituals were performed to honor both Tāne (whose child was being sacrificed) and Tangaroa (whose realm the canoe would enter).

In Hawaiian tradition, Tangaroa's counterpart Kanaloa is particularly associated with the deep ocean, healing, and the underworld — sometimes paired with Kāne (the Hawaiian counterpart of Tāne) as a complementary deity. In Samoan and Tongan mythology, Tagaloa is elevated to a supreme creator level, credited with creating the islands, sky, and humanity. This variation in status — from supreme creator in Western Polynesia to elemental deity in Eastern Polynesia and Aotearoa — reflects the diversification of Polynesian religion as people spread across the Pacific over millennia.

Form

Attested descriptions of Tangaroa in Māori tradition focus more on his domain and genealogical status than on detailed physical form. He is an atua—divine personification—of the sea, lakes, rivers, and aquatic life, especially fish. Some sources note that he is sometimes depicted as a whale, emphasizing his vastness and power rather than anthropomorphic traits. Carved figures associated with Tangaroa in Rarotonga and other islands often show stylized human-like forms with elongated bodies and prominent eyes, typical of Polynesian wood sculpture, but these are regional artistic conventions rather than standardized iconography. Overall, Tangaroa’s appearance is best understood as reconstructed from carvings and metaphorical descriptions: a powerful, often abstract figure embodying the ocean’s depth and movement, rather than a clearly defined humanoid body.

Powers

Lordship over the sea and aquatic life Tangaroa is attested as atua of the sea, lakes, rivers, and all creatures that live within them, especially fish. He is regarded as the origin and ancestor of fish, with his offspring—including Punga and Ikatere—representing lineages of marine creatures. This lordship encompasses both the nurturing provision of food and the potential for destructive storms and drowning. Māori traditions; Encyclopedia.com overview; EBSCO Research Starter His authority is primarily over aquatic domains; land, forests, and birds fall under Tāne Mahuta, and storms under Tāwhirimātea. Human access to his resources is mediated by ritual offerings and respect; failure to observe proper protocol can result in danger or poor catches. In some Polynesian traditions, the cognate deity (Tagaloa/Ta‘aroa) is also a creator god, expanding his powers beyond the sea. Māori sources, however, consistently treat Tangaroa as a departmental atua rather than a sole creator, creating tension in pan-Polynesian reconstructions.

Control of tides (Tangaroa-whakamau-tai) Under the epithet Tangaroa-whakamau-tai, Tangaroa is recognized as controller of the tides. This power reflects the rhythmic rise and fall of the sea and is invoked by seafarers and fishers who depend on tidal cycles for safe travel and successful harvest. Māori traditions; Encyclopedia.com; Wikipedia summary Control of tides is a specific aspect of his broader oceanic authority and does not necessarily imply control over all weather phenomena, which are often attributed to Tāwhirimātea. No major internal contradictions are recorded regarding this epithet; differences lie mainly in how strongly various iwi emphasize this aspect of Tangaroa’s power.

Ancestral power over fish and reptiles Tangaroa’s son Punga has two children: Ikatere, ancestor of fish, and Tū-te-wehiwehi (or Tū-te-wanawana), ancestor of reptiles. When Tāwhirimātea attacks, fish seek shelter in the sea and reptiles in the forests, establishing enduring domains. Tangaroa’s ancestral power thus extends to these lineages, and his relationship with Tāne Mahuta is shaped by the latter’s sheltering of reptilian descendants. Māori traditions as summarized in Wikipedia and ethnographic literature The narrative emphasizes genealogical and domain allocation rather than explicit magical abilities; Tangaroa’s influence over reptiles is indirect through ancestry and contested by Tāne’s forest domain. Some versions differ on the exact names and roles of descendants, and not all Māori iwi emphasize reptiles in Tangaroa’s lineage, leading to variation in how broadly his ancestral power is understood.

Patronage of seafaring and fishing Tangaroa is frequently appealed to by seafarers and fishers. Offerings are made to him before setting out to sea for travel or fishing, acknowledging his control over the ocean and its creatures. Successful voyages and abundant catches are seen as signs of his favor. Māori traditions; Encyclopedia.com; EBSCO Research Starter Patronage is conditional on proper ritual observance and respect. Human skill, weather, and other atua also influence outcomes, so Tangaroa’s favor does not guarantee safety or success. Missionary and colonial accounts sometimes downplay or reinterpret this patronage within Christian frameworks, leading to conflicting portrayals of Tangaroa as either a 'pagan god' to be rejected or a symbolic figure of nature.

Elemental association with cold and stone (Te Anu-matao, Poutini, Te Pounamu) In some legends, Tangaroa marries Te Anu-matao (chilling cold), and they are parents of atua of the fish class, including Te Whata-uira-a-Tangawa, Te Whatukura, Poutini, and Te Pounamu. These associations link Tangaroa to cold waters and to stone, particularly greenstone (pounamu), which is culturally significant in Aotearoa. Māori traditions summarized in Wikipedia and ethnographic sources These powers are largely genealogical and symbolic; explicit control over temperature or stone is not elaborated in surviving narratives. Different tribal traditions vary in the list and roles of these offspring, and some do not connect Tangaroa directly to pounamu, making this association regionally specific.

Fire deity role in Manihiki In Manihiki (Cook Islands), Tangaroa is described as a fire deity from whom Māui steals fire. This role parallels, but does not duplicate, the Māori story in which Māui obtains fire from the goddess Mahuika. Cook Islands traditions summarized in Wikipedia and comparative Polynesian studies This fire role is localized to Manihiki and does not appear in Māori cosmology, where Tangaroa remains primarily a sea god. The divergence between Manihiki and Māori traditions creates a clear contradiction in elemental association: sea versus fire. Scholars debate whether this reflects a transformation of the same deity or separate local gods sharing a name.

Accounts

In Māori cosmology, the world begins with Ranginui (Sky Father) and Papatūānuku (Earth Mother) locked in a close embrace, their children confined in darkness between them. The departmental atua—Tāne Mahuta, Tangaroa, Rongo, Tū, Haumia, and others—debate how to gain space and light. Eventually, Tāne forces his parents apart, creating the world of light. Tangaroa joins his brothers in this forcible separation. After the separation, Tāwhirimātea, atua of storms, is enraged and attacks his brothers. Tangaroa, assaulted by storms, flees and hides in the sea, establishing his domain. His descendants, especially fish, remain with him in the ocean, while some—reptiles—seek refuge in the forests of Tāne. This narrative explains the opposition between sea and land and the enduring tension between Tangaroa and Tāne.

Tangaroa fathers Punga, who in turn has two children: Ikatere and Tū-te-wehiwehi (also known as Tū-te-wanawana). Ikatere becomes ancestor of fish, while Tū-te-wehiwehi becomes ancestor of reptiles. When Tāwhirimātea unleashes storms against the other atua, Ikatere and his fish descendants seek shelter in the sea, returning to Tangaroa’s domain. Tū-te-wehiwehi and the reptiles flee to the forests, finding refuge with Tāne Mahuta. This division explains why fish live in the sea and reptiles on land, and it underpins Tangaroa’s grudge against Tāne for harboring his runaway descendants.

In Kāi Tahu tradition, Takaroa is the son of Temoretu and husband of Papatūānuku. While Takaroa is away, Papatūānuku commits adultery with Rakinui (Ranginui). Upon his return, Takaroa discovers the betrayal and confronts Rakinui on the beach. In the ensuing battle, Takaroa’s spear pierces Rakinui through both thighs. After this conflict, Papatūānuku marries Rakinui. This story reconfigures the usual genealogy by making Takaroa Papatūānuku’s first husband and portrays him as a wronged spouse and warrior.

In one legend, Tangaroa marries Te Anu-matao, personification of chilling cold. Together they become parents of atua of the fish class, including Te Whata-uira-a-Tangawa, Te Whatukura, Poutini, and Te Pounamu. These offspring represent various aspects of the marine and mineral world, linking cold waters, fish, and stone—especially greenstone (pounamu)—to Tangaroa’s lineage.

In Manihiki, Tangaroa is portrayed as a fire deity. Māui, the trickster demigod, steals fire from Tangaroa, a narrative parallel to other Polynesian fire-theft stories. In Māori tradition, Māui obtains fire from the goddess Mahuika, but in Manihiki the role is assigned to Tangaroa, shifting his elemental association from sea to fire.

Scholarly Debates
Genealogical position and relationship to Ranginui and Papatūānuku
Tangaroa is one of the children of Ranginui (Sky Father) and Papatūānuku (Earth Mother), alongside other departmental atua such as Tāne, Rongo, Tū, Haumia, and Tāwhirimātea. ⇌ Takaroa (Tangaroa) is the son of Temoretu and husband of Papatūānuku; Papatūānuku commits adultery with Rakinui (Ranginui), whom Takaroa later wounds in battle.
Elemental domain: sea versus fire
Tangaroa is primarily atua of the sea, lakes, rivers, and aquatic life, with control over tides and ancestral authority over fish. ⇌ In Manihiki (Cook Islands), Tangaroa functions as a fire deity from whom Māui steals fire.
Status as supreme creator versus departmental god
In Māori tradition, Tangaroa is a departmental atua of the sea and fish, one among several major gods, not the sole creator. ⇌ In some Polynesian traditions (e.g., Sāmoan Tagaloa, Tahitian Ta‘aroa), the cognate deity is elevated to supreme creator of the world.
Extent of Tangaroa’s cult and sacrificial practices
Tangaroa is widely venerated as patron of seafaring and fishing, with offerings made before voyages and expeditions. ⇌ Some comparative accounts suggest that major Polynesian gods, including Tangaroa, may have received more intense forms of sacrifice, possibly including human sacrifice.
