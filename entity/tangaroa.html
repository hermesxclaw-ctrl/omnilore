Who It Is

Imagine standing on a black-sand beach in Aotearoa as the tide breathes in and out—each surge a reminder that, in Māori thought, the ocean is not just water but the living body of Tangaroa, ancestor of fish, rival of forests, and one of the oldest powers to shape the world of light.

Tangaroa’s personality is expressed through his actions in pūrākau rather than psychological description. He is a powerful, sometimes implacable atua whose domain can both sustain and destroy. As ancestor of fish and marine life, he is a life-giver and provider, yet the sea he embodies is dangerous, requiring respect and ritual observance. In the narrative of the separation of Ranginui and Papatūānuku, Tangaroa joins his brothers in forcing the parents apart, participating in a decisive, disruptive act that enables the world of light. Powers
Lordship over the sea and aquatic life

Tangaroa is attested as atua of the sea, lakes, rivers, and all creatures that live within them, especially fish. He is regarded as the origin and ancestor of fish, with his offspring—including Punga and Ikatere—representing lineages of marine creatures. This lordship encompasses both the nurturing provision of food and the potential for destructive storms and drowning.

Limit: His authority is primarily over aquatic domains; land, forests, and birds fall under Tāne Mahuta, and storms under Tāwhirimātea. Human access to his resources is mediated by ritual offerings and respect; failure to observe proper protocol can result in danger or poor catches.

Control of tides (Tangaroa-whakamau-tai)

Under the epithet Tangaroa-whakamau-tai, Tangaroa is recognized as controller of the tides. This power reflects the rhythmic rise and fall of the sea and is invoked by seafarers and fishers who depend on tidal cycles for safe travel and successful harvest.

Limit: Control of tides is a specific aspect of his broader oceanic authority and does not necessarily imply control over all weather phenomena, which are often attributed to Tāwhirimātea.

Ancestral power over fish and reptiles

Tangaroa’s son Punga has two children: Ikatere, ancestor of fish, and Tū-te-wehiwehi (or Tū-te-wanawana), ancestor of reptiles. When Tāwhirimātea attacks, fish seek shelter in the sea and reptiles in the forests, establishing enduring domains. Tangaroa’s ancestral power thus extends to these lineages, and his relationship with Tāne Mahuta is shaped by the latter’s sheltering of reptilian descendants.

Limit: The narrative emphasizes genealogical and domain allocation rather than explicit magical abilities; Tangaroa’s influence over reptiles is indirect through ancestry and contested by Tāne’s forest domain.

Patronage of seafaring and fishing

Tangaroa is frequently appealed to by seafarers and fishers. Offerings are made to him before setting out to sea for travel or fishing, acknowledging his control over the ocean and its creatures. Successful voyages and abundant catches are seen as signs of his favor.

Limit: Patronage is conditional on proper ritual observance and respect. Human skill, weather, and other atua also influence outcomes, so Tangaroa’s favor does not guarantee safety or success.

Elemental association with cold and stone (Te Anu-matao, Poutini, Te Pounamu)

In some legends, Tangaroa marries Te Anu-matao (chilling cold), and they are parents of atua of the fish class, including Te Whata-uira-a-Tangawa, Te Whatukura, Poutini, and Te Pounamu. These associations link Tangaroa to cold waters and to stone, particularly greenstone (pounamu), which is culturally significant in Aotearoa.

Limit: These powers are largely genealogical and symbolic; explicit control over temperature or stone is not elaborated in surviving narratives.

Fire deity role in Manihiki

In Manihiki (Cook Islands), Tangaroa is described as a fire deity from whom Māui steals fire. This role parallels, but does not duplicate, the Māori story in which Māui obtains fire from the goddess Mahuika.

Limit: This fire role is localized to Manihiki and does not appear in Māori cosmology, where Tangaroa remains primarily a sea god.

Domains — Attested: Tangaroa is atua of the sea, lakes, rivers, and all creatures that live within them, especially fish. In Māori cosmology, his domain is the oceanic realm opposed to the land domain of Tāne Mahuta. As Tangaroa-whakamau-tai, he controls tides, linking lunar and oceanic cycles. In some Cook Islands traditions, he also has roles in fertility, and in Manihiki he is associated with fire, indicating a broader elemental reach in those locales.

Accounts I
The separation of Ranginui and Papatūānuku and Tangaroa’s retreat to the seaMāori cosmological narratives as recorded by Whatahoro, Best, and later summaries (Wikipedia, EBSCO)

Pre-contact oral tradition, recorded in late 19th–early 20th century ethnographic texts

In Māori cosmology, the world begins with Ranginui (Sky Father) and Papatūānuku (Earth Mother) locked in a close embrace, their children confined in darkness between them. The departmental atua—Tāne Mahuta, Tangaroa, Rongo, Tū, Haumia, and others—debate how to gain space and light. Eventually, Tāne forces his parents apart, creating the world of light. Tangaroa joins his brothers in this forcible separation. After the separation, Tāwhirimātea, atua of storms, is enraged and attacks his brothers. Tangaroa, assaulted by storms, flees and hides in the sea, establishing his domain. His descendants, especially fish, remain with him in the ocean, while some—reptiles—seek refuge in the forests of Tāne. This narrative explains the opposition between sea and land and the enduring tension between Tangaroa and Tāne. The separation of sky and earth creates the world of light and establishes distinct domains for the atua. Punga, Ikatere, and Tū-te-wehiwehi: the division of fish and reptilesMāori genealogical narratives as summarized in Wikipedia and ethnographic literature

Pre-contact oral tradition, recorded in 19th–20th century

Tangaroa fathers Punga, who in turn has two children: Ikatere and Tū-te-wehiwehi (also known as Tū-te-wanawana). Ikatere becomes ancestor of fish, while Tū-te-wehiwehi becomes ancestor of reptiles. When Tāwhirimātea unleashes storms against the other atua, Ikatere and his fish descendants seek shelter in the sea, returning to Tangaroa’s domain. Tū-te-wehiwehi and the reptiles flee to the forests, finding refuge with Tāne Mahuta. This division explains why fish live in the sea and reptiles on land, and it underpins Tangaroa’s grudge against Tāne for harboring his runaway descendants. The narrative establishes the ecological and spiritual separation of fish and reptiles and reinforces the opposition between Tangaroa’s sea and Tāne’s forest. It also provides a mythic rationale for Tangaroa’s hostility toward Tāne, which in turn shapes human ritual obligations when moving between land and sea.

Accounts II
Takaroa, Papatūānuku, and Rakinui: the Kāi Tahu adultery and spear woundingKāi Tahu traditions as summarized in Wikipedia and regional ethnographic accounts

Pre-contact oral tradition, recorded in 19th–20th century

In Kāi Tahu tradition, Takaroa is the son of Temoretu and husband of Papatūānuku. While Takaroa is away, Papatūānuku commits adultery with Rakinui (Ranginui). Upon his return, Takaroa discovers the betrayal and confronts Rakinui on the beach. In the ensuing battle, Takaroa’s spear pierces Rakinui through both thighs. After this conflict, Papatūānuku marries Rakinui. This story reconfigures the usual genealogy by making Takaroa Papatūānuku’s first husband and portrays him as a wronged spouse and warrior. The narrative alters the relational dynamics among earth, sky, and sea, emphasizing themes of adultery, conflict, and wounded honor. It provides a Kāi Tahu-specific explanation for cosmic relationships and may inform local understandings of Tangaroa/Takaroa’s character as both husband and combatant.

Tangaroa and Te Anu-matao: parents of fish-class atuaMāori legends summarized in Wikipedia and ethnographic sources

Pre-contact oral tradition, recorded in 19th–20th century

In one legend, Tangaroa marries Te Anu-matao, personification of chilling cold. Together they become parents of atua of the fish class, including Te Whata-uira-a-Tangawa, Te Whatukura, Poutini, and Te Pounamu. These offspring represent various aspects of the marine and mineral world, linking cold waters, fish, and stone—especially greenstone (pounamu)—to Tangaroa’s lineage. The story extends Tangaroa’s genealogical network into domains of cold and stone, reinforcing his role as ancestor of diverse natural phenomena. It also provides mythic grounding for the cultural significance of pounamu and its association with waterways.

Tangaroa as fire deity and Māui’s theft of fire (Manihiki)Cook Islands (Manihiki) traditions summarized in Wikipedia and comparative Polynesian studies

Pre-contact oral tradition, recorded in 19th–20th century

In Manihiki, Tangaroa is portrayed as a fire deity. Māui, the trickster demigod, steals fire from Tangaroa, a narrative parallel to other Polynesian fire-theft stories. In Māori tradition, Māui obtains fire from the goddess Mahuika, but in Manihiki the role is assigned to Tangaroa, shifting his elemental association from sea to fire. The story explains the origin of human access to fire and highlights Māui’s cunning. It also demonstrates the flexibility of deity roles across Polynesian cultures, with Tangaroa’s domain expanding beyond the sea in this local context.
