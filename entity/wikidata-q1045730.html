Who It Is

He was the ugliest man at Troy, a bow-legged, pointy-headed soldier who dared to tell the kings what everyone else thought—and was beaten for it. Thersites, the voice of the common man or a foul-mouthed buffoon, has haunted Western literature for three thousand years, from Homer's Iliad to Shakespeare's stage, and his name still echoes in philosophy and medicine.

Thersites is portrayed as vulgar, obscene, and dull-witted, yet he is also bold and outspoken. He disrupts the Greek assembly by criticizing Agamemnon, calling him greedy and cowardly. He is not afraid to speak truth to power, but his manner is crude and his motives are questionable. Some later interpretations see him as a proto-social critic, while others view him as a buffoon.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Thersites (Greek: Θερσίτης) may derive from the Greek word 'therso' meaning 'boldness' or 'audacity', reflecting his bold speech. Alternatively, it could be related to 'therion' (wild beast), hinting at his brutish nature, but this is speculative.

Pronunciation — ther-SY-teez (English), ther-SEE-tes (classical Greek)

Aliases — Thersites of Aetolia, Thersites the Ugly

Powers
Rhetorical boldness

Thersites possesses the ability to speak boldly and critically in public assemblies, even against kings. He is not afraid to voice unpopular opinions, but his delivery is crude and offensive.

Limit: His speech is often counterproductive, as it alienates his audience and leads to his punishment. He lacks the eloquence and wisdom of a true orator.

Survival in the afterlife

In Plato's Gorgias, Thersites is mentioned as an example of a soul that can be cured in the afterlife because of his lack of might. In the Republic, he chooses to be reborn as an ape.

Limit: This is not a power but a philosophical point about the soul's potential for improvement.

Form & Signs

Appearance — According to Homer, Thersites was the ugliest man at Troy: bow-legged, lame, with shoulders that caved inward, a head covered in tufts of hair and coming to a point. This description is exaggerated and likely intended to reflect his moral deformity. Later sources, such as Shakespeare, describe him as 'deformed and scurrilous'.

Symbols — No specific symbols are associated with Thersites in ancient art. In modern times, he is often depicted as a hunchbacked or grotesque figure in illustrations of the Iliad. His name has become a byword for a carping critic.

Domains — He is a soldier in the Greek army at Troy. No specific domain or habitat is associated with him.

Accounts I
The Iliad: Thersites' OutburstHomer, Iliad, Book II

8th century BCE (traditional date)

During the Greek assembly, Thersites rises to criticize Agamemnon, accusing him of greed and cowardice, and suggesting that the Greeks should return home. Odysseus rebukes him sharply, threatens to strip him naked, and beats him with Agamemnon's sceptre. Thersites weeps and sits down, while the Greeks laugh at his humiliation. Thersites is silenced and humiliated, but his words remain in the record, and his criticism of the war resonates with some readers.

Death at Achilles' HandsAethiopis (lost epic) and Quintus Smyrnaeus, Posthomerica

7th century BCE (Aethiopis) and 4th century CE (Posthomerica)

After Achilles kills the Amazon Penthesilea, Thersites mocks Achilles for his grief and love for her, and possibly tears out her eyes. Achilles, enraged, punches him so hard that he dies. This causes a quarrel with Diomedes, Thersites' cousin, and Achilles is forced to go to Lesbos to be purified. Achilles is forced to undergo purification; the incident highlights the tension between Achilles and the other Greeks.

Accounts II
The Calydonian Boar HuntEuphorion (fragment)

3rd century BCE

Thersites was assigned by Oeneus to guard during the Calydonian Boar Hunt, but he abandoned his post for a safer, higher place. Meleager, annoyed, pursued him, and Thersites fell off a cliff, causing his disfigurement. This explains Thersites' deformity.

The Overthrow of OeneusApollodorus, Library (probably from lost sources)

1st-2nd century CE

Thersites and his brothers overthrew Oeneus from the throne of Calydon and gave the kingdom to their father Agrius. Later, Diomedes restored Oeneus and killed Thersites' brothers, but Thersites himself may have escaped. This establishes Thersites' family background and his enmity with Diomedes.
