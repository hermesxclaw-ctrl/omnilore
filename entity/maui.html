Who It Is

Maui is the great trickster-hero of Polynesian mythology — a demigod whose exploits are told in varying forms from Hawai'i to New Zealand, from Tahiti to Tonga, and across thousands of miles of the Pacific. He is simultaneously a culture hero who gave humanity essential gifts (fire, the secret of making fire, the islands themselves, the domesticated dog) and a trickster whose cleverness and mischief frequently backfire, resulting in his death. His stories constitute one of the most widespread and coherent myth cycles in Polynesian religion.

Physically, Maui is described in Māori tradition as a fully human-looking figure, often of smaller-than-average stature but supernaturally strong and quick. His full name — Maui-Tikitiki-a-Taranga ('Maui, the topknot of Taranga') — refers to the circumstances of his miraculous birth: his mother Taranga wrapped him in her topknot (tikitiki) and cast him into the sea, believing him to be stillborn. Ocean spirits and his ancestor Tama-nui-ki-te-Rangi rescued and raised him, giving him supernatural powers. Maui later returned to the human world and rejoined his family, revealing his divine nature through his extraordinary deeds.

Maui's exploits form the core of Polynesian mythic narrative. Among the most famous:

1. **Maui Fishes Up Islands**: Using his grandmother Murirangawhenua's jawbone as a fishhook, Maui went fishing with his brothers. He lowered his magical hook into the sea and, with supernatural strength, pulled up a great fish — the North Island of New Zealand (Te Ika-a-Maui, 'The Fish of Maui'). His canoe became the South Island (Te Waka-a-Maui), and the anchor stone became Stewart Island. In Hawaiian tradition, Maui similarly fished up the Hawaiian Islands.

2. **Maui Snares the Sun**: The sun (Tama-nui-te-Ra in Māori tradition) raced across the sky too quickly, giving humans insufficient daylight for work. Maui and his brothers wove ropes from flax and journeyed to the sun's rising place. Maui trapped the sun in a snare and, with his jawbone weapon, beat the sun until it agreed to travel more slowly across the sky — thus lengthening the day for humanity.

3. **Maui Brings Fire**: After extinguishing all fires in the world, Maui visited his fire-keeping ancestor Mahuika (in Māori tradition) and tricked her into giving him her fingernails of fire, one by one, until she realized his deception and attacked him. Maui fled, transforming into a hawk, but Mahuika's fire pursued him and hid in various trees. Maui showed humanity how to rub certain woods together to release the trapped fire.

4. **Maui's Death**: After all his victories over gods and elements, Maui attempted his greatest exploit: conquering death itself. He decided to enter the body of the death goddess Hine-nui-te-po through her vagina while she slept, and emerge through her mouth — thus reversing the birth process and eliminating death. But as he was entering, a fantail bird (piwakawaka) watching laughed at the ridiculous sight, waking the goddess. She crushed Maui between her thighs, killing him. Thus, death entered the human world definitively through Maui's failure.

Maui's legacy spans the Pacific and has been popularized globally through films like Disney's 'Moana' (which, despite Hollywood liberties, draws on authentic Maui traditions). In Polynesian cultures, Maui remains a beloved figure — clever, cocky, fallible, and profoundly human despite his demigod status.

Form

Māui is usually depicted as a young, strong demigod with a mischievous smile, often shown wielding his magical fishhook. In Māori visual art, his figure is stylized with traditional tattoo patterns (moko) and sometimes has a distinctive topknot hairstyle referencing 'tikitiki'. These depictions vary regionally and are largely modern artistic reconstructions rather than ancient iconography.

Powers

Fishing up islands Māui used his magical fishhook to pull islands from the ocean depths, creating land for humans to inhabit. Māori mythology Requires magical fishhook; feat symbolic rather than literal in some interpretations. Some Polynesian versions substitute different methods or attribute island creation to different deities.

Slowing the sun Māui lassoed the sun's rays to slow its journey across the sky, thereby lengthening the day to allow more time for human activities. Hawaiian and Māori traditions This act provoked divine wrath in some versions; the sun's speed was partially slowed, not stopped. Not all traditions include this episode; some focus more on other feats.

Stealing fire Māui stole fire from the fire goddess or a supernatural being to give to humans, enabling civilization and warmth. Māori and other Polynesian myths Fire is a sacred element; theft is risky and sometimes punished. Different myths name different owners of fire; in some versions, fire is acquired through negotiation or trickery.

Shapeshifting Māui could transform into various animals or forms to achieve his goals. Māori and wider Polynesian folklore Not all versions emphasize this power; transformational abilities sometimes limited to specific contexts. Extent and forms of shapeshifting vary.

Accounts

Māui uses his magical fishhook to pull the North Island of New Zealand from the sea floor. The island is shaped by the struggles of his brothers who try to pull the fishhook free, creating the island's mountains and valleys.

Māui, frustrated by the sun's rapid passage, catches it with a lasso made of flax and beats it to slow its progress, lengthening daylight hours for humans.

Māui steals fire from the fire guardian or goddess, often from the nails or fingernails of the fire deity Mahuika, to give to humanity.

Māui attempts to achieve immortality for mankind by entering the goddess Hine-nui-te-pō’s body, but is crushed and killed, thus death becomes permanent for humans.

Content Notes

Māui's attempt to conquer death by entering the goddess Hine-nui-te-pō leads to his death, establishing mortality as permanent for humans.

Interpreted as a myth explaining human mortality with tragic overtones.

Māui steals fire from the fire goddess and uses deception against other gods, reflecting morally ambiguous behavior.

Scholarly Debates
Nature of Māui's birth
Māui is the mortal son of Taranga, born and cast into the sea wrapped in her topknot. ⇌ Māui is a divine or semi-divine being born through supernatural means, sometimes from the god Tangaroa or other deities.
Evidence for A: Māori oral tradition recorded by ethnographers like White and Best.
Evidence for B: Alternative Polynesian traditions and interpretive mythology studies.
Role in island creation
Māui fishes up the North Island (New Zealand) with his magical hook. ⇌ In some traditions, island formation is credited to other deities or natural processes, and Māui's role is symbolic or absent.
Evidence for A: Māori myth collections.
Evidence for B: Other Polynesian myths and some Māori iwi versions.
Character alignment
Māui as a benevolent culture hero who improves human life. ⇌ Māui as a trickster with morally ambiguous, sometimes selfish or reckless behavior.
Evidence for A: Traditional narratives emphasizing his gifts to humanity.
Evidence for B: Stories of deceit, theft, and fatal hubris.
What We Don't Know

There is limited direct archaeological evidence specifically linked to Māui beyond iconographic interpretations. Many stories are preserved only in oral form with variations and no single authoritative text exists. Early ethnographic sources reflect colonial-era biases and may omit or alter indigenous perspectives. Some Polynesian language sources remain untranslated or inaccessible. Modern popular culture interpretations often blend multiple traditions, complicating scholarly clarity.

A way to hold it in your mind

Few figures in Polynesian mythology embody the paradox of divine mischief and heroic creation as vividly as Māui: a demigod who fished entire islands from the ocean depths, tamed the wild sun, and stole fire for humankind—all while challenging gods and fate itself. His stories ripple across the vast Pacific, from New Zealand’s rugged shores to Hawaii’s volcanic peaks, inviting us into a world where cunning and courage reshape the very fabric of nature.
