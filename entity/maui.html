Who It Is

Maui is the great trickster-hero of Polynesian mythology — a demigod whose exploits are told in varying forms from Hawai'i to New Zealand, from Tahiti to Tonga, and across thousands of miles of the Pacific. He is simultaneously a culture hero who gave humanity essential gifts (fire, the secret of making fire, the islands themselves, the domesticated dog) and a trickster whose cleverness and mischief frequently backfire, resulting in his death. His stories constitute one of the most widespread and coherent myth cycles in Polynesian religion.

Physically, Maui is described in Māori tradition as a fully human-looking figure, often of smaller-than-average stature but supernaturally strong and quick. His full name — Maui-Tikitiki-a-Taranga ('Maui, the topknot of Taranga') — refers to the circumstances of his miraculous birth: his mother Taranga wrapped him in her topknot (tikitiki) and cast him into the sea, believing him to be stillborn. Ocean spirits and his ancestor Tama-nui-ki-te-Rangi rescued and raised him, giving him supernatural powers. Maui later returned to the human world and rejoined his family, revealing his divine nature through his extraordinary deeds.

Maui's exploits form the core of Polynesian mythic narrative. Among the most famous:

1. **Maui Fishes Up Islands**: Using his grandmother Murirangawhenua's jawbone as a fishhook, Maui went fishing with his brothers. He lowered his magical hook into the sea and, with supernatural strength, pulled up a great fish — the North Island of New Zealand (Te Ika-a-Maui, 'The Fish of Maui'). His canoe became the South Island (Te Waka-a-Maui), and the anchor stone became Stewart Island. In Hawaiian tradition, Maui similarly fished up the Hawaiian Islands.

2. **Maui Snares the Sun**: The sun (Tama-nui-te-Ra in Māori tradition) raced across the sky too quickly, giving humans insufficient daylight for work. Maui and his brothers wove ropes from flax and journeyed to the sun's rising place. Maui trapped the sun in a snare and, with his jawbone weapon, beat the sun until it agreed to travel more slowly across the sky — thus lengthening the day for humanity.

3. **Maui Brings Fire**: After extinguishing all fires in the world, Maui visited his fire-keeping ancestor Mahuika (in Māori tradition) and tricked her into giving him her fingernails of fire, one by one, until she realized his deception and attacked him. Maui fled, transforming into a hawk, but Mahuika's fire pursued him and hid in various trees. Maui showed humanity how to rub certain woods together to release the trapped fire.

4. **Maui's Death**: After all his victories over gods and elements, Maui attempted his greatest exploit: conquering death itself. He decided to enter the body of the death goddess Hine-nui-te-po through her vagina while she slept, and emerge through her mouth — thus reversing the birth process and eliminating death. But as he was entering, a fantail bird (piwakawaka) watching laughed at the ridiculous sight, waking the goddess. She crushed Maui between her thighs, killing him. Thus, death entered the human world definitively through Maui's failure.

Maui's legacy spans the Pacific and has been popularized globally through films like Disney's 'Moana' (which, despite Hollywood liberties, draws on authentic Maui traditions). In Polynesian cultures, Maui remains a beloved figure — clever, cocky, fallible, and profoundly human despite his demigod status.
