Aker governs the akhet — the horizon, the liminal zone between the underworld (Duat) and the living world. He is also lord of the earth's depths, the caverns beneath the earth where the sun travels at night. His domain is the boundary between day and night, life and death, order (ma'at) and chaos (isfet).

Form

Aker is most commonly depicted as a double-headed lion — one head facing east (toward the rising sun) and one facing west (toward the setting sun). He is often shown with a sun disk between his two heads, or with the solar barque resting on his back. In some representations, he appears as a single lion with a human head (rare), or as a human figure with two lion heads. His body is typically shown as a long, stretched-out lion form, emphasizing the horizon line between the two worlds.

Powers

Earth-Opening Aker can open and close the earth, creating a passage for the sun god Ra's barque to travel from the western horizon (sunset) through the underworld to the eastern horizon (sunrise). This is his primary cosmic function. Attested in Pyramid Texts, Coffin Texts, Amduat He only opens the earth for the solar barque and the blessed dead; he does not open it for the unworthy or for the forces of chaos. None known; his role as earth-opener is consistent across all periods.

Guardianship of the Underworld Entrance Aker guards the entrance to the Duat (underworld). He is the 'gatekeeper of the horizon' who allows the deceased to pass into the afterlife. Attested in Pyramid Texts, Book of the Dead He only grants passage to those who have passed the judgment of the dead (the Weighing of the Heart). The unrighteous are devoured or turned away. Some texts (e.g., Book of the Dead Chapter 17) describe him as the one who 'opens the earth' for the deceased, while others (e.g., Amduat) describe him as the one who 'closes the cavern' against Apophis. These are complementary rather than contradictory roles.

Serpent-Subduing (Apophis Countermeasure) Aker physically restrains the chaos serpent Apophis, pinning him down or holding him in place so that the solar barque can pass safely. In some depictions, he is shown biting or gripping the serpent's body. Attested in Book of the Dead Chapter 39, Amduat, Book of Gates Aker does not destroy Apophis (who is immortal); he only restrains him temporarily. The battle against Apophis is a nightly, repeated event. In some texts, the god Atum is credited with defeating Apophis, while in others it is the goddess Isis or the god Set. Aker's role is specifically that of a physical restrainer, not the primary combatant.

Solar Transport In some representations, Aker carries the solar barque on his back as it travels through the underworld. His body forms the ground or the path that the sun travels. Attested in Amduat, Book of Gates He only serves as transport for Ra's barque, not for other deities or the deceased (who must walk or use their own barques). None known.

Protection of the Deceased Aker protects the souls of the blessed dead as they travel through the underworld. He is invoked in funerary spells to guard against hostile forces. Attested in Coffin Texts, Book of the Dead His protection is conditional on the deceased having lived a righteous life (i.e., having passed the judgment of the dead). None known.

Behavior & Habitat

Aker is a guardian and a threshold-keeper. He does not act aggressively toward the deceased or the gods; rather, he is a passive-but-vigilant sentinel. He opens and closes the earth, allowing the sun to pass through the underworld each night and emerge at dawn. He is also a protector against the chaos serpent Apophis, physically holding the serpent in place or pinning it down so it cannot disrupt the solar cycle.

Accounts

Every night, the sun god Ra descends into the underworld (Duat) to travel from the western horizon to the eastern horizon. At the entrance to the underworld, Aker — the double-lion — opens the earth, allowing Ra's barque to pass between his two heads. As the barque travels through the twelve hours of the night, Aker guards the path, restraining the chaos serpent Apophis who tries to swallow the sun. At dawn, Aker opens the earth again, and Ra emerges victorious into the sky.

The deceased, after being judged in the Hall of Two Truths, is granted passage through the underworld to the Field of Reeds (Aaru). To reach this paradise, the soul must pass through the earth, a journey guarded by Aker. The deceased recites spells invoking Aker to open the earth and allow safe passage. Aker examines the soul's worthiness — only those who have lived in accordance with ma'at are permitted through.

Apophis, the enormous chaos serpent, lies in wait in the underworld, attempting to swallow the solar barque and plunge the universe into darkness. Aker, in his double-lion form, physically restrains the serpent, pinning its body to the ground while other deities — such as Set, Serqet, or Isis — attack it with spears and magic. Aker's role is crucial: without his physical restraint, the serpent would be free to devour the sun.

In later periods, Aker's double-lion form becomes a symbol of the horizon itself — the two lions represent yesterday and today, the past and the present, the east and the west. The sun disk sits between them, symbolizing the eternal cycle of dawn and dusk. This motif is often used in temple decorations and funerary art as a symbol of rebirth and the eternal nature of time.

What We Don't Know

1. No dedicated scholarly monograph exists on Aker; he is always treated as part of broader studies of Egyptian religion. 2. The exact vocalization of his name in ancient Egyptian is unknown. 3. His cultic worship (if any) is poorly attested — he appears primarily in funerary and solar texts, not in temple cult records. 4. The relationship between Aker and the 'two lions' (Sefekh-Abwi) remains unclear and requires further study. 5. No images of Aker from the Old Kingdom survive — his earliest visual attestation is from the Middle Kingdom. 6. The Ptolemaic syncretism of Aker with Geb is understudied. 7. No modern popular media (film, TV, major video game) features Aker as a named character, limiting public awareness.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Aker as the cosmic equivalent of a drawbridge operator at a castle. He doesn't fight the invading army (that's other gods like Set or Isis); he just raises and lowers the bridge at exactly the right times, and when the enemy tries to cross, he holds the bridge closed. Without him, the king (Ra) can't get in or out, and the enemy (Apophis) gets in. He's not the hero who slays the dragon — he's the one who makes sure the dragon never gets past the gate in the first place. He's the unsung infrastructure of the universe: you don't notice him when he's working, but everything falls apart when he's not.
