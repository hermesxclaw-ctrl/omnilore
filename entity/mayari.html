Who It Is

Mayari is the moon goddess who rules the world at night, battles her brother the sun, and is sometimes described as having one eye. In the Philippine archipelago where 175-plus ethnolinguistic groups each maintained distinct deity traditions, Mayari stands out as the most widely documented moon goddess across Tagalog, Kapampangan, and Bicol sources — and yet almost nothing certain is known about her. No pre-colonial image, no primary narrative, no attested ritual formula survives in her name. What remains are fragments: a 384-character Wikipedia stub built on Spanish colonial records and early 20th-century folklore collections, a Wikidata entry in ten languages, a Marvel Comics cameo, and a 2025 NPR Tiny Desk Contest-winning song by Ruby Ibarra. She is simultaneously one of the most prominent Philippine deities in global popular culture and one of the most underdocumented in academic lit

Mayari governs the world during nighttime — a position of active sovereignty rather than passive personification. She is simultaneously goddess of beauty and war, of revolution and the hunt, suggesting a deity whose character is neither gentle nor exclusively martial but encompasses creative and destructive force in the same frame. Powers
Lunar Dominion

Governance over the moon and night sky. Mayari's primary domain is the moon itself — she is not merely a personification of lunar light but an active ruler whose sovereignty extends over the entire nighttime world. In Kapampangan tradition this is explicitly stated: she 'rules the world during nighttime.'

Limit: Her dominion is temporally bounded — she rules only at night, ceding the daytime to the sun god Apolaki/Arao. The Bakunawa's ability to attack and potentially devour the moon represents a vulnerability in her domain during eclipse events.

War and Revolution

Mayari is explicitly identified as a goddess of war and revolution — attributes shared with very few other Philippine deities and unusual for a moon goddess cross-culturally. The war attribute likely connects to the sun-moon war myth in which she battles her brother Apolaki, and to the broader Austronesian pattern of moon deities associated with conflict and cyclical overthrow. The revolution attribute, noted in Bicol religious narratives, may encode pre-colonial resistance ideology or reflect a genuine ancient association of lunar deities with upheaval and renewal.

Limit: No surviving narrative details a specific war or revolution Mayari led. The attribute is attested but its original context is unknown.

Beauty

Goddess of beauty — in the Philippine context this likely encompasses both physical attractiveness and the aesthetic ordering of the cosmos (the moon as beautiful celestial object). This attribute may connect to the Tala myth variant where Buan's/Mayari's star-children are 'beautiful' and their destruction motivates the celestial conflict.

Limit: No surviving narratives describe beauty contests or aesthetic judgments involving Mayari. The attribute is stated but not dramatized in surviving sources.

Strength, Weaponry, and the Hunt

Goddess of strength, weaponry, and the hunt — these three attributes form a consistent martial cluster. In the pre-colonial Philippines, hunting was a prestigious activity tied to warrior status, and weaponry was consecrated through ritual. Mayari's association with these domains likely reflects the role of lunar deities in sanctioning hunting cycles and martial valor.

Limit: No specific weapons are attributed to Mayari in surviving sources. The Bagobo and other groups consecrated sword hilts with Bakunawa imagery; whether Mayari had analogous weapon cults is unknown.

One-Eye Vision (Possible Attribute)

The Wikipedia List of lunar deities notes that Mayari is 'sometimes identified as having one eye.' If authentic, this would parallel the Austronesian and wider Indo-Pacific motif of one-eyed deities possessing exceptional perception — Odin sacrificed an eye for wisdom; the Hawaiian Kane-Milo is one-eyed. In the Philippine context, the one-eye could relate to the moon's phases (half-moon as half-blind), to the wound received in battle with Apolaki, or to shamanic vision practices. However, no primary source explicitly documents this attribute, and it may derive from modern comparative mythology rather than traditional belief.

Limit: No primary source confirms the one-eye attribute. It may be a comparative-mythology inference rather than a traditional attribution.

Accounts I
The Three Daughters of BathalaTala (goddess) Wikipedia article; Philippine mythology oral tradition

Pre-colonial (oral tradition documented 16th-20th centuries)

Mayari is one of three daughters born to the supreme creator god Bathala and a mortal woman. Her sisters are Tala, goddess of the morning and evening star (Venus), and Hanan, goddess of morning. This tripartite sisterhood — moon, evening star, morning — encodes a precise astronomical schema: Mayari rules the night, Tala marks the transitions between night and day as Venus, and Hanan governs the dawn. The mortal mother of the three is unnamed in surviving sources, emphasizing the pattern of divine-mortal interbreeding common in Philippine mythology (paralleled by Malakas and Maganda, the first humans, born from bamboo). Establishes Mayari's position within the highest tier of the Tagalog pantheon as a direct offspring of the creator. The sisterhood also provides the genealogical anchor for the sun-moon war myth (below), since Tala's role as dawn-star positions her as the mediator between her warring siblings.

The Sun-Moon War: Arao Pursues Buan/MayariTala (goddess) article; Philippine mythology; Bakunawa article

Pre-colonial (oral tradition)

In this cosmogonic narrative, the sun god Arao (probably Apolaki) and the moon goddess Buan (probably Mayari) each had large families of stars. Buan believed her stars could not survive the intense heat of the sun, so she proposed that both destroy their stellar offspring. Arao agreed and devoured all his stars, but Buan hid hers in the clouds, where they would occasionally emerge. Upon seeing this betrayal, Arao was filled with rage and began an eternal pursuit of Buan, trying to destroy her and her remaining stars. Eclipses occur when Arao gets close enough to bite the moon. At dawn, Buan hides the stars and brings them forth only when her eldest daughter Tala (the evening and morning star) judges that the sun is too far away to pursue them. This myth simultaneously explains the daily cycle, the origin of eclipses, the visibility of stars at night, and the positional astronomy of Venus as the harbinger of safe starlight. Accounts II
The Seven Moons and the BakunawaBuyser, Mga Sugilanong Karaan (1913); Buyser, Mga Sugilanong Pilipinhon (1926); Eugenio, Philippine Folk Literature: The Myths

Pre-colonial; documented 1913-1926

In this Visayan narrative, the supreme deity Bathala (or Kaptan in Visayan tradition) created seven moons to illuminate the night sky, each intended for one night of the week. The Bakunawa, a dragon-like serpent with a looped tail and a single horn on its nose, was amazed by their beauty and rose from the ocean to swallow them whole. Enraged, Bathala made the Bakunawa his eternal enemy. To prevent the complete devouring of the final moon, early Filipinos rushed outdoors clanging pots and pans to startle the Bakunawa into disgorging it. In one variant, the Bakunawa had a sister in the form of a sea turtle; when locals killed the turtle, the Bakunawa consumed the moon in vengeance. In another variant, the Bakunawa fell in love with a human woman; when tribal leaders destroyed their dwelling by fire, the Bakunawa attempted to devour all seven moons as vengeance, and was ultimately banished from the sea by the supreme deity. Amihan Saves the First HumansAmihan (mythology) Wikipedia article

Pre-colonial (oral tradition)

Amihan, a primordial deity depicted as a bird, is the first creature to inhabit the universe alongside Bathala and Aman Sinaya. In the creation narrative, Amihan saves the first humans — Malakas (strong) and Maganda (beautiful) — who emerge fully grown from a split bamboo plant. Amihan's intervention positions her as a protective force within the cosmic order that Mayari later governs at night. While Mayari is not a direct participant in this specific narrative, it establishes the mythological context: the universe was ordered by Bathala and inhabited by competing and cooperating deities, of which Mayari is a significant member. Establishes the cosmic setting in which Mayari operates. Demonstrates that the Tagalog creation myth involves multiple cooperating deities rather than a single creator working alone.

The Bakunawa as Cultural AdversaryBakunawa Wikipedia article; Buyser collections; Fr. Tomas Ortiz, Practica del Ministerio (1731)

Pre-colonial through colonial

The Bakunawa is not merely a cosmological threat but a figure embedded in ritual practice, material culture, and community identity. Fr. Tomas Ortiz (1731) documented that during eclipses, 'the Indians of various districts generally go out into the street or into the open fields, with bells, panastanes, etc. They strike them with great force and violence in order that they might thereby protect the moon which they say is being eaten or swallowed by the dragon.' The Bakunawa's image decorated the hilts of ancient Panay swords (hangaway or mandirigma), transferring its fearsome presence to the warrior class. A children's game called Bulan Bulan / Buwan Buwan / Bakunawa enacts the chase between moon and dragon. The creature's syncretization with the Hindu Nāga, Rahu, and Ketu reflects centuries of trade contact with South and Southeast Asia. Demonstrates that Mayari's lunar domain is not a passive celestial realm but a defended territory requiring communal ritual intervention.
