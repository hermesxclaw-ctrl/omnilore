Who It Is

In the white-sand country of Inaba, eighty gods marched as suitors to win one woman — and the only god who stopped to help a flayed hare won her instead. Her name was Yagami-hime, the Princess of Yakami, and her choice set off murder, resurrection, jealousy, and the founding of a shrine that still stands in Tottori.

Attested behavior is minimal but decisive. In Kojiki, when the eighty gods each instruct Ōnamuchi to carry their baggage to court her, she herself speaks only once, delivering a clear choice: 'I will not marry the eighty gods; I will marry Ōnamuchi no Kami.' This is reported via the hare's prophecy and then fulfilled when she affirms him. She shows agency in mate selection against collective pressure. Powers
Legitimating Choice / Divine Election

Her declaration 'I will marry Ōnamuchi' functions as a mythic legitimation. The hare prophesies that because Ōnamuchi showed compassion, Yagami-hime will choose him despite his low status. Her choice transfers prestige and territorial claim in Inaba to the future Great Land Master.

Limit: Not a supernatural power per se; a social-ritual authority. She cannot prevent the eighty gods' subsequent murder attempts on Ōnamuchi.

Fertility / Childbearing (Mythic Mother)

Bears a child to Ōkuninushi: Ko-no-mata no Kami / Kinomata no Kami (Tree-Fork Deity), named for being placed in a tree fork.

Limit: Child does not survive as heir; lineage is not continued through Yagami-hime in later genealogy, which shifts to Suseri-hime's children. In some Nihon Shoki variants the pregnancy ends without viable offspring.

Tutelar Protection (Shrine Tradition)

As saijin of Yakami Jinja, venerated as protector of Yakami district, grantor of en-musubi (marriage ties), safe delivery, and local prosperity. This is a cultic power attributed by worshippers, not a narrative feat in myth.

Limit: No mythic feat demonstrates this power; it is post-mythic shrine theology. Efficacy is faith-based, not textually attested as miracle.

Beauty as Narrative Power

Her beauty is so renowned that eighty gods simultaneously seek her, driving the plot. This is an aesthetic-social power, not magic.

Limit: No active use; she is object of desire rather than agent of enchantment. No text says she uses beauty magically.

Accounts I
The Eighty Gods Court Yagami-himeKojiki, Book 1, Chapters 20-23

712 CE compilation; narrative set in Age of the Gods (Kamiyo)

The eighty gods (Yasogami), all sons of Kami-musubi or brothers of Ōnamuchi depending on genealogy, hear of the beautiful Yagami-hime in Yakami, Inaba, and decide to court her. They compel their youngest brother, the kind but lowly Ōnamuchi (later Ōkuninushi), to carry their baggage as servant. On the road to Inaba they encounter the flayed hare. This opening establishes Yagami-hime as the prize that motivates the fraternal conflict and Ōnamuchi's elevation. Sets up Ōnamuchi's humiliation and subsequent vindication; establishes Yagami-hime's agency in choosing a husband against the majority of suitors; triggers the brothers' jealousy and murder attempts.

The Hare of Inaba Prophesies Yagami-hime's ChoiceKojiki, Book 1, Chapter 23-24; Nihon Shoki does not include this episode in main text

712 CE (Kojiki)

At Cape Keta (Keta no Saki) the eighty gods tell the skinned hare to bathe in seawater and lie in wind, worsening its pain. Ōnamuchi, arriving last, pities the hare, instructs it to wash in fresh river water and roll in cattail (gama) pollen, healing it. In gratitude the hare, revealed as a kami, prophesies: 'The eighty gods shall not get Yagami-hime; though you carry the baggage, you shall obtain her.' Ōnamuchi proceeds to Yakami and, as foretold, Yagami-hime declares she will marry him, rejecting the eighty gods. Divine reward for compassion; Yagami-hime's choice fulfills prophecy and legitimates Ōnamuchi as worthy despite low status; becomes the most retold scene in Japanese folklore, art, and shrine ritual at Hakuto Jinja.

The Murder Attempts on Ōnamuchi by the Jealous BrothersKojiki, Book 1, Chapters 25-26; Nihon Shoki Jindai variant

712/720 CE

Enraged that Yagami-hime chose Ōnamuchi, the eighty gods plot to kill him. First they crush him with a heated boulder disguised as a wild boar on Mt. Tema; his mother Sashikuni Wakahime (Kushimi no Kami's daughter) revives him with clam juice and mother’s milk via the kami Kisagai-hime and Umugai-hime. They then trap him by splitting a tree and wedging him inside. Again his mother rescues him and sends him to seek refuge with Susanoo in Ne no Kuni. Yagami-hime remains in Inaba during this violence, already considered Ōnamuchi's betrothed. Forces Ōnamuchi's exile to Susanoo, leading to his trials and marriage to Suseri-hime; explains why Yagami-hime's marriage is not immediately consummated in Inaba.

Accounts II
Yagami-hime's Marriage, Pregnancy, and Birth of Kinomata no KamiKojiki, Book 1, Chapter 26; Nihon Shoki Jindai issyo 7

712/720 CE

Before fleeing, Ōnamuchi had already been accepted by Yagami-hime and she became pregnant. Kojiki states: 'Yagami-hime had already been betrothed to Ōnamuchi; though the eighty gods harassed him, she bore him a child, Ko-no-mata no Kami (also read Kinomata no Kami, Tree-Fork Deity).' The name reflects the circumstances of the birth. This is the only child of their union listed in the genealogy. Provides a genealogical link between Inaba (Yagami-hime) and Izumo (Ōkuninushi) lineages, but the line is dead-ended and does not continue Ōkuninushi's main succession, which passes through Suseri-hime's children.

The Abandonment / Return to Inaba: Fear of Suseri-himeKojiki, Book 1, Chapter 36-37

712 CE

After surviving Susanoo's trials (sleeping in snake and centipede rooms, retrieving an arrow from a burning field, retrieving Susanoo's hair ornaments from a room of centipedes), Ōnamuchi elopes with Susanoo's daughter Suseri-hime, stealing Susanoo's sword, bow, and koto, and returns to Izumo as Ōkuninushi. Kojiki then notes Yagami-hime's fate: 'When Suseri-hime came, Yagami-hime, fearing the jealousy of the principal wife, stuck the child in the crotch/fork of a tree and went back to Inaba.' Some translations render as she left the child in a tree fork and departed, abandoning the marriage. Thus the first wife yields to the second. Explains why Yagami-hime disappears from subsequent Izumo myths and why Ōkuninushi's primary consort becomes Suseri-hime; etiology for tree-fork deity name; establishes polygynous hierarchy and jealousy motif common in Kojiki marriages.

Enshrinement at Yakami and Hakuto: The Living CultEngishiki Jinmyōchō 927; Yakami Jinja engi manuscripts (Muromachi-Edo period); Hakuto Jinja engi; Tottori Domain records

Heian period to present (continuous cult)

Yagami-hime is enshrined as principal deity at Yakami Jinja (Yakagami Jinja) in Yakami, Tottori City, listed as an official shrine in Engishiki. Local engi identifies the shrine as the site of her residence or palace. She is also secondarily venerated at nearby Hakuto Jinja (White Rabbit Shrine) alongside the Hare deity, where the myth is reenacted in festivals. Annual rites pray for en-musubi and safe childbirth. The shrine claims continuity from the mythic Yakami palace. Transforms mythic princess into living tutelary goddess; anchors myth to landscape; drives modern tourism (Hakuto Kaigan, 'White Rabbit Coast') and municipal identity.

Yagami-hime is the Japanese goddess of love or equivalent to Venus/Aphrodite. — No primary source assigns her a domain of 'love.' She is a local princess/tutelary deity whose myth involves a marriage choice. Her modern association with en-musubi (matchmaking) is a medieval-to-modern shrine development, amplified by tourism, not an ancient cosmic portfolio.

Yagami-hime and Kushinada-hime are the same person or interchangeable wives of Susanoo/Ōkuninushi. — Distinct figures: Kushinada-hime is daughter of Ashinazuchi/Tenazuchi, rescued by Susanoo from Yamata no Orochi and becomes Susanoo's wife and mother of Yashimajinumi; Yagami-hime is of Inaba, wife of Ōkuninushi (Susanoo's son-in-law). Different generations and regions.

The White Hare of Inaba is Yagami-hime's pet or messenger. — In Kojiki the hare is an independent kami/trickster who is helped by Ōnamuchi and then prophesies his success with Yagami-hime. It is not described as belonging to or serving Yagami-hime; their connection is indirect via prophecy. Later shrine art pairs them, but text does not make hare her attribute.

Yagami-hime remained Ōkuninushi's chief wife and mother of his heirs. — Kojiki explicitly says she left him after Suseri-hime arrived and her child did not continue the lineage. Ōkuninushi's main heirs (e.g., Ajisukitakahikone, Kotoshironushi) are children of other wives, especially Suseri-hime and Nunakawa-hime. Yagami-hime's line is dead-ended.

Yagami-hime appears in the Nihon Shoki as a fully fleshed character with dialogues like in Kojiki. — Nihon Shoki's treatment is fragmentary, preserved only in brief mentions and issyo variants, without the hare prophecy or her famous declaration verbatim. The vivid dialogue is Kojiki-specific.
