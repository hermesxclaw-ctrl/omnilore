Who It Is

Pah is the Pawnee moon god, one of the principal celestial deities in the sophisticated Pawnee astronomical system. He governs the night, the lunar cycles, and the passage of time by which the Pawnee organized their agricultural calendar, their ceremonies, and their hunting seasons. Pah was believed to be the husband of the sun goddess Shakuru, and together they produced the first human beings. The phases of the moon — waxing, full, waning, dark — represented the life cycle of Pah himself: birth, maturity, aging, death, and rebirth each month. This lunar cycle was intimately connected to women's reproductive cycles in Pawnee thought, and Pah was invoked by women for fertility and safe childbirth. Unlike his wife Shakuru, who ruled the hot, active daytime, Pah's night was a time of rest, dreaming, and the cooling of the earth. He was also associated with the counting of months and the determination of ceremonial timing. In Pawnee cosmology, the Moon was one of the four great star gods who served Tirawa as intermediaries, and Pah was specifically responsible for the west direction — symbolically the direction of rest, conclusion, and the afterlife. His regular, predictable cycle made him a model of reliability and order in the cosmos. When the U.S. government forcibly relocated the Pawnee to Oklahoma in the 1870s and suppressed their ceremonial system, much of the detailed star lore associated with Pah was lost, though fragments survive in the ethnographic records of Alice Fletcher and James Murie.
