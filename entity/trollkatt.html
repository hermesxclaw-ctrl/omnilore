Who It Is

The Trollkatt (literally 'troll-cat' or 'magic-cat,' also called Bjära in Swedish, Tilberi in Icelandic) is a supernatural creature created by a witch to steal milk and other resources from neighbouring farms. In Norwegian tradition, the witch creates the Trollkatt by shaping it from various materials (old rags, human hair, wood shavings, stolen communion bread) and breathing life into it with a drop of her own blood (often from her left ring finger). The Trollkatt then rolls across the countryside as a ball of yarn or a cat-like creature, entering barns to suck milk from cows and goats, returning to the witch to vomit the stolen milk into her churn. The milk is tainted and yields only thin, bluish butter — a sign of witchcraft known as 'trollsmør' (troll butter). In some traditions, the Trollkatt is a living creature that must be fed constantly; it grows insatiable, eventually turning on and devouring its creator. In Icelandic folklore, the Tilberi is created from a human rib stolen from a graveyard and a stolen communion wafer, wrapped in wool and kept between a woman's breasts — it lives by sucking her blood from the inside of her thigh and stealing sheep's milk. The Trollkatt is documented extensively in Scandinavian witch-trial records (16th-17th centuries), where accused witches confessed to creating such creatures. It represents the deep anxiety of pre-industrial Scandinavian rural life: milk was wealth, and a neighbour's inexplicable dairy prosperity implied supernatural theft. The Trollkatt is the economic terror of the smallholder — the supernatural explanation for the milk that was there yesterday and gone today.

Form

Usually an ordinary-looking cat, but with telltale signs: it may be larger than normal, unusually fat (from drinking all the stolen milk), have glowing eyes in the dark, or leave no footprints in snow. Some accounts describe it as having a singed or singed-looking patch of fur, or being oddly silent. In some tales it is coal-black, but not always. It may appear at night, often near the barn or the neighbor's cowshed.

Powers

Milk theft The trollkatt can sneak into a neighbor's cowshed and suck milk directly from the cow's udder, or drink from milk bowls, causing the cow to dry up or the milk to spoil. It is a form of magical theft that transfers the cow's yield to the witch's own household. Swedish and Norwegian folk tradition, 18th-19th century records The trollkatt must be sent by the witch; it does not act independently. It is vulnerable to certain counter-magic (see below). Some accounts say the witch herself becomes the cat; others say the cat is a separate spirit or a transformed object (like a knitting needle or a stick).

Shapeshifting (witch's form) The witch can transform herself into a cat, or send her spirit out in cat form while her body lies at home in a trance. In this form she can travel great distances, spy, and cause harm. Pan-Scandinavian shapeshifting tradition, with parallels in saga literature (e.g., the 'hamrammr' tradition) The witch is vulnerable while her body is in trance; if the cat form is injured, the witch's body may show the same wound. Not all trollkatter are witches; some are independent spirits or demons in cat form.

Cursing and hexing The trollkatt can cause livestock to sicken, milk to curdle, butter to fail to churn, and children to fall ill. It may also bring nightmares or a sense of dread to a household. Folk belief, 19th century The cat's power is limited by the witch's intent and by the household's protective measures. Some accounts attribute these powers to the witch herself, with the cat merely as her messenger.

Elusiveness The trollkatt is nearly impossible to catch, can fit through tiny openings, and may vanish into thin air when cornered. Folk tales and personal experience narratives It can be caught or injured if one uses the right counter-magic, such as a silver bullet or a Bible verse. None significant.

Sending (as a witch's tool) The witch can 'send' the trollkatt to do her bidding, like a magical projectile. The cat is not a companion but a weapon or a tool. Swedish and Norwegian 'sending' tradition (related to the 'bjära' and 'vättar') The cat must be sent with intent and ritual; it is not an autonomous spirit. This overlaps heavily with the bjära tradition, and some scholars treat them as the same concept.

Behavior & Habitat

The trollkatt is a creature of errands: it is sent by a witch to steal milk from neighbors' cows, to sour cream, to hex livestock, or to spy on households. It may also simply be a witch in cat form, going about her business. It is nocturnal, secretive, and loyal to its witch master. When not on a mission, it may sit by the hearth or on the witch's roof, watching. It is said to be able to slip through the smallest crack, and to be nearly impossible to catch. If cornered, it may hiss, scratch, or vanish. It has a particular appetite for cream and fresh milk, and may be found lapping at a cow's udder or at a milk bowl left out.

Accounts

A farmer notices his best cow is giving less and less milk. He watches the barn at night and sees a large, strange cat lapping at the cow's udder. He tries to catch it, but it slips away. He consults a wise woman (or a pastor) who tells him it is a trollkatt sent by a neighbor witch. The farmer is advised to heat a poker in the fire and brand the cat the next time it comes. He does so, and the next day the neighbor woman is found with a burn mark on her arm or face. The milk theft stops.

A traveler stays at a farm and is warned not to harm the large cat that sits by the fire. That night, he sees the cat leave the house and return with a neighbor's cow's milk in its mouth. He scratches the cat's ear, and the next day the farmer's wife (or a neighbor woman) has a scratch on her ear. The traveler realizes the cat is the witch in disguise.

A witch transforms a common object—a knitting needle, a stick, or a ball of yarn—into a cat and sends it to steal milk. The cat is not a real animal but a temporary construct. If the object is broken, the cat disappears. This is a variant that emphasizes the witch's skill and the cat's artificiality.

A hunter or farmer decides to shoot the trollkatt that has been plaguing his barn. He uses a silver button or a silver bullet, which is said to be effective against magical creatures. He wounds the cat, and the next day a neighbor woman is found with a wound in the same place. The cat is never seen again.

Field Notes

۩ The word 'troll' in 'trollkatt' is related to the Old Norse 'troll', which could mean any supernatural being, not just a giant.

۩ In some accounts, the trollkatt was said to be able to squeeze through a keyhole, making it a perfect spy.

۩ A common counter-measure was to place a Bible under a bowl of milk; the cat would be unable to drink and would be trapped.

۩ The trollkatt is closely related to the 'bjära', which could take the form of a cat, a hare, or even a ball of yarn.

۩ Some scholars see the trollkatt as a demonized echo of Freyja's sacred cats from Norse mythology.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of the trollkatt as the medieval Scandinavian version of a drone: a small, hard-to-catch, remotely controlled device used for espionage and sabotage. It's not a loyal companion; it's a piece of magical hardware deployed by a witch to steal the enemy's most valuable resource—milk, the liquid gold of the farm. The cat is the technology, the witch is the operator, and the cow is the target. The fear it inspired was not just about cats; it was about the invisible malice of your neighbors, the fragility of your livelihood, and the terrifying possibility that the creature purring by your fire was not a cat at all.
