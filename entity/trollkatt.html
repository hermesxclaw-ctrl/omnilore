Who It Is

Imagine a cat that is not a cat. It slips through the barn door at midnight, not to hunt mice, but to drink the milk straight from the cow's udder. It leaves no footprints in the snow, and when you try to catch it, it vanishes like smoke. The next morning, your neighbor—the one who always seemed a little too interested in your prize cow—has a scratch on her arm, and you didn't see her anywhere near the barn. In rural Scandinavia, that cat had a name: trollkatt. And it wasn't a pet. It was a weapon.

The trollkatt is a creature of errands: it is sent by a witch to steal milk from neighbors' cows, to sour cream, to hex livestock, or to spy on households. It may also simply be a witch in cat form, going about her business. It is nocturnal, secretive, and loyal to its witch master. When not on a mission, it may sit by the hearth or on the witch's roof, watching. It is said to be able to slip through the smallest crack, and to be nearly impossible to catch. Name & Tongue

Etymology — Compound of Swedish 'troll' (magic, sorcery, or a supernatural being) + 'katt' (cat). Literally 'troll-cat' or 'magic cat'. The word 'troll' in older Scandinavian usage could mean any sort of malevolent or ambiguous supernatural entity, not just the modern giant.

Powers
Milk theft

The trollkatt can sneak into a neighbor's cowshed and suck milk directly from the cow's udder, or drink from milk bowls, causing the cow to dry up or the milk to spoil. It is a form of magical theft that transfers the cow's yield to the witch's own household.

Limit: The trollkatt must be sent by the witch; it does not act independently. It is vulnerable to certain counter-magic (see below).

Shapeshifting (witch's form)

The witch can transform herself into a cat, or send her spirit out in cat form while her body lies at home in a trance. In this form she can travel great distances, spy, and cause harm.

Limit: The witch is vulnerable while her body is in trance; if the cat form is injured, the witch's body may show the same wound.

Cursing and hexing

The trollkatt can cause livestock to sicken, milk to curdle, butter to fail to churn, and children to fall ill. It may also bring nightmares or a sense of dread to a household.

Limit: The cat's power is limited by the witch's intent and by the household's protective measures.

Elusiveness

The trollkatt is nearly impossible to catch, can fit through tiny openings, and may vanish into thin air when cornered.

Limit: It can be caught or injured if one uses the right counter-magic, such as a silver bullet or a Bible verse.

Sending (as a witch's tool)

The witch can 'send' the trollkatt to do her bidding, like a magical projectile. The cat is not a companion but a weapon or a tool.

Limit: The cat must be sent with intent and ritual; it is not an autonomous spirit.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Usually an ordinary-looking cat, but with telltale signs: it may be larger than normal, unusually fat (from drinking all the stolen milk), have glowing eyes in the dark, or leave no footprints in snow. Some accounts describe it as having a singed or singed-looking patch of fur, or being oddly silent. In some tales it is coal-black, but not always. It may appear at night, often near the barn or the neighbor's cowshed.

Symbols — The cat itself, especially a black cat, is a symbol of witchcraft and bad luck in Scandinavian folk belief. The trollkatt symbolizes theft, hidden malice, the fear of the neighbor's envy, and the magical economy of the farm (where milk is wealth). It also represents the uncanny aspect of the domestic cat: a familiar animal that is also a predator, a companion that is also a stranger.

Domains — Farms, barns, cowsheds, dairies, the witch's household, the boundary between the village and the wild. It is a liminal creature, moving between the human world and the witch's magical world, and between the farm and the forest.

Accounts I
The Cat That Sucked the Cow DrySwedish folklore archives (e.g., Nordiska Museet, ULMA records)

19th century

A farmer notices his best cow is giving less and less milk. He watches the barn at night and sees a large, strange cat lapping at the cow's udder. He tries to catch it, but it slips away. He consults a wise woman (or a pastor) who tells him it is a trollkatt sent by a neighbor witch. The farmer is advised to heat a poker in the fire and brand the cat the next time it comes. He does so, and the next day the neighbor woman is found with a burn mark on her arm or face. The milk theft stops. The witch is exposed (or at least suspected), the cow recovers, and the community learns to be wary of the neighbor.

The Witch in Cat FormEva Wigström, 'Folkdiktning' (1880), and similar collections

19th century

A traveler stays at a farm and is warned not to harm the large cat that sits by the fire. That night, he sees the cat leave the house and return with a neighbor's cow's milk in its mouth. He scratches the cat's ear, and the next day the farmer's wife (or a neighbor woman) has a scratch on her ear. The traveler realizes the cat is the witch in disguise. The witch is exposed, and the traveler leaves with a warning about the dangers of interfering with trollkatter.

Accounts II
The Knitting Needle CatSwedish and Norwegian folk tales

19th century

A witch transforms a common object—a knitting needle, a stick, or a ball of yarn—into a cat and sends it to steal milk. The cat is not a real animal but a temporary construct. If the object is broken, the cat disappears. This is a variant that emphasizes the witch's skill and the cat's artificiality. The theft is discovered when the object is broken, and the witch is shamed.

The Cat and the Silver BulletSwedish folk tradition

19th century

A hunter or farmer decides to shoot the trollkatt that has been plaguing his barn. He uses a silver button or a silver bullet, which is said to be effective against magical creatures. He wounds the cat, and the next day a neighbor woman is found with a wound in the same place. The cat is never seen again. The witch is injured or dies, and the community is freed from her malice.

The trollkatt is the same as the modern 'witch's familiar' in popular culture (e.g., a black cat that helps a witch with spells). — In Scandinavian folk belief, the trollkatt is not a companion or helper; it is a tool or a form of the witch herself, used for theft and harm. It is closer to a curse or a weapon than a pet.

The trollkatt is a giant troll that looks like a cat. — The 'troll' in 'trollkatt' refers to magic/sorcery, not the giant trolls of later folklore. It is a cat, not a troll.

All black cats in Scandinavia were considered trollkatter. — While black cats were often viewed with suspicion, not all were considered trollkatter. The label was applied to cats that behaved strangely or were associated with a suspected witch.

The trollkatt belief is ancient, dating back to the Viking Age. — While cats had magical associations in the Viking Age (e.g., Freyja's cats), the specific 'trollkatt' as a witch's milk-stealing familiar is primarily documented in the 17th-19th centuries, during and after the witch trials.
