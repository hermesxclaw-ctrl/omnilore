Who It Is

She is the phantom queen who washes the armour of the doomed, who shape-shifts into crow, eel, and wolf, and who makes love to a god on the eve of cosmic battle. The Morrígan is not a single, fixed goddess but a presence that haunts the edges of Irish myth—sometimes one, sometimes three, always a herald of fate and war.

Ambiguous, capricious, powerful; incites warriors, foretells death, can aid or hinder heroes. Often associated with sovereignty and guardianship of the land.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name is debated. 'Mor' may derive from Proto-Indo-European root meaning 'terror' or 'monstrous', cognate with Old English 'maere' (nightmare) and Scandinavian 'mara', giving 'Phantom Queen'. Alternatively, Middle Irish spelling 'Mórrígan' suggests 'Great Queen' from Old Irish 'mór' (great), but

Pronunciation — Approximately 'MOR-ree-gan' (with variations: 'MOH-ree-gan' for Mórrígan).

Aliases — Mórrígan, Morrígu, Mór-ríoghan, Anand, Phantom Queen, Great Queen

Ownership — Irish mythology, specifically the Ulster Cycle and Mythological Cycle of early Irish literature. Part of the Tuatha Dé Danann.

Powers
Shape-shifting

Can transform into various animals: crow, eel, wolf, heifer, and possibly other forms.

Limit: Wounds sustained in animal form appear on her human form.

Prophecy and foretelling

Foretells doom, death, and victory in battle; appears as a washer at the ford to predict death.

Limit: Not always clear; often cryptic.

Battlefield magic

Can incite warriors, strike fear, and influence battle outcomes. Chants poems that affect the battle.

Limit: Not omnipotent; can be wounded.

Cursing and blessing

Can bring ill luck or heal; in Táin Bó Cúailnge, she curses Cú Chulainn, but he heals her wounds with blessings.

Limit: Healing requires blessing; curses may be avoided.

Transformation of others

Turns Odras into a pool of water.

Limit: Requires the victim to be asleep.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Shape-shifter; often appears as a crow or raven (badb). In human form, she appears as a young woman (to Cú Chulainn) or an old hag (washing armour). Also takes forms of eel, wolf, and white red-eared heifer.

Symbols — Crows/ravens, washing at fords, sovereignty, war, fate. Sometimes depicted with weapons or as a trio.

Domains — Battlefields, fords, rivers, caves (e.g., cave of Cruachan), the Otherworld. Associated with Samhain.

Accounts I
The Cattle Raid of RegamainTáin Bó Regamna

Early medieval (8th-9th century)

Cú Chulainn encounters the Morrígan driving a heifer; he insults her, she warns him of his death, and they part in enmity. Sets the stage for their later conflict in Táin Bó Cúailnge.

The Cattle Raid of CooleyTáin Bó Cúailnge

8th-12th century

Morrígan offers love to Cú Chulainn, he rejects her; she interferes in his combats as eel, wolf, and heifer; he wounds her; later she appears as an old woman and he heals her unknowingly. Cú Chulainn defeats his opponents despite her interference; their relationship remains hostile.

Accounts II
The Death of Cú ChulainnVarious versions (e.g., Book of Leinster)

12th century

Morrígan appears as a hag washing Cú Chulainn's bloody armour, foretelling his death. After his death, a crow lands on his shoulder. Confirms his death to his enemies.

The Battle of Mag TuiredCath Maige Tuired

9th century

On Samhain, Morrígan meets the Dagda at a river and they have sex; she promises to help the Tuatha Dé by summoning magicians and destroying Indech. She chants poems that influence the battle. Tuatha Dé victory; Morrígan brings two handfuls of Indech's blood to the river.

The Story of OdrasGlosses (e.g., in H.3.18)

Medieval

Morrígan lures away Odras's bull; Odras follows her to the Otherworld via the cave of Cruachan; Morrígan turns her into a pool of water feeding the Shannon. Odras becomes a river; Morrígan is called the Dagda's envious queen.

The Morrígan is a single goddess of war. — She is often a triple goddess, and her role includes sovereignty, fate, and fertility.

The Morrígan is the Celtic version of the banshee. — She may be associated with the banshee, but they are distinct figures.

The Morrígan is the same as Morgan le Fay. — No linguistic or cultural connection; different origins.

The Morrígan is evil. — She is ambiguous, aiding heroes sometimes and hindering them other times.
