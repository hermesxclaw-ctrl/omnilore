Who It Is

The Rainbow Serpent is the most important and widely recognized figure in Aboriginal Australian mythology. Known by different names across hundreds of language groups (Ngalyod among the Kunwinjku, Julunggul in Arnhem Land, Yingarna as the mother serpent, Mamaragan as the thunder-being, and many others), the Rainbow Serpent is the great Ancestral Being who created the landscape during the Dreaming (Tjukurrpa). As the great serpent traveled across the barren earth, her body carved the rivers, valleys, and gorges. Where she rested, her body formed waterholes. Her rainbow-colored scales became the colors of the landscape. The Rainbow Serpent is the guardian of water — the most precious resource in the Australian environment — and she dwells in the deepest waterholes. She is associated with the monsoon, the wet season, and the fertility of the land. However, she is also dangerous: she punishes those who pollute water sources, break taboos, or speak disrespectfully of the Dreaming. The Rainbow Serpent can swallow humans, cause floods, and bring cyclones. The figure has been part of Aboriginal culture for tens of thousands of years — rock paintings of the Rainbow Serpent at sites like Ubirr (Kakadu) are among the oldest continuous religious artworks in the world, dating back at least 6,000 years. The Rainbow Serpent was adopted as a pan-Aboriginal symbol during the Indigenous cultural revival of the 1970s and appears on Aboriginal flags, art, and literature. She represents the creative and destructive power of nature, the sacredness of water, and the unity of all Aboriginal peoples under one Dreaming.

Form

The Rainbow Serpent is typically described as a colossal serpent, often with the scales of a python or a kangaroo, and sometimes with the head of a crocodile or other animal. It is frequently associated with the colors of the rainbow, either as a multi-hued body or as a rainbow that emanates from it. In some traditions, it is a female being, in others male or androgynous. It is a being of immense size and power, capable of shaping the landscape.

Powers

Creation of Landscape The Rainbow Serpent is a primary creator being. It moved across the land during the Dreamtime, its body carving out rivers, gorges, and waterholes. It is responsible for the physical form of the Australian landscape. Pan-Aboriginal, with specific local variations. Its creative power is tied to the Dreamtime, though its presence and power remain in the land and water. None.

Control over Water and Rain As a being of water, the Rainbow Serpent controls the rain, the filling of waterholes, and the health of aquatic life. It can bring life-giving rain or destructive floods. Pan-Aboriginal, particularly in arid regions where water is scarce. Its control is tied to the seasons and the maintenance of the natural order. None.

Guardianship and Punishment The Rainbow Serpent is a guardian of sacred sites and the laws of the Dreamtime. It punishes those who break taboos, disrespect the land, or harm the community. This can take the form of floods, illness, or other misfortunes. Pan-Aboriginal, with specific local laws and taboos. Its power is a moral force, maintaining social and cosmic order. None.

Transformation and Regeneration The Rainbow Serpent is associated with initiation and transformation. It can shed its skin, symbolizing rebirth and renewal. It is also connected to the wet season and the regeneration of life. Particularly strong in Arnhem Land traditions (Ngalyod, Julunggul). This power is often linked to initiation ceremonies and esoteric knowledge. None.

Accounts

In the Dreamtime, the Rainbow Serpent emerged from the earth and traveled across the land. Its immense body carved out the rivers, creeks, and waterholes. Where it rested, it created billabongs and swamps. The mountains and gorges were formed by its movements. This story explains the physical geography of Australia as the work of a powerful, creative being.

The Wagyl, a great serpent, is said to have created the Swan River and the surrounding landscape. As it moved, it carved out the river's path. Its movements created the bends and curves of the river, and it is the guardian of the water and the land.

Julunggul is a powerful female Rainbow Serpent who is central to the initiation ceremonies of the Yolngu people. She is associated with fertility and the wet season. Her power is invoked during initiation rituals, where young men are symbolically swallowed and reborn, undergoing a transformation into adulthood.

Content Notes

The Rainbow Serpent is a punisher of those who break the law. This can involve sending floods, causing illness, or even killing the offender. The punishment is not arbitrary but is a consequence of violating sacred laws and taboos.

This aspect of the Rainbow Serpent highlights its role as a moral and cosmic enforcer, maintaining balance and order.

Various oral traditions

Many stories and ceremonies associated with the Rainbow Serpent are secret-sacred, meaning they are only known to initiated members of the community. This restriction is a form of cultural protection.

The secrecy is an essential part of the tradition and must be respected.

Scholarly Debates
Gender
The Rainbow Serpent is a male being. ⇌ The Rainbow Serpent is a female being.
Evidence for A: In some traditions, the Serpent is associated with male initiation and is referred to as 'he'.
Evidence for B: In other traditions, particularly in Arnhem Land, the Serpent (e.g., Julunggul) is a powerful female figure.
Benevolent vs. Malevolent
The Rainbow Serpent is a benevolent creator and protector. ⇌ The Rainbow Serpent is a dangerous and destructive force.
Evidence for A: It brings life-giving water and creates the landscape.
Evidence for B: It punishes lawbreakers with floods, illness, and death.
Unity vs. Diversity
There is a single, pan-Australian 'Rainbow Serpent' figure. ⇌ There are many distinct Rainbow Serpent beings, each with its own specific story and name.
Evidence for A: The term 'Rainbow Serpent' is widely used in English to describe a common figure.
Evidence for B: Aboriginal cultures have many different names and stories for their serpent beings (e.g., Ngalyod, Julunggul, Wagyl).
What We Don't Know

The full extent of the Rainbow Serpent's mythology is impossible to document in a single entry, as it is a living tradition with thousands of distinct stories and practices. Much of the secret-sacred knowledge is restricted and not publicly available. Further research would require consultation with specific Aboriginal communities and a deeper understanding of their languages and cultural protocols.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of the Rainbow Serpent not as a single god, but as a vast, interconnected network of related forces, like the concept of 'water' itself. There is not one 'water', but many forms — rain, rivers, oceans, ice — each with its own properties and importance. Similarly, the Rainbow Serpent is a family of powerful ancestral beings, each one a unique expression of a fundamental creative and destructive force, intimately tied to the land and water of a specific place.
