The Approach

In the shadow of the Alborz mountains, where the wind carries the scent of sulfur and the cries of the damned, the divs hold court. These are not the petty demons of later folklore, but the ancient giants of Persian myth—creatures of immense strength, cunning, and malice, who once waged war against the heroes of the Shahnameh. To understand the div is to peer into the heart of Zoroastrian darkness, where the lie (druj) takes form and the world's greatest champions find their match.

Content advisory. Contains depictions of violence, demonic entities, and mythological warfare. Some traditions describe divs as cannibalistic or engaging in grotesque acts. Reader discretion advised.
Identity

Div

Etymology

The term 'div' derives from Avestan 'daēva', which originally meant 'celestial' or 'divine' in Indo-Iranian, but in Zoroastrianism was demonized to denote a class of evil beings. The shift reflects the theological revolution attributed to Zoroaster, who reclassified the old gods as demons.

Cultural Ownership

Persian (Iranian) culture, with roots in Zoroastrianism and pre-Zoroastrian Indo-Iranian religion. The concept evolved through the Middle Persian period and into Islamic-era Persian literature, notably the Shahnameh.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars identify divs with the Hindu Asuras (e.g., in Vedic religion, Asura originally meant 'lord' but later became demonic), but this is contested. The exact relationship between Avestan daēvas and Vedic devas (gods) is a matter of ongoing scholarly debate. Additionally, the divs of the Shahnameh are often conflated with the 'jinn' of Islamic tradition, though they are distinct beings.

Description

Appearance

Divs are typically described as giant, monstrous beings, often with animal features such as horns, tusks, claws, and wild hair. In the Shahnameh, the White Div is specifically described as a huge, white-fleshed demon with a face like a leopard, and eyes that glow. They may have multiple heads (as in some depictions) or be shape-shifters. In Zoroastrian texts, they are formless or have shadowy, terrifying forms, often associated with darkness and filth.

Symbols & Iconography

Divs are associated with darkness, night, desert, mountains, and waste places. They are often depicted with weapons like clubs or rocks. In art, they are shown as hairy, muscular giants, sometimes with animal heads. The White Div is often depicted as a massive, pale-skinned monster.

Behaviour & Personality

Divs are malevolent, destructive, and deceitful. They embody the druj (lie) and work to corrupt creation. They are often portrayed as aggressive, warlike, and cannibalistic, but also as cunning and capable of trickery. In the Shahnameh, they are shown as arrogant and cruel, taking pleasure in harming humans. Some divs are also depicted as guardians of treasures or captives.

Domains & Habitats

Divs inhabit dark, desolate places: mountains, caves, deserts, and the underworld. In the Shahnameh, the White Div resides in the dark cavern of Mazandaran, a land of sorcerers and demons. In Zoroastrian cosmology, the daēvas dwell in the northern region of darkness, the realm of Angra Mainyu.

Version Differences

In the Avesta, daēvas are primarily demonic entities opposed to the Amesha Spentas (Holy Immortals). In Middle Persian texts (Bundahishn, Denkard), they are organized into a hierarchy under Angra Mainyu, with specific demons for specific sins (e.g., Aēshma for wrath, Akoman for evil mind). In the Shahnameh, divs are more like monstrous, physical enemies, often with individual names (e.g., Div-e Sepid, Div-e Akvan) and are less theological. In later Persian folklore, divs become more like ogres or trolls, sometimes foolish and easily tricked.

Powers & Abilities

Superhuman Strength

Source Version Shahnameh

Description

Divs possess immense physical strength, capable of crushing rocks, uprooting trees, and overwhelming even the mightiest heroes.

Limitations

They can be overcome by clever heroes who exploit their arrogance or weaknesses.

Contradictions

Some sources portray them as invincible except by specific means, while others show them being defeated in direct combat.

Shape-shifting

Source Version Zoroastrian texts, Shahnameh (e.g., Div-e Akvan)

Description

Some divs can change their form, appearing as animals, humans, or other beings to deceive their victims.

Limitations

They may be unable to change form in certain circumstances or may be recognized by their eyes or odor.

Contradictions

Not all divs have this ability; it is often attributed to specific demons like Akvan.

Illusion and Deception

Source Version Avesta, Zoroastrian theology

Description

Divs can create illusions, deceive the senses, and spread lies, corrupting the minds of mortals.

Limitations

The power of truth (asha) and the recitation of holy verses can dispel their illusions.

Contradictions

None noted.

Magic and Sorcery

Source Version Shahnameh

Description

In the Shahnameh, divs are associated with sorcery and dark magic, able to cast spells, curse, and control supernatural forces.

Limitations

Their magic can be countered by the power of God (Yazdan) and by heroes with divine support.

Contradictions

In Zoroastrian texts, magic is often attributed to the Yatus (sorcerers) who are allies of the daēvas, not necessarily the daēvas themselves.

Immortality or Longevity

Source Version Zoroastrian cosmology, Shahnameh

Description

Divs are often considered immortal or extremely long-lived, surviving for millennia.

Limitations

They can be killed in combat, as demonstrated by Rostam's victory over the White Div.

Contradictions

Some texts imply they will be destroyed at the end of time, suggesting they are not truly immortal.

Stories & Myths

The Daēvas in the Avesta

Source Avesta (Yasna, Vendidad)Culture ZoroastrianPeriod c. 1000-600 BCE

Summary

The Avesta repeatedly condemns the daēvas as false gods who lead humanity astray. Zoroaster's revelation establishes Ahura Mazda as the supreme god, and the daēvas are cast as demons, servants of Angra Mainyu. The Vendidad provides rituals to ward off their influence, and the Yashts describe how heroes and gods battle them.

Participants

Zoroaster

Ahura Mazda

Angra Mainyu

Daēvas

Consequences

The daēvas become the archetypal demons of Zoroastrianism, shaping the religion's dualistic worldview.

Variants

The Avesta does not provide detailed narratives of individual daēvas, but later texts expand on them.

The Creation of the Daēvas

Source BundahishnCulture Zoroastrian (Middle Persian)Period 9th-12th century CE

Summary

According to the Bundahishn, Angra Mainyu created the daēvas to oppose the good creation of Ahura Mazda. Each daēva is assigned to counter a specific Amesha Spenta or to promote a particular sin. For example, Akoman (Evil Mind) opposes Vohu Manah (Good Mind), and Aēshma (Wrath) opposes Sraosha (Obedience).

Participants

Angra Mainyu

Ahura Mazda

Amesha Spentas

Daēvas

Consequences

This establishes a cosmic order where every good force has an evil counterpart, and the world is a battleground between them.

Variants

Different Pahlavi texts list different numbers and names of daēvas, indicating a fluid tradition.

Rostam and the White Div

Source ShahnamehCulture Persian (Islamic-era)Period c. 1010 CE

Summary

In the Shahnameh, the king Kay Kavus is lured to Mazandaran by a div in disguise, and he and his army are captured by the White Div. Rostam, the great hero, undertakes a perilous quest to rescue them. After a seven-stage journey, he defeats the White Div in a fierce battle, cutting off its head and using its blood to cure the blinded king and his warriors.

Participants

Rostam

White Div (Div-e Sepid)

Kay Kavus

Divs of Mazandaran

Consequences

Rostam's victory cements his reputation as the greatest hero of Iran and frees the king. The White Div's death is a blow to the demonic forces.

Variants

The story is also known from earlier oral tradition and is depicted in Persian miniature paintings. Some versions have Rostam aided by the Simurgh.

Div-e Akvan (Akvan the Div)

Source ShahnamehCulture Persian (Islamic-era)Period c. 1010 CE

Summary

In this episode, the div Akvan kidnaps a horse and taunts Rostam. Rostam eventually captures him, but Akvan uses his shape-shifting power to escape. Later, Akvan appears while Rostam is sleeping and throws him into the sea, but Rostam survives and eventually kills the div.

Participants

Rostam

Div-e Akvan

Consequences

The story highlights the cunning and malice of divs, as well as Rostam's resilience and cleverness.

Variants

The tale is often cited as an example of Rostam's wit overcoming supernatural adversaries.

Violence and Mutilation

Source Shahnameh

Description

Divs are often depicted as committing horrific violence, including tearing apart victims, eating humans, and blinding captives. In the Shahnameh, the White Div blinds the captured Iranian warriors.

Scholarly Context

Such depictions serve to emphasize the evil nature of divs and the heroism of those who oppose them.

Cannibalism

Source Persian folklore, later texts

Description

Some folklore traditions describe divs as cannibals, consuming human flesh.

Scholarly Context

This is a common trope for ogres and demons in world mythology, symbolizing their inhumanity.

Religious Demonization

Source Avesta, scholarly analysis

Description

The term 'daeva' was used to demonize the gods of other cultures, particularly in the context of Zoroastrianism's rivalry with other Iranian religions and later with Islam. This has been a point of scholarly discussion regarding religious intolerance.

Scholarly Context

The demonization of old gods is a common phenomenon in religious reform, but it has historical consequences for how 'other' religions are perceived.

Contradictions & Disputes

Origin of the term 'daeva'

Position A

The term 'daeva' originally meant 'god' in Indo-Iranian, and was demonized by Zoroaster.

Position A — evidence

Linguistic evidence from Vedic Sanskrit, where 'deva' means god, and the Avestan usage as demon.

Position B

The term may have already had negative connotations in some contexts before Zoroaster.

Position B — evidence

Some scholars argue that the demonization was a gradual process, not a single event.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars accept that the Avestan daēvas were originally gods, but the exact timeline and process of demonization is debated.

Physical form of divs

Position A

Divs are physical, monstrous beings with bodies.

Position A — evidence

The Shahnameh describes them in physical terms, engaging in combat.

Position B

Divs are spiritual or formless entities.

Position B — evidence

Zoroastrian theological texts often treat them as spiritual beings, without physical form.

Scholarly Consensus

It is likely that the concept evolved: in earlier texts they were more spiritual, while later, in folklore, they became more physical.

Relationship to jinn

Position A

Divs are the same as jinn in Islamic tradition.

Position A — evidence

Some later Persian texts use 'div' and 'jinn' interchangeably.

Position B

Divs and jinn are distinct beings.

Position B — evidence

Jinn are created from smokeless fire in Islam, while divs have a different origin in Zoroastrianism.

Scholarly Consensus

They are distinct, but there may have been cultural exchange and conflation over time.

Primary Evidence

Avesta (Yasna, Vendidad, Yashts)

Type textDate c. 1000-600 BCE (oral tradition earlier)Locator Yasna 30.6; Vendidad 10.9; Yasht 19 (Zamyad Yasht)Language Avestan

Translation

The Avesta refers to daēvas as objects of abhorrence, to be rejected by followers of Ahura Mazda. They are associated with the druj (lie) and are led by Angra Mainyu (Ahriman).

Bundahishn (Greater/Bundahishn)

Type textDate c. 9th-12th century CE (based on earlier Zand)Locator Chapter 3, 28; Chapter 27Language Middle Persian (Pahlavi)

Translation

The Bundahishn describes the creation of the daēvas by Angra Mainyu, listing specific demons and their roles (e.g., Akoman, Indar, Saurva).

Denkard (Book 3, 5, 7)

Type textDate c. 9th-10th century CELocator Denkard 3.115; 5.24; 7.3Language Middle Persian (Pahlavi)

Translation

The Denkard provides theological commentary on the nature of demons, their creation, and their opposition to the good creation.

Shahnameh (Book of Kings) by Ferdowsi

Type textDate c. 1010 CELocator Sections on Sam, Zal, and Rostam; particularly the story of Rostam and the White Div (Div-e Sepid)Language New Persian

Translation

The Shahnameh portrays divs as monstrous, powerful enemies of Iranian heroes, with the White Div as a prominent antagonist.

Sasanian rock reliefs and seals
