The Approach

In the humid heart of the Maya world, when the sky darkens and the first fat drops of rain begin to fall, it is said that Chaac—the ancient god of rain, thunder, and lightning—has swung his mighty axe against the clouds. His image, with a long, curling nose and reptilian scales, adorns ancient temples and modern altars alike, a testament to a deity whose power has endured for millennia.

Content advisory. Contains references to ritual animal sacrifice and mythological violence.
Identity

Chaac

Pronunciation Chahk (roughly 'chahk' with a glottal stop at the end)

Known As

Etymology

The name 'Chaac' (also spelled 'Chac' or 'Chaahk') likely derives from a root meaning 'rain' or 'storm' in Mayan languages. The Classic Mayan form 'Chaahk' is reconstructed as [t͡ʃaːhk].

Cultural Ownership

Maya civilization (Mesoamerica), specifically the Yucatec, Chorti, Tzotzil, and Q'eqchi' Maya groups.

Disputed Identifications

Chaac is often equated with the Aztec god Tlaloc and the Zapotec god Cocijo as rain deities, but these are distinct deities from different cultures. Some scholars debate the relationship between Chaac and the Classic Maya god K'awiil, who is sometimes personified as Chaac's lightning axe. There is also debate about the identity of the 'rain deity' in certain Classic period scenes.

Description

Appearance

Chaac is typically depicted with a human body covered in reptilian or amphibian scales. His head is non-human, with fangs and a long, pendulous nose that curls upward. In Classic period art, he often wears a shell as an ear ornament. He carries a shield and a lightning axe, which is personified by the deity K'awiil (also known as Bolon Dzacab in Yucatec).

Symbols & Iconography

Chaac's primary symbols are the lightning axe, the serpent (especially as a rain-carrying snake), and the turtle (in the context of the maize god's resurrection). He is also associated with caves and cenotes, which were believed to be sources of water and clouds. In the Dresden Codex, he is shown in various activities, including pouring water from a jar.

Behaviour & Personality

Chaac is a complex deity: he is a benevolent provider of rain essential for agriculture, but also a fierce warrior whose wrath is manifested in thunderstorms. He is a patron of agriculture and a protector of maize. In myths, he can be a trickster, as in the Q'eqchi' myth where he commits adultery and is punished, his tears becoming rain.

Domains & Habitats

Chaac resides in the sky, but also in caves and cenotes, which are seen as entrances to the underworld and sources of water. He is associated with the four cardinal directions and their colors: East (red), North (white), West (black), South (yellow), and Center (green).

Version Differences

In the Classic period, Chaac is often depicted as a single deity, but by the Postclassic period, he is clearly divided into four directional Chaacs. Different Maya groups have distinct names and attributes for rain deities: the Chorti have complex hierarchies of rain deities, the Tzotzil associate the rain deity with maize maidens, and the Q'eqchi' have the deity Choc (or Chocl) 'Cloud' as a brother of Sun.

Powers & Abilities

Rain-making

Source Version General Maya; specifically Yucatec and Chorti

Description

Chaac produces rain by striking clouds with his lightning axe, causing thunder and rain.

Limitations

Requires the cooperation of other deities or spirits, such as rain-carrying snakes.

Contradictions

None noted.

Lightning and thunder

Source Version Classic and Postclassic Maya

Description

Chaac wields a lightning axe that he uses to strike clouds and enemies. His wrath is expressed through violent storms.

Limitations

In some myths, his lightning is used as a weapon in battle, but it can also be a source of destruction.

Contradictions

The identity of the axe as K'awiil is sometimes debated.

Opening the mountain of maize

Source Version Classic period sources; also in Tzotzil and other Maya mythologies

Description

Chaac is credited with opening the mountain where maize was hidden, using his lightning axe, allowing maize to be accessed by humans.

Limitations

This act is a one-time mythological event.

Contradictions

Some versions attribute this act to other deities, such as the hero twins.

Shape-shifting

Source Version Chorti folklore

Description

In some myths, Chaac can transform into a serpent or other forms.

Limitations

Not widely attested.

Contradictions

None noted.

Stories & Myths

The Opening of the Maize Mountain

Source Classic Maya hieroglyphic texts; Tzotzil mythologyCulture MayaPeriod Classic to modern

Summary

In this myth, maize is hidden inside a mountain. Chaac, using his lightning axe, strikes the mountain to open it, releasing maize to the people. This act establishes him as a patron of agriculture.

Participants

Chaac

Maize

Mountain

Consequences

Humans gain access to maize, ensuring their survival.

Variants

In some versions, other deities or heroes perform this act.

Chaac and the Sun's Wife

Source Q'eqchi' mythology (recorded in the Popol Vuh and other sources)Culture Q'eqchi' MayaPeriod Postclassic to modern

Summary

In a Q'eqchi' myth, the rain deity Choc (or Chocl) 'Cloud' is the brother of Sun. They defeat their aged adoptive mother and her lover. Later, Choc commits adultery with Sun's wife. As punishment, he is castrated or killed, and his tears of agony become rain.

Participants

Choc/Chocl

Sun

Sun's wife

Adoptive mother

Consequences

The origin of rain is explained. The myth also explains the enmity between rain and sun.

Variants

In some versions, Chaac is shown in a war-like fury, pursuing the fleeing Sun and Moon and attacking them with lightning.

Chaac and the Resurrection of the Maize God

Source Classic Maya vase paintings and inscriptionsCulture Classic MayaPeriod Classic period

Summary

Chaac is present at the resurrection of the Maya maize god from the carapace of a turtle, which may represent the earth. This event is central to Maya mythology and kingship.

Participants

Chaac

Maize god

Consequences

The maize god's rebirth ensures the continuation of life and agriculture.

Variants

Relationships

K'awiil (Bolon Dzacab)

Type aspect/companionSource Classic Maya inscriptions and iconography

Description

K'awiil is a deity of lightning and royal lineage, often depicted as the axe of Chaac. They are closely related, with K'awiil sometimes considered an aspect of Chaac.

Version Note

In the Classic period, K'awiil is a separate deity, but in Yucatec, he is called Bolon Dzacab and is the personification of Chaac's axe.

Conflicting Accounts

Some scholars see them as distinct deities, while others see K'awiil as a manifestation of Chaac.

Bacabs

Type related deitySource Diego de Landa, Relación de las cosas de Yucatán

Description

The Bacabs are aged gods who support the sky and govern the subterranean water. In Landa's account, the four Chaacs and four Bacabs are combined into one concept.

Version Note

In the Dresden Codex, the Chaacs and Bacabs appear together in rituals.

Conflicting Accounts

The relationship is complex; they may be separate but closely associated deities.

Ixchel

Type associated deitySource Dresden Codex

Description

Ixchel is an aged goddess associated with the moon, fertility, and medicine. In the Dresden Codex, she is related to the Chaacs in rain rituals.

Version Note

The Chaacs, Bacabs, and Ixchel are involved in rituals for rain and agriculture.

Conflicting Accounts

None noted.

Maya kings

Type patron/impersonatorSource Classic and Postclassic inscriptions

Description

Maya kings often impersonated Chaac in rituals, especially in warfare and rain-making ceremonies. The title 'Chac Xib Chaac' was used by rulers of Chichen Itza.

Version Note

In the Classic period, kings are shown carrying war implements and taking prisoners while impersonating the rain deity.

Conflicting Accounts

None noted.

Ritual sacrifice of ocellated turkey

Source Ethnographic accounts of the Ch'a Cháak ceremony

Description

In Yucatec rain ceremonies, an ocellated turkey is hunted and sacrificed, and its blood is offered to the fields to ensure a good harvest.

Scholarly Context

Animal sacrifice was a common practice in Mesoamerican rituals, intended to nourish the gods and maintain cosmic order.

Mythological violence and adultery

Source Q'eqchi' mythology

Description

In the Q'eqchi' myth, Chaac commits adultery with his brother's wife and is punished, leading to the origin of rain. In some versions, he is killed or castrated.

Scholarly Context

Such myths often encode social norms and explain natural phenomena through the actions of deities.

Modern vandalism of Poseidon statue

Source News reports

Description

In 2024, a statue of Poseidon in Progreso, Yucatán, was targeted by locals who believed it angered Chaac. Some attempted to vandalize it, and activist lawyers sought its removal.

Scholarly Context

This reflects contemporary Maya religious sentiment and cultural tensions.

Contradictions & Disputes

Chaac as one or many

Position A

Chaac is a single deity.

Position A — evidence

Classic period iconography often depicts a single rain deity.

Position B

There are four Chaacs, one for each cardinal direction.

Position B — evidence

Postclassic and colonial sources, such as Landa, describe four directional Chaacs.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars agree that the concept of four Chaacs developed over time, with the single deity being earlier.

Relationship with K'awiil

Position A

K'awiil is a separate deity who serves as Chaac's axe.

Position A — evidence

Classic texts distinguish between Chaac and K'awiil.

Position B

K'awiil is an aspect or manifestation of Chaac.

Position B — evidence

In Yucatec, Bolon Dzacab is equated with Chaac's axe.

Scholarly Consensus

The relationship is complex; they are distinct but closely associated.

Role in the resurrection of the maize god

Position A

Chaac is a passive observer.

Position A — evidence

Vase scenes show Chaac present but not acting.

Position B

Chaac plays an active role in the resurrection.

Position B — evidence

Some inscriptions suggest he opens the mountain of maize, a similar act.

Scholarly Consensus

The exact role is uncertain; more research is needed.
