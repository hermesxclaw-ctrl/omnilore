Who It Is

In the ninth year of the Trojan War, when the fighting had ground to a bloody stalemate, it was not a hero's spear but an archer's bow that nearly changed the course of history. Pandarus, a Lycian captain renowned for his marksmanship, loosed an arrow that broke the truce between Greeks and Trojans—and in doing so, sealed his own doom and set the stage for the epic duels of the Iliad. Yet his story does not end on the plains of Ilium; centuries later, his name would be reborn in the courts of medieval romance, where he became a go-between in a love affair as famous as any war.

In Homer, Pandarus is impulsive and easily swayed by Athena's persuasion (Iliad 4.86-104), breaking the truce by shooting Menelaus. He is boastful and overconfident in his archery skills, as seen in his exchange with Aeneas (Iliad 5.171-216). In Chaucer, Pandarus is witty, manipulative, and loyal to his friend Troilus, acting as a go-between in the affair with Criseyde. He is pragmatic and somewhat cynical about love, but also protective of Criseyde's honor. In Shakespeare, Pandarus is a bawdy, cynical figure who revels in the salacious aspects of the affair.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Pandarus (Greek: Πάνδαρος) is likely derived from the Greek roots 'pan-' (all) and 'daros' (possibly 'burning' or 'cunning'), though the exact meaning is uncertain. In some traditions, it is associated with the verb 'dara' (to flay or tear), but no definitive etymology exists.

Pronunciation — PAN-də-rəs (English), PAN-dah-ros (Classical Greek)

Aliases — Pandaros, Pandareus (in some traditions)

Ownership — Greek mythology and epic poetry; later adopted into medieval European literature (Chaucer, Shakespeare).

Powers
Archery

Pandarus is renowned for his skill with the bow. His greatest feat is shooting Menelaus, though the arrow is deflected by Athena, and he later wounds Diomedes with an arrow, though Diomedes continues to fight.

Limit: His arrows are not always fatal; Menelaus survives due to Athena's intervention, and Diomedes is only wounded. He is not invincible and is killed by Diomedes with a spear.

Diplomacy and manipulation (in later versions)

In Chaucer and Shakespeare, Pandarus is skilled at manipulating social situations to bring Troilus and Criseyde together. He uses flattery, persuasion, and even deception to achieve his ends.

Limit: His manipulation ultimately fails when Criseyde is exchanged for Antenor and later betrays Troilus with Diomedes.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In the Iliad, Pandarus is described as a skilled archer, likely carrying a bow made of horn from a wild goat he killed himself (Iliad 4.105-111). No detailed physical description is given, but he is a leader of the Lycians, so he would be armed and armored in the manner of a Trojan ally. Later medieval depictions, such as in Chaucer, do not provide physical descriptions, focusing instead on his role as a confidant and go-between.

Symbols — His primary symbol is his bow, which is described in detail in the Iliad. The bow is made from the horns of a goat he slew, crafted by a skilled artisan, and strung with sinew. In later art and literature, he is sometimes depicted with a bow or in the context of the Trojan War.

Domains — As a Lycian captain, Pandarus hails from Lycia, a region in Anatolia allied with Troy. In the Iliad, he is active on the Trojan plain and in the Greek camp. In medieval literature, his habitat is the courtly setting of Troy, though the setting is anachronistic and reflects medieval court life.

Accounts I
The Breaking of the TruceHomer, Iliad, Book 4

Heroic Age (traditionally c. 12th century BCE, but poem composed c. 8th century BCE)

After Paris and Menelaus duel to decide the war, a truce is declared. Athena, desiring the destruction of Troy, incites Pandarus to shoot an arrow at Menelaus. Pandarus, persuaded by the goddess, shoots and wounds Menelaus, breaking the truce and reigniting the full-scale war. The truce collapses, and the war resumes with renewed intensity. This act also sets the stage for Pandarus's own death at the hands of Diomedes.

Accounts II
The Death of PandarusHomer, Iliad, Book 5

Heroic Age

During the fighting, Pandarus and Aeneas face Diomedes. Pandarus wounds Diomedes with an arrow, but Diomedes, aided by Athena, kills Pandarus with a spear thrown at his head. Aeneas then defends Pandarus's body, but he too is wounded and saved by Aphrodite. Pandarus dies, and his death is avenged by Aeneas, who continues the fight. This episode also highlights Diomedes's prowess and Athena's favor.

The Love Affair of Troilus and CriseydeChaucer's Troilus and Criseyde (based on Boccaccio's Il Filostrato)

c. 1380s (set during the Trojan War)

In Chaucer's poem, Pandarus is the uncle of Criseyde and the best friend of Troilus. He orchestrates their love affair, bringing them together in his own house. The affair flourishes until Criseyde is exchanged for the Trojan prisoner Antenor. She promises to return, but she is seduced by Diomedes and never returns, breaking Troilus's heart. The affair ends tragically, and Pandarus is left to blame himself for his role in it. The story became a major source for Shakespeare's play.

Pandarus's arrow killed Menelaus. — Menelaus was only wounded; he survived.

Pandarus is a major hero in the Iliad. — He appears in only a few books and is a minor character, though pivotal.

Pandarus was a panderer in ancient Greek myth. — The word 'pander' derives from his name due to Chaucer's character, but in ancient sources he has no such role.
