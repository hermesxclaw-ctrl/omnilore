Spider Grandmother

Kokyangwuti, Thought Woman, The Wise Weaver

I sit in the dark and weave. Thread by thread, I hold the world together. Every web is a prayer; every strand connects a grandmother to a child not yet born.

Who It Is

Spider Grandmother (Kokyangwuti in Hopi) is one of the most important creator figures in Pueblo mythology — particularly among the Hopi people of Arizona. She is the grandmother of the world, a wise and ancient being who helped create humanity, taught the people how to weave, and continues to guide them with her ancient wisdom. Unlike the chaotic tricksters of other Native American traditions, Spider Grandmother is a benevolent, nurturing figure — the wise elder whose patience and creativity shaped the world.

Physically, Spider Grandmother is imagined as a grandmotherly spider-woman — sometimes appearing as a large spider with a gentle, human-like face, sometimes as an elderly woman with spider-like characteristics, and sometimes as a woman weaving at a loom in her underground kiva. In Hopi art, she is occasionally represented in kachina form or referenced through spider imagery in pottery and textile designs. She is associated with the below, the dark interior spaces, and the creative potential of weaving — each thread representing a connection between all living things.

In Hopi cosmology, Spider Grandmother plays a central role in the emergence of humanity from the previous worlds. The Hopi believe humanity has passed through multiple worlds (the First, Second, and Third Worlds) before emerging into the current Fourth World. Spider Grandmother was one of the spirit beings who guided the people through these transitions, offering wisdom and protection. During the emergence, she sat in her kiva weaving the patterns of destiny, and the people consulted her for guidance at critical moments.

Spider Grandmother's most distinctive role is as the teacher of weaving — an art form of profound spiritual significance in Pueblo culture. Weaving is a sacred act that mirrors creation itself: just as Spider Grandmother wove the world into being, human weavers participate in this creative act. Hopi brides traditionally receive a wedding robe (the white cotton 'oya' mantle) woven by the groom's male relatives, and the bride wears Spider Grandmother's blessing in the form of spider-web patterns. The loom itself is considered a spiritual technology that Spider Grandmother gave to humanity.

In Hopi stories, Spider Grandmother often appears to offer wisdom and magical assistance to heroes. In the story of the twin war gods, she provides the protective items and advice that allow them to succeed in their quest. She is associated with kivas (subterranean ceremonial chambers), where her presence is felt in the dark, quiet spaces of contemplation. Her web is a metaphor for the interconnectedness of all life — each thread touches others, and a vibration at any point resonates through the whole.

Spider Grandmother's influence extends beyond Hopi tradition. Among the Navajo (Diné), a similar figure called Spider Woman (Na'ashjé'ii Asdzáá) taught the Diné how to weave, gave them the gift of the loom, and is credited with teaching the Hero Twins important skills. The concept of a wise, creative spider grandmother appears in other Southwestern traditions and has been adopted by some contemporary feminist spiritual movements as a symbol of feminine creative power and intergenerational wisdom.
