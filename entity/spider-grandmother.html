Who It Is

Spider Grandmother (Kokyangwuti in Hopi) is one of the most important creator figures in Pueblo mythology — particularly among the Hopi people of Arizona. She is the grandmother of the world, a wise and ancient being who helped create humanity, taught the people how to weave, and continues to guide them with her ancient wisdom. Unlike the chaotic tricksters of other Native American traditions, Spider Grandmother is a benevolent, nurturing figure — the wise elder whose patience and creativity shaped the world.

Physically, Spider Grandmother is imagined as a grandmotherly spider-woman — sometimes appearing as a large spider with a gentle, human-like face, sometimes as an elderly woman with spider-like characteristics, and sometimes as a woman weaving at a loom in her underground kiva. In Hopi art, she is occasionally represented in kachina form or referenced through spider imagery in pottery and textile designs. She is associated with the below, the dark interior spaces, and the creative potential of weaving — each thread representing a connection between all living things.

In Hopi cosmology, Spider Grandmother plays a central role in the emergence of humanity from the previous worlds. The Hopi believe humanity has passed through multiple worlds (the First, Second, and Third Worlds) before emerging into the current Fourth World. Spider Grandmother was one of the spirit beings who guided the people through these transitions, offering wisdom and protection. During the emergence, she sat in her kiva weaving the patterns of destiny, and the people consulted her for guidance at critical moments.

Spider Grandmother's most distinctive role is as the teacher of weaving — an art form of profound spiritual significance in Pueblo culture. Weaving is a sacred act that mirrors creation itself: just as Spider Grandmother wove the world into being, human weavers participate in this creative act. Hopi brides traditionally receive a wedding robe (the white cotton 'oya' mantle) woven by the groom's male relatives, and the bride wears Spider Grandmother's blessing in the form of spider-web patterns. The loom itself is considered a spiritual technology that Spider Grandmother gave to humanity.

In Hopi stories, Spider Grandmother often appears to offer wisdom and magical assistance to heroes. In the story of the twin war gods, she provides the protective items and advice that allow them to succeed in their quest. She is associated with kivas (subterranean ceremonial chambers), where her presence is felt in the dark, quiet spaces of contemplation. Her web is a metaphor for the interconnectedness of all life — each thread touches others, and a vibration at any point resonates through the whole.

Spider Grandmother's influence extends beyond Hopi tradition. Among the Navajo (Diné), a similar figure called Spider Woman (Na'ashjé'ii Asdzáá) taught the Diné how to weave, gave them the gift of the loom, and is credited with teaching the Hero Twins important skills. The concept of a wise, creative spider grandmother appears in other Southwestern traditions and has been adopted by some contemporary feminist spiritual movements as a symbol of feminine creative power and intergenerational wisdom.

Form

Spider Grandmother is described as a grandmotherly spider-being, existing in a liminal space between human and arachnid forms. Sometimes she appears as a large spider with a gentle, human-like face — her eyes wise and kind, her movements deliberate and graceful. Sometimes she appears as an elderly woman with spider-like characteristics: thin, nimble fingers that can work thread with supernatural skill, or the ability to produce silk. Most commonly, she is imagined as a woman sitting at a loom in her underground kiva, weaving the patterns of destiny. In this form, she is indistinguishable from a human grandmother — small, patient, wise — except that the cloth on her loom is the fabric of reality itself. She is associated with the colors of the earth (brown, red-brown, ochre), with the textures of cotton and wool cloth, and with the darkness of the kiva — the interior, underground space where creation happens. Unlike many mythological figures, she is never depicted as young, beautiful, or physically powerful in the conventional sense — her power is age, wisdom, and the transformative potential of patient craft.

Powers

Cosmic Weaving / Pattern Creation Spider Grandmother's fundamental power is the ability to weave patterns of reality. Like a spider spinning a web — patient, geometric, comprehensive — she weaves the structure of the world. This is simultaneously literal (she sits at a loom) and metaphorical (her weaving IS creation). Every thread is a connection, every knot is a relationship, every pattern is a destiny. The world is her loom, and all beings are threads in her web. This power is expressed most dramatically in the Keresan conception of Thought Woman: she thinks patterns, and those thoughts become reality. Her creative power is cognitive and textile — she thinks and weaves the world into being. Hopi emergence narrative; Keresan creation narratives (Acoma/Laguna); Silko (1977) Her weaving is not arbitrary or absolute — it establishes patterns and connections but does not dictate every detail. Human free will operates within the web she has spun. The weaving requires patience and cannot be rushed. The degree of her creative agency varies: in Keresan tradition she creates from thought alone; in Hopi tradition she weaves patterns into an existing cosmos.

Guide Through the Emergence Spider Grandmother guided humanity through the emergence from the previous worlds to the current Fourth World. As the people moved from the First World (Tokpela, endless space) through the Second and Third Worlds (destroyed by corruption), Spider Grandmother offered wisdom, protection, and practical guidance. She knew the way upward — the route through the sipapu (the emergence hole) — and she helped the people navigate the challenges of each world. Her guidance is gentle but essential: without her, the people would have been lost in the transitions between worlds. Hopi emergence narrative; Waters (1963); Hopi oral tradition She guides and advises — she does not carry or force. Humanity must make the journey itself; Spider Grandmother provides the knowledge of how to do so. The specific role of Spider Grandmother in each world's emergence varies across Hopi oral traditions. Some emphasize her role more in certain worlds than others.

Gift of Weaving and Pottery Spider Grandmother taught humans the arts of weaving (cotton and wool textiles) and pottery — the two foundational crafts of Pueblo material culture. Weaving is her primary gift: the loom, the spindle, the techniques of spinning and dyeing and patterning. In Pueblo culture, weaving is not a secular craft but a sacred act that mirrors Spider Grandmother's cosmic weaving. Pottery is her secondary gift: the art of taking earth, shaping it, firing it in darkness, and producing vessels of beauty and utility. Both gifts connect human makers to the creative power of Spider Grandmother herself. Hopi and Pueblo oral tradition; material culture evidence The gifts must be received, practiced, and passed on — they are not automatic. If a community stops weaving or making pottery, the connection to Spider Grandmother weakens. Some Pueblo traditions attribute the origin of pottery to other figures (e.g., Clay Old Woman) rather than exclusively to Spider Grandmother. Weaving is more consistently attributed to her.

Grandmotherly Wisdom and Counsel Spider Grandmother is the source of wise counsel: when the people face difficult decisions, when the Hero Twins (in some Pueblo traditions) need guidance on their quest, when leaders must choose the right path — Spider Grandmother is consulted. Her wisdom is not abstract philosophy but practical, grandmotherly advice: how to resolve a conflict, how to prepare for a journey, how to treat others with respect. She embodies the Pueblo ideal of the wise elder whose knowledge, accumulated through long experience, guides the community. Hopi and Keresan oral tradition Her counsel must be sought and respected — she does not impose it. Communities and individuals who ignore her advice suffer the consequences of their own choices. The specific content of her counsel varies by story and tradition. In some stories, her advice is cryptic and requires interpretation; in others, it is straightforward.

Thought-Creation (Keresan Thought Woman) In Keresan tradition, Spider Grandmother as Thought Woman (Ts'its'tsi'nako) possesses the power of creation through thought and language. She thinks things, and as she thinks them, they come into being. She names things, and the act of naming creates them. This is a fundamentally linguistic and cognitive creative power — the world is spun from mind and words, not from physical substance. As Leslie Marmon Silko writes: 'Thought-Woman, the spider, / named things and / as she named them / they appeared.' Keresan (Acoma/Laguna) creation narratives; Silko (1977, 1981) Thought-creation requires the right thoughts, the right names. If she thought incorrectly or named things wrongly, the creation would be flawed. The power is precise but fragile. This power is specifically Keresan and is not present in the same form in Hopi tradition, where Spider Grandmother is a helper in emergence, not a primordial thinker-creator.

Accounts

In Hopi cosmology, humanity has passed through multiple worlds. In the First World (Tokpela, endless space), the people lived in harmony with the Creator Taiowa and his nephew Sotuknang, but divisions arose. In the Second World (Tokpa, dark midnight), the people developed greed and materialism. In the Third World (Kuskurza), they became sexually corrupt and war-like. Each world was destroyed, and the people who remained faithful to the Creator's way were led upward through the sipapu by Spider Grandmother. She guided them through the dark passages between worlds, offered counsel during crises, and helped maintain the spiritual focus needed to complete the journey. When they finally emerged into the Fourth World (the current world), Spider Grandmother remained with them, continuing to offer guidance and the gifts of weaving and pottery. The emergence narrative frames Spider Grandmother as the people's constant guide through cosmic transitions.

In the beginning, there was only Thought Woman (Ts'its'tsi'nako), the spider. She was alone in the darkness. She began to think — and her thoughts became things. She thought of the earth, and it appeared. She thought of the sky, and it appeared. She named the mountains, the rivers, the animals, the plants — and as she named them, they came into being. Finally, she thought of human beings, named them, and they appeared. The entire universe is the result of Spider Grandmother's thinking — her mind weaving reality as a spider weaves a web. This is one of the most radically idealist creation narratives in world mythology: the material world is literally made of thought.

After the emergence into the Fourth World, Spider Grandmother gathered the people and taught them the essential arts. She showed the women how to weave — how to spin cotton, how to set up the loom, how to create patterns, how to dye the thread with plant colors. She taught them that weaving is a sacred act: every thread is a prayer, every pattern a story, every completed textile a connection to the cosmic web. She also taught pottery — how to dig the clay, how to shape the vessel, how to fire it, how to paint it with designs. These arts, she explained, are not mere crafts but spiritual practices that maintain the people's connection to creation. She then withdrew to her kiva, where she continues to weave, watching over the people she taught.

In some Hopi traditions, Spider Grandmother aids the Twin War Gods (or Hero Twins) in their quests. She provides them with protective charms, advice, and the knowledge of how to overcome obstacles. Like the Diné Spider Woman who helps the Navajo Hero Twins, Hopi Spider Grandmother serves as the wise grandmother-ally who empowers younger, more active heroes. Her role is not to fight but to equip, advise, and protect through her wisdom and magical gifts.

Content Notes

The Hopi emergence narrative, in which Spider Grandmother guides the people, is framed by the destruction of three previous worlds. In each case, humanity became corrupt — through division, greed, materialism, sexual misconduct, or warfare — and Taiowa and Sotuknang destroyed the world, killing all but the faithful remnant whom Spider Grandmother guided upward. This is a narrative of repeated apocalypse: entire worlds, entire humanities, annihilated for moral failure. The current Fourth World is understood to be on the same trajectory and will also be destroyed if humanity does not maintain the Hopi way. Spider Grandmother's role is thus framed by cosmic violence — she is the guide through serial genocides.

The destruction of previous worlds is a common motif in Indigenous American emergence narratives. The Hopi version is particularly elaborated and morally specific. The narrative functions as both a cosmological account and a warning about the consequences of straying from the Creator's teachings.

Hopi emergence narrative; Waters (1963)

Frank Waters' 'Book of the Hopi' (1963), which presents Spider Grandmother's role in the emergence, has been criticized by some Hopi scholars and community members for publishing sacred ceremonial knowledge without proper authorization. The book, compiled with Hopi informants (some of whom were later alleged to have shared knowledge they were not authorized to share), made Hopi cosmology widely available to a non-Hopi audience. This publication is part of a broader pattern of non-Native researchers (Cushing, Parsons, Waters, and others) collecting and publishing Pueblo sacred narratives without community consent, causing ongoing cultural harm.

The ethics of publishing Indigenous sacred knowledge is a central concern in contemporary Native American studies. Many Pueblo communities restrict access to certain narratives and ceremonial details, and publication without authorization violates both cultural protocols and, increasingly, research ethics standards.

What We Don't Know

The living practice of Spider Grandmother veneration in contemporary Pueblo communities is under-documented in accessible scholarly literature, partly because of appropriate cultural restrictions on sharing sacred knowledge. The relationship between Keresan Thought Woman and Hopi Kokyangwuti — specifically, how these two traditions developed from a shared ancestral Pueblo spirituality — is a fascinating archaeological-religious question that has not been definitively addressed. The transmission of weaving knowledge from grandmother to granddaughter, and how that transmission carries Spider Grandmother's spiritual presence, would benefit from ethnographic documentation by Pueblo scholars working within their own communities. The impact of Frank Waters' 'Book of the Hopi' on Hopi communities — both the harms of unauthorized publication and any benefits of increased cultural visibility — is a complex topic that deserves more community-led discussion.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Spider Grandmother as the universe's backend code. She doesn't appear in the user interface — you won't see her in the sky, she doesn't answer prayers directly, she's not standing at the gates of heaven. She's in the kiva, in the dark, at the loom, writing the operating system. Every thread is a subroutine. Every pattern is an algorithm. The world as you experience it — the sun rising, the corn growing, the people loving and fighting and dying and being reborn — is the frontend. Spider Grandmother is the backend. She doesn't intervene because she doesn't need to — she wrote the code, and the code is elegant. But when the system crashes (as it did three times before, in the previous worlds), she's the one who guides the faithful users through the backup and restore. She's the ultimate sysadmin: quiet, patient, ancient, and absolutely essential. And like any good sysadmin, she taught the users how to maintain the system themselves — the weaving, the pottery, the prayers, the protocols. She's not a god who demands worship. She's a grandmother who taught her grandchildren to tie their own shoes.
