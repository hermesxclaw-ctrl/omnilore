Who It Is

Before the river was a highway of steamboats and barges, before the bluffs were quarried for limestone, there was a painted monster. In 1673, Father Jacques Marquette and his party, the first Europeans to explore the Mississippi, saw it and were so struck with fear and awe that they wrote it down in their journals. It was a creature of the cliffs—part bird, part fish, part man—a guardian of a place where the water meets the sky. But the story we know today, of a dragon that ate villagers, is not the story the Illini might have told. It is a ghost of a legend, a myth about a myth, and its true meaning was lost when the people who made it were scattered and the stone it was painted on was ground to dust.

The behavior attributed to the Piasa is largely derived from John Russell's 1836 myth, which is not considered authentic. In this story, it was a man-eater that lurked in a cave and would swoop down to carry off villagers. In the more plausible, older interpretation aligned with the Thunderbird archetype, it would have been a powerful, supernatural being associated with the sky, storms, and the upper world, not a common predator.

Form & Signs

Appearance — According to Father Marquette's 1673 account, the Piasa was depicted as a creature the size of a calf, with a human-like face, a beard, a scaly, fish-like body, and a long, serpentine tail. It had horns on its head, and its body was painted in a variety of colors—red, green, and black—making it a striking and fearsome sight on the limestone cliff.

Symbols — The Piasa's symbolism is debated. It is most commonly linked to the Thunderbird archetype, representing power, protection, and the untamed forces of nature. In the popular (and likely invented) tradition, it symbolizes a dangerous, monstrous threat that must be overcome. For the city of Alton, it has become a symbol of local identity and heritage, though this is a modern, non-Indigenous appropriation.

Domains — Territorial (River Bluffs). The Piasa is intrinsically linked to the Mississippi River and its high, rocky bluffs. It is a liminal figure, existing between the water (its fish-like body) and the sky (its wings and horns), guarding a specific, dramatic landscape.

Accounts I
Marquette's EncounterFather Jacques Marquette's journal, 1673

17th century

While exploring the Mississippi River, Marquette and his party saw the painted images on the bluffs. He described them in detail as monstrous creatures, noting their terrifying appearance and the skill of the artists. He did not record any Indigenous story about them at the time. This account became the foundational description of the Piasa for all later Euro-American audiences.

Accounts II
The Legend of Chief OuatogaJohn Russell's article in the Alton Telegraph, 1836

19th century

According to Russell, the Piasa was a monstrous bird that preyed on the Illini people, carrying them off to its cave. The great chief Ouatoga, after a period of fasting and prayer, devised a plan. He and his warriors ambushed the creature as it emerged from its cave, killing it with a hail of poisoned arrows. The image on the bluff was painted to commemorate this victory. This story, while almost certainly a fabrication, became the most popular and enduring 'origin' of the Piasa, shaping its public image for nearly two centuries.

The Destruction and Rebirth of the ImageLocal history of Alton, Illinois

19th - 21st century

The original limestone bluff was quarried in the 19th century, destroying the original Piasa painting. In the 20th century, a series of murals were painted on the remaining bluff to recreate the image, and later a large concrete representation was built. These modern images are based on Marquette's description and popular imagination. The Piasa's image was preserved and popularized, but the original physical artifact was lost. The modern images are a form of historical recreation and civic branding.
