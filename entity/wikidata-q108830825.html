Who It Is

On the morning of 14 July 1789, the most feared fortress in Paris held just seven prisoners - four forgers, two madmen and a debauched count - behind walls 24 meters high and 3 meters thick. By sunset its governor's head was on a pike, its gunpowder was in the hands of artisans, and its stones were already being hawked as relics of freedom. The Bastille was never really the hell its enemies painted, nor the orderly police house its defenders claimed - it was something more dangerous: a secret that let everyone imagine the worst.

Not a person, but as an institution the Bastille behaved as a hybrid fortress-prison-police annex. Under Charles V-VI it was a royal refuge and garrison (captain, knight, eight squires, ten crossbowmen). Under Louis XI and later Louis XIV it operated as an extrajudicial detention centre for high-status enemies, Huguenots after 1685, and Jansenists. Under Louis XV and XVI it became primarily an instrument of the Lieutenant Général de Police: holding writers, booksellers and printers for censorship violations, often for days to months, with interrogations in the Arsenal. Powers
Integrated Artillery Platform and Uniform Curtain Defence

By building towers and curtain walls to identical height (24m) with a continuous broad wall-walk, the Bastille allowed rapid lateral movement of troops and cannon across the entire perimeter, unlike Vincennes-style keeps. Contemporary military architects praised its ability to concentrate firepower on any approach.

Limit: Innovative for 1370 but obsolete by the 16th century against cannon siege; required addition of external earthen bastion in 1553. High walls presented large target for artillery; moat could be drained.

Control of the Porte Saint-Antoine Gateway

Straddling the Rue Saint-Antoine, the fortress controlled the sole eastern gate through the Charles V wall, with four drawbridges regulating civilian, military and river-moat traffic. Whoever held the Bastille controlled entry to Paris from the east.

Limit: After Louis XIV demolished the Paris walls (1670s) and created boulevards, its strategic gateway function declined; remained symbolically important but militarily peripheral.

Extrajudicial Detention via Lettre de Cachet

As a prison d'État, the Bastille could hold individuals indefinitely without trial on a sealed royal letter, bypassing Parlement. Used for political, religious (Huguenots post-1685), family discipline (at family's request), and police censorship cases.

Limit: Detention required royal or police authority; prisoners retained right to petition, receive visitors with permission, and were often released after weeks-months. By 1780s, reformist ministers (Malesherbes, Breteuil) required justification and limited duration.

Arsenal and Gunpowder Store

Housed a major royal arsenal and, in July 1789, 250 barrels of gunpowder (c. 13,000 kg) transferred from the Arsenal for safekeeping, plus cannon, muskets and ammunition. This made it a logistical target.

Limit: Powder storage made it vulnerable to explosion and motivated the crowd's attack more than desire to free prisoners. Garrison was undermanned to defend both fortress and powder.

Psychological Deterrence and Secrecy

Its windowless mass, reputation for oubliettes and secret burials, and prohibition on communication created terror disproportionate to its size. Police intentionally cultivated mystery by burying dead prisoners at night in Saint-Paul cemetery and forbidding turnkeys to speak to inmates.

Limit: Secrecy backfired: memoirs like Henri Masers de Latude's (1787) and Simon-Nicolas-Henri Linguet's 'Mémoires sur la Bastille' (1783) amplified horror and fueled calls for demolition, even as actual conditions improved.

Accounts I
Construction of the Fortress (1357-1382)Royal accounts of Charles V, chronicle of Jean Froissart, and Hugues Aubriot's provost records

1357-1382 (Hundred Years' War)

After the English capture of King John II at Poitiers (1356), Provost Étienne Marcel fortified the Porte Saint-Antoine with two towers and a ditch in 1357. Charles V, fearing English and mercenary attacks on vulnerable eastern Paris, ordered Hugues Aubriot to expand this into a full fortress from 1370. Two towers were built behind Marcel's gate, then two to the north and two to the south, creating eight irregular towers linked by thick curtain walls. Charles V built the Hôtel Saint-Pol palace nearby to shelter under its guns. The innovative uniform-height design was completed under Charles VI by 1382 and immediately copied at Pierrefonds and in England at Nunney Castle. Created the strongest fortress in Paris and a royal safe-haven; established the Bastille's dual role as gate-defence and royal refuge that defined its next four centuries.

Refuge and Prison in the Armagnac-Burgundian Civil WarJournal d'un bourgeois de Paris (1418), Enguerrand de Monstrelet chronicle

1417-1420

During the civil war between Armagnacs and Burgundians, the Bastille was formally declared a state prison in 1417. In May-June 1418, Burgundian partisans massacred Armagnacs in Paris. The Dauphin (future Charles VII) fled to the Bastille for safety before escaping through the Porte Saint-Antoine. Its creator Hugues Aubriot, previously imprisoned there for heresy and extortion, exemplified its early use for political detention. The fortress changed hands with the city, demonstrating its value as both prison and citadel. Established precedent of Bastille as political prison and royal last redoubt inside Paris; foreshadowed its role in every subsequent Parisian uprising.

English Occupation (1420-1436)Henry V's ordinances, Journal d'un bourgeois de Paris, Thomas Beaufort's captaincy records

1420-1436

Following the Treaty of Troyes (1420), Henry V occupied Paris and installed Thomas Beaufort, Duke of Exeter, as captain of the Bastille. The English expanded its prison use and installed a dedicated gaoler (first mentioned 1430 after a failed prisoner escape where inmates overpowered a sleeping guard). When Charles VII recaptured Paris in 1436, English-aligned Parisians fortified themselves in the Bastille but surrendered after a siege when food ran out, paying ransom to leave. The episode showed the fortress could not hold Paris without external support. Consolidated Bastille's identity as state prison under both French and English regimes; created first documented prison staff structure.

Accounts II
The Fronde and the Battle of the Faubourg Saint-Antoine (1652)Mémoires of Cardinal de Retz, La Rochefoucauld, and contemporary Mazarinades

1648-1653, climax 2 July 1652

During the Fronde (noble revolt against Cardinal Mazarin and the regency of Anne of Austria), the Bastille sided with the rebels. On 2 July 1652, the Battle of the Faubourg Saint-Antoine was fought directly beneath its walls between Louis II de Bourbon, Prince de Condé (Frondeur) and royal troops under Turenne. The Bastille's cannon fired on the royal army, and its governor opened the gates to allow Condé's defeated forces to escape into Paris, saving the rebellion temporarily. Louis XIV, watching from Charonne, never forgot the humiliation. Convinced Louis XIV to tame Paris and use the Bastille thereafter primarily as a prison for high-status opponents rather than a semi-autonomous fortress; led to later demolition of Paris walls to prevent future barricaded revolts.

Louis XIV's State Penitentiary and the Man in the Iron MaskArchives de la Bastille, letters of Saint-Mars, Voltaire's 'Siècle de Louis XIV', Alexandre Dumas later retellings

1659-1715 (Louis XIV), with legend extending to 1703 death

From 1659 Louis XIV systematized the Bastille as a state penitentiary for those who offended the king: nobles involved in the Affair of the Poisons, Jansenists, and after the Revocation of the Edict of Nantes (1685) Huguenot pastors. Its most famous inmate arrived in 1698: Eustache Dauger, transferred by gaoler Bénigne Dauvergne de Saint-Mars from Pignerol and the Îles Sainte-Marguerite, always veiled with a black velvet (later mythologized as iron) mask. He died in the Bastille 19 November 1703 and was buried as 'Marchioly'. Voltaire, imprisoned twice (1717-1718 and 1726) for satirical verses, wrote that he found good food and visitors, shaping the Enlightenment critique that the Bastille's evil was arbitrariness, not squalor. By 1789, 5,279 prisoners had passed through since 1659. Created the enduring literary myth of the masked prisoner and cemented Bastille's reputation as the king's personal prison; provided Enlightenment philosophes with firsthand material to attack lettres de cachet.

The Storming of the Bastille - 14 July 1789Procès-verbal de l'Hôtel de Ville, reports of Lieutenant de Launay, eyewitness accounts by Jean-Philippe-Guy Le Gentil and Jean-Baptiste Humbert

14 July 1789

Amid financial crisis, the National Assembly's formation, and fear of royal troops around Paris, the Permanent Committee at the Hôtel de Ville sought gunpowder. After seizing arms at Les Invalides, a crowd of ~900-1,000, mainly artisans and shopkeepers from the Faubourg Saint-Antoine, marched on the Bastille at 10am to demand its powder (13 tonnes) and cannon. Governor Bernard-René Jourdan de Launay, with 82 Invalides and 32 Swiss soldiers, initially negotiated but fired on the crowd after a drawbridge accident, killing ~100. After four hours of musket fire and the arrival of Gardes Françaises with cannon, de Launay surrendered. The crowd freed seven prisoners (four forgers, two lunatics, one aristocratic deviant - the Comte de Solages), killed de Launay and three officers, and paraded their heads on pikes. The Committee ordered demolition the next day. Immediate collapse of royal authority in Paris; Louis XVI withdrew troops and recalled Necker; 14 July became Fête de la Fédération (1790) and later French national holiday; demolition began and relics were distributed as anti-despoti

Demolition and Afterlife as Symbol (1789-1840)Pierre-François Palloy accounts, Hubert Robert paintings, Revolutionary festivals records

1789-1840

Entrepreneur Pierre-François Palloy, a master mason, obtained the contract to demolish the Bastille (July 1789 - 1791) employing up to 800 workers. He marketed the demolition as patriotic labour, sending carved stone models of the fortress to all 83 départements, forging medals from its chains, and sending its keys to George Washington via Lafayette. Almost nothing remained except foundation traces. The empty Place de la Bastille hosted the guillotine in June 1794, the Fontaine de la Régénération, and later Napoleon's planned elephant fountain. After the July Revolution of 1830, Louis-Philippe erected the Colonne de Juillet (1835-1840) on the site to honour the 1830 dead, permanently fusing Bastille ground with republican martyrdom. Historians from the Restoration onward debated whether the fortress had been a well-run police detention house rather than a dungeon. Transformed physical fortress into portable relic and national myth; established Place de la Bastille as France's premier site for revolutionary commemoration and protest, used in 1830, 1848, 1871 and beyond.

The Bastille was a crowded medieval dungeon holding hundreds of innocent political prisoners in chains when it was stormed. — On 14 July 1789 it held seven prisoners, none of them political in the modern sense, in relatively spacious rooms. The 18th-century Bastille typically held fewer than 20 at a time and functioned as a discreet police detention house for censorship and family discipline cases, with furnished apartments for those who could pay.

The crowd stormed the Bastille solely to liberate prisoners and overthrow tyranny. — Primary objective was to seize the 13 tonnes of gunpowder and cannon stored there after the royal arsenal was moved for safety; liberation of prisoners was secondary and symbolic. The Hôtel de Ville committee minutes explicitly list powder as motive.

Prisoners in the Bastille were routinely tortured and left to die in oubliettes. — Judicial torture was legal but rarely used in the Bastille after the 1720s and abolished 1780-1788; cachots existed as punishment cells but not trapdoor oubliettes. Skeletons found in walls during demolition were invented by pamphleteers; Palloy's official reports found none.

The Bastille was destroyed by an angry mob on 14 July itself. — The fortress surrendered 14 July but was demolished systematically by contractor Pierre-François Palloy with 800 paid workers from July 1789 to 1791 under municipal order; it took nearly two years to dismantle the 24-meter walls.

The Man in the Iron Mask wore a solid iron mask welded shut. — Contemporary Saint-Mars correspondence says a black velvet mask worn only when being moved or seen; the iron version was Voltaire's embellishment (1771) to heighten horror, later popularized by Alexandre Dumas.
