Who It Is

Orunmila (also called Ifa or Orula) is the Yoruba orisha of wisdom, knowledge, divination, and destiny. He is the divine witness present at the creation of every human soul, who knows every person's destiny (ori) from the moment of their birth. As the keeper of the Ifa divination system — one of the most elaborate and sophisticated systems of geomancy in the world, recognized by UNESCO as a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity — Orunmila mediates between human ignorance and divine knowledge, interpreting the will of Olodumare for those who seek guidance.

Physically, Orunmila is depicted as a sage-like figure, often elderly, dressed in green and yellow, carrying an iroke (divination tapper) and an opon Ifa (divination tray). He is associated with the palm tree and the palm nut, which serve as his primary divination tools. In Yoruba art, Orunmila appears less frequently than warrior or kingly orishas, reflecting his introspective, intellectual nature. His priesthood — the Babalawos ('Fathers of Secrets') — are exclusively male (in traditional practice) and undergo decades of training to master the 256 Odu Ifa, the sacred verses that comprise the system.

The Ifa corpus contains thousands of verses (ese Ifa) organized into 256 chapters (Odu). Each Odu corresponds to a specific pattern made by casting sixteen palm nuts or a divination chain (opele), and each contains hundreds of stories, proverbs, prescriptions, and moral teachings. When a person consults Ifa, the Babalawo casts the chain, identifies which Odu has appeared, and recites verses relevant to the client's situation. The system is astonishingly comprehensive, addressing everything from health and marriage to business and warfare. Orunmila himself is said to have been present at the beginning of creation, recording the destinies of all souls in the divine ledger.

A key myth tells how Orunmila acquired his knowledge. He traveled widely across the world and to the realm of the dead, learning from every experience and recording his wisdom. When he returned, he systematized this knowledge into Ifa. Another myth describes how Death (Iku) attempted to claim Orunmila, but the god of wisdom tricked Death through his superior knowledge, escaping into the divination system itself — meaning that as long as Ifa exists, Orunmila lives through it.

In Santería, Orula (Orunmila) is syncretized with Saint Francis of Assisi. He is the orisha of destiny, and many followers receive the 'hand of Orula' (mano de Orula) as their first major initiation. His sacred number is sixteen, and offerings include palm nuts, yams, and green and yellow objects.

Form

Orunmila is typically depicted as an elderly man of immense wisdom, often with a calm, contemplative expression. He is associated with the colors green and yellow (or sometimes brown, reflecting his connection to the palm tree from which divination nuts come). He carries the instruments of Ifá divination: the divination chain (ọ̀pẹ̀lẹ̀) in one hand, and the palm nuts (ikin) or the divination tray (ọpọ́n Ifá) in the other. The divination tray — a circular or rectangular wooden board, often beautifully carved — is his most iconic attribute. In some depictions, he wears beads of green and brown and is accompanied by the opon Ifá (divination tray) and iroke Ifá (a tapper used to invoke his presence). In Cuban Santería, Orula is visualized as a wise elder in green and yellow, sometimes shown writing or interpreting signs. Unlike many orishas, Orunmila is never depicted in warrior poses or with weapons — his power is intellectual and spiritual, not martial. His sacred numbers are 16 (the principal Odù) and its multiples.

Powers

Witness and Interpreter of Destiny Orunmila was present when Olodumare assigned destinies (àyànmọ́) to every soul before birth. He witnessed each soul's choice of life-path — their lifespan, prosperity, spouse, children, and death. Because of this, he possesses complete and perfect knowledge of every individual's destiny. Through the Ifá divination system, he reveals this knowledge to humans, helping them understand their chosen path and identify the obstacles and opportunities ahead. Ifá Literary Corpus / Universal tradition Orunmila reveals destiny but does not force it. Humans retain free will and can deviate from their chosen path. His knowledge is about the 'chosen' destiny, not necessarily the 'actual' destiny if the person has strayed from it. Some traditions emphasize the immutability of the witnessed destiny; others emphasize Orunmila's ability to prescribe ẹbọ (sacrifice) that can change even a 'chosen' bad destiny.

Mastery of Ifá Divination Orunmila is the divine source and master of the Ifá divination system — the most elaborate indigenous knowledge system in Africa. The system uses 256 Odù (signs), each containing hundreds of verses (ẹsẹ) of poetry, mythology, medicine, ethics, and practical advice. Through the manipulation of palm nuts (ikin) or the divination chain (ọ̀pẹ̀lẹ̀), a trained babaláwo can access Orunmila's wisdom and provide detailed guidance on any life situation. The system is so sophisticated that it has been compared to a library or computer database in its structure and comprehensiveness. Ifá tradition (universal) Access to Ifá's full wisdom requires years of training and initiation. Laypeople can consult Ifá through a babaláwo but cannot perform divination themselves. The system is traditionally male-only for full priesthood (iyanifá — female Ifá priests — exist in some but not all lineages). The male-only restriction on Ifá priesthood is contested, with some lineages ordaining women as iyanifá and others maintaining the traditional prohibition.

Prescription of Sacrifice (Ẹbọ) When divination reveals a problem, obstacle, or bad destiny, Orunmila prescribes the specific ẹbọ (sacrifice) that can resolve it. These sacrifices may involve offerings of food, animals, money, or ritual actions, and are tailored to the specific Odù that appeared in the divination. The ẹbọ is not a bribe but a cosmic adjustment — it realigns the person with their proper destiny or redirects negative forces. Ifá tradition (universal) Ẹbọ must be performed correctly according to the prescription; an incorrect or incomplete ẹbọ is ineffective. Some destinies cannot be fully changed, only mitigated. The exact mechanism by which ẹbọ works — is it a spiritual transaction, a psychological realignment, or a combination of both? — is debated within the tradition as well as by outside scholars.

Omniscience (Divine Knowledge) Orunmila is described as knowing everything — past, present, and future. His knowledge encompasses not just individual destinies but the entire cosmic order. The Odù Ifá contain comprehensive knowledge on topics ranging from herbal medicine and agriculture to astronomy, ethics, and political philosophy — all attributed to Orunmila's revealed wisdom. He is sometimes called 'Ẹlẹ́rìí Ìpín' (Witness of Destiny) and 'A-kéré-ò-ṣá-iyan' (the small one who knows all secrets). Ifá Literary Corpus / Yoruba oral tradition His omniscience is expressed through the mediated form of divination — it is not direct prophecy. Humans must consult him through the proper ritual channels to access his knowledge. The scope of Orunmila's knowledge — does he know literally everything, or is his knowledge limited to destinies and matters accessible through the Odù? — is debated.

Longevity and Physical Resilience In mythology, Orunmila is described as having lived for an extraordinarily long time — in some accounts, he was present at creation and will be present at the end. He is said to have survived attempts on his life, illnesses, and other dangers through his wisdom and the sacrifices prescribed by Ifá. His long life embodies the enduring quality of wisdom itself. Ifá Literary Corpus / Yoruba oral tradition Longevity is a mythological attribute, not a power he grants to devotees (though he can prescribe sacrifices for long life). Some traditions describe Orunmila as dying or departing the earth (ascending to heaven), suggesting his earthly presence was finite; others describe him as eternally present.

Behavior & Habitat

Orunmila is the embodiment of wisdom, knowledge, and intellectual depth. He is calm, deliberate, and infinitely patient — no question is too small, no confusion too tangled for his insight. He speaks in proverbs, parables, and poetic verses, revealing truth through layered meanings rather than direct statements. He is not a trickster (like Eshu) but a revealer — his purpose is to illuminate, not to confuse. He is deeply compassionate toward human ignorance and suffering, and the Ifá system exists specifically because he sought to provide humanity with a way to understand their destinies and navigate life's difficulties. He is described as having been present at creation, witnessing Olodumare's distribution of destinies, and thus possesses perfect knowledge of every soul's chosen path. His consultations through divination are considered authoritative because he 'was there' when destinies were assigned.

Accounts

Before each soul is born, it appears before Olodumare to choose its destiny (àyànmọ́). Orunmila is present as the witness (ẹlẹ́rìí) — the one who observes and remembers each soul's choice. The soul selects its parents, its lifespan, its prosperity, its spouse, its children, and its manner of death. After choosing, the soul passes through the waters of forgetfulness and is born into the world with no memory of its choice. The purpose of Ifá divination, established by Orunmila, is to help individuals remember their chosen destiny and align their lives with it. When a person consults Ifá and receives their Odù, they are, in effect, being reminded of the choice they made before birth.

After the creation of the world, Olodumare observed that humans, having forgotten their destinies, were suffering unnecessarily. He commissioned Orunmila to create a system by which humans could recover knowledge of their destinies and receive guidance. Orunmila, using his infinite wisdom, developed the Ifá divination system — the 16 principal Odù, the 256 volumes of verse, the methods of divination, and the prescription of sacrifices. He then taught the system to the first babaláwo (Ifá priests), who transmitted it to succeeding generations. In some versions, Orunmila descended to earth personally to teach Ifá; in others, he trained disciples in heaven who then brought the knowledge to earth.

Death (Ikú) challenged Orunmila to a contest of wisdom, confident that it could defeat even the god of knowledge. They posed riddles to each other, each trying to outsmart the other. Death's riddles were about endings, loss, and oblivion; Orunmila's riddles were about beginnings, cycles, and renewal. The contest continued for an impossibly long time — in some versions, 16 days and 16 nights. Finally, Orunmila posed a riddle that Death could not answer, and Death conceded defeat. As a reward, Orunmila gained power over Death — not the ability to abolish it, but the knowledge to delay it, to prescribe sacrifices that extend life. This is why Ifá divination can prescribe ẹbọ for long life and why those who follow Ifá's guidance are believed to live fuller, longer lives.

Orunmila, having created the Ifá system, initially kept its secrets to himself, unwilling to share the full knowledge with others — even with the other orishas. When the orishas needed guidance and Orunmila was unavailable, they were helpless. Oshun, using her charm, beauty, and cunning, seduced Orunmila (or in some versions, tricked him) and learned the secrets of divination. She then taught these secrets to humanity (or to the other orishas), ensuring that wisdom was not hoarded by one being. Orunmila, upon discovering what had happened, was initially angry but eventually accepted that knowledge must be shared. The story explains Oshun's status as a patroness of divination alongside Orunmila and emphasizes the principle that wisdom belongs to all who seek it, not just to its originator.

What We Don't Know

The historical origins of Ifá — its development from a simpler divination practice into the elaborate 256-Odù system — are not well understood. The relationship between Ifá and other West African divination systems (e.g., Fa in Benin, Afa among the Igbo, systems among the Edo and Fon) suggests a broader regional knowledge complex whose history has not been fully reconstructed. The adaptation of Ifá in the digital age — online divination, Ifá apps, the tension between traditional secrecy and digital accessibility — is an emerging phenomenon that challenges centuries-old norms. The gender question (iyanifá / female Ifá priests) continues to evolve and requires ongoing ethnographic documentation.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Orunmila not as a 'god of wisdom' but as the librarian of the universe. Before you were born, you wrote a book — the book of your life. Every choice you would make, every person you would love, every obstacle you would face, every moment of joy and sorrow. You wrote it, and then you put it on a shelf in a cosmic library. When you were born, you forgot which shelf. That's the human condition — amnesia about your own story. And Orunmila is the librarian. He knows where every book is. He was there when you wrote it. If you go to him — through an Ifá priest, through the divination chain, through the palm nuts — he can pull your book off the shelf and read you passages from it. Not to tell you what will happen (your book has multiple endings), but to remind you what choices you made before you forgot them, and what you need to do to align with your own deepest intentions. He is the ultimate therapist, the ultimate counselor — not because he judges, but because he remembers. And the Ifá system is his card catalog — 256 drawers, each containing hundreds of cards, each card a poem, a story, a prescription, a piece of the cosmic puzzle. It has survived the Middle Passage, colonialism, missionary condemnation, and the digital age. Because humans will always need someone who remembers.
