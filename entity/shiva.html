Who It Is

Few gods anywhere fuse annihilation and bliss, ascetic silence and householder warmth, terror and grace into a single blue-throated, trident-carrying figure. Shiva — 'the Auspicious One', Mahādeva, the first yogi of Kailash — is the destroyer who dances the world apart in a ring of cosmic fire and the meditative hermit who taught the oneness of the Self and the Absolute without a word. His third eye burned the god of desire to cinders; his throat drank the poison churned from the cosmic ocean so that nothing else would die; his mother-and-consort may be the same Goddess in half of his body. He is worshipped as a mute pillar of stone (the lingam), yet is called 'god of cool things' and a bona fide rock hero in the same century that a statue of his cosmic dance was unveiled at CERN to celebrate particle physics. Powers
Cosmic destruction and dissolution (samhara / Tandava)

Shiva is 'The Destroyer' of the Trimurti, who dissolves the universe at the end of each cosmic cycle. He performs this via the Tandava dance (Kala-Mahakala), the powerful, masculine cosmic dance of destruction, versus the Lasya, the gentle feminine dance of creation credited to Parvati. As Nataraja, the majestic dancing form within a ring of cosmic fire tramples the dwarf-demon Apasmara (personified spiritual ignorance).

Limit: Destruction is cyclical and paired with re-creation; he 'destroys in order to create, tearing down to build again'. The dance's symbolism is theological rather than a literal omnipotent display in some readings (Padma Kaimal questions the assumed meaning; see contradictions).

Third eye (Tryambakam)

The third eye on Shiva's forehead turns whatever it gazes upon to ashes. It is most famously used to burn the love-god Kama to ash when Kama tried to interrupt his meditation (see stories). The name Tryambakam occurs in many scriptural sources.

Limit: Generally used with restraint; Shiva is often in deep meditation (the eye 'closed' to the world). When incited (e.g. by Kama, or Andhaka's darkness), the glance is annihilating.

Immortality and mastery of time (Kāla / Mahākāla)

As Kāla ('Time') and Mahākāla ('Great Time') Shiva is the being who ultimately destroys all things and is beyond time. He repeatedly resurrects and restores others (e.g. resurrecting Daksha with a goat's head, restoring bitten limbs, reviving the gods' fortunes).

Limit: Restorations are usually conditional on atonement/devotion; the cosmic order (dharma) is preserved rather than arbitrarily overturned.

Third force of transformation — churning-ocean blessings

During the churning of the cosmic ocean (Samudra Manthana), Shiva drank the lethal Halahala poison to save the universe, so that Parvati squeezed his neck, turning it blue (Nīlakaṇtha). He thus embodies the power to neutralize and absorb destructive forces for the sake of cosmic welfare.

Limit: The poison is contained in his stomach/neck; the act was churned from the ocean, illustrating his role within a larger collaborative cosmic process (involving Vishnu, the Devas, etc.).

Ascetic powers — tapas, yoga and the arts (Adiyogi / Mahāyogi / Dakshinamurti)

Shiva is the first yogi (Adiyogi), patron of yoga and meditation, whose tapas (ascetic heat) sustains and imperils the cosmos. As Dakshinamurti he is the silent teacher who transmits the oneness of atman and Brahman; as Nataraja he is lord of dance and dramatic arts. He is credited as the origin of the Śiva Sutras and the inspiration of the Shiva Samhita and of Abhinavagupta's Kashmir Shaiva philosophy (10th c.).

Limit: His ascetic 'heat' is so potent that even the gods fear it — jealous gods sent a prostitute to tempt him in one early Christian-era-era myth (see dark history).

Fee-fierce martial power — slaying demons (Andhaka, Tripurasura, Gajasura)

Shiva is a relentless demon-slayer. He vanquished Andhaka (impaling him on the trident, generating the Matrikas/two blood-consuming beings), destroyed the three cities of the Asuras (Tripurasura — with an axe and deer in his upper hands, bow and arrow in the lower), flayed Gajasura (the elephant demon), and produced Virabhadra from a lock of hair to destroy Daksha's sacrifice.

Limit: Each victory is mythically conditional (boons, penance, cleverness); Andhaka is only killable because he unwittingly lusted after his own mother (see stories).

Aniconic manifestation as the lingam

Shiva is worshipped primarily in aniconic/abstract form as the lingam (a cylindrical pillar set in a yoni platform) — 'outward symbol of the formless reality'. The Linga Purana states 'Shiva is signless, without colour, taste, smell…' The lingam-yoni symbolizes the union of Purusha (pure consciousness) and Prakriti (primordial matter), male and female principles of creation and regeneration.

Limit: The lingam's meaning is split between a literal phallic emblem and a purely abstract spiritual 'sign' — a live scholarly and cultural controversy (see contradictions and dark history on Orientalism).

Divine arts patron and giver of boons (Varaprada)

Shiva is the archetypal grants-of-boons deity. He gave Hiranyaksha a son (Andhaka), granted Brahma-favouring demons their wishes (with caveats: Brahmā told Andhaka he could still be killed by Shiva), and is regularly petitioned for boons across the Puranas. He is also the source of the arts — credited in Bali as the god who created dance, and in Java/Cambodia as Nrittesvara.

Limit: Boons are granted within Brahmanic constraint — 'all that takes birth must die'; immortality is never absolute.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Shiva is depicted with a strikingly ambivalent iconography. He is a meditating ascetic yogi on Mount Kailash, and simultaneously a householder with wife and children. Core attributes: a third eye on the forehead (Tryambakam) with which he burned the god of desire Kama to ashes; a crescent moon on his head (epithet Candraśekhara, 'having the moon as his crest'); his body smeared with ashes (bhasma/vibhuti, a reminder of the impermanence of materia

Symbols — The trishula (trident) symbolizes his three aspects of creator/preserver/destroyer or the three guṇas (sattva, rajas, tamas). The damaru drum symbolizes the rhythm of creation and time (used in Nataraja's ḍamaru-hasta mudra). The third eye symbolizes higher knowledge (jnana). The ash (vibhuti) marks impermanence. The lingam (aniconic cylindrical emblem, often within a yoni/Shakti platform) is his primary object of worship in Shaivite temples

Domains — Dwelling on Mount Kailash (Tibet/Himalayas), considered the axis of the universe; Varanasi/Kashi (India) is 'the city specially loved by Shiva', one of the holiest pilgrimage sites. He is worshipped as Kāla ('Time') and Mahākāla ('Great Time') who ultimately destroys all things, and as the god of cremation grounds (linked to death, dissolution and liberation). Accounts I
The churning of the ocean and the drinking of poisonPuranic (Mahabharata and Puranas recounting Samudra Manthana)

Epic/Puranic (c. last centuries BCE-1st millennium CE)

The Devas and Asuras churn the cosmic ocean with Mount Mandara as the churning-rod and Vasuki the serpent as the rope. They obtain a series of treasures, but first the lethal Halahala poison emerges, threatening to destroy all creation. Shiva drinks the poison to save the universe; Parvati grips his neck to prevent it spreading, and it stains his throat blue — giving him the name Nīlakaṇtha, 'blue-throat'. He also takes the serpent Vasuki as a garland/ornament. Explains Shiva's blue throat and his serpent-garland; establishes his role as self-sacrificing cosmic protector.

The burning of KamaPuranic (Kumarasambhava of Kalidasa is the classical literary adaptation)

Classical (c. 4th-5th century, Kalidasa); Puranic

To persuade the ascetic Shiva to father a son capable of destroying the demon Taraka, the gods send Kama, the god of desire, to shoot him with a love-arrow during his meditation. Shiva's third eye opens and burns Kama to ashes — earning Shiva the name 'Andhakari'/'Kamaripu' (enemy of Kama) and leaving Kama bodiless until his wife Rati petitions for his restoration. From the union/ascetic contact Shiva eventually marries Parvati, and their son Kartikeya (Skanda) is born. Sets up Shiva as both destroyer of desire and ultimately a father-figure; foundational for the Skanda/Kartikeya birth story and the marriage to Parvati.

The Daksha Yajna and the self-immolation of SatiLinga Purana, Shiva Purana, Vayu Purana and other Puranas; classical retellings

Puranic (c. 3rd-10th century CE)

Daksha, a Prajapati and father of Shiva's first wife Sati, holds a great fire-sacrifice (yajna) and deliberately refuses to invite Shiva and Sati. Sati, against Shiva's advice, attends alone, is insulted and snubbed by her father, and immolates herself in the sacrificial fire rather than bear the dishonour. In Shiva's rage he plucks a lock of his hair and dashes it to the ground, producing Virabhadra and Bhadrakali, who march with the Gaṇas to the sacrifice, devastate it, and behead Daksha (and mutilate several gods: Vahni's hands cut, Bhaga's eyes plucked, Pusha's teeth broken, Yama's mace broken, the goddesses' noses cut, and the priest Bhrigu's beard torn out). Shiva is eventually pacified, forgives Daksha, and resurrects him with the head of a goat; the sacrifice is completed with Vishnu as chief priest giving a share of oblations to Shiva. Replaces Sati with Parvati as Shiva's consort; underpins the later stories of Ganesha and Kartikeya; central to multiple sectarian identities in Hinduism.

Accounts II
Andhaka: the blind demon-king born of Shiva and ParvatiShiva Purana, Kurma Purana, Linga Purana, Matsya Purana, Vamana Purana, Harivamsha; Ramayana and Mahabharata mention

Puranic (c. 3rd-10th century CE)

In the Shiva Purana, when Parvati covered Shiva's eyes in play, the universe plunged into darkness, and sweat from her hands touching Shiva's third eye fell to the ground and produced a blind boy, whom Shiva called their child (Andhaka, 'he who darkens'). Shiva gave him to the demon king Hiranyaksha, who sought a son. After Hiranyaksha's death, a boon from Brahma made Andhaka nearly immortal and gave him divine vision. Realizing he lacked a matching consort, he was told the world's most beautiful woman was Parvati, wife of the matted ascetic on Kailash. Andhaka demanded her, attacked Shiva, and waged war (lasting variously seven hundred years). Whenever his blood touched the ground, a duplicate demon spawned; the goddesses (Matrikas/Chandi, and later the boy ManGala and girl Carcika created from Shiva's sweat) drank the blood to stop the proliferation. Shiva finally impaled Andhaka on his trident. Andhaka realized he had lusted after his own mother (Parvati) and was her husband's (Shiva's) son, repented, and was forgiven and made a chief of the Gaṇas. Demonstrates Shiva's destructive

The destruction of the three cities (Tripura) and the TripurasuraShiva Purana; Kartik Purnima festival commemorates it

Puranic

The three demon-cities of the Asuras (Tripura) — moving celestial fortresses of gold, silver and iron — which had become a menace, were destroyed by Shiva. As Tripurantaka ('ender of Tripura') he is depicted four-armed, holding an axe and a deer in the upper pair and a bow and arrow in the lower, and he slew the demon Tripurasura. The victory is commemorated at the festival Kartik Purnima, when Shiva temples are illuminated through the night. Canonical 'destroyer of evil' story; basis of the Kartik Purnima celebration.

Bhairava: the beheading of Brahma's fifth headShiva Purana; Kashmir Shaivism (Trika); Vajrayana Buddhism (Vajrabhairava/Yamantaka)

Puranic; Tantric (c. 8th-14th century)

To settle a dispute over superiority, Shiva manifested as a pillar of light. Brahma dishonestly claimed he had found the pillar's upper end, so Shiva produced Bhairava (from between his brows, per one version) and directed him to punish Brahma. Bhairava decapitated the fifth head of Brahma for the falsehood, thus committing brahmahatya (the sin of killing a Brahmin). The giant skull stuck to his hand, and he was pursued by the personified feminine sin until he reached Kashi (Varanasi), where the skull fell away and he was freed. In Kashmir Shaivism, Bhairava is the Supreme Reality (Para Brahman); in Vajrayana Buddhism, Vajrabhairava/Yamantaka is a fierce emanation defeating the 'Lord of Death'. Establishes Bhairava as the punishing/guardian form of Shiva and a key deity at the Shakta pithas and Jyotirlingas; the sin-and-expiation narrative informs temple guardianship.

Ardhanarishvara and the marriage to Parvati (Kalyanasundara)Puranic and iconographic tradition; Thiruvathira/Kalyanasundara murti; Chithirai festival

Puranic to classical

Shiva and Parvati are iconographically shown as Ardhanarishvara, a single being half-male and half-female, symbolizing the unity of Purusha and Prakriti and of Shiva and Shakti as inseparable. Their divine marriage is depicted in the Kalyanasundara-murti, showing Shiva performing the panigrahana (hand-acceptance) wedding ritual with Parvati; the richly attended form includes Vishnu and Lakshmi standing as Parvati's parents and Brahma as the officiating priest. The festival Thiruvathira (Kerala) celebrates the day Parvati, after long penance, won Shiva as her husband; the Chithirai festival (Madurai) celebrates the wedding of Minakshi (a form of Parvati) and Shiva, where the Vaishnava and Shaiva communities both join. Central to Shaiva-Shakta complementarity; liturgical basis of Shiva-Parvati joint worship and several festivals.

Origins: Vedic Rudra vs. non-Vedic/Indus 'proto-Shiva'Shiva is essentially the Rigvedic storm-god Rudra, and the two are the same deity (identifications in Hindu scripture; Saiva-Smarta devotional texts).Shiva emerged from non-Vedic / Indus-Valley / indigenous (possibly Dravidian, Munda or tribal) cults that were later Sanskritized ('Sanskritization') into a composite deity identified with Rudra (Vijay Nath; Flood's note that Rudra itself may be non-Vedic).
The lingam: sexual phallus vs. abstract spiritual 'sign'The lingam is a phallic symbol, literally Shiva's sexual organ (Wendy Doniger; Rohit Dasgupta; the Gudimallam lingam, 3rd c. BCE, is unambiguously phallic).The lingam is an abstract 'sign' (liṅga = mark/evidence) symbolizing formless Shiva and the union of Purusha/Prakriti; any sexual reading is a Western misreading (Kramrisch, Winternitz, Sivananda Saraswati, Wayman, Vivekananda). Tamil Shaivites call it kuri ('sign, mark', asexual); Lingayats deny any sexual connotation.
Nataraja's origin and symbolismNataraja is a Chola-period innovation (classic bronzes ~10th c.).Dancing-Shiva/Nataraja iconography and its cosmic symbolism predate the Cholas, being a Pallava (and possibly earlier) innovation (Sharada Srinivasan), and may have meant different things to different medieval audiences (Padma Kaimal, e.g. lord of cremation or a Chola dynastic emblem).
Shvetashvatara Upanishad: monotheistic Shiva-theism or pluralism/henotheism?The Shvetashvatara presents Rudra-Shiva as the one supreme creator (theistic Shaivite monism).The text presents pluralism, pantheism or henotheism rather than exclusive Shiva-theism (Robert Hume; Doris Srinivasan).
Who killed Andhaka, and was he Shiva's son?Andhaka was slain by Kali, or by Shiva's third eye (Ramayana says third eye; Mahabharata credits Kali).Andhaka was Shiva's (and Parvati's) biological-genesis son from their physical contact, was impaled/kept-alive on Shiva's trident, repented and was redeemed as a Gaṇa chief (Shiva/Kurma/Linga/Matsya/Vamana Puranas).
The god's 'avatars' — are Shiva avatars (ansh) real or a minority view?Shiva manifests in divine avatars/ansh (portions) — e.g. Hanuman as 'eleventh avatar' (Hanuman Chalisa), sage Durvasa, Adi Shankara as an incarnation (Vaishnava and medieval claims); Linga Purana lists 28 forms.Shiva avatar doctrine is relatively rare and not universally accepted in Shaivism, unlike Vishnu's avatar system.
Rudra-Shiva and cross-cultural identifications (Dionysus, Indra, Agni)Shiva shows an Indo-European/Aryan lineage and parallels the Greek Dionysus ('Indian Dionysus'), and absorbed Indra's and Agni's traits.Shiva emerged from indigenous non-Aryan tribal or Indus origins, and cross-cultural parallels (bull, snake, dance) are coincidental or diffusion-based rather than genealogical.

Shiva is 'just the destroyer' / a purely destructive god. — He is the creator-preserver-destroyer-transformer (pañcakṛtya — five activities incl. concealing and revealing grace), simultaneously the ascetic yogi, the householder-father, the teacher, and the giver of boons. In Shaivism he is the Supreme Being who creates, protects and transforms. 'Destroyer' within the Trimurti is one role, not his whole identity.

The 'sati' of Shiva's wife is the same as the banned widow-burning practice. — The goddess Sati's self-immolation in her father's sacrificial fire is a Puranic mythic act of dishonour-response. The real-world practice of 'widow immolation' shares an English name ('sati') but is a separate, historically documented and since-banned practice. Conflating them misreads both the myth and the social controversy.

The Shiva lingam is 'only' a phallic sex symbol. — The lingam is the primary aniconic emblem of formless Shiva as 'sign/evidence' (liṅga = mark), and lingam-yoni symbolize the union of Purusha and Prakriti. While some early (Gudimallam) and esoteric (Kaula/tantric) forms are explicitly phallic, and Victorian/Orientalist literatures sexualized it, Shaivites largely treat it as the abstract symbol of the formless Reality — eminently non-sexual for most worshippers.

Shiva has always been the single supreme god of the whole of Hinduism. — Shiva is supreme within the Shaivite tradition (one of four major sects, with Vaishnavism, Shaktism, Smartism). Other traditions revere Vishnu or the Goddess as supreme; Smartism treats the five panchayatana deities as equivalent icons of one Brahman. His all-Hindu supremacy is sect-specific, not universal.

The Indus 'Pashupati seal' proves Shiva was worshipped at Mohenjo-daro. — The seal's identification as a yogic proto-Shiva (Marshall) is speculative and widely doubted (Flood, Keay, Srinivasan, Possehl, Parpola). It may depict a seated bull, a buffalo, or a ritual figure. No deciphered Indus text confirms Shiva. Shiva's earliest certain attestation is the Vedic Rudra.

Departure of the 'Indian Dionysus'/Indra/Agni parallels prove Shiva is an imported Aryan god. — The Greek 'Indian Dionysus' epithet, Doniger's Indra-inheritance thesis, and the Agni-Rudra link are scholarly hypotheses of traits or parallels. They do not settle Shiva's origins, and competing indigenous-substrate theories exist. Asserting a definitive Aryan import overstates the evidence.

Record of Ragnarok's and other foreign depictions are faithful retellings of Hindu Shiva. — Foreign pop-culture Shivas (Record of Ragnarok's fighter, Western 'Indian Dionysus' framing, The Destroyer's Remo-as-Shiva-avatar) are creative, often anachronistic appropriations bearing little relation to Shaivite theology, and have caused offense/bans in India. They are adaptations, not scripture.
