Form

In Prudentius's Psychomachia, Avaritia is described as a woman with a withered, pale appearance, clutching her treasure. In Dante's Purgatorio, the avaricious are depicted bound face-down on the ground, their hands tied behind them, weeping into the dust. Medieval art often depicts Avaritia as a gaunt woman with a moneybag, sometimes with a toad or a wolf at her feet — symbols of coldness and insatiable hunger. In some Renaissance allegories (e.g., Giotto's Scrovegni Chapel frescoes), Avaritia appears as a hag with a purse hanging from her neck, her mouth twisted in a grimace.

Powers

Insatiable Hunger Avaritia can never be satisfied. The more one acquires, the more one desires. This is not a supernatural power but a psychological and spiritual trap — the vice perpetuates itself. Gregory the Great, Moralia in Job; Aquinas, Summa Theologica Self-defeating; leads to spiritual emptiness and social isolation. Some traditions describe avarice as a sin of the elderly (loss of vitality), others as a sin of the ambitious young.

Corruption of Virtue Avaritia corrupts other virtues — justice becomes self-interest, charity becomes calculation, faith becomes superstition about wealth. Prudentius, Psychomachia; Dante, Purgatorio Cannot touch the truly virtuous; in the Psychomachia, Avaritia is defeated by Good Works. None attested.

Deceptive Allure In Dante's dream, Avaritia appears as a beautiful siren before revealing her true foul nature. The vice seduces before it destroys. Dante, Purgatorio, Canto XIX The deception is temporary; the truth always reveals itself. In some traditions, avarice is depicted as openly ugly (Prudentius), in others as seductive (Dante).

Behavior & Habitat

Avaritia personifies the insatiable desire for wealth and possessions. Unlike simple greed for survival, Avaritia represents accumulation for its own sake — hoarding, miserliness, the refusal to share or spend. In allegorical literature, she is depicted as blind to the suffering of others, cold, calculating, and ultimately self-destructive. She whispers promises of security and happiness but delivers only isolation and spiritual emptiness.

Accounts

In this allegorical epic, Avaritia (Greed) rides into battle against the Virtues. She attempts to corrupt the soul with promises of wealth and security. She is defeated by Good Works (Operatio), who strikes her down. The poem established the template for medieval allegorical battles between virtues and vices.

Dante dreams of a stammering, ugly woman who transforms into a beautiful siren, singing a seductive song. A 'holy lady' (representing divine grace) exposes her, tearing her clothes to reveal a foul belly. The dream is an allegory of the avaricious soul, seduced by false promises of material fulfillment. In the third terrace of Purgatory, the avaricious lie face-down, bound, weeping into the earth — unable to look up at heaven because their eyes were fixed on earthly treasure.

Jesus tells of a rich man whose land yielded abundantly. He decides to tear down his barns and build larger ones, saying to his soul, 'You have plenty of goods laid up for many years; relax, eat, drink, be merry.' But God says to him, 'Fool! This night your soul is required of you.' The parable condemns the folly of hoarding wealth rather than being 'rich toward God.'

Gehazi, servant of the prophet Elisha, secretly runs after Naaman the Syrian to extort silver and garments from him after Naaman was healed of leprosy. Elisha confronts him: 'The leprosy of Naaman shall cling to you and to your descendants forever.' Gehazi leaves Elisha's presence 'a leper, like snow.'

Ananias and Sapphira sell a piece of property and secretly keep part of the proceeds while claiming to give the full amount to the apostles. Peter confronts Ananias: 'You have lied to the Holy Spirit.' Ananias falls dead. Sapphira, unaware, repeats the lie and also falls dead. The story is a stark warning about greed and deception within the early church.

Content Notes

Avaritia has been associated with usury (lending money at interest) since ancient times. Medieval Christian theology condemned usury as a form of avarice, leading to the persecution of Jewish moneylenders and, later, the development of alternative financial systems. The charge of avarice was frequently weaponized against marginalized groups.

The association of Jews with usury in medieval Europe was a key driver of antisemitism. The accusation of avarice was a stereotype used to justify expropriation and violence.

Scholastic theology; medieval economic history

The concept of avarice has been used to critique colonial exploitation — the extraction of resources and labor from colonized peoples. In this context, Avaritia is not a personal vice but a systemic force driving imperial violence.

Modern scholarship has connected the traditional vice of avarice to structural economic injustice, including slavery, resource extraction, and global inequality.

Scholarly Debates
Is Avaritia a sin of the rich or the poor?
Avaritia is primarily a sin of the rich — those who hoard wealth. ⇌ Avaritia can afflict anyone, including the poor who hoard what little they have.
Evidence for A: Biblical parables (Rich Fool, Lazarus and the Rich Man) focus on the wealthy.
Evidence for B: Aquinas argues that avarice is an inordinate love of possessions, which can occur at any economic level.
Is Avaritia the root of all evil?
Yes — 1 Timothy 6:10 says 'the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil.' ⇌ No — Pride is the root of all sin, as argued by Gregory the Great and others.
Evidence for A: Biblical text; popular proverb.
Evidence for B: Gregory the Great's Moralia in Job; Aquinas's ordering of the sins.
Is Avaritia a demon or an abstraction?
Avaritia is a personified demon — a real spiritual entity that tempts humans. ⇌ Avaritia is an abstraction — a literary or psychological concept, not a literal being.
Evidence for A: Medieval demonology; some allegorical texts.
Evidence for B: Prudentius's Psychomachia is explicitly allegorical; modern scholarship reads Avaritia as a personification.

A way to hold it in your mind

Avaritia is not just a medieval concept — she is the patron saint of every pyramid scheme, every crypto crash, every 'too good to be true' investment. She is the reason we have 'buyer's remorse' and the reason we keep buying anyway. She is the voice that says 'just a little more' and never stops saying it. In a world of infinite scroll and endless consumption, Avaritia is the algorithm — always hungry, never satisfied, always suggesting 'one more thing.'
