Who It Is

She is the silent partner in every get-rich-quick scheme, the whisper behind every 'just one more deal.' Avaritia — greed personified — has haunted the Western imagination for nearly two millennia, from the battlefields of a Roman poet's soul to the trading floors of Wall Street. She is the vice that builds empires and then eats them from within. In Dante's vision, she is a beautiful siren whose song promises everything and whose belly is full of rot. In the modern world, she wears a suit and gives TED talks. She is the oldest con artist in history — and she never stops working.

Avaritia personifies the insatiable desire for wealth and possessions. Unlike simple greed for survival, Avaritia represents accumulation for its own sake — hoarding, miserliness, the refusal to share or spend. In allegorical literature, she is depicted as blind to the suffering of others, cold, calculating, and ultimately self-destructive. She whispers promises of security and happiness but delivers only isolation and spiritual emptiness.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Latin 'avaritia' — greed, avarice, rapacity. The root 'avarus' means greedy, itself derived from 'aveo' (to desire, to long for).

Pronunciation — /ˌævəˈrɪʃə/ (Latin: ah-wah-REE-tee-ah)

Aliases — Greed, Avarice, Cupidity

Ownership — Roman/Latin literary tradition; personified vice in Christian moral theology; universal concept across cultures

Powers
Insatiable Hunger

Avaritia can never be satisfied. The more one acquires, the more one desires. This is not a supernatural power but a psychological and spiritual trap — the vice perpetuates itself.

Limit: Self-defeating; leads to spiritual emptiness and social isolation.

Corruption of Virtue

Avaritia corrupts other virtues — justice becomes self-interest, charity becomes calculation, faith becomes superstition about wealth.

Limit: Cannot touch the truly virtuous; in the Psychomachia, Avaritia is defeated by Good Works.

Deceptive Allure

In Dante's dream, Avaritia appears as a beautiful siren before revealing her true foul nature. The vice seduces before it destroys.

Limit: The deception is temporary; the truth always reveals itself.

Form & Signs

In Dante's Purgatorio, the avaricious are depicted bound face-down on the ground, their hands tied behind them, weeping into the dust. Accounts I
The Battle of the Soul (Psychomachia)Prudentius, Psychomachia (c. 400 CE)

c. 400 CE

In this allegorical epic, Avaritia (Greed) rides into battle against the Virtues. She attempts to corrupt the soul with promises of wealth and security. She is defeated by Good Works (Operatio), who strikes her down. The poem established the template for medieval allegorical battles between virtues and vices. Established Avaritia as a stock character in Western allegory; influenced countless medieval morality plays.

Dante's Dream of the SirenDante Alighieri, Divine Comedy - Purgatorio, Canto XIX

c. 1310-1320 CE

Dante dreams of a stammering, ugly woman who transforms into a beautiful siren, singing a seductive song. A 'holy lady' (representing divine grace) exposes her, tearing her clothes to reveal a foul belly. The dream is an allegory of the avaricious soul, seduced by false promises of material fulfillment. In the third terrace of Purgatory, the avaricious lie face-down, bound, weeping into the earth — unable to look up at heaven because their eyes were fixed on earthly treasure. One of the most memorable passages in the Divine Comedy; influenced countless artistic depictions of avarice.

Accounts II
The Parable of the Rich FoolThe Gospel of Luke 12:16-21

c. 60-90 CE

Jesus tells of a rich man whose land yielded abundantly. He decides to tear down his barns and build larger ones, saying to his soul, 'You have plenty of goods laid up for many years; relax, eat, drink, be merry.' But God says to him, 'Fool! This night your soul is required of you.' The parable condemns the folly of hoarding wealth rather than being 'rich toward God.' Became a foundational text for Christian teaching on avarice.

The Destruction of GehaziThe Bible - 2 Kings 5:20-27

c. 6th century BCE (composition)

Gehazi, servant of the prophet Elisha, secretly runs after Naaman the Syrian to extort silver and garments from him after Naaman was healed of leprosy. Elisha confronts him: 'The leprosy of Naaman shall cling to you and to your descendants forever.' Gehazi leaves Elisha's presence 'a leper, like snow.' A cautionary tale about the consequences of greed and deception.

The Death of Ananias and SapphiraThe Bible - Acts 5:1-11

c. 60-90 CE

Ananias and Sapphira sell a piece of property and secretly keep part of the proceeds while claiming to give the full amount to the apostles. Peter confronts Ananias: 'You have lied to the Holy Spirit.' Ananias falls dead. Sapphira, unaware, repeats the lie and also falls dead. The story is a stark warning about greed and deception within the early church. Fear seized the whole church; a foundational story about the seriousness of hypocrisy and greed.

Is Avaritia a demon or an abstraction?Avaritia is a personified demon — a real spiritual entity that tempts humans.Avaritia is an abstraction — a literary or psychological concept, not a literal being.

Greed is the root of all evil. — The actual verse (1 Timothy 6:10) says 'the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil' — not greed per se, and not 'the' root but 'a' root. The popular proverb oversimplifies the biblical text.

Avaritia is the same as Mammon. — Mammon is a personified demon of wealth; Avaritia is the abstract vice. While related, they are distinct concepts.

The Seven Deadly Sins are a biblical list. — The list was formalized by Gregory the Great (c. 590 CE) and later by Thomas Aquinas. The Bible never presents a definitive list of seven capital sins.

Avaritia is purely a medieval concept. — The concept of greed as a moral failing predates Christianity — it appears in Greek philosophy, Roman satire, and Jewish wisdom literature.

The punishment for avarice in Dante's Inferno is being boiled in oil. — In Dante's Inferno, the avaricious and prodigal are in the Fourth Circle, pushing heavy weights against each other. The face-down punishment in the earth is in Purgatorio, for the purgation of avarice.
