The Approach

In the granaries of Manipur, a gleaming black stone rests upon a bed of rice, and as long as it shines, prosperity is believed to abide. This stone is Phouoibi, the Lady of the Paddy, a goddess of harvest and wealth whose love is as fleeting as the monsoon rains. Her story is one of passion, betrayal, and transformation, echoing the rhythms of a land where rice is life itself.

Etymology

Phouoibi (or Phouleima) derives from Meitei 'phou' (unhusked rice, paddy) and 'oibi', from the verb 'oiba' (to become) with feminine suffix 'i', thus 'Lady of the Paddy'.

Cultural Ownership

Meitei people of Manipur, India; associated with Ancient Kangleipak.

Disputed Identifications

Sometimes identified as a manifestation of Panthoibi or an incarnation of Leimarel Sidabi; not a member of the Umang Lais, as she is a spirit of rice.

Description

Appearance

Often depicted as a beautiful maiden in a glowing dress; also represented as a gleaming black stone kept in a pot inside the granary.

Symbols & Iconography

Round black stone in a pot; associated with paddy, grains, and harvest; appears in ancient potteries.

Behaviour & Personality

Fickle in love, inconstant in favor to mankind, reflecting the unpredictability of wealth and harvest; anger provoked by neglect of paddy or rice.

Domains & Habitats

Agriculture, crops, fertility, grains, harvest, paddy, rice, wealth; associated with granaries and fields.

Version Differences

Some versions describe her as having sisters, others as friends; in one version she stays at the yenakha (side of house) leading to the place name Phayeng; in another she transforms into a chicken to escape.

Stories & Myths

Phouoibi and Akongjamba

Source Moirang Saiyon legends; Phouoibi WaronCulture MeiteiPeriod Ancient

Summary

Phouoibi, journeying with her sisters/friends Ngaleima and Thumleima, meets Akongjamba at a river in Moirang; they fall in love. Later, she visits his home disguised as a tribal woman, but his mother treats her poorly. She flees to a henhouse and transforms into a chicken. The next day, she reveals herself and showers golden grain before leaving. Akongjamba follows, but she refuses to stay, saying they are not destined to be together. She drowns crossing a river, but is saved by a fish. She curses the deer that lied about the river's depth.

Fickleness and sexual relationships

Source Phouoibi Waron; general mythology

Description

Phouoibi is described as fickle in love, having relationships with many mortals and leaving them, symbolizing the transient nature of wealth.

Scholarly Context

This reflects the cultural understanding of wealth and harvest as unpredictable.

Curse on deer

Source Myth of Akongjamba

Description

Phouoibi cursed deer so that their teeth would fall out if they ate paddy.

Scholarly Context

An etiological tale explaining why deer avoid paddy.
