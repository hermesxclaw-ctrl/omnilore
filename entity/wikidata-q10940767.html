Form

No classical source describes Amanomichine's physical appearance. As an amatsukami of the heavenly descent block he is conventionally imagined in male, formal, heavenly-court kami form, but any image is a modern reconstruction. The honest record is that nothing survives describing his bodily iconography or attributes.

Powers

Ancestral-generative status (musubi-lineage trunk) As a named member of the Tenson-zoku and a descending root of the Takamimusubi/Kamimusubi producing pantheon, Amanomichine's defining 'power' is genealogical fecundity: he is positioned where heavenly descent turns into earthly clan legitimacy. In social-and-ritual terms this is the ability to lend a divine trunk to whichever priestly lineage successfully claims him. Shinsen Shōjiroku; Tenson-zoku grouping with Amenohoakari, Amatsuhikone, Ame no Hohi. Strictly a claimed, register-based attribute. It exists only if the genealogy is credited — Toshio Hoga's forgery thesis removes it entirely. Hoga argues the Amabe/Owari/Tsumori/Tanba heavenly descent is fabricated and the real descent is the sea deity Watatsumi; the equation of Hikosachi (= Taokihooi, Amanomichine's father lineage) with Suwa-related Taokihooi introduces a second, conflicting geographic thread.

Domination over 'the way/root' (name-domain) The name root 道根 ('michi-ne', 'root of the way/path') suggests a grounding, foundational function over roads, ways, or the moral/ritual 'Way'a meaning that, if earnestly pressed, would make him a keeper of pathways and foundations. However, no text attributes any deed to this and any such power is inferred from onomastics alone, not attested. Name etymology (reconstructed; no ancient gloss). Inferred, not attested. Modern guides that assign domain meanings to such names are interpretive. None in the traditions; the tension is only between etymology-inference and the total absence of narrative confirmation.

Bequeathing regional rulership to offspring His son Hikoma no Mikoto is recorded as the second-generation Kii no Kuni no Miyatsuko (provincial governor of Kii). This makes Amanomichine the point at which a heavenly kami becomes the ancestor of an actual territorial governor — a hereditary 'power' exercised through a human heir rather than through mythic action. Wikidata P40 (child Hikoma no Mikoto); Kii no Kuni no Miyatsuko records. The office of Kuni no Miyatsuko was appointed by the Yamato court and only later became hereditary, so 'through my offspring' is a retrospective genealogical construction. The kuni no miyatsuko system was abolished in the Taika Reforms (645) and its ritual authority carried on by the new shin-kokuzō — so any 'rule' by Hikoma is proto-period and reconstructed.

Accounts

The imperially commissioned Shinsen Shōjiroku, begun under Emperor Kanmu and completed under Saga, was meant to settle the ambiguous origins of the court clans. In its account of the Tenson-zoku — the 'descendants of kami' — it names four ancestor kami: Amenohoakari, Amatsuhikone, Ame no Hohi, and Amanomichine no Mikoto. Their descendants, the register states, came down from Takamagahara, the Plain of High Heaven, into the provinces of Owari and Tanba, and from them sprang the Owari, Tsumori, Amabe, and Tanba clans. Amanomichine here is not a protagonist of any drama; he is one of the load-bearing lists in a text whose whole purpose was political ancestry.

Amanomichine's paternal root runs through Hikosachi no Mikoto, whom one Suwa tradition equates with Taokihooi-no-Mikoto (手置帆負命), back to Te-Oki-Hoi-no-Kami (手置帆負神). The Nihon Shoki expressly names Te-Oki-Hoi as 紀伊國忌部遠祖, 'the distant ancestor of the Kii Province Inbe (忌部) clan.' The Kii Inbe were among the regional ritual clans that, per the Enki-Shiki and clan records, served the court — the Kii division notably delivering wood. The Kogo Shūi, the Inbe clan's own 807 apologetic written by Inbe no Hironari, cast the same descent toward the heavenly producing kami Kamimusubi, whom it records as ancestor of the Ki (紀) clan. Through this priestly genealogy, Amanomichine stands as the heavenly stem of the Inbe clergy who guarded the shrines of Kii.

After Empress Jingū's campaign against the Three Han (Korea), her returning boat could not make straight for Nanba and turned in at Muko no minato (Kobe). There the goddess Wakahirume delivered a divine message: 'I would like to stay in Ikuta Nagao no kuni.' Unagami no Isachi was thereupon ordered to enshrine her, founding what is today Ikuta Shrine in Kobe. Unagami no Isachi is recorded as one of the two patron-line ancestors of Hikosachi no Mikoto (Amanomichine's father), tying the heavenly-descendant chain to one of Japan's oldest shrines.

A local Suwa legend about the horse-breeding villain Yatsukao no Mikoto, known as Ganigawara (the 'Crab-river lord'), says that Ganigawara had contempt for Moriya's surrender to Takeminakata and harassed him until violence threatened the god's dwelling. Takeminakata retaliated and mortally wounded Ganigawara, who then begged forgiveness and entrusted his youngest daughter to Takeminakata. Takeminakata gave her in marriage to the god Taokihooi-no-Mikoto (手置帆負命), also called Hikosachi-no-Kami (彦狭知神), who had been injured by Ganigawara's men. This is the tradition that equates Amanomichine's father Hikosachi with Taokihooi and plants the parent deity in a very different, Suwa-centred ritual world.

Content Notes

The entire Tenson-zoku register, including Amanomichine's placement, is bound up in claims of heavenly descent that modern scholars treat as politically motivated constructions. Toshio Hoga argues specifically that the Amabe clan genealogy — the 82-generation scroll used to assert heavenly descent — is a forged document and that the clans actually descend from the sea deity Watatsumi. A kami whose entire identity is a line in a contested family tree therefore exists partly as a product of legitimating fiction.

Clan genealogy in Heian Japan was a vehicle for court standing, office, and ritual rights; the Nakatomi vs. Inbe feud (the context of the Kogo Shūi) shows how contested these registers were. Amanomichine is caught in that contest.

Amabe Clan Genealogy analysis (Toshio Hoga); Shinsen Shōjiroku; Amabe clan literature

The Kogo Shūi, the Inbe clan's own record, reverses the chronicles' emphasis to give the Inbe (and, through Kamimusubi-as-Ki-clan-ancestor, the Kii lineage) a primacy that the Nakatomi-centred official histories give instead to Ame no Koyane and the Nakatomi. Amanomichine's priestly descendants thus sit in a written feud over which house deserved the emperor's rituals.

The Inbe clan lost ground politically in the Nara/early-Heian period as the Fujiwara-backed Nakatomi rose; Hironari's text is a documented attempt to argue the injustice of that decline. Regional Inbe (Kii included, delivering wood) are separately attested and were subordinated to the central ritual clans.

Kogo Shūi (807); Inbe clan analysis

A lineage deity who survives only as a name in a register is extremely vulnerable to loss and to being overlooked. The full Shinsen Shōjiroku itself is lost — only an abridged tenth is said to survive — which means the fuller entry on Amanomichine is gone and his 'story' depends on a fragment plus later clan claims. This is a quiet form of erasure-by-elision common to subordinate genealogical kami.

The register was revised between 814 and 815 and the original lost by the Kamakura period; modern knowledge rests on the abridgement and later citations.

Shinsen Shōjiroku (abridged surviving text); Japanese genealogical scholarship

Heavenly-descent claims (the Tenson-zoku) were used to rank clans above grounded local origins and to justify provincial offices such as the Kii no Kuni no Miyatsuko, which the Yamato court appointed and later formalised. Amanomichine's family tree was, in effect, currency in a system that converted sacred ancestry into secular authority over a province — a kind of structural, non-violent domination of place by an arriviste nobility.

Kuni no Miyatsuko were appointed by and accountable to the Yamato court, became hereditary, and were absorbed into the post-645 gunji office — where, tellingly, they 'often sided with peasants against the ruling kokushi elite', a hint that such ancestry-backed local houses retained real, sometimes anti-central, weight.

Scholarly Debates
Kii/Owari/Tanba heavenly descent: fact or forgery?
The Owari, Tsumori, Amabe, and Tanba clans genuinely descend from the Tenson-zoku block that includes Amanomichine, giving them a heavenly (Takamagahara) pedigree. ⇌ Toshio Hoga argues the Amabe genealogy asserting this is a forged document; the clans really descend from the sea deity Watatsumi, whose great-grandson Takakuraji the Owari clan lists as ancestor (which Hoga reads as the original genealogy).
Evidence for A: The Shinsen Shōjiroku register and the Amabe-shi Keizu National Treasure scroll.
Evidence for B: Hoga's textual comparison of the Amabe genealogy against the Watatsumi-rooted Owari lineage.
Who fathered Amanomichine's line? Te-Oki-Hoi vs. Unagami no Isachi
The paternal root runs to Te-Oki-Hoi-no-Kami, the Nihon Shoki's 'Kii Inbe far ancestor', and to the artisan-roofing (craft) company of the heavenly court. ⇌ The paternal root alternatively runs to Unagami no Isachi, the sea-ward (海上) lineage figure and Ikuta Shrine founder, connecting the family to the Kii coast and Empress Jingū's Korea campaign.
Evidence for A: Nihon Shoki's 紀伊國忌部遠祖 identification; Wikidata P22 (Te-Oki-Hoi).
Evidence for B: Ikuta Shrine / Wakahirume tradition naming Unagami no Isachi as shrine founder; Wikidata P22 (Unagami no Isachi).
Is Hikosachi the same god as Taokihooi?
Hikosachi no Mikoto (Amanomichine's father) is a distinct deity with his own descent. ⇌ A Suwa-region legend expressly equates Hikosachi-no-Kami with Taokihooi-no-Mikoto, folding both into a single deity who married Ganigawara's daughter and was wounded by his men.
Evidence for A: The separate entry of Hikosachi in genealogical scaffolds beside the artisan Taokihooi.
Evidence for B: The Takeminakata-associated Suwa legend in which Taokihooi 'also known as Hikosachi-no-Kami' receives the bride.
The ritual primacy of the Inbe (and Kii line) vs. the Nakatomi
The Inbe clan (whose Kii branch is Amanomichine's priestly descent) held co-equal, heaven-ordered ritual primacy with the Nakatomi at the court. ⇌ The Nakatomi clan (later named Saibe, unrelated) was the superior ritual house, with the Inbe a subordinate regional group.

Evidence for B: The Nakatomi-dominated chronicles and the documented late-Nara/early-Heian decline of the central Inbe, who changed name
What We Don't Know

Amanomichine no Mikoto is an obscure genealogical kami, and the gaps far exceed the certain data. (1) No surviving primary text narrates him; he exists only via the abridged Shinsen Shōjiroku Tenson-zoku list and compiled descendant registers, and the full register is lost (only about a tenth survives per tradition). (2) No Japanese-language primary chronicle passage (Kojiki/Nihon Shoki) that names 'Amanomichine' as a standalone actor was located in this tick; his father-link to Hikosachi and sons Hikoma/Makiri rest on the Wikidata scaffold and secondary genealogical summaries, not on a direct primary citation. A Japanese-language source (e.g. the full Shinsen Shōjiroku edition or a Kii genealogy monograph) is needed to confirm the exact generation and the Kii no Kuni no Miyatsuko numbering — 'second generation' (紀伊国造2代) is recorded as a Wikidata description but the precise primary register behind it was not verified here. (3) No dedicated shrine claiming Amanomichine as its enshrined kami was identified in English-language sources; his worship is via lineage, and the specific shrine-cult, if any, is unverified. (4) The relationship-data (P1038 to Kamimusubi etc.) is broad and untyped in detail; the exact nature of these genealogical bonds is under-specified on Wikidata and could not be fully confirmed. (5) Modern reception is essentially nil — no film, game, anime, manga, or artwork reception was found, which is expected for this register-only figure and is honestly reported rather than filled in. (6) All research here was conducted through the English Wikipedia corpus and Wikidata (Firecrawl billing exhausted); no Japanese primary editions were directly consulted, so all renderings of 紀伊國忌部遠祖 and the Kii genealogies are per secondary English citation.

A way to hold it in your mind

There is a kami who never once speaks, fights, or appears in any surviving story, yet whole noble houses — the Kii priests who carried timber to the imperial court, the provincial governors of Kii, the shrine-tenants of the Amabe and Owari and Tanba clans — once built their legitimacy on the quiet fact that they could name him in their family trees. His name, Amanomichine, means roughly 'the heavenly root-of-the-way,' and that is precisely his role: a root laid down in the Plain of High Heaven from which a branching trunk of earthly clans claimed to have grown. He is the rare deity whom historians know not through myth but through genealogy at its most political — the kind of ancestor who appears exactly once, in the right line of a Heian register, and quietly decides which families got to claim heaven.
