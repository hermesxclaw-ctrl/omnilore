Who It Is

Somewhere in Sicily — the sources refuse to say which city — there once stood a temple to a goddess whose name literally means 'voracious eating'. The Greeks, who had altars and cults to fear, violence, and rivalry, apparently found room in the sacred landscape for Gluttony herself, and they placed her shrine beside an image of the grain-goddess Demeter, so that the deity who fills the belly stood shoulder to shoulder with the deity who fills the fields. Before that single pairing collapses the modern assumption that overeating was always a sin, it is worth asking what the ancient city actually feared and honoured when it gave appetite a house of its own.

Unattested. As a personified abstraction, Adephagia had no mythic deeds, no adventures, and no personality in any surviving narrative. She is a noun wearing a divine name — her 'character' is her meaning: unbridled food-appetite — but the ethical colouring of that meaning in antiquity is ambiguous. For a Greek, to eat beyond satiety was comedic vice when indulged by the low-born but was not yet the mortal sin that Christianity later made of it.

Powers
Personification of gluttony / abundant satiety

Adephagia's essential 'power' is lexical: she is the animating of the abstract noun meaning excessive eating. As a personified abstraction she embodies the appetite for food in its most voracious register. In the 'abundant satiety' reading (Wernicke), she powers not destructive greed but the fullness of the harvest — she makes the belly content.

Limit: She has no mythic agency: she performs no feats, overcomes no enemies, grants no named boons. Her 'power' is entirely her territorial meaning, and it is bounded by the fact that no cultic action toward her is actually attested beyond the existence of her temple and her proximity to Demeter Sito.

Sanctuary-holding divinity

Unlike most vice-personifications (who had altars and cult at most as scare-off rituals), Adephagia is recorded as having a temple (hieron) in Sicily. This is the anomalous, historically interesting feature: the Greeks rarely built temples to personified vices, and a temple to 'Gluttony' is striking.

Limit: The temple is asserted but its location, rites, festival, and priests are entirely unrecorded. We do not know who worshipped there, when it functioned, or what was done.

Satiety-granting / grain-blessing aspect

Because she is linked to Demeter Sito ('Demeter of the Grain'), Adephagia participates in the agrarian guarantee of full stomachs. She is thus, in her positive register, a minor abundance deity — the force that sees the harvest reach the table.

Limit: This is reconstruction, not report: no text says worshippers asked Adephagia for a good harvest. The link is inferred from her topos beside Sitō.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No reliable ancient description of Adephagia's physical form survives. No literary passage describes her in bodily terms, and no securely identified ancient iconography (vase painting, statuary, coinage) of her has been recognised. Any attempt to picture her — as a bloated or ravenous figure — is anachronistic invention projected back from the modern idea of gluttony, not attested ancient evidence. The only concrete visual datum is the adjacent s

Symbols — By implication only: grain, the harvest, and the Sicilian agrarian economy. Because she was tied to Demeter Sito, her semantic apparatus was agricultural (plenty, satiety, grain) rather than the moralising satire later attached to the word 'gluttony'. No symbol is directly attested on her person.

Domains — Her single attested domain is a sanctuary in Sicily (Sicily in the broad Magua Graecia sense; earlier scholarship sometimes localised the datum to a specific Syracusan context). Her sphere of influence is the raw appetite for food and the abundance that makes plenty possible.

Accounts I
The Sicilian sanctuary and the neighbouring grain-goddessPolemon fragment, preserved by Athenaeus (Deipn. 10.420e) and Claudius Aelianus (Varia Historia)

Origin of notice: Hellenistic (c. 2nd c. BCE); preservation: late 2nd–3rd c. CE

The entirety of Adephagia's 'myth' is a single topographical notice. In Sicily there stood a temple of the goddess Adephagia. Beside (or within the same sacred precinct as) that temple stood a statue of Demeter Sito, the grain-giving Demeter. The two deities were therefore co-resident: the goddess who embodies eating paired with the goddess who supplies the grain to eat. The pairing anchored Adephagia to the agrarian world and gave later readers the raw material to interpret her as an epithet of Demeter rather than an independent vice.

Accounts II
Athenaeus and the catalogue of gluttonsAthenaeus, Deipnosophistae, Book 10 (the long discourse on eating)

c. 200 CE

Athenaeus' fictional dinner guests, midway through their obsession with food, gather together the gods, heroes, peoples, and strange eaters connected with gluttony and comedic appetite. Adephagia is swept into this catalogue as the surname-goddess whose very name IS the vice the symposium is discussing. Her inclusion is the reason she survived at all: she was a curiosity in a scholarly anthology about eating. The Deipnosophistae made Adephagia into a recurring cultural reference point for later lexicographers, encyclopaedists, and the line of scholarship stretching to Bez and Whitehead.

Adephagia was a Greek goddess of the 'deadly sin' of gluttony, analogous to the Christian demon. — The Christian deadly sin is a much later moral-theological framework. In her own ancient context Adephagia paired with Demeter Sito and carried an agrarian, largely benign cast; the full 'mortal sin' colouring is a retrojection via the word 'gluttony'.

Adephagia had a rich mythology of fat-gobbling monsters and heroic eaters. — No myth at all survives for her. She is a personified abstraction known from a single temple notice and its surrounding lexical use. Any narrative is invented.

She is securely a separate goddess from Demeter. — The sources only say a statue of Demeter Sito stood near her temple. Whether she is independent or a Demeter-epithet is unresolved (Whitehead 2002).
