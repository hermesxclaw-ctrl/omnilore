The Approach

Sisyphus, the mortal king who chained Death itself and now rolls a boulder up a hill for eternity, is one of the most haunting figures in Greek mythology. His punishment is so vivid that it has become shorthand for futile labor, yet his story is far richer than a single image—a tale of cunning, hubris, and divine retribution that has inspired philosophers, writers, and artists for millennia.

Content advisory. The myth contains themes of deception, divine punishment, and eternal suffering. Some artistic depictions may include violence or nudity. No explicit sexual content or graphic violence is present in the core myth.
Identity

Sisyphus

Pronunciation Classical: /ˈsɪsɪfəs/ (English), /sí.sy.pʰos/ (Ancient Greek). Modern Greek: /ˈsi.si.fos/.

Known As

SisyphosSísyphosSisyphus of Ephyra

Etymology

The name Sisyphus (Greek: Σίσυφος, Sísyphos) is of uncertain etymology. Some ancient sources connected it to the Greek verb σισύειν (sisyein, 'to shake' or 'to move to and fro'), possibly alluding to his restless cunning or the eternal rolling of the boulder. Modern linguists have proposed a Pre-Greek (Pelasgian) origin, as the name does not have a clear Indo-European root.

Cultural Ownership

Greek mythology, specifically the Corinthian cycle. Sisyphus is a figure from ancient Greek religion and folklore, later adopted into Roman literature and then into global culture. He belongs to no single modern community but is a shared cultural symbol.

Disputed Identifications

Some ancient authors conflated Sisyphus with other cunning figures, such as Autolycus or Odysseus, but these are separate characters. Later allegorists interpreted Sisyphus as a symbol of human vanity or the futility of ambition, but this is an interpretive overlay, not a historical identity.

Description

Appearance

Sisyphus is typically depicted as a muscular, bearded man in Greek art, often shown straining to push a large round boulder up a hill. In underworld scenes he is sometimes nude, emphasizing his physical exertion. No ancient source gives a detailed physical description beyond his heroic build.

Symbols & Iconography

The boulder is his primary symbol, representing futile toil. In ancient art, he is often shown with a hill or mound, and sometimes with Hermes or other underworld figures. The boulder is usually depicted as a large round stone, not a cube or regular shape.

Behaviour & Personality

Sisyphus is characterized by extreme cunning, deceit, and audacity. He is a trickster figure who outwits gods, including Thanatos (Death) and Zeus. He is also portrayed as ambitious and prideful, willing to risk divine wrath for personal gain. His punishment reflects his character: he is forced to perform an endlessly futile task, a poetic irony for one who believed he could outsmart fate.

Domains & Habitats

In life, Sisyphus was king of Ephyra (later Corinth), a city he founded. He is associated with the region of Corinth and the Isthmus. In death, he resides in Tartarus, the deepest part of the underworld, where he endures his eternal punishment.

Version Differences

The core myth is consistent: Sisyphus is punished in the underworld to roll a boulder uphill, only to have it roll back down. However, the reasons for his punishment vary: (1) revealing Zeus's abduction of Aegina; (2) chaining Thanatos so that no one died; (3) escaping from Hades after being released by Persephone; (4) general deceitfulness and hubris. Some versions combine these. Additionally, a variant tradition makes Sisyphus the father of Odysseus, which is not universally accepted.

Powers & Abilities

Cunning and Deception

Source Version All major sources (Homer, Pindar, Apollodorus) attest to his cunning.

Description

Sisyphus was renowned for his craftiness and ability to deceive even gods. He tricked Thanatos into chains, thereby halting death for a time. He also deceived Hades and Persephone to secure his temporary release from the underworld.

Limitations

Despite his cleverness, he could not ultimately escape death or divine punishment.

Contradictions

No contradictions; his cunning is universally attested.

Physical Strength

Source Version Homer's Odyssey and later art depict him straining with the boulder.

Description

In his punishment, Sisyphus is depicted as able to push a massive boulder uphill, suggesting superhuman strength, though he is mortal.

Limitations

He cannot complete the task; the boulder always rolls back.

Contradictions

No explicit contradictions, but the boulder's size varies in art.

Founding and Kingship

Source Version Pherecydes, Apollodorus, and others.

Description

Sisyphus founded the city of Ephyra (Corinth) and established a dynasty. He was a mortal king with political power.

Limitations

Mortal kingship, subject to death.

Contradictions

None.

Stories & Myths

The Founding of Corinth

Source Pherecydes, ApollodorusCulture GreekPeriod Mythical, set in the Heroic Age

Summary

Sisyphus, son of Aeolus, founded the city of Ephyra, later known as Corinth. He became its first king and established a line that would include famous figures like Bellerophon.

Consequences

Variants

Some sources say he was a son of Poseidon, but the majority say Aeolus.

The Deception of Thanatos

Source Scholia on Homer, later mythographersCulture GreekPeriod Mythical

Summary

Sisyphus, angered by the death of his son or for other reasons, captured Thanatos (Death) and bound him, so that no mortal could die. This disrupted the natural order. Ares eventually freed Thanatos and delivered Sisyphus to him.

Participants

Sisyphus

Thanatos

Ares

Consequences

Sisyphus is punished for his audacity. This story explains why he is in the underworld.

Variants

Some versions say he tricked Thanatos with words, others with physical chains.

The Betrayal of Zeus

Source Apollodorus, Pindar, scholiaCulture GreekPeriod Mythical

Summary

Zeus abducted Aegina, daughter of the river god Asopus. Sisyphus, seeing this, revealed the abduction to Asopus in exchange for a spring on the Acrocorinth. Zeus was furious and punished Sisyphus in the underworld, though some say he was already destined for punishment.

Participants

Sisyphus

Zeus

Aegina

Asopus

Consequences

Sisyphus is punished with the eternal boulder rolling.

Variants

Some say the punishment was for this betrayal, others for general deceit.

The Escape from Hades

Source Scholia on Homer, HyginusCulture Greek and RomanPeriod Mythical

Summary

Before his final punishment, Sisyphus was allowed to return to the upper world on the condition that he return. He told his wife, Merope, not to give him proper burial rites. When he arrived in Hades, he complained to Persephone that his wife had not honored him, and he persuaded her to let him return to the world to punish her. Once back, he refused to return, living to old age. Eventually, he was forcibly brought back by Hermes or other gods.

Participants

Sisyphus

Merope

Persephone

Hades

Hermes

Consequences

He is punished more severely, perhaps with the boulder.

Variants

Some versions say he was allowed to return because he had chained Death, and once Death was freed, he was taken.

Type family/romanticSource Apollodorus, Hyginus

Sisyphus married Merope, one of the Pleiades, daughter of Atlas. They had several children, including Glaucus and Ornytion.

Dark History

Violence and murder

Source Scholia on Homer, later mythographers

Description

Some late sources (e.g., scholia) suggest Sisyphus was a cruel king who killed travelers and guests, possibly as a means of acquiring wealth or power. This is not in Homer or Pindar.

Scholarly Context

This may be an invention to justify his punishment, as the Greeks often attributed crimes to those punished in Tartarus.

Deception of gods

Source Various

Description

Sisyphus's repeated deception of gods, including Thanatos and Persephone, is a form of hubris that leads to his punishment.

Scholarly Context

Hubris against the gods was a serious crime in Greek religion.

Contradictions & Disputes

Reason for punishment

Position A

Sisyphus was punished for revealing Zeus's abduction of Aegina.

Position A — evidence

Apollodorus, Pindar

Position B

Sisyphus was punished for chaining Thanatos and causing a disruption in death.

Position B — evidence

Hyginus, scholia

Scholarly Consensus

Modern scholars often see the punishment as a combination of his general deceitfulness, with the Aegina betrayal being the most commonly cited reason in ancient sources.

Father of Odysseus

Position A

Sisyphus was the father of Odysseus by Anticleia.

Position A — evidence

Scholia on Homer, some ancient authors

Position B

Laertes was the father of Odysseus.

Position B — evidence

Homer, Odyssey

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars consider the Sisyphus paternity a later invention, as it conflicts with the Homeric account and is not widely supported.

Escape from Hades

Position A

Sisyphus escaped from Hades by tricking Persephone.

Position A — evidence

Scholia on Homer

Position B

Sisyphus was allowed to leave temporarily by Hades as a punishment for his wife.

Position B — evidence

Hyginus

Scholarly Consensus

The escape story is a later addition, and the details vary; the core is that he temporarily returned to the living.
