Who It Is

Sisyphus, the mortal king who chained Death itself and now rolls a boulder up a hill for eternity, is one of the most haunting figures in Greek mythology. His punishment is so vivid that it has become shorthand for futile labor, yet his story is far richer than a single image—a tale of cunning, hubris, and divine retribution that has inspired philosophers, writers, and artists for millennia.

Sisyphus is characterized by extreme cunning, deceit, and audacity. He is a trickster figure who outwits gods, including Thanatos (Death) and Zeus. He is also portrayed as ambitious and prideful, willing to risk divine wrath for personal gain. His punishment reflects his character: he is forced to perform an endlessly futile task, a poetic irony for one who believed he could outsmart fate.

Powers
Cunning and Deception

Sisyphus was renowned for his craftiness and ability to deceive even gods. He tricked Thanatos into chains, thereby halting death for a time. He also deceived Hades and Persephone to secure his temporary release from the underworld.

Limit: Despite his cleverness, he could not ultimately escape death or divine punishment.

Physical Strength

In his punishment, Sisyphus is depicted as able to push a massive boulder uphill, suggesting superhuman strength, though he is mortal.

Limit: He cannot complete the task; the boulder always rolls back.

Founding and Kingship

Sisyphus founded the city of Ephyra (Corinth) and established a dynasty. He was a mortal king with political power.

Limit: Mortal kingship, subject to death.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Sisyphus is typically depicted as a muscular, bearded man in Greek art, often shown straining to push a large round boulder up a hill. In underworld scenes he is sometimes nude, emphasizing his physical exertion. No ancient source gives a detailed physical description beyond his heroic build.

Symbols — The boulder is his primary symbol, representing futile toil. In ancient art, he is often shown with a hill or mound, and sometimes with Hermes or other underworld figures. The boulder is usually depicted as a large round stone, not a cube or regular shape.

Domains — In life, Sisyphus was king of Ephyra (later Corinth), a city he founded. He is associated with the region of Corinth and the Isthmus. In death, he resides in Tartarus, the deepest part of the underworld, where he endures his eternal punishment.

Accounts I
The Founding of CorinthPherecydes, Apollodorus

Mythical, set in the Heroic Age

Sisyphus, son of Aeolus, founded the city of Ephyra, later known as Corinth. He became its first king and established a line that would include famous figures like Bellerophon. The city of Corinth becomes a major Greek city-state with a rich mythology.

The Deception of ThanatosScholia on Homer, later mythographers

Mythical

Sisyphus, angered by the death of his son or for other reasons, captured Thanatos (Death) and bound him, so that no mortal could die. This disrupted the natural order. Ares eventually freed Thanatos and delivered Sisyphus to him. Sisyphus is punished for his audacity. This story explains why he is in the underworld.

Accounts II
The Betrayal of ZeusApollodorus, Pindar, scholia

Mythical

Zeus abducted Aegina, daughter of the river god Asopus. Sisyphus, seeing this, revealed the abduction to Asopus in exchange for a spring on the Acrocorinth. Zeus was furious and punished Sisyphus in the underworld, though some say he was already destined for punishment. Sisyphus is punished with the eternal boulder rolling.

The Escape from HadesScholia on Homer, Hyginus

Mythical

Before his final punishment, Sisyphus was allowed to return to the upper world on the condition that he return. He told his wife, Merope, not to give him proper burial rites. When he arrived in Hades, he complained to Persephone that his wife had not honored him, and he persuaded her to let him return to the world to punish her. Once back, he refused to return, living to old age. Eventually, he was forcibly brought back by Hermes or other gods. He is punished more severely, perhaps with the boulder.

Sisyphus was forced to roll a boulder uphill for eternity as a punishment for his crimes against humanity. — The punishment is specifically for his hubris against the gods, not for crimes against humanity, though some late sources add such crimes.

Sisyphus's boulder is a symbol of the futility of human existence. — In ancient Greek thought, the punishment is a poetic justice for his deception; the existential interpretation is a modern philosophical overlay.

Sisyphus was a king of Corinth. — He founded Ephyra, which later became Corinth, but he was not king of Corinth as it was known in historical times.
