Who It Is

A 13th-century Pisan nobleman, condemned for treason, locked with his sons and grandsons in a tower to starve. In Dante, he is frozen in the ice of Cocytus, eternally gnawing the skull of his betrayer, Archbishop Ruggieri. His narration of his children's deaths and his own cannibalism is among the most harrowing passages in literature.

Attested in Inferno (Canto XXXIII), Divine Comedy (1320)

Cultural Significance

The line 'Then hunger did what sorrow could not do' (Poscia, più che 'l dolor, poté 'l digiuno) is one of Dante's most debated — does it mean Ugolino starved, or that he ate his dead children? The ambiguity has fueled centuries of horrified fascination.
