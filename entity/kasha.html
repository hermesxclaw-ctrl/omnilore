The kasha is the reason Japanese funerals were guarded: a monstrous cat yokai — often two-tailed, wreathed in flames — that descends from storm clouds during funeral processions and burial rites to steal the corpse of a sinner, either dragging it to hell (its name means fire cart, borrowed from the flaming cart that carries the damned) or, in hungrier tellings, devouring it on the spot. Who It Is

The kasha is the reason Japanese funerals were guarded: a monstrous cat yokai — often two-tailed, wreathed in flames — that descends from storm clouds during funeral processions and burial rites to steal the corpse of a sinner, either dragging it to hell (its name means fire cart, borrowed from the flaming cart that carries the damned) or, in hungrier tellings, devouring it on the spot. Cats that lived long, grew large, or were left alone with the dead were suspected of graduating into kasha; a household cat was banished from the room where a body lay, and coffins in kasha country were weighted with stones or watched in shifts through the night. The kasha operates on a precise moral trigger — it takes only the corpses of the wicked — which made its appearance at a funeral a devastating public verdict: a sudden black squall over the procession, the coffin suddenly light, and the whole village now knowing exactly what kind of person was being buried. Temples developed countermeasures with the confidence of institutions that expected the problem: two-stage funerals, decoy coffins filled with stones sent out first, and priests whose specific prayers made corpses too heavy for cats to lift. In the kasha, Japan fused its two oldest funeral anxieties: the weather turning, and the cat that watched the body a little too attentively.

Origins

Buddhist hell-cart imagery fused with Japanese cat-suspicion (the nekomata tradition of long-lived cats turning yokai); codified in Edo collections and temple procedure.

Attested in Edo-period tales and temple loreEra Medieval Japan to present

Form

A cat grown huge and wrong, walking upright, twin-tailed, mantled in flame, riding down from black cloud onto the funeral path — or the family cat, by lamplight, staring at the coffin a beat too long.

Corpse theft / body-snatching The kasha whisks corpses out of coffins and graves, so that a coffin suddenly becomes light or is blown out of pallbearers' hands by a sudden gust. It targets funeral processions and gravesites across Japan. Cat-monster and cloud/Raijin registers, regional folklore and Edo kaidan Can be fended off by monks throwing a Buddha-rosary (juzu) at the coffin, chanting, or sealing it; by placing a stone, blade, reed-weave, hair-razor, or bamboo basket over/on the corpse or coffin; or by playing a myōhachi percussion instrument. Requires storm/cloud cover in the weather registers. Whether it only takes the villainous is contested — Inga monogatari has it carry off a woman while still alive, and later tales do not always target the wicked.

Storm-raising (thunder, wind, lightning) The kasha arrives amid dark clouds, thunderclaps, lightning, and a fearsome wind strong enough to lift a coffin into the sky out of the bearers' hands. The Raijin-form carries thunder-drums. Early-modern (16th-17th c.) ogre/Raijin register and many regional tales None attested beyond its dependence on stormy conditions for the coffin-lift. In the vehicular register the storm is incidental iconography attached to the hell-car; in the ogre register it is the kasha's own weather power.

Corpse-consumption / evisceration In several regional and classical accounts the kasha tears its stolen victim's body apart and leaves it hanging on a rock or tree (Bōsō Manroku), or eats the corpse (Tōno kyasha, Kagoshima kimotori analog, Miyazaki lore). Regional folklore; Bōsō Manroku (1833) None attested. Some registers only describe the body vanishing (possessed/removed), not eaten.

Possession / lethal contact A cat leaping over a corpse causes the dead to revive and walk away (Gunma, Miyazaki), or contact leaves victims 'possessed by the kasha'. The vehicular kasha could also spirit away living sinners while still alive (Inga monogatari carrying off the woman who 'went straight to hell while still alive'; Shin Chomonjū's man who cried 'a kasha is coming' and died of rotting lower body days later). Regional folklore; Inga monogatari (c.1558 / 1661); Shin Chomonjū (1749) The killing-contact effect overlaps heavily with cat-in-folklore taboos rather than a distinct kasha power. Whether the kasha acts directly or corrupts a cat to do its bidding varies by tale.

Shapeshifting / impersonation (limited) The Tōno (Iwate) kyasha appears as a human woman wearing a kinchaku money-bag tied to her front obi belt, mimicking ordinary human dress to approach funerals before stealing and eating corpses. Tōno, Iwate regional lore Attested only for the female kyasha variant, not the cat-form. Whether the kyasha is a kasha at all, or a separate yama-uba-like figure, is a local classification point.

Weaknesses & Wards

Decoy coffins, stone-weighted or rope-bound caskets, priestly weight-prayers, and vigilance: a watched corpse in a two-stage funeral defeated it, and the rites were designed accordingly.

Accounts

A sōzu-rank priest (bettō) of Yakushi-ji, upright and never embezzling temple property, is alarmed on his deathbed that the pick-up party from nirvana has not come — instead the fire cart from hell (kasha) has arrived. He asks the ogre pulling it why. The oni explains the priest had once borrowed five to of rice he never repaid. The priest orders his subordinates to chant one sho of rice worth of prayer (double the amount owed); the fire cart dismisses itself, and the paradise escort arrives instead. The priest rubs his palms in delight and dies.

A 30-year-old pet cat of an elder of the Jōdo-shū sect in Kyoto is overheard speaking human language at night, revealing itself as the chief bakeneko (changed-cat) of Kyoto and commander of the cats who transform into kasha to steal bodies when certain people die. It quarrels with the other cats that have decided to target a certain noblewoman nun who is due to die the next day — profligate and ill-considered, she is nevertheless the priest's patroness, and the cat cannot bear to betray its master. It reveals the cats' secret weakness: the Daruma juzu rosaries can deliver mortal wounds to the monsters. The next day, as the priest performs the indō last rite, storm clouds overshadow the area, thunderous rain falls, and lightning is about to strike the coffin — the priest strikes with his rosary and clears the weather. The pet cat returns three days later with its eye popped out from its wounds, is nursed, and eventually dies.

At a funeral procession at Ueda Manor, Echigo (Minamiuonuma, Niigata), a kasha assaulted the cortege, nearly succeeding in stealing the corpse, but a priest of the Untō-an temple manifested his esoteric powers and protected the remains, fainting in the process. The kasha arrived amidst heavy rain and thunder; the woodcut depicts it like the Raijin thunder-god, an ogre with a tiger's-pelt loincloth and thunder-drums. The later Hokuetsu Seppu retelling credits the priest as Hokkō Zenshuku (13th abbot, anachronistically) and recasts the thief as a twin-tailed cat nekomata: during a snowstorm funeral in Saburōmaru, Uonuma, a sudden gust blew dark clouds over the procession and a ball of fire enveloped the coffin, within which a twin-tailed huge cat tried to steal it; Hokkō clubbed it on the head with an iron nyoi, driving it away. His kesa vestment bloodied in the fight was thereafter called the 'kasha-otoshi no kesa' and kept at the temple.

In Yao, Kawachi Province (now Yao, Osaka), the wife of a shōya (village-chief samurai) is avaricious, fails to feed the servants properly, and is mean. A witness on the highway sees a cluster of light like a burning torch speeding closer; within it a pair of colossal samurai-like men eight shaku (2.4 m) tall grab the woman by both hands and carry her away somewhere unknown. The frightened witness goes to the shōya's house and learns the wife has fallen ill and bedridden, dying on the third day. It was said she went straight to hell while still alive due to her poor conduct. Related Katakana-bon tale: a priest of Mino who preached there was no Buddha and destroyed holy trees and images is seized by the kasha at death; in the hiragana redaction his hands and ankles are torn and hung over tree branches.

(a) At the funeral procession of a samurai's cousin, thunder rumbles and from a dark cloud covering the sky a kasha sticks out a bear-like foreleg with three dreadful nails 'covered by hair-like silver needles' to steal the corpse; the samurai Matsudaira Gozaemon severs the forepaw with a sword stroke. (b) The daimyo Ōmura Inaba-no-kami and his retinue, passing through Bizen bay, see a black cloud approach; a scream of 'Oh, woe' resounds and human feet stick out of the cloud. When the vassals drag the figure down it is the corpse of an old woman — a terribly stingy hag detested by all, who was snatched by a black cloud while going to the bathroom. The narrator calls this the work of the devil people call kasha.

Along the mountain pass between Ayaori (Kamihei District) and Miyamori in Tōno stands Kasa-nokayō-yama, where a woman wearing a kinchaku money-bag ties to her front obi appears; she steals corpses from coffins at funerals and digs up and eats bodies from graveyards. The same 'kyasha' is reported at Minamimimaki (Saku, Nagano). In Yamagata Prefecture a kasha-neko cat appeared before a wealthy dead man to steal his corpse but was driven off by the head priest of Seigen-ji (Hasedō); its alleged tail was offered as a charm against evil spirits and kept (displayed at new year) at the Hase-kannon-dō pavilion at Nan'yō.

Field Notes

⍟ Temple legends record priests whose prayer made the coffin immovable, the kasha departing in visible fury.

Cultural Significance

The kasha shaped actual Japanese funeral procedure — corpse-watching, cat-banishment, decoy rites — and survives in idiom: kasha still names a hag or a hellish workload's cart in Japanese expressions.

A way to hold it in your mind

The kasha is the rare yōkai that literally changed species over a few centuries. It began not as a monster at all but as a cart — a blazing, ox-headed funeral carriage that Buddhist hell-scrolls cranked down the road to ferry the guilty to damnation. Then, roughly around the Genroku era, it swapped its wheels for claws: the fire-cart to hell became a two-tailed cat that waits beside your coffin, and by 1776 an artist had drawn it staring out of a flame. To understand the kasha you must hold two images at once — a burning chariot and a hungry cat — and accept that the first is not a metaphor for the second but its ancestor. No yōkai better dramatizes how a culture's dread migrates, generation by generation, from the theology of the afterlife to the terror sitting in your own front room licking lamp oil.
