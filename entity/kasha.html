Who It Is

The kasha is the rare yōkai that literally changed species over a few centuries. It began not as a monster at all but as a cart — a blazing, ox-headed funeral carriage that Buddhist hell-scrolls cranked down the road to ferry the guilty to damnation. Then, roughly around the Genroku era, it swapped its wheels for claws: the fire-cart to hell became a two-tailed cat that waits beside your coffin, and by 1776 an artist had drawn it staring out of a flame. To understand the kasha you must hold two images at once — a burning chariot and a hungry cat — and accept that the first is not a metaphor for the second but its ancestor. No yōkai better dramatizes how a culture's dread migrates, generation by generation, from the theology of the afterlife to the terror sitting in your own front room licking lamp oil.

The kasha is a thief of the dead, targeting corpses at funerals and graveyards. Pallbearers describe the coffin suddenly lightening or being blown skyward out of their hands when the kasha strikes; they explain this as the body having been 'possessed by the kasha'. It typically appears with a sudden storm and a fearsome wind. Despite a common modern gloss that it only takes the bodies of the wicked, the attested record is broader: in the Inga monogatari it carries off a mean-hearted woman while alive, and in Rōō sawa and Bōsō Manroku it devours the corpses of miserly or murderous

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name kasha (火車) literally means 'fire cart' or 'burning chariot' (kasha can also be read as 'fire vehicle'), with an alternate orthography 化車 glossed as 'changed wheel'. It derives directly from the Buddhist concept of the fiery cart that conveys sinful humans at death to hell. The obsolete read

Pronunciation — KAH-shah (Japanese: /kaɕa/). Obsolete reading kwasha. The Tōno variant is kyasha.

Aliases — kwasha (obsolete), hi no kuruma (colloquial, "fire cart"), kasha-neko (カシャ猫), kyasha (Tōno/Iwate & Saku/Nagano regional variant), kasha-baba (fire-cart hag, Harima), kusha (Himakajima, Aichi variant)

Ownership — Japanese — yōkai tradition, originating in mediaeval and early-modern Buddhist hell imagery (jigoku-zōshi hellscapes, rokudō-e) and evolving through Edo-period kaidan (ghost tales)

Powers
Corpse theft / body-snatching

The kasha whisks corpses out of coffins and graves, so that a coffin suddenly becomes light or is blown out of pallbearers' hands by a sudden gust. It targets funeral processions and gravesites across Japan.

Limit: Can be fended off by monks throwing a Buddha-rosary (juzu) at the coffin, chanting, or sealing it; by placing a stone, blade, reed-weave, hair-razor, or bamboo basket over/on the corpse or coffin; or by playing a myōhachi percussion instrument. Requires storm/cloud cover in the weather registers.

Storm-raising (thunder, wind, lightning)

The kasha arrives amid dark clouds, thunderclaps, lightning, and a fearsome wind strong enough to lift a coffin into the sky out of the bearers' hands. The Raijin-form carries thunder-drums.

Limit: None attested beyond its dependence on stormy conditions for the coffin-lift.

Corpse-consumption / evisceration

In several regional and classical accounts the kasha tears its stolen victim's body apart and leaves it hanging on a rock or tree (Bōsō Manroku), or eats the corpse (Tōno kyasha, Kagoshima kimotori analog, Miyazaki lore).

Limit: None attested.

Possession / lethal contact

A cat leaping over a corpse causes the dead to revive and walk away (Gunma, Miyazaki), or contact leaves victims 'possessed by the kasha'. The vehicular kasha could also spirit away living sinners while still alive (Inga monogatari carrying off the woman who 'went straight to hell while still alive'; Shin Chomonjū's man who cried 'a kasha is coming' and died of rotting lower body days later).

Limit: The killing-contact effect overlaps heavily with cat-in-folklore taboos rather than a distinct kasha power.

Shapeshifting / impersonation (limited)

The Tōno (Iwate) kyasha appears as a human woman wearing a kinchaku money-bag tied to her front obi belt, mimicking ordinary human dress to approach funerals before stealing and eating corpses.

Limit: Attested only for the female kyasha variant, not the cat-form.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Attested forms are stratified by period. (a) Vehicle: a glowing fire cart (hi no kuruma) spouting flames, drawn by the hell-wardens gokusotsu or by Gozu/Mezu (Ox-Head and Horse-Head), ferrying the sinner to hell. (b) Ogre/thunder-being: an oni-like or gokusotsu/Raijin figure wearing a tiger's-pelt loincloth and carrying thunder-drums, arriving inside a black storm-cloud — per the Kii zōdanshū/Kanwa kii illustration. (c) Cat-monster (canonical sin

Symbols — Fire and storm are the defining motifs: black clouds, thunderclaps, lightning, and strong winds attend the kasha in almost every register. The fire-cart itself, the tiger-pelt thunder drums of the Raijin-form, the ball of fire, and (in the cat form) the twin-tailed cat silhouette are the recurring iconography. In Buddhist art it appears in jigoku-zōshi hellscapes as a flaming chariot.

Domains — Funeral processions, coffins, cemeteries and graveyards across Japan — the 'ubiquitous' corpse-snatching yōkai of regional folklore. Classical sources fix episodes in Echigo (Ueda manor, now Minamiuonuma, Niigata), Kōzuke (Gunma), Mino (Gifu), Kawachi (Yao, Osaka), Bizen, Shimotsuke (Utsunomiya), Mutsu (Iwaki, Fukushima), and Musashi (Setagaya). Accounts I
The Yakushi-ji high priest and the fire cart (Uji Shūi)Uji Shūi Monogatari (c.1212-1221), Book 4, Tale 3; also Konjaku monogatarishū Book 15

Kamakura (tale), Heian setting

A sōzu-rank priest (bettō) of Yakushi-ji, upright and never embezzling temple property, is alarmed on his deathbed that the pick-up party from nirvana has not come — instead the fire cart from hell (kasha) has arrived. He asks the ogre pulling it why. The oni explains the priest had once borrowed five to of rice he never repaid. The priest orders his subordinates to chant one sho of rice worth of prayer (double the amount owed); the fire cart dismisses itself, and the paradise escort arrives instead. The priest rubs his palms in delight and dies. Establishes the fire-cart kasha as a morally-graded vehicle: it can be repelled by repentance and prayer, and its appearance is about the debtor's un-repaid moral/economic obligation, not mere evil.

The priest's pet cat, commander of the kasha-cats (Kōsekishū)Kōsekishū (礦石集, 1693), Book 1, tale 15

Early-Modern (Genroku era)

A 30-year-old pet cat of an elder of the Jōdo-shū sect in Kyoto is overheard speaking human language at night, revealing itself as the chief bakeneko (changed-cat) of Kyoto and commander of the cats who transform into kasha to steal bodies when certain people die. It quarrels with the other cats that have decided to target a certain noblewoman nun who is due to die the next day — profligate and ill-considered, she is nevertheless the priest's patroness, and the cat cannot bear to betray its master. It reveals the cats' secret weakness: the Daruma juzu rosaries can deliver mortal wounds to the monsters. The next day, as the priest performs the indō last rite, storm clouds overshadow the area, thunderous rain falls, and lightning is about to strike the coffin — the priest strikes with his rosary and clears the weather. The pet cat returns three days later with its eye popped out from its wounds, is nursed, and eventually dies. The Echigo priest fends off the kasha (Kii zōdanshū / Kanwa kii / Hokuetsu Seppu)Kii zōdanshū (1687) entry 'Concerning how in the manor of Ueda, Echigo, at the time of funeral, a lightning cloud comes and steals the corpse'; Kanwa kii; retold by Suzuki Bokushi in Hokuetsu Seppu (1841)

Supposed event Tenshō 2/1574

At a funeral procession at Ueda Manor, Echigo (Minamiuonuma, Niigata), a kasha assaulted the cortege, nearly succeeding in stealing the corpse, but a priest of the Untō-an temple manifested his esoteric powers and protected the remains, fainting in the process. The kasha arrived amidst heavy rain and thunder; the woodcut depicts it like the Raijin thunder-god, an ogre with a tiger's-pelt loincloth and thunder-drums. The later Hokuetsu Seppu retelling credits the priest as Hokkō Zenshuku (13th abbot, anachronistically) and recasts the thief as a twin-tailed cat nekomata: during a snowstorm funeral in Saburōmaru, Uonuma, a sudden gust blew dark clouds over the procession and a ball of fire enveloped the coffin, within which a twin-tailed huge cat tried to steal it; Hokkō clubbed it on the head with an iron nyoi, driving it away. His kesa vestment bloodied in the fight was thereafter called the 'kasha-otoshi no kesa' and kept at the temple. Shows the transition from vehicular/cloud kasha to cat-nekomata within a single evolving tale; a patron-saint legend of the defending priest.

Accounts II
The woman taken by the kasha while still alive (Inga monogatari)Suzuki Shōsan, Hiragana-bon Inga monogatari (c.1558), Book 4, Tale 5; Katakana-bon variant

Sengoku-early Edo

In Yao, Kawachi Province (now Yao, Osaka), the wife of a shōya (village-chief samurai) is avaricious, fails to feed the servants properly, and is mean. A witness on the highway sees a cluster of light like a burning torch speeding closer; within it a pair of colossal samurai-like men eight shaku (2.4 m) tall grab the woman by both hands and carry her away somewhere unknown. The frightened witness goes to the shōya's house and learns the wife has fallen ill and bedridden, dying on the third day. It was said she went straight to hell while still alive due to her poor conduct. Related Katakana-bon tale: a priest of Mino who preached there was no Buddha and destroyed holy trees and images is seized by the kasha at death; in the hiragana redaction his hands and ankles are torn and hung over tree branches. Fixes the kasha as an agent of karmic retribution that can seize the living, not just the dead, reinforcing the fire-cart's hell-ferry roots.

The Shin Chomonjū body-stealing ogre and the miserly hagShin Chomonjū (新著聞集, 1749), Part 10 'Strange Events' and Part 14 'Calamities'

Mid-Edo

(a) At the funeral procession of a samurai's cousin, thunder rumbles and from a dark cloud covering the sky a kasha sticks out a bear-like foreleg with three dreadful nails 'covered by hair-like silver needles' to steal the corpse; the samurai Matsudaira Gozaemon severs the forepaw with a sword stroke. (b) The daimyo Ōmura Inaba-no-kami and his retinue, passing through Bizen bay, see a black cloud approach; a scream of 'Oh, woe' resounds and human feet stick out of the cloud. When the vassals drag the figure down it is the corpse of an old woman — a terribly stingy hag detested by all, who was snatched by a black cloud while going to the bathroom. The narrator calls this the work of the devil people call kasha. Demonstrates the martial rebuttal (sword-cutting the monster's limb) and the moral frame (the miserly are kasha-doomed); the foreclaw becomes a relic theme echoed at Shōkō-in.

The Tōno kyasha woman and the corpse-eating funerary terrorsTōno (Iwate) and regional folklore; Yamagata kasha-neko tale

Modern-period collected folklore

Along the mountain pass between Ayaori (Kamihei District) and Miyamori in Tōno stands Kasa-nokayō-yama, where a woman wearing a kinchaku money-bag ties to her front obi appears; she steals corpses from coffins at funerals and digs up and eats bodies from graveyards. The same 'kyasha' is reported at Minamimimaki (Saku, Nagano). In Yamagata Prefecture a kasha-neko cat appeared before a wealthy dead man to steal his corpse but was driven off by the head priest of Seigen-ji (Hasedō); its alleged tail was offered as a charm against evil spirits and kept (displayed at new year) at the Hase-kannon-dō pavilion at Nan'yō. Shows how deeply the corpse-snatching kasha spread in modern regional lore, including a human-woman variant and an enduring exorcised-tail relic.
