The Approach

In a quiet corner of the literary imagination, there exists a figure whose name has become synonymous with loyalty, service, and the blurred line between companionship and servitude. Friday, the faithful companion of Robinson Crusoe, steps out of the pages of Daniel Defoe's 1719 novel not merely as a supporting character, but as a mirror reflecting the complexities of colonialism, race, and human connection. His story, though often overshadowed by his master's, is a tale of survival, adaptation, and the enduring question of what it means to be 'civilized.'

Content advisory. Depictions of colonialism, enslavement, and racial hierarchy; the character's name and portrayal reflect 18th-century colonial attitudes and may be offensive to some readers.
Identity

Friday

Pronunciation /ˈfraɪdeɪ/

Etymology

Named by Robinson Crusoe for the day of the week on which he was rescued. The name is not his indigenous name, which is never revealed.

Cultural Ownership

English literature (public domain). The character originated in Daniel Defoe's 1719 novel and is part of Western literary canon.

Disputed Identifications

No disputed identifications. Some scholars debate his exact ethnic origin, but the text is ambiguous (he is described as 'a handsome fellow, well shaped' with olive skin, not black).

Description

Appearance

Friday is described by Crusoe as 'a comely, handsome fellow, perfectly well made, with straight strong limbs, not too large, tall and well shap'd, and, as I reckon, about twenty six years of age.' He has a 'very good countenance, not a fierce and surly aspect, but seem'd to have something very manly in his face, and yet he had all the sweetness and softness of an European in his countenance too, especially when he smil'd.' His skin is 'not quite black, but very tawny' with a 'nose not flat, like the negroes, a very small high forehead, and a mouth not large.' He is described as having 'long black hair'.

Symbols & Iconography

Friday is often depicted in illustrations and adaptations as a young, bare-chested man with a loincloth, carrying a spear or bow. He is frequently shown kneeling before Crusoe, symbolizing his submission. In modern iconography, he is sometimes portrayed as a native American or Pacific Islander, though the text is ambiguous.

Behaviour & Personality

Friday is depicted as loyal, grateful, and eager to learn. He quickly adopts Crusoe's customs and language, and he repeatedly expresses his devotion, saying 'O master! you die? no, no, I die, you no die.' He is also resourceful and brave, as shown when he helps Crusoe fight cannibals and later when he is sent to rescue others. He shows curiosity and a desire to understand Christianity, but he also retains some of his own beliefs, leading to theological discussions with Crusoe. He is depicted as having a 'simple, untaught' mind, which Crusoe sees as evidence of his 'savage' nature.

Domains & Habitats

Friday's original home is not explicitly named, but it is implied to be an island in the Caribbean or South America. After his rescue, he lives on Crusoe's island, then travels to England and later to the Pyrenees, where he helps fight off wolves.

Version Differences

In the novel, Friday is a native of the Caribbean, possibly of Arawak or Carib origin. In many adaptations, he is portrayed as a generic 'native' or as an African. In the 1960s television series 'The Adventures of Robinson Crusoe', Friday is played by a white actor in makeup. In J.M. Coetzee's novel 'Foe' (1986), Friday is a mute African slave, a deliberate reimagining. In the 1997 film 'Robinson Crusoe', Friday is played by a Native American actor, and the relationship is more egalitarian.

Survival and hunting skills

Friday is skilled in hunting, fishing, and navigating the wilderness, as he demonstrates when he kills a bear and helps Crusoe hunt. He is also knowledgeable about local plants and animals.

Language acquisition

Source Version Original novel

Description

Friday learns English quickly, and within a few years he can converse fluently. He also teaches Crusoe some of his own language, but these words are not recorded.

Limitations

His English is initially broken and simplistic.

Loyalty and service

Source Version Original novel and most adaptations

Description

Friday's primary 'power' is his unwavering loyalty to Crusoe, which is so strong that he would risk his life for his master. This is depicted as a moral virtue, but also as a product of his 'savage' simplicity.

Limitations

His loyalty is often seen as a form of subservience, limiting his autonomy.

Contradictions

In some modern retellings, Friday's loyalty is reinterpreted as a form of resistance or survival strategy.

Stories & Myths

The Rescue of Friday

Source Daniel Defoe, 'Robinson Crusoe', 1719Culture EnglishPeriod 17th century (set), 18th century (written)

Summary

Crusoe spots a group of cannibals on his island who are preparing to kill one of their captives. He shoots the pursuers and rescues the victim, who then kneels and shows gratitude. Crusoe names him Friday after the day of his rescue. Friday becomes Crusoe's servant and companion.

Participants

Friday

Consequences

Friday becomes Crusoe's loyal servant and helps him in various tasks. He also provides information about the mainland and the presence of other Europeans (the Spanish shipwreck survivors).

Variants

In some adaptations, the rescue is less violent or Friday is already free. In Coetzee's 'Foe', Friday is mute and has been mutilated, and the rescue is not depicted.

Relationships

Robinson Crusoe

Type master-servant / companionSource Original novel

Description

Friday is Crusoe's servant and companion. Crusoe teaches him English and Christianity, and Friday saves Crusoe's life on multiple occasions. Their relationship is complex, marked by affection and mutual dependence, but also by a clear power imbalance.

Version Note

In the novel, Friday is submissive and grateful. In some modern adaptations, the relationship is more equal or critical of colonialism.

Conflicting Accounts

Some critics argue that the relationship is a model of benevolent colonialism, while others see it as an example of racial hierarchy and exploitation.

Friday's father

Type familySource Original novel

Description

Friday's father is rescued from cannibals by Crusoe and Friday. He is depicted as an old man who is grateful and respectful. He later helps in the plans to leave the island.

Version Note

He appears only briefly.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Cannibals

Type hostileSource Original novel

Description

Friday's people are threatened by cannibals, and he himself is captured and nearly killed by them. Crusoe's rescue of Friday is the beginning of their relationship.

Version Note

The cannibals are depicted as savages, though Friday himself is not a cannibal.

Conflicting Accounts

Some critics argue that the depiction of cannibals reflects colonial fears and justifications for conquest.

Enslavement and servitude

Source Original novel, critical analyses

Description

Friday becomes Crusoe's servant after being rescued. Crusoe teaches him the word 'Master' first, and Friday willingly accepts this role. The novel presents this as a natural and positive outcome, but it is clearly a form of enslavement, albeit a 'benevolent' one.

Scholarly Context

Postcolonial critics like Peter Hulme and Edward Said have analyzed Friday's character as a symbol of colonial subjugation.

Racial hierarchy

Source Original novel, critical analyses

Description

Crusoe repeatedly describes Friday as 'savage' and 'simple', and he believes he is doing Friday a favor by 'civilizing' him. This reflects the racial hierarchies of the time.

Scholarly Context

The novel is often cited as an example of early colonial literature that justifies European domination.

Violence and cannibalism

Source Original novel

Description

The novel depicts cannibalism as a savage practice, and Crusoe and Friday kill many cannibals. Friday is initially horrified by the idea of eating human flesh, but he later suggests eating his enemies, which Crusoe rebukes.

Scholarly Context

Cannibalism was a common trope in colonial literature to dehumanize indigenous peoples.

Contradictions & Disputes

Friday's ethnicity

Position A

Friday is a Carib or Arawak native from the Caribbean.

Position A — evidence

The novel is set in the Caribbean and Friday's description (tawny skin, long black hair) matches indigenous peoples.

Position B

Friday is of African descent.

Position B — evidence

Some readers assume Friday is African because of the historical context of slavery.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars agree he is indigenous Caribbean, but the text is ambiguous.

Nature of the master-servant relationship

Position A

The relationship is a positive example of cross-cultural friendship and mutual respect.

Position A — evidence

Friday is loyal and Crusoe teaches him useful skills; they eventually work together as equals in some tasks.

Position B

The relationship is a tool of colonial oppression, with Friday being a subjugated 'other'.

Position B — evidence

Crusoe never frees Friday, and Friday's voice is largely suppressed.

Scholarly Consensus

Modern scholarship tends to view the relationship as colonial and problematic, but some argue for a more nuanced reading.

Friday's agency

Position A

Friday has agency and makes his own choices, such as choosing to stay with Crusoe.

Position A — evidence

Friday could have left but chooses to stay.

Position B

Friday has no real agency; he is coerced by circumstances and power dynamics.

Position B — evidence

He has no other options and is dependent on Crusoe for survival.

Scholarly Consensus

Debated; some see Friday as a complex character, others as a passive symbol.

Research Scope

This dossier focuses on Friday as depicted in Daniel Defoe's 'Robinson Crusoe' (1719) and its immediate literary and cultural aftermath. It does not cover the unrelated character 'Friday' from the television series 'Friday the 13th' or other homonymous figures. Research is based on the novel's text, major critical interpretations, and notable adaptations. Due to the age of the source, primary evidence is the novel itself and subsequent scholarly analysis.
