Who It Is

Before she was a nation, before she was a desert of oil and conflict, Arabia was a woman on a coin. In 106 CE, the Roman Emperor Trajan looked at the wealthy Nabataean kingdom—a land of frankincense, spices, and the rose-red city of Petra—and simply... took it. To celebrate, he minted a new goddess: Arabia herself, a dignified lady with a camel at her feet, forever stamping the conquest into history. She is not a goddess of myth, but a goddess of empire—a symbol of what happens when a superpower wants your resources and has the legions to get them.

As a personification, Arabia embodies the wealth, exoticism, and strategic importance of the province. She is a symbol of Roman conquest and the successful incorporation of a new territory, projecting an image of abundance, peace, and imperial order.

Provincial Personification

Represents the Roman province of Arabia Petraea, its people, and its resources. Her image legitimizes Roman rule and celebrates the province's incorporation into the empire.

Limit: She is an allegorical figure, not a narrative deity with myths or powers.

Symbol of Imperial Abundance

Embodying the wealth of the East, she is used to project Roman prosperity, the success of the imperial project, and the 'civilizing' influence of Rome.

Limit: A propaganda function, not a literal power.

Form & Signs

Appearance — On Roman coins, Arabia is depicted as a female figure, often wearing a long robe (stola) or a distinctive Eastern-style dress. She may hold a branch of frankincense or myrrh, a small camel at her feet, or a standard. On Hadrian's 'province series' coins, she is shown as a mature, dignified woman, sometimes with a turreted crown (representing the province's cities).

Symbols — Primary symbols: camel (for the desert and trade), frankincense branch (for the region's famous incense trade), turreted crown (for the province's cities), and the military standard (for Rome's conquest).

Domains — Her domain is the Roman province of Arabia Petraea (modern southern Syria, Jordan, Sinai, and northwestern Saudi Arabia). She represents the land's resources: frankincense, myrrh, spices, and its position as a trade hub between the Mediterranean and the East.

Accounts I
The Annexation of the Nabataean KingdomRoman historical accounts (Cassius Dio, epitome of Book 68); numismatic evidence

106 CE

Following the death of the last Nabataean king, Rabbel II Soter, Emperor Trajan ordered the annexation of the Nabataean kingdom. The region became the Roman province of Arabia Petraea, with Bostra as its capital. The personification 'Arabia' was created for the coinage to commemorate this event. The province became a vital frontier and trade hub, connecting the Mediterranean to the Red Sea and the Indian Ocean trade routes.

Arabia is a pre-Islamic Arabian goddess. — She is a Roman personification of the province of Arabia Petraea, created by the Roman state. She is not a native Arabian deity.

She is the same as the modern nation of Saudi Arabia. — Her domain was the Roman province of Arabia Petraea (modern Jordan, southern Syria, Sinai), which is distinct from the modern Kingdom of Saudi Arabia.

She had a complex mythology. — She has no myths, narratives, or personal attributes beyond her role as a personification of a province.
