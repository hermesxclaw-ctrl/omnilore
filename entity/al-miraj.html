On the Island of the Dragon there is a hare called Al-Mi'raj, yellow of fur, with a single black horn upon its forehead. Though small, it is a fierce beast, and even the dragon fears its charge.

Who It Is

Al-Mi'raj (also spelled Al-Miraj) is a strange and fascinating creature from Arabic folklore, described as a yellow hare or rabbit with a single, black, spiral horn projecting from its forehead — essentially an Islamic unicorn-hare. According to the 13th-century Arab cosmographer Zakariya al-Qazwini in his 'Aja'ib al-Makhluqat' (Wonders of Creation), Al-Mi'raj lives on an island called Jezirat al-Tennyn (Dragon Island or Serpent Island) in the Indian Ocean, one of the many fabled islands of the Arabian Nights tradition. Despite its small, harmless appearance, Al-Mi'raj is described as a fierce and aggressive creature that can frighten away or even attack animals much larger than itself, including dragons and serpents. The creature's horn is said to have magical properties. On Dragon Island, Al-Mi'raj is one of several remarkable beings that inhabit this remote paradise, including the dragon itself, which terrorizes the island until subdued. In some accounts, Al-Mi'raj represents the principle of the small but fierce — a being whose spirit is larger than its body. Al-Qazwini's description places Al-Mi'raj within a rich tradition of Islamic 'Aja'ib' (wonders) literature, which catalogued the marvels and strange creatures of the known and unknown world. The name may derive from 'mi'raj' (literally 'ascension' or 'ladder' in Arabic), possibly referring to the horn's shape or the creature's elevated status among beasts. Al-Mi'raj bears a striking resemblance to the European heraldic 'yale' (a goat-like creature with horns that can move independently) and to the modern concept of the 'rabbicorn' or horned hare. In contemporary fantasy, Al-Mi'raj has been adapted in role-playing games and has become a minor but distinctive figure in modern bestiary collections. The horned hare appears in Jorge Luis Borges' 'Book of Imaginary Beings' as 'The Myrmecoleon, or Ant-Lion' — though Borges conflates several sources.

The Approach

On a small island in the Indian Ocean, a golden hare with a single black horn once caused every wild beast to flee in terror. This is the al-mi'raj, a creature of medieval Arabic wonder-literature, a gift to Alexander the Great after he slew a dragon, and a being that has hopped from ancient manuscripts into modern video games and anime.

Content advisory. None. The al-mi'raj is a legendary creature with no violent or disturbing cultural baggage. Some modern adaptations depict it as a hostile monster, but this is a creative interpretation, not historical fact.

Etymology

The name 'al-mi'raj' is Arabic, derived from the root 'ʿ-r-j' which relates to lameness or limping, but its specific application to this creature is uncertain. Some variants like 'ʿarāj' and 'baqrāj' appear in different manuscripts.

Cultural Ownership

Arabian / Islamic (medieval Arabic and Persian literature)

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars suggest the al-mi'raj may be related to the karkadann, another one-horned creature in Arabic lore, but the al-mi'raj is distinctly a hare or rabbit. The name 'al-mi'raj' is not present in all manuscripts; it may have been added by later copyists.

Description

Appearance

According to Qazwini, the al-mi'raj resembles a yellow hare with a single black horn. Later accounts, such as in Idrisi, describe it as having a coat of shiny gold and a single black horn. Depictions vary: some manuscripts show a hare, others a hound-like hybrid.

Symbols & Iconography

The al-mi'raj is primarily depicted as a one-horned hare or rabbit. Its golden color and black horn are distinctive. In some illustrations, it appears alongside the dragon it is associated with.

Behaviour & Personality

The al-mi'raj is said to cause all wild animals to flee at the sight of it. It is not described as aggressive toward humans; rather, it is a curious and wondrous creature.

Domains & Habitats

It is said to inhabit an island called Jazīrat al-Tinnīn ('Sea-Serpent Island' or 'Dragon Island') in the Indian Ocean, according to Qazwini. In Idrisi's account, it is found on an island called Mustashiayn in Western Africa.

Version Differences

The name varies: al-mi'raj, ʿarāj, baqrāj, al-Maharāj. The color of its coat is sometimes yellow, sometimes gold. The island location differs between the Indian Ocean and Western Africa. Some accounts do not name the creature at all.

Stories & Myths

The Dragon of Dragon Island

Source Qazwini, Marvels of Things CreatedCulture Arabian / IslamicPeriod 13th century

Summary

Iskandar (Alexander the Great) arrives at an island in the Indian Ocean where a dragon is devouring livestock. He defeats the dragon by using decoy oxen stuffed with reactive substances (sulfur, quicklime, arsenic, etc.), causing the dragon to explode or die. The grateful islanders gift him the al-mi'raj, a one-horned hare that causes wild beasts to flee.

Participants

Iskandar

The dragon

Islanders

Al-mi'raj

Consequences

Iskandar obtains the al-mi'raj as a gift.

Variants

In Idrisi's version, the island is called Mustashiayn and is located in Western Africa; the dragon is killed with oxen stuffed with active substances. In the Persian Shahnama, Iskandar uses five oxen filled with poison and oil. In the Syriac Alexander Romance, the dragon is killed with gypsum, pitch, lead, sulfur, and heated brass balls. In Ahmedî's Iskendername, Iskandar uses poisoned hooks on a chariot.

The Aloeswood Trade

Source Idrisi, Nuzhat al-Mushtaq (modern edition)Culture Arabian / IslamicPeriod 12th century

Summary

Iskandar harvests aloeswood on the island of Lāqā. The wood initially has no fragrance but becomes fragrant after leaving the island. He trades the best specimen for other goods, including the baqrāj, a hare-like creature with a shiny gold coat and a single black horn, which causes wild animals to flee.
