Longobard Worms

The Dragon of Lombardy, Lombard Serpent-Kings

also known asThe Dragon of LombardyLombard Serpent-KingsWorms of the LombardsLangobardic Wyrms
TypeDragon / Ancestor Spirit
CultureItalian (Lombard / Northern Italy)
Who It Is
Form

The Longobard Worms are typically depicted as massive, wingless serpents, often with a crown or a golden mane, reflecting their royal association. Unlike the fire-breathing dragons of later medieval romance, they are described as having a venomous breath or a corrosive saliva, and their scales are said to be as hard as iron, impervious to ordinary weapons. In some accounts, they have a single horn or a jewel embedded in their forehead, symbolizing their connection to the earth's treasures. They are often shown coiled around ancient ruins or guarding burial mounds, with eyes that glow like embers, and their size is said to be so great that they can encircle a hill or a castle. The worms are also associated with water, often dwelling in lakes or rivers, where they appear as dark, sinuous shapes beneath the surface.

Powers

Venomous Breath The Longobard Worms can exhale a cloud of poisonous vapor that can wither crops, sicken livestock, and kill humans. This ability is often used to defend their territory or to punish those who desecrate their sanctuaries.

Prophecy and Omen-Sending The worms are said to possess the gift of foresight, and their appearances are interpreted as omens of war, famine, or the death of a king. In some tales, they communicate prophecies to seers or directly to rulers in dreams.

Shape-Shifting In certain legends, the worms can assume human form, often as a beautiful maiden or a wise elder, to interact with humans, test their character, or lead them astray. This ability is rare and is used sparingly, as it is believed to weaken the worm's connection to the earth.

Invulnerable Scales The worms' scales are impervious to conventional weapons, requiring a hero to discover a weak spot, often under the jaw or on the belly, or to use a magical weapon, such as a sword forged by a dwarf or blessed by a saint.

Accounts

Before the Lombard invasion of Italy in 568 CE, King Alboin is said to have dreamed of a great worm emerging from the Po River, encircling the land and devouring the cities. His seers interpreted this as a sign that the Lombards would conquer Italy but also that their rule would be plagued by internal strife. The worm was seen as both a blessing and a curse, foretelling the Lombard kingdom's rise and eventual fall.

The Tarantasio, a Longobard worm, terrorized the shores of Lake Gerundo near Milan, causing floods and devouring livestock and children. The dragon was eventually slain by a knight of the Visconti family, who became the lords of Milan. The Visconti adopted the biscione, a serpent devouring a child, as their heraldic emblem, commemorating the victory and claiming the worm's power as their own.

After the death of a Lombard king, a great worm was seen coiled around his burial mound, and it was said to guard the tomb from grave robbers. The worm was believed to be the king's spirit in animal form, protecting his treasure and ensuring his peaceful afterlife. When a group of treasure hunters attempted to dig into the mound, the worm emerged and killed them with its venomous breath, and the villagers thereafter left the tomb undisturbed.

Modern Interpretations

Heraldry Biscione of the Visconti Visconti family (later Sforza) 14th century (adopted) Family emblem The biscione became one of the most recognizable heraldic symbols in Europe, later used by the Duchy of Milan and the Alfa Romeo automobile company, ensuring the Longobard worm's legacy in modern branding. Literature Leggende della Lombardia Carlo Dossi 1881 Collection of folk tales Dossi's anthology preserved many oral traditions about the Longobard Worms, including the Lake Gerundo legend, and influenced later Italian folklore studies. Video Game Assassin's Creed II Ubisoft 2009 Easter egg / Symbol The biscione appears in the game as a decorative element in Florence and Venice, subtly referencing the Visconti and their serpent symbol, though not the worms directly. Academic Study The Lombards: The Ancient Longobards Neil Christie 1995 Historical analysis Christie's work discusses Lombard mythology and the role of serpent imagery in their culture, providing scholarly context for the Longobard Worms.

Scholarly Debates
Origin of the Worms
The worms are indigenous to Italy, arising from the swamps and lakes of Lombardy, as in the Tarantasio legend. ⇌ The worms were brought by the Lombards from their Germanic homeland, as evidenced by parallels with Norse and Old English dragon lore.
Nature of the Worms
The worms are malevolent beings, demanding tribute and causing destruction, as seen in the Lake Gerundo tales. ⇌ The worms are benevolent guardians, protecting the land and its people, as in the tomb-guardian stories, and are only hostile when provoked.
Connection to Worms, Germany
The name 'Worms' in 'Longobard Worms' refers to the city of Worms, Germany, where the Burgundians were based, and thus the entity is a misnomer. ⇌ The name 'Worms' is a generic term for dragon, and the connection to the city is coincidental, as the Longobard Worms are purely Italian.
What We Don't Know

There is a lack of primary sources specifically detailing the Longobard Worms as a distinct entity; most references are embedded in broader Lombard chronicles or later folklore. The connection between the Lombard serpent imagery and the Germanic wurm tradition is understudied, and the exact etymology of 'Longobard Worms' as a compound term is unclear. Additionally, the relationship between the Tarantasio and the Longobard Worms is often assumed but not critically examined. Further research is needed to trace the evolution of the worm from an omen to a heraldic symbol.
