Who It Is

Imagine a dragon that doesn't breathe fire, but whispers prophecies of kings and kingdoms—welcome to the world of the Longobard Worms, where serpents guard the tombs of Lombard warriors and shape the destiny of Northern Italy.

The Longobard Worms are ambivalent beings: they can be benevolent guardians of the land and its people, but they are also fiercely territorial and vengeful if provoked. In legends, they are often associated with the foundation of cities or the protection of treasure, and they are said to have a deep wisdom, understanding human speech and even prophecy. They are not inherently evil, but their wrath is terrible, and they are known to demand tribute or sacrifices to appease them. Powers
Venomous Breath

The Longobard Worms can exhale a cloud of poisonous vapor that can wither crops, sicken livestock, and kill humans. This ability is often used to defend their territory or to punish those who desecrate their sanctuaries.

Limit:

Prophecy and Omen-Sending

The worms are said to possess the gift of foresight, and their appearances are interpreted as omens of war, famine, or the death of a king. In some tales, they communicate prophecies to seers or directly to rulers in dreams.

Limit:

Shape-Shifting

In certain legends, the worms can assume human form, often as a beautiful maiden or a wise elder, to interact with humans, test their character, or lead them astray. This ability is rare and is used sparingly, as it is believed to weaken the worm's connection to the earth.

Limit:

Invulnerable Scales

The worms' scales are impervious to conventional weapons, requiring a hero to discover a weak spot, often under the jaw or on the belly, or to use a magical weapon, such as a sword forged by a dwarf or blessed by a saint.

Accounts I
The Omen of King AlboinPaul the Deacon, Historia Langobardorum

c. 790 CE (recording events of the 6th century)

Before the Lombard invasion of Italy in 568 CE, King Alboin is said to have dreamed of a great worm emerging from the Po River, encircling the land and devouring the cities. His seers interpreted this as a sign that the Lombards would conquer Italy but also that their rule would be plagued by internal strife. The worm was seen as both a blessing and a curse, foretelling the Lombard kingdom's rise and eventual fall. The dream was taken as a divine mandate for the invasion, but it also instilled a sense of fatalism among the Lombards, which some historians argue contributed to their eventual assimilation into the Italian population.

Accounts II
The Dragon of Lake Gerundo and the ViscontiLocal Lombard folklore, collected in 19th-century anthologies

Medieval (12th-13th century) and later folk tradition

The Tarantasio, a Longobard worm, terrorized the shores of Lake Gerundo near Milan, causing floods and devouring livestock and children. The dragon was eventually slain by a knight of the Visconti family, who became the lords of Milan. The Visconti adopted the biscione, a serpent devouring a child, as their heraldic emblem, commemorating the victory and claiming the worm's power as their own. The slaying of the worm established the Visconti dynasty's legitimacy and their control over Milan. The biscione became one of the most famous heraldic symbols in Italy, later adopted by the Sforza and even used by the luxury car company Alfa Romeo.

The Worm of the Lombard King's TombFolklore from the Lombardy region, collected by Carlo Dossi

19th century (recording older oral traditions)

After the death of a Lombard king, a great worm was seen coiled around his burial mound, and it was said to guard the tomb from grave robbers. The worm was believed to be the king's spirit in animal form, protecting his treasure and ensuring his peaceful afterlife. When a group of treasure hunters attempted to dig into the mound, the worm emerged and killed them with its venomous breath, and the villagers thereafter left the tomb undisturbed. The tomb was preserved, and the site became a place of local veneration, with offerings left to the worm to ensure the king's continued protection of the land.
