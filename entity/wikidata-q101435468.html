The Approach

In 1917, amid the collapse of empires and the birth of a nation, a group of young Estonian poets and writers gathered under the sign of a mythical fire-bird. They called themselves Siuru, and their manifesto was simple: freedom of the human spirit, the joy of creation, and a daring eroticism that scandalized the conservative public. Though their collective flame burned for only two years, the members of Siuru would go on to shape Estonian literature for decades, in independence and in exile.

Content advisory. The poetry of Siuru is known for its erotic themes, which were considered scandalous in its time. Some works may contain explicit or suggestive content. No other sensitive material is known.

Appearance

Symbols & Iconography

The group's symbol was a white chrysanthemum, representing purity and artistic renewal. The name itself evokes the mythical fire-bird, symbolizing passion and creative fire.

Behaviour & Personality

The movement was characterized by its rebellious spirit, embracing expressionism and neo-romanticism. It valued individual freedom, emotional intensity, and a break from formalist traditions. The poetry was often erotic and scandalous, challenging conservative norms.

Domains & Habitats

Estonia, particularly the intellectual circles of Tallinn and Tartu, during the period of 1917-1919.

Version Differences

The movement evolved over time: initially a cohesive group, it split in 1919, with some members leaving and others joining. The program and style remained consistent, but the membership changed.

Stories & Myths

Founding and early years

Source Provided source materialCulture EstonianPeriod 1917-1919

Summary

Siuru was founded in 1917 by August Gailit and included poets and writers such as Marie Under, Henrik Visnapuu, Johannes Semper, Friedebert Tuglas, Peet Aren, Otto Krusten, and Artur Adson. The group published three volumes of poetry between 1917 and 1919.

Consequences

The movement gained notoriety for its erotic and scandalous poetry, but also popularized literature in Estonia.

Split and later developments

Source Provided source materialCulture EstonianPeriod 1919 onwards

Summary

In 1919, conflicts arose within the group. Visnapuu and Gailit left, while Johannes Barbarus and August Alle joined. The movement effectively ended, but its members continued to be major figures in Estonian literature.

Participants

Consequences

The members went on to have distinguished careers, both in Estonia and in exile during the Soviet occupation.
