Who It Is

In the vast pantheon of Mahāyāna bodhisattvas, Sarvanivāraṇaviṣkambhin stands as the silent guardian of the meditator's mind, the remover of every obstacle that stands between you and enlightenment. Unlike his more famous counterparts, he is not worshipped with grand rituals but invoked in the quiet stillness of practice, his name a promise that all hindrances—mental, emotional, and spiritual—can be cleared.

As a bodhisattva, he embodies compassion and wisdom, specifically focused on removing hindrances to meditation and spiritual progress. He is invoked for clearing obstacles, both mental and external.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Sanskrit: sarva (all) + nivāraṇa (hindrance, obstacle) + viṣkambhin (one who blocks, obstructs, or removes). The name means 'He who blocks all hindrances'.

Pronunciation — sar-vah-nee-VAH-rah-nah-veesh-KAHM-bhin

Aliases — Sarvanīvaraṇaviṣkambhin, Sarvanivaranaviskambhin, Sarvanivarana-Viskambhin

Ownership — Mahāyāna Buddhism, particularly in East Asian and Vajrayāna traditions. Also revered in Tibetan Buddhism.

Powers
Removal of Hindrances

His primary power is to remove all obstacles (nivāraṇa) that hinder meditation, spiritual practice, and daily life. This includes mental defilements and external impediments.

Limit: His power is effective when invoked with faith and proper practice; it is not a magical cure-all but a spiritual aid.

Mantra and Dhāraṇī

His mantra, as given in the Vairocanābhisaṃbodhi Sūtra, is: 'namaḥ samantabuddhānāṃ/ aḥ/ sattva hitābhyudgata/ traṃ/ traṃ/ raṃ/ raṃ/ svāhā'. A longer dhāraṇī from the Dhāraṇīsaṃgraha is also used to purify all negative karma and obstacles.

Limit: The mantra should be recited with proper ritual and intention; its efficacy depends on the practitioner's purity and dedication.

Interlocutor in Sutras

He serves as the main questioner or speaker in certain Mahāyāna sutras, indicating his role as a teacher and guide.

Limit: Not a supernatural power per se, but his role highlights his wisdom.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In iconography, Sarvanivāraṇaviṣkambhin is often depicted holding a flower in one hand, sometimes with a wishing gem (cintāmaṇi). He may be shown in bodhisattva attire, with peaceful expression, and sometimes with a blue or green body color, though specifics vary by tradition.

Symbols — Flower (often a lotus or utpala), cintāmaṇi (wish-fulfilling gem), and sometimes a vajra or bell in Vajrayāna contexts. He may be depicted seated or standing, with a serene expression.

Domains — Resides in the bodhisattva realms, particularly associated with the eastern direction in some mandalas. He is not typically associated with a specific pure land but is present in the assembly of Buddhas.

Accounts I
Homage to AvalokiteśvaraLotus Sutra, Chapter 25

Early centuries CE

In the Lotus Sutra, Sarvanivāraṇaviṣkambhin is present in the assembly and pays homage to Avalokiteśvara, acknowledging his compassionate deeds. Highlights the unity of bodhisattvas and the importance of Avalokiteśvara.
