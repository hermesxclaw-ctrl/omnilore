Who They Are

Veles is Perun's eternal adversary and necessary opposite: the shape-shifting god of cattle, commerce, magic, music, oaths, and the watery world of the dead, coiled among the roots of the world tree while the thunderer rules its crown. Where Perun is the god of princes and war-bands, Veles belongs to herdsmen, merchants, sorcerers, and poets — in the medieval Tale of Igor's Campaign, the bard Boyan is called grandson of Veles. Rus treaty-oaths invoked both gods as a complete legal system: break your word and Perun strikes you from above with lightning while Veles curses you from below with cattle-plague, wealth turned to sickness. His annual theft — of cattle, waters, or the storm god's bride — and Perun's thunderous pursuit was the Slavic explanation of the seasonal cycle itself: drought and hoarding, then storm, then release and abundance. Christianity split his inheritance between Saint Blaise, protector of herds, and the devil of a hundred folk tales who hides in the millpond.

Origins

Attested as Volos, god of cattle, in Rus-Byzantine treaties of 907 and 971 CE. His underworld and magical associations are reconstructed from folk custom, place-names, and the pan-Slavic pattern of the storm-serpent duel.

Form

A shapeshifter without a fixed form: a great serpent or dragon among the roots, a bear in the forest, a long-bearded herdsman-sorcerer among men — wet, wooly, dark, and rich.

Shape-shifting Veles can transform into various animals, particularly serpents and cattle, allowing him to interact with both the mortal and spiritual realms. Tale of Igor's Campaign Shape-shifting is limited by the presence of oaths and the binding nature of promises made to Perun. While often depicted as a trickster, some accounts also emphasize his protective nature, causing tension in interpretations.

Control over livestock As the god of cattle, Veles has the power to bless herds and influence their fertility and health. Folklore collections His blessings may be withdrawn if transgressions against him occur, leading to disease or misfortune among livestock. Some sources suggest that his control can also lead to misfortune, thus being seen as a double-edged sword.

Magical abilities Veles is associated with magic, particularly in the context of oaths and commerce, granting him the ability to influence trade and relationships. Primary Chronicle His powers are often countered by Perun's authority, emphasizing a balance between chaos and order. Different sources depict varying degrees of Veles's magical prowess, with some attributing more supernatural capabilities than others.

Protection of wealth Veles is invoked for prosperity and financial success, particularly in trade and agriculture. Folk traditions Invocation requires rituals and offerings; failure to adhere to these can lead to financial ruin instead of prosperity. Some traditions argue that wealth can also bring misfortune under Veles's watch if not handled respectfully.

Weaknesses & Wards

The thunderbolt. Veles wins by theft, disguise, and retreat — never by standing his ground against the axe.

Accounts

Veles and Perun engage in a cosmic struggle, symbolizing the eternal conflict between order and chaos. This narrative illustrates their opposing natures and highlights the necessity of both deities in the Slavic pantheon.

Stories recount how Veles aids herdsmen in times of need, providing guidance and protection against predators.

Boyan, a bard, invokes Veles during his performances, emphasizing the god's connection to music and storytelling.

Oaths during treaties invoked both Veles and Perun, showcasing their roles in societal law and order.

Cultural Significance

Slavic peasants left the last sheaf of grain in the field as an offering — curling Veles' beard — into the twentieth century. His feast merged into Saint Blaise's day in February, when cattle were blessed.

A way to hold it in your mind

In the depths of the Slavic mythological landscape lies Veles, a divine entity embodying both the richness of life and the shadows of chaos. As the Serpent of the Roots, he coils around the foundations of existence, a figure whose duality pervades the cultural consciousness of Eastern Europe. His tales intertwine with the fates of herdsmen, merchants, and poets, revealing a god whose influence stretches from the pastoral to the perilous.
