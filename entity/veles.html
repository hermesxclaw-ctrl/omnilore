Who They Are

Veles is Perun's eternal adversary and necessary opposite: the shape-shifting god of cattle, commerce, magic, music, oaths, and the watery world of the dead, coiled among the roots of the world tree while the thunderer rules its crown. Where Perun is the god of princes and war-bands, Veles belongs to herdsmen, merchants, sorcerers, and poets — in the medieval Tale of Igor's Campaign, the bard Boyan is called grandson of Veles. Rus treaty-oaths invoked both gods as a complete legal system: break your word and Perun strikes you from above with lightning while Veles curses you from below with cattle-plague, wealth turned to sickness. His annual theft — of cattle, waters, or the storm god's bride — and Perun's thunderous pursuit was the Slavic explanation of the seasonal cycle itself: drought and hoarding, then storm, then release and abundance. Christianity split his inheritance between Saint Blaise, protector of herds, and the devil of a hundred folk tales who hides in the millpond.

Origins

Attested as Volos, god of cattle, in Rus-Byzantine treaties of 907 and 971 CE. His underworld and magical associations are reconstructed from folk custom, place-names, and the pan-Slavic pattern of the storm-serpent duel.

Attested in Primary Chronicle (12th c.), folk traditionEra Pre-Christian Slavic Europe

Form

A shapeshifter without a fixed form: a great serpent or dragon among the roots, a bear in the forest, a long-bearded herdsman-sorcerer among men — wet, wooly, dark, and rich.

Powers

Dominion over cattle and all movable wealth; magic, prophecy, and the inspiration of poets and musicians; guardianship of the dead in the grassy underworld Nav; oath-enforcement by disease and ruin.

Weaknesses & Wards

The thunderbolt. Veles wins by theft, disguise, and retreat — never by standing his ground against the axe.

Cultural Significance

Slavic peasants left the last sheaf of grain in the field as an offering — curling Veles' beard — into the twentieth century. His feast merged into Saint Blaise's day in February, when cattle were blessed.
