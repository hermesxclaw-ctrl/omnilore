Who It Is

On a small oil-flask painted in Paestum more than 2,300 years ago, seven young women stand in a garden at the edge of the world. Six of their names are familiar to students of Greek myth — Calypso, Antheia and others — but the seventh is a ghost: Nelisa. She appears nowhere else in surviving literature, no hymn calls her, no poet gives her a speech. She exists only as white letters above a painted head, a single brushstroke preserving a Hesperid whom every other source forgot.

No individual personality attested for Nelisa. Collective Hesperides characterization: guardians/tenders of Hera's orchard, diligent yet pleasure-loving, fond of singing (Euripides calls them 'aoidoi korai'), hospitable when supplicated (they create a spring for the Argonauts out of pity after initial lamentation), mournful when Ladon is slain (Apollonius: they turn to dust/trees in grief). Powers
Tending of the Golden Apples

As Hesperid, duty to cultivate and guard Hera's apple trees that produce golden apples (possibly immortality-conferring). Not agricultural magic but divine horticultural guardianship.

Limit: They do not create the apples; Gaia grew them as wedding gift. They cannot prevent theft by Heracles without Ladon. No evidence of combat power.

Enchanting Song

Euripides (Hippolytus 742-751) calls them 'minstrel maids' (mousa-like) with sweet song; Apollonius describes their lament as melodious. Suggests siren-like vocal power, though not used to lure sailors.

Limit: No attested compulsion or magical effect beyond beauty and mourning; not a weapon.

Hydrokinesis / Spring Creation (collective)

In Argonautica, after pitying the Argonauts' thirst, the Hesperides cause a spring to bubble from the earth where Ladon's corpse lay, or reveal a hidden spring created by Heracles' kick.

Limit: One-time miraculous act born of pity and perhaps residual power of the garden; not freely repeatable. Requires divine permission / garden context.

Longevity / Divine Nymph Status

As nymphs, presumably long-lived or immortal, not aging as mortals. Daughters of primordial or Titan figures.

Limit: Not invulnerable: in Apollonius they turn into trees/dust from grief, suggesting mortality or metamorphic vulnerability. No individual immortality feat for Nelisa.

Metamorphic Lament (Tree Transformation)

Apollonius: upon finding Ladon slain, the three Hesperides wail and become in turn a poplar, elm, and willow (or dust to trees). Suggests shape-shifting under extreme emotion.

Limit: Only attested for the canonical three; not said of Nelisa; involuntary grief response, not controlled power.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No literary description of Nelisa's individual appearance survives. As a Hesperid, she is iconographically a nymph (nymphē): young adult female, fully human in form, not monstrous. On the Asteas lekythos she is rendered in Paestan red-figure style — likely wearing peplos or chiton, with elaborated hairstyle, standing in garden context. Other Hesperides on contemporary vases are shown with floral wreaths, jewelry, and occasionally holding apples o

Symbols — Collective symbols of the Hesperides: golden apples (mēla chrysea), apple tree/grove, serpentine dragon Ladon coiled around tree, evening/western light, singing. Individual symbol for Nelisa: none attested. On the vase, her name label is her only identifier. In broader tradition, Hesperides are sometimes shown with hydriai (water jars) in Argonautica water-giving episode, or with musical instruments reflecting Euripides' 'minstrel maids

Domains — Domain: the Garden of the Hesperides (Kēpos Hesperidōn), a blissful orchard at the far western edge of the world, near Atlas Mountains, Oceanus, or the Atlantic islands depending on source. Associated with evening (hesperos), sunset, the West, liminal boundary between known world and Ocean. Also associated with Hera's bridal gift domain (fertility, marriage). Accounts I
The Wedding Gift of Gaia and Planting of the GardenPherecydes (?) via Apollodorus 2.5.11; scholia on Apollonius; Diodorus 4.26

Archaic to Hellenistic (mythic time: wedding of Zeus and Hera)

When Zeus married Hera, Gaia brought branches with golden apples as bridal gift. Hera, admiring them, asked Gaia to plant them in her garden near Atlas. The Hesperides — however many and by whatever parentage — were appointed to tend the grove, with Ladon the hundred-headed sleepless dragon set to guard it. This aetiology establishes the garden as Hera's property and the Hesperides as its caretakers. Nelisa, as one of the seven on the Asteas vase, would be among those tenders in that local variant, though no text names her in this scene. Creates the central locus for Heracles' labour and Argonauts' thirst episode; explains why apples are both desirable and forbidden; establishes western paradise motif.

The Eleventh Labour of Heracles — Theft of the Golden ApplesApollodorus Bibliotheca 2.5.11; Apollonius Argonautica 4.1396ff (retrospective); Euripides Heracles; vase paintings including Meidias Hydria and Asteas lekythos

Classical/Hellenistic codification of mythic heroic age

After ten labours, Eurystheus demands the apples. Heracles seeks the garden's location via Nereus/Proteus (Old Man of the Sea), frees Prometheus en route, kills Antaeus and Busiris, and reaches the garden. In one version he kills Ladon and takes apples himself; in the more famous trickster version he asks Atlas — father of the Hesperides — to fetch them while he holds the sky, then tricks Atlas into resuming his burden. Athena later returns the apples. On the Asteas lekythos depicting this labour, Nelisa is one of the nymphs present, presumably witnessing the theft or lamenting Ladon. Ladon's death (in some versions); Hesperides' grief/metamorphosis; Heracles completes labour; apples prove unusable by mortals and are returned. Establishes Heracles' cunning as well as strength.

The Argonauts' Thirst in the Hesperian PlainApollonius of Rhodes Argonautica 4.1396-1449

3rd century BCE, set in generation before Trojan War

The day after Heracles has killed Ladon and taken apples, the Argonauts, wandering Libya after carrying the Argo, arrive parched at the Hesperian plain. They find the Hesperides wailing around Ladon's corpse, dust-covered and turned tree-like in grief. Orpheus supplicates them; moved to pity, the nymphs cause a spring to appear (or reveal Heracles' spring) and direct the heroes to water, also pointing out Heracles' footprints and the fallen dragon. Argonauts survive desert crossing; provides etiology for desert spring; shows Hesperides' compassionate nature despite grief; links Heracles and Argonautic sagas chronologically.

Accounts II
The Daughters of Evening — Genealogies of the HesperidesHesiod Theogony 215-216; Servius on Aeneid 4.484; Hyginus Fabulae; Diodorus 4.26

Archaic to Imperial

Mythographers debate who the Hesperides are: Hesiod makes them daughters of Nyx (Night) alone, guarding apples 'beyond Ocean'; later authors make them daughters of Atlas and Hesperis (hence Atlantides), or of Phorcys and Ceto (sea lineage), or Zeus and Themis (order), or Hesperus the Evening Star. Their number shifts accordingly. This genealogical instability reflects attempts to explain their western location and evening name. Nelisa's inclusion in a 7-daughter Atlas-group fits the Atlas+Hesperis parentage variant, though no text explicitly attaches her to that genealogy. Determines epithets (Atlantides, Hesperides, Nyktides); affects iconography and cult associations; creates contradictory family trees that persist into modern handbooks.

Eris and the Apple of DiscordPost-Homeric tradition; epitome of Cypria; later scholia; Ovid; Hyginus

Archaic epic cycle, codified Hellenistic/Roman

At the wedding of Peleus and Thetis (or in some late conflations, from Hera's garden), Eris, goddess of strife, not invited, obtains a golden apple — in some rationalizations taken from the Garden of the Hesperides — and inscribes it 'to the fairest,' throwing it among Hera, Athena and Aphrodite. Their contest, judged by Paris, precipitates the Trojan War. While Apollodorus does not explicitly source Eris's apple to the Hesperides, later syncretic accounts conflate the two golden-apple traditions, making the Hesperides' orchard the ultimate source of the war's catalyst. Judgement of Paris; Trojan War; demonstrates dangerous potency of Hesperidean apples beyond Heracles' labour; blurs distinction between Hesperidean apples and Atalanta's apples.

The Western Garden Rationalized — Pliny and the Atlantic IslandsPliny the Elder Natural History 5.2 and 6.36-37; Silius Italicus Punica 3.253

1st century CE

Pliny places the Garden at Lixus (modern Larache, Morocco) where Heracles fought Antaeus, and elsewhere says the Hesperides live on two Atlantic islands (perhaps Canaries or Cape Verde). Silius Italicus locates the Massylii near Hesperidean woods. By Roman times the garden had become poetic geography and paradoxography rather than cult reality, explaining why Renaissance poets could transplant it freely. Euhemerization and geographic speculation; Renaissance revival of garden as literary topos; botanical naming (Hesperidoeidē for citrus) based on assumed western orange = golden apple equation.

The Hesperides are a fixed group of three sisters named Aegle, Erytheia and Hesperethusa, and any other name is a mistake. — Three is only one literary variant (Apollonius). Four (Apollodorus, Servius), two (Hyginus), seven (Diodorus, Asteas vase) and other quartets (Meidias Hydria) are equally attested in ancient sources. Nelisa is not a mistake but a preserved South Italian variant.

The golden apples were oranges, and the garden was therefore in Portugal/Spain where oranges grow. — Oranges were unknown in Mediterranean antiquity (introduced Middle Ages). Botanical name Hesperidoeidē and modern Greek portokali are Renaissance/modern back-formations. Ancient apples were mythic, possibly quinces, pomegranates or purely imaginary immortality fruits.

Nelisa was a major goddess with her own myths and cult. — No literary myth, hymn, cult site, or sanctuary for Nelisa survives. She is known from a single vase inscription; her biography is entirely collective as one of the garden tenders. Any individual story attributed to her is modern invention.

The Hesperides are the same as the Atlantides/Pleiades/Hyades. — Epithet Atlantides (daughters of Atlas) is shared, but Hesperides, Pleiades (seven daughters of Atlas and Pleione) and Hyades are distinct nymph groups with different functions and stories, though ancient sources sometimes confuse them due to overlapping parentage.

Calypso on the Asteas vase is the same Calypso who detained Odysseus. — Homonymy, not identity. The Odyssean Calypso is daughter of Atlas (Odyssey 1.14) living on Ogygia; the Hesperid Calypso is one of seven garden nymphs. Vase painters reused attractive names; LIMC treats them as separate despite shared name.
