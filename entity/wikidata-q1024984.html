The Approach

In the shadowy margins of Roman religion, a minor goddess named Caca endures—a figure whose very name has become an embarrassment, yet whose story hints at a forgotten hearth, a stolen herd, and a sister's loyalty.

Content advisory. None.
Identity

Caca

Pronunciation KAH-kah

Etymology

The name Caca derives from the Latin verb cacare, meaning 'to defecate.' This etymology is often considered a later folk etymology, but it may reflect an original function related to the hearth or cooking (from *cac-, 'to cook' or 'to bake').

Cultural Ownership

Roman (ancient Italic)

Disputed Identifications

Caca is sometimes identified with the goddess Cacus, a male deity or monster, but this is generally rejected. The connection between Caca and the Greek goddess Hecate is speculative and not supported by ancient evidence.

Description

Appearance

No ancient descriptions of Caca's appearance survive. She is not depicted in any known ancient artwork.

Symbols & Iconography

No symbols or iconographic attributes are attested for Caca. Some modern scholars speculate that she may have been associated with the hearth, but this is conjectural.

Behaviour & Personality

Caca is portrayed in the myth as a loyal sister who, despite her brother's wrongdoing, chooses to aid Hercules. This act of betrayal is seen as just, as it restores the stolen cattle.

Domains & Habitats

Caca is primarily associated with the hearth and household, though this is inferred from her name and her role as a domestic deity. She may have been a protector of the home.

Version Differences

The myth is consistent across ancient sources, though some details vary. Lactantius and Servius both emphasize her role in revealing Cacus's hiding place, but they differ on whether she was deified for this act or was already a goddess.

Powers & Abilities

Stories & Myths

The Betrayal of Cacus

Source Lactantius, Servius, Vatican MythographerCulture RomanPeriod Mythic, set in the time of Hercules

Summary

After Hercules defeated the monster Cacus, who had stolen cattle from him, Caca, Cacus's sister, revealed the hiding place of the stolen cattle to Hercules. In gratitude, Hercules or the gods deified her, and she was subsequently worshipped as a goddess.

Participants

Caca

Hercules

Cacus

Consequences

Caca was honored as a goddess, and her cult was established, though it seems to have been minor.

Variants

Some versions may have made Caca a mortal who was deified after her death, but the details are not fully preserved.

Contradictions & Disputes

Caca's status as a goddess

Position A

Caca was a pre-existing goddess who helped Hercules.

Position A — evidence

Servius implies she was already a goddess, calling her a 'genius' or 'Lara'.

Position B

Caca was a mortal who was deified after her act.

Position B — evidence

Lactantius says she was 'worshipped' after her betrayal, suggesting she was deified.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars accept that Caca was a minor goddess, but the exact nature of her divinity is uncertain.

Primary Evidence

Lactantius, Divinae Institutiones 1.20.36

Type textDate early 4th century CELocator Book 1, chapter 20, section 36Language Latin

Translation

Lactantius mentions Caca as a goddess who was worshipped because she betrayed her brother Cacus, who was a cattle thief, and revealed his hiding place to Hercules.

Servius, Commentary on the Aeneid of Virgil, 8.190

Type textDate late 4th century CELocator On Aeneid 8.190Language Latin

Translation

Servius states that Caca was a goddess who was honored because she told Hercules where her brother Cacus had hidden the stolen cattle. He also notes that she was considered a kind of household deity (genius or Lara).

Mythographus Vaticanus I, 1.49

Type textDate medieval, but based on earlier sourcesLocator Chapter 49Language Latin

Translation

The Vatican Mythographer repeats the story that Caca revealed Cacus's theft to Hercules and was deified for this service.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

The archaeological record for Caca is nonexistent; no inscriptions or images have been found. The exact nature of her cult, if any, is unknown. The etymology remains uncertain. The relationship between Caca and other Roman household deities is speculative.

Research Scope

This dossier focuses on the Roman goddess Caca, as attested in ancient sources and discussed in modern scholarship. It does not cover the modern slang term 'caca' or other unrelated uses of the name.
