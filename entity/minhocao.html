The earth splits open in a straight line through the forest, trees tumbling on both sides. There is no earthquake, no flood -- only the Minhocao, burrowing far beneath, carving its path as a plow cuts a furrow.

Who It Is

The Minhocao ('giant earthworm' in Portuguese) is a cryptid reported from the interior of southern Brazil, described as a massive subterranean creature that creates enormous trenches and channels as it burrows beneath the earth. Reports from the 19th century describe a serpentine animal 25 to 150 feet long, covered in bony, scaly armor, with a large, flat head, and visible horns or tentacles. When it moves underground, it uproots trees, diverts rivers, collapses houses, and creates chasms across roads. When it emerges into rivers, it overturns boats and devours livestock. Brazilian naturalist Augustin de Saint-Hilaire documented Minhocao legends in the 1840s, noting that multiple rural communities described the same creature. In 1878, a German journal published accounts of a giant worm seen near the Rio dos Papagaios in Parana that had diverted a stream. Cryptozoologist Bernard Heuvelmans suggested the Minhocao could be a surviving glyptodont (giant armadillo), an undiscovered giant caecilian (limbless amphibian), or a giant lungfish. Another theory points to the South American anaconda -- reports may exaggerate its size and burrowing behavior. A more exotic hypothesis suggests a surviving titanosaur or similar sauropod that adapted to burrowing. The Minhocao's name is also used in Brazilian Portuguese for the elevated highways in Sao Paulo (Minhocoes), indirectly preserving the creature's folkloric memory. The last major spate of Minhocao reports occurred in the late 19th century, making it one of cryptozoology's historical mysteries rather than an active search target.
