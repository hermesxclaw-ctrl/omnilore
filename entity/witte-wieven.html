Who It Is

Witte Wieven ('White Women') are spectral female figures from the folklore of the eastern Netherlands — primarily Drenthe, Gelderland, and Overijssel — as well as neighboring parts of Germany. They appear as ethereal women dressed entirely in white, sometimes veiled, who are most commonly seen in misty fields, near ancient burial mounds (grafheuvels), and at crossroads during twilight. Witte Wieven are deeply connected to the landscape of the eastern Netherlands, where burial mounds from the Funnelbeaker culture (3500–2800 BCE) and later Iron Age barrows still dot the countryside. The figure of the Witte Wieven is complex and regionally varied: in some traditions, they are the ghosts of wise women (wicca or völva) who served their communities in life and now guard ancient wisdom; in others, they are the spirits of unchristened children or women who died in childbirth; in still others, they are the remnants of ancient Saxon priestesses who now haunt the heathlands. Witte Wieven are generally ambivalent — they may lead lost travelers to safety or warn of approaching danger, but they also lure people into marshes or bury them under the mist. In Drenthe, offerings of bread and milk were traditionally left at burial mounds to appease them. The name 'Witte Wieven' is linguistically related to the English 'wise women' (witches) and the Anglo-Saxon 'wicca.' Modern neo-pagan traditions in the Netherlands have revived the Witte Wieven as goddess-like figures representing the ancestral feminine spirit of the land.
