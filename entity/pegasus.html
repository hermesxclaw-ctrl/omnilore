Who It Is

Imagine a horse that is not just a horse, but a thunderbolt waiting to be ridden. Born from a severed Gorgon's neck, this divine steed has carried heroes to glory and poets to inspiration. He is the ultimate symbol of freedom, yet he bows only to the gods. This is Pegasus, the winged horse of Greek myth, a creature whose story is as wild and untamable as the sky he flies through.

Wild and untamable by mortal hands, but gentle when bridled by a divine gift. He is associated with springs and the creative inspiration of poets. He is loyal to a worthy master but will throw an unworthy rider.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name's origin is disputed. Some ancient sources connect it to the Greek 'pēgē' (spring, fountain), referencing his birth at the springs of Oceanus or his association with the Hippocrene spring on Mount Helicon. Others suggest a pre-Greek or Luwian origin, possibly related to a storm god's horse.

Pronunciation — PEG-uh-sus

Aliases — The Winged Horse, Pegasos

Ownership — Greek mythology; later adopted by Roman culture and global popular culture.

Names are cult layers, not one fixed label.

Powers
Flight

Can fly at great speed, faster than any bird, and can ascend to the heavens.

Limit: None known; he is immortal and tireless.

Creation of Springs

By striking his hoof, he can create a spring of water. Most famously, he creates the Hippocrene spring on Mount Helicon.

Limit: Only when his hoof strikes the ground.

Immortality

He is an immortal divine being, not subject to death or aging.

Limit: None.

Divine Bridle

He can only be tamed by a golden bridle, a gift from the gods (Athena). Without it, he is untamable.

Limit: The bridle is a divine artifact, not a power of Pegasus himself.

Form & Signs

Appearance — A divine, immortal winged horse, usually pure white, with large feathered wings. In ancient art, he is often depicted without a rider, or with Bellerophon. He is smaller and more elegant than a typical warhorse, with a proud bearing.

Symbols — White wings, the Hippocrene spring, the golden bridle, the constellation, and the idea of poetic inspiration and freedom.

Domains — Sky, Mount Helicon, the Hippocrene spring, Olympus, and the constellation Pegasus.

Accounts I
Birth from MedusaHesiod, Theogony

Archaic

When Perseus beheads the Gorgon Medusa, the winged horse Pegasus and his brother Chrysaor spring from her neck, fully grown. This miraculous birth links him to the monstrous Gorgon, yet he is a noble, divine creature. Pegasus becomes a free, wild creature, later associated with springs and the Muses.

Bellerophon and the ChimeraHomer, Iliad; Pindar; Apollodorus

Archaic to Classical

The hero Bellerophon, tasked with killing the fire-breathing Chimera, is advised to capture Pegasus. He sleeps in Athena's temple, and she gives him a golden bridle. With it, he tames Pegasus at the spring of Pirene, and from the air, he slays the Chimera with arrows or a spear. He later uses Pegasus in other exploits. Bellerophon wins fame, but his pride later leads to his downfall.

Accounts II
The Fall of BellerophonPindar, Olympian Odes; later sources

Classical

After his successes, Bellerophon attempts to fly to Olympus on Pegasus, seeking to become a god. Zeus, angered, sends a gadfly to sting Pegasus, causing him to buck off Bellerophon, who falls to earth, crippled and doomed to wander. Pegasus continues to Olympus alone. Bellerophon is punished for hubris; Pegasus becomes a servant of Zeus, carrying his thunderbolts.

Hippocrene SpringOvid, Metamorphoses; later sources

Roman

When Pegasus strikes his hoof on Mount Helicon, he creates the Hippocrene spring, a sacred fountain of the Muses. Drinking from it inspires poets. The spring becomes a symbol of poetic inspiration, and Pegasus becomes associated with the arts.

Pegasus is always white — Ancient art often depicts him as white, but not always. In the 2010 Clash of the Titans, he is black. There is no canonical color.

Pegasus is a gentle, friendly creature — In myth, he is wild and untamable by mortals, only subdued by a divine bridle. He is not a pet.

Pegasus is a symbol of wisdom — He is more accurately a symbol of poetic inspiration and freedom, not wisdom.

Pegasus and the Unicorn are the same — They are entirely different creatures from different mythologies.
