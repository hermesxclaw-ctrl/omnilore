Who It Is

On a rainy night under an old kaya or pine, you look up and something drops — not a fruit, not a branch, but a well-bucket, a severed head, or a ball of cold fire that falls like a bucket on a rope. In the Kansai and Tōkai mountains they call it tsurube-otoshi, the thing that fishes for humans from the canopy.

Behavior is predatory and ambush-based, not social. Attested actions: waiting above a dark road or dim grove, dropping suddenly onto or in front of a passerby, laughing or speaking (Hōki: 'yagyō sunda ka, tsurube oroso ka, gii-gii' — mocking night work), catching the eye then dropping, luring with a shining object (Kuroe: koban illusion), scooping/dragging victim into bucket and hoisting into canopy, devouring/eating, then remaining absent 2-3 days before reappearing (Tera). Powers
Vertical Drop Ambush

Instantaneous plummet from canopy onto or before victim, often from kaya, pine, or other large tree. Described as 'suddenly drops' (kyū ni ochiru) with no warning except dim light or eye contact.

Limit: Requires a large, old tree over a path; not attested in open fields or young groves. In Hōki, returns to tree after drop, suggesting single-target ambush rather than area attack.

Bucket Entrapment and Aerial Hoist

Victim lured to shining object at tree base (illusory koban), then dragged into a bucket and hoisted high into the tree for torment. Bucket acts as container and lift.

Limit: Requires victim to reach for lure; not used in head-drop variants. Victim dies from fall impact after being menaced, not from bucket itself.

Anthropophagy (Human Devouring)

Consumption of humans, either by severed head feeding or by bucket-associated entity devouring victim in canopy. Tera head 'eats and feeds on someone' and then abstains 2-3 days; Ōi and Kuroe explicitly 'devour' (kurau).

Limit: Tera variant imposes refractory period of 2-3 days without reappearance after feeding, implying satiation or ritual interval. Not all variants include eating — Hōki emphasizes fright/laughing.

Yin-Fire Manifestation (Tree-Spirit Flame)

Manifestation as yin-fire (in no hi) — a cold or rainy-day flame that hangs from or rains down from large trees. Explained via gogyō: tree (wood) + rain (water) + fire = tree spirit's fire phase.

Limit: Only mature trees with fully developed ki can manifest; young trees cannot. Appears specifically on rainy days, not clear nights, as yin-fire. Not a solid body; cannot be grappled like bucket/head.

Lure and Illusion (Shining Koban)

Projection of a shining light at tree base resembling a koban (oval gold coin) to induce greed/curiosity and bring victim within bucket range.

Limit: Single attested lure type (coin); no shape-shifting into human form attested for tsurube-otoshi itself. Illusion dissipates once victim is seized.

Mocking Vocalization

Utterance of phrase 'yagyō sunda ka, tsurube oroso ka, gii-gii' (has your night work ended, shall we drop the bucket?) accompanied by sniggering/creaking laugh gii-gii, mimicking well-pulley sound.

Limit: Only attested in Hōki variant; not a universal speech ability. Suggests intelligence and mockery but no extended dialogue.

Symbols — Core symbol is the tsurube (well bucket) and by extension the well sweep (hane-tsurube) — a domestic tool made uncanny by appearing in a tree where no well exists. Associated symbols: kaya (Torreya nucifera) and old pine trees, ivy-wrapped 'eerie trees,' rain/water, yin-fire, and illusory koban (gold coin) as lure. Iconography in Edo prints: fire hanging from branch like a lantern/bucket; in modern yōkai art (Mizuki and successors): buc

Domains — Strictly arboreal and nocturnal/crepuscular. Habitats: large, old trees — kaya, pine, sugi (cryptomeria), and unnamed 'great trees' — standing over roads, village boundaries, or in places 'dim even at noon' (Kuze, Gifu). Also associated with rainy days (Kokon Hyakumonogatari Hyōban's yin-fire theory) and sunset/night work hours. Accounts I
The Kaya Tree of Hōki — 'Has Your Night Work Ended?'Kuchitanba Kōhi Shū (Collection of Oral Legends about Kuchitanba), Hōki section, Sogabe village, Kyoto Prefecture (now Kameoka)

Oral legend recorded Taishō period (1910s); transmission likely Meiji or earlier

From a kaya tree, a tsurube-oroshi suddenly drops, sniggering 'yagyō sunda ka, tsurube oroso ka, gii-gii' — asking if night work is done and proposing to drop the bucket — then rises back up above the tree. No victim is described as eaten in this telling; the emphasis is on startling sound and vertical motion. Fright and avoidance of the kaya tree at night; reinforces taboo on late night work under that tree. No death recorded in this variant.

The Severed Head Pine of TeraKuchitanba Kōhi Shū, Tera section, Sogabe village, Kyoto Prefecture

Taishō record; oral antiquity unknown

From an old pine, a tsurube-oroshi appears as a severed head (namakubi) that drops down, eats and feeds on a passerby, then does not appear for two to three days before appearing again. The interval suggests a feeding cycle. Death by devouring; temporary safety window of 2-3 days exploited by locals to pass. Establishes the most explicitly anthropophagous form.

The Ivy-Wrapped Eerie Tree of TomimotoKuchitanba Kōhi Shū, Tomimoto village, Funai District, Kyoto (now Nantan)

Taishō record

An eerie tree wrapped with ivy (tsuta) was feared because tsurube-oroshi would appear from it. No detailed attack is narrated; the tree itself is the warning — its appearance marks the yōkai's dwelling. Avoidance of the tree; local fear and oral taboo. Demonstrates habitat marking without elaborated plot.

The Human-Devouring Tree of Ōi TsuchidaKuchitanba Kōhi Shū, Tsuchida section, Ōi village, Kyoto Prefecture

Taishō record

In Tsuchida, tsurube-oroshi are said to devour humans (hito o kurau). The entry is terse, without tree species or method, but confirms anthropophagy as regional expectation. General terror; reinforces that tsurube-oroshi is lethal, not merely mischievous.

Accounts II
The Dim-at-Noon Tree of Tsukumi, KuzeOral legend from Tsukumi, Kuze village, Gifu Prefecture (now Ibigawa), cited in Shōwa yōkai compendia drawing on Tōkai field reports

Oral tradition; recorded Shōwa period in folklore collections

Above a large tree that stands in a place dim even at noon, a tsurube-oroshi appears. The darkness of the grove even by day marks the spot as otherworldly; the entity is associated with perpetual shade. Travelers learn to avoid the dim grove at midday. Emphasizes environmental omen — unnatural darkness as yōkai sign.

The Eye-Catching Bucket of HikoneOral legend from Hikone, Shiga Prefecture (Lake Biwa eastern shore)

Oral tradition; cited in modern yōkai dictionaries referencing Shiga field reports

From a tree's branches, a tsurube-oroshi catches the eye of passersby and then drops buckets (tsurube o otosu). The act of being seen triggers the drop, suggesting a gaze-mediated ambush. Fright and potential injury; bucket drop as projectile/entrapment. Illustrates bucket-specific mechanics outside Kyoto core.

The Koban Lure of Kuroe, KainanGenroku-period yōkai tale from Kuroe, Kainan, Wakayama Prefecture (transmitted as Genroku kaidan, recorded in later Shōwa compilations)

Genroku period (1688-1704) told; recorded 20th century

At the base of a certain old tree, something shines when passersby look. Thinking it a koban (gold coin), a person reaches out and is dragged into a bucket, lifted high into the tree, menaced by the tsurube-otoshi living there, then killed as they strike the ground after being dropped. The light is a lure; the bucket is a trap and elevator. Death by fall after aerial torment; warns against greed and touching shining objects at night near old trees. Most elaborate plot among tsurube-otoshi tales.

The Rainy-Day Tree Spirit FireKokon Hyakumonogatari Hyōban by Yamaoka Genrin (1686), entry 'Tsurube-oroshi'

Edo period, 1686

Tsurube-oroshi is explained as the spirit of a large tree that has become flames raining down. Using five phases (gogyō) theory, Genrin argues that ki becomes wood, fire, earth, metal, water; on rainy days (water) from trees (wood) comes fire (fire), so the phenomenon unites water-wood-fire and thus is a tree spirit. Young trees lack sufficient ki; only great trees develop the fire phase, which being yin-fire appears on rainy days. Provides Edo intellectual framework for the phenomenon; shifts from eating monster to natural-philosophical omen. Source for all later 'fire' interpretations.

Tsurube-otoshi is a well bucket that is possessed and flies around like a tsukumogami. — No source describes a well bucket becoming a tsukumogami. The bucket is a metaphor for motion (dropping like a well bucket), not an animated tool. The entity drops like a bucket or drops a bucket; it is not a bucket that lived 100 years and gained a soul. Tsukumogami interpretation is modern invention conflating tsurube-otoshi with karakasa or chōchin-obake.

Tsurube-otoshi always devours humans and is one of the most dangerous yōkai. — Only some variants devour (Tera, Ōi, Kuroe); Hōki variant only laughs and returns to tree, with no eating described. Edo literary fire has no eating at all. Lethality is regional, not universal. Modern summaries universalize the most sensational (severed head eating) to all tsurube-otoshi.

Tsurube-otoshi and tsurubebi are completely unrelated yōkai — one is a bucket monster, one is a fireball. — They share direct textual lineage: Sekien's tsurubebi (1776) is explicitly Genrin's tsurube-oroshi fire (1686) renamed. Shōwa/Heisei separation is editorial, not historical. Folklorists note they likely began as same tree-hanging light phenomenon, later diverging into bucket/head (folk concretization) and fire (literary).

The phrase 'akino hi wa tsurube-otoshi' (autumn dusk falls like a bucket) comes from the yōkai dropping quickly. — The idiom describes the rapid sunset in autumn, likening the sun's fall to a well bucket dropping — same bucket metaphor but applied to daylight, not to the yōkai. The yōkai and idiom share the bucket-drop simile independently; the idiom is not named after the yōkai's attack speed.
