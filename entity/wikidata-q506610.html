Form

Ajisukitakahikone is a Shinto kami of thunder and agriculture, a son of the great terrestrial deity Ōkuninushi (head of the earthly kunitsukami) by Tagori-hime, one of the three Munakata goddesses who guard the sea routes between Japan and the Korean peninsula. His sister is Shitateruhime (also Takahime), and he is the father of the waterfall god Takitsuhiko ('Prince Cataract') and nephew-by-marriage of the thunder/combat god Takemikazuchi. In the Kojiki he is portrayed as a close friend of the heavenly messenger Ame-no-Wakahiko, whom the high gods sent down to demand Ōkuninushi's surrender of the land in the kuni-yuzuri ('transfer of the land'). He is best remembered for two things: his unrestrained infant thunder-cries, which his mother soothed by carrying him up and down a ladder (the origin of the sound of rumbling thunder), and his violent outburst at Ame-no-Wakahiko's funeral, when — mistaken for the dead god, whose body was treated as unclean — he hacked the funeral hut to pieces with his ten-span sword, kicked the wreckage away, and flew off radiating a light that illuminated two hills and two valleys. As the tutelary deity of Kamo, his spirit was installed by Ōkuninushi in the sacred grove of Kamo in Kaduraki, a dedication recorded in the Izumo chieftain's congratulatory address; the Kamo shrines of Kyoto (Kamigamo and Shimogamo) that descend from this cult remain a UNESCO World Heritage Site. He straddles an agrarian-thunder identity: Japanese deity lists routinely describe him as 'a kami of agriculture and thunder.'

Powers

Thunder generation / control Ajisukitakahikone is classed among the thunder gods. The Kojiki explains the growling sound of thunder as the echo of his crying while his mother carried him up and down a ladder as an infant — an etiological myth, but one that firmly attaches the thundering register to him. He is listed in Category:Thunder gods and in lists of thunder/agricultural deities. Kojiki infant-crying passage; Category:Thunder gods; List of Japanese deities ('a kami of agriculture and thunder').

Radiant / luminous aspect After destroying the funeral hut, Ajisukitakahikone is described as flying off with a radiance that 'illuminated the space of two hills and two valleys' — a solar/luminous divine display that is a recurring motif for high kami in the Kojiki and that, in his legend, finally distinguishes him from the (now-corpse) Ame-no-Wakahiko he resembled. Kojiki funeral episode: 'the radiance that exuded from him was such that it illuminated the space of two hills and two valleys.'

Ten-span sword (totsuka no tsurugi) wielding In his anger he draws his ten-span sword and physically hacks the funeral hut to pieces, then kicks it away — a display of the destructive martial power that also characterises his relatives Takemikazuchi and the thunder deity Kamo Wake-ikazuchi at the Kamo shrines. Kojiki funeral episode; ten-span sword is the standard divine arm in the chronicles (cf. Izanagi's Totsuka-no-Tsurugi that slew Kagutsuchi).

Agricultural / ploughing patronage His name's 'suki' element ('spade'/'plough') and his classification as an agricultural deity link him to farming prosperity; the Kamo shrines he patronises were historically the objects of prayers for a successful rice harvest (Shimogamo's association with the annual rice-harvest prayers). List of Japanese deities; Kamo shrines article (Shimogamo associated with rice-harvest prayers); Futarasan-shrine listing as 'god of agriculture and thunder'.

Accounts

In the Kojiki, Ajisukitakahikone is frequently portrayed as a baby who could not sleep. His mother, Tagori-hime, carried him up and down a ladder in an attempt to lull him to sleep, and the sound this produced is given as the origin of the growling of thunder. His crying and screaming in infancy grew so loud that he had to be placed in a boat and sailed around the islands of Japan until he was calm — an etiological notice that ties a child-god's fussiness to the meteorology of thunder and rolling clouds racing across the archipelago.

When the heavenly messenger Ame-no-Wakahiko — who had sided with Ōkuninushi and married Shitateruhime, then killed the remonstrating pheasant and was himself struck dead by the gods' returned arrow — lay in a funeral hut (moya), his close friend Ajisukitakahikone came to pay his respects. Because he bore a strong resemblance to the dead god, the mourners mistook him for Ame-no-Wakahiko returned to life. Offended — for corpses were regarded as unclean (kegare), and to be compared with a dead person was a grave insult — Ajisukitakahikone drew his ten-span sword, hacked the funeral hut to pieces, and kicked it away. The wrecked hut landed in the land of Mino and became a mountain called Moyama ('Mourning Mountain'). Still fuming, he flew off, and his radiance illuminated two hills and two valleys. His sister Shitateruhime then composed a song revealing her brother's identity to the assembled mourners.

In the 'Congratulatory Words of the Chieftain of Izumo' (Izumo-no-Kuni-no-Miyatsuko no Kan'yogoto), the narrative of Ōkuninushi transferring control of the realm to the heavenly gods in the kuni-yuzuri includes a passage where Ōkuninushi distributes the spirits of his children to sacred groves across the land. He attaches his own peaceful spirit to a large mirror placed in the sacred grove of Ō-miwa, causes 'the spirit of his son Aji-suki-taka-hiko-ne-no-mikoto' to dwell in the sacred grove of Kamo in Kaduraki, places Kotoshironushi's spirit at Unade, and Kayanarumi's at Asuka. This is the foundation legend that installs Ajisukitakahikone as the tutelary deity (ujigami) of Kamo.

Modern Interpretations

Kamo shrines (Kamigamo & Shimogamo), UNESCO World Heritage The enduring institutional reception of Ajisukitakahikone is the Kamo shrine complex in Kyoto, which descends from the installation of his spirit in Kamo. Kamigamo and Shimogamo are among the 'Historic Monuments of Ancient Kyoto' on the UNESCO World Heritage List, and the Aoi Matsuri / Kamo no Matsuri they host is the oldest of Kyoto's three great festivals, still celebrated today. web-verified via Kamo shrines article. Mino/Moyama etiological geography Mount Moyama ('Mourning Mountain') in the land of Mino (modern Gifu Prefecture) is etymologically linked to the funeral hut (moya) whose wreckage became the mountain, fixing the myth into the Japanese landscape and place-name tradition of Mino. web-verified via Kojiki funeral episode; Kuni-yuzuri narrative. Modern deity-list and shrine popularity He appears in 'List of Japanese deities' and 'List of legendary creatures from Japan' as a kami of agriculture and thunder, and is listed among the deities at Futarasan Shrine in Nikkō (alongside Tagori-hime). These entries keep him in circulation as a recognised, widely-distributed thunder/agrarian kami. web-verified via search results (List of Japanese deities; Futarasan Shrine; List of legendary creatures from Japan). A way to hold it in your mind

The great thunder-god of Kamo wept so loudly as a baby that his mother had to sail him around the whole of Japan just to calm him down — and the boom you hear in a summer storm is, the oldest Japanese chronicle insists, nothing more than that divine infant's tantrum echoing across the islands. Then, grown, he once destroyed a funeral for a friend who looked too much like him — because being mistaken for a corpse, in a world ruled by the fear of kotodama and defilement, was an insult no god could swallow silently. This is Ajisukitakahikone: the storm-wielding, pride-violent, ever-radiant son of the great land-master Ōkuninushi, whose living shrine still guards the gates of ancient Kyoto.
