Form

No direct physical description survives in the mythological texts. As a son of the Dagda, he would have been part of the divine race of the Tuatha Dé Danann, often depicted as tall, beautiful, and radiant. Given his name's meaning, literary convention would associate him with fiery hair or a blazing, warlike aspect. This is a reconstructed hypothesis, not an attested fact.

Powers

Pyrokinesis / Fire Manipulation As a god whose name is 'Fire', it is a logical, though unattested, inference that he had command over flames. His cultic role would have been to embody the destructive and purifying aspects of fire. Reconstructed from etymology and the broader Indo-European context of fire gods. No specific limitations are recorded. No direct mythological feat of fire-wielding is attributed to him in surviving texts.

Combat Prowess Aed is explicitly described as a warrior who participates in the slaying of his brother's murderer, Corcc. This confirms his martial ability within the context of divine warfare. Cath Maige Tuired; Tochmarc Étaíne He is himself slain in combat, indicating he is not invincible. None.

Divine Kingship The name 'Aed' was the most popular royal name in early Ireland. This does not confer a 'power' in the mythological sense, but rather indicates a deep cultural association between the god, the concept of fire, and the legitimacy of the king. Behavior & Habitat

The scant sources depict Aed as a warrior-god, embroiled in the violent, honor-driven feuds of his divine family. He is a participant in the cycle of vengeance, acting as an avenger for his slain brothers. This suggests a personality defined by martial prowess and fierce family loyalty, characteristic of the early Irish aristocratic warrior ethos.

Accounts

Corcc, a god of the Tuatha Dé Danann, kills Aed. In retaliation, the Dagda, Aed's father, hunts down and kills Corcc. This story is a stark illustration of the blood-feud logic that governs the divine pantheon.

Aed is one of several sons of the Dagda who are locked in a violent struggle for power and inheritance. His death is part of a larger pattern of divine fratricide that includes the killing of Cermait and the machinations of Aengus and Bodb Derg.

Scholarly Debates
Identity as a Solar Deity vs. Underworld Deity
Aed is a solar deity, a god of light, fire, and the sun's beneficent power. ⇌ Aed is an aspect of Donn, the dark god of the dead, with his fire representing the funeral pyre and the boundary between worlds.
Evidence for A: The name's meaning, 'fire', and its use in royal inauguration rites.
Evidence for B: The name's connection to the 'dark' Donn, and the association of fire with the passage to the Otherworld in Celtic belie
Aed the God vs. Aed the Man
The mythological Aed is a distinct, if minor, deity. ⇌ The name 'Aed' was so common that the mythological figure is a conflation of historical kings and warriors.
Evidence for A: His clear inclusion in the genealogies of the Tuatha Dé Danann.
Evidence for B: The dozens of historical kings named Aed, which could have led to a 'back-formation' of a mythological figure to explain
What We Don't Know

The primary gap is the complete absence of any surviving myth where Aed is the central figure. His character is defined entirely by his genealogy and his death. The reason for his slaying by Corcc is unknown. The exact nature of his cult, if any, is a mystery. The relationship between the mythological Aed and the historical kings who bore his name is a complex, unresolved question. The potential link to Donn requires further scholarly investigation.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Aed not as a major god like Zeus or Thor, but as a 'divine cameo' — a character who exists in the background of a much larger saga. He's the kind of figure a modern fantasy author would use to add depth to a world: a dead god, a legendary ancestor, a name that carries the weight of fire and royalty. He is a ghost in the archive, a name that fueled a thousand kings, and a single spark of mythology that never grew into a flame of its own.
