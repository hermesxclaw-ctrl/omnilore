Who It Is

In the annals of ancient Ireland, the name 'Aed' blazed like a wildfire across a thousand years of kings, saints, and warriors. Yet the god behind that fiery name is one of the most elusive figures in the Celtic pantheon — a divine son of the great Dagda, whose entire story survives in a single, brutal episode of murder and revenge. He is the patron saint of a thousand namesakes, a god of fire who may have been the dark king of the dead, and a reminder that in the oldest myths, even the gods burn brightly and briefly.

The scant sources depict Aed as a warrior-god, embroiled in the violent, honor-driven feuds of his divine family. He is a participant in the cycle of vengeance, acting as an avenger for his slain brothers. This suggests a personality defined by martial prowess and fierce family loyalty, characteristic of the early Irish aristocratic warrior ethos.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Proto-Celtic *aidus, meaning 'fire'. Cognate with the Welsh 'Aeddan', the Irish 'Aodh', and the Latin 'aedes' (temple, hearth). The name is a direct linguistic descendant of the Proto-Indo-European root *h₂eydʰ- ('to burn').

Pronunciation — AY-eth (Modern Irish: /eːd̪ˠ/; Old Irish: /aið/). The 'dh' in the anglicized 'Aedh' is a lenited 'd', producing a 'y' or 'th' sound depending on dialect.

Aliases — Áed, Aodh, Aod, Hugh (anglicized), Áed mac Lir (patronymic)

Ownership — Celtic (primarily Irish and Scottish Gaelic), with strong presence in early medieval Irish literature and genealogy. The name and cult are deeply intertwined with the god of the un

Powers
Pyrokinesis / Fire Manipulation

As a god whose name is 'Fire', it is a logical, though unattested, inference that he had command over flames. His cultic role would have been to embody the destructive and purifying aspects of fire.

Limit: No specific limitations are recorded.

Combat Prowess

Aed is explicitly described as a warrior who participates in the slaying of his brother's murderer, Corcc. This confirms his martial ability within the context of divine warfare.

Limit: He is himself slain in combat, indicating he is not invincible.

Divine Kingship

The name 'Aed' was the most popular royal name in early Ireland. This does not confer a 'power' in the mythological sense, but rather indicates a deep cultural association between the god, the concept of fire, and the legitimacy of the king.

Limit: N/A

Form & Signs

Appearance — No direct physical description survives in the mythological texts. As a son of the Dagda, he would have been part of the divine race of the Tuatha Dé Danann, often depicted as tall, beautiful, and radiant. Given his name's meaning, literary convention would associate him with fiery hair or a blazing, warlike aspect. This is a reconstructed hypothesis, not an attested fact.

Symbols — Fire itself is his primary symbol. The bonfire, the hearth, and the flame of the warrior's torch. His name was also a potent symbol of sovereignty, worn by dozens of kings, suggesting a divine endorsement of royal power.

Domains — Fire, the sun (as a solar aspect), warfare, the underworld (via his association with Donn), and kingship. His name and its royal usage tie him directly to the sacred fire of the king's hearth and the blazing spirit of the warrior.

Accounts I
The Slaying of CorccCath Maige Tuired

Mythological (pre-Christian)

Corcc, a god of the Tuatha Dé Danann, kills Aed. In retaliation, the Dagda, Aed's father, hunts down and kills Corcc. This story is a stark illustration of the blood-feud logic that governs the divine pantheon. The Dagda's vengeance, and the cycle of violence it represents, is a central theme in the mythology of the Tuatha Dé Danann.

Accounts II
The Fraternal Feuds of the Dagda's SonsTochmarc Étaíne, Cath Maige Tuired

Mythological (pre-Christian)

Aed is one of several sons of the Dagda who are locked in a violent struggle for power and inheritance. His death is part of a larger pattern of divine fratricide that includes the killing of Cermait and the machinations of Aengus and Bodb Derg. This internal conflict weakens the Tuatha Dé Danann and sets the stage for their eventual retreat into the sídhe (fairy mounds).

Aed was a major, widely worshipped god like the Dagda or Lugh. — Aed is a minor figure. He appears in a few genealogies and one myth of his death. There is no evidence of a major cult center or a cycle of myths dedicated to him. His importance is primarily etymological and onomastic (the study of names).

Aed is the same as the Scottish 'Aodh', the fairy king. — The Scottish Aodh (or Aoidh) is a figure from later folklore, often associated with the sìdhe and specific fairy hills. While the name is the same, there is no direct mythological link to the Irish Aed. They are separate figures who share a name.

Aed had a cult with specific rituals and temples. — There is zero direct evidence for a cult of Aed. The Dindsenchas mentions places named after him, but this is a common toponymic practice and does not prove a cult site.
