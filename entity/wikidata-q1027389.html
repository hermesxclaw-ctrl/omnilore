Who It Is

In the Yoruba world, twins are not merely children but divine beings, and when one dies, the living twin's counterpart is carved in wood to become a cherished, living presence in the home. The Ibeji figures, as they are known, are among the most emotionally charged and visually striking objects in African art, embodying a profound dialogue between the living and the dead, the human and the divine.

The Ibeji figure is treated as a living being: it is fed, bathed, dressed, and spoken to. It is believed to have its own personality and will, and can bring good fortune or misfortune to the family depending on how it is treated. The figure is often consulted in divination and may be used in rituals to protect the surviving twin.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The term 'Ibeji' is derived from Yoruba, meaning 'born twice' (bi = to give birth, eji = two). It refers both to the twin pair and to the carved figures representing them.

Pronunciation — ee-BEH-jee

Aliases — Ibejí, Ibeyí, Jimaguas, Taiwo and Kehinde (individual names), Ere Ibeji (carved figures)

Ownership — Yoruba people of Nigeria and Benin, and their diasporic communities in the Americas (especially Cuba, Brazil, and the Caribbean).

Names are cult layers, not one fixed label.

Powers
Protection of twins

Ibeji are believed to protect twins, both living and deceased, from harm and evil forces.

Limit: The protection is conditional on proper veneration and care of the Ibeji figure.

Bringers of fortune

Ibeji can bring good luck, wealth, and prosperity to the family if they are honored properly.

Limit: Neglect can result in misfortune, illness, or even death.

Intercession with Shango

As children of Shango, the Ibeji can intercede with the Orisha of thunder and lightning on behalf of their devotees.

Limit: This power is specific to the mythological association with Shango.

Divination

The Ibeji figure may be used in divination rituals to communicate with the spirit of the deceased twin and gain insight into the future.

Limit: This practice is performed by a babalawo or other ritual specialist.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Ibeji figures are usually carved from wood, typically between 15 to 30 centimeters tall. They depict a human figure, often with the distinctive features of the deceased twin's sex: male figures may have a hairstyle with a crest, while female figures may have braids or a head tie. The figures are often adorned with beads, cowrie shells, coins, and paint, and are dressed in clothing that reflects the status of the parents. The faces are often seren

Symbols — Cowrie shells represent wealth and the sea, linking the twins to the Orisha Yemoja and Olokun. Beads of various colors may indicate the family's lineage or the twin's own preferences. The figures may be placed on altars or in shrines, and are often kept in a small container or hung on the wall. The number two is significant, and the figures are often displayed in pairs even if only one twin has died, to represent the unity of the twins.

Domains — The Ibeji figures are found in domestic shrines within Yoruba households. They are also present in public shrines dedicated to the Orisha, and in the context of the diaspora, they are incorporated into Santería and Candomblé temples. The figures are also collected in museums and private collections worldwide.

Accounts I
The Myth of Taiwo and KehindeOral tradition, recorded in various ethnographic works

Mythic origin

The twins Taiwo and Kehinde are believed to be inseparable companions. Before birth, they decide that Taiwo (the firstborn) will go first to see if the world is a good place. Kehinde, the elder, waits behind. If Taiwo does not return, Kehinde knows the world is not fit and may choose not to be born. This explains the special bond and the reverence for twins. This myth establishes the sacred nature of twins and the belief that one twin is the elder even if born second.

Accounts II
The Ritual of the Carved IbejiEthnographic accounts (e.g., Verger, Chemeche)

Contemporary traditional practice

When a twin dies, the parents commission a babalawo to carve a wooden figure to represent the deceased. The figure is consecrated and becomes the dwelling place of the twin's spirit. The parents care for the figure as if it were alive, dressing it, feeding it, and consulting it in times of need. The ritual ensures the well-being of the surviving twin and the family, and maintains the bond between the living and the dead.

The Ibeji in the DiasporaStudies of Santería and Candomblé

Post-16th century

Enslaved Yoruba people brought their beliefs to the Americas, where the Ibeji were syncretized with Catholic saints Cosmas and Damian. In Cuba, they are known as Ibeyí or Jimaguas, and are celebrated with elaborate festivals. In Brazil, they are associated with the orishas known as the Ibeji in Candomblé. The Ibeji tradition evolved and adapted, incorporating new elements while preserving core Yoruba beliefs.
