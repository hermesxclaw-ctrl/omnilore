The Approach

In the Yoruba world, twins are not merely children but divine beings, and when one dies, the living twin's counterpart is carved in wood to become a cherished, living presence in the home. The Ibeji figures, as they are known, are among the most emotionally charged and visually striking objects in African art, embodying a profound dialogue between the living and the dead, the human and the divine.

Content advisory. This entry discusses cultural beliefs and practices surrounding twin mortality in Yoruba tradition. Some readers may find the subject of infant death and its ritual commemoration sensitive.
Identity

Ibeji

Pronunciation ee-BEH-jee

Known As

IbejíIbeyíJimaguasTaiwo and Kehinde (individual names)Ere Ibeji (carved figures)

Etymology

The term 'Ibeji' is derived from Yoruba, meaning 'born twice' (bi = to give birth, eji = two). It refers both to the twin pair and to the carved figures representing them.

Cultural Ownership

Yoruba people of Nigeria and Benin, and their diasporic communities in the Americas (especially Cuba, Brazil, and the Caribbean).

Disputed Identifications

In Latin American Santería/Lucumí, Ibeji are syncretized with Saints Cosmas and Damian, but this is a colonial-era overlay; some practitioners emphasize the Orisha nature over the Catholic association.

Description

Appearance

Ibeji figures are usually carved from wood, typically between 15 to 30 centimeters tall. They depict a human figure, often with the distinctive features of the deceased twin's sex: male figures may have a hairstyle with a crest, while female figures may have braids or a head tie. The figures are often adorned with beads, cowrie shells, coins, and paint, and are dressed in clothing that reflects the status of the parents. The faces are often serene and stylized, with attention to scarification marks or other ethnic markers.

Symbols & Iconography

Cowrie shells represent wealth and the sea, linking the twins to the Orisha Yemoja and Olokun. Beads of various colors may indicate the family's lineage or the twin's own preferences. The figures may be placed on altars or in shrines, and are often kept in a small container or hung on the wall. The number two is significant, and the figures are often displayed in pairs even if only one twin has died, to represent the unity of the twins.

Behaviour & Personality

The Ibeji figure is treated as a living being: it is fed, bathed, dressed, and spoken to. It is believed to have its own personality and will, and can bring good fortune or misfortune to the family depending on how it is treated. The figure is often consulted in divination and may be used in rituals to protect the surviving twin.

Domains & Habitats

The Ibeji figures are found in domestic shrines within Yoruba households. They are also present in public shrines dedicated to the Orisha, and in the context of the diaspora, they are incorporated into Santería and Candomblé temples. The figures are also collected in museums and private collections worldwide.

Version Differences

Regional variations exist in the carving style and ritual practices. For example, in some areas, the figures are more abstract, while in others they are more naturalistic. In the diaspora, the Ibeji are syncretized with Catholic saints, and their veneration may incorporate Catholic elements such as candles and prayers. In Cuba, the Ibeji are also identified with the orishas known as the Jimaguas, and are associated with the color red and green.

Stories & Myths

The Myth of Taiwo and Kehinde

Source Oral tradition, recorded in various ethnographic worksCulture YorubaPeriod Mythic origin

Summary

The twins Taiwo and Kehinde are believed to be inseparable companions. Before birth, they decide that Taiwo (the firstborn) will go first to see if the world is a good place. Kehinde, the elder, waits behind. If Taiwo does not return, Kehinde knows the world is not fit and may choose not to be born. This explains the special bond and the reverence for twins.

Participants

Taiwo

Kehinde

The world

Consequences

This myth establishes the sacred nature of twins and the belief that one twin is the elder even if born second.

Variants

Some versions say that Taiwo is the scout, and Kehinde is the wise one; others emphasize that the firstborn is the one who takes risks.

The Ritual of the Carved Ibeji

Source Ethnographic accounts (e.g., Verger, Chemeche)Culture YorubaPeriod Contemporary traditional practice

Summary

When a twin dies, the parents commission a babalawo to carve a wooden figure to represent the deceased. The figure is consecrated and becomes the dwelling place of the twin's spirit. The parents care for the figure as if it were alive, dressing it, feeding it, and consulting it in times of need.

Participants

Parents

Babalawo

Deceased twin

Ibeji figure

Consequences

The ritual ensures the well-being of the surviving twin and the family, and maintains the bond between the living and the dead.

Variants

In some regions, the figure is carved from a specific wood, such as iroko, and may be adorned with specific colors or materials.

The Ibeji in the Diaspora

Source Studies of Santería and CandombléCulture Afro-Cuban and Afro-BrazilianPeriod Post-16th century

Summary

Enslaved Yoruba people brought their beliefs to the Americas, where the Ibeji were syncretized with Catholic saints Cosmas and Damian. In Cuba, they are known as Ibeyí or Jimaguas, and are celebrated with elaborate festivals. In Brazil, they are associated with the orishas known as the Ibeji in Candomblé.

Participants

Enslaved Africans

Catholic Church

Practitioners of Santería and Candomblé

Consequences

The Ibeji tradition evolved and adapted, incorporating new elements while preserving core Yoruba beliefs.

Variants

In Cuba, the Ibeji are often depicted as childlike figures and are associated with the color red and green. In Brazil, they are sometimes linked to the orisha twins known as the Ibeji.

Infant mortality and twin death

Source Ethnographic and historical studies

Description

The high rate of infant mortality in pre-modern Yoruba society meant that the death of one or both twins was a common occurrence. The Ibeji tradition arose as a way to cope with this tragedy, providing a means to continue the bond with the deceased child.

Scholarly Context

The practice should be understood within its cultural context, not as a morbid curiosity but as a profound expression of love and resilience.

Colonial suppression

Source Missionary records and colonial archives

Description

During the colonial period, European missionaries and administrators often suppressed African religious practices, including the Ibeji cult. They viewed it as idolatry and sought to replace it with Christianity.

Scholarly Context

This suppression led to the syncretism seen in the diaspora, where African deities were hidden behind Catholic saints.

Transatlantic slave trade

Source Historical studies of the slave trade

Description

The forced migration of millions of Africans to the Americas resulted in the transplantation of Yoruba religious beliefs, including the Ibeji tradition, to the New World. This was a traumatic process, but it also led to the survival and adaptation of these traditions.

Scholarly Context

The Ibeji tradition in the diaspora is a testament to the resilience of African culture in the face of unimaginable suffering.

Contradictions & Disputes

Elder twin designation

Position A

The second twin (Kehinde) is considered the elder.

Position A — evidence

Yoruba oral tradition and ethnographic accounts state that Kehinde is the elder because Taiwo was sent to scout the world.

Position B

The first twin (Taiwo) is the elder by birth order.

Position B — evidence

Western biological perspective assumes the firstborn is the elder.

Scholarly Consensus

Yoruba cultural logic prevails in the tradition; scholars acknowledge the cultural relativity of this designation.

Parentage of the Ibeji

Position A

The Ibeji are the children of Shango.

Position A — evidence

Myths and prayers often invoke Shango as the father of twins.

Position B

The Ibeji are not directly the children of Shango but are under his protection.

Position B — evidence

Some sources suggest that twins are simply sacred to Shango, not his offspring.

Scholarly Consensus

The exact parentage is debated; most accept a close association with Shango.

Syncretism with Catholic saints

Position A

The Ibeji are fully identified with Saints Cosmas and Damian in Santería.

Position A — evidence

In many Cuban households, the Ibeji are honored on the feast day of Cosmas and Damian.

Position B

The syncretism is superficial and the Ibeji retain their distinct Orisha identity.

Position B — evidence

Practitioners often distinguish between the saints and the orishas, using the saints as a cover.

Scholarly Consensus

The syncretism is historically real but the underlying Yoruba identity remains primary.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

Further research is needed into the contemporary practice of the Ibeji cult in Nigeria and the diaspora, including interviews with practitioners. The specific rituals and beliefs vary by region, and this dossier does not capture all local variations. The role of Ibeji in gender dynamics and family structures could be explored more deeply. The provenance of many Ibeji figures in Western collections is often undocumented, raising ethical questions about colonial acquisition. The impact of modernization and globalization on the tradition remains an open question.

Research Scope

This dossier focuses on the Yoruba Ibeji tradition as documented in ethnographic, art-historical, and religious studies literature up to the research cutoff. It covers origins, cultural context, ritual practice, artistic representation, and diaspora adaptations, with attention to regional variations and scholarly debates.
