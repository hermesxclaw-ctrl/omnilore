Belphegor tempts men with wealth, promising them riches without labor, and through idleness leads the soul to its ruin.

Who It Is

Belphegor (or Belfagor) is the demonic embodiment of sloth and ingenious laziness — a devil who tempts humanity not through grand evil but through the promise of effortless wealth and idle comfort. His origins trace to the Moabite god Baal-Peor, whose worship at Mount Peor in the Book of Numbers (25:1-3) involved ritual prostitution that led Israel into apostasy. The name 'Belphegor' derives from Baal-Peor, literally 'Lord of the Opening' — a reference to the mountain's cleft where the cult operated and to the orifice symbolism associated with the deity. The early Church Fathers transformed this local Canaanite deity into a demon, but Belphegor's distinctive character emerged in medieval demonology. He was classified as one of the seven princes of Hell, presiding over the sin of sloth (acedia) — but with a peculiar twist. Unlike the spiritual despair of standard acedia, Belphegor promotes sloth through material comfort: he seduces humans with the promise of wealth obtained without labor, inventions that make work unnecessary, and the pleasures of idle luxury. The Dictionnaire Infernal (Collin de Plancy, 1818) describes Belphegor as appearing as a young, attractive woman or as a demon seated upon a toilet. The latter image — revolting and scatological — connects to his association with filth, waste, and the degradation of dignity that accompanies spiritual sloth. A separate Italian tradition, most famously told by Machiavelli in his novella Belfagor arcidiavolo (The Devil Takes a Wife), portrays Belphegor as a demon dispatched to earth to investigate the complaints of damned souls who blame their wives for their ruin. He marries a Florentine woman, is driven to despair by her demands, and flees back to Hell preferring damnation to matrimony. This comic tale established Belphegor as a figure of misogynistic satire as well as demonic power. Peter Binsfeld's 1589 classification of demons associated Belphegor with the sin of sloth, and subsequent grimoires elaborated his appearance: a man with shaggy hair and a phallic mouth, emitting the stench of decay. Belphegor represents a uniquely modern-seeming evil — not the grand rebellion of Lucifer but the quiet, comfortable corruption that comes from choosing ease over effort, wealth over worth, and idleness over engagement with the divine.

Form

Belphegor's most famous visual depiction is from Louis Breton's 1863 engraving for the Dictionnaire Infernal: a heavy, monstrous figure seated on a toilet-like throne, with a gaping, wide mouth, horns, a bloated body, and a long tail. This image — the 'demon on the toilet' — has become his iconic representation, blending the concepts of sloth (inactivity), gluttony (consumption and excretion), and the 'lord of opening' etymology. In other traditions, he appears as a beautiful young woman (his 'seductive' form for tempting through discoveries), a bearded man with bat wings, or a shapeshifting entity whose form adapts to the temptation he offers. In the Shin Megami Tensei/Persona games, Belphegor is consistently depicted seated on a toilet, reading a newspaper or appearing in a state of torpor, emphasizing the sloth aspect with dark, sometimes comedic imagery. His color palette tends toward purples, browns, and deep reds.

Powers

Gift of Invention and Discovery Belphegor's signature power: he grants humans brilliant ideas, ingenious inventions, and the ability to make discoveries. The recipient suddenly 'sees' the solution to a problem, conceives of a revolutionary device, or stumbles upon a breakthrough. This is not artificial inspiration — the idea is genuine and workable. The corruption lies not in the idea itself but in the shortcut: the recipient achieves without earning, invents without understanding, discovers without the intellectual journey that gives discovery meaning. Dictionnaire Infernal (1818); various grimoires; Kabbalistic writings The power is strongest in April (according to De Plancy). The inventions and discoveries, while real, come with a spiritual cost — the recipient's soul or moral integrity is compromised. The ideas granted may also lead to wealth that corrupts the recipient and sows discord among others. Whether the ideas are genuinely Belphegor's own genius or stolen from human minds assigned to other tasks is unclear. Some interpretations suggest he is a facilitator rather than a creator — he removes the mental barriers that prevent people from accessing their own latent creativity, but takes credit (and payment) for doing so.

Wealth Distribution and Economic Corruption Belphegor bestows wealth on his supplicants. This is not illusory treasure (a common demonic trick) but real, spendable wealth that transforms the recipient's material circumstances. The wealth is used not for happiness but as a tool of corruption: it makes the recipient lazy (sloth), inspires envy in others, and creates the conditions for conflict and discord. In Kabbalistic writings, he is described as using wealth distribution as his primary method for sowing 'dispute between people.' Kabbalistic texts; Dictionnaire Infernal; demonological tradition The wealth must be spent or distributed to create its corrupting effects. Hoarded wealth may corrupt the hoarder alone; distributed wealth corrupts communities. The wealth cannot create genuine satisfaction — the recipient will always want more, a classic demonic trap. Some traditions distinguish between Belphegor's 'gift' wealth (which corrupts) and wealth earned through honest labor (which does not). The line between the two can be difficult to discern, which is itself part of the temptation.

Inducement of Sloth and Spiritual Apathy As the demon of Sloth, Belphegor's core power is the erosion of motivation, ambition, and spiritual vigilance. He doesn't force inactivity — he makes it appealing. His influence manifests as procrastination, the 'I'll do it tomorrow' mindset, the gradual abandonment of goals and disciplines, and the replacement of active engagement with passive consumption. In the theological framework, this is a mortal sin because it deadens the soul to both virtue and vice — the slothful person doesn't even commit interesting sins, they simply stop caring. Binsfeld's Classification of Demons (1589); Christian demonology Strong-willed, purpose-driven, or spiritually disciplined individuals are largely immune. Community, accountability, and structure are natural countermeasures — Belphegor thrives in isolation and unstructured time. The power is gradual and cumulative rather than instantaneous. The Lanterne of Light's classification of Belphegor as the demon of Gluttony rather than Sloth suggests the power set overlaps — gluttony (overconsumption, the sin of too-much) and sloth (under-action, the sin of too-little) are arguably two sides of the same coin: the corruption of desire and will.

Discord Sowing / Disputation In Kabbalistic texts, Belphegor is described as 'the disputer' and 'the quarreler,' an enemy of the sefira Tiferet (Beauty/Harmony). He disrupts harmony and sows discord among people, particularly through arguments over wealth, resources, and status. His method is not direct conflict but the subtle introduction of envy, resentment, and competitive grievance. He turns communities against themselves. Kabbalistic writings (Zohar and related texts) Harmonious, well-governed, or spiritually aligned communities are resistant. The discord must find existing cracks in relationships to exploit — Belphegor cannot create division where there is genuine unity. His power is strongest in communities already struggling with inequality or resentment. This power seems at odds with his role as sloth demon: quarreling requires energy and engagement. The resolution may be that Belphegor uses the wealth he distributes to create idle resentment in others — the slothful recipients become objects of envy, and the envious become quarrelsome. He corrupts both the wealthy (through sloth) and the poor (through envy).

Demonic Diplomacy / Ambassadorial Authority According to De Plancy, Belphegor is Hell's ambassador to France — suggesting a formal role in the infernal hierarchy as a diplomat and negotiator. This power is organizational rather than personal: Belphegor represents Hell's interests in a specific geopolitical domain, coordinating demonic activity and temptation strategies. As an ambassador, he presumably has immunity, authority, and access beyond that of a common demon. Dictionnaire Infernal (1818); De Plancy His authority is geographically limited to France (in De Plancy's account). His enemy is explicitly Mary Magdalene, patron saint of France, suggesting a cosmic checks-and-balances system where each demon-ambassador faces a countervailing holy patron. Victor Hugo repeated De Plancy's claim in Toilers of the Sea (1866), lending it literary credibility, but no earlier source for Belphegor-as-ambassador has been identified. The claim may be De Plancy's original invention, though it has been widely accepted into demonological lore.

Behavior & Habitat

Belphegor tempts not through aggression or fear, but through the promise of effortless reward. He offers wealth without work, genius without study, discoveries without research, inventions without labor. He is the voice that says 'there's an easier way.' This makes him arguably more insidious than demons who tempt through overt vice: Belphegor corrupts by making excellence and achievement feel attainable without effort, thereby destroying the very drive that makes human accomplishment meaningful. In the Machiavelli novella, his behavior is different — he is a demon on an assignment, trying to understand human marriage, and his behavior is more comic-pathetic than predatory. He is overwhelmed by human domestic chaos, suggesting that even Hell's ambassadors have limits. In Kabbalistic writings, he is described as 'the disputer' or 'the quarreler' — a demon who sows discord through argument and division, using wealth distribution as his primary tool for creating envy and conflict.

Accounts

While Israel is camped on the plains of Moab at Shittim, the men begin to engage in sexual relations with Moabite women, who invite them to the sacrifices of their god Baal-Peor. The Israelites 'yoke themselves' to the god, a phrase suggesting both religious and sexual union. Yahweh's anger is kindled, and a plague breaks out among the people. At the height of the crisis, an Israelite man named Zimri brings a Midianite woman named Cozbi into the camp in full view of the weeping congregation. Phinehas, grandson of Aaron, takes a spear, follows them into the tent, and drives it through both of them. The plague stops, but 24,000 have already died. Yahweh rewards Phinehas with a 'covenant of peace' and a perpetual priesthood. This incident becomes the defining example of apostasy through foreign seduction in Israelite memory, referenced repeatedly throughout the Hebrew Bible.

Pluto, king of Hell, is troubled by the number of damned souls claiming their wives drove them to sin. He dispatches the archdevil Belfagor to earth with 100,000 ducats and instructions to take a wife and report back on whether marriage is truly the cause of so much damnation. Belfagor, in human guise as Roderigo of Castile, marries the beautiful but demanding Onesta Donati of Florence. She drives him into catastrophic debt with her extravagant tastes, and he is soon fleeing from creditors. He takes refuge with a peasant, Gianmatteo, who shelters him. In gratitude, Belfagor helps Gianmatteo earn money by possessing wealthy women and then allowing Gianmatteo to exorcise him for a fee. Eventually, the scheme backfires when Belfagor is summoned to exorcise a woman who is actually the king of France's daughter, and he must flee from the royal court. In the end, Belfagor returns to Hell, reporting that marriage is indeed worse than any infernal torment. This story is unusual in demonology: it is a comedy in which the demon is the pursued rather than the pursuer, and human vices (especially female extravagance, in Machiavelli's misogynistic telling) prove more terrifying than Hell itself.

In Milton's epic, Belphegor is mentioned briefly but significantly among the fallen angels — the pagan gods who seduced humanity after the Fall. Milton lists 'Belial' and 'Peor' (a shortened form of Baal-Peor/Belphegor) among the demons who will later become the false gods of the nations. This places Belphegor in the grand Miltonic cosmology of the war in Heaven and the fall of the rebel angels. In Milton's telling, the pagan gods are not merely imaginary — they are the fallen angels themselves, who, after their defeat, wandered the earth and tricked humanity into worshipping them under various names. Belphegor is thus an angel who fought against God, was cast into Hell, and then rose to earth to become the god of Peor, seducing the Israelites and countless others into idolatry and sexual sin.

Field Notes

⛧ Belphegor is the only demon with a prime number named after him: 1,000,000,000,000,066,600,000,000,000,001 — a palindromic prime with 666 at its center, known as 'Belphegor's prime.'

⛧ Machiavelli, best known for 'The Prince' — the ruthless manual of statecraft — also wrote a comedy about Belphegor being defeated by his human wife. It suggests the author of Realpolitik found domestic life more terrifying than Hell.

⛧ In the Persona/SMT games, Belphegor is always depicted sitting on a toilet reading a newspaper — a design so memorable that it has become his default pop-culture iconography, all traceable back to a single 1863 engraving.

⛧ The Polish PZL M-15 'Belphegor' was a crop-dusting plane so ugly, noisy, and impractical that it was named after the demon. It remains the world's only jet-powered agricultural aircraft, and only 175 were ever built.

⛧ Belphegor's powers are supposedly strongest in April — just in time for people to abandon their New Year's resolutions. The symbolism writes itself.

⛧ In Kabbalistic tradition, Belphegor is the enemy of 'Tiferet' — the sefira of Beauty and Harmony — making him a demon of discord as well as sloth. He doesn't just make you lazy; he makes you quarrelsome about the things you're too lazy to fix.

⛧ The French TV miniseries Belphégor (1965), about a phantom haunting the Louvre, was so popular in France that it inspired a 2001 film starring Sophie Marceau and created a lasting association between the name Belphégor and French Gothic mystery.

Scholarly Debates
Is Belphegor the demon of Sloth or of Gluttony?
Belphegor is the demon of Sloth (Acedia), as classified by Peter Binsfeld (1589). He tempts through laziness, spiritual apathy, and the seduction of easy solutions. His toilet-throne iconography reinforces this: the demon who sits rather than acts. ⇌ Belphegor is the demon of Gluttony (Gula), as classified by the Lanterne of Light (c. 1410). His origins in the Peor cult (which involved feasting on sacrificial meals) and his association with overconsumption and excess align him with gluttony, not sloth.

What We Don't Know

The precise relationship between the historical cult of Baal-Peor (what actually happened at Mount Peor in Moab) and the biblical account is archaeologically unresolvable — no extra-biblical texts from the Peor cult survive. The transition from Moabite deity to Christian demon — the precise intellectual history of how Baal-Peor became Belphegor — has not been comprehensively traced across the Church Fathers and early medieval demonology. The relationship between Belphegor's assigned sin (Sloth) and his actual portfolio (wealth, inventions, discord) suggests there may have been intermediate classification systems that have been lost. The iconographic evolution from the Breton engraving (1863) to the SMT/Persona toilet-demon (1990s-present) — a direct line of visual influence — has not been formally documented in art history. The specificity of Belphegor as 'Hell's ambassador to France' is attested only in De Plancy and Hugo; whether this reflects an older folk tradition or is a 19th-century invention is unknown.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Belphegor as the anti-Muse. The classical Muses inspire artists to create, to labor, to bring something new into the world through effort and craft. Belphegor does the opposite: he gives you the idea without the work, the genius without the discipline, the discovery without the journey. And because you didn't earn it, you don't value it — so you never finish it. He is the demonic patron of potential energy that never becomes kinetic. He is why your screenplay is still on page three, why your invention exists only as a sketch on a napkin, why your novel is permanently 'in progress.' He doesn't destroy your creativity; he puts it on a shelf and lets it gather dust. And the most insidious part? You never blame him. You blame yourself — for being lazy, for being undisciplined, for not being good enough. That's the real trap of sloth: it disguises itself as a personal failure when it's actually a spiritual one. Belphegor is not just the demon on the toilet. He's the demon who convinced you that the toilet is where you belong.
