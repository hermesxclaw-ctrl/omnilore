Who It Is

Telmius never speaks in the Odyssey, never draws a bow, never begs for mercy - he is one name in a long catalogue of doomed young men who ate a king's cattle, drank his wine, and pressed his wife to choose a new husband while his house stood empty. His entire existence hangs on a single line in Apollodorus.

No individual personality, speech, or motive is preserved. By collective characterization in Homer (Odyssey 1-2, 20-22) and Apollodorus, the suitors as a group are described as hybristai: they occupy Odysseus' oikos without invitation, consume his livestock and wine, pressure Penelope to remarry, insult guests (including Odysseus disguised as beggar), plot to murder Telemachus, and violate xenia (guest-host reciprocity). Whether Telmius individually participated in each act is unattested but assumed by inclusion in the group. No dissenting or pious suitor tradition attaches to Telmius

Mortal Aristocratic Status

As a mnestēr from Dulichium, Telmius is by status a free, noble-born mortal youth with the social right to seek marriage with Penelope and, by extension, claim to the Ithacan kingship. This is a social/legal capacity, not a supernatural power. No lineage, parentage, or patron deity is recorded.

Limit: Mortal; subject to hunger, wounds, death; no divine protection is mentioned. His claim is contingent on Penelope's choice and on outlasting rival suitors. No evidence of kingship, priesthood, or military command.

Collective Impunity and Hall Privilege (Group Ability)

While lodged in Odysseus' hall, suitors collectively exercise de facto power to consume the estate's resources, command servants, and intimidate the household, enabled by Penelope's delaying tactics and Telemachus' youth and by their numerical strength. No individual feat is credited to Telmius.

Limit: Conditional and temporary; ends immediately upon Odysseus' return and the stringing of the bow. Violates xenia and thus incurs divine disfavor (Athena, Zeus). Reversed in Mnesterophonia where all suitors are killed by Odysseus with Telemachus, Eumaeus, and Philoetius.

Accounts I
The Catalogue of Suitors from DulichiumApollodorus, Bibliotheca, Epitome 7.26

1st-2nd century CE (codifying 8th century BCE epic tradition)

Apollodorus lists those who courted Penelope in Odysseus' absence. From Dulichium came 52 men, including Telmius, alongside 24 from Same, 20 from Zacynthus, and 12 from Ithaca itself. The list preserves names otherwise lost, functioning as a mythographic roster rather than a narrative episode. Telmius appears only here, with no patronymic, epithet, or deed. Establishes Telmius as one of the canonical suitors; provides the sole attestation of his name; enables later scholars to count and compare Homeric vs. mythographic totals.

The Occupation of Odysseus' HouseHomer, Odyssey Books 1-2, 16-20; summarized in Apollodorus Epitome 7.26-32

Archaic (Homer) / Imperial (Apollodorus)

During Odysseus' twenty-year absence (ten at Troy, ten wandering), young nobles from Dulichium and neighboring islands settle in his palace on Ithaca. They feast daily on his cattle, sheep, goats, and wine, compel his servants, and press Penelope to declare Odysseus dead and choose a new husband. Penelope delays by weaving and unweaving a shroud for Laertes. Though Telmius is not individually named in Homer, as a Dulichium suitor he is narratively present in this collective occupation. Creates the moral and legal justification for the suitors' eventual killing as punishment for violation of xenia and oikos; establishes the suitors as hybristai in Greek ethical terms.

The Plot Against TelemachusHomer, Odyssey 4.660-672, 16.342-406

Archaic

When Telemachus sails to Pylos and Sparta to seek news of Odysseus, the suitors, led by Antinous, plan to ambush and murder him on his return, stationing a ship at Asteris. The plot fails when Telemachus evades them with Athena's help. The episode demonstrates the suitors' escalation from parasitism to attempted murder. Telmius, as part of the collective, is complicit by membership, though no individual role is assigned. Deepens guilt of suitors; triggers divine support for Odysseus' vengeance; foreshadows Mnesterophonia.

Accounts II
The Contest of the BowHomer, Odyssey Book 21; Apollodorus Epitome 7.32

Archaic / Imperial

Penelope, prompted by Athena and Odysseus (still disguised as beggar), proposes to marry whoever strings Odysseus' great bow and shoots through twelve axe heads. Leodes fails first; other suitors, including Eurymachus, strain and fail; Antinous suggests postponing. Telemachus nearly succeeds but is stopped by Odysseus' signal. The disguised Odysseus then asks to try, despite suitor protests, and succeeds effortlessly. Telmius is not singled out as attempting the bow, but as a suitor present in the hall he is among those who fail or watch the failure. Reveals Odysseus' identity and rightful kingship through the heirloom weapon; marks the suitors as unworthy successors; sets up the slaughter.

The Mnesterophonia - Slaughter of the SuitorsHomer, Odyssey Book 22; Apollodorus, Epitome 7.33

Archaic / Imperial

Immediately after the bow contest, Odysseus, joined by Telemachus, Eumaeus, and Philoetius, slaughters the suitors in the hall. Antinous falls first, shot through the throat as he drinks. Eurymachus is killed next while pleading. The rest are cut down with swords and spears after Telemachus secures arms. Apollodorus states laconically: 'Odysseus with Telemachus and Eumaeus and Philoetius slew the suitors.' Telmius is included in this collective death; no individual death scene, last words, or burial detail is preserved for him. Ends the suitors' occupation; restores Odysseus' oikos; triggers subsequent vengeance of suitors' kin (Odyssey 24) and Odysseus' need for purification/exile in later tradition.

The Aftermath - Shades in Hades and Kin's RevengeHomer, Odyssey Book 24.1-204, 412-548; Apollodorus Epitome 7.33-34

Archaic / Imperial

Hermes leads the shades (psychai) of the slain suitors to Hades, where they recount their deaths to Achilles and Agamemnon. Agamemnon contrasts Penelope's fidelity with Clytemnestra's betrayal. On Ithaca, the fathers and kin of the suitors, led by Eupeithes (father of Antinous), attempt to avenge them but are defeated by Odysseus, Telemachus, and Laertes with Athena's intervention, followed by a divinely enforced reconciliation. Telmius' shade is implicitly among those led by Hermes, and his kin (unnamed) among the avengers, though neither is singled out. Provides eschatological closure; frames suitors' story as cautionary tale about xenia; establishes cultic need for purification after mass killing.

Telmius has his own myth, appearance, or heroic deed. — No individual myth, description, power, or deed is preserved. He is only a name in a list; all narrative details are collective suitor actions. Any image or biography assigning him distinct traits is modern invention.

All 108 suitors are named in Homer's Odyssey. — Homer names only about 10-15 suitors individually; the full 108-name roster is a later mythographic systematization by Apollodorus (and variants by Hyginus). Telmius is among the 90+ names Homer never mentions.

Telmius was a prince or son of a known king. — No parentage, patronymic, or royal title is given for Telmius in Apollodorus. While suitors are generally aristocratic, his specific lineage is unattested. He should not be called son of Phyleus, Nisus, or Meges without evidence.
