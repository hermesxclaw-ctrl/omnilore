Who It Is

Telmius never speaks in the story that kills him. He is one of fifty-seven young lords who sailed from Dulichium to Ithaca to claim a queen who would not choose them, ate a absent king's cattle and wine for years in his hall, and died in a single afternoon when that king walked through his own doorway disguised as a beggar.

No individual speech, decision, or personality trait is recorded. In Odyssey typology the Dulichian suitors are generally less individualized than the Ithacan leaders Antinous, Eurymachus, and Amphinomus, who speak and plot. Apollodorus lists Telmius without anecdote, implying he shares the collective behavior: arrival at Ithaca after Penelope's delay, residence in the palace, daily feasting and drinking, pressuring Penelope to choose a husband, plotting against Telemachus (Odyssey 16.342-406), and participating in the bow contest. Aristocratic status and eligibility as suitor

As a suitor from Dulichium, Telmius is implicitly of noble birth (agathos, basileus) with sufficient wealth to bring gifts and to sustain a claim on Penelope. Suitors are described as 'the best of the Cephallenians' (Odyssey 16.247). No supernatural power is implied; status is social and economic.

Limit: Status does not confer authority in Ithaca; Penelope repeatedly defers choice and Odysseus as rightful basileus outranks him. His claim is voided by Odysseus's return.

Occupation and consumption (parasitic hospitality)

Collective ability to occupy the palace, consume livestock, wine, and servants' labor, and to intimidate the household. Odyssey 1.106-112 describes suitors 'feasting, playing draughts, and wasting the house.' Telmius as one of 57 would have participated in this sustained occupation.

Limit: Dependent on Penelope's delay and Telemachus's youth; cannot compel marriage without her consent and is vulnerable to return of the householder. Explicitly condemned as violation of themis and xenia.

Martial presence (implied combatant)

Suitors are armed and attempt to kill Telemachus (ambush at Asteris, Odyssey 16.365) and later fight Odysseus in the Mnesterophonia. Telmius would have been present with spear/sword in the hall, but no individual feat or weapon is named. All suitors fail and are killed.

Limit: No attested skill, strength, or divine favor; collectively outmatched by Odysseus, Telemachus, and the two herdsmen, with Athena's support in Odyssey. Dies without wounding any opponent in surviving accounts.

No supernatural powers attested

Unlike figures such as Circe, Proteus, or the Sirens in the same cycle, Telmius has no attested metamorphosis, divination, magic, or divine parentage. He is fully mortal and his death is final; no transformation or apotheosis follows.

Limit: Mortal vulnerability to weapons, hunger, age; no resistance to death

Accounts I
The Catalogue of the Suitors from DulichiumApollodorus, Bibliotheca, Epitome 7.26-27 (Frazer edition)

1st-2nd century CE compilation, reflecting 8th-7th century BCE epic tradition

Apollodorus, attempting to give a complete roster absent in Homer, enumerates the suitors by homeland. From Dulichium he lists 57 names: ... Telmius is named among them, between other obscure names (in Frazer's order: ... Sostratus, Halius, Polyidus, Telmius, Sthenelaus ... though order varies by manuscript). The catalogue establishes Telmius as a Dulichian noble who left his island to compete for Penelope. No individual backstory, parentage, or deed is given; the entry is purely onomastic, serving to inflate the epic's 108 to a more 'complete' 136 in some manuscripts. This is the sole attestation of Telmius by name in surviving antiquity. Provides Telmius his only textual existence; later lexica (e.g., Roscher, RE) copy the name from this list. Establishes his origin and his status as one of the largest contingent, implying Dulichium's importance in the Cephallenian kingdom.

The Occupation of Odysseus's PalaceHomer, Odyssey Books 1-2, 16, 19; summarized in Apollodorus Epitome 7.32

Archaic (8th century BCE) for Homer; Imperial for Apollodorus summary

After Odysseus fails to return from Troy and Penelope delays remarriage by weaving and unweaving Laertes's shroud, suitors from Ithaca and the surrounding islands install themselves in his palace. They feast daily on his oxen, sheep, goats, and wine, command his maids, and pressure Penelope to choose one of them. Telmius, as part of the Dulichian contingent, participates in this multi-year occupation (traditionally three to four years). The Odyssey emphasizes their collective hybris: they 'devour the house' (oikon katakoptousin), violate xenia, and plot to murder Telemachus when he asserts himself. No individual action is assigned to Telmius; he is subsumed in the collective 'suitors' (mnēstēres). Creates the moral justification for the suitors' eventual killing; establishes Ithaca as a household under siege. For Telmius, it is the period of his active presence in mythic time before his death.

The Plot Against TelemachusHomer, Odyssey 16.342-412, 20.241-242; Apollodorus Epitome 7.32

Archaic

When Telemachus returns from Pylos and Sparta, Antinous proposes ambushing and killing him at the strait between Ithaca and Same (Asteris). The suitors agree and send a ship to intercept him. Athena warns Telemachus and he evades them. The plot fails and the suitors return to the hall, debating whether to try again. As a Dulichian suitor, Telmius is counted among those who assented to the murder plan, though he is not singled out as speaker or dissenter (unlike Amphinomus, who opposes killing Telemachus in 16.400, or Leodes). His complicity in attempted murder of the host's son deepens his guilt in epic moral terms. Marks suitors as not merely importunate guests but murderers, justifying capital punishment in the poem's ethics. For Telmius, it confirms his inclusion in the condemned group; no source exempts any Dulichian from the plot.

Accounts II
The Contest of the BowHomer, Odyssey 21.1-434; Apollodorus Epitome 7.32-33

Archaic / Imperial summary

Penelope, prompted by Athena and Eurycleia, brings out Odysseus's great bow and announces she will marry whoever strings it and shoots through twelve axe heads. Leodes fails, then other suitors try in order, none succeeding; Eurymachus nearly strings it but fails. The Dulichian suitors, including Telmius, are among those who attempt and fail, demonstrating they are not the bow's rightful owner. Telemachus almost succeeds but is stopped by Odysseus's signal. The contest is rigged by Odysseus's prior arrangement with Eumaeus and Philoetius to secure the hall. Telmius's failure, like that of all suitors except Odysseus, proves his lack of arete and divine favor. Establishes the test that legitimizes Odysseus's violence: only the true husband can wield the bow. Telmius's failure is his final public demonstration of unworthiness before death.

The Mnesterophonia – Slaughter of the Suitors and Death of TelmiusHomer, Odyssey 22.1-389; Apollodorus, Epitome 7.33

Archaic / Imperial

After Odysseus strings the bow and shoots through the axes, he reveals himself, and with Telemachus, the swineherd Eumaeus, and the cowherd Philoetius (and in Homer, with Athena as Mentor), he kills the suitors in the megaron. Doors are barred; the suitors, trapped without armor, are shot and then speared. Apollodorus states succinctly: 'Odysseus with Telemachus and Eumaeus and Philoetius slew the suitors' (Epitome 7.33). Telmius is among the slain; no individual death scene, last words, or killer is assigned to him (unlike Antinous shot while drinking, Eurymachus killed mid-plea, Amphinomus speared by Telemachus, Leodes begging for mercy). His body would have been among the corpses that Eumaeus and Philoetius later removed and that the suitors' families later reclaimed for burial (Odyssey 24.186-190, 24.412-421). Ends the suitor crisis, restores Odysseus's household, and triggers the suitors' families' brief revolt (Odyssey 24.421-548). For Telmius, it is the terminus of his mythic biography; no afterlife cult or return is attested.

The Souls of the Suitors in HadesHomer, Odyssey 24.1-204

Archaic

Hermes Psychopompos leads the psychai of the slain suitors, 'like bats squeaking in a cave,' to Hades, where they encounter Achilles, Patroclus, and Agamemnon. Amphimedon, a suitor from Ithaca, narrates their death to Agamemnon, describing Penelope's trick and Odysseus's revenge. The Dulichian dead, including Telmius, are present in this ghostly procession but are not individually named or given speech; Amphimedon's account speaks for all. Agamemnon contrasts Penelope's fidelity with Clytemnestra's betrayal. This coda confirms the suitors' fate is death without honor or burial rites initially, and their souls' restless transit. Provides epic closure and moral commentary; establishes that Telmius, like all suitors, receives no heroic afterlife. Later sources (e.g., Pausanias 9.41.3) mention suitors' tombs shown to tourists, but none for Telmius specifically.

Telmius appears in Homer's Odyssey and has a speaking role or death scene. — Telmius is never named in Homer. The Odyssey names only a handful of suitors individually (Antinous, Eurymachus, Amphinomus, Leodes, Ctesippus, Agelaus, Amphimedon, Demoptolemus, Euryades). Telmius appears only in the much later mythographer Apollodorus's catalogue (Epitome 7.27). He has no speeches, no individual deeds, and no described death in any ancient source.

Telmius was a prince, hero, or had supernatural powers or a famous parent. — No parentage, divine ancestry, or power is attested. Unlike suitors such as Amphinomus (son of Nisus) or Antinous (son of Eupeithes), Telmius's father is not given. He is a minor, fully mortal aristocrat whose only attribute is his origin in Dulichium. No cult, oracle, or heroic deed attaches to him.

All suitors were Ithacans or that Dulichium was Ithaca itself. — Suitors came from four distinct regions: Dulichium (52/57), Same (Cephalonia, 23/24), Zacynthus (20), and Ithaca (12). Telmius is specifically Dulichian, not Ithacan, meaning he traveled by sea to Ithaca and was not a local neighbor. Dulichium is consistently treated as a separate island, though its exact location is disputed.

The suitors, including Telmius, were innocent victims of Odysseus's cruelty and Penelope was forced to marry one. — Ancient ethics (and the Odyssey's narrative) present suitors as guilty of serious violations: prolonged violation of xenia, consumption of another's property, conspiracy to murder Telemachus, and coercion of Penelope. While modern readers may question the proportionality of killing 108/136 men, the epic presents their death as dike (justice) for hybris. Telmius shares collective guilt by participation, even without individualized crime scene.
