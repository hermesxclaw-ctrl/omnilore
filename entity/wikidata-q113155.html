Form

The Shilpa Shastras and Puraṇic descriptions agree he is the pinnacle of male beauty: a golden complexion, a broad and muscular chest 'likened to a doorframe', well-rounded limbs with plump thighs and calves, a slender waist and deep navel, his whole body suffused with the intoxicating scent of an elephant in rut. His eyes are shaped like lotus petals and tinged red, his nose straight and delicate, his eyebrows thick, his moon-like face framed by soft blue-black waves of hair, his teeth flawless, his neck shaped like a conch, and his breath naturally fragrant. He rides a makara (a mythical aquatic creature, symbol of fertility and primal desire), wears a blue garment, and is armed with a bow of sugarcane stringed with a thread of buzzing bees and five flower-tipped arrows. At his feet traditionally sits a parrot (his vāhana, symbol of playful affection and the link between love and speech).

Powers

The five flower arrows (Shara/Panchabāṇa) and the sugarcane bow Kamadeva's defining armament is a bow cut of sugarcane, strung not with sinew but with a living thread of buzzing bees, and five arrows fletched from blossoms — ashoka flower, mango flower, jasmine, blue lotus, and white lotus. Each arrow is traditionally said to induce a distinct emotional-erotic effect (different registers of desire, infatuation, and delusion); the Mohana arrow in particular specialises in delusion and infatuation. Even in the Atharvaveda the 'arrow of kāma that is terrible' is described as piercing the heart and being 'feathered with longing, tipped with love, necked with resolve'. Wounds are metaphorical but disabling: victims of Manmatha's arrows are said to be driven to 'madness, ecstasy, and suffering'. Puraṇic canon (Shiva Purana, Matsya Purana, Skanda Purana); root motif in Atharvaveda 3.25; epic usage The arrows can be resisted. Against a sufficiently composed ascetic they fail to break concentration: in the Shiva Purana Kama's arrows do not fully penetrate Shiva's meditation, and Shiva absorbs Kama rather than yield. The god of desire cannot force the will of one who has truly conquered kāma — which is precisely the theological point of the Madana-bhasma. The count and attribution of the five arrows vary by text (the list of ashoka/mango/jasmine/blue-lotus/white-lotus is the widely cited Puraṇic set, but not every source enumerates all five). Indian textual tradition itself does not give a single canonical name for all five; Western summary lists collapse the variants.

Manmatha, the power to disturb the mind The epithet Manmatha ('mind-disturber') names his core ability: to agitate, destabilise, and redirect the emotions of gods, sages, and mortals alike. In the Rāmāyaṇa, Rama himself becomes kāmavaśaṃgata — 'overpowered by love' — after Sita's abduction, 'perpetually absorbed in thought and sorrow' (R 5.36.41f.), and the epic repeatedly describes lovers struck by 'the sharp arrows of Manmatha'. The Kamagita verses have Kama boast: 'None can destroy me… I am the one immortal and indestructible.' Ramayana; Mahabharata Kamagita; Puranas Mind-disturbance is indiscriminate and can misfire: in the Puranas Kama's first arrows are turned on Brahma himself, inciting the creator's forbidden desire for his own daughter Sandhya and earning Kama the curse of his future incineration. His power is a dangerous weapon that wounds the wielder's ecology of desire as much as its target. None of substance; the 'mind-disturber' role is stable across the epics. The only tension is whether the mental effect is moral or amoral — the tradition deliberately leaves that unresolved.

Ananga, existence without a body After Shiva incinerates him, Kama is reconstituted as Ananga — 'the bodiless one' — an unseen but potent force. Death by the third-eye fire does not end desire; it converts personified desire into a subtler, unlocated compulsion that operates in the world without any visible agent. The epithet emerges in the Mahabharata's allusion at 12.183.10 ('the universal lord, the husband of Umā, when Kāma was getting the better of him, subdued him by making him bodiless') and becomes his most enduring name thereafter. Mahabharata 12.183; Puranas Bodilessness means he can no longer act as a subjective deity in his old form; his 'presence' is purely effectual. The tradition makes clear this is a transformation, not oblivion — desire persists, simply de-incarnated. The 'bodiless' endpoint coexists, in different texts, with the rebirth-as-Pradyumna body and with the Kalika Purana's corporeal resurrection by Kamakhya — three different post-immolation fates that the tradition does not harmonise.

Lordship over Gandharvas, Apsarasas, and the Maras The Harivamsa identifies Kama as 'lord' (prabhu) of the Gandharvas (celestial musicians) and Apsarasas (celestial nymphs) — the beings most emblematic of beauty and seduction. He also commands a cohort of violent ganas called the Maras. In the Indra-led missions of the Puranas it is Kama who dispatches the apsaras to disturb the penance of sages, lest they accumulate world-shaking ascetic power. Harivamsa 270; Puranas; iconographic tradition His lordship over the apsaras is exercised on behalf of Indra (the king of heaven), so his agency is partly instrumental — he is a manager of divine-level seduction as much as an independent sovereign. None of substance; this is a consistent stratification across texts.

Godhead over kāma as a puruṣārtha (end of life) Beyond any single act, Kamadeva personifies kāma, one of the recognised ends of human striving alongside dharma and artha. The Mahabharata's Shanti and Anushasana Parvas elevate him as a dharaṇīdhara — an 'upholder of the earth' — and equate kāma with saṅkalpa (mental intention): 'For everlasting intention is called desire; it is Rudra's vigour which leapt out and fell in the fire' (Mbh 13.84.11). Desire here is not temptation but a generative, world-sustaining principle. Mahabharata Shanti/Anushasana Parvas; Vedic substrate This cosmic valuation sits in deliberate tension with the ascetic renunciatory pole of the same tradition (Shiva's tapas, yoga's dismissal of kāma), and the two views are never dialectically reconciled in a single voice. The tradition itself argues both positions: kāma as creative sustainer vs kāma as the obstacle to liberation. The dossier keeps both registers and does not pick a winner.

Accounts

Before there was a god of love there was a force of desire. In the primal state of indistinguishable darkness and undifferentiated water, kāma 'arose as the first seed of mind', the spark by which non-being passes into being. The hymn is a theogony of consciousness: nothing existed, not even the sky or death or non-death, until desire — the mind's first stirring — connected the two. Franklin Edgerton reads this kāma as a 'cosmic Will', an impersonal creative principle; John Muir notes that at this stage it does not yet mean sexual desire but desire for goodness and being in general. It is the oldest stratum of Kamadeva's lineage, and it is already recognisably the same force he will personify.

In a love-charm that reads like a sorcerer's incantation, the Vedic poet turns Kāma into an agent with weapons: 'May the up-thruster thrust you up; do not abide in your own lair; with the arrow of kāma that is terrible I pierce you in the heart' (AV 3.25.1), and 'The arrow feathered with longing, tipped with love, necked with resolve, let love pierce you in the heart' (AV 3.25.2). For Catherine Benton this is the moment Kāma acquires the decisive trait that stamps all later iconography — the arrow that wounds the heart with desire. It is also a conspicuously instrumental power: the supplicant is not worshipping an amorous deity but weaponising desire against a specific woman.

In the dominant lineage, Brahma creates from his mind the prajapatis (bringers of progeny) and then the maiden Sandhya, whereupon a stunningly handsome youth emerges and asks what he is for. Brahma names him Kama and orders him to go out and spread love with his flower arrows. Kama's first act is to shoot his own creator: Brahma, pierced by the arrows, burns with desire for his daughter Sandhya. The prajapati Dharma, alarmed, summons Shiva, who laughs at both Brahma and Kama. Regaining his senses, the humiliated Brahma curses Kama to be burnt to ashes by Shiva in the future — but, moved by Kama's pleading, assures him he will be reborn. From the agitation of the incestuously stirred prajapatis come the Pitrs, and Sandhya, shamed by the forbidden passion she provoked, dies and is reborn as the chaste Arundhati. The Skanda Purana reframes the origin: Kama is created because the prajapatis refused to procreate at all.

The demon Tarakasura holds the universe hostage; a boon makes him invincible except to the son of Shiva. But Shiva, withdrawn and ascetic since the death of his first wife Sati, refuses to engage the world, and no such son will come. The gods, led by Indra, summon Kama to break Shiva's meditation and steer him toward Parvati, the reincarnation of Sati. Kama hesitates, foreseeing his own destruction; Indra convinces him the mission is world-preserving. Kama prepares a calculated assault on the meditating god: bees buzz, fragrant flowers bloom, a soft breeze blows, and the Mohana arrow is loosed straight at Shiva's heart. For a moment Shiva is stirred toward Parvati — then he regains his composure, sees the desire being forced on him, and opens the third eye. Fire springs from it and reduces Kamadeva to ashes on the spot. In the Kalika Purana the burning is more directly punitive, tied to the Brahma-incest curse. From Kama's incinerated remains, in the Lalitopakhyana of the Brahmanda Purana, rises the demon Bhandasura. The Shiva Purana's Shiva absorbs the vanquished god into his ganas, symbolising desire brought under ascetic discipline.

Witnessing her husband collapse to ash, Rati's mourning becomes a story in its own right and perhaps the tradition's most celebrated scene of wifely grief. Kālidāsa gives it its highest literary form (Canto IV of the Kumārasambhava): Rati laments Kama's death and, bereft, attempts to immolate herself on a funeral pyre, only to be stayed by a heavenly voice that promises her husband will be restored once Shiva marries. In the Bhagavata Purana she goes mad with grief and undertakes severe penance, begging Parvati to intercede; in the Matsya and Padma versions she smears her body with Kama's ashes. In the Skanda Purana's Kedara Khanda a very different Rati confidently takes the mission on herself and begins austerities to revive her husband. Every version shares one resolution: Kama will return — either immediately, roused by the Goddess, or in a reborn form.

Kama is reborn as Pradyumna, eldest son of Krishna (Vishnu's avatar) and his chief consort Rukmini (who is Vishnu's wife Lakshmi). The demon Sambara, foretold that the reborn Kama will destroy him, kidnaps the infant Pradyumna and casts him into the ocean, where a fish swallows him; the fish is caught and cut open in Sambara's kitchen, and the child is found by the kitchen-maid Mayavati — actually Rati in disguise. Narada reveals to her that the boy is the reborn Kama and she must rear him. As Pradyumna grows, the kitchen-maid's maternal care deepens into a wife's passion; the boy resists, believing her his mother, until she tells him the secret of their past lives and trains him in magic and war. Pradyumna slays Sambara and returns with Mayavati-Rati to Dvaraka, where the pair are reunited and wed. Later Vaishnava theology (Gaudiya tradition) reads an even higher register: Krishna himself is the 'original Kamadeva' of Vrindavana, the ever-fresh transcendental god of love who is the source of the mundance demigod's inciting power — so the reborn Kama is placed inside Krishna's own divine family.

A way to hold it in your mind

Somewhere in the universe's earliest dark and undifferentiated water, before gods, before ancestors, before even death or non-death, something stirred — and the Rigveda names that first stirring kāma, 'the first seed of mind'. From that cosmogonic whisper, a tradition two and a half millennia long grew a god: Kamadeva, the mind-born prince of desire, who carries a bow of sugarcane strung with bees and five arrows cut from flowers. It is a myth whose protagonist is destroyed at his own mission's climax — burned to ash by the third eye of the god he was sent to seduce — and yet whose ending is not death at all. The fire that unmakes Kamadeva's body remakes him as a bodiless force called Ananga, the eternal ache that lives in the gap between wanting and having. He is at once the creator's cosmic impulse, a meddling celestial cupid with a heart-piercing arrow (antiquity's first 'love at first sight' weapon), and the husband whose widowed consort wipes his ashes across her skin and refuses to let him be gone. To meet Kamadeva is to meet the paradox at the centre of every ascetic tradition: desire is the very spark that began the world, and it is also the fire a yogi flees. He died for love and returned bodiless for love — and the name for that, in twenty-five languages from Tamil to Japanese to the shadow-theatres of Java, is still, unmistakably, the god of desire.
