The name suggests a connection to the Alban Hills (Colli Albani) southeast of Rome, and possibly to the ancient city of Alba Longa, the legendary mother-city of Rome. The Alban Mount (Mons Albanus, modern Monte Cavo) was the site of the sanctuary of Iuppiter Latiaris, where the Latin League held its annual festival (Feriae Latinae). If Albarinus were a genuine deity, his domain would likely have been this region.

Form

No ancient description exists. If Albarinus were a genuine Roman deity, he would likely have been depicted in the standard Roman anthropomorphic style — a mature male figure with a toga, possibly holding a scepter or a patera (libation bowl), or possibly as a youthful genius figure. He might have been associated with white clothing or white animals for sacrifice (alba hostia). This is pure reconstruction based on Roman iconographic conventions, not attested evidence.

Civic Identity If associated with Alba Longa, Albarinus might have been a civic patron deity of the legendary city, embodying its identity and its claim to be the mother-city of Rome. The city was said to have been destroyed by Tullus Hostilius in the 7th century BCE, but its religious traditions were absorbed into Roman cult. Behavior & Habitat

If Albarinus were a genuine local deity of the Alban region, he would likely have been a protective numen — a spirit of place — overseeing the territory of the Alban Hills, its springs, groves, and agricultural fertility. Such local gods were typically invoked for protection of boundaries, crops, and livestock. This is a reconstruction based on the typical role of local Roman deities, not attested evidence.

Accounts

There are no myths, legends, or stories associated with Albarinus in any ancient source. The name is not attested in any literary text, inscription, or artifact. Any narrative associated with this name is necessarily a modern invention.

The legends of Alba Longa — its founding by Ascanius, the white sow prophecy, the reign of the Silvian dynasty, and its destruction by Tullus Hostilius — are well-attested. If Albarinus is a corruption or modern coinage related to these legends, these stories could be tangentially relevant. However, no ancient source connects the name Albarinus to any of these narratives.

What We Don't Know

Complete absence of primary evidence. The name Albarinus is not attested in any ancient source — no inscription, no literary text, no calendar, no coin, no artifact. The Wikidata entity (Q126599438) appears to be based on no verifiable source. The name may be: 1) a modern invention; 2) a database error or typographical corruption; 3) an extremely obscure local cult name that has left no trace in surviving sources; 4) a conflation of 'Albanus' (the god of the Alban Mount) with another name. Without access to the original source that created the Wikidata entry, it is impossible to determine the provenance of this name. The research is complete in the sense that all available evidence has been thoroughly searched (TLL, CIL, RE, standard handbooks, all surviving literary texts and calendars) — the conclusion is that the name is unattested.
