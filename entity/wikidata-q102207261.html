The Approach

In the rolling hills of southern France, a name whispers through centuries: Aude, the betrothed of Roland, whose tragic fate in the Chanson de Roland echoes the region's own turbulent history. But Aude is more than a legend—it is a land of Cathar castles, Roman roads, and winds that shape both the landscape and the people. From the ancient skull of Tautavel to the modern wind turbines, Aude is a living testament to the enduring interplay between myth and reality.

Content advisory. Contains references to medieval warfare, religious persecution (Cathar Crusade), and historical violence. No explicit sexual or graphic content.

Aude

Etymology

The name Aude derives from the Germanic element 'ald' or 'aud' meaning 'old' or 'prosperity', respectively. It is a feminine form of the name Aldo or Otto.

Cultural Ownership

French, Occitan, and broader European medieval literature

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars conflate Aude with historical figures such as Oda, wife of Bertrand, Duke of Aquitaine, but the literary character is distinct. The name is also used for the Aude department in France, which has no direct connection to the legendary figure.

Description

Appearance

No detailed physical description is provided in the Chanson de Roland. She is depicted as a young noblewoman, likely of high social standing.

Symbols & Iconography

In medieval art and later illustrations, Aude is often depicted as a sorrowful maiden, sometimes shown fainting or dying upon hearing of Roland's death. She symbolizes loyalty and tragic love.

Behaviour & Personality

Aude is portrayed as devoted, loyal, and emotionally vulnerable. In the Chanson de Roland, she is proactive in seeking confirmation of Roland's fate and dies of grief, demonstrating the depth of her love.

Domains & Habitats

She resides at the court of Charlemagne in Aix-la-Chapelle (Aachen), as depicted in the Chanson de Roland.

Version Differences

In the Chanson de Roland, Aude dies of grief upon hearing of Roland's death. In later versions, such as the Italian 'La Spagna' and other chansons de geste, her fate varies: some have her marry another, others have her enter a convent, and some have her die later. The Nota Emilianense does not mention her, suggesting she may be a later addition.

Stories & Myths

The Death of Aude (Chanson de Roland)

Source Chanson de Roland, Oxford manuscriptCulture French medieval epicPeriod c. 1100-1125

Summary

After the Battle of Roncevaux, Charlemagne returns to Aix-la-Chapelle. Aude, Roland's betrothed, approaches Charlemagne and asks if Roland is alive. Charlemagne, unable to tell her the truth, says Roland is dead. Aude then collapses and dies at Charlemagne's feet. Charlemagne mourns her and orders her burial with honor.

Participants

Aude

Charlemagne

Roland (offstage)

Consequences

Aude's death underscores the tragic cost of the war and the personal loss suffered by Roland's loved ones. It also highlights Charlemagne's grief and the human toll of his campaigns.

Variants

In some later versions, Aude does not die immediately but instead faints and later dies. In others, she is said to have died of a broken heart. The Nota Emilianense omits her entirely, suggesting she may have been introduced later in the tradition.

Historical Oda, Wife of Bertrand

Source Vita Sancti HubertiCulture Christian hagiographyPeriod 8th-9th century

Summary

Oda, also called Aude, was the wife of Bertrand, Duke of Aquitaine, and the mother of Eudo, who was a brother of Saint Hubertus. This historical figure may have influenced the naming of the literary character.

Death from grief

Source Chanson de Roland

Description

Aude dies upon hearing of Roland's death, which is a form of psychological trauma leading to death. This reflects medieval literary tropes of lovesickness and the fragility of women.

Scholarly Context

Scholars interpret Aude's death as a literary device to emphasize the tragic consequences of war and the emotional impact on women. It also reinforces the ideal of female loyalty and devotion.

Contradictions & Disputes

Aude's death

Position A

Aude dies immediately upon hearing of Roland's death (Chanson de Roland).

Position A — evidence

Oxford manuscript of the Chanson de Roland, laisses 258-259.

Position B

In some later versions, she does not die immediately but later, or she survives and enters a convent.

Position B — evidence

Later chansons de geste and Italian adaptations (e.g., La Spagna).

Scholarly Consensus

Scholars generally accept the Oxford version as the earliest and most authoritative, but acknowledge the existence of variants.

Historical existence

Position A

Aude is purely fictional.

Position A — evidence

No contemporary evidence of a real Aude connected to Roland.

Position B

The name may derive from a historical figure, Oda, wife of Bertrand of Aquitaine.

Position B — evidence

The Vita Sancti Huberti mentions Oda, but there is no direct link.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars see the literary character as fictional, with the name possibly borrowed from historical or hagiographic sources.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

Detailed physical description of Aude is absent in primary sources. Later medieval and Renaissance adaptations may contain more details, but these were not fully researched. The relationship between the historical Oda and the literary character remains speculative. The exact origin of the name 'Aude' in the epic is uncertain.

Research Scope

This dossier focuses on the fictional character Aude (also known as Oda) as depicted in medieval chansons de geste, particularly the Chanson de Roland. It also covers the historical and geographical context of the Aude department in France, which shares the name and is often associated with the legend. The research draws from primary medieval texts, historical chronicles, and modern scholarly interpretations.
