Who It Is

It's past midnight on a farm in the Scottish Borders. The family is asleep, the fire has died to embers, and silence fills the house. But in the barn, something stirs. The threshing flail moves on its own, grain separating from chaff in perfect rhythm. The butter churn rocks gently, though no hand touches it. A small, brown, hairy figure pads silently across the hearthstones and pauses before the bowl of cream left there — the only payment it will ever accept. This is the Brownie, and it has served this family since before anyone living can remember.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Scots 'brownie,' diminutive of 'brown,' referring to the creature's characteristic brown skin and hair. The Gaelic 'brùnaidh' and 'gruagach' (literally 'hairy one') describe the same entity.

Aliases — Broonie, Brùnaidh, Gruagach, Urisk/Ùruisg (Highland variant), Hob (English variant), Bwbach (Welsh variant)

Accounts I
The Brownie of CranshawsBerwickshire, Scottish Borders — recorded by William Henderson (1866)

The Brownie of Cranshaws was unusually industrious even by Brownie standards — it could thresh an entire barn of grain in a single night. However, it was also fiercely sensitive. When a servant girl mocked its ragged appearance, the Brownie appeared to her the next night and twisted her neck. The family, fearing worse, never spoke of the creature again. The Brownie continued its work in absolute silence and was never mocked again — the servant girl survived but was said to walk with a permanent crick in her neck for the rest of her life.

The Brownie of BodsbeckDumfriesshire — recorded by Sir Walter Scott

The Brownie of Bodsbeck served a farm family for generations, performing every conceivable domestic task. The family, deeply grateful, decided to reward it with a new set of clothes — a green coat and trousers. When the Brownie found the gift, it was devastated. 'Green coat, green coat — Brownie'll work no more!' it wailed, and vanished into the night. The farm fell into disrepair, and the family learned too late that the only acceptable offering was a bowl of cream, never anything that implied they thought the Brownie needed improvement.

Accounts II
The Brownie of the BlackfordsPerthshire — oral tradition

A farmer's family faced ruin after a harsh winter killed most of their livestock. The Brownie, who had served the family for four generations, revealed itself for the first and only time. It appeared as a small, hairy man, and spoke: 'Leave a measure of oats by the hearth each night and ask nothing.' The family obeyed. Each morning, the measure was refilled, and a new calf stood in the barn. After seven nights, the herd was restored. The Brownie was never seen again, but the family prospered — and never failed to leave cream by the fire.

The Ungrateful Servant GirlAberdeenshire — recorded by Robert Chambers

A new servant girl at a great house was warned to leave a bowl of cream for the Brownie each night. She scoffed at the superstition and purposely spilled the cream on the hearth. That night, every dish in the kitchen shattered, the milk soured in the pail, and the grain stores were scattered through the barn. The family dismissed the girl, but the Brownie had already left. It took a year of nightly offerings and apologies before a new Brownie — more cautious than the first — agreed to return.
