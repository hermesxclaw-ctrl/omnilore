Who It Is

Imagine a god who lives on a mountain, and from his cold, misty palace he hurls lightning bolts like javelins, floods entire cities with a flick of his wrist, and yet also whispers gentle rain onto your cornfield so it can grow. That's Tlaloc. He's not a benevolent 'sky father' or a purely destructive storm god; he's both. He's the reason the Aztecs built a temple right next to the one for their war god, Huitzilopochtli. One half of the Great Temple was for the sun and war, the other for rain and agriculture. You couldn't have an empire without both, and you couldn't have either without keeping Tlaloc happy. And keeping him happy meant a lot of things, including, sometimes, the tears of children.

Tlaloc is an ambivalent god. He is a benevolent provider who sends the gentle rains that make crops grow, but he is also a wrathful punisher who sends torrential storms, hail, lightning, and floods that destroy crops and villages. He is seen as a capricious and demanding deity who requires constant propitiation. He is associated with cold, damp, and mountainous places. He is also a god of the underworld-like paradise of Tlalocan, where those who died from water-related causes (drowning, lightning, water-borne diseases) went, regardless of their moral conduct in life.

Powers
Control of Rain and Weather

Tlaloc could summon or withhold rain, causing droughts or floods. He could send gentle rain, torrential storms, hail, and snow. This was his primary and most celebrated power.

Limit: His power was not absolute; he was often seen as needing to be propitiated with sacrifices and rituals. He could be overruled or influenced by other gods (e.g., Tezcatlipoca).

Lightning and Thunder

He wielded a lightning axe (xiuhcoatl) which he threw to create storms and strike the earth. Thunder was considered his voice or the sound of his axe.

Limit: Not explicitly limited in sources, but often linked to his role as a storm god.

Creation and Sustenance of Life

He was essential for agriculture, providing the water for maize and other crops. He was seen as a creator figure, linked to the fourth sun (Nahui Quiahuitl).

Limit: His power was tied to the cycle of seasons; he could not create life without the cooperation of other gods (like Xipe Totec for renewal).

Judgment and Punishment

He could punish individuals or communities with storms, floods, frost, and diseases (such as leprosy and dropsy, which were associated with water). He was a god to be feared.

Limit: His punishments were often seen as responses to ritual neglect or moral failings.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Tlaloc is most famously depicted with large, goggle-like eyes (often made of serpent or jade rings) and a distinctive upper lip that is curled or turned up, sometimes showing fangs. He is often painted in dark colors (black or dark blue) with red or yellow accents. He holds a lightning axe (xiuhcoatl, or a serpent-shaped scepter) or a vessel of water. He wears a netted or pleated headdress, often with heron feathers and a fan of quetzal feathers.

Symbols — Goggle eyes, fangs, lightning axe, water vessel, netted headdress, heron feathers, quetzal feathers, jade, serpent imagery, frogs, seashells, mountains, caves, the color blue (and black), the day sign 'Rain' (Quiahuitl).

Domains — Rain, storms, lightning, thunder, hail, floods, water, mountains, caves, springs, rivers, lakes, agriculture (especially maize), fertility, Tlalocan (the paradise of the rain god), and the earthly realm of the fourth sun (Nahui Quiahuitl, 'Four Rain'). He was also a patron of the day sign 'Rain' (Quiahuitl) and the trecena (13-day period) of Quiahuitl.

Accounts I
The Fourth Sun (Nahui Quiahuitl)Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas, Anales de Cuauhtitlan

Postclassic (c. 1300-1521 CE)

In the Aztec creation myth, the fourth era of the world was called 'Four Rain' (Nahui Quiahuitl). This sun was destroyed by a great flood, sent by Tlaloc. The people who survived this flood were turned into fish (or turkeys, in some versions). This story establishes Tlaloc's power over both creation and destruction. The fourth sun was destroyed, and a new era (the fifth sun, the current one) began, requiring the sacrifice of the gods (Nanahuatzin and Tecciztecatl) to create the sun and moon.

Tlaloc's Wrath and the Maize GodFlorentine Codex, Book I

Postclassic (c. 1300-1521 CE)

Tlaloc is said to have once hidden or taken away the maize god (Centéotl) to his watery paradise, Tlalocan, causing a famine on earth. The other gods had to retrieve him. This story highlights Tlaloc's control over the life-giving crop and his capricious nature. The maize god was returned, and the famine ended, but the story emphasizes the need to keep Tlaloc happy.

Accounts II
The Children of Tlaloc and the TlaloqueFlorentine Codex, Book I, Chapter 4

Postclassic (c. 1300-1521 CE)

Tlaloc was associated with a group of helper gods called the Tlaloque, who were his assistants or children. They were believed to live in the mountains and were responsible for distributing rain from different vessels: one for gentle rain, one for storms, one for hail, etc. They also had a role in the punishment of sinners. This story explains the different types of rain and the need to appease multiple divine beings.

Tlaloc and the Sacrifice of ChildrenCodex Borbonicus, Florentine Codex (Book II)

Postclassic (c. 1300-1521 CE)

During the festival of Atlcahualo (or Quauitleua), the Mexica would sacrifice children to Tlaloc. The children were often adorned with feathers and jade, and their tears were seen as a favorable omen for rain. If they did not cry, the ritual was considered less effective. This was a public and state-sponsored ritual. The ritual was believed to ensure the arrival of the rainy season and the success of crops.

Tlaloc is a purely benevolent rain god, similar to a 'weather god' in other mythologies. — Tlaloc is a highly ambivalent and capricious deity. He sends both life-giving rain and destructive storms, hail, and floods. He is a punisher as much as a provider.

Tlaloc is the same as the Maya god Chaac. — Tlaloc and Chaac are distinct gods from different cultures, though they share a common Mesoamerican archetype of a rain god. They have different iconography, myths, and associated rituals. The Aztecs themselves recognized the difference.

Tlaloc is a 'minor' god in the Aztec pantheon. — Tlaloc was one of the most important gods in the Aztec pantheon, second only to Huitzilopochtli (and perhaps Tezcatlipoca) in state importance. He had a major temple at the Templo Mayor, his own paradise (Tlalocan), and numerous festivals dedicated to him.

Tlaloc is a 'mythical' figure with no connection to real places. — Tlaloc was associated with specific, real geographic locations, most notably Mount Tlaloc, which was a major pilgrimage site with a shrine at its summit. His domain was not just an abstract 'heaven' but a physical place on earth.
