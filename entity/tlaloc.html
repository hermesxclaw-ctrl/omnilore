I am the rain that feeds your children and the flood that drowns them. I am the storm that breaks the drought and the drought that breaks you. The mountains are my home, and tears are my tribute.

Who They Are

Tlaloc is the Aztec god of rain, storms, lightning, and agricultural fertility — one of the oldest and most continuously worshipped deities in Mesoamerica. His cult predates the Aztecs by over a thousand years, with recognizable Tlaloc imagery appearing at Teotihuacan (where he may have been the primary deity) and continuing through Toltec and Mexica civilizations. Unlike the warrior gods Huitzilopochtli and Tezcatlipoca, Tlaloc's power is elemental and amoral — he gives life through rain but also takes it through flood, drought, and hail. His dual role as giver and destroyer reflects the harsh reality of Mesoamerican agriculture, where crops depended entirely on seasonal rains that could be abundant one year and catastrophically absent the next.

Physically, Tlaloc is one of the most instantly recognizable Mesoamerican deities. He is depicted with goggle-like circular eyes (representing rain clouds or the surface of water), a protruding upper lip with jaguar-like fangs, and often a blue or black body the color of storm clouds. His headdress features heron feathers and paper ornaments. In Aztec sculptures and codices, he holds a lightning serpent (a zigzag representation of the lightning bolt) and a water vessel or maize plant. The massive Tlaloc monolith (weighing 168 tons) now displayed at the National Museum of Anthropology in Mexico City demonstrates the scale of his worship.

Tlaloc governed the Tlalocan — the earthly paradise located inside mountains, a realm of eternal spring, abundant food, and perfect happiness. Those who died by water-related causes (drowning, lightning strike, diseases associated with water like dropsy) were not cremated (the usual fate of the dead) but buried, and their souls ascended to Tlalocan rather than descending to the dark underworld of Mictlan. This made Tlaloc's realm one of the most desirable afterlives in Aztec religion.

Tlaloc's worship involved some of the most heart-wrenching rituals in Mesoamerican religion. He was assisted by the Tlaloque — lesser rain gods who dwelt in the mountains and poured rain from their vessels. To ensure adequate rainfall, the Aztecs offered sacrifices at mountain shrines — particularly the sacrifice of children, whose tears were believed to be especially pleasing to Tlaloc as they symbolically anticipated the rain. Before sacrifice, the children were made to cry; the more tears, the better the omen for a rainy season. The Spanish chroniclers recorded these ceremonies with horror, but within Aztec theology, they represented cosmic reciprocity: humans gave their most precious possession (their children's lives and tears) in exchange for the rain that sustained all life.

Archaeology has revealed Tlaloc shrines throughout the Valley of Mexico. The Templo Mayor of Tenochtitlan was crowned by dual shrines — one to Huitzilopochtli and one to Tlaloc — representing the twin foundations of the Aztec state: military power (war captives for sacrifice, tribute from conquered peoples) and agricultural fertility (rain that made farming and therefore civilization possible). This architectural pairing enshrined the central paradox of Aztec imperialism: conquest and cultivation were inseparable.

Form

Tlaloc is most famously depicted with large, goggle-like eyes (often made of serpent or jade rings) and a distinctive upper lip that is curled or turned up, sometimes showing fangs. He is often painted in dark colors (black or dark blue) with red or yellow accents. He holds a lightning axe (xiuhcoatl, or a serpent-shaped scepter) or a vessel of water. He wears a netted or pleated headdress, often with heron feathers and a fan of quetzal feathers. His face is sometimes shown with a bifurcated tongue, emphasizing his serpentine nature. In his hands, he may hold maize stalks, a digging stick, or a rain vessel from which he pours water.

Powers

Control of Rain and Weather Tlaloc could summon or withhold rain, causing droughts or floods. He could send gentle rain, torrential storms, hail, and snow. This was his primary and most celebrated power. Attested across all primary sources (Florentine Codex, codices, and archaeological context). His power was not absolute; he was often seen as needing to be propitiated with sacrifices and rituals. He could be overruled or influenced by other gods (e.g., Tezcatlipoca). Some sources suggest he was more benevolent than others. The Florentine Codex emphasizes his role as a punisher, while other texts focus on his provision.

Lightning and Thunder He wielded a lightning axe (xiuhcoatl) which he threw to create storms and strike the earth. Thunder was considered his voice or the sound of his axe. Attested in codices and the Florentine Codex. Not explicitly limited in sources, but often linked to his role as a storm god. None known.

Creation and Sustenance of Life He was essential for agriculture, providing the water for maize and other crops. He was seen as a creator figure, linked to the fourth sun (Nahui Quiahuitl). Attested in the 'Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas' and other creation myths. His power was tied to the cycle of seasons; he could not create life without the cooperation of other gods (like Xipe Totec for renewal). His role in the creation of the fifth sun is not as prominent as that of Quetzalcoatl or Tezcatlipoca.

Judgment and Punishment He could punish individuals or communities with storms, floods, frost, and diseases (such as leprosy and dropsy, which were associated with water). He was a god to be feared. Attested in the Florentine Codex and other colonial-era texts. His punishments were often seen as responses to ritual neglect or moral failings. Some sources suggest that punishments were more often delegated to his helpers (the Tlaloque).

Rain Generation and Control Tlaloc's fundamental power is the ability to generate, withhold, and control rain. He (or the Tlaloque under his command) dispenses rain from mountain-vessels, and the quantity, timing, and gentleness/violence of rainfall is at his disposal. The Aztec agricultural calendar and indeed the entire Nahua food system depended on Tlaloc providing adequate seasonal rains—making him arguably the most economically significant deity in the pantheon. Sahagún, Florentine Codex; Aztec ritual calendar; agricultural practices Tlaloc requires ritual sustenance (offerings, sacrifices) to maintain the rain cycle. Prolonged neglect or insufficient offerings can cause drought. Some droughts may be beyond his control or will, suggesting that even a major god operates within cosmic constraints. Other water deities (Chalchiuhtlicue for terrestrial waters, Huixtocihuatl for salt water) have complementary domains; the exact boundaries between their jurisdictions are not entirely consistent across sources.

Lightning and Storm Generation Tlaloc wields the lightning serpent (xiuhcoatl), casting lightning bolts that split the sky and strike the earth. Lightning is both destructive (killing, burning, felling trees) and productive (breaking open the mountain-clouds to release rain, fixing nitrogen in soil—a process the Aztecs observed empirically if not scientifically). Tlaloc's storms include thunder, hail, and flooding as well as beneficial rain, reflecting the ambivalent power of Mesoamerican storm systems. Sahagún; Aztec storm theology; iconography of Tlaloc with lightning serpent Destructive storms (hail, flood, crop-destroying winds) are Tlaloc's power used negatively—the same mechanism that produces gentle rain can be turned to destruction, meaning Tlaloc's 'blessing' is never guaranteed. Some sources distinguish between types of Tlaloque responsible for different storm phenomena, suggesting specialization within the rain deity complex.

Mountain Water Storage Tlaloc's control over mountains extends to the water contained within them. In Aztec cosmology, mountains are hollow repositories of water, and the Tlaloque dwell inside them, managing the water reserves. This power gives Tlaloc territorial dominion over the entire Mexican landscape—every mountain is a potential source of water, and every mountain requires Tlaloc's temples and offerings to release its stored rain. Sahagún; Aztec mountain cult (tepetl); archaeological evidence of mountain shrines Individual mountains are assigned to specific Tlaloque, suggesting that Tlaloc delegates this power rather than exercising it directly at every location. Some mountains were associated with other deities (Mixcoatl for hunting mountains, Tepeyollotl for earthquake mountains), complicating the 'every mountain = Tlaloc' model.

Afterlife Provision (Tlalocan) Tlaloc rules over Tlalocan, the earthly paradise where those who died by water-related causes spend their afterlife. He grants eternal abundance, perpetual harvest, and freedom from labor to those under his posthumous care. This power makes him the 'landlord' of one of the most desirable afterlife destinations, giving him jurisdiction over a specific category of the dead. Sahagún, Florentine Codex, Book III (origin of the gods and afterlife descriptions) Access to Tlalocan is restricted to specific causes of death—Tlaloc does not grant general salvation but receives only those who died in ways he 'claimed.' Some sources describe multiple paradises (the sun's house for warriors, the maize goddess's realm, Tlalocan) without clearly specifying their relationships or hierarchy.

Agricultural Fertility Beyond rain itself, Tlaloc directly governs agricultural fertility—the sprouting of seeds, the growth of maize, the abundance of harvests. He is 'He Who Makes Things Sprout,' and his power extends from the meteorological (rainfall) to the biological (plant growth). The Aztec agricultural cycle was entirely structured around Tlaloc's ritual calendar, with ceremonies at planting, growing, and harvest phases. Sahagún; Codex Borbonicus veintena ceremonies; archaeological evidence of agricultural offerings Fertility requires the cooperation of other deities: Chicomecoatl (maize goddess), Xipe Totec (spring renewal), and Centeotl (young maize god) share agricultural responsibilities with Tlaloc. The exact division of agricultural labor among these deities is not perfectly systematized in the sources—different festivals invoke different deities for overlapping functions.

Behavior & Habitat

Tlaloc is an ambivalent god. He is a benevolent provider who sends the gentle rains that make crops grow, but he is also a wrathful punisher who sends torrential storms, hail, lightning, and floods that destroy crops and villages. He is seen as a capricious and demanding deity who requires constant propitiation. He is associated with cold, damp, and mountainous places. He is also a god of the underworld-like paradise of Tlalocan, where those who died from water-related causes (drowning, lightning, water-borne diseases) went, regardless of their moral conduct in life.

Accounts

In the Aztec creation myth of the Five Suns, Tlaloc presides over the fourth cosmic age—the Sun of Water (Nahui Atl, 'Four Water'). During this era, humans were created and lived on earth under Tlaloc's governance. However, after 676 years, the age ended in catastrophe: Tezcatlipoca, the rival god, stole Tlaloc's wife Xochiquetzal, and in his grief and rage, Tlaloc refused to send rain. When the people begged for rain, Tlaloc sent a rain of fire instead. Eventually, Quetzalcoatl destroyed this sun with a great flood that turned humans into fish. The story establishes Tlaloc as a solar-age ruler whose emotional state—grief, anger, neglect—determines cosmic conditions, and demonstrates the catastrophic consequences of divine conflict on humanity.

During the first month of the Aztec year (Atlcahualo, 'Ceasing of Water,' mid-February), priests conducted ceremonies to summon the rains for the coming agricultural season. Children—purchased from their parents for the purpose—were dressed in fine clothing, adorned with quetzal feathers and jade, and carried in processions to mountain shrines. The children were required to weep copiously; their tears, flowing like rain, were the ritual mechanism that stimulated Tlaloc to send precipitation. If a child did not weep, priests would tear off its fingernails, induce pain, or otherwise force tears—a practice documented by Sahagún with evident horror. The children were sacrificed at specific mountain locations (including Mount Tlaloc, the primary shrine east of Tenochtitlan), and their bodies were interred with precious offerings. The more the children wept, the more abundant the rains were expected to be.

When the Mexican government decided to move the 168-ton Tlaloc monolith from San Miguel Coatlinchán to the new Museo Nacional de Antropología in Mexico City, the event became a modern legend. Thousands of residents of Coatlinchán protested the removal, forming human chains around the statue. On the day of transport (April 16, 1964), an unprecedented torrential downpour struck Mexico City—the heaviest rainfall in decades—causing severe flooding throughout the capital. The rain was immediately interpreted by many Mexicans as Tlaloc's protest at being moved from his ancestral home. The coincidence of moving the rain god's statue and an extraordinary rainfall became part of modern Mexican folklore, demonstrating the persistence of Tlaloc-related belief in a contemporary, nominally Catholic society.

Scholarly Debates
The Teotihuacan Rain God = Tlaloc?
The Teotihuacan 'Tlaloc' is the direct predecessor of the Aztec Tlaloc—the same rain deity worshipped continuously from the Classic period (c. 200-600 CE) through the Postclassic (c. 1300-1521 CE). The iconographic similarities (goggle eyes, fangs) demonstrate unbroken continuity. ⇌ The Teotihuacan rain god is a distinct deity (or deities) whose specific identity and name are unknown, and labeling it 'Tlaloc' is a misleading Aztec retrojection that obscures Classic-period theological diversity. The visual similarities may reflect a shared Mesoamerican rain deity iconographic vocabulary rather than a single continuous deity.

What We Don't Know

The pre-Aztec history of Tlaloc—specifically the relationship between the Teotihuacan rain/storm deity and the Aztec Tlaloc—remains one of the most significant unresolved questions in Mesoamerican religious history. What was the Classic-period name of the Teotihuacan rain god? How much continuity is there between Teotihuacan, Toltec, and Aztec rain deity theology? The child sacrifice component of Tlaloc's cult, while archaeologically confirmed, raises questions about scale: how many children were sacrificed annually across the Aztec empire? Recent archaeological discoveries continue to add to the known number of child victims, suggesting the total may be significantly higher than previously estimated. The economic dimension of child sacrifice—the market in which children were purchased for ritual purposes—is poorly understood and deserves more research. The experience of Tlalocan in Aztec theology (what the paradise was actually imagined to be like) is described only briefly in colonial sources and may have been more elaborated in lost pre-conquest traditions. The post-conquest survival of Tlaloc-related beliefs and practices in syncretic Mexican Catholicism (Tlaloc's relationship to San Isidro Labrador, the patron of rain and agriculture in Mexican folk Catholicism) needs further ethnographic documentation.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Tlaloc as the ultimate weather system personified. He is not a 'person' in the human sense, but a force of nature with a personality. He is the rain that feeds your crops and the flood that destroys your village. He is the cool mist on a mountain and the terrifying thunderclap that shakes your bones. He is a reminder that nature is both life-giving and deadly, and that you are at its mercy. He is the ancient world's way of saying: 'You can't control the weather, but you can try to make it like you.' He's a god for farmers, for travelers, for anyone who has ever looked up at a stormy sky and felt both awe and fear.
