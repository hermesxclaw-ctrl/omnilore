Who It Is

In the quiet of an old Japanese bathhouse, when the last lantern is snuffed and the steam settles, a small, childlike figure might creep from the shadows. Its head is strangely pebbled, its eyes round and unblinking, and from its mouth unfurls a tongue impossibly long. It is the akaname, the filth-licker, a creature born not of malice but of neglect—a living admonition to keep one's bath and one's soul clean.

Akaname are nocturnal, emerging when humans are asleep to lick filth from bathtubs and floors. They are generally harmless, but their presence is unsettling. They are said to spawn from negative energy (inki) of accumulated grime. A more sinister variant appears as a beautiful woman and licks humans to death.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Japanese 'aka' (垢) meaning filth, scum, or dead skin, and 'name' (舐) meaning to lick. The variant 'akaneburi' uses 'neburu' (舐る), also to lick. A secondary association with 'aka' (赤) meaning red has influenced modern depictions.

Pronunciation — ah-kah-NAH-meh

Aliases — Akaneburi (垢ねぶり / 垢舐), Filth-licker, Scum-licker, Bathhouse licker, Akana-me

Ownership — Japanese

Form & Signs

Appearance — Classical Edo-period depictions show a human child with clawed feet, cropped head, and an extraordinarily long tongue. Sekien's illustration shows a figure peeking around the corner of a bathhouse. Nittō honzō zusan describes a pebbly head and round eyes. Later depictions often color the creature red, due to the pun on 'aka' (red/filth).

Symbols — The long tongue is the defining feature. The setting is typically a bathhouse or bathroom. The red color in modern art is a conventional pun, not an attested historical attribute.

Domains — Old bathhouses, dilapidated homes, bathrooms. They are attracted to uncleanliness.

Accounts I
The Hot Spring IncidentNittō honzō zusan

Edo period

A man at a hot spring in Banshū (Harima Province) allowed a woman to scrub his back. She was actually an akaneburi in disguise and licked him down to his bones, killing him. The man died; the anecdote serves as a warning.
