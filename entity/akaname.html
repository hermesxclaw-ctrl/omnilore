Who It Is

A filthy yōkai that licks the grime and filth from poorly maintained bathrooms and bathtubs, using its long, sticky tongue. Its name literally means 'filth licker'. Though disgusting, it is generally harmless and even helpful in cleaning.

Form

Small, goblin-like creature with red skin, greasy hair, a single clawed toe on each foot, and an extraordinarily long, sticky tongue

Powers

Filth consumption

Long prehensile tongue

Nocturnal stealth

Prehensile Tongue The akaname possesses an extraordinarily long tongue, capable of reaching into corners and crevices of bathing areas to lick up accumulated filth. In the lethal variant, the tongue can strip flesh from bone. Edo period (Sekien and Nittō honzō zusan) The tongue is the akaname's only apparent functional organ — it is not described as having manipulative hands or any other tool-using capability. The tongue's exact length, texture, and whether it can be weaponized are not consistently described across sources. The lethal variant's ability to devour flesh with the tongue is only attested in a single source (Nittō honzō zusan).

Spontaneous Generation (Keshō) The akaname is said to emanate or materialize (keshō 化生) from the ki or negative energy (inki) accumulated in places where filth and neglect have built up over time. This suggests the akaname is not born or created in the conventional sense but arises naturally from conditions of decay and abandonment. Kokon hyakumonogatari hyōban (1686) Presumably cannot manifest in clean environments — the akaname's existence depends on the presence of unwashed filth. Not all sources explicitly endorse spontaneous generation; some imply the akaname is a pre-existing creature drawn to filth rather than created by it.

Shapeshifting (Lethal Variant Only) A variant of the akaneburi is described as capable of assuming the guise of an attractive woman to approach and deceive bathers in hot springs. Nittō honzō zusan (early Edo period) Only attested in one source; the shapeshifting ability is not attributed to the standard akaname form. The deception only works in contexts where bathing makes the victim vulnerable. This ability contradicts the harmless nature of the akaname as depicted by Sekien and most later folklorists. It may represent a conflation with a different yōkai tradition (such as the hone-onna or bone-woman).

Behavior & Habitat

Appears in filthy bathrooms at night to lick them clean; more a warning about cleanliness than a threat

Accounts

Sekien's illustration depicts the akaname as a child-like figure with a shaved head and long tongue, standing at the corner of a bathhouse outbuilding. Notably, Sekien provided no written description or narrative — the image stands alone, relying on the viewer's familiarity with oral tradition to interpret its meaning. The bathhouse setting is an outhouse-style private bath separate from the main residence, suggesting the akaname was understood as a household rather than public-bath phenomenon.

In the hot springs of Banshū (Harima Province, modern Hyōgo Prefecture), a man encountered a woman who offered to scrub his back. The woman was in fact an akaneburi in disguise. As she scrubbed, she began licking the man, stripping away his flesh until only his skeleton remained, and he died. This anecdote is the primary textual evidence for the lethal variant of the akaname/akaneburi tradition.

In the Yo-Kai Watch anime, the protagonist Nate (Keita in Japanese) becomes possessed by an Akaname named Tublappa (localized from Akaname). The possession gives him an uncontrollable urge to lick food clean, then progresses to licking people's hair. His friend Katie shares her salty-sweet candy, which satisfies the Akaname's impulse and allows it to leave Nate's body. The Akaname then descends to a store to purchase more candy, revealing its child-like, impulse-driven nature.

Content Notes

The Nittō honzō zusan describes an akaneburi that takes the form of a beautiful woman, approaches men at hot springs under the pretense of scrubbing their backs, and then licks away their flesh and blood until nothing remains but a skeleton. The anecdote concerning a man at the Banshū hot springs who died in exactly this manner is the only detailed account. This variant represents a drastically more violent interpretation of the akaneburi than the harmless filth-licker of Sekien's illustration.

This text is a Japanese materia medica (pharmacological) work, not a dedicated yōkai compendium. The inclusion of the akaneburi in a medical text may reflect the era's folk-biological understanding that connected environmental filth, miasma, illness, and supernatural entities. The story may also serve as a public health caution about accepting services from strangers at mixed-gender or unsupervised hot springs.

Nittō honzō zusan (日東本草図纂), compiled by Kanda Genki

Early Western scholars of Japanese folklore, writing during the Meiji and Taishō periods, sometimes described yōkai like the akaname using exoticizing or infantilizing language. Lafcadio Hearn and other early translators occasionally sanitized or romanticized the more disturbing elements of yōkai lore for Western audiences. The akaname's darker variant has been particularly affected — many English-language summaries omit the lethal akaneburi entirely.

The selective transmission of yōkai lore through translation has been documented by folklorists including Michael Foster (Pandemonic Japan, 2008) and Hiroko Yoda (Yokai Attack!, 2008). The akaname's dual nature (harmless vs. lethal) is a case study in how translation and compilation choices shape folklore reception.

Cross-reference: Lafcadio Hearn, Kwaidan (1904); Fanny Hagin Mayer's translations

A way to hold it in your mind

There is a creature that lives in the forgotten corners of bathhouses — not to harm, but to consume the scum and dead skin that accumulates where people wash. Its tongue, unnaturally long, reaches into the spaces between floorboards and around the edges of wooden tubs, lapping up the evidence of neglect. If you keep your bath clean, you may never see the akaname. But if you let the filth build, if you leave the water standing and the grime untouched, do not be surprised when a red-skinned child with a shaved head and an impossibly long tongue stands waiting in the shadows, licking — not you, but everything you left behind.
