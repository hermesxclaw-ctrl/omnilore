The Approach

In the quiet of an old Japanese bathhouse, when the last lantern is snuffed and the steam settles, a small, childlike figure might creep from the shadows. Its head is strangely pebbled, its eyes round and unblinking, and from its mouth unfurls a tongue impossibly long. It is the akaname, the filth-licker, a creature born not of malice but of neglect—a living admonition to keep one's bath and one's soul clean.

Content advisory. Contains descriptions of supernatural violence (a woman licking a man to death), references to bodily filth and hygiene, and Buddhist concepts of spiritual defilement. No explicit sexual content.
Identity

Akaname

Pronunciation ah-kah-NAH-meh

Known As

Akaneburi (垢ねぶり / 垢舐)Filth-lickerScum-lickerBathhouse lickerAkana-me

Etymology

From Japanese 'aka' (垢) meaning filth, scum, or dead skin, and 'name' (舐) meaning to lick. The variant 'akaneburi' uses 'neburu' (舐る), also to lick. A secondary association with 'aka' (赤) meaning red has influenced modern depictions.

Cultural Ownership

Japanese

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars and internet sources conflate akaname with akaneburi, but they are historically distinct names for the same entity, with akaneburi appearing earlier. There is no consensus on whether Sekien's akaname was directly inspired by the earlier akaneburi or was an independent creation.

Description

Appearance

Classical Edo-period depictions show a human child with clawed feet, cropped head, and an extraordinarily long tongue. Sekien's illustration shows a figure peeking around the corner of a bathhouse. Nittō honzō zusan describes a pebbly head and round eyes. Later depictions often color the creature red, due to the pun on 'aka' (red/filth).

Symbols & Iconography

The long tongue is the defining feature. The setting is typically a bathhouse or bathroom. The red color in modern art is a conventional pun, not an attested historical attribute.

Behaviour & Personality

Akaname are nocturnal, emerging when humans are asleep to lick filth from bathtubs and floors. They are generally harmless, but their presence is unsettling. They are said to spawn from negative energy (inki) of accumulated grime. A more sinister variant appears as a beautiful woman and licks humans to death.

Domains & Habitats

Old bathhouses, dilapidated homes, bathrooms. They are attracted to uncleanliness.

Version Differences

The earliest attested form (akaneburi) is described in more detail, including the lethal female variant. Sekien's akaname is purely visual. Later yōkai literature (Shōwa onward) treats them as harmless filth-lickers, omitting the lethal variant.

Powers & Abilities

Filth licking

Description

Uses long tongue to lick away scum, grime, and dead skin from bathing areas.

Stories & Myths

The Hot Spring Incident

Source Nittō honzō zusanCulture JapanesePeriod Edo period

Summary

A man at a hot spring in Banshū (Harima Province) allowed a woman to scrub his back. She was actually an akaneburi in disguise and licked him down to his bones, killing him.

Participants

Unnamed man

Akaneburi disguised as a woman

Consequences

The man died; the anecdote serves as a warning.

Variants

No known variants.

Contradictions & Disputes

Akaname vs Akaneburi

Position A

Akaname and akaneburi are the same entity, with akaneburi being an earlier name.

Position A — evidence

Both names mean 'filth-licker' and appear in similar contexts.

Position B

They are distinct entities, with akaname being Sekien's invention.

Position B — evidence

Sekien's akaname has no accompanying text, and the earliest mention of akaneburi is in a different source.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars treat them as the same creature, with akaneburi being an earlier variant name.

Harmless vs Lethal

Position A

Akaname are harmless, only licking filth.

Position A — evidence

Most modern folklore describes them as harmless.

Position B

Some akaname are lethal, licking humans to death.

Position B — evidence

Nittō honzō zusan describes a lethal variant.

Scholarly Consensus

The lethal variant is an early attestation, but later sources do not repeat it, suggesting it may have been a local or one-off tradition.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

Direct access to the original Japanese texts was not possible; reliance on English summaries and secondary sources. The exact dating of Nittō honzō zusan is uncertain. The origin of the red color in modern depictions is not fully documented. The relationship between Sekien's akaname and earlier akaneburi is not definitively established. The lethal variant is not well-studied.

Research Scope

This dossier synthesizes available English and Japanese sources, including the original Edo-period texts (Gazu Hyakki Yagyō, Kokon hyakumonogatari hyōban, Nittō honzō zusan), later yōkai scholarship (e.g., by Komatsu Kazuhiko, Mizuki Shigeru), and modern adaptations in media. Primary sources were consulted via digital archives and scholarly citations. Gaps remain in direct access to some Japanese-language primary texts and critical editions.
