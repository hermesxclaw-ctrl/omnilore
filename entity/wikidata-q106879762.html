Who It Is

In the shadow of a father's grief, two sisters met a fate that would stain a city's conscience—yet history remembers them only as 'the daughters of Scedasus,' their names lost to time. One of them, known simply as Hippo, became a symbol of innocence betrayed and a catalyst for vengeance that echoed through the halls of Spartan justice.

Inferred from the narrative: she is portrayed as virtuous and honorable, choosing death over dishonor. The act of suicide reflects the cultural value placed on female chastity in ancient Greece.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Hippo' (Ἱππώ) is derived from the Greek word for 'horse' (ἵππος), possibly reflecting a family connection to horses or a nickname. It is not a common personal name in Greek mythology.

Pronunciation — HIP-poh (English approximation); in ancient Greek: hip-PŌ

Aliases — Hippo

Accounts I
The Daughters of ScedasusPlutarch (Moralia and Life of Pelopidas), Diodorus Siculus, Pausanias

Classical/Hellenistic (the story is set in the early 4th century BCE, but the earliest surviving accounts are later)

Scedasus, a wealthy man of Leuctra in Boeotia, had two daughters, one of whom was Hippo (according to Diodorus). While the daughters were away from home, they were encountered by a group of Spartan ambassadors who were traveling to Delphi or elsewhere. The Spartans, overcome by lust, violated the girls. In shame and despair, the daughters took their own lives. Scedasus, upon discovering the tragedy, went to Sparta to demand justice from the ephors, but they dismissed his pleas. In his grief and rage, he cursed the Spartans and then killed himself on his daughters' grave. The curse was said to be fulfilled years later when the Thebans, led by Epaminondas, defeated the Spartans at the Battle of Leuctra in 371 BCE, a defeat that shattered Spartan military supremacy. The immediate consequence was the deaths of the daughters and Scedasus. The long-term consequence was the curse that contributed to the Spartan defeat at Leuctra, which marked a turning point in Greek history.
