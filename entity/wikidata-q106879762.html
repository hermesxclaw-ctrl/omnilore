The Approach

In the shadow of a father's grief, two sisters met a fate that would stain a city's conscience—yet history remembers them only as 'the daughters of Scedasus,' their names lost to time. One of them, known simply as Hippo, became a symbol of innocence betrayed and a catalyst for vengeance that echoed through the halls of Spartan justice.

Content advisory. Contains themes of sexual violence, suicide, and murder; historical accounts may include graphic details.
Identity

Hippo

Pronunciation HIP-poh (English approximation); in ancient Greek: hip-PŌ

Etymology

The name 'Hippo' (Ἱππώ) is derived from the Greek word for 'horse' (ἵππος), possibly reflecting a family connection to horses or a nickname. It is not a common personal name in Greek mythology.

Cultural Ownership

Greek (specifically associated with the region of Thessaly and the city of Sparta in later retellings)

Disputed Identifications

Some modern scholars have conflated this Hippo with other mythological figures of the same name, such as the Amazon Hippo or the nymph Hippo. The story of Scedasus' daughters is sometimes confused with the myth of the Leuctrides, but they are distinct.

Appearance

No specific physical description survives. As a legendary figure, she is typically imagined as a young maiden of marriageable age, likely of noble birth given her father's status.

No known iconography. The story is primarily textual, and no ancient artistic representations of Hippo or her sister are known.

Inferred from the narrative: she is portrayed as virtuous and honorable, choosing death over dishonor. The act of suicide reflects the cultural value placed on female chastity in ancient Greece.

Domains & Habitats

The story is set in Leuctra, a town in Boeotia, and later in Sparta. No specific domains or habitats beyond these locations.

The story's details vary slightly: in some versions they are violated by two Spartans, in others by more; the manner of suicide (hanging vs. other) may also vary.

Stories & Myths

The Daughters of Scedasus

Source Plutarch (Moralia and Life of Pelopidas), Diodorus Siculus, PausaniasCulture GreekPeriod Classical/Hellenistic (the story is set in the early 4th century BCE, but the earliest surviving accounts are later)

Summary

Scedasus, a wealthy man of Leuctra in Boeotia, had two daughters, one of whom was Hippo (according to Diodorus). While the daughters were away from home, they were encountered by a group of Spartan ambassadors who were traveling to Delphi or elsewhere. The Spartans, overcome by lust, violated the girls. In shame and despair, the daughters took their own lives. Scedasus, upon discovering the tragedy, went to Sparta to demand justice from the ephors, but they dismissed his pleas. In his grief and rage, he cursed the Spartans and then killed himself on his daughters' grave. The curse was said to be fulfilled years later when the Thebans, led by Epaminondas, defeated the Spartans at the Battle of Leuctra in 371 BCE, a defeat that shattered Spartan military supremacy.

Participants

Hippo

Scedasus' other daughter (unnamed or named Miletia)

Scedasus

Spartan ambassadors (two named in some versions: Phrourarchos and Parthenios)

Consequences

The immediate consequence was the deaths of the daughters and Scedasus. The long-term consequence was the curse that contributed to the Spartan defeat at Leuctra, which marked a turning point in Greek history.

Variants

Diodorus names the daughters Hippo and Miletia. Plutarch does not name them. Some later sources may name them differently. The story is sometimes localized to different places, but the core elements remain consistent.

Sexual violence

Source Diodorus Siculus, Plutarch

Description

Hippo and her sister were raped by Spartan ambassadors. This act is described in ancient sources as a violation and a crime.

Scholarly Context

Ancient Greek society placed high value on female chastity, and rape was considered a severe crime against the family and the individual. The daughters' suicide reflects the stigma and shame associated with rape in that culture.

Suicide

Source Diodorus Siculus, Plutarch

Description

Hippo and her sister took their own lives after the assault, unable to bear the dishonor. Their father also committed suicide after failing to obtain justice.

Scholarly Context

Suicide in ancient Greece was often seen as a means of preserving honor, particularly for women who had been violated. The act was not universally condemned, and in this context, it is portrayed as a tragic but understandable response.

Type textDate c. 100 CELocator 856C-DLanguage Ancient Greek

Translation

Plutarch recounts that Scedasus, a man of Leuctra in Boeotia, had two daughters who were violated by Spartan ambassadors. The daughters, unable to bear the shame, killed themselves. Scedasus then went to Sparta seeking justice, but was ignored. He cursed the Spartans and killed himself on his daughters' tomb. Type textDate c. 2nd century CELocator 9.13.4-6Language Ancient Greek

Pausanias mentions the story of Scedasus and his daughters, stating that their tomb was at Leuctra and that the Spartans' defeat was attributed to their curse. He does not name the daughters individually.

Plutarch recounts the story of the daughters' violation and the curse, linking it to the Battle of Leuctra. He mentions that Scedasus' daughters were violated by Spartans and that their father's curse was fulfilled.
