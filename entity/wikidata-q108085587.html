Who It Is

In the dust of northern Mesopotamia, when a wound festered or a curse settled in the bones, they did not call a god — they called a doctoress. Her name was Ninkarrak, the Akkadian Lady who bandaged with one hand and could loose wasting disease with the other, whose dogs waited at the threshold of her temples in Sippar and Terqa to heal or to devour.

Attested behavior is twofold. (1) Healing: compassionate surgeon, great doctoress (azugallatu), who responds to incantation, cleanses, bandages, makes flesh grow, undoes witchcraft and disease. Invoked in Šurpu and healing spells as beneficent. (2) Wrathful disease-inflictor in curse formulas: paired with Išḫara, she is called to impose incurable illness, skin disease, or wasting on oath-breakers and monument violators. Name & Tongue

Etymology — Unknown and disputed. No consensus. Standard spelling dnin-kar-ra-ak not attested before 2nd millennium BCE; earlier Ur III forms dnin-kar7, dnin-kar-ra; Old Babylonian variants include dnin-kar, dnin-kar-ak, dnin-ni-ka-ra-ak, dnin-ḫar-ra (Mari). Proposals: (1) Tallqvist: topographical Lady of Karra

Pronunciation — Akkadian /ninkarrak/ or /ninkarrak/; Sumerogram reading NIN.KAR.RA.AK; syllabic spellings suggest /ninkarak/ ~ /nikarak/ with gemination variable. Emphasis on final syllable in Akkadian. Ugaritic syllabic ni-ka-rak (x) suggests /nikarak/; Luwian Nikarawa /nikarawa/ if identical.

Powers
Divine Surgery and Bandaging

Attested as core healing power. In incantations she is invoked to clean the wound (simmu), remove pus, apply bandage (ligature), and make flesh and skin regrow. Texts describe her as using hands, bandages, and possibly scalpel. She acts as prototype for human asû (physician).

Limit: Requires incantation and ritual; not instantaneous miracle. Limited to conditions understood as wound, skin disease, witchcraft-induced illness. Does not include resurrection of dead; does not override divine decrees of fate without proper ritual.

Infliction of Disease and Wasting (Curse Power)

In oath and monument curse formulas, Ninkarrak is empowered to strike transgressors with incurable disease, skin lesions, or consumption. Paired with Išḫara, she embodies punitive disease. This is not malevolent but juridical — disease as sanction.

Limit: Conditional on oath violation or monument damage; not random. In later periods healing aspect eclipses cursing, but curse function persists in peripheral Luwian context.

Command of Dogs as Agents

Dogs act as extensions of the goddess: they can lick/heal, guard the temple, or devour offenders. Votive dogs at Terqa suggest dogs were kept in temple precincts. Carchemish Luwian curse explicitly: may her dogs devour his...

Limit: Dogs are symbolic/ritual agents, not independent monsters; efficacy tied to goddess's will and to violation. No attestation of Ninkarrak transforming into a dog herself.

Incantation Authority as Azugallatu (Great Physician)

Title azugallatu (great doctoress) in Šurpu gives her authority over asûtu (physician craft) and āšipūtu (exorcism). She authorizes and empowers human healers, legitimizes medical incantations, and counters witchcraft (kaššāpūtu).

Limit: Operates within Mesopotamian ritual framework; requires correct recitation, offerings, and purity. Cannot act without invocation. Vulnerable to theological syncretism where Gula usurps title in later first millennium.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No canonical anthropomorphic description survives in literary texts. Unlike Ishtar or Shamash, Ninkarrak is not given epithets of radiance, stature, or dress in preserved hymns. What is attested is functional iconography: she is the divine physician who cleans wounds and binds bandages, implying human-like surgical hands. Visual representation is indirect via votive symbolism. Attested: dog figurines and canine remains in votive deposits at Terqa

Symbols — Primary symbol is the dog (kalbu). Attested archaeologically: terracotta dog figurines, dog burials, and dog imagery in Terqa temple deposits; textually dogs as agents of Nikarawa/Ninkarrak in Carchemish curse. Dogs are interpreted as both healing companions (licking wounds, guarding) and instruments of punishment (devouring offenders). Shared with other healing goddesses (Gula's dog is most famous), so dog alone is not diagnostic for Ninkar

Domains — Domains: medicine, surgery, wound treatment, incantation healing, control of disease, protection against witchcraft, and by extension justice via disease-punishment in oaths. Habitats: temples (E-karakh / Temple of Ninkarrak) at Sippar and Terqa as earthly residences; divine realm not cosmologically distinct (not underworld or heaven-specific). Accounts I
The Curse of Ninkarrak and Išḫara - Sargonic Oath SanctionRoyal inscriptions of Sargon, Rimush, and Naram-Sin of Akkad (RIME 2); later copies of Sargonic curse formulas

ca. 2334-2154 BCE, Old Akkadian period

In the earliest secure attestations of Ninkarrak, she does not heal but punishes. At the end of royal decrees and treaty oaths, the king calls upon Ninkarrak and Išḫara together: whoever breaks the oath, alters the inscription, or harms the king's foundation, may Ninkarrak afflict him with an incurable wound and Išḫara with a wasting disease. The pairing is formulaic and appears in multiple Sargonic texts, indicating a fixed divine duo for juridical curses. This is the evidence used to argue Ninkarrak's original sphere was disease-infliction, later inverted into healing. Establishes Ninkarrak's early character as feared enforcer of oaths; provides chronological anchor for her cult; later healing incantations must be understood against this punitive background. Shows northern Akkadian pantheon distinct from southern Sumerian.

May Ninkarrak Bandage - The Healing IncantationOld Babylonian healing incantations from Sippar, Terqa region, and Alalakh (AlT 126 restored as ne-ni-ka-ra-ak); parallels in BAM medical corpus

ca. 18th-14th century BCE, Old Babylonian to Middle Babylonian

A patient suffering from wound (simmu), skin lesion, or witchcraft-induced illness lies before the exorcist. The incantation recites: May Ninkarrak, the great doctoress, cleanse the wound, may she bandage it, may she make flesh grow, may she close the lesion. In some versions she is asked to apply a bandage with her pure hands and to pour healing oil. The spell is to be recited over bandages or salve, which then become efficacious through her authority. Unlike curse texts, here she is supplicated as compassionate surgeon. Illustrates functional shift to healer; provides model for human medical practice; votive dog figurines at Terqa may have been offered after recitation of such spells. Formula is shared with Gula/Ninisina, showing interchangeability in ritual practice.

The Lady of Terqa - Temple and Votive DogsArchaeological and administrative texts from Terqa (Tell Ashara): temple architecture, votive deposits, administrative tablets naming Ninkarrak; excavations by Buccellati, Kelly-Buccellati, Rouault

ca. 19th-17th century BCE, Old Babylonian/Mari period, with use into Middle Babylonian

At Terqa, on the Euphrates bend, Ninkarrak possessed the city's main healing temple. Excavators uncovered a mudbrick temple with benches, altars, and pits containing hundreds of votive gifts: faience beads, bronze tools, and notably terracotta dogs, some with inscribed bases dedicated to Ninkarrak. Administrative tablets list deliveries of oil, grain, and textiles for the goddess, and mention a priesthood serving her. The temple's stratigraphy helped date the Middle Euphrates chronology. The dogs, often interpreted as thank-offerings from healed patients, physically manifest her symbol and suggest live dogs may have been kept in the precinct. Confirms Terqa as second major cult center alongside Sippar; provides material correlate for textual dog association; demonstrates northern distribution and economic importance of healing cult; finds now in Deir ez-Zor Museum and Louvre.

Accounts II
Ninkarrak the Default Akkadian Physician - Theology in An = AnumGod list An = Anum (Tablet V) and its forerunners; commentary by Litke, Cavigneaux-Krebernik

Old Babylonian forerunner ca. 1800 BCE; canonical version Middle Babylonian to Neo-Assyrian ca. 1400-700 BCE

In the great god list that systematized the pantheon for scribes, theologians needed to organize the crowded field of healing goddesses. They assigned Ninkarrak as the Akkadian counterpart to Sumerian healing goddesses: where Ninisina is lady of Isin, Ninkarrak is the Akkadian one. The list gives her alternate name Ninekisiga (lady of the house of funerary offerings) and equates logographic dNIN.IN.DUB with her, explaining it as derived from Ninisina's NIN.IN. This is not a myth but a scholarly story about identity: it tells how Babylonian scholars made sense of her foreign name by giving her a Sumerian-looking writing and a place in the system. Codifies syncretism while preserving distinct entry; explains variable orthography as scholarly Sumerianization; influences later lexical lists and modern identification of logograms. Shows conscious theological construction noted by Westenholz and Sibbing-Plantholt.

Ninkarrak in Šurpu - The Great Doctoress Against WitchcraftŠurpu incantation series, Tablet II and V-VI, Nineveh and Assur manuscripts (Reiner edition)

Standard version ca. 8th-7th century BCE, based on second-millennium prototypes

In the ritual to undo curses caused by witchcraft, the exorcist lists gods who can loosen the ban. Ninkarrak is invoked as azugallatu, great doctoress, alongside Asalluhi/Marduk and Gula. The patient is described as bound by a witch's spell, suffering bodily pain. The incantation asks Ninkarrak to cut the witchcraft, cleanse the body, and restore health, using her authority over both disease and magic. Burning of figurines and recitation accompany the invocation. Elevates Ninkarrak to high rank in anti-witchcraft pantheon; demonstrates persistence of her healing authority into first millennium; provides epithet azugallatu as key identifier in later texts.

Nikarawa's Dogs at Carchemish - The Luwian Monument CurseHieroglyphic Luwian inscription KARKAMIŠ A6 (and related A11b), Storm-God temple, Carchemish; editions by Gelb 1938, Hawkins 2000, Hutter-Braunsar 2020

ca. 9th-8th century BCE, Iron Age

A ruler of Carchemish dedicates a monument and seals it with a curse: Whoever erases this inscription, damages the stele, or harms the temple, may Nikarawa's dogs devour him, may his flesh be torn. The goddess's name is written dni-ka+ra/i-wa/i-sa2, read Nikarawa. Since Gelb, Assyriologists have identified Nikarawa with Ninkarrak, seeing the Luwian form as phonetic adaptation of the Akkadian name, with dogs as diagnostic link. If correct, this is the westernmost and latest attestation, showing Ninkarrak's curse function survived 1500 years after Sargonic formulas, now in Anatolian hieroglyphs. Hutter-Braunsar recently challenged the reading, proposing alternative deity, but as of 2022 identification remains plausible. Would extend Ninkarrak's geography to Luwian Anatolia and chronology into Iron Age; confirms continuity of dog-curse motif; fuels debate on Syrian substrate survival. If rejected, shows limits of dog-based identification.

Ninkarrak is just another name for Gula, or all Mesopotamian healing goddesses are the same. — Theology did equate them (An = Anum lists Ninkarrak as Akkadian equivalent), but cult, geography, and early history differ. Ninkarrak is northern Akkadian, best attested at Sippar and Terqa, with early curse role; Gula becomes dominant later in south and first millennium. Treating them as identical erases distinct origins and regional variation documented by temple archives and distribution.

Ninkarrak's name means Lady of the Harbor who is a prostitute, and she was a goddess of sacred prostitution. — Harbor/prostitution etymology (Lambert/Steinkeller) is one of several unproven proposals and is now rejected by specialists. It relied on discredited temple prostitution model and has no link to Ninkarrak's attested sphere as physician and disease-controller. No text associates her with sexuality or prostitution.

Ninkarrak was a Sumerian goddess from the earliest Sumerian pantheon. — No attestation before late 3rd millennium, standard spelling not before 2nd millennium, variable orthography suggests non-Sumerian origin. Current scholarship (Westenholz, Sibbing-Plantholt) argues she is Akkadian/northern Syrian, possibly from pre-Semitic substrate, Sumerianized secondarily with NIN prefix. She is absent from earliest Sumerian god lists and emesal glossaries.

Images of a goddess with a dog are always Ninkarrak. — Dog is shared symbol of several healing goddesses, most famously Gula. Without inscription naming Ninkarrak, a dog-associated goddess cannot be securely identified as Ninkarrak. Terqa votive dogs are attributed to Ninkarrak because of inscribed bases and temple context, not dog alone.
