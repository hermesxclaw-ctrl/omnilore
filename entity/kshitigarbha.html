Until the last hell is emptied, until the last soul finds peace, I will remain—not as a Buddha, but as a companion in the dark.

Who It Is

Kshitigarbha (Kṣitigarbha), whose name means 'Earth Womb' or 'Earth Treasury,' is the bodhisattva who made the most extraordinary of all vows: he would not attain Buddhahood until all the hell realms were emptied of suffering beings. This monumental commitment makes him the patron of all who suffer in the lower realms of existence—particularly beings in the hells, hungry ghosts, and those facing the judgment of the afterlife. He is also the protector of children, travelers, and pregnant women, a role which has made him one of the most beloved figures in Japanese Buddhism, where he is known as Jizo. The Kshitigarbha Sutra tells that in a past life, Kshitigarbha was a Brahmin girl whose mother had been reborn in hell due to slandering the dharma. Through her filial piety and immense offerings to the Buddha of her era, she not only rescued her mother but vowed to liberate all beings from hell for all future time. Kshitigarbha is typically depicted as a Buddhist monk with a shaved head, holding a wish-fulfilling jewel (cintamani) that radiates light to dispel the darkness of the hells, and a staff (khakkhara) with six rings—representing the six realms of existence—that he shakes to awaken beings from their delusion. In Japan, Jizo statues are ubiquitous: they line roadsides, temple grounds, and cemeteries. They are often dressed in red bibs and caps by grieving parents who have lost children, for Jizo is believed to protect the souls of children who have died before their parents. In the Sai no Kawara myth, deceased children are forced to pile stones on the riverbank of the underworld as penance, but demons knock them down; Jizo hides the children in his robe and carries them to safety. This role as the comforter of the most vulnerable—children, suffering spirits, those in hell—has made Kshitigarbha one of Buddhism's most emotionally resonant figures.
