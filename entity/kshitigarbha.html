Who It Is
Form

In East Asian iconography, Kṣitigarbha is uniquely depicted as a Buddhist monk — shaven head, simple saffron or grey monk's robes, standing on a lotus base — unlike other bodhisattvas who wear royal Indian attire. He carries a khakkhara (six-ringed pilgrim's staff) in his right hand to alert small creatures of his approach and to force open the gates of hell. In his left hand he holds a cintamani (wish-fulfilling jewel), tear-shaped or spherical, symbolizing enlightenment and the ability to illuminate darkness. His face bears the idealized features of a buddha: third eye, elongated ears, serene expression. In Chinese traditions he often wears the Five Buddha Crown (五佛冠), bearing images of the Five Tathāgatas. In Japanese practice, statues are frequently adorned with red bibs, children's clothing, or kasa hats, reflecting his role as protector of the young. In Vajrayāna contexts he may appear as a peaceful white Indian prince with a sprout and bell, or as the wrathful Lokastotrapūjā-nātha — dark blue, three faces (right yellow, left purple), six arms holding vajra, skull bowl, severed heads, embracing a blue consort. Mount Osorezan in Aomori Prefecture, Japan, is visually identified with the Sai no Kawara landscape of children's limbo.

Powers

Hell Liberation Kṣitigarbha's primary power is his ability to descend into the hell realms (Naraka) and liberate suffering beings. His vow not to attain Buddhahood until all hells are emptied makes him the supreme savior of the damned. In the Kṣitigarbha Sūtra, he instructs beings in the six worlds during the intermediate age between Śākyamuni's parinirvana and Maitreya's advent.

Wisdom of the Earth His name derives from the earth (kṣiti), and he is associated with Prithvī Devī, the Earth goddess. In agricultural and subterranean protective rites, he embodies the awakened earth principle, pacifying disturbances of earthly and subterranean spirits. His mantra from the Mahāvairocana Tantra produces the 'Samādhi Realm of Adamantine Indestructible Conduct.'

Psychopomp of Children In Japanese culture, Kṣitigarbha (Jizō) is the patron of deceased and unborn children. He protects mizuko (water children) — stillborn, miscarried, or aborted souls — in the ritual of mizuko kuyō. He hides children in his robes at the Sai no Kawara when oni attack. Stone piles left at his statues generate merit to shorten the children's karmic suffering.

Truth-Discernment His mount Diting possesses a single horn that receives information across the universe and hound ears capable of distinguishing virtuous from foolish, good from evil. This reflects Kṣitigarbha's own role as a judge and evaluator of karmic character.

Wrathful Subjugation As Lokastotrapūjā-nātha in Tibetan Buddhism, Kṣitigarbha transforms into a destroyer of haughty ones (dregs pa kun 'dul). In this Heruka form he is dark blue, three-faced (right yellow, left purple), six-armed, four-legged, winged, adorned with bone ornaments and flames of pristine awareness, holding a vajra, skull bowl, and severed heads. This form subdues worldly disturbances and arrogant spirits.

Cosmic Directional Governance As one of the Eight Great Bodhisattvas, Kṣitigarbha is associated with one of the eight directions (in some enumerations the southwest or earth direction). In the Bardo Thödol mandala he sits to the right of Vajrasattva-Akṣobhya in the eastern direction, associated with the vajra family, water element, and mirror-like wisdom.

Behavior & Habitat

Kṣitigarbha's defining characteristic is his salvific patience. He has sworn not to attain full Buddhahood until every being in the six realms — especially the denizens of hell — has been liberated. This makes him the most approachable and accessible of bodhisattvas for ordinary petitioners: he does not withdraw into transcendent radiance but remains embedded in the darkest, most painful realms of existence. In Japanese folklore he is the roadside deity (dōsojin) who shelters travelers, children, and the deceased; grieving parents leave toys, bibs, and stone piles at his statues to shorten their children's karmic suffering. The stone-stacking practice at Sai no Kawara is an act of merit-making on behalf of dead children, though the piles are repeatedly toppled by oni. Jizō is said to hide the children in his robes when demons torment them. At temples like Tōsen-ji in Tokyo, petitioners tie ropes to his statue and untie them when wishes are granted; ungranted wish-ropes are cut at New Year.

Modern Interpretations

East Asian Buddhism Kṣitigarbha is one of the most widely venerated bodhisattvas in Chinese, Japanese, Korean, and Vietnamese Buddhism. His shrines occupy central positions in temple memorial halls and mausoleums. In Japan, Jizō statues are ubiquitous at roadsides, graveyards, and temple entrances. Japanese Folk Religion Jizō is one of the most beloved of all Japanese divinities, functioning as protector of children, travelers, pilgrims, and the souls in hell. The mizuko kuyō ritual for aborted and stillborn children has become a defining practice of contemporary Japanese Buddhism, with entire shrines dedicated to Mizuko Jizō. Chinese Buddhism and Taoism In China, Dizang is one of the Four Great Bodhisattvas and the central figure of the Dizang Bodhisattva Pūrvapraṇidhāna Sūtra. His birthday is celebrated on the 30th day of the 7th lunar month during the Hungry Ghost Festival. Dizang'an temples blend Buddhist and Taoist elements, enshrining underworld judges alongside the bodhisattva. Korean Buddhism Kṣitigarbha (Jijang Bosal) is deeply associated with Mount Jiuhua through the Silla prince Kim Gyo-gak. Korean Joseon dynasty paintings depict him as the supreme ruler of the underworld alongside the Ten Kings of Hell. Tibetan Buddhism As one of the Eight Great Bodhisattvas and a Heruka, Kṣitigarbha appears in Nyingma refuge fields and Mahāyoga mandalas. Scholarly Debates
What We Don't Know

The Kṣitigarbha Bodhisattva Pūrvapraṇidhāna Sūtra has no extant Sanskrit manuscript, which prevents direct philological comparison of the Chinese translation with its presumed original. The debate between Indian-origin and Chinese-composition hypotheses remains unresolved and would benefit from a critical edition of parallel Tibetan or Central Asian fragments. The precise historical formation of the Sai no Kawara legend is fragmentary: the Otogizōshi texts from the Muromachi period are the earliest written attestations, but oral and performative precursors may have circulated in Heian-period Japan. The mizuko kuyō phenomenon exploded in visibility after the 1970s, but its precise pre-modern history is poorly documented. The Tibetan tantric traditions specifically devoted to Lokastotrapūjā-nātha are fewer than for other Eight Great Herukas, and a full reconstruction of his sādhana cycles would require access to the Rinchen Terdzö and Nyingma rgyud collections. Korean Joseon dynasty paintings of Jijang with the Ten Kings of Hell are documented but a systematic iconographic study of those paintings is not yet complete in available sources.

A way to hold it in your mind

Kṣitigarbha is not the transcendent bodhisattva of distant vows — he is the one who stays. While Avalokiteśvara withdraws and Śākyamuni enters nirvana, Kṣitigarbha pledges to remain in the darkest, most painful realms until every single being has been liberated. This makes him the most immanent and accessible figure in the entire Buddhist pantheon: the deity parents petition when they have lost a child, the traveler invokes when lost on a dark road, and the dying call upon when they fear falling into hell. He is the patron of the forgotten, the karmically damned, and the prematurely dead — a cosmic social worker who has sworn never to quit.
