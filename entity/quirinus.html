Who They Are

Quirinus was an ancient Roman god who formed, with Jupiter and Mars, the Archaic Triad that predated the Capitoline Triad. He was the deified Romulus, the founder and first king of Rome, who was said to have been taken up to heaven in a whirlwind during a military review on the Campus Martius and transformed into the god Quirinus. His name is the root of Quirites — the term for Roman citizens in their civilian capacity — and he represented the Roman people in their peaceful, assembled state, as Mars represented them in arms.

Quirinus was depicted as a mature, bearded man in the prime of life, dressed in the trabea (the short purple-striped toga of Roman kings and augurs) and carrying the lituus (the curved augur's staff). He was not a warrior god, despite his origin as Romulus — rather, he embodied the settled, lawful, and civic aspects of Roman identity. His temple stood on the Quirinal Hill (which bears his name), one of Rome's seven hills, where the Sabines — among whom Quirinus may have originated — had their settlement.

Quirinus's cult was served by the Flamen Quirinalis, the third-ranking flamen after the Flamen Dialis (Jupiter) and Flamen Martialis (Mars). His festival, the Quirinalia, was celebrated on February 17. The god's Sabine origins are suggested by the tradition that the Quirinal Hill was settled by Sabines led by King Titus Tatius, who co-ruled Rome with Romulus after the Rape of the Sabine Women. Some scholars propose that Quirinus was originally a Sabine war god (perhaps cognate with the Sabine word curis, 'spear') who was transformed into a civic deity as Rome absorbed Sabine elements.

The apotheosis of Romulus into Quirinus was a foundational myth of Roman state religion. According to Livy, Romulus was reviewing troops on the Campus Martius when a storm descended and he vanished from sight. The senators present proclaimed that he had been taken up to heaven by Mars, his divine father. A witness, the senator Proculus Julius, later reported that Romulus had appeared to him in divine form and declared: 'Go, tell the Romans that it is the will of heaven that my Rome should be the capital of the world. Let them cultivate the arts of war, and let them know and teach their children that no human power can resist Roman arms.' This narrative established the precedent for imperial apotheosis, by which Roman emperors could be deified after death.

Quirinus faded in importance during the later Republic as the Capitoline Triad (Jupiter, Juno, Minerva) supplanted the Archaic Triad. Yet his conceptual significance — the god who embodied the Roman people as a civic community — remained latent in the term Quirites, which Roman magistrates used when addressing the assembled citizenry in their peaceful, civilian identity.

Form

No definitive, attested description of Quirinus's physical appearance survives. Unlike gods with established Greek statuary types (like Jupiter or Mars), Quirinus was not typically rendered in a unique, recognizable form. He was a numen, a divine force, rather than a fully anthropomorphized character in early Roman religion. When depicted on coins (rarely), he is usually shown as a bearded, mature man in military or civic dress, indistinguishable from a generic Roman citizen or a depiction of Romulus. This lack of a visual identity is itself significant: it suggests he was a god of the collective rather than a personality.

Powers

Protector of the Citizen Body Quirinus's primary power was his guardianship over the Roman people as a collective. He ensured the survival, unity, and rights of the Quirites. He was the divine embodiment of the state itself. All versions His protection was tied to the performance of proper ritual by his flamen and the state. Neglect of his cult could lead to his withdrawal of favor. None.

God of the Civic Assembly He was the patron of the comitia curiata, the earliest assembly of the Roman people. This made him a god of civic law, order, and political participation. Archaic Roman As Rome's political system evolved, his direct relevance to the assembly diminished, though the rituals remained. None.

Spear God His symbol, the spear, was not just an emblem but a potential object of power. The ancient Romans believed that a spear thrown by a general into enemy territory was a declaration of war, a ritual called 'hastus ferre'. Some scholars see Quirinus as an early god of this war-declaring power, distinct from Mars's battlefield fury. Archaic Roman This power is more inferred from his symbol and etymology than explicitly attested in myths. His war aspect is often overshadowed by Mars, and some scholars question whether it was a major function or just a symbolic one.

Deified Founder In the later tradition, Quirinus was the divine form of Romulus, the founder of Rome. This gave him the power of a founding ancestor, a figure of ultimate legitimacy and authority for the state. Late Republican / Imperial This is a late identification and not universally accepted in antiquity. The identification with Romulus conflicts with the view of Quirinus as an independent, ancient deity.

Behavior & Habitat

As a god of the citizen body, his 'behavior' was the stability and prosperity of the Roman state. He was a guardian, a protector of the community's boundaries and its rights. In his role as a god of the spear, he was a militant guardian, but his war aspect was defensive, protecting the home and the harvest, in contrast to Mars's aggressive, expansionist war. He was a god of order, civic duty, and the assembly.

Accounts

After ruling Rome for many years, Romulus was reviewing his troops on the Campus Martius when a sudden storm and eclipse occurred. He was enveloped in a cloud and disappeared. The people initially suspected the senators of murdering him, but a nobleman named Proculus Julius swore he saw Romulus descend from heaven, telling him that it was the will of the gods that Rome become the capital of the world. Romulus then ascended again. From that moment, he was worshipped as the god Quirinus.

Quirinus's priest, the Flamen Quirinalis, performed rituals that were essential for the state's survival but were not part of the grand, spectacular festivals. He oversaw the Robigalia, a festival to protect the crops from rust, and the Consualia, a harvest festival. This shows Quirinus's role as a god of the community's material well-being, not just its military or political power.

The ritual of declaring war involved a special priest (a fetial) throwing a spear from Roman territory into enemy territory. Some scholars believe this ritual was originally connected to Quirinus, whose symbol was the spear. This would make him the god who sanctioned and initiated conflict, a more formal and civic form of war than the chaotic battle-lust of Mars.

What We Don't Know

The most significant gap is the lack of a detailed, contemporary account of his cult. We have his name, his priest, his festivals, and his symbols, but we lack the myths and stories that would have made him a fully realized character. The exact nature of his relationship to the Sabines and the etymology of his name remain disputed. The 'dark history' of his cult, particularly the political use of the Romulus myth, is a rich area for further research.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Quirinus as the god of 'civic duty'. He's not the god of war (Mars), not the god of kings (Jupiter), but the god of the citizen-soldier who farms his land, votes in the assembly, and takes up a spear when the city is threatened. He is the divine patron of the 'res publica' — the public thing. If Rome were a company, Jupiter would be the CEO, Mars the head of security, and Quirinus the loyal, hard-working employee base that actually makes the whole thing run.
