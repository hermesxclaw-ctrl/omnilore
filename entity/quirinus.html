Who It Is

In the beginning, Rome had three great gods: Jupiter, the king of heaven; Mars, the fury of war; and Quirinus, the beating heart of the city itself. He was the god of the people, the divine embodiment of every citizen who picked up a spear to defend his home. But unlike his more flamboyant colleagues, Quirinus was a quiet, ancient force, a numen of the state rather than a character in a story. He was so deeply woven into the fabric of Rome that his name became a synonym for the Roman people themselves: the Quirites. Yet today, he is almost forgotten, overshadowed by the very gods he once stood beside. His story is a detective story, a hunt through fragments of stone and text to find the ghost of a god who was once the soul of a civilization.

As a god of the citizen body, his 'behavior' was the stability and prosperity of the Roman state. He was a guardian, a protector of the community's boundaries and its rights. In his role as a god of the spear, he was a militant guardian, but his war aspect was defensive, protecting the home and the harvest, in contrast to Mars's aggressive, expansionist war. He was a god of order, civic duty, and the assembly.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The etymology is highly disputed and ancient. The most common ancient Roman derivation connects the name to 'quiris' or 'quirites', meaning 'spear' (Sabine word for spear was 'curis') or 'citizen' (the citizens of Rome were collectively the 'Quirites'). This dual connection was profoundly important:

Pronunciation — kwi-REE-nus (Classical Latin: /kwiˈriː.nʊs/)

Aliases — Quirinus, Curinus, Quiris, Quiritis, Curetes, Quirinalis

Ownership — Roman, with strong Sabine and Indo-European heritage. He is one of the most ancient and obscure members of the Roman pantheon, a god of the earliest civic community.

Powers
Protector of the Citizen Body

Quirinus's primary power was his guardianship over the Roman people as a collective. He ensured the survival, unity, and rights of the Quirites. He was the divine embodiment of the state itself.

Limit: His protection was tied to the performance of proper ritual by his flamen and the state. Neglect of his cult could lead to his withdrawal of favor.

God of the Civic Assembly

He was the patron of the comitia curiata, the earliest assembly of the Roman people. This made him a god of civic law, order, and political participation.

Limit: As Rome's political system evolved, his direct relevance to the assembly diminished, though the rituals remained.

Spear God

His symbol, the spear, was not just an emblem but a potential object of power. The ancient Romans believed that a spear thrown by a general into enemy territory was a declaration of war, a ritual called 'hastus ferre'. Some scholars see Quirinus as an early god of this war-declaring power, distinct from Mars's battlefield fury.

Limit: This power is more inferred from his symbol and etymology than explicitly attested in myths.

Deified Founder

In the later tradition, Quirinus was the divine form of Romulus, the founder of Rome. This gave him the power of a founding ancestor, a figure of ultimate legitimacy and authority for the state.

Limit: This is a late identification and not universally accepted in antiquity.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No definitive, attested description of Quirinus's physical appearance survives. Unlike gods with established Greek statuary types (like Jupiter or Mars), Quirinus was not typically rendered in a unique, recognizable form. He was a numen, a divine force, rather than a fully anthropomorphized character in early Roman religion. When depicted on coins (rarely), he is usually shown as a bearded, mature man in military or civic dress, indistinguishable

Symbols — The spear (curis), the myrtle branch (which his priest, the Flamen Quirinalis, was forbidden to touch, suggesting it was sacred to him), and the Quirinal Hill itself. His association with the spear is his most prominent symbol, linking him to the citizen-soldier.

Domains — Civic life, the citizen body (Quirites), the comitia curiata (the oldest citizen assembly), peace, the protection of the state, and possibly the harvest. His priest, the Flamen Quirinalis, performed rituals for the welfare of the state, including the Robigalia (to protect crops from blight) and the Consualia (for the harvest). This agricultural connection suggests he was also a god of the stored harvest, ensuring the community's survival.

Accounts I
The Apotheosis of RomulusLivy, Ovid, Plutarch, and others

The story is set in the 8th century BCE; the first literary versions date to the 1st century BCE and 1st century CE.

After ruling Rome for many years, Romulus was reviewing his troops on the Campus Martius when a sudden storm and eclipse occurred. He was enveloped in a cloud and disappeared. The people initially suspected the senators of murdering him, but a nobleman named Proculus Julius swore he saw Romulus descend from heaven, telling him that it was the will of the gods that Rome become the capital of the world. Romulus then ascended again. From that moment, he was worshipped as the god Quirinus. The deification of Romulus as Quirinus gave the Roman state a divine founder and a direct link to the gods. It also provided a political mechanism for legitimizing power, as the story of Proculus's vision was clearly used to quell political unrest.

Accounts II
The Flamen Quirinalis and the Harvest RitualsFestus, Ovid, and other antiquarian sources

Archaic to Imperial period (rituals performed throughout Roman history)

Quirinus's priest, the Flamen Quirinalis, performed rituals that were essential for the state's survival but were not part of the grand, spectacular festivals. He oversaw the Robigalia, a festival to protect the crops from rust, and the Consualia, a harvest festival. This shows Quirinus's role as a god of the community's material well-being, not just its military or political power. These rituals ensured the agricultural prosperity of Rome, and their performance by the Flamen Quirinalis tied Quirinus directly to the daily survival of the people.

The Spear of WarInferred from Festus and the ritual of 'hastus ferre'

Archaic period

The ritual of declaring war involved a special priest (a fetial) throwing a spear from Roman territory into enemy territory. Some scholars believe this ritual was originally connected to Quirinus, whose symbol was the spear. This would make him the god who sanctioned and initiated conflict, a more formal and civic form of war than the chaotic battle-lust of Mars. This ritual legitimized Roman warfare and made it a sacred act, with Quirinus as a possible early patron.

Quirinus was just another name for Romulus. — This is a late Roman interpretation, not the original belief. Quirinus was an ancient, independent deity who was later syncretized with the deified founder.

Quirinus was a 'minor' or 'obscure' god. — He was one of the three most important gods in the Archaic Triad (with Jupiter and Mars). His flamen was one of the three major flamens, and his festival, the Quirinalia, was a major civic holiday. His obscurity today is a result of his cult being eclipsed by the more famous gods of the later Republic and Empire.

Quirinus was a god of war, similar to Mars. — While he had a martial symbol (the spear), his primary domain was the citizen body and its civic institutions. His war aspect was defensive and civic, not aggressive and expansionist like Mars's.
