The Approach

In the shifting winds of Greek myth, the name Chloris—'the pale green one'—blooms in several distinct lives: a nymph beloved by the West Wind, a queen of Pylos, a grieving Niobid, and a mother of seers. Each Chloris is a thread in a larger tapestry of nature, mortality, and prophetic power, yet their stories often blur and intertwine, inviting us to untangle the verdant threads.

Content advisory. Contains references to mythological violence (the slaughter of the Niobids) and sexual union between deities and mortals.
Identity

Chloris

Pronunciation KLOR-is (English approximation); Greek: Χλῶρις (KHLŌ-ris).

Known As

ChlōrisAregonis (for the mother of Mopsus in the Orphic Argonautica)

Etymology

From Greek χλωρός (chlōrós), meaning 'greenish-yellow', 'pale green', 'pale', 'pallid', or 'fresh'.

Cultural Ownership

Greek mythology, with later Roman literary reception (e.g., Ovid).

Disputed Identifications

The name is applied to multiple distinct figures; ancient and modern sources sometimes conflate them. For instance, the wife of Neleus may or may not be the same as the nymph loved by Zephyrus; the mother of Mopsus is sometimes called Aregonis.

Description

Appearance

No explicit physical description survives for any Chloris. The name suggests a connection to pale green or fresh vegetation, possibly implying a nature spirit or a pale complexion.

Symbols & Iconography

No specific symbols are attested in ancient sources. In Ovid's Fasti, Chloris is associated with flowers and spring, and she is later identified with the Roman goddess Flora, who is typically depicted with flowers.

Behaviour & Personality

No detailed personality traits are recorded for any Chloris. As a nymph, she may embody the freshness of spring; as a queen, she is a mortal wife; as a Niobid, she is a victim of divine wrath.

Domains & Habitats

Chloris the nymph is associated with the natural world, particularly flowers and the West Wind. Chloris of Pylos is a mortal queen. Chloris the Niobid is a Theban princess. Chloris the mother of Mopsus is connected to seers and prophecy.

Version Differences

The name is used for at least four distinct figures. The nymph loved by Zephyrus is clearly separate from the Niobid, but the wife of Neleus and the mother of Mopsus might be the same person in some traditions, though sources are unclear. The Orphic Argonautica uses the name Aregonis for the mother of Mopsus, suggesting a variant tradition.

Powers & Abilities

None (as a mortal or nymph)

Source Version All versions

Description

No supernatural powers are explicitly attributed to any Chloris, except for the nymph's association with vegetation and possibly transformation (in Ovid, she becomes Flora).

Limitations

As a mortal, she is subject to death (e.g., the Niobid is killed by Apollo and Artemis).

Contradictions

None

Stories & Myths

Chloris and Zephyrus

Source Ovid, Fasti 5.195-214Culture Roman (adaptation of Greek myth)Period Augustan era

Summary

Chloris, a nymph, is pursued by Zephyrus, the West Wind. She yields to him, and he grants her dominion over flowers, renaming her Flora. This is an aetiological myth for the Roman goddess Flora.

Participants

Chloris

Zephyrus

Consequences

Chloris becomes Flora, goddess of flowers and spring.

Variants

This story is unique to Ovid; no earlier Greek version identifies Chloris with Flora.

Chloris, wife of Neleus

Source Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 1.9.9Culture GreekPeriod Classical or Hellenistic

Summary

Chloris marries Neleus, king of Pylos, and bears him many children, including Nestor. She is also mentioned as daughter of Amphion and Niobe in some sources, but this is uncertain.

Participants

Chloris

Neleus

Nestor

Consequences

She becomes queen of Pylos and mother of the famous Nestor, a hero of the Trojan War.

Variants

Some sources say she is the daughter of Amphion and Niobe, but this is disputed. Hyginus lists a Chloris among the Niobids, possibly the same person.

Chloris, a Niobid

Source Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.5.6; Hyginus, Fabulae 14Culture GreekPeriod Classical or Hellenistic

Summary

Chloris is one of the daughters of Niobe, who boasted of her children and was punished by Apollo and Artemis, who killed all the Niobids. Chloris is said to have been spared, but this is not consistent.

Participants

Chloris

Niobe

Apollo

Artemis

Consequences

Chloris is killed or possibly spared; the myth explains the fate of the Niobids.

Variants

The number and names of the Niobids vary. Some say Chloris survived because she prayed to Leto, but others say she was killed.

Chloris, mother of Mopsus

Source Hyginus, Fabulae 170; Orphic Argonautica 127Culture GreekPeriod Classical to Late Antiquity

Summary

Chloris, daughter of Orchomenus, marries the seer Ampyx and bears Mopsus, who becomes a renowned seer and joins the Argonauts. In some accounts, Mopsus is fathered by Zeus.

Variants

Slaughter of the Niobids

Source Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.5.6; Hyginus, Fabulae 14

Description

Chloris, as a Niobid, is a victim of divine retribution. Niobe boasted that she had more children than Leto, so Apollo and Artemis killed all her offspring. Chloris may have been among the slain.

Scholarly Context

The myth serves as a cautionary tale about hubris and the dangers of comparing oneself to the gods.

Contradictions & Disputes

Identity of Chloris as wife of Neleus and as Niobid

Position A

They are the same person, as both are daughters of Amphion and Niobe.

Position A — evidence

Some genealogies list Chloris as a daughter of Amphion and Niobe, and she is also said to be the wife of Neleus.

Position B

They are distinct figures; the name is just common.

Position B — evidence

Other sources do not connect the two, and Pseudo-Apollodorus lists a Chloris among the Niobids but does not mention her as Neleus's wife.

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus; the sources are ambiguous.

Mother of Mopsus: Chloris or Aregonis?

Position A

The mother is Chloris.

Position A — evidence

Hyginus and some other sources call her Chloris.

Position B

The mother is Aregonis.

Position B — evidence

The Orphic Argonautica calls her Aregonis.

Scholarly Consensus

Likely variant traditions; no definitive resolution.
