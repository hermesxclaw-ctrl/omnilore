Who It Is

On the rocky road from Athens to Troezen, travelers faced a grim toll: a club-wielding giant, lame like his divine father, who used his bronze weapon as a crutch and as an instrument of murder. This was Periphetes, also called Corynetes, the Club-Bearer, whose reign of terror ended only when a young hero named Theseus turned the giant's own weapon against him.

The bandit Periphetes was a violent highwayman who preyed on travelers, killing them with his club. Other Periphetes figures are less characterized: the Arcadian king is a genealogical figure; the son of Copreus is a warrior; the king of Mygdonia is a suitor; the Trojan is a minor casualty.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Periphetes (Περιφήτης) likely derives from Greek peri (around) and phemi (to speak), possibly meaning 'famed on all sides' or 'renowned'. The epithet Corynetes (Κορυνήτης) means 'club-bearer' from koryne (club).

Pronunciation — pe-RIF-uh-teez (English); pe-ri-PHE-tes (classical Greek).

Aliases — Corynetes, Club-Bearer, Periphetes the Arcadian, Periphetes of Mygdonia, Periphetes son of Copreus, Periphetes the Trojan

Ownership — Greek mythology, specifically the Theseus cycle and genealogical traditions of Arcadia and the Trojan War.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The club-bearer Periphetes is described as lame, possibly in the feet, and of great size. He carried a bronze club that served as both a crutch and a weapon. Other figures named Periphetes have no distinct physical descriptions.

Symbols — The bronze club is the defining attribute of the bandit Periphetes, symbolizing both his physical power and his disability. In art, he is likely depicted as a bearded, muscular man with a club, though no specific ancient images are securely identified.

Domains — The bandit Periphetes haunted the road from Athens to Troezen, near Epidaurus. The Arcadian Periphetes was associated with the region of Arcadia and the city of Psophis. The king of Mygdonia ruled in Thrace. The Trojan Periphetes fought at Troy.

Accounts I
Theseus and PeriphetesPlutarch, Life of Theseus; Hyginus, Fabulae; Pausanias

Mythical age (Heroic Age)

On his journey from Troezen to Athens, Theseus encountered Periphetes near Epidaurus. Periphetes, the club-bearer, attacked him, but Theseus wrested the club away and killed him with it. This was the first of Theseus' six labors on the road. Theseus took the club as a trophy, and it became one of his identifying attributes. The road was made safe for travelers.

Periphetes of ArcadiaPausanias, Description of Greece

Mythical age

Periphetes, son of Nyctimus, was a king of Arcadia. He was the father of Parthaon, and through him an ancestor of Psophis, one of the eponyms of the city Psophis. His lineage established the royal house of Psophis.

Accounts II
Periphetes at TroyHomer, Iliad

Trojan War

Two Periphetes appear in the Iliad: one, son of Copreus, is killed by Hector; another, a Trojan, is killed by Teucer. Their deaths are minor incidents in the larger war. Their deaths contribute to the casualty lists of the war.

Periphetes of MygdoniaConon, Narrations (via Photius)

Mythical age

Periphetes, king of Mygdonia, sought the hand of Pallene, daughter of Sithon. He fought with another suitor, Dryas, and was killed. His death left Pallene to marry the victor, Dryas.
