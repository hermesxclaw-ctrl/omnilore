The Approach

On the rocky road from Athens to Troezen, travelers faced a grim toll: a club-wielding giant, lame like his divine father, who used his bronze weapon as a crutch and as an instrument of murder. This was Periphetes, also called Corynetes, the Club-Bearer, whose reign of terror ended only when a young hero named Theseus turned the giant's own weapon against him.

Content advisory. Contains descriptions of violence, murder, and mythological brigandage.

Etymology

The name Periphetes (Περιφήτης) likely derives from Greek peri (around) and phemi (to speak), possibly meaning 'famed on all sides' or 'renowned'. The epithet Corynetes (Κορυνήτης) means 'club-bearer' from koryne (club).

Cultural Ownership

Greek mythology, specifically the Theseus cycle and genealogical traditions of Arcadia and the Trojan War.

Disputed Identifications

The name is shared by multiple distinct figures; ancient sources do not always clarify which is meant. Some modern scholars have questioned whether the club-bearer was truly a son of Hephaestus or a local brigand mythologized later.

Description

Appearance

The club-bearer Periphetes is described as lame, possibly in the feet, and of great size. He carried a bronze club that served as both a crutch and a weapon. Other figures named Periphetes have no distinct physical descriptions.

Symbols & Iconography

The bronze club is the defining attribute of the bandit Periphetes, symbolizing both his physical power and his disability. In art, he is likely depicted as a bearded, muscular man with a club, though no specific ancient images are securely identified.

Behaviour & Personality

The bandit Periphetes was a violent highwayman who preyed on travelers, killing them with his club. Other Periphetes figures are less characterized: the Arcadian king is a genealogical figure; the son of Copreus is a warrior; the king of Mygdonia is a suitor; the Trojan is a minor casualty.

Domains & Habitats

The bandit Periphetes haunted the road from Athens to Troezen, near Epidaurus. The Arcadian Periphetes was associated with the region of Arcadia and the city of Psophis. The king of Mygdonia ruled in Thrace. The Trojan Periphetes fought at Troy.

Version Differences

The parentage of the bandit Periphetes varies: Plutarch names Hephaestus and Anticleia, while other sources (e.g., Apollodorus) may name Poseidon. The Arcadian Periphetes is a separate figure, as are the others. The club-bearer's lameness is consistent across sources.

Stories & Myths

Theseus and Periphetes

Source Plutarch, Life of Theseus; Hyginus, Fabulae; PausaniasCulture GreekPeriod Mythical age (Heroic Age)

Summary

On his journey from Troezen to Athens, Theseus encountered Periphetes near Epidaurus. Periphetes, the club-bearer, attacked him, but Theseus wrested the club away and killed him with it. This was the first of Theseus' six labors on the road.

Participants

Theseus

Periphetes

Consequences

Theseus took the club as a trophy, and it became one of his identifying attributes. The road was made safe for travelers.

Variants

Some versions may name the location as the Isthmus of Corinth or the road to Troezen. The parentage of Periphetes varies.

Periphetes of Arcadia

Source Pausanias, Description of GreeceCulture GreekPeriod Mythical age

Summary

Periphetes, son of Nyctimus, was a king of Arcadia. He was the father of Parthaon, and through him an ancestor of Psophis, one of the eponyms of the city Psophis.

Participants

Periphetes

Parthaon

Psophis

Consequences

His lineage established the royal house of Psophis.

Variants

Pausanias notes that Psophis was also said to be named after another figure, so the eponymy is uncertain.

Periphetes at Troy

Source Homer, IliadCulture GreekPeriod Trojan War

Summary

Two Periphetes appear in the Iliad: one, son of Copreus, is killed by Hector; another, a Trojan, is killed by Teucer. Their deaths are minor incidents in the larger war.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

The full text of Conon's Narrations is lost; only summaries remain. The parentage of Periphetes the club-bearer is not consistently reported across all sources. No ancient artistic depictions of Periphetes have been securely identified. The exact relationship between the various figures named Periphetes is not always clear. The genus of stick insects named Periphetes is noted but not researched in depth.

Research Scope

This dossier covers the five distinct mythological figures named Periphetes, focusing primarily on the club-wielding son of Hephaestus encountered by Theseus. It also includes the Arcadian king, the son of Copreus, the king of Mygdonia, and the Trojan warrior. The genus of stick insects is noted for disambiguation. Research is based on classical sources including Plutarch, Pausanias, Hyginus, Conon, and Homer, with scholarly commentary where available. The research cutoff is August 1, 2026.
