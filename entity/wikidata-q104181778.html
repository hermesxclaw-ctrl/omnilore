The Approach

In the dying years of the Roman Republic, a soldier named Lucilius stood on a battlefield at Philippi, stripped of hope, and chose to die for a friend. When his ruse was revealed, the enemy commander—Marcus Antonius, the man who would one day rule the East—was so moved that he spared him and made him a friend. This is the story of a name that flickers through Roman history: a poet who forged a genre, a jurist who shaped law, and a man whose loyalty outshone the chaos of civil war.

Content advisory. Contains historical accounts of political violence, civil war, execution, and torture (e.g., Sextus Lucilius thrown from the Tarpeian Rock; centurion Lucilius killed in a mutiny).
Identity

Lucilia gens

Pronunciation loo-KIL-ee-us

Known As

LuciliiGens Lucilia

Etymology

The nomen Lucilius is a patronymic surname derived from the Latin praenomen Lucius, meaning 'light' or 'illumination'.

Cultural Ownership

Ancient Roman, plebeian family

Disputed Identifications

The cognomen Rufus appears on coins of the Lucilii but is not mentioned by any ancient writer, leading to uncertainty about which Lucilius used it.

Description

Appearance

No specific physical descriptions of Lucilii are recorded in the source material. Cognomina such as Balbus (stammerer), Bassus (stout), Capito (large-headed), and Rufus (red-haired) suggest physical or speech characteristics, but these are not detailed.

Symbols & Iconography

Coins minted by Marcus Lucilius Rufus feature Pallas (Minerva) on the obverse and Victoria in a biga on the reverse, indicating the family's engagement with Roman state iconography.

Behaviour & Personality

Members display a range of traits: literary genius (Gaius Lucilius), legal expertise (Lucius Lucilius Balbus), Stoic philosophy (Quintus Lucilius Balbus), political ambition (Sextus Lucilius, tribune), loyalty (Lucilius at Philippi), and brutality (centurion 'Cedo Alteram').

Domains & Habitats

The gens originated from Suessa Aurunca, an ancient town of the Aurunci, where a Latin colony was established in 313 BC. Members were active in Rome, across the empire (Asia, Sicily, Judaea), and in military campaigns.

Version Differences

The source material is a single modern summary; no conflicting versions are presented. However, the cognomen Rufus is known only from coins, not literary sources, indicating a potential discrepancy between numismatic and literary evidence.

Several Lucilii served as military tribunes, cavalry officers, and fleet commanders, including Marcus Lucilius Rufus (moneyer), Sextus Lucilius (tribune killed at Mount Amanus), and Lucilius Bassus (fleet commander under Vitellius and Vespasian).

The Loyalty of Lucilius at Philippi

Source The provided source material; also known from ancient historians (e.g., Appian, Plutarch)Culture RomanPeriod 42 BC

Summary

After the Battle of Philippi, a partisan of Brutus named Lucilius pretended to be Brutus to allow his friend to escape. He was captured, but when his identity was revealed, Marcus Antonius pardoned him and they became friends.

Execution of Sextus Lucilius

Source Provided source material

Description

Sextus Lucilius, tribune of the plebs and a Sullan partisan, was hurled from the Tarpeian Rock by his successor Publius Popillius Laenas in 86 BC, a brutal political execution.

Scholarly Context

This reflects the violent factional struggles between Marians and Sullans during the late Republic.

Centurion Lucilius's brutality

Source Provided source material

Description

A centurion named Lucilius, known as 'Cedo Alteram' ('Fetch Me Another'), was killed in the Pannonian mutiny in AD 14. He was notorious for breaking his vine-staff during beatings of soldiers.

Scholarly Context

This illustrates the harsh discipline and resentment within the Roman army.

Civil war and betrayal

Source Provided source material

Description

Lucilius Bassus initially served Vitellius, then defected to Vespasian, delivering the fleet to him. This was a pragmatic shift during the Year of the Four Emperors.

Scholarly Context

The civil wars of AD 69 saw many such changes of allegiance.
