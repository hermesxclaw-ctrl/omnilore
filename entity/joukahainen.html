Who It Is

Joukahainen is a young Laplander whose jealous pride sets in motion the first great tragedy of the Kalevala. He is a competent but impetuous singer of runes, a youth who has heard of Väinämöinen's fame and cannot bear that anyone should be more skilled in magic than himself. Against the advice of his parents, he hitches his horse to a golden sleigh and drives to Kalevala to challenge the eternal sage — a decision that will cost him everything.

His magical duel with Väinämöinen is the Kalevala's first demonstration of the power of words. Joukahainen sings first, boasting of his knowledge, but his songs are mere tricks and illusions. Väinämöinen responds with genuine songs of creation and origin, and the power differential becomes immediately apparent. Väinämöinen sings Joukahainen into the ground — first to his waist, then to his chest, then to his chin — complete with his horse, sleigh, and all his belongings. Terrified, Joukahainen offers increasingly desperate bribes for his life: two splendid bows, two swift boats, two fine horses, gold, silver, fields of grain — all refused. Finally, he promises his sister Aino as Väinämöinen's wife. Väinämöinen, delighted, releases him.

Aino's tragedy is Joukahainen's legacy. When she learns that she has been promised to an old man, she drowns herself rather than submit. Grief-stricken and blaming Väinämöinen for his sister's death, Joukahainen seeks revenge. He lies in wait along the hero's path and shoots at Väinämöinen, killing his horse from under him and sending him tumbling into the sea. Väinämöinen drifts for days before an eagle (sent by Louhi or perhaps the gods) rescues him. This act of vengeance fails as completely as his earlier challenge.

Joukahainen is a cautionary figure: the inadequately prepared youth who aspires to greatness without understanding its costs. He possesses real magical ability — he is no fraud — but his knowledge is shallow, his judgment poor, and his character weak. His name has been connected to the Finnish word joukko ('group'), perhaps marking him as the representative of collective tradition rather than individual mastery. His fate is to be the one who, through arrogance, destroys what he loves most.
