Who It Is

A black wind rolls off Black Wind Mountain, snuffing temple flames in a single breath — and when it clears, the Buddha's own glittering kasaya is gone. The thief is not a wind at all, but a bear who has learned to speak, to wear armor, to wield a spear, and to crave enlightenment the wrong way: by stealing it.

Highly cultivated, literate and eloquent — he hosts a birthday feast, exchanges poetry, and debates Buddhism with Tang Sanzang's party. He is not a man-eater like many Xiyouji demons; his vice is aesthetic and spiritual greed (tan 贪) and attachment to prestige objects rather than flesh. He cultivates Daoist longevity techniques, practices martial arts, and values reputation among neighboring demons. Powers
Animal Spirit Cultivation and Longevity

Centuries of self-cultivation (xiulian) granting human speech, bipedal form, literacy, and extended lifespan without formal Daoist or Buddhist ordination. Implied to have achieved a human-like 'human form' (renxing) through absorbing mountain qi.

Limit: Still subject to Buddhist dharma restraints and to more advanced bodhisattva magic; cultivation does not grant immunity to Guanyin's elixir or fillet

Mastery of the Black Tassel Spear

Expert polearm combat; duels Sun Wukong (armed with Ruyi Jingu Bang) to a stalemate for extended bouts. Strength sufficient to parry the Monkey King's blows, indicating superhuman strength.

Limit: No magical projectile from spear itself; relies on physical skill. Cannot overcome Sun Wukong's greater speed and transformations indefinitely

Black Wind / Black Mist Manipulation

Can summon a black wind (heifeng) to travel, extinguish fire, and obscure battlefield. In the novel he rides black wind to the Guanyin Temple fire and back. In game this becomes a gameplay mechanic: arena-filling black mist, wind dashes, and teleportation.

Limit: Wind does not damage Sun Wukong directly; primarily transport and concealment. Guanyin easily pierces it

Transformation and Human Guise

Full transformation between bear and armored man; can pass as human cultivator to converse with monks and demons. Not as versatile as Sun Wukong's 72 transformations — limited to his two forms.

Limit: True form revealed under duress (pain from elixir) or when using full power; cannot mimic other specific persons

Fire Resistance and Fire Extinguishing

Approaches the blazing Guanyin Temple without injury and uses wind/magic to help extinguish flames (while stealing). The stolen kasaya itself is fireproof, but the demon also shows no burn injury.

Limit: Not immune to later divine fire; Guanyin's power still binds him. Game inverts this: second phase uses fire attacks, suggesting affinity rather than resistance

Vulnerability to Guanyin's Elixir and Golden Cincture (Jinjin)

Guanyin, disguised as Lingxuzi, offers a longevity elixir (actually a transformative poison/pill) that causes excruciating headache and forces reversion to true form when swallowed. She then places the Golden Cincture (Jinjin) on his head, which tightens and causes pain when its spell is recited, compelling obedience — parallel to Sun Wukong's Golden Hoop (Jingu).

Limit: Only Guanyin (and by extension the Buddha who gifted the fillets) can control the spell; without recitation, no pain. Does not kill, only compels

Form & Signs

Appearance — True form is a massive black bear (xiongpi) of unusual size, capable of bipedal stance and speech after long cultivation. Human guise is described as a tall, dark-complexioned (literally 'black face') martial man clad in black armor, wearing a helmet and wielding the Black Tassel Spear (Heiying Qiang 黑缨枪 / 烏纓槍). The novel emphasizes his martial bearing rather than grotesque demon features; illustrations from Qing woodblock editions show a bear-he

Symbols — Black wind and black mist as territorial and martial sign; the stolen Jinlan Jiasha (Brocade/Brocaded Cassock, literally 'Gold-thread Kasaya') — a Buddha-gifted treasure that glows and is impervious to fire, water and dust — as symbol of misplaced attachment; the Golden Cincture / Tight Fillet (Jinjin 禁箍儿, sometimes called Jingu in popular conflation) as symbol of Buddhist restraint. Iconographically he is paired with the burning Guanyi

Domains — Heifeng Shan (Black Wind Mountain) and Heifeng Dong (Black Wind Cave), a forested mountain with a fortified cave-palace near the Guanyin Temple (Guanyin Chanyuan 觀音禪院). After subjugation, his domain shifts to Luojia Shan (Mount Potalaka, the South Sea) as guardian deity (shoushan dahu shen 守山大護神) under Guanyin, patrolling the sacred mountain. Accounts I
The Guanyin Temple and Elder Jinchi's GreedWu Cheng'en, Xiyouji, Chapter 16

Ming dynasty, c. 1592 (narrative set in Tang dynasty pilgrimage, 7th century)

Tang Sanzang and Sun Wukong lodge at the Guanyin Temple (Guanyin Chanyuan), a wealthy monastery famed for its collection of kasayas. Its abbot, Elder Jinchi (Jinchi Zhanglao), 270 years old, proudly displays his own treasures and becomes insanely jealous upon seeing Tang Sanzang's Jinlan Jiasha, a brocade kasaya gifted by Guanyin/Buddha that glows with jewels and is impervious to elements. Jinchi cajoles Tang Sanzang into lending it overnight, then conspires with his monks to murder the pilgrims by arson — planning to keep the robe after they burn. Sun Wukong, warned by the temple's guardian deities, protects his master by conjuring a wind and later borrowing a fire cover from the heavens, so the fire consumes the temple instead of the pilgrims. Temple burns, Jinchi's plot fails; the fire creates opportunity for Heifeng Guai to intervene and sets up theft of the kasaya. Establishes thematic pairing of Jinchi and Heifeng Guai as twin embodiments of greed.

The Black Wind and the Theft of the Brocade KasayaWu Cheng'en, Xiyouji, Chapter 16

Ming, c. 1592

Heifeng Guai, lord of Black Wind Cave, sees the fire from his mountain and rides a black wind to the temple, ostensibly to help extinguish it. Finding the unattended, glowing Jinlan Jiasha in Tang Sanzang's lodging, he seizes it and returns to his cave, where he admires it and plans to wear it at his upcoming birthday feast. Tang Sanzang discovers the loss at dawn and despairs; Sun Wukong vows to retrieve it. Pilgrimage is stalled; Sun Wukong's retrieval quest begins. Theft is portrayed not as random banditry but as opportunistic attachment to a sacred object — mirroring Jinchi's greed.

Spear Duel at Black Wind Cave — Sun Wukong's Failed RetrievalWu Cheng'en, Xiyouji, Chapter 17

Ming, c. 1592

Sun Wukong tracks the black wind to Black Wind Mountain and demands the robe. Heifeng Guai, in black armor with Black Tassel Spear, meets him in open combat. The two duel from morning to evening with no victor — a rare stalemate for Sun Wukong, demonstrating the bear's martial prowess. Sun tries to trick the demon by transforming into a small object and sneaking into the cave, but is discovered. He then enlists Guanyin Temple's remaining monks and even the celestial deities, but brute force fails. Recognizing the demon's cultivation and that killing him would be wasteful, Sun seeks Guanyin's help. Establishes Heifeng Guai as one of the few demons who can match Sun Wukong in single combat without external treasure. Forces pivot from martial to salvific solution — subjugation and conversion rather than extermination.

Accounts II
Guanyin Becomes Lingxuzi — The Elixir TrapWu Cheng'en, Xiyouji, Chapter 17

Ming, c. 1592

Sun Wukong flies to the South Sea to petition Guanyin. She already knows the affair and reveals that the bear is a cultivated neighbor of her Potalaka who slipped away. She transforms herself into Lingxuzi (also called Lingxu Zi), an old Daoist friend of Heifeng Guai who was expected for the birthday celebration, and has Sun Wukong transform into a small Dan (elixir) pill. As Lingxuzi, Guanyin presents the 'elixir' as a birthday gift. Heifeng Guai, delighted, swallows it. Guanyin reverts and recites a spell, causing the pill — now inside his body — to cause searing pain and force him to reveal his true bear form, rolling on the ground. Demon is incapacitated without being killed, allowing Guanyin to recover the kasaya intact and to apply the fillet. Demonstrates Buddhist skillful means (upaya): deception for salvation.

Subjugation and Conversion — Guardian of Mount PotalakaWu Cheng'en, Xiyouji, Chapter 17-18

Ming, c. 1592

With Heifeng Guai writhing, Guanyin retrieves the Jinlan Jiasha and returns it to Tang Sanzang. She then places the Golden Cincture (Jinjin) on the bear's head, reciting the tightening spell. The bear begs for mercy and vows to follow the Buddhist path. Guanyin shaves his head in effect, gives him a Buddhist name (Shou Shan Da Hu Shen, Guardian of the Mountain), and takes him to the South Sea to guard Luojia Mountain (Potalaka), her sacred isle. He becomes a Dharma protector rather than being executed, paralleling Sun Wukong's own fillet-controlled discipleship. Tang Sanzang's party resumes the journey. Resolves the episode with conversion (shoufu) rather than extermination — a model for taming 'mind demons' (xinmo). Establishes Heifeng Guai's final canonical status as a benevolent guardian, explaining why he never reappears as antagonist.

Antecedent — The Black Wind King as Zhu Bajie (Yuan Zaju Layer)Yang Jingxian, Xiyouji zaju; discussion in modern scholarship (Geng Chuanming, Zhao Shuang)

Yuan dynasty, 13th-14th century

In the earliest known dramatic version of the pilgrimage, there is no bear demon. The figure called Heifeng Dawang (Black Wind Great King) is Zhu Bajie himself (then called Tianpeng Yuanshuai), who dwells in Heifeng Dong. The 'Black Wind Cave' setting and title are thus originally Bajie's. When Wu Cheng'en novelizes the story, he separates the elements: Bajie is given a new backstory (expelled Marshal Tianpeng, reborn as pig-man in Yunzhan Cave, later Gao Village) and the Black Wind Cave is reassigned to a newly invented bear spirit, allowing both characters to coexist and doubling the theme of appetite/greed. Explains why Heifeng Guai has no deep folklore prior to Ming novel — he is a literary creation synthesizing bear lore and a recycled toponym. Scholarly debate continues on how much of Bajie's gluttony was transferred symbolically to the bear.

Heifeng Guai is an ancient folklore bear god predating Journey to the West. — He is a Ming literary invention for the novel (c. 1592), synthesizing generic bear-spirit folklore and the recycled 'Black Wind Cave' toponym from Yuan drama. No pre-Ming temple or myth attests this specific Black Wind Demon.

He ate humans or was a man-eating monster like many Xiyouji demons. — In the canonical episode he does not eat humans; his crime is theft and greed for the kasaya. He is notably cultivated and hosts a refined birthday banquet. Man-eating is not attested for him.

Sun Wukong defeated him in single combat. — Sun Wukong fought him to a draw and failed to retrieve the robe by force; he needed Guanyin's intervention and trickery (elixir + fillet). The stalemate is thematically important — brute force cannot cure greed.

The hoop on his head is the same one Sun Wukong wears and was removed after conversion. — It is a separate Golden Cincture (Jinjin) that remains as a control device; like Sun's hoop, it is not removed after conversion but ensures ongoing obedience as guardian. Text does not describe removal.

Black Myth: Wukong's story (Craving Eyes relic, reviving Elder Jinchi) is from the original novel. — Craving Eyes, undead Jinchi, and two-phase fire bear are original to Game Science's 2024 game to expand Chapter 1 into a full arc. The novel has no relic and Jinchi dies by suicide without resurrection.
