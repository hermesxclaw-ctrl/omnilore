The Approach

A black wind rolls off Black Wind Mountain, snuffing temple flames in a single breath — and when it clears, the Buddha's own glittering kasaya is gone. The thief is not a wind at all, but a bear who has learned to speak, to wear armor, to wield a spear, and to crave enlightenment the wrong way: by stealing it.

Content advisory. Mild fantasy violence (spear combat, magical subjugation); arson and attempted murder (monks set fire to lodging pilgrims); themes of greed and religious coercion via magical headband/cincture causing pain. No sexual violence is attested in canonical sources.
Identity

Heifeng Guai

Pronunciation Mandarin: Hēi Fēng Guài [xéɪ fə́ŋ kwâɪ]; Cantonese: Hak1 Fung1 Gwaai3; Japanese on'yomi: Kokufū Kai; Korean: Heukpung Goe

Known As

Etymology

Chinese 黑风怪 Hēi Fēng Guài: hēi 'black' + fēng 'wind' + guài 'strange, monster, demon, anomalous spirit'. The epithet refers to his dwelling on Heifeng Shan (Black Wind Mountain) and to the black wind/mist he commands. His species epithet 黑熊精 Hēi Xióng Jīng means 'Black Bear Essence/Spirit', jing denoting a creature that has attained sentience and powers through cultivation. The novel's description of him as xiongpi (熊罴) uses an archaic classical term for a large, powerful bear, sometimes glossed as brown bear or giant bear, invoking antiquity and martial strength.

Cultural Ownership

Han Chinese literary creation within Ming dynasty vernacular fiction (shenmo xiaoshuo), drawing on Chinese Buddhist and Daoist cosmology, folklore of animal spirits (jingguai), and earlier Yuan drama. No living-tradition worship community claims exclusive ownership; figure is part of shared Sinosphere literary heritage. Modern interpretations are secular literary and gaming adaptations.

Disputed Identifications

1) In Yang Jingxian's Yuan zaju Journey to the West, the title 'Black Wind King' (Heifeng Dawang 黑风大王) refers to Zhu Bajie, who lives in Black Wind Cave — not to a bear demon. Wu Cheng'en's Ming novel splits this: Zhu Bajie is relocated to Yunzhan Cave and a new Black Bear Spirit is created to occupy Black Wind Mountain. Popular summaries conflate the two. 2) Species identification: xiongpi is sometimes translated as 'black bear' and sometimes as 'brown bear' or 'grizzly'; zoological precision is anachronistic — the term is a literary archaism signaling monstrous size and strength, linked by critic Zhao Shuang to bear-worship and Yu the Great bear-transformation myths. 3) Not to be confused with other bear spirits in Chinese folklore (e.g., bear of Jizha) or with the unrelated 'Black Wind' demons in other shenmo novels.

Description

Appearance

True form is a massive black bear (xiongpi) of unusual size, capable of bipedal stance and speech after long cultivation. Human guise is described as a tall, dark-complexioned (literally 'black face') martial man clad in black armor, wearing a helmet and wielding the Black Tassel Spear (Heiying Qiang 黑缨枪 / 烏纓槍). The novel emphasizes his martial bearing rather than grotesque demon features; illustrations from Qing woodblock editions show a bear-headed armored warrior or a bearded black-faced general. In Black Myth: Wukong he appears first as a towering anthropomorphic bear in tattered monk-like robes, later transforming into a flame-wreathed giant bear with glowing eyes when empowered by the Craving Eyes. Attested: bear true form, black-armored man, spear. Reconstructed: exact height/color of fur varies by illustrator. Adaptation-specific: fire and black-mist aura in game.

Symbols & Iconography

Black wind and black mist as territorial and martial sign; the stolen Jinlan Jiasha (Brocade/Brocaded Cassock, literally 'Gold-thread Kasaya') — a Buddha-gifted treasure that glows and is impervious to fire, water and dust — as symbol of misplaced attachment; the Golden Cincture / Tight Fillet (Jinjin 禁箍儿, sometimes called Jingu in popular conflation) as symbol of Buddhist restraint. Iconographically he is paired with the burning Guanyin Temple and with Guanyin herself, marking conversion from demon to Dharma protector. Bear symbolism draws, per Zhao Shuang, on archaic bear veneration (Yu the Great's bear transformation during flood control) connoting brute power and mountain sovereignty.

Behaviour & Personality

Highly cultivated, literate and eloquent — he hosts a birthday feast, exchanges poetry, and debates Buddhism with Tang Sanzang's party. He is not a man-eater like many Xiyouji demons; his vice is aesthetic and spiritual greed (tan 贪) and attachment to prestige objects rather than flesh. He cultivates Daoist longevity techniques, practices martial arts, and values reputation among neighboring demons. He is cautious (steals only when fire provides cover), proud (duels Sun Wukong rather than fleeing), and capable of deference: once subdued by Guanyin's elixir and fillet, he submits, repents, and accepts guardianship. Version differences: novel emphasizes his Buddhist conversion; game emphasizes corruption by celestial relic and later remorse.

Domains & Habitats

Heifeng Shan (Black Wind Mountain) and Heifeng Dong (Black Wind Cave), a forested mountain with a fortified cave-palace near the Guanyin Temple (Guanyin Chanyuan 觀音禪院). After subjugation, his domain shifts to Luojia Shan (Mount Potalaka, the South Sea) as guardian deity (shoushan dahu shen 守山大護神) under Guanyin, patrolling the sacred mountain. In Black Myth: Wukong he rebuilds the ruined Guanyin Monastery on the same mountain and presides over Bodhi Peak. No underworld or celestial court domain is attested.

Version Differences

Ming novel (canonical): bear demon, steals kasaya, subdued by Guanyin disguised as Lingxuzi with elixir and Golden Cincture, becomes South Sea guardian. Yuan zaju: no bear; Black Wind King = Zhu Bajie. Qing woodblock illustrations: more explicitly bear-headed. 1986 CCTV series: bear suit/puppet with human face, comic tone, retains novel plot closely. 2011 TV series: CGI bear transformation, expanded fight choreography. Black Myth: Wukong (2024): adds Craving Eyes relic from Celestial Court, resurrection of Elder Jinchi as undead, two-phase boss mechanics (wind/mist phase → fire bear phase), and post-defeat voluntary return to Guanyin — original to game, not in Ming text.

Powers & Abilities

Animal Spirit Cultivation and Longevity

Source VersionXiyouji ch. 16-17, attested; Lingxuzi dialogue confirms long friendship and cultivation

Centuries of self-cultivation (xiulian) granting human speech, bipedal form, literacy, and extended lifespan without formal Daoist or Buddhist ordination. Implied to have achieved a human-like 'human form' (renxing) through absorbing mountain qi.

Limitations

Still subject to Buddhist dharma restraints and to more advanced bodhisattva magic; cultivation does not grant immunity to Guanyin's elixir or fillet

Contradictions

Novel does not specify years of cultivation; game adds external empowerment via Craving Eyes relic

Mastery of the Black Tassel Spear

Source VersionXiyouji ch. 17, attested; both Yu and Jenner translations describe prolonged spear vs. staff duel

Expert polearm combat; duels Sun Wukong (armed with Ruyi Jingu Bang) to a stalemate for extended bouts. Strength sufficient to parry the Monkey King's blows, indicating superhuman strength.

Limitations

No magical projectile from spear itself; relies on physical skill. Cannot overcome Sun Wukong's greater speed and transformations indefinitely

Contradictions

None significant; game expands to claw swipes and body slams beyond spear

Black Wind / Black Mist Manipulation

Source VersionXiyouji ch. 16 (rides black wind to steal kasaya); game adaptation expands to mist/fog attacks

Can summon a black wind (heifeng) to travel, extinguish fire, and obscure battlefield. In the novel he rides black wind to the Guanyin Temple fire and back. In game this becomes a gameplay mechanic: arena-filling black mist, wind dashes, and teleportation.

Limitations

Wind does not damage Sun Wukong directly; primarily transport and concealment. Guanyin easily pierces it

Contradictions

Novel's wind is transport; game's wind is offensive — adaptation-specific inflation

Transformation and Human Guise

Source VersionXiyouji ch. 17, attested

Full transformation between bear and armored man; can pass as human cultivator to converse with monks and demons. Not as versatile as Sun Wukong's 72 transformations — limited to his two forms.

Limitations

True form revealed under duress (pain from elixir) or when using full power; cannot mimic other specific persons

Contradictions

None

Fire Resistance and Fire Extinguishing

Source VersionXiyouji ch. 16, attested

Approaches the blazing Guanyin Temple without injury and uses wind/magic to help extinguish flames (while stealing). The stolen kasaya itself is fireproof, but the demon also shows no burn injury.

Limitations

Not immune to later divine fire; Guanyin's power still binds him. Game inverts this: second phase uses fire attacks, suggesting affinity rather than resistance

Contradictions

Game's fire-wreathed bear form contradicts novel's wind/water association; noted as adaptation-specific

Vulnerability to Guanyin's Elixir and Golden Cincture (Jinjin)

Source VersionXiyouji ch. 17, attested; Yu notes textual distinction between Sun's Jingu and Bear's Jinjin but popular conflation is common

Guanyin, disguised as Lingxuzi, offers a longevity elixir (actually a transformative poison/pill) that causes excruciating headache and forces reversion to true form when swallowed. She then places the Golden Cincture (Jinjin) on his head, which tightens and causes pain when its spell is recited, compelling obedience — parallel to Sun Wukong's Golden Hoop (Jingu).

Limitations

Only Guanyin (and by extension the Buddha who gifted the fillets) can control the spell; without recitation, no pain. Does not kill, only compels

Contradictions

Some popular summaries claim Guanyin used the same hoop as Sun Wukong's; text specifies a separate, similar artifact among several given by the Buddha (Yu translation notes three fillets: gold, tight, and restraint)

Stories & Myths

The Guanyin Temple and Elder Jinchi's Greed

Source Wu Cheng'en, Xiyouji, Chapter 16Culture Ming Chinese Buddhist vernacular fictionPeriod Ming dynasty, c. 1592 (narrative set in Tang dynasty pilgrimage, 7th century)

Summary

Tang Sanzang and Sun Wukong lodge at the Guanyin Temple (Guanyin Chanyuan), a wealthy monastery famed for its collection of kasayas. Its abbot, Elder Jinchi (Jinchi Zhanglao), 270 years old, proudly displays his own treasures and becomes insanely jealous upon seeing Tang Sanzang's Jinlan Jiasha, a brocade kasaya gifted by Guanyin/Buddha that glows with jewels and is impervious to elements. Jinchi cajoles Tang Sanzang into lending it overnight, then conspires with his monks to murder the pilgrims by arson — planning to keep the robe after they burn. Sun Wukong, warned by the temple's guardian deities, protects his master by conjuring a wind and later borrowing a fire cover from the heavens, so the fire consumes the temple instead of the pilgrims.

Participants

Tang Sanzang

Sun Wukong

Elder Jinchi

Guanyin Temple monks

Temple guardian deities (Jialan, Jiedi)

Consequences

Temple burns, Jinchi's plot fails; the fire creates opportunity for Heifeng Guai to intervene and sets up theft of the kasaya. Establishes thematic pairing of Jinchi and Heifeng Guai as twin embodiments of greed.

Variants

Yuan zaju has no Jinchi or kasaya theft; Jinchi's age (270) varies slightly by edition (200+ in some). TV adaptations expand Jinchi's backstory as Guanyin's former devotee.

The Black Wind and the Theft of the Brocade Kasaya

Source Wu Cheng'en, Xiyouji, Chapter 16Culture Ming ChinesePeriod Ming, c. 1592

Summary

Heifeng Guai, lord of Black Wind Cave, sees the fire from his mountain and rides a black wind to the temple, ostensibly to help extinguish it. Finding the unattended, glowing Jinlan Jiasha in Tang Sanzang's lodging, he seizes it and returns to his cave, where he admires it and plans to wear it at his upcoming birthday feast. Tang Sanzang discovers the loss at dawn and despairs; Sun Wukong vows to retrieve it.

Participants

Heifeng Guai

Tang Sanzang

Sun Wukong

Elder Jinchi (indirectly)

Consequences

Pilgrimage is stalled; Sun Wukong's retrieval quest begins. Theft is portrayed not as random banditry but as opportunistic attachment to a sacred object — mirroring Jinchi's greed.

Variants

Some illustrated editions show the bear carrying the robe in his mouth; game version has him taking the Craving Eyes relic alongside rebuilding the monastery.

Spear Duel at Black Wind Cave — Sun Wukong's Failed Retrieval

Source Wu Cheng'en, Xiyouji, Chapter 17Culture Ming ChinesePeriod Ming, c. 1592

Summary

Sun Wukong tracks the black wind to Black Wind Mountain and demands the robe. Heifeng Guai, in black armor with Black Tassel Spear, meets him in open combat. The two duel from morning to evening with no victor — a rare stalemate for Sun Wukong, demonstrating the bear's martial prowess. Sun tries to trick the demon by transforming into a small object and sneaking into the cave, but is discovered. He then enlists Guanyin Temple's remaining monks and even the celestial deities, but brute force fails. Recognizing the demon's cultivation and that killing him would be wasteful, Sun seeks Guanyin's help.

Participants

Heifeng Guai

Sun Wukong

Black Wind Mountain subordinate demons (including White Flower Snake and Lingxuzi's implied network)

Guanyin Temple monks

Consequences

Establishes Heifeng Guai as one of the few demons who can match Sun Wukong in single combat without external treasure. Forces pivot from martial to salvific solution — subjugation and conversion rather than extermination.

Variants

1986 TV series shortens duel to minutes; 2011 series adds extended wire-fu; Black Myth: Wukong turns this into a two-phase boss fight where player as Destined One replicates Sun's role.

Guanyin Becomes Lingxuzi — The Elixir Trap

Source Wu Cheng'en, Xiyouji, Chapter 17Culture Ming Chinese Buddhist fictionPeriod Ming, c. 1592

Summary

Sun Wukong flies to the South Sea to petition Guanyin. She already knows the affair and reveals that the bear is a cultivated neighbor of her Potalaka who slipped away. She transforms herself into Lingxuzi (also called Lingxu Zi), an old Daoist friend of Heifeng Guai who was expected for the birthday celebration, and has Sun Wukong transform into a small Dan (elixir) pill. As Lingxuzi, Guanyin presents the 'elixir' as a birthday gift. Heifeng Guai, delighted, swallows it. Guanyin reverts and recites a spell, causing the pill — now inside his body — to cause searing pain and force him to reveal his true bear form, rolling on the ground.

Participants

Guanyin (as Lingxuzi)

Heifeng Guai

Sun Wukong (as elixir pill)

Lingxuzi (original Daoist friend, offstage)

Consequences

Demon is incapacitated without being killed, allowing Guanyin to recover the kasaya intact and to apply the fillet. Demonstrates Buddhist skillful means (upaya): deception for salvation.

Variants

Some popular retellings mistakenly say Sun Wukong disguised himself as Lingxuzi; text is clear Guanyin takes that form while Sun becomes the pill. The identity of Lingxuzi is debated: novel implies a wolf or Daoist immortal friend; some commentaries identify him as a separate demon (Canglang Jing) who appears later in chapter 17's expanded demon assembly.

Subjugation and Conversion — Guardian of Mount Potalaka

Source Wu Cheng'en, Xiyouji, Chapter 17-18Culture Ming ChinesePeriod Ming, c. 1592

Summary

With Heifeng Guai writhing, Guanyin retrieves the Jinlan Jiasha and returns it to Tang Sanzang. She then places the Golden Cincture (Jinjin) on the bear's head, reciting the tightening spell. The bear begs for mercy and vows to follow the Buddhist path. Guanyin shaves his head in effect, gives him a Buddhist name (Shou Shan Da Hu Shen, Guardian of the Mountain), and takes him to the South Sea to guard Luojia Mountain (Potalaka), her sacred isle. He becomes a Dharma protector rather than being executed, paralleling Sun Wukong's own fillet-controlled discipleship. Tang Sanzang's party resumes the journey.

Participants

Guanyin

Heifeng Guai / Guardian Deity

Sun Wukong

Tang Sanzang

Consequences

Resolves the episode with conversion (shoufu) rather than extermination — a model for taming 'mind demons' (xinmo). Establishes Heifeng Guai's final canonical status as a benevolent guardian, explaining why he never reappears as antagonist.

Variants

Qing continuations (Xiyou Bu) do not reuse him; modern fanfiction often has him regretfully guarding the mountain. Game ending mirrors this: after defeat, he bows and returns to South Sea for judgment.

Antecedent — The Black Wind King as Zhu Bajie (Yuan Zaju Layer)

Source Yang Jingxian, Xiyouji zaju; discussion in modern scholarship (Geng Chuanming, Zhao Shuang)Culture Yuan Chinese dramaPeriod Yuan dynasty, 13th-14th century

Summary

In the earliest known dramatic version of the pilgrimage, there is no bear demon. The figure called Heifeng Dawang (Black Wind Great King) is Zhu Bajie himself (then called Tianpeng Yuanshuai), who dwells in Heifeng Dong. The 'Black Wind Cave' setting and title are thus originally Bajie's. When Wu Cheng'en novelizes the story, he separates the elements: Bajie is given a new backstory (expelled Marshal Tianpeng, reborn as pig-man in Yunzhan Cave, later Gao Village) and the Black Wind Cave is reassigned to a newly invented bear spirit, allowing both characters to coexist and doubling the theme of appetite/greed.

Participants

Zhu Bajie / Tianpeng (as Heifeng Dawang)

Wu Cheng'en (as redactor)

Heifeng Guai (as later invention)

Consequences

Explains why Heifeng Guai has no deep folklore prior to Ming novel — he is a literary creation synthesizing bear lore and a recycled toponym. Scholarly debate continues on how much of Bajie's gluttony was transferred symbolically to the bear.

Variants

No theft plot in zaju; Bajie's Black Wind King is a mountain bandit-like figure rather than robe thief.

Relationships
Heifeng Guai→Tang Sanzang (Tripitaka)victim

Owner of the stolen Jinlan Jiasha; Heifeng Guai steals the robe entrusted to him by Guanyin/Buddha. Tang Sanzang is the moral center whose attachment to the robe (and Jinchi's) triggers the episode, but he does not fight the demon directly.

Heifeng Guai→Sun Wukong (Monkey King)hostile

Primary martial rival. Sun Wukong duels Heifeng Guai to a stalemate with staff vs. spear, attempts trickery, then fetches Guanyin. Their parallel fillets (Sun's Jingu, Bear's Jinjin) link them as 'restrained minds' in Buddhist allegory.

Heifeng Guai→Guanyin (Avalokiteshvara, Bodhisattva of Compassion)worshipper

Heifeng Guai's subduer, converter and final master. After subjugation he becomes her mountain guardian on Luojia Shan (Potalaka) in the South Sea. Guanyin uses skillful means (disguise as Lingxuzi, elixir pill, Golden Cincture) to tame rather than kill him.

Heifeng Guai→Elder Jinchi (Jinchi Zhanglao) and Guanyin Templeally

Neighbor and implicit ally in greed. Jinchi, abbot of Guanyin Chanyuan, covets the same kasaya; his arson creates the opportunity for theft. Heifeng Guai attends the temple's affairs and later, in game lore, rebuilds the monastery and revives Jinchi as undead. In novel they are thematic doubles — both cultivated (Jinchi 270 years old) yet undone by attachment (tan).

Heifeng Guai→Lingxuzi (Lingxu Zi)ally

Daoist friend and birthday guest of Heifeng Guai, whose identity Guanyin borrows. In the novel's demon assembly, Lingxuzi is described as a cultivated friend who practices elixir arts; later commentary identifies him as a wolf or other animal spirit. His absence allows Guanyin's impersonation.

Heifeng Guai→White Flower Snake Demon (Baihuashe Guai) and Black Wind Mountain demonsally

Subordinate demon at Black Wind Mountain who appears in chapter 17's expanded cave network, sometimes paired with Heifeng Guai as his lieutenant. In some readings, the mountain hosts a small demon court that celebrates the bear's birthday.

Heifeng Guai→Jinlan Jiasha (Brocade Kasaya) and the Buddhacreator

The kasaya is a Buddha-bestowed treasure (via Guanyin) that Heifeng Guai steals; it symbolizes Dharma and is the object of his attachment. The Buddha is ultimate source of the fillets Guanyin uses to bind both Sun Wukong and Heifeng Guai.

Arson and Attempted Murder (Elder Jinchi's Plot)

Source Xiyouji ch. 16

Description

Elder Jinchi and his monks plan to murder Tang Sanzang and Sun Wukong by setting fire to their lodgings to steal the kasaya. The plan would have killed sleeping pilgrims. Sun Wukong's protection averts it, but the temple itself burns, killing several monks. Heifeng Guai exploits the fire to steal.

Scholarly Context

Scholars read this as critique of monastic corruption and attachment; Geng Chuanming pairs Jinchi and Heifeng Guai as twin greed figures — one institutional, one wild — both highly cultivated yet morally failed.

Magical Coercion and Bodily Pain (Golden Cincture)

Source Xiyouji ch. 17; Yu notes on Jinjin vs Jingu

Description

Guanyin forces Heifeng Guai to swallow a pill that causes agonizing internal pain and then binds him with a tightening fillet that causes headache when spell is recited. Compliance is obtained through pain, not consent. Parallels Sun Wukong's own subjugation.

Scholarly Context

Buddhist allegorical reading: uncontrolled desire (bear) and restless mind (monkey) must be restrained by Dharma. Modern ethical readings note the coercive aspect — conversion via torture — and question romanticized 'taming' narratives.

Suicide of Elder Jinchi

Source Xiyouji ch. 17

Description

After his temple burns and theft fails, Elder Jinchi, in despair and shame, kills himself by banging his head against a wall (or pillar). Text presents it as karmic retribution for greed.

Scholarly Context

Ming fiction often uses suicide as moral closure for corrupt clergy; not sanitized in children's abridgments, which sometimes omit it.

Contradictions & Disputes

Species - Black Bear vs Xiongpi (Giant Bear)

Position A

Heifeng Guai is a black bear (heixiong) — common translation as Black Bear Spirit.

Position A — evidence

Modern translations (Jenner, Yu) and popular name Heixiong Jing; black color matches Heifeng (black wind) motif and black armor.

Position B

Text calls him xiongpi (熊罴), an archaic term for a huge, powerful bear, possibly brown bear/grizzly, used to evoke antiquity and mythic strength (Yu the Great).

Position B — evidence

Original Chinese: '那怪是熊罴得道' and Zhao Shuang's analysis linking xiongpi to pre-Qin bear worship and Yu the Great's bear transformation; xiongpi in classics often denotes large brown bear.

Scholarly Consensus

No zoological consensus needed; xiongpi is literary archaism, not Linnaean species. Most scholars accept 'bear spirit' as sufficient, noting Wu Cheng'en deliberately used archaic term for grandeur.

Location of Guardianship - Black Wind Mountain vs South Sea Potalaka

Position A

He becomes guardian of Black Wind Mountain itself after conversion.

Position A — evidence

Popular summaries and some TV adaptations keep him on Black Wind Mountain as 'mountain god' for narrative convenience.

Position B

He becomes guardian of Luojia Shan (Mount Potalaka) in the South Sea under Guanyin.

Position B — evidence

Xiyouji ch. 17 text: Guanyin takes him to South Sea to be 'Shoushan Dahushen' (守山大護神) of Luojia Shan; Yu translation notes this explicitly.

Scholarly Consensus

Text supports South Sea Potalaka; Black Wind Mountain guardianship is a conflation or game simplification (Black Myth keeps him on Black Wind Mountain until post-defeat return).

The Fillet - Same as Sun Wukong's vs Distinct Jinjin

Position A

Guanyin used the same Golden Hoop (Jingu) that restrains Sun Wukong.

Position A — evidence

Popular retellings collapse all fillets into 'the tight hoop' for simplicity.

Position B

Guanyin used a separate but similar artifact, the Golden Cincture / Restraint Fillet (Jinjin 禁箍儿), one of three fillets gifted by Buddha (Jinjin, Jingu, and another).

Position B — evidence

Xiyouji ch. 17 and Yu's notes: Buddha gave Guanyin three fillets; she uses one on Sun Wukong (Jingu), one on Heifeng Guai (Jinjin), one later on another demon. Text distinguishes names and spells.

Scholarly Consensus

Distinct but functionally identical fillets; conflation is common but textual distinction is clear.

Lingxuzi's Identity

Position A

Lingxuzi is a Daoist immortal friend of Heifeng Guai.

Position A — evidence

Novel calls him Lingxu Zi with Daoist title 'Zi', presents him as elixir-making recluse invited to birthday.

Position B

Lingxuzi is himself an animal spirit (wolf/Canglang) or subordinate demon of Black Wind Mountain.

Position B — evidence

Later chapter demon lists and Qing commentary identify Lingxuzi with Canglang Jing (Wolf Demon) and White Flower Snake as cave subordinates; some editions treat him as wolf spirit.

Scholarly Consensus

Ambiguous; novel leaves Lingxuzi offstage and Guanyin impersonates him, so his true nature is underdetermined. Both readings coexist.

Research Scope

Dossier covers Heifeng Guai (Black Wind Demon / Black Bear Spirit) as a fictional demon in the Ming novel Xiyouji (Journey to the West) attributed to Wu Cheng'en, its Yuan zaju antecedent, and its modern reception. Focus is on Chapters 16-17 of the 100-chapter novel and critical translations. Modern adaptation coverage prioritizes verifiable screen and game appearances; exhaustive indexing of all regional opera and manhua versions remains incomplete. Research cutoff 2026-05-11.
