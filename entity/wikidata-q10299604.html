The Approach

In the rain-soaked hills of Roman Britain, a god was worshipped under two names carved in stone. Ialonus Contrebis, a deity of meadows and settled communities, left only three inscriptions—two in Lancashire, one in distant Gaul. Who was this shadowy figure, and why did his worshippers link him to both the open land and the hearth?

Ialonus Contrebis

Pronunciation ee-AL-oh-nus KON-treh-bis

Known As

IalonusContrebis

Etymology

The name Ialonus is thought to derive from a Celtic root meaning 'meadow' or 'cleared land' (cf. Welsh 'ial'? or Gaulish 'ialo-'). Contrebis may be related to a term for 'settled community' or 'dwelling together' (cf. 'contrebia' in Celtiberian, meaning 'community' or 'meeting place').

Cultural Ownership

Romano-British and Gallo-Roman; likely of Celtic origin, adopted into Roman religious practice.

Disputed Identifications

It is unclear whether Ialonus and Contrebis were two separate deities invoked together, or two aspects/epithets of a single god. Some scholars treat them as a compound deity, others as a pair.

Domains & Habitats

Based on etymological interpretation, Ialonus may have been a god of meadows or cleared land; Contrebis a god of the settled community. These domains are inferred from name meanings, not explicit textual evidence.

Version Differences

The inscriptions from Britain and Gaul may reflect regional variations in worship or nomenclature. The British inscriptions combine both names; the Gaulish inscription uses only Contrebis.
