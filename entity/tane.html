Who It Is

Before there was light, before there was sky or open earth, the children of Rangi and Papa lived their entire lives pressed between the chest of their sky father and the belly of their earth mother — a crowded, warm, and utterly dark existence. While Tūmatauenga called for parricide and Rongo, Tangaroa, and Haumia-tiketike exhausted themselves trying to pry the parents apart, it was Tāne — the forest god — who solved the riddle. He did not strike or shout. He lay on his back, braced his legs against his mother's body, and pushed with the steady, unrelenting force of a tree growing toward light. Rangi rose. The stars went up. The world began. Tāne is characterized by patient deliberation in contrast to his hot-headed brother Tūmatauenga. Where Tū proposes parricide, Tāne advocates separation — a solution that preserves both parents while freeing the children. His method is physical and determined: after multiple failed attempts by Rongo, Tangaroa, Haumia-tiketike, and Tū, Tāne succeeds through sustained effort and correct technique (lying on his back, pushing with his legs). Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Tāne derives from Proto-Polynesian *tane, meaning 'man' or 'male'. In Māori, the word tāne signifies both 'man' and 'husband', reflecting the deity's role as progenitor of humankind and the masculine creative principle. Powers
Separation of Rangi and Papa

Tāne's defining feat: he forces the sky father (Ranginui) and earth mother (Papatūānuku) apart, ending the primordial darkness in which the children of the primal couple were confined. After four failed attempts by his brothers, Tāne lies on his back, braces his legs against Papatūānuku, and pushes Rangi upward into the heavens. This act creates the world of light, air, and space — the environment in which all subsequent life unfolds.

Limit: None attested; the separation is permanent. However, Rangi and Papa remain close together in loving embrace, and their lingering intimacy generates the moisture that feeds the forest — Tāne's own domain depends on their residual closeness.

Celestial ornamentation

After separating his parents, Tāne searches for luminous bodies to adorn Rangi, obtaining the stars and throwing them into the sky along with the Moon and the Sun. This transforms the bare sky into a decorated realm, ensuring Rangi looks handsome.

Limit: Not attested.

Progenitor of humankind and forest life

Tāne is credited with creating or shaping the first humans. In one tradition he makes Tiki, the first man. In another, he attempts to find a wife among non-human beings, fathering insects, birds, and plants, then shapes Hine-ahuone ('earth-formed maid') from the soil. This makes him the ultimate ancestor of humanity in a cosmogonic sense.

Limit: The Tiki and Hine-ahuone traditions are variant, not simultaneous.

Three baskets of knowledge (ngā kete o te wānanga)

Tāne is said to have brought down the three baskets of knowledge from the highest heaven (Io-matua-kore in some traditions), conferring wisdom, sacred lore, and cultural practices upon humanity. This is a major esoteric tradition within some iwi, especially Te Arawa and Ngāi Tūhoe, and provides theological legitimation for tohunga learning.

Limit: The Io-matua-kore tradition is iwi-specific and was recorded mainly in the 19th century; some scholars debate whether it represents an ancient layer or a later development influenced by Christian monotheism.

Forest and bird patronage / resource mediation

As atua of the forest, Tāne presides over all arboreal and avian life. He supplies his brothers' descendants with canoes, fishhooks, and nets — forest products used to catch Tangaroa's fish. This role establishes him as an economic mediator among the atua, linking the forest economy to the sea economy.

Limit: Not attested.

Accounts I
The separation of Rangi and PapaGrey, G. (1956). Polynesian Mythology. Whitcombe and Tombs: Christchurch, pp. 2-3.

Oral; first written transcription 1840s–1850s

The children of Rangi (Sky Father) and Papa (Earth Mother) live in cramped darkness between their embracing parents. Tū proposes killing them; Tāne advocates separation. Rongo, Tangaroa, Haumia-tiketike, and Tū all attempt and fail. Tāne succeeds by lying on his back and pushing with his legs, raising Rangi into the sky. He then decorates the sky with stars, moon, and sun. Tāwhirimātea, god of storms, furious at the separation, joins Rangi in the sky and unleashes violent storms on Tāne's forests and the world generally. The world of light, air, and space is created. Tāwhirimātea's storms become the template for weather and tempest. The enmity between Tāne (forest) and Tangaroa (sea) is established, explaining the perpetual tension between land and ocean in Māori resource use.

Tāne and Hine-tītama: the origin of deathGrey, G. (1956). Polynesian Mythology, p. 38; Orbell, M. (1998). The Concise Encyclopedia of Māori Myth and Legend, p. 38.

Oral; recorded 1840s–1850s

Tāne seeks a wife. He first encounters non-human females, fathering insects, birds, and plants. One such union with Rangahore produces a stone; he abandons her. He then shapes Hine-ahuone from soil and fathers Hine-tītama with her (in some versions, Hine-tītama is shaped directly by Tāne). Without knowing his identity, Hine-tītama marries Tāne. Upon discovering he is her father, she flees to the underworld and becomes Hine-nui-te-pō, goddess of night and death. Tāne pursues her and begs her to return. She refuses, telling him to raise their children in the upper world while she receives their spirits at death. Death enters the world as a permanent condition. The underworld (Rarohenga) gains its first ruler. Tāne's role as progenitor is shadowed by the incestuous origin of the death-deity.

Accounts II
Tāne and the three baskets of knowledgeOrbell, M. (1998). The Concise Encyclopedia of Māori Myth and Legend, p. 145; iwi-specific traditions.

Oral; recorded 19th–20th century

In the highest heaven dwells Io-matua-kore ('Io the parentless'), the supreme being in some Māori cosmologies. Tāne ascends through the layered heavens and receives the three baskets of knowledge (ngā kete o te wānanga): the basket of light, the basket of darkness, and the basket of pursuit (or ritual, prayer, and spell in other versions). He brings these down to humanity, establishing the basis of Māori esoteric learning, karakia (incantation), and cultural practice. Humanity gains access to sacred knowledge and tohunga learning frameworks. The narrative provides theological legitimation for esoteric traditions within iwi.

Tāne and Tangaroa: the forest-sea rivalryGrey, G. (1971). Nga Mahi a Nga Tupuna, 4th ed. Reed: Wellington, pp. 5-6.

Oral; recorded 19th century

After Tāwhirimātea's storms, Tangaroa's descendants Ikatere (fish ancestor) and Tu-te-wehiwehi (reptile ancestor) flee to sea and forest respectively. Tāne shelters the reptiles in the forest while Tangaroa's fish remain in the ocean. Tangaroa resents Tāne for hiding his runaway children. Tāne compensates by supplying Tūmatauenga's descendants with canoes, fishhooks, and nets to catch Tangaroa's fish. Tangaroa retaliates by overturning canoes and sending floods that sweep away houses, land, and trees. The forest-sea reciprocity becomes a structural principle in Māori resource use. The rivalry explains why fishing is dangerous and why canoes require ritual protection.

Tāne is a single, unified Māori god with one fixed story. — Tāne narratives vary significantly across iwi. There is no single canonical version; each tribal grouping has its own whakapapa-linked variant of his stories, names, and domains.

Tāne is the Māori equivalent of the Christian God or a supreme creator deity. — Tāne is a culture-hero and creator-atua within a polytheistic framework, not a monotheistic supreme being. The Io tradition exists but is iwi-specific and debated; it does not make Tāne supreme.

Tāne Mahuta is just a named tree, not a living embodiment of the god. — For many Māori, especially Te Roroa and Ngāpuhi, Tāne Mahuta is treated as the physical body of Tāne himself — a living taonga with mauri (life force), not merely an eponym.
