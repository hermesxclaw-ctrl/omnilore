Who It Is

Tāne (full name Tāne Mahuta, 'Tāne the Great') is the Māori god of forests, birds, light, and the divine force that separated his parents — Ranginui (Sky Father) and Papatūānuku (Earth Mother) — bringing light into the world and enabling life to flourish. He is one of the most important and beloved deities in Māori cosmology: the creator of humanity, the planter of the great forests, and the source of the wood that built waka (canoes), wharenui (meeting houses), and all things of the forest.

Physically, Tāne is represented in Māori art through the curvilinear forms of whakairo (wood carving) — the art that is his gift to humanity. He is depicted as a powerful male figure, often with elaborate facial moko (tattoo) and adorned with the patterns of the forest: fronds, leaves, and birds. The most iconic living representation of Tāne is Tāne Mahuta, a giant kauri tree in the Waipoua Forest of Northland, New Zealand — the largest known kauri tree, estimated to be 2,000 years old, standing over 50 meters tall, and considered by Māori to be a living embodiment of the god.

Tāne's central myth — one of the most important creation narratives in Māori culture — is the separation of Rangi and Papa. In the beginning, Rangi (the Sky) and Papa (the Earth) lay locked in a tight embrace, their 70+ children trapped in the darkness between them. The children debated what to do. Tūmatauenga (god of war) advocated killing the parents; Tāwhirimātea (god of winds) argued they should be left alone. Tāne proposed a middle way: separate them so that light could enter the world. One by one, the other gods attempted and failed: Rongo, Tangaroa, Haumia-tiketike, and Tū all tried to push the parents apart but could not. Finally, Tāne lay on his back on Papa and pushed with his powerful legs against Rangi. Slowly, agonizingly, with a cry that echoed through the cosmos, Tāne separated his parents. Rangi rose high into the sky, and light flooded the world for the first time. The tears of Rangi became the rain; the mists rising from Papa became the morning dew — the eternal grief of separated lovers.

After bringing light, Tāne created humanity. According to Māori tradition, Tāne sought a partner among the elements but found none suitable. He then formed a woman (Hine-ahu-one, 'Earth-formed Maiden') from the red clay of Papa's body. He breathed life into her nostrils, and she became the first human woman — the mother of all humanity. This myth imbues humans with a sacred connection to both Tāne (who gave us form) and the earth (from which we are made).

Tāne is also the planter of the forests. After separating his parents, he clothed Papa (the naked earth) in vegetation, planting the great trees of the forest. He is the source of the birds — his children who fill the forests with song. All woodcarving is sacred to Tāne; before felling a tree for carving, Māori carvers traditionally perform karakia (prayers) to Tāne, asking forgiveness for taking one of his children. The meeting house (wharenui) — the most important Māori architectural form — is conceptualized as Tāne's body, with the ridgepole as his spine, the rafters as his ribs, and the interior as the space he created between Rangi and Papa.

Form

Tāne is represented in Māori art through the curvilinear forms of whakairo (wood carving) — the art that is his gift to humanity. He is depicted as a powerful male figure, often with elaborate facial moko (tattoo) in the spiral patterns that evoke fern fronds (koru), tree rings, and the unfurling growth of the forest. His body may be adorned with the patterns of the forest: leaves, birds, and the intertwining forms of climbing plants. Unlike Tūmatauenga's aggressive stance with protruding tongue, Tāne is typically shown with a more serene, balanced posture — the upright dignity of the great tree. The most iconic living representation of Tāne is Tāne Mahuta, a giant kauri tree in the Waipoua Forest — the largest known kauri, estimated at 1,250-2,500 years old, standing over 50 meters tall with a trunk girth of 13.8 meters. This tree is considered by Māori to be a living embodiment of the god, and its name is given with the deepest reverence.

Powers

Separation of Heaven and Earth Tāne's greatest feat was pushing Ranginui (Sky Father) and Papatūānuku (Earth Mother) apart. After every other god had tried and failed, Tāne lay on his back on Papa's body and pushed upward with his powerful legs against Rangi. Slowly, with a cry that echoed through the cosmos, he forced them apart. Light flooded into the world for the first time. This act — the first creative act in Māori cosmology — established space for life to grow between earth and sky. Māori creation narrative (Te Wehenga) The separation was not total — Rangi and Papa remain connected by their longing, expressed through mist, rain, and dew. The separation caused cosmic pain that reverberates in nature. Tāne could separate but not heal the wound. In some traditions, the separation is achieved through a collective effort, with Tāne succeeding where others failed; in others, the kauri tree itself grows between earth and sky, doing the work naturally.

Creation of Humanity (Tāne's Breath) After separating his parents, Tāne sought to create a being who would populate the world. He went to Kurawaka, a sacred place, and took red earth (oneone) from the body of Papa. He shaped the earth into the form of a woman — Hine-ahu-one (the Earth-formed Woman). He breathed life into her nostrils, giving her the breath of life (te hā). From this first woman, all humanity descends. In some traditions, Tāne later created a male companion from the earth as well. This act makes Tāne the divine father of humanity — the source of our life, our form, and our connection to the earth. Māori creation narrative The first humans were mortal. Tāne breathed life into them but could not grant immortality — that was attempted later (and failed) by Māui. Some iwi attribute human creation to different gods or give Tūmatauenga a role as humanity's ancestor rather than Tāne as sole creator.

Forest and Bird Sovereignty Tāne governs all forests, trees, and birds. He causes trees to grow from the soil and birds to fill the branches with song. Every tree — from the smallest shrub to the mightiest kauri — is his child. Every bird — from the tiny riroriro (grey warbler) to the great moa (now extinct) — is under his protection. The forest is his wharenui (meeting house), a living temple of leaves and branches. Wood, bark, leaves, berries, and birdlife — all the resources of the forest — are Tāne's gifts to humanity. Māori oral tradition and ecological knowledge Tāne's forest domain is vulnerable to Tūmatauenga's revenge — humans cut his trees for firewood, building materials, and canoes. His birds are prey to introduced predators and human hunting. The forest is not inviolable; it exists in tension with human survival needs. In some traditions, certain forest areas or specific trees are under the protection of other beings (taniwha, patupaiarehe) rather than Tāne alone.

Gift of Wood Carving (Whakairo) Tāne gave humanity the art of whakairo — the sacred craft of carving wood into canoes, meeting houses, weapons, tools, and art. This gift enables humans to transform the raw material of the forest into objects of beauty, utility, and spiritual significance. The carved meeting house (wharenui) represents Tāne's body: the ridge pole is his spine, the rafters are his ribs, and the interior is the space between earth and sky that Tāne created. Māori artistic tradition Wood carving is a tapu (sacred) practice with strict protocols. Only properly trained carvers (tohunga whakairo) may perform it. Errors in ritual or technique are considered offenses against Tāne. Some traditions attribute the origin of specific carving styles or tools to other figures rather than Tāne directly.

Solar and Celestial Authority In some traditions, Tāne is responsible for placing the sun, moon, and stars in the sky — adorning the body of his father Rangi with celestial lights after the separation. He also governs the light of day and the warmth of the sun, which enables the forest to grow and life to flourish. In Hawaiian tradition, Kāne is specifically the god of sunlight and fresh water. Māori and Hawaiian traditions This role is less emphasized in Māori tradition than Tāne's forest and human-creation roles. The placement of heavenly bodies is sometimes attributed to other figures. The exact assignment of celestial bodies varies across Polynesian cultures.

Behavior & Habitat

Tāne embodies the creative, generative, life-giving principle of the cosmos. His defining act — separating Rangi and Papa by lying on his back and pushing the sky away with his legs — is the archetypal creative act: bringing light into darkness through effort, intelligence, and persistence. Unlike Tūmatauenga's direct violence or Rongo's passive silence, Tāne's approach is active but measured — he proposes a middle path (separation rather than killing). His behavior reflects the patience and steady growth of the forest: he creates over time, builds complex ecosystems, and nurtures his children (the trees, birds, and humans). As the god who gave humanity the art of wood carving, Tāne is also the divine artist — the one who transforms raw material into beautiful, meaningful form. His behavior toward humanity is paternal and generous: he gave us forests for wood, birds for song and food, and the breath of life itself.

Accounts

In the beginning, Ranginui (Sky Father) and Papatūānuku (Earth Mother) lay locked in a tight, passionate embrace, their 70+ children trapped in the suffocating darkness between them. The children debated their fate. Tūmatauenga wanted to kill the parents. Tāwhirimātea wanted to leave them alone. Tāne proposed a middle path: separate them gently so that light could enter the world. One by one, the gods attempted to push the parents apart — Rongo, Tangaroa, Haumia, and Tū all tried and failed. Finally, Tāne lay on his back on Papa's body. He placed his shoulders against the earth and his feet against the sky. Slowly, with immense effort, he began to push. The separation was agonizing — Rangi and Papa cried out in grief, their tears becoming rain and mist. Tāne pushed and pushed until, with a tremendous cry, the parents were forced apart. Light flooded the world. The children were free.

After separating his parents and bringing light to the world, Tāne looked upon the empty earth and desired to create a being who would inhabit it. He went to Kurawaka, a sacred place where red earth lay exposed. He gathered the earth — the flesh of his mother, Papa — and began to shape it with his hands. He formed a body, molding clay into the figure of a woman. He breathed into her nostrils — the hā (breath of life) passed from Tāne into the earth-formed figure. She sneezed, and life entered her. Her name was Hine-ahu-one — 'the Earth-formed Woman.' From her, all humanity descends. Tāne later mated with Hine-ahu-one, and they produced a daughter, Hine-tītama, who became the first woman born of human parents.

Tāne desired to obtain knowledge for humanity. He ascended through the twelve heavens to the highest realm — Te Toi-o-ngā-rangi (the Uppermost Heaven) — the dwelling place of Io-matua-kore (Io the Parentless One, the supreme being in some traditions). There, Tāne received three kete (baskets) of knowledge: Te Kete Tuauri (the basket of sacred knowledge, ritual, and spiritual understanding), Te Kete Tuatea (the basket of ancestral knowledge, history, and tradition), and Te Kete Aronui (the basket of secular knowledge, arts, crafts, and practical wisdom — the knowledge of the world before us). Tāne also received two sacred stones (whatukura) — the Hukatai and the Rehutai — which represent the power to increase knowledge. He brought these gifts down from the heavens and gave them to humanity, enabling civilization, culture, and spiritual understanding.

The kauri tree (Agathis australis) is sacred to Tāne. The most famous kauri — Tāne Mahuta in the Waipoua Forest — is considered a living embodiment of the god himself. Standing over 50 meters tall with a trunk circumference of 13.8 meters, this tree is estimated to be 1,250-2,500 years old. Māori tradition holds that the kauri was created when Tāne shed his skin — the bark of the kauri is Tāne's cast-off essence. Tāne Mahuta is approached with reverence, karakia (prayers), and a sense of awe. The tree is a pilgrimage site, a national treasure, and a reminder of the ancient forest that once covered Aotearoa. Its health is monitored closely, and kauri dieback disease is treated as a serious threat not just to a tree but to a living god.

Scholarly Debates
Origin of Humanity: Tāne vs. Tūmatauenga
Tāne is the sole creator of humanity — he shaped the first woman from earth and breathed life into her at Kurawaka. ⇌ Tūmatauenga is humanity's ancestor — humans descend from Tū's line and inherit his warrior nature.
Evidence for A: The Kurawaka creation narrative and related whakapapa, widely attested across iwi.
Evidence for B: Whakapapa traditions that position Tū as the primary human ancestor and emphasize humanity's aggressive nature.
The Io Tradition and Tāne's Ascent
Tāne's ascent to the twelve heavens and receipt of the three baskets of knowledge from Io represents an authentic, pre-European esoteric Māori tradition. ⇌ The Io tradition is a post-contact development influenced by Christian missionary concepts of a supreme being, and the baskets narrative is a relatively recent addition to Māori mythology.
Evidence for A: Some tohunga (priests) preserved esoteric knowledge including references to Io that predate European contact.

What We Don't Know

The specific rituals of Tāne worship — the karakia offered before cutting trees, the ceremonies at Kurawaka, and the full protocols of forest tapu — are incompletely documented due to colonial disruption. The exact relationship between Māori Tāne and Hawaiian Kāne (and other Polynesian cognates) in terms of shared vs. independently developed attributes requires further comparative study. The Io tradition and Tāne's three baskets narrative remain controversial among scholars regarding pre-European authenticity. The complex family dynamics of Tāne's creation narrative (shaping a woman from earth and then mating with her) have been analyzed by modern scholars through gender and postcolonial lenses but lack comprehensive treatment. Direct archaeological evidence for Tāne worship is limited; most evidence comes from oral tradition and living cultural practice.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Tāne as the ultimate DIY creator god. Most creator deities snap their fingers or speak a word and things appear. Tāne? He lay on his back and bench-pressed the sky. He knelt in the mud and threw pottery until a human shape held. He climbed to the top of the universe for knowledge and carried it back in actual baskets. He's the god of effort — the one who shows that creation is not magic but work. He's also the god of the in-between: the forest is not earth or sky but a living bridge between them, and Tāne created that bridge first with his own body. Every tree you see is Tāne still doing the work of holding the sky up. Every carved figure in a meeting house is Tāne's art continuing through human hands. He is the breath in your lungs — literally, by his own account — and the wood beneath your feet. He doesn't demand worship so much as he invites you to notice: you are breathing, you are standing, you are here because a god once lay on his back and pushed.
