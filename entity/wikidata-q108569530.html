Who It Is

A name that sounds soft enough for a flower turns up everywhere from a sailor's foundling baby to a Southside Serpent with a leather jacket and a shaved head — Sweet Pea is not one character but a nickname that keeps attaching itself to the tough, the tender, and the overlooked, and every version carries the same quiet joke: something sweet that refuses to wilt.

Version-specific:

- Swee'Pea: Pre-verbal infant; behavior is curiosity, babbling, crawling, magical summoning (in later comics he has a limited ability to whistle or use 'magic' depending on writer). Personality is innocent, trusting, in need of protection; Popeye and Olive Oyl act as adoptive parents. Attested: Segar strips show him as well-behaved but occasionally superhumanly strong or prophetic for gag purposes.

Powers
Infant innocence / plot-device vulnerability (Popeye)

Swee'Pea has no consistent superpower in Segar's original; his narrative power is to motivate Popeye/Olive to protect him. Later comic writers occasionally give him a magical whistle or ability to summon Popeye, and in some 1960s cartoons he exhibits brief super-strength or precognition for comedic effect.

Limit: Powers are gag-dependent, not canonically stable. No combat ability. Requires adult guardians.

Physical intimidation and street fighting (Riverdale)

Peak-human strength, brawling, intimidation, motorcycle riding, gang tactics. No superhuman abilities. Narrative role is enforcer and loyal soldier.

Limit: Vulnerable to gunfire, legal system, gang politics. No powers beyond human. Character is not bulletproof; is beaten and arrested multiple times.

Combat proficiency and survival instinct (Sucker Punch)

In fantasy layers, Sweet Pea is proficient with firearms, hand-to-hand combat, and tactical movement (WWI trench, dragon battle). In 'real world' layer, these are imagined coping mechanisms for trauma, not literal powers.

Limit: Fantasy feats do not translate to real-world escape; in reality she is an institutionalized young woman with no weapons. Her survival depends on sacrifice and cooperation.

Social invisibility and methodical killing (Sweetpea novel/TV)

Rhiannon's 'power' is being overlooked by society, allowing her to stalk and kill without initial suspicion. Methodical planning, use of everyday objects as weapons. No supernatural ability.

Limit: Psychologically unstable, leaves evidence, ultimately pursued by police. Grounded thriller logic.

Flight (avian) (Secret Life of Pets)

As a budgerigar, Sweetpea can fly short distances within apartment, mimic speech.

Limit: Small bird, physically vulnerable, comedic role only.

Accounts I
The Arrival of Swee'Pea - Popeye Adopts a FoundlingE. C. Segar, Thimble Theatre daily strip

1933

A baby boy is left on Popeye's doorstep (in later retellings, found by Olive Oyl). Popeye, despite being a rough sailor, adopts him and names him Swee'Pea (sometimes explained as 'Sweet Pea'). The strip follows Popeye learning to care for an infant while protecting him from villains like the Sea Hag and Brutus. The story establishes Popeye's softer paternal side and introduces a recurring child character who humanizes the cast. Swee'Pea becomes a permanent member of the Popeye cast, appearing in Fleischer cartoons (1936 onward), Famous Studios shorts, and later comic books. The adoption story is retold as origin in almost every reboot.

Southside Serpents Initiation - Sweet Pea vs. Archie and JugheadRiverdale, Season 2 (The CW)

2017-2018

Sweet Pea is introduced as a militant member of the Southside Serpents, hostile to Jughead Jones (new Serpent) and to Northside intruders. He participates in the Serpent trials, the riot at Riverdale High, and the conflict with the Ghoulies. Initially antagonistic, he questions Jughead's loyalty but later fights alongside him against Penny Peabody and the Ghoulies, and participates in the cover-up of the Shadow Lake incident. Establishes Sweet Pea as core Serpent ensemble; his loyalty arc mirrors the Serpents' integration into Riverdale High. Character remains through Season 7, graduating and persisting as Serpent elder.

The Five Girls' Escape Plan - Sweet Pea's SacrificeSucker Punch (Warner Bros., 2011)

2011

In 1960s Vermont, five young women institutionalized at Lennox House imagine their escape attempts as elaborate fantasy missions to retrieve five objects (map, fire, knife, key, and a final sacrifice). Sweet Pea, the oldest and most cynical, initially dismisses Babydoll's plan. After her younger sister Rocket is killed during the third mission (retrieving the knife), Sweet Pea is devastated and nearly gives up, but Babydoll sacrifices herself so Sweet Pea can escape. In the real world, Sweet Pea escapes the asylum on a bus, implied to be free, while Babydoll undergoes lobotomy. Sweet Pea is the only one of the five to physically escape; her survival is the emotional payoff. The film's ambiguous reality/fantasy structure makes her escape both literal and symbolic. The extended cut adds additional character beats for Sweet Pea and Rocket's sisterhood.

Accounts II
Rhiannon Lewis Becomes Sweetpea - The Wallflower KillerC.J. Skuse, Sweetpea (novel) and Sky Atlantic series

2017 (novel), 2024 (series)

Rhiannon Lewis, a quiet admin assistant in a small English town, has been invisible and bullied all her life. After a personal humiliation and the death of her father, she begins killing people who wrong her or others - starting with a man who harasses her and escalating to serial murder. She narrates with deadpan humor, maintaining a double life as 'Sweetpea' - a nickname from childhood. The novel is a first-person diary of murders; the TV series externalizes her kills with visual dark comedy and adds a police investigation closing in. Novel spawned sequels In Bloom (2018) and Dead Head (2021) and a TV adaptation starring Ella Purnell, which renewed interest in the 'cozy serial killer' subgenre. The story reclaims 'Sweetpea' as ironic mask for female rage.

Sweetpea the Budgerigar's Apartment AdventureThe Secret Life of Pets (Illumination, 2016) and sequel 2019

2016

Sweetpea (also spelled Sweet Pea) is a green-and-yellow budgerigar who lives in the same Manhattan apartment building as Max and Duke. Though a minor character, Sweetpea appears in ensemble scenes where the pets interact while owners are away, notably in the opening 'what pets do when you're gone' montage and in the climactic rescue. The character returns in The Secret Life of Pets 2 (2019) with a brief cameo. Sweetpea became a recognizable minor mascot for merchandising (plush toys) and is often cited in lists of 'pets named Sweetpea' influencing real-world pet naming trends after 2016.

Sweetpea Beauty and the Princess LessonVeggieTales: Sweetpea Beauty (Big Idea, 2010)

2010

A retelling of the fairy tale 'Sleeping Beauty' via VeggieTales' vegetable cast. Sweetpea Beauty, an asparagus princess, learns that true beauty comes from inner character, not outward appearance, after a vain queen's curse. The episode teaches Proverbs-based lessons about self-worth, with songs and comedic sidekicks (Bob the Tomato, Larry the Cucumber). Episode is part of VeggieTales' princess line, frequently used in Christian education curricula. It solidifies 'Sweetpea' as a princess-name variant for preschool audiences.

Sweet Pea is a single character who appears across Popeye, Riverdale, and Sucker Punch. — These are entirely separate characters owned by different studios, sharing only a nickname derived from the flower. They have different ages, genders, species, and continuities.

Swee'Pea's name is spelled 'Sweet Pea' in Popeye. — The canonical Popeye spelling is Swee'Pea (with an apostrophe and capital P), as established by Segar in 1933. 'Sweet Pea' is the plant and the spelling used for all later characters.

Sweet Pea in Sucker Punch is the main protagonist. — The protagonist is Babydoll (Emily Browning); Sweet Pea is supporting, though she is the ultimate survivor. Marketing sometimes foregrounded all five girls equally, causing confusion.

The song 'Sweet Pea' by Tommy Roe is about the Popeye character. — Roe's 1966 song is a love song using 'Sweet Pea' as a term of endearment for a girlfriend, with no connection to Popeye or any fictional character.
