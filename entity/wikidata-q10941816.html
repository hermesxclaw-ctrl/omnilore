Who It Is

In the Warring States courts, power was not declared — it was sheathed. To hold the hilt of Tai'e was to hold the state itself, and to hand that hilt to another man was to hand him your throat. The sword never needed to be drawn to kill.

As an object, Tai'e has no personality, but texts anthropomorphize its 'behavior': it 'recognizes its lord' (識主) — only a true king can wield it without harm; in the hands of an unworthy minister it turns against the holder (Han Feizi moral). Liezi attributes to it a paradoxical nature: it does not compete to display sharpness, yet nothing withstands it — a Daoist ideal of hidden efficacy (藏鋒). Name & Tongue

Etymology — Chinese 太阿 (also written 泰阿), Pinyin Tài'ē. 太/泰 (tài) = 'great, supreme, vast'; interchangeable in early texts. 阿 (ē) = 'mound, hill, slope' and by extension 'to lean on, support, pillar, to flatter/protect; that which is relied upon.' The compound thus means 'Great Support' or 'Great Hill/Pillar' —

Powers
Supreme Sharpness — Cuts Jade Like Mud

Attested ability to cut through jade, metal, and armor as if cutting mud, despite appearing blunt. Liezi: 'Tai'e appears to lack an edge, yet it severs jade.' Yuejue Shu: it 'splits light and cleaves the wind.' This is legendary hyperbole for high-tin bronze differential hardening, not a magical power in early texts.

Limit: Only effective in hands of one who understands its inverted edge; to the unskilled it seems useless. Cannot be evaluated by common standards of sharpness. In Han Feizi, its power is political, not martial — it harms the ruler if misused.

Sovereign Authority — Symbol of Kingship and Mandate

Possession of Tai'e legitimizes rule; its display inspires submission without fighting. In Yuejue Shu, King Zhao of Chu's possession of Tai'e makes neighboring states fear Chu. Han Feizi makes it the embodiment of the ruler's exclusive power to reward and punish.

Limit: Power is contingent on legitimate ownership and proper ritual custody; if the hilt is handed to a minister, the sword's authority inverts and destroys the ruler. It does not confer military victory by itself — Chu still falls to Qin in history.

Numinous Responsiveness — Humming, Self-Unsheathing, Choosing Its Master

Later zhiguai and wuxia tradition: Tai'e hums (鳴), trembles, or flies from scabbard to warn of treachery or to return to its true lord. It refuses to be drawn by the unworthy.

Limit: Adaptation-specific; not considered historical. In games, often requires cultivation level or blood-binding.

Inverted Edge and Hidden Pattern (倒韜, 逆理)

Technical-mystical trait: the blade's patterning and edge geometry are opposite to common swords — the 'edge' is inside, the spine is sharp. Only a sage can discern it; fools think it dull. Represents Daoist 'great skill appears clumsy.'

Limit: Requires connoisseurship (相劍術); ordinary appraisal fails. Not a combat limitation but an epistemic one.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No contemporary illustration or surviving object is identified as Tai'e; appearance is reconstructed from textual epithets. Yuejue Shu and Wu Yue Chunqiu describe Tai'e as one of three swords cast by Ou Yezi at Chijin Mountain (赤堇山) using tin from Chijin and copper from Ruoye Stream (若耶溪). Liezi and later compendia describe its blade pattern as 'like flowing water' (如流水) or 'like frost and snow' and state its edge is 'inverted' (倒韜/逆理) — seemingl

Symbols — Core symbol is legitimate kingship and the indivisibility of sovereign power. In Han Feizi, the hilt (柄, bing) =柄权, the handles of reward and punishment; the blade = the state. To 'hand the hilt of Tai'e to another' (授人以柄) is the canonical metaphor for delegating coercive authority and courting usurpation. Domains — Textual habitat: the Wu-Yue frontier (modern Zhejiang) — forges at Chijin Mountain (south of modern Shaoxing), Ruoye Stream; then treasuries of Yue and later Chu. Political habitat: the ruler's private chamber and ancestral temple, not the battlefield — Tai'e is a regalia sword (禮劍/威劍), displayed to awe vassals, not a field weapon. Accounts I
The Casting of the Three Swords at Chijin MountainYuejue Shu, Chapter 11 'Ji Bao Jian'; parallel in Wu Yue Chunqiu, Chapter 4

Legend set in Spring and Autumn period, c. 496-465 BCE (reign of King Goujian of Yue); text compiled c. 80-122 CE

King Goujian of Yue, humiliated by Wu, seeks divine weapons to restore his state. He summons the master smith Ou Yezi (欧冶子) — in Yuejue Shu alone, in Wu Yue Chunqiu together with Gan Jiang (干将) — to Chijin Mountain (赤堇山). Using tin from Chijin and copper from Ruoye Stream (若耶溪), and quenching with its waters, Ou Yezi forges three precious swords over three years: Longyuan (龍淵), Tai'e (泰阿), and Gongbu (工布). Each is graded: Longyuan is 'sincere and brave,' Tai'e is 'majestic and awe-inspiring' (威), Gongbu is 'divine and artful.' Tai'e's inscription and pattern are like flowing water. Goujian treasures them but later, as Yue declines, the swords pass to Chu. Establishes Tai'e's origin as a Yue state treasure and explains its later presence in Chu's treasury; creates the canonical trio Longyuan-Tai'e-Gongbu that dominates later 'Ten Famous Swords' lists. Provides etiology for Yue's famed metallurgy, corroborated archaeologically by Wu-Yue bronzes.

King Zhao of Chu Refuses to Display Tai'eYuejue Shu, Chapter 11; cited in Taiping Yulan, juan 343

Legend set c. 515-489 BCE (King Zhao of Chu); text Eastern Han

After Yue's swords enter Chu, King Zhao of Chu (楚昭王) possesses Tai'e and keeps it in his treasury. Envoys from Jin (晉) hear of its fame and request to view it. The king's minister Feng Huzi (風胡子), a famed sword appraiser, advises that Tai'e is a 'treasure of the state' (國之寶) and must not be shown lightly, lest others covet it and bring war. King Zhao follows the advice and declines, saying the sword's awe (威) is for the altars of state, not for entertainment. The Jin envoys leave in awe, and Chu's prestige rises without drawing the blade. Illustrates the Legalist-ritual principle that regalia swords are instruments of deterrence, not display; reinforces Tai'e as symbol of state sovereignty rather than personal weapon. Later cited as precedent for 'not showing the state's sharp tools.'

Handing the Hilt to Another — The Han Feizi ParableHan Feizi, Chapter 33 'Shuo Nan' (and elaborated in Chapter 7 'Er Bing')

Warring States, c. 233 BCE

Han Fei tells the ruler: 'In antiquity, the sword Tai'e was held with its hilt toward oneself and its blade toward others; if one hands the hilt to another and grasps the blade oneself, one will be wounded.' He analogizes: the hilt = the two handles of power (reward and punishment, 刑德二柄). A ruler who delegates these to ministers 'hands the hilt of Tai'e to them' and will be murdered or controlled, just as King Zhuang of Chu or Duke Jian of Qi were. The anecdote gives birth to the idiom shou ren yi bing (授人以柄, to hand the hilt to another — to give leverage to an adversary). No forging story is needed; Tai'e's fame is assumed. Makes Tai'e the most quoted sword in Chinese political thought; the idiom enters standard Classical Chinese and remains in modern Mandarin (授人以柄/授人以把柄). Establishes Tai'e as metaphor for indivisible sovereignty, influencing all later imperial discourse on autocracy.

Accounts II
The Inverted Edge — Liezi's Paradox of the Sage's SwordLiezi, Chapter 'Shuo Fu' (說符) and 'Li Ming' (力命); variant in Zhuangzi 'Shuo Jian'

Material Warring States, compilation 3rd-4th century CE

Liezi describes three grades of swords. The common sword is shiny and seems sharp but breaks on jade. The fine sword cuts jade but is still visible. Tai'e is supreme: 'Its edge is inverted, its pattern is against the grain; it seems blunt, yet when swung it severs jade like mud, cuts metal like wood.' Only a sage who 'knows swords' (善相劍者) can recognize it; fools mock it as dull. The passage uses Tai'e to illustrate the Daoist principle that 'great perfection seems flawed' (大巧若拙) and that true power is hidden. Transforms Tai'e from political symbol into epistemological symbol — the hidden virtue of the Daoist sage. Influences later sword appraisal literature (相劍經) and Tang-Song poetry where Tai'e stands for unrecognized talent.

Qin Shi Huang's Sword — The Imperial RetrojectionTang poetry (Li Bai, Du Fu), Song encyclopedias (Taiping Yulan, Gujin Tushu Jicheng), Ming folklore and modern popular history; no Qin contemporary source

Legend retrojected to Qin dynasty 221-210 BCE; texts Tang (618-907) to Ming (1368-1644)

By the Tang, poets begin calling the First Emperor's sword Tai'e. Li Bai writes 'Qin king's sword, Tai'e, once illuminated the palace' (秦王掃六合，虎視何雄哉，劍舞若太阿). Later anecdotal histories claim that after conquering Chu, Qin Shi Huang seized Tai'e from Chu's treasury and wore it as his personal佩剑, and that it was buried with him in the Terracotta Army mausoleum at Mount Li. The sword thus becomes the ultimate symbol of unification and autocracy — 'Tai'e in hand, all under Heaven submits.' Modern tourism and TV dramas repeat this as fact, showing Qin Shi Huang drawing a long steel Tai'e against assassins. Makes Tai'e the most famous 'Qin sword' in popular imagination, despite no mention in Sima Qian's Shiji (c. 94 BCE) which details Qin regalia without naming Tai'e. Drives modern cultural production (film, games) and the persistent internet claim that the Terracotta Army pit contains Tai'e. Archaeologically, no sword inscribed Tai'e has been found in the Qin mausoleum (which remains largely unexcavated).

The Sword That Cannot Be Appraised — Feng Huzi's ConnoisseurshipWu Yue Chunqiu, Chapter 4; Yuejue Shu, Chapter 11; Feng Huzi Liezhuan fragment

Eastern Han compilation of Spring and Autumn lore

Feng Huzi (風胡子), the legendary sword judge from Wu, is summoned to appraise Ou Yezi's new swords. He examines Longyuan and Gongbu easily, but when Tai'e is presented, he steps back and says: 'This sword's principle is inverted; its glory is hidden. It cannot be judged by ordinary standards. It is a sword for a hegemon-king, not for a common warrior.' He declares Tai'e's value is not in cutting but in its awe (威). The king therefore stores it in the temple rather than awarding it to a general, preserving its ritual power. Establishes the tradition of sword appraisal (相劍) and the idea that Tai'e is a 'king's sword' (王劍) versus a 'warrior's sword.' Influences later connoisseurship manuals and the wuxia trope of the sword that chooses its master.

Tai'e is the Sword of Goujian excavated in 1965 and displayed in Hubei Provincial Museum. — The excavated sword is inscribed '越王勾践自作用剑' and is a historical 5th-century BCE bronze jian. No inscription names it Tai'e. Tai'e is a legendary name from texts compiled 400-600 years later; no excavated sword has been securely identified as Tai'e.

Tai'e was made of steel and was unbreakable. — Spring and Autumn Yue swords were high-tin bronze, not steel. 'Cuts jade like mud' is legendary hyperbole. Indestructibility is a wuxia/game accretion.

Tai'e required human sacrifice to be completed. — No Warring States or Eastern Han text says Tai'e required human sacrifice. The motif belongs to Gan Jiang/Moye and is merged with Tai'e in later compendia.
