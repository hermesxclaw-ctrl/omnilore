Archive Dossier — Identity

Minokawa

Pronunciation mee-NOH-kah-wah

Known As

The Eclipse BirdMinokawa of the Sky-Hole

Etymology

The name 'Minokawa' is of Bagobo origin. The exact linguistic derivation is uncertain, but it may relate to 'minok' (chicken) or 'kaw' (a term for a large bird), though these are speculative. In Bagobo folklore, the name is used solely for this specific cosmic bird.

Cultural Ownership

The Minokawa is a creature from the mythology of the Bagobo people, an indigenous group in Mindanao, Philippines. It is also recognized in broader Philippine folklore, particularly in regions influenced by Bagobo culture.

Disputed Identifications

Some sources conflate the Minokawa with the Bakunawa, a serpent-like dragon in other Philippine mythologies. However, these are distinct entities: the Minokawa is a bird, while the Bakunawa is a serpent. The Minokawa is also sometimes compared to the Garuda of Hindu-Buddhist mythology, but there is no direct evidence of cultural transmission; similarities may be coincidental or due to shared regional motifs.

Archive Dossier — Approach

In the darkness before an eclipse, the Bagobo people of Mindanao do not look to the sky for a celestial alignment—they listen for the beating of immense wings and the creak of a steel beak. For they know that the Minokawa, a bird as vast as an island, is trying once more to swallow the Moon, and only the clamor of human voices can force it to disgorge its prey.

The minokawa is the eclipse made animal for the Bagobo people of Mindanao: a bird the size of an island — the ethnographer Laura Benedict recorded it precisely as large as the island of Negros or Bohol — with a beak and talons of steel, eyes of mirror, and feathers like sword-blades, living in the space beyond the eastern horizon where the sky meets the sea. Each day it waits there, and each month it hunts: the moon, crossing the sky through her many doors, is the minokawa's prey, and a lunar eclipse is the hunt succeeding — the great bird has caught and swallowed her.

Who It Is

The minokawa is the eclipse made animal for the Bagobo people of Mindanao: a bird the size of an island — the ethnographer Laura Benedict recorded it precisely as large as the island of Negros or Bohol — with a beak and talons of steel, eyes of mirror, and feathers like sword-blades, living in the space beyond the eastern horizon where the sky meets the sea. Each day it waits there, and each month it hunts: the moon, crossing the sky through her many doors, is the minokawa's prey, and a lunar eclipse is the hunt succeeding — the great bird has caught and swallowed her. The Bagobo response was participatory astronomy at its finest: when the moon began to vanish, the whole community erupted — shouting, beating gongs, pounding rice mortars, screaming at the sky — until the minokawa, startled by the noise of the world below, opened its beak to look down... and the moon slipped out and sailed on. The noise always worked. The moon always escaped. The tradition even carries its own doomsday clause: should the minokawa ever swallow the moon and keep her, it will come down and devour the earth next — meaning every gong beaten at every eclipse was, quite literally, planetary defense. The minokawa is the archive's perfect closing entry: a monster the size of an island, defeated every single time by ordinary people, together, making noise.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

The Minokawa is described as a colossal bird, as large as an island—specifically compared to the islands of Negros or Bohol. Its feathers are sharp swords, its eyes are mirrors, and its beak and claws are made of steel. It is a dragon-like bird, though no specific mention of limbs or tail is made in the sources. The creature's size and metallic parts emphasize its otherworldly and fearsome nature.

Symbols & Iconography

In Bagobo culture, the Minokawa symbolizes the forces of chaos and cosmic danger. Its metallic features (steel, mirrors, swords) may represent invulnerability and sharpness, while its association with eclipses links it to celestial order and disruption. The bird is not typically depicted in art, but its imagery is invoked in rituals during eclipses.

Behaviour & Personality

The Minokawa is a predator of celestial bodies, specifically the Moon and Sun. It is relentless in its pursuit, attempting to swallow the Moon daily. It is also curious and distracted by noise, as it opens its mouth to look down when it hears loud sounds, allowing the Moon to escape. This suggests a combination of cunning and folly.

Domains & Habitats

The Minokawa lives 'outside the sky, at the eastern horizon.' It is also said to lurk under the earth after an unsuccessful attempt. The Moon has eight holes in the eastern horizon to exit and eight in the western horizon to enter, to avoid the bird. This indicates a cosmological geography where the sky is a dome with openings.

Version Differences

Benedict's account describes the Minokawa as living at the eastern horizon, while some Visayan traditions mention a cave called 'calulundan' above the sky, but this is associated with the Baua, not the Minokawa. There may be regional variations in the bird's residence and behavior, but the core eclipse myth is consistent. In modern popular culture, the Minokawa is depicted as a creature that can be controlled or killed, which is a departure from its mythological role.

Origins

Bagobo tradition of Mindanao, recorded in Laura Watson Benedict's early twentieth-century ethnography; kin to the pan-Philippine bakunawa (the moon-eating serpent-dragon of the Visayas) — two solutions, one archipelago, to the same vanishing moon.

Archive Dossier — Powers

Cosmic Devouring

Source Version Bagobo mythology

Description

The Minokawa can swallow or cover the Sun and Moon, causing eclipses. It is also feared that it could swallow the Earth and all humans if it succeeded in swallowing both celestial bodies.

Limitations

The Minokawa can be thwarted by loud noises, which cause it to open its mouth and release its prey. It also appears to be unable to catch the Moon if the Moon uses the eight holes in the horizon.

Contradictions

None noted in traditional sources.

Immense Size and Strength

Source Version Bagobo mythology

Description

The bird is as large as an island, with steel beak and claws, and feathers like swords, indicating immense physical power.

Limitations

Despite its size, it is easily distracted by noise.

Contradictions

None noted.

Form

A raptor vast as an island: steel beak and claws, mirror eyes that flash like sun on tide-pools, blade feathers that ring when it banks — visible to humans only as the shadow that crosses the moon.

Powers

Moon-swallowing (the lunar eclipse, on schedule), island-scale flight at the sky's edge, and the standing threat of descending on the earth itself should the moon ever fail to escape.

Weaknesses & Wards

Noise. Gongs, mortars, and massed human voices startle it into opening its beak — the single most democratic monster-countermeasure in this archive: everyone participates, and it has never once failed.

Archive Dossier — Stories

The Minokawa and the Moon

Source Bagobo oral tradition, recorded by Laura Watson BenedictCulture Bagobo (Philippines)Period Pre-colonial, recorded in early 20th century

Summary

Before time began, the Minokawa swallowed the Moon. The people screamed and made noise, causing the bird to open its mouth, and the Moon escaped. The Minokawa continues to try to swallow the Moon, and during eclipses, the Bagobo people make noise to force it to release the Moon. If it were to swallow both the Sun and Moon, it would descend and eat all humans.

Participants

Minokawa

Moon

Bagobo people

Consequences

The eclipse myth explains the origin of lunar eclipses and the Bagobo practice of making noise during eclipses.

Variants

In some tellings, the Minokawa also swallows the Sun, but the focus is usually on the Moon.

Archive Dossier — Archivist

Known Gaps

The original Bagobo language texts are not directly available; reliance on English translations. No archaeological or iconographic evidence has been found. The exact etymology of 'Minokawa' is uncertain. The extent of the Minokawa's role in other Philippine cultures is not fully documented. Modern adaptations may have significantly altered the original myth.

Research Scope

This research focuses on the Minokawa as documented in Philippine mythology, particularly within Bagobo (Manobo) traditions. It covers primary ethnographic sources, comparative mythology, and modern popular culture appearances. The research cutoff date is 2026-08-01. Due to the oral nature of the tradition and limited written records, some aspects remain uncertain and are flagged as such.

Cultural Significance

Eclipse gong-beating was living practice across Mindanao and echoes the bakunawa rites of the Visayas; the minokawa turns celestial mechanics into communal duty — the sky kept safe by the whole village, loudly.
