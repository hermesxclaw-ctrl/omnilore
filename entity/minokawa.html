Who It Is

In the darkness before an eclipse, the Bagobo people of Mindanao do not look to the sky for a celestial alignment—they listen for the beating of immense wings and the creak of a steel beak. For they know that the Minokawa, a bird as vast as an island, is trying once more to swallow the Moon, and only the clamor of human voices can force it to disgorge its prey.

The Minokawa is a predator of celestial bodies, specifically the Moon and Sun. It is relentless in its pursuit, attempting to swallow the Moon daily. It is also curious and distracted by noise, as it opens its mouth to look down when it hears loud sounds, allowing the Moon to escape. This suggests a combination of cunning and folly.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Minokawa' is of Bagobo origin. The exact linguistic derivation is uncertain, but it may relate to 'minok' (chicken) or 'kaw' (a term for a large bird), though these are speculative. In Bagobo folklore, the name is used solely for this specific cosmic bird.

Pronunciation — mee-NOH-kah-wah

Aliases — The Eclipse Bird, Minokawa of the Sky-Hole

Powers
Cosmic Devouring

The Minokawa can swallow or cover the Sun and Moon, causing eclipses. It is also feared that it could swallow the Earth and all humans if it succeeded in swallowing both celestial bodies.

Limit: The Minokawa can be thwarted by loud noises, which cause it to open its mouth and release its prey. It also appears to be unable to catch the Moon if the Moon uses the eight holes in the horizon.

Immense Size and Strength

The bird is as large as an island, with steel beak and claws, and feathers like swords, indicating immense physical power.

Limit: Despite its size, it is easily distracted by noise.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The Minokawa is described as a colossal bird, as large as an island—specifically compared to the islands of Negros or Bohol. Its feathers are sharp swords, its eyes are mirrors, and its beak and claws are made of steel. It is a dragon-like bird, though no specific mention of limbs or tail is made in the sources. The creature's size and metallic parts emphasize its otherworldly and fearsome nature.

Symbols — In Bagobo culture, the Minokawa symbolizes the forces of chaos and cosmic danger. Its metallic features (steel, mirrors, swords) may represent invulnerability and sharpness, while its association with eclipses links it to celestial order and disruption. The bird is not typically depicted in art, but its imagery is invoked in rituals during eclipses.

Domains — The Minokawa lives 'outside the sky, at the eastern horizon.' It is also said to lurk under the earth after an unsuccessful attempt. The Moon has eight holes in the eastern horizon to exit and eight in the western horizon to enter, to avoid the bird. This indicates a cosmological geography where the sky is a dome with openings.

Accounts I
The Minokawa and the MoonBagobo oral tradition, recorded by Laura Watson Benedict

Pre-colonial, recorded in early 20th century

Before time began, the Minokawa swallowed the Moon. The people screamed and made noise, causing the bird to open its mouth, and the Moon escaped. The Minokawa continues to try to swallow the Moon, and during eclipses, the Bagobo people make noise to force it to release the Moon. If it were to swallow both the Sun and Moon, it would descend and eat all humans. The eclipse myth explains the origin of lunar eclipses and the Bagobo practice of making noise during eclipses.
