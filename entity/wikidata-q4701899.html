Form

No specific, canonical description of Aktzin's appearance survives in the sources. Given his identification with the water/rain complex, he would likely be depicted in the iconographic tradition of Tlaloc and the Tlaloque: a figure with goggle-like eyes (often made of serpents), fangs, and a headdress of heron feathers. His body would likely be painted black, the color of rain-bearing storm clouds. He might be shown holding a lightning axe (a copper or stone axe) and a rattle staff, or pouring water from a vessel. As a lesser deity, he might be depicted as smaller than Tlaloc or as one of a group of identical figures, differentiated only by color or specific attributes.

Powers

Rain and Storm Control Aktzin, as a water deity, would have the power to summon, withhold, or direct rain, storms, and other atmospheric phenomena. Attested for the broader Tlaloc complex; inferred for Aktzin. His power would be subject to the will of higher gods, such as Tlaloc or the supreme creator couple Ometeotl. He would be one of many rain gods working under Tlaloc's command. None specific to this power.

Agricultural Fertility The ability to make crops grow, ensuring bountiful harvests, or to cause famine and blight through drought or excessive rain. Attested for the broader Tlaloc complex; inferred for Aktzin. His power was dependent on proper ritual observance by humans. None specific to this power.

Lightning and Thunder The power to wield lightning as a weapon or tool, often depicted as a symbolic axe. Attested for Tlaloc and the Tlaloque. This power was often seen as a punitive or destructive aspect of the rain gods. None specific to this power.

Behavior & Habitat

As a divine entity of water, Aktzin's behavior would be understood as the cause of natural phenomena. He would be seen as bringing the life-giving rains that nourish crops, but also the destructive storms, hail, and lightning that could ruin harvests. His actions were not arbitrary but were responses to human behavior, particularly the offerings and rituals performed by priests and commoners. He would be a dispenser of both blessing and punishment, a typical duality in Aztec water gods.

Accounts

The gods gathered at Teotihuacan to create the current era (the Fifth Sun). The humble god Nanahuatzin threw himself into a sacred fire to become the Sun, while the wealthy god Tecciztecatl hesitated and became the Moon. The gods then had to sacrifice themselves to set the new Sun in motion. Aktzin is listed in some versions of this story as one of the gods who participated in this sacrifice or in the subsequent creation of people.

This text describes the creation of the world by the four Tezcatlipocas (Black, White, Red, and Blue). They created the fire, the sun, the earth, and eventually people. The text lists various gods who were created in these early stages. Aktzin may be listed among these early creations, though the text is fragmentary and the identification is uncertain.

Scholarly Debates
Independent Deity vs. Manifestation of Tlaloc
Aktzin is a distinct, independent deity with his own identity and cult. ⇌ Aktzin is an epithet or localized form of Tlaloc, not a separate being.
Evidence for A: The Anales de Cuauhtitlan lists Aktzin as a separate name among the gods.
Evidence for B: Most other sources do not mention Aktzin at all, and the name fits the pattern of Tlaloc's many epithets.
Nature of the Deity
Aktzin was purely a rain/water deity. ⇌ Aktzin was a deity of a specific body of water, such as a particular lake or spring, with a more localized domain.
Evidence for A: The name's etymology points to a general water connection.
Evidence for B: Many Aztec minor deities were connected to specific geographical features.
What We Don't Know

The most significant gap is the lack of primary sources that explicitly and unambiguously describe Aktzin. Most of our knowledge is inferred from the broader context of Aztec water deities. Specific gaps include: (1) No surviving depiction of Aktzin has been identified with certainty. (2) No specific myths or rituals dedicated solely to Aktzin are documented. (3) The exact nature of his relationship to Tlaloc (independent vs. subordinate) is unclear. (4) The geographical extent of his worship is unknown. (5) His role in the Aztec calendar is not documented.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of the Aztec pantheon as a massive corporation. Tlaloc is the CEO, the famous face. Chalchiuhtlicue is the CFO, managing the liquid assets. The Tlaloque are the middle managers, each in charge of a specific region. And Aktzin? Aktzin is the field technician — the one who actually shows up at your local reservoir to turn the valve. He's not on the org chart in the annual report, but he's essential to the day-to-day operations. He's the god you pray to when you're a farmer on the edge of a drought, hoping the local guy has the power to get the water flowing.
