Who It Is

Imagine a mother so powerful that she wears a skirt of live serpents and a necklace of human hearts—and yet she is the giver of all life. That is Coatlicue, the Aztec earth goddess whose own son burst from her womb fully armed to slay his siblings. She is both the womb and the tomb, the terrifying mother who creates and devours the cosmos.

Coatlicue is the earth mother who gives birth to the gods, the sun, and the moon. She is both nurturing and devouring: she sustains life but also consumes the dead. She is associated with fertility, childbirth, and warfare. She is depicted as a passive figure in the myth of Huitzilopochtli's birth, but her power is immense—she is the source of all life and death.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Classical Nahuatl 'cōātl' (serpent) + 'īcue' (her skirt), literally 'Serpent Skirt'. Her name is often interpreted as 'She of the Serpent Skirt' or 'The One with the Skirt of Serpents'.

Pronunciation — koh-aht-LEE-kweh (Classical Nahuatl: [koːaːˈtɬiːkʷe])

Aliases — Teteoh Inan, Tonantzin, Cihuacoatl, Ilamatecuhtli, Coatl Icue, Toci

Ownership — Aztec (Mexica) culture, central Mexico, post-classic period (c. 14th–16th centuries CE)

Form & Signs

Appearance — The monumental Coatlicue statue (2.5 m tall) depicts a woman with a skirt woven from live rattlesnakes, a necklace of human hearts, hands, and a skull pendant, and clawed feet. Her head is formed by two serpent heads facing each other, a fountain of blood gushing from her decapitated neck. She has sagging breasts, indicating she is a mother past childbearing age, and her skirt is held by a belt with a skull buckle. She is often shown with serpent

Symbols — Serpents (skirt, head, blood), human hearts, hands, skulls, claws, earth, caves, mountains, the moon (as mother of Coyolxauhqui), the sun (as mother of Huitzilopochtli), and the stars (as mother of the Centzon Huitznahua).

Domains — Earth, fertility, childbirth, death, cosmic renewal, warfare (as the mother of the war god), and the cycle of life and death. She is also associated with the stars, the moon, and the sun.

Accounts I
The Birth of HuitzilopochtliLeyenda de los Soles, Florentine Codex, Códice Chimalpopoca

Post-classic (c. 14th-16th century CE)

Coatlicue was sweeping a temple on Coatepec (Serpent Mountain) when a ball of feathers (or a feather) fell from the sky and impregnated her. Her daughter Coyolxauhqui and her sons the Centzon Huitznahua (Four Hundred Southerners) were enraged and plotted to kill her. When they attacked, Huitzilopochtli burst from her womb fully armed, slew his sister, and scattered his brothers. He threw Coyolxauhqui's head into the sky to become the moon, and her body was dismembered and thrown down the mountain. Huitzilopochtli became the patron god of the Mexica, and the myth justified their warfare and human sacrifice. The dismemberment of Coyolxauhqui was reenacted in ritual sacrifices at the Templo Mayor.

Accounts II
Coatlicue as the Mother of All GodsFlorentine Codex, Historia general

Post-classic

Coatlicue is described as the mother of all gods, the earth from which everything is born. She is also associated with the creation of the world and the sun. She is venerated as the ultimate creator goddess.

Coatlicue and the SunLeyenda de los Soles

Post-classic

In some accounts, Coatlicue is the mother of the sun (Huitzilopochtli) and the moon (Coyolxauhqui), and her children's struggle represents the daily battle between day and night. This myth explains the celestial movements.
