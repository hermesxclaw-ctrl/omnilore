My skirt is serpents. My necklace is hearts. My head is twin floods of blood — the life that pours from the wound. I am the earth. Walk on me, be born from me, and when you die, I will swallow you whole.

Who It Is

Coatlicue is the Aztec earth mother goddess — simultaneously the womb from which all life emerges and the tomb to which all life returns. Her name ('She of the Serpent Skirt') and her iconography are among the most powerful and terrifying in world art. She represents the fundamental Aztec understanding of the cosmos as a cycle of birth, death, and rebirth — the mother who gives life to her children and devours them when they die, only to give birth again.

The most famous representation of Coatlicue is the colossal stone statue discovered in 1790 beneath the Zócalo (main plaza) of Mexico City — now one of the crown jewels of the National Museum of Anthropology. The statue depicts Coatlicue as a monumental female figure, her head replaced by two facing serpents (representing streams of blood spurting from a decapitated neck — life born from death). She wears a skirt of intertwined rattlesnakes, a necklace of human hearts, hands, and a skull pendant. Her hands and feet are clawed for tearing flesh. From her body hang symbols of both life (breasts, indicating she has nursed the gods) and death (skulls, serpents, claws). She is not beautiful by human standards — she is awesome, terrifying, and sublime.

Coatlicue's most famous myth concerns the birth of Huitzilopochtli. As the temple sweeper at Coatepec (Serpent Mountain), she was impregnated by a ball of feathers that fell from the sky. Her 400 children (the Centzon Huitznahua) and her daughter Coyolxauhqui were outraged by this miraculous pregnancy and plotted to kill their mother. But at the moment of attack, Huitzilopochtli burst from Coatlicue's womb fully armed and defended her, dismembering Coyolxauhqui and scattering her body down the mountain. This myth is the cosmic narrative enacted daily: the earth (Coatlicue) gives birth to the sun (Huitzilopochtli), who defeats the moon and stars, and the cycle continues.

Coatlicue is also the goddess of the earth as a devouring entity. In Aztec thought, the earth surface is Coatlicue's back; we walk upon her body. When we die, we are returned to her — buried in the earth, consumed by the mother from whom we came. Her statue was reportedly discovered with offerings of green stone beads placed in its mouth, 'feeding' the goddess. This dual aspect — the nurturing mother and the devouring monster — reflects the Aztec understanding that creation and destruction are a single process: the mother who gives life is the same mother who reclaims it.

The Coatlicue statue itself has a dramatic history. After its discovery in 1790, it was quickly reburied because its terrifying appearance disturbed both the colonial authorities and the Indigenous population (who reportedly began leaving offerings at the site). It was excavated again, buried again, and eventually installed at the university — where it was covered with a cloth because students found it too frightening. Only in the 20th century did it receive its proper place as a masterpiece of world sculpture. The Mexican writer Octavio Paz described Coatlicue as representing 'the petrified horror of the Aztec vision of the cosmos' — and yet also its profound philosophical depth.

Form

The monumental Coatlicue statue (2.5 m tall) depicts a woman with a skirt woven from live rattlesnakes, a necklace of human hearts, hands, and a skull pendant, and clawed feet. Her head is formed by two serpent heads facing each other, a fountain of blood gushing from her decapitated neck. She has sagging breasts, indicating she is a mother past childbearing age, and her skirt is held by a belt with a skull buckle. She is often shown with serpent imagery, earth symbols, and death motifs.

Behavior & Habitat

Coatlicue is the earth mother who gives birth to the gods, the sun, and the moon. She is both nurturing and devouring: she sustains life but also consumes the dead. She is associated with fertility, childbirth, and warfare. She is depicted as a passive figure in the myth of Huitzilopochtli's birth, but her power is immense—she is the source of all life and death.

Accounts

Coatlicue was sweeping a temple on Coatepec (Serpent Mountain) when a ball of feathers (or a feather) fell from the sky and impregnated her. Her daughter Coyolxauhqui and her sons the Centzon Huitznahua (Four Hundred Southerners) were enraged and plotted to kill her. When they attacked, Huitzilopochtli burst from her womb fully armed, slew his sister, and scattered his brothers. He threw Coyolxauhqui's head into the sky to become the moon, and her body was dismembered and thrown down the mountain.

Coatlicue is described as the mother of all gods, the earth from which everything is born. She is also associated with the creation of the world and the sun.

In some accounts, Coatlicue is the mother of the sun (Huitzilopochtli) and the moon (Coyolxauhqui), and her children's struggle represents the daily battle between day and night.

Scholarly Debates
Identification with Tonantzin
Coatlicue is Tonantzin, the earth mother. ⇌ They are separate deities.
Evidence for A: Colonial chroniclers often conflated them.
Evidence for B: Most Nahua sources distinguish them.
Mother of all gods vs. specific offspring
She is the mother of all gods. ⇌ She is specifically the mother of Huitzilopochtli, Coyolxauhqui, and the Centzon Huitznahua.
Evidence for A: Some texts call her 'Mother of the Gods'.
Evidence for B: The birth myth only mentions these children.
Role as devourer
She is a benevolent earth mother. ⇌ She is a terrifying devourer of the dead.
Evidence for A: Her role as fertility goddess.
Evidence for B: Her iconography includes hearts and skulls.
What We Don't Know

Further research needed on the full ritual significance of the Coatlicue statue and its relation to the Templo Mayor. Also, more sources on pre-Columbian oral traditions are needed. The exact meaning of the feather impregnation is debated.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Coatlicue as the ultimate cosmic mother—imagine if Mother Earth had a dark side, wearing a serpent skirt and a heart necklace, giving birth to the sun and the moon, and then devouring the dead to recycle them back into life. She's the original 'terrifying mother' archetype, like a divine version of both a nurturing womb and a black hole.
