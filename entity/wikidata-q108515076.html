Who It Is

Say Duval aloud — in French it is a breath of valley air, *du val*, 'of the valley' — and you have summoned a name that rode with the Normans into England, was inked into the Domesday Book, galloped on the heath with a lace-cuffed highwayman, governed a raw American frontier, chained itself to prison gates with suffragettes, and now walks the neon streets of Vice City as Grand Theft Auto's next anti-hero.

As a surname it has no inherent personality, but onomastic behavior is consistent: habitational, initially marking a migrant ('the man from the valley / from Déville') then becoming hereditary by c. 1300-1400 in France and England. Transmission is patrilineal in civil law, with matrilineal retention in Quebec (Barsalou-Duval hyphenates). Social role historically artisanal, clerical, and minor gentry in Normandy; in England, Norman tenant class; in North America, planter, military, and later professional classes. Name & Tongue

Etymology — French toponymic surname from Old French *du val* < Latin *vallis* 'valley' — literally 'of the valley'. In its Norman form specifically linked by onomasts to the town of Déville (historically Deville) in present-day Ardennes / Seine-Maritime, Normandy. The preposition *du* is the contraction of *de

Pronunciation — French: [dy.val] (doo-VAHL), with nasalized /y/ and silent final l in some regional speech as [dy.va]; English anglicized: /duːˈvɑːl/ doo-VAHL or /ˈdjuːvəl/ JOO-vəl for Duvall variant; American variants also /duˈvɔːl/. Stress on second syllable in French, variable in English.

Aliases — DuVal, Duvall, Du Vall, Deval, Devall, DeVall

Ownership — French and Norman origin, now pan-Francophone and Anglophone. Densest historically in Normandy, Picardy, Île-de-France, and later in Quebec, Louisiana, Haiti, Mauritius, and French

Powers
Toponymic identification

As a habitational byname, Duval functioned to distinguish migrants by origin — 'the one from the valley / from Déville' — in communities where given names alone were insufficient. This identificatory power underlies all later hereditary use.

Limit: Only functional where the bearer was actually non-local; in the home valley itself the byname was useless and alternative bynames were used. Not a legal title.

Hereditary patronymic transmission

After c. 1350 in France and c. 1400 in England, Duval became a fixed hereditary surname passed patrilineally, conferring family continuity, inheritance rights, and civil identity under French Code civil and English common law.

Limit: Transmission interrupted by name changes, anglicization (Duval → Duvall), and hyphenation; not conferring nobility per se despite some armigerous families.

Heraldic bearing (armigerous branches)

Certain Duval/Devall families obtained or assumed coats of arms, allowing use of heraldic achievement on seals, plate, and monuments as a mark of gentility.

Limit: Arms belong to specific lineages, not to all bearers of the surname; no single 'Duval coat of arms' exists despite commercial heraldry sellers implying otherwise.

Form & Signs

Appearance — As a written form, Duval is five letters, capital D + uval, with common stylized capital V in DuVal among American bearers (Barry E. DuVal, Fred DuVal). In French civil records the particle is lower-case when preceding given name (Charles Duval) but capitalized as surname headword (DUVAL). No fixed heraldic appearance; Norman families bearing Duval/Devall used varied arms. Burke's General Armory records Devall: Argent, a chevron gules between thr

Symbols — Topographic symbolism dominates: valley (val), often rendered heraldically as chevron or mount. Fleur-de-lis appears in anglicized Devall arms signaling Norman-French origin. In popular culture, the name carries connotations of Frenchness, elegance, or Creole heritage (Jeanne Duval as Baudelaire's muse; Louisianan Duval families). In 19th-century caricature, Marie Duval's signature 'M. Duval' played on gender ambiguity. No sin

Domains — Core habitat: northern France (Normandy, Picardy, Île-de-France, Ardennes), with early diffusion to England (London, Hampshire, Yorkshire via Norman estates), then via French colonization to Quebec (17th c.), Louisiana, Haiti, Mauritius, and via English colonization to Maryland/Virginia (Mareen Duvall c. 1650) and Florida. 1881 British census shows 64 Duvals concentrated in southeast England; 1891 French census shows >15,000 Duvals nationally

Accounts I
From the Valley: The Toponymic Birth at DévilleAlbert Dauzat, Dictionnaire étymologique (1951); Marie-Thérèse Morlet, Dictionnaire étymologique des noms de famille (1997); Archives départementales de Seine-Maritime

11th-13th century

In the pays de Caux outside Rouen, the village of Déville (Latin De Villa, later Devilla, 'farmstead in the valley') gives its name to men who leave it. A man called Willelmus de Devilla appears in Norman charters; his sons, moving to neighboring parishes or across the Channel, are called 'du Val' — of the valley — to mark them as outsiders. Over generations the byname sticks, loses its preposition's separability, and becomes Duval. The alternative candidate, Deville in the Ardennes, follows the same pattern on the Meuse frontier. The story is not of a founding ancestor but of a repeated, polygenetic habit: many unrelated valley-dwellers independently become Duvals. Creates a highly polygenetic surname — thousands of unrelated Duval lineages arise independently from different valleys, explaining the name's modern frequency and lack of single family tree.

Across the Channel: Devall in Domesday EnglandDomesday Book (1086); Reaney & Wilson (1991); Oxford Dictionary of Family Names in Britain and Ireland (2016)

1066-1242

After the Norman Conquest, Norman tenants bearing the byname de Eyvill / Devall appear in the Domesday survey and subsequent Pipe Rolls and Curia Regis rolls (de Eyvill 1202, de Eyvill 1242). They hold small fees in Yorkshire and Hampshire. Unlike great baronial names, Devall remains a modest knightly/yeoman name, anglicized in spelling but retaining its French valley memory. By the 17th century a branch, Mareen Duvall (1625-1694), Huguenot-descended, emigrates to Maryland, founding the large American Duvall lineage that later produces the variant spelling Duvall dominant in the US South. Establishes the English/American Duvall/Devall line as a Norman import, separate from later French-Canadian Duval immigration. Explains why American Duvalls often trace to Maryland, while Canadian Duvals trace to New France.

The Gallant Highwayman: Claude Duval (1643-1670)Contemporary pamphlets: 'The Memoirs of Monsieur Du Vall' (1670); William Pope's 'The History of the Lives and Robberies of the Most Noted Highwaymen' (1714); Oxford Dictionary of National Biography entry for Claude Duval

1660-1670

Born in Domfront, Normandy, Claude Duval comes to England as a groom to the Duke of Richmond, then turns highwayman on the heaths around London. Pamphleteers transform him into a chivalric figure: he robs with exaggerated courtesy, allegedly spares a lady who dances a courante with him, and speaks fluent French. Arrested drunk at the Hole-in-the-Wall tavern in Covent Garden, he is hanged at Tyburn on 21 January 1670, aged 27. His body is buried in Covent Garden church, with an epitaph claiming 'Here lies Du Vall'. The legend outlives the man, becoming the archetype of the romantic French highwayman in English ballads, plays, and later comic operas. Fixes Duval in English popular imagination as the courteous criminal; inspires numerous literary and musical adaptations for three centuries and cements the name's association with dashing transgression.

Accounts II
Governor of the Frontier: William Pope Duval (1784-1854) and Florida TerritoryJames M. Denham, 'William Pope Duval: Frontier Lawman' (Florida Historical Quarterly); US Territorial Papers; Letters of William Pope Duval

1822-1834

Kentucky-born lawyer William Pope Duval, veteran of the War of 1812, is appointed by President James Monroe as the first civil governor of Florida Territory after its cession from Spain. He governs from St. Augustine and then Tallahassee — a town he helps select as capital — mediating between Spanish inhabitants, American settlers, and Seminole nations. His correspondence reveals a frontier administration plagued by land fraud, duels, and the looming Second Seminole War. A slaveholder himself, his policies facilitate American settlement and Indigenous removal. His sons Burr H. Duval and John Crittenden Duval and Thomas Howard DuVal extend the family into Texas Revolution martyrdom, Texas literature, and federal judgeship. Establishes Duval County, Florida (Jacksonville) named in his honor — the largest county by area in Florida — and embeds the surname in American geography. His family's Texas branch links Duval to Alamo/Goliad memory.

Militant Bodies: Elsie and Una Duval and the Suffragette Hunger StrikeSuffragette records, Women's Social and Political Union (WSPU) archives; Diane Atkinson, 'Rise Up, Women!' (2018); The National Archives, Kew, HO 144/1235; Elsie Duval hunger strike medal (Museum of London)

1908-1919

Elsie Duval (1892-1919) and her mother Una Duval (1879-1975), née Dugdale, join the WSPU. Elsie, a teenager, participates in window-smashing campaigns, is imprisoned three times in Holloway, hunger-strikes, and is forcibly fed. She is released under the Cat and Mouse Act and goes into hiding. Awarded a hunger strike medal 'for Valour', she later marries and dies young of heart failure attributed to force-feeding trauma. Una continues as a militant, later as a marriage-law reformer. Their actions, documented in police surveillance photos and prison records, exemplify the Duval name's entanglement with bodily protest and state violence against women. Elsie's medal and prison records become key artefacts in British feminist history; her early death is cited in debates on the health consequences of force-feeding. The Duval women illustrate how a French-origin surname became part of British radical heritage.

The Anarchist Burglar: Clément Duval (1850-1935) and the Birth of IllegalismClément Duval, 'Outrage: An Anarchist Memoir of the Penal Colony' (1929, English trans. 2012); Jean Maitron, Dictionnaire biographique du mouvement ouvrier français; prison records of Île du Diable

1886-1935

Invalided Franco-Prussian War veteran Clément Duval, turned anarchist, justifies burglary as 'individual reappropriation' — stealing from the rich. In 1886 he burgles the mansion of socialite Madeleine Lemaire, sets fire to it, and stabs policeman Rossignol. Sentenced to death, commuted to life on Devil's Island (bagne de Cayenne), he survives fourteen escape attempts and is finally liberated in 1901, emigrating to New York where he writes memoirs. His trial popularizes the doctrine of illegalism that later influences the Bonnot Gang. His surname, identical to the valley name, becomes in anarchist press a symbol of the valley's revenge on the mansion on the hill. Makes Duval a byword in French anarchist history; his memoirs remain a primary source on the bagne. The case forces French courts to confront anarchist theories of property.

Is Duval a noble name?Popular genealogy and heraldry sellers present Duval as a noble family with a single coat of arms and imply the particle 'Du' denotes nobility.Academic onomastics and French heraldry classify Duval as a commoner habitational name (roturier); arms belong to specific ennobled branches, not to the surname as a whole, and 'Du' is a simple contraction of de + le, not a nobiliary particle (which would be 'de' or 'du' before a fief name for nobles, but Duval's *val* is topographic, not seigneurial).

Duval means 'of the devil' or is related to 'devil' (Duval = du diable). — Duval is from *val* 'valley' (Latin vallis), not *diable* 'devil'. Confusion arises from English phonetic similarity and from variant spellings like DeVile/Devill which reflect medieval scribal variation of *val*, not *devil*. French *diable* would give Diable, not Duval.

All Duvals/Duvalls are related and share one family crest. — Duval is highly polygenetic — many unrelated families independently adopted the name from different valleys. There is no single Duval crest; dozens of distinct arms exist for different branches, and most bearers are not armigerous at all.

The Duval surname is exclusively French. — While French in origin, Duval/Devall has been an English surname since 1086 via Norman settlement, with continuous English records. The American Duvall branch (Maryland, 1650) is English-colonial, not recent French immigration. Haitian, Mauritian, and Québécois Duvals reflect French colonial diffusion, not metropolitan exclusivity.
