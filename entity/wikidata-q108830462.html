Who It Is

Say 'Ao' aloud - ah-oh - and you have just spoken four different worlds. In Māori, it is the light that breaks after endless night, the world you live in. In ancient China, it is a turtle so vast that islands ride on its back until a goddess saws off its legs to hold up the sky. In a Tokyo street, it is the 'blue' light that means go, even though it shines green. And above the Forgotten Realms, it is Lord Ao, the god who judges gods and never answers prayers. One sound, no shared origin - a perfect case of how a name can echo across cultures without ever being the same story.

Māori Ao: not a trickster or warrior; is condition of existence - being, awareness, life. When personified, Ao is benevolent, passive, the desired state that humans inhabit after emergence from darkness. Karakia invoke moving from Te Pō into Te Ao Mārama. Chinese Ao: docile, enduring, almost elemental - carries weight without complaint until mutilated. Not moral agent; victim of divine necessity. In Liezi, Ao are obedient to Celestial Emperor, carrying mountains at command; later one Ao's legs are severed without consent. Lord Ao: aloof, lawful-neutral absolute, unconcerned with mortal

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Multiple independent etymologies converged on same romanization. 1) Māori 'Ao' < Proto-Polynesian *qaho 'day, daylight, world' < Proto-Oceanic *qaco 'sun, day'; conceptually 'light, day, world, cloud'. Cognate with Hawaiian Ao 'light, dawn'. 2) Chinese 鰲/鳌 (áo) 'giant sea turtle' < Old Chinese *ŋaːw

Pronunciation — Māori: /ˈaɔ/ (ah-oh, two vowels, glottal separation) - IPA [ɑɔ]. Mandarin 鰲: /ɑʊ̯³⁵/ (áo, rising tone). Japanese 青: /a.o/ [äo̞] (two mora). D&D Lord Ao: /eɪ.oʊ/ (AY-oh) per Greenwood audio interview. Ao Naga: /aʊ/ (ow) in Angami-influenced English.

Aliases — AO, Ao (Māori deity), Ao (Chinese turtle), Lord Ao, Aoshima reference, Ao Naga ethnonym

Ownership — No single ownership. Distinct custodians: 1) Māori iwi (especially Te Arawa, Ngāti Awa, Ngāi Tahu traditions) for Te Ao cosmology - living tradition, care required. 2) Han Chinese

Names are cult layers, not one fixed label.

Powers
Embodiment of Light/Consciousness (Māori)

Ao is not a power but the condition of illumination and knowing. To be in Te Ao is to exist, see, and understand. Karakia credit Ao with enabling growth, sight, and human thought.

Limit: Not an active miracle; cannot be invoked like a war god. Requires emergence from Te Pō; some traditions say Ao can be lost (return to darkness) if tapu violated.

World-Bearing (Chinese turtle)

Supports entire mountains/islands on carapace indefinitely. Fifteen Ao together support five xian mountains (Daiyu, Yuanqiao etc.) preventing them drifting.

Limit: Obedient to Jade Emperor/Celestial Emperor; cannot refuse command. Vulnerable to divine mutilation.

Cosmic Pillar Creation

Four legs severed become four pillars holding up sky after Gong Gong breaks Mount Buzhou. Legs become stone pillars at cardinal points.

Limit: Fatal to Ao; requires Nüwa's intervention; one-time cosmological act, not repeatable.

Overgod Authority (Lord Ao)

Creates crystal spheres and deities, assigns portfolios, can strip any god of divinity, create or destroy Tablets of Fate that define divine law. Gods cannot lie to Ao; his word is reality.

Limit: Bound by higher 'luminous being' hinted in Waterdeep epilogue; chooses non-intervention; does not grant spells or answer prayers; cannot be contacted by commune.

Divine Judgment and Portfolio Reassignment

During Time of Troubles (1358 DR), Ao cast all gods to walk Toril as mortals for stealing Tablets, judged Myrkul, Bane, Mystra, etc., promoted Cyric, Midnight (Mystra) etc.

Limit: Only acts when cosmic balance broken; not everyday intervention.

Linguistic/Color Domain (Japanese ao)

Covers semantic field of blue, green, pale, unripe. Used in compounds: aozora (blue sky), aoba (green leaves), aoi (hollyhock, also 'blue').

Limit: Context-dependent; modern speakers distinguish ao vs midori but traditional usage overlaps.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No single appearance; version-dependent. Māori Ao: not anthropomorphized in early cosmogony. Ao is light itself - described as brightness, openness, the illuminated world, sometimes personified as a tall, radiant male figure standing in contrast to Te Pō (darkness). In later whakapapa personifications and modern Māori art, Ao appears as a luminous cloaked figure or as daylight sky. No traditional carving specifically labelled 'Ao' distinct from R

Symbols — Māori: light, white/red dawn, the koru unfolding, the separation of Rangi and Papa letting light in. Associated with knowledge (mātauranga) and consciousness. Te Ao Mārama (world of light and understanding) is central symbol. Chinese: turtle shell as cosmic pillar, four legs as four cardinal pillars, mountains on back as stability. Symbol appears on maps and in 'Ao carrying mountain' incense burners. Japanese ao: color chip for blue/gre

Accounts I
From Te Kore to Te Ao - The Emergence of LightTe Rangikaheke manuscripts (GNZMMSS 43) as published by Grey 1855 and Simmons 1976; Best 1924 synthesis

Oral tradition pre-contact, recorded 1849-1855

In beginning was Te Kore (Nothing, potential). From Te Kore came Te Pō (Night, darkness, long night of becoming, with many stages). From Te Pō, through striving and desire, came Te Ao (Day, light, world), then Te Ao Mārama (world of light and understanding), then Te Ao Tūroa (enduring world). Some whakapapa list twelve nights, each producing a stage, until light breaks when Ranginui and Papatūānuku are separated by Tāne, letting Ao flood in. Ao is thus not created but emerged - the universe becoming knowable. Humans are children of Te Ao Mārama. Establishes Māori cosmological framework; explains why knowledge and light are sacred; karakia for new learning recapitulate this emergence.

Ao Carries the Five Immortal MountainsLiezi, Chapter Tang Wen (列子·汤问)

c. 4th century CE, material possibly Warring States

Far east of Bohai Sea, beyond billions of miles, are five sacred mountains: Daiyu, Yuanqiao, Fanghu, Yingzhou, Penglai. They float unsupported, drifting with tides. Immortals dwell there. The mountains would drift away, so the Celestial Emperor (Di) ordered fifteen giant Ao to take turns, in three shifts, lifting heads to support the mountains steady. For millions of years this held. Then a giant from Longbo country fished with a line, caught six Ao on one hook and carried them away. Consequently Daiyu and Yuanqiao floated north and sank, displacing immortals. The Emperor was angry and reduced Longbo people's size. Explains why only three immortal mountains remain in later lore; etiology for Longbo giants becoming small; demonstrates Ao as cosmic infrastructure.

Nüwa Cuts Ao's Legs to Mend the HeavensHuainanzi 6.1, Fengsu Tongyi, commentary to Liezi

c. 139 BCE (Huainanzi)

Water god Gong Gong, in anger after losing battle with Zhurong or Zhuanxu, smashed his head against Mount Buzhou, the pillar holding sky. Sky tilted northwest, earth sank southeast, waters flooded. Nüwa, creator goddess, melted five-colored stones to patch sky, cut off the four legs of the giant Ao turtle, and set them as four pillars at four corners to support sky anew, tamed flood, and restored order. The Ao is sacrificed without consent; its legs become eternal pillars. Foundational Chinese cosmology - explains why sky tilts, rivers flow southeast, and why turtle legs symbolize stability; Ao becomes martyr for cosmic repair.

Accounts II
Lord Ao Creates Realmspace and the Tablets of FateForgotten Realms Campaign Set (1987), Faiths & Avatars (1996), Avatar Trilogy prologue

1987-1989

Before time, Lord Ao, the Overgod, created Realmspace crystal sphere and seeded it with gods, giving each a portfolio (war, magic, etc.) inscribed on the Tablets of Fate, two stone tablets that are both record and law. Ao decreed that gods must earn worship through service, not demand it. For millennia gods ruled. Ao remained hidden, unknown even to most gods, observing. Establishes metaphysical law of Forgotten Realms; Tablets become plot device; explains why gods need worshippers in this setting.

The Time of Troubles - Ao Casts Down the GodsAvatar Trilogy: Shadowdale (1989), Tantras (1989), Waterdeep (1989); AD&D module Shadowdale (FRE1)

1358 DR (in-universe), published 1989

Gods Bane and Myrkul stole the Tablets of Fate from Ao to gain power over their portfolios. Ao, enraged, summoned all gods to the Hall of Concordance, declared 'You have forgotten your purpose,' and cast them down to walk Toril as mortal avatars, vulnerable to death, until Tablets returned. During this Time of Troubles, magic failed, gods died (Mystra killed by Helm, Bane and Myrkul killed), mortals rose. Ao watched, then after Tablets recovered by mortal heroes (Cyric, Kelemvor, Midnight, Adon), Ao judged: destroyed Tablets, promoted mortals to godhood (Midnight becomes new Mystra, Cyric becomes god of lies), and lifted gods back, warning them to be worthy. Major Realms-shaking event still referenced in all later editions; explains 2e to 3e deity changes; defines Ao's role as ultimate judge.

Ao the Color - The Blue-Green That Is OneMan'yōshū (c.759), Heian color records, modern JIS

Nara period to present

Ancient Japanese had one term ao for the grue range (blue/green). Man'yōshū poems speak of 'ao yama' (green/blue mountains), 'ao uma' (pale horse). Heian court ranked ao as one of five cardinal colors. Traffic lights installed 1930s were called 'ao shingō' (blue signal) even though lens is green - linguistic persistence. Modern debate: is ao blue or green? Answer: both, historically one category, now context splits. Explains why Japanese say 'blue' traffic light, 'blue' apples (ao-ringo = green apple), 'blue' youth (seishun). Demonstrates linguistic relativity.

Ao is a single god/creature worshipped across Māori, Chinese, and Japanese traditions. — Three unrelated homonyms sharing romanization. Māori Ao = light/world concept, Chinese Ao = giant turtle, Japanese ao = color term, D&D Lord Ao = modern invented overgod. No cultural diffusion links them.

Māori Ao is the 'god of the clouds' or 'god of daylight' with temples and prayers. — No traditional temples to Ao; Ao is cosmological state (Te Ao Mārama) invoked in karakia about moving into light/knowledge, not a personal deity receiving offerings like Tāne or Tū. Calling Ao 'god' imposes Western pantheon model.

Chinese Ao is the same as the Dragon King Ao Guang from Journey to the West. — Different characters and roles: Ao 鰲 is turtle that supports world/islands; Ao Guang 敖广 is dragon king of East Sea, father of Nezha's enemy. Same Pinyin 'Ao' but different Chinese characters, different myths. Nüwa cuts turtle Ao, not dragon king.

Lord Ao is a standard D&D god players can worship and receive spells from. — Lord Ao explicitly does not grant spells, does not want worship, and is not available as player patron in any edition. Clerics of Ao do not exist in lore; Ao is plot device for overgod judgment, not playable deity.

Japanese people cannot distinguish blue and green because they call both 'ao'. — Japanese distinguishes midori (green) and ao (blue) in modern speech; ao's historical grue range persists in compounds (blue sky, green light, green apple) but speakers know difference. It's linguistic categorization, not color blindness.

Ao Naga are named after the turtle or deity. — Ao Naga ethnonym is Tibeto-Burman self-designation of unrelated etymology (possibly from 'to come' or place name), with no link to turtle or Māori concept. Conflation erases living Indigenous identity.
