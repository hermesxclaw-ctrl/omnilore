Who It Is

Athtart (Astarte).

Athtart (Astarte). Love, fertility, sexuality, war, hunting, the morning and evening star (Venus), kingship, and the sea (in her Phoenician aspect as patroness of sailors). She was a state goddess, a protector of the royal house, and a personal deity of individuals seeking love and prosperity.

Form

In Ugaritic texts, Athtart appears as a powerful, often martial goddess. In Phoenician and Egyptian iconography, she is depicted as a woman, sometimes nude or semi-nude, holding lotus flowers, or as a warrior goddess on horseback or in a chariot, wielding a spear and shield. In Egyptian art, she is sometimes shown wearing the tall crown of a goddess, holding a was-scepter, and is associated with horses and chariots. Later Greek depictions, conflating her with Aphrodite, show her in the nude or semi-draped, accompanied by Eros, doves, or a swan.

Powers

Martial Prowess Athtart is described as a formidable warrior. In Ugaritic texts, she is called 'Athtart the Terrible' or 'Athtart of the Mighty Name.' She is depicted as a huntress and a chariot-riding goddess of war, capable of instilling fear in enemies. Ugaritic, Egyptian (as the goddess of the horse and chariot), and Phoenician traditions. Her martial power is often exercised in service to other gods (like Baal) or the king, rather than as an independent conquering force. Her martial aspect is sometimes downplayed in favor of her love-goddess role in later Greek and Roman interpretations.

Goddess of Love and Fertility As a goddess of love, sexuality, and fertility, she was invoked for romantic success, childbirth, and agricultural abundance. Her cult likely involved sacred prostitution or sexual rites in some periods, though this is debated and often exaggerated in later polemical sources. Phoenician, Hebrew (as condemned 'Ashtoreth'), and Greco-Roman (as Aphrodite/Venus). The practice of sacred prostitution connected to her cult is a matter of scholarly debate; the evidence is thin and often comes from hostile or late sources. The extent and nature of sexual rites in her worship is contested. Some scholars argue it was a minor aspect, while others see it as central.

Astral Power (Venus) She is the goddess of the planet Venus, the morning and evening star. This astral association connects her to kingship, cosmic order, and the cycle of day and night. Ugaritic (as ‘Athtart), Akkadian (as Ishtar), and South Arabian (as Athtar). The specific mythology of her astral role is not as well-preserved as Ishtar's. The relationship between Athtart and the male god Athtar, also associated with Venus, is complex and not fully understood.

Patronage of Kingship and the Sea In Phoenician city-states, she was a protector of the royal dynasty and a patron of maritime trade and navigation. Sailors would invoke her for safe passage. Phoenician inscriptions and Greek accounts of Phoenician religion. The specific rites for sailors are not well documented. None noted.

Behavior & Habitat

Athtart is a deity of action and power. In the Ugaritic Baal Cycle, she is not a passive fertility goddess; she is a fierce warrior who assists Baal, speaks boldly in the divine assembly, and is associated with the hunt. She is a goddess who gets things done, often acting as a mediator or enforcer. In later sources, her behavior is more aligned with love and sexuality, but she retains a warlike edge, as seen in her identification with the martial Aphrodite Areia in some Greek contexts.

Accounts

Athtart is depicted as a fierce huntress. In one scene, she is described as going to the steppe to hunt, capturing wild bulls and stags. Her martial and hunting prowess is a key part of her character, placing her alongside Anat as a warrior goddess.

Athtart plays a supporting but crucial role in Baal's rise to power. She helps him secure divine approval, and after Baal's death and resurrection, she is present at his restoration. She is not the main protagonist but a powerful ally and enforcer of divine order.

The biblical text condemns the worship of 'Ashtoreth' (a deliberately distorted name) alongside other foreign deities. King Solomon is said to have built a high place for her, and King Josiah's reforms destroyed these sites. The biblical narrative frames her cult as a dangerous foreign influence that led Israel astray.

Lucian describes the great temple at Hierapolis (Mabbug) where the goddess was worshipped, often identified with Astarte. He describes rituals, processions, and the practice of ritual eunuchs, blending Greek and Near Eastern elements. This shows the living, evolving tradition of her cult into the Roman era.

Content Notes

The biblical narrative condemns her cult, and King Josiah's reforms involved the destruction of her altars and images (2 Kings 23:13). This is an example of religious violence and the erasure of a rival cult.

This reflects the Deuteronomistic historian's theological agenda, which sought to centralize worship in Jerusalem and eliminate all other cultic practices.

Hebrew Bible, 2 Kings 23

Her cult is often associated with sacred prostitution or ritual sexual activity. This is based on later Greek and biblical accounts, which may be exaggerated or hostile.

The evidence for sacred prostitution in the ancient Near East is debated. Many scholars now argue that the term 'sacred prostitution' is a misnomer and that the practices were more complex or even non-existent as described.

Herodotus (for Babylonian Ishtar), biblical prophets (Hosea, Jeremiah)

Her distinct identity was often erased or absorbed by other cultures. The Greeks and Romans saw her as a version of Aphrodite, and the biblical writers demonized her. This process of syncretism and polemic led to the loss of her original, independent character.

This is a common pattern in ancient history, where conquering or dominant cultures reinterpret the deities of the conquered.

Greco-Roman literature, Hebrew Bible

Scholarly Debates
War vs. Love Goddess
Athtart is primarily a goddess of war and hunting, as seen in the Ugaritic texts. ⇌ Astarte is primarily a goddess of love and fertility, as seen in Greek and Roman sources.
Evidence for A: Ugaritic Baal Cycle, Egyptian iconography of the horse and chariot.
Evidence for B: Greco-Roman syncretism with Aphrodite, biblical condemnation of her 'sexual' cult.
Relationship with Tanit
Tanit is a local Carthaginian form of Astarte. ⇌ Tanit is a distinct Carthaginian goddess who absorbed some of Astarte's attributes.
Evidence for A: Similarities in iconography and domains; the Carthaginian colony of Tyre, where Astarte was the main goddess.
Evidence for B: Tanit's name is not a direct derivation of Astarte; her cult has unique features, such as the open hand symbol.
Sacred Prostitution
The cult of Astarte (and Ishtar) involved ritual sexual activity, often called 'sacred prostitution.' ⇌ The evidence for 'sacred prostitution' is weak and based on hostile or misunderstood sources.
Evidence for A: Later Greek accounts (e.g., Herodotus on Babylon), biblical prophetic condemnations.
Evidence for B: Lack of contemporary Near Eastern evidence for such practices; the term 'sacred prostitution' is a modern construct with
Identity with Ishtar
Astarte is essentially the same goddess as Ishtar, just with a different regional name. ⇌ Astarte and Ishtar are distinct deities who share a common Semitic heritage but developed different cults, myths, and personalities.
Evidence for A: Shared name root, similar domains (love, war, Venus), ancient syncretism.
Evidence for B: Different mythologies, different cultic practices, different positions in their respective pantheons.
What We Don't Know

Further research is needed on: 1) The specific rituals of her Phoenician cult, especially in Tyre and Sidon. 2) The full extent of her iconography in the Levant, which is less well-preserved than in Egypt. 3) The relationship between her cult and that of Tanit in Carthage, which requires more archaeological data. 4) The social role of her temples and priesthood, which is not well-documented outside of the biblical polemic. 5) The specific hymns or prayers dedicated to her, which are largely lost.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Astarte as the ultimate ancient Near Eastern power player. She's not just a 'love goddess' — she's a divine CEO who also happens to be the head of the military and the patron of the merchant marine. She's the goddess you pray to before a battle, before a wedding, and before setting sail on a trading voyage. She's the one who gets things done, whether that means smiting your enemies or making sure your crops grow. She's a complex, multifaceted deity who was later simplified and distorted by cultures that wanted to use her for their own purposes — either as a symbol of exotic beauty or as a cautionary tale of pagan idolatry.
