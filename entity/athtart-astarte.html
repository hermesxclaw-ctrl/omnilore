Who It Is

Before she was a demon in a medieval grimoire, before she was a footnote in a biblical rant, Astarte was the undisputed queen of the ancient Mediterranean coast — a goddess who rode into battle on a chariot, hunted wild bulls, and commanded the love-struck hearts of sailors and kings alike. She was the power behind the throne in Ugarit, the protector of Phoenician fleets, and the divine lover whose cult spread from the mountains of Syria to the sands of Carthage. She was the morning and evening star, the bringer of life and the terror of the battlefield. And then, history happened: the Greeks made her a beautiful love goddess, the Hebrews made her a shameful idol, and the Christians made her a demon. This is the story of how a great goddess was conquered, distorted, and demonized by the very cultures that feared her power.

Athtart is a deity of action and power. In the Ugaritic Baal Cycle, she is not a passive fertility goddess; she is a fierce warrior who assists Baal, speaks boldly in the divine assembly, and is associated with the hunt. She is a goddess who gets things done, often acting as a mediator or enforcer. In later sources, her behavior is more aligned with love and sexuality, but she retains a warlike edge, as seen in her identification with the martial Aphrodite Areia in some Greek contexts.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Athtart (Ugaritic: ‘ṯtrt) is a feminine form related to the name of the god Athtar, the morning star. The root is connected to the Semitic word for the planet Venus (Ishtar in Akkadian, Athtar in South Arabian). The Greek 'Astarte' (Ἀστάρτη) is a Hellenized form of the Phoenician name.

Pronunciation — /ˈæʃtɑːrt/ (Ashtart) or /əˈstɑːrtiː/ (Astarte). Ugaritic: ‘Ath-ta-rat.

Aliases — Astarte, ‘Athtart, Ashtart, Astaroth, Ashtoreth, Tanit (in some interpretations)

Ownership — Northwest Semitic (Ugaritic, Phoenician, Hebrew, Aramean), with major cult centers in Ugarit, Sidon, Tyre, and Byblos. Later adopted into Egyptian, Greek, and Roman religious spher

Martial Prowess

Athtart is described as a formidable warrior. In Ugaritic texts, she is called 'Athtart the Terrible' or 'Athtart of the Mighty Name.' She is depicted as a huntress and a chariot-riding goddess of war, capable of instilling fear in enemies.

Limit: Her martial power is often exercised in service to other gods (like Baal) or the king, rather than as an independent conquering force.

Goddess of Love and Fertility

As a goddess of love, sexuality, and fertility, she was invoked for romantic success, childbirth, and agricultural abundance. Her cult likely involved sacred prostitution or sexual rites in some periods, though this is debated and often exaggerated in later polemical sources.

Limit: The practice of sacred prostitution connected to her cult is a matter of scholarly debate; the evidence is thin and often comes from hostile or late sources.

Astral Power (Venus)

She is the goddess of the planet Venus, the morning and evening star. This astral association connects her to kingship, cosmic order, and the cycle of day and night.

Limit: The specific mythology of her astral role is not as well-preserved as Ishtar's.

Patronage of Kingship and the Sea

In Phoenician city-states, she was a protector of the royal dynasty and a patron of maritime trade and navigation. Sailors would invoke her for safe passage.

Limit: The specific rites for sailors are not well documented.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In Ugaritic texts, Athtart appears as a powerful, often martial goddess. In Phoenician and Egyptian iconography, she is depicted as a woman, sometimes nude or semi-nude, holding lotus flowers, or as a warrior goddess on horseback or in a chariot, wielding a spear and shield. Symbols — The lion (in Egypt and some Near Eastern contexts), the horse and chariot, the spear and shield, the dove, the sphinx, the star (Venus), the lotus flower, and the cone (a symbol of fertility in Phoenician art).

Domains — Love, fertility, sexuality, war, hunting, the morning and evening star (Venus), kingship, and the sea (in her Phoenician aspect as patroness of sailors). She was a state goddess, a protector of the royal house, and a personal deity of individuals seeking love and prosperity.

Accounts I
The Hunt of AthtartThe Baal Cycle (KTU 1.3-1.4)

c. 14th-12th century BCE

Athtart is depicted as a fierce huntress. In one scene, she is described as going to the steppe to hunt, capturing wild bulls and stags. Her martial and hunting prowess is a key part of her character, placing her alongside Anat as a warrior goddess. Reinforces her role as a powerful, independent deity who can provide for herself and her followers.

Athtart and the Baal CycleThe Baal Cycle (KTU 1.2, 1.6)

c. 14th-12th century BCE

Athtart plays a supporting but crucial role in Baal's rise to power. She helps him secure divine approval, and after Baal's death and resurrection, she is present at his restoration. She is not the main protagonist but a powerful ally and enforcer of divine order. Establishes her as a member of the highest tier of the Ugaritic pantheon, a goddess of action and consequence.

Accounts II
The Biblical CondemnationHebrew Bible (1 Kings 11:5, 2 Kings 23:13)

c. 8th-6th century BCE

The biblical text condemns the worship of 'Ashtoreth' (a deliberately distorted name) alongside other foreign deities. King Solomon is said to have built a high place for her, and King Josiah's reforms destroyed these sites. The biblical narrative frames her cult as a dangerous foreign influence that led Israel astray. The biblical polemic has shaped Western perceptions of Astarte for centuries, casting her as a symbol of pagan idolatry and sexual excess.

The Hellenistic AstarteLucian of Samosata, 'De Dea Syria'

2nd century CE

Lucian describes the great temple at Hierapolis (Mabbug) where the goddess was worshipped, often identified with Astarte. He describes rituals, processions, and the practice of ritual eunuchs, blending Greek and Near Eastern elements. This shows the living, evolving tradition of her cult into the Roman era. Provides a unique, first-hand account of a late, syncretic form of her cult.

Astarte is the same as Ishtar. — They are parallel deities from different cultures (Northwest Semitic vs. Mesopotamian) with distinct myths, cults, and personalities. They share a common origin but are not the same.

The biblical 'Ashtoreth' is an accurate name for the goddess. — 'Ashtoreth' is a deliberate Hebrew distortion. The original name was 'Ashtart,' but the Masoretic scribes pointed it with the vowels of 'boshet' (shame) to insult the goddess and her worshippers.

Astarte was a demon. — In her original cults, she was a major, respected goddess. The demonization occurred later, primarily through Christian demonology (e.g., the Ars Goetia), which transformed pagan deities into demons.

Her cult was primarily about sexual orgies. — While she was a goddess of love and fertility, the evidence for ritual sexual activity is debated and likely exaggerated by hostile sources. Her cult also involved kingship, war, and the protection of the state.
