Who It Is

Before Aphrodite rose from the foam, before the Syrens sang, there was a goddess who dove into a lake and never came out the same. Atargatis, the great Syrian goddess of Hierapolis, was a being of startling contradictions: a queen of heaven who was also a fish, a goddess of fertility whose priests castrated themselves in her honor, a protector of cities who abandoned her own daughter to the desert. She was worshipped for over a thousand years, from the banks of the Euphrates to the streets of Rome, and then she was forgotten, surviving only as a half-remembered name and a strange story about a mermaid. But her story is the story of how the ancient world made sense of the divine: by merging, transforming, and ultimately, by diving into the deep.

Atargatis is a goddess of fertility, water, and sovereignty. She is a bestower of blessings, a protector of cities, and a source of prophetic knowledge. Her cult involved ecstatic and sometimes violent rites, including self-castration among her most devoted male followers (the Galli), who were eunuch priests. She is a demanding goddess, requiring purity and devotion from her followers, and punishing those who offend her.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Atargatis is a Greek rendering of a Syriac/Aramaic compound name, likely 'Atar-' (a form of the goddess 'Attar' or 'Ashtart') combined with '-gatis' (possibly a form of 'Gad', a Semitic deity of fortune). The name thus blends the identities of two great Near Eastern goddesses.

Pronunciation — uh-TAR-guh-tiss

Aliases — Dea Syria, Syrian Goddess, Atarat, Atergatis, Derceto, Derketo

Ownership — Ancient Syria, specifically the city of Hierapolis (modern Manbij) and the region of Cyrrhestica. Her worship spread throughout the Hellenistic and Roman worlds.

Names are cult layers, not one fixed label.

Powers
Fertility and Agricultural Blessing

As a great mother goddess, Atargatis could grant or withhold fertility to the land, animals, and people.

Limit: Not explicitly detailed in surviving texts but implied by her nature.

Prophecy

Her temple at Hierapolis was a major oracular center, where prophecy was delivered through the movements of her sacred fish and through her priests.

Limit: The exact mechanics of the divination are not fully described.

Divine Protection

She protected her city, Hierapolis, and her worshippers. Her role as a city goddess is symbolized by her mural crown.

Limit: Protection was conditional on proper worship and ritual purity.

Transformation

In one myth, she transformed herself into a fish (or a mermaid-like form) to escape a monster, and in another, she died and was transformed into a fish by the gods.

Limit: This power is tied to specific mythological narratives and is not a general ability she is said to use willingly.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Atargatis is typically depicted as a mature, stately goddess, often seated on a throne, wearing a mural crown (a crown shaped like city walls) and a long, elaborate robe. She is frequently flanked by lions, which serve as her sacred animals. In some depictions, she holds a scepter or a spindle, and her dress may be decorated with zodiacal symbols or other celestial imagery. Symbols — The fish (especially the sacred carp in her temple pool at Hierapolis), the dove, the lion, the mural crown, the scepter, the spindle, and the rayed nimbus. Her temple at Hierapolis was a major symbol of her power.

Domains — Fertility, agriculture, water (especially springs and pools), fish, city protection, sovereignty, prophecy, and the underworld. Her domain over fish is unique and central to her identity, stemming from the myth of her transformation.

Accounts I
The Transformation into a Fish (The Derceto Myth)Diodorus Siculus, Bibliotheca historica 2.4; Hyginus, Fabulae 197

Recorded in the 1st century BCE and later

In the most famous version, a goddess (called Derceto by the Greeks) falls in love with a mortal youth. She becomes pregnant and bears a daughter, but then, in shame and rage, kills her lover and abandons the child in the desert. The child is miraculously saved by doves, who feed her. The goddess, overcome with grief or shame, throws herself into a lake and is transformed into a fish. In some versions, only her lower half becomes a fish, explaining the origin of mermaids. The abandoned child is Semiramis, the legendary queen of Assyria. This myth establishes her sacred animals (fish and doves), the prohibition on eating fish among her worshippers, and the founding narrative of the Assyrian royal line through Semiramis.

Accounts II
The Temple of Hierapolis and the Sacred FishLucian, De Dea Syria

2nd century CE

Lucian describes the great temple of Atargatis at Hierapolis in detail. He describes the sacred fish pool, where fish were kept and fed, and where worshippers would come to consult them as oracles. He describes the elaborate rituals, the procession to the sea, and the practice of castration among the priests. He notes that eating fish was strictly forbidden to worshippers. This account provides the most complete picture of her cult practice and confirms the importance of fish and water in her worship.

The Syrian Goddess in Rome (as seen by Apuleius)Apuleius, The Golden Ass, Book 8

2nd century CE

In this satirical novel, the protagonist encounters a band of itinerant priests of the Syrian Goddess in a Roman village. They are depicted as flamboyant, effeminate, and fraudulent, performing ecstatic dances, self-flagellating, and collecting alms. They are accused of theft and eventually driven out of town. While clearly a comic and hostile portrayal, it shows the presence and visibility of her cult in the Roman Empire. Provides evidence for the spread of her cult to the West and for the negative stereotypes associated with its ecstatic practices.

Atargatis was a 'mermaid goddess' and the original mermaid. — This is a modern popular interpretation. The earliest sources say she was transformed into a fish, not a mermaid. The mermaid interpretation appears in later, more ambiguous sources and has been popularized in modern media. Her connection to fish is central, but her form is uncertain.

Atargatis is the same as Astarte or Aphrodite. — While classical authors and some modern scholars identify them, Atargatis is best understood as a distinct Syrian deity with her own unique traits (fish, lions, eunuch priests) that developed in the region around Hierapolis.

The cult of Atargatis involved temple prostitution. — While temple prostitution is attested for some Near Eastern goddesses, there is no clear evidence for it in the cult of Atargatis. Her cult is known for ritual castration and ecstatic rites, but not prostitution.

Lucian's account of her temple is a complete and accurate description. — Lucian's account is the most detailed we have, but it is a literary text with satirical elements and may not be entirely accurate. The temple itself has been destroyed, so we cannot verify all his claims.
