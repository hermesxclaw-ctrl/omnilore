Form

Atargatis is typically depicted as a mature, stately goddess, often seated on a throne, wearing a mural crown (a crown shaped like city walls) and a long, elaborate robe. She is frequently flanked by lions, which serve as her sacred animals. In some depictions, she holds a scepter or a spindle, and her dress may be decorated with zodiacal symbols or other celestial imagery. Lucian describes her golden cult statue at Hierapolis as showing a fully robed goddess holding a scepter in one hand and a spindle in the other, with a rayed nimbus around her head.

Powers

Fertility and Agricultural Blessing As a great mother goddess, Atargatis could grant or withhold fertility to the land, animals, and people. Attested in her widespread cult in agricultural Syria and in her identification with other fertility goddesses. Not explicitly detailed in surviving texts but implied by her nature. None recorded.

Prophecy Her temple at Hierapolis was a major oracular center, where prophecy was delivered through the movements of her sacred fish and through her priests. Attested by Lucian, who describes the fish as oracular. The exact mechanics of the divination are not fully described. None recorded.

Divine Protection She protected her city, Hierapolis, and her worshippers. Her role as a city goddess is symbolized by her mural crown. Attested in iconography and in the general function of Near Eastern city goddesses. Protection was conditional on proper worship and ritual purity. None recorded.

Transformation In one myth, she transformed herself into a fish (or a mermaid-like form) to escape a monster, and in another, she died and was transformed into a fish by the gods. The myths are recorded by Diodorus Siculus and Hyginus, among others. This is a foundational aition (origin myth) for her fish form. This power is tied to specific mythological narratives and is not a general ability she is said to use willingly. The different versions of the myth (escape vs. punishment) are contradictory.

Behavior & Habitat

Atargatis is a goddess of fertility, water, and sovereignty. She is a bestower of blessings, a protector of cities, and a source of prophetic knowledge. Her cult involved ecstatic and sometimes violent rites, including self-castration among her most devoted male followers (the Galli), who were eunuch priests. She is a demanding goddess, requiring purity and devotion from her followers, and punishing those who offend her.

Accounts

In the most famous version, a goddess (called Derceto by the Greeks) falls in love with a mortal youth. She becomes pregnant and bears a daughter, but then, in shame and rage, kills her lover and abandons the child in the desert. The child is miraculously saved by doves, who feed her. The goddess, overcome with grief or shame, throws herself into a lake and is transformed into a fish. In some versions, only her lower half becomes a fish, explaining the origin of mermaids. The abandoned child is Semiramis, the legendary queen of Assyria.

Lucian describes the great temple of Atargatis at Hierapolis in detail. He describes the sacred fish pool, where fish were kept and fed, and where worshippers would come to consult them as oracles. He describes the elaborate rituals, the procession to the sea, and the practice of castration among the priests. He notes that eating fish was strictly forbidden to worshippers.

In this satirical novel, the protagonist encounters a band of itinerant priests of the Syrian Goddess in a Roman village. They are depicted as flamboyant, effeminate, and fraudulent, performing ecstatic dances, self-flagellating, and collecting alms. They are accused of theft and eventually driven out of town. While clearly a comic and hostile portrayal, it shows the presence and visibility of her cult in the Roman Empire.

What We Don't Know

The primary gap is the destruction and inaccessibility of the Temple of Atargatis at Hierapolis/Manbij due to the Syrian Civil War. This prevents any new archaeological verification of Lucian's account. Additionally, the exact nature of her original Semitic name and the relationship between Atargatis and Derceto remain unclear. The role of women in her cult, beyond the mention of female priests, is under-documented. Finally, the origin and full meaning of her fish form are debated, with no clear consensus.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Atargatis as the ancient Near East's answer to a 'franchise reboot'. She was a local Syrian goddess (the 'original'), who was then 'remixed' by the Greeks and Romans into a new, more universal form (the 'sequel'). The 'canon' of her myths was never fixed; different cities and different authors had their own 'headcanon'. Her story shows us that ancient religion was not a set of static beliefs, but a dynamic, creative process of adaptation and reinterpretation. She is the ultimate example of how a god can be both deeply rooted in one place and yet travel the world.
