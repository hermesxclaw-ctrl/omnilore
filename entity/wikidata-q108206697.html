Who It Is

In the plain of the Jordan, Genesis says, there were five cities so lush they looked like the garden of the Lord — until fire fell from the sky and left nothing but smoke rising like a furnace. One of them was Gomorrah, a name that has never stopped burning: first as scripture, then as warning, then as shorthand for organized crime, aerial annihilation, and every modern Sodom we build for ourselves.

As a city, Gomorrah is anthropomorphized through its inhabitants. Genesis 13:13: 'the people of Sodom were wicked, great sinners against the LORD' — extended by implication to Gomorrah as its twin. Genesis 18-19 frames the sin as so grievous that its 'outcry' reaches heaven. Powers
Exceptional fertility and wealth

The plain of Gomorrah is described as abundantly watered and agriculturally rich, supporting dense urban settlement and trade. This prosperity underpins its political significance in Genesis 14.

Limit: Wealth is presented as morally corrosive; prosperity does not confer military resilience — the cities are subjugated by the eastern kings.

Fortified urban status

As a city-state with a king (Birsha, king of Gomorrah in Genesis 14:2) and a gate where elders/leading men sit (Lot in the gate, Genesis 19:1), Gomorrah possesses walls, governance, and militia.

Limit: Defeated and plundered by Chedorlaomer's coalition; walls offer no protection against divine fire.

Archetypal wickedness / outcry

Narrative attribute: the city's sin is so great its zāʿaqah (outcry) ascends to YHWH, triggering divine investigation (Genesis 18:20-21). Functions as a moral amplifier — Gomorrah exemplifies collective guilt.

Limit: Genesis 18 shows the outcry is verifiable — YHWH sends messengers to see — and Abraham's intercession suggests even ten righteous would have spared it, implying conditional judgment.

Vulnerability to divine overturning

Gomorrah is the paradigmatic object of herem-like annihilation: sulfur and fire from heaven, overturning (hapakh), turning to ashes. Becomes the standard comparison for total destruction.

Limit: Destruction is total and irreversible in the biblical frame; the city is never rebuilt in narrative. Prophetic reuse treats its desolation as permanent warning.

Accounts I
The Cities of the Plain and Lot's ChoiceGenesis 13:5-13; 10:19

Pentateuchal narrative, final form Persian period (5th century BCE)

When Abram and Lot separate due to strife between herdsmen, Lot lifts his eyes and sees the kikkar of the Jordan, well-watered everywhere like the garden of YHWH before YHWH destroyed Sodom and Gomorrah. He chooses the plain and settles among its cities, pitching his tents toward Sodom. The narrator adds proleptically that the men of Sodom were wicked. Genesis 10:19 gives the geographic boundary: the territory of the Canaanites extended 'as far as Sodom, Gomorrah, Admah and Zeboiim, as far as Lasha.' Establishes Gomorrah as part of a fertile but morally precarious urban cluster; sets up Lot's residence in the plain and the later conflict between hospitality and urban corruption.

The War of the Valley of Siddim — Gomorrah SackedGenesis 14:1-12

Pentateuchal narrative; often considered an archaic independent tradition

Four eastern kings led by Chedorlaomer of Elam subjugate five kings of the plain — Bera of Sodom, Birsha of Gomorrah, Shinab of Admah, Shemeber of Zeboiim, and the king of Bela (Zoar) — for twelve years. In the thirteenth year the plain cities rebel; in the fourteenth, Chedorlaomer campaigns and defeats them in the Valley of Siddim, full of bitumen pits. The kings of Sodom and Gomorrah flee, some falling into the pits, and the victors plunder Sodom and Gomorrah, taking goods and captives including Lot. Demonstrates Gomorrah as a political entity with a king and army, vulnerable to Mesopotamian power; Lot's capture precipitates Abram's rescue raid (Genesis 14:13-16), first biblical war narrative.

Abraham Bargains for Sodom and GomorrahGenesis 18:16-33

Pentateuchal narrative

YHWH, accompanied by two messengers, tells Abraham He will go down to see whether the outcry from Sodom and Gomorrah matches its deeds. Abraham intercedes, asking if the Judge of all the earth will sweep away righteous with wicked. He negotiates from fifty righteous down to ten: if ten righteous are found in the city, YHWH will spare it for their sake. YHWH agrees and departs. The scene establishes divine justice as investigatory and merciful, not arbitrary. Theological foundation for collective punishment and intercession; implies Gomorrah's destruction is not capricious but after due inquiry and lack of minimal righteous remnant. Becomes central in Jewish, Christian, and Islamic ethics of justice.

Accounts II
Fire from Heaven — The Overturning of GomorrahGenesis 19:1-29

Pentateuchal narrative

Two angels arrive at Sodom at evening; Lot, sitting in the gate, hosts them. The men of the city, young and old, surround the house demanding to 'know' the visitors — universally read as threatened sexual assault. Lot offers his daughters, the mob refuses, and the angels strike the crowd with blindness. At dawn the angels urge Lot to flee with his wife and two daughters to Zoar; YHWH rains sulfur and fire from heaven upon Sodom and Gomorrah, overthrowing the cities, the plain, and its vegetation. Lot's wife looks back and becomes a pillar of salt. Abraham looks from Mamre and sees smoke rising like a furnace. Etiology for the Dead Sea wasteland; archetype of divine judgment, hospitality violated, and rescue of a remnant. Lot's wife becomes enduring icon. Prophets thereafter use 'like Sodom and Gomorrah' as shorthand for irreversible desolation.

In the Cave — Lot's Daughters and the Origin of Moab and AmmonGenesis 19:30-38

Pentateuchal narrative

After the destruction, Lot fears to stay in Zoar and dwells in a cave with his two daughters. Believing no men remain to give them offspring, the daughters make Lot drink wine on successive nights and lie with him. Both conceive: the elder bears Moab, ancestor of the Moabites; the younger bears Ben-ammi, ancestor of the Ammonites. Provides Israelite etiology for neighboring peoples Moab and Ammon as kin but of incestuous origin — a polemical genealogy. Also extends Gomorrah's aftermath: trauma and survival after catastrophe produce new nations.

Gomorrah as Prophetic WarningIsaiah 1:9-10; Jeremiah 23:14; Amos 4:11; Zephaniah 2:9; Ezekiel 16:46-56

8th-6th century BCE

Prophets invoke Sodom and Gomorrah not as history but as present accusation. Isaiah calls Jerusalem's rulers 'rulers of Sodom' and says only a remnant prevents Zion from becoming like Gomorrah. Jeremiah says Jerusalem's prophets have become like Sodom and Gomorrah. Amos has YHWH say 'I overthrew some of you as when God overthrew Sodom and Gomorrah' yet Israel did not return. Ezekiel 16 reframes Sodom's sin as pride, surfeit, and neglect of the poor, and imagines Sodom as Jerusalem's sister to be restored. Transforms Gomorrah from geographic memory into moral-theological typology that persists through New Testament (Romans 9:29) and Qur'an. Enables later Christian and Islamic homiletics to label any city as 'another Sodom and Gomorrah.'

Sodom and Gomorrah were destroyed solely because of homosexuality. — Genesis 19 depicts threatened gang rape and severe inhospitality; prophetic texts (Ezekiel 16:49-50, Isaiah 1, Jeremiah 23) define the sin as pride, social injustice, oppression of the poor, and general wickedness. Jude 7 does link to sexual immorality, but the Hebrew Bible's own interpretation is broader. Modern scholarship stresses violation of hospitality and violence, not orientation.

Gomorrah is just another name for Sodom, or they were one city. — Sources consistently list them as two distinct cities among five (Genesis 10:19, 14:2, Deut 29:23). They share a fate and are paired rhetorically, but are separate polities with separate kings (Bera of Sodom, Birsha of Gomorrah).

Archaeologists have definitively found Gomorrah at Bab edh-Dhra/Numeira or Tall el-Hammam. — Both identifications are hypotheses, not proof. Bab edh-Dhra/Numeira are Early Bronze Age (c. 3300-2350 BCE) and abandoned long before any plausible patriarchal period; Tall el-Hammam's Middle Bronze destruction and airburst claim are contested and not accepted as consensus. No inscription identifies any site as Gomorrah.

Lot's wife was turned to salt for simply disobeying, and the pillar still stands as a specific rock you can visit. — Genesis 19:26 says she looked back and became a pillar of salt, but does not locate it. Josephus claimed to have seen it, and many salt formations at Jebel Usdum (Mount Sodom) have been marketed as 'Lot's wife' — all are natural halite pillars, not a preserved human. The story is etiological.

Operation Gomorrah was named randomly, or Gomorrah (book/film) is about the biblical city. — Operation Gomorrah was deliberately named after the biblical annihilation to evoke total destruction by fire; Saviano's Gomorrah is a pun on Camorra, using the biblical name metaphorically for systemic criminal power, not a retelling of Genesis.

The Gomorrah casino in Fallout: New Vegas is based on a real Las Vegas casino called Gomorrah. — No real casino named Gomorrah exists on the Strip; it is a fictional creation for the game, designed to evoke biblical decadence. Its real-world analog is loosely the Tropicana/Flamingo-era mob casinos, not a namesake.
