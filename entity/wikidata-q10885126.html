Who It Is

In 1926 a Tokyo businessman who had lost his wife, his business and nearly his faith said a light entered his body. Nine years later he called it Johrei — a palm-raised channel of divine light — and built a religion around three arts: healing, farming, and beauty. The largest surviving branch of that religion is Izunome, the hiragana-named community that still hangs his calligraphy around its members' necks and tends sacred grounds from Atami's cloud-wrapped hills to a reservoir outside São Paulo.

Organizational ethos is defined by Okada's 'three pillars': Johrei (purification through Light), Nature Farming (shizen nōhō, farming without chemical fertilizers, respecting soil vitality), and Art of Beauty (creation and appreciation of beauty to elevate spirit). Practice is daily: members offer Johrei to self, family, and others; practice nature farming or support organic distribution; arrange flowers and visit museums/gardens. Emphasis on altruism (usefulness to others, as noted by Matsuoka in Brazil), gratitude, and construction of paradise on earth rather than afterlife

Powers
Johrei (Jōrei, Purification by Divine Light)

Core claimed practice: channeling of God's Healing Light through the palm of an authorized member (wearing O-Hikari) to purify the recipient's spirit, thereby addressing spiritual causes of physical illness, misfortune, and conflict. Performed silently with raised hand, typically 10-30 minutes, without touch or incantation.

Limit: Requires O-Hikari authorization; efficacy described as dependent on recipient's spiritual clouds (kumori) and divine will, not guaranteed. Organization presents it as complementary to, not replacement for, medical care in contemporary statements (post-1970s). No biomedical evidence accepted outside faith community; anthropologists (Davis 1980) describe it as ritual healing.

O-Hikari Consecration and Authorization

Material artifact that confers spiritual authority: pendant containing Okada's sacred calligraphy (Goshinsho). Wearing it after initiation ceremony (nyūkai) permits channeling Johrei. Considered to enshrine divine light itself.

Limit: Must be received from authorized minister; can be revoked; requires regular attendance and offering (o-hōnō). Replacement requires re-consecration. Not effective without faith/purity of channeler per doctrine.

Nature Farming (Shizen Nōhō / Art of Nature)

Agricultural method avoiding chemical fertilizers, pesticides, and animal manure, emphasizing soil's inherent power and respect for nature's vitality. Produce is considered spiritually purer and health-promoting. Second pillar alongside Johrei.

Limit: Yields lower than conventional farming; requires specific compost methods (not fully codified in English sources); not universally adopted by all members, many participate as consumers rather than farmers.

Art of Beauty (Geijutsu / Ikebana and Museum Aesthetics)

Elevation of spirit through creation and appreciation of beauty: ikebana flower arranging, tea ceremony, ceramics, and museum curation. Beauty is said to purify and raise vibration, complementing Johrei.

Limit: Requires training and access to materials/collections; spiritual benefit described as indirect and cumulative rather than immediate healing.

Purification of Spiritual Clouds (Kumori) and Ancestral Healing

Doctrinal ability to dissolve spiritual clouds accumulated through sin, illness, and ancestral karma, including Johrei offered to ancestors and to places. Linked to eschatology of transition to Age of Day under Miroku Ōmikami.

Limit: Attested only as doctrinal claim; no empirical verification; requires sustained practice and moral reform (not ritual alone).

Accounts I
The 1926 Revelation and the Founding of Sekai Kyuseikyo (1935)Okada, Mokichi. Foundation of Paradise Vol. 1; Staemmler & Dehn 2011 pp. 89-95; Agency for Cultural Affairs registry

1926-1935

Mokichi Okada (1882-1955), former businessman in the lacquer and jewelry trade who had experienced bankruptcy and family deaths, claimed that on a night in 1926 a divine revelation empowered him as a channel for God's Healing Light. He described being shown that illness, poverty and conflict stem from spiritual impurity and that a new Messianic Age of Light was imminent under Miroku Ōmikami. After years as a member of Ōmoto (a Shinto-derived new religion), he began healing activities in Tokyo and in 1935 formally founded Dai Nihon Kannon Kai, soon renamed Sekai Kyūseikyō. He taught that anyone could channel Johrei after receiving his consecrated calligraphy (O-Hikari) and began publishing his revelations. Established doctrinal core (Johrei + Miroku eschatology) and ritual object (O-Hikari) that persist in Izunome today. Created institutional lineage that would later split into Izunome and other kyodan. Provided narrative charter for all later sacred ground construction.

Building Paradise: Zuiunkyo and Shinsenkyo (1945-1955)MOA Museum of Art history; Hakone Museum of Art charter 1952; Staemmler & Dehn 2011 pp. 102-108

1945-1955

After wartime suppression of new religions, Okada resumed expansion. He acquired land in Atami (Shizuoka) and Hakone (Kanagawa) to build model paradises on earth. Zuiunkyō (Land of Auspicious Clouds) in Atami became headquarters, with gardens, villas, and later the MOA Museum site. Shinsenkyō (Land of Divine Immortals) in Hakone became the site of the Hakone Museum of Art (founded 1952), exhibiting Okada's collection of Japanese ceramics and art as an act of beauty's salvific power. Construction was presented as literal preparation for the Age of Day, with nature farming plots and flower gardens integral to the design. Created permanent sacred geography inherited by Izunome and sister kyodan; established Art of Beauty as institutional pillar via museums; provided postwar legitimacy through cultural patronage. Atami and Hakone remain pilgrimage and tourist sites.

Succession Crisis and the Birth of the Three Kyodan (1955-1990s)Staemmler & Dehn 2011 pp. 110-118; Clarke 1999 entry Sekai Kyuseikyo; Shukyo Nenkan 1990s editions

1955-1998

Okada died 10 February 1955 without a single undisputed successor. Leadership was contested among his widow Yoshi Okada, daughter and son-in-law, and senior ministers. Over decades, lawsuits, separate incorporations, and negotiated settlements produced multiple legally independent religious corporations claiming Sekai Kyuseikyo lineage. By the late 1990s the Agency for Cultural Affairs recognized three main bodies as constituting Sekai Kyuseikyo: Sekai Kyuseikyo Izunome Kyodan, Sekai Kyuseikyo Toho no Hikari Kyodan, and Sekai Kyuseikyo Su no Hikari Kyodan. Izunome, centered on Atami/Hakone heritage and led by figures linked to the Okada family, emerged as the largest. Splinter groups such as Sukui no Hikari Kyodan (1972) and Shinji Shumeikai (1970) departed entirely. Explains why 'Izunome' today is not the whole Church but one branch; explains shared yet contested use of Zuiunkyo/MOA heritage; creates ongoing need to distinguish Izunome publications and O-Hikari lineage from sister groups.

Accounts II
Crossing the Pacific: Messianity in Brazil and Guarapiranga (1930s-2000)Matsuoka 2000 presentation; Igreja Messiânica Mundial do Brasil official history; Staemmler & Dehn 2011 pp. 145-150

1930s-present

Japanese new religions arrived in Brazil with immigrants from the 1930s. Sekai Kyuseikyo missionaries established the Igreja Messiânica Mundial do Brasil, which grew beyond the Japanese-Brazilian community to include at least hundreds of thousands of non-Japanese Brazilians by 2000 (Matsuoka reports Messianity among four largest Japanese new religions in Brazil). To anchor the faith, the church built the Guarapiranga Sacred Grounds (Solo Sagrado de Guarapiranga) on a large property by the Guarapiranga Reservoir in Parelheiros, São Paulo — landscaped gardens, Johrei centers, and gathering halls modeled on Zuiunkyo. Matsuoka's ethnography notes the Brazilian emphasis on becoming a 'useful person' (pessoa útil) through Johrei service and flower distribution. Brazil became the largest overseas base for Messianity, with Izunome-affiliated activities; Guarapiranga functions as Western Hemisphere pilgrimage site; demonstrates successful inculturation of a Japanese new religion. Membership claims of 800,000 worldwide include many Brazilians.

The Museum as Ministry: Hakone (1952) and MOA (1982)MOA Museum of Art official chronology; Hakone Museum of Art records; Japan Times cultural reporting

1952-1982

Okada taught that beauty purifies the spirit. To institutionalize this, the church opened the Hakone Museum of Art (Hakone Bijutsukan) in Shinsenkyo in 1952, focusing on Japanese ceramics, and three decades later the MOA Museum of Art (MOA Bijutsukan) in Zuiunkyo, Atami, in 1982, housing a broader collection including National Treasures (e.g., Ogata Kōrin's Red and White Plum Blossoms). Both museums are legally operated by foundations linked to Sekai Kyuseikyo/Izunome heritage. Shinji Shumeikai, a splinter, later built the architecturally famed Miho Museum (1997, I.M. Pei) in Shiga, showing how the museum-temple model propagated across schisms. Gave Izunome lineage high cultural visibility and legitimacy; provided public access to art as religious practice; created heritage disputes over collection ownership after splits. MOA and Hakone remain major Atami/Hakone tourist attractions.

The Offshoots: Mahikari and Shumei (1959-1972)Davis 1980; Staemmler & Dehn 2011 pp. 118-125; Clarke 1999

1959-1972

Two major offshoots illustrate Izunome's lineage impact. Yoshikazu Okada (1901-1974, no relation to Mokichi), a former Sekai Kyuseikyo member, founded Sekai Mahikari Bunmei Kyodan (later Sukyo Mahikari) in 1959/1963, teaching okiyome, a hand-raised light ritual strikingly comparable to Johrei. Anthropologist Winston Davis documented the ritual comparability. Separately, in 1970 businessman Nobuhiko Koyama, a former Sekai Kyuseikyo leader, founded Shinji Shumeikai (Shumei) claiming to fulfill Okada's unfinished paradise plan, building its own sacred grounds and the Miho Museum. Both groups are considered descendants of the Church of World Messianity by researchers (CFAR classification) but are legally and doctrinally independent from Izunome. Shows pattern of post-Okada charismatic succession; explains why Johrei-like practices appear in multiple organizations; requires careful distinction between Izunome Johrei and Mahikari okiyome despite surface similarity.

Izunome is an ancient Shinto goddess or kami. — Izunome is a modern religious corporation (1950s-1990s) within the Church of World Messianity, founded by Mokichi Okada. The name may echo Shinto theonyms but the organization has no pre-modern attestation as a deity; Wikidata's 'deity' typing is an error.

Johrei is the same as Reiki or can cure any disease by itself. — Johrei is a specific Messianity practice tied to O-Hikari authorization and Miroku Ōmikami theology, distinct from Usui Reiki. Church materials historically claimed broad healing, but contemporary Izunome statements present it as spiritual purification complementary to medical care, not a guaranteed cure.

The MOA Museum of Art is a secular private museum unrelated to religion. — MOA and Hakone Museums were founded by Mokichi Okada and operated by foundations linked to Sekai Kyuseikyo (Izunome heritage) explicitly to embody the Art of Beauty pillar. While they function as public art museums with National Treasures, their origin and purpose are religious.

Igreja Messiânica Mundial in Brazil is a Brazilian invention or Catholic offshoot. — It is the Brazilian branch of a Japanese new religion (Sekai Kyuseikyo), brought by Japanese immigrants from the 1930s and now inculturated with many non-Japanese Brazilian members. Its sacred ground at Guarapiranga is modeled on Atami's Zuiunkyo.
