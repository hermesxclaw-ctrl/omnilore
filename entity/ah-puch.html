Archive Dossier — Identity

Ah Puch

Pronunciation Ah Puch: /ɑː ˈpuːtʃ/; Cizin: /ˈsiːzɪn/; Kisin: /ˈkiːsin/; Yum Cimil: /juːm ˈsiːmil/

Etymology

The name Cizin likely derives from the Yucatec Maya root 'ciz', meaning flatulence or foul stench, hence 'Stinking One'. Yum Cimil means 'Lord of Death', Hun Ahau means 'One Ruler', and Ah Puch may relate to 'to melt' or 'to decompose'.

Cultural Ownership

Maya civilization, particularly Yucatec and Lacandon Maya; also present in broader Mesoamerican death deity complex.

Disputed Identifications

Scholars sometimes conflate Ah Puch with other Maya death gods or with Mictlāntēcutli of the Aztecs, but these are distinct deities with overlapping functions. The identification of Hun-Came and Vucub-Came from the Popol Vuh as aspects of Ah Puch is debated.

Archive Dossier — Approach

In the dark recesses of the Maya underworld, a lord of decay and earthquakes presides over the fate of the dead. Known by many names—Ah Puch, Cizin, Yum Cimil—this god embodies the inevitable stench of mortality, burning souls to nothing in a cycle of torment. Yet his power is not absolute; even he answers to a greater force, and his story weaves through ancient texts, modern games, and contemporary novels.

Ah Puch is the Maya lord of death at its most anatomically frank: a skeletal god, his spine exposed, his belly distended with decay, wearing a collar of dangling eyeballs harvested from the dead, ruling Mitnal, the ninth and lowest layer of the underworld — the floor beneath every other floor. The codices paint him with brutal honesty: black spots of putrefaction on his bones, bells in his hair that ring as he walks (jingling, in Yucatec folk memory, through houses where someone is about to die), an owl — the muan bird — as his herald.

Who They Are

Ah Puch is the Maya lord of death at its most anatomically frank: a skeletal god, his spine exposed, his belly distended with decay, wearing a collar of dangling eyeballs harvested from the dead, ruling Mitnal, the ninth and lowest layer of the underworld — the floor beneath every other floor. The codices paint him with brutal honesty: black spots of putrefaction on his bones, bells in his hair that ring as he walks (jingling, in Yucatec folk memory, through houses where someone is about to die), an owl — the muan bird — as his herald. Where death gods elsewhere are stern judges, Ah Puch is a hunter with a hunter's patience: Yucatec tradition says he prowls the houses of the sick, and gave the culture one of its most practical pieces of supernatural advice — moan and wail loudly when in danger, because Ah Puch, hearing groans, assumes his work is already done and passes on. Screaming, in his jurisdiction, is a survival technology. His alternate name Cizin — the Flatulent One — is not comic relief but sensory reporting: he is the smell of death given a throne.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

Typically depicted as a skeletal figure, often with a bloated belly, representing decay. May have a death collar made of eyes, and a black body with white spots. Sometimes shown with a skull-like head and protruding ribs.

Symbols & Iconography

Associated with the number 10 (as Lord of the tenth day), the dog, the owl, and the 'death eyes' motif. Often shown with a vulture or a moan bird, and with a downward-pointing hand gesture indicating death.

Behaviour & Personality

Malevolent and terrifying, associated with earthquakes, death, and decay. In Lacandon myth, he tortures souls by burning them, deriving pleasure from their suffering. He is also a god of war and human sacrifice.

Domains & Habitats

Ruler of the underworld (Metnal or Xibalba), the realm of the dead. He is also associated with the earth's interior, causing earthquakes.

Version Differences

In Yucatec traditions, he is more prominent as a single death god, while in K'iche' traditions, the underworld is ruled by a pair (Hun-Came and Vucub-Came). Lacandon beliefs emphasize the burning of souls, which may not be universal.

Origins

Designated God A in the codex classifications; his skeletal iconography saturates the Dresden Codex almanacs of death-days, and his cult persisted into colonial Yucatec belief as Yum Kimil.

Attested in Dresden and Madrid Codices, Yucatec traditionEra Classic Maya onward

Form

A skeleton mid-decomposition: bare skull or bloated death's-head, eyeball collar, black corruption-spots, bells at hair and ankles, attended by the owl and the dog — both death-omens across the Maya world.

Powers

Dominion over Mitnal and the final destination of most dead; death-omens dispatched through owls; house-stalking of the sick; command of the underworld's lesser lords in later folk synthesis.

Weaknesses & Wards

Deceivable by performance — the loud moaner is passed over as already-claimed. Like all Xibalban powers, he loses to cleverness, never to strength.

The Burning of Souls

Source Lacandon mythology, recorded by Robert BruceCulture Lacandon MayaPeriod 20th century

Summary

When a person dies, Cizin burns the soul on his mouth and anus. The soul complains, and Cizin douses it in cold water, causing more pain, and burns it again until it disintegrates. Then Sucunyum cleanses the soul, allowing it to go free.

Participants

Cizin

the deceased soul

Sucunyum

Consequences

Explains the soul's journey after death and the role of Cizin as a tormentor.

Variants

Other Maya groups do not describe this specific torment.

Ah Puch and Cizin are the same deity.

Many sources use the names interchangeably.

Position B

Most scholars treat them as the same god, but note regional variations.

Cultural Significance

The owl's cry is still a death-omen across the Maya region — when the muan bird sings, a Yucatec proverb says, someone dies. Ah Puch fixed the sensory reality of tropical death — smell, bloat, bells, birds — into a theology.
