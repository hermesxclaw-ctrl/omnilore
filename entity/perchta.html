Who It Is

In the deep midwinter, when the Alps lie silent under snow and the Twelve Nights of Christmas stretch between the old and new year, a figure stirs in the folklore of Austria, Bavaria, and Slovenia. She is Frau Perchta, the Bright One or the Hidden One, a being of dazzling beauty and terrifying ugliness, who rewards the diligent spinner with golden thread and slits the belly of the lazy or the gluttonous, stuffing it with flax or straw. She is the guardian of the spinning wheel, the mistress of the wild, and the judge of human conduct during the holiest nights of the year.

Ambivalent: she rewards diligence and generosity but punishes laziness, gluttony, and breaking taboos. She is a guardian of spinning and enforces fasting rules. She appears during the Twelve Days of Christmas, especially on Twelfth Night (Epiphany eve). She is also a bogeyman figure to frighten children, but this is a later development.

Powers
Supernatural judgment

Perchta inspects spinning work during the Twelve Nights and rewards diligent spinners with full spindles, golden threads, flax tow, or coins in a bucket. She punishes lazy spinners by scratching their faces, smacking their fingers, or slitting their bellies and stuffing them with flax.

Limit: Punishment is limited to those who violate taboos; she does not punish arbitrarily.

Enforcement of fasting

She punishes those who eat forbidden food on fast days by slitting their bellies and stuffing them with straw, sewing it up with a plowshare and chains. In some variants, the belly is filled with stones and the body sunk in a well.

Limit: Only applies during fast days.

Shapeshifting

Her large foot (goose or swan foot) is a mark of her higher nature and ability to shapeshift into animal form, as suggested by Grimm.

Limit: Not explicitly described in all sources; inferred from folklore.

Guardianship of children's souls

As Perchta-Holle, she is associated with water-springs and is a guardian of children's souls, particularly unbaptized ones.

Limit: Limited to specific regional beliefs.

Bestowal of abundance

She is responsible for the growth of grain and can bring wealth and abundance to those who leave offerings.

Limit: Requires offerings.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Perchta has two forms: the beautiful and the ugly. In the Tyrol, the beautiful version is portrayed as a little old woman with a very wrinkled face, bright lively eyes, and a long hooked nose; her hair is disheveled, her garments tattered and torn. In processions (Perchtenlauf), the beautiful masker wears dresses decked with ribbons and galloons, while the ugly ones look hideous, with rats and mice, bells and chains hanging from them. Symbols — Spinning implements (distaff, spindle, flax), food offerings (milk, gruel), the plowshare and chains (used in punishments), the bucket at the well (where coins are found), the stove (as a hiding place), and the Twelve Nights of Christmas.

Domains — She roams the countryside at midwinter, enters homes during the Twelve Nights, is associated with water-springs (as Perchta-Holle), and is guardian of woodland beasts. She is also connected to the growth of grain.

Accounts I
The Farmhand and the PerchtlgobaFolk tale from Kalwang, Styria

Unknown (collected 19th century)

A farmwoman put out milk as an offering to the Perchtl (Perchtlgoba). A disbelieving farmhand hid in a stove and peeped through a borehole on Perchtlnacht. When Perchtlgoba and her retinue of unbaptized children arrived, she ordered the opening covered, which struck the man blind. The next Perchtlnacht, he hid again and she ordered 'Uncover the opening', restoring his sight. The farmhand was blinded and then healed, demonstrating Perchta's power.

Perchta is purely a Christmas witch or bogeyman for children. — Initially, Perchta enforced communal taboos and rewarded diligence, not primarily a children's bogeyman. The bogeyman aspect is a later development.

Perchta is the same as Krampus. — Perchta is a female figure with her own mythology, while Krampus is a male companion of Saint Nicholas. They are distinct, though both appear in Alpine folklore.

Perchta's name means 'bright one' with certainty. — This is one proposed etymology, but it is not universally accepted; other derivations exist.
