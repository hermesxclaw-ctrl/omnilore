Who It Is

In the flower-decked courts of the Aztec gods, where blood and blossom shared the same earth, there walked a goddess whose beauty was a weapon and whose smile hid a thousand-year captivity. Xochiquetzal, the 'Precious Flower,' was the patroness of love, fertility, and the arts of women—yet her own story is one of abduction and transformation, a divine mirror of the human women she protected.

As a goddess of love and desire, she is passionate and associated with pleasure and excess. She is also a protector of young mothers and a patroness of women's crafts, implying a nurturing aspect.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Xochiquetzal is a compound of xochitl ('flower') and quetzalli ('precious feather; quetzal tail feather'), meaning 'flower precious feather' or 'flower quetzal feather'. Her alternative name, Ichpochtli, derives from ichpochtli ('maiden, young woman').

Pronunciation — Classical Nahuatl: [ʃoːt͡ʃiˈket͡sal]

Aliases — Ichpochtli, The Toltec Flower Goddess

Ownership — Aztec (Mexica) culture of central Mexico, with roots in earlier Mesoamerican traditions. She is also identified with the Maya Goddess I.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Xochiquetzal is always depicted as an alluring and youthful woman, richly attired, with symbols of vegetation and flowers. She is often shown with elaborate headdresses and jewelry, embodying beauty and fertility.

Symbols — Flowers, particularly marigolds and other blossoms; quetzal feathers; weaving implements; sometimes accompanied by butterflies or birds. She is associated with the earth's fertility and the moon.

Domains — She presides over flowers, love, fertility, pregnancy, childbirth, and the arts of weaving and embroidery. She dwells in the paradisiacal realm of Tamoanchan, a place of origin and abundance.

Accounts I
Abduction by TezcatlipocaFlorentine Codex, other colonial chronicles

Post-classic to colonial era

Xochiquetzal was married to Tlaloc, the rain god, until Tezcatlipoca, the god of sorcery and fate, kidnapped her and forced her to marry him. This abduction explains the changing seasons and the cycles of life and death. She became associated with Tezcatlipoca, and her story became a metaphor for the loss of innocence and the inevitability of change.

Accounts II
Festival of HueypachtliSahagún's chronicles

Pre-Columbian

During this festival, Xochiquetzal was honored with flower offerings, drinking, and fornications. A young woman impersonated the goddess, was decapitated, flayed, and her skin was worn by a man who then weaved, symbolizing gender ambiguity. The ritual reinforced the goddess's association with fertility and the cyclical nature of life and death.
