Lebe

Earth God of Renewal, Lord of Vegetation, The Reborn Ancestor

My body became stones, my flesh became serpent, my spirit became the god who makes the millet grow. Dig the earth, and you will find me. Plant the seed, and you will feed me. Harvest the grain, and I will feed you.

Who It Is

Lebe is the Dogon earth god of agricultural fertility, vegetation, renewal, and the cycle of death and rebirth. His worship is intimately connected to the Hogon — the spiritual and political leader of each Dogon village, who is considered Lebe's living representative on earth. Lebe embodies the principle that from death comes new life, and his mythology is expressed through the most important annual rituals of the Dogon agricultural calendar.

The myth of Lebe's origin is profoundly tied to the Dogon experience of death and migration. According to tradition, the Dogon people originally lived in the land of Mandé to the southwest, but were driven out and migrated to the Bandiagara Escarpment. During this migration, the Dogon's revered ancestor — Lebe Serou — died. The people wanted to take his body with them, but it was decaying. They decided to bury him in a temporary grave and return later. When they returned to retrieve his remains, they found that a great serpent had consumed the flesh of the body, leaving only the bones, but had vomited up the flesh in the form of stones. These stones became the first Lebe altars, and the serpent became a symbol of Lebe's power of death and regeneration. The ancestor had been transformed into a god.

Lebe's role in Dogon society centers on the Hogon — the village chief who is chosen from among the oldest men of the dominant clan and is considered to be the living embodiment of Lebe's power. The Hogon is the guardian of the village's spiritual and agricultural well-being. At the time of planting, the Hogon performs rituals at the Lebe shrine, sacrificing chickens, offering millet beer, and invoking Lebe's blessing for a bountiful harvest. The Hogon lives apart from the community in a special house, observing ritual prohibitions (he cannot cut his hair or nails, cannot be touched by menstruating women, and his food must be prepared according to strict rules) that maintain his ritual purity and connection to Lebe.

The annual Lebe festival — the bulu — is one of the most important events in the Dogon calendar. During this three-day ceremony, the Hogon is ritually purified and renewed, the Lebe shrine is repainted with sacred designs, sacrifices are offered, and the entire community participates in feasting, dancing, and mask performances. The festival marks the beginning of the agricultural year and ensures the fertility of the fields.

Lebe is also deeply connected to the Sigui ceremony — the most important and rarest Dogon ritual, celebrated only once every sixty years to mark the cycle of Sirius B around Sirius A and to renew the world. The Sigui involves the creation of a great snake mask (the 'Great Mask'), ritual journeys between villages, secret language, and the transmission of esoteric knowledge from elder initiates to the next generation. Lebe's presence permeates this ceremony, linking agricultural fertility to cosmic renewal.

Lebe represents the Dogon understanding that death is not an end but a transformation that feeds new life. Just as Lebe Serou's body became the fertilizing stones of the shrine, and just as crops grow from the decay of previous harvests, the cycle of death and rebirth is the fundamental rhythm of existence. Lebe's worship connects the individual Dogon to the ancestors, to the land, and to the cosmic cycles that sustain all life.
