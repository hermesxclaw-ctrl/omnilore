Who It Is

Lono (full name Lono-i-ka-makahiki, 'Lono of the Makahiki') is the Hawaiian god of agriculture, peace, fertility, rain, music, and the harvest festival that bears his name. He is one of the four major Hawaiian akua and is characterized by his gentleness compared to the war god Kū. The Makahiki festival, which runs for approximately four months from October/November to February/March (the Hawaiian rainy season), was Lono's sacred time. During Makahiki, all warfare was forbidden, taxes were collected by priests carrying the Lono image (a wooden crossbar with white tapa cloth), and the people engaged in games, feasting, hula, and athletic competitions. Lono is associated with dark clouds, thunder, and the gentle rain that nurtures crops, especially the sweet potato and taro. One of the most remarkable moments of Hawaiian history involves Lono: when Captain James Cook arrived in Kealakekua Bay in January 1779 during Makahiki season, the Hawaiians initially identified him as Lono returned in fulfillment of prophecy and treated him accordingly. When Cook returned after damaging circumstances forced him back, he was killed — a complex event that scholars continue to debate as either the ritual execution of a failing god or a political conflict. In the Kumulipo, Lono is the son of the gods and is associated with the pig and the sweet potato. His spirit lives on in contemporary Hawaii through revived Makahiki celebrations and the enduring Hawaiian respect for agricultural cycles and peaceful community life.
