Form

Sekien's Konjaku Hyakki Shūi plate depicts Ameonna as a woman standing out in falling rain, lifting one hand to her mouth to lick the raindrops — a quiet, almost meditative pose that is the creature's only canonical visual. No claws, fangs, or overt terror are shown; the uncanniness lies in her indifference to the storm and in her being out in it at all. The Shimoina Ameonba variant is said to be an eerie old woman (or ogress-form) who appears specifically on rainy nights. There is no established colour scheme, garment, or accessory: appearance is essentially the lone rain-soaked female figure. (attested for the Sekien illustration, reconstructed/regional for the others)

Powers

Rain-calling / rain command Ameonna is 'thought to call forth rain' — the core attributed ability. Wherever she appears or is about, precipitation gathers. In the beneficent reading she deliberately makes it rain to end drought and save crops. Yōkai compendia; the folkloric rain-god register The ability is inconsistently active: in the unlucky companion register she does not control the rain so much as attract it passively, to her own (and everyone's) inconvenience. Whether the rain is a volitional blessing or an involuntary jinx is unresolved across sources.

Unlucky attendance (jinx aura) In modern Japanese idiom, being an ameonna (or ameotoko) means rain has a habit of appearing for you — ruining weddings, outdoor sports days, festivals and photos. Everyday contemporary Japanese usage This is a social idiom, not an active supernatural power; no modern source treats it as literal weather control by an individual. Not a contradiction as such, but a category shift: the label moved from a yōkai noun to a human epithet.

Child-seizing / sack abduction (Ameonba register) In the Shimoina strand, the rain-night woman carries a large sack and is feared as a child-catcher — she appears before crying children, presumably to spirit them away (kamikakushi). Regional Nagano belief; the kamikakushi origin legend Confined to the Ameonba variant and directly contradicted by the rain-god-saviour register elsewhere. Direct tension with the benign drought-breaker: one source calls the rain woman a saviour, the other a child-stealing ogress.

Cloud-and-rain transformation (literary analogue) Through the Song Yu analogy, Ameonna inherits the Wu Shan goddess's ability to become morning cloud and evening rain — a shape-shifting weather duality rooted in Classical Chinese poetics. Sekien's essay gloss; Chaoyunmuyu imagery This is an allusive/verbal inheritance made literal by Sekien's analogy, not an attested independent Japanese power-set. It may be purely literary rather than a living folk ability.

Accounts

In the final volume of his great yōkai catalogue series, Sekien pictures a woman standing in the rain licking water from her hand and titles her 'Ameonna.' His essay reaches across to China: the goddesses of Wu Mountain turn into clouds by morning and rain by evening, so the rain woman is evidently 'one of this kind.' Because no older rain-spirit folklore is securely attested in the earlier compendia, later commentators (including the English Wikipedia's own sourcing) have suggested Sekien may have invented Ameonna outright, using the old Chaoyunmuyu trope to poke gentle satire at the courtesans of the Yoshiwara pleasure quarter.

King Huai (or his son King Xiang, in the variant) sleeps and dreams of a beautiful goddess of Wu Mountain. Before she departs she leaves the haunting vow: 'I will be clouds in the morning and rain in the evening, so at morning and evening we can meet on the balcony.' The episode gives Chinese its poetry for cloud-and-rain as a metaphor of secret love. Sekien lifts this exact logic to gloss why a 'rain woman' should exist at all.

A grief-laden origin is offered for Ameonna: a woman whose newborn was spirited away (kamikakushi) on a day of rain is transformed into a rain woman. In this form she wanders, and is said to appear before other crying children carrying a large sack — an echo of bereavement turned to menace, her own lost child reimagined as a hunger to take another's.

In the Shimoina District an 'eerie woman' known as Ameonba (雨おんば) is said to appear on rainy nights. Folk readings diverge: some call her a yōkai that kidnaps children; others suppose she is a god that visits on rainy days who has fallen from grace and degenerated into a yōkai. Either way she concentrates the dread register of the creature.

The children's anime stages Ameonna as a rain-calling yo-kai and pairs her with the clear-sky man Hareotoko (晴男) — a neat explicit dramatisation of the folk idiom that rain and fair weather are personified, opposed presences. The episode title itself ('Yōkai Ameonna to Hareotoko') is the strongest concrete pop-culture citation of the figure's weather duality.

Content Notes

The darkest strand of Ameonna lore is the claim that she is the ghost of a woman whose newborn was spirited away (kamikakushi) on a rainy day, and who now appears before other crying children carrying a large sack — a child-seizing figure. This turns maternal grief into something actively threatening to other families' children.

Fits the wider family of grieving-mother / child-losing spirits in Japanese folk belief (cf. ubume and its many regional permutations), where loss of a child in childbirth or infancy recurrently yields a restless female spirit.

Modern Japanese folklore compendia; the kamikakushi belief complex

In Shimoina District, Nagano, the rain-night figure Ameonba is recorded specifically as a yōkai that kidnaps children (or, alternatively, a fallen rain-god). The prospect of a woman appearing on rainy nights to take children is the creature's most direct malevolent, victimising register.

Rainy-night and boundary-weather settings are classic contexts for abduction and punishment spirits in Japanese rural belief, where storms erase tracks and create liminal conditions.

Regional Nagano folk belief; yōkai compendia

If Sekien invented Ameonna as a satire of the Yoshiwara Yūkaku (the licensed pleasure quarter), then the 'rain woman' is a chiffre for courtesans — women whose fleeting, purchasable affections are figured as weather. The Chaoyunmuyu ('morning cloud, evening rain') idiom itself frames the courtesan-client encounter in lyrical terms that soften an exploitative institution.

Edo-period encyclopaedic satire routinely used mythological or folkloric masquerade to comment on contemporary urban life, and the pleasure quarter was a favourite subject. Reading Ameonna as such a commentary neither confirms nor denies real folk roots.

Scholarly reading of Sekien's intent; Chaoyunmuyu tradition

A folk (possibly invented) figure has been lifted into global pop culture — a major videogame (Shadow Warrior), an EDM single (deadmau5's 'Ameonna'), and a children's anime (Yo-kai Watch). None of these treat the figure with much fidelity to the darker folklore, and they risk flattening a folk-religious and regional-dread subject into a nameable mascot.

A recurring dynamic in yōkai reception since at least Mizuki Shigeru's manga: creatures stripped of regional menace and recast as cute or marketable. Not unique to Ameonna but worth flagging for a figure whose core register is partly dread.

Scholarly Debates
Real folk creature vs. invented Sekien satire
Ameonna descends from genuine pre-Edo folk belief in a rain spirit. ⇌ Ameonna was fabricated by Toriyama Sekien for the Konjaku Hyakki Shūi as an allegory of the Yoshiwara pleasure quarter; no earlier rain-yōkai source survives.
Evidence for A: The creature's integration into later compendia and modern catalogue tradition gives the impression of antiquity.
Evidence for B: English Wikipedia's own surveying notes 'there are no statements about any rain-related yōkai to be seen' before Sekien;
Bothersome rain-caller vs. holy drought-saver
Ameonna is a nuisance yōkai that irritably calls forth rain, disrupting events. ⇌ Ameonna is a holy rain-god who, during drought, saves people by deliberately making it rain.
Evidence for A: The modern idiom of the rain-ruined event and the 'bothersome yōkai' gloss.
Evidence for B: The explicit drought-relief register recorded in compendia ('a yōkai who saves people by calling forth rain').
Child-kidnapping ogress vs. benign-visiting god (Ameonba)
The Shimoina Ameonba is a yōkai that kidnaps children. ⇌ The Ameonba is a fallen rain-god who visits on rainy days and has degenerated into a yōkai.
Evidence for A: Regional fear of child-spiriting; the sack-carrying image.
Evidence for B: The alternative local reading framing her as a degraded deity rather than a born demon.
Water deity vs. yōkai (categorisation)
Ameonna is a water deity (suishin) — the English Wikipedia description field. ⇌ Ameonna is simply a yōkai — the Japanese Wikipedia description.
Evidence for A: Her rain/kami associations and the Chaoyunmuyu divine-cloud lineage.
Evidence for B: Japanese encyclopaedia framing her plainly as 日本の妖怪, a folk creature rather than a formal deity.
Ancient weather spirit vs. modern human epithet
Ameonna names a supernatural being. ⇌ Ameonna (and ameotoko) merely names an unlucky human — rain following them is coincidence, not magic.
Evidence for A: Folklore compendia and the Sekien plate.
Evidence for B: Everyday modern Japanese usage, where no supernatural claim is implied.

Japanese-language secondary scholarship (Murakami, Tada, Yamaguchi, Inoue Enryō's yōkai studies) was not individually retrieved; these are structural gaps and need Japanese-fluent source work to close. The Ameonba child-kidnapping register, and the 'traded child becomes a rain woman' origin, rest on the English Wikipedia distillation of folklore compendia and should be verified against primary Japanese folklore field records. The Shadow Warrior and deadmau5 reception details were cross-checked against the English Wikipedia articles for those works, not against primary game/press materials. Whether Sekien invented Ameonna entirely remains a genuinely open scholarly question that no source consulted resolves.

A way to hold it in your mind

Strip away every monster movie and the rain woman is stranger still: in her one canonical image she is not raging or killing but standing quietly in the downpour, catching a drop on her hand and licking it — the goddess of the storm reduced to a woman tasting the weather, indifferent to the soaking world around her. That is the whole founding portrait of Ameonna, 雨女, and nobody can quite decide whether she is a god, a ghost, a satire, or a joke. Depending on which village tells you, she is the saint who ends a killing drought with grateful sheets of rain, or the bereaved mother who now stalks other people's children with a sack on rainy nights, or simply the poor guest nobody wants at their wedding because the sky always seems to follow her. Her oldest explanation is lifted wholesale from a Chinese king's wet dream; her future is a videogame where her tears literally fall as rain. Ameonna is less a fixed monster than a argument about weather, grief, and desire, handed from hand to hand and never once made to agree with itself.
