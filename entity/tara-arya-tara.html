Etymology

The name Tārā is Sanskrit for 'star' and also derives from the root √tṝ, meaning 'to cross' or 'to save,' thus 'she who carries across' or 'she who saves.' In Tibetan, she is called Sgrol-ma (Dölma), meaning 'she who saves' or 'the Liberator.'

Cultural Ownership

Buddhism, particularly Vajrayana Buddhism; originated in India, flourished in Tibet, Nepal, Mongolia, and other Himalayan regions.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars debate whether Tārā was originally a Hindu goddess (e.g., as a form of Durgā or Kālī) adopted into Buddhism, or an originally Buddhist deity. In Hinduism, Tārā appears as a goddess in Tantra and as the wife of Bṛhaspati, but these are distinct figures.

Archive Dossier — Approach

In the vast pantheon of Buddhist deities, few figures embody compassion in motion as vividly as Ārya Tārā, the 'Lady of the Star,' whose swift aid is said to rescue beings from every conceivable peril—from physical danger to spiritual blindness. Her emerald form, poised with one leg extended, ready to rise at a moment's notice, has inspired countless devotees across the Himalayas and beyond for over a millennium.

Who It Is

Tara is the most important female bodhisattva and goddess in Mahayana and Vajrayana Buddhism, the embodiment of enlightened compassion in action. She is the 'Mother of the Buddhas' who ferries beings across the ocean of suffering to liberation. According to tradition, Tara was a princess named Yeshe Dawa (Moon of Primordial Awareness) who vowed always to be reborn as a woman to help all beings overcome suffering. Her compassion is said to be faster and more responsive than any other bodhisattva — she is called 'the swift liberator.' Tara appears in multiple forms, most commonly two: Green Tara (the active, swift saviouress) and White Tara (the peaceful goddess of long life and healing). But there are also the 21 Taras, each with a specific function. Green Tara is depicted seated with one leg extended (ready to leap into action), holding blue lotuses. White Tara has seven eyes (forehead, palms, and soles) symbolizing her ability to see suffering everywhere. Tara is invoked for protection from the 'eight great fears' (lions, elephants, fire, snakes, thieves, drowning, imprisonment, and demons) which also represent spiritual obstacles. Her mantra is Om Tare Tuttare Ture Svaha. She is especially revered in Tibet, Mongolia, and the Himalayan regions.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

Tārā is typically depicted as a beautiful, youthful goddess with a serene expression. She is often shown in green (Green Tārā) or white (White Tārā), seated on a lotus throne. Green Tārā is usually depicted with one leg extended, symbolizing her readiness to act. She wears silk robes and precious jewels, and her right hand is in the gesture of granting boons (varada-mudrā), while her left hand holds a blue lotus (utpala).

Symbols & Iconography

The blue lotus (utpala) represents purity and compassion. The extended right leg signifies active compassion. Her green color symbolizes the air element and enlightened activity. In the 21 forms, each has specific attributes, such as different colors and postures.

Behaviour & Personality

Tārā is known for her swift, compassionate action. She is considered a bodhisattva who has vowed to help beings as a female form, and she is often called the 'Mother of all Buddhas.' She is approachable and responsive to prayers, especially for protection and overcoming obstacles.

Domains & Habitats

Tārā is not associated with a specific earthly realm but is present in the pure land of Potalaka, where she is said to reside with Avalokiteśvara. She is also invoked in meditation and ritual spaces.

Version Differences

There are 21 principal forms of Tārā, each with distinct colors and attributes. The most popular are Green Tārā (the swift liberator) and White Tārā (associated with long life and healing). Other forms include Red Tārā (associated with magnetism and subjugating), Black Tārā (fierce protector), and Yellow Tārā (wealth).

Origins

Tara was born from a tear of Avalokiteśvara, the bodhisattva of compassion, who wept at the suffering of the world. His tears formed a lake, and from the lake, Tara rose on a lotus. Another tradition describes her as a princess who vowed to achieve enlightenment in female form to demonstrate that women too can become Buddhas.

Attested in Tantric Buddhist Canon / Sādhanas / Twenty-One Taras SutraEra c. 3rd-7th centuries CE onward
