Who It Is

You're on a research vessel in the South Pacific. The stars are wrong tonight — you can see that now, though you couldn't have explained it an hour ago. The water bulges upward, not like a wave but like the skin of the world is stretching. What rises is not a creature. It is a cathedral of flesh, a geometry of madness, a thing so vast and wrong that your mind simply… gives up. You are weeping. You are laughing. You are nothing. And you are grateful, because in the last fraction of a second before everything ends, you understand that to be noticed by something this immense is the closest to meaning your life has ever had.

Cthulhu is the most famous creation of H.P. Lovecraft, introduced in the 1928 short story 'The Call of Cthulhu.' He is a Great Old One — an ancient, god-like entity that came to Earth from the stars millions of years before humanity evolved. He lies 'dead but dreaming' in the sunken city of R'lyeh somewhere in the South Pacific, waiting for the stars to align correctly so he can rise again. His physical form is impossible to properly describe — a mountain of green, gelatinous flesh with an octopus-like head, bat wings, and clawed appendages — but Lovecraft emphasizes that any human who truly sees Cthulhu is destroyed by the experience, not physically, but psychologically. Sanity shatters in the presence of the truly alien.

Cthulhu represents the core idea of cosmic horror: that humanity is not important. We are a temporary accident on a tiny rock, and the universe is full of beings so vast, so ancient, so utterly indifferent that the discovery of our existence would destroy everything we believe about ourselves. Cthulhu is not evil. He is not interested in human souls or worship. His cultists serve him hoping for mercy when he wakes, but Cthulhu will no more notice humanity's destruction than you notice the bacteria you kill when you walk across a lawn. This is the true horror — not that the universe hates us, but that it does not care at all.

Emergence

Cthulhu debuted in the February 1928 issue of 'Weird Tales' magazine in H.P. Lovecraft's 'The Call of Cthulhu.' The story is presented as a found document — the notes of Francis Wayland Thurston, who inherits his great-uncle's research into a worldwide cult that worships a sleeping god. Through artist Henry Wilcox's fever-dream sculptures, Inspector Legrasse's raid on a Louisiana bayou cult, and the tragic log of the crew of the Emma (who accidentally raised R'lyeh from the Pacific), Thurston assembles the horrifying truth: Cthulhu is real, his psychic emanations influence human dreams, and his awakening is inevitable. Lovecraft himself wrote Cthulhu as part of a shared mythology, encouraging his writer friends to reference the creature and the forbidden Necronomicon in their own stories.

Form

Lovecraft's description is deliberately destabilizing: 'A monster of vaguely anthropoid outline, but with an octopus-like head whose face was a mass of feelers, a scaly, rubbery-looking body, prodigious claws on hind and fore feet, and long, narrow wings behind.' The key word is 'vaguely' — Cthulhu defies clear visualization. His body is described as gelatinous, green, mountainous in scale, and semi-corporeal (the sailors' ship passes THROUGH him and the wound instantly heals). He drips with cosmic slime and smells of ancient, rotting seas. His form is a compromise between his true extradimensional nature and the limited three-dimensional space humans can perceive. The popular image of a winged squid-man is a simplification of something far stranger.

Powers

Telepathic influence on a planetary scale — his dreams reach sensitive humans worldwide, driving artists to madness and inspiration. Immortality and apparent invulnerability to physical harm. His mere presence causes insanity in humans who perceive him directly. He can manipulate geometry and physics (the non-Euclidean architecture of R'lyeh is an extension of his nature). His awakening would likely end human civilization as a side effect, not an intention. He and his fellow Great Old Ones possess knowledge of cosmic secrets that predate the universe. In some extended Mythos works, Cthulhu is a high priest, not the supreme power — suggesting even he serves something greater.

Behavior & Habitat

Cthulhu's behavior is defined by absence — he is sleeping, not acting. The horror is anticipation. Even in his dreams, his passive influence reshapes human culture, creating cults, inspiring art, and leaking forbidden knowledge into the collective unconscious. When briefly awakened (as in the encounter with the crew of the Alert in Lovecraft's story), he does not pursue or fight — he simply begins to reform as the city sinks back beneath the waves. His cultists are not commanded; they interpret dreams and fragments of lore, building their own monstrous practices around an entity that has never spoken to them directly.

Weaknesses & Wards

Cthulhu is 'dead but dreaming' — he is currently inactive and trapped in sunken R'lyeh, unable to act directly on the world. The stars must be 'right' for him to fully awaken, and this alignment is astronomically rare. In Lovecraft's story, a steamship ramming his head causes temporary disruption (though he immediately regenerates). Some extended mythos works suggest the Elder Sign offers protection. His greatest weakness may be his passivity — he does not appear to be in any hurry, and his plans unfold on geological timescales.

Field Notes

⁙ Lovecraft himself pronounced Cthulhu as 'Khlûl'-hloo' — he said the spelling was an attempt to represent a sound the human vocal apparatus cannot properly produce.

⁙ Cthulhu's daughter, Cthylla, was invented by Brian Lumley in the 1970s as part of the expanded Mythos — she exists to bear Cthulhu's reincarnation if he is ever destroyed.

⁙ The famous Cthulhu idol sketch (a squat, winged, squid-headed figure on a pedestal) was drawn by Lovecraft himself in 1934.

⁙ In 'The Call of Cthulhu,' the creature is only active for about one page — the rest of the story is the slow, dreadful accumulation of evidence.

⁙ Cthulhu has an electoral platform: the slogan 'Why vote for the lesser evil?' with Cthulhu as a satirical presidential candidate dates back to at least the 1990s.

Cultural Significance

Cthulhu has transcended his pulp fiction origins to become one of the most recognizable monsters in global culture. The adjective 'Lovecraftian' describes an entire mode of horror: cosmic, indifferent, sanity-eroding. Cthulhu's image appears on everything from plush toys to political satire (the 'Cthulhu for President' phenomenon on the satirical platform is that choosing the lesser evil is still choosing evil). His name is invoked in discussions of existential risk, artificial intelligence dangers, and climate change — the idea that humanity might summon forces it cannot control. The Cthulhu Mythos, collaboratively built by Lovecraft and his circle, is the foundation of modern cosmic horror and has influenced authors from Stephen King to China Miéville.

Modern Interpretations

Cthulhu has been adapted, parodied, and commercialized to an extent Lovecraft could never have imagined. Cthulhu appears in tabletop RPGs ('Call of Cthulhu' by Chaosium), video games ('Bloodborne' draws heavily from Lovecraft, while 'Call of Cthulhu' directly adapts the story), board games, and countless films. South Park's Cthulhu episodes turned him into a joke. The internet has produced endless Cthulhu memes ('Why is Cthulhu a good conversationalist? Because he's a great Old One'). Yet the core terror remains potent: in an era of climate crisis and AI, the idea that humanity is a temporary speck in an indifferent cosmos feels more relevant than ever. Lovecraft's virulent racism — which deeply informed his fiction — has prompted a necessary reckoning in how we engage with his work.
