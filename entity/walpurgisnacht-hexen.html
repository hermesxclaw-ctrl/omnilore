Who It Is

Imagine standing on the windswept summit of the Brocken as the clock strikes midnight on April 30, surrounded by the cackling of unseen voices and the flicker of distant bonfires. This is the night when the Walpurgisnacht Hexen rise, not just as figments of folklore, but as living symbols of humanity's oldest fears and desires. Are they the remnants of a forgotten goddess cult, or the victims of a paranoid Church? The answer may lie in the very mountain itself.

The Walpurgisnacht Hexen are characterized by their chaotic, ecstatic behavior during the sabbath. They are said to dance wildly around bonfires, often in a counter-clockwise direction, and to engage in feasting, drinking, and blasphemous rituals. They renew their pact with the devil, who presides over the gathering, often in the form of a black goat or a man with horns. They are also known to play tricks on humans, such as leading travelers astray, souring milk, or causing illness. Name & Tongue

Etymology — The term 'Walpurgisnacht' derives from Saint Walpurga, an 8th-century Anglo-Saxon missionary and abbess whose feast day, May 1, coincided with the traditional Germanic spring festival. The name 'Hexen' is the German plural for 'witches,' and 'Brocken' refers to the highest peak of the Harz Mountains

Pronunciation — VAHL-poor-gis-nahkt HEK-sen

Aliases — Witches of Walpurgis Night, Brocken Revelers, Hexen des Brocken, Walpurgiswitches, Sabbath Spirits of the Harz

Powers
Flight

The witches can fly through the air on broomsticks, pitchforks, or animals, often with the aid of a magical ointment. This ability allows them to travel long distances to reach the Brocken for the sabbath. The flight is often depicted as a wild, chaotic ride, accompanied by shrieks and laughter.

Limit:

Shapeshifting

They can transform themselves into animals, particularly cats, owls, wolves, or hares, to spy on humans or evade capture. This ability is often used to cause mischief or to attend the sabbath unnoticed. In some tales, they can also transform others into beasts.

Limit:

Weather Manipulation

The witches are believed to control storms, hail, and rain, which they use to destroy crops or cause harm. This power is particularly feared by farmers, who might blame a bad harvest on a witch's curse. They are also said to be able to raise winds to aid their flight.

Limit:

Cursing and Hexing

They can cast spells on humans, livestock, and property, causing illness, death, infertility, or misfortune. These curses are often enacted through rituals, such as tying knots in a cord or making a wax figure. The only defense is counter-magic, such as iron horseshoes or blessed herbs.

Limit:

Illusion and Deception

The witches can create illusions to deceive humans, such as making a barren field appear fertile or a path seem safe when it is dangerous. They can also make themselves appear beautiful or young to lure victims. This power is central to their trickster nature, blurring the line between reality and fantasy.

Accounts I
The Brocken SabbathGoethe's Faust I

1808

In Goethe's Faust, Mephistopheles takes Faust to the Brocken on Walpurgis Night to witness the witches' sabbath. The scene is a chaotic, sensual, and grotesque spectacle, with witches dancing, singing, and engaging in orgiastic revelry. Faust encounters a beautiful young witch, who represents temptation, but the experience is ultimately hollow, leading Faust to a deeper existential crisis. The sabbath serves as a metaphor for the dark, irrational forces within the human soul. Faust is momentarily distracted from his despair, but the sabbath's emptiness deepens his sense of alienation. The scene also reinforces the Romantic fascination with the supernatural and the sublime, influencing later depictions of Walpurgisnacht.

Accounts II
The Farmer's RevengeGrimm's Deutsche Sagen

1816

A farmer in the Harz region suspects his neighbor of being a witch who attends the Walpurgisnacht sabbath. He follows her to the Brocken, where he sees her dancing with the devil. He returns home and, with the help of a priest, performs a ritual to break her power. The next day, the witch is found dead in her bed, and the farmer's crops flourish. The story serves as a cautionary tale about the dangers of witchcraft and the power of faith. The witch is destroyed, and the farmer is celebrated as a hero. The story reinforces the belief in the reality of witchcraft and the efficacy of religious counter-magic.

The May BrideOral tradition, collected by Karl Bartsch

1879

A young woman is chosen as the 'May Bride' to be offered to the witches on Walpurgis Night to ensure a good harvest. She is left alone on the Brocken, but instead of being harmed, she is taken by the witches to their sabbath, where she is taught the secrets of herbs and healing. She returns home with knowledge that makes her a wise woman, but she is forever marked by her experience, unable to fully rejoin human society. The story reflects the dual nature of the witches as both dangerous and bestowers of knowledge. The May Bride becomes a healer, but she is ostracized by her community. The village prospers, but the bride's fate is ambiguous, suggesting the cost of supernatural contact.
