Walpurgisnacht Hexen

Witches of Walpurgis Night, Brocken Revelers

also known asWitches of Walpurgis NightBrocken RevelersHexen des BrockenWalpurgiswitchesSabbath Spirits of the Harz
TypeWitches / Sabbath Spirits
CultureGerman (Harz Mountains / Brocken)
Who It Is
Form

The Walpurgisnacht Hexen are typically depicted as old, haggard women with wild, unkempt hair, often riding on broomsticks, pitchforks, or goats. They are commonly shown wearing tattered black or dark-colored clothing, sometimes with a red or green shawl, and carrying a staff or a lantern. In many illustrations, they have exaggerated facial features, such as hooked noses, warts, and sharp teeth, reflecting the medieval stereotype of the witch. However, some Romantic-era depictions, such as those by Goethe, present them as more alluring and youthful, blending beauty with menace. The appearance varies widely across regional traditions, with some sources describing them as spectral figures that can shapeshift into animals like cats, owls, or wolves.

Powers

Flight The witches can fly through the air on broomsticks, pitchforks, or animals, often with the aid of a magical ointment. This ability allows them to travel long distances to reach the Brocken for the sabbath. The flight is often depicted as a wild, chaotic ride, accompanied by shrieks and laughter.

Shapeshifting They can transform themselves into animals, particularly cats, owls, wolves, or hares, to spy on humans or evade capture. This ability is often used to cause mischief or to attend the sabbath unnoticed. In some tales, they can also transform others into beasts.

Weather Manipulation The witches are believed to control storms, hail, and rain, which they use to destroy crops or cause harm. This power is particularly feared by farmers, who might blame a bad harvest on a witch's curse. They are also said to be able to raise winds to aid their flight.

Cursing and Hexing They can cast spells on humans, livestock, and property, causing illness, death, infertility, or misfortune. These curses are often enacted through rituals, such as tying knots in a cord or making a wax figure. The only defense is counter-magic, such as iron horseshoes or blessed herbs.

Illusion and Deception The witches can create illusions to deceive humans, such as making a barren field appear fertile or a path seem safe when it is dangerous. They can also make themselves appear beautiful or young to lure victims. This power is central to their trickster nature, blurring the line between reality and fantasy.

Accounts

In Goethe's Faust, Mephistopheles takes Faust to the Brocken on Walpurgis Night to witness the witches' sabbath. The scene is a chaotic, sensual, and grotesque spectacle, with witches dancing, singing, and engaging in orgiastic revelry. Faust encounters a beautiful young witch, who represents temptation, but the experience is ultimately hollow, leading Faust to a deeper existential crisis. The sabbath serves as a metaphor for the dark, irrational forces within the human soul.

A farmer in the Harz region suspects his neighbor of being a witch who attends the Walpurgisnacht sabbath. He follows her to the Brocken, where he sees her dancing with the devil. He returns home and, with the help of a priest, performs a ritual to break her power. The next day, the witch is found dead in her bed, and the farmer's crops flourish. The story serves as a cautionary tale about the dangers of witchcraft and the power of faith.

A young woman is chosen as the 'May Bride' to be offered to the witches on Walpurgis Night to ensure a good harvest. She is left alone on the Brocken, but instead of being harmed, she is taken by the witches to their sabbath, where she is taught the secrets of herbs and healing. She returns home with knowledge that makes her a wise woman, but she is forever marked by her experience, unable to fully rejoin human society. The story reflects the dual nature of the witches as both dangerous and bestowers of knowledge.

Modern Interpretations

Literature Faust I Johann Wolfgang von Goethe 1808 Primary literary source Goethe's Walpurgisnacht scene is the most famous depiction, cementing the Brocken as the witches' meeting place and influencing all subsequent portrayals. Opera Faust Charles Gounod 1859 Adaptation Gounod's opera omits the Walpurgisnacht scene, focusing instead on the romance, but the ballet music from the opera includes a 'Witches' Dance' that evokes the sabbath. Film Walpurgisnacht Georg Wilhelm Pabst 1935 Cinematic adaptation This film, part of a trilogy, uses the Walpurgisnacht theme to explore psychological horror, though it is not a direct adaptation of the folklore. Music Walpurgisnacht Felix Mendelssohn 1832 Cantata Mendelssohn composed a cantata based on Goethe's scene, which was later revised and performed, contributing to the Romantic musical interpretation of the witches. Festival Walpurgisnacht Festival in the Harz Local communities Contemporary Cultural reenactment Modern celebrations in the Harz include costumed witches, bonfires, and theatrical performances, blending tourism with folk tradition.

Scholarly Debates
Origin of the Myth
The Walpurgisnacht witches are a survival of pre-Christian fertility rites and goddess worship. ⇌ The myth is a late medieval Christian invention, created to demonize folk practices and justify witch hunts.
Nature of the Witches
The witches are malevolent, devil-worshipping beings who cause harm to humans and livestock. ⇌ The witches are benign or even beneficial figures, associated with healing and agricultural fertility, later demonized by the Church.
Location of the Sabbath
The Brocken is the authentic and original meeting place, as attested by local folklore. ⇌ The Brocken association is a literary invention, popularized by Goethe, and other sites like the Venusberg or Horselberg were equally significant.
What We Don't Know

Further research is needed to explore the regional variations of Walpurgisnacht celebrations outside Germany, particularly in Eastern Europe and Scandinavia. The role of gender in the myth, especially the predominance of female witches, warrants deeper analysis. Additionally, the influence of the Walpurgisnacht myth on modern neo-pagan movements and popular culture, such as in anime and video games, remains understudied. The historical accuracy of witch trial records referencing Walpurgisnacht is also a gap, as many confessions were obtained under torture.

A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine standing on the windswept summit of the Brocken as the clock strikes midnight on April 30, surrounded by the cackling of unseen voices and the flicker of distant bonfires. This is the night when the Walpurgisnacht Hexen rise, not just as figments of folklore, but as living symbols of humanity's oldest fears and desires. Are they the remnants of a forgotten goddess cult, or the victims of a paranoid Church? The answer may lie in the very mountain itself.
