Who It Is

Imagine a god whose anger feels like the sudden heave of the ocean floor—a force that can lift islands, shatter walls, and keep a single man from seeing home for ten relentless years. That is Poseidon: not just the sea made human, but the deep foundations of the world given will, memory, and grudges. To follow his stories is to trace the fault lines of Greek myth itself, where every wave and tremor might be the echo of a divine decision.

Attested: Poseidon is volatile, proud, and easily angered, yet capable of generosity and protection. In Homer, he is quick to take offense at slights (e.g., Odysseus blinding Polyphemus, Ajax’s hubris) and holds long-lasting grudges. He is competitive with Zeus and other gods, participating in conspiracies and contests. He can be protective of sailors and cities that honor him, rallying Greek forces in the Iliad and aiding heroes such as Theseus. Name & Tongue

Etymology — Attested form Ποσειδῶν (Poseidōn) in Ancient Greek. Scholarly proposals link the first element to πόσις (posis, "husband") and the second to a pre-Greek or Indo-European root for "earth" (e.g., *dā-), yielding interpretations such as "husband of the earth" or "lord of the earth." Powers
Control of the sea

Attested: Poseidon can stir or calm the sea, raise storms, and create waves. In Homer, he lashes the sea into fury against Odysseus and can also grant smooth sailing to favored mortals.

Limit: His actions are sometimes constrained by Zeus’s overarching authority and by decisions of the other Olympians; he does not act entirely without regard for divine consensus.

Earthquakes (Earth-shaker)

Attested: Poseidon can shake the earth, topple walls, and cause seismic destruction. Homer repeatedly calls him Ennosigaios (Earth-shaker), and he is credited with destroying the wall built around the Greek camp at Troy.

Limit: No explicit textual limits, but his use of earthquakes is episodic and often tied to specific conflicts or punishments.

Horses and horsemanship

Attested: Poseidon is associated with horses, sometimes credited with creating the first horse and with bestowing horses on mortals. He bears epithets such as Hippios and is linked to chariot races and aristocratic equestrian culture.

Limit: His equine role is more prominent in certain regions (e.g., Arcadia, Thessaly) and less emphasized in others.

Storms and weather at sea

Attested: Poseidon can summon storms, winds, and dangerous conditions at sea, often as punishment. In the Odyssey, he sends storms that drive Odysseus off course.

Limit: Wind control overlaps with other deities (e.g., Aeolus), and the exact division of meteorological powers is not systematically defined.

Shaping and destroying land

Attested: Poseidon can raise islands, split continents, and destroy walls. He is credited with creating certain islands and with destroying the wall around Troy.

Limit: Such feats are rare and tied to major mythic events rather than everyday phenomena.

Protection and patronage of cities and sailors

Attested: Poseidon can protect cities that honor him, grant safe passage to sailors, and support armies (e.g., the Achaeans in the Iliad). He is invoked as a protector in maritime contexts.

Limit: Protection is conditional on proper worship and offerings; he can withdraw favor or turn hostile.

Shape-shifting and epiphanies

Attested: Poseidon appears in different forms, including as a man, a horse, or in other guises, especially in myths involving Demeter and certain mortal women.

Limit: Shape-shifting is episodic and tied to specific narratives, not a constant feature.

Genealogical power (father of heroes and monsters)

Attested: Poseidon fathers numerous offspring, including heroes (Theseus), kings (Neleus, Pelias), giants (Otus and Ephialtes), and monsters (Polyphemus, Pegasus via Medusa). His progeny often embody aspects of the sea, strength, or transgression.

Limit: Genealogies vary by source; not all claimed offspring are universally accepted.

Accounts I
Birth and division of the cosmosHesiod, Theogony; Apollodorus, Bibliotheca

Archaic (Hesiod) and early Imperial (Apollodorus)

Poseidon is born to Cronus and Rhea and, like his siblings, is swallowed by Cronus. Zeus is hidden and later forces Cronus to disgorge his children. After the Titanomachy, Zeus, Poseidon, and Hades draw lots to divide the cosmos: Zeus receives the sky, Poseidon the sea, and Hades the underworld. Poseidon becomes lord of the sea and an Olympian god. Establishes Poseidon’s cosmic domain and his status as one of the three major gods. Sets the framework for later conflicts and collaborations among the brothers.

Conspiracy against Zeus and the walls of TroyHomer, Iliad; Apollodorus, Bibliotheca; later mythographic summaries

Archaic epic and later mythography

Poseidon, Hera, and Apollo conspire to overthrow Zeus. The plot fails, and Zeus punishes them. Poseidon and Apollo are forced to serve the mortal king Laomedon of Troy, building the city’s walls. When Laomedon refuses to pay them, Poseidon sends a sea monster to ravage Troy, while Apollo sends a plague. Demonstrates Poseidon’s willingness to challenge Zeus and his capacity for vengeance against mortals who break oaths. The sea monster episode becomes part of the broader mythic prehistory of the Trojan War.

Poseidon and OdysseusHomer, Odyssey; Apollodorus, Bibliotheca

Archaic epic and later mythography

Odysseus blinds the Cyclops Polyphemus, who is Poseidon’s son. Polyphemus prays to Poseidon to avenge him. Poseidon responds by sending storms, wrecking ships, and delaying Odysseus’ return to Ithaca for years. Despite the other gods’ eventual support for Odysseus, Poseidon’s wrath persists until Odysseus finally reaches home. Illustrates Poseidon’s capacity for long-term vengeance and his control over maritime travel. The story shapes the entire narrative arc of the Odyssey.

Accounts II
Contest for AthensPausanias, Description of Greece; Apollodorus, Bibliotheca; various local traditions

Classical and later

Poseidon and Athena compete to become the patron deity of the city that will be called Athens. Poseidon strikes the rock of the Acropolis with his trident, producing either a saltwater spring or a horse (depending on the version). Athena offers the olive tree. The citizens (or the gods) judge Athena’s gift more beneficial, and she becomes the city’s patron. Poseidon remains honored but loses the primary role. Explains the dual presence of Poseidon and Athena in Athenian cult and the city’s name. Highlights Poseidon’s association with horses and the sea, and his competitive relationship with Athena.

Poseidon and Demeter (the horse and Arion)Pausanias, Description of Greece; Apollodorus, Bibliotheca

Classical and later

Poseidon pursues Demeter, who is unwilling. Demeter transforms into a mare to escape him, but Poseidon becomes a stallion and mates with her. From this union are born the divine horse Arion and, in some versions, a mysterious goddess known as the Mistress. The story links Poseidon to horses and to chthonic fertility. Reinforces Poseidon’s equine associations and his capacity for violent, coercive sexuality. Connects him to Arcadian cults and to chthonic aspects of Demeter.

Poseidon’s offspring and heroic genealogiesApollodorus, Bibliotheca; Pausanias; Homer, Odyssey

Archaic and later

Poseidon fathers numerous children with goddesses, nymphs, and mortal women, including Theseus (via Aethra), Triton and Rhode (via Amphitrite), Polyphemus (via Thoosa), Neleus and Pelias (via Tyro), and others. These offspring become heroes, kings, and monsters, shaping local myths and political identities. Establishes Poseidon as a major genealogical source of heroic and royal lineages. His offspring often embody maritime or violent traits, reinforcing his thematic associations.
