Who It Is

The Hibagon is Japan's most famous Bigfoot-like cryptid, reported from the forested slopes of Mount Hiba in Hiroshima Prefecture. Sightings peaked between 1970 and 1974, when over a dozen credible witnesses -- including farmers, schoolchildren, and forestry workers -- reported encounters. The creature is described as approximately 5 feet tall (smaller than American Bigfoot), covered in dark brown or black hair, with a triangular, somewhat gorilla-like face, broad shoulders, and a distinctive shuffling gait. Unlike Sasquatch, the Hibagon's face is said to be dark brown rather than bare-skinned, and its feet appear more human-like. The most famous sighting occurred in 1974, when a 29-year-old man working at a mountain lodge reported seeing a hairy, apelike creature walking upright across a rice paddy in broad daylight. Footprints measuring approximately 10 inches were recovered and cast. Subsequent investigations by local authorities, university researchers, and the media failed to locate the creature, though several more footprints were found. Skeptics propose misidentified Japanese macaques (snow monkeys), Asiatic black bears standing upright, or a hoax -- though the Japanese macaque is much smaller, and bears are well-known to rural Japanese communities. Some researchers have linked the Hibagon to historical accounts of the 'Yamako' (mountain child) in Japanese folklore. The creature is Japan's primary contribution to the global Bigfoot phenomenon and remains a fixture of Japanese cryptid and pop culture.

Form

Attested descriptions from eyewitness reports and compiled cryptozoological sources portray Hibagon as a relatively small, ape-like humanoid, typically around 1.5 meters (5 feet) tall, sometimes up to 1.8 meters in exceptional accounts. Its body is covered in dark hair—variously described as black, dark brown, or reddish-brown—with occasional reports of white patches on the chest, arms, or feet. Some accounts emphasize stark contrast: a ‘black creature with white clay hands and large white feet’. The head is often said to be disproportionately large, sometimes inverted-triangular in shape, with bristly hair up to 5 cm long. The face is described as either human-like with a thin coat of hair or more gorilla-like, with a snub or flat nose, protruding teeth, and large, glaring eyes. Witnesses frequently mention an intense, foul odor likened to rotting flesh, manure, or a septic tank. These traits are attested in multiple secondary sources; precise details vary between reports and are therefore partially reconstructed from overlapping testimonies.

Powers

Stealth and evasiveness Hibagon is consistently reported as able to appear suddenly and vanish into forested terrain, evading search parties and leaving only ambiguous traces such as footprints. Its ability to avoid capture or clear documentation is central to its cryptid status, though this may reflect human limitations rather than any supernatural power. Modern cryptozoological reports (1970–1982) as compiled by Cryptid Vault, Cryptid Wiki, and Japanese newspaper retrospectives. Sightings are brief and often at a distance; no sustained observation or tracking has yielded conclusive evidence. Its ‘stealth’ may be no greater than that of ordinary wildlife in dense forest. Some accounts describe Hibagon as clumsy or comical in appearance, which would contradict an image of extraordinary stealth. Skeptical interpretations argue that the failure to capture the creature simply indicates misidentification or non-existence.

Bipedal and quadrupedal locomotion Witnesses report Hibagon moving both on two legs and on all fours, sometimes hopping like a monkey. This dual-mode locomotion aligns with ape-like movement patterns and contributes to its ambiguous classification between human and non-human. Eyewitness narratives summarized in Cryptid Wiki and Encyclopaedia of Cryptozoology timelines. Reports are anecdotal and vary in detail; no biomechanical analysis is possible from available data. If Hibagon were a Japanese macaque or bear, its typical locomotion patterns would differ from some descriptions; however, brief sightings under stress can distort perception of movement.

Intense odor emission Multiple witnesses describe a powerful, unpleasant smell associated with Hibagon, likened to rotting flesh, manure, or a septic tank. This odor is treated as a distinctive trait, similar to reports of other hairy humanoid cryptids. Encyclopaedia of Cryptozoology description; Cryptid Vault summary of witness reports. Odor descriptions are subjective and may be influenced by environmental factors (e.g., nearby decay, agricultural waste). No chemical analysis exists. Some sightings do not mention odor at all, suggesting either variability in the phenomenon or selective reporting.

Silence or unusual vocalization Several sources note that Hibagon produces no vocalizations, remaining eerily silent even when observed at close range. Other reports, however, claim sounds reminiscent of a dolphin. This ambiguity contributes to its uncanny character. Cryptid Wiki description; Encyclopaedia of Cryptozoology behavioral notes. The absence of sound in some encounters may be coincidental; the ‘dolphin-like’ vocalization is reported in only a few secondary sources and cannot be verified. Silence and distinctive vocalization are mutually contradictory traits; the discrepancy likely reflects differing witness experiences or later embellishment.

Symbolic power as regional mascot Beyond any physical abilities, Hibagon has acquired cultural power as a symbol of Mount Hiba and Hiroshima Prefecture. Its image is used in tourism, local branding, and media, functioning as a playful embodiment of mystery and rural identity. Cryptid Vault discussion of tourism; Japanese newspaper retrospectives on Hibagon’s role in local culture. This is a cultural, not literal, ‘power’; it depends on human reception and may wane over time. The benign mascot image contrasts with earlier media portrayals emphasizing fear and panic, revealing a shift from threatening unknown to commodified curiosity.

Accounts

Beginning around 1970, farmers, forestry workers, and drivers in the Saijō and Shōbara areas reported encounters with a small, dark, ape-like figure near Mount Hiba. One early report describes a calf-sized creature crossing in front of a light truck near the Rokunohara River and dam, appearing too large to be a monkey yet too small to be a bear. Other witnesses saw a bristly, foul-smelling humanoid with a large head and glaring eyes in forest clearings and along rural roads. The creature fled when approached, leaving behind footprints of unusual size and shape. Local newspapers covered the sightings extensively, and the town council reportedly allocated funds to investigate, organizing search parties that failed to capture or definitively identify the creature.

Between 1974 and 1975, additional sightings and physical traces attributed to Hibagon were reported. On multiple occasions, footprints ranging from 14 to 30 cm in length and 7 to 23 cm in width were found in forested areas. Some prints were identified as human or monkey, but others were judged by local officials to be neither. On December 16, 1970 (referenced in later timelines), town officials and police made a plaster mold of a footprint, which was preserved and later displayed at Bingo-Saijō Station. Witnesses during this period continued to describe a small, hairy humanoid that never harmed humans and fled when seen.

Decades after the original sightings, Japanese newspapers revisited the Hibagon story, marking fifty years since the first reports. Journalists traveled to Mount Hiba’s ‘legendary land’, interviewed local residents, and examined the hypothesis that Hibagon was simply a Japanese macaque seen under unusual conditions. These retrospectives balanced nostalgia for the era of mysterious sightings with critical evaluation of the evidence, highlighting how the creature had become part of local identity and tourism while likely lacking any extraordinary zoological basis.

A way to hold it in your mind

On the mist-wrapped slopes of Mount Hiba, where cedar forests swallow sound and narrow roads thread between fields, witnesses in the 1970s began telling the same unnerving story: a small, shaggy figure with a too-large head and glaring eyes stepping out of the trees, reeking of rot, watching for a heartbeat—and then vanishing. They called it Hibagon, Japan’s answer to Bigfoot, but its scale, smell, and strangely human silhouette made it feel less like a giant and more like an unsettling neighbor at the edge of vision. Whether misseen macaque, media-born monster, or something still unnamed, Hibagon sits at the crossroads of rural anxiety, atomic-era imagination, and the global hunger for creatures that might exist just beyond the reach of proof.
