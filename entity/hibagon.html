Who It Is

On the mist-wrapped slopes of Mount Hiba, where cedar forests swallow sound and narrow roads thread between fields, witnesses in the 1970s began telling the same unnerving story: a small, shaggy figure with a too-large head and glaring eyes stepping out of the trees, reeking of rot, watching for a heartbeat—and then vanishing. They called it Hibagon, Japan’s answer to Bigfoot, but its scale, smell, and strangely human silhouette made it feel less like a giant and more like an unsettling neighbor at the edge of vision. Whether misseen macaque, media-born monster, or something still unnamed, Hibagon sits at the crossroads of rural anxiety, atomic-era imagination, and the global hunger for creatures that might exist just beyond the reach of proof.

Eyewitness accounts and compiled timelines consistently describe Hibagon as reclusive and non-lethal. The creature is said to flee when confronted by humans, even when witnesses were armed or in groups. Reports mention it moving both bipedally and on all fours, sometimes ‘hopping like a monkey’. No credible account describes Hibagon attacking or injuring humans; at most, it startles observers and causes local panic. Powers
Stealth and evasiveness

Hibagon is consistently reported as able to appear suddenly and vanish into forested terrain, evading search parties and leaving only ambiguous traces such as footprints. Its ability to avoid capture or clear documentation is central to its cryptid status, though this may reflect human limitations rather than any supernatural power.

Limit: Sightings are brief and often at a distance; no sustained observation or tracking has yielded conclusive evidence. Its ‘stealth’ may be no greater than that of ordinary wildlife in dense forest.

Bipedal and quadrupedal locomotion

Witnesses report Hibagon moving both on two legs and on all fours, sometimes hopping like a monkey. This dual-mode locomotion aligns with ape-like movement patterns and contributes to its ambiguous classification between human and non-human.

Limit: Reports are anecdotal and vary in detail; no biomechanical analysis is possible from available data.

Intense odor emission

Multiple witnesses describe a powerful, unpleasant smell associated with Hibagon, likened to rotting flesh, manure, or a septic tank. This odor is treated as a distinctive trait, similar to reports of other hairy humanoid cryptids.

Limit: Odor descriptions are subjective and may be influenced by environmental factors (e.g., nearby decay, agricultural waste). No chemical analysis exists.

Silence or unusual vocalization

Several sources note that Hibagon produces no vocalizations, remaining eerily silent even when observed at close range. Other reports, however, claim sounds reminiscent of a dolphin. This ambiguity contributes to its uncanny character.

Limit: The absence of sound in some encounters may be coincidental; the ‘dolphin-like’ vocalization is reported in only a few secondary sources and cannot be verified.

Symbolic power as regional mascot

Beyond any physical abilities, Hibagon has acquired cultural power as a symbol of Mount Hiba and Hiroshima Prefecture. Its image is used in tourism, local branding, and media, functioning as a playful embodiment of mystery and rural identity.

Limit: This is a cultural, not literal, ‘power’; it depends on human reception and may wane over time.

The 1970 Saijō–Shōbara sighting waveCompiled from Japanese local reports referenced in Wikipedia, Cryptid Vault, and fan encyclopedias.

1970–1972 (modern era)

Beginning around 1970, farmers, forestry workers, and drivers in the Saijō and Shōbara areas reported encounters with a small, dark, ape-like figure near Mount Hiba. One early report describes a calf-sized creature crossing in front of a light truck near the Rokunohara River and dam, appearing too large to be a monkey yet too small to be a bear. Other witnesses saw a bristly, foul-smelling humanoid with a large head and glaring eyes in forest clearings and along rural roads. The creature fled when approached, leaving behind footprints of unusual size and shape. Local newspapers covered the sightings extensively, and the town council reportedly allocated funds to investigate, organizing search parties that failed to capture or definitively identify the creature. The wave of reports generated regional panic and national media attention, cementing Hibagon as a named cryptid. It prompted official and unofficial investigations, the creation of plaster casts of footprints, and the emergence of Hibagon as a subject of cryptozoological interest. The 1974–1975 footprint and sighting clusterEncyclopaedia of Cryptozoology timeline; Cryptid Wiki sightings section.

1974–1975 (modern era)

Between 1974 and 1975, additional sightings and physical traces attributed to Hibagon were reported. On multiple occasions, footprints ranging from 14 to 30 cm in length and 7 to 23 cm in width were found in forested areas. Some prints were identified as human or monkey, but others were judged by local officials to be neither. On December 16, 1970 (referenced in later timelines), town officials and police made a plaster mold of a footprint, which was preserved and later displayed at Bingo-Saijō Station. Witnesses during this period continued to describe a small, hairy humanoid that never harmed humans and fled when seen. The preservation of plaster casts provided tangible artifacts that sustained interest in Hibagon beyond the initial sighting wave. The continued reports reinforced the creature’s status as a regional mystery and encouraged speculative theories about its nature.

Media retrospectives and the 50-year reflectionMainichi Shimbun and Asahi Shimbun feature articles (2020, 2025).

2020–2025 (contemporary era)

Decades after the original sightings, Japanese newspapers revisited the Hibagon story, marking fifty years since the first reports. Journalists traveled to Mount Hiba’s ‘legendary land’, interviewed local residents, and examined the hypothesis that Hibagon was simply a Japanese macaque seen under unusual conditions. These retrospectives balanced nostalgia for the era of mysterious sightings with critical evaluation of the evidence, highlighting how the creature had become part of local identity and tourism while likely lacking any extraordinary zoological basis. The articles reinforced Hibagon’s place in Japanese cultural memory, reframing it as a charming regional legend rather than a pressing mystery. They also contributed to the scholarly and popular consensus that naturalistic explanations (macaques, bears, misperceptions) are more plausible than a new species.
