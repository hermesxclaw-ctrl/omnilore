Who It Is

At the eastern foot of Mount Hiei, where the mountain's springs cut down toward Lake Biwa, macaques still loiter around vermilion gates as if they own the place — and in a sense they do. The kami who lives there, Ōyamakui no Kami, is the Great Mountain Stake, a deity who holds the mountain in place, brews its rice into sake, and sends monkeys as his messengers. For more than thirteen centuries he has been both a local guardian of a single sacred peak and the protector of Japan's most powerful Buddhist monastery, Enryakuji, caught at the crossroads of Shinto and Buddhism.

In early myth, Ōyamakui is genealogical rather than narrative — no speeches or deeds are recorded, unlike Susanoo or Ōkuninushi. Shrine tradition and medieval setsuwa portray him as a stable, protective, somewhat stern mountain master who 'holds' the land, controls water flowing from Hiei to Lake Biwa and Kyoto, and punishes trespass or impurity on the mountain. As Sannō Gongen he is a guardian of the Dharma (gohōjin), protector of Enryakuji and by extension the imperial capital (kimon - demon gate - northeast of Kyoto). Powers
Mountain Stabilization and Land Holding

Etymological and ritual power to 'stake' or 'pin' the mountain, preventing landslides and earthquakes, stabilizing the land of Ōmi and the capital. Invoked in jichinsai (ground-pacification) rites for construction near Hiei.

Limit: Bound to Mount Hiei; power weakens with distance unless via bunrei. Requires proper ritual purity; defilement of mountain angers kami.

Water and Agricultural Fertility

Controls springs, rainfall, and irrigation from Hiei to the paddies of Sakamoto and Lake Biwa basin. Ensures rice growth and prevents drought/flood. Closely linked to Toshigami (year/harvest deity) parentage.

Limit: Seasonal; must be petitioned at spring rice-planting festivals. Competing water kami (Suijin) in other basins not under his control.

Sake Brewing Patronage (Miki no Kami)

Patron of sake, mirin, and fermentation. Brewers offer hiyoshi sake and enshrine Ōyamakui in breweries to ensure successful fermentation and prevent spoilage. Hiyoshi Taisha's Sannō Matsuri includes offering of sacred sake.

Limit: Requires offering and purity of brewery; does not guarantee quality without craft.

Apotropaic Warding and Demon-Gate Protection

As guardian of Kyoto's kimon (northeast demon gate), wards evil spirits, disease, and misfortune. Monkey messenger acts as expeller; masaru charms (masharu = ward off evil) placed on roofs and gates. Historically invoked against epidemics.

Limit: Protection is spatially oriented (northeast); requires placement of charm or shrine talisman. Destroyed in 1571 burning, seen as temporary loss of protection.

Control of Monkeys and Messenger Command

Commands Japanese macaques as shinshi; can restrain monkeys from crop raiding or send them as warnings. Monkeys at Hiyoshi are not to be harmed; to harm one invites retribution. In art, monkeys perform kagura.

Limit: Monkeys are messengers, not puppets; folklore says they act capriciously if disrespected. Modern ecological pressure limits live monkey presence.

Enmusubi and Household Protection

Later folk attribution as matcher of marriages and protector of households, especially in Sakamoto and Tokyo Hie shrines. Popular for weddings at Hiyoshi Taisha.

Limit: Secondary domain, shared with Ōnamuchi (Ōkuninushi) enshrined in Nishi Hongū; not in early myth.

Accounts I
The Birth of Ōyamakui — Son of the Harvest DeityKojiki (712), Book 1

Nara period, reflecting earlier oral genealogy

In the genealogy following Susanoo's descent, Susanoo weds Kamuōichi-hime (daughter of Ōyamatsumi) and begets two children: Ōtoshi no Kami (Great Harvest Deity) and Ukanomitama (Food Spirit). Ōtoshi no Kami then weds Kaguyo-hime and begets two sons: Ōyamakui no Kami and Niinome? The text lists: 'Ōtoshi no Kami wedded Kaguyo-hime, daughter of ... and begot Ōyamakui no Kami.' This places Ōyamakui two generations below Susanoo, in the lineage of food and mountain deities, linking harvest (toshi) to mountain (yama). No deeds are narrated; his birth establishes his place in the divine kinship that will populate the land with local kami. Establishes Ōyamakui's legitimacy as a land-owning kami (kunitsukami) descended from both storm and mountain lines, explaining his dual harvest-mountain portfolio. Later shrine genealogy uses this to claim seniority among Hie deities.

Variant Genealogy in the Nihon Shoki — The Unstable LineageNihon Shoki (720), Kamiyo, variant notes (issho)

Nara period

The Nihon Shoki main text omits Ōyamakui, but a variant (issho) notes: 'In one writing, Ōtoshi no Kami begot Ōyamakui no Kami.' Another variant attaches Ōyamakui to the lineage of Yashimajinumi (son of Susanoo via Kushinadahime) — showing the compilers were aware of multiple oral traditions and left them unresolved. The text writes the name as 大山杭神 (Mountain Stake Deity), reinforcing the 'stake' etymology. The inclusion as issho rather than main text signals his status as a local Ōmi kami not central to the imperial lineage but important enough to record. Demonstrates that even in the 8th century Ōyamakui's genealogy was fluid, reflecting regional traditions around Hiei vs Yamato court synthesis. Modern scholarship cites this as evidence of local mountain cults being grafted onto the Susanoo lineage.

The Descent from the Summit — Enshrinement at HiyoshiHiyoshi Sha engi, Hie Sannō Rishōki (13th-14th c.), and Hiyoshi Taisha shrine tradition

Heian to Muromachi (tradition claims 8th century, documented medieval)

Originally Ōyamakui dwelt at the summit of Mount Hiei (Hiei no Okumiya). When Saichō (767-822) founded Enryakuji on Hiei in 788-807, the kami was entreated to descend to protect the new Tendai monastery. According to engi, the deity moved from the peak to the eastern foot at Sakamoto, where the Higashi Hongū (Eastern Main Hall) was built for Ōyamakui, while Ōnamuchi (Ōkuninushi) was enshrined in the Nishi Hongū (Western Main Hall) as his guest/companion. A ritual of yamabiraki still reenacts opening the mountain. The move sacralized the entire mountain as mandala and made Ōyamakui the jishu (landlord deity) of Enryakuji. Created the dual-shrine structure of Hiyoshi Taisha (East = Ōyamakui, West = Ōnamuchi) and the theological basis for Sannō Shintō. Established the pattern of mountain kami as protector of Buddhist institutions, replicated elsewhere.

Accounts II
Sannō Gongen — The Mountain King as Buddhist AvatarSannō Ichijitsu Shintō texts, Keiran Shūyōshū (1311-1348), and Sannō mandara paintings

Heian to Muromachi (9th-16th century)

Under honji suijaku theory, Ōyamakui was revealed as the suijaku (trace manifestation) of Shakyamuni Buddha (or Yakushi Nyorai in some lineages) under the title Sannō Gongen (Mountain King Avatar). As Sannō, he became the chief of the Nijūissha (21 shrines of Hie) and the guardian of the Tendai Dharma. The Keiran Shūyōshū, a Tendai encyclopedia, explains ichijitsu (one reality) — the kami and Buddha are one essence. Sannō mandara depict him enthroned above Enryakuji, with monkeys dancing below. Monks performed Sannō hō (rites) before battles, and the Hie shrines fielded sōhei (warrior monks) under his banner. Made Hie Sannō one of the most politically powerful shrine-temple complexes in medieval Japan, controlling trade routes on Lake Biwa and influencing Kyoto court. The 1571 burning of Hie by Oda Nobunaga was seen as killing the Mountain King, requiring Toyotomi Hideyoshi's 1586 reconstruction to restore cosmic order.

The Burning and Rebirth — Oda Nobunaga's Destruction and Hideyoshi's RestorationShinchō Kōki (Chronicle of Lord Nobunaga, c.1582), Hiyoshi Taisha records

Azuchi-Momoyama (1571-1586)

In 1571, Oda Nobunaga, angered by Enryakuji's alliance with his enemies (Azai-Asakura) and its military power, burned Mount Hiei, slaughtering thousands of monks and destroying both Enryakuji and Hiyoshi Taisha. Contemporary chronicles describe the shrines of Sannō as consumed, the kami forced to flee. For 15 years the mountain lay desolate, interpreted as the kami's wrath and absence causing disorder. In 1586, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, consolidating power, sponsored reconstruction of Hiyoshi Taisha in the opulent Momoyama style, rebuilding Higashi Hongū for Ōyamakui. The rebuilt hiyoshi-zukuri halls survive as National Treasures, and the annual Sannō Matsuri commemorates restoration. Physical shrine architecture today dates to this reconstruction, preserving medieval layout. The trauma reinforced the kami's role as protector whose shrine destruction signals political upheaval; restoration legitimized Hideyoshi's rule.

The Monkey Festival — Sannō Matsuri and the Divine Messenger TodayHiyoshi Taisha Sannō Matsuri records, contemporary ethnography

Documented from medieval period to present (annual April 14-15)

Each spring Hiyoshi Taisha holds the Sannō Matsuri, one of the Lake Biwa region's great festivals. Mikoshi (portable shrines) of Ōyamakui and Ōnamuchi are paraded from the mountain to Lake Biwa, accompanied by monkey-costumed performers and children wearing masaru masks. Priests offer sacred sake brewed for the kami, reaffirming Ōyamakui's role as sake patron. The festival's yabusame (horseback archery) and saru mawashi (monkey performance) recall the messenger bond. Pilgrims purchase masaru charms — monkey dolls with the pun ma-saru (evil warding) — to place on roofs. The festival is designated an Important Intangible Folk Cultural Property. Keeps Ōyamakui worship alive as community practice, supports local sake breweries who donate sake, and sustains the monkey taboo (no harming monkeys in Sakamoto). Tourism and heritage protection now fund shrine upkeep.

Ōyamakui is the same as Ōyamatsumi, the general mountain god. — They are distinct kami. Ōyamatsumi (大山祇) is father of Konohanasakuya-hime, enshrined at Ōyamazumi Jinja on Ōmishima Island, overseeing mountains generally. Ōyamakui (大山咋) is specific to Mount Hiei, son of Ōtoshi, enshrined at Hiyoshi Taisha. Different genealogies, shrines, and norito. Conflation arises from similar names and shared yama no kami category.

The monkey is the god itself — Ōyamakui is a monkey deity. — The Japanese macaque is the shinshi (messenger/vehicle), called masaru or shin'en. The kami is anthropomorphic (court noble) or aniconic; monkeys act as intermediaries and apotropaic symbols via the pun ma-saru (ward off evil). Shrine priests explicitly correct this, though folk charms blur the line.

Ōyamakui is an ancient sake god dating to Jōmon brewing. — Sake patronage is medieval to early modern, tied to Hiyoshi shrine's role supplying sake to Enryakuji and Kyoto court, and to his parentage as son of harvest deity Ōtoshi. No Jōmon evidence; Kojiki does not mention sake. Other sake kami (Matsuo, Ukanomitama) have competing claims.

Sannō Gongen is a separate Buddhist deity unrelated to Shinto. — Sannō Gongen is the honji suijaku Buddhist title for Ōyamakui (and the Hie collective) in medieval Tendai. Gongen means 'avatar' — a Buddha manifesting as kami. Meiji separation made them seem separate, but historically they were one cult.
