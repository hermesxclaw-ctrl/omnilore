Who It Is

In the swirling currents of the Yellow River, there dwells a god of paradox: Hebo, the Lord of the River, who can be both a benevolent bestower of wisdom and a terrifying force of destruction. He rides a chariot drawn by dragons, yet his origins are as murky as the river's floodwaters. From ancient sacrifices to modern video games, his legacy flows through Chinese culture like the river itself—life-giving, unpredictable, and eternal.

Hebo's character reflects the dual nature of the Yellow River: benevolent and life-giving, but also greedy, unpredictable, and destructive. He is portrayed as proud and arrogant, as seen in the Zhuangzi when he believes all beauty belongs to him before meeting the God of the Sea. He is also capable of anger and vengeance, as shown when he complains to the Supreme God after being shot by Yi. His actions are often impulsive and self-centered, yet he can also aid heroes like Yu the Great.

Powers
Control over the Yellow River

Hebo has the power to control the flow of the Yellow River, including its floods and currents. He can cause destructive floods or provide water for irrigation, reflecting the river's dual nature.

Limit: His control is not absolute; he is subject to the will of the Supreme God (Di) and other higher powers. He can be challenged or harmed by mortals, as seen in the story of Yi.

Shape-shifting

Hebo can transform into a white dragon, as seen in the story of Yi shooting him.

Limit: In the story, his transformation into a beast makes him vulnerable to attack, and the Supreme God implies that he should have remained in his divine form to avoid harm.

Riding a dragon-drawn chariot

Hebo travels in a chariot pulled by two dragons, allowing him to move through the sky and water.

Limit: No specific limitations are mentioned, but it is a mode of transport rather than a combat ability.

Bestowing the River Diagram (Hetu)

Hebo gave Yu the Great a map of the Yellow River, which helped him control the Great Flood and establish hydraulic works.

Limit: This is a one-time act of assistance, not a continuous power.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Hebo is described in various ways. In some early accounts, such as the Shizi, he has a white face and the body of a fish. In illustrations noted by the Jin scholar Guo Pu, he is depicted riding a chariot pulled by two dragons through the clouds. In the 'Nine Songs', he is portrayed as a divine figure on a wedding journey, suggesting a regal and human-like appearance. The white dragon form is also mentioned in the story of Yi shooting him.

Symbols — Hebo is associated with the Yellow River, dragons, chariots, and water. His iconography often includes a chariot drawn by dragons, symbolizing his control over the river and its floods. He is also linked to the River Diagram (Hetu) that he gave to Yu the Great, a symbol of cosmic order and hydraulic knowledge.

Domains — Hebo resides in the Yellow River, which is believed to originate from Mount Kunlun. He is primarily associated with the river itself, but also with its floods, irrigation, and the surrounding regions. His domain extends to the river's course and its tributaries, and he is worshipped in various locations along the river.

Accounts I
The Wedding Journey in the Nine SongsChu Ci, 'Nine Songs'

c. 3rd-2nd century BCE

In the 'Hebo' section of the Nine Songs, the performer narrates a journey with Hebo as a wedding procession. The poem describes riding in a chariot drawn by two dragons, moving through the water and clouds, with a sense of longing and beauty. It is likely part of a ritual drama that celebrated the god's marriage to a river goddess or a human bride. The poem reflects the ritual practice of offering brides to Hebo, though it is interpreted as a symbolic or theatrical representation.

Yi Shoots HeboChu Ci, 'Heavenly Questions'; Huainanzi; Wang Yi's commentary

c. 3rd-2nd century BCE (texts), with later commentary

The 'Heavenly Questions' asks why Yi shot Hebo and took his wife Luoshen. Wang Yi's commentary provides a narrative: Hebo transformed into a white dragon and was shot in the left eye by Yi. Hebo complained to the Supreme God, who rebuked him for behaving like a beast. The Huainanzi states that Yi shot Hebo because he had drowned people. Hebo loses his left eye and his wife Luoshen is taken by Yi. The story explains the origin of Luoshen as a separate deity and highlights the moral that gods should not abandon their duties.

Accounts II
Hebo and the Autumn FloodsZhuangzi, Chapter 17

c. 4th-3rd century BCE

During autumn floods, Hebo is filled with pride as the Yellow River swells. He journeys east to the North Sea and sees the vastness of the ocean, realizing his own insignificance. He humbles himself before Ruo, the God of the Sea, and acknowledges that he has been foolish to think he was the greatest. Hebo gains wisdom and humility, serving as a philosophical lesson about the limits of one's perspective.

Hebo Aids Yu the GreatShizi and other early texts

c. 4th-3rd century BCE (text dates)

Hebo gives Yu the Great a map of the Yellow River (the River Diagram), which helps Yu control the Great Flood and bring order to the land. This act is seen as a benevolent contribution to civilization. Yu successfully manages the flood, establishing the foundation for the Xia dynasty. The River Diagram becomes a symbol of cosmic knowledge.
