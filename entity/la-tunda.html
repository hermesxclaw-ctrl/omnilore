Who It Is

La Tunda is a shape-shifting demon from the Pacific coastal folklore of Colombia and Ecuador, particularly among Afro-Colombian communities. Described as a grotesque female entity, La Tunda has one leg that is a normal human foot and another leg that is a wooden stump or a molinillo (a wooden whisk used for chocolate) -- or, in some versions, a goat's hoof or animal leg. Her primary tactic is deception: she can shape-shift into the form of a loved one -- a mother, a sibling, a lover -- to lure victims, especially children and young men, deep into the jungle. Once isolated, she feeds them shrimp (camarones) and mangrove crabs, which, consumed under her spell, make them docile and enslaved to her will. She then keeps them captive, growing fat on their life force or consuming them entirely. To break La Tunda's spell, a victim must be found by family or a healer who can recognize the deception and administer a ritual purification with holy water, prayers, and certain herbs. The legend serves multiple functions: as a warning to children not to wander alone in the forest, as an explanation for disappearances, and as a cautionary tale about trusting appearances. The Tunda also has a subversive dimension: some versions describe her as punishing unfaithful men or abusive husbands. Her origin is sometimes traced to a woman who was cursed for her sins, or to a spirit of the forest that protects its boundaries against human encroachment. The Tunda is deeply embedded in the syncretic spiritual traditions of the Colombian Pacific, blending African, indigenous, and Catholic elements.
