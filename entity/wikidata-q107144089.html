The Approach

In the vast genealogies of Greek myth, Eurybia stands as a quiet force of the sea—a goddess whose name means 'wide-rushing' or 'broad strength,' embodying the raw, untamed power of the deep. Though she seldom appears in surviving tales, her lineage and offspring tie her to the very foundations of the ancient cosmos, making her a figure of subtle but enduring significance.

Content advisory. No dark material is present in the mythological sources; the figure is a minor goddess with no recorded violent or disturbing narratives.
Identity

Eurybia

Pronunciation yoo-RIB-ee-ə (English approximation); in Greek: eu-ry-BEE-a.

Etymology

From Greek Εὐρυβία (Eurybia), derived from εὐρύς (eurys) 'wide' and βία (bia) 'force, strength', thus 'wide-rushing' or 'broad strength'.

Cultural Ownership

Ancient Greek religion and mythology; specifically the Hesiodic tradition.

Disputed Identifications

None known. She is distinct from the plant and butterfly genera, and from the conspiracy theory 'Eurabia'.

Description

Appearance

No physical description survives in ancient sources. She is a goddess of the sea, likely imagined in anthropomorphic form, but no specific attributes are recorded.

No known symbols or iconographic representations from antiquity.

Behaviour & Personality

Hesiod describes her as having 'a heart of flint', suggesting a stern, unyielding character. No other behavioral traits are recorded.

Domains & Habitats

As a daughter of Pontus (Sea) and Gaia (Earth), she is a sea goddess, but her specific domain is not detailed. She may represent the rough or dangerous aspects of the sea.

Version Differences

No significant variations in ancient sources; she appears only in Hesiod's Theogony.

Stories & Myths

Genealogy in the Theogony

Source Hesiod, TheogonyCulture Ancient GreekPeriod Archaic period (c. 8th-7th century BCE)

Summary

Eurybia is born to Pontus (Sea) and Gaia (Earth). She marries the Titan Crius and bears him three sons: Astraeus (god of stars and planets), Pallas (god of battle), and Perses (god of destruction). No other myths involving her are recorded.

Consequences

Through her sons, she becomes the grandmother of important figures: Astraeus fathers the winds and stars; Pallas is associated with the giant of the same name or possibly the father of Nike; Perses is the father of Hecate in some traditions.

Contradictions & Disputes

Hecate's parentage

Position A

Hecate is the daughter of Perses and Asteria (per Hesiod, Theogony 404-411)

Position A — evidence

Hesiod explicitly states Hecate's parents as Perses and Asteria

Position B

Some later sources may conflate Perses, son of Crius and Eurybia, with the Titan Perses, leading to confusion

Position B — evidence

Later mythographers sometimes refer to Perses as a Titan without specifying which

Scholarly Consensus

Hesiod's account is canonical; the conflation is a modern misunderstanding.
