Who It Is

The deformed, 'salvage and deformed slave' of Prospero's island, son of the witch Sycorax. Caliban speaks some of Shakespeare's most beautiful poetry ('The isle is full of noises…') while being portrayed as monstrous and brutish. His name is an anagram of 'cannibal.'

Attested in The Tempest (c. 1611)

Form

Shakespeare never describes Caliban's physical form in precise detail. He is called 'a freckled whelp, hag-born' and 'not honoured with a human shape' by Prospero. He is described as 'a savage and deformed slave' in the Folio's dramatis personae. His mother Sycorax was a witch, and he is often portrayed as a half-human, half-monster, with webbed fingers, fins, or a fish-like tail in later illustrations. In the play, Trinculo mistakes him for a fish, and Stephano calls him a 'monster of the isle'. He is likely of dark complexion, given the colonial symbolism, but this is not explicit. He is covered in 'freckles' and has 'long nails' (he is told to 'foot' them). He is also described as 'the beast Caliban' and 'a thing of darkness'.

Powers

Knowledge of the island Caliban knows every 'fresh spring, brine-pit, barren place and fertile' on the island. He can find 'young scamels' and 'crabs', and he knows where to find 'pignuts' and 'juniper'. This is a practical, survivalist knowledge that makes him invaluable to Prospero. Shakespeare's play He cannot use this knowledge to free himself from Prospero's servitude. None, but his knowledge is often dismissed by Prospero as 'savage'.

Ability to communicate with spirits Caliban claims to be able to summon the island's spirits, which he does to help Stephano. He says 'I'll show thee every fertile inch o' th' island; and I will kiss thy foot'. He also knows how to 'charm' and 'unmuzzle' the spirits. Shakespeare's play He is not able to control the spirits against Prospero, who commands Ariel and the other spirits. It is unclear if this ability is real or a boast. In the play, the spirits are only seen obeying Prospero.

Cursing Caliban is famously skilled at cursing, as he says 'You taught me language; and my profit on't / Is, I know how to curse.' He curses Prospero and Miranda, calling down 'all the infections that the sun sucks up / From bogs, fens, flats'. Shakespeare's play His curses are verbal, not magical; they have no effect on Prospero. None

Physical strength Caliban is strong and able to carry wood and do heavy labor. He is also able to resist the spirits that Prospero sends to pinch him, though he is eventually subdued. Shakespeare's play He is not superhuman; he can be overpowered by spirits and by Prospero's magic. None

Behavior & Habitat

Caliban is initially presented as a savage, cursing, and resentful creature. He claims the island was his by right of his mother, and he rails against Prospero's usurpation. He is capable of great eloquence, as shown in his famous speech 'Be not afeard; the isle is full of noises'. He is shown to be easily manipulated, as when he pledges himself to Stephano and Trinculo, who are drunk and foolish. He is also shown to be capable of tenderness and a deep connection to the natural world, knowing 'all the qualities o' th' isle'. He is not inherently evil; he is a complex figure of nature, nurture, and colonial subjugation.

Accounts

Caliban tells Prospero and Miranda that the island was his by right of his mother Sycorax, who was banished from Algiers and left him on the island. He claims he was the sole owner and that Prospero, when he arrived, 'made much of me' and 'wouldst give me / Water with berries in't'. But when Caliban attempted to violate Miranda's honor, Prospero enslaved him and confined him to a rock.

Caliban meets Stephano, a drunken butler, and Trinculo, a jester, and mistakes them for gods because they give him 'celestial liquor' (alcohol). He pledges his allegiance to Stephano, promising to show him all the island's riches, and plots with them to kill Prospero. The plot fails when Ariel, at Prospero's command, distracts them with music and leads them into a filthy pool. They are later chased by spirits in the shape of hounds.

Caliban, trying to encourage Stephano, delivers a speech of extraordinary beauty: 'Be not afeard; the isle is full of noises, / Sounds and sweet airs, that give delight and hurt not.' He describes the island's sounds, dreams, and 'clouds' that 'open and show riches'. This speech is often cited as evidence of Caliban's innate nobility and his capacity for poetry.

Field Notes

▣ Caliban's name is an anagram of 'cannibal', a word that was itself derived from the Caribbean 'Carib' people.

▣ In the 1956 science fiction film Forbidden Planet, the character of the 'Monster from the Id' is a direct adaptation of Caliban, representing the destructive power of the subconscious.

▣ The German metalcore band Caliban named themselves after the character, but they have said they were drawn to the name's association with rebellion and alienation.

▣ Caliban's speech 'Be not afeard; the isle is full of noises' is one of the most quoted passages in all of Shakespeare, and it has been set to music by multiple composers.

▣ In Aimé Césaire's A Tempest, Caliban's final word is 'Freedom!', a direct rebuttal to Prospero's 'Set Caliban free' at the end of Shakespeare's play.

Cultural Significance

The most politically contested figure in Shakespeare. His cry — 'You taught me language, and my profit on't / Is, I know how to curse' — is one of the most quoted indictments of colonial 'civilizing' missions.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Caliban as the original 'colonized native' in Western literature — the one who has the land, the language, and the culture stolen from him, and who responds with a mixture of rage, poetry, and desperate scheming. He is the 'other' who refuses to be silent, the 'monster' who is more human than his master. In a modern context, you can think of him as the archetype of the 'subaltern' who speaks back to power, a figure that has been embraced by postcolonial writers, activists, and artists. He is the original 'rebel with a cause', even if that cause is doomed.
