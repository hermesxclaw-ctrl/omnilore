Who It Is

He is the monster who speaks poetry, the slave who curses his master in the master's own language, the 'savage' who knows the island's every secret. Caliban is Shakespeare's most unsettling creation — a being who is at once a victim and a villain, a symbol of the colonized world and a mirror of the colonizer's fears. Four hundred years after his first appearance, he still haunts our imagination, from the Caribbean to the silver screen, a 'thing of darkness' we cannot quite acknowledge as our own.

Caliban is initially presented as a savage, cursing, and resentful creature. He claims the island was his by right of his mother, and he rails against Prospero's usurpation. He is capable of great eloquence, as shown in his famous speech 'Be not afeard; the isle is full of noises'. He is shown to be easily manipulated, as when he pledges himself to Stephano and Trinculo, who are drunk and foolish. Powers
Knowledge of the island

Caliban knows every 'fresh spring, brine-pit, barren place and fertile' on the island. He can find 'young scamels' and 'crabs', and he knows where to find 'pignuts' and 'juniper'. This is a practical, survivalist knowledge that makes him invaluable to Prospero.

Limit: He cannot use this knowledge to free himself from Prospero's servitude.

Ability to communicate with spirits

Caliban claims to be able to summon the island's spirits, which he does to help Stephano. He says 'I'll show thee every fertile inch o' th' island; and I will kiss thy foot'. He also knows how to 'charm' and 'unmuzzle' the spirits.

Limit: He is not able to control the spirits against Prospero, who commands Ariel and the other spirits.

Cursing

Caliban is famously skilled at cursing, as he says 'You taught me language; and my profit on't / Is, I know how to curse.' He curses Prospero and Miranda, calling down 'all the infections that the sun sucks up / From bogs, fens, flats'.

Limit: His curses are verbal, not magical; they have no effect on Prospero.

Physical strength

Caliban is strong and able to carry wood and do heavy labor. He is also able to resist the spirits that Prospero sends to pinch him, though he is eventually subdued.

Limit: He is not superhuman; he can be overpowered by spirits and by Prospero's magic.

His mother Sycorax was a witch, and he is often portrayed as a half-human, half-monster, with webbed fingers, fins, or a fish-like tail in later illustrations. Accounts I
The Usurpation of the IslandThe Tempest, Act I, Scene 2

c. 1610-1611

Caliban tells Prospero and Miranda that the island was his by right of his mother Sycorax, who was banished from Algiers and left him on the island. He claims he was the sole owner and that Prospero, when he arrived, 'made much of me' and 'wouldst give me / Water with berries in't'. But when Caliban attempted to violate Miranda's honor, Prospero enslaved him and confined him to a rock. Caliban is forced into servitude, carrying wood and doing labor. He becomes resentful and curses Prospero. This event establishes the central conflict of the play.

Accounts II
The Conspiracy with Stephano and TrinculoThe Tempest, Act II, Scene 2; Act III, Scene 2; Act IV, Scene 1

c. 1610-1611

Caliban meets Stephano, a drunken butler, and Trinculo, a jester, and mistakes them for gods because they give him 'celestial liquor' (alcohol). He pledges his allegiance to Stephano, promising to show him all the island's riches, and plots with them to kill Prospero. The plot fails when Ariel, at Prospero's command, distracts them with music and leads them into a filthy pool. They are later chased by spirits in the shape of hounds. The conspiracy fails, and Caliban and his co-conspirators are humiliated. Caliban is forced to recant and accept his servitude, saying 'I'll be wise hereafter, / And seek for grace.'

The 'Be not afeard' SpeechThe Tempest, Act III, Scene 2

c. 1610-1611

Caliban, trying to encourage Stephano, delivers a speech of extraordinary beauty: 'Be not afeard; the isle is full of noises, / Sounds and sweet airs, that give delight and hurt not.' He describes the island's sounds, dreams, and 'clouds' that 'open and show riches'. This speech is often cited as evidence of Caliban's innate nobility and his capacity for poetry. This speech is a key moment in the play, revealing Caliban's depth and his connection to the island's natural magic. It is often used to argue against a purely 'savage' reading of the character.

Caliban is a fish-like creature. — The play never says this. Trinculo, upon seeing him, says he 'smells like a fish', but this is a joke. Caliban's physical form is never described in detail, and he is more likely a human-like 'savage'.

Caliban is a purely evil villain. — Caliban is a complex character. He is a villain in that he plots murder, but he is also a victim of colonialism and enslavement. He is capable of poetry and tenderness, and his cursing is a response to his oppression.

Caliban's name means 'cannibal'. — The name is an anagram of 'cannibal', and it is likely intended to evoke the New World 'cannibal' stereotype, but it is not a direct synonym. Shakespeare likely coined the name.

Caliban is the 'hero' of The Tempest. — In postcolonial readings, Caliban is often seen as a heroic rebel, but in the play itself, he is a secondary character who is ultimately defeated and submits to Prospero. He is not the protagonist.
