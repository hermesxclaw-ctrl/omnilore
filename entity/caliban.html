The Approach

Caliban enters "The Tempest" already condemned—a "salvage and deformed slave," a "thing of darkness" whose very body seems to offend the civilized eye. Yet when this supposed monster finally speaks, he gives the island its voice: a rush of sound and dream in which "the isle is full of noises" that "give delight and hurt not." Between these two images—the cursed brute and the unexpected poet—stretches four centuries of argument, painting, performance, and political struggle. To follow Caliban through canvases, stages, and manifestos is to watch a single figure become a mirror for changing ideas about race, empire, and what counts as human.

Content advisory. Readers should be aware that Caliban’s story and its reception involve depictions and discussions of sexual violence, enslavement, physical and psychological abuse, racist caricature, and colonial exploitation. Quotations from early modern texts and later criticism may contain terms and stereotypes that are offensive and harmful. These materials are presented for critical examination, not endorsement.

Etymology

The name "Caliban" is generally understood as a near-anagram of "cannibal," a term circulating in early modern Europe from Spanish "caníbal" (itself derived from "Carib") to denote allegedly man‑eating indigenous peoples of the Caribbean. Many scholars see this as Shakespeare’s deliberate evocation of New World colonial discourse. Alternative proposals—such as derivation from Romani or Arabic roots—remain speculative and lack strong documentary support.

Cultural Ownership

Caliban is a fictional character created by William Shakespeare for the play "The Tempest" (c. 1610–1611). As a literary figure he belongs to early modern English drama, but his symbolic afterlives have been claimed by a wide range of cultures, especially in postcolonial, Caribbean, African, and Latin American thought, where he is reimagined as an emblem of the colonized subject.

Disputed Identifications

Caliban has no historical or mythological prototype that can be securely identified. Critical attempts to link him to specific New World peoples, Irish ‘wild men,’ or Mediterranean demons are interpretive rather than evidentiary. In fine art and criticism he is variously read as an indigenous American, African, Irish, Mediterranean ‘other,’ or generalized symbol of the colonized and racialized subject; these are metaphorical identifications, not historical ones.

Description

Appearance

Attested in Shakespeare’s text, Caliban is repeatedly described by other characters as "deformed," "salvage," and "monster," with fish‑like attributes. Trinculo calls him "a fish" whose legs are like a man’s and "his fins like arms" (II.ii.25–35), later "half a fish and half a monster" and "debosh’d fish." Prospero refers to him as a "mis‑shapen knave" (V.i.268) and once as "thou tortoise" (I.ii.317), likely indicating slowness rather than literal form. The Folio’s cast list labels him "a salvage and deformed slave." These descriptions are attested textual evidence, but they remain vague, leaving his exact anatomy open to interpretation. In fine art, especially from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, artists often exaggerate his monstrosity: Fuseli’s painting shows a squat, muscular, grotesque figure with coarse features and animalistic posture; later illustrators (e.g., Arthur Rackham) give him scales, claws, or amphibious traits. Such visualizations are adaptation‑specific reconstructions rather than canonical anatomy.

Symbols & Iconography

Caliban’s iconography in fine art and theater frequently emphasizes earthiness, bestiality, and marginality. Common motifs include: hunched posture, coarse or mottled skin, exaggerated facial features, and association with rocks, caves, or shoreline, underscoring his link to the island’s raw nature. Fish‑like elements (scales, fins, webbed hands) derive from Trinculo’s jokes and have become a recurring visual symbol. In some postcolonial and modern artworks, Caliban is depicted with explicitly racialized features—dark skin, shackles, or tribal adornment—signifying his role as colonized subject rather than generic monster. Religious or magical symbols are rare in canonical art, though references to his mother Sycorax and the god Setebos occasionally inspire occult or pagan imagery. These iconographic choices are largely interpretive and adaptation‑specific.

Behaviour & Personality

In the play, Caliban is coarse, resentful, and capable of violence, yet also poetic and deeply attuned to the island’s sensory richness. He curses Prospero, plots to kill him, and admits to attempting sexual violation of Miranda, which he frames as a desire to "people this isle with Calibans" (I.ii). At the same time, he delivers some of the most lyrical lines in the play, notably the "Be not afeard" speech (III.ii), revealing sensitivity to the island’s sounds and dreams. His behavior oscillates between drunken gullibility (following Stephano and Trinculo) and fierce assertion of his claim to the island: "This island’s mine, by Sycorax my mother" (I.ii). Personality traits—resentful, imaginative, sensuous, stubborn—are attested in the text. Later criticism reconstructs him as either villain, victim, or revolutionary, depending on ideological lens; these are interpretive positions rather than new canonical facts.

Domains & Habitats

Caliban’s domain is Prospero’s unnamed island, particularly its rough, marginal spaces: caves, shoreline, and wooded areas. Before Prospero’s arrival, he appears to have roamed the island freely, knowing its springs, berries, and natural resources. After enslavement, he is confined to menial labor—fetching wood, showing paths—and treated as a subordinate inhabitant. Symbolically, he is associated with earth and water, in contrast to Ariel’s airy nature. In fine art, he is almost always placed in wild, rocky landscapes or dim interiors that emphasize his exclusion from the civilized courtly world of the shipwrecked nobles.

Version Differences

Across versions, Caliban’s characterization shifts markedly. In Shakespeare’s original text, he is a "deformed slave" whose attempted rape and murderous plot mark him as dangerous, yet his poetic speeches complicate a purely villainous reading. Eighteenth‑century stage and visual traditions often emphasize his drunken bestiality, turning him into comic grotesque. Nineteenth‑century interpretations recast him as "noble savage" or evolutionary "missing link," reflecting contemporary racial and scientific discourses. Twentieth‑century and postcolonial adaptations—such as Aimé Césaire’s "Une Tempête"—transform him into a politically conscious, colonized subject who rejects Prospero’s language and authority, sometimes minimizing or reinterpreting his sexual violence to foreground resistance. Fine‑art depictions track these shifts: earlier works stress monstrosity; later ones highlight racialized oppression or tragic dignity. These differences are adaptation‑specific and should not be merged into a single, unified Caliban.

Powers & Abilities

Intimate knowledge of the island

Source Version Attested in Shakespeare’s "The Tempest," especially I.ii and III.ii.

Description

Caliban possesses detailed, practical knowledge of the island’s geography, resources, and sensory qualities. He knows where to find fresh water, edible berries, and safe paths, and he guides Prospero and others—albeit under compulsion. His "Be not afeard" speech suggests a profound attunement to the island’s sounds and atmospheric phenomena, almost a phenomenological ‘power’ of perception.

Limitations

This is not supernatural omniscience; his knowledge is experiential and bounded by the island. Prospero’s magic still surpasses him in control over spirits and weather.

Contradictions

No major contradictions; some critics metaphorically elevate this knowledge to a quasi‑mystical bond with nature, while others see it as simple familiarity born of residence.

Resistance through language

Source Version Attested in Shakespeare’s text (I.ii, where he recalls learning language from Miranda and Prospero) and elaborated in postcolonial criticism and adaptations such as Césaire’s "Une Tempête."

Description

Caliban learns Prospero’s language and uses it to curse, protest, and assert his claim to the island. Postcolonial critics interpret this as a form of discursive resistance: he appropriates the colonizer’s tongue to articulate grievance and identity, even as he laments losing his native speech.

Limitations

In the original play, his linguistic resistance does not free him; he remains enslaved at the end. His curses are mocked or punished, and Prospero retains power.

Contradictions

Traditional humanist readings treat language as civilizing gift, seeing Caliban’s speech as evidence of Prospero’s benevolence. Postcolonial readings argue the opposite: language is a tool of domination that simultaneously enables resistance. These positions are interpretive, not textual contradictions.

Capacity for poetic vision

Source Version Attested in Shakespeare’s "The Tempest," III.ii.

Description

Caliban’s speeches, especially "Be not afeard; the isle is full of noises," reveal a striking poetic imagination. He describes sounds that "give delight and hurt not," dreams of riches that vanish upon waking, and an emotional response to the island’s music. This aesthetic sensibility functions as an inner ‘power’ that complicates his monstrous image.

Limitations

This is not a magical ability; it is a rhetorical and imaginative capacity. It does not grant him control over the island’s phenomena, only perception and expression.

Contradictions

Some critics see this as evidence of innate nobility; others argue that Shakespeare uses poetic language ironically to heighten the pathos of a degraded figure. The text itself does not resolve this tension.

Physical strength and capacity for labor

Source Version Attested in Shakespeare’s text (Prospero’s commands and references to his labor) and reinforced by theatrical and artistic convention.

Description

Caliban is repeatedly tasked with heavy physical labor—carrying logs, gathering wood, performing menial tasks. Stage and visual traditions often depict him as muscular and robust, suggesting significant physical strength.

Limitations

His strength does not overcome Prospero’s magic or social hierarchy. He is easily subdued by threats and by Ariel’s invisible torments.

Contradictions

No direct textual contradictions; however, some modern productions downplay brute strength to emphasize vulnerability or victimhood.

Stories & Myths

Caliban’s enslavement and claim to the island

Source William Shakespeare, "The Tempest," Acts I–V.Culture Early modern EnglishPeriod c. 1610–1611 (composition); 1623 (First Folio)

Summary

Prospero, the deposed Duke of Milan, arrives on a remote island where the witch Sycorax once lived and where her son Caliban now dwells. Prospero teaches Caliban language and initially treats him kindly, but after Caliban attempts to violate Prospero’s daughter Miranda, Prospero enslaves him, forcing him to perform menial labor. Caliban insists that the island belongs to him by inheritance—"This island’s mine, by Sycorax my mother"—and resents Prospero’s usurpation. Throughout the play he oscillates between sullen obedience and open rebellion, ultimately failing to overthrow Prospero and remaining subject to his power at the play’s close.

Consequences

Caliban’s enslavement becomes a central axis for later interpretations of the play as an allegory of colonization. Within the narrative, his failed resistance reinforces Prospero’s authority and the restoration of his dukedom. The unresolved status of Caliban at the end—still on the island, still subordinate—leaves open questions about justice and sovereignty.

Variants

In some stage and film adaptations, Prospero’s treatment of Caliban is softened or intensified, altering audience sympathy. Postcolonial rewritings (e.g., Césaire) change the ending to give Caliban a more assertive or emancipatory role, sometimes leaving Prospero isolated or undermined.

The attempted violation of Miranda

Source William Shakespeare, "The Tempest," Act I, scene ii.Culture Early modern EnglishPeriod c. 1610–1611

Summary

Prospero and Miranda recall how Caliban, after being welcomed and taught language, attempted to "violate" Miranda. Caliban does not express remorse; instead, he says he would have "peopled else this isle with Calibans." Prospero responds by tightening Caliban’s bondage and framing him as irredeemably base. This episode is brief but crucial, shaping the audience’s perception of Caliban’s sexuality and moral character.

Consequences

The attempted sexual violence justifies, within the play’s moral framework, Prospero’s harsh treatment and reinforces Caliban’s status as dangerous "other." It also provides a key site for modern debates about gender, race, and colonial power, as critics question whether the narrative uses sexual threat to legitimize enslavement.

Variants

Some modern productions downplay the threat, suggest misunderstanding, or reframe the event to emphasize Prospero’s overreaction. Postcolonial and feminist readings often interrogate the scene as a constructed justification for colonial violence rather than a neutral moral fact.

The conspiracy with Stephano and Trinculo

Source William Shakespeare, "The Tempest," Acts II–III.Culture Early modern EnglishPeriod c. 1610–1611

Summary

Encountering the drunken butler Stephano and jester Trinculo, Caliban mistakes them for powerful beings and pledges allegiance to Stephano, promising to show him the island’s riches if he will kill Prospero. Together they plot to murder Prospero, seize his books, and take Miranda as Stephano’s prize. The conspiracy collapses when Ariel leads them astray and torments them; they are humiliated and punished but survive.

Consequences

The episode underscores Caliban’s desperation and gullibility, as well as his desire for liberation from Prospero’s rule. It also provides comic relief, juxtaposing serious themes of rebellion and sovereignty with slapstick drunkenness. For later critics, the plot illustrates how colonized subjects may seek new masters rather than true autonomy, or how their resistance is undermined by internalized subordination.

Variants

In some productions, Caliban’s alliance with Stephano and Trinculo is played purely for comedy; in others, it is staged as tragic misrecognition, emphasizing his exploitation by opportunistic Europeans.

Caliban as postcolonial rebel

Source Aimé Césaire, "Une Tempête" (A Tempest).Culture Caribbean (Martinican), FrancophonePeriod 1969

Summary

Césaire’s adaptation relocates Shakespeare’s story into an explicitly colonial framework. Caliban is portrayed as a Black, colonized subject who rejects Prospero’s authority, refuses to speak his language, and embraces a revolutionary identity. He calls Prospero "white master," invokes anti‑imperial rhetoric, and aligns himself with liberation movements. The play ends with Prospero isolated on the island, while Caliban remains defiantly present, symbolizing ongoing resistance.

Consequences

Césaire’s Caliban becomes a central figure in postcolonial discourse, inspiring readings that see him as emblem of Third World resistance. The adaptation shifts the moral center of the narrative away from Prospero’s reconciliation toward Caliban’s unfinished struggle.

Variants

Other postcolonial rewritings—by authors such as Roberto Fernández Retamar and George Lamming—similarly reclaim Caliban as a positive symbol, though each text offers distinct ideological inflections.

Relationships

Sycorax

Type familySource Shakespeare, "The Tempest," I.ii (Prospero’s exposition).

Description

Sycorax is Caliban’s mother, an Algerian witch who was banished to the island while pregnant. She dies before the events of the play, but her legacy shapes Caliban’s identity and claim to the island.

Version Note

Attested in the original play; Sycorax never appears on stage and is known only through Prospero’s account.

Conflicting Accounts

Some critics question the reliability of Prospero’s description of Sycorax as a monstrous witch, suggesting that his narrative may be colored by misogyny and colonial prejudice. No alternative textual account exists.

Prospero

Type hostileSource Shakespeare, "The Tempest," throughout.

Description

Prospero is Caliban’s master and primary oppressor. He enslaves Caliban, controls his labor, and punishes him with magical torments. Their relationship is marked by mutual hatred: Prospero calls Caliban "thing of darkness" and "born devil," while Caliban curses him and plots his death.

Version Note

Canonical relationship in the original play; later adaptations reconfigure the dynamic to emphasize colonial power or psychological dependence.

Conflicting Accounts

Humanist readings portray Prospero as benevolent educator whose severity is justified by Caliban’s attempted rape. Postcolonial readings see Prospero as colonizer whose narrative demonizes Caliban to legitimize dispossession.

Miranda

Type hostileSource Shakespeare, "The Tempest," I.ii.

Description

Miranda initially shows compassion toward Caliban, teaching him language and treating him kindly. After his attempted violation, she regards him with horror and disgust, calling him "abhorred slave." Caliban’s desire to "people this isle with Calibans" positions her as target of sexual aggression.

Version Note

Canonical relationship in the original play.

Conflicting Accounts

Some modern critics and productions attempt to nuance the relationship, emphasizing Miranda’s limited perspective and the possibility that Prospero’s account shapes her view. The text, however, presents her condemnation as straightforward.

Ariel

Type hostileSource Shakespeare, "The Tempest," esp. II.ii and III.ii.

Description

Ariel, Prospero’s airy servant, frequently torments Caliban at Prospero’s command—pinching him, leading him astray, and frightening him with music and illusions. They rarely interact directly, but Ariel functions as instrument of Caliban’s oppression.

Version Note

Canonical relationship; in some productions Ariel is staged with more empathy toward Caliban, but the text emphasizes obedience to Prospero.

Conflicting Accounts

Postcolonial readings sometimes contrast Ariel’s more compliant servitude with Caliban’s overt resistance, framing them as two models of colonized subjectivity rather than simple antagonists.

Stephano

Type exploitativeSource Shakespeare, "The Tempest," II–III.

Description

Stephano, a drunken butler, becomes Caliban’s temporary "master" when Caliban pledges allegiance to him, hoping he will kill Prospero. Stephano exploits Caliban’s desperation, promising freedom while planning to use him and Miranda for his own gain.

Version Note

Canonical relationship; often played for comedy.

Conflicting Accounts

Some critics see Stephano as parody of colonial adventurer, highlighting how Caliban’s search for liberation leads him into another exploitative hierarchy.

Trinculo

Type exploitativeSource Shakespeare, "The Tempest," II–III.

Description

Trinculo, a jester, mocks Caliban, calling him "monster" and treating him as a curiosity. He joins Stephano in exploiting Caliban’s knowledge of the island and in plotting against Prospero.

Version Note

Canonical relationship.

Conflicting Accounts

Interpretive debates focus on whether Trinculo’s mockery reflects broader European attitudes toward racialized others or is merely comic relief.

Setebos

Type ritualSource Shakespeare, "The Tempest," I.ii.

Description

Setebos is a god worshipped by Sycorax and, by implication, associated with Caliban’s inherited belief system. Prospero mentions Setebos when describing Sycorax’s "mischiefs manifold and sorceries terrible."

Version Note

Attested only in Prospero’s speech; Caliban himself does not invoke Setebos in the play.

Conflicting Accounts

Scholars debate whether Setebos is Shakespeare’s invention or derived from travel literature about Patagonian or other non‑European deities. The lack of clear source makes this uncertain.

Frankenstein’s creature (Frankenstein’s monster)

Type creativeSource Modern criticism on Gothic and postcolonial literature; comparative studies of monstrosity.

Description

In later literary and critical traditions, Caliban is frequently compared to Frankenstein’s creature as a figure of the marginalized, misunderstood, and monstrous "other" who seeks recognition and rebels against his creator/master. This is a conceptual relationship, not a narrative one.

Version Note

Not present in Shakespeare; emerges in nineteenth‑ and twentieth‑century scholarship and creative works.

Conflicting Accounts

Some critics emphasize strong parallels; others caution against over‑identification, noting differences in origin, agency, and narrative focus.

Dark History
Content advisory. Caliban’s story involves sexual violence (an attempted rape), enslavement, physical and psychological coercion, racialized dehumanization, and colonial exploitation. Later receptions include racist caricature and stereotyped visual representations.

Sexual violence

Source Shakespeare, "The Tempest," I.ii.

Description

In Act I, scene ii, Prospero and Miranda recount that Caliban attempted to "violate" Miranda. Caliban does not deny the charge; instead, he expresses a desire to "people this isle with Calibans." The text presents this as attempted rape, used to justify Prospero’s harsh enslavement and Miranda’s revulsion.

Scholarly Context

Feminist and postcolonial critics analyze this episode as a key site where sexual threat is mobilized to legitimize colonial domination and racialized fear. Some argue that the narrative reinforces stereotypes of the hypersexualized, dangerous "native"; others interrogate how Prospero’s account frames the event and whether it should be read as absolute moral truth.

Enslavement and physical coercion

Source Shakespeare, "The Tempest," multiple scenes.

Description

Caliban is repeatedly called "slave" and subjected to forced labor and magical punishment. Prospero threatens him with cramps, pinches, and other torments inflicted by Ariel. Caliban’s lack of autonomy and constant fear mark his existence as one of enslavement and abuse.

Scholarly Context

Postcolonial scholarship reads Caliban’s bondage as emblematic of colonial slavery and forced labor. Critics such as Sahabuddin Ahamed and the Vaughans highlight how the play naturalizes hierarchical relations while also exposing their brutality. The ideological use of Caliban’s enslavement in later political discourse—both to justify empire and to critique it—forms a major strand of reception history.

Racialization and dehumanization

Source Shakespeare’s language; stage and fine‑art iconography documented in Virginia Mason Vaughan’s work and in "Shakespeare’s Caliban: A Cultural History."

Description

Caliban is described as "savage," "deformed," "monster," and "thing of darkness." Later visual and theatrical traditions often depict him with exaggerated, racialized features, aligning him with caricatures of Africans, indigenous Americans, or other colonized peoples. These representations dehumanize him and associate non‑European bodies with monstrosity.

Scholarly Context

Critical race and postcolonial studies examine how Caliban becomes a vehicle for projecting anxieties about race, evolution, and civilization. Nineteenth‑century depictions of Caliban as "missing link" between ape and human reflect pseudo‑scientific racism. Modern scholarship exposes and critiques these traditions, while some artists deliberately reclaim Caliban as a dignified, resistant figure.

Colonial ideology and propaganda

Source Historical uses of "The Tempest" in political and cultural discourse; analyses such as "Caliban’s Afterlives: The Tempest and the Ideological Justifications of Colonial Empire."

Description

The relationship between Prospero and Caliban has been used historically to justify colonial rule, portraying colonization as civilizing mission and the colonized as ungrateful, brutish subjects. Caliban’s rebellion is framed as ingratitude and treachery, while Prospero’s control appears orderly and rational.

Scholarly Context

Recent scholarship emphasizes that "The Tempest" both reflects and produces colonial ideology. Caliban’s figure becomes a battleground for competing interpretations: some readings reinforce imperial narratives; others, especially in anti‑colonial movements, reclaim him as symbol of resistance. The ideological instability of the play allows for both uses.

Contradictions & Disputes

Caliban as villain vs victim

Position A

Caliban is primarily a villain: a "born devil" whose attempted rape and murderous plot justify his enslavement and condemnation.

Position A — evidence

Prospero’s and Miranda’s testimony about the attempted violation; Caliban’s lack of remorse; his willingness to murder Prospero and exploit Stephano’s drunkenness.

Position B

Caliban is primarily a victim: a dispossessed native whose violence is shaped by colonial oppression and whose humanity is obscured by racist discourse.

Position B — evidence

Caliban’s claim to the island by inheritance; his poetic sensitivity; the asymmetry of power between him and Prospero; historical context of early modern colonial expansion.

Scholarly Consensus

No single consensus. Traditional humanist criticism leans toward villainy, while postcolonial and critical race scholarship foreground victimhood and resistance. Many contemporary readings acknowledge the paradox and treat Caliban as complex figure embodying both culpability and oppression.

Language as gift vs instrument of domination

Position A

Prospero’s teaching of language is a civilizing gift that elevates Caliban from brutishness to human speech.

Position A — evidence

Prospero and Miranda recall their efforts to teach Caliban words and concepts, framing this as benevolent education.

Position B

Language is an instrument of domination that erases Caliban’s native tongue and imposes colonial discourse, even as it enables his resistance.

Position B — evidence

Caliban’s complaint that learning language taught him how to curse; postcolonial theory (e.g., Said, Bhabha, Fanon) on language and power; Césaire’s adaptation emphasizing linguistic colonization.

Scholarly Consensus

Postcolonial criticism has significantly shifted the conversation toward position B, though some pedagogical and humanist readings still emphasize the civilizing aspect. The play’s ambiguity allows both interpretations.

Caliban’s race and species

Position A

Caliban is a non‑human monster, perhaps part fish or tortoise, whose deformity places him outside humanity.

Position A — evidence

Folios and characters call him "monster," "salvage and deformed slave," and refer to fish‑like features.

Position B

Caliban is human, albeit racialized and deformed by prejudice; his "monstrosity" is a projection of colonial and racist discourse.

Position B — evidence

Trinculo ultimately calls him "an islander" rather than a fish; his human speech, emotions, and social relations; modern scholarship emphasizing racialization rather than literal non‑human status.

Scholarly Consensus

Most contemporary scholars treat Caliban as human, reading his monstrosity as metaphorical and ideological. Earlier traditions sometimes literalized his non‑human traits. The text itself remains deliberately imprecise.

Setebos’s origin

Position A

Setebos is a fictional deity invented by Shakespeare.

Position A — evidence

Lack of clear, documented pre‑Shakespearean references to Setebos; the play’s isolated mention.

Position B

Setebos derives from travel literature or reports about non‑European religions, possibly Patagonian or other indigenous belief systems.

Position B — evidence

Some scholars note parallels with names in travel accounts and speculate about Shakespeare’s sources, though evidence is circumstantial.

Scholarly Consensus

Unresolved. Many reference works cautiously describe Setebos as likely Shakespearean invention influenced by travel narratives, but definitive proof is lacking.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

This dossier focuses on Caliban’s literary origin and fine‑art reception, with emphasis on English‑language scholarship and widely documented visual works. Several gaps remain: (1) A comprehensive catalogue of all fine‑art depictions of Caliban—paintings, sculptures, prints—across global collections was not exhaustively assembled; many minor or regional works may be missing. (2) Non‑English scholarship, especially in languages such as Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, and Japanese, likely contains additional perspectives on Caliban’s iconography and colonial significance that are not represented here. (3) Detailed archival records of early stage costumes and set designs, which would further illuminate Caliban’s visual history, were not individually consulted. (4) The precise origin of Setebos and possible extra‑textual sources remain uncertain. (5) Reception in contemporary internet culture, gaming, and niche fandoms is only broadly sketched, not systematically documented. Research is current to late July 2026 and limited by access to openly available digital resources and standard reference works.

Research Scope

This research treats Caliban as a Shakespearean literary character whose image has been extensively developed in fine art, theater, and postcolonial discourse. Primary focus is on: (1) Shakespeare’s "The Tempest" and major critical editions; (2) documented fine‑art and illustrative depictions, especially Fuseli and widely reproduced book illustrations; (3) key scholarly works on Caliban’s cultural history and postcolonial reinterpretations; and (4) selected modern adaptations such as Aimé Césaire’s "Une Tempête." The scope is historically broad—from the early seventeenth century to 2026—but geographically and linguistically partial, privileging Anglophone and some Francophone sources accessible through standard academic channels. It does not claim to cover all existing artworks or interpretations, and it flags areas where evidence is uncertain or contested.

Cultural Significance

The most politically contested figure in Shakespeare. His cry — 'You taught me language, and my profit on't / Is, I know how to curse' — is one of the most quoted indictments of colonial 'civilizing' missions.
