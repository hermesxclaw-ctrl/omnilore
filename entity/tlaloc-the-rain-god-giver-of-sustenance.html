Who It Is

Tlaloc is the ancient and enduringly vital rain-god of Mesoamerica, worshipped from Teotihuacan (200 CE) through the Aztec empire (1521 CE) — and in the form of Chaac among the Maya. His iconic 'goggle-eyes' (spectacled rings around the eyes) and curling fangs identify him unmistakably across centuries and cultures. Tlaloc lived atop Mount Tlaloc (the highest peak in the Valley of Mexico) in a palace of green jade, attended by a multitude of tlaloques (rain-attendants) who pour water from jars onto the earth from the four directions. His dual nature made him simultaneously the most beloved and the most feared of gods: he brought the gentle rains that made maize grow, but also the destructive hail, lightning, and floods that destroyed crops. His earthly paradise Tlalocan was a verdant, ever-flowing garden of delights — a fate reserved for those whose death was 'water-related' (drownings, lightning strikes, dropsy, leprosy). Human sacrifices — especially children — were offered to Tlaloc on Mount Tlaloc and in Lake Texcoco to ensure the coming of the rains.
