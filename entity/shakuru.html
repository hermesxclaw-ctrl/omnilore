I am the sun, and I am a mother. Every morning I rise to feed my children with light. Every evening I rest, knowing they have grown another day.

Who It Is

Shakuru is the Pawnee sun goddess — one of the relatively rare female solar deities in world mythology. She is the wife of Pah (the moon god) and together they are the parents of the first human beings. In Pawnee cosmology, Shakuru governs the day, providing the light and warmth that sustain all life on the Great Plains. Her daily journey across the sky, from east to west, mirrors the proper path of human life in Pawnee thought: born in the east (the direction of beginnings), reaching full strength at midday, and descending in the west (the direction of endings and the afterlife). Unlike her peaceful husband Pah, Shakuru's nature contains the potential for harshness — the scorching summer sun could destroy crops as easily as nurture them, and she demanded proper ritual attention. The Sun Dance, a ceremony shared by many Great Plains tribes, was in part directed toward her, and Pawnee sacred bundles contained items dedicated to her worship. She is associated with the color yellow and with the sacred tobacco that was offered to the sky in prayer. The gendering of the sun as female among the Pawnee is notable and sets them apart from the more common male sun / female moon pattern seen across many world mythologies. It speaks to a Pawnee understanding of the sun as fundamentally maternal — the source of warmth and life, like a mother nursing her children. After the Pawnee removal to Oklahoma and the suppression of their ceremonial system, Shakuru's worship declined, but her role in Pawnee cultural memory endures.
