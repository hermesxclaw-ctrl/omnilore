The Approach

The name Yamato resonates through Japanese history like a great bell—from a misty province in Nara to the very soul of a nation. It adorns the world's largest battleship, a legendary prince, and even a Martian meteorite. But what is Yamato truly? A place, a people, a spirit, a sword? Its story is a tapestry woven from myth, empire, war, and identity.

Content advisory. This dossier covers the Yamato period and the battleship Yamato, including themes of militarism, war, and nationalism. Discussions of Yamato-damashii and wartime propaganda are presented with scholarly context.
Identity

Yamato

Pronunciation Yah-mah-toh

Known As

大和DaiwaTaiwaYamato ProvinceYamato KingshipYamato-damashiiYamato Takeru

Etymology

The name Yamato (大和) is written with characters meaning 'great harmony,' but this is an ateji (phonetic) spelling. The original meaning is obscure; it may derive from 'Yama-to' (mountain place) or from the name of the Yamato clan. The older form was 'Yamato' (倭), written with a character meaning 'submissive' or 'dwarf,' later replaced by the more auspicious 大和.

Cultural Ownership

Japan, specifically the Yamato people and the Imperial House of Japan. The name is deeply intertwined with Japanese national identity and the Japanese language.

Disputed Identifications

The identification of Yamatai (邪馬台国) with Yamato is a major scholarly debate. Some equate Yamatai with Yamato, while others place it in northern Kyushu. This is a long-standing controversy in Japanese history.

Description

Appearance

Yamato as a region is characterized by mountainous terrain and valleys, with the Yamato River flowing through it. As a province, it corresponds to modern Nara Prefecture. The Yamato people are the dominant ethnic group of Japan, sharing common physical features typical of East Asians.

Symbols & Iconography

The chrysanthemum is the symbol of the Imperial House of Japan, which is also known as the Yamato dynasty. The Yamato-e style of painting is characterized by Japanese landscapes and scenes from literature, often using bright colors and gold leaf.

Behaviour & Personality

Yamato-damashii, or the 'Japanese spirit,' is a cultural concept emphasizing bravery, loyalty, and national pride. Yamato nadeshiko refers to the idealized Japanese woman, embodying grace, modesty, and domestic virtue. Yamato Takeru is depicted as a heroic and fearless prince, though also tragic and flawed.

Domains & Habitats

Yamato Province is in the Kansai region of Japan, centered on the Nara Basin. The Yamato River flows from Nara into Osaka. The Yamato Kingship was based in this region from the Kofun period.

Version Differences

The name Yamato has been used for different entities: the ancient province, the entire country of Japan, the imperial dynasty, a group of people, and various modern towns and ships. The battleship Yamato is a specific historical vessel, not directly related to the ancient province except in name.

Powers & Abilities

Yamato-damashii (Japanese Spirit)

Source Version Historically used in Japanese nationalism, particularly during the Meiji and Showa periods.

Description

A cultural concept representing the unique qualities of the Japanese people, including courage, perseverance, and patriotism. It is not a supernatural power but a set of ideals.

Limitations

Not a physical ability; its meaning is subjective and has been criticized for its nationalist connotations.

Contradictions

Some argue it is a modern invention, while others see it as a continuation of ancient ideals.

Yamato Takeru's martial prowess

Source Version Kojiki and Nihon Shoki

Description

In legend, Yamato Takeru is a prince with extraordinary strength and martial skills. He uses a sword (possibly the Kusanagi no Tsurugi) and has the ability to defeat enemies and even control nature through his divine lineage.

Limitations

In the legends, he is not invincible; he dies due to a curse and a snake.

Contradictions

The exact nature of his powers is mythological, and historical evidence of his existence is lacking.

Battleship Yamato's firepower

Source Version Imperial Japanese Navy, commissioned 1941

Description

The battleship Yamato was the largest battleship ever built, with 18.1-inch (46 cm) main guns, capable of firing massive shells over 40 kilometers.

Limitations

Despite its power, it was vulnerable to air attack and was sunk by carrier aircraft.

Contradictions

Some argue it was a strategic waste, while others see it as a symbol of Japanese naval power.

Stories & Myths

The Tale of Yamato Takeru

Source Kojiki and Nihon ShokiCulture JapanesePeriod Legendary, possibly 4th-5th century CE

Summary

Yamato Takeru, son of Emperor Keiko, is a legendary prince known for his bravery. He is sent to subdue the Kumaso in Kyushu, and later the Emishi in the east. He famously kills his brother, disguises as a woman to assassinate the Kumaso chief, and later dies after defeating a deity's curse, turning into a white bird.

Participants

Yamato Takeru

Emperor Keiko

Kumaso leaders

Miyazu-hime

Emishi

Consequences

He is considered a cultural hero and his exploits are celebrated in literature and art. His death is seen as a tragedy caused by divine retribution.

Variants

The Kojiki and Nihon Shoki have differing accounts of his genealogy and some events.

The Rise of the Yamato State

Source Scholarly consensus based on archaeology and Chinese chroniclesCulture JapanesePeriod 3rd-7th century CE

Summary

The Yamato Kingship emerged in the Kofun period, centered in the Nara Basin. It consolidated power through military conquest and alliances, and its rulers claimed descent from the sun goddess Amaterasu. The state controlled trade and built massive keyhole-shaped burial mounds (kofun).

Participants

Yamato clan

Various local chieftains

Korean kingdoms

Consequences

The Yamato state laid the foundation for the Japanese imperial system and the modern nation.

Variants

The process of state formation is debated; some emphasize migration, others internal development.

The Sinking of the Battleship Yamato

Source Historical records from the Imperial Japanese Navy and Allied forcesCulture JapanesePeriod April 7, 1945

Summary

The battleship Yamato, the largest ever built, was sent on a suicidal mission to Okinawa to support the defenders. It was intercepted by American carrier aircraft and sunk after receiving heavy damage, with the loss of over 3,000 crew.

Participants

Japanese Navy

US Navy

Crew of Yamato

Consequences

The sinking marked the end of the Japanese battleship era and became a symbol of Japanese sacrifice and futility in WWII.

Variants

The mission is often portrayed as a suicide mission; some argue it was intended to be a final glorious battle.

Relationships

Type dynasticSource Kojiki, Nihon Shoki

Description

Yamato is the dynastic name of the Japanese imperial family, which claims descent from the sun goddess Amaterasu.

Version Note

The term 'Yamato dynasty' is used by historians to refer to the imperial line during the Kofun and Asuka periods.

Conflicting Accounts

The divine descent is mythological; historical origins are debated.

Yamato-damashii and Nationalism

Source Historical analyses of Japanese nationalism, e.g., by John Dower and Carol Gluck

Description

During the Meiji and Showa periods, the concept of Yamato-damashii was used to promote Japanese nationalism and militarism, often in a racial and supremacist manner, leading to the justification of imperial expansion and war crimes.

Scholarly Context

Scholars argue that Yamato-damashii was a constructed ideology rather than an ancient tradition, used to mobilize the population for war.

The Yamato as a Symbol of Military Sacrifice

Source Historical accounts of the Battle of Okinawa, e.g., by Paul Dull and John Toland

Description

The battleship Yamato's final mission is often romanticized as a symbol of self-sacrifice, but it resulted in the deaths of thousands of sailors in a futile operation. Some view it as a tragic waste of life due to flawed military leadership.

Scholarly Context

The mission is criticized as a kamikaze-like strategy that prioritized honor over effectiveness.

Yamato as a Name for Colonial Expansion

Source The document 'An Investigation of Global Policy with the Yamato Race as Nucleus' (1943)

Description

The term Yamato was used to refer to the Japanese race in colonial and wartime propaganda, such as in the document 'An Investigation of Global Policy with the Yamato Race as Nucleus,' which envisioned Japanese dominance in Asia.

Scholarly Context

This document is a prime example of the racist ideology behind Japanese imperialism.

Contradictions & Disputes

Location of Yamatai

Position A

Yamatai was located in the Kinai region (Yamato).

Position A — evidence

Later Japanese chronicles associate Yamatai with Yamato; some archaeological finds in Nara.

Position B

Yamatai was located in northern Kyushu.

Position B — evidence

Chinese records (Wei Zhi) describe Yamatai as being accessible by ship from Korea, suggesting Kyushu; some archaeological sites in Kyushu.

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus; the debate is ongoing.

Historical existence of Yamato Takeru

Position A

Yamato Takeru was a real historical figure.

Position A — evidence

The Kojiki and Nihon Shoki present him as a historical prince.

Position B

Yamato Takeru is a legendary or mythical figure.

Position B — evidence

No contemporary records; his exploits are legendary; some scholars see him as a composite.

Scholarly Consensus

Most historians treat him as legendary, though some argue for a historical basis.

Value of the battleship Yamato

Position A

The Yamato was a symbol of Japanese naval power and a necessary deterrent.

Position A — evidence

Its firepower and size were unmatched; it boosted morale.

Position B

The Yamato was a wasteful expenditure that drained resources and had little strategic value.

Position B — evidence

It was rarely used in combat and was vulnerable to air attacks; its construction consumed huge resources.

Scholarly Consensus

Most historians consider it a strategic failure.

Known Gaps

This research focuses on the historical, cultural, and mythological dimensions of Yamato, including its geographical origins, the Yamato period, Yamato-damashii, Yamato Takeru, and the battleship Yamato. It also surveys the name's modern usage in media and entertainment. The research draws on encyclopedic sources and reputable references but acknowledges limitations in accessing primary Japanese texts and scholarly articles.
