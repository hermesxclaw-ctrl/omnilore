Form

No attested physical description exists in the sources reviewed. If Akycha is a Sun deity, she may be depicted in modern art as a woman with a torch or a glowing figure, but this is modern interpretation, not traditional attestation. Some Inuit traditions describe the Sun as a woman fleeing from her brother (the Moon), but whether this applies specifically to Akycha is unconfirmed.

Powers

Solar control (reconstructed) If Akycha is a Sun deity, she would control the sun's path, light, and warmth. In Arctic contexts, her presence or absence would explain day/night and the long polar nights. Reconstructed from general Inuit solar myths; not directly attested for 'Akycha' by name. None attested. In some Inuit stories, the Sun is not all-powerful but is a character in a family drama (fleeing the Moon), so her power may be limited by her personal circumstances. Some sources present the Sun as a benevolent provider; others (in shamanic contexts) as a being to be respected but not worshipped. No consensus.

Accounts

A brother and sister (often unnamed or named as Moon and Sun) are in a camp. The sister is assaulted by a man in the dark; she discovers it is her brother. In shame/anger, she cuts off her breasts and gives them to him, or she flees with a torch. She becomes the Sun, he becomes the Moon, and he chases her forever. The Sun's light is her torch; the Moon's pale light is his shame. This story explains the celestial chase and the sun's warmth.

Beyond the incest narrative, the Sun Woman (often identified as Malina, Seqinek, or in some obscure sources Akycha) embodies the profound relationship between the Inuit peoples and the Arctic solar cycle. In a land where the sun disappears entirely for months during the polar night and blazes continuously during the midnight sun of summer, the Sun deity is not merely a distant celestial body but a living presence whose movements dictate the rhythm of all existence. Inuit stories describe the Sun Woman traversing the sky in her sled or on foot, carrying a great torch or lamp that provides light and warmth to the world below. During winter, she retreats to her dwelling in the south, resting and gathering strength for her return. Her reappearance at the end of the long polar night was a moment of profound relief and celebration, marking the renewal of life and the return of hunting opportunities. Shamans were sometimes said to journey to the Sun Woman's dwelling during trance states, seeking her favor or appealing to her for the health of the community.

Content Notes

The most common Inuit Sun-Moon myth involves a brother's sexual assault of his sister. If Akycha is the Sun, this story is central to her identity. It is a narrative about shame, flight, and eternal separation.

This myth is often analyzed as a social cautionary tale about family boundaries and the consequences of violence. It is not unique to Inuit culture; similar motifs appear in other Arctic and sub-Arctic traditions.

General Inuit mythology (Boas, Rasmussen); not directly tied to 'Akycha' by name in available sources.

Early European ethnographers (e.g., Boas, Rasmussen) recorded Inuit traditions through a colonial lens, sometimes Christianizing or rationalizing them. The name 'Akycha' may be a product of such a record, possibly a mishearing or a regional variant that was not fully contextualized.

Modern Inuit scholars and community members are re-evaluating these records. The term 'Akycha' may not be used by contemporary Inuit communities, or it may be a specific local name that is now obscure.

Critical historiography of Arctic anthropology (e.g., work by L. J. Dorais, B. Saladin d'Anglure).

Gender of the Sun in Inuit mythology
