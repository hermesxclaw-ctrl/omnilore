Who It Is

A single filament of drawn metal — thinner than a finger, stronger than it looks — holds up bridges, carries voices across oceans, and once made gold gleam on the necks of Egyptian queens. Wire is civilization pulled thin.

As an inanimate artifact, wire exhibits material behaviors: high tensile strength along its axis, flexibility allowing bending and coiling, elastic springback (especially in steel music wire), conductivity for heat and electricity (highest in copper, silver, aluminum), and fatigue failure under repeated bending. Stranded wire trades mechanical ruggedness for flexibility and fatigue resistance. Behavior is governed by metallurgy and temper: annealed wire is ductile and soft; hard-drawn wire is springy and stronger but brittle. Powers
Mechanical load bearing (wire rope and structural wire)

Single wire has high tensile strength; multiple wires helically laid into strand and rope multiply capacity for cranes, elevators, suspension cables, and guy wires. Steel music wire exceeds 2000 MPa tensile strength.

Limit: Fails under shear, kinking, fatigue, corrosion and overload. Requires proper termination and lubrication. Not attested as load-bearing in earliest gold jewelry wires (decorative only).

Electrical conduction

Copper, silver, aluminum and other metals conduct electricity with low resistance; wire is the primary medium for power distribution and signal transmission. Conductivity maximized by annealing; purity and cross-sectional area determine ampacity.

Limit: Resistance causes heating (I²R losses); skin effect at high frequencies confines current to surface, increasing effective resistance. Insulation required to prevent short circuits. Aluminum has lower conductivity than copper for same gauge.

Telecommunications signal transmission

Wire carries telephony, telegraphy, data and RF signals; coaxial construction (central conductor, dielectric, shield) enables high-frequency transmission with shielding. Submarine cables carry intercontinental signals.

Limit: Attenuation, crosstalk, electromagnetic interference; requires repeaters, shielding and proper impedance matching. Fiber optics has superseded long-haul copper for bandwidth.

Flexibility and fatigue resistance (stranded construction)

Stranded wire composed of many small filaments bundled together is more flexible and resistant to metal fatigue than solid wire of same cross-section. Used for moving connections, appliance cords, musical instrument cables, welding and mining trailing cables.

Limit: Higher cost, larger diameter for same conductance due to interstitial gaps, more surface area exposed to corrosion, not ideal for breadboard or permanent fixed wiring where solid wire is cheaper and more rugged.

Formability into specialty geometries

Wire can be drawn to non-circular cross-sections (square, hexagonal, flattened rectangular) for decorative or technical purposes: high-efficiency voice coils in loudspeakers, edge-wound coil springs such as Slinky, beaded and notched decorative wires.

Limit: Requires specialized dies (hard steel, diamond, ruby) and precise annealing; non-standard sections increase die wear and cost. Diamond dies must be rebored when worn; metal dies hammered and drifted back to size.

Thermal and joining utility

Wire serves as filler in welding (electrode wire), as heating element (nichrome), and as binding/lashing material. Annealed wire is easily soldered, brazed or welded.

Limit: Melting point and oxidation limit high-temperature use; requires flux or shielding gas for welding

Accounts I
The Egyptian Strip and the Spiral SeamArchaeological jewelry analysis; Oddy 1977, Ogden 1982

Early Dynastic to New Kingdom, c.2890-1070 BCE

The earliest Egyptian gold wire was not drawn but made by cutting thin strips from sheet metal and pulling them through perforations in stone beads, folding the strip into a tube. By the mid-2nd millennium BCE, craftsmen twisted these strips and rolled them between flat surfaces to make solid round wires, leaving a characteristic spiral seam visible under magnification. This technique dominated gold jewelry for over a millennium. Enabled mass production of fine chains and applied decoration; established the technological lineage that later evolved into true drawing. Spiral-seam wires become diagnostic for dating and provenance.

Beads Without Granules: The Phoenician Beaded WireOgden 1982, Williams & Ogden 1994

7th century BCE - 10th century CE

True beaded wire - a round wire mechanically distorted to create a line of bead-like swellings that imitates granulation - appears in the 7th century BCE in the Levant and Italy, likely spread by Phoenician traders. It allowed jewelers to achieve the look of granulated beads without soldering individual granules. It remained popular until about the 10th century CE, when twisted pairs of drawn round wires ('ropes') offered a simpler alternative. Widespread adoption in Mediterranean jewelry; later decline marks technological shift to drawn-wire ropes. Beaded wire revived in modern jewelry as heritage technique.

From Grooved Anvil to Draw-Plate: Swaging and the Triumph of DrawingOddy 1977; Theophilus Presbyter De Diversis Artibus III.8-9

c.2000 BCE - 10th century CE

Square and hexagonal wires for torcs and fibulae were made by swaging - striking a rod between grooved blocks or punch and anvil - from the early 2nd millennium BCE. Twisted square-section wire became hallmark Etruscan filigree. The strip-twist method persisted until the Old World transition to true wire drawing through a die, dated between the 8th and 10th centuries AD, with evidence suggesting earlier drawing in the East. Theophilus in the 12th century describes drawing gold and silver through holes in an iron plate. Drawing enabled finer, longer, more uniform wire and industrial scaling; swaging survived for specialty sections. The transition is a key horizon in archaeometallurgy.

Accounts II
Tintern and the English Wire MonopolyStatutes of the Realm 3 Edward IV c.4 (1463); Day 1973; King 2002

1463 - late 17th century

Edward IV banned import of wool cards and pins - both wire-dependent goods - to protect English manufacture. The first English wire mill was established at Tintern c.1568 by the Company of Mineral and Battery Works, holders of a royal monopoly, with a second mill at Whitebrook. Despite water-powered mills, fine drawing remained manual. No other mills existed before the second half of the 17th century, constraining supply. Created domestic wire industry; monopoly and limited mills kept prices high and technique artisanal. Expansion after 1650 fueled Industrial Revolution demand for pins, needles, cards and later industrial wire.

Diamond Dies and Polymer Jackets: The Modern WireEncyclopaedia Britannica 11th ed. 1911; modern engineering handbooks; source material production section

19th century - present

Industrial wire is reduced to exact diameter by repeated drawing through progressively smaller dies, with intermediate annealing to restore ductility and conductivity. Dies evolved from hard iron/steel to diamond and ruby for fine wire to maintain dimensional accuracy; diamond dies are rebored, metal dies hammered and drifted. Insulation shifted from treated cloth/paper and oil compounds to extruded plastic and rubber-like polymers from the mid-1960s. Concentric wrapping machines produce coaxial and multi-stranded cables, with rotating cages and perforated disks laying cotton, tape or polymer in helical overlaps. Specialty flattened wire enables edge-wound coils like the Slinky (invented 1943, marketed 1945). Standardized wire gauges, electrification, telecommunications, and mass-produced consumer goods. Enabled submarine cables, power grids, and miniaturized electronics.

Solid vs. Stranded: Two Philosophies of CopperSource material forms section; Grob's Basic Electronics; NFPA 70 National Electrical Code

20th century - present

Solid-core wire (single strand) is cheap, rugged, corrosion-resistant and ideal for fixed wiring and breadboards. Stranded wire bundles many fine filaments for flexibility and fatigue resistance, essential for moving connections (circuit-board interconnects, appliance cords, mining trailing cables) but at cost of higher resistance per diameter due to air gaps and greater exposed surface. At high frequencies, skin effect drives current to the surface; ordinary stranded wire does not mitigate this because strands are shorted together. Codified distinct applications in wiring codes; informs choice between solid building wire and stranded flexible cord. Misconception about stranded wire and skin effect persists in hobbyist literature.

Wire has always been made by drawing through a die — For over 3000 years wire was made by strip folding, strip-twisting and swaging. True drawing through a die only became dominant between the 8th-10th centuries AD in the West (earlier debated in the East).

Wire is always cylindrical — While usually cylindrical, wire is intentionally made in square, hexagonal, flattened rectangular and beaded cross-sections for decorative (Etruscan filigree, beaded wire) and technical (voice coils, Slinky) purposes, as noted in source material.

Stranded wire is always better than solid wire because it is more flexible — Stranded is more flexible and fatigue-resistant, but solid is cheaper, more rugged, more corrosion-resistant (less exposed surface) and preferred for fixed wiring and breadboards. Choice depends on application; codes specify each.

Beaded wire is made by soldering tiny beads onto wire — True beaded wire is made by mechanically distorting a single round wire to create bead-like swellings that imitate granulation, not by adding beads. This technique appeared in the 7th century BCE.

Wire gauges are universal — Multiple incompatible gauge systems exist (American Wire Gauge AWG, Standard Wire Gauge SWG/British, metric cross-section mm², Birmingham gauge). A gauge number means different diameters in different systems.
