Who It Is

Kobolds are D&D's smallest dragon-kin — reptilian humanoids standing a mere 2 to 3 feet tall, with scaly hides ranging from rust-red to brownish-black, small horns, a dog-like snout (in some editions), and a long rat-like or lizard-like tail. Despite their diminutive size and individual weakness, kobolds are among the most dangerous low-level threats in D&D — not because of their combat prowess, but because of their intelligence, craftiness, and fanatical devotion to dragons. A kobold warren is a deathtrap labyrinth filled with pit traps, rockslides, poison darts, collapsing ceilings, and boiling oil — every inch engineered to compensate for their physical frailty.

Kobolds believe themselves to be the direct descendants of dragons (specifically, they claim kinship with the chromatic dragon Tiamat), and their culture revolves around draconic worship. A kobold tribe that discovers a dragon — or even just a drake or wyvern — will serve it as slaves, worshippers, and minions, considering the mere presence of a dragon to be a divine blessing. Kobolds who display sorcerous talent, particularly with fire or acid magic, are elevated to positions of leadership as 'dragonshields' or 'scale sorcerers'.

The most ingenious kobold is the trapsmith, whose elaborate mechanical contraptions can challenge even high-level adventurers. The most famous representation of this is 'Tucker's Kobolds' — a legendary story from D&D history in which a Dungeon Master named Tucker created a kobold warren so lethally engineered that a high-level adventuring party was nearly wiped out by what should have been trivial enemies. The story has become a touchstone of encounter design: 'Tucker's Kobolds' proves that intelligent tactics and prepared terrain matter more than raw stats.

Kobolds are also known for their rapid breeding and short lifespans (they reach adulthood in 6 years and rarely live past 20), creating a cultural attitude of suicidal bravery — a kobold who dies in service to the tribe or its dragon master is honored, and a dead kobold is simply replaced tomorrow. This makes them relentless in their attacks, utterly fearless when defending their warren, and terrifyingly pragmatic about sacrificing waves of their own kind to wear down intruders. Kobolds have appeared across D&D video games and have become fan favorites, with many players choosing to play kobold adventurers as scrappy underdogs. Their name comes from Germanic folklore, where a Kobold was a household or mine spirit — sometimes helpful, sometimes mischievous — but D&D's version is entirely its own creation.
