The Approach

Mena is a name that spans continents and centuries, from a Roman goddess overseeing the monthly cycle to a Hindu queen of the mountains, from a quiet hamlet in Cornwall to a crater on Mars. This single word carries a mosaic of meanings, each with its own story, and yet the threads that connect them are often as thin as a shared syllable.

Content advisory. No dark material is associated with the mythological figures named Mena. However, the name is also used for places and entities with no mythological content; this dossier focuses on the mythological uses.
Identity

Mena

Pronunciation Latin: MEH-nah; Sanskrit: MAY-nah

Known As

Dea MenaMenavatiMainavati

Etymology

The name Mena likely derives from the Latin 'menstrua' meaning 'monthly' or 'menses', relating to the menstrual cycle. In Hindu context, Mena (मेना) is a Sanskrit name possibly derived from 'mena' meaning 'a kind of bird' or 'female', but its exact etymology is uncertain.

Cultural Ownership

Roman (ancient) and Hindu (Indian)

Disputed Identifications

The Roman Mena is sometimes confused with other minor goddesses like Mena (goddess of menstruation) and may be conflated with Menstrua or Mene. In Hindu tradition, Mena is also associated with the goddess Parvati's mother, but there are regional variations in her story.

Description

Appearance

Roman: No specific physical description survives; she was likely personified as a female deity. Hindu: In iconography, Mena is depicted as a beautiful queen, often shown with her husband Himavan. She is usually dressed in royal attire, with a gentle demeanor.

Symbols & Iconography

Roman: No known symbols; she was invoked for protection during menstruation. Hindu: She is sometimes shown with a lotus or in the company of her daughter Parvati. Her association with the Himalayas is symbolized by mountains.

Behaviour & Personality

Roman: As a functionary deity, she was believed to oversee the menstrual cycle, ensuring regular periods and fertility. Hindu: Mena is portrayed as a loving mother, devoted wife, and sometimes as a protective figure who initially opposes her daughter's asceticism but later relents.

Domains & Habitats

Roman: She was invoked in domestic rituals, particularly by women. Hindu: She resides in the Himalayas with her husband Himavan, the personification of the mountain range.

Version Differences

In the Roman tradition, Mena is a minor deity with a specific function. In Hindu tradition, she is a significant character in the mythology of Shiva and Parvati. There is no connection between the two figures; the name is coincidental or derived from different roots.

Powers & Abilities

Menstrual cycle regulation

As a Roman goddess, Mena was believed to control the menstrual cycle, ensuring its regularity and the ability to conceive.

Limitations

Not well-documented; only known through brief mentions in theological texts.

Motherhood and fertility

Source Version Hindu

Description

In Hindu tradition, Mena is the mother of Parvati and Ganga, and her role emphasizes fertility and maternal care.

Limitations

No independent powers; her significance is derived from her family relationships.

Contradictions

Some regional tales may attribute specific blessings or boons to her, but these are not universally attested.

Stories & Myths

Birth of Parvati

Source Shiva Purana, KumarasambhavaCulture HinduPeriod Classical India

Summary

Mena, wife of Himavan, performs austerities to obtain a daughter who will marry Shiva. She gives birth to Parvati, who grows up to be a great devotee of Shiva and eventually marries him after undergoing severe penance.

Participants

Mena

Himavan

Parvati

Shiva

Consequences

Parvati becomes Shiva's consort, and their union produces sons like Ganesha and Kartikeya, influencing Hindu mythology significantly.

Variants

In some versions, Mena is initially reluctant to let Parvati marry the ascetic Shiva, but later accepts. In others, she is supportive from the start.

Mena's Initial Opposition

Source Regional folklore, Tamil and Bengali versionsCulture HinduPeriod Medieval to modern

Summary

When Parvati decides to perform severe penance to win Shiva, Mena opposes her, fearing for her daughter's well-being. She tries to distract her with worldly pleasures but fails. Eventually, she accepts Parvati's choice.

Participants

Mena

Parvati

Himavan

Consequences

This story highlights Mena's maternal love and the tension between worldly attachment and spiritual devotion.

Variants

In some versions, Mena is aided by the sage Narada, who convinces her to support Parvati.

Contradictions & Disputes

Identity of Mena

Position A

Mena is a distinct Roman goddess of menstruation.

Position A — evidence

Augustine and Varro list her separately.

Position B

Mena might be a title or epithet for another goddess like Juno or Diana.

Position B — evidence

Some scholars argue minor deities were often aspects of major ones.

Scholarly Consensus

Most accept Mena as a distinct minor deity, though her cult is poorly attested.

Mena as mother of Ganga

Position A

Mena is the mother of both Parvati and Ganga.

Position A — evidence

Some Puranic texts and regional traditions.

Position B

Ganga is not the daughter of Mena but of Himavan or another origin.

Position B — evidence

Other texts describe Ganga as emerging from Vishnu's foot or being the daughter of Meru.

Scholarly Consensus

There is no consensus; it varies by text and region.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

The Roman Mena is poorly attested; there is no dedicated archaeological or epigraphic evidence. The Hindu Mena is better documented, but regional variations are not exhaustively catalogued. The etymology of the name in both traditions is uncertain. Further research into primary sources and scholarly analyses is needed to deepen the dossier.

Research Scope

This dossier covers the mythological figures named Mena: the Roman goddess Dea Mena and the Hindu goddess Mena (also known as Menavati or Mainavati). It also briefly touches on other notable uses of the name to disambiguate, but does not provide exhaustive coverage of non-mythological entities.
