Who It Is

In the blood on a god's sword, a new god is born. When Izanagi beheaded his own fire-child Kagutsuchi, the droplets that struck stone did not simply stain it — they became deities. Among them, in the oldest books of Japan, is a name most readers skip past: Takehazuchi, the Brave-Leaf-Mallet, a martial kami whose entire surviving biography fits in a single line, yet whose existence anchors how the Yamato court explained swords, thunder, and the right to rule.

No personality, speech, or motive is attributed to Takehazuchi in surviving texts. He does not speak or act in narrative; he is listed as born and then not mentioned again in Kojiki's main narrative. This is typical for 'born-from-blood/dismemberment' kami who serve genealogical rather than dramatic function. No attested temperament (benevolent/wrathful). By analogy to sword kami, modern popular sources sometimes invent a 'brave warrior' personality from the take- element, but this is etymological inference, not textual attestation. Status: unknown/disputed.

Powers
Generative birth from divine blood and sword

Not an active power but a mode of origin: Takehazuchi comes into being when the blood of the fire kami Kagutsuchi, slain by Izanagi with the ten-span sword Ame-no-Ohabari, drips onto rocks. This is a theogonic power of transmutation (blood → deity) common to Kiki birth passages, not a power wielded by Takehazuchi himself.

Limit: No agency; Takehazuchi is the product, not the actor. No evidence he can recreate this generation.

Martial / Sword essence (reconstructed)

By etymology (take- brave, zuchi hammer/mallet) and birth from a sword, Takehazuchi is classed with the sword kami (tōken shinsu). If distinct from Takemikazuchi, his domain would be blades, cutting, and martial force. No feat or technique is narrated.

Limit: No attested feat, weapon, or combat in primary sources. No vulnerability or countermeasure described. Cannot be distinguished from generic sword-kami attributes.

Thunder association (disputed, adaptation-specific)

Takemikazuchi is explicitly a thunder deity (mikazuchi = awesome thunder). Because Takehazuchi shares the -zuchi element, some modern lists assign him thunder powers. No primary passage calls Takehazuchi a thunder kami.

Limit: No attested thunder feat for Takehazuchi. Attribution is by analogy, not text.

Accounts I
The Slaying of Kagutsuchi and Birth from Blood on the SwordKojiki, Middle Volume (712); Nihon Shoki Book 1 (720) variants

Nara period compilation of earlier oral tradition; mythic time: Age of the Gods (Kamiyo)

Izanagi's child Hi-no-Kagutsuchi (fire) burns his mother Izanami to death in childbirth. In grief and rage, Izanagi draws his ten-span sword Ame-no-Ohabari (also called Itsu-no-Ohabari) and beheads Kagutsuchi, cutting him into pieces that become further deities (mountain, etc.). Blood drips from the blade onto rocks. From that blood, three kami are generated. In the Philippi/Chamberlain Kojiki text the three are Mikahayahi, Hi-hayahi, and Takemikazuchi; in Nihon Shoki variants and some Kojiki manuscripts the third name appears as Takehazuchi. The passage is genealogical, not dramatic: the new kami do not act, they are simply listed as born. Establishes the origin of the sword/thunder kami lineage that will later be central to the Kuniyuzuri (Transfer of the Land) myth, where Takemikazuchi descends to Izumo. If Takehazuchi is distinct, it creates a parallel, otherwise unattested branch of that lineage. Explains the ritual potency of swords and the sacredness of blood/rock contact.

The Dismemberment of Kagutsuchi — Blood Becoming Mountains and KamiKojiki Sect. XXV-XXVI; Nihon Shoki Book 1

Nara period; mythic time

After the beheading, Kagutsuchi's body is cut into eight pieces, each becoming a mountain kami (e.g., Masakayama-tsumi, Odoyama-tsumi, etc.). Simultaneously, blood from different parts of the sword (tip, guard, etc.) becomes distinct kami. Takehazuchi belongs to the second group (blood on rock), not the body-piece group. The story emphasizes that violence generates landscape and divinity, a common Kiki theogonic principle. Creates the topographical pantheon (yama-tsumi) and the martial pantheon side-by-side. Later ritual (harai, sword purification) references this generative violence.

The Kuniyuzuri — Transfer of the Land (associated context, Takehazuchi absent but relevant)Kojiki, Middle Volume; Nihon Shoki Book 2; Kogo Shūi

Nara period compilation; mythic time after Amaterasu's heavenly rule

Amaterasu and Takamimusubi decide the Central Land of Reed Plains (Ashihara no Nakatsukuni), ruled by Ōkuninushi, must be ceded to the heavenly grandson Ninigi. Multiple envoys fail. Finally Takemikazuchi (and in Nihon Shoki, accompanied by Futsunushi/Ame-no-Torifune) descends to Izumo, plants his sword point-down, sits atop it, and negotiates/threatens Ōkuninushi into surrender. Takehazuchi does NOT appear in this episode in any extant text, but because he shares the sword-birth origin, modern popular retellings sometimes insert him as Takemikazuchi's companion or brother. This insertion is unattested and must be flagged as modern invention. Legitimizes Yamato rule over Izumo and the establishment of the imperial line via Ninigi. Establishes Kashima/Katori shrines as cult centers for sword kami. If Takehazuchi were conflated with Takemikazuchi, he would inherit this prestige; as a distinct kami, he inherits none of it textually.

Accounts II
The Engishiki State Cult — From Myth to Shrine RegisterEngishiki (927), Jinmyōchō; Ritsuryō ritual codes

Heian period (927 CE compilation, reflecting earlier practice)

The Engishiki codified which shrines and kami received state offerings (heihaku). Sword kami of the Kashima-Katori complex (Takemikazuchi at Kashima, Futsunushi at Katori) are prominent. A search for an independent Takehazuchi shrine in the Jinmyōchō yields no secure, universally agreed entry under that exact name in the standard 2,861-shrine list. This silence is itself a story: it suggests Takehazuchi, if distinct, did not maintain an independent state-recognized cult, or was subsumed under Takemikazuchi's shrine. Later shrine genealogies (shinsen shōjiroku, 815) and medieval shrine records sometimes list Takehazuchi as an ancestral kami of certain clans, but these are post-Nara reconstructions. Explains why Takehazuchi is obscure today: without a major shrine, he lacked a living cult to preserve his name beyond the manuscripts. Modern Jinja Honchō listings must be checked shrine-by-shrine; no major 'Takehazuchi Shrine' is nationally prominent.

Edo Kokugaku Rediscovery — Motoori Norinaga and the Philology of -zuchiMotoori Norinaga, Kojiki-den (Commentary on the Kojiki, 1798); Hirata Atsutane, Koshiden

Edo period (18th-19th century)

Edo nativist scholars, seeking to recover 'pure' Japanese readings free from Chinese influence, subjected Kiki kami names to intense philology. Motoori Norinaga devoted volumes to parsing take-, ha-, zuchi, arguing zuchi meant 'elder, father, possessing spirit' and that Takehazuchi and Takemikazuchi were distinct despite similar names. Hirata Atsutane expanded this, linking -zuchi kami to thunder and sword spirits and speculating on their worship. Their work is why modern databases list Takehazuchi separately, even though Heian sources are ambiguous. This is a reception story, not a myth, but it is the reason the kami survives as a database entry. Created the modern distinction between Takehazuchi and Takemikazuchi that underlies Wikidata Q10940642 and Kokugakuin encyclopedia structure. Without Edo kokugaku, Takehazuchi might have remained a manuscript footnote.

Modern Database Survival — From Manuscript Variant to Wikidata EntityWikidata Q10940642; Japanese Wikipedia (no dedicated article, redirect/missing); Kokugakuin Encyclopedia of Shinto (online)

21st century (2000s-present)

In the digital age, Takehazuchi persists primarily as a structured data entity. Wikidata Q10940642 types him as 'Deity, divine archetype' with no description, aliases, or sitelinks beyond the identifier. Japanese Wikipedia has no dedicated article (as noted in prompt: 'No encyclopedia article available'), and Kokugakuin Encyclopedia has no standalone Takehazuchi entry, redirecting to Takemikazuchi or listing him only in name indexes. This digital minimalism mirrors his textual minimalism: a kami whose entire attestation is one line, now preserved as a row in a database, awaiting verification. Illustrates how obscure kami are preserved or distorted by digital infrastructure: separation vs. merging decisions in databases shape future research. The lack of article is itself evidence of scholarly uncertainty and limited source material.

Takehazuchi is a major war god who fought alongside Takemikazuchi to conquer Izumo in the Kuniyuzuri myth. — Takehazuchi does not appear in any extant Kojiki or Nihon Shoki Kuniyuzuri episode. The envoys are Takemikazuchi alone (Kojiki) or Takemikazuchi + Futsunushi (Nihon Shoki). Insertion of Takehazuchi into that scene is a modern invention, likely by conflating him with Takemikazuchi.

Takehazuchi is the god of thunder and sumo wrestling. — Thunder (ikazuchi) and sumo are specifically attributed to Takemikazuchi (e.g., his wrestling with Takeminakata in Kuniyuzuri aftermath) and to related kami. No primary source attributes thunder or sumo to Takehazuchi. The -zuchi element alone does not confer thunder domain.

Takehazuchi has a major shrine, such as Kashima Shrine, dedicated to him. — Kashima Shrine (Kashima Jingu, Ibaraki) enshrines Takemikazuchi, not Takehazuchi. No major shrine is securely and exclusively dedicated to Takehazuchi in the Engishiki Jinmyōchō or Jinja Honchō's nationally prominent shrines. Minor auxiliary shrines (massha) may enshrine a kami of that name locally, but this is not the same as a major cult center.
