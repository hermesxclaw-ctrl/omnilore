Who It Is

She has no name. In the oldest surviving English epic, she is known only as 'Grendel's Mother' — a title that defines her entire existence. She is not a queen, not a witch, not a demon, but a grieving mother who swims through a blood-dark lake to avenge her son, and in doing so, nearly kills the greatest hero of the age. Her story is a reminder that in the world of Beowulf, even monsters have mothers — and mothers, in that world, do not forgive.

She acts with a clear motive: revenge for her son's death. Unlike Grendel, who attacks Heorot for years out of envy and spite, she makes a single, swift, devastating raid — killing Æschere, Beowulf's closest advisor — and then retreats to her underwater lair. She is cunning (she waits for night, strikes when the hall is asleep), powerful (she nearly kills Beowulf in her own hall), and relentless (she would have killed him had his mailcoat and God's favor not protected him). Her behavior is driven by the Germanic kinship-code: a mother's duty to avenge her son.

Powers
Superhuman Strength

She grapples with Beowulf on equal terms — the poem says she 'dragged him down' and 'crushed him in her grip.' She tears the mailcoat of the hero, who only survives because of his armor and divine protection.

Limit: She is ultimately overpowered by Beowulf, who finds a giant's sword in her hall and decapitates her.

Invulnerability to Ordinary Weapons

Beowulf's sword, Hrunting, fails to bite her flesh — it 'refused to wound her,' blunted by her monstrous nature.

Limit: She is vulnerable to the giant-forged sword in her own hall, which Beowulf seizes and uses to kill her.

Aquatic Adaptation

She lives beneath a lake and can swim and fight underwater indefinitely. She drags Beowulf down to her cavern-hall, which is somehow dry and lit by fire.

Limit: She is killed in her own hall, suggesting her power is tied to her domain.

Kin-Avenger (Moral Power)

Her motivation is the Germanic revenge-code: she must avenge her son. This gives her a moral dimension that Grendel lacks — she is not a mindless monster but a mother acting on a sacred duty.

Limit: Her revenge is ultimately futile — she dies, and her death ends the monstrous line.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The poem gives no physical description of Grendel's mother. She is described by her actions and her dwelling. She is 'a monstrous woman' (wælgyre wīfes), 'a troll-wife' (grundwyrgenne), and 'the mighty mere-woman' (merewīf mihtig). Modern adaptations invent appearances — a hag, a serpentine woman, a beautiful witch — but the original text leaves her form to the reader's imagination, focusing instead on her strength and her terrible purpose.

Symbols — The mere itself is a symbol: a boundary between the human world of Heorot and the monstrous, chthonic world below. Her underwater hall is a parody of the mead-hall — a place of kinship and community inverted into a place of death and isolation. Accounts I
The Attack on HeorotBeowulf, lines 1255-1298

Composed c. 8th-11th century, set in a legendary 6th-century Scandinavian past

After Beowulf kills Grendel and hangs the monster's arm from Heorot's rafters, the Danes celebrate. That night, Grendel's mother arrives. She is consumed by grief and rage — 'sorrowful, roaming, doomed to a dire fate.' She seizes one warrior — Æschere, the king's closest advisor — and drags him to her lair. She also retrieves Grendel's arm, a detail that shows her cunning and her maternal devotion. The hall, once again, is stained with blood. Beowulf vows to avenge Æschere. Hrothgar, devastated, describes the mere where she lives — a place of horror and mystery. Beowulf sets out with his men to the mere, beginning the second great battle of the poem.

Accounts II
The Battle in the MereBeowulf, lines 1492-1590

Composed c. 8th-11th century

Beowulf dives into the mere. Grendel's mother seizes him and drags him to her underwater hall. Her grip is so strong she crushes him; his sword Hrunting fails to wound her. She draws a knife and tries to stab him, but his mailcoat saves him. Beowulf, desperate, sees a giant-forged sword hanging on the wall of her hall. He seizes it, swings, and decapitates her. The blade melts in her toxic blood, leaving only the hilt. Beowulf finds Grendel's corpse and takes his head as a trophy. He swims back to the surface, carrying the hilt and the head. Beowulf returns to Heorot with Grendel's head and the sword-hilt, which bears a runic inscription telling of the giants and the flood. He is celebrated as a hero. The monstrous line is ended. But the poem's narrator reminds us that Beowulf's own kingdom will one day fall — the cycle of violence continues.

She is a beautiful seductress who tempts Beowulf. — The poem gives her no physical description and no seductive role. She attacks Beowulf with brute force. The seductive portrayal is a 2007 film invention.

She is a 'witch' or 'hag'. — The Old English text calls her a 'mere-woman' and 'troll-wife.' She has no magical powers beyond her strength and aquatic nature. 'Witch' is a modern mistranslation.

She is Grendel's wife or lover. — She is explicitly his mother. The poem is unambiguous on this point.

She is a minor character. — She is the antagonist of the poem's second act, a major figure who drives the plot and is the subject of one of the poem's most famous set-pieces (the mere-scene).
