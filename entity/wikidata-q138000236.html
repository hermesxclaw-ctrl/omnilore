Who It Is

In the year 733, a provincial governor's clerks in the far west of Japan wrote down why a village was called Uga: because a goddess ran away from a god, and the god followed her and watched her from hiding, and the place where he watched her became the village — and the goddess, Ayatohime, the daughter of the creator kami Kamimusubi, is remembered in exactly one sentence of history after that. She is the bride who said no in the only nearly complete Fudoki to survive from the Nara period, a footnote to the land-making god Ōkuninushi's career who nonetheless outlived most of her better-documented sisters: Suseri-bime and Nunakawahime have the Kojiki; Ayatohime has a village name, two shrines on the edge of the underworld's slope, and a story so spare that scholars still argue whether she was a goddess of brocade, a curse at the gate, or simply a girl the land-god peeped at from behind a t

The Fudoki paints her as resistant and quick: when the land-making god Ōnamochi pressed his suit, she would not consent, fled, and hid herself; the god then tracked her by stealth and secretly watched her, and the place where he sought her out became the village of Uga. Folk-narrative scholarship classifies her as a nabitsuma (隠び妻, 'hiding wife') figure — the bride who refuses once and hides before being found. Powers
Divine bride of the land-making god

The Fudoki's only explicit claim about Ayatohime is relational: she is the bride whom Ōnamochi — the great deity who made the land under heaven (所造天下大神命) — courted in Uga. The folk-narrative scholarship reads the marriage compact as the story's real engine: a local woman's acceptance of the incoming great god binds the Uga community into his order. One line of interpretation (summarized in the Japanese Wikipedia's citation of the Fudoki literature) holds that by marrying Ayatohime, Ōnamochi acquired the right to have the king-god enshrined and worshipped in this territory — the marriage as a charter of cult.

Limit: Attested only as narrative function; no independent divine acts are recorded for her.

Tutelary of weaving and silk (reconstructed)

If the etymology of aya ('figured silk, brocade') and to ('place') is accepted, Ayatohime is a goddess of the weaving-house or silk-working place — a patron of one of the most valued craft industries of the ancient Izumo region, where sericulture and textile tribute were economically significant.

Limit: The weaving reading is one of four competing etymologies; the Fudoki gives her no weaving role in the narrative.

Liminal or gate authority (reconstructed)

The 'harm + door' reading of her name (aya as mischief, to as gate) yields a very different figure: a curse-kami who blocks or harms people passing through gateways — the kind of dangerous threshold spirit that Japanese folklore places at borders. Combined with the Yomi-cave notice that follows her entry in the Fudoki, this reading makes her a guardian of a boundary between worlds rather than merely a bride.

Limit: Explicitly flagged in the literature as speculative; no ritual evidence of a gate-curse cult survives.

Narrative power of refusal (attested as motif)

Within the story economy of the Fudoki, Ayatohime's refusal-and-hiding has generative force: it is the pivot on which the village name Uga turns (the place where the god secretly watched her — ukagai — 'therefore it is called Uga'). Her flight, not the god's power, produces the toponym. Folklorists class this as the nabitsuma ('hiding wife') motif, a subtype of the difficult-courtship tale.

Limit: Motif-level power, not an attributed divine ability.

Absence of attested powers

Honest bottom line: Ayatohime has no recorded miracles, no healing cult, no oracles, no military feats, no transformation tales, and no independent festival traditions in any source I could verify. Everything beyond her one narrative act is reconstruction. This absence is itself characteristic: she is a place-name-legend heroine who was later elevated to shrine kami, not a major operational deity.

Limit: Negative evidence only; a lost cult cannot be ruled out.

Accounts I
The courtship at Uga (Uga-no-sato no kyūkon)Izumo no Kuni Fudoki, Izumo District, Uga-no-sato entry (733 CE)

Nara period text; story-world of the Age of the Gods (kamiyo)

The great deity Ōnamochi — styled 'the great deity who made the land under heaven' (所造天下大神命) — set his heart on Ayatohime, the daughter of Kamimusubi. When he courted her, the goddess would not consent. She fled and hid herself. The great god then secretly sought her out and watched her in concealment, and the spot where he thus pursued and won her is this very village — which is why the place is called Uga. The entry is a classic etiological tale: the village name is derived from the god's act of covert watching (ukagai). Nothing in the text says what happened after the finding — no wedding feast, no children, no gifts; the narrative stops at the name-origin, which is its sole purpose. The foundation of Uga-no-sato's name; the establishment of the kami's bond with the Uga community; the basis for the later shrine cults (Uga Shrine, Yomi Shrine).

Accounts II
The hiding-bride type: Ayatohime among the nabitsumaComparative folk-narrative scholarship; Seki 1998; Japanese Wikipedia's citation of the Fudoki literature

Type attested from the Kojiki (712) and Nihon Shoki (720) through the regional Fudoki (8th century)

Japanese narrative scholarship groups Ayatohime's story with a well-attested tale type, the nabitsuma or 'hiding wife' story, in which a suitor god or king courts a woman who refuses once, hides, and is then found — the refusal and concealment being a preliminary to marriage. The classic parallels are the Kojiki's courtship of Nunakawahime by Yachihoko (another name of Ōnamochi), in which the bride answers the suitor's song from across the river and they marry the next day; the Nihon Shoki's Keikō-tennō 4th-year entry; and three entries of the Harima Fudoki (Kako District's Hiresaka tomb, Shisō District's Asai village, and Taka District's Tsuma village). Scholars read the pattern in two ways: as simulated resistance encoding the marriage of a local woman to an incoming male ruler — a ritualized concession marking the absorption of the land into a new order, with the suitor's successful pursuit functioning as a rite of passage to kingship — and, more simply, as a test meant to prove the suitor's sincerity and to kindle his desire. The gate to Yomi: reading the Uga entry at the underworld boundaryFudoki text sequence; interpretation noted in the Japanese Wikipedia's citation of Izumo Fudoki scholarship

Nara-period text; modern scholarly synthesis

Immediately after the Uga entry, the Fudoki records Nazuki-no-iso, a rocky shore with a cave known as the 'slope of Yomi' and the 'hole of Yomi' (黄泉の坂・黄泉の穴) — the slope and pit of the underworld. Because the Izumo Fudoki rarely includes lore unconnected to place-name origins, scholars have proposed reading the two notices as one composition: the cave description is the ground on which Ōnamochi pursued Ayatohime, and the whole Uga section quietly asserts that Ōnamochi — whom the Nihon Shoki names the god who administers hidden (幽) affairs — holds the boundary between the living world and Yomi in his domain. In this reading Ayatohime is not merely a village bride but a figure standing at the threshold of the underworld, which lends her flight-and-concealment an edge of the uncanny: the goddess the land-maker chased to the mouth of Yomi. Places the Uga legend within Izumo's underworld topography; connects her to the region's famous Yomi associations (cf. the Izumo myth of Ōnamochi's earlier banishment to the underworld).

Ayatohime appears in the Kojiki or Nihon Shoki. — She is attested in exactly one ancient text, the Izumo no Kuni Fudoki (733 CE). The Japanese Wikipedia article is explicit that she is 'not seen elsewhere' (他には見られない). Her co-wives (Suseri-bime, Nunakawahime, etc.) are chronicle figures; she is not.

The Uga of her story is connected to the Buddhist deity Ugajin (宇賀神) or the uga harvest kami. — The kanji 宇賀 are shared, but the Uga of the Fudoki is a toponym whose entry derives the name from the god's covert watching (ukagai). No source connects Ayatohime's Uga to the snake-bodied Ugajin or to uga-no-mitama harvest cults.

She is a major national kami with a wide cult. — She is a hyper-local kami of one Fudoki village, with two Izumo shrines and one merged enshrinement in Mie. Her 'importance' is textual — she is a rare surviving specimen of a Nara-period local bride legend — not cultic.

The Fudoki entry is invented fiction. — The Izumo Fudoki is a state-commissioned gazetteer (713 edict, compiled 733) recording regional tradition — 'myths and legends told orally by old people' — alongside geography and products. The Uga entry is an etiological local legend faithfully recorded, not a novelist's invention; but it is also administrative in function (place-name charter), so it is neither pure fiction nor neutral history.
