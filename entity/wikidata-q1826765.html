Form

There is no single canonical iconography, because the enshrined spirit differs by site. Where the deity is represented as the generic 三宝荒神 (Sanbō-Kōjin), it is typically a fierce, wrathful figure — three-faced and six-armed, or eight-faced and six-armed, with flames, glaring eyes, and fangs, wielding ritual implements (vajra, lotus, jewel, bow and arrow) in its hands. Where the deity is identified with 青面金剛 (Shōmen-Kongō), it is the blue-faced, three-eyed, often-six-armed oni-like deity of the Kōshin faith, frequently carved in stone kōshin-tō pillars. Where the enshrined figure is the historical warlord, no divine image is used — the object of worship is a natural stone, a stūpa fragment, a round mirror-stone, or an inscribed stele. Common across all sites is the material culture of the shrine itself: dense, centuries-old accumulations of wooden hand and foot votive plaques, and sometimes real discarded medical aids (plaster casts, crutches, back-braces).

Powers

Curing diseases and injuries of the hands and feet The core, defining power. Worshippers who suffer from illnesses, wounds, swellings, or disabilities of the hands and feet pray to the Ashite Kōjin for cure (快癒). The cult's entire reason to exist is the reported healing of limbs, and shrines are crowded with votive plaques testifying to recoveries. Folk belief recorded in the 足手荒神 article; affirmed at the head shrine Kai Jinja Efficacy is a matter of religious faith, not demonstrated medicine; testimonies are votive and anecdotal. The cult's own lore (e.g. the soldier who remained unable to walk for a time before recovery) implies healing that is not automatic or absolute. None internal; the 'power' is by nature unverifiable.

Healing through contact with votive plaques A distinctive ritual power-channel: a recovered devotee donates a wooden hand- or foot-shaped plaque, and future supplicants rub their own afflicted limb against the donated plaques of others — transferring the healing. Some do the same with the real casts and crutches left behind by the healed. Documented in the 足手荒神 article and Kai Jinja records; the 井上円了 diary (1908) describes the plaques piling 'into a small mountain' Ritual, not medical practice; symbolic contagion of cure. None.

Patronage of athletes and safe travel Because healthy, uninjured limbs are also in its care, the deity is prayed to by sportspeople for freedom from injury (無病息災) and by travellers and drivers for traffic safety (交通安全), especially at the head shrine. 甲斐神社 article; shrine's stated principal prayers None documented beyond faith context. None.

Absorption of the Kōshin deity's disease-dispelling power Where the Ashite Kōjin is concretely identified with 青面金剛 (Shōmen-Kongō), it inherits that deity's apotropaic power to drive away pestilence and lingering miasmic disease — the reason the name is affixed to some kōshin-tō and kōshin halls. 足手荒神 article (医王寺, 西巌殿寺 enshrine Shōmen-Kongō); English 'Kōshin' article Limited to sites that make the identification. Directly contradicts a 'historical warlord' origin at other sites — see contradiction register.

Curative intercession attributed to medicine-kami forms At the 二日市足手荒神社 in Kujū, Ōita, the enshrined kami 少彦名神 (Sukunahikona — a divine physician in Japanese myth) plus a stone pagoda carved with Yakushi (the Healing Buddha) give the site a medical-kami power profile consonant with the limb-cure function. 足手荒神 article (二日市足手荒神社, Kujū) Also site-specific. Not all sites share this identification — see register.

Protection of household, hearth, and goods (inherited generically) As a form of kōjin, the deity belongs to a class that protects the home, watches the hearth-fire, and turns destructive force to beneficial ends. Some 地荒神-type images guard land, family line, and cattle/horses. 荒神 (Kōjin) article; JAANUS 'Koujin' Generic kōjin inheritance; not specific to the limb-cult function. None.

Accounts

In 1587, after Toyotomi Hideyoshi's subjugation of Kyūshū, the new governor of Higo province, Sassa Narimasa, imposed a taikō land survey (tainchi-kenchi). The local warrior-chieftains (kokujin) and farmers rose in revolt — the Higo Kokujin Ikki. Kai Munetate (also read Kai Mimihito), castle-lord of Mifune, led some 35,000 kokujin against Sassa's stronghold Kumamoto Castle. The rebels nearly took it, but a large pro-Hideyoshi relief force arrived from Kyūshū and Shikoku, and a turncoat among Munetate's own retainers sealed the defeat. Munetate, mortally wounded in the hands and feet during the rout, fled as far as present-day Kamito-roku, Kashima-machi. There he was hidden and nursed by villagers (ancestors of the shrine's ritual family). Deeply moved that these commoners would shelter and tend a defeated general, he declared: 'My spirit shall remain in this world, watching over descendants and saving those who suffer in their hands and feet' — then died. A shrine was later built to console his soul: this is the origin of the head shrine of the Ashite Kōjin.

This independent origin at Beppu's Minami Tateishi concerns a soldier of Kuroda Yoshitaka (Kuroda Josui). On the night before the 1600 Battle of Ishigaki-hara — called the 'Kyūshū Sekigahara' — fought between Kuroda and Ōtomo Yoshimune, the soldier fell from a cliff while scouting, breaking both legs and arms. Captured by Ōtomo's men, he declared: 'Had I not had my limbs broken, you would not have taken me. Cut me down and make your name; for a hundred years after death my soul will remain in this world and save those whose hands and feet are broken.' He was beheaded. The Ōtomo commander Yoshihiro Tsugunaka, moved with pity, buried him honorably; wounded soldiers from the battle likewise recovered around that site. So a shrine-house was raised and he came to be worshipped as 手足荒神 — the god of hands and feet.

A master stonemason (御用石工), Shimojō Tokumatsu, was travelling to Kumamoto at the lord's summons when severe foot pain stopped him from walking. He prayed devoutly to the Ashite Kōjin he had always trusted, and the pain eased, letting him continue. Later, during construction work ordered by the domain, he fell from a cliff with a great boulder, wrenching his arm and losing consciousness, his life in danger. He prayed again to the Ashite Kōjin and recovered within a short time. In gratitude he built a shrine hall and invited a 分霊 (branch spirit) of the deity, founding the local Ashite Kōjin at Oguni-machi. The shrine holds its festival on 15 July.

The Meiji-era philosopher, Buddhist scholar, and founder of Tōyō University, Inoue Enryō, came to lecture at the primary school beside what would become the present head shrine. He wrote in his diary: 'Beside the school grounds is a small votive shrine called Ashite Kōjin for making limb-vows; people from near and far come to pray and donate wooden boards shaped like hands and feet. The boards have piled into a small mountain — it is laughable.' His bemused, rationalist eye — viewing folk votive practice as a curiosity — offers a period witness to how densely the offerings accumulated by the early 20th century.

A person who had been unable to walk made a pilgrimage to the head shrine and was later able to stand and walk again. As a thank-offering, the devotee cut off their own thumb and presented it at the shrine. The thumb was kept there for a long time preserved in alcohol before eventually disappearing from the collection.

Content Notes

Every major origin legend is built on a dying man whose hands and feet were broken or cut in war: Kai Munetate mortally wounded fleeing the 1587 Higo revolt; the Beppu soldier with both arms and legs broken, beheaded by the Ōtomo; the anonymous Hōtaku samurai who died because his broken legs prevented escape. The deity is the apotheosis of battlefield limb-suffering.

Classic Japanese folk-motif of the pacified 荒魂 (ara-mitama, wild spirit) of a wronged or slain person transformed through enshrinement into a tutelary — here a healing one.

足手荒神 and 甲斐神社 articles

The Beppu legend explicitly celebrates — through the soldier's own words — his being cut down while unable to fight: 'Had I not had my limbs broken, you would not have taken me. Cut me down and make your name.' The Ōtomo general who ordered it pity-buried him.

Warring-period norms of ritual decapitation and honorable burial; the line is the soldier's defiant self-justification, not an endorsement of his own killing.

足手荒神 article

Accounts of Kai Munetate's death include his committing seppuku — at a jizō hall after being cornered, or (per shrine founder Arao) at the future shrine site after being cared for. One local tradition even claims the temple Zōfuku-ji was called the 'hidden/ seppuku temple' (潜伏寺 / 切腹寺) because he cut his belly there.

Codified warrior suicide; its legend site became holy, the same mechanism that sacralized many Sengoku battle-death locales.

甲斐神社 article

The shrine tradition records that a formerly lame devotee, once able to walk, cut off his own thumb and presented it as thanks; it was kept in alcohol for years. This is a real, documented (if extreme) example of the physical-votive faith carried to bodily sacrifice.

Sits at the far end of the offering spectrum from wooden plaques to actual body parts; exceptional and notable, not the norm.

甲斐神社 (嘉島町) article — shrine oral tradition

The generic kōjin is a deity of 'uncertain temper' who must be propitiated; folk tradition holds that the unpacified wild spirit (荒魂) can bring harm, and that only proper offering turns it into a blessing. This 'volatile' underside shadows the otherwise-benevolent limb-cult.

The yin of the healing face: a tutelary that requires vigilant good relations lest its fierce nature strike.

荒神 article; Ashkenazy, Handbook of Japanese Mythology

Scholarly Debates
Who is the enshrined deity? Warlord vs Kōshin deity vs medicine-kami
The deity is the spirit of the slain warlord Kai Munetate — the head-shrine (Kashima) identification and the Bunrei-derivative sites. ⇌ The deity is 青面金剛 (Shōmen-Kongō) of the Kōshin faith (医王寺, 西巌殿寺) or the medicine-kami 少彦名神 / a Yakushi image (Kujū 二日市).
Evidence for A: Kai Jinja lineage and many 分霊 shrines; the 肥後国志 supplement (1884) records a Munetate-tutelary shrine.
Evidence for B: 医王寺's 青面金剛堂 and 西巌殿寺's mirror-stone, both independently identified as 'Ashite Kōjin' in the same regional source.
How did Kai Munetate actually die?
He was captured by Mori Katsunobu's troops and beheaded (the most common documentary reading). ⇌ Brought down by Sassa retainer Matsubara Gorōbyōe, he committed seppuku at a jizō hall in Mutsu-no-sato (Rokkamon) village; the temple Zōfuku-ji gained the epithets 'hidden/ seppuku temple.'
Evidence for A: Historical records entered in the 甲斐神社 article.
Evidence for B: Local Mutsu-no-sato traditions and the temple epithet.
How old is the cult?
Very old — the shrine founder Arao claims the spirit was present at the site for 'over 300 years' before any building record. ⇌ Documentable only to the late Edo period — a 1935 book says a small shrine was built 'about ninety years before,' i.e. c. Tenpō (1830s) or Kōka (1840s); a roof-tile inscribed 'Culture 2' (1805) suggests construction no earlier than 1805; the first clear textual record is the 1884 肥後国志 supplement.
Evidence for A: Arao's founding claim and shrine oral tradition.
Evidence for B: Dated tile (1805), 1935 account (c. 90 years), 1884 text.
Is 'hands and feet' ('ashi-te') the actual ordering, and does it matter?
The cult name is standardized as 足手荒神 (ashite) — literally 'feet-hands.' ⇌ In many regions it is written 手足荒神 (te-ashi, 'hands-feet'), and one Ōita shrine writes 皇神 rather than 荒神.
Evidence for A: The main ja.wiki article and the Kumamoto head shrine use 足手.
Evidence for B: The article itself lists 手足荒神 for Ōita/Tamana sites and 足手皇神 for one Takeda site.
Is Ashite Kōjin a 'true' kami or a Buddhist-adjacent folk effigy?
It is a kami in the Shinto folk sense, enshrined as a 神社 (jinja). ⇌ At several sites it is housed in a Buddhist temple (医王寺, 西巌殿寺, 宗泉寺, 本覚寺, 福王寺) as a subsidiary image, sometimes identical to a kōshin deity — a Buddhist/folk 習合 (syncretic) figure.
Evidence for A: Jinja naming and Shinto-organization registration (e.g. Kashima temple-body as a Shinto religious corporation from 1953)
Evidence for B: Temple locations and Buddhist iconography (Yakushi, Shōmen-Kongō).

A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine a shrine crowded not with flowers but with hundreds of painted wooden hands and feet — left hands, right feet, small casts, crutches — and an old practice in which a person whose broken leg has mended presses their still-suffering neighbour's shoulder, guiding them to stroke the very same wooden foot that, a year before, had been offered in gratitude for a cure. That is the world of the Ashite Kōjin, Japan's god of hands and feet: a quiet, fierce folk-healing cult of Kyūshū built upon the broken limbs of samurai who died in the mud of sixteenth-century battles, and upon the stubborn, everyday hope of the lame.
