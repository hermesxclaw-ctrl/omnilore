Who It Is

Imagine a shrine crowded not with flowers but with hundreds of painted wooden hands and feet — left hands, right feet, small casts, crutches — and an old practice in which a person whose broken leg has mended presses their still-suffering neighbour's shoulder, guiding them to stroke the very same wooden foot that, a year before, had been offered in gratitude for a cure. That is the world of the Ashite Kōjin, Japan's god of hands and feet: a quiet, fierce folk-healing cult of Kyūshū built upon the broken limbs of samurai who died in the mud of sixteenth-century battles, and upon the stubborn, everyday hope of the lame.

As a folk-healing kami, the deity's 'personality' is that of a compassionate but transactionally approached helper of the lame and injured. Its origin legends repeatedly frame it as the spirit of a man or soldier whose own limbs were broken or cut in battle — one who died promising to rescue those whose hands and feet were hurt. This makes it a suffering-empathizing tutelary: it 'knows' the pain of the injured because its enshrined other-self died in that pain. In the darker kōjin inheritance, the generic 荒神 is of 'uncertain temper' and must be propitiated

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The term 足手荒神 (Ashite Kōjin) is a compound of 足 (ashi, 'foot / feet'), 手 (te, 'hand / hands'), and 荒神 (kōjin), a class of Japanese folk-kami whose name literally means 'fierce god' or 'rough deity'. Read あしてこうじん (ashite-kōjin), the two-body-part characters are ordered 'feet then hands' — the reverse

Pronunciation — Approx. AH-shee-teh KOH-jin. Native Japanese: あしてこうじん. The 足手 is read ashi-te (not the modern te-ashi order), and 荒神 is read kōjin in this context.

Aliases — Ashite-Kōjin, Ashite Kojin, 手足荒神, Ashide-Kōjin, Ashite Kōjinshinsha, the god of hands and feet

Ownership — Japan. The cult is squarely a form of Japanese folk religion (minkan shinkō), with strong local grounding in the western island of Kyūshū — chiefly Kumamoto and Ōita prefectures —

Names are cult layers, not one fixed label.

Powers
Curing diseases and injuries of the hands and feet

The core, defining power. Worshippers who suffer from illnesses, wounds, swellings, or disabilities of the hands and feet pray to the Ashite Kōjin for cure (快癒). The cult's entire reason to exist is the reported healing of limbs, and shrines are crowded with votive plaques testifying to recoveries.

Limit: Efficacy is a matter of religious faith, not demonstrated medicine; testimonies are votive and anecdotal. The cult's own lore (e.g. the soldier who remained unable to walk for a time before recovery) implies healing that is not automatic or absolute.

Healing through contact with votive plaques

A distinctive ritual power-channel: a recovered devotee donates a wooden hand- or foot-shaped plaque, and future supplicants rub their own afflicted limb against the donated plaques of others — transferring the healing. Some do the same with the real casts and crutches left behind by the healed.

Limit: Ritual, not medical practice; symbolic contagion of cure.

Patronage of athletes and safe travel

Because healthy, uninjured limbs are also in its care, the deity is prayed to by sportspeople for freedom from injury (無病息災) and by travellers and drivers for traffic safety (交通安全), especially at the head shrine.

Limit: None documented beyond faith context.

Absorption of the Kōshin deity's disease-dispelling power

Where the Ashite Kōjin is concretely identified with 青面金剛 (Shōmen-Kongō), it inherits that deity's apotropaic power to drive away pestilence and lingering miasmic disease — the reason the name is affixed to some kōshin-tō and kōshin halls.

Limit: Limited to sites that make the identification.

Curative intercession attributed to medicine-kami forms

At the 二日市足手荒神社 in Kujū, Ōita, the enshrined kami 少彦名神 (Sukunahikona — a divine physician in Japanese myth) plus a stone pagoda carved with Yakushi (the Healing Buddha) give the site a medical-kami power profile consonant with the limb-cure function.

Limit: Also site-specific.

Protection of household, hearth, and goods (inherited generically)

As a form of kōjin, the deity belongs to a class that protects the home, watches the hearth-fire, and turns destructive force to beneficial ends. Some 地荒神-type images guard land, family line, and cattle/horses.

Limit: Generic kōjin inheritance; not specific to the limb-cult function.

Accounts I
The Death-Vow of the Wounded Warlord (Kai Munetate and the Higo Uprising)甲斐神社 (嘉島町) article; transmitted by shrine founder Arao Gumpē; variant in 荒尾延寿, '肥後の足手荒神縁起' (1978)

1587 (Tenshō 15) event; shrine founded c. late Edo era

In 1587, after Toyotomi Hideyoshi's subjugation of Kyūshū, the new governor of Higo province, Sassa Narimasa, imposed a taikō land survey (tainchi-kenchi). The local warrior-chieftains (kokujin) and farmers rose in revolt — the Higo Kokujin Ikki. Kai Munetate (also read Kai Mimihito), castle-lord of Mifune, led some 35,000 kokujin against Sassa's stronghold Kumamoto Castle. The rebels nearly took it, but a large pro-Hideyoshi relief force arrived from Kyūshū and Shikoku, and a turncoat among Munetate's own retainers sealed the defeat. Munetate, mortally wounded in the hands and feet during the rout, fled as far as present-day Kamito-roku, Kashima-machi. There he was hidden and nursed by villagers (ancestors of the shrine's ritual family). Deeply moved that these commoners would shelter and tend a defeated general, he declared: 'My spirit shall remain in this world, watching over descendants and saving those who suffer in their hands and feet' — then died. A shrine was later built to console his soul: this is the origin of the head shrine of the Ashite Kōjin. Foundation of the naka-no

The Samurai Who Broke His Legs and Vowed to Save the Lame (Beppu Hands-and-Feet Kōjin)足手荒神 article (別府市南立石 手足荒神)

1600 (Keichō 5)

This independent origin at Beppu's Minami Tateishi concerns a soldier of Kuroda Yoshitaka (Kuroda Josui). On the night before the 1600 Battle of Ishigaki-hara — called the 'Kyūshū Sekigahara' — fought between Kuroda and Ōtomo Yoshimune, the soldier fell from a cliff while scouting, breaking both legs and arms. Captured by Ōtomo's men, he declared: 'Had I not had my limbs broken, you would not have taken me. Cut me down and make your name; for a hundred years after death my soul will remain in this world and save those whose hands and feet are broken.' He was beheaded. The Ōtomo commander Yoshihiro Tsugunaka, moved with pity, buried him honorably; wounded soldiers from the battle likewise recovered around that site. So a shrine-house was raised and he came to be worshipped as 手足荒神 — the god of hands and feet. Foundation of the 手足荒神 shrine at Beppu-Minami Tateishi, with its own regional diffusion across Ōita and Hayami district.

Accounts II
The Stonemason Healed Twice (Shimojō Tokumatsu's vow)足手荒神 article (阿蘇郡小国町 足手荒神)

Edo period (17th-19th c.)

A master stonemason (御用石工), Shimojō Tokumatsu, was travelling to Kumamoto at the lord's summons when severe foot pain stopped him from walking. He prayed devoutly to the Ashite Kōjin he had always trusted, and the pain eased, letting him continue. Later, during construction work ordered by the domain, he fell from a cliff with a great boulder, wrenching his arm and losing consciousness, his life in danger. He prayed again to the Ashite Kōjin and recovered within a short time. In gratitude he built a shrine hall and invited a 分霊 (branch spirit) of the deity, founding the local Ashite Kōjin at Oguni-machi. The shrine holds its festival on 15 July. Another self-contained founding legend showing the cult's gift for attaching to a specific local healer/devotee.

The Philosopher's Laugh (Inoue Enryō's 1908 visit)井上円了 diary, 1908, cited in 井上円了選集 第12巻 and 甲斐神社 article

1908 (Meiji 41)

The Meiji-era philosopher, Buddhist scholar, and founder of Tōyō University, Inoue Enryō, came to lecture at the primary school beside what would become the present head shrine. He wrote in his diary: 'Beside the school grounds is a small votive shrine called Ashite Kōjin for making limb-vows; people from near and far come to pray and donate wooden boards shaped like hands and feet. The boards have piled into a small mountain — it is laughable.' His bemused, rationalist eye — viewing folk votive practice as a curiosity — offers a period witness to how densely the offerings accumulated by the early 20th century. First-person documentary evidence of the cult's popularity and visual character in Meiji Japan.

The Severed Thumb (a devotee's extreme gift)甲斐神社 (嘉島町) article — shrine tradition

undated (modern-era oral tradition)

A person who had been unable to walk made a pilgrimage to the head shrine and was later able to stand and walk again. As a thank-offering, the devotee cut off their own thumb and presented it at the shrine. The thumb was kept there for a long time preserved in alcohol before eventually disappearing from the collection. The starkest example of the cult's physical-votive logic carried to bodily sacrifice; now only oral shrine history.

Is 'hands and feet' ('ashi-te') the actual ordering, and does it matter?The cult name is standardized as 足手荒神 (ashite) — literally 'feet-hands.'In many regions it is written 手足荒神 (te-ashi, 'hands-feet'), and one Ōita shrine writes 皇神 rather than 荒神.
Is Ashite Kōjin a 'true' kami or a Buddhist-adjacent folk effigy?It is a kami in the Shinto folk sense, enshrined as a 神社 (jinja).At several sites it is housed in a Buddhist temple (医王寺, 西巌殿寺, 宗泉寺, 本覚寺, 福王寺) as a subsidiary image, sometimes identical to a kōshin deity — a Buddhist/folk 習合 (syncretic) figure.

Ashite Kōjin is just the kitchen/hearth kōjin found in ordinary Japanese homes. — It is a specialized folk-healing cult focused on the cure of hand-and-foot ailments, with its own shrines, votive plaques, and origin legends. The generic Sanbō-Kōjin hearth-kami is a related but distinct figure.

The deity literally has hands and feet it is named for, or is a god of one specific body part. — The name refers to its DOMAIN of care — healing the hands and feet of the afflicted — not to any anatomical feature of the deity's own iconography. Some images have six or eight arms, but that is the generic kōjin form, not a reference to its healing function.

It is one single, universally defined god with one origin story. — There is no single identity: it is variously the soul of Kai Munetate, of anonymous slain samurai, 青面金剛, or 少彦名神/Yakushi depending on the site. The category is functional, not a unified persona.

The hand/foot plaques are merely decorative ema. — They are functional: the healed rub their votive plaques on the afflicted limbs of the still-suffering to transfer the cure — an act of sympathetic healing, not just ornament.
