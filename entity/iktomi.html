The Spider Trickster, Iktomi the Trickster, Wisdom in Folly

I am the spider who outsmarts everyone — including myself. Watch me succeed brilliantly, then watch me lose it all. Both lessons are the same lesson.

Who It Is

Iktomi is the spider trickster of Lakota and other Plains tribes' mythology — a figure of profound cultural importance who embodies the paradoxes of wisdom and foolishness, creativity and destruction, teaching and misdirection. The name 'Iktomi' means 'spider' in Lakota, and like his Akan counterpart Anansi (with whom he shares striking similarities despite no historical contact), Iktomi is a small, physically weak creature who relies entirely on his wits to navigate a world of larger, more powerful beings.

Physically, Iktomi is depicted as a spider, often a brown recluse-like spider, who can also take human form — typically appearing as a small man with spider-like characteristics, dressed in a spider-web patterned robe. In his human form, he is often described as having an intelligent, crafty face with quick, darting eyes. He is associated with the colors red and black, and his movements are described as quick and darting.

Iktomi's most distinctive characteristic is that his schemes almost always backfire on himself. He is the embodiment of the maxim that the cleverest person is often their own worst enemy. In one classic story, Iktomi tricks ducks into dancing with their eyes closed so he can kill and eat them — and he succeeds, but then his greed leads him to fall asleep while the ducks cook, and Coyote steals his feast. In another, he convinces a rock to give him its beautiful blanket, but the rock comes to life and rolls after him, flattening his camp. These tales are simultaneously hilarious and morally instructive: Iktomi's intelligence is real, but his lack of wisdom — his inability to control his greed, vanity, or impulsiveness — repeatedly undoes him.

Iktomi functions as a 'negative exemplar' in Lakota oral education. Rather than tell children 'don't be greedy,' elders tell an Iktomi story in which greed leads to disaster, and the children absorb the lesson through laughter and recognition. This pedagogical method — teaching by counterexample through humor — is one of the most effective traditional educational techniques. Iktomi's foolishness makes moral instruction palatable and memorable.

Iktomi also has a cosmological dimension in some Lakota narratives. He is sometimes described as the first-born son of Inyan (the Rock, the primordial creator being) and Wakinyan (the Thunder Being), linking him to the deepest structures of Lakota cosmology. In some accounts, after the great flood, Iktomi was one of the beings who helped shape the post-flood world, though his contributions are... mixed, reflecting his chaotic nature.

Iktomi has survived into modern Lakota culture as a figure of both humor and cultural critique. Contemporary Lakota storytellers and writers have used Iktomi to satirize everything from reservation bureaucracy to consumer culture, continuing the trickster's traditional role as a figure who exposes foolishness wherever it appears. The Yankton Sioux writer Zitkala-Ša (Gertrude Simmons Bonnin) published Iktomi stories in the early 20th century, helping preserve and popularize the spider trickster's legacy. Iktomi reminds us that the line between wisdom and foolishness is thinner than we think — and that sometimes the best teacher is the one who makes you laugh at the fool within yourself.

Form

Iktomi is fundamentally a spider — specifically described as a small, quick spider, often associated with the brown recluse or a similar species of the Plains region. He can also take human form, appearing as a small man with spider-like characteristics: quick darting movements, long thin limbs, and often dressed in a spider-web patterned robe or blanket. In his human form, he is described as having an intelligent but crafty face with quick, darting eyes that miss nothing. He is associated with the colors red and black, and his presence is often signaled by unusually elaborate spider webs. In some traditions, he can transform fully between spider and human form, while in others he exists in a liminal state — a spider-like man or a man-like spider. He is never described as large, powerful, or physically imposing — his small size is essential to his character as a trickster who must rely on wit, not strength.

Powers

Cunning and Deception Iktomi's primary power is his cleverness — his ability to devise schemes that exploit the weaknesses, vanity, and greed of others. He can talk animals out of their food, their clothing, their homes, and even their lives. His rhetoric is his weapon: he flatters, persuades, and confuses. He is a master of misdirection, getting his targets to look one way while he acts in another. This is genuine intelligence — Iktomi's schemes are often brilliant in concept — but his power always fails at the execution stage because of his own character flaws. Universally attested across all Lakota/Dakota Iktomi stories Iktomi's cunning always fails when his own greed, vanity, or impulsiveness intervenes. He cannot control himself — he is his own worst enemy. Additionally, there is often a bigger trickster (Coyote) or a humbler creature (Turtle) who outwits him. Some stories depict Iktomi as genuinely clever and successful in certain contexts, suggesting that his intelligence is real even if his wisdom is lacking.

Shape-Shifting (Spider/Human) Iktomi can shift between spider form and human form, appearing as a full spider, as a small man, or as a hybrid spider-man. This ability allows him to observe unnoticed (as a spider in the corner), to entrap victims (spinning webs), and to interact socially (in human form). The transformation is not always clearly depicted — sometimes Iktomi simply IS a spider who talks and acts like a person. Implicit in many Iktomi stories; explicit in some The transformation is not unlimited — Iktomi remains small and spider-like even in human form. He cannot become large, powerful, or genuinely intimidating. Some versions treat Iktomi as always a spider (who happens to talk and scheme like a person) rather than a shape-shifter. The boundary between 'spider who acts human' and 'human who becomes a spider' is blurred.

Web-Spinning (Literal and Metaphorical) Iktomi spins webs — both the physical webs of a spider and the metaphorical webs of deception. His physical webs can trap insects and small creatures; his metaphorical webs (his schemes) can trap larger beings through psychology and misdirection. The web is both his tool and his symbol. Universally attested — the web is fundamental to Iktomi's identity as a spider His webs, like his schemes, often trap himself. Iktomi gets tangled in his own complexity. The web can be a prison as easily as a trap. None — the web is universally associated with Iktomi.

Negative Exemplar / Pedagogical Power Iktomi's greatest cultural 'power' is his function as a negative exemplar: by watching Iktomi fail spectacularly, Lakota children learn not to be greedy, vain, impulsive, or foolish. This is an indirect power — Iktomi doesn't teach; he demonstrates what not to do. The laughter at Iktomi's disasters is pedagogical laughter: it encodes moral lessons in memory. This function is culturally engineered: Elders deliberately tell Iktomi stories to teach values without direct instruction. Lakota oral tradition and cultural practice; described by Deloria, Standing Bear, and contemporary Lakota educators This power is socially constructed — it depends on the interpretive framework of Lakota culture. Audiences must understand that Iktomi is a negative exemplar, not a role model. Outside that framework, Iktomi's stories might be read as simply entertaining without moral content. Some Lakota thinkers argue that reducing Iktomi to a 'negative exemplar' oversimplifies him — that his foolishness contains genuine wisdom and his failures are also celebrations of creativity and survival against the odds.

Accounts

Iktomi, hungry, sees a flock of ducks swimming on a lake. He tells them he has learned a wonderful new dance and offers to teach them — but the dance must be done with eyes closed. The ducks, flattered and curious, agree. Iktomi has them dance in a circle, eyes shut, singing a song he invents on the spot. As each duck dances past him, he grabs it, wrings its neck, and stuffs it into his bag. The ducks, eyes closed, hear nothing amiss. He kills nearly the entire flock before one duck peeks, sees what's happening, and cries a warning. The remaining ducks fly away, but Iktomi has a bag full of dead ducks — a brilliant success. He builds a fire, plucks the ducks, and sets them to roast. Then, exhausted from his effort, he decides to nap while they cook, instructing his own legs to wake him if anyone comes near. But his legs, being legs, do not wake him. While he sleeps, Coyote (or a passing wolf) smells the roasting ducks, eats every last one, and leaves only bones. Iktomi wakes to find his feast stolen — his perfect scheme undone by his own inability to stay awake. He is left hungry, furious, and humiliated.

Iktomi, walking across the prairie on a hot day, encounters a large rock wearing a beautiful blanket — a blanket someone has draped over it as an offering. Iktomi admires the blanket and, being Iktomi, decides to take it. He tells the rock, 'You don't need this — you're just a rock,' and takes the blanket for himself. The rock, now animate and deeply offended, begins to roll after him. Iktomi runs. The rock rolls after him, faster and faster, growing larger as it rolls. Iktomi runs to animals for help — he begs a buffalo, a bear, a deer to stop the rock — but each animal, learning what Iktomi has done, refuses to help a thief. Finally, Iktomi reaches a spider hole in the ground and dives in just as the rock rolls over it, crushing the entrance but missing Iktomi himself. He is saved but trapped, and the story often ends with Iktomi still in the hole, waiting for the rock to leave.

Iktomi, hungry again, encounters Turtle sunning himself on a rock. He decides to make turtle soup. But Turtle, unlike the ducks, is not easily fooled. When Iktomi tries to grab him, Turtle retreats into his shell. Iktomi builds a fire and tries to boil Turtle alive, but Turtle's shell protects him. Iktomi tries various methods — fire, drowning (which doesn't work on a turtle), burying alive — but Turtle survives each attempt and taunts Iktomi. Finally, Iktomi in frustration throws Turtle into the river, intending to drown him — and Turtle, of course, swims away, laughing. Iktomi has been outwitted by a creature even slower and humbler than himself. The story reverses the expected hierarchy: the trickster is tricked.

Iktomi acquires a beautiful red feather (or a fancy blanket, or some other prized possession) and becomes consumed with vanity. He parades past the other animals, demanding their admiration. 'Look at me!' he cries. 'Am I not the most splendid being on the prairie?' The animals are unimpressed or mock him. In his vanity, Iktomi neglects to watch where he's going, falls into a hole or a river, and loses his prized possession. Or, he trades it away for something worthless because a clever animal flatters him and he can't resist praise. The story is a pure vanity fable: Iktomi's need for admiration destroys his actual advantage.

In some Lakota traditions, Iktomi is not just a character IN stories but the source OF stories. The first stories were Iktomi's tales — narratives of his foolishness that the Lakota people began to tell among themselves. In some versions, Iktomi deliberately gave stories to the people as a gift (perhaps the only genuinely generous thing he does). In others, the people simply observed his disasters and began recounting them, and this became the tradition of Iktomi storytelling. This story frames Iktomi not just as a trickster but as a culture hero of sorts — the unwitting founder of Lakota oral literature.

Field Notes

☵ Iktomi's name literally means 'spider' in Lakota — so every time a Lakota speaker refers to a spider, they are speaking Iktomi's name.

☵ Iktomi stories are traditionally only told during winter months, when spiders are dormant and the storytelling season is open. Telling Iktomi stories in summer is considered inappropriate in many Lakota traditions.

☵ The 'Iktomi and the Ducks' story is so widespread that variants appear across multiple Plains tribes, each with their own trickster figure — but Iktomi is the most famous spider-specific version.

☵ Zitkala-Ša, the first Native American to publish Iktomi stories in English (1901), was also a prominent activist for Native American citizenship and civil rights — she used traditional stories as a form of cultural resistance.

☵ Iktomi is sometimes credited with inventing certain Lakota art forms — including the intricate geometric patterns in quillwork and beadwork — which, like spider webs, are complex, beautiful, and patterned.

☵ In some Lakota traditions, it is said that if you see an unusually large or elaborately-woven spider web, Iktomi is nearby, watching — and probably planning something.

Content Notes

Many Iktomi stories involve him killing animals — wringing the necks of ducks, attempting to boil a turtle alive, and so forth. These scenes can be graphic, and Iktomi's attitude is purely instrumental: animals are food, obstacles, or tools for his schemes. While the stories are humorous, the violence is real within the narrative frame. This reflects the reality of hunting cultures (animals are killed for food) but also raises questions about Iktomi's moral status: he is not just a fool but a killer.

The violence in Iktomi stories is rarely gratuitous — it serves narrative and pedagogical purposes (greed leads to cruelty; cruelty leads to consequences). However, modern retellings for children often soften or omit the violence.

Multiple Iktomi stories (Iktomi and the Ducks, Iktomi and the Turtle, etc.)

Iktomi stories, like much Indigenous American oral tradition, have been collected, published, and retold by non-Native authors without community consent. 19th and early 20th-century ethnographers (Walker, Leland for other traditions, etc.) sometimes distorted the stories through their own cultural lenses, publishing versions that Lakota communities did not authorize. More recently, non-Native children's authors have published Iktomi stories as 'multicultural' material, often stripping the cultural context and presenting them as generic folktales. This ongoing appropriation causes cultural harm and erases the specific Lakota ownership and sacred context of the stories.

The ethics of collecting and publishing Indigenous oral tradition is a major concern in contemporary Native American studies. Lakota communities are increasingly asserting control over their own narratives.

What We Don't Know

The full corpus of Iktomi stories in living Lakota oral tradition — as opposed to the published collections — is largely inaccessible to outside scholars, both because winter storytelling is community-internal and because many stories are family-specific. The relationship between Iktomi and the heyoka (sacred clown) tradition deserves more focused study from Lakota scholars working within the tradition. The existence (or non-existence) of a female Iktomi counterpart is underexplored. The contemporary use of Iktomi stories in Lakota-language education and cultural revitalization is an active area that would benefit from more documentation (again, ideally by Lakota scholars). The Iktomi-Anansi comparative question — two independent spider tricksters on separate continents — remains a fascinating puzzle for comparative mythology that has not been definitively addressed.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Iktomi as the original internet troll — not in the modern sense of anonymous cruelty, but in the sense of a being whose entire mode of existence is to provoke, to scheme, to take things that aren't his, and to get spectacularly owned in the process. Like the best trolls, Iktomi is genuinely clever — his schemes are often brilliant — but his ego and impulsiveness guarantee his downfall. And like the best troll-busting, the audience's laughter at Iktomi's failures is genuinely cathartic and instructional. We see ourselves in Iktomi — our own greed, our own vanity, our own tendency to sabotage our successes — and we laugh at him, and that laughter is also self-recognition. Iktomi is us at our worst, and that's why we love him.
