Who It Is

Coatlicue ('Serpent Skirt') is the Aztec earth-mother in her most terrifying and majestic form — the goddess who simultaneously gives life and devours corpses, who gestates the sun and demands blood. Her colossal 3.5-meter monolith (discovered 1790 in Mexico City's Zócalo) shows her as a decapitated, dismembered figure: her head is two facing rattlesnakes (a double-stream of blood transformed into serpents), her necklace is of severed human hands, hearts, and a skull, her skirt is a woven lattice of serpents, and her feet and hands are eagle claws. Yet Coatlicue is not only the 'devourer of filth' (as the Tzitzimime star-demons who flank her); she is also Tonantzin ('Our Mother') and Teteoinan ('Mother of the Gods'), the fertile womb from which all life — gods, humans, plants, and animals — emerges. The paradox of Coatlicue is the paradox of the earth itself: it bears crops and then receives the dead; it is the 'grid' of life-and-death on which all Aztec religion is woven. Her identification with the Christian Virgin of Guadalupe (appearing at her former sanctuary of Tepeyacac) is an enduring testament to the power of this archetype.
