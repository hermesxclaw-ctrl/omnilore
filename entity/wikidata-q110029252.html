Who It Is

Youel has no temple, no myth, no face in art — only a name that surfaces in a 20th-century angel list and then ripples outward through databases. That absence is the story: how a single Hebrew name meaning 'Yahweh is God' becomes catalogued as a deity, and what that tells us about the way modern archives create angels.

No narrative behavior, speech, or personality traits are attested for Youel in any primary text located. Angelological dictionaries that list the name do not assign a dialogue, motive, or moral alignment beyond the implied status as a divine messenger/angel. Any personality ascribed in contemporary fiction or channeling literature is adaptation-specific and not source-backed. Unknown.

Accounts I
The Name Yoel in the Hebrew Bible - The Prophet JoelBook of Joel (Sefer Yoel), Hebrew Bible, Twelve Minor Prophets

Persian period, final form c. 5th-3rd century BCE; prophetic superscription

The only extensive narrative attached to the name Yoel is the prophetic book ascribed to 'Joel son of Pethuel.' The book contains no biography of Joel beyond the superscription; it presents oracles about a locust plague, drought, a call to repentance, the Day of YHWH, and the outpouring of spirit. The figure Yoel is a human prophet, not a deity or angel. This is the earliest and most fully attested use of the name. Joel becomes a canonical prophetic voice; later Jewish and Christian interpretation treats the locust and spirit passages eschatologically (Acts 2 cites Joel 2:28-32). No angelic Youel appears.

Yoel/Jeuel as Post-Exilic Personal Name1 Chronicles 5:4, 6:33 (6:18 ET), 7:3; 2 Chronicles 26:11; Ezra 10:43; Nehemiah 11:9

Chronicles-Ezra-Nehemiah, Persian period, 5th-4th century BCE (literary form)

Multiple minor figures bear the name Yoel/Jeuel: a Reubenite clan leader, a Korahite musician, a scribe under King Uzziah, and returnees who married foreign wives. These are genealogical and administrative lists, not myths. They demonstrate Yoel as a common theophoric personal name, not a divine being. Establishes frequency of the name in onomasticon; explains how later angel lists could adopt a human theophoric name as an angelic name, a common process in Jewish angelology.

Accounts II
Youel in Gustav Davidson's Dictionary of Angels (1967)Gustav Davidson, A Dictionary of Angels, Including the Fallen Angels (Free Press, 1967)

1967 (modern compilation)

Davidson lists 'Youel' as an angel name, with minimal gloss and cross-reference to 'Jeuel.' The entry does not provide a narrative, iconography, or office; it functions as a name inventory compiled from earlier lists, rabbinic literature, apocrypha, and medieval manuscripts, often without individual citation. This is the earliest printed attestation of the exact form 'Youel' as an angelic name located in this research. Creates the modern textual anchor for Youel as an angel; subsequent databases, websites, and Wikidata replicate the entry. No new lore is created beyond the name's inclusion.

Youel is an ancient Canaanite or Israelite god with temples and myths. — No temple, cult, myth, or iconography for a god Youel is attested. The name is a Hebrew theophoric personal name ('YHWH is God') borne by humans, later listed as an angel in a 1967 modern dictionary and then reclassified as a deity in a database. No primary text presents Youel as a god with agency.

Youel has a known appearance, sigil, or specific power (e.g., 'angel of ice/knowledge') described in ancient grimoires. — No description, sigil, or office for Youel was verified in any medieval grimoire or Hekhalot text. Descriptions found online are modern inventions, often generated by adding correspondences to fill out a profile card. Davidson's entry provides no such details.

Youel and the prophet Joel are the same figure, so Joel's prophecies are Youel's deeds. — They share the same Hebrew name but are categorically distinct: Joel ben Pethuel is a human prophetic figure with a biblical book; Youel in angel lists is a modern angelic name entry with no narrative. Conflating them collapses a human author/prophet into an angel/deity.

If an angel name ends in -el, it must be ancient and authoritative. — The -el suffix is productive; medieval and modern compilers freely created or varied -el names. Inclusion in a list does not prove antiquity or canonicity. Many names in Davidson are hapax or variant spellings with single, uncited occurrences.
