Who It Is

In the vast catalogue of Greek mythology, most names belong to heroes and gods with grand stories — but some belong to figures who exist only as a whisper. Anchirhoe is such a name: a nymph of the flowing waters whose story has been lost to time. She is a ghost in the archive, a reminder that for every Zeus or Athena, there were hundreds of minor spirits of springs and streams whose names survive while their tales do not. To study Anchirhoe is not to learn a story, but to contemplate the vast silence of antiquity — and to wonder what was once told beside her waters that we will never hear.

Unknown. By analogy with other nymphs of her name-type, she might be imagined as a gentle, elusive presence associated with springs or streams, but this is conjecture. No myth, cult, or narrative preserves her personality.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Anchirhoe (Ἀγχιρρόη) is composed of the Greek elements ἀγχί (ankhi, 'near') and ῥόη (rhoē, 'flow', 'stream'), yielding the sense 'she who flows near' or 'near-flowing'. This is a typical nymph name, evoking proximity to water and the natural world.

Pronunciation — an-KEE-roh-ee (ang-KEER-oh-ay in reconstructed Classical Greek)

Aliases — Anchirrhoe, Anchiroe, Anchirhoë

Ownership — Greek mythology / Classical antiquity — precise regional attribution unknown; name pattern suggests a water nymph or minor female deity

Powers
Nymph powers (presumed)

As a water nymph, Anchirhoe would conventionally possess the abilities of her kind: longevity (though not true immortality), the capacity to bless or curse springs and waters, prophetic ability (some nymphs were oracular), and the power to shape-shift or vanish into water. These are generic nymph abilities, not specifically attested for Anchirhoe.

Limit: Nymphs were generally bound to their local water source; if the source dried up, they suffered or died. Their powers were local, not universal.

Form & Signs

As a nymph whose name suggests flowing water, she would conventionally be imagined as a beautiful young woman, possibly with water-associated attributes such as a hydria (water jar), reeds, or a watery veil. This is reconstructed from the standard iconography of Greek water nymphs, not from any specific source on Anchirhoe.

Symbols — No attested symbols. Water-related attributes (urns, reeds, aquatic plants) would be conventional guesses for a water nymph but are unattested for this specific figure.

Domains — Likely fresh water — a spring, stream, or fountain — based on her name. The precise location is unknown, and no specific sanctuary or cult site is attested.

Accounts II
The Several Nymphs Named AnchirhoePseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca; scholia on Homer; Pauly-Wissowa 'Realencyclopädie' entry on Anchirrhoe

Genealogies compiled in the 2nd century CE; scholia as late as the 6th century CE

Rather than a single unified myth, the name Anchirhoe is attached in antiquity to several distinct water-nymphs. One is a daughter of the river god Nilus who bore a son to the seer Ampyx; another is a daughter of the Argive river Erasinus; and another is named as the wife of Erichthonius, king of Troy, and mother of Tros the eponymous ancestor of the Trojans. Because Greek mythology freely reused such 'flowing-water' nymph names across regions, the ancients themselves did not insist on a single Anchirhoe. Modern encyclopedists, mindful of the confusion, treat them as separate figures unless the sources explicitly link them. This is not a story of heroic deeds but a story about how the classical tradition remembered and multiplied nymph names — and about the careful work of disentangling homonyms in fragmentary texts. The proliferation of the name across different regional genealogies left a distinct footprint in the mythographic record, so that any attempt to describe 'the' Anchirhoe must first reckon with the fact that antiquity knew several. Anchirhoe is the same as the wife of Erichthonius of Troy. — This is one possible identification, but other sources name the wife of Erichthonius as Astyoche or Calirrhoe. The identification is uncertain.

All nymphs named Anchiroe are the same figure. — Greek mythology frequently reused nymph names across regions. The daughter of Nilus and the daughter of Erasinus are likely distinct figures who happened to share a name.
