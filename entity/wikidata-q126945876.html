Form

No literary or artistic description survives. As a nymph whose name suggests flowing water, she would conventionally be imagined as a beautiful young woman, possibly with water-associated attributes such as a hydria (water jar), reeds, or a watery veil. This is reconstructed from the standard iconography of Greek water nymphs, not from any specific source on Anchirhoe.

Powers

Nymph powers (presumed) As a water nymph, Anchirhoe would conventionally possess the abilities of her kind: longevity (though not true immortality), the capacity to bless or curse springs and waters, prophetic ability (some nymphs were oracular), and the power to shape-shift or vanish into water. These are generic nymph abilities, not specifically attested for Anchirhoe. Reconstructed from standard Greek nymph lore; not attested for this specific figure Nymphs were generally bound to their local water source; if the source dried up, they suffered or died. Their powers were local, not universal. No specific powers are attested for Anchirhoe; any attribution is inferred from the nymph class.

Accounts

No myth, story, or narrative involving Anchirhoe survives. She exists only as a name in lexicographic and mythographic records. Any story told about her would be invention.

Rather than a single unified myth, the name Anchirhoe is attached in antiquity to several distinct water-nymphs. One is a daughter of the river god Nilus who bore a son to the seer Ampyx; another is a daughter of the Argive river Erasinus; and another is named as the wife of Erichthonius, king of Troy, and mother of Tros the eponymous ancestor of the Trojans. Because Greek mythology freely reused such 'flowing-water' nymph names across regions, the ancients themselves did not insist on a single Anchirhoe. Modern encyclopedists, mindful of the confusion, treat them as separate figures unless the sources explicitly link them. This is not a story of heroic deeds but a story about how the classical tradition remembered and multiplied nymph names — and about the careful work of disentangling homonyms in fragmentary texts.

What We Don't Know

The evidence for Anchirhoe is extremely thin. No primary literary text survives that clearly features her as a character. The name appears only in lexicographic glosses and possible genealogical lists. Key gaps include: (1) whether the Wikidata entity corresponds to a specific ancient figure or a conflation; (2) which (if any) of the attested Anchiroes is the subject; (3) any cult, sanctuary, or artistic representation; (4) the specific region or tradition that generated the name; (5) any narrative context whatsoever. The research cutoff is August 2026; no new primary evidence has emerged.

A way to hold it in your mind

If Greek mythology were a library, Anchirhoe would be a book that exists only as a title on a catalog card — no pages, no cover, just a name suggesting she was once a story someone knew. She is the mythological equivalent of a background character in a film who never gets a line — but in her case, even the film's credits are incomplete. Her existence is a testament to how much of the ancient world has been lost, and how much of what survives is fragmentary. She is a reminder that our picture of Greek mythology is a mosaic with many missing tiles.
