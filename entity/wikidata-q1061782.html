The Approach

In the dead of night, while the Thracian king Rhesus slept in his camp, two Greek heroes slipped past the guards, killed him and his men, and made off with his magnificent horses—horses that were said to be whiter than snow and swifter than the wind. This brief, brutal episode from Homer's Iliad would spawn a tragic play, a divine resurrection, and a legacy that reaches from ancient Thrace to an Antarctic glacier.

Content advisory. Contains depictions of wartime violence, killing in sleep, and death of a young king.
Identity

Rhesus

Pronunciation REE-sus (English); Rhêsos in Ancient Greek.

Known As

RhesosRhêsosRézas

Etymology

The name is Thracian and probably derives from the Proto-Indo-European root *reg-, meaning 'to rule', showing a satem sound change. A Thracian spelling is given as Rézas, cognate to Latin rex.

Cultural Ownership

Greek mythology, specifically Thracian-Greek cultural sphere.

Disputed Identifications

Rhesus is sometimes confused with a river god of the same name in Bithynia. His parentage varies: Homer says his father was Eioneus, but later writers claim his mother was a Muse (Euterpe, Calliope, or Terpsichore) and his father the river god Strymon. Some accounts also associate him with Bithynia through the huntress Arganthone.

Description

Appearance

No detailed physical description is given in Homer. Later sources emphasize his magnificent armor and horses, which are described as white as snow and swift as the wind, fit for gods.

Symbols & Iconography

His iconic attributes are his horses and golden armor. In ancient art, he is typically depicted as a young warrior being killed in his sleep or his horses being led away by Diomedes and Odysseus.

Behaviour & Personality

In Homer, Rhesus is a latecomer to the war, arriving after the other allies. He is not shown fighting; he is killed in his sleep. In the tragedy Rhesus, he is portrayed as a boastful and confident young king, eager to prove himself.

Domains & Habitats

Rhesus is king of Thrace, a region north of Greece. His camp is near Troy, and he is associated with the river Strymon and later with Bithynia.

Version Differences

Homer presents Rhesus as a mortal king, son of Eioneus. Later traditions make him the son of a Muse and a river god, and also introduce a prophecy that if his horses drink from the Xanthus river, Troy would be invincible. The tragedy Rhesus expands his role and gives him a resurrection.

Stories & Myths

The Night Raid on Rhesus's Camp

Source Homer, Iliad, Book 10Culture GreekPeriod Heroic Age (traditionally 12th century BCE, but composed 8th century BCE)

Summary

During the Trojan War, the Greeks are in a desperate situation. Diomedes and Odysseus volunteer to spy on the Trojan camp. They capture the Trojan spy Dolon, who reveals that Rhesus, the Thracian king, has just arrived with his army and has magnificent horses. The two Greeks go to the Thracian camp, kill Rhesus and twelve of his men, and steal the horses.

Participants

Diomedes

Odysseus

Rhesus

Dolon

Consequences

The Greeks gain the horses, and Rhesus's death demoralizes the Trojans. The episode is a major success for the Greeks.

Variants

Later sources add that a prophecy said if Rhesus's horses drank from the Xanthus river, Troy would be invincible, so the Greeks acted to prevent this. This prophecy is not in Homer.

The Tragedy of Rhesus

Source Pseudo-Euripides, RhesusCulture GreekPeriod 5th century BCE (but possibly later)

Summary

The play dramatizes the events of the Iliad episode, expanding Rhesus's character. He arrives late, boasts of his abilities, and is killed while sleeping. After his death, his mother, the Muse, appears and laments, blaming Odysseus, Diomedes, and Athena. She announces that Rhesus will be resurrected and made immortal, but will live in a cave.

Participants

Rhesus

Odysseus

Diomedes

Athena

The Muse

Consequences

Rhesus is granted a form of immortality, but confined. The play explores themes of fate and heroism.

Variants

The play is attributed to Euripides but its authorship is debated. It may have been written by a later playwright.

Rhesus and Arganthone

Source Parthenius of Nicaea, Erotika Pathemata, chapter 36Culture GreekPeriod 1st century BCE

Summary

Rhesus falls in love with the Bithynian huntress Arganthone. The story is a love story, but details are scant.

Participants

Rhesus

Arganthone

Consequences

This story associates Rhesus with Bithynia and gives him a romantic subplot.

Variants

This is a later mythographic tradition, not in Homer.
