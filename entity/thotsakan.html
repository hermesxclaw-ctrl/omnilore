Who It Is

Thotsakan is the Thai version of the demon king Ravana from the Ramayana, known in Thailand through the Ramakien — the national epic of Thailand composed during the Ayutthaya and Rattanakosin periods. Thotsakan is a ten-faced, twenty-armed rakshasa (demon) king of Longka, renowned for his immense power, wisdom, and mastery of magic. Despite being the antagonist of the Ramakien, Thotsakan is portrayed with greater depth and nuance in Thai versions than in some Indian retellings. He is a devoted son, a passionate lover of Nang Sida (Sita), a formidable warrior, and a patron of the arts. His ten faces represent ten aspects of his character: ambition, desire, anger, pride, greed, delusion, envy, cruelty, fear, and — hidden beneath the others — a spark of genuine nobility. His defeat by Phra Ram (Rama) is not a simple triumph of good over evil but a tragic recognition that Thotsakan's greatness was consumed by his own flaws. The Ramakien is performed in Thailand through khon (masked dance-drama) and shadow puppetry (nang yai). Thotsakan's mask — with its green face, multiple crowns, and fanged mouth — is one of the most iconic images in Thai classical performance.

The Approach

In the shadow of Bangkok's Grand Palace, a towering green demon with ten faces and twenty arms stands guard—Thotsakan, the demon king of Lanka, whose name in Thai means 'ten necks.' He is the great antagonist of the Ramakien, Thailand's national epic, yet he is far more than a villain: a scholar, a devotee of Shiva, a master of illusion, and a figure of tragic complexity whose story has been retold for centuries in Thai art, theater, and literature.

Content advisory. Contains depictions of abduction, warfare, violence, and sexual coercion as part of the epic narrative.

Etymology

Thotsakan (ทศกัณฐ์) is the Thai pronunciation of the Sanskrit 'Dashaanana' (दशानन), meaning 'ten-faced' or 'ten-headed'. In Thai, 'thotsa' (ทศ) means ten, and 'kan' (กัณฐ์) means neck or face, thus 'ten necks'.

Cultural Ownership

Thailand (as Thotsakan), with origins in Indian epic tradition (as Ravana). The figure is central to Thai national epic, the Ramakien, and is featured in Thai classical dance (khon), literature, and art.

Disputed Identifications

While Thotsakan is clearly identified with the Indian Ravana, the Thai version has distinct characteristics and narrative details. Some scholars note that Thotsakan is more sympathetic and less purely evil than Ravana, reflecting Thai cultural values. Additionally, in some local traditions, Thotsakan is worshipped as a guardian figure, particularly in southern Thailand.

Description

Appearance

Thotsakan is typically depicted as a giant demon with green skin, ten heads, and twenty arms. In Thai art, he wears elaborate royal attire, often with a crown and jewelry, and may hold a variety of weapons. His faces are often shown with fierce expressions, but in some depictions, he is portrayed with a more noble bearing, reflecting his status as a king and scholar.

Symbols & Iconography

In the Ramakien, Thotsakan is associated with the color green, symbolizing his demonic nature, and with the number ten (heads). He is often shown with a sword, a trident, or a bow. In Thai iconography, he is also linked to the mythical city of Lanka, which is sometimes depicted as a golden palace. His vehicle is the aerial chariot, and he is associated with the lion or the peacock in some artistic representations.

Behaviour & Personality

In the Ramakien, Thotsakan is portrayed as a powerful and intelligent king, but also as arrogant, lustful, and deceitful. He is a devoted follower of Shiva and a scholar of the Vedas. In contrast to the Indian Ravana, Thotsakan is sometimes shown to have a softer side, particularly in his love for Sita, and he is known to be a just ruler to his subjects. His character is complex, with both heroic and villainous traits.

Domains & Habitats

Thotsakan rules the island kingdom of Lanka, which in Thai tradition is often identified with Sri Lanka. In the Ramakien, his palace is described as a magnificent golden city. He also has a hermitage in the forest of Janasthana, where he often retreats.

Version Differences

In the Thai Ramakien, Thotsakan's abduction of Sita is motivated by a curse from a hermit, making him more sympathetic. Additionally, in the Thai version, Thotsakan is not killed by Rama but by Phra Ram's arrow, with his death being more tragic. The Thai version also includes additional episodes, such as Thotsakan's attempt to create a false Sita, and his son's (Ongkhot) role is more prominent.

Ten Heads

Source Version Both Ramakien and Ramayana

Description

Thotsakan has ten heads, which symbolize his vast knowledge and ability to see in all directions. In some stories, he can detach and reattach them.

Limitations

In Thai tradition, it is said that if his head is cut off, he can only be killed by a Brahma arrow.

Contradictions

In the Indian version, he is depicted with ten heads, but in some Thai depictions, he may have only one head with multiple faces.

Thotsakan received a boon from Brahma that made him invincible to gods, demons, and celestial beings, but not to humans or animals.

Stories & Myths

The Abduction of Sita

Source Ramakien, based on RamayanaCulture ThaiPeriod Treta Yuga (epic age)

Summary

Thotsakan, driven by lust and a curse, disguises himself as a hermit and abducts Sita, the wife of Phra Ram (Rama), taking her to Lanka. He tries to win her love but is rejected. This act leads to the great war between Phra Ram and Thotsakan.

Participants

Consequences

The abduction triggers the war in which Thotsakan is ultimately killed and Sita is rescued.

Variants

In the Ramakien, Thotsakan is cursed by a hermit to be infatuated with Sita, making his actions less malicious and more tragic.

The Battle with Hanuman

Source RamakienCulture ThaiPeriod Treta Yuga

Summary

Participants

The Death of Thotsakan

Source RamakienCulture ThaiPeriod Treta Yuga

Summary

In the final battle, Thotsakan is killed by Phra Ram using a special arrow (Brahma arrow) that pierces his vital point. Before dying, Thotsakan asks Phra Ram to honor him by performing his funeral rites, which Phra Ram does, showing respect for his enemy.

Participants

Thotsakan

Phra Ram

Lakshaman

Consequences

Thotsakan's death ends the war, and Sita is freed. Phra Ram's act of honoring Thotsakan's last wish highlights the complexity of their relationship.

Variants

In the Indian Ramayana, Ravana is killed by Rama with a Brahma weapon, but the detail of the funeral rites is less emphasized.

Known Gaps

While the Ramakien is well-documented, there are gaps in the specific Thai adaptations of Thotsakan's character in contemporary media. The research relies heavily on the Valmiki Ramayana for details of Ravana's backstory, as the Ramakien assumes knowledge of the broader epic.
