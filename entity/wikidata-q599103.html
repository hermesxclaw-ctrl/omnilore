Who It Is

Before the Queen of Sheba was a legend, before the spice routes made Saba the 'Fortunate Arabia' of the Greeks, there was a god who demanded the horns of a bull and the blood of the battlefield. His name was Almaqah, and he was the divine engine of an empire. His temple at Marib was not a quiet place of prayer; it was a colossal statement of power, a throne for a god who crowned kings and crushed their enemies. To understand Almaqah is to understand how a people built a civilization out of sand, stone, and a fierce, unyielding faith.

As a national god, Almaqah was the divine guarantor of Sabaean state power. He was a god of war, granting victory to his kings. He was a god of agriculture, responsible for the fertility of the land and the success of the irrigation systems that made Saba's wealth possible. He was a god of justice and order, receiving oaths and settling disputes. He was a god of oracles, communicating his will through divination. Powers
Divine Kingship

Almaqah was the source of royal legitimacy. Kings of Saba were described as 'beloved of Almaqah' and their reigns were seen as sanctioned by him. His favor was essential for a successful reign.

Limit: His favor could be withdrawn, leading to the downfall of a king or dynasty.

Military Victory

Almaqah was a god of war. Kings dedicated their victories to him, and his name was invoked in campaigns. The 'Great Inscription of Sirwah' describes Karib'il Watar's conquests as being carried out 'with the support of Almaqah'.

Limit: His support was not guaranteed; sacrifices and oaths were required.

Agricultural Fertility

Almaqah was responsible for the fertility of the land. The success of the Marib dam, the lifeblood of Sabaean agriculture, was attributed to his blessing. Votive offerings of ibex, a mountain animal associated with water sources, were common.

Limit: His blessing was contingent on the proper maintenance of the temples and the performance of rituals.

Oracular Guidance

Almaqah provided divine guidance through oracles. Inscriptions record requests for oracles (mlk) from Almaqah, likely through divinatory practices at his temples.

Limit: The specific methods of divination are unknown.

Divine Protection

Almaqah protected his worshippers, particularly the king and the state. He was invoked to protect individuals, families, and the kingdom as a whole from harm.

Limit: His protection was conditional on proper worship.

Cosmic Order

As a solar/astral deity (per modern scholarly interpretation), Almaqah was associated with the maintenance of cosmic order and the cycles of the sun, which were essential for agriculture and the calendar.

Limit: This is a reconstruction, not a directly attested belief.

His presence was symbolized by his sacred animals, particularly the bull, whose horns adorned the temple entrances, and the ibex, which was a common votive offering. Accounts I
The Great Inscription of Sirwah (Res Gestae of Karib'il Watar)The Great Inscription of Sirwah

c. 685 BCE

This monumental text records the military campaigns of the Sabaean king Karib'il Watar. It details his conquests and the consolidation of Sabaean power, all undertaken 'by the power of Almaqah'. The inscription is a political and religious manifesto, demonstrating how Almaqah was invoked as the divine patron of Sabaean imperialism. It lists the gods of conquered peoples, establishing Almaqah's supremacy over them. Established the historical narrative of Sabaean dominance and the divine sanction of the kingdom.

The Founding of the Awwam TempleSabaean inscriptions

Late 2nd millennium BCE (earliest phases) to 3rd century CE (final phases)

The Awwam Temple, also known as the Mahram Bilqis (Sanctuary of the Queen of Sheba), was the most important temple of Almaqah. Its construction and expansion over centuries was a major state project. Inscriptions record kings and nobles dedicating building works to Almaqah, including the monumental entrance, the peristyle court, and the 'throne'. The temple was the physical center of Almaqah's cult and the political heart of Saba. The temple became the symbol of Sabaean religious and political identity.

Accounts II
The Temple of Almaqah at SirwahSabaean inscriptions

7th century BCE

The town of Sirwah was a secondary religious and political center. Its temple to Almaqah was built and expanded by kings, including Karib'il Watar. The temple's architecture and inscriptions mirror the larger Awwam temple, indicating a coordinated religious policy. Established Sirwah as a key site for the cult of Almaqah.

The Queen of Sheba's Visit to Solomon (External Story)Hebrew Bible/Old Testament (1 Kings 10; 2 Chronicles 9)

Textual traditions from the 1st millennium BCE

The biblical account tells of the Queen of Sheba (Saba) visiting King Solomon to test his wisdom. This story, while not mentioning Almaqah, is a vital external reference to the wealth and power of the Sabaean kingdom. The queen's visit is a testament to the fame of Saba, the kingdom whose national god was Almaqah. In later Islamic tradition, she is known as Bilqis and is said to have been a sun-worshipper before her conversion. The story cemented Saba's place in world history and religious tradition.

Almaqah was the moon god. — This is an outdated 19th-century theory. Modern scholarship rejects it, and there is no lunar iconography associated with Almaqah. He is more likely a solar or astral deity.

The Awwam Temple was the palace of the Queen of Sheba. — The Awwam Temple (Mahram Bilqis) was a temple dedicated to Almaqah. The name 'Mahram Bilqis' is a later Islamic-era attribution, not an ancient one. The Queen of Sheba is not mentioned in any Sabaean inscription.

Almaqah was a god of a single, unified 'South Arabian' religion. — Ancient South Arabia was not a single religious unit. Each kingdom (Saba, Ma'in, Qataban, Hadramawt) had its own national god and its own distinct religious traditions.

The Sabaeans were a mysterious, isolated people. — The Sabaeans were a major trading power with extensive connections to the Mediterranean, Africa, and India. They were known to the Greeks, Romans, and Israelites.
