Form

No anthropomorphic image of Almaqah from the Sabaean period has been identified. He was worshipped primarily in aniconic form. His presence was symbolized by his sacred animals, particularly the bull, whose horns adorned the temple entrances, and the ibex, which was a common votive offering. The 'throne' of Almaqah, a large stone structure within the Awwam temple, was a key cult object, representing his presence and rule. The temple itself was his 'house' (mḥrm), and its architecture was a physical manifestation of his divine order, with a processional way leading from the entrance to the inner sanctum.

Powers

Divine Kingship Almaqah was the source of royal legitimacy. Kings of Saba were described as 'beloved of Almaqah' and their reigns were seen as sanctioned by him. His favor was essential for a successful reign. Sabaean royal inscriptions His favor could be withdrawn, leading to the downfall of a king or dynasty. None, this is a consistent theme.

Military Victory Almaqah was a god of war. Kings dedicated their victories to him, and his name was invoked in campaigns. The 'Great Inscription of Sirwah' describes Karib'il Watar's conquests as being carried out 'with the support of Almaqah'. Sabaean royal inscriptions His support was not guaranteed; sacrifices and oaths were required. None, this is a consistent theme.

Agricultural Fertility Almaqah was responsible for the fertility of the land. The success of the Marib dam, the lifeblood of Sabaean agriculture, was attributed to his blessing. Votive offerings of ibex, a mountain animal associated with water sources, were common. Sabaean inscriptions and votive practice His blessing was contingent on the proper maintenance of the temples and the performance of rituals. The exact nature of his agricultural role is debated; some scholars see it as secondary to his political role.

Oracular Guidance Almaqah provided divine guidance through oracles. Inscriptions record requests for oracles (mlk) from Almaqah, likely through divinatory practices at his temples. Sabaean inscriptions The specific methods of divination are unknown. None, though the interpretation of the term 'mlk' is debated.

Divine Protection Almaqah protected his worshippers, particularly the king and the state. He was invoked to protect individuals, families, and the kingdom as a whole from harm. Sabaean inscriptions His protection was conditional on proper worship. None.

Cosmic Order As a solar/astral deity (per modern scholarly interpretation), Almaqah was associated with the maintenance of cosmic order and the cycles of the sun, which were essential for agriculture and the calendar. Behavior & Habitat

As a national god, Almaqah was the divine guarantor of Sabaean state power. He was a god of war, granting victory to his kings. He was a god of agriculture, responsible for the fertility of the land and the success of the irrigation systems that made Saba's wealth possible. He was a god of justice and order, receiving oaths and settling disputes. He was a god of oracles, communicating his will through divination. His behavior, as recorded in inscriptions, was transactional: the king and the people offered him sacrifices, temples, and tribute, and in return, he protected them, granted them victory, and ensured their prosperity.

Accounts

This monumental text records the military campaigns of the Sabaean king Karib'il Watar. It details his conquests and the consolidation of Sabaean power, all undertaken 'by the power of Almaqah'. The inscription is a political and religious manifesto, demonstrating how Almaqah was invoked as the divine patron of Sabaean imperialism. It lists the gods of conquered peoples, establishing Almaqah's supremacy over them.

The Awwam Temple, also known as the Mahram Bilqis (Sanctuary of the Queen of Sheba), was the most important temple of Almaqah. Its construction and expansion over centuries was a major state project. Inscriptions record kings and nobles dedicating building works to Almaqah, including the monumental entrance, the peristyle court, and the 'throne'. The temple was the physical center of Almaqah's cult and the political heart of Saba.

The town of Sirwah was a secondary religious and political center. Its temple to Almaqah was built and expanded by kings, including Karib'il Watar. The temple's architecture and inscriptions mirror the larger Awwam temple, indicating a coordinated religious policy.

The biblical account tells of the Queen of Sheba (Saba) visiting King Solomon to test his wisdom. This story, while not mentioning Almaqah, is a vital external reference to the wealth and power of the Sabaean kingdom. The queen's visit is a testament to the fame of Saba, the kingdom whose national god was Almaqah. In later Islamic tradition, she is known as Bilqis and is said to have been a sun-worshipper before her conversion.

What We Don't Know

1. The precise cosmic role of Almaqah (sun vs. moon vs. other) remains unresolved. 2. The exact meaning of his name is unknown. 3. The specific rituals and divination practices of his cult are not fully understood. 4. The relationship between the cult of Almaqah and the Queen of Sheba legend is entirely conjectural, as no Sabaean text mentions her. 5. The full extent of his cult outside of Saba (in D'mt and Aksum) is poorly documented. 6. Access to primary sources is limited by the ongoing conflict in Yemen.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Almaqah not as a god of a quiet village, but as the divine CEO of a multinational corporation. He was the patron deity of the 'Saba Inc.', a state whose wealth came from monopolizing the spice trade. His temples were the corporate headquarters, his priests the executives, and his kings the CEOs. He was not a god of personal salvation or mystical insight; he was a god of power, conquest, and prosperity. His worship was a state-sponsored enterprise, and his favor was the ultimate currency.
