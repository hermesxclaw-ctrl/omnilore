Form

In the Hebrew Bible, Adramelech is not described visually; the focus is on the cultic practice of child sacrifice. In later Christian demonology, specifically in Collin de Plancy's 'Dictionnaire Infernal' and related grimoires, Adramelech is described as having the body of a peacock and the head of a human (sometimes a mule's head). The peacock body is a distinctive and striking image. In some later occult texts, he is depicted as a more humanoid figure with a crown, or as a figure with the head of a peacock. The peacock imagery likely derives from the bird's association with pride and vanity in Christian symbolism, fitting for a demon of pride.

Behavior & Habitat

As a deity in the Hebrew Bible, Adramelech is associated with the practice of child sacrifice, a practice condemned by the biblical authors. In demonology, he is described as a high-ranking demon, often a Chancellor or President of the High Council of Hell, suggesting a role in governance and counsel rather than direct combat. He is sometimes said to be the demon of pride or vanity. In grimoires, he is often invoked for knowledge and counsel, though always with the caveat of the dangers of dealing with demons.

Accounts

The text describes the people of Sepharvaim, a city deported to Samaria by the Assyrians, as burning their children in fire to Adrammelech and Anammelech. This is presented as a horrific practice, a key example of the idolatry that the biblical authors condemned.

Adrammelech, a son of the Assyrian king Sennacherib, murders his father while he is worshiping in the temple of his god Nisroch. Adrammelech and his brother Sharezer then flee to the land of Ararat (Urartu). Sennacherib is succeeded by another son, Esarhaddon.

Adramelech is re-imagined as a powerful demon, the Grand Chancellor of Hell. He appears in lists of demons and in instructions for invocation. He is often depicted with a peacock's body, symbolizing pride. His role is that of a counselor and administrator in the infernal court.

Content Notes

The biblical text explicitly states that the people of Sepharvaim burned their children in fire to Adramelech. This is the most significant and disturbing aspect of his historical worship. The practice is condemned by the biblical authors and is a key example of the 'abominations' of non-Israelite religions.

Scholars debate the prevalence and nature of child sacrifice in ancient Near Eastern religions. Some argue that the biblical accounts are polemical exaggerations, while others see evidence for the practice in archaeological and textual sources. The exact nature of the rite is debated, with some suggesting it may have been a dedication or a ritual of 'passing through fire' rather than a fatal burning, though the biblical text clearly implies death.

2 Kings 17:31

The human Adrammelech, son of Sennacherib, murders his father. This is a political assassination, a common occurrence in ancient Near Eastern courts, but it is still an act of extreme violence.

Assyrian records confirm that Sennacherib was assassinated, though they do not name the assassin. The biblical account provides a specific name and motive (divine judgment). The act is a significant historical event.

2 Kings 19:37; Isaiah 37:38

The transformation of a deity into a demon is a form of religious polemic. This process, known as 'demonization', reflects the Christian view of pagan deities as demons (as seen in Psalm 96:5: 'For all the gods of the nations are idols'). This is not unique to Adramelech but is part of a broader pattern.

This process is well-documented in religious studies. It serves to delegitimize the worship of other gods and to assert the supremacy of the Christian God.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Adramelech as a political cartoon villain who got a promotion. The Bible drew a cartoon of a foreign god as a monster who demanded child sacrifice — a way of saying 'those people are evil.' Centuries later, the Church took that cartoon and gave it a job in Hell's bureaucracy, complete with a fancy title. And now, video games have turned him into a boss you can fight. He's less a coherent figure and more a mirror reflecting what each era fears or wants to mock.
