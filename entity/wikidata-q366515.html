Who It Is

Before he was a demon with a peacock's body and a corner office in Hell, Adramelech was a god whose worshippers burned their children alive. That's the trajectory of this entity: from a terrifying local deity, condemned in the Bible, to a high-ranking bureaucrat in the infernal court, and finally to a recurring boss in your favorite JRPG. His story is a case study in how cultures 'other' their enemies' gods, and how those same gods get reborn in the unlikeliest of places.

As a deity in the Hebrew Bible, Adramelech is associated with the practice of child sacrifice, a practice condemned by the biblical authors. In demonology, he is described as a high-ranking demon, often a Chancellor or President of the High Council of Hell, suggesting a role in governance and counsel rather than direct combat. He is sometimes said to be the demon of pride or vanity. In grimoires, he is often invoked for knowledge and counsel, though always with the caveat of the dangers of dealing with demons.

Powers
Divine Authority (as a deity)

As a god of Sepharvaim, Adramelech was presumably believed to have power over the city and its people, with the right to demand worship and sacrifice. The specific nature of his power is not detailed in the biblical text.

Limit: Not detailed; the biblical text is polemical and does not explore the deity's powers.

Chancellor of Hell

In demonology, Adramelech is a high-ranking official in the infernal hierarchy. This grants him authority over other demons, a role in counsel, and the ability to grant knowledge or favors to those who invoke him (per grimoires).

Limit: As a demon, he is ultimately subordinate to Satan and bound by the rules of the demonic hierarchy. Invoking him is dangerous.

Granting Knowledge and Counsel

In occult practices, Adramelech is invoked for knowledge, particularly in matters of statecraft, law, or governance, given his role as Chancellor.

Limit: The knowledge comes at a price, typically the soul of the invoker or other sacrifices.

Solar Association (disputed)

Some scholars and occultists associate Adramelech with the sun, based on the possible etymology connecting him to a solar deity. This is not a widely attested power.

Limit: The association is speculative and not clearly supported by primary sources.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In the Hebrew Bible, Adramelech is not described visually; the focus is on the cultic practice of child sacrifice. In later Christian demonology, specifically in Collin de Plancy's 'Dictionnaire Infernal' and related grimoires, Adramelech is described as having the body of a peacock and the head of a human (sometimes a mule's head). The peacock body is a distinctive and striking image. In some later occult texts, he is depicted as a more humanoid

Symbols — The peacock (in demonology) is his primary symbol. In the biblical context, the symbol is implicit — the act of child sacrifice itself. In occult contexts, he may be associated with a crown or scepter (as a chancellor) and with the sun (as a solar deity).

Domains — As a deity: associated with the city of Sepharvaim, possibly a sun god or a god of a specific region. In demonology: Chancellor of Hell, President of the High Council of Devils, associated with pride, vanity, and possibly with the sin of gluttony (in some lists). He is also sometimes associated with the planet Mars or the Sun.

Accounts I
The Worship of Adramelech in SepharvaimHebrew Bible (2 Kings 17:31)

c. 8th-7th century BCE (the events described) / c. 7th-6th century BCE (the text)

The text describes the people of Sepharvaim, a city deported to Samaria by the Assyrians, as burning their children in fire to Adrammelech and Anammelech. This is presented as a horrific practice, a key example of the idolatry that the biblical authors condemned. The biblical text uses this as evidence of the wickedness of the non-Israelite peoples and as a justification for God's judgment. It also serves as a warning to Israel.

Accounts II
The Assassination of Sennacherib by Adrammelech (the human)Hebrew Bible (2 Kings 19:37; Isaiah 37:38)

c. 681 BCE (event); c. 7th-6th century BCE (text)

Adrammelech, a son of the Assyrian king Sennacherib, murders his father while he is worshiping in the temple of his god Nisroch. Adrammelech and his brother Sharezer then flee to the land of Ararat (Urartu). Sennacherib is succeeded by another son, Esarhaddon. The assassination destabilizes the Assyrian throne, leading to a succession crisis. The biblical text presents this as divine judgment on Sennacherib for his arrogance and his failed siege of Jerusalem.

Adramelech in the GrimoiresDictionnaire Infernal, Pseudomonarchia Daemonum, various grimoires

16th-19th centuries

Adramelech is re-imagined as a powerful demon, the Grand Chancellor of Hell. He appears in lists of demons and in instructions for invocation. He is often depicted with a peacock's body, symbolizing pride. His role is that of a counselor and administrator in the infernal court. The demonological Adramelech becomes the basis for most modern pop-culture depictions, far removed from the biblical deity.

Adramelech is a purely fictional demon invented by modern media. — The name and concept have ancient roots in the Hebrew Bible as a deity, and a long history in Christian demonology. Modern media draws on this older tradition.

Adramelech is always depicted with a peacock's body. — The peacock body is a specific depiction from Collin de Plancy's 'Dictionnaire Infernal' (19th century). Earlier and later depictions vary significantly, and some modern depictions use a more humanoid form.

The biblical Adramelech was a demon. — In the Bible, Adramelech is a deity worshipped by a foreign people. He is not called a demon in the text, though the practice of worshipping him is condemned. The demonization happens later in Christian tradition.

Adramelech and Moloch are the same deity. — While both are associated with child sacrifice in biblical texts, they are distinct deities. Adramelech is associated with Sepharvaim, while Moloch is associated with the Ammonites. The names are different, and there is no evidence they were the same god.
