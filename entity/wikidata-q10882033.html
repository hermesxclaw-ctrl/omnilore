Who It Is

In the alleys of old Fuzhou they do not pray to Emperor Wufu because he is kind. They pray because he is dangerous — one of five scholar-ghosts who drank poisoned well water to prove a plague was coming, died for it, and were then promoted by Heaven to decide who lives and who dies when the next epidemic arrives.

In lore, the Wufu are ambivalent: righteous scholars in life, stern and fearsome as gods. They are not compassionate healers like Baosheng Dadi; they are inspectors and executioners who patrol the human world on behalf of Heaven to distribute pestilence where moral order has collapsed, but can be petitioned to withhold or remove plague. Behavior is bureaucratic and martial: they tour (chuxun), investigate, report to the Jade Emperor, and command lesser plague demons and soldiers. Name & Tongue

Etymology — Chinese 五福大帝 Wǔfú Dàdì. Wu (五) 'five'; Fu (福) 'blessing/fortune' but in this cult euphemistic for plague-averting power; Dadi (大帝) 'Great Emperor', an imperial title granted by Heaven. The name is a euphemism: like calling plague gods 'Blessing Emperors' to propitiate them. Minnan (Hokkien) Gō͘-hok

Powers
Plague Distribution and Withdrawal

Authority to release and retract epidemic disease across a territory. Ritual texts describe them carrying Heaven's edict to spread wenyi (epidemic qi) as punishment and to collect it when propitiated.

Limit: Cannot act without Jade Emperor's warrant; limited to appointed jurisdictions and seasons; can be countered by higher deities (e.g., Baosheng Dadi, Mazu) or by proper ritual sending-away; not omnipotent — must be invited and sent off.

Inspection Tour (Chuxun)

Periodic invisible or processional tour of human settlements to observe morality and hygiene, accompanied by demon soldiers. Presence during tour explains outbreaks.

Limit: Tours are calendrically bound (often 3-year cycle) and require human invitation via ritual; they must leave when boat is burned/launched.

Command of Plague Demons and Spirit Soldiers

Commands wengui (plague ghosts) and wubing (pestilential soldiers) who actually spread disease; the Emperors are generals, not foot soldiers.

Limit: Control is bureaucratic — soldiers can be unruly; requires seals and tallies.

Protection by Propitiation (Apotropaic Protection)

If properly worshipped with offerings, opera, and plague-boat send-off, they protect community from disease for the ritual cycle. Protection is contractual, not unconditional grace.

Limit: Protection expires; requires renewal; fails if ritual is improperly performed or community morality lapses.

Poison Detection / Self-Sacrifice Martyrdom (Origin Power)

In origin legend, ability to detect poisoned well water through scholar's knowledge or divine insight, and willingness to drink to prove danger — transformed posthumously into divine authority.

Limit: This is mortal feat, not post-deification power; not repeated as miracle after death.

Accounts I
The Five Scholars and the Poisoned WellMin Du Bie Ji (He Qiu) and Bailong'an temple legend, summarized in Dean 1993

Legend set in Sui-Tang transition (c. 610-636), recorded in Qing text (1790s-1840s)

Five jinshi (presented scholars) traveling to or residing in Fuzhou — Zhang Yuanbo, Zhong Shiying, Liu Yuanda, Shi Wenye, Zhao Guangming — encounter a plague demon who poisons the wells to spread pestilence. To convince skeptical villagers who see clear water, the scholars drink the water themselves, prove it is poisoned, and die. Their sacrifice reveals the demon and saves the city. The Jade Emperor, moved by their righteousness, canonizes them as Wuling Gong / Wufu Dadi to govern pestilence. Establishes moral charter of cult: plague gods were once moral humans; justifies both fear and respect; explains well and wine-cup iconography; foundation for Bailong'an.

Investiture by the Jade EmperorRitual scriptures (Wuling jing) and Fuzhou Fuzhi temple records

Ming-Qing codification of earlier oral tradition

After death, the five spirits are brought before the Jade Emperor's court. Because they died protecting the people, they are not judged as ordinary ghosts but enfeoffed as Great Emperors with bureaucratic rank, given seals, armies, and jurisdiction over pestilence. They are instructed to patrol the southeast and to punish communities that violate Heaven's order, but also to respond to proper sacrifice. Explains divine-bureaucratic status; integrates local cult into celestial hierarchy; provides ritual logic for petitions and edicts.

The Founding of Bailong'an (White Dragon Hermitage)Bailong'an stele inscriptions and Fuzhou Fuzhi

Temple traditionally founded Tang-Song, rebuilt Ming Wanli (late 16th c.), major Qing expansions

After their deification, the Five Emperors manifest to cure or threaten Fuzhou. Locals build a shrine at the site of the poisoned well or where white dragon was seen. The hermitage becomes Bailong'an, the mother temple of the cult. Successive epidemics and recoveries reinforce its power, attracting official patronage and merchant guild support. Creates central pilgrimage site; model for branch temples; anchors cult to specific urban landscape in Cangshan.

Accounts II
Crossing to Taiwan — The Tainan BranchTaiwan County Gazetteer (Taiwan Xianzhi), Tainan Bai Long An Wu Ling Tang temple history, and Soo Khin Wah research

Qing Kangxi-Qianlong (late 17th-18th c.), with 19th c. cholera legends

Fuzhou migrants bring incense and division-spirits (fenxiang) of Wufu Dadi to Taiwan. In Tainan, the Bai Long An Wu Ling Tang is established. During 19th-century cholera and plague outbreaks, the Emperors are credited with halting epidemics after processions and plague-boat rituals, cementing their role as protectors of the migrant community. Transplants cult to Taiwan where it becomes part of Wangye pluri-cult; establishes Taiwan as second center; ritual practices diverge.

The Plague Boat Send-Off (Wangchuan / Wangkang)Living ritual practice documented by Dean, Katz, and UNESCO ICH nomination (2020)

Attested Ming-Qing to present; UNESCO inscription 2020

When plague threatens or on a fixed cycle (typically every three years), the community builds an elaborate plague boat (wangchuan) — a large paper or wooden ship loaded with offerings, paper soldiers, and symbolic plague. The Five Emperors and their demon armies are invited, feasted, entertained with opera, and then ritually escorted to the boat, which is burned or launched to sea to carry pestilence away. The Emperors are thus both honored and expelled. Core apotropaic ritual; reinforces contractual relationship; now recognized as intangible cultural heritage; major communal festival.

Contention with Baosheng Dadi and MazuOral legends collected in Fujian and Taiwan, referenced in Shahar & Weller and Feuchtwang

Qing to contemporary oral tradition

In some legends, the plague-dealing Wufu clash with healing gods like Baosheng Dadi (God of Medicine) or protective Mazu. Baosheng Dadi is said to block or cure the plagues sent by Wufu, leading to territorial truces where villages worship both — one to prevent disease, the other to cure it. Processional routes are negotiated to avoid confrontation. Explains temple co-existence and ritual boundaries; illustrates moral ambiguity of plague gods versus benevolent healers.

Historical Dating of MartyrdomSui Daye era (605-618) — five scholars died during Sui.Tang Zhenguan 10 (636) — death dated precisely to 636.
Nature of Deity: Blessing vs Plague GodWufu are benevolent 'Five Blessings' fortune gods granting longevity, wealth, health, etc.Wufu are dangerous plague gods who distribute and remove pestilence and must be expelled.

Emperor Wufu is the Chinese god of good fortune who grants the 'Five Blessings'. — The name is a euphemism. Wufu Dadi's core function in all pre-modern sources is control of plague and untimely death. The 'Five Blessings' (wufu) from the Shujing are a separate classical concept; conflation is a modern folk etymology and tourism rebranding that erases the epidemic core.

There is one Emperor Wufu with a single biography. — Attested worship is of five sworn brothers co-deified. Temples house five statues with distinct names (Zhang Yuanbo et al.). Singular 'Emperor Wufu' is shorthand for the group.

Wufu Dadi is the same as the Five Plague Messengers or Wangye everywhere in China. — They belong to a broader South China plague-god complex but are a distinct Min (Fuzhou) lineage with its own mother temple, legend, and roster. Wangye is a larger, heterogeneous category with many regional systems (e.g., Marshal Wen in Zhejiang).

The cult is ancient, with Tang dynasty temples still standing. — Legend is set in Tang, but extant temples and steles are Ming-Qing and later, with major 20th-century destruction and reconstruction. No Tang archaeological evidence for the cult has been published.
