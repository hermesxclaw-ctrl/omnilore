Who It Is

At the mouth where a brown river dissolves into the Taiwan Strait, a low hill holds a red-walled fort that has flown Spanish, Dutch, Zheng, Qing, Japanese and Republic of China flags without ever moving. Below it, a street smells of iron eggs, squid and sea wind, ferries cut across the estuary at sunset, and the same water the Ketagalan called Hoba — "stream's mouth" — still decides the town's fate. Tamsui is not large — 205,706 people on the northwestern tip of New Taipei — but for four centuries every power that wanted Taiwan had to take Tamsui first.

As a place, Tamsui's 'personality' in sources is that of a threshold and entrepôt: pragmatic, multilingual, outward-facing. Spanish and Dutch accounts describe a pacified but resistant aboriginal periphery and Han intermediary traders. Qing customs reports describe a bustling tea/camphor/opium port with foreign enclave. Mackay and 19th-century consular diaries describe a tolerant but stratified port town where missionaries, merchants and temple committees coexisted. Powers
Natural harbor and river-mouth control

Controls access to the Tamsui River system draining the Taipei Basin, historically the only navigable entry to inland Taipei (Banka/Twatutia). Fort on hill commands estuary and Strait.

Limit: River siltation from 1890s onward made harbor too shallow for modern steamships; function transferred to Keelung. Typhoon and monsoon silting requires constant dredging.

Treaty-port trade gateway

Under Treaty of Tientsin, legal export of tea, camphor, sulfur, coal, dyes and opium; import of opium, textiles, kerosene. British Consulate and Customs Officers' Residence administered maritime customs.

Limit: Trade dependent on foreign gunboat enforcement; subject to blockade (1884). Revenue to Qing, not local district.

Fortified military node

Successive forts: Spanish Santo Domingo (wood/earth), Dutch Fort Antonio (brick/stone quadrangle with bastions), Qing Hobe Fort (1886, modern artillery with Armstrong guns), Japanese coastal batteries.

Limit: Each fort obsolete within decades; Hobe Fort never fired in combat after 1884. Dutch fort required VOC supply line.

Missionary and educational center

Base for Presbyterian mission: hospitals practicing Western medicine, Oxford College (1882, now Aletheia University) — oldest European-style higher education institution in Taiwan — and girls' schools. Created literate Hokkien Christian community.

Limit: Limited to northern Taiwan; faced opposition from temple elites and periodic persecution.

Cultural and tourism generator

Heritage economy: museums (Fort Santo Domingo, Tamsui Historical Museum, Customs Wharf, Art Gallery), temples, Old Street, Fisherman's Wharf, Lover's Bridge, light rail and Metro access generate domestic tourism (Metro Tamsui Line 1997). Educational cluster draws students nationally.

Limit: Dependent on Taipei day-trippers; vulnerable to over-tourism, gentrification and sea-level rise.

Accounts I
Hoba and the naming of the river mouthKetagalan oral toponymy via Spanish and Dutch transcriptions; summarized in Tamsui District Office history

Pre-1629, recorded 1629-1640s

Before colonization, the estuary was called Hoba, 'stream's mouth', by Ketagalan inhabitants who fished and gathered at the river mouth. Hoklo settlers transliterated it as Hobe, which persisted as the Taiwanese name for the old village even after the Mandarin name 淡水 (fresh water) became official. Spanish labeled the wider region Casidor and the river Kimalon; Dutch wrote Tamsuy/Tampsui. The persistence of Hobe in Hokkien shows indigenous toponymy surviving beneath successive colonial renamings. Dual naming system (Hobe in Hokkien, Tamsui/Danshui in Mandarin) that continues today; modern district retains both in signage.

Spanish Santo Domingo — the first non-aboriginal townSpanish Formosa records; Andrade, Borao Mateo; district office history

1629-1638 (Spanish occupation of north Formosa 1626-1642)

To secure the Manila-Fujian-Japan trade route against the Dutch in the south, the Spanish in fall 1629 built the town and mission of Santo Domingo at Tamsui, the first major non-aboriginal settlement there. The fort was earth and wood, garrisoned from Manila. Harassed by Ketagalan resistance and supply difficulties, the Spanish abandoned Tamsui in 1638 to concentrate at Keelung (Santísima Trinidad), leaving the fort in ruins. Established Tamsui as strategic chokepoint; ruins provided foundation for Dutch fort; introduced Catholic mission precedent later replaced by Protestantism.

Dutch Fort Antonio and Han colonizationVOC Dagregisters 1642-1662; Fort Santo Domingo museum archaeological reports

1642-1662 (with Han settlement continuing under Zheng 1662-1683)

After expelling the Spanish from Keelung in 1642, the Dutch in 1644 rebuilt the Tamsui fort in brick and stone over the ruins, renaming it Fort Antonio after Governor-General Antonio van Diemen. The quadrangular fort with corner bastions — today called the 'Red-Hair Fortress' (Âng-mn̂g-siâⁿ) — pacified nearby villages and promoted Han immigration to grow rice and exploit sulfur, deerskins and trade. The Dutch left Taiwan in 1662 after Koxinga's siege of Fort Zeelandia and evacuated Keelung in 1668 after aboriginal harassment from Tamsui. Permanent brick fort survives as Taiwan's oldest European-style building core; began large-scale Han settlement of the Tamsui plain.

Accounts II
Treaty port and the tea-camphor boomTreaty of Tientsin 1858; Imperial Maritime Customs Reports 1862-1895; British Consular records

1862-1895

Under the Treaty of Tientsin, Tamsui was opened to foreign trade in 1862. Qing established a customs house and naval patrol outpost (1808). British consulate was housed in the former Dutch fort. Tamsui rapidly became Taiwan's largest port, exporting Formosa Oolong tea, camphor (for celluloid), sulfur, coal and dyes, and importing opium and textiles. A foreign settlement with warehouses, churches and cemeteries grew beside Hobe village. By the mid-1880s the port handled more tonnage than Anping or Keelung. Created wealthy merchant class and foreign enclave; funded modern infrastructure; made Tamsui target in Sino-French War.

The Battle of Tamsui and the Goddess MazuQing military memorials; French Navy reports of Admiral Amédée Courbet; Liu Mingchuan biography; temple oral tradition

October 1884 (Sino-French War, Keelung Campaign)

During the Sino-French War, France blockaded Tamsui harbor in 1884 and attempted a landing. Qing defender Liu Mingchuan recruited Han militia and Aboriginal auxiliaries to reinforce regular troops. On 8 October 1884 French marines landed near Hobe Fort but were repulsed with heavy losses and forced to re-embark; the French remained pinned at Keelung for eight months before withdrawing. Traditional temple accounts credit divine intervention by Mazu, goddess of the sea, whose Fuyou Temple overlooks the harbor, for confusing the French or calming waters. French failure preserved Qing control until 1895; Hobe Fort (1886) built afterward to prevent repeat; Mazu legend became core local identity and annual procession.

George Leslie Mackay — hospitals, schools and the name 'Tamsui'Mackay, From Far Formosa (1895); Mackay diaries; Aletheia University archives

1872-1901

Canadian medical missionary George Leslie Mackay landed at Tamsui 9 March 1872 and made it his base for 29 years. He founded Taiwan's first Western hospitals, trained local clinicians, and established Oxford College (1882, later Aletheia University), the first European-style higher-education institution in Taiwan, plus girls' schools and over 60 churches. His 1895 book From Far Formosa popularized the Hokkien-based spelling 'Tamsui' in English, which by 1900 dominated maps. He married Minnie Tien, a Taiwanese woman, and is buried in Tamsui. Established Presbyterian Christianity in northern Taiwan; created enduring educational institutions; fixed English toponym for a century.

Silt, rail and the suburb — from port to tourist townJapanese Government-General reports 1895-1945; Taiwan Railways history; Taipei Metro construction records 1997; New Taipei tourism statistics

1895-present

After cession to Japan in 1895 following the First Sino-Japanese War, Tamsui's harbor silted rapidly; by the 1910s port operations moved to Keelung. Japanese public works made Tamsui an administrative and cultural center (Tansui-gai) with population ~6,000. Post-1945 it reverted to a fishing township of Taipei County. Expansion of Taipei and completion of the Taipei Metro Tamsui Line in 1997 transformed it: riverside parks, open-air craft markets, Fisherman's Wharf (1998-2003) and ferry piers drew millions of day-trippers. In 2010 it became Tamsui District of New Taipei City; Danhai Light Rail (2018) serves the new town. Real estate now markets Tamsui as a Taipei suburb with sea views. Economic shift from primary production to services, education and real estate; heritage preservation vs gentrification tension; population 205,706 by 2024.

Tamsui and Danshui are two different towns. — Same place, same characters 淡水. Tamsui reflects Hokkien Tām-chúi/Tām-súi (Mackay), Danshui reflects Mandarin Dànshuǐ pinyin. Official English is now Tamsui since 2011.

Fort Santo Domingo is a perfectly preserved Spanish fort from 1629. — Spanish fort was abandoned and ruined by 1638. What stands is Dutch Fort Antonio (1644) brick core with later British consular residence (1891) and modern restoration. Spanish layer is archaeological only.

Tamsui is still Taiwan's major international port. — Tamsui has been a shallow fishing/ferry harbor since early 20th century; deep-water port functions moved to Keelung. Tamsui's economy is now tourism, education and commuter housing.

The name Tamsui means 'sweet water' or 'light water' poetically. — 淡 literally 'insipid, light,淡' + 水 'water' — technical term for fresh water vs salt water (鹹水), referring to estuarine mixing zone. Not poetic 'sweet'.

George Leslie Mackay founded Tamkang University. — Mackay founded Oxford College (1882), now Aletheia University. Tamkang University (1950) is a separate institution in Tamsui with no Mackay connection beyond location.

The French won the Battle of Tamsui in 1884. — French landing force was repulsed 8 October 1884 and blockade ultimately failed; French remained pinned at Keelung and withdrew 1885. Qing victory is well-documented in both French and Qing sources, though French sources minimize it.
