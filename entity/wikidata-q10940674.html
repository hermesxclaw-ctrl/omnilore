Form

The texts do not provide a physical description. As a heavenly deity, he is implied to be radiant and noble, but specifics are absent. In later artistic renditions, he is typically depicted as a youthful courtier or warrior in ancient Japanese dress, often holding a bow, which reflects his role as a hunter.

Powers

Divine Messenger Authority As a chosen envoy of the heavenly deities, he possessed the authority and divine mandate to travel between the realms of heaven and earth. Kojiki and Nihon Shoki His authority was contingent on his obedience. Failure to fulfill his mission revoked his divine protection. None stated.

Archery He is depicted as a skilled archer, capable of shooting a pheasant with a single arrow. Kojiki and Nihon Shoki His skill is only demonstrated against a bird, not in combat against another deity. None stated.

Behavior & Habitat

Ame no Wakahiko is portrayed as a deity who is initially obedient to the heavenly mandate but is ultimately swayed by earthly temptations, specifically the affections of a beautiful earthly woman. He is characterized by a fatal combination of ambition and gullibility, leading to his downfall.

Accounts

The heavenly deities, led by Amaterasu, decide to pacify the earthly realm of Ashihara no Nakatsukuni. They dispatch Ame no Wakahiko as a messenger to subdue the unruly earthly deities. However, upon his arrival, Ame no Wakahiko forgets his divine mission. He marries Shitateru-hime, the daughter of the earthly deity Okuninushi, and does not report back to heaven for eight years. The heavenly deities, wondering at his long absence, send the pheasant Nakime to inquire about his progress. In a fit of anger or perhaps guilt, Ame no Wakahiko shoots the pheasant with his heavenly deer-arrow. The arrow, after killing the bird, flies all the way back to heaven and lands at the feet of the heavenly deities. Recognizing the arrow, the gods hurl it back down to earth, striking and killing Ame no Wakahiko. His funeral is held on earth, and his grieving wife's cries reach heaven, prompting his old friends and heavenly companions, Ame no Koyane and Ame no Futodama, to descend to earth to mourn him.

Frustrated by Ame no Wakahiko's failure to return from his mission to the earth, the heavenly deities dispatched a pheasant, Nakime, to ask why he had not come home. When Ame no Wakahiko shot down the pheasant with a single arrow, the arrow, stained with the bird's blood, fell back up into the heavens and landed before Amaterasu and Takamimusubi. Recognizing the arrow as that which they had given to Ame no Wakahiko upon his departure, the two heavenly deities pronounced a curse and returned the shaft through the arrow-hole of the heavenly rock-house. The arrow plunged down and struck Ame no Wakahiko dead upon his bed, punishing his betrayal of the divine mission.

Scholarly Debates
Reason for shooting the pheasant
He was angered by the bird's sudden appearance and startled by its call. ⇌ He was trying to silence the bird to hide his disobedience from the heavenly deities.
Evidence for A: The Kojiki simply states he shot it, without providing a clear motive.
Evidence for B: The Nihon Shoki offers a version where he is explicitly worried about the bird reporting his failure.
The identity of the one who threw the arrow back
The heavenly deities collectively threw it back. ⇌ A specific deity, such as Amaterasu or Takamimusubi, threw it back.
Evidence for A: The Kojiki says 'the heavenly deities'.
Evidence for B: The Nihon Shoki version specifies a particular deity or deities.
What We Don't Know

No dedicated academic monograph focuses solely on Ame no Wakahiko. His story is always treated as part of a larger narrative. The specific rituals or cultic practices, if any, associated with him are not documented. The visual depictions in pre-modern art are rare and not well-cataloged.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Ame no Wakahiko as a divine James Dean: a promising figure with the world at his feet, whose rebellious streak and impulsive nature lead to a spectacular, self-inflicted downfall. He is the original 'could-have-been-a-contender' of the Shinto pantheon, a cautionary tale about the cost of letting personal desires override a higher calling.
