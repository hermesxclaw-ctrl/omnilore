The Approach

In the barren wastes of Scythia, where even the wind seems to starve, there dwells a gaunt and ravenous figure whose very presence is a curse. This is Fames, the Roman personification of hunger—a deity not of abundance but of insatiable need, who can twist the mortal body into a prison of endless craving. Her story, most famously told in Ovid's Metamorphoses, is a chilling reminder of the gods' power to inflict suffering that no feast can satisfy.

Content advisory. Contains themes of extreme hunger, starvation, and divine punishment; may be distressing to those sensitive to eating disorders or famine.
Identity

Fames

Pronunciation FAH-mes (Classical Latin: /ˈfa.meːs/)

Known As

FamisLimos (Greek equivalent)

Etymology

Latin 'fames' meaning 'hunger, famine, starvation'.

Cultural Ownership

Roman; Greek equivalent Limos in Greek mythology.

Disputed Identifications

Some late antique sources conflate Fames with the Furies or other Underworld spirits, but this is not universally accepted.

Description

Appearance

Ovid describes Fames as having a gaunt face, hollow eyes, pallid lips, thin neck, and dry, scaly skin; her joints are visible, her belly is sunken, and her breasts hang loosely. She is depicted as an old woman with shaggy hair and a rough, unkempt appearance.

Symbols & Iconography

No known ancient artistic depictions; in later art, she is often shown as a thin, emaciated woman with a hungry expression, sometimes holding a mouth or a bitten arm.

Behaviour & Personality

Fames is a personification of hunger itself, embodying insatiable appetite and the physical wasting caused by starvation. She is relentless and cruel when commanded by the gods, as seen in her punishment of Erysichthon.

Domains & Habitats

Ovid places her in Scythia, a barren and desolate region, where she scrabbles for scant vegetation. She is also said to dwell at the entrance to the Underworld, among other evils.

Version Differences

In Greek mythology, the equivalent is Limos, but there is little detailed description. Some sources, like Hyginus, list Fames as a child of Erebus and Nox, while others link her to the Underworld. Later writers may conflate her with other personifications like Furies, but this is not consistent.

Powers & Abilities

Induce insatiable hunger

Source Version Ovid's Metamorphoses (Book 8)

Description

Fames can inflict an unending, insatiable hunger upon mortals, causing them to consume vast amounts of food without ever feeling full, leading to eventual self-destruction.

Limitations

She acts under the command of other gods, such as Ceres; her power appears to be limited to causing hunger, not other forms of suffering.

Contradictions

None recorded.

Stories & Myths

The Punishment of Erysichthon

Source Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.784-822Culture RomanPeriod Classical antiquity (8 CE)

Summary

Erysichthon, a Thessalian king, cut down a sacred grove of Ceres, the goddess of grain. As punishment, Ceres sent Fames to afflict him with an insatiable hunger. Despite eating enormous quantities, Erysichthon grew thinner and more desperate, eventually consuming his own flesh.

Participants

Erysichthon

Ceres

Fames

Erysichthon's daughter Mestra

Consequences

Erysichthon's relentless hunger led him to sell his daughter into slavery multiple times (she was rescued by Poseidon and given the power to change shape), and ultimately he devoured himself.

Variants

Callimachus' Hymn to Demeter (Greek) tells a similar story with Limos, but the details differ slightly; Ovid's version is the most detailed.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

No archaeological evidence of Fames worship has been found; her role is purely literary. Further research could explore later artistic depictions and comparative mythology with other cultures' hunger spirits.

Research Scope

This dossier focuses on the Roman personification Fames as depicted in classical Latin literature, particularly Ovid's Metamorphoses, and her Greek counterpart Limos. It covers primary sources, iconography, cultural context, and reception in later art and literature. Due to the scarcity of direct sources, much is inferred from the broader mythological context and scholarly commentary.
