Who It Is

Eleven lights govern fate in the Chinese sky — seven you can see, and four you cannot. The Seven Regulators are the Sun, Moon and five naked-eye planets, the visible clockwork of heaven. The Four Shadows are invisible, calculated phantoms — Purple Qi, Moon Incandescence, Rahu and Ketu — that never shine yet were believed to pull at human destiny as strongly as Mars or Saturn. Together they form Qizheng Siyu, the Eleven Luminaries, the mathematical backbone of a thousand years of East Asian horoscopy.

As astronomical bodies, behavior is strictly kinematic: each has period, retrogradation, station, and brightness variation described in calendrical treatises. As astrological agents, each has personality: Sun = sovereign, father, honor; Moon = queen, mother, changeability; Jupiter = benevolence and year-ruler; Venus = martial, executions; Mars = rebellion, drought; Saturn = stability, harvest; Mercury = intelligence, cunning. Name & Tongue

Etymology — Chinese 七政四餘 (qī zhèng sì yú). 七政 qizheng = 'seven regulators/governors' — zheng meaning to govern, regulate, or administer, implying the seven bodies that regulate calendrical and cosmic order. 四餘 siyu = 'four remainders/shadows/surpluses' — yu meaning remainder, surplus or shadow, indicating four

Pronunciation — Mandarin: qī zhèng sì yú (chi-jeng suh-yu), Cantonese: cat1 zing3 sei3 jyu4, Japanese on-reading: shichisei shiyo, Korean: chiljeong sayeo

Aliases — Qizheng Siyu, Qi Zheng Si Yu, Seven Governors and Four Remainders, Qizheng Siyu, Shichiyō Shiyō (Japanese), Chiljeong Sayeo (Korean)

Ownership — Han Chinese astronomical and astrological tradition, originating in Han dynasty classics and systematized in Tang-Song court astronomy. Integral to Chinese state cosmology, Daoist

Powers
Calendrical Regulation

The seven visible bodies regulate time: Sun governs year and day, Moon governs month, five planets govern longer cycles and intercalation. Their conjunctions and stations were used to fix calendar, predict seasons, and legitimize dynasty's Mandate of Heaven.

Limit: Limited to naked-eye observation; Mercury often invisible, calculations required. Pre-Tang methods had errors of several degrees.

Horoscopic Determination (Mingyun)

At birth moment, the positions of all eleven luminaries in twelve palaces and 28 mansions determine lifetime fortune: rank, wealth, marriage, children, lifespan. Each luminary acts as benefic or malefic depending on palace, brightness, retrogradation, and aspects to other luminaries.

Limit: Requires precise birth time (to double-hour) and accurate ephemeris; disputed by Confucian rationalists as fatalistic. Different schools give contradictory interpretations for same chart.

Eclipse and Calamity Causation (Rahu/Ketu)

Luohou (Rahu) and Jidu (Ketu), as lunar nodes, are said to devour Sun and Moon causing eclipses, and by extension to devour fortune, causing sudden downfall, exile, or illness when afflicting natal Sun/Moon or transiting.

Limit: Only effective at nodes; outside eclipse season they are 'dormant'. Some Ming manuals downgrade their power relative to Ziqi/Yuebei.

Invisibility and Shadow Influence (Ziqi and Yuebei)

Ziqi (Purple Qi) and Yuebei (Moon Incandescence) exert influence without being seen, representing hidden virtue, solitary talent, or sudden brilliance. Ziqi associated with reclusion and Daoist transcendence; Yuebei with literary fame that burns bright and fades.

Limit: Slow-moving (Ziqi) or fast-fluctuating (Yuebei); influence considered subtle, only strong when conjunct Sun/Moon or ascendant. No observational check possible.

Ritual Aversion and Talismanic Protection

Daoist priests could petition, pacify or 'cover' malefic luminaries through jiao offerings, talismans (fu), and lamp rituals (eleven lamps), redirecting or mitigating their influence. Each luminary has specific incantation, direction, and offering.

Limit: Requires ordained priest, proper date, and imperial permission for state rituals; efficacy not verifiable. Confucian officials often opposed as heterodoxy.

Accounts I
The Shun Emperor Aligns the Seven RegulatorsShujing (Book of Documents), 'Canon of Shun' and Shiji 1

Textualized Warring States to Western Han (c. 300-90 BCE), claiming high antiquity (legendary Shun, c. 23rd century BCE)

The sage-emperor Shun, succeeding Yao, uses the xuanji yuheng (jade armillary) to 'align the Seven Regulators' (qi qizheng) and thereby harmonize heaven and earth, test officials, and establish correct calendar. This is the locus classicus for the term qizheng and establishes the political theology: ruler who regulates the seven regulates the realm. Establishes Qizheng as imperial prerogative; all later dynasties claim to observe and regulate the seven. Provides no mention of four shadows, proving later addition.

Gautama Siddha Imports the Nine and Creates the ElevenKaiyuan Zhanjing, preface and juan 104; Jiu Tang Shu, Tianwen Zhi

Tang Kaiyuan era, 729 CE

Court astronomer Gautama Siddha, of Indian Brahmin descent, compiles Kaiyuan Zhanjing at imperial order, synthesizing Chinese observations with Indian Navagraha (nine luminaries) and Yavanajataka methods. To reconcile Indian Rahu/Ketu with Chinese Qizheng, his team formalizes Four Shadows (Siyu): retaining Rahu/Ketu as Luohou/Jidu and adding two native calculated points Ziqi and Yuebei to make eleven, providing calculation algorithms for all. Creates the canonical Qizheng Siyu system that dominates East Asian horoscopy for next millennium. Introduces the concept of invisible calculated planets to Chinese thought.

Song Court Debate: Are the Four Shadows Real Stars?Song Shi, Tianwen Zhi; Zhu Xi, Zhuzi Yulei; Song Huiyao Jigao

Northern to Southern Song, 11th-13th century

With Qizheng Siyu horoscopy popular among officials, court astronomers and Neo-Confucian philosophers debate ontology: Shen Kuo and astronomers defend Siyu as useful mathematical points like epicycles; Zhu Xi and Cheng Yi attack them as 'empty calculations without form or qi' (wuxing wuqi), arguing only visible seven have real influence. Emperor Huizong's Daoist-leaning court favors ritual use, while reformers seek to purge Siyu from official ephemerides. Debate never resolved; official calendars continue to compute Siyu for horoscopy while philosophical texts dismiss them. Illustrates tension between mathematical astrology and lixue rationalism.

Accounts II
The Xingxue Dacheng Codifies a Thousand ChartsXingxue Dacheng, preface by Wan Minying; Ming Shilu

Ming Wanli era, c. 1590-1610

During late Ming commercial printing boom, scholar Wan Minying compiles Xingxue Dacheng, gathering Tang-Song-Yuan Qizheng Siyu methods into 10 juan with tables for computing all eleven luminaries, twelve palaces, and over a hundred example horoscopes of historical figures (from Confucius to contemporary officials) to prove efficacy. The work becomes the bible of Qizheng Siyu practitioners and is banned and reprinted repeatedly. Standardizes horoscopic practice; transmits system to Qing and to modern folk astrology. Provides richest source for modern reconstruction, though many example charts are retrospective fabrications to fit known biography.

Kukai Brings the Eleven Luminaries to JapanSukuyō Kyō (Xiuyao Jing, translated by Amoghavajra), Kukai's catalogue, Sukuyōdō manuals

Early Heian, 806-835 CE, with later medieval development

Monk Kukai (Kōbō Daishi), returning from Tang China, imports Xiuyao Jing (Sukuyō Kyō) and Kaiyuan Zhanjing excerpts, establishing Sukuyōdō (Way of Constellations) in Shingon Buddhism. The eleven luminaries are taught as shichiyō shiyō, with Rahu/Ketu as eclipse demons to be pacified, and integrated with Onmyōdō and later samurai birth horoscopy. Japanese court uses them to select auspicious days and diagnose illness. Transplants Qizheng Siyu to Japan where it survives as Sukuyō astrology, influencing Japanese calendar, festivals, and later manga/anime depictions of planetary deities. Japanese calculation methods diverge, creating distinct ephemerides.

The Daoist Jiao to Cover the LuminariesDaozang, DZ 1221 Daofan Yigui and ritual memorials (biao) for Qizheng Siyu

Song to Ming, 12th-15th century

In times of eclipse, planetary retrogradation, or personal crisis indicated by horoscope, Daoist priests perform a jiao retreat, lighting eleven lamps in a starry canopy, reciting each luminary's secret name and presenting a memorial to the Jade Emperor to 'cover' (zhe) or 'conceal' the malefic influence. The Four Shadows, being most malefic, require extra offerings of purple silk (for Ziqi) and incense. Transforms astronomical calculation into liturgy; temples preserve talismans for each luminary still sold today in Taiwan and Hong Kong. Shows living ritual afterlife of system.

The Seven Regulators are the seven stars of the Big Dipper (Beidou). — Qizheng refers to Sun, Moon and five planets, not Beidou. Confusion arises from Kong Anguo's early commentary and modern popular conflation of all 'seven star' groups. Beidou is a separate asterism (Ursa Major) with its own deities.

The Four Shadows are the same as Western astrology's hypothetical planets or the lunar nodes as used in modern Western astrology. — Only two (Luohou/Jidu) correspond to lunar nodes; Ziqi and Yuebei have no Western equivalent and are not the 'Black Moon Lilith' or 'Part of Fortune'. Western nodes are interpreted differently and Ziqi/Yuebei are unique Chinese cycles.

Qizheng Siyu is the same as Ziwei Doushu (Purple Star Astrology). — Distinct systems: Qizheng Siyu is based on actual planetary positions calculated astronomically; Ziwei Doushu is a numerological system where 'stars' are placed by formula unrelated to real sky. They share palace names but have different stars, methods and histories. Qizheng Siyu is earlier and more astronomical.

The eleven luminaries are ancient, dating to the time of the Yellow Emperor. — Seven regulators have Han or earlier roots, but the eleven as a set is medieval (Tang, 8th century). The four shadows are not attested before Tang and were added via Indian transmission. Claims of 4000-year antiquity are retrospective legitimization in Ming manuals.

Rahu and Ketu in Qizheng Siyu are the same as the Hindu mythological demons who drink amrita. — Chinese Luohou/Jidu retain the names and eclipse association but are redefined as mathematical nodes with precise periods (18.6 years retrograde), stripped of Puranic narrative. Daoist ritual re-mythologizes them differently as bureaucratic devourers, not amrita-thieves.
