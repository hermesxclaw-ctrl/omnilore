Who They Are

Epona is the Celtic goddess of horses, mules, donkeys, and the stables — a uniquely widespread deity whose cult was adopted by the Roman army across the empire. She is the only Celtic deity to have been officially incorporated into the Roman imperial cult with her own festival (December 18). Epona is depicted as a woman seated sidesaddle on a horse or mare, often holding a cornucopia (abundance), a patera (offering dish), or fruit. She is sometimes accompanied by a foal. Her name derives from the Gaulish word for horse (epos). Epona's cult was particularly strong in Gaul (France), Britain, and along the Rhine frontier. She was worshipped by cavalry units, stable hands, and farmers, but also as a goddess of fertility and the afterlife — inscriptions invoke her as a protector of the dead, and funeral monuments often feature her image. In some depictions, she carries keys (guardian of the underworld) or stands before a horse nursing a foal (maternal protection). Epona represents the profound relationship between humans and horses in Celtic society — a bond that was economic, military, and spiritual. In modern Neopaganism, she is honored as a goddess of animals and the natural world.

Form

Attested iconography presents Epona as a human woman closely associated with horses, usually mares and foals. In reliefs and statuettes from Gaul and the Germanies, she is frequently shown seated sidesaddle on a horse, a posture that emphasizes both control and intimacy rather than martial aggression. In other images she sits on a throne or bench, flanked by horses or with a foal on her lap, evoking a maternal and protective presence. Her clothing follows Graeco-Roman conventions: long flowing garments, sometimes a mantle, and occasionally a veil, rather than any clearly distinguishable "Celtic" costume. This reflects the Roman provincial artistic repertoire through which local deities were visualized. Her facial features are generally idealized and generic, not individualized, suggesting that the focus of the image is her role and attributes rather than a portrait-like identity. The horses around her are often rendered with care, emphasizing their musculature and attentive posture, reinforcing her status as their guardian. All of these details are attested in surviving sculptures and reliefs; no textual description of her appearance survives from antiquity, so our knowledge is entirely iconographic.

Powers

Protection of horses and stables Epona’s most securely attested power is the protection of horses, ponies, mules, and donkeys, along with the stables and people who depend on them. Inscriptions dedicated by cavalry units and stable workers, as well as literary references by Juvenal and Tertullian, situate her cult directly within equestrian spaces. Reliefs showing her surrounded by horses and foals visually encode this protective function. Worshippers appear to have sought her favor to ensure the health, safety, and reliability of their animals, especially in the demanding conditions of military service and long-distance transport. Attested in Gallo-Roman inscriptions, Roman literary references, and archaeological iconography from the 1st–3rd centuries CE. No text specifies formal limits to her protective power, but the distribution of dedications suggests that her protection was primarily sought by those directly involved with horses and cavalry rather than by the broader populace. Her cult seems to have been largely private and localized, even when present in Rome, which may imply that her protection was conceived as focused on specific communities rather than universal. There is no direct contradiction about her protective role; however, some scholars debate whether she should be classified primarily as a horse goddess or as a more general fertility deity whose equine aspect is one specialization among others. This affects how her protective function is framed but does not negate its existence.

Fertility and abundance Epona is frequently depicted with a cornucopia, baskets of fruit, bread, and ears of grain. These symbols align her with fertility, abundance, and the provisioning of both animals and humans. Some scholars interpret her as a mother goddess whose domain includes the fecundity of mares and the prosperity of rural households. The presence of foals on her lap or at her side reinforces the theme of reproductive success and continuity of herds. Attested in iconography from Gaul, the Germanies, and the Danubian frontier; inferred from the combination of equine and agricultural symbols. No ancient text explicitly states that Epona governs human fertility or agricultural cycles in the way that deities like Ceres do. Her fertility aspect is therefore primarily reconstructed from visual symbols and the broader Graeco-Roman repertoire of cornucopia-bearing goddesses. It may have been more focused on animal fertility and the economic abundance derived from horses than on human reproduction. Some scholars caution against overextending her fertility role, arguing that the cornucopia and food offerings may simply reflect a general iconographic language of divine favor rather than a specific agricultural function. Others, especially in modern spiritual literature, emphasize her as a mother goddess, sometimes beyond what the evidence supports. The contradiction lies in the degree of emphasis, not in the existence of the theme.

Patroness of cavalry and equestrian units Epona’s cult is strongly associated with auxiliary cavalry units and the equites singulares Augusti, the imperial horse guards. Dedications from frontier regions and from Rome itself show soldiers invoking her protection before campaigns and honoring her in collective rituals. This patronage suggests that she was believed to safeguard riders in battle, ensure the readiness of horses, and perhaps influence the success of military operations involving cavalry. Attested in inscriptions catalogued by Henri de Mégille and in the collective dedications of the equites singulares Augusti discussed by Gregori et al. (2021). Her patronage appears to be focused on cavalry rather than on the entire army. There is no evidence that infantry units or naval forces honored her in the same way. Moreover, her cult seems to have been time-limited in Rome, with dedications concentrated in a relatively short period (c. 132–141 CE), suggesting that her prominence as a military patroness could fluctuate with changing institutional preferences. Some scholars emphasize the military dimension of her cult, while others stress its rural and private character, arguing that Epona’s worship pre-existed Roman conquest and was only later adopted by soldiers. The tension lies in whether her identity is primarily military or primarily rustic, with different scholars weighting the evidence differently.

Psychopompic escort of souls A number of modern scholars have proposed that Epona may have functioned as a psychopomp, escorting souls to the afterlife, particularly those of riders and horse-owners. This interpretation draws on the broader Indo-European symbolism of horses as vehicles between worlds and on parallels with figures like Rhiannon, who is associated with the Otherworld in Welsh literature. Some iconographic arrangements, in which Epona is enthroned and surrounded by horses, have been read as suggesting a sovereign of a liminal realm. Speculative reconstruction based on comparative mythology and thematic analysis of horse symbolism; occasionally mentioned in modern scholarship and popular summaries. There is no explicit ancient text stating that Epona guides souls to the afterlife. The psychopompic role is therefore hypothetical and must be treated as an interpretive model rather than an attested function. It may reflect a modern desire to integrate Epona into a broader Indo-European pattern of horse-associated Otherworld figures rather than a historically documented belief. Some scholars accept the possibility of a psychopompic dimension, while others reject it as overinterpretation. The absence of direct evidence makes consensus impossible. The contradiction is between a cautious, evidence-based view that limits Epona’s role to what is attested (horses, stables, fertility, cavalry) and a more expansive comparative approach that situates her in a larger mythic system.

Guardian of everyday equestrian labor Beyond the military sphere, Epona appears to have been invoked by those whose daily work involved horses: stable hands, carters, rural landowners, and perhaps urban transport workers. The small, portable reliefs and statuettes found in domestic and stable contexts suggest that she was believed to watch over the routine tasks of feeding, grooming, and harnessing horses, as well as the risks of travel and transport. Attested in the archaeological distribution of Epona images in non-military contexts and in literary references to her presence in stables. The specific rituals and prayers used by non-military worshippers are unknown; no liturgical texts survive. Her guardianship must therefore be inferred from the placement of images and dedications rather than from explicit descriptions of her powers. There is no direct contradiction about this role, but some scholars focus more on her military patronage, potentially underplaying her importance in civilian equestrian life. The balance between these spheres remains a matter of interpretation.

Accounts

In one of his satires, Juvenal mocks provincial religious practices and the decoration of stables, mentioning Epona as a goddess whose image is painted on stable walls. The passage does not narrate a myth about Epona herself but uses her cult as a satirical example of the perceived absurdity of certain forms of worship. The story is thus more about Roman attitudes toward provincial religion than about Epona’s own deeds. Nevertheless, it provides a vivid glimpse of how her presence was imagined in everyday spaces: her image watching over horses, integrated into the visual environment of labor and care.

In Apuleius’ novel, the protagonist Lucius, transformed into a donkey, encounters various religious and magical practices. In a scene set in northern Greece, he observes stables adorned with images of Epona. The narrative uses her presence to evoke the atmosphere of the stable and to signal the cultural reach of her cult beyond Gaul. As in Juvenal, Epona does not act as a character; she appears as a visual motif within the story world, a goddess whose image is familiar enough to be mentioned without explanation.

The fragmentary Guidizzolo calendar from Mantua records a feast of Epona on December 18. Although the inscription does not narrate a story, it implies a recurring ritual event in which worshippers honored Epona on a specific date. One can infer that on this day, offerings were made, perhaps in stables or at altars dedicated to her, and that cavalry units and horse-owners participated in ceremonies seeking her favor. The calendar entry thus encodes a cyclical narrative of devotion: each year, Epona’s feast punctuated the religious life of those who depended on horses.

In the first branch of the Mabinogion, the Welsh figure Rhiannon rides a mysterious horse and appears to Pwyll, lord of Dyfed and "Head of Annwn" (the Otherworld). Modern scholars have noted parallels between Rhiannon and Epona: both are female figures associated with horses, both have connections to sovereignty and the Otherworld, and both occupy liminal spaces between worlds. Some comparative studies suggest that Epona and Rhiannon may reflect a shared underlying Celtic horse-goddess archetype. However, there is no ancient text that directly links them, and the Mabinogion is a medieval Welsh compilation, not a Gallo-Roman source.

Content Notes

Although no specific sacrificial ritual to Epona is described in surviving texts, it is highly likely that her cult, like many ancient religious practices, involved offerings that could include the slaughter of animals. Given her association with horses, one must consider the possibility that horses or other livestock were sacrificed in her honor, or at least that their labor and lives were ritually framed as offerings. At the same time, Epona is portrayed as a protector of horses, creating a tension between the economic exploitation of animals and their sacralization. The Roman military’s heavy use of horses in campaigns, often leading to injury and death, further complicates the picture: Epona’s protection may have been invoked in contexts where horses were simultaneously cherished and expendable.

Scholars of ancient religion note that animal sacrifice was a pervasive practice, and it would be unusual for a deity so closely tied to animals to be entirely detached from such rituals. However, the lack of direct textual evidence for horse sacrifice to Epona requires caution. Some researchers emphasize the protective and nurturing aspects of her cult, while others highlight the economic realities of animal use in the Roman Empire. The dark dimension lies in the structural exploitation of animals within a system that also sacralizes them.

Inferred from general patterns of Roman and Celtic religious practice; epigraphic and iconographic evidence of offerings to Epona; broader scholarship on animal sacrifice in antiquity.

Epona’s close relationship with cavalry units and the equites singulares Augusti situates her cult within the machinery of Roman imperial expansion. The soldiers who honored her participated in campaigns that involved conquest, suppression of revolts, and the enforcement of imperial rule. While Epona herself is not depicted as a war goddess in the sense of actively inciting violence, her protection of cavalry indirectly supports military operations that could be brutal and coercive. The spread of her cult along the frontiers reflects the movement of soldiers and the establishment of Roman power in regions where local populations experienced dispossession and cultural transformation.

Historians of the Roman Empire emphasize the role of auxiliary troops in maintaining control over conquered territories. The religious life of these soldiers, including devotion to Epona, is part of the broader system of imperial ideology and practice. Some scholars focus on the cultural hybridity of provincial religion, seeing Epona’s cult as a site of negotiation between local and Roman identities. Others foreground the violence inherent in imperial structures, arguing that any deity embedded in military life participates, however indirectly, in that dark history.

Epigraphic evidence of Epona’s cult among cavalry units; historical studies of Roman frontier warfare and imperial policy.

Christian authors such as Tertullian use Epona’s cult as a target in their critiques of pagan religion, mocking the painting of her image in stables and framing her worship as foolish or idolatrous. This polemical discourse is part of a larger conflict in which Christian communities sought to delegitimize traditional cults and, eventually, to suppress them. The transition from pagan to Christian dominance involved legal measures, social pressure, and sometimes violent destruction of shrines and images. Epona’s cult, like many others, would have been affected by these processes, though specific acts of persecution or destruction directed at her shrines are not documented.

Studies of late antiquity highlight the complex dynamics of religious change, including the use of polemic, legislation, and social coercion to reshape religious landscapes. Epona’s presence in Christian texts is relatively minor compared to major deities, but it illustrates how even localized cults became objects of critique. The dark aspect here lies in the broader pattern of religious intolerance and the erasure or transformation of older traditions.

Tertullian’s references to Epona; general scholarship on Christianization and the suppression of pagan cults.

Etymology of the suffix -ona/-ana
The suffix -on- in Epona’s name is an augmentative element, combined with the feminine ending -a, yielding the meaning "Great Mare" or "Divine Mare." This interpretation aligns with other Celtic theonyms such as Sirona and Matrona, where -on- appears to magnify or honor the base word. ⇌ The suffix -ona/-ana may be linked to water or springs, functioning as a hydronymic marker. In this view, Epona’s name could imply a connection to sacred waters, suggesting that her cult may have originated at springs or other water sources.

What We Don't Know

Several significant gaps remain in the dossier on Epona. First, no complete corpus of inscriptions and reliefs has been consulted directly for this research; instead, the analysis relies on secondary syntheses by Linduff, de Mégille, Gregori, and others. Precise texts, object numbers, and find contexts for many monuments (Naix-en-Forges, Kapersburg, frontier reliefs) require direct access to epigraphic corpora (e.g., CIL) and museum catalogues. Second, the literary references to Epona in Juvenal, Apuleius, and Tertullian have not been checked against critical editions, leaving line numbers and exact phrasing uncertain. Third, there is no surviving mythological narrative about Epona comparable to those of Greek or Roman deities; her personality and possible psychopompic role must therefore be reconstructed cautiously from iconography and comparative models. Fourth, the pre-Roman phase of her cult in independent Gaul is poorly documented archaeologically, making it difficult to determine whether she had aniconic or natural sanctuaries (such as springs or groves) before Roman conquest. Fifth, the social composition of her worshippers—gender, status, ethnic background—remains largely unknown beyond the broad categories of cavalry units and equestrian workers. Sixth, modern neopagan and revivalist uses of Epona have not been systematically surveyed; this dossier notes their existence but does not map their diversity or internal debates. Finally, the dark history dimensions (animal sacrifice, military violence, religious conflict) are inferred from general patterns rather than from specific documented rituals or events involving Epona, and thus remain partly speculative. These gaps mark areas where further archival, archaeological, and philological research would deepen and refine the picture.

A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine walking into a Roman frontier stable at dusk: the air thick with the smell of hay and sweat, horses shifting in their stalls, iron tack clinking softly. Above the doorway, carved into a small stone relief, a woman sits sidesaddle on a mare, a basket of fruit in her lap and foals pressing close to her knees. Soldiers heading out on patrol touch the image as they pass, murmuring a brief prayer. For them, this is not an anonymous decoration but a presence—Epona, the Great Mare, whose favor might mean the difference between a safe return and a broken horse on a muddy road. She is at once intimate and distant, a goddess whose world is the everyday labor of horses yet whose name travels from Gallic villages to the stables of Rome itself.
