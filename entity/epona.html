Who It Is

Imagine walking into a Roman frontier stable at dusk: the air thick with the smell of hay and sweat, horses shifting in their stalls, iron tack clinking softly. Above the doorway, carved into a small stone relief, a woman sits sidesaddle on a mare, a basket of fruit in her lap and foals pressing close to her knees. Soldiers heading out on patrol touch the image as they pass, murmuring a brief prayer. For them, this is not an anonymous decoration but a presence—Epona, the Great Mare, whose favor might mean the difference between a safe return and a broken horse on a muddy road. She is at once intimate and distant, a goddess whose world is the everyday labor of horses yet whose name travels from Gallic villages to the stables of Rome itself.

No ancient mythological narrative describes Epona’s personality in the way Greek or Roman texts characterize Olympian gods. Instead, her "behavior" must be inferred from the functions encoded in inscriptions and iconography. Dedications by cavalry units and stable workers present her as a protector and patron, someone who safeguards horses, riders, and the success of equestrian endeavors. Powers
Protection of horses and stables

Epona’s most securely attested power is the protection of horses, ponies, mules, and donkeys, along with the stables and people who depend on them. Inscriptions dedicated by cavalry units and stable workers, as well as literary references by Juvenal and Tertullian, situate her cult directly within equestrian spaces. Reliefs showing her surrounded by horses and foals visually encode this protective function. Worshippers appear to have sought her favor to ensure the health, safety, and reliability of their animals, especially in the demanding conditions of military service and long-distance transport.

Limit: No text specifies formal limits to her protective power, but the distribution of dedications suggests that her protection was primarily sought by those directly involved with horses and cavalry rather than by the broader populace. Her cult seems to have been largely private and localized, even when present in Rome, which may imply that her protection was conceived as focused on specific communities rather than universal.

Fertility and abundance

Epona is frequently depicted with a cornucopia, baskets of fruit, bread, and ears of grain. These symbols align her with fertility, abundance, and the provisioning of both animals and humans. Some scholars interpret her as a mother goddess whose domain includes the fecundity of mares and the prosperity of rural households. The presence of foals on her lap or at her side reinforces the theme of reproductive success and continuity of herds.

Limit: No ancient text explicitly states that Epona governs human fertility or agricultural cycles in the way that deities like Ceres do. Her fertility aspect is therefore primarily reconstructed from visual symbols and the broader Graeco-Roman repertoire of cornucopia-bearing goddesses. It may have been more focused on animal fertility and the economic abundance derived from horses than on human reproduction.

Patroness of cavalry and equestrian units

Epona’s cult is strongly associated with auxiliary cavalry units and the equites singulares Augusti, the imperial horse guards. Dedications from frontier regions and from Rome itself show soldiers invoking her protection before campaigns and honoring her in collective rituals. This patronage suggests that she was believed to safeguard riders in battle, ensure the readiness of horses, and perhaps influence the success of military operations involving cavalry.

Limit: Her patronage appears to be focused on cavalry rather than on the entire army. There is no evidence that infantry units or naval forces honored her in the same way. Moreover, her cult seems to have been time-limited in Rome, with dedications concentrated in a relatively short period (c. 132–141 CE), suggesting that her prominence as a military patroness could fluctuate with changing institutional preferences.

Psychopompic escort of souls

A number of modern scholars have proposed that Epona may have functioned as a psychopomp, escorting souls to the afterlife, particularly those of riders and horse-owners. This interpretation draws on the broader Indo-European symbolism of horses as vehicles between worlds and on parallels with figures like Rhiannon, who is associated with the Otherworld in Welsh literature. Some iconographic arrangements, in which Epona is enthroned and surrounded by horses, have been read as suggesting a sovereign of a liminal realm.

Limit: There is no explicit ancient text stating that Epona guides souls to the afterlife. The psychopompic role is therefore hypothetical and must be treated as an interpretive model rather than an attested function. It may reflect a modern desire to integrate Epona into a broader Indo-European pattern of horse-associated Otherworld figures rather than a historically documented belief.

Guardian of everyday equestrian labor

Beyond the military sphere, Epona appears to have been invoked by those whose daily work involved horses: stable hands, carters, rural landowners, and perhaps urban transport workers. The small, portable reliefs and statuettes found in domestic and stable contexts suggest that she was believed to watch over the routine tasks of feeding, grooming, and harnessing horses, as well as the risks of travel and transport.

Limit: The specific rituals and prayers used by non-military worshippers are unknown; no liturgical texts survive. Her guardianship must therefore be inferred from the placement of images and dedications rather than from explicit descriptions of her powers.

Accounts I
Epona in the stables (Juvenal’s satire)Juvenal, Satires

Early 2nd century CE

In one of his satires, Juvenal mocks provincial religious practices and the decoration of stables, mentioning Epona as a goddess whose image is painted on stable walls. The passage does not narrate a myth about Epona herself but uses her cult as a satirical example of the perceived absurdity of certain forms of worship. The story is thus more about Roman attitudes toward provincial religion than about Epona’s own deeds. Nevertheless, it provides a vivid glimpse of how her presence was imagined in everyday spaces: her image watching over horses, integrated into the visual environment of labor and care. The satire contributes to the literary record of Epona’s cult, shaping later perceptions of her as a goddess of stables and horses. It also illustrates the tension between Roman elite attitudes and provincial religious practices, with Epona’s worship becoming a target of ridicule. For the goddess herself, the story does not record any action or decision; it is a human commentary on her cult.

Epona in Apuleius’ MetamorphosesApuleius, Metamorphoses (The Golden Ass)

2nd century CE

In Apuleius’ novel, the protagonist Lucius, transformed into a donkey, encounters various religious and magical practices. In a scene set in northern Greece, he observes stables adorned with images of Epona. The narrative uses her presence to evoke the atmosphere of the stable and to signal the cultural reach of her cult beyond Gaul. As in Juvenal, Epona does not act as a character; she appears as a visual motif within the story world, a goddess whose image is familiar enough to be mentioned without explanation. The mention of Epona in a Greek setting underscores the spread of her cult and the integration of Celtic deities into the broader Roman cultural sphere. For readers, it reinforces the association between Epona and stables, contributing to her enduring image as a horse goddess. The story does not attribute any specific miracle or intervention to her; she remains a background presence.

Accounts II
Epona’s feast in the Guidizzolo calendarGuidizzolo calendar inscription

Late Roman Imperial period

The fragmentary Guidizzolo calendar from Mantua records a feast of Epona on December 18. Although the inscription does not narrate a story, it implies a recurring ritual event in which worshippers honored Epona on a specific date. One can infer that on this day, offerings were made, perhaps in stables or at altars dedicated to her, and that cavalry units and horse-owners participated in ceremonies seeking her favor. The calendar entry thus encodes a cyclical narrative of devotion: each year, Epona’s feast punctuated the religious life of those who depended on horses. The calendar evidence elevates Epona from a purely provincial goddess to one with recognized status in Italy, at least in certain communities. It shows that her cult had a temporal structure and that she was integrated into the rhythm of religious observance. For the goddess, the feast day represents a focal point of attention and offerings, though no specific mythic event is attached to the date.

Comparative echo: Epona and RhiannonModern comparative scholarship and the Mabinogion (Pwyll Pendefig Dyfed)

Medieval narrative (Mabinogion), modern scholarship (20th–21st centuries)

In the first branch of the Mabinogion, the Welsh figure Rhiannon rides a mysterious horse and appears to Pwyll, lord of Dyfed and "Head of Annwn" (the Otherworld). Modern scholars have noted parallels between Rhiannon and Epona: both are female figures associated with horses, both have connections to sovereignty and the Otherworld, and both occupy liminal spaces between worlds. Some comparative studies suggest that Epona and Rhiannon may reflect a shared underlying Celtic horse-goddess archetype. However, there is no ancient text that directly links them, and the Mabinogion is a medieval Welsh compilation, not a Gallo-Roman source. The comparative narrative does not describe an event in Epona’s own cult but shapes modern understanding of her by situating her within a broader Celtic mythological pattern. It encourages readers to see Epona not only as a practical patroness of horses but also as a potential figure of sovereignty and Otherworld mediation.
