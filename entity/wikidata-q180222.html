Form

In Greek art, Amphitrite is typically depicted as a mature, regal woman, often enthroned beside Poseidon, wearing a crown or diadem (often of crab claws or seaweed), and holding a scepter, trident, or a dolphin. She is sometimes shown with a net or an 'amphitrite' (a type of sea-urchin shell) as an attribute. Unlike her husband's often wild, bearded vigor, she is portrayed with serene, composed features, embodying the calm, majestic power of the sea itself. In later Roman art, she appears as Salacia, often with a ship's rudder or a dolphin.

Powers

Sovereignty over the Sea As the wife of Poseidon and a Nereid herself, she commands the respect and obedience of sea-creatures, nymphs, and the waves. Her presence can calm or stir the waters. All Greek sources Her power is typically exercised in conjunction with or subordinate to Poseidon's; she is rarely shown acting independently to create storms or earthquakes. None explicitly stated, but her power is more implied than demonstrated in surviving myths.

Shapeshifting In one late myth (often conflated with Thetis' story), Amphitrite is said to have fled Poseidon's advances and hidden, possibly shapeshifting into a crab or other sea-creature to avoid him. Late antique mythographers (e.g., Hyginus, Fabulae) This is a rare, late-attested ability, likely borrowed from the more famous tale of Thetis. It is not a core part of her character in the Homeric or Hesiodic tradition. The myth of her flight is not in the earliest sources and may be a later invention.

Marine Healing and Blessing As a Nereid, she shares the Nereids' association with the beneficial aspects of the sea, including aiding sailors and providing food. She is invoked for safe voyages. Homeric Hymn to Poseidon; various cultic inscriptions This is an inferred function from her nature as a sea-goddess and her cultic presence, not a specific miracle-working power. None.

Behavior & Habitat

In myth, she is primarily a consort and a mother, rarely taking an active role in the major heroic narratives. She is depicted as a figure of immense, quiet authority, capable of anger (as in the myth of the Nereids' jealousy) but also of benevolence, granting safe passage to sailors and heroes who honored her. She is the archetypal 'queen consort' of a primordial realm—essential to the cosmic order but often operating in the background, her power being the sea itself rather than a specific action.

Accounts

Poseidon, the god of the sea, sees Amphitrite, a Nereid, dancing with her sisters on the island of Naxos and desires her. In the earliest versions, she is simply his bride. In a later, more elaborate version (likely Hellenistic), she flees his advances, and Poseidon sends the dolphin (or a man named Delphinus) to find her and persuade her to return. The dolphin succeeds, and Poseidon, in gratitude, places the dolphin among the stars as the constellation Delphinus. The wedding is a grand cosmic event, depicted on many vases, with Amphitrite enthroned beside her husband.

In a lesser-known myth, Amphitrite, jealous of the Nereid Scylla, who was beloved by Poseidon (or a rival), threw magical herbs into Scylla's bathing pool, transforming her into a monstrous, man-eating creature with six heads and twelve feet. This is a darker, more jealous aspect of the sea-queen, turning her from a passive consort into an active, vengeful force.

Scholarly Debates
Role as an Independent Goddess
She is a major, independent goddess with her own cult and power. ⇌ She is a minor figure, mostly a literary and artistic consort of Poseidon, with little independent cultic significance.
Evidence for A: Pausanias mentions her statue at a major sanctuary; she is invoked in hymns; her name appears in some inscriptions.
Evidence for B: She has no major temples of her own, no major festivals, and is rarely the subject of independent myths.
The Scylla Myth
Amphitrite was the one who transformed Scylla out of jealousy. ⇌ This is a late, marginal variant; Circe or Poseidon are more commonly credited.
Evidence for A: Late scholia and mythographers (e.g., on Homer) mention Amphitrite's role.
Evidence for B: Homer himself does not name the culprit; later poets like Ovid name Circe.
Her 'Flight' from Poseidon
She actively fled Poseidon's advances and had to be persuaded to return. ⇌ This is a late, romanticized addition, not part of the archaic myth.
Evidence for A: Hyginus (Latin) tells the story of the dolphin's mission.
Evidence for B: Hesiod and Homer present the marriage as a fait accompli, with no hint of flight.
What We Don't Know

The primary gap is the lack of a dedicated cult or major sanctuary to Amphitrite, which makes it difficult to assess her religious significance beyond her literary and artistic role. The later myth of her flight and the Scylla episode are poorly attested and may be the result of later literary invention. Her specific attributes and powers beyond her general sovereignty over the sea are not well-defined in the surviving sources.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Amphitrite as the CEO of the ocean. Poseidon is the charismatic, volatile founder who makes headlines with earthquakes and storms. Amphitrite is the one who actually runs the day-to-day operations—the tides, the currents, the wellbeing of the marine life, the safe passage of ships. She is the steady, unflappable force that keeps the whole enterprise running, and while she rarely speaks in public, everyone knows that crossing her is a terrible idea. She is the power behind the throne, the quiet storm, the deep, calm, and utterly essential heart of the sea.
