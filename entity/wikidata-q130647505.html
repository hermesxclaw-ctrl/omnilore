Form

As a city personification on Roman coins, Arelate is depicted as a female bust. She typically wears a mural crown (a crown shaped like city walls with towers), which is the standard iconographic attribute for city deities and personifications (like Tyche of Antioch or Dea Roma). Her features are matronly and dignified, reflecting the city's status and prosperity. On some issues, she may be shown with other attributes like a cornucopia (symbolizing abundance) or a patera (offering dish).

Powers

Civic Prosperity As the divine embodiment of the city, Arelate was believed to ensure the city's wealth, trade, and agricultural abundance. The city's strategic position on the Rhône made it a vital trading hub, and the deity's favor was sought for continued prosperity. Roman Imperial cult Her power was tied to the city's fortune; if the city suffered, the deity's power was seen as diminished. None known.

Imperial Favor Arelate was closely associated with the Roman Emperor, particularly from the reign of Constantine, who resided in the city. The deity's cult reinforced the city's loyalty and its special relationship with the imperial house. Roman Imperial cult, especially Constantinian This power was political and symbolic, not mythic. None known.

Guardianship Like all city genii, Arelate's primary function was to protect the city, its citizens, and its institutions. This was a protective, tutelary role, ensuring the city's survival against enemies and natural disasters. Roman religious belief This guardianship was invoked through ritual and sacrifice; it was not a proactive power. None known.

Accounts

Arelate was founded as a Roman colony for veterans of the Legio VI Ferrata and possibly other legions, following Julius Caesar's campaigns in Gaul. The city was established on the site of an earlier Greek or Gaulish settlement. It was a deliberate act of Romanization, creating a bastion of Roman culture and loyalty in southern Gaul.

Arelate's rise was partly at the expense of Massalia (Marseille), a Greek city that had sided with Pompey against Caesar. After Caesar's victory, Massalia was punished, and Arelate, which had supported Caesar, was favored. This led to a transfer of commercial and political influence to Arelate.

Emperor Constantine I spent considerable time in Arelate, making it a de facto imperial residence. He hosted a major church council (the Council of Arles in 314 CE) and used the city as a base for campaigns. This imperial presence elevated the city's status and prestige.

Content Notes

The foundation of Arelate as a Roman colony involved the settlement of Roman veterans on land that may have been taken from local Gaulish communities. This was part of the broader Roman conquest of Gaul, which involved significant violence and subjugation.

The process of Roman colonization often involved land confiscation and the imposition of Roman law and culture, which could be disruptive or oppressive to indigenous populations.

Roman historical tradition

Like all Roman cities, Arelate's economy and society were dependent on slavery. The city's prosperity was built on the labor of enslaved people, who had no rights and were considered property.

Slavery was a fundamental institution in the Roman world, and Arelate would have been no exception. This is a critical context for understanding the city's wealth and status.

General Roman social history

The rise of Christianity in Arelate, particularly after Constantine, led to the decline and eventual suppression of the traditional Roman and Gaulish cults, including the cult of Arelate. This was a gradual process of religious transformation.

The Christianization of the Roman Empire involved the suppression of pagan practices, though the process was complex and varied regionally. The cult of Arelate would have been among those displaced.

Scholarly Debates
Personification vs. Genius
Arelate is a distinct female personification of the city, as depicted on coins. ⇌ Arelate is essentially the same as the 'Genius' of the colony, a tutelary spirit that could be depicted in either male or female form.
Evidence for A: Numismatic depictions consistently show a female figure.
Evidence for B: Inscriptions often refer to the 'Genius' of the colony, and the roles of personification and genius overlap.
Gaulish vs. Roman Origin
Arelate was a purely Roman foundation, established by Caesar on a relatively uninhabited site. ⇌ Arelate was built on an existing Gaulish or Greek settlement, and its deity may have pre-Roman roots.
Evidence for A: Roman historical sources emphasize the colonial foundation.
Evidence for B: The Gaulish etymology of the name 'Arelate' suggests a pre-Roman settlement.
What We Don't Know

The primary gap is the lack of a detailed literary description of the cult of Arelate. Most evidence is numismatic and epigraphic. Specific details about rituals, festivals, and the temple(s) dedicated to her are unknown. The relationship between the personification and the Genius of the colony remains ambiguous. Further research into the Archaeological Museum of Arles and the Musée de l'Arles et de la Provence antiques could provide more concrete evidence.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Arelate not as a character in a story, but as a city's 'brand' in the ancient world. She was the visual and spiritual embodiment of Arles' pride, wealth, and loyalty to Rome — like a combination of a city's coat of arms, its mascot, and its patron saint, all rolled into one. She was a reminder that the city was not just a collection of buildings, but a living entity with its own divine spirit.
