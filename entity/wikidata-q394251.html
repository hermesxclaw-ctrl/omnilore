Who It Is

Imagine a city of merchants and sand, where the night sky was not just a map but a god. In Palmyra, the moon had a name and a face: Aglibol, the 'Calf of Bel,' a youthful deity whose lunar disk promised safe passage for the caravans that made his city rich. He was the light in the desert darkness, the celestial guardian of a people who lived and died by the rhythms of trade and the stars.

As a lunar god, Aglibol's behavior is not described in narrative myths (none survive), but his cultic role suggests he was a protector and guide, particularly of caravans traveling at night through the desert. The moon's light would have been a practical and symbolic guide for Palmyra's merchant caravans, and Aglibol likely served as a divine patron of safe passage, trade, and the night sky. His association with the spring Efqa suggests a chthonic or fertility aspect, linking the moon to the rhythms of water and agriculture in an arid environment.

Powers
Lunar Dominion

Aglibol controlled the moon and its phases, governing the night sky and providing light in darkness. This power was both literal (guiding travelers) and symbolic (ordering time through the lunar calendar).

Limit: No specific limitations are recorded in surviving sources; his power is inferred from his divine role.

Protector of Caravans

As a Palmyrene deity, Aglibol likely offered protection to merchants and travelers crossing the dangerous Syrian desert. His lunar light would have been a practical aid and a divine symbol of safe passage.

Limit: Not explicitly stated in any text, but a highly probable cultic function.

Bestower of Fertility and Water

Associated with the Efqa spring, Aglibol was linked to the life-giving waters of the oasis, a vital resource for the city. This connection suggests a power over fertility and agricultural abundance.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Aglibol is typically rendered in Palmyrene relief sculpture as a beardless youth, his hair styled in classical curls. He wears a Greek-style tunic (chiton) and a himation (cloak). His primary distinguishing attribute is the lunar disk — a crescent moon encompassing a full moon — positioned behind his shoulders, often resting on a staff or pole. Symbols — Crescent and full moon (often combined as a single disk), radiate nimbus, staff, and his frequent pairing with the sun god Malakbel. The spring Efqa was a sacred natural feature associated with his cult. The bull or calf (from his name's etymology) may have been a symbolic animal, though it does not appear prominently in surviving iconography.

Domains — Moon, night, celestial order, caravan travel, trade, fertility (via the spring Efqa), and divine protection. As a member of the Palmyrene triad (with Baalshamin and Malakbel), Aglibol held a place in the city's overarching cosmic structure.

Accounts I
The Divine Dyad of Aglibol and MalakbelPalmyrene inscriptions and reliefs

1st–3rd centuries CE

No narrative mythology survives for Aglibol. Instead, his 'story' is told through his consistent pairing with the sun god Malakbel. Together they formed a complementary celestial pair, representing the moon and the sun, night and day, and the eternal cosmic cycle. They were worshipped together in a joint sanctuary, and their images stood side-by-side, their lunar and solar disks creating a powerful image of celestial totality. This dyad was a central feature of Palmyrene civic religion, embodying the city's cosmic order and its reliance on the predictable rhythms of the heavens for trade and agriculture. The dyad became a defining symbol of Palmyrene identity, distinguishing its pantheon from those of neighboring cultures while also showing clear influences from Mesopotamian (Sin/Shamash) and Greek (Selene/Helios) traditions.

Accounts II
The 'God of the Spring of Efqa'Dedicatory inscription (CIS II 3917)

1st century CE (or earlier)

A key inscription names Aglibol and Malakbel as 'the gods of the spring of Efqa.' This title ties the celestial deities to the earthly, life-giving waters of Palmyra's most important oasis. The spring was a vital resource that made the city's existence possible, and its association with the divine pair elevated a natural feature into a sacred site. The gods were likely seen as the source of the water's abundance, their celestial power manifested in the terrestrial world. The spring remained a focus of religious life, and the gods' association with it underscored their role as providers and sustainers of the city.

Aglibol was the same as the Greek god Men or the Mesopotamian god Sin. — While all are lunar deities, Aglibol has distinct Palmyrene characteristics, a unique name, and a specific cultic context. He was not a simple copy of another god.

The destruction of Palmyra's temples means all evidence of Aglibol is lost. — Many artifacts, including reliefs and inscriptions, were removed from the site before the destruction and are now in museums around the world. These preserved pieces continue to provide evidence for Aglibol's cult.

Aglibol was a major, universally worshipped deity of the Roman Empire. — Aglibol's cult was largely confined to Palmyra and its immediate sphere of influence. He was a significant local god, not a widely worshipped imperial deity.
