Who It Is

Before a Kui mahout dares to throw a 40-meter noose around a wild elephant, he kneels before a small thatched shrine to the east of his house — a shrine women and children may never enter — where a coil of buffalo-hide rope is not a tool but a god. Inside it lives Phi Pakam Ajing, the forest's supreme spirit, who can give you an elephant or take your child.

Described as ambivalent and contractual rather than omnibenevolent. Phi Pakam protects Kui mahouts and elephants when properly propitiated, grants success in capture, safe transport, and healing, but inflicts illness, accident, elephant disobedience or death on those who violate taboos (entering shrine if prohibited, disrespecting rope, failing to sacrifice). Personality is that of a strict ancestral master: demanding, easily offended, but loyal to its lineage. Powers
Blessing of Elephant Capture and Taming

Grants success, safety and legitimacy to the capture (phra pen) and subsequent taming of wild elephants. Without propitiation, capture is believed to fail or cause death. Ritual homage before entering forest and again after tethering is obligatory.

Limit: Power is conditional on correct ritual sequence, lineage purity of officiant (Mor Chang), and maintenance of taboos. Does not guarantee capture; failure is interpreted as insufficient merit or taboo breach, not lack of power. Only works for members of Pakam lineage, not outsiders.

Protection of Elephants and Keepers

Protects both domestic elephants and their keepers from illness, accident, forest spirits, and disobedience. Extends to safe passage when elephant is taken outside village territory, which requires separate propitiation.

Limit: Protection is not absolute; elephants still fall ill/die, interpreted as spirit warning. Requires periodic renewal with offerings (chicken, pork, liquor, khan five). If family neglects shrine, protection lapses.

Healing and Child Welfare

When children of elephant families fall sick, parents make vows (bon) to Phi Pakam; recovery is attributed to spirit's intervention. Also invoked for general household well-being.

Limit: Healing is limited to lineage members and requires fulfillment of vow (often offering or ordination). Not a general medical deity for outsiders. Modern Kui families also use biomedical care alongside ritual.

Punitive Retribution for Taboo Violation

Inflicts illness, misfortune, elephant aggression, or death on those who violate prohibitions: women/children entering shrine, stepping over rope, disrespecting offerings, or failing to honor vows. Punishment can extend to entire household.

Limit: Retribution is avoidable through confession and expiatory sacrifice led by Mor Chang. Not arbitrary; follows known taboo code. Severity scales with intentionality.

Dominion over Forest and Wild Elephants

As supreme forest lord, commands wild elephants and forest spirits; wild elephants are understood to be under his permission to be taken. Forest entry without his leave is dangerous.

Limit: Dominion is ritual, not ecological control; deforestation and legal bans on wild capture have rendered this aspect largely symbolic since 1989 logging ban and 1992 wild elephant protection. Spirit cannot override state law.

Residence and Animation of the Rope

Animates the Pakam rope itself; rope is considered alive, must be fed, housed, and not placed on ground. Rope's twisting and coiling is treated as spirit's body movement.

Limit: Rope must be physically maintained; if hide rots or is damaged, spirit is said to be uncomfortable and must be rehoused. Rope cannot act without human handler.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Phi Pakam has no fixed anthropomorphic icon. It is aniconic and invisible, understood to reside within the Pakam rope itself and within the shrine. When described by elders, it is sometimes anthropomorphized as an old master or as two presences: Phra Khru as a luminous, ascetic holy figure (comparable to a rishi/angel) and Phi Banphachon as ancestral elders in traditional Kui dress. Secondary domain is the household lineage and the shrine space to the east of the house - an auspicious direction in both Kui and Thai cosmology. Accounts I
The Origin of the First PakamKui oral tradition as recorded in Ministry of Culture ICH Inventory (2015) and Boonprasert thesis (2012) pp.45-52; summarized in Lair 1997

Undated oral tradition; transcribed 2010-2015, claimed as ancestral time (samai bohran)

In the time when forests covered all land and elephants roamed free, the first Kui elephant master had no means to hold an elephant. He ascended into the forest to pray. Phra Khru, the holy forest ascetic, appeared and taught him to take buffalo hide and sacred wood (Wal Praeng) and twist them into a noose that would not break, breathing his spirit into it. He instructed that the rope must be housed to the east and fed with offerings, and that ancestral masters who died would join Phra Khru inside it to guide descendants. The master returned, captured his first elephant, and became the first Mor Chang. All Pakam lineages trace to this transmission. Establishes charter for Pakam lineage, explains why rope is sacred, why shrine faces east, and why two spirits cohabit. Legitimates patrilineal transmission of Mor Chang status.

Phra Khru Teaches the Noose-Making (Katha Pakam Chant Narrative)Katha Pakam chant transcription, Surin Rajabhat University Cultural Center audio 2014; excerpt in Ministry ICH dossier p.10

Living ritual chant, claimed ancient; recorded 2014

The chant narrates in first person how Phra Khru descends from the forest canopy, selects a white buffalo, and shows the people how to flay, cut, twist hide with wood, and consecrate with khan five and incantation. Each twist is accompanied by a verse. The chant warns that if the rope is made without invocation, it will snap and the elephant will kill the thrower. Provides procedural mnemonic for rope manufacture and reinforces ritual necessity. Chant is still recited during rope consecration ceremonies.

The Woman Who Entered the ShrineEthnographic anecdote collected by Boonprasert 2012 p.88 and Ministry ICH 2015 p.11; also reported to Thai PBS 2017 documentary crew

Contemporary oral anecdote, told as cautionary tale

A daughter-in-law, unaware of the prohibition, entered the Pakam shrine to shelter from rain and touched the coiled Pakam skins. That night she developed high fever and the family's elephant refused food and trumpeted restlessly. The Mor Chang divined that Phi Pakam was offended. An expiatory rite was performed with pig head, liquor, and new khan five, and the woman had to apologize outside the shrine. After the rite, both woman and elephant recovered. Reinforces gender/age exclusion and notion of collective household liability. Used to socialize children and newcomers. Demonstrates expiation mechanism rather than permanent curse.

Accounts II
The Vow for the Sick ChildSource material summary + Boonprasert 2012 pp.78-82; consistent with Lair 1997 p.91 on mahout family health practices

Ongoing practice, documented 2012-2015

When a child of an elephant family falls gravely ill and clinic medicine fails, parents go to the Pakam shrine, light incense, offer khan five and promise (bon) that if the child recovers they will offer a pig, liquor, and performance. If the child recovers, the family must fulfill the vow within a set time, inviting kin and Mor Chang to witness. Failure to fulfill is said to bring relapse. Integrates spirit belief with health-seeking behavior; creates social accountability for vow fulfillment. Illustrates benevolent aspect of spirit.

Taking the Elephant Outside the VillageMinistry ICH 2015 p.9; field observation notes from Elephant Study Center, Ban Ta Klang

Contemporary, post-1989 (after wild capture ban)

Traditionally before taking a domestic elephant beyond village boundaries - for work, festival, or sale - the owner must inform and ask permission from Phi Pakam, offering incense, candles, and liquor at the shrine and tying a white string to the elephant's tusk or ear. The elephant is led around the shrine clockwise. If permission is not sought, the elephant is believed to become agitated, break away, or fall ill on the journey. Regulates mobility and sale of elephants, reinforcing community oversight. Now applied to transport to Surin Elephant Round-up or tourist camps.

The Elephant Who ReturnedOral anecdote recorded by Cuasay fieldwork notes summarized in Lair 1997 p.92 and retold in Tourism Authority of Thailand feature 2018

Late 20th century anecdote

A newly tamed elephant, inadequately blessed, broke its tether at night and fled to the forest. The Mor Chang consulted Phi Pakam, who revealed through dream that the elephant was not yet accepted. After a night of chanting and re-consecration of the Pakam rope, the elephant was found the next morning standing calmly by the shrine, waiting. It thereafter became a docile and prized animal. Demonstrates spirit's authority over elephant will and legitimates need for proper consecration. Often told to young mahouts to stress ritual completeness.

Phi Pakam is an evil ghost like Thai Phi Krasue or Phi Pret that haunts and possesses people — Phi Pakam is a tutelary/ancestral guardian, not a malevolent haunting ghost. While it can punish taboo breaches, its primary role is protective and contractual within lineage. Thai word Phi covers both, causing conflation, but Kui Pakam is revered, not exorcised.

Phi Pakam is the same as Ganesha/Phra Phikhanet, the Hindu elephant-headed god — No iconographic, textual, or ritual connection. Ganesha is anthropomorphic elephant-headed deity of Hindu/Buddhist pantheon with distinct mythology; Phi Pakam is aniconic forest/ancestral spirit embodied in a noose. Kui do revere Ganesha separately in some contexts due to Thai influence, but they are distinct entities.

The Pakam rope is just a practical lasso with superstitious decoration — For Kui, rope is sacred person/ancestor body requiring housing, feeding, and taboo observance. It is consecrated, not merely decorated. Treating it as secular tool violates its ontology and offends custodians.

Women can enter the Pakam shrine if they are family members, or that the taboo is outdated — All documented Kui lineages maintain prohibition on women and children entering shrine and touching rope, with consistent expiation narratives for violation. While some families allow women to prepare offerings outside shrine, entry remains forbidden. Presenting it as obsolete misrepresents living practice.

Phi Pakam worship is ancient, unchanged since prehistory — Current form shows layers: Austroasiatic forest/ancestor cult base, Thai Buddhist vocabulary (Phra Khru, khan five), and modern heritage-tourism adaptation post-1960. Rope materials, vow contents, and transmission rules have changed with wild-capture ban and economic shifts. It is a living, evolving tradition, not frozen relic.
