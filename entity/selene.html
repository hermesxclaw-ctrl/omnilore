Archive Dossier — Identity

Selene

Pronunciation Ancient Greek: [selɛ̌ːnɛː] (seh-LEH-neh); Modern English: sə-LEE-nee or sə-LAY-nee.

Known As

ΣελήνηMeneΜήνηLuna

Etymology

Attested Greek Σελήνη (Selēnē), probably related to σέλας (selas, "light, brightness"), hence "the shining one" or "brightness"; Mene (Μήνη) is an older poetic term for "moon" and for the lunar month. The Roman equivalent Luna derives from Latin lux/lumen ("light"). Etymologies beyond these descriptive associations are speculative and disputed in philological scholarship.

Cultural Ownership

Primarily Archaic and Classical Greek religion and mythology, later integrated into Hellenistic and Roman religious syncretism as Luna. Her cult and imagery belong to the wider Eastern Mediterranean Greco-Roman cultural sphere.

Disputed Identifications

Later antiquity and modern popular culture frequently conflate Selene with Artemis and Hecate as "moon goddesses". In Archaic and Classical texts she is a distinct personification of the Moon, whereas Artemis is primarily a huntress and virgin goddess with secondary lunar aspects, and Hecate is associated with crossroads, magic, and liminal spaces. Scholarly consensus treats the identification of Selene with Artemis and Hecate as a product of Hellenistic and Roman syncretism rather than original identity. Some genealogical traditions also dispute her parentage, naming her as daughter of Hyperion and Theia (Hesiod) versus daughter of Pallas (Homeric Hymn to Hermes).

Archive Dossier — Approach

Every night in Greek imagination, a luminous woman rose from the ocean, bathed, crowned herself with gold, and drove a silver chariot across the dark sky—pausing, it was said, to descend to a sleeping lover who would never wake. This is Selene: not just a moon goddess, but the Moon itself made person, whose light marks months, whose desire freezes beauty in time, and whose identity has been stretched, blurred, and reclaimed across three millennia of myth and scholarship.

Selene descends from the moon to Mount Latmus each night, pressing a kiss upon the lips of her sleeping Endymion.

Who It Is

Selene is the Titaness of the moon in Greek mythology, riding her silver chariot drawn by two white horses (or sometimes oxen or mules) across the night sky. She is the daughter of Hyperion and Theia, sister of Helios (sun) and Eos (dawn). Selene is depicted as a beautiful, pale-faced woman with a crescent moon crown, often shown descending from the sky toward her lover, the mortal Endymion. The myth of Selene and Endymion is one of the most romantic in Greek mythology: Selene fell in love with the beautiful shepherd (or king) Endymion and asked Zeus to grant him eternal youth. Zeus instead granted Endymion eternal sleep, preserving his beauty forever in a cave on Mount Latmus, where Selene visits him every night. In other accounts, Selene herself put Endymion into an endless sleep to preserve his perfection. Selene bore Endymion fifty daughters (the Menae, the lunar months) and possibly the beautiful Narcissus. Selene is distinct from Artemis in early Greek religion: Artemis is the goddess of the hunt and the wild, associated with the moon but not the moon itself. Selene is the actual celestial body and the cosmic force that governs its phases. In the Orphic tradition, she is identified with Hecate and Persephone as part of a triple moon goddess trinity. Her Roman counterpart is Luna.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

Attested descriptions present Selene as a strikingly beautiful goddess, often crowned with a crescent moon and radiating soft, silver light. The Homeric Hymn 32 depicts her rising from the ocean after bathing, wearing a shining golden crown, and yoking her long-maned horses to a chariot that traverses the night sky. Artistic representations from Greek and Roman antiquity show her as a woman with a veil or mantle billowing behind her, sometimes with a crescent diadem on her forehead, riding a chariot drawn by horses, bulls, or mules. In Orphic and later iconography she may appear horned, symbolizing the crescent moon. These features are attested; any specific coloration beyond "white" or "silver" is largely reconstructed from artistic conventions rather than textual description.

Symbols & Iconography

Selene’s primary symbol is the Moon itself, especially the crescent. She is associated with a silver or pale chariot, white horses or oxen, a golden crown, and sometimes horns representing the crescent. In Orphic and later magical texts she is linked to torches, nocturnal light, and the cyclical phases of the moon. Coins and reliefs from the Roman period depicting Luna often show a crescent behind her shoulders or above her head, a visual quotation of Selene’s Greek iconography. The number fifty, associated with her fifty daughters by Endymion, functions symbolically as the lunar months of an Olympiad. These symbolic associations are attested in literary and artistic sources; allegorical readings (e.g., Selene as the soul, Endymion as sleep) are interpretive rather than original cult doctrine.

Behaviour & Personality

Selene is portrayed as serene, luminous, and constant, yet emotionally intense in her love for Endymion. The Endymion myth emphasizes her devoted, repetitive descent from the heavens to gaze upon a beloved who cannot reciprocate, suggesting a personality marked by steadfastness and longing. Her union with Zeus and Pan indicates that she participates in the broader erotic and generative dynamics of Greek myth, but she is not characterized as capricious or vengeful in the way some Olympian deities are. In Orphic and hymn traditions she is invoked as benevolent, life-giving, and protective, governing months, seasons, and fertility. These traits are attested; any deeper psychological motives are interpretive extrapolations from the narrative patterns.

Domains & Habitats

Selene’s domain is the Moon and the night sky. She rises from the ocean at evening, drives her chariot across the heavens, and illuminates the earth with her light. She is associated with the cyclical passage of time, particularly months and seasons, and with nocturnal phenomena such as dew and dreams. Her mythic visits to Endymion occur on Mount Latmos in Caria, and some traditions connect her to Elis through Endymion’s kingship. In Orphic and magical contexts she is linked to fertility, growth, and the regulation of natural rhythms. Her habitat is thus both cosmic (the sky, the oceanic horizon) and localized (Latmos, Elis) in different narrative strands.

Version Differences

Archaic sources (Hesiod, Homeric Hymns, Sappho) emphasize Selene as a distinct personification of the Moon, daughter of Hyperion and Theia, with a clear celestial role. Later Hellenistic and Roman texts increasingly conflate her with Artemis and Hecate, treating "moon goddess" as a shared category and sometimes using names interchangeably. Genealogical traditions differ: Hesiod names Hyperion and Theia as parents; the Homeric Hymn to Hermes calls her daughter of Pallas; Orphic traditions may vary. The Endymion myth shows significant variation: Endymion is a shepherd, hunter, or king; his sleep is chosen, imposed by Zeus, or caused by Selene; his location is Mount Latmos in Caria or Elis in the Peloponnese. Selene’s children also differ by source: Pandia, Ersa, the Menai, the Horai, and others are variously attributed to her unions with Zeus and Endymion. These differences are attested and should not be harmonized into a single canonical version.

Archive Dossier — Powers

Personification and governance of the Moon

Source Version Hesiod, Theogony; Homeric Hymn 32 to Selene; Orphic Hymn to Selene; EBSCO Research Starter on Selene.

Description

Selene embodies the Moon itself, not merely a goddess associated with it. She governs its phases, light, and cyclical movement across the night sky, bringing illumination to the earth and marking the passage of months.

Limitations

Her power is cosmic but circumscribed to the lunar domain; she does not, in early sources, wield the broader Olympian authority over fate or justice. The extent to which she controls versus embodies the moon’s cycles is not explicitly defined.

Contradictions

Later conflation with Artemis and Hecate blurs whether Selene alone personifies the Moon or shares lunar governance with other goddesses.

Driving the lunar chariot

Source Version Homeric Hymn 32 to Selene; Orphic Hymn to Selene; iconographic evidence from Greek and Roman art.

Description

Selene rises from the ocean, bathes, and yokes her long-maned horses to a chariot that she drives across the heavens at evening, her golden crown casting light over the darkened earth.

Limitations

The chariot is a mythic representation of the moon’s movement rather than a mechanical vehicle; its exact nature (horses vs oxen vs mules) varies by tradition.

Contradictions

Some sources depict her on horseback or standing with a crescent rather than in a chariot, reflecting artistic variation rather than doctrinal disagreement.

Regulation of months and seasons

Source Version Pausanias, Description of Greece 5.1.4; Orphic Hymn to Selene; later scholia on the Endymion myth.

Description

Selene is associated with the measurement of time, particularly lunar months. Her fifty daughters by Endymion (Menai) are interpreted as personifications of lunar months, and Orphic hymns invoke her as governing months and seasons.

Limitations

The connection between the fifty daughters and the lunar calendar is interpretive and not explicitly stated in primary texts; her role in calendrical regulation is more symbolic than administrative.

Contradictions

Some traditions emphasize her daughters as nymphs or minor goddesses without explicit calendrical function.

Influence on fertility and growth

Source Version Orphic Hymn to Selene; Orphic and magical papyri; later astrological traditions.

Description

Selene is invoked in Orphic and magical texts as a goddess who influences fertility, growth of plants, and reproductive cycles, reflecting ancient associations between lunar phases and biological rhythms.

Limitations

These associations are more prominent in later religious and magical contexts than in early epic; they may reflect syncretism with Artemis and Hecate.

Contradictions

Some scholars argue that fertility aspects belong primarily to Artemis and Hecate, with Selene’s role limited to illumination and timekeeping.

Bestowal or mediation of eternal sleep and youth (Endymion)

Source Version Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 1.7.5; Pausanias, Description of Greece 5.1.4; Lucian, Dialogues of the Gods; EBSCO Research Starter on Selene and Endymion.

Description

In some versions of the Endymion myth, Selene is directly involved in granting or causing Endymion’s eternal sleep, preserving his youth and beauty. In others, she is the beneficiary of a fate chosen or imposed by Zeus.

Limitations

Her agency varies by source: she may request the gift, cause the sleep herself, or simply love the result of Zeus’s decision. The myth does not clearly define her power over mortality beyond this narrative.

Contradictions

Some versions attribute the sleep to Zeus’s choice or punishment; others to Selene’s desire; still others to Endymion’s own choice, creating a complex web of agency and responsibility.

Erotic and generative power

Source Version Homeric Hymn 32 to Selene; Pausanias, Description of Greece; Virgil, Georgics; later scholia and mythographic compilations.

Description

Selene engages in unions with gods and mortals—Zeus, Endymion, Pan—resulting in divine and semi-divine offspring such as Pandia, Ersa, and the Menai. Her erotic power is linked to nocturnal beauty and desire.

Limitations

Her erotic power is narrative rather than systematically defined; she is not a generalized love goddess like Aphrodite.

Contradictions

The list of her children and the nature of each union vary significantly across sources.

Description

Titan father of Selene, associated with the Sun and celestial light.

Version Note

Archaic Greek tradition; widely accepted.

Conflicting Accounts

Homeric Hymn to Hermes names Pallas as Selene’s father, creating a genealogical contradiction.

Theia

Type familySource Hesiod, Theogony 371–374.

Description

Titan mother of Selene, associated with sight and shining light.

Version Note

Archaic Greek tradition; widely accepted.

Conflicting Accounts

Alternative genealogies do not mention Theia, focusing instead on Pallas.

Helios

Description

Brother of Selene, personification of the Sun, driving a golden chariot across the daytime sky.

Version Note

Consistent across major sources.

Conflicting Accounts

None significant; later syncretism identifies Helios with Apollo but maintains sibling relationship.

Eos

Type familySource Hesiod, Theogony; Orphic Hymns.

Description

Sister of Selene, personification of Dawn.

Version Note

Consistent across major sources.

Conflicting Accounts

None significant.

Zeus

Type romanticSource Homeric Hymn 32 to Selene; later mythographic sources.

Description

Selene’s lover in the Homeric Hymn 32; father of her daughter Pandia and, in some traditions, Ersa and other children.

Version Note

Archaic hymn with later elaborations.

Conflicting Accounts

Some traditions attribute Pandia to other parentage or treat her as a festival rather than a goddess.

Pandia

Type familySource Homeric Hymn 32 to Selene; later scholia.

Description

Daughter of Selene and Zeus, a maiden of surpassing beauty, associated with the full moon and possibly with an Athenian festival.

Version Note

Archaic mention with limited detail.

Conflicting Accounts

The exact nature of Pandia (goddess vs festival) and her cult is debated.

Ersa

Type familySource Later mythographic compilations and scholia.

Description

Personification of dew, sometimes named as a daughter of Selene and Zeus.

Version Note

Hellenistic and Roman elaboration.

Conflicting Accounts

Not universally attested; some sources omit Ersa or assign her different parentage.

Endymion

Type romanticSource Apollodorus, Bibliotheca; Pausanias, Description of Greece; Lucian, Dialogues of the Gods; Theocritus; EBSCO Research Starter on Selene and Endymion.

Description

Mortal beloved of Selene, placed in eternal sleep to preserve his youth and beauty. Selene visits him nightly and, in some traditions, bears fifty daughters by him.

Version Note

Central myth for Selene’s character in later literature.

Conflicting Accounts

Endymion’s status (shepherd, hunter, king), location (Latmos vs Elis), and the cause of his sleep (choice, Zeus’s decision, Selene’s action) vary widely.

Menai (fifty daughters)

Type familySource Pausanias, Description of Greece 5.1.4; later scholia.

Description

Fifty daughters of Selene and Endymion, interpreted as personifications of lunar months.

Version Note

Local tradition with symbolic interpretation.

Conflicting Accounts

Some sources mention the daughters without calendrical interpretation; others omit them entirely.

Pan

Type romanticSource Virgil, Georgics 3.391–393; later scholia.

Description

Rustic god who seduces Selene by disguising himself in a white fleece, luring her into the woods.

Version Note

Roman poetic transmission of a Greek myth.

Conflicting Accounts

Some versions simply state that Pan and Selene were lovers without mentioning deception.

Artemis

Type syncreticSource Hellenistic and Roman religious texts; EBSCO Research Starter on Selene.

Description

Goddess of the hunt and virginity, later identified with Selene as a lunar goddess. In post-classical times, Selene and Artemis are often conflated, with Artemis taking over many lunar functions.

Version Note

Syncretic identification rather than original mythic relationship.

Conflicting Accounts

Archaic sources treat Selene and Artemis as distinct; later sources merge their identities.

Hecate

Type syncreticSource Hellenistic and Roman religious texts; later magical papyri.

Description

Goddess of magic, crossroads, and liminal spaces, sometimes associated with the moon and identified with Selene and Artemis as a triad of lunar goddesses.

Version Note

Syncretic association.

Conflicting Accounts

Early sources do not present Hecate as a lunar goddess; the triadic association is a later development.

Luna

Type adaptationSource Roman religious texts and inscriptions; standard classical scholarship.

Description

Roman equivalent of Selene, personification of the Moon, worshipped with temples and cult in Rome.

Version Note

Cultural adaptation of Selene into Roman religion.

Conflicting Accounts

Some Roman sources treat Luna as distinct from Selene; others explicitly equate them.
