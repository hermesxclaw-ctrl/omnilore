Who It Is

The Cherubim (singular: cherub) have undergone one of the most dramatic transformations in religious iconography: from the awe-inspiring, multi-faced, winged guardians of Ezekiel's vision to the chubby, baby-faced putti of Renaissance art. In the Hebrew Bible, cherubim are fearsome celestial beings who serve as guardians of sacred space and bearers of YHWH's throne. Their first appearance in Genesis 3:24 is as the guardians stationed east of Eden with a flaming, ever-turning sword, preventing humanity's return to the garden after the expulsion. In Exodus, two golden cherubim with outstretched wings adorn the mercy seat atop the Ark of the Covenant, their wings forming a space from which God speaks to Moses. Solomon's Temple featured massive olive-wood cherubim (each 10 cubits tall) in the Holy of Holies, their wings spanning the width of the chamber. The most detailed description comes from Ezekiel's vision (Ezekiel 1 and 10), which presents the cherubim as chimeric beings of immense complexity: each has four faces (human, lion, ox, and eagle), four wings, human hands beneath their wings, and legs ending in calves' hooves that gleam like burnished bronze. They move in perfect synchronicity with the ophanim (wheels within wheels), their bodies and wings covered in eyes. Ezekiel identifies these living creatures as cherubim — the very beings that bear the throne-chariot (Merkabah) of God. The Book of Revelation's four living creatures before God's throne echo Ezekiel's cherubim, though with six wings and ceaseless worship. In the Quran, similar beings (the hamalat al-arsh) bear the throne of God. Over centuries of Christian art, the terrifying, multi-faced guardian transformed into the cherubic infant (putto) of popular imagination — a domestication that reflects the broader shift from the awesome, numinous divine of the ancient Near East to the approachable, sentimental piety of the Renaissance. In Jewish mysticism (Merkabah literature), contemplation of the cherubim and the divine chariot was considered the most esoteric and dangerous form of mystical practice, reserved for the most learned scholars. The cherubim remain the most paradoxically depicted beings in angelology: terrifying in scripture, adorable in art.
