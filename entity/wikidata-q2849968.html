Form

Anila is typically depicted as a youthful, fair-skinned deity riding a deer (or antelope). He holds a white flag or banner that billows in the wind. In some iconography, he carries a goad (ankusha) or a staff. His complexion is often associated with the pale, thin air itself.

Powers

Control of Wind and Storms Anila can summon, direct, and still the winds. He can create gentle breezes or devastating storms. Vedic and Purāṇic His power is typically subordinate to the will of the greater gods, especially Indra and Vishnu. In the Vedas, Vāyu is a powerful independent deity; in the Purāṇas, he is a functionary.

Giver of Life-Breath (Prāṇa) As the deity of air, Anila is the source of the vital breath that sustains all living beings. He is invoked for health, vitality, and longevity. Vedic and Yogic traditions His life-giving aspect is tied to the physical breath; it is not a power to resurrect the dead. None noted.

Messenger and Carrier Anila carries prayers, offerings, and even divine weapons across the cosmos. He is the swiftest of the gods, able to traverse the universe in an instant. Vedic and Epic (Ramayana, Mahabharata) He can only carry what is given to him; he does not initiate the messages himself. His role as a messenger overlaps with Agni (fire), who also carries offerings to the gods.

Guardian of the Northwest As a Dikpāla, Anila protects the northwest quarter. He is invoked in rituals to secure the boundaries of a sacred space. Purāṇic His guardianship is directional and ritualistic; it is not a general power of protection. None noted.

Accounts

Anila (as Vāyu) carries the divine energy of Shiva to the womb of Anjana, resulting in the birth of Hanuman, the monkey god and devotee of Rama. Hanuman inherits his father's speed, strength, and ability to fly.

Anila serves as the bearer of Indra's banner in battle, symbolizing the king of the gods' dominion over the atmosphere and his ability to command the winds.

Anila (as Vāyu) fathers Bhima, the second Pandava, on Kunti. Bhima inherits his father's immense strength and is known for his prowess in battle and his love of food.

Anila (as Vāyu) assists Agni, the fire god, in consuming the Khandava forest. He fans the flames with his winds, enabling the fire to spread and destroy the forest and its inhabitants.

Scholarly Debates
Identity as separate deity vs. epithet of Vāyu
Anila is a distinct deity, a Dikpāla (directional guardian) with his own iconography and role. ⇌ Anila is simply another name for Vāyu, the Vedic wind god.
Evidence for A: Purāṇic texts (Vishnu Purana, Matsya Purana) list Anila as a separate guardian.
Evidence for B: The Amarakośa lists Anila as a synonym for Vāyu; Vedic hymns use both names interchangeably.
Paternity of Hanuman
Anila (Vāyu) is the father of Hanuman. ⇌ Shiva is the father, with Vāyu merely carrying the divine energy.
Evidence for A: Ramayana (Valmiki), many Purāṇas.
Evidence for B: Shiva Purana, some regional traditions.
What We Don't Know

Specific temple iconography of Anila as a Dikpāla is not fully documented in available sources. The exact ritual practices associated with Anila in Vedic and Purāṇic contexts require further research. A more detailed examination of Jain and Buddhist texts on wind deities is needed.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Anila as the Hindu equivalent of a cosmic UPS driver — but instead of packages, he delivers prayers, divine weapons, and even the essence of gods to the right wombs. He's the fastest being in the universe, but he's always working for someone else: Indra, Vishnu, or Agni. He's the ultimate supporting character — the wind beneath the wings of the heroes.
