Dangun

Founder of Gojoseon; Grandson of Heaven; Bear's Son

also known asTan'gunTan'gun WanggŏmDan'gunDangun Wanggeom檀君단군왕검
TypeFounding Mythic King / Demigod
CultureKorean
Who It Is
Form

Dangun is represented in Korean iconography as a regal bearded monarch wearing a royal crown (myeollyugwan) and robes, often holding a ritual tablet or seated on a throne. In traditional paintings he appears as a middle-aged sage-king with an elongated, serene face, white robes symbolizing purity, and the sacred birch tree motif in the background. In modern nationalist iconography he is depicted as a robust, muscular founder-king, sometimes bare-chested and wielding a sword or ceremonial bell, emphasizing martial legitimacy. In shamanic contexts he is honored as Wangshin ('king-god') and his likeness appears on temple scrolls and talismans rather than as a fixed anthropomorphic form.

Powers

Founding and ruling Gojoseon — establishes the first Korean kingdom in 2333 BCE, creating its capital at Asadal and instituting the royal ancestral ritual to Heaven.

Divine descent — inherits heavenly authority through his grandfather Hwanin (Lord of Heaven) and father Hwanung (Supreme Divine Regent), giving his kingship cosmological legitimacy.

Longevity — Samguk yusa and Joseon-era chronologies credit him with a reign of roughly 1,500 years before his ascension, placing him among the long-lived sage-kings of East Asian myth.

Attribution of cultural inventions — later tradition credits Dangun with the development of acupuncture and moxibustion, linking him to the founding of Korean medicine.

Heavenly ascension — upon completing his mortal reign he returns to the heavens, transforming into a mountain spirit (Sanshin) and remaining accessible through shamanic ritual.

Behavior & Habitat

As founder-king, Dangun is portrayed as a lawgiver and ritual sovereign. He institutes moral codes and governance at Sinsi ('City of God'), teaches humans agriculture, medicine, and the arts, and establishes the ancestral rites to Hwanin that bind the polity together. He is said to have reigned for over 1,500 years before ascending to heaven as a mountain spirit. In Daejongist theology he functions as the earthly incarnation of the one God manifested in three persons (Hwanin-Hwanung-Dangun). In modern South Korea he is commemorated as the father of the nation; in North Korea his tomb is treated as a verified archaeological site legitimizing the regime's claim to the ancient Korean lineage.

Accounts

Hwanung descends to earth with 3,000 followers and founds Sinsi on Taebaeksan. A bear and a tiger pray to become human. Hwanung gives them sacred garlic and mugwort and commands them to avoid sunlight for 100 days. The tiger abandons the trial after twenty days; the bear persists and transforms into a beautiful woman, Ungnyeo. She prays beneath the divine birch tree (shindansu) for a child, and Hwanung takes her as his wife, bearing Dangun Wanggŏm. This tale is the central etiology of the Korean people, encoding a myth of tribal synthesis — the bear-tribe and tiger-tribe — under the heavenly prince's lineage.

Dangun ascends to the throne, builds the walled city of Asadal (believed to be near modern Pyongyang or on Mount Paegak), and names his kingdom Joseon — later distinguished as Gojoseon ('Old Joseon') to separate it from the 1392 dynasty. He moves the capital to Mount Gunghol and institutes the annual ancestral ritual to Hwanin, which doubles as a political assembly for the tribal confederation. The location of Asadal remains disputed; both North and South Korea claim sites near Pyongyang as the true capital.

After a reign computed at roughly 1,500 years, Dangun ascends back to heaven. The date 15 March in the Dangi calendar (4340 Dangi year) is celebrated as Eocheonjeol ('Royal Day Festival' or 'Day of Returning to Heaven'), commemorating his departure. This myth anchors the religious calendar of Daejongism, which treats Dangun not as a remote ancestor but as an ever-present divine king who will return.

Until 1961, South Korea used the Dangi (단기; 'Dangun calendar') era, counting years from 2333 BCE. This made 1961 equivalent to Dangi 4294. The holiday Gaecheonjeol ('Opening of Heaven') on 3 October celebrates the founding date, originally a Daejongist religious anniversary and now a national public holiday. North Korea also recognizes the date but emphasizes archaeological proof through the Mausoleum of Tangun excavation (1993–1994), claiming the tomb confirms Dangun as a historical figure ruling c. 3010 BCE.

Modern Interpretations

Goryeo dynasty (918–1392) Tan'gun began to attract significant attention during the late Goryeo period, when Koreans fought wars against the Mongol Yuan dynasty. The Samguk yusa compilation itself was partly motivated by a desire to assert Korean cultural sovereignty against Mongol cultural pressure. Joseon dynasty (1392–1897) Dangun was worshiped as a national ancestor-king. A shrine dedicated to Dangun and King Chumo of Goguryeo was built in Pyongyang, and the Samseongdang (三聖堂) was erected to Hwanin, Hwanung, and Dangun. Confucian elites accepted the myth as ancestral history but treated it with increasing skepticism as Neo-Confucian rationalism grew. Late 19th–early 20th century Dangun became the central symbol of Korean nationalism during the independence movement against Japan. Dangunkyo (Dangun religion) emerged, and historical works like Daedong Sagang and Gyuwon Sahwa elevated the Tan'gun era as the authentic golden age of Korean civilization. The Dangi calendar (starting 2333 BCE) was used until 1961. Modern South Korea Dangun is taught as the legendary founder in all Korean school curricula. National Foundation Day (Gaecheonjeol, 3 October) is a public holiday. Taekwondo's Dan-Gun pattern (the second ITF teul) memorializes him. Academic debate continues over his historicity, with mainstream Korean historians treating him as a foundational myth rather than a verifiable person. Scholarly Debates
What We Don't Know

The historicity of Dangun remains unresolved. No contemporary archaeological evidence from the 24th–23rd century BCE supports the existence of an organized kingdom on the Korean Peninsula; the earliest reliably dated state-level societies appear much later. The Book of Wei passages cited by Samguk yusa have not survived in any extant version, making it impossible to verify whether the Dangun legend circulated in Chinese historiography before the 13th century. The etymology connecting Dangun to Tengri is debated and lacks conclusive linguistic evidence. The Mausoleum of Tangun in North Korea has never been subjected to independent international dating. The lost text Gogi (古記), if it existed, might have contained an earlier version of the myth, but no trace survives.

A way to hold it in your mind

Before there was a Korea on any map, there was a bear, a tiger, and a heavenly prince on a mountain. Out of that cave — and the persistence of one ursine penitent — came Dangun, the demigod who founded the first Korean kingdom in 2333 BCE and whose name still echoes from Pyongyang to Seoul every 3 October.
