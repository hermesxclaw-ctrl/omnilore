Who It Is

Forget the Kraken. For centuries, the waters to the west of China were ruled not by a tentacled beast, but by a bureaucratic dragon in imperial robes, a deity who could summon a hurricane with a flick of his whiskers and yet still had to file paperwork with the Jade Emperor. This is Ao Run, the Dragon King of the Western Sea — a being of immense power and, paradoxically, a middle manager in the celestial cosmos.

Ao Run is a dignified and powerful deity, but his behavior is often portrayed as cautious and politically minded. Unlike his more impulsive brother Ao Guang (Dragon King of the East Sea), Ao Run is more likely to seek celestial arbitration than to take direct action. He is a guardian of his realm, but also a vassal who understands the importance of not overstepping his bounds.

Powers
Hydrokinesis

Ao Run has absolute control over the waters of the Western Sea, including the ability to create or calm storms, summon tsunamis, and manipulate currents.

Limit: His power over water is limited to his own realm. He cannot command the seas of other Dragon Kings without their permission.

Weather Manipulation

He can summon rain, wind, and storms. This is a key function of his role as a Dragon King, as he is responsible for providing rain for agriculture.

Limit: This power is often depicted as being subject to the approval of the Jade Emperor. He cannot create weather that goes against celestial decree.

Shapeshifting

Like all Dragon Kings, Ao Run can take on a fully human form, a fully draconic form, or a hybrid form.

Limit: The transformation is often depicted as being uncomfortable or requiring a specific artifact (like a dragon robe) to maintain.

Immortality & Longevity

As a divine being, Ao Run is immortal, or at least has a lifespan measured in millennia.

Limit: He is still subject to the laws of heaven and can be punished or killed by more powerful deities.

Authority over Marine Life

He commands all creatures of the sea, from the smallest fish to the largest whales, and can summon them as an army or as servants.

Limit: This authority is limited to the creatures of his sea.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Ao Run is typically depicted as a majestic dragon in full imperial regalia, often shown with a dragon's head, human-like body, and a long, serpentine tail. He wears elaborate robes embroidered with wave patterns and pearls, and holds a ceremonial tablet (hu) or a pearl (the 'Dragon Pearl' or 'Flame Pearl') representing his power over the sea and weather. In some depictions, he appears as a fully anthropomorphic figure with a dragon headdress.

Symbols — The dragon itself, the Flaming Pearl, the trident or other naval weaponry, coral, pearls, and the color white/black (associated with the West in Chinese cosmology). His palace is known as the 'Crystal Palace' or 'Jade Palace' of the West Sea.

Domains — Western Sea (the ocean to the west of China, which in the classical worldview included the Indian Ocean and beyond), weather control (especially rain and storms), marine life, navigation, flood control, and the protection of seafarers.

Accounts I
The Confrontation with NezhaFengshen Yanyi, Chapter 13

Ming Dynasty (16th century)

After the young hero Nezha kills Ao Bing, the son of Ao Guang (Dragon King of the East Sea), Ao Guang summons his three brothers — Ao Qin (South), Ao Run (West), and Ao Shun (North) — to seek revenge. They collectively confront Nezha and his father, Li Jing. Ao Run, as the Dragon King of the West, is portrayed as a formidable ally, but the Dragon Kings are ultimately outmaneuvered by Nezha's cleverness and his connection to the celestial sage Taiyi Zhenren. The Dragon Kings are forced to appeal to the Jade Emperor for justice, but Nezha's teacher intervenes, and the matter is resolved in a way that humiliates the Dragon Kings. The story establishes the Dragon Kings as powerful but not supreme beings. They are subject to the celestial hierarchy and can be defied by mortals with divine backing. It also establishes the rivalry between the Dragon Kings and the heroes of the Zhou dynasty.

Accounts II
The Dragon Kings and the Journey to the WestJourney to the West

Ming Dynasty (16th century)

The Dragon King of the West Sea appears as a minor character, often providing assistance or information to Sun Wukong and his companions. He is depicted as a bureaucratic official of the celestial court, managing his realm and responding to requests from more powerful figures. This portrayal cements the Dragon Kings' role as celestial functionaries, responsible for maintaining order in the natural world.

Local Folk Rituals for RainLocal temple records and ethnographies

Tang dynasty to present

In many coastal and riverine communities, Ao Run is venerated as a rain-bringer and protector against floods. Local officials and villagers would perform rituals at his temples, offering sacrifices and processions to beseech him for good weather and bountiful harvests. These rituals often involved the symbolic 'awakening' of the dragon from his slumber. This tradition demonstrates the importance of the Dragon Kings in agricultural and maritime societies, where their favor was seen as essential for survival.

Ao Run is a god of the ocean in a general sense, equivalent to Poseidon or Neptune. — Ao Run is specifically the Dragon King of the Western Sea in Chinese mythology. He is part of a pantheon of four Dragon Kings, each ruling a different sea. Unlike Poseidon, he is not a supreme god of the sea but a regional deity under the Jade Emperor.

The Dragon Kings are purely benevolent figures. — The Dragon Kings can be wrathful and destructive, causing floods and storms when angered or when celestial order is disrupted. They are also depicted as bureaucratic and sometimes petty.

All Dragon Kings are named 'Ao'. — The 'Ao' surname is common but not universal. In some texts, the Dragon Kings are referred to by other titles or epithets.
