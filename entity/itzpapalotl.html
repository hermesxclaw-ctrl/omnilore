Who It Is

Imagine a butterfly made of volcanic glass, its wings sharp enough to slice the sky. That is Itzpapalotl, the Obsidian Butterfly, queen of the star demons who descend during eclipses to devour the sun. She is the dark mirror of creation, a goddess of both paradise and destruction, whose claws you feel when the light goes out.

As a tzitzimitl, she is a star demon who descends during solar eclipses, threatening to devour the sun and bring darkness. She is also associated with the paradise of Tamoanchan, where she may serve as a guardian or a punishing force for those who violate cosmic order. Her behavior is ambiguous: she is both a destroyer and a potential protector, depending on the context. Ritual specialists during the Aztec period understood Itzpapalotl as a figure to be propitiated rather than worshipped in the conventional sense. Name & Tongue

Etymology — Nahuatl: itztli (obsidian) + papalotl (butterfly). The name literally means 'Obsidian Butterfly' or 'Clawed Butterfly', referencing her association with the volcanic glass used for sacrificial knives and her fierce, martial aspect.

Pronunciation — eets-pah-pah-LOHT

Aliases — Obsidian Butterfly, Clawed Butterfly, Queen of the Tzitzimimeh, Tzitzimimeh Queen, Lady of the Obsidian Knives

Ownership — Aztec (Mexica) and broader Nahua cultures of central Mexico, pre-Columbian and colonial period.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Itzpapalotl is depicted as a skeletal or partially skeletal female figure with butterfly wings made of obsidian blades. Her hands and feet are clawed, and she often wears a skirt of stars or a headdress of knives. In some codices, she has a death's-head or a face painted with black and red stripes, emphasizing her martial and sacrificial nature. Accounts I
The Threat to the Fifth SunHistoria de los mexicanos por sus pinturas

Pre-Columbian / colonial

During the creation of the fifth sun, the tzitzimimeh, led by Itzpapalotl, threatened to destroy the new sun and plunge the world into darkness. The gods had to sacrifice themselves to ensure the sun's rise, and the tzitzimimeh were banished to the west, only to return during eclipses. The tzitzimimeh became eternal enemies of the sun, feared during eclipses.

The Legend of the Obsidian Butterfly in Chichimec Migration MythsHistoria Tolteca-Chichimeca; various codices and oral tradition

Postclassic period (c. 900-1521 CE)

In the migration narratives of the Chichimec peoples who settled in the Valley of Mexico, Itzpapalotl appears as a powerful and dangerous figure encountered during the ancestral journey. According to one tradition recorded in the Historia Tolteca-Chichimeca, the migrating Chichimecs encountered Itzpapalotl in a sacred place. She was described as a terrifying goddess who devoured men, but she could also be overcome through ritual knowledge and bravery. In one version, the god Mixcoatl (the Cloud Serpent, associated with hunting and the Milky Way) defeated Itzpapalotl by kindling a sacred fire and using his divine hunting skills to subdue her. After her defeat, she became a patroness and source of power for certain Chichimec lineages, who claimed descent from her or maintained her cult.
