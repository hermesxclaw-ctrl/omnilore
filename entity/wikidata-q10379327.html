Who It Is

In the shadow of the great Earth Mother Tellus, there stands a figure barely whispered in the annals of Roman religion: Tellumo, the male counterpart of the Earth goddess. While Tellus was venerated in grand temples and public festivals, Tellumo lingers as a ghost in the margins of ancient texts, mentioned only by a handful of authors. Who was this obscure deity, and why did the Romans, who so often paired male and female forces, leave him so scantily recorded?

No information survives. As a personification of the male aspect of the earth, he might have been conceived as a fertilizing or supporting force, but this is conjecture.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Tellumo is derived from Tellus, the Roman earth goddess, with the masculine suffix -mo, parallel to other divine pairs such as Faunus/Fauna or Liber/Libera. The root is likely connected to the Proto-Indo-European *telh₂- meaning 'to bear, support', as suggested by Michiel de Vaan.

Pronunciation — tel-LOO-mo (Classical Latin: /telˈluː.moː/)

Aliases — Tellurus, Tellumo (variant spelling), Tellurus (variant)

Ownership — Roman religion, specifically the Republican and Imperial periods. No evidence of worship outside Roman context.

Personification of the Earth (male aspect)

As the male counterpart of Tellus, Tellumo likely represented the masculine principle of the earth, possibly associated with fertility and support. However, no specific powers are attested.

Limit: No specific limitations are recorded; the deity is essentially a name.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No ancient descriptions of Tellumo's appearance survive. Given the pattern of male counterparts, he may have been depicted as a male figure associated with the earth, perhaps holding a cornucopia or agricultural implements, but this is purely speculative.

Symbols — No specific symbols are attested. By analogy with Tellus, he might have been associated with the cornucopia, but there is no evidence.

Domains — The earth itself, but no specific cult sites or temples are recorded.
