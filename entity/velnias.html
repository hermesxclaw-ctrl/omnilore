Etymology

The name Velnias is derived from the Proto-Indo-European root *wel- meaning 'to see' or 'to know', related to the Lithuanian vėlės (souls of the dead) and the Slavic Veles. It is cognate with the Latin vultus (face) and the Old Norse Valhöll (hall of the slain). The Latvian form Velns is a direct cognate. Some scholars also connect it to the word for 'devil' in Baltic languages, reflecting the Christianization of the deity.

Cultural Ownership

Baltic (Lithuanian and Latvian) paganism and folklore. Velnias is a central figure in Latvian and Lithuanian mythology, often depicted as a devil or trickster, but with roots in a pre-Christian chthonic deity.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars identify Velnias as a direct cognate of the Slavic Veles, while others see him as a separate but related deity. There is debate over whether he was originally a god of the underworld, cattle, or magic, or if he was always a devil figure. The Christianization of the Baltic region heavily influenced his later portrayal as a malevolent being.

Archive Dossier — Approach

In the shadowed forests and misty marshes of the Baltic lands, there lurks a being of cunning and chaos—Velnias, the devil who is not merely a demon but a fractured reflection of an ancient god. He is the trickster who bargains with farmers, the tempter who steals the sun, and the chthonic lord whose name echoes the Slavic Veles and the Vedic Varuna. To understand Velnias is to peer into the very soul of Baltic paganism, where good and evil are not simple opposites but intertwined forces in a cosmic dance.

Velnias hides in the hollow oak, but Perkūns sees him — a flash of lightning and the devil must run.

Who It Is

Velnias is the chthonic devil-trickster of Baltic mythology, a complex figure who is neither purely evil (like the Christian Satan) nor merely a demon. He is the lord of the underworld, cattle, and wealth — the god of the dark, wild, chthonic realm that contrasts with Dievs's orderly heavens. Velnias is frequently depicted as a horned, black man with cloven hooves, a tail, and one eye — or as a snake, a black dog, or a raven. In the dainas, Velnias steals the sun or the moon from the sky, and Perkūns (the thunder god) pursues him with lightning. Velnias hides in rocks, trees, and water; when lightning strikes a tree, it is Perkūns striking at the devil. Despite being a divine adversary, Velnias is often more foolish than evil in folk tales — he is constantly outwitted by humans. He is bound by strict rules: he cannot break a promise, and he loses his power if anyone speaks his name in daylight. In Prussian myth, Velnias (Patollu) was worshipped as a god of death and wealth. The Velnias-Perku¯nas conflict represents the fundamental Baltic cosmic dualism: order vs. chaos, sky vs. earth, culture vs. wilderness. Unlike the wholly evil Christian devil, Velnias is an integral part of the cosmic balance — without him, the world would have no contrast.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

Velnias is often depicted as a dark, hairy being with horns, hooves, and a tail, similar to the Christian devil. However, in older folklore, he may appear as a handsome man, a black dog, a serpent, or a shadowy figure. He can shapeshift into various animals, especially black cats, goats, or ravens.

Symbols & Iconography

Velnias is associated with the underworld, darkness, fire, and the wilderness. He is often depicted with a whip or a club, and sometimes with a bag of coins. In Latvian folklore, he is connected to the sea and the forest, and is said to live in a castle under the earth or in a deep lake.

Behaviour & Personality

Velnias is a cunning trickster, often trying to outwit humans or gods. He is greedy, lustful, and malicious, but also foolish and easily deceived. He is not entirely evil; he is a force of chaos and temptation, testing the morality of humans. He is also a guardian of the underworld and the dead.

Domains & Habitats

Velnias dwells in the underworld, called Velniava or Velns' realm, but he frequently comes to the surface to meddle in human affairs. He is associated with forests, marshes, crossroads, and bodies of water. He is also linked to storms and fire, and is the enemy of the thunder god Perkūnas.

Version Differences

In Lithuanian tradition, Velnias is more clearly a devil figure, often subordinate to God or Perkūnas. In Latvian tradition, he is more ambiguous, sometimes appearing as a trickster hero or a culture hero who challenges the gods. The Christian influence is stronger in later versions, where he is equated with Satan.

Archive Dossier — Powers

Shapeshifting

Source Version Common in both Lithuanian and Latvian folklore

Description

Velnias can transform into various animals, objects, or even humans to deceive or tempt others.

Limitations

He may be forced to reveal his true form if his name is spoken or if he is confronted with iron or holy symbols.

Contradictions

Some tales show him as a master of disguise, while others show him being easily tricked due to his vanity.

Control over wealth and treasure

Source Version Latvian and Lithuanian tales

Description

Velnias is often associated with hidden treasures, gold, and riches. He may offer wealth in exchange for a soul or a service.

Limitations

The wealth he gives often turns to leaves, stones, or dung after a short time, or comes with a curse.

Contradictions

He is sometimes depicted as a wealthy lord, but also as a miserly hoarder.

Supernatural strength and speed

Source Version Various tales

Description

Velnias is physically powerful and can move quickly, often outrunning humans or gods.

Limitations

He can be outwitted by cleverness or by the use of specific objects like a needle in a haystack.

Contradictions

Despite his strength, he is often shown as clumsy or easily fooled.

Cursing and hexing

Source Version Folklore and folk magic

Description

Velnias can place curses on people, animals, or crops, causing illness, misfortune, or death.

Limitations

His curses can be broken by counter-spells, holy water, or the help of a wise person (zintnieks).

Contradictions

Some curses are permanent unless the victim outwits him.

Underworld authority

Source Version Lithuanian and Latvian beliefs

Description

Velnias rules over the dead and the underworld, judging souls or tormenting sinners.

Limitations

He cannot keep a soul if a deity or a clever mortal intervenes.

Contradictions

He is sometimes seen as a mere punisher, not a judge, with the final judgment left to God.

Archive Dossier — Relationships

Perkūnas

Type enemySource Lithuanian and Latvian mythology

Description

Perkūnas is the thunder god and the arch-enemy of Velnias. They are locked in an eternal battle, with Perkūnas chasing Velnias and striking him with lightning.

Version Note

This is a central mythic opposition, similar to Perun and Veles in Slavic mythology.

Conflicting Accounts

Some sources say Velnias is a servant of Perkūnas, while others say he is an equal opponent.

Dievs

Type creatorSource Latvian mythology

Description

Dievs is the sky god and creator in Baltic mythology. Velnias is often seen as his opposite or adversary, but also as his helper in some creation myths.

Version Note

In some creation myths, Velns helps Dievs create the world by diving into the primordial sea to bring up mud.

Conflicting Accounts

Velns is sometimes portrayed as a fallen angel or a trickster who tries to ruin Dievs' work.

Laima

Type allySource Latvian mythology

Description

Laima is the goddess of fate and fortune. In some tales, she works with Velnias to determine the destiny of humans, though she is generally benevolent.

Version Note

They are both associated with the underworld and the afterlife, but Laima is not evil.

Conflicting Accounts

Some tales show Laima opposing Velnias, helping humans escape his clutches.

Saule

Type victimSource Latvian and Lithuanian mythology

Description

Saule is the sun goddess. Velnias often tries to steal or extinguish her, but he is always thwarted by Perkūnas or other gods.

Version Note

This is a common motif in Baltic folklore, where the devil is associated with darkness and cold.

Conflicting Accounts

In some stories, Saule is his daughter or wife, but this is rare.

Humans

Type victimSource Folklore

Description

Velnias frequently interacts with humans, tempting them, bargaining with them, or trying to capture their souls. He is often outwitted by clever peasants.

Version Note

Humans can be his victims or his opponents, depending on the tale.

Conflicting Accounts

Some tales show him as a harsh master to those who serve him, while others show him as a foolish being who can be easily tricked.
