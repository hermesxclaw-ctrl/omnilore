Who It Is

Hel is not the devil. She is older, stranger, and far more bureaucratic than the Christian Hell that borrowed her name. As the daughter of a trickster and a giantess, she rules an underworld of mist and bureaucracy where the non-heroic dead file paperwork between a dish called Hunger and a knife called Famine. The most powerful goddess in Norse mythology never got a movie—but she did inspire a Marvel villain, a video-game pantheon, and a 2017 naming ban in Iceland.

Hel is portrayed as the stern, impartial administrator of an underworld bureaucracy. She does not maliciously torment the dead; rather, she provides 'board and lodging' for those assigned to her. Her domain is not a hell of punishment but a realm of reception for the non-heroic dead. In the Baldr episode, she acts within the bounds of a binding contractual condition (universal weeping), demonstrating that her authority is procedural rather than arbitrary. Powers
Underworld Sovereignty

Hel holds absolute authority over her namesake realm, delegated by Odin himself. She administers board and lodging to all dead who come to her—those who perish by sickness, old age, or non-heroic causes. Her jurisdiction spans nine worlds, indicating cosmological scope rather than mere local rule.

Limit:

Reception and Custody of the Dead

Unlike Valkyries who select slain warriors, Hel receives the passively dead. She maintains vast mansions with many servants, suggesting a bureaucratic afterlife rather than a chaotic one. The Poetic Edda repeatedly uses 'in Hel' as a euphemism for death, confirming her function as a posthumous destination.

Limit:

Conditional Release Authority

In the Baldr episode, Hel possesses the power to release the dead back to the living world, but only if the stipulated condition (universal weeping) is met. This contractual magic demonstrates that her authority operates within Norse juridical-magical conventions.

Limit:

Ragnarök Host Leadership

The Prose Edda foretells that when Loki breaks free at Ragnarök, 'all of Hel's people' will arrive with him at the battlefield Vígríðr. This implies command over a substantial dead army, positioning her as a military leader in the eschatalogical battle.

Limit:

Theriomorphic / Bipartite Embodiment

Hel's half-blue, half-flesh-coloured form represents a composite of living and dead, warm and cold. Scholars connect this to Indo-European death-goddess imagery (Kali, Badb, Morrígan) where the terrifying appearance masks a maternal or judicial function.

Limit:

She is further characterized as 'rather downcast and fierce-looking', a demeanor that reflects her domain over the dead. Accounts I
The Banishment to NiflheimProse Edda, Gylfaginning ch. 34 (Faulkes 1995:26–27)

When the gods discovered that Loki and Angrboða's three children—Hel, Fenrir, and Jörmungandr—would bring great mischief, Odin intervened. He cast Jörmungandr into the ocean surrounding Midgard, placed Fenrir in bonds, and threw Hel into Niflheim. There Odin appointed her ruler over nine worlds, charging her to administer lodging to all who die of sickness or old age. This banishment mirrors the binding of Fenrir and the casting of Jörmungandr: a recurring Norse motif in which threatening beings are confined but destined to break free at Ragnarök. Hel's appointment is not merely punitive; it establishes her as a necessary cosmological functionary.

Hermóðr's Ride to Hel and the Baldr RansomProse Edda, Gylfaginning ch. 49 (Byock 2005:68–69; Faulkes 1995:49–50)

After Baldr's death, Frigg asked who would ride to Hel to offer a ransom for Baldr's return. Hermóðr volunteered and mounted Odin's eight-legged horse Sleipnir. He rode for nine nights through deep, dark valleys and reached the river Gjöll, crossed the bridge Gjallarbrú, and entered Hel's hall. He found Baldr there and stayed the night. In the morning, he begged Hel to let Baldr return, weeping for the Æsir's grief. Hel set a condition: if all things in the world, alive or dead, wept for Baldr, he could return; if anyone refused, he would remain with her. The Æsir sent messengers throughout the world, and everything wept—except the giantess Þökk, who refused. She responded in verse: 'let Hel hold what she has.' Thus Baldr remained in the underworld, and Hel's authority was affirmed.

Accounts II
Hel's Host at RagnarökProse Edda, Gylfaginning ch. 51 (Faulkes 1995:54)

The Prose Edda foretells that at Ragnarök, Loki will arrive at the battlefield Vígríðr accompanied by 'all of Hel's people.' This confirms that Hel commands a substantial host drawn from her underworld population. She fights alongside the forces of chaos against the Æsir, fulfilling the pattern established by her siblings Fenrir (who kills Odin) and Jörmungandr (who poisons Thor). The three children of Loki and Angrboða thus constitute a unified apocalyptic threat, each attacking from a different domain: sky (Fenrir), sea (Jörmungandr), and underworld (Hel).

Hel Takes the Kings of SwedenHeimskringla, Ynglinga saga chs. 17, 45–47 (Hollander 2007:20–21, 46–47)

The 9th- and 10th-century Ynglingatal poems, preserved in Snorri's Heimskringla, repeatedly invoke Hel as the collector of Swedish royal dead. In chapter 17, Hel takes King Dyggvi 'to whore with him'; in chapter 45 she is 'howes'-warder' who takes King Halfdan Hvitbeinn; in chapter 46, King Eystein Halfdansson 'fared to' Hel; in chapter 47, King Halfdan the Mild is summoned to her thing. These skaldic kennings treat Hel as an active, almost predatory collector of high-status dead, not a passive repository.

Egils saga: 'Death, Close Sister of Odin's Enemy'Egils saga, Sonatorrek (Scudder 2001:159)

In Egils saga (13th c., based on 10th-century tradition), the skald Egill Skallagrímsson composes the poem Sonatorrek after his son Gunnar dies. The final stanza obliquely names Hel as 'Death, close sister of Odin's enemy'—an epithet that links Hel to Loki (Odin's enemy) through sibling relation, while avoiding direct naming. The stanza expresses Egill's resigned acceptance of death, framing Hel as an inevitable kin-relation rather than a villain. This passage is significant because it reflects a 10th-century heathen poetic tradition predating Snorri's more elaborate Gylfaginning portrait.
