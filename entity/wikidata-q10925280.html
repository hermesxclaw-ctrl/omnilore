Who It Is

He is white as a Himalayan dawn, four arms moving at once - two pressed together in prayer at his heart, one lifting a crystal rosary that never stops turning, one holding a lotus that never wilts. He does not speak. He hums. Six syllables - Om Ma Ni Pad Me Hum - spill from him and, Buddhists say, each syllable reaches into a different realm of suffering to pull someone out. This is Shadakshari Lokeshvara, not a different god from the great compassionate Avalokiteshvara, but Avalokiteshvara distilled to sound itself: the Lord of the Six Syllables.

Attested personality is that of Mahākaruṇā (Great Compassion) itself. He is the bodhisattva who vowed not to rest until all beings are freed, who weeps for suffering and whose tears become Taras. He is approachable, gentle, and active - not wrathful. His behavior is to hear (avalokita) the cries (svara) of the world (loka) and respond instantly. In Karandavyuha, he is cosmocratic: from his body emanate gods, worlds, and even other buddhas. Powers
Ṣaḍakṣarī Mahāvidyā - Power of the Six Syllables

Embodiment and lordship over the mantra Om Mani Padme Hum. Recitation, hearing, seeing, or touching the mantra - even in written form - is said to purify negative karma, close rebirth in lower realms, and plant seeds of enlightenment. In Karandavyuha, the Buddha declares that reciting this vidya is superior to performing countless other dharanis.

Limit: Efficacy depends on faith and bodhicitta motivation in later commentaries; not a mechanical spell. Some scholastic sources (e.g., Tsongkhapa's Lamrim) stress that mantra alone without understanding emptiness does not confer full buddhahood.

Purification of the Six Realms (Ṣaḍgati-viśodhana)

Each syllable purifies a specific realm and its root affliction: Om (gods/pride), Ma (asuras/jealousy), Ni (humans/desire), Pad (animals/ignorance), Me (pretas/greed), Hum (hell beings/hatred). Light of corresponding color radiates from his body to each realm. Meditators visualize this during sadhana.

Limit: Doctrinal mapping varies slightly between sources; some swap Pad/Me associations. Function is soteriological, not literal teleportation.

Cosmocratic Emanation

In Karandavyuha, Avalokiteshvara as Shadakshari emanates the entire cosmos from his body: sun and moon from his eyes, Shiva and Vishnu from his shoulders, Brahma from his forehead, etc. He generates buddhas and bodhisattvas innumerable.

Limit: Mythic hyperbole; not taken as literal cosmogony in later Madhyamaka philosophy, but as skillful means (upaya) to show his supremacy within that sutra's rhetoric.

Compassionate Hearing (Avalokita-svara)

Ability to hear all cries of suffering simultaneously and manifest in whatever form needed to aid beings - as human, animal, bridge, medicine, or mantra. Basis for his title 'He who looks down with compassion.'

Limit: Response is conditioned on karma and receptivity of beings; not all suffering is instantly ended.

Protection and Wish-Fulfillment (Raksha and Cintamani)

In sadhana, the jewel held at his heart (when depicted) grants wishes and protects from fear, poison, weapons, and untimely death. His rosary subdues obstacles. Devotees wear his mantra for protection in travel and at death (liberation through wearing - btags grol).

Limit: Protection is karmic/spiritual; not claimed as physical invulnerability in orthodox exegesis.

Emanation Lineage (Nirmanakaya)

Ability to emanate as kings, lamas, and teachers to guide beings. Most famously, King Songtsen Gampo, Princess Bhrikuti, Princess Wencheng, and all Dalai Lamas are considered emanations of Shadakshari/Four-Armed Chenrezig.

Limit: This is a retrospective hagiographic claim, not a historically verifiable power; scholarly historians treat as legitimation narrative.

Accounts I
The Descent of the Six Syllables - Kāraṇḍavyūha SūtraKāraṇḍavyūha Sūtra (Sanskrit Mahayana Sutra)

4th-8th century CE

The Buddha Shakyamuni, on Jetavana, tells how the bodhisattva Avalokiteshvara entered the hell Avici to teach the six-syllable vidya. The Buddha searches for the vidya, and is told even the Buddha Vairocana does not possess it without Avalokiteshvara's permission. Finally Avalokiteshvara reveals Om Mani Padme Hum, and the entire cosmos shakes. The sutra declares that merely hearing the six syllables, or seeing them written, or touching them, ensures rebirth in Sukhavati and closes the doors to the six realms. Avalokiteshvara's body is revealed as the source of all deities: Brahma from his forehead, Vishnu from his shoulder, Shiva from his eyes, Saraswati from his teeth, etc., establishing his supremacy. Establishes scriptural authority for the six-syllable mantra and for Shadakshari as the supreme compassionate savior. Becomes the locus classicus for all later Shadakshari worship and the doctrinal basis for mani stones and prayer wheels.

The Conversion of Tibet - Songtsen Gampo as Shadakshari's EmanationMani Kambum (Mani bKa' 'bum) and Pillar of Mani (Ma ni bka' 'bum) - Tibetan terma literature

Attributed to 7th century, compiled 11th-13th century

Avalokiteshvara, in the form of Shadakshari, vows to tame the demoness of Tibet (Srin mo) whose body is the Tibetan plateau. He emanates as King Songtsen Gampo (7th century historical king), while his consort Tara emanates as the king's two wives Bhrikuti (Nepali princess) and Wencheng (Chinese princess), who bring Buddha images. Songtsen Gampo builds the Jokhang and Ramoche temples to pin down the demoness's limbs, establishes the Tibetan script through Thonmi Sambhota, and disseminates the six-syllable mantra. The king is revealed to be Shadakshari himself, his wives are Tara's forms, and his ministers are other bodhisattvas. The story makes Tibet a mandala and Shadakshari its lord. Provides mythic charter for Tibetan Buddhism's identity as Avalokiteshvara's field (zhing) and for the Dalai Lama lineage as continuing emanations of Shadakshari. Every subsequent Dalai Lama is understood as Shadakshari/Four-Armed Chenrezig incarnate.

The Sadhana Vision - How the Four-Armed White Lord Should Be VisualizedSādhanamālā Sādhana 16: Ṣaḍakṣarī Lokeśvara Sādhana

7th-12th century CE

The text instructs the sadhaka: 'One should visualize oneself as Shadakshari Lokesvara, white as crystal, four-armed, with matted hair piled high, bearing Amitabha in his crown. With two hands in anjali, and with the other two holding a crystal rosary and a lotus. Seated on a lotus and moon disc, radiating light that purifies the six realms. One should recite the six syllables Om Mani Padme Hum.' The sadhana then details nyasa (placing syllables on body parts), mandala construction with the six syllables around, and the resulting siddhis: eloquence, long life, and ultimately rebirth in Sukhavati. It is not a narrative story but a ritual script that tells the practitioner to become the deity. Fixes the canonical iconography that survives in all later bronzes, thangkas, and temple images. Becomes the basis for daily sadhana in both Newar and Tibetan lineages to the present.

Accounts II
The Six Lights - Purifying the Six RealmsTibetan oral and commentarial tradition, systematized in Patrul Rinpoche's Kunzang Lame Shyalung (Words of My Perfect Teacher) and earlier in Karma Chagme's Mountain Dharma

Systematized ca. 11th-19th century, rooted in Karandavyuha

A teaching story told to meditators: When Shadakshari recites Om, white light from Om purifies the god realm and pride; Ma, green light, purifies the asura realm and jealousy; Ni, yellow light, purifies the human realm and desire; Pad, blue light, purifies the animal realm and ignorance; Me, red light, purifies the preta realm and greed; Hum, black/blue light, purifies the hell realm and hatred. As each syllable is recited, beings of that realm are lifted out and dissolve into his heart. The story is enacted in visualization: the practitioner imagines Shadakshari above their head, nectar and light streaming down, cleansing their own six afflictions. It transforms the abstract mantra into a narrative of rescue. Provides the standard meditation instruction for Om Mani Padme Hum recitation used by millions daily. Explains why prayer wheels and mani stones are placed in the landscape - to radiate this purifying light to all beings who pass.

The Mani Stone and the Old Woman's Tooth - The Power of Faith Over FormTibetan folk and didactic tale, recorded in many collections, e.g., Khenpo Konchog Gyaltsen, The Jewel Ornament of Liberation commentary stories; also in Dilgo Khyentse Rinpoche teachings

Oral tradition, recorded 19th-20th century

An old woman asks her son, a merchant traveling to India, to bring her a relic of the Buddha. He forgets twice. The third time, fearing her disappointment, he picks up a dog's tooth by the roadside, wraps it in silk, and tells her it is the Buddha's tooth. The woman places it on her altar and prays with intense faith to Shadakshari, reciting Om Mani Padme Hum before the 'relic.' The tooth begins to emit rainbow light and relics. When the son confesses, the light does not fade. Masters explain: it was not the tooth but her faith in the Buddha and in Shadakshari's compassion that made it powerful. The story is used to teach that devotion to Shadakshari and his mantra matters more than material authenticity. Illustrates the Tibetan teaching of 'faith as the cause' (dad pa) in Shadakshari practice and justifies the proliferation of mani relics, stones, and prints as supports for faith rather than mere superstition.

The Golden Temple's Lord - Hiranya Varna Mahavihar and the Living ShadakshariNewar Buddhist chronicles (vamsavalis) and living ritual practice of Patan, Nepal

Temple founded 12th century, continuous worship to present

At Hiranya Varna Mahavihar (Golden Temple) in Patan, the main image is a gilt Shadakshari Lokeshvara, considered the temple's living lord. Chronicles tell that the image was established by King Bhaskara Varman and that the mantra Om Mani Padme Hum was inscribed on the temple's prayer wheels surrounding the courtyard. During the month of Gunla (July-August), Newar Buddhists circumambulate the valley reciting the six syllables before this image, and during daily worship, the priest (Vajracharya) visualizes himself as Shadakshari. The image is bathed, dressed, and fed as a living king. Pilgrims believe seeing (darshan) this Shadakshari purifies sight itself. Shows the continuity of Shadakshari worship in a living urban Buddhist tradition outside Tibet, and explains why Patan's metalworkers are renowned for Shadakshari bronzes exported worldwide.

Number of arms and attributesShadakshari is strictly four-armed, white, with anjali, mala, and lotus (Sadhanamala 16).Some Sadhanamala manuscripts and Newar bronzes show variations: two-armed forms labeled Shadakshari, or four-armed forms where the jewel replaces the mala, or where a book is held.
Meaning and translation of the six syllablesOm Mani Padme Hum is translatable as 'Om, the Jewel in the Lotus, Hum' (classic 19th-century translation).The mantra is a vidya/dharani, not a translatable sentence; 'mani padme' is a vocative or locative with esoteric rather than grammatical meaning, and the six syllables function as purifiers of six realms, not as a proposition.
Origin of the mantra and deity - Indian vs. Tibetan inventionShadakshari and Om Mani Padme Hum are Tibetan inventions, created to indigenize Buddhism.Both originate in Indian Mahayana (Karandavyuha Sutra) centuries before transmission to Tibet, and were systematized in Indian sadhana literature before Tibetan adoption.
Shadakshari as independent deity vs. form of AvalokiteshvaraShadakshari is a distinct bodhisattva, separate from Avalokiteshvara, counted among 108 Lokeshvaras.Shadakshari is Avalokiteshvara himself in his six-syllable aspect; not a different being.

Shadakshari Lokeshvara is a Hindu god or a form of Shiva/Vishnu because of the name 'Ishvara/Lord.' — Lokesvara here is a Buddhist bodhisattva title meaning 'Lord of the World' (loka + isvara), not the Hindu god Shiva (also called Ishvara). In Karandavyuha, Shiva and Vishnu are actually shown as emanations from Avalokiteshvara, not the other way around. The Buddhist Lokesvara is a compassionate savior, not a creator/destroyer deity.

Om Mani Padme Hum literally means 'Om, the jewel in the lotus, hum' and describes a jewel inside a flower. — This is a forced grammatical parsing from the 19th century. The mantra is a vidya (magical knowledge/sonic embodiment), not a descriptive sentence. Traditional exegesis interprets each syllable as purifying a realm/affliction, and 'mani padme' is better understood as vocative ('O Jewel-Lotus One') or as symbolic union of compassion (jewel) and wisdom (lotus), not a physical jewel in a flower.

Shadakshari is a separate, lesser deity from Chenrezig/Avalokiteshvara. — Shadakshari is Chenrezig/Avalokiteshvara himself in his most essential mantra form. Tibetan 'Chenrezig Chagdakpa' (Four-Armed Chenrezig) IS Shadakshari. The six syllables are his heart mantra; to meditate on Shadakshari is to meditate on Avalokiteshvara.

The four arms represent four different powers or four gods combined. — The four arms are iconographic shorthand for all-pervading compassion reaching in four directions, and specifically for the two primary functions: prayer/devotion (anjali) and compassionate activity (mala for continuous prayer, lotus for purity). They do not represent four separate deities fused together, though his crown does show his connection to Amitabha.

Reciting Om Mani Padme Hum is just a calming meditation with no doctrinal content. — In its own tradition, recitation is a complete path: each syllable is linked to purification of a realm, a paramita (generosity, ethics, etc.), and a wisdom. The mantra is said to contain the entire Mahayana path in six sounds. Its use on prayer wheels and mani stones is not decoration but a technology for spreading compassion to all beings who see, touch, or hear it.
