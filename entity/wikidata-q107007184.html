The Approach

In the sky above the earth, the high god Wulbari once dwelt so close that the first humans could reach up and touch the fabric of heaven itself. But a single act of human carelessness—an offending hand, a splash of dirty water—severed that intimacy forever, and Wulbari withdrew to a distant celestial realm, leaving humanity to toil below. This is the story of how the world lost its heavenly neighbor, and what the Krachi people of West Africa remember about the god who turned away.

Identity

Wulbari

Pronunciation Not recorded in available sources. Likely /wʊlˈbɑːri/ in English approximation, but this is speculative.

Etymology

The name 'Wulbari' is often glossed as 'God' or 'the Supreme Being' in the Krachi language. The exact linguistic components are not clearly documented in available sources; it may be a compound or a proper name.

Cultural Ownership

Wulbari is a sky god of the Krachi people, who live in the Oti Region of Ghana (formerly Volta Region) and parts of Togo. The Krachi language is a Guang language of the Niger-Congo family.

Disputed Identifications

Some comparative studies link Wulbari to other West African sky gods, such as Nyame of the Akan or Mawu of the Ewe, but these identifications are speculative and not confirmed by Krachi sources. Wulbari is not the same as the Oti-Volta river spirit or any other local deity, but confusion may arise in pan-African myth overviews.

Description

Appearance

Wulbari is described as residing in the sky, and is often conceived anthropomorphically as a powerful, distant male figure. No specific physical form is detailed in available sources; he is more of a celestial presence than a visual deity.

Symbols & Iconography

The sky itself is the primary symbol of Wulbari. He is associated with the heavens, rain, and thunder, though specific iconographic representations are not documented. In Krachi culture, he may be invoked in prayers for rain or good fortune.

Behaviour & Personality

Wulbari is portrayed as a beneficent but remote creator who originally lived close to humans, but after an offense, he withdrew to the sky, becoming distant and less directly involved in human affairs. He is not considered malicious, but rather justly offended and now inaccessible.

Domains & Habitats

Wulbari's domain is the sky, specifically the upper atmosphere. He is a celestial deity, not tied to a specific earthly location, though he is worshipped by the Krachi people.

Version Differences

There may be regional variations in the myth across Krachi communities, but these are not documented. Some sources may conflate Wulbari with other sky gods, leading to inconsistencies in descriptions.

Stories & Myths

The Withdrawal of Wulbari

Source Oral tradition of the Krachi people; documented in secondary sourcesCulture Krachi (Ghana/Togo)Period Pre-colonial; exact dating unknown

Summary

In the beginning, Wulbari lived so close to the earth that the sky was within arm's reach of the first people. However, a woman (or in some versions, a man) was pounding fufu with a long pestle, and the pestle struck the sky, or a woman's hand touched the sky while she was working, and she wiped her dirty hands on it. Offended by this pollution, Wulbari withdrew to the distant heavens, leaving humanity to live apart from him. Since then, he has been remote and is only approached through prayers and rituals.

Participants

Wulbari

First woman (or man)

First humans

Consequences

The separation of heaven and earth; humans lost direct access to the divine; the need for intermediaries (priests, rituals) arose.

Variants

Some versions involve a woman's hand touching the sky; others involve a pestle striking it. The exact event varies, but the outcome is the same.

Human offense and divine withdrawal

Source Oral tradition

Description

The myth explains the distance between God and humanity as a consequence of human carelessness or pollution. The act of wiping dirty hands on the sky is a breach of taboo, leading to the loss of paradise-like proximity.

Scholarly Context

This myth is a common etiological tale explaining why the sky is far away, found in many African cultures. It reflects cultural values of purity and respect for the divine.
