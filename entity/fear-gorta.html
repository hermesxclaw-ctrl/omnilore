Who It Is

The fear gorta is famine given feet: an emaciated phantom — skin over bone, hand outstretched — who walks the roads of Ireland in hungry times, begging alms door to door. And here is the extraordinary thing about him, recorded by Yeats and the folk tradition alike: he is a test, not a predator. Give to the walking skeleton — a crust, a coin, anything from whatever little you have — and he brings good luck and prosperity; the giver's family, the tales insist, does not starve. Turn him away, and want follows you in his place. In a country that lost a million people to the Great Hunger, the fear gorta is folklore doing moral bookkeeping at the door: generosity in scarcity is the one virtue famine cannot digest. His companion phenomenon is stranger still — the fear gortach or hungry grass: patches of ground, said to grow where a famine victim died unshriven or where the fear gorta passed, that curse anyone who walks across them with sudden, bottomless, supernatural hunger — a starvation that strikes in seconds, miles from any table. Country people carried a crust of bread in the pocket crossing suspect fields; eating a bite the instant the weakness hit broke the spell. The fear gorta asks at the door what the hungry grass demands underfoot: remember what hunger was, carry bread, and share it — or the famine walks again, and this time it knows your name.

Death & Rising

Irish tradition entwined with the famines of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, collected by Yeats (Fairy and Folk Tales of the Irish Peasantry) and rural memory; the hungry-grass belief was reported as living practice into the twentieth century.

Attested in Irish oral tradition; Yeats's collectionsEra Recorded 19th century; tradition older

Form

A walking skeleton in rags, grey-skinned, eyes deep in the skull, one thin hand out for alms — indistinguishable at the door, and this is the point, from the human starving he commemorates.

Powers

The prosperity-blessing of the generous and the ruin of the refusing; the sowing of hungry grass; and the moral X-ray of every household he knocks at — the fear gorta always knows what you could have spared.

Weaknesses & Wards

He is not fought but answered: alms disarm him utterly. Against the hungry grass, a crust in the pocket is complete protection — folklore's cheapest insurance policy.

Field Notes

Cultural Significance

The fear gorta and the hungry grass are Ireland's folklore of famine memory — the Great Hunger encoded as a walking examiner and a cursed ground — and the crust-in-the-pocket custom is remembrance made portable.
