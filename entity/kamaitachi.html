A trio of weasel-like yōkai that ride the wind and attack travelers with invisible, painless cuts. The first knocks the victim down, the second slices with razor-sharp claws, and the third applies a healing salve — all in an instant, leaving the victim with bleeding wounds but no pain.

Who It Is

A trio of weasel-like yōkai that ride the wind and attack travelers with invisible, painless cuts. The first knocks the victim down, the second slices with razor-sharp claws, and the third applies a healing salve — all in an instant, leaving the victim with bleeding wounds but no pain.

Form

Three weasel-like creatures with sickle-like claws; sometimes depicted riding dust devils or whirlwinds; sharp, angular features

Powers

Wind-riding

Invisible cutting attacks

Instantaneous wound healing

Sickle-claw attacks

Wind-cutting / sickle-claw slash Rides dust devils and cuts victims with sickle-like front claws, leaving a sharp but initially painless, bloodless wound, contradictorily followed by intense pain and heavy bleeding. Sekien standard; Hokusai-adjacent folk canon; Mimibukuro razor/sickle limbs Per Miyoshi Shōzan cannot jump higher than about 1 shaku (~30 cm), hence injuries cluster low on the body; treatable by practiced medics. Painless-and-bloodless vs later exacting pain and heavy bleeding (reconciled by the 'medicine-god' explanation in Hida and the 'blood-sucking' explanation in Aichi).

Invisibility In Nara's Yoshio District the creature is invisible to the human eye; one is 'bit' and tumbles over with a large flesh-opening but no external blood. Nara Prefecture (Yoshio District) Regional only; in other areas a manifest animal/wind is reported. Whether the creature is visible differs by region.

Whirlwind / dust-devil riding Manifests within or as whirlwinds and dust-devils (tsumujikaze), and is closely tied to cold wind and snow-country conditions; in some regions the whirlwind itself is called 'kamaitachi'. Kōshin'etsu, Chūbu, Kinki, snowy-region traditions Winds-to-sickle association seasonal and geographic. Whether it causes the wind or merely rides it is unresolved across traditions.

Blood-sucking (izuna variant) In eastern Aichi the runaway izuna (飯綱 weasel-spirit) that was never properly sealed rides whirlwinds and attacks people to suck their living blood — the recorded reason the cuts appear bloodless. Eastern Aichi Prefecture (idzuna/izuna) Regional and tied to the izuna-tsukai shamanic practice; distinct from the standard kamaitachi etiology. Directly competes with the Hida three-gods 'medicine applied' explanation for bloodlessness.

Shapeshifting / haunted-tool agency (tsukumogami form) In western Japan a forgotten grass-cutting sickle (or, in Iya/Tokushima, a grave-digging sickle or hoe left out seven days) turns into a nogama yōkai that cuts people; a 'wild sickle' that is the tool's vengeful spirit (onryō). A jealous woman's severed hair likewise became a kamaitachi that decapitated her rival. Kōchi, Tokushima (Iya), Ōme anecdotes Tsukumogami causation limited to western-Japanese no-kama and the Ōme hair anecdote. Conflicts with the weasel identity: the true cause may be a haunted object rather than an animal-spirit.

Curse / after-effect malady Distant analogues: yama-misaki (Yamaguchi) causes severe fever on encounter; Amami Ōshima's lukewarm-graveyard-wind leaves body speckles then fever requiring a yuta shaman's exorcism; muchi (Kōchi) kills horses and cattle at night on the roads (countered by covering the animals' eyes). Yamaguchi, Amami Ōshima, Kōchi regional traditions These are related but distinct folkloric phenomena grouped under kamaitachi family folklore. Whether they are the same entity is explicitly disputed in the sources.

Shikigami / familiar-use (izuna-tsukai) The kamaitachi's weasel kin, izuna, function as shikigami familiars in izuna-tsukai / kitsune-mochi practice (Tōhoku, Shinshū); a runaway unsealed izuna becomes the attacking weather-spirit that is called kamaitachi in Aichi. Tōhoku/Shinshū izuna-tsukai; Weasel cultural context Applied to the izuna form rather than the canonical sickle-weasel; reflects the wider weasel-as-familiar folklore. None recorded in source; linkage is linguistic/folkloric.

Behavior & Habitat

Attack is so fast it's imperceptible; the cuts bleed profusely but cause no pain; considered a natural explanation for mysterious cuts found after wind storms

Accounts

In the Niu River basin of Hida, the kamaitachi is said to be a company of three evil gods who work in sequence: the first knocks the victim down, the second cuts them with a blade, and the third applies medicine to the wound. This is why a kamaitachi wound does not bleed or hurt. Some regions instead see these three as a parent, child, and brother acting together as a family of wind-deities.

A woman whose lover had been stolen by a rival, consumed by resentment, cut off her own hair. That hair transformed into a kamaitachi and decapitated the rival with a single stroke.

A giant mantis that once lived at the 'Mantis Slope' (Kamakiri-zaka) hill was crushed to death under heavy snow. Since then, anyone who falls on the hill may receive a wound as if cut by a sickle, with black blood flowing out — attributed to the mantis's curse.

Miyoshi Shozan recorded that kamaitachi wounds are 'painless and bloodless at first, then intensely painful and bleeding,' so much that sometimes the bone shows, with risk of death, and that because the wounds are usually low, the creature cannot jump above a shaku. This quasi-medical/'evidentiary' account, later absorbed into Meiji yokaigaku, transformed an amorphous folk fear into a testable, bodied creature — the platform on which both rationalist debunking (it is a torn-skin/cold injury) and artistic reimagination (Hosoe) rest.

In Shin'etsu, a kamaitachi is said to be the work of an evil god, and one who steps on a calendar is held fated to encounter calamity. Counted among the 'seven mysteries of Echigo,' the injury is treated across the Tōhoku by burning an old calendar black and pressing it on the wound to heal it.

Japanese folklore (yōkai)
A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine a fine, invisible blade that flicks across your shin while you idle in a cold field — no sting, no blood — and only hours later does the flesh bloom open, deep enough to show bone, while a breeze that was never there finishes its dance down the road. That is the kamaitachi: a Japanese field-spirit too small to see (barely a foot tall, if it has a body at all) that slices first and lets the pain arrive on a delay. Whether it is truly a sickle-clawed weasel, a cursed farming tool that grew a temper, the winged ghost of a monstrous mantis, or merely a name borrowed from a Chinese 'beast of exhaustion' to explain why the mountain wind cuts so sharply, seems to depend entirely on which prefecture is telling you the story — and every version agrees the wound does not bleed until it is too late.
