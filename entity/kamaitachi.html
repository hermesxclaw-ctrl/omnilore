Who It Is

Imagine a fine, invisible blade that flicks across your shin while you idle in a cold field — no sting, no blood — and only hours later does the flesh bloom open, deep enough to show bone, while a breeze that was never there finishes its dance down the road. That is the kamaitachi: a Japanese field-spirit too small to see (barely a foot tall, if it has a body at all) that slices first and lets the pain arrive on a delay. Whether it is truly a sickle-clawed weasel, a cursed farming tool that grew a temper, the winged ghost of a monstrous mantis, or merely a name borrowed from a Chinese 'beast of exhaustion' to explain why the mountain wind cuts so sharply, seems to depend entirely on which prefecture is telling you the story — and every version agrees the wound does not bleed until it is too late.

A wind-spirit that 'cuts' humans without warning. Its wounds are classically painless and bloodless at the instant of the cut — a signature that folk-medicine immediately recognized — after which intense pain and heavy bleeding (sometimes bone-exposing, occasionally fatal) set in. A Hida (Niu River basin) legend frames it as a coordinated trio of three evil gods: the first knocks the victim down, the second slashes with a blade, and the third applies medicine, which is why the wound neither bleeds nor hurts. Kamaitachi rides on dust devils and whirlwinds, striking when one is idle

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name is a compound of kama (鎌, sickle) + itachi (鼬, weasel). Originally, however, the term was thought to be a corruption of kamae tachi (構太刀, 'stance sword'), a fencing posture — an etiological folk-explanation that re-interpreted an invisible wind-blade injury as the work of a named creature.

Pronunciation — [ka.ma.iꜜ.ta.tɕi] — four-syllable compound, pitch-accent falling on /i/ of the third syllable.

Aliases — kama-itachi, kamakaze (鎌風, sickle-wind, Musashi/Kanagawa), kazakama (風鎌, wind-sickle, western Japan), nogama (野鎌, wild-sickle, western Japan/Kōchi/Tokushima), akuzen-kaze (悪旋風, evil whirlwind, Shizuoka), idzuna/izuna (飯綱, eastern Aichi)

Ownership — Japanese yōkai of the Kōshin'etsu, Chūbu, Kinki, Tōhoku, and western Japanese regional traditions; established in Edo-period print and encyclopedic sources (Sekien's Gazu Hyakki Ya

Powers
Wind-cutting / sickle-claw slash

Rides dust devils and cuts victims with sickle-like front claws, leaving a sharp but initially painless, bloodless wound, contradictorily followed by intense pain and heavy bleeding.

Limit: Per Miyoshi Shōzan cannot jump higher than about 1 shaku (~30 cm), hence injuries cluster low on the body; treatable by practiced medics.

Invisibility

In Nara's Yoshio District the creature is invisible to the human eye; one is 'bit' and tumbles over with a large flesh-opening but no external blood.

Limit: Regional only; in other areas a manifest animal/wind is reported.

Whirlwind / dust-devil riding

Manifests within or as whirlwinds and dust-devils (tsumujikaze), and is closely tied to cold wind and snow-country conditions; in some regions the whirlwind itself is called 'kamaitachi'.

Limit: Winds-to-sickle association seasonal and geographic.

Blood-sucking (izuna variant)

In eastern Aichi the runaway izuna (飯綱 weasel-spirit) that was never properly sealed rides whirlwinds and attacks people to suck their living blood — the recorded reason the cuts appear bloodless.

Limit: Regional and tied to the izuna-tsukai shamanic practice; distinct from the standard kamaitachi etiology.

Shapeshifting / haunted-tool agency (tsukumogami form)

In western Japan a forgotten grass-cutting sickle (or, in Iya/Tokushima, a grave-digging sickle or hoe left out seven days) turns into a nogama yōkai that cuts people; a 'wild sickle' that is the tool's vengeful spirit (onryō). A jealous woman's severed hair likewise became a kamaitachi that decapitated her rival.

Limit: Tsukumogami causation limited to western-Japanese no-kama and the Ōme hair anecdote.

Curse / after-effect malady

Distant analogues: yama-misaki (Yamaguchi) causes severe fever on encounter; Amami Ōshima's lukewarm-graveyard-wind leaves body speckles then fever requiring a yuta shaman's exorcism; muchi (Kōchi) kills horses and cattle at night on the roads (countered by covering the animals' eyes).

Limit: These are related but distinct folkloric phenomena grouped under kamaitachi family folklore.

Shikigami / familiar-use (izuna-tsukai)

The kamaitachi's weasel kin, izuna, function as shikigami familiars in izuna-tsukai / kitsune-mochi practice (Tōhoku, Shinshū); a runaway unsealed izuna becomes the attacking weather-spirit that is called kamaitachi in Aichi.

Limit: Applied to the izuna form rather than the canonical sickle-weasel; reflects the wider weasel-as-familiar folklore.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In its Sekien-derived folkloric standard, kamaitachi appears as a small invisible or rarely-seen weasel (itachi) with sickle-shaped front claws. Older/alternate accounts describe a flying beast with wings, hedgehog-like fur, and a dog-like cry, attacking with limbs 'like a sickle or razor' (Mimibukuro). Its canonical attribute is the pair of kama (sickles) on its forelimbs, with which it slashes victims. It reaches no more than roughly 1 shaku (~

Symbols — Sickle/kama (harvesting tool), razor-limbs, weasel (鼬) anatomy, whirlwind / dust devil (tsumujikaze). Its canonical provenance runs from Sekien's Gazu Hyakki Yagyō woodblock, which is the single most reproduced image. In Hosoe's 1969 series the 'creature' is symbolized by Hijikata's body in the Tōhoku landscape rather than by a literal animal.

Domains — Agricultural settings, rice paddies, fields, mountain paths, snow-country roads, dust devils, whirlwinds, and post-rain puddles (the latter per Miyoshi Shōzan). Native to the Kōshin'etsu, Shin'etsu/Echigo, Chūbu, Hokuriku, Tōhoku, Kinki (Nara), and western (Kōchi, Tokushima, Yamaguchi) regions. Accounts I
The Three Gods of Hida (the trio on the Niu River)Local legend, Hida Province, Niu River basin

Pre-Meiji oral/recorded folklore

In the Niu River basin of Hida, the kamaitachi is said to be a company of three evil gods who work in sequence: the first knocks the victim down, the second cuts them with a blade, and the third applies medicine to the wound. This is why a kamaitachi wound does not bleed or hurt. Some regions instead see these three as a parent, child, and brother acting together as a family of wind-deities. Establishes the canonical 'painless, bloodless wound' trope and the divine (rather than merely animal) status of the creature in Hida tradition.

The Hair of the Ome Jealous WomanLocal anecdote, Ome, Tokyo

Edo period

A woman whose lover had been stolen by a rival, consumed by resentment, cut off her own hair. That hair transformed into a kamaitachi and decapitated the rival with a single stroke. Links kamaitachi to the classical Japanese motif of resentful spirits (onryō) and cursed hair, and to crimes of passion; a domestic, indoor turn for an otherwise field-dwelling yōkai.

Accounts II
The Mantis Curse of Kamakiri-zakaLocal legend, Katakai, Sando District, Niigata Prefecture

Folk tradition

A giant mantis that once lived at the 'Mantis Slope' (Kamakiri-zaka) hill was crushed to death under heavy snow. Since then, anyone who falls on the hill may receive a wound as if cut by a sickle, with black blood flowing out — attributed to the mantis's curse. Shows the East-Japan tradition that explains a sickle-like wind-injury as the work of an insect ghost rather than a weasel.

Inoue Enryo's yokaigaku reading and the 'evidentiary' injuryYokai discourse / Sosan Chomon Kishu (Miyoshi Shozan)

Edo–Meiji scholarship

Miyoshi Shozan recorded that kamaitachi wounds are 'painless and bloodless at first, then intensely painful and bleeding,' so much that sometimes the bone shows, with risk of death, and that because the wounds are usually low, the creature cannot jump above a shaku. This quasi-medical/'evidentiary' account, later absorbed into Meiji yokaigaku, transformed an amorphous folk fear into a testable, bodied creature — the platform on which both rationalist debunking (it is a torn-skin/cold injury) and artistic reimagination (Hosoe) rest. Provided the precise wound-timeline description (bloodless-then-bleeding) that all later tellings and parodies inherit.

The Echigo seven mysteries and the calendar tabooRegional belief, Echigo/Shin'etsu

Folk tradition

In Shin'etsu, a kamaitachi is said to be the work of an evil god, and one who steps on a calendar is held fated to encounter calamity. Counted among the 'seven mysteries of Echigo,' the injury is treated across the Tōhoku by burning an old calendar black and pressing it on the wound to heal it. Couples the injury with calendar avoidances and a specific healing ritual — prime evidence of the belief's shamanic/ritual texture and its regional codification as one of Echigo's sacred mysteries.
