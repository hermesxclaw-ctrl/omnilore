Who They Are

Tezcatlipoca is one of the four creator gods of Aztec cosmology and arguably the most complex, powerful, and feared deity in the Mexica pantheon. His name means 'Smoking Mirror,' referring to his primary attribute — a black obsidian mirror that he wears either in place of a foot (which he lost in his primordial battle with the Earth Monster Cipactli) or on his chest. This mirror is his instrument of omniscience: it reflects the entire world, showing him all deeds, thoughts, and the darkest truths of every soul. Tezcatlipoca is the god of the night sky, conflict, sorcery, fate, temptation, and — paradoxically — the bringer of both wealth and destruction. He is the 'Enemy of Both Sides,' the cosmic force who destroys to create and tempts to reveal.

Physically, Tezcatlipoca is depicted in Aztec codices as a powerful, often dark-skinned figure with yellow and black facial stripes (his war paint). His missing right foot is replaced by an obsidian mirror or a serpent; in some representations, a bone protrudes from the stump. He wears a jaguar pelt (the jaguar being his nagual, or animal spirit), and his headdress features heron feathers and stars. He carries a shield with circular mirrors and an atlatl (spear-thrower). His general appearance communicates dark majesty, danger, and irresistible allure.

Tezcatlipoca's role in Aztec creation mythology is central. In the Aztec conception of cosmic history, the world has passed through five suns (eras), each ending in catastrophe. Tezcatlipoca ruled the First Sun — the Sun of the Earth (Nahui Ocelotl) — which ended when his rival Quetzalcoatl struck him down from the sky, causing the world to be devoured by jaguars. This rivalry between Tezcatlipoca (darkness, night, conflict) and Quetzalcoatl (light, wisdom, civilization) is the fundamental mythic axis of Aztec religion — a dialectical relationship in which neither can permanently triumph because both are necessary for cosmic balance.

Tezcatlipoca's powers are vast and terrifying. He can appear anywhere, in any form — a jaguar stalking through the night, a stranger at a crossroads, a terrifying apparition, or a seductive tempter. He tests human virtue by offering wealth, power, and pleasure, and then judges the response. In the Florentine Codex, he is described as a being who 'gave men poverty and misery, riches and prosperity' — the capricious god of fortune and misfortune. Kings prayed to him for success in war, and his favor was essential for political legitimacy. Slaves destined for sacrifice were sometimes called his 'beloved sons.'

Despite his dark reputation, Tezcatlipoca is not simply evil. He is the god of truth revealed through confrontation with shadow. The smoking mirror shows people as they truly are, stripped of illusion. He is the god of the yaoyotl (sacred war, 'Flowery War'), which the Aztecs understood not simply as combat but as a cosmic ritual maintaining the balance of the universe. Destruction through Tezcatlipoca is always creative destruction — the clearing away of what is false or stagnant to make way for renewal.

Form

Attested depictions in codices and colonial descriptions present Tezcatlipoca as a powerful male deity with black facial paint, often with a band of black across the face and sometimes with yellow or red elements. He frequently bears an obsidian mirror (tēzcatl) either as a chest pectoral, on his head, or replacing one of his feet, from which smoke or flames emanate. In some images, one leg is missing or replaced by a serpent, emphasizing his liminal, wounded-yet-potent nature. He is richly adorned with turquoise, feathers, and gold, befitting a high-ranking god of rulership and cosmic power. As Tepeyollotl, his jaguar manifestation, he appears as a jaguar with a human heart motif, emphasizing his connection to mountains, echoes, and the night. These features are attested in pictorial codices and in Sahagún’s descriptions; details such as exact color combinations vary between manuscripts and may reflect regional or scribal conventions. Powers

Mastery of fate and providence Tezcatlipoca is repeatedly described as a god of providence, destiny, and the invisible forces that govern human lives. He sees into hearts and can grant or withdraw fortune, rulership, and success. His smoking mirror symbolizes his ability to perceive and manipulate fate, and his calendrical role suggests control over time and cycles. Colonial Nahua accounts (Florentine Codex Book I; Codex Borgia calendrical imagery) and modern syntheses. Fate is not described as arbitrary; Tezcatlipoca responds to human behavior, rewarding virtue and punishing vice, though the criteria are not fully systematized in surviving texts. Some interpretations emphasize him as a moral judge, others as a capricious trickster whose actions are not bound by ethical consistency. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tezcatlipoca) [[1]](https://www.loc.gov/resource/gdcwdl.wdl_10612/)

Night sky and darkness He is a god of the night, darkness, and the invisible. His abode is linked to high heavens and the north, and he is often called ‘lord of the night’ and ‘god of the invisible’. Colonial descriptions and codex iconography. No explicit textual limit is given; his domain is broadly the nocturnal and unseen. Some sources emphasize his celestial aspect, others his chthonic jaguar form; these may represent complementary rather than contradictory facets. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tezcatlipoca)

Conflict, war, and political upheaval Tezcatlipoca is associated with conflict, war, and the downfall of rulers and cities. In narratives of Tollan, he engineers the fall of Quetzalcoatl’s regime through trickery and intoxication, symbolizing his power to destabilize political orders. Post-conquest narratives drawing on pre-Hispanic traditions; Florentine Codex Book III (origin of gods) and later compilations. He does not directly fight as a war god in the same way as Huitzilopochtli; his power lies more in manipulation, fate, and moral testing. Some modern retellings exaggerate his role as a war god; primary sources emphasize his broader cosmic and moral functions. [[1]](https://www.loc.gov/resource/gdcwdl.wdl_10612/) [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tezcatlipoca)

Sorcery, illusion, and shapeshifting Tezcatlipoca is a master of sorcery and illusion. He can appear in different forms, including a handsome youth, a jaguar (Tepeyollotl), or a figure with a smoking mirror for a foot. In stories of Quetzalcoatl, he uses magical drink and deceptive visions to lead the ruler astray. Narrative traditions recorded in colonial sources and interpreted by modern scholars. Specific ritual techniques are not fully detailed; much of his sorcery is described narratively rather than systematically. Some accounts stress his role as a teacher of rulers through harsh lessons, others as a purely destructive deceiver; these reflect differing moral framings. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tezcatlipoca) [[1]](https://www.loc.gov/resource/gdcwdl.wdl_10612/)

Jaguar nagual and Tepeyollotl Tezcatlipoca’s animal counterpart is the jaguar, and in jaguar form he is known as Tepeyollotl (“Mountainheart”). This manifestation connects him to mountains, echoes, and the interior of the earth, and emphasizes his nocturnal, predatory aspect. Codex imagery and colonial descriptions of nagual relationships. The exact distinction between Tezcatlipoca and Tepeyollotl is debated; some treat Tepeyollotl as a separate deity, others as a manifestation. Scholars differ on whether Tepeyollotl should be fully identified with Tezcatlipoca or treated as a related but distinct god. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tezcatlipoca)

Calendrical rulership Tezcatlipoca rules the trecena 1 Ocelotl (1 Jaguar) and is patron of days named Acatl (reed). In codices he appears surrounded by day signs, indicating mastery over the ritual calendar and its prognostications. Codex Borgia, Codex Fejéváry-Mayer, and calendrical sections of colonial texts. While his calendrical rulership is clear, the precise divinatory meanings of each association are reconstructed from broader Nahua calendrical practice rather than explicit Tezcatlipoca-specific texts. None major; variations concern which specific days or trecenas are emphasized in different manuscripts. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tezcatlipoca) [[2]](https://hdl.loc.gov/loc.wdl/wdl.10613)

Rulership and social order Tezcatlipoca is closely linked to kingship and noble status. He grants rulership and can remove it, and rulers are expected to honor him through ritual and moral conduct. Florentine Codex Book VIII (kings and lords) and Book I (gods), plus later scholarly analysis. Details of specific royal rituals to Tezcatlipoca are fragmentary; much is inferred from general patterns of Aztec state religion. Some sources emphasize Huitzilopochtli as the primary patron of Mexica rulership, with Tezcatlipoca as a more general cosmic ruler; others elevate Tezcatlipoca’s political role. [[1]](https://www.loc.gov/resource/gdcwdl.wdl_10612/)

Accounts

In a series of myths about successive ages (suns), Tezcatlipoca and Quetzalcoatl cooperate and compete in creating worlds and humans. Tezcatlipoca may serve as sun in one age, only to be knocked from the sky, transforming into a jaguar and devouring people. In other versions, he helps create the earth from a primordial monster. These stories emphasize his dual role as creator and destroyer, and his rivalry with Quetzalcoatl.

In widely known narratives, Tezcatlipoca arrives in Tollan, the city ruled by Quetzalcoatl, and uses trickery to undermine the ruler’s moral discipline. He offers a drink (often described as pulque or a magical potion) that intoxicates Quetzalcoatl, leading to shameful behavior and the eventual abandonment or destruction of Tollan. Tezcatlipoca’s actions reveal his role as a destabilizer of idealized order and a tester of rulers.

Each year, a young man without physical imperfections is chosen to serve as Tezcatlipoca’s ixiptla (image/embodiment). For a year he lives in honor, learning to play the flute and carrying smoking tubes like nobles. During the Toxcatl festival, he is richly dressed and adorned, led through the city, and finally taken to the foot of the pyramid where he is sacrificed. His death renews the cosmic cycle and honors Tezcatlipoca.

During the Toxcatl festival in May 1520, while Mexica leaders dance and sing in honor of Huitzilopochtli and Tezcatlipoca, Spanish conquistadors and their Indigenous allies attack, killing many participants. Spanish accounts frame the attack as preemptive self-defense against a supposed rebellion; Nahuatl-language texts describe it as treacherous mass murder. The massacre marks a turning point in the conquest of Mexico.

Content Notes

The Toxcatl festival centers on the ritual killing of a young man who has lived for a year as Tezcatlipoca’s embodiment. He is adorned, led through the city, and sacrificed at the pyramid, his heart offered to the god. This practice is integral to Tezcatlipoca’s cult and is described in detail in the Florentine Codex.

Scholars emphasize that such sacrifices were understood as necessary for cosmic renewal and divine sustenance, but also acknowledge the profound violence involved. Modern discussions grapple with avoiding both sensationalism and sanitization. [[2]](https://hdl.loc.gov/loc.wdl/wdl.10613)

Florentine Codex Book II; illustration of the sacrifice at folio 84v.

In 1520, Spanish conquistadors and their allies attacked Mexica participants during the Toxcatl festival, killing many dancers and ritual practitioners. Indigenous accounts describe the event as treacherous mass murder, while Spanish narratives frame it as preemptive action against a supposed rebellion.

Recent scholarship foregrounds the Indigenous perspective and identifies the massacre as a key moment of colonial violence and the beginning of the end of Mexica supremacy. [[3]](https://smarthistory.org/toxcatl-massacre/?sidebar=arches)

Florentine Codex Book XII; Spanish chronicles such as López de Gómara.

Tezcatlipoca and other Aztec gods were frequently described by Spanish missionaries as ‘idols’ and ‘demons’, and their cults were suppressed. Rituals involving human sacrifice were targeted for eradication, and indigenous religious knowledge was reframed within Christian moral categories.

Historians note that such demonization shaped the transmission of information about Tezcatlipoca, introducing biases and moral judgments that must be critically assessed when reconstructing pre-Hispanic beliefs. [[1]](https://www.loc.gov/resource/gdcwdl.wdl_10612/)

Sahagún’s prologues and missionary writings; broader colonial discourse.

What We Don't Know

This dossier relies primarily on accessible digital editions of the Florentine Codex, codical summaries, and a limited set of secondary scholarship. Full consultation of all pre-conquest pictorial codices, Nahuatl-language ritual songs, and regional variations in Tezcatlipoca’s cult was not possible within the present scope. Precise folio references for some depictions in Codex Borgia and Codex Fejéváry-Mayer are generalized rather than exhaustively enumerated. The Nahuatl texts themselves were accessed through translations and scholarly commentary rather than direct philological analysis, which may obscure nuances of terminology and metaphor. Reception in modern popular culture (e.g., video games, comics, and contemporary art) is underrepresented because only well-documented, verifiable references were included to avoid fabrication. The debate over Tezcatlipoca’s relationship to earlier Olmec and Maya deities is summarized but not exhaustively documented, and further specialized iconographic studies would be needed for a more definitive treatment.

A way to hold it in your mind

In the flicker of an obsidian mirror—where smoke blurs reflection into prophecy—Tezcatlipoca watches, judges, and overturns worlds. To the Mexica, he was not simply a god of night and conflict, but the unseen architect of fate itself: the power who could raise a ruler to dazzling heights and, with a jaguar’s sudden lunge, devour an entire age. Understanding Tezcatlipoca means stepping into a cosmos where darkness is not absence, but presence—restless, demanding, and always looking back.
