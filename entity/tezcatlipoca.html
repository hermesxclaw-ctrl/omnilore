Who It Is

In the flicker of an obsidian mirror—where smoke blurs reflection into prophecy—Tezcatlipoca watches, judges, and overturns worlds. To the Mexica, he was not simply a god of night and conflict, but the unseen architect of fate itself: the power who could raise a ruler to dazzling heights and, with a jaguar’s sudden lunge, devour an entire age. Understanding Tezcatlipoca means stepping into a cosmos where darkness is not absence, but presence—restless, demanding, and always looking back.

Colonial and indigenous sources portray Tezcatlipoca as a complex, morally ambivalent deity: a supreme power of providence and fate, yet also a trickster, instigator of conflict, and destroyer of cities. He is invisible, omnipresent, and capable of seeing into hearts, rewarding and punishing humans according to their conduct. He can be benevolent, granting rulership and success, but also capricious, bringing misfortune, disease, and war. Powers
Mastery of fate and providence

Tezcatlipoca is repeatedly described as a god of providence, destiny, and the invisible forces that govern human lives. He sees into hearts and can grant or withdraw fortune, rulership, and success. His smoking mirror symbolizes his ability to perceive and manipulate fate, and his calendrical role suggests control over time and cycles.

Limit: Fate is not described as arbitrary; Tezcatlipoca responds to human behavior, rewarding virtue and punishing vice, though the criteria are not fully systematized in surviving texts.

Night sky and darkness

He is a god of the night, darkness, and the invisible. His abode is linked to high heavens and the north, and he is often called ‘lord of the night’ and ‘god of the invisible’.

Limit: No explicit textual limit is given; his domain is broadly the nocturnal and unseen.

Conflict, war, and political upheaval

Tezcatlipoca is associated with conflict, war, and the downfall of rulers and cities. In narratives of Tollan, he engineers the fall of Quetzalcoatl’s regime through trickery and intoxication, symbolizing his power to destabilize political orders.

Limit: He does not directly fight as a war god in the same way as Huitzilopochtli; his power lies more in manipulation, fate, and moral testing.

Sorcery, illusion, and shapeshifting

Tezcatlipoca is a master of sorcery and illusion. He can appear in different forms, including a handsome youth, a jaguar (Tepeyollotl), or a figure with a smoking mirror for a foot. In stories of Quetzalcoatl, he uses magical drink and deceptive visions to lead the ruler astray.

Limit: Specific ritual techniques are not fully detailed; much of his sorcery is described narratively rather than systematically.

Jaguar nagual and Tepeyollotl

Tezcatlipoca’s animal counterpart is the jaguar, and in jaguar form he is known as Tepeyollotl (“Mountainheart”). This manifestation connects him to mountains, echoes, and the interior of the earth, and emphasizes his nocturnal, predatory aspect.

Limit: The exact distinction between Tezcatlipoca and Tepeyollotl is debated; some treat Tepeyollotl as a separate deity, others as a manifestation.

Calendrical rulership

Tezcatlipoca rules the trecena 1 Ocelotl (1 Jaguar) and is patron of days named Acatl (reed). In codices he appears surrounded by day signs, indicating mastery over the ritual calendar and its prognostications.

Limit: While his calendrical rulership is clear, the precise divinatory meanings of each association are reconstructed from broader Nahua calendrical practice rather than explicit Tezcatlipoca-specific texts.

Rulership and social order

Tezcatlipoca is closely linked to kingship and noble status. He grants rulership and can remove it, and rulers are expected to honor him through ritual and moral conduct.

Limit: Details of specific royal rituals to Tezcatlipoca are fragmentary; much is inferred from general patterns of Aztec state religion.

Accounts I
The creation and destruction of the sunsNahua mythic cycles recorded in colonial compilations (including Sahagún’s works and later collections)

Post-conquest recording of pre-Hispanic traditions (16th century)

In a series of myths about successive ages (suns), Tezcatlipoca and Quetzalcoatl cooperate and compete in creating worlds and humans. Tezcatlipoca may serve as sun in one age, only to be knocked from the sky, transforming into a jaguar and devouring people. In other versions, he helps create the earth from a primordial monster. These stories emphasize his dual role as creator and destroyer, and his rivalry with Quetzalcoatl. Each sun ends in catastrophe, often linked to Tezcatlipoca’s actions, underscoring the fragility of cosmic order and the need for ongoing sacrifice and ritual.

Tezcatlipoca and the fall of TollanColonial narratives of Quetzalcoatl and Tollan, drawing on pre-Hispanic traditions

16th-century compilations

In widely known narratives, Tezcatlipoca arrives in Tollan, the city ruled by Quetzalcoatl, and uses trickery to undermine the ruler’s moral discipline. He offers a drink (often described as pulque or a magical potion) that intoxicates Quetzalcoatl, leading to shameful behavior and the eventual abandonment or destruction of Tollan. Tezcatlipoca’s actions reveal his role as a destabilizer of idealized order and a tester of rulers. The fall of Tollan becomes a paradigmatic story of the fragility of righteous rule and the inevitability of change; it also serves as a mythic precedent for Mexica claims to power.

Accounts II
The Toxcatl festival and the sacrifice of Tezcatlipoca’s ixiptlaFlorentine Codex, Book II: The Ceremonies

Late Postclassic ritual practice recorded in the 16th century

Each year, a young man without physical imperfections is chosen to serve as Tezcatlipoca’s ixiptla (image/embodiment). For a year he lives in honor, learning to play the flute and carrying smoking tubes like nobles. During the Toxcatl festival, he is richly dressed and adorned, led through the city, and finally taken to the foot of the pyramid where he is sacrificed. His death renews the cosmic cycle and honors Tezcatlipoca. The sacrifice sustains the gods and the cosmos, reinforcing social hierarchies and religious obligations. It also provides a dramatic focus for communal participation in Tezcatlipoca’s cult.

The Toxcatl Massacre during the conquestFlorentine Codex, Book XII; Indigenous and Spanish conquest narratives

1520 event, recorded in mid–late 16th century

During the Toxcatl festival in May 1520, while Mexica leaders dance and sing in honor of Huitzilopochtli and Tezcatlipoca, Spanish conquistadors and their Indigenous allies attack, killing many participants. Spanish accounts frame the attack as preemptive self-defense against a supposed rebellion; Nahuatl-language texts describe it as treacherous mass murder. The massacre marks a turning point in the conquest of Mexico. The massacre incites war, undermines Mexica supremacy, and contributes to the eventual fall of Tenochtitlan. It also becomes a focal point for later historical memory and debates about colonial violence.
