Who It Is

He has no face, no weapon, no monster to slay — only a name that means 'comb-true-knowing.' Kushimachi no Mikoto is the quiet god in the back room of Japanese creation, the deity the court called when it needed to know what the gods wanted, and whose children became the ritualists who kept the emperor pure.

No personality, speech, or motive is narrated in surviving texts. Functional role is inferred: a kami of wisdom, deliberation, and divinatory knowledge serving the heavenly court. Kogo Shui and later commentaries portray him as a wise counselor kami who knows the will of heaven via divination, complementing Futodama's ritual preparation and Ame no Koyane's prayer. This is a reconstructed role based on clan function, not a story with dialogue. No myths of anger, trickery, or romance are attested. Behavior: unattested; function: divination/wisdom.

Powers
Divination (Futomani / Ura)

Attested function: mastery of greater divination by heating turtle plastron or deer scapula and interpreting cracks (futomani), and general divinatory wisdom. Inbe clan's hereditary office was providing divination materials and interpreting omens for the court. Kushimachi as their ancestor embodies this knowledge. Not a magical attack power but a ritual technology to ascertain divine will.

Limit: Requires ritual purity, correct materials (tortoise shell, cherry bark, etc.), and is interpretive - not omniscient. In mythic context, divination still required performance; it could be contested by other kami or fail if ritual was impure. No attested ability to alter fate, only to reveal it.

Ritual Purity and Counsel (Saiku / Chie)

Embodiment of correct ritual knowledge (chie) that allows the heavenly court to act without pollution. As Inbe ancestor, associated with preparation of offerings, sacred timber, and hemp for rituals. Provides counsel to higher kami via knowledge, not coercion.

Limit: Subordinate to higher heavenly kami (Amaterasu, Takamimusubi). Acts as advisor/preparer, not decision-maker. No independent creation or destructive power attested.

Ancestral Protection of Lineage (Ujigami)

As ujigami, protects and legitimizes descendant clans (Inbe, Sazakibe, etc.) and grants them hereditary right to serve at court. This is a sociopolitical-religious power: the clan's legitimacy derives from descent.

Limit: Limited to lineage members; not a universal protector kami like Hachiman. Power is genealogical legitimacy, not interventionist miracle. If lineage loses court status (as Inbe did vs. Nakatomi/Fujiwara), the kami's institutional power wanes.

Accounts I
Genealogy of the Heavenly Kami - Child of KamimusubiKojiki, Kamitsumaki

Nara period, 712 CE (describing Age of Gods)

In the Kojiki's terse genealogical list after the birth of the heavenly kami, Kamimusubi no Kami (one of the three creator kami, Zoka Sanshin) begets Kushimachi no Mikoto. The text provides no narrative episode, only the name in the lineage that leads toward the Inbe-related clans and, indirectly, toward the preparation for the descent of Ninigi. The notice establishes Kushimachi as an amatsukami (heavenly kami) of the creation generation, senior to many later heavenly kami but junior to the primordial trio. Establishes Kushimachi's heavenly status and his later role as ancestor of earthly ritualists. Provides the charter for Inbe clan's claim to heavenly descent, parallel to Nakatomi descent from Ame no Koyane.

Variant Parentage - Child of TakamimusubiNihon Shoki, Jindai-ki, variant traditions (issho)

Nara period, 720 CE

The Nihon Shoki's Age of the Gods includes multiple quoted variants (in the style 'In one writing it says...'). One variant makes Kushimachi no Mikoto the child of Takamimusubi no Kami rather than Kamimusubi. Takamimusubi is the more politically central heavenly ancestor of the imperial line, so this variant elevates Kushimachi's status and ties him closer to the imperial ancestral pole. The Nihon Shoki does not narrate any deed for him in either version; the interest is purely genealogical politics. Creates the enduring scholarly contradiction about parentage. Reflects competition between different court lineages claiming descent from different creator kami. Later genealogies choose one or harmonize by making the two Musubi kami co-parents.

The Inbe Charter - Ancestor of the RitualistsShinsen Shojiroku (815) and Kogo Shui (807)

Early Heian period, codifying Nara-period clan claims

Both texts present Kushimachi no Mikoto not as a mythic actor but as a founding ancestor (sojin). Shinsen Shojiroku lists multiple uji that claim descent: the Inbe (Imibe) proper, the Sazakibe, and several Awa and Yamato lineages. Kogo Shui, written by Inbe no Hironari to protest the loss of Inbe privileges to the Nakatomi (Fujiwara), describes the ancestral Inbe kami Futodama and his kin (including Kushimachi) as the kami who performed divination and prepared offerings at the time of Amaterasu's concealment in the rock cave (Ame no Iwato) and at Ninigi's descent. Kushimachi's specific contribution is divinatory wisdom that complements Futodama's making of offerings. Provides the only functional 'story' for Kushimachi: participation in the heavenly ritual preparations. Legitimizes Inbe hereditary offices (preparing hemp, paper mulberry, sacred timber, divination). Failure of this charter narrative in political terms led to Inbe's decline vs. Nakatomi.

Accounts II
The Divination at the Heavenly Court (Reconstructed Ritual Narrative)Kogo Shui + Engishiki Jingi + commentary on Ame no Iwato myth

Mythic time (Age of Gods) as retrojected by Heian ritualists; textual attestation 807-927 CE

No single text narrates a scene where Kushimachi performs divination, but the ritual corpus implies one: when the heavenly kami needed to know how to lure Amaterasu from the cave or whether Ninigi should descend, futomani divination was performed. Inbe tradition attributes the knowledge of futomani to Kushimachi/Futodama. The Engishiki preserves the actual Heian-period performance: Inbe provided the turtle shells and divined before major state rites. The 'story' is thus a ritual etiology rather than a plotted myth: Kushimachi taught or embodied the correct way to crack the shell and read the divine will. Etiology for the court's divination bureau (Urabe) and Inbe's role. Explains why Kushimachi is called 'true knowing.' No defeat or resolution drama survives.

Enshrinement in Awa and Yamato - The Earthly Afterlife of the KamiEngishiki Jinmyocho (927) + medieval shrine records + modern Jinja Honcho register

Heian to modern (927 CE - present)

After the mythic age, Kushimachi no Mikoto lives on as a co-enshrined kami (aidono) in shrines of the Inbe lineage. The Engishiki lists Inbe Jinja in Awa Province (modern Tokushima) as a major shrine (myojin taisha) enshrining Futodama; subsidiary tradition includes Kushimachi as ancestral companion. Medieval documents (Awa no Kuni Fudoki fragments, shrine engi) and modern shrine listings note veneration at Inbe Jinja (Tokushima City), Awa Jinja, and at branch Inbe shrines in Chiba and Nara where Inbe migrants settled. No independent 'Kushimachi Jinja' of high rank is attested in Engishiki, indicating his cult was always lineage-bound rather than territorial. Preserves the kami's worship into the present, though as a secondary figure. Explains why he is obscure to the general public but known to shrine historians. Modern Inbe Jinja festivals still invoke the ancestral divination kami.

Kokugaku Reinterpretation - The Comb of WisdomEdo-period kokugaku commentaries: Motoori Norinaga (Kojikiden, 1798) and Hirata Atsutane (Koshicho, 1812)

Early modern, 18th-19th century, commenting on 8th-century texts

Motoori Norinaga, in his exhaustive Kojikiden commentary, discusses the name Kushimachi, parsing kushi as both 'comb' and 'wondrous' and machi as 'true wisdom,' linking the comb to ritual parting/purification and to divinatory implements made of wood. Hirata Atsutane further elevates Kushimachi as a kami of esoteric wisdom whose comb symbolizes ordering chaos. These are scholarly reinterpretations, not ancient stories, but they become the dominant popular explanation for the name and generate the modern image of a 'comb god.' Shapes modern encyclopedia entries and internet descriptions. Introduces the 'comb' iconography that has no ancient visual source but is now repeated as fact.

Kushimachi no Mikoto is the 'Japanese god of combs and hairdressing' — Primary sources never describe him making, using, or presiding over combs as craft. The kushi element is part of his name, linked by tradition to divinatory wisdom (machi = true knowing) and possibly to ritual purification implements, not to hairdressing. The craft deity for combs/mirrors is Ishikoridome. The 'comb god' label is a modern literalization of the name.

Kushimachi no Mikoto appears as a major character in the Ame no Iwato (cave) myth with a dramatic role — He is not named in the Kojiki or Nihon Shoki telling of Ame no Iwato. Futodama and Ame no Koyane are named. Kushimachi's connection to that myth is retrospective: Kogo Shui and later Inbe tradition include him as part of the ancestral ritualist group that would have performed divination at that time, but no ancient narrative gives him dialogue or action in the cave episode.

Kushimachi no Mikoto is the same as Kushinada-hime or Kushitamahiko — These are separate deities with distinct genealogies and myths. Kushinada-hime is Susanoo's wife; Kushitamahiko is a different kami. Shared kushi element reflects common archaic prefix, not identity. Kushimachi's lineage is Musubi-line heavenly kami; Kushinada-hime is earthly Izumo lineage.
