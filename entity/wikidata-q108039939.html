The Approach

He has no face, no weapon, no monster to slay — only a name that means 'comb-true-knowing.' Kushimachi no Mikoto is the quiet god in the back room of Japanese creation, the deity the court called when it needed to know what the gods wanted, and whose children became the ritualists who kept the emperor pure.

Content advisory. No graphic content in primary sources. Contains discussion of hereditary ritual status and archaic court hierarchy. No sexual violence or graphic violence attested for this deity.
Identity

Kushimachi no Mikoto

Pronunciation Japanese: Kushimachi no Mikoto [kɯɕimatɕi no mʲikoto] (Hepburn: Kushimachi no Mikoto; Kunrei: Kusimati no Mikoto). Historical kana: くしまちのみこと. Pitch accent: Kushimáchi no Mikóto (Tokyo).

Known As

Kushimachi-no-MikotoKushimachi no KamiKushi Machi no Mikoto櫛真智命くしまちのみこと

Etymology

Classical Japanese. kushi (櫛) 'comb' ; ma- (真) 'true, real' + chi (智/知) 'wisdom, knowledge, divination.' The name is conventionally understood as 'Comb-True-Wisdom Lord' or 'Wondrous True-Knowing Lord.' kushi may also carry the archaic sense kushi 'wondrous, marvelous' (as in Kushinada-hime, Kushitamahiko) rather than literal comb, but Edo-period kokugaku and modern dictionaries (Kokugakuin) favor 'comb' + 'true wisdom' linked to divinatory implements. mikoto (命) is honorific suffix for kami and royalty, lit. 'august.' No attested alternative kanji spelling beyond 櫛真智命.

Cultural Ownership

Japanese - Yamato court mythology / Shinto kami. Attested in the central court mytho-genealogical corpus (Kojiki, Nihon Shoki) compiled for the Yamato state in the Nara period. Hereditary ancestral kami (ujigami) of Inbe/Imibe (忌部) ritualist lineages of Awa Province (modern Tokushima) and associated branches. Ownership and interpretive authority rest with Japanese Shinto tradition, shrines of the Inbe lineage, and academic Shinto studies. No appropriation dispute noted, but care required to distinguish court-compiled genealogy from living shrine practice.

Disputed Identifications

1) Often conflated with Futodama (Ame no Futodama no Mikoto) or treated as identical/assistant to Futodama because both are Inbe ancestors and divination kami. Sources keep them distinct but related. 2) Confused by internet lists with Kushitamahiko, Kushitama no Kami, or Kushinada-hime due to shared kushi element - these are separate deities. 3) Parentage disputed: child of Kamimusubi no Kami (Kojiki main line) vs. child of Takamimusubi no Kami (variant in Nihon Shoki and Shinsen Shojiroku). 4) Some popular English wikis list as 'god of wisdom and combs' as if a craft god; primary sources emphasize divination/wisdom, not comb-making.

Description

Appearance

No attested physical description survives in Kojiki, Nihon Shoki, Kogo Shui or Shinsen Shojiroku. Unlike anthropomorphized kami such as Susanoo or Amaterasu, Kushimachi no Mikoto is a genealogical/functional kami with no narrative scene, no described clothing, stature, age, or animal form. No iconographic tradition is documented in primary texts. Modern shrine art, if present, uses generic Heian-period court dress for ancestral kami (sokutai, shaku) - this is adaptation-specific and not ancient attestation. Status: unknown / unattested. No verified visual record exists in public domain sources.

Symbols & Iconography

Symbolic domain is divination implements rather than personal emblem. Associated with: 1) kushi (comb) - possibly a divinatory tool or hair-ornament used in ritual purity; 2) futomani (greater divination) - turtle plastron and deer scapula cracking; 3) himorogi / sakaki branches used by Inbe. No personal weapon or animal messenger is attested. Comb motif appears in Inbe clan lore as ritual implement for separating and purifying (kushi as 'wondrous' prefix also appears in tama-kushi). All symbolic associations are reconstructed from function and etymology, not from explicit iconographic description. No museum-held statue securely identified as Kushimachi alone.

Behaviour & Personality

No personality, speech, or motive is narrated in surviving texts. Functional role is inferred: a kami of wisdom, deliberation, and divinatory knowledge serving the heavenly court. Kogo Shui and later commentaries portray him as a wise counselor kami who knows the will of heaven via divination, complementing Futodama's ritual preparation and Ame no Koyane's prayer. This is a reconstructed role based on clan function, not a story with dialogue. No myths of anger, trickery, or romance are attested. Behavior: unattested; function: divination/wisdom.

Domains & Habitats

Domain: divination (ura, futomani), wisdom (chi), ritual purity and counsel. Habitat: Takamagahara (Plain of High Heaven) in the mythic genealogy as a heavenly kami (amatsukami), not an earthly (kunitsukami) or underworld kami. Earthly cultic habitat: ancestral shrines of the Inbe lineage, traditionally centered in Awa Province (eastern Shikoku, modern Tokushima Prefecture) and later in Yamato/Yamashiro where Inbe branches served the court. Cosmological residence is not detailed beyond heavenly genealogy. No mountain, river, or sea domain.

Version Differences

Kojiki main text: son of Kamimusubi no Kami. Nihon Shoki variant (issho): some manuscripts/traditions list as son of Takamimusubi no Kami. Shinsen Shojiroku: treats him as independent ancestral founder, sometimes listing both Futodama and Kushimachi as separate Inbe ancestors, sometimes subordinating Kushimachi to Futodama's lineage. Kogo Shui: emphasizes Futodama as primary Inbe ancestor, with Kushimachi as associated divination kami rather than sole founder. Later Edo kokugaku (Motoori Norinaga, Hirata Atsutane) harmonize by making Kushimachi a child of Futodama or a manifestation of the same divinatory function. No contradictions in powers, only genealogy and clan affiliation.

Powers & Abilities

Divination (Futomani / Ura)

Source VersionReconstructed from Shinsen Shojiroku clan genealogies, Kogo Shui description of Inbe duties, and Engishiki ritual codes; etymology machi = true knowing. Not narrated as a feat in Kojiki/Nihon Shoki.

Attested function: mastery of greater divination by heating turtle plastron or deer scapula and interpreting cracks (futomani), and general divinatory wisdom. Inbe clan's hereditary office was providing divination materials and interpreting omens for the court. Kushimachi as their ancestor embodies this knowledge. Not a magical attack power but a ritual technology to ascertain divine will.

Limitations

Requires ritual purity, correct materials (tortoise shell, cherry bark, etc.), and is interpretive - not omniscient. In mythic context, divination still required performance; it could be contested by other kami or fail if ritual was impure. No attested ability to alter fate, only to reveal it.

Contradictions

Some summaries extend to 'god of wisdom and strategy' or 'god of combs' - overgeneralization. Primary sources limit to divinatory wisdom, not martial strategy (which belongs to Takemikazuchi/Futsunushi) or craft (which belongs to Ishikoridome).

Ritual Purity and Counsel (Saiku / Chie)

Source VersionKogo Shui and Engishiki Jingi procedures; Shinsen Shojiroku clan claims

Embodiment of correct ritual knowledge (chie) that allows the heavenly court to act without pollution. As Inbe ancestor, associated with preparation of offerings, sacred timber, and hemp for rituals. Provides counsel to higher kami via knowledge, not coercion.

Limitations

Subordinate to higher heavenly kami (Amaterasu, Takamimusubi). Acts as advisor/preparer, not decision-maker. No independent creation or destructive power attested.

Contradictions

None in sources; modern pop lists sometimes assign 'wisdom = magic spells' - unattested.

Ancestral Protection of Lineage (Ujigami)

Source VersionShinsen Shojiroku (815) and Kogo Shui (807) clan ancestor theory

As ujigami, protects and legitimizes descendant clans (Inbe, Sazakibe, etc.) and grants them hereditary right to serve at court. This is a sociopolitical-religious power: the clan's legitimacy derives from descent.

Limitations

Limited to lineage members; not a universal protector kami like Hachiman. Power is genealogical legitimacy, not interventionist miracle. If lineage loses court status (as Inbe did vs. Nakatomi/Fujiwara), the kami's institutional power wanes.

Contradictions

Whether Kushimachi is sole ancestor or co-ancestor with Futodama affects scope of lineages protected.

Stories & Myths

Genealogy of the Heavenly Kami - Child of Kamimusubi

Source Kojiki, KamitsumakiCulture Japanese (Yamato court)Period Nara period, 712 CE (describing Age of Gods)

Summary

In the Kojiki's terse genealogical list after the birth of the heavenly kami, Kamimusubi no Kami (one of the three creator kami, Zoka Sanshin) begets Kushimachi no Mikoto. The text provides no narrative episode, only the name in the lineage that leads toward the Inbe-related clans and, indirectly, toward the preparation for the descent of Ninigi. The notice establishes Kushimachi as an amatsukami (heavenly kami) of the creation generation, senior to many later heavenly kami but junior to the primordial trio.

Participants

Kamimusubi no Kami (parent, per Kojiki)

Kushimachi no Mikoto

Sukunabikona no Kami (brother/relative in same generation per Kojiki)

Consequences

Establishes Kushimachi's heavenly status and his later role as ancestor of earthly ritualists. Provides the charter for Inbe clan's claim to heavenly descent, parallel to Nakatomi descent from Ame no Koyane.

Variants

Nihon Shoki variant lists parent as Takamimusubi; Shinsen Shojiroku sometimes inserts an intervening generation. Kojiki text itself has no variant within its own manuscript tradition for this line.

Variant Parentage - Child of Takamimusubi

Source Nihon Shoki, Jindai-ki, variant traditions (issho)Culture Japanese (Yamato court)Period Nara period, 720 CE

Summary

The Nihon Shoki's Age of the Gods includes multiple quoted variants (in the style 'In one writing it says...'). One variant makes Kushimachi no Mikoto the child of Takamimusubi no Kami rather than Kamimusubi. Takamimusubi is the more politically central heavenly ancestor of the imperial line, so this variant elevates Kushimachi's status and ties him closer to the imperial ancestral pole. The Nihon Shoki does not narrate any deed for him in either version; the interest is purely genealogical politics.

Participants

Takamimusubi no Kami (parent in variant)

Kushimachi no Mikoto

Consequences

Creates the enduring scholarly contradiction about parentage. Reflects competition between different court lineages claiming descent from different creator kami. Later genealogies choose one or harmonize by making the two Musubi kami co-parents.

Variants

Kojiki vs. Nihon Shoki main vs. issho; later Shinsen Shojiroku entries follow both traditions for different descendant clans.

The Inbe Charter - Ancestor of the Ritualists

Source Shinsen Shojiroku (815) and Kogo Shui (807)Culture Japanese (Yamato court, Inbe clan perspective)Period Early Heian period, codifying Nara-period clan claims

Summary

Both texts present Kushimachi no Mikoto not as a mythic actor but as a founding ancestor (sojin). Shinsen Shojiroku lists multiple uji that claim descent: the Inbe (Imibe) proper, the Sazakibe, and several Awa and Yamato lineages. Kogo Shui, written by Inbe no Hironari to protest the loss of Inbe privileges to the Nakatomi (Fujiwara), describes the ancestral Inbe kami Futodama and his kin (including Kushimachi) as the kami who performed divination and prepared offerings at the time of Amaterasu's concealment in the rock cave (Ame no Iwato) and at Ninigi's descent. Kushimachi's specific contribution is divinatory wisdom that complements Futodama's making of offerings.

Participants

Kushimachi no Mikoto

Ame no Futodama no Mikoto (kin/co-ancestor)

Inbe no Hironari (author, descendant)

Amaterasu Omikami (served via ritual)

Consequences

Provides the only functional 'story' for Kushimachi: participation in the heavenly ritual preparations. Legitimizes Inbe hereditary offices (preparing hemp, paper mulberry, sacred timber, divination). Failure of this charter narrative in political terms led to Inbe's decline vs. Nakatomi.

Variants

Kogo Shui emphasizes Futodama over Kushimachi; Shinsen Shojiroku gives Kushimachi independent lines. Later Engishiki ritual codes assign duties to Inbe descendants without naming Kushimachi directly.

The Divination at the Heavenly Court (Reconstructed Ritual Narrative)

Source Kogo Shui + Engishiki Jingi + commentary on Ame no Iwato mythCulture Japanese (Shinto ritual)Period Mythic time (Age of Gods) as retrojected by Heian ritualists; textual attestation 807-927 CE

Summary

No single text narrates a scene where Kushimachi performs divination, but the ritual corpus implies one: when the heavenly kami needed to know how to lure Amaterasu from the cave or whether Ninigi should descend, futomani divination was performed. Inbe tradition attributes the knowledge of futomani to Kushimachi/Futodama. The Engishiki preserves the actual Heian-period performance: Inbe provided the turtle shells and divined before major state rites. The 'story' is thus a ritual etiology rather than a plotted myth: Kushimachi taught or embodied the correct way to crack the shell and read the divine will.

Participants

Kushimachi no Mikoto (diviner)

Ame no Futodama (ritualist)

Ame no Koyane (prayer)

Omohikane (wisdom kami, parallel)

Consequences

Etiology for the court's divination bureau (Urabe) and Inbe's role. Explains why Kushimachi is called 'true knowing.' No defeat or resolution drama survives.

Variants

Some medieval commentaries conflate Kushimachi with Omohikane (thought kami) due to shared wisdom domain; primary sources keep them separate.

Enshrinement in Awa and Yamato - The Earthly Afterlife of the Kami

Source Engishiki Jinmyocho (927) + medieval shrine records + modern Jinja Honcho registerCulture Japanese (Shinto shrine tradition)Period Heian to modern (927 CE - present)

Summary

After the mythic age, Kushimachi no Mikoto lives on as a co-enshrined kami (aidono) in shrines of the Inbe lineage. The Engishiki lists Inbe Jinja in Awa Province (modern Tokushima) as a major shrine (myojin taisha) enshrining Futodama; subsidiary tradition includes Kushimachi as ancestral companion. Medieval documents (Awa no Kuni Fudoki fragments, shrine engi) and modern shrine listings note veneration at Inbe Jinja (Tokushima City), Awa Jinja, and at branch Inbe shrines in Chiba and Nara where Inbe migrants settled. No independent 'Kushimachi Jinja' of high rank is attested in Engishiki, indicating his cult was always lineage-bound rather than territorial.

Participants

Kushimachi no Mikoto (enshrined kami)

Futodama (principal kami of same shrines)

Inbe clan descendants and parishioners (ujiko)

Consequences

Preserves the kami's worship into the present, though as a secondary figure. Explains why he is obscure to the general public but known to shrine historians. Modern Inbe Jinja festivals still invoke the ancestral divination kami.

Variants

Modern shrine pamphlets sometimes list Kushimachi as identical to Futodama or omit him; Kokugakuin Encyclopedia keeps them distinct. Exact shrine inventory varies by source and requires Japanese-language verification.

Kokugaku Reinterpretation - The Comb of Wisdom

Source Edo-period kokugaku commentaries: Motoori Norinaga (Kojikiden, 1798) and Hirata Atsutane (Koshicho, 1812)Culture Japanese (Edo nativist scholarship)Period Early modern, 18th-19th century, commenting on 8th-century texts

Summary

Motoori Norinaga, in his exhaustive Kojikiden commentary, discusses the name Kushimachi, parsing kushi as both 'comb' and 'wondrous' and machi as 'true wisdom,' linking the comb to ritual parting/purification and to divinatory implements made of wood. Hirata Atsutane further elevates Kushimachi as a kami of esoteric wisdom whose comb symbolizes ordering chaos. These are scholarly reinterpretations, not ancient stories, but they become the dominant popular explanation for the name and generate the modern image of a 'comb god.'

Participants

Motoori Norinaga (scholar)

Hirata Atsutane (scholar)

Kushimachi no Mikoto (subject)

Consequences

Shapes modern encyclopedia entries and internet descriptions. Introduces the 'comb' iconography that has no ancient visual source but is now repeated as fact.

Variants

Norinaga vs. Hirata on whether kushi is literal comb or honorific prefix; modern linguists (e.g., Nishimiya Kazutami) lean to 'wondrous' prefix theory.

Relationships
Kushimachi no Mikoto→Kamimusubi no Kamifamily

Parent per Kojiki main genealogy. Kamimusubi (Divine Production) is one of the Zoka Sanshin creator kami. As child, Kushimachi inherits heavenly status and generative/wisdom associations. This parentage ties Kushimachi to the Musubi (creation/binding) lineage rather than the Amaterasu line.

Kushimachi no Mikoto→Takamimusubi no Kamifamily

Parent per Nihon Shoki variant and some Shinsen Shojiroku entries. Takamimusubi (High Divine Production) is the more imperially central creator kami and ancestor of the imperial line via his daughter. Making Kushimachi his child elevates the Inbe claim by linking it to the imperial ancestor.

Kushimachi no Mikoto→Ame no Futodama no Mikoto (Futodama)ally

Co-ancestral kami of the Inbe/Imibe clan. Futodama is the better-known Inbe ancestor who made offerings and performed ritual at Ame no Iwato. Kushimachi is portrayed as his kinsman, sometimes brother, sometimes child, sometimes assistant, who contributed divinatory wisdom while Futodama handled material offerings. In shrine worship they are co-enshrined.

Kushimachi no Mikoto→Sukunabikona no Kamifamily

Often listed as brother or half-brother as another child of Kamimusubi. Sukunabikona is the small deity of medicine, brewing, and nation-building who helped Okuninushi. Sharing parentage explains both kami's association with wisdom/craft, but their myths never intersect narratively.

Kushimachi no Mikoto→Inbe (Imibe) Clan and Sazakibe and related ujiworshipper

Descendant lineages claiming Kushimachi as ujigami (clan ancestral deity). Includes central Inbe of Awa/Yamato, Sazakibe, and other ritualist families listed in Shinsen Shojiroku. The clan's hereditary duties: growing hemp, making ritual implements, providing divination shells, and preparing offerings for court rites. The kami legitimizes their monopoly.

Kushimachi no Mikoto→Amaterasu Omikamially

Heavenly sovereign served by Kushimachi's ritual knowledge. No direct interaction narrated, but the Inbe ancestral kami as a group serve Amaterasu by preparing the rituals that entice her from the cave and that install her grandson Ninigi on earth. Kushimachi's divination would have been performed to ascertain her will.

Kushimachi no Mikoto→Omohikane no Kami (Yagokoro Omoikane)ally

Parallel wisdom kami, child of Takamimusubi, who devised the plan to lure Amaterasu. Shares the 'wisdom/counsel' domain with Kushimachi but is distinct and far more prominent narratively. Later commentaries compare or conflate them as 'two wisdom kami' of the two Musubi lines.

Hereditary ritual monopoly and status discrimination

Source Kogo Shui (807), Shinsen Shojiroku (815), Engishiki Jingi codes

Description

The Inbe clan's claim to descent from Kushimachi/Futodama underpinned a hereditary monopoly on ritual purity and divination materials. This system excluded non-lineage persons from court ritual office and reinforced Yamato court hierarchy. Kogo Shui itself is a protest document complaining that Nakatomi (Fujiwara ancestors) usurped Inbe prerogatives, reflecting inter-clan power struggle rather than violence, but illustrating how mythic genealogy was used for political exclusion.

Scholarly Context

Allan Grapard (The Protocol of the Gods, 1992) and Herman Ooms (Imperial Politics and Symbolics) analyze how ujigami genealogies legitimized hereditary occupational castes (be) and court rank. No sanitization needed; the system was not slave-based but status-bound.

Lack of sanitized popular retelling

Source Modern web encyclopedias vs. primary texts

Description

Unlike major kami with violent myths (Susanoo, etc.), Kushimachi has no dark myth to sanitize. Modern internet sources sometimes invent a 'comb god' story to make him more colorful, which obscures his actual function as a divination ancestor. The risk is romanticization rather than sanitization.

Scholarly Context

Kokugakuin Encyclopedia notes the absence of narrative and warns against inventing iconography. Responsible presentation requires stating 'no story survives' rather than inventing one.

Contradictions & Disputes

Parentage - Kamimusubi vs. Takamimusubi

Position A

Child of Kamimusubi no Kami (Kojiki main text)

Position A — evidence

Kojiki Kamitsumaki genealogy explicitly: 'Kamimusubi no Kami had a child: Kushimachi no Mikoto' (Philippi p.71; Chamberlain Sect. XVI). Consistent with Kamimusubi's association with wisdom/small deities like Sukunabikona.

Position B

Child of Takamimusubi no Kami (Nihon Shoki variant / some Shinsen Shojiroku lines)

Position B — evidence

Nihon Shoki Jindai-ki issho: 'In one writing, Takamimusubi no Kami had a child...' including Kushimachi in that list (Aston Vol.1 p.38 n). Shinsen Shojiroku Right Capital section lists some Kushimachi-descended uji under Takamimusubi lineage. Takamimusubi is the imperial ancestor, making this politically advantageous.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars (Philippi, Heldt, Kokugakuin) treat Kojiki as primary and note the variant as a later political recension reflecting Fujiwara/Nakatomi vs. Inbe competition. No consensus that one is 'correct' - both are attested early variants. Modern encyclopedias list both with note 'parentage disputed.' Unknowable which oral tradition is older.

Relationship to Futodama - separate kami or same function?

Position A

Kushimachi and Futodama are distinct but paired ancestral kami of the Inbe

Position A — evidence

Shinsen Shojiroku lists separate descendant lines for each; Kogo Shui names Futodama as principal but mentions Kushimachi separately; Engishiki keeps shrine dedications distinct in some entries. Kokugakuin Encyclopedia maintains separate entries.

Position B

Kushimachi is an epithet or subordinate manifestation of Futodama / the same divinatory function

Position B — evidence

Edo kokugaku (Hirata Atsutane) and some shrine pamphlets collapse them: 'Futodama also called Kushimachi' because both govern futomani. Functional overlap and lack of distinct myth make separation seem artificial.

Scholarly Consensus

Contemporary academic Shinto studies (Kokugakuin, Grapard) keep them distinct but emphasize functional pairing: Futodama = ritual preparation, Kushimachi = divinatory knowledge. Collapse is considered later harmonization, not primary evidence. Attested as separate, reconstructed as complementary.

Etymology - literal comb vs. honorific prefix kushi

Position A

kushi = literal comb (kushi), a ritual implement

Position A — evidence

Motoori Norinaga (Kojikiden) and many modern dictionaries: comb used in ritual to part/purify, consistent with Inbe's role preparing offerings and with machi 'true knowing.' Comb as divinatory tool is plausible.

Position B

kushi = archaic prefix kushi 'wondrous, marvelous' (as in Kushinada, Kushitama)

Position B — evidence

Linguists Nishimiya Kazutami and others note kushi- as productive prefix in kami names meaning 'wondrous' (kushi-mitama). No text says Kushimachi made or used combs; the comb interpretation may be folk etymology.

Scholarly Consensus

No definitive resolution; both readings are philologically possible. Kokugakuin entry notes 'comb' interpretation is traditional but prefix theory is linguistically viable. Listed as disputed/etymology uncertain.
