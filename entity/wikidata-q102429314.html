Who It Is

Before there were ogres, before there were titans, there were the giants—beings whose bones were said to litter the earth, whose footsteps carved valleys, and whose existence haunted the edges of every culture's map. From the Cyclops of Homer to the Nephilim of Genesis, from the jentilak of the Pyrenees to the Quinametzin of Mesoamerica, the giant is not a single monster but a universal mirror held up to humanity, reflecting our fears of the monstrous, our awe of the natural world, and our fascination with the limits of the human body.

Giants are often portrayed as violent, dim-witted, and gluttonous, sometimes cannibalistic. However, exceptions exist: some giants are wise, friendly, or protective. Basque giants are generally righteous, while some literary giants like the BFG are kind and intelligent.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The English word 'giant' is first attested in 1297 in Robert of Gloucester's chronicle, derived from the Ancient Greek 'gigas' (plural 'gigantes'), which is of uncertain origin but associated with the earth-born giants of Greek mythology.

Pronunciation — /ˈdʒaɪənt/

Aliases — Gigas, Gigantes, Nephilim, Jentilak, Mairuak, Ispolini

Powers
Superhuman Strength

Giants possess immense physical strength, capable of moving mountains, throwing boulders, and crushing humans.

Limit: Varies by myth; some giants are vulnerable to specific weaknesses (e.g., blackberries for Bulgarian ispolini).

Size Manipulation

Some giants can change their size, growing larger or smaller at will.

Limit: Not commonly attested; often a feature of individual tales.

Magical Abilities

Some giants possess magical powers, such as shape-shifting, invisibility, or enchantments.

Limit: Often tied to specific objects or conditions.

Longevity

Giants are often extremely long-lived, sometimes immortal.

Limit: Some can be killed by heroes.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Giants are typically described as humanoid beings of prodigious size and strength. Their exact appearance varies widely: some are simply larger versions of humans, while others have monstrous features such as multiple heads, one eye, or animalistic traits. In many traditions, they are depicted as ugly and terrifying, but some are handsome and noble, like Hayk.

Symbols — Giants are often associated with mountains, large stones, and megalithic structures. They are frequently depicted in art as towering over landscapes or battling heroes. In Christian iconography, they may symbolize pride or rebellion.

Domains — Giants are said to inhabit remote places: mountains, forests, caves, islands, and sometimes the underworld. They are often connected to the creation of geological features, such as the Giant's Causeway.

Accounts I
The GigantomachyGreek mythology

Archaic/Classical

The Gigantes, born from Gaia, waged war against the Olympian gods. They were defeated with the help of Heracles. The defeat of the giants established the supremacy of the Olympian gods.

David and GoliathBook of Samuel

Iron Age

The young shepherd David defeats the Philistine champion Goliath with a sling and stone. David becomes a hero and later king of Israel.

Accounts II
Jack the Giant KillerEnglish fairy tale

Medieval/Early Modern

A clever young man named Jack kills a series of giants through cunning and trickery, often using pits, disguises, and magical items. Jack gains wealth and honor.

Hayk and NimrodMovses Khorenatsi, History of Armenia

Late Antiquity

Hayk, a giant and ancestor of the Armenians, leads his people against the tyrannical Nimrod. Hayk kills Nimrod with an arrow near Lake Van. The Armenian nation is founded.

Fionn mac Cumhaill and the Giant's CausewayIrish mythology

Mythological cycle

The hero Fionn mac Cumhaill is said to have built the Giant's Causeway as stepping stones to Scotland to fight a rival giant. The causeway remains as a geological feature.
