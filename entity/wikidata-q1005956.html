The Approach

In the far northwest of the ancient Chinese world, beyond the Kunlun range, there once stood a mountain that held up the sky—until a god's furious blow broke it, tilting the heavens and sending all of China's rivers forever southeastward. This is the tale of Mount Buzhou, the Unrevolving Mountain.

Content advisory. Contains mythological accounts of divine violence (a god smashing his head against a mountain) and cosmic catastrophe. No graphic detail.
Identity

Mount Buzhou

Pronunciation Boo-joe (approximate Mandarin: bù zhōu)

Known As

不周山Buzhou MountainUnrevolving MountainBu-zhou

Etymology

The name 'Buzhou' (不周) is traditionally interpreted as 'unrevolving' or 'not complete', possibly referring to its role as a pillar that does not revolve with the heavens, or as a damaged/imperfect mountain.

Cultural Ownership

Chinese mythology; specifically associated with ancient cosmological geography and the Chu cultural sphere.

Disputed Identifications

Some modern scholars have attempted to identify Mount Buzhou with a real geographical location, such as the Pamir Mountains, but this is speculative and not universally accepted. The mythological mountain's location is symbolic rather than literal.

Description

Appearance

Mount Buzhou is described as a towering pillar that supported the heavens in the northwest corner of the world. It was part of a set of eight pillars, one for each cardinal and intercardinal direction. No detailed physical description is given in ancient sources; its significance is cosmic rather than visual.

Symbols & Iconography

In later art and literature, Mount Buzhou symbolizes cosmic stability and the fragility of the world order. It is often depicted as a broken or crumbling pillar in illustrations of the Gonggong myth.

Behaviour & Personality

As a geographical feature, the mountain does not have personality. However, its mythological role is passive: it is a structural support that fails when struck.

Domains & Habitats

Located northwest of the Kunlun Mountains, in the direction associated with the northwest. It is part of the mythological geography of ancient China, often placed in the far west near the Moving Sands and Jade Mountain.

Version Differences

The Huainanzi and Liezi present slightly different versions of the Gonggong myth. In the Huainanzi, Gonggong smashes his head against Buzhou, breaking the pillar and causing the sky to tilt. In the Liezi, the same event is recounted but with more detail about the cosmic consequences. The Classic of Mountains and Seas mentions Buzhou as a geographical feature but does not include the Gonggong episode.

Stories & Myths

Gonggong's Rebellion and the Breaking of the Pillar

Source Huainanzi, Liezi, and other textsCulture ChinesePeriod Attested from the Han dynasty (2nd century BCE) onward; likely earlier oral origins

Summary

The water god Gonggong, in a fit of rage over a cosmic dispute (often with Zhurong, the fire god, or over control of the world), smashed his head against Mount Buzhou, the pillar supporting the northwest corner of the sky. The pillar broke, causing the sky to tilt northwest and the earth to sink in the southeast. This is why the sun, moon, and stars move toward the northwest and why all rivers in China flow southeast. The goddess Nüwa later repaired the sky by melting stones of five colors.

Participants

Gonggong

Zhurong (in some versions)

Nüwa (as repairer)

Consequences

Cosmic imbalance: the sky tilted, the earth shifted, rivers flow southeast. Nüwa's repair work is a separate but connected myth.

Variants

In some versions, Gonggong is defeated by Zhurong and then destroys the pillar in a final act of defiance. In others, he is fighting for supremacy with another deity.

Qu Yuan's Spiritual Journey

Source Li Sao (Songs of Chu)Culture Chinese (Chu)Period c. 3rd century BCE

Summary

In the poem Li Sao, the poet Qu Yuan, in his shamanic flight across the cosmos, passes by Mount Buzhou, using it as a waypoint in his journey. This indicates the mountain's significance in the Chu cultural imagination as a boundary or threshold.

Participants

Qu Yuan (the poet-narrator)

Consequences

Literary: establishes Buzhou as a poetic symbol of the far reaches of the world.

Variants

None noted.

Relationships

Gonggong

Type victimSource Huainanzi, Liezi

Description

Gonggong is the water god who destroyed the mountain by striking it with his head.

Version Note

In the Huainanzi, Gonggong acts out of anger; in the Liezi, the context is a battle with Zhurong.

Conflicting Accounts

The exact reason for Gonggong's anger varies; some sources say he lost a battle, others that he was envious of the fire god.

Nüwa

Type repairerSource Huainanzi

Description

After the pillar broke, Nüwa repaired the sky, but the earth remained tilted.

Version Note

Nüwa's repair is often told as a separate myth, but linked to Buzhou's destruction.

Conflicting Accounts

Some versions say Nüwa cut off the legs of a giant turtle to support the sky, others that she melted stones.

Dark History

Divine violence and cosmic catastrophe

Source Huainanzi, Liezi

Description

The myth of Gonggong destroying Mount Buzhou involves self-harm and the destruction of a world-supporting structure, leading to widespread environmental consequences.

Scholarly Context

Scholars interpret this as an etiological myth explaining natural phenomena (river flow, celestial motion) and possibly reflecting ancient power struggles.

Contradictions & Disputes

Location of Mount Buzhou

Position A

Mythological: located northwest of Kunlun, not identifiable on a map.

Position A — evidence

Classic of Mountains and Seas and other mythological texts describe it in symbolic geography.

Position B

Historical-geographical: some scholars identify it with the Pamir Mountains or other real ranges.

Position B — evidence

Modern attempts to locate mythical places often seek real-world counterparts, but this is speculative.

Scholarly Consensus

Most scholars treat Buzhou as purely mythological; any identification with real mountains is conjectural.

Cause of Gonggong's anger

Position A

Gonggong was fighting Zhurong, the fire god, for supremacy.

Position A — evidence

Huainanzi version mentions a battle with Zhurong.

Position B

Gonggong was envious of the god Zhuanxu's rule.

Position B — evidence

Some later texts attribute the conflict to a struggle with Zhuanxu.

Scholarly Consensus

There is no single canonical version; the myth has multiple variants.

Number of pillars

Position A

There are eight pillars, one for each cardinal and intercardinal direction.

Position A — evidence

Hawkes (2011) describes eight pillars.

Position B

Some texts mention four pillars, one for each cardinal direction.

Position B — evidence

Other sources, such as the Huainanzi, may refer to four pillars in different contexts.

Scholarly Consensus

The eight-pillar scheme is common, but variations exist.
