Who It Is

On the banks of the Hi River in Izumo, an old couple weeps over their last surviving daughter. Seven times they have watched a crimson-eyed, eight-headed serpent come for a child, and seven times they have been powerless. When a ragged stranger with a sword at his hip asks why they grieve, they answer with names that sound like a caress — Ashinazuchi, the Foot-Stroking Elder, and Tenazuchi, the Hand-Stroking Elder — and set in motion the most famous monster-slaying in Japanese mythology.

Behavior is consistent across versions: lamentation, helplessness, deference to a superior kami (Susanoo), and compliance with his plan. Kojiki: they weep because each year the serpent has come and they cannot protect their children; when Susanoo offers to save Kushinadahime in exchange for marriage, they consent immediately and follow his instructions to brew sake and prepare. Nihon Shoki adds their genealogical self-identification: 'We are children of Ohoyamatsumi, the Great Mountain Possessor.' Powers
Status as Earthly Kami (Kunitsukami)

The couple are classified as kunitsukami (earthly deities) as opposed to amatsukami (heavenly deities). This confers divine longevity and ancestral authority but not the storm or martial powers of Susanoo. Their divinity is attested by Susanoo's address and by their claimed descent from Oyamatsumi.

Limit: No supernatural feats are attributed to them; they are explicitly helpless against Yamata no Orochi and require Susanoo's intervention. They cannot protect their own children.

Sake Brewing and Ritual Preparation

On Susanoo's instruction, the couple brews eight-fold sake (yashio) and builds an eight-gated fence with eight platforms, each holding a vat of sake to intoxicate the serpent. This is technical/ritual knowledge rather than magical power, but it is essential to the monster's defeat.

Limit: The brewing is done at Susanoo's direction; they do not invent the plan. The sake itself is not magical without the serpent's susceptibility.

Protective Parenthood and Hospitality (Social Power)

Their primary narrative function is parental: they offer hospitality to a wandering deity (Susanoo), negotiate a marriage alliance (offering Kushinadahime), and secure divine protection through kinship. This reflects Yamato-period social values where marriage alliance creates obligation.

Limit: Their protection fails for seven daughters; only through external alliance is the eighth saved. No apotropaic power is shown.

Ancestral Authority / Ujigami Function (Later Shrine Tradition)

In medieval and early modern shrine theology, Ashinazuchi and Tenazuchi are venerated as ancestral/parental kami who confer legitimacy on the Inada region and on Kushinadahime's descendants. Prayers for safe childbirth, child protection, and family harmony are directed to them at Inada Jinja.

Limit: This is a post-classical cultic development, not attested in 8th-century myth. No classical text attributes oracular or blessing powers to them.

Accounts I
The Weeping Parents on the Hi River — Kojiki Main AccountKojiki, Book 1, Section 19 (712 CE)

Nara period codification of earlier oral tradition (mythic time: Age of the Gods)

Susanoo, expelled from the High Plain of Heaven, descends to the headwaters of the Hi River in Izumo where he finds an old man and old woman weeping with a young girl between them. Asked why they weep, Ashinazuchi explains: 'I am an earthly deity, child of Ohoyamatsumi. My wife is Tenazuchi. We had eight daughters, but each year the eight-forked serpent of Koshi, Yamata no Orochi, has come and devoured one. Now only Kushinadahime remains, and the serpent is due to come.' Its eyes are like red cherries, its body spans eight valleys and eight hills, its belly always bloody. Susanoo asks for Kushinadahime's hand in marriage in exchange for slaying the serpent; the couple consents. Susanoo transforms Kushinadahime into a comb and places her in his hair, instructs the couple to brew eight-fold sake and build an eight-gated fence, intoxicates the serpent, and slays it, finding the sword Kusanagi in its tail. Kushinadahime is saved and marries Susanoo; their child Yashimajinumi (also called Yashimajinumi no Kami) continues the Izumo lineage leading to Okuninushi; the sword Kusanagi becomes

Nihon Shoki Main Text — Eight Vats of SakeNihon Shoki, Book 1, Jindai 1, main text (720 CE)

Nara period

Essentially the same as Kojiki but with Classical Chinese phrasing and added detail: Susanoo meets Ashinazuchi and Tenazuchi weeping; they state they are descendants of Oyamatsumi and had eight daughters, seven already taken by Yamata no Orochi. Susanoo promises to kill the beast if given Kushinada-hime. He orders the couple to brew eight vats of sake, build a fence with eight gates, place a vat at each gate, and wait. The serpent arrives, drinks, becomes drunk, and is dismembered. The text emphasizes the brewing as a task performed by the couple under divine direction. Reinforces the Kojiki narrative as official history; establishes the sake-intoxication motif as canonical. Provides locus classicus for sake's mythic origin.

Nihon Shoki Variant 1 — Inada no Miyanushi Susa no YatsumimiNihon Shoki, Book 1, Variant note (一書曰) (720 CE)

Nara period

One account says: At the headwaters of the Hi River there was a man called Inada no Miyanushi Susa no Yatsumimi (Lord of Inada, Susa's Eight-Eared Master). His daughter was Inada-hime. Susanoo met him and asked why he wept; he answered that Yamata no Orochi had devoured his other daughters. This variant omits the paired elders entirely, presenting a single patriarchal figure. Scholars read this as a localized Inada tradition where the father is a chieftain rather than a divine couple, later harmonized with the Ashinazuchi/Tenazuchi version. Creates the central contradiction in the dossier: are Ashinazuchi/Tenazuchi distinct kami or epithets for the Inada chieftain? Medieval commentators (e.g., Kaneyoshi) attempted to harmonize by making Susa no Yatsumimi an alternate name for Ashinazuchi.

Accounts II
Nihon Shoki Variant 2 — Genealogical Claim to OyamatsumiNihon Shoki, Book 1, Variant note 2 (720 CE)

Nara period

In this variant, when Susanoo asks 'Who are you?' Ashinazuchi answers at length: 'I am the son of Oyamatsumi, grandson of ...' tracing a genealogy that links the Izumo earthly kami to the mountain deity Oyamatsumi, who is also father of Konohanasakuya-hime. Tenazuchi is named as his wife but does not speak. The variant dwells on lineage to legitimize Kushinadahime as a suitable bride for a heavenly deity (Susanoo), addressing status asymmetry between amatsukami and kunitsukami. Provides the only genealogical anchor for the couple, making them descendants of Oyamatsumi and thus distant kin to other major kami. This genealogy is repeated in later genealogical compendia (Shinsen Shojiroku).

Aftermath — Marriage of Kushinadahime and Birth of YashimajinumiKojiki Book 1, Section 20-21; Nihon Shoki Book 1 continuation

Nara period

After the slaying, Susanoo builds a palace at Suga in Izumo and makes Kushinadahime his wife. In Kojiki, Ashinazuchi and Tenazuchi are then made heads (miyanushi) of Suga, or in some readings, given the palace to dwell in — a reward for their daughter. Kushinadahime bears Yashimajinumi (also called Yashimajinumi no Kami), who marries Konohanachiru-hime and fathers Fuha no Moji Ku-nu-su-nu, continuing the line to Okuninushi (Ohnamuchi). The elders thus become maternal grandparents of the central Izumo deity Okuninushi, linking them to the entire kunitsukami genealogy. Establishes the couple as ancestors of the Izumo divine dynasty and, through Okuninushi's eventual submission to the heavenly grandson Ninigi, as ancestors integrated into the imperial myth. Explains why they are enshrined jointly with Susanoo and Kushinadahime.

Living Tradition — Iwami Kagura 'Orochi' PerformanceIwami Kagura (石見神楽), designated Important Intangible Folk Cultural Property, Shimane Prefecture; performance tradition documented from Edo period to present

Edo period (17th-19th c.) to contemporary (recorded performances 1970s-present)

In the kagura play 'Yamata no Orochi,' Ashinazuchi and Tenazuchi appear as masked elders (okina and uba) who enter weeping, carrying or shielding Kushinadahime (often a child dancer or doll). They deliver extended lamentation dialogues in local dialect, not found in Kojiki, describing each lost daughter by name, and interact comically/tragically with Susanoo before the serpent (played by up to eight costumed dancers with a long serpent body) appears. The sake-brewing is staged with large prop vats. The performance ends with Susanoo's victory and the elders' rejoicing. This is not a classical text but a living oral/performative variant that expands the couple's emotional role. Keeps the couple's story in active community ritual; provides the most vivid modern visualization of Tenazuchi. Demonstrates how a brief classical episode is elaborated in folk practice. Tourist and shrine performances now number hundreds annually.

Etymology of -nazuchi / -nadzuchi'Stroking Elder' — from nadzu 'to stroke, caress' + chi 'elder/deity.' Names mean 'Foot-Stroking Elder' and 'Hand-Stroking Elder,' describing the couple caressing their daughter in grief.'Name-Mallet / Name-Elder' — from na 'name' + tsuchi 'hammer, mallet' or 'earth,' meaning 'Foot-Name-Mallet' / 'Hand-Name-Mallet,' possibly a title for a clan or tool association.

Number and identity of daughtersEight daughters total, seven devoured, Kushinadahime is the eighth and sole survivor (Kojiki/Nihon Shoki main text).Variant folk and medieval accounts suggest different numbers (e.g., eight including Kushinadahime were all to be devoured, or the daughters had individual names).

Ashinazuchi and Tenazuchi are major gods with their own shrines and powers like Amaterasu or Susanoo. — They are minor, single-episode kami whose only attested myth is as the grieving parents in the Yamata no Orochi story. They have no independent myths, no domain over nature, and are enshrined only jointly with Susanoo and Kushinadahime as attendant/ancestral kami, not as major independent cults.

Tenazuchi is a male deity or the names are interchangeable. — In Kojiki and Nihon Shoki main text, Ashinazuchi is explicitly the husband (old man) and Tenazuchi the wife (old woman). The pairing is gendered and consistent. Confusion arises because both names share the -nazuchi suffix and some databases list Tenazuchi alone without context.

The seven devoured daughters are named in the Kojiki (e.g., as Inada-hime variations). — Kojiki and Nihon Shoki never name the seven lost daughters; only Kushinadahime/Inadahime is named. Any list of seven names (sometimes seen online) is a modern invention or from Kagura fiction, not classical text.

Ashinazuchi and Tenazuchi brewed magical sake that alone could kill Yamata no Orochi. — The sake is described as strong eight-fold sake (yashio) that intoxicates the serpent, but the slaying is done by Susanoo's sword. The couple's role is preparation under Susanoo's instruction, not magical brewing. The serpent's drunkenness makes it vulnerable; the sake is not poison.

The Yamata no Orochi story is proof of ancient human sacrifice in Japan. — The story uses a folktale motif (dragon demanding maidens, ATU 300) and may metaphorize river flooding or political tribute, but there is no archaeological or textual evidence for historical annual child sacrifice in Izumo. Scholars treat it as myth, not history.
