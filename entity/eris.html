Who It Is

Imagine being the only god not invited to the party of the millennium—the wedding of a mortal hero and a sea-nymph, attended by every deity in the pantheon. Most would sulk. Eris, the goddess of discord, instead rolled a golden apple into the crowd and started the most destructive chain reaction in Western mythology: the Trojan War. She didn't need an army or a weapon. She just needed a single word—'kallisti'—and the vanity of three goddesses to do the rest. Eris is the goddess who proves that the most powerful force in the universe isn't lightning or the sea, but a well-timed insult.

Eris is not a subtle goddess. She delights in the clash of arms, the shouting of armies, and the weeping of widows. Homer calls her 'insatiable' and 'relentless'. She is the sister and companion of Ares, the god of war, and she thrives in his wake. Yet her domain is broader than just battlefields—she is present in any quarrel, any family feud, any political schism. She is the goddess of the argument that starts over nothing and ends with everything in ruins. Name & Tongue

Etymology — From the ancient Greek ἔρις (éris), meaning 'strife', 'discord', or 'quarrel'. The word is used in Homeric Greek both as a common noun for conflict and as a proper name for the goddess personifying it. The Roman equivalent, Discordia, derives from the Latin discors, meaning 'disagreeing' or 'at vari

Pronunciation — AIR-iss (English); É-ris (Classical Greek, with a rolled 'r' and short 'e' as in 'bet')

Aliases — Discordia, Eris of the Golden Apple, The Strife-Bringer, Daughter of Nyx

Ownership — Ancient Greek, later adopted into Roman religion and literature. Modern interpretations are largely Western, influenced by classical reception, Discordianism, and popular culture.

Powers
Incarnation of Strife

Eris does not merely cause conflict; she is conflict. Her presence on a battlefield or in a home is enough to escalate tensions. She is not a warrior goddess who fights with weapons; she fights with whispers, suspicions, and wounded pride.

Limit: Her power is indirect. She cannot force anyone to fight; she can only create the conditions where fighting seems inevitable. She is powerless in a society without ego or ambition.

Growth and Expansion

As described in the Iliad, Eris begins small and grows to colossal size. This is not just a physical transformation but a metaphor for her influence—a small slight can snowball into a war.

Limit: This growth seems tied to the escalation of the conflict she is feeding. If the conflict is resolved, she presumably shrinks back to nothing.

Progenitor of Evils

In Hesiod's Theogony, Eris gives birth to a host of personified evils: Ponos (Toil), Lethe (Forgetfulness), Limos (Famine), Algea (Pains), Hysminai (Battles), Makhai (Wars), Phonoi (Murders), Androktasiai (Manslaughters), Neikea (Quarrels), Pseudologoi (Lies), Amphilogiai (Disputes), Dysnomia (Lawlessness), and Ate (Ruin).

Limit: She is the mother, not the direct agent, of these evils. They act independently once born.

The Golden Apple

Eris's most potent single act—rolling a golden apple inscribed with 'kallisti' into a gathering of goddesses—is not a physical power but a psychological weapon. It exploits vanity and rivalry, setting off a chain of events that leads to the Trojan War.

Limit: The apple only works on beings with vanity. It would be useless against a truly humble person.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Homer's most famous description of Eris in the Iliad is paradoxical: she begins as a small, barely noticeable figure, but as she strides through the battlefield, she grows until her head touches the heavens. This visual metaphor captures her essence—discord often starts small, a whispered rumor or a slight, but can escalate into something cosmic in scale. Symbols — The Golden Apple of Discord is her most infamous symbol, inscribed with the word 'kallisti' (to the fairest). She is also associated with torches (symbolizing the fire of conflict), snakes (representing venomous words), and the whip or goad. In Roman art, Discordia is sometimes shown tearing her hair or rending her garments, a visual representation of the civil strife she embodies.

Domains — Eris is most at home on the battlefield, but she is also found at weddings, banquets, and councils—anywhere people gather and egos clash. Her most famous appearance is at the wedding of Peleus and Thetis, where she arrives uninvited to sow discord among the gods. She has no fixed sanctuary or temple in the historical record, which is fitting—she is a force of displacement and disruption, not a goddess of hearth or home.

Accounts I
The Wedding of Peleus and ThetisCypria (lost epic), summarized by Proclus; later told by many authors including Lucian and Hyginus

Archaic to Classical period (the story is assumed to be old, but first attested in the Epic Cycle)

All the gods and goddesses are invited to the wedding of the mortal Peleus and the sea-nymph Thetis—except Eris. Furious at being excluded, she crashes the party and rolls a golden apple among the guests, inscribed with the word 'kallisti' (to the fairest). Hera, Athena, and Aphrodite each claim the apple, and Zeus, wisely refusing to judge, sends them to the mortal prince Paris of Troy. Paris, bribed by Aphrodite's promise of the most beautiful woman in the world (Helen), awards her the apple. This sets in motion the abduction of Helen and the Trojan War. The Trojan War, the destruction of Troy, countless deaths, and the eventual fall of many heroes. The Judgement of Paris is often cited as the proximate cause of the war, making Eris the goddess who indirectly killed more people than Ares himself.

Accounts II
Eris in the IliadHomer, Iliad

c. 8th century BCE

Throughout the Iliad, Eris appears as a background force driving the conflict. She is described as the sister of Ares, growing from a small figure to a giant as the battle intensifies. Zeus sends her to the Achaean ships to rouse the warriors. She is present in the melee, rejoicing in the slaughter. Her presence ensures the war continues, preventing any lasting peace. She embodies the relentless, self-perpetuating nature of conflict.

The Birth of Eris's ChildrenHesiod, Theogony

c. 700 BCE

Hesiod lists Eris as the daughter of Nyx (Night), born without a father. She then gives birth, also without a father, to a catalogue of evils: Toil, Forgetfulness, Famine, Pains, Battles, Wars, Murders, Quarrels, Lies, and Ruin. This genealogy establishes Eris as a primordial, self-replicating force of negativity. This genealogy provides a mythological explanation for the existence of suffering and conflict in the world—they are the natural children of Strife.

The Duality of ErisThere are two distinct Eris figures in Hesiod's Works and Days: one who fosters healthy competition and one who incites war.There is only one Eris, and Hesiod is describing two aspects of her nature rather than two separate beings.
The Antiquity of the Golden Apple MythThe story of the Judgement of Paris is an ancient, integral part of the Trojan War mythos.The story is a later addition, first attested in the Epic Cycle and possibly a post-Homeric invention.

Eris is the same as Ares, the god of war. — Ares is the god of the physical act of war—fighting, bloodshed, and courage. Eris is the goddess of the emotional and social state of discord—strife, quarrels, and rivalry. She is the cause, not the executioner.

Eris is a purely evil figure. — Hesiod's Works and Days presents a 'good' Eris who inspires healthy competition and drives human progress. This duality is often overlooked in modern retellings.

Eris is a minor goddess with little importance. — While she has no major temples or cults, Eris is a cosmic force in Greek mythology. Her actions directly cause the Trojan War, the most significant event in the Greek mythic cycle, and she is the mother of many personified evils.
