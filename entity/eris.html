Who It Is

Eris is the Greek goddess of strife, discord, and rivalry — a disruptive force who delights in conflict and the collapse of harmony. In Hesiod's Theogony, she is the daughter of Nyx (Night), and the mother of numerous personified evils: Ponos (Toil), Lethe (Forgetfulness), Limos (Famine), Algea (Pains), Hysminai (Battles), and Phonoi (Murders). But Eris is best known for her starring role in the myth that began the Trojan War: offended at not being invited to the wedding of Peleus and Thetis, she tossed the golden apple 'for the fairest' (tē kallistēi) among the three goddesses Hera, Athena, and Aphrodite, sparking the Judgment of Paris that led to the Trojan War. In Hesiod's Works and Days, however, Eris has a dual nature: there is a 'good Eris' who inspires healthy competition and craftsmanship (a more archaic, less malicious figure), and a 'bad Eris' who drives men to war. This duality has been explored by later philosophers and poets — the idea that conflict is both destructive and creative. In the Hellenistic period, the literary tradition generally focused on her malevolent aspect, making Eris the archetypal troublemaker goddess. Her Roman counterpart is Discordia, and she remains a potent symbol of the chaos that lurks beneath the order of civilization.

The Approach

Imagine being the only god not invited to the party of the millennium—the wedding of a mortal hero and a sea-nymph, attended by every deity in the pantheon. Most would sulk. Eris, the goddess of discord, instead rolled a golden apple into the crowd and started the most destructive chain reaction in Western mythology: the Trojan War. She didn't need an army or a weapon. She just needed a single word—'kallisti'—and the vanity of three goddesses to do the rest. Eris is the goddess who proves that the most powerful force in the universe isn't lightning or the sea, but a well-timed insult.

Identity

Eris

Pronunciation AIR-iss (English); É-ris (Classical Greek, with a rolled 'r' and short 'e' as in 'bet')

Known As

Etymology

From the ancient Greek ἔρις (éris), meaning 'strife', 'discord', or 'quarrel'. The word is used in Homeric Greek both as a common noun for conflict and as a proper name for the goddess personifying it. The Roman equivalent, Discordia, derives from the Latin discors, meaning 'disagreeing' or 'at variance'.

Cultural Ownership

Ancient Greek, later adopted into Roman religion and literature. Modern interpretations are largely Western, influenced by classical reception, Discordianism, and popular culture.

Disputed Identifications

In Hesiod's Works and Days, Eris is presented as having a dual nature—one that fosters healthy competition and another that incites war and conflict. Some scholars argue these are two separate aspects of the same goddess, while others see them as distinct entities that Hesiod conflated. The Roman Discordia is often treated as a direct equivalent, but Roman sources sometimes emphasize her role in civil strife rather than personal quarrels, reflecting Roman political anxieties.

Description

Appearance

Homer's most famous description of Eris in the Iliad is paradoxical: she begins as a small, barely noticeable figure, but as she strides through the battlefield, she grows until her head touches the heavens. This visual metaphor captures her essence—discord often starts small, a whispered rumor or a slight, but can escalate into something cosmic in scale. Later Greek and Roman art often depicts her as a woman with wild, unkempt hair, sometimes holding a torch or a snake, symbols of the chaos she unleashes. She is rarely shown as beautiful or serene; her face is usually twisted in fury or gleeful malice.

Symbols & Iconography

The Golden Apple of Discord is her most infamous symbol, inscribed with the word 'kallisti' (to the fairest). She is also associated with torches (symbolizing the fire of conflict), snakes (representing venomous words), and the whip or goad. In Roman art, Discordia is sometimes shown tearing her hair or rending her garments, a visual representation of the civil strife she embodies.

Behaviour & Personality

Eris is not a subtle goddess. She delights in the clash of arms, the shouting of armies, and the weeping of widows. Homer calls her 'insatiable' and 'relentless'. She is the sister and companion of Ares, the god of war, and she thrives in his wake. Yet her domain is broader than just battlefields—she is present in any quarrel, any family feud, any political schism. She is the goddess of the argument that starts over nothing and ends with everything in ruins. Unlike Ares, who enjoys the physical act of fighting, Eris enjoys the emotional and social devastation that conflict leaves behind.

Domains & Habitats

Eris is most at home on the battlefield, but she is also found at weddings, banquets, and councils—anywhere people gather and egos clash. Her most famous appearance is at the wedding of Peleus and Thetis, where she arrives uninvited to sow discord among the gods. She has no fixed sanctuary or temple in the historical record, which is fitting—she is a force of displacement and disruption, not a goddess of hearth or home.

Version Differences

Hesiod's Works and Days presents a significant internal contradiction: he describes two Eris figures. One is 'good'—she inspires the potter to compete with potter, the bard with bard, driving human progress. The other is 'bad'—she incites war and bloodshed. This duality is unique to Hesiod and may reflect a philosophical attempt to reconcile the existence of conflict with a just world order. Homer's Eris is uniformly negative. Roman sources, particularly those dealing with civil war (like Lucan's Pharsalia), emphasize Discordia as a political force that tears states apart, a more specifically civic version of the Greek goddess.

Powers & Abilities

Incarnation of Strife

Source Version Homer, Iliad; Hesiod, Theogony

Description

Eris does not merely cause conflict; she is conflict. Her presence on a battlefield or in a home is enough to escalate tensions. She is not a warrior goddess who fights with weapons; she fights with whispers, suspicions, and wounded pride.

Limitations

Her power is indirect. She cannot force anyone to fight; she can only create the conditions where fighting seems inevitable. She is powerless in a society without ego or ambition.

Contradictions

None noted.

Growth and Expansion

Source Version Homer, Iliad, Book 4

Description

As described in the Iliad, Eris begins small and grows to colossal size. This is not just a physical transformation but a metaphor for her influence—a small slight can snowball into a war.

Limitations

This growth seems tied to the escalation of the conflict she is feeding. If the conflict is resolved, she presumably shrinks back to nothing.

Contradictions

None noted.

Progenitor of Evils

Source Version Hesiod, Theogony, lines 225-232

Description

In Hesiod's Theogony, Eris gives birth to a host of personified evils: Ponos (Toil), Lethe (Forgetfulness), Limos (Famine), Algea (Pains), Hysminai (Battles), Makhai (Wars), Phonoi (Murders), Androktasiai (Manslaughters), Neikea (Quarrels), Pseudologoi (Lies), Amphilogiai (Disputes), Dysnomia (Lawlessness), and Ate (Ruin).

Limitations

She is the mother, not the direct agent, of these evils. They act independently once born.

Contradictions

None noted.

The Golden Apple

Source Version Later Greek myth (the Cypria, lost; summarized by Proclus)

Description

Eris's most potent single act—rolling a golden apple inscribed with 'kallisti' into a gathering of goddesses—is not a physical power but a psychological weapon. It exploits vanity and rivalry, setting off a chain of events that leads to the Trojan War.

Limitations

The apple only works on beings with vanity. It would be useless against a truly humble person.

Contradictions

The story of the Judgement of Paris is not in Homer or Hesiod. It is first attested in the Epic Cycle, specifically the Cypria, which survives only in summary. Some scholars argue it was a later addition to the mythos.

Stories & Myths

The Wedding of Peleus and Thetis

Source Cypria (lost epic), summarized by Proclus; later told by many authors including Lucian and HyginusCulture GreekPeriod Archaic to Classical period (the story is assumed to be old, but first attested in the Epic Cycle)

Summary

All the gods and goddesses are invited to the wedding of the mortal Peleus and the sea-nymph Thetis—except Eris. Furious at being excluded, she crashes the party and rolls a golden apple among the guests, inscribed with the word 'kallisti' (to the fairest). Hera, Athena, and Aphrodite each claim the apple, and Zeus, wisely refusing to judge, sends them to the mortal prince Paris of Troy. Paris, bribed by Aphrodite's promise of the most beautiful woman in the world (Helen), awards her the apple. This sets in motion the abduction of Helen and the Trojan War.

Consequences

The Trojan War, the destruction of Troy, countless deaths, and the eventual fall of many heroes. The Judgement of Paris is often cited as the proximate cause of the war, making Eris the goddess who indirectly killed more people than Ares himself.

Variants

Some versions say the apple was inscribed 'for the fairest' without specifying who the judge would be. Others say Eris specifically intended the apple for the three most vain goddesses. The story is absent from Homer, leading to scholarly debate about its antiquity.

Eris in the Iliad

Source Homer, IliadCulture GreekPeriod c. 8th century BCE

Summary

Throughout the Iliad, Eris appears as a background force driving the conflict. She is described as the sister of Ares, growing from a small figure to a giant as the battle intensifies. Zeus sends her to the Achaean ships to rouse the warriors. She is present in the melee, rejoicing in the slaughter.

Participants

Eris

Ares

Zeus

The Achaean and Trojan armies

Consequences

Her presence ensures the war continues, preventing any lasting peace. She embodies the relentless, self-perpetuating nature of conflict.

Variants

Homer never mentions the Golden Apple. His Eris is a more abstract, atmospheric force of war rather than a scheming individual.

The Birth of Eris's Children

Source Hesiod, TheogonyCulture GreekPeriod c. 700 BCE

Summary

Hesiod lists Eris as the daughter of Nyx (Night), born without a father. She then gives birth, also without a father, to a catalogue of evils: Toil, Forgetfulness, Famine, Pains, Battles, Wars, Murders, Quarrels, Lies, and Ruin. This genealogy establishes Eris as a primordial, self-replicating force of negativity.

Participants

Eris

Nyx

Her numerous offspring

Consequences

This genealogy provides a mythological explanation for the existence of suffering and conflict in the world—they are the natural children of Strife.

Variants

Hyginus, a Roman mythographer, gives Eris a father (Erebus) and places her in a slightly different cosmic hierarchy.

Goddess of War and Slaughter

Source Hesiod, Theogony; Homer, Iliad

Description

Eris is not a gentle or benign deity. She is explicitly associated with battles, murders, and manslaughter in Hesiod's Theogony. Her delight in the destruction of the Trojan War is a central part of her myth.

Scholarly Context

Ancient Greek religion did not sanitize its gods. Eris represents the terrifying, chaotic aspects of human nature that the Greeks recognized as divine forces.

Psychological Manipulation

Source Cypria (summary)

Description

The Golden Apple is a weapon of psychological warfare. Eris exploits the vanity and rivalry of three goddesses, knowing that their pride will lead to catastrophic consequences. This is a form of indirect, malicious manipulation.

Scholarly Context

This act is often analyzed as a commentary on the destructive power of vanity and the ease with which it can be weaponized.

Contradictions & Disputes

The Duality of Eris

Position A

There are two distinct Eris figures in Hesiod's Works and Days: one who fosters healthy competition and one who incites war.

Position A — evidence

Hesiod explicitly says 'there is not only one kind of Strife' and describes them with different attributes.

Position B

There is only one Eris, and Hesiod is describing two aspects of her nature rather than two separate beings.

Position B — evidence

Both are described as 'Eris', and the broader mythological tradition treats her as a single goddess.

Scholarly Consensus

There is no consensus. Some scholars see a deliberate philosophical distinction, while others see a rhetorical flourish.

The Antiquity of the Golden Apple Myth

Position A

The story of the Judgement of Paris is an ancient, integral part of the Trojan War mythos.

Position A — evidence

The story is found in the Cypria, which was part of the Epic Cycle and dates to the Archaic period, even if the text is lost.

Position B

The story is a later addition, first attested in the Epic Cycle and possibly a post-Homeric invention.

Position B — evidence

Homer does not mention the Judgement of Paris in the Iliad or Odyssey, which would be a strange omission if the story were central to the war's cause.

Scholarly Consensus

The scholarly consensus leans toward the story being old, but its exact date and origin are unknown. It may have been a later elaboration on an older tradition.

Type textDate c. 700 BCELocator Theogony, line 225: 'And hateful Eris bore painful Ponos (Toil)...'Language Ancient Greek

Translation

In the Theogony, Eris is the daughter of Nyx (Night), born without a father. She gives birth to a brood of personified evils: Toil, Forgetfulness, Famine, Sorrows, Battles, Murders, Quarrels, Lies, and Oath. In Works and Days, Hesiod distinguishes between a 'good' Eris (who spurs competition) and a 'bad' Eris (who incites war).

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

The full text of the Cypria, the epic that detailed the Judgement of Paris, is lost. All knowledge of it comes from later summaries. Direct archaeological evidence of a cult to Eris is absent. The exact nature of Hesiod's 'good' Eris remains a topic of scholarly debate.
