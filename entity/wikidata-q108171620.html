Who It Is

The name Capote looks simple on a book spine, but it splinters the moment you pull it. One Capote is a 5'3" boy from Monroeville and New Orleans who taught America how to listen to murderers. Another is a band of Núuchi-u (Ute) people whose name was written down by Spanish and American officials who barely understood it. Another is a heavy wool coat cut from a Hudson's Bay blanket, another is the pink cape a matador snaps at a bull, another is a dark silk frock coat buttoned for Sabbath in Brooklyn and Jerusalem, and another is a bay colt who burned a mile in 1:43 at Belmont. They share letters, not history — and only one of them ever claimed he invented a new kind of truth.

Truman Capote: attested as precociously verbal, socially brilliant, and acutely observant; also described as manipulative, indiscreet, and self-mythologizing. Harper Lee called him a 'precocious, difficult' childhood friend. Contemporaries (Dunphy, Clarke, Plimpton) document charm that elicited confidences, followed by betrayal via publication (notably Answered Prayers excerpts). Powers
Invention of the Nonfiction Novel (Literary Technique)

Capote claimed to create a new form where factual reporting is structured with novelistic techniques — scene-setting, dialogue reconstruction, interior perspective, and narrative pacing — while asserting every word was verifiably true. In Cold Blood is the exemplar.

Limit: Claim disputed; contemporaries (Lillian Ross, John Hersey's Hiroshima 1946) preceded him. Capote's assertion of 95% verbatim recall without notes is not supported by surviving notebooks and is contradicted by witnesses. Later fact-checks found composite scenes and invented dialogue.

Interview and Elicitation (Social Ability)

Exceptional ability to elicit intimate confidences from both high-society women and convicted killers, attributed to childlike voice, non-threatening stature, performative vulnerability, and relentless follow-up correspondence and visits (e.g., 200+ hours with Perry Smith).

Limit: Dependent on alcohol and personal charisma; failed when trust was broken (swans after Answered Prayers). Could not elicit cooperation from Richard Hickock to same depth. Ethical cost — subjects felt betrayed.

Stylistic Precision and Dialogue Ear

Prose marked by crystalline sentences, precise visual detail, and uncanny reproduction of idiom — from Holly Golightly's Manhattan patter to Kansas farmers' plain speech. Early stories show Faulknerian Southern Gothic influence refined into lapidary clarity.

Limit: Style criticized as precious or mannered in early work; later unfinished Answered Prayers shows deterioration. Not a supernatural power.

Athletic Performance (Equine - Horse Capote)

As a racehorse, elite early speed and ability to carry speed over a mile at age two; won Breeders' Cup Juvenile (G1) 1986 by 1.25 lengths, Eclipse Award Champion 2-Year-Old Colt.

Limit: Did not train on at three; finished unplaced in Kentucky Derby (1987, 13th), never won beyond 1 1/16 miles; sire career modest (sired Grade 1 winner Capote Belle but not a leading sire). Fragile — retired after injury.

Functional Protection (Garment Capote)

Blanket capote provided windproof, water-resistant, warm outer layer using fulled wool point blanket; hood protected head/neck; could serve as blanket at night. Kapoteh provides modesty, identity signaling, and Sabbath distinction; matador capote provides visual lure and protection via size and movement.

Limit: Blanket capote heavy when wet, not waterproof; kapoteh not protective; matador capote offers no armor — symbolic tool only.

Accounts I
Other Voices, Other Rooms — Joel Knox's Search for His FatherTruman Capote, Other Voices, Other Rooms (Random House, 1948)

1948 (set in 1930s rural South)

Thirteen-year-old Joel Knox, lonely after his mother's death, travels from New Orleans to isolated Noon City to live with his estranged father Edward Sansom. He finds his father paralyzed and mute after a gun accident, and becomes entangled with his flamboyant transvestite cousin Randolph and the tomboy Idabel. The novel is a coming-of-age gothic about identity, sexuality, and abandonment, culminating in Joel's acceptance of a queer, liminal world rather than conventional masculinity. Largely autobiographical transposition of Capote's own childhood displacement. Established Capote at 23 as literary prodigy; infamous Harold Halma photograph on dust jacket created celebrity author image; criticized for precocious style but praised by Tennessee Williams; set template for Capote's lifelong theme of the sensitive outsider child.

Breakfast at Tiffany's — Holly Golightly in ManhattanTruman Capote, Breakfast at Tiffany's (Random House, 1958; originally in Esquire, Nov 1958)

1958 (set 1943-44, narrated retrospectively)

An unnamed narrator (writer, Capote surrogate) recalls his neighbor in a Manhattan brownstone, Holly Golightly — a 19-year-old country girl turned Manhattan café-society darling who lives off cocktail parties, declared income $50, and a dream of Tiffany's as a place where nothing bad could happen. She befriends the narrator, evades a gangster boyfriend (Sally Tomato), and ultimately abandons a wealthy Brazilian diplomat to seek herself. Unlike the film, the novella ends with Holly disappearing to Brazil/Africa, leaving her cat behind, and the narrator keeping her as memory. Created one of American literature's enduring heroines; defined New York 'it girl' archetype; film adaptation (1961) diverged sharply, romanticizing ending and casting Audrey Hepburn, which Capote resented (he wanted Marilyn Monroe). Novella's frank treatment of Holly's escort-adjacent survival was sanitized on screen.

In Cold Blood — The Clutter Murders and Their AftermathTruman Capote, In Cold Blood (The New Yorker serialization Sept-Oct 1965; Random House 1966)

1959-1965 (events Nov 15, 1959; trial 1960; executions April 14, 1965)

In Holcomb, Kansas, respected farmer Herbert Clutter, his wife Bonnie, and teenagers Nancy and Kenyon are bound and shotgunned by ex-convicts Perry Smith and Richard Hickock during a botched robbery based on a false tip of a safe full of cash. Capote reconstructs the Clutters' last day, the killers' drift across America, the Kansas Bureau of Investigation hunt led by Alvin Dewey, the trial in Garden City, and the five-year wait on death row, ending with the hangings at Lansing. Interwoven is Perry's traumatic childhood and Capote's growing entanglement with him. Bestseller, credited with popularizing the 'nonfiction novel' and true-crime genre; made Capote the most famous writer in America; raised ethical debates about novelistic liberties with fact, payment to killers, and exploitation of victims' families; Clutter family and Holcomb residents contested portrayal. Smith and Hickock were executed; Capote never completed another major book.

Accounts II
A Christmas Memory — Sook and Buddy's Fruitcake ChristmasTruman Capote, A Christmas Memory (Mademoiselle, Dec 1956; collected in Breakfast at Tiffany's, 1958)

1956, recalling early 1930s Alabama

Seven-year-old Buddy (Capote) and his elderly cousin Sook Faulk — his best friend, childlike and ostracized by family — save pennies to bake fruitcakes for strangers, make kites, and celebrate a last perfect Christmas together before Buddy is sent to military school. Narrated by adult Buddy looking back, it ends with Sook's death and the kite Buddy made still flying in memory. Based directly on Capote's life with his cousin Nanny Rumbley Faulk (Sook) in Monroeville. Became Capote's most beloved short work, perennially anthologized and adapted for television (Emmy-winning 1966 version with Geraldine Page, directed by Frank Perry, adapted by Capote). Established Capote's ability to write tender, non-cynical memoir, contrasting with later reputation for cruelty.

Answered Prayers (Unfinished) — La Côte Basque and the Fall of the SwansTruman Capote, Answered Prayers: The Unfinished Novel (excerpts in Esquire 1975-76; posthumous Viking Press 1986, ed. Joseph M. Fox)

1975-1984 (set in 1960s-70s New York high society)

Intended as Capote's Proustian magnum opus, a novel about high society narrated by P.B. Jones, a bisexual hustler-writer who betrays every confidence. Four published chapters — 'Mojave,' 'La Côte Basque 1965,' 'Unspoiled Monsters,' 'Kate McCloud' — thinly veiled real New York socialites (Babe Paley, Slim Keith, Lee Radziwill, Gloria Vanderbilt) in unflattering, sexually explicit, and gossipy portraits. 'La Côte Basque 1965' depicted characters recognizable as William and Babe Paley and others, revealing infidelities, abortions, and murders. Social exile: Capote's 'swans' cut him off permanently; Babe Paley never spoke to him again. The scandal destroyed his social standing and, combined with addiction, prevented completion. Only four chapters finished at his death; published fragment is 150 pages. Became cautionary tale about art vs. loyalty.

The Capote Band's Removal and Reservation — From Spanish Contact to Southern Ute ReservationVirginia McConnell Simmons, The Ute Indians of Utah, Colorado, and New Mexico (University Press of Colorado, 2000); Treaty of 1868; Annual Reports of Commissioner of Indian Affairs

1598-1895 (Spanish entrada to allotment)

Spanish colonists in New Mexico named three Southern Ute bands: Capote (Kapota), Mouache, and Weeminuche, based on territory along the upper Rio Grande and San Juan Mountains. The Capote Band wintered in the San Luis Valley and summered in the San Juans, trading buckskins and captives. After U.S. conquest (1848), successive treaties (1849, 1868, 1873 Brunot Agreement) stripped 90% of Ute land, culminating in forced removal to the Southern Ute Reservation in southwestern Colorado (1873-1895) after the Meeker Massacre (1879) led to removal of Northern Utes. Capote descendants today are part of the Southern Ute Indian Tribe (Ignacio, Colorado). Loss of 12 million acres; transition from seasonal hunting to forced agriculture and boarding schools; survival as federally recognized tribe with modern governance and energy resources (coal, gas). The exonym 'Capote' persists in histories but is not used as official tribal designation today.

Did Truman Capote invent the nonfiction novel?Capote invented a wholly new form — the nonfiction novel — with In Cold Blood, as he claimed in interviews 1966: 'I invented it; no one had done it before.'The form existed before: John Hersey's Hiroshima (1946), Lillian Ross's Picture (1952), and earlier documentary novels; Capote synthesized and marketed the term, not invented the technique.
Are the Capote, Mouache, and Weeminuche distinct tribes or seasonal bands of one Southern Ute group?Three distinct, politically autonomous bands with separate territories and headmen (early Bureau of Indian Affairs and Spanish records).Fluid, intermarrying seasonal divisions of a single Southern Ute population whose band identity shifted with resource availability and leadership; 'Capote' is an administrative convenience.
How much of In Cold Blood is verbatim fact vs. invention?Capote claimed 95% verbatim accuracy, based on memory without notes, and that every scene was fact-checked.Significant invention: composite characters (e.g., composite detective), invented dialogue, altered timelines, and suppressed facts (e.g., Clutter family complexities, Perry Smith's version contested).

Truman Capote's name was originally Truman Capote and he was related to the Capote Band of Utes or the capote garment family. — He was born Truman Streckfus Persons; Capote was his stepfather José García Capote's surname, taken in 1935. No relation to Ute band (Spanish exonym) or garment makers; homonym only.

Breakfast at Tiffany's film is faithful to Capote's novella and Holly Golightly ends up with Paul. — Capote's novella ends with Holly disappearing, alone, leaving her cat; the narrator never romances her. The 1961 film invented the romantic reunion and softened Holly's sex-work-adjacent survival to meet Production Code.

The Hudson's Bay blanket capote and the Hasidic kapoteh are the same coat. — Same French root (capote < cappa) but entirely different garments: blanket capote is a hooded wool blanket coat for fur-trade winter travel (often white with stripes); kapoteh is a long black silk/wool frock coat for Hasidic Sabbath dress, with religious law and dynastic variations. Construction, community, and meaning are distinct.

Capote (horse) was named after the blanket coat. — Thoroughbred names are chosen by breeders/owners; Capote (1984) was named after Truman Capote the author — common practice to name horses after famous people — not the garment, though the word's origin is the same French root.

In Cold Blood is a completely true, objective account and the 'nonfiction novel' means no invention. — Capote used composite characters, reconstructed dialogue from memory, altered chronology, and omitted inconvenient facts (e.g., Herbert Clutter's business troubles, Perry Smith's conflicting confession). The book is literary nonfiction with documented embellishments.
