Who It Is

Fuxi (Fúxī) is one of the three legendary sovereigns of ancient China, a primordial culture hero credited with laying the very foundations of Chinese civilization. He is most commonly depicted as a figure with a human upper body and a serpent's or dragon's tail, often intertwined with his sister-wife Nuwa. Together they represent the founding pair of humanity. Fuxi's mythological contributions are vast and fundamental: he is said to have invented the Eight Trigrams (Bagua), the core symbols of the I Ching (Book of Changes), after observing the patterns on the back of a dragon-horse emerging from the Yellow River (the Hetu diagram). This act established the basis for Chinese divination, philosophy, and cosmology. He taught humanity how to hunt, fish with nets, domesticate animals, cook food, and use fire productively. He also established the institution of marriage, created the first musical instruments (the guqin and the se), and is credited with the invention of writing, replacing the older system of knotted cords. His civilizing work marks the transition of humanity from a brutish, animal-like state to one of culture, order, and social organization. In Han dynasty iconography, Fuxi often holds a carpenter's square (ju), symbolizing his role as the architect of civilization who measured and ordered the world. As a mythological figure, he belongs to the oldest stratum of Chinese mythology, predating the structured Taoist pantheon and reflecting Neolithic cultural memories. He is the archetype of the sage-king whose wisdom transforms chaos into order.

Era Warring States period texts (c. 4th–3rd century BCE); Han dynasty tomb art

Form

Attested descriptions of Fuxi emphasize a hybrid, serpentine form. Han dynasty tomb art and later illustrations depict him with a human upper torso—often male, bearded, and clothed in robes or animal skins—and a long serpent or dragon tail instead of legs. In many images, his tail intertwines with that of Nüwa, forming a loop or knot that visually binds them as a primordial pair. Some representations show him with a leaf-wreathed or crown-like headdress emerging from a mountain, suggesting a fusion of human, vegetal, and geological motifs. Other depictions present him more human-like, with subtle snake characteristics such as scaled garments or a sinuous posture. These visual traditions are attested in archaeological finds and museum collections and are reinforced by textual references to Fuxi as “a divine being with a serpent’s body.” Later religious art sometimes humanizes him further, aligning his appearance with other anthropomorphic deities while retaining symbolic attributes such as the compass or the Eight Trigrams diagram.

Powers

Creation and ordering of humanity Fuxi is credited, together with Nüwa, with creating or re-creating humanity. In some narratives, Nüwa fashions humans from yellow earth or clay, while Fuxi establishes the social and marital structures that allow them to live as a stable community. His power here is not the physical molding of bodies but the ordering of human relationships—instituting marriage, kinship rules, and social norms. This civilizational creation is cosmic in scale, transforming scattered individuals into a coherent society. Huainanzi; later mythographic compilations; EBSCO Research Starter synthesis The division of labor between Fuxi and Nüwa varies by source: some emphasize Nüwa’s creative act and Fuxi’s institutional role; others blur the distinction. There is no single canonical account of how many humans they create or the exact mechanisms. The mythic timeframe is prehistorical and not tied to specific historical dates. Some sources treat Fuxi as directly creating humans alongside Nüwa; others restrict his role to social organization. Modern scholars debate whether the creation narrative is older than the flood-repopulation story or a later synthesis.

Invention of hunting and fishing Fuxi is widely credited with teaching humans how to hunt and fish effectively. This includes the use of nets, traps, and other implements, as well as the conceptual shift from purely gathering to managed exploitation of animal resources. These skills are presented as foundational technologies that allow early humans to survive and thrive. Shiji tradition (via later citations); Huainanzi; modern summaries (EBSCO, Britannica) The myths do not specify particular species, techniques, or regions; they operate at a symbolic level. Archaeological evidence for early hunting and fishing in China is independent of the Fuxi narrative and cannot be directly tied to him. Some regional traditions attribute aspects of hunting or fishing to other culture heroes or local spirits, but Fuxi’s overarching credit remains dominant in mainstream mythography.

Domestication of animals and animal husbandry Fuxi is said to have introduced animal domestication, transforming wild creatures into managed herds. This includes the keeping of livestock for food, labor, and ritual purposes. In mythic terms, this power represents the taming of nature and the establishment of a stable food supply. EBSCO Research Starter; International Journal of History article on Three Sovereigns; later encyclopedic sources The myths do not detail specific domesticated species or techniques. Historical domestication processes in Neolithic China are complex and multi-centered; Fuxi’s role is symbolic rather than historical. Some reconstructions emphasize Shennong’s role in agriculture and may downplay Fuxi’s contribution to domestication, but many sources treat their roles as complementary rather than mutually exclusive.

Institution of marriage and social norms One of Fuxi’s most prominent powers is the establishment of marriage as an institution. He is said to have created rules governing the union of men and women, ceremonies, and kinship structures. This transforms human reproduction from a chaotic or incestuous state into a regulated social practice, aligning family life with cosmic order. Shiji tradition; Huainanzi; EBSCO Research Starter; modern historical analyses of San Huang Details of the marriage rites are not preserved in early texts; later ritual manuals are not explicitly attributed to Fuxi. The mythic narrative simplifies complex historical developments in kinship and marriage practices. Some feminist and critical-decolonial readings question whether attributing marriage to a single male culture hero obscures the role of women and communities in shaping social norms. These critiques focus on modern ideological uses of the myth rather than ancient evidence.

Invention of the Eight Trigrams (bagua) Fuxi is traditionally credited with deriving the Eight Trigrams (bagua) from observations of nature. By studying patterns in heaven, earth, water, fire, thunder, wind, mountain, and lake, he discerned eight fundamental configurations of yin and yang lines. These trigrams became the basis for the Yijing (I Ching) divination system and later cosmological and philosophical frameworks. Later commentarial traditions on the Yijing; Wikipedia and Britannica summaries; EBSCO Research Starter The earliest textual evidence for Fuxi’s direct authorship of the bagua is relatively late and embedded in interpretive traditions. The historical development of the Yijing is complex, and attributing the trigrams to Fuxi is part of a mythic genealogy rather than a verifiable historical fact. Some scholars argue that the association of Fuxi with the bagua is a Han or later construction designed to give the Yijing a primordial pedigree. Others accept the mythic attribution as part of the text’s traditional framing without treating it as historical.

Creation of writing (Cangjie system) In some accounts, Fuxi is linked to the origin of Chinese writing through the figure of Cangjie, a scribe who allegedly invented characters by observing animal tracks and natural patterns. Fuxi’s role is sometimes described as commissioning or inspiring this system, thereby enabling record-keeping and governance. Later mythographic compilations; Wikipedia summary; modern popular accounts Primary textual evidence directly connecting Fuxi to Cangjie is limited and often late. The historical development of Chinese writing is archaeologically attested through oracle bones and inscriptions that cannot be traced to a single inventor. Scholars generally treat the Cangjie story as a mythic explanation of writing rather than historical fact. The linkage to Fuxi varies by source and is not universally accepted.

Musical and calendrical innovations Fuxi is sometimes credited with inventing musical instruments and the calendar. These powers extend his civilizing role into the realms of ritual, timekeeping, and aesthetic expression. Music, in this framing, harmonizes human emotion with cosmic rhythms, while the calendar aligns agricultural and ritual activities with celestial cycles. EBSCO Research Starter; later encyclopedic sources Specific instruments or calendar systems are not detailed in early texts. Historical calendars and musical systems in China evolved over centuries and cannot be attributed to a single figure. Some traditions attribute musical innovations to other culture heroes or historical rulers; Fuxi’s role here is part of a generalized civilizing portfolio rather than a precise technical claim.

Accounts

In one widely attested narrative complex, Fuxi and Nüwa appear as a primordial pair with human upper bodies and intertwined serpent tails. The Chu Silk Manuscript presents Baoxi (Fuxi) as a cosmogonic figure who orders heaven and earth, while other texts and visual art depict Nüwa as the creator of humans and repairer of the sky. Together, they stand at the threshold between chaos and order. In some versions, after a cosmic catastrophe—often a flood or the tilting of heaven—Nüwa repairs the sky with five-colored stones, and Fuxi helps re-establish human society by instituting marriage and social norms. Their intertwined bodies symbolize the unity of male and female principles and the co-creation of humanity and cosmic structure.

A recurring story describes a time when humans lived without regulated marriage, engaging in chaotic or incestuous unions. Fuxi, observing the disorder and its social consequences, establishes marriage as a formal institution. He sets rules for who may marry whom, introduces ceremonies, and defines kinship structures. In some versions, he and Nüwa themselves marry as siblings, modeling the union that will populate the world. The narrative frames marriage as a civilizing force that transforms raw sexuality into socially meaningful relationships aligned with cosmic order.

In a widely circulated story, Fuxi observes patterns in the natural world—heaven, earth, water, fire, thunder, wind, mountain, and lake—and discerns that these phenomena can be represented by combinations of broken and unbroken lines. He arranges these into eight trigrams (bagua), each corresponding to a fundamental aspect of reality. These trigrams become the basis for divination and cosmological reflection, eventually forming the core of the Yijing. The narrative portrays Fuxi as a sage who reads the cosmos and translates its structure into symbolic form.

In the framework of the Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors (San Huang Wu Di), Fuxi is often placed as the first sovereign. He rules during a legendary period when humans transition from a primitive state to organized society. Under his reign, hunting, fishing, and basic technologies are established; under subsequent sovereigns such as Shennong and Suiren, agriculture and fire-making develop. The narrative sequence presents human history as a progression through stages of civilizing innovation, with Fuxi at the head.

A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine a tomb carved into the earth two thousand years ago: on its walls, a man and a woman coil together, their human torsos crowned and robed, their bodies tapering into serpentine tails that knot in an endless loop. In the man’s hands, a compass traces the circle of heaven; in the woman’s, a square marks the corners of earth. For the Han artisans who carved them, this intertwined pair was not mere decoration but the memory of a time before dynasties, when a serpent-bodied sovereign named Fuxi and his sister-wife Nüwa stood at the threshold between chaos and order, turning raw landscape and scattered humans into a world of marriages, calendars, and cosmic diagrams. To follow Fuxi through texts and stone is to watch Chinese civilization imagine its own beginning—lawful, measured, and deeply strange.
