Who It Is

Imagine a tomb carved into the earth two thousand years ago: on its walls, a man and a woman coil together, their human torsos crowned and robed, their bodies tapering into serpentine tails that knot in an endless loop. In the man’s hands, a compass traces the circle of heaven; in the woman’s, a square marks the corners of earth. For the Han artisans who carved them, this intertwined pair was not mere decoration but the memory of a time before dynasties, when a serpent-bodied sovereign named Fuxi and his sister-wife Nüwa stood at the threshold between chaos and order, turning raw landscape and scattered humans into a world of marriages, calendars, and cosmic diagrams. To follow Fuxi through texts and stone is to watch Chinese civilization imagine its own beginning—lawful, measured, and deeply strange.

Fuxi is portrayed as a benevolent, law-giving culture hero rather than a capricious or violent deity. His actions are consistently framed as constructive: he organizes marriage customs, teaches humans to hunt and fish sustainably, introduces animal domestication, and establishes cooking and musical practices. In philosophical texts, he appears as an exemplar of alignment between human institutions and cosmic patterns, embodying a kind of proto-Confucian or cosmological virtue. Powers
Creation and ordering of humanity

Fuxi is credited, together with Nüwa, with creating or re-creating humanity. In some narratives, Nüwa fashions humans from yellow earth or clay, while Fuxi establishes the social and marital structures that allow them to live as a stable community. His power here is not the physical molding of bodies but the ordering of human relationships—instituting marriage, kinship rules, and social norms. This civilizational creation is cosmic in scale, transforming scattered individuals into a coherent society.

Limit: The division of labor between Fuxi and Nüwa varies by source: some emphasize Nüwa’s creative act and Fuxi’s institutional role; others blur the distinction. There is no single canonical account of how many humans they create or the exact mechanisms. The mythic timeframe is prehistorical and not tied to specific historical dates.

Invention of hunting and fishing

Fuxi is widely credited with teaching humans how to hunt and fish effectively. This includes the use of nets, traps, and other implements, as well as the conceptual shift from purely gathering to managed exploitation of animal resources. These skills are presented as foundational technologies that allow early humans to survive and thrive.

Limit: The myths do not specify particular species, techniques, or regions; they operate at a symbolic level. Archaeological evidence for early hunting and fishing in China is independent of the Fuxi narrative and cannot be directly tied to him.

Domestication of animals and animal husbandry

Fuxi is said to have introduced animal domestication, transforming wild creatures into managed herds. This includes the keeping of livestock for food, labor, and ritual purposes. In mythic terms, this power represents the taming of nature and the establishment of a stable food supply.

Limit: The myths do not detail specific domesticated species or techniques. Historical domestication processes in Neolithic China are complex and multi-centered; Fuxi’s role is symbolic rather than historical.

Institution of marriage and social norms

One of Fuxi’s most prominent powers is the establishment of marriage as an institution. He is said to have created rules governing the union of men and women, ceremonies, and kinship structures. This transforms human reproduction from a chaotic or incestuous state into a regulated social practice, aligning family life with cosmic order.

Limit: Details of the marriage rites are not preserved in early texts; later ritual manuals are not explicitly attributed to Fuxi. The mythic narrative simplifies complex historical developments in kinship and marriage practices.

Invention of the Eight Trigrams (bagua)

Fuxi is traditionally credited with deriving the Eight Trigrams (bagua) from observations of nature. By studying patterns in heaven, earth, water, fire, thunder, wind, mountain, and lake, he discerned eight fundamental configurations of yin and yang lines. These trigrams became the basis for the Yijing (I Ching) divination system and later cosmological and philosophical frameworks.

Limit: The earliest textual evidence for Fuxi’s direct authorship of the bagua is relatively late and embedded in interpretive traditions. The historical development of the Yijing is complex, and attributing the trigrams to Fuxi is part of a mythic genealogy rather than a verifiable historical fact.

Creation of writing (Cangjie system)

In some accounts, Fuxi is linked to the origin of Chinese writing through the figure of Cangjie, a scribe who allegedly invented characters by observing animal tracks and natural patterns. Fuxi’s role is sometimes described as commissioning or inspiring this system, thereby enabling record-keeping and governance.

Limit: Primary textual evidence directly connecting Fuxi to Cangjie is limited and often late. The historical development of Chinese writing is archaeologically attested through oracle bones and inscriptions that cannot be traced to a single inventor.

Musical and calendrical innovations

Fuxi is sometimes credited with inventing musical instruments and the calendar. These powers extend his civilizing role into the realms of ritual, timekeeping, and aesthetic expression. Music, in this framing, harmonizes human emotion with cosmic rhythms, while the calendar aligns agricultural and ritual activities with celestial cycles.

Limit: Specific instruments or calendar systems are not detailed in early texts. Historical calendars and musical systems in China evolved over centuries and cannot be attributed to a single figure.

Accounts I
Fuxi and Nüwa as serpent-bodied creator pairChu Silk Manuscript; Han tomb art; Huainanzi; later mythographic compilations

Warring States (c. 4th–3rd century BCE) through Han dynasty (206 BCE–220 CE)

In one widely attested narrative complex, Fuxi and Nüwa appear as a primordial pair with human upper bodies and intertwined serpent tails. The Chu Silk Manuscript presents Baoxi (Fuxi) as a cosmogonic figure who orders heaven and earth, while other texts and visual art depict Nüwa as the creator of humans and repairer of the sky. Together, they stand at the threshold between chaos and order. In some versions, after a cosmic catastrophe—often a flood or the tilting of heaven—Nüwa repairs the sky with five-colored stones, and Fuxi helps re-establish human society by instituting marriage and social norms. Their intertwined bodies symbolize the unity of male and female principles and the co-creation of humanity and cosmic structure. The world becomes habitable and ordered; humans gain both physical existence and social organization. The pair’s actions establish a template for later rulers and deities, and their image becomes a recurring motif in tomb art, signaling protection and cosmic harmony for the deceased.

Fuxi’s institution of marriage after primordial chaosShiji tradition (via later citations); Huainanzi; modern retellings

Han dynasty historiographical tradition, with later elaborations

A recurring story describes a time when humans lived without regulated marriage, engaging in chaotic or incestuous unions. Fuxi, observing the disorder and its social consequences, establishes marriage as a formal institution. He sets rules for who may marry whom, introduces ceremonies, and defines kinship structures. In some versions, he and Nüwa themselves marry as siblings, modeling the union that will populate the world. The narrative frames marriage as a civilizing force that transforms raw sexuality into socially meaningful relationships aligned with cosmic order. Human society becomes more stable, with clear family structures and inheritance patterns. The story legitimizes marriage as a divinely instituted practice and positions Fuxi as the originator of social morality.

Accounts II
Derivation of the Eight Trigrams from natureYijing commentarial tradition; later mythographic sources

Likely Han and later, as part of Yijing exegesis

In a widely circulated story, Fuxi observes patterns in the natural world—heaven, earth, water, fire, thunder, wind, mountain, and lake—and discerns that these phenomena can be represented by combinations of broken and unbroken lines. He arranges these into eight trigrams (bagua), each corresponding to a fundamental aspect of reality. These trigrams become the basis for divination and cosmological reflection, eventually forming the core of the Yijing. The narrative portrays Fuxi as a sage who reads the cosmos and translates its structure into symbolic form. The creation of the bagua provides a framework for understanding change and fate. It underpins divination practices, philosophical speculation, and later arts such as feng shui. Fuxi’s role as originator of the trigrams elevates him as a foundational figure in Chinese intellectual history.

Fuxi as first sovereign in the San Huang sequenceInternational Journal of History article; traditional lists of Three Sovereigns

Han dynasty historiography and later interpretations

In the framework of the Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors (San Huang Wu Di), Fuxi is often placed as the first sovereign. He rules during a legendary period when humans transition from a primitive state to organized society. Under his reign, hunting, fishing, and basic technologies are established; under subsequent sovereigns such as Shennong and Suiren, agriculture and fire-making develop. The narrative sequence presents human history as a progression through stages of civilizing innovation, with Fuxi at the head. Fuxi’s reign marks the beginning of Chinese civilization in mythic historiography. Later rulers and dynasties trace their legitimacy back to this primordial era, and the San Huang framework becomes a key organizing principle in traditional historical thought.
