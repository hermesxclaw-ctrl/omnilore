Hel, the half-living queen of the dead, who rules the shadowy halls where all mortals go who did not fall by the sword.

Who It Is

Hel is the ruler of the underworld realm of Helheim, where most of the dead go (those who did not die in battle). She is the daughter of Loki and the giantess Angrboða, sister to Fenrir and Jörmungandr. Her appearance is striking and terrible: half of her body is the flesh of a living woman, and half is blue-black like a corpse — some accounts say she is half white and half black, or half alive and half dead. The gods, fearing Loki's monstrous children, gave Hel dominion over the dead in the underworld. Her realm is divided: those who died of sickness or old age go to her cold, shadowy hall (Eljudnir, 'the Damp Hall'); the wicked go to Náströnd (Corpse Shore), where Níðhöggr gnaws their bodies. Hel is not evil in the Christian sense — she is a grim, just, and impartial ruler who neither tortures nor rewards but simply houses the dead. When Baldr is killed, Hermóðr rides to Hel to plead for his return; Hel agrees to release Baldr only if every living thing weeps for him — a condition that fails due to Loki's interference. The modern English word 'Hell' derives from her name.

Attested in Poetic Edda (Gylfaginning, Baldrs Draumar)Era Viking Age (c. 800-1100 CE), recorded 13th century

Form

Per Gylfaginning ch. 34, Hel is 'half black and half flesh-coloured' (half blue and half flesh-coloured in some renderings), a stark bicolour used in both older scholarship (Jacob Grimm's 'a greedy, unrestoring female deity') and Hilda Ellis Davidson's comparison to the dark goddess Kali. Her face is described as gloomy and downcast, 'fierce-looking'. In the Marvel adaptation of this heraldry, Hela's body is healthy and beautiful on one half and decaying on the other — a comic-book literalization of the half-rotten/half-living description.

Powers

Rulership of the dead Odin 'gave to her power over nine worlds, to apportion all abodes among those that were sent to her' in Niflheim. She is the mistress of those who die of sickness or old age, and receives a portion of the dead. At Ragnarök, 'all of Hel's people' march with Loki to the field Vígríðr — she commands an army of her dead. Prose Edda (Gylfaginning ch. 34, 51) Her authority is bounded: Gylfaginning assigns her specifically those dead of disease/old age, not warriors (who go to Valhalla or Fólkvangr). In Gylfaginning she cannot simply release Baldr — a universal-weeping condition is imposed, and when one being (Þökk) refuses, Baldr must stay. Whether this lordship over the nine worlds is Snorri's systematization rather than genuine myth is disputed; Davidson argues the 'disease and old age' division may be Snorri's attempt to reconcile Hel with Valhalla.

Authority as a respondent negotiator In the Hermóðr episode Hel 'speaks with authority as ruler of the underworld' (Davidson), imposing a condition on Baldr's release and, when it fails, keeping him. Gifts — a linen smock for Frigg and a gold ring for Fulla — are sent from her realm back to the gods, treated 'as from a friendly kingdom'. Prose Edda (Gylfaginning ch. 49) Her power is exercised within the constraints of the cosmic order; she does not override the weeping-condition. None major; the friendly-gifts detail sits in tension with the gloomy 'greedy' portrait.

Fatal embrace / claim over the dying Skaldic verse frames death as Hel's taking: Sigurd says the dying Fáfnir lies 'where Hel can take him'; Atlamál uses 'Hel has half of us'; Ynglingatal describes kings who die of sickness/accident being taken by Hel (she is 'howes'-warder', 'guardian of the graves', and the 'joy of the troll-woman' in Hamðismál's euphemism for death). Poetic Edda (Fáfnismál, Atlamál, Hamðismál); Heimskringla (Ynglingatal) In older verse this is often the place or a personified death-phenomenon rather than a sentient goddess. The personification-vs-place ambiguity is the central contradiction of her 'powers'.

Domain symbology (hunger, famine, sickness) Her hall Éljúðnir is furnished with personified privations — dish 'Hunger', knife 'Famine', bed 'Sick-bed', threshold 'Stumbling-block', curtains 'Gleaming-bale' — a found-poem of the banality and grimness of a death by decay and disease. Prose Edda (Gylfaginning ch. 34) Simek argues this 'allegorical description' is 'clearly... in the Christian tradition', not pre-Christian belief. Whether this is genuine inherited imagery or Snorri's Christian-inflected moralizing is debated (Davidson, Simek).

Musterer at Ragnarök At Ragnarök, when Loki arrives at Vígríðr, 'all of Hel's people' accompany him — Hel's domain provides a corps of the dead in the final battle. Prose Edda (Gylfaginning ch. 51) She herself is not described fighting; it is her dead who march. The Völuspá 'sooty-red cock from the halls of Hel' sounds a Ragnarök alarm. The precise composition of 'Hel's people' (all her dead? only her chosen?) is unspecified.

Accounts

Hel is one of three children born to Loki and the jötunn Angrboða in Jötunheimr, the siblings being the wolf Fenrir, the world-serpent Jörmungandr, and Hel (a female being). The gods, having traced prophecies 'that from these siblings great mischief and disaster would arise', gathered the children. Odin threw Jörmungandr into the deep sea encircling the lands, and 'cast Hel into Niflheim, and gave to her power over nine worlds, to apportion all abodes among those that were sent to her: that is, men dead of sickness or of old age.'

After Baldr is killed by Höðr's mistletoe spear, Frigg asks who will earn her love by riding to Hel to ransom Baldr. Hermóðr volunteers and rides eight-legged Sleipnir. He travels nine nights through deep dark valleys to the river Gjöll and its gold-roofed bridge Gjallarbrú, guarded by Móðguðr, who notes the bridge echoes more beneath him than the party of five (Baldr, Nanna and those cremated on the funeral pyre). She tells him the dead in Hel are a different color and he must go 'down and to the North'. Hermóðr leaps the Gates of Hel on Sleipnir, finds Baldr in a seat of honor, and stays the night. In the morning Hel makes her condition: every thing alive or dead must weep for Baldr. Messengers are sent worldwide; all weep — until, in a cave, a giantess called Þökk refuses: 'Thökk will weep / waterless tears / For Baldr's bale-fare; / Living or dead, / I loved not the churl's son; / Let Hel hold to that she hath!' It is presumed Þökk was Loki in disguise. Baldr remains in Hel.

A series of verses describes Hel taking Swedish/Norwegian kings who die 'of sickness or old age': King Dyggvi (described as Hel's doing), Halfdan Hvitbeinn ('howes'-warder' Hel takes him from life), Eystein Halfdansson (knocked overboard by a sail-yard, 'fared to' Hel, called 'Býleistr's-brother's-daughter'), and Halfdan the Mild (death by illness). The formula 'given to Hel' recurs for a later king (Harald Sigurdsson's saga).

Troubled by Baldr's ominous dreams, Odin rides Sleipnir to the edge of Hel by night. Garmr the blood-stained hound encounters him near Niflhel, and Odin approaches the 'high hall of Hel'. At the eastern doors he awakens the corpse of a dead völva by galdr, lies about his name, and extracts her prophecies about Baldr's death and Ragnarök. The final stanza has the völva tell Odin to ride home and that none will come again 'until Loki is loose'.

At the end-times, when Loki breaks his bonds and the forces of destruction assemble at Vígríðr, 'all of Hel's people' arrive with him. Three cocks crow the alarm — Fjalar in Jötunheimr, Gullinkambi in Valhalla, and a 'sooty-red cock from the halls of Hel' (Völuspá). After the world's burning, Höðr and Baldr are prophesied to return from Hel in the reborn world.

Content Notes

Hel's essential portfolio is death by sickness or old age (Gylfaginning) — the un-glorious deaths that Viking martial culture did not celebrate. Her hall's furnishings — dish 'Hunger', knife 'Famine', bed 'Sick-bed', threshold 'Stumbling-block', curtains 'Gleaming-bale' — personify the privations of a wasting death. This is a dark, anti-heroic counterweight to Valhalla's feast of the slain: most of humanity ends with Hel, not with the einherjar.

Davidson and Simek frame this as likely Snorri's systematizing, but note the 'fierce destructive side of death' and its 'physical horrors' are not wholly invented (Davidson compares Hel's gruesomeness to Kali).

Gylfaginning ch. 34; scholarly commentary (Davidson, Simek)

The 11th-century Old English Gospel of Nicodemus personifies Seo Hell as a female who engages in flyting with Satan (refusing him entry: 'out of my dwelling'); the Old Norse Bartholomeus saga postola has a devil call Jesus the one 'who made war on Hel our queen'. These are Christian underworld figures — the grammar of hell's gender made into a person — but they inherit and intensify Hel's association with damnation and punishment.

Bell warns these Seo Hell/'Queen Hel' figures are 'the queen of the Christian, not pagan, underworld' and must not be conflated with the pagan goddess.

Old English Gospel of Nicodemus; Bartholomeus saga postola; Michael Bell's analysis

Hel's weeping-condition, engineered to be sabotaged by Þökk (Loki in disguise), ensures the beloved god stays dead. Hel is thus the agent — juridically blameless but morally complicit — in the permanent loss of the god most loved by the Æsir, an act that precipitates Ragnarök.

The story frames Hel as bound by cosmic rule rather than malice; the blame is directed at Loki. Yet Hel's 'let Hel hold what she has' makes her the keeper of the irreversible death.

Gylfaginning ch. 49

Marvel's Hela (Lee & Kirby, 1964) and especially Cate Blanchett's MCU iteration in Thor: Ragnarok (2017) recast the ambiguous Norse ruler as a genocidal 'Goddess of Death' who terrorizes the Nine Realms, wields Necroswords, and is killed only when Surtur destroys Asgard. The poster's figure of a villainized death-goddess departs sharply from the source material's juridical, sorrowful queen.

Marvel's Hela is an adaptation that flattens a complex figure into a simple antagonist; critics and the MCU marketing itself describe her as 'violent, vicious, brutal and cruel'. This is one of the clearest cases of modern media reshaping a wary, rule-bound deity into a pure villain.

What We Don't Know

No Old Norse primary texts were accessed directly (all evidence is drawn from English secondary/Wikipedia-extract sources). The Hermodr and Ragnarok article extracts returned the persistent empty-extract class and could not be verified from primary sources — Hermóðr's ride and Ragnarök are reconstructed from Gylfaginning, Móðguðr's article, and Völuspá/Gylfaginning passages already covered. The Seo Hell / Old English and Bartholomeus saga texts were described via Bell's scholarship rather than read in the original. Whether Hel is 'half black' or 'half blue' cannot be resolved from the secondary English summaries (both are manuscript variants). Cross-cultural comparisons to Kali/Bhavani/Badb/Morrígan are scholarly analogies, not genealogical proof. A dedicated reading of the original Old Norse Gylfaginning chapter 34 text, and of Saxo's Latin, is the recommended next step.

A way to hold it in your mind

She rules the hall where the worthy are punished and the unworthy simply end: a realm of hunger and sickness, where the knife is named Famine, the dish Hunger, the bed Sick-bed — and where even a beloved god cannot leave once his release is refused. Hel, the daughter of Loki and a giantess, was cast by Odin into the mist-world Niflheim and 'given power over nine worlds,' yet almost every word we have about her is a medieval Christian-era poet's personification of a place that may originally have been no more than 'the grave.' She is at once the most humanly present of the Norse death-figures — the sovereign your ancestors reached when they died of age or disease rather than in glory — and the one most thoroughly reshaped by later imagination: the raw sorrowful queen of the Eddas has, in our own century, become the grinning, Necrosword-wielding destroyer of Asgard on a cinema screen.
