Who It Is

She rules the hall where the worthy are punished and the unworthy simply end: a realm of hunger and sickness, where the knife is named Famine, the dish Hunger, the bed Sick-bed — and where even a beloved god cannot leave once his release is refused. Hel, the daughter of Loki and a giantess, was cast by Odin into the mist-world Niflheim and 'given power over nine worlds,' yet almost every word we have about her is a medieval Christian-era poet's personification of a place that may originally have been no more than 'the grave.' She is at once the most humanly present of the Norse death-figures — the sovereign your ancestors reached when they died of age or disease rather than in glory — and the one most thoroughly reshaped by later imagination: the raw sorrowful queen of the Eddas has, in our own century, become the grinning, Necrosword-wielding destroyer of Asgard on a cinema screen.

Gylfaginning portrays Hel as a stern but rule-bound negotiator: she receives the dead 'of sickness or old age', holds court over her mansions, and when Hermóðr begs for Baldr's release she imposes a test — 'If all things in the world, alive or dead, weep for him, then he will be allowed to return... If anyone speaks against him or refuses to cry, then he will remain with Hel.' Powers
Rulership of the dead

Odin 'gave to her power over nine worlds, to apportion all abodes among those that were sent to her' in Niflheim. She is the mistress of those who die of sickness or old age, and receives a portion of the dead. At Ragnarök, 'all of Hel's people' march with Loki to the field Vígríðr — she commands an army of her dead.

Limit: Her authority is bounded: Gylfaginning assigns her specifically those dead of disease/old age, not warriors (who go to Valhalla or Fólkvangr). In Gylfaginning she cannot simply release Baldr — a universal-weeping condition is imposed, and when one being (Þökk) refuses, Baldr must stay.

Authority as a respondent negotiator

In the Hermóðr episode Hel 'speaks with authority as ruler of the underworld' (Davidson), imposing a condition on Baldr's release and, when it fails, keeping him. Gifts — a linen smock for Frigg and a gold ring for Fulla — are sent from her realm back to the gods, treated 'as from a friendly kingdom'.

Limit: Her power is exercised within the constraints of the cosmic order; she does not override the weeping-condition.

Fatal embrace / claim over the dying

Skaldic verse frames death as Hel's taking: Sigurd says the dying Fáfnir lies 'where Hel can take him'; Atlamál uses 'Hel has half of us'; Ynglingatal describes kings who die of sickness/accident being taken by Hel (she is 'howes'-warder', 'guardian of the graves', and the 'joy of the troll-woman' in Hamðismál's euphemism for death).

Limit: In older verse this is often the place or a personified death-phenomenon rather than a sentient goddess.

Domain symbology (hunger, famine, sickness)

Her hall Éljúðnir is furnished with personified privations — dish 'Hunger', knife 'Famine', bed 'Sick-bed', threshold 'Stumbling-block', curtains 'Gleaming-bale' — a found-poem of the banality and grimness of a death by decay and disease.

Limit: Simek argues this 'allegorical description' is 'clearly... in the Christian tradition', not pre-Christian belief.

Musterer at Ragnarök

At Ragnarök, when Loki arrives at Vígríðr, 'all of Hel's people' accompany him — Hel's domain provides a corps of the dead in the final battle.

Limit: She herself is not described fighting; it is her dead who march. The Völuspá 'sooty-red cock from the halls of Hel' sounds a Ragnarök alarm.

Accounts I
The birth and banishment of HelProse Edda, Gylfaginning ch. 34 (Snorri Sturluson)

13th-century recording of older tradition

Hel is one of three children born to Loki and the jötunn Angrboða in Jötunheimr, the siblings being the wolf Fenrir, the world-serpent Jörmungandr, and Hel (a female being). The gods, having traced prophecies 'that from these siblings great mischief and disaster would arise', gathered the children. Odin threw Jörmungandr into the deep sea encircling the lands, and 'cast Hel into Niflheim, and gave to her power over nine worlds, to apportion all abodes among those that were sent to her: that is, men dead of sickness or of old age.' Hel becomes ruler of the underworld in Niflheim; the division of the dead among the afterlife-hosts is established (Hel for the sick/old, alongside Valhalla and Fólkvangr). The 'bound monster' motif is set: the gods bind what they fear, destined to break loose at Ragnarök.

The ride of Hermóðr and the ransom of BaldrProse Edda, Gylfaginning ch. 49 (Snorri Sturluson)

13th-century recording of older tradition

After Baldr is killed by Höðr's mistletoe spear, Frigg asks who will earn her love by riding to Hel to ransom Baldr. Hermóðr volunteers and rides eight-legged Sleipnir. He travels nine nights through deep dark valleys to the river Gjöll and its gold-roofed bridge Gjallarbrú, guarded by Móðguðr, who notes the bridge echoes more beneath him than the party of five (Baldr, Nanna and those cremated on the funeral pyre). She tells him the dead in Hel are a different color and he must go 'down and to the North'. Hermóðr leaps the Gates of Hel on Sleipnir, finds Baldr in a seat of honor, and stays the night. In the morning Hel makes her condition: every thing alive or dead must weep for Baldr. Messengers are sent worldwide; all weep — until, in a cave, a giantess called Þökk refuses: 'Thökk will weep / waterless tears / For Baldr's bale-fare; / Living or dead, / I loved not the churl's son; / Let Hel hold to that she hath!' Accounts II
Hel's taking of kings (the Ynglingatal catalogue)Ynglingatal, recorded in Heimskringla (Snorri Sturluson)

9th-century poem recorded in the 13th century

A series of verses describes Hel taking Swedish/Norwegian kings who die 'of sickness or old age': King Dyggvi (described as Hel's doing), Halfdan Hvitbeinn ('howes'-warder' Hel takes him from life), Eystein Halfdansson (knocked overboard by a sail-yard, 'fared to' Hel, called 'Býleistr's-brother's-daughter'), and Halfdan the Mild (death by illness). The formula 'given to Hel' recurs for a later king (Harald Sigurdsson's saga). Establishes Hel's role as the consistent, non-heroic afterlife destination for rulers — the 'guardian of the graves' who receives those whose deaths are not heroic.

Odin's necromantic ride to Hel's edgePoetic Edda, Baldrs draumar (Vegtamskviða)

pre-13th-century oral material

Troubled by Baldr's ominous dreams, Odin rides Sleipnir to the edge of Hel by night. Garmr the blood-stained hound encounters him near Niflhel, and Odin approaches the 'high hall of Hel'. At the eastern doors he awakens the corpse of a dead völva by galdr, lies about his name, and extracts her prophecies about Baldr's death and Ragnarök. The final stanza has the völva tell Odin to ride home and that none will come again 'until Loki is loose'. Frames Hel as a place of divination where the dead can be consulted — a liminal boundary between worlds.

Ragnarök: Hel's people marchProse Edda, Gylfaginning ch. 51; Poetic Edda, Völuspá

13th-century recording

At the end-times, when Loki breaks his bonds and the forces of destruction assemble at Vígríðr, 'all of Hel's people' arrive with him. Three cocks crow the alarm — Fjalar in Jötunheimr, Gullinkambi in Valhalla, and a 'sooty-red cock from the halls of Hel' (Völuspá). After the world's burning, Höðr and Baldr are prophesied to return from Hel in the reborn world. Globally, this is the ultimate release of the gods' bound monsters and the transformation of the world; locally for Hel it confirms she marshals a host of the non-heroic dead in the final conflict.
