Who It Is

In the oldest hymn of creation, a cosmic giant is dismembered by the gods—and from his body the entire universe springs forth. This is Purusha, the primordial being whose sacrifice forms the bedrock of Hindu cosmology, yet whose identity shifts from a literal cosmic man to the inmost, unchanging consciousness within every living soul.

As a cosmic being, Purusha is passive and silent, allowing the sacrifice to happen. In philosophy, Purusha is described as inactive, unchanging, and detached, merely witnessing the activities of Prakriti (matter). He is not an agent but a conscious presence.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — From Sanskrit puruṣa, meaning 'man', 'person', or 'cosmic being'. Related to the root pr̥, 'to fill' or 'to be before', suggesting the primordial being who fills the universe.

Pronunciation — POO-roo-sha (Sanskrit: पुरुष)

Aliases — Puruṣa, Purusa, Narayana (in some contexts), Paramatman (as universal Self), Jivatman (as individual self)

Ownership — Hinduism (Vedic and classical), with significant influence on Indian philosophy and yoga traditions.

Names are cult layers, not one fixed label.

Cosmic Creation

In the Rigveda, the sacrifice of Purusha creates the universe: his body parts become the elements, social classes, celestial bodies, and natural phenomena.

Limit: None stated; the creation is a one-time event.

Pure Consciousness

In Samkhya, Purusha is pure consciousness (cit), eternal, unchanging, and unattached. It is the silent witness to the activities of Prakriti (matter).

Limit: Purusha is not an agent; it cannot act or create.

Inner Self (Antaryamin)

In the Upanishads, Purusha is the inner controller residing in the heart of all beings, guiding them from within.

Limit: Not a personal deity but an impersonal presence.

Transcendence of Limitations

In Kashmir Shaivism, Purusha (as Paramatman) is limited by five sheaths: time (kala), desire (raga), restriction (niyati), knowledge (vidya), and separatedness (kala). Through spiritual practice, these limitations are transcended to realize the universal Self.

Limit: Initially limited by the five sheaths.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In the Rigveda, Purusha is described as having a thousand heads, a thousand eyes, and a thousand feet. He is said to be vast, covering the earth on all sides and extending beyond by ten fingers' breadth. In later philosophical texts, Purusha is formless and beyond sensory perception.

Symbols — In Vedic ritual, Purusha is associated with the cosmic sacrifice (yajna). In iconography, he is rarely depicted; when shown, he is a cosmic figure with multiple heads and limbs. In Samkhya, Purusha is often represented as a witness (sakshi) or pure consciousness, sometimes symbolized by a clear crystal or a light.

Domains — Purusha is said to be all-pervasive, filling the universe. In the Upanishads, he is described as residing in the heart of all beings as the inner self (antaryamin). In Samkhya, Purusha is not located in space but is omnipresent.

Accounts I
The Cosmic Sacrifice of PurushaRigveda 10.90

c. 1500–1200 BCE

Purusha is a cosmic being with a thousand heads, eyes, and feet. The gods perform a sacrifice with Purusha as the offering. From his body, the universe is created: his mouth becomes the Brahmin (priest), his arms the Rajanya (warrior), his thighs the Vaishya (commoner), and his feet the Shudra (servant). His mind becomes the moon, his eye the sun, his breath the wind, and his head the sky. From his navel comes the atmosphere, from his head the sky, from his ears the directions, and from his feet the earth. The creation of the cosmos, the social order, and the natural elements. This hymn is often recited in Vedic rituals.

Purusha as the Inner SelfBrihadaranyaka Upanishad

c. 700 BCE

The Upanishads describe Purusha as the inner self that resides in all beings. In one famous passage, the sage Yajnavalkya explains that the Purusha is the self (atman) that is beyond all suffering and death. He is described as 'not this, not this' (neti neti), indicating his ineffable nature. This teaching establishes the identity of the individual self (atman) with the universal self (Brahman), a central tenet of Vedanta.

Accounts II
Purusha and Prakriti in SamkhyaSamkhya Karika

c. 350–450 CE

In Samkhya philosophy, Purusha is pure consciousness, plural, and inactive. Prakriti (matter) is primal nature, composed of three gunas (qualities). The union of Purusha and Prakriti leads to the evolution of the material world. Liberation (kaivalya) is achieved when Purusha realizes its distinction from Prakriti. This dualistic framework profoundly influenced Indian philosophy and the Yoga system.

Purusha in Kashmir ShaivismShiva Sutras

c. 850–950 CE

In Kashmir Shaivism, Purusha is the universal Self (Paramatman) that becomes limited by five sheaths (kala, raga, niyati, vidya, kala) to appear as individual selves (jivatman). The goal is to recognize one's true nature as Shiva, the supreme consciousness. This non-dualistic philosophy offers a path to liberation through recognition of one's identity with Shiva.

Purusha as Creator vs. Non-AgentIn the Rigveda, Purusha is the material cause of creation through his sacrifice.In Samkhya, Purusha is not an agent and does not create; creation is from Prakriti.
Identification with Vishnu or ShivaVaishnava traditions identify Purusha with Vishnu (Narayana).Shaiva traditions, especially Kashmir Shaivism, relate Purusha to Shiva.

Purusha is a single, universal soul in all traditions. — In Samkhya, Purusha is plural; in Advaita Vedanta, it is singular. The concept varies across schools.

The Purusha Sukta describes a literal human sacrifice. — Most scholars interpret it symbolically, as a metaphor for creation and the ordering of society.

Purusha is the same as the Christian concept of a personal God. — Purusha is often impersonal, especially in Samkhya and Advaita, and is not a creator god in theistic sense.
