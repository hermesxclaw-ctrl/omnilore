Who It Is

Imagine three impossibly beautiful women in glittering mountain palaces, each holding a thread that is your entire life. One can bless you, one can break you, and one can weigh both with a cold, clear sense of reason. They are not superheroes who swoop in to fix your mistakes—they are the ones who decide which mistakes you’re even allowed to make. In Romani lore, these are the Urmen, fate-spirits whose decisions on the third day after your birth echo louder than any horoscope, and whose allies include gigantic birds that kill out of mercy and drive people mad for insulting destiny.

Urmen’s core behavior is the allotment of human fate, especially at birth. In Romani tradition, they decide the destiny of a child on the third day after birth, sometimes framed as a ritual visit in which they pronounce the child’s life path. They may bless up to seven human favorites, whom they protect and guide; if they extend favor to an eighth, they themselves perish miserably, a narrative that emphasizes the scarcity and danger of divine favoritism. Powers
Allotment of fate at birth

Urmen (or Ursitory) determine the life path of a newborn, including major events such as prosperity, marriage, number of children, and manner of death. In Romani tradition, this occurs on the third day after birth; in Romanian lore, they visit over the first three nights. Their pronouncements are considered binding in essential matters, though minor aspects may be modifiable.

Limit: Their role is focused on structural destiny rather than everyday choices. Rituals, offerings (bread and wine), and divine providence may soften or redirect minor outcomes, but the core thread of life remains fixed.

Protection and favor of up to seven favorites

Urmen may choose up to seven human favorites whom they bless and protect. These protégés receive special luck and guidance, effectively living under the guardianship of fate-spirits.

Limit: If an Urmen favors an eighth person, she perishes miserably. This strict numerical limit functions as both narrative device and moral warning against overextension of divine favor.

Punitive madness via Charana flesh

When humans insult or offend Urmen, their queen Matuya may punish them by having Charana birds deliver Urmen flesh (from mercy-killed Urmen who exceeded their favorite limit). Matuya throws this flesh at humans; those who eat it go mad.

Limit: This punishment is tied to specific narrative circumstances—insulting Urmen and consuming the flesh. It is not a general curse applied to all offenders.

Mercy-killing of overburdened Urmen by Charana birds

Charana birds, gigantic and phoenix-like, take pity on Urmen who have accidentally favored an eighth favorite and are doomed to die miserably. They kill these Urmen by pecking the flesh from their bones, ending their suffering.

Limit: This ability belongs to Charana rather than Urmen themselves, but it operates within the Urmen mythos as a specialized function of fate’s ecosystem.

Triadic angelic judgment (good, bad, impartial)

In Maximoff’s literary adaptation, the three Urmen/Ursitory are framed as angels of fate: one good, one bad, and one impartial angel of reason. Together they weigh the child’s destiny, balancing beneficent and malevolent tendencies with rational judgment.

Limit: This is a literary-theological reinterpretation rather than a purely folkloric description. It reflects Maximoff’s creative synthesis of Romani tradition and Christian angelology.

Accounts I
The third-day fate ritual around the newbornMatéo Maximoff, "The Ursitory"; Wikipedia summary; Romani oral tradition as reported in secondary sources.

Modern literary adaptation (20th century) drawing on older folklore

On the third day after a child’s birth, the mother prepares a ritual space: three pieces of bread and three glasses of wine are placed in a circle around the baby. She whispers the child’s true name into its ear, a name kept secret from the father and even from the child until adulthood because it carries power. That night, the three fate-spirits—Urmen/Ursitory—arrive. In Maximoff’s version, they are three angels: the good angel, the bad angel, and the impartial angel of reason. They deliberate over the child’s destiny, weighing blessings and hardships, and pronounce a fate that will shape the child’s life. The child’s life-course is set, with a mix of good and bad elements moderated by reason. The secret name becomes a key to personal power and identity, reinforcing the importance of naming and ritual in Romani culture.

The seven favorites and the death of the eighthBerger, "Mythologie der Zigeuner"; DBpedia abstract.

Documented in 20th-century ethnographic work; likely older oral roots

Urmen, extraordinarily beautiful fate-fairies, live in shining palaces in the mountains or under trees where they sing and dance. Each Urmen may choose up to seven human favorites, whom she blesses and protects. If she mistakenly extends favor to an eighth person, she is doomed to die miserably. Charana birds, gigantic and powerful, take pity on such Urmen. They kill her by pecking the flesh from her bones and carry the flesh to Matuya, queen of the Urmen. The story underscores the scarcity of divine favor and the danger of overextension. It also introduces Charana and Matuya as key figures in the Urmen mythos, linking mercy and punishment in a single narrative.

Accounts II
Matuya and the punishment of those who insult UrmenBerger; DBpedia abstract; Wikipedia references.

20th-century ethnographic documentation of older folklore

Matuya, queen of the Urmen, rules from mountain palaces and commands the Charana birds. When humans insult or disrespect Urmen, Matuya punishes them. Charana bring her the flesh of mercy-killed Urmen, and she throws this flesh at offending humans. Those who eat it go mad, their minds shattered by the violation of fate’s dignity. The story functions as a warning against mocking or trivializing fate-spirits. Madness becomes a mythic consequence of disrespecting the cosmic order embodied by Urmen.

The migration of Rom, Dom, and LomWikipedia "Ursitory" article; Romani legends referenced therein.

Modern recorded legend with older narrative roots

In a legend associated with Muslim Roma communities, Matuya has three sons: Rom, Dom, and Lom. They are expelled from their homeland Hindustan (India) to Misr (Egypt). From there, their descendants spread across the world: the Roma to Europe, the Dom to Mesopotamia, and the Lom to the Caucasus. The story connects the fate-queen Matuya and her family to the historical dispersion of Romani-related peoples. The legend provides a mythic genealogy for Romani and related groups, embedding historical migration in a sacred narrative framework. It situates Urmen’s queen within a broader cosmological story of exile and diaspora.
