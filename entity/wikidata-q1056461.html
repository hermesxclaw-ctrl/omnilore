Who It Is

In the shadowed folds of Japanese mountains, where the cedar trees grow dense and the wind carries the scent of moss and mystery, there dwells an ancient crone known as Yama-uba—the mountain hag. She is neither wholly monster nor wholly woman, but a figure of profound ambiguity: a devourer of the lost, a guardian of the forest, a weaver of fate, and a mother to demons. Her story is not one tale but many, each layer revealing a different face of the wild and the human fear of the unknown.

Yama-uba is ambiguous: sometimes a man-eating monster who devours travelers, sometimes a benevolent mountain spirit who offers shelter and gifts, and sometimes a tragic figure who was once a human woman abandoned in the mountains. Her behavior varies widely by region and tale. She is often associated with the wilderness, and her actions reflect the dangers and mysteries of the mountain.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Yama-uba (山姥) is composed of the kanji for 'mountain' (山, yama) and 'old woman' or 'hag' (姥, uba). The term 'uba' can also mean 'wet nurse' or 'midwife', adding a layer of maternal connotation. Alternative reading 'Yamanba' is a colloquial variant.

Pronunciation — Yah-mah-oo-bah

Aliases — Yamanba, Yama-uba, 山姥, 山女, Yama-uba (mountain crone), Onibaba (in some contexts)

Ownership — Japanese folklore; regionally associated with mountainous areas, particularly the Kiso region and the Japanese Alps, but tales appear throughout Japan.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Yama-uba is typically depicted as an old woman with long, disheveled white hair, a gaunt face, and a hunched back. She often wears tattered mountain robes or leaves. In some tales, she can transform into a beautiful young woman to lure victims. In art, she is sometimes shown with a gaping mouth, sharp teeth, and claws, emphasizing her monstrous nature.

Symbols — Associated with mountains, forests, and wild animals. She is often depicted with a staff or walking stick, a bundle of firewood, or a spinning wheel (spinning thread is a common motif). In Noh theater, she wears a distinctive mask and wig, representing her dual nature as both terrifying and pitiable.

Domains — Mountain forests, remote valleys, and caves in the Japanese Alps and other mountainous regions. She is a liminal being, dwelling at the boundary between human settlements and the untamed wild.

Accounts I
The Mountain Hag (Otogi-zōshi)Otogi-zōshi

Muromachi period (14th-16th century)

A young warrior encounters a beautiful woman in the mountains who invites him to her home. At night, she transforms into a monstrous hag and attempts to devour him. He escapes and, with the help of a magic sword, slays her. The warrior gains fame and the hag is destroyed.

Yama-uba and KintarōFolk tradition

Various

Yama-uba raises the legendary hero Kintarō (Golden Boy) in the mountains after his mother abandons him. She teaches him superhuman strength and skills, and he later becomes a famous warrior. Kintarō becomes a hero, and Yama-uba is sometimes revered as his mother.

Accounts II
The Noh Play 'Yamanba'Noh theater

15th century

A traveling monk meets an old woman who dances and reveals her identity as Yama-uba. She explains her suffering and her role as a mountain spirit, blurring the line between demon and enlightened being. The monk gains insight into the nature of suffering and salvation.

Regional Folk TalesOral tradition, collected by Yanagita Kunio and others

20th century collections

Numerous tales depict Yama-uba as a child-stealing witch who devours children, or as a helpful spirit who gives gifts of food or wealth to those who show kindness. In some stories, she is a guardian of the forest who punishes those who harm nature. Depending on the tale, she may be defeated, appeased, or become a local guardian.

Yama-uba is always evil. — She is often ambiguous, with many tales depicting her as helpful or tragic.

Yama-uba is the same as Onibaba. — While similar, Onibaba is a distinct figure in some contexts, though the terms are sometimes used interchangeably.

Yama-uba is a yokai. — In classical texts, she is often a human-like witch or spirit, not always classified as a yokai.

Yama-uba is unique to Japan. — Similar mountain hag figures exist in other cultures, but Yama-uba is specifically Japanese.
