Powers
Shapeshifting (hamrammr)

Ability to change shape and sex, attested in Eddas: transforms into mare to distract Svaðilfari (Gylfaginning 42), into salmon to escape (Gylfaginning 50), into fly to prick Baldr's brother, into woman Þökk. Basis for 'actor' as role-player who becomes other persons.

Limit: Requires external trigger or is voluntary but leaves Loki vulnerable; as mare he becomes pregnant and bears Sleipnir, showing transformation has physical consequences. Cannot escape binding after Baldr's death.

Cunning eloquence and flyting

Mastery of verbal insult, riddles, and manipulation; entire Lokasenna is Loki systematically slandering each god with knowledge of their secrets. Also persuades dwarfs to craft treasures (Skáldskaparmál).

Limit: Eloquence fails when confronted by Thor (Lokasenna st. 58-64) and leads to capture; words do not prevent punishment.

Craft and invention

Invents the fishing net (Gylfaginning 50) which later leads to his capture when gods copy it; associated with acquisition of magical objects (Sif's hair, Gungnir, Mjölnir via dwarfs in Skáldskaparmál).

Limit: Invention is double-edged; net becomes instrument of his own capture.

Magical travel (shoes that run through air and water)

Possesses shoes with which he can run through air and water (Gylfaginning 33). Also borrows Freyja's falcon cloak in Thrymskvida.

Limit: Requires artifact; not innate flight like Odin.

The Building of Asgard's Wall and the Birth of Sleipnir (comparative context)Snorri Sturluson, Gylfaginning 42

13th c. text, mythic time

A builder (a jötunn in disguise) offers to build Asgard's wall in one winter for Freyja, the sun and moon. Gods agree on Loki's advice, with condition he finish alone. When builder nears success with help of stallion Svaðilfari, Loki transforms into a mare, lures the stallion away, delaying work. Builder is revealed as jötunn and Thor kills him. Loki, as mare, later bears Odin's eight-legged horse Sleipnir. Loki fathers Sleipnir, Odin's steed; illustrates shapeshifting, gender fluidity, and trickster's both helpful and complicating role. Frequently cited in scholarship on Loki's liminality.

The Theft of Idunn and Her Apples (comparative context)Snorri Sturluson, Skáldskaparmál (Bragaræður) and Gylfaginning 26

13th c.

Loki is seized by jötunn Þjazi in eagle form and forced to lure Idunn out of Asgard with her immortality apples. Deprived of apples, gods age. They threaten Loki with death unless he retrieves her. Loki borrows Freyja's falcon cloak, rescues Idunn, Þjazi pursues as eagle and is killed by gods. Establishes Loki as both endangerer and savior of gods; pattern repeats in Baldr story. Demonstrates use of borrowed magical cloak.

Lokasenna - Loki's Flyting at Ægir's Feast (comparative context)Lokasenna, Poetic Edda

10th-11th c. poem, preserved c. 1270

Excluded from Ægir's feast, Loki enters and systematically insults each god and goddess, revealing their alleged sexual and moral failings (accuses Freyja of promiscuity, Odin of effeminacy/seiðr, Tyr of cuckoldry, etc.). Gods respond; Thor finally drives him away with threats of Mjölnir. Prose epilogue describes his binding. Provides fullest portrait of Loki as social critic/trickster; prose ending leads directly to binding and foreshadows Ragnarök. Central to modern interpretations of Loki as queer or transgressive figure.

The Death of Baldr and Loki's Binding (comparative context)Snorri Sturluson, Gylfaginning 49-50; also Baldrs draumar (Poetic Edda) and Gesta Danorum (Saxo Grammaticus, Book 3)

13th c. (Snorri), c. 1208 (Saxo)

Frigg makes all things swear not to harm Baldr; mistletoe is overlooked. Loki fashions a mistletoe dart and guides blind Höðr to throw it, killing Baldr. Gods send Hermóðr to Hel to ransom Baldr; Hel agrees if all things weep. Loki as old woman Þökk refuses to weep, so Baldr remains dead. Gods capture Loki (who invented net and hides as salmon), bind him with his son Narfi's entrails to three rocks, place venomous serpent above to drip poison; Sigyn holds bowl to catch poison. Loki's ultimate punishment; his writhing causes earthquakes; he remains bound until Ragnarök when he will lead forces against gods. Saxo's version euhemerizes Baldr and Loki (as Loke) into rival human princes, showing variant tradition.

Loki's Wager and the Stitched Mouth (comparative context)Snorri Sturluson, Skáldskaparmál 35

13th c.

Loki wagers his head with dwarfs Brokkr and Eitri (or Sindri) that they cannot forge treasures finer than the Sons of Ivaldi. Dwarfs forge Mjölnir, Gungnir, Draupnir, etc. Loki loses but argues dwarfs cannot take his head without harming his neck, which was not wagered. Dwarfs instead sew his mouth shut as punishment. Explains acquisition of gods' greatest treasures; iconographic motif of stitched lips (Snaptun stone debated). Reinforces trickster's punishment for hubris.
