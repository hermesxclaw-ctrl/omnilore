Who It Is

In Romanian folklore, the vârcolac is a wolf-demon of immense cosmic power. Unlike the standard werewolf, the vârcolac is a mythological being that devours the sun and moon during eclipses — it serves as a celestial predator. In some accounts, vârcolaci (plural) are the souls of unbaptized children that rise to the heavens and consume the luminaries. The term is etymologically related to the Slavic 'vukodlak' (werewolf-vampire hybrid) and Bulgarian 'varkolak.' In later Romanian folklore, the vârcolac became conflated with the more terrestrial werewolf (pricolici), but its earlier, more primordial form remains a creature of cosmic scale — a wolf whose hunger is measured in heavenly bodies.

Form

Attested descriptions of the vârcolac are strikingly inconsistent, which is itself part of its folkloric character. Romanian dictionaries emphasize that it is a „ființă fabuloasă fără reprezentare concretă”—a fabulous being without a fixed, concrete form—whose defining trait is the cosmic act of devouring the Sun and Moon. Ethnographic and secondary sources reconstruct a range of possible shapes: wolf demons, large dogs (sometimes in pairs), smaller dog-like animals, dragon- or serpent-like monsters (zmei, balauri), and even octopus-like beings with multiple mouths capable of engulfing celestial bodies. In some accounts, the vârcolac is not a visible beast at all but a human whose soul leaves the body during deep sleep, ascending to the sky to feed on the Sun or Moon. The sleeping body is pale-faced and motionless, while the soul takes on a monstrous, often lupine or vampiric aspect. These descriptions are a mix of attested lexicographic notes, reconstructed from comparative folklore, and popular modern elaborations; no single canonical visual form exists.

Devouring the Sun and Moon The defining power of the vârcolac is the ability to ascend to the heavens and consume the Sun or Moon, causing eclipses and, in some traditions, the phases of the Moon. This act is imagined as literal eating: the celestial body is bitten, covered, or swallowed, sometimes bleeding visibly as a reddish hue. Romanian lexicographic tradition (DEX, DLRLC) and Romanian folklore summaries; Romanian Wikipedia; Ghinoiu’s „Dicționar de mitologie română”. The vârcolac’s attack can be interrupted by communal noise, especially the ringing of church bells or the clashing of metal objects. In thread-based variants, its power lasts only as long as the spinning thread remains unbroken; cutting or breaking the thread forces the creature to move or retreat. Some sources treat this power as metaphorical, while others insist on a literal monster in the sky. Modern scientific understanding of eclipses obviously contradicts the folkloric explanation, but within the mythic system the devouring is taken seriously.

Soul-flight during deep sleep In several accounts, the vârcolac is not a separate beast but the soul of a human (often one predisposed by birth or transgression) that leaves the body during a profound sleep, travels to the sky, and feeds on the Sun or Moon. Romanian Wikipedia and fan encyclopedias summarizing Romanian folklore. If the sleeping body is moved, disturbed, or the mouth is blocked, the returning soul cannot find its way back, resulting in the person’s death. The vulnerability of the host body is a key limitation. Some traditions emphasize the vârcolac as an independent demon or animal, not a human soul; others merge the two ideas. The exact mechanics of soul-flight are not standardized.

Shapeshifting between human, wolf, and other monstrous forms The vârcolac is variously described as a wolf demon, a dog-like creature, a dragon, an octopus-like being with multiple mouths, or a pale-faced human whose soul becomes monstrous. This implies a capacity for shapeshifting or, at minimum, a highly variable representation across regions. Encyclopedia of Monsters Wiki; Romanian Wikipedia; lexicographic notes about „oameni-lupi” (wolf-people). No single, consistent set of forms is attested; the variability reflects regional storytelling rather than a codified power. Some sources treat the vârcolac as purely zoomorphic, others as a human revenant. Scholars and compilers disagree on whether vârcolac should be classified primarily as a werewolf, a vampire, a ghost, or a unique eclipse demon.

Association with misfortune and death Beyond eclipses, the vârcolac is considered a harbinger of doom, associated with bad luck, illness, and death, especially for those who encounter or „host” it. Online folklore essay „Vârcolac: Romanian Mythical Wolf-Spirit of Doom and Darkness”; Romanian folklore context on strigoi and moroi. This is more a cultural association than a discrete supernatural ability; its effects are not described in detail. Some lexicographic sources focus narrowly on the eclipse function and do not mention broader misfortune, suggesting regional variation or later elaboration.

Behavior & Habitat

The vârcolac’s core behavior is cosmological predation: it „mănâncă Soarele și Luna”—eats the Sun and Moon—thus explaining eclipses and lunar phases. Romanian folk belief, as reflected in dictionaries and folklore studies, associates vârcolaci with disorder and transgression. They arise from broken norms: unbaptized children, children of unmarried parents, people born with anomalies, those who have killed a sibling, or individuals who violate ritual rules (sweeping dust out of the house at sunset, spinning thread at night without a candle, casting spells while spinning). In some traditions, the vârcolac fastens itself to the thread of a woman spinning at midnight, using that thread as a kind of cosmic tether to climb into the sky and reach the Moon. While the vârcolac is feeding, the human host falls into a deep, deathlike sleep; if the body is moved or the mouth is obstructed, the returning soul cannot re-enter and the person dies. Communities respond with noise and metal—ringing church bells, banging iron tools—to drive the monster away and „free” the Sun or Moon. Behaviorally, the vârcolac is both a cosmic devourer and a social warning: any deviation from communal order risks producing a creature that literally eats the light.

Accounts

In this recurring motif, a woman spins thread at midnight, sometimes while uttering spells or working without a candle. The vârcolac fastens itself to the thread, using it as a path to climb into the sky and reach the Moon. As it feeds, the woman falls into a deep, unnatural sleep. The Moon darkens or turns red, and villagers realize that a vârcolac is devouring it. They rush to ring church bells and bang iron tools, making a clamor that breaks the thread or forces the monster to release its prey. If the thread is broken, the vârcolac moves to another part of the sky or retreats, and the Moon slowly returns to normal.

A child dies before baptism, or is born to unmarried parents, or otherwise outside accepted social norms. Because proper rites were not performed, the child’s soul cannot rest. Over time, it becomes a vârcolac—a monstrous being that rises to devour the Sun and Moon. The community interprets eclipses as signs that such restless souls are active, and responds with noise and prayer to drive them away.

A person predisposed to become a vârcolac falls into a deep, pale-faced sleep during an eclipse. Their soul leaves the body through the mouth and ascends to the sky, where it feeds on the Sun or Moon. Those who know the signs avoid touching or moving the sleeper. One day, someone ignorant of the danger moves the body or covers the mouth. When the soul returns, it cannot find the entrance and is lost forever, leaving the body dead and the soul condemned to wander.

Content Notes

Traditional belief that children who die without baptism, or who are born to unmarried parents, may become vârcolaci—restless souls that devour celestial bodies. This places intense moral and emotional pressure on families and stigmatizes those outside normative religious and marital structures.

Ethnographers of Romanian folklore note that such beliefs are part of a broader system in which improper rites lead to dangerous revenants (strigoi, moroi). The vârcolac extends this logic to cosmic phenomena, linking social failure to cosmic catastrophe.

Romanian dictionaries (DEX, DLRLC); Encyclopedia of Monsters Wiki; Romanian folklore summaries.

Some accounts list people who have killed a sibling or committed other grave sins as potential origins for vârcolaci, suggesting that extreme moral violations can produce cosmic monsters.

This reflects a moral cosmology in which severe social violence has repercussions beyond the human realm, echoing other traditions where murderers become demons or cursed beings.

Romanian Wikipedia; folklore summaries.

The image of the Moon turning red during an eclipse is interpreted as the Moon’s blood dripping from the vârcolac’s teeth. While metaphorical, this is a vivid depiction of cosmic violence that can be frightening, especially for children.

Such imagery is common in eclipse myths worldwide, where reddening is explained as blood or fire; the vârcolac’s version emphasizes predation and gore.

Romanian Wikipedia; Ghinoiu’s dictionary; folklore commentary.

A way to hold it in your mind

If the Western cinematic werewolf is a personal horror story—a cursed individual hunting under the full moon—the vârcolac is a cosmic horror story. It’s what happens when social and ritual failures are imagined not just to haunt a family, but to bite the sky itself. Think of it as a glitch in the universe’s operating system triggered by human neglect: a wolf-shaped error message that appears whenever the community lets its most vulnerable souls slip through the cracks.
