Who It Is

Most beings in this archive survive in books. The Quinkan survive on the wall — in situ, in ochre on pale sandstone, in a few cases painted perhaps fifteen thousand years ago and maintained across generations, older than agriculture, with Traditional Owners still standing guard. They are the spirits of the rock: crack-thin tricksters who slip through fissures to enforce the law, and squat knob-limbed predators who bound across the country at dusk to snatch the solitary. Their portraits have hung through the glacial ages, through the arrival of mining and guns and forced-removal policy, still recognisable, still guarded — the oldest continuously interpreted religious images on Earth, and a spiritual register that a pair of brothers hid inside mailbags to preserve.

The Quinkan are divided into morally opposed castes. The Timara are tricksters — capricious but broadly moral enforcers of the law: they punish wrongdoers, teach hard lessons, and guard the country's law, protecting those who respect country. The Imjim (Anurra) are the 'bad ones': child-snatchers and predators of the solitary who bound across country at dusk and prey on those who wander alone. Respect for country is complete protection from one caste and negotiated safety from the other; the Imjim are vulnerable in daylight and on open ground.

Powers
Passage through rock

The Quinkan live inside the sandstone itself, emerging from cracks and fissures at night and slipping back into the stone. The thin, crack-like bodies of the Timara caste literally mirror this ability — they are painted as figures that could vanish into the rock's fissures.

Limit: Rock passage is a night ability; daylight exposes the Imjim (predator caste) on open ground, where they are at least vulnerable / avoided.

Law-enforcement trickery (Timara)

The Timara, though tricksters, are broadly moral: they punish wrongdoers, teach hard lessons and guard the country's law. Their trickery is a didactic tool that enforces right conduct rather than merely causing mischief.

Limit: Their protection is conditional on respect for country; breaking the law turns them against the offender.

Child-snatching and predatory ambush (Imjim)

The Imjim (or Anurra) are the dangerous caste: fat-bodied, big-headed, knob-limbed goblin-things who snatch children and the solitary. They bound across the country on their knobbed limbs — described in the archive's fun-fact register as 'mythology's only documented pogo predators'.

Limit: They prey at dusk and in the shadows; daylight and open ground are their weakness. Respect for country and the negotiated safety it brings is the defence.

Repainting / renewal maintenance

In a metonymic sense the Quinkan, or the duty towards them, manifests in the ritual repainting and renewal of their images over generations — the maintenance of the spirit register as a sacred custodial act. The Queensland elders George Musgrave and Tommy George devoted their lives to protecting these galleries and teaching younger custodians.

Limit: This is human custodianship in relation to the spirits rather than a power of the spirits themselves; it demonstrates the beings' intimate binding to the paintings.

Accounts I
The Quinkins / The Quinkan dwell in the rockCape York (Gugu-Yalanji / Kuku Yalanji) tradition, retold by Dick Roughsey and Percy Trezise in 'The Quinkins' (1978)

Deep time to present (oral/living tradition)

The foundational narrative of the Quinkan is that they are the spirit-people of the sandstone country: they live inside the rock, and the thousands-of-years-old ochre galleries around Laura are not decoration but documentation — a public register of the country's inhabitants. The galleries have been repainted and renewed across millennia by generation after generation, making the Quinkan's portraits among the oldest continuously interpreted religious images on Earth. The two principal castes — thin law-guarding tricksters (Timara) and squat big-headed predators (Imjim) — populate this register, together with individual giants such as Turramulli. Establishes the Quinkan as living religion and establishes the custodial duty that persists: the Traditional Owners' claim to and protection of the sacred sites, formalised on the National Estate register (1980) and the National Heritage List (2018).

The Imjim and the solitary travellerCape York tradition (predator-caste register)

Traditional, un-dated (still current)

The Imjim are the 'bad ones' of the Quinkan. Big-headed, fat-bodied goblin-things with knob-ended limbs, they bound across the country on their knobs like living pogo sticks and snatch children and the solitary at dusk. They are creatures of shadow and the failing light; daylight and open ground deny them, and a person who respects country and keeps to the law of the land moves under protection that the predators cannot easily breach. The tale is a cautionary narrative that enforces both literal safety practices (not wandering alone at dusk) and the broader law of respect for country. Functions as a moral and practical injunction: it guards against recklessness while encoding the principle that respect for country is the defence against its predators — one caste is fully protective, the other negotiable.

Accounts II
Molnar names the crocodile 'Quinkana'Paleontological taxonomy — Molnar (1981), Megirian (1994), Mackness & Willis (1996)

1981–1997

Ralph Molnar, describing the first fossil Australian ziphodont crocodylian (a deep-snouted terrestrial form with blade-like serrated teeth first found in the 1970s at Tea Tree Cave, Chillagoe, and Texas Caves), named the genus Quinkana after the Quinkan spirits. Molnar explicitly linked the choice to iconography: Quinkans were represented by crocodiles in at least one southeastern Cape York rock-painting location. Later species names preserved Gugu-Yalanji vocabulary: Q. timara after the 'thin spirits', and Q. babarra after the Gugu-Yalanji word for 'older sister'. Subsequent palaeontology established that no Australian rock art is actually inferred to depict any mekosuchine — the naming is cultural homage, not a depiction record. The extinct crocodile became the scientific namesake of the spirits, extending the Quinkan's reach into natural history and making the caste terms (timara) part of zoological taxonomy.

The Quinkans carried into Australian children's literatureDick Roughsey & Percy Trezise picture books: 'The Quinkins' (1978), 'Turramulli the Giant Quinkin' (1982)

1970s–1980s

Artist Dick Roughsey (Goobalathaldin, Lardil people, Mornington Island) and pilot-explorer Percy Trezise collaborated on a series of children's picture books that retold traditional Aboriginal stories. 'The Quinkins' (1978) won the Children's Book Council of Australia Book of the Year (Picture Book) in 1979 and an IBBY Honour Diploma in 1980; 'Turramulli the Giant Quinkin' (1982) was commended for the CBCA Picture Book award in 1983. These were among the first books to introduce Aboriginal culture to Australian children and carried the Quinkan into the national childhood mainstream. Widened public knowledge of the Quinkan dramatically and contributed to the late-20th-century rehabilitation and celebration of Indigenous culture in Australia; both men were honoured (Trezise OAM 1996; Roughsey OBE 1978).

The invaders: 'Night of the Quinkan' (2005)Krome Studios video game, 'Ty the Tasmanian Tiger 3: Night of the Quinkan' (2005; HD remaster 2018 on Steam)

2005 / 2018

In the third Ty the Tasmanian Tiger platformer, the Bunyip Elder Nandu Gili reveals that evil spirits called the Quinkan have invaded The Dreaming, and later Southern Rivers, and are immune to conventional weapons. The hero Ty must obtain a Bunyip Gauntlet to fight them, confronts Quinkan guardians (the Hexaquin, Dragonquin, Spiderquin) and their leader the Quinking, and lures them into a vortex to suck them out of The Dreaming. The name borrows directly from Cape York tradition, but the beings are recast from the traditional guardian/trickster + predator castes into a unified, generic evil spirit-invader horde that an anthropomorphic marsupial hero must defeat. The most divergent popular-fiction layering of the Quinkan: a commercial Australian game that reuses the famous name for a cartoon antagonist army, divorcing it from the rock-art religion.
