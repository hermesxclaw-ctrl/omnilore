Who It Is

Deep beneath the roots of the world-tree, where the silence is so complete you can hear the earth itself breathing, dwells Alako — the Finnish deity of the below, a figure so obscure that even the Kalevala, the great epic of the Finnish people, barely mentions them. But obscurity is not weakness. Alako is the keeper of the dead, the guardian of the earth's hidden treasures, and the source of the healing waters that flow from the depths. In a world that worships the sun and the sky, Alako is a reminder that the foundation of all things lies in the dark, cold, and mysterious realm beneath our feet. To understand Alako is to understand the power of the unseen, the silent, and the deep.

Alako is generally a passive and reclusive deity, preferring the quiet of the deep over the affairs of the surface world. When disturbed, Alako can be dangerous, causing floods, earthquakes, or diseases. In some stories, Alako is a guardian of the dead, ensuring that souls do not escape the underworld and that the living do not enter prematurely. Alako is also a keeper of ancient knowledge and treasures, and those who seek such things must make offerings or perform rituals to gain Alako's favor.

Powers
Control over the depths

Alako has absolute dominion over the underworld, the depths of the sea, and the interior of the earth. This includes the ability to cause earthquakes, floods, and the collapse of the ground.

Limit: Alako's power is limited to the below; Alako cannot directly influence the sky or the surface world without a conduit or a ritual.

Guardianship of the dead

Alako is the warden of the underworld, preventing the dead from returning to the land of the living and ensuring that the living do not trespass into the realm of the dead.

Limit: Alako can be bypassed by powerful shamans or heroes who know the correct rituals and have the proper charms.

Knowledge of hidden things

Alako possesses knowledge of all that is hidden beneath the earth: mineral veins, buried treasure, the roots of plants, and the secrets of the dead.

Limit: This knowledge is only granted to those who make the correct offerings and perform the proper rituals.

Healing and cursing

Alako can cause and cure diseases, particularly those affecting the skin, bones, and internal organs. Alako is often invoked in healing rituals to drive out the 'worms' or 'spirits' that cause illness.

Limit: Alako's healing power is specific to ailments of the 'lower' parts of the body; ailments of the head or chest are typically attributed to other deities.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Alako is not explicitly described in primary sources, but based on the general Finnish mythological framework, Alako can be reconstructed as a being of the depths. Likely depicted as a shadowy, formless entity that resides in the dark waters beneath the earth. In some interpretations, Alako may appear as a giant serpent or a fish-like creature, echoing the world-serpent motif found in other Finno-Ugric traditions. The being is often associated wi

Symbols — The serpent, the fish, the spiral, the color black, deep water, the root of a tree, the north, and the winter solstice. Alako is often invoked in spells related to healing (especially skin diseases and wounds), protection from the dead, and the discovery of hidden treasures. The symbol of the 'world pillar' or 'world tree' (Sampo) is also connected to Alako, as its roots are believed to reach Alako's domain.

Domains — The underworld, the depths of the earth, the bottom of the sea, the root system of the world-tree, the realm of the dead, and the source of all water and mineral wealth. Alako's domain is the 'Ala' (the below), a place of silence, cold, and primordial power. This is distinct from the Finnish term 'Tuonela', which is the specific realm of the dead, though Alako may dwell within or near Tuonela.

Accounts I
The Creation of the WorldKalevala, Runo 1

Pre-Christian, recorded 19th century

In the beginning, there was only the primordial water, and above it, the sky. A diving bird (the eagle or the duck) seeks a place to rest and finds the knee of the hero Väinämöinen, who rises from the waters. The bird lays eggs on Väinämöinen's knee, which hatch and form the world. The lower parts of the eggs become the earth, and the upper parts become the sky. Alako is not named, but the 'lower parts' (alinen) are the domain of Alako, suggesting that Alako is the personification of the primordial earth that emerges from the waters. The world is created, and the division between the upper (taivas) and lower (ala) worlds is established. Alako becomes the ruler of the lower realm, the foundation upon which the world rests.

Accounts II
The Journey to the UnderworldKalevala, Runo 16

Pre-Christian, recorded 19th century

Väinämöinen, seeking the secret words of origin, journeys to the underworld (Tuonela). He must cross the river of Tuoni, where he is challenged by the daughter of Tuoni. After his journey, Väinämöinen is unable to escape the underworld until he transforms into a serpent and slips through the net. Alako is not explicitly mentioned, but the underworld is Alako's domain, and the journey is a trespass into Alako's realm. The story highlights the dangers of the below and the power of those who dwell there. Väinämöinen fails to get the words of origin from the underworld, but he learns of the power of the depths. The story establishes the underworld as a place of knowledge and danger.

The Theft of the SampoKalevala, Runo 42-43

Pre-Christian, recorded 19th century

The Sampo, a magical artifact that brings prosperity, is forged by the smith Ilmarinen. It is kept in the stronghold of Pohjola (the North). Väinämöinen and his companions steal the Sampo and flee, but they are pursued by Louhi, the mistress of Pohjola. During the battle, the Sampo is broken and falls into the sea. The fragments of the Sampo are scattered, and the sea becomes rich with its bounty. Alako is not named, but the Sampo's fragments sink into the depths, and the sea's wealth is a gift from the below. The story connects Alako's domain to the source of all prosperity. The Sampo is destroyed, but its fragments enrich the sea and the land. The story explains the origin of the sea's wealth and the importance of the depths as a source of life.

Alako is the Finnish god of the dead. — Alako is the deity of the underworld and the depths, but the god of the dead is Tuoni. Alako is associated with death, but is not the primary ruler of the dead.

Alako is a demon or evil spirit. — Alako is a neutral or even benevolent deity, associated with healing and the protection of the dead. While Alako can be dangerous if angered, this is true of all Finnish deities.

Alako is a water god like Ahti. — Alako's domain is the underworld and the interior of the earth, not the sea. While Alako is associated with the depths of the sea, this is only one aspect of the broader domain.

Alako is a modern invention. — Alako is mentioned in 19th-century collections of folk poetry and incantations, and the concept of an underworld deity is ancient and widespread in Finno-Ugric cultures.
