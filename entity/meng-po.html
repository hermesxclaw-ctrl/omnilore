Who It Is

Every soul that has ever died in the Chinese cosmos must, before it may be born again, stand before an old woman with a ladle and drink the broth she presses into its hands — and then remember nothing of the life it just lost. Not the wife it outlived, not the enemy it wronged, not the child it buried, not the name it answered to. The forgotten dead are her customers, the amnesia is her craft, and the bowl is never refused. This is Meng Po, the 'Old Lady Meng' who stews the soul-beguiling soup at the far edge of the underworld bridge — at once the gentlest and the most total act of mercy, or of coercion, that any mythology has ever assigned to a grandmother with a pot.

Meng Po embodies a dual, seemingly contradictory temperament. On the one hand she is the merciful 'Old Lady' who relieves the dead of the unbearable burden of memory — the pain of severed loves, hatreds, debts, and sins — letting each soul enter the next life unencumbered; in the popular account she performs this as an act of compassion born of her own grief. On the other hand her duty is enforced and relentless: every soul must drink, by the command of the heavenly bureaucracy, so that none crosses to the next life clutching the grievances of the last. Powers
Soul-Beguiling Soup (Meng Po Soup / Míhúntāng)

Meng Po's defining power is the preparation and administration of an amnesia draught, the Míhúntāng (迷魂汤), 'soul-beguiling soup.' It is brewed, in the standard account, from herbs gathered from the ponds and streams of the earth, to a Five-Flavored recipe. A soul that drinks it immediately and permanently loses every memory of its past life and of its time in hell, so that it is reborn clean of the burdens, guilt, loves, and hatreds of the previous existence.

Limit: The power depends on the soul drinking the soup; the tradition holds that souls who manage to avoid drinking it can carry past-life memories into the next incarnation. Popular folklore credits miracle births and precocious children who can speak at birth to a soul that slipped past the brew without drinking.

Enforcement of the Forgetting Ritual at the Bridge

Beyond merely brewing soup, Meng Po is the gatekeeper who ensures the amnesia ritual is enforced. Every soul bound for rebirth must pass her station and drink; her function guarantees that grievances and debts of one incarnation do not bleed into the next, maintaining the orderly working of the karmic cycle.

Limit: Her authority is bounded to the moment of crossing; once a soul leaves the bridge and the underworld for rebirth, her influence ends and other deities' domains take over.

Amnesia-adjacent Control of Wind and Vital Qì (archaic)

The earliest stratum of the figure, documented in the Song dynasty, had nothing to do with the underworld soup: Meng Po was a deity in charge of wind (氣風). This primitive power is superseded in all later traditions by the forgetfulness function.

Limit: This is an obsolete archaic attribution, retained here to document the figure's evolution rather than as a living power claim.

Accounts I
The Lady Meng Jiang Origins StoryPopular variant transmitted in English via the en 'Meng Po' article; deeply entangled with the legend of Meng Jiang Nü (Lady Meng Jiang), one of China's Four Great Folktales.

Variant folk tradition (popular retellings; the Liang/Lady Meng Jiang core, in its Qin-dynasty Wall-weeping form, crystallized in later periods)

In the origin variation of Meng Po's identity, she is the Lady Meng Jiang of the Great Wall legend. Driven to grief after the death of the husband conscripted to build the Great Wall (and, in the fullest forms, after her suicidal end), Meng Jiang found herself unable to reincarnate because of the force of her sorrow. Moved by compassion and pity for the suffering of all spirits, she resolved to relieve the pain of other souls and took the initiative to create the bowl of soup — the amnesia draught — so that spirits might forget the suffering of their material life. This makes the 'Old Lady Meng' not a detached bureaucrat but a suffering woman who transmutes her own unceasing grief into a mercy for every other soul crossing over. The story grounds Meng Po's empathy: her forgetfulness is presented as a gift born of personal agony, easing the reincarnation of the multitudes. It also ties the underworld functionary to one of the most emotionally charged figures in Chinese folklore.

The Brewing of the Five-Flavored Soup of OblivionEn 'Meng Po' article ('she collects herbs from various earthly ponds and streams to make her Five Flavored Soup of oblivion') and the Diyu tradition.

Ming-Qing folk consolidation

Meng Po's daily work is to gather herbs from the ponds and streams of the mortal earth and brew them into the Five-Flavored Soup of oblivion. Each soul ready for reincarnation, having left Diyu, drinks this before leaving the underworld. The brew works instantly and permanently, erasing memories of all prior lives and of the ordeal of hell; purged of past sins and knowledge, the spirit is then reborn in a new incarnation according to the karma it has accrued. The story encodes the core theological mechanism of Chinese folk reincarnation: that the karma a soul carries forward must be its moral balance, not its autobiographical memory. The soup guarantees that each new life begins without the psychological weight of the last, allowing karmic accounts to be settled through conduct rather than vendetta. It is the mechanism by which past-life grievances are prevented from poisoning the next incarnation.

Accounts II
The Miracle-Birth Memory LeaksEn 'Meng Po' article; corroborated broadly in Chinese folk accounts of children who recall past lives (see also the 'Past life regression' and 'Pre-existence' articles' treatment of reincarnation memory).

Folk tradition (no fixed period)

Because Meng Po's soup induces amnesia, any soul that fails to drink — or the brew that does not take — leaves the memory intact. Chinese tradition preserves legends of miracle births in which a newborn can speak, because the soul of the baby had not drunk the Five Flavored Tea of Forgetfulness; occasionally people are able to avoid drinking, with the result that past-life memories surface in children. These tales are the folk explanation for the handful of children who reportedly remember former lives, and they position Meng Po's draught as the ordinary barrier whose occasional failure produces extraordinary prodigies. The stories provide the folk-theological accounting for the phenomenon of childhood past-life memory that modern 'past life regression' therapy also claims to recover — the two traditions meeting on the same raw material, one supernatural, the other psychological.

The Interrogation Variant: The Soup as Truth-Agentzh-language folk-religion account of the late Ming (Longqing third year / 1569 era): the claim that Meng Po is an assistant of the Ten Kings who makes the dead drink the amnesia soup so that, in their dazed state, they reveal their deeds to support the judges' verdicts.

Late Ming dynasty (Longqing era, mid-16th century)

In this earlier Ming understanding, Meng Po's soup functioned not to grant mercy but to expose truth. The dead were drugged into a dazed, unguarded state in which they would reveal everything they had done in life; Yanluo and the Ten Kings used this elicited confession as the basis for judgment. This is the transitional stage between the figure's archaic wind-spirit role and her final mercy-of-forgetting role — a nightmarish inversion in which the old woman's brew is an instrument of interrogation before it becomes an act of compassion. The variant shows that the moral valence of Meng Po's power changed over time: the 'soul-beguiling' draught was at one point a police instrument for extracting truth under the influence, only later fixed as a merciful eraser of memory.

The Five-Flavored Soup is a permanent, universal erase of ALL memory with no exceptions. — The folk corpus explicitly allows exceptions: souls who avoid drinking or whose brew fails keep their memories, producing the miracle-births of speaking newborns and past-life-recall in children. The 'permanent and universal' framing is the ideal type, not the whole tradition.

The figure and the amnesia concept date to antiquity alongside Lethe. — The Chinese-language record dates the underworld-soup Meng Po to well after the Song; the amnesia role is Ming-Qing, and the specific figure consolidated only in the Qianlong era. She is not an ancient stable goddess but a late-imperial folk-religious invention layered onto a Song wind/qì deity and a Wall-widow legend.

'Meng Jiang' in Lady Meng Jiang is a personal name meaning 'Lady Meng of the Meng family.' — In the Qi state, 'Meng Jiang' meant 'the eldest daughter born to a secondary wife of the Jiang clan' — a class-title/genealogy formula, not a surname-plus-given-name. Likewise 'Meng Po' = 'elderly woman surnamed Meng' is a folk title, not the historic name of the Wall widow.

Meng Po is interchangeable with the Greek Lethe and they are the same 'river of forgetting.' — Meng Po is an anthropomorphic functionary who administers a soup at a bridge; the Greek Lethe is a river in the underworld whose waters induce forgetfulness. R.F. Kuang's Katabasis explicitly wed the two by making Meng Po the guardian of the Lethe — a creative syncretism, not a traditional equivalence.
