Archive Dossier — Identity

Meng Po

Pronunciation Mèng Pó (Mandarin pinyin, roughly 'mung pwaw'); Wade-Giles Meng-p'o. The two syllables are Mèng (Fourth tone, falling) and Pó (Second tone, rising); the zh pinyin given in the primary sources is Mèng Pó.

Etymology

The name Mèng Pó (孟婆) is conventionally parsed as 'Old Lady Meng': Mèng (孟) is a common Chinese surname (and in archaic usage the word for the eldest-born child), while Pó (婆) means 'old woman,' 'granny,' or 'woman,' the respectful-homely term for an elder woman. The name is not a proper name in the modern sense but a folk title literally describing 'the old woman surnamed Meng.' The two most famous objects she is said to brew are the Míhúntāng (迷魂汤), literally 'soul-beguiling soup' or 'soul-confusing soup,' also rendered in English as the 'Five-Flavored Soup' or 'Soup of Forgetfulness,' and the compounds Mèngpó tāng (孟婆汤) and Mèngpó chá (孟婆茶), in which her own name is folded into the drink's name. The zh-language aliases recorded in her Wikidata entry are precisely Mèngpó tāng (孟婆湯) and Mèngpó chá (孟婆茶), showing how closely the figure and her brew are identified in the folk imagination. In Vietnamese the name is rendered Mạnh Bà.

Cultural Ownership

Chinese popular religion and folk Buddhism/Taoism. Meng Po is an underworld functionary figure of Diyu, the Chinese realm of the dead, and is especially prominent in the devotional literature and morality texts of the Ming and Qing dynasties, as well as in the living folk tradition of the Naihe Bridge and reincarnation theology shared among Han Chinese communities in China, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore, Malaysia, and the Chinese diaspora.

Archive Dossier — Approach

Every soul that has ever died in the Chinese cosmos must, before it may be born again, stand before an old woman with a ladle and drink the broth she presses into its hands — and then remember nothing of the life it just lost. Not the wife it outlived, not the enemy it wronged, not the child it buried, not the name it answered to. The forgotten dead are her customers, the amnesia is her craft, and the bowl is never refused. This is Meng Po, the 'Old Lady Meng' who stews the soul-beguiling soup at the far edge of the underworld bridge — at once the gentlest and the most total act of mercy, or of coercion, that any mythology has ever assigned to a grandmother with a pot.

Who They Are

At the far edge of the Chinese underworld, where the courts of judgment are finished and the punishments are paid, an old woman keeps a kiosk on the Bridge of Helplessness — the Naihe Bridge — over the river that divides death from rebirth. Her name is Meng Po, Old Lady Meng, and her job is mercy disguised as theft. From tears and the waters of forgetting she brews the Five-Flavored Soup of Oblivion — sweet, sour, bitter, pungent, salty, one flavor for each taste of a human life — and every soul must drink it before crossing into its next incarnation. The soup erases everything: your name, your loves, your griefs, your grudges, the faces of your children. Without it, the tradition says, rebirth would be unbearable — every infant would arrive already mourning. The whole vast machinery of hell exists to balance accounts, but Meng Po performs the underworld's one indispensable kindness: she makes it possible to begin again. Chinese folklore is full of loopholes — souls who bribed her, spilled the cup, or dodged the drink — and every one of those stories is a tragedy about someone who remembered.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

Meng Po is conventionally imagined and depicted as an elderly woman — the 'old lady' of her name — bearing the maternal-featured, weathered face of a granny tending a great cauldron or a stall at the edge of the underworld bridge. In folk iconography, temple statuary, and the dioramas of places like Haw Par Villa and Fengdu Ghost City, she is shown as a seated or standing old woman in simple pale or grey Han-style robes, either ladling soup from a chained cauldron or holding a bowl and a long spoon. On film and television she is often costumed as an ageless or gently eerie crone, sometimes with a softening maternal tenderness to make her amnesia a mercy rather than a cruelty. Her attributes are the soup-stall and the bowl; she has no martial appearance.

Symbols & Iconography

Her identifying symbols are the Meng Po Soup itself (the soul-beguiling brew), the cauldron or tripod in which it is brewed, the bowl from which souls drink, the Forgetlessness Platform/Pavilion (醧忘台), and the Naihe Bridge (奈何桥, the 'Bridge of Helplessness' or 'Bridge of Forgetfulness') at whose far bank her stall sits. The bridge, the soup-bowl, and the brewed potion together form the iconography of memory-erasure and the threshold of rebirth.

Behaviour & Personality

Meng Po embodies a dual, seemingly contradictory temperament. On the one hand she is the merciful 'Old Lady' who relieves the dead of the unbearable burden of memory — the pain of severed loves, hatreds, debts, and sins — letting each soul enter the next life unencumbered; in the popular account she performs this as an act of compassion born of her own grief. On the other hand her duty is enforced and relentless: every soul must drink, by the command of the heavenly bureaucracy, so that none crosses to the next life clutching the grievances of the last. The figure is therefore simultaneously the kindly mother who eases suffering and the infallible functionary who administers an inescapable draught. Her demeanor is serene, patient, and implacable.

Domains & Habitats

Meng Po dwells in Diyu, the Chinese realm of the dead, stationed where the Naihe Bridge crosses the boundary before rebirth. The most common placement is at the entrance to the underworld's procession toward the wheel of rebirth; several traditions set her at the Tenth Court of the Ten Kings (the wheel-turning court governed by Zhuanlun Wang), beneath the Forgetfulness Platform at the Sixth Bridge. Some accounts also place her at the approach to Fengdu, the earthly 'Ghost City' said to be the capital of the underworld, awaiting souls at the entrance of the ninth round of Fengdu. On Earth, her likeness is enshrined in the afterlife-themed complexes of Fengdu Ghost City and in the Ten Courts of Hell diorama of Singapore's Haw Par Villa.

Version Differences

Regional and period versions diverge in three axes. (1) Identity: in many versions she is an anonymous divinity appointed by the Jade Emperor; in a popular variant she is identified with Lady Meng Jiang, the grieving widow of the Great Wall legend, who turned her own deathless grief into the gift of forgetfulness for others; the zh tradition adds that some say she was the eldest princess of the Jade Emperor ('玉皇大公主') sent as a princess to guard the underworld by imperial jade decree. (2) Historical role: the Song-era figure was a deity of wind and vital qì with no underworld-soup function; the later Ming-Qing figure is wholly the goddess of forgetfulness. (3) Powers: the soup's function shifts from, at first, a drink to stop the dead returning to earth, then to a drug that makes the dead confess their deeds to Yanluo, and finally to a permanent amnesia draught.

Origins

Emerges in Tang-Song Buddhist-Taoist syncretism as the underworld bureaucracy of Diyu took shape. One tradition makes her a woman who spent her life unable to forget her dead husband, appointed after death to spare others what she endured.

Archive Dossier — Powers

Soul-Beguiling Soup (Meng Po Soup / Míhúntāng)

Source Version Folk-religion and Ming-Qing devotional account (see the en 'Meng Po' article: the Five-Flavored Soup of oblivion induces 'instant and permanent amnesia'); the 'Past life regression' summary adds the literal gloss 'soul beguiling soup' and states it erases all memories before the soul climbs the wheel of reincarnation.

Description

Meng Po's defining power is the preparation and administration of an amnesia draught, the Míhúntāng (迷魂汤), 'soul-beguiling soup.' It is brewed, in the standard account, from herbs gathered from the ponds and streams of the earth, to a Five-Flavored recipe. A soul that drinks it immediately and permanently loses every memory of its past life and of its time in hell, so that it is reborn clean of the burdens, guilt, loves, and hatreds of the previous existence.

Limitations

The power depends on the soul drinking the soup; the tradition holds that souls who manage to avoid drinking it can carry past-life memories into the next incarnation. Popular folklore credits miracle births and precocious children who can speak at birth to a soul that slipped past the brew without drinking.

Contradictions

Narratives differ on whether the forgetfulness is permanent in every case; the miracle-birth stories imply exceptions and leaks, and the late-Ming 'interrogation' version casts the soup not as a reliever of memory but as a truth-agent that makes the dead talk while dazed.

Enforcement of the Forgetting Ritual at the Bridge

Source Version Diyu and Yanwang Jing traditions: souls destined for rebirth are delivered to the Tenth Court (the wheel-turning court) and drink beneath the Forgetfulness Platform at the Sixth Bridge before reincarnation.

Description

Beyond merely brewing soup, Meng Po is the gatekeeper who ensures the amnesia ritual is enforced. Every soul bound for rebirth must pass her station and drink; her function guarantees that grievances and debts of one incarnation do not bleed into the next, maintaining the orderly working of the karmic cycle.

Limitations

Her authority is bounded to the moment of crossing; once a soul leaves the bridge and the underworld for rebirth, her influence ends and other deities' domains take over.

Contradictions

Some traditions seat her by the Naihe Bridge itself, others specifically at the Tenth Court under Zhuanlun Wang; a minority of accounts give other underworld functionaries overlapping gatekeeping roles.

Amnesia-adjacent Control of Wind and Vital Qì (archaic)

Source Version zh-language folk-religion account: '孟婆最初在宋朝時期被視為掌管氣風的神靈' ('Meng Po was at first, in the Song period, regarded as a spirit governing wind/qì').

Description

The earliest stratum of the figure, documented in the Song dynasty, had nothing to do with the underworld soup: Meng Po was a deity in charge of wind (氣風). This primitive power is superseded in all later traditions by the forgetfulness function.

Limitations

This is an obsolete archaic attribution, retained here to document the figure's evolution rather than as a living power claim.

Contradictions

It stands in flat contradiction to the underworld-soup Meng Po and illustrates how radically the figure's powers were reassigned between the Song and the Qing.

Form

A stooped old woman at a bridge-side stall, ladling soup from a great cauldron into small bowls, patient as a grandmother feeding children who do not want their medicine.

Powers

The brewing of perfect forgetfulness; gatekeeping of reincarnation itself. No rank, wealth, or magic exempts a soul from her kiosk.

Weaknesses & Wards

Folk tales record evasions — the drink spat out, the bribe, the soul that carried a memory through as a birthmark. Children born remembering past lives are the ones Meng Po missed.

Archive Dossier — Relationships

Yanluo Wang (閻羅王), King of Death / Lord of Diyu

Type superior in the underworld hierarchySource Traditions of Diyu and the Ten Kings; the en 'Meng Po' / 'Yanluo Wang' articles.

Description

Yanluo Wang, the ruler of the underworld and overseer of the Ten Kings of Hell, presides over the judgment of the dead before they are sent for reincarnation; in the late-Ming interrogation variant Meng Po operates as one of his functionaries, drugging the dead to elicit their deeds, and in the standard later form she administers the amnesia soup to souls after their judgment as they leave Diyu toward rebirth. She sits at the end of the judicial-and-spiritual pipeline of which Yanluo is the apex.

Version Note

The relationship is institutional/functional; Meng Po is a servant or functionary of the hell-bureaucracy rather than a rival or consort of Yanluo.

Conflicting Accounts

The degree of subordination varies: the famous chapter of Journey to the West involving Sun Wukong in hell features Yanluo, Ox-Head, and Horse-Face but does NOT involve Meng Po, so any claim that she appears there is a common error.

The Ten Kings of Hell / Wheel-Turning King (Zhuanlun Wang, 轉輪王)

Type hierarchical/placementSource Yanwang Jing as summarized in the zh 'Meng Po' article; Ten Courts of Hell tradition (Diyu article).

Description

The Ten Courts tradition places Meng Po's soup-station in the domain of the Tenth Court, ruled by the Wheel-Turning King who governs rebirth. Per the Yanwang Jing, souls after punishment are delivered to the Tenth Court and made to drink the amnesia soup beneath the Forgetfulness Platform at the Sixth Bridge before they are sent for rebirth — the placement that directly links Meng Po to the court that manages reincarnation.

Version Note

This is a specific placement variant; other accounts simply place her at the Naihe Bridge.

Conflicting Accounts

The exact court and bridge number (the Sixth Bridge of the Tenth Court) is a detail of the scripture tradition that does not recur in every telling.

The Naihe Bridge (奈何桥, the 'Bridge of Helplessness' / 'Bridge of Forgetfulness')

Type domain/locationSource En 'Meng Po', 'Diyu', and 'Pre-existence' articles.

Description

The Naihe Bridge is the metaphysical threshold souls cross on the way to reincarnation, and in the standard folk account Meng Po's soup-stall is set there so that every crossing soul must drink before passing. The bridge is also the setting of moral sorting: in the Diyu tradition the wicked may be pushed into the Blood River Pool by Ox-Head and Horse-Face while the virtuous cross easily — Meng Po's soup being the last threshold before rebirth.

Version Note

Placement at the Naihe Bridge is the most widespread and iconic setting.

Conflicting Accounts

Some traditions locate the forgetting not at the bridge but specifically at the Tenth Court's Forgetfulness Platform, and the bridge's own crossing rules vary by sex, age, and marital status in the Fengdu reenactment tradition.

Ox-Head and Horse-Face (牛頭馬面), underworld guards

Type fellow functionaries / adjacent gatekeepersSource Ox-Head and Horse-Face article; Diyu article.

Description

Ox-Head and Horse-Face are the first underworld guards most dead souls meet; their job is to seize and escort souls and bring them before the underworld courts. They are the grim escort of the arrival side of Diyu, whereas Meng Po is the gentler (but no less inexorable) figure of the departure side; together they bracket the soul's passage through hell, Ox-Head and Horse-Face bringing the newly dead in and Meng Po sending the reborn out cleansed.

Version Note

They are colleagues in the same bureaucratic afterlife rather than relatives.

Conflicting Accounts

None of substance; the two functionaries operate in different stages of the same process, so conflation of them into a single narrative with Meng Po is usually an adaptation embellishment.

Heibai Wuchang (黑白無常), the Black and White Impermanence

Type adjacent underworld functionariesSource Heibai Wuchang article.

Description

The Black and White Impermanence (Xie Bi'an and Fan Wujiu) are the escorts who fetch spirits of the newly dead to the underworld. Like Ox-Head and Horse-Face they work the ingress side of Diyu, complementing Meng Po's egress-side forgetting work; all are stock figures of the Chinese popular afterlife who staff the city of Youdu alongside Yanluo and the City God (Cheng Huang Gong).

Version Note

A structural parallel, not a direct story relationship.

Conflicting Accounts

None material; the two are independent personnel of the same folk-hell bureaucracy.

Lady Meng Jiang (孟姜女)

Type identity-variantSource En 'Meng Po' article; historical Lady Meng Jiang legend (Idema's Meng Jiangnü Brings Down the Great Wall traces the legend's development from the Zuozhuan to the Ming).

Description

In a major folk variation, Meng Po is identified with Lady Meng Jiang, the legendary widow of the Great Wall. Meng Jiang's inconsolable grief at the death and loss of her husband is said to have kept her from reincarnating; out of mercy she invented the soup so that no other soul would suffer the pain of memory. This relationship is an identity-conflation, not a spatial encounter: the two names are alternative masks of the same folk figure.

Version Note

This is explicitly a variant identification, not constant across all tellings.

Conflicting Accounts

The identification is popular but not universal or historically attested as ancient; the name itself is folk-etymologized, since in the Qi state 'Meng Jiang' meant 'eldest daughter of the Jiang clan,' not a personal name.

The Jade Emperor (玉皇大帝)

Type appointing superior (variant)Source zh 'Meng Po' article.

Description

In the zh folk-religion account, Meng Po is sent to the underworld by the Jade Emperor, the supreme deity of the celestial bureaucracy. One variant holds that she was the eldest princess ('玉皇大公主') dispatched by jade decree, and that the Emperor ordered the construction of the Forgetfulness Platform (醧忘台) for her use so that the dead might forget their former lives and no longer leak the secrets of past incarnations (泄露天机).

Version Note

This Jade-Emperor/princess strand is one of several origin stories, coexisting with the Lady Meng Jiang strand and the anonymous-divinity account.

Conflicting Accounts

The origin of Meng Po is contested among these variants; the scholar or folklorist cannot harmonize them into one biography.

The souls of the dead / those who pass through reincarnation

Type ward/subjectsSource En 'Meng Po', 'Diyu', 'Pre-existence', and 'Past life regression' articles.

Description

Meng Po's entire purpose is oriented toward the ordinary dead: every soul bound for rebirth passes her station and drinks. She is at once their tormentor (in the interrogation variant), their benefactor (in the merciful-amnesia variant), and their gatekeeper. The moral weight of the figure — mercy vs. enforced oblivion vs. coercion — is defined entirely by her relationship to these dependent souls.

Version Note

The wards are the constant across all versions; only the moral interpretation shifts.

Conflicting Accounts

Whether the forgetting is a mercy, a cruelty, or a bureaucratic necessity is the central interpretive dispute surrounding the figure.

Enforced Amnesia as Coercion

Source En 'Meng Po' article; zh 'Meng Po' article (the Jade-Emperor command that the dead forget to stop them leaking the secrets of past lives).

Description

Meng Po's central act is compelling every soul to drink a drugged broth and forget. Read critically, this is a state-monopoly on memory: the heavenly bureaucracy forbids the dead from carrying their identities, grievances, or 'secrets of past incarnations' (泄露天机) into the next life. The compassionate framing does not erase that the forgetting is involuntary and total — a permanent erasure of personhood ordered from above.

Scholarly Context

This is a theological account of reincarnation-memory suppression; it parallels but is distinct from the Greek Lethe tradition. The consent/agency question is a modern-critical lens, not an ancient one, and should be flagged as such.

The Interrogation Variant: Drugging the Damned to Confess

Source zh 'Meng Po' article (Longqing third year claim that Meng Po is an assistant who drugs the dead to make them confess).

Description

The late-Ming (Longqing-era) strand portrays Meng Po's soup not as mercy but as a truth-agent: the dead are made to drink the disorienting brew so that, while dazed, they reveal everything they did in life, and Yanluo's courts judge them on that confession. This is an afterlife built on invasive, coercive extraction of information — an unsettling inversion of the merciful granny image.

Scholarly Context

The interrogation role is historically documented as an earlier layer (mid-to-late Ming) that the amnesia tradition later overwrote; it belongs to the moral-policing apparatus of the folk hell.

The Violent World She Slots Into: Punishments of Diyu

Source Diyu article (the tortures of the Eighteen Hells and Ten Courts).

Description

Meng Po functions at the exit of a hell defined by spectacular cruelty: the Eighteen Levels and the Ten Courts inflict being boiled in oil, sawed in half, ground in mills, eaten by rats and wasps, flung into pools of blood, and endless cycles of death-and-restoration so the torture repeats. Her gentle soup stands in stark contrast to, and is inseparable from, this machinery of punishment through which souls have just passed.

Scholarly Context

The amnesia is the mercy that follows the horror; depicting Meng Po without the punitive context would sanitize the cosmology. The violence is exegetical/moral-philosophical, intended to deter sin.

Gender, Aging, and the Enforced-Oblivion crone

Source Cultural-critique reading of the figure's crone iconography; implied by the elder-female depiction across folk art.

Description

Meng Po is a powerless-looking elderly woman whose entire function is to drug souls into forgetting — a figure freighted with cultural anxiety about old women, memory, and the loss of self. The aged-woman-as-angel-of-forgetfulness image carries unease (the mother who erases your past, the granny who drugs you), a gender- and age-coded ambivalence.

Scholarly Context

This is an interpretive/symbolic reading and should be labeled as such; it is not an attested cultic fact.

Colonial and Orientalizing Framing of Chinese 'afterlife'

Source Haw Par Villa and Fengdu Ghost City as tourist afterlife-theme sites; Western game/film appearances discussed in Reception below.

Description

In Western reception, Meng Po and the Naihe Bridge are often exoticized as the quaint 'Chinese River Lethe' of an orientalist other, mined for theme-park spectacle (Haw Par Villa, Fengdu Ghost City tourist reenactments) and dropped into games and films as flavor. This flattening strips the figure of its theological seriousness and its historical evolution, reducing a folk-religious deity to a meme.

Scholarly Context

Orientalism critique; parallel to the more extreme colonial distortion seen in other Umbrella-Category figures. The tourism and media appropriations are documented but the religious tradition they borrow from is often erased in the borrowing.

Archive Dossier — Stories

The Lady Meng Jiang Origins Story

Source Popular variant transmitted in English via the en 'Meng Po' article; deeply entangled with the legend of Meng Jiang Nü (Lady Meng Jiang), one of China's Four Great Folktales.Culture Chinese folk religionPeriod Variant folk tradition (popular retellings; the Liang/Lady Meng Jiang core, in its Qin-dynasty Wall-weeping form, crystallized in later periods)

Summary

In the origin variation of Meng Po's identity, she is the Lady Meng Jiang of the Great Wall legend. Driven to grief after the death of the husband conscripted to build the Great Wall (and, in the fullest forms, after her suicidal end), Meng Jiang found herself unable to reincarnate because of the force of her sorrow. Moved by compassion and pity for the suffering of all spirits, she resolved to relieve the pain of other souls and took the initiative to create the bowl of soup — the amnesia draught — so that spirits might forget the suffering of their material life. This makes the 'Old Lady Meng' not a detached bureaucrat but a suffering woman who transmutes her own unceasing grief into a mercy for every other soul crossing over.

Participants

Meng Po / Lady Meng Jiang

her dead husband (Qi Liang / Wan Xiliang in variants)

the souls of the dead

Consequences

The story grounds Meng Po's empathy: her forgetfulness is presented as a gift born of personal agony, easing the reincarnation of the multitudes. It also ties the underworld functionary to one of the most emotionally charged figures in Chinese folklore.

Variants

The identity of Meng Po with Meng Jiang Nü is one popular strand among several (the Jade-Emperor's-princess strand is another); it is not universal, and the historical Meng Jiang legend itself spans the Zuozhuan anecdote of Qi Liang's wife, Liu Xiang's Han expansions, the Tang consolidation, and the Ming-era Qin-dynasty Wall-weeping finale.

The Brewing of the Five-Flavored Soup of Oblivion

Source En 'Meng Po' article ('she collects herbs from various earthly ponds and streams to make her Five Flavored Soup of oblivion') and the Diyu tradition.Culture Chinese folk religionPeriod Ming-Qing folk consolidation

Summary

Meng Po's daily work is to gather herbs from the ponds and streams of the mortal earth and brew them into the Five-Flavored Soup of oblivion. Each soul ready for reincarnation, having left Diyu, drinks this before leaving the underworld. The brew works instantly and permanently, erasing memories of all prior lives and of the ordeal of hell; purged of past sins and knowledge, the spirit is then reborn in a new incarnation according to the karma it has accrued. The story encodes the core theological mechanism of Chinese folk reincarnation: that the karma a soul carries forward must be its moral balance, not its autobiographical memory.

Participants

Meng Po

the departing souls of Diyu

the karmic wheel of rebirth

Consequences

The soup guarantees that each new life begins without the psychological weight of the last, allowing karmic accounts to be settled through conduct rather than vendetta. It is the mechanism by which past-life grievances are prevented from poisoning the next incarnation.

Variants

The number and flavor of the five flavors are not fixed across tellings; the drink is variously called the Five-Flavored Soup, the Five Flavored Tea of Forgetfulness, the Soul-beguiling Soup (míhúntāng), Meng Po tea, or 'waters of oblivion.' The imperative 'you must drink a bowl before crossing the Naihe Bridge' recurs across tellings.

The Miracle-Birth Memory Leaks

Source En 'Meng Po' article; corroborated broadly in Chinese folk accounts of children who recall past lives (see also the 'Past life regression' and 'Pre-existence' articles' treatment of reincarnation memory).Culture Chinese folk religionPeriod Folk tradition (no fixed period)

Summary

Because Meng Po's soup induces amnesia, any soul that fails to drink — or the brew that does not take — leaves the memory intact. Chinese tradition preserves legends of miracle births in which a newborn can speak, because the soul of the baby had not drunk the Five Flavored Tea of Forgetfulness; occasionally people are able to avoid drinking, with the result that past-life memories surface in children. These tales are the folk explanation for the handful of children who reportedly remember former lives, and they position Meng Po's draught as the ordinary barrier whose occasional failure produces extraordinary prodigies.

Participants

the neglected or rebellious soul

the newborn child who speaks

Meng Po (by her absence/exception)

Consequences

The stories provide the folk-theological accounting for the phenomenon of childhood past-life memory that modern 'past life regression' therapy also claims to recover — the two traditions meeting on the same raw material, one supernatural, the other psychological.

Variants

The severity of the penalty for dodging the soup varies; some versions imply the soul is simply rare and lucky, others that the gods take notice of such leakages.

The Interrogation Variant: The Soup as Truth-Agent

Source zh-language folk-religion account of the late Ming (Longqing third year / 1569 era): the claim that Meng Po is an assistant of the Ten Kings who makes the dead drink the amnesia soup so that, in their dazed state, they reveal their deeds to support the judges' verdicts.Culture Chinese folk religionPeriod Late Ming dynasty (Longqing era, mid-16th century)

Summary

In this earlier Ming understanding, Meng Po's soup functioned not to grant mercy but to expose truth. The dead were drugged into a dazed, unguarded state in which they would reveal everything they had done in life; Yanluo and the Ten Kings used this elicited confession as the basis for judgment. This is the transitional stage between the figure's archaic wind-spirit role and her final mercy-of-forgetting role — a nightmarish inversion in which the old woman's brew is an instrument of interrogation before it becomes an act of compassion.

Participants

Meng Po

the souls being judged

Yanluo Wang and the Ten Kings of Hell

Consequences

The variant shows that the moral valence of Meng Po's power changed over time: the 'soul-beguiling' draught was at one point a police instrument for extracting truth under the influence, only later fixed as a merciful eraser of memory.

Variants

Historical accounts differ on whether this interrogation function coexisted with or preceded the amnesia function; by the Wanli era the 'makes the dead forget everything' formulation was current, and the Qianlong-era print consolidation won out.

Archive Dossier — Primary Evidence

Yanwang Jing (阎王经, the 'Scripture of King Yama'), a late-imperial morality/daoist-hell scripture

Type textDate Ming-Qing dynasty (composition attributed to the late imperial period)Locator Account of the Tenth Court: souls who have been tortured and processed by the Ten Kings are sent to the Tenth Court (ruled by the Wheel-Turning King, Zhuanlun Wang, who governs rebirth), where at the Sixth Bridge, beneath the Forgetfulness Platform (醧忘台, Yu-wang-tai), they are forced to drink the amnesia soup (迷魂汤); cited in the zh-language article on Meng Po.Language Classical Chinese

Translation

According to the Yanwang Jing, ghosts who have been interrogated and punished by the Ten Kings of Hell are delivered in order from the First and Second Courts, finally to the Tenth Court, given to the Wheel-Turning King; the Tenth Court oversees rebirth, and every soul sent there to be reborn must drink the amnesia soup (Meng Po soup) beneath the Forgetfulness Platform at the Sixth Bridge of the Tenth Court, so that the ghosts forget their former lives.

Guiyuan Jing (歸元鏡, the 'Mirror of Return to Origin'), a Buddhist-authored play

Type text (folk opera)Date Early Qing dynasty, Shunzhi era (1643-1661 CE)Locator The play's account held that Meng Po administered the forgetfulness soup; reprinted several times during the Kangxi era (1661-1722), spreading the belief through popular religion.Language Classical/vernacular Chinese

Translation

The Buddhist-authored play Guiyuan Jing declared that Meng Po administers matters relating to the Meng Po Soup; its repeated reprinting in the Kangxi period and the circulation of related works helped establish her as the goddess of the underworld who controls the amnesia brew.

Circulating print tradition including Quanxiang Guanyin Chushen Nanyoujizhuan (全像觀音出身南遊記傳), Cuhulu (醋葫蘆, 'The Vinegar Gourd'), Xieduo (諧鐸), and Wuxiayin Jie (吳下諺解)

Type text (folk print circulation)Date Qianlong era (1735-1796 CE) and earlier printingsLocator The zh-language article on Meng Po notes these works were printed and reprinted, consolidating the story that Meng Po is a deity dwelling in the kingdom of the Yellow Springs (Huangquan) who controls the Meng Po soup.Language Vernacular Chinese

Translation

With the continuous republishing of the Nanyoujizhuan, the Vinegar Gourd, Xieduo, and Wuxiayin Jie, the claim that Meng Po is a spirit resident in the Yellow Springs who oversees the amnesia soup was consolidated as part of folk religion by the Qianlong era.

Chinese Wikipedia article '孟婆' (Meng Po) and the English-language Wikipedia article 'Meng Po'

Type textDate accessed 2026-08-01Locator zh article details the diachronic evolution of the figure (Song-era wind/qì deity, Ming-era turn toward the soup, Qing consolidation); en article gives the identity, the Five-Flavored Soup, and the Lady Meng Jiang variant.Language Chinese and English

Translation

The zh article (consulted in full) traces Meng Po from a Song-dynasty deity associated with wind and vital qì, through the mid-Ming belief that the dead drank a soup to stop them returning, to her late-Ming identification as an assistant of the Ten Kings who drugs the dead to make them confess, and finally to the Ming-Qing and Qianlong consolidation of the forgetfulness-soup tradition. The en article summarizes her role at Diyu and the Lady Meng Jiang identification.

The 'Past life regression' article's note on Chinese religious mythology

Type text (reincarnation-theology reference)Date accessed 2026-08-01Locator States that in Chinese religious mythology, Meng Po, also known as the 'Lady of Forgetfulness,' prevents souls from remembering their past lives, giving them the 'Mi Hun Tang' (literally 'soul beguiling soup') that erases all memories before they climb the wheel of reincarnation.Language English

Translation

Meng Po is the deity who administers the amnesia draught so that souls forget their past lives before they climb onto the wheel of reincarnation.

Archive Dossier — Contradictions

Was Meng Po originally an underworld amnesia goddess at all?

Position A

Meng Po has always been the goddess of forgetting at the Naihe Bridge.

Position A — evidence

Modern folk- and pop-culture retellings that present the forgetfulness role as timeless.

Position B

The earliest documented Meng Po (Song dynasty) was a deity of wind and vital qì with no underworld function; the amnesia-soup role accrued only from the mid-Ming onward.

Position B — evidence

The zh 'Meng Po' article's diachronic account: Song wind/qì deity, mid-Ming 'dead drinking a soup,' late-Ming Longqing assistant-of-the-Ten-Kings, Wanli amnesia, Qianlong print-consolidation.

Scholarly Consensus

The historical development is clear in the Chinese-language record: the figure's powers were gradually reassigned over several centuries. The 'timeless amnesia goddess' is a modern synthesis, not the oldest layer.

Is the soup a mercy (forgetting suffering) or an interrogation drug (extracting truth)?

Position A

Her brew is an act of compassion that lets souls forget the suffering of their material lives.

Position A — evidence

The Lady-Meng-Jiang origin story and the merciful-forgetting consensus of later folklore.

Position B

The earlier Ming version made the brew a truth-agent that drugged the dead into confessing their deeds to Yanluo for judgment.

Position B — evidence

The zh article's Longqing-era account naming Meng Po an assistant who drugs the dead to make them reveal their deeds.

Scholarly Consensus

Both are attested but belong to different historical strata; the interrogational function is earler (mid-late Ming), the merciful amnesia function becomes the standard from the Wanli/Qianlong period. Neither is 'wrong'; they are successive folk inventions.

Who is Meng Po really — a Jade Emperor's princess, an anonymous divinity, or Lady Meng Jiang?

Position A

She is a mortal-born woman, the grief-stunned widow Lady Meng Jiang, who created the soup from empathy.

Position A — evidence

The Lady-Meng-Jiang variant in the en article.

Position B

She is an immortal princess, the eldest daughter of the Jade Emperor (玉皇大公主), sent by imperial decree to guard the underworld and pour the soul-beguiling brew, with the Forgetfulness Platform built for her by order of the Jade Emperor.

Position B — evidence

The zh article's statement that she 'is (suspected to be) the Jade Emperor's eldest princess... sent by jade decree to the underworld.'

Scholarly Consensus

Irreconcilable in single biography; both are folk-origin inventions. The name-anchor (a deified mortal woman surnamed Meng) is the most common, but the princess and the Wall-widow strands coexist.

Where exactly does she serve the soup — the Naihe Bridge or the Tenth Court's Forgetfulness Platform?

Position A

She is iconic at the Naihe Bridge, the final threshold before rebirth.

Position A — evidence

The standard folk and pop-culture image of the bridge-stall.

Position B

The Yanwang Jing places the drinking at the Tenth Court (Wheel-Turning King), at the Sixth Bridge beneath the Forgetfulness Platform (醧忘台).

Position B — evidence

The Yanwang Jing as summarized in the zh article.

Scholarly Consensus

The two loci coexist in the tradition; the bridge is the literary-iconic setting, the Tenth Court platform is the scripture-attested bureaucratic setting. The corpus does not demand a single answer.

Is the amnesia permanent and total, or are there leaks (miracle births)?

Position A

The soup induces instant and permanent amnesia; memory of all past lives is lost.

Position A — evidence

The standard 'permanent amnesia' formulation in the en article.

Position B

Souls can dodge the brew or the brew can fail, producing children who speak at birth and recount past lives.

Position B — evidence

The same article's miracle-birth legends and the whole 'past life regression' tradition built on recoverable past-life memories.

Scholarly Consensus

Contradictory within the folk corpus; the permanent-amnesia claim is the default, and the leak-stories are explicitly framed as exceptions that explain otherwise-unexplained childhood prodigies.

Why must memory be erased at all — theological necessity or bureaucratic control?

Position A

Forgetting is necessary mercy: carrying past-life memory would burden and poison the next life with unspent grief and vendetta.

Position A — evidence

The compassionate Lady-Meng-Jiang rationale and the standard mercy framing.

Position B

The erasure is a control mechanism: the dead must not leak 'the secrets of past incarnations' (泄露天机) — memory is suppressed because knowledge of past lives is dangerous or forbidden.

Position B — evidence

The zh article's note that Meng Po's duty is precisely to prevent the dead who knew past-life cause-and-effect from disclosing forbidden heavenly secrets.

Scholarly Consensus

Both motives are present in the sources; the compassionate rationale dominates popular telling while the secrecy/control rationale is explicit in the zh account of the Jade Emperor's decree. They are best read as two coexisting justifications rather than an either-or.

Archive Dossier — Reception

The Ferry Man Manjusaka (靈魂擺渡·黃泉篇)

Medium Film (web / television film)Creator Directed by Wang Baohe (as the 'Huangquan pian' installment of the Soul Ferry web-film); starring He Hua, Richards Wang, Yue Lina (as Old Lady Meng / Meng Qi)Year 2018Role direct adaptation

Notes

A 2018 Chinese fantasy-romance web film released on iQiyi on 1 February 2018, which 'increased the popularity' of Meng Po in 2018. Yue Lina played 'Old Lady Meng.' It reportedly accumulated about 42 million RMB in shared revenue, a figure comparable with then-current theatrical releases — making it the single most influential modern adaptation of the figure.

Katabasis

Medium NovelCreator R.F. KuangYear 2025Role direct adaptation (guardian of the Lethe)

Notes

In Kuang's 2025 fantastical academic-descend-into-hell novel, the main characters encounter Lady Meng Po as the guardian of the Lethe; 'much like her appearances in mythology, Meng Po brews drinks from the Lethe's waters that erase the memories of those who pass through.' The novel was a #1 New York Times bestseller and a Nebula Award finalist. Kuang's syncretism folds the Chinese goddess directly into the Greek Lethe/amnesia framework.

Meng-p'o Macula (Pluto)

Medium Planetary nomenclatureCreator IAU / NASA New Horizons teamYear 2015+Role namesake/visual quotation

Notes

Meng-pʻo Macula is the easternmost of Pluto's 'Brass Knuckles,' a series of equatorial dark regions. It straddles the zero meridian under Charon, just west of the 'Whale' (Belton Regio), and is named after Meng Po, the Chinese underworld deity of forgetfulness. The naming is an explicit homage to the deity.

Dislyte

Medium Video gameCreator Lilith GamesYear 2022Role inspired / ability reference

Notes

In the 2022 gacha role-playing game Dislyte, the character Jiang Man is said to be blessed with Meng Po's abilities. The exact relationship (whether Jiang Man literally is Meng Po under another name) is a game-lore matter; the en 'Meng Po' article states she is 'blessed with Meng Po's abilities.'

A 2021 Taiwanese drama titled Meng Po Kezhan (孟婆客棧) reimagines the underworld as an inn; the zh search results and Taiwanese-drama indexing list it as a 2021 drama re-working the Butterflly-Lovers and Meng Po mythology as a tragic-romantic underworld series.

A 2001 Hong Kong film titled 'Ghost Story: Horrible Tea,' whose Chinese title literally contains Meng Po Tea (孟婆茶), further linking the amnesia-brew to the figure in Cantonese popular cinema.

Madam Cutie On Duty

In the TVB drama 'Madam Cutie On Duty,' the actress Lily Li plays 'Mang Po,' a cleaning lady and garbage lady-in-charge at a housing-village cafe — a mundane, comic reincarnation of the Old Lady Meng as a janitorial matriarch.

Ten Courts of Hell (Haw Par Villa, Singapore) and Fengdu Ghost City reenactments

Medium Theme park / museumCreator Haw Par Villa / Hell's Museum (Singapore); Fengdu Ghost City (Chongqing)Year 1930s-present (Haw Par); Ming-bridge era-present (Fengdu)Role visual quotation / cultural heritage

Notes

The Ten Courts of Hell diorama at Singapore's Haw Par Villa (whose Hell's Museum, the world's first dedicated to death and the afterlife, opened in 2021) and the afterlife-set of Fengdu Ghost City both preserve visual depictions of the Chinese hellscape including the bridge-and-forgetting motif, keeping Meng Po's imagery in living public display.

Cultural Significance

The phrase Meng Po soup is everyday Chinese shorthand for anything that makes you forget. Her bridge and broth structure the Chinese imagination of reincarnation as profoundly as any scripture.
