Who It Is

In the weeds behind the tomb of the Yellow Emperor, something rustled with too many beaks. When she stood up, she was not a bird at all, but a woman in a searing red robe with eyes like autumn water — beautiful enough to topple a dynasty, and loyal enough to a goddess to do it on command. The Nine-Headed Pheasant Spirit, Hu Ximei, is the most overlooked of the three demons who destroyed the Shang, remembered only as Daji's "sister" — yet without her, the Fox could never have held King Zhou's palace.

As written, Zhiji Jing is not an autonomous schemer but a loyal lieutenant. Her defining traits are obedience to Nuwa's mandate (however perversely executed) and sororal loyalty to Daji. She is less cunning and less cruel than Daji, but willingly complicit. The novel gives her little interiority or speech independent of Daji's plans; she 'plays along' (陪) with Daji's seduction of King Zhou, enjoys palace luxury ('drowned herself with luxurious wine'), and follows Daji's lead in fleeing. Powers
Shapeshifting and Illusion (bianhua)

Core yaojing ability to assume perfect human female form, including clothing and beauty that surpasses ordinary women. Can also revert to true pheasant form and, in some readings, manifest intermediate hybrid features. The transformation is physical, not merely illusory, as Bi Gan's examination reveals true forms.

Limit: Transformation can be seen through by those with Daoist training or ritual insight (Bi Gan's discernment, Jiang Ziya's arts). Requires concentration; extreme fear or injury forces reversion. Cannot mimic male forms in the novel.

Enchantment and Seduction (meihuo)

Supernatural allure that amplifies natural beauty to bewitch King Zhou and courtiers, clouding judgment and fostering infatuation. Works in concert with Daji's stronger fox enchantment; Hu Ximei's role is to reinforce and normalize decadence in the harem.

Limit: Ineffective against those with righteous qi or Daoist protection (e.g., Huang Feihu's initial resistance, Jiang Ziya, Yang Jian). Dependent on proximity and King's willingness; does not grant mind control at distance.

Longevity and Cultivation (xiulian)

As a jing that has cultivated for centuries/millennia at Xuanyuan Tomb, she possesses extended lifespan and resilience beyond ordinary animals, and the ability to absorb lunar/solar essence. Implied ability to command lesser yao at the tomb.

Limit: Cultivation is explicitly inferior to orthodox Daoist or celestial cultivation; vulnerable to fire, celestial weapons, and exorcistic charms. The burning of Xuanyuan Tomb kills all lesser spirits, showing limits.

Survival and Evasion

Demonstrated ability to escape destruction where peers die — sole survivor of the Xuanyuan Tomb conflagration, and able to flee Zhaoge temporarily before final capture. Suggests superior speed or cunning compared to lesser yao.

Limit: Evasion fails against Yang Jian's tracking and Jiang Ziya's encirclement; not true invulnerability or teleportation.

Accounts I
The Mandate of Nuwa — Three Demons DispatchedFengshen Yanyi, Chapter 1: 'Nuwa Niangniang at the Palace, King Zhou Writes a Poem and Offends the Goddess'

Ming dynasty (16th century), set in mythic Shang (c. 1600-1046 BCE literary time)

After King Zhou of Shang offends Goddess Nuwa by writing a lewd poem on her temple wall during a pilgrimage, Nuwa resolves to hasten the Shang's fall. She summons three yaojing who have cultivated at Xuanyuan Tomb: the thousand-year-old fox spirit, the nine-headed pheasant spirit, and the jade pipa (lute) spirit. She charges them to bewitch King Zhou and corrupt his court, thereby gathering the karmic cause for his overthrow and the investiture of new gods. The pheasant spirit, though unnamed in this first summons, bows with the others and accepts the mandate. This divine commission frames all subsequent actions as both obedience and transgression — they are to ruin a king, but not to harm the innocent excessively, a line they will cross. Establishes the trio's cosmological role as instruments of dynastic change and sets the tragic irony that their service to Nuwa will ultimately lead to their execution when they exceed their mandate with cruelty.

The Xuanyuan Tomb and the Banquet of Heavenly MaidensFengshen Yanyi, Chapters 25-26

Ming literary Shang setting

Daji, now installed in the palace as Su Daji, returns to Xuanyuan Tomb to recruit her sisters for a scheme. She proposes they disguise themselves as heavenly maidens (xiannu) and appear at a banquet to entrap King Zhou further and humiliate his court. Zhiji Jing dons the red robe and autumn-water eyes, and with the fox cohorts transforms into radiant maidens. The trio enters the palace precincts, but Vice Prime Minister Bi Gan — noted for his loyal heart and ritual discernment — perceives their demonic qi. He exposes their true forms, forcing them to flee and revert. The episode demonstrates the limits of their illusion against orthodox virtue. Directly precipitates Bi Gan's investigation of Xuanyuan Tomb and the subsequent purge, and establishes Bi Gan as the spirits' nemesis.

The Burning of Xuanyuan TombFengshen Yanyi, Chapter 26-27: 'Bi Gan Burns the Foxes'

Ming literary Shang

After the exposure, Bi Gan petitions to extirpate the yao. He leads soldiers and, with the aid of Huang Feihu (or in some recensions, Bi Gan alone with fire), sets Xuanyuan Tomb ablaze. The fire consumes the ancient mound, killing every yao inside — foxes, pheasants, and lesser spirits — except Zhiji Jing herself, who is absent or escapes. The text lingers on the slaughter of foxes, with Daji later discovering the charred remains of her kin. Zhiji Jing, as the sole survivor found by Daji upon her return, becomes a figure of both luck and survivor guilt. Daji vows revenge on Bi Gan, leading to his eventual death via the infamous 'seven-orifice exquisite heart' demand. Annihilation of the spirits' home base, severing their retreat; motivates Daji's lethal revenge on Bi Gan; leaves Zhiji Jing dependent on Daji and the palace for survival.

Accounts II
Entry into Zhaoge as Hu XimeiFengshen Yanyi, Chapter 47-48 onward

Ming literary Shang

Having lost her mountain refuge, Zhiji Jing accepts Daji's invitation to enter Zhaoge permanently. She assumes the human identity Hu Ximei, presented to King Zhou as a distant beauty or tribute maiden even more beautiful than before. The King, already enthralled by Daji, welcomes her into the harem. As Hu Ximei, she 'spends personal time with King Zhou' and participates in palace pleasures, cementing the trio (Daji, Hu Ximei, Wang Guiren — the pipa spirit now also in palace as Wang Guiren) as a bloc that monopolizes the King's attention and isolates loyal ministers. The narrative notes she 'decided to stay with the king at Zhaoge instead of living in the mountains,' signaling a shift from cultivation to hedonism. Consolidates the three demons' control over the inner palace; accelerates Shang decadence (wine pool, meat forest, Deer Terrace); alienates the court and hastens defections to the Zhou.

Palace Decadence and the Wine PoolFengshen Yanyi, Chapters 48-70 (episodic)

Ming literary Shang

Through the middle third of the novel, Hu Ximei is a constant presence in scenes of excess: the wine pool (jiuchi) and meat forest (roulin), the Star-Picking Tower, and the construction of the Deer Terrace (Lutai) — a colossal palace of luxury built with corvée labor. While Daji is credited with inventing tortures like the paoluo (heated bronze pillar), Hu Ximei and Wang Guiren are shown enjoying the spectacle, drinking 'luxurious wine' to the point of self-drowning, and encouraging the King's neglect of state affairs. The text uses her enjoyment as moral shorthand for the court's rot, though she is not singled out for individual atrocities. Literary demonstration of the Mandate of Heaven being lost through misrule; provides narrative justification for the Zhou coalition's righteous rebellion.

Flight and Execution at the Fall of ZhaogeFengshen Yanyi, Chapter 97: 'The Three Demons Meet Their End'

Ming literary Shang

As Jiang Ziya's Zhou army approaches and Zhaoge collapses, the three spirits abandon the palace and attempt to flee in their true forms. They are intercepted by Jiang Ziya's forces. The novel's climactic judgment scene has Jiang Ziya order their execution for exceeding Nuwa's mandate — they were to bewitch the King, not to commit wanton cruelty and harm the loyal. Yang Jian (Erlang Shen) is tasked with carrying out the sentence. He executes Hu Ximei (and her sisters) — in most editions by beheading or by revealing true form and striking down. Their heads are displayed, and their souls are sent to the Investiture platform, though unlike many generals they are not ennobled as gods, underscoring their punishment. Closes the trio's arc; demonstrates that even divinely commissioned agents are accountable for excess; clears the moral stage for the Zhou founding and the final investiture of gods.

Hu Ximei is just another name for Daji, or that Daji herself is the nine-headed pheasant. — They are distinct sworn sisters. Daji is the thousand-year-old fox spirit possessing Su Daji; Hu Ximei is the nine-headed pheasant spirit who becomes Lady Hu. The novel consistently treats them as two individuals with separate species, entries into the palace, and executions.

The nine-headed pheasant is an ancient Chinese god or folk deity with temples. — Hu Ximei has no temple cult, iconography, or ritual worship. She is a Ming literary yaojing, not a deity. The nine-headed bird (Jiufeng) had folk beliefs as an omen, but not as Hu Ximei, and even Jiufeng was more prodigy than god.

Hu Ximei was a real consort of the historical King Zhou of Shang. — No Shang oracle bone or Zhou historical text (Shiji, etc.) mentions Hu Ximei. The historical Di Xin's consorts are poorly documented, but none correspond to this figure. She is a 16th-century fictional insertion to moralize the Shang's fall.

Her powers include nine lives, nine separate consciousnesses, or control over wind/plague. — The novel attributes no such powers. Her abilities are shapeshifting and enchantment; nine heads are symbolic/monstrous, not functional extra lives. Wind/plague powers belong to other Fengshen characters or to game adaptations that invent mechanics.
