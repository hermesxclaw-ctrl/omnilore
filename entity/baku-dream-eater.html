Who It Is

When the nightmare closes in, and the dark feels too real, the Japanese call out a single name: 'Baku, kurae!' — and a shadowy, tapir-like creature emerges from the space between worlds to devour your fear. It is not a god, not a demon, but a spiritual scavenger, a guardian of the fragile border between sleep and waking. The baku is the original dream-eater, a myth that has evolved from a Chinese chimera to a beloved protector of children's sleep, and its story is a journey through the very nature of fear itself.

The baku is a nocturnal, solitary creature that enters bedrooms at night. It does not attack humans. Its primary function is to devour nightmares. It is not inherently malicious, but it is also not a 'friendly' pet; it is a force of nature, a spiritual scavenger. It is drawn to the psychic energy of fear and dread. Calling upon it is a desperate act, and its response is not guaranteed.

Powers
Dream Consumption

The baku's primary and defining power. It enters a person's dream and devours the nightmare, allowing the dreamer to wake peacefully. It does not eat pleasant dreams, and it does not grant wishes.

Limit: The baku must be invoked. Simply having a nightmare is not enough; the dreamer (or someone nearby) must call upon it. The invocation is a specific phrase: 'Baku, kurae' (バク、食らえ), meaning 'Baku, eat it.' If the baku is not invoked, it will not help. Some sources say that if the baku is called upon too often, it may eat the dreamer's good dreams as well, or even their hopes and ambitions, leaving them hollow.

Apotropaic Protection

The image or name of the baku, when placed near a sleeping person, provides protection from nightmares. This is a passive, magical property of its iconography.

Limit: The protection is not absolute. It is a charm, not a guarantee. Its efficacy is tied to the belief of the person using it.

Ambiguous Consumption

While its primary function is to eat nightmares, the baku is also a consumer of other negative psychic phenomena, such as fear, dread, and anxiety. It is a spiritual scavenger.

Limit: This is a less-attested power. It is not explicitly described in most primary sources, but is a logical extension of its dream-eating role.

Form & Signs

Appearance — In Japanese art, the baku is almost universally depicted as a tapir: a stout, pig-like body, short legs, a thick tail, and a distinctive, elongated, flexible snout (proboscis). It is often shown with a shaggy or bristly coat. In earlier Chinese-influenced depictions, it appears as a chimera with a bear's body, an elephant's trunk, a rhino's eyes, a buffalo's tail, and a tiger's legs. The modern visual identity is firmly that of a tapir.

Symbols — The baku is a symbol of protection against nightmares and, by extension, against the psychological and spiritual harm they represent. Its image is used on amulets, pillows, and children's bedding. In the modern day, it is a popular motif in anime, manga, and games, often representing dreams, sleep, or the consumption of negative energy. Its iconography is that of a gentle, powerful guardian of the subconscious.

Domains — The baku's primary domain is the liminal space between sleep and waking, the bedside of a person experiencing a nightmare. It is said to reside in the 'shadow realm' or the space between worlds, and is more likely to appear in times of psychological distress. It is also believed to inhabit quiet, dark places in the human world, such as attics, closets, or shadowed corners of a room.

Accounts I
The Call for the BakuEhon Hyaku Monogatari (1841) and other Edo-period yokai collections

Edo period (1603-1868)

A person is plagued by a recurring, terrifying nightmare. In desperation, they cry out 'Baku, kurae!' (Baku, eat it!). The baku appears, a shadowy tapir-like form, and silently consumes the nightmare. The dreamer wakes, the fear gone. The baku, having fed, vanishes back into the shadows until it is needed again. The dreamer is relieved of their fear. The baku is sated. The act of calling the baku is a form of self-help, a recognition that one's own fear is a tangible thing that can be consumed.

Accounts II
The Baku and the ChildModern Japanese children's literature and folklore collections

20th-21st century

A child has a nightmare about a monster. Their parent tells them to call on the baku. The child does, and the nightmare is eaten. The child learns to face their fears, knowing they have a protector. This story is a common trope in modern retellings, emphasizing the baku's role as a gentle, protective figure for children. The child's fear is alleviated. The baku becomes a symbol of comfort and safety.
