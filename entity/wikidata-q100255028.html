Who It Is

Before there was Jules Verne, before Jonathan Swift, there was a Syrian satirist who sent his narrator sailing to the Moon, sold philosophers at auction, and made the gods look ridiculous. His name was Lucian of Samosata, and his pen was sharper than any sword in the Roman Empire.

Known for his sharp wit, sarcasm, and skepticism. He ridiculed pretension, superstition, and philosophical dogmatism. His writing style is characterized by irony and parody.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Lucian' derives from the Latin 'Lucianus', a common Roman name. 'Of Samosata' indicates his birthplace.

Pronunciation — LOO-shan of sa-mo-SAH-ta

Aliases — Lucian, Lukianos, Lukinos, The Syrian

Ownership — Hellenized Syrian culture of the Roman Empire; primarily associated with Greek literature and the Second Sophistic movement.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No reliable descriptions of Lucian's physical appearance exist. He may have resembled typical Syrian men of his time, possibly with dark hair and complexion, but this is speculative.

Symbols — No specific symbols associated with Lucian. In later art, he is often depicted as a classical scholar or satirist.

Domains — Lived in Samosata, Ionia, Athens, and possibly Egypt. He traveled extensively as a lecturer.

Accounts I
A True StoryLucian's own work

2nd century CE

A satirical tale of a voyage to the Moon and fantastical adventures, mocking improbable travel narratives. Considered an early precursor to science fiction.

Accounts II
The DreamLucian's own work

2nd century CE

Lucian describes a dream where personifications of Statuary and Culture compete for his future, leading him to choose education. Often read as autobiographical, but likely fictional.

The Passing of PeregrinusLucian's own work

2nd century CE

A satirical account of the Cynic philosopher Peregrinus who immolated himself at the Olympic Games. Provides insight into early Christian and Cynic movements.
