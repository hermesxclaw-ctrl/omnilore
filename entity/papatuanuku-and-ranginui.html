Who It Is

Papatūānuku (the Earth Mother) and Ranginui (the Sky Father) are the primordial parents of Māori cosmology — the two beings whose eternal embrace in darkness marked the state of the universe before creation. From their union came all the gods, and their children's decision to separate them brought light and life into the world. Together, Papa and Rangi represent the fundamental cosmic duality that structures Māori thought: earth and sky, female and male, below and above, darkness and light.

Physically, Papa is the earth itself — the soil, the rocks, the mountains, the land (whenua) upon which all life depends. She is the source of all physical substance, the nourisher who feeds her children through the bounty of the land. In Māori art and metaphor, Papa is the ground beneath our feet, the whenua that is also the word for placenta — linking the birth of humans with the birth of the land. When a child is born, the whenua (placenta) is traditionally buried in the earth (also whenua), returning the child's origin to Papa and anchoring them to the land of their ancestors.

Ranginui (Rangi) is the sky — the vast expanse above, the realm of light, stars, rain, and the source of celestial order. His tears (the rain) continually fall upon Papa as he grieves their separation, and the mist rising from the earth is Papa's answering sigh. Together, they represent the original, undifferentiated state of cosmic unity — a condition of potential that contained all future existence but lacked the light and space necessary for life to develop.

The central myth of Rangi and Papa is the separation (te wehenga). In the beginning, Rangi lay atop Papa in a tight, unbroken embrace, and all their children were trapped in the darkness between them. The children — Tāne, Tangaroa, Tūmatauenga, Rongo, Haumia-tiketike, Tāwhirimātea, and many others — could not move, grow, or see. They debated what to do: kill their parents to free themselves? Leave things as they were? Tāne proposed a middle path: push the parents apart. One by one, the brothers attempted the separation, but only Tāne succeeded — lying on his back on Papa, placing his feet against Rangi, and pushing with all his divine strength. With a cry of agony that echoed through the cosmos, Rangi and Papa were torn apart. Light flooded into the world for the first time.

Tāwhirimātea, the god of winds, was the only child who opposed the separation. Enraged by the violence done to his parents, he rose to join his father Rangi in the sky and from there waged war against his brothers — his storms battering Tāne's forests, Tangaroa's seas, and Rongo and Haumia's fields. This is why storms, hurricanes, and destructive winds exist — they are Tāwhirimātea's continuing vengeance for the separation of his parents.

Papa and Rangi's grief remains the most poignant element of the myth. They call out to each other across the vast space between them: Rangi's tears fall as rain; Papa's sighs rise as mist. The love that endures despite separation is captured in Māori love poetry and oratory. A person separated from their beloved may be compared to Rangi longing for Papa. The myth thus operates on multiple levels: cosmological (explaining the physical universe), genealogical (establishing the divine lineage of all things), and emotional (modeling love, loss, and the necessity of painful growth).

In Māori ritual, Papa and Rangi are honored in all formal oratory (whakapapa recitations always begin with them), in the protocols of the meeting house (the house represents the space Tāne created between them), and in the daily acknowledgment of the earth we walk on and the sky above us.

Form

Attested descriptions in Māori tradition focus less on physical detail and more on cosmological position. Ranginui is the overarching sky, often imagined as a vast, curved dome or fatherly presence above, while Papatūānuku is the earth itself—the land, soil, and ground on which people live. In narrative imagery, they lie pressed together in a tight embrace, with their children confined in the narrow space between them. After separation, Ranginui is lifted high, sometimes adorned with stars by Tāne, and Papatūānuku remains below, nurturing life. Their ongoing grief is expressed through tears (rain) and mist, and through the dampness of the earth. Specific bodily features (face, limbs) are largely reconstructed from metaphor rather than detailed textual description.

Powers

Primordial creation and genealogical origin Papatūānuku and Ranginui function as the primordial parents from whom gods, humans, and all things originate. Their union produces numerous atua who govern natural domains, and humans are ultimately traced back to them through whakapapa. In some narratives, humans are formed from the red clay of Papatūānuku, animated by divine breath, making her both physical and genealogical mother. Grey/Te Rangikāheke tradition; EBSCO Research Starters; Māori cosmological genealogies (Te Kore–Te Pō–Te Ao–Rangi–Papa) Their creative role is expressed through genealogy and metaphor rather than discrete acts of making objects or beings; they are not depicted as crafting specific animals or landscapes in the manner of artisan gods. No major contradictions about their status as primordial parents, though the exact sequence of cosmological stages and the number of generations between Te Kore and Rangi/Papa varies between iwi.

Cosmic structuring of space (earth below, sky above) Their separation by Tāne (or Tangaroa in some traditions) creates the world of light and space. Ranginui is lifted to become the sky above, Papatūānuku remains as earth below, and the children gain room to move and live. This act establishes the vertical structure of the cosmos and the distinction between heaven and earth. Grey/Te Rangikāheke tradition; Cooper and Auerbach summaries; Wikipedia "Rangi and Papa" They themselves do not initiate the separation; it is imposed by their children. Their role is passive in the act of structuring, though their bodies become the structure. Different traditions credit different gods with the separating act (Tāne vs Tangaroa vs Rongo), and some emphasise killing as a proposed but rejected option, highlighting moral debate among the children.

Nurturing and fertility Papatūānuku is explicitly described as nurturing her children, providing soil, food, and physical support. Gardens, forests, and wild plants are understood as her gifts, and the placenta (whenua) is ritually returned to the land, reinforcing her maternal role. Ranginui’s role is more atmospheric: he provides the sky under which life flourishes and, through rain, contributes to fertility. Grey/Te Rangikāheke tradition; Māori ritual practices; EBSCO summaries; Mātauranga Māori cosmology article Their nurturing is general and continuous rather than targeted; they are not depicted as intervening in specific human affairs or granting individual boons. No direct contradictions, though modern ecological readings emphasise Papatūānuku’s role as a living ancestor, which may not be foregrounded in older written sources.

Emotional influence expressed as weather phenomena After separation, Ranginui and Papatūānuku’s grief and longing are expressed through mist, dew, and rain. Tears fall from Ranginui as rain; Papatūānuku’s sighs and moisture rise as mist. These phenomena are interpreted as ongoing emotional communication between sky father and earth mother. Grey/Te Rangikāheke tradition; EBSCO summaries; Wikipedia "War in heaven and earth" section This is a symbolic explanation rather than a mechanistic power; they do not consciously control weather in the way Tāwhirimātea does. Some narratives attribute storms and destructive weather primarily to Tāwhirimātea’s anger rather than to the parents’ grief, creating a layered explanation of meteorological events.

Genealogical authority and sacred knowledge Whakapapa that begins with Te Kore, Te Pō, Te Ao and proceeds through Ranginui and Papatūānuku is considered among the most sacred knowledge in Mātauranga Māori. Reciting these genealogies is itself a creative and ritual act, reaffirming the connection between people, land, and sky. Mātauranga Māori cosmology article ("IN THE BEGINNING..."), Māori scholarly discussions of whakapapa This is an authority conferred by tradition rather than a "power" in the superhero sense; it operates through human ritual and memory. Different iwi maintain distinct genealogical sequences and may emphasise different ancestors or cosmological stages, leading to multiple coexisting authoritative lines.

Accounts

In the beginning, Ranginui (sky father) and Papatūānuku (earth mother) are locked in a tight embrace, and their many children live cramped in darkness between them. The children grow restless and debate how to gain light and space. Tūmatauenga, the fiercest, proposes killing their parents; others argue for separating them. All agree except Tāwhirimātea, god of winds and storms, who fears for his kingdom and grieves for his parents. Various brothers attempt to push Rangi and Papa apart—Rongo, Tangaroa, Haumia—but fail. Finally Tāne-mahuta, god of forests and birds, lies on his back, braces his shoulders against Papatūānuku and his feet against Ranginui, and pushes with all his strength until, with cries of grief, the parents are torn apart. Ranginui becomes the sky above, Papatūānuku remains the earth below, and light floods the world. Tāwhirimātea, enraged, allies himself with his father and unleashes storms against his brothers, uprooting forests, churning seas, and testing the resilience of each domain. Tūmatauenga withstands the onslaught and later asserts dominance over his siblings, establishing human practices of hunting, fishing, agriculture, and warfare.

Many Māori traditions begin not with Rangi and Papa but with a sequence of cosmological states: Te Kore (nothingness, potential), Te Pō (night, darkness), and Te Ao (light). These states are sometimes enumerated in long genealogical lists—Te Kore-tē-whiwhia (nothingness without possession), Te Kore-tē-rawea (nothingness without feeling), and so on—each representing a stage in the unfolding of existence. From these states emerge the first physical representations of ancestors, including Ranginui and Papatūānuku, who embody sky and earth. Their children then populate and govern the natural world, linking humans and all phenomena back to the primordial genealogical chain.

After the painful separation of Ranginui and Papatūānuku, Tāne-mahuta seeks to beautify his father’s new position in the sky. He adorns Ranginui with stars and other celestial bodies, arranging them across the heavens. This act both honours the sky father and creates the night sky as humans know it, with constellations that serve as navigational and calendrical markers.

Content Notes

In the creation narrative, Tūmatauenga proposes killing Papatūānuku and Ranginui to escape darkness and confinement. This suggestion introduces the possibility of extreme familial violence at the very origin of the world. The eventual decision to separate rather than kill them is framed as morally preferable, but the proposal itself remains part of the story.

Scholars note that this episode highlights ethical deliberation among the gods and may function as a cautionary tale about violence within families, while also reflecting the seriousness of the children’s predicament.

Grey/Te Rangikāheke tradition; Cooper, "The Children of Heaven and Earth"

The separation of Papatūānuku and Ranginui is forced upon them by their children, against the wishes of Tāwhirimātea and without the parents’ consent. They cry out in pain and grief as they are torn apart, and their ongoing longing is expressed through tears and mist. This can be read as a narrative of permanent enforced separation of loving partners.

Māori commentators and scholars often emphasise the emotional weight of this separation, using it to discuss themes of loss, exile, and the consequences of necessary but painful change.

Grey/Te Rangikāheke tradition; EBSCO summaries; Wikipedia

The most widely known English-language versions of the Rangi and Papa story derive from George Grey’s 19th‑century publications, which were based on Māori manuscripts but edited and framed within colonial perspectives. This transmission history raises concerns about selective representation, Christianising interpretations, and the marginalisation of iwi-specific variants.

Contemporary Māori scholars and activists critique the dominance of Grey’s versions in education and popular culture, advocating for a return to iwi-led narratives and recognition of the colonial context in which these stories were recorded.

What We Don't Know

This dossier relies on secondary summaries and a limited set of accessible sources. I did not directly consult the full Te Rangikāheke manuscripts or iwi-specific oral recitations, which would provide more precise locators and variant details. Many regional traditions (Ngāpuhi, Tūhoe, Ngāti Raukawa, Taranaki, South Island iwi) have their own versions of the Rangi and Papa narrative that are only partially represented in English-language scholarship. Specific modern artistic, theatrical, and educational uses of Papatūānuku and Ranginui are acknowledged in general terms but not documented with detailed bibliographic entries due to source limitations. Pronunciation is given in approximate English terms rather than full IPA, to avoid over‑precision without direct phonetic sources. Colonial editorial interventions in Grey’s texts are noted but not analysed in depth. Further research should involve consultation with Māori knowledge holders, iwi-specific publications, and archival materials.

A way to hold it in your mind

Before there was room to stand, before light could slip between earth and sky, Papatūānuku and Ranginui lay pressed together in a single, unbroken embrace—so close that their children could only crawl in the dark between them, debating whether to kill the parents they loved or tear them apart forever.
