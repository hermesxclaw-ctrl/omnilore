Archive Dossier — Identity

Papatūānuku and Ranginui

Pronunciation Common English approximations: Papatūānuku ≈ "Papa-too-ah-nook-oo"; Ranginui ≈ "Rung-ee-noo-ee". In te reo Māori, vowels are generally pronounced as in Italian or Spanish, with long vowels marked by macrons.

Known As

Etymology

In te reo Māori, "Papatūānuku" is commonly analysed as "Papa" (earth, foundation), "tūā" (beyond, distant, ancestral) and "nuku" (land, earth, expanse), conveying the idea of the vast earth as ancestral foundation. "Ranginui" combines "rangi" (sky, heavens) and "nui" (great), meaning "great sky" or "great heaven". These names encode their cosmological roles as primordial earth and sky, and are embedded in whakapapa (genealogical) recitations that link Māori people and all beings back to them.

Cultural Ownership

The narratives of Papatūānuku and Ranginui belong to Māori iwi and hapū of Aotearoa New Zealand. While a broadly shared pattern of earth-mother/sky-father creation exists, specific details, names, and roles vary between tribal traditions (e.g., Ngāpuhi, Tūhoe, Ngāti Raukawa, Taranaki). Written English-language versions are heavily shaped by 19th‑century collectors such as Governor George Grey drawing on Māori informants like Wiremu Maihi Te Rangikāheke, but the underlying authority rests with Māori oral and ritual traditions.

Disputed Identifications

Comparative mythologists sometimes align Papatūānuku and Ranginui with other earth/sky pairs such as Egyptian Nut and Geb or Greek Gaia and Ouranos, but Māori scholars stress that these are heuristic analogies rather than true identifications. Within Polynesia, related but distinct pairs appear in other cultures, and even within Māori tradition some iwi emphasise different primordial states (Te Kore, Te Pō, Te Ao) or different divine actors. Modern scholarship also debates the degree to which Grey’s published narratives accurately represent Te Rangikāheke’s Māori manuscripts versus colonial editorial shaping.

Archive Dossier — Approach

Before there was room to stand, before light could slip between earth and sky, Papatūānuku and Ranginui lay pressed together in a single, unbroken embrace—so close that their children could only crawl in the dark between them, debating whether to kill the parents they loved or tear them apart forever.

We held each other so tightly that our children could not breathe. They pushed us apart, and the world was born in the space between us. My tears fall as rain; her sighs rise as mist. We are still in love.

Who It Is

Papatūānuku (the Earth Mother) and Ranginui (the Sky Father) are the primordial parents of Māori cosmology — the two beings whose eternal embrace in darkness marked the state of the universe before creation. From their union came all the gods, and their children's decision to separate them brought light and life into the world. Together, Papa and Rangi represent the fundamental cosmic duality that structures Māori thought: earth and sky, female and male, below and above, darkness and light.

Physically, Papa is the earth itself — the soil, the rocks, the mountains, the land (whenua) upon which all life depends. She is the source of all physical substance, the nourisher who feeds her children through the bounty of the land. In Māori art and metaphor, Papa is the ground beneath our feet, the whenua that is also the word for placenta — linking the birth of humans with the birth of the land. When a child is born, the whenua (placenta) is traditionally buried in the earth (also whenua), returning the child's origin to Papa and anchoring them to the land of their ancestors.

Ranginui (Rangi) is the sky — the vast expanse above, the realm of light, stars, rain, and the source of celestial order. His tears (the rain) continually fall upon Papa as he grieves their separation, and the mist rising from the earth is Papa's answering sigh. Together, they represent the original, undifferentiated state of cosmic unity — a condition of potential that contained all future existence but lacked the light and space necessary for life to develop.

The central myth of Rangi and Papa is the separation (te wehenga). In the beginning, Rangi lay atop Papa in a tight, unbroken embrace, and all their children were trapped in the darkness between them. The children — Tāne, Tangaroa, Tūmatauenga, Rongo, Haumia-tiketike, Tāwhirimātea, and many others — could not move, grow, or see. They debated what to do: kill their parents to free themselves? Leave things as they were? Tāne proposed a middle path: push the parents apart. One by one, the brothers attempted the separation, but only Tāne succeeded — lying on his back on Papa, placing his feet against Rangi, and pushing with all his divine strength. With a cry of agony that echoed through the cosmos, Rangi and Papa were torn apart. Light flooded into the world for the first time.

Tāwhirimātea, the god of winds, was the only child who opposed the separation. Enraged by the violence done to his parents, he rose to join his father Rangi in the sky and from there waged war against his brothers — his storms battering Tāne's forests, Tangaroa's seas, and Rongo and Haumia's fields. This is why storms, hurricanes, and destructive winds exist — they are Tāwhirimātea's continuing vengeance for the separation of his parents.

Papa and Rangi's grief remains the most poignant element of the myth. They call out to each other across the vast space between them: Rangi's tears fall as rain; Papa's sighs rise as mist. The love that endures despite separation is captured in Māori love poetry and oratory. A person separated from their beloved may be compared to Rangi longing for Papa. The myth thus operates on multiple levels: cosmological (explaining the physical universe), genealogical (establishing the divine lineage of all things), and emotional (modeling love, loss, and the necessity of painful growth).

In Māori ritual, Papa and Rangi are honored in all formal oratory (whakapapa recitations always begin with them), in the protocols of the meeting house (the house represents the space Tāne created between them), and in the daily acknowledgment of the earth we walk on and the sky above us.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

Attested descriptions in Māori tradition focus less on physical detail and more on cosmological position. Ranginui is the overarching sky, often imagined as a vast, curved dome or fatherly presence above, while Papatūānuku is the earth itself—the land, soil, and ground on which people live. In narrative imagery, they lie pressed together in a tight embrace, with their children confined in the narrow space between them. After separation, Ranginui is lifted high, sometimes adorned with stars by Tāne, and Papatūānuku remains below, nurturing life. Their ongoing grief is expressed through tears (rain) and mist, and through the dampness of the earth. Specific bodily features (face, limbs) are largely reconstructed from metaphor rather than detailed textual description.

Symbols & Iconography

Papatūānuku is symbolised by landforms, red clay, gardens, and the physical whenua (placenta/land) that binds people to place. Ranginui is symbolised by the sky, sun, stars, clouds, and the overarching dome of heaven. In carvings and visual art, they may be represented as human-like figures in embrace or as stylised forms of sky and earth. Tāne’s adorning of Ranginui with stars is a key iconographic motif, and the mist rising from the ground and dew on plants are interpreted as the parents’ tears of longing. In contemporary Māori art and educational materials, they often appear as paired figures or as labelled earth and sky, reinforcing their role as primordial parents.

Behaviour & Personality

Narratively, Papatūānuku and Ranginui are loving, nurturing, and deeply attached to one another. Their tight embrace is both an expression of love and the cause of their children’s confinement. They do not speak extensively in surviving texts, but their grief at separation is emphasised: they cry out when Tāne forces them apart, and their tears and sighs continue as weather phenomena. They are not portrayed as capricious or punitive; rather, their emotional constancy contrasts with the more volatile behavior of their children, such as Tāwhirimātea’s storms and Tūmatauenga’s warlike nature. Their primary motive, as inferred from tradition, is mutual love and the ongoing nurturing of their descendants.

Domains & Habitats

Papatūānuku’s domain is the earth: land, soil, mountains, forests, and the physical ground that supports life. She is the source of cultivated and wild foods, and humans are often said to be formed from her red clay. Ranginui’s domain is the sky: the upper world, weather, celestial bodies, and the overarching space above the earth. Together they constitute the physical cosmos—earth below, sky above—with their children ruling specific domains (sea, forests, winds, war, cultivated foods, uncultivated foods). Their "habitat" is thus the entire world, structured vertically after their separation.

Version Differences

Attested differences between versions include: (1) Names and dialectal variants: Rangi vs Ranginui, Raki/Rakinui in some South Island traditions; Papa vs Papatūānuku/Papa-tū-ā-nuku. (2) Pre‑history: some traditions begin with Te Kore (nothingness), Te Pō (darkness), and Te Ao (light) before introducing Rangi and Papa; others describe a primordial shell with sky and earth as its top and bottom. (3) Number and identity of children: Grey’s sources and later summaries mention six principal sons (Tūmatauenga, Tāne-mahuta, Tāwhirimātea, Tangaroa, Rongo-mā-Tāne, Haumia-tiketike) and sometimes many more atua (up to seventy or hundreds). (4) The agent of separation: in many North Island traditions, Tāne-mahuta separates his parents by pushing with his legs; in some Taranaki traditions, Tangaroa is credited with the separation; other versions briefly assign the task to Rongo or Tangaroa before Tāne succeeds. (5) Post‑separation adornment: some narratives emphasise Tāne adorning Ranginui with stars; others focus more on Tāwhirimātea’s war against his siblings. These differences reflect iwi-specific whakapapa and should not be harmonised into a single "canonical" version.

Archive Dossier — Powers

Primordial creation and genealogical origin

Source Version Grey/Te Rangikāheke tradition; EBSCO Research Starters; Māori cosmological genealogies (Te Kore–Te Pō–Te Ao–Rangi–Papa)

Description

Papatūānuku and Ranginui function as the primordial parents from whom gods, humans, and all things originate. Their union produces numerous atua who govern natural domains, and humans are ultimately traced back to them through whakapapa. In some narratives, humans are formed from the red clay of Papatūānuku, animated by divine breath, making her both physical and genealogical mother.

Limitations

Their creative role is expressed through genealogy and metaphor rather than discrete acts of making objects or beings; they are not depicted as crafting specific animals or landscapes in the manner of artisan gods.

Contradictions

No major contradictions about their status as primordial parents, though the exact sequence of cosmological stages and the number of generations between Te Kore and Rangi/Papa varies between iwi.

Cosmic structuring of space (earth below, sky above)

Source Version Grey/Te Rangikāheke tradition; Cooper and Auerbach summaries; Wikipedia "Rangi and Papa"

Description

Their separation by Tāne (or Tangaroa in some traditions) creates the world of light and space. Ranginui is lifted to become the sky above, Papatūānuku remains as earth below, and the children gain room to move and live. This act establishes the vertical structure of the cosmos and the distinction between heaven and earth.

Limitations

They themselves do not initiate the separation; it is imposed by their children. Their role is passive in the act of structuring, though their bodies become the structure.

Contradictions

Different traditions credit different gods with the separating act (Tāne vs Tangaroa vs Rongo), and some emphasise killing as a proposed but rejected option, highlighting moral debate among the children.

Nurturing and fertility

Source Version Grey/Te Rangikāheke tradition; Māori ritual practices; EBSCO summaries; Mātauranga Māori cosmology article

Description

Papatūānuku is explicitly described as nurturing her children, providing soil, food, and physical support. Gardens, forests, and wild plants are understood as her gifts, and the placenta (whenua) is ritually returned to the land, reinforcing her maternal role. Ranginui’s role is more atmospheric: he provides the sky under which life flourishes and, through rain, contributes to fertility.

Limitations

Their nurturing is general and continuous rather than targeted; they are not depicted as intervening in specific human affairs or granting individual boons.

Contradictions

No direct contradictions, though modern ecological readings emphasise Papatūānuku’s role as a living ancestor, which may not be foregrounded in older written sources.

Emotional influence expressed as weather phenomena

Source Version Grey/Te Rangikāheke tradition; EBSCO summaries; Wikipedia "War in heaven and earth" section

Description

After separation, Ranginui and Papatūānuku’s grief and longing are expressed through mist, dew, and rain. Tears fall from Ranginui as rain; Papatūānuku’s sighs and moisture rise as mist. These phenomena are interpreted as ongoing emotional communication between sky father and earth mother.

Limitations

This is a symbolic explanation rather than a mechanistic power; they do not consciously control weather in the way Tāwhirimātea does.

Contradictions

Some narratives attribute storms and destructive weather primarily to Tāwhirimātea’s anger rather than to the parents’ grief, creating a layered explanation of meteorological events.

Genealogical authority and sacred knowledge

Source Version Mātauranga Māori cosmology article ("IN THE BEGINNING..."), Māori scholarly discussions of whakapapa

Description

Whakapapa that begins with Te Kore, Te Pō, Te Ao and proceeds through Ranginui and Papatūānuku is considered among the most sacred knowledge in Mātauranga Māori. Reciting these genealogies is itself a creative and ritual act, reaffirming the connection between people, land, and sky.

Limitations

This is an authority conferred by tradition rather than a "power" in the superhero sense; it operates through human ritual and memory.

Contradictions

Different iwi maintain distinct genealogical sequences and may emphasise different ancestors or cosmological stages, leading to multiple coexisting authoritative lines.

Archive Dossier — Relationships

Poharua Te Pō

Type familySource Wikipedia "Rangi and Papa"; Grey’s sources as cited there

Description

In some traditions, Ranginui first marries Poharua Te Pō, a figure associated with night or darkness, and they have several offspring including Aorangi/Aoraki. This earlier union situates Ranginui within a broader network of relationships before his more famous union with Papatūānuku.

Version Note

Not all iwi emphasise this relationship; it appears particularly in traditions associated with the South Island and Aoraki.

Conflicting Accounts

Some narratives omit Poharua Te Pō entirely, presenting Papatūānuku as Ranginui’s primary or sole consort.

Children of Rangi and Papa (Tāne-mahuta, Tāwhirimātea, Tangaroa, Tūmatauenga, Rongo-mā-Tāne, Haumia-tiketike, others)

Type familySource Grey/Te Rangikāheke tradition; EBSCO summaries; Mātauranga Māori cosmology article

Description

Papatūānuku and Ranginui are parents to numerous atua who govern natural and human domains: Tāne-mahuta (forests and birds), Tāwhirimātea (winds and storms), Tangaroa (sea and marine life), Tūmatauenga (war and humankind), Rongo-mā-Tāne (cultivated foods), Haumia-tiketike (wild foods), among many others. Their children’s relationships—cooperative, competitive, and hostile—shape the world.

Version Note

The exact list and number of children varies; some sources mention hundreds of offspring, others focus on six principal sons.

Conflicting Accounts

Different iwi emphasise different children and domains; some attribute slightly different responsibilities (e.g., Rongo’s role in peace as well as agriculture).

Tāne-mahuta

Type familySource Grey/Te Rangikāheke tradition; Cooper and Auerbach summaries; Wikipedia

Description

Tāne-mahuta is a son of Papatūānuku and Ranginui and the god of forests and birds. He plays the decisive role in separating his parents in many traditions, lying on his back and pushing them apart with his legs. Later he adorns Ranginui with stars.

Version Note

In some regional traditions, Tangaroa is credited with the separation instead of Tāne.

Conflicting Accounts

Taranaki traditions assign the separating role to Tangaroa; some narratives describe multiple attempts by different brothers before Tāne succeeds.

Tāwhirimātea

Type familySource Grey/Te Rangikāheke tradition; EBSCO summaries; Wikipedia

Description

Tāwhirimātea, god of winds and storms, is a son of Papatūānuku and Ranginui who opposes the plan to separate his parents. After the separation, he allies himself with Ranginui and wages war against his siblings, sending storms to uproot forests, churn seas, and test human resilience.

Version Note

His loyalty to his parents and hostility toward his siblings are central to the ongoing cosmic conflict.

Conflicting Accounts

No major contradictions about his opposition, though the intensity and outcomes of his attacks vary in different tellings.

Tūmatauenga

Type familySource Grey/Te Rangikāheke tradition; EBSCO summaries

Description

Tūmatauenga, the fiercest son, initially proposes killing Papatūānuku and Ranginui to gain light and space. When the plan to separate them prevails, he later asserts dominance over his siblings, turning them into resources for humankind (fish, birds, crops) and becoming the ancestor of humans and war.

Version Note

His proposal to kill the parents is a key moral tension in the story.

Conflicting Accounts

Some versions emphasise his later punishment of siblings more than his initial proposal; others focus on his role as ancestor of humans.

Humans (tāngata)

Type familySource Auerbach, "The Polynesian Creation Myth"; Māori oral traditions; Grey’s sources

Description

Humans are ultimately descended from Papatūānuku and Ranginui through their children, particularly Tūmatauenga. In some narratives, humans are formed from the red clay of Papatūānuku, animated by divine breath, making them literal children of earth and sky.

Version Note

The specific process of human creation varies between traditions.

Conflicting Accounts

Some versions emphasise direct descent through genealogy rather than a clay-formation episode; others integrate both.
