Who It Is

The jiangshi is China's vampire — a corpse gone rigid with death but not still, arms locked straight ahead, hopping in stiff-legged bounds because its joints no longer bend, draining the living not of blood but of qi, the life-breath itself. Qing dynasty tales explain where they come from: a corpse struck by lightning, a cat jumping over a coffin, a body left unburied too long as resentment pools in it, or — most hauntingly — the corpse-driving trade of Xiangxi, where Taoist priests were hired to walk the dead home. Migrant workers who died far away had to be returned for burial in ancestral ground, so priests moved them at night in hopping caravans, ringing a bell to warn the living to shutter their windows, the corpses bound to bamboo poles that made them bob as porters carried them — a folk memory of a real funerary logistics business that became a monster. The jiangshi hunts by breath: hold yours, and it cannot find you.

Death & Rising

Qing-dynasty literati collections codified older corpse-lore; the Xiangxi corpse-walking trade gave it its unforgettable gait. The pale rigid face and Qing official's robes of the modern image come from Hong Kong cinema.

Attested in Qing-dynasty tales (Zibuyu, Liaozhai zhiyi)Era Qing dynasty to present

Form

A rigid corpse in Qing burial robes, greenish-white flesh, arms outstretched, a paper talisman fluttering from its forehead — hopping, always hopping.

Powers

Qi-draining, tireless pursuit in bounds, superhuman rigidity-strength; advanced jiangshi in the old tales grow white fur, then wings, ascending a whole taxonomy of corpse-evolution.

Weaknesses & Wards

An extensive folk arsenal: glutinous rice, red-inked chicken-blood talismans, the wood of a peach tree, mirrors, a rooster's crow, the breath held in your chest, and an eight-sided ba gua mirror above the door.

The Approach

Imagine a corpse that never rests. It does not shamble like a zombie, nor does it seduce its victims like a vampire. Instead, it hops. Rigid as a board, arms outstretched, it pursues the living with a relentless, silent hunger for their breath. This is the jiangshi, the 'stiff corpse' of Chinese folklore, a creature that has terrorized villages for centuries and hopped its way into modern pop culture via kung-fu horror comedies. It is a monster that is at once deeply terrifying and absurdly comic, a product of very real fears about death and the afterlife, and a testament to the power of folklore to adapt and endure.

Etymology

The term 'jiangshi' (殭屍/僵屍) literally translates as 'stiff corpse' or 'rigid corpse.' The character 'jiang' (僵) means 'stiff,' 'rigid,' or 'numb,' while 'shi' (屍) means 'corpse' or 'dead body.' The name directly references the creature's most distinctive trait: its body is so rigid with rigor mortis that it cannot bend its joints, forcing it to hop rather than walk.

Cultural Ownership

Chinese folk religion and mythology, with deep roots in Qing Dynasty-era ghost stories and folklore. The concept is shared across Greater China, including Hong Kong, Taiwan, and overseas Chinese communities, with regional variations in Southeast Asia. It is distinct from, though often confused with, the European vampire.

Disputed Identifications

There is scholarly debate about whether the jiangshi should be classified as a vampire, zombie, or a unique category of Chinese undead. Some argue it is a form of 'hopping ghost' (related to the 'pò' soul), while others see it as a reanimated corpse with vampiric traits. The modern 'hopping vampire' interpretation is heavily influenced by 1980s Hong Kong cinema and may not reflect older folk traditions.

Description

Appearance

The jiangshi is typically depicted as a corpse in advanced stages of rigor mortis, with pale or greenish skin, often wearing Qing Dynasty-era official robes or funeral attire. Its arms are outstretched rigidly in front of it, and its body cannot bend. The face is often described as having a blank, staring expression, with perhaps a glowing or vacant gaze. In modern cinema, it often has a paper talisman (fu) stuck to its forehead, placed there by a Taoist priest to control or immobilize it. The skin may be dry and leathery, and the creature may emit a foul odor of decay.

Behavior

The jiangshi moves by hopping, with its legs held together and stiff, unable to bend at the knees or ankles. It is driven by a primal hunger for life force (qi) from the living, which it absorbs through breathing or by draining blood. It is most active at night and is said to be able to sense the breath of living creatures, tracking them by their exhalations. It is mindless and relentless, but can be controlled by a Taoist priest using a talisman, or stopped by various countermeasures such as holding one's breath, throwing sticky rice, or using a mirror. It is not inherently malicious in the sense of having a personality; it is a driven, mindless corpse.

Domains

Jiangshi are found in liminal spaces: roadsides, abandoned buildings, graveyards, mountains, and especially along routes where coffins are transported. They are said to appear in moonlit villages and near places where death has occurred recently or where burial rites were improperly performed. They are creatures of the night, and are rarely, if ever, seen in daylight. They are associated with the boundary between the living and the dead, and with places where qi is stagnant or corrupted.

Symbols

The jiangshi is symbolized by the paper talisman (fu) used to control it, the Taoist Eight Trigrams mirror (bagua) used to repel it, sticky rice, and the color yellow (associated with Taoist ritual). It is also symbolized by the rigid, outstretched arms and the hopping motion, which have become iconic in popular culture. The number 'four' is considered unlucky and associated with death, and jiangshi are often linked to the fourth month or the Ghost Festival (Zhongyuan).

Powers & Abilities

Rigid Body / Hopping Locomotion

Source Version All versions, from Qing texts to modern cinema

Description

The jiangshi's body is completely rigid, forcing it to move by hopping with both legs together. This is its defining trait and is often seen as a limitation, but it also makes it impervious to many forms of physical attack that would require bending or joint manipulation.

Limitations

Cannot run, climb stairs easily, or turn corners smoothly; can be outrun by a fast human, though it is relentless.

Contradictions

Some modern portrayals show it moving more fluidly, but the hopping is the canonical trait.

Qi Absorption / Life Force Drain

Source Version Qing-era texts and folk tradition

Description

The jiangshi feeds by absorbing the qi (life force) of living beings, often through their breath. It can also drain blood, though this is more emphasized in modern adaptations. It is said to kill by 'breathing in' the victim's life force.

Limitations

Needs to be close to its victim; can be foiled if the victim holds their breath.

Contradictions

Older sources emphasize qi absorption; modern films often show it biting necks like a vampire.

Superhuman Strength

Source Version Qing texts and modern cinema

Description

The jiangshi possesses immense physical strength, able to break through doors, crush bones, and toss aside grown men with ease.

Limitations

Not invincible; can be restrained or repelled by Taoist rituals.

Contradictions

None significant.

Sensing the Living

Source Version Folk tradition and Qing texts

Description

The jiangshi can sense the breath of living creatures, allowing it to track prey even in darkness. It is drawn to the sound of breathing and the warmth of life.

Limitations

If a person holds their breath, the jiangshi may lose track of them.

Contradictions

Some sources say it also senses by smell or sound.

Controlled by Talisman (Fu)

Source Version Modern cinema, with roots in Taoist folk magic

Description

A Taoist priest can control a jiangshi by affixing a paper talisman to its forehead, rendering it motionless or directing its movements. This is a common trope in 'Mr. Vampire' films and is rooted in Taoist ritual practice.

Limitations

The talisman can be removed or become ineffective, causing the jiangshi to go berserk.

Contradictions

In older texts, talismans are used to repel or immobilize, not necessarily to command.

Invulnerability to conventional weapons

Source Version Qing texts and modern cinema

Description

The jiangshi's rigid body is resistant to swords, knives, and even bullets, which may simply bounce off or cause minimal damage. It is not truly immortal, but is hard to kill.

Limitations

Vulnerable to Taoist magic, fire, and certain ritual objects.

Contradictions

Some sources say it can be killed by decapitation or burning.

Stories & Myths

The Corpse That Rose Due to a Cat

Source Chinese folk traditionCulture Han ChinesePeriod Qing Dynasty and earlier

Summary

A common folk tale explains that if a cat (especially a black cat) jumps over a corpse before burial, the corpse will rise as a jiangshi. The cat's electric energy or its own spirit animates the dead body, which then attacks the living.

Participants

A corpse

A cat

Villagers

Consequences

The villagers must call a Taoist priest to subdue the jiangshi, often resorting to burning the body.

Variants

Some versions say a dog or other animal can also cause this.

The Improper Burial

Source Qing Dynasty tale collections (e.g., Ji Xiaolan, Yuan Mei)Culture Han ChinesePeriod 18th century

Summary

A person who dies a violent death, is buried in an improper location (e.g., in a 'yin' or shadowy place), or whose funeral rites are not properly performed, may rise as a jiangshi. The corpse's soul (the 'po') is unable to depart and instead animates the body.

Participants

The deceased

Family members

Taoist priest

Consequences

The jiangshi terrorizes the family and village until a priest performs corrective rituals and reburies the body properly.

Variants

In some versions, the corpse rises due to an excess of yin energy in the burial site.

The Coffin Transporters

Source Folk tradition and modern cinemaCulture Han Chinese, particularly southern ChinaPeriod Qing Dynasty to modern

Summary

A common motif involves 'coffin carriers' who transport corpses over long distances for reburial in their ancestral homeland. To save energy, they use the jiangshi's hopping motion, making the corpse 'walk' or hop behind them, controlled by a Taoist priest with a talisman. This is the origin of the famous 'hopping corpse' image.

Participants

Coffin carriers

Taoist priest

The jiangshi

Consequences

If the priest loses control, the jiangshi attacks the carriers and nearby villagers.

Variants

This practice is sometimes cited as a historical reality, but its connection to jiangshi is likely a folkloric explanation for the 'hopping' motion used to transport corpses.

The Painted Skin (adapted as a jiangshi story)

Source Pu Songling's 'Strange Stories from a Chinese Studio'Culture Han ChinesePeriod 17th-18th century

Summary

While not strictly a jiangshi story, 'The Painted Skin' is often grouped with jiangshi lore. A man encounters a beautiful woman who is actually a demon wearing a painted human skin. The demon is eventually exorcised by a Taoist priest. This story emphasizes the theme of the corpse-like entity that preys on the living.

Participants

The scholar Wang

The demon

A Taoist priest

Consequences

Wang is killed by the demon but is resurrected by the priest's magic.

Variants

This story has been adapted into films and is often associated with jiangshi in popular culture, though the creature is more of a fox spirit or demon.

Meng Po (孟婆)

Type related entitySource Chinese mythology

Description

Meng Po is the goddess of forgetfulness who serves the 'Soup of Forgetfulness' to souls before reincarnation. A jiangshi is a corpse whose soul has not properly transitioned to the afterlife, so it is conceptually linked to Meng Po's domain, though they do not directly interact in traditional stories.

Version Note

The jiangshi is stuck between worlds, a failure of the afterlife process that Meng Po oversees.

Contradictions & Disputes

Vampire or Zombie?

Position A

The jiangshi is a type of vampire, as it drains life force and can be repelled by religious symbols.

Position A — evidence

Modern films like 'Mr. Vampire' emphasize blood-drinking and the use of Taoist talismans, which are analogous to Christian crosses.

Position B

The jiangshi is a type of zombie, as it is a reanimated corpse without a soul or consciousness.

Position B — evidence

Qing-era texts describe it as a mindless, driven corpse, more akin to a zombie than a cunning vampire.

Scholarly Consensus

There is no consensus. The jiangshi is best understood as a unique category of Chinese undead, with traits of both vampire and zombie, but not fully either.

Method of Feeding

Position A

The jiangshi drains blood through a bite.

Position A — evidence

Hong Kong cinema explicitly shows jiangshi biting necks.

Position B

The jiangshi absorbs qi through the victim's breath.

Position B — evidence

Folk tradition and Qing texts describe the jiangshi as inhaling the victim's breath to steal their qi.

Scholarly Consensus

The qi-absorption is likely the older, more authentic folkloric belief, while the blood-drinking is a modern cinematic addition influenced by Western vampires.

Origin of the Hopping Motion

Position A

The hopping motion is a folkloric invention, representing rigor mortis.

Position A — evidence

Rigor mortis makes joints stiff, so a corpse would indeed hop if it could move at all.

Position B

The hopping motion is based on the real practice of 'corpse walking' or transporting bodies by tying them to poles and making them 'hop' or sway.

Position B — evidence

Historical accounts describe 'corpse walkers' in rural China who used a pole to make a corpse appear to hop or walk, as a way to transport it over long distances for burial.

Scholarly Consensus

The truth is likely a combination: the folkloric image may have been reinforced by the real practice of corpse transport.
