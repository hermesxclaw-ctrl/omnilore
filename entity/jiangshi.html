Who It Is

The jiangshi is China's vampire — a corpse gone rigid with death but not still, arms locked straight ahead, hopping in stiff-legged bounds because its joints no longer bend, draining the living not of blood but of qi, the life-breath itself. Qing dynasty tales explain where they come from: a corpse struck by lightning, a cat jumping over a coffin, a body left unburied too long as resentment pools in it, or — most hauntingly — the corpse-driving trade of Xiangxi, where Taoist priests were hired to walk the dead home. Migrant workers who died far away had to be returned for burial in ancestral ground, so priests moved them at night in hopping caravans, ringing a bell to warn the living to shutter their windows, the corpses bound to bamboo poles that made them bob as porters carried them — a folk memory of a real funerary logistics business that became a monster. The jiangshi hunts by breath: hold yours, and it cannot find you. Jiangshi are found in liminal spaces: roadsides, abandoned buildings, graveyards, mountains, and especially along routes where coffins are transported. They are said to appear in moonlit villages and near places where death has occurred recently or where burial rites were improperly performed. They are creatures of the night, and are rarely, if ever, seen in daylight. They are associated with the boundary between the living and the dead, and with places where qi is stagnant or corrupted.

Death & Rising

Qing-dynasty literati collections codified older corpse-lore; the Xiangxi corpse-walking trade gave it its unforgettable gait. The pale rigid face and Qing official's robes of the modern image come from Hong Kong cinema.

Attested in Qing-dynasty tales (Zibuyu, Liaozhai zhiyi)Era Qing dynasty to present

Form

A rigid corpse in Qing burial robes, greenish-white flesh, arms outstretched, a paper talisman fluttering from its forehead — hopping, always hopping.

Powers

Rigid Body / Hopping Locomotion The jiangshi's body is completely rigid, forcing it to move by hopping with both legs together. This is its defining trait and is often seen as a limitation, but it also makes it impervious to many forms of physical attack that would require bending or joint manipulation. All versions, from Qing texts to modern cinema Cannot run, climb stairs easily, or turn corners smoothly; can be outrun by a fast human, though it is relentless. Some modern portrayals show it moving more fluidly, but the hopping is the canonical trait.

Qi Absorption / Life Force Drain The jiangshi feeds by absorbing the qi (life force) of living beings, often through their breath. It can also drain blood, though this is more emphasized in modern adaptations. It is said to kill by 'breathing in' the victim's life force. Qing-era texts and folk tradition Needs to be close to its victim; can be foiled if the victim holds their breath. Older sources emphasize qi absorption; modern films often show it biting necks like a vampire.

Superhuman Strength The jiangshi possesses immense physical strength, able to break through doors, crush bones, and toss aside grown men with ease. Qing texts and modern cinema Not invincible; can be restrained or repelled by Taoist rituals. None significant.

Sensing the Living The jiangshi can sense the breath of living creatures, allowing it to track prey even in darkness. It is drawn to the sound of breathing and the warmth of life. Folk tradition and Qing texts If a person holds their breath, the jiangshi may lose track of them. Some sources say it also senses by smell or sound.

Controlled by Talisman (Fu) A Taoist priest can control a jiangshi by affixing a paper talisman to its forehead, rendering it motionless or directing its movements. This is a common trope in 'Mr. Vampire' films and is rooted in Taoist ritual practice. Modern cinema, with roots in Taoist folk magic The talisman can be removed or become ineffective, causing the jiangshi to go berserk. In older texts, talismans are used to repel or immobilize, not necessarily to command.

Invulnerability to conventional weapons The jiangshi's rigid body is resistant to swords, knives, and even bullets, which may simply bounce off or cause minimal damage. It is not truly immortal, but is hard to kill. Qing texts and modern cinema Vulnerable to Taoist magic, fire, and certain ritual objects. Some sources say it can be killed by decapitation or burning.

Behavior & Habitat

The jiangshi moves by hopping, with its legs held together and stiff, unable to bend at the knees or ankles. It is driven by a primal hunger for life force (qi) from the living, which it absorbs through breathing or by draining blood. It is most active at night and is said to be able to sense the breath of living creatures, tracking them by their exhalations. It is mindless and relentless, but can be controlled by a Taoist priest using a talisman, or stopped by various countermeasures such as holding one's breath, throwing sticky rice, or using a mirror. It is not inherently malicious in the sense of having a personality; it is a driven, mindless corpse.

Weaknesses & Wards

An extensive folk arsenal: glutinous rice, red-inked chicken-blood talismans, the wood of a peach tree, mirrors, a rooster's crow, the breath held in your chest, and an eight-sided ba gua mirror above the door.

Accounts

A common folk tale explains that if a cat (especially a black cat) jumps over a corpse before burial, the corpse will rise as a jiangshi. The cat's electric energy or its own spirit animates the dead body, which then attacks the living.

A person who dies a violent death, is buried in an improper location (e.g., in a 'yin' or shadowy place), or whose funeral rites are not properly performed, may rise as a jiangshi. The corpse's soul (the 'po') is unable to depart and instead animates the body.

A common motif involves 'coffin carriers' who transport corpses over long distances for reburial in their ancestral homeland. To save energy, they use the jiangshi's hopping motion, making the corpse 'walk' or hop behind them, controlled by a Taoist priest with a talisman. This is the origin of the famous 'hopping corpse' image.

While not strictly a jiangshi story, 'The Painted Skin' is often grouped with jiangshi lore. A man encounters a beautiful woman who is actually a demon wearing a painted human skin. The demon is eventually exorcised by a Taoist priest. This story emphasizes the theme of the corpse-like entity that preys on the living.

Cultural Significance

The talisman-browed hopping vampire of Mr. Vampire (1985) and its imitators made the jiangshi a pan-Asian pop icon; the corpse-driving trade of Hunan remains one of folklore's best examples of real logistics mythologized.

Vampire or Zombie?
The jiangshi is a type of vampire, as it drains life force and can be repelled by religious symbols. ⇌ The jiangshi is a type of zombie, as it is a reanimated corpse without a soul or consciousness.
Evidence for A: Modern films like 'Mr. Vampire' emphasize blood-drinking and the use of Taoist talismans, which are analogous to Christi
Evidence for B: Qing-era texts describe it as a mindless, driven corpse, more akin to a zombie than a cunning vampire.
Method of Feeding
The jiangshi drains blood through a bite. ⇌ The jiangshi absorbs qi through the victim's breath.
Evidence for A: Hong Kong cinema explicitly shows jiangshi biting necks.
Evidence for B: Folk tradition and Qing texts describe the jiangshi as inhaling the victim's breath to steal their qi.

What We Don't Know

Further research is needed into regional variations of jiangshi lore within China (e.g., differences between northern and southern traditions). The exact historical origins of the 'hopping corpse' practice are still debated and require primary source documentation. The influence of the jiangshi on other Asian horror traditions (e.g., Japanese and Korean) is under-explored. Additional research into the jiangshi's role in modern Chinese internet culture and its adaptation in non-Hong Kong media (e.g., mainland Chinese films) would be valuable.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of the jiangshi as a 'rigid revenant' — a corpse that is all stiffness and hunger, a stark contrast to the Western vampire's fluid grace. It's a monster that embodies the fear of a bad burial and a restless soul, hopping through the night like a broken marionette, its only goal to steal the breath of the living. It's a creature that is both a nightmare and a punchline, a testament to the enduring power of folklore to evolve and entertain.
