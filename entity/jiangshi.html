Who It Is

Imagine a corpse that never rests. It does not shamble like a zombie, nor does it seduce its victims like a vampire. Instead, it hops. Rigid as a board, arms outstretched, it pursues the living with a relentless, silent hunger for their breath. This is the jiangshi, the 'stiff corpse' of Chinese folklore, a creature that has terrorized villages for centuries and hopped its way into modern pop culture via kung-fu horror comedies. It is a monster that is at once deeply terrifying and absurdly comic, a product of very real fears about death and the afterlife, and a testament to the power of folklore to adapt and endure.

The jiangshi moves by hopping, with its legs held together and stiff, unable to bend at the knees or ankles. It is driven by a primal hunger for life force (qi) from the living, which it absorbs through breathing or by draining blood. It is most active at night and is said to be able to sense the breath of living creatures, tracking them by their exhalations. It is mindless and relentless, but can be controlled by a Taoist priest using a talisman, or stopped by various countermeasures such as holding one's breath, throwing sticky rice, or using a mirror. It is not inherently malicious

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The term 'jiangshi' (殭屍/僵屍) literally translates as 'stiff corpse' or 'rigid corpse.' The character 'jiang' (僵) means 'stiff,' 'rigid,' or 'numb,' while 'shi' (屍) means 'corpse' or 'dead body.' The name directly references the creature's most distinctive trait: its body is so rigid with rigor mortis

Pronunciation — jyahng-shir (Mandarin: jiāngshī, literally 'jyahng-shrr'); Cantonese: geong si (roughly 'gawng see')

Aliases — Chinese Hopping Vampire, Geong Si (Cantonese), The Stiff Corpse, Chiang-shih (Wade-Giles), Giang Shi (Vietnamese), Kiang Shi

Powers
Rigid Body / Hopping Locomotion

The jiangshi's body is completely rigid, forcing it to move by hopping with both legs together. This is its defining trait and is often seen as a limitation, but it also makes it impervious to many forms of physical attack that would require bending or joint manipulation.

Limit: Cannot run, climb stairs easily, or turn corners smoothly; can be outrun by a fast human, though it is relentless.

Qi Absorption / Life Force Drain

The jiangshi feeds by absorbing the qi (life force) of living beings, often through their breath. It can also drain blood, though this is more emphasized in modern adaptations. It is said to kill by 'breathing in' the victim's life force.

Limit: Needs to be close to its victim; can be foiled if the victim holds their breath.

Superhuman Strength

The jiangshi possesses immense physical strength, able to break through doors, crush bones, and toss aside grown men with ease.

Limit: Not invincible; can be restrained or repelled by Taoist rituals.

Sensing the Living

The jiangshi can sense the breath of living creatures, allowing it to track prey even in darkness. It is drawn to the sound of breathing and the warmth of life.

Limit: If a person holds their breath, the jiangshi may lose track of them.

Controlled by Talisman (Fu)

A Taoist priest can control a jiangshi by affixing a paper talisman to its forehead, rendering it motionless or directing its movements. This is a common trope in 'Mr. Vampire' films and is rooted in Taoist ritual practice.

Limit: The talisman can be removed or become ineffective, causing the jiangshi to go berserk.

Invulnerability to conventional weapons

The jiangshi's rigid body is resistant to swords, knives, and even bullets, which may simply bounce off or cause minimal damage. It is not truly immortal, but is hard to kill.

Limit: Vulnerable to Taoist magic, fire, and certain ritual objects.

Form & Signs

Appearance — The jiangshi is typically depicted as a corpse in advanced stages of rigor mortis, with pale or greenish skin, often wearing Qing Dynasty-era official robes or funeral attire. Its arms are outstretched rigidly in front of it, and its body cannot bend. The face is often described as having a blank, staring expression, with perhaps a glowing or vacant gaze. Symbols — The jiangshi is symbolized by the paper talisman (fu) used to control it, the Taoist Eight Trigrams mirror (bagua) used to repel it, sticky rice, and the color yellow (associated with Taoist ritual). It is also symbolized by the rigid, outstretched arms and the hopping motion, which have become iconic in popular culture. The number 'four' is considered unlucky and associated with death, and jiangshi are often linked to the fourth month

Domains — Jiangshi are found in liminal spaces: roadsides, abandoned buildings, graveyards, mountains, and especially along routes where coffins are transported. They are said to appear in moonlit villages and near places where death has occurred recently or where burial rites were improperly performed. Accounts I
The Corpse That Rose Due to a CatChinese folk tradition

Qing Dynasty and earlier

A common folk tale explains that if a cat (especially a black cat) jumps over a corpse before burial, the corpse will rise as a jiangshi. The cat's electric energy or its own spirit animates the dead body, which then attacks the living. The villagers must call a Taoist priest to subdue the jiangshi, often resorting to burning the body.

The Improper BurialQing Dynasty tale collections (e.g., Ji Xiaolan, Yuan Mei)

18th century

A person who dies a violent death, is buried in an improper location (e.g., in a 'yin' or shadowy place), or whose funeral rites are not properly performed, may rise as a jiangshi. The corpse's soul (the 'po') is unable to depart and instead animates the body. The jiangshi terrorizes the family and village until a priest performs corrective rituals and reburies the body properly.

Accounts II
The Coffin TransportersFolk tradition and modern cinema

Qing Dynasty to modern

A common motif involves 'coffin carriers' who transport corpses over long distances for reburial in their ancestral homeland. To save energy, they use the jiangshi's hopping motion, making the corpse 'walk' or hop behind them, controlled by a Taoist priest with a talisman. This is the origin of the famous 'hopping corpse' image. If the priest loses control, the jiangshi attacks the carriers and nearby villagers.

The Painted Skin (adapted as a jiangshi story)Pu Songling's 'Strange Stories from a Chinese Studio'

17th-18th century

While not strictly a jiangshi story, 'The Painted Skin' is often grouped with jiangshi lore. A man encounters a beautiful woman who is actually a demon wearing a painted human skin. The demon is eventually exorcised by a Taoist priest. This story emphasizes the theme of the corpse-like entity that preys on the living. Wang is killed by the demon but is resurrected by the priest's magic.

The jiangshi is just a Chinese vampire. — While it shares some traits with vampires (draining life force, being repelled by talismans), it is fundamentally different. It is a reanimated corpse, not a person who has been 'turned.' It lacks the cunning, seduction, and personality of a Western vampire. It is more accurately a unique type of undead.

The jiangshi is a zombie. — It is not a zombie in the modern sense (a decaying, flesh-eating corpse). A jiangshi is a stiff, hopping corpse that drains life force, not a shambling, brain-eating monster. It is also tied to specific Taoist rituals and beliefs about the soul.

Jiangshi are afraid of the color red. — This is a common misconception. The jiangshi is repelled by Taoist talismans, which are often written in red ink, but the color red itself has no inherent power over it. It is the talisman's spiritual power, not the color, that is effective.

Jiangshi can be killed by a stake through the heart. — This is a Western vampire trope. Traditional Chinese methods for dealing with a jiangshi include burning the body, decapitation, or using a Taoist priest to perform a ritual to 'send it away' or 'destroy' it. A wooden stake is not a standard method.
