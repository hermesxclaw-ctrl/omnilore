Who It Is

Majora is the titular antagonist of The Legend of Zelda: Majora's Mask and one of the darkest, most unsettling villains in Nintendo's catalog. Not a traditional demon king but an ancient, sentient mask imbued with chaotic, malevolent power, Majora was created by an unspecified 'ancient tribe' for use in hexing rituals. Abandoned and sealed away, the mask was eventually stolen by the Skull Kid — a lonely, mischievous forest imp who, under Majora's influence, becomes a vessel for its apocalyptic designs. Majora's goal is not conquest or power, but destruction for its own sake — it pulls the Moon from orbit toward Termina with the apparent intent of annihilating all life, treating the world as a game to be discarded when its amusement ends.

Emergence

According to the Happy Mask Salesman, Majora's Mask is an ancient artifact created by a long-vanished tribe for use in their hexing and cursing rituals. The mask was imbued with so much dark power that the tribe eventually sealed it away in shadow, never to be used — fearing the catastrophe it could unleash. How the Happy Mask Salesman acquired it is never fully explained; he mentions it was stolen from him by the Skull Kid. The Skull Kid, already bitter and lonely after being abandoned by his giant friends (the Four Giants who departed to guard the four directions of Termina), was easy prey for Majora's influence. The mask amplified his mischief into malice, his pranks into destruction. The Skull Kid gradually became less the wearer and more the puppet — Majora speaks through him, acts through him, and when Link finally defeats the Skull Kid atop the Clock Tower, the mask discards him as 'garbage' and takes direct control.

Form

Majora's Mask itself is a heart-shaped wooden mask with a striking, unnerving expression: large, bulbous yellow eyes with tiny black pupils, a painted nose, and a wide, thin-lipped grin. Red and purple paint decorate its surface, and four spikes — two at the top, two at the sides — protrude outward. When worn, the eyes glow and roll with disturbing autonomy. In its first battle form, Majora floats in midair, the mask spinning and twisting on a shadowy body from which fleshy tendrils dangle — it moves with a strange, alien grace. The second form, Majora's Incarnation, sprouts thin, spindly limbs and begins sprinting and dancing around the arena with unnerving, jerky movements. The final form, Majora's Wrath, abandons all pretense of the mask — it becomes a muscular, whipping mass of tentacles and claws, the mask's features stretched into a monstrous, screaming face with jagged teeth.

Powers

Majora possesses the power to pull celestial bodies from their orbits — the Moon's descent toward Termina is its doing. It can curse individuals into other forms (Link into a Deku Scrub), control the minds of those who wear it or come near it, and create barriers of dark magic. In battle, its abilities evolve: in its floating form, it spins like a top, fires energy beams, and summons smaller masks to attack; in its Incarnation, it gains blinding speed, wall-running, and a screaming attack that stuns; as Majora's Wrath, it wields massive whipping arms, generates shockwaves, and fights with monstrous ferocity. Its most insidious power is psychological — it preys on loneliness, bitterness, and resentment, amplifying those emotions until the host becomes a willing instrument of destruction. The mask seems to have a consciousness that survives all three battle forms — only the complete destruction of the mask's physical form ends its influence.

Behavior & Habitat

Majora's behavior is the most disturbing aspect of the entity. It treats the annihilation of a world as a game. When Link enters the Moon, he finds a tranquil field with a single tree in the center, where children wearing the masks of the Four Bosses play. They speak to Link with cheerful, cruel detachment: 'Your true face... What kind of... face is it? I wonder... The face under the mask... Is that... your true face?' When Link has given away all his masks, the children vanish, and only the final mask — Majora's Mask — remains. During the boss fight, Majora delivers only one line after discarding the Skull Kid, dismissing him as 'garbage.' This casual cruelty — treating sentient beings as disposable toys — is the core of Majora's personality. It creates elaborate, torturous scenarios (poisoning Woodfall's swamp, freezing Snowhead in eternal winter, polluting Great Bay, cursing Ikana Canyon with undead) not for any strategic purpose, but simply because it can — because the suffering is amusing.

Weaknesses & Wards

Majora's primary weakness is the power of the Four Giants, whose combined strength can halt the Moon's descent and weaken the mask's hold on Termina. Link can only summon them after freeing each Giant from the curse Majora placed on their respective temples. In combat, Majora's floating form is vulnerable when it stops spinning; its Incarnation can be stunned with arrows; and Majora's Wrath is vulnerable to well-timed sword strikes when it pauses between attacks. The Fierce Deity's Mask — obtained by collecting all 24 (or in the 3DS version, all 20) non-transformation masks and trading them to the Moon Children — transforms Link into the Fierce Deity, a towering, white-haired warrior whose powered-up sword attacks make short work of Majora's final form. Symbolically, Majora is defeated by the very forces it cannot comprehend: friendship (the Four Giants answering the Skull Kid's old friends' call), community (the masks collected from the people Link helps), and selfless courage (Link's refusal to abandon Termina despite having no personal stake in it).

Accounts

The Happy Mask Salesman, traveling through the Lost Woods, is ambushed by the Skull Kid — a forest imp wearing Majora's Mask. The Skull Kid knocks him unconscious and steals the mask, along with Link's Ocarina of Time. The mask's power immediately amplifies the Skull Kid's mischief, cursing Link into a Deku Scrub. The Salesman, frantic, begs Link to recover the mask within three days, promising to return him to human form. The stolen mask sets the entire plot in motion.

Link, now human again, discovers he has only 72 hours before the Moon — pulled from orbit by Majora's power — crashes into Termina, annihilating everything. Using the Ocarina of Time to reset the three-day cycle, he must free the Four Giants imprisoned in temples across Termina, collect masks by healing the troubled people of Clock Town and its environs, and ultimately confront the Skull Kid atop the Clock Tower at the Carnival of Time. Each cycle, Link grows stronger, but the emotional toll of seeing friends' suffering reset and repeat — especially the doomed romance of Anju and Kafei — makes this one of gaming's most poignant narratives.

After summoning the Four Giants to halt the Moon, Link is pulled inside it. He finds himself in a beautiful green field with a single tree, where five masked children play. Each child, wearing a boss mask, asks Link to play. Only when Link gives away all his non-transformation masks — symbolically surrendering the identities he's collected — does the final room open, where Majora's Mask sits alone on a pedestal. The surreal, childlike dialogue and the peaceful setting contrasted with the apocalyptic stakes make this one of gaming's most haunting sequences.

Field Notes

⁙ Majora's Mask was developed in only one year — an unprecedented feat — using recycled assets from Ocarina of Time, which gave the developers the freedom to experiment with darker themes.

⁙ The Moon's face becomes more menacing as the three days progress — its expression shifts from neutral to angry, and tears of fire begin falling on the final day.

⁙ The name 'Majora' may be a play on 'Jora,' a Hindi word for 'pair' or 'joined,' referencing the mask's dual nature, or derived from 'major,' suggesting importance.

⁙ In the Japanese version, Majora's Incarnation is named 'Mujura no Kamen no Keshin' — Mujura being the original Japanese name for Majora, a nonsense word with an alien, otherworldly feel.

⁙ The Happy Mask Salesman's manic behavior when Link fails to retrieve the mask — 'You've met with a terrible fate, haven't you?' — is one of the most quoted lines in Zelda history.

⁙ The Fierce Deity's Mask transforms Link into a being possibly connected to Majora — its single glowing eye and the fact it's stored inside the Moon suggest the Fierce Deity may be Majora's counterpart or rival.

Cultural Significance

Majora's Mask (the game) is widely regarded as one of the greatest video games ever made, and Majora as its villain is uniquely disturbing in a way that separates it from Ganon's more conventional evil. The game's themes of grief, loss, and healing — and Majora's role as a force that represents the opposite: nihilism, cruelty, and the refusal to heal — have been the subject of extensive analysis. The three-day cycle creates an atmosphere of impending doom that no other Zelda game has replicated. Fan theories about Majora's Mask abound: the game is an allegory for the five stages of grief (denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance); Link is dead and Termina is a purgatory; Majora is a deity from an ancient, forgotten religion. The 'Ben Drowned' creepypasta — an early internet horror story about a haunted Majora's Mask cartridge — became one of the most famous video game urban legends. Majora's design as a heart-shaped mask with staring eyes and a frozen grin is instantly recognizable in gaming culture.

Modern Interpretations

Majora has appeared in Hyrule Warriors as a playable weapon — not the mask itself but a character (Skull Kid) summoning the Moon's power — and in Super Smash Bros. as an Assist Trophy and stage hazard on the Great Bay stage. The Moon — now a beloved meme — appears looming over Termina Field, and in Smash Ultimate it can crash into the stage if an Assist Trophy summons it. The 'Dawn of a New Day' scene, where the Moon is destroyed and a rainbow forms, is one of the most cathartic moments in gaming and has been celebrated in fan art and remixes. The Zelda community has created extensive mods, randomizers, and the 'Majora's Mask 3D Restoration Project' that patches the controversial changes in the Nintendo 3DS remake. A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine a mask that contains the concentrated malice of a dead civilization's darkest rituals — a mask that was never meant to be worn, only feared. Now imagine a lonely, heartbroken child finds it. The mask whispers to the child's pain, amplifying every grievance: 'They left you. They don't care. Make them pay. It would be funny, wouldn't it?' And soon, the child isn't the one in control. The mask is pulling a moon from the sky because it finds the terror on people's faces amusing. This isn't a villain with a tragic backstory or a world-conquering plan. This is chaos in its purest form — destruction as a game, suffering as entertainment. That's Majora: the mask that turns loneliness into apocalypse, and thinks it's hilarious.
