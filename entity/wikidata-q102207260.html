The Approach

Four is the only number that is both the sum and product of two equal numbers—2+2 and 2×2—and its shape has evolved from a simple cross scratched in ancient India to the open-topped glyph on your calculator. Yet in East Asia, it is a whisper of death, so feared that buildings skip the fourth floor. This is the story of a digit that is at once a mathematical cornerstone, a cultural taboo, and a typographic chameleon.

Content advisory. Discusses cultural superstitions linking the number 4 to death in East Asian cultures; includes mention of tetraphobia and associated practices such as avoiding floor numbers. No graphic or violent content.

Etymology

The English word 'four' descends from Proto-Indo-European *kʷetwóres, which also gave rise to Latin 'quattuor', Greek 'téssares', and Sanskrit 'catvāraḥ'. The digit's shape evolved from Brahmi numerals, where four was represented by a cross-like symbol.

Cultural Ownership

The concept of the number 4 is universal, but the specific glyph has a history rooted in Indian Brahmi numerals, later transmitted through Arabic and European adaptations. Cultural significance varies widely: sacred in some Native American traditions (Zia), unlucky in East Asia, and neutral in most Western cultures.

Disputed Identifications

There is no significant dispute about the identity of the number 4 itself. However, the origin of the modern glyph's shape has been debated; some attribute it to Indian origins, while others note independent developments in different scripts. The exact evolution from Brahmi to modern forms is not fully documented in the source material.

Description

Appearance

The digit 4 in most modern typefaces has an ascender, with a closed top and a diagonal stroke. In text figures (old-style numerals), the glyph has a descender and may appear as an open-top variant. On seven-segment displays, the 4 is typically shown with an open top, resembling a 'h' without the left vertical. Some television channel logos use an 'open 4' with the opening on the side, resembling the Canadian Aboriginal syllabics letter ᔦ.

Symbols & Iconography

The number 4 is often associated with the square, the cross, and the tetrahedron. In alchemy, it represents the four elements (earth, air, fire, water). In Chinese culture, it is a homophone for death and is often avoided. In the Zia tribe, four is sacred, representing the four cardinal directions, the four seasons, and the four stages of life.

Behaviour & Personality

As a number, 4 is stable, square, and foundational. It is the smallest composite number and the first square number after 1. In numerology, 4 is often associated with practicality, order, and hard work.

Domains & Habitats

The number 4 appears throughout mathematics, science, culture, and technology. It is the number of dimensions in spacetime (Minkowski space), the number of fundamental forces, and the number of seasons in temperate climates. It is ubiquitous in everyday life, from the four cardinal directions to the four suits in a deck of cards.

Version Differences

The glyph has evolved from a cross in Brahmi numerals to a more cursive form in Arabic, and finally to the modern closed-top shape in Europe. Different fonts and display technologies (e.g., seven-segment displays) show different variants. The cultural perception of 4 varies dramatically: unlucky in East Asia, sacred in some Native American cultures, and neutral in most others.

Evolution of the Hindu-Arabic digit

Source Wikipedia (supplied source material)Culture Indian, Arabic, EuropeanPeriod Ancient to medieval

Summary

The Brahmi numeral for 4 was a cross, which later gained a horizontal line in Shunga, and evolved through Kshatrapa and Pallava scripts. Arabic numerals simplified the cross into a single stroke, and Europeans eventually closed the top to form the modern digit.

Tetraphobia in East Asia

Source Wikipedia (supplied source material)Culture Chinese, Japanese, KoreanPeriod Ongoing

Summary

In many East Asian languages, the word for 'four' is a homophone for 'death'. This has led to the number being avoided in daily life: buildings often skip the fourth floor, phone numbers may avoid 4, and gifts are not given in sets of four.

Participants

East Asian societies

Consequences

The number 4 carries a powerful negative connotation, influencing architecture, business, and social customs.

Variants

The superstition is strongest in China, Taiwan, Japan, Korea, and Vietnam, but the specific severity varies.

Tetraphobia

Source Cultural studies, linguistic analysis

Description

In East Asian cultures, the number 4 is avoided because it sounds like the word for 'death'. This can lead to skipping floors in buildings, avoiding phone numbers with 4, and even affecting pricing. The fear is strong enough to impact real estate and business practices.

Scholarly Context

Tetraphobia is a well-documented cultural phenomenon, studied in anthropology and linguistics. It is not a mere superstition but a deeply ingrained social norm.
